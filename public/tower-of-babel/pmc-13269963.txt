Learner-Educator Co-creation of Student Assessment in Health Professional Education Courses: A Scoping Review
Authors: Laura A. Killam, Christina Godfrey, Mercedes Lock, Renée Gordon, Amanda Ross‐White, Marian Luctkar‐Flude
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCo-creation means engaging learners in working collaboratively to make decisions about course planning, implementation, or student assessment. Involving students in these decisions potentially enhances learning. When co-creation is part of assessment, it may reach more learners but may also pose pragmatic challenges. In this review, we focused on co-creation of assessment that may occur at any time during a health professional course. Systematic examination of learner-educator co-creation is needed to inform how educators can engage strategically in co-creation with healthcare students.ObjectivesThe objective of this scoping review was to examine strategies for collaborative learner-educator decision-making during the design, implementation, or evaluation of student assessment in health professional education.Search MethodsSearches were conducted until August 1, 2024, in six databases with no date limitation (MEDLINE, Embase, Education Source Complete, CINAHL, ERIC and APA PsycInfo). Reference lists of included full-text papers and papers close to meeting inclusion criteria were manually searched, along with publications by conference abstract authors.Selection CriteriaWe included all papers focused on how learners and educators share decision making regarding student assessment during a health professional course. Information about co-creation that occurs outside of a course or is not related to student assessment was excluded.Data Collection and AnalysisStandard procedures for reviewing and summarizing data according to JBI methodology were used. Two reviewers independently screened documents and extracted data. Data about paper characteristics, where co-creation occurs, decisions that are co-created, process descriptions, and author conclusions were extracted and summarized.Main ResultsWe screened 11,517 titles and abstracts, then 341 full-text reports for eligibility. In total, we identified 27 reports published between 1980 and 2025 that describe 25 unique courses where co-creation of assessment has been reported in the literature.Co-creation of assessment was reported in nursing, social work, medicine, health services navigation, paramedicine, pharmacy, occupational therapy and dental technology disciplines, at the undergraduate, graduate, and postgraduate levels. Most courses where co-creation occurred were mandatory. Reports spanned various years of study. Many assessment decisions were made together, predominantly assessment instructions and grading criteria. Diverse ways of co-creating with different levels of student influence over decisions were reported. The most reported strategy for co-creation was discussion during class time or in individual meetings. A wide range of benefits and ways to support co-creation were reported. Barriers, disadvantages, and future considerations were also reported.ConclusionsThis review identifies and synthesizes existing examples of co-creation of assessment that have been integrated into health professional education. While it was mainly portrayed positively across included literature, there remains a lack of rigorous and diverse research in this area. The interventions identified in this review are diverse and highly contextual, limiting generalizability. Our findings provide a preliminary list of reported cases of co-creation of assessment and highlight contextual influences on implementation. Implementation processes need to be described in more detail to promote replication. Future research is needed to explore educator and student experiences, evaluate learning outcomes, and evaluate applicability across contexts to support evidence-informed practice. Co-creation of assessment offers a promising yet under-researched approach to creating more collaborative and authentic assessment strategies. It warrants thoughtful, context-specific application, research, and reporting in the literature.

Background

The Problem

There is growing interest in inclusive and empowering ways of teaching in health professional education. Teacher-directed approaches in education where knowledge is transmitted from experts to passive students such as through lecture and memorizing textbooks may create excessive cognitive load and be ineffective for preparing learners for applied tasks in health professional education (Salari et al., 2018; Spicer et al., 2018), particularly when they are overused or fail to engage learners. Educational reformers throughout history have advocated for learner-engaged approaches to education where the goal is to develop learner metacognition through involving learners more actively in knowledge discovery and problem-solving (Dewey, 1938; Freire, 1973; Giroux, 2020; Thomas, 2022). Several approaches for actively engaging learners in their education have been integrated into health professional education. For example, collaborative problem-solving may occur during clinical placements, simulation, or other interactive teaching strategies. Another approach is to engage learners as partners in decision-making in areas such as curriculum, content, learning activities, and assessment.

For health professional learners, shared decision making with patients, interprofessional teams, and other key persons following graduation has been argued to be a critical skill needed for patient-centered care (Cribb et al., 2017). Cribb et al. (2017) argued that for medical graduates to be prepared to make joint decisions about how to solve healthcare problems with key persons after graduation, it is important to embed opportunities to learn how to co-create during their education.

The term co-creation is used in a variety of ways to describe shared decision making with different parties. In healthcare, co-creation has involved patients, healthcare professionals, and other key persons in collaborative problem-solving (Cribb et al., 2017). Working with others who are more knowledgeable than the learner in an interactive way is theorized to enhance learner development (Vygotsky, 1978). For example, nursing and midwifery learners have been found to gain confidence, improve relationships, and develop their skills when they work with service users and carers to make decisions (O'Connor et al., 2021). Similarly, in both nursing and educational contexts, co-creation has been described as a transformative practice that repositions learners as partners in knowledge construction (Cook-Sather et al., 2019; Dyson, 2018). However, this type of collaboration often occurs in a clinical context, where placement experiences vary widely and it is not possible to guarantee that learners will have the opportunity to co-create. In contrast, embedding co-creation into courses is an equitable strategy that enables educators to intentionally design opportunities for collaborative decision-making that are consistently available to all learners.

However, health professional education is often regulated by external accreditation processes, which prioritize quality assurance, define competencies, and establish measurable outcomes, while at the same time inadvertently limiting flexibility and innovation (Burdick & Dhillon, 2020; Frenk et al., 2010). As Frenk et al. (2010) explain, while these systems aim to ensure quality and accountability they lead to standardized, rigid, and outdated curricula that contribute to a mismatch between graduate competencies and real-world health priorities. This problem further underscores the need for more adaptive, evidence-based participatory pedagogical approaches like learner-educator co-creation in health professional education.

Learner-educator co-creation may occur during course planning, discussions, activities, and assessment. The intent of sharing decision-making power with learners is to engage them in reflection, develop critical thinking skills, and improve learner preparation for practice (Dyson, 2018; Killam, et al., 2023). Other benefits of active learner participation in designing courses may include improved relationships, enhanced learner confidence, more depth in learning, improved academic performance, and development of collaboration skills (Bovill, 2020a, 2020b; Bovill & Bulley, 2011; Doyle et al., 2020; Ha & Pepin, 2017). Co-creation also promotes a shift from hierarchical teacher-student dynamics toward relational pedagogies rooted in trust, respect, reciprocity, and shared responsibility (Cook-Sather, 2014; Cook-Sather et al., 2019). These benefits are particularly important for graduates of health professional programs who need to be equipped to be both clinically competent and function effectively within teams. Further, co-creation has been described as mutually beneficial for both learners and educators as new insights into pedagogy are gained (Cook-Sather, 2014; Dyson, 2018; Killam, Akerlind, et al., 2024). Health professional learners face emotional challenges and a recent scoping review identified co-creation as a reported strategy to positively influence learner well-being in multiple ways (Suliman et al., 2025). However, learner-educator co-creation may be pragmatically challenging within a course due to the time-consuming nature of participative pedagogy and uncertainty about how to involve learners in curriculum decisions (Bovill & Bulley, 2011). Concerns have also been raised about the scalability and sustainability of leveraging the potential of partnerships with learners (Barradell & Bell, 2021). There remains a need to balance the pressures of industrialized education with the benefits of discursive teaching strategies in health professional education in a way that is feasible.

There are also concerns about equitable access to these partnership opportunities. Unfortunately, opportunities for learner educator co-creation during formal education are often limited to small groups of volunteers (Bovill, 2020b; Suliman et al., 2025). For example, learners may work outside of a course context to co-create curriculum or work on research projects. When co-creation opportunities occur outside of coursework, it does not reach all learners in a program, and often, already socially privileged learners are the ones who benefit (Bovill, 2020b; Mercer-Mapstone & Bovill, 2020). Typically, these partnership opportunities are offered to learners who are already highly engaged in their education and can afford to participate (Mercer-Mapstone & Bovill, 2020). Some learners may not be able to access extracurricular activities (e.g., learners who need to work in order to pay for their education and may therefore not have the time to volunteer). Purposeful action is therefore needed to ensure that all learners enrolled in a program have the opportunity to co-create with educators (Mercer-Mapstone & Bovill, 2020).

One way to achieve the goal of reaching all learners in a course is to embed opportunities for co-creation into assessment practices. Integrating co-creation into assessments provides a structured and equitable opportunity for all learners to participate regardless of their ability to engage in extracurricular or voluntary initiatives. Embedding co-creation in assessment also aligns with calls for more authentic, participatory approaches to assessment that build learners’ evaluative judgment and readiness for collaborative professional practice (Killam, Camargo-Plazas et al., 2024). This strategy also enhances the preparedness of health professional graduates for real-world practice by developing collaborative decision-making skills through simulating it in the formal curriculum. Although co-creation in any aspect of education may also be beneficial, we focused this review on assessment co-creation because of the complex, high-stakes, and regulated nature of assessment in health professional education. In addition, health professional courses may enroll large numbers of students and meaningful co-creation is reportedly difficult with larger groups (Suliman et al., 2025). Despite discourse around the benefits of co-creation, little is known about how co-creation has been used in the context of assessment within health professional education. Therefore, the focus of this review was learner-educator co-creation of assessment during courses in health professional education.

Learner-Educator Co-Creation of Assessment

Conceptual clarity about what co-creation involves is complicated by the many terms used to describe it, ambiguity in how it is defined, and overlapping and inconsistently defined terminology in existing literature (Martens et al., 2019). Bovill and Bulley’s (2011) ladder of participation was used in this review to differentiate co-creation from less active forms of learner participation in assessment design. In this review, we included Bovill and Bulley’s (2011) top four levels of learner participation where learner influence was limited to specific areas of a course that were pre-defined by the educator (level 5) or selected by learners (level 6) to where learners partnered with educators in negotiating curriculum (level 7) or were in control over decision-making (level 8) (Bovill & Bulley, 2011). We excluded the lower levels of learner participation on this ladder where educators remained in control over planned curriculum (level 1), portrayed a false sense that learners can influence decisions (level 2), or limited choices to a pre-defined set of prescribed options (levels 3 and 4). Martens et al. (2019) also conceptualized co-creation as falling on the upper end of this ladder since learners have more influence on decision-making during co-creation than in other forms of participation. Suliman et al. (2025) refer to involvement in design without interaction with educators as ‘pseudo-partnerships,’ further highlighting the need for conceptual precision in this review. During co-creation, educators do more than listen to student feedback; they focus on empowerment and partnership with learners by including them in decision-making at a level that is appropriate for the course context and learner readiness (Bovill & Bulley, 2011; Killam, Lock, et al., 2023). The key differentiating feature of papers that were included was the active involvement of learners in influencing or substantially shaping decisions about learner assessment, since it is a critical aspect of health professional education, influencing learner progression, licensure, and career opportunities that is uniquely high-stakes and highly influenced by power dynamics.

In health professional education co-creation of assessment has been used in course contexts, as illustrated by examples such as: (a) creating simulations with undergraduate nursing and occupational therapy master’s learners (Killam & Luctkar-Flude, 2021; Killam, McAuliffe, et al., 2022; MacKenzie et al., 2018), (b) determining limited undergraduate nursing exam design characteristics (Killam, Luctkar-Flude, et al., 2022), (c) teaching qualitative research at the graduate level (Abboud et al., 2017), and (d) determining postgraduate nursing learner oral viva grades (Henderson et al., 2022). Despite the use of co-creation of assessment in health professional education, ambiguity exists in how educators engage learners in co-creation within the physical or virtual classroom. A beginning understanding of principles to guide equity-centered learner-educator co-creation have been proposed, but the authors indicate that they require refinement based on existing evidence, disciplinary context, and awareness of pragmatic limitations (Killam, Lock, et al., 2023). These principles were derived based on reflections on practice and literature and include: prioritizing equity, reflecting on values, negotiating power-sharing, engaging in active and honest dialogue, integrating choice and flexibility, building respectful relationships, and promoting a psychologically safer environment (Killam, Lock, et al., 2023). Reflecting on examples of how co-creation of assessment has occurred, what decisions were made together, and how those decisions were made is an important step for educators considering co-creation of assessment in their own contexts.

Co-creating assessment activities during a course can be intentionally designed to mitigate potential unintended harm arising from power dynamics (Killam, Camargo-Plazas et al., 2024; Killam, et al., 2023). The relationship between learners and educators during a course is influenced by the power educators hold over grades. This hierarchical structure leads to an imbalance, where learners may feel personally at risk when redefining traditional roles (Könings et al., 2021). In contrast, co-creation in informal or clinical settings may evolve more organically and feel less constrained by grading-related power dynamics. Unfortunately, informal or clinical opportunities may reach only a few students. When embedded in a course, co-creation can reach all learners, but learners may experience vulnerability during learner-educator co-creation of assessment during a course since a productive experience involving honest feedback could mean exposing their limitations or possibly offending their educator (Johnson et al., 2020), which may cause learners to avoid critical dialogue (Könings et al., 2021). Learners may therefore want to conceal these thoughts to protect their grades, which typically reflect learner progression through programs and thereby influence scholarships. Without careful planning, decision-making processes could also inadvertently lead to feelings of exclusion and disadvantage for already marginalized students (Killam, et al., 2023). Educators may also face challenges in implementing co-creation, particularly when assessment is involved. These challenges could include discomfort with power-sharing, fear of unpredictable outcomes, and skepticism about the value of learner input (Könings et al., 2021). These tensions may be heightened in the context of assessment, where educators are responsible for grading accuracy and fairness. Other potentially negative consequences of learner-educator co-creation of assessment need to be identified to help educators decide if co-creation is an appropriate tool for their context.

The Need for This Review

More evidence about learner-educator co-creation of assessments in course contexts is needed to inform educational practices. Learner-educator co-creation in health care contexts may serve to support quality education through enhancing how education meets learner needs, which may support United Nations (2015) Sustainable Development Goal 4 (quality education; United Nations, 2015). Learner-educator co-creation may also be a strategy to help develop learner teamwork, complex problem-solving, and advocacy skills (Blau & Shamir-Inbal, 2017; Dyson, 2018), which are necessary to develop during health professional education to promote a workforce capable of addressing global health challenges, such as advocating for United Nations (2015) Sustainable Development Goal 3 (good health and well-being).

In this review we have chosen to focus on co-creation related to learner assessment because in higher education literature (1) co-creation outside of a course has been criticized for failing to be accessible to all learners in a program (Bovill, 2020b; Mercer-Mapstone & Bovill, 2020), (2) co-creation within a course that is not part of assessment may also fail to reach all learners in the course, (3) the stakes for learners are higher during assessment, (4) co-creation of assessment may be complicated by potential learner vulnerability, and (5) the feasibility of co-creation related to assessments remains unknown.

A preliminary search of MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Epistemonikos, and JBI Evidence Synthesis was conducted in July 2023. We were unable to identify any published or ongoing scoping reviews about co-creation of assessment in the context of health professional education. A discussion on existing reviews that cover some aspects of this topic can be found in our protocol (Killam, Egan, et al., 2024). Most of the findings in these reviews focused on co-creation outside of a course context and findings specific to co-creation of assessments were not captured. At that time there were no known systematic reviews or scoping reviews that described strategies for learner-educator co-creation of assessment in health professional education. Owing to the broad nature of our research questions, a scoping review was needed to understand how learner-educator co-creation of assessment is implemented, investigated, and reported in the literature.

Objectives

The objectives of this scoping review were to answer the following question: What has been reported in the literature about learner-educator co-creation of assessment in health professional education? Methods

Inclusion Criteria

We included peer-reviewed and other publications produced globally that outlined how learner-educator co-creation of assessment has been used in health professional education.

Participants (Role and Discipline)

Perspectives of both learners and course educators were included in this review. Reports could detail the perspectives of one or both participant types. Partners in learner-educator co-creation needed to include both learner(s) and educator(s). Co-creation with others was excluded unless the learner and course educator were both also part of the co-creation process.

To be included, participants needed to be from health professional disciplines, meaning that they were from any program that prepares them for practice. Reports with mixed disciplines in the same course were also included. Participants who were co-creating in hospital or other service provider settings were excluded. In addition, coursework that was part of obtaining a degree in pure sciences (i.e., biology) was excluded. We excluded veterinarian professional education since their practice is with animals.

Concept (Co-Creation of Assessment)

Learner-educator co-creation of any aspect of student assessment during a course was the concept of interest for this scoping review. To be classified as co-creation, the report needed to describe an effort to share power with learners during any assessment-related decision-making process in an interactive way. Learners must also have explicit control or substantial influence over assessment decisions to be classified as at or above level 5 on Bovill and Bulley’s (2011) ladder of participation. Where the level on the ladder was unclear, we included the study if there was an indication that it was most likely level 5 or above based on article wording. The kinds of decisions that learners and educators collaborated on could be limited to prescribed areas of the assessment, as long as the choices that learners had were not predetermined. Learner-educator co-creation during a course could range from influencing one decision about course assessments to making many decisions together. Any situation where an educator invited current learners to substantially influence assessment decisions through collaboration with them, which is traditionally only made by an educator, was included.

To be included, a major focus of the report must be on learner-educator co-creation of assessment. To be considered a major focus of the report, co-creation must be evident in the abstract. We did not consider reports for full-text review if they did not refer to co-creation in the abstract. During the search, a broad definition of co-creation as interactive shared decision making was used. The term “co-creation” is often misused (Bovill, 2020b), which means that a definition or description is essential to ensure that co-creation is interactive and not simply an act of seeking learner input on a decision. Any article that used the terms co-creation, co-design, co-production, co-construction, consensus grading, or other related terms alongside a definition or description of the process was included.

To be included, learner-educator co-creation must relate to any aspect of learner assessment during the course. Evaluation is another term that was included when it referred to making a value judgment about learner work that led to a grade. There are many kinds of assessment, which may be labeled as objective (sometimes called traditional), performance-based, and/or authentic assessment. All types of assessment may involve co-creation and were therefore included in this review. Articles were included if they discussed co-creation of assessment and answered RQ1 and RQ2 in a way that was separate from content in the article related to other interventions or forms of co-creation. Specifically, in some cases, findings related to co-creation of assessment were embedded within broader discussions of co-creation or other student empowerment initiatives. We only included articles where the content related to co-creation of assessment could be clearly identified and meaningfully extracted to address our review questions.

Publications that say they engaged in collaborative decision making but did not describe or define how decisions were made were excluded. Publications about co-construction of knowledge or content curation that did not address course assessment decisions were excluded. Self-assessment in the absence of written or verbal dialogue with an educator was also excluded. Articles about providing feedback, mentorship, or guidance were also excluded unless co-creation was used in these processes.

Context (During a Course)

The context of the review was academic course assessment. Learner-educator co-creation must have occurred during the time learners were registered in a mandatory or elective course of any size. Decisions they co-created must have impacted the course that students were registered in.

Reports that described co-creation at a curriculum level or where learners volunteered for co-creation opportunities outside of a course context were excluded because their involvement impacts future learners (not themselves). Publications about co-design of educational materials (such as mobile applications or simulations) were excluded unless it occurred during a course for use in that same course. Co-creation in a placement or clinical context was also excluded since collaboration is understood as a natural part of these placements and is not the focus of this review. We excluded hospital-based training programs for similar reasons. In addition, learners working as co-researchers outside of a course context were excluded. Publications that referred to co-creation in a variety of contexts were only included if the use of co-creation in an academic course context was clearly distinguished from clinical and other contexts. If the source provided general guidance for learner-educator co-creation but did not refer to it in the context of a specific course, it was excluded.

Types of Sources

We included peer-reviewed reports published globally, as well as dissertations and editorials, discussion papers or commentaries published in peer-reviewed journals, as well as publicly available conference proceedings, white papers and reports on websites of reputable organizations.

We excluded conference abstracts, posters, study protocols, social media posts, and opinions published outside of peer-reviewed journals. Study protocols, poster presentations, and conference abstracts were excluded because they did not provide adequate data to answer the research questions. We excluded other types of evidence due to feasibility. Without these sources, we already needed to screen a high number of indexed sources due to the broad range of terms we are using to identify co-creation. A previous review about learners as partners in health professional education, which was limited to qualitative research, noted that they needed to limit their search due to the impractical nature of screening high volumes of literature, which they stated may detract from the meaningfulness of a review’s findings (Barradell & Bell, 2021). In addition, during preliminary searches of Google and known blog posts, the data from these sources was limited in the quality of data that would answer the research questions. Searching for gray literature may also present challenges related to search methods, replicability, and resource intensity (Adams et al., 2016). To promote transparency and feasibility, we focused our search strategy on sources that were most relevant, accessible, and feasible to identify.

Methodology

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with standard procedures for conducting and reporting scoping reviews using the JBI methodology (Peters et al., 2020, 2021; Pollock et al., 2023), and our protocol was published in March 2024 (Killam, Egan, et al., 2024). JBI methodology was chosen since it is a rigorous and well-recognized approach for scoping reviews that was developed to address broad questions and synthesize the state of knowledge in topics relevant to health professions. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses extension for scoping review (PRISMA-ScR) checklist (Tricco et al., 2018) and relevant sections of the Methodological expectations of Campbell Collaboration intervention reviews (The Methods Coordinating Group of the Campbell Collaboration, 2017) were used to guide the reporting of the review.

Search Strategy

The search strategy was designed by an information specialist (ARW) and the lead author (LK) to locate published information. A three-step search strategy was utilized in this review: (1) the initial search to identify keywords, (2) a full search of all databases, and (3) a review of relevant reference lists and publications by conference abstract authors.

First, an initial limited search of JBI, Cochrane, MEDLINE (Ovid), Scopus and Education Source (EBSCOhost) was undertaken to identify articles on the topic. Then, the words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, and the index terms used to describe the articles, were used to develop a full search strategy for MEDLINE and Education Source. This search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, was adapted for each included database (see Appendix 1). The databases searched included MEDLINE (via OVID, 1946–present), CINAHL (via EBSCOhost, 1981–present), Embase (via Ovid, 1947–present), Education Source (via EBSCOhost, 1929–present), ERIC (via EBSCOhost, 1966–present), and APA PsycInfo (via OVID, 1806–present). Each database was searched from the date of inception. Databases were last searched on August 1, 2024.

The reference list of all included sources, as well as existing reviews and relevant reports related to co-creation of assessment identified during full text screening, was screened for additional papers. We also hand-searched the authors of conference abstracts excluded during full-text review by identifying a list of their publications online, reviewing titles, and importing on-topic records into Covidence. We contacted Dr. Bovill, Dr. Napan, and Dr. Thompson for assistance locating some of their work. We also hand searched The Journal of Educational Innovation, Partnership, and Change in July 2024, which is a non-indexed journal focused on learner-educator partnerships. We used the journal-specific search feature to search Teaching and Learning Together in Higher Education using a modified search strategy on July 12, 2025, and imported 20 results into Covidence for screening. We also conducted forward searching of included studies in July 2025.

Papers published in any language were included if they were identified during the English-language search. No limitation on the date of publication was used. Gray literature searches were restricted to dissertations indexed by the respective databases and websites of referenced organizations.

Evidence Selection

Following the search, all identified citations were exported and uploaded into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation). Duplicates were removed using Covidence and verified by the lead author. Following a pilot test, titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers (LK, ML, RG, or FC) in Covidence for assessment against the inclusion criteria for the review. Reviewers were guided by an inclusion criteria summary document (Appendix 2). LK screened all papers except the ones she co-authored.

Full-text review was also assessed within Covidence by two independent reviewers (LK, ML, RG, or FC). Again, LK screened all papers except ones she co-authored. Reviewers were guided by a hierarchy of reasons for exclusion (See Appendix 3). For example, all articles were assessed for the concept of co-creation prior to being assessed for the participants in co-creation. EndNote 20 (Clarivate Analytics) was used by individual reviewers to make notes on their articles.

Any disagreements that arose between reviewers at each stage of the selection process were resolved through discussion. Additional reviewer(s) were involved in these discussions as needed (CG and/or MLF). Authors of reports were contacted to request missing or additional data, where necessary and possible.

Quality Appraisal

We had planned to conduct a quality appraisal of included studies. However, quality appraisal was not completed as it is not a requirement in a scoping review and should only be completed when there is a rationale for doing so (Levac et al., 2010; Peters et al., 2021; Pollock et al., 2021). In a scoping review, data pertaining to the impact of interventions is not synthesized, and claims regarding the effectiveness of interventions are not made. Therefore, quality appraisal or discussion of the risk of bias is generally not recommended (Peters et al., 2021; Tricco et al., 2018).

Data Extraction

Data were extracted from reports included in the scoping review by two or more independent reviewers using a Covidence data extraction tool developed by the reviewers. The lead author extracted data from all included sources except the ones she co-authored. Consensus was checked by the lead author and verified by a second reviewer as needed. Disagreements between reviewers and items that were difficult to decipher in articles were resolved through discussion or with an additional reviewer.

The data extracted included specific details about the participants, concept, context, study methods and key findings relevant to the review questions. As per the protocol, the data extraction tool was piloted by the lead author and modified during the review to include additional items necessary for the characteristics of the included reports table, as well as a free-text question for items to discuss that were unclear to the extractor.

Data Analysis

During data extraction, data were summarized in Covidence through simple deductive data categorization and basic inductive content analysis. Deductive data categorization involved selecting one or more multiple-choice options from pre-determined lists during data extraction. Comments or report quotations were added to support these selections. Selection lists were developed by the research team as part of the protocol (Killam, Egan, et al., 2024) and expanded to include more options and questions based on preliminary data extraction. For example, we added a selected response question for which assessment decisions were co-created to enable us to create an accurate bar chart. The options for this question were inductively created during preliminary data extraction then used during a second round of extraction to ensure data were complete.

Basic inductive content analysis was done to summarize qualitative data pertaining to interventions presented in each report, reasons for co-creation (part of RQ1), which assessment decisions were being made together (RQ2), and overall author conclusions (RQ5) (Peters et al., 2021; Pollock et al., 2023). Quotations were extracted alongside codes used to summarize data extraction by two independent reviewers. Both summaries were reviewed again during consensus, collapsed, and re-checked by data extractors for completeness and accuracy. Differing interpretations were resolved through discussion.

Reinterpretation of evidence was avoided to the extent possible (Pollock et al., 2023). However, inferences were sometimes made about whether courses were mandatory (part of RQ1), which assessment decisions were being made (RQ2), whether all learners were required to participate, and what strategies were used to promote feasibility (part of RQ4). In several papers, it was challenging to identify the answer to RQ3. When a paper included a definition of co-creation that referred to interactive decision-making, but no process description, it met our inclusion criteria. Where possible, we used the wording of the article to interpret how much influence learners had on decision-making. Where the level of influence was unclear, we categorized it as Level 5 on Bovill and Bulley’s (2011) ladder of participation when creating charts.

Consensus data were exported from Covidence and managed in Microsoft Excel, to create charts and assist with counting categorized data. Narrative data were copied and edited in Microsoft Word to create summary tables to answer each research question. Then, LK collapsed data through coding similar narrative descriptions from tables and grouping data into categories, which were checked by a second reviewer. Adobe Illustrator was used to refine charts and create figures.

Data Presentation

Data are displayed in tabular form, charts, and accompanied by a narrative summary to answer each research question.

Excluded Reports

We sought 344 reports, but three could not be located. We retrieved and assessed 341 full-text reports for eligibility and contacted authors for clarification as needed. Non-responses from 7 authors resulted in team discussions to make the best possible decision about article inclusion based on available data, 1 of which was included.

Included Reports

We included 30 papers published between 1980 and 2025, which described 27 distinct reports including three pairs of companion papers. Companion papers were treated as the same report since they describe the same course and approach to assessment co-creation in similar ways where: (1) negotiation was embedded in a task-based syllabus (Martyn, 2000; Martyn & Husain, 1993), (2) Academic Co-Creative Inquiry was used for course co-creation within limitations (Napan, 2009; Napan et al., 2020), and (3) student and tutor consensus was used during grading (Thompson, Houston, & Dansie, 2017; 2017b). There were also two reports that described multiple courses.

Description of Reports

Included reports originated from 8 countries. Close to half (n = 13 of 27 reports) originated from North America; 6 were from Canada and 7 were from the United States. A total of 10 reports originated from Oceania, with 5 from New Zealand and 5 from Australia. The remaining 4 reports originated from Germany in Europe, Pakistan and China in Asia and South Africa.

In 12 other reports, co-creation of assessment during courses was discussed as a strategy to support course co-creation (n = 3), simulation development (n = 3), and a variety of other initiatives. Co-creation of assessment was a major focus in 21 reports, whereas 6 reports focused on other interventions but answered RQ1 and RQ2 and were therefore included.

Design

In health professional education, approximately half of the existing evidence pertaining to co-creation of student assessment is from non-research sources (n = 13 of 27 reports), such as innovation papers or educator reflections. All of these non-research reports included educator perspectives of co-creation, and four included both learner and educator perspectives. Waddell and Stephens (2000) and Limin (2005) were considered quality improvement. These authors summarized data collected from students about learning contract use in nursing courses but did not conduct formal research.

Of the 12 included research reports, 5 were quantitative studies, 5 were qualitative studies, and two used mixed methods. All research reports included student participants; three studies integrated the perspective of one, two or seven educators. All 5 quantitative researchers collected data using questionnaires; Fox and West (1983) also collected educator observations. The number of participants in quantitative studies varied from 46 graduate social worker students to 92 medical students. Qualitative researchers collected data using class recordings, course assignments, educator field notes, focus groups, interviews, open-ended surveys, participant observation, and self-reflection. The number of participants in qualitative studies ranged from 13 postgraduate students to author reflections and observations of 69 undergraduate students during a course. In the two mixed methods studies, interviews, student scores, and questionnaires were used to collect data from 46 nursing students in a course.

Where Co-creation of Assessment Is Occurring (Question 1)

Table 2 summarizes 25 courses in which co-creation of assessment was reported including the disciplines involved, reasons for it, and assessment decisions made together. Data were extracted from 27 included reports, which included three pairs of companion papers that described the same course and use of assessment co-creation. In addition, 2 reports described multiple courses. In one case, the report referenced multiple courses but did not provide sufficient detail to distinguish them clearly, so it was counted as a single course in this review (Martenson & Schwab, 1993). Two reports described separate assignments in the same course and were therefore counted as one course (with parts a and b in the table).

Co-creation of assessment was reported across courses representing diverse disciplines and academic levels. Courses included the following disciplines: Nursing (n = 11), Social Work (n = 3), Paramedicine (n = 3), Medicine (n = 2), Health Services Navigation (n = 1), Pharmacy (n = 1), Occupational Therapy (n = 1), Dental Technology (n = 1), and multiple disciplines in the same course (n = 3). Co-creation of assessment was reported in courses at undergraduate (n = 16 of 25 courses), graduate (n = 6), and postgraduate (n = 2) levels, with one course at an unspecified level. Most courses were mandatory (n = 11) or likely mandatory (n = 10). Some (n = 4) courses were electives. Courses spanned various years of study, from first-year (n = 6), second-year (n = 2), third-year (n = 4), fourth-year (n = 1), or senior or exit level (n = 3), and multiple or unspecified levels (n = 9).

Educators engaged students in co-creation for a variety of reasons. They wanted to promote learning, personalize courses around learners’ unique needs and interests, make assignments more meaningful, balance flexibility with structure required by accreditation, foster mutual planning, and clarify objectives. Co-creation of assessment was viewed as a tool to enhance how educator feedback is received and implemented in difficult teaching contexts. Co-creation of assessment was also used to empower students, improve equity, support self-direction, self-regulation, and shift learner focus from grades to authentic learning. Through co-creation educators hoped to give students more autonomy and promote independence. It was used to prepare students for real-world practice and collaboration by promoting transferable skills such as critical thinking, reflection, evaluative judgment, and self-assessment. Additional goals included producing graduates capable of leading change in healthcare systems, addressing inequities through anti-oppression and accessibility practices, mentoring students in writing and communication, and managing competitive cultures or dissatisfaction with traditional grading. Partnering with students in writing also aimed to simultaneously help educators be productive in an ethical way. In addition, co-creation of assessment was used to address challenges in prior course delivery, enhance engagement, commitment, promote lifelong learning, and build student confidence.

Commonly co-created assignments involved learning contracts (n = 11), writing (n = 8), and aspects of simulation (n = 8), which were reported across all included disciplinary categories as well as at both undergraduate and graduate or postgraduate levels of education (Figure 3). More specifically, written assignments were used to develop skills in research, scholarly writing, and reflection. Learning contracts were used to plan a variety of individualized projects or to plan course objectives and content with an entire class (not individual students). Aspects of simulation creation, implementation, and/or performance evaluation were co-created to develop transferable skills. In addition, co-created assignments included presentations, module debriefing and reflection, self/peer/educator evaluation processes, and other student-driven assignment types.

What Assessment Decisions Are Being Made Together (Question 2)

The final column in Table 2 and Figure 4 summarizes the different types of assessment planning (n = 20), completion (n = 6), and student evaluation (n = 12) decisions that were co-created by course. Only 4 courses engaged students in all three phases of assessment (although not always in the same assignment) and all 6 instances of co-created assignment completion also engaged learners in assignment planning or student evaluation.

Across disciplines, co-creation of assessment was reported in all phases. Planning emerged as the most common co-created aspect of assessment, followed by evaluation and then completion. In nursing, 10 of 11 courses included the planning stage with about half (n = 5 of 11) including evaluation and 3 involving co-completion. All 6 courses in social and human services included students in planning, with 2 including evaluation and 1 including completion. In medicine and other allied health (n = 8 courses), students were most commonly included in evaluation (n = 5), followed by planning (n = 4), and completion (n = 2).

When examined by course level, co-creation of assessment was also reported in all phases of assessment. Undergraduate students were most often involved in planning assessments (n = 12 of 16) and less often involved in co-completion (n = 4 of 16) or co-evaluation (n = 7 of 16) of assessments. All 6 reports of co-creation of assessment with graduate students included assessment planning, with one report including all three phases of assessment and three including evaluation. Both reports with postgraduate students included co-evaluation with one course including students in all three phases of assessment. The course at an unspecified level included co-creation during the planning phase.

Course outcomes were co-created in only one course where co-creation occurred with the entire class and not individual students (Martenson & Schwab, 1993). In the remaining courses, learners and educators engaged in co-creation of assessment during the course to plan in a variety of ways. The most common co-created aspects of planning included deciding on grading criteria or the evidence needed to show learning (n = 16), and assessment instructions or plan (n = 15). In 12 courses co-creation was used to plan the assessment format (i.e., written, presentation, or other). Students were also included in co-creating assessment learning outcomes (n = 7), the focus of the assignment to meet pre-determined assessment learning outcomes (n = 10), due dates (n = 8), weighting of assignments (i.e., how much each is worth) (n = 6), and more.

During assignment implementation, co-creation involved completing the assignment together in 5 courses. And in another course learners worked with educators to address formative feedback derived from peers, the writing center, and educators (Esambe, 2018), which we counted as co-completion.

Co-created learner evaluation included generation of performance feedback in 11 courses and deciding on final grades together in 6 courses.

How Much Influence Learners Have (Question 3)

The 25 courses were classified according to Bovill and Bulley’s (2011) ladder of participation at one or more levels (see Table 3). Multiple levels of participation were evident in 3 courses based on different student understandings of co-creation (Killam, Akerlind, et al., 2024) or where different assignments were described in separate reports (Killam & Luctkar-Flude, 2021; Killam et al. (in press)) and are counted at the highest level in this review. The lowest level of participation, level 5, was the most common (n = 11) where learners had control over prescribed areas of assessment. In addition, 4 courses could not be placed at a specific level but based on article wording they were likely at or above level 5 and are counted at level 5 in this review. There were 4 courses that contained level 6 participation where students had the option to chose what areas to change (i.e., they designed a project to achieve prescribed learning outcomes). Lastly, 6 courses contained level 7 participation where there was a partnership, and students worked with educators to negotiate assessment.

Figure 6 shows the level of influence according to Bovill and Bulley’s (2011) ladder of participation by course discipline and course level. In nursing, co-creation was either at level 5 or 7, with reports of undergraduate, graduate, and postgraduate courses at level 5. Almost all instances of Level 7 co-creation reported in this review occurred in undergraduate nursing courses (n = 5). The one postgraduate navigation course with Level 7 co-creation occurred in collaboration with a nursing course. In social work, one graduate course was at level 5 and another 4 courses were at level 6. All courses in Medicine and other allied health contained level 5 participation.

The level of influence based on the phase of assessment is shown in Figure 7. While planning is the most frequently reported phase across all levels of participation, when co-creation reached level 7 a higher proportion of reports involved assignment completion or student evaluation.

How Learners Influence Decisions (Question 4)

A summary of how co-creation was implemented in health professional courses is presented in Table 3.

Assessment co-creation design was guided by various frameworks across the 25 unique courses, which are grouped into one of three overlapping categories based on the main emphasis in reports: fostering self-direction and autonomy, educational design to promote learning, and/or real-world application. Together, all categories of frameworks guiding co-creation highlight a common aim of equipping learners with the skills, mindset, and confidence needed for lifelong learning and competent professional practice.

Learner-centered frameworks to foster self-direction and autonomy were evident in 7 courses. For example, leveraging adult learning principles, including Knowles guidance for learning contracts, meant aligning assessment with learner goals, promoting autonomy, and enabling independent learning. Educators in these courses empowered students to set goals, plan, and take responsibility for their own learning.

Educational design frameworks to promote learning were the most common category, observed in 12 courses. These courses used frameworks such as learning psychology, inquiry-based learning, constructivism, or instructional design to scaffold learning, encourage reflection, inform dialogue, and promote metacognition. According to learning psychology, effective learning requires new knowledge to be connected to prior understanding, individualized pacing, learner autonomy, and a supportive environment for interaction and feedback. Through inquiry-based learning the educator’s role shifts from a knowledge provider to a facilitator. Constructivist and progressive education philosophies also emphasize learner-centeredness, critical thinking, and interaction between learners and educators in different and sometimes emancipatory ways. Instructional design theories were also used to emphasize the need to be deliberate, inclusive, and reflective when structuring courses.

Real-world application was a closely related category, emphasizing preparation for professional practice or authentic tasks as part of educational design. This category was evident in 6 courses focused on preparing learners for future professional challenges and bridging classroom learning with real-world expectations. For example, assessment-focused frameworks included assessment as learning, authentic assessment, or sustainable assessment, which share a common goal of developing learner evaluative judgement. Evaluative judgement refers to the capacity to self-assess and assess the performance of others. Assessment as learning emphasizes scaffolded skill-building. Authentic assessment also involves scaffolding with an emphasis on aligning assessment with real-world tasks and contexts. Likewise, sustainable assessment emphasizes the need to equip learners with the skills to face future challenges. All 3 of these assessment frameworks aim to build real-world transferrable skills that are essential for lifelong learning and professional practice.

Learner participation in co-creation was either required or optional (See Figure 8). All learners were required to participate in co-creation in 14 courses, most of which were at level 5 on Bovill and Bulley’s (2011) ladder of participation. In 4 courses, all learners had a choice if they wanted to participate in co-creation or not. In 2 courses, all learners must participate in some aspects of co-creation, but others were optional. In one course, learners chose to participate at the onset of the course and if all of the students agreed, then all of them participated (Napan, 2009). In 4 courses, it was unclear if participation was mandated or optional.

Interaction methods are summarized in Figure 9. At all levels, both synchronous and asynchronous interaction methods were used, and, in most courses (n = 13 of 18), multiple interaction strategies were used (ranging from 2 to 7 strategies). Discussion was used as a strategy for interaction with students across all reports where interaction methods were reported (n = 18 of 18 courses). Across reports, verbal discussions occurred with an entire class, again, after educator refinement of plans based on class discussions, during individual meetings, during class time with smaller groups of students, and/or in groups during class time. Discussion also occurred through anonymous online discussion boards during classes and asynchronous online group discussion boards. Other strategies used to co-create assessment included a range of approaches, such as reaching consensus. Of note, the way in which this agreement took place varied across the cited literature and uses other strategies such as discussion, forms, or email to reach an agreement. In addition, educators used open-ended surveys to gather input, email communication, document submission, or editing live documents together synchronously or asynchronously. Additional methods involved providing written constructive feedback before grade requests, exchanging feedback on draft work, and holding meetings by request rather than with each student. Other techniques included voting during the nominal group process, distributing an invitation letter, offering an anonymous comment box, and facilitating student-led performance critiques with a guide (i.e., rubric).

Reported Impacts and Lessons (Question 5)

Table 4 and Figure 10 summarizes reported facilitators, barriers, benefits, disadvantages, and future considerations for learner-educator co-creation of assessment.Figure 10.Reported Impacts and Lessons Learned

Reported Impacts and Lessons Learned

Facilitators

Facilitators that made it more possible for co-creation of assessment to occur were reported in 19 of 27 reports. These facilitators included student factors, educator skills and preparation, process design and clarity, institutional alignment, and collaborative relationships.

Student factors identified in 7 reports included engagement through multiple strategies, openness to self-challenge, willingness to receive and process critical feedback, and ability to self-assess, speak up, and collaborate. Additional influences were perceived need and acceptance, maturity, motivation, prior skill building, shared commitment, interest, and development of shared values. Educator skills and preparation, extracted from 8 reports, included strong planning, organization, design, and facilitation skills, psychological readiness and responsiveness to learner needs, respect for diverse expertise, commitment to shared goals, flexibility and ongoing reflection.

The most common facilitator identified across 16 reports was clear and flexible process design. It was critical to establish transparent expectations, structured yet adaptable guidance, and intuitive processes to support student engagement. Helpful strategies included planning for multiple engagement methods, leveraging technology such as anonymous online discussion boards and collaborative online document editing, providing clear templates and planning tools to reduce confusion, offering detailed guidance, integrating diverse assessment methods, ongoing communication, in-class activities, monitoring for confusion, and negotiating flexible syllabi that balanced structure with student choice. These approaches supported clarity, encouraged student engagement, and allowed processes to adapt to varying learner experience levels. Institutional alignment, identified in 4 reports, involved ensuring fit with curriculum and learner needs, scaffolding within the program, and securing organizational support while remaining aware of contextual limitations.

Relationship-building and collaboration were facilitators described across 9 reports. Creating a humanistic, collaborative environment that recognized student uniqueness, built trust and mutual appreciation, encouraged openness to working together, and fostered supportive relationships with continuous guidance and feedback supported co-creation efforts.

Barriers

Barriers specific to co-creation of assessment were described in 19 of 27 reports and were framed as factors that limited or prevented co-creation from occurring. These barriers may be context-specific and related to emotional factors, cognitive challenges, workload and logistical issues, program limitations, and difficulties with participation.

Emotional factors were identified in 8 reports and included student anxiety, fear, confusion, discouragement from revisions, apathy, or grade-focused attitudes. Likewise, cognitive challenges described in 7 reports included unfamiliarity with processes, terminology confusion, differing perceptions of content value, content difficulty, and limited readiness or preparedness among students and educators. These emotional and cognitive challenges were often reduced or resolved over time.

Workload and logistical issues hindering co-creation of assessment were described in 12 reports and related to high educator demands, limited time, systemic pressures, inadequate support/resources, scheduling, and scalability challenges. It was also described as a misfit with memorization-heavy courses. Similarly, program limitations were identified in 4 reports due to institutional rules, fixed outcomes, and accreditation requirements that restrict flexibility. In addition, difficulties with participation stemmed from power imbalances, educator discomfort with sharing control, educator difficulty critiquing students who were trying their best, student reluctance, hierarchical norms, and concerns around mitigating cheating in 5 reports.

Disadvantages

Disadvantages specific to co-creation of assessment were closely related to barriers experienced, but positioned as negative outcomes of co-creation, and reported in 14 of 27 reports. Disadvantages related to emotional challenges, workload and logistical barriers, as well as participation challenges.

Emotional challenges (n = 8) such as initial confusion, disbelief, uncertainty, hesitancy, or anxiety were predominantly described as temporary disadvantages, which may occur initially and/or when expectations change. Confusion was also attributed to terminology as well as the uniqueness of co-creation of assessment. Another concern was the potential for learners to become overwhelmed, particularly if too much change occurred. The root cause was sometimes identified as fear over the inability to earn grades by chance. Authors identified that these concerns may be resolved during the co-creation process or limited to a few students.

Processes were also reported as time intensive, leading to workload and logistical concerns in 6 reports. Co-creation of assessment placed high demands on educator skills, organization, expertise, and preparation. Scheduling may be difficult when meetings are required and large class size may delay feedback. Concern was also raised around potential for unclear or unrealistic plans and objectively grading diverse assignments. When poorly planned, co-creation was reported to become burdensome, and learners contributed minimally. Careful planning was therefore identified as essential to balance workload with making the process meaningful.

Participation challenges, though only surfaced in 4 reports, included resistance, reliance on participation, and the impact of power dynamics in the classroom. Resistance originated from students or other educators, and a perception that co-creation of assessment meant students ‘doing the educator’s job’. One author suggested that class attendance should be mandated since a student who did not attend classes struggled to understand the process. During discussions, power dynamics meant that inevitably some voices may dominate, and the voices of those who oppose dialogue may be marginalized or excluded. Mitigating these participation challenges requires careful planning.

Taken together, 21 reports identified emotional and/or cognitive challenges (n = 15), process, workload, and logistical issues (n = 14), participation obstacles (n = 8), and program limitations (n = 4) as disadvantages or barriers.

Benefits

Benefits of assessment co-creation were outlined in most reports (n = 25 of 27), highlighting that it was an overall valuable experience. Benefits included process improvements, creation of a supportive and collegial learning environment, educator benefits, learner empowerment and equity, general learning gains, specific skill development, as well as personal growth and lifelong learning.

Process improvements from working with students identified in 20 reports included flexibility, meaningful choices, personalization, and shifting power dynamics in feasible ways. Co-creation incorporated student voices, promoted autonomy, clearly communicated negotiable elements of assessment, and led to manageable customized assignments. Reflective two-way dialogue, immediate feedback, active student involvement in the process, and detailed feedback enhanced student satisfaction, clarity, fairness, understanding, and engagement while aligning assessment with learning needs, curriculum and real-world practice. The process fostered clarity, relevance, and efficiency. It also supported active collaboration between students and educators, was suitable for a wide range of learners, and enhanced the perceived credibility and authenticity of assessment.

Co-creation fostered a supportive and collegial learning environment (n = 6) characterized by trust, respect, support, authentic dialogue, and mutual understanding. Relationship-building and growing familiarity with the process reduced hierarchy, anxiety, stress, and fear while building motivation, pride, and enthusiasm for learning. Learners held themselves to high standards, which further contributed to building a positive environment. The process promoted meaningful engagement, reflection, and collaboration with educators and peers. This environment led to an enjoyable, and memorable learning experience that leveraged contributors’ strengths and supported real-world relevance.

Educator benefits included professional growth and scholarly gains (n = 8), such as professional development, compensating for limited facilities, co-authored publications, and increased research productivity. Co-creation supported quality resource and simulation development, addressed learner diversity, and empowered educators by enhancing credibility and allowing them to leverage their expertise. Educators preferred consensus grading over traditional methods.

Co-creation fostered empowerment and equity (n = 13 reports) through authentic discussions, inclusivity, shifting power to students, valuing diverse voices, recognizing learners as adults, and promoting ownership of learning. Learner-centered approaches enhanced accessibility, reduced competition and anxiety, and encouraged social responsibility and authentic engagement.

Learning was a major outcome of co-creation of assessment in 22 reports. Authors reported general learning gains in 17 reports, including authentic and practice-relevant learning, enriched course content, improved understanding, insights into performance, competence, and higher-quality academic results/performance. Learning resulted from practical and customized assignments, clarified objectives, deeper reflection, meaningful feedback, role modeling, increased motivation, deep learner engagement, and stronger links between learning outcomes and real-world application. This learning led to goal attainment, a sense of accomplishment, high approval from students, and unexpected learning.

In addition, specific skill development (n = 15 reports), included growth in cognitive skills such as reasoning, problem-solving, metacognition, creative thinking, lateral thinking, and critical thinking. Additional transferable skills developed included communication, collaboration, team-work, self-assessment, self-advocacy, and negotiation skills, as well as improved writing and co-authorship abilities. Critical conversations during co-creation fostered real-world skills, leadership abilities, and reflection. Closely related, personal growth and lifelong learning benefits emerged across 16 reports and centered around developing readiness for professional practice. Benefits of co-creation in this category included components of independent lifelong learning such as autonomy, self-responsibility, self-direction, self-management, accountability, professional identity, practical wisdom, reflective practice, and evaluative judgment. In addition, learners grew their confidence in areas such as self-advocacy and self-regulated learning. Overall, co-creation promoted ownership of learning, preparing students for real-world practice and supporting ongoing personal and professional development.

Future Considerations

Ideas to improve future co-creation of assessment efforts were identified in 21 of 27 reports. Future considerations across the reports emphasized the need to continue or adapt approaches for future use (n = 14) and broaden application to other contexts such as more courses in a program, clinical, and professional development settings (n = 6). In addition, educators can support student engagement in co-created assessment through clear guidance, flexible options, feedback, addressing anxiety, ensuring fairness, monitoring group dynamics, and using structured frameworks with scaffolded, team-based strategies to empower learners while responding to potential challenges and barriers (n = 4). Educators also need to engage in ongoing reflection and evaluation to develop guiding principles and refine processes for diverse contexts (n = 4), and address system and cultural change (n = 2).

Gaps in the Literature

Limitations and research gaps reported in the literature are summarized in Table 5 alongside each report’s aims and designs. Among the research studies, 8 reports explicitly acknowledged limitations due to small, homogeneous, convenience, or single site samples, which limits generalizability. In some cases, different instructors or researcher involvement as educator posed a risk of bias or pressure to participate. In addition, designs were limited by non-randomized, single-group designs without controls, making it difficult to infer causation. Findings relied on student perceptions, risking social desirability bias, and did not capture educator perspectives. At times, data collection was based on untested or limited instruments (e.g., no neutral option, simple open-ended questions). Some data collection methods prevented in-depth exploration, limiting the ability to draw conclusions. Findings, particularly from qualitative studies, were also context-specific, making results hard to apply broadly.

Three non-research reports noted limitations in relation to the report being based on subjective interpretations that may change, the inability to replicate any context-specific syllabus negotiation process, and the reflection-based nature of the report with no objective data collection.

Authors of 16 reports noted a need for research related to (1) perspective exploration, (2) strategy refinement, (3) outcome identification, and (4) improved methodologies.

Authors of 6 reports called for future research to build a more complete understanding of co-creation from multiple perspectives, including educator perspectives (n = 4) as well as different and diverse student experiences (n = 4). Hardigan (1994) identified that it would also be interesting to explore why students who liked using learning contracts did not want to use them in all courses.

Strategy refinement to improve how co-creation of assessment is implemented was called for in 11 reports. Researchers need to explore principles, strategies, and processes in multiple and diverse contexts to further develop pedagogy (n = 7). For example, researchers need to test ways to communicate what co-creation of assessment involves clearly to help students understand it (n = 2) and explore approaches to engage students in feedback dialogue that develop their evaluative judgment (Henderson et al., 2022). In addition, to facilitate implementation, researchers should identify ways to mitigate resistance from other educators to sharing power with students through co-creation (Killam et al. (in press)). Likewise, exploring the impact of changes in administrative structures on the process may offer interesting insights into the contexts where co-creation of assessment may flourish (Martyn & Husain, 1993).

Outcome-oriented research was identified as a need in 6 reports to identify student and instructional impacts of co-created assessment. Researchers should investigate and compare the impact of different approaches to assessment co-creation and grading methods. For example, outcomes needing further investigation included student satisfaction, learner-educator relationships, classroom performance, depth in learning, future practice, anxiety, and self-assessment skills.

Authors of 7 reports also called for improved methodologies in future research that leverages both qualitative and quantitative approaches (n = 2), includes larger studies (n = 3), and longitudinal designs. Outcome testing should involve randomized experimental studies (n = 3). When comparing courses with and without co-creation it would be ideal to have the same instructor teaching the experimental and control groups (Huff & McNown Johnson, 1998).

Discussion

Summary of Main Results

We identified 27 distinct reports published between 1980 and 2025 that describe 25 unique courses where co-creation of assessment was reported in health professional educational settings, highlighting that there is insufficient research in this area.

Included reports originated from 8 different countries, with most coming from North America; the global south was underrepresented. Despite a geographic imbalance in the literature, this finding supports the international applicability of co-creation of assessment in health professional education. While there may be a global interest in co-creation of assessment, a lack of representation across countries and languages in our findings underscores the need for more research across diverse educational and cultural contexts.

Co-creation of assessment has been reported in both mandatory and elective undergraduate, graduate, and postgraduate health professional education courses in 8 disciplines: nursing, social work, medicine, health services navigation, paramedicine, pharmacy, occupational therapy and dental technology as well as multidisciplinary courses. This reporting across diverse course types and disciplines indicates that co-creation of assessment has potential in a variety of contexts for a variety of learners.

Co-created assessments included learning contracts, writing assignments, simulation, presentations, and more. Many assessment decisions were made together, predominantly during the planning stage when determining grading criteria and instructions. In most situations, learners had control over prescribed areas of assessment (RQ3), with higher levels of partnership with students noted in nursing and social service courses. The reported diversity across approaches to co-creation points to the contextual nature of co-creation, which educators should consider when choosing how to approach co-creating with learners in their context.

Discussion during and outside of class time was the most common way educators engaged learners in making decisions (RQ4). This finding may be because without some form of dialogue, co-creation efforts may be pseudo-partnerships (Suliman et al., 2025), which would fall lower on Bovill and Bulley's (2011) ladder of participation and would have been excluded from this review. Educators typically used multiple synchronous and asynchronous interaction strategies. Courses with higher levels of partnership also tended to offer more methods of interaction. Multiple engagement strategies support equitable participation (Dyson, 2018; Killam, Lock, et al., 2023) and are therefore recommended.

Overall, reports were highly positive. Many benefits and ways to support co-creation were described, but no attempt was made in this review to draw conclusions about the benefits of co-creation. Barriers, disadvantages, and ways to make co-creation more successful were also reported across all levels of learner influence. These findings are primarily intended to promote educator reflection while planning for co-creation of assessment.

The highly contextual nature of co-creation may explain conflicting reports. For example, some authors noted initial confusion as a temporary challenge that was resolved as students engaged in the process. However, others reported confusion without indicating if it diminished over time, presenting it as a lasting disadvantage. Confusion may relate to contextual factors such as how new co-creation is to students and how clearly the process is communicated to them. In another example, some authors position co-creation as feasible and practical whereas others note practical challenges with its implementation. Authors who experienced practical challenges indicated that improved planning could help to mitigate them. Additional factors that may have impacted their experience include class size, level of learner, and availability of support and resources.

Potential Biases in the Review Process

To reduce potential sources of bias, this review was designed to capture all dates and broad co-creation terminology in multiple databases. However, to ensure feasibility, we did not conduct comprehensive searches for non-English language publications or systematically search grey literature. Thus, this review may be subject to publication bias. We attempted to minimize this bias by hand searching the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews, as well as screening organizational reports and websites referenced in included publications.

Bias may have been introduced due to different interpretations of article content or inclusion criteria. At times, reports were difficult to understand. We attempted to mitigate this concern by ensuring all reports were reviewed by two or more team members and resolving conflicts through discussion. We involved a third and sometimes fourth reviewer in these conversations as needed and described any key reports that were challenging to interpret or close to meeting the inclusion criteria in Appendix 4. It was also challenging and sometimes seemed artificial to separate co-creation of assessment from other kinds of co-creation occurring in a course. We attempted to mitigate this concern by reporting strategies that supported co-creation of assessment in Table 3. Likewise, we excluded co-creation occurring within clinical placement settings to support the feasibility of this review, as they would have been captured by Barradell and Bell’s (2021) review. However, some of the reports we excluded were outside of the timeframe covered in their review and may have contributed to this review.

We also restricted this review to co-creation of assessment in health professional education. Many reports (n = 54) were excluded for not being in health professional education, meaning that these reports may have described assessment co-creation in higher education contexts that could be transferable to health professional education.

Agreements and Disagreements With Other Reviews

Our findings are mainly consistent with 5 other recent reviews on co-creation in health professional education (Abbonizio et al., 2024; Ambrosetti et al., 2025; Barradell & Bell, 2021; O'Connor et al., 2021; Suliman et al., 2025).

Together, recent reviews of co-creation in health professions education highlight disciplinary and geographical variation in existing literature. Barradell and Bell (2021) found that most qualitative reports of students as partners were from North America and focused on medicine. Another recent scoping review, which examined curriculum co-design, similarly reported a dominance of medical studies in Western contexts, particularly the USA and the UK (Abbonizio et al., 2024). In contrast, Suliman et al. (2025) located most of their literature about wellness outcomes because of co-creation in Europe, again mainly in medicine. O’Connor et al. (2021) focused specifically on nursing and midwifery, with most studies based in the UK. We found that most papers were from North America and Oceania, but unlike others, only 2 originated in medicine and nursing was the most represented profession. These differences are likely attributed to our narrower focus on co-creation of assessment during a course as well as multiple included reports from the same author in these regions. Geographical patterns across reviews suggest that research from the Global South is notably underrepresented. Overall patterns may also reflect both global publication trends and our use of English-language search terms and databases.

Only two reports in our review were explicitly co-authored with students. Barradell and Bell (2021) also found low rates of co-authorship, acknowledging that it may be owing to challenges inherent in involving students as co-authors. However, Abbonizio et al. (2024) identified that 13 of the 21 included papers in their review were co-authored with students, suggesting that co-authorship remains possible. Mercer-Mapstone (2020) advocates for a more consistent and concerted effort to include learners as co-authors in papers about co-creation, which remains an area where future reviews or studies could improve. Our review includes student co-authors, which serves as a study strength.

In Abbonizio et al.’s (2024) review students were positioned as agents for change where students were empowered to change education or partners where they collaborated with educators to co-create outputs. Studies in our review rarely positioned students at the forefront of driving educational change, but there was a strong emphasis on learner empowerment in some reports. While transforming education was sometimes cited as a goal in some reports, the main focus of co-creation of assessment was on a partnership with learners to improve the educational experience and prepare them for real-world practice.

Our review surfaced reports of similar outcomes related to learner well-being as identified by Suliman et al. (2025), namely positive emotions, engagement, positive relationships, meaning, and accomplishment. Our review surfaced more reports of accomplishment-related outcomes (i.e., increased autonomy, evaluative judgment, and confidence in professional abilities) and fewer reports of specific emotional responses such as gratitude, pride, or reduced stress. These differences may reflect our narrower focus on assessment co-creation, variation in how data were analyzed, or our inclusion of non-research reports. Still, our findings reinforce that assessment co-creation can meaningfully support learner well-being.

Our review also echoes findings that the ways learner-educator co-creation is occurring are diverse and reports are mostly positive (Barradell & Bell, 2021; Matthews et al., 2019; O’Connor et al., 2021). The highly contextual and diverse ways in which co-creation is occurring are not surprising given the wider educational discourse around customizable education. Our review adds depth to the findings of previous reviews by offering clear examples of starting points into co-creation of assessment, namely learning contracts in the planning phase and writing assignments or simulation in one or more phases of assessment.

In addition, our review confirms Barradell and Bell’s (2021) finding that challenges reported during co-creation related to logistics, time commitment, limited support from colleagues or institutions, shifting roles, and difficulty building trust. We did not find reports expressing concern around future translation or single disciplinary perspectives during co-creation of assessment as they did in their review. However, their finding that the literature contains infrequent mention of challenges with co-creation of assessment is surprising since co-creation has previously been identified as challenging (Bovill et al., 2016), and health professional programs are highly regulated and content-heavy. Authors publishing about co-creation may be advocates for it and therefore less likely to focus on describing these challenges. Despite limited depth in reports of disadvantages and barriers, 20 reports in our review identified challenges such as initial confusion, terminology, feedback delays, resistance, concern over marginalization of student voices, and maintaining objectivity in grading diverse work, which adds to existing literature. Some of these challenges may be specific to co-creation of assessment and not experienced with co-creation of curriculum or learning.

Our findings also support the conclusion from Abbonizio et al. (2024) and O’Connor et al. (2021) that overall, descriptions of learner-educator partnerships are lacking in depth across the literature. We agree with them that more research is needed to refine pedagogy and evaluate the outcomes of co-creation.

Limitations

Our scoping review has limitations. First, this review was limited to health professional education, meaning it did not capture the ways in which co-creation of assessment is implemented in other higher education settings. While we acknowledge that principles of co-creation may be transferable from other domains, our focus on health professions education was intentional to ensure contextual relevance. This field is influenced by distinct regulatory and competency-based constraints that may impact how co-creation of assessment is implemented. As a result, this review does not capture the full range of theoretical or conceptual frameworks on co-creation emerging from broader higher education literature.

Second, despite our efforts to be inclusive, this review may not have captured all the ways in which co-creation of assessment is reported. One contributing factor is the wide variety of ways it may have been described. In addition, to make the review feasible, we only examined articles where there was an indication that co-creation was occurring in the abstract. It remains possible that co-creation of assessment was integrated into papers but was not evident in abstracts, as it was not the focus of the report. To maintain feasibility, we limited our grey literature search to known or referenced websites, meaning that all possible websites were not captured in our search. We also did not track website screening separate from other sources in Covidence, limiting our ability to report on specific numbers of websites screened. Likewise, we also did not conduct a separate search for dissertations, conference proceedings, grey literature, or government reports.

Third, it was often difficult to place reports on Bovill and Bulley’s (2011) ladder of participation due to insufficient detail. Therefore, we made our best efforts to include reports that most likely met our definition of co-creation but may have missed some. Lastly, this scoping review was labour-intensive and may have been impacted by investigator fatigue when reading difficult-to-decipher reports.

Authors’ Conclusions

Implications for Educational Practice

Overall, co-creation of assessment was portrayed positively across included literature. Still, these findings cannot be generalized, meaning that educators should not change their practice without first reflecting on the contextual appropriateness of co-creation. While our findings show that co-creation has been used in many contexts, we cannot make any claims about where it should be used. Educators who are interested in trying co-creation of assessment in their courses are encouraged to use the findings of this review to generate ideas appropriate for their contexts.

We identified diverse approaches to co-created assessment in this review; however, learning contracts, written assignments, and simulation as well as planning assignments and grading together were frequently reported and represented across all disciplinary categories. These strategies may therefore serve as a starting point for educators to consider feasible and context-appropriate ways to engage learners in co-creation of assessment in health professional education.

Implications for Research

Co-creation of assessment remains an understudied intervention in health professional education. Most identified studies explored student perspectives; research exploring educator perspectives of co-creation is lacking across the reports included in this review, thereby leaving a critical gap in understanding how educators approach decision-making and manage institutional or accreditation-related constraints. However, the overall limited available research, diversity of interventions, and highly contextual nature of co-creation also points to the need to conduct further research on student experiences with context-specific forms of assessment co-creation.

There remains a need for both qualitative and quantitative studies in this area to identify how co-creation of assessment impacts learning, engagement, and evaluative judgement. Qualitative studies should include a more in-depth examination of educator and diverse student experiences with co-creation. Quantitative studies are also needed to measure the impact of co-creation on quantifiable learning outcomes. Researchers should also isolate the impact of different assessment co-creation decisions (e.g., deciding on grading criteria vs. grades) and specific strategies for co-creation (e.g., individual meetings vs. back and forth written feedback) to enable educators to make informed decisions about what and how they are co-creating.

Several reports in this review included co-creation of assessment as a component of a larger pedagogy or process. It would therefore be interesting to examine whether co-creation is more effective when used as part of an overall strategy or if it can be effective when used in isolation. Likewise, examining how the type or depth of assessment co-creation influences the benefits and barriers experienced could yield meaningful insights. Given the paucity of literature in this area, a systematic review of evidence across higher education contexts may be needed, but to keep it manageable, we recommend examining qualitative and quantitative evidence in separate reviews. Reviews that incorporate specific searches to locate non-English literature may also help to uncover reports not captured in our review.

Final Message

Co-creation of assessment, though complex and context-specific, offers an opportunity for inclusive, meaningful, and authentic assessment practices in health professional education. While implementation challenges remain, this review identifies promising examples of educational practices and directions for future research. Moving forward, educators are encouraged to implement, test, and report co-created assessment innovations. Scholars must explore how to implement co-creation of assessment in a way that upholds the rigorous academic standards in health professional contexts while enabling evidence-informed innovative assessment design that prepares learners for health professional practice.
