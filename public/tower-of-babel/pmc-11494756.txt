Preoperative frailty tendency predicts delirium occurrence in older people undergoing spinal surgery
Authors: Yu Zhang, Xiaochen Zhang, Peng Qi, Jinfang Lu, Mei Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis prospective cohort study focused on the predictive value of frailty or pre-frailty assessed by Edmonton Frailty Scale (EFS) for postoperative delirium in spinal surgery patients.MethodsThe primary outcome measurement was postoperative delirium (POD) evaluated by Confusion Assessment Method at day 1, day 2, and day 3 after the surgery. Secondary outcomes included severity and duration of POD, severe postoperative pain measured by Faces Pain Scale-Revised. Introduction

Postoperative delirium (POD), defined as an acute and fluctuating cognitive disturbance in attention and awareness [1], is a prevalent complication observed in older people following surgery [2]. The reported incidence rates of POD vary between 13% and 50% [3]. The presence of POD has been linked to unfavorable surgical outcomes [4], prolonged hospital stays, increased healthcare costs [5], functional decline [6], and elevated mortality rates [7]. It is widely recognized that advanced age and frailty serve as significant risk factors for the development of POD [8]. Frailty is a complex and diverse clinical syndrome that encompasses various domains, such as physical, cognitive, social, and psychological aspects. It is characterized by a deterioration in functioning across multiple physiological systems, leading to heightened susceptibility to stressors [9] and an elevated risk of negative outcomes [10]. This condition affects a significant number of older individuals globally, with a prevalence ranging from 9.9 to 17.4% among community-dwelling older adults [11, 12] and 30–40% among older surgical patients [13]. However, there is currently a lack of a universally accepted gold standard for assessing frailty. The prevailing instruments utilized in clinical practice are variations of the frailty phenotype (FP) or frailty indexes (FI) [9]. The use of FI in a preoperative setting can be time-consuming, while FP primarily focuses on physical frailty. On the other hand, the Edmonton frailty scale (EFS) is a multidimensional assessment tool that encompasses nine domains. In contrast to FI, EFS shows promise as a tool specifically designed for the preoperative setting and has demonstrated feasibility in preoperative clinics, as well as predictive value for postoperative outcomes [14]. To determine whether frailty and pre-frailty assessed by the Edmonton Frailty Scale have a predictive value for POD in spinal surgery patients, we performed this prospective cohort study.

Materials and methods

This is a single-center prospective observational cohort study at Changzheng hospital (Shanghai, China). The protocol of this study has been approved by the Ethic Committee of Shanghai Changzheng Hospital (Approval file number: 2022SL044) and informed consent was obtained from all the patients. The trial was retrospectively registered at chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2300073306) on 6th July 2023. The primary outcome measurement was POD outcome evaluated by Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) at day 1, day 2, and day 3 after the surgery. Secondary outcomes included severity and duration of POD, severe postoperative pain measured by Faces Pain Scale-Revised (FPS-R).

Patients aged more than 65-years old, who scheduled elective spinal surgery between December 2021 and June 2022, were enrolled in this study. Potentially eligible patients were identified by daily reviewing electric medical record of Changzheng Hospital. Patients with emergency surgery, expected length of survive ≤ 24 h, severe preoperative cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE) ≤ 10), visual or hearing impairment (unable to communicate with interviewer with mandarin) were excluded.

Baseline demographic, comorbidities (sensory impairments, diabetes, hypertension, peripheral vascular diseases, and cerebrovascular events history including stroke, heart attacks), anesthesia and surgical events were collected. Anesthesiologists received minimal directions regarding the choice of general anesthetic, as previous literature has not found a difference between total intravenous anesthesia, inhalational anesthesia and POD [15]. We also collected the intraoperative and postoperative information, including intraoperative bleeding, duration of surgery, the time when surgeries ended.

Sample size

We computed the sample size at a total 220 patients in study, based on a power calculation of patients required for 80% power for a odds ratio of 3.0 at the P = 0.05 level between fit patients and frailty patients, according to our pretest results, with a baseline incidence of POD of 16% and a 5% loss to follow up.

Frailty assessment (edmonton frailty scale)

The EFS is an 11-question questionnaire which evaluate nine domains including cognition, general health status, functional independence, social support, medication use, nutrition, mood, continence, functional performance [16]. The maximum score is 17, and according the official EFS website (Edmontonfrailscale.org) suggestion, frailty condition of patients is classified as Fit (score 0–3), Vulnerable (score 4–5), and frail (score 6–17) [14].

POD assessment (confusion assessment method)

The Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) includes an instrument and diagnostic algorithm for identification of delirium, which assesses presence, severity, and fluctuation of nine delirium character [17]. This instrument includes 2 essential features, acute onset and fluctuating course (feature 1) and inattention (feature 2), and 2 inessential features, disorganized thinking (feature 3) and altered level of consciousness (feature 4). The diagnosis of delirium by CAM requires the presence of feature 1 and 2 and either 3 or 4 [18]. The severity of delirium was measured by CAM-severity short-form (CAM-S). Each feature of CAM, except fluctuation, was rated as absent (0), mild (1), or marked (2), while acute onset or fluctuation was rated as absent (0) or present (1). The sum of the 4 ratings yielded a CAM-S severity score, ranging from 0 to 7, and delirium severity is classified as no delirium (CAM-S score = 0), mild (CAM-S score = 1), moderate (CAM-S score = 2), severe (CAM-S score 3–7) [19]. The CAM and CAM-S were assessed once per day 1–3 days after surgery.

Cognitive testing (mini-mental status examination)

The Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE) is a 30-point scale, assessing 7 cognitive domains including orientation, registration, attention and calculation, recall, language, and visuoconstruction [20]. The MMSE scores of 30 for no, 26–29 for questionable, 21–25 for mild, 11–20 for moderate, 0–10 for severe dementia [21] and 3-point change considered clinically significant [22].

Postoperative maximum rest pain assessment (faces pain scale-revised)

The Faces Pain Scale-Revised (FPS-R) uses 6 line-drawing faces scored 0–10, representations of increasing pain severity [23]. Though FPS-R is designed for assessment of the intensity of children’s acute pain, it has shown good reliability and sensitivity for assessing postoperative pain intensity in elder [24, 25]. Face number 4 (score 6) and up indicated clinically significant pain (moderate-to-severe pain) among children [26, 27] and adults [28].

Statistical analysis

Data were evaluated for normality and outliers. Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to check the normal distribution of continuous data. We conducted independent t test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables in univariate analysis, data reported as mean ± SD or median [25th, 75th percentiles], respectively, and for categorical variables, using chi-square test and Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel chi-square tests, with data reported as count [%]. Univariable logistic regression was used to screen the potential confounders. The variables with P < 0.2 in the univariable logistic regression and the important risk factors of postoperative delirium based on literature reports were selected as covariates, which might confound the relationship of interest. Finally, a total number of 9 variables (DBP, surgery started after 17:00, duration of surgery more than 3 h, intraoperative blood refusion, using flumazenil at the end of surgery, performing regional anesthesia at the end of surgery, using Butorphanol in postoperative patient-controlled intravenous analgesia, moderate-to severe pain 1 day after surgery, moderate-to severe pain 2 day after surgery, seen in Supplement Table 2) were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. A value of P < 0.05 was defined as significance. All analyses were conducted with IBM SPSS statistics, version 26 (IBM Corp, USA).

Results

Demographics

A total of 325 patients aged more than 65-years old scheduled for elective spine surgery in Shanghai Changzheng Hospital were enrolled in this study. Of these, 250 patients who met our inclusion criteria were recruited into the study. nineteen patients were excluded: 17(6.8%) for surgery cancelled, 2(0.8%) for dropouts. The remaining 231(71.5%) patients gave informed consent and were included in this analysis (Fig. 1). As Table 1 shows that the median age of the cohort 69 years with 36.8% being frail (EFS score ≥ 6) and 28.5% being vulnerable (EFS score 4–5). The frail group had a less proportion of men (32.9%, P < 0.001), more comorbidities (P < 0.001) and less educational level (P = 0.017), but there is no significant difference in terms of Body Mass Index (BMI), age, MMSE score as well as ASA score among people with different frailty condition (Table 1). All continuous data did not follow normal distribution (Supplement Table 1).

POD occurred in 17.7% of all patients (41 in 231 patients). Patients who assessed as frail or vulnerable had significantly higher proportion of POD (23.5% in the frail cohort, 21.2% in the Vulnerable cohort, and 8.8% in the fit cohort, P = 0.031).

Validity of EFS to predict severity and duration of postoperative delirium

In the cohort, 41 (17.75%) patients developed POD during day 1 to 3 after surgery, most of which developed at 1 day after surgery (36 in 41, Table 4). A higher proportion of frail and vulnerable patients underwent POD compared to the fit group (P = 0.031, Table 4). A statistically significant difference in median CAM-S score during day 1 to 3 after surgery was found between frailty, vulnerable, and fit group (Fig. 2). And patients with frailty had longer duration of POD compared with fit group (Fig. 3). Furthermore, we tested association between each item of EFS and POD using univariate analysis and found that item 3 (functional independence, P = 0.018), item 8 (continence, p = 0.048), and item 9 (functional performance, P = 0.011) are associated with POD (Fig. 4). All of the 3 items are sensitive parameter in identifying frail and pre-frail aging adults [29–32]. Figure 5 shows a statistically significant difference in FPS score during day 1 to 3 after surgery was found between frailty conditions (Fig. 5).

Other risk factors for postoperative delirium

What’s more, the results of multivariable model (Table 3) showed duration of surgery more than 3 h (OR = 3.984, 95% CI: 1.347 to 11.778, P = 0.012), using flumazenil at the end of surgery (OR = 4.210, 95% CI: 1.047 to 16.925, P = 0.043), using butorphanol only in postoperative patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (OR = 19.004, 95% CI: 1.779 to 203.003, P = 0.015), moderate-to-severe pain at day 1 (OR = 7.790, 95% CI: 3.018 to 20.103, P < 0.001) or 2 (OR = 11.133, 95% CI: 1.580 to 78.450, P = 0.016) after surgery are strong independent predictor of POD.

Validity of EFS to predict severe postoperative pain

For the independent influence of severe postoperative pain on POD (Table 3), we compared severe postoperative pain among patients with different frailty condition and conducted multivariable logistic regression to confirm the association between frailty condition and severe postoperative pain after adjusted potential confounders. Results of Chi-square test showed more patients with frailty and vulnerable to frailty experienced severe postoperative pain than fit patients (P < 0.001, Table 5). Result of multivariable logistic regression (Table 6) confirmed the positive association between frailty condition and severe postoperative pain (Frailty vs. Fit: OR = 5.007, 95% CI: 1.903 to 13.174, P = 0.001; Vulnerable vs. Fit: OR = 2.525, 95% CI: 1.008 to 6.329, P = 0.048).

To evaluate the effect of intraoperative and postoperative administration on POD in different frailty condition, Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel stratified tests were performed. Table 7 shows the proportion of POD in vulnerable group with Sufentanil regimen in intravenous patient controlled analgesia (PCA) (7.7% in regimen without Sufentanil in intravenous PCA vs. 30.0% in Sufentanil regimen in intravenous PCA, P = 0.030) significantly increased, while no significantly difference was found in frailty group (23.1% in regimen without Sufentanil in intravenous PCA vs. 23.9% in Sufentanil regimen in intravenous PCA, P = 0.872) or fit group (9.4% in regimen without Sufentanil in intravenous PCA vs. 88.3% in Sufentanil regimen in intravenous PCA, P = 0. 928). However, we failed to find difference in the test of homogeneity of the odds ratio between different frailty conditions (P = 0.176).

Discussion

Frailty develops as a consequence of age-related decline in many physiological systems and diminishing the body adaptation to stressful events and vulnerable to stressors [9, 10]. Frailty is a heterogeneous clinical syndrome, including physical, cognitive, social, and psychological domines, and consequently, have 4 principal phenotypes respectively [33]. Edmonton frailty scale is a multidimensional frailty assessment tool with 9 domains, covering all the four principal phenotypes, most of which has been reported as risk factor for postoperative delirium [8]. In this study, we found that vulnerable to frailty, along with duration of surgery more than 3 h, using flumazenil at the end of surgery, using butorphanol only in postoperative patient-controlled intravenous analgesia and moderate-to-severe pain at day 1 or 2 after surgery, is a strong predictor of postoperative delirium, especially in older adults with difficulty in functional independence, continence and functional performance. Our results also confirmed that frailty was associated with longer duration and stronger severity of postoperative delirium.

Surgery and postoperative pain can trigger proinflammatory cytokines, which are major causes of postoperative delirium [34]. Moderate-to-Severe postoperative pain has been confirmed as an independently strong risk factor in previous studies [35, 36], which means more powerful stressor and attack for aged patients. A study has proved that frailty was significantly associated with moderate to severe pain after robot-assisted laparoscopic radical prostatectomy (RARP), and might be a potential predictor of postoperative pain [37]. High level of postoperative pain and following using high opioid doses increase risk for delirium [38]. However, few studies considered acute pain effects and perioperative opioid using when evaluating association between frailty and POD. Our study confirmed that high pain score in the first two days after surgery is independently risk factor for POD (Table 3) and FPS-R score in frailty group are significantly higher than fit group in first 3 days after surgery (Fig. 5). Results of Chi-square test and multivariable logistic regression analysis confirmed that frailty condition is independent risk factor of severe postoperative pain (Tables 5 and 6), which might indicate that patients with frailty and vulnerable to frailty are apt to experience POD for more serious pain suffering. Furthermore, we compared the odds of POD between regimen of intravenous PCA with or without Sufentanil in different frailty conditions. The proportion of POD in vulnerable group with Sufentanil regimen in intravenous PCA significantly increased while we failed to find significant difference in fit group or frailty group (Table 7). Results of Mann–Whitney U test and Pearson correlation test showed no significant difference in FPS-R score in day 1, day 2 and day 3 after surgery between regimen with or without sufentanil in intravenous PCA (Supplement Table 4). These results might indicate that Sufentanil regimen in intravenous PCA should be carefully used in patients vulnerable to frailty.

Previous studies found that risk factors of postoperative delirium included age, function dependency, pre-existing cognitive impairment, psychiatric disorders, cerebrovascular disease, end stage renal failure, low albumin, higher ASA score, and intra-operative blood transfusion [1, 39, 40]. However, we failed to find significant association between postoperative delirium and preoperative cognition impairment (lower MMSE score), comorbidities, social independence (not in marriage and living independently) in this cohort. Several studies found flumazenil, as a specific benzodiazepine receptor antagonist, presented a potential optional for delirium associated with benzodiazepine exposure [41–43] and improved cognitive and neuromotor emergence after volatile anesthesia [44]. To our surprise, flumazenil administration at the end of general anesthesia showed a promotor to postoperative delirium in this study (Tables 1 and 3). Previous animal study found that flumazenil could reverse accrual of sleep, specifically rapid eye movement (REM), is known to occur with the administration of volatile anesthetics and promoted an awake state [45]. Therefore, flumazenil administration might be associated with longer awake state at night of the day operation finished in this cohort, and consequently, might cause mean circadian rhythm disturbances, which is associated with delirium [46]. The assured association between flumazenil administration and postoperative delirium in aged and frail population still need further study.

This study has certain limitations. Firstly, this single-center observational study was conducted in a limited geographical region, and the study cohort is rather small. Only Chinese patients were included, and all patients only underwent spinal surgery. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings should be limited, and further research on this topic around different patient populations and different types of surgeries is warranted. Secondly, the incidence of postoperative delirium might be underestimated because the CAM assessment was performed by anesthesiologists once per day at the first 3 days after surgery, and the CAM results can only reflect the condition of patients when anesthesiologists visited. Hence, patients developed postoperative delirium in 4–7 day after surgery were not included. Furthermore, some potential risk factor information was missed, such as preoperative pain, which is common in spinal surgery patients. Though we perform logistic regression analysis to examine the effects of significant variables in univariate analysis, the effects of these missed variables cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In conclusion, frailty can increase the risk, severity, and duration of delirium in the first 3 days after surgery of patients.
