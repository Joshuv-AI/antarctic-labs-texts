Predicting movement intensity through anthro-fitness profiling and its association with match outcomes in youth badminton: a logistic regression-based machine approach
Authors: Mohamad Nizam Nazarudin, Rabiu Muazu Musa, Anwar P. P. Abdul Majeed, Naresh Bhaskar Raj, Vijayamurugan Eswaramoorthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe fast-paced nature, coupled with the dynamic movement of badminton, renders movement intensity an essential indicator for success. In youth badminton, anthropometric attributes and fitness profile varies across players, yet little is known about the association of these indicators with competitive outcomes. In this study, we aim to classify movement intensity levels and examine the relationship between intensity classification and winning probability in youth badminton players.MethodsA total of 67 youth badminton players (mean age = 14.13 ± 1.67 years; badminton experience = 5.55 ± 2.05 years) drawn from different badminton programs in Malaysia participated in the study. Inertial measurement units were used to capture movement intensity during match play, while anthropometric, physiological and fitness evaluations were completed.ResultsK-means clustering grouped players into high and low movement intensities (HMI; LMI), respectively. The logistic regression model yielded an excellent performance in classifying the HMI and LMI with 97% accuracy. Feature importance analysis identified 11 essential predictors comprising velocity, agility, reaction time, core muscle endurance, height and weight as the most influential variables in differentiating intensity levels. Match outcome analysis revealed that HMI players had a winning probability of 68.4%, compared to 60.4% for LMI players.ConclusionThe ability for players to sustain higher movement intensity during match play is non-trivial for ensuring success in youth badminton. The application of machine learning in athlete profiling could assist coaches in enhancing training design and workload monitoring, leading to long-term success in youth badminton.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01954-9.

Introduction

Badminton is a high-intensity racket sport characterised by intermittent, multidirectional movements, rapid changes of pace, and short bursts of explosive actions. These actions place considerable stress on the physiological, anthropometric, and fitness attributes of youth athletes [1]. Monitoring movement intensity on court is important for optimising training loads and reducing injury risk in youth racket sports, especially badminton, where rallies involve rapid directional changes and substantial fluctuations in mechanical load [2]. Due to the complex and intermittent nature of badminton match play, youth athletes are exposed to various demands that are not easily captured through conventional training load measures. Accurate measurement and evaluation of these demands are essential not only for enhancing performance but also for preventing injuries, particularly during the sensitive stages of physical and psycho-physiological development in youth athletes [3].

In this vein, wearable inertial-measurement units (IMUs) become popular in sports performance measurement and are increasingly shown to be reliable and valid for quantification of performance demands, wherein such IMUs show high reliability in badminton-specific court drills [4]. While wearable IMUs are increasingly validated for court drills, their practical application in grassroots and youth developmental settings is often limited by high costs, the technical expertise required for data processing, and the physical discomfort sensors may cause during rapid growth phases. Consequently, development of a proxy model that can predict movement intensity using accessible field-based assessments could be essential.

Certain inherent qualities, such as players’ characteristics and fitness levels, could serve as an indicator of how intensely young athletes can move. For instance, preceding researchers have documented that core muscle endurance plays an essential role in dynamic balance and stroke velocity among adolescent badminton players [5]. Similarly, agility, aerobic capacity, and biological maturation have consistently been identified as key determinants of sprint speed, change-of-direction ability, and jump performance in youth male athletes [6]. Other studies have recognised the importance of stature, limb length, and body composition for shuttlecock reach and stroke effectiveness [7], as well as aerobic and anaerobic capacities for rally sustainability [8]. Despite these attempts, however, empirical research that simultaneously considers anthropometric, fitness, and physiological profiles relative to movement intensity and match success remains scarce.

Quantifying the relationship between movement intensity and match outcomes presents both conceptual and methodological constraints. For example, traditional approaches in sports performance analysis have often relied on summary statistics to link discrete performance indicators with competitive success [9]. However, the nonlinear and interdependent nature of anthropometric and fitness determinants requires non-conventional analytical approaches capable of capturing complex associations within high-dimensional datasets [10–12]. In senior elite athletes, anthropometric markers are relatively stable; however, youth athletes undergo rapid, non-linear changes in stature, limb length, and body composition due to biological maturation. These dynamic developmental differences create complex, interdependent relationships between physical traits and movement intensity that traditional linear statistics cannot easily capture. Therefore, a machine learning approach is required to navigate this high-dimensional data and provide accurate, individualized athlete profiling. Logistic regression-based machine learning modelling provides interpretable probabilistic predictions of outcome likelihoods and has previously shown to be effective in classifying winning and losing performances as well as discriminating between two different groups in various sports and fitness task-based activities [13–16].

Against this background, the present investigation aimed at developing a logistic regression–based machine learning pipeline to elucidate the interaction of movement intensity with anthropometric, fitness and physiological athlete profiles as well as to ascertain the likelihood of winning or losing with respect to intensity levels of youth badminton players during competition. The technique adopted herein is a non-trivial task as it contributes to performance analytics by advancing a non-conventional framework coupled with practical interpretability for coaches, trainers and other relevant stakeholders seeking data-driven talent identification and training prescription strategies to better tailor training interventions to support safe and effective athlete development.

Materials and methods

Participant’s characteristics

A total of 67 youth badminton players (mean age = 14.13 ± 1.67 years; badminton experience = 5.55 ± 2.05 years), comprising 45 boys and 22 girls, were recruited from various regional training centres affiliated with the national badminton development programme in Malaysia. Inclusion criteria were: (i) actively competing at the junior level, (ii) free from musculoskeletal injury within the past 6 months, and (iii) having completed standardised fitness, maturity, and competition assessments. Prior to the commencement of data collection in this study, written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians, and ethical approval was obtained from the university’s Malaysia Terengganu Human Research Ethic Committee Review Board with an ethical endorsement letter (UMT/JKEPM/2023/164). It is worth noting that this study adhered to standard guidelines for research involving human subjects in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.

Justification for samples size recruitments

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software to determine the minimum sample size required for adequate statistical power in the modelling analyses. Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.23), an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power of 0.80, the recommended sample size was approximately 65 participants. The final sample of 67 youth badminton players, therefore, met and slightly exceeded this threshold, reducing the risk of Type II error and ensuring sufficient power to detect meaningful associations between fitness, anthropometric profiles, and intensity levels classification [17].

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted to classify movement intensity levels among senior youth badminton players using fitness, anthropometric, and physiological profiles. The investigation was embedded within a real-world training environment, ensuring ecological validity and capturing movement intensities representative of athletes actively engaged in high-performance development programs. By situating the study in a naturalistic training context, the findings provide practical insights into how movement intensity relates to performance outcomes, offering coaches and practitioners evidence-based guidance for athlete monitoring and program design.

Physiological, fitness and anthropometrics evaluation

Participants completed a series of anthropometric, physiological, and physical fitness assessments following established protocols [18]. It is important to note that all participants underwent a standardized familiarization session 48 h prior to the actual data collection. During this session, the protocols for each fitness assessment were demonstrated by certified sport scientists, and players performed practice trials for each test in order to mitigate the learning effect whereby performance improvements, occur due to task familiarity rather than physical capacity. Anthropometric measures included standing height, body mass, and leg length, recorded using a stadiometer, calibrated electronic scale, and tape measure from the anterior superior iliac spine to the medial malleolus. Physical fitness and motor performance were evaluated through a comprehensive test battery. Balance and postural control were assessed using the Y-balance test (YBT) and stork balance test, while lower-limb muscular endurance was measured with the single-leg wall sit. Coordination was evaluated using the hand wall toss test, while core and upper muscle endurance were assessed via the plank, sit-up, and push-up tests (maximum repetitions per minute) respectively. Explosive lower-body power was measured using the standing broad jump (SBJ) and vertical jump (VJ). Reaction time was determined with the ruler drop test, and speed–agility performance was assessed using a shuttle run specifically designed for badminton [19]. Hand grip strength was measured with a hydraulic dynamometer (Jamar Hydraulic, Patterson Medical, USA). Finally, players’ internal loads were tracked during competitive matches using RPE, recorded immediately after each game. The RPE was recorded using the Borg 6–20 scale immediately after each session to assess internal load, while fitness and anthropometric assessments were conducted prior to the first session under standardised conditions.

Settings

All assessments were conducted in the morning (08:30–11:00) on non-consecutive weekdays (Tuesday and Thursday) to minimise fatigue-related variability. Testing conditions were standardised, with indoor temperatures maintained at 24–26 °C and relative humidity at 60–65%. Players had at least 24 h of recovery before each session, and a standardised dynamic warm-up (~ 10 min) preceded all assessments. The test sequence followed a fixed order: anthropometry, balance and coordination, muscular endurance, strength, power, speed, and agility. Rest intervals of 2–5 min were provided between tests, depending on intensity. All procedures were supervised by certified sport scientists, and for performance-based assessments, three trials were conducted where applicable, with the average value used for statistical analysis [20].

Match performance analysis for determining winners and losers

Winners and losers were determined from organised selection competitions conducted by each club, ensuring strong relevance to real-game scenarios. To maintain fairness, matches were paired according to players’ age groups and gender. Each match followed a standardised format of three sets, with the best of 15 points deciding the outcome. This structure provided reliable comparisons of movement intensity across diverse player profiles and match contexts, while preserving ecological validity in the assessment of competitive performance.

Measurement of players’ movement intensity

For the quantification of player velocity during match play, wearable inertial sensors (Xsens) were positioned at the players’ lumbar region to capture raw rotational angles (Rx, Ry, Rz) [21]. To ensure optimal data recording, the sampling frequency was set at 30 Hz as recommended by the preceding investigators [22]. These signals were processed using numerical differentiation to derive angular velocities (Wx, Wy, Wz). To quantify movement intensity, angular velocity values were aggregated across all sampled timepoints for each player, providing a cumulative measure of total movement output. For the purpose of this study, this cumulative velocity measure is operationally defined as movement intensity.

Clustering of players’ movement intensities

This study utilised the k-means algorithm to cluster the movement intensity of the players during the competitive matches into distinct, non-overlapping groups. Silhouette analysis identified two optimal clusters (k = 2), with a score of 0.61, indicating strong internal cohesion and clear separation between groups. Each data point was assigned to a single cluster using Euclidean distance, ensuring consistency within clusters and differentiation between them. This method enabled a structured analysis of load patterns, offering deeper insights into player-specific workload profiles.

Data treatment and pre-processing

The data on the evaluated fitness, anthropometric, and physiological attributes of the players were accumulated and pre-processed. To enhance model stability and prevent overfitting given the number of predictors, L_2 regularization (Ridge penalty) was utilized. This approach constrains the magnitude of the model coefficients, ensuring that no single feature disproportionately influences the prediction. Additionally, the pre-processing pipeline included a standardized min–max scaling technique to normalize all 18 features into a range of 0 to 1, ensuring uniform contribution across variables with different units of measurement [23, 24]. Moreover, to ensure the stability of the logistic regression coefficients and mitigate the risk of multi-collinearity, collinearity diagnostics were performed. Variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values were calculated for all independent variables. By normalising the data and including a comprehensive set of anthropometric, fitness and physiological indicators, we aim to develop a robust predictive model that can provide valuable insights into the factors influencing match outcomes in elite badminton. Model development pipeline

The logistic regression (LR) model was developed using a dataset of 67 participants each representing a single datapoint. To ensure a rigorous evaluation, the dataset was partitioned into a training/validation set (70%, n = 47) and an independent test set (30%, n = 20). Within the training phase (n = 47), a stratified 5-fold cross-validation strategy was applied to optimize the model. Additionally, an internal holdout validation (n = 15) was used to generate the confusion matrix and initial performance metrics before the final model was evaluated against the completely unseen independent test set (n = 20). This multi-layered approach was selected to mitigate the risk of overfitting and ensure the model’s generalization capability on new data. A feature importance analysis was conducted to examine the physiological and anthropometric parameters that most strongly influenced model performance. This technique was selected because it effectively quantifies the relevance of each attribute by considering the mutual information between predictors and the target variable, thereby providing a balanced assessment of both linear and non-linear relationships within the dataset [25, 26]. By identifying the most influential variables, the method highlights the parameters that significantly impact performance, ultimately enhancing both the interpretability and predictive power of the model [27, 28].

Model evaluation metrics

To evaluate the efficacy of the developed model, several performance metrics were employed, including classification accuracy (ACC), area under the curve (AUC), recall, precision (PREC), F1 score, Cohen’s Kappa, and Matthew’s correlation coefficient (MCC). Accuracy represents the proportion of correctly classified instances (true positives and true negatives) out of all cases, providing a general measure of overall effectiveness. AUC refers to the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, which plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate across different thresholds, indicating the model’s ability to distinguish between classes. Recall (sensitivity) measures the proportion of actual positive cases correctly identified, while precision indicates the proportion of true positives among all positive predictions, reflecting the reliability of positive classifications. The F1 score, as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, balances both metrics and is particularly useful in the presence of class imbalance. Cohen’s Kappa evaluates the agreement between predicted and actual classifications, adjusted for chance, offering insight into consistency beyond simple accuracy. MCC, ranging from − 1 to + 1, provides a balanced correlation measure that accounts for true and false positives and negatives, even under imbalanced conditions. Collectively, these metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation of the model’s performance, highlighting its strengths and limitations across multiple dimensions.

Discussion

It was demonstrated that both movement intensity clusters exhibit broadly comparable anthropometric and general fitness profiles, suggesting that foundational physical qualities such as balance, flexibility, and core muscle endurance may not independently differentiate high- and low-intensity performers within similarly trained youth cohorts (Table 2). Such homogeneity is consistent with developmental sport contexts, where standardised training exposure often minimises variability in basic fitness components. However, velocity clearly distinguishes the groups, with the HMI cluster showing substantially higher values, along with slightly superior agility, endurance, grip strength, and upper-body muscular endurance. These findings align with physiological profiling research in badminton, showing that higher competitive intensity is associated with greater neuromuscular efficiency and repeated explosive court movements [8, 21]. For instance, during high-level junior matches, players who can accelerate rapidly into the forecourt after a drop shot and recover swiftly to the baseline after a smash are better positioned to dictate the tempo of the rally, thereby reflecting the functional importance of intensity-related attributes. Moreover, the slightly older and heavier profile of the HMI group further suggests maturational influences, potentially enhancing force production and acceleration capacity [30]. Interestingly, while the HMI group exhibited superior on-court movement intensity, they recorded slower simple reaction times compared to the LMI group. This suggests that high movement intensity in youth badminton may rely more on anticipatory cues and complex agility rather than isolated simple reaction speed. Furthermore, the HMI group's slightly older and heavier profile may influence raw neuromuscular response times in non-sport-specific tasks, even as they dominate the physical demands of match play. Consequently, while general fitness similarities persist across clusters, velocity-linked neuromuscular characteristics emerge as the principal discriminators of higher-intensity movement performance in youth badminton.

The excellent overall performance of the LR model in the classification of the HMI and LMI clusters is demonstrated in Table 3 and Figs. 1, 2 and 3. The near-perfect discriminative performance (AUC = 1.0) and high accuracy observed are attributed to the clear separation of the velocity clusters initially identified through K-means analysis. While such high metrics can sometimes indicate overfitting, the consistency between the cross-validation scores (0.97) and the independent test set performance (0.95) suggests that the model has captured robust underlying patterns rather than noise. Furthermore, the use of regularization within the machine learning pipeline served to penalize complex coefficients, further safeguarding against overfitting. These results highlight the robustness of integrating logistic regression within a machine learning pipeline, where systematic feature selection and cross-validation enhance predictive consistency across heterogeneous athlete profiles [14, 31, 32]. Such predictive accuracy is consistent with recent applications of machine learning in sports, which have successfully classified performance outcomes and injury risks by integrating physiological and fitness-related variables [16, 33, 34]. The excellent prediction demonstrated in Table 4 on fresh unseen data is essential in youth badminton, where differentiating high- and low-intensity players informs tailored training loads, injury prevention strategies, and talent identification. For example, coaches can use these predictive insights to design individualised conditioning programs that emphasise velocity and agility for high-intensity performers, while focusing on endurance and flexibility for lower-intensity counterparts, thereby aligning training interventions with developmental needs and competitive demands.

The recursive feature elimination applied in the study to extract essential anthropometric, physiological and fitness-related variables identified 11 key predictors comprising height, weight, velocity, reaction time, agility–endurance, standing broad jump, grip strength, core muscle endurance, flexibility, and both static and dynamic balance as the most influential contributors to movement intensity classification with noteworthy accuracy as depicted in Figs. 4 and 5 respectively. These findings are congruent with prior research showing that anthropometric and fitness-related variables such as agility, reaction time, and muscle strength are essential determinants of performance in youth racket sports [2, 35, 36]. Similarly, the inclusion of both static and dynamic balances further accentuates the importance of postural control during rapid deceleration and directional changes, particularly on lunging movements common in competitive badminton play [37]. This implies that training programs should be designed enhance explosive power, balance, and reaction speed, as these attributes directly translate into rally dominance and winning probability in badminton. In the same vein, targeted plyometric drills could be supplemented to improve jump performance or core stability exercises to enhance balance, which could assist badminton players in sustaining high-intensity play, while flexibility training supports injury prevention during multidirectional movements. Overall, this feature importance analysis reflects the importance of integrating physiological, anthropometric, and fitness markers into player profiling, offering actionable insights for tailoring youth badminton training to maximise competitive outcomes.

The association between movement intensity and match outcome, with high-intensity players demonstrating a greater probability of winning as well as a lower probability of losing compared to their low-intensity counterparts, is depicted in Fig. 6. This pattern is consistent with performance analysis literature, suggesting that successful badminton play is characterised by the ability to sustain high rally tempo, execute rapid directional changes, and impose physical pressure on opponents [2, 38]. Elevated movement intensity often reflects superior neuromuscular efficiency and aerobic–anaerobic integration, enabling players to recover quickly between explosive actions and maintain tactical aggressiveness throughout extended rallies. In real-world competitive scenarios, such as national junior tournaments, high-intensity players are more likely to dominate exchanges by accelerating rapidly to intercept tight net shots, transitioning explosively into attacking smashes, and sustaining pressure during long rallies, thereby increasing the opponent’s physiological strain and error likelihood. On the other hand, players performing at lower movement intensities may adopt more conservative pacing strategies, which can limit offensive dominance and increase vulnerability during high-tempo phases of play. While movement intensity alone does not determine match outcome, given the influence of technical skill and tactical decision-making, the observed probability differentials accentuate its meaningful contribution to competitive success in youth badminton. In essence, the predictive value of movement intensity for match outcomes provides actionable insights for coaches seeking to align training loads with winning probabilities in youth badminton.

Conclusion

The findings from the current investigation reveal that movement intensity is a catalyst for determining match outcomes among youth badminton players, wherein high movement-intensive players are characterised with higher chances of winning a game. Through the integration of a logistic regression-based machine learning pipeline, we observed an excellent prediction capability, demonstrating physiological, anthropometric, and fitness-related variables such as velocity, agility, reaction time, and core muscle endurance as essential determinants of performance. Likewise, recursive feature elimination further highlighted the importance of these markers in differentiating intensity levels, thereby accentuating their relevance for athlete profiling. Overall, these findings indicate that the application of advanced analytical approaches can help coaches and trainers enhance workload monitoring, optimise training interventions, and support injury prevention in youth badminton.

Practical implications

For coaches and other relevant stakeholders, the current findings echoed the need to prioritize training prescription and load monitoring based on parameters associated with high-intensity performance. For instance, plyometric drills and agility ladders could be introduced to enhance explosive power and directional speed, while core stability and grip-strength exercises could be applied for balance and stroke control. Flexibility training remains essential for injury prevention, especially considering the explosive nature and multidirectional demands of badminton. Moreover, taking into cognisance the influence of maturation and body composition on movement intensity can help tailor conditioning programs to developmental stages, ensuring that younger athletes build foundational endurance while older players refine velocity and reaction speed. This profiling is an essential task for coaches to align training loads with winning probabilities, consequently leading to safer and more effective pathways for long-term athlete success.

Strength and limitation

A key strength of this study revolves around the integration of machine learning with comprehensive athlete profiling, resulting in accurate classification of movement intensity and match outcomes. Similarly, the ecologically-based design applied in real-world training and competition settings warrants a direct application of the findings to youth badminton performance and coaching practice. However, the present study is subject to certain limitations. First, nutritional and psychological indicators were not included, which may influence movement intensity and performance outcomes. Secondly, although sex was accounted for as an independent variable, subgroup analyses considering age, sex, and maturational status were not conducted, leaving potential confounding effects unexplored. While the sample size of 67 participants exceeded the threshold required by our a priori power analysis (Power = 0.80), it is acknowledged that this size is relatively small when considering the 'Rule of Ten' for the 11 identified essential predictors. This may limit the external validity and generalizability of the model to elite senior players or different age cohorts. Future research should involve larger, multi-center cohorts to further validate these findings across broader demographics. Similarly, future studies should employ larger, longitudinal cohorts and integrate direct physiological, psychological, and sport-specific measures to strengthen causal inferences and refine predictive models.
