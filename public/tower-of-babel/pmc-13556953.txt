Pharmacovigilance signals of dupilumab among pediatric patients: A real‑world analysis based on the FAERS database
Authors: Xueqing Ma, Jiaojiao Fan, Ben Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDupilumab is effective for the treatment of moderate‑to‑severe type 2 inflammatory diseases; however, its real‑world safety profile in pediatric patients remains to be further clarified. This retrospective pharmacovigilance study analyzed U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) data from 2017 to 2025, including 44,593 reports where dupilumab was the primary suspect drug in children. Disproportionality analyses (ROR, PRR, BCPNN, MGPS), time-to-onset analysis, stratified analysis, and Weibull modeling assessed adverse event (AE) distribution and patterns. Results showed balanced sex distribution (male 50.1%, female 48.6%), with adolescents (12–17y) most common (45.3%), and report counts increasing over time. 93 positive safety signals were detected. The most common core AEs were rash (4,105 cases; ROR = 4.55, 95% CI: 4.38–4.72), injection site pain (3,582 cases; ROR = 8.59, 95% CI: 8.22–8.97), and skin exfoliation (1,834 cases; ROR = 3.41, 95% CI: 3.23–3.59). Potential unlisted signals included vitiligo (33 cases; ROR = 10.46, 95% CI: 6.52–16.78), skin depigmentation (32 cases; ROR = 13.04, 95% CI: 7.85–21.66), and eye color change (13 cases; ROR = 7.06, 95% CI: 3.54–14.11). Stratified analyses indicated distinct patterns of adverse‑event reporting across age and sex strata. Among cases with complete temporal information, most AEs occurred within 30d after dupilumab initiation, with a median time‑to‑onset of 14d. In conclusion, this large-scale spontaneous-reporting study detected potential safety signals in children. Given FAERS data limitations, the associations should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, and may inform future signal validation and prospective studies.KEYWORDS: Dupilumab, pediatric, adverse drug reactions, pharmacovigilance, FAERS, real-world study

Introduction

Dupilumab is a fully human monoclonal antibody targeting the interleukin (IL)-4 receptor α subunit, which blocks shared IL‑4/IL‑13 signaling and suppresses type 2 immune responses.1 As such, dupilumab has become a cornerstone biologic for multiple type 2 inflammatory disorders. In children, it is approved for moderate‑to‑severe atopic dermatitis (AD) aged ≥ 6 months and moderate‑to‑severe asthma aged ≥ 6 y. Accumulated randomized controlled trial (RCT) and real‑world data support its therapeutic benefits across these pediatric indications.2 For pediatric AD, one real‑world study reported that 74.54% of children aged 6–11 y achieved EASI‑75 at week 16, accompanied by substantial pruritus relief and improved quality of life.3 In pediatric asthma, dupilumab yields favorable efficacy compared with alternative type‑2‑targeted biologics for eligible children4; it reduces exacerbations and improves lung function in real‑world clinical practice.5 A Chinese prospective two‑year cohort further demonstrated that most reported adverse events were mild‑to‑moderate among treated children.6

Nevertheless, RCTs are limited by relatively short follow‑up periods and restrictive enrollment criteria. Emerging post‑marketing safety signals have been described in adult dupilumab recipients in real‑world settings.7,8 Importantly, children are not merely miniature adults: developmental differences including somatic growth, immune ontogeny, and changing drug sensitivity may lead to distinct adverse‑event reporting profiles compared with adults.9 It is therefore valuable to characterize real‑world adverse‑event reporting patterns in pediatric dupilumab users. Such real‑world observations may support clinical risk monitoring and inform treatment‑related decision‑making.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) is one of the world’s largest post‑marketing spontaneous reporting databases, compiling over two decades of safety submissions and enabling signal detection for rare or delayed adverse events that may not be captured within clinical trials.10 It offers strengths including large sample size, broad demographic coverage, and multi‑source reporting, which improve sensitivity for identifying potential pharmacovigilance signals. Notably, FAERS is designed for signal generation only. Due to reporting bias, duplicate submissions, missing clinical details, and the absence of reliable exposure denominators, it cannot establish causality, calculate true incidence, or disentangle underlying biological mechanisms. To identify potential pharmacovigilance signals and describe adverse‑event reporting patterns associated with dupilumab use in children, we performed a retrospective FAERS analysis. After rigorous data cleaning and standardization, this curated dataset was used to generate hypothesis‑generating observations that may inform clinical risk monitoring.

Materials and methods

Data sources and processing

Data for this study were retrieved from FAERS, and we included adverse event reports spanning the first quarter of 2017 to the fourth quarter of 2025. To enhance the specificity of drug–event attribution in signal detection, we restricted our analysis to reports in which dupilumab was designated as the primary suspect drug. AGE_COD was unit-converted to years, with patient age limited to 0–17 y. Drug names were normalized to uppercase, cleansed of formulation and strength details, and mapped to RxNorm concepts using the terms “DUPILUMAB,” “DUPIXENT,” “SAR231893,” and “REGN668.” Non-matching entries were fuzzy-matched using Jaro–Winkler (threshold 0.90), and all matches were manually adjudicated. Following FDA recommendations, the data extraction and deduplication procedure was performed as follows: PRIMARYID, CASEID, and FDA_DT were extracted from the DEMO table. For multiple reports sharing the same CASEID, the record with the latest FDA_DT was retained; if FDA_DT were identical, the one with the largest PRIMARYID was kept. After deduplication, the DRUG and REAC tables were linked by PRIMARYID. In the REAC table, each record corresponds to one MedDRA Preferred Term (PT). A single report may contain multiple different PTs, but each PT appears only once within the same report. After linkage, each drug–PT combination was treated as an independent observation unit and included in the disproportionality analysis.11 Figure 1(A) illustrates the screening process for dupilumab‑related AE reports in the pediatric population from the FAERS database.

Adverse event signal detection

Disproportionality analyses were conducted using four established algorithms: ROR, PRR, BCPNN, and MGPS. ROR and PRR are frequentist approaches with favorable sensitivity; ROR effectively adjusts for reporting bias when AE counts are low, whereas PRR is considered relatively less affected by underreporting. The Bayesian methods, BCPNN and MGPS, offer advantages in signal specificity, stability, and false‑positive control. BCPNN efficiently integrates multisource data and supports cross‑validation, while MGPS is particularly effective at detecting signals for rare AEs.11 To ensure robust signal detection, a positive signal was defined only when all four algorithms satisfied their prespecified thresholds. PTs meeting these criteria were classified as positive; all others were considered negative. All algorithms were constructed using 2 × 2 contingency tables (Supplementary Table S1); corresponding formulas and signal criteria are detailed in Supplementary Table S2. To focus on the analysis of adverse drug reactions, this study excluded terms related to symptoms of the underlying disease, clinical procedures and therapeutic procedures, and medication errors (Supplementary Table S3).

Time‑to‑onset (TTO) analysis

This study defined the TTO of AEs as the interval from the initiation date of dupilumab to the date of AE occurrence. Before analysis, data cleaning was performed to exclude cases with missing drug or event dates, invalid date formats, or temporal inconsistencies (TTO < 0). Given that the FAERS database provides only dates without precise time points, cases with events occurring on the same day as drug initiation (TTO = 0) were handled using a differentiated approach: for descriptive statistics of TTO and Kaplan-Meier analysis, the original values were retained to faithfully reflect the distribution of reported latencies; however, because the Weibull model strictly requires positive time variables, TTO = 0 values were uniformly recoded as 1 d for modeling purposes. The distribution of TTO was summarized using the minimum, median, maximum, and interquartile range. The Kaplan-Meier method was employed to characterize the latency distribution based on complete sequential reports, and the log‑rank test was used to compare TTO distributions between groups. Weibull distributions were refitted using the corrected dataset, and based on the shape parameter β and its 95% confidence interval, AE time profiles were classified into three types: early failure, random failure, and wear-out failure patterns.12 All tests were two‑sided, with a P value < .05 considered statistically significant. Data management was performed using Microsoft Excel 2019, and all statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3).

Results

Adverse event and population characteristics

This analysis included data from the FAERS database spanning from 2017 to 2025. After deduplication to exclude duplicate reports from the same patient, a total of 44,593 unique pediatric cases were identified with dupilumab as the suspect drug. As summarized in Table 1, the sex distribution of patients was relatively balanced, with females accounting for 48.6% (21,681 cases) and males for 50.1% (22,321 cases). Adolescents aged 12–17 y constituted the largest age group, representing 45.3% (20,217 cases) of the cohort, while children aged 0–5 y and 6–11 y accounted for 23.0% (10,266 cases) and 31.6% (14,110 cases), respectively. The majority of reports were submitted by healthcare professionals, with physicians contributing 50.2% (22,374 cases) and other health professionals an additional 28.7% (12,799 cases). Adverse event outcomes were predominantly classified as “other,” accounting for 97.7% (43,588 cases). Among cases with documented outcomes, hospitalization was reported in 2.0% (891 cases), disability in 0.2% (70 cases), life-threatening events in 0.1% (23 cases), and death in 0% (19 cases). Reports originated predominantly from the United States (99.0%, 44,146 cases). Since the FDA approval of dupilumab in 2017, the volume of pediatric adverse event reports submitted to FAERS has demonstrated a consistent upward trend. Reporting remained relatively low initially but increased markedly from 2021 onward, reaching 12,046 cases in 2024 and rising further to 16,419 cases by 2025 (Figure 1(B)).

Safety signal detection

Among SOCs (Figure 1(C), Supplementary Table S4), skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders accounted for the highest number of reports (37,649 cases), followed by injury, poisoning and procedural complications (24,022 cases), general disorders and administration site conditions (21,375 cases), eye disorders (6,339 cases), and infections and infestations (4,563 cases). In terms of signal strength (Figure 1(D), Supplementary Table S4), skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders exhibited the highest ROR (ROR = 4.74), followed by eye disorders (ROR = 4.35) and social circumstances (ROR = 1.79). After excluding PTs in the categories of indication-related, disease manifestations, medication errors, drug-related issues, and clinical operations, a total of 93 positive preferred terms were identified (Supplementary Table S5). The most frequently reported PTs included rash (4,105 cases; ROR = 4.55, 95% CI: 4.38–4.72), injection site pain (3,582 cases; ROR = 8.59, 95% CI: 8.22–8.97), skin exfoliation (1,834 cases; ROR = 3.41, 95% CI: 3.23–3.59), injection site swelling (1,427 cases; ROR = 23.20, 95% CI: 21.20–25.39), and therapeutic response shortened (1,425 cases; ROR = 78.00, 95% CI: 67.49–90.14) (Table 2). Further analyses of the top 30 positive PTs identified via ROR, grouped by SOC, are shown in Figure 2. Rebound eczema (432 cases) exhibited the highest signal strength (ROR = 1,648.95, 95% CI: 529.72–5,132.98). Other noteworthy PTs included periorbital irritation (30 cases; ROR = 171.17, 95% CI: 40.91–716.26), eyelid skin dryness (121 cases; ROR = 81.28, 95% CI: 48.92–135.05), therapeutic response shortened (1,425 cases; ROR = 78.00, 95% CI: 67.49–90.14), and eyelid margin crusting (62 cases; ROR = 70.77, 95% CI: 36.29–138.02). Of note, several positive signals not currently documented in the dupilumab product labeling were also identified in this study, including vitiligo (33 cases; ROR = 10.46, 95% CI: 6.52–16.78), skin depigmentation (32 cases; ROR = 13.04, 95% CI: 7.85–21.66), and eye color change (13 cases; ROR = 7.06, 95% CI: 3.54–14.11) (Supplementary Table S5).

Specifically (Supplementary Table S6,S7), the vitiligo group (n = 33) had patients aged 2–17 y (mean: 9 y), while the skin dyspigmentation group (n = 32) had patients aged 1–17 y (mean: 10 y). The 6–12 y subgroup was predominant in the vitiligo group (18/33), whereas patients aged ≥ 12 y were most common in the dyspigmentation group (15/32). Gender distribution was generally balanced between the two groups. The vitiligo group showed a slight male predominance (17 males/14 females), while the dyspigmentation group had marginally more females (18 females/13 males). Atopic dermatitis was the leading indication for dupilumab use in both groups, accounting for nearly two-thirds of all cases. Other indications included asthma, eczema, neurodermatitis, psoriasis, and eosinophilic esophagitis. Most patients received dupilumab monotherapy, and only 5 cases had combined medication use. TTO data on adverse reactions were limited, with 52 cases lacking relevant records. Valid TTO data were available for 5 patients in the vitiligo group (median: 2 months, range: 4–412 d) and 5 patients in the dyspigmentation group. In the latter group, adverse reactions occurred on the treatment day in 3 cases, at 36 d in 1 case, and at 446 d in 1 case. All adverse outcomes were categorized as “other,” without death, hospitalization, or disability events. Beyond cutaneous dyspigmentation, common concomitant manifestations included rash, pruritus, xerosis, and erythema; inappropriate medication regimens were also documented in several cases.

Stratified analysis

Safety signals including, but not limited to rash, injection site pain, injection site swelling, and injection site irritation were identified in all age‑and‑sex subgroups among children receiving dupilumab. (Figure 3(A–D)). Sex-stratified analysis showed that in male pediatric patients, the strongest signal was rebound eczema (ROR = 763.21, 95% CI: 244.13–2386.02), followed by eyelid skin dryness (ROR = 126.38, 95% CI: 50.66–315.30) (Figure 3(A), Supplementary Table S8). In female pediatric patients, the AE with the highest signal strength was therapeutic response shortened (ROR = 89.09, 95% CI: 71.54–110.95), followed by eyelid skin dryness (ROR = 58.21, 95% CI: 31.39–107.92) (Figure 3(A), Supplementary Table S9).

The results of age-stratified analyses are shown in Figure 3(B–D) and Supplementary Tables S10–S12. In children aged 0–5 y, therapeutic response shortened displayed the strongest signal (ROR = 523.53, 95% CI: 327.84–836.01), with eyelid irritation ranking second (ROR = 79.06, 95% CI: 17.69–353.28). In children aged 6–11 y, eyelid margin crusting was the leading high-risk signal (ROR = 70.65, 95% CI: 16.24–307.29), followed by dry eye (ROR = 45.42, 95% CI: 32.87–62.75). In the 12–17-y-old subgroup, eyelid margin crusting exhibited the strongest signal (ROR = 110.21, 95% CI: 25.57–474.99), followed by periorbital irritation (ROR = 85.71, 95% CI: 19.48–377.14).

We further performed stratified analyses by indication (atopic dermatitis and asthma) among pediatric reports of dupilumab use. The results showed that in children with atopic dermatitis, the adverse event with the highest signal strength was therapeutic response decreased (ROR = 38.22, 95% CI: 5.37–271.75), followed by injection site swelling (ROR = 16.93, 95% CI: 4.22–67.88) (Figure 3(E), Supplementary Table S13). In children with asthma, the adverse event with the highest signal strength was rebound effect (ROR = 87.22, 95% CI: 11.92–638.37), followed by dry eye (ROR = 50.44, 95% CI: 12.16–209.34) (Figure 3(F), Supplementary Table S14).

Time-to-onset analysis

A total of 4,910 cases with complete incubation period data were included in the final analysis. The median time to AE onset was 14 d (95% CI: 11.5–16 d). Among the reported cases, 56.2% (2,759/4,910) of AEs occurred within 30 d of drug administration (Figure 4(A–C)). Stratified Kaplan–Meier analysis with log‑rank testing revealed a statistically significant difference in the distribution of incubation periods between serious and non‑serious AEs (P < .05), whereas no significant differences were observed by sex or age subgroup (Figure 4(D–F)). We further employed the Weibull distribution model to evaluate the temporal onset characteristics of the five most frequently reported PTs and the top five reported SOCs. At the PT level, rash (β = 0.45, 95%CI: 0.43–0.48), injection site pain (β = 0.41, 95%CI: 0.39–0.44), skin exfoliation (β = 0.47, 95%CI: 0.42–0.52), injection site swelling (β = 0.44, 95%CI: 0.40–0.48), and therapeutic response shortened (β = 0.48, 95%CI: 0.41–0.54) demonstrated early-failure profiles (Figure 4(G)). This indicates that these events were more likely to be reported earlier in the observation period; however, this finding should be interpreted as reflecting the observed temporal pattern of reported onset times derived from spontaneous reporting data, rather than as an inference of the true temporal risk profile of these adverse events. Similarly, the five most frequently reported SOCs exhibited the same decreasing conditional hazard pattern at the SOC level (Figure 4(H)).

Discussion

In this large-scale pharmacovigilance analysis, we characterized adverse-event reporting patterns associated with dupilumab in pediatric patients using the FAERS database. A total of 44,593 pediatric reports in which dupilumab was designated as the primary suspect drug were identified, and 93 PTs met the predefined signal criteria across all four disproportionality algorithms. The most frequently reported signals were primarily cutaneous and administration-site events, including rash, injection-site pain, skin exfoliation, and injection-site swelling. Ocular disorders also constituted a prominent signal category. In addition, several less frequently reported PTs, including vitiligo, skin depigmentation, and eye color change, generated positive disproportionality signals. Collectively, these findings extend the safety information derived from recent pediatric clinical trials and real-world studies of dupilumab.13–15 Importantly, signals detected by disproportionality analysis represent reporting imbalances and should not be interpreted as estimates of incidence, relative risk, or causal association.8,16

The substantial increase in pediatric reports after 2021 should be interpreted in the context of the progressive expansion of dupilumab use across pediatric age groups and indications. Dupilumab has increasingly been used in younger children with moderate-to-severe AD, following randomized clinical evidence supporting its efficacy and safety in children as young as 6 months.13,14 Long-term extension studies have subsequently demonstrated sustained clinical benefit and a generally consistent safety profile during prolonged treatment in this young population.15 These trial findings have been complemented by multicenter and real-world studies conducted in Italy, Spain, China, Germany, and other settings, which have documented effectiveness and treatment persistence across broader pediatric populations.3,17–22 Dupilumab has also become established as an add-on therapy for children with uncontrolled moderate-to-severe type 2 asthma. The phase 3 LIBERTY ASTHMA VOYAGE trial demonstrated reductions in severe asthma exacerbations and improvements in lung function and asthma control in children aged 6–11 y.23 Long-term follow-up in LIBERTY ASTHMA EXCURSION showed that the safety profile remained generally consistent during extended treatment.24 Subsequent analyses have further supported sustained improvement in lung-function parameters and efficacy across clinically relevant pediatric asthma subgroups.25,26 More recently, randomized evidence demonstrated the efficacy of dupilumab in children aged 1–11 y with eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE), further broadening pediatric exposure to the drug.27 Evidence from older children and adolescents with EoE similarly supports the effectiveness and tolerability of prolonged IL-4/IL-13 pathway blockade.28 These successive expansions in age eligibility, indication, and clinical utilization provide an important context for the marked increase in FAERS reports after 2021. Therefore, increasing report counts should not be interpreted as evidence of an increasing incidence of adverse reactions; they may instead reflect growth in the exposed population, longer cumulative treatment experience, increased clinician awareness, and stimulated reporting.

Cutaneous and administration-site events represented the most prominent findings of the current analysis. Rash, injection-site pain, skin exfoliation, and injection-site swelling were among the most frequently reported PTs and were observed across multiple demographic strata. Injection-site reactions and cutaneous complaints have also been reported in pediatric randomized trials and observational cohorts, although most studies have described an overall acceptable safety profile.3,13,20 In a large Spanish multicenter study of pediatric AD, for example, injection-site reactions were among the recorded adverse events, while overall drug survival remained high during prolonged follow-up.20 Similarly, multicenter real-world studies in children aged 6–11 y and younger children have generally demonstrated sustained effectiveness with relatively few serious treatment-related events.3 Recent prospective and registry-based evidence further supports the overall tolerability of dupilumab in routine pediatric practice. The PEDISTAD registry found favorable effectiveness and treatment persistence among children receiving dupilumab compared with conventional systemic therapies.29 A prospective UK-Irish registry analysis likewise provided comparative real-world evidence regarding the effectiveness and safety of dupilumab, ciclosporin, and methotrexate in patients with moderate-to-severe AD.30 Together, these findings provide an important clinical context for interpreting spontaneous reports, which preferentially capture adverse or unusual events rather than the overall experience of treated populations.

However, several treatment-course–related PTs identified in the present study, including rebound eczema, therapeutic response shortened, and therapeutic response decreased, warrant particularly cautious interpretation. Unlike PTs that directly reproduce the indication itself, these terms describe events reported during the treatment course and were therefore retained in the signal-detection analysis. The absence of detailed longitudinal disease-severity measurements, dosing history, adherence information, and treatment-interruption data in FAERS prevents differentiation between true secondary loss of effectiveness and background disease fluctuation. These signals should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating observations requiring confirmation in longitudinal datasets with serial measures of disease activity and drug exposure.

This concern is also relevant to our indication-stratified analyses. Different reporting patterns were observed between pediatric patients treated for AD and those treated for asthma; however, such differences should not be interpreted as evidence of indication-specific pharmacological susceptibility. The underlying disease phenotype and therapeutic environment differ substantially between these populations. Pediatric AD is a relapsing inflammatory skin disease frequently treated with topical corticosteroids, topical calcineurin inhibitors, antimicrobial agents, antihistamines, and, in selected patients, systemic immunomodulators.29,30 By contrast, children receiving dupilumab for asthma commonly remain on inhaled corticosteroid-containing controller treatment and other asthma therapies during biologic treatment.23,24 Concomitant medication use represents an additional unresolved source of confounding. Restricting the dataset to reports in which dupilumab was designated as the primary suspect drug increases the specificity of drug-event attribution but does not exclude contributions from other therapies. Real-world pediatric registry data demonstrate that patients with moderate-to-severe AD may receive multiple topical or systemic treatments during their disease course.29,30 Similarly, background controller medications remain an integral component of pediatric asthma treatment in dupilumab trials.23,24 Because concomitant medication information in FAERS is incomplete and heterogeneous, and because temporal relationships between individual co-medications and reported events cannot always be reconstructed reliably, adequate adjustment for these factors was not possible. Therefore, some detected signals may partly reflect background treatments, treatment changes, underlying disease, or interactions among these factors rather than an effect of dupilumab alone.

Ocular disorders represented another important reporting category in our study. Signals including dry eye, eyelid margin crusting, eyelid skin dryness, periorbital irritation, and other ocular symptoms were identified, with several ocular PTs particularly prominent in older pediatric strata. These findings are compatible with the growing literature on dupilumab-associated ocular surface disease (DAOSD) in pediatric patients with AD.31,32 In a pediatric Asian cohort, ophthalmologist-diagnosed DAOSD was observed among children and adolescents treated with dupilumab, with conjunctivitis, ocular redness, pruritus, and other ocular-surface abnormalities forming part of the clinical spectrum.31 More recently, a prospective long-term pediatric study from the BioDay registry confirmed that ocular surface disease may develop during dupilumab treatment and emphasized the importance of recognizing ocular symptoms during routine care.32 Broader systematic evidence has similarly established ocular surface disorders as an important adverse-event category associated with dupilumab treatment for AD.33 At the same time, ocular signals are especially susceptible to confounding by indication. AD itself can involve the eyelids and periocular skin and may coexist with allergic conjunctivitis, blepharitis, keratoconjunctivitis, dry-eye symptoms, and other ocular-surface abnormalities. The high prevalence of concurrent eyelid or head-and-neck eczema among pediatric patients who develop DAOSD illustrates the difficulty of separating underlying disease manifestations from treatment-emergent events.31 Accordingly, stronger ocular disproportionality observed in particular age groups in our analysis should not be interpreted as demonstrating a corresponding age-dependent increase in biological susceptibility. Current expert guidance emphasizes clinical awareness, symptom recognition, and appropriate ophthalmologic referral for significant ocular disease rather than universal assumptions of severe ocular toxicity.34 The available pediatric data also do not establish that DAOSD is simply an age-dependent phenomenon.32 Therefore, although our findings support vigilance for new or worsening ocular symptoms, the present FAERS analysis alone is insufficient to recommend routine baseline ophthalmologic screening for every pediatric patient receiving dupilumab. Mechanistic studies suggest that the ocular complications observed during IL-4/IL-13 blockade may involve complex changes in local ocular-surface immunity rather than a simple direct toxic effect. Alterations in inflammatory pathway balance at the ocular surface have been demonstrated in patients with dupilumab-associated ocular disease.35 However, such mechanistic findings derive from specialized clinical and molecular studies and cannot establish the mechanism underlying individual FAERS reports. The ocular signals identified here should therefore remain clinically contextualized pharmacovigilance findings.

Several low-frequency signals that are not prominent features of the established dupilumab safety profile were also identified, notably vitiligo, skin depigmentation, and eye color change. Vitiligo was reported in 33 cases and skin depigmentation in 32 cases, whereas eye color change was reported in only 13 cases. Although these PTs satisfied the predefined disproportionality criteria, their small case numbers substantially limit precision, particularly when estimates are associated with wide confidence intervals. In addition, usable time-to-onset information was available for only a small proportion of pigmentation-related reports. These findings should therefore be described as potential pharmacovigilance signals requiring independent validation, rather than established adverse drug reactions. The vitiligo and depigmentation findings nevertheless warrant further investigation because an increasing number of published reports have described new-onset or worsening vitiligo during dupilumab therapy. Ren et al. reported a case series of vitiligo occurring during dupilumab treatment.36 A subsequent review of biologic-associated vitiligo also identified cases arising in the setting of dupilumab exposure.37 Additional individual reports have documented similar clinical observations.38 Several biological hypotheses have been proposed to explain paradoxical immune phenomena during targeted type 2 cytokine blockade.7 Inhibition of IL-4 and IL-13 signaling could theoretically alter the balance between type 2 and type 1 inflammatory responses and thereby modify immune pathways involved in melanocyte injury. Published discussions of dupilumab-associated vitiligo have proposed Th1/interferon-related mechanisms as one possible explanation.36,37 However, these mechanistic hypotheses remain incompletely established and should not be inferred directly from FAERS data.

The stratified analyses further demonstrated heterogeneity in reporting patterns according to age and sex. Rebound eczema showed a particularly high disproportionality estimate among male reports, whereas therapeutic response shortened was prominent among female reports and younger children. Ocular signals including eyelid margin crusting, dry eye, and periorbital irritation were prominent in some older pediatric strata. Recent FAERS research directly comparing dupilumab-associated reporting patterns between children and adults has likewise demonstrated age-related heterogeneity in detected signals.16 These subgroup differences, however, should not be regarded as evidence of biological effect modification. Indication distribution, baseline disease severity, treatment duration, concomitant medications, healthcare utilization, and reporting propensity may differ by age and sex and thereby influence subgroup-specific ROR estimates. Furthermore, very large ROR values generated from small numbers of reports are statistically unstable. Point estimates should consequently be interpreted alongside the number of reports and confidence intervals rather than ranked solely according to ROR magnitude. This caution is consistent with recent large-scale FAERS analyses of dupilumab, which emphasize the signal-generating rather than causal nature of disproportionality methods.8,16

Our time-to-onset analysis provides additional descriptive information regarding the temporal distribution of reported events. Among the 4,910 cases with complete temporal information, the median reported time to onset was 14 d, and 56.2% of events occurred within the first 30 d after dupilumab initiation. Weibull analyses classified the five most frequently reported PTs and SOCs as early-failure patterns. These observations indicate that reports with evaluable dates were concentrated relatively early after treatment initiation. Nevertheless, an early-failure Weibull pattern derived from spontaneous reports should not be interpreted as demonstrating a true decline in biological hazard over time. Patients may receive more intensive clinical surveillance shortly after treatment initiation, early adverse experiences may be more likely to trigger reports, and patients experiencing early intolerance may discontinue treatment. In addition, treatment-start dates and event dates are incompletely recorded in FAERS, introducing substantial selection into time-to-onset analyses. Recent FAERS analyses of dupilumab similarly emphasize that disproportionality and temporal analyses are valuable primarily for signal detection and characterization rather than estimation of time-dependent clinical risk.8,16

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, FAERS is a spontaneous-reporting database and is inherently affected by underreporting, stimulated reporting, notoriety bias, selective reporting, incomplete information, and potential duplicate submissions. Consequently, disproportionality estimates quantify reporting imbalance rather than incidence, prevalence, absolute risk, or relative clinical risk.8,16 Second, reliable exposure denominators are unavailable, and the substantial increase in pediatric reports over time cannot distinguish increased drug utilization from changes in adverse-event frequency. Third, this study only analyzed reports in which dupilumab was identified as the primary suspect, and excluding secondary‑suspect reports may impair the ability to detect its contributory risk signals.31,33 Fourth, concomitant medications, disease severity, dupilumab dose, treatment adherence, interruptions or discontinuations, previous therapies, and relevant medical history were incompletely reported and could not be adequately controlled. Fifth, approximately 99% of included reports originated from the United States, which substantially limits generalizability to populations with different ethnic backgrounds, healthcare systems, prescribing practices, and pharmacovigilance structures. Sixth, complete temporal data were available for only a subset of reports, and very small numbers of evaluable cases were available for several potential novel signals, limiting meaningful temporal characterization. Finally, several large disproportionality estimates were derived from sparse cells and were accompanied by wide confidence intervals, indicating substantial statistical uncertainty.

Conclusion

In this FAERS analysis of pediatric dupilumab reports, cutaneous, administration-site, and ocular events were the predominant signals, mostly occurring early post-initiation – consistent with known safety data. Although the positive signals not listed in the prescribing information (e.g., vitiligo, skin depigmentation, and eye color change) met the disproportionality screening criteria, their conclusions remain to be independently validated due to the small number of cases and high risk of bias. Given FAERS limitations (no denominators, reporting bias, confounding, incomplete data), causality or incidence cannot be established. These findings are hypothesis-generating; prospective studies with comparators and detailed data are needed.
