Design of a wearable compact pulsed electromagnetic field (PEMF) device for orthodontic treatments
Authors: M D Geyikoglu, A K Ozen, I Ceylan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article explains the design and experimental results of a compact, wearable pulsed electromagnetic field (PEMF) generator for orthodontic treatments. The design schematic features provide for adjustable pulse frequency, adjustable pulse duration, and adjustable magnetic field output. A cascade-connected integrated circuit structure is preferred to adjust both pulse frequency and pulse exposure time. The cascade connection structure provides for a more compact device. The adjustment of pulse frequency and duration is based on the relationship between the resistor and capacitor. Transistor driver circuits and switches are used for the magnetic field generator. The proposed design has dimensions of 14 × 12 × 0.8 mm³ and is fabricated with SMD materials. In the experiments, an adjustable operating frequency between 1 and 100 Hz, and an adjustable pulse duration between 10 and 30 min., and an adjustable magnetic field output between 0.1 and 2 mT are obtained. According to the results, it has been determined that there is a strong harmony and symmetry between the simulation and experimental results. Additionally, electromagnetic and thermal simulation studies have been performed in the CST Microwave Studio Suite program for an intraoral use scenario. A realistic 3D phantom of the upper and lower jaw has been created for the simulations. The H-field, current density, Specific Absorption Rate (SAR), and temperature distributions are presented for the device at 1, 50, and 100 Hz frequency values. The SAR and temperature results have been compatible with the World Health Organization (WHO) limit values. The proposed prototype demonstrates significant potential for shortening treatment time in orthodontic applications due to its adjustable properties and operational stability.Keywords: Orthodontic, Pulsed electromagnetic field, Wearable deviceSubject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

Orthodontic treatment aims to improve the shape and function of a patient’s oral and facial structures. Orthodontic tooth movement is a dynamic bone remodeling process involving both anabolic and catabolic responses to mechanical loading. One of the major disadvantages of current orthodontic treatment is its long duration and the need for repeated appointments. The average treatment time with fixed appliances is approximately 20 months; in some cases, it can extend up to 36 months1. Prolonged orthodontic treatment increases the risk of potential side effects, such as gingivitis, gingival hypertrophy, and gum recession. In addition, it may contribute to increased bone loss. Orthodontic appliances facilitate bacterial accumulation on tooth surfaces and alter the microbial environment, leading to periodontal inflammation. Furthermore, several studies have shown that periodontopathogenic bacterial colonization increases during orthodontic treatment. Consequently, there is a growing trend toward shortening the duration of orthodontic treatment to minimize the adverse effects associated with prolonged treatment2. Therefore, the development and investigation of methods to accelerate orthodontic tooth movement are of great importance. Currently, various surgical and non-surgical approaches are used to accelerate tooth movement. However, some clinical studies have not demonstrated the superiority of surgical methods over non-surgical ones. As a result, non-surgical approaches, including biological and mechanical stimulation techniques, are more widely accepted by patients. Various non-invasive methods to enhance tooth movement have been investigated, including pulsed and static magnetic fields3, surgical manipulation of the alveolar bone4, and electrical currents5.

PEMF is a non-invasive treatment technique that generates pulsed electromagnetic fields within biological tissues. Fundamentally, PEMF devices consist of a wire coil through which current flows, producing a pulsed magnetic field6. PEMFs induce an electric field within biological tissues, particularly in bone, as a consequence of the time-varying nature of the applied magnetic field. This induced, or secondary, electric field is not externally applied but arises intrinsically within the medium through electromagnetic induction mechanisms governed by Maxwell’s equations. In this context, the secondary electric field is defined as the electric field generated within the tissue due to temporal variations in magnetic flux, rather than by direct electrical stimulation. The magnitude and spatial distribution of this induced field are strongly dependent on both the characteristics of the applied pulsed magnetic field—such as frequency, amplitude, and waveform—and the inherent electrical properties of the biological tissue, including conductivity and permittivity. These parameters collectively determine the extent of field penetration and localization within the tissue microenvironment. Importantly, the secondary electric field plays a pivotal role in modulating cellular behavior by influencing ion transport mechanisms, altering membrane polarization, and activating intracellular signaling pathways. Through these interactions, PEMF-induced electric fields contribute significantly to the therapeutic outcomes observed in various biomedical applications, including bone regeneration and orthodontic treatments6. PEMF devices are widely used in medical applications such as transcranial magnetic stimulation7, orthodontics8, and pain management9. Over the past decade, numerous histological studies have investigated the effects of PEMF treatment on histochemical pathways directly related to orthodontic tooth movement. Following PEMF treatment, increased osteoblastic and osteoclastic activity has been observed in both in vivo and in vitro studies10.

The capacity of electromagnetic fields to modulate biological processes in medical and dental applications has been extensively explored in the literature. A comparative summary of representative orthodontic studies is provided in Table 1. Evidence from studies by Stark and Sinclair11, Maulana et al.4, and Bhad Patil et al.12 indicates that PEMF exposure can enhance the rate of orthodontic tooth movement and promote bone formation relative to control conditions. Notably, Bhad Patil et al.12 reported that this enhancement may reach up to 41%. These effects are generally attributed to increased osteoblastic activity and the regulation of osteoclast function, including the suppression of osteoclastogenesis. However, findings regarding the analgesic effects of PEMF remain inconsistent. Menini et al.13 observed no significant reduction in pain following minimally invasive dental implant procedures, whereas Jung et al.14 reported a significant decrease in pain after orthodontic wire adjustments. This inconsistency suggests that the impact of PEMF on pain modulation may depend on treatment conditions, stimulation parameters, and patient-specific factors. Beyond orthodontic applications, Lin et al.15 demonstrated that PEMF exposure can restore bone mineral density in animal models with low bone mass, highlighting its potential in the treatment of metabolic bone disorders. Nevertheless, the clinical translation of these findings requires further validation through well-controlled human studies. A major limitation across the existing literature is the lack of standardization in electromagnetic exposure parameters, including frequency, waveform, field intensity, and duration16. Reported magnetic field intensities vary substantially, ranging from 0.5 mT to 1 T, while frequency selection often lacks a clear theoretical basis. Such variability complicates direct comparison between studies and limits the reproducibility of reported outcomes. Additionally, practical limitations related to device design persist. The relatively large size of current PEMF systems reduces their usability and may negatively affect patient compliance. In particular, externally attached devices, such as cheek-mounted patches, can lead to aesthetic concerns and physical discomfort, especially in everyday social contexts. Consequently, electromagnetic PEMF stimulation systems have taken their place in academic standards as a safe, non-invasive technology with no thermal effects that optimizes tissue healing across a wide spectrum.

This study aimed to develop a simple and wearable PEMF device with adjustable features (pulse frequency, pulse duration, and magnetic field strength) that prioritize patient comfort and short treatment duration, influencing treatment success rates. For this purpose, a novel design is developed for a compact, wearable device with adjustable pulse frequency (1–100 Hz), adjustable pulse duration (10–30 min), and adjustable magnetic field output (0.1–2.1 mT). The use scenario of the PEMF device, designed to accelerate tooth movement in orthodontic treatment, is given in Fig. 1. According to the usage scenario, the device is designed to be used by being placed inside the mouth. Therefore, it has a compact and wearable form.

The PEMF device use scenario for orthodontic treatment31.

The proposed circuit architecture is an electromagnetic system composed of three primary functional blocks designed for controlled PEMF generation. The control layer, comprising Stage-1 and Stage-2, regulates the temporal characteristics of the current flowing through the inductor by modulating the signal frequency and pulse width (on-time) using NE555 precision timer integrated circuits (U1 and U2). In Stage 3, which constitutes the power switching unit, the M1 MOSFET is driven by these control signals, enabling rapid energy discharge through the L2 inductor and thereby generating the desired pulsed magnetic field. According to Ampère’s law, the transient current (I) passing through the coil windings produces a magnetic flux density (B) at the coil center, which is determined by the coil geometry and the number of turns. The system is designed to generate a low-intensity magnetic field of approximately 2 mT, which can be achieved with relatively low current levels (~ 300 mA). Compared to high-field applications operating in the Tesla range (e.g., electroporation or magnetic permeabilization), this low-power operation enhances thermal stability and extends component lifespan. In accordance with established international standards and safety guidelines, the operational parameters of the device—including frequency, magnetic field intensity, and power supply characteristics—remain within ranges considered safe for biological tissues, thereby minimizing potential adverse health effects. A review of the existing literature indicates that, despite numerous PEMF-based systems, there is a lack of compact device designs specifically optimized for intraoral use. This highlights a significant gap in current technological solutions and underscores the novelty of the proposed system in terms of miniaturization and application-specific design.

The design of pemf device

Electromagnetic-based therapeutic devices directly influence treatment efficacy and clinical outcomes through key parameters such as magnetic field intensity, frequency, and exposure duration16. Therefore, in bioelectromagnetic applications, the effectiveness of PEMF is commonly evaluated using the window effect approach, which considers the combined influence of frequency, field intensity, exposure time, and waveform characteristics16. To achieve optimal performance, the proposed PEMF device is designed based on three fundamental components. Reconfigurable magnetic field (Stage-3)

This last stage is the output stage, which converts low-power control signals into high-power magnetic pulses. The pulsed magnetic field generator is constructed as a pulsed driver circuit using transistors (Q1: BC817, Q2: 2PA1576), a MOSFET (M1: 2N7002), and an inductor. The series-connected transistors provide switching and feedback oscillation, respectively. The L2 component is the main electromagnetic coil that generates the magnetic field. The stage-3 converts the control signals into high-energy magnetic outputs. The M1 MOSFET switching element in this stage conducts with the trigger signal from Stage 2, causing the energy to be discharged suddenly through the L2 inductor. The R2 resistor in the circuit provides current-sensing feedback, both monitoring system safety and allowing monitoring of the peak current (Ipeak) flowing through the inductor.

The performance results of pemf device

The proposed device is implemented on a double-sided printed circuit board (PCB) using a 0.8 mm thick FR-4 substrate (Fig. 3-a). Since the recommended device will be used by inserting it into the mouth, it must be lightweight and of a compact size. The overall dimensions of the circuit are 14 × 12 × 0.8 mm³. Surface-mount device (SMD) components are utilized during the assembly process to achieve compactness. The system is powered by a 3 V lithium coin cell battery, and the total weight of the device, including the battery, is approximately 3 g. To evaluate the performance of the system, multiple prototypes incorporating three distinct sub-design configurations were fabricated. For frequency variation, prototypes were developed at ten discrete frequencies ranging from 1 to 100 Hz (10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, and 100 Hz). For duty cycle variation, three exposure durations (10 min, 20 min, and 30 min) were implemented. In addition, to assess magnetic field intensity effects, prototypes were designed to operate at 0.1 mT, 0.2 mT, 0.5 mT, 1 mT, and 2 mT. Prototypes were fabricated, and their performance was systematically evaluated in terms of device efficiency. To validate the operational characteristics and target outputs of the proposed PEMF system, a dedicated measurement setup was established. This setup enables the simultaneous analysis of electrical signals and magnetic field distributions, ensuring accurate characterization of system behavior. All measurements were conducted at room temperature in an environment free from significant electromagnetic interference sources (e.g., transformers and high-power motors). The experimental setup and instrumentation used for characterization are illustrated in Fig. 3.

The view of the measurement setup: a) Battery and fabricated antenna, b) Gaussmeter, c) Oscilloscope, d) Power Supply, and e) Measurement probe.

The magnetic field measurements were conducted using a calibrated high-sensitivity Tesla/Gaussmeter within a controlled experimental setup to ensure accuracy and repeatability. At the center of the measurement system, the L2 inductor in the Stage-3 layer serves as the primary magnetic field source. A high-sensitivity tesla meter was positioned at the geometric center of the coil, perpendicular to the magnetic flux lines, to ensure accurate field detection. The probe was mounted on a rigid non-metallic holder to eliminate mechanical disturbances and undesired electromagnetic perturbations during data acquisition. A fixed probe-to-coil distance of 10 cm was maintained throughout all experiments, and precise alignment with the dominant magnetic flux direction was ensured. Given the pulsed nature of the magnetic field, the measurement system was equipped with a peak-hold function to reliably capture the maximum field amplitude occurring within the microsecond time scale. In this study, a MODEL Dx-160 Gauss/tesla meter was employed. Before measurements, the device was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s specifications. To improve measurement reliability, multiple acquisitions were performed at each spatial point and averaged to reduce random noise effects. Simultaneously, the electrical performance of the system was monitored using an oscilloscope connected across the R2 current-sensing resistor, enabling real-time tracking of current variations. This allows systematic analysis of the influence of pulse width and frequency parameters defined by Stage-1 and Stage-2 on the resulting magnetic field distribution (Fig. 4). During the characterization phase, the measured current across R2 was correlated with theoretical magnetic field estimations, while instantaneous magnetic flux variations (dB/dt) were monitored using the tesla meter positioned at the coil center. Temporal characteristics of the pulsed magnetic field were recorded using a data acquisition system with a sampling rate of 1 kHz, sufficient to resolve the transient behavior of the PEMF waveform. The recorded signals were processed to extract both peak and effective magnetic field values, providing a comprehensive representation of the spatial and temporal field behavior. All experiments were conducted in an electromagnetic interference (EMI)-free environment, and ferromagnetic materials that could distort the magnetic field distribution were strictly avoided. This integrated measurement approach enables validation of the theoretically derived current–magnetic field relationship against experimental data and ensures system repeatability, particularly for subsequent biological applications. Although the experimental measurements provide reliable field characterization under controlled conditions, it is acknowledged that minor deviations may arise due to sensor positioning tolerances and ambient electromagnetic noise, which are considered in the interpretation of the results.

The experimental findings indicate that the proposed device exhibits high operational stability, with a pulse frequency response of 98%, a duty cycle response of 95%, and stable magnetic field generation performance. To quantitatively evaluate the agreement between simulation and experimental data, error metrics including mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) were calculated (Fig. 6). The analysis shows that the deviation between the two datasets remains within acceptable limits, supporting the validity of the proposed model. Furthermore, the relative error distribution suggests that the observed differences are mainly due to fabrication tolerances, uncertainties in material properties, and measurement limitations. Overall, both MAE and RMSE results confirm the strong correlation between simulation and experimental outcomes, thereby validating the accuracy and reliability of the proposed system. During experimental implementation, real-time current monitoring via the R2 sensing resistor serves as a critical validation layer for ensuring the accuracy and repeatability of biological exposure conditions. The measured current confirms that the magnetic field is consistently generated with each pulse, thereby maintaining a stable dose applied to the biological sample. Organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection (ICNIRP) have established comprehensive guidelines and safety standards governing the use of electromagnetic fields in relation to public health24,25. Ensuring that devices emitting electromagnetic fields comply with these standards is essential, particularly with respect to their operational parameters, including frequency, field intensity, and exposure duration. Compliance assessment is typically conducted through a combination of in silico, in vitro, in vivo, and in situ studies, each providing complementary insights under different experimental conditions. Among these, in silico approaches are widely employed for electromagnetic and thermal analysis using simplified or anatomically realistic phantom models. While simplified models enable rapid analysis with reduced computational cost, realistic models provide more accurate and reliable results by incorporating multiple physiological parameters such as blood perfusion, saliva, and tissue heterogeneity. In this context, CST Studio Suite offers an extensive phantom library for simulating the interaction between electromagnetic fields and biological tissues. Given that biological tissues behave as dispersive media, their electromagnetic properties can be effectively modeled using the dual Debye formulation over the relevant frequency range26. The propagation and distribution of electromagnetic fields within tissues are governed by Maxwell’s equations27, while Ampère’s law plays a critical role in determining the induced current density within biological media.

To evaluate the potential impact of the proposed wearable device on human health and to assess its compliance with established safety limits, comprehensive electromagnetic and thermal analyses were conducted using a realistic anatomical phantom. The simulations were performed using CST Microwave Studio Suite on a high-performance workstation equipped with an Intel® Core™ i9-10900KF processor (3.7 GHz) and 128 GB of RAM. A three-dimensional model of the upper and lower jaw was constructed based on voxel data obtained from the built-in phantom library. In accordance with the intended application scenario, the device is designed to be positioned on the upper palate; therefore, the upper jaw structure was specifically extracted and utilized within the simulation environment (Fig. 8).

At low frequencies (1–100 Hz), electromagnetic interactions are more accurately described using a quasi-static field approximation rather than classical wave propagation models28. In this regime, the wavelength is significantly larger than the characteristic dimensions of biological tissues, making induced electric field effects a more appropriate descriptor of tissue interaction mechanisms24. The time-varying magnetic field generates an induced electric field within the tissue, leading to the formation of current density distributions29. Accordingly, the evaluation of biological effects should not rely solely on the SAR, which is inherently a mass-averaged parameter. While SAR remains a relevant metric at higher frequencies, quantities such as local power density and induced current density provide a more direct representation of energy deposition and potential biophysical interactions in low-frequency regimes24,30. In this context, a combined analysis based on magnetic field intensity (H-field), induced current density, and SAR offers a more comprehensive and physically consistent framework than approaches relying exclusively on SAR28,29. Therefore, H-field, current density, and SAR analyses were performed to evaluate the proposed device, which operates in the 1–100 Hz frequency range with variable exposure durations, in terms of both biomedical safety and design performance. Representative simulation results at 1 Hz, 50 Hz, and 100 Hz are presented in Fig. 9. In addition, thermal analysis was conducted by incorporating electromagnetic power absorption (SAR or volumetric power density) as a heat source term in the bioheat equation, enabling the assessment of temperature distribution within biological tissues. The resulting temperature profiles are also illustrated in Fig. 9.

Evaluation of electromagnetic and thermal simulations31.

The simulation results provide insight into the electrical and thermal responses of biological tissues under electromagnetic exposure. Analysis of the data indicates that increasing the frequency from 1 Hz to 100 Hz does not result in a significant variation in the H-field distribution. The magnetic field is observed to be relatively homogeneous throughout the jaw structure, with localized concentrations in specific regions. The current density (A/m²) results demonstrate that the induced currents follow a consistent trend with increasing frequency; however, their magnitudes remain relatively low, on the order of 1500 A/m². SAR distributions show that energy deposition within the tissue becomes more pronounced at higher frequencies, particularly at 50 Hz and 100 Hz. Nevertheless, according to the corresponding color scale, SAR values remain below the 1 W/kg threshold, indicating that the applied electromagnetic field operates within accepted biological safety limits. Thermal analysis further reveals that the impact of electromagnetic exposure on tissue temperature is minimal. Across all evaluated frequencies (1 Hz, 50 Hz, and 100 Hz), the maximum temperature increase is approximately 0.5 °C. This temperature rise is primarily localized at the outer regions of the dental arch and at specific points within the structure. Importantly, a temperature increase of 0.5 °C falls within the physiological tolerance range of biological tissues, suggesting a low risk of thermally induced tissue damage under the investigated operating conditions.

The time-dependent effects of an electromagnetic field applied at a frequency of 100 Hz and a magnetic flux density of 2 mT on dental structures are illustrated in Fig. 10. The spatial distribution of the magnetic field during a 30-minute exposure period, along with the corresponding temperature variations within the tissue, was systematically evaluated. The magnetic field distribution results indicate a concentrated yet spatially extended field pattern across the dental arch and surrounding tissues, with the highest intensity observed in the vicinity of the device placement. Overall, the field exhibits a relatively symmetric distribution within the oral cavity. Observations at different exposure durations (5, 15, and 30 min) reveal that the magnetic field pattern remains stable over time, indicating consistent field generation throughout the exposure period. The initial tissue temperature was set to 36.5 °C, and measurements were recorded at the 5th, 15th, and 30th minutes. After 30 min of continuous exposure, the temperature increased slightly to 36.7 °C. Considering the thermal tolerance of biological tissues, a temperature rise of 0.2 °C remains well within physiological limits, indicating no significant risk in terms of dental safety standards. This minimal increase, observed even after prolonged exposure, demonstrates that the applied magnetic field does not induce significant thermal stress within the tissue.

Conclusion

In this study, a wearable, compact, and adjustable pulsed magnetic field generator was designed, simulated, and experimentally validated. The results demonstrate that a magnetic field generator with tunable frequency, pulse duration, and waveform characteristics can achieve highly accurate and stable output performance. Owing to its adjustable operating parameters and miniaturized structure, the proposed device represents a promising platform for the development of personalized treatment strategies. To further evaluate its biomedical applicability, comprehensive in silico analyses were conducted using a realistic anatomical phantom to investigate both electromagnetic and thermal effects on biological tissues. The findings indicate that frequency variations within the 1–100 Hz range do not induce significant thermal effects in dental structures. Induced current density and SAR distributions remain well-controlled, while temperature increases stay below critical safety thresholds. Notably, under maximum operating conditions (100 Hz, 2 mT), the magnetic field distribution maintains a thermally safe profile across the dental tissues. Even under prolonged exposure (30 min), the observed temperature rise is limited to approximately 0.2 °C, which is well within physiological tolerance limits. This outcome provides strong evidence supporting the biocompatibility and safety of the proposed electromagnetic approach. Furthermore, the results confirm that the applied fields do not lead to energy deposition levels capable of compromising tissue integrity. Overall, the proposed system combines portability, practicality, and controllability, making it a strong candidate for integration into personalized orthodontic treatment applications. In the future, in vivo and in vitro studies will be carried out to verify the biological effectiveness of the proposed device.
