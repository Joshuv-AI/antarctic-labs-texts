Achievement of real-world adapted DORIS remission targets among patients with systemic lupus erythematosus initiating belimumab in the USA: A retrospective, observational, cohort study
Authors: Aarat M Patel, Renee L Gennarelli, Temitope Bello, Ali Bonakdar, Karen Worley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRemission is a key treatment goal associated with improved patient outcomes in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), often measured by Definition of Remission In SLE (DORIS) criteria. In a post hoc analysis of clinical trial data, belimumab, a B-cell modulator targeting the central immunopathogenic pathway in SLE, plus standard therapy, improved DORIS remission rates versus placebo plus standard therapy; however, US real-world remission rates remain limited. Measuring DORIS criteria in real-world clinical practice is challenging because required variables, particularly the SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) component, are often unavailable or incompletely recorded. Therefore, we evaluated an adapted DORIS definition for real-world datasets and examined predictors of remission among US patients initiating belimumab.MethodsThis retrospective observational cohort study analysed data for patients with SLE initiating belimumab from the OM1 PremiOM™ SLE dataset, comprising electronic health records/healthcare claims data (2013–2024) for patients with SLE. The primary outcome was probability of achieving adapted DORIS remission at 28, 48, and 52 weeks post-treatment initiation, defined as clinician-recorded SLEDAI (crSLEDAI) or estimated SLEDAI (eSLEDAI) = 0, Physician Global Assessment (PGA) <2 on a 0–10 numerical rating scale, and prednisone-equivalent dose ≤5 mg/day. Kaplan–Meier estimates provided remission probabilities and logistic regression identified predictors of remission.ResultsMost patients (N = 398) were White, female, from the Southern region of the USA, and had commercial insurance. The probability of achieving adapted DORIS remission reached 28.3% (95% confidence interval: 19.8–35.9) by 52 weeks post-belimumab initiation. Older patients (≥50 years), non-White individuals, and patients with cr/eSLEDAI ≤5 demonstrated slightly higher remission probabilities than their counterparts; however, estimates differed by censoring approach, and confidence bounds overlapped. Adjusted multivariable analysis confirmed increasing age, non-White race, and cr/eSLEDAI ≤5 as significant predictors of remission.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the application of an adapted DORIS definition to large real-world observational datasets. The probability of adapted DORIS remission in US patients initiating belimumab was similar to rates reported in observational studies outside the USA, supporting the real-world effectiveness of belimumab. The increased likelihood of remission in patients with cr/eSLEDAI ≤5 supports the benefits of initiating belimumab treatment early in the disease course.Keywords: real-world data, remission, systemic lupus erythematosus

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) disease activity assessment in clinical practice remains complex due to the presentation of diverse clinical manifestations affecting multiple organ systems.1,2 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guidelines state that SLE disease activity should be monitored regularly using a validated instrument; however, use in clinical practice is limited by time constraints, training requirements and the need for laboratory measures.3–5

A key treatment goal in SLE is achievement of remission, which is associated with improved patient outcomes. 2 Remission is defined in the 2025 ACR Guideline for the Treatment of Systemic Lupus Erythematosus as “symptoms and signs of disease activity are significantly reduced or absent for an extended period of time” noting that “specific definitions vary” and the Definition Of Remission In SLE (DORIS) is given as one example measure of remission. 4 DORIS criteria were developed to provide alignment on defining remission in patients with SLE.6,7 DORIS is defined as: (1) a clinical Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) score of 0, irrespective of serology; (2) a Physician Global Assessment (PGA) score of <0.5 (0–3 visual analogue scale [VAS]); (3) the patient may receive treatment with antimalarials, low-dose glucocorticoids (prednisone ≤5 mg/day) and/or stable immunosuppressives including biologics. 7 DORIS can be calculated in the clinical setting only when all three components are captured in the patient’s medical record. In the absence of any component, practitioners can utilise estimates of remission derived in part from these and other measures of disease improvement.

Belimumab is a B-cell modulator that targets the central immunopathogenic pathway in SLE by selectively inhibiting B-lymphocyte Stimulator (BLyS) and reducing the autoreactive B cells that drive disease activity.8,9 In a post hoc analysis of belimumab clinical trials, adding belimumab to standard therapy increased the number of patients with SLE in DORIS remission compared with those who received placebo with standard therapy. 10 This was observed even though these trials were conducted before the DORIS criteria were developed. 10 These trials were also conducted without a formal forced steroid taper recommendation, which may have limited the number of patients able to achieve DORIS remission. 11 In a further analysis, exclusion of the glucocorticoid element of DORIS resulted in remission rates that were higher than recorded with the standard DORIS. 11 In the real-world setting, DORIS achievement at 52 weeks in belimumab-treated patients with SLE has ranged from 11.6% to 36.3% across multiple studies and countries, although no data are available in a US population.12–14

Due to the design of DORIS for use in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) it can be difficult to measure all the required variables in real-world clinical practice, particularly the SLEDAI component; thus, it is challenging to apply the DORIS criteria to large observational datasets.5,15 Therefore, this study used adapted DORIS criteria to assess real-world remission. 16 Adapted DORIS criteria included SLEDAI scores estimated using a validated algorithm 16 (eSLEDAI, in addition to clinician-recorded SLEDAI [crSLEDAI] scores, where available) and PGA assessments on a numerical rating scale (NRS), rather than a VAS. Using the adapted DORIS criteria, the study also sought to evaluate predictors of remission among US patients with SLE receiving belimumab in a real-world setting.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective, observational, real-world cohort study among patients in the USA with SLE initiating treatment with belimumab (GSK Study 222161). Data were derived from OM1 PremiOM™ SLE (OM1, Boston, MA, USA), a multisource real-world dataset with linked electronic medical record and healthcare claims data for over 64,000 patients between 2013 and 2024.16,17 The study period (1 January 2013–31 May 2024) comprised the identification period in which patients could be included in the study (1 January 2014–31 October 2023), the index date, and baseline and follow-up periods (Figure 1). The index date was the date of first receipt of intravenous or subcutaneous belimumab (medical or pharmacy encounter) during the identification period. The baseline period was defined as the 12 months prior to the index date and the follow-up period was the period from the index date to the end of follow-up.

No direct patient contact or primary collection of individual patient data occurred; study results are presented as aggregate analyses with no patient identification. Therefore, informed consent, ethics committee and institutional review board approval were not required.

Patients

Patients were included in the OM1 PremiOM™ SLE database if they had: (1) ≥1 encounter with a rheumatologist; (2) ≥2 diagnosis codes for SLE ≥30 days apart from a rheumatologist visit, or ≥1 inpatient visit with an SLE diagnosis, or ≥2 outpatient records with an SLE diagnosis ≥30 days apart (within 1 year; regardless of physician specialisation), or ≥1 outpatient record with an SLE diagnosis and prescription/fill for a specified SLE-related medication (and no non-SLE condition for which these medications may be prescribed); and (3) ≥2 disease activity or patient-reported outcome measures ≥30 days apart, which included fatigue score, Multidimensional Health Assessment Questionnaire, patient-reported global status VAS, patient-reported pain VAS, PGA score, crSLEDAI score, or ≥2 clinical notes from a rheumatologist ≥30 days apart.

The crSLEDAI is recorded in the patient’s electronic health record by the healthcare provider on the 0–105 scale. The SLEDAI criteria used are not recorded; however, SLEDAI-2K is considered widely adopted. 18 Given the nature of these data, the SLEDAI is unlikely to include serological results; however, it is possible that clinicians considered anti-double stranded DNA and complement measurements, but laboratory results were not required for crSLEDAI.

Further inclusion criteria specific to this study included: ≥1 pharmacy or medical encounter for belimumab during the identification period; ≥18 years at belimumab encounter; ≥1 crSLEDAI or eSLEDAI score and ≥1 PGA score during follow-up; and activity within the healthcare system for ≥12 months prior to the index and ≥28 weeks from the index date. A cr/eSLEDAI or PGA score was not required pre-index/at baseline. Patients were excluded if they had: received belimumab during the baseline period prior to the index date; only one medical or pharmacy encounter for belimumab during the follow-up period; a diagnosis of drug-induced lupus during baseline period (≥1 International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision, M32.0 diagnosis code); or received rituximab or anifrolumab during the baseline period.

Adapted DORIS criteria

The clinical DORIS criteria were not directly applicable to the data available from this electronic health record and healthcare claims data source because crSLEDAI was not always available, PGA was only reported on an NRS, and glucocorticoid doses were reported without knowledge of actual dose consumption. Where crSLEDAI was not available, the dataset included eSLEDAI scores derived by OM1 using a validated machine learning algorithm that inferred the eSLEDAI score category based on the clinical notes from physician visits. 16 A PGA score of <2 on the 10-point NRS has been previously shown as the best predictor of physician-defined remission, and is approximately equivalent to 0.5 on the 0–3 VAS used for the clinical DORIS remission definition.6,19 Therefore, adapted DORIS was established and defined as: (1) no disease activity (cr/eSLEDAI score = 0); (2) a PGA score <2 on an NRS of 0–10; and (3) average daily prednisone equivalent dose of oral glucocorticoids ≤5 mg/day as calculated by prescriptions written. Cr/eSLEDAI was reported as a categorical measure classified as: no disease activity: cr/eSLEDAI score = 0; low disease activity: cr/eSLEDAI score = 1–5; moderate disease activity: cr/eSLEDAI score = 6–10; high disease activity: cr/eSLEDAI score ≥11. 16

Objectives

The primary objective was to determine the probability of patients with SLE achieving adapted DORIS by 28, 48, and 52 weeks post-belimumab initiation. Secondary objectives included describing SLEDAI scores before initiating belimumab and probability of achieving a cr/eSLEDAI score = 0 and PGA scores <2 by 28, 48, and 52 weeks after initiation. Timepoints evaluated were selected for consistency with previous belimumab post hoc analyses. 10 The exploratory objective was to develop a multivariable model to identify patient demographic and clinical characteristics associated with achieving adapted DORIS remission post-belimumab initiation.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were exploratory and descriptive with no hypothesis testing; study size was determined by application of the specified inclusion and exclusion criteria. For the primary and secondary objectives, all analyses were descriptive and stratified by race (White vs non-White), age (<50 vs ≥50 years), and baseline cr/eSLEDAI score (SLEDAI score ≤5 vs >5).

The primary objective was reported as the probability of achieving adapted DORIS after initiating belimumab using Kaplan–Meier estimates and described using point estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The secondary objectives included descriptive baseline and follow-up cr/eSLEDAI and PGA scores, defined as the score documented closest to and on or prior to each timepoint of interest, and the probability of achieving no disease activity (per cr/eSLEDAI score) and achieving a PGA score <2 after initiating belimumab using Kaplan–Meier estimates and described using point estimates and 95% CI, where all scores were assessed for remission qualification. No imputation for missing values was performed for the secondary objectives.

To address some of the potential bias from missing disease activity data in medical records, we used two different censoring approaches to manage uncertainty in remission status. In the main analysis, patients were censored using the last available score approach, defined as the earlier of (1) the latest cr/eSLEDAI or PGA score in the follow-up period, or (2) the end of the follow-up period. This method assumes that remission status after the last observed score is unknowable and makes no assumptions about disease activity after the most recently observed score (i.e. it does not assume that absence of scores indicates absence of remission as there may have been insufficient clinical notes to infer an estimate).

As a sensitivity analysis, patients were censored at end of follow-up, defined as the earlier of (1) 52 weeks post-index, (2) last activity date, or (3) date of data cutoff. This approach assumed that patients not meeting remission criteria per available data did not achieve remission, and represents a more conservative estimate of remission, where time without scores reported is considered time not in remission.

For the exploratory objective, characteristics associated with achieving remission were identified using a multivariable logistic regression model with adapted DORIS achievement by 52 weeks as the dependent variable. Covariates were selected a priori, identifying demographic and clinical characteristics that have been reported or hypothesised to influence SLE disease activity and treatment outcomes. Additionally, a follow-up duration variable was included to adjust for variability in follow-up time across patients. An alpha level of 0.05 was used to identify statistical significance. Odds ratios, 95% CI and p-values were calculated.

Results

Patient population

Of 7589 patients initiating belimumab in the identification period, 398 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis (Supplemental Figure S1). Most patients were female, White, from the Southern region of the USA (southern USA), and had commercial insurance (Table 1). The number of patients included were generally evenly distributed across 2017–2022 (year of belimumab initiation) with an overall increase over time, apart from the last year that was not a full year of index. 74% of patients had ≥1 cr/eSLEDAI score available during the 12-month baseline period, with those scores reported at any time from 1 day to 12 months pre-index date. During the baseline period, 9% of patients had a cr/eSLEDAI score of 0 (indicating no disease activity at that time), 23% had a low disease activity score (cr/eSLEDAI = 1−5), 22% had a moderate disease activity score (cr/eSLEDAI = 6−10), and 19% had a high disease activity score (cr/eSLEDAI ≥11). Almost half (48.9%) of patients had ≥1 crSLEDAI score during follow-up, with the remainder having exclusively eSLEDAI scores.

Real-world SLE remission per adapted DORIS

Patients had a 7.7% (95% CI: 4.7%, 10.7%) probability of achieving adapted DORIS by 28 weeks post-belimumab initiation, which increased to 28.3% (95% CI: 19.8%, 35.9%) by 52 weeks using the last available score censoring approach (Figure 2). The probability of achieving real-world adapted DORIS by 48 weeks was 25.9% (95% CI: 18.8%, 32.4%).

Probability of achieving real-world SLE remission post-belimumab initiation, per adapted DORIS, as assessed by the last available score censoring approach. CI, confidence interval; DORIS, Definition Of Remission In SLE; SLE, systemic lupus erythematosus.

The sensitivity analysis showed that the probability of achieving adapted DORIS was lower with the end of follow-up censoring approach than the last available score approach: 5.0% (95% CI: 2.9%, 7.1%) by 28 weeks and 13.8% (95% CI: 10.3%, 17.2%) by 52 weeks (Supplemental Figure S2); the probability by 48 weeks was 13.0% (95% CI: 9.5%, 16.3%).

Unadjusted probability estimates showed that patients aged ≥50 years, non-White patients and those with cr/eSLEDAI score ≤5 at baseline had a slightly higher probability of achieving adapted DORIS than those aged <50 years, White patients, and those with a cr/eSLEDAI score >5 at baseline (univariate analysis) (Figure 3 and Supplemental Figure S3). SLEDAI and PGA components

Patients had a mean number of 1.9 cr/eSLEDAI scores reported at baseline and a mean number of 2.9 scores reported during follow-up (52 weeks). Among those patients with a known cr/eSLEDAI score at each specific timepoint of interest, the observed proportion of patients with no disease activity (cr/eSLEDAI score = 0) increased from 13% at baseline to 21% at 52 weeks post-belimumab initiation.

Results from the Kaplan–Meier analysis show the probability of achieving a cr/eSLEDAI score of 0 was 21.3% (95% CI: 16.8%, 25.6%) by 28 weeks of belimumab initiation, increasing to 45.8% (95% CI: 36.6%, 53.7%) by 52 weeks using the last available score censoring approach (Figure 4); by 48 weeks, the probability was 40.7% (95% CI: 33.3%, 47.3%). A similar pattern was observed with the end of follow-up censoring approach (which represents a more conservative estimate of remission) albeit with lower probability values (28 weeks: 18.6% [95% CI: 14.7%, 22.3%]; 52 weeks: 29.5% [95% CI: 24.8%, 33.9%]; Supplemental Figure S4).

Probability of achieving no disease activity (cr/eSLEDAI score = 0) and a PGA score of <2 on the NRS post-belimumab initiation, as assessed by the last available score censoring approach. CI, confidence interval; crSLEDAI, clinician-recorded SLEDAI; eSLEDAI, estimated SLEDAI; NRS, numerical rating scale; PGA, Physician Global Assessment; SLEDAI, Systematic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index.

Patients had a mean number of 2.3 and 3.8 PGA scores reported during the baseline and follow-up periods, respectively. At index, the observed mean (standard deviation) PGA score value was 3.2 (2.9), which decreased to 2.8 (2.9) at 52 weeks. Results from the Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that the probability of achieving a PGA <2 on the NRS by 28 weeks was 45.9% (95% CI: 40.4%, 51.0%), which increased to 68.5% (95% CI: 58.2%, 76.3%) by 52 weeks using the last available score censoring approach (Figure 4); the probability by 48 weeks was 60.6% (54.2%, 66.1%). The end of follow-up censoring approach again revealed a similar pattern but with lower probability values (28 weeks: 43.7% [95% CI: 38.6%, 48.4%]; 52 weeks: 56.3% [95% CI: 51.1%, 60.9%]; Supplemental Figure S4).

The unadjusted, stratified probability estimates revealed that patients aged ≥50 years, non-White patients and those with cr/eSLEDAI score ≤5 at baseline were slightly more likely to achieve a cr/eSLEDAI score of 0 (no disease activity) or a PGA <2 on the NRS by 52 weeks than their counterparts, although confidence bounds for the strata overlapped (Supplemental Figure S5), and probabilities differed between censoring approaches (Supplemental Figure S5 and S6).

Predictors of adapted DORIS achievement

In the multivariable model, after adjusting for all other factors, increasing age, non-White race, and cr/eSLEDAI score ≤5 at baseline were significantly associated with achieving real-world SLE remission by 52 weeks, when compared with younger age, White race, or cr/eSLEDAI score >5, respectively (Figure 5).

Discussion

This real-world study used adapted DORIS criteria to evaluate remission rates based on a large observational dataset (electronic health records and healthcare claims data) of patients with SLE in the USA. To reflect clinical practice data availability, the adapted DORIS criteria included: the use of crSLEDAI and eSLEDAI scores; reporting PGA on an NRS instead of a VAS; and assessment of glucocorticoid doses based on prescriptions written and filled rather than from case report forms used in clinical trials. Our study demonstrated a real-world probability of achieving remission, per adapted DORIS, after initiating belimumab of 7.7% by 28 weeks, and 28.3% by 52 weeks. The probability of achieving real-world remission by Week 52 with belimumab in this study was generally consistent with other real-world studies (11.6%–36.3%), allowing for differences in study populations, study design, timescales and DORIS remission criteria, demonstrating the feasibility of this approach.12–14

The probability of achieving real-world SLE remission by 52 weeks was higher using adapted DORIS in our study compared with data from a post hoc pooled analysis of belimumab clinical trials (8%); 10 however, some discrepancy may be expected due to differences in patient populations, study designs and definitions of remission. In addition, the clinical trial data were collected at an earlier timepoint (2006–2019) than the real-world data (2013–2024) and during these times clinical guidelines and remission definitions evolved with a potential impact on outcome; for example, clinical guidelines aimed at minimising glucocorticoid use were introduced in 2019 (target dose ≤7.5 mg/day) and further updated in 2023 (target dose ≤5 mg/day),2,20,21 while DORIS remission criteria, including the aim to minimise glucocorticoid use, were established in 2017 and refined in 2021.6,7 A reanalysis of the clinical trial data excluding the glucocorticoid component from the composite DORIS criteria showed a higher remission rate with belimumab than when the glucocorticoid component was included (18% vs 8%), highlighting the impact that the lack of a glucocorticoid taper in these trials had on the ability to achieve remission.10,11 Another reason for the discrepancy between clinical trial and real-world data may relate to assessment tools. In the present study, the probability of achieving the PGA component of the adapted DORIS criteria was notably higher than the SLEDAI component, with the difference more marked than observed for the clinical trial data. 10 Since PGA is a simpler tool than SLEDAI, it may be used more often in real-world settings, evidenced in this study by the higher mean number of PGA scores, compared with SLEDAI.

This study also revealed predictors of adapted DORIS achievement, including increasing age, non-White patients and those with a cr/eSLEDAI score ≤5 at baseline. The association between lower baseline disease activity and higher odds of remission is consistent with previous findings, 22 and may be expected among patients experiencing a low level of disease activity. However, it should be noted that within the group of patients with a cr/eSLEDAI score ≤5 at baseline it would be expected that there was some level of disease activity for belimumab to be initiated, and although it is possible that these patients were taking a high level of steroids we did not stratify disease activity by steroid dose. The publication by Yang et al., also identified older age at diagnosis as a predictor of remission 22 in line with studies by Steiman et al., Tselios et al. and Medina-Quinones et al.23–25 potentially supporting the association between increasing age and remission that we observed. A possible explanation for the improved outcomes seen in older patients in our study may be that older age has been associated with higher treatment adherence among patients with SLE;26,27 however, adherence was not measured in our study. Furthermore, the clinical significance of age as a predictor (rather than age at diagnosis) could warrant further exploration as, although statistically significant, it was based on a narrow confidence interval with a proximity to unity and reflects the impact of 1 year of age. Regarding non-White race as a predictor of remission, previous studies support the efficacy of belimumab in non-White populations;10,28 however, it has also been demonstrated that African American patients with SLE take longer to achieve remission and a Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS), and fewer African American patients achieve LLDAS for >50% of the observation time (LLDAS-50), compared with an overall SLE population.29–31 Also, the above-mentioned post hoc pooled analysis of belimumab clinical trials showed that patients with SLE of Asian, Indigenous American and White race experienced a significant benefit of belimumab versus placebo as measured by LLDAS achievement, though for DORIS achievement a statistically significant association was only seen for Asian patients. 10 Thus, the association between non-White race and increased likelihood of remission warrants further investigation to ascertain the factors underlying this observation, including possible socioeconomic factors associated with the data set. The study comprised primarily commercially insured, White patients and those residing in Southern USA, which may not represent diverse populations or those without insurance coverage. In addition, SLEDAI and PGA scores may not be routinely collected in all clinical practice settings, highlighting variations in standards of care. Additionally, the study was not designed to evaluate the impact of treatment adherence on remission so the effects of continuous treatment on remission, and possible reasons behind the observed associations between patient characteristics and remission, could not be explored.

Strengths of this analysis include that it was the first to investigate real-world remission with belimumab in the USA and that it contained both electronic health record and healthcare claims data, providing stronger evidence than either source alone. The approach taken to select the covariates for the regression analysis also ensured that the model was informed by established clinical knowledge, accounted for potential differences in observation periods, and was constrained to variables available within our dataset. However, unmeasured confounders not captured in the dataset may influence early remission and represent a limitation of the analysis. Other limitations included that the study relied on a large amount of estimated data, remission was biased towards the use of PGA scores, and the missing data may not be completely random, which may be a source of bias. Additionally, given that the cr/eSLEDAI scores did not require serological results, it is possible that the scores did not capture serologically active but clinically quiescent disease, potentially underestimating total disease activity compared with the full SLEDAI, inclusive of serology. These limitations, taken with the small sample size, may restrict the generalisability of the study findings to some extent.

Where crSLEDAI data were not available, eSLEDAI scores were generated by OM1 using a machine learning algorithm, based on the text in clinical notes; however the clinical parameters within the clinical notes had to meet defined thresholds. 16 It should also be noted that if a patient was not included due to not meeting the inclusion criteria of at least one PGA and cr/eSLEDAI score during the follow-up period, it is possible that these data were not missing at random. This may mean that patients excluded due to missing cr/eSLEDAI and PGA scores might be inherently more or less likely to achieve remission and that missing scores might be an indicator of disease status. Although an internal validation step was not possible with the cohorts evaluated, a similar approach was reported by Alves et al. in which a machine learning model was used to estimate SLEDAI scores from real-world data sources. 16 Given that regular recording of disease activity is a recommendation in the 2025 ACR SLE guidelines, 4 this may improve in clinical practice, reducing this limitation in future studies.

To address the bias and inherent limitations resulting from missing data, two censoring approaches were used. The last available score censoring approach treated remission status as unknowable after the last observed score, where observation time without scores present was removed from the denominator to avoid classifying those patients who might be doing well, and therefore not being measured on their disease status, from being classified as failing to achieve remission. The probability of achieving adapted DORIS was lower using the end of follow-up censoring approach, which assumed that the absence of remission-qualifying cr/eSLEDAI or PGA scores indicated failure to achieve remission. However, the end of follow-up censoring approach was likely overly conservative, as patients reporting well controlled disease may be less likely to have their disease activity measured than those with poorly controlled disease, as the physician may consider additional assessments unnecessary given the positive patient feedback. Alternatively, if some of the patients without additional scores had poorly controlled disease, the last available score approach could be underestimating those who failed to achieve remission, but were not considered as such due to lack of observed scores. Notwithstanding these differences, while the two censoring approaches resulted in differing real-world SLE remission estimates, trends towards a greater likelihood of real-world SLE remission per adapted DORIS over time were consistently observed with both methods; the true estimate is likely to be between the reported values for each approach. Additionally, the methodology for calculating adapted DORIS has also not been validated, and a risk of misclassification bias exists, and sustained remission was also not evaluated. There is not yet clear consensus on how DORIS should be applied to real-world data, 32 so remission status may have been underestimated.

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the real-world effect of belimumab on achievement of remission in the USA, and the viability of using adapted DORIS from a record-based algorithm to ascertain real-world SLE remission from large observational datasets. The observation that real-world remission was observed even in patients with lower levels of disease activity supports the benefits of initiating belimumab treatment early in the disease course.
