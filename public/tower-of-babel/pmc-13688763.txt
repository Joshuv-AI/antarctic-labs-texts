Background

Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide, and most cases are attributable to uterine atony [1]. Active management of the third stage of labor, including routine administration of a uterotonic agent, is recommended to reduce PPH after both vaginal and cesarean delivery, and oxytocin is the established first-line prophylactic agent [2, 3].

Carbetocin is a long-acting, heat-stable analogue of oxytocin that produces sustained uterine contraction after a single dose and has a safety profile comparable to oxytocin [4, 5]. Its efficacy for PPH prophylaxis is well established: a large randomized noninferiority trial supported heat-stable carbetocin as an alternative to oxytocin after vaginal birth [6], an earlier Cochrane review reported that carbetocin reduces blood loss and the need for additional uterotonics, particularly at cesarean delivery [7], although the most recent Cochrane network meta-analysis found that, compared with oxytocin, carbetocin makes little or no difference to severe (≥ 1000 mL) postpartum haemorrhage while it may reduce the use of additional uterotonics [8], and a large propensity-matched cohort reported a lower risk of severe hemorrhage at cesarean delivery with carbetocin [9].

Despite this evidence, real-world effectiveness data are limited in settings where carbetocin is not reimbursed and is therefore uncommon in routine practice. In Taiwan, carbetocin is not covered by the National Health Insurance and is paid for by patients; earlier local experience was confined to small single-center series [10]. In addition, blood loss in routine practice is frequently documented as a clinician’s visual estimate rather than measured, which complicates outcome ascertainment in observational data. How carbetocin performs under these real-world conditions, and which outcomes can be reliably assessed from routinely collected records, are not well characterized.

We therefore conducted an 8-year retrospective cohort study at a Taiwanese institution where carbetocin use rose from negligible to common over the study period. Because allocation was determined by patient self-payment rather than clinical criteria, and because recorded blood loss is visually estimated, we examined the association—rather than the efficacy—of carbetocin with an objective laboratory outcome, the peri-operative change in hemoglobin at cesarean delivery, and interpreted all findings as associations.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of deliveries at Shuang Ho Hospital, Taipei Medical University, between 2017 and 2025. Records were retrieved from the institutional electronic medical record system and classified by delivery mode (vaginal or cesarean) and by institutional risk category (low- or high-risk). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shuang Ho Hospital under two protocols: the original study (N201912119) and the expanded 8-year cohort (N202512016); the requirement for informed consent was waived given the retrospective design.

Participants

All deliveries with a documented carbetocin status during the study period were included. Records with conditions expected to alter blood loss independent of uterotonic choice (e.g., preterm or terminated pregnancy, placenta previa or abruption, prolonged third stage, fourth-degree laceration) were handled as pre-specified; high-risk deliveries were retained and analyzed as a separate stratum rather than excluded.

Treatment allocation

Carbetocin (100 µg, single intravenous dose) was not reimbursed by Taiwan’s National Health Insurance and was self-paid (approximately US$100). Receipt therefore reflected patient choice after counseling on admission, rather than clinical allocation. Patients not receiving carbetocin received the institutional standard regimen: oxytocin with the addition of misoprostol (800 µg) and methylergonovine (0.2 mg) per protocol for vaginal delivery, and oxytocin alone for cesarean delivery. Allocation was non-randomized, and carbetocin uptake rose from 0% (2017) to approximately 65% (2023–2025); this is addressed analytically and in the limitations.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the peri-operative change in hemoglobin (post-delivery minus pre-delivery, g/dL) for cesarean delivery, chosen as an objective laboratory measure. Pre-delivery hemoglobin was the closest value within 72 h before through the delivery day, and post-delivery hemoglobin the closest value within 0–72 h after delivery. Secondary outcomes were the incidence of PPH (estimated blood loss ≥ 500 mL for vaginal and ≥ 1000 mL for cesarean delivery) and a severe hemoglobin decline (≥ 2 g/dL). Blood transfusion was extracted from the record as a binary indicator for the whole cohort, but audit showed that it undercounts true transfusion; it was therefore too incompletely captured to serve as an outcome and is reported only as a lower-bound, exploratory measure (Additional file 1).

At our institution, blood loss after both vaginal and cesarean delivery is documented as a clinician’s visual estimate rather than measured gravimetrically, and visual estimation of blood loss is known to be imprecise. Recorded blood loss at cesarean delivery additionally includes suctioned amniotic fluid. For these reasons we did not use recorded estimated blood loss as a continuous measure and selected an objective laboratory outcome (peri-operative hemoglobin change) as the primary endpoint. For vaginal delivery, audit of the recorded blood-loss field further showed value heaping that differed systematically by treatment arm and was inconsistent with measurement (Results); we therefore report only the binary PPH endpoint for vaginal delivery. The reliability of the record fields underlying these decisions — the plausibility of hemoglobin values, the contrast between the manually recorded blood-loss field and the instrument-generated laboratory value, the internal consistency and completeness of the hemoglobin outcome, and the incomplete capture of transfusion — is documented in Additional file 1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as median (interquartile range) and compared with the Mann–Whitney U test; categorical variables with the chi-square or Fisher exact test. For the primary cesarean hemoglobin outcome, multivariable linear regression adjusted for maternal age, BMI, parity, neonatal birth weight, and high-risk status; calendar year was added as a categorical term to account for the secular trend in carbetocin uptake. The primary model used complete cases; a propensity score for carbetocin receipt was estimated on the same covariates and applied as inverse-probability weighting in a sensitivity analysis, and effects were examined separately in low- and high-risk cesarean strata. To quantify robustness to unmeasured confounding we computed E-values for the adjusted difference and its confidence limit (VanderWeele and Ding). To address missingness of post-delivery hemoglobin we tested whether availability was random (comparing arms and modelling the probability of a recorded value on the observed covariates and exposure), performed multiple imputation by chained equations (m = 20) as a missing-at-random sensitivity analysis, and conducted delta-adjusted pattern-mixture (tipping-point) analyses for not-missing-at-random scenarios in both directions (shifting unrecorded carbetocin-arm values toward larger declines, and unrecorded control-arm values toward smaller declines). To address the alternative possibility that post-delivery blood sampling practice itself differed between arms, we additionally examined whether hemoglobin availability tracked clinical severity, using the recorded transfusion flag as an imperfect severity marker, and whether any such association differed by arm (exposure × transfusion interaction); because the flag undercounts transfusion, these tests are supportive rather than definitive. All sensitivity analyses are summarized in Additional file 2 (Table S1). Analyses were observational and exploratory; no multiplicity adjustment was performed, and estimates are interpreted as associations. Analyses used Python with statsmodels and SciPy; two-sided p < 0.05 was considered significant. A large language model (Claude, Anthropic) was used as an assistive tool in two delimited roles: drafting and debugging portions of the Python analysis code, and language editing. It was not used to generate, select, or interpret data, or to draw statistical or clinical conclusions. All analyses were independently verified by the authors: every reported statistic was re-derived from the source-data extraction, the primary and sensitivity models were re-run and checked against hand calculations and standard references, and all figures and tables were reconciled against the underlying data. The authors had full access to the data and take full responsibility for the accuracy and integrity of all analyses and of the manuscript; the study’s design, interpretation, and conclusions are the authors’ own. The analysis code is available for editorial review.

Results

Cohort

Of 6,471 captured deliveries, 6,266 had a documented carbetocin status (vaginal 4,154; cesarean 2,112). Carbetocin was administered in 41.7% of vaginal and 56.8% of cesarean deliveries, with uptake increasing across the study period. Baseline characteristics by arm within two eras are shown in Table 1. Within each era, maternal age, BMI, and pre-delivery hemoglobin were comparable between arms, whereas parity, neonatal birth weight, and the proportion of cesarean deliveries differed; high-risk proportions were similar. These residual imbalances motivated multivariable and calendar-year adjustment.

Primary outcome: cesarean peri-operative hemoglobin change

Among cesarean deliveries with paired peri-operative hemoglobin (carbetocin n = 734; no carbetocin n = 484), the median hemoglobin change was − 0.60 g/dL (IQR − 1.30 to + 0.10) with carbetocin versus − 1.30 g/dL (IQR − 2.00 to − 0.80) without (Mann–Whitney p ≈ 6 × 10⁻³⁶; Cliff’s δ=+0.42) (Fig. 1A). Pre-delivery hemoglobin was identical between arms (median 11.7 g/dL), so the difference was not attributable to baseline anemia. The association was stable across models (Table 2): the adjusted mean difference was + 0.85 g/dL (95% CI 0.72 to 0.97) with covariate adjustment and + 0.79 g/dL (95% CI 0.65 to 0.94) with calendar year added. Estimates were consistent in low-risk (+ 0.84 g/dL, 95% CI 0.65 to 1.03) and high-risk (+ 0.76 g/dL, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.98) cesarean strata, and propensity-score weighting gave concordant results. The E-value for the adjusted difference was 3.2 (2.8 for the confidence limit closest to the null), meaning an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both carbetocin receipt and hemoglobin change by a risk ratio of about 3, beyond the measured covariates, to fully explain the association.

A severe hemoglobin decline (≥ 2 g/dL) was recorded in 11.2% of carbetocin versus 25.4% of no-carbetocin cesarean deliveries (Fig. 1B). Because post-delivery hemoglobin was available for 61% of carbetocin versus 53% of no-carbetocin cesareans, this binary endpoint is sensitive to missing-data assumptions and is reported as supportive rather than confirmatory; the adjusted continuous difference was robust, whereas the most adversarial imputation could attenuate the binary comparison. Availability of post-delivery hemoglobin differed marginally by arm (61% carbetocin vs. 53% control; Fisher p = 2.6 × 10⁻⁴); however, this difference was largely attributable to calendar time: recording completeness rose over the study period, and after adjustment for calendar year exposure was no longer an independent predictor of availability (p = 0.92). Availability was instead predicted by calendar year, parity, and BMI, and the data were not missing completely at random (global likelihood-ratio test χ²=146, p < 0.001). Under multiple imputation (m = 20, fixed random seed) the pooled adjusted difference was + 0.75 g/dL (95% CI 0.62 to 0.88), close to the complete-case estimate; in delta-adjusted pattern-mixture analyses the association was nullified only under substantial departures from the missing-at-random prediction, in either direction: when unrecorded carbetocin-arm declines were assumed to be about 2.0 g/dL larger, or when unrecorded control-arm declines were assumed to be about 2.0 g/dL smaller.

Blood transfusion, extracted as a binary flag for the whole cohort, was recorded in 1.4% of all deliveries and in 30 of 1,201 carbetocin (2.50%) versus 29 of 911 no-carbetocin cesarean deliveries (3.18%; crude odds ratio 0.78, p = 0.35; fully adjusted odds ratio 0.88, 95% CI 0.45 to 1.73). The flag is a lower bound: all nine patients with a hemoglobin fall of ≥ 4 g/dL, and 71% of those with a post-delivery hemoglobin < 7 g/dL, carried no transfusion record, combinations that are clinically improbable and indicate incomplete capture. Transfusion is therefore reported as exploratory and directional only and is not used to support any treatment claim. Despite the undercounting, the flag rose monotonically with the severity of hemoglobin decline (1.4% at a fall of 0–1 g/dL, 2.0% at 1–2 g/dL, and 5.9% at ≥ 2 g/dL), supporting the clinical interpretability of the severe-decline endpoint. Using the same flag as an imperfect severity marker, hemoglobin availability was not predicted by transfusion (odds ratio 1.14, p = 0.76) and this association did not differ significantly by arm (exposure × transfusion interaction odds ratio 0.37, p = 0.11), although the undercounting limits the sensitivity of these tests. Full detail is given in Additional file 1.

Cesarean estimated blood loss

Recorded cesarean estimated blood loss was lower with carbetocin (median 650 vs. 750 mL; p ≈ 0.001), but by institutional convention this volume includes suctioned amniotic fluid and cannot isolate blood; PPH defined as ≥ 1000 mL did not differ (24.7% vs. 28.5%; p = 0.11). This measure is reported descriptively only.

Vaginal delivery

The recorded vaginal blood-loss field, a clinician’s visual estimate at our institution, could not be interpreted as measured blood loss: approximately 60% of carbetocin cases were recorded as exactly 30 or 50 mL—below the expected loss of a normal vaginal delivery—versus a clustering at 150–300 mL without carbetocin, and within every year from 2019 onward the carbetocin median was fixed at 50 mL while the no-carbetocin median remained 150–200 mL (Fig. 2). The between-arm difference also narrowed toward higher, clinically meaningful thresholds (Fig. 3). We therefore report only the binary endpoint: PPH (≥ 500 mL) occurred in 3.2% of carbetocin versus 4.2% of no-carbetocin vaginal deliveries (year-adjusted odds ratio 0.68, 95% CI 0.46 to 1.01; p = 0.057). In addition, the vaginal comparator was a combined regimen (oxytocin plus misoprostol and methylergonovine) rather than oxytocin alone, so any vaginal contrast would compare carbetocin against triple uterotonic therapy; this further undermines interpretation of the vaginal blood-loss field and reinforces our decision not to analyse it as a continuous outcome.

Discussion

In this 8-year single-center cohort, carbetocin given at cesarean delivery was associated with a smaller peri-operative decline in hemoglobin than the institutional standard, with a fully adjusted difference of approximately + 0.79 g/dL that was stable across risk strata, calendar year, and propensity weighting. Because pre-delivery hemoglobin was identical between groups, the difference is unlikely to reflect baseline anemia. We frame this as an association, not a causal effect.

Comparison with existing evidence

The direction of our finding is consistent with the randomized and observational literature. A propensity-matched cohort of over 14,000 cesarean deliveries reported a lower risk of severe PPH with carbetocin than oxytocin (2.1% vs. 3.3%; odds ratio 0.62, 95% CI 0.48 to 0.79) [9]. A 2025 meta-analysis of randomized trials in low-risk cesarean delivery found less need for additional uterotonics, less blood transfusion (odds ratio 0.57), and a smaller post-operative hemoglobin drop with carbetocin [11].

Two calibrations against that evidence are important and we state them plainly. First, the pooled randomized estimate for the hemoglobin drop is small (mean difference about − 0.08 g/dL) [11], whereas our observed adjusted difference is an order of magnitude larger. The most parsimonious explanation is not a larger true effect but residual confounding from non-randomized, self-paid allocation, which inflates the apparent benefit. Consistent with this, the E-value (about 3.2) is well within the confounding plausibly generated by a self-paid, patient-elected exposure that marks socioeconomic status and health literacy, so the + 0.79 g/dL is best read as an upper bound rather than a causal effect. Second, both the randomized meta-analysis (PPH odds ratio 1.08, not significant) [11] and our data (vaginal PPH odds ratio 0.68, not significant) show no clear reduction in the incidence of PPH as a categorical event. Our results are therefore best read as directionally concordant with high-quality evidence on continuous blood-loss surrogates, while not demonstrating a reduction in PPH events.

A second, non-exclusive explanation for the size of the hemoglobin difference deserves explicit consideration: that it partly reflects how post-delivery hemoglobin came to be recorded rather than how much blood was lost. If sampling were performed preferentially when bleeding was suspected, and more so in one arm, the recorded values in that arm would be selectively enriched for larger declines and a spurious difference could be manufactured. Two lines of evidence argue against this as the main driver. First, the arm difference in availability was largely explained by calendar time, and exposure no longer predicted availability once calendar year was taken into account (p = 0.92), so sampling does not appear to have been driven by the exposure itself. Second, pattern-mixture analyses bounded the possibility quantitatively: unrecorded values would have to depart from the missing-at-random prediction by roughly 2 g/dL, in either direction, before the association vanished; against a median control decline of 1.30 g/dL, the direction implied by this hypothesis would require the unmeasured control patients to have had essentially no decline at all. Tests using the recorded transfusion flag as a severity marker were concordant — availability was not predicted by transfusion, and the association did not differ by arm — but the flag undercounts transfusion, so these tests are supportive rather than definitive and we do not rest the argument on them. We cannot exclude some contribution from recording practice, and the more general possibility that measurement processes rather than biology generate apparent treatment effects in real-world data warrants attention; the available evidence indicates it does not explain our finding, but this uncertainty reinforces our central message that the hemoglobin difference should be read as an upper bound and not as a demonstrated clinical benefit.

What this study adds

Randomized efficacy of carbetocin at cesarean delivery is already established, so the contribution here is not discovery but external validity in an under-represented context: an East-Asian setting where carbetocin is not reimbursed, is paid for by patients, and where uptake rose from essentially none in 2017 to roughly two-thirds of deliveries by 2023–2025. Documenting an association consistent with trial evidence under these real-world conditions is relevant to implementation and funding discussions, while the self-paid design simultaneously illustrates why such real-world signals must be interpreted with care.

Vaginal delivery

We deliberately do not report a continuous vaginal blood-loss effect. On audit, the recorded vaginal blood-loss field—a visual estimate at our institution—was heaped at implausibly low values in a manner tied to treatment status rather than to measurement, and the between-group difference narrowed toward clinically meaningful thresholds. We therefore restricted the vaginal analysis to the binary PPH endpoint, which showed a small, non-significant association. This conservative choice avoids overstating an artifactual finding.

Cost considerations

Carbetocin is self-paid in Taiwan at approximately US$100 per dose. A formal cost-effectiveness analysis reported carbetocin to be economically favorable for elective cesarean delivery but less clearly so for vaginal delivery [12], consistent with the cesarean focus of the defensible signal here. We did not perform an economic analysis and make no cost-effectiveness claim.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include an 8-year, multi-physician cohort, an objective laboratory primary outcome, and adjustment for measured confounders, calendar year, and propensity score. Several limitations bound the interpretation. First and most importantly, allocation was non-randomized and determined by patients’ willingness to self-pay; this introduces selection by socioeconomic status, health literacy, and motivation that statistical adjustment and propensity weighting can reduce but not remove, and the larger-than-trial effect size suggests such residual confounding is present. Second, carbetocin uptake rose steeply over the study period, confounding treatment with calendar time; we adjusted for year but cannot exclude residual secular effects. Third, blood loss at our institution is recorded as a visual estimate rather than measured, and recorded cesarean blood loss additionally includes suctioned amniotic fluid; recorded blood loss was therefore not used as a continuous measure. Fourth, post-delivery hemoglobin was available for 61% of carbetocin versus 53% of no-carbetocin cesareans; the primary continuous estimate was analyzed as complete cases and was robust, but the binary severe-decline comparison is sensitive to extreme missing-data assumptions and is reported only as supportive. Fifth, transfusion was not systematically collected as a research variable. We extracted a binary transfusion flag from the record for the entire cohort, but it undercounts true transfusion — all nine patients with a hemoglobin fall of ≥ 4 g/dL, and 71% of those with a post-delivery hemoglobin < 7 g/dL, carried no flag — so it is reported as a lower-bound, exploratory measure and can neither confirm nor exclude a difference in clinically significant bleeding (Additional file 1). Sixth, this is a single-center experience, limiting generalizability.

Conclusions

In a real-world setting of self-paid, increasingly adopted carbetocin use, carbetocin at cesarean delivery was associated with a smaller peri-operative hemoglobin decline, directionally consistent with randomized evidence but of larger magnitude likely reflecting selection, and was not associated with a lower incidence of PPH. These observational associations support, but cannot establish, a protective role and should be interpreted alongside existing randomized data.

The clinical datasets generated and analysed during the current study are not publicly available because they contain identifiable clinical information from the hospital electronic medical record and are subject to institutional privacy and ethics restrictions, but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request and with the approval of the institutional review board. The analysis code used for the sensitivity analyses is provided as a supplementary file.
