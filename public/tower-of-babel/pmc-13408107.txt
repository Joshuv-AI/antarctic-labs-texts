SGCM-Net: structure-guided mural inpainting with state space model
Authors: Yiyin Qiu, Jianjun Chen, Yan Fan, Md Rakib Hossain, Fei Wang, Xibei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe degraded regions of ancient murals often contain intricate textures and structural curves, presenting major challenges for traditional mural restoration. Although digital image inpainting offers a viable approach, Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based methods are commonly constrained by these complex scenarios and struggle with global consistency due to their limited receptive fields. To address these limitations, a CNN-Mamba hybrid inpainting architecture is proposed based on the two-stage task decomposition paradigm. This framework employs Structure-Guided Fusion Blocks (SGFBs) to adaptively fuse structural priors from the edge inpainting stage across multi-scale levels. To enhance holistic consistency, the proposed Multi-Way Mamba Process Blocks (MMPBs) are integrated into the bottleneck, specifically adapting State Space Models (SSMs) to capture global relations in 2D murals with linear complexity. Comprehensive evaluations on mural and landscape painting datasets show that the proposed method properly restores global styles, fills in coherent details, and achieves competitive performance compared to well-established methods.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Ancient murals represent one of the world’s most significant archives of art and history. Spanning many civilizations, these works have recorded a huge collection of visual data, serving as a priceless record of the past. Yet, this invaluable legacy is in peril. Natural factors such as rock erosion and disturbance of the environment, combined with the impacts of human activity, have led to inevitable degradation. Traditionally, the approach to restoring damaged murals involves manual procedures, a tricky process handled by professional conservators. Such manual restoration is usually time-consuming and demands a deep understanding of ancient art. To overcome these limitations, digital image inpainting provides a practical solution. The underlying concept of image inpainting technique is consistent with the process of manual restoration: it reconstructs the missing regions through intelligent inference based on surrounding textures and overall style. This technology is not only efficient but also offers a visual guidance for conservators.

In recent years, deep learning approaches, particularly those1,3,4 based on the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), have made substantial progress in image reconstruction. However, directly inpainting large missing regions in artworks with rich details, such as the Dunhuang murals, often lacks spatial constraints, leading to a loss of curve continuity and structural consistency, as shown in Fig. 1. Recognizing that high-fidelity murals are full of delicate lines and aiming to lower the difficulty of mural inpainting, the proposed framework follows the two-stage task decomposition paradigm13: it first restores the structural prior and then the inpainted structural prior serves as a reliable constraint for inpainting the mural with coherent textures and colors.

Qualitative comparison with the state-of-the-art methods1,2 on Dunhuang murals. The proposed method aims to address curve continuity and structural consistency issues, which tend to occur in complex scenes.

The choice of structural prior is critical to the success of this paradigm. State-of-the-art methods like ZITS5, for example, combine two priors: Canny edges and wireframes. Although such fusion method is effective for images with polygonal structures, its wireframe component is unsuitable for the murals because it is designed to detect straight lines. The prevalence of curves in artistic murals means that straight-line detection provides sparse and inadequate guidance, leading to suboptimal restoration. In this work, the proposed method utilizes edges generated from the recently advanced MuGE (Multiple Granularity Edge Detection)6 model. Leveraging this state-of-the-art edge detector provides a way to create a holistic structural prior and capture macroscopic structural semantics of murals. In addition, to bridge this structure-guided pipeline, a novel Structure-Guided Fusion Block (SGFB) is proposed. This block is designed to fuse the restored structural priors from the edge inpainting network (the first stage) into the mural inpainting network (the second stage) in a multi-scale way. This design preserves structural information from the preceding inpainting network and progressively incorporates it to high-fidelity murals.

More than just structural prior, achieving a globally consistent restoration requires the inpainting network to understand the entire image context. This is a known shortcoming of standard CNN-based methods, whose convolutional kernels mainly focus on local receptive field3,7. This fact essentially restricts the ability of model to acquire image’s global contextual awareness. As a result, researchers start seeking solutions among Transformer architectures8,9, which are built for modeling token-wise attention and learning global relations. However, Transformer blocks have a major computational drawback: their cost grows quadratically. This becomes a critical issue for images, since their high number of pixels results in long token sequences. The Vision Transformer10 attempts to alleviate this issue by restructuring pixels into a patch-formed sequence, but this introduces a trade-off where small patch sizes lead to a high computational cost and large patch sizes result in lower accuracy. Considering these limitations, State Space Models (SSMs)11 have been investigated as a favorable alternative. Specifically, the recent Mamba12 model, notable for modeling long-range dependencies at a linear computational cost and parallel training, making it highly suitable for mural inpainting task. Inspired by benefits of CNN and Mamba models, this work proposes a Structure-Guided Conv-Mamba Network (SGCM-Net): a CNN encoder-decoder structure with intermediate Multi-way Mamba Processing Blocks (MMPBs). The proposed MMPB consists of two components: a two-directional zigzag scanning strategy to capture global information, and a feed-forward network (FFN) incorporating a gating mechanism to capture more expressive feature relationships.

Related work

Structure-based methods

In order to obtain more realistic and high-quality details, many approaches shift attentions toward explicitly utilizing structural priors to facilitate images inpainting. For instance, Nazeri et al.13 first used Canny edge maps to guide the images restoration. Guo et al.14 proposed a two-stream network that mixes structure and texture with each other, enabling them to work in a complementary way. Yang et al.15 developed a structure embedding layer to incorporate gradient maps into image up-sample stage. Li et al.16 simultaneously processed Canny edges, grayscale maps and smoothed images and utilized the fusion module from Guo et al.14 to fully integrate them. Ren et al.17 used the smoothed image from which high-frequency details have been removed, as its structural prior. Li et al.18 reconstructed their Canny structure in a progressive approach. Wang et al.19 employed a monochrome image as a bridge for linking the external and internal learning. Cao et al.20 utilized structural priors like Canny edges and wireframes to guide image inpainting. Dong et al.5 advanced this approach by introducing a Transformer-based network for structure completion and leveraging a strong baseline LaMa1 for texture restoration, achieving state-of-the-art results. In a follow-up work, Cao et al.21 investigated various types of structural priors and analyzed their respective advantages for the inpainting task. Aside from these structural priors, other types of guidance, such as depth image22, local binary pattern23, contour24, and sketch25, have been actively explored. The proposed strategy employs salient MuGE edge to capture semantic structures, avoiding the low-level edges often captured by Canny edge detection. Furthermore, it decouples structure and texture inpainting into two sequential stages for better restoration results.

Mamba-based methods: from general vision to image inpainting

Having succeeded in Natural Language Processing (NLP), the Mamba architecture has recently been explored as an efficient approach for computer vision tasks. Zhu et al.26 employed a bidirectional mechanism to process sequences of image patches, integrating the patch-based representation method of Vision Transformer10 with the linear-efficiency of Mamba’s state-space modeling. Liu et al.27 expanded patches scanning strategies to four distinct ways and achieved competitive performance in image classification and segmentation. Extending the application of Mamba to cultural heritage, Xian et al.28 leveraged a Multi-scan Mamba framework to capture global spatial dependencies, successfully addressing the complex pose estimation of figures in traditional Thangka artworks. Guo et al.29 first applied Mamba to image restoration and proposed a tailored architecture that maximized the efficacy of Mamba blocks for image reconstruction. Shifting the focus to the domain of image inpainting, Chen et al.30 combined Mamba with Transformer for image inpainting task, reducing the heavy computational complexity from Transformer’s attention mechanism while leveraging its global receptive field. Within a coarse-to-fine framework, Hou et al.31 incorporated Mamba into the features encoding module and utilized Mamba’s global modeling ability to capture spatial features comprehensively. To deal with the challenge of large missing areas in image inpainting, Jiang et al.32 introduced a multi-directional spiral scanning method that enhanced the information exchange within the feature map. Following the design of MambaIR29 that introduced additional CNN layers to preserve locality coherence, this work adopts an efficient multi-way processing strategy and proposes a gating mechanism incorporated feed-forward network to bring locality and strengthen the robustness of features.

The edge inpainting network

Structure priors offer powerful guidance. Unfortunately, existing methods are often constrained by using Canny edges for restoration13,14,16. Since Canny operator is highly sensitive to low-level and irrelevant structure details, this makes it difficult to represent the semantic structural information that images contain. To overcome this issue, the proposed edge inpainting network utilizes the MuGE6 edge as a robust guidance. This deep learning-based method pays more attention to semantic structure of the murals and suppresses fine-grained edge details that might misguide the restoration process.

The mural inpainting network

The proposed mural inpainting network leverages an encoder-decoder backbone, with several specialized fusion blocks introduced in the encoder. Specifically, all the convolutional down-sample blocks are replaced with the proposed Structure-Guided Fusion Blocks (SGFBs). This substitution is designed to leverage multi-scale structural information from the edge generator and guide the mural restoration network to generate more semantically plausible and structurally coherent murals. In addition, the proposed MMPBs are stacked between the convolutional down-sample and up-sample blocks. In this position, they can effectively model the long-rage dependencies between regional patches.

Experiments

In this section, the datasets, experimental settings, competing methods and metrics are first described. Then, the presented model is evaluated and analyzed through a series of comparative evaluations and ablation analysis.

Experimental settings

Datasets

A specialized dataset was constructed by combining two public sources: the DhMurals dataset47 and Dunhuang Challenge dataset48. The former dataset contains 1714 mural images which mainly depict Buddhist and religious themes. The latter contains 600 paintings digitized from Cave 7 of Mogao Grottoes, with a collection of various categories such as architecture, traditional dance, Buddha statues, and stylistic motifs. The composite mural dataset of 2314 images was partitioned into a training set (1920 images), a validation set (214 images), and a test set (180 images). In addition, the training set was augmented with horizontal flips to prevent overfitting and strengthen the generalization performance. This process yielded a total of 3840 training samples. To evaluate the generalization ability of the proposed network, the Chinese landscape painting dataset49 was selected. This choice is motivated by the inherent stylistic consistency between the two domains, as Chinese landscape paintings share similar patterns and textures with traditional murals. This dataset contains 2192 high-quality Chinese landscape paintings collected from open-access museum galleries. Following the similar data configuration, the Chinese landscape painting dataset was partitioned into a training set of 1920 images, a validation set of 92 images, and a test set of 180 images. The same augmentation strategy was applied to the Chinese landscape painting dataset.

Implementation details

Training details. The proposed model was implemented in Pytorch. Training was launched on a single NVIDIA 3090TI GPU (24GB) with the batch size of 6. To stabilize the adversarial training, the network was optimized using the Adam optimizer50 with a learning rate of , and decay rates and . Parameters of loss functions were opted empirically. All the edge inpainting network models were trained for 200 epochs using the constant learning rate mentioned above. As for the mural inpainting network models, the initial training was conducted for 200 epochs with a fixed learning rate. For the state-of-the-art comparison, the mural inpainting network model was subsequently finetuned for an additional 100 epochs and the learning rate was linearly decayed from the aforementioned value to a minimum of .

MuGE edge generation. All the MuGE edge maps were generated from the images within the train, val, and test splits using the pre-trained model publicly released by the authors6.

Masks settings. To simulate different damage ratios on the murals, experiments were performed on irregular mask datasets7. Irregular masks from this work were classified into four groups with masking ratios ranging from 10% to 50%, in increments of 10%. To avoid leakage of masks, 200 masks are randomly sampled from every mask group for evaluation.

In the experiments, all the images were resized to 256256 pixels.

As the data shows, the proposed method demonstrates increasingly competitive performance across all metrics, showing particular robustness at higher mask ratios. The presented model achieves the highest PSNR scores for both datasets at every mask ratio. Regarding SSIM, the proposed approach achieves the highest scores on the mural dataset for all mask ratios above 20%. For the landscape painting dataset, it exhibits a slight performance gap compared to T-former and CoordFill. A possible explanation for this result is that the Multi-head attention remains an effective mechanism for modeling global structure, and the continuous coordinate querying mechanism in CoordFill naturally preserves local smoothness. Meanwhile, this performance gap in SSIM indicates that there is room for optimization in the Mamba-based approach to narrow the metric margin while maintain the linear complexity. For LPIPS and error, lower scores are better. The proposed method shows encouraging robustness, with its performance advantage becoming more evident at larger mask ratios. These results indicate that the presented model generates images with improved perceptual fidelity and promising structural consistency.

On alternative fusion methods of priors

The impact of the Structure-Guided Fusion Block(SGFB) is analyzed by comparing the proposed module against a vanilla(No fusion) base model and two established fusion strategies. The first method, denoted as SK, represents Selective Kernel Convolution from SKNets56. The second method, denoted as Bi-GFF, is an effective method validated by multiple models14,16 within the image inpainting domain. The configuration (B) in Table 4 serves as the baseline, which is obtained by replacing all SGFBs with the convolutional blocks shown in Fig. 2. The other two variants are constructed by incorporating the SK and Bi-GFF fusion blocks into the baseline. The quantitative results are presented in Table 5. As expected, the baseline without utilizing any prior as a guidance exhibits the lowest performance, validating the fundamental necessity of prior integration. Notably, among the fusion methods comparison, the Selective Kernel method(SK) outperforms the inpainting-specialized method Bi-GFF. A potential explanation for this effectiveness can be drawn from the hypothesis in the work of Dong et al.5, which suggests that edge features reside in a sparse tensor space. Consequently, the adaptive channel selection mechanism of SK is inherently suitable for prior fusion task in inpainting domain. This perspective also rationalizes the performance of the proposed method, which achieves the highest rank in the study. The proposed design employs a gating mechanism that shares the fundamental selection capability with SK. Meanwhile, the integration of depth-wise convolution extends this selection into the spatial dimensions. This filtering mechanism combined with multi-scale fusion approach leads to the best performance across all evaluated metrics.

On varying the number of stacked MMPB blocks

In this ablation study, the configuration (C) from Table 4 is used for the base model and all the variants are created from adding different numbers of MMPB. As shown in Table 6, a consistent improvement is observed across all evaluated metrics as blocks are gradually added between the convolutional encoder and decoder. Specifically, increasing the model depth from 0 to 8 blocks yields an average PSNR gain of 0.22dB and an average error reduction of 0.09 across all mask ratios. Furthermore, Table 6 also reveals a consistent reduction in the LPIPS metric. This outcome is attributed to the core design of the MMPB blocks, which leverage Mamba to efficiently model global long-range dependencies. Since LPIPS is highly sensitive to the overall coherence and realism of an image, it benefits directly from the enhanced global context provided by a deeper depth of these blocks. Discussion and limitations

Practical and ethical considerations in cultural heritage

The integration of structure-guided pipeline and the CNN-Mamba fusion architecture into mural inpainting introduces valuable insights into the field of cultural heritage preservation. From a practical perspective, the proposed method serves as a high-quality digital completion tool. Unlike manual restoration, which cannot be readily reversed, this virtual approach provides experts with intuitive visual outputs before any hands-on work. This auxiliary capability is particularly useful for ancient mural restoration, where historical art documentation is often fragmented and original details may be partially or entirely lost. By training on extensive mural datasets, the proposed method allows conservators to leverage the artistic knowledge of thousands of examples to reduce the reliance on rare records and specialized expertise. However, the ethical usage of AI in this domain should be performed with caution. A major concern involves the misinformation of AI-generated outputs. Despite generating visually plausible murals, the synthesized pixels are predictions rather than original artistic works. Since the model may generate contents that never existed, it is essential to label these AI-generated murals with explicit digital watermarks to avoid fostering public misconceptions or introducing“artificial noise” into the verified cultural heritage archive.

Limitations

Although the proposed method delivers promising performance in mural inpainting, certain limitations call for further exploration. The primary challenge appears in the context of dealing with extremely large-scale missing areas. To analyze the model’s performance limit, an evaluation was conducted on masks exceeding the 50% ratio. As shown in Table 9, a continuous degradation is presented: values decrease from 20.34 dB (50%–60%) to 14.33 dB (80%–90%). It is observed that as the PSNR drops below 20 dB, the presence of blurry contents and inconsistent structures becomes prominently noticeable. As illustrated in Fig. 11, under such extreme degradation, the remaining ground truth information is insufficient. A deeper analysis indicates that this performance breakdown stems from two coupled factors. First, the edge inpainting network produces incomplete edges and generating noise-like artifact patches (Fig. 11c). Second, as evidenced by Grad-CAM57 visualizations, the MMPB fails to model sufficient global dependencies when the token sequences contain excessive missing data. For regions with relatively successful restoration, the MMPB selectively attends to visually consistent contexts across the image (Fig. 11d). However, in extensively corrupted areas where the inpainting fails, the heatmap becomes misaligned, highlighting entirely irrelevant regions(Fig. 11e). Similar limitations have been reported in established studies4,8, where even state-of-the-art models have difficulty in restoring high-fidelity details in such challenging scenarios.

Future work

To address the failing cases observed in scenarios with large-scale missing regions, future research could focus on the integration of historical style priors and semantic guidance. Given the limitations of sparse effective information, the external historical knowledge from multiple mural datasets could provide meaningful cues to enrich the details of the restored areas. Specifically, knowledge-aware fusion blocks could be developed within the CNN-Mamba backbone to align latent features with historical artistic priors. This strategy ensures that the synthesized parts in large damaged areas consistent with the original textures and artistic structures.

In addition to maintaining semantic consistency, the reconstruction of fine-grained textures needs deeper investigation. By decoupling the inpainting pipeline into multiple stages, an additional refinement network can pay more attention to high-frequency detail restoration, which is a task typically fraught with difficulty in one-step frameworks. Furthermore, the incorporation of the proposed Mamba process modules into the refinement network will serve to further validate their effectiveness and adaptability. Specifically, the Mamba process blocks can be placed inside encoders and decoders to leverage preliminary inpainted results from previous stage, thus restoring the intricate patterns of murals while remaining the computational efficiency.

Conclusion

This paper introduces a specialized mural inpainting framework called SGCM-Net. Initially, the proposed method employs an encoder-decoder architecture to restore the structures of corrupted murals. Then, Structure-Guided Fusion Blocks are leveraged to incorporate structures into mural inpainting network in a multi-scale way. Following this, a series of novel Multi-Way Mamba Process Blocks are integrated to model global-range relations across the feature maps to attain high-level semantic consistency. Extensive experiments show that SGCM-Net delivers competitive performance compared with the existing popular counterparts.
