Hallmarks of primary headache: part 4 – rare headache syndromes
Authors: Gabriele Sebastianelli, Woo-Seok Ha, Roberta Messina, Li-Ling Hope Pan, Alejandro Labastida-Ramirez, Eloisa Rubio-Beltran, Doga Vuralli, William Wells-Gatnik, Sophie Merve Yener, Paolo Martelletti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aim“Other primary headaches” is a group of heterogeneous headache disorders described in Chapter 4 of the third edition of the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-III). Due to the limited number of cases, most of these disorders are considered rare and represent a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge for clinicians and headache specialists. To increase knowledge, improve their recognition, and foster research on these rare headache disorders, this review provides a comprehensive up-to-date description of their hallmarks, including epidemiologic data, key clinical characteristics, proposed pathophysiological mechanisms, and suggested treatments.Principal findingsDiagnosing rare headache disorders is challenging. A few key clinical features can help improve their recognition and distinction. These include identifying a specific trigger or stimulus for headache development such as cough, exercise, or sexual activity, a particular pattern of onset or recurrence, like for hypnic headache, primary thunderclap headache, or new daily persistent headache, or a unique scalp location for the pain such as for nummular headache or epicrania fugax. These clinical details are crucial for narrowing the differential diagnosis and making the correct diagnosis. However, regardless of the specificity of the clinical presentation, secondary causes must be ruled out when facing patients with these rare clinical presentations. Limited evidence on the pathophysiology and treatment of these disorders is available, and high-quality studies are lacking.ConclusionsAccurate diagnosis and additional research on these rare headache disorders are needed. Multicenter and international collaborations within the framework of international headache societies and among tertiary headache centers are key to developing efficient and high-quality registries and databases. Additionally, clinical trials are needed to develop evidence supporting the treatments current in use and to address the unmet needs of these rare diseases. Only through these efforts it will be possible to design specific studies to increase our understanding of these rare disorders and improve the quality of life of individuals living with these conditions.

Introduction

Headache disorders are one of the leading causes of disability among neurological diseases, with a substantial contribution made by the most common primary forms, especially migraine and tension-type headache [1]. Conversely, rare headache disorders, as those described in Chap. 4 of the third edition of the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-III), affect only a small number of patients; however, they can still significantly reduce the quality of life of patients and, on an individual basis, have an even more negative impact than the most common ones. This is mainly due to the paucity of evidence about effective treatments and the lack of expertise, which represents a disadvantage for the patients suffering from these rare conditions. Furthermore, given the limited number of cases, rare primary headaches also represent a diagnostic challenge with a high chance of misdiagnosis for both clinicians and headache specialists.

Chap. 4 of the ICHD-III describes “Other primary headaches” as a group of different and heterogeneous headache disorders whose pathogenesis is poorly understood and treatment lacks evidence [2]. Depending on the presence of a specific trigger, stimulus, or localization, the ICHD-III groups them into four categories: headaches associated with physical exertion, headaches attributed to direct physical stimuli (physiological [non-damaging] stimuli), epicranial headaches (i.e., localization of headache over the scalp), and other miscellaneous primary headache disorders. Different definitions and metrics are used around the world to classify a disease as rare, ranging from a prevalence below one in every 200,000 persons in the USA or fewer than 5 per 10,000 persons in Europe [3]. Despite the definition applied, most of the disorders included in Chap. 4 could be considered rare, as their prevalence is low, very low, or unknown, and their description primarily relies on case reports, case series, or small cohorts collected from tertiary headache centers. Additionally, mainly due to scarcity of knowledge about their pathophysiology, they lack evidence for their treatment and only anecdotal reports, or uncontrolled trials are available.

Headache associated with physical exertion

Primary cough headache

Definition, epidemiology, and clinical phenotype

Primary cough headache (PCH) is a primary headache disorder described in Chap. 4.1 of the ICHD-III [2]. As its name suggests, PCH is characterized by headache precipitated by coughing or other Valsalva maneuvers but not by prolonged physical exercise, in the absence of any underlying intracranial disorder [2]. In 1932, Jules Tinel first described a headache associated with different exertional maneuvers [4], while cases with benign cough headache, now defined as PCH, were reported by Symonds in 1956 [5]. PCH has an estimated lifetime prevalence of around 1% [6], with a prevalence in headache clinics between 0.4–1.2% [7, 8]. It generally affects individuals over the age of 40 years [9] and is considered to have a male prevalence [5, 10]. However, there are also series reporting female dominance [11].

Clinically (Table 2, Fig. 1), the pain is described as sharp, stabbing, or throbbing in quality [11–13], with an intensity that varies from moderate to severe [12, 13] and a duration from one second to several minutes, but can last up to 2 hours according to the current diagnostic criteria [2]. Typically, it has a sudden onset, is located bilaterally [5, 12], and is predominantly experienced in the frontotemporal regions [8, 12, 14]. However, it can also be unilateral [8] and experienced in the occipital areas or holocranial [8, 12, 14]. Associated symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, and dizziness are rare [12]. PCH is considered a diagnosis of exclusion, and in the differential diagnosis, potential causes of symptomatic cough headache should be considered and ruled out through detailed neuroimaging evaluations. Among the several secondary causes, Chiari malformation type 1 is the most common secondary cause, followed by structural posterior fossa lesions such as arachnoid cysts, dermoid tumours, and meningiomas [13]. Additionally, obstructive hydrocephalus, subdural hematoma, multiple brain metastases, sphenoid sinusitis, non-ruptured cerebral aneurysm, spontaneous intracranial hypotension, pneumocephalus, and pneumococcal meningitis are also reported as causes of symptomatic cough headache [8, 16–20].

Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of PCH remains largely unknown, although various hypotheses have been proposed. These included transient elevations in the intracranial pressure, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) hypervolemia, and transient nociceptor sensitization after an infection.

Transient elevations in the intracranial pressure have been suggested as a possible cause of PCH. Indeed, coughing may cause a rise in the intrathoracic and intra-abdominal pressures, leading to an elevation in the central venous pressure [9]. Additionally, using magnetic resonance imaging with venography, one study has shown transverse or jugular venous sinus stenosis in patients with PCH [21]. However, whether the stenosis is a primary condition or is secondary to the elevated intracranial pressure remains a matter of debate [21]. Additionally, as shown by Chen et al., a relative CSF flow obstruction around the craniocervical region caused by a more crowded posterior cranial fossa can potentially contribute to the pathophysiology of PCH, leading to increased intracranial pressure during coughing [22].

CSF hypervolemia is another suggested cause of PCH, which may cause a higher-pressure gradient in the downward pressure wave and lead to subsequent compression of the dura during coughing [23]. This hypothesis is further supported by the fact that lumbar puncture and indomethacin, which can reduce CSF pressure, are possible effective treatments for PCH [8, 10–13]. Interestingly, topiramate, which may reduce CSF pressure as well, is not helpful for the treatment of PCH [9], suggesting that other mechanisms, such as the nitrergic mechanism, may be responsible for the efficacy of indomethacin in this rare form [24].

Finally, transient receptor sensitization following a previous infection that alters the vascular tone, especially in the venous system, has been proposed as another mechanism underlying PCH [25].

Treatment

Acute treatment is generally not required because of the short duration of attacks. In contrast, a preventive treatment should be considered in most patients due to the debilitating nature of the symptoms. The first treatment of choice is indomethacin. The daily dose of 50–200 mg is found to be effective in several studies [8, 10–13]. Chen et al. reported that 72.7% of the patients responded to indomethacin at doses up to 75 mg [8]. There is no consensus on the duration of the treatment. Pascual et al. suggested a maximum treatment period of 5 months [11], while Chen et al. reported that pain freedom was achieved 6 months after the start of indomethacin treatment in patients with a good initial response [8]. However, recurrences were also reported [8]. As mentioned before, a possible mechanism by which indomethacin exerts its effect in PCH could be through the reduction of the intracranial pressure [26]. However, additional mechanisms may play a role [24].

The CSF drainage by lumbar puncture was also shown to be effective in PCH patients [8, 10], and potentially curative in some cases [10]. However, since lumbar puncture is an invasive procedure, it is suggested as a second-line treatment [8]. Despite some conflicting results [9], topiramate, acetazolamide, methysergide, propranolol, naproxen, and intravenous metoclopramide have also been reported to have therapeutic effects in smaller case series [23, 27–31]. Additionally, the greater occipital nerve (GON) block has been reported to have beneficial effects in small case reports [32, 33]; however, the optimal frequency and the duration of GON block treatment remain undefined. A case of PCH who responded to non-invasive vagal nerve stimulation has also been reported in the literature [34].

Primary exercise headache

Definition, epidemiology, and clinical phenotype

Primary exercise headache (PEH) is a primary headache disorder described in Chap. 4.2 of the ICHD-III [2]. As its name suggests, PEH has a close relationship with physical activity and exertion. It was first described by Rooke in 1968, who reported 103 patients with exertional headaches and noted that approximately 90% followed a benign course [35]. For this reason, he termed the condition benign exertional headache, and it was later incorporated into ICHD-I (1988) [36]. However, subsequent studies broadened the definition to include headaches induced by sustained, physically strenuous exercise [11, 37, 38]. Subsequently, the terminology was revised in the following ICHD-II to primary exertional headache and in ICHD-III (2018) to the current term primary exercise headache [2, 39]. In the current classification, PEH is restricted to headaches triggered by “strenuous exercise,” while Valsalva-related headaches, such as those induced by sneezing or straining are categorized separately as PCH [2]. “Weight-lifter’s headache” is currently recognized as a PEH subtype but is not independently classified, given its overlap with Valsalva-induced headache. PEH prevalence varies widely in population-based studies, ranging from about 1% in some studies to 7–30% in others [38, 40–43] and reaching higher prevalence in specific populations like sportsmen. Indeed, in one survey among cyclists, the estimated prevalence of PEH reached 26% [44]. Much of this variation reflects methodological differences and recall bias [45]. Still, the low numbers in clinic-based series and the predominance of case reports suggest that many cases are mild, short-lived, and do not prompt medical consultation. The mean age of onset is in the early forties, though prevalence is also high among adolescents aged 13–15 years [38, 41, 43]. Sex distribution has been inconsistent: some studies found a marked male predominance (up to 4:1) [13, 35], while others reported a modest female excess, with female-to-male ratios of 1.4–2.0 [38, 42].

Clinically (Table 2, Fig. 2), PEH typically occurs during or within 30 minutes of sustained strenuous physical activity. Pain is throbbing or compressive in quality, bilateral in 60–78% of cases, and most often occipital or frontotemporal, though diffuse or vertex pain is also reported [38, 42]. Attacks usually last from 5 minutes to 24 hours, with an average duration of 3–4 hours [38, 41, 42]. In adolescents, most resolve within 1 hour [43], whereas in adults prolonged episodes, occasionally up to 48 hours, have been described [13, 42]. In the Vågå study, Sjaastad et al. found that half of patients reported attacks shorter than 1 hour and half longer than 1 hour, but the two groups did not differ in clinical characteristics [46]. In a Japanese cohort, 43% of patients rated pain as mild, 17% as moderate, and 40% as severe [41]. The headache typically peaks during exertion and subsides after cessation [45, 47]. Triggers include running, swimming, gym training, and skiing, with hot weather and high altitude frequently reported as aggravating factors [42, 48]. Up to half of patients have comorbid migraine, and migraine-like features such as nausea, vomiting, photophobia, or phonophobia are reported in 10% of the cases [38]. Although secondary causes are less frequent than in PCH, the possibility of an underlying lesion must always be considered at the first presentation [49]. Notably, even in Rooke’s original cohort, some patients with exertional headache were found to have intracranial pathology such as subdural hematoma or tumour [35]. Differential diagnoses include subarachnoid hemorrhage, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome, cervical artery dissection, and Chiari malformation [2, 45, 50]. In line with this, the European Headache Federation guideline recommends a diagnostic work-up in suspected PEH, including brain magnetic resonance with angiography and venography (MRA/MRV), carotid or computed tomography angiography (CTA), and vertebral ultrasound to exclude secondary causes [51]. Lumbar puncture may be warranted when idiopathic intracranial hypertension is suspected. Cardiac cephalalgia must also be considered, as myocardial ischemia provoked by exertion can mimic migraine-like or exertional headache [51]. Several reports have described patients initially presenting with typical exertional headache who were ultimately diagnosed with cardiac cephalalgia [52, 53]. Accordingly, in patients with cardiovascular risk factors, careful inquiry into exertional chest pain, dyspnoea, or ischemic equivalents is essential, and in selected cases cardiac evaluation may be indicated.

Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of PEH is not fully understood. Nevertheless, its abrupt onset during exertion and the frequent observation of vascular abnormalities have long supported the hypothesis that PEH arises from a disturbance of cerebrovascular autoregulation. Doepp et al. demonstrated internal jugular valve incompetence in up to 70% of patients, raising the possibility that transient venous reflux increases intracranial pressure during exercise [54]. The benefit of indomethacin, which reduces intracranial pressure and influences neurovascular coupling, appears in line with this hypothesis [55, 56]. More recently, Tarperi et al. measured plasma CGRP levels before and after a half-marathon in 48 amateur runners and found a 1.5-fold increase, though only three participants had PEH. Then, its clinical relevance remains uncertain [57] and, to date, no studies have evaluated anti-CGRP therapies in this disorder.

Treatment

Evidence for treatment is limited, although some non-pharmacological and pharmacological interventions are available. Regular sports training, proper warm-up before the activity, and adequate hydration during exercise are suggested as possible non-pharmacological interventions [45]. The decision about the type of pharmacological interventions depends on the predictability of attacks, as well as on headache and exercise frequency.

Indomethacin taken preemptively is the most consistently effective option if attacks are predictable and the patient does not exercise regularly. Additionally, ergotamine and triptans also reported as useful [13, 37]. For patients with frequent or prolonged attacks, preventive therapy with beta-blockers or daily indomethacin may provide benefit [11, 13, 45, 47].

Primary headache associated with sexual activity

Definition, epidemiology, and clinical phenotype

Primary headache associated with sexual activity (PHSA) is a primary headache disorder described in Chap. 4.3 of the ICHD-III [2]. PHSA has been recognized since ancient Greece [58]. In modern times, Paulson and Klawans (1974) reported 14 patients with headache precipitated by orgasm and introduced the term benign orgasmic cephalgia [59]. Shortly thereafter, in 1976, Lance described 21 additional cases, including patients with headache developing before orgasm, and proposed the broader term headache related to sexual activity [60], which laid the foundation for the current nomenclature. Accordingly, ICHD-I and ICHD-II divided PHSA into two subtypes: type 1, a dull bilateral pressure pain gradually increasing with sexual arousal (pre-orgasmic headache), and type 2, a sudden, explosive pain at orgasm (orgasmic headache) [36, 39]. However, ICHD-III abandoned this subdivision [2], particularly after the study by Frese et al., which followed 60 PHSA patients for an average of 3 years and demonstrated no major differences in clinical characteristics or prognosis between the two types [61]. Thus, PHSA is now regarded as a single entity, usually but not necessarily related to orgasm. The reported lifetime prevalence of PHSA is roughly 1% [40], although this may be underestimated due to patients’ reluctance to disclose symptoms related to sexual activity. Although PHSA is typically benign and self-limiting, it can cause significant sexual distress, negatively impact quality of life, and lead some patients to avoid sexual activity altogether [76]. Its abrupt onset, particularly when thunderclap in quality, can also be alarming to patients and clinicians. Because PHSA can mimic serious secondary conditions such as subarachnoid hemorrhage or reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome, urgent neuroimaging is mandatory at first presentation. Interestingly, some authors suggest that the absence of headache triggered by exertion or cough in a patient with PHSA is a green flag for its benignity [77]. However, according to ICHD-III, PHSA remains a diagnosis of exclusion [2]. Postural headaches following sexual activity have also been reported and were previously categorized as a PHSA subtype in ICHD-I [36]. However, these are now considered manifestations of spontaneous intracranial hypotension due to CSF leakage and must be carefully distinguished from PHSA.

Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of PHSA remains largely unclear, but several mechanisms have been proposed, including increased sympathetic response and structural predispositions [21, 64, 69]. Based on cerebral blood flow velocity changes after acetazolamide administration, a case-control study in 2003 reported enhanced metabolic neurovascular coupling in PHSA patients compared with migraine and healthy controls [64]. PHSA may also share clinical or pathophysiological features with other primary headache disorders. Since the earliest literature, similarities to PEH have been recognized, forming the basis for therapeutic approaches including indomethacin and propranolol [60, 63, 78]. One study found that more than half of patients with either PCH or PHSA had findings of transverse sinus or jugular vein stenosis on imaging [21]. More recently, a case report described successful treatment of a woman with both PHSA and migraine using erenumab 70 mg, suggesting a potential link between PHSA, migraine, and CGRP mechanisms [79].

More substantial insights have come from two recent cohort studies from Taiwan [67, 72]. Over a 15-year period, all patients presenting with at least two episodes of headaches associated with sexual activity were evaluated according to ICHD-III criteria, with MRI and MRA performed within one month of onset and follow-up imaging obtained at least three months later [67]. Among 245 patients, 174 (71.0%) were diagnosed with reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS), 26 (10.6%) with probable RCVS, and only 38 (15.5%) with PHSA. Importantly, apart from PHSA being more likely to follow a chronic course, the groups were otherwise similar in demographics and headache characteristics, supporting the hypothesis that PHSA and RCVS may share a disease spectrum. In this context, the same authors, in line with the existing recommendations of the ICHD-III, emphasized the need for exhaustive neuroimaging, including MRI, MRA, and MRV [68]. Given that the timing of imaging relative to onset is crucial for the diagnosis of RCVS, future studies should take into account and explicitly report the timing of imaging in their protocols.

Treatment

The clinical course of PHSA is usually episodic, though one-third to one-quarter of patients develop a chronic course lasting longer than one year [61, 67]. Nevertheless, prognosis is generally favourable, with remission rates approaching 69% within 3 years [61]. Preemptive treatment options include indomethacin, NSAIDs, or triptans taken before sexual activity [69, 73, 76]. For long-lasting or recurrent attacks, preventive medications such as propranolol, nimodipine, or topiramate have been reported to be effective [69, 80]. Some patients abstain from sexual activity during active bouts in an attempt to prevent further attacks [67, 81]. While this strategy may be effective in principle, it is often impractical or undesirable for most patients and should not be considered a formal therapeutic recommendation.

Primary thunderclap headache

Definition, epidemiology, and clinical phenotype

Primary thunderclap headache (PTH) is a primary headache disorder described in Chap. 4.4 of the ICHD-III [2]. PTH is defined by a sudden-onset and high-intensity headache that reaches its maximum intensity within one minute, mimicking the presentation of a ruptured cerebral aneurysm but in the absence of any intracranial pathology [2]. Given its close resemblance to life-threatening conditions [82, 83], such as subarachnoid hemorrhage, PTH must be considered as a neurological emergency until other secondary causes have been excluded. Of note, a comment on the ICHD-III states that evidence that PTH exists as a primary disorder is poor and the search for an underlying cause should be both expedited and exhaustive [2]. The term “thunderclap headache” was first used by Day and Raskin in 1986, describing a severe headache that occurred in patients with unruptured cerebral aneurysms [84]. The differential diagnosis for PTH is broad. The most crucial conditions to exclude are subarachnoid hemorrhage, particularly from a ruptured aneurysm. Then, patients presented with such recurrent episodes should be re-evaluated 2–4 weeks after the onset of PTH to check for evidence of vasoconstriction for the diagnosis of RCVS rather than primary PTH. Pathophysiology

The exact pathophysiology of PTH remains largely hypothetical, given that it is a diagnosis of exclusion. One hypothesis suggests that PTH may share some mechanisms with RCVS [88]. This is supported by the evidence that oral nimodipine showed a good response in both RCVS and PTH without vasoconstriction [95], suggesting shared mechanisms. However, in contrast to RCVS, vasoconstriction has never been proved in PTH. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms.

Treatment

Given that PTH is a diagnosis of exclusion and a self-limiting condition, treatment is primarily symptomatic once all serious secondary causes have been definitively ruled out [96]. Acute pain management typically involves non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or other abortive headache medications while opioids are generally avoided due to concerns about dependence and the need to maintain vigilance for any evolving neurological symptoms [97]. Due to its monophasic course, prophylactic treatment is generally not needed. A case report suggests the benefit of oral nimodipine [95], but no standardized treatment exists.

Headache attributed to direct physical stimuli

Cold-stimulus headache

Definition, epidemiology, and clinical phenotype

Cold-stimulus headache (CSH) is a primary headache disorder described in Chap. 4.5 of the ICHD-III [2]. CSH is defined as a transient headache triggered by a cold stimulus applied either externally to the head or ingested/inhaled [2].

Early reports date back to the nineteenth century, but systematic classification started with the ICHD system in 1988 [98]. The reported prevalence of CSH is quite variable. Population-based studies have estimated a lifetime prevalence ranging from 15% to 74%, with higher rates seen in children and adolescents, affecting both males and females relatively equally [99–101]. There is some evidence that a family history of CSH may increase the likelihood of experiencing it [100]. Additionally, subjects with migraine have a higher prevalence and susceptibility to CSH compared to subjects with tension-type headache [102], suggesting shared pathophysiological mechanisms between migraine and CSH.

According to the type and source of the cold stimulus, the ICHD-III subclassifies CSH into 4.5.1 headache attributed to external application of cold stimulus – which follows an exposure of the unprotected head to a very low environmental temperature – and 4.5.2. headache attributed to ingestion or inhalation of a cold stimulus (previously known as ice-cream/brain-freeze headache). Apart from the different type of stimuli that triggers the headache, the two subtypes are distinguished by the timing of resolution after removal of the cold stimulus, which is 30 and 10 minutes, respectively. For both diagnoses, at least two acute headache episodes fulfilling the ICHD-III criteria are required. However, when only a single episode occurs, ICHD-III classifies it as 4.5.3 probable cold-stimulus headache [2]. Clinically (Table 2, Fig. 5), CSH is characterized by a rapid onset of intense pain that peaks rapidly (within 20–60 seconds), with most people experiencing a stabbing or pressing, bilateral frontal or temporal headache [101, 103]. Typically, the pain is brief, lasting only seconds to a few minutes, and, depending on the subtype, resolves spontaneously within 10 or 30 minutes of stimulus removal. Although cranial autonomic symptoms (CAS), such as lacrimation, and visual phenomena (e.g., flickering lights) have been frequently reported, it is generally not consistently accompanied by any associated symptoms [101, 103]. Interestingly, subjects with migraine usually report that pain in CSH is throbbing in quality and located ipsilateral to their usual migraine attacks [102]. The diagnosis of CSH is relatively straightforward due to its short-lasting duration and identifiable triggering factors. However, some secondary headaches can rarely mimic primary CSH and should be considered as differential diagnoses. These included cardiac cephalalgia, where cardiac ischemia presents as headache, especially after cold-weather exposure [105], and carotid or vertebral artery dissection, especially if triggered by neck movement in cold air [106]. Red flags in CSH include additional atypical headache features, such as longer duration and different location/quality, and associated neurological or cardiac symptoms, necessitating thorough evaluation and neuroimaging when suspected [107].

Pathophysiology

The underlying pathophysiology of CSH remains incompletely understood. A neurovascular mechanism initiated by palatal cooling has been proposed. The cold stimulus, by stimulating trigeminal (V2, V3) cold receptors, may lead to sympathetic vasoconstriction of cranial/cerebral arteries and reactive vasodilation mediated via trigeminal afferents and potentially the sphenopalatine ganglion [104, 108]. The activation of trigeminal nociceptors in the palate or posterior pharyngeal wall by a cold stimulus results in pain referred to the frontal or temporal area [103]. Additionally, the strong association between CSH and migraine suggests shared pathways involving trigeminovascular activation and neuronal hyperexcitability as a common background [102].

Treatment

Given the transient and self-limiting nature of CSH, specific pharmacological treatment is generally unnecessary. Management focuses primarily on prevention through avoidance of triggers or behavioral strategies able to minimize the likelihood of a headache after a cold stimulus. These included slow ingestion of cold substances, avoiding direct palatal contact, and keeping the head covered in cold environments [99]. When trigger avoidance is impractical or insufficient, a preventive regime of naproxen 750 mg taken 30 minutes prior to cold exposure seems to prevent symptoms [109].

External-pressure headache

Definition, epidemiology, and clinical phenotype

External pressure headache (EPH) is a primary headache disorder described in Chap. 4.6 of the ICHD-III [2]. EPH is characterized by headache resulting from sustained compression of or traction upon pericranial soft tissues by non-noxious physiological stimuli [2].

The prevalence of EPH has been poorly quantified due to underreporting, though case series suggest it is common in occupations requiring prolonged headgear use (e.g., athletes, construction workers, healthcare workers, etc.) [110–112]. Two recent cross-sectional studies in frontline healthcare workers using personal protective equipment for an extended duration of time revealed that females experienced more EPH than males [111, 113]. Amongst the healthcare workers who experienced EPH, up to 50% had a previous history of migraine and tension-type headache. No age- or demographic-specific risk factors have been established; however, adults appear to be most affected due to occupational exposure [110–112].

EPH was initially documented as “swim-goggle headache” [114], gaining formal recognition in 2004 and further refined in the ICHD-III [2]. According to the type of pressure, which could be traction or compression, the ICHD-III subclassified it into 4.6.1 external-compression headache and 4.6.2 external-traction headache. For both diagnoses, at least two episodes fulfilling the ICHD-III criteria are required. However, when only a single episode occurs or two episodes occur without satisfying both the criteria C and D, ICHD-III classifies it as 4.6.3 probable external-pressure headache. The diagnosis of EPH is also relatively straightforward due to its short-lasting duration and identifiable triggers. Key diagnostic features include maximal pain at the site of external compression/traction and pain resolving completely within one hour of pressure/traction removal [2]. The differential diagnosis of EPH includes secondary headaches where pressure exacerbates pain, such as intracranial pathologies (brain tumors, chronic subdural hematoma), which can manifest as pressure-aggravated pain due to mass effect or altered cerebrospinal fluid dynamics [117, 118]. Occipital neuralgia can overlap with EPH triggered by occipital pressure (e.g., tight headbands) but typically presents with neuropathic features (i.e., shooting pain) and may persist after stimulus cessation [119]. Red flags include persistent pain beyond 1 hour after pressure removal, progressive severity and severe pain intensity, or neurological deficits like papilledema, warranting neuroimaging for atypical cases [107].

Pathophysiology

The underlying pathophysiology of EPH centers on peripheral nerve compression [120], with sustained external pressure directly activating and sensitizing cutaneous nociceptors innervated by sensory branches of the trigeminal nerve (e.g., supraorbital/supratrochlear nerves for frontal pressure) or the greater occipital nerve for posterior pressure, inducing localized pain via mechanotransduction and Aδ fibers activation [121]. Central sensitization is believed to play a role in individuals with comorbid migraine, where pre-existing trigeminovascular hyperexcitability lowers mechanical pain thresholds (i.e., allodynia) [122], amplifying EPH severity.

Treatment

Treatment prioritizes non-pharmacological interventions due to the transient nature of EPH. Immediate removal of the external pressure/traction (e.g., loosening headgear, adjusting hairstyles) resolves most attacks within minutes to a few hours [111, 113]. Preventive strategies should focus on avoiding sustained compression, such as using soft padded interfaces for headgear and ensuring proper fit. For occupational scenarios where pressure exposure is unavoidable (e.g., healthcare workers), intermittent removal of the personal protective equipment is recommended. Pharmacotherapy is rarely indicated, but short-term non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAIDs, e.g., ibuprofen) may alleviate refractory discomfort or residual pain. In patients with comorbid migraine and cutaneous allodynia, migraine prophylaxis may reduce EPH susceptibility by raising mechanical pain thresholds [123], though direct evidence is limited.

Epicranial headaches

Primary stabbing headache

Definition, epidemiology, and clinical phenotype

Primary Stabbing Headache (PSH) is a primary headache disorder described in Chap. 4.7 of the ICHD-III [2]. PSH is defined as a transient headache with brief, intensely sharp stabs of pain that are predominantly localized to the temporal and fronto-ocular areas [2, 124, 125]. Attacks typically last less than 3 seconds and recur in an unpredictable manner, ranging from a solitary stab less than once per day to multiple daily clusters involving dozens of stabs [126, 127].

The first systematic description of PSH dates to 1964, recognized under the clinical term of “ophthalmodynia periodica” [128]. Researchers expanded on these findings, leading to multiple rounds of changes in nomenclature, including “jabs and jolts syndrome,” “ice-pick pains” and other terms that generally pay homage to the quality of the paroxysmal attacks [124, 125, 129, 130]. At this time, PSH was recognized as a cephalalgia that strictly affected the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve (V1). While defining the newly proposed disorder, two specific findings were apparent. Firstly, PSH was a purely sensory syndrome that entirely lacked autonomic involvement [2, 127, 131]. Secondly, outside of acute stabbing episodes, PSH was not associated with sustained craniofacial discomfort [127]. The lack of prodromal and postdromal symptomatology was directly in contrast to the vastly more recognized headache disorders of migraine and tension-type headache. Additional findings, including the discovery that PSH is indomethacin-responsive, further propelled recognition of PSH as a unique primary headache disorder worthy of distinct classification [125, 130, 132]. Then, despite early clinical observations, it was not until the publication of the second edition of the ICHD that PSH was formally classified as a distinct headache disorder [39]. While a notable advancement in the recognition of PSH, it should be noted that the ICHD-II continued to define PSH as a headache disorder that exclusively, or predominantly involved V1 distribution [39]. With further clinical observation, PSH was redefined in the ICHD-III (beta) to better account for the greater anatomic and pathophysiologic heterogeneity characteristic of the disorder [2, 133]. While advances in the classification and management of PSH have provided novel insight, the disorder remains underdiagnosed and likely undertreated [131]. Along with the lack of standardized epidemiological methodology, these difficulties make it challenging to accurately define epidemiological measures and specific risk factors for PSH across the general population, resulting in significant variations in prevalence across various global populations [125, 134]. The prevalence of PSH, as estimated by community-based studies, spans a large range of approximately 2–35% with a mean age of onset between 28 and 53 years [125, 127, 135, 136]. While data remains conflicting, the prevalence of PSH may have a slight female predominance of approximately 1.5–2:1 [125, 127, 129, 134]. Furthermore, it appears that PSH is less common in the paediatric population, varying between 2.5–10% of children and adolescents [137]. As shown by early studies, subjects with PSH commonly reported comorbid headache disorders, including migraine (30–50%) and tension-type headache (20–40%) [138].

Clinically (Table 2, Fig. 7), PSH is characterized by brief stabbing pains that occur spontaneously, in clearly defined circumscribed areas ( < 1 cm2) without preceding aura or autonomic features, and may occur singularly or in clusters [2]. Approximately 80% of stabs last less than 3 seconds; however, rare instances of stabs lasting 10–120 seconds have been reported [2]. Additionally, in children, the duration of stabs may be longer, varying from a few seconds to 30 minutes [139]. Furthermore, in contrast to early suspicion, extratrigeminal involvement occurs in approximately 70% of cases, most commonly involving the upper cervical dermatomes [2]. The attack frequency of PSH is generally considered to be low, with most patients reporting less than one attack per day. In contrast, rare cases of repetitive attacks, including dozens of stabs daily, have been reported [124, 125, 127]. Photophobia and phonophobia may appear as accompanying symptoms in around 30% of patients [140]. Furthermore, between stabbing episodes, patients remain entirely asymptomatic, with no ongoing background headache nor aura-like manifestations. While the ICHD-III criteria specifically exclude patients with CAS, a minority of those with PSH report accompanying symptoms, further complicating the diagnostic process and necessitating specialist consultation [2]. Several primary, secondary, and other headache disorders can mimic PSH, necessitating careful consideration when faced with a patient who reports stabbing craniofacial pain. These included trigeminal neuralgia, occipital neuralgia, short-lasting unilateral neuralgiform headache with conjunctival injection and tearing (SUNCT), short-lasting unilateral neuralgiform headache attacks with cranial autonomic symptoms (SUNA), and secondary headaches. Trigeminal neuralgia is likely the most common mimic of PSH. Similar to acute attacks reported by those with PSH, trigeminal neuralgia presents with brief electric shocks that follow a trigeminal distribution [141]. However, in contrast to PSH, paroxysms of trigeminal neuralgia are frequently precipitated by environmental and physical triggers such as facial touch, chewing, shaving, speaking, or exposure to cold wind [141, 142]. Furthermore, pain in trigeminal neuralgia may elicit a refractory period in which further paroxysms may not be provoked, which is absent in those with PSH and alternate disorders of mechanical allodynia [142]. Occipital neuralgia is another headache disorder that may mimic PSH, which is characterized by unilateral or bilateral paroxysmal shooting or stabbing pain localized to the posterior scalp [2]. Although occipital distribution of pain is less frequently experienced in PSH, significant diagnostic overlap between these conditions remains. Notably, occipital neuralgia is associated with appreciable dysesthesia and allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair [2, 143]. In contrast, in those with PSH, signs and symptoms of dysesthesia and allodynia outside of acute paroxysms are rarely noted, representing a major distinguishing point between these disorders. Additionally, SUNCT and SUNA can also mimic PSH. Indeed, these headache disorders have significant overlap in the intensity, frequency, and anatomic location of acute pain episodes [2, 144]. However, in contrast to PSH, acute episodes of SUNCT/SUNA are frequently associated with major ipsilateral CAS that localizes to the side of the head pain, effectively excluding PSH in these patients [2]. Finally, in certain high-risk populations, especially in those presenting with features concerning for intracranial or severe neurologic pathology, secondary causes of craniofacial pain must be excluded [125, 127, 145]. In those presenting with new-onset disease with severe or atypical features, such as prolonged duration, autonomic disturbances, focal neurologic deficits, a thorough history, neurological examination, and targeted laboratory/imaging may be recommended. Secondary disorders that may mimic PSH and necessitate exclusion include intracranial masses, lesions, tumors, arteriovenous malformations, and temporal arteritis [127, 145, 146].

Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of PSH remains poorly understood, with no established animal models to guide experimental exploration. Currently, the leading hypothesis implicates spontaneous, transient discharges from hyperexcitable nociceptive nerve endings, particularly Aδ fibers within the trigeminal and occipital distributions, as the primary mechanism [129, 131, 147, 148]. This is supported by the brief, localized, and spontaneous quality that is attributed to PSH stabs. Emerging evidence further points to the critical role of nociceptive Schwann cells, which appear to regulate pain gating. Dysfunction or transient loss of nociceptive Schwann cells, potentially due to aging, inflammation, or mechanical insult, may facilitate aberrant nociceptive signalling and promote afferent hyperexcitability [131, 149, 150]. Furthermore, data from cases of patients with multiple sclerosis, stroke, or cervical myelitis suggest that focal lesions or disinhibition within central pain modulatory systems can produce stabbing headache phenotypes, possibly via spontaneous peripheral discharges triggered by central dysregulation [131]. All considered, these hypotheses support a multifactorial pathogenesis for PSH, where peripheral nerve dysfunction, nociceptive Schwann cell abnormalities, local inflammation, and Aδ fibers hyperexcitability may act independently or synergistically to generate the hallmark stabbing pains of this disorder [131].

Treatment

Although the therapeutic approach to PSH is generally limited by the brief nature of stabbing episodes, it is worthy of note that PSH is considered an indomethacin-responsive headache disorder [151]. Indeed, indomethacin appears to significantly reduce or completely resolve stabbing episodes in approximately 65% of those with PSH [125]. While considered the first-line approach to the treatment of PSH, data supporting the use of indomethacin remains scarce. While the mechanism in which indomethacin reduces stabbing episodes in PSH remains poorly understood, proposed mechanisms include cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibition, nitric oxide (NO) pathway antagonism, and phosphodiesterase inhibition [125, 152, 153]. Selective COX-2 inhibitors, such as celecoxib and etoricoxib have shown benefit in patients with PSH. However, long-term cardiovascular risks remain a concern [125]. Other reported treatments include paracetamol, gabapentin, nifedipine, and melatonin, each supported by small case series or individual reports [125, 154]. In particular, melatonin, with its favourable side-effect profile and potential anti-inflammatory properties, is a promising option, especially in patients who cannot tolerate indomethacin [154]. Notably, many cases of PSH resolve spontaneously, and treatment is often unnecessary, as persistent symptoms occur in only a minority of patients [126].

Nummular headache

Definition, epidemiology, and clinical phenotype

Nummular headache (NH) is a rare primary headache disorder described in Chap. 4.8 of the ICHD-III [2]. It was previously termed “coin-shaped headache”, from which the term “nummular” is derived. Indeed, NH is characterized by pain localized to a small, round or elliptical area of the scalp [2]. This condition was first comprehensively described by Pareja et al. in 2002 [155]. The current diagnostic criteria for NH according to ICHD-III are described in Table 17.

There are no population-based epidemiological studies on the incidence or prevalence of NH. However, NH has been observed across diverse geographic and ethnic groups, and the estimated annual incidence is between 6 and 9 cases per 100,000 individuals. This figure is likely an underestimation, as the condition may be underdiagnosed due to limited awareness among clinicians and patients. Most cases are reported in middle-aged adults, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 1.6:1. Clinically (Table 2, Fig. 8), patients with NH describe a focal area of pain that is round or elliptical in shape with well-defined borders. The size and form of the painful area tend to be stable over time and the majority of cases measured between 1 and 6 cm in diameter. NH may present as bifocal, multifocal, or over the midline, though unifocal involvement is more common [158–163]. Although the ICHD-III does not distinguish between episodic and chronic forms of NH [2], reported cases suggest a persistent rather than an episodic course. The pain quality in NH can vary, most often described as pressure-like, although stabbing, burning, throbbing, or electric-like qualities are also reported. Pain intensity is generally mild to moderate and may be accompanied by exacerbations or superimposed paroxysms [157, 158, 164, 165]. A recent long-term observational study involving 168 patients with NH and over 1100 patient-years of follow-up found that approximately 68% of individuals exhibited a chronic pattern at diagnosis, defined as headache occurring on 15 or more days per month. Exacerbations were present in nearly half of the patients (48.2%), with a median intensity of 8 on a 0–10 verbal rating scale (baseline median intensity of 6) and a median duration of 10 seconds [166]. Although the affected scalp area in NH is typically normal on inspection and palpation, some patients report mild cutaneous changes such as tenderness, temperature changes, hypoesthesia, or hyperesthesia within the painful region. In rare cases, visible changes including slight erythema, dermal atrophy, or alopecia have been documented, possibly reflecting chronic mechanical irritation or underlying inflammatory processes [2, 159, 165, 167–169]. In the largest long-term study of NH to date, superficial sensory disturbances were found to be highly prevalent, allodynia was reported in 37.5% of patients and hyperalgesia in 34.5% [166]. Similarly, a separate case series of 72 patients found that more than 40% experienced sensory symptoms or signs, suggesting a possible role of peripheral nerve sensitisation mechanisms in the pathophysiology of NH [158]. Identifiable triggers are also reported in a subset of patients, with tactile stimuli such as brushing, touching, or pressure to the affected area being the most common. Exacerbations may also be provoked by head movement, Valsalva manoeuvres, or physical activity [158, 167, 168, 170]. Some cases of NH have been reported with features commonly associated with migraine, including nausea, photophobia and phonophobia [176]. Interestingly, a small case series involving two patients described a favourable response to beta-blocker therapy, while another case identified menstruation as a potential trigger, suggesting a possible overlap in underlying pain processing mechanisms [176–178]. The differential diagnosis of NH is broad and includes a range of cranial neuralgias and other primary headache disorders, particularly when pain localises to the scalp. These include auriculotemporal or occipital neuralgia, epicrania fugax (EF), PSH, and linear headache (LH). Auriculotemporal neuralgia or occipital neuralgia may resemble NH but differ in pain quality, location, and alignment with specific nerve distributions, as well as the presence of nerve tenderness [179, 180]. EF is another relevant differential diagnosis. It presents with brief, stabbing paroxysms that radiate across the scalp and may be confused with NH due to its epicranial localization. EFs dynamic, migrating pain pattern typically distinguishes it from the fixed pain of NH. However, in some cases, patients with EF have also reported a persistent, well-localized, round-shaped headache, consistent with NH, confined to the site where EF paroxysms typically originate. This clinical overlap has led to the hypothesis that EF and NH may share a common pathogenic mechanism [157, 181, 182]. PSH is another primary headache disorder to consider in the differential diagnosis of NH. NH typically presents as intermittent or continuous pain confined to a fixed, well-circumscribed area, whereas PSH is characterised by brief, sharp, and stabbing pain lasting only a few seconds, and often recurring irregularly over hours or days. In addition, NH tends to remain stable in location, while PSH is migratory, affecting different regions of the head across episodes [2, 126, 157]. LH is a recently described headache disorder, characterized by well-localized, continuous or intermittent pain along a narrow, linear trajectory on the scalp. Its features resemble those of NH, with the key distinction that the painful area is linear rather than round or elliptical. In a prospective series of 16 patients, some individuals reported migraine-like features such as photophobia, phonophobia, and nausea; however, most did not meet diagnostic criteria for migraine. The authors proposed that LH may represent a distinct primary headache syndrome or a phenotypic variant of NH with a different spatial distribution of pain [183–185]. Although NH is classified as a primary headache disorder, an increasing number of cases have been attributed to underlying structural or systemic conditions. Reported secondary causes include conditions affecting the skin and subcutaneous tissue, such as varicella zoster rash, linear scleroderma, and subcutaneous cysts; cranial bone abnormalities like osteomas and eosinophilic granulomas, intracranial lesions including pituitary adenoma, meningioma, and arachnoid cysts [168, 186–190]. Additionally, a study reported an association between autoimmune conditions and NH. In the cohort by Chen et al., 69.6% of patients had at least one abnormal autoimmune marker, and nearly half were subsequently diagnosed with systemic autoimmune diseases, including Sjögren’s syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, or antiphospholipid antibody syndrome. These findings raise the possibility that autoimmune processes may underlie or trigger some cases of NH [191]. A recent study reported that 25.9% of NH cases over a 20-year period were secondary, suggesting that secondary NH may be more common than previously recognised. These secondary forms often share similar clinical features with primary NH. However, a history of remote head trauma, pre-existing headache disorders, longer disease duration, and right-sided localization were more frequently observed in secondary cases. Importantly, patients with symptomatic NH benefited significantly from cause-specific treatments [175]. Considering the range of reported secondary cases, detailed clinical evaluation including skin assessment and neuroimaging is always recommended when NH is clinically suspected.

Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of NH is not yet fully understood, but current evidence mostly supports a peripheral origin. The focal and well-defined nature of the pain, along with associated sensory abnormalities including allodynia, paresthesia, or hypoesthesia, suggests involvement of the terminal branches of the epicranial cutaneous nerves [192, 193]. Quantitative sensory testing and pressure algometry studies have shown reduced pain thresholds within the symptomatic zone, without evidence of widespread pericranial sensitivity, supporting the hypothesis of peripheral sensitization [193, 194]. This is further supported by evidence from cases of NH occurring over areas of cranial bone or skin abnormalities, which improve following treatments targeting peripheral structures [168, 175, 186, 187, 190, 195]. It was also reported that pain reappeared in a nearby region after surgical excision of the symptomatic scalp, emphasizing the peripheral sensitisation model [196]. Additionally, a recent study further supported a peripheral mechanism, based on the observation that both unifocal and bifocal cases of NH exhibit consistent characteristics, including a well-defined, circumscribed area of pain with stable shape, size, and location over time. This uniformity across different affected scalp regions may reflect a shared localized origin with involvement of the terminal branches of superficial sensory nerves [174]. While most cases support a peripheral origin, some reports of midline pain localization and partial responses to nerve blocks suggest that central sensitization may also play a role in selected cases [155]. Additionally, a case of NH remission following treatment with a calcitonin gene–related peptide monoclonal antibody was reported, pointing to a possible overlap with neuroinflammatory pathways implicated in migraine [197]. Another recent report describing new-onset NH after middle cerebral artery stenting suggests that endothelial injury and subsequent vascular inflammation might trigger focal pain through activation of sensory afferents, highlighting a potential role for systemic or neurovascular signalling pathways [198].

Treatment

The therapeutic approach to NH remains largely based on clinical experience due to the absence of randomized controlled trials and limited pathophysiological understanding.

Acute management of NH primarily involves simple analgesics and NSAIDs. Indomethacin has been used with variable response [155, 159, 167, 168, 172]. Triptans have rarely shown benefit, particularly in patients with migraine-like features or secondary NH to arterial aneurysms [176, 199]. Given the typically mild to moderate intensity and focal nature of the pain, these agents are usually preferred during exacerbations of background pain.

Preventive medications are preferred in patients with frequent or disabling attacks. A recent large observational study has shown favourable long-term outcomes with preventive treatments. Over a median follow-up of 6.7 years, 67% of patients required preventive therapy. The most commonly used agents were gabapentin, BoNT-A, amitriptyline, and lamotrigine. At least partial response was achieved in over 80% of cases, and nearly half of the patients became headache-free by the end of follow-up. The proportion of patients with chronic NH decreased from 68% at diagnosis to 11% at follow-up [166].

Onabotulinumtoxin-A (BoNT-A) is considered the first-line option for NH [159, 168, 171, 200]. In a prospective open-label trial, 53 patients with frequent NH attacks received 25 units of BoNT-A injected into the symptomatic area. After 24 weeks, 77.4% achieved at least a 50% reduction in headache days per month, and 52.8% achieved a 75% reduction [171]. These results were reinforced by the NUMITOR study, which found that BoNT-A had the highest treatment response among all tested options, with a 50% responder rate in 62.5% of cases and a 75% responder rate in 47.5% of cases. It was also the best-tolerated agent, with fewer discontinuations than oral preventives. The injection technique typically involves five subcutaneous points distributed in a cross pattern over the symptomatic area, with 5 units per site [173].

Gabapentin is a commonly used oral alternative to BoNT-A for preventive therapy in NH [159, 162, 166–168, 172, 173, 186, 190]. In a large observational study involving 183 patients, gabapentin was trialled in 71 patients. It demonstrated a 50% responder rate in 43.7% of cases and 75% responder rate in 35.2% of cases at weeks 8 and 12 of treatment. Despite its effectiveness, 21.1% of patients discontinued due to tolerability issues [173]. Other oral agents, including amitriptyline, pregabalin, topiramate, carbamazepine, duloxetine, and lamotrigine, have also been trialled, though with inconsistent efficacy and notable tolerability concerns [159, 167, 168, 172, 173, 176, 187, 189].

Given the focal nature of NH, peripheral nerve blocks, including the auriculotemporal, greater occipital, and supraorbital branches, have been explored as both diagnostic and therapeutic interventions [155, 168, 201–203]. However, the efficacy of peripheral nerve blocks in NH appears to be limited, with most reports indicating ineffectiveness or transient relief [201]. These findings suggest that nerve blocks may be useful in selected cases but should be considered an adjunct rather than a primary treatment strategy.

Additionally, there are case reports and small series describing potential benefits from treatments including ketogenic diet, galcanezumab, neurostimulation techniques, metoprolol, palmitoylethanolamide, neurotropin, and minimally invasive arterectomy [178, 197, 199, 202–206]. In secondary cases, effective management relies on identifying and treating the underlying pathology. Surgical resection of the causative lesion has been associated with complete symptom resolution in several reports [175, 188, 190, 207].

Overall, current observational data support the use of BoNT-A as a first-line treatment for patients with frequent or refractory NH. Gabapentin remains a reasonable alternative, although adverse effects may limit its use in some individuals. Peripheral nerve blocks may offer diagnostic value or short-term relief, but they are not consistently effective as a long-term treatment option. Further prospective and controlled studies are clearly needed to establish optimal treatment strategies and better understand the mechanisms underlying treatment response.

Epicrania fugax

Definition, epidemiology, and clinical phenotype

Epicrania fugax (EF) is a primary headache disorder described in the appendix of Chap. 4.11 of the ICHD-III [2]. EF is a unique and relatively recently described primary headache syndrome characterized by very brief (“fugacious”), paroxysmal pain that moves rapidly across the surface of one hemicranium. The pain typically follows a linear or zigzag trajectory, commencing and terminating in the territories of different cranial nerves [2]. The term “fugax” (Latin for “fleeting” or “fugitive”) aptly describes the transient nature of the pain. EF was first formally described recently by Pareja et al. in 2008 [208], who distinguished it from other epicranial headaches and neuralgiform pains by its characteristic origin, duration, and radiating pattern. Since its initial description, EF first appeared in the appendix of the ICHD-III beta edition and its clinical spectrum has been expanded with further case reports and series, including variations in pain trajectory and associated symptoms [209–211].

EF is considered a very rare headache disorder, and population-based epidemiological data are currently scarce. Most of the available information comes from case series and specialized headache clinics. Following the review by Cuadrado et al. [212], an additional five case reports or case series [182, 209, 213–215] that were initially not included in the original review due to publication dates were identified through further literature search. To date, approximately 100 cases have been reported in the literature. It appears to affect both sexes, though some studies suggest a slight female predominance. The mean age of onset is typically in middle age at 46.2 ± 15.0 years old, but it can occur across a wide age range (from 19 to 84). Based on a case series, psychiatric comorbidities, particularly anxiety, have been reported in a significant proportion of patients with EF, with some studies reporting anxiety in up to 60% of cases and depression in 10% [215]. Clinically (Table 2, Fig. 9), the hallmark of EF is the distinctive, rapidly moving pain. The pain paroxysms are ultra-brief, typically lasting from 1 to 10 seconds. The pain quality is often described as stabbing, electric shock-like, or pressing, and its intensity can range from moderate to severe [157, 212, 215]. The pain typically originates in a focal area, most commonly in the posterior scalp (occipital or parietal regions), and then rapidly radiates forward to the ipsilateral eye, nose, or forehead along a linear or zigzag pathway. This is known as “forward EF.” Less commonly, “backward EF” can occur, where the pain originates in the frontal area and radiates posteriorly [213]. A facial variant of EF has been characterized as well; these patients presented with pain that crosses several dermatomes of the face, either upwards or downwards, with or without extension into the cranial region [209–211]. Among them, two cases reported combined episodes of facial and cranial EF [209]. The frequency of attacks varies, ranging from two attacks per month to ‘countless’ attacks per day [208]. Between paroxysms, some patients may experience continuous or intermittent mild pain or tenderness in the stemming area. While the attacks are often spontaneous, some patients report triggers such as tactile stimulation of the scalp. Accompanying symptoms can include ipsilateral ocular or nasal autonomic features like lacrimation, conjunctival injection, or rhinorrhea, though these are not consistently present [208]. Hyperesthesia in the affected scalp area has also been noted [216].

The unique migratory nature of pain in EF makes its differential diagnosis challenging, as it can mimic or be confused with several other headache disorders and neuralgias, including trigeminal neuralgia, occipital neuralgia, SUNA/SUNCT and linear headache. Both trigeminal [217] and occipital neuralgia [218] may share some clinical characteristics with EF, such as the duration, quality and part of the localization of the pain. However, they are confined to the territory of the trigeminal and the greater or lesser occipital nerve, respectively. Then, in these conditions the pain does not exhibit the moving, linear trajectory characteristic of EF, which generally migrates across different nerve territories. Similarly, SUNCT and SUNA shared the short-lived nature of the pain with EF, but contrarily to EF, the pain in these two disorders is generally not described as moving across the scalp in a linear fashion and the autonomic features are prominent [219]. PSH may mimic EF for its brief, sharp and jabbing quality of pain, but in contrast the pain is typically multifocal and fleeting, without a specific trajectory across the scalp [220]. Linear Headache is a distinct headache disorder, not included in the current ICHD version, that involves a strictly unilateral, motionless head pain confined to a line-shaped area. While the involved area might appear similar to EF, the pain itself is stationary and not “moving” [183]. Finally, when facing a patient with suspected EF, it is crucial to rule out any structural lesions or other underlying pathologies that could cause similar symptoms, especially in atypical presentations or cases resistant to standard treatments. Neuroimaging should be considered to exclude conditions like skull base meningiomas or brainstem lesions, which have been reported as symptomatic causes of EF-like pain [221]. Positional triggers of EF may also suggest a secondary headache.

Pathophysiology

The exact pathophysiology of EF is not fully understood, but current evidence suggests a combination of peripheral and central mechanisms. The localized onset and rapid, linear, or zigzag spread of pain across different nerve territories suggest the involvement of epicranial nerves and their connections. One leading theory involves ephaptic transmission, suggesting an abnormal cross-talk or short-circuiting between adjacent, possibly demyelinated or damaged, nerve fibers in the superficial scalp [208]. This could lead to the unusual propagation of pain across different nerve territories. A closely related concept is the dysfunction of epicranial nerve terminals, where abnormal excitability or sensitization of peripheral nerve endings in the scalp contributes to the initiation and spread of the characteristic pain [208]. Beyond purely peripheral mechanisms, central sensitization is also thought to play a role, given the characteristic spread of pain of EF. Pain irradiation follows a broad trajectory, crossing different dermatomes, which purely peripheral mechanisms can hardly explain. Additionally, in certain instances, particularly with facial variants of EF, neurovascular compression of cranial nerves (like the trigeminal nerve) has been observed, suggesting it could be an etiologic factor in some cases [222]. The distinctive dynamic nature of the pain, which literally “runs” across territories of various nerves (e.g., occipital, trigeminal), strongly supports a phenomenon that extends beyond simple localized nerve irritation, pointing instead towards a more complex, possibly centrally modulated, propagation of abnormal neural activity [157]. However, studies are much needed to understand the pathophysiological mechanisms of EF.

Treatment

Treatment for EF aims to reduce the frequency and severity of paroxysmal pain attacks. Given its relatively recent description and rarity, large-scale randomized controlled trials are lacking, and treatment strategies are largely based on case reports and small series.

While spontaneous remissions have been reported in some patients, commonly used treatments include antiepileptic drugs, antidepressants, NSAIDs, and injectable options. Antiepileptic drugs are often considered as the first-line choice; gabapentin and lamotrigine have been reported to be effective in many cases [181, 209–211, 214, 222–228]. Pregabalin [211], carbamazepine [211, 213], oxcarbazepine [210, 211, 223], lacosamide [211], and levetiracetam [214, 226] have also been reported to be effective in EF. Eslicarbazepine was reported to be effective in a patient with highly resistant EF [229]. Amitriptyline [181, 209, 214, 223, 228], duloxetine [210], and indomethacin [214, 226] have also been used in a small group of patients. Steroid [226] or BoNT-A [214, 230] injection to the origin of EF pain has also been used in patients with EF. It is important to keep in mind that if the response to the initial treatment is unsatisfactory, another treatment should be tried, as to-date, no solid evidence of the treatment effects of either option has been provided.

Other miscellaneous primary headache

Hypnic Headache

Definition, epidemiology, and clinical phenotype

Hypnic Headache (HH) is a primary headache disorder described in Chap. 4.9 of the ICHD-III [2]. As its name suggests, HH develops exclusively during sleep, causing awakening, and it is also called “alarm clock” headache. The first description of this disorder dates back to 1988, when Raskin described six cases of patients regularly awakened from sleep by a diffuse headache accompanied by nausea but not by CAS and proposed that it may be caused by perturbation of a biological clock [231].

The exact prevalence of HH is currently unknown with different estimations among studies. Overall, HH could be considered a rare disorder with an estimated prevalence ranging from 0.07% to 1.1% [232–235]. However, it is important to note that most of these estimations were made referring to patients from different headache clinics, possibly causing some bias. One population study on Iceland population showed a prevalence of 0.22% for probable HH, while none of the patients had a definite diagnosis [236]. The same inconsistencies could be applied to the female-to-male sex ratio. A review of 348 published cases from 1988 to 2018 showed a female prevalence with a ratio of 2.2:1 [237]. It generally affects individuals over 50 years [237], with some cases in younger ages [238]. Pathophysiology

Although the precise pathophysiology of HH remains unclear, converging evidence highlights the involvement of hypothalamic dysfunction and disrupted chronobiological regulation [264]. A structural neuroimaging study by Holle et al. [265] investigated grey matter volume differences between 14 patients with HH and 14 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. The study revealed a significant bilateral reduction in grey matter volume in the hypothalamus of patients with HH, with the most pronounced alterations localized to posterior hypothalamic regions. In addition, this study identified grey matter reductions in pain-related brain areas, including the anterior cingulate cortex, frontal, and temporal regions. Indeed, the hypothalamus is a key structure in regulating sleep–wake transitions, arousal mechanisms, and circadian rhythmicity [266, 267]. Furthermore, converging evidence implicates the hypothalamus in the modulation of trigeminal nociceptive processing, highlighting its involvement in other primary headache disorders, such as migraine and cluster headache [268, 269]. In the context of HH, the observed hypothalamic grey matter volume reduction may represent a chronic disruption of central chronobiological networks and pain-inhibitory circuits, contributing to the pathophysiology of the disease.

While neuroimaging studies have revealed similarities between HH and other primary headache disorders, neurophysiological findings suggest a distinct underlying pattern in HH. In a study assessing the nociceptive blink reflex and pain-related evoked potentials, no significant differences were found in latency, amplitude, or habituation between patients with HH and healthy controls [270]. This stands in contrast to observations in migraine and cluster headache, where impaired habituation and altered brainstem excitability are well-documented [271]. These results indicate that HH may involve unique mechanisms of central pain processing, further supporting its distinct pathophysiological identity within the spectrum of primary headaches.

Although initial reports suggested a predominant association between HH and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep [262], subsequent serial polysomnographic studies have consistently shown that attacks can originate from both REM and non-REM sleep stages. Multiple studies have demonstrated that approximately 50–70% of HH attacks occur during NREM sleep, particularly stage 2, while 20–50% arise during REM sleep [233, 256, 259–261]. Notably, several patients experienced attacks during both sleep phases within the same night. These converging findings indicate that HH is not selectively linked to a specific sleep stage but rather reflects a broader dysfunction in sleep–wake regulation or arousal mechanisms. Furthermore, analyses of nocturnal melatonin secretion patterns revealed no significant abnormalities in HH patients compared to controls, suggesting preserved circadian pineal function and arguing against a primary melatonergic dysfunction [272].

Treatment

Management of HH remains challenging due to its rarity and the lack of randomized controlled trials. Current therapeutic approaches are primarily based on case reports and small case series [241]. Caffeine is considered the first-line treatment, used both acutely and prophylactically. When taken at bedtime, either as caffeine oral tablets (e.g. 65 to 200 mg) or as a cup of strong coffee, it has been reported to abort or prevent attacks in approximately 60–80% of cases, with a favourable safety profile and rare reports of insomnia, even in elderly patients [237, 239, 273]. Other alternatives consist of combination analgesics containing caffeine [239]. Given the substantial impact of hypnic headache on patients’ daily functioning, various acute treatments have been investigated, including NSAIDs, acetaminophen, oxygen therapy, metamizole, opioids, indomethacin, nimesulide, and ergotamine, though most have shown little to no effectiveness in alleviating hypnic headache attacks [241]. Triptans, although not routinely prescribed, may offer benefit when taken at attack onset or prophylactically before sleep [239].

Lithium represents the most thoroughly evaluated prophylactic treatment for HH, with multiple case series reporting favourable outcomes [237, 273]. In up to 90% of patients, a dose of 300 mg at bedtime was associated with either complete remission or a significant reduction in attack frequency. Higher doses, ranging from 300 to 600 mg per day, have also proven effective in more refractory cases [273]. Despite its efficacy, lithium therapy is frequently constrained by tolerability issues, including tremor, gastrointestinal discomfort, and the potential for renal or thyroid dysfunction. These risks necessitate regular laboratory monitoring and may limit its use, particularly in older or medically complex patients [274]. Indomethacin, administered at daily doses ranging from 25 to 150 mg, has been evaluated in several case reports, showing variable outcomes. Response rates of approximately 50% to 60% suggest moderate efficacy in a subset of patients [239]. Due to its limited tolerability, particularly gastrointestinal side effects leading to frequent treatment discontinuation, indomethacin is not generally recommended as a first-line preventive option [241, 274]. Melatonin, despite being attractive due to the circadian nature of hypnic headache, has produced mixed outcomes in clinical practice [241]. Other preventive medications with anecdotal benefit include topiramate, lamotrigine, agomelatine, gabapentin, valproate, BoNT-A, antidepressants, beta-blockers, and calcium channel blockers but results are inconsistent and based on individual reports [241, 274, 275].

Overall, caffeine represents a well-tolerated first-line option, and lithium or indomethacin is considered in more resistant forms. Randomized controlled trials with larger patient cohorts are needed to define evidence-based treatment guidelines for this rare headache disorder.

New daily persistent headache

Definition, epidemiology, and clinical phenotype

New Daily Persistent Headache (NDPH) is a debilitating primary headache disorder described in Chap. 4.10 of the ICHD-III [2]. It is characterized by the abrupt onset of a daily, unremitting headache, persisting for at least three months [2]. A remarkable feature of this disorder is that a large majority of patients can recall the exact day, and sometimes even the hour, when their headache began [276, 277]. NDPH was first described in 1986 by Vanast as a “benign form of chronic daily headache”, primarily noted in young adults, often preceded by a viral illness [278]. However, it was not until 1994 that Silberstein and Lipton proposed formal diagnostic criteria, leading to increased recognition of the disorder [279]. These criteria were eventually adopted into the ICHD in its second edition and have since been refined in ICHD-III. Although NDPH is rare, it is increasingly recognized due to improved awareness and diagnostic tools. The estimated prevalence in the general population is between 0.03% and 0.1% [300]. Admittedly, this estimation was calculated using the ICHD-II and the current classification encompasses broader criteria, therefore, the incidence is expected to be 2–3 times higher. A recent meta-analysis showed that NDPH has a prevalence in the general adult population of 0.06% (95% confidence interval: 0.02–0.18), while it has a prevalence of 1.23% and 2.39% in adults and children, respectively, evaluated in a headache clinic [301].

Although still less common than chronic migraine, NDPH appears to make up a larger proportion of chronic daily headache in children and adolescents than in adults, representing 21–28% of pediatric and 1.7–10.8% of adult chronic daily headache patients [280, 300, 302]. The reported female-to-male ratio ranges from 0.8:1 to as high as 2.5:1 [276, 303–306]. In studies of children in the northern hemisphere, the onset of NDPH is most common either in early-autumn or January likely associated to the school calendar, since no seasonal pattern has been observed in adults [303, 307–309]. NDPH is often accompanied by psychiatric comorbidities, most notably anxiety and depression [310], but patients may also report panic attacks or symptoms consistent with somatic symptom disorder [280, 310]. These comorbidities can exacerbate the headache or reduce the likelihood of successful treatment.

Clinically (Table 2, Fig. 11), the presentation of NDPH is remarkably consistent in its onset but heterogeneous in symptomatology. As previously mentioned, most patients report an exact date when their headache began, often linked to a stressful life event, an infection, a single Valsalva event or minor trauma [276, 277, 303, 311, 312]. Since NDPH is a diagnosis only made in retrospect, there may be strong recall bias with such events, and attributing causality should be cautiously considered. Pain is typically bilateral, rarely side shifting and described as either pressing (tension-type) or pulsating (migraine-like) in quality, but must not be attributable to another disorder [276, 312]. Intensity varies from mild to severe, with many patients experiencing moderate impairment in daily functioning. The ICHD-III describes two subtypes: a self-limiting type that typically resolves within several months without therapy and a refractory type that is resistant to aggressive treatment regimens [2, 312]. Associated symptoms are common; photophobia, phonophobia and nausea are frequently reported, especially in patients with personal or family history of migraine. Additionally, sleep disturbances, light-headedness, blurred vision, neck stiffness, concentration problems, sensory disturbances such as numbness or tingling, vertigo, lethargy and other non-specific syndromes have been reported [303, 312, 313]. Nonetheless, its defining characteristic is the abrupt switch to daily headache, which distinguishes NDPH from other chronic headaches, such as chronic migraine or tension-type headache, which typically escalate over time. Importantly, patients with a prior history of episodic headaches are still eligible for an NDPH diagnosis, provided that the new daily headache did not evolve gradually from previous headache patterns. NDPH is a diagnosis of exclusion. It is crucial first to rule out secondary causes of new daily headaches. Conditions that should not be missed include carotid or vertebral artery dissection, infection (e.g., meningitis, sphenoid sinusitis), persistent post-traumatic headache, cervical facet syndrome, subarachnoid hemorrhage, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, spontaneous intracranial hypotension, neoplasms (primary and metastatic), temporal arteritis and intranasal contact point headache [305, 314]. Appropriate investigations typically include MRI of the brain, often with venography, to rule out vascular abnormalities. If there is suspicion of altered intracranial pressure, a lumbar puncture with opening pressure measurement can be considered. In selected cases, viral serologies for infections such as Epstein-Barr virus or herpesvirus may be considered, particularly when a post-infectious etiology is suspected [315]. NDPH must also be distinguished from primary headache disorders that can mimic its presentation. Chronic migraine and chronic tension-type headache are the most common; however, these typically have a history of increasing frequency over time. Medication overuse headache can coexist with NDPH but should not be considered causal unless analgesic use preceded the daily headache pattern. Hemicrania continua, a strictly unilateral headache with autonomic features, may resemble NDPH but is uniquely responsive to indomethacin [316].

Additionally, some case reports of post-COVID-19 headache with NDPH phenotype have been described over the recent years [317]. Indeed, although headache has a median duration of 2 weeks during the acute phase of COVID infection, in approximately one-fifth of patients, it persists for 3 or 9 months with a daily pattern [318]. Notably, patients with persistent headache have migraine-like features more often than those without persistent headache. However, similar to NDPH, no differences emerged in the proportions of patients with tension-type-like headache and migraine-like headache. In contrast, headache intensity during the acute phase of COVID-19 infection was the only predictor of headache duration [318].

Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of NDPH remains elusive, but current research supports a multifactorial origin. Considering that a significant number of NDPH patients report a viral infection prior to headache onset, researchers have explored the role of neuroinflammation. Studies have found elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (i.e., TNF-alpha, IL-6) in the CSF and plasma of patients [316, 319, 320] when compared to healthy controls; however, the levels of cytokines did not differ from the values observed in chronic migraine patients, suggesting that this feature is not unique to NDPH but rather related to its chronicity. Studies have also suggested that NDPH may result from subtle neck injuries, cervical spine instability, or joint hypermobility since there is convergence of trigeminal and cervical afferents in the trigeminal nucleus caudalis, which suggest that these patients may respond to physical therapy or nerve blocks, supporting a structural component to the headache [321]. Finally, studies suggest that, as seen in migraine, NDPH may involve brain remodeling and dysfunction of central pain processing pathways. Even though no macroscopic brain changes have been observed [322], several studies have observed differences in cortical thickness, functional connectivity, neurite density, and cerebral blood flow [282, 284, 288–290, 294, 296, 297], with some of these alterations observed in structures involved in pain processing and modulation [282, 287, 296].

Treatment

Treatment of NDPH remains challenging due to its poorly understood pathophysiology, lack of robust clinical trials, and the poor response to treatment [291]. Current management strategies are largely empirical and based on the headache phenotype. Given the continuous nature of the headache in NDPH, acute treatments are not used to abort attacks but to reduce pain severity during exacerbations. Triptans have been reported to improve pain severity in 32% of patients, and are possibly more effective in cases that present migraine-like features [312]. For preventative treatment, tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., amitriptyline) are commonly used first-line agents [295, 308, 323, 324]. Additionally, venlafaxine, a serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor with off-target effects on the modulation of TNF-alpha, has been suggested as a treatment option, particularly for patients with significant comorbid anxiety or depression [325]. Notably, oral doxycycline – another TNF-alpha inhibitor – dosed at 100 mg BID for 3 months has been shown to have good results in some case reports of patients with NDPH [326].

Anticonvulsants like topiramate, gabapentin, and valproic acid may also be effective [323, 324]. Beta-blockers (e.g., propranolol), calcium channel blockers (e.g., flunarizine, nimodipine), and angiotensin receptor blockers (e.g., candesartan) have also been shown to be effective [295, 327, 328]. Some studies have evaluated the efficacy of CGRP (receptor) antibodies in daily chronic headache patients, with encouraging results [329, 330]. In cases suspected to have an inflammatory or post-infectious origin, a short course of corticosteroids, such as intravenous methylprednisolone, may provide transient relief, especially if initiated early [326, 331]. BoNT-A injections have been reported to help some patients, particularly those with a migraine phenotype, though evidence is limited [281, 298]. Other interventions, including intravenous lidocaine, ketamine, or dihydroergotamine infusions, are occasionally used in refractory cases within headache specialty clinics [332–336].

Due to the refractory nature of NDPH, non-pharmacological interventions play a crucial role in its management. Physical therapy, particularly focusing on the cervical spine and posture, may relieve symptoms in patients with neck involvement. Cognitive behavioral therapy, biofeedback, and mindfulness training can address the emotional distress and central sensitization associated with chronic pain. Patients are also advised to maintain a healthy lifestyle, including adequate sleep, regular exercise, and stress reduction. It is important to avoid medication overuse, as excessive use of analgesics may lead to secondary medication overuse headache. Occipital nerve blocks have shown benefit in select cases [312, 337–339]; however, approaches to nerve blocks have been reported to vary widely in terms of injection site (e.g., greater or lesser occipital nerve, supraorbital nerve, auriculotemporal nerve and supratrochlear nerve) and medication used (e.g., whether or not corticosteroids are used in addition to a local anesthetic) [312, 338, 340]. Finally, there is no strong evidence to support the use of occipital nerve stimulation [341].

The prognosis for NDPH varies widely. While some patients experience spontaneous remission or intermittent episodes, many report persistent daily headache for years [276, 312]. The condition is generally considered refractory, and only a small proportion achieve long-term relief with current treatment options. However, early diagnosis and prompt, aggressive intervention may improve outcomes [308, 342]. Unfortunately, many patients go years without a correct diagnosis, often receiving ineffective treatments or being misclassified as having migraine or tension-type headache.

Conclusion

The disorders classified under Chap. 4 of the ICHD-III constitute a group of rare and heterogeneous primary headache syndromes. They represent a considerable diagnostic challenge for clinicians and headache specialists. It is important to note that secondary causes must be ruled out when facing patients with these clinical presentations, as some of them may be life-threatening conditions.

Due to their rarity, to date, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these disorders remain poorly understood and only a few studies were specifically designed with this aim. This knowledge gap has negatively impacted the current understanding of these disorders, with important implications for available treatments, which are generally empirical or based on anecdotes while lacking high-quality evidence and a disease-driven approach.

Accurate diagnosis and additional research studies are needed. Multicenter and international collaborations – particularly within the framework of international headache societies and among tertiary headache centers – are key to improving data collection and building registries and databases of these rare headache disorders.

Finally, clinical trials are needed to develop evidence supporting the treatments currently in use and to address the unmet needs of these rare diseases. Only through these efforts will it be possible to deepen our understanding of these rare disorders and improve the quality of life and outcomes of individuals living with these disorders.
