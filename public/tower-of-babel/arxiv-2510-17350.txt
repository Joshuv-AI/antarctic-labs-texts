Sobolev instability for perturbations of periodic transport equations
Gabriel Rivière, Maria Teresa Rotolo

Abstract
We consider linear and time-dependent perturbations of periodic transport equations on the two-dimensional torus. For generic perturbations, we prove the existence of a large class of initial data whose Sobolev norms diverge exponentially fast. In higher dimensions, this remains true under a Morse-Smale assumption on the resonant part of the perturbation. In both cases, this is achieved by a normal form procedure and by studying Sobolev instabilities for time-dependent perturbations of Morse-Smale transport equations. The latter are analyzed on general compact manifolds using techniques from microlocal analysis and hyperbolic dynamics.



1
Introduction and main results

In this article we construct unstable solutions for certain classes of linear and time dependent transport equations, through the analysis of their Sobolev norms. The study of instabilities is, in its full generality, a standard topic in linear and nonlinear partial differential equations, which has many facets. In particular, a widely used method is to look at the evolution in time of the Sobolev norms (
1.4
) of solutions: an unbounded growth in time of the positive Sobolev norms is an indicator of energy transfer phenomena, in the form of energy cascades towards higher frequencies. A class of equations that we will study consists of small-in-size, time dependent perturbations of Morse-Smale transport equations on compact manifolds (see equation (
1.3
)). However, since our analysis is motivated by applications to the dynamics of integrable partial differential equations and recent results in KAM theory, we begin by giving an application of our main theorem in this direction.

Let
n\geq 1
and denote by
\mathbb{T}^{n+1}
(with
{\mathbb{T}}:=\mathbb{R}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
) the Euclidean
(n+1)
-dimensional torus. Given

and

\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=\left(\nu+{\varepsilon}V(\omega t,x)\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}u_{\varepsilon}+\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2}\text{div}_{x}(V(\omega t,x))u_{\varepsilon},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in{\mathbb{T}}^{n},\ t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ {\varepsilon}>0,

(1.1)

where

is a time-dependent vector field and where

denotes the divergence with respect to the standard Euclidean volume on

. In particular, note immediately that the transport operator on the right-hand side of the equation is formally skew adjoint on

\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}}=\|u_{\varepsilon}(0)\|_{L^{2}},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \forall{\varepsilon}\geq 0.

Moreover, it is worth noting that, for

\|u_{0}(t)\|_{\sigma}=\|u_{0}(0)\|_{\sigma},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \forall\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}},\

where

fall in what is usually called

In the present work, we consider instead a

1.1
Morse-Smale transport equations on manifolds

We consider a smooth (

), compact and oriented manifold

\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}={\mathcal{L}}_{V+{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{P}}(t)}u_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\text{div}_{\upsilon_{g}}(V+{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{P}}(t))u_{\varepsilon},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in M,\ t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ {\varepsilon}>0,

(1.3)

where
V\in{\mathfrak{X}}(M)
,
{\mathcal{P}}(t)\in C^{0}_{b}({\mathbb{R}};{\mathfrak{X}}(M))

\text{div}_{\upsilon_{g}}

{\mathcal{L}}
denotes the usual lie derivative, i.e., the order one differential operator defined by

{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}u:={\rm d}u[Y],

for every
Y\in{\mathfrak{X}}(M)
, and
C^{0}_{b}({\mathbb{R}};{\mathfrak{X}}(M))
denotes the set of time dependent vector fields on
M

Note that, as in equation (
1.1

1.3

so that

\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}(M,\upsilon_{g})}=\|u_{\varepsilon}(0)\|_{L^{2}(M,\upsilon_{g})},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \forall{\varepsilon}>0.

Our main goal is to prove existence of unstable solutions for equation (
1.3

{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}
are defined as

{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}(M):=\left\{u\in L^{2}(M;{\mathbb{C}}):\left\|(1-\Delta_{g})^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}u\right\|_{L^{2}(M,\upsilon_{g})}<+\infty\right\},\hskip 10.00002pt\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}},

(1.4)

\|.\|_{\sigma}

[
36

\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{\sigma}\leq C_{\sigma}e^{C_{\sigma}|t|}\|u_{\varepsilon}(0)\|_{\sigma},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \text{for some}\ C_{\sigma}>0.

Our main theorem shows that this exponential growth is in fact sharp for the class of Morse-Smale vector fields.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
V\in{\mathfrak{X}}(M)
and
{\mathcal{P}}\in C^{0}_{b}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathfrak{X}}(M))
. Suppose that
V
is a Morse-Smale vector field (see Definition

, suppose in addition that it has at least one critical point.

Then, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that, for every
0\leq\sigma\leq\delta_{0}
and for every
0\leq{\varepsilon}\leq\delta_{0}\sigma^{2}
, one can find
v_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{H}^{\sigma}(M)
and

is the solution to equation (

The class of Morse-Smale vector fields was introduced by Smale in
[

and it forms an open subset of

which contains an open and dense subset of all gradient vector fields. Yet note that the corresponding flow may have finitely many closed hyperbolic orbits that are not reduced to a point. We refer to Section

The overall strategy in order to prove Theorem

and into combining this construction with microlocal tools like Gårding inequality. Such a strategy yields the exponential growth result thanks to a positive commutator type argument. Hence the main difficulty when proving Theorem

is of dynamical nature and it lies in the construction of this escape function. The key dynamical ingredient to deal with this problem is issued from the microlocal point of view that was introduced in
[
21
]
to study the correlation (or Ruelle) spectrum of dynamical systems with hyperbolic behavior and that was further developped in

37

among others. See also
[

[
29
]
for a recent book describing these microlocal methods and some of their applications to dynamical systems.

One of the key steps when considering these spectral problems is also the existence of an escape function for the lifted symplectic dynamics or the use of the related radial estimates. In that spectral set-up, this leads to the construction of (microlocal) functional spaces adapted to the spectral analysis of operators like
\mathcal{L}_{V}
and we will adapt these constructions to fit the PDE problem at hand in this article. We emphasize that, in view of our application to Theorem

This requires to pay attention when adapting the dynamical results from these references and, in particular, it seems that the anisotropic Sobolev norms from the above works cannot be used directly to deal with our problem except for

Remark 1.3

Prior to these microlocal methods involving the construction of an escape function, other (more geometric) functional spaces adapted to hyperbolic dynamics were developed in

1.2
Comparison with the Schrödinger case

We conclude this introduction by briefly discussing the related question of Sobolev instabilities for time dependent perturbations of Schrödinger equations,

Organization of the article.

We now quickly describe the structure of the article. As already alluded, the core of our proof is the construction of an escape function for the transport operator in the Morse-Smale case. This requires a detailed description of the Hamiltonian flow induced by its principal symbol. Thus we devote Section
2
to a brief reminder of Morse-Smale dynamics and their symplectic lifts and then Section

to the construction of the corresponding escape function. In Section

by a positive commutator argument making use of the escape function from Section

. Once this is done, we prove Theorem

in Section

A

{\rm d}h(Y)=\omega({\mathcal{X}}_{h},Y),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\ Y\in{\mathfrak{X}}(M).

(2.6)

The upcoming definitions and results in this paragraph hold for any vector field, not necessarily Morse-Smale and we denote by
\pi:(x,\xi)\in T^{*}M\mapsto x\in M
the canonical projection.

Definition 2.7

(Symplectic lift)
.

Let
V\in{\mathfrak{X}}(M)
and let
\varphi^{t}(x)
be a flow on
M
.

I)

The symplectic lift of
V
to
T^{*}M
is the vector field
Y\in{\mathfrak{X}}(T^{*}M)
such that

a)

{\rm d}\pi(Y)=V
;

b)

the vector field
Y
satisfies
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}\omega=0.

II)

The symplectic lift of a
flow
\varphi^{t}
on
M
is the flow
\Phi^{t}
on
T^{*}M
such that

a)

\pi\circ\Phi^{t}=\varphi^{t}\circ\pi
, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, as maps on
T^{*}M
;

b)

\left(\Phi^{t}\right)^{*}\omega=\omega,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}},

where
∗
denotes the pullback of
\omega
with respect to
\Phi^{t}
.

In order to write down explicitly the relation between a flow on
M
and its symplectic lift on
T^{*}M
, we recall the following definition.

Definition 2.8

(Transposed operator)
.

Let
\Phi:TM\to TM
and set
\pi_{0}:(x,v)\in TM\mapsto x\in M
. We define the transposed map
\Phi^{\top}
as

\Phi^{\top}:T^{*}M\to T^{*}M,\hskip 10.00002pt\Phi^{\top}(\xi)(\mathrm{w}):=\xi(\Phi(\mathrm{w})),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\xi\in{T^{*}_{\pi_{0}\circ\Phi(\mathrm{w})}M},\ \mathrm{w}\in TM.

(2.7)

The following Lemma explicits the relation between a vector field
V(x)
and the Hamiltonian vector field
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
on
T^{*}M
, as defined in (
2.5
) and (
2.6
). We only state the next result, since the proof is standard and follows directly applying Definitions
2.7
and
2.8
.

Lemma 2.9
.

For every
V\in{\mathfrak{X}}(M)
, the vector field
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
defined by (
2.6
) is the symplectic lift of
V
on
T^{*}M
. Moreover, for every flow
\varphi^{t}(x)
on
M
, its symplectic lift is given by

\Phi^{t}(x,\xi)=\left(\varphi^{t}(x),\left[{\rm d}_{x}\varphi^{t}(x)\right]^{-\top}\xi\right),

(2.8)

where
\left[{\rm d}_{x}\varphi^{t}(x)\right]^{-\top}
is the inverse of the transposed operator of
{\rm d}_{x}\varphi^{t}(x)
, as in Definition
2.8
.

In particular remark that, as an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.9
, the flow of the Hamiltonian vector field
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
on
T^{*}M
, with
h
in (
2.5
), coincides with the lift of the flow
\varphi^{t}_{V}
. Thus we will use the notation
\Phi^{t}_{V}(x,\xi)
for such flow, whose explicit expression is the one in (
2.8
).

2.3
Dynamics of Morse-Smale symplectic lifts

In this paragraph, we take
V\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{MS}(M)
and we describe the dynamics of the Hamiltonian flow induced by the Hamiltonian vector field
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
, with
h:T^{*}M\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined in (
2.5
). In particular, we describe global attractors and repellors for the lifted flow on
T^{*}M
and some of their properties, using that
V
is Morse-Smale.
We closely follow
[
12
]
, to which we refer for the proof of most results.

Let
S^{*}M
denote the unit cotangent bundle of
M
:

S^{*}M:=\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}M\ :\ \left\|\xi\right\|_{x}=1\},

(2.9)

where
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{x}
is the norm on the cotangent space at the point
x
,
T^{*}_{x}M
, induced by the metric
g_{x}
.
The flow
\Phi^{t}_{V}
of
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
on
T^{*}M
, which is explicitly given in (
2.8
), induces a projected flow on
S^{*}M
:

\tilde{\Phi}_{V}^{t}(x,\xi):=\left(\varphi^{t}_{V}(x),\frac{\left[{\rm d}_{x}\varphi^{t}_{V}(x)\right]^{-\top}\xi}{\left\|\left[{\rm d}_{x}\varphi^{t}_{V}(x)\right]^{-\top}\xi\right\|_{x}}\right),

(2.10)

and we denote by
\tilde{{\mathcal{X}}}_{h}
the induced smooth vector field on
S^{*}M
, which is the generator of
\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{V}
.

We now identify global attractors and repellors for such flows on
S^{*}M
. To this aim, we start with the general definition of
conormal bundle
of a submanifold. Let
S\subset M
be a smooth submanifold of
M
. We define the submanifold
N^{*}S
of
T^{*}M
as

N^{*}S:=\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}M\mbox{ such that }x\in S,\ \xi\neq 0,\ \xi(\mathrm{w})=0,\ \forall\ \mathrm{w}\in T_{x}S\},

(2.11)

and we call it the conormal bundle of
S
. We define the following sets:

R_{p}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}N^{*}(W^{s}(\Lambda_{i}))\cap S^{*}M,\hskip 10.00002ptA_{p}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}N^{*}(W^{u}(\Lambda_{i}))\cap S^{*}M.

(2.12)

Next we recall that every stable and unstable manifold is foliated by a family of submanifolds (see Definition
A.6
):

W^{u}(\Lambda_{i})=\bigcup_{x\in\Lambda_{i}}W^{uu}(x),\hskip 20.00003ptW^{s}(\Lambda_{i})=\bigcup_{x\in\Lambda_{i}}W^{ss}(x),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall 1\leq i\leq K.

(2.13)

On the one hand, this foliation is trivial for critical points. On the other hand, for every
x\in\Lambda_{i}
with
\Lambda_{i}
closed orbit,
W^{ss}(x)
has codimension one in
W^{s}(\Lambda_{i})
and analogously
W^{uu}(x)
in
W^{u}(\Lambda_{i})
. As we shall see, they also turn out to be relevant for the dynamics. Hence, we define

R^{r}_{p}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}\bigcup_{x\in\Lambda_{i}}N^{*}(W^{ss}(x))\cap S^{*}M,\hskip 10.00002ptA^{a}_{p}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}\bigcup_{x\in\Lambda_{i}}N^{*}(W^{uu}(x))\cap S^{*}M.

(2.14)

Remark 2.10
.

The transversality assumption in Definition
2.2
can be written explicitely as follows: for any
(i,j)\in\{1,\ldots,K\}^{2}
, for any
x\in W^{s}(\Lambda_{i})\cap W^{u}(\Lambda_{j})
we have

T_{x}M=T_{x}W^{s}(\Lambda_{i})+T_{x}W^{u}(\Lambda_{j}).

(2.15)

Using this decomposition of
T_{x}M
, one can verify that transversality implies

R_{p}\cap A_{p}=\emptyset,\hskip 10.00002ptR_{p}\cap A^{a}_{p}=\emptyset,\hskip 10.00002ptR^{r}_{p}\cap A_{p}=\emptyset.

(2.16)

We are now in position to describe the dynamics of
\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{V}(x,\xi)
in (
2.10
) on
S^{*}M
.

Proposition 2.11

(
[
12
]
, Lemma 5.1, Theorem 5.2)
.

Let
V\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{MS}(M)
. Then, the sets
R_{p}
,
A_{p}
,
R^{r}_{p}
,
A^{a}_{p}
in (
2.12
) and (
2.14
) are compact subsets of
S^{*}M
. Moreover, denoting by
{\bf d}
the induced Riemannian distance on
S^{*}M
, the following holds:

\mbox{ for every }(x,\xi)\in S^{*}M\setminus A_{p}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ we have }\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{t\to-\infty}{\bf d}(\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{V}(x,\xi),R^{r}_{p})=0,

(2.17)

\mbox{ for every }(x,\xi)\in S^{*}M\setminus R^{r}_{p}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ we have }\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{t\to+\infty}{\bf d}(\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{V}(x,\xi),A_{p})=0,

(2.18)

and

\mbox{ for every }(x,\xi)\in S^{*}M\setminus R_{p}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ we have }\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{t\to+\infty}{\bf d}(\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{V}(x,\xi),A^{a}_{p})=0,

(2.19)

\mbox{ for every }(x,\xi)\in S^{*}M\setminus A^{a}_{p}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ we have }\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{t\to-\infty}{\bf d}(\tilde{\Phi}^{t}_{V}(x,\xi),R_{p})=0.

(2.20)

We refer to
[
12
]
for the proof of this statement, which involves a careful study of the Morse-Smale structure of the flow induced by
V
on
M
. Strictly speaking, the proof in that reference is given under the extra assumption that the vector field is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-linearizable near critical elements. Yet, this hypothesis is removed by Meddane in
[
37
, Lemma 3.2, Prop. 3.3]
where he deals with general Axiom A flows satisfying the corresponding transversality property. We emphasize that one of the main technical issue in proving this statement is the compactness property. Finally, for later purpose, we define:

A:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}N^{*}(W^{s}(\Lambda_{i})),\hskip 20.00003ptR:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}N^{*}(W^{u}(\Lambda_{i})).

(2.21)

Analogously we define
A^{a}
and
R^{r}
as subsets of
T^{*}M\setminus\{0\}
, obtained extending by homogeneity the projective ones in (
2.14
).

3
Construction of the escape function

We are now in position to construct the main dynamical ingredient for the proof of Theorem
1.2
: an
escape (or Lyapunov) function
for the Hamiltonian
h(x,\xi)
defined in (
2.5
). Roughly speaking, we are looking for a function that increases along the flow lines of
\Phi_{V}^{t}
in view of applying a positive commutators argument in our analysis of the transport equation. Before stating the main dynamical result we are aiming at, we start by giving a general definition of escape functions in our context.

Definition 3.1

(Positively homogeneous function)
.

We say that a smooth function
f:T^{*}M\setminus\{0\}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a positively homogeneous function of degree
\sigma\geq 0
if

f(x,\lambda\xi)=\lambda^{\sigma}f(x,\xi),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\lambda>0,\ (x,\xi)\neq 0.

Definition 3.2

(Escape function)
.

Let
h:T^{*}M\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the Hamiltonian function defined in (
2.5
). We say that a smooth function
a:T^{*}M\setminus\{0\}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is an escape function of order
\sigma>0
for
h
if
a
is positively homogeneous of degree
\sigma>0
and if there exist
\delta>0
and a closed conic set
E\subset T^{*}M\setminus\{0\}
such that

{\mathcal{X}}_{h}(a)\geq\delta\left\|\xi\right\|_{x}^{\sigma},\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ on }\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}M\setminus E\},

(3.1)

{\mathcal{X}}_{h}(a)\geq 0\mbox{ and }a(x,\xi)\geq\delta\left\|\xi\right\|^{\sigma}_{x},\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ on }\{(x,\xi)\in E\},

(3.2)

where we recall the notation
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}
in (
2.6
) for the Hamiltonian vector field defined by
h
.

Remark 3.3
.

We remark that, if
h
is a positively homogeneous function of degree
\sigma_{1}
and
a
is a positively homogeneous function of degree
\sigma_{2}
, then
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}(a)
is a positively homogeneous function of degree
\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}-1
. In particular this means that, in Definition
3.2
, both
{\mathcal{X}}_{h}(a)
and
a
are homogeneous of degree
\sigma
.

The main result of this section is the following Proposition:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
V\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{MS}(M)
be a Morse-Smale vector field on
M
and let
h(x,\xi):=\xi(V(x))
. Then there exist a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
\operatorname{NW}(V)
(see
A.3
),
c_{0}>0
and
0<\sigma_{0}<1
, depending on
V
such that, for every
0<\sigma<\sigma_{0}
,
h
admits an escape function
a
of order
\sigma
in the sense of Definition
3.2
, with
E
being a closed conic neighborhood of
A^{a}\cap R^{r}\cap T^{*}\mathcal{U}
and with
\delta=c_{0}\sigma
. Moreover, if
n\geq 2
or if
V
has at least one critical point, there exists a nonempty open conic subset
C
satisfying

a(x,\xi)\leq-\frac{c_{0}}{2}\|\xi\|_{x}^{\sigma}\ \text{on}\ \{(x,\xi)\in C\}.

(3.3)

Remark 3.5
.

Observe that when
V
has no closed orbits,
A^{a}\cap R^{r}
is empty and our proof will in fact show that
E
is empty.

In order to prove Proposition
3.4
, we will use all the dynamical ingredients introduced in Section
2
. We will construct an escape function
a\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}M\setminus\{0\})
of the form

a(x,\xi):=m(x,\xi)f(x,\xi),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(x,\xi)\in T^{*}M\setminus\{0\},

(3.4)

where
m\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}M\setminus\{0\})
is a positively homogeneous function of degree zero (recall Definition
3.1
) that we call
order function
following the literature (see e.g.
[
21
]
) and
f(x,\xi)
is a positively homogeneous function of degree
\sigma>0
, with
\sigma
to be determined depending on the vector field. Our construction is close to the one appearing in the microlocal approach to Ruelle resonances in dynamical systems and we refer for instance to
[
21
,
12
,
37
]
for presentations close to ours. The main difference with these references is that we pick
f
to be
\sigma
-homogeneous while these earlier works use symbols of order
\sigma
for any
\sigma>0
. Roughly speaking, they use
\log(1+\|\xi\|_{x}^{2})
while we use
\|\xi\|_{x}^{\sigma}
. This is due to the different nature of the problems at hand: growth of Sobolev norms for time dependent vector fields in our case compared with the spectral study that is considered in these previous contributions.

We will first construct the function
m
that will strictly decay away from the repellors and attractors of the lifted flow. A similar construction has been already achieved in
[
12
]
in the case of Morse-Smale flows. In view of our application, the only thing we need to pay attention to, compared with this reference, is to specify the values of
m
near the various subsets
A_{p}
,
A^{a}_{p}
,
R_{p}
and
R_{p}^{r}
. This is the content of paragraph
3.1
and more specifically of Lemma
3.8
. Once this is done, we will construct in paragraph
3.2
the function
f
and show that it has the expected properties (up to signs issues) near the attractors and repellors. We will also verify that its Poisson bracket with
h
behaves nicely away from these sets. Once this is done, we will gather these two functions as in (
3.4
) and prove Proposition
3.4
in paragraph
3.3
.

4
Instability and growth of Sobolev norms

We are now in position to prove Theorem
1.2
. The two main ingredients we use are the existence of an escape function for the Hamiltonian
h(x,\xi)=\xi(V(x))
with
V
a Morse-Smale vector field on
M
(proven in Proposition
3.4
) and some results of symbolic calculus on
M
. For this reason, before proving Theorem
1.2
, we will start this section by reviewing some preliminary material on pseudodifferential operators in paragraph
4.1
. We refer to
[
48
, Ch. 14]
and
[
16
, App. E]
for an introduction to pseudodifferential calculus on compact manifolds. See also
[
11
, Appendix A2, A3]
for a brief presentation close to ours.

Once these preliminary tools are settled, we introduce a generalized transport equation (

) in paragraph

for which we will prove existence of solutions whose positive Sobolev norms grow exponentially. This is achieved in two steps. We introduce first in paragraph

).

4.1
Pseudodifferential operators

Pseudodifferential operators in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

We start by giving here some results of symbolic calculus in the case of pseudodifferential operators on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, as the case of manifolds will be locally modeled on it. First, we recall the definition of the set of symbols of order

{\bf S}^{\rho}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n}):=\{a\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n}):\forall\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}\ |\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}a(x,\xi)|\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}\left\langle\xi\right\rangle^{\rho-|\beta|}\}

\operatorname{Op^{w}}(a)[u]:=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{{\rm i}(x-y)\xi}a\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\right)u(y){\rm d}y{\rm d}\xi.

hold. We refer for example to
[

]
for proofs.

\operatorname{Op^{w}}(a_{i})
is defined as in (

2.

for every

is bounded from

(recall (

We denote by

the class of pseudodifferential operators of order
\rho
on

It is non-trivial to associate to each symbol in
{\bf S}^{\rho}(T^{*}M)
an operator in

and vice versa (see
[

, Theorem 14.1]
). Following
[
25
, Sect. 4]
(see also
[
11
, App. A]
) and in view of simplifying some aspects of the discussion, we now assume that the atlas in (

) is an isochore atlas in the sense of the following definition:

Definition 4.5

(Isochore atlas)
.

We say that the atlas
\{({\mathcal{U}}_{i},\gamma_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
is
isochore
if

\gamma_{i}^{*}({\rm d}\mu_{g})=\mbox{Leb}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\ i=1,\ldots,N,

(4.10)

where
{\rm d}\mu_{g}
is the Riemmanian volume form on
M
and Leb is the Lebesgue measure.

Roughly speaking, Definition
4.5

can be rephrased saying that an atlas is isochore if, for every change of coordinates between two patches
{\mathcal{U}}_{i}
and
{\mathcal{U}}_{j}
, the determinant of the Jacobian matrix related to such transformation is equal to one. It is a general result that there always exists an isochore atlas on a compact smooth and oriented Riemmanian manifold (see
[
39
]
). Next, we fix a partition of unity
\{\chi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
subordinated to the atlas, i.e.,

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}^{2}(x)=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall x\in M,\ \mbox{ and }\ \chi_{i}\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathcal{U}}_{i};[0,1]),\,\forall i=1,\ldots,N.

(4.11)

Finally, following
[
48
, Ch. 14]
, we can define the quantization of a symbol
a\in{\bf S}^{\rho}(T^{*}M)
:

\operatorname{Op^{M}}(a):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}\gamma_{i}^{*}\operatorname{Op^{w}}(({\widetilde{\gamma}}_{i}^{-1})^{*}({\widetilde{\chi}}_{i}a))(\gamma_{i}^{-1})^{*}\chi_{i},

(4.12)

where we recall the notation in (
4.6
) for
{\widetilde{\gamma_{i}}}
and where
{\widetilde{\chi}}_{i}\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathcal{U}}_{i};[0,1])
is such that
{\widetilde{\chi}}_{i}\chi_{i}=\chi_{i}
.

Remark 4.6
.

Let us briefly motivate the expression in (
4.12
). Given a symbol
a\in{\bf S}^{\rho}(T^{*}M)
, in order to define its quantization we need to use its expression in local charts to rewrite it as sum of symbols over
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Indeed, each
\{({\widetilde{\gamma}}_{i}^{-1})^{*}(\chi_{i}a)\}_{i=1}^{N}
is a symbol over
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. Thus we can quantize it using the Weyl quantization over
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(see (
4.2
)), and then go back to the manifold localizing in the desired chart, thanks to the composition with
(\gamma_{i}^{-1})^{*}\chi_{i}
and its inverse.

We now state the main result related to this choice of the quantization and of an isochore atlas: roughly speaking, the quantization of a symbol is independent on the choice of charts, up to lower order remainders. Precisely, we have the following result (see
[
25
]
or
[
11
, Remark A.2]
, to which we refer for a proof):

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
a\in{\bf S}^{\rho}(T^{*}M)
be a symbol of order
\rho
over
M
and let
\{({\mathcal{U}}_{i},\gamma_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
be an isochore atlas in the sense of Definition
4.5
. Let
\operatorname{Op^{M}}(a)
be the quantization of
a
in the sense of (
4.12
). Then the principal symbol of
\operatorname{Op^{M}}(a)
is well defined and independent of the chart, up to symbols in
{\bf S}^{\rho-2}(M)
.

We also record the following useful properties (see
[
11
, Remark A.3]
for a proof of the second point):

Lemma 4.8
.

1.

Let

be a smooth function on

and
\operatorname{Op^{M}}(f)\in\Psi^{0}(M)

\operatorname{Op^{M}}(f)u(x):=f(x)u(x),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall x\in M,\,\forall u\in C^{\infty}(M).

Let

be a smooth vector field on

{\mathcal{L}}_{X}u(x)=\operatorname{Op^{M}}(i\xi(X(x))+r(x))u(x),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall u\in C^{\infty}(M),

(4.13)

where

is a smooth function on

We now state the main properties of pseudodifferential operators that we will need in our proof. We proceed in analogy with the ones of Lemma
4.1
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
a\in{\bf S}^{\rho_{1}}(T^{*}M)
and
b\in{\bf S}^{\rho_{2}}(T^{*}M)
, with
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then

1.

For any

(here

3.

(4.15)

Observe that item 2 follows from our choice of isochore charts. Finally, we also need the following Gårding inequality:

Let

,

(4.16)

We refer to

for a proof in

from which the case on

4.2
Generalized transport equations

In the rest of this section, we prove our main result, namely Theorem
1.2
. The starting point is to rewrite the transport equation (
1.3
) in terms of the symbols associated to pseudodifferential operators in view of using the above microlocal tools. Precisely, using Lemma
4.8
, we can in fact consider more generally the transport like equation:

\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=\operatorname{Op^{M}}(i\xi(V(x)+{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{P}}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)))u_{\varepsilon}+\operatorname{Op^{M}}(ib_{\varepsilon}(t,x,\xi))u_{\varepsilon},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in M,\ t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ {\varepsilon}>0,

(4.17)

where

•

V
belongs to
{\mathfrak{X}}^{MS}(M)
,

•

\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)
is a smooth time dependent vector field belonging to
\mathcal{C}^{0}_{b}({\mathbb{R}};{\mathfrak{X}}(M))
with all semi-norms uniformly bounded in terms of
0\leq{\varepsilon}\leq 1
,

•

there exists
\rho<1
such that
b_{{\varepsilon},1}(t,x,\xi):=\text{Re}\,b_{\varepsilon}(t,x,\xi)\in C^{0}_{b}({\mathbb{R}};{\bf S}^{\rho}(T^{*}M))

0\leq{\varepsilon}\leq 1
,

•

b_{{\varepsilon},2}(t,x):={\varepsilon}^{-1}\text{Im}\,b_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\in C^{0}_{b}({\mathbb{R}};{\bf S}^{0}(T^{*}M))
with all seminorms uniformly bounded in terms of

Note that the function

appearing in Theorem

would not depend on

(thanks to Lemma

) and that it would be of order

(and proportional to

). Yet, as our proof allows to handle this more general case, we consider such a general

Remark 4.11
.

According to
[
36

, one has that, for any

and for any

, the solution

exists globally in time and belongs to

when

. Here, we want to allow this extra selfadjoint term and we postpone the existence of the solution to Appendix
B
following the results from this reference and classical perturbative arguments from ordinary differential equations.

Our main result reads

Theorem 4.12
.

Suppose that the above assumptions on

,
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
and

hold and, if

has at least one critical point.

(4.18)

where

Observe that this Theorem implies Theorem

and of the order. As anticipated, the key ingredient of our proof is the existence of an escape function for

, proven in Proposition

In particular, for every choice of

(where the threshold

(recall the Definition

\tilde{a}(x,\xi):=a(x,\xi)(1-\chi(x,\xi))\in{\bf S}^{\sigma}(T^{*}M),

(4.20)

a smooth symbol associated with the escape function. We remark that, choosing

as in (

in

4.3

In this last section we prove Theorem

for the solution of equation (

Let us start by giving a scheme of the proof, in order to explain why this result comes as an application of Theorem

]
, conjugating equation (

Indeed, after the normal form procedure, the transport term

Equivalently,

Next, using Theorem

Finally, we show that, for

This is the reason why the only possible choice (through our approach) to state Theorem

V(t,x):=(V_{1}(t,x),\ldots,V_{n}(t,x)),

we use the standard compact notation for the Fourier coefficients of

:

V(t,x):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\\
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}\end{subarray}}\underbrace{v_{k,\ell}}_{\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}e^{i(k\cdot x+\ell t)},\hskip 20.00003pt\forall(t,x)\in{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{n},

(5.2)

where
\{v_{k,\ell}\}_{k,\ell}
are the Fourier coefficients of

. Then, we can define the resonant average of a function as follows.

Definition 5.1

(Resonant average)
.

Let

and recall the notation in (
5.2
) for the Fourier coefficients of
V
. For any
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}^{n}
, we define the

of

with respect to the frequency vector

as

\left\langle V\right\rangle_{\nu}(x):=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}v_{k,\nu\cdot k}e^{ik\cdot x},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in{\mathbb{T}}^{n}.

(5.3)

Remark 5.2
.

One can verify that this definition coincides with the earlier convention,

\left\langle V\right\rangle_{\nu}(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}V(t,x-\nu t){\rm d}t.

(5.4)

The main result of this section is the following Proposition:

.

the constant

4.

Remark that equation (

.

Equivalently, the behavior of the Sobolev norms of solutions to (

5.1.1
Preliminary constructions

As already explained, we drop the index
{\varepsilon}
at several places (e.g.
u,v,\Phi
instead of
u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon},\Phi_{\varepsilon}
) in view of alleviating the notations. We now need some preliminary remarks and notation that we introduce following
[
4
, Section 3.1]
. First of all, recalling the definition in (
1.4
) of the Sobolev spaces
{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}
,
\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}
, let
A(t):{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}\to{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}
be any linear invertible transformation, periodic in time of period
2\pi
. For any differential equation of the form
\partial_{t}u=Hu
, defining the function
v(t,x)
as
u=A(t)v
, one can check that
v
solves
\dot{v}=A(t)_{*}Hv
, where the pushforward
A(t)_{*}H
is given by

A(t)_{*}H:=A(t)^{-1}[HA(t)-\partial_{t}A(t)].

(5.9)

We remark, to avoid any confusion in the notation, that
\partial_{t}A(t)
is the derivative in time of the linear transformation (and not the composition
\partial_{t}\circ A(t)
).
The core of the proof of Proposition
5.3
consists in choosing the proper transformation
A(t)
and computing the corresponding pushforward in (
5.9
). To this aim, we consider a family of diffeomorphisms of the torus of the form

\varphi(t):{\mathbb{T}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{T}}^{n},\hskip 10.00002ptx\mapsto x+{\varepsilon}\beta(t,x),

(5.10)

where
\beta\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{n};{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is a function to be determined.
It has been proved (see for example
[
2
, Lemma B.4]
) that, for
{\varepsilon}>0
small enough, this diffeomorphism is invertible and its inverse has the form

\varphi(t)^{-1}:{\mathbb{T}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{T}}^{n},\hskip 10.00002pty\mapsto y+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,y),

(5.11)

for
\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{n};{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Moreover,
\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}
has all its derivatives uniformly bounded in terms of
{\varepsilon}>0
in the admissible range of
{\varepsilon}
. Note that
\beta
is chosen independently of
{\varepsilon}
in our construction and that
\varphi
depends implicitly on
{\varepsilon}
.

We define the transformation associated with (
5.10
) as:

\tilde{\Phi}(t)u(t,x):=\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}u\circ\varphi(t,x),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall u\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}};{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}({\mathbb{T}}^{n}))\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{T}},\forall\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}},

(5.12)

with inverse

\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}u(t,x):=\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}u\circ\varphi^{-1}(t,x),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall u\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}};{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}({\mathbb{T}}^{n}))\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{T}},\forall\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(5.13)

Remark that

\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{y}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,y))=\frac{1}{\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))|_{x=y+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}(t,y)}},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall y\in{\mathbb{T}}^{n}.

(5.14)

Using (
5.14
) and via a direct computation one can check that

\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{*}=\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\in{\mathbb{T}},

(5.15)

where
*
denotes the adjoint with respect to the standard inner product on
L^{2}
. Finally, defining

H(t):=(\nu+{\varepsilon}V(t,x))\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2}\textup{div}(V(t,x)),

(5.16)

where
\nu
and
V
are as in (
5.1
), (so that equation (
5.1
) reads
\dot{u}=H(t)u
), and using formula (
5.9
), we see that the pushforward of
H(t)
in (
5.16
) with respect to
\tilde{\Phi}(t)
in (
5.12
) is given by

\tilde{\Phi}(t)_{*}H=\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}H\tilde{\Phi}(t)-\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}(t).

(5.17)

The following Lemma computes explicitly this pushforward.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
H
as in (
5.16
) and
\tilde{\Phi}
as in (
5.12
). Then the order one differential operator
\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}H\tilde{\Phi}(t)
is skew adjoint and the following identities hold:

\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}H\tilde{\Phi}(t)=\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi(\nu+{\varepsilon}V(t,x)+{\varepsilon}\nu\cdot\nabla_{x}\beta+{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{1}(t,x))),

(5.18)

and

\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}(t)=\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi({\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\beta+{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{2}(t,x))),

(5.19)

with
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{1/2}(t,x)\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{n+1};{\mathbb{R}})
having all their seminorms uniformly bounded in terms of
{\varepsilon}\in[0,{\varepsilon}_{0}]
.

Here, we use the formalism of pseudodifferential operators (see section
4.1
) in order to identify the symbols of
\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}H\tilde{\Phi}(t)
and
\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}(t)
, that we will need to prove Proposition
5.3
. We remark that on the torus it is possible to use the Weyl quantization exactly as over
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and that all properties in Lemma
4.1
hold. See
[
48
, Ch. 5]
for details.

Proof.

First of all remark that, since
H(t)
defined as in (
5.16
) is skew adjoint, using (
5.15
) we have

[\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}H\tilde{\Phi}(t)]^{*}=\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{*}H^{*}(\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1})^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{unitary}}}{{=}}-\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}H\tilde{\Phi}(t),

which proves skewadjointness of
\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}H\tilde{\Phi}(t)
. Moreover, from its definition,
\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}H\tilde{\Phi}(t)
is a real valued differential operator of order one. Thus, from point 2 of Lemma
4.1
, we have

\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}H\tilde{\Phi}(t)=\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi(f_{\varepsilon}(t,x))),

(5.20)

for some
f_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{n+1})
, real valued. We now compute such a function
f_{\varepsilon}
in order to prove (
5.18
). For every
u\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{n+1})
we have

\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}H\tilde{\Phi}(t)u(t,x)=\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}[(\nu+{\varepsilon}V(t,x))\cdot\nabla_{x}(\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}u(t,x+{\varepsilon}\beta(t,x)))]\ \mbox{ mod }\Psi^{0}({\mathbb{T}}^{n+1})\\
=\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}[(\nu+{\varepsilon}V(t,x))\cdot\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))\cdot\nabla_{x}u(t,x+{\varepsilon}\beta(t,x))]\ \mbox{mod}\Psi^{0}({\mathbb{T}}^{n+1})\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\begin{subarray}{c}\eqref{Phi.inv}\\
\eqref{inv.det}\end{subarray}}}{{=}}(\nu+{\varepsilon}V(t,x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}(t,x)))(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}(t,x)))\nabla_{x}u(t,x)\quad\mbox{ mod }\quad\Psi^{0}({\mathbb{T}}^{n+1}).

Since we are interested in finding the first term of the expansion in
{\varepsilon}
of
f_{\varepsilon}
in (
5.20
), we now perform a Taylor expansion in a neighborhood of
{\varepsilon}=0
and we obtain:

\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}H\tilde{\Phi}(t)u(t,x)=[(\nu+{\varepsilon}V(t,x))(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))+O_{C^{\infty}}({\varepsilon}^{2})]\nabla_{x}u(t,x)\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ mod }\hskip 10.00002pt\Psi^{0}({\mathbb{T}}^{n+1}),

(5.21)

which gives the expansion in (
5.18
).

We now prove (
5.19
). We first show that there exists
g_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{n+1})
such that

\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}(t)=\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi g_{\varepsilon}(t,x)).

(5.22)

This proves that
\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}(t)
is a skew adjoint operator (see Lemma
4.1
). Next, we derive the explicit expression in (
5.19
).
In order to prove (
5.22
), we first remark that

\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}(t)[u](t,x)=\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}[\tilde{\Phi}(t)u](t,x)-\partial_{t}u(t,x),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall u\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{n+1}).

(5.23)

Next we compute
\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}[\tilde{\Phi}(t)u](t,x)
:

\begin{split}\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}[\tilde{\Phi}(t)u](t,x)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Phi}}}{{=}}\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}[\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}u(t,x+{\varepsilon}\beta(t,x))]\\
&=\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\left[\left(\partial_{t}\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)u(t,x+{\varepsilon}\beta(t,x))\right]\\
&+\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\left[\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial_{t}u(t,x+{\varepsilon}\beta(t,x))\right]\\
&+\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\left[\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\beta(t,x)\cdot\nabla_{x}u(t,x+{\varepsilon}\beta(t,x)))\right]\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Phi.inv}}}{{=}}\left(\partial_{t}\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)|_{y=x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}}\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}u(t,x)\\
&+\partial_{t}u(t,x)+{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\beta(t,x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))\cdot\nabla_{x}u(t,x).\end{split}

(5.24)

Thus, plugging (
5.24
) in (
5.23
), we obtain that

\begin{split}\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}(t)[u](t,x)&=\underbrace{\left(\partial_{t}\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)|_{y=x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}}\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}}_{g_{{\varepsilon},1}(t,x)}u(t,x)\\
&+\underbrace{{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\beta(t,x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))}_{g_{{\varepsilon},2}(t,x)}\cdot\nabla_{x}u(t,x).\end{split}

(5.25)

Recalling now that, from the composition rule for pseudodifferential operators (see Lemma
4.1
), for any
h(t,x)\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{n+1})
we have

h(t,x)\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{\textup{div}_{x}h(t,x)}{2}=\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi\cdot h(t,x)),

(5.26)

we are done with the proof of (
5.22
) if we show that

\frac{\textup{div}{g_{{\varepsilon},2}(t,x)}}{2}=g_{{\varepsilon},1}(t,x),

(5.27)

with
g_{{\varepsilon},1}
and
{g_{{\varepsilon},2}}
given in (
5.25
). Indeed, plugging (
5.27
) in (
5.25
) we obtain (
5.22
) with
g_{{\varepsilon}}(t,x):={\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\beta(t,x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))
. We show that (
5.27
) holds with a direct computation:

\begin{split}g_{{\varepsilon},1}(t,x)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{computation}}}{{=}}\left(\partial_{t}\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)|_{y=x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}}\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{inv.det}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta(t,x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)))\det(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left((\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta)^{-1}\partial_{t}(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\beta)\right)\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{inv.det}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left((\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}){\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\nabla_{x}\beta)\right)\end{split}

(5.28)

where for the unlabeled equality we used Jacobi identity. On the other hand we also have:

\begin{split}\textup{div}g_{{\varepsilon},2}(t,x)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{computation}}}{{=}}\textup{div}({\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\beta(t,x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)))\\
&=\text{tr}\left({\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\nabla_{y}\beta(t,y)|_{y=x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x)}(\text{Id}+{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))\right).\end{split}

(5.29)

Putting together (
5.28
) and (
5.29
), we obtain (
5.27
). Thus we have shown that

\tilde{\Phi}(t)^{-1}\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}(t)[u](t,x)=\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi\cdot{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\beta(t,x+{\varepsilon}\tilde{\beta}_{\varepsilon}(t,x))).

(5.30)

Finally, we obtain (
5.19
) performing a Taylor expansion of (
5.30
) in a neighborhood of
{\varepsilon}=0
, concluding the proof of the lemma.
∎

:

T(t,x)=\nu+{\varepsilon}V+{\varepsilon}\nu\cdot\nabla_{x}\beta-{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\beta=\nu+{\varepsilon}\sum_{(k,\ell)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n+1}}(v_{k,\ell}+{\rm i}(k\cdot\nu-\ell)\beta_{k,\ell})e^{ikx+i\ell t},

(5.37)

where
V(t,x)=\sum_{k,\ell}v_{k,\ell}e^{i(kx+\ell t)}
and
\beta(t,x)=\sum_{k,\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\beta_{k,\ell}e^{i(kx+\ell t)}
. Define

\beta_{k,\ell}:=\begin{cases}\frac{v_{k,\ell}}{i(\ell-k\cdot\nu)}&\mbox{ if }\ell\neq k\cdot\nu,\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5.38)

The corresponding
\beta
is a well defined and smooth function over
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
.
Substituting the Fourier coefficients of
\beta
(see (
5.38
)), in the expression of
T(t,x)
in (
5.37
), we get:

T(t,x)=\nu+{\varepsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell+\nu\cdot k=0\\
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\end{subarray}}v_{k,\nu\cdot k}e^{ik(x+\nu t)}=\nu+{\varepsilon}V_{R_{\nu}}(t,x),

(5.39)

where we recall the definition of
V_{R_{\nu}}
in (
5.33
). Thus recognizing that
\Phi_{*}(t)H(t)=\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi(T(t,x)+O_{C^{\infty}}({\varepsilon}^{2})))
(se (
5.37
) and (
5.36
)) and plugging (
5.39
) in (
5.36
), we obtain

\Phi_{*}(t)H(t)=\operatorname{Op^{w}}(i\xi(\nu+{\varepsilon}V_{R_{\nu}}(t,x)+\tilde{W}_{\nu,{\varepsilon}})),

where
\tilde{W}_{\nu,{\varepsilon}}
is the remainder in the order one operator. This gives (
5.32
). Remark that
{\varepsilon}_{0}
is chosen so that both (
5.11
) and the Taylor expansions hold for all
0<{\varepsilon}<{\varepsilon}_{0}
.

5.2
Genericity in dimension
2
and proof of Theorem
1.1

In this last section we conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
Fix any
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+}^{2}
and define

{\mathcal{A}}_{\nu}:=\{V\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{3};{\mathbb{R}}^{2}):\left\langle V\right\rangle_{\nu}(x)\in{\mathfrak{X}}^{MS}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})\}.

(5.40)

Recall that
C^{\infty}
is endowed with its natural Fréchet topology, i.e. the one induced by the seminorms
(\|.\|_{C^{k}})_{k\geq 0}
. One can verify that the map

V\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{3};{\mathbb{R}}^{2})\mapsto\langle V\rangle_{\nu}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}}^{2})

is linear and continuous with respect to the Fréchet topology on each space. In particular,
{\mathfrak{X}}^{MS}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
being an open subset of
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, one finds that
{\mathcal{A}}_{\nu}
is open for the Fréchet topology on
C^{\infty}
. Regarding density, we let
V_{0}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{3};{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
and we decompose it as

V_{0}(t,x)=\langle V_{0}\rangle_{\nu}(x+t\nu)+\left(V_{0}(t,x)-\langle V_{0}\rangle_{\nu}(x+t\nu)\right).

The second term on the right hand side has all its Fourier coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}\setminus\{(k\cdot\nu,k):k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\}
. In particular, it average
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\nu}
is identically zero. Now recalling that
{\mathfrak{X}}^{MS}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
is a dense subset of
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}^{2};{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, one can find
W
in
{\mathfrak{X}}^{MS}({\mathbb{T}}^{2})
arbitrarly close to
\langle V_{0}\rangle_{\nu}
. Letting

V(t,x):=W(x+t\nu)+\left(V_{0}(t,x)-\langle V_{0}\rangle_{\nu}(x+t\nu)\right),

one finds that
V
can be made arbitrarly close to
V_{0}
. This shows that
{\mathcal{A}}_{\nu}
is dense and concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
when combined with Theorem
5.5
.

Appendix A
Definitions in dynamical systems

In this appendix, we review the definitions of dynamical systems theory that we use in Sections
2
and
3
. We refer to
[
40
]
for Morse-Smale theory and to
[
8
,
24
]
for a complete review of the theory related to hyperbolic dynamical systems.
Let
V
be a smooth vector field on a smooth manifold
M
of dimension
n\geq 1
. We denote by
\varphi_{V}^{t}(x)
its flow at time
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
(see (
2.1
)).

Definition A.1
.

A point
\Lambda\in M
is a
critical point
of
V
if
V(\Lambda)=0
. We say that a critical point is
hyperbolic
if
{\rm d}V(\Lambda):T_{\Lambda}M\to T_{\Lambda}M
has eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
such that Re
(\lambda_{i})\neq 0
, for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Definition A.2
.

A point
x_{0}\in M
is a periodic point if
V(x_{0})\neq 0
, and there exists
T_{0}>0
such that
\varphi^{T_{0}}_{V}(x_{0})=x_{0}
. Moreover, a periodic point
x_{0}
is hyperbolic if
{\rm d}\varphi_{V}^{T_{0}}(x_{0})
has
1
as a simple eigenvalue and no other eigenvalues of modulus
1
. The set
\Lambda=\{\varphi_{V}^{t}(x_{0}):0\leq t\leq T_{0}\}
is then called a hyperbolic closed orbit.

Definition A.3

(Non-wandering set)
.

We say that a point
x\in M
is
wandering
if there exist some open neighborhood
{\mathcal{O}}
of
x
and a time
T_{x}>0
such that

{\mathcal{O}}\cap\left(\bigcup_{|t|>T_{x}}\varphi^{t}_{V}({\mathcal{O}})\right)=\emptyset.

We denote by
\operatorname{NW}(V)=\operatorname{NW}(\varphi_{V}^{t})
the
non-wandering set
of
V
, i.e., the union of all points
y\in M
which are not wandering.

Definition A.4

(
\alpha
and
\omega
limit sets)
.

Let
x\in M
. We define

\alpha(x):=\bigcap_{T\leq 0}\overline{\{\varphi_{V}^{t}(x):t\leq T\}},\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 10.00002pt\omega(x):=\bigcap_{T\geq 0}\overline{\{\varphi_{V}^{t}(x):t\geq T\}}.

We remark that, if
x\in\Lambda_{i}
, with
\Lambda_{i}\in\operatorname{Crit}(V)
(see (
2.2
)), then
\alpha(x)=\omega(x)=\Lambda_{i}
.

Definition A.5

(Stable and unstable manifolds)
.

Let
K
be a closed invariant subset of
M
. We define the stable and unstable manifolds associated with
K
as

W^{s}(K):=\{x\in M:\omega(x)\subset K\}\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{ and }\hskip 10.00002ptW^{u}(K):=\{x\in M:\alpha(x)\subset K\}.

Definition A.6
.

Let
\Lambda\in\text{Crit}(V)
be a hyperbolic closed orbit of period
T_{0}
. The stable and unstable manifolds
W^{s/u}(\Lambda)
are foliated by the family of smooth submanifolds of codimension one
\{W^{ss/uu}(x_{0})\}_{x_{0}\in\Lambda}
, where

W^{ss}(x_{0}):=\{x\in M\ :\lim_{n\to+\infty}\varphi^{nT_{0}}(x)=x_{0}\},

and

W^{uu}(x_{0}):=\{x\in M\ :\lim_{n\to+\infty}\varphi^{-nT_{0}}(x)=x_{0}\}.

We remark that the same foliation can be trivially defined also in the case of critical points, for which
W^{ss/uu}(x_{0})=W^{s/u}(x_{0})
.

Appendix B
Existence of solutions to transport equations

In this appendix, we discuss the existence of solutions to (
4.17
). Recall that the main difference with
[
36
]
is the presence of a non-skew adjoint term
{\varepsilon}\operatorname{Op^{M}}(b_{{\varepsilon},2})
in the right-hand side of the equation with
b_{{\varepsilon},2}(t)
being real valued, lying in
\mathcal{C}^{0}_{b}({\mathbb{R}},{\bf S}^{0})
and having all its seminorms uniformly bounded in terms of
0\leq{\varepsilon}\leq 1
and
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. To deal with this issue, we will apply a perturbative argument compared with the results from this reference. For the sake of simplicity, we set

\mathcal{B}_{0}(t)=\operatorname{Op^{M}}(i\xi(V(x)+{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}(t,x))+ib_{{\varepsilon},1}),\ \text{and}\ \mathcal{R}_{0}(t)={\varepsilon}\operatorname{Op^{M}}(b_{{\varepsilon},2}).

We denote by
\mathcal{U}_{0}(t,s)\in\mathcal{C}^{0}({\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}};\mathcal{L}({\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}))
the flow associated with
\mathcal{B}_{0}(t)
. Recall from
[
36
, Th. 1.2]
that it satisfies

\|\mathcal{U}_{0}(t,s)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}}\leq C_{\sigma}e^{C_{\sigma}|t-s|}

(B.1)

for every
\sigma\geq 0
and
t,s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and that it is unitary in
L^{2}
(i.e., for
\sigma=0
). Thanks to Duhamel’s principle, the equation we are interested in can be rewritten under an integral form, for every
t
in
{\mathbb{R}}
,

u_{\varepsilon}(t)=\mathcal{U}_{0}(t,0)u_{\varepsilon}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{U}_{0}(t,\tau)\mathcal{R}_{0}(\tau)u_{\varepsilon}(\tau)d\tau.

Hence, we fix
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}
,
T>0
and we consider the functional

F:u\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([-T,T];{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma})\mapsto\mathcal{U}_{0}(t,0)u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{U}_{0}(t,\tau)\mathcal{R}_{0}(\tau)u(\tau)d\tau\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([-T,T];{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}).

Our goal is to prove the existence of a fixed point
F(v)=v
in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}
. To do that, we proceed by induction and we set

v_{0}=u_{0},\hskip 10.00002ptv_{n+1}=F(v_{n}).

One has

\|v_{n+1}(t)-v_{n}(t)\|_{\sigma}\leq C_{\sigma,T}\int_{0}^{t}\|v_{n}(\tau)-v_{n-1}(\tau)\|_{\sigma}d\tau,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall-T\leq t\leq T,\ \forall n\in{\mathbb{N}},

where we used (
B.1
) and the property that
\mathcal{R}_{0}(\tau):{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma}
is a bounded operator thanks to (
4.3
). We remark that
C_{\sigma,T}>0
depends only on
\sigma
,
T
and the norms of
\mathcal{B}_{0}
and
\mathcal{R}_{0}
. By induction, one finds

\|v_{n+1}(t)-v_{n}(t)\|_{\sigma}\leq\frac{(C_{\sigma,T}t)^{n}}{n!}\sup_{\tau\in[-T,T]}\|v_{1}(\tau)-v_{0}(\tau)\|_{\sigma},

from which we can infer that
(v_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{C}^{0}([-T,T],{\mathcal{H}}^{\sigma})
. By continuity of the functional
F
, we find that the limit
v_{\infty}
satisfies
F(v_{\infty})=v_{\infty}
. The same argument shows that the solution is unique. As
T
can be chosen arbitrarily, we find that there exists an unique solution to (
4.17
) that is defined on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
