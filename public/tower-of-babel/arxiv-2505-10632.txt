Measuring Emission Lines with JWST-MegaScience Medium-Bands: A New Window into Dust and Star Formation at Cosmic Noon
Brian Lorenz, Katherine A. Suess, Mariska Kriek, Sedona H. Price, Joel Leja, Erica Nelson, Hakim Atek, Rachel Bezanson, Gabriel Brammer, Sam E. Cutler, Pratika Dayal, Anna de Graaff, Jenny E. Greene, Lukas J. Furtak, Ivo Labbé, Danilo Marchesini, Michael V. Maseda, Tim B. Miller, Abby Mintz, Ikki Mitsuhashi, Richard Pan, Natalia Porraz Barrera, Bingjie Wang (王冰洁), John R. Weaver, Christina C. Williams, Katherine E. Whitaker

Abstract
We demonstrate the power of JWST-NIRCam medium-band photometry to measure emission line fluxes and study dust and star formation properties of galaxies at cosmic noon. In this work, we present photometric emission line measurements and spatially-resolved maps of H ​ α \textrm{H}\alpha and Pa ​ β \textrm{Pa}\beta for a sample of 14 galaxies at 1.3 ≤ z ≤ 2.4 1.3\leq z\leq 2.4 , observed by the MegaScience medium-band survey and the UNCOVER deep spectroscopic survey. We measure line fluxes directly from the medium-band photometry and compare with spectroscopic measurements from UNCOVER. We find reasonable agreement between the photometric and spectroscopic emission line fluxes for both H ​ α \textrm{H}\alpha and Pa ​ β \textrm{Pa}\beta , with scatter < 0.15 <0.15 dex down to emission line equivalent widths of 10 10 Å. We also make a nebular dust measurement from the ratio Pa ​ β \textrm{Pa}\beta / H ​ α \textrm{H}\alpha , finding an average nebular A V A_{\mathrm{V}} of 1.4. Our photometric A V A_{\mathrm{V}} measurements show a slightly larger scatter of 0.5 0.5 magnitudes when compared to spectroscopic measurements; however, this scatter may be partially caused by aperture effects. Finally, we produce spatially resolved maps of H ​ α \textrm{H}\alpha emission, Pa ​ β \textrm{Pa}\beta emission, and stellar continuum. We find that offsets in H ​ α \textrm{H}\alpha and Pa ​ β \textrm{Pa}\beta emission are common, especially for galaxies with the highest A V A_{\mathrm{V}} , indicating dusty sub-structures. Furthermore, the correlation between H ​ α \textrm{H}\alpha and continuum emission decreases with increasing A V A_{\mathrm{V}} , suggesting that the dustiest objects have clumpy dust and star formation distributions. Our study demonstrates the power of medium-band photometry to directly probe emission line strengths, star formation, and dust attenuation for hundreds of galaxies in UNCOVER and thousands of galaxies in upcoming JWST medium-band surveys.



I
Introduction

Understanding dust is essential to accurately understand observations of distant galaxies. Measurements of star formation rate (SFR), the shape of galaxy spectral energy distributions (SEDs), and the stellar mass growth of galaxies over time are all significantly influenced by dust attenuation
(
Calzetti 2001
)
. In order to better interpret observations, we must understand both the total quantity and spatial distribution of dust in distant galaxies.

However, dust at cosmic noon is still not fully understood. The amount of dust in galaxies tends to increase with increasing stellar mass and SFR
(
Garn & Best 2010
;
Domínguez et al. 2013
;
Price et al. 2014
;
Whitaker et al. 2017
;
Cullen et al. 2018
;
Battisti et al. 2022
;
Shapley et al. 2022
;
Runco et al. 2022
;
Maheson et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, but may not be controlled by these properties alone. The shape of the dust attenuation curve is still uncertain, as well as which factors influence this shape
(
Kriek & Conroy 2013
;
Reddy et al. 2015
;
Reddy et al. 2020
;
Salmon et al. 2016
;
Salim & Narayanan 2020
;
Shivaei et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
. Additionally,
z=2
star-forming galaxies tend to have messy, clumpy morphologies
(
Elmegreen et al. 2007
;
Swinbank et al. 2010
;
Tadaki et al. 2014
)
, and so the geometry of dust in these systems is not well understood. Dusty, submillimeter-detected galaxies at cosmic noon have equally complex dust geometries
(
Gillman et al. 2024
)
, with visible dust lanes that seem to trace the NIR stellar continuum
(
Price et al. 2025
)
. A recent study of dusty star-forming galaxies measured their attenuation curves, finding that they have lower
R_{V}
than expected at bluer wavelengths
(
Cooper et al. 2025
)
. Previous studies indicate that dust may be clumped in small spherically symmetric regions rather than spread throughout the ISM
(
Reddy et al. 2015
;
Lorenz et al. 2023
;
Lorenz et al. 2024
)
, and may also exist in large, kiloparsec-scale star-forming clumps
(
Schreiber et al. 2011
;
Wuyts et al. 2012
)
. Finally, studies have probed the geometry of dust and star formation at cosmic noon with spatially resolved maps
(
Nelson et al. 2016
;
Tacchella et al. 2018
;
Matharu et al. 2023
)
suggesting higher dust-obscured star formation in the centers, but the full picture of where dust and star formation occur within these distant galaxies remains uncertain.

One of the best ways to examine dust is through spectroscopic ratios of hydrogen emission lines, as their intrinsic ratios are well-understood assuming case B recombination, so any deviations from these ratios are caused by dust
(
Reddy et al. 2023
)
. Unfortunately, spectroscopy of galaxies is typically observationally expensive, requiring long observations of a limited number of objects in a field. Alternatively, dust can be studied through stellar population synthesis modeling of galaxy photometry
(
Leja et al. 2017
;
Johnson et al. 2021
, e.g.
Prospector
,)
, but many assumptions go into modeling galaxy SEDs, including the age-dust-metallicity degeneracy
(
Papovich et al. 2001
)
, causing larger uncertainties for their dust contents. It is also possible to study dust through its infrared emission
(
Boquien & Salim 2021
)
, but for most galaxies, this emission is difficult to observe. These observational challenges have made it difficult to collect large samples of high-quality dust measurements at cosmic noon.

Fortunately, with the launch of the James Webb Space Telescope
(
Gardner et al. 2023
, JWST,)
, observers now have access to another method to directly study dust quantity and geometry: medium-band photometry with NIRCam
(
Rieke et al. 2023
)
. The JWST medium-bands have typical widths of 0.2
\mu
m, which are narrow enough to contain only one or two strong emission lines in their bands at specific redshifts. Additionally, the full suite of NIRCam medium bands densely samples wavelength space, allowing for a local continuum measurement to be made near each strong emission line. Instead of relying on spectroscopy, observers are now able to measure emission line fluxes directly from photometry
(
Geach et al. 2008
;
Roberts-Borsani et al. 2021
;
Simmonds et al. 2023
;
Withers et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
, allowing for larger-scale surveys of dust attenuation at cosmic noon. Additionally, the infrared capabilities of JWST allow us to push these emission line measurements to even higher redshifts, reaching out to cosmic noon. Since they are such a powerful tool, a number of medium-band surveys have already been conducted (e.g., JEMS,
Williams et al. 2023
; CANUCS/Technicolor,
Willott et al. 2023
; JOF,
Eisenstein et al. 2023
; MegaScience,
Suess et al. 2024
), with more planned in Cycle 4 (e.g.PID 7814 MINERVA and PID 8559 SPAM)

In this work, we demonstrate a method for measuring
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission line fluxes from photometry alone at
1.3<z<2.4
, the redshift range that contains both emission lines in the medium-bands. This sample is a pilot study that contains 14 galaxies that have both medium-band photometry from MegaScience
(
Suess et al. 2024
)
and spectroscopy from UNCOVER
(
Bezanson et al. 2024
;
Price et al. 2024
)
to assess the accuracy of this technique, similar to
Withers et al. 2023
. We show that the photometric emission line fluxes are in good agreement with the spectroscopy. In combination with the low photometric errors, this implies that this technique is a promising way to conduct much larger surveys of SFR and dust at cosmic noon. Additionally, we use the same technique for measuring line fluxes on the medium-band images, creating spatially resolved maps of
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
that can be used to trace dust and star formation.

Throughout this work we assume a
\Lambda
CDM cosmology with
\Omega_{m}=0.3
,
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.7
, and
H_{0}=70
km s
-1
Mpc
-1
.

II
Data and Sample Selection

We obtain our sample of galaxies with NIRCam medium-band photometry and NIRSpec prism spectroscopy from the UNCOVER and MegaScience surveys
(
Bezanson et al. 2024
;
Suess et al. 2024
)
. The UNCOVER survey targets a field covering the Abell 2744 cluster, a gravitational lens with a very wide area of high magnification. This field was additionally selected due to its overlap with the Hubble Frontier Fields
(
Lotz et al. 2017
)
. The MegaScience survey complements UNCOVER, filling in the medium-bands that had not yet been observed. Together, these surveys cover the field with all 20 broad- and medium-band NIRCam filters, providing a powerful dataset for this work. With it, we can capture
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission lines in the medium bands at a wide range of redshifts.

We use the public DR3 photometric catalog
(
Suess et al. 2024
, internal catalog v5.2.0)
and the associated
Prospector
fits
(
Wang et al. 2024
, v5.3.0)
. The
Prospector
fits give photometric redshifts, stellar mass, SFR, metallicity, and dust measurements. The
\approx
70,000 sources are fit with
Prospector
-
\beta
priors on mass and star formation history, and the parameter space is explored with
dynesty

(
Speagle 2020
)
. Of particular interest to this work, the dust is fit with a two-component model
(
Charlot & Fall 2000
)
— one component attenuating all light, and an additional component that affects only young stars — with a variable-slope
Noll et al. 2009
attenuation curve.

Additionally, we use the public DR4 NIRSpec prism spectra from UNCOVER
(
Jakobsen et al. 2022
;
Böker et al. 2023
;
Price et al. 2024
)
. These low-resolution spectra allow for accurate redshift measurements, and a direct measurement of emission line fluxes. We use the spectra to make direct emission line measurements (Section
III.2
) as a comparison sample to assess the accuracy of photometric emission line measurements. In addition, these accurate redshifts allow us to locate exactly where emission lines land in the photometric filters, as well as identify any other contaminating lines in the medium-bands.

We aim to select a sample of galaxies for which we can make both photometric and spectroscopic measurements of the
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission lines, allowing us to measure nebular
A_{\mathrm{V}}
. We impose the following selection criteria onto the spectroscopic sample of nearly 700 galaxies. First, we ensure high-quality spectra by selecting objects with
flag_zspec_qual
= 3, following
Price et al. 2024
. Next, we place a redshift cut of
1.3\leq z\leq 2.4
to ensure that both the
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission lines are observed in one of the medium bands and that there is a continuum band on both sides of the emission line. We then discard any galaxies where the filter transmission is
\leq 50\%
of its maximum within 20Å of the spectroscopic center of the emission line, as these are possibly too close to the filter edge. Finally, we require that both the spectroscopically measured
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
line fluxes have a signal-to-noise ratio greater than 2. We note that more than half of the possible targets are excluded due to low
\textrm{Pa}\beta
signal-to-noise ratios, so we could double the sample size for science cases that only require
\textrm{H}\alpha
. The selected sample of 14 galaxies is shown in the context of the full photometric sample in Figure
1
. The galaxies are distributed equally above and below the star-forming main sequence within
\approx
0.5 dex. They are spread throughout the redshift range
1.3\leq z\leq 2.4
with apparent F444W magnitudes less than 27 and most with
\log(M_{*}/M_{\odot})>9
. Our sample consists of preferentially higher-mass galaxies that have typical SFRs for their mass range.

III
Measuring Emission Lines from Photometry and Spectroscopy

III.1
Measuring Emission Lines from Medium-Band Photometry

We measure the fluxes of the
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission lines from only three medium-band observations per line, which we will compare to the spectroscopic fluxes. The process is nearly identical for
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
, with the exception of a few correction factors discussed below.

First, we identify the continuum filters as the medium bands surrounding the target emission line. For example, if
\textrm{Pa}\beta
falls within the F360M band, then F335M and F410M are used for continuum measurement. The wavelength coverage of some of the medium bands overlap
(
Rieke et al. 2023
)
. If an emission line falls in multiple bands, we use the next-closest filter for continuum measurement.

To compute the continuum flux
F_{\lambda\mathrm{,cont}}
, we linearly interpolate the flux measurement between the two continuum filters to the effective wavelength of the target filter. Then, we subtract
F_{\lambda\mathrm{,cont}}
from the flux density in the target filter. The remaining flux density in the target filter should be entirely due to the line. Next, we convert the excess flux density due to the line to a total flux by multiplying by the effective width of the target filter;

F_{\mathrm{line}}=F_{\lambda}\times\mathrm{width}.

(1)

These width values are roughly 2000Å for
\textrm{H}\alpha
filters and 4000Å for
\textrm{Pa}\beta
filters
(
Rieke et al. 2023
)
. We do not apply any corrections for the varying response function across the line profile since the filters have nearly flat response functions, which would lead to less than a 10% change in flux at worst. We have ensured through selection that the lines do not fall near the edges of the filters, and therefore should have strong transmission. As we expand this technique to purely photometric samples, uncertain redshifts will make correcting for filter transmission very challenging. To ensure that this pilot study provides a fair representation of the errors, we choose not to correct for this
<10\%
effect in our spectroscopic subsample. Fortunately, we find a low median redshift uncertainty in MegaScience of 0.035 by comparing the spectroscopic redshift to the photometric redshifts for galaxies in our targeted redshift range
(
Price et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. This redshift uncertainty corresponds to roughly 250Å at the wavelength of
\textrm{H}\alpha
at
z=2
. The typical filter width is 2000Å, so even in fully photometric samples, we can determine that the line is contained within the filter to high degrees of certainty.

Unfortunately, neither
\textrm{H}\alpha
nor
\textrm{Pa}\beta
can be completely isolated in their photometric filters. The
\textrm{H}\alpha
line is blended with
[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
at 6550Å and 6585Å in both the UNCOVER prism spectra and medium-band photometry, while
\textrm{Pa}\beta
is typically blended with
[\mathrm{Fe}\textsc{ ii}]
12570Å in the photometry, but well-resolved in the prism spectra. We correct for each of these cases.

The ratio of
[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
/
\textrm{H}\alpha
is well-studied at cosmic noon, and depends strongly on the galaxy’s metallicity. We estimate the
[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
6585Å/
\textrm{H}\alpha
ratio using the fundamental metallicity relation (FMR) from
Sanders et al. 2021
for MOSDEF galaxies, which is a redshift-independent link between mass, SFR, and gas-phase metallicity. For our sample, we compute a gas-phase metallicity from the FMR using the
Prospector
mass and SFR measurements, then an
[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
6585Å/
\textrm{H}\alpha
value from that metallicity, finding a median ratio of 0.09. Finally, we take the theoretical transition probability ratio of
[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
6550Å to
[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
6585Å as 1/3
(see
Dojčinović et al. 2023
, table 1)
. We use these estimates to correct both the spectroscopic and photometric measurements of
\textrm{H}\alpha
to remove the contribution of the
[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
doublet.

We only need to correct for
[\mathrm{Fe}\textsc{ ii}]
in the photometric measurements for
\textrm{Pa}\beta
. The
[\mathrm{Fe}\textsc{ ii}]
line seems to be associated with shocked gas
(
Hartigan et al. 2004
)
, and therefore may be indicative of AGN
(
Calabrò et al. 2023
)
. Unlike the
[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
line, there is not a strong relation for
[\mathrm{Fe}\textsc{ ii}]
with galaxy properties, so we use the objects in our sample with spectroscopic
[\mathrm{Fe}\textsc{ ii}]
detections to extrapolate the correction factor for objects without spectroscopic
[\mathrm{Fe}\textsc{ ii}]
. To determine the factor for this correction, we fit the flux of the
[\mathrm{Fe}\textsc{ ii}]
emission lines with signal to noise ratios of at least 2 for all of the objects with UNCOVER spectra at
1.3\leq z\leq 2.4
. The
[\mathrm{Fe}\textsc{ ii}]
fluxes for these 10 objects do not show strong correlations with redshift,
Prospector
mass, metallicity, or SFR. We correct each object where
[\mathrm{Fe}\textsc{ ii}]
is detected at 2
\sigma
with its individual measurement, and correct by the median
[\mathrm{Fe}\textsc{ ii}]
/
\textrm{Pa}\beta
value (0.27) for the other objects. Additionally,
[\mathrm{He}\textsc{ i}]
10830Å occasionally falls within the blue continuum band for
\textrm{Pa}\beta
. Correcting for this emission line spectroscopically only changes the result by at most 2%, so we opt to not make any corrections for
[\mathrm{He}\textsc{ i}]
.

Both
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission lines have underlying stellar absorption. These absorption lines affect both the photometry and spectroscopy equally, but the absorption line strength differs for
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
. Thus, the absorption lines do not impact our comparison of the spectroscopic and photometric fluxes from the same line, but slightly impact the line ratio
\textrm{Pa}\beta
/
\textrm{H}\alpha
. Therefore, we make a correction for hydrogen absorption lines when computing the line ratio. For each galaxy, we measure the equivalent widths of its absorption features from its
Prospector
stellar population synthesis model without emission lines, and use these absorption line strengths to correct both the photometric and spectroscopic line fluxes.

We measure uncertainties for all objects with Monte Carlo simulations, perturbing the three photometric data points randomly by their uncertainties and recalculating the flux. We carry these simulations through all the way to an
A_{\mathrm{V}}
measurement, taking 1
\sigma
uncertainties as the 16th and 84th percentiles of 1000 draws.

We apply the same method of line flux measurement to the full images (PSF-matched to F444W) to measure emission spatially. Figure 3 of
Suess et al. 2024
shows that the PSF-matching is reliable to sub-1%. Pixel by pixel, we repeat the above method using the medium-band images, but do not apply the
[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
correction. We generate these maps for both
\textrm{H}\alpha+[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
, and propagate uncertainties from the associated noise maps. The
\textrm{Pa}\beta
line is fairly weak, so most objects do not have high signal-to-noise measurements of
\textrm{Pa}\beta
in these images. When visualizing the line maps, we show only
\textrm{Pa}\beta
contours around the regions with high signal-to-noise ratios.

III.2
Flux Calibration and Emission Fitting

In order to assess the accuracy of photometric emission line fits, we also measure the
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
line fluxes spectroscopically. We first flux-calibrate the UNCOVER prism spectroscopy by fitting a fifth-order polynomial to the 20-band JWST photometry, finding correction factors ranging from 1.3 to 6.0. We then fit each of the emission lines directly from the flux-calibrated spectra. We linearly fit the continuum around each of the emission lines from 5500-7700Å and 11800-14200Å, masking the regions from 6200-7000Å and 12200-13100Å to avoid contamination from the lines themselves. After continuum subtracting the lines, we simultaneously fit
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
with Gaussian profiles, allowing the amplitude and width of each line to vary. We find that the blended
\textrm{H}\alpha
+
[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
complex is not perfectly centered at a rest wavelength of 6563Å, so we allow for a redshift offset between
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
of
z_{\mathrm{offset}}\leq 0.005
. This accounts for both the varying
[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
line width and the uncertain NIRSpec wavelength calibration
(
D’Eugenio et al. 2024
;
Graaff et al. 2025
)
. We estimate uncertainties on the fit fluxes and line ratios with 1000 Monte Carlo simulations.

III.3
A_{\mathrm{V}}
measurement

With both photometric and spectroscopic line flux measurements, we can compute a nebular
A_{\mathrm{V}}
. The intrinsic ratio of
\textrm{H}\alpha
to
\textrm{Pa}\beta
is 18:1 assuming case B recombination
(
Reddy et al. 2023
)
. Any deviation from this ratio is due to dust affecting the bluer
\textrm{H}\alpha
line more strongly than the redder
\textrm{Pa}\beta
.

We compute the
A_{\mathrm{V}}
nebular values:

A_{\mathrm{V}}=R_{V}\times k\times\log_{10}\left(\frac{\textrm{H}\alpha/\textrm{Pa}\beta}{18}\right),

(2)

where
k
is computed as

k=\frac{2.5}{k^{\prime}\mathrm{(\textrm{H}\alpha)}-k^{\prime}\mathrm{(\textrm{Pa}\beta})},

(3)

and
k^{\prime}(\lambda)
is described in
Calzetti et al. 2000
. We assume
R_{V}=4.05
.

IV
Results

In this paper, we demonstrate the use of NIRCam medium-band photometry to measure nebular emission lines, dust properties, and create emission line maps. To this end, we measure
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission lines for 14 galaxies from both medium-band photometry and NIRSpec prism spectroscopy. First, we use the spectra to assess the accuracy of photometric emission line flux and nebular
A_{\mathrm{V}}
measurements. Then, we present spatially resolved maps of both
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
in each galaxy, allowing us to study the locations of dust and star formation.

IV.1
Accuracy of Emission Line Measurements

In Figure
2
, we compare the photometric and spectroscopic line flux measurements and
A_{\mathrm{V}}
. We find strong agreement in
\textrm{H}\alpha+[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
line fluxes with scatters of 0.10 and 0.13 dex, respectively. Even at low
\textrm{Pa}\beta
signal-to-noise ratios, the individual flux measurements are still fairly accurate. While we are directly measuring the line flux from the photometric bands, we note that the spectroscopic
\textrm{H}\alpha
equivalent widths range from 100Å to 1000Å (typically 200Å), and the
\textrm{Pa}\beta
equivalent widths range from 10Å to 250Å (typically 40Å).

Similarly, the spectroscopic and photometric line ratios
\textrm{Pa}\beta
/
\textrm{H}\alpha
are reasonably consistent, with a scatter of 0.49 in
A_{\mathrm{V}}
. Uncertainties are fairly large, and primarily driven by the uncertainty in
\textrm{Pa}\beta
measurement. With the points color-coded by mass, we generally recover the well-known relation that more massive galaxies are more dusty.

For many points, the measurement uncertainties do not reach the one-to-one line, particularly in the individual emission line flux comparisons. This result points towards other systematics dominating the uncertainties of this technique, rather than measurement errors. One likely systematic uncertainty may be due to slit losses between the photometry and spectroscopy. For example, a slit that is offset from regions of high star-formation (i.e. object 44283) could cause a difference in the
\textrm{H}\alpha
flux measured by spectroscopy and photometry. We attempted to improve the accuracy of the nebular
A_{\mathrm{V}}
measurement by redoing the calculation with only the pixels contained in the MSA shutters, but this correction does not reduce the observed scatter. However, we noted that five out of the six farthest points from the one-to-one line show clearly visible offsets between the shutter locations and the peak of either the
\textrm{H}\alpha+[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
or
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission line map. Additionally, the six objects closest to the one-to-one line do not have clear visual offsets between the shutter location and the peak of the emission line map. These tests point towards some differences in coverage between the shutters and aperture photometry as a source of error.

Withers et al. 2023
conducted a similar study, measuring the equivalent widths of
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
[\mathrm{O}\textsc{ iii}]
+
\textrm{H}\beta
from medium-band photometry and NIRSpec prism spectroscopy for 15 objects at
1.7\leq z\leq 6.7
. They seem to find a slightly tighter relation for the
\textrm{H}\alpha
flux, and similar scatter to our work for
[\mathrm{O}\textsc{ iii}]
+
\textrm{H}\beta
. The reasonable consistency of our measurements points towards the medium-bands as an excellent means to measure strong, isolated emission line fluxes photometrically. These methods allow us to measure bright emission line fluxes for large samples of galaxies without requiring spectra.

IV.2
Emission Line and Dust Maps at Cosmic Noon

With the same technique used to calculate photometric emission line fluxes, we created
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
line maps (Figure
3
). The leftmost column shows a three-color image, with
\textrm{H}\alpha+[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
falling in the green filter, and the position of the NIRSpec shutters shown in white. The second column shows
\textrm{H}\alpha
contours overlaid on the continuum map (measured near
\textrm{H}\alpha
). The third column shows the
\textrm{H}\alpha+[\mathrm{N}\textsc{ ii}]
linemap. Finally, due to low signal-to-noise ratios of the
\textrm{Pa}\beta
maps, we show the
\textrm{Pa}\beta
contours overlaid on the
\textrm{H}\alpha
maps in the fourth column.

The majority of the objects (9/14) seem to have their
\textrm{H}\alpha
emission overlapping with their brightest points in the continuum at the galaxy’s center. These overlaps may be indications of strong central star-formation, as found by other studies at cosmic noon
(
Barro et al. 2016
;
Ellison et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
. However, as the integrated
A_{\mathrm{V}}
increases, the
\textrm{H}\alpha
emission tends to be visually offset from the peak continuum flux, as seen in all three of the most dusty galaxies. Notably, object 54625 shows a very strong secondary clump of
\textrm{H}\alpha
emission that does not show up strongly in the continuum. We quantified the offsets between
\textrm{H}\alpha
and continuum emission by measuring the Pearson correlation coefficient (
r
) between every pixel in the emission line map and continuum map within the galaxy. We show these
r
values plotted against our medium-band
A_{\mathrm{V}}
measurement in Figure
4
. We find that the correlation coefficient decreases as
A_{\mathrm{V}}
increases, indicating that dustier galaxies tend to have larger differences in their
\textrm{H}\alpha
and continuum maps. This quantitatively confirms the trends that we can see visually. These offsets between
\textrm{H}\alpha
and continuum emission may indicate the presence of dust or galaxy growth, as the galaxies are forming stars in regions where there are not many stars already.

The galaxies in our sample have SFRs that range from 0.1-50
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}/
yr, and therefore show strong
\textrm{H}\alpha
emission. The majority of these galaxies show large regions of
\textrm{H}\alpha
emission that either cover most of the galaxy or are grouped into multiple clumps. Prior observations at cosmic noon find star formation occurring in large, kpc-scale clumps
(
Schreiber et al. 2011
;
Wuyts et al. 2012
)
. Our maps are qualitatively consistent with these findings, showing large regions of strong
\textrm{H}\alpha
emission. The aforementioned object 54625 may be the clearest example of one such large star-forming clump, with object 44283 potentially showing another.

By examining the strong regions of
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission compared to the
\textrm{H}\alpha
maps, we find that
\textrm{Pa}\beta
appears to be generally slightly offset from the
\textrm{H}\alpha
emission (9/14 galaxies). Regions where we observe
\textrm{H}\alpha
but not
\textrm{Pa}\beta
are likely due to the low SNR of the
\textrm{Pa}\beta
maps. On the other hand, locations where
\textrm{Pa}\beta
is visible without
\textrm{H}\alpha
are likely indicating the presence of dust, which suppresses
\textrm{H}\alpha
flux much more strongly than
\textrm{Pa}\beta
. All 5 of the galaxies with photometric
A_{\mathrm{V}}

>1.5
show these offsets. The offsets tend to be fairly small (
<0.1
”), with the strongest region of
\textrm{Pa}\beta
being close to the strongest region of
\textrm{H}\alpha
. This proximity may indicate that the dust is somewhat patchy, with some star-forming regions being obscured, while others are not. Dusty star-forming regions are consistent with prior studies which claim that dust at cosmic noon is clumpy and found around star-forming regions rather than evenly spread throughout the ISM
(
Reddy et al. 2015
;
Lorenz et al. 2023
;
Lorenz et al. 2024
)
. Patchy star-formation is also consistent with the fact that
\textrm{H}\alpha
and continuum emission are more offset as
A_{\mathrm{V}}
increases, as the dust is not spread evenly among the star-forming regions. Finally, the centers of the dustiest objects show
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission, indicating that the centers may have higher dust obscuration.

One of our objects, 37182, shows a very strong signal in
\textrm{Pa}\beta
to the lower right of the galaxy that is not seen in the
\textrm{H}\alpha
emission or continuum maps. This indicates an extremely dusty region. The nature of this source is unclear — perhaps this is a galaxy merger with a second core that is very dust-obscured.

V
Discussion and Conclusions

In this work, we measured both the total flux and spatial distribution of
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission lines from the MegaScience NIRCam medium-band photometry for a sample of 14 galaxies at cosmic noon, and then compared these measurements to NIRSpec prism spectra from the UNCOVER survey. We summarize our main findings:

•

The photometric and spectroscopic measurements of the
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
line flux are generally in agreement with a scatter of
<0.15
dex (Figure
2
), giving confidence to the accuracy of measuring isolated, high equivalent-width line fluxes from medium-band photometry.

•

We measured the nebular
A_{\mathrm{V}}
from the
\frac{\textrm{Pa}\beta}{\textrm{H}\alpha}
ratio, finding reasonable agreement between the photometry and spectra with a scatter of 0.5 dex. The photometric nebular
A_{\mathrm{V}}
increases with increasing galaxy mass, as expected.

•

We created spatially resolved
\textrm{H}\alpha
emission line maps and overlaid
\textrm{Pa}\beta
line map contours on them. There appear to be offsets between rest-frame optical stellar continuum,
\textrm{H}\alpha
emission, and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
emission, particularly in the most dusty systems. Our findings are consistent with studies suggesting patchy dust geometries
(
Reddy et al. 2015
;
Lorenz et al. 2023
;
Lorenz et al. 2024
)
.

Our study demonstrates that the medium bands are capable of measuring isolated emission lines with a scatter of
0.15
dex down to equivalent widths of 10Å. However, applying this technique to a sample that has only medium-band photometry will bring its own set of challenges. Since galaxies would not have spectroscopically confirmed redshifts, it will be difficult to ensure that the targeted line falls entirely within the photometric filter. However, we find that the median redshift uncertainty in MegaScience is 0.035 for galaxies in our targeted redshift range. This uncertainty is on the order of 250Å at the wavelength of
\textrm{H}\alpha
, so the typical redshift uncertainty is much smaller than the typical filter width of 2000Å. Therefore, redshift uncertainties likely will not be a concern for the majority of star-forming galaxies with
\textrm{H}\alpha
emission.

Applying these photometric emission line measurements to larger numbers of galaxies can unlock new ways to study dust and star formation at cosmic noon. In the current UNCOVER field, this would allow for emission line measurements and maps for hundreds of galaxies without requiring the observationally expensive spectra. While we restricted our sample to
\textrm{H}\alpha
and
\textrm{Pa}\beta
, other strong emission lines, such as
[\mathrm{O}\textsc{ iii}]
+
\textrm{H}\beta
or
\textrm{Pa}\alpha
, should be measurable. With this technique, we can conduct large surveys of star formation rate or dust properties without requiring spectra, which currently is limited to less than 1000 galaxies across the full redshift range for UNCOVER.

The medium bands also allow us to generate spatially resolved emission line maps for a much larger sample of galaxies, giving insight into the locations of dust and star formation at a wide range of redshifts. The maps have spatial resolutions of
\approx
1kpc at
z=2
and show clear structure even with per-pixel signal-to-noise ratios of 1, allowing for detailed studies of where star formation is occurring, including whether galaxies are showing inside-out or outside-in growth
(
Nelson et al. 2016
;
Tacchella et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
. Additionally, lensing from the Abell 2744 cluster allows for even higher spatial resolution for some galaxies in the full UNCOVER/MegaScience photometric sample.

This pilot study shows very promising potential for the JWST medium-bands. They hold the ability to effectively isolate individual emission lines, measure their fluxes, and spatially resolve their locations with maps. With the wealth of data available through recent surveys
(
Williams et al. 2023
;
Willott et al. 2023
;
Eisenstein et al. 2023
;
Suess et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, and more medium-band surveys on the horizon (e.g., JWST 7814 MINERVA), the medium-bands provide an exciting new lens into galaxies at cosmic noon.
