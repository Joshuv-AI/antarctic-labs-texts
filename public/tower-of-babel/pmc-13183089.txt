The Gallstone That Wouldn't Budge: From Bouveret Syndrome to Duodenal Necrosis
Authors: Harsimran Kalsi, Yeshika Thapa, Aseed Mestarihi, Eve Pendergraft, Yaseen Perbtani, Tony S. Brar
Published: 2026

CASE REPORT

Bouveret syndrome is a rare cause of gastric outlet obstruction resulting from impaction of a gallstone in the duodenum or pylorus.1–4 It represents an uncommon variant of gallstone ileus, accounting for approximately 1%–3% of cases. The condition typically arises from formation of a bilioenteric fistula, most commonly cholecystoduodenal, permitting gallstone migration into the gastrointestinal tract.1–4

We present a case of 69-year-old woman with a history of cholelithiasis presented with 2 weeks of progressively worsening epigastric pain, nausea, and vomiting. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated a large, laminated gallstone (4.5 × 5.3 cm, red arrow) affected in the proximal duodenum, with pneumobilia, intraluminal gallbladder gas, and a markedly distended stomach (yellow arrow) on coronal (Figure 1) and axial (Figure 2) views, consistent with Bouveret syndrome.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography, coronal view, demonstrating a large, laminated gallstone (red arrow) affected in the proximal duodenum with marked gastric distension (yellow arrow), consistent with gastric outlet obstruction in Bouveret syndrome.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography, axial view, showing an ectopic gallstone within the duodenum (red arrow) associated with pneumobilia and intraluminal gallbladder gas, forming the classic imaging features of Bouveret syndrome.

Upper endoscopy revealed a cholecystoduodenal fistula (Figure 3, red arrow) and a large gallstone completely obstructing the duodenal lumen. Endoscopic electrohydraulic lithotripsy was initiated (Figure 4, black arrow), achieving partial fragmentation. In addition, removal attempts using a 20-mm extraction balloon and rat-tooth forceps were unsuccessful. However, endoscopic management has limited success, particularly in cases involving large gallstones (>2.5 cm) migrating through a bilioenteric fistula, often necessitating subsequent surgical intervention.3,4

Upper endoscopy revealing a cholecystoduodenal fistula (red arrow) adjacent to a large gallstone completely obstructing the duodenal lumen.

Endoscopic view during electrohydraulic lithotripsy, demonstrating targeted fragmentation of the obstructing duodenal gallstone (black arrow).

Surgical exploration uncovered a giant gallstone measuring 10 × 8 cm, severely affected in the duodenum and causing transmural ischemia with necrosis. The stone was removed via a longitudinal gastrotomy. Owing to necrosis of the duodenal wall, partial gastrectomy with gastrojejunostomy, cholecystectomy, and takedown of the fistula were performed. The patient had complete symptom resolution postoperatively and was discharged on postoperative day 7.
