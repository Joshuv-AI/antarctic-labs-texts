A novel and application-oriented inverse nodal problem for Sturm-Liouville operators
Yuchao He, Mengda Wu, Yonghui Xia, Meirong Zhang

Abstract
This paper develops a methodological framework for addressing a novel and application-oriented inverse nodal problem in Sturm-Liouville operators, having significant applications in seismic wave analysis and submarine underwater radar (sonar) detection. By utilizing a given finite set of nodal data, we propose an optimization framework to find the potential q ^ \hat{q} that is most closely approximating a predefined target potential q 0 q_{0} . The inverse nodal optimization problem is reformulated as a solvability problem for a class of nonlinear Schrödinger equations, enabling systematic investigation of the inverse nodal problem. As an example, when the constant target potential q 0 q_{0} is considered, we find that the Schrödinger equations are completely integrable and conclude that the potential q ^ \hat{q} is ‘periodic’ in a certain sense. Furthermore, the reconstruction of q ^ \hat{q} is reduced to solving a system of three featured parameters, thereby establishing an explicit quantitative relationship between ‖ q ^ ‖ ℒ p \|\hat{q}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}} and T ∗ T_{*} . Of importance, we prove the uniqueness of the potential q ^ \hat{q} when p > 3 / 2 p>3/2 . These new findings represent a substantial advancement in this field of study. Our methodology also bridges theoretical rigor with practical applicability, addressing scenarios where only partial nodal information is available.

Introduction

Let
\Omega=[0,1]
be the unit interval. For a fixed exponent
p\in(1,\infty)
, the
L^{p}
Lebesgue space on
\Omega

is denoted by

{\mathcal{L}}^{p}:=L^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}).

For an integrable potential
q\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}
, we consider the Sturm-Liouville operator

{\mathcal{L}}y:=-y^{\prime\prime}+q(x)y,\quad x\in(0,1)

(1.1)

with the Dirichlet boundary conditions

y(0)=y(1)=0.

(1.2)

According to the classical spectral theory (e.g. see
[
2
]
), problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) admits an increasing eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}(q)<\lambda_{2}(q)\cdots<\lambda_{m}(q)<\cdots

and their associated eigenfunctions
E_{m}(x;q)
,
m=1,2,\cdots
. The interior zeros of the eigenfunctions
E_{m}(x;q)
are called nodes of problem (
1.1
)-(
1.2
). Note that
E_{1}(x;q)
has no zero in
(0,1)
and for
m=2,3,\cdots
,
E_{m}(x;q)
has precisely
m-1
zeros inside
(0,1)
, which are ordered as

0<T_{1,m}<T_{2,m}<\cdots<T_{m-1,m}<1.

One can consider
T_{i,m}=T_{i,m}(q)
as (implicitly defined) nonlinear functionals of
q\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}
. For further details, we refer the reader to references
[
1
,
2
,
10
,
21
]
.

It is well-known that nodes are critically important and observable physical quantities. For instance, in oscillating systems, nodes correspond to points where the amplitude is zero-positions at which the system does not vibrate.
Therefore, determining the optimal location for nodes
T_{i,m}(q)
based on given the potential data is a significant topic. Recently, Guo and Zhang
[
7
]
applied the results of
[
6
]
to obtain optimal location of the nodes
T_{1,2}(q)
. Moreover, Chu et al.
[
3
]
studied the optimal characterizations of locations for all nodes
T_{i,m}(q)
. Different from the usual studies on estimations of nodes, they have deduced the critical equations, which are used to obtain the optimal locations of nodes. The aforementioned studies focus on the direct nodal problem using given potential data. However, in practice, the potential function is usually unknown. Instead, nodal data is typically observable since the system does not vibrate at nodal-positions. In such cases, it becomes necessary to reconstruct the potential function from these known nodal properties.
The pioneering works on these inverse nodal problems go back to McLaughlin
[
11
]
. The main result stated that any
dense subset
of the nodal set can uniquely determine the potential
q
up to a constant. Subsequently, Yang
[
19
]
has established explicit reconstruction formulas for the potential and the boundary data by using
a dense subset
of the nodal set. For further results along this line, see, for example, Guo and Wei
[
5
]
, Wang and Yurko
[
17
]
, Yang
[
18
]
.

However, in real-world application, it is impossible to obtain all or a lot of nodes. In contrast to aforementioned works based on the
dense subset
of the nodal set (McLaughlin
[
11
]
, Yang
[
19
,
20
]
, Guo and Wei
[
5
]
, Wang and Yurko
[
17
]
, Yang
[
18
]
), we propose a novel and application-oriented inverse nodal problem for Sturm-Liouville operators based on
only finitely known nodal data
. As a starting work, we will study the following problem. For a given observed node
T_{*}
and a target potential
q_{0}
, does there exist an optimal potential function
\hat{q}
that best approximates the observed potential
q_{0}
while satisfying the nodal constraints?
Additionally, if the potential
\hat{q}
exists, can we recover it by the observed node
T_{*}
? To enhance both mathematical rigor and pedagogical clarity, we reformulate the preceding questions as a novel inverse nodal optimization problem, formally defined as follows:

Problem
(INP)
:
Let
T_{*}\in\mathbb{(}0,1)
and
q_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}
be given. It is required to find a potential
\hat{q}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}

such that the
(i,m)
-th node
T_{i,m}(\hat{q})
coincides with the given value
T_{*}
and

\|\hat{q}-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}=\min\{\|q-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}:T_{i,m}(q)=T_{*},\ q\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}\}.

(1.3)

This type of inverse problem is of great practical significance. For example, in seismic wave analysis,
T_{*}
denotes the location where the seismic wave amplitude vanishes (zero-crossing point), while
q_{0}
represents an initial estimate of the subsurface structure
q
, inferred from auxiliary data such as geological surveys, well-log measurements, regional models, physical constraints, or empirical observations. The formulation in Problem (INP), Equation (
1.3
), integrates
q_{0}
as a prior constraint, ensuring that the inversion process yields an optimal solution
\hat{q}
that not only fits the observed seismic wave data
T_{*}
but also best aligns with existing geological knowledge
q_{0}
. Another important application of this type of inverse problem is the study of submarine underwater radar (sonar) detection.
T_{*}
represents the location where the amplitude of the underwater acoustic wave is zero, and
q_{0}
denotes an initial estimate of the true structural parameters
q
of the underwater object based on prior knowledge (e.g., object shape, density, volume, radius, curvature, acoustic impedance, etc.). In the inverse Problem (INP), Equation (
1.3
) incorporates
q_{0}
as a prior constraint by introducing object shape and other known information. This guides the inversion process to select, from possible solutions, the optimal solution
\hat{q}
that not only fits the observed acoustic wave data
T_{*}
but also aligns best with our existing understanding of the detected object
q_{0}
.

This class of inverse nodal problems had never been studied before. However, when nodes
T_{i,m}(q)
are replaced by eigenvalues
\lambda_{m}(q)
, similar inverse spectral problems with finitely many data on known eigenvalues had been extensively studied by Valeev and Il’yasov
[
9
,
16
,
15
]
. Their results contain the critical systems of equations and the uniqueness of the desired potential (for several cases). For more recent researches on these inverse spectral problems, we refer to
[
8
,
14
,
12
,
13
]
. In this sense,
problem
(INP)
above can be considered as a counterpart of the problems in these papers. However, as for the existence of the optimal potential
\hat{q}
for
problem
(INP)
, the proof of this paper is in some sense a direct method, which is much simpler and completely different from the previous papers. In fact, with the constant target potential
q_{0}
, our problems and the approaches we will develop are more or less some inverse to those in the recent paper
[
3
]
. Moreover, for these inverse nodal problems, the critical equations obtained are more concise than in
[
3
,
7
]
.
When the target potential
q_{0}
is constant, the critical equations are nonlinear Schrödinger equations. We will find three new characteristic features to construct
\hat{q}
and to establish a direct and quantitative relationship between
\|\hat{q}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}
and
T_{*}
.
Note that for the direct nodal problem, Chu et al.
[
3
]
deduced the Schrödinger equations and the nonlinear equations characterizing nodal properties, but no rigorous mathematical proof for uniqueness was provided for the nonlinear equations in
[
3
]
. In contrast, for the present inverse problem, we rigorously prove the uniqueness of solutions to these nonlinear equations, thereby establishing the uniqueness of
\hat{q}
for the case
p>3/2
. These new results have significantly advanced the study in this direction.

This paper is organized as follows. In next section, firstly, we prove the existence of the optimal potential
\hat{q}
for
problem
(INP)
based on the complete continuity of nodes
T_{i,m}(q)
in
q
(see
[
4
]
and
[
6
]
).
Secondly, by using the formula for the Fréchet derivatives of
T_{i,m}(q)
in
q
, we deduce a critical equation for
problem
(INP)
which is a (piecewise) second-order nonlinear Schrödinger equation. In Section 3, we show that, if
q_{0}
is a constant, the critical equation is ‘integrable’ and its solution has periodicity from the phase portraits of the critical equation in subsection 3.1. Moreover, the direct relationship between
\|\hat{q}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}
and
T_{*}
is expressed by three new characteristic features in subsection 3.2. This implies that the original infinitely dimensional inverse nodal problem is reduced to solve a system of three nonlinear equations for three parameters. Through three parameters, we present the expression of
\|\hat{q}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}
.
We further prove that the parameters in the three new characteristic nonlinear equations are uniquely determined, which leads to the uniqueness of
\hat{q}
when
p>3/2
in subsection 3.3.
We present a special example (
p=2
) to further illustrate our results, and demonstrate the relationship between
\|\hat{q}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}
and
T_{*}
through numerical simulations in subsection 3.4. Finally, a conclusion with an open problem is given.

2
The critical system

2.1
Existence of optimal potentials

Firstly, we introduce the following lemma, which is crucial for the subsequent proofs in this paper.

Lemma 2.1
.

(see
[
6
]
)
Given
(i,m)
,
T_{i,m}(q)
is continuously Fr
\acute{e}
chet differentiable in
q\in(L^{1},\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}})
and

\partial_{q}T_{i,m}(q)(x)=H_{i,m}(x;q)E_{m}^{2}(x;q)\in C^{1}[0,1],

(2.1)

where

H_{i,m}(x;q)=\begin{cases}+a_{i,m},\ {\rm for}\ x\in[0,T_{i,m}],\\
-b_{i,m},\ {\rm for}\ x\in(T_{i,m},1],\end{cases}

(2.2)

with the positive constants
a_{i,m}=a_{i,m}(q)
and
b_{i,m}=b_{i,m}(q)
being given by

\begin{split}a_{i,m}&=\int_{T_{i,m}(q)}^{1}\left(\frac{E_{m}(v;q)}{E_{m}^{\prime}(T_{i,m}(q);q)}\right)^{2}dv,\\
b_{i,m}&=\int_{0}^{T_{i,m}(q)}\left(\frac{E_{m}(v;q)}{E_{m}^{\prime}(T_{i,m}(q);q)}\right)^{2}dv.\end{split}

Let us notice from formulas (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) that

\int_{0}^{T_{i,m}(q)}a_{i,m}(q)E_{m}^{2}(v;q)dv=\int_{T_{i,m}(q)}^{1}b_{i,m}(q)E_{m}^{2}(v;q)dv.

(2.3)

Formula (
2.3
) implies that
\int_{0}^{1}H_{i,m}(v;q)E_{m}^{2}(v;q)dv=0
, which is coincident with the translation invariance of nodes

T_{i,m}(q+c)=T_{i,m}(q)\qquad\forall c\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Namely, in the inverse problems using nodes, there may be multiple different potentials which are corresponding to the same observation values
T_{i,m}
. The uniqueness can be obtained only up to a constant.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
q_{0}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}
be a given potential. Then there exists a potential
\hat{q}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}
which solves
problem
(INP)
.

Proof.

Let

S_{T_{*}}=\{q\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}:T_{i,m}(q)=T_{*}\}.

Since
\partial_{q}T_{i,m}(q)\neq 0
(see (
2.1
)), one knows that
S_{T_{*}}
is a 1-codimension differentiable sub-manifold of
({\mathcal{L}}^{p},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}})
. For
problem
(INP)
, there exists a minimizing potential sequence
\{q_{n}\}
such that
q_{n}\in S_{T_{*}}

(n\in\mathbb{N})
and

\|q_{n}-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}\to\inf_{q\in S_{T_{*}}}\|q-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}<+\infty.

(2.4)

This implies that
\|q_{n}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}

(n\in\mathbb{N})
are bounded. Without loss of generality, let us assume that
q_{n}\rightharpoonup\hat{q}
in
{\mathcal{L}}^{p}
. According to the property of weak convergence, from (
2.4
), we can obtain

\|\hat{q}-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}\leq\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\|q_{n}-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}=\inf_{q\in S_{T_{*}}}\|q-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}.

(2.5)

Since nodes are completely continuous in potentials
[
6
, Theorem 1.2]
, we have

T_{i,m}(\hat{q})=\lim_{n\to\infty}T_{i,m}(q_{n})=T_{*},

i.e.
\hat{q}\in S_{T_{*}}.
From inequality (
2.5
), it follows that

\|\hat{q}-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}=\inf_{q\in S_{T_{*}}}\|q-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}=\min_{q\in S_{T_{*}}}\|q-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}=:R_{T_{*}}.

(2.6)

This completes the proof.
∎

2.2
The critical equation for optimal potentials

Let us assume in
problem
(INP)
that
T_{i,m}(q_{0})\neq T_{*}
. Therefore
\hat{q}\neq q_{0}
. Otherwise, the trivial solution to
problem
(INP)
is simply
\hat{q}=q_{0}
.

In this subsection, we apply the Lagrange multiplier method to transform the inverse nodal optimization problems into the solvability of a class of Schrödinger equations. Due to this, some systematic investigations of the inverse nodal problems can be studied using the usual approach. However, we have not addressed the uniqueness of the minimal potentials
\hat{q}
.

Due to Theorem
2.2
,
problem
(INP)
is a minimization problem on the aim functional
\|q-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}
with a constraint

T_{i,m}(q)=T_{*}.

For an exponent
p\in(1,\infty)
, the increasing homeomorphism
\phi_{p}(s):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\phi_{p}(s)=|s|^{p-2}s,\quad\rm{for}\ s\in\mathbb{R},

(2.7)

and
\phi_{p}^{-1}(t)=|t|^{p^{*}-2}t
, where
p^{*}=\frac{p}{p-1}\in(1,\infty)
is the conjugate exponent of
p
. Note that the Fréchet derivatives
\partial_{q}T_{i,m}(q)
are given by (
2.1
) and (
2.2
). Moreover, one has

\partial_{q}\|q-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}(x)=\|q-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}^{1-p}\phi_{p}(q(x)-q_{0}(x)),\quad x\in\Omega,

(2.8)

for
q\in{\mathcal{L}}^{p}\setminus\{q_{0}\}
. Applying the Lagrange multiplier method to a minimizing potential
\hat{q}
of
problem
(INP)
, we obtain

\|\hat{q}-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}^{1-p}\phi_{p}(\hat{q}(x)-q_{0}(x))=cH_{i,m}(x;\hat{q})(E_{i,m}(x;\hat{q}))^{2},\quad x\in{\Omega},

(2.9)

where the constant
c=c_{\hat{q}}\neq 0
.

By using (
2.7
), equation (
2.9
) reads as

\phi_{p}(\hat{q}(x)-q_{0}(x))=\begin{cases}+\tilde{a}_{i,m}(E_{m}(x;\hat{q}))^{2},\quad x\in{[0,T_{*}]},\\
-\tilde{b}_{i,m}(E_{m}(x;\hat{q}))^{2},\quad x\in{(T_{*},1]},\end{cases}

(2.10)

where
T_{*}
is as in
problem
(INP)
and the constants
\hat{a}_{i,m}
and
\hat{b}_{i,m}
are

\hat{a}_{i,m}=\|\hat{q}-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}^{p-1}a_{i,m}(\hat{q})c,\quad\hat{b}_{i,m}=\|\hat{q}-q_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}^{p-1}b_{i,m}(\hat{q})c.

For simplicity, denote the sign

\varepsilon:={\rm sign}(c)=\pm 1.

Then

{\rm sign}(\hat{a}_{i,m})={\rm sign}(\hat{b}_{i,m})={\rm sign}(c)=\varepsilon.

By (
2.10
), let us introduce the function

u_{m}(x):=\begin{cases}\sqrt{|\hat{a}_{i,m}|}\,E_{m}(x;\hat{q}),\quad x\in[0,T_{*}],\\
\sqrt{|\hat{b}_{i,m}|}\,E_{m}(x;\hat{q}),\quad x\in(T_{*},1].\end{cases}

(2.11)

This is a piecewise scaling ‘eigenfunction’ of
E_{m}(x;\hat{q})
, because
u_{m}(x)
is no longer differentiable at
x=T_{*}
.
Then we can write the critical equation (
2.10
) as

\hat{q}(x)-q_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}+\varepsilon\phi_{p^{*}}(u_{m}^{2}(x))=+\varepsilon|u_{m}(x)|^{2p^{*}-2},\quad x\in[0,T_{*}],\\
-\varepsilon\phi_{p^{*}}(u_{m}^{2}(x))=-\varepsilon|u_{m}(x)|^{2p^{*}-2},\quad x\in(T_{*},1].\end{cases}

(2.12)

An important observation on
u_{m}(x)
which is deduced from (
2.3
) is the following equality

\int_{0}^{T_{*}}u_{m}^{2}(x)dx=\int_{T_{*}}^{1}u_{m}^{2}(x)dx.

(2.13)

Moreover, due to (
2.6
), we have

R_{T_{*}}=\left\||u_{m}|^{2p^{*}-2}\right\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}=\left(\|u_{m}\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2p^{*}}}\right)^{2/(p-1)}.

(2.14)

Since
u_{m}(x)
can change signs on both of
[0,T_{*}]
and
(T_{*},1]
, the critical equation has the following different forms on the intervals
[0,T_{*}]
and
[T_{*},1]
.
Let us notice from the first line of (
2.11
) and from the equation for eigenfunction
E_{m}(x;\hat{q})
that

-u^{\prime\prime}_{m}+\hat{q}(x)u_{m}=\lambda_{m}u_{m},\quad x\in[0,T_{*}].

By using the first case of (
2.12
), this equation is transformed into the following form:

-u_{m}^{\prime\prime}+q_{0}(x)u_{m}+\varepsilon\phi_{2p^{*}}(u_{m})=\lambda_{m}u_{m},\quad x\in[0,T_{*}],

because

|u_{m}(x)|^{2p^{*}-2}\cdot u_{m}(x)\equiv\phi_{2p^{*}}(u_{m}(x)).

For the case where

x\in[T_{*},1]
, repeating the above derivation and incorporating the Dirichlet boundary conditions (
1.2
), we immediately obtain the following critical equation.

\begin{cases}-u_{m}^{\prime\prime}+q_{0}(x)u_{m}+\varepsilon\phi_{2p^{*}}(u_{m})=\lambda_{m}u_{m},\quad x\in[0,T_{*}],\\
-u_{m}^{\prime\prime}+q_{0}(x)u_{m}-\varepsilon\phi_{2p^{*}}(u_{m})=\lambda_{m}u_{m},\quad x\in[T_{*},1],\end{cases}

(2.15)

where
\lambda_{m}:=\lambda_{m}(\hat{q})
.
Moreover,
u_{m}(x)
satisfies

u_{m}(0)=u_{m}(T_{*})=u_{m}(1)=0,

(2.16)

and
u_{m}(x)
has precisely
(i-1)
zeros in
(0,T_{*})
, and
(m-1-i)
zeros in
(T_{*},1)
. At this stage, we have transformed the original optimization problem into a Schrödinger equation problem and present the following theorem.

Theorem 2.3
.

For
problem
(INP)
, the piecewise scaling ‘eigenfunction’
u_{m}(x)
satisfies the critical equation
(
2.15
).

Remark 2.4
.

The critical equation derived in this paper differs slightly from that in
[
3
]
. This difference arises from our adoption of a new normalization method, which removes the influence of the function
H(x)
on the critical equation, thereby simplifying it. Interestingly, unlike direct nodal problems, the sign of
\varepsilon
in the critical equation of the inverse nodal problem is not fixed and may vary. A detailed analysis of the sign of
\varepsilon
is provided in the next section.

3
The reconstruction of minimal potentials

In this section, we focus on the case when
q_{0}
is a constant and find the optimal potential by solving the critical equation. A necessary result is as follows.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
q_{0}
is a constant, then the
m
-
th
eigenvalue in (
2.15
) satisfies
\lambda_{m}=\lambda_{m}(\hat{q})>q_{0}
.

Proof.

By integrating equations (
2.15
) on
[0,T_{*}]
and on
[T_{*},1]
respectively and using conditions in (
2.16
), we can obtain

\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{T_{*}}(u_{m}^{\prime})^{2}dx+\int_{0}^{T_{*}}q_{0}(x)u_{m}^{2}dx+\frac{\varepsilon}{p^{*}}\int_{0}^{T_{*}}|u_{m}|^{2p^{*}}dx=\lambda_{m}\int_{0}^{T_{*}}u_{m}^{2}dx,\\
\int_{T_{*}}^{1}(u_{m}^{\prime})^{2}dx+\int_{T_{*}}^{1}q_{0}(x)u_{m}^{2}dx-\frac{\varepsilon}{p^{*}}\int_{T_{*}}^{1}|u_{m}|^{2p^{*}}dx=\lambda_{m}\int_{T_{*}}^{1}u_{m}^{2}dx.\end{cases}

(3.1)

If
\varepsilon=+1
, according to the first equation of (
3.1
), we obtain that
\lambda_{m}>q_{0}
. If
\varepsilon=-1
, the conclusion is obtained through the second equation of (
3.1
).
∎

3.2
The direct relationship between
T_{*}
and
\|\hat{q}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}^{p}}

In the preceding section, we have demonstrated that the inverse nodal problem can be transformed into solving critical equations (a class of Schrödinger equations). Here, we further elucidate that the sign of
\varepsilon
in the critical equations correlates with
T_{*}
, which is precisely governed by the following relationship.

Theorem 3.2
.

The value of
\varepsilon
is given as:

\varepsilon=\begin{cases}-1,&\quad{\rm if}\ 0<T_{*}<\frac{i}{m},\\
+1,&\quad{\rm if}\ \frac{i}{m}<T_{*}<1.\end{cases}

(3.4)

Proof.

We now consider the first equation of (
3.3
). Since
\lambda_{m}>0
, the solution
u_{m}(x)
is a
P_{1}
-periodic function. The parameters
{h_{m}}
and
\alpha_{m}
are given by

{h_{m}}:=\max_{x\in[0,T_{*}]}|u_{m}(x)|,\quad\alpha_{m}:=\frac{h_{m}^{2p^{*}-2}}{\lambda_{m}},

and they can be determined by each other as follows

\begin{cases}{h_{m}}=(\lambda_{m}\alpha_{m})^{\frac{p-1}{2}},\\
-\varepsilon\frac{p-1}{p}{h_{m}}^{\frac{2p}{p-1}}+\lambda_{m}{h_{m}}^{2}=k,\\
-\varepsilon\frac{p-1}{p}\alpha_{m}^{p}+\alpha_{m}^{p-1}=\frac{k}{\lambda_{m}^{p}}.\end{cases}

It should be noted that for different value of

, the range of values for parameters
\alpha_{m}
and
h_{m}
are different.

If
\varepsilon=-1
, it is not difficult to see that
\alpha_{m},\ h_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}=(0,+\infty)
.

If
\varepsilon=+1
,
\lambda_{m}
must satisfy the condition
\lambda_{m}^{p}>pk
to ensure that

\max_{h\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\left(\lambda_{m}h^{2}-\frac{p-1}{p}h^{\frac{2p}{p-1}}\right)=\left(\lambda_{m}h_{m}^{2}-\frac{p-1}{p}h_{m}^{\frac{2p}{p-1}}\right)\bigg|_{h_{m}={\lambda_{m}^{\frac{p-1}{2}}}}=\frac{\lambda_{m}^{p}}{p}>k.

Moreover, we deduce that

h_{m}\in(0,\lambda_{m}^{\frac{p-1}{2}})
. It follows form the relationship between
h_{m}
and
\alpha_{m}
that
\alpha_{m}\in(0,1)
. Combining (
3.5
) with (
3.3
), we obtain that

\frac{du_{m}}{dx}=\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}\sqrt{{h_{m}}^{2}-u_{m}^{2}-\varepsilon\frac{p-1}{p\lambda_{m}}({h_{m}}^{2p^{*}}-u_{m}^{2p^{*}})},\quad x\in\left[0,{P_{1}}/{4}\right].

(3.6)

It follows form the periodicity of
u_{m}
that
u_{m}(0)=0
and
u_{m}(P_{1}/4)={h_{m}}
.
Therefore,

\frac{P_{1}}{4}=\int_{0}^{P_{1}/4}dx=\int_{0}^{h_{m}}\frac{du_{m}}{u_{m}^{\prime}}.

By setting
u_{m}={h_{m}}t
, we have

P_{1}=\frac{4}{\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}-\varepsilon\frac{\alpha_{m}}{p^{*}}(1-t^{2p^{*}})}}.

Moreover, we define

\underline{{\bf T}}_{p}(\alpha)\equiv\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}-\varepsilon\frac{\alpha}{p^{*}}(1-t^{2p^{*}})}},\ \alpha\in(0,+\infty).

Then

P_{1}=\frac{4}{\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}}\underline{{\bf T}}_{p}(\alpha_{m}).

Noting that the gap between the neighboring zeros is
\frac{2\underline{{\bf T}}_{p}(\alpha_{m})}{\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}}
and
u_{m}(T_{*})=0
, we obtain that

T_{*}=\frac{2i\underline{{\bf T}}_{p}(\alpha_{m})}{\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}}.

(3.7)

Next, we consider the second equation of (
3.3
). Set

{\widetilde{h}}_{m}\equiv\max_{x\in[0,T]}|u_{m}(x)|,\ \beta_{m}\equiv\frac{\widetilde{h}_{m}^{2p^{*}-2}}{\lambda_{m}}.

(3.8)

Similar to the previous discussion, we have

{\widetilde{h}}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\ \beta_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\quad{\rm for}\ \varepsilon=+1

and

{\widetilde{h}}_{m}\in(0,\lambda^{p-1}),\ \beta_{m}\in(0,1),\quad{\rm for}\ \varepsilon=-1.

According to (

Since

(3.10)

which implies that the period of

According to the information of the node, we obtain that

It follows from (

For the case

Based on (

\varepsilon<0.

3.3
The uniqueness of the potential
\hat{q}

As an important problem, is the solution to
problem
(INP)
unique? We prove that for

p>\frac{3}{2}
, the uniqueness of
\hat{q}
is guaranteed. We first prove the uniqueness of
\lambda_{m}
.

Theorem 3.7
.

For
p>\frac{3}{2}
, the solution
\lambda_{m}
of equation (
3.20
) is unique. Consequently, the minimal potential
\hat{q}
which optimizes
problem
(INP)
is also unique.

Proof.

Here, we only prove the case where

T_{*}\in(0,\frac{i}{m})
.
The proof for the case where

T_{*}\in(\frac{i}{m},1)
is analogous.

Let

G(\lambda_{m})=i\underline{{\bf V}}_{p}\left(\underline{{\bf T}}_{p}^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}T_{*}}{2i}\right)\right)-(m-i)\overline{{\bf V}}_{p}\left(\overline{{\bf T}}_{p}^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}(1-T_{*})}{2(m-i)}\right)\right),\quad{\rm for}\ \lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

Define

\begin{split}D(\alpha_{m},t)&=1-t^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{m}}{p^{*}}(1-t^{2p^{*}}),\\
\overline{D}(\beta_{m},t)&=1-t^{2}-\frac{\beta_{m}}{p^{*}}(1-t^{2p^{*}}).\end{split}

Note that

G^{\prime}(\lambda_{m})=i\frac{d}{d\lambda_{m}}\underline{{\bf V}}_{p}(\alpha_{m})-(m-i)\frac{d}{d\lambda_{m}}\overline{{\bf V}}_{p}(\beta_{m}),

where

\begin{split}\frac{d}{d\lambda_{m}}\underline{{\bf V}}_{p}(\alpha_{m})&=\underbrace{\left[(p-1)\alpha_{m}^{p-2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}}{\sqrt{D(\alpha_{m},t)}}\,dt+\alpha_{m}^{p-1}\frac{d}{d\alpha_{m}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}}{\sqrt{D(\alpha_{m},t)}}\,dt\right)\right]}_{\frac{d\underline{{\bf V}}_{p}}{d\alpha_{m}}}\\
&\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{\underline{{\bf T}}_{p}^{\prime}(\alpha_{m})}}_{<0}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{T_{*}}{4i\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}}}_{>0}\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\frac{d}{d\lambda_{m}}\overline{{\bf V}}_{p}(\beta_{m})&=\underbrace{\left[(p-1)\beta_{m}^{p-2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}}{\sqrt{\overline{D}(\beta_{m},t)}}\,dt+\beta_{m}^{p-1}\frac{d}{d\beta_{m}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}}{\sqrt{\overline{D}(\beta_{m},t)}}\,dt\right)\right]}_{>0}\\
&\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{\overline{{\bf T}}_{p}^{\prime}(\beta_{m})}}_{>0}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1-T_{*}}{4(m-i)\sqrt{\lambda_{m}}}}_{>0}>0.\end{split}

To obtain the result, it is suffices to study
\frac{d\underline{{\bf V}}_{p}}{d\alpha_{m}}
.
Using the product rule, we obtain that

\frac{d\underline{{\bf V}}_{p}}{d\alpha_{m}}=(p-1)\alpha_{m}^{p-2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}}{\sqrt{D(\alpha_{m},t)}}\,dt-\alpha_{m}^{p-1}\frac{d}{d\alpha_{m}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}}{\sqrt{D(\alpha_{m},t)}}\,dt\right).

Thus

\frac{d\underline{{\bf V}}_{p}}{d\alpha_{m}}=(p-1)\alpha_{m}^{p-2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}}{\sqrt{D(\alpha_{m},t)}}\,dt-\frac{\alpha_{m}^{p-1}}{2p^{*}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}(1-t^{2p^{*}})}{D(\alpha_{m},t)^{3/2}}\,dt.

We need to show

(p-1)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}}{\sqrt{D(\alpha_{m},t)}}\,dt-\frac{\alpha_{m}}{2p^{*}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}(1-t^{2p^{*}})}{D(\alpha_{m},t)^{3/2}}\,dt>0.

It follows that

\begin{split}&(p-1)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}}{\sqrt{D(\alpha_{m},t)}}\,dt-\frac{\alpha_{m}}{2p^{*}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}(1-t^{2p^{*}})}{D(\alpha_{m},t)^{3/2}}\\
=&(p-1)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}(1-t^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{m}}{p^{*}}(1-t^{2p^{*}}))dt}{D^{\frac{3}{2}}}-\frac{\alpha_{m}}{2p^{*}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}(1-t^{2p^{*}})}{D^{\frac{3}{2}}}dt\\
=&(p-1)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}(1-t^{2})}{D^{\frac{3}{2}}}dt+\frac{2p-3}{2p^{*}}\alpha_{m}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{2}(1-t^{2p^{*}})}{D^{\frac{3}{2}}}dt>0.\end{split}

Then we obtain that
G^{\prime}(\lambda_{m})<0
and the solution of (
3.20
) is unique.

The uniqueness of the eigenvalue
\lambda_{m}
ensures that the solution
u_{m}(x)
to the Schrödinger equation (
3.2
) is uniquely determined. Furthermore, the one-to-one correspondence in (
2.12
) between
\hat{q}
and
u_{m}
guarantees the uniqueness of the potential
\hat{q}
.

∎

4
Further discussion and open problem

This paper develops a method for addressing a novel and application-oriented inverse nodal problem in Sturm-Liouville operator. Unlike McLaughlin
[
11
]
, Yang
[
19
,
20
]
, Guo and Wei
[
5
]
, Wang and Yurko
[
17
]
, Yang
[
18
]
, who rely on
a dense subset
of the nodal set, we propose an optimization framework that recovers the potential
\hat{q}
from
merely a finite set
of nodal data, achieving optimal approximation to the target potential
q_{0}
. The proposed approach capitalizes on the complete continuity of nodes with respect to potentials, thereby establishing the existence of solutions to the inverse problem. Using the Lagrange multiplier method, we reformulate the inverse nodal optimization problem as a solvability problem of a class of Schrödinger equations. This reformulation enables systematic investigation for the inverse nodal problem, consequently, with constant targets, the infinite-dimensional inverse problem simplifies to solving three nonlinear equations for three parameters. Furthermore, the periodicity of the reconstructed potential
\hat{q}
is shown to be completely determined by the periodicity of solutions to the corresponding Schrödinger equations. This approach reveals three novel characteristics that establish an explicit relationship between
\|\hat{q}\|_{L^{p}}
and the crucial parameter
T_{*}
.

Given potentials in the Sturm-Liouville operators, Chu et al.
[
3
]
studied the direct nodal problem and
obtained the optimal characterizations of locations for all nodes. By analyzing the node minimization problem, they established the critical equations, which yield two equivalent formulations for characterizing the minimal nodes, expressed as nonlinear systems involving of
4
-dimensional or
2
-dimensional parameters. While they asserted the uniqueness of these nodes, no rigorous mathematical proof was provided for the uniqueness of solutions to the nonlinear equations in
[
3
]
. Crucially, such solutions inherently contain significant physical information, particularly the parameter
\lambda_{m}
governing the system’s intrinsic frequency-a key factor in quantum vibrational modes and energy structures. In this paper, we establish a sufficient condition ensuring the uniqueness of solution for the nonlinear system in the inverse problem, thereby advancing and refining the original framework of direct nodal problem in Chu et al.
[
3
]
.

Of importance, we proved the uniqueness of the potential
\hat{q}
under the assumption
p>\frac{3}{2}
. For
1<p\leq\frac{3}{2}
, we guess that the uniqueness still holds. However, the proof in this case remains open, because we cannot find an efficient method to prove it. We leave it as an open problem.

Finally, in this paper, for
one
given node, we present a framework to study the inverse nodal problems in Sturm-Liouville operators. It is believed that it could be extended to the cases of given
two
or
finite
nodes. However, there will be a challenge of identifying the uniqueness of the potential
\hat{q}
.
