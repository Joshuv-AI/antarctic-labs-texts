Understanding the link between ocular shape and myopia: a systematic review
Authors: Kan Xu, Chi Liu, Shiran Zhang, Yu Jiang, Xuanzhang Tang, Nathan Congdon, Xiaotong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo present an up-to-date summary of the association between ocular shape and myopia.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, CNKI and Wanfang from June 2009 to September 2025 for cross-sectional and cohort studies examining the association between ocular shape and myopia (PROSPERO: CRD42024558271). The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality were used to evaluate the quality of included studies.ResultsThirty-four studies were included. Ocular growth appears relatively uniform in non-myopic eyes whereas myopic eyes tend to show non-uniform expansion. Greater myopia is associated with asymmetric changes in both anterior and posterior ocular shape. Highly myopic eyes frequently display spheroid shapes with equatorial pyriform contours and macular staphylomas, with more pronounced posterior deformities linked to a higher risk of myopic complications. Longitudinal evidence suggests that baseline ocular shape may be associated with subsequent ocular morphological changes and myopia progression.ConclusionsOcular shape is closely associated with myopia and myopic retinopathy. Existing studies remain mostly observational and heterogeneous, with small sample sizes in some studies limiting causal inference and precluding meta-analysis. Future studies are needed to better understand the potential value of ocular shape in myopia control.PROSPERO registration numberCRD 42024558271.Keywords: Eye (Globe), Optics and Refraction

Introduction

Myopia is a major global public health concern. It is estimated that one in two people will have myopia by 2050, with 10% experiencing high myopia (HM).1 2 HM can lead to severe complications like retinal detachment, choroidal neovascularisation and glaucoma, causing irreversible vision loss.3–9 These impairments reduce quality of life and may limit career choices,10 11 while the widespread prevalence of myopia creates significant economic and social burdens.12

Myopia is thought to result from a mismatch between the eye’s refractive power and axial length (AL), primarily due to axial elongation.13–15 Recent studies indicate that the deformation of myopic eyes involves a complex three-dimensional (3D) configuration.16 17 Highly myopic eyes display more pronounced shape deformities,18 and these deformities are significantly associated with an increased risk of myopic macular degeneration and other complications.19 20

A review from 2019 investigated the potential relationship between ocular shape and myopia.20 The findings indicated that ocular shape changes in non-myopic eyes aligned with the global expansion model, while those in myopic eyes adhered to the axial elongation model. However, at that time, the number of studies was limited, research on the anterior segment and equatorial morphology of the eye was lacking, and there were no cohort studies to assess the causal relationship between ocular morphologic changes and myopia. With advances in imaging technologies, new evidence and insights have emerged.21 22

This review aims to summarise recent findings on ocular shape changes in myopic individuals, providing a comprehensive understanding of the association between ocular shape and myopia.

Methods

This systematic review followed the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.23 The review protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO, accessed on 3 July 2024, registration number CRD 42024558271).

Search strategy

A comprehensive search of the PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, CNKI, Embase and Wanfang databases from June 2009 to September 2025 was conducted to identify all studies related to ocular shape and myopia, independently by KX and CL. The search terms included (“myopia” or “high myopia” or “pathological myopia” or “refractive error” or “nearsightedness”) and (“ocular shape” or “eyeball shape” or “posterior ocular shape” or “fundus shape” or “retina shape” or “posterior retinal contour” or “scleral curvature” or “staphyloma” or “eye morphology”). Online supplemental file 1 provides additional details on the search strategy, and equivalent searches were conducted in the additional databases where applicable. Reference lists, citation tracking and grey literature sources were also screened where available.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) cohort studies or cross-sectional design; (2) reported associations between ocular shape and myopia; (3) published in English. Exclusion criteria included: (1) case reports, reviews and letters; (2) studies only reporting AL or anatomical changes instead of overall ocular shape.

Study selection and data extraction

Data extraction was performed using a predefined form by KX and checked by X.H. Any uncertainties or disagreements were resolved through discussion. Extracted information included first author, publication year, country, sample size, spherical equivalent (SE), study design, ocular shape measure(s), parameters used to assess ocular shape and main findings.

Study quality assessment

The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) methodological checklist, containing 11 items scored as 0 (no/unclear) or 1 (yes), was used to evaluate the quality of cross-sectional studies. Overall scores of 0–3, 4–7 and 8–11 indicated low, moderate and high quality, respectively.24 For cohort studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale was used. This scale is widely used to assess the methodological quality of observational cohort studies in systematic reviews, with three domains: selection of study groups (0–4 points), comparability of groups (0–2 points) and ascertainment of exposure or outcomes (0–3 points).25 26 Total scores of 0–3, 4–6 and 7–9 indicated low, moderate and high quality, respectively.

Results

Study selection

The initial search revealed 644 studies. After removing 212 duplicates, 432 studies were screened based on titles and abstracts. A further 365 studies were excluded: 74 non-original studies, 120 conference abstracts, letters, brief reports or non-full-text publications, 56 irrelevant articles, 53 that did not report ocular shape and 62 unrelated to myopia. During the full-text screening of the remaining 67 studies, 33 were further excluded: 26 did not report the association between ocular shape and myopia, 7 reported isolated ocular biometric/refractive parameters or local anatomical changes without ocular shape assessment. No study was excluded based on further quality appraisal (online supplemental file 2). The mean quality scores for cohort and cross-sectional study were 7 and 6, respectively. Ultimately, 34 studies were included (figure 1). Given the substantial heterogeneity across studies, formal meta-analysis was not performed. Instead, we conducted a structured direction-of-effect synthesis by anatomical region and imaging modality (online supplemental table 1). Ocular shape was classified into global ocular shape, referring to the overall three-dimensional configuration of the globe, and segmental ocular shape, including anterior, equatorial and posterior regions.

Anterior segment ocular shape and myopia

Four cross-sectional studies assessed anterior ocular shape in relation to myopia (table 1).27–30 These studies used corneoscleral profile imaging,27 MRI28 or eye surface profiler29 30 to evaluate anterior scleral or corneoscleral morphology. Overall, greater myopia was associated with non-uniform anterior scleral enlargement, a more prolate anterior scleral configuration,27 regional differences in surface area or sagittal height28 29 and reduced nasal-temporal asymmetry.29 30 These cross-sectional findings suggest an association between anterior ocular shape and myopia severity.

MRI-based studies generally suggested that myopia was associated with changes in global ocular shape, including larger ocular dimensions, predominant axial enlargement and a less oblate or more prolate configuration.31 34–36 38–41 In paediatric populations, Mohd-Ali et al35 reported that longitudinal AL increased more than vertical height or horizontal width per diopter of myopia, and Kneepken et al34 found that most 10-year-old eyes were oblate, but prolate configurations were more common in myopic than hyperopic eyes. In a birth cohort, Lim et al31 found that larger and more prolate eyes at birth, assessed using MRI, were associated with smaller AL increases over the first 3 years. Similar patterns were reported in adult or mixed-age studies: larger ocular dimensions, less oblate asphericity and predominant axial enlargement were associated with more myopic SE, longer AL and a shift towards spherical or prolate configurations.36 38–41

OCT-based studies assessed posterior ocular shape mainly through retinal or Bruch’s membrane curvature.33 37 42 These studies suggested that myopia was associated with regional posterior curvature asymmetry, particularly in horizontal, temporal, inferior or peripheral regions.37 42 Wu et al41 found that as myopia progressed, AL was linked to peripheral retinal curvature (RC) and the macular retina bulged while the peripheral retina flattened, leading to higher irregularity across the four quadrants. In high myopes, the reduction in RC was less in the inferior and nasal quadrants compared with the superior and temporal quadrants. Breher et al37 discovered that the horizontal RC and the directional ratio between horizontal and vertical retinal shape significantly correlated with AL, but not vertical retinal shape. In longitudinal paediatric studies, Zhang et al,32 reported increasing horizontal retinal asymmetry over 4 years, particularly on the temporal side, and Wu et al33 showed that with increasing myopia, regional RC increase was irregular, particularly in the temporal and inferior regions within the central 6 mm area.

MRI-based studies mainly described global ocular shape, equatorial ocular shape and posterior ocular shape in highly or pathologically myopic eyes.2 43 44 47–49 52 55 Luo et al44 identified pyriform and round shapes as common equatorial region, whereas bulbous shape predominated posteriorly. Guo et al49 noted that spheroidal shapes predominated in highly myopic eyes, and posterior staphyloma (PS) was significantly associated with high-grade myopic maculopathy. Yu et al47 demonstrated that non-uniform globe expansion and staphyloma formation were associated with myopic traction maculopathy. Moriyama et al55 found that pointed posterior configurations were associated with traction maculopathy and chorioretinal atrophy. Ohno-Matsui52 reported that pathologically myopic eyes commonly showed either barrel-shaped global ocular shape or PS, mainly the wide macular type. In anisometropic patients, Sun et al57 found that the more myopic eye exhibited a flatter peripheral retina compared with the less myopic eye.

Several studies focused on PS classification and related abnormalities.45 46 48 51 Ohno-Matsui et al48 and Saito et al45 reported that wide macular PS was the most frequent type, including in eyes with dome-shaped macula. An et al46 showed that complex or macular PS was associated with greater myopic refractive error, longer AL or macular retinoschisis. Frisina51 found Curtin types I and II were common PS types, with deeper staphylomas tending to be wider. Peripapillary atrophy was more common in eyes with type I PS, while macular atrophy was associated with type II.

OCT-based and combined imaging studies further suggested that posterior ocular shape abnormalities, including posterior retinal or scleral curvature changes, were associated with HM-related complications.2 50 53 54 56 Liu et al56 observed that greater posterior scleral staphyloma severity was associated with a higher prevalence of macular retinoschisis, particularly outer retinoschisis. Miyake et al53 reported that myopic choroidal neovascularisation was associated with a flatter posterior pole, while eyes with chorioretinal atrophy exhibited a steeper curve. Ohno-Matsui et al54 confirmed that irregular scleral curvatures were linked to choroidal neovascularisation, chorioretinal atrophy and myopic traction maculopathy. Wakazono et al50 highlighted that sharp-pointed staphylomas were associated with myopic traction maculopathy. Furthermore, eyes with two protrusions exhibited greater myopic chorioretinal atrophy than those with just one.2

Longitudinal studies provided preliminary evidence that baseline ocular shape may be associated with subsequent morphological or clinical changes in highly myopic eyes. In a retrospective cohort study.21 22 43 Chen et al21 observed longitudinal changes in scleral curvature, particularly in vertical cross-section, which correlated with increasing AL and myopia. Wakazono et al22 reported time-dependent posterior pole changes and found that baseline ocular shape was associated with subsequent myopia progression. Li et al43 showed that ocular shape deformity or pre-existing staphyloma was associated with myopic maculopathy progression over 4 years.

Discussion

This review summarises evidence on the association between ocular shape and myopia using a framework based on anatomical region and measurement approach. Online supplemental table 1 provides a structured direction-of-effect synthesis by anatomical region, imaging modality and overall direction of findings. Overall, apart from axial elongation, the onset and progression of myopia involve three-dimensional changes in ocular shape. Understanding these changes is important for better management of myopia (figure 2). Existing evidence demonstrates different patterns in ocular shape changes in non-myopic versus myopic eyes, highlighting that ocular shape changes are closely linked to the risk of future SE changes and related complications.

Ocular shape changes across the myopia spectrum. This schematic summarises the main ocular shape patterns identified in this review. Emmetropic eyes are generally oblate and relatively symmetric. Myopic eyes tend to show non-uniform three-dimensional enlargement, predominant axial elongation, reduced oblateness or a more prolate configuration, and increasing posterior retinal or scleral curvature asymmetry. In high or pathologic myopia, equatorial deformation, posterior pole deformation and PS may become more evident and are associated with myopia-related complications. Representative quantitative trends include greater axial than transverse enlargement in MRI-based studies and regional curvature irregularity in OCT-based studies. In non-myopic children, the growth of the eye exhibits relatively uniform expansion. This sharply contrasts with the uneven horizontal and vertical anterior scleral expansion observed in myopic eyes, perhaps due to variations in the equatorial surface of the ocular and orbital walls as well as the interaction of the internal blood supply with the cornea, ciliary muscles and regional choroid.58 59

It is noteworthy that more abnormal posterior configurations are linked to an increased risk of myopic retinopathy, though results are inconsistent. For instance, Moriyama2 found no significant association between myopic choroidal neovascularisation and posterior segment shape, while Ohno-Matsui et al reported choroidal neovascularisation was more common in eyes with irregular curvature,54 and Miyake and Liu indicated that the posterior pole was relatively flat in eyes with choroidal neovascularisation.53 56 It has been suggested that deformation of the posterior segment may affect the blood supply to the choroid and retina, potentially leading to damage.56 This could be one of the mechanisms leading to the increased risk of retinopathy. It has also been suggested that Bruch’s membrane regulates axial elongation, and that defects in the basement membrane may cause scleral outward migration and PS formation.13 60

The few existing longitudinal observational studies on ocular shape have found that baseline ocular shape in both children and adults is associated with future refractive and axial changes. This association may stem from various factors, such as an uneven peripheral ocular shape causing imbalanced optical signals and irregular equatorial growth.61 62 These findings suggest that ocular shape may serve as a potential structural marker associated with myopia progression and myopia-related complications, which warrants further investigation.

The findings of this review may also have implications for current and emerging myopia control strategies. Optical interventions that modify peripheral retinal defocus, such as myopia control spectacle lenses and orthokeratology, may influence regional ocular growth signals and slow axial elongation during myopia progression.63–65 Experimental and clinical studies suggest that peripheral defocus can regulate eye growth through locally mediated mechanisms, which may contribute to the asymmetric ocular expansion observed in myopic eyes.63 64 In addition, increasing attention has been directed towards the role of scleral biomechanics in ocular shape remodelling, particularly in relation to posterior pole deformation and staphyloma formation in highly myopic eyes.13 66

A standardised classification system is essential for better understanding and investigating ocular shape. In 2011, Moriyama first used 3D-MRI to classify PM ocular shapes into four types: nasally distorted, temporally distorted, cylindric and barrel-shaped.2 Guo added spheroidal and ellipsoidal shapes and redefined the cylindric type as conical.49 In 2023, Luo enhanced this classification system by introducing five equatorial segment shapes, round, rectangular, pyriform, vertical-elliptical and horizontal-elliptical, and redefining six posterior segment shapes, spheroidal, conical, bulb-shaped, ellipsoidal, multidistorted and barrel-shaped.44 Future studies should explore the relationships between various segments of the ocular globe, to develop a more comprehensive and widely accepted morphological classification. The lack of standardised definitions, imaging protocols and quantitative metrics for ocular shape remains a major barrier to cross-study comparability and formal quantitative synthesis.

Measurement heterogeneity was substantial across the included studies. MRI-based studies primarily assessed global or posterior three-dimensional globe morphology, whereas OCT-based studies mainly quantified local posterior retinal or scleral curvature. Anterior segment methods, including corneoscleral profile imaging and eye surface profiler, assessed anterior scleral or corneoscleral morphology. Currently, MRI is the most used device for studying ocular shape. However, the relatively low resolution and high costs restrict its use in research settings. In comparison, OCT is more readily available and provides results consistent with MRI.42 50 53 54 Wide-field swept-source OCT offers a broad scanning range, high resolution and deep focus, and has been recommended as the preferred method for examining the morphology of PS.67 68 However, MRI is still recommended for assessing overall ocular shape and larger PS.

Most of the included studies were conducted in East Asian populations, particularly in China, Japan and Singapore. This geographic concentration may limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations. Ethnic anatomical differences, such as variations in orbital geometry and ocular biometric characteristics, may contribute to differences in ocular morphology and refractive development across populations.4 36 69 In addition, environmental factors—including near-work activities, educational intensity and outdoor exposure—have been shown to influence ocular growth and the risk of myopia.6 70 Future studies from other regions are needed to better understand population-specific patterns of ocular shape and myopia progression.

Several limitations should be considered. First, a formal meta-analysis was not performed because of substantial heterogeneity in anatomical regions, imaging modalities, ocular shape definitions and outcome measures. Second, although no low-quality studies were included, the predominance of moderate-quality cross-sectional studies limits causal inference, highlighting the need for larger longitudinal studies with standardised imaging protocols. Therefore, our confidence in the conclusions is moderate, particularly for temporal or predictive relationships between ocular shape and myopia progression. Third, the predominance of East Asian populations may limit generalisability. Finally, publication and language bias cannot be fully excluded because only English-language full-text articles were included.

Conclusion

This systematic review summarises current evidence on the association between ocular shape and myopia. Myopic eyes tend to show non-uniform ocular expansion and posterior segment deformation, particularly in high/pathologic myopia. Future research on myopia should integrate the three-dimensional conformation of the eye with optical, physiological and biochemical information to enhance our understanding of the mechanisms that modulate eye development and myopia.
