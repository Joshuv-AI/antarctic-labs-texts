An ensemble of Gaussian fields with multifractal statistics for turbulence
Mark Warnecke 1, Lukas Bentkamp 2, Gabriel B. Apolinário 2, Michael Wilczek 2, Perry Johnson 1

Abstract
Obtaining accurate field statistics continues to be one of the major challenges in turbulence theory and modeling. From the various existing modeling approaches, multifractal models have been successful in capturing intermittency in velocity gradient and increment distributions. On the other hand, superstatistical models from nonequilibrium statistical mechanics have shown the capacity to model PDFs of various statistical turbulent quantities as ensembles of simpler stochastic processes. Here, we present an approach that generates field statistics in the form of a characteristic functional by promoting a model for multifractal increment statistics to an ensemble of Gaussian fields. By carefully designing the correlation function and the corresponding weight of each subensemble, we are able to define a functional that exhibits multifractal two-point inertial-range and dissipation-range statistics, and that blends into realistic large-scale behavior. Additionally, the method is capable of producing multifractal statistics with any of the widely used singularity spectra. We characterize the fidelity of our approach through comparisons to literature results from direct numerical simulations. Overall, our framework thereby bridges between three different perspectives to turbulence: superstatistics, multifractals, and functional approaches to turbulence.

1
Introduction

Turbulence is characterized by velocity fluctuations whose statistics are strongly scale-dependent. Probability density functions (PDFs) of the velocity increments are approximately Gaussian at separation distances commensurate to and larger than the integral length scale, but display heavier tails as the separation length scale decreases through the inertial range and into the dissipative range of scales. This phenomenon, known as intermittency, presents a major challenge to creating accurate turbulence models. Over the years, a number of techniques have been developed to model intermittency, such as multifractals and superstatistics, which are reviewed in the following.

In the language of multifractal theory, multiscale observables can be written as a statistical mixture of a continuum of Hölder exponents. Each exponent
h
occupies a set of dimension
D(h)
and this determines the contribution of each
h
to the full statistics
(
Mandelbrot 1977
;
Parisi & Frisch 1985
;
Falconer 1990
;
Meneveau & Sreenivasan 1991
)
. Among the observables to which multifractal theory has been successfully applied are velocity gradients
(
Nelkin 1990
;
Chevillard & Meneveau 2006
;
Ishihara et al. 2009
;
Johnson & Meneveau 2017
;
Pereira et al. 2018
;
Luo et al. 2022
)
, Eulerian velocity increments
(
Benzi et al. 1984
;
Meneveau & Sreenivasan 1991
)
, Lagrangian velocity increments
(
Biferale et al. 2004
;
Arnèodo et al. 2008
;
Chevillard et al. 2005
;
Chevillard et al. 2012
)
, and Lagrangian acceleration
(
Biferale et al. 2004
)
. Crucially, it provides a connection between the asymptotic scaling of all of these observables in terms of a single
D(h)
function.

At first glance, superstatistics
(
Beck & Cohen 2003
;
Beck 2004
;
Beck 2011
)
represents a complementary approach to model intermittency based on a mixture distribution. Applied to nonequilibrium statistical mechanics, superstatistics describes a mixture of Boltzmann statistics with the temperature as the mixing parameter
(
Beck & Cohen 2003
;
Beck 2004
;
Beck 2011
)
. In the context of turbulence, the mixing parameter may be, for instance, the energy dissipation.
This description has been shown to capture non-Gaussian turbulence statistics both in the Eulerian
(
Beck 2000
)
and Lagrangian frames
(
Reynolds 2003
;
Reynolds et al. 2005
;
Beck 2003
;
Beck 2007
)
.
It is also worth noting that the multifractal model can be formulated in a way akin to superstatistics. Specifically,
Chevillard et al. 2012
considered a superposition of Gaussian increment statistics with a fluctuating variance. This indicates that intimate relations between the two approaches exist.

Previous applications of the multifractal and superstatistical approaches have focused on the modeling of individual random variables, e.g., two-point statistics. While the multifractal language provides a connection between the universal properties of different observables
(
Benzi et al. 1991
;
Benzi et al. 1998
;
Chevillard et al. 2012
)
, the interest in extending these approaches to modeling the statistics of entire random fields remains.
Wilczek 2016

showed that a superposition of Gaussian characteristic functionals (i.e., a statistical ensemble of Gaussian fields) with a fluctuating correlation function is capable of producing non-Gaussianity and intermittency.
This naturally leads to the question whether a mixture model with realistic multifractal properties comprising entire field statistics can be developed. A first step into this direction was recently taken by
Friedrich et al. 2021
, who developed a model for field statistics with classic K62 multifractal scaling
(
Kolmogorov 1962
;
Obukhov 1962
)
. The approach has been extended to enable a statistical refinement of sparse data sets by
Lübke et al. 2023
and has furthermore been applied to the stochastic modeling of turbulent wind fields
(
Friedrich et al. 2022
)
.
We mention as well a complementary approach to field statistics, where realizations of a random field are generated with properties compatible with multifractal theory, either in a static
(
Chevillard et al. 2019
)
or dynamic way
(
Apolinário et al. 2022
;
Beck et al. 2024
)
.

In this paper, we establish a relation between existing multifractal models and the superstatistical Gaussian field ensemble approach.
We construct an ensemble of Gaussian fields that features increment and gradient statistics complying with a given multifractal singularity spectrum, albeit only for its even moments. From a conceptual point of view this highlights close connections between the discussed previous approaches and lays the foundation for a future connection to statistical field theories based on the Navier-Stokes equation such as the functional approach
(
Hopf 1952
;
Ohkitani 2020
)
.

The content is arranged as follows.
In
Section

2
, we review the multifractal modeling of
Chevillard et al. 2012
and the ensemble of Gaussian fields of
Wilczek 2016
.
In
Section

3
, we show how the multifractal description can be generalized to a superstatistical characteristic functional.
Section

4
demonstrates the approach with a simple example—the classic lognormal model. In
Section

5
, a more general model is considered, with an arbitrary singularity spectrum, and realistic large-scale and viscous-range behavior. Conclusions are drawn in Section
6
.

2
Theoretical background

2.1
Gaussian mixtures for multifractal velocity increment PDFs

In this section, we summarize the formulation of multifractals as Gaussian mixtures put forward by
Chevillard et al. 2012
.
The paper considers velocity increments
v=\delta_{\ell}u
across a scale
\ell
in the inertial range, i.e.,
\eta\ll\ell\ll L
where
\eta
denotes the Kolmogorov scale and
L
the integral scale of the flow. The velocity increment, conditional on a given Hölder exponent
h
, is modeled as a zero-mean Gaussian random variable, with PDF

g(v;S(\ell;h))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi S(\ell;h)}}\exp\left[-\frac{v^{2}}{2S(\ell;h)}\right]\,.

(1)

Notice that we have adopted a notation for the Gaussian PDF
g(x;\sigma^{2})
where
\sigma^{2}
is the variance. Here,
S(\ell;h)
is the second-order structure function conditional on a given Hölder exponent, and behaves as

S(\ell;h)=2\sigma_{u}^{2}\left(1+\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)^{2}\right)^{-h}\,,

(2)

in the inertial and large-scale ranges. Compared to
Chevillard et al. 2012
, we have added a large-scale saturation. The large-scale root mean square velocity is denoted
\sigma_{u}
, and
S(\ell,h)
correctly asymptotes to
2\sigma_{u}^{2}
as
\ell\to\infty
. Additionally, this function shows an
(\ell/L)^{2h}
scaling in the inertial range.
In this framework, the PDF of velocity increments is given by

f_{v}(v;\ell)=\int\mathrm{d}h\,f_{h}(h;\ell)\,g(v;S(\ell;h))\,,

(3)

where

f_{h}(h;\ell)
is the probability density of Hölder exponents at fixed scale
\ell
.
This mixture of Gaussian random variables with fluctuating variance naturally gives rise to non-Gaussian statistics.

The mixture distribution
f_{h}(h;\ell)
provided by multifractal phenomenology, including the inertial and large-scale ranges, is given by

f_{h}(h;\ell)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{Z(\ell)}\,\left(1+(L/\ell)^{2}\right)^{-(1-D(h))/2},&\text{if }h_{\min}\leq h\leq h_{\max}\,,\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(4)

where
D(h)
is the multifractal singularity spectrum,
h_{\min}
and
h_{\max}
are bounds on the Hölder exponent (explained below), and
Z(\ell)
is the normalization of the PDF.
A key assumption of the multifractal model, relevant for the subsequent discussion, is that
D(h)
is assumed to be independent of
\ell
over the inertial range of scales.

With this formulation, the velocity increment displays multifractal scaling exponents, at least for its even moments,

\langle v^{n}\rangle\sim\ell^{\zeta_{n}},\ \text{where }\ \zeta_{n}=\min_{h}\left[nh+1-D(h)\right],

(5)

as can be shown with a steepest descent approximation in the limit of vanishing scale separation
(
Chevillard et al. 2012
)
. Due to the use of Gaussians, all odd-order moments of the velocity increments are zero. We remark, however, that
Chevillard et al. 2012

addresses the skewness of the velocity increments by modifying the Gaussian base distribution.

There are several empirical models for
D(h)
in the turbulence literature, and in
Section

3
we consider a change of variables where
D(h)
can be freely specified.
As explicit examples, we consider three representative models of singularity spectra: The lognormal, with a quadratic spectrum

D(h)=1-\frac{(h-h_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{h}^{2}};

(6)

the She-Lévêque model
(
She & Lévêque 1994
)
, with spectrum

D(h)=-1+3\left[\frac{1+\log\log\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)}{\log\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)}-1\right]\left(h-\frac{1}{9}\right)-\frac{3}{\log\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)}\left(h-\frac{1}{9}\right)\log\left(h-\frac{1}{9}\right);

(7)

and the Sreenivasan-Yakhot model
(
Sreenivasan & Yakhot 2021
)
, defined by

D(h)=1-c-\beta h+2\sqrt{c\beta h}.

(8)

The free parameters of the lognormal model can be determined by fits to the statistics of direct numerical simulation (DNS) or experimental data. Typical values are
h_{0}=0.37
,
\sigma_{h}^{2}=0.025

(
Chevillard et al. 2012
)
. For the Sreenivasan-Yakhot model,
\beta=3c-3
and
c=7.3
are predicted by
(
Sreenivasan & Yakhot 2021
)
.

The range of the Hölder exponents,
h_{\min}\leq h\leq h_{\max}
, important for a complete description of the multifractal exponents, requires accurate statistical data at high Reynolds numbers to be reliably inferred. This topic has recently received attention, with numerical simulations done at increasing spatial resolution
(
Buaria & Pumir 2023
)
and novel techniques for measuring exponents at negative order
(
Lashermes et al. 2008
)
.
Estimates of
h_{\min}
range from
-0.88

(
Debue et al. 2018
;
Dubrulle 2019
)
to
0.15

(
Lashermes et al. 2008
)
.
For this study, we rely on simple estimates by choosing
h_{\min}
and
h_{\max}
as the solutions to
D(h)=0
.

In the remainder of this paper we introduce a change of variables that allows to map the multifractal framework as outlined above to an ensemble of Gaussian fields.

2.2
Ensemble of Gaussian characteristic functionals for non-Gaussian field statistics

While the statistics of velocity increments (and two-point statistics, more generally) have been a central focus for turbulence theory over the past century, they provide an incomplete characterization of turbulent fields.
A complete description is provided, for instance, by the characteristic functional, satisfying a closed and infinite-dimensional dynamical equation (the Hopf equation, see
Hopf 1952
;
Monin & Yaglom 1971,1975
). This section reviews the approach of
Wilczek 2016
, where non-Gaussian and intermittent characteristic functionals are built as mixtures (ensembles) of Gaussian random fields (compare also
Bentkamp et al. 2019
;
Friedrich et al. 2021
;
Friedrich et al. 2022
;
Lübke et al. 2023
).
While not able to describe skewness, this approach provides a simple and analytically tractable basis for capturing the statistics of turbulent fields.

In the following we consider one-dimensional fields for simplicity. When comparing theoretical results to data from experiments or DNS data, we interpret these fields
cum grano salis
as one-dimensional cuts through three-dimensional fields. An extension of the approach to three-dimensional vector fields is briefly outlined in Appendix
B
.
For a field
u(x)
, the two-point statistics are encapsulated in the following joint PDF,

f_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})=\left\langle\delta(u_{1}-u(x_{1}))\delta(u_{2}-u(x_{2}))\right\rangle,

(9)

or alternatively in its Fourier transform, the two-point characteristic function,

\phi_{2}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=\left\langle\exp\left[\mathrm{i}\left(\alpha_{1}u(x_{1})+\alpha_{2}u(x_{2})\right)\right]\right\rangle.

(10)

Field statistics include the
N
-point statistics for any number of points
N
. This can be captured in a single object, the characteristic functional, given by the expression
(
Kolmogorov 1991
;
Hopf 1952
)

\Phi[\alpha]=\left\langle\exp\left[\mathrm{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,\alpha(x)u(x)\right]\right\rangle.

(11)

Here, the argument of
\Phi
is the test function
\alpha(x)
. This characteristic functional contains complete information of the probability densities of observing any velocity field configuration
u(x)
.

In particular, the characteristic functional of a zero-mean Gaussian field is

\Phi^{G}[\alpha;\gamma]=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\alpha(x)\widetilde{C}(x-x^{\prime};\gamma)\alpha(x^{\prime})\right],

(12)

where
\widetilde{C}(x-x^{\prime};\gamma)=\langle u_{\gamma}(x)u_{\gamma}(x^{\prime})\rangle
denotes the two-point velocity covariance function. We have added
\gamma
as a parametric dependence. We restrict ourselves to statistically homogeneous fields, such that the covariance function is an even function depending only on
x-x^{\prime}
.
Note that the two-point covariance is sufficient to determine the entire field statistics of the Gaussian field.
This provides a practical advantage in developing theories based on Gaussian field statistics, and non-Gaussianity may be recovered by superposing the statistics of an ensemble of Gaussian fields
(
Wilczek 2016
)
. In particular, the covariance function can be chosen to vary depending on a parameter
\gamma
, allowing for its interpretation as a conditional covariance function. In this way, the Gaussian fields become subensembles of the full model. The characteristic functional for the resulting ensemble statistics thus takes the form

\Phi[\alpha]=\int\mathrm{d}\gamma\,f_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,\Phi^{G}[\alpha;\gamma]

(13)

where
f_{\gamma}(\gamma)
is the PDF of the parameter
\gamma
. This parameter could be related, for example, to a fluctuating length scale, the local dissipation rate or, as discussed below, the fluctuating Hölder exponent of a turbulent field. Note that, by restricting ourselves to zero-mean Gaussian fields, we do not capture odd-order moments. As a result, our model statistics lacks skewness. The possibility of addressing skewness within our framework, in analogy to the discussion in
Chevillard et al. 2012
, is left for future investigation.

The apparent similarity between
Eq.

3
for expressing multifractal two-point statistics
(
Chevillard et al. 2012
)
and
Eq.

13
for the superstatistics of random fields invites a closer inspection of the relationship between these two approaches.
This is done in the next section, and for that we outline the statistics of velocity increments and gradients that we obtain from the functional description. The velocity increment statistics are obtained by choosing the test function
(
Wilczek 2016
;
Monin & Yaglom 1971,1975
)

\alpha(x)=\Delta\left[\delta(x-y-\ell)-\delta(x-y)\right],

(14)

which, inserted into
Eq.

13
, yields the characteristic function of the increment at scale
\ell

\phi_{v}(\Delta;\ell)=\int\mathrm{d}\gamma\,f_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{S}(\ell;\gamma)\Delta^{2}\right),

(15)

with the conditional structure function

\widetilde{S}(\ell;\gamma)=2\left[\widetilde{C}(0;\gamma)-\widetilde{C}(\ell;\gamma)\right].

(16)

The Fourier transform of this characteristic function produces the increment PDF

f_{v}(v;\ell)=\int\mathrm{d}\gamma\,f_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,g\left(v;\widetilde{S}(\ell;\gamma)\right).

(17)

Furthermore, the gradient statistics can be found in a similar way, by choosing the test function
(
Wilczek 2016
)

\alpha(x)=-\beta\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\delta(x-y).

(18)

Using integration by parts before evaluating the delta function, one obtains the characteristic function of the gradient
A=\partial_{x}u

\phi_{A}(\beta)=\int\mathrm{d}\gamma\,f_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{C}^{\prime\prime}(0;\gamma)\beta^{2}\right)=\int\mathrm{d}\gamma\,f_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\widetilde{S}^{\prime\prime}(0;\gamma)\beta^{2}\right),

(19)

where primes denote derivatives with respect to
\ell
. A Fourier transform returns the PDF of the velocity gradient,

f_{A}(A)=\int\mathrm{d}\gamma f_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,g\left(A;\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{S}^{\prime\prime}(0;\gamma)\right)\,.

(20)

3
Connecting multifractal mixtures and Gaussian ensembles

We may now build a connection between the multifractal formulation of
Chevillard et al. 2012
and the ensemble of Gaussian fields of
Wilczek 2016
. Let us start with the multifractal formulation, in particular
Eq.

3
which we repeat:

f_{v}(v;\ell)=\int\mathrm{d}h\,f_{h}(h;\ell)\,g(v;S(\ell;h)).

(21)

For this distribution,
h
is the mixing parameter.
On the other hand, notice that
Eq.

17
, obtained from the ensemble of Gaussians, is also a mixture, this time with the scale-independent mixing distribution
f_{\gamma}
:

f_{v}(v;\ell)=\int\mathrm{d}\gamma\,f_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,g\left(v;\widetilde{S}(\ell;\gamma)\right).

(22)

In
Eq.

21
, the scale dependence of
f_{h}
is a requirement of multifractal phenomenology, while in
Eq.

22
, its absence in
f_{\gamma}
is necessary for the functional formulation: As can seen in
Eq.

13
, the mixing distribution
f_{\gamma}(\gamma)
cannot depend on the scale of the observable, which only comes in through the test function
Eq.

14
and thus becomes an argument of the subensemble structure function
\widetilde{S}
.
In order to write an ensemble of Gaussian fields that is compatible with the multifractal formulation of
Eq.

21
, we introduce a change of variables which connects
\gamma
and
h
.

Let us introduce the cumulative distribution functions (CDFs)

\displaystyle F_{h}(h;\ell)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{h}\mathrm{d}h^{\prime}\,f_{h}(h^{\prime};\ell),\ \text{and}

(23)

\displaystyle F_{\gamma}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\gamma}\mathrm{d}\gamma^{\prime}\,f_{\gamma}(\gamma^{\prime})\,.

(24)

We claim that, given the PDFs
f_{\gamma}(\gamma)
and
f_{h}(h;\ell)
, as well as a structure function
S(\ell;h)
, both superpositions are equivalent, i.e.,

f_{v}(v;\ell)=\int\mathrm{d}\gamma\,f_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,g\left(v;\widetilde{S}(\ell;\gamma)\right)=\int\mathrm{d}h\,f_{h}(h;\ell)\,g(v;S(\ell;h)),

(25)

given that

\widetilde{S}(\ell;\gamma)=S\left(\ell;F_{h}^{-1}(F_{\gamma}(\gamma);\ell)\right)\,.

(26)

To show this, we choose the following
\ell
-dependent substitution that maps
\gamma
to
h
:

h(\gamma;\ell)=F_{h}^{-1}(F_{\gamma}(\gamma);\ell),

(27)

such that

F_{h}(h(\gamma;\ell);\ell)=F_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,.

(28)

A derivative with respect to
\gamma
results in

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}F_{h}(h(\gamma;\ell);\ell)=\frac{\mathrm{d}F_{h}(h(\gamma;\ell);\ell)}{\mathrm{d}h}\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}=f_{h}(h;\ell)\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}=\frac{\mathrm{d}F_{\gamma}(\gamma)}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}=f_{\gamma}(\gamma),

(29)

and thus the usual change of probability measures

f_{h}(h;\ell)\,\mathrm{d}h=f_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma\,.

(30)

Applying this substitution to the integral, we can write

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\gamma\,f_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,g\left(v;\widetilde{S}(\ell;\gamma)\right)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\gamma\,f_{\gamma}(\gamma)\,g\left(v;S\left(\ell;F_{h}^{-1}(F_{\gamma}(\gamma);\ell)\right)\right)

(31)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}h\,f_{h}(h;\ell)\,g(v;S(\ell;h)),

(32)

which concludes the argument.
This means that the increment statistics from an
h
ensemble with any scale-dependent PDF
f_{h}
can be reproduced by a
\gamma
ensemble with a scale-independent PDF
f_{\gamma}
, subject to an appropriate change of the structure function. Note that only the transformed structure function
\widetilde{S}
is fixed by the transform, whereas the parameter distribution
f_{\gamma}(\gamma)
can be chosen freely in advance.

Therefore, we can transform a multifractal model for velocity increment PDFs with arbitrary singularity spectrum into a Gaussian mixture increment PDF with scale-independent mixing distribution. The latter expression can then be generalized to a mixture (ensemble) of characteristic functionals. The resulting functional reproduces the increment statistics of the original multifractal model. Furthermore, it is capable of producing multifractal velocity gradient statistics as well. In
Chevillard et al. 2012
, in comparison, velocity gradient statistics are constructed through a compatibility argument between random variables at different scales.
We illustrate the change of variables
Eq.

27
with various examples in the following sections.

4
Example: Lognormal model

As a first example to illustrate the mapping from the multifractal formulation to the ensemble of Gaussian fields, we choose a quadratic singularity spectrum,
D(h)=1-\frac{(h-h_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{h}^{2}}
(Eq.
6
), the lognormal multifractal model, without bounds on
h
(
h_{\min}=-\infty
,
h_{\max}=\infty
).
According to
Eq.

4
, the resulting PDF
f_{h}
is Gaussian,

f_{h}(h;\ell)=\frac{1}{Z(\ell)}\exp\left[-\log\left(1+\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)^{2}\right)\frac{(h-h_{0})^{2}}{4\sigma_{h}^{2}}\right]=g\left(h-h_{0};\Sigma^{2}(\ell)\right)\,,

(33)

where
Z(\ell)
is the appropriate normalization, and

\displaystyle\Sigma^{2}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sigma_{h}^{2}}{\log\left(1+\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)^{2}\right)}

(34)

is the
\ell
-dependent variance.

Its CDF reads

F_{h}(h;\ell)=\int_{-\infty}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}h^{\prime}f_{h}(h^{\prime};\ell)=G\left(\frac{h-h_{0}}{\Sigma(\ell)}\right)\,,

(35)

where
G(x)
is the CDF of a standard Gaussian. As laid out in the previous section, we want to express the increment PDFs given by
Eq.

21
as a superposition with a scale-independent mixing parameter
\gamma
(
Eq.

22
). Since we can freely choose the distribution of
\gamma
, we set it to be a standard Gaussian, with PDF
f_{\gamma}(\gamma)=g(\gamma;1)
and CDF
F_{\gamma}(\gamma)=G(\gamma)
. The mapping is given by
Eq.

27
:

\displaystyle h(\gamma;\ell)

\displaystyle=F_{h}^{-1}(F_{\gamma}(\gamma);\ell)=h_{0}+\Sigma(\ell)G^{-1}(G(\gamma))=h_{0}+\Sigma(\ell)\gamma\,.

(36)

For the superposition,
Eq.

22
, we also need the structure function conditional on
\gamma
,
\widetilde{S}
.
To this end, we choose the
h
-dependent structure function
S(\ell,h)
according to the multifractal formulation
Eq.

2
,
from which we compute the
\gamma
-dependent structure function by
Eq.

26
:

\begin{split}\widetilde{S}(\ell;\gamma)&=S(\ell;h(\gamma;\ell))\\
&=2\sigma_{u}^{2}\left(1+\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)^{2}\right)^{-h(\gamma;\ell)}\\
&=2\sigma_{u}^{2}\left(1+\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)^{2}\right)^{-h_{0}-\gamma\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{h}^{2}}{\log(1+(L/\ell)^{2})}}}\,.\end{split}

(37)

Through
Eq.

16
, this translates into the
\gamma
-dependent covariance function

\widetilde{C}(\ell;\gamma)=\sigma_{u}^{2}-\sigma_{u}^{2}\left(1+\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)^{2}\right)^{-h_{0}-\gamma\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_{h}^{2}}{\log(1+(L/\ell)^{2})}}}\,.

(38)

which can be used to construct the full ensemble of Gaussian fields,
Eq.

13
. Therefore, we have defined a fully explicit characteristic functional, comprehensively describing field statistics, including lognormal multifractal increment statistics.

While analytically simple, the model allows for arbitrarily negative Hölder exponents, which are unphysical. This issue can be fixed by adding bounds
h_{\min}\leq h\leq h_{\max}
, as discussed in
Section

2.1
. The corresponding PDF of Hölder exponents,
Eq.

4
, then becomes a truncated Gaussian,

f_{h}(h;\ell)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{Z(\ell)}\exp\left[-\log\left(1+\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)^{2}\right)\frac{(h-h_{0})^{2}}{4\sigma_{h}^{2}}\right],&\text{if }h_{\min}\leq h\leq h_{\max}\,,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(39)

where
Z(\ell)
is a new appropriate normalization. Its CDF is

F_{h}(h;\ell)=\int_{-\infty}^{h}\,\mathrm{d}h^{\prime}f_{h}(h^{\prime};\ell)=\frac{G\left(\frac{h-h_{0}}{\Sigma(\ell)}\right)-G\left(\frac{h_{\min}-h_{0}}{\Sigma(\ell)}\right)}{G\left(\frac{h_{\max}-h_{0}}{\Sigma(\ell)}\right)-G\left(\frac{h_{\min}-h_{0}}{\Sigma(\ell)}\right)}\,,\quad\text{if }h_{\min}\leq h\leq h_{\max}\,,

(40)

with which we compute a new mapping,

\displaystyle h(\gamma;\ell)

\displaystyle=F_{h}^{-1}(F_{\gamma}(\gamma);\ell)

(41)

\displaystyle=h_{0}+\Sigma(\ell)\,G^{-1}\left(G\left(\frac{h_{\min}-h_{0}}{\Sigma(\ell)}\right)+G(\gamma)\left(G\left(\frac{h_{\max}-h_{0}}{\Sigma(\ell)}\right)-G\left(\frac{h_{\min}-h_{0}}{\Sigma(\ell)}\right)\right)\right)\,.

(42)

This expression can be used in
Eq.

37
for an improved version of the Gaussian ensemble with lognormal statistics.

Recently, a Gaussian ensemble with lognormal statistics was introduced by
Friedrich et al. 2021
. This approach relies on the combination of a fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with a rescaling of space depending on a lognormal, scale-independent random parameter. It can be shown that the specific transformation in
Friedrich et al. 2021
to construct the ensemble of characteristic functionals corresponds to the lognormal case of the general transform method presented here, but with the scale-dependent parameter being the coarse-grained dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{\ell}
instead of the Hölder exponent
h
.

5
General multifractal Gaussian ensemble

The mapping
Eq.

27
is applicable to general Hölder exponent PDFs
f_{h}(h;\ell)
and structure functions
S(\ell;h)
. In this section, we apply this mapping to a multifractal model with an arbitrary spectrum of exponents. We include bounds for the Hölder exponent, as in the previous section, and add a realistic viscous-range cut-off to the formulation. The main goal of this section is to demonstrate the capacity of the model to reproduce the features of general multifractal models for the increment and gradient statistics of turbulence. For the sake of presentation, we make particular choices for the form of
f_{h}
,
S
, and various parameters, to be able to evaluate the model numerically and compare to DNS data.

In general, various modeling choices for
S(\ell;h)
and
f_{h}(h;\ell)
are possible as long as they have certain asymptotic scalings in the dissipation, inertial, and large-scale ranges. The onset of the dissipation range is built into the multifractal formalism by local Kolmogorov scales
\eta(h)\ll L
that are functions of the Hölder exponent. They depend on the Reynolds number in the following way
(
Paladin & Vulpiani 1987
;
Nelkin 1990
;
Frisch 1995
)
:

\eta(h)=LR^{-\tfrac{1}{1+h}}\,,

(43)

where
R=\Rey/\Rey_{*}
, with
\Rey
the large-scale Reynolds number and
\Rey_{*}
a tuning coefficient. A general Hölder exponent PDF
f_{h}(h;\ell)
, for
h_{\min}\leq h\leq h_{\max}
, must have the asymptotic scaling:

f_{h}(h;\ell)\sim\begin{cases}{\displaystyle\left(\frac{\eta(h)}{L}\right)^{1-D(h)}\,,}&\text{if }\ell\ll\eta(h)\,,\par\\
{\displaystyle\left(\frac{\ell}{L}\right)^{1-D(h)}\,,}&\text{if }\eta(h)\ll\ell\ll L\,,\par\\
{1\,,}&\text{if }L\ll\ell\,.\par\end{cases}

(44)

Additionally, a general
S(\ell;h)
must have the asymptotic scaling:

\frac{S(\ell;h)}{2\sigma_{u}^{2}}\sim\begin{cases}{\displaystyle\left(\frac{\ell}{\eta(h)}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\eta(h)}{L}\right)^{2h}\,,}&\text{if }\ell\ll\eta(h)\,,\par\\
{\displaystyle\left(\frac{\ell}{L}\right)^{2h}\,,}&\text{if }\eta(h)\ll\ell\ll L\,,\par\\
1&\text{if }L\ll\ell\,.\par\end{cases}

(45)

Using the general transformation outlined in
Section

3
, any such choice of
f_{h}
and
S
can be used to construct a Gaussian ensemble with multifractal scaling. In the dissipative range, if the conditional structure function,
S(\ell;h)
,
matches
Eq.

45
, then it shows smooth behavior for a fixed Hölder exponent.
Therefore, the gradient variance of each subensemble is then readily computed as

\sigma_{A}^{2}=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow 0}\frac{S(\ell;h)}{\ell^{2}}=2\frac{\sigma_{u}^{2}}{L^{2}}R^{\frac{2(1-h)}{1+h}}\,.

(46)

For the subsequent discussion, let us specify particular forms of
f_{h}
,
S
, and the distribution of
\gamma
. For the conditional structure function, we choose the following extension to
Eq.

2
:

S(\ell;h)=2\sigma_{u}^{2}\frac{\left(1+(L/\ell)^{2}\right)^{-h}}{(1+(\eta(h)/\ell)^{2\lambda})^{(1-h)/\lambda}}\,,

(47)

which is essentially how dissipation range statistics are included in
Chevillard et al. 2012
.
The additional parameter
\lambda
is a tuning coefficient that controls the onset of the dissipation range.
Analogous to
Eq.

47
, we also add a viscous-range cut-off to the distribution of Hölder exponents:

f_{h}(h;\ell)=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z(\ell)}\,\frac{\left(1+(\eta(h)/\ell)^{2\lambda}\right)^{(1-D(h))/2\lambda}}{\left(1+(L/\ell)^{2}\right)^{(1-D(h))/2}}\,,}&\text{if }h_{\min}\leq h\leq h_{\max}\,,\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(48)

where
Z(\ell)
is the normalization of this PDF. The unfixed model parameters
\Rey_{*}
and
\lambda
are chosen in
Section

5.2
by matching our model with the DNS results in Fig.
3
for the case
n=2
.

Since the distribution of the scale-independent parameter
\gamma
can be freely chosen, for simplicity, we opt here for a uniform distribution between
0
and
1
. As a result, the ensemble of functionals
Eq.

13
can be written as

\Phi[\alpha]=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\gamma\,\Phi_{G}[\alpha;\gamma]\,.

(49)

Recognizing that the CDF of
\gamma
is
F_{\gamma}(\gamma)=\gamma
(for
\gamma
between
0
and
1
),
Eq.

28
tells us that
\gamma
is equal to the CDF of the Hölder exponent,

\gamma(h;\ell)=F_{h}(h;\ell)=\int_{-\infty}^{h}\mathrm{d}h^{\prime}f_{h}(h^{\prime};\ell).

(50)

6
Conclusion

The central contribution of this work is that it establishes a connection between multifractal models and functional approaches to turbulence based on ensembles of Gaussian fields. We developed an approach that promotes a multifractal model for increment statistics to a model for entire fields. This is accomplished by a change of variables that transforms a scale-dependent distribution of fluctuating Hölder exponents to a scale-independent distribution over the new parameter
\gamma
. This allows for the superposition of Gaussian characteristic functionals parameterized on
\gamma
, which then enables the computation of arbitrary field statistics, involving velocity, gradients or increments, for instance.

In the current work, we first demonstrated an analytically-tractable instance of the transformation procedure that reproduces velocity increment statistics consistent with the lognormal model. After that we introduced a more general form of the transformation that can encode multifractal increment and gradient statistics for any singularity spectrum
D(h)
. The resulting characteristic functional can be used to model data from direct numerical simulations (or experiments).

Beyond the present work, the hope is that characteristic functionals as constructed here could also be helpful in the context of functional approaches to turbulence such as the Hopf equation
(
Hopf 1952
)
, which is an elegant way to encode the field statistics of turbulence.
A major open question towards this goal is the inclusion of skewness into the current framework, which is crucial to model the effects of self-advection and energy transfer across scales.
Another topic for future investigation is the prediction of multiscale correlations of velocity increments
(
Benzi et al. 1998
;
Benzi et al. 1999
;
Friedrich et al. 2018
)
by the characteristic functional models constructed in this work.

Appendix A
Connection to the ensemble by Friedrich et al. (2021)

In their work on the explicit construction of joint multipoint statistics,
Friedrich et al. 2021
constructed an ensemble of characteristic functionals exhibiting statistics of the Kolmogorov-Obukhov model (K62;
Obukhov 1962

Obukhov 1962
;
Kolmogorov 1962

Kolmogorov 1962
). The K62 model is formulated in terms of
\varepsilon_{\ell}
, the energy dissipation rate coarse-grained over scale
\ell
. For simplicity, we focus on the inertial range,
\eta\ll\ell\ll L
, with
\eta
the classic Kolmogorov dissipative scale, independent of
h
. Increment statistics in K62 can be implemented as a superposition of Gaussian distributions
(
Castaing et al. 1990
;
Friedrich 2025
)
,

\displaystyle f_{v}(v;\ell)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{\ell}\,f_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon_{\ell};\ell)g\left(v;S(\ell;\varepsilon_{\ell})\right)\,,

(56)

with

\displaystyle S(\ell;\varepsilon_{\ell})

\displaystyle=U^{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{\ell}}{\overline{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2/3}\left(\frac{\ell}{L}\right)^{2/3}\,.

(57)

The distribution of
\varepsilon_{\ell}
is assumed to be a scale-dependent lognormal distribution

\displaystyle f_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon_{\ell};\ell)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{\ell}\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}(\ell)}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\log(\varepsilon_{\ell})-M(\ell))^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}(\ell)}\right)

(58)

with location parameter

\displaystyle M(\ell)=\log(\overline{\varepsilon})-\frac{\sigma^{2}(\ell)}{2}

(59)

and scale parameter

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\ell)=\mu\log\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)\,.

(60)

The K62 model is equivalent to a multifractal model with quadratic singularity spectrum (
6
) and without bounds on
h
. The difference is that
\varepsilon_{\ell}
is used as a fluctuating parameter instead of
h
. The equivalence can be seen by applying the transformation
\varepsilon_{\ell}=\overline{\varepsilon}\left(\ell/L\right)^{3h-1}
to (
2
), (
3
), and (
4
) and setting
h_{0}=1/3+\mu/6
and
\sigma_{h}^{2}=\mu/9
while taking the inertial range limit.

With this in mind, we can apply the methodology outlined in
Section

3
to the K62 model, while replacing each instance of
h
by
\varepsilon_{\ell}
. Contrary to
Section

4
, we choose a unit lognormal distribution for
\gamma
, whose CDF is

\displaystyle F_{\gamma}(\gamma)=G(\log(\gamma))\,.

(61)

The CDF of the fluctuating parameter
\varepsilon_{\ell}
with scale-dependent PDF (
58
) reads

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon_{\ell}}(\varepsilon_{\ell};\ell)=G\left(\frac{\log(\varepsilon_{\ell})-M(\ell)}{\sigma(\ell)}\right)\,.

(62)

Then, the structure functions of the
\gamma
-ensemble can be computed by
Eq.

26
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}(\ell;\gamma)

\displaystyle=S\left(\ell;F_{\varepsilon_{\ell}}^{-1}(F_{\gamma}(\gamma);\ell)\right)

(63)

\displaystyle=U^{2}\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)^{-2/3-\mu/3}\gamma^{\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\mu\log\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)}}

(64)

This turns out to be the same inertial-range form of the structure function as proposed in
Friedrich et al. 2021
. As the basis for their model, they use fractional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes with Hölder exponent
H=1/3
. These are Gaussian processes with a specific, analytically known correlation function. For our considerations, it is only important to know that the corresponding structure function scales as

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{K41}}(\ell)\sim\ell^{2H}=\ell^{2/3}

(65)

for
\ell\ll L
, which is K41 scaling
(
Kolmogorov 1941
)
. Then, the subensemble structure functions (
64
), which we obtained by application of our method to the K62 model, can also be obtained by applying the following length-scale transform to the K41 structure functions (
65
):

\displaystyle\ell\rightarrow\gamma^{\sqrt{\mu\log\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)}}\left(\frac{L}{\ell}\right)^{-\mu/2}\ell\,.

(66)

This is very close to the transform proposed in
Friedrich et al. 2021
for a fluctuating parameter
\xi\equiv\gamma
.
Overall, this means that the length scale transform proposed by
Friedrich et al. 2021
can be considered a special case of the more general transform presented here. However, choices regarding the small- and large-scale cut-off were made differently.

Appendix B
Generalization to vector fields

Our ensemble approach can be straightforwardly generalized to vector fields. To illustrate that, consider a three-dimensional incompressible velocity field
\bm{u}(\bm{x})
. Analogous to
Eq.

11

the characteristic functional generalizes to

\Phi[\bm{\alpha};\gamma]=\left\langle\exp\left[\mathrm{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\alpha_{i}(x)u_{i}(x)\right]\right\rangle

(67)

where summation over repeated indices is implied and
\bm{\alpha}(\bm{x})
is a three-dimensional test function. If
\bm{u}(\bm{x})
is an incompressible statistically homogeneous Gaussian vector field with zero mean, its characteristic functional takes the form

\Phi^{G}[\bm{\alpha}]=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}^{\prime}\alpha_{i}(\bm{x})\widetilde{C}_{ij}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime};\gamma)\alpha_{j}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\right],

(68)

analogous to
Eq.

12
. Assuming statistical isotropy and incompressibility, the covariance tensor
\widetilde{C}_{ij}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime};\gamma)=\langle u_{i}(\bm{x})u_{j}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\rangle
can be written as
(
Pope 2000
)

\widetilde{C}_{ij}(\bm{r};\gamma)=\sigma_{u}^{2}\left(\left[\tilde{f}(r;\gamma)+\frac{1}{2}r\tilde{f}^{\prime}(r;\gamma)\right]\delta_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}r\tilde{f}^{\prime}(r;\gamma)\,\hat{r}_{i}\hat{r}_{j}\right)

(69)

where
\tilde{f}(r;\gamma)
is the longitudinal velocity autocorrelation function, prime indicates a derivative with respect to
r
;
\hat{\bm{r}}
denotes the unit vector in the direction of
\bm{r}
.

As a next step, we can relate the longitudinal autocorrelation function to the conditional structure function

\tilde{f}(r;\gamma)=1-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{u}^{2}}\tilde{S}(r;\gamma)

(70)

which then allows to establish the relationship between a multifractal model for the increment PDFs as outlined in
Section

3
. Once the covariance tensor is determined this way, the Gaussian ensemble
Eq.

13
straightforwardly generalizes.

Quite obviously, addressing the question of how to include skewness into this framework is even more relevant for the generalization to vectorial statistics. This is, however, beyond the scope of the current work.
