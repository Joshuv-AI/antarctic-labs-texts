Proton‐Coupled Electron Transfer in Reaction of Catecholamines: Experimental and Computational Approach
Authors: Ana Karković Marković, Mia Jovanić, Dijana Kolezarić, Viktor Pilepić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biogenic catecholamines (dopamine, noradrenaline, adrenaline) exhibit a dual role in biological systems, acting as both endogenous antioxidants and potential sources of oxidative stress. The core of their antiradical activity is the catechol moiety that enables efficient radical scavenging via hydrogen and/or electron transfer. In this study, the reactivity of catecholamines toward the DPPH• radical was investigated experimentally and computationally in predominantly nonpolar 1,4‐dioxane–water media. Experimental rate constants, kinetic isotope effects, and thermodynamic activation parameters suggest that, under these conditions, the reactions proceed via a concerted hydrogen transfer mechanism, best described as proton‐coupled electron transfer (PCET). The proposed PCET‐like reaction mechanism is supported by intrinsic bond orbital analysis along the intrinsic reaction coordinate path. Average local ionization energy (ALIE) analysis showed that the most reactive electron‐donating sites are associated with π electrons above and below the aromatic ring of the catecholamine compounds. Additionally, kinetic isotope effects and anomalous Arrhenius parameters provide evidence for the involvement of hydrogen tunneling. Complementary pH‐dependent kinetic studies with dopamine suggest a mechanistic shift toward a faster sequential proton loss‐electron transfer pathway with increasing catecholate formation. These findings offer new insight into the antioxidant mechanisms of catecholamines and provide the first experimental indication of hydrogen tunneling in their radical‐scavenging reactions.

Introduction

Catecholamines—dopamine (DA), noradrenaline (NA), and adrenaline (AD)—are a group of bioactive amines that function as neurotransmitters and hormones (Figure 1). DA acts mainly within the brain to regulate motor control, motivation, reward, and executive functions, while NA and AD operate across neural and endocrine systems to modulate autonomic activity via adrenergic receptors [1, 2]. Catecholamines exhibit a dual nature in the organism, acting as both radical scavengers and potential triggers of oxidative stress. They can undergo autooxidation to form aminochromes, a process often catalyzed by transition metals like iron and copper, which generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) and causes oxidative DNA damage [3]. This prooxidant activity may be a key factor in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s diseases [4, 5, 6]. On the other hand, catecholamines exhibit pronounced antioxidant and antiradical activity that primarily arises from the catechol (ortho‐dihydroxybenzene) moiety, which enables efficient hydrogen atom and/or electron donation to ROS. Across chemical, biochemical, and cellular studies, catecholamines act as endogenous radical scavengers, although their effectiveness strongly depends on pH, redox conditions, and the microenvironment (aqueous vs. lipid systems) [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

Structures of catecholamines.

A comprehensive classification of the reaction mechanisms governing antioxidant‐radical interactions distinguishes between single‐step and multistep pathways [12]. Single‐step mechanisms include hydrogen atom transfer (HAT), radical adduct formation (RAF), and proton‐coupled electron transfer (PCET), while multistep mechanisms comprise sequential proton loss‐electron transfer (SPLET) and sequential electron‐proton transfer (SEPT or SET‐PT). The predominance and thermodynamic or kinetic feasibility of these mechanisms depend on multiple factors, including the concentration and intrinsic reactivity of both the antioxidant species and the radicals involved, the polarity of the surrounding medium, and, particularly in aqueous systems, the pH of the environment. Consequently, elucidating the operative pathways typically requires integrating experimental measurements with theoretical modeling due to the complexity of these reactive processes [12]. Several recent investigations have examined the reaction mechanisms underlying the antioxidant activity of catecholamines using both theoretical and experimental approaches, each contributing complementary insights into their chemical reactivity.

Experimental studies on hydrogen atom abstraction from catecholamines by cumylperoxyl, cumyloxyl, and galvinoxyl radicals in acetonitrile or propionitrile have demonstrated that these compounds exhibit pronounced radical‐scavenging activity—particularly DA, whose reactivity is comparable to that of potent antioxidants such as (+)‐catechin [10, 13]. The reaction of catecholamines with the galvinoxyl and cumyloxyl radical has been proposed to proceed via a one‐step HAT mechanism, whereas DA additionally appears to react against the galvinoxyl radical through a metal ion‐coupled electron transfer (MCET) mechanism followed by PT in the presence of Mg2+ ions [10]. AD has been shown to possess antiradical reactivity toward tert‐butoxyl radicals and benzophenone triplet states in acetonitrile, acting as an efficient hydrogen donor [14]. The antioxidant properties of catecholamines have also been investigated in micellar and liposomal model systems that mimic biological membranes [7, 15, 16]. These studies suggest that catecholamines may function as retardants or inhibitors of lipid peroxidation, depending on the specific lipid system, the nature of their interaction with the membrane, and the presence of additional bioactive components such as vitamin E analogs or superoxide dismutase.

Theoretical studies on the reactions of catecholamines and hydroxyl (•OH), hydroperoxyl (•OOH), methylperoxy, and DPPH• radicals in aqueous and lipidic simulated environments have provided insight into different plausible reaction mechanisms by which these processes may occur [8, 9, 17, 18]. In aqueous solutions at physiological pH, the dominant pathways governing the global reactivity of DA toward •OH radicals are RAF and SEPT, although HAT and PCET also remain energetically accessible. In contrast, in a low‐polarity medium such as pentyl ethanoate, the principal reaction channels shift to RAF and HAT [9]. In the case of catecholamine reactions with the DPPH• radical, the SPLET mechanism is considered the most probable pathway in polar solvents such as water and methanol, while in nonpolar media, SPLET and HAT/PCET pathways appear to be competitive. Furthermore, as the authors noted, due to the structural complexity and steric hindrance associated with the DPPH• radical, its reaction with catecholamines cannot be described purely as a HAT or PCET process [8]. Indeed, distinguishing between HAT and PCET is inherently challenging as both mechanisms yield identical products. The key mechanistic difference lies in the fact that in HAT the proton and electron are transferred together as a hydrogen atom from the same donor to the same acceptor, whereas in PCET they are transferred concertedly in a single elementary step but as distinct particles, typically involving different donor and acceptor orbitals [12, 19].

Recent experimental work on the reactions of catecholamines with the DPPH• radical in polar methanol‐water media demonstrated that changes in pH, through modulation of catecholamine protonation states, significantly affect reaction rates and support the SPLET mechanism [7]. At the same time, experimental studies addressing the reaction mechanisms of catecholamines with the DPPH• radical in predominantly nonpolar environments are largely lacking. Therefore, the objective of this study was to examine both experimental and computational evidence for HAT/PCET mechanisms in the reactions of catecholamines with the DPPH• radical in a largely nonpolar 1,4‐dioxane–water solvent system (0.95:0.05 v/v), where these pathways are expected to dominate. To achieve this, reaction rate constants, kinetic isotope effects (KIEs), and thermodynamic activation parameters were determined and analyzed for the reactions of DA, AD, and NA with the DPPH• radical in acidic conditions within this solvent mixture. Complementary density functional theory (DFT) calculations of transition‐state structures, followed by intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) and intrinsic bond orbital (IBO) analyses, provided further insight into the nature of these reactions. Additionally, reaction rates for DA with DPPH• were experimentally determined in a series of buffered 1,4‐dioxane–water systems (0.75:0.25 v/v) over the pH range 4.61–8.16, revealing a mechanistic shift. Altogether, the findings of this study support a PCET‐like mechanism in the reactions of catecholamines with the DPPH• radical and, to the best of our knowledge, offer the first indication of hydrogen tunneling in antioxidant reactions of catecholamines.

Results and Discussion

Reaction of Catecholamines and DPPH• Radical

The radical‐scavenging activity of catecholamines toward DPPH• has been recognized for decades [20, 21, 22]. However, detailed experimental investigations into the mechanisms underlying these reactions have only recently emerged, motivated by efforts to better understand the potential role of catecholamines as endogenous antioxidants [7]. DPPH• is a stable organic radical distinguished by a strong absorption band in the visible region (λ max ≈ 520 nm). It is widely employed as a model radical for probing the reactivity of phenolic compounds toward peroxyl radicals (ROO•), which play a central role in oxidative processes in biological systems [23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28].

In general, the reaction between phenols and DPPH•, commonly described as formal hydrogen atom abstraction from the phenol, can be represented by the Equation (1), followed by one of two possible irreversible reaction steps (2 and 3) [19, 23, 24]:

(1) ArOH+DPPH• → ArO•+DPPH–H

(2) ArO• + DPPH• → products

(3) ArO•+ArO• →dimer or disproportionation products

Reaction (1) is expected to proceed via a HAT or PCET mechanism in nonpolar or predominantly nonpolar media [8, 19]. Reactions (2) and (3) are radical–radical reactions and are therefore expected to proceed more rapidly than reaction (1). Considering Reaction (2), in which an additional DPPH• radical is consumed, the overall reaction stoichiometry is commonly assumed to be ArOH:DPPH• = 1:2. This stoichiometry has also been experimentally confirmed for several catechol‐containing molecules with similar structure [29, 30, 31].

The reactions of the catecholamines DA, AD, and NA with DPPH• were carried out in a 1,4‐dioxane–water solvent system (0.95:0.05 v/v), acidified with HCl (or DCl) to ensure that the catecholamines remained fully protonated. This precaution was necessary because even trace amounts of catecholate anions, which could arise due to minor solvent impurities, can substantially increase the reaction rate, as previously demonstrated by Foti et al. in similar reaction systems [29]. The second‐order rate constants were calculated from the pseudo‐first‐order rate constants according to the rate law previously established for the reactions of similar catecholic antioxidants with DPPH• under similar reaction conditions [30, 31]:

−d[DPPH•]/dt = 2k[DPPH•][ArOH]

kobs = 2k[ArOH]

A representative pseudo‐first‐order decay trace of DPPH• for the reaction of the catecholamines with DPPH• under the reaction conditions employed is shown in Figure 2. Experimental second‐order rate constants determined in the present study (Tables 1, S1–S4 and Figures S1–S4) are generally in the range of the values 10−1–102 dm3 mol−1 s−1 predicted by calculations for the reactions of catecholamines with DPPH• proceeding via a HAT/PCET mechanism [8]. Furthermore, the obtained rate constants are comparable to those reported for the reactions of caffeic acid, methyl caffeate, hydroxytyrosol, and homovanillyl alcohol with DPPH• (0.15–2.56 dm3 mol−1 s−1), measured in similar reaction media, in which the PCET mechanism has been confirmed through both experimental and computational approach [30, 31]. In contrast, the reactions of catecholamines with DPPH• in polar methanol‐water media, where a fraction of catecholamines is present in the deprotonated form (presumably due to deprotonation of the first catecholic hydroxyl group), were shown to proceed via a SPLET mechanism, with rate constants 2–4 orders of magnitude higher than those observed in this study [7]. A similar trend of rate constant enhancement with increasing pH, attributable to the formation of the corresponding catecholate anion, was detected in the present study, as discussed in a later section. Thermodynamic activation parameters for the reactions between catecholamines and DPPH• were determined through kinetic measurements over the temperature range of 14–45°C (Tables 1, S5–S7 and Figures S5–S7). The values obtained are in close agreement with experimental values reported for analogous reaction systems involving antioxidants bearing an o‐diphenol functional group [30, 31]. Also, the experimentally determined ΔG ‡ values for the reactions of DA, AD, and NA reported here, are comparable to the corresponding values calculated for these reactions in vacuum (77.1, 56.4 and 60.1 kJ mol−1, respectively) [8].

Experimental Evidence of HAT/PCET Mechanism in the Reaction of Catecholamines and DPPH• Radical

To elucidate and gain deeper insight into the mechanism of hydrogen transfer in the reaction of catecholamines and DPPH•, an analysis of KIEs was carried out. KIEs were determined as the ratio k H/k D of reaction rate constants measured in 1,4‐dioxane–water (H2O or D2O) 0.95:0.05 v/v medium (Table 2). The deuterated catecholamine molecules were generated through rapid exchange of the catechol hydroxyl protons with deuterons from D2O. In general, KIE values greater than 1 indicate that PT is involved in the rate‐determining step of a reaction, thereby excluding the ET as the slowest step in the reaction [32, 33, 34]. In this study, the measured KIE values for the reactions of DA, AD, and NA with the DPPH• radical were 11.0, 12.2, and 11.5, respectively. Such KIEs strongly suggest that PT is involved in the rate‐determining step, and consequently the SPLET and SEPT mechanisms (based on ET as the slowest step) can be excluded as viable pathways under the experimental conditions employed. Specifically, a significant contribution of the SPLET mechanism is unlikely under the present experimental conditions because the formation of catecholate species is expected to be highly unfavorable owing to the increased pK a values of phenolic groups in low‐polarity media [35]. This conclusion is further supported by our experimental observation that increasing the solvent polarity by raising the water content from 5% to 25% (v/v) at a constant acid concentration did not significantly affect the second‐order rate constant (0.256 (0.001) dm3 mol−1 s−1 vs. 0.244 (0.005) dm3 mol−1 s−1) indicating that any contribution of catecholate species, and consequently of the SPLET mechanism, to the observed kinetics is negligible.

Also, the consecutive PT/ET mechanism with PT as the rate‐determining step is highly unlikely due to expected high pK a values of phenolic groups in a low‐polarity solvent such as 1,4‐dioxane [35], which would render the standard reaction Gibbs energy Δr G ⊖ for this step greater than the experimentally determined ΔG ‡ values (Table 1). According to thermochemical analysis and with the assumption that ΔG ‡ is always at least equal to or greater than the Δr G ⊖ initial PT in the consecutive PT/ET mechanism could be eliminated as the possible first step [30, 36]. By eliminating stepwise mechanisms of hydrogen transfer, the only viable pathway becomes the single‐step HAT or PCET mechanism, as was proposed earlier in the computational study on the reactions of catecholamines with DPPH• in a medium of low polarity [8]. Although the experimental kinetic data from this study indicate that PT is involved in the rate‐determining step, they do not by themselves allow an unambiguous distinction between concerted PCET and HAT. Therefore, a computational analysis was necessary to identify the operative reaction mechanism.

Another observation arising from the KIEs and the isotopic differences in thermodynamic activation parameters (Table 2) is the involvement of hydrogen tunneling in the reactions of catecholamines with DPPH•. For OH bonds, the upper limit of KIE within semiclassical theory is approximately 8, or up to 13 when bending vibrational modes are included in the calculation [37]. In this study, the experimentally observed KIEs are closer to upper semi‐classical limits. Moreover, the determined ratios of Arrhenius pre‐exponential factors, A H/A D, from 0.017 to 0.049, fall well outside the semi‐classical range of 0.7–1.2, and the isotopic differences in activation energy (ΔE a) from 13.5 to 16.1 kJ mol−1 exceed 5.1 kJ mol−1 for OH bond dissociation [37, 38]. Hydrogen tunneling in reactions involving DPPH• has also been documented for several natural phenolic antioxidants—caffeic acid, methyl caffeate, hydroxytyrosol, and homovanillyl alcohol—in similar reaction conditions [30, 31], as well as in the reaction of α‐tocopherol with β‐CD‐solubilized DPPH• in water [39]. Moreover, earlier experimental studies have confirmed hydrogen tunneling in small reaction systems of other antioxidants, notably those of vitamin C with TEMPO• radical and hexacyanoferrate(III) ions [40, 41, 42].

Effect of pH on Reaction Rate of Dopamine and DPPH• Radical

The reaction between DA and the DPPH• radical was further examined in a series of buffered 1,4‐dioxane–water mixtures (0.75:0.25, v/v) across the pH range 4.61–8.16. The corresponding reaction rate constants and KIEs are summarized in Table 3 and Figure 3 (additional data in Tables S8–S11 and Figures S8–S11). The observed increase in the rate constants can be rationalized by considering the growing fraction of the catecholate anion formed upon deprotonation of DA. The acid–base properties of DA have been characterized in previous studies using a variety of experimental approaches and computational methods as well, typically in aqueous media, with reported pK a1 values ranging from 8.59 to 9.05 [43, 44, 45, 46]. Although the order of deprotonation of the phenolic and amino groups in DA remains under debate, the most widely supported interpretation is that the pK a1 and pK a2 cannot be unambiguously assigned to the deprotonation of individual functional groups. Instead, these constants reflect a superposition of the two deprotonation processes [7]. In a recent study by Jodko‐Piórecka et al., the reaction between DA and DPPH• investigated in a methanol‐water (1:1 v/v) system yielded a slightly lower pK a1 value of 8.37, and according to this value, the degree of deprotonation α was 0.13% at pH 5.5 and 9.69% at pH 7.4 [7]. The corresponding second‐order rate constants under these conditions were 1200 and 170 000 dm3 mol−1 s−1 respectively, indicating an approximately two‐order‐of‐magnitude increase in k that parallels the increase in α. In this study, although the rate constants are overall lower compared to those from methanol‐water (1:1, v/v) system, a four‐order‐of‐magnitude increase in k H was observed, across the range of ~3.5 pH units, as presented in Table 3. The lower magnitude of the rate constants may be attributed to the reduced polarity of the buffered 1,4‐dioxane–water (0.75:0.25, v/v) reaction medium compared with the methanol‐water (1:1, v/v) system due to both, the lower dielectric constant of 1,4‐dioxane relative to methanol and the lower water content in the 1,4‐dioxane–containing mixture. The observed increase in the rate constants in buffered 1,4‐dioxane–water system can be attributed to the formation of the DA catecholate anion, whose concentration rises with increasing pH of the reaction medium, as governed by the pK a1 value of DA. Under these conditions, the measured rate constant k obs reflects contributions from two parallel reactions: a slower reaction involving protonated phenolic groups of neutral DA, and a faster reaction involving the DA catecholate anion (or zwitterion), both reacting with DPPH•. Notably, the experimental rate constants show very good agreement with values calculated using a parallel‐reaction model (Table S12 and Figure S12), supporting this interpretation (see Supporting Information for details). The pH‐dependent increase in k H was accompanied by a pronounced decrease in the KIE value, from 10.9 in acidic media to approximately 3 at pH 6.70 and 7.75, indicating a likely shift in the reaction mechanism from a slower HAT/PCET pathway to a significantly faster SPLET mechanism.

Computational Analysis of Hydrogen Transfer in Reaction of Catecholamines and DPPH•

In the study of HAT from DA, AD, and NA to DPPH•, a series of DFT computational analyses were performed, including transition state (TS), IRC [47] and IBO calculations [48, 49, 50]. TSs were obtained for hydrogen transfer from both catecholic OH groups, with the reaction pathway involving the 4‐OH group having lower activation energies for all catecholamines and representing the more favorable reaction pathway. The obtained TS structures are shown in Figures S13 and S20. The normal mode displacement vector for a unique imaginary frequency is associated with a motion of the H atom of the 4‐OH or 3‐OH moiety of catecholamines to the N‍ ‍atom of DPPH•. The hydrogen atom donor (H···O) and hydrogen atom acceptor (N···H) distances and the N···H···O bond angles for the obtained TS structures are shown in Tables 4 and S13. These structural parameters are comparable to the corresponding values obtained for the calculated TS in similar reactions of DPPH• with dopamine [8], phenol and p‐metoxyphenol [19], homovanillic acid, hydroxytyrosol and caffeic acid [30] and methyl caffeate [31]. The calculated Gibbs activation energies, Table 5, agree with the corresponding obtained experimental values, which support the obtained calculated TS structures. The IBO analysis, proposed by Knizia et al. [48, 49, 50], enables the visualization of molecular orbitals, their changes and the electron flow along the reaction path, and therefore has proven useful in the investigation and elucidation of reaction mechanisms. In the context of hydrogen transfer reactions, IBO analysis has proven successful in distinguishing between HAT and PCET reaction mechanisms [50, 52]. The IBOs involved in hydrogen transfer from the 4‐OH and 3‐OH groups of DA, AD, and NA to DPPH• were analyzed and plotted along the IRC pathway. For clarity of presentation, IBOs are shown separately for the electron transfer and PT parts of the overall hydrogen transfer reaction; α and β spin electrons are also shown separately as α IBO and β IBO. In the case of DA, Figures 4 and 5 represent IBOs that have undergone changes corresponding to the electron transfer and PT components, respectively, of the hydrogen transfer reaction. The IBO changes shown in these figures occur simultaneously, from the activated complex resembling the reactants (R), through the TS, to the activated complex resembling the products (P), and are presented separately for clarity as mentioned above. In Figure 4, structures (a) and (d), α IBO and β IBO, are arranged in the same way and occupy the same space around the atoms and represent the electron pair in the bonding molecular orbital of the aromatic ring, which will be shown to be the electron‐donor site. The α IBO in (g) corresponds to the unpaired electron on the DPPH• N10 atom, which serves as the electron‐acceptor site. In the TS, the transferred electron, represented by β IBO in (e), is distributed between the electron‐donor and electron‐acceptor sites and is finally transferred to the N10 atom of DPPH (f). In the activated complex resembling products (P), α IBO (i) and β IBO (f) represent the α and β spin electrons of the nonbonding lone pair on the N10 atom of DPPH. The α IBO on the aromatic ring (c) represents the unpaired electron of the formed DA radical. It should be noted that none of these IBOs involved in the electron transfer part of the hydrogen transfer reaction are associated with the transferred H atom.

The IBOs involved in the PT of the overall hydrogen transfer reaction between DA and DPPH• are shown in Figure 5. The α IBO (a) and β IBO (d), which occupy the same space around the atoms, form the electron pair of the bonding σ orbital and the proton‐donor site of the DA 4‐OH group, while α IBO (g) and β IBO (j) represent the lone pair at the N9 atom of DPPH• and the proton‐acceptor site. During the reaction, the α and β spin electrons represented by α IBO and β IBO, remain paired forming the N9‐H σ bond on the proton‐acceptor (IBOs in (i) and (l)), and leaving a lone pair on the 4‐O DA radical (IBOs in (c) and (f)). Changes in spin and localized charge on the transferred H51 atom, the N10 atom of DPPH• representing the electron‐acceptor site, and the aromatic C45 atom representing the electron‐donor site along the IRC path for hydrogen transfer from the 4‐OH moiety of DA to DPPH• are shown in Figure 6. There is no significant change in the spin or charge of the transferred H51 atom along the IRC path, indicating that the transferred electron is not associated with the transferred proton. In contrast, the N10 atom of DPPH•, on which the spin of the DPPH• radical is initially centered, loses its spin by accepting an electron from the electron‐donor, while the C45 atom of the aromatic group, which represents the electron‐donor, gains spin by losing an electron, and thereby reflects the resulting product, the DA radical. A similar and comparable IBO analysis was obtained for hydrogen transfer from the 4‐OH moiety of AD and NA to DPPH•, as well as for hydrogen transfer from the 3‐OH moiety of the catecholamines, Figures S14–S29. The DFT calculation results are consistent with a PCET‐like reaction mechanism for hydrogen transfer in these cases, because the PT and electron transfer components of HAT are distinct, originate from different reaction sites, and involve different donor and acceptor molecular orbitals. It should also be taken into account that the nonspecific solvation model applied here has limitations and that explicit solvent molecules and hydrogen‐bonding interactions could affect transition‐state structures, activation barriers, and the relative reactivity of the two catecholic OH groups.

The average local ionization energy (ALIE) Ī(r) is a descriptor of local reactivity that provides insight into the reaction sites within the molecule suitable for donating electrons in the reaction with electrophiles or radicals [53]. The calculated Ī(r) plotted on the electron density isosurface of DA, AD, and NA is shown in Figure 7. The lowest values of Ī(r), blue areas, indicate the most preferred electron‐donating sites, and for all catecholamines they are associated with π electrons above and below the aromatic rings, which supports the IBO analysis of the electron transfer part (Figure 4) of the overall hydrogen transfer reaction. The lowest ALIE Ī S,min has values of 7.30 eV, 8.13 eV and 8.17 eV for DA, AD, and NA, respectively, and this sequence is consistent with the experimentally determined rate constants for these catecholamines, Table 1. The calculated KIEs corrected for quantum tunneling effects (KIETUNN) in Table 5 are substantially larger than the experimentally observed values, suggesting only a partial contribution of tunneling to the overall process.

Conclusion

In this work, reaction rate constants, thermodynamic activation parameters, and KIEs were experimentally determined for the reactions of DA, AD, and NA with the DPPH• radical in predominantly nonpolar 1,4‐dioxane–water solvent systems. These measurements, supported by analysis of IBOs along the IRC path, are consistent with hydrogen transfer proceeding via a PCET‐like reaction mechanism under the present conditions and can be distinguished from a classical HAT pathway. The experimental KIEs, calculated KIETUNN values, and anomalous Arrhenius parameters suggest a partial contribution of hydrogen tunneling to the reaction mechanism. However, these findings, which point to the PCET mechanism with hydrogen tunneling in catecholamine radical scavenging reactions, apply only to the low‐polarity acidic medium investigated in this study and should not be generalized to biological aqueous environments, where the catecholate form predominates. As the present study shows, increasing pH promotes a mechanistic shift toward SPLET through the increased availability of catecholate species, as reflected by higher reaction rates and lower KIEs. In addition, since DPPH• differs substantially from biologically relevant radical species in both steric and electronic properties, the mechanistic insights obtained in this study should be interpreted within the context of the DPPH• model system. Future studies involving biologically more relevant radical species and heterogeneous environments will further expand our understanding of the antioxidant behavior of catecholamines under physiologically relevant conditions.

Experimental Procedures

Reagents and Chemicals

1,4‐dioxane, 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH•), HCl (1 M, Titrisol), NaOH (1 M, Titrisol), DCl (99% D), NaOD (40 wt%, 99.5% D), buffer standards pH 4 and pH 7, and acetic acid (glacial, 100%) were obtained from Merck, KGaA, (Darmstadt, Germany). Dopamine hydrochloride (≥98%), (±)‐adrenaline hydrochloride (≥98%), and DL‐noradrenaline hydrochloride (≥97%) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). K2HPO4 and KH2PO4 were supplied by J. T. Baker (Deventer, The Netherlands), while trisodium citrate dihydrate and sodium acetate anhydrous were obtained from Alkaloid (Skopje, Republic of North Macedonia). Water and heavy water (99.9% D Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were twice distilled and carbon dioxide‐ and oxygen‐free (bubbled with 99.999% N2, Messer, Germany).

Preparation of Buffer Solutions

Acetate buffer solutions were prepared by dissolving acetic acid and sodium acetate in water (H2O or D2O) and titrated with strong acid (HCl or DCl) or base (NaOH or NaOD) to the desired pH (pD). The pD values were corrected for measurements performed with a glass electrode in D2O using the relation pD = pH + 0.41 [54]. The resulting buffer solutions were then mixed with 1,4‐dioxane at a volume ratio of 1,4‐dioxane:buffer = 0.75:0.25 (v/v), after which the pH was remeasured. All pH measurements were performed using a Mettler Toledo MP 230 pH meter equipped with a combined InLab Science Pro‐ISM electrode (Mettler Toledo, Greifensee, Switzerland), suitable for measurements in organic solvent mixtures and providing a precision of ±0.01 pH units. A systematic pH drift (ΔpH) resulting from the transition from aqueous to mixed 1,4‐dioxane–water media of lower polarity was observed, with ΔpH values ranging from 2.45 to 2.66 pH units over the pH range employed for the acetate buffer. This behavior is consistent with expectations and comparable to, for example, a reported ΔpH value of 1.78 for potassium hydrogen phthalate buffer in a 1,4‐dioxane:water (0.5:0.5, w/w) mixture [55]. Citrate and phosphate buffer solutions were prepared by dissolving the appropriate buffer salts, and the remaining steps were analogous to those described previously for the acetate buffer. The observed pH drift was from 1.51 to 1.82 pH units for the citrate buffer and 1.16 for the phosphate buffer. All buffer solutions were prepared weekly and stored at 4°C.

Kinetic Measurements

The reactions between catecholamines and DPPH• were monitored by following the decrease in the characteristic absorbance of DPPH• at 518 nm. Kinetic experiments were performed under pseudo‐first‐order conditions, with catecholamines present in at least 10‐fold excess relative to DPPH•. Stock solutions of catecholamines were freshly prepared daily by dissolution in water or buffer, while DPPH• stock solutions were prepared in 1,4‐dioxane. Prior to initiation of the reaction, the reaction medium was thermostated for 20 min. The reaction was initiated by the addition of an appropriate volume of the thermostated DPPH• stock solution to the reaction mixture. For stopped‐flow experiments, reactant solutions were prepared at twice the desired final concentrations and rapidly mixed in a 1:1 volumetric ratio.

All spectral and absorbance–time data were collected using an Avantes AvaSpec 2048L StarLine spectrometer (Avantes B.V., Apeldoorn, The Netherlands), equipped with a QPOD temperature‐controlled sample compartment for fiber‐optic spectroscopy (Quantum Northwest, Liberty Lake, WA, USA). For reactions with pseudo‐first‐order rate constants greater than 0.15 s−1 (t 1/2 < 4.6 s), an RX2000 stopped‐flow rapid‐kinetics instrument (Applied Photophysics, Leatherhead, UK) was employed to enable rapid mixing of the reactants. Kinetic measurements were performed under strictly controlled temperature conditions, maintained within ±0.1 °C. In a typical kinetic run, at least 200 absorbance–time data points were collected and analyzed using a standard least‐squares fitting procedure (Figure 2). To determine rate parameters under a given set of conditions, a minimum of three to four pseudo‐first‐order rate constants (k obs) was obtained. The second‐order rate constants k were determined from the slopes of linear plots of k obs vs. catecholamine concentration (Figures S1–S4 and S8–S11). The stability of DPPH• was evaluated under all experimental conditions employed in the absence of catecholamines, and no changes in DPPH• concentration were observed over the timescale of the experiments.

Computational Methods

Calculations were performed using the GAUSSIAN 16 program [56] at the (U)B3LYP/def2‐TZVPP level of theory, taking into account nonspecific solvent effects via the polarizable continuum model (PCM) of the self‐consistent reaction field (SCRF) method [57] with 1,4‐dioxane used as the solvent. The structures of the reactants and TSs obtained by geometry optimizations were verified as minima or saddle points by vibrational analysis at the same theoretical level. The IBO along the IRC path, including the TS, was performed and plotted using the IBOVIEW program [58] at the same (U)B3LYP/def2‐TZVPP level of theory. The ALIE was calculated from the wave functions in the checkpoint files obtained from the above calculations using the MULTIWFN program [59, 60]. The tunneling correction for reaction constants and KIEs was estimated by the Eckart method with the TAMkin program [61].
