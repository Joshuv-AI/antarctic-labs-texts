Discriminative efficacy and comparative analysis of functional performance tests for lower-limb injuries in Chinese elite female basketball athletes: a pilot cross-sectional study
Authors: Lu Zhang, Xingjian Xiong, Muxi Zhang, Enming Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElite female basketball players experience a high incidence of lower-limb injuries, yet evidence remains limited regarding the applicability of common functional performance tests in this population. This study aimed (1) to compare the performance of the Functional Movement Screen (FMS), Landing Error Scoring System (LESS), and Y-Balance Test (YBT) between athletes with and without knee or ankle/foot injuries, and (2) to examine the correlations among these three functional performance tests.MethodsEighteen elite female basketball players from the Chinese National Team completed three functional performance tests: FMS, LESS, and YBT. Differences in total and subtest scores between injured and non-injured athletes were analyzed using independent samples t-tests and the Mann–Whitney U test. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to examine relationships among the three tests.ResultsNo significant differences were found in total FMS, LESS, or YBT scores between injured and non-injured athletes. However, a large effect size suggested a potential clinical trend between knee injuries and total FMS scores, and the FMS Deep Squat subtest significantly differentiated athletes with knee injuries. No significant correlations were observed among FMS, LESS, and YBT scores.ConclusionThe FMS Deep Squat component may help identify knee-related functional limitations in elite female basketball players, while the three tests collectively provide complementary perspectives on movement quality assessment in applied settings. However, given the small sample and cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and warrant validation in larger, prospective studies.Keywords: Elite female basketball athletes, Sports injury, Functional performance tests, Correlation

Introduction

Basketball is characterized by high intensity, rapid pace, and significant physical contact. During competition, athletes perform repetitive running, cutting, jumping, and landing maneuvers, placing substantial stress on joints and muscles and contributing to a high incidence of lower-limb injuries [1]. Notably, compared to male basketball players, female athletes experience a higher proportion of non-contact injuries and are particularly susceptible to severe injuries such as anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears [2–4]. Epidemiological studies consistently identify the knee and ankle as the most frequently injured regions in female basketball players [5]. Lateral ankle sprains are the most common injury type [6], while ACL tears represent the predominant knee injury in the WNBA (37%) [7], often resulting in prolonged absence and significant career disruption [6], especially prevalent at elite levels [2].

Given the high incidence and severity of knee and ankle injuries in female basketball players, establishing effective evaluation systems and clarifying injury-related functional profiles is of paramount importance. Traditional injury assessment methods, such as three-dimensional (3D) motion capture, kinematic analysis, and force profiling, are laboratory-based, often costly, complex to interpret, and time-consuming, limiting their practical utility for athletes and coaches in field settings [8]. For elite athletes, an ideal injury assessment tool should be: (1) quick and simple to administer within demanding competition seasons and large teams; (2) sport-specific and sensitive to movement patterns associated with common injuries in high-level athletes; and (3) adaptable throughout the season to overcome the limitations of pre-season assessment alone and capture functional changes induced by training and competition [9].

In the field of sports rehabilitation, several functional performance tests (FPTs) have been developed for injury assessment. Tests such as the Functional Movement Screen (FMS), Landing Error Scoring System (LESS), and Y-Balance Test (YBT) offer practical advantages, utilizing minimal equipment for on-field assessment and demonstrating clinical utility for competitive athletes. The FMS, comprising seven movement patterns scored out of 21, assesses fundamental movement quality, flexibility, and stability. While previous studies have noted an association between higher FMS scores and better movement function (with lower perceived risk of movement-related issues cited in earlier literature [10, 11]), the tool itself is primarily valued for its established reliability [12, 13] in evaluating baseline movement capacity. The LESS is a field-based tool designed to assess landing biomechanics that have been linked to non-contact ACL injuries in prior research [14]. It demonstrates good validity and reliability [15] and is widely used in military and athletic populations to evaluate landing function [16]. The YBT, evolved from the Star Excursion Balance Test [17], is a dynamic neuromuscular control test with established reliability [18, 19]. These tests offer convenient, on-site assessment with documented psychometric properties.

However, significant gaps remain in the application of these FPTs to elite female basketball athletes: (1) Limited specificity on elite athletes. Existing research primarily involves recreational or collegiate athletes [20]. The sport-specific applicability and discriminative validity of FMS, LESS, and YBT for assessing injury in elite female basketball players require further investigation. (2) Temporal limitation. Most studies conduct pre-season tests [21–24]. However, athletes’ functional status can change dynamically throughout a long season due to training and competition loads. Pre-season screening may not accurately reflect the relationship with injuries occurring later or currently present [9]. The dynamic nature of athletes’ functional status (shaped by training and competition) necessitates assessments beyond a single timepoint [8]. The ability of these tests to identify functional deficits associated with existing lower-limb injuries is a critical, understudied question. (3) Lack of comparative data. While FMS, LESS, and YBT are all used to assess aspects of lower-limb function, the extent of overlap or divergence in what they measure within an elite population is unclear. Studies examining the comparative discriminative efficacy of these tests for functional deficits related to specific injuries (knee vs. ankle) and their inter-correlations in elite female basketball players are notably scarce. (4) Limited focus on elite female athletes. Despite these documented sex-based injury disparities, sports medicine research remains disproportionately focused on male athletes, studies specifically targeting elite female basketball players remain scarce. This lack of focused research has resulted in insufficient evidence for developing effective, sex-specific functional assessment and injury management protocols tailored to the unique demands faced by this population [25–28].

This study aims to address these gaps by applying three common FPTs (FMS, LESS, YBT) to elite Chinese female basketball players with the following objectives: (1) To compare the performance of FMS, LESS, and YBT between athletes with and without existing knee or ankle/foot injuries; (2) To examine the correlations among FMS, LESS, and YBT, providing evidence for optimizing protocols for coaches and physiotherapists by understanding potential redundancies or complementarity.

Methods

Experimental approach to the problem

The present investigation was designed as a cross-sectional study. Participants were divided into an injured group and a non-injured group based on the presence or absence of knee, ankle or foot injuries. The study then compared whether there were differences in FMS scores, LESS scores, and YBT composite scores between these groups. Additionally, participants were grouped according to their playing positions on the field to compare if there were differences in FMS scores, LESS scores, and YBT composite scores. Through linear correlation analysis, the study explored whether there is content overlap between total FMS scores, total LESS scores, and YBT composite scores.

Participants

The sample comprised the entire training roster of the Chinese Women’s National Basketball Team, totaling 18 players (age: 24.3 ± 3.1 years; height: 186.9 ± 11.0 cm; mass: 78.6 ± 11.5 kg; BMI: 22.4 ± 1.5), comprising 5 centers, 8 forwards, and 5 guards.

Procedures

Basic information collection

All tests were completed at the Rehabilitation Center of the Training Bureau under the General Administration of Sport of China. Measurements were conducted over three days. The experimental protocol was illustrated in Fig. 1. To minimize fatigue interference, tests were conducted on non-training days. Prior to testing, all participants were informed of the study’s background, procedures, potential benefits, and side effects, and provided written consent before data collection. On the first day, all team members underwent examinations by experienced therapists to determine the current location and severity of injuries. Sports injuries were defined as incidents meeting all of the following criteria: (a) occurred during training or competition; (b) required medical attention or evaluation; (c) resulted in the athlete missing at least three consecutive days of practice or competition [24]. Functional tests (FMS, LESS, YBT) were administered under standardized conditions. FMS was conducted on Day 1, followed by LESS on Day 2, and YBT on Day 3. Each test was assessed by two qualified and experienced therapists. The study was approved by the Sports Science Experiment Ethics Committee of Beijing Sport University (Ethics Approval: 2025300 H).

Experiment flowchart

Functional movement screen

The FMS consisted of seven movement tests: Deep Squat (DS), Hurdle Step (HS), In-Line Lunge (ILL), Shoulder Mobility (SM), Active Straight Leg Raise (ASLR), Trunk Stability Push-Up (TSPU), and Rotary Stability (RS). Each movement was scored from 0 to 3 based on execution quality. A score of 0 indicated pain in any body region during the movement; 1 indicated inability to complete the movement pattern; 2 indicated existence of compensatory movements; and 3 represented correct execution without compensations, yielding a maximum total score of 21. Five tests (HS, ILL, SM, ASLR, RS) required bilateral assessment, with the lower score from either side recorded. Prior to testing, all participants received verbal instructions and demonstrations of the FMS protocol [10, 11]. FMS was assessed without any prior warm-up activities by the athletes [24].

Landing error scoring system

The LESS (Landing Error Scoring System) test used two cameras to record videos of the participants’ jump-landing movements in both the sagittal and coronal planes. It evaluated 17 kinematic variables of the lower limbs and trunk, with a total score of 19. Higher scores indicate poorer landing biomechanics and a greater likelihood of non-contact lower-limb injuries [14].

The testing procedure for LESS was as follows: participants jumped off a 30-cm-high platform onto a landing area located at a distance equal to half their body height from the edge of the platform; immediately after landing, they performed a maximal vertical jump as high as possible. Following the instructions, participants completed one practice jump. They then performed three recorded trials, with a 30-second rest interval between each trial. The movement requirements were that participants had to (1) leave the platform with both feet simultaneously, (2) land forward rather than vertically, and (3) make full-foot contact with the ground while (4) performing the test smoothly. No verbal instructions were given during the test unless an error occurred. Before formal testing, participants were given two practice trials. Two tripod-mounted video cameras were used to record the test—one recorded from the front and the other from the side. Each camera was placed 3 m away from the landing area to capture motion from different angles.

The LESS assessment used a 19-point scale to evaluate the position of the trunk and lower limbs throughout the entire drop vertical jump movement (Table 1) [14]. There were 17 items in total. For the first 15 items, correct performance received a score of 0, while incorrect performance received a score of 1. Items 16 and 17 were scored based on joint displacement and overall impression, and could receive scores of 0, 1, or 2 depending on performance [14]. Padua categorized LESS scores into four levels [14]: LESS = 4 or less indicated excellent performance, LESS = 5 indicated good, LESS = 6 indicated moderate, and LESS > 6 indicated poor performance.

Y-Balance Test

The YBT (Y Balance Test) required participants to stand on one leg and reach with the other leg in three specific directions: anterior (A), posteromedial (PM), and posterolateral (PL). The distances reached were marked and compared by calculating the differences between left and right sides, as well as normalized scores for each side of the lower-limb using limb length [17]. The testing apparatus consisted of a platform connected to three PVC pipes extending in the anterior, posteromedial, and posterolateral directions. The two posterior pipes formed a 135° angle with the anterior pipe, and a 90° angle with each other. Each pipe was marked at 5 mm intervals, with sliders used to record the distance reached.

Before formal testing, participants watched a demonstration of the standard testing procedure. They were then allowed six practice trials in each direction and given a 5-minute rest period before performing three official tests per direction. A 10–15 s rest interval was provided between each trial, and after completing all three directions. For example, when testing the right side first, the participant stood on their left foot on the YBT platform, with their big toe touching the red line and the axis from the heel to between the second and third toes positioned centrally on the platform. Hands were placed on the iliac crests, and the non-supporting leg was extended as far as possible forward. The distance reached was recorded from the scale for each direction. After completing three reaches, the other leg was tested. The test sequence for the three directions was anterior, posteromedial, and posterolateral. During testing, participants had to maintain balance on the supporting foot without losing stability, moving, deviating from the center, or lifting the heel. The non-supporting leg could not touch the ground during the test. It had to return to its initial position before the next reach, hands should remain on the hip joints, and the slider had to be pushed continuously rather than intermittently. If any of these requirements were not met, the test needed to be repeated [29].

For analyzing YBT scores, the furthest reaching distances in three directions for each lower-limb, the differences in reach distances between the left and right sides in the same direction, and the composite scores for each side were evaluated. To eliminate the influence of limb length on the test data, YBT values were standardized as a percentage of the average lower-limb length (the mean of both sides), where limb length was measured from the anterior superior iliac spine to the medial malleolus.

The calculation method for the YBT single-side composite score:

The YBT composite score was calculated by averaging the composite scores of both sides [30, 31].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (Version 26.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois). The outcome measures of every test were verified for normality of distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Independent samples t-tests were used to examine the correlation between the presence or absence of knee, foot, or ankle injuries and the total scores of FMS, LESS, and YBT composite scores, with Cohen’s d calculated to quantify the effect size. According to the classification, an effect size of Cohen’s d: 0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium, 0.8 = large [32]. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the correlation between injury and the scores of FMS sub-items. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships among the three tests. The correlation coefficients were interpreted as follows: 0 to 0.3 indicated a small correlation, 0.31 to 0.49 indicated a moderate correlation, 0.5 to 0.69 indicated a large correlation, 0.7 to 0.89 indicated a very large correlation, and 0.9 to 1 indicated an almost perfect correlation [33]. All statistical tests were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Lower-limb injury patterns

Among the 18 athletes, 8 reported current knee injuries, including one case of medial collateral ligament injury and one case of patellar tendon injury, with two cases each of meniscus injury, osteoarthritis, and synovitis. One injury was located in the left knee, five in the right knee, and two involved both knees. A total of 9 athletes reported ankle or foot injuries: one injury was located in the left ankle, four in the right ankle, and two involved both ankles; there were also two cases of plantar fasciitis.

Characteristics of functional performance tests

Figure 2 and 3 shows the distribution of total FMS scores and sub-item scores. The average total FMS score was 15.7±2.1, with the lowest score being 11 and the highest score 19. Among the participants, 4 athletes had a total FMS score of ≤14, while 14 athletes scored greater than 14. For the lower-limb-related items (Deep Squat, Hurdle Step, Inline Lunge, and Active Straight Leg Raise), the median total score was 9.5, with an interquartile range of 2.

Discussion

This study describes the current lower-limb joint injury status of athletes from the Chinese Women’s National Basketball Team and their corresponding performance on three functional tests (FMS, LESS, and YBT). Among the participants, only four athletes (10.3% of the sample) achieved an FMS score of ≤14, reflecting the overall high baseline movement quality of this elite cohort. This finding may be attributed to the enhanced functional adaptations associated with their high-level training status [34, 35]. Sub-item analysis of the FMS demonstrated that the Deep Squat (42.9% full score rate), Hurdle Step (35.7%), and Rotary Stability (38.1%) exhibited the lowest proportions of 3 scores, suggesting potential deficiencies in lower extremity mobility and core stability among the athletes. The LESS further corroborated these observations: 50.0% of participants received "poor" ratings, while only 11.2% achieved"excellent" scores, highlighting prevalent biomechanical inefficiencies during landing tasks. For the YBT composite scores (91.8% of limb length) aligned with reference values from Plisky’s systematic review (92.8%). However, directional asymmetries emerged: anterior reach (91.8%) was lower than the reference threshold of 70.8%, while posteromedial (105.6%) and posterolateral (108.7%) scores exceeded the reference values (102.3% and 102.0%, respectively).

Association between lower-limb injuries and functional performance test scores

The Functional Movement Screen (FMS) has been widely utilized across various sports as a tool for assessing athletes' movement functionality [36–38]. Previous studies have explored the association between FMS scores and injuries in athletic populations. For instance, Łyp et al. [23] demonstrated that the FMS serves as an effective diagnostic tool for identifying a history of prior injuries in young soccer players, providing evidence that FMS performance may correlate with past injury experiences in athletes. Shimoura et al. divided 81 male collegiate basketball players into previously injured and non-injured groups, finding significant differences only in the Deep Squat and Hurdle Step tests between the two groups, with no significant difference in overall FMS scores [39]. Our study did not find a significant correlation between total FMS scores and lower-limb injuries, though high effect sizes were found between knee injuries and FMS total scores. Lower-limb injuries can be caused by multiple factors. Though FMS evaluates flexibility and stability across joints throughout the body, it may not specifically highlight knee or ankle issues as intended in this context. McCunn et al. suggested that FMS was a multifactorial assessment tool reflecting overall movement functionality rather than focusing on specific joints such as the knee and ankle [13]. Although the difference in total FMS scores did not reach statistical significance, the large effect size suggests the potential for a clinically relevant difference, which warrants further investigation with a larger sample size. Clinical decision-making should consider both the size of the effect and the P-value. This is especially important when studying rare or unique groups like elite athletes, where it can be harder to find statistically significant results due to small sample sizes. In such cases, looking at the actual size of the effect helps give a clearer picture of whether a treatment or intervention is meaningful in real-world practice.

Performance in the Deep Squat (DS) item of the FMS was associated with knee injuries, consistent with Shimoura's research [39]. Deep Squat requires high-load knee flexion (>90°) and eccentric control of the quadriceps. Higher Deep Squat scores correlate with greater knee flexion capacity during the descent phase [40], revealing deficits in knee flexion mechanics. Furthermore, DS requires participants to have good ankle dorsiflexion, knee flexion, hip flexion, thoracic extension, and shoulder flexion-extension mobility in a closed kinetic chain scenario, along with high demands on core stability [41]. Many functional activities in sports require stable trunk movement to efficiently transfer force symmetrically between the upper and lower limbs and vice versa [42]. In basketball, rebounding is a common example of such force transmission. If the trunk does not provide sufficient stability during these activities, energy cannot be transferred efficiently, potentially leading to microtrauma or even injury [43]. Although we did not find statistical correlations between other core-related items, such as the Trunk Stability Push-Up and Rotary Stability, and lower-limb injuries, several cases were observed where athletes with knee pain experienced back tension and forward tilting of the trunk during testing, preventing them from achieving the full squat position, resulting in point deductions. This suggests that core stability issues might indirectly affect knee health or exacerbate existing knee conditions [44]. Future researchers should continue to explore whether there is a correlation between knee injuries and core stability. Understanding these relationships could provide insights for more targeted injury management and rehabilitation strategies, particularly focusing on improving core strength and stability to support overall athletic performance and address functional deficits associated with existing lower-limb injuries.

Research investigating the association between the LESS and lower-limb injuries remains notably scarce, particularly in elite female basketball athletes. This critical gap limits our understanding of the test’s utility in this specific population. Gomes et al. [45] failed to identify a correlation between LESS scores and a history of lower-limb injuries in youth basketball players. This lack of association may partly stem from the multifactorial nature of sports injuries: lower-limb injuries arise from the interplay of biomechanical, physiological, training load, competition intensity and other factors, and thus cannot be reliably inferred or predicted by a single functional test like the LESS. Several additional factors may further contribute to the uncertainty surrounding LESS’s association with injuries in elite female basketball athletes. Firstly, the total possible score for LESS is 19, but the actual score range tends to be quite narrow, which limits its discriminatory power. For instance, the minimum score was 4 and the maximum was 11, suggesting that the scoring system might not adequately differentiate between varying levels of landing mechanics quality [46]. Secondly, LESS testing may have better injury identification capabilities in younger or less specialized populations [47]. Elite athletes have undergone extensive professional training and received considerable health education aimed at reducing landing-related injuries [48]. The effectiveness of LESS in this group could be diminished. Moreover, it is important to note that the LESS is performed under low cognitive load, which does not replicate the dual-task demands of actual game situations. Emerging evidence suggests that cognitive distraction can exacerbate biomechanical deficits, such as increased knee valgus and delayed muscle activation, particularly in athletes at risk for ACL injury [49, 50]. Therefore, the absence of cognitive stress during standard LESS assessment may limit its sensitivity in assessing movement patterns that only emerge under more ecologically valid, cognitively loaded conditions. To address this limitation, some scholars have proposed incorporating dual-task paradigms—such as a visual-based or number-based cognitive task—into the LESS assessment [51]. These additions aim to simulate real-world cognitive demands of actual competition, thereby making the assessment more aligned with ecologically valid scenarios.

Limited studies have investigated the relationship between YBT total scores and lower-limb injuries in athletic populations, with existing evidence consistently indicating no significant correlation [52, 53]. This aligns with the findings of the present study. This lack of association may, in part, relate to the multifaceted nature of the YBT itself: the test evaluates not only neuromuscular control but also challenges balance abilities and lower-limb joint mobility. Some athletes who have not reported injuries might still perform poorly on the YBT due to insufficient flexibility or other factors [54]. Future research should focus on identifying which specific parameters of the YBT are most closely related to existing lower-limb injury status and determining appropriate threshold values that can effectively distinguish between athletes with and without such injuries. This could enhance the precision of functional assessment for athletes with current injuries and provide a basis for developing more targeted, individualized injury management and rehabilitation strategies tailored to athletes’ specific functional deficits.

In summary, practical sports settings often require efficient assessment of athletes’ functional status, particularly in relation to existing injuries. While tests like FMS and LESS can be time-consuming and technically challenging for on-site interpretation, focusing on sport-specific movement demands—such as analyzing individual FMS items rather than composite scores—may yield more actionable insights [55]. Delving into correlations between simplified test metrics and current injury status could enhance real-world utility, enabling targeted identification of persistent functional deficits in athletes with existing injuries.

Intercorrelations among FMS, LESS, and YBT

FMS, LESS, and YBT are widely used functional assessment tools that offer practical advantages over more complex laboratory-based evaluations such as isokinetic strength testing [56]. These tests can be easily administered in the field with minimal equipment, making them accessible and applicable for coaches, trainers, and athletes across various settings. Given their popularity, researchers have sought to determine whether these assessments measure overlapping constructs by examining the correlations among them, yet findings remain inconsistent and context-dependent. For instance, a study by Asgari et al. [57] on Iranian collegiate athletes found minimal to negligible correlations between FMS, LESS, and YBT scores, suggesting that each test may evaluate largely distinct aspects of movement quality. Similarly, Kraus et al. [58] did not observe significant correlations between overall FMS and LESS scores, although they noted a relationship between performance on the Trunk Stability Push-Up subtest of the FMS and improved LESS scores, indicating some potential overlap in core-related components. Everard et al. [59] reported moderate correlations between total FMS scores and LESS scores among college athletes but found weaker associations when analyzing individual FMS items, highlighting the complexity of interpreting composite versus subscale scores. In contrast, other studies have identified relationships between certain combinations of these tests. Sikora and Linek [60] observed moderate correlations between FMS composite scores and YBT composite scores in soccer players, suggesting that these two tests may share common elements related to dynamic balance and movement control. Harshbarger et al. [61] found moderate association emerged when comparing FMS scores with YBT composite scores in younger populations. Additional evidence from studies on youth athletes by Linek et al. and Plisky et al. [30, 62] further supports the notion that age, training status may influence the degree of correlation among these tests. Overall, the current literature does not provide a clear consensus regarding the interrelationships between FMS, LESS, and YBT. The variability in results likely stems from differences in study design, population characteristics, selected outcome measures, and statistical approaches. Therefore, while these functional tests are valuable tools for assessing different dimensions of movement quality, their specific applications and interpretations should consider the context in which they are used, and future research should aim to clarify the conditions under which meaningful relationships emerge.

This study revealed no significant correlations between total FMS scores, total LESS scores, and YBT composite scores. Despite some similarities among these tools, each focuses on different aspects of functional performance. The LESS emphasizes landing biomechanics, YBT evaluates dynamic balance, and FMS focuses on fundamental movement patterns [56]. The low inter-correlation among these tests suggests that they assess fundamentally distinct dimensions of physical capability. This independence underscores the complementary nature of functional performance tests, highlighting their multidimensional value despite sharing a common goal of evaluating lower-limb function. While FMS, LESS, and YBT all aim to assess lower-limb functionality, their underlying principles and application contexts differ significantly [56, 63]. FMS comprises seven functional movement assessments designed to evaluate stability and flexibility across multiple tasks and joints throughout the body [10, 11]. In contrast, the movement patterns assessed by LESS and YBT are more specific and focused. However, FMS only examines functionality under stable conditions and does not reflect performance under unstable conditions. Additionally, while LESS fails to capture unilateral lower-limb control, combining it with FMS and YBT can provide a comprehensive assessment. Thus, relying solely on a single test like FMS cannot fully represent an individual's functional status [64, 65]. Although there is overlap among functional tests, they offer unique insights from different functional perspectives and should not be considered interchangeable [57, 61]. Moreover, varying results can be obtained depending on the population tested, such as differences based on playing positions. Sports teams should avoid treating these three tools as interchangeable and instead select the most appropriate tool based on specific needs [57, 64], taking into account factors such as time, venue, and the age, injury history, and occupation of the test subjects. Combining practical experience with these considerations can help choose the most suitable evaluation tool. In resource-limited training environments, selecting the tool that best aligns with current objectives rather than using multiple tools simultaneously can enhance efficiency. For instance, when evaluating athletes with potential knee injuries, prioritizing the FMS Deep Squat component may be beneficial, as it can help identify residual functional deficits related to knee flexion mechanics. When assessing directional change and landing function, combined use of the LESS or YBT proves more targeted for detecting biomechanical or balance-related limitations. Comprehensive functional baseline assessments at the beginning of the season might opt for the FMS, while monitoring changes in landing mechanics amid in-season training and competition loads could leverage the LESS. For evaluating post-injury balance recovery, the YBT would be the preferred choice. Tailoring the use of these tools to specific contexts ensures a more effective and efficient approach to supporting injury management, rehabilitation, and ongoing functional monitoring for athletes.

Limitations

Despite the insights provided by this study on the correlation between functional tests and injuries among female basketball players, there are several limitations. Firstly, regarding the FMS analysis, the conventional approach is to determine or validate cut-off values (e.g., ≤14) or to calculate population-specific thresholds using ROC curves. However, given our small sample of elite athletes, such methods were not feasible. We therefore adopted correlation- and effect-size–based analyses as exploratory alternatives. Additionally, because of the cross-sectional design of the study, it is impossible to establish causal relationships or predict long-term injury trends.

Thirdly, the definition of injury was based on field assessments and self-reports from the athletes. Although evaluations were conducted by experienced, certified rehabilitation specialists of the national team, there may still be psychological effects and assessment biases. Last, while tests were administered on non-training days during the off-season (when athletes were transitioning from vacation to preseason training), residual fatigue was not objectively monitored through physiological markers such as heart rate variability or session-RPE. Although the low-intensity nature of the functional tests (e.g., single-set FMS assessment, limited LESS trials) likely mitigated fatigue interference, future studies should implement standardized load-tracking protocols to isolate these effects.

Conclusions

This pilot cross-sectional study applied the Functional Movement Screen (FMS), Landing Error Scoring System (LESS), and Y-Balance Test (YBT) to elite female basketball players from the Chinese National Team to examine (1) associations between functional performance and lower-limb injuries and (2) the correlations among these three tests. No significant differences were observed in total FMS, LESS, or YBT scores between athletes with and without knee or ankle/foot injuries. However, a large effect size suggested a potential clinical trend between knee injuries and total FMS scores, and the FMS Deep Squat subtest significantly differentiated athletes with knee injuries. Furthermore, no significant correlations among FMS, LESS, and YBT were found.. These findings suggest that the FMS Deep Squat component may help identify knee-related functional limitations in elite female basketball players. Practitioners and clinicians should consider combining FMS, LESS, and YBT to obtain complementary insights. Given the limited sample and cross-sectional nature of this study, the present results should be interpreted as exploratory. Future research should validate these findings with larger, prospective samples and refine population-specific reference values.
