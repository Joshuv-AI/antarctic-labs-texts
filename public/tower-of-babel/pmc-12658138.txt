Versatile on-chip plasmonic lattices enabled by Kekulé metasurfaces
Authors: Jinguo Liu, Yufan Luo, Airong Zhao, Pin Lyu, Ying Liu, Wenhui Sun, Xiaofan Wang, Chaoling Du, Mingming Jiang, Caixia Kan, Shuiyan Cao, Yanpeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurface plasmon polaritons (SPPs) lattices created by artificial plasmonic metasurfaces, can carry tremendous optical information and have implications for resilient and secure optical communication. However, tailoring the intensity, spectral, and spatial distributions of these SPP lattices intentionally remain challenging. Here, we develop, theoretically and experimentally, a variety of optical SPP lattices in a category of metasurface named as Kekulé metasurface. This plasmonic nanostructure localizes surface plasmonic polaritons into assorted optical lattices at the two-dimensional limit, visualized by noninvasive leakage radiation microscopy. The spatial distribution and relative intensity of the SPP lattice sites are evolved and tailored by superposing an extra wing-shaped nanoslit set. A configurable on-chip light-emitter array and high-security imaging encryption/decryption using such optical lattices are further demonstrated. This work reports a versatile Kekulé metasurface platform and well-defined SPP lattices with multiple degrees of freedoms, featuring rich physic phenomena and potential photonic elements for applications into existing technologies.Subject terms: Metamaterials; Nanophotonics and plasmonics; Information storage; Lasers, LEDs and light sources

Introduction

Analogous to electrons, surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs), recognized as transverse magnetic-polarized optical surface wave at the outset, have been recently casted enormous interest as quasiparticles at their most fundamental levels1–5. The quasiparticle properties of SPPs are defined by the electromagnetic field, material dielectric response function, geometry of light-matter interaction, and multiparticle scattering6. On generation, SPP quasiparticles confines in a two-dimensional (2D) metal surface, is somehow liquid-like7 and decays exponentially or into incoherent particles, varies in intensity, spectral, and spatiotemporal evolution that are otherwise accessible8. The controlled ordering of such SPP quasiparticles into demanded lattices is attractive because not only exotic but elusive multiparticle properties and quantum dynamics are uncovered but potentially it also satisfies Maxwell’s equations6 and applicable boundary conditions as required as the case for developing planar SPP amplifiers, compact radiation sources and integrated (nano) photonics devices. However, a versatile planar platform for probing macroscopic ordering and evolutions of such SPP quasiparticles is lacking, especially in 2D limit.

Herein, we demonstrate a range of highly-tunable SPP lattices based on a category of plasmonic nanostructures at optical frequency, termed as Kekulé metasurface (Fig. 1), in which patterned nanoslits in head-to-tail fashion assemble into regular hexagon nanostructures. This multivariant platform enables us to manifest the lattice type, spatial distributions, and relative intensity of lattice sites, with controllable parameters and symmetry, which has three salient features. First, most SPP lattice possesses distinguished orbital angular momentum, evolving into another SPP lattice under different combinations of helical excitation and detection lights. Secondly, the lattice topography, for example, the relative intensity of in-plane lattice sites, could be selectively cumulatively augmented by chiselling extra open nanoslits along six corner vertices acting as the outer-wings, while the shape of other lattice sites orientationally and radially smoothly evolves into blister-like lattice geometry. Thirdly, high-fidelity SPP lattices from Kekulé metasurfaces are 2D planar and maintain even under white light excitation. Here, SPP localizations are in-plane confined and presumably enable the compact integration of miniaturized plasmonic circuits with modern-day electronic circuits, differentiating from conventional 3D localization or focusing techniques, using nano-tips9–12, Fresnel lenses13, circular lenses14–16, metalenses17–19, or plasmonic Archimedean spirals20,21. As conceptual proofs, we further show that SPP lattices with amplitude-amplified sites can be principally applied as configurable on-chip light-emitting arrays and image encryptions/decryptions, enriching the metasurface family for advanced nanolasers and information security.

In chemistry, the Kekulé theory of benzene and its derivatives with distinct aromaticity intuitively provides a structural and electronic picture, and extremely accelerates the diversified design of organic molecules, for instance, Kekulene and plural core/edge-extended Kekulene, where the π-electrons are delocalized in individual benzene-type rings. We first patterned multiple nanoslits into a benzene-type ring with alternatively neighboring structures. Learning from the fruitful C-H functionalization, we further mimicked the benzene-type ring structure with extra C-H bond-like nanoslits to manifest SPP lattices. The controllable SPP localizations are in-plane confined, detected by leakage radiation microscopy, and readily compatible in feasible systems, such as gold/PET scheme. The enriched structure of Kekulé metasurface, together with diversified LRM setups generate kaleidoscopic SPP lattices.

Results

Tunable SPP lattices in Kekulé metasurface

Illuminated under circularly polarized laser, the nanoslits milled in gold film interact with the perpendicularly polarized component, and emit out intensive SPPs tailored by the geometric dimensions and orientation angles of nanoslits (please see SI Section S1)22. To be specified, as-generated SPPs stem from the superposition of co-polarized and cross-polarized fields. The extra phase shift in the latter allows the engineering of Pancharatnam-Berry (PB) phase (also termed as geometric-phase factor, expressed as exp(i2α), where α is the in-plane rotation angle of nanoslits)23. Departing from these, we develop a Kekulé metasurface via integrating such individual nanoslit (Fig. 2a) to create SPP lattices. The multiple open nanoslits are head-to-tail arranged into ‘benzene-like’ configuration with the same rotation angle (α), maintaining a fixed gradient, which can convert the incident circularly polarized light into a stable hexagonal lattice in the co-polarized state and customized lattices in cross-polarized state. Equation (1) implies that several degrees of freedom (DOFs) including l, σ and η±σ, emerge for manipulating the SPP lattice in real space. For instance, when the phase gradient is zero under right circularly polarized (RCP) incidence (TC = 0), we obtain a stable hexagonal SPP lattice with TC equals to 0 in left circularly polarized (LCP) state (Fig. 2b). Meanwhile, by combining with the axial (out-of-plane) electric field (Fig. 2c, see SI Section S3 for more details), meron-antimeron like lattice in normalized 3D electric field (Fig. 2d) affirms the quasiparticle characteristic of SPP lattice24. Figure 2e shows the feasible transformation of hexagonal SPP lattice into hexagonal vortex lattices (TC = 1), Kagome lattices (TC = 2), and honeycomb lattices (TC = 3) in LCP state, at quasi-2D limit, by modulating l to be 0.5, 1, 1.5 (see SI Fig. S4), respectively. From Fig. 2a−e, we conclude that versatile SPP lattices could be manifested by Kekulé metasurfaces, enriching the lattice family and their effective control by metasurface designs (please see SI Table S1 Section S5).

Figure 2g presents that the center spot (lattice site A) intensity monotonically increases with M increment and exhibits no sign of saturation for neglected SPP decay. For M = 4, the in-plane electric field at hexagonal SPP lattice site A (central image, Fig. 2h) is enhanced up to 390% with respect to that of M = 0 metasurface (Fig. 2b), while the intensity of surrounding lattice site B remains barely changed (normalized 3D electric field, see SI Fig. S5). The rigid shift of wing-shaped nanoslit set relative to the nanoslit hexagon along three high-symmetry axis (offsets equal to λspp/sin(π/3), Fig. 2f) leads to remarkable intensity enhancement (~320%) of SPP lattice site B. These good correlations clearly show the custom-tailoring of the designed Kekulé metasurface over SPP lattice, which is superior to other hexagonal metasurfaces (see SI Fig. S6).

Realization and control of versatile SPP lattices

We next reproduce the SPP lattice in real space based on M = 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 Kekulé metasurfaces via finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulation (Fig. 3a–e, please see Methods for more details). For M = 0, lattice sites B located at the vertices of a regular hexagon is surrounding lattice site A with a quite weak secondary maximum. After incorporating wing-shaped nanoslit arrays varying M from 0 to 4, two strikable features arise: (1) the intensity of lattice site A grows linearly and increases by approximate fourfold, accompanied by an increasingly intensified ring emerging between lattice site A and B; (2) the outer lattice site B tend to evolve from regular hexagon into a ring-like pattern composed of multiple bright spots. These salient features are reminiscent of the recently reported electronic crystallization and melting behaviors in two-dimensional van der Waals heterostructures25–31. Interestingly, such evolution phenomena are also visualized for other TC Kekulé metasurfaces by adding wing-shaped nanoslit sets (see SI Fig. S6).

To validate our predictions, we fabricated a series of Kekulé metasurfaces with M varying from 0 to 4 (Fig. 3f–j, bottom panels, please also see Methods). Considering that these SPP lattices dynamically consist in-plane SPPs under strong local-field and are highly susceptible to external fluctuations, in particular a sharp metallic tip based on near-field interactions9–12,32, leakage radiation microscopy (LRM)‒a versatile optical far-field method to direct quantitative imaging and analysis of SPPs in 2D limit is adopted33. Moreover, a mask and a filter were employed for both spatially and spectrally filtering the light directly transmitted through the sample, so that only pure SPPs were collected. The observed SPP ring in Fourier space (see SI Fig. S8) proves that pure SPP modes in real space (upper panels, Fig. 3f–j) were recorded by our home-made leakage radiation microscope. Surprisingly, patterns of measured SPP lattices from M = 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 Kekulé metasurfaces consistently dovetail with experimental and simulated references. The strong regularity between the selection of extra nanoslit positions and the optical lattice field intensity is also validated (see SI Fig. S9). Yet another interesting observation is that SPP localizations at lattice site A near linearly increase with added wing-shaped nanoslit sets, by carefully examining the intensity line profiles from experimental results (Fig. 3k). These features coincide with our simulated results, and suggest the cumulative SPP localizations and instinctive information-carrier nature of SPP lattices. Besides the in-plane SPP modes, our proposed Kekulé metasurface is also suitable for enhancing the scattering field, which is experimentally verified with optical lattice distribution as middle panels in Fig. 3f–j (more experimental details, please see SI section S12). The corresponding intensity profile (Fig. 3k) similarly exhibits optical lattices and tunable intensity. To further verify the universality of our proposed wing-shaped Kekulé metasurfaces, we also demonstrate the feasibility with different sizes and different plasmonic metals (see SI Note S11). Overall, the unique combination of extra wing-shaped nanoslits and basic Kekulé metasurface offers a major boost to the field intensity enhancement both for in-plane SPPs and scattering light.

Reconfiguring on-chip light-emitting array

The achievement of high-tunable SPP lattices by an extra wing-shaped nanoslit set in our Kekulé structure is promising in various applications. For example, reconfigurable light-emitting array uses SPP lattices with different lattice sites A intensity to enable on-chip patterned light sources, to the best of our knowledge, which has barely been reported before34,35. Kekulé metasurfaces with M = 0 and M = 4 were configured into array on a single chip, as sketched in Fig. 4a. Multiple M = 4 Kekulé structures were assembled into ‘N-U-A-A’ letters while the rest sites were occupied by M = 0 Kekulé structures. The ‘N’ and ‘U’ letters consist of ten M = 4 structures while ‘A’ letter is composed of twelve M = 4 structures. By collecting spatial coordinates and localized SPPs at the lattice site A, the real-space pattern of ‘N-U-A-A’ array (Fig. 4b) appears, and there exist no obvious SPP crosstalk between adjacent Kekulé structures. We further compared the normalized optical field intensity distribution data (see SI Fig. S13 for details), where the intensity of central lattice site A collected from M = 4 structure is four times than that for M = 0 one, consistent with the measured intensity ratio from two independent structures. Noted that scattering mechanism may occur and emit light out from the basal gold plane, once the thickness of gold film surpasses 70 nm due to limited leakage transmission. The conceptual validation of out-of-plane light-emitting arrays is displayed in SI Fig. S12. In addition, the on-chip planar Kekulé arrays exhibit similar optical lattices and ‘N-U-A-A’ emission pattern even under broad white light irradiation (please see SI Fig. S16), indicating their appropriateness as broadband nanolenser arrays, at wafer-scale levels.

In-plane SPP lattices for imaging encryption/decryption

The variable SPP lattices with multiple DOFs are potentially detached channels for in-plane imaging encryption36. We propose an encryption/decryption scheme by employing an in-plane Kekulé metasurface under RCP incident light (Fig. 5a), and the image information stored in different encryption channels of co-polarized (RCP) and cross-polarized (LCP) analyzers, which behaved as the keys. When the LCP analyzer is the key, a binary Cameraman cover image is constructed (Fig. 5b), which can be recognized as a matrix Aa×b(a=b=256) with values of 0 and 1, corresponding to black and white in the targeted cameraman binary image, respectively. Here, we encrypted two stable in-plane hexagonal and honeycomb lattices (Fig. 5c) as 1 and 0, respectively.

With the RCP analyzer as the other key, a Couple gray cover image is encrypted and each pixel intensity value is fitted for a grayscale ranging from 0 to 255 (Fig. 5d). Different from aforementioned LCP channel, we employed the widely continuously tunable amplitude of lattice site A as another matrix ARa×b(a=b=512) with 0 to 255 different values according to its brightness. Afterwards, this Couple image could be decoded into a series of binary images based on the conversion between decimal and binary (Fig. 5e), and in our scheme, two cipher binary images of the logo of Chinese characters of ‘南京航空航天大学‘ (360 pixels × 360 pixels, Fig. 5f) and letters of ‘NUAA’ (181 pixels × 70 pixels, Fig. 5g) are stored in the 0th and 1st of the least significant bit (LSB) of upper left corner of Couple cover image (details see SI section S17). Such anti-counterfeiting process by encrypting and decrypting is based on in-plane optical SPP lattices rather than reflected or transmitted light in conventional optical encryption with metasurfaces36.

Discussion

In this work, we have theoretically and experimentally demonstrated the controlled formation of in-plane SPP lattices enabled by Kekulé metasurface, in terms of ordered arrays of surface plasmon polaritons. This on-chip metasurface platform enables us to explore the dynamic localization and evolution behavior of surface plasmon polaritons in 2D limit. We further show the relative intensity amplification of SPP lattice site by superposing extra wing-shaped nanoslit sets, which facilitates cumulative SPP localization at specifically purpose-customized lattice site spot to take place and allows us to develop configurable both in-plane and out-of-plane light-emitting arrays, including ‘N-U-A-A’ patterned one. Furthermore, we demonstrate the encryption of two cipher images into a cover image with a LSB steganography by correlating the lattice parameters with different specific matrix operations as the keys, which is more intuitive and easier in comparison with the previously adopted four basic DOFs of light37. With respect to reported metasurfaces (please see SI Section S5), our well-designed structures, especially coupled with the out-wing pattern, could manifest versatile SPP lattices not only in terms of topology charges but also the intensity, demonstrating themselves greater potentials in modern-day applications.

It is envisioned that the formation and evolution of such SPP lattices might be extended to other light-matter (quasi)particles that potentially emerge in artificial and natural 2D systems, including MoO3 flakes, transition metal dichalcogenides, moiré heterostructures, twisted bilayer/multilayers and photonic quasicrystals38–44. The creation of such SPP lattice in Kekulé metasurface may be adopted for investigating liquid-solid transitions of quasiparticles in fundamental optoelectronic and photonic systems, may also be employed to develop micro-(nano-) lasers without the necessity of conventional laser cavity45,46. Finally, we note that vortices occur in optical SPP lattice and thus plasmonic topological phenomena spontaneously thrives, further studies could lead deeper understandings of spins and momentums and beyond.

Methods

Sample fabrication

Using an electron-beam evaporation system (Syskey E-Beam Evaporation system), 5 nm thick Ti (serving as an adhesion layer, deposition rate of 0.5 Å/s) and 50 nm (200 nm) thick Au layers (deposition rate of 2 Å/s) were sequentially deposited on a quartz substrate or a PET film. Afterward, Kekulé structures were precisely milled within Ti/Au film using a FEI Helios G4 UX focused ion beam (FIB) system. The FIB milling parameters were set to: voltage of 30 kV and current of 7 pA. Under a scanning electron microscope, the uniform geometry of nanoslits-the building block of Kekulé structure-is confirmed to be ~230 nm in length and ~118 nm in width. The processing field for the array is 50 μm, with a processing time of approximately 2 minutes. When processing a small field (such as 7 μm), the processing time for a single Kekulé metasurface structure is approximately 10 seconds.

Optical characterization

Using an inverted optical microscope (Nano Wizard Sense) with an oil immersion objective lens (100× magnification, NA = 1.30), the leakage radiation from the underside of the sample was collected. The collected radiation was then directed through a custom-built optical path (the signal was further processed via a filter and mask), and finally imaged using a CCD camera (Princeton Instrument PIXIS 1024) for real-space and Fourier-plane imaging. The detailed design optical path is shown in SI Fig. S8.

Numerical simulations

Full-wave numerical simulations were performed using the finite-different time-domain (FDTD) technique. A single nanoslit unit cell in 50 nm-thick gold film was adopted. The incident wave was polarized along x- or y-axes and illuminated the nanoslits from the substrate side. Along x- and y-axes, periodic boundary conditions were applied, and a perfectly matched layer (PML) boundary condition was used along the z direction. The polarization conversion efficiency (PCE) is obtained as a function of nanoslit length L and width W at a wavelength of 633 nm (see Supplementary Fig. S2) by sweeping these values between 50 nm and 250 nm with an interval of 5 nm.
