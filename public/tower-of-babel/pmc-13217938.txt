Dry Mouth by Report or by Measure? Comparison of Fox and Xerostomia Inventory Scores with Sialometry
Authors: Magdalena Ostrowska, Layla Settaf-Cherif, Jan Kiersztyn, Adam Ostrowski, Tomasz A Drewa, Jan Adamowicz, Maciej J Wróbel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite its high prevalence, xerostomia remains frequently misunderstood and often incorrectly assumed to be synonymous with reduced salivary flow. Xerostomia was assessed using the Fox questionnaire (0–10) and Xerostomia Inventory (XI; 11–55). Unstimulated salivary flow (mL/min) was measured with the 5‑min spitting method. Background

Dryness of the oral cavity (xerostomia) is a widespread complaint, affecting approximately 22% of the general population [1]. At some point, every physician, regardless of specialty, is likely to encounter a patient experiencing xerostomia This is largely due to the fact that various groups of medications are among the most common causes of dry mouth [2].

Despite its high prevalence, xerostomia remains frequently misunderstood and often incorrectly assumed to be synonymous with reduced salivary flow [3]. In reality, these two terms - xerostomia and hyposalivation - are not interchangeable [4–6]. Xerostomia, by definition, is a subjective perception of dry mouth, whereas hyposalivation refers to objectively reduced salivary secretion, typically quantified by sialometry [7].

Xerostomia can be assessed using single screening questions or self-reported instruments (e.g., Fox, Xerostomia Inventory, RODI, Bother Index) [8–12]. Among available patient‑reported tools, we focused on the Fox questionnaire and the Xerostomia Inventory (XI) because they are among the most widely used xerostomia instruments and represent two contrasting formats: a short dichotomous symptom checklist (Fox) versus a multi‑item Likert scale capturing the frequency of dryness‑related experiences, including extra‑oral symptoms (XI). Importantly, xerostomia symptom reports and these instruments (or closely related xerostomia questionnaires) have been examined against objective salivary measures in prior work, providing a foundation for evaluating criterion validity against sialometry [6, 8, 13, 14].

These measures are intended to capture the patient’s symptom experience and functional impact; however, symptom severity is sometimes used in practice as a surrogate for salivary gland hypofunction, despite the constructs being distinct [3].

Although diagnostic thresholds vary with the measurement protocol, hyposalivation is commonly defined as salivary flow ≤ 0.1 mL/min for unstimulated methods, and ≤ 0.5–0.7 mL/min. for stimulated [8, 9].

Consequently, it remains important to clarify how patient‑reported xerostomia relates to measured salivary output. Clinically, symptom management targets xerostomia, whereas prevention of oral complications depends on identifying and addressing true salivary hypofunction.

Therefore, our study aimed to compare two widely used xerostomia instruments - the Fox questionnaire and the Xerostomia Inventory against objectively measured unstimulated salivary flow to quantify criterion validity. We further examined which individual questions were most closely associated with objective flow and evaluated the ability of total scores and item-based models to discriminate clinically relevant hyposalivation thresholds.

We believe that accurate assessment and management of xerostomia are critical for patient comfort and prevention of complications.

Methods

Study design and participants

The analysis was based on a paired repeated-measures dataset that included patient visits occurring both during periods with and without pharmacological treatment for overactive bladder (OAB); the analytic aim was to quantify agreement between patient-reported xerostomia and objective salivary output, and to assess the ability of questionnaire scores (and their individual items) to identify hyposalivation. We performed complete-case analyses for each comparison. Each participant contributed two assessments in a fixed order (off‑treatment baseline followed by on‑treatment follow‑up). Follow-up assessments were performed after 2 weeks of pharmacotherapy initiation.

Participants were recruited from the Department of Urology, University Hospital No 1 in Bydgoszcz, Poland.

Demographic data, including age, sex, weight, and height, were recorded, along with medical history details such as comorbid conditions, concurrent medications, and smoking status.

Enrollment criteria were: adults (≥ 18 years) with overactive bladder diagnosed by a specialist urologist according to contemporary definitions [10, 11]. Symptoms were considered to have a clinically meaningful impact based on patient-reported bother and the treating clinician’s decision to initiate pharmacotherapy (no separate QoL instrument was administered).

All participants provided written informed consent; the study was approved by the Local Bioethics Committee (KB/205/2023).

Measures

I Questionnaires

Xerostomia was assessed with the Fox questionnaire (ten yes/no items; total 0–10, higher scores indicate worse dryness) [15] and the Xerostomia Inventory (XI; eleven items rated on a 5‑point Likert scale; total 11–55, higher scores indicate worse dryness) [8]. Questionnaires were administered in Polish using a forward‑translation and clinical‑review process; however, formal cross‑cultural validation of the Polish versions was not performed.

II Objective salivary flow

Unstimulated saliva was measured using the 5-minute spitting method. The collected mass (grams) over 5 min was recorded and converted to flow (mL/min) assuming 1 g ≈ 1 mL Due to logistical constraints, all collections were scheduled in the afternoon (approximately 14:00–16:00) at both time points; participants were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking, smoking, or oral rinsing for at least two hours before testing.

Hyposalivation was evaluated using three pre-specified unstimulated salivary flow rates

Hyposalivation was evaluated using three pre-specified unstimulated salivary flow thresholds: Threshold I: <0.10 mL/min; Threshold II: <0.066 mL/min; Threshold III: <0.033 mL/min [9]. These cutoffs reflect a graded severity scheme (mild/moderate/severe hypofunction) used in prior salivary dysfunction literature; we acknowledge that other unstimulated cutoffs (e.g., < 0.20 mL/min and < 0.30 mL/min) are also reported depending on protocol and population [15–17].

For exploratory analyses, a deterioration endpoint was defined as either (I) a ≥ 50% reduction in unstimulated salivary flow from the patient’s baseline measurement, or (II) incident hyposalivation (≤ 0.10 mL/min) after treatment initiation. Baseline was always the off‑treatment assessment, and follow‑up was always the on‑treatment assessment for the same participant (fixed order).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using TIBCO Statistica 13.3. Penalized regression (LASSO) was implemented in Python. Analyses were conducted as complete cases for each comparison. All tests were two‑sided.

Because each participant contributed two assessments, analyses using both visits accounted for within-person clustering. Confidence intervals for correlations and ROC/AUCs were obtained using patient-level (cluster) bootstrap resampling; key estimates were repeated using the baseline assessment only as a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S3).

Concordance and correlations. Associations between Fox total score, XI total score, and unstimulated salivary flow (mL/min) were summarized using Pearson correlation coefficients. Spearman correlations were computed as a sensitivity analysis. Within-person baseline vs. follow-up changes in salivary flow and questionnaire totals were summarized descriptively and tested using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Table 5).

Within-person changes from baseline (off treatment) to follow-up (on treatment) are summarized in Table 5. Mean (SD) unstimulated flow decreased from 0.34 (0.25) to 0.27 (0.22) mL/min. The proportion of assessments below the predefined hyposalivation thresholds increased at follow-up (< 0.10: 9/101 to 20/101; <0.066: 7/101 to 17/101; <0.033: 3/101 to 8/101). Overall, 29/101 participants met the deterioration endpoint; 26/101 had a ≥ 50% reduction in flow and 14/101 developed incident hyposalivation (≤ 0.10 mL/min), with overlap in 11 participants.

Across paired assessments, reductions in salivary flow did not translate into proportional changes in questionnaire totals. Within-person changes in flow were weakly associated with changes in FOX and XI totals (Δflow vs. ΔFOX: Pearson r = − 0.00; Δflow vs. ΔXI: r = 0.11). Changes in FOX and XI totals were moderately correlated with each other (Pearson r = 0.44), indicating that both instruments captured a similar direction of symptom change despite limited correspondence with measured flow.

4.Prediction of hyposalivation LASSO versus Sum Scores.

Prediction of hyposalivation LASSO versus Sum Scores.

In patient-level cross-validated logistic models, the Fox sum-score outperformed the XI sum-score (CV-AUC 0.697 vs. 0.578) for hyposalivation < 0.10 mL/min. At the item level, LASSO applied to XI items achieved CV-AUC 0.689 using 7 items (X1, X2, X3, X4, X5, X6, X8), whereas LASSO on Fox items showed limited shrinkage (CV-AUC 0.638; all 10 items retained). A combined LASSO over all items achieved the highest CV-AUC (0.718) but selected 12 predictors (F1, F4, F7, F8, F9, F10, X1, X2, X4, X5, X8, X10). Nested cross-validation yielded similar, slightly more conservative estimates (Table S4). Internal consistency was acceptable for the Fox questionnaire (Cronbach’s α = 0.693; n = 201 assessments with complete Fox items) and good for the Xerostomia Inventory (α = 0.812; n = 202).

Discussion

Several validated questionnaires have been developed to assess the subjective perception of dry mouth. They are usually low-cost, easy for patients to complete, and can be evaluated by clinicians [18]. One of the earliest tools was introduced by Fox [13], and Thomson et al. later developed the Xerostomia Inventory (XI) [14]. Both aimed to identify a small number of key questions that could reliably indicate true salivary gland dysfunction in patients reporting oral dryness. Accordingly, we compared Fox and XI scores against unstimulated sialometry to quantify their criterion validity. In our cohort, Fox scores were more closely aligned with objective salivary hypofunction than XI. However, the magnitude of both correlations also highlights an important clinical point: subjective xerostomia is not equivalent to hyposalivation. Notably, some individuals experience dryness despite producing normal or even above-normal saliva volumes, whereas others demonstrate markedly reduced salivary flow yet report no subjective discomfort [3]. In Nielander’s study, a hyposalivation was found in 28.6% patients with xerostomia [19]. In a systematic review by Aliko et al. In the majority of the 129 reviewed papers, it was not documented whether xerostomia was accompanied by decreased salivary flow [20]. In a pharmacologically induced dry-mouth experiment involving healthy volunteers, Wolff and Kleinberg noted that oral dryness typically appeared once the total salivary flow rate fell below half its normal level—a threshold consistent with the earlier findings of Dawes [21].

In a study of the effects of anticholinergic agents on salivary flow, patients experienced a sensation of dry mouth when the normal unstimulated salivary flow rate was reduced by 40–50% [22].

Although Fox and XI were designed to capture patient-reported dryness, they differ in item content and focus. Several questions are conceptually overlapping or nearly identical across instruments, particularly those addressing compensatory behaviors (e.g., drinking fluids to aid swallowing, waking at night to drink) and functional difficulties with dry foods or swallowing.

This conceptual overlap is reflected in the strong correlation between Fox and XI total scores suggesting that both instruments largely capture the same subjective xerostomia construct. The highest cross‑questionnaire item correspondences clustered around closely matched coping and functional items (Table 3), indicating that agreement between instruments is driven mainly by behaviors and eating/swallowing difficulties rather than by extra‑oral symptoms. In contrast, XI includes a broader set of symptoms that extend beyond the oral cavity, such as dryness of the facial skin, eyes, lips, and the inside of the nose. These items may reflect a wider “mucosal dryness” experience, but they are not expected to map as directly onto salivary gland output as questions that specifically probe oral wetness or perceived saliva volume.

Our item-level results suggest that the strongest objective signal is concentrated in questions that directly probe persistent oral dryness and perceived lack of saliva, whereas context-dependent symptoms (e.g., night-time dryness or dryness during meals) may be influenced by factors other than gland output such as mouth breathing, sleep environment, food characteristics, and compensatory drinking behaviors.

Sreebny et al., in their study, calculated that a simple question, “Does your mouth usually feel dry?”, demonstrated high sensitivity (93%) for identifying individuals with objectively low salivary flow rates. However, the specificity was 68% [8].

Within XI, items focused on intra-oral dryness showed the clearest relationship with salivary flow, while extra-oral dryness symptoms (skin/eyes/nose) were weakly related. This pattern is consistent with XI capturing a broader ‘mucosal dryness’ experience, which may not map directly to salivary secretion.

Consistent with prior efforts to shorten xerostomia instruments, our exploratory modeling indicates that a small subset of XI items can retain most of the discrimination for objective hypofunction while reducing respondent burden. Importantly, higher internal consistency does not imply better criterion validity; instrument choice should reflect the clinical question (symptom burden vs. salivary hypofunction).

The deterioration analysis further underscores that changes in measured flow do not necessarily translate into proportional changes in symptom scores over time. In clinical settings where medication changes or new oral complications occur, objective flow measurement can help distinguish symptom fluctuation from clinically meaningful hyposecretion. Limitations

Limitations include the afternoon timing of saliva collections. Because salivary flow shows diurnal variation, afternoon sampling may underestimate “morning‑worst” low‑flow cases, although comparisons within a fixed time window should remain internally valid. Moreover, the lack of formal cross-cultural validation of the Polish questionnaire versions, and limited characterization of potential confounders such as obstructive sleep apnea or permanent salivary gland damage after head-and-neck radiotherapy might be potential limitation. Follow-up timing reflected real-world pharmacotherapy exposure and was not standardized to a fixed interval. Finally, detailed medication regimens (agents and dosages beyond OAB therapy and concomitant xerogenic medications) will be added in the revised submission to enable more granular confounding assessment.

Conclusions

Questionnaire scores showed limited criterion validity for objectively reduced unstimulated salivary flow. Fox performed modestly better than XI for screening, but neither instrument can replace sialometry when confirmation of salivary hypofunction is clinically relevant; item‑focused models may support triage but require external validation.
