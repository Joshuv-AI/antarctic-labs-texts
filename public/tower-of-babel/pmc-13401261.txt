Molecular Mediators Associated With Myelination, Demyelination, and Remyelination in the Peripheral Nervous System
Authors: Kathleen Margaret Hagen, Shalina Sheryl Ousman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe peripheral nervous system (PNS) is responsible for innervating all regions of the body outside of the central nervous system (CNS), the latter consisting of the brain, spinal cord, and optic nerves. While myelin is an essential component for the efficient functioning of both CNS and peripheral nerve cells, it is particularly important for PNS neurons since their cell bodies lie at considerable distances from their innervated targets. As such, regulation of myelin content and formation in the PNS is a carefully controlled process that involves many cellular and molecular players. The peripheral nerve is a multicellular environment containing several non‐neuronal cell types such as fibroblasts, endothelial cells, pericytes, resident macrophages, and nonmyelinating and myelinating Schwann cells, all of which contribute to PNS homeostasis. This review focuses on the contributions of Schwann cells and macrophages and their molecular mediators in the formation, maintenance, degradation, and reformation of peripheral myelin during homeostasis of, and after damage to the PNS.

PNS Myelin Development and Homeostasis

Peripheral Nerve

The peripheral nerve is a multicellular environment that is structured into bundles of fascicles composed of axons (upwards of 1 m from the cell body in the adult human sciatic nerve) and homeostasis‐supporting non‐neuronal cells encased in collagen fibers known as the endoneurium [1, 2, 3]. The endoneurium is further enclosed by the perineurium [2], which is composed of concentric layers of perineurial cells that express tight junctions and collagen fibers that lie between the layers [3, 4]. Grouping together fascicles is the third layer of connective tissue known as the epineurium; this layer is primarily made up of collagen, elastin fibers, and fine filaments [2, 3]. Several non‐neuronal cell types such as fibroblasts, endothelial cells, pericytes, resident macrophages, and nonmyelinating and myelinating Schwann cells support the function of PNS neurons during homeostasis and after damage. This review focuses on the role of Schwann cells and macrophages in myelination, demyelination, and remyelination in the PNS, with a focus on molecular mediators.

PNS Myelin Composition

When myelin is formed in the PNS, the newest portions are in the innermost layers and the oldest furthest from the axon [5]. Myelin is composed of lipids and proteins, with the ratio of the two varying according to species. In general, PNS myelin is composed of approximately 20%–30% protein with the remaining 70%–80% being lipids [6, 7, 8]. The majority of the protein content consists of myelin protein zero (P0), myelin basic protein (MBP), and P2 basic protein [9, 10, 11], with myelin‐associated glycoprotein (MAG), myelin protein 22, proteolipid protein, myelin and lymphocyte protein (MAL), and connexin 32 present in lower amounts [11, 12, 13, 14, 15].

The lipids in PNS myelin include cholesterol, ethanolamine phosphoglycerides, choline phosphoglycerides, serine phosphoglycerides, inositol phosphoglycerides, sphingomyelin, galactocerebrosides, galactocerebroside‐derived sulfatides, gangliosides, ceramides, and fatty acids [6, 7, 8, 16, 17, 18]. Cholesterol is the dominant lipid in PNS and CNS myelin [6, 7, 8], and its presence is required for proper myelination in both nervous systems [19, 20, 21]. Further, a close relationship exists between the proteins and lipids in PNS myelin. For instance, Saher et al. [21] showed that cholesterol is critical for the trafficking of P0 from the endoplasmic reticulum to areas of active myelin formation, while lipid rafts that express specific markers such as MAL and CD59 are necessary for the trafficking of myelin proteins [12].

Schwann Cells and Their Myelinating and Nonmyelinating Fate

Schwann cells, the glial cells of the PNS, develop from the neural crest [22]. This occurs at embryonic day (E) 14–15 in rats and E12‐13 in mice [23]. A Schwann cell precursor is first generated that then differentiates into an immature Schwann cell (E15 to E17 in rats; E13–15 in mice). The immature Schwann cell subsequently becomes a nonmyelinating Schwann cell (also known as Remak cells) or a myelinating Schwann cell, with the latter involving a pro‐myelinating stage [24]. As well described in other reviews [22, 23, 24], each Schwann cell stage is identified by multiple markers, and selective factors are involved in their survival. For instance, the health of Schwann cell precursors is dependent on axon‐derived signals that are primarily composed of the neuregulin family of growth factors [25, 26, 27]. Immature Schwann cells (differentiated from Schwann cell precursors) lose this axon dependence and support their own survival by establishing autocrine loops to block apoptosis. This involves the release of growth factors such as insulin‐like growth factors, platelet‐derived growth factor, leukemia inhibitory factor, lysophosphatidic acid, and neurotrophin‐3 (NT‐3) [23, 28, 29, 30, 31]. At this time, immature Schwann cells destined for myelination will proliferate vigorously and differentiate into pro‐myelinating Schwann cells. These cells will then extend their cytoplasmic processes into bundles of axons, progressively separating them into even smaller bundles, and finally establishing a 1:1 relationship with each axon (generally large diameter ones), in a process known as radial sorting [32, 33]. These cells, known as myelinating Schwann cells, will further differentiate and wrap axons concentrically with the extension of their membrane to produce myelin sheaths. In contrast, nonmyelinating Schwann cells ensheath multiple small‐caliber axons (C fibers, < 1 μm diameter) to form Remak bundles and keep individual axons separated by membrane extensions, but they do not form myelin sheaths [34]. This review summarizes the cellular and extracellular molecular factors associated with these cellular phenotypes and how they contribute to myelination, demyelination, and remyelination in the PNS across lifespan and after injury (Table 1). For the sake of focus, the cellular components discussed are Schwann cells and macrophages. We are also cognizant that absolute confirmation of myelination/ensheathment and function requires morphological and electrophysiological validation.

The fate of Schwann cells (i.e., nonmyelinating versus myelinating) is established during development and was originally believed to be the result of axon caliber. Voyvodic [35] showed that by increasing the size of the target organ being innervated, this caused the caliber of the axon to be increased, resulting in myelination of the axon by Schwann cells; a molecular mechanism to explain this phenomenon may have recently been identified by Park et al. [36], who showed that mechanosensory Schwann cells can sense axon diameter via actin‐like 6A. While there is an association between myelination and axon diameter [37], Schwann cell phenotype and thus PNS myelination, are associated with a number of molecular markers intrinsic to Schwann cells or present within the nerve environment such as the basal lamina and neurons [38, 39].

Remak Schwann Cells

In addition to providing metabolic support to their enclosed axons [40, 41], the importance of Remak Schwann cells to neuronal function was revealed when ErbB signaling was selectively blocked in nonmyelinating Schwann cells using transgenic mice that expressed a dominant‐negative ErbB receptor in only nonmyelinating Schwann cells. This resulted in a progressive loss of small‐caliber axons that subsequently led to a lack of sensitivity to hot and cold [42]. These animals also displayed an increase in survival and proliferation of Schwann cells, which implied that in adult mice, ErbB signaling in nonmyelinating Schwann cells inhibits proliferation. ErbB is a receptor for the neuronal transmembrane protein neuregulin (NRG) 1‐Type III, the latter of which is expressed at low levels in small diameter axons. Interestingly, the fate of Remak Schwann cells can be changed under appropriate conditions to become myelinating glia [43, 44]. Aguayo et al. [43] showed that when nerves from the cervical sympathetic trunk in mice (i.e., nonmyelinated fibers) were grafted into regenerating sural nerves (i.e., myelinated fibers), the cervical sympathetic trunk Schwann cells were able to myelinate. A similar effect was observed in vitro when superior cervical ganglion rat neurons that normally express low levels of NRG1‐Type III were transfected with an NRG1‐Type III lentivirus. This led to more MBP+ myelin segments upon co‐culture with rat Schwann cells relative to nontransfected neurons [44].

Myelinating Schwann Cells

Myelinating Schwann cells tend to myelinate larger caliber motor axons and sensory A fibers. Zorick et al. [45], Jaegle et al. [46], and Arroyo et al. [47] were some of the first to show that the different stages within myelinating Schwann cell lineages were associated with certain molecular signatures (i.e., transcription factors). For example, in mice, SCIP/Tst‐1/transcription factor 1 (octamer transcription factor 6: Oct‐6) and transcription factor 2 (Brn‐2) [48, 49] are transiently increased in pro‐myelinating Schwann cells in their transition to myelinating Schwann cells, while Krox‐20/Egr2 (early growth response protein 2) [50, 51] and Brn‐5 [52] are associated with a commitment to myelinating Schwann cells. Other transcriptional factors involved in the differentiation of Schwann cells into a myelinating phenotype and positive regulators of myelination include: Ski, Nab1/2, Zinc‐finger E‐box‐binding homeobox 2 (Zeb‐2), nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells (NF‐κB), and SRY‐Box transcription factor 10 (Sox10) [34, 46, 49, 50, 53, 54]. More recently, using Schwann cell signal transducer and activator of transcription (Stat)1 conditional null mice, Xu et al. [55] have added to the list by showing that Stat1 acts as an inducer of Schwann cell differentiation and subsequently myelination through interactions with the Rab11‐family interacting protein 1 promoter.

Among the many identified transcription factors, Krox‐20/Egr2 and Oct‐6 are the two best characterized markers shown to be essential for the differentiation of myelinating Schwann cells. Oct‐6 is required by pro‐myelinating Schwann cells for their timely differentiation into myelinating Schwann cells, as shown in Oct‐6 knockout mice [46, 48]. However, its expression in Schwann cells is transient, peaking in the pro‐myelinating stage before progressively downregulating during postnatal development. The induction of Oct‐6 in pro‐myelinating Schwann cells is strongly induced by an axonal contact‐related signal and subsequently activates a set of genes including Krox‐20/Egr2 [56]. Krox‐20/Egr2 is expressed only in myelin‐producing Schwann cells, and these glia continue to express Krox‐20/Egr2 protein throughout life in mice [51]. High expression of Krox‐20/Egr2 is required for the downregulation of Oct‐6 after the peak of myelination and for the activation of enzymes required for the synthesis of myelin lipids, as well as for the activation of the major PNS myelin genes, P0 and MBP.

With respect to other identified transcription factors, Brn‐2 is expressed in a similar developmental profile as Oct‐6 in chick and mouse sciatic nerves, and its function largely overlaps with Oct‐6 in regulating the transition from pro‐myelinating to myelinating Schwann cells [49]. Nab1/2 proteins form a complex with Krox‐20/Egr2, as seen in Nab1/2 transfected JEG3 cells and neuregulin‐treated rat Schwann cells [57], and are critical transcription modulators of Krox‐20/Egr2 in myelinating Schwann cells [23, 58]. Quintes et al. [59] have additionally demonstrated that Zeb‐2 is essential for proper axon sorting and myelination since, when it is knocked out in only Schwann cells in mice, there is a failure to repress genes associated with the repression of differentiation.

As discussed above, it is well established in vivo that myelinating Schwann cells progress through various stages during their development, with each stage characterized by multiple markers [22, 23, 24]. With the development of new technologies such as single‐cell RNA sequencing, a recent analysis of rat Schwann cells co‐cultured with dorsal root ganglia (DRG) demonstrated that there may also be three distinct phenotypes at the transcriptional level during PNS myelination [60]. First, there are immature proliferative Schwann cells that possess expression profiles associated with the cell cycle and microtubule dynamics. A second pro‐myelinating cluster is associated with the c‐Jun N‐terminal Kinase (JNK) cascade, cell differentiation, synapse signaling, neuron projection development, and the myelin sheath, while a third stage is a myelinating cluster associated with actin cytoskeleton polarity, cell–cell adhesion, neuronal ensheathment, and myelination.

Whether these phenotypes and/or others are also observed in vivo still needs to be ascertained. However, identification of in vivo Schwann cell transcriptional subsets and the already known developmental stages would allow one to determine if particular group(s) of Schwann cells are more negatively impacted or not after PNS damage or in PNS developmental myelination pathologies such as the inherited neuropathy Charcot–Marie–Tooth (CMT) or the autoimmune disease, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP). If this were the case, one can envision therapeutic selectivity against markers specific to the involved Schwann cell sub‐population(s).

PNS Myelination

Role of Extracellular Space Molecules

Over the decades, a number of molecular factors have been identified as inducers or inhibitors of myelinating Schwann cell function (Figure 1). Some of the earliest identified were extracellular space components [61, 62, 63, 64]. For instance, in vitro rat systems revealed that calcium levels affect Schwann cell axolemma junctions in the paranodal space [65], while fibroblast surface molecules and secretory factors impacted Schwann cell ensheathment and proliferation [66]. Extracellular matrix collagen was also found to be required for Schwann cell survival, fasciculation, and myelination [67].

Laminin

During these works, laminin emerged as an important factor secreted by Schwann cells that promoted rat neurite outgrowth in vitro. This was noteworthy because Eldridge et al. [68] had demonstrated in vitro that the initiation of myelination by Schwann cells during development depends on the ability of these glia to form a basal lamina which encloses each Schwann cell/axon unit. These researchers [69, 70] also showed that ascorbic acid completes Schwann cell differentiation, thereby allowing these glia to form the basal lamina. Of the four isoforms of laminin produced by Schwann cells, laminin‐211 and laminin‐411 have been shown to contribute to Schwann cell myelination as well as proliferation during radial sorting [71]. The supporting evidence was that a loss of laminin‐211 in mice was associated with bundles of unmyelinated axons of mixed calibers, while a loss of laminin‐411 led to variable levels of myelination within bundles of axons. Further, using wildtype (WT) and dystrophic mice, Previtali et al. [72] demonstrated that laminin function is mediated via beta1 integrins during axonal sorting but through dystroglycan, alpha6beta1 (α6β1), and alpha6beta4 (α6β4) during myelination.

Some of the transcriptional regulation of laminin receptor expression has been elucidated with Yes‐associated protein (YAP) and Taz (transcriptional co‐activator with PDZ‐binding motif) (both effectors of the Hippo pathway) playing important roles in both myelinating and nonmyelinating Schwann cells [73]. For instance, dystroglycan and integrins α6β1 and α6β4, which have been shown to be the laminin receptors acting during radial sorting [74, 75], were diminished when the conditional knockout of Taz in mouse Schwann cells was homozygous, and YAP was expressed in a heterozygous manner. In their study, Poitelon et al. [73] showed that YAP and Taz localize to the nucleus of Schwann cells in the presence of laminin as well as during mechanical stimulation (e.g., stretching). Through a series of conditional knockout experiments in which one allele of both genes was spared, the authors noted that when at least one allele of Taz was present, the clinical phenotype of altered radial sorting was not seen. Thus, while YAP and Taz do function in redundancy, Taz is required for proper sorting to occur. Further, when Taz was conditionally knocked out with or without the presence of one YAP allele, Schwann cell proliferation was significantly lower than WT animals alongside reductions in myelinated fibers. Thus, Taz is also involved in Schwann cell proliferation. YAP and Taz are TEA domain family member 1 transcriptional coactivators, and as such, their knockout led to declines in integrin α6 and dystroglycan expression. Of note, RNA sequencing revealed that the knockout led to dysregulated lipid and cholesterol biosynthesis with a notable downregulation of sterol regulatory element‐binding protein 2 (Srebf2) mRNA (discussed more later). It is also important to note that the correct timing and amount of myelination in the PNS is imparted by laminin‐211 inhibition of neuregulin and protein kinase A (PKA) signaling, which are required to initiate myelination [76]. Using NRG1‐Type III and laminin‐211 null and overexpressing mouse and rat Schwann cells co‐cultured with DRG neurons, it was found that enhanced PKA activation amplifies pro‐myelinating signals downstream of neuregulin such as ErbB2 and its effector Grb2‐Associated Binder‐1, which subsequently increased the expression of the pro‐myelinating transcription factors Oct‐6 and Krox‐20/Egr2. Thus, laminin‐211 appears to inhibit NRG1‐Type III by limiting PKA activation, but this is mainly in smaller diameter axons.

Neurotrophins

Subsequent to research on the extracellular space, a series of studies revealed that neurotrophins play both positive and negative roles in the modulation of myelination and regulating Schwann cells [77, 78]. The neurotrophin family includes nerve growth factor (NGF), brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), NT‐3, and NT‐4/5 [79]. Their signals are mediated through two types of receptors: the high‐affinity Trk receptor tyrosine kinases and the low‐affinity p75 neurotrophic receptor (NTR) [44]. p75NTR binds all four neurotrophins with similar affinity and acts as a co‐receptor for Trk receptors, while three different types of Trk receptors are specific for a particular neurotrophin: TrkA binds NGF, TrkB binds BDNF and NT‐4/5, and TrkC interacts preferentially with NT‐3 [44]. Using rat and quail Schwann cell/DRG co‐cultures and p75NTR−/− mice, BDNF was identified as a positive modulator of PNS myelination [78, 80] while NT‐3 was found to be a negative mediator [78]. Further, application of neurotrophin fusion proteins in mice showed that the myelin‐promoting effect of BDNF is mediated by p75NTR [78] and TrkC receptors [58], whereas NT‐3 mediates its myelin inhibitory effect via TrkC [58].

NRG1

In addition to extracellular space factors and neurotrophins, NRG1‐Type III and its cleavage regulators, beta‐site amyloid precursor protein‐cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1) and tumor necrosis factor‐α‐converting enzyme (TACE/a disintegrin and metalloprotease domain (ADAM)17) emerged as contributors to PNS myelination [44, 81]. Myelinating Schwann cells tend to myelinate larger caliber axons that express high levels of NRG1‐Type III [81] and require ErbB signaling for survival and myelination [26, 81]. If NRG1‐Type III is overexpressed in neurons in mice, hypermyelination occurs [81], while selective blocking of ErbB signaling in myelinating Schwann cells results in myelination deficits [26].

NRG1‐Type III binds to ErbB3 receptors on Schwann cells, which then form heterodimers with ErbB2 due to a conformational change. This interaction activates a number of signal transduction pathways such as phosphatidylinositol 3‐kinase PI3‐kinase (PI3K) [26, 82] and mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK), the latter of which was shown to mediate rat Schwann cell differentiation, alignment, and early myelination in vitro [83, 84]. The phosphoinositide phospholipase C‐γ pathway is also stimulated, and in mice lacking calcineurin B1 in the neural crest this leads to differentiation of Schwann cells from precursors as well as expression of Krox‐20/Egr2 that activates the myelination program [85, 86]. NRG1‐Type III is cleaved at different sites by BACE1 and TACE/ADAM17. These enzymes are positive and negative regulators of PNS myelin formation, respectively, as knockout of BACE1 in mice results in hypomyelination [87, 88], while mice conditionally null for TACE/ADAM17 in motor neurons or lentivirus‐mediated knockdown of TACE in vitro in DRG neurons display hypermyelinated axons [89]. Their levels therefore contribute to determining the amount of myelination that occurs. As to a possible neuronal mechanism postcleavage, Trimarco et al. [90] showed in NRG1‐Type III‐transfected rat DRG neurons that once NRG1‐Type III is cleaved, the intracellular portion migrates to the nucleus where it promotes increased expression of prostaglandin D2 synthase gene, followed by activation of G protein‐coupled receptor 44 that subsequently associated with myelination in vivo in prostaglandin D2 synthase knockout mice.

Others—PI3K, AKT, mTOR, GPR126

As mentioned above, the PI3K pathway is involved in Schwann cell differentiation during development. Interestingly, there appear to be two distinct pathways arising from its activation that function to positively and negatively regulate myelination [91]. The negative regulatory pathway involves the expression of laminin‐211 in the basal lamina, where signaling via α6β4 integrin on mouse Schwann cells activates PI3K and serum/glucocorticoid regulated kinase 1, leading to the phosphorylation of N‐myc downstream‐regulated gene‐1, and subsequently inhibition of myelination. The positive regulatory pathway, also identified by Heller et al. [91], involves axonal NRG1‐Type III binding to the ErbB receptor complex in Schwann cell/DRG neuron co‐cultures from rats and mice, where activation of PI3K led to alpha serine/threonine kinase (AKT) stimulation and subsequently activation of mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) and S6 ribosomal protein. Using BACE1 null mice, Hu et al. [87] have additionally demonstrated that activation of NRG1 via BACE1 cleavage [87] leads to downstream phosphorylation of AKT and subsequent activation of the mTOR complex. Of note, other studies have shown that when Dlg1 (MAP kinase‐responsive inhibitor of the Ste12p transcription factor) and thereby phosphatase and tensin homolog (a negative regulatory pathway of AKT activation induced by NRG1/ErbB signaling) is inhibited in vivo in mice with lentiviruses, hypermyelination occurs; therefore, proper signaling of this pathway is necessary to regulate myelin sheath thickness [92].

Subsequently, the involvement of mTOR in promoting PNS myelination was realized when mice conditionally lacking the factor in Schwann cells (the authors did not distinguish between the two mTOR complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2), displayed hypomyelination [93]. Further, Norrmén et al. [94] examined downstream effectors of its activation in the context of myelination and noted that when the Raptor gene encoding mTORC1 was knocked out, there was hypomyelination reflecting the findings of Sherman et al. [93]. Interestingly, the knockout did not affect the differentiation of Schwann cells but instead impaired the initiation of myelination by these glia (i.e., many Schwann cells exhibited pro‐myelinating factors rather than transitioning into a myelinating phenotype). As to the mechanism underlying the hypomyelination, Norrmén et al. [94] found that through activation of the nuclear receptor retinoid X receptor gamma and subsequent expression of the Srebp1c gene through selective knockout of mTORC1 but not mTORC2 in mouse Schwann cells, lipid synthesis in myelination was promoted, along with an association with fatty acid synthase (FASN) and stearoyl‐CoA desaturase 1 expression. The authors also found that through an unknown sequence of activation, mTORC1 impacted downstream targets but not the expression of SREBP2 because its downstream products, isopentenyl‐diphosphate δ‐isomerase 1 (IDI1) and 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methylglutaryl‐CoA reductase (HMGCR), were downregulated by the knockout of the Raptor gene, as was SREBP1c and its downstream target genes.

Finally, there is an involvement of G‐proteins in myelination and radial sorting. Using globally null and Schwann cell conditional knockout mice, Mogha et al. [95] and Monk et al. [96] showed that adhesion G‐protein‐coupled receptor (aGPCR) Gpr126 is required for PNS myelination and radial sorting, as Remak bundles were not observed, and Schwann cells were arrested at the pro‐myelinating stage. In addition, ectopic perineurial fibroblasts formed aberrant fascicles throughout the endoneurium of the mutant sciatic nerve [96]. This appears to be via elevation of cAMP levels or PKA activation that is associated with enhanced levels of Pou3f1, Krox‐20/Egr2, P0, and MBP.

Role of Sterol Regulatory Element‐Binding Protein in Cholesterol Biosynthesis in the PNS

As mentioned, cholesterol is the dominant lipid in PNS myelin. Myelinating Schwann cells synthesize both fatty acids and cholesterol de novo [97, 98], with the synthesis of cholesterol appearing to be regulated by intracellular sterol levels [99]. The initiation of cholesterol biosynthesis is controlled by SREBP transcription factors [100], which are expressed by myelinating mouse Schwann cells [101] and which are post‐transcriptionally activated by the sterol sensor SREBP cleavage‐activating protein (SCAP) in mice [102]. In the PNS, SCAP is critical for mediating the synthesis of lipids for myelin formation because when it was knocked out from Schwann cells in mice, it resulted in downregulation of Srebp, Hmgcr, and Fasn (rate‐limiting enzyme in fatty acid synthesis) mRNA, coinciding with abnormal peripheral myelin lipid composition. The knockout of SCAP also resulted in the uptake of extracellular lipids in an attempt to compensate for impaired synthesis.

In general, SCAP regulates lipid synthesis by acting as an escort for SREBPs by shuttling SREBPs from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi [103]. There, the SREBPs are activated by Site 1 and Site 2 proteases. This leads to the cleavage of the N‐terminal domain of SREBP, which then enters the nucleus to bind to the sterol regulatory element in the promoter of its target genes. The chaperoning function of SCAP is regulated by insulin‐induced gene 1 and 2 (INSIG1 and INSIG2, respectively) which bind SCAP to the endoplasmic reticulum via SCAP's sterol‐sensing domain thus preventing the movement of SREBPs to the Golgi for activation [104, 105]; these data were derived from 293S human embryonic kidney cells [105] and Chinese hamster ovary cells [104]. LeBlanc et al. [106] found that the expression of the master regulator of PNS myelination, Krox‐20/Egr2, is paralleled by increased gene expression of SCAP, SREBP1 (both SREBP1a and SREBP1c), and SREBP2. This was also reflected at the protein level for the N‐terminus of SREBP1 and SREBP2 in mice. To test if the parallel increase in SREBP pathway molecules impacted myelination, the authors found that prior to myelination, SREBP2 and INSIG1, but not SREBP1, SCAP, or INSIG2, were significantly downregulated in mice deficient in Krox‐20/Egr2, suggesting a relationship between SREBP2 and INSIG1 and myelination. However, as Camargo et al. [100] observed that Schwann cell SREBP2 may play a role in cholesterol biosynthesis whereas SREBP1c may be more involved in fatty acid synthesis, it appears that the SREBP activation pathway contributes to, but is not essential for PNS myelination as only some of its genes are directly regulated by Krox‐20/Egr2. Its contribution may be via cooperation with certain components such as SREBP2 that may assist Krox‐20/Egr2 in binding to the promoter sites of HMGCR and cytochrome P450 family 51.

NRG1 And Cholesterol Biosynthesis

Interestingly, NRG1 may also be involved in regulating cholesterol biosynthesis in the PNS rather than only functioning as a contributing factor to myelination [107]. Pertusa et al. [107] observed that NRG1 on axons transactivates ErbB receptors in rat Schwann cells in vitro in a pattern similar to that seen when plasma membrane cholesterol is depleted. The NRG1 stimulation results in activation of PI3K and subsequently AKT, leading to augmentation of one of the cAMP‐responsive element‐binding protein/activating transcription factors. This subsequently increases the activity of the HMGCR promoter and consequently cholesterol biosynthesis. As cholesterol biosynthesis proceeds, the Schwann cell plasma membrane expands around the axon to form the myelin sheath.

Another pathway by which NRG1 on axons activates ErbB receptors on Schwann cells to modulate cholesterol biosynthesis and thus initiation of myelination seems to be through Ca2+/calmodulin‐dependent protein kinase (CAMK) and MAPK signaling [108]. This was demonstrated by Kim, Maynard, et al. [109] who examined the effect of proto‐oncogene c‐Maf (Maf) knockout in mouse Schwann cells. Maf is expressed by myelinating Schwann cells, and its specific deletion in these glia in mice resulted in reduced cholesterol biosynthesis, hypomyelination, and reduced axon conduction velocity. Towards understanding how Maf regulates the transcription of cholesterol biosynthesis genes, they noted an increased enrichment of Maf at enhancer regions for the cholesterol biosynthesis enzymes Hmgcr and squalene synthase (Fdft1), but no changes in Srebp1/2 mRNA levels. Interestingly, overexpression of Maf in the presence of a cellular cholesterol depleter (increases de novo synthesis) upregulated the cholesterol biosynthesis enzymes Hmgcr, Fdft1, and Idi1. In terms of mechanism, the authors determined that MAPK signaling acts to stabilize Maf, which can then bind to enhancer regions of cholesterol biosynthesis enzymes. Concurrently, CAMK acts to sequester histone deacetylase (HDAC)5 to the cytoplasm, allowing for the de‐repression of Maf. This is via increased histone H3 acetylation at the Maf promotor that augments Maf, thereby increasing cholesterol biosynthesis enzyme gene expression.

Discussion

It is thus evident that the differentiation of Schwann cells and the formation of myelin in the PNS during development are intricate and highly regulated processes which involve multiple cellular and molecular mediators and signaling pathways that are intrinsic to Schwann cells and PNS neurons but also present within the extracellular matrix (e.g., secreted adhesion molecules). As the reported effects on Schwann cell differentiation, proliferation, or myelination are partial for each molecular factor identified thus far, it suggests that the various factors collaborate to contribute to the aforementioned functions, or they form a redundancy system that could serve as a fail‐safe mechanism in case of defects at the gene or protein levels. For instance, if there were developmental and autoimmune myelination disorders that were associated with mutations in Schwann cell myelin protein genes or reduced Schwann cell neurotrophic factor secretion, one may consider a multi‐pronged approach to boost myelination by Schwann cells: genetic repair of mutated genes while augmenting extracellular space pro‐myelinating factors such as collagen and laminin, and boosting positive intracellular signals such as neurotrophins, NRGI‐Type III, SREBPs, mTORC1, etc. However, the timing of these manipulations would be important so as to obtain the correct amount of myelination (e.g., laminin‐211 transiently early on for myelination promotion and then re‐expression later to halt myelination) while understanding that myelination initiating factors (e.g., mTORC1) should likely be applied after Schwann cell differentiation is complete.

Because multiple components contribute to myelination, it is likely that similar complexities exist for the breakdown of PNS myelin and its subsequent reformation. This is indeed revealed in the next two sections, which inform on the current knowledge around the equally elegant processes of PNS myelin degradation after injury and disease, and remyelination.

Demyelination in the PNS and the Contributions of Schwann Cells and Macrophages

Axonal and Myelin Breakdown After PNS Injury

Axon Degeneration

Wallerian degeneration is the process by which the portion of an axon distal to an injury of sufficient magnitude degenerates [110]. As comprehensively reviewed by Coleman and Höke [111], for peripheral nerves, the degeneration of axons is reliant on the activation of sterile‐α and Toll/interleukin 1 receptor motif containing protein 1 (SARM1) due to the loss of signal delivery of nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyl‐transferase 2 to the damaged distal axons. SARM1 drives degeneration via two mechanisms: (1) a reduction in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, and (2) mobilization of calcium from intracellular stores. Along with the influx of calcium from the extracellular space into damaged axons, calcium activates calpain proteases, which mediate degradation of the cytoskeleton and proteolysis of neurofilaments [112, 113, 114, 115]. This process is rapid—within an hour of calcium influx, there is beading and fragmentation of the axons, which is an indication that the majority of axonal disintegration has occurred [116]. Of note, there is also a downregulation of cholesterol biosynthesis during axonal degeneration [117].

Myelin Breakdown

Not surprisingly, the myelin sheath surrounding degenerating axon segments also disintegrates. Myelin begins to fragment at Schmitt–Lantermann incisures, which are gaps in the myelin sheath that consist of Schwann cell cytoplasm [118]. Webster [118] observed that after axonal injury to guinea pig sciatic nerves, areas of the myelin sheath with the most incisures were the first to show segmentation of the myelin. Over time, more of these incisures appeared, and as such, the author proposed that Schwann cell processes were being inserted between myelin layers leading to a discontinuity of the myelin and thus formation of myelin ovoids. The finding that more processes were being inserted by Schwann cells was later contested by Ghabriel and Allt [119, 120], who determined in crushed rat sural nerves that rather than an increase in their overall quantity, the Schwann cell processes that encompass Schmitt–Lantermann incisures undergo an enlargement after injury, thus making them more visible. Jung et al. [121] additionally noted that the formation of myelin ovoids in teased mouse sciatic nerves, which is prominent at 36 h postinjury [120], occurs adjacent to the incisures rather than directly within them [121].

Like that of axonal breakdown [122], actin polymerization is key for myelin fragmentation, as pharmacological prevention of actin polymerization after injury in teased mouse sciatic axons resulted in a lack of myelin ovoid formation [121]. Jung et al. [121] also observed that the junction protein E‐cadherin gradually decreased in Schmitt–Lantermann incisures after injury while appearing to build up in the cytoplasm of Schwann cells at the site where the ovoids would separate. This occurred in an F‐actin polymerization‐dependent manner, leading the authors to postulate that the increase in actin polymerization altered the membrane in the Schmitt–Lantermann incisures, leading to disruption of the nearby myelin sheath and consequently fragmentation at those adjacent sites.

In addition to actin polymerization, phospholipase A2 (PLA2) also appears to play a role in myelin breakdown following Wallerian degeneration [123] that is likely due to the ability of PLA2 to produce lysophosphatidylcholine [124]. De et al. [123] showed that injection of a PLA2 inhibitor prevented demyelination in mice with transected sciatic nerves, and that the expression of both the secreted and cytosolic forms of PLA2 correlated with the rate of degeneration in the PNS after crush injury to rat sciatic nerves. That is, Schwann cells express it early on (~5 h), while both Schwann cells and macrophages expressed PLA2 later at day 5 post‐damage. As noted by the authors, phosphatidylcholine, which is acted on by PLA2, is expressed on both the intra‐ and extracellular sides of the myelin sheath, thus opening it to attack from the increased expression of both the secreted and cytosolic forms of PLA2 after nerve injury.

Schwann Cells and Demyelination After PNS Injury

Repair Schwann Cells

As Wallerian degeneration and demyelination are occurring, there are non‐neuronal processes that facilitate PNS degeneration and regeneration. These include de‐differentiation of Schwann cells, activation of resident macrophages, and recruitment of monocytes from the blood circulation. With respect to Schwann cells, a major shift in phenotype occurs after peripheral nerve damage, where both denervated myelinating and nonmyelinating Schwann cells de‐differentiate into a unique repair phenotype, along with an increase in proliferation [125]. Griffin et al. [126] elucidated that once myelinating Schwann cells begin to proliferate, they shed their myelin sheath, allowing it to be degraded and removed by repair Schwann cells and immune cells such as macrophages. The transition into a repair Schwann cell involves a change in morphology (elongated bipolar) [127] and shifts in transcriptional program [125]. In the case of myelinating Schwann cells, there is a silencing of pro‐myelinating factors such as Krox‐20/Egr2, Oct‐6, Brn‐2, NF‐κB, and Sox10 [34, 53], and genes associated with cholesterol biosynthesis. In contrast, factors associated with Schwann cell immaturity or premyelination such as laminin 1 (L1), neural cell adhesion molecule (NCAM), p75NTR, and glial fibrillary acidic protein are upregulated [125]. These repair Schwann cells also acquire other capabilities that promote a growth supportive environment—(1) production of neurotrophic factors that aid survival of neurons until they regenerate back to their targets, (2) secretion of cytokines and chemokines that promote the infiltration and activation of blood‐borne monocytes (reviewed in [128, 129, 130]), (3) myelin degradation (myelinophagy) (reviewed in [131, 132]), and (4) production of adhesion molecules that contribute to guiding regenerating axons along the bands of Bungner (columnar alignment of repair Schwann cells within the basal lamina) [125, 133] (Figure 2).

PNS demyelination. Multiple molecular factors from Schwann cells, macrophages, and fibroblasts are involved in promoting differentiation of repair Schwann cells, and breakdown and phagocytosis of myelin during demyelination in the PNS. These include transcription factors, cytokines and chemokines, neurotrophic factors, cell adhesion molecules, and TLR/Notch/MAPK/TRPV signaling. Role of c‐Jun, Notch, TRPV4 and TLR

The de‐differentiation of Schwann cells following PNS injury is regulated by several factors, but the transcription factor c‐Jun stands out as a primary player in rats and mice in vivo and in vitro [134, 135]. It appears that c‐Jun promotes reprogramming of the Schwann cell epigenome by binding to injury‐induced enhancers upstream of Sonic hedgehog in cultured rat Schwann cells [136], followed by activation of the Raf/MAPK/extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (using Raf‐transfected cultured rat Schwann cells and Raf‐Schwann cell conditional null mice) [137, 138], and p38 MAPK signaling pathways in mice [139]. c‐Jun is, however, not the sole modulator of de‐differentiation. Notch signaling, transient receptor potential vanilloid‐type 4 (TRPV4), and Toll‐like receptors (TLRs) have also been shown to impact the function of Schwann cells in the damaged PNS. For instance, when Notch activation is impaired, there are significantly more Schwann cells of a pro‐myelinating phenotype after a transect injury in mice [140]. In contrast, when Notch signaling is transgenically increased in mice, accelerated de‐differentiation and subsequent demyelination was noted, even in uninjured nerves. With respect to TRPV4, which is expressed by nonmyelinating Schwann cells [141], its expression increases after mouse sciatic nerve transection injury in conjunction with the proliferation of repair Schwann cells, and when it is knocked out, there are significant delays in demyelination which further retards the remyelination process.

Regarding TLRs, Schwann cells are deemed the main expressors in the PNS since they are expressed by all Schwann cells at the mRNA and protein levels (minimally on sensory and motor neurons), but with TLR3 and TLR4 expression being dominant [142]. As to their role, it appears that the receptors impact the immunological functions of the glia. For example, TLR2 and TLR3 contribute to the immune cell‐like activation of rat Schwann cells in response to necrotic axons in vitro [143], and Boivin et al. [144] documented that in mice globally null for MyD88 (central molecular convergence in TLR signaling), TLR2 or TLR4 resulted in decreased mRNA and protein expression of interleukin (IL)‐1β relative to WT animals after sciatic nerve injury. This would negatively impact other immune cells, and indeed fewer CD68+ macrophages were seen in all three transgenic lines postinjury. Not surprisingly, myelin debris clearance was also negatively impacted to varying degrees in the TLR‐deficient mice, which resulted in delayed regeneration and functional recovery. The immunological and debris clearance deficits could, however, be rescued with ligands for TLR2 and TLR4. That is, increases in IL‐1β and monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)‐1 were noted along with increases in phagocytosis and debris clearance, leading to improved functional recovery. Thus, TLRs play an important role in Schwann cell function (presumably repair Schwann cells) post‐PNS injury.

Myelinophagy

In recent years, Schwann cells have been found to participate in an autophagic process for myelin degradation called myelinophagy [131]. This is distinct from phagocytosis as the myelin consumed by these Schwann cells is cell‐intrinsic given that it was a component of the Schwann cell membrane. With respect to its discovery, when the mechanisms underlying myelin breakdown within Schwann cells were examined, alterations in the expression of autophagy‐related genes (e.g., Atg1/ULK complex, Atg9 cycling system) relative to uncut nerves, as well as elevations in proteins such as ATG7, ATG12, ATG5/ATG12 complex, ATG16L1, Wipi2, Beclin‐1, and LC3 II were noticed after a crush damage to rat sciatic nerves [145, 146]. The autophagy expression correlated with the presence of myelin debris within Schwann cell autophagosomes, and associated with JNK/c‐Jun but not mTOR signaling. As to their function and regulatory elements, there appears to be a partial role for autophagy markers in mediating Schwann cell clearance of myelin debris [147, 148]. Interruption of the autophagic process in mouse Schwann cells via Schwann cell‐specific knockout of the Atg7 gene or pharmacological disruption of lysosomal function, led to delayed or reduced myelin clearance by these glia in sciatic nerve crush or transection injury models. However, there appears to be a temporal element; the myelinophagic process peaked at 4 days postinjury but was no longer a significant factor after 9 days postinjury as demonstrated by Brosius Lutz et al. [147]. This is in alignment with findings by Jang et al. [148] who noted that despite a delay in myelin clearance after interfering with myelinophagy, the PNS repair program (e.g., Schwann cell de‐differentiation and re‐differentiation, c‐Jun induction, regeneration, or remyelination) was not significantly impacted. To identify other factors contributing to myelinophagy by Schwann cells, Brosius Lutz et al. [147] used RNA sequencing data from intact and crushed rat sciatic nerve and showed that phagocytosis by Schwann cells was almost completely dependent on expression of the Axl and Mertk receptors, which are members of the TAM family of receptor tyrosine kinases. When these receptors were knocked out individually in mice, in vitro phagocytosis by Schwann cells decreased by ~40%–50%, while almost no phagocytosis was performed when both receptors were knocked out. These findings were verified in the mice in vivo and indicated a necessity of Axl and Mertk receptors in clearance of myelin debris via myelinophagy by Schwann cells.

Discussion

Significant progress has thus been made in identifying the molecular regulators associated with repair Schwann cells not only with respect to their immune functions (i.e., cytokine/chemokine production) and myelin phagocytosis, but also their de‐differentiation. However, it would be prudent to understand how to prolong the phenotype of these beneficial Schwann cells since these cells appear to require eventual contact with axons otherwise they apoptose or adopt abnormal morphologies [149, 150, 151, 152, 153]. As there are currently no pharmaceutical therapeutics for peripheral nerve injuries and standard clinical care is to wait 3–4 months to see if spontaneous recovery occurs, this is a major problem biologically since repair Schwann cells would have long lost their protective and regenerative ability. Thus, maintaining the presence and function of repair Schwann cells until regeneration of peripheral axons occurs is an important research goal.

Macrophages and Demyelination After PNS Injury

Macrophages are essential for PNS regeneration. Due to chemokine secretion by repair Schwann cells, blood‐derived monocytes infiltrate into damaged peripheral nerves and mature into macrophages, where they, along with resident macrophages and Schwann cells, play a critical role in phagocytosis of myelin and axonal debris [154, 155, 156]. Some of the molecular factors that regulate myelin debris clearance by macrophages have been identified. This is of particular importance since, with aging, not only do fewer of these immune cells infiltrate nerves after damage, but for the few that do enter, their phagocytic capacity is lower thus negatively impacting debris clearance [157, 158, 159]. Further, as myelin contains inhibitors against axon growth (e.g., MAG), axonal regeneration and thus functional recovery are impeded if myelin debris is not cleared [160, 161]. Thus, identification of molecular regulators provides potential targets to increase macrophage influx and function after PNS injury.

PNS Macrophages

The infiltrating monocytes that enter peripheral nerves have distinct functions compared to the resident macrophages after injury [162]. Using fluorescent reporter mice to distinguish between resident and infiltrating macrophage populations, Boissonnas et al. [162] found that the kinetics of the two populations were different in a chronic mouse constriction injury model—the infiltrating population increased rapidly during the demyelinating phase (~days 5–10 postinjury) before decreasing during remyelination (~days 20–60 postinjury). The resident population, on the other hand, increased after day 10 and peaked at day 30 before decreasing. The researchers also performed RNA sequencing on the two populations and found that the infiltrating population was associated with extracellular matrix remodeling, whereas the resident population was associated with extracellular matrix regeneration and displayed higher proliferative activity. Distinct functionality of the populations was also confirmed using live‐cell imaging and macrophage‐depletion approaches, where recruited monocytes were the main mediators of debris clearance while resident macrophages participated in the regeneration process. By inhibiting infiltration of monocytes via C‐C chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2) deletion and parabiosis experiments, the authors ruled out phenotypic conversion as a cause for the increase in the resident population at later time points. Rather, the increase in resident populations was most likely due to rapid proliferation. Indeed, others have also observed the proliferation of resident macrophages post‐injury, although along a much more accelerated time course, potentially due to methodological and injury type differences [156, 163]. In support of the findings of Boissonnas et al. [162], Ydens et al. [164] found that at 1 and 5 days after injury in mice post‐crush damage, approximately 90% of macrophages in the nerve were monocyte‐derived as determined by single‐cell RNA sequencing. These immune cells still predominated at 1 month after the injury and did not revert to a resident macrophage phenotype.

Chemotaxis

As mentioned above, repair Schwann cells have the ability to secrete C‐C motif chemokine ligand (CCL)2/MCP‐1, CCL3/macrophage inhibitory protein (MIP)‐1α, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α, IL‐1α, and IL‐1ß which recruit immune cells [130, 165, 166]. Additionally, resident endoneurial macrophages upregulate Ccl6, Ccl7, Ccl8, and Ccl12 at day 1 post‐injury, which led to the recruitment of circulating monocytes, thus aligning with the pattern of increased epineurial macrophages. Siebert et al. [167] have gone on to show that the macrophage chemokine receptor CCR2, which binds CCL2/MCP‐1, is critical for recruitment of these immune cells, since when it is knocked out globally in mice, there were significantly fewer infiltrating macrophages in the nerve after sciatic nerve axotomy. Similarly, Perrin et al. [161] demonstrated that CCL2/MCP‐1, CCL3/MIP‐1α, and IL‐1ß are expressed in the PNS after sciatic transection in mice but not in the CNS. When expression of these factors was prevented with function‐blocking antibodies in the sciatic nerve, there was a lack of macrophage presence and consequently debris clearance. Further, when these chemokines and cytokines were induced in the spinal cord with recombinant proteins, it led to prompt macrophage infiltration and debris clearance. Thus, the secretion of chemokines by Schwann cells and resident macrophages is vital for peripheral macrophage recruitment and overall function.

Phenotype

Macrophage function and tissue specificity are dependent on markers expressed by these cells and their activation status [168]. In the process of regeneration, two main macrophage phenotypes are seen: pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory [169]. Presently, there are no reliable markers to distinguish the origin of macrophages after nerve injury (i.e., resident vs. infiltrating) [170], however, there are markers that inform on the functionality of the cells. With regard to pro‐inflammatory macrophages, markers such as iNOS, CD68, CD86, MHCII, and CD16/32 have been used [169, 171], while Arginase 1 and CD206 have been used to identify anti‐inflammatory macrophages [169, 171]. CD16/32, also known as FcγIII (CD16) and FcγII (CD32) receptors, is involved in Fcγ‐mediated phagocytosis [172] and its expression would therefore be expected to increase under pro‐inflammatory phagocytic conditions. CD206, also known as macrophage mannose receptor, is typically associated with pro‐regenerative macrophages and, in conjunction with pan‐macrophage markers, would be indicative of anti‐inflammatory macrophages. Interestingly, Ydens et al. [173] demonstrated that initially after an axotomy injury in mice (i.e., within 24 h), there was upregulation of the cytokine IL‐13, which is known to induce an anti‐inflammatory macrophage phenotype. This finding is in contrast to the known pro‐inflammatory macrophage presence that leads to debris clearance. The authors reasoned that initially after injury, an anti‐inflammatory presence may contribute to neurite outgrowth and neurotrophic support [173, 174].

Once in the nerve, Galectin‐3/MAC‐2 expression by both mouse Schwann cells and macrophages propels them to destroy myelin debris after sciatic nerve transection [175]. The production of Galectin‐3/MAC‐2 in these cells is caused by a rapid upregulation of granulocyte macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM‐CSF) by mouse nerve fibroblasts, macrophages, and Schwann cells in vitro and in vivo [176]. Upon arrival, the infiltrated monocyte‐derived macrophages contribute to further production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (i.e., TNF‐α, IL‐1α, IL‐1ß) to stimulate debris clearance [165]. IL‐10 expression in mouse macrophages slowly increases with time at the injury site and eventually leads to a phenotypic switch into an anti‐inflammatory state that thereby downregulates Wallerian degeneration [177, 178]. Mietto et al. [178] have shown that mice lacking IL‐10 do not have a phagocytosis‐induced downregulation of their pro‐inflammatory macrophage phenotype, and this leads to poor regeneration and functional recovery in vivo. In addition to the increased expression of IL‐10 in macrophages, these cells also produce IL‐6, which could contribute to the change to an anti‐ inflammatory phenotype [130]. Zou et al. [179] have also shown that silent information regulator 6 (SIRT6) is increased after a transect injury in the distal segment at 3 and 5 days post‐damage in mice. This increase in SIRT6 expression correlated with increases in macrophage infiltration and phagocytosis. However, the authors found in in vitro experiments that SIRT6 is likely involved in the conversion of pro‐inflammatory macrophages to an anti‐inflammatory phenotype. That is, when SIRT6 was knocked down in vitro, there was impaired macrophage migration and phagocytosis along with increased pro‐inflammatory polarization activated by NF‐κB and TNF‐α expression. It is also worth noting that this group has found that SIRT6 was a negative regulator of c‐Jun in Schwann cells [180].

Resolution

Once the role of macrophages is completed, the excess infiltrating cells must be cleared. Kuhlmann et al. [181] have shown in mice that a subpopulation of macrophages undergoes apoptosis while the others were found to have migrated into the circulation, draining lymph nodes, and spleen. Signaling between newly formed myelin and macrophages has also been shown to play a role in macrophage efflux, also termed the macrophage disappearance reaction [182]. Fry et al. [182] have shown that Nogo receptors, NgR1 and NgR2, expressed by rat macrophages interact with factors in newly synthesized myelin (e.g., MAG) repelling the macrophages away into the endoneurial space where they then migrate out of the nerve or die locally. This NgR/MAG signaling is mediated by Ras Homolog Family Member A activation in the immune cells.

Role of Scavenger Receptors, Complement, Antibodies, and Others in Phagocytosis of PNS Myelin by Macrophages

Using a 2F8 antibody which depletes both type AI and II scavenger receptors (SRAI/II) on macrophages, da Costa et al. [183] showed in an in vitro mouse nerve/macrophage co‐culture system that these receptors play a role in both macrophage infiltration as well as myelin phagocytosis since at the highest dose of the antibody, there was almost no migration of macrophages into the nerves, and thus myelin density was very high. These findings were supported in vivo, where significantly more lipid‐laden macrophages were still present at later time points after PNS crush injury in mice null for SRAI/II. This was accompanied by morphological abnormalities such as numerous onion bulbs and demyelinating and remyelinating axons throughout the fascicle [184]. Eto et al. [185] have validated these findings by showing a role for a class B scavenger receptor, CD36, in myelin debris clearance and subsequent remyelination after sciatic nerve crush injury in mice.

Another major contributor to myelin debris phagocytosis by macrophages after peripheral nerve injury is the complement system. Early in vitro studies showed that areas containing membrane attack complex were selectively targeted by macrophages and contributed to demyelination in a rat DRG/peritoneal macrophage co‐culture system [186]. Further, inhibition of macrophage complement receptor type 3 (CR3) resulted in attenuation of myelin phagocytosis in vitro in mouse sciatic nerves co‐cultured with peritoneal macrophages [187]. Interestingly, the activity of CR3‐dependent myelin phagocytosis can be increased or decreased depending on which subunit (αM/CD11b or β2/CD18) is bound [188]; antibodies against αM/CD11b led to inhibition, whereas antibodies against β2/CD18 led to promotion of purified mouse myelin in mouse peritoneal macrophages in vitro. Moreover, in vitro, macrophages were unable to enter the nerve when C3 was depleted from serum, and treatment with anti‐CR3 antibodies in vivo led to less infiltration of, and activation/phagocytosis of macrophages, along with deficits in myelin debris clearance that delayed regeneration in rats with sciatic nerve crush damage [160]. Of note, Hirata et al. [189] found that ED1+ but not ED2+ phagocytic macrophages displayed similar associations with recruitment and phagocytic ability as CR3+ profiles following sciatic nerve crush in rats, indicating that these different phenotypes may have divergent functions [162, 164]. Altogether, these studies highlight that complement is critical for opsonization and recognition of myelin debris by macrophages [190].

While complement factors in serum are capable of opsonizing myelin debris, so too are anti‐myelin antibodies [191]. Indeed, Hann Bonnekoh et al. [172] found in co‐cultures of mouse peripheral nerve segments exposed to peritoneal macrophages that infiltrated macrophages were positive for Fc receptors as well as CR3. Vargas et al. [192] subsequently showed that endogenous antibodies are critical for the phagocytosis of debris and that they may induce complement activation. Using JHD mice, which do not have mature B cells and therefore no antibodies, it was found that there was no accumulation of Ig in degenerating myelin sheaths after a PNS injury along with delays in myelin debris clearance. This deficit was rescued with bone marrow, sera, purified IgG, or purified IgM transplants of WT into JHD mice. The postulated mechanism is that antibodies present after nerve injury in WT mice may recruit macrophages by activating the complement system, given that complement products can attract immune cells. This is in keeping with an older study by Hann et al. [193] who found that IgM depletion resulted in significantly more myelin profiles in mice after a transection injury.

Interestingly, myelin is capable of inhibiting its own phagocytosis. This appears to be via the expression of CD47 on myelin and activation of the receptor signal regulatory protein‐α (SIRPα) on the immune cell in rats [194]. Other factors may be involved, as elements in serum may also activate SIRPα without the contribution of CD47. Elberg et al. [195] provided some specificity for this assertion by showing that when SIRPα is knocked out in mice, although the number of macrophages was not altered, there was increased debris clearance by macrophages and therefore more timely regeneration of axons after injury.

Schwann Cell‐Macrophage Interactions During PNS Demyelination

Not surprisingly, Schwann cells and macrophages do not act in isolation in peripheral nerves, especially considering that monocyte influx is dependent on cytokine/chemokine production by the glia [129]. This was evidenced by Perry et al. [196] who showed that when mice were irradiated prior to nerve injury (thus depleting circulating monocytes), degeneration and clearance of debris progressed as normal early after injury. The conclusion was that degeneration of myelin structures is initially independent of macrophages and is most likely due to de‐differentiated Schwann cells acting as phagocytes. Indeed, Stoll et al. [197] observed that rat Schwann cells contain myelin debris prior to the significant appearance of macrophages. The entry of blood‐derived macrophages occurs later and maintains the process of phagocytosis, thus demonstrating that the myelin degeneration process occurs in multiple stages. There are some shared molecular regulators. For instance, Galectin‐3/MAC‐2 expression by both Schwann cells and macrophages in mice promotes them to destroy myelin debris [175]. This production of Galectin‐3/MAC‐2 is caused by a rapid upregulation of GM‐CSF in transected mouse sciatic nerves [176].

While an interaction between Schwann cells and macrophages is important for demyelination, and for Schwann cell recruitment of monocyte‐derived macrophages, Stratton et al. [198] have found a role for growth arrest specific 6 (Gas6) expression by macrophages in mediating the transition of Schwann cells from a repair phenotype into a remyelinating phenotype. In their study, it was found that macrophage depletion with clodronate administration in rats after sciatic nerve crush injury just prior to the onset of remyelination resulted in significantly more repair phenotype Schwann cells. Further, when bone marrow was transplanted from Gas6 null mice into WT, it resulted in increased numbers of Schwann cells, decreased internode length, and less P0+ myelin segments, supporting the notion that Gas6 from macrophages is important for regulating the re‐differentiation of myelinating Schwann cells during PNS regeneration and remyelination. Conversely, Stratton and Shah [199] have proposed a role for Schwann cells in determining the phenotype of macrophages through secretion of cytokines and other unidentified ligands based on the literature; however, they state that the specific mechanisms underlying this control have yet to be definitively elucidated.

Discussion

In sum, numerous studies have shown that blood‐borne macrophages are critical for axonal and myelin debris clearance as they create a supportive environment for axon regeneration. However, resident macrophages and Schwann cells start the clearance process early on before monocyte infiltration. The question is whether these resident cells are possible targets to enhance debris clearance when infiltrating blood monocytes are underperforming as with aging. Indeed, we have shown that pro‐inflammatory but not anti‐inflammatory macrophages are likely the main phagocytes of myelin debris, and that a small heat shock protein called CRYAB (aka alphaB‐crystallin/heat shock protein (HSP)B5) plays a role in dampening the presence of these immune cells after they have cleared myelin debris, especially in the older, injured PNS [200, 201].

PNS Remyelination

During Wallerian degeneration, repair Schwann cells will re‐differentiate back into a myelinating or ensheathing phenotype upon contact with regenerating axons [202]. With respect to myelinating Schwann cells, multiple molecular factors contribute to a remyelinating state, with many of these factors overlapping with developmental myelination mediators (Figure 3).

Many of the molecular factors involved in remyelination in the PNS overlap with those during developmental myelination. Cholesterol sequestration from macrophages, as well as NRG1‐Type III/ErbB, ADAM19, and Gnao1/bFGF signaling promote or dampen differentiation of myelinating Schwann cells and synthesis of myelin proteins during remyelination in the PNS. Positive Regulators of Remyelinating Schwann Cells

Oct‐6, Krox‐20/Egr2, Zeb‐2, YAP, Taz, Sin3A, P2 Basic Protein, and Merlin

Positive regulators of remyelinating Schwann cells include the transcription factors Oct‐6, Krox‐20/Egr2, Zeb‐2, YAP, Taz, Sin3A, P2 basic protein, and Merlin among others. With respect to transcription factors, the remyelinating shift is caused by an upregulation of Oct‐6 and Krox‐20/Egr2 [202]. The evidence was provided by Topilko et al. [51] who noted arrested development and an inability to transition into a myelinating phenotype when Krox‐20/Egr2 was knocked out of mouse Schwann cells. This was accompanied by rapid myelin breakdown and a lack of mRNA for several myelin proteins including P0 and MBP. Similar to Krox‐20/Egr2 knockouts, Oct‐6 null mice display a lack of myelination, but P0 and MBP mRNA levels are preserved [48]. As in development, Zeb‐2 also appears to play a role in re‐differentiation of Schwann cells since markers of de‐differentiation could still be detected 8 weeks following sciatic nerve crush injury in the absence of Zeb‐2 in mice [59, 203]. Not surprisingly then, remyelination was incomplete at that timepoint [59].

As with most molecules, multiple regulators may interact together or act in parallel and this appears to be the case with remyelinating Schwann cells. For example, Zhang et al. [60] noted that after sciatic nerve crush in rats, Sin3A (also known as HDAC2) was localized to Schwann cells and its expression mirrored that of Krox‐20/Egr2, where it decreased initially after injury and then increased progressively over time. To understand its function, Sin3A was knocked down with viral vectors injected into the crushed sciatic nerve. This led to decreased expression of MAG and P0 protein and thinner myelin after regeneration. Whether the Sin3A and Krox‐20/Egr2 pathways converge is, however, unknown. A similar phenomenon of possible convergence was noted for P2 basic protein (also known as peripheral myelin protein 2), where P2 basic protein‐deficient mice with nerve crush injuries showed thinner myelin postinjury as well as deficits in nerve conduction velocity at 14 days postinjury compared to controls [204, 205]. This may be because P2 basic protein expression is downstream of the cleavage of NRG1‐Type III by BACE1, and as P2 is involved in regulating energy homeostasis during remyelination, the knockout in mice overexpressing NRG1‐Type III leads to reduced ATP production and basal respiration and thus impaired remyelination.

These studies reveal that for the most part, the molecular machinery is very similar between developmental myelination and remyelination in adult mammals postdamage. This is well exemplified by the interconnections among Merlin, YAP, and Taz during peripheral nerve regeneration and remyelination [206, 207]. Deletion of Merlin (tumor suppressor encoded by the neurofibromatosis Type 2 gene) in mouse Schwann cells leads to thinner myelin sheaths and increased endoneurial space after injury [207]. A similar observation was made by Mindos et al. [206] who noted hypomyelination in the absence of injury and significant declines in the number of myelinated axons 21 and 60 days postinjury upon loss of Merlin in Schwann cells in mice; there were also deficits in regeneration and an increased presence of macrophages. The authors speculate that these dysregulations occurred through the Merlin‐dependent regulation of c‐Jun in Schwann cells and consequently declines in Hippo signaling and the activation of YAP, a key transcription factor during myelination in the developing PNS. Grove et al. [208] subsequently examined the impact of an inducible double knockout of YAP and Taz from Schwann cells and found that after a transect injury, mice null for YAP and Taz had normal proliferation and de‐differentiation of Schwann cells but remyelination was markedly impaired compared to controls. The authors noted that the knockout Schwann cells did downregulate de‐differentiation‐associated genes compared to controls (e.g., c‐Jun) but failed to upregulate Krox‐20/Egr2 to initiate the remyelination process. However, as the mice were not viable past 14 days posttamoxifen injection, the long‐term consequences of a loss of YAP and Taz in an injury context could not be elucidated. Jeanette et al. [209] were able to address this unknown in a model that was viable for a longer term. The inducible mouse model involved inactivity of YAP and Taz in Schwann cells (i.e., YAP was heterozygously knocked out and Taz was fully knocked out). In these animals, peripheral nerve regeneration and remyelination were drastically impaired, with very few remyelinated axons present at 20 days postsciatic nerve crush and approximately half remyelinated by 60 days postinjury; the thickness of the myelin in the knockouts was, however, similar to controls. As c‐Jun was elevated in Schwann cells at both 5 and 60 days postinjury, it was concluded that the remyelination deficits seen were likely due to prolonged expression of c‐Jun.

NRG And BACE1

Similar to development, remyelination is contributed by the axonal level of NRG1 and its cleavage regulator, BACE1. For instance, major deficits in both regeneration and remyelination were evident after PNS injury when NRG1 was conditionally knocked out from subsets of mouse sensory and motor axons; although these processes attained WT levels at later timepoints [210, 211]. These studies did not assess for NRG1 isoforms, but both NRG1‐Type I and NRG1‐Type III may be involved in remyelination. NRG1‐Type I is upregulated in axons after sciatic nerve crush injury in mice [212], while NRG1‐Type III decreases early after damage before returning to normal levels as regenerating rat axons reinnervate peripheral targets [213]. Stassart et al. [212] suggest that the transient downregulation of NRG1‐Type III may be the trigger for the increase in NRG1‐Type I.

It is intriguing that following PNS regeneration myelin sheaths are thinner, as is also observed in the CNS [214]. The reasons are unknown, but it is noteworthy that when NRG1‐Type I and ‐Type III are overexpressed in mice, these structural differences are no longer present following regeneration [212]. The thickness deficit does not seem to negatively impact functional recovery in PNS systems that do regenerate. However, recurrent injuries may eventually display a defect in function if myelin reformation becomes thinner and thinner, at which point knowing how to augment NRG1‐Type I and NRG1‐Type III to above homeostatic levels may become imperative.

As mentioned earlier, the enzyme BACE1 that cleaves NRG1 is a positive regulator of myelination during development [215, 216]. BACE1 appears to have a similar positive role in remyelination as mice null for BACE1 are hypomyelinated after peripheral axon injury and display thinner myelin sheaths than WT [215]. As BACE1 cleaves both NRG1‐Type I and ‐Type III as well as NRG3, the authors speculate that without the cleaved domain of NRG1 binding to ErbB receptors in Schwann cells, there would be less upregulation of myelin‐associated genes in the knockouts. In a follow‐up study, Hu et al. [216] demonstrated that expression of BACE1 in both Schwann cells and peripheral axons is needed for remyelination. That is, BACE1 is increased at the mRNA and protein levels along with NRG1‐Type I in mouse Schwann cells after injury, and it is the cleavage of NRG1‐Type I by BACE1 that is important for remyelination. No changes in Schwann cell proliferation, macrophage recruitment, or phagocytosis were evident, indicating that BACE1 cleavage of NRG1‐Type I is key for timely PNS remyelination since this process does reach WT levels at later timepoints [210, 211].

Others (Meltrin‐β /ADAM19, O‐GlcNAc Transferase, Gpr126/Adgrg6, Stat1, CRYAB)

A number of other positive mediators of remyelination in the PNS have been identified that have not (yet) been shown to have a function in PNS myelin development. One of these is Meltrin‐β/ADAM19 which when knocked out in mice, results in delayed upregulation of Krox‐20/Egr2 due to deficits in AKT activation. As a consequence, the upregulation of other myelin‐associated genes is protracted, which further delays remyelination [217]. A similarly impaired remyelination was seen when O‐GlcNAc transferase was knocked out from mouse Schwann cells, as the metabolic status of the glia is disrupted, thereby preventing the re‐differentiation of repair Schwann cells [109]. Gpr126/Adgrg6 is another pro‐remyelinator as its global deletion results in persistence of myelin debris postinjury and fewer myelinated axons with thin myelin sheaths [218]. Its function is not selective to Schwann cells, however, as removal of Gpr126/Adgrg6 from mouse Schwann cells did not impact demyelination, proliferation, or c‐Jun activation, but it did impair macrophage recruitment to the site of injury due to impaired TNF expression that further impacted chemokine expression by Schwann cells. Finally, we have shown that CRYAB, which is expressed by mouse and rat Schwann cells and PNS axons [219, 220, 221], plays a role in the re‐differentiation of repair Schwann cells back to a myelinating phenotype; its absence also results in reduced remyelination after sciatic nerve crush damage [221]. This small heat shock protein also declines with age in the PNS and correlates with thinner myelin sheaths under homeostatic and PNS injurious scenarios [201].

Negative Schwann Cell Remyelination Regulators

In addition to pro‐remyelinators, negative regulators of remyelination have been identified in the PNS. SRY‐Box Transcription Factor 2 (Sox2) is one such negative mediator—its overexpression in mouse Schwann cells after injury results in deficits in remyelination as well as increases in macrophage recruitment [222]. A similar effect was noted for the Gpr, G protein alpha subunit o (Gnao1) [223], where its selective knockdown in mouse Schwann cells led to more MAG expression and thicker myelin sheaths relative to controls. Schwann cell overexpression, on the other hand, had the opposite effect, with both MAG expression and regenerated myelin thickness being greatly reduced. Basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) may be another negative regulator of remyelination following regeneration, as the growth factor was found to suppress the production of P0 in Schwann cells in vitro [224]. Further, when bFGF was knocked out globally in mice, it resulted in an increased number of regenerated myelinated axons [225]. Finally, it appears that cell adhesion molecule expression by Schwann cells needs to be dampened for successful remyelination to occur. Martini and Schachner [226] noted that when remyelination occurred, both L1 and NCAM expression were greatly reduced after the myelinating Schwann cell process was approximately 1.5 loops around the axon in mice. However, Pereira et al. [227] have shown that mice deficient for integrin‐linked kinase display reduced AKT activation as well as extremely impaired remyelination even though there were numerous Schwann cells present. Thus, the role of cell adhesion molecules in Schwann cells for remyelination may depend on the adhesion factor involved. That is, some promote remyelination while others are inhibitory.

Role of Schwann Cells and Macrophages in Cholesterol Recycling in PNS Remyelination

As in myelination during development, cholesterol is critically important for the reformation of myelin after PNS damage [228]. In an early study by Rawlins et al. [228], injection of radioactive cholesterol into the nerves of mice after a crush injury revealed its detection not only in the degenerating myelin, but it was found to be reincorporated into the sheath during remyelination. This process involves de novo synthesis and trafficking of cholesterol and fatty acids [229, 230]. Within 1 week, regenerating axons start to be observed within Schwann cell structures known as bands of Büngner that also contain macrophages full of debris and lipid droplets. After 2 weeks, these laden macrophages leave the bands of Büngner, and that egress is accompanied by an increase in apolipoprotein E (ApoE) and the beginnings of low‐density lipoprotein (LDL)‐mediated transport of cholesterol into Schwann cells for the initiation of remyelination. The transport of recycled cholesterol is dependent on lipoproteins containing ApoE, a cholesterol transport protein [231] that is highly expressed in the nerve following PNS injury and during regeneration [232, 233]. Like Posse de Chaves et al. [234, 235], who observed an uptake of lipids via LDL‐mediated transport in regenerating axons, Boyles et al. [236] have also shown that regenerating axons express elevated levels of LDL receptors in the first 1–2 weeks postinjury as they are regenerating and while myelin and axonal debris are being degraded by Schwann cells and macrophages. This provides regenerating axons with the cholesterol that they need to form their membranes. The levels of ApoE in the nerve peaked at weeks 3–4 postinjury, however, myelination still continued even though de novo cholesterol biosynthesis remained low up to 10 weeks postinjury in their model. This may be because around week three postinjury when remyelination is occurring, Schwann cells express LDL receptors allowing for cholesterol transport from lipid droplet‐laden macrophages to complement their de novo synthesis. Furthermore, in vitro, macrophages that are loaded with cholesterol secrete more ApoE [237]. This ApoE appears to be important for the recycling process as Goodrum [238] demonstrated that a portion of the cholesterol that is freed during degeneration becomes associated with ApoE and other lipoproteins. Given that this transport method exists, Goodrum [239] also explored the role of serum cholesterol levels in regeneration and found no effect when serum cholesterol was depleted. Thus, the local macrophage cholesterol recycling process appears to be key during PNS remyelination.

Summary and Conclusions

As reviewed above, the process of myelin formation during PNS development and regeneration, and its destruction after peripheral nerve damage, is a complex and highly regulated process that involves multiple neuronal and non‐neuronal cell types and signaling mediators often overlapping in time and space. With so many participants, one postulates that the many components are redundancies to ensure/increase the odds that efficient remyelination occurs. Indeed, the adult mammalian PNS is capable of successful axon regeneration and remyelination unlike the damaged CNS. However, it may also explain why PNS remyelination and axon regeneration are deficient in some adult mammals after PNS injury (e.g., humans), as many factors have to be coordinated in a timely manner at different stages. What is curious is why remyelinated myelin sheaths are thinner in both the PNS and CNS. As the environment of the intact adult PNS is different molecularly and structurally than during development (e.g., some developmental factors such as GAP‐43 decline upon maturity), it is possible that the level of pro‐myelinators may need to be expressed at higher levels after PNS damage as shown by Stassart et al. [212] who found that normal myelin thickness after PNS damage could be achieved when NRG1‐Type I and Type III are overexpressed. Further, even though the adult mammalian PNS can in general regenerate after injury, this is not so for the human PNS. Based on the multitude of factors required for remyelination and their role at specific stages after injury, it is likely that combinatorial therapy would need to be administered at specific times (e.g., early vs. late postdamage). While many components and mechanisms of the myelination process at homeostasis and postinjury have been elucidated, more studies are needed to explore the time course of these molecular patterns with the eventual hope of achieving complete remyelination and full functional recovery for the human PNS.
