Government Insurance and Higher Area Deprivation Index Associated With Increased Time to Care in Patients With Traumatic Patellar Instability
Authors: Vibav H. Mouli, Allan K. Metz, Eileen A. Crawford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Patellar instability is an increasingly prevalent condition in the adolescent population, often requiring surgical intervention for traumatic or recurrent dislocations. Despite growing recognition of health disparities in orthopaedic surgery, little is known about sociodemographic factors associated with delays to care. We hypothesized that government insurance and higher Area Deprivation Index (ADI) would be associated with increased time to care.Methods:A retrospective review was conducted of pediatric patients (ages 7 to 19 years) undergoing surgery for traumatic patellar instability at a single institution from September 2015 to September 2022. Demographic characteristics, ADI scores, and time-to-care variables were collected including time to first visit, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and day of surgery. Multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted with sex, race, insurance status, and ADI as independent variables.Results:A total of 118 patients were included (mean age 15.0 ± 2.0 years; 54.2% female). Higher ADI was significantly associated with longer time from injury to the first visit (β = 0.1, P = 0.042) and from the first visit to MRI (β = 1.9, P = 0.041). Government insurance was significantly associated with longer time from surgical decision to surgery (β = 24.6, P = 0.019). No notable associations were found for sex or race.Conclusion:ADI and public insurance are two sociodemographic factors associated with delays to care for pediatric patients treated with surgery after traumatic patellar instability. Longer care journeys in this population may affect patients' quality of life. Additional efforts are warranted to mitigate these potential health disparities and ensure more equitable care delivery.

Patellar instability is a functionally limiting condition that has become increasingly common over the past 30 years, particularly within the adolescent population, with recent estimates suggesting an incidence rate of 29 to 43 per 100,000 persons in the 10- to 17-year-old age group.1-3 The etiology of patellar instability is often multifactorial and may be influenced by demographic factors (e.g., female sex), anatomic factors (e.g., trochlear dysplasia or elevated tibial tubercle to trochlear groove distance), genetic factors (e.g., syndromic ligamentous laxity), and exposure to knee trauma.4 While most initial episodes without notable chondral injury are treated nonsurgically, recurrent episodes may require surgery and subsequent physical therapy. Although it is generally accepted that early diagnosis and management are key to optimal patient outcomes in this population, health disparities may exist, which prevent equal or equitable care management.

While there has been much investigation into health disparities in anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction, there is comparatively little in the realm of patellar instability and medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction, which is the most common procedure performed to reconstruct the patella's primary restraint to lateral dislocation.5 Like the ACL injury population, patellar instability typically occurs in young, active patients. However, initial events are most often treated nonsurgically, and there is often an extended asymptomatic period before recurrence and surgical discussion. In the ACL literature, for example, Patel et al6 described the association of government health insurance status among 127 pediatric patients requiring ACL reconstruction with delays to receiving a first appointment, advanced imaging in the form of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), time to surgery, and returning to play. Black and Hispanic patients undergo fewer physical therapy visits and have longer times to ACL reconstruction.7 Black and uninsured patients were less likely to receive ACL surgery altogether.8 There has been an important and ongoing shift toward recognizing and addressing social determinants of health (SDOH) throughout orthopaedic surgery,9 but there remains an important knowledge gap for pediatric patients with patellar instability who can experience prolonged and asymmetric care journeys.

As such, the purpose of this study was to examine the association of various patient sociodemographic characteristics with time to care for patients with traumatic patellar instability. Our hypothesis was that patients with government insurance and a higher Area Deprivation Index (ADI) would be associated with increased time to care.

Methods

Study Setting and IRB Approval

We conducted a retrospective chart review at a single urban academic medical center. Approval for this study was obtained from our institutional review board (IRB) and a waiver of consent was granted.

Cohort Selection

We enrolled pediatric patients who underwent surgery for patellar instability between September 2015 and September 2022 with a sports medicine fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeon at a single institution. Inclusion criteria were (1) patients aged 7 to 19 years at the time of initial injury; (2) appropriate documentation of all relevant time points in the electronic medical record; and (3) a diagnosis of traumatic patellar instability treated with surgery, which was defined as MPFL reconstruction or medial retinacular repair. These cases were identified using initial CPT codes 27427, 27566, 27405, 27420, and 27418. Traumatic patellar instability was defined as an acute injury with patellar dislocation. Patients who had chronic, congenital, or syndromic ligamentous laxity or a history of subacute subluxation episodes greater than 3 months were not included given inherent discrepancies in time to presentation. Similarly, those with prior knee surgery, including surgery for patellar instability, were excluded.

Data Collection

Patient identification was done using DataDirect, which is an institutional EMR tool that enables deidentified data extraction. It was used in this study to provide demographic information on the cohort including age at injury, sex, race, body mass index (BMI) at initial encounter, insurance type, and primary nine-digit zip code associated with insurance at the time of the injury. This zip code was then used to generate an Area Deprivation Index (ADI) score for each patient. ADI is a scoring system previously established to validate variables pertinent to neighborhood-specific socioeconomic disadvantage,10-13 with data publicly available for all areas of the United States. The index is a composite measure of 17 census variables encompassing four key domains: education, income/employment, housing, and household characteristics. Higher ADI scores indicate a higher socioeconomic disadvantage. We also collected state and national ADI percentiles for each patient.

The clinical history of each patient was reviewed to determine the date and mechanism of their injury. Time-to-care variables were calculated in days and included time from injury to the first visit, time from injury to advanced imaging (MRI), time from injury to the day of surgery (DOS), time from the first visit to MRI, time from the first visit to DOS, time from MRI to DOS, and time from the date of decision to proceed with surgery to DOS itself.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline descriptive statistics were conducted with Student t-tests, Mann-Whitney U test, or chi-square/Fisher exact tests where appropriate. Multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to evaluate associations within our cohort with individual time-to-care variables as dependent variables and patient sex, race, insurance status, and ADI as independent variables. An a priori power analysis with a power of 0.90 and alpha value of 0.05 was conducted using ACL health disparities literature to determine our target cohort size.6

Results

Cohort Demographics

After chart review, 118 patients met the inclusion criteria (Table 1). The cohort had a mean age at initial injury of 15.0 ± 2.0 years and mean BMI of 25.3 ± 6.6 points, with 64 (54.2%) female and 107 (90.7%) White patients. In total, 26 patients (22.0%) had government insurance, and the mean ADI was 42.4 ± 22.8 points. Our power analysis revealed a target cohort size of 84 patients required to achieve a detectable difference in a two-tailed independent samples Student t-test with an effect size of 0.72, power of 0.90, and allocation ratio of 1.0.

Time to Care

No significant differences were noted on Student t-test regarding time-to-care variables and insurance status (P > 0.05). Multivariable linear regression analysis evaluated the impact of sociodemographic factors on time to care in patients with patellar instability (Table 2). The time from injury to the first clinical visit showed no notable associations with sex, race, or insurance status. However, there was an effect with ADI (β = 0.1, P = 0.042). Similarly, the time from injury to MRI was not significantly influenced by sex or race, but ADI approached a statistically significant result (β = 1.4, P = 0.057).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest study of surgical patients related to health disparities in patellar instability and the only study to date that is dedicated to acute instability. We found that ADI and public health insurance are associated with delays to care for pediatric patients treated with surgery after traumatic patellar instability. In other words, patients from neighborhoods with higher socioeconomic deprivation experience delays in being seen after their patellar dislocations and longer times before receiving MRI scans after their initial visit. In addition, patients with government insurance have a prolonged care journey from deciding to undergo surgery to having the surgery performed.

The results of our study have important implications because timely surgical care for patellar instability has been associated with improved Kujala scores, a faster rate of return to sport and play, and lower incidence of recurrent instability.14,15 Evidence also suggests notable psychological and mental health tolls of living with untreated instability, including a fear and apprehension of patellar dislocations, avoidance behaviors surrounding those circumstances, and low self-esteem, features that are poorly quantified.16 The chronicity of untreated patellar instability clinically may also lead to worsening patellofemoral chondral injuries,17 making subsequent surgical interventions more difficult.

Our research represents an important addition to the sparse but important literature in this field. Allahabadi et al,18 for example, conducted a direct comparison of 38 publicly insured and 40 privately insured pediatric patients undergoing surgery for patellar instability, finding clear delays across all stages of the care journey (clinical evaluation, imaging, and surgery). However, an important distinction in this work is their inclusion of patients initially managed nonsurgically but later undergoing surgery after symptom recurrence, which may inherently influence time-to-care outcome measurements. Regardless, the study benefits from a safety-net hospital setting, providing more representative racial and ethnic representation and eliminating any preferential access to care within the health systems. Shankar et al19 more recently demonstrated that owning a home and having a full-time job, both important markers of socioeconomic stability, play a role in driving optimal postoperative outcomes in patellar instability. Finally, Li et al8 conducted a large-scale, 2557-patient analysis, finding that Black and uninsured patients had a lower likelihood to receive surgery for patellar instability, with Black patients experiencing higher overall charges.

Unlike prior research emphasizing individual predictors of delays to care, our work uses ADI, a composite measure that may provide an improved understanding of our patients' resources in obtaining care. ADI accounts for variables such as percentage of high school graduates, level of unemployment, mean rent or mortgage cost, percentage of single-parent households, and percentage of households with access to a motor vehicle. All of these factors can affect the ability of a patient's caregiver to get them to multiple appointments and navigate the numerous steps to treatment and recovery. Future efforts should also evaluate whether ADI has an impact on clinical and functional outcomes in patellar instability.

Our results suggest the need to better incorporate individual patient sociodemographic circumstances into our care plans and optimize clinical care pathways accordingly. A number of areas of attention and interventions have been proposed within orthopaedic literature. Shankar et al,19 for example, noted that having a general primary care physician appointment within 1 year of a patellar instability episode was predictive of shorter time to evaluation. Close follow-up through care navigators and the utilization of formal medical interpreters for non-English–speaking patients may ensure that patients requiring surgical intervention are not lost to follow-up. Targeted assessments of relevant familial socioeconomic circumstances at the initial acute instability episode or in the clinic may also help to mitigate delays to care and involve appropriate ancillary team members. Preoperative education programs and standardized care pathways for specific diagnoses like patellar instability may facilitate a smoother care journey. In the pediatric population, existing relationships between medical providers and school athletic programs may also open up another communication pathway to help support the patient and their caregivers.

There are limitations of this study that must be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective review of electronic medical records, there may be inherent inaccuracies in documentation or in collection of data. This retrospective design also limits the ability to distinguish between patient-level and institutional contributors to delays in care. For example, we acknowledge that the delay observed from surgical decision to surgery for patients with government insurance may also reflect, in part, patient-level or system-level barriers—such as prior authorization requirements or scheduling flexibility. Future prospective studies with granular data on authorization time lines and OR scheduling practices are needed to characterize the specific mechanisms involved. Second, as we required documentation of specific time points in the care journey, there are likely a number of patients lost in the care journey who may have well provided important context to understanding broader determinants of health for patients with patellar instability. Finally, the results from a single academic medical center in the midwestern United States may not appropriately represent the broader population. Particularly for race and ethnicity, the composition of this cohort may limit the generalizability of these findings to more racially and ethnically diverse patient populations. It is therefore possible that this study underestimates true impacts of socioeconomic burden on delays to care. A multicenter, prospective study across multiple urban and rural sites would be justified in creating more externally generalizable results.

Conclusion

Area Deprivation Index and public insurance are two sociodemographic factors associated with delays to care for pediatric patients treated with surgery after traumatic patellar instability. Longer care journeys in the patellar instability population mean longer time away from activities that are socially, emotionally, and physically rewarding to kids. As our level of awareness improves, it is important to focus additional efforts on recognizing patients in need and implementing processes to mitigate these disparities and ensure more equitable care delivery.
