Study on the emission characteristics of VOCs under the condition of biomass blending combustion
Authors: Zhonghao Yan, Yu Gao, Yong Zhang, Ning Jiang, Linmao Pu, Lijuan Ji, Xinyu Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to understand the emission characteristics of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in the flue gas under the mixed combustion of biomass, the study on the emission characteristics of VOCs in the flue gas was carried out on a 58 MW circulating fluidized bed (CFB) unit. The results show that the co-firing of biomass can significantly reduce the emissions of VOCs and NOx and SO2. Changes in blended fuel particle size and combustion temperature reduce VOCs emissions. The most obvious change in the emission reduction of VOCs is reflected in the increase of the biomass mixing ratio from 20 % to 30 %. Biomass contains less S and N elements is the reason for the reduction of NOx and SO2 emissions. The emission of pollutants such as VOCs was the lowest when the biomass blending ratio was 40 %. Based on the actual operation of the power plant, 30 % is the optimal mixing ratio. The analysis showed that the amount of VOCs components had a strong positive correlation with the proportion of biomass in the fuel. The emission of VOCs under the condition of biomass blending has different characteristics from coal-fired boilers and biomass boilers. Under the two different mixing ratios, benzene series accounted for the largest proportion of VOCs emissions, reaching 44.38 % (20 %) and 33.75 % (40 %), respectively. The emission of benzene series is dominated by benzene and toluene, the emission of alkanes is dominated by n-hexane, and the emission of esters is dominated by ethyl acetate. The ozone formation potential (OFP) was analyzed by the maximum incremental reactivity method. The contribution of ozone generation potential at 20 % and 40 % mixing ratios was mainly from benzene series, which contributed 69.88 % and 70.24 %, respectively, and alkanes. contribution can also account for 25.76 % and 17.75 %.Keywords: Volatile organic compounds (VOCs), Biomass, Emission characteristics, Ozone formation potential, CFB combustion

Introduction

Along with the growing concern over global climate issues, China, as a major G20 country, has proposed to peak its carbon dioxide emissions by 2030 and work towards carbon neutrality by 2060. Although China has been undergoing a transition from traditional to clean energy sources, coal still accounts for nearly 60 % of energy consumption, with 70 % of this coal used to generate electricity in coal-fired power plants.

Biomass is the fourth largest energy source after coal, oil, and natural gas in the world in terms of total energy consumption. Because of its huge production, storability, and renewability, it has been the focus of many scientists [1]. In addition, compared to clean energy sources such as wind and water, biomass energy has better stability. The huge carbon reduction potential of biomass, especially agricultural and forestry biomass, comes from the fact that the amount of CO2 produced during the growth process and the amount of CO2 absorbed during plant growth can be kept in balance [2]. This feature is particularly important in the context of today's urgent need to reduce pollution and carbon emissions. Biomass has good potential as a fuel due to its high calorific value and other advantages [3]. There are two main methods of utilizing it as a fuel, one is direct combustion and the other is mixed combustion [4]. Direct combustion technology is limited by inorganic impurities such as potassium chloride, which are relatively active in biomass, and will enter the gas phase and form deposits on the surface of the heating surface during the combustion process of biomass boilers, impeding heat transfer to the heating surface and causing high temperature corrosion of the heating surface metal, threatening the normal operation of combustion equipment [5]. In addition, compared to dedicated biomass boilers, mixing biomass in coal-fired power plants not only saves the economic cost of renovating the plant but also significantly improves the efficiency of biomass fuel utilization [6]. Its advantage is also reflected in the biomass mixed with coal can dilute the alkali metals in biomass fuel to a certain extent, effectively alleviating the problems of ash caking and corrosion that usually exist in direct combustion of biomass [7].

Previous studies on biomass-coal blending have focused on combustion characteristics, slagging characteristics, and emission characteristics of NOx, SOx, PM2.5, etc. SaHu [8] reviewed the advantages of biomass-coal co-firing in terms of combustion characteristics and pollutant emissions, and showed that co-firing of biomass and coal can improve the combustion characteristics of the fuel and significantly reduce the emissions of NOx, SOx, etc. Yang [9] investigated the correlation between combustion characteristics and NO emissions during the blending of biomass and coal using a combination of experimental and simulation methods. The interrelationship between the combustion characteristics and NO emissions of mixed fuels during the blending of pine wood chips and coal was investigated using a combination of experiments and simulations, and the results showed that NO emissions showed a significant decrease with the increase of biomass blending ratio, and the maximum decrease of NO emissions was up to 24 %, and this decrease occurred when the biomass blending ratio was 50 %.Wang [10] analyzed the NO emission characteristics of three biomasses (corn cob, corn stover, and wood chips) blended with coal, and the results showed that the blending of these three biomasses with coal significantly increased the combustion rate of coal and reduced the combustion time and NO emissions with the increase of biomass blending ratio. In addition, the elemental analysis of biomass and NO emission characteristics showed that the blending of biomass with high nitrogen content reduced NO emissions more significantly. Fei [11]conducted a blending experiment with algal biomass (Chlorella, Sargassum, etc.) and Hualing coal, and the experimental results showed that NO and SO2 emissions decreased significantly with the increase of Sargassum blending ratio, but the PM2.5 concentration showed an increasing trend. Rokni [12] simulated the emissions of NOx, SO2, and HCl after blending two types of biomass (corn straw and rice husk) with two types of coal (high-sulfur bituminous coal and low-sulfur sub-bituminous coal) at different mass ratios under experimental conditions, and the results of the study showed that the emissions of NOx and SO2 decreased significantly after blending biomass, while the emissions of HCl varied depending on the coal type, and the emissions of HCl from high-sulfur coal blended with biomass were emissions from high sulfur coal blending with biomass are much higher than those from low sulfur coal blending with biomass.

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are generally defined as organic compounds with a vapor pressure greater than or equal to 0.01 kPa at room temperature and a boiling point less than or equal to 250 °C at atmospheric pressure [13]. With the increasingly stringent environmental policies, the reduction of traditional pollutants has become more and more mature, so VOCs, a coal-fired pollutant that has received less attention before, has become increasingly valuable for research [14]. Coal-fired flue gas VOCs are characterized by complex types and low total mass concentrations (0–20 mg/m³) [15]. However, since coal combustion flue gas has huge emissions, the VOCs produced by it should not be underestimated. Pudasainee [16] found that the VOCs emitted during coal combustion in Korean power plants are mainly halogenated hydrocarbons and benzene, among which 1,2-dichloroethane, benzene, carbon tetrachloride, trichloromethane, and trichloroethylene are the characteristic VOCs components. Wang [17] studied the characteristics of PAHs emitted from different types of coal-fired boilers, and the results showed that the different combustion efficiencies of coal led to different PAHs emitted from boilers of different capacities and operating modes.

In addition to coal-fired boilers, the emission characteristics of VOCs from biomass combustion and biomass-forming boilers have also been studied. Paris [18] conducted combustion experiments on poplar wood chips with different moisture contents in a small thermal power plant and measured the VOCs emitted. Geng [19] conducted the combustion experiments of wood pellets and straw pellets on industrial biomass boilers and examined the VOCs emitted from the boilers, and the results showed that the VOCs emitted from biomass combustion were mainly C2–C4 alkanes, olefins, acetylene and aromatic hydrocarbons, and the VOCs emitted from biomass boilers were almost the same as those from coal-fired boilers. Shi [15] also conducted combustion experiments on two different wood biomass fuels on industrial biomass boilers, and the results of the study showed that the TVOCs emissions from two biomass combustion were 21.6 ± 2.8 mg/kg-286.2 ± 10.8 mg/kg, and oxygenated VOCs were the VOCs components accounting for the largest proportion of which formaldehyde and acetaldehyde were the most abundant.

In summary, the effect of biomass mixed with coal on traditional pollutant emissions has been studied more. There is also a certain research base about the emission characteristics of VOCs from the combustion of coal-fired and biomass boilers. However, there is no systematic study on the emission characteristics of VOCs from biomass and coal blending. In this paper, we selected formed peanut shell biomass and conducted biomass and coal blending experiments in circulating fluidized bed boilers. Analysis of experimental flue gas by GC-MS. Investigated the effect of biomass and coal blending on the emission characteristics of VOCs and the mechanisms that produce the emission characteristics. In order to control the emission of VOCs under the conditions of biomass and coal blending and provide reference for the development of relevant industry standards.

Experiments and methods

Equipment and raw materials

This experiment was conducted in a coal-fired boiler of a cogeneration company in Shenyang, Liaoning Province, China, for a field experimental study of biomass blending with coal. The boiler is a circulating fluidized bed boiler with a rated load of 58 MW and an actual load of 40 MW, a coal consumption of 15 t/h and a flue gas capacity of 60,000 m³/h.

In this blending experiment, the coal used was Class II bituminous coal; the blended biomass was mechanically processed peanut shells, which were cylindrical solids, 1 cm in diameter and 3–8 cm in length. Sample collection and analysis

Data of pollutants such as sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides for on-site detection by online flue gas analyzers of power plants. VOCs were detected using a post-sampling analysis method. The QC-1S atmospheric sampler (0.1–1.5 L/min) was utilized to collect the flue gas, and the samples were collected by Teflon FEP gas sampling bags. The sampling flow rate was 1 L/min, and the gas volume was 10 L. The sampling port was set at the exit of the boiler to sample the untreated boiler flue gas, and three parallel samples were collected at each sampling point. After sampling, the gas bag was transported and stored away from light, and the laboratory analysis was completed within 2 d. The gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) method was used.

The samples were analyzed by thermal desorption-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). The analyzing instrument was a QP2010GC-MS gas chromatograph-mass spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan), and the chromatographic column was a DB-5MS elastomeric quartz capillary column (60 m × 0.32 mm × 1 μm) with a high purity helium carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The temperature of the injection port was 190 °C. The temperature was maintained at 190 °C for 2 min at 40 °C, then increased to 180 °C at 10 °C/min, and then increased to 220 °C at 40 °C/min and maintained for 4 min. An electron bombardment ionization source was used with an ionization voltage of 70 eV. The qualitative and quantitative results were obtained in the selective ionization mode, with the retention time qualitatively determined by the internal standard method (4-bromofluorobenzene, bromochloromethane, chlorobenzene-d5, and 1,4-difluorobenzene).

The total number of VOCs to be detected in this experiment is 64, including 2 aliphatic hydrocarbons (isobutylene, n-hexane), 14 benzenes (benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, m/p-xylene, o-xylene, styrene, isopropylbenzene, propylbenzene, tert-butylbenzene, 1,2,4-trimethylbenzene, 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene, butylbenzene, p-isopropylbenzene, sec-butylbenzene), 24 halogenated aliphatic hydrocarbons (vinyl chloride, 1,1-dichloroethylene, methylene chloride, 1,1-dichloroethane, trichloromethane, 1,1-dichloropropene, carbon tetrachloride, 1,2-dichloroethane, trichloroethylene, 1,2-dichloropropane, Bromodichloromethane, cis-1,3-dichloropropene, trans-1,3-dichloropropene, 1,1,2-trichloroethane, 1,3-dichloropropane, tetrachloroethylene, dibromochloromethane, 1,2-dibromoethane, 1,1,1,2-tetrachloroethane, 1,2,3-trichloropropane, 1,2-dibromo-3-chloropropane, hexachlorobutadiene, chloromethane, bromomethane), 9 halogenated benzenes (chlorobenzene, 2-chlorotoluene, 4-chlorotoluene, 1,4-dichlorobenzene, 1,3-dichlorobenzene, 1,2-dichlorobenzene, 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene, 1,2,3-trichlorobenzene, bromobenzene), naphthalene and 14 oxygenated VOCs (ethanol, acetone, 2-methacrolein, acetic acid, methyl formate, ethyl acetate, butyl acetate, ethyl ether, acetonitrile, methyl acetate, propionaldehyde, benzaldehyde, 1,4-dioxane, dimethyl-1,4-dioxane).

Quality assurance and quality control

Each sampling gas bag is cleaned with high purity nitrogen for more than 5 times before sampling, and kept in vacuum. When testing the samples, one full program blank and one blank spiked sample were made for every 7 samples. The recoveries of the spiked samples ranged from 96 % to 110 %, and the relative deviations of the spiked recoveries were ≤20 %.

Temperature is a key factor affecting the emission of VOCs. 300°C–600 °C is the temperature at which VOCs are released, of which 80 % of VOCs are released above 400 °C [20]. Past studies have shown that the addition of biomass not only reduces the ignition and burn-up temperatures of the fuel mix [21]. In addition, the addition of biomass can also improve the efficiency of the fuel mix [6].

It can be seen that when the biomass blending ratio is 0 % and 20 %, the total mass of VOCs does not change significantly, and the VOCs emissions are higher for both 0 % and 20 % blending compared to 30 % and 40 % blending. The reason for this is that the lower biomass percentage may preempt the combustion and affect the combustion temperature of the blended fuel, which is why there is no significant change in VOCs emissions when the blending ratio is 0 % and 20 %.

The emission of VOCs was greatly reduced when the proportion of biomass blending reached 30 %. This change results from the improved combustion characteristics of the blended fuel due to the addition of biomass, and the reduction in ignition and combustion temperatures reduces the emissions of VOCs.

When the blending ratio was increased to 40 %, the concentration of VOCs was only slightly reduced compared to 30 %, taking into account the fluctuation of operating conditions in the boilers of the power plant, which led to a large error in one of the samples. This characteristic indicates that the combustion characteristics and burnout temperature have been greatly improved and stabilized at 30 %, and the continued addition of biomass can still reduce the emission of VOCs, but the change is not obvious.

NOx emission characteristics, as shown in Fig. 2 with the increase of the blending ratio NOx emissions show a trend of first increase and then decrease.

NOx emissions with different biomass blending ratios.

The SO2 emission decreases significantly with the increase of the blending ratio, as shown in Fig. 3. Previous studies [22]have shown that the main source of SO2 production in coal-fired boilers is the sulfur and organic sulfur components in coal. In contrast, elemental sulfur was not detected in the blended biomass, which is the main reason for the decrease in SO2 emissions with the increase in the blending ratio.

SO2 emissions with different biomass blending ratios.

In summary, biomass blending reduces VOCs emissions, probably as a result of the change in combustion temperature caused by mixing biomass with coal. 40 % biomass blending ratio reduces VOCs more, and as the percentage of biomass continues to increase, too much high volatile matter biomass can also lead to corrosion problems in the boiler.

There is no significant reduction in NOx and VOCs at 30 % and 40 % blending ratios, as well as issues such as losses and economics of power plant boilers, and 30 % is a superior blending ratio for actual power plant operation.

Biomass blending VOCs source composition profile

Benzene is generally regarded as the most common VOCs in coal-fired boilers [26]. Benzene is still the dominant VOCs in CFB boilers under biomass blending conditions. The mass fractions of different components of VOCs are shown in Fig. 5.

Mass score map of different types of VOCs.

As can be seen from the figure,40 % has more types of VOCs than the 20 % blending ratio. The alcohols (isopropyl alcohol) and ethers (anisole) are the specific VOCs at the 20 % blending ratio, with mass fractions of 0.60 % and 0.34 %, respectively. Among all VOCs at 20 % blending ratio, benzene was the most emitted with 44.38 %, followed by alkanes (33.62 %), esters (9.96 %), ketones (7.65 %), olefins (2.68 %), and aldehydes (0.77 %), respectively. At 40 % blending ratio, benzene remained the most emitted component with 33.75 %, followed by esters (22.75 %), alkanes (16.16 %), ketones (13.88 %), olefins (10.55 %), and aldehydes (2.91 %), respectively.

Hu [27] showed that the production of n-alkanes was mainly related to the process and fuel particle size, and the carbon atom chains were more easily cyclized and dehydrogenated to form cyclic organic compounds in the reaction chamber in an airless environment. The mass fraction of hydrocarbons decreased as the blending ratio increased, with a larger decrease in alkanes. The alkanes emitted at the two different blending ratios were all n-alkanes, and one of the reasons for the decrease in the mass fraction of alkane (mainly n-hexane) components may be the result of the change in fuel particle size due to the increase in the proportion of biomass. The results of Bi [28] suggest that alkane compounds with lower boiling points during coal combustion are more likely to be emitted with the flue gas. The boiling points of the two alkanes are similar to the exhaust temperatures, which may be one of the reasons for the emission of alkane VOCs. The most emitted category is benzene for both different biomass blending ratios. It has been shown [29] that acetylene is a characteristic marker of biomass combustion, but it was not detected in the flue gas of this field test. Wu [24] conducted flue gas analysis of biomass-forming fuel boilers, and the results showed that olefins were the largest component of VOCs, with a percentage of 41.25 %–59.57 %. Both different types of boilers have olefins as the VOCs component with the largest proportion. However, the proportion of olefins was low under different proportions of biomass blending conditions, only 2.70 %–11.02 %, which is significantly different from the emission characteristics of the first two boilers. BTEX characteristic ratio analysis

BTEX stands for benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene, respectively, which are the most common volatile organic compounds in the atmosphere [30]. The relative ratio of B:T:E:X is often used to distinguish between different emission sources [31]、 [32]、 [33]、 [34]、 [35]、 [36] and to indicate the degree of aging of VOCs [37]. The relative ratio of BTEX at 20 % blending ratio was 21.98:8.88:1:5.27 and the relative ratio of BTEX at 40 % blending ratio was 11.04:1.55:1:2.36. The benzene/toluene ratio (B/T) is widely used as an indicator to identify various sources, as there are significant differences in B/T ratios for different emission sources [38]、 [39]. It can be seen that at 20 % blending ratio, benzene is the species that contributes the most to the ozone generation potential with 69.88 %. Next in order are alkanes (25.76 %), ketones (2.78 %), olefins (1.33 %), and alcohols (0.25 %).

At 40 % blending ratio, the OFP characteristics were similar to those at 20 %, with aromatic compounds (70.24 %), alkanes (17.75 %), olefins (6.56 %), and ketones (5.45 %) in order of contribution.

By comparing the mass fractions of the different VOCs components in the OFP percentage diagram, it can be seen that the amount of ozone generation potential is mainly influenced by the mass fraction of VOCs components and the maximum incremental reactivity factor. Aromatic compounds are the species that contribute the most to the ozone generation potential under biomass blending conditions because of their higher concentration and high reactivity, unlike the study on the contribution of biomass-formed fuels to OFP [24] (olefins contribute the most). However, there are also some species whose lower reactivity leads them to have higher concentrations but not as high contribution to the ODP, such as ketones.

Conclusion

①Biomass blending reduces the emissions of VOCs by affecting the combustion characteristics of the blended fuel, and the significant decrease in VOCs emissions occurs during the change from 20 % to 30 % blending ratio. The total VOCs emission concentration decreases by 84.79 % at 40 % blending ratio compared to no biomass blending; and the NOx and SO2 emissions continue to decrease with increasing biomass blending ratio due to the fact that biomass fuel has less N and S elements compared to coal.
