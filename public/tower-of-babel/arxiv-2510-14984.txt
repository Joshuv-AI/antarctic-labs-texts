The mechanics of Less In More Out : modeling fabric-based soft robotic hearts Journal: Journal of the Mechanics and Physics of Solids
Marin Lauber, Mathias Peirlinck

Abstract
Fabric-based soft robots combine high load-carrying capacity, efficiency, and low weight with the ability to bend, twist, contract, or extend with ease, making them promising candidates for biomedical applications such as soft total artificial hearts. While recent experiments have demonstrated their potential, predictive numerical models are urgently needed to study their complex mechanics, guide design optimization and improve their reliability. We develop a computational model of the Less In More Out device, a fluidically actuated soft total artificial heart constructed from heat-sealed layers of woven fabric. Our model reproduces the nonlinear deformation, strain fields, and pressure–volume relationships measured in quasi-static experiments. Devices with fewer pouches deliver higher stroke volumes but exhibit up to 50% higher peak von Mises stresses. Fatigue analysis using a strain–life approach identifies heat-sealed seams and buckling regions as durability-limiting features. Our framework enables detailed evaluation of stress concentrations, buckling, and fatigue life, providing mechanistic insights that are difficult to obtain experimentally. It also offers a foundation for the optimization of artificial hearts and other fluid actuated fabric-based soft robotic systems.



1
Introduction

Soft robotics is emerging as a compelling alternative to rigid robotics in many areas of science and engineering.
By replacing rigid parts with soft components made from elastomers, silicone, or fabrics, soft robots can outperform conventional devices in adaptability and compliance while relying on simple actuation mechanisms
[
1
,
2
]
.
Among these, fabric-based devices have gained attention for their compliance, low weight, durability, and biocompatibility
[
3
]
.
Fabrics also offer superior force transmission and can be tailored for anisotropic responses, enabling actuators and artificial muscles capable of contraction, extension, bending, and twisting
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

These attributes make fabric-based soft robots promising for biomedical applications
[
10
]
.
In particular, combining soft robotics with the development of total artificial hearts offers the potential to mitigate complications associated with rigid mechanical designs and actuation methods
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
.
Soft artificial hearts can reduce the risk for thrombogenesis, generate physiological pulsatile flows using fluidic actuation, and be formed into organic geometries through straightforward manufacturing methods.
Examples include devices with embedded McKibben muscles to reproduce natural ventricular contraction and twisting
[
13
,
17
]
, and recent designs relying entirely on fabric-based ventricles and pouch-based actuators that can operate under physiological loading in mock circulatory loops
[
15
,
16
]
.

Despite these advances, there is currently no numerical framework for modelling soft total artificial hearts made of woven fabric and actuated purely by fluidic pressure.
Such a framework is needed to study device mechanics, identify critical stress locations, and optimize performance, tasks that are difficult to achieve through experiments alone.

The soft nature of these devices leads to large, nonlinear deformations and often anisotropic responses, which are challenging to measure experimentally.
Computational models offer an attractive complement, enabling detailed analysis of stress, strain, and fatigue that would otherwise be inaccessible
[
18
]
.
Previous work has combined prototyping and numerical simulation to optimize soft robotic devices
[
13
,
5
,
19
,
17
]
.
Additionally, for artificial hearts, patient-specific loading conditions can be incorporated through coupling with calibrated lumped parameter circulation models
[
20
,
21
,
17
]
.
However, modeling woven fabrics introduces additional complexity.
Their mechanical response depends on fiber properties, yarn density, and weave pattern, and while detailed models exist at filament-level (microscopic), yarn-level (mesoscopic), and fabric-level (macroscopic)
[
22
,
23
]
, their fabric-specific calibration requires multi-directional tensile testing data that is rarely available.
As a result, many studies on fabric-based soft robots assume linear isotropic behavior
[
6
,
7
]
.

Durability is another critical challenge.
Soft artificial hearts operate under millions of loading cycles, and the fatigue resistance of their constituent materials is often low.
As a result, device optimization to limit stress concentration is critical
[
12
,
15
]
.
Fatigue can be addressed through advanced experimental techniques, such as Digital Image Correlation
[
24
]
, to measure strain fields, or through purely numerical methods.
Numerical fatigue analysis can be conducted through continuum damage mechanics or through decoupled approaches that use separate constitutive and damage models
[
25
]
.
While fatigue prediction for metals is mature, woven fabrics present unique difficulties due to the influence of weave type and fiber level properties
[
26
,
27
]
, and the lack of experimental data hampers calibration of predictive models.

In this work, we present the numerical modeling of a soft total artificial heart made of woven fabric and actuated by pressurized pouches, replicating the device recently introduced and experimentally characterized in
[
15
]
.
Our first aim is to validate the model against these experimental measurements to establish a reliable framework for further studies.
Our second aim is to use the model to investigate intrinsic mechanical responses, including stress concentration and fatigue risk, to inform device optimization.
We begin by describing the device geometry, material properties, and numerical implementation (Section
2
), followed by a comparison of simulated and experimental pressure–volume relationships during quasi-static inflation (Section
3
).
We then use the full field simulation results to examine local stress, buckling behavior, and fatigue life.
Finally, in Section
4
we discuss the implications of these findings, outline limitations, and suggest directions for future work.

2
Methods

The original device exhibits several recurring design features common to all fabric-based soft artificial hearts and relies on a simple and efficient fluidic actuation mechanism, rendering it an ideal inspiration for our numerical model.
After building our numerical model based on an idealized version of the LIMO heart, we use the accompanying experimental data for validation.

2.1
Geometry

Real device
.
The physical device is an airtight fabric ventricle made of thermoplastic polyurethane-coated nylon fabric, approximately 0.2 mm thick (Riverseal 70 LW, 78 Dtex; 170 g/m²; Rivertex, Culemborg, The Netherlands).
It is about 140 mm in length and holds 300 ml when fully inflated.
Flat pouches on its surface inflate into roughly cylindrical shapes (Figure
1
).
These pouches are assembled from two layers of heat-sealed fabric, then folded along one edge and sealed onto themselves to close the ventricle.
This process produces an odd number of pouches, with one pouch located along the fold line.
The pouches are inflated by compressed air, reducing the internal volume of the ventricle and ejecting fluid, mimicking the natural contraction of a healthy human heart.
The ventricle attaches to a rigid valve support providing two valves for single ventricle function.
Two such ventricles combined form a complete bi-ventricular soft artificial heart.
During the experimental characterization
[
15
]
, a water column is connected to the device to provide a fixed afterload pressure
P_{\text{aft}}
.
The device is actuated through a pressure-controlled valve, and the pouches are inflated.

Numerical idealization
.
Our computational model uses a simplified, symmetric geometry with an even number of pouches on the front and back surfaces.
This differs from the physical device, which has an odd number of pouches, because our geometry parameterization is based on Boolean operations on curves and surfaces, which cannot easily represent the asymmetry of the fold-line pouch arrangement.
Pouch connections are omitted, and seams run continuously along pouch length rather than being open as in the experiments, allowing the actuation pressure to spread evenly.
We also omit the small pressure actuation inlet on the pouch itself.
The valve support is modeled without valves, since they are not required for quasi-static loading.

2.2
Kinematics

The geometry of the device undergoes two major transformations:
first, from the flat reference geometry to the initial configuration fitted onto the rigid valve support, and second, from this initial state to the inflated configuration during loading.
The mapping to the initial configuration accounts for geometric changes and pre-strain introduced during attachment to the valve support, ensuring that simulations start from a physically realistic state (Figure
1
).

Mapping definitions
.
The reference configuration
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
corresponds to the flat state of the device before fitting to the valve support.
The initial configuration
\Omega
is obtained through the mapping
\varphi_{0}
,

\varphi_{0}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0},t):\Omega_{0}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad\text{such that}\quad\boldsymbol{X}=\varphi_{0}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0},t=0),

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{X}_{0}
is a point in
\Omega_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{X}
is the corresponding point in
\Omega
.

The current (inflated) configuration
\Omega_{t}
is obtained from
\Omega
via
\varphi
:

\varphi(\boldsymbol{X},t):\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad\text{such that}\quad\boldsymbol{x}=\varphi(\boldsymbol{X},t),

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{x}
is a position in
\Omega_{t}
.
Mapping directly from
\Omega_{0}
to
\Omega_{t}
is given by
\hat{\varphi}
,

\hat{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0},t):\Omega_{0}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\quad\text{such that}\quad\boldsymbol{x}=\hat{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0},t).

(3)

Deformation gradient.

The total deformation from
\Omega_{0}
to
\Omega_{t}
is described by
\hat{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0},t)
.
The associated total deformation gradient is

\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0},t)=\frac{\partial\hat{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0},t)}{\partial\boldsymbol{X}_{0}},

(4)

which characterizes local stretches and rotations between the reference and current configurations.
This total deformation gradient can be multiplicatively decomposed into contributions from the fitting step (mapping
\varphi_{0}
) and the inflation step (mapping
\varphi
):

\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{F}_{\varphi}\,\mathbf{F}_{\varphi_{0}},\quad\mathbf{F}_{\varphi}=\frac{\partial\varphi(\boldsymbol{X},t)}{\partial\boldsymbol{X}},\quad\mathbf{F}_{\varphi_{0}}=\frac{\partial\varphi_{0}(\boldsymbol{X}_{0})}{\partial\boldsymbol{X}_{0}}.

(5)

Here
\mathbf{F}_{\varphi_{0}}
represents the pre-stretch induced during fitting of the membrane to the valve support, while
\mathbf{F}_{\varphi}
describes the subsequent deformation due to inflation.

Geometric parametrization.

The computational domain
\Omega_{0}
is divided into the endocardium
\Omega_{\text{endo},0}
, the epicardium
\Omega_{\text{epi},0}
, and the pouches
\Omega_{\text{pouch},0}
.
The edges of these subdomains are denoted as
\Gamma_{\text{valve},0}
for the valve boundary,
\Gamma_{\text{pouch},0}
for the pouch seams, and
\Gamma_{\text{outer},0}
for the outer edge.

The geometry is parameterized by its outer edge curves
\{\Gamma_{\text{outer},0},\,\Gamma_{\text{valve},0}\}
and the seam lines of the pouches
\Gamma_{\text{pouch},0}
(Figure
1
).
The different surfaces of the model are obtained by fitting planar patches through these curves.

Computation of the initial configuration.

During the construction process, the flat stack of fabric is fitted to a (rigid) valve support with an ellipsoidal cross-section.
The initial map
\varphi_{0}
from the flat state of the membrane
\Omega_{0}
onto this 3D geometry (to
\Omega
in Figure
1
) induces pre-stretch in the membrane and initializes some of the folds present on the device; this initial stretch must be accounted for.
We start all the simulations presented in the manuscript by solving this initial configuration.

To compute this configuration, we treat the flat reference geometry
\Omega_{0}
as the starting point and apply displacement constraints to match the reference outer edge
\Gamma_{\text{valve},0}
to the initial valve support
\Gamma_{\text{valve}}
.
The valve support has an aspect ratio
\Lambda=2
and a semi-major axis
a/L\sim 0.3275
, where
L
is the device length, so points on
\Gamma_{\text{valve}}
in the current configuration satisfy

g_{\text{valve}}(\boldsymbol{u})=\begin{cases}u,w\quad\big|(X+u)^{2}+\frac{1}{\Lambda^{2}}(Z+w)^{2}=a^{2},\\
v=0\end{cases}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma_{\text{valve}},

(6)

where
(X,Y,Z)
are coordinates on
\Gamma_{\text{valve},0}
in the reference configuration and
(u,v,w)
are the component of the displacement on
\Gamma_{\text{valve}}
in the current configuration.

To avoid introducing artificial stretch along
\Gamma_{\text{valve}}
, we impose the additional constraint

\Delta l_{0}=\Delta l=\oint_{0}^{l}C(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\quad\forall\,l\in(0,\pi],

(7)

where
C(\phi)
parameterizes
\Gamma_{\text{valve}}
, and
\Delta l_{0}
and
\Delta l
denote the segment lengths in the reference and current configurations.

This approach ensures the fixed valve edge is stretch-free while allowing the rest of the membrane to deform to fit the support, inducing realistic pre-stretch in the pouches and walls.
The constraints are applied gradually using a linear ramp in a quasi-static solve.
Figure
1
shows the device before (left) and after (center) the computation of this initial configuration.

2.3
Governing equations

The large-deformation behavior of the soft fabric ventricle is governed by the conservation of linear momentum

\rho\frac{\text{D}\vec{u}}{\text{D}t}-\nabla\cdot{\vec{\sigma}}-{\vec{f}}=0\quad\forall\vec{x}\,\in\Omega\times(0,T],

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\equiv u\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}+v\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}+w\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
and
\vec{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the displacement vector, Cauchy stress tensor, and external force vector, respectively.
The Cartesian basis vectors are denoted
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\hat{\boldsymbol{y}},\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
.
Equation (
8
) states that, at each material point, the inertial forces, internal stresses, and external forces must be in equilibrium.

The second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor,
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
, can be obtained from a pull-back operation on the Cauchy stress tensor,

\boldsymbol{S}=J\boldsymbol{F}^{-1}{\vec{\sigma}}\boldsymbol{F}^{-\top}

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is the total deformation gradient tensor (Eq.
4
) and
J\equiv\det\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}
.
To ensure thermodynamic consistency, we introduce the Helmholtz free energy as a function of the deformation gradient
\psi(\boldsymbol{F})
. Assuming no dissipative energy losses within the material, and rewriting the Clausius-Duhem entropy inequality following the Coleman and Noll principle
[
28
]
, we derive the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor

\boldsymbol{S}=\frac{\partial\psi(\boldsymbol{F})}{\partial\boldsymbol{E}}

(10)

with the Green-Lagrange strain tensor
\boldsymbol{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{C}-\mathbb{I}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
and the Cauchy-Green deformation tensor
\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
.

In this work, we assume quasi-static loading conditions, with Eq.
8
subject to the initial conditions

{\vec{u}}({\vec{x}},0)={\vec{0}},\quad\dot{\vec{u}}({\vec{x}},0)={\vec{0}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega

(11)

and the Dirichlet initial boundary conditions on the valve support

{\vec{u}}({\vec{x}},t)=g_{\text{valve}}({\vec{u}})\quad\forall\vec{x}\in\Gamma_{\text{valve}}.

(12)

described in Eqs.
6
and
7
.
Since we are only interested in the static response of the device, we drop the transient term from Eq.
8
, and the internal fluid loading from the water column onto the endocardium is approximated by a Neumann condition on the pressure

{\vec{F}}{\vec{S}}\hat{\vec{n}}=-P_{v}(t)J{\vec{F}}^{-\top}\hat{\vec{n}}\quad\forall\vec{x}\in\Omega_{\text{endo}}\times(0,T],

(13)

where
P_{v}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
is the (ventricular) pressure and
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the unit normal vector.
We assume that the ventricular pressure arising from the water column (
P_{v}
) is constant throughout the depth of the ventricle; in reality, a (small) hydrostatic pressure difference is present between the top and bottom of the ventricle.

The epicardium
\Omega_{\text{epi}}
is left traction-free in the present static inflation simulations.

2.4
Constitutive modeling

Fabric-specific constitutive material models require multi-modal tensile testing data
[
29
,
30
]
for the thermoplastic polyurethane-coated nylon fabric used in the LIMO heart, which are not readily available in the literature.
Given this material’s flexible, but mostly non-compliant nature, we therefore model the fabric as a St. Venant–Kirchhoff material undergoing large geometric displacements but small local deformations.
For this St. Venant–Kirchhoff material model, the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is given by

\boldsymbol{S}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\boldsymbol{E}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{E}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}[\text{ tr}(\boldsymbol{E})]^{2}+\mu\text{ tr}(\boldsymbol{E}^{2})\right)=\lambda\text{ tr}(\boldsymbol{E})+2\mu\boldsymbol{E},

(14)

where
\lambda
and
\mu
are
Lamé
constants that take the value of
0.746
GPa and
0.751
GPa, respectively. These correspond to the classical Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio of
E=1
GPa and
\nu=0.33
.

2.5
Numerical solver

We use the open-source finite element software
CalculiX

[
31
]
to solve the governing equations of the problem.
We discretize the geometry with quadratic continuum shell elements (hexahedral), resulting in meshes of approximately
10^{4}
elements, depending on the specific geometry, and about
2.5\times 10^{5}
degrees of freedom.

A.1
and
A.2
demonstrate that this element type accurately captures complex nonlinear deformation and buckling in membrane and shell structures under internal pressure.
The mesh resolution yields discretization errors of about 2% for the present geometry, which we consider sufficiently resolved for the analyses in this work.
True membranes have a negligible bending stiffness, leading to an asymptotically infinite number of wrinkles and folds
[
32
]
.
Resolving these fine wrinkles would require prohibitively large meshes.
To address this, we approximate the quasi-inextensible fabric as a thin, stiff shell that retains a finite bending stiffness.
This suppresses wrinkles with characteristic lengths smaller than the element size while preserving the global deformation characteristics of the structure.

To assess fatigue risk, we use a simple approach based on E–N (strain–life) curves.
Assuming the weakest points of the device are the TPU–TPU bonds in the heat-sealed seams, we focus on strain–life curves for the TPU material itself.
Complete strain–life curves are commonly modeled with the

\varepsilon_{a,t}

\varepsilon_{\text{a,t}}=\varepsilon_{\text{a,e}}+\varepsilon_{\text{a,p}}=\frac{\sigma_{\text{f}}^{\prime}}{E}(2N_{\text{f}})^{b}+\varepsilon_{\text{f}}^{\prime}(2N_{\text{f}})^{c},

(17)

where the Young’s modulus

\sigma_{\text{f}}^{\prime}
,
\varepsilon_{\text{f}}^{\prime}
,
b
, and
c
are the fatigue strength coefficient, fatigue ductility coefficient, fatigue strength exponent, and fatigue ductility exponent, respectively.
Since these coefficients require experimental measurements out of the scope of this paper, we use those from
[
34
]
for the elastic strain part of the curve
\varepsilon_{a,e}
.

\varepsilon_{a,p}<\varepsilon_{a,e}
for

Since we do not expect the device to operate in the plastic regime under the assumed cyclic loading, only calibrating the elastic part of the curve and using the previous argument for the plastic part is justified.
We use the maximal principal strain in the quasi-static inflation as the strain amplitude measure.
Given that the device is not expected to operate in the plastic regime under the considered cyclic loading, this calibration is sufficient for a first-order fatigue screening.

3.5
Principal strain fields

Anisotropy can significantly influence the mechanical response of soft robotic structures
[
6
,
35
]
, and can also be exploited to tailor materials for specific load paths
[
36
]
.
To characterize the directional loading in our device, we compute the principal strains and their associated directions in the fabric.

The principal strains are obtained as the eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{i}
of the Green–Lagrange strain tensor
\boldsymbol{E}
:

\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\,,\quad\rho(\varepsilon_{i})=\det(\boldsymbol{E}-\varepsilon_{i}\mathbb{I})=0\quad(i=1,2,3;\,\,\text{no summation}),

(18)

with
\varepsilon_{1}
,
\varepsilon_{2}
, and
\varepsilon_{3}
corresponding to the maximum, intermediate, and minimum principal strain, respectively. For each
\varepsilon_{i}
, we solve

(\boldsymbol{E}-\varepsilon_{i}\mathbb{I})\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=\boldsymbol{0}\qquad(i=1,2,3;\,\,\text{no summation}),

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
is the corresponding eigenvector (principal direction).

Figure
8
visualizes the maximum principal strain, i.e. tension, direction
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
on the outer epicardial and inner endocardial surfaces of the device.
These surfaces coincide everywhere except on the pouch walls, which have distinct inner and outer faces.
On the outer surface, the maximum principal strain is oriented predominantly circumferentially, consistent with resisting global expansion (ballooning) of the pouches and device.
On the inner pouch surface, the maximum principal strain is rotated approximately
90^{\circ}
relative to the outer surface, oriented to pull the lower surface inward and upward during inflation.
At the seam lines, where the two surfaces merge, the principal strain field aligns across the thickness. This location also coincides with the peak von Mises stress and the persistent tension lines identified in Section
3.4
. This correlation further supports the role of these seam regions as critical load bearing paths and potential fatigue initiation sites.

4
Discussion

Our numerical model captures the states of nonlinear deformation of the real soft robotic device.

We investigate the effect of different geometries, through varying numbers of pouches, on the pressure–volume relationship of the device under different ventricular pressures.
To this end, we perform static inflation tests and compare our numerical model to the experimental characterization of the
Less In More Out
(LIMO) soft robotic artificial heart
[
15
]
.
We find that a low pouch number results in a higher ejected volume for an identical actuation pressure. Importantly, all designs achieve an output/imput ratio greater than one, regardless of ventricular pressure, consistent with the experiments.
Our model accurately predicts the onset of inflation, where ventricular volume drops abruptly once the actuation pressure reaches the ventricular pressure, followed by a reconfiguration into the inflated shape (Figure
2
).
During this reconfiguration phase, the
P
–
V
relationship closely matches that of the physical device.
Differences emerge at higher actuation pressures, where the physical device maintains nearly constant volume while the simulation predicts further changes.
These discrepancies can be attributed to simplifying assumptions, including geometric symmetry (front and back) and the use of a slightly compliant St. Venant Kirchhoff material law. The need for these assumptions highlights the need for precise characterization of the multi-axial tensile response of these fabrics. Combining such targeted tensile testing with innovative material model discovery approaches
[
30
,
29
,
37
]
would enable more faithful predictions of device behavior under extreme loading.

Our model captures the afterload sensitivity of the soft artificial heart.

The native human heart operates under highly variable hemodynamic loading conditions, adapting to afterload changes while preserving its much needed stroke volume pumping efficiency
[
38
]
.
Our model reproduces this sensitivity: as afterload increases, the
P
–
V
relationship changes.
Similar to the zero-afterload case, our model captures the onset of inflation generation to the reconfiguration of the device once the actuation pressure overcomes the ventricular pressure.
Mechanical efficiency increases with increased afterload, in agreement with the experimental LIMO observations
[
15
]
.
These results demonstrate that our modeling framework can identify optimal design parameters for performance under different loads.
At the same time, the use of quasi-static loading neglects fluid inertia effects, which in reality could dampen rapid buckling events and slightly alter efficiency estimates.
Further studies will combine this model with a lumped parameter model for the cardiovascular circulation and a 3D unsteady fluid dynamics model, using, for example, an immersed-boundary method
[
39
]
, to solve the fully coupled fluid-structure interaction problem
[
40
]
, providing more accurate estimates of the loads occurring during a cardiac cycle and enabling flow diagnostic (recirculation, blood stagnation region, hemolysis and vortex identification) to be included into the optimization of the device
[
13
,
41
,
42
]
.

Our model provides detailed insights into local stress concentrations and strain directionality.

One of the main challenges for soft robotic devices is durability.
While classical engineering materials such as metals have well-characterized fatigue properties, the fatigue of soft fabrics is less understood and depends on multiple environmental factors, such as temperature and humidity
[
43
]
.
We use the von Mises criterion to identify stress concentration zones, finding peaks at seam lines and in regions of device edge buckling during reconfiguration (Figure
6
).
The direction of maximum principal strain (
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
) shows an intriguing pattern: outer pouch surfaces stretch mostly circumferentially to resist ballooning, while inner surfaces stretch axially, approximately 90 degrees out of phase from each other.
This difference is reminiscent of the native left ventricle’s torsional twist during systole, where epicardial and endocardial fibers contract in different orientations to produce an upward and rotational pumping motion
[
44
,
45
,
17
]
.
This finding suggests that certain pouch design aspects, including a.o. local anisotropic reinforcements or elaborate optimization of woven fabric fiber directions during device construction, may inherently mimic beneficial aspects of natural cardiac mechanics — an opportunity for future optimization.
Indeed, while the present model treats the fabric as stiff isotropic material relevant for TPU, real woven fabrics exhibit anisotropy that should be exploited to further tailor deformation patterns.

Our model can be used to predict fatigue life and identify critical regions.

We implement a simplified, decoupled fatigue life estimate using a Coffin–Manson–Basquin strain–life relationship calibrated to TPU data from the literature
[
34
]
.
The predicted high-risk regions align with seam lines and buckling zones, again consistent with prior experimental observations
[
15
]
.
Although our fatigue estimates are preliminary, relying on quasi-static principal strain amplitudes and pure tensile testing TPU data rather than bonded (woven) fabric tests, the workflow demonstrates how simulation-driven stress maps can guide reinforcement strategies, material optimization, or geometry modifications.
These fatigue predictions will become more robust once targeted experimental strain–life data for bonded TPU fabric seams becomes available.

Our framework advances the design of fabric-based, fluid-actuated soft robotic devices

By capturing global inflation behavior, afterload sensitivity, local stress concentration, strain directionality, and preliminary fatigue risk, our model provides a multi-scale view of intrinsic device mechanics.
While developed for a soft total artificial heart, the computational framework is applicable to a broad class of fabric-based pneumatic actuators, including assistive wearables
[
5
]
, high-performance pouch-based actuators
[
46
]
, and other soft robotics systems
[
47
]
.
This integrated approach enables cost-efficient targeted geometry optimization, informed placement of anisotropic reinforcements, and design strategies that exploit strain patterns to mimic native cardiac mechanics.
Future coupling of this solid mechanics model with fluid–structure interaction and cardiovascular circulation models will open the door to predictive simulations that optimize performance not only for pumping efficiency but also for hemodynamic safety, long-term durability, and patient-specific design optimization.

5
Conclusion

We have developed and validated a computational framework for the mechanical analysis of a fabric-based, fluidic actuated soft robotic, total artificial heart.
Our model reproduces key experimental observations, including global inflation behavior, afterload sensitivity, and provides quantitative predictions of strain field directionality, local stress concentrations, and preliminary fatigue risk.
By linking macroscopic performance to local mechanical states, the framework offers a physics-based route to optimize geometry, material choice, and reinforcement placement.
While demonstrated for a soft total artificial heart, the methodology is directly transferable to a broad class of fabric-based soft robotic actuators.
We believe that the developed framework will be instrumental in advancing the design of next-generation soft robotic systems for biomedical, assistive, and industrial applications, enabling designs that are not only efficient and durable but also tailored to the specific demands of their operating environment.
