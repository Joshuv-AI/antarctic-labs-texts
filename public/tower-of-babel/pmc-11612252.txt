Salinomycin induces apoptosis and potentiates the antitumor effect of doxorubicin against feline mammary tumor 2.5D organoids
Authors: Yishan LIU, Mohamed ELBADAWY, Haru YAMAMOTO, Amira AUGOMAA, Yusuke ISHIHARA, Masahiro KANEDA, Tatsuya USUI, Kazuaki SASAKI
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFeline mammary tumors (FMT) are the third most common form of neoplasm in cats. The prognosis of FMT is poor due to its high malignancy and metastatic potential. The outcomes of treatment using the common anticancer drug doxorubicin (DOX) are unsatisfactory, with resistance inevitably leading to treatment failure and disease recurrence. Salinomycin (SAL), an antibiotic, has been reported to exert anticancer effects on both human and canine mammary tumors. To recapitulate the genetic and molecular imprints of the original tumor sample, we generated four strains of patient-derived FMT 2.5D organoids (FMTO) to examine the anti-tumor potential of SAL. Our results revealed that SAL decreased cell viability in a dose-dependent manner. Treatment of FMTO with SAL-induced cell apoptosis, represented by an upregulation of P21, Caspase-8, and Caspase-9, and increased activity of Caspase-3/7. The combination of low-dose SAL with DOX (SD) potentiated the cytotoxicity of the latter in both DOX-resistant and DOX-sensitive strains, promoting cell apoptosis and cell-cycle arrest. In vivo, experiments using FMTO-derived xenografts engrafted into mice revealed decreased tumor growth following SAL administration. In conclusion, SAL showed anticancer activity against FMTO and potentiated the anticancer effect of DOX by inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest. These results suggest that SAL may represent a new adjuvant treatment option for patients with FMT.Keywords: cell apoptosis, feline mammary tumor, organoids, pet adjuvant chemotherapy, Salinomycin

Feline mammary tumors (FMT) are the third most common form of malignancy in cats, accounting for approximately 17% of all feline neoplasms [14], with a high metastasis rate of 50% to 90%, predominantly affecting the regional lymph nodes (83%), lungs (83%), liver (25%), and pleura (22%) [13]. As a critical disease in veterinary clinical practice, the average survival time after the common cancer therapy of removing malignant mammary glands is only 8–12 months [5]. Reoccurrences commonly develop in regional lymph nodes and internal organs. Adjuvant treatment is therefore necessary to improve the quality of life of cats and extend survival after surgery. In clinical practice, doxorubicin (DOX) is commonly applied as a single agent or combined with other chemotherapeutic drugs. However, several studies have reported that adjuvant chemotherapy treatments do not improve patient survival [29, 31], while the toxicity of DOX remains a problem during treatment [27]. Furthermore, DOX is ineffective in P-glycoprotein (P-gp)-expressing cells [20]. As such, new chemotherapeutic agents and combinations of therapies need to be investigated.

Salinomycin (SAL) is a polyether ionophore antibiotic isolated from Streptomyces albus that is widely applied as an anticoccidial drug in farm animals [28]. SAL has been shown to exert anticancer effects in several human cancer cells by inducing apoptosis in carcinoma cells via caspase activation and autophagy induction in breast and prostate cancer cells [6]. SAL has also been found to inhibit cell invasion and migration in various cancer types [21]. In small-animal cancer research, SAL has also been shown to exert anticancer activities in canine mammary carcinoma [8], feline sarcoma, and carcinoma [4] cell lines by inhibiting cell proliferation. However, the anticancer potential of SAL in patient-derived FMT cells remains unclear. As a multiple drug-resistant gene-1 (P-gp) inhibitor, SAL has been shown to reverse DOX resistance in the DOX-resistant MCF-7 human breast cancer cell line by diminishing the expression and activity of DOX efflux pumps [15, 17]. The same result has been observed in human soft tissue sarcoma cell lines, with combination treatment of SAL and DOX (combination SD) being shown to synergize to increase caspase-mediated apoptotic activity [16]. Experiments using feline sarcoma and carcinoma cell lines have further reported a similar synergistic effect of SD combination [4]. As FMT is similar to human breast cancer in terms of the biological behavior and pattern of metastasis, combination SD may also work synergistically in FMT.

In the present study, we established four strains of FMT 2.5D organoids (FMTO) from patient tumor tissues, and investigated whether SAL treatment has anticancer potential and whether it potentiates the effect of DOX treatment on these models. Furthermore, we explored the mechanisms of action underlying the anticancer potential of SAL in vitro, and verified this potential in vivo in FMTO-xenografted mice. To recapitulate the in vivo functions and genetic and molecular imprints of the original tumor sample, which have a comparatively high proliferation rate, all FMT patient-derived cells were cultured in Matrigel-free medium as 2.5D organoids, as reported in our previous reports [2, 3].

Sample information

In this study, FMTO was produced using tumor tissue samples from four cats with mammary cancer. Tumor samples were immediately transferred from the animal hospital after surgery in a cooled shipping medium [32, 34] to our laboratory. The FMTO was generated as described previously [2, 3]. Details of the culture conditions, 2.5D culture medium, supplements, growth factors, and processing were the same as in our previous reports.

Cell viability screening

Cytotoxicity activities were evaluated using PrestoBlue™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) Cell Viability assay, as previously reported [1, 11]. In brief, cells (1 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates with 100 µL 2.5D medium. After 24 hr, the culture medium was changed with one containing DMSO (vehicle 1:1,000), or SAL (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) at concentrations of 2.5 μM, 5 μM, 10 μM, and 20 μM, and incubated at 37°C for 72 hr. After incubation, 10 μL of PrestoBlue™ Cell Viability Reagent was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 3 hr at 37°C.

The combined effect of SAL and doxorubicin (Cayman Chemical) was assessed by incubating cells with four sets of combinations: vehicle only, DOX 100 nM, SAL 1 μM, and SAL 1 μM + DOX 100 nM at 37°C for 72 hr. Fluorescence (emission wavelength: 585 nm) was read using a microplate reader (TECAN, Seestrasse, Switzerland) to quantify the data. Representative phase contrast images were obtained using a light microscope (BX-52; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Boyden chamber invasion assay

Matrigel (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA) was diluted in serum-free medium (advanced DMEM) at 1:50. For invasion assays, a drop (50 μL) of diluted Matrigel was added to each Transwell® insert (8 μm PET membrane, Corning 3464, Glendale, AZ, USA). The gel was fixed at 37°C incubator for 1 hr. After fixation, the extra medium was aspirated into the insert. The cells were then trypsinized and resuspended in serum-free medium. Next, 3 × 104 cells in 300 μL serum-free media with 10 μM SAL or vehicle were seeded into the upper chamber. Subsequently, 600 μL of culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) as a chemoattractant was added to the lower chambers. Cells were cultured in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 overnight. Transwell inserts were then washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 2 min, and methanol at room temperature for 20 min. The cells on the upper side of the inserts were removed using cotton swabs, while those on the lower side of the membrane were stained with Giemsa solution for 15 min at room temperature. The invading and migrating cells were then observed and imaged under an optical microscope (Olympus BX52).

TUNEL analysis

TUNEL staining was carried out as previously described [9, 10]. In brief, cells were treated with vehicle, 10 μM SAL for 72 hr and fixed in 4% PFA. Subsequently, the cells were permeabilized with 0.2% TritonX-100 at 4°C for 5 min. Thereafter, they were washed and stained with TUNEL reaction mixture at 37°C for 60 min. Positive controls were prepared by treating fixed FMT cells with DNase I. All samples were then observed under a fluorescence microscope (DAPI, FITC), and representative images were captured and analyzed.

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated using the NucleoSpin RNA kit (TAKARA, Tokyo, Japan), and converted to first-strand cDNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (Toyobo Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan), following the manufacturer’s protocols. Real-time PCR was performed using the QuantiTect SYBR I kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Netherlands) and StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). The ΔΔCq method was applied to quantify the data and calculate the fold change. Each cDNA sample was prepared in triplicate using primers. The expression level of each gene was normalized to the GAPDH level in each sample.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

The P21 antibody was purchased from Bioss Antibodies Inc. (Woburn, MA, USA). Hoechst 33258 Solution (1 mg/mL) was purchased from NACALAI TESQUE, INC.

Frozen sections prepared for P-21 IF staining were firstly washed with PBS for 5 min. The sections were blocked with 1.5% normal goat serum (diluted by PBS) for 30 min at room temperature. The primary antibody, P21, was diluted (1:100) with PBS and incubated overnight at 4°C in a humid box. After washing with PBS, the slides were incubated with Hoechst (1:1,000) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (Alexa Fluor 488, ab150077) (1:500) for 60 min at room temperature in a dark environment.

FMTO was seeded on round cover glass in a 6-well plate. After 80% confluency, the cells were treated with vehicle (DMSO) 1 μM SAL, 100 nM DOX, or 1 μM SAL with 100 nM DOX for 72 hr and then fixed with 4% PFA for 1 hr at room temperature. Staining procedures were the same as the frozen tissue. All samples were then observed under a fluorescence microscope (DAPI, FITC). Representative images were captured and analyzed.

Caspase activity assay

To detect cell apoptosis, FMTO were treated with vehicle or SAL 1 μM for 24 hr. Caspase-3/7 activity was detected by Caspase-Glo® 3/7 Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer.

Xenograft mouse

Mouse experiments were conducted with permission from the Animal Ethics Committee of the Tokyo University of Agriculture and Technology (Approval number: R04-120). Eight five-week-old female SCID mice (C.B-17/IcrHsd-Prldc sid) were purchased from Sankyo Laboratory (Tokyo, Japan), and raised in a pathogen-free environment. After 1 week of acclimation, FMTO cells (1 × 106) mixed with Matrigel/culture medium (1:1, 100 μL) were injected under the mammary fat pad of mice. Two mice failed to develop tumors; thus, the remaining six mice were randomly divided into two groups (SAL- and vehicle-treated groups, three mice each) after tumor colonization for 10 days. Vehicle (saline) or SAL (4 mg/kg) was then administered (intraperitoneal injection) every day for 3 weeks (21 days). The weight and tumor dimensions of the mice were measured weekly post-administration. Tumor volumes (V) were calculated as V=1/2 (L×W2), where (L) was the length and (W) was the width. Mice were sacrificed under isoflurane anesthesia, while xenografts were isolated and washed with PBS. Tumor tissues were then fixed in 4% PFA overnight and embedded in paraffin or 30% sucrose solution, followed by OCT compound. Four-micrometer paraffin-embedded and frozen sections were subsequently prepared for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining.

H&E staining

Paraffin sections were prepared for H&E staining using standard methodologies. All slides used for H&E staining were stained with H&E for 4 min and 30 sec, respectively. The slides were subsequently observed under an optical microscope (Olympus BX52), and examined by a veterinary pathologist.

Immunochemistry (IHC) staining

IHC staining was performed on 4 μm thick paraffin-embedded slides. The antibody (Anti-Ki67, Rabbit-Mono (SP6)) was purchased from Novus Biologicals. Slides were deparaffinized with xylene and subsequently rehydrated with ethanol and distilled water (DW). To deactivate antigens, slides are autoclaved at 121°C with sodium citrate buffer for 5 min, then cooled at room temperature. After washing, slides were quenched with 1% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min. The primary antibody was diluted (1:100) with 0.5% casein and then added to the slides overnight at 4°C in a humid box. After washing with PBS, the slides were incubated with the secondary antibody (1:100 biotinylated goat anti-rabbit IgG) for 30 min at room temperature. The slides were then incubated with the Vectastain ABC reagent for 30 min. Color development was achieved by applying a diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) solution for 10 min. After washing with distilled water, the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated using ethanol and xylene, and mounted in Polymount. Images were captured using a light microscope (BX-52) for analysis.

The quantification was under ×20 magnification (n=6, 3:3), and the percentage of pixels in the image that have been stained by DAB in dark brown were calculated by ImageJ software. Data of each field was normalized by the Hematoxylin-stained nuclei area (%).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Sigma Plot and all data were shown as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Bonferroni’s test were used to conduct the statistical analyses for multi-group comparison (Fig. 3B, 3C). T‐test was also used to compare data between two groups (Figs. 1, 2, 4). P-values <0.05 were thought to have statistical significance.

RESULTS

Effects of SAL on cell viability and cell invasion ability of FMTO

Since the anticancer effect of SAL has been reported in several feline carcinoma cell lines, we carried out a cell viability assay on patient-derived FMTO. SAL showed a concentration-dependent inhibitory effect on all cell lines (Fig. 1A, 1B). In the invasion assays, the 10 μM SAL-treated group showed significantly fewer invasive cells than the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 1C), indicating that SAL could effectively inhibit the invasion of FMTO.

Effects of SAL on cell apoptosis in FMTO

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the inhibitory effect of SAL on FMTO, we examined whether SAL treatment induced apoptosis. TUNEL analysis revealed that the FITC-positive cell ratio, which indicated that the cell apoptosis ratio in the SAL-treated group, was significantly higher than that in the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 2A, 2B). After 24 hr treatment of SAL treatment, luminescence indicated that the Caspase-3/7 activity was significantly higher than that in vehicle-treated FMTO (Fig. 2C). Correspondingly, we examined the expression levels of P21, Caspase-8, and Caspase-9, observing significant increases after 24 hr of treatment with SAL compared to the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 2D). These data suggested that SAL decreased the viability of FMTO by inducing apoptosis.

Effects of a combination of SAL and DOX (combination SD) on cell viability and cell apoptosis of FMTO

As the anticancer effect of combination SD has previously been reported in several feline carcinoma cell lines, we examined its potential in patient-derived FMTO. Combination SD showed concentration-dependent inhibitory effects on both DOX-resistant (FMT22017, IC50 of DOX >100 nM) and DOX-sensitive (FMT22018, IC50 of DOX=44.4 nM) FMTO cell lines, with significantly lower cell viability observed in the combination SD-treated group than in the SAL-alone and vehicle-treated groups (Fig. 3A).

In the DOX-resistant FMTO strain, real-time PCR showed that the expression levels of Caspase-8 and P21 were significantly higher after 24 hr of treatment with the combination of SD compared to SAL alone-treatment, while Cyclin E1 was significantly down-regulated in combination SD-treated group compared to other treatments (Fig. 3B). Although Caspase-9 was upregulated after 24 hr of treatment with combination SD compared to the other three treatment methods (not the vehicle-treated group), there was no significant difference compared to the SAL-alone or DOX-alone treatments (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence staining results also confirmed that the expression level of P21 in the combination-SD-treated group was significantly higher than that in the DOX-alone and SAL-treated groups (Fig. 3C). These findings indicate that combination SD could exert anticancer effects on both DOX-sensitive and DOX-resistant FMTOs by inducing cell apoptosis and inhibiting cell proliferation.

Effects of SAL on growth of FMTO-derived xenograft in mice

To evaluate the in vivo anticancer effects of SAL alone, a xenograft experiment using patient-derived FMTO was performed (Fig. 4A). After allowing tumor growth, SAL was administered daily for 3 weeks. The tumor volumes in the SAL-administered group were significantly decreased on days 7, 14, and 21 in the SAL-treated group compared to those in the vehicle-administered control group (Fig. 4B and 4C). Furthermore, Ki-67 immunohistochemical examination revealed the positive cell ratio in the SAL-administrated group was significantly lower than that in the control group (Fig. 4D). The expression level of P21 in the SAL-administrated group was higher than that in the control group (Fig. 4E).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we examined the effects of SAL on FMTO alone and in combination with DOX (combination SD). The main findings were as follows: First, SAL decreased the cell viability of FMTO in a concentration-dependent manner and effectively inhibited the invasion of FMTO (Fig. 1). Second, SAL upregulated the expression levels of the cell proliferation marker P21, and the apoptosis markers Caspase-8 and -9 and Caspase-3/7 activity in FMTOs (Fig. 2). The combination of SD inhibited the viability of FMTO, with reduced expression of G1/S-specific Cyclin E1 and up-regulated Caspase-8, Caspase-9, and P21 (Fig. 3). In vivo experiments further showed that SAL inhibited the development of FMTO-derived xenograft tumors. The proliferation marker Ki-67 was further found to be down-regulated with the upregulation of P21 in SAL-treated xenograft mouse tumor tissues compared with the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 4). These data suggest that SAL is a promising anticancer drug for FMT, and that combination SD is a potential treatment method for FMT.

In the present study, we evaluated the anticancer effects of SAL on FMTO both in vitro and in vivo. The cytotoxicity of SAL has been reported in human colorectal cancer [19], pancreatic cancer [33], and breast cancer [12] cells, as well as canine mammary tumors [8]. Although the effects of SAL on some feline cell lines have been demonstrated, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Our study is the first to investigate the anticancer effect of SAL on FMT using patient-derived 2.5D organoids, which are faithful models that can recapitulate the characteristics of the original patient’s tumor tissue [2, 3]. Our results showed that the anti-tumor effect of SAL in FMTO was mediated by the inhibition of cell invasion, targeting the signaling pathways of cell proliferation and apoptosis.

Similar to a report using canine mammary tumor cells and other feline cell lines, in our study, SAL efficiently inhibited the viability of FMTO in a concentration-dependent manner, with the different cell lines showing different sensitivities to SAL. Morphological changes of FMTO were observed after the treatment (Fig. 1A). These data agree with those of reports using the human breast cancer cell line MCF-7 and feline sarcoma and carcinoma cell lines [4, 25], thus verifying the anti-tumor potential of SAL.

Although P21 has been identified as a cell cycle regulatory protein that can lead to cell cycle arrest and inhibit cell proliferation, it may also have oncogenic and pro-oncogenic functions depending on the cell type and cellular environment [24]. As such, changes in the expression of P21 vary between different tumor types. For example, a reduction of P21 has been reported in human mammary tumors [26]. In the present study, the up-regulation of P21 expression following drug administration, as well as a trend towards the upregulation of the extrinsic apoptosis marker Caspase-8 and the pro-apoptotic Caspase-9 in the mitochondrial, along with the activity of Casapase-3/7, were observed in vitro following SAL treatment (Fig. 2). Combined with the results of TUNEL staining, the effects of SAL on the inhibition of cell proliferation and promotion of apoptosis after treatment were demonstrated. In previous studies on human ovarian cancer [22, 30], acute promyelocytic leukemia [37] and human osteosarcoma [18], SAL-induced cell apoptosis was reported to be responsible for its anti-cancer activity.

As high concentrations of SAL have been reported to induce polyneuropathy in cats [35], a relatively low concentration was chosen for the combination SD experiment. The combination of SD was effective for both DOX-sensitive and DOX-resistant FMTO strains, consistent with the results of a previous study on the inhibitory efficiency of combination SD in feline sarcoma. Other studies on human MDR mammary tumor cells [15, 17] and human soft tissue sarcomas [4, 23] have further reported the availability of combination therapy with SAL and DOX. The current study showed that the combination of SD upregulated P21, the extrinsic and intrinsic apoptosis marker Caspase-8 and Caspase-9, suggesting the induction of cell apoptosis, while it downregulated Cyclin E1, indicating cell cycle arrest. Increased apoptotic activity was also confirmed in human soft tissue sarcoma cell lines, indicating that the synergistic effect is probably a consequence of SAL increasing the toxicity of DOX.

In the present study, SAL administration significantly reduced the volume of FMTO-xenografted mouse tumors (Fig. 4). Similarly, other in vivo experiments using human breast cancer cell lines and mouse Matrigel plug assays have further reported the suppressive effect of SAL on tumor formation [36] and angiogenesis [7]. These data correspond to the effects of SAL on tumor growth in vivo.

In conclusion, in the present study, we examined the anti-tumor potential of SAL using patient-derived FMTO, finding that SAL inhibited FMTO by inducing apoptosis and affecting cell proliferation. Furthermore, we found that a low dose of SAL potentiated the anticancer effect of DOX, a commonly used anticancer drug in pet clinics, promoting both cell apoptosis and cell-cycle arrest in vitro. This combination method may allow dose de-escalation, thereby reducing side effects and toxicities in FMT patients during therapy. The in vivo anti-tumor effect was verified using a xenograft mouse model of FMTO. These results correspond with those of the in vitro experiments, presenting further proof of the anti-tumor potential of SAL and suggesting its further application in pet clinics. However, the availability of SAL treatment alone requires further verification with other models, while the molecular mechanism of the anti-tumor activity of combination SD against FMT requires further investigation. Therefore, an evaluation of the dosage is necessary. In the future, SAL may provide a new choice as an adjuvant chemotherapy drug for more efficient treatment, thereby decreasing the chemoresistance in DOX-resistant FMT patients.
