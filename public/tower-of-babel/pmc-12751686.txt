Effect of carperitide on clinical outcomes among patients with acute heart failure: a meta-analysis of randomized and propensity‑matched studies
Authors: Tsuyoshi Shiga, Atsushi Suzuki, Sachie Inoue, Masayuki Hashiguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarperitide, which is an α-human A-type natriuretic peptide, is used intravenously for the treatment of acute heart failure (AHF) primarily in Japan. However, its clinical effectiveness has not been established. The aim of this meta-analysis was to assess the effect of carperitide on the clinical outcomes of patients with AHF.MethodsWe searched the MEDLINE, ICHUSHI, Cochrane Library and Web of Science databases using a defined search strategy to identify eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and propensity score matching (PSM) studies. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. The secondary outcomes were long-term mortality, HF rehospitalization, length of hospital stay and hypotension. Pooled odds ratios (ORs)/mean differences and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated by using the random effects model.ResultsA total of nine studies were evaluated in the meta-analysis, including four RCTs and five PSM studies. A meta-analysis of RCTs and PSM studies revealed that the in-hospital mortality rate was higher in the carperitide group than in the noncarperitide group (OR 1.38; 95% CI 1.07–1.78; I2 = 77%). However, a meta-analysis of the RCTs alone revealed no significant difference in in-hospital mortality (OR 0.85; 95% CI: 0.07–10.49; I2 = 39%). There were no significant differences observed between the two groups in terms of long-term mortality, HF rehospitalization, incidence of hypotension, or length of hospital stay.ConclusionsThis meta-analysis suggested that carperitide therapy may not be superior to noncarperitide therapy in terms of reducing in-hospital mortality rates or improving long-term outcomes in patients with AHF.Trial registrationThe review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251071777).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05394-0.Keywords: Acute heart failure, Atrial natriuretic peptide, Carperitide, Mortality, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Carperitide is a recombinant human atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) that exhibits vasodilator and diuretic effects and improves dyspnea associated with pulmonary congestion by reducing preload and afterload in patients with congestive heart failure (HF) [1]. Carperitide was developed in Japan and has been used mainly in that country for the treatment of acute heart failure (AHF) over the past three decades. Previous Japanese Circulation Society (JCS) guidelines recommended the use of carperitide as an intravenous vasodilator for the treatment of pulmonary congestion in patients with decompensated HF [2]. As a result, carperitide has been widely used in clinical practice in Japan for the treatment of AHF [3–6].

Early small-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrated that carperitide improves hemodynamics in patients with AHF [7, 8]. Furthermore, it was suggested that carperitide also improves mortality and HF rehospitalization rates compared with conventional treatment [9]. Nevertheless, several observational studies reported in Japan have yielded inconclusive results regarding the effects of carperitide on the prognosis and outcomes of AHF [10]. A multicenter, randomized, open-label, controlled trial of 247 patients with AHF was recently conducted. The patients were treated with carperitide intravenously in conjunction with standard HF treatment or with matched standard HF treatment for 72 h. The results indicated that carperitide did not demonstrate superiority in long-term prognosis; however, the study was underpowered [11]. Consequently, the prognostic benefits of carperitide in patients with AHF remain unclear [10]. More recent JCS guidelines do not recommend the routine use of carperitide as a vasodilator for AHF [12].

In general, RCTs are needed to estimate the causal effect of treatment. However, RCTs focusing on carperitide for the treatment of AHF exhibit inherent limitations due to the fact that the oldest trial was conducted 30 years ago; moreover, the sample sizes of all of the trials were small. The propensity score matching (PSM) method can reduce selection bias and control units that are balanced in terms of covariates [13]. In this study, we conducted a meta-analysis that included RCTs and PSM studies to provide a more comprehensive comparison of the effects of carperitide on the prognosis and outcomes of patients with AHF.

Methods

This meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) statement [14]. The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251071777). This study used publicly available data from previously published studies. Therefore, ethical approval was not needed.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. The secondary outcomes were long-term mortality after hospital discharge, HF rehospitalization, the occurrence of hypotension during treatment, and the length of hospital stay.

Search strategy and study selection

We searched the MEDLINE, ICHUSHI, Cochrane Library and Web of Science databases up to March 31, 2025, to identify relevant articles. The following key words were used: (carperitide OR hANP) and heart failure. Details regarding the keyword searches in each database are shown in Table S1. Two reviewers (MH and TS) independently extracted the following data from the text, tables, and figures: characteristics of the included studies, baseline characteristics of the study populations (including AHF/acute decompensated HF), and outcomes of interest. The studies were screened based on prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were as follows: AHF patients > 18 years of age who received carperitide during the index hospitalization. The exclusion criteria were as follows: pregnant patients, patients on dialysis, non-English or non-Japanese articles, case series, non-PSM case‒control studies, and meta-analyses. Conference abstracts were also excluded because they lacked the necessary data to assess study quality.

After removing duplicates, two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining publications. Any publication deemed to be relevant by either reviewer was subjected to a full-text review. Any disagreements among the reviewers during the full-text review were resolved via consensus. A third reviewer (AS) was consulted to make the final decision if a consensus could not be reached through discussion.

Data extraction and quality assessment

The following characteristics of the included studies were extracted into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet to facilitate data quality checks: year of publication; study design; number of participating facilities; details of the control group; number of patients; dose of carperitide; mean or median age of the patients; sex ratio; baseline characteristics and group similarities; randomization processes (RCTs); blinding (RCTs); follow-up strategies and period; criteria for patient inclusion (confirmation of AHF definition); and endpoints including efficacy and safety outcomes.

Two independent reviewers (MH and TS) assessed and extracted data from all of the included studies; additionally, they resolved disagreements via consensus or with the help of third reviewers (AS and SI). Any disagreements among the reviewers during the full-text review were resolved via consensus. Study quality was assessed by using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool version 2 [15] for RCTs and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool [16] for observational studies.

Statistical analysis

In the meta-analysis, for each outcome, a pooled odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using the random effects Mantel‒Haenszel method. The mean difference and 95% CI were calculated for the length of hospital stay using the inverse variance method. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The results were visualized by using a forest plot, and publication bias was assessed via the Egger’s regression test. Heterogeneity was estimated by using the I2 statistic. Moreover, subgroup analyses were performed (stratified by study design) to explore potential causes of data heterogeneity. To verify the robustness of the meta-analysis results, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed. All of the results were analyzed by using RevMan 5.4.1 (Nordic Cochrane Centre, Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen).

Results

A total of 319 studies were screened and 16 studies were assessed for eligibility. Of these, nine studies were ultimately included in this meta-analysis (Fig. 1). Among the four RCTs, one RCT was a single-center trial [8] and three RCTs were multicenter trails [7, 9, 11]. The dose of carperitide varied among the three RCTs, with two RCTs using a high dose (0.1–0.2 µg/kg/min) [7, 8] and two RCTs using a low dose (0.01–0.05 µg/kg/min) [9, 11]. Furthermore, the utilized control treatments varied across three trials, including the use of placebo [7], normal saline [8], and standard treatment [9, 11]. Five PSM studies exhibited significant variation in the sample size and substantial differences in patient sampling, ranging from single-center [18] and multicenter [17, 21] to nationwide [19, 20] studies. Additionally, the dosage and duration of carperitide use varied. The study by Nagai restricted the control group to users of the vasodilator nitrate [20]. In contrast, the control groups in the other studies included non-carperitide users, with nitrate usage rates ranging from 7% to more than 50% [17–19, 21].

Risk of bias of the included studies

The Cochrane risk of bias assessment results for the four RCTs [7–9, 11] and the ROBINS-I results for the five PSM studies [17–21] are shown in Tables S2 and S3, respectively.

Two RCTs were defined as having a low risk of overall quality [7, 11]. The remaining two RCTs raised some concerns regarding their overall quality [8, 9] (Table S2). These two RCTs were inadequate in two areas [8, 9]. While one RCT documented the randomization method in its design paper [22], the others did not describe it. With the exception of one RCT [7], three RCTs involved open-label designs and did not employ blinding [8, 9, 11]. One RCT clearly defined the criteria for HF hospitalization and hypotension [11], whereas another RCT lacked defined criteria for hypotension and relied on researcher reporting [7]. Therefore, the present meta-analysis utilized in-hospital mortality (all-cause death) as the primary outcome measure, which is free from assessment bias.

All five PSM studies were defined as having a moderate risk of overall quality [17–21] (Table S3). The domains of bias that influenced this overall quality assessment were participant selection, deviation from the intended intervention, and missing data. The risk of selection bias and bias due to deviation from the intended intervention was moderate in all the studies [17–21]. One PSM study that conducted follow-ups after discharge from index hospitalization demonstrated some loss to follow-up [21]. Missing data bias was deemed to pose a low risk in all studies except for this one [21]. The definition of HF rehospitalization after discharge was explicitly stated in an original study by Suzuki [6], and the definition of hypotension was stated in the study by Ogiso [18]. PSM is a potent statistical instrument for enhancing balance with respect to observed covariates. However, complete elimination of unobserved confounding factors was not possible.

Primary outcome

In-hospital mortality rates were significantly higher in the carperitide group than in the noncarperitide group (OR 1.38; 95% CI 1.07–1.78; P = 0.01; I2 = 77%) (Fig. 2). Due to significant heterogeneity (I² = 77%) in the results of the meta-analysis, the findings were examined via stratification according to study design. A meta-analysis of the RCTs revealed a smaller effect size for in-hospital mortality (OR 0.85; 95% CI: 0.07–10.49; I² = 39) compared to that of the PSM studies (OR 1.39; 95% CI: 1.08–1.80 I² = 84), with no significant difference being observed between the carperitide and noncarperitide groups (Fig. 2).

Afterwards, a leave-one-out meta-analysis was conducted to examine the robustness of the findings regarding the difference in in-hospital mortality rates between patients with and without carperitide. The exclusion and subsequent re-analysis of each study individually produced inconsistent results. These results demonstrated an absence of consistent patterns across the studies (Fig. 3). Consequently, the findings of this meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution, as minor alterations to the dataset could significantly impact the outcomes.

Secondary outcomes

Although the postdischarge follow-up periods varied across the studies, including follow-up periods of 1 year after discharge (PSM study) [21], 1.5 years after discharge (RCT) [9], and 2 years after randomization (RCT) [11], there was no significant difference observed in long-term mortality between the carperitide group and the noncarperitide group (Table 2, Figure S1). HF rehospitalization rates were not significantly different between the carperitide group and the noncarperitide group (Table 2, Figure S2). Moreover, the incidence rate of hypotension, a common adverse event associated with carperitide, was not significantly different between the carperitide and noncarperitide groups (Table 2, Figure S3). Additionally, there was no difference observed in the length of hospital stay between the carperitide group and the noncarperitide group (Table 3, Figure S4).

Publication bias and certainty of evidence

The Egger’s regression test yielded no evidence of publication bias for the primary outcome of in-hospital mortality (P = 0.9235). With respect to the other outcomes, the number of stratified studies was insufficient, and publication bias could not be statistically assessed. In this study, the certainty of the outcome estimates from the RCTs was observed to be low. This effect may be due to insufficient statistical power in small-scale studies, as indicated by the small number of observed events and wide CIs.

Discussion

This meta-analysis of nine studies evaluated the clinical efficacy of intravenous carperitide administration both in the short term (during hospitalization) and in the long term (after discharge). The following results were observed: (1) the in-hospital mortality rate in the carperitide group did not demonstrate a significant superiority compared to the noncarperitide group; (2) there were no significant differences in long-term mortality rates detected between the two groups; (3) there were no significant differences detected in HF rehospitalization rates after discharge between the two groups; and (4) the length of hospital stay was not shorter in the carperitide group, and there was no increase in hypotension detected with intravenous administration.

In-hospital mortality

This meta-analysis revealed that compared with noncarperitide therapy, carperitide therapy was not effective at reducing in-hospital mortality. A meta-analysis that exclusively relied on RCTs revealed no significant difference in in-hospital mortality between the carperitide and noncarperitide groups. However, a meta-analysis of PSM studies revealed that the carperitide group exhibited a higher in-hospital mortality rate than the non-carperitide group; nevertheless, the results were highly heterogeneous. All of these studies were retrospective observational studies, and various factors are believed to be responsible for this heterogeneity. These factors include differences in the dosage of carperitide, regional differences, single- or multicenter studies, and differences in sample sizes. Additionally, the inconsistent treatments in the control groups across the different studies may have resulted in a relative assessment of the efficacy of carperitide, which lacks reliability. This issue has a substantial impact on the evaluation of superiority and represents a significant limitation of this meta-analysis.

A leave-one-out meta-analysis revealed that the elimination of two PSM studies using nationwide databases [19, 20] abolished the difference between the carpritide and noncarpritide groups. These findings suggest that these two studies significantly distorted the results of the meta-analysis. It is possible that carperitide was prescribed to patients with more severe AHF in the Japanese clinical setting, despite matching being performed for background factors. Nagai et al. reported that compared with patients receiving nitrates, patients receiving carperitide were older, exhibited more comorbidities (such as atrial fibrillation and chronic renal failure), demonstrated a higher frequency of emergency admissions, and were less frequently classified as New York Heart Association functional class IV [20].

If carperitide adversely affects in-hospital mortality in patients with AHF, this scenario could be associated with the potential to worsen renal impairment, which could lead to further complications and poor patient outcomes. Nesiritide (a recombinant B-type natriuretic peptide) has been reported to be associated with worsening renal function, which is a predictor of increased mortality risk in HF patients [23, 24] or during AHF treatment [25]. A pooled analysis of three RCTs revealed that, compared with non-inotrope vasodilator therapies for AHF, nesiritide may increase the short-term risk of death (regardless of dose) [26]. Furthermore, the occurrence of hypotension during acute treatment for AHF may contribute to worsening renal function [27]. The study by Mizuno revealed an increased risk of in-hospital mortality with carperitide use in patients with heart failure and renal disease [19]. In an RCT, Honda et al. reported a significant decrease in the estimated glomerular filtration rate from baseline to 72 h in the low-dose carperitide group compared with the standard treatment group [11]. Carperitide has vasodilatory and natriuretic effects; however, it may reduce the glomerular filtration rate during acute hypotension-induced renal perfusion impairment. In patients with AHF and unstable hemodynamics, carperitide is likely to contribute to worsening renal failure and death through this mechanism.

Nevertheless, a definitive conclusion cannot be obtained regarding the potential superiority of carperitide in terms of in-hospital mortality in AHF patients compared with conventional therapeutic interventions.

Long-term outcomes

Only three studies utilized long-term mortality as an endpoint; however, the studies demonstrated different follow-up periods after discharge [9, 11, 21]. Interestingly, the use of carperitide in patients with AHF was initially thought to exert a beneficial effect on prognosis after hospital discharge based on the results of a small RCT [9]. However, in this meta-analysis of 2 RCTs and 1 PSM study, no significant difference in long-term mortality after discharge was observed between the carperitide group and the noncarperitide group.

The HF rehospitalization rates after discharge were also not significantly different between the carperitide and noncarperitide groups. Similarly, a previous meta-analysis revealed that nesiritide demonstrated no effect on long-term prognosis in patients with AHF [28]. Carperitide affects only hemodynamics in the early phase of AHF and does not affect the long-term prognosis of patients after they are discharged alive.

Hata et al. demonstrated the efficacy of low-dose (0.01–0.05 µg/kg/min) carperitide on long-term outcomes (death and rehospitalization) in an RCT with a modest sample size [9]. Additionally, Nogi et al. reported that low-dose carperitide (median 0.025 µg/kg/min) also affects long-term outcomes (death and HF rehospitalization) in an observational study but not in a PSM study [29]. However, Honda et al. conducted an RCT and demonstrated that low-dose carperitide (0.02 µg/kg/min) did not improve long-term outcomes (death and HF rehospitalization) [11]. These findings suggest that there is no beneficial effect of low-dose carperitide on long-term outcomes.

Hypotension

The occurrence of hypotension during intravenous carperitide infusion is a major adverse reaction due to its vasodilating effect and limits the use of carperitide for patients with AHF and low blood pressure in clinical practice. Therefore, the results of this meta-analysis revealed no difference in the incidence of hypotension between the carperitide and noncarperitide groups. Matsue et al. suggested that carperitide may lead to more frequent hypotension, as well as subsequent worsening renal function and worse outcomes in hypertensive AHF patients; however, this study did not provide specific data on the incidence of hypotension and worsening renal function [17]. In this meta-analysis, it is unclear whether the higher in-hospital mortality rates observed in the carperitide group were associated with hypotension.

Length of hospital stay

Two RCTs [9, 11] and one PSM study [21] reported no significant difference in the length of hospital stay between the carperitide and noncarperitide groups. Despite the observed heterogeneity among the studies, these findings suggest that compared with conventional therapy, carperitide use does not necessarily promote recovery from AHF or hasten hospital discharge.

Clinical implications

Our meta-analysis suggested that carperitide therapy did not significantly outperform non-carperitide therapy in reducing in-hospital mortality, shortening the length of hospital stay, or improving long-term prognosis for patients with AHF. Although carperitide has been widely used in Japan, a reevaluation of its usage guidelines is needed. To optimize safety and efficacy, guidelines should implement strict eligibility criteria, renal function monitoring, and prioritization of alternative therapies such as nitrate. Based on the potential for renal function impairment, hypotension and other adverse outcomes, clinicians should exercise caution when selecting patients for this treatment.

Study limitations

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, the studies included in the meta-analysis involved different study designs, including retrospective observational cohort studies and RCTs. Therefore, the quality of the studies may vary; however, for each study, we assessed the study quality by using the ROBINS-I [15] for retrospective cohort studies and version 2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for RCTs [16]. Second, the noncarperitide group (control group) included patients who were treated with a variety of drugs for HF (depending on the study). Third, the detailed dosage/administration of carperitide used in most of the observational studies could not be determined. Fourth, few safety outcomes could be obtained in this study; therefore, the safety of carperitide (including clinically important safety outcomes) could not be comprehensively investigated. Finally, this meta-analysis included a limited number of articles, with all of the included studies being conducted in Japan. This limitation stems from regulatory constraints. Specifically, carperitide is only approved in Japan, where its use is also restricted. Consequently, there is no evidence regarding the use of carperitide in patients with AHF outside of Japan. Therefore, these results cannot be generalized. Furthermore, when assessing the effects of natriuretic peptides, various factors should be considered. First, compared with individuals in the United States and Europe, Japanese individuals tend to consume more salt [30]. Additionally, nonischemic etiologies are more prevalent among HF patients in Japan [3–6]. Therefore, there are limitations to generalizing these findings to Asian or global populations.

Conclusions

Our meta-analysis suggested a trend toward higher in-hospital mortality rates in the carperitide group than in the noncarperitide group; however, due to significant heterogeneity, caution is needed when interpreting the results. Among patients who were alive at discharge, carperitide use did not affect postdischarge mortality or HF rehospitalization. Overall, these findings suggest that compared with noncarperitide therapy, carperitide therapy does not offer a significant advantage in reducing in-hospital mortality rates or improving long-term outcomes for patients with AHF.
