Letter to the Editor Re: Moghib K, Ahmed MT, Ghanm TIE, et al. Optimal duration of postoperative drainage following burr-hole surgery for chronic subdural hematoma: a systematic review and network meta-analysis. Langenbeck’s archives of Surgery. 2025;410:278. doi:10.1007/s00423-025-03853-y
Authors: Anders Schack, Mads Hjortdal Grønhøj, Frantz Rom Poulsen, Mette Haldrup, Rares Miscov, Carsten Reidies Bjarkam, Anders Rosendal Korshøj, Kåre Fugleholm, Thorbjørn Søren Rønn Jensen
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Chronic subdural hematoma primarily affects older adults, and small changes in postoperative drain policy can shift recurrence rates, time to mobilization, and length of stay for large patient populations each year. We read with interest the review by Moghib et al. (2025) [1]. The authors rank 6-day drainage as most effective and suggest that 3–6 days reduces recurrence versus 17–24 h, without a mortality difference. We disagree. The DACSUHS group supports a fixed 24-hour drain time after burr-hole evacuation with a passive subdural drain.

First, the highest-quality evidence comes from two DACSUHS randomized controlled trials.

DRAIN TIME 1 [2] (n = 420): 24 h vs. 48 h passive subdural drainage showed no difference in recurrence (14% vs. 13%) or mortality within a 90 day follow-up, while 48 h prolonged admission by ~ 19 h.DRAIN TIME 2 [3] (n = 331): 6 h and 12 h were not non-inferior to 24 h; recurrence was higher with shorter drainage. Mortality within 90 days follow-up did not differ significantly; deaths were 7, 8, and 5 in the 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h arms.

Taken together, these RCTs support a fixed 24-hour duration, as longer drainage did not improve outcomes, and shorter drainage increased recurrence.

Second, study misclassification risks double counting. Moghib et al. [1] include the early drain-cessation analysis [4]—an observational substudy of DRAIN TIME 1—yet Table 1 and the risk-of-bias figure list it under RCTs (ROB2 applied with “high risk” for randomization). Treating this substudy as an independent randomized comparison likely double counts patients from DRAIN TIME 1, inflating that population’s weight. It should be handled as a substudy with de-duplication or excluded from the network meta-analysis.

Third, the meta-analysis pools heterogeneous practices and outcome windows. Active subgaleal drains are combined with passive subdural drains; 1–2-day protocols are mixed with older, small, retrospective 3–6-day cohorts; recurrence definitions and follow-up windows (60/90/180 days) vary; and mortality shows moderate heterogeneity. Pooling across these differences without prespecified subgrouping reduces the interpretability of pooled estimates. Moreover, with only seven studies total, the “power of many” that justifies a meta-analysis is not achieved. For long-duration nodes, the network relies on single small cohorts, yielding unstable effects and extreme rank probabilities that reflect sparse data rather than a reliable clinical signal.

Fourth, drainage durations were post-hoc rebinned into arbitrary groups (0–8 h, 9–16 h, 17–24 h, 24/25–48 h, 3 d, 6 d), with labels that are inconsistent between text and figures. The network is especially sparse for the 3–6-day nodes, which helps explain extreme odds ratios and wide intervals. Such rank probabilities are fragile and should not outweigh two large RCTs.

Fifth, several internal errors require correction. The 24 h vs. 48 h comparison is attributed to DRAIN TIME 2 but belongs to DRAIN TIME 1. Baiser et al. [5] are cited for infection rates across drain durations, yet that paper reports CSDH incidence, not surgical-site infections. Claims that 6-day drainage reduces reoperations by ~ 80% versus 24 h and that extended drainage adds 2.1 hospital days lack a clear analytic source and do not align with trial data.

Sixth, contemporary non-randomized evidence does not support >24 h drainage. A study using subgaleal drains with vacuum suction [6] found no difference in recurrence or complications for < 24 h vs. >24 h, with earlier mobilization when drainage duration was shorter. A United Kingdom multicenter cohort [7] found no difference between 1 vs. 2 days and no effect of subdural vs. subgaleal location.

Clinical implications

In routine pathways, patients are often kept on bed rest while a subdural drain remains in situ, thereby delaying mobilization. Some “standard care” protocols mandate strict bed rest until drain removal, whereas ERAS pathways permit sitting and short walks from 6 h, also with a drain in place [8]. The randomized GET UP study compared early out-of-bed care with a 48-hour bed-rest protocol after burr-hole surgery, documenting the bed-rest requirement in usual care [9].

Observational data show that postoperative bed rest independently predicts worse functional outcome [10], and the early-mobilization literature consistently identifies tubes/lines/drains (including IV infusions) as practical barriers that tether patients to bed [11]. Minimizing drain duration and removing devices promptly when clinically appropriate facilitate earlier mobilization and may have secondary benefits such as a reduction of thromboembolic risks and patient discomfort.

Conclusion

On current evidence, a fixed 24-hour drain time should remain standard when a fixed-time approach is used. Early removal at 6–12 h is not supported. Claims for 3–6 days rest on sparse, heterogeneous observational data and should not change practice.

Request for re-analysis

We encourage a corrected re-analysis that: (1) removes duplicate populations; (2) treats Grønhøj et al. (2023) as an observational substudy of DRAIN TIME 1 [4]; (3) stratifies by drain type and by planned vs. observed duration; (4) standardizes outcome windows; and (5) includes sensitivity analyses excluding long-duration observational nodes that drive extreme ranks.
