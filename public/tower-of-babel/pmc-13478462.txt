Monovalent and multivalent OspA mRNA-LNP vaccines elicit functional antibodies and protect against Borrelia burgdorferi in mice
Authors: Meredith B Finn, Graham Willsey, Carol Lyn Piazza, Luke Clendenen, Muskan Shrestha, Stephanie Soee You, Lingzhi Ma, Yukti Dhingra, Adrian R Laciak, Kevin A Uggowitzer, Joshua M Obiero, Grace Freeman-Gallant, Harini Natarajan, Shannon Green, Elham Pirayesh, Yen-Ting Lai, Sunny Himansu, Andrea Carfi, Linden T Hu, Nicholas Mantis, Obadiah Plante, Christina Dold
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOuter surface protein A (OspA) is a ~30 kDa lipoprotein displayed on the surface of Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato, the etiological agent of Lyme disease. Here we report on the preclinical evaluation of OspA-encoding nucleoside-modified mRNA lipid nanoparticle (OspA mRNA-LNP) vaccines for the prevention of Lyme disease. Crystallographic and binding studies using a panel of transmission-blocking antibodies confirmed that the mRNA-encoded OspA serotype 1 (ST1) expressed in mammalian cells assumes its native structure and retains known protective epitopes. Immunization of mice with OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP elicited functional serum antibodies that promoted spirochete agglutination and complement-dependent borreliacidal activity in vitro. We also examined the impact of combining ST1 OspA mRNA with OspA STs 2-7, which are associated with predominant Borrelia genospecies in Europe (B. garinii, B. afzelii, and B. bavariensis). The additional six STs mRNA did not interfere with ST1 antibody titers and functionality. Finally, mice vaccinated two or three times with different dose levels of OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP or with the heptavalent mRNA vaccine were protected against B. burgdorferi infection in a tick-mediated challenge model. The monovalent and the heptavalent OspA mRNA vaccines (mRNA-1982 and mRNA-1975, respectively) are currently undergoing testing in a Phase 1 clinical trial (NCT05975099).Subject terms: Biochemistry, Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Lyme disease (Lyme borreliosis) is the most common vector-borne disease in the Northern hemisphere with an estimated global seroprevalence of 14.5%1,2 and over 89,000 cases reported in the United States alone in 20233. Lyme disease is also a significant concern in Europe, where between 65,000 and 85,000 cases are reported annually4. The number of reported Lyme disease cases on both continents is likely an underestimate of the true incidence rates due to limitations associated with diagnostics5. Lyme disease typically presents with a characteristic bullseye rash (erythema migrans) at the site of a recent tick bite, often accompanied by flu-like symptoms. If left untreated, the disease can progress to more severe manifestations, including Lyme arthritis, carditis, and acute neurological involvement6. In North America, Lyme disease is primarily caused by the spirochete Borrelia burgdorferi sensu stricto, while in Europe other genospecies are drivers of disease, including B. afzelii, B. garinii, and B. bavariensis as well as B. burgdorferi7. The spirochete is vectored by ticks of the Ixodes genus that feed on mammalian, reptilian, and avian hosts8.

Borrelia utilizes several surface lipoproteins for infection and survival in ticks7. One such protein, the Outer Surface Protein A (OspA), is expressed when the spirochetes reside in the tick midgut, and its expression is subsequently dampened once the spirochete enters a mammalian host9. As a surface lipoprotein, OspA is tethered to the outer membrane at its N-terminus by a lipid anchor10, and projects outwards from the bacterial surface to interact with its receptor, TROSPA, in the tick midgut11. There are multiple serotypes (ST) of OspA expressed by Borrelia genospecies depending on geographical locations. OspA ST1 is expressed by B. burgdorferi and is predominant in North America, whereas ST1–7 are in circulation in Europe, with ST2 expressed by B. afzelii, ST4 expressed by B. bavariensis, and ST3, 5, 6, and 7 expressed by B. garinii12,13.

Currently, no Lyme disease vaccines are available for human use. Previous Phase 3 clinical trials demonstrated that OspA ST1 is a protective vaccine antigen. Two OspA-based vaccines, LYMErix™ (SmithKline Beecham) and ImuLymeTM (Sanofi) demonstrated 76% and 92% vaccine efficacy, respectively, following three doses in Phase 3 studies14,15. However, a speculated and since disproven association of OspA antibodies with Lyme arthritis16 resulted in discontinuation of LYMErixTM and the decision not to pursue licensure for ImuLymeTM. Since then, no other Lyme vaccine has been licensed for human use, leaving an unmet medical need as Lyme cases rise alongside the expanding geographic range of ticks17, a trend expected to continue with climate change18. Owing to the urgent need for vaccines to prevent the increasing incidence of Lyme borreliosis, efforts are ongoing to target OspA in an effective vaccine strategy.

Several approaches have been employed to engineer a vaccine aimed at broad coverage against Borrelia genospecies in the US and Europe. A vaccine consisting of the fusion of five OspA STs to ferritin nanoparticles has been shown to protect mice against tick-mediated infection with B. afzelii (ST2)19. In addition, fusion of the C-terminal regions of six OspA STs resulting in three hybrid lipidated proteins has demonstrated protection against several Borrelia strains in both tick and subcutaneous mouse challenge models and is now in a Phase 3 efficacy study (NCT05477524)20–22.

Recently, two studies with OspA ST1 encoding mRNA vaccines demonstrated that the mRNA platform can elicit robust antibody responses that protect mice from B. burgdorferi both in needle-stick23 and tick-bite challenge models24, highlighting the promise of the mRNA platform for Lyme disease prevention. Building on this progress, our study expands the scope of OspA-based mRNA vaccine development in two key innovations. First, we evaluate multiple antigen presentations of full-length OspA ST1, identifying a transmembrane-anchored format that preserves conformational epitopes and enhances functional immunogenicity. Second, we advance a multivalent strategy, delivering mRNA encoding OspA serotypes 1–7 to target the most clinically relevant Borrelia species across North America and Europe. Both monovalent and heptavalent formulations elicited comparable ST1-specific functional antibodies and conferred protection in a stringent tick-bite challenge model of B. burgdorferi infection across multiple doses and schedules, suggesting that increasing valency of mRNAs does not compromise efficacy. These findings support the versatility and potential of mRNA-based OspA vaccines and provide the rationale for ongoing clinical evaluation of mRNA-1982 (OspA ST1 monovalent) and mRNA-1975 (OspA ST1-7 heptavalent) (NCT05975099).

RESULTS

An mRNA-based OspA ST1 vaccine elicits a robust functional antibody response in mice

OspA is an elongated molecule comprising 21 consecutive antiparallel β-strands capped by a single C-terminal α-helix. OspA is anchored to the outer leaflet of the Borrelia outer membrane via a post-translational N-terminal lipid moiety. Several factors were considered when designing an mRNA-based OspA vaccine. First, to promote expression in mammalian cells, we engineered an mRNA sequence lacking the first 25 N-terminal residues, which encompass the lipidation motif not processed in mammalian systems. Four predicted N-linked glycosylation sites were also eliminated to prevent host-cell glycosylation and potential epitope masking. Additionally, to reduce the risk of immunological cross-reactivity, despite prior reports linking this epitope to antibiotic-refractory Lyme arthritis having been conclusively disproven25–27, residues 165–173, corresponding to the putative human LFA-1 (hLFA-1) T cell epitope (YVLEGTLTA), were substituted with FILEGRFTA.

The resulting construct, termed OspA ST1, exhibited robust expression in mammalian cells following mRNA transfection. To evaluate the preservation of conformational epitopes, we compared the binding kinetics of Fabs from three neutralizing mAbs, LA-2, 184.1, and 857-2, each directed against distinct regions of OspA, between mammalian-expressed OspA ST1 and bacterially expressed WT protein. All three Fabs bound both proteins with comparable affinity and kinetics, indicating that key conformational epitopes are retained in the mammalian-expressed version (Table 1).

To verify the structural integrity of the engineered antigen, we solved a 1.85 Å resolution crystal structure of mammalian-expressed OspA ST1 in complex with the Fab fragment of the neutralizing monoclonal antibody LA-2. The structure shows that OspA ST1 adopts the native OspA fold, with Cα RMSDs <2 Å relative to previously reported bacterially expressed OspA structures28–31. Importantly, the local conformation surrounding the substituted peptide segment (165-173) is preserved, indicating that the engineered modification does not perturb the overall architecture of the antigen (Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 1A).

To assess the impact of antigen display on immunogenicity, OspA ST1 was encoded and produced in four formats: (1) membrane-anchored via an N-terminal transmembrane domain from influenza neuraminidase, (2) C-terminally fused to the hemagglutinin transmembrane domain, (3) fused to a C-terminal ferritin nanoparticle scaffold, and (4) as a secreted soluble protein.

The different vaccine designs of the four OspA-encoding mRNA constructs were formulated in lipid nanoparticles (LNP) and administered intramuscularly (2 µg) to groups of mice on days 1, 21, and 42. For comparison, an additional group of animals was vaccinated with recombinant, non-lipidated soluble OspA protein produced in E. coli adjuvanted with alum (1 µg) on the same schedule. Each of the OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP formulations (soluble, Nterm-TM, Cterm-TM, Ferritin) elicited OspA ST1 antibody titers that were higher than those elicited by the recombinant, non-lipidated protein (Fig. 1B). Notably, the ferritin-displayed construct showed a modestly higher ST1-binding IgG response following the first immunization, which may reflect enhanced early B cell activation associated with multivalent antigen display. However, after the second and third immunizations, binding titers were comparable across the mRNA constructs encoding nanoparticles and cell membrane-anchored proteins, and differences were not statistically significant, indicating that this early advantage was not sustained after boosting.

We next examined the capacity of the OspA mRNA-LNP vaccines to elicit LA-2 equivalent antibodies. LA-2 is a protective mouse mAb directed against a conformational epitope within OspA’s C-terminus32. In pre-clinical and clinical studies of previous OspA ST1 vaccines, LA-2 equivalence (as defined in a competitive ELISA) correlated with protection against B. burgdorferi infection and Lyme disease15,32,33. To evaluate LA-2 equivalence elicited by the OspA mRNA-LNP vaccines, immune sera from day 56 were diluted 1:10 and competed with LA-2 IgG1. While the LA-2 competition assay shows variability across all vaccine groups, likely due to the polyclonal nature of each mouse’s antibody response with diverse affinities and overlaps with the LA-2 monoclonal antibody binding site, the Nterm-TM anchored design elicited the strongest signal reduction compared to the other vaccines. (Fig. 1C). This result supports the notion that anchoring OspA via its N-terminus results in a presentation more reflective of the native molecule and better presentation of the LA-2 epitope, thus eliciting a more potent immune response. For these reasons, the Nterm-TM OspA construct was selected as the leading candidate vaccine.

A heptavalent OspA serotype mRNA vaccine does not interfere with immune response against B. burgdorferi

While OspA ST1 is the dominant ST in B. burgdorferi isolates in North America, there are at least six additional OspA STs circulating in Europe, represented by Borrelia genospecies B. burgdorferi, B. garinii, B. afzelii, and B. bavariensis34. Therefore, we generated mRNA LNPs encoding the other six major OspA STs (ST2-7) each with the same N-terminal transmembrane domains used for OspA ST1 (Supplementary Table 2) and investigated the antigenicity of a heptavalent formulation. Expression of each of the OspA serotypes was confirmed by probing with 857-2, a mAb directed against a conserved epitope (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Groups of mice were vaccinated intramuscularly on days 1 and 21 with 1 μg of OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP, recombinant lipidated OspA ST1 protein (Recombitek) adjuvanted with alum, 3.5 μg of OspA ST1–7 mRNA-LNP (0.5 μg per ST), or 7 μg of alum-adjuvanted, non-lipidated recombinant OspA ST1-7 proteins (1 μg per ST). Recombitek was adjuvanted with alum to create a control that most closely resembles the original LYMErixTM and because alum has been demonstrated to enhance the immune response to OspA35. Comparator vaccination with non-lipidated OspA ST1-7 recombinant proteins, adjuvanted with alum, was administered as well to evaluate the immunogenicity of the non-lipidated recombinant protein relative to the non-lipidated protein expressed from the mRNA constructs. Serum samples collected on day 36 were assessed for OspA ST-specific IgG titers.

The heptavalent OspA mRNA-LNP vaccine elicited ST1-7 reactive antibodies that were significantly (p < 0.0001) greater than those induced by recombinant OspA ST1-7 protein vaccines (Fig. 2A). This dramatic reduction is likely due to the lack of lipidation of the recombinant proteins, as the lipidated Recombitek adjuvanted with alum elicited higher total OspA ST1 IgG titers than the non-lipidated protein. Interestingly, the level of total OspA ST1 IgG elicited with the Recombitek was significantly less than the titer resulting from OspA ST mRNA-LNP immunization (Fig. 2A), further suggesting that the mRNA-LNP delivery can compensate for the lack of lipidation on the OspA protein encoded by the mRNA. Moreover, there was no evidence for interference, as the levels of OspA ST1 antibodies elicited by the heptavalent OspA ST1-7 mRNA-LNP vaccine were not significantly different than the monovalent OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP vaccine (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the heptavalent OspA mRNA-LNP vaccine induced levels of LA-2 equivalent antibodies similar to the monovalent vaccine (Fig. 2B) and significantly greater than those elicited by recombinant lipidated monovalent and non-lipidated heptavalent OspA protein.

OspA mRNA-LNP vaccination induces complement-independent activities against B. burgdorferi

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain how OspA antibodies inhibit tick-mediated transmission of B. burgdorferi, including complement-mediated borreliacidal activity, spirochete agglutination, and antibody-induced alterations in spirochete membrane integrity33,36–40. To characterize the nature of antibodies induced following OspA mRNA-LNP vaccination, sera from mice immunized with two doses of either monovalent or heptavalent OspA mRNA-LNP vaccines or with an equivalent amount of alum-adjuvanted recombinant protein were first evaluated for the ability to recognize native OspA on the surface of B. burgdorferi B31 (ST1). Using flow cytometry and indirect immunofluorescence staining, we found that monovalent and heptavalent OspA mRNA-LNP vaccination in mice elicited antibodies that bound live B. burgdorferi cells as effectively as those from mice vaccinated with alum-adjuvanted lipidated Recombitek OspA protein, and significantly higher than antibodies from alum-adjuvanted non-lipidated OspA ST1–7 protein vaccination (Fig. 3A).

Additionally, sera from OspA mRNA-LNP vaccinated mice promoted spirochete agglutination, as evidenced by an increase in forward- and side-scatter properties of the bacteria when examined by flow cytometry (Fig. 3B)40. While OspA mRNA-LNP vaccination elicited higher levels of agglutination than the alum-adjuvanted Recombitek, variability was observed within and across all groups, likely due to the use of polyclonal sera in a live bacteria-based assay. Statistical significance was only achieved between the OspA ST1-7 mRNA-LNP and the alum-adjuvanted Recombitek groups (Fig. 3B). Finally, sera from OspA mRNA-LNP vaccinated mice induced greater alterations in spirochete outer membrane permeability than did sera from mice immunized with and equivalent dose of OspA protein (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, while only the 7 μg OspA ST1-7 mRNA-LNP group elicited significantly higher readouts compared to 1 μg of alum-adjuvanted Recombitek, the 1 μg OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP group did reach significance when compared to mice immunized with a 2 μg dose of alum-adjuvanted Recombitek (data not shown). From these data we conclude that OspA mRNA-LNP immunization induces antibodies that can recognize native OspA ST1 on B. burgdorferi and cause bacterial agglutination and changes in outer membrane integrity.

OspA mRNA-LNP vaccination elicits potent complement-dependent serum borreliacidal activity against B. burgdorferi

We next examined the ability of the monovalent and heptavalent OspA mRNA-LNP vaccines to elicit complement-dependent serum borreliacidal activity (SBA). Using an IPTG-inducible fluorescent viability reporter strain of B. burgdorferi B31-5A441, we observed significantly higher SBA titers (EC50) associated with sera derived from OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP vaccinated mice compared to OspA ST1 protein-immunized mice (Fig. 3D). In fact, sera from mice vaccinated with the ST1-7 protein cocktail had no detectable bactericidal activity, even though the serum-derived antibodies were capable of recognizing OspA on the surface of B. burgdorferi strain B31 as determined by flow cytometry. Collectively, these results demonstrate that vaccination with OspA mRNA-LNPs elicits a functional serum IgG antibody response against B. burgdorferi strain B31. These vaccine-elicited anti-OspA bactericidal antibodies may promote clearance in the event that the spirochetes are able to exit the tick and are successfully transmitted into the human host, suggesting a dual antibody firewall: one potentially preventing spirochete egress from the midgut and another clearing the bacteria should they be transmitted to the human host.

OspA mRNA-LNP vaccination elicits protective immunity in a mouse model of tick-mediated B. burgdorferi infection

Given that vaccination with OspA mRNA-LNP produces high titers of IgG with a variety of effector functions, we next sought to test whether our monovalent OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP or heptavalent OspA ST1-7 mRNA-LNP protects mice from B. burgdorferi via an infected tick challenge model.

To explore an optimal dosage and regimen to afford protection, we immunized mice with either the monovalent OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP or heptavalent OspA ST1–7 mRNA-LNP at a variety of doses given either twice (on days 1 and 21) or three times (on days 1, 21, and 42). For each study, we included a control group immunized with 1 μg of lipidated OspA protein (Recombitek) adjuvanted with alum. Mice were challenged with five B. burgdorferi B31-infected ticks two weeks after receiving their final vaccine dose. Two weeks later, the mice were euthanized, and skin, hearts, ears, ankles, and bladders were collected and cultured for four weeks to detect the presence of viable spirochetes. Results were further evaluated by assessing seroconversion using antibody reactivity against the B. burgdorferi proteins DpbB and OspC-A (C10) (Supplementary Fig. 2). Nearly complete protection was achieved even with two doses of 0.2 μg of the monovalent OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP or two doses of 0.7 μg of the heptavalent OspA ST1–7 mRNA-LNP, (0.1 μg per ST) (Fig. 4A, B), suggesting that the immune response elicited by OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP is highly potent even at very low doses. The observation that mice remain protected across all tested doses of the OspA ST1-7 mRNA-LNP suggests that the inclusion of six additional OspA mRNAs does not diminish the protective efficacy of the OspA ST1 mRNA.

To further evaluate the protective efficacy of our OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP vaccine, mice were immunized with two doses of progressively lower amounts of OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP ranging from 1 μg to 0.01 μg. Mice were protected from infection at a dose of 0.05 μg with only one mouse becoming infected. Most of the protection was lost at the 0.01 μg dose with four of the five mice becoming infected (Fig. 4C).

Functional antibody readouts from the OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP dose de-escalation study revealed that even low levels of binding and functional antibodies can protect mice from infection (Fig. 4D–I). Notably, while binding antibody, percentage spirochete agglutination and propidium iodide incorporation functional antibody readouts at lower levels were similar in some protected and infected mice, a clear differentiation was observed in SBA titers and LA-2 equivalent antibody levels between the two groups (Fig. 4E, I). This result reflects the previous observation that LA-2 is a key epitope for protection42 and also suggests that complement-mediated bactericidal antibodies may play a role in OspA mRNA-LNP vaccine-elicited protection.

With the exception of the 0.01 µg group in the OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP dose de-escalation study, mRNA-vaccinated mice were significantly more likely to be protected from infection than non-vaccinated mice by pairwise Fisher’s exact test (Table 2). Interestingly, in all challenge studies, mice that received no vaccine typically had all three organ cultures or co-cultures test positive for spirochetes, consistent with widespread dissemination following tick bite (Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, vaccinated mice that became infected generally had only one or two tissues positive, indicating a substantially reduced spirochete burden. While not quantitative, this pattern suggests that immunization with OspA mRNA-LNP significantly limits the extent of infection, even in the context of a stringent challenge involving five ticks, approximately 90% of which are infected, representing a high infectious dose. Notably, in some experiments, a single vaccinated mouse may exhibit infection in only one tissue at a given dose, while in other replicates of the same or lower dose group, no infection is observed. This variability likely reflects the occasional escape of very low numbers of spirochetes, further supporting the robustness of protection conferred by the vaccine under high challenge pressure.

Discussion

The mRNA platform has emerged as a novel vaccine technology against infectious agents. The COVID-19 mRNA vaccines have proven to be highly effective and safe across all age groups and have sparked significant interest in the mRNA platform and its potential broad applicability beyond infectious disease43,44. Furthermore, the development of mRNA vaccines encoding for multiple antigens highlighted the versatility of the platform and its advantages for the development of combination vaccines45,46. This feature is particularly crucial for diseases caused by infectious agents prevalent in diverse geographical locations and with multiple serotypes. Lyme borreliosis, caused by various Borrelia genospecies, is one such disease and therefore an attractive target for this technology47. In this study, we examined an mRNA vaccine encoding the Lyme disease antigen, OspA, a structurally unique molecule, composed of 21 anti-parallel beta-strands and a single C-terminal alpha-helix31. Our findings indicate that mRNA-encoded OspA ST1 expressed in mammalian cells is structurally and antigenically indistinguishable from an E. coli-expressed OspA ST1, previously used in human clinical studies. Notably, key transmission blocking epitopes defined by LA-2 and 857-2 are both preserved.

While both soluble and Cterm-TM presentations of OspA ST1 mRNA-LNP have been examined by other groups23,24, this work represents the initial investigation of the impacts of Nterm-TM and ferritin-linked OspA ST1 on the resulting immune responses and protection. Our data show that preserving the protein’s natural orientation (Nterm-TM) resulted in superior levels of antibodies that compete with the protective LA-2 mAb compared to other orientations in the mouse immunization model. This result is perhaps unsurprising given previous studies demonstrating protection is antibody-mediated, and that antibodies targeting the C-terminal region of the protein are bactericidal42,48,49. The selected Nterm-TM construct elicited a robust functional antibody response with equivalent antibody levels induced by a protective dose of Recombitek adjuvanted with alum, mimicking the efficacious LYMErix vaccine.

Although mRNA vaccines are capable of inducing both humoral and cellular immune responses, protection mediated by OspA-based vaccines is antibody-dependent. OspA is expressed by Borrelia in the tick midgut and downregulated after transmission to the mammalian host, such that protective immunity primarily relies on transmission-blocking antibodies that prevent spirochete migration during tick feeding11,37,50. Furthermore, clinical studies identified antibody correlates or protection49. Accordingly, while prior work demonstrated that OspA ST1 mRNA vaccination induces CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses in mice23, this study focused on the detailed characterization of humoral immune function as the principal mediator of efficacy. Various antibody functional properties were observed to be elicited in a dose-dependent manner and correlated with protection in the tick-bite challenge model. While some of the mice that were protected and not protected have similarly low levels of ST-1-specific binding antibodies or spirochete-agglutinating antibodies, there is a greater differentiation of the higher levels of LA-2 equivalent and bactericidal antibodies in protected mice, suggesting these functional readouts may provide more insight into the mechanisms of protection. Protection was evaluated at peak immunogenicity, with challenge performed two weeks after the final dose; therefore, these studies were designed to assess maximal protective capacity rather than durability of immunity. Ongoing systems serology studies with a larger number of mice vaccinated with both protein-based and mRNA-LNP OspA vaccines aim to clarify the mechanism of protection and inform key clinical trial readouts.

We also reported that a multivalent, full-length OspA mRNA-LNP vaccine encoding ST1–7, relevant to both U.S. and European Borrelia species, is immunogenic and protective in mice. While these STs share some structural homology, key differences, particularly in the C-terminal region where known bactericidal epitopes are located, suggest that encoding a single OspA ST is unlikely to provide broad protection against the diverse serotypes in circulation31. A multivalent approach may result in biased expression of select mRNAs or immunological interference where the responses to one or multiple antigens are diminished, a known phenomenon in multi-component vaccines that may occur in multivalent vaccine compositions51–53. However, our preliminary data suggest that the expression and immunogenicity of OspA ST1 mRNA is not adversely affected when administered with OspA ST2-7 mRNAs, paving the way for a multivalent mRNA vaccine strategy. The inclusion of ST2–7, which are associated with B. afzelii, B. garinii, and B. bavariensis, is intended to broaden geographic and genospecies coverage. Additionally, while each ST is distinct, there is a degree of cross-reactivity among these STs, especially within the N-terminal regions, including epitope 857-2. The full-length OspA proteins encoded by the multivalent OspA ST1-7 mRNA-LNP vaccine include these conserved, cross-reactive and protective epitopes that may lend to enhanced cross-protection. In this study, protective efficacy was evaluated using a B. burgdorferi ST1 tick challenge model. Therefore, the breadth of protection against additional genospecies remains to be directly established. Nevertheless, the absence of immunological interference and the induction of robust ST-specific antibody responses support the feasibility of a multivalent mRNA approach for expanded coverage. There were several limitations to this study. Although the heptavalent vaccine elicited robust ST-specific antibody responses without evidence of interference, further studies are required to define the protective performance in mice of the multivalent formulation across Borrelia genospecies. Ongoing efforts will evaluate the protective capacity of OspA ST1-7 mRNA-LNP for other ST using both Ixodes ricinus tick-bite challenge models and ST2-7 expressing target strains in the SBA assay. Durability of protection was not evaluated in the present study, as animals were challenged at peak immunogenicity. Persistence of immunogenicity and functional antibody responses is being assessed in the ongoing Phase 1/2 clinical study (NCT05975099), which will inform the duration of immunity in humans. Finally, although the in vitro immunoassays provide insight into protective thresholds in mice, these studies were not powered to establish a quantitative cutoff. Breakthrough infections occurred primarily at the lowest dose tested, limiting the number and distribution of infected animals needed for robust threshold estimation. Ongoing repeat, multi-dose challenge studies using standardized assays are expected to more precisely define protective thresholds and strengthen the quantitative relationship between functional antibody activity and protection.

In summary, our data demonstrate that an mRNA vaccine that encodes for OspA ST1 presented on the mammalian cell surface via an N-terminal transmembrane anchor promotes an effective immune response similar to that elicited by a protein-based vaccine representative of one shown to be efficacious against Lyme borreliosis in humans. Furthermore, this approach can be expanded to include the major circulating OspA STs without diminishing the response to any one of the STs. The safety and immunogenicity of these vaccines are currently being evaluated in a clinical trial (NCT05975099).

Methods

mRNA designs

OspA sequences from representative strains of serotypes 1-7 (Supplementary Table 2) were used as template sequences for mRNA designs. For all designs, the first 25 NH2-terminal amino acids were excluded to remove the lipidation motif. For soluble OspA designs, the NH2-terminal signal sequence from influenza haemagglutinin (GenBank ID: AYM50401.1)54 was incorporated to ensure proper trafficking and exportation from the cell. For OspA designs with a C-terminal transmembrane anchor, the influenza hemagglutinin transmembrane domain (GenBank ID ACD47243.1)55,56 was incorporated into the COOH-terminus of the protein. For OspA designs with an N-terminal transmembrane anchor, the transmembrane domain from influenza neuraminidase was incorporated (GenBank ID: AHL98203.1)57. All designs had predicted N-linked glycosylation sites removed (Supplementary Table 2). OspA ST1 mRNA designs have additional mutations to the putative hLFA-1 domain (Y165F, V166I, T170R, and L171F).

In vitro mRNA expression

OspA mRNA ST1-7 TM constructs were assessed for in vitro expression using Expi293F suspension cells (ThermoFisher) as described previously58,59. Briefly, 500 ng of unformulated mRNA was transfected into Expi293F cells according to TransIT-mRNA transfection kit (Mirus). Cells were incubated with shaking at 37 °C and 8% CO2 for 48 hours (transmembrane constructs) or 72 hours (secreted constructs). For analysis of membrane-bound antigen expression, cells were harvested, blocked, stained with 212-55 or 857-241,60,61 as the primary antibody and a DyLight650-labeled anti-human IgG secondary, in addition to an Aqua viability dye (BD Biosciences). Flow cytometry was performed on an iQue3 instrument (Sartorius) with a sip rate of 15 µL/s for 5 seconds. Data analysis was performed using ForeCyt software (Sartorius) by gating on cells, singlets, viable cells, and positive cells to determine antigen positivity rates and median fluorescence intensities (MFI) for construct expression comparisons and further analyzed using Prism software (GraphPad). For analysis of ST1 secreted constructs, supernatants, and protein standards were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. LA-2 was used as the primary antibody with DyLight 650-labeled anti-human IgG as a secondary. The membrane was then visualized with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (BioRad)

Purification of OspA nanoparticles

OspA nanoparticles were purified based on a method previously described by Koho et al. (2012) with modifications to accommodate mRNA-expressed material in mammalian cells62. Briefly, Expi293 cells were harvested 72 hours post-transfection and lysed by sonication in Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 8.0). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation, and nanoparticles were precipitated by incubation with 7% PEG 6000 and 2% NaCl (w/v) overnight at 4 °C. Precipitated material was collected by centrifugation and resuspended in Tris-HCl buffer.

Nanoparticles were further purified by anion exchange chromatography on a HiTrap Q HP column using a step gradient of NaCl. Peak fractions eluting at 20% NaCl were pooled, concentrated by ultrafiltration (100 kDa MWCO), and dialyzed into PBS prior to sample preparation and imaging via negative stain electron microscopy as described previously by Deal et al. (2023)63.

mRNA and LNP synthesis

For each vaccine, T7 RNA polymerase-mediated transcription was used in vitro to synthesize the mRNA from a linearized DNA template, which flanked the immunogen open-reading frames with the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions and a poly-A tail as described previously64. mRNA was then purified, diluted in citrate buffer to the desired concentration, and encapsulated into lipid nanoparticles (LNP) by ethanol drop nanoprecipitation. At a molar ratio of 50:10:38.5:1.5 (ionizable lipid:DSPC:cholesterol:PEG-lipid), lipids were dissolved in ethanol and combined with a 6.25-mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 5) containing mRNA at a ratio of 3:1 (aqueous:ethanol). Formulations were dialyzed against phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) for at least 18 hours, concentrated using Amicon ultracentrifugal filters (EMD Millipore), passed through a 0.22-μm filter and stored at −20 °C until use. All formulations underwent quality control for particle size, RNA encapsulation, and endotoxin. LNP were between 80 – 100 nm in size, with > 90% encapsulation on mRNA and < 10 EU/mL endotoxin. No additional adjuvants were mixed with the final mRNA-LNP formulations.

Mouse immunizations and serum collection

Female C3H mice (Charles River, Wilmington, MA) were immunized intramuscularly in the hind leg quadriceps with 50 μL of the indicated vaccine formulation and doses on days 1 and 21 for two-dose studies, and days 1, 21, and 42 for three-dose studies. Mice were conscious and dosed without the use of anesthesia. Sera were collected at the indicated timepoints and two weeks post-final dose. Individual serum samples were used for all antibody assays. When sacrificing mice for serum collection, euthanasia was performed with carbon dioxide and confirmed by cervical dislocation. Animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines65. Animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (IACUC) at Tufts University and Moderna.

Luminex Assay

Recombinant OspA ST1–7 proteins were coupled to Luminex Magplex carboxylated microspheres (Luminex Corporation) using EDC-NHS (1-ethyl-3-[3-dimethylaminopropyl]carbodiimide hydrochloride-N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide) (ThermoFisher Scientific) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2.5 ×106 beads were transferred into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes following 10 s of vigorous vortexing. Beads were placed on a magnetic rack, the storage buffer was removed, and the beads were washed once with 100 μL distilled H2O. The beads were then resuspended in 80 μL of 100 mM sodium phosphate monobasic (pH 6.2, activation buffer). Sulfo-NHS and EDC were diluted to 50 mg/mL in activation buffer and vortexed at a low speed. These solutions were prepared immediately prior to the addition of the beads. Subsequently, 5 μL each of sulfo-NHS and EDC were added to the tube containing the beads and briefly vortexed. The beads were covered with aluminum foil, mixed end-over-end for 20 min at room temperature, and washed three times. For each wash, the beads were placed on the magnet. After allowing the beads to collect on the magnet, the buffer was removed, and 200 μL 50 mM MES (2-[N-morpholino] ethanesulfonic acid) pH 5 (coupling buffer) was added; the beads were then vortexed briefly to mix and placed back on the magnet. Following the washes, 50 pmol per million beads of each antigen of interest (Lyme OspA ST 1–7) were added to the beads, and the mixture was brought to a final volume of 250 μL in coupling buffer. The beads were covered in foil and mixed end-over-end for 2 hours at room temperature. Similar to the aforementioned process, the beads were washed three times in 200 μL 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)-TBN (1X PBS, 0.1% bovine serum albumin, 0.05% TWEEN-20, 0.1% sodium azide). The beads were resuspended in 200 μL 1X PBS-TBN, vortexed briefly, covered in foil, and mixed end-over-end for 30 min at room temperature to block the beads. The beads were then washed once in 200 μL PBS-TBN and stored at 4 °C until use.

The Luminex assay was performed according to a previous study58. Samples were serially diluted in a 384-well plate, starting from 1:100 with sixteen 2-fold serial dilutions. A single serum dilution was selected from this and used for subsequent analyses. The final bead mixture was prepared in assay wash buffer (1X PBS, 0.1% BSA, 0.05% TWEEN-20). The beads were mixed to a final concentration of 1000 beads per region per well in a final volume of 45 μL per well and vortexed at maximum speed for 30 s immediately prior to addition to the plate. Diluted serum was added to a black, clear-bottom 384-well plate at 5 μL per well, and 45 μL of the vortexed bead mix was added to generate a final concentration of 1:50,000. Plates were then sealed with a foil plate cover, wrapped in aluminum foil, and incubated with shaking at 900 rpm for 2 hours at room temperature. Following this, the plate was washed six times in 60 μL assay wash buffer using a 405TS BioTek Microplate Washer (BioTek). Goat anti-mouse IgG coupled with R-phycoerythrin (R-PE) (Southern Biotechnology) was diluted to a final concentration of 0.65 μg/mL in assay wash buffer and added at 40 μL per well. The plate was covered with a foil plate cover, vortexed for 30 s, and sonicated in a bath sonicator for 30 s. This step was repeated once to resuspend the beads in solution and break up bead aggregates. Following vortexing and sonication, the plate was covered with aluminum foil and incubated on the orbital plate shaker for 1 hour with shaking at 900 rpm. The plate was washed in assay wash buffer as stated above, and Luminex Sheath Fluid Plus (Luminex Corporation) was added to the plate at 40 μL per well. The plates were heat sealed and run on a FlexMap 3D (Luminex Corporation) using enhanced PMT (photomultiplier tube) reporter gain to measure median fluorescence intensity (MFI). Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1), and the limit of detection (LOD) was calculated using an average of the assay blanks.

LA-2 competitive ELISA

MaxiSorp 96-well microtiter plates (Thermo Fisher, 439454) were coated at 4 °C overnight with recombinant OspA ST1 [1 µg/mL] in PBS. The plates were then washed four times with PBS + 0.05% Tween-20, blocked for 90 min at 37 °C with 200 µL Superblock (Thermo Fisher, 37515) per well, and washed four times again. After the final wash, 50 µL of assay diluent (1 × PBST + 5% goat serum) was added to all wells in the plate. Pre-dilutions of the standard (a starting dilution of 10 µg/mL, followed by 6 × two-fold serial dilutions of LA-2 mAb [custom synthesized by GenScript; Piscataway, NJ], and the test samples were prepared in polypropylene dilution tubes in assay diluent. Subsequently, 50 µL of pre-dilutions were added to the assay plate so that the maximum dilution for each sample was a 1:10 dilution, and two-fold serial dilutions were generated by transferring 50 µL of the sample to 50 µL assay diluent down the assay plate. Plates were incubated for 1 hour at 37 °C, and then 50 µL biotinylated LA-2 monoclonal antibody (generated using ThermoFisher cat#A39256 according to manufacturer’s instructions) at a concentration of 40 ng/ml was added to each well. Plates were incubated for 1 hour at 37 °C, washed four times with PBS + 0.05% TWEEN-20, and 100 µL of horse radish peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin (Southern Biotech, 7105-05) was added at a 1:10,000 dilution in assay diluent. Plates were incubated for 1 hour at 37 °C, washed four times with PBS + 0.05% TWEEN-20, and 100 µL of TMB (3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine) substrate was added to each well. Plates were incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Reactions were stopped by adding 100 µL/well TMB stop solution, and absorbance was measured at optical density (OD) 450 nm using an ELISA plate reader (Molecular Device microplate reader). The amount of functional LA-2 antibody raised by OspA vaccines to block OspA/LA-2 binding was calculated as a signal reduction percentage when including serum at a 1:20 dilution.

Analysis and LA-2 equivalent antibody level calculations were performed in Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism (version 9.2.0) as follows for each assay plate: (1) The average blank for each plate was calculated, and the average of the blank was subtracted from all wells. (2) The average reading for the PBS control group was calculated. (3) The % signal reduction was determined using the following formula: % signal reduction = (1 − [OD of serum samples/average OD of PBS]) × 100.

Data were inspected using the following inspection rules: (1) The OD of blank wells (no biotinylated LA-2) should have been less than 0.1 OD units for all assay plates. In general, the OD would be less than 0.05 OD units. (2) The unlabeled/un-biotinylated antibody itself served as a competitive positive control; if no competition was observed, the plate would need to be re-assayed. (3) If the OD of the sample was less than or equal to the OD of the blank, we reported the percentage signal reduction as 100%. (4) If the OD of the samples was greater than or equal to the average OD of the PBS samples group, we reported the percentage signal reduction as 0%.

Crystal structure determination and antibody affinity determination

Protein Expression and purification

The open reading frame of OspA-ST1, containing mutations to modify hLFA-1 region and N-linked glycosylation sites and C-term tandem 6xHistidine-StrepTag II affinity tags, was codon optimized, synthesized and cloned into pCDNA3.4 vector (Genscript). A thrombin cleavage site was introduced between the OspA ST1 and the affinity tags to allow removal of the affinity tags when needed. The expression vector was transfected into EXPI293 cells using transfection reagent Turbo293 (SPEED Biosystems), and the culture was grown at 33 °C, 125 RPM, 8% CO2 for 5 days. The cultures were then harvested by centrifuging at 5500 x g for 1 hr and cleared supernatant was subjected to affinity purification using Strep-Tactin XT 4flow resin. The affinity tags were cleaved using 1U/mg of Thrombin protease and further characterized by size exclusion chromatography with and without the presence of antibody Fabs. The prepared complexes were used in crystallization experiments.

OspA ST1 was complexed with LA-2 Fab at a 1:1.05 molar ratio and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The complex was then dialyzed into distilled water and concentrated to 15 mg/mL with a 50 kDa Amicon concentrator for crystallization studies. The protein complex was screened at 20 °C with an SPT Labtech Mosquito using commercial screens with 100 nL protein to 100 nL reservoir, and a condition producing crystals was found (0.2 M magnesium acetate tetrahydrate, 20% (w/v) PEG 3350). The condition was further optimized to 0.1 M magnesium acetate tetrahydrate and 10% (w/v) PEG 3350 in hanging drop plates to produce high-quality crystals for diffraction experiments. The crystals were harvested in nylon loops and frozen in a solution containing the reservoir solution supplemented with 25% (v/v) glycerol as cryogenic protectant. Diffraction data were collected at the Canadian Light Source beamline CMCF-ID using a Dectris Eiger X 9 M detector.

Data was processed using XDS66,67 and AIMLESS68 to 1.85 Å. Crystal presents merohedral twinning with two domains at fractions of 0.495:0.505 and appeared with P6122 space group The correct crystal space group was determined to be P61 with unit cell dimensions: a = 68.5, b = 68.5, c = 619.6 (α = β = 90°, γ = 120°). The structure was determined with molecular replacement methodology using PDB entry 1FJ131 with PHASER69 in CCP4 suite70. REFMAC571 (also in CCP4 suite) was used for refinement and COOT72 for model building. Intensity-based twin refinement was used in REFMAC5. Two OspA ST1 and LA-2 complexes were observed in each asymmetric unit. The structure was deposited into the PDB with entry code 9CDS.

Affinities between OspA ST1 and the Fab versions of 184.1, 857-2, and LA-2 antibodies were determined by first conjugating OspA ST1 with intact affinity tags onto NTA biosensors on an Octet HTX instrument (Sartorius), followed by buffer wash, and then association/dissociation with titrated concentrations of respective antibodies in Fab format to avoid the avidity effect. The binding curves were analyzed and fitted by using the Octet BLI Systems Software (Sartorius). Phospho-buffered saline was used as buffer for all steps in the BLI experiments.

IgG binding, agglutination, and B. burgdorferi membrane permeability studies

Bacterial cell culturing and flow cytometry were performed as described previously40,73, with slight modifications. Briefly, B. burgdorferi strain B31 (ATCC, American Type Culture Collection) was cultured in BSK-II media (minus gelatin) (Wadsworth Center Media Core) at 33 °C with 2% CO2 to mid-log phase, then diluted 1:10 in media, and grown at 23 °C to early log phase. Cells were collected by centrifugation (3300 × g), washed with PBS, resuspended in media (minus phenol red), and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. A total of 5 ×106 cells in 50 µL were incubated with mouse serum at a final dilution of 1:200 at 37 °C for 1 hour. Incubation with 10 µg/ml of anti-OspA antibody chimeric LA-2 was run as a positive control. Reaction volumes were increased with the addition of 450 µL of media (minus phenol red). For detection of bacterial surface-bound antibodies, the cells were then incubated with goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen) (for chimeric LA-2 control, goat anti-human IgG [H + L] cross-adsorbed secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen)) at a 1:500 dilution at 37 °C for 30 min, and then transferred to a 5 mL round bottom polystyrene test tube. Subsequently, 250 µl of PBS was added, followed by 0.75 µM propidium iodide (PI) (Sigma) for the detection of membrane permeability immediately before analysis on a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). Cells were gated on forward scatter and side scatter to assess aggregate size and granularity. Alexa Fluor 647 fluorescence, PI staining, and agglutination were measured on 20,000 events using CellQuest Pro software (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). Background reactivity of the PBS-immunized mouse serum for fluorescence and PI staining was considered baseline. Agglutination was calculated by the sum of events in the upper-left, upper-right, and lower-right quadrants relative to total events counted, as shown previously40.

Serum bactericidal activity (SBA) assay

Mouse serum samples were examined for complement-dependent borreliacidal activity using a previously described fluorescence-based viability assay41, with several modifications. Briefly, 1 mL frozen aliquots of a recombinant B. burgdorferi B31-5A4 strain harboring an IPTG-inducible mScarlet-I reporter plasmid (GGW979) were thawed and transferred to 50 mL centrifuge tubes containing 45 mL of BSK-II supplemented with 40 µg/mL of gentamicin. Normalized fluorescence readouts generated from this strain have been demonstrated to have a linear correlation to the OD600 of the bacterial culture (Supplementary Fig. 3). Spirochete cultures were then incubated for 72 h at 33 °C without agitation. On the day of each assay, spirochetes were collected via centrifugation at 4000 x g, the culture media removed, and the cells were resuspended in phenol-free BSK-II supplemented with 20% guinea pig complement (Sigma) and 40 µg/mL of gentamicin at 3 × 107 spirochetes per mL.

Mouse serum samples were incubated at 56 °C for 30 minutes to heat-inactivate endogenous complement prior to serial dilution in phenol-free BSKII containing guinea pig complement (20%) and gentamicin (40 μg/mL). 95 µL of diluted sera were then transferred to white 96-well flat-bottom tissue culture plates (Costar) containing 95 µL of the spirochete culture per well and mixed via pipetting. Following sample addition, each 190 µL reaction contained 1.5 × 106 spirochetes per well and final sera dilutions between 1:800 and 1:51,200 for mRNA and Recombitek immunized groups, or between 1:100 and 1:3,200 for OspA ST1-7 protein immunized groups. Each assay plate also included an OspA-specific borreliacidal monoclonal antibody treatment group (LA-2) to serve as a control for spirochete death, and an untreated/non-IPTG induced spirochete control to determine baseline/background fluorescence.

After serum addition, assay plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Twenty hours later, 1 mM of IPTG was added to the appropriate wells to induce mScarlet-I expression in surviving spirochetes. Assay plates were then incubated for 48 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Seventy-two hours post-serum addition, Median Fluorescence Intensity (MFI) was recorded three times per plate at 569 nm (Ex)/611 nm (Em) using a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (Molecular Biosystems) and SoftMax Pro version 7.1 software. The resulting values were averaged, and the data from each assay plate (set of ten mice) was then independently normalized using the untreated/non-induced control and PBS-immunized mouse dilution series to set baseline (0) and peak (100) MFI, respectively. Following normalization, the data were analyzed using Graphpad Prism Version 9.0. Samples below the lower limit of detection were assigned a value equal to half of the lowest dilution tested for graphical purposes only and were not used to infer quantitative titer values.

Tick-mediated mouse challenge with B. burgdorferi

C3H/HeN mice were challenged with B. burgdorferi via infected tick feeding as described previously19 two weeks post final immunization. On the day prior, a patch of exposed skin was created by shaving the upper back of each mouse using an electric shaver. I. scapularis nymphs previously fed on SCID mice infected with B. burgdorferi strain B31, and with a greater than 75% positive infection rate by culture, were used. Capsules were constructed using microcentrifuge tubes, mesh, and wax made of 1:4 beeswax:rosin gum. The constructed capsule was cylindrical with a diameter of 10 mm, a height of 5 mm, and a breathable mesh cap secured in place with wax. Capsules were attached to the shaved patch of skin on each mouse using the same wax used to bind the capsule frame and mesh. Once the capsule was attached, a temporary hole was opened in the capsule mesh using fine-tip forceps, and five ticks were placed inside the capsule to feed. Once all ticks were in place, the hole in the mesh was closed using forceps and a small piece of tape. The capsules were removed after 4 days, and tick feeding was monitored for up to 1 week. Ticks were allowed to naturally fall off after feeding.

Organs were harvested 2 weeks following the recovery of the last engorged tick. A skin biopsy from the site of infection, the heart, ear, ankle, and bladder were removed from each mouse following CO2 inhalation and cervical dislocation. A sample tissue of each of these organs was placed into Barbour-Stonner-Kelly (BSK-II) media supplemented with rifampicin, amphotericin B, and phosphomycin and incubated at 37 °C. The heart was cultured on its own in 1.2 mL of media; the ankle and bladder were cultured together in 1.8 mL of media, and the ear and skin were cultured together in 1.8 mL of media. The cultures were monitored by removing 10 μL of solution, which was placed on a slide with a coverslip, and a minimum of 20 fields were observed at 100× by darkfield microscopy for the presence of B. burgdorferi spirochetes. Presence of motile spirochetes observed at 100× was subsequently confirmed at 400×. Culture monitoring was repeated twice per week for 4 weeks. The presence of spirochetes was recorded for each tissue sample.

Serological analysis of B. burgdorferi challenged mice

Recombinant OspC-A (C10) and DbpB (residues 21 to 187; UniProt ID P0CL68) were coupled to MagPlex-C microspheres with spectral regions 16 and 19 (MC12016-01 and MC10019-04, respectively, as indicated above. Recombinant DbpB was kindly provided by Dr. Michael Rudolph (New York Structural Biology Center). For the Luminex assay, diluted microspheres (50 μL) and diluted serum or buffer (50 μL) were combined in black, clear-bottomed, non-binding, chimney 96-well plates (Greiner Bio-One, Monroe, NC) and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour in a tabletop shaker. Plates were washed three times using a magnetic separator and wash buffer (1 x PBS, 2% BSA, 0.02% TWEEN-20, 0.05% sodium azide, pH 7.4), then probed with goat anti-mouse IgG, human-ads-PE (SouthernBiotech, Birmingham, AL) diluted 1:500. Plates were analyzed using a FlexMap 3D (Luminex Corporation). To establish reactivity cutoffs for OspC-A (C10) and DbpB, the average median fluorescent intensity (MFI) of buffer-only wells was multiplied by six. MFIs for each mouse serum sample were divided by the antigen-specific reactivity cutoffs, yielding an index value. An index value greater than 1 suggests reactivity above background for the given antigen.
