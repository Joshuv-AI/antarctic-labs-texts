Wearable-derived measures of stress physiology in patients with opioid use disorder treated with methadone: A stress-task study in a real-world clinic with healthy controls
Authors: Tyler S. Oesterle, Yi Xiao, Nicholas L. Bormann, Dessa K. Bergen-Cico, Benjamin Lai, Harshit Sharma, Asif Salekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWearable biosensors enable real-time monitoring of physiological stress. While methadone maintenance treatment (MMT) is effective in opioid use disorder (OUD), inter-dose withdrawal and stress dysregulation remain challenges. We sought to identify wearable-derived digital biomarkers of stress in individuals with OUD receiving MMT.MethodsIn this cross-sectional, laboratory-based study, 72 adults completed 76 sessions (14 OUD pre-methadone dose, 15 OUD post-dose, 47 healthy controls) of a standardized protocol of calm tasks (e.g., relaxing videos, positive memory recall) followed by stress-inducing tasks (e.g., horror video, mental arithmetic, negative memory recall). Physiological data were continuously recorded using the Empatica E4 wristband (heart rate, heart rate variability [HRV], electrodermal activity [EDA], skin temperature, accelerometry). Features were extracted using open-source toolkits. Linear mixed-effects models tested calm–stress differences and group effects; Random Forest classifiers evaluated calm versus stress classification.ResultsAmong 340 extracted features, 63 (18.5%) significantly differentiated calm from stress across all groups, with EDA- and accelerometry-derived features showing the most consistent stress sensitivity. Compared with controls, OUD participants demonstrated higher baseline arousal and altered stress reactivity, with the largest responses observed in the pre-dose group. Random Forest models performed best for cognitively demanding tasks (up to 98% accuracy) and more modestly for other tasks.ConclusionsWearable sensors can detect multidimensional alterations in resting-state physiology and stress reactivity among individuals with OUD on methadone. Future studies should validate these candidate biomarkers and test whether integrating wearable-derived stress detection into treatment improves clinically meaningful outcomes, such as stress management, craving, risk of return to opioid use, and retention.

Introduction

Wearable remote monitoring uses biosensors to continuously track physiology. In chronic conditions like asthma, diabetes, and hypertension, wearable monitoring has improved disease self-management, facilitated earlier clinical assessment, enhanced life quality, and reduced hospitalizations and mortality (Paul et al., 2025, Peyroteo et al., 2021). Patients are generally receptive to biosensor monitoring by healthcare professionals and willing to share this data (Oesterle et al., 2022). There is growing interest in applying wearable technology to behavioral health and substance use disorders (SUD), where continuous physiological data could offer insights into stress regulation, relapse, and treatment response (Oesterle et al., 2022).

Opioid use disorder (OUD) is a chronic condition characterized by continued opioid use, craving, and impaired control despite accumulating harms. It is associated with alterations in reward, motivation, and stress regulation systems that increase vulnerability to returns to use (Koob and Volkow, 2016). Methadone maintenance treatment (MMT) is effective in reducing opioid cravings and supporting use cessation (Sordo et al., 2017). In addition to activating the µ-opioid receptor, methadone inhibits serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake, potentially influencing stress responses (Eap et al., 2002). However, methadone’s slow onset and wide inter-individual variability in elimination half-life can lead to inter-dose withdrawal and increased physiological stress (Oesterle et al., 2019). More than half of individuals receiving MMT report inter-dose withdrawal at least occasionally, and about one-third experience it frequently-to-consistently (Dyer and White, 1997). These symptoms are associated with heightened psychological stress, particularly when methadone levels nadir (Dyer et al., 1999).

Individuals with OUD often show altered baseline stress physiology and dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis in response to stressors (Shin et al., 2025, Sinha, 2008). Physiological stress responses include changes in heart rate (HR), blood pressure, electrodermal activity (EDA), and autonomic processes influenced by hormones (Russell and Lightman, 2019). Understanding these real-time physiological patterns may help clinicians identify periods of potential returns to use.

Recent advances in wearables allow researchers to capture stress-related signals in clinical contexts. Current devices can passively and continuously monitor HR, blood pressure, temperature, EDA, oxygen saturation, respiration rate (RR), electrocardiogram (ECG), and biochemicals in sweat (Oesterle et al., 2022). Historically, laboratories relied on in-house signal-processing pipelines to preprocess and feature-engineer wearable data, limiting reproducibility (Oesterle et al., 2022). Feature generation tooLkIt for weaRable daTa (FLIRT) addresses this through an open-source, standardized pipeline for wearable data feature extraction (Foll et al., 2021). To our knowledge, FLIRT has not been used in a SUD cohort.

Several wearable-sensor studies in opioid-exposed or substance-using individuals have examined physiological correlates of intoxication, craving, withdrawal, or stress (Carreiro et al., 2020, Carreiro et al., 2025, Hampton et al., 2025, Li et al., 2024, Pataca et al., 2025, Rigatti et al., 2023, Vos et al., 2023). However, it is unknown how stress manifests physiologically in people with OUD receiving MMT compared with healthy controls. The present study is intended to complement previous literature by providing a controlled, task-based framework for systematically comparing stress-related physiological responses across clinically relevant OUD states (control, pre-dose, and post-dose), with an emphasis on interpretable feature-level modeling. In addition, this approach uses a standardized, open-source feature-extraction toolkit, combined with linear mixed-effects modeling with Random Forest (RF) validation to link statistical characterization with practical discriminative performance.

Here, “arousal” refers to general physiological activation, such as changes in HR or EDA. “Stress” refers to task-evoked responses to the experimental paradigm. “Features” refers to the specific data streams within a distinct biosensor that reflect distinct measurable sensor changes. The following research questions (RQ) were asked:1.Can physiological features reliably differentiate calm versus stress conditions across participants?2.How do baseline physiological patterns differ between healthy controls and individuals with OUD?3.How do stress responses differ between healthy controls and individuals with OUD?4.Do healthy controls and individuals with OUD at different points in their dosing cycle differ in the magnitude or pattern of physiological change from calm to stress conditions?

Methods

Participants

72 participants completed 76 study sessions (14 OUD pre-dose, 15 OUD post-dose, and 47 healthy controls). Four participants contributed to both the OUD pre-dose and post-dose groups, with 1–3 months between their visits; therefore, analyses were conducted cross-sectionally rather than as paired within-subject comparisons.

Participants with OUD were recruited from the Crouse MMT program and were required to be in active methadone treatment for OUD. Control participants were recruited through community advertisements and could not have a history of OUD, be actively taking opioids, or be in structured SUD treatment. All participants were adults aged 18 years or older, fluent in English, and provided written informed consent prior to participation. All procedures were approved by the institutional review board of Syracuse University (#21–322).

Procedures

The experimental session followed a standardized block design adapted from established multimodal stress paradigms, including the Trier Social Stress Test (Allen et al., 2017, Dickerson and Kemeny, 2004). Participants completed a series of calm activities to establish a stable baseline, followed by tasks that engaged stress domains, cognitive load, emotional valence, and fear (Allen et al., 2017, Dickerson and Kemeny, 2004).

The calm block included a relaxing “jellyfish” video, a simple counting task, a positive memory recall exercise, and a nature video (Anderson et al., 2017, Ulrich et al., 1991, Xiao et al., 2025, Xiao et al., 2024). The subsequent stress block consisted of a horror-themed video, a brief song preparation period, a challenging mental arithmetic task, and a negative memory recall exercise (Brouwer and Hogervorst, 2014, Schaefer et al., 2010). Short rest periods were provided between tasks to permit partial physiological recovery. Together, these tasks created repeated periods of low and high stress with standardized duration and content across all participants (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study Task Timeline. Signal acquisition, feature extraction, and processing

Physiological data were collected using the Empatica E4 wristband, a research-grade wearable biosensor that continuously records multiple autonomic signals. The E4 was worn on the non-dominant wrist throughout the protocol, consistent with prior work indicating improved sensor validity (Ravindran et al., 2022, Schuurmans et al., 2020). The device recorded HR and inter-beat intervals via a blood volume pulse (BVP) sensor, EDA via skin conductance electrodes, peripheral skin temperature (TEMP) via an infrared sensor, and motion via three-axis accelerometry (ACC). All signals were time-stamped and sampled at manufacturer-specified frequencies. As the protocol was conducted in a controlled laboratory setting, participants were primarily standing in place during tasks, limiting large-amplitude movement during recording.

Because photoplethysmography (PPG) is susceptible to motion artifacts and is the optical sensing modality used to infer cardiovascular dynamics, signal-quality assessment centered on it. PPG quality was quantified using NeuroKit2’s ppg_quality function (method=“dissimilarity”), which measures how closely each cardiac pulse resembles neighboring pulses. Quality scores were uniformly high (mean=0.995 ± 0.005, range=0.92–1.00), and no segment fell below the pre-specified validity threshold, indicating that sensors’ waveform morphology remained stable and that motion-induced distortions were negligible.

After data collection, recordings were organized by task, and a uniform 2-minute analysis window was defined for each. For tasks longer than 2 min, the central 2-minute portion was used; for shorter tasks, recordings were padded symmetrically with adjacent data until they reached 2 min. The impromptu song-preparation task (30 s) was excluded from quantitative analysis because it would have required extensive padding. Analyses were therefore based on the remaining seven tasks. Feature extraction was performed using FLIRT and NeuroKit2, two open-source toolkits for wearable sensor analysis (Foll et al., 2021, Makowski et al., 2021). Within this pipeline, each 2-minute analysis window was converted into feature-level summary measures for statistical analysis, rather than modeled as raw continuous waveform samples. Across all channels, a common set of statistical and spectral features (e.g., mean, variance, signal energy, entropy) (Foll et al., 2021) and modality-specific metrics and counts (e.g., time- and frequency-domain HRV indices, ACC features) were derived (Makowski et al., 2021). Detailed descriptions of feature definitions for each physiological modality are in Appendix A.

Analytic strategy

Four calm–stress contrasts were defined: RQ1) all calm tasks versus all stress tasks; RQ2) calm versus stress video; RQ3) counting versus arithmetic; and RQ4) positive versus negative memory (see Procedures). Feature-level summary measures were extracted from each 2-minute analysis window and labeled as calm or stress based on task type.

Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) tested the planned feature-level contrasts while accounting for repeated 2-minute task windows within participant sessions. For each extracted feature, a separate model was fit using the feature value from each 2-minute analysis window as the dependent variable.

Fixed effects were specified according to the research question. Condition (calm vs. stress) was included for calm-versus-stress contrasts, group (healthy control, OUD pre-dose, OUD post-dose) was included for group comparisons, and condition-by-group interaction terms were included for analyses examining whether calm-to-stress changes differed across groups. Each model included a random intercept for participant-session identifier to account for within-subject dependence across repeated observations. For contrasts that did not require all terms, only the relevant fixed effects were included.

P-values for fixed effects were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, with adjusted p < 0.05 considered statistically significant (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995).

To assess robustness to potential demographic confounding, supplementary covariate-adjusted models additionally included age, gender, and race/ethnicity as fixed effects. A reduced-sample sensitivity analysis further incorporated recent nicotine-use status as well as pre-task self-reported stress and craving ratings.

To summarize feature-level findings across physiological modalities, the number and proportion of features meeting the statistical significance threshold were quantified (n/N, %). The distribution of absolute standardized beta coefficients from the feature-level mixed-effects models were used to compare effect size across modalities. Features were standardized within the all-task contrast, and the same model specification as in the primary calm-versus-stress analysis was applied.

Additionally, RF classifiers were used as a secondary validation analysis to assess whether physiological feature sets identified by the mixed-effects models retained practical discriminative value for calm-versus-stress classification. Model performance was estimated using 5-fold user-disjoint cross-validation (Gorriz et al., 2024, Saeb et al., 2017), such that each participant’s data appeared in only one fold.

For each task contrast, RF models were trained using both the full feature set and the subset of features identified as significant in the corresponding LMM analysis. For the all-task contrast, supplementary single-modality RF models were also fit separately for ACC, EDA, BVP, HR, HRV, and TEMP feature sets. Performance was summarized using accuracy, balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score. All analyses were conducted in Python.

Results

72 participants (52 women, 19 men, and 1 non-binary) were included, 25 with OUD and 47 healthy controls. Participants in the OUD group were generally older, with an average age of 35.7 years (SD=4.1, range 29–47), compared to the control group, which had an average age of 30.9 years (SD=9.9, range 22–65). Race and ethnicity were distributed as White (n = 42, 58.3%), Asian (n = 15, 20.8%), Black or African American (n = 10, 13.9%), Other (n = 3, 4.2%), and American Indian or Alaska Native (n = 2, 2.8%). Data completeness and physiological signal quality were high, and no segments were excluded based on quality criteria. As highlighted in each RQ below, participants exhibited clear physiological changes during stress tasks compared to calm tasks, with some features differentiating these states more effectively than others.

RQ2: group differences under calm conditions

Participants with OUD, whether before or after their daily methadone dose, had distinct baseline physiology compared with healthy controls. Descriptively, controls exhibited modestly higher HF-HRV (mean calm HF-HRV = 0.045 vs 0.041 and 0.040 in pre-dose and post-dose groups) and lower overall movement, reflected in lower mean ACC L2 line-integral values (≈ 2379 vs ≈ 4028 and 4296 in pre-dose and post-dose individuals), a summary metric of total movement magnitude (arbitrary units; see Appendix for feature definitions). ACC features differed in 31% of controls and pre-dose comparisons, and 47% of controls and post-dose comparisons. HRV features showed similarly pronounced differences (41% and 56%). BVP also differed substantially in the controls–post-dose comparison (49%).

Pre-dose and post-dose individuals, however, were physiologically similar under calm conditions. Only 1% of EDA features, and none of the HRV, BVP, or TEMP features, differed between the two OUD subgroups, indicating minimal methadone-related effects on baseline physiology within the assessment window.

RQ3: group differences under stress conditions

Group differences increased under stress. A larger proportion of features within several physiological modalities under stress conditions differed between controls and OUD participants as compared to under calm conditions. For example, the proportion of ACC features showing significant group differences increased from 31 to 47% during calm conditions to 53–56% under stress. HR features showed a comparable pattern, rising from approximately 14–27% to 25–52%, and EDA features also increased, from roughly 2–13% under calm conditions to 7–20% under stress. Additionally, all groups showed increased limb movement (Fig. 3) and higher tonic skin conductance levels (Fig. 4) during stress relative to calm conditions.

Pre-dose and post-dose individuals, who were nearly indistinguishable during calm conditions, diverged under stress. The negative memory task produced the strongest separation: nearly half of EDA features (47%) and more than half of HRV features (52%) differed between the two OUD subgroups. Quantitatively, pre-dose individuals showed a markedly larger increase in tonic EDA during the negative memory task (rising from a mean of 2.81–6.14, compared with 2.38–1.99 in post-dose participants), consistent with greater sympathetic reactivity in the pre-dose group during this task.

RQ4: does group status modulate stress response

Across all tasks combined, a small number of features showed significant condition-by-group interactions, but task-specific analyses revealed clearer patterns. For movement, both OUD groups showed larger increases in ACC from calm to stress than controls, who exhibited only modest ACC changes (Fig. 3). In contrast, some EDA metrics showed larger calm-to-stress changes in controls (Fig. 4).

The negative memory-recall task produced the largest number of pre-dose versus post-dose interaction features, with pre-dose and post-dose participants showing clearer separation during stress than during calm conditions. In the pre-dose versus post-dose interaction model for memory recall, 39 of 94 EDA features (41.5%) and 14 of 44 TEMP features (31.8%) showed significant Condition × Group effects. Quantitatively, skin conductance response peak counts, which index rapid phasic sympathetic responses, showed the same pattern. Pre-dose participants displayed a pronounced increase from calm to stress (mean 5.82→12.00), whereas post-dose participants showed a more moderate rise (mean 8.43→11.79). Temperature (°C) also differentiated the two groups, with pre-dose individuals showing no change (32.68→32.66) and post-dose individuals showing an increase (33.00→33.36).

RF classification

RF classification was used as a secondary validation analysis to test whether the physiological features identified by the mixed-effects models retained practical discriminative value for calm-versus-stress classification. Across all tasks combined, RF performance was moderate. Using the full feature set, mean 5-fold accuracy was 67.7%, with a balanced accuracy of 67.3% (sensitivity 62.8%, specificity 71.8%). Using the LMM-selected subset for the all-tasks contrast yielded similar and slightly improved performance, with an accuracy of 68.6% and balanced accuracy of 68.1% (sensitivity 62.4%, specificity 73.8%).

Performance varied substantially by task. The counting contrast showed the strongest discrimination, consistent with the LMM results. Using either the full feature set or the counting-related LMM-selected subset, RF achieved 98.3% accuracy and 98.3% balanced accuracy (sensitivity 97.9%, specificity 98.8%) for distinguishing calm counting from arithmetic stress tasks. Video classification was more modest: accuracy increased from 64.1% with the full feature set to 68.0% with the LMM-selected subset, with corresponding balanced accuracies of 64.2% and 68.1%. Memory-recall classification was weakest, although the LMM-selected subset again performed slightly better than the full feature set (61.4% vs 60.0% accuracy; 61.3% vs 59.9% balanced accuracy).

To further examine modality-level contributions, we also evaluated all-task RF models using single-modality feature sets. Among these, ACC and EDA were the strongest individual modalities, with balanced accuracies of 64.8% and 64.7%, respectively, followed by BVP (61.9%). TEMP (58.5%), HRV (54.9%), and HR (54.4%) were weaker. However, both ACC-only and EDA-only performance remained below that of the multimodal LMM-selected subset, indicating that ACC- and EDA-related features captured much of the core stress-related signal, while multimodal integration provided additional complementary information.

Overall, these classification results support the practical relevance of the LMM-derived features. The strongest classification performance was observed for the counting contrast, whereas the LMM-selected subsets generally preserved or modestly improved performance relative to the full feature set. At the modality level, ACC and EDA were the most informative single channels, but the best overall performance was achieved when multiple physiological modalities were considered jointly.

Robustness to Demographic and Behavioral Covariates

Two supplementary analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary findings. First, full-sample demographic-adjusted models incorporated age, gender, and race/ethnicity as additional covariates. These demographic variables were available for all participants and were therefore included in analyses using the complete dataset. Second, an exploratory reduced-sample sensitivity analysis additionally incorporated recent nicotine-use status and self-reported current stress and craving ratings (0–10 scale) collected immediately before the laboratory task. These behavioral covariates were available for all OUD sessions (14 pre-dose and 15 post-dose sessions) but for only 14 of the 47 control sessions. This missingness reflected a protocol update during data collection, rather than participant-level selective nonresponse. Therefore, results from this reduced-sample analysis were considered exploratory and were interpreted as sensitivity analyses rather than confirmatory findings.

Adding these covariates did not materially change the main results. In the demographic-adjusted models, the within-group stress findings were unchanged in controls and the pre-dose OUD group, whereas the post-dose group showed only a modest reduction in the number of significant features (103/119). In the reduced sample sensitivity analysis, the same overall pattern was retained: nearly all significant pre-dose features were preserved (170/171), and most post-dose features were preserved (116/120). Thus, although a small number of feature-level findings fluctuated after covariate adjustment, the main group- and stress-related patterns described above were not meaningfully altered.

To further characterize the measured covariates, covariate beta summaries were examined only as descriptive sensitivity checks and were not interpreted as evidence of independent covariate effects. In the full-sample demographic-adjusted models, gender and race/ethnicity showed the largest mean absolute standardized beta values across feature-level covariate terms (0.363 and 0.329, respectively), whereas age was smaller (0.168). In the reduced-sample sensitivity analysis, gender again showed the largest mean absolute standardized beta (0.597), followed by race/ethnicity (0.444), nicotine use (0.299), age (0.214), craving (0.199), and current stress (0.131). However, these covariate terms rarely survived FDR correction, further supporting that covariate adjustment did not materially alter the primary findings.

Finally, a supplementary repeated-measures multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) using representative modality-level features produced results broadly consistent with the primary LMM findings; full model details and results are provided in the Appendix. Calm-versus-stress effects were significant for all task contrasts (all-task: Pillai’s V=0.530, p < 0.001; video task: V=0.325, p < 0.001; counting task: V=0.570, p < 0.001; memory recall: V=0.496, p < 0.001). Stress-response-by-group effects were significant for the all-task contrast (V=0.450, p = 0.003), counting task (V=0.465, p = 0.002), and memory recall (V=0.411, p = 0.011), but not for video task (V=0.294, p = 0.110).

Taken together, these results suggest that the measured covariates had quantifiable but limited effects and did not materially alter findings.

Discussion

This study showed that wearable sensors can capture stress-related physiological changes and detect group differences between individuals with OUD and healthy controls. Across participants, EDA and ACC features were the most consistent markers of stress, whereas simple HR–based measures were less reliable; HRV showed clearer stress effects in controls but was attenuated in participants with OUD. Stress responses were task-dependent, with increasing cognitive demand producing the greatest physiological separation between calm and stressed states. Relative to controls, both OUD groups exhibited changes in wearable data suggestive of higher baseline arousal and altered stress reactivity, with pre-dose participants displaying the largest EDA and movement responses. RF models classified calm vs stress significantly above chance, particularly for cognitive tasks, and showed that a reduced set of significant features preserved most of the performance obtained with all features.

Prior studies have shown that wearable sensors can capture clinically relevant physiology in people who use substances; these have focused on distinct states (e.g., intoxication, withdrawal) versus reactivity during longitudinal medication treatment. In an outpatient sample of individuals with SUD (n = 30), Carreiro et al. used a wrist-worn device to compare physiological data during self-reported stress and craving episodes versus periods when no stress or craving was reported (Carreiro et al., 2020). Using machine-learning models, an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.80–0.82 was reported for state classification (e.g., stress/not stressed, craving/no craving). Notably, participants reported that wearing the device led to a greater awareness of stress and craving fluctuations (Carreiro et al., 2020). Li et al. extended this approach to drug-use events in a community sample (n = 9) using Fitbit devices, showing that personalized convolutional neural networks had some ability to detect self-reported substance-use windows from biometric data (AUC=0.695). The incorporation of self-supervised pretraining modestly improved performance (AUC=0.729) (Li et al., 2024). Zhao et al. assessed nicotine vaping among young adults and found that HR levels and slopes in the minutes before a vaping episode were elevated compared with non-use periods, suggesting that pre-event HR dynamics can flag high-risk windows for use (Zhao et al., 2025). Taken together, these studies demonstrate that substance-related states are detectable from wearable data, but they do not characterize how stress itself manifests physiologically in patients with OUD receiving methadone, nor do they directly compare these patients with healthy controls.

Opioid-focused candidate digital biomarker studies have mainly examined chronic exposure and misuse. In a hospitalized cohort, Carreiro et al. combined wearable features with clinical data to classify patients (n = 41, 39 features) as opioid-naïve, occasional, or chronic users, with participant-level accuracies in the 70–90% range, depending on the model used (Carreiro et al., 2025). Gullapalli et al. attempted to detect opioid misuse among patients with chronic pain (n = 169), with a temporal fusion model incorporating physiological data (HR, RR) and cognitive task data. They achieved an AUC= 0.81, with sensitivity and specificity of 0.78 (Gullapalli et al., 2025). Our findings are consistent with the above, that chronic opioid exposure can be detected by a distinct physiological signature, but incorporate how stress may alter this signature, both before and after methadone dosing. Both OUD groups had greater movement, and lower HRV at baseline than controls. These differences persisted during stress tasks, with pre-dose participants exhibiting large EDA and ACC responses, and minimal HRV modulation, whereas post-dose participants showed more muted reactivity, characterized by continued low vagal tone.

Clinical implications and future directions

These findings may have several implications for how stress is considered in OUD treatment. Therapeutic interventions should address both tonic dysregulation and phasic stress responses. Intervention timing is also relevant, as the pre-dose state showed the most pronounced changes. Patients may benefit more from therapeutic sessions after they are pharmacologically stabilized, whereas individuals may be more preoccupied by their physiological symptoms pre-dose.

For wearable-assisted interventions, these findings help refine which physiologic signals may be most useful. Eddie et al. recently tested biofeedback as a way to reduce craving and negative affect in people with SUD. They trained participants to modify HRV through biofeedback and, compared with treatment as usual, this led to lower craving and negative affect over time (Eddie et al., 2025). Qualitative work suggests that both patients and clinicians are interested in detecting stress and craving to support recovery, and that patients feel more attuned to their bodies when they can see or be notified of these changes (Hampton et al., 2025). In our sample, EDA and ACC were the most reliable indicators of acute stress, and these signatures were strongest during cognitively demanding tasks. Prioritizing these modalities when designing algorithms to flag high-risk states may improve both the accuracy and clinical relevance of wearable-based decision support in OUD treatment.

These results also highlight EDA and ACC as practical, stress-sensitive signals for real-time monitoring in patients receiving medications for OUD. Algorithms that prioritize these modalities, with HRV incorporated where signal quality permits (Eddie et al., 2025, Eddie et al., 2015), may offer a feasible path forward. The RF findings suggest that relatively narrow feature sets may be adequate, which will be important for real-time computation in clinical settings (Oesterle et al., 2022). Future studies should assess whether feedback based on these signals improves distress coping, cravings, or supports safer decision-making in daily life. Longitudinal studies examining how changes in these markers relate to lapse, relapse, and treatment retention will further support clinical utility. Finally, determining whether these measures add value for risk stratification and tailoring care for individuals at risk for more persistent or treatment-refractory addiction will inform generalizability (Bormann et al., 2025).

Limitations

Several limitations exist. The sample was recruited from people in treatment at one site, and therefore may not generalize to those not in care. Although supplementary covariate-adjusted and sensitivity analyses suggested that the main findings were reasonably robust to measured demographic and behavioral variables, other clinically relevant sources of variability in the OUD groups were not fully captured. These include time in methadone treatment, duration on a stable dose, withdrawal severity, concurrent medications, psychiatric comorbidity, and recent opioid or other substance use, all of which may have contributed to residual between-group differences and may limit generalizability. The design was cross-sectional and a single laboratory session; therefore, it is unclear how these findings may evolve over the treatment course. The stress paradigm, while standardized, may not fully capture the complexity and unpredictability of real-world stressors that precipitate craving and use. Technical and signal-quality concerns are common in sensor studies (Oesterle et al., 2022). HRV estimation from wrist-based PPG was challenged by motion artifacts and relatively short task durations, which may have reduced sensitivity to more subtle autonomic differences. BVP-derived features were occasionally noisy under movement, and TEMP measures are sensitive to ambient conditions, contributing to variability in these features. Finally, the OUD sample size was modest, with variations in opioid use histories, other substance use, and concurrent medications.

Conclusion

Individuals with OUD receiving methadone displayed multidimensional alterations in stress-response systems that could be detected using wearable sensor data. Across tasks, EDA and ACC features were the most reliable markers of acute stress, whereas simple heart-rate measures were less informative. No single feature completely differentiated participant types; however, OUD groups consistently showed higher baseline arousal and less adaptable autonomic responses compared with controls. As digital health tools become more integrated into addiction treatment, leveraging stress-sensitive signals may help deliver more timely, personalized interventions.
