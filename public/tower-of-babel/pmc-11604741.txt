Revealing the molecular links between coronary heart disease and cognitive impairment: the role of aging-related genes and therapeutic potential of stellate ganglion block
Authors: Zhehao Jin, Yuling Xing, Pengyu Duan, Yonghong Bi, Xiaoyan Li, Weiyu Feng, Bing Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Coronary heart disease (CHD) and cognitive impairment frequently co-occur in aging populations, yet the molecular mechanisms linking these conditions remain unclear. This study aims to elucidate the roles of key aging-related genes (ARGs), specifically FKBP5 and DDIT3, in the pathophysiology of CHD and cognitive impairment, and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of stellate ganglion block (SGB). Using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and bulk RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq) data, we identified FKBP5 and DDIT3 as pivotal genes upregulated in both conditions. Experimental findings show that SGB effectively modulates these ARG-related pathways through autonomic regulation, specifically suppressing estrogen and NF-κB signaling pathways, thereby reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as SRC, MMP2, FKBP5, IRAK1, and MYD88, while upregulating the vasodilation-related gene NOS3. This modulation improved endothelial and cardiac function and enhanced cerebral blood flow (CBF), leading to cognitive improvement. Behavioral assessments, including novel object recognition (NOR) and Morris water maze (MWM) tests, demonstrated that SGB-treated rats outperformed untreated MI rats, with significant cognitive recovery over time. Further support from laser Doppler flowmetry (LDF) and electroencephalogram (EEG) analyses revealed increased left frontal blood flow and stabilized neural activity, indicating a favorable neurophysiological environment for cognitive rehabilitation. Our findings suggest that left stellate ganglion block (LSGB) provides both cardiac and cognitive benefits through targeted gene modulation, establishing its therapeutic potential for addressing the intersecting pathologies of CHD and cognitive impairment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10522-024-10159-x.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD) and cognitive impairment are prevalent among the elderly, severely affecting patients' quality of life. CHD is characterized by the progressive formation of atherosclerotic plaques within coronary arteries, leading to obstructed blood flow and potentially severe cardiovascular events (Fihn et al. 2015). In 2021, CHD caused 375,476 deaths in the United States (World Health Organization 2021). Globally, cardiovascular diseases result in approximately 17.9 million deaths annually, highlighting their significant public health burden (Benjamin et al. 2019).

Elderly individuals with CHD often also experience insomnia, cognitive impairment, and dementia, suggesting potential pathological links between CHD and cognitive impairment (de Bruijn and Ikram 2014; Deckers et al. 2017; Johansen et al. 2023). Studies indicate that cellular aging is a common factor in these diseases, contributing to cardiovascular and neurological health deterioration by promoting inflammatory responses and endothelial dysfunction (Franceschi and Campisi 2014; Fulop et al. 2018).

Despite recent advancements in understanding the molecular mechanisms of CHD and cognitive impairment, specific pathological connections remain unclear. As the role of aging-related genes (ARGs) in these diseases becomes clearer, understanding changes in their expression and mechanisms is increasingly important. These genes may serve as potential biomarkers, offering new perspectives for early diagnosis and targeted treatment (Wang et al. 2022).

SGB, an emerging therapeutic technique that regulates the autonomic nervous system (ANS), has shown significant potential in treating both CHD and cognitive impairment. SGB has been particularly effective in alleviating post-myocardial infarction (MI) complications, such as arrhythmias, oxidative stress, and pathological cardiac remodeling (Li 2022; Baldi et al. 2023; Wei et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2022a, b; Yang et al. 2016). Additionally, it can relieve pain, improve sleep quality, and potentially enhance cognitive function (Yang et al. 2023; Summers and Nevin 2017; Zhang et al. 2022a, b). Given the current limitations of comprehensive treatment approaches for CHD and cognitive impairment, SGB offers a promising multi-system regulatory pathway that addresses both cardiovascular and neurological complications.

This study aims to reveal the molecular links between CHD and cognitive impairment, focusing on the role of ARGs and the therapeutic potential of SGB. Using single-cell transcriptome sequencing and RNA sequencing data, we identified and validated key genes and further assessed the therapeutic effects of SGB through animal experiments. This study hopes to advance personalized treatment strategies for these diseases by revealing the key roles of ARGs in CHD and cognitive impairment.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data related to CHD were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession number: GSE121893). The datasets GSE66360 (related to CHD) and GSE42148 were used as the training set and validation set, respectively. A total of 279 ARGs were obtained from the CellAge database.

Single-cell transcriptome analysis

The 'Seurat' R package was used for quality control and normalization of scRNA-seq data. Principal component analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) were used for dimensionality reduction and cell clustering. The 'SingleR' package was used for cell type identification. Differential expression analysis was conducted between CHD and control cells to identify differentially expressed genes (scRNA-DEGs).

Bulk RNA-seq analysis (training set)

The 'limma' R package was used to analyze differential gene expression between CHD and control samples in the training set to identify DEGs. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was performed using SRGs as features to identify gene modules associated with cellular aging.

Key gene identification

Differentially expressed ARGs were identified by intersecting scRNA-DEGs, DEGs, and WGCNA module genes. Feature (gene) selection was performed using Random Forest (RF), LASSO regression, and XGBoost. Feature genes with consistent performance in both the training and validation sets were retained.

Diagnostic Nomogram development

The 'rms' R package was used to construct diagnostic nomograms. Calibration curves, decision curves, and clinical impact curves were generated to assess the performance of the nomograms.

Functional exploration

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted on individual biomarkers to elucidate possible biological pathways and processes. CIBERSORT was used to quantify and compare the abundance of 22 immune cell types in CHD and control samples. The correlation between differentially infiltrating immune cells and biomarkers was studied. Potential therapeutic drug candidates were predicted using the DGIdb database. Molecular docking analysis was performed to evaluate drug-target interactions.

Correlation analysis

Biomarker expression patterns in circadian rhythm, insomnia, and neurological diseases were examined to explore their potential correlations beyond CHD.

Biomarker expression validation

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Approval No: KY2023-150, ChiCTR2400081833), ensuring adherence to ethical guidelines and protecting the rights and welfare of research participants. Blood samples were collected from 10 CHD patients and 10 controls for RT-qPCR validation. RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA was synthesized. qPCR was performed using a CFX Connect thermocycler, and mRNA relative quantification was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method.

Animal experiments and SGB treatment

The animal experiment protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Approval No: SYDW2023-102) with the aim to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used. Male Sprague–Dawley rats were used to study biomarker expression changes and the therapeutic potential of SGB for CHD. Rats were divided into control, MI, and SGB treatment groups. SGB treatment was performed using 1% lidocaine under ultrasound guidance. Routine electrocardiogram (ECG) and echocardiogram examinations were conducted, and tissues were collected for analysis post-experiment.

Animal behavior testing

To explore cognitive changes in rats with CHD, novel object recognition (NOR) and water maze tests were conducted 1 month post-MI. The NOR test measured the rats' ability to recognize a new object by presenting two similar objects and then replacing one object. The water maze task assessed spatial learning and memory (Ahmadi et al. 2017; Szczepańska et al. 2023). In this study, we used 6 animals per group for behavioral testing. Outlier analysis was conducted on the collected behavioral data using both the Interquartile Range (IQR) method and the z-score method. No significant outliers were identified in any group.

Rat physiological signal monitoring and cardiac structure assessment

ECGs were recorded to assess heart rhythm and function. LDF and EEG were performed to assess brain function after MI. Echocardiography was performed to measure cardiac function indices. Cardiac tissues were examined for pathology, and serum cardiac biomarkers were quantified using ELISA.

Transcriptomic analysis of rat heart, peripheral blood, and stellate ganglia

Total RNA was extracted from heart, peripheral blood, and stellate ganglia tissues, and mRNA was isolated. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform. Differentially expressed genes were identified, and Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R language (v4.1.0). Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. Differences among multiple groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s post hoc test. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Detailed experimental methods and the software, R packages, and references used can be found in the supplementary materials.

Therapeutic effects of SGB post-MI

Experimental ECGs showed significant ST-segment changes and ventricular arrhythmias in rats post-myocardial infarction, indicating infarction pathology. These symptoms were alleviated by SGB treatment, demonstrating its therapeutic potential. Early stabilization of ECG features in the SGB treatment group suggested that SGB might mitigate myocardial ischemia and stress (Fig. 7a). Echocardiographic assessment showed improvement in ejection fraction and fractional shortening in the SGB treatment group, supporting the therapeutic benefits of SGB in post-myocardial infarction cardiac remodeling (Fig. 7b). Myocardial HE staining showed extensive necrosis and structural damage in the MI group, accompanied by acute inflammation. The SGB treatment group showed improved cellular integrity and reduced fibrosis. The control group showed dense and organized myocardial cells with no signs of fibrosis or inflammation (Fig. 7c, d). Behavioral assessments at 1 month and 10 months following MI and LSGB treatment

In the NOR test conducted 1 month after MI, the LSGB group exhibited a trend towards greater interest in the novel object compared to the MI group. The exploration time and distance traveled by the LSGB group surpassed that of the MI group, although these differences did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 8a–d). In the NOR test conducted 10 months post-MI, significant differences were observed among the groups. The LSGB group demonstrated a markedly increased exploration time for the novel object compared to both the SHAM and MI groups, indicating a sustained interest in novel stimuli. Additionally, the total exploration distance during both the familiarization and recognition phases was significantly greater in the LSGB group, suggesting enhanced engagement with the environment and better cognitive function (Fig. 8e–h). Similarly, 1 month after MI, in the MWM test, the LSGB group showed a better trend in learning and memory retention, as evidenced by a reduced escape latency and a higher proportion of time spent in the target quadrant. However, these improvements were also not statistically significant (Fig. 8i–k). In the 10 months post-MI MWM test, which included a memory retention test for the original platform location and a learning test for a new platform, the LSGB group again outperformed the MI group. The LSGB group had a significantly shorter escape latency during the initial trial, indicating better retention of the platform's original location (long-term memory). While the number of platform crossings and the time spent in the target quadrant did not differ significantly, qualitative video analysis revealed that the LSGB group explored the area less after not finding the platform, instead redirecting their search to other areas more effectively (Fig. 8l). In the test assessing the ability to learn a new platform location, conducted four days after the initial MWM test, the LSGB group displayed superior learning and adaptability. Effects of MI and SGB on left frontal lobe blood flow and EEG activity

Analysis of left frontal lobe blood flow revealed significant differences among the MI, LSGB, and Sham groups. The MI group exhibited reduced blood flow (maximum 132.98 BPU, minimum 104.14 BPU, range 56.26–57.37%), while the LSGB group showed increased blood flow (maximum 199.36 BPU, minimum 112.23 BPU, range 71.70–78.36%). The Sham group maintained the highest and most stable blood flow levels (maximum 198.44 BPU, minimum 147.93 BPU, range 51.40–54.22%). Both the LSGB and Sham groups showed significantly higher blood flow compared to the MI group, suggesting that LSGB may assist in restoring cerebral perfusion following MI (Fig. 9a).Fig. 9LDF and EEG related results. a LDF measurement images and statistical graphs. b EEG measurement images and statistical graphs. c Line graphs showing time-course changes for each EEG band

In EEG analysis, δ wave activity (1–4 Hz) was highest and most stable in the LSGB group (36.92–44.50%), reflecting a relaxed and stable neural state conducive to cognitive improvement observed at the 10-month follow-up. In contrast, the MI group displayed greater fluctuations in δ wave activity (31.13–50.76%), indicating impaired deep relaxation, potentially leading to unstable cognitive performance. θ wave activity (4–8 Hz) was relatively stable in both the Sham (19.91–24.44%) and LSGB (19.07–22.46%) groups, supporting attention and memory functions, whereas the MI group exhibited larger fluctuations (17.44–25.80%), suggesting disruption in shallow relaxation, which may impact memory processing and attentional focus. α wave activity (8–14 Hz), associated with relaxed wakefulness, was highest in the Sham group (16.47–20.73%), while both the MI (14.07–18.78%) and LSGB (15.05–18.62%) groups exhibited lower α wave activity, with further suppression in the LSGB group. This reduction in α wave stability in the LSGB group may limit relaxed alertness, potentially affecting attentional management. β wave activity (14–30 Hz), linked to cognitive alertness, was most variable in the MI group (17.73–26.79%), possibly due to increased sympathetic activation, while β wave activity was more stable in the LSGB group (21.01–24.77%), indicating improved cognitive focus (Fig. 9b, c).

These results indicate that MI leads to instability in relaxation and alertness states (δ, θ, and β waves), disrupting neural stability. LSGB, by stabilizing δ, θ, and β wave activity and improving cerebral perfusion, may support cognitive function and promote neural balance.

Transcriptomic analysis results highlight LSGB’s protective effects on myocardial repair, aging, and cognitive function

Following MI, signaling pathways across different tissues displayed regulatory changes, notably in the estrogen signaling pathway, PI3K/Akt anti-apoptotic pathway, and NF-κB pro-inflammatory pathway. Inflammatory and fibrotic levels increased in the infarct area, normal myocardium, peripheral blood, and the left and right stellate ganglia, accompanied by regulatory shifts in repair signals. LSGB treatment demonstrated protective effects across these tissues.

In the infarct region, significant changes in the estrogen signaling pathway were observed, with upregulation of Src, Mmp2, and Mmp9, leading to enhanced local inflammation and fibrosis, exacerbating myocardial damage. Concurrently, downregulation of Akt1, Akt2, and Nos3 limited anti-apoptotic and vasodilatory signaling, hindering myocardial repair. Post-LSGB treatment, Src, Mmp2, and Mmp9 expression was downregulated, effectively reducing inflammation and fibrosis while enhancing myocardial stability. Additionally, Jun, Fos, Akt1, and Prkacb were upregulated, strengthening anti-apoptotic signaling and blood flow restoration, thus promoting myocardial repair. Discussion

This study delves into the molecular and cellular mechanisms linking CHD and cognitive impairment, with a particular focus on the roles of ARGs such as FKBP5 and DDIT3. Additionally, we assessed the therapeutic potential of SGB. Through scRNA-seq, bulk RNA-seq, machine learning models, and WGCNA, we identified key molecular pathways associated with CHD and cognitive impairment. Our findings indicate that SGB can modulate critical pathways, including NF-κB, estrogen signaling, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK, to reduce inflammation and improve cerebral blood flow (CBF), thereby enhancing cardiovascular and neurological function and promoting cognitive recovery.

Our study highlights that FKBP5 and DDIT3 play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of CHD and cognitive impairment. FKBP5 was significantly upregulated in both conditions and serves as a key regulator of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and glucocorticoid signaling. The upregulation of FKBP5 enhances stress responses and activates the NF-κB pathway, promoting the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IRAK1, NFKBIA, and SRC, leading to increased systemic inflammation. Chronic activation of NF-κB exacerbates inflammatory responses, known as ‘inflammaging’, which accelerates the progression of atherosclerosis and neuroinflammation in elderly individuals (Zannas et al. 2019; Touma et al. 2011). Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis reveals that FKBP5 plays a critical role in protein quality control under stress conditions, including functions in peptidyl-prolyl isomerization and protein folding, which help maintain protein stability and cellular homeostasis (Klengel et al. 2013; Zannas et al. 2016).

DDIT3 (also known as CHOP), as a regulator of the endoplasmic reticulum stress response and the unfolded protein response (UPR), is significantly upregulated during acute MI, participating in apoptosis and inflammation (Garcia and Shaw 2017; Zhang et al. 2019). In neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, sustained upregulation of DDIT3 is closely associated with neuronal damage and functional loss, suggesting its critical role in these conditions. Although DDIT3 returns to baseline levels after MI, its role in acute stress responses complements the chronic effects of FKBP5, highlighting their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets for CHD and associated cognitive impairment.

Our study also demonstrates a strong association between CHD biomarkers and circadian rhythm-related genes, especially the differential expression of FKBP5 and DDIT3 in conditions such as insomnia, MCI, and AD (see Fig. 6b–d). Circadian rhythm disruptions, common in elderly CHD patients, can dysregulate the HPA axis, leading to altered glucocorticoid signaling, increased sympathetic activity, and sleep disturbances. These factors collectively exacerbate chronic inflammation and cognitive impairment (Sasaki et al. 2009; Tranah et al. 2011; Javaheri and Redline 2017; Faborode et al. 2022; Aminyavari et al. 2019). The involvement of FKBP5 in these pathways further emphasizes its potential as a therapeutic target for CHD and related cognitive impairment.

SGB primarily exerts its therapeutic effects in MI by modulating the ANS and reducing systemic inflammation. Our results demonstrate that SGB downregulates pro-inflammatory genes such as IL1b, TNF, NFKBIA, and FKBP5 in the stellate ganglion, myocardium, and peripheral blood, thereby reducing both local and systemic inflammation. Immune infiltration analysis showed a significant increase in inflammatory cells, such as macrophages and activated T cells, in CHD patients, indicating systemic inflammation in cardiovascular and neural tissues. SGB effectively reduced the infiltration of these pro-inflammatory immune cells, alleviating the inflammatory burden (see Figs. S1–S5). Postmortem studies have shown significant inflammation in approximately 77% of the left stellate ganglia in patients with sudden cardiac death, supporting the link between excessive sympathetic activation and increased cardiac stress (Tse et al. 2022). By reducing inflammation in the stellate ganglion, SGB helps prevent excessive sympathetic activation, promotes parasympathetic dominance, and improves cardiac function.

Additionally, SGB activates the PI3K/Akt pathway, upregulating anti-apoptotic genes such as NOS3 and AKT, which promotes vasodilation, improves coronary artery function, enhances myocardial survival, and facilitates tissue repair (Bastian et al. 2018; Merx et al. 2014). SGB also reduced myocardial fibrosis by inhibiting genes such as MMP2 and MMP9, leading to improved myocardial structure. Echocardiographic, ECG, and pathological findings supported these therapeutic effects by showing improved myocardial contractility, increased ejection fraction, and reduced arrhythmias and myocardial fibrosis.

SGB's neuroprotective effects were validated in the MI rat model. The SGB-treated group showed enhanced memory retention and spatial learning abilities in the NOR and MWM tests, likely due to improved CBF and reduced neuroinflammation (Staffaroni et al. 2019; Au et al. 2016). LDF and EEG analyses further supported the impact of SGB on CBF and neural activity stability, particularly in the regulation of δ, θ, and β waves. Stable δ waves in the SGB group contributed to deep relaxation and brain recovery, while larger δ wave fluctuations in the MI group indicated impaired neural inhibition. Stable θ wave activity in the SGB group reflected improved autonomic balance and reduced stress levels, essential for memory retention and sustained attention. The stability of β waves in the SGB group indicated reduced cognitive fluctuations and enhanced task focus, whereas variability in β wave activity in the MI group suggested cognitive instability (Başar et al. 2001; Laptinskaya et al. 2020; Başar and Güntekin 2008).

Although improvements in EEG stability and CBF were observed as early as 1 month post-SGB treatment, significant cognitive differences between the MI, Sham, and SGB groups only became apparent at 10 months. This suggests that while SGB provides early neuroprotection through reduced inflammation and improved autonomic balance, long-term cognitive benefits require sustained hemodynamic and neurophysiological stability. MI exacerbates cognitive impairment by reducing CBF and inducing neuroinflammation (Johansen et al. 2023; Evonuk et al. 2017; Levine et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2014). However, early post-MI cognitive effects are subtle because cerebral hypoperfusion and EEG abnormalities at this stage are not severe enough to cause detectable cognitive changes (Johansen et al. 2023). This explains why, despite significant improvements in cardiac function and CBF 1 month after LSGB treatment, cognitive differences among the MI, Sham, and LSGB groups did not emerge until the 10-month mark.

Key signaling pathways related to FKBP5 are involved in both CHD and cognitive impairment, including estrogen signaling, NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK pathways. FKBP5 directly participates in estrogen signaling, which interacts with the NF-κB pathway (Mishra et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2016, 2020; Yun et al. 2018). Estrogen signaling exerts anti-inflammatory effects by downregulating NF-κB activity, suggesting that FKBP5 may act as a molecular bridge between estrogen and NF-κB signaling, thereby regulating inflammation and stress responses in both cardiovascular and nervous systems. Our data also show that SGB downregulated FKBP5 and NF-κB-related genes such as IRAK1, NFKBIA, and SRC, reducing inflammation, restoring ANS balance, and alleviating the burden on cardiovascular and neural systems.

Additionally, the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways play critical roles in cell survival, proliferation, and stress responses, acting downstream of both estrogen and NF-κB signaling (Deng and Zhou 2023; Yi et al. 2019; Fu et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024). Targeting these interconnected pathways, SGB not only improved cardiovascular health but also showed broad therapeutic effects on cognitive function. These effects are partly mediated by enhancing myocardial repair, reducing fibrosis, and improving blood flow to both the heart and brain, ultimately benefiting cognitive outcomes.

Molecular docking analysis indicated that small molecules such as nefazodone, venlafaxine, and clomipramine might interact with FKBP5 and DDIT3. However, these drugs carry risks of hepatotoxicity and cardiotoxicity (Stewart 2002; Howell et al. 2007). In contrast, SGB regulates ANS activity without these cardiovascular risks, making it a safer alternative for treatment. By modulating autonomic balance and reducing systemic inflammation, SGB offers a less toxic approach for managing CHD and cognitive impairment compared to traditional pharmacological agents.

Our study provides insights into the molecular links between CHD and cognitive impairment, emphasizing the therapeutic potential of SGB. However, several limitations remain. The modest sample size restricts the generalizability of our findings, necessitating larger cohort studies to validate gene associations in diverse populations. While animal models provided mechanistic insights, cross-species studies and human trials are essential to confirm their clinical relevance. The variability and reproducibility of SGB’s effects across different patient groups also warrant further investigation, ideally through multi-center trials to ensure consistency and safety.

Although no adverse effects were observed in animals, the long-term safety of repeated SGB use in humans remains unclear. Future research should evaluate potential side effects and refine SGB treatment protocols for sustainable clinical benefits. Expanding the focus beyond FKBP5 and DDIT3 to include other aging-related genes might uncover additional mechanisms and new therapeutic targets for CHD and cognitive impairment.

Conclusion

This study enhances our understanding of the molecular links between CHD and cognitive impairment, particularly highlighting the roles of ARGs FKBP5 and DDIT3. Our findings suggest that SGB can modulate key pathways, including NF-κB, estrogen, and PI3K/Akt, to reduce inflammation and increase CBF, thereby improving cardiovascular and neurological function and supporting cognitive recovery. LSGB, as a minimally invasive approach, demonstrates immediate and sustained effects in alleviating both local and systemic inflammation, mitigating sympathetic overactivity, and contributing to improvements in CHD and associated cognitive issues. By targeting multiple interconnected pathways, SGB emerges as a promising therapy with both cardiovascular and neuroprotective benefits.
