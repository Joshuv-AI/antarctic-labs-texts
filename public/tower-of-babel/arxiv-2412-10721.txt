Model checking for high dimensional generalized linear models based on random projections Corresponding author. Wen Chen and Jie Liu are equally contributed to this work.
Wen Chen, Jie Liu, Heng Peng, Falong Tan, Lixing Zhu, Department of Statistics, Hunan University, Changsha, China, Department of Mathematics, Hong Kong Baptist University, Hong Kong, China, Center for Statistics and Data Science, Beijing Normal University at Zhuhai, Zhuhai, China, School of Arts and Sciences, China University of Petroleum-Beijing at Karamay, Karamay, China

Abstract
Most existing tests in the literature for model checking do not work in high dimension settings due to challenges arising from the “curse of dimensionality”, or dependencies on the normality of parameter estimators. To address these challenges, we proposed a new goodness of fit test based on random projections for generalized linear models, when the dimension of covariates may substantially exceed the sample size. The tests only require the convergence rate of parameter estimators to derive the limiting distribution. The growing rate of the dimension is allowed to be of exponential order in relation to the sample size. As random projection converts covariates to one-dimensional space, our tests can detect the local alternative departing from the null at the rate of n − 1 / 2 h − 1 / 4 n^{-1/2}h^{-1/4} where h h is the bandwidth, and n n is the sample size. This sensitive rate is not related to the dimension of covariates, and thus the “curse of dimensionality” for our tests would be largely alleviated. An interesting and unexpected result is that for randomly chosen projections, the resulting test statistics can be asymptotic independent. We then proposed combination methods to enhance the power performance of the tests. Detailed simulation studies and a real data analysis are conducted to illustrate the effectiveness of our methodology. Key words: asymptotically independent, generalized linear models, goodness of fit, high dimension, random projection, integrated p p -values.



1
Introduction

In the last three decades, there have been substantial works on developing estimation methodology for high dimensional regression models when the dimension
p
of covariates may be much larger than the sample size
n
, see
Bühlmann and Van De Geer 2011
for a review. To avoid possible wrong conclusions, any statistical analysis based on high dimension regression models should be accompanied by a check of whether the model is valid. However, high dimensional model checking has not been systemically studied in the literature. This paper is devoted to develop goodness of fit tests for regression models when the dimension
p
may substantially exceed the simple size
n
. Consider the regression model:

Y=m(X)+\varepsilon,

(1.1)

where
Y
is an integral response variable associated with a predictor
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\varepsilon=Y-E(Y|X)
is the error term. Our interest herewith is to check whether
(Y,X)
follows a parametric regression models
Y=m(X,\beta_{0})+\varepsilon(\beta_{0})
with
E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})|X]=0
for some unknown parameter
\beta_{0}
in high dimension settings, such as high dimensional linear models or generalized linear models.

In low dimension settings when the dimension
p
is fixed and smaller than the sample size
n
, there are mainly two types of goodness of fit tests for regression models in the literature: global smoothing tests and local smoothing tests. Global smoothing tests construct the test statistics based on empirical processes, see
Stute 1997
,
Stute et al. 1998a
,
Stute et al. 1998b
,
Zhu 2003
,
Khmaladze and Koul 2004
,
Escanciano 2006a
,
Stute et al. 2008
,
Cuesta-Albertos et al. 2019
, among many others.
The tests of this type usually require the asymptotic linear expansion or the normality of parameter estimators to derive the limiting distributions. However, the asymptotic linear expansion or the normality of parameter estimators in high dimension settings, such as lasso estimators, may not hold anymore. Thus, existing global smoothing tests cannot be directly extended to high dimension settings.
While local smoothing tests utilize nonparametric estimation of
E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})|X]
to construct the test statistics, see
Härdle and Mammen 1993
,
Zheng 1996
,
Dette 1999
,
Fan and Huang 2001
,
Horowitz and Spokoiny 2001
,
Koul and Ni 2004
,
Van Keilegom et al. 2008
, for instance.
Because of the use of nonparametric estimations, this type of tests usually can only detect local alternative hypotheses at the rate
1/\sqrt{nh^{p/2}}
, where
h
is bandwidth and
p
is the dimension of covariates, see
Zheng 1996
for instance. When
p
becomes large, the statistical power of these tests deteriorates very quickly. Thus, local smoothing tests suffer severely from the “curse of dimensionality”.
Nevertheless, one merit of local smoothing tests is that they only rely the convergence rate of parameter estimators to derive the asymptotic properties. Thus, unlike global smoothing tests, local smoothing tests may be applied in high dimension settings if the dimensionality problem can be properly handled.

There are some works in the literature on high dimensional model checking. In diverging dimension settings,
Tan and Zhu 2019
;
Tan and Zhu 2022
proposed two global smoothing tests for testing parametric single-index models and multi-index models, respectively. Both of these two methods utilized sufficient dimension reduction and projected empirical processes to address the dimensionality problem, making it difficult to extend directly to higher dimension settings.
In high dimension with
p>n
,
Shah and Bühlmann 2018
first proposed a residual prediction test to check the goodness of fit of high-dimensional linear models with fixed design and utilized some form of parametric bootstrap to determine the critical values.

Janková et al. 2020
further extended the idea of residual prediction and proposed a generalized residual prediction (GRP) test to check the goodness of fit of generalized linear models. By utilizing the data splitting and a debiasing strategy involving the square-root lasso, the GRP test of
Janková et al. 2020
is asymptotic normal, avoiding the need for resorting to bootstrap methods to determine the critical values. Thus, the GRP test is easy to implement in practice. However, the use of data splitting may reduce the power of the GRP test and also makes it challenging to extend to settings where there exists dependence between the observations, such as in time series data.

In this paper, we proposed a simple local smoothing test based on random projections to check the goodness of fit of high dimensional generalized linear models with random design. Our method utilizes the nonparametric estimation of
E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X]
to construct the test statistic, where the projection
\alpha
belongs to the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
We only require the convergence rate of parameter estimators to derive the asymptotic normality of the test statistic under the null hypothesis for any given projection
\alpha
.
The growing rate of the dimension
p
of covariates
X
is allowed to be of exponential order in relation to the sample size.
As the test statistics do not rely on the asymptotic linear expansion of parameter estimators in the cases of
p>n
, they can be applied to test high dimensional models with any sparse estimation methodology including lasso.
Under the global alternative hypothesis, the proposed tests are consistent with asymptotic power 1 for almost every projection in the unit sphere
\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
.
Further, as random projection converts high dimensional covariates to one dimension space, our tests can detect the local alternatives departing from the null at the rate of
n^{-1/2}h^{-1/4}
. Note that this sensitive rate is not related to the dimension
p
, thus the “curse of dimensionality” for our tests would be significantly alleviated, even when the dimension
p
may be much larger than the sample size
n
. The simulation results in Section 5 validates these theoretical results. As a by-product, we also derive the convergence rate of penalized estimators such as lasso estimators under the misspecified models (the alternative hypotheses) in high dimension settings.

An unexpected result is that for randomly chosen projections, the resulting test statistics can be asymptotic independent under the null hypothesis.
Then any combination of the test statistics (or the corresponding
p
-values) based on a single projection, such as the classic Fisher’s combination (
Fisher 1992
), the harmonic mean
p
-value (
Wilson 2019
), or the Cauchy combination (
Liu and Xie 2020
), can be adopted to enhance the power of the tests. This theoretical result is of independent interest and can be applied to other statistical problems based on random projections.
The resulting integrated tests are asymptotic distribution-free and do not need to resort resampling methods such as the wild bootstrap to determine the critical values. Thus they are easy to implement in practice, especially in high dimension settings.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we construct the test statistic based on random projections. The asymptotic properties of the test statistics is investigated in Section 3. In Section 4, we show that for randomly chosen projections, the resulting test statistics can be asymptotic independent and we then propose two integrated test statistics based random projections for practical use. In Section 5, simulation studies and a real data analysis are conducted to assess the finite sample performance of our tests. Section 6 contains discussions and topics for future study. The technical lemmas and the proofs for the theoretical results are postponed to Supplementary Material.

2
Test statistic construction

To illustrate our method, we mainly discuss goodness of fit tests for high dimensional generalized linear models (GLMs). The extension to more generalized settings can be straightforward.
In generalized linear models, the conditional density of
Y
given
X=x
is

f(y|x,\beta)=c(x,y)\exp\left(\frac{y\theta(x)-b(\theta(x))}{\sigma^{2}}\right),

where
\sigma^{2}
is a dispersion parameter,
c(x,y)
is a positive function and
\theta(x)=\beta_{0}^{\top}x
for some unknown
\beta_{0}=(\beta_{0}^{(1)},\cdots,\beta_{0}^{(p)})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
It is well known that
E(Y|X=x)=\mu(\beta_{0}^{\top}x)
and
var(Y|X=x)=V(\mu(\beta_{0}^{\top}x))
for some given inverse link function
\mu(\cdot)
and known function
V(\cdot)
. In this paper, we focus only on the model checking problem for the conditional mean function
m(x)=E(Y|X=x)
. Then the null hypothesis we want to test is

H_{0}:\mathbb{P}\{E[Y|X]=\mu(\beta_{0}^{\top}X)\}=1,\quad{\rm for\ some}\ \beta_{0}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p},

while the alternative is that the null is totally incorrect, i.e.,

H_{1}:\mathbb{P}\{E[Y|X]\neq\mu(\beta^{\top}X)\}>0,\quad{\rm for\ any}\ \beta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p},

where
\top
denotes the transpose and
\Theta
is a compact set.
Let
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be an i.i.d. sample with the same distribution as
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
.
In high dimensional settings with
p\geq n
, as commented by
Janková et al. 2020
, if
{\bf X}=(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})^{\top}
is of full rank, there always exist a solution
\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
of the system of linear equations
m(X_{i})=\mu(\beta_{0}^{\top}X_{i})
for
i=1,\cdots,n
. This means that high dimensional generalized linear models can never be misspecified in practice without any model structural assumption. Following the setting in
Janková et al. 2020
, we consider the sparse regression models under both the null and alternative hypotheses.

Before introducing the test methodology, we first give some notations. For a vector
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, let
\beta^{(j)}
denote the
j
-th entry of
\beta
and let
\|\beta\|_{q}=(\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta^{(j)}|^{q})^{1/q}
for
q\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
\|\beta\|_{0}
be the number of non-zero entries of
\beta
. Let
I\subset\{1,2,\cdots,p\}
and
\beta_{I}
denotes the vector containing only the entries for
\beta
whose indices are in
I
. For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, let
A_{I}
be the matrix only with the columns from
A
whose indices are in
I
and let
A_{I^{c}}
be the columns of
A
with the indices being in the complement of
I
.
Let
S\subset\{1,\cdots,p\}
be the active set which is the index of
X=(X^{(1)},\cdots,X^{(p)})^{\top}
truly related to the response
Y
.
Under the null
H_{0}
, the true regression parameter
\beta_{0}
is sparse, and the active set becomes
S=\{j:\beta_{0}^{(j)}\neq 0\}
.

Our methodology for checking the goodness of fit of high dimensional generalized linear models depends on the following result.

Lemma 1
.

(i)
Let
Z\in\mathbb{R}
and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be random variables. Then we have

\displaystyle E[Z|X]=0\ \ a.s.

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle E[Z|\alpha^{\top}X]=0\ \ a.s.\quad\forall\ \alpha\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1},

where
\mathcal{S}^{p-1}=\{\alpha|\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\|\alpha\|_{2}=1\}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

(ii)
Suppose that
E|Z|^{2}<\infty
,
E\|X\|_{2}^{k}<\infty
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(E\|X\|_{2}^{k})^{-1/k}=\infty
. If we write

\mathcal{A}=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:E[Z|\alpha^{\top}X]=0\ a.s.\}
, it follows that

\displaystyle E[Z|X]=0\ \ a.s.\ \

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \ \mathcal{A}\ {\rm has\ positive\ Lebesgue\ measure.}

The part (i) of Lemma
1
follows from Lemma 2.1 of Zhu and Li (1998), Lemma 1 of
Escanciano 2006b
, or Lemma 2.1 of
Lavergne and Patilea 2008
. The part (ii) of Lemma
1
is similar to Part (B) of Lemma 1 in
Patilea et al. 2016
and Theorem 2.4 of
Cuesta-Albertos et al. 2019
. The condition
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(E\|X\|_{2}^{k})^{-1/k}=\infty
is the so-called Carleman’s condition which is satisfied if the random vector
X
has a finite moment generating function around the neighbourhood of the original point, see
Cuesta-Albertos et al. 2007
for more details about this condition.
The proof of this lemma will be included in Supplementary Material. Based on Lemma
1
, we can readily obtain the following result which is crucial to the construction of the test statistic.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose that the conditions in part
(ii)
of Lemma
1
holds. If we write
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}:E[Z|\alpha^{\top}X]=0,a.s.\}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{E(Z|X)=0\}=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{0})=1,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{E(Z|X)\neq 0\}>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{0})=0,

where
\mathcal{L}
is the uniform probability measure on the unit sphere
\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
.

Write
\varepsilon(\beta)=Y-\mu(\beta^{\top}X)
, it is readily seen that the null hypothesis
H_{0}
is tantamount to
E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})|X]=0\ a.s.
for some
\beta_{0}\in\Theta
.
For testing the null
H_{0}
, according to Corollary
1
, we can firstly choose a random projection
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
and then conditional on this projection, test the projected null hypothesis:

H_{0}^{\alpha}:\mathbb{P}\{E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X]=0\}=1\quad{\rm for\ some}\ \beta_{0}\in\Theta.

The principle of this testing procedure is as follows. Under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
, the projected null hypothesis
H_{0}^{\alpha}
also holds. Under the alternative
H_{1}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\{E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})|X]\neq 0\}>0
and then the projected null hypothesis
H_{0}^{\alpha}
does not hold
\mathcal{L}
-a.s..
This means that the projected null
H_{0}^{\alpha}
fails for almost all projection
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
under the alternative
H_{1}
.
It is worth mentioned that
Cuesta-Albertos et al. 2019
also used this idea for testing the goodness of fit of functional linear models. Their test statistic is based on projected empirical processes and thus can not be extended directly to high dimensional settings with
p\geq n
, as we have pointed out in the introduction.

2.1
The test statistics

According to Lemma
1
and Corollary
1
, the null hypothesis
H_{0}
is
\mathcal{L}
-a.s. equivalent to test the projected null

H_{0}^{\alpha}:E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X]=0\ a.s.
for some
\beta_{0}\in\Theta
.
With randomly chosen projection
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
,
H_{0}^{\alpha}
is tantamount to

\displaystyle E\{\varepsilon(\beta_{0})E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X]f_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\top}X)\}=E\{E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X]^{2}f_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\top}X)\}=0,

(2.1)

for some
\beta_{0}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, where
f_{\alpha}(\cdot)
denotes the density function of
\alpha^{\top}X
.
Let
\hat{\beta}
be a penalized estimator of
\beta_{0}
under the generalized linear model setting, that is,

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\beta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}}\{L_{n}(\beta)+\sum_{j=1}^{p}p_{\lambda}(|\beta_{j}|)\},

(2.2)

where
L_{n}(\beta)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho(Y_{i},X_{i}^{\top}\beta)
with a general loss function
\rho(\cdot,\cdot)
,
p_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is a penalty function, and
\lambda\geq 0
is the regularization parameter.
The minimizer in (
2.2
) with different penalty functions contains many popular sparse estimators, such as the GLM Lasso estimator, the GLM SCAD estimator, etc.
Then we construct the (informal) test statistic for checking
H_{0}
based on (
2.1
) as

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\hat{\beta})\varepsilon_{j}(\hat{\beta})K_{h}(\alpha^{\top}X_{i}-\alpha^{\top}X_{j}),

where
\varepsilon_{i}(\hat{\beta})=Y_{i}-\mu(\hat{\beta}^{\top}X_{i})
, and
K_{h}(\cdot)=h^{-1}K(\cdot/h)
with
K(\cdot)
being a univariate kernel function and
h
being a bandwidth.

Note that the generalized linear model with link function
\mu(\cdot)
is misspecified under the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}
. In that case, the sparse estimator
\hat{\beta}
is still obtained by minimizing the penalized loss function in (
2.2
). We write
\tilde{S}=\{j:\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{(j)}\neq 0\}
, where

\tilde{\beta}_{0}=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\beta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}}E[\rho(Y,X^{\top}\beta)].

(2.3)

Bühlmann and van de Geer 2015
investigated the asymptotic properties of the lasso estimator
\hat{\beta}
under high dimensional misspecified linear models. They also showed that the support
\tilde{S}
of
\tilde{\beta}_{0}
satisfies that
\tilde{S}\subset S
, when the predictor
X
follows a Gaussian distribution with positive definite covariance matrix.

Lu et al. 2012
derived the oracle property of the adaptive lasso estimator
\hat{\beta}
under the misspecified generalized linear model when the dimension
p
of covariates is fixed. They further showed that
\tilde{S}=S
if the true model is also a generalized linear model with a misspecified link function and the linearity condition is satisfied for the predictor
X
, that is,
E(\beta^{\top}X|\beta_{0}^{\top}X=\beta_{0}^{\top}x)
exists and is linear in
\beta_{0}^{\top}x
for all
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

3
Theoretical results

3.1
Limiting null distribution

In this subsection we investigate the asymptotic properties of
\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}
under the null
H_{0}
when the dimension
p
of covariates may be much larger than the sample size
n
. For this, we further introduce some notions. A random variable
Z\in\mathbb{R}
is called sub-Weibull of order
\tau>0
, if

\|Z\|_{\psi_{\tau}}:=\inf\{\eta>0:E\psi_{\tau}(\frac{|Z|}{\eta})\leq 1\}<\infty,

where
\psi_{\tau}(x)=\exp(x^{\tau})-1
for
x\geq 0
.
By Markov inequality, if
Z
is sub-Weibull of order
\tau
, then

\mathbb{P}(|Z|>t)\leq 2\exp(-\frac{t^{\tau}}{\|Z\|_{\psi_{\tau}}^{\tau}}),\quad{\rm for\ all}\ t\geq 0.

It is readily seen that sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential random variables are the special cases of sub-Weibull distributions with
\tau=2
and
\tau=1
, respectively. Note that the sub-Weibull variables are not required to be zero-mean. More details about sub-Weibull random variables can be found in
Vladimirova et al. 2020
and
Kuchibhotla and Chakrabortty 2022
. The following conditions are needed to derive the asymptotic properties of
\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}
under the null
H_{0}
. Write
\hat{S}=\{j:\hat{\beta}_{0}^{(j)}\neq 0\}
,
\hat{s}=|\hat{S}|
,
s=|S|
, and
\tilde{s}=|\tilde{S}|
.
The notation
C
throughout this paper denotes a constant free of
n
that may be different for each appearance.

(A1) Under the null
H_{0}
, the penalized estimator
\hat{\beta}
satisfies
\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{2}=O_{p}(\sqrt{\frac{s\log{p}}{n}})
and
\|\hat{\beta}-\beta_{0}\|_{1}=O_{p}(\sqrt{\frac{s^{2}\log{p}}{n}})
.

(A2) The random variables
\varepsilon(\beta_{0})
,
\mu^{\prime}(\beta_{0}^{\top}X)
, and
\mu^{\prime\prime}(\beta_{0}^{\top}X)
are sub-Weibull of order
\tau=2
with
\max\{\|\varepsilon(\beta_{0})\|_{\psi_{2}},\|\mu^{\prime}(\beta_{0}^{\top}X)\|_{\psi_{2}},\|\mu^{\prime\prime}(\beta_{0}^{\top}X)\|_{\psi_{2}}\}\leq C<\infty
.
The covariates
X\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is centered and
\alpha^{\top}X
is sub-Weibull of order
\tau=2
with
\|\alpha^{\top}X\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C<\infty
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
.

(A3) The link function
\mu(\cdot)
admits third derivatives, and
|\mu^{\prime\prime\prime}(\beta^{\top}x)|\leq F(x)
for all
\beta\in\Theta
with
F(X)
being sub-weibull of order
\tau\geq 1/3
.

(A4) The univariate kernel function
K(\cdot)
is a continuous, bounded, and symmetric function such that
\int K(u)du=1
,
\int|u|K^{2}(u)du<\infty
,
\int|u|K^{4}(u)du<\infty
, and
\int|u+v|^{i}K^{2}(u+v)dudv<\infty
for
i=1,2,3
.

(A5) We write
\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}(t)=E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})^{2}|\alpha^{\top}X=t]
,
\kappa_{\alpha}^{4}(t)=E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})^{4}|\alpha^{\top}X=t]
, and
g_{\alpha,\theta}(t)=E[\theta^{\top}X\mu^{\prime}(\beta_{0}^{\top}X)|\alpha^{\top}X=t]
. The functions
\sigma^{2}_{\alpha}(t)
,
\kappa^{2}_{\alpha}(t)
, and
g_{\alpha,\theta}(t)
satisfy that for any
u\in\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha,\theta\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
,

\displaystyle|\sigma^{2}_{\alpha}(t+u)f_{\alpha}(t+u)-\sigma^{2}_{\alpha}(t)f_{\alpha}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L|u|,

\displaystyle|\kappa^{4}_{\alpha}(t+u)f_{\alpha}(t+u)-\kappa^{4}_{\alpha}(t)f_{\alpha}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L|u|,

\displaystyle|g_{\alpha,\theta}(t+u)f_{\alpha}(t+u)-g_{\alpha,\theta}(t)f_{\alpha}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L|u|,

where
f_{\alpha}(t)
denotes the density function of
\alpha^{\top}X
.

(A6) The asymptotic variance
\Sigma^{\alpha}
of
nh^{1/2}\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}
under the null
H_{0}
satisfies that
\Sigma^{\alpha}\geq C>0
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
.

Condition (A1) is satisfied by many popular sparse estimators under some regularity conditions, such as the GLM Lasso estimator and the GLM SCAD estimator, when the underlying GLMs are correct. In Supplementary Material, we present the detailed conditions such that (A1) will be satisfied for Lasso estimators.
The assumption of sub-weibull of order
2
(sub-Gaussian) in (A2) is usually imposed in the literature of high dimension data analysis, see
Bühlmann and Van De Geer 2011
for instance. It is used to bounded the tail probability of
U
-statistics in the decomposition of
\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}
when the dimension
p
may be much larger than the sample size
n
.
Condition (A3) is satisfied by many generalized linear models that are usually used in practice, such as Gaussian linear model, logistical regression models, and probit regression models. It is used to control the convergence rate of the remainder in the decomposition of
\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}
.
The sub-Weibull order
\tau\geq 1/3
of
F(X)
is just a technical condition which can be weakened if we imposed more restrict condition on divergence rate of
p
in Theorem
3.1
.
Conditions (A4) and (A5) are usually used in the literature of nonparametric estimations, see
Rao 1983
and
Zhu and Fang 1996
for instance.
Condition (A6) is used to ensure the weak convergence of our test statistic
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
in (
3.1
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that Conditions (A1)-(A6) hold. If
sh^{1/2}\log{p}=o(1)
,
s^{4}\log^{3}{p}=o(nh)
, and
\log^{5}{p}=o(n)
as
n\to\infty
, then under the null
H_{0}
and conditional on
\alpha
, we have

nh^{1/2}\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}/(\Sigma^{\alpha})^{1/2}\longrightarrow N(0,1),\quad in\ distribution,

where
\Sigma^{\alpha}=2\int\sigma_{\alpha}^{4}(t)f_{\alpha}^{2}(t)dt\int K^{2}(u)du
is the asymptotic variance of
nh^{1/2}\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}
, which can be estimated by

\hat{\Sigma}^{\alpha}=\frac{2h}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\varepsilon_{i}(\hat{\beta})^{2}\varepsilon_{j}(\hat{\beta})^{2}K_{h}^{2}(\alpha^{\top}X_{i}-\alpha^{\top}X_{j}).

According to Theorem
3.1
, our final test statistic is

\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}=\sqrt{\frac{n-1}{n}}\frac{nh^{1/2}\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}}{(\hat{\Sigma}^{\alpha})^{1/2}}=\frac{\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\varepsilon_{i}(\hat{\beta})\varepsilon_{j}(\hat{\beta})K(\frac{\alpha^{\top}X_{i}-\alpha^{\top}X_{j}}{h})}{\left(2\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}\varepsilon_{i}(\hat{\beta})^{2}\varepsilon_{j}(\hat{\beta})^{2}K(\frac{\alpha^{\top}X_{i}-\alpha^{\top}X_{j}}{h})^{2}\right)^{1/2}}.

(3.1)

The limiting null distribution of
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
follows from Theorem
3.1
.

Corollary 2
.

Suppose that Conditions (A1)-(A6) hold. If
sh^{1/2}\log{p}=o(1)
,
s^{4}\log^{3}{p}=o(nh)
, and
\log^{5}{p}=o(n)
as
n\to\infty
, under the null
H_{0}
and conditional on
\alpha
, we have
\hat{\Sigma}^{\alpha}=\Sigma^{\alpha}+o_{p}(1)
and then

\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}\longrightarrow N(0,1),\quad in\ distribution.

The proofs of Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
2
are given in Supplementary Material.
It is worth mentioned that we do not require the variable selection consistency of the penalized estimator
\hat{\beta}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(\hat{S}=S)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, in Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
2
.
If the variable selection consistency holds for
\hat{\beta}
, then we only need to work on the event
\{\hat{S}=S\}
and the proofs for Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
2
can be much easier.
However, the variable selection consistency for lasso estimators requires some restrictive condition, such as Irrepresentable Condition, see
Meinshausen and Bühlmann 2006
,
Zhao and Yu 2006
, and
Zou 2006
for more details about this issue. By exploring the tail probabilities of the sums of sub-Weibull random variables and
U
-statistics, we found that our proofs for Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
2
do not need the variable selection consistency of the penalized estimator
\hat{\beta}
. Thus, the proposed test is applicable to any penalized estimator satisfying Condition (A1).
Further, the growing rate of
p
in Theorem
3.1
is allowed to be of exponential order in relation to the sample size when the bandwidth
h
is properly chosen. This shows that our test can be applied for the goodness of fit of ultra high dimensional regression models.

3.2
Power analysis

We now investigate the power analysis of the test statistic
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
under the local and global alternative hypotheses. Consider a sequence of alternative hypotheses that converge to the null hypothesis
H_{0}
at the rate
r_{n}
:

H_{1n}:m(x)=E(Y|X=x)=\mu(\beta_{0}^{\top}x)+r_{n}R(x),

where
r_{n}
is a sequence of positive numbers satisfying
r_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and
R(\cdot)
is a non-constant function with
\mathbb{P}(R(X)=0)<1
.
Here we also assume the sparsity for the regression function
m(\cdot)
.
To derive the asymptotic properties of
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
under the alternatives, we need some additional conditions. Recall that
\tilde{\beta}_{0}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the minimizer of
L(\beta)=E[\rho(Y,X^{\top}\beta)]
.

(A7). Suppose
\tilde{\beta}_{0}
is unique and lies in the interior of
\Theta
. Under the global alternative
H_{1}
,

\|\hat{\beta}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}=O_{p}(\tilde{s}\sqrt{\frac{\log{p}}{n}})\quad{\rm and}\quad\|\hat{\beta}_{0}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{2}=O_{p}(\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{s}\log{p}}{n}}),

where
\tilde{s}=|\tilde{S}|
with
\tilde{S}
being the support of
\tilde{\beta}_{0}
.
Under the local alternative
H_{1n}
with
r_{n}\to 0
, we have
\tilde{\beta}_{0}-\beta_{0}=r_{n}M^{L}+o_{p}(r_{n})
with
M^{L}_{\tilde{S}_{1}^{c}}=0
and
\|M^{L}\|_{2}=O(1)
,

\|\hat{\beta}_{0}-\beta_{0}\|_{1}=O_{p}(\tilde{s}\sqrt{\frac{\log{p}}{n}}+\sqrt{\tilde{s}_{1}}r_{n}),\quad{\rm and}\quad\|\hat{\beta}_{0}-\beta_{0}\|_{2}=O_{p}(\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{s}\log{p}}{n}}+r_{n}),

where
\tilde{s}_{1}=|\tilde{S}_{1}|
and
\tilde{S}_{1}=S\cup\tilde{S}
with
S
being the support of
\beta_{0}
.

(A8) The random variables
\varepsilon(\tilde{\beta}_{0})
,
\mu^{\prime}(\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)
, and
\mu^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)
are sub-Weibull of order
\tau=2
with
\max\{\|\varepsilon(\tilde{\beta}_{0})\|_{\psi_{2}},\|\mu^{\prime}(\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)\|_{\psi_{2}},\|\mu^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)\|_{\psi_{2}}\}\leq C<\infty
.

(A9) We write
\tilde{\sigma}_{\alpha}^{2}(t)=E[\varepsilon^{2}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X=t]
,
\tilde{g}_{\alpha}(t)=E[\varepsilon(\tilde{\beta}_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X=t]
,
\tilde{g}_{\alpha,\theta}(t)=E[\theta^{\top}X\mu^{\prime}(\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)|\alpha^{\top}X=t]
, and
R_{\alpha}^{(i)}(t)=E[R^{i}(X)|\alpha^{\top}X=t]
with
i=1,2
.
The functions
\tilde{\sigma}_{\alpha}^{2}(t)
,
\tilde{g}_{\alpha}(t)
,
\tilde{g}_{\alpha,\theta}(t)
, and
R^{(i)}_{\alpha}(t)
satisfy that for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha,\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
,

\displaystyle|\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{\alpha}(t+u)f_{\alpha}(t+u)-\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{\alpha}(t)f_{\alpha}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L|u|,

\displaystyle|\tilde{g}_{\alpha}(t+u)f_{\alpha}(t+u)-\tilde{g}_{\alpha}(t)f_{\alpha}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L|u|

\displaystyle|\tilde{g}_{\alpha,\theta}(t+u)f_{\alpha}(t+u)-\tilde{g}_{\alpha,\theta}(t)f_{\alpha}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L|u|

\displaystyle|R^{(i)}_{\alpha}(t+u)f_{\alpha}(t+u)-R^{(i)}_{\alpha}(t)f_{\alpha}(t)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L|u|.

In Supplementary Material, we will show that the GLM Lasso estimator satisfies Condition (A7) under the global and local alternative hypotheses. The asymptotic properties of other penalized estimators, such as the GLM SCAD estimator, can be similar established under the alternatives. According to
Lu et al. 2012
and
Bühlmann and van de Geer 2015
, the support
\tilde{S}
of
\tilde{\beta}_{0}
can be a subset of the true active set under the alternative hypotheses (misspecified models). Conditions (A8)-(A9) are similar to (A2) and (A5) under the null
H_{0}
, respectively.
The next theorem presents the asymptotic properties of the test statistic
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
conditional on the projection
\alpha
under various alternative hypotheses in high dimensional settings and its proof is provided in Supplementary Material.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that Conditions (A2)-(A9) hold.

(1) Under the global alternative
H_{1}
, if
\tilde{s}^{2}\log{p}=o(nh)
and
\log{p}=o(n^{1/5})
as
n\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\{[E(\varepsilon(\tilde{\beta}_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X)]^{2}f_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\top}X)\}+o_{p}(1)

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int\tilde{\sigma}_{\alpha}^{4}(t)f_{\alpha}^{2}(t)dt\int K^{2}(u)du+o_{p}(1).

(2) Under the local alternative
H_{1n}
with
r_{n}=1/\sqrt{nh^{1/2}}
, if
\tilde{s}_{1}h^{1/2}\log{p}=o(1)
and
\tilde{s}_{1}=o(nh^{5/2})
as
n\to\infty
, then

\displaystyle nh^{1/2}\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}/\hat{\Sigma}^{\alpha}\longrightarrow N(\gamma,1),\quad in\ distribution,

where
\gamma
is a non-zero constant.

Under the global alternative
H_{1}
, it follows from Corollary
1
that
\mathbb{P}\{E[\varepsilon(\tilde{\beta}_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X]\neq 0\}>0
for almost every
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
.
Conditional on
\alpha
with
\mathbb{P}\{E[\varepsilon(\tilde{\beta}_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X]\neq 0\}>0
, we have

E\{[E(\varepsilon(\tilde{\beta}_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X)]^{2}f_{\alpha}(\alpha^{\top}X)\}>0.

Theorem
3.2
shows that the test statistic
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}=nh^{1/2}\hat{V}_{n}^{\alpha}/\hat{\Sigma}^{\alpha}
diverges to infinity at the rate
nh^{1/2}
. Therefore, our test
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
is consistent with asymptotic power 1 under the alternative
H_{1}
for almost every
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
in high dimensional settings.
Further, combining Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
(2), the proposed test can detect the local alternatives distinct from the null at the rate of order
1/\sqrt{nh^{1/2}}
.
This rate is in line with the results of dimension reduction tests such as
Lavergne and Patilea 2008
;
Lavergne and Patilea 2012
and
Guo et al. 2016
in fixed dimensional settings.
Recall that the classic local smoothing tests (see
Härdle and Mammen 1993
and
Zheng 1996
for instance) in low dimension settings usually can only detect local alternatives that converge to the null at the rate
1/\sqrt{nh^{p/2}}
. Thus, these tests suffer severely from the “curse of dimensionality” and can not be applied in high dimension settings. While the proposed test has the sensitive rate
1/\sqrt{nh^{1/2}}
, even when the dimension
p
may substantially exceed the sample size. Note that this sensitive rate is independent of the dimension
p
of covariates, this indicates that our test can largely alleviate the “curse of dimensionality” in high dimension settings. Simulation studies in Section 5 also show that the proposed test is less affected by the dimension of covariates.

4
Integrated test statistics based random projections

With randomly chosen projection
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}^{p}
, we can obtain a series of test statistics
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
. Recall that the proposed test is
\mathcal{L}
-a.s. consistent under the global alternative hypothesis. Thus it may still lose power for some chosen projections.
Another potential problem is that the values of our tests may vary for different projections, which may also affect the power performance of the proposed tests. To attack these problems, a natural idea is to combine the various projected test statistics
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
to form a final test statistic to enhance the power.

An interesting and unexpected result is that for randomly chosen projections
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
, the resulting test statistics
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{1}},\cdots,\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{d}}
can be asymptotically independent under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
. The basic principle is as follows. We assume without loss of generalization the random projections
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}
are pairwise linear uncorrelated and take
d=2
in the following for illustration. According to the proofs for Theorem
3.1
and Corollary
2
, we have under the null
H_{0}
,

\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}=\frac{nh^{1/2}V_{n0}^{\alpha}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha})^{1/2}}+o_{p}(1),

where
V_{n0}^{\alpha}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\varepsilon_{i}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{j}(\beta_{0})K_{h}(\alpha^{\top}X_{i}-\alpha^{\top}X_{j})
and
\Sigma^{\alpha}=2\int\sigma_{\alpha}^{4}(t)f_{\alpha}^{2}(t)dt\int K^{2}(u)du
.
Next we show that the covariance of
(nh^{1/2}V_{n0}^{\alpha_{1}},nh^{1/2}V_{n0}^{\alpha_{2}})
converges to zero when
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
are linear uncorrelated. For this, we further impose some additional assumption.

(A10) We write
\sigma_{\alpha_{12}}^{2}(s,t)=E(\varepsilon^{2}(\beta_{0})|\alpha_{1}^{\top}X=s,\alpha_{2}^{\top}X=t)
and
f_{\alpha_{12}}(s,t)
is the joint density function of
(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X,\alpha_{2}^{\top}X)
. The function
\sigma_{\alpha_{12}}^{2}(s,t)f_{\alpha_{12}}(s,t)
satisfies that for any
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
,

|\sigma_{\alpha_{12}}^{2}(s+u,t+v)f_{\alpha_{12}}(s+u,t+v)-\sigma_{\alpha_{12}}^{2}(s,t)f_{\alpha_{12}}(s,t)|\leq L(|u|+|v|).

By Condition (A10) and some elementary calculations, we can show that

cov(nh^{1/2}V_{n0}^{\alpha_{1}},nh^{1/2}V_{n0}^{\alpha_{2}})=2h\int\sigma_{\alpha_{12}}^{4}(s,t)f_{\alpha_{12}}^{2}(s,t)dsdt+O(h^{2})=o(1).

Note that under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
,

(\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{1}},\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{2}})=\left(\frac{nh^{1/2}V_{n0}^{\alpha_{1}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}},\frac{nh^{1/2}V_{n0}^{\alpha_{2}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}\right)+o_{p}(1).

Under some regularity conditions, we can show that
(\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{1}},\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{2}})
is asymptotic independent under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
.
More specific result is summarized in the following theorem and its proof is given in Supplementary Material.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that (A10) and the conditions in Theorem
3.1
hold and the random projections
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
are pairwise linear uncorrelated. Then under the null
H_{0}
and conditional on
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}
, we have

(\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{1}},\cdots,\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{d}})\longrightarrow N(0,I_{d}),\quad in\ distribution,

where
N(0,I_{d})
denotes the
d
-dimension standard normal distribution.

For each projection
\alpha_{i}
, the asymptotic
p
-value of
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{i}}
is given by

\hat{p}_{\alpha_{i}}=1-\Phi(\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{i}})
,
where
\Phi(\cdot)
denotes the standard normal distribution.
By Theorem
4.1
and the Continuous mapping theorem, we have under
H_{0}
,

\displaystyle(\hat{p}_{\alpha_{1}},\cdots,\hat{p}_{\alpha_{d}})\longrightarrow(U_{1},\cdots,U_{d}),\quad in\ distribution,

(4.1)

where
U_{1},\cdots,U_{d}
are i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
(0,1)
.
Then the integrated test statistic can be any combination of these
p
-values, such as the classic Fisher’s combination method (
Fisher 1992
), the harmonic mean
p
-value (HMP,
Wilson 2019
), and the Cauchy combination method (
Liu and Xie 2020
).
Although these
p
-values are asymptotic independent, they may be correlated at the sample level. Thus we construct the integrated test statistics based on the harmonic mean
p
-value and the Cauchy combination method in this paper, as both of these two combination of
p
-values are robust to the dependency of the
p
-values, see
Wilson 2019
and
Liu and Xie 2020
for more details.
The integrated test statistic based on the harmonic mean
p
-value is

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{hmp}=(\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}/\hat{p}_{\alpha_{i}})^{-1}=(\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}/[1-\Phi(\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{i}})])^{-1},

(4.2)

while the integrated test statistic based on the Cauchy combination is

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{Cauchy}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}\tan\{(\frac{1}{2}-\hat{p}_{\alpha_{i}})\pi\}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}\tan\{(\Phi(\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{i}})-\frac{1}{2})\pi\},

(4.3)

where
w_{i}\geq 0
are the weights satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}=1
.
In this paper, we simply use the equal weights, i.e.,
w_{i}=1/d
for
i=1,\cdots,d
, which is also suggested by
Wilson 2019
and
Liu and Xie 2020
.
By (
4.1
) and applying the Continuous Mapping Theorem again, we have under the null
H_{0}
,

\hat{T}_{hmp}\longrightarrow(\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}/U_{i})^{-1},\quad in\ distribution,

(4.4)

and

\hat{T}_{Cauchy}\longrightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}\tan\{(\frac{1}{2}-U_{i})\pi\},\quad in\ distribution.

(4.5)

It is easy to see that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}\tan\{(\frac{1}{2}-U_{i})\pi\}
has a standard Cauchy distribution
Cauchy(0,1)
. Thus, the asymptotic critical values of
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
can be determined by quantiles of the standard Cauchy distribution.
For the critical values of
\hat{T}_{hmp}
, by Corollary 1.3.8 of
Mikosch 1999
or the formula (5) of
Wilson 2019
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{(\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}/U_{i})^{-1}\leq\tau\}=\mathbb{P}\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}/U_{i}\geq\tau^{-1}\}\sim(\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i})\mathbb{P}\{U_{i}^{-1}\geq\tau^{-1}\}=\tau,\quad{\rm as}\ \tau\to 0^{+}.

Here
f(\tau)\sim g(\tau)
as
\tau\to 0^{+}
means
\lim_{\tau\to 0^{+}}f(\tau)/g(\tau)\to 1
.
Thus, for any (small) significant level
\tau
, we have
\mathbb{P}\{(\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}/U_{i})^{-1}\leq\tau\}\approx\tau
and the asymptotic critical value of
\hat{T}_{hmp}
is
\tau
itself. More detailed properties of the harmonic mean
p
-value can be found in
Wilson 2019
.

Corollary
2
and the assertions (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) show that the test
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
with random projection
\alpha
and the integrated tests
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
and
\hat{T}_{hmp}
are asymptotically distribution-free. Thus we do not need to resort resample methods such as the wild bootstrap to approximate the limiting null distribution.
Under the global alternative
H_{1}
, by Theorem
3.2
, the asymptotic
p
-values
\hat{p}_{\alpha_{i}}
tends to
0
. Then for almost all
\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}
, the integrated test statistic
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
tends to infinity while the harmonic mean
p
-value
\hat{T}_{hmp}
tends to zero.
In practice,
Liu and Xie 2020
showed that a small value of
\hat{p}_{\alpha_{i}}
leads to a very large value of
\tan\{(\frac{1}{2}-\hat{p}_{\alpha_{i}})\pi\}
and the integrated test statistic
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
is essentially dominated by a few of the smallest
p
-values. Similar phenomenon for the harmonic mean
p
-value
\hat{T}_{hmp}
is also discovered by
Wilson 2019
.
Thus, the integrated tests
\hat{T}_{hmp}
and
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
can still have good power performance even if some test statistics
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{i}}
in (
4.2
) or (
4.3
) may be not consistent for the projection
\alpha_{i}
.
The simulations studies in Section 5 also show that the integrated tests
\hat{T}_{hmp}
and
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
perform slightly better than
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
based on a single projection
\alpha
in finite samples.

5
Numerical studies

5.1
Simulation studies

In this subsection, we investigate the performance of the proposed tests in finite samples when the dimension
p
of covariates
X
may exceed the sample size
n
. We consider the test statistics
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
with a random projection
\alpha
and
\hat{T}_{n}^{\hat{\beta}}
with the projection
\hat{\beta}/\|\hat{\beta}\|_{2}
, where
\hat{\beta}
is the the estimated parameter of
\beta_{0}
. This projected direction
\hat{\beta}
is also used in
Stute and Zhu 2002
. The harmonic mean
p
-value
\hat{T}_{hmp}
and the Cauchy combination test
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
are based on the estimated projection
\hat{\beta}/\|\hat{\beta}\|_{2}
and
10
randomly chosen projections.
We also conduct some simulations with various numbers of random projections. It turns out the integrated tests
\hat{T}_{hmp}
and
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
cannot maintain the significant level when the number of projections becomes large.
To compute the proposed test statistics, similar to
Lavergne and Patilea 2008
, we use the normal kernel and choose the bandwidth
h=2n^{-1/(4+\hat{q})}
where
\hat{q}
is the number of the nonzero elements of the estimated parameter
\hat{\beta}
. To get an accurate estimation of
\beta_{0}
under the null, we consider the post-lasso estimators of
\beta_{0}
which apply the least square or maximum likelihood to the model selected by the lasso estimator.
Belloni and Chernozhukov 2013
showed that the post-lasso estimator can perform at least as well as the lasso estimator in terms of the rate of convergence, and has the advantage of a smaller bias.
A R-package PLStests is available to implement our test statistics.

We compare our tests with recent goodness-of-fit tests
RP_{n}
proposed by
Shah and Bühlmann 2018
and
GRP_{n}
proposed by
Janková et al. 2020
in high dimensional settings. We first consider the sample size
n=200
and the dimensions
p=10,100,200,400
. For comparison, we also conduct the simulations with the cases of
(n,p)=(800,500),(2000,3000)
. These two cases follow the configurations in the simulation study of
Janková et al. 2020
.
In the simulations that follow, the simulation results are based on the average of 1000 replications, the significant level is
\tau=0.05
, and
a=0
corresponds to the null hypothesis while
a\neq 0
to the alternative hypotheses. The simulation results of the test
RP_{n}
are obtained by implementing the R package RPtests, while the results of
GRP_{n}
are obtained by running codes on the website
https://github.com/jankova/GRPtests
which are posted by the authors of
GRP_{n}
proposed by
Janková et al. 2020
.

In the first simulation study, we consider the case of testing Gaussian linear models in high dimension settings.

Study 1.
Generate data from the following models:

\displaystyle H_{11}:Y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{0}^{\top}X+a\exp(-(\beta_{0}^{\top}X)^{2})+\varepsilon;

\displaystyle H_{12}:Y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{0}^{\top}X+a\cos(0.6\pi\beta_{0}^{\top}X)+\varepsilon;

\displaystyle H_{13}:Y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{0}^{\top}X+a(\beta_{1}^{\top}X)^{2}+\varepsilon;

\displaystyle H_{14}:Y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{0}^{\top}X+a\exp(\beta_{1}^{\top}X)+\varepsilon;

where
\beta_{0}=(1,1,1,1,1,0,\cdots,0)^{\top}/\sqrt{5}
,
\beta_{1}=(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{p_{1}},0,\dots,0)^{\top}/\sqrt{p_{1}}
with
p_{1}=\min(p,[2n^{1/3}])
. The covariate
X
is
N(0,\Sigma_{1})
or
N(0,\Sigma_{2})
independent of the standard Gaussian error term
\varepsilon
, where
\Sigma_{1}=I_{p}
and
\Sigma_{2}=(1/2^{|i-j|})_{p\times p}
.

The simulation results are reported in Tables 1-4. It can be observed that our tests
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
and
\hat{T}_{n}^{\hat{\beta}}
can maintain the significant level very well in all cases. While the integrated tests
\hat{T}_{hmp}
and
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
usually have slightly larger empirical sizes than
0.05
. This may be because of the double approximation of the critical values for the integrated tests. The tests
RP_{n}
and

are generally conservative with smaller empirical sizes. For the empirical power, we can see that the proposed tests
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
,
\hat{T}_{n}^{\hat{\beta}}
,
\hat{T}_{hmp}
, and
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
show excellent power performance and are insensitive to the correlation and the dimensionality of covariates
X
. This means our tests are less affected by the “curse of dimensionality”.
It is worth to mention that despite being based on a single projection, the tests
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
and
\hat{T}_{n}^{\hat{\beta}}
exhibit good power performance in most cases. This may validate our theoretical result that the projected tests are
\mathcal{L}
-a.s. consistent under the alternatives.
The integrated tests demonstrate their superior performance as
\hat{T}_{hmp}
and
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
usually have higher empirical powers compared to tests
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
and
\hat{T}_{n}^{\hat{\beta}}
with a single projection.
In contrast, the tests
RP_{n}
and
GRP_{n}
can have high empirical powers when the predictor

). However, they have very low empirical powers in most cases, especially when the dimensionality of the predictor

5.2
A real data example

In this subsection, we evaluate the proposed tests through an analysis of a classification task aimed at distinguishing between sonar signals bounced off a metal cylinder and those bounced off a roughly cylindrical rock. The dataset is available at

https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/151/connectionist+bench+sonar+mines+vs+rocks
. There are 208 observations in this dataset collected by bouncing sonar signals off a metal cylinder or a roughly cylindrical rock. Each observation has 60 features ranging from
0
to
1
, representing the energy within a particular frequency band, integrated over a certain period of time.
Let
X=(X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{60})
represent the predictor vector and
Y
be the response variable, where
Y=0
if the signal is bounced off a metal cylinder and
Y=1
if it is bounced off a roughly cylindrical rock. All predictor variables are standardize separately for easy explanation. We first check whether a sparse linear logistical regression model is adequate for a classification task. Then we apply our tests
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
,
\hat{T}_{n}^{\hat{\beta}}
,
\hat{T}_{hmp}
, and
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
to check whether
E(Y|X)=\exp{(\beta^{T}X)}/(1+\exp{(\beta^{T}X)})
is plausible or not. The choice for the bandwidth
h
and the projections are the same as in the simulation studies. The
p
-values of
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
,
\hat{T}_{n}^{\hat{\beta}}
,
\hat{T}_{hmp}
, and
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
are about
0.046
,
0.01
,
0.002
, and
0.002
, respectively, which indicate that a linear logistical regression model may be not adequate to fit this data. We then consider a quadratic logistical regression model by incorporating the squared predictors. Write
X_{new}=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{60},X_{1}^{2},X_{2}^{2},\dots,X_{60}^{2})
. When applying the proposed tests to the quadratic logistical regression model, the corresponding
p
-values of
\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}
,
\hat{T}_{n}^{\hat{\beta}}
,
\hat{T}_{hmp}
, and
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
are
0.723,0.688,0.933
, and
0.684
, respectively. This means that a quadratic logistical regression model may be plausible.

We further compare the predictive accuracy of the linear logistical regression model with the quadratic logistical regression model. We conducted
100
runs to reduce the bias resulting from the randomness in selecting samples for the training and testing sets. In each run, the dataset was randomly shuffled and split into a training set (
80\%
) and a testing set (
20\%
). The models are estimated using the training set, and the predictive accuracy of these models is computed using the testing set. We then get an average predictive accuracy of
76.51\%
for the quadratic logistical regression model, which is better than the linear logistical regression model with the accuracy of
72.46\%
. This confirms that a quadratic logistical regression model may be useful to fit this dataset.

6
Conclusion

This paper developed goodness of fit tests for checking generalized linear models when the dimension of covariates may substantially exceed the sample size. It is well known that existing goodness of fit tests for regressions in the literature usually cannot be extended to high dimension settings due to challenges arising from the “curse of dimensionality”, or dependencies on the normality of parameter estimators. While our tests do not depend on the asymptotic expansion or the normality of parameter estimators. We investigated the asymptotic properties of the proposed test statistics under the null and the alternatives, when the growing rate of the dimension
p
is of exponential order in relation to the sample size.
Further, our tests can detect the local alternative departing from the null at the rate of
n^{-1/2}h^{-1/4}
. As this detective rate is not related to the dimension
p
, the “curse of dimensionality” for our tests can be largely alleviated. The simulation results in Section 5 validates these theoretical results in finite samples. An interesting phenomenon is that for randomly chosen projections, the resulting test statistics may be asymptotic independent. This result can also be applied to other statistical problems based on random projections, such as high dimensional significant tests.
However, employing random projections introduces variability in the values of the test statistics across various projections, even for the integrated tests
\hat{T}_{hmp}
and
\hat{T}_{Cauchy}
. One potential solution is to utilize the optimal projection under the alternatives to construct the test statistics.
Further, the data splitting is completely avoided when constructing our test statistic and deriving its asymptotic properties in high dimension settings. Thus, our method can be extended to develop goodness of fit tests for high-dimensional dependent data.
This research is ongoing.

7.1
Technical lemmas

In this subsection, we give some lemmas which will be used in the proofs of the theoretical results in the main text.
Consider an
U
-statistic

U_{n}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}H(Z_{i},Z_{j}),

where the kernel function
H(\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric, and
Z_{i}
and
H
may depend on the sample size
n
. Let
G(x,y)=E[H(Z_{1},x)H(Z_{1},y)]
,
\sigma^{2}_{H}=var\{H(Z_{1},Z_{2})\}
, and
\sigma^{2}_{U_{n}}=var(U_{n})
.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
E[H(Z_{1},Z_{2})|Z_{1}]=0
a.s. and
E[H^{2}(Z_{1},Z_{2})]<\infty
. If

\{E[G^{2}(Z_{1},Z_{2})]+n^{-1}E[H^{4}(Z_{1},Z_{2})]\}/[EH^{2}(Z_{1},Z_{2})]^{2}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, then

\frac{nU_{n}}{\{2E[H^{2}(Z_{1},Z_{2})]\}^{1/2}}\longrightarrow N(0,1),\quad{in\ distribution}.

Lemma
2
is a slightly modified version of Theorem 1 of
Hall 1984
and its proof is based on the martingale central limit theorem, see
Hall 1984
for more details.
The next two lemmas will be used to bound the tail probability of random variables in high dimensional settings.
Recall that a random variable
X\in\mathbb{R}
is called sub-Weibull of order
\tau>0
, if

\|X\|_{\psi_{\tau}}:=\inf\{\eta>0:E\psi_{\tau}(\frac{|X|}{\eta})\leq 1\}<\infty,

where
\psi_{\tau}(x)=\exp(x^{\tau})-1
for
x\geq 0
.
The following lemma presents the tail probability for sums of independent sub-Weibull random variables of order
\tau
. Write
\|x\|_{p}=(\sum_{j=1}^{p}|x^{(i)}|^{p})^{1/p}
for any
x=(x^{(1)},\cdots,x^{(p)})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
p\geq 1
.

Lemma 3
.

If
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
are independent mean zero random variables in
\mathbb{R}
with
\|X_{i}\|_{\psi_{\tau}}<\infty
for all
1\leq i\leq n
and some
\tau>0
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}|\geq a_{1\tau}\|\beta\|_{2}\sqrt{t}+a_{2\tau}\|\beta\|_{c(\tau)}t^{1/\tau})\leq 2\exp(-t),\quad\forall\ t\geq 0,

where
\beta=\frac{1}{n}(\|X_{1}\|_{\psi_{\tau}},\cdots,\|X_{n}\|_{\psi_{\tau}})^{\top}
,
a_{1\tau}
and
a_{2\tau}
are two constants relying only on
\tau
,
c(\tau)=\infty
for
\tau\leq 1
, and
c(\tau)=\tau/(\tau-1)
for
\tau>1
.
By setting
u=a_{1\tau}\|\beta\|_{2}\sqrt{t}+a_{2\tau}\|\beta\|_{c(\tau)}t^{1/\tau}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}|>u)\leq 2\exp\left(-(\frac{u}{2a_{1\tau}\|\beta\|_{2}})^{2}\wedge(\frac{u}{2a_{2\tau}\|\beta\|_{c(\tau)}})^{\tau}\right),

where
s\wedge t=\min\{s,t\}
.

Lemma
3
is a variant of Theorem 3.4 in
Kuchibhotla and Chakrabortty 2022
and its proof can be found in the Appendix of their paper. We may also need the results for the tail probability of
U
-statistics. Let
\Phi(x)
denote the cumulative distribution function of a standard normal distribution.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that
E[H(Z_{1},Z_{2})]=0
and
var\{E[H(Z_{1},Z_{2})|Z_{1}]\}>0
.

(1) If there exist a constant
C>0
such that

E\exp\{tH(Z_{1},Z_{2})\}<\infty,\quad t\in[-C,C],

then, in the interval
0<x\leq o(n^{1/6})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\sigma_{U_{n}}^{-1}U_{n}<-x\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi(-x)(1+o(1)),

\displaystyle 1-\mathbb{P}\{\sigma_{U_{n}}^{-1}U_{n}<x\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[1-\Phi(x)](1+o(1)),\quad{\rm as}\ n\to\infty.

(7.1)

(2) If there exist two constants
C>0
and
\gamma>0
such that

E\exp\{t|H(Z_{1},Z_{2})|^{1/(1+\gamma)}\}<\infty,\quad t\in[-C,C],

then, in the interval
0<x\leq o(n^{\frac{1}{2(3+2\gamma)}})
, (
4
) continues to hold.

The results of Lemma
4
can be found in Theorems 1 and 2 of
Aleshkyavichene 2006
(the notations may be different from here). Note that (
4
) implies that for
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}\{|\sigma_{U_{n}}^{-1}U_{n}|>x\}=[1-\Phi(x)](2+o(1))\leq C\exp(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}).

7.2
Lasso estimators under the null and alternative hypotheses

In this subsection, we investigate the convergence rate of the lasso estimators under the null (specified models) and alternative (misspecified models) when the dimension
p
of covariates may be larger than the sample size
n
.
Let
\hat{\beta}
be the Lasso estimator of
\beta_{0}
under the generalized linear model setting:

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}:=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\beta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}}\{L_{n}(\beta)+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta^{(j)}|\},

(7.2)

where
L_{n}(\beta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho(Y_{i},X_{i}^{\top}\beta)
,
\rho
denotes some general loss function, and
\lambda>0
is the regularization parameter.
Write
L(\beta)=E[\rho(Y,X^{\top}\beta)]
and

\tilde{\beta}_{0}=\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{\beta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}}L(\beta).

For simplicity, we focus on Gaussian linear models in this paper with the square loss function

\rho(Y,\beta^{\top}X)=(Y-\beta^{\top}X)^{2}.

The extension for lasso estimators for generalized linear models with general loss function in high dimension settings can be similar.
Note that under the null hypothesis
H_{0}
, the linear model is correctly specified for some true parameter
\beta_{0}
. It is easy to see that
\tilde{\beta}_{0}=\beta_{0}
under the null
H_{0}
. While under the alternative hypotheses
H_{1}
, the linear model is misspecified with a pseudo-parameter
\tilde{\beta}_{0}
.
Suppose
\tilde{\beta}_{0}
is unique and lies in the interior of
\Theta
.

To investigate the convergence rate of
\hat{\beta}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}
under both the null
H_{0}
and the alternative
H_{1}
, we introduce some notations and conditions.
Write

\displaystyle W(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L(\beta)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0}),

\displaystyle W_{n}(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L_{n}(\beta)-L_{n}(\tilde{\beta}_{0}),

\displaystyle W_{d}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq r}|W_{n}(\beta)-W(\beta)|.

Recall that
\tilde{S}=\{j:\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{(j)}\neq 0,1\leq j\leq p\}
be the support of
\tilde{\beta}_{0}
and
\tilde{s}=|\tilde{S}|
.
Consider cones of the form

\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}=\{\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\Delta_{\tilde{S}^{c}}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon\|\Delta_{\tilde{S}}\|_{1}\},

where
\epsilon>0
.
We need the following Compatibility Condition (CC) to derive the asymptotic properties of
\hat{\beta}
.

(CC) For the set
\tilde{S}
, there exists a constant
\phi_{\tilde{S}}\geq C>0
such that

\|\beta_{\tilde{S}}\|_{1}^{2}\leq\frac{\tilde{s}\beta^{\top}E[XX^{\top}]\beta}{\phi_{\tilde{S}}^{2}},\quad{\rm for\ all}\ \beta\in\mathcal{C}_{2},

where
C
is a constant independent of
n
.
The Compatibility Condition (CC) is usually imposed in the literature for investigating the asymptotic properties of lasso estimators, see Chapter 6 of
Bühlmann and Van De Geer 2011
for instance.
Write
\epsilon^{*}=\tilde{s}\lambda^{2}/\phi_{\tilde{S}}
,
r=\epsilon^{*}/\lambda_{0}
, and define an event

\mathcal{F}=\{W_{d}(r)\leq\epsilon^{*}\}=\{W_{d}(r)\leq r\lambda_{0}\}.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose the Compatibility Condition (CC) holds for
\tilde{S}
. If
\lambda\geq 6\lambda_{0}
, then on the event
\mathcal{F}=\{W_{d}(r)\leq\epsilon^{*}\}
, we have

\frac{L(\hat{\beta})-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})}{2}+\lambda\|\hat{\beta}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq 3\epsilon^{*}.

Proof of Lemma
5
.
Write
t=\frac{r}{r+\|\hat{\beta}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}}
and
u=t\hat{\beta}+(1-t)\tilde{\beta}_{0}
.
It is easy to see that
\|u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq r
.
By the convexity of
L_{n}(\beta)+\lambda\|\beta\|_{1}
, we have

L_{n}(u)+\lambda\|u\|_{1}\leq L_{n}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})+\lambda\|\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}.

Then on this event
\mathcal{F}=\{W_{d}(r)\leq\epsilon^{*}\}
,

\displaystyle L(u)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})+\lambda\|u\|_{1}\leq W(u)-W_{n}(u)+\lambda\|\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq W_{d}(r)+\lambda\|\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon^{*}+\lambda\|\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}.

Note that
\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}^{c}}=0
, so we have

L(u)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})+\lambda(\|u_{\tilde{S}}\|_{1}+\|u_{\tilde{S}^{c}}\|_{1})\leq\epsilon^{*}+\lambda\|\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}}\|_{1},

whence

L(u)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})+\lambda\|u_{\tilde{S}^{c}}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon^{*}+\lambda\|u_{\tilde{S}}-\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}}\|_{1}.

(7.3)

Next we complete this proof by considering two cases.

Case (i). If
\lambda\|u_{\tilde{S}}-\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}}\|_{1}\geq\epsilon^{*}
, it follows from (
7.3
) that

L(u)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})+\lambda\|u_{\tilde{S}^{c}}\|_{1}\leq 2\lambda\|u_{\tilde{S}}-\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}}\|_{1}.

So
\|u_{\tilde{S}^{c}}\|_{1}=\|u_{\tilde{S}^{c}}-\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}^{c}}\|_{1}\leq 2\|u_{\tilde{S}}-\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}}\|_{1}
and then
u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
.
Also note that
\|u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}=\|u_{\tilde{S}}-\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}}\|_{1}+\|u_{\tilde{S}^{c}}\|_{1}
,
using (
7.3
) again, we have

L(u)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})+\lambda\|u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon^{*}+2\lambda\|u_{\tilde{S}}-\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}}\|_{1}.

As
u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
, it follows from the Compatibility Condition (CC) that

\displaystyle L(u)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})+\lambda\|u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon^{*}+2\lambda\|u_{\tilde{S}}-\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon^{*}+2\lambda\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{s}(u-\tilde{\beta}_{0})^{\top}E[XX^{\top}](u-\tilde{\beta}_{0})}}{\phi_{\tilde{S}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon^{*}+\frac{2\tilde{s}\lambda^{2}}{\phi_{\tilde{S}}^{2}}+\frac{(u-\tilde{\beta}_{0})^{\top}E[XX^{\top}](u-\tilde{\beta}_{0})}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\epsilon^{*}+\frac{(u-\tilde{\beta}_{0})^{\top}E[XX^{\top}](u-\tilde{\beta}_{0})}{2}.

Recall that
E(Y-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)X=0
, then for any
\beta
,

\displaystyle L(\beta)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(Y-\beta^{\top}X)^{2}-E(Y-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(\beta^{\top}X-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0})^{\top}E(XX^{\top})(\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0})

Consequently,

\frac{L(u)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})}{2}+\lambda\|u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq 3\epsilon^{*}.

We also obtain that

\|u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq\frac{3\epsilon^{*}}{\lambda}=\frac{3r\lambda_{0}}{\lambda}\leq\frac{r}{2}.\quad({\rm since}\ \lambda\geq 6\lambda_{0})

Also note that

\|u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}=t\|\hat{\beta}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}=\frac{r}{r+\|\hat{\beta}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}}\|\hat{\beta}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq\frac{r}{2},

some elementary calculations show that
\|\hat{\beta}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq r
.
Then we can replace
u
by
\hat{\beta}
in the above process and obtain the result

\frac{L(\hat{\beta})-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})}{2}+\lambda\|\hat{\beta}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq 3\epsilon^{*}.

Case (ii). If
\lambda\|u_{\tilde{S}}-\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}}\|_{1}<\epsilon^{*}
, then by (
7.3
), we have

L(u)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})+\lambda\|u_{\tilde{S}^{c}}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon^{*}+\lambda\|u_{\tilde{S}}-\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}}\|_{1}\leq 2\epsilon^{*}.

As
\|u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}=\|u_{\tilde{S}}-\tilde{\beta}_{0\tilde{S}}\|_{1}+\|u_{\tilde{S}^{c}}\|_{1}
, it follows that

\frac{L(u)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})}{2}+\lambda\|u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq L(u)-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})+\lambda\|u-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq 3\epsilon^{*}.

Similar to the arguments in Case (i), we have

\frac{L(\hat{\beta})-L(\tilde{\beta}_{0})}{2}+\lambda\|\hat{\beta}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq 3\epsilon^{*}.

Combining the results in the Cases (i) and (ii), we reach the result of this lemma.

\Box

Next we investigate the probability of the event
\mathcal{F}=\{W_{d}(r)\leq\epsilon^{*}\}=\{W_{d}(r)\leq r\lambda_{0}\}
.
Let
\varepsilon_{i}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})=Y_{i}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X_{i}
and
X_{i}^{(j)}
denotes the
j
-th component of
X_{i}
.

Lemma 6
.

Suppose
\varepsilon_{i}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})
and
X_{i}^{(j)}
are sub-Weibull of order
2
for all
1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq p
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{W_{d}(r)>\epsilon^{*}\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{n{\epsilon^{*}}^{2}}{Cr^{4}}+2\log{p}\right)\vee 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{*}}{Cr^{2}}+2\log{p}\right)

\displaystyle+2\exp\left(-\frac{n{\epsilon^{*}}^{2}}{Cr^{2}}+\log{p}\right)\vee 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{*}}{Cr}+\log{p}\right).

Proof of Lemma
6
.
Recall that
W_{d}(r)=\sup_{\|\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq r}|W_{n}(\beta)-W(\beta)|
with

\displaystyle W(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(Y-\beta^{\top}X)^{2}-E(Y-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)^{2},

\displaystyle W_{n}(\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\beta^{\top}X_{i})^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X_{i})^{2}.

Note that
E[(Y-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)X]=0
, it is easy to see that

W_{d}(r)=\sup_{\|\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq r}\big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{(\beta^{\top}X_{i}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X_{i})^{2}-E(\beta^{\top}X-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)^{2}-2\varepsilon_{i}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})(\beta^{\top}X_{i}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X_{i})\}\big|,

where
\varepsilon_{i}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})=Y_{i}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X_{i}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{W_{d}(r)>\epsilon^{*}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\|\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq r}\big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{(\beta^{\top}X_{i}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X_{i})^{2}-E(\beta^{\top}X-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)^{2}-2\varepsilon_{i}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})(\beta^{\top}X_{i}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X_{i})\}\big|>\epsilon^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\|\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq r}\big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[(\beta^{\top}X_{i}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X_{i})^{2}-E(\beta^{\top}X-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X)^{2}]\big|>\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\|\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq r}\big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})(\beta^{\top}X_{i}-\tilde{\beta}_{0}^{\top}X_{i})\big|>\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\|\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq r}\|\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}^{2}\max_{1\leq k,l\leq p}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)}-EX_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)})|>\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\|\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\leq r}\|\beta-\tilde{\beta}_{0}\|_{1}\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})X_{i}^{(k)}|>\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq k,l\leq p}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)}-EX_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)})\big|>\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{2r^{2}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq p}|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})X_{i}^{(k)}|>\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{4r}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l=1}^{p}\mathbb{P}\left(|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)}-EX_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)})|>\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{2r^{2}}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbb{P}\left(|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})X_{i}^{(k)}|>\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{4r}\right)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle I_{1}+I_{2}.

For
I_{1}
, note that
X_{i}^{(k)}
is sub-Weibull of order
2
with
\|X_{i}^{(k)}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C<\infty
for all
k=1,\cdots,p
.
According to Proposition D.2 of
Kuchibhotla and Chakrabortty 2022
, we have

\|X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)}\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq\|X_{i}^{(k)}\|_{\psi_{2}}\|X_{i}^{(l)}\|_{\psi_{2}}.

This means that
X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)}
is sub-Weibull of order
\tau=1
with
\|X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)}\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq C<\infty
and so is
X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)}-E(X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)})
for all
k,l
.
Applying Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)}-EX_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)})|>\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{2r^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{n|\epsilon^{*}/(2r^{2})|^{2}}{4a_{1\tau}^{2}\|X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)}-E(X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)})\|^{2}_{\psi_{\tau}}}\wedge\frac{n\epsilon^{*}/(2r^{2})}{2a_{2\tau}\|X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)}-E(X_{i}^{(k)}X_{i}^{(l)})\|_{\psi_{\tau}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{n{\epsilon^{*}}^{2}}{Cr^{4}}\wedge\frac{n\epsilon^{*}}{Cr^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{n{\epsilon^{*}}^{2}}{Cr^{4}}\right)\vee 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{*}}{Cr^{2}}\right),

where
a\vee b=\max\{a,b\}
and
a\wedge b=\min\{a,b\}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2p^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{n{\epsilon^{*}}^{2}}{Cr^{4}}\right)\vee 2p^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{*}}{Cr^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{n{\epsilon^{*}}^{2}}{Cr^{4}}+2\log{p}\right)\vee 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{*}}{Cr^{2}}+2\log{p}\right).

Similarly, we can show that

I_{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbb{P}\left(|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{i}(\tilde{\beta}_{0})X_{i}^{(k)}|>\frac{\epsilon^{*}}{4r}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n{\epsilon^{*}}^{2}}{Cr^{2}}+\log{p}\right)\vee 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{*}}{Cr}+\log{p}\right).

Altogether we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{W_{d}(r)>\epsilon^{*}\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-\frac{n{\epsilon^{*}}^{2}}{Cr^{4}}+2\log{p}\right)\vee 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{*}}{Cr^{2}}+2\log{p}\right)

\displaystyle+2\exp\left(-\frac{n{\epsilon^{*}}^{2}}{Cr^{2}}+\log{p}\right)\vee 2\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^{*}}{Cr}+\log{p}\right).

Hence we complete the proof of this lemma.
\Box

7.3
The proofs of the theoretical results

In this subsection, we will provide the proofs of the theoretical results in the main text based on the results in the previous sections of Supplementary Material.

Proof of Lemma 1.
The part (i) of Lemma 1 in the main text is the same as Lemma 2.1 of Zhu and Li (1998), Lemma 1 of
Escanciano 2006b
, or Lemma 2.1 of
Lavergne and Patilea 2008
. Thus, we omit the details here.

The proof of part (ii) of this lemma is similar to that of Theorem 2.4 of
Cuesta-Albertos et al. 2019
. We also give a detail proof here for the sake of completeness.
If
E[Z|X]=0
a.s., by part (i) of Lemma 1 in the main text, we have

\mathcal{A}=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:E[Z|\alpha^{\top}X]=0\ a.s.\}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Thus,
\mathcal{A}
has positive Lebesgue measure.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{A}
has positive Lebesgue measure.
It follows that
E(Z)=0
and
E[|Z||X]<\infty
.
Let us assume that
\mathbb{P}\{E(Z|X)=0\}<1
. Write

\displaystyle\phi^{+}(x)=(E[Z|X=x])^{+}=\max\{E[Z|X=x],0\}

\displaystyle\phi^{-}(x)=(E[Z|X=x])^{-}=\max\{-E[Z|X=x],0\}.

Since
E(Z)=0
and
\mathbb{P}\{E(Z|X)=0\}<1
, it yields that

W:=\int\phi^{+}(x)dP_{X}(x)=\int\phi^{-}(x)dP_{X}(x)>0,

where
P_{X}
denotes the probability measure of
X
.
Let us further define two probability measures
P^{+}_{\phi}
and
P^{-}_{\phi}
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
which respectively admit the Radon-Nikodym derivatives with respect to
P_{X}
as

\frac{dP^{+}_{\phi}}{dP_{X}}(x)=\frac{\phi^{+}(x)}{W}\quad{\rm and}\quad\frac{dP^{-}_{\phi}}{dP_{X}}(x)=\frac{\phi^{-}(x)}{W}.

Then it is easy to see that for any positive integer
k
,

\displaystyle\int\|x\|_{2}^{k}dP^{+}_{\phi}(x)=\frac{1}{W}\int\|x\|_{2}^{k}\phi^{+}(x)dP_{X}(x)\leq\frac{1}{W}E[|Z|\|X\|_{2}^{k}]\leq\frac{(EZ^{2})^{1/2}}{W}(E\|X\|_{2}^{2k})^{1/2}.

Similarly, we have

\int\|x\|_{2}^{k}dP^{-}_{\phi}(x)\leq\frac{(EZ^{2})^{1/2}}{W}(E\|X\|_{2}^{2k})^{1/2}.

Since
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(E\|X\|_{2}^{k})^{-\frac{1}{k}}=\infty
and
W\leq(E|Z|^{2})^{1/2}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\int\|x\|_{2}^{k}dP^{+}_{\phi}(x)\right)^{-1/k}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{(EZ^{2})^{1/2}}{W}\right)^{-1/k}(E\|X\|_{2}^{2k})^{-\frac{1}{2k}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{(EZ^{2})^{1/2}}{W}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}2(E\|X\|_{2}^{2k})^{-\frac{1}{2k}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{(EZ^{2})^{1/2}}{W}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\{(E\|X\|_{2}^{2k})^{-\frac{1}{2k}}+(E\|X\|_{2}^{2k+1})^{-\frac{1}{2k+1}}\}=\infty.

We can also show that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\int\|x\|_{2}^{k}dP^{-}_{\phi}(x)\right)^{-1/k}=\infty
.
This means the probability measures
P^{+}_{\phi}
and
P^{-}_{\phi}
satisfy the Carleman condition and thus they are uniquely determined by their moments.

Now for any positive integer
n
, write

p(\alpha)=\int(\alpha^{\top}x)^{n}dP^{+}_{\phi}(x)-\int(\alpha^{\top}x)^{n}dP^{-}_{\phi}(x).

It is readily seen that
p(t\alpha)=t^{n}p(\alpha)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus,
p
is a homogeneous polynomial of order
n
(
Cuesta-Albertos et al. 2007
).
For any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, we have
E[Z|\alpha^{\top}X]=0
a.s. and then

\displaystyle p(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(\alpha^{\top}x)^{n}dP^{+}_{\phi}(x)-\int(\alpha^{\top}x)^{n}dP^{-}_{\phi}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W^{-1}\int(\alpha^{\top}x)^{n}\phi^{+}(x)dP_{X}(x)-W^{-1}\int(\alpha^{\top}x)^{n}\phi^{-}(x)dP_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W^{-1}E\{(\alpha^{\top}X)^{n}[\phi^{+}(X)-\phi^{-}(X)]\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W^{-1}E\{(\alpha^{\top}X)^{n}E(Z|X)\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W^{-1}E\{(\alpha^{\top}X)^{n}E[E(Z|X)|\alpha^{\top}X]\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

where the last equation is due to
E[E(Z|X)|\alpha^{\top}X]=E[E(Z|\alpha^{\top}X)|X]=0
.
Thus, we obtain that

\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{P}_{h}=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:p(\alpha)=0\}.

Note that if
p
is not identically zero, then
\mathcal{P}_{h}
is the so-called projective hypersurface (
Cuesta-Albertos et al. 2007
). Also note that any projective hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
has Lebesgue measure zero.
Since
\mathcal{A}
has positive Lebesgue measure and
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{P}_{h}
,
\mathcal{P}_{h}
is of positive Lebesgue measure. This means that
p(\alpha)\equiv 0
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and then

\int(\alpha^{\top}x)^{n}dP^{+}_{\phi}(x)=\int(\alpha^{\top}x)^{n}dP^{-}_{\phi}(x),\quad\forall\ \alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

Thus these two probability measures
P^{+}_{\phi}
and
P^{-}_{\phi}
have the same moments. As we have shown that they both satisfy the Carleman condition, they are uniquely determined by their moments.
Then we have
P^{+}_{\phi}=P^{-}_{\phi}
and their density functions are the same, i.e.,
W^{-1}\phi^{+}(x)=W^{-1}\phi^{-}(x)
.
Then it is readily seen that
E[Z|X]=0
a.s.. This completes the proof of the lemma.
\Box

According to the proofs for Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 2, we have under
H_{0}
,

\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha}=\frac{nh^{1/2}V_{n0}^{\alpha}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha})^{1/2}}+o_{p}(1),

where
\Sigma^{\alpha}=2\int\sigma_{\alpha}^{4}(t)f_{\alpha}^{2}(t)dt\int K^{2}(u)du
with

\sigma^{2}_{\alpha}(t)=E[\varepsilon^{2}(\beta_{0})|\alpha^{\top}X=t]
and

V_{n0}^{\alpha}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\varepsilon_{i}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{j}(\beta_{0})K_{h}(\alpha^{\top}X_{i}-\alpha^{\top}X_{j}).

Consequently,

\displaystyle(\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{1}},\cdots,\hat{T}_{n}^{\alpha_{d}})=\left(\frac{nh^{1/2}V_{n0}^{\alpha_{1}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}},\cdots,\frac{nh^{1/2}V_{n0}^{\alpha_{d}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{d}})^{1/2}}\right)+o_{p}(1).

Thus it remains to show that

nh^{1/2}\left(\frac{V_{n0}^{\alpha_{1}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}},\cdots,\frac{V_{n0}^{\alpha_{d}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{d}})^{1/2}}\right)\longrightarrow N(0,I_{d}),\quad{\rm in\ distribution}.

Without of loss of generality, we prove the theorem in the case of
d=2
. By the Cramér and Wold device, we only need to show that

\displaystyle nh^{1/2}\left(t_{1}\frac{V_{n0}^{\alpha_{1}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}}+t_{2}\frac{V_{n0}^{\alpha_{2}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}\right)\longrightarrow N(0,1),\quad{\rm in\ distribution},

for all
t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}=1
.

Note that

\displaystyle t_{1}\frac{V_{n0}^{\alpha_{1}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}}+t_{2}\frac{V_{n0}^{\alpha_{2}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\varepsilon_{i}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{j}(\beta_{0})\left(\frac{t_{1}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}}K_{h}(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{i}-\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{j})+\frac{t_{2}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}K_{h}(\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{i}-\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}K(W_{i},W_{j}),

where
W_{i}=(\varepsilon_{i}(\beta_{0}),\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{i},\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{i})
.
Next we utilize Lemma
2
to show that

\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}K(W_{i},W_{j})

is asymptotic normal for all
t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}=1
. Since
E[\varepsilon(\beta_{0})|X]=0
under the null
H_{0}
, it is easy to see that
E[K(W_{1},W_{2})|W_{1}]=0
.
Further, write

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\alpha_{1}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E[\varepsilon^{2}(\beta_{0})|\alpha_{1}^{\top}X=t],

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\alpha_{2}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E[\varepsilon^{2}(\beta_{0})|\alpha_{2}^{\top}X=t],

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha_{12}}^{2}(u_{1},v_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E[\varepsilon^{2}(\beta_{0})|\alpha_{1}^{\top}X=u_{1},\alpha_{2}^{\top}X=v_{1}].

Let
f_{\alpha_{12}}(v_{1},v_{2})
be the density function of
(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X,\alpha_{2}^{\top}X)
.
Some elementary calculations show that

\displaystyle E|K(W_{1},W_{2})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\varepsilon_{1}^{2}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{2}^{2}(\beta_{0})\left(\frac{t_{1}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}}K_{h}(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{1}-\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{2})+\frac{t_{2}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}K_{h}(\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{1}-\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{2})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t_{1}^{2}}{\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}}h^{2}}E\{\varepsilon_{1}^{2}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{2}^{2}(\beta_{0})K^{2}(\frac{\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{1}-\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{2}}{h})\}

\displaystyle+\frac{t_{2}^{2}}{\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}}h^{2}}E\{\varepsilon_{1}^{2}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{2}^{2}(\beta_{0})K^{2}(\frac{\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{1}-\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{2}}{h})\}

\displaystyle+\frac{2t_{1}t_{2}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}}\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}E\{\varepsilon_{1}^{2}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{2}^{2}(\beta_{0})K_{h}(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{1}-\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{2})K_{h}(\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{1}-\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{2})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t_{1}^{2}}{\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}}h}\int\sigma_{\alpha_{1}}^{4}(t)f_{\alpha_{1}}^{2}(t)dt\int K^{2}(u)du+O(1)

\displaystyle+\frac{t_{2}^{2}}{\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}}h}\int\sigma_{\alpha_{2}}^{4}(t)f_{\alpha_{2}}^{2}(t)dt\int K^{2}(u)du+O(1)

\displaystyle+\frac{2t_{1}t_{2}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}}\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}\int\sigma_{\alpha_{12}}^{4}(v_{1},v_{2})f_{\alpha_{12}}^{2}(v_{1},v_{2})dv_{1}dv_{2}\int K(u_{1})K(u_{2})du_{1}du_{2}+O(h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2h}+\frac{2t_{1}t_{2}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}}\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}\int\sigma_{\alpha_{12}}^{4}(v_{1},v_{2})f_{\alpha_{12}}^{2}(v_{1},v_{2})dv_{1}dv_{2}+O(h)<\infty,

where the last equation is due to the facts
\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}}=2\int\sigma_{\alpha_{1}}^{4}(t)f_{\alpha_{1}}^{2}(t)dt\int K^{2}(u)du
and
t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}=1
.

To finish the proof, according to Lemma
2
, it remains to show that

\frac{E[G^{2}(W_{1},W_{2})]+n^{-1}E[K^{4}(W_{1},W_{2})]}{[EK^{2}(W_{1},W_{2})]^{2}}\to 0,\quad as\ n\to\infty,

where
G(W_{1},W_{2})=E[K(W_{1},W_{3})K(W_{2},W_{3})|W_{1},W_{2}]
.
Note that

\displaystyle G(W_{1},W_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{2}(\beta_{0})E\{\varepsilon^{2}_{3}(\beta_{0})[\frac{t_{1}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}}K_{h}(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{1}-\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{3})+\frac{t_{2}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}K_{h}(\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{1}-\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{3})]

\displaystyle\times[\frac{t_{1}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}}K_{h}(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{2}-\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{3})+\frac{t_{2}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}K_{h}(\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{2}-\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{3})]|W_{1},W_{2}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t_{1}^{2}}{\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}}}\varepsilon_{1}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{2}(\beta_{0})\int\sigma^{2}_{\alpha_{1}}(u_{3})f_{\alpha_{1}}(u_{3})K_{h}(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{1}-u_{3})K_{h}(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{2}-u_{3})du_{3}

\displaystyle+\frac{t_{2}^{2}}{\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}}}\varepsilon_{1}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{2}(\beta_{0})\int\sigma^{2}_{\alpha_{2}}(v_{3})f_{\alpha_{2}}(v_{3})K_{h}(\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{1}-v_{3})K_{h}(\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{2}-v_{3})dv_{3}

\displaystyle+\frac{t_{1}t_{2}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}}\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}\varepsilon_{1}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{2}(\beta_{0})\int\sigma^{2}_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{3},v_{3})f_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{3},v_{3})K_{h}(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{1}-u_{3})K_{h}(\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{2}-v_{3})du_{3}dv_{3}

\displaystyle+\frac{t_{1}t_{2}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}}\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}\varepsilon_{1}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{2}(\beta_{0})\int\sigma^{2}_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{3},v_{3})f_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{3},v_{3})K_{h}(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{2}-u_{3})K_{h}(\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{1}-v_{3})du_{3}dv_{3}.

By Condition (A10) and some tedious calculations, we have

\displaystyle E|G(W_{1},W_{2})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t_{1}^{4}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{2}h}\int\sigma^{8}_{\alpha_{1}}(u_{1})f_{\alpha_{1}}^{4}(u_{1})du_{1}\int\left(\int K(u_{3})K(u_{2}+u_{3})du_{3}\right)^{2}du_{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{t_{2}^{4}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{2}h}\int\sigma^{8}_{\alpha_{2}}(v_{1})f_{\alpha_{2}}^{4}(v_{1})dv_{1}\int\left(\int K(v_{3})K(v_{2}+v_{3})dv_{3}\right)^{2}dv_{2}+O(1),

where
\sigma^{2}_{\alpha_{1}}(t)=E[\varepsilon^{2}(\beta_{0})|\alpha_{1}^{\top}X=t]
,
\sigma^{2}_{\alpha_{2}}(t)=E[\varepsilon^{2}(\beta_{0})|\alpha_{2}^{\top}X=t]
, and
f_{\alpha_{1}}(\cdot)
and
f_{\alpha_{2}}(\cdot)
are the density functions of
\alpha_{1}^{\top}X
and
\alpha_{2}^{\top}X
, respectively.

Similarly, we can show that

\displaystyle E|K(W_{1},W_{2})|^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\varepsilon_{1}^{4}(\beta_{0})\varepsilon_{2}^{4}(\beta_{0})\left(\frac{t_{1}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}}K_{h}(\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{1}-\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{2})+\frac{t_{2}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}K_{h}(\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{1}-\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{2})\right)^{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t_{1}^{4}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{2}h^{3}}\int\phi_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{1},v_{1})\phi_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{1},v_{2})f_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{1},v_{1})f_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{1},v_{2})du_{1}dv_{1}dv_{2}\int K^{4}(s)ds

\displaystyle+\frac{t_{2}^{4}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{2}h^{3}}\int\phi_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{1},v_{1})\phi_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{2},v_{1})f_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{1},v_{1})f_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{2},v_{1})du_{1}du_{2}dv_{1}\int K^{4}(s)ds+O(\frac{1}{h^{2}}),

where
\phi_{\alpha_{12}}(u_{1},v_{1})=E[\varepsilon_{1}^{4}(\beta_{0})|\alpha_{1}^{\top}X_{1}=u_{1},\alpha_{2}^{\top}X_{1}=v_{1}]
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{E[G^{2}(W_{1},W_{2})]+n^{-1}E[K^{4}(W_{1},W_{2})]}{[EK^{2}(W_{1},W_{2})]^{2}}=O(h)+O(\frac{1}{nh})\longrightarrow 0,

as
n\to\infty
.
It follows from Lemma
2
that

\displaystyle\frac{n\left(t_{1}\frac{V_{n0}^{\alpha_{1}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}}+t_{2}\frac{V_{n0}^{\alpha_{2}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}\right)}{[2E|K(W_{1},W_{2})|^{2}]^{1/2}}\longrightarrow N(0,1),\quad{\rm in\ distribution},

Recall that
E|K(W_{1},W_{2})|^{2}=1/(2h)+O(1)
, then we have

\displaystyle nh^{1/2}\left(t_{1}\frac{V_{n0}^{\alpha_{1}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{1}})^{1/2}}+t_{2}\frac{V_{n0}^{\alpha_{2}}}{(\Sigma^{\alpha_{2}})^{1/2}}\right)\longrightarrow N(0,1),\quad{\rm in\ distribution},

for all
t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}=1
. Hence we complete the proof of this theorem.
\Box
