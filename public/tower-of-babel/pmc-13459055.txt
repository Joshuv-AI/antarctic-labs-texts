Centrifugally spun ZnO fibers: synthesis and antibacterial activity against Acne vulgaris
Authors: Nasrollah Naseri Joda, Denisa Fenclova, Martina Rihova, Kristyna Cihalova, Zbynek Heger, Jan M. Macak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this work, a novel approach for the synthesis of zinc oxide (ZnO) fibers is presented that is based on centrifugal spinning from optimized spinning solutions, followed by tailored annealing. The resulting ZnO fibers were evaluated for their antibacterial properties and compared to commercial ZnO nanoparticles (NPs). Initially, precursor fibers were produced by centrifugal spinning using polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and zinc nitrate solutions. The as-spun fibers were subsequently annealed at 500 °C to produce ZnO fibers with an average diameter of 1 µm. The fibers were then milled for 20 seconds at a constant frequency using a ball mill, yielding an average fiber length of 6.6 µm. Comprehensive characterization was performed using SEM, XRD, and BET analyses together with evaluation of antibacterial activity against Acne vulgaris (AV)-causing bacteria, Cutibacterium acnes, which was compared with ZnO NPs with an average diameter of 40 nm. The obtained results revealed antibacterial activity of as-synthesized ZnO fibers from a concentration of 1 wt% when dispersed in suspension. These findings underscore the potential of ZnO fibers for antibacterial applications, particularly in the treatment of AV.

Introduction

One-dimensional nanostructured materials such as nanofibers, nanowires, nanorods, and nanotubes have attracted scientific and technological attention due to their unique electrical, optical, and antimicrobial properties, fine-grained structure, and high surface area.1–4 The antibacterial properties of different nanoparticles (NPs) such as silver (Ag), silver oxide (Ag2O), copper oxide (CuO), iron oxide (Fe2O3), magnesium oxide (MgO), titanium dioxide (TiO2), and zinc oxide (ZnO) have been studied. Results of these studies showed that their antibacterial effect depends on their size, morphology, concentration, and other physicochemical parameters.5–8 Among various types of nanomaterials, ZnO has attracted growing interest due to its unique bioactivity and antibacterial properties, especially bacteriostatic and bactericidal effects.9–11 Numerous studies have focused on assessing the antibacterial activity of ZnO in different forms.12,13

El-Habib et al.14 showed that smaller ZnO NPs exhibit stronger antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli (E. coli), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus). Hao et al.15 reviewed green synthesis methods, synergistic effects with antibiotics, and improved performance of ZnO nanocomposites and functionalized nanoparticles. Sirelkhatim et al.16 and Jones et al.17 explained antibacterial mechanisms, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, membrane damage, and zinc ion release, exhibit significantly higher antibacterial activity with smaller particles. Pangprasit et al.18 demonstrated that ZnO NPs reduced bacterial growth and biofilm formation by up to 95% in wound-related bacteria.

The antibacterial performance of ZnO NPs also depends on physico-chemical properties such as crystallinity and morphology. Rathore19 and Hanif et al.20 reported enhanced antibacterial activity with higher crystallinity, larger surface area, and smaller particle size. Manzoor et al.21 showed that heat-treated ZnO NPs exhibited strong activity against Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, while Jin et al.22 confirmed light-driven antimicrobial effects through ROS formation and membrane damage in E. coli. In our previous study,23 ZnO NP-loaded biopolymeric fibers synthesized by centrifugal spinning showed effective antibacterial activity against Acne vulgaris (AV)-causing bacteria, indicating promising dermatological applications.

AV is a widespread dermatological disease caused by different factors, such as hormones, environment, genetics, and diet. Cutibacterium acnes (C. acnes) and Staphylococcus epidermidis (S. epidermidis) contribute to the development of AV and cause inflammation. The effectiveness of traditional treatments, such as antibiotics and retinoids, is gradually decreasing, resulting in the need for alternative treatments.24–26 NPs and particularly those based on ZnO, have been introduced as sustainable alternatives due to their antimicrobial properties, high stability, ability to release Zn2+ ions, and ability to induce ROS production.

Various methods have been used to synthesize ZnO nanomaterials, including hydrothermal,27,28 chemical vapor deposition,29 sol–gel,30 and electrospinning (ES).31–34 Samanta et al.31 fabricated aligned ZnO nanofibers by ES and demonstrated their effectiveness in low-concentration gas sensing. Park et al.32 combined sol–gel and ES to obtain crystalline ZnO fibers with diameters of 35–100 nm after annealing. Thakur et al.33 reported strong antibacterial activity of electrospun ZnO fibers against E. coli and S. aureus, while Thangavel et al.34 fabricated ZnO nanofibers with superior antibacterial activity, suitable for textiles and biomedical applications.

Recently, centrifugal spinning has emerged as a scalable, less dependent on environmental conditions, and cost-effective alternative for producing polymeric35–40 and inorganic41–43 fibers. The method uses centrifugal force to transform polymer solutions into continuous fibers.44–46 Liu et al.47 produced ZnO-hybrid fibers by centrifugal spinning using ZnO powder and citric acid, demonstrating the potential of metal citrate complex polymerization for designing functional materials. Sanchez et al.48 incorporated surfactant-coated ZnO NPs into PVP fibers via centrifugal spinning and achieved strong antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, highlighting their potential in wound dressing applications. In our previous study,49 ZnO-infiltrated PVA fibers fabricated by centrifugal spinning and vapor phase infiltration showed significant inhibitory activity against C. acnes and S. epidermidis, presenting promising potential for localized treatment of AV. No study has shown so far the direct synthesis of crystalline ZnO by centrifugal spinning, to the best of our knowledge.

In this study, we are extending the portfolio of existing ZnO nanomaterials and we demonstrate a novel synthetic approach to produce pure and crystalline ZnO fibers via centrifugal spinning of PVP/Zn(NO3)2·6H2O aqueous solutions, followed by a two-step annealing process. Furthermore, the antibacterial activity of the obtained ZnO fibers against C. acnes was evaluated and compared with that of commercial spherical ZnO NPs.

Materials and methods

Materials

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, reagent grade, 98%) and zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O, ACS reagent, ≥99.0%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Czech Republic), polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP, K90, average Mw ≈ 600 000 g mol−1) was purchased from Prochema (Austria), and commercial ZnO NPs were obtained from Bochemie Ltd (Czech Republic).

Centrifugal spinning

A constant content of zinc nitrate (15 wt%) or zinc acetate (15 wt%) and PVP (15 wt%) was used for the preparation of spinning solutions. All solutions were prepared in a 50 mL beaker and stirred at a speed of 300 rpm using a magnetic stirrer at a temperature of 80 °C for 3 hours. The dynamic viscosity of the spinning solution was measured using an SV-10 vibro viscometer (A&D, Japan), and was determined to be 1.44 Pa s at 22.5 °C. The centrifugal spinning pilot tool, Cyclone Pilot G2 (Pardam Nano4Fibers, Ltd, Czech Republic), was used for the fabrication of fibers as previously described.50 The diameter of the nozzles was 150 µm, the distance of the nozzles from the collectors was 50 cm, the rotation speed was 10 000 rpm, and the temperature and chamber humidity were 35 ± 5 °C and 20 ± 5%, respectively.

Annealing procedure

A laboratory furnace (LE 05/11) was used for annealing of fibers. Fig. S1 shows the two-step annealing profile used, consisting of 12 hours at 180 °C and 6 hours at 500 °C, respectively.

Milling

The annealed fibers were milled using a ball mill, CryoMill, Retsch (Germany), under optimized conditions for 20 seconds at a frequency of 5 Hz. The ZnO fibers were milled after annealing to reduce their length. The reason behind this is that (as tested practically within our preliminary experiments), longer fibers tend to entangle and do not interact efficiently with bacterial cells in suspension. Their limited mobility and reduced surface contact resulted in lower antibacterial activity. By decreasing the fiber length, we achieved better dispersion in the testing medium and enhanced the contact between fibers and bacteria, thereby improving the overall antimicrobial performance.51,52

Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-EDS, MIRA3-XMU, TESCAN, Czech Republic) was used to analyze the morphology and composition of the fibers and ZnO NPs. To increase the conductivity of fibers and to prevent the movement of fibers under high voltage conditions of SEM analysis, the fibers were coated with a carbon layer (approximately 15 nm thick), using an evaporation device (EM ACE600, Leica, Germany). A Smart Lab 3 kW Rigaku setup equipped with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) (Rigaku, Japan) was used for X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD). A current of 30 mA and a voltage of 40 kV were used for Cu radiation, and diffraction patterns were collected from 10° to 70°, with a step size of 0.02° and a scanning rate of 4° min−1. Samples were degassed using nitrogen as the adsorbate gas at 150 °C for 24 hours before the adsorption/desorption isotherms were recorded using the Micro 300 C1 (3P Instrument, Germany). The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) approach was used to analyze the textural parameters.

Evaluation of antibacterial activity

Bacterial culture of C. acnes (CCM 3343) used for evaluating the antibacterial effect of ZnO fibers and ZnO NPs was obtained from the Czech Collection of Microorganisms, Faculty of Science, Masaryk University Brno (Czech Republic). The grown bacteria were then diluted in saline to an optical density of OD600 = 0.1 AU and spread onto 5% Columbia blood agar using a sterile cotton swab. The antibacterial effect of ZnO NPs and ZnO fibers was investigated using a modified cellulose disk diffusion assay adapted from EUCAST guidelines.53

ZnO NPs and ZnO fibers were dispersed in 10 mL of distilled water to prepare individual suspensions (Fig. S2) with the ZnO content of 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 wt%. Each solution was sonicated using an ultrasonic tip for 10 s. Then, 10 µL of the sample was immediately applied (to minimize the possibility of particle sedimentation) to a cellulose disk.

Positive control disks, containing gentamicin (Oxoid, Czech Republic), were used to verify bacterial susceptibility, while phosphate-buffered saline-loaded disks (10 µL) served as negative controls. All tests, including controls, were performed in triplicate. C. acnes was cultured on 5% Columbia blood agar (Lab Media Servis, Czech Republic) at 37 °C for 48 hours under anaerobic conditions.

To assess the antibacterial activity, 6 mm cellulose disks were used in the diffusion assay. The inhibition zones (and their diameters) were measured as the clear zone beyond the disk (disk diameter subtracted).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of centrifugally spun ZnO fibers

Both zinc nitrate–PVP and zinc acetate–PVP aqueous solutions were evaluated as precursor spinning solutions within preliminary experiments. However, the fibers produced from the zinc acetate-based solution exhibited poor morphological quality and were therefore deemed unsuitable for subsequent application studies. As shown in the SEM image (Fig. S3), these fibers contained numerous bead-like structures, indicating non-uniform fiber formation. Consequently, only the zinc nitrate–PVP solution was selected for further investigation and application studies. SEM images in Fig. 1 show the morphology of the fibers synthesized from a zinc nitrate–PVP aqueous solution. As-spun zinc nitrate–PVP composite fibers are shown in Fig. 1a and b. As can be seen, fabricated fibers have the typical appearance of a random fiber structure, consisting of long fibers with a smooth surface. Morphologies of the ZnO fibers annealed at 500 °C and milled ZnO fibers (at the frequency of 5 Hz for 20 s) are shown in Fig. 1(c and d), and (e and f), respectively. The average diameter of annealed fibers was 1 ± 0.3 µm, and the average fiber length after the milling procedure was 6.6 ± 3 µm. These fibers are too thick to be investigated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), hence, no TEM images are provided in this work.

SEM images of (a and b) as-spun precursor PVP/Zn fibers, (c and d) ZnO fibers annealed at 500 °C, same fibers (e and f) milled for 20 s. Scale bars are 20 µm for the left column and 5 µm for the right column.

Characterization of the crystalline structure of fibers and NPs was performed using XRD analysis. The XRD patterns of as-spun fibers, ZnO fibers, and ZnO NPs are shown in Fig. 2. The as-spun fibers exhibited an amorphous structure, which is not surprising given the polymeric nature of fibers. However, after annealing these precursor fibers, numerous well-pronounced diffraction peaks emerged at 2θ ≈ 31.66°, 34.34°, 36.16°, 47.46°, 56.50°, 62.78°, 66.28°, 67.74°, 69.02°, and 76.98°, which correspond to hexagonal ZnO crystallographic planes (100), (0024), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112), (201), and (202) (the index hkl based on ICSD no. 1011258). Considering the intensities and shapes of the peaks, high-purity and crystalline ZnO fibers were obtained. The average crystallite size of the ZnO fibers and ZnO NPs was estimated using the Scherrer equation:where D is the crystallite size, K is the shape factor (0.9), λ is the wavelength of Cu Kα radiation (0.15406 nm), β is the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the diffraction peak expressed in radians, and θ is the Bragg angle. The crystallite sizes of both ZnO fibers and commercial ZnO NPs were calculated from the (101) diffraction peak. For the ZnO fibers, the calculation was performed using the peak at 2θ = 36.16° with an FWHM of 0.33°, yielding an average crystallite size of 25.3 nm. For the commercial ZnO NPs, the calculation was performed using the peak at 2θ = 36.02° with an FWHM of 0.40°, resulting in an average crystallite size of 20.8 nm. It is noteworthy that the XRD patterns of milled fibers look the same as those for un-milled fibers (data not shown). This is due to the fact that shortening of annealed fibers (as a mechanical operation) does not influence their crystallinity, but only their length.

XRD patterns of as-spun PVP/Zn fibers, ZnO fibers annealed at 500 °C, and commercial ZnO NPs. The baseline shows the reference PDF pattern of ZnO.

To gain more details on the composition, EDS analyses were carried out on both ZnO fibers and ZnO NPs. Results are shown in Fig. S4 and confirm the expectation – only Zn and O species were detected.

XPS analysis was performed to investigate the surface chemical composition of the ZnO fibers and commercial ZnO NPs (Fig. S5). Survey spectra confirmed the presence of Zn, O, and C only. High-resolution Zn 2p and Zn LMM spectra verified that zinc was present as ZnO in both samples, with no significant differences in surface chemical composition.

The optical properties of the ZnO fibers and commercial ZnO NPs were further evaluated by UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (Fig. S6). The estimated optical band gaps of both samples were approximately 3.2 eV, which is in good agreement with the values typically reported for crystalline ZnO. As expected, no significant difference in the band gap was observed between the two samples.

Furthermore, BET analyses have been carried out to measure surface area, pore volume, and pore size of the ZnO nanofibers and ZnO NPs. As one can see from Table 1. ZnO fibers possess a moderate surface area, at a level similar to that of inorganic fibers by centrifugal spinning achieved before, such as WO3 (ref. 41) and mesoporous structures.54 The specific surface area and pore volume of the commercial ZnO NPs are two times larger than those of the ZnO fibers. It should be noted, however, that the textural properties of the precursor PVP/Zn fibers cannot be assessed, as they cannot be reliably degassed, thanks to their instability at elevated temperatures, which would damage the polymer fibers’ structure.

ZnO NPs

The commercial ZnO NPs used in this study are the same as those we used and characterized in our previous study.23 Briefly, these ZnO NPs are relatively homogenous in size and shape, with an average diameter of approximately 40 nm and an average crystallite size of 20.8 nm estimated from the XRD patterns using the Scherrer equation, as noted above. The XRD pattern of ZnO NPs shown in Fig. 2 also corresponds to hexagonal ZnO planes, as in ZnO fibers. Based on the textural analyses in Table 1, NPs are also of a mesoporous nature.

Antibacterial activities

Aqueous suspensions of ZnO fibers and ZnO NPs were prepared at different concentrations, ranging from 0.25 to 4 wt%, to evaluate their antibacterial activity, as described in the Experimental part. The use of distilled water as the dispersing medium was important not only to ensure the diffusion of the samples into the agar, as the effectiveness of the disk diffusion method relies on the ability of the tested material to migrate through the medium, but also to preserve Zn2+ ions in their free, unbound form. In contrast, buffered media such as PBS contain phosphate ions that readily react with Zn2+ to form insoluble zinc phosphate precipitates, thereby reducing the concentration of bioavailable Zn2+ and potentially altering the antibacterial mechanism of ZnO. Therefore, the results obtained in this study should not be directly compared with those obtained in PBS or other buffered or growth media. The results for C. acnes are presented in Fig. 3. The inhibition zone against C. acnes for ZnO fiber suspensions was 1.78 ± 0.66 mm at 1 wt%, 3.75 ± 0.57 mm at 2 wt%, and 6.23 ± 1.86 mm at 4 wt%. However, for ZnO NPs, the inhibition zone against C. acnes was already observed at 0.5 wt% (0.80 ± 0.85 mm), increasing to 4.30 ± 0.47 mm at 1 wt%, 6.16 ± 1.03 mm at 2 wt%, and 9.36 ± 0.36 mm at 4 wt%. To provide a complete overview, the digital photographs of the tested materials are presented in Fig. S7 to demonstrate the differences in the antibacterial activity of the samples.

Overall, these results confirm the effective antibacterial properties of ZnO-based materials against C. acnes. The antibacterial efficacy of ZnO materials varies depending on their size, shape, specific surface area, and surface defects. Smaller ZnO NPs and those with a higher specific surface area and a higher number of surface edges and corners generally exhibit stronger antibacterial activity. Additionally, shape is another key factor influencing their biological properties. A higher surface area provides more active sites exposed to the aqueous environment, leading to increased Zn2+ ion release. Similarly, a larger pore volume, as well as structural features such as edges and corners, can further enhance ion dissolution.16,23,51,55

To date, only one study has reported the antibacterial activity of ZnO NPs (with a particle size of 40 nm) in a suspension against C. acnes. Namely, Rihova et al.23 showed that activity onset begins at 0.6 wt% for C. acnes.

Moreover, the incorporation of ZnO within fibrous carriers, or even using ZnO fibers directly, as outlined in this work, represents a highly effective approach to combat skin pathogens. These fibrous materials offer a major advantage, as they do not require preservatives, which are typically needed for wet formulations such as hydrogels or gels.23,49 These observations have established an important foundation for the subsequent research focusing on ZnO-loaded fibers, aiming to achieve antibacterial activity for dermatological applications. To date, research on ZnO-loaded fibers for AV treatment has been limited, with only three studies reported. Until now, Jeong and Oh56 reported the development of PVA/ZnO composite nanofibers crosslinked with citric acid as facial sheet masks, demonstrating strong antibacterial activity against C. acnes without penetration of ZnO NPs into the skin. Rihova et al.23 presented the fabrication of biopolymeric fibers blended with ZnO nanoparticles using centrifugal spinning, showing effective antibacterial activity against C. acnes and S. epidermidis for AV treatment. Another recent approach is the infiltration of ZnO nanocrystals by Vapor Phase Infiltration into fibers, which also exhibit significant antibacterial activity against Acne vulgaris-associated bacteria.49

In this work, C. acnes was incubated under anaerobic conditions in the dark, and ZnO nanomaterials loaded on cellulose disks were tested on inoculated agar plates. Conventional light-driven ROS generation would therefore not be expected here. However, dissolved oxygen can still accept electrons stored at oxygen-vacancy sites, producing superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide even in the dark.57

Another antibacterial mechanism is the release of Zn2+ ions, which we quantified for ZnO nanoparticles in our previous study.49 In addition, a contributing factor is direct interaction between the nanoparticles and the bacterial membrane, causing membrane disruption.58

C. acnes is a Gram-positive and anaerobic bacterium that is part of the physiological skin microbiota. Due to the absence of an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane, Gram-positive bacteria are generally more susceptible to ZnO nanoparticles. In Gram-negative bacteria, this membrane acts as a protective barrier that limits nanoparticle interaction with the cell. Therefore, a stronger antibacterial effect of ZnO nanomaterials against C. acnes than against Gram-negative bacteria can be expected.58,59

However, since ROS generation, Zn2+ release, and direct membrane damage by nanomaterials were not directly measured in the present study, the specific and relative contributions of these mechanisms to the observed antibacterial activity against C. acnes cannot be determined from the current data.

Overall, our results indicate that the surface area and pore volume of ZnO nanomaterials play a significant role in promoting Zn2+ release and thereby enhancing the antibacterial activity of ZnO materials, as evidenced by the more potent antibacterial activity of ZnO NPs. Although the present results demonstrate the promising antibacterial potential of ZnO fibers against acne-associated bacteria, further in vitro cytotoxicity studies and more comprehensive biological evaluations are required to confirm their safety and support their potential biomedical application.

Conclusions

In this work, a novel type of ZnO fibers was successfully synthesized using a centrifugal spinning method from a suitable precursor solution and subsequent annealing at 500 °C. Afterward, ZnO fibers were mechanically milled to obtain a dispersion containing fiber fragments of homogeneous length. In the investigation of antibacterial activity, these fibers were compared with commercial ZnO NPs. The resulting ZnO fibers exhibited excellent antibacterial activity against C. acnes. However, in comparison to commercial ZnO NPs, it was found that NPs show slightly stronger antibacterial activity against C. acnes than ZnO fibers, plausibly due to their higher surface area. Overall, this study highlights the potential of ZnO fibers as a promising antibacterial material, paving the way for their application in acne vulgaris treatment.
