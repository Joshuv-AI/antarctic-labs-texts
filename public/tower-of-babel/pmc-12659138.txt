Rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease: clinical predictive model and external validation
Authors: Chuanhui Yao, Yuhang Lu, Dan Dou, Congmin Xia, Xieli Ma, Yuchen Yang, Hui Xu, Weixiang Liu, Mengge Song, Jianying Yang, Juan Jiao, Xiaopo Tang, Xun Gong, Ge Gao, Jingdong Yang, Quan Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe early diagnostic delay in rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD) underscores the importance of presymptomatic identification of at-risk populations. Here, we developed and validated RA-ILD diagnostic prediction models via machine learning (ML) algorithms with routine clinical and laboratory data.MethodsThe model was developed on the basis of retrospective data from a single-center cohort of 1,156 RA patients (RA-ILD = 400, RA-non-ILD = 756) and subsequently validated in the China Rheumatoid Arthritis Registry of Patients with Chinese Medicine (CERTAIN) which included 178 RA-ILD patients and 178 RA-non-ILD patients. Candidate variables for the predictive model were selected through a multifactor regression analysis. Model performance was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and precision‒recall (PR) curves. Significant features were identified through the application of the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) model.The SHAP model results indicated that the anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) titer was the most significant contributor to the classification, followed by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). ResultsThe final model incorporates 13 predictors. Among the nine ML algorithms evaluated, the random forest (RF) and LightGMB (LGBM) algorithms were robust. In the derivation cohort, RF had an AUC of 0.773 and a mAP of 0.776, and LGBM had an AUC of 0.768 and a mAP of 0.787. In the external validation cohort, the RF model achieved an AUC of 0.713 and a mAP of 0.703, and the LGBM model had an AUC of 0.704 and a mAP of 0.750. The SHAP model results indicated that the anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) titer was the most significant contributor to the classification, followed by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). ConclusionsOur ML models, derived from routine clinical data, identify RA patients at high risk for ILD but require prospective validation in diverse cohorts (including radiologic subtyping) before clinical deployment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03416-1.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic inflammatory autoimmune disorder, with approximately 50% of patients experiencing extra-articular manifestations, the most prevalent being interstitial lung disease (ILD) [1–3]. Research has demonstrated substantial variability in the reported incidence of RA-ILD, ranging from 2% to 58% [4–7]. This variability may be attributed to the heterogeneity of the populations studied and differences in diagnostic criteria. Importantly, RA-ILD is associated with a progressive decline in respiratory function and an elevated risk of infection, which significantly increases patient mortality [5, 8, 9]. Studies indicate that mortality rates in RA-ILD patients are 2 to 10 times higher than those in RA patients without ILD [4, 10]. A systematic review of 78 studies revealed that the median survival of patients with RA-ILD ranges from 2 -−14 years, with a 5–10-year survival rate of approximately 50% [11]. Early diagnosis is essential for identifying disease progression and optimizing treatment regimens, thereby enhancing patient quality of life and survival outcomes.

High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) is currently regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing and monitoring the progression of RA-ILD [12]. Nevertheless, achieving an early and accurate diagnosis remains challenging owing to the nonspecific nature of initial clinical manifestations, the heterogeneity of imaging features, and the complexity of the underlying pathological mechanisms [13]. HRCT findings indicate that more than 50% of patients with rheumatoid arthritis exhibit pulmonary abnormalities, with approximately 10% progressing to clinically significant interstitial lung disease [14]. However, a substantial proportion of patients with rheumatoid arthritis may not display prominent pulmonary symptoms during the early stages. HRCT frequently detects abnormalities only after the lesions have advanced to a severe state, potentially leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment. Furthermore, the clinical application of HRCT is constrained by concerns regarding ionizing radiation exposure and associated costs. Consequently, the development of simple and accurate diagnostic prediction models is urgently needed. These models could improve physicians’ ability to accurately identify and diagnose RA-ILD and provide essential support in patient management and therapeutic decision-making.

Machine learning (ML), a branch of artificial intelligence (AI), involves the creation of algorithms and statistical models. In the healthcare sector, ML is increasingly employed and has made significant contributions to advancements within the field. Primarily, ML facilitates the processing and analysis of extensive and complex medical datasets, such as electronic health records, medical images, and genomic information, thereby enabling the identification of potential disease patterns and risk factors [15–17]. Additionally, ML enhances patient management and treatment decisions by developing clinical predictive models that aid physicians in identifying high-risk patients and optimizing treatment regimens, ultimately improving patient outcomes and quality of life [18]. Undoubtedly, ML has substantially advanced research progress across various diseases. In this study, we utilized ML to integrate data on clinical features and biomarkers to develop reliable predictive models for diagnosing RA-ILD.

Methods

Study design and participants

The derivation cohort data were obtained from Guang’anmen Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences. A retrospective analysis was conducted on 1,156 patients diagnosed with RA, comprising 400 individuals in the RA-ILD group and 756 individuals in the RA-non-ILD group. These patients were hospitalized in the Department of Rheumatology at Guang’anmen Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences, between December 2016 and September 2023. Hospitalization facilitated standardized data collection and comprehensive evaluation, including patients with early RA and those undergoing screening for extra-articular manifestations. All participants satisfied the 2010 revised classification criteria for RA established by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) [19]. Patients under the age of 18, those with malignant diseases (cancer/tumors), or those with concurrent autoimmune diseases were excluded from the study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Guang’anmen Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (approval number 2022XLA060-3), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. To mitigate model overfitting and enhance model stability, sample equalization within the derivation cohort was conducted via the SMOTE algorithm. Crucially, SMOTE was applied only after the dataset was split, and solely to the training set (70%). The internal testing set (30%) and the external validation cohort remained completely untouched by SMOTE to ensure an unbiased evaluation of model performance.

The external validation cohort was sourced from the CERTAIN cohort, which has been comprehensively detailed in a prior publication [20]. Given that the CERTAIN cohort includes data from multiple centers, we applied additional exclusion criteria beyond those of the derivation cohort, specifically excluding patients with inconsistent test standards. Data collection

The demographic and clinical data of all patients were retrospectively gathered. Demographic information included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and family medical history. The clinical data included age at disease onset, disease duration, medication history, tender and swollen joint counts, and physician and patient global assessments. The routine hematological parameters included white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), platelet count (PLT), hemoglobin (HGB), lymphocytes (LYMPH), neutrophils (NEUT), and monocytes (MONO). The biochemical parameters included cholesterol (CHOL), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), albumin (ALB), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels. The inflammatory and immune parameters included the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), complement C3 and C4, and immunoglobulins IgG, IgM, and IgA. The autoantibodies assessed were rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP) antibodies. In addition, disease activity was quantified via indices such as the Disease Activity Score 28 (DAS28) with erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP), along with the Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) and the Simplified Disease Activity Index (SDAI). Other composite indices, such as the systemic immunity index (SII) and the prognostic nutritional index (PNI), were also calculated.

Per institutional ethics protocol, HRCT scans were de-identified prior to analysis, precluding retrospective linkage with clinical data for radiologic subtyping.

HRCT diagnosis of ILD

The diagnosis of ILD was confirmed by a consensus between experienced rheumatologists and radiologists based on the presence of characteristic findings on HRCT of the chest. These findings included, but were not limited to, reticulation, traction bronchiectasis, honeycombing, ground-glass opacities, and architectural distortion, which are inconsistent with alternative diagnoses such as infection, heart failure, or drug-induced lung injury. While the specific radiologic pattern (e.g., usual interstitial pneumonia [UIP], nonspecific interstitial pneumonia [NSIP], or organizing pneumonia [OP]) was not systematically adjudicated or subtyped for the purposes of this study—as stated previously due to the de-identified nature of the scans—the overarching diagnosis of ILD required the presence of these definitive radiographic abnormalities.

Classification and definition of indicators

Disease activity indices were assessed according to the specified value ranges outlined in the guidelines. RF positivity was defined as RF titers > 20 IU/mL, whereas anti-CCP positivity was defined as anti-CCP titers > 25 U/mL. A medication was considered in use if its total duration of administration surpassed one year. For patients with RA-ILD, medications were documented up to the time of RA-ILD diagnosis, whereas for patients with RA-non-ILD, medications were recorded up to the time of inclusion in this study. Similarly, disease duration statistics were calculated up to the time of RA-ILD diagnosis for RA-ILD patients and up to the time of study inclusion for RA-non-ILD patients.

Data quality control

Peripheral serum samples were collected from all patients in a fasting state during the morning hours and analyzed by standardized laboratory personnel via consistent testing methods and protocols to ensure the reliability and standardization of the laboratory results. Clinical data were meticulously gathered by highly qualified rheumatologists. Furthermore, data cleansing was conducted synchronously by two individuals, and the final results were verified for accuracy prior to proceeding to the data analysis phase.

Statistical analysis

All the data were analyzed via SPSS software (version 10.0) and the R package (version 4.0.2). The normality of the data was evaluated through histograms and the Shapiro‒Wilk test. For continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed via the independent samples t test. Nonnormally distributed variables are reported as medians (Q25, Q75) and were assessed via the Mann‒Whitney U test. Categorical variables are represented as percentages and were compared via the chi-square test. Variables with a p value of less than 0.10 in the multivariate analysis, along with those deemed potentially clinically significant by clinicians, were subsequently incorporated into the model for further analysis. In this study, ML was trained via 10-fold cross-validation on a 70% subset of the dataset, with the remaining 30% reserved for validating the final model. The model’s generalization performance was assessed by partitioning the dataset into 10 folds, systematically alternating the training and test sets. The feature importance was elucidated via the SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP) model, which computes the marginal contribution of individual features to the model’s output. The primary evaluation metrics employed were accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, sensitivity, and specificity. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision-recall (PR) curves were utilized to assess the model’s strengths and weaknesses. Significant features were identified via the aforementioned SHAP method. A P value < 0.05 was considered indicative of a statistically significant difference.

Results

Characteristics of participants

The baseline characteristics of the study participants, including their demographic data and clinical attributes, are detailed in Table 1. The study included a total of 1,156 patients who were diagnosed with RA, with a mean age of 62.04 ± 11.20 years, of whom 923 (62%) were female. Within the cohort, 400 participants were identified as having RA-ILD, while 756 were classified as having RA-non-ILD. Comparative analysis revealed that patients with RA-ILD were significantly older, had a longer disease duration, and a greater age of onset than those in the RA-non-ILD group were. Additionally, the RA-ILD group included a greater proportion of males, smokers, and individuals with a positive family history. They also reported higher PGA scores, a greater prevalence of prior GC and MTX use, lower ALB levels, elevated LDH levels, reduced complement C4 levels, increased rates of RF and anti-CCP positivity, and a lower PNI. These differences were statistically significant.

Model feature filtering

Univariate variables with P < 0.10 were incorporated into the binary logistic regression model. Age was excluded from the analysis because of its direct correlation with both the age of onset and the duration of the disease. The outcomes of the multivariate logistic regression are presented in Table 2. To ensure the model’s stability and reliability, variables with P < 0.10 were included in the multivariate analyses within the machine learning model. Additionally, sex, RF titers ≥ 100 IU/mL, and the PNI were incorporated on the basis of findings from previous studies and clinical considerations. Ultimately, 13 potential predictors were identified for model development in the training dataset: disease duration, age at onset, sex, smoking status, family history, GC use, MTX use, RF titers ≥ 100 IU/mL, anti-CCP titers ≥ 2000 U/mL, complement C4, PGA, LDH, and PNI.

Models performance evaluation

The comprehensive performance metrics for each predictive model are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The current analysis indicates that the RF model has robust generalizability, as evidenced by an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.773, an accuracy of 0.776, a sensitivity of 0.759, a specificity of 0.787, and an F1 score of 0.768. As illustrated in Fig. 2, ROC curves and PR curves were employed to evaluate the performance of the nine models. Model validity was primarily assessed via AUC values, with RF achieving the highest mean AUC of 0.773, followed by LGBM with an AUC of 0.768 and XGB with an AUC of 0.742. Model accuracy was further evaluated via mean average precision (mAP) values, where LGBM attained the highest mAP of 0.787, followed by RF, with an mAP of 0.776, and XGB, with an mAP of 0.758. These findings suggest that the RF, LGBM, and XGB methods exhibit superior overall performance.

Model explainability

The tree SHAP algorithm was employed to generate an importance ranking of features. SHAP values were derived from the feature importance results produced by the random forest model, and the top eight ranked features were selected for further analysis. The anti-CCP titer was the most significant contributor to the classification, followed by LDH (Fig. 3A). A random sample of 700 patients was subsequently statistically ranked according to SHAP values, and feature scatter plots were subsequently constructed. Features with higher contribution rankings were selected for detailed exploration. In these scatter plots, the horizontal axis represents the SHAP value, where a positive value indicates a positive effect of the feature on the model’s prediction, and a negative value indicates a negative effect (RA for the positive class of samples and RA-ILD for the negative class of samples). As illustrated in Fig. 3B, patients with anti-CCP titers ≥ 2000 U/ml tended to exhibit negative SHAP values, indicating a negative impact on the model’s prediction, similar to the effect observed for LDH. Conversely, the opposite trend was noted for the PNI.

The SHAP feature waterfall plot (Fig. 3C) for a randomly selected patient reveals a SHAP value of 0.497. At this threshold, the probabilities for the positive and negative categories are each 50%. If the final calculated SHAP value exceeds 0.497, the condition is classified as RA-ILD. For this patient, the final SHAP value is 0.81, leading the classifier to identify the condition as RA-ILD. Conversely, for another patient, the SHAP feature waterfall plot (Fig. 3D) indicates a SHAP value of 0.13. On the basis of this calculation, the patient is classified as having RA-non-ILD. In this instance, the duration of the disease was the most significant factor influencing the outcome.

External validation

A cohort of 356 patients from various centers was utilized for external validation (baseline characteristics are shown in Supplementary Table 2). The comprehensive outcomes of the machine learning analysis are presented in Supplementary Table 3. The RF model demonstrated robust generalization capabilities, achieving satisfactory classification performance with an AUC of 0.713 (Fig. 4A) and a high mAP of 0.703 (Fig. 4B). Similarly, the LGBM model exhibited notable performance, with an AUC of 0.704 and an mAP of 0.750.

Discussion

ILD is a common, serious, and frequently underrecognized complication associated with RA. Patients with RA-ILD may exhibit symptoms such as breathlessness and coughing; however, these initial symptoms are often subtle or even absent, complicating early diagnosis. By the time RA-ILD is definitively diagnosed, patients may already experience significant functional impairment [21]. Therefore, early detection and assessment of RA-ILD are essential for the timely initiation of treatment. Identifying accurate risk factors for RA-ILD and developing diagnostic prediction models can facilitate the early identification of individuals at high risk, thereby promoting prompt diagnosis and intervention. Recent meta-analyses have identified several risk factors for RA-ILD, including male sex (relative risk [RR] 1.94, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.33–2.85, P < 0.001), advanced age (>60 years, RR 1.42, 95% CI 1.05–1.94, P = 0.02), older age at RA onset (RR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01–1.10, P = 0.02), and smoking (RR 1.37, 95% CI 1.09–1.71, P = 0.006) [22]. In addition to these characteristics, this study identified additional potential risk factors that warrant further investigation.

The diagnosis of ILD in patients with RA presents considerable challenges, given that ILD can emerge at any stage of RA and is characterized by a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations, alongside a paucity of early diagnostic biomarkers. Our study suggests that patients with RA of prolonged duration are at an elevated risk for developing ILD. This observation highlights the critical need for increased vigilance and comprehensive evaluation in patients with extended disease duration, which may be instrumental in arresting ILD progression and ultimately improving long-term outcomes. It is evident that screening for specific biomarkers can aid in early diagnosis. Several biomarkers, such as periostin [23], endothelin-1 [24], CHI3L1 [25], and interleukin-22 [26], have been linked to RA-associated ILD, with elevated serum levels in affected patients compared with those without ILD. However, these biomarkers demonstrate significantly lower specificity than do autoantibodies. Our findings indicate that patients with RA-ILD exhibit elevated titers of RF and anti-CCP antibodies, with a significantly greater proportion of anti-CCP levels exceeding 2000 in RA-ILD patients than in those with RA-non-ILD (P < 0.001). A study by Chase S. Correia established a significant correlation between increased anti-CCP titers and a higher incidence of ILD (odds ratio [OR] 1.47, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.96, P < 0.001) [27]. Additionally, a progressive elevation in anti-CCP titers was associated with an increased incidence of ILD within the cohort (P < 0.01). Research has revealed a relationship between lung imaging findings and antibody titers in RA patients, suggesting that elevated RF and anti-CCP titers may be linked to more severe pulmonary damage [28]. Therefore, it is crucial for clinicians to consider the antibody profile of patients and its potential pulmonary implications when formulating individualized treatment strategies. For patients with high anti-CCP titers, enhanced clinical monitoring and evaluation of pulmonary symptoms are advised to optimize therapeutic outcomes and improve patient prognosis.

The potential impact of drug exposure on the risk of ILD in patients with RA is a significant concern, as it influences treatment regimen decisions. MTX is commonly employed as a foundational drug in RA management; however, its association with an elevated risk of ILD in RA patients remains contentious [29]. Our findings suggest that a history of MTX exposure is a risk factor for RA-associated ILD. Similarly, Rocha-Muñoz et al. demonstrated that patients with RA-ILD had higher MTX doses, prolonged exposure durations, and greater cumulative doses than those without ILD [30]. Conversely, other studies have reported that MTX treatment is not associated with an increased risk of RA-ILD and may even delay the onset of ILD [31]. These discrepancies may be attributable to variations in study design, racial differences among participants, and differential drug responses.Glucocorticoids (GCs) play a crucial role in the management of RA-ILD. However, a study has revealed a significant association between the use of oral GCs and the development of ILD, with an OR of 3.562 and a 95% CI of 2.160–5.874 [32]. Our findings support these observations. Currently, the timely tapering of GCs presents a clinical challenge, as prolonged use can result in immunosuppression and an elevated risk of infection, potentially contributing to an increased risk of ILD. The treatment of RA-ILD is further complicated by the challenge of appropriate drug selection, necessitating more comprehensive studies that examine drug dosage, duration, and combinations. Currently, there is a lack of research data on drug exposure and the risk of ILD development among Chinese patients with rheumatoid arthritis, which limits the ability of clinicians to develop precise and personalized treatment strategies.

Moreover, our research revealed a notable increase in LDH levels among patients with RA-ILD. Similarly, variations in LDH levels across different types of ILD exhibit distinct characteristics. A comprehensive study involving serum LDH analysis in 172,933 patients and 9,528 healthy individuals across 48 diseases demonstrated significantly elevated LDH levels in patients with pulmonary fibrosis, suggesting its potential as a biomarker, with an AUC of 0.80 [33]. In addition to its diagnostic utility, LDH is associated with ILD prognosis and serves as an independent risk factor for acute exacerbations in both idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and non-IPF ILD patients [34]. Although LDH has diagnostic and prognostic value in ILD, its role in RA-ILD remains insufficiently explored, necessitating further investigation.Additionally, our study revealed a diminished PNI in patients with RA-ILD. The PNI is frequently utilized to assess postoperative nutritional status and predict the prognosis of patients with tumors [35–37]. A lower PNI suggests compromised nutritional and immune function in RA-ILD patients, potentially correlating with adverse prognostic outcomes. Consequently, we propose that the clinical evaluation of the PNI in individuals with rheumatoid arthritis, especially those with concomitant interstitial lung disease, may improve patient management strategies.

We subsequently employed nine ML algorithms to differentiate between RA-ILD patients and RA-non-ILD patients, evaluating the significance of various predictive parameters in patient classification. Both the RF and the LGBM demonstrated robust predictive capabilities in the training set as well as in the external validation set. Further analysis via SHAP values indicated that anti-CCP titers ≥ 2000 were the most significant predictor for RA-ILD, followed by advanced age at onset and elevated LDH levels. The development of disease prediction models utilizing diverse biomarkers has emerged as a prominent research focus, with several models exhibiting strong discriminative power. Nonetheless, the implementation of biomarker testing poses challenges for many clinical settings, particularly extensive primary care facilities, owing to the complexity and specificity of certain biomarkers, thereby hindering their widespread clinical adoption and validation.

In this study, we developed a predictive model for RA-ILD using widely recognized clinical metrics, which demonstrated robust performance in both internal and external validation settings. This enhances its potential applicability in routine healthcare environments. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective design of the study may introduce analytical bias due to variability in clinical data from certain patients, despite stringent data quality controls. Second, the model was trained on data from a single center, and although it was validated with external multicenter data, the external validation dataset was limited. Lastly, Our cohort likely includes mixed fibrotic and non-fibrotic RA-ILD subtypes, which may confound predictive accuracy.

The early and accurate identification of RA-ILD is a critical clinical goal in itself because it enables targeted screening, timely intervention, and informed therapeutic decisions. However, our model does not directly predict longitudinal outcomes such as disease progression, survival, or response to treatment. The inclusion of prognostic biomarkers like LDH, which is linked to acute exacerbations in ILD, and the PNI, a marker of nutritional and immune status, suggests that our model may capture elements of disease severity. However, future prospective studies with long-term follow-up are essential to validate these predictors for prognostic modeling and to assess whether our diagnostic model can be extended to predict the risk of future ILD development in RA patients without current lung involvement. Future work should stratify analyses by HRCT patterns to refine risk models. Consequently, large-scale, multicenter prospective studies that integrate radiologic subtyping are required to validate subtype-specific predictive signatures and enhance the generalizability of the model’s findings.

Conclusions

In summary, we developed a simple yet effective RA-ILD prediction model using standard clinical indicators and machine-learning algorithms. The model identifies at-risk RA patients for targeted HRCT screening, irrespective of histopathologic subtype and holds promise for facilitating the early diagnosis and management of RA-ILD.
