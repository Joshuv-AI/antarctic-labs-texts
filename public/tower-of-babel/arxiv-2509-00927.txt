Non-minimizing Axially Symmetric Cavity Flow
Masoud Bayrami, Morteza Fotouhi, Parisa Vosooqnejad

Abstract
. Cavity flow problems in two dimensions, as well as in the axially symmetric three-dimensional case, have been extensively studied in the literature from a qualitative perspective. While numerous results exist concerning minimizers or stable solutions-particularly regarding the regularity of the free boundary and the analysis of singularities—much less is known about the critical points of the corresponding energy functional. In this paper, we focus on investigating the properties of such critical points in the axially symmetric cavity flow problem with a free boundary, in relation to the known variational solutions. Moreover, our approach extends naturally to the case of jet flow problems.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview of the Mathematical Framework

The classical theory of jets and cavities is initially focused on studying incompressible inviscid two-dimensional (ideal) flows; see, e.g.,
[
6
,
24
,
25
]
and references therein.
Related problems, such as those arising in the theory of steady two-dimensional free-surface water waves, have also been the subject of extensive research. For overviews of these developments, we refer to
[
14
,
30
,
31
]
; and also
[
27
]
, which presents a framework that accommodates both a variational approach to various fluid equilibrium problems and the construction of min–max solutions to Bernoulli-type free boundary problems.

A significant development in the study of jets and cavities for compressible fluids using novel variational approaches began with the seminal works of Alt, Caffarelli, and Friedman
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
12
]
.
These variational problems, have been studied within a geometric measure-theoretic framework, employ geometric and blow-up arguments as well as novel monotonicity formulas. For further details, see for instance
[
2
,
7
,
8
,
13
,
16
,
18
,
17
,
22
,
28
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
15
]
.

In this paper, we build upon this line of research by focusing not on energy minimizers, but on
non-minimizing critical points
, a class of solutions that is comparatively less understood yet of substantial physical and mathematical interest.
Our investigation is motivated in part by recent advances in the understanding of variational limits of singular perturbation problems, particularly the work of Kriventsov and Weiss
[
26
]
, who developed a refined notion of variational solutions that lies between the classical weak and viscosity solution frameworks.

The cavity flow problem we consider arises in both two-dimensional and axially symmetric three-dimensional configurations and involves a free boundary separating a region occupied by fluid from a cavity or vacuum.
While numerous results exist concerning minimizers or stable solutions—particularly regarding free boundary regularity and singularity analysis—much less is known about the critical points (specifically saddle points) of the corresponding energy functional.

In the axisymmetric setting, the natural variational functional governing steady ideal incompressible flows is given by:

(1)

J_{\lambda}(\psi)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\left|\dfrac{\nabla\psi}{y}\right|^{2}+\lambda^{2}\chi_{\{\psi>0\}}\right)y\,dxdy,

where
\Omega
lies in the half-plane
\{(x,y)\,:\,y\geq 0\}
,
\lambda>0
is the cavity speed and
\psi
is the stream function.

However, the functional (
1
) is not convex, and it is evident that there exist critical points that are not minimizers.
In fact, such saddle points are not merely mathematical artifacts but can correspond to physically relevant configurations, particularly in time-dependent or perturbed problems such as those appearing in combustion theory or free surface flows with instabilities. See
[
38
]
for a detailed discussion in the context of parabolic singular perturbations.
More precisely, in
[
38
]
, Weiss showed that variational solutions naturally arise as limits of more regular solutions in the study of singular perturbations of semilinear equations. An important observation made there is that, although inner-variation solutions are stable under taking limits, this stability holds only in a generalized sense. Specifically, the limit
I
of
\chi_{\{\psi_{k}>0\}}
, which forms part of the energy functional, is indeed the characteristic function of a set, but it does not necessarily coincide with the positivity set of the limiting function
\{\psi_{0}>0\}
. Instead, only the inclusion
\chi_{\{\psi_{0}>0\}}\leq I
is guaranteed, which motivates a relaxed definition of variational solutions.

As highlighted in
[
26
]
, classical notions of weak solutions, as introduced by Alt and Caffarelli in
[
1
]
, are often too restrictive to capture limits of approximating sequences arising from singular perturbations or viscous regularization.
Conversely, the class of viscosity solutions, although more robust under limits, is too broad to permit fine structural or regularity results.
The intermediate class of
variational solutions
, as developed in
[
38
,
26
]
, provides a more natural and analytically tractable setting for studying such limit configurations.

Motivated by this framework, we study the Bernoulli-type free boundary problem associated with variational solutions of the functional (
1
):

(2)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}\psi:=\mathrm{div\,}\left(\dfrac{1}{y}\nabla\psi\right)=0,\qquad&\text{in}\ \{\psi>0\}\cap\Omega,\\
\psi=0,\qquad&\text{on}\ \partial\{\psi>0\}\cap\Omega,\\
\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nu}=\lambda,\qquad&\text{on}\ \partial\{\psi>0\}\cap\Omega,\end{cases}

which describes a steady axisymmetric incompressible ideal flow in three dimensions, with the
x
-axis serving as the axis of symmetry.
The differential operator
\mathcal{L}
is singular at
y=0
, reflecting the degeneracy introduced by axial symmetry in cylindrical coordinates.
This dynamical boundary condition which equivalently can be represented as
|\nabla\psi|=\lambda y
on the free boundary
\partial\{\psi>0\}\cap\Omega
indicates that the flow speed scales linearly with the radial distance from the axis, and degenerates as
y\to 0
.
Points on the free boundary where the gradient of the stream function vanishes are referred to as
degenerate points
, such as those lying on the symmetry axis
y=0
. For our analysis, we also distinguish free boundary points in the region
y>0
that are not regular free boundary points, which we call
singular points
. The set of such points is denoted by
\Sigma_{H}
and is precisely defined in (
27
).

The focus of this paper is the qualitative and structural analysis of non-minimizing critical points of the functional (
1
) near the free boundary.
While our analysis includes both degenerate and nondegenerate points, we place particular emphasis on the singular points lying in the domain
\{y>0\}
.
Understanding the structure of the free boundary near such points is crucial for capturing the qualitative behavior of the flow and the associated variational solutions.

1.2.
Notations

We use
X\cdot Y
to denote the Euclidean inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
|X|
to denote the Euclidean norm.
The Euclidean distance from a point
X\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
to a set

is written as
d(X,L)
.
The upper half-plane is denoted by
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:=\{X=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,y\geq 0\}
, and the standard Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is written as
dX=dxdy
.
When the context is clear, we may omit the measure of integration notation for brevity.

The open ball of radius
r
centered at a point
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is denoted by
B_{r}(X_{0}):=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,|X-X_{0}|<r\}
.
The intersection of this ball with the upper half-plane is denoted by
B_{r}^{+}(X_{0}):=B_{r}(X_{0})\cap\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
.
We refer to the
circular boundary
of this set as

\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0}):=\{X=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,y\geq 0\text{ and }|X-X_{0}|=r\}.

Note that
\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})
is not the topological boundary of
B_{r}^{+}(A)
, and
B_{r}^{+}(A)
is not necessarily a true half-ball.
When the center is omitted, it is understood to be the origin; thus we write
B_{r}:=B_{r}(0)
and
B_{r}^{+}:=B_{r}^{+}(0)
.

We assume
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
is an open set. The boundary
\partial\Omega
refers to the portion of the boundary lying strictly in the upper half-plane and is distinct from the full topological boundary
\overline{\Omega}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The volume (Lebesgue measure) of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is denoted by
|A|
, representing the two-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{H}^{2}
.
In addition, we denote by
|A|_{{\mathrm{w}}}
the weighted volume of
A
with respect to the measure
y\,dX
, defined by
|A|_{{\mathrm{w}}}:=\int_{A}y\,dX
.

To define the solutions of the problem in an appropriate sense, we require certain weighted Sobolev spaces, denoted as
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(E)
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
be an open set. The weighted Lebesgue space
L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(E)
and its local version
L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(E)
are defined as follows:

L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(E):=\left\{g:E\to\mathbb{R}\,:\,g\text{ is measurable and }\int_{E}\frac{1}{y}|g|^{2}\,dX<+\infty\right\},

and

L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(E):=\left\{g\in L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(K)\text{ for every compact set }K\subseteq E\right\}.

The corresponding norm on
L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(E)
is

\|g\|_{L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(E)}:=\left(\int_{E}\frac{1}{y}|g|^{2}\,dX\right)^{1/2}.

The weighted Sobolev space
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(E)
and its local version
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(E)
are given by:

W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(E):=\left\{g\in L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(E)\,:\,\partial_{x}g,\partial_{y}g\in L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(E)\right\},

and

W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(E):=\left\{g\in L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(E)\,:\,\partial_{x}g,\partial_{y}g\in L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(E)\right\},

where
\partial_{x}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}=\partial_{1}
and
\partial_{y}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}=\partial_{2}
denote the weak partial derivatives with respect to
x
and
y
, respectively.

We define the degenerate elliptic operator

\mathcal{L}u:=\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{1}{y}\nabla u\right).

A function
u
is called an
\mathcal{L}
-subsolution if it satisfies
\mathcal{L}u\geq 0
in the distributional (or viscosity) sense. If both
u
and
-u
are
\mathcal{L}
-subsolutions, then
u
is referred to as an
\mathcal{L}
-solution.

We use
\chi_{A}
to denote the characteristic function of a set
A
.
For any real number
a\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
a^{+}:=\max(a,0)
and
a^{-}:=\min(a,0)
.
The symbol
\nu
will always refer to the inward-pointing unit normal vector of a surface. The notation
\mathcal{H}^{s}
denotes the
s
-dimensional Hausdorff measure.

We use the space of functions of bounded variation, denoted by
BV(\Omega)
, consisting of functions
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
whose distributional derivatives are vector-valued Radon measures. The total variation measure of such a function is denoted by
|\nabla f|
. In particular, for a smooth open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the measure
|\nabla\chi_{U}|
corresponds to the surface measure on
\partial U
. We also use the concept of the reduced boundary, denoted
\partial^{\star}U
, from geometric measure theory.

1.3.
Main Result

We begin by introducing the class of non-minimizing solutions to the free boundary problem (
2
).
In particular, we define the notion of a
variational solution
, which includes certain critical points (such as saddle points) of the functional (
1
) corresponding to the Euler-Lagrange equation (
2
).
This formulation is inspired by the frameworks developed in
[
26
,
36
,
38
]
.
For simplicity, we assume
\lambda=1
throughout the paper.

Definition 1.2

(Variational solutions)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
be an open set.
A pair
(\psi,I)
, with
\psi:\Omega\to[0,+\infty)
and
\psi\in W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
, and
I:\Omega\to\{0,1\}
, is called a
variational solution
(with constant
C_{V}
) if the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

\psi\in C^{0}(\Omega)\cap C^{2}(\Omega\cap\{\psi>0\})
, with
\psi\geq 0
in
\Omega
,
\psi=0
on
\{y=0\}\cap\Omega
, and

(3)

\dfrac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|\leq C_{V},\qquad\text{ in }\,\Omega;

(2)

I
is a Borel measurable function;

(3)

\chi_{\{\psi>0\}}\leq I
,
\mathcal{H}^{2}
-a.e in
\Omega
;

(4)

the first variation of the functional

J(\tilde{\psi},\tilde{I})=\int_{\Omega}\left(\left|\dfrac{\nabla\tilde{\psi}}{y}\right|^{2}+\tilde{I}\right)y\,dX,

with respect to domain variations, vanishes at
(\tilde{\psi},\tilde{I})=(\psi,I)
; that is, for every vector field
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
\eta_{2}=0
on
\{y=0\}
, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\dfrac{d}{dt}J\left(\psi(X+t\eta(X)),I(X+t\eta(X))\right)_{\big|_{t=0}}

(4)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi\right|^{2}+yI\right)\mathrm{div\,}\eta-\dfrac{2}{y}\nabla\psi\cdot D\eta\nabla\psi-\eta_{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-I\right)\,dX.

Remark 1.1
.

The Lipschitz condition (
3
) follows from the boundedness of the velocity of the flow, which is known to hold at least for minimizing solutions of (
1
); see
[
8
]
.

Our main result establishes structural and regularity properties of variational solutions:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution in a domain
\Omega
. Then:

(i)

Either
\psi\equiv 0
or
I=\chi_{\{\psi>0\}}
;

(ii)

The whole topological free boundary
\partial\{\psi>0\}
is countably
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-rectifiable and has locally finite
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-measure.

(iii)

The solution
\psi
satisfies the following distributional equation

\mathcal{L}\psi=\mathcal{H}^{1}\llcorner\partial^{\star}\{\psi>0\}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi y}}\sqrt{\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}r^{-3}H_{X}(\psi,r)}\mathcal{H}^{1}\llcorner\Sigma_{H}.

Here,
\partial^{\star}
denotes the reduced boundary,
H_{X}(\psi,r)
at the point
X=(x,y)
is defined in (
8
), and
\Sigma_{H}
denotes the set of all singular points
X_{0}\in\partial\{\psi>0\}
such that
\psi(X)=a\,\big|\,(X-X_{0})\cdot\nu\,\big|+\mathrm{o}(|X-X_{0}|)
, for some
a=a(X_{0})>0
and some unit vector
\nu=\nu(X_{0})
.

(iv)

For
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e. singular point
X_{0}\in\Sigma_{H}
, we have:

\frac{r\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\left(\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi(x,y)\right|^{2}+y\left(I-1\right)\right)\,dxdy}{\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}(x,y)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}}\to 1,

and

\frac{r\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\left(\psi(x,y)-\alpha(X_{0})\left|(x,y)-X_{0}\right|\right)\cdot\nu(X_{0})\right|^{2}\,dxdy}{\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}(x,y)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}}\to 0

as
r\to 0
, for some unit vector
\nu(X_{0})
. The constant
\alpha(X_{0})=\sqrt{y_{0}/\pi}
is an explicit normalizing factor.

2.
Preliminaries

We begin with the proof of the first-variation formula (
4
).
Although a similar result can be found in
[
23
, Section 3.2]
or
[
37
, Lemma 9.5]
,
special care is required to handle the singularity at
y=0
, which arises due to the axial symmetry of the problem.
For the sake of completeness, we include the proof in the following lemma.
The assumption
\eta_{2}=0
on
\{y=0\}
, is crucial to ensure that the flat portion of the boundary of
\Omega
remains fixed under the variation.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded open set and that
\psi\in W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
.
Let
\eta\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a given vector field with compact support in
\Omega
satisfying
\eta_{2}=0
on
\{y=0\}
.
Define the perturbation map
p_{t}
by

p_{t}(X)=X+t\eta(X),\qquad\text{for every $X\in\Omega$}.

Then the following hold:

(i)

for sufficiently small
t
(depending on the vector field
\eta
), the map
p_{t}:\Omega\to\Omega
is a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism and setting
q_{t}:=p_{t}^{-1}
, the function
\psi_{t}:=\psi\circ q_{t}
is well-defined and belongs to
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
;

(ii)

the function
t\to J(\psi_{t},I_{t})
, where
I_{t}:=I\circ q_{t}
,
is differentiable at
t=0
, and its derivative is given by:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}J(\psi_{t},I_{t})\right|_{t=0}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi\right|^{2}+yI\right)\mathrm{div\,}\eta-\dfrac{2}{y}\nabla\psi\cdot D\eta\nabla\psi-\eta_{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-I\right)\,dX,

which is referred to as the inner-variation of the functional
J(\tilde{\psi},\tilde{I})

at
(\psi,I)
, in direction of the vector field
\eta\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proof.

The first claim follows directly from the fact that
\eta\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Therefore, we focus solely on proving the latter claim.
By the change of variable
z=q_{t}(X)
, thus
X=p_{t}(z)=\left(p_{1}(z),p_{2}(z)\right)
, we get:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi_{t}|^{2}(X)+y\,I_{t}(X)\,dX

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\dfrac{1}{p_{2}(z)}\left|Dq_{t}(p_{t}(z))\nabla\psi(z)\right|^{2}+p_{2}(z)\,I(z)\,\right)\left|\det Dp_{t}(z)\right|\,dz,

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\dfrac{1}{p_{2}(z)}\nabla\psi(z)\cdot[Dp_{t}(z)]^{-T}[Dp_{t}(z)]^{-1}\nabla\psi(z)+p_{2}(z)\,I(z)\right)\,\left|\det Dp_{t}(z)\right|\,dz.

We notice that

\displaystyle Dp_{t}=Id+tD\eta,\quad[Dp_{t}]^{-1}=Id-tD\eta+\mathrm{o}(t),\quad\det Dp_{t}=1+t\,\mathrm{div\,}\eta+\mathrm{o}(t),

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{p_{2}(z)}=\dfrac{1}{z_{2}}-t\frac{\eta_{2}(z)}{z_{2}^{2}}+\mathrm{o}(t),

and we continue calculation as follows (for
t
small):

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\left(\dfrac{1}{z_{2}}-t\dfrac{\eta_{2}(z)}{z_{2}^{2}}\right)\nabla\psi(z)\cdot[Id-tD\eta]^{T}[Id-tD\eta]\nabla\psi(z)+\left(z_{2}+t\eta_{2}(z)\right)\,I(z)\right)\,\left|1+t\,\mathrm{div\,}\eta\right|\,dz+\mathrm{o}(t),

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\left(\dfrac{1}{z_{2}}-t\dfrac{\eta_{2}(z)}{z_{2}^{2}}\right)\left(|\nabla\psi(z)|^{2}-2t\nabla\psi(z)\cdot D\eta\nabla\psi(z)\right)+\left(z_{2}+t\eta_{2}(z)\right)I(z)\right)\,\left(1+t\,\mathrm{div\,}\eta\right)\,dz+\mathrm{o}(t),

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{z_{2}}|\nabla\psi(z)|^{2}-\dfrac{2t}{z_{2}}\nabla\psi(z)\cdot D\eta\nabla\psi(z)-t\dfrac{\eta_{2}(z)}{z_{2}^{2}}|\nabla\psi(z)|^{2}+z_{2}I(z)+t\eta_{2}(z)I(z)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+t\,\mathrm{div\,}\eta\left(\dfrac{1}{z_{2}}|\nabla\psi(z)|^{2}+z_{2}I(z)\right)\,dz+\mathrm{o}(t).

Thus

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi_{t}|^{2}(X)

\displaystyle+y\,I_{t}(X)\,dX=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}+yI(X)\,dX

\displaystyle+t\int_{\Omega}\left(\dfrac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}+yI\right)\mathrm{div\,}\eta-\dfrac{2}{y}\nabla\psi\cdot D\eta\nabla\psi-\dfrac{\eta_{2}}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}+\eta_{2}I\,dX+\mathrm{o}(t),

which concludes the proof of
(ii)
.
∎

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution in
\Omega
.
Then
\psi
is a
\mathcal{L}
-subsolution in
\Omega
, and
\mathcal{L}
-solution in
\{\psi>0\}
.

Proof.

Take
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\{\psi>0\},{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
. Then
I\geq\chi_{\{\psi>0\}}=1
Lebesgue-a.e. on the support of
\eta
, so

(5)

\int_{\{\psi>0\}}\left(\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi\right|^{2}+y\right)\mathrm{div\,}\eta-\dfrac{2}{y}\nabla\psi\cdot D\eta\nabla\psi-\dfrac{1}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\eta_{2}+\eta_{2}\,dX=0.

Note that
\eta=0
on
y=0
since
\psi
vanishes there. Therefore, integrating by parts in the first term of (
5
) yields:

\displaystyle-\int_{\{\psi>0\}}\dfrac{2\eta_{1}}{y}\left(\psi_{x}\psi_{xx}+\psi_{y}\psi_{xy}\right)+\int_{\{\psi>0\}}\dfrac{\eta_{2}}{y^{2}}\left(\psi_{x}^{2}+\psi_{y}^{2}\right)-\int_{\{\psi>0\}}\dfrac{2\eta_{2}}{y}\left(\psi_{x}\psi_{xy}+\psi_{y}\psi_{yy}\right)-\int_{\{\psi>0\}}\eta_{2};

and by integrating by parts on the second term of (
5
), we’ll have:

\displaystyle\int_{\{\psi>0\}}\dfrac{4}{y}\eta_{1}\psi_{x}\psi_{xx}+\int_{\{\psi>0\}}\eta_{1}\left(\dfrac{-2}{y^{2}}\psi_{x}\psi_{y}+\dfrac{2}{y}\psi_{xy}\psi_{y}+\dfrac{2}{y}\psi_{x}\psi_{yy}\right)+\int_{\{\psi>0\}}\dfrac{2}{y}\eta_{2}\left(\psi_{xx}\psi_{y}+\psi_{x}\psi_{yx}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\{\psi>0\}}\eta_{2}\left(\dfrac{-2}{y^{2}}\psi_{y}^{2}+\dfrac{4}{y}\psi_{y}\psi_{yy}\right).

Now by adding all of them up including the third term of (
5
), we will have:

\displaystyle 2\int_{\{\psi>0\}}\left(\eta\cdot\nabla\psi\right)\,\mathrm{div\,}\left(\dfrac{\nabla\psi}{y}\right)=2\int_{\{\psi>0\}}\left(\eta\cdot\nabla\psi\right)\,\mathcal{L}\psi=0.

This implies that
\psi
is an
\mathcal{L}
-solution in
\{\psi>0\}\cap\{\nabla\psi\neq 0\}
.
Hence,
\mathcal{L}\psi=0
in
\{\psi>0\}
due to the assumption
\psi\in C^{2}(\{\psi>0\})
.
Moreover, as
\psi
is continuous in
\Omega
, it follows that
\psi
is an
\mathcal{L}
-subsolution in
\Omega
, for instance by verifying that it satisfies the subsolution property in the viscosity sense.
∎

The following equality for variational solutions will be useful in the subsequent sections.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution in
\Omega
.
Then, for every ball
\overline{B_{s}^{+}(X_{0})}\subset\Omega
, the following equality holds:

(6)

\int_{B_{s}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dX=\int_{\partial B_{s}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\psi\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

where
\nu
denotes the outward unit normal on
\partial B_{s}^{+}(X_{0})
.

Proof.

Using the dominated convergence theorem, Proposition
2.2
, and integration by parts applied to the sublevel sets
\{\psi>\epsilon\}
, for a sequence of
\epsilon>0
such that
\partial\{\psi>\epsilon\}
is smooth (guaranteed for Lebesgue-a.e.
\epsilon
by Sard’s theorem), we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{B_{s}^{+}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dX

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{B_{s}^{+}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla(\psi-\epsilon)^{+}\,dX

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{B_{s}^{+}(X_{0})}(\psi-\epsilon)^{+}\,\mathrm{div}\left(\dfrac{\nabla\psi}{y}\right)\,dX+\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\partial B_{s}^{+}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}(\psi-\epsilon)^{+}\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{s}^{+}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on
\Omega
, and
B_{2r}^{+}(X_{0})\Subset\Omega
. Then
yI\in BV(B_{r}^{+}(X_{0}))
, and

\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\left|\nabla\left(yI\right)\right|\leq Cr(y_{0}+r),

where the constant
C
depends only on
C_{V}
.

Proof.

We derive the following basic estimate for
\mathcal{L}\psi
, understood as a non-negative Borel measure.
Let
\zeta
be a cutoff function such that
\zeta\equiv 1
on
B_{3r/2}^{+}(X_{0})
and
\zeta\equiv 0
outside
B_{2r}^{+}(X_{0})
. Then, we have

\mathcal{L}\psi\left(B_{3r/2}^{+}(X_{0})\right)\leq\int_{\Omega}\zeta\,d\mathcal{L}\psi=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{y}\nabla\psi\,\cdot\,\nabla\zeta\leq\int_{B_{2r}^{+}\setminus B_{3r/2}^{+}}\left|\frac{1}{y}\nabla\psi\right|\,|\nabla\zeta|\leq C_{V}Cr.

Let
\phi_{t}
be a standard mollifier, and define
\psi_{t}=\phi_{t}*\psi
. Then
\psi_{t}
is smooth, and from (
4
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi_{t}\right|^{2}\operatorname{div}\eta-\frac{2}{y}\nabla\psi_{t}\cdot D\eta\nabla\psi_{t}-\eta_{2}\left(\frac{1}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi_{t}|^{2}-I\right)\,dX

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi\right|^{2}\operatorname{div}\eta-\frac{2}{y}\nabla\psi\cdot D\eta\nabla\psi-\eta_{2}\left(\frac{1}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-I\right)\,dX

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}yI\,\operatorname{div}\eta\,dX,

for every
\eta\in C_{c}^{0,1}(B_{3r/2}^{+}(X_{0}),\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Using integration by parts (as in the proof of Proposition
2.2
), we can rewrite the expression as follows:

\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi_{t}\right|^{2}\operatorname{div}\eta-\frac{2}{y}\nabla\psi_{t}\cdot D\eta\nabla\psi_{t}-\eta_{2}\left(\frac{1}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi_{t}|^{2}-I\right)\,dX=2\int_{\Omega}\eta\cdot\nabla\psi_{t}\,\mathcal{L}\psi_{t}+\int_{\Omega}\eta_{2}I.

Invoking the Lipschitz condition (
3
) and Proposition
2.2
which guarantees
\mathcal{L}\psi\geq 0
, we have:

\left|\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi_{t}\right|^{2}\operatorname{div}\eta-\frac{2}{y}\nabla\psi_{t}\cdot D\eta\nabla\psi_{t}-\eta_{2}\left(\frac{1}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi_{t}|^{2}-I\right)\,dX\right|\leq CC_{V}\sup|\eta|\,r(y_{0}+r)+C\sup|\eta|r^{2}.

In particular,

\left|\int_{\Omega}yI\,\operatorname{div}\eta\,dX\right|\leq C\sup|\eta|\,r(y_{0}+r),

which implies that
yI\in BV(B_{r}^{+}(X_{0}))
, along with the stated estimate.
∎

3.
Monotonicity Formulas and Classification of Blowups

Given a variational solution
(\psi,I)
in
\Omega
, with free boundary
\Gamma:=\Omega\cap\partial\{\psi>0\}
,
we refer to the points of the free boundary that lie on the axis
y=0
as
degenerate
points.
The set of all such points is denoted by

S_{\psi}:=\Gamma\cap\{y=0\}.

It is important to note that the gradient condition
|\nabla\psi|=y
on the free boundary governs the behavior of the solution near degenerate points.
More specifically, near a degenerate point
X_{0}=(x_{0},0)
, the stream function
\psi
typically exhibits quadratic growth, i.e.,
\psi(X)\sim r^{2}
as
X\to X_{0}
.
This behavior is fundamentally different from that near other free boundary points.

To study the free boundary in regions away from the symmetric axis, we introduce the set of
non-degenerate
free boundary points.
Formally, this set is given by

N_{\psi}:=\left\{X_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Gamma:\,y_{0}>0\right\}.

We also define the
growth exponent

\mu
, which captures the asymptotic growth rate of
\psi
near free boundary points, as follows:

(7)

\mu=\mu(X_{0}):=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if }y_{0}=0,\\
1,&\text{if }y_{0}>0.\end{cases}

Now, for
X_{0}\in\Omega
, we define the following energy and boundary functionals:

D_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)=\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}+yI\,dX,

and

(8)

H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)=\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

where
B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})=B_{r}(X_{0})\cap\{y>0\}\subseteq\Omega
.
We now introduce the following monotonicity-based functional:

- For degenerate points
X_{0}
on the axis
\{y=0\}
,

M_{X_{0}}(\psi,r):=r^{-3}D_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)-2r^{-4}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r).

- For non-degenerate points
X_{0}\in\{y>0\}
,

M_{X_{0}}(\psi,r):=r^{-2}D_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)-r^{-3}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r).

To unify the notation, we use the growth constant
\mu=\mu(X_{0})
, and rewrite both expressions in the unified form:

M_{X_{0}}(\psi,r):=r^{-\mu-1}D_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)-\mu r^{-\mu-2}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r).

When the context is clear, we may omit the argument
\psi
and write simply
M_{X_{0}}(r)
.
Moreover, when the center point
X_{0}
is fixed or understood, we further abbreviate to
M(r)
. The same conventions apply to
D
and
H
.

The primary use of the monotonicity formula is to identify explicit blow-up limits.
We demonstrate this in Proposition
3.3
and Proposition
3.4
.
In particular, we apply the formula to show that any limit of a blow-up subsequence is a homogeneous function of degree
\mu
.

Lemma 3.1

(Monotonicity Formula)
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution in
B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
with
\psi(X_{0})=0
. Then the function
M_{X_{0}}(r)
is absolutely continuous and non-decreasing when
y_{0}=0
and almost non-decreasing when
y_{0}>0
.
Moreover, for Lebesgue-a.e.
r\in(0,r_{0})
, it satisfies the identity:

\displaystyle\frac{dM_{X_{0}}(r)}{dr}=

\displaystyle 2r^{-\mu-1}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\frac{\mu}{r}\psi\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

(9)

\displaystyle+(2-\mu)r^{-\mu-2}\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}(y-y_{0})I-\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dX+(2-\mu)r^{-\mu-3}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Proof.

Consider the function
\xi_{\epsilon}(t)
for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
as follows:

\xi_{\epsilon}(t):=\max\left(0,\min\left(1,\dfrac{t}{\epsilon}\right)\right)=\begin{cases}1\quad&t\geq\epsilon,\\
t/\epsilon\quad&0<t<\epsilon,\\
0\quad&t\leq 0.\end{cases}

We now define the vector field:

\eta(X):=\xi_{\epsilon}(r-|X-X_{0}|)\left[\begin{matrix}x-x_{0}\\[8.0pt]
\xi_{\epsilon}(y)(y-y_{0})\end{matrix}\right],

which satisfies the boundary condition
\eta_{2}=0
on
y=0
, and use it as a test function in the domain variation formula (
4
).
Passing to the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
, and using assumption (
3
), we obtain:

(10)

\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}2yI+(y-y_{0})I-\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dX-r\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\left[\left(\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi\right|^{2}+yI\right)-\frac{2}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\right)^{2}\right]d\mathcal{H}^{1}=0.

We now compute the derivative of the monotonicity functional.
Using the change of variables
Z=\dfrac{X-X_{0}}{r}
, a direct computation yields

\displaystyle\dfrac{d\left(r^{-\mu-2}H_{X_{0}}(r)\right)}{dr}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{d}{dr}\left(r^{-\mu-1}\int_{\partial B_{1}^{+}}\dfrac{1}{y_{0}+rz_{2}}\psi^{2}(X_{0}+rZ)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right)

\displaystyle=-(\mu+1)r^{-\mu-2}\int_{\partial B_{1}^{+}}\dfrac{1}{y_{0}+rz_{2}}\psi^{2}(X_{0}+rZ)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}-r^{-\mu-1}\int_{\partial B_{1}^{+}}\dfrac{z_{2}}{(y_{0}+rz_{2})^{2}}\psi^{2}(X_{0}+rZ)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+r^{-\mu-1}\int_{\partial B_{1}^{+}}\dfrac{2\psi}{y_{0}+rz_{2}}\nabla\psi(X_{0}+rZ)\cdot Z\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

(11)

\displaystyle=-(\mu+1)r^{-\mu-3}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}-r^{-\mu-3}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2r^{-\mu-2}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi\nabla\psi(X)\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Note that when
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
and
r>y_{0}
, the domain
\partial B_{1}^{+}
corresponds to the truncated unit circle
z_{2}>-y_{0}/r
. However, since the integrand vanishes on
y=0
, the result remains unaffected.
Also, we have

(12)

\dfrac{d\left(r^{-\mu-1}D_{X_{0}}(r)\right)}{dr}=-(\mu+1)r^{-\mu-2}\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\left(\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}+yI\right)\,dX+r^{-\mu-1}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\left(\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}+yI\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

When
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
is a degenerate point, we have
\mu=2
and
y_{0}=0
. Invoking equations (
10
) and (
12
), we obtain:

(13)

\dfrac{d\left(r^{-3}D_{X_{0}}(r)\right)}{dr}=-4r^{-4}\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dX+r^{-3}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{2}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Now, applying equation (
11
) together with (
6
), we get:

\displaystyle\dfrac{dM_{X_{0}}(r)}{dr}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{d\left(r^{-3}D_{X_{0}}(r)\right)}{dr}-2\dfrac{d\left(r^{-4}H_{X_{0}}(r)\right)}{dr}=2r^{-3}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\dfrac{2}{r}\psi\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

When
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
is a non-degenerate point, we have
\mu=1
. Once again, applying equations (
10
) and (
12
), together with (
6
) yields:

\displaystyle\dfrac{d\left(r^{-2}D_{X_{0}}(r)\right)}{dr}=

\displaystyle-2r^{-3}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi\nabla\psi(X)\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+r^{-3}\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}(y-y_{0})I-\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dX

(14)

\displaystyle+r^{-2}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{2}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\right)^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Combining this with (
11
), we obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{dM_{X_{0}}(r)}{dr}=

\displaystyle 2r^{-2}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\dfrac{\psi}{r}\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+r^{-3}\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}(y-y_{0})I-\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dX

\displaystyle+r^{-4}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

From relation (
3.1
), when
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
is a degenerate point, we have
\mu(X_{0})=2
, and
M_{X_{0}}(r)
is monotone. However, when
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
is a non-degenerate point,
\mu(X_{0})=1
, and there exists a universal constant
C_{M}=C(C_{V})
such that the function
M_{X_{0}}(r)+C_{M}r
is monotone. This behavior is referred to as almost monotonicity.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
r_{n}>0
be a sequence converging to
0
as
n\to+\infty
.
The corresponding blow-up sequence
\{\psi_{n}\}
is defined for
|X|<\frac{1}{r_{n}}{\rm dist}(X_{0},\partial\Omega)
as follows:

\psi_{n}(X):=\dfrac{\psi(X_{0}+r_{n}X)}{r_{n}^{\mu(X_{0})}},\qquad\text{for any }X_{0}\in\Gamma.

Here,
\mu(X_{0})
is the growth constant defined in (
7
).
If the functions
\psi_{n}
are uniformly bounded in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(B^{+}_{r})
, then there exists a subsequence converging weakly in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
to a limit function
\psi_{0}
, which we refer to as a blow-up limit of
\psi
at
X_{0}
. If
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
, the domain extends to all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and convergence is in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

We use the monotonicity formula to demonstrate that blow-up limits of variational solutions are one-homogeneous functions.
Our goal is to prove that for every
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
and for a variational solution
(\psi,I)
in
\Omega
, the blow-up limit of
\psi
can be classified into three types, as described in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.3

(Blow-up limits of the variational solutions at non-degenerate points)
.

Let
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
and let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on

B_{1}^{+}(X_{0})
, then the following statements hold:

(1)

The rescalings
\psi_{n}
converge to
\psi_{0}
strongly in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

(2)

Every blow-up limit
\psi_{0}
of
\psi_{n}
is a one-homogeneous function, i.e.
\psi_{0}(rX)=r\psi_{0}(X)

for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
r>0
.

(3)

If
\psi_{0}\not\equiv 0
, then the only blow-up limits are:

\psi_{0}(X)=y_{0}(X\cdot\nu)_{+}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi_{0}(X)=A|X\cdot\nu|

for some unit vector
\nu
and some positive constant
A>0
.

Proof.

(1)
Considering the rescaled functions
\psi_{n}(X)=\frac{\psi(X_{0}+r_{n}X)}{r_{n}}
and
I_{n}(X)=I(X_{0}+r_{n}X)
; these functions also satisfy the variational solution properties in the ball
B_{R}
when
n
is sufficiently large (however, a rescaled version of (
4
) must be used, where the weight
1/y
replaced by
1/(y_{0}+r_{n}y)
).
In particular,
\psi_{n}
remains uniformly bounded in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}
with respect to
n
.
For any sequence
r_{n}\to 0
, we can extract a subsequence (still denoted
r_{n}
) along which
\psi_{n}
converges locally uniformly and weakly in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}
to a Lipschitz function
\psi_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,+\infty)
.
Furthermore, this convergence can be strengthened on the set
A=\{\psi_{0}>0\}
.
Indeed, for each
Y\in A
, there exists a small ball
B_{\epsilon}(Y)\subseteq A
such that
\psi_{n}>0
in
B_{\epsilon}(Y)
for all sufficiently large
n
and

\Delta\psi_{n}=\dfrac{r_{n}}{y_{0}+r_{n}y}\partial_{y}\psi_{n},\quad\text{ in }B_{\epsilon}(Y).

By elliptic regularity, we conclude that
\nabla\psi_{n}\to\nabla\psi_{0}
in
B_{\epsilon}(Y)
,
which implies that
\psi_{0}
is a harmonic function in
A
.

Now, we prove the strong convergence in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Take any
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\eta\geq 0
, and a sequence
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
such that
\{\psi_{0}>\epsilon\}
is smooth on a neighborhood of
\operatorname{supp}\eta
.
We have

\displaystyle\int_{\operatorname{supp}\eta}|\nabla\psi_{0}|^{2}\eta\,dX

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\operatorname{supp}\eta}\nabla\psi_{0}\cdot\nabla\left(\psi_{0}-\epsilon\right)_{+}\eta\,dX

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\operatorname{supp}\eta}\mathrm{div}\left(\eta\nabla\psi_{0}\right)\left(\psi_{0}-\epsilon\right)_{+}\,dX

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\operatorname{supp}\eta}\eta\Delta\psi_{0}\left(\psi_{0}-\epsilon\right)_{+}\,dX-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\operatorname{supp}\eta}\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla\psi_{0}\left(\psi_{0}-\epsilon\right)_{+}\,dX

\displaystyle=-\int_{\operatorname{supp}\eta}\psi_{0}\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla\psi_{0}\,dX,

where the first and last steps used dominated convergence and the middle step that
\psi_{0}
is harmonic on
\{\psi_{0}>\epsilon\}\subseteq A
. The same computation is valid with
\psi_{n}
in place of
\psi_{0}
with weight
1/(y_{0}+r_{n}y)
, and so we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\operatorname{supp}\eta}|\nabla\psi_{0}|^{2}\eta\,dX

\displaystyle=-\int_{\operatorname{supp}\eta}\psi_{0}\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla\psi_{0}\,dX

\displaystyle=-\lim_{r_{n}\to 0}\int_{\operatorname{supp}\eta}\frac{y_{0}}{y_{0}+r_{n}y}\psi_{n}\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla\psi_{n}\,dX=\lim_{r_{n}\to 0}\int_{\operatorname{supp}\eta}\frac{y_{0}}{y_{0}+r_{n}y}|\nabla\psi_{n}|^{2}\eta\,dX.

This implies that
\psi_{n}\to\psi_{0}
strongly in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as
n\to+\infty
.

(2)
By integrating the equality (
3.1
) with
\mu=1
over the interval
(r_{n}\rho_{1},\,r_{n}\rho_{2})
, for
0<\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{r_{n}\rho_{1}}^{r_{n}\rho_{2}}\dfrac{dM_{X_{0}}(r)}{dr}\,dr=

\displaystyle\int_{r_{n}\rho_{1}}^{r_{n}\rho_{2}}2r^{-2}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\dfrac{\psi}{|X-X_{0}|}\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}dr

\displaystyle+\int_{r_{n}\rho_{1}}^{r_{n}\rho_{2}}r^{-3}\left(\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}(y-y_{0})I-\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\right)\;dXdr

\displaystyle+\int_{r_{n}\rho_{1}}^{r_{n}\rho_{2}}r^{-4}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r_{n}\rho_{2}}(X_{0})\setminus B_{r_{n}\rho_{1}}(X_{0})}\dfrac{2}{y}|X-X_{0}|^{-2}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\dfrac{\psi}{|X-X_{0}|}\right)^{2}\;dX

\displaystyle+\int_{r_{n}\rho_{1}}^{r_{n}\rho_{2}}r^{-3}\left(\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}(y-y_{0})I-\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\right)\;dXdr

\displaystyle+\int_{B_{r_{n}\rho_{2}}(X_{0})\setminus B_{r_{n}\rho_{1}}(X_{0})}|X-X_{0}|^{-4}\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}\psi^{2}(X)\,dX.

After rescaling, we can compute

\displaystyle\int_{r_{n}\rho_{1}}^{r_{n}\rho_{2}}\dfrac{dM_{X_{0}}(r)}{dr}\;dr=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\rho_{2}}\setminus B_{\rho_{1}}}\dfrac{2}{y_{0}+r_{n}\overline{y}}\left|\overline{X}\right|^{-4}\left(\nabla\psi_{n}\cdot\overline{X}-\psi_{n}\right)^{2}\;d\overline{X}

\displaystyle+r_{n}\int_{\rho_{1}}^{\rho_{2}}r^{-3}\left(\int_{B_{r}}\overline{y}I_{n}-\dfrac{\overline{y}}{\left(y_{0}+r_{n}\overline{y}\right)^{2}}\left|\nabla\psi_{n}\right|^{2}\right)\,d\overline{X}dr

\displaystyle+\int_{B_{\rho_{2}}\setminus B_{\rho_{1}}}\left|\overline{X}\right|^{-4}\dfrac{r_{n}\overline{y}}{\left(y_{0}+r_{n}\overline{y}\right)^{2}}\psi_{n}^{2}\,d\overline{X}.

so

\limsup_{r_{n}\to 0}\int_{B_{\rho_{2}}\setminus B_{\rho_{1}}}\dfrac{2\left|\overline{X}\right|^{-4}}{y_{0}+r_{n}\overline{y}}\left(\nabla\psi_{n}\cdot\overline{X}-\psi_{n}\right)^{2}\;d\overline{X}\leq\lim_{r_{n}\to 0}M_{X_{0}}(r_{n}\rho_{2})-M_{X_{0}}(r_{n}\rho_{1})=0.

The desired homogeneity then follows from the strong convergence of
\psi_{n}
to
\psi_{0}
.

(3)

We now derive the form of the function
\psi_{0}(X)
in polar coordinates, under the assumption that
\{\psi_{0}>0\}
is nonempty.
From
(1)
, we know that
\psi_{0}
is harmonic in
\{\psi_{0}>0\}
.
This together with the homogeneity, i.e.
\psi_{0}(X)=r\phi(\theta)
, gives
\phi^{\prime\prime}+\phi=0
in
\{\phi>0\}
, where
\phi
is a
2\pi
-periodic function.

Suppose
\phi>0
on an interval
(0,a)
with
\phi(0)=\phi(a)=0
, then
a=\pi
and
\phi(\theta)=A_{+}\sin(\theta)
on
[0,\pi]
for some constant
A_{+}>0
.
If
\phi
is not identically zero on
[\pi,2\pi]
, a similar argument yields
\phi(\theta)=A_{-}\sin(\theta)
on
[\pi,2\pi]
for some
A_{-}>0
.
Therefore, in general,

\psi_{0}(X)=A_{+}\left(X\cdot\nu\right)_{+}+A_{-}\left(X\cdot\nu\right)_{-},

for some unit vector
\nu
, with at least one of the constants
A_{\pm}
nonzero.

On the other hand, the rescaled functions
\psi_{n}
satisfy the following domain variation formula:

\displaystyle\int\left(\frac{1}{y_{0}+r_{n}y}\left|\nabla\psi_{n}\right|^{2}+(y_{0}+r_{n}y)I_{n}\right)\mathrm{div\,}\xi

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{y_{0}+r_{n}y}\nabla\psi_{n}\cdot D\xi\nabla\psi_{n}

\displaystyle-r_{n}\xi_{2}\left(\frac{1}{(y_{0}+r_{n}y)^{2}}\left|\nabla\psi_{0}\right|^{2}-I_{n}\right)\,dX=0.

Considering the strong
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
-limit of
I_{n}
along a subsequence, and denoting this limit by
I_{0}\in\{0,1\}
(Lebesgue-a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) and using the fact that
I_{0}\geq\chi_{\{\psi_{0}>0\}}
together with the local uniform convergence of
\psi_{n}
to
\psi_{0}
, we get
I_{0}=1
Lebesgue-a.e. in
\{\psi_{0}>0\}
.
Now, after passing to the limit (notice that we have the strong convergence of
\psi_{n}
’s and also the compact embedding of
BV
in
L^{1}
), we obtain:

(15)

\int\left(\frac{1}{y_{0}}\left|\nabla\psi_{0}\right|^{2}+y_{0}I_{0}\right)\mathrm{div\,}\xi-\frac{2}{y_{0}}\nabla\psi_{0}\cdot D\xi\nabla\psi_{0}\,dX=0.

We observe from (
15
) that if
\psi_{0}=0
on
\operatorname{supp}\xi
, then one has

\int I_{0}\ \mathrm{div\,}\xi\,dX=0;

which gives that
I_{0}
is a constant in each connected component of
\{\psi_{0}=0\}
.

First, consider the case when one of the constants
A_{\pm}
is zero; for instance,
\psi_{0}(X)=A_{+}\left(X\cdot\nu\right)_{+}
.
We already know that
I_{0}=1
on
\{X\cdot\nu>0\}
and that
I_{0}
is constant on
\{X\cdot\nu<0\}
, denoted as
\overline{I}_{0}
.
The outer normal to
\partial\{\psi_{0}>0\}=\{X\cdot\nu=0\}
is the constant normal
-\nu
.
Let
\xi(X):=\zeta(X)\nu
for any
\zeta\in C^{1}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
and insert
\xi
into the equation (
15
). Integrating by parts gives that:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{\{X\cdot\nu>0\}}\left(\frac{A_{+}^{2}}{y_{0}}+y_{0}\right)\mathrm{div\,}\xi-\frac{2A_{+}^{2}}{y_{0}}\nu\cdot D\xi\nu\,dX+\int_{\{X\cdot\nu<0\}}y_{0}\overline{I}_{0}\ \mathrm{div\,}\xi\,dX

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\{X\cdot\nu=0\}}\left(\frac{A_{+}^{2}}{y_{0}}+y_{0}(\overline{I}_{0}-1)\right)\zeta\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Since this holds for arbitrary
\zeta
, it follows that
A_{+}^{2}=y_{0}^{2}(1-\overline{I}_{0})
on
\{X\cdot\nu=0\}
. Because we assumed
A_{+}>0
, we deduce that
\overline{I}_{0}=0
, and hence
\psi_{0}(X)=y_{0}\left(X\cdot\nu\right)_{+}
.

Now, suppose that both of
A_{\pm}
are not zero. We will show that
A_{+}=A_{-}
. In this case, obviously we have
I_{0}=1
Lebesgue-a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Therefore, using (
15
) and testing with a vector field
\xi(X):=\zeta(X)\nu
implies that

0=\int\left(\mathrm{div\,}\xi-2\nu\cdot D\xi\nu\right)\left(A_{+}^{2}\chi_{\{X\cdot\nu>0\}}+A_{-}^{2}\chi_{\{X\cdot\nu<0\}}\right)\,dX=\int_{\{X\cdot\nu=0\}}\left(A_{+}^{2}-A_{-}^{2}\right)\zeta\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Since this holds for arbitrary
\zeta
, we conclude
A_{+}=A_{-}:=A
. Therefore,
\psi_{0}(X)=A|X\cdot\nu|
.
∎

In the next proposition, we once again apply the monotonicity formula to show that blow-up limits at degenerate points are two-homogeneous functions, and we explicitly determine the form of the blow-up limit.

Proposition 3.4

(Blow-up limits of the variational solutions at degenerate points)
.

Let
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
and let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on

B_{1}^{+}(X_{0})
, then the following statements hold:

(1)

\psi_{n}
converges to
\psi_{0}
strongly in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
.

(2)

Every blow-up limit
\psi_{0}
of
\psi_{n}
is a two-homogeneous function, i.e.
\psi_{0}(rX)=r^{2}\psi_{0}(X)

for each
X\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
and
r>0
.

(3)

If
\psi_{0}\not\equiv 0
, then we have
\psi_{0}(X)=Ay^{2}
for some
A>0
.

Proof.

The proofs of
(1)
and
(2)
are analogous to those in the non-degenerate case.
For
(3)
, we refer to Proposition 4.3 in
[
16
]
.
∎

In the following proposition, we characterize all possible values of
M_{X_{0}}(0^{+})
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution in
\Omega
, and suppose
B_{1}^{+}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
. Then
M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})=-\infty
if and only if
\psi(X_{0})>0
. If
\psi(X_{0})=0
, then

(16)

M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\left|\left\{X\in B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})\,:\,I(X)=1\right\}\right|_{{\mathrm{w}}}}{r^{\mu+1}}\in\left[0,\omega(X_{0})\right],

where
\mu=\mu(X_{0})
is the growth exponent defined in (
7
), and
\left|\,\cdot\,\right|_{{\mathrm{w}}}
denotes the weighted volume with respect to the measure
y\,dX
, given by

\left|A\right|_{{\mathrm{w}}}:=\int_{A}y\,dX.

The energy upper bound
\omega(X_{0})
is given by

\omega(X_{0}):=r^{-\mu-1}\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}y\,dX=\begin{cases}\dfrac{2}{3},\quad&X_{0}\in S_{\psi},\\
y_{0}\pi,\quad&X_{0}\in N_{\psi}\text{ and }r<y_{0}.\end{cases}

Furthermore, the map
X_{0}\mapsto M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})
is upper semi-continuous in
\Omega
.

Proof.

The fact that
M_{X_{0}}(r)\to-\infty
when
\psi(X_{0})>0
is straightforward.
In this case,
X_{0}
must lie away from the axis
y=0
, and thus
\mu(X_{0})=1
.
Moreover, the quantity
r^{-2}D_{X_{0}}(r)
remains bounded, while

r^{-1}H_{X_{0}}(r)\to\frac{1}{y_{0}}\psi^{2}(X_{0})|\partial B_{1}^{+}|
.
Therefore, we may assume
\psi(X_{0})=0
and proceed to establish (
16
).

First, in this case,
M_{X_{0}}(r)
is bounded in terms of the constant
C_{V}
. More precisely, when
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
we have

D_{X_{0}}(r)\leq\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}C_{V}^{2}y+yI\,dX\leq(1+C_{V}^{2})\omega(X_{0})r^{3},

while

H_{X_{0}}(r)\leq\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}C_{V}^{2}y^{3}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\leq C_{V}^{2}\left|\partial B_{1}^{+}\right|r^{4}.

In particular,
M_{X_{0}}(0^{+})
is finite.
When
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
and
r<y_{0}/2
, we similarly obtain
D_{X_{0}}(r)\leq(1+C_{V}^{2})\omega(X_{0})r^{2}
and
H_{X_{0}}(r)\leq 5y_{0}C_{V}^{2}\left|\partial B_{1}^{+}\right|r^{3}
.

Consider the rescaled functions
\psi_{r}(X)=\frac{\psi(X_{0}+rX)}{r^{\mu}}
and
I_{r}(X)=I(X_{0}+rX)
.
These functions also satisfy the variational solution properties in half-ball
B_{R}^{+}
when
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
and in the ball
B_{R}
when
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
and
r
is sufficiently small. In the latter case, a rescaled version of (
4
) must be used, where the weight
1/y
is replaced by
1/(y_{0}+ry)
.

By Proposition
3.3
and Proposition
3.4
, for any sequence
r_{k}\to 0
we can extract a subsequence (still denoted
r_{k}
) along which
\psi_{r_{k}}
converges strongly in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}
to a function
\psi_{0}
, which is homogeneous of degree
\mu(X_{0})
, an
\mathcal{L}
-solution in
\{\psi_{0}>0\}
when
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
, and harmonic when
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
.

From the strong convergence of
\psi_{r_{k}}
in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(B_{1})
, we obtain in case
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}

\int_{B_{1}}\frac{1}{y_{0}}|\nabla\psi_{0}|^{2}\,dX-\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{1}{y_{0}}\psi_{0}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=\lim_{r_{k}\to 0}\int_{B_{1}}\frac{1}{y_{0}+r_{k}y}|\nabla\psi_{r_{k}}|^{2}\,dX-\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{1}{y_{0}+r_{k}y}\psi_{r_{k}}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Arguing as in (
6
), the left-hand side vanishes, since
\psi_{0}
is a homogeneous harmonic function on
\{\psi_{0}>0\}
.
It follows that

0=\lim_{r_{k}\to 0}r_{k}^{-2}\int_{B_{r_{k}}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dX-r_{k}^{-3}\int_{\partial B_{r_{k}}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=\lim_{r_{k}\to 0}M_{X_{0}}(\psi,r_{k})-\frac{\left|\{X\in B_{r_{k}}(X_{0})\,:\,I(X)=1\}\right|_{{\mathrm{w}}}}{r_{k}^{2}}.

Therefore,

M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\left|\left\{X\in B_{r}(X_{0})\,:\,I(X)=1\right\}\right|_{{\mathrm{w}}}}{r^{2}}.

Since this holds along every sequence
r_{k}\to 0
, the conclusion follows.
The argument for
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
proceeds in an analogous manner.

The upper semi-continuity of the map
X_{0}\mapsto M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})
follows directly from the fact that it is the monotone limit of continuous functions.
Indeed, when
y_{0}>0
, let
\delta>0
and
K<+\infty
. Then, Lemma
3.1
implies that

M_{X}(\psi,0^{+})\leq M_{X}(\psi,r)+C_{M}r\leq M_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)+C_{M}r+\frac{\delta}{2}\leq\begin{cases}M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})+\delta,&\quad\text{if \,}M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})>-\infty,\\
-K,&\quad\text{if \,}M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})=-\infty,\end{cases}

provided we first fix
r>0
(depending on
X_{0}
), and then take
|X-X_{0}|
sufficiently small.

However, when
y_{0}=0
, since
M_{X}(\psi,r)
is not continuous in
X
near
X_{0}
; thus, we must distinguish between sequences approaching
X_{0}
along
\{y=0\}
and along
\{y>0\}
.
If
\{y=0\}\ni X_{n}\to X_{0}
, same reasoning as above shows upper semi-continuity.

If
\{y>0\}\ni X_{n}\to X_{0}
, and
\psi(X_{n})>0
then
M_{X_{n}}(\psi,0^{+})=-\infty
which implies that

\limsup_{n\to+\infty}M_{X_{n}}(\psi,0^{+})\leq M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})
.
In the case
\psi(X_{n})=0
, we have already shown that
M_{X_{n}}(\psi,0^{+})\in[0,\omega(X_{n})]
, so

\limsup_{n\to+\infty}M_{X_{n}}(\psi,0^{+})\leq\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\omega(X_{n})=0\leq M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+}).

∎

4.
The Frequency Formula

Given a variational solution
(\psi,I)
in
\Omega
, the
frequency formula
is defined as

{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)=\frac{rD_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)-r\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}y\,dX}{H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)},

and any
B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
, where the denominator is positive.

For convenience, we also introduce the
volume difference
as

{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)=\frac{r\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}y\left(1-I\right)\,dX}{H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)}\geq 0.

With these definitions, we have the identity

(17)

{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)+{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)=\frac{r\int_{B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dX}{H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)},

which corresponds to the classical Almgren frequency formula for harmonic functions.
When there is no ambiguity, we suppress the parameters
\psi
and
X_{0}
and simply write
\mathcal{N}(r)
and
\mathcal{V}(r)
.

We will establish the monotonicity of the frequency formula in separate subsections.

4.1.
Monotonicity of the Frequency Formula: Degenerate Case

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution in
\Omega
, and suppose
B_{r_{0}}^{+}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
. Assume that
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
and that
M_{X_{0}}(\psi,t)\geq\omega(X_{0})
for some
t<r_{0}
.
Then, on the interval
(t,r_{0})
, the frequency formula
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)
satisfies
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)\geq 2
, and
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)
is absolutely continuous and non-decreasing. Moreover, the derivative of the frequency formula is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)

\displaystyle=\frac{2r^{-1}}{H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\frac{\psi}{r}\left({\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)+{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)\right)\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

(18)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2}{r}\left({\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}^{2}(\psi,r)+{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)\left({\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)-2\right)\right)

for Lebesgue-a.e.
r\in(t,r_{0})
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
X_{0}=\mathbf{0}
.
First, notice that by Lemma
3.1
, we have
M_{\mathbf{0}}(r)\geq M_{\mathbf{0}}(t)\geq\omega(\mathbf{0})
, for
r\in(t,r_{0})
which implies
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{0}}(r)\geq 2
. Furthermore, this lemma implies that
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{0}}(r)
is absolutely continuous.
Recalling the computations in (
13
) and (
11
), we obtain

(r^{-3}D)^{\prime}=-4r^{-4}\int_{B_{r}^{+}}\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dX+r^{-3}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}}\frac{2}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

and

(r^{-4}H)^{\prime}=-4r^{-5}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+2r^{-4}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Writing
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{0}}
for short, we have

{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}=\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{r^{-3}D-\omega}{r^{-4}H}\right)=\frac{(r^{-3}D)^{\prime}}{r^{-4}H}-\frac{(r^{-4}H)^{\prime}\left(r^{-3}D-\omega\right)}{(r^{-4}H)^{2}}=\frac{(r^{-3}D)^{\prime}-(r^{-4}H)^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}}{r^{-4}H}.

Expanding the numerator and simplifying yields

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{H}\left(-4\int_{B_{r}^{+}}\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dX+r\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}}\frac{2}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{{\mathcal{N}}}{H}\left(-4r^{-1}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+2\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=-\frac{4}{r}({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}})+\frac{2r}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}}\frac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\frac{4}{r}{\mathcal{N}}-\frac{2}{r}{\mathcal{N}}({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}}),

where in the last line we have used (
6
) and (
17
), together with the definition of
H
.
The boundary integral can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{2r}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}}\frac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=\frac{2r}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}}\frac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\frac{\psi}{r}({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}})\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\frac{2}{r}({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}})^{2}.

Substituting this into (
19
) gives

{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}=\frac{2r}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}}\frac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\frac{\psi}{r}({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}})\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\frac{2}{r}{\mathcal{V}}({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}}-2).

Finally, to confirm that
{\mathcal{N}}
is nondecreasing, note that
{\mathcal{V}}\geq 0
and, as established earlier,
{\mathcal{N}}\geq 2
. These conditions guarantee that all terms in the expression for
{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}
are non-negative.
∎

4.2.
Almost Monotonicity of the Frequency Formula: Nondegenerate Case

Before proving the monotonicity of the frequency formula, we need a modified monotonicity result for
M
, which provides a lower estimate for
M(r)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on
\Omega
, and suppose
B_{r_{0}}^{+}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
.
Fix a constant
\delta_{0}>0
, and assume that
X_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})\in N_{\psi}
with
y_{0}\geq\delta_{0}
.
Moreover, suppose that
M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})=\omega(X_{0})
.
Then there exists a constant
C_{\star}=C(\delta_{0})
such that the inequality

M_{X_{0}}(r)+C_{\star}r^{-5/2}H_{X_{0}}(r)\geq\omega(X_{0}),

holds for
r<\min(r_{0},y_{0}/128)
.

Proof.

Recall (
3.1
) and (
11
), and note that
\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}(y-y_{0})\,dX=0\quad\text{for }r<y_{0}
, thus for
r<y_{0}
we can compute

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}(M+C_{\star}r^{-5/2}H)\geq

\displaystyle\ r^{-3}\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}(y-y_{0})(I-1)-\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dX+r^{-4}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

\displaystyle-\frac{3C_{\star}}{2}r^{-7/2}H+2C_{\star}r^{-5/2}\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dX-C_{\star}r^{-7/2}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{r^{-2}}{y_{0}-r}\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}y(1-I)+\dfrac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dX-\frac{r^{-3}+C_{\star}r^{-5/2}}{y_{0}-r}H-\frac{3C_{\star}}{2}r^{-7/2}H

\displaystyle+2C_{\star}r^{-5/2}\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dX,

where we have also used the inequality
-ry/(y_{0}-r)\leq y-y_{0}\leq ry/(y_{0}-r)
when
r<y_{0}
.
Now, using the definition of
M
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}\left(M+C_{\star}r^{-5/2}H\right)\geq

\displaystyle\left(2C_{\star}r^{-1/2}-\frac{1}{y_{0}-r}\right)\left(M-\omega(X_{0})+C_{\star}r^{-5/2}H\right)+\left(2C_{\star}r^{-5/2}-\frac{2r^{-2}}{y_{0}-r}\right)\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}y(1-I)\,dX

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{C_{\star}}{2}r^{-7/2}-2C_{\star}^{2}r^{-3}-\frac{2r^{-3}}{y_{0}-r}\right)H.

Choosing
C_{\star}=\sqrt{2/\delta_{0}}
and restricting to
r\leq y_{0}/128
, the coefficients of all three terms are positive. Hence,

\frac{d}{dr}\left(M-\omega(X_{0})+C_{\star}r^{-5/2}H\right)\geq\left(2C_{\star}r^{-1/2}-\frac{1}{y_{0}-r}\right)\left(M-\omega(X_{0})+C_{\star}r^{-5/2}H\right).

This proves the statement, since
r^{-3}H(r)
is bounded and therefore

\lim_{r\to 0}M(r)-\omega(X_{0})+C_{\star}r^{-5/2}H(r)=0.

∎

Lemma 4.3

(non-degenerate case)
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on
\Omega
, and suppose
B_{r_{0}}^{+}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
.
Fix a constant
\delta_{0}>0
, and assume that
X_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})\in N_{\psi}
with
y_{0}\geq\delta_{0}
.
Moreover, suppose that
M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})=\omega(X_{0})
.
Then, the frequency function
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)
satisfies

{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)\;\geq\;1-C_{\star}r^{1/2}.

Furthermore, the function
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)
is absolutely continuous and almost monotone.
In particular, there exists a constant
C=C(\delta_{0})
such that for Lebesgue-a.e.
r<\min(r_{0},y_{0}/128,\delta_{0}/2)
,

(20)

(y_{0}-r)^{2}\dfrac{d}{dr}\left[(y_{0}-r)^{-2}{\mathcal{N}}\right]\geq\dfrac{2r}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\dfrac{\psi}{r}\left({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}}\right)\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\frac{2}{r}\left({\mathcal{V}}^{2}+{\mathcal{V}}\left({\mathcal{N}}-1-\frac{r}{y_{0}-r}\right)\right).

Proof.

From Lemma
4.2
, we deduce the first statement of the lemma. Indeed,

(21)

{\mathcal{N}}(r)=\frac{rD-r^{3}\omega(X_{0})}{H}=\frac{r^{3}M+H-r^{3}\omega(X_{0})}{H}\geq 1-C_{\star}r^{1/2}.

To analyze the derivative of the frequency function, recall from (
13
) and (
11
) that

\displaystyle(r^{-2}D)^{\prime}=

\displaystyle-2r^{-3}\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dX+r^{-3}\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}(y-y_{0})I-\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dX

\displaystyle+r^{-2}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\frac{2}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu\right)^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

and

(r^{-3}H)^{\prime}=-2r^{-4}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}-r^{-4}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+2r^{-3}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi\nabla\psi(X)\cdot\nu\,d\mathcal{H}^{1};

Thus, computing
{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}
as in the degenerate case gives

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{r^{-2}D-\omega(X_{0})}{r^{-3}H}\right)=\frac{(r^{-2}D)^{\prime}-(r^{-3}H)^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}}{r^{-3}H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{2r}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\dfrac{\psi}{r}\left({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}}\right)\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\frac{2}{r}\left({\mathcal{V}}^{2}+{\mathcal{V}}({\mathcal{N}}-1)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{H}\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}(y-y_{0})(I-1)-\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dX+\frac{r^{-1}{\mathcal{N}}}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{y-y_{0}}{y^{2}}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

where we have used the relation
\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}(y-y_{0})\,dX=0
for
r<y_{0}
in the last line.
Applying the inequality
-ry/(y_{0}-r)\leq y-y_{0}\leq ry/(y_{0}-r)
for
r<y_{0}
, and using that
{\mathcal{N}}\geq 0
when
r<\delta_{0}/2
(by (
21
)), we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}\geq

\displaystyle\dfrac{2r}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\dfrac{\psi}{r}\left({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}}\right)\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\frac{2}{r}\left({\mathcal{V}}^{2}+{\mathcal{V}}({\mathcal{N}}-1)\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{r}{(y_{0}-r)H}\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}y(1-I)+\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}dX-\frac{r^{-1}{\mathcal{N}}}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{r}{y(y_{0}-r)}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{2r}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}-\dfrac{\psi}{r}\left({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}}\right)\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\frac{2}{r}\left({\mathcal{V}}^{2}+{\mathcal{V}}({\mathcal{N}}-1)\right)-\frac{2({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}})}{y_{0}-r}

Therefore,

{\mathcal{N}}^{\prime}+\frac{2{\mathcal{N}}}{y_{0}-r}\geq\dfrac{2r}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\dfrac{1}{y}\left(\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\dfrac{\psi}{r}\left({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}}\right)\right)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\frac{2}{r}\left({\mathcal{V}}^{2}+{\mathcal{V}}\left({\mathcal{N}}-1-\frac{r}{y_{0}-r}\right)\right).

∎

Remark 4.4
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
4.3
, we get

\displaystyle(y_{0}-r)^{-2}{\mathcal{N}}(r)-(y_{0}-s)^{-2}{\mathcal{N}}(s)\geq

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}}{t(y_{0}-t)^{2}}\ dt-C_{\star}\left(\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}}{t(y_{0}-t)^{2}}\ dt\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2}{(y_{0}-t)^{2}}\ dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle-\left(\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}}{t(y_{0}-t)^{2}}\ dt\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2t}{(y_{0}-t)^{4}}\ dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}}{t(y_{0}-t)^{2}}\ dt-2\left(C(\delta_{0})(r-s)\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}}{t(y_{0}-t)^{2}}\ dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-C(\delta_{0})(r-s)

Thus the almost monotonicity relation

(22)

{\mathcal{N}}(s)\leq{\mathcal{N}}(r)+C_{N}(r-s)(1+{\mathcal{N}}(r))

holds for
s\leq r<\min(y_{0}/128,\delta_{0}/2)
.

Remark 4.5
.

By multiplying through and applying (
6
) and (
17
), the right hand side of formulas (
18
) and (
20
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{2r}{H}\int_{\partial B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\frac{\psi}{r}{\mathcal{N}}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+\frac{2}{r}{\mathcal{V}}\left({\mathcal{N}}-\mu(X_{0})-\frac{r\left(2-\mu(X_{0})\right)}{y_{0}-r}\right).

This alternative formulation is more convenient when handling the first term on the right-hand side. Conversely, (
18
) is more beneficial when focusing on the
{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}^{2}(\psi,s)
term or when finer control over the coefficient associated with the homogeneity order of
\psi
is needed.
In subsequent sections, we will repeatedly integrate the above identity, for which the following estimate, when
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
, will be useful.
Using the estimate
{\mathcal{N}}(t)\geq 1-C_{\star}t^{1/2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2t}{H}\int_{\partial B_{t}^{+}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi\cdot\nu-\frac{\psi}{r}{\mathcal{N}}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}dt

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{y_{0}-s}{y_{0}-r}\right)^{2}{\mathcal{N}}(r)-{\mathcal{N}}(s)+(y_{0}-s)^{2}\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2C_{\star}{\mathcal{V}}}{t^{1/2}(y_{0}-t)^{2}}+\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}}{(y_{0}-t)^{3}}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{N}}(r)-{\mathcal{N}}(s)+C(r-s){\mathcal{N}}(r)+Cr^{1/2}\left(\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}}{t(y_{0}-t)^{2}}\,dt\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{N}}(r)-{\mathcal{N}}(s)+Cr{\mathcal{N}}(r)+Cr^{1/2}\left({\mathcal{N}}(r)^{1/2}+r^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{N}}(r)-{\mathcal{N}}(s)+Cr^{1/2}{\mathcal{N}}(r)+Cr,

where we have used the computation in Remark
4.4
to estimate the integral term involving
{\mathcal{V}}
.
Here the constant
C
depends on
\delta_{0}
, and the estimate holds for sufficiently small
r
, as in Lemma
4.3
.

4.3.
Further Properties of the Frequency Formula

Lemma 4.6

(degenrate case)
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on
\Omega
, and
B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
. Assume that
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
,

M_{X_{0}}(\psi,r/2)\geq\omega(X_{0})
and that
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)\leq N_{+}
for some
N_{+}>2
. Then, there is a constant

C=C(N_{+})
such that for any
s\in[r/2,r]
, we have:

\displaystyle s^{-4}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,s)\leq r^{-4}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)\leq Cs^{-4}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,s).

Proof.

We can assume that
X_{0}=\mathbf{0}
. From the assumptions
{\mathcal{N}}_{0}(s)\geq 2
, and since
{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{0}}(s)\geq 0
, we have
{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{0}}(s)+{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{0}}(s)\geq 2
. Additionally, we have the following expression for the derivative:

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{ds}s^{-4}H_{\mathbf{0}}(\psi,s)

\displaystyle=-4s^{-5}\int_{\partial B_{s}^{+}}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}+2s^{-4}\int_{\partial B_{s}^{+}}\dfrac{1}{y}\psi\nabla\psi(X)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1};

which, with the help of (
6
) and (
17
), gives:

\frac{d}{ds}s^{-4}H_{\mathbf{0}}(\psi,s)=\frac{H_{\mathbf{0}}}{s^{5}}\left(-4+2({\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{0}}+{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{0}})\right)\geq 0.

This yields the lower bound of this lemma. For the upper bound, we can proceed directly using Lemma
4.1
as follows:

\int_{r/2}^{r}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{0}}^{2}(s)}{s}\,ds\leq\int_{r/2}^{r}\frac{d}{ds}{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{0}}(s)\,ds={\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{0}}(r)-{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{0}}\left(r/2\right)\leq N_{+}-2;

hence,

\int_{r/2}^{r}{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{0}}(s)\,ds\leq\sqrt{r/2\int_{r/2}^{r}{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{0}}^{2}(s)\,ds}\leq\sqrt{r^{2}\int_{r/2}^{r}\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{0}}^{2}(s)}{2s}\,ds}\leq\frac{r\sqrt{N_{+}-2}}{2}.

Now, noting that
s\geq r/2
, by integrating the following formula

\frac{d}{ds}\log s^{-4}H_{\mathbf{0}}(s)=\frac{\frac{d}{ds}s^{-4}H_{\mathbf{0}}(s)}{s^{-4}H_{\mathbf{0}}(s)}=\frac{2}{s}\left({\mathcal{N}}_{\mathbf{0}}+{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{0}}-2\right)\leq\frac{4}{r}\left(N_{+}+{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{0}}-2\right)

over
[s,r]
, we obtain:

\log r^{-4}H_{\mathbf{0}}(r)-\log s^{-4}H_{\mathbf{0}}(s)\leq\frac{4}{r}\int_{s}^{r}\left(N_{+}+{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbf{0}}-2\right)\,ds\leq 2\sqrt{N_{+}-2}+2\left(N_{+}-2\right).

Thus, we have:

r^{-4}H_{\mathbf{0}}(r)\leq s^{-4}H_{\mathbf{0}}(s)e^{2\sqrt{N_{+}-2}+2(N_{+}-2)}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.7

(non-degenrate case)
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on
\Omega
, and suppose
B_{r}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
.
Assume that
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
with
y_{0}\geq\delta_{0}
.
Moreover, suppose that
M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0+)=\omega(X_{0})
and that
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)\leq N_{+}
for some
N_{+}>1
.
Then, there is a constant
C=C(N_{+},\delta_{0})
such that for any
s\in[r/2,r]
with
r<\min(y_{0}/128,\delta_{0}/2)
, we have:

\displaystyle C^{-1}s^{-3}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,s)\leq r^{-3}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)\leq Cs^{-3}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,s).

Proof.

We have
{\mathcal{N}}(t)\geq 1-C_{\star}t^{1/2}
according to Lemma
4.3
.
Additionally, we compute the derivative as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(t^{-3}H(t)\right)=

\displaystyle 2t^{-4}H(t)\left({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}}-1\right)-t^{-4}\int_{\partial B_{t}(X_{0})}\dfrac{t-y_{0}}{y^{2}}\psi^{2}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-2C_{\star}t^{-7/2}H(t)-\frac{t^{-3}}{y_{0}-t}H(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-t^{-3}H(t)\left(2C^{\star}t^{-1/2}+\frac{1}{y_{0}-t}\right).

Hence,

\frac{d}{dt}\log(t^{-3}H)\geq-\left(2C^{\star}t^{-1/2}+1/(y_{0}-t)\right),

which establishes the desired lower bound in the lemma.

For the upper bound, we use the estimate in Remark
4.4
:

(y_{0}-r)^{-2}{\mathcal{N}}(r)-(y_{0}-s)^{-2}{\mathcal{N}}(s)\geq\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}}{t(y_{0}-t)^{2}}\ dt-2\left(C(\delta_{0})(r-s)\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}}{t(y_{0}-t)^{2}}\ dt\right)^{1/2}.

Using
{\mathcal{N}}(s)\geq 1-C_{\star}s^{1/2}
, we obtain

\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}}{t(y_{0}-t)^{2}}\ dt\leq C_{1}(N_{+},\delta_{0})(1+r).

Thus,

\int_{s}^{r}\dfrac{{\mathcal{V}}(t)}{t}\,dt\leq\left(\int_{s}^{r}\dfrac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}(t)}{t(y_{0}-t)^{2}}\,dt\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{s}^{r}\dfrac{(y_{0}-t)^{2}}{2t}\,dt\right)^{1/2}\leq C_{2}\sqrt{1+r}.

Now, recalling (
22
), we integrate the following inequlaity

\frac{d}{dt}\log t^{-3}H(t)\leq\frac{2}{t}\left({\mathcal{N}}+{\mathcal{V}}-1\right)+\frac{1}{y_{0}-t}\leq\frac{2}{t}\left(C_{3}({\mathcal{N}}(r)+r)+{\mathcal{V}}\right)+C_{4}

over the interval
[s,r]
. This yields

\log r^{-3}H(r)-\log s^{-3}H(s)\leq 2C_{3}({\mathcal{N}}(r)+r)\ln\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)+C_{4}(r-s)+\int_{s}^{r}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}(t)}{t}\,dt\leq C_{5}(1+r).

Thus, we have:

r^{-3}H(r)\leq s^{-3}H(s)\exp\left(C_{5}(1+r)\right).

∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on
\Omega
, and suppose
B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
.
Assume that
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
with
y_{0}\geq\delta_{0}
.
Moreover, suppose that
M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0+)=\omega(X_{0})
and that
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)\leq N_{+}
for some
N_{+}>1
.
Then, there is a constant
C=C(N_{+},\delta_{0})
such that for every
r<\min(y_{0}/128,\delta_{0}/2)

\sup_{s\in\left[r/2,r\right]}{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,s)\leq C{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r).

An analogous estimate holds when
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
.

Proof.

Note that directly from the definitions,

{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,s)=\frac{s\int_{B_{s}^{+}}y\left(1-I\right)\,dX}{H_{X_{0}}(\psi,s)}\leq\frac{r\int_{B_{r}^{+}}y\left(1-I\right)\,dX}{H_{X_{0}}(\psi,s)}=\frac{H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)}{H_{X_{0}}(\psi,s)}{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r).

By Lemma
4.7
, we have:

\frac{H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)}{H_{X_{0}}(\psi,s)}\leq C\left(\frac{r}{s}\right)^{3}\leq 2^{3}C;

which finishes the proof.
∎

The next lemma essentially asserts that

{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,t)\to 0
as
t\to 0^{+}

at any point
X_{0}
, where
M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})=\omega(X_{0})
.
Notice that the limit
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,0+)
exists due to Lemmas
4.1
and
4.3
, and the condition
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,2t)-{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,t)\to 0
automatically holds as
t\to 0^{+}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on
B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
, and let
t\in\left(0,r/2\right]
.
Assume that
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
with
y_{0}\geq\delta_{0}
, that
M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0+)=\omega(X_{0})
,

{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,2t)-{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,t)<\delta,

and that
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)\leq N_{+}
for some
N_{+}>1
, with
r<\min(y_{0}/128,\delta_{0}/2)
.
Then, there is a constant
C=C(N_{+},\delta_{0})
such that

{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,t)\leq C\sqrt{\delta+t}.

Moreover, in the degenerate case
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
, the estimate reduces to

{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,t)\leq C\sqrt{\delta}.

Proof.

Degenerate Case (
X_{0}\in S_{\psi}
):

From Lemma
4.1
, we have:

\int_{t}^{2t}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}(s)}{s}\,ds\leq\int_{t}^{2t}\frac{d}{ds}{\mathcal{N}}(s)\,ds={\mathcal{N}}(2t)-{\mathcal{N}}(t)<\delta.

Thus, there must be at least one
s\in[t,2t]
such that
{\mathcal{V}}(s)\leq\sqrt{\delta}
.
Applying Lemma
4.8
, we obtain:

{\mathcal{V}}(t)\leq C(N_{+})\sqrt{\delta}.

Non-degenerate Case (
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
):

From the estimate in Remark
4.4
, we have:

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{2t}\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}^{2}}{s(y_{0}-s)^{2}}\ ds\leq

\displaystyle C(\delta_{0})\left({\mathcal{N}}(2t)-{\mathcal{N}}(t)\right)+C(N_{+},\delta_{0})t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(N_{+},\delta_{0})(\delta+t),

Applying again Lemma
4.8
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on
\Omega
, and suppose
B_{r}^{+}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
.
Assume that
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
with
y_{0}\geq\delta_{0}
.
Moreover, suppose that
M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0+)=\omega(X_{0})
and that
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,2r)\leq N_{+}
for some
N_{+}>1
, with
3r<\min(y_{0}/128,\delta_{0}/2)
.
Then, there exists a constant
C=C(N_{+},\delta_{0})
such that for any
Y\in B_{r/4}(X_{0})\cap N_{\psi}
, and
s<r/2
,

{\mathcal{N}}_{Y}(\psi,s)\leq C{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,2r)+Cr\qquad\text{and}\qquad H_{Y}\left(\psi,5r/8\right)\geq CH_{X_{0}}(\psi,r).

Proof.

First, from Lemma
4.7
, we have:

r^{-3}\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\leq C^{2}s^{-3}\int_{\partial B_{s}}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

for any
s\in\left[r/4,r\right]
. Integrating this gives:

\int_{\partial B_{r}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\leq Cr^{-1}\int_{B_{3r/8}(X_{0})\setminus B_{r/4}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\leq Cr^{-1}\int_{B_{3r/8}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Taking any
Y\in B_{r/4}(X_{0})\cap N_{\psi}
, then
\frac{d}{dt}\log\left(t^{-3}H_{Y}(t)\right)\geq-C\left(1+t^{-1/2}\right)
, and one can check that

\int_{B_{5r/8}(Y)}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,dX=\int_{0}^{5r/8}H_{Y}(s)\,ds\leq\int_{0}^{5r/8}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)^{3}\exp(Cr^{1/2})\,ds\times H_{Y}(5r/8)\leq CrH_{Y}(5r/8).

Thus, this gives

\int_{B_{3r/8}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,dX\leq\int_{B_{5r/8}(Y)}\frac{1}{y}\psi^{2}\,dX\leq CrH_{Y}(5r/8).

All these together, we imply that
H_{X_{0}}(r)\leq CH_{Y}\left(5r/8\right)
.

On the other hand, we have the trivial inequality:

\int_{B_{5r/8}(Y)}\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dX\leq\int_{B_{r}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dX,

showing that:

{\mathcal{N}}_{Y}\left(5r/8\right)\leq C\left({\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(r)+{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{0}}(r)\right)\leq C\left({\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(r)+\sqrt{{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(2r)+r}\right),

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
4.9
.
Finally, for some
s<r/2
, it follows from (
22
) that:

\displaystyle 1+{\mathcal{N}}_{Y}(s)\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+Cr\right)\left(1+{\mathcal{N}}_{Y}\left(5r/8\right)\right)\leq\left(1+Cr\right)\left(1+{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(r)+C\sqrt{{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(2r)+r}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+Cr\right)\left(\left(1+Cr\right)\left(1+{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(2r)\right)+C\left({\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(2r)+r\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+Cr\right)\left(1+C\left({\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(2r)+r\right)\right).

Here we used the estimate
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(2r)\geq 1-C_{\star}(2r)^{1/2}>c>0
to deduce that
\sqrt{{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(2r)+r}\leq C({\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(2r)+r)
.
Hence,
{\mathcal{N}}_{Y}(s)\leq C({\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(2r)+r)
.
∎

5.
Analysis of frequency blow-ups

Throughout this section, we analyze a blow-up sequence of a variational solution
(\psi_{k},I_{k})
with
\partial\{\psi>0\}\ni X_{k}\to X_{0}
and

(23)

M_{X_{k}}(\psi,0+)=\omega(X_{k}).

Furthermore, consider a sequence
r_{k}\to 0
and assume
{\mathcal{N}}(2r_{k})\leq N_{+}
for some constant
N_{+}
, as well as

(24)

\delta_{k}:={\mathcal{N}}_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},2r_{k})-{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},r_{k}/2)\to 0,\qquad\text{ as }k\to+\infty.

Besides, we assume that the following limit exists:

(25)

\lim_{k\to+\infty}{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},r_{k})=N_{\infty}.

Given such a sequence, we define the re-normalized sequence

v_{k}(X)=\frac{\psi_{k}(X_{k}+r_{k}X)}{\sqrt{r_{k}^{\mu(X_{k})-2}H_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},r_{k})}},

which satisfies the property

\int_{\partial B_{1}^{+}}\frac{r_{k}^{\mu(X_{k})-2}}{r_{k}^{-1}y_{k}+y}\ v_{k}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=1.

Lemma 5.1

(non-degenerate case)
.

Suppose that
X_{k}\in N_{\psi_{k}}\cap\{y\geq\delta_{0}\}
, for some fixed constant
\delta_{0}>0
, and that assumptions (
23
), (
24
) and (
25
) are satisfied.
A sub-sequence of the re-normalized sequence
v_{k}
converges weakly in
W^{1,2}(B_{1})
and strongly in
L^{2}(B_{1})
and
L^{2}(\partial B_{1})
to a function
v\in W^{1,2}(B_{1})
. The function
v
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

v\geq 0
on
B_{1}
;

(ii)

v
is a subharmonic on
B_{1}
;

(iii)

the restriction of
v
to
B_{1}\setminus B_{1/2}
is homogeneous of order
N_{\infty}\geq 1
;

(iv)

\int_{\partial B_{1}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=y_{0}
.

Proof.

We assume that
r_{k}\leq\delta_{0}/256
to ensure that all results from the previous section can be applied.
By Lemma
4.9
, we know that
{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},r_{k})\to 0
, so

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\frac{1}{y_{k}+r_{k}y}|\nabla v_{k}|^{2}\,dX=\frac{r_{k}\int_{B_{r_{k}}(X_{k})}\frac{1}{y}|\nabla\psi_{k}|^{2}\,dX}{H_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},r_{k})}={\mathcal{N}}_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},r_{k})+{\mathcal{V}}_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},r_{k})\to N_{\infty}.

Given that
\int_{\partial B_{1}}\frac{1}{y_{0}}v_{k}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=1
, this implies that the sequence
v_{k}
is uniformly bounded in
W^{1,2}(B_{1})
.
Therefore, by Rellich’s theorem and the compact embedding on the boundary, we can extract a subsequence that converges weakly in
W^{1,2}(B_{1})
to some
v\in W^{1,2}(B_{1})
and strongly in
L^{2}(B_{1})
and in
L^{2}(\partial B_{1})
. Consequently,
(iv)
holds.

Since
v_{k}\geq 0
, it follows that
v\geq 0
, which proves
(i)
.

Let
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1}^{+})
be any test function with
\eta\geq 0
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\frac{1}{y_{0}}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\eta\,dX=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{1}}\frac{1}{y_{k}+r_{k}y}\nabla v_{k}\cdot\nabla\eta\,dX\leq 0,

since the functions
\psi_{k}
(and thus the functions
v_{k}
) are
\mathcal{L}
-subsolutions, as shown in Proposition
2.2
. Therefore,
v
is also a subharmonic, which proves
(ii)
.

Applying now Remark
4.5
to
\psi_{k}
, and integrating from
r_{k}/2
to
r_{k}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\delta_{k}+O(r_{k}^{1/2})=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r_{k}}\setminus B_{r_{k}/2}}\frac{|X|^{-1}}{H_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},|X|)}\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi_{k}\cdot(X-X_{k})-\psi_{k}{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},|X|)\right|^{2}\,dX

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{1/2}}\frac{|X|^{-1}}{y_{k}+r_{k}y}\left|\nabla v_{k}\cdot X-v_{k}{\mathcal{N}}(\psi_{k},r_{k}|X|)\right|^{2}\,dX\to 0.

Since
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},r_{k}|X|)\to N_{\infty}
uniformly in
|X|
and
v_{k}
is bounded in
W^{1,2}(B_{1})
, we have:

\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{1/2}}\left|\nabla v_{k}\cdot X-v_{k}N_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,dX\to 0.

The functional

w\mapsto\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{1/2}}\left|\nabla w\cdot X-wN_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,dX,

is convex, and thus weakly lower semi-continuous under the weak convergence in
W^{1,2}
.
This leads to the following inequality:

\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{1/2}}\left|\nabla v\cdot X-vN_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,dX\leq\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{1/2}}\left|\nabla v_{k}\cdot X-v_{k}N_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,dX=0,

which implies that

\nabla v\cdot X-vN_{\infty}=0,\qquad\text{almost everywhere on }B_{1}\setminus B_{1/2}.

This condition is equivalent to the order
N_{\infty}
homogeneity, i.e.,
v(rX)=r^{N_{\infty}}v(X)
almost everywhere.
Notice that the estimate
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{k}}(\psi_{k},r_{k})\geq 1-C_{\star}r_{k}^{1/2}
implies that
N_{\infty}\geq 1
.
This completes the proof of
(iii)
.
∎

6.
Rectifiability of free boundary

In this section, we apply a quantitative method from geometric measure theory, pioneered by Naber and Valtorta in
[
29
]
, to establish the rectifiability of the free boundary.
This approach relies on an (almost) monotone quantity, which in our context is the Almgren frequency function,
{\mathcal{N}}
.
To carry out this analysis, we restrict attention to the subset
N_{\psi}^{t}:=N_{\psi}\cap\{y>t\}
, for a fixed constant
t>0
, and prove the rectifiability of this set.

For a variational solution
(\psi,I)
in
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we define the set of highest-density points as

(27)

\Sigma_{H}:=\left\{X_{0}\in\Omega\,:\,X_{0}\in N_{\psi}\,\text{and}\,M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})=\omega(X_{0})\right\}.

Recall from Proposition
3.5
that
\omega(X_{0})
is the maximal possible value that
M_{X_{0}}(0^{+})
may attain at
X_{0}
, and from Lemma
4.3
, the frequency function
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(r)
is almost non-decreasing and satisfies
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})\geq 1
.

It will be convenient to also define:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{H}^{t}=\left\{X_{0}\in\Omega\,:\,B_{t}(X_{0})\Subset\Omega,X_{0}\in N_{\psi},\,y_{0}>t\,\text{ and }\,M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})=\omega(X_{0})\right\}.

The sets
\Sigma_{H}^{t}
are relatively closed in
\Omega_{t}=\{X_{0}\in\Omega:B_{t}^{+}(X_{0})\Subset\Omega\}
.

We will show
\Sigma_{H}^{t}
is rectifiable for every fix

, which in turn implies the rectifiability of
\Sigma_{H}
.
The proof follows from the next two lemmas.

Lemma 6.1

(Quantitative splitting)
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on
\Omega
. For any
\rho,\gamma,N_{+}>0
, there exists

\{Z\in B_{r}(X_{0}):{\mathcal{N}}_{Z}(3\rho r)\geq N^{\mathrm{max}}-\eta\}\subset B_{\rho r}(Y).

Definition 6.1

Let

Proof.

As discussed at the beginning of this section, we have that
I=\chi_{\{\psi>0\}}
on
\Omega\setminus\partial\{\psi>0\}
, and on
N_{\psi}
, we have
M_{X_{0}}(0^{+})\in[\omega(X_{0})/2,\omega(X_{0}))
, due to Lemma
7.1
.
In particular, on
N_{\psi}
, the Lebesgue density of
\{I=1\}
lies in
[1/2,1)
, so
N_{\psi}
is contained in the essential boundary
\partial^{e}\{I=1\}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle M_{X_{0}}(0^{+})=

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left|\{I=1\}\cap B_{s}(X_{0})\right|_{{\mathrm{w}}}}{s^{2}}=\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{1}{s^{2}}\int_{B_{s}(X_{0})}yI\,dX

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{s+y_{0}}{s^{2}}\int_{B_{s}(X_{0})}I\,dX=y_{0}\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left|\{I=1\}\cap B_{s}(X_{0})\right|}{s^{2}}.

From Lemma
2.4
, this implies that

\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(B^{+}_{r}(X_{0})\cap N_{\psi}\right)\leq\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(B^{+}_{r}(X_{0})\cap\partial^{e}\{I=1\}\right)=\int_{B^{+}_{r}(X_{0})}\left|\nabla\left(yI-y\right)\right|\leq C(C_{V})r+r^{2}

when
B^{+}_{2r}(X_{0})\Subset\Omega
.
This implies that
|N_{\psi}|=0
,
I=\chi_{\{\psi>0\}}
Lebesgue-a.e., and that
\{\psi>0\}
has finite perimeter.
∎

To proceed, it will be useful to introduce the notion of a viscosity solution for our problem.

Definition 7.1

(Viscosity solution)
.

Let
\psi
be a continuous function
\psi:\Omega\to[0,+\infty)
that is an
\mathcal{L}
-solution in
\{\psi>0\}
. Then,
\psi
is a viscosity solution if, for any smooth function
\phi
satisfying either
\phi\leq\psi
or
\phi_{+}\geq\psi
in
B^{+}_{r}(X_{0})\subseteq\Omega
, with
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
and
\phi(X_{0})=0
, the following conditions hold:

(i)

\phi\leq\psi
implies
|\nabla\phi(X_{0})|\leq y_{0}
.

(ii)

\phi_{+}\geq\psi
implies
|\nabla\phi(X_{0})|\geq y_{0}
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on
\Omega
, and assume
\Sigma_{H}=\emptyset
. Then,
\psi
is a viscosity
solution on
\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\leq\psi
(or
\phi_{+}\geq\psi
) in
B^{+}_{r}(X_{0})
, where
\phi(X_{0})=\psi(X_{0})=0
and
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that if the first inequality holds, then
|\nabla\phi(X_{0})|\neq 0
.
The condition
X_{0}\in N_{\psi}
ensures, by Lemma
7.1
, that
M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})\geq\omega(X_{0})/2
.
However, since
\Sigma_{H}=\emptyset
, it follows that
M_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})<\omega(X_{0})
.
Then by Proposition
3.3
, along a sequence
s_{k}
, we obtain

\psi_{k}(X)=\frac{\psi(X_{0}+s_{k}X)}{s_{k}}\;\to\;\psi_{0}(X)=y_{0}(X\cdot\nu)_{+}

locally uniformly and strongly in
W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1,2}
, where
\nu
is a unit vector.
On the other hand, we have

(44)

\lim_{s_{k}\to 0^{+}}\frac{\phi(X_{0}+s_{k}X)}{s_{k}}=\nabla\phi(X_{0})\cdot X.

By the homogeneity of
\psi_{0}
, it follows that
\nabla\phi(X_{0})\cdot X\leq\psi_{0}(X)
(or
(\nabla\phi(X_{0})\cdot X)_{+}\geq\psi_{0}(X)
).
Therefore,
\nabla\phi(X_{0})
is parallel to
\nu
and
|\nabla\phi(X_{0})|\leq y_{0}
(or
|\nabla\phi(X_{0})|\geq y_{0}
).
∎

Corollary 7.4
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on a connected open set
\Omega
, and assume that
\Sigma_{H}=\emptyset
.
Then, the reduced boundary
\partial^{\star}\{\psi>0\}
is identical to
\partial\{\psi>0\}
and is locally represented by graphs of analytic functions, along which
|\nabla\psi|=y
from the
\{\psi>0\}
side.
Moreover,
\psi
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\psi=\mathcal{H}^{1}\llcorner\partial^{\star}\{\psi>0\},

in the sense of distributions.

Proof.

First note that by
\epsilon
-flatness the free boundary of a viscosity solution
\psi

in
B^{+}_{1}
in the sense

\displaystyle\left(x-\epsilon\right)^{+}\leq\psi(X)\leq(x+\epsilon)^{+}

implies the free boundary to be
C^{1,\alpha}
in
B^{+}_{1/2}
; see e.g. the developed theory in
[
15
]
or
[
17
,
18
]
.
This, in turn, implies that the reduced boundary
\partial^{\star}\{\psi>0\}
, which coincides with the topological free boundary
\partial\{\psi>0\}
, is locally given by the graph of an analytic function, according to well-established theory (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
).
Next we observe that by the assumption
\Sigma_{H}=\emptyset
as well as Lemma
7.1
, a limit of
the blow-up family
\psi_{r}(X)=\psi(X_{0}+rX)/r
as a sequence
r\to 0
at free boundary points
X_{0}
cannot be zero.
Therefore, by Proposition
3.3
we obtain the blowup solution must be
\psi_{0}(X)=y_{0}(X\cdot\nu)_{+}
for some unit vector
\nu
.
Therefore, for sufficiently small
r
, the
\epsilon
-flatness condition holds for
\psi_{r}
.
∎

7.2.
Analysis of singular points

The analysis of regular points does not rely on Theorem
6.4
. However, in the presence of singular points, the absence of this theorem leads to significant challenges: the set
\Sigma_{H}
, and consequently the support of
\mathcal{L}\psi
, could be large and exhibit a highly irregular structure. Theorem
6.4
resolves the most major irregularities, allowing for more accurate characterization of
\mathcal{L}\psi
and
I
.

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution in
\Omega
. Then, for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
X_{0}\in\Sigma_{H}
, the following hold:

(1)

The renormalized functions

\displaystyle v_{X_{0},r}(X)=\frac{\psi(X_{0}+rX)}{\sqrt{r^{-1}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r)}}\to\alpha(X_{0})|X\cdot\nu|,

converge strongly in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}},\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, where
\nu
is a unit vector.

(2)

The Lebesgue density of
\{\psi>0\}
at
X_{0}
exists and equals
1
:

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\left|B_{r}(X_{0})\cap\{\psi>0\}\right|}{\left|B_{r}(X_{0})\right|}=1.

Proof.

From Theorem
6.4
, for
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
X_{0}\in\Sigma_{H}
, we have
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{0}}(\psi,0^{+})=1
, and
\Sigma_{H}
possesses an approximate tangent. That is, the measures

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{X_{0},r}(E)=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\left(X_{0}+rE\right)\cap\Sigma_{H}\right)}{r^{2}}

converge in the weak-* sense to
\mathcal{H}^{1}\llcorner L
for some line
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
passing through the origin.

We now show that at any such point
X_{0}
, both
(1)
and
(2)
hold.
By the continuity of the mapping
X\mapsto{\mathcal{N}}_{X}(\psi,r)
for each small positive
r
, we may assume that for every
\eta>0
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that

{\mathcal{N}}_{X}(\psi,r)\leq 1+\eta\qquad\text{for}\quad X\in B_{\delta}\cap\Sigma_{H}\text{ and }r<\delta.

Given any sequence
r_{k}\to 0
, consider the renormalized sequence

\displaystyle v_{k}(X)=\frac{\psi(X_{0}+r_{k}X)}{\sqrt{r_{k}^{-1}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r_{k})}}.

By Lemma
5.1
, the sequence
v_{k}
converges weakly to
v
in
W^{1,2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(B_{R})
, and strongly in
L^{2}_{{\mathrm{w}}}(B_{R})
, as well as Lebesgue-a.e., as
k\to+\infty
for each
R>0
(after passing to a subsequence if necessary).

From this, it follows that
\chi_{\{v>0\}}\leq\liminf_{k\to+\infty}\chi_{\{v_{k}>0\}}
Lebesgue-a.e. Then, applying Fatou’s lemma, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\left|B_{1}\cap\{v>0\}\right|}{|B_{1}|}\leq\liminf_{k\to+\infty}\frac{\left|B_{1}\cap\{v_{k}>0\}\right|}{|B_{1}|}=\liminf_{k\to+\infty}\frac{\left|B_{r_{k}}(X_{0})\cap\{\psi>0\}\right|}{|B_{r_{k}}(X_{0})|}.

Since
|X\cdot\nu|>0
Lebesgue-a.e., property
(2)
follows directly from
(1)
.

Due to the existence of an approximate tangent for
\boldsymbol{\nu}
, for any
B_{\tau}(X)
with
X\in L
, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to+\infty}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{X_{0},r_{k}}(B_{\tau}(X))\geq\mathcal{H}^{1}(B_{\tau}(X)\cap L)\geq 2\tau.

In particular, for sufficiently large
k
, the set
B_{\tau r_{k}}(X_{0}+r_{k}X)\cap\Sigma_{H}
is non-empty.
Choosing the unit vector
\nu
such that
L=\{X\cdot\nu=0\}
and let
\boldsymbol{n}
be the unit vector perpendicular to
\nu
.
We can find points
X_{k}\in B_{r_{k}/40}(X_{0}+r_{k}\boldsymbol{n})\cap\Sigma_{H}
; let
X_{k}:=X_{0}+r_{k}\tilde{X}_{k}
.

Using Lemmas
4.7
and
4.10
, we know that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r_{k})\leq R^{-3}r_{k}^{3}H_{X_{k}}(\psi,Rr_{k})\leq CH_{X_{0}}(\psi,r_{k}),

for every
R\in[1/40,40]
and for some constant
C
independent of
r_{k}
and
X_{k}
. Then, from the formulas in Remark
4.5
and Remark
4.4
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{r_{k}/4}^{4r_{k}}\int_{\partial B_{s}(X_{k})}

\displaystyle\frac{2s^{-1}}{H_{X_{0}}(\psi,r_{k})}\frac{1}{y}\left|\nabla\psi(X)\cdot(X-X_{k})-{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{k}}(\psi,s)\psi(X)\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq(y_{k}-4r_{k})^{-2}{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{k}}(\psi,4r_{k})-(y_{k}-r_{k}/4)^{-2}{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{k}}(\psi,r_{k}/4)+Cr_{k}^{1/2}\leq C\eta+Cr_{k}^{1/2},

using
{\mathcal{N}}_{X_{k}}(r)\in[1-C_{\star}r^{1/2},1+\eta]
for sufficiently large
k
. Applying this bound again, we find

\displaystyle\int_{B_{4}(\tilde{X}_{k})\setminus B_{1/4}(\tilde{X}_{k})}\frac{1}{y_{0}+r_{k}y}\left|\nabla v_{k}(X)\cdot(X-\tilde{X}_{k})-v_{k}(X)\right|^{2}\,dX\leq C\eta+Cr_{k}^{1/2}.

The same estimate holds at
X_{0}
:

\displaystyle\int_{B_{4}\setminus B_{1/4}}\frac{1}{y_{0}+r_{k}y}\left|\nabla v_{k}(X)\cdot X-v_{k}(X)\right|^{2}\,dX\leq C\eta+Cr_{k}^{1/2}.

Applying Lemma
6.5
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B^{+}_{2}(X_{0})\setminus B^{+}_{1/2}(X_{0})}\frac{1}{y_{0}+r_{k}y}\left|\nabla v_{k}(X)\cdot\tilde{X}_{k}\right|^{2}\,dX\leq C\eta+Cr_{k}^{1/2}.

This implies

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2}\setminus B_{1/2}}\frac{1}{y_{0}+r_{k}y}\left|\nabla v_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\right|^{2}\,dX\leq C\eta+Cr_{k}^{1/2},

due to the choice of
X_{k}
.
Since
\eta
was arbitrary, we conclude

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{B_{2}\setminus B_{1/2}}\frac{1}{y_{0}+r_{k}y}\left|\nabla v_{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\right|^{2}\,dX=0.

Applying Theorem
5.3
, we deduce that
v(X)=\alpha(X_{0})|X\cdot\nu|
on
B_{1}\setminus\overline{B_{1/2}}
.
A rescaled version of the same argument extends this conclusion to any annulus
B_{R}\setminus\overline{B_{R/2}}
, and thus to all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

To establish the strong convergence
v_{k}\to v
, consider
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\int\frac{\eta}{y_{0}}|\nabla v|^{2}\,dX

\displaystyle=-2\int\frac{v}{y_{0}}\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla v\,dX=\lim_{k\to+\infty}-2\int\frac{v_{k}}{y_{0}+r_{k}y}\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla v_{k}\,dX=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int\frac{\eta}{y_{0}+r_{k}y}|\nabla v_{k}|^{2}\,dX,

where the first and last terms follow from the fact that
\Delta v=0
in
\{v>0\}
, and that each
v_{k}
satisfies

\mathrm{div\,}\left(\frac{1}{y_{0}+r_{k}y}\nabla v_{k}\right)=0
in
\{v_{k}>0\}
.
Since the limit function
\alpha(X_{0})|X\cdot\nu|
is independent of the chosen subsequence, we conclude that
(1)
holds.
∎

7.3.
Identification of the limit
I

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution in an open and connected domain
\Omega
. Then, either
\psi\equiv 0
or
I=\chi_{\{\psi>0\}}
Lebesgue-a.e.

Proof.

From Lemma
7.2
and Theorem
6.4
, we obtain that
|\partial\{\psi>0\}|=0
. Moreover, by definition, on
\{\psi>0\}
we have
I(X)=1=\chi_{\{\psi>0\}}(X)
Lebesgue-a.e. Therefore, it suffices to show that
I=0
Lebesgue-a.e. in the interior
\{\psi=0\}^{\circ}
.

Consider a non-empty connected component
U
of
\{\psi=0\}^{\circ}
on which
I=1
, recalling that
I
is locally constant on
\{\psi=0\}^{\circ}
. Clearly, we have
\partial U\cap\Omega\subseteq\partial\{\psi>0\}
. For any
X_{0}\in\partial U
, it follows from Proposition
3.5
and the upper semi-continuity of
X_{0}\mapsto M_{X_{0}}(0^{+})
that

M_{X_{0}}(0^{+})\geq\limsup_{U\ni X\to X_{0}}M_{X}(0^{+})=\omega(X_{0}).

In particular, we have
\partial U\cap\Omega\subseteq\Sigma_{H}
.
By applying Theorem
6.4
, we conclude that
\partial U\cap\Omega
has locally finite Hausdorff measure, implying that
U
has locally finite perimeter.

Assume that
U\neq\Omega
. Then, there exists a smooth, connected, open set
\Omega^{\prime}\Subset\Omega
such that
|U\cap\Omega^{\prime}|>0
and
|\Omega^{\prime}\setminus U|>0
.
By the relative isoperimetric inequality, we obtain
\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\partial^{\star}U\cap\Omega^{\prime}\right)>0
, where
\partial^{\star}U
denotes the reduced boundary.
At each point
X_{0}\in\partial^{\star}U
, the set
U
has Lebesgue density
1/2
, so
\{\psi>0\}\subseteq\Omega\setminus U
can have at most Lebesgue density
1/2
at point
X_{0}
.
On the other hand, by Lemma
7.5
, the Lebesgue density of
\{\psi>0\}
at
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e. point in
\Sigma_{H}
, which contains
\partial^{\star}U\subseteq\partial U
, is
1
. This leads to a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
(\psi,I)
be a variational solution on
\Omega
. Then, the set
\partial\{\psi>0\}
is countably
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-rectifiable, has locally finite
\mathcal{H}^{1}

Proof.

The measure
\mathcal{L}\psi
is supported on
\partial\{\psi>0\}
, and applying Corollary
7.4
to
\psi
on the open set
\Omega\setminus\Sigma_{H}
, yields:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\psi\llcorner(\Omega\setminus\Sigma_{H})=\mathcal{H}^{1}\llcorner\partial^{\star}\{\psi>0\}.

Additionally, from Lemma
2.4
, we have the estimate:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\psi\left(B^{+}_{r}(X_{0})\right)\leq C(C_{V})r

when
B^{+}_{2r}(X_{0})\Subset\Omega
. This implies that:

\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\left(\partial\{\psi>0\}\setminus\Sigma_{H}\right)\cap\Omega^{\prime}\right)=\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\partial^{\star}\{\psi>0\}\cap\Omega^{\prime}\right)<+\infty,

for any
\Omega^{\prime}\Subset\Omega

6.4
, we have
\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\Omega^{\prime}\cap\Sigma_{H}\right)<+\infty

Moreover, since
\Sigma_{H}
is countably
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-rectifiable, it follows that
\partial\{\psi>0\}
is also countably
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-rectifiable.

By
[
21

and the Radon-Nikodym theorem, if we define the density function

\displaystyle\theta(X_{0})=\limsup_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{L}\psi\left(B_{r}(X_{0})\right)}{2r}\leq C(C_{V}),
