Interpol review of gunshot residue, 2022 to 2024
Authors: Sébastien Charles, Nadia Geusens, Clémence Tarenne, Florian Vanneste, Bart Nys
Published: 2026

Introduction

This review paper covers advances in scientific methods applied to Gunshot Residues analysis reported since the 20th Interpol Forensic Science Symposium in March 2021, when the same type of review was carried out, leading to publication in 2023 [1]. A literature search was conducted covering articles published in the main analytical and forensic journals in 2022, 2023 and 2024. The terms searched in the titles, abstracts and keywords were “GSR”, “gunshot residue”, “shooting distance”, “firing distance” and “muzzle-to-target distance”. A total of 104 publications were identified, of which 86 were selected for inclusion in this review.

When a firearm is discharged, primer and gunpowder residues, as well as metal particles from the projectile and cartridge case, are expelled through the muzzle of the barrel and other openings in the firearm. These residues are called primer residue, firearm discharge residue or gunshot residue (GSR).

Throughout the text, this last name and acronym will be used, as this appears to be the most commonly used designator in the literature. The gunshot residue can be divided widely as particulate material comprised of metallic elements (Inorganic GSR or IGSR) and compounds comprised of organic materials (Organic GSR or OGSR). With the advent of new legislature banning (heavy) metal elements from ammunition (because of health and environmental concerns), the ammunition industry is looking more and more to formulate heavy-metal-free primers, spurring a growing interest in the development of analysis methods for organic compounds in the forensic domain over the last few years.

Meanwhile, Scanning Electron Microscopy coupled with energy dispersive X-ray microanalysis (SEM/EDS) has been and remains to be the method of choice for the identification of inorganic GSR (IGSR) on samples since the eighties. This technique is well suited to the detection and characterisation of small particles (down to 0.5 μm) containing heavy metals such as lead, barium and antimony from primers of conventional composition (e.g. sinoxid primers). In addition, it allows the determination of the correlation between the morphology and chemical composition of individual particles. According to the relevant ASTM norm E1588-20, particles ‘showing a spheroidal morphology and composed of lead, barium and antimony’ are considered to be ‘characteristic’ of ‘classic’ GSR. However, spectrometric techniques such as Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (AAS) or Optical Emission Spectrometry (OES) are still used in some forensic laboratories because of their high sensitivity, speed and ease of use, despite the fact that morphological information of the particles is in these cases not provided and the analysis is destructive to the sample.

The steady increase in the number of scientific publications in the forensic field was the subject of a study published in 2023, with a particular focus on GSR [2]. Notably, half of such forensic-related publications come from non-forensic sources, indicating that research is often conducted in disciplines other than forensic science. In addition, this information is increasingly diluted with the advent of new digital journals, sometimes limited to a single (forensic) field. The number of reviews is also on the increase, but they are often limited to listing and describing published articles, without providing a critical view of their quality or even added value. For over a decade, forensic science has faced various challenges, and this paper also evaluates the role of research and its dissemination in addressing these issues. For instance, forensic research has focused mainly on methods and technology rather than practical application, which often results in proofs of concept that are not developed further and therefore have limited day-to-day application in the laboratory or in the field. The authors propose refocusing forensic research on fundamental and practical questions to enhance its effectiveness in real-world crime investigation. An initiative generally known as the ‘Sydney Declaration’, launched by prominent academic and practicing forensic scientists active in several forensic domains, has formulated this viewpoint on forensic science by proposing a number of standpoints which should guide the thinking of forensic science practitioners of all levels forward towards more scientifically solid ground in the future. These suggestions aim to promote discussions and establish a common knowledge base in the forensic science community, focusing on concepts such as the exploitation of trace materials and their relevance in specific criminal cases. A similar topic is discussed in the review by Bailey et al. [3] about the use of surface analysis techniques in forensic science. The authors mention that numerous studies have led to many proposed applications but only to a few routine applications of surface analysis techniques in the world of forensic science. As in the article of Weyermann et al. [2], one of the reasons for this limited adoption of new techniques is said to be a lack of understanding of forensic science by the non-forensic researchers within the analytical community. This review gives an overview of routinely used and promising surface analysis techniques within several forensic domains, i.e., fingerprints, hair, forensic medicine, drugs, explosives and microtraces (GSR, paint and glass). Looking specifically at the field of GSR analysis, no less than ten surface analysis techniques are discussed next to SEM/EDS, which has been the gold standard for decades now.

As described in the review by Ahuja et al. [4], the development of analytical techniques for detecting GSR has continued to evolve over time. The authors start by examining the initial techniques employed in the 1970s, which primarily involved colorimetric tests and Atomic Absorption Spectrometry. More advanced methods, still in use today, were then developed, notably SEM/EDX and the Chromatographic and Mass Spectrometric techniques. Even more innovative advances have since emerged in the field, such as LA-ICP-MS and organic GSR analysis. The review also includes important topics such as GSR persistence, challenges related to shooting distance determination, and issues arising due to the introduction of lead-free ammunitions. It also covers future aspects such as the introduction of portable devices for on-the-scene use and AI applications for investigations in forensics.

The manuscript by Familia et al. [5] reviews the analytical methods currently used for detecting, characterising and identifying the components of the primer and gunpowder. In the light of the evolution to remove heavy metals from ammunition, there has been a trend of focusing more on the detection of OGSR. In fact, researchers evaluate it more opportune to combine IGSR detection with OGSR detection by either a combination of two techniques such as SEM/EDX with chromatographic or spectroscopic analysis, or by a single technique to detect both IGSR and OGSR such as Raman or FTIR. SPME followed by GC-MS is one of the most promising methodologies for achieving standardisation for OGSR analysis. Chemometrics is a required and suitable method for data analysis. It includes pattern recognition and regression methods such as HCA (Hierarchical Clustering Analysis), PCA (Principal Components Analysis), PLS (Partial Least Squares), PLS-DA (PLS associated with Discriminant Analysis), KNN (K-Nearest Neighbour), MARS (Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines), RF (Random Forests), ANN (Artificial Neural Networks) and SVM (Support Vector Machines).

Inorganic GSR

Fundamentals of GSR formation

On the basis of shots fired with a 9 mm pistol and different brands of ammunition, Kara examined in Ref. [6] the particle size distribution of the GSR particles on the hands of shooters, showing that this distribution follows a Boltzmann-type distribution. This implies that particle dimensions depend on splitting-merging processes, but also on external factors (collisions among GSR particles and mechanical system) causing fractures and fragmentation.

In [7], examining various ballistic elements, from the cartridge case to the weapon and the particles deposited on the targets, Burnett and Nunziata investigated and characterised in-depth the memory effect when using .22 calibre pistols and revolvers, mainly by SEM/EDX analysis. Different types of ammunition were investigated. The various phenomena, both physical and thermodynamic, were addressed in order to provide an understanding of the weapon's memory effect. In the end, many parameters influence the weapon's memory effect, such as the design of the firearm, the composition of the primer and the projectile's surface, as well as the temperature and pressure conditions during the firing process.

Guo et al. examined in Ref. [8] the possibility of discriminating between casings on the basis of lead isotope ratios. This would be of interest when ballistic marks are unusable, or even if the study were to be extended to compare ballistic elements of a different nature (cartridge case vs. GSR found on POI). One of the first things the authors checked was that this ratio was not affected by the use of the ammunition, i.e. that the ratio remained stable between the unfired cartridge and the fired cartridge; this is indeed what was observed. The authors then examined around forty cartridge cases for three lead isotope ratios. They conclude that their method is feasible, with possible differentiation between ammunition on the basis of the combination of calculations of the three ratios.

Nunziata et al. [9] conducted a study on gunshot residues from primers containing mercury, a component that was once common in ammunition but is now increasingly rare. It has been reported that the residues from mercury primers have not been the subject of extensive study in the past. This is due to the gradual replacement of mercuric compounds by other formulations that are safer and less toxic. The current ASTM standard procedure for gunshot residue analysis does not make any mention of GSR particles originating from mercuric primers. However, knowledge of the specific characteristics of these GSRs remains important in forensic contexts, particularly in cases involving old or specific ammunitions that are still fitted with mercuric primers. The study focuses on the structural segregation of chemical elements within these residues, especially how mercury, lead, barium, and antimony are distributed in a single GSR particle. The research is carried out by employing a combination of a highly powerful scanning electron microscope and X-ray spectroscopy that in itself allows one to perform an extremely detailed examination of GSR particles. The results show that the chemical composition of the residue is highly inhomogeneous and that a segregation of the studied elements is noticeable, which could influence the interpretation in forensic investigations. The authors propose a classification of mercury-containing particles and discuss the thermodynamic and chemical phenomena occurring during detonation that may influence the observed segregation. They also suggest that further research is needed to refine these models and thus gain a better understanding of the underlying mechanisms governing the formation and distribution of (mercuric) GSR particles.

In another study [10], Nunziata et al. describes iron-containing GSR particles from a physiochemical point of view. The erosion and corrosion mechanism during GSR formation and the use of firearms with rusty barrels is studied and compared with iron oxide particles not originating from the firing. Based on the boiling points of iron oxides compared to muzzle flash temperatures (close to melting point), an iron vaporization phenomenon can be excluded in the GSR formation process. Instead, they are formed by collision and uptake of iron particles by semisolid GSR during their solidification. Three weapons with rusted barrels were used: a Belgian revolver cal 320 (.32 ACP); a pistol UNIQUE cal. 7.65 mm and a pistol Beretta 9x19 mm using Fiocchi ammunition. GSR from the spent cartridge cases and from the barrels was collected and analysed with SEM/EDX.

The aim of the study by Valderez Ponte Rocha et al. [11] is to determine the presence as well as to evaluate the health and environmental impacts of chemical elements from firearm shots during shooter practice at outdoor shooting ranges, both in the environment and on the shooters’ hands. A .40 Taurus PT100 pistol was used with CBC NTA Clean Range® ammunition (Pb and Ba free). Air borne GSR samples were collected with a Mini Partisol 2100, using Whatman Glass GF/C microfiber (1.2 μm pore size). Right hand samples were collected after 10 shots, and analysed using SEM/EDX. After concentration of the atmospheric metals they were analysed with: F-AAS for Ba–Ca–Fe–Mg–K; a GF-AAS for Al–Sb–As–Cd–Cu–Pb–Mn–Ni–Zn; and the DMA-80 mercury determination system for Hg. The results showed airborne GSR elements containing higher quantities of Zn–Cu–Pb–Ni. The main element detected on the hands is zinc. The maximum concentration of lead detected was 0.2 μg/m3, which remains within the permissible exposure limits of 50 μg/m3 according to the occupational Health & Safety Administration.

The memory effect of firearms, i.e. their ability to release inorganic gunshot residue (IGSR) even after cleaning, was examined by Merli et al. [12]. The results of this article demonstrate and reiterate the fact that current cleaning procedures are often ineffective in completely removing these residues. The study also reveals that all internal components of the gun, not just the barrel, contribute to this memory effect. Old residues can also be recovered from the shooter's hands, complicating GSR analysis. Another approach to diminishing the memory effect involves the conditioning of the firearm through the discharge of a batch of ammunition with an alternative primer composition. In this study, this approach was tested using new pistols and various types of ammunition, including lead-based, lead-free, and heavy metal-free primers. The samples were then analysed by SEM/EDX and/or ICP-OES. The study showed that after discharging several rounds of the ‘new’ ammunition, the number of old residues ejected from the gun muzzle decreased, but some ‘old’ particles could still be recovered and detected by SEM/EDX. This method also showed that this tendency was not observed for the shooter's hand, as old residues could still be found in significant amounts, even after 100 new rounds had been discharged. Although this method appears to be more efficient than conventional cleaning techniques, the question of how to eliminate completely the memory effect therefore still remains unanswered.

Sampling

The covid19 pandemic led to the widespread use of surgical masks, which were compulsory to wear and at the same time enabled criminals to conceal their faces more easily. Yildirim et al. therefore investigated the possibility of sampling these masks for IGSR [13]. Various experiments were carried out in indoor and outdoor conditions using a 9 mm pistol. The authors also took the opportunity to evaluate the results for both the shooter and a bystander. In the end, the masks appear to be interesting objects to collect, insofar as a significant number of IGSR particles were collected in each test. Of course, it was the exposed face of surgical masks that was the most contaminated with IGSR, but the inner surfaces were also contaminated, probably due to air flowing through the side of the mask. It is also to be expected that indoor conditions are more favourable to the deposition of IGSR due to reduced air movement. Finally, the authors claim that it is possible to differentiate between a shooter and a bystander on the basis of the quantity of IGSR particles found; however, this claim is only valid under controlled conditions, and function of the type of weapon used in the study; and a comparison of the results is necessary because the bystander is still contaminated to a significant degree.

In multi-disciplinary sampling of clothing involving GSR and DNA analysis, it is often necessary to focus on specific areas for each forensic evidence type, as the analysis of one trace is likely to influence the analysis of the other. Stamouli et al. examined the possibility of taking a single type of sample, which was analysed first for GSR and then for DNA [14]. Sampling was done using so-called DNA-stubs. Authors showed that this could be achieved, with no substantive change to the final results, although the preparation protocol would have to be modified slightly: i.e. the coating of the sample with a thicker carbon layer to prevent any electronic charging was necessary. The issue of DNA contamination via the SEM environment was also considered: a few precautions during the introduction and the extraction of the sample can be taken to avoid this risk of contamination. This method has since also been applied by the authors in real cases.

The sampling of GSR can be strongly affected by the presence of blood on the sampled areas, which can (partially) mask their presence. In an attempt to remedy this problem, Rotter et al. treated the surface of the stubs with a solution based on sodium hypochlorite and calcium chloride [15]. By applying this solution, the blood crusts were totally or partially removed, sometimes revealing small underlying GSR particles. However, the authors noted an alteration in the composition and morphology of the particles, which leads to the conclusion that these samples should be treated with caution, as the treatment is partially destructive. In the end, this treatment is not necessarily to be preferred, with the authors now examining possibilities of physical treatment of the stubs instead for the future.

Husak has successfully applied a non-destructive visualization technique for detecting GSR using a forensic light source on shooters' hands [16]. This procedure constitutes a reliable pre-screening method for GSR on the crime scene, with a confirmation rate of 89% by SEM/EDX. Particles fluoresce at 520 nm (orange filter) with, among others, the Spex Forensics Mini-CrimeScope light source; and under IR at 850 nm they appear as black particles using the UltraFire A100-IR 850 nm Light Torch. Images were taken with a Nikon D7200 DSLR camera and a Fujifilm XT-1 IR mirrorless full spectrum camera in black and white, with a Tiffen #87 IR filter (images were later enhanced in Adobe Lightroom). In the shooting experiments, American Eagle ammunition 9 mm, .40 cal and .45 cal was used by police officers while performing their annual firearm qualifications test. The fluorescent particles were collected with SEM stubs for confirmation, and a number of volunteer's hands were treated with a presumptive chemical test that turns blue after reaction with nitrates (BlueClue Gunpowder Particle Test). The author suggests that, as the equipment used in this study is readily available to CSI teams on crime scenes, a presumptive testing of GSR contamination on suspects' hands can be performed, making GSR sampling more efficient. This technology could also be used by legal medicine examiners during autopsy procedures.

Parmar et al. [17], compare different collection methods of GSR residues: barrel wash, hand swab, and samples taken off paper targets at a shooting distance of 1 m under angles of 40° and 90°. IGSR was measured with EDX-RF, and OGSR was analysed with MS. Weapons used in the tests were AK47 7.62x39 mm, SLR 7.62x51 mm and Carabine rifle 9x19 mm. The barrel wash was performed with a Flannel gun clean cotton cloth, the hand was swabbed with cotton swab/acetone, and the GSR at 1 m distance was collected from A4 sheets of paper. According to this study, the best collection method turns out to be the barrel wash.

Non-GSR sources of GSR-like particles

To identify the source of specific particles, Bilbrey et al. [18] used a Few-Shot Learning (FSL) approach to define a multimodal similarity measure between particles examined by automated particle analysis (APA). In particular, the model is used here to differentiate firearm discharge from firework residue and automobile brake dust, which may have a similar composition and morphology. The analysis workflow of EDX spectra combined with SEM images consists of comparing all particles in a sample against one another. More specifically, particles are grouped into pairs and the FSL network aims to predict similarity scores between particle pairs. The set of similarity scores obtained is then mathematically represented as a similarity graph. To ensure that the addition of SEM and EDX data to the FSL network would improve the characterisation of the particles analysed, several sets were tested in two different networks: one containing only EDX spectra and the other using a combination of EDX spectra and SEM images. Finally, the results of this study showed that the method used was capable of discriminating samples obtained from firearm discharges from samples obtained from fireworks residue or automobile brake dust.

Prevalence, persistence and contamination studies

A large-scale study of the prevalence of both IGSR and OGSR was carried out by Menking-Hoggatt et al. [19] on thousands of samples from people who had or had not recently fired a gun. Mainly LIBS and electrochemistry were used to detect GSR. Some samples were also analysed by SEM/EDX for confirmation. The data was processed using neural networks and likelihood ratios were derived. The authors of this study conclude that it was possible to discriminate between shooters and non-shooters in this way. The combination of these two very rapid techniques meant that a large number of samples could be screened, in order to select those of interest for more in-depth SEM/EDX analysis.

Following up on a study published in 2015, Gorey et al. [20] continued to examine the prevalence of different primer compositions encountered in cases in Ireland. About sixty cartridge cases were analysed and some trends were identified, namely that none of these cases had a non-toxic (lead free) primer, confirming the general tendency that this type of lead free ammunition is not yet found in regions such as Europe. Another interesting observation is that all shotgun ammunition examined was rich in PbBaSbAl type primer particles. The authors recommend compiling this type of statistic for every country/jurisdiction, because while this type of information is very useful for establishing general patterns and for pre-assessing cases, the situation in one country is not necessarily the same as in another country.

Many studies concerning the prevalence of and contamination by IGSR have been performed over the years. However, an overarching analysis of most of these results was never performed until the meta-analysis published by Malmborg et al. [21]. In total, the authors have included and analysed data from 80 publications (≈ 4500 measurements) and divided them into several topics: transfer (9 publications), persistence (10 publications), background (15 publications), contamination (18 publications) and deposition of IGSR on shooters’ hands (28 publications). Additionally, they performed four in-house experiments focusing on the transfer and persistence of IGSR from gloves. Part of the analysed data was used to calculate half-lives of IGSR on hands and gloves and to model the probability of finding any amount of IGSR on the hands of an arrested person whose hands were “clean” before arrest (8% probability of non-zero transfer for arrests performed by non-special unit officers).

Shortly after firing, a shooter has great potential to transfer GSR particles onto all objects he touches. Akçan et al. investigated secondary transfer of Pb/Ba/Sb particles to commonly encountered surfaces, i.e., ceramic, glass, metal, paper and plastic [22]. All experiments were performed using the same semi-automatic shotgun and ammunition. Between the initial shot and the secondary transfer, a waiting time of 1 h without touching anything was imposed. All experiments show the same sequence for possibly retaining GSR particles on a surface: the highest numbers were found on paper cups, followed by plastic, ceramic, metal and glass. The results furthermore show that cleaning hands has an enormous impact on the number of particles found. After using a paper towel, the number of particles recovered is about half of what is found without any prior cleaning. This number further decreases when cleaning hands with wet wipes, water and water/soap respectively. For example, less than 10% of the initial number of particles is retrieved after the hands were cleaned with water and soap prior to sampling.

In Israel, police officers carry a personal pistol and many police vehicles are equipped with a rifle. Therefore, Azulay et al. [23] decided to perform a population study (of 51 samples) on the rear seats of random police vehicles, searching for inorganic GSR particles. No characteristic GSR particles were found on 47% of the samples and only 10% of the samples contained more than three particles, with a maximum of seven particles. Their study shows that the material of the rear seat upholstery influences the number of characteristic GSR particles. On vinyl seats (30 samples) no more than two particles were ever found and 63% contained none. Fabric-covered seats, on the other hand, were more prone to retaining particles. Only 24% of the fabric seats’ samples contained no particles, while 29% contained three particles or more. Although the chance of significant transfer during the transport of a suspect is considered low, the authors do advise using police vehicles with vinyl upholstery whenever possible.

The persistence of GSR on hands has been the subject of numerous studies and publications. Following up on these studies, Tahirukaj et al. examined [24] the persistence of GSR on the hands of shooters using large-calibre (Zastava rifle) and small-calibre (9 mm pistol) firearms (these are two popular types of firearms in Kosovo), this for up to 8 h of daily activity. The results are in line with previous findings, namely that after several hours, in this case 7, persistence can be considered to be nil, particularly in the case of small-calibre weapons.

Blakey et al. performed a population study [25], investigating the prevalence of GSR and indicative particles in public locations across the United Kingdom. More than 260 samples from trains, train stations, taxis, hired cars, buses, underground railway stations and “tube” trains were collected and analyzed. Not a single characteristic particle was found on these stubs. A maximum of 4 indicative particles on a single sample was found. These results indicate that GSR particles found on someone are unlikely to have originated from public location contaminations. However, the authors stress that these results might be different in other geographical regions and, therefore, additional studies are necessary.

The impact of clothing handling variables following a shot on the persistence of gunshot residues on fabrics was studied by Weyermann et al. [26]. In this study, a survey was conducted among 18 forensic services. The aim was to ascertain standard practices or variations in procedures that could affect the preservation of GSR on fabrics, with particular reference to the drying method, drying time and storage conditions. The survey revealed that a wide range of practices were employed concerning these three factors. The authors studied two main variables: the drying position (vertical or horizontal) of the fabrics and the time elapsed before the search for GSR particles. Fabrics were exposed to gunfire, then slightly wetted with water and finally subjected to the various drying conditions to examine how these factors affect the persistence of residues. The percentage loss of GSR particles was calculated for each sample between the number of particles before wetting and after drying. The particles were visualized by IR fluorescence using a video spectral comparator, counted using Adobe Photoshop software and confirmed manually. Despite slightly different percentages of loss, the results of the study show that the drying position of the fabrics does not significantly affect the detection of GSR. The same applies to drying time. However, it was noted that the wetting of the fabric is the most critical stage for GSR loss. Nevertheless, this is not a factor investigated in the present study. It was also observed that the drying position and time did not affect the position of the GSR on the fabric, allowing for the determination of the shooting distance. The study concludes that the procedures reported by forensic services, although different, are adequate for drying slightly wet clothing contaminated with relatively high levels of GSR.

The study carried out by Guilbeau-Frugier et al. [27] compares two analytical techniques, SEM/EDX and ICP-MS, for the examination of gunshot residues on victims that were buried. The main objectives were to assess GSR retention after burial in winter after two months of decomposition, and whether entry and exit injuries can still be distinguished. In the case of a buried body, the problem of decomposition is linked to the problem of soil contamination, which is not uncommon but has not been sufficiently studied. As an experimental model, bovine feet, shot with 7 mm calibre semi-jacketed bullets, were buried during different periods of time, allowing simulation of decomposition under burial conditions. After burial, but before sampling, the samples had to undergo special treatment due to their decomposition. The results show that both techniques can detect characteristic GSR particles after up to 60 days of burial. However, only the SEM/EDX technique can clearly distinguish entry wounds from exit wounds, ICP-MS data were difficult to interpret, as the exit wounds exhibited greater concentrations of antimony and lead, compared to the entry wounds. The authors conclude that SEM/EDX analysis is particularly useful when the ballistic trajectory cannot be determined macroscopically, thus providing a reliable method for GSR analysis to be carried out – even after burial. Reconstructing the ballistic event is also facilitated by identifying the entry and exit wounds.

A similar study [28] by Guilbeau-Frugier et al. investigated the effect of carbonization on the detection of GSR particles using an animal model. Specifically, the persistence of and differentiation between entry and exit wound orifices was investigated. To simulate a realistic scenario, sheep limbs were used as a model. They were exposed to gunfire before being subjected to intense heat to simulate carbonization. An accelerant was also used prior to charring. Two analytical techniques, SEM/EDX and ICP-MS, were then tested. Following carbonization, the visual distinction between the entry and exit orifices is difficult to make, but analytically it is still possible: SEM/EDX analysis revealed a greater number and density of particles in the entry orifices than in the exit wounds. Furthermore, the analysis showed that lead particles were present exclusively in the exit wounds, both before and after carbonization, while characteristic particles were exclusively found in the entry wounds. With ICP-MS, characteristic particles were found in both the entry and exit orifices, but in different concentrations. After carbonization, although the number of particles is drastically reduced, it is still possible to find the same ratio. Although ICP-MS is quantitative and sensitive, it proved to be not the most effective technique. The amount of barium measured was often very low and there were large variations between samples. Carbonization, depending on the temperature and duration of exposure, alters GSR particles, which undergo chemical transformations that render them more difficult to detect and identify. However, even at low levels, it is still possible to detect them and to distinguish entry wounds from exit wounds under the conditions tested in this study.

Interpretation of results

In the same line as the study published in 2019, Osnat et al. continued [29] to investigate the evaluative approach applied to the field of GSR, in order to work on activity level hypotheses (presence or not of a POI in a shooting environment, or even having fired or not) on the basis of the number of IGSR particles recovered from samples. The article contains a literature review of various studies that have been conducted to estimate the presence of IGSR particles on hands in various circumstances (taking into account persistence, prevalence and contamination effects). The data were completed by two experiments conducted by the authors, namely mock arrests and a GSR survey on CSI personnel. A mathematical model of the double exponential decay type was used to fit the particle distribution as a function of time. Based on this model and the data published and self-obtained, the authors defined three scenarios (low, medium and heavy background) centred on the risk of contamination, in order to obtain the different possible likelihood ratios with their verbal equivalent. The example of finding eight GSR particles (which may be considered a borderline case) is discussed in the article.

While the hypotheses of interest centre often on whether or not the POI discharged a firearm, other working hypotheses must sometimes be considered. For instance in the context of comparing particles found on a POI with reference elements, such as particles from the crime scene (e.g. a bullet hole or a recovered cartridge case). Following up on a study published in 2017 [30], Matzen et al. worked on this type of hypothesis, this time proposing models evaluated using artificial intelligence [31]. The data introduced into these models was drawn from a multitude of spectral acquisitions obtained from real cases (around 200 cases over a 5 year period, corresponding to almost 2000 stubs). Although, according to the authors, there is still work to be done before their model can be implemented in routine casework, this study has validated a model using artificial intelligence with very interesting performances.

In the context of a court decision in 2002, convicting a person of murder but for which the hypothesis of suicide had been raised by the defence, various clues, evidence and testimonies were re-examined by Burnett et al. This study was published in 2022 [32]. More specifically in relation to the GSR field, the firing distance was reviewed, with a discussion on gunpowder stippling around the wound. The question of the deposition time of GSR particles on a person who has entered a room where a shot had been recently fired was also discussed, since numerous particles of GSR were found on the suspect, even though he declared he had only entered the room after the shot had already fallen. The GSR expert called to testify in the case referred to a time of 30 s after which the GSR particles had settled; however study results produced later showed that this time interval may be much longer, in the order of 10 min [33], or even several hours – if also the smallest particles are considered [34].

It is always interesting to assess the extent to which the results of forensic examinations add value to the judicial investigation and whether the results are correctly understood and used by those involved in the judicial process, in particular the magistrates responsible for judging the case. It was in this context that Charles and Jonckheere undertook a review of some forty Belgian cases in which at least one GSR expert opinion had been requested [35]. Further, the relationship with other (forensic) elements of the case was also examined. The study revealed that in the vast majority of cases, the court correctly interpreted and used the GSR results, although some minor errors in interpretation were identified, mainly involving the misquotation of the expert's words, sometimes termed the “prosecutor's fallacy”. Ultimately, a higher conviction rate was observed in the presence of incriminating GSR results. Surprisingly, in half of the cases where a guilty verdict was reached, GSR was the only forensic evidence submitted in the file.

Quality aspects and efficiency

Pixel size, closely linked to magnification, is one of the parameters that influence the analytical performance of SEM/EDX for the detection of IGSR. The smaller the pixel size, the more efficient is the detection of the smallest particles, but the longer the analysis requires to run. Onetto et al. [36] therefore examined this important parameter in more detail. They used data from 320 real samples and opted for a Bayesian treatment to calculate the probability of not detecting any GSR particles as a function of pixel size. According to the authors’ findings, increasing the pixel size by a factor of two (from 0.16 μm2 to 0.32 μm2) considerably shortens the analysis time, while only increasing with 5% the chance of not detecting any IGSR particles. In their view this is still an acceptable proposition.

Validating and optimizing the analysis method used for searching IGSR is crucial after buying new equipment or after performing extensive repair work on defective equipment. Brozek-Mucha and Klag have done this exercise on their SEM/EDX system using synthetic samples from the ENFSI GSR proficiency tests and a real GSR sample of the FTS-2021-GSR testing scheme [37]. They optimized the analysis in such a way that they found almost all GSR particles of all different sizes present on the ENFSI samples. However, to be more time-efficient they decided to alter their method in such a way as to detect only particles of 1 μm and above. The article gives an overview of the different tests they performed and the results that were obtained. It shows that measuring at a lower magnification (120x instead of 250x) results in a considerably shorter analysis time, but does reduce the detection efficiency of the smaller particles.

Luminescent markers and doped ammunition

In 2022, Weber et al. published a study [38] in which they show the potential of yttrium orthovanadate phosphors doped with erbium and ytterbium as luminescent markers for the identification of gunshot residue. The markers, synthesized by the authors, show a strong green luminescence which can be observed using both UV and IR forensic light sources. After introducing the markers in 9 mm non-toxic ammunition and firing several shots, luminescent residues were visible on the shooter's hands, the firearm itself and the targets. This result shows that the produced markers possess a sufficiently high thermal stability, as they do not lose their luminescent properties when exposed to the high temperature and pressure of the firing process. GSR particles containing the marker elements were identified using SEM/EDX, making these particles much more specific thanks to the introduction of the uncommon lanthanide elements.

Another luminescent marker for GSR was proposed by Serra et al. [39]. They managed to synthesize hydrogels based on benzene tricarboxylic acid which show a strong reddish luminescence that could be associated to the europium and zirconium ions in the marker complex. Their experiments showed an increased thermal stability when combining europium and zirconium compared to when only europium was present. After extensive characterization of the produced material, the luminescent marker was added to gunpowder to perform shooting tests. The luminescent marker was positively identified after the shots on different relevant surfaces under UV light. However, it was not investigated whether these luminescent particles merged with IGSR or OGSR particles during the shooting process. Furthermore, the GSR – from either classic or non-toxic ammunition types – can be collected from the surfaces directly on a carbon gel electrode. This electrode can be used without pretreatment in a measuring cell for Square Wave Voltammetry (SWV) which can yield analysis results on the spot. Alternatively, the GSR particles can be collected on SEM stubs and analysed later by SEM/EDX equipment in those laboratories that are able to use this technique. The authors point out that in Brazil the voltammetric technique is more advantageous because of the high cost and low availability of SEM/EDX equipment.

Europium has been used as a luminescent marker by Marques et al. as well [40]. However, instead of combining it with zirconium, they decided to synthesize mixed lanthanide-organic frameworks containing europium and terbium. The synthesis and characterization of the homometallic counterparts and the possibility to use them as luminescent markers in ammunition was already discussed in earlier papers. The advantage of the mixed metal compound is that it shows a bright red emission while maintaining a thermal stability similar to the homometallic compounds. According to the authors, the most promising compound is the one where europium and terbium are mixed in a 1:9 ratio.

Most studies focusing on luminescent markers in gunshot residues have investigated the possibilities of using rare earths. Some of the advantages of these fluorescent compounds are their often very high thermal stability and the fact that the used rare earths are not omnipresent in the environment and can hence be considered specific to GSR. However, as pointed out by Mayti et al. [41], their high cost may pose a difficult obstacle for large-scale production. Therefore, their study focused on using more common small fluorescent dye molecules (pyrene, fluorescein and a Pt–CNN complex). The authors present the luminescence characteristics of the different markers in solid state and solution phase, as well as how they can be observed after shooting a firearm. Additionally, they have shown that it is possible to detect the luminescent markers and several OGSR compounds simultaneously using positive ion electron spray ionization mass spectrometry, even though the markers have only limited thermal stability.

Organic GSR

Sampling

In the context of combining the analysis of IGSR and OGSR, different strategies have been worked out: i) the fifty-fifty procedure, which generally consists of using one area of the stub for the IGSR sampling and the other (which surface may have been modified) for the OGSR sampling, ii) sequential sampling (often with priority given to IGSR sampling), and iii) sequential analysis (also often with priority given to IGSR analysis). So far, sequential analysis seems to be the most promising strategy. Following up on a study published in 2020 [42], Redouté Minzière et al. examined sequential analysis in greater detail by varying the order of sampling/analysis (IGSR vs. OGSR) and assessing the impact this had on the results [43]. To do this, the authors devised a protocol for extracting OGSR that was significantly different from what had previously been proposed, and that would interfere as little as possible with the subsequent analysis of IGSR by SEM/EDX. Their protocol consists of depositing a solvent on the stub and, after waiting 30 s, recuperating the solution for analysis. In the end, the authors saw no significant differences between the two methods, except at the margin and only for one or other of the samples and/or OGSR analytes analysed.

With the development of combined analyses of IGSR and OGSR, comparative studies on the persistence of these different types of gunshot residue can be considered. For example, Redouté Minzière and Weyermann examined [44] the persistence of IGSR and OGSR on different parts of shooters' bodies after 30 min. In this study, samples were taken from the hands, forearms, face and nostrils. Differences were observed in relation to deposition, with IGSR contaminating preferentially one side of the body of the shooter, while OGSR compounds were more evenly dispersed. Various hypotheses, such as transfer and persistence mechanisms, have been put forward to explain these differences. Furthermore, according to the authors’ study, the added value of nostril sampling is relatively limited, particularly given the small sampling area available. Instead, they suggest combining this sample with that of the face.

Prevalence and contamination studies

As was started for IGSR already some time ago, several studies on prevalence and contamination have recently been conducted for OGSR compounds, in order to enable and facilitate the interpretation of OGSR results in the analysis of real cases.

In their paper, Senješ et al. [45] studied the contamination of police vehicles by IGSR in the Zagreb County Police Administration (Croatia). The study aimed to assess the extent of this contamination and to discover possible sources of secondary contamination. Samples were collected from various parts of the vehicles and analysed for the presence of characteristic GSR particles. It was found that GSR particles were widespread in many areas of the vehicles. Significant contamination was specifically identified on the vehicle's seats and floor mats, demonstrating that these areas are more susceptible to GSR transfer. The study identified several variables that increase the level of secondary contamination, such as the transport of suspects in the vehicle, the presence of weapons or the handling of contaminated evidence. The authors underline that robust GSR management is essential to guarantee the admissibility of evidence in criminal trials. They therefore argue that police officers and forensic scientists should adhere to strict guidelines in order to minimise contamination.

One of the issues with searching and characterizing OGSR is that their presence as individual compounds may not be of high probative value, given the individual prevalence of most compounds in the everyday environment. Instead, it is the combination of several OGSR components detected at the same time that may increase their probative value. The same reasoning can be applied to elements related to IGSR (i.e. Pb and Ba), which are widely present in the environment on an individual basis. The idea behind a study published by Feeney et al. [46] was to use the LC-MS/MS method (a rapid technique already widely used in forensic laboratories) to detect most OGSR as well as lead and barium (using complexing agents). A large prevalence study was therefore carried out on populations considered to be at low risk (general population) and at high risk of exhibiting GSR-like particles/compounds, as well as individuals in contact with GSR such as sports shooters and bystanders to shooting incidents. The results were processed using machine learning algorithms and likelihood ratio calculations in order to obtain reliable conclusions. According to the authors, it could be possible using their approach to distinguish with a certain degree of confidence between the different populations studied, even if some overlaps are observed. Especially, being able to make a distinction between bystanders and witnesses to shootings on the one hand and the shooters themselves on the other seems a promising prospect.

A large study aimed at examining the transfer and persistence of IGSR and OGSR following different types of actions and involving hundreds of samples was carried out by Vander Pyl et al. and published in 2023 [47]. The key feature of the study is that the authors attempted to obtain the exact quantity of OGSR and IGSR at time zero, in particular by producing synthetic solutions. Different substrates were also investigated, including a synthetic skin substrate to study the behavior of the analytes, in particular OGSR, which may interact with the human skin in a complex way. The results were compared with those obtained from real shootings, enabling the latter to be normalized to some extent. In the end, the results seem to confirm that the persistence of OGSR depends more on physicochemical properties, whereas that of IGSR depends more on pure physical properties of the substrate. And, logically, IGSR particulates are more likely to undergo secondary transfers than OGSR compounds. This type of study is useful for understanding the complex phenomena involved, and can help to formulate activity level conclusions with future models.

Interpretation of results

In line with the development of an evaluative approach for IGSR at the activity level, Roux et al. propose [48] the same type of approach for OGSR, taking into account that recent studies on persistence, prevalence and transfer now offer possibilities for evaluating likelihood ratios. To illustrate the approach, a case simulation is presented with the prosecutor's hypothesis that the POI fired a shot a few minutes before his arrest, vs. the defense hypothesis that the POI was contaminated during his arrest by a police officer who had just fired a warning shot. The evaluation was based on the results of the analysis of three organic compounds. The simulation shows that, like in the case of IGSR, the risk of transfer is one of the most important parameters to consider, and knowledge of the contamination level of the police officer who arrested the POI could greatly reduce this uncertainty.

Development of new instrumentations and methods

Dey et al. investigated the potential of Fluorescence Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) for detecting and quantifying GSR components [49]. To this end, they studied the energy transfer efficiency between two fluorescent dyes, acriflavine and rhodamine B, with and without GSR components present. A small amount of clay (2 ppm) was added to the testing solution to increase the FRET efficiency to almost 80% when no GSR was present. Adding cationic GSR components (Pb2+, Ba2+ and Sb3+) effectively lowers the FRET efficiency because of their electrostatic interaction with the cationic dye molecules. The efficiency decreases more when Pb2+ or Ba2+ ions are added to the solution compared to when only Sb3+ ions are present. However, when the three ions are present together, the Sb3+ concentration seems to have the highest impact on the recorded FRET efficiency. For example, a high concentration of Sb3+ ions results in FRET efficiencies that are more or less independent of the concentration of the other ions. This work demonstrates that FRET could be used for the sensing of GSR and its quantification to a certain extent. However, it has yet to be explored how sensitive the method is and how it reacts to other ions with a similar size and ionic charge.

Laser-ablation inductively coupled plasma Mass Spectrometry and laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy

In [50] Segador et al. investigated and compared the use of portable LIBS equipment on the crime scene and in a lab environment (using a scanning stage) on bullet holes made in different support materials. The list of materials includes building materials (concrete, clay, ceramic tile, plaster, wood, etc), clothing fabrics and leather, but also less common items such as a riot helmet and a bullet-proof vest. The LIBS system was set up to detect elements common for bullet hole damage such as lead, barium, antimony, copper and zinc. In order to check for false positives in the targets, measurements before shooting tests were made as blanks. Furthermore, the tests were conducted immediately after, a day after and a month after shooting, to test the persistence in different materials and environments. Also, shots through multiple layers of material (for example fabrics) were performed to monitor loss of GSR material through ‘bullet wiping’. This proved, however, not to pose a large problem. The results of the study therefore show that LIBS is a very good technology to perform tests on bullet holes – even at the crime scene – and may be used to guide GSR sampling for SEM/EDX analysis. Of course, care must be taken to also perform analyses of uninvolved material to test for elements present in the raw material and to assess general contamination due to the shooting environment.

In yet another attempt at using portable LIBS to perform screenings at the crime scene, Trejos et al. show [51] that this technology indeed shows good results in detecting IGSR elements with a mobile unit. Furthermore, this mobile unit shows no less performance in terms of LOD than the lab benchtop model which was also tested on the same samples. They conclude therefore that this type of technology can offer screening for GSR sampling, thereby increasing efficiency both at the crime scene and in the laboratory. The authors mention also that the portable LIBS instrument is equipped with a CMOS sensor which enables the visual search of the GSR particles on the surface to be sampled. One may wonder, however, how a CMOS imaging sensor is able to reliably detect microscopic GSR on the hand of a (living) person of interest. The authors show only images of macroscopic GSR particles as an illustration in the text.

In [52] Marangoni et al. report on their work to complement existing research in LIBS adoption in micro trace forensics by developing a novel probabilistic protocol for GSR identification based on: (a) the analysis by low cost Laser Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS) of samples collected from suspect hands using a double-sided adhesive tape; and (b) the subsequent application of a machine learning model to train the samples in order to predict the probability of them belonging to a “shooter” (an individual who has discharged a firearm) or “non-shooter” (an individual who has not discharged a firearm) category; (c) perform an external validation by establishing a minimum probability threshold for classification with predicted classes of “Shooter”, “Non-shooter”, and “Undefined” (samples uncertain about the classification); and (d) testing the protocol using samples artificially enriched with characteristic elements of GSR to verify the robustness of the generated model and the capability of eliminating samples not originating from GSR.

X-ray analysis

The goal of the paper by Yüksel et al. [53] is to provide the elemental profiling of both toxic and lead-free cartridge primers available in the Turkish ammunition market. SEM/EDX and X-Ray mapping were used for morphological assessment of GSR, together with ICP-MS for the elemental profiling, and XPS to interpret the chemical state designations in the primer. Statistical analysis using SPSS® Software and PCA (Principal Component Analysis) was performed on the collected data. Both short and long barrelled guns were used (S&W5906; AK47; MPT76; MPT55; Armson RSX), with toxic primers (9x19 MKEK, S&B, Yavex; 7.62x39 Barnaul Plant; 12 Gauge Fiocchi, Nobel Sport) and non-toxic primers (9x19 Fiocchi, Unis Ginex; Unis Ginex 7.62x39, 7.62x51, 5.56x45). PCA and Pearson's Correlation Coefficient (PCC) analysis were successfully applied as multivariate statistical approaches to assess metal levels in the cartridge primers. Chemical state assignments were specified using XPS binding energies.

In their technical note [54] Huang et al. researched the use of TRXRF (Total Reflexion X-ray Fluorescence spectrometry) to identify and quantify bulk and trace elements (Pb–Cu–Ba–Sb–Fe–Zn) in IGSR, producing elemental profiles to differentiate between different ammunitions in order to discern the type of jacketing used. In this study, 2 pistols (.40 S&W Glock model 23; .45 Auto Springfield Armory model XD-45) combined with 6 different ammunitions were used (.40 Winchester unjacketed lead flat nose, FMJ Cu, Silver tip hollow point, Federal hollow point Cu jacket, Speer TMJ Cu + .45 ARX inceptor copper polymer). Cotton twill targets were shot at a distance of 15 cm to provide the GSR. Sample material was collected by cutting out a circle with a radius of 10 cm surrounding the bullet hole to be subsequently dissolved in concentrated nitric acid. A 10 μL spot containing an internal yttrium standard for quantification was dried on a quartz disk and scanned at 4000x for 1 s at 50 kV and 600 μA current, after which the data was analysed through Bruker S2 PICOFOX software to produce elemental profiles for each ammunition. The most prominent results obtained were the lead concentration of 239 ppm derived from the lead unjacketed flat nose ammunition, and the copper concentration of 114 ppm resulting from the Cu polymer .45 ammunition.

Electrochemical analysis

In the comparison of portable and benchtop electrochemical instruments made by Arroyo et al. [55], the authors describe the results obtained in detection of both inorganic and organic gunshot residues in authentic shooter samples. These were acquired both from aluminum SEM stubs and directly from the hands of the shooter group (after 5 shots were fired) and a non-shooters control group. Both lead-containing (Winchester 9 mm) and lead-free (Fiocchi) ammunition was used in the tests. The motivation of the study was to demonstrate and compare the reliability of both portable and benchtop electrochemical sensors in the application of a fast screening method for GSR in the field. This entailed not only a direct comparison of the results obtained on the samples by the two systems, but also a detailed assessment of the error rates and accuracy obtained on the measurements of the non-shooter background (200 persons), leaded shooters (100 persons) and unleaded shooters (50 persons). The authors conclude that both instruments show very comparable quality of results and detection limits for both IGSR and OGSR analytes, demonstrating the utility of a portable electrochemical potentiostat in the field in order to make fast triages between suspects – even at the crime scene. Further tests are ongoing in collaborations with practicing forensic laboratories to further showcase the importance and efficiency of on-site GSR screening.

In [56], Dempsey et al. use Magnetic Nano Particles (MNP) in combination with electrochemical and spectrochemical analysis techniques to detect both organic and inorganic GSR components. The work was motivated by the on-going demand for rapid organic residue testing as a tool for on-site use by forensic scientists and the lack of reports of modified electrodes for propellant stabiliser residue analysis. Being able to measure molecules that survive the firing event opens new opportunities for trace analysis and persistence studies. Persistence times of OGSR on the hands of the shooter are typically 4 h with a half-life of 1 h, so time is of the essence in relation to their detection. The proposed methodology is capable of sample to result times under 2 min. Their work also advanced understanding of the electrooxidation of diphenylamine (DPA) in organic and aqueous electrolytes, realising key insights into follow up products and processes which could arise during degradation. MNPs were prepared using the accessible and controlled process of electrooxidation at iron anodes, followed by characterisation via surface, thermal, spectroscopic and electrochemical techniques. The cathodic limit enhanced the DPA electrooxidation which was followed by formation of a surface-confined electroactive product of the initial oxidation step. Peaks associated with the latter process diminished at the MNP electrode which has benefits of signal reproducibility upon cycling and calibration. Spectro-electrochemical studies pointed to the formation of poly (diphenylamine) via potential cycling on a Pt gauze electrode. DPA quantitation was achieved via differential pulse voltammetry realising precise and robust analytical performance with sensitivity of 1.13×10−3 A cm−2 mM−1. Real sample analysis of both unburnt and firearm propellant residue provided qualitative and quantitative DPA analysis, resulting in clear signals for the stabiliser residue in addition to the main metal residue (lead), both of which were found to be enhanced by the magnetic nanoparticles. Recovery and DPA spiking studies were performed in the base of an unburnt propellant powder sample and the recovered residue, realising 10.7 ± 0.3 mg/g and 0.427 μg/cm2 (21.3 μg in total) respectively. The authors will further progress towards a total analysis system for firearm residue with development of ferrofluids (graphite inks with inclusion of MNP). Overall, this work makes a positive contribution to (a) the currently limited number of reports of electrochemical synthesis and optimisation of magnetic nanoparticles with optimisation and full characterisation, (b) the novel examination of the influence of this modifier on quantitation of DPA propellant stabiliser using both CV and DPV and (c) our means to realise a rapid on-site analytical test for forensic chemical analysis of the organic components of firearm residue.

Limbut et al. describe [57] how they used a Screen-Printed Carbon Electrode (SPCE), modified with a nanohybrid of Silver NanoPrisms and Phosphorus-doped Carbon NanoTubes (AgNPr@P-CNT), coupled with a simple, portable flow system, to produce an efficient amperometric sensor for the detection of nitrite ions. This sensing system was finally applied to determine nitrites in GSR on various substrates for forensic evaluation – specifically, metal, mirrored glass, plastic, and a human hand were tested. According to the authors, their new sensor, when compared to reported nitrite sensors, exhibited superior analytical performances in terms of the working potential, linear range, and detection limit. The developed sensor can be applied for forensic purposes to the screening and quantification of nitrite in gunshot residues on different substrates. Furthermore, the proposed nanohybrid sensor also has potential in other electrochemical applications.

For their study to detect nitrites in different application domains using on the spot electrochemical analysis, Hatamie et al. describe [58] development of a mini-electrochemical cell within a single micropipette tip. The developed tool can perform microsampling when connected to a micropipette, enabling the microcell to conduct microsampling (20–40 μL) and microanalysis using the microscale electrodes installed inside the tip in just a few seconds. Micro gold wire coated with Gold NanoParticles (AuNPs) fabricated by cathodic deposition as the working microelectrode and a fabricated Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode were used as electrochemical cell components. The authors have successfully tested this device in the field on different nitrite-containing applications including medical, environmental and forensics. This last domain application entailed the detection of nitrites on samples acquired from Persons Of Interest (i.e. shooters) after the firing of a shot. The obtained results indicate the tool's high capability and potential for real-world applications.

The responses obtained by electroanalytical procedures using Screen Printed Electrodes (SPE) can be selective and sensitive when enhanced through the use of chemically modified electrodes – a bismuth coating in the case of this study, performed by Pinto et al. [59]. Also, chemometric pattern recognition techniques, to detect GSR patterns from different guns, were used to help elucidate the dynamics and authorship of firearm-related crimes. The profile firearm type, calibre, and the ammunition used in the discharge was identified. The authors used chemometric analysis of the voltammetric data, combined with a fast and low-cost on-site methodology. A second set of samples was part of the “non-shooting” group, collected for the purpose of evaluating the electroanalytical methodology and the classification models developed, primarily concerning possible false-positive results. Samples in this group were obtained from [1]: professionals who work with firearms and ammunition but did not fire any shots [2]. Samples labelled as “hold after shooting”, which were obtained from situations where an individual did not fire a shot, but had the firearm placed in their hands by the shooter after the shot – as you would see in a suicide case. Further samples from a “mechanic shop” group, were collected from mechanics working in different mechanics shops. Finally, samples from a “blank” group were obtained from random people who had not had any type of contact with firearms before. The authors show that they were able to build robust models that are able to correctly identify a sample as belonging to one of these type groups. The correct classification of these samples - especially the samples obtained from the hands of individuals who merely held the weapon after the shot – demonstrate that the electrochemical methodology associated with the chemometric tools presented here holds promise for evaluating cases of firearm-related false suicides, something that the current SEM/EDX technology is unable to perform, and which can help elucidate the dynamics and authorship of firearm-related crimes.

In [60], Wongpakdee et al. show that electrochemical detection is more approachable and suitable for on-the-spot analysis of GSR compounds than colorimetric tests or more elaborate technology such as electrophoresis, IR, Raman or mass analysis. In particular, disposable electrochemical sensors provide an interesting alternative. In their study, they present a screen-printed carbon electrode (SPCE) with surface modifications for the forensic analysis of GSR and low explosive materials. In particular, they use an initial coating with gold nanoparticles for metal detection and a further coating with copper for direct detection of nitrites and nitrates in the environment of GSR and low explosives. The presence of gold nanoparticles already present on the electrode surface increases the surface roughness and provides more active sites for copper deposition, leading to better coverage and adhesion of the electrodeposited copper particles. Due to the reactivity of copper with the air, the authors suggest performing the copper deposition onto the gold-plated electrode in-situ (which takes only a few minutes to perform). Further steps are needed to completely validate the detection of metals and nitrate/nitrite in the field.

Limbut et al. show [61] that a cost-effective electrochemical analysis instrument for in-the-field use can be built by modifying the electrode surface using Spent Coffee Grounds (SCG) as a source of activated carbon. The authors propose a novel electrochemical sensor for the co-determination of Cu2+ and Pb2+ ions in bullet holes as an example application. Their sensor is based on a Glassy Carbon Electrode (GCE) modified with Spent Coffee Grounds and further decorated with FeO particles (FeO/SCG). To test the electrode, three shots per sample were fired on paper targets using 9 mm and .38 cal ammunition. These were sampled with cotton swabs which were extracted in acetate buffer. The filtrates were analysed both electrochemically and by means of ICP-OES. The results from the developed method were compared with the results from the standard method (ICP-OES). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed to compare results, which showed good agreement between both methods. Finally, the recovery of the developed method for Pb2+ and Cu2+ determination was found to be very good.

A study by Islas et al. [62] proposes a Dispersive Solid Phase Microextraction (DSPME) method for the simultaneous determination of Pb(II), Cu(II) and Al(III) in GSR by Capillary Electrophoresis (CE). It involves the synthesis of an amino silica adsorbent with an optimal molar ratio of (3:2) of the cross-linking agent tetramethyl orthosilicate (TMOS) and the functional monomer 3-aminopropyl trimethoxy silane (APTMS) by sol-gel precipitation. The optimization utilized Fourier Transform infrared (FTIR), SEM, ThermoGravimetric Analysis (TGA) and adsorption isotherm analysis. Critical factors in the DSPME were optimized. A .22 Caliber ammunition was used in the shooting tests, and the palm and dorsal region of the hand of POIs was sampled with a swab moistened with 0.01 M HNO3.

Gas and Liquid Chromatography Mass Spectrometry

In [63] Trejos et al. investigate the capabilities and limitations of Liquid Chromatography-tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and Gas Chromatography Mass Spectrometry (GC–MS) for the trace detection of Organic GunShot Residue (OGSR). As the interest of researchers in the detection of OGSR grows as a response to the advent of heavy metal-free ammunition types, the need for a solid framework for sampling and analysis of OGSR emerges. As GC-MS and LC-MS/MS are at the moment the most dominant analysis techniques capable of detecting and quantifying OGSR in realistic situations, most of the attention goes to these two techniques. Of course, as some OGSR components may also be present in the (non-shooting) environment, it is vital to determine which components (compounds) should be targeted and in what concentration ranges they would be indicative of a contact with a shooting incident by a Person of Interest (suspect). The authors note that, using the ammunition calibres used in the study at least, GC-MS is not sufficiently accurate to detect OGSR reliably on its own and it should be used in combination with LC-MS/MS in a routine case work environment. LC-MS/MS on the other hand, turns out to be a very accurate and sensitive technique that can be used in a realistic forensic setting, attaining accuracies up to 80% in the triple quadrupole configuration. Continued efforts by the authors include development of a multi-technique workflow for comprehensive analysis of both IGSR and OGSR on a single sample, where accuracies above 95% have been demonstrated.

Other Mass Spectrometry techniques

Single-particle Inductively Coupled Plasma Time-Of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (spICP-TOFMS) allows to detect and quantify the elements within individual IGSR particles. In Ref. [64], Gundlach-Graham et al. used this method to study the composition of particles collected from possible leaded and lead-free GSR-contaminated material. In spICP-TOFMS, a liquid sample containing individual particles is introduced into a plasma via nebulization. In the plasma, particles are vaporized and atomized; the resultant gaseous atoms are ionized and extracted into the mass spectrometer. The ions from each particle are mass separated and detected as transient signal ‘spikes’ using a time-of-flight mass analyser. Each recorded spike is composed of ion signals from the detectable elements within each particle. Therefore, spICP-TOFMS provides a high throughput, sensitive method to analyse thousands of particles per minute that may help decrease ambiguity in IGSR classification – especially in cases where presence of ‘GSR-like’ particles is feared. In this study, particles collected from hands of shooters using lead containing and lead-free ammunition, together with brake pad and fireworks samples, were successfully analysed and characterized. This is of course a destructive technique, making confirmation by SEM/EDX afterwards impossible, but it provides on the other hand quantitative compositional information regarding particles present on the sampled surfaces.

The same technology of spICP-TOFMS was used by Brünjes et al. [65]. They used samples collected from police officers who shot 5 rounds of 9 mm Luger and .38 munitions wearing different gloves for each shot. Each third glove was sampled for spICP-TOFMS analysis, while each fourth glove was sampled for SEM/EDX analysis. About 2000 particles were analysed by spICP-TOFMS from each glove, with hundred to four hundred of them being characteristic GSR particles. Samples from revolver shooters registered as more positive than those from pistol shooters, confirming that the weapon construction has a definite influence on the production of GSR particles. Due to the high sensitivity of the single-particle method and its focus on nanometer-sized particles, large numbers of non-GSR particles were also detected. Since mass spectrometry is very specific, precise and quantifiable, the possibilities exist to go much deeper into the interpretation and sub-classification of GSR particles – using especially the extra trace elements present in the characteristic particles – through which valuable forensic insights can be learnt. It is however clear that, as the particles are essentially consumed in the analysis, it is difficult to investigate the particulate nature of particles as is possible with SEM/EDX analysis. Finally, the authors conclude that, while it is possible to analyse with SEM/EDX micron-size particles per hour, MS analysis reveals the presence of large numbers of nanometer-sized particles per minute. In a time where doped and lead free ammunitions become more prevalent, the possibility to detect and characterize these very small particles may become a major prerequisite in GSR research.

Direct Sample Analysis Time of Flight Mass Spectrometry (DSA-TOF-MS) is another emergent technology that is introduced in forensic science. Gonzalez et al. used it [66] to ascertain its usefulness for directly analyzing organic GSR compounds on the hands of shooters. The 200 samples acquired from the hands of volunteers who had shot with CDC Luger 9 mm and .38 munitions were searched for the presence of a number of typical OGSR compounds such as TNT, 2,4-DNT, and Methyl and Ethyl Centralite (MC and EC). All these analytical species were observed in the samples without any indication of false positives. The authors therefore conclude that this technology can be an outstanding improvement in the forensic efforts in Chile to control gun crime and may, after further validation, be incorporated in the routine analyses in the future.

In their study [67], Adav et al. explore the use of Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time of Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-ToF-MS) in the detection of GSR components in the fingerprints of individuals suspected of being involved in shooting incidents. This technology is able to detect both Inorganic as well as Organic GSR components in the material of fingerprints, as well as endogenous compounds (coming from the body of the suspect), thus enabling the extraction of lifestyle patterns of the suspect. In order to extract the fingerprints from support materials (paper, ceramic tile, metallic plate), both hydrophilic (PristAPT) and hydrophobic (PristPTE) dusting powders were tested to visualise the latent prints. After visualisation, the print material was extracted from the support using a conventional lifting tape and analysed with MALDI-ToF-MS. Also the effect of hand washing on the detectability of GSR components was tested. Results show that in all cases the GSR-contaminated prints can be differentiated from GSR-free fingerprints. Further testing will identify the specific compounds detected, as well as offer a quantifiable result of their detection.

In their study [68], Yü et al. aim to detect GSR traces that are covered by bloodstains. After pretreatment, analysis is performed with ICP-MS. This method has a low detection limit and a high sensitivity. The best pretreatment method for the bullet incident hole with bloodstains is microwave digestion, and for the region around the bullet hole and the shooter's bloodstained hand it is ultrasonic vibration. A 5.8 mm rifle and a 9 mm pistol, targets of polyamide fabric and pig blood were used in the experiments. Shooting distances were 20–60 and 160 cm. Statistical analyses were performed to validate the results. The authors conclude that the results show that GSR-contaminations on items with bloodstains could be successfully detected by ICP-MS and that the bloodstains did not interfere with the GSR analysis.

Infrared and Raman Spectroscopy

In their study [69], Lednev et al. applied Raman Spectroscopy to the analysis of smokeless powders from different manufacturers. Their total dataset consisted of twelve different types of ammunition from three manufacturers (Winchester, Remington and Federal) and two different calibers (9 mm and .38). Chemometric modelling was then used to try to allocate the experimental results to the correct manufacturer and caliber size. A hierarchical modelling framework in which a sample is first linked to one of the manufacturers and afterwards to a caliber size proved to be the most effective. The first classification is performed using a Support Vector Machine-Discriminant Analysis model, and the second one using a Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis model, optimized for a specific manufacturer. Remington and Federal ammunition are consistently classified correctly, while Winchester ammunition is sometimes incorrectly assigned to Remington. The authors acknowledge that the used dataset is limited and plan to include a larger dataset in future studies.

Image analysis

In [70], Silva et al. present a model for estimating the uncertainty of Feret's Diameter (DF) measurements of Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) images from regular and irregular GSR particles. The correct morphometry definition of these particles allows to study their distinction from other possible sources of GSR (eg. air bags in cars) and, above all, their correlation with the type of ammunition used when firing the firearm.

Shooting distance estimation and Bullet Hole characterization

Methods and instrumentations

Most of the GSR produced by a shot is projected onto the target (object or victim), provided the target is close enough to the shooter. The diameter and density of the deposition pattern of the GSR particles will help determine the firing distance. This deposition pattern is usually revealed chemically by the use of colour tests, the most popular colour tests being the sodium rhodizonate test (detects lead and barium) and the modified Griess test (detects nitrites). Other tests exist, such as dithiooxamide for copper and zincon for visualization of zinc.

The aim of the paper by Merli et al. [71] is to estimate the concentration decay of nitrocellulose (NC) on targets with increasing shooting distance through quantitative analysis using a differential pulse voltammetric method without the need for modification of the electrode. This could prove useful in cases where determination of NC in OGSR can replace the lead detection with the Sodium Rhodizonate test, for example when non toxic ammunition was used. With a LOD of 1.3 μg/L and a LOQ of 4 μg/L, NC can be quantified up to 120 cm to estimate the shooting distance using an analytical procedure for the reduction of the nitro groups. An Amel model 4330 was used to obtain cyclic (CV) and differential pulse (DPV) voltammetric profiles. The linearity (up to 2 g/L) of the regression curves was assessed by linear least squares regression (LLSR). Test shootings were fired at distances of (5-20-60-80-100-120 cm) on 30x30 cm white cotton cloth, using two Beretta pistols (92FS model; PX4 Storm) and two 9x19 mm ammunition types of Fiocchi (SJSP ZetaPi Zero Pollution; FMJ Sinoxid).

The aim of the comparative study performed by Prahlow et al. [72], is to determine the influence of silencers on the deposition of gunshot residue on the targets. It is in the context of medical examination useful to distinguish a gunshot wound caused by a gun with or without a suppressor, because it could potentially influence the determination of the cause of death (suicide or homicide). A Walther .22, a Glock 9x19 mm and a FNX 0.45 were used at varying distances from contact shot to 22 inch with intervals of 1 inch. Two silencers were tested (Silencerco Switchback and Silencerco Osprey). The targets used were cardboard panels, painted with white rubber sealant liquid paint (Flex Seal®). The .45 and the 9 mm have a comparable pattern of decreasing residue deposition between the non-silenced and the silenced gun, where from respectively 7 inch and 6 inch the differences between defects from both are indistinguishable. The observations with the .22 shows decreasing patterns between the non-silenced and the silenced gun up to 4 inch, from where paradoxically the deposition increases up to 14 inch, and from there on, the defects between both are indistinguishable.

The aim of the research by Rodriguez-Pascual et al. [73], was to assess the possibility of using the distribution patterns of copper-containing GSR particles. In cases where lead-free ammunition is used, copper is unrelated to the primer compositions, and so the focus lies on the bullet, cartridge casing and the primer anvil as source of the copper. For this purpose, the copper concentration and distribution on the surface of white cotton T-shirts was scanned by means of Laser Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy, producing density maps (process time 140 min) showing the relative concentration of copper, and after using AI models, resulting in an estimation of the shooting distance. Three distances were evaluated (short – medium – long range) with 9x19 mm ammunition of three different lead-free brands. S&B, GFL and RUAG were tested at different shooting distances (8-15-20-50-75-100-140-200 cm). By means of SEM/EDX the percentage of copper containing GSR particles was determined in a radius of 5 cm around the entrance hole, reaching approximately 40%. A first initial estimation is the classification in short (8–25 cm) – medium (25–100 cm) and long range (100–200 cm), and secondly a % is given within this class to zoom in on the most probable distance: short (8–15–25 cm) – medium (25–50–75–100 cm) – long range (100–140–200 cm). GFL had a slightly higher concentration of copper in the primer cap and somewhat less in the cartridge case, while RUAG tended to have lower distance values due to the higher concentration of copper in its projectile. Up to 50 cm, the density of the copper distribution is the most obvious, but up to 200 cm there still is a distribution pattern visible. It is possible to detect other elements specific to each of the ammunitions in order to identify it: S&B (boron), RUAG (zinc) and GFL (aluminum). The estimation of the shooting distances was in all ranges correctly attributed.

The study by Ahuja et al. [74] focused on the morphology and the elemental composition of OGSR collected from different distances (muzzle end: 0–30 cm – trajectory: 100–150 cm – target: 470–500 cm), in an effort to evaluate the correlation with the distance by analyzing the IGSR on the surface of the OGSR. 9x19 mm FMJ KF Indian ammunition was used (mercury containing). Visual examination showed the morphological aspects using Micro Capture Plus software. There was a common trend observed: an increase in size of GSR and adherence of primer/bullet associated elements on the burned residue with an increasing distance.

The study [75] by Castillo Moreno et al. was prompted by the observation that the use of “less than lethal” ammunition sometimes unexpectedly causes lethal injuries. Injuries from these types of munition needed to be assessed to determine the possibility to distinguish them from injuries caused by traditional ammunition in order to possibly restrict their use. A Turkish blank firearm EKOL firat magnum was used for this purpose, with P.A.K. 9 mm rubber ball ammunition (headstamp Y.A.S.), and a porcine cadaver as the target. Several shooting distances were tested (contact shot–10 cm–60 cm–110 cm) on different parts of the pig (head–hind leg–ribs), so as to mimic the skeletal resemblance to human victims. It was found that at contact and short distances this ammunition does have bone fracturing capacities, although stellate defects were not observed due to the smaller chamber pressures generated by this ammunition. At 10 cm distance, soot deposition and at 60 cm a powder tattooing pattern were also observed.

The study by Brinkmeyer [76] evaluates the effect of different barrel lengths on GSR patterns when different models of the same revolver are fired at varying muzzle-to-target differences. White twill denim cloth was shot at from various distances (1–3–6–9–12–18–24–30–36–48 inch) with 4 S&W revolvers of different models (19-4; 19-3; 19-3; 27) having different barrel lengths (2.5–4–6–8.38 inch). Ammunition used was the .357 Magnum Remington (HP; flattened ball powder). Both Modified Griess and Sodium Rhodizonate methods were applied to the targets. It was confirmed that longer-barreled firearms produce tighter and more dense GSR patterns than shorter-barreled ones, as historically demonstrated in previous studies (comparing carabine rifle to revolver). Though the differences appear to be more subtle in this research. The fear for possible inaccurate distance estimation is moderately confirmed, as the pattern of the 2.5 inch barreled revolver at a distance of 18 inch mimics the pattern of the 8.38 inch barreled revolver at a distance of 24 inch.

The study by Martarelli et al. [77] integrated active infrared (IR) thermography with image processing techniques to visualize the relationship between shooting distance and spatial dispersion of gunshot residues. Different temperature variations were documented using a thermal camera with a temperature resolution up to 0.02 K (@25 °C). White cotton cloth was shot at different distances (5–10–15–20–25–30–35–40–50 cm) using a Beretta pistol (92SB) with 9x19 mm NATO ammunition (Fiocchi GFL). Images were acquired at 60 cm after heating the targets for 10 s with 4 IR halogen linear lamps (1 kW). This method is effective for distance estimations up to 30 cm.

The study on IR thermography by Honner et al. [78] focused on optimizing the thermal response of excited GSR, recorded by a FLIR camera through comparing two different heating methods: Flash-Pulse Thermography (FPT) (7μs) and Long-Pulse Thermography LPT (5 s), as well as different data processing approaches (maximum contrast, temperature derivation, TSR thermographic signal reconstruction, PPT pulse phase (Fourier transformation), PCT principal component and kurtosis analysis, which is a higher order statistic. For this purpose, 9 mm FMJ S&B was shot at cotton fabric at different distances (20–30–40–50–60 cm). Distances to 30 cm show circular patterns. Conclusion of the authors is that Flash-Pulse Thermography combined with kurtosis analysis yield the highest Contrast to Noise Ratio (CNR) and produces sharp, clear images of GSR.

The research by Brozek-Mucha et al. [79] studied the behavior of inorganic gunshot residues produced by Serbian ammunition in relation to the shooting distance. For this purpose, a Pietro Beretta model 70 pistol was fired using 7.65 mm FMJ PPU ammunition on white cotton cloth targets at different shooting distances (25–50–75–100–125 cm). The particles were collected within a 10 cm radius from the entrance hole, and analyzed by SEM/EDX to obtain data on numbers, chemical classes and sizes of deposited GSR particles. The elemental composition was determined as containing Pb–Ba–Sn–Sb, where tin originates from the discs that are used to seal the primer mixture in the cup. The frequency of occurrence was calculated as a function of the shooting distance, revealing the maximum incidence of barium-containing particles at 50 cm, lead particles becoming dominant at ranges greater than 75 cm, and the behavior of PbSb particles that shows the occurrence diminishing up to 75 cm and becoming dominant at 125 cm. The GSR distribution of particles with sizes between 0.75μm-5μm was studied. The frequency of occurrence as a function of the particle size showed an exponential fall. This data analysis allowed to estimate the shooting distance in a case study, where there was no weapon nor ammunition available, and where the damage in the victim's clothing gave no indication of contact or close range shot, to lie between 50 to 100 cm.

The aim of the study by Brozek-Mucha et al. [80] was to focus on the shooting distance determinations in cases where the litigious firearm and ammunition are not available to perform reference shootings. Therefore the effects of contact and close range shooting were examined on 6 different types of cotton textiles using traditional 9 mm ammunition (FMJ S&B). The focus was on the changes of the fibers within the textiles as well as the GSR distribution. These were chemically and morphologically examined using an optical microscope, SEM/EDX and IR. It was established that the appearance of the pattern and the distribution of the GSR (collected within a 5 cm radius around each defect) depends on the properties of the textiles (type, structure, density, thickness: e.g. woven or knitted). The contact/near contact shots produced multiple inorganic sub-micron spheres on the fibers collected from the edges of the entrance holes. For this purpose 6 cotton textiles were used in two different experiments. The first to study the patterns (shots were fired at 0–1–2–10–20–30 cm), and the second to study the effect on the damage of the fibers (contact shots were fired on woven and knitted cotton). The authors conclude that differences in type and density of the target material do produce variations of the damage caused by the same ammunition shot at the same distance from the target. This effect is also observed through the number of GSR particles observed on the target material (both on the entry and the exit side). These experiments corroborate well with observations made on real case samples where multiple layers of clothing and/or bedding material had been perforated. The authors therefore suggest that the interaction of GSR particles, hot gases and soot with the textile materials during close-range shooting should be investigated in detail and the results explored.

The study by Sen [81] demonstrated a huge difference in gunshot residue patterns deposited by supersonic and subsonic ammunitions, which may lead to wrong conclusions in shooting distance estimations. Subsonic ammunition is designed to reduce the acoustic signature produced by the discharge of a firearm and are often used in combination with a silencer. They have a lower velocity than the speed of sound due to a smaller amount of gunpowder, a slower burning rate and a lower gas pressure. A 9x19 mm TP9 Elite Combat Model Canik pistol was used to perform the test firings with 9x19 FMJ Sterling supersonic and subsonic ammunition. Muzzle velocity was measured by Lab Radar® V.1.3. The tested distances were 0–15–30–60–100 cm. After performing the Sodium Rhodizonate tests, GSR pattern areas were measured using ImageJ® Software, after which the data was subjected to a T-test with SPSS® Software 20th version. The most obvious differences were observed at distances of 15 and 30 cm, where the diameters of patterns were 24.5 cm vs 4.6 cm and 9 cm vs 1 cm respectively, with the subsonic ammunition producing the smallest patterns. The authors therefore recommend that shooting distance tests take into account the fact that subsonic ammunition might have been used and to make sure that test firings be performed with the same type of ammunition as used in the shooting incident.

Quality aspects

The Best Practice Manual for Chemographic methods [82] was published by ENFSI in 2015; there has been no revision since then. It provides a framework of procedures, quality principles, training processes and approaches to the forensic examination in the domain of shooting distance estimation.

A Best Practice Manual for the Forensic Examination of Inorganic Gunshot Residue by SEM/EDS [83] also provides a framework of procedures, quality principles, training processes and approaches to the forensic examination in the domain of the detection of microscopic IGSR particles using SEM/EDX. It received an update in May 2022. This work was funded by the European Union's Internal Security Fund – Police (ISF–P) in the framework of the Direct Grant offered by the EU Commission to ENFSI.

Case reports

A case report by Levy et al. [84] demonstrates how scene reconstruction may also benefit from the examination of inner layer clothing items. The scenario regards two victims of a shooting incident where one victim has an entrance wound in his clothing that didn't correspond with a shooting distance of 30 m, which was the estimated shooting distance based on entrance holes in the second victim's dress. This prompted a second hypothesis that the first victim accidently shot himself with his gun that was lodged in between his underwear and his pants. Inside this garment vaporous lead was revealed, thus corroborating the second theory. The procedures used in this case study were the DTO test for copper, Sodium Rhodizonate for lead and Griess Test for nitrites. The authors conclude that forensic science – as is formulated in the Sydney Declaration – is case-based, depending on logic reasoning and scientific knowledge. In the case of scene reconstruction it is dangerous to depend on following standard procedures alone, and the examiner should always engage in critical thinking while evaluating the possible scenario's and be open to alternatives.

A technical note by Azulay et al. [85] examines the possible influence on the distance estimation on victims that are also involved as shooters in a mutual shooting incident. More specifically, the possible contamination of victim's clothing by nitrites originating from the victim also shooting was evaluated to assess the final distance determination. For this purpose, 16 shooters fired 8x with a Jericho 941 SA pistol using 9 mm IMI ammunition, after which their T-shirts were removed and used as targets at different shooting distances: 50–75–100–125–150 cm. This group of results was then compared to T-shirts that were simply used as targets, and T-shirts that were merely worn inside the firing range prior to firing (blanks). The Griess test was used to visualize the total nitrite deposition on all the garments. Authors show that there was no interfering visual addition of nitrites on the targets of the victim/shooters, and no influence on the final distance determinations by the expert was observed.

In one case study by Geusens et al. [86] a shooting distance estimation of a birdshot with 9 mm Flobert is investigated. The (non-lethal) shot was fired at the back of the head of the victim and reference shots using the alleged weapon on a dummy, equipped with skin simulant and wig to simulate the circumstances of the shooting, were fired to estimate the shooting distance. The resulting pellet distribution was evaluated using the equivalent circle diameter method. Pre-examination of the victims’ clothing was performed under IR light using a Crime-Lite Auto camera. Subsequent colorimetric Sodium Rhodizonate testing revealed a pattern distribution that was compared with the patterns obtained on reference clothing. The estimated distance was in a range between 75 cm and 175 cm, whereas the victim stated to have been shot at 150 cm.

Bullet hole examination

The rotating bullet usually produces a wipe ring around the entry hole. The presence or absence of a wipe ring therefore will help to determine the nature of the bullet hole as an entry or exit site.

The aim of the study by Franceschetti et al. [87] is to explore the non-destructive X-Ray Fluorescence method as a screening tool for detecting gunshot residues on cremated and fragmented bone materials, to use in cases of charred human remains. It was established that GSR can survive the burning of completely skeletonized bones. To mimic a realistic situation, bovine ribs, still containing soft tissues, were shot at near contact range with 9 mm jacketed and unjacketed ammunition and subsequently burnt until completely calcined at 800 °C in an electric oven. GSR particles from unjacketed bullets prove to be well detectable in real cases, whereas particles from Full Metal Jacketed bullets prove to be more critical.

Time since discharge estimation

In forensic science, the analysis of gunshot exhausts from spent cartridge casings has been proposed for estimating the time since discharge and/or establishing chemical links with a specific bullet entry hole.

In [88] Gallidabino et al. tested the use of four commercially available Solid-Phase Micro-Extraction (SPME) fibres to assess the selectivity and the relative performances for the analysis of GSR exhausts from spent cartridge cases and the discrimination of different ammunition types analysed by GC-MS. Spent cartridge casings from four small arms ammunition types (.357 Geco – S&B – Samson – Magtech) were extracted by headspace SPME after discharge (from a Colt python revolver) using four different fibres and at two different extraction temperatures. The identities of peaks in the GC chromatograms were investigated by non-targeted analysis, and a total of 120 analytes were observed to be recurrent between samples. Amongst them, 113 could be labelled and identified as OGSR compounds. As the calibres used in this study correspond well to the ammunition predominantly used in criminal shootings, these results show a marked better usability to previous studies in which hunting (shot) ammunition was used to conduct experiments. Although these cartridges are larger, contain more powder mass, and are therefore better suited to detect residual exhaust, they were less interesting from a forensic point of view as these types of weapons/ammunition are but rarely used in (European) criminal cases.
