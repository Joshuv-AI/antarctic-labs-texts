PRMT1 drives oral squamous cell carcinoma progression by activating STAT3 and suppressing ferroptosis via GPX4
Authors: Huawei Liu, Chuyan Shi, Bing Liu, Bo Wei, Yingying Wu, Peng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) exhibits poor prognosis due to aggression, metastasis, and chemoresistance. Protein Arginine Methyltransferase 1 (PRMT1) and Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) are implicated in oncogenesis, but their interplay and downstream effects, particularly concerning ferroptosis and immune evasion in OSCC remain unclear.ResultsPRMT1 was significantly upregulated in OSCC tissues and cell lines, correlating with advanced grade, metastasis, and poor patient survival. PRMT1 knockdown inhibited OSCC proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and chemoresistance in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, PRMT1 directly interacted with, methylated, and activated STAT3 (increased p-STAT3 and target genes VEGFA, IL-6, c-myc). The PRMT1/STAT3 axis suppressed ferroptosis; PRMT1 knockdown decreased GPX4 expression, increased Fe2 + , ROS, and MDA, and decreased GSH, effects rescued by STAT3 overexpression. STAT3 directly bound and activated the GPX4 promoter. Crucially, inhibiting ferroptosis with liproxstatin-1 reversed the anti-tumor effects (chemosensitization, reduced proliferation/invasion) of PRMT1 knockdown. PRMT1 KO also enhanced anti-PD-1 therapy efficacy in vivo.ConclusionPRMT1 drives OSCC aggressiveness by methylating and activating STAT3. This axis promotes chemoresistance and oncogenic phenotypes primarily by suppressing ferroptosis through STAT3-mediated transcriptional upregulation of GPX4. Targeting PRMT1 represents a promising strategy to overcome chemoresistance and inhibit progression in OSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01506-7.Keywords: PRMT1, STAT3, Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), Ferroptosis, Chemoresistance

Background

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the most common type ofhead and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCC) and represents a significant global health burden with persistently poor clinical outcomes [1]. Despite multimodal therapeutic strategies incorporating surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy, the 5-year overall survival rate for patients with advanced OSCC remains low, at approximately 50% [2]. This poor prognosis is largely due to tendency of OSCC for aggressive local invasion, frequent spread to cervical lymph node metastasis, and the eventual development of distant metastatic disease [3]. Compounding these challenges is the frequent emergence of intrinsic or acquired resistance to standard-of-care cytotoxic agents like cisplatin, which significantly compromises treatment efficacy and facilitates tumor relapse [4]. Consequently, a deeper, mechanistic understanding of the molecular pathways governing OSCC progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance is imperative for the development of novel, more effective treatment paradigms.

Protein Arginine Methyltransferase 1 (PRMT1), the predominant mammalian enzyme catalyzing the formation of asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA) residues on substrate proteins and is increasingly recognized as a pivotal regulator in diverse oncogenic contexts [5]. Aberrant PRMT1 expression and activity have been linked to the dysregulation of cellular processes fundamental to cancer development and progression across various malignancies. For instance, recent investigations have highlighted PRMT1’s role in sustaining proliferative signaling pathways essential for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell growth [6]. PRMT1 can promote resistance to chemotherapy and exacerbate tumorigenesis in pancreatic cancer [6]. Its contribution to fostering epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) dynamics and enhancing the invasive potential of tumor cells has been recently elucidated in experimental models of lung cancer [7]. Intriguingly, emerging data also position PRMT1 as a modulator of the tumor immune microenvironment, potentially influencing anti-tumor immune surveillance mechanisms, although this remains understudied [8, 9]. While these studies underscore PRMT1’s multifaceted oncogenic roles, its specific regulatory functions and downstream effector pathways within the pathogenesis of OSCC require significant further investigation.

Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3), a critical transcription factor that is canonically activated downstream of cytokine and growth factor receptor signaling, is frequently observed to be constitutively hyperactivated in a broad spectrum of human cancers, including OSCC [10]. Within the context of OSCC, persistent STAT3 signaling activity has been mechanistically linked to the promotion of tumor cell proliferation and survival, often mediated through the transcriptional upregulation of target genes such as CCND1 (Cyclin D1) and BCL2L1 (Bcl-xL) [11, 12]. Beyond these cell-intrinsic effects, activated STAT3 plays a crucial role in orchestrating tumor invasion and metastatic dissemination in OSCC, partly by inducing the expression of genes involved in the EMT and matrix remodeling [11].

An emerging layer of complexity involves potential post-translational regulatory crosstalk impacting STAT3 function, including arginine methylation [13]. Indeed, previous biochemical studies have suggested a direct link between PRMT activity and STAT3 signaling pathways [14]. Nevertheless, the precise functional consequences of PRMT1-mediated STAT3 regulation specifically within the context of OSCC remain largely undefined. Critical questions persist regarding how this specific regulatory axis influences the intertwined processes of metastatic progression, responsiveness to chemotherapy response, host anti-tumor immunity, and emerging modes of regulated cell death such as ferroptosis.

Ferroptosis, a non-apoptotic form of regulated cell death driven by the iron-dependent accumulation of lipid peroxides, has recently emerged as a critical process influencing cancer cell fate and therapeutic response [15]. The selenoenzyme Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4) functions as a key negative regulator of ferroptosis by reducing lipid hydroperoxides [16]. Intriguingly, recent evidence has begun to connect STAT3 signaling pathways with the modulation of ferroptosis sensitivity in various cancer contexts, often through the direct or indirect regulation of GPX4 expression [17]. Simultaneously, enhancing anti-tumor immunity remains a paramount objective in OSCC immunotherapy is frequently subverted through mechanisms such as the upregulation of immune checkpoints like PD-1/PD-L1 and compromised CD8 + T cell effector function [18]. Given the established roles of STAT3 in orchestrating immune [19] and the demonstrated capacity of PRMT1 to modulate STAT3 activity, investigating whether the PRMT1/STAT3 axis serves as a central node coordinating both ferroptosis resistance and immune evasion represents a highly compelling and unexplored area in OSCC biology.

Based on this collective evidence and identified knowledge gaps, we hypothesized that PRMT1 functions as a critical upstream driver of OSCC aggressiveness by directly methylating and thereby activating STAT3. We further posited that this activated PRMT1/STAT3 signaling axis promotes a multi-faceted pro-tumorigenic state characterized by enhanced resistance to ferroptosis, potentially mediated via transcriptional upregulation of GPX4, and the facilitation of an immune-evasive microenvironment, ultimately culminating in increased metastatic propensity and resistance to chemotherapy. Therefore, the primary objectives of this study were to: (1) comprehensively evaluate PRMT1 expression patterns in OSCC clinical specimens and determine their association with clinicopathological variables and patient prognosis; (2) elucidate the functional impact of PRMT1 modulation on OSCC cell proliferation, invasion, metastatic colonization, and sensitivity to chemotherapy using both in vitro and in vivo models; (3) biochemically validate the direct interaction between PRMT1 and STAT3, confirm PRMT1-dependent STAT3 methylation, and assess the consequences for STAT3 activation; (4) investigate the downstream effects of the PRMT1/STAT3 axis on key components of the tumor immune microenvironment (e.g., CD8 + T cell infiltration, PD-1 expression) and on the cellular machinery governing ferroptosis, with specific focus on the STAT3-GPX4 regulatory link; and (5) functionally determine whether the suppression of ferroptosis mediated by the PRMT1/STAT3/GPX4 pathway is a critical mechanism underlying PRMT1-driven chemoresistance and tumor progression phenotypes in OSCC.

Methods

Bioinformatic analysis of PRMT1 expression and clinical correlation using TCGA data

Publicly available RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data (Level 3 HTSeq—Counts and Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads [FPKM]) and associated clinical information, including overall survival data, for the Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma (HNSC) cohort were accessed and downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Data processing and normalization pipelines adhered to TCGA standards. For differential expression analyses comparing tumor versus adjacent normal tissues, normalized expression values (e.g., Transcripts Per Million [TPM]) were used. Where appropriate for statistical testing, Log2 transformation (log2[TPM + 1] or log2[FPKM + 1]) was applied to the expression data to approximate a normal distribution.

The OSCC subset within of TCGA-HNSC cohort was identified based on primary tumor site annotations (e.g., oral cavity, tongue, floor of mouth) within the clinical data, and only these designated samples were included in the subsequent OSCC-specific analyses. To compare PRMT1 mRNA expression between tumor and adjacent normal tissues, we utilized publicly accessible online tools integrating TCGA data were employed, primarily the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) web server (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) and/or the UALCAN portal (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/).

Survival analysis

To assess the prognostic significance of PRMT1 expression in OSCC, survival analysis was conducted using the clinical follow-up data linked to the RNA-seq profiles of the TCGA-OSCC patient subset. This analysis utilized integrated tools within GEPIA or cBioPortal (http://www.cbioportal.org/), or was performed using custom scripts in R (version 3.6) with the survival and survminer packages. OSCC patients were stratified into “High PRMT1 expression” and “Low PRMT1 expression” groups. The stratification cutoff was determined by the median expression value across the OSCC cohort, though optimal cutoffs determined by the platform was considered. The specific cutoff method used (e.g., median, quartile) was noted from the analysis output. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to visualize the overall survival probability over time for the high- and low-PRMT1 expression groups. The statistical significance of the difference in overall survival between the two groups was assessed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered indicative of a statistically significant difference in survival outcomes associated with PRMT1 expression levels. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals were also recorded from the analysis tools.

Ethical approval and tissue sample collection

This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Chinese PLA General Hospital (S2025-018–01). The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients prior to sample collection. OSCC tissues, categorized by grade (Grade I, Grade II, Grade III) and lymph node metastasis status (with LN metastasis, without LN metastasis), along with adjacent non-cancerous tissues, were surgically procured from patients undergoing treatment at Chinese PLA General Hospital. Immediately following resection, tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored for subsequent molecular analyses.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Four-micrometer thick sections were prepared from paraffin-embedded blocks human OSCC tumor tissues. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol solutions. Heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed in an appropriate buffer solution. Following blocking of non-specific binding sites, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against PRMT1 (1:500 dilution; ab190892; Abcam, Shanghai, China). After washing, sections were incubated with an appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (ab6721, 1:1000 dilution; Abcam, Shanghai, China) for 2 h at room temperature. Visualization was achieved using a 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Stained sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Images were captured using a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). For immunohistochemical analysis of Vimentin and E-cadherin in xenograft tumors, similar procedures were followed using respective primary antibodies after tissue processing.

Cell lines and culture conditions

Human OSCC cell lines HN6, SCC25, Cal27, and SCC15, along with normal human oral keratinocytes (HOK), were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). All cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco Laboratories, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with an atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Plasmid construction and cell transfection

Expression vectors based on pcDNA3.1 for overexpressing PRMT1 (OE-PRMT1) and a corresponding empty vector control (OE-NC) were also obtained from GenePharma. For transient transfections, HN6 and SCC25 cells were seeded to reach appropriate confluency. Plasmids were transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for subsequent experiments.

Lentiviral shRNA knockdown and generation of stable cell lines

shRNA oligonucleotides targeting human PRMT1 and STAT3 (two independent hairpins per gene) and a non-targeting control (sh-NC) were cloned into pLKO.1-puro. Lentiviruses were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the shRNA vector and packaging plasmids (pLP1, pLP2, pLP/VSVG; Invitrogen) using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h, clarified by 0.45-µm filtration, supplemented with 4 µg/mL polybrene, and used to infect HN6 or SCC15 cells (two 24-h rounds, MOI ~ 2–5). Forty-eight hours after the final infection, cells were selected in puromycin (2 µg/mL) for 2–3 days to generate stable pooled populations. Knockdown efficiency was assessed by qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. S1a–b) and immunoblotting (Fig. 2a). For subsequent experiments we used sh-PRMT1-1 and sh-STAT3-2, which showed the strongest silencing, while results for both hairpins are reported to exclude off-target effects.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from cultured OSCC cells using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein (20–40 μg) were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk or bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. The primary antibodies used were: anti-PRMT1 (1:1000; ab190892; Abcam), anti-E-cadherin (1:1000; ab40772; Abcam), anti-N-cadherin (1:5000; ab76011; Abcam), anti-Vimentin (1:1000; ab92547; Abcam), anti-ADMA (Asymmetric dimethylarginine; 1:100; ab413; Abcam), anti-phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705) (p-STAT3; 1:2000; ab76315; Abcam), anti-Lamin B (1 µg/mL; ab232731; Abcam), anti-VEGFA (1 µg/mL; ab46154; Abcam), anti-IL-6 (1:1000; ab9324; Abcam), anti-c-myc (1:1000; ab32072; Abcam), anti-GPX4 (concentration not specified in draft, standard dilutions 1:1000), anti-GAPDH (concentration not specified, 1:5000–1:10,000), and anti-β-actin (1 µg/mL; ab8226; Abcam). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (e.g., ab7090, 1:2000; Abcam) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) and imaged using a suitable detection system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) or similar software, with GAPDH or β-actin serving as loading controls.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). HN6 and SCC25 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well. After adherence and appropriate treatments (e.g., varying concentrations of Doxorubicin (DOX) or Cisplatin (CDDP)), 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Dose–response curves were fitted using a four-parameter logistic regression (4PL) model in GraphPad Prism 9.0, and half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50 values, mean ± SD) were calculated from 3 independent experiments. Statistical significance between IC50 values of sh-NC and sh-PRMT1 groups was assessed using an extra-sum-of-squares F-test.

Colony-formation assay

Cells were trypsinized to single-cell suspensions and seeded in 6-well plates (500–1,000 cells/well) in complete medium. After 10–14 days (with medium changes every 3–4 days), colonies were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde, 15 min) and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (30 min), rinsed, air-dried, and colonies ≥ 50 cells were counted by two blinded observers. For quantification, colony counts per well were averaged across three biological replicates and analyzed by two-sided Student’s t-test (Supplementary Fig. S1c).

Cell proliferation assay (BrdU and EdU)

Cell proliferation was evaluated using Bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) incorporation. Cells were incubated with BrdU labeling solution for a specified period, 3 h. Subsequently, cells were fixed, permeabilized, and treated with DNase to expose incorporated BrdU. Detection was performed using an anti-BrdU antibody conjugated to a fluorophore, followed by counterstaining with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) to visualize nuclei. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Hilden, Germany), and the percentage of BrdU-positive cells relative to the total number of DAPI-stained cells was calculated.

For experiments related to ferroptosis rescue, cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell-Light EdU DNA Cell Proliferation Kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). Briefly, HN6 and SCC25 cells were incubated with 50 μM EdU solution for 2 h. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. EdU incorporation was detected by click chemistry using an Apollo dye solution according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. EdU-positive cells were visualized and quantified using fluorescence microscopy (Leica, Hilden, Germany).

Transwell invasion assay

Cell invasion capacity was measured using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size; Corning, NY, USA) coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). HN6 or SCC25 cells (approximately 5 × 104 to 1 × 105 cells) were resuspended in 200 μL of serum-free DMEM and seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 600 μL of DMEM containing 20% FBS as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 48 h at 37 °C, non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had invaded through the Matrigel and membrane to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution. Invaded cells were photographed and counted in several randomly selected fields under a microscope (Olympus Optical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

In Vivo xenograft tumor model

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Beijing Viewsolid Biotechnology Co. LTD. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). For subcutaneous xenografts, 1 × 106 transfected HN6 cells (e.g., sh-NC, sh-PRMT1, STAT3-WT, STAT3-KO) suspended in 100 μL PBS were injected into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor growth was monitored regularly by measuring tumor dimensions with calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Volume = (length × width2) / 2. For therapeutic studies, treatments began when tumors reached a palpable size. Treatment groups received intraperitoneal injections of Cisplatin (CDDP; 150 mg/kg, administered twice a week), anti-PD-1 antibody (200 μg per mouse, frequency specified if different), or MS023 (PRMT1 inhibitor; 80 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection, frequency specified if different), or saline control. After 28 days (or as specified), mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Tumors were excised, weighed, photographed, and processed for histological or molecular analysis. For metastasis studies, lung tissues were also collected at necropsy.

Lung metastasis assessment and hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining

Harvested lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Serial Sects. (4 μm thickness) were prepared and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) following standard protocols. The number of visible metastatic nodules on the lung surface was counted macroscopically before fixation, and/or microscopically on HE-stained sections. Representative images of lung histology were captured using a microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

To investigate protein interactions, Co-IP assays were performed using a Co-IP kit (Abison Biotechnology Co. Ltd, China). HN6 or SCC25 cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 (10 µM) for several hours before lysis to stabilize protein complexes. Cells were lysed in immunoprecipitation buffer. Cell lysates were clarified by centrifugation, and the supernatants were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the target protein (e.g., anti-STAT3 for detecting interaction with ADMA-modified proteins, or anti-HA/Flag for tagged proteins, or anti-PRMT1) or control IgG. Protein A/G agarose beads were added and incubated for another 2–4 h to capture antibody-protein complexes. Beads were extensively washed with lysis buffer. Immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer and analyzed by Western blotting using antibodies against the potential interaction partners (e.g., anti-ADMA, anti-STAT3, anti-PRMT1, anti-HA, anti-Flag). Input lysates were simultaneously analyzed to confirm the expression of target protein.

Flow cytometry analysis of immune cells and ROS

For analysis of immune cell in peripheral blood from treated mice, 100 μL of whole peripheral blood was collected. Red blood cells were lysed, and remaining leukocytes were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against surface markers: anti-CD3 (ab38483; Abcam), anti-CD8 (ab38483; Abcam). For intracellular staining, cell were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with an antibody against Granzyme B (GranB; ab38483; Abcam). Staining was performed in the dark for 30 min at 4 °C. After washing, cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer to quantify the percentages of CD3 + CD8 + T cells and GranB + cells within the CD8 + population. For the detection of intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), cultured cells were incubated with the ROS-sensitive fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (included in kit S0033S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation, cells were washed, harvested, and resuspended in PBS. Fluorescence intensity, which correlates with ROS levels, was measured using flow cytometry. Data analysis included measuring the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP assays were conducted using a commercial kit (Beyotime, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 293 T cells (or OSCC cells if applicable) were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Cross-linking was quenched with glycine. Cells were lysed, and chromatin was sheared into fragments of approximately 200–500 base pairs using sonication. The sheared chromatin was pre-cleared and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with an antibody against STAT3 or a control IgG antibody. Immune complexes were captured using Protein A/G agarose beads. After extensive washing, cross-links were reversed, and DNA was purified. The enrichment of specific GPX4 promoter regions (P1, P2, P3, and P4, defined by primer pairs) in the immunoprecipitated DNA was quantified by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) using specific primers for these regions. Results were normalized to input DNA.

Luciferase reporter assay

A putative promoter region of the human GPX4 gene containing predicted STAT3 binding sites (WT2 region: -1136 to -491), alongside a version with the binding sites mutated (MUT2), were cloned into the pGL3-Basic luciferase reporter vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The empty pGL3-Basic vector served as a negative control. 293 T cells were co-transfected with one of these GPX4 promoter-luciferase constructs along with a STAT3 expression vector (or an empty vector control) and a Renilla luciferase vector (pRL-TK) for normalization. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were lysed, and luciferase activities (Firefly and Renilla) were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity to control for transfection efficiency.

Measurement of ferrous iron (Fe2 +) levels

Intracellular labile ferrous iron (Fe2 +) levels were quantified using a commercial Iron Assay Kit (ab83366; Abcam, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cell lysates were prepared, and the assay utilizes a chromogen that reacts specifically with Fe2 + to produce a colored product. The absorbance was measured at the recommended wavelength using a microplate reader, and Fe2 + concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve.

Measurement of lipid peroxidation (MDA) and glutathione (GSH)

Levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), an indicator of lipid peroxidation, and glutathione (GSH), a key antioxidant, were measured in cell lysates or tissue homogenates using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. MDA levels were determined using the MDA assay kit (ab118970; Abcam, Shanghai, China), and GSH levels were measured using the GSH assay kit (ab65322; Abcam, Shanghai, China), strictly following the protocols provided by the manufacturer. Absorbance readings were taken using a microplate reader, and concentrations were determined by comparison to standard curves provided with the kits.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments or biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Software (version 9, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were made using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by an appropriate post-hoc test (e.g., Tukey’s test). Correlations were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. A P-value less than 0.05 (P < 0.05) was considered statistically significant, with specific P-values provided in the figures.

Results

PRMT1 is highly expressed in OSCC and associated with aggressive clinicopathological features

To investigate the role of PRMT1 in OSCC, we first examined its expression using publicly available data and clinical samples. Analysis of TCGA database revealed significantly higher PRMT1 mRNA expression levels in HNSC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1a), a finding that was confirmed specifically within the OSCC subset (Fig. 1b). Importantly, higher PRMT1 expression in OSCC patients correlated significantly with poorer overall survival (Fig. 1c), suggesting clinical relevance. We validated these findings at the protein level using IHC on OSCC patient samples. Analysis of primary OSCC lesions stratified by histological grade showed that PRMT1 levels progressively increased from normal tissue through Grade I–III tumors (Fig. 1d), reinforcing the association between PRMT1 upregulation and tumor aggressiveness. Furthermore, when stratified by nodal status, primary tumors from patients with pathologically confirmed cervical LN metastasis exhibited stronger PRMT1 staining than primary tumors from patients without LN metastasis (Fig. 1e). Consistent with the tissue data, Western blot analysis demonstrated markedly higher PRMT1 protein expression in various OSCC cell lines (Cal27, HN6, SCC15, SCC25) relative to normal HOK (Fig. 1f).

Collectively, these results establish that PRMT1 is overexpressed in OSCC, and its high expression is associated with advanced tumor stage, metastasis, and poor patient outcome.

PRMT1 promotes OSCC tumorigenesis and metastasis In Vitro and In Vivo

Given the elevated expression of PRMT1 in OSCC, we next sought to determine its functional role in OSCC progression using loss-of-function approaches. We efficiently knocked down PRMT1 expression in HN6 and SCC15 OSCC cell lines using sh-PRMT1, as confirmed by Western blot (Fig. 2a). Functionally, PRMT1 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation, as assessed by BrdU incorporation assays (Fig. 2b), and markedly reduced the invasive capacity of both HN6 and SCC15 cells in Transwell Matrigel assays (Fig. 2c). Consistent with a role in suppressing metastasis, silencing PRMT1 induced molecular changes characteristic of EMT, including upregulation of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and downregulation of the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin and Vimentin (Fig. 2d). To validate these findings in vivo, HN6 cells stably transfected with sh-NC or sh-PRMT1 were subcutaneously injected into nude mice. PRMT1 knockdown significantly suppressed xenograft tumor growth, resulting in smaller tumor volumes and lower tumor weights compared to control tumors (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, in an experimental metastasis model, mice injected with PRMT1-knockdown cells exhibited significantly fewer lung metastatic nodules compared to the control group (Fig. 2f).

Taken together, these data strongly indicate that PRMT1 functions as an oncogene in OSCC, promoting tumor growth and metastatic potential both in vitro and in vivo.

PRMT1 facilitates chemoresistance in OSCC

Chemoresistance is a major barrier in OSCC treatment. We investigated whether PRMT1 contributes to this phenotype. In vitro, knockdown of PRMT1 in HN6 and SCC15 cells significantly increased their sensitivity to the chemotherapeutic agent DOX, as indicated by a lower IC50 (HN6: 1.969 ± 0.182 vs 0.919 ± 0.109 μg/mL; SCC15: 1.275 ± 0.321 vs 0.823 ± 0.089 μg/mL; Fig. 3a). Nude mice bearing HN6 xenografts (sh-NC or sh-PRMT1) were treated with CDDP or saline. While CDDP treatment alone reduced tumor growth, the combination of PRMT1 knockdown and CDDP treatment resulted in a significantly greater suppression of tumor volume and weight compared to either CDDP treatment or PRMT1 knockdown alone (Fig. 3b). Examination of EMT markers in these xenograft tumors by IHC revealed that the combination treatment led to a more pronounced decrease in Vimentin expression and increase in E-cadherin expression compared to CDDP treatment in the control group (Fig. 3c).

PRMT1 interacts with, methylates, and activates STAT3 signaling

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying PRMT1’s functions, we investigated potential downstream effectors. Based on prior studies, we hypothesized an interaction with STAT3. Co-IP assays confirmed a physical interaction between exogenous HA-tagged STAT3 and Flag-tagged PRMT1 when co-expressed in cells (Fig. 3d, left panel). Furthermore, immunoprecipitation of endogenous STAT3 followed by immunoblotting for ADMA revealed that STAT3 is arginine-methylated, and this methylation was reduced upon PRMT1 knockdown (Fig. 3d, right panel). Functionally, modulating PRMT1 levels directly impacted STAT3 activation; PRMT1 knockdown decreased the levels of phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3 Tyr705) in nuclear extracts, whereas PRMT1 overexpression increased p-STAT3 levels (Fig. 3e). To rule out off-target effects of the STAT3 inhibitor, we combined PRMT1 overexpression with genetic STAT3 depletion. In both HN6 and SCC15, PRMT1 overexpression increased VEGFA, IL-6, and c-Myc, whereas either STAT3 inhibition or sh-STAT3 markedly blunted these inductions to near-baseline levels (Fig. 3f; sh-STAT3 validation in Supplementary Fig. S1b). These data indicate that PRMT1 drives this transcriptional program in a STAT3-dependent manner.

These findings demonstrate that PRMT1 interacts with, methylates, and enhances the activation of STAT3 signaling in OSCC cells.

PRMT1 regulates the immune microenvironment and ferroptosis via the STAT3/GPX4 Axis

Given STAT3’s role in immune modulation and cell death pathways, we explored these connections downstream of PRMT1. Analysis of TCGA data revealed a significant negative correlation between PRMT1 expression and CD8 + T cell infiltration levels in HNSC (Fig. 4a–b). Although the pan-cancer Spearman correlations between PRMT1 and immune features were modest in magnitude, the directions were consistent with an immunosuppressive role. Given the heterogeneity and purity confounders inherent to bulk-RNA analyses, small ρ values are expected. Importantly, xenograft experiments independently demonstrated increased CD4+/CD8+ infiltration and decreased PD-1 signal upon PRMT1 silencing, with partial reversal by STAT3 re-activation (Supplementary Fig. S2b–c), supporting functional relevance in OSCC.

Functionally investigating the STAT3 link in vivo, inhibition of STAT3 using MS023 in mice bearing STAT3-WT tumors led to an increase in peripheral CD3 + CD8 + T cells and their activation marker Granzyme B, an effect not observed in STAT3-KO tumors (Fig. 4c). Supporting the functional relevance of the PRMT1-PD-1 correlation, genetic knockout of PRMT1 in HN6 tumor cells significantly enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibody treatment in reducing xenograft tumor growth in vivo (Fig. 4d).

Next, we investigated the connection between PRMT1/STAT3 signaling and ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death. PRMT1 knockdown led to a significant decrease in the protein levels of GPX4 (Fig. 4e), a key negative regulator of ferroptosis. ChIP assays revealed that STAT3 directly binds to a specific region (P2) within the GPX4 promoter (Fig. 4f), and luciferase reporter assays confirmed that STAT3 overexpression activates transcription from the GPX4 promoter containing this site (Fig. 4g). Linking this axis functionally, PRMT1 knockdown induced hallmarks of ferroptosis, including increased intracellular labile iron (Fe2 +) (Fig. 4h), elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels (Fig. 4i), increased lipid peroxidation (MDA levels), and depleted antioxidants (GSH levels) (Fig. 4j). Critically, overexpression of STAT3 rescued these ferroptosis-inducing effects caused by PRMT1 knockdown (Fig. 4h, i, j).

These results collectively indicate that PRMT1, acting through STAT3, suppresses ferroptosis by maintaining GPX4 expression, and also modulates the tumor immune microenvironment potentially via STAT3-dependent mechanisms and PD-1 regulation.

PRMT1-mediated suppression of ferroptosis contributes to OSCC aggressiveness and chemoresistance

Finally, to directly test whether the suppression of ferroptosis mediates the pro-tumorigenic and chemoresistance functions of PRMT1, we performed rescue experiments using the specific ferroptosis inhibitor liproxstatin-1 (lipro). As expected, lipro treatment effectively reversed the induction of ferroptosis markers (increased MDA, decreased GSH) caused by PRMT1 knockdown in HN6 cells (Fig. 5a). Importantly, inhibiting ferroptosis with Lipro significantly counteracted the effects of PRMT1 silencing on OSCC cell phenotype. lipro treatment reversed the enhanced sensitivity to Doxorubicin conferred by PRMT1 knockdown (Fig. 5b), rescued the inhibited cell proliferation (Fig. 5c), restored the impaired invasive capacity (Fig. 5d), and reversed the MET-like changes in EMT marker expression (decreased E-cadherin, increased N-cadherin and Vimentin) (Fig. 5e).

RMT1 knockdown remodels the tumor immune microenvironment in vivo

We characterized the immune contexture of our xenografts. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumors from the sh-NC and sh-PRMT1 groups (HN6 model) were stained for CD8 and CD4, and positive cells were quantified in five random 20 × fields per tumor under blinded conditions. PRMT1 silencing significantly increased intratumoral CD8+ T-cell density, with a more modest increase in CD4+ cells (Supplementary Fig. S2a–c). Consistent with a less exhausted phenotype, the proportion of PD-1+ cells within the infiltrates was reduced in sh-PRMT1 tumors (Supplementary Fig. S2c). Pharmacologic modulation of STAT3 partially reversed these effects, supporting that PRMT1 promotes an immunosuppressive milieu via STAT3 signaling (Supplementary Fig. S2b, c). These findings complement our transcriptomic analyses (Fig. 2) and indicate that PRMT1 depletion not only restrains tumor growth but also favors a T-cell–permissive tumor microenvironment.

Discussion

The complex molecular landscape governing OSCC progression presents significant therapeutic hurdles, underscoring the need for a deeper understanding of its core driving mechanisms. In this study we illuminate a critical, therapeutically actionable pathway centered on PRMT1. Our comprehensive analysis establishes PRMT1 as a key oncogenic driver in OSCC, evidenced by its pronounced overexpression in patient tissues, which significantly correlates with advanced tumor grade, nodal metastasis, and inferior overall survival. This clinical association is consistent with recent reports detailing PRMT1’s upregulation and prognostic significance in other challenging malignancies, such as esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, where high PRMT1 levels similarly predicted poorer outcomes [20]. Moreover, our functional investigations rigorously substantiate this clinical correlation; targeted PRMT1 knockdown markedly impaired OSCC cell proliferation in vitro and dramatically curtailed xenograft tumor growth in vivo. This anti-proliferative effect upon PRMT1 inhibition mirrors findings in glioblastoma models where PRMT1 silencing similarly arrested cell cycle progression, [21] suggesting a conserved role for PRMT1 in maintaining the proliferative drive across diverse cancer types, including OSCC.

Furthermore, our data position PRMT1 as a key facilitator of the metastatic cascade in OSCC. PRMT1 silencing effectively suppressed OSCC cell invasion in vitro and significantly reduced the incidence of lung metastases in our in vivo model. Mechanistically, this anti-metastatic effect correlated with a reversal of EMT markers, specifically the upregulation of E-cadherin and downregulation of N-cadherin and Vimentin. This finding is particularly pertinent given recent studies in hepatocellular carcinoma demonstrating that PRMT1 can directly methylate and stabilize Snail, a master EMT-inducing transcription factor, thereby promoting invasion and metastasis [22]. While we did not investigate Snail methylation directly, our results strongly suggest PRMT1 engages EMT programs, likely via STAT3 as discussed below, to enhance the metastatic competence of OSCC cells. In parallel, we identified PRMT1 as a significant contributor to chemoresistance, a major clinical obstacle. PRMT1 knockdown rendered OSCC cells more susceptible to both Doxorubicin and Cisplatin, aligning with observations in ovarian cancer where PRMT1 inhibition resensitized resistant cells to platinum-based chemotherapy [23]. This chemo-sensitizing effect further solidifies the potential of PRMT1’s as a therapeutically relevant target in OSCC.

A central contribution of our study is the elucidation of STAT3 as a direct downstream effector regulated by PRMT1-mediated arginine methylation. While constitutive STAT3 activation is a recognized hallmark of OSCC pathogenesis, often attributed to upstream receptor signaling, [11] our work unveils a novel regulatory layer wherein PRMT1-mediated arginine methylation serve as a distinct, potentially synergistic mechanism to amplifying STAT3’s functional output. Indeed, the marked reduction in STAT3 phosphorylation (Tyr705) and target gene expression (VEGFA, IL-6, c-myc) upon PRMT1 knockdown strongly indicates that methylation positively regulates STAT3 activity. This resonates with structural and biochemical insights suggesting arginine methylation can influence protein conformation and interaction interfaces, [24] potentially facilitating STAT3 dimerization, nuclear translocation, or recruitment of co-activators. The specific upregulation of targets such as VEGFA and IL-6 is particularly significant, as a comprehensive study by Jin (2020) highlighted the role of the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway in the induction of EM [25]. Our data thus implicate PRMT1 as a key upstream regulator that amplifies the oncogenic output of this crucial STAT3 signaling node in OSCC.

The functional consequences of this PRMT1-STAT3 axis extend into the complex interplay between the tumor and its microenvironment, impacting both immune surveillance and ferroptotic cell death pathways. Our finding that high PRMT1 expression correlates inversely with CD8 + T cell infiltration and positively with PDCD1 (PD-1) expression is consistent with reports demonstrating STAT3’s capacity to directly induce PD-L1 expression and suppress T cell effector functions [26]. The enhanced efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy observed upon PRMT1 depletion in our model provides compelling preclinical evidence that targeting PRMT1 could synergize with immune checkpoint blockade, potentially by alleviating STAT3-mediated immune suppression. This possibility warrants dedicated investigation in immunocompetent OSCC models.

Simultaneously, our study uncovers a novel regulatory link connecting PRMT1/STAT3 signaling to ferroptosis resistance via GPX4. The observation that PRMT1 knockdown diminishes GPX4 levels and induces hallmarks of ferroptosis is significant, as the maintenance of GPX4 activity is crucial for preventing lethal lipid peroxidation [27, 28]. By demonstrating that STAT3 directly binds the GPX4 promoter to drive its transcription positions, we establish GPX4 as a bona fide STAT3 target gene mediating ferroptosis protection. This specific STAT3-GPX4 regulatory axis has recently been validated in other cancer contexts, where its disruption was shown to sensitize cells to ferroptosis-inducing therapies [17]. Our work uniquely identifies PRMT1 as the upstream activator of this protective STAT3/GPX4 pathway in OSCC, thereby forging a direct mechanistic link between arginine methylation and the regulation of ferroptosis.

The functional centrality of this anti-ferroptotic mechanism to PRMT1’s overall oncogenic program was powerfully demonstrated by rescue experiments using the ferroptosis inhibitor liproxstatin-1. The capacity of this ferroptosis inhibitor to significantly reverse the phenotypic effects of PRMT1 knockdown – including increased chemosensitivity, reduced proliferation, impaired invasion, and EMT modulation – strongly supports the conclusion that the active suppression of ferroptosis via the STAT3/GPX4 pathway is a key effector mechanism downstream of PRMT1. This finding resonates with the growing appreciation that inhibiting ferroptosis is a strategy employed by cancer cells to survive stress conditions and resist therapy [29] and highlights the PRMT1/STAT3 axis as a critical regulator of this survival strategy in OSCC.

Despite the robustness of our findings, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The reliance on established cell lines and nude mouse xenografts, while standard, necessitates future validation in more complex preclinical models like patient-derived organoids or genetically engineered mouse models capturing OSCC heterogeneity and immune interactions more faithfully. Furthermore, a direct assessment of STAT3 methylation stoichiometry at specific arginine residues using advanced proteomics would provide definitive biochemical validation. Furthermore, while STAT3 is clearly a key target, identifying the broader cohort of PRMT1 substrates in OSCC through methylome profiling could reveal parallel pathways contributing to the observed phenotypes. For example, PRMT1 is known to methylate histone tails, influencing chromatin structure and gene expression globally [30, 31] and these epigenetic effects likely cooperate with direct STAT3 methylation.

The therapeutic implications of our findings are substantial. The development and clinical testing of specific, potent PRMT1 inhibitors are currently progressing for various cancers [32]. Our data provide a strong rationale for evaluating such inhibitors in OSCC preclinical models, both as single agents and, perhaps more effectively, in combination regimens. Given the multifaceted effects downstream of PRMT1/STAT3, combining PRMT1 inhibition with standard chemotherapy (like cisplatin), immune checkpoint inhibitors (like anti-PD-1), or potentially even ferroptosis-inducing agents could yield synergistic anti-tumor activity. Furthermore, biomarker development based on PRMT1 expression or potentially STAT3 methylation status could help stratify OSCC patients most likely to benefit from PRMT1-targeted therapies.

Conclusions

In summary, this investigation delineates a PRMT1–STAT3–GPX4 axis that links arginine methylation to immune regulation in OSCC. By facilitating STAT3 activation, PRMT1 sustains STAT3-dependent cytokines (e.g., IL-6, VEGFA) and antioxidant defenses (GPX4), creating a low-oxidative, immunosuppressive niche. PRMT1 inhibition dampens STAT3 activity, reduces GPX4, increases lipid peroxidation, and permits effector CD8+ T-cell infiltration. While our xenograft model demonstrates causality via STAT3 re-activation, future studies in immunocompetent systems will be important to generalize these mechanisms and to dissect additional PRMT1 substrates that may contribute to immune modulation. Our findings not only deepen the fundamental understanding of OSCC molecular mechanisms but also strongly advocate for the exploration of PRMT1 inhibition as a novel and potentially multi-pronged therapeutic strategy to combat this challenging malignancy.
