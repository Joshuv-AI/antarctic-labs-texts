Infrared Object Detection with Ultra Small ConvNets: Is ImageNet Pretraining Still Useful?
Srikanth Muralidharan, Heitor R. Medeiros, Masih Aminbeidokhti, Marco Pedersoli

Abstract
Many real-world applications require recognition models that are robust to different operational conditions and modalities, but at the same time run on small embedded devices, with limited hardware. While for normal size models, pre-training is known to be very beneficial in accuracy and robustness, for small models, that can be employed for embedded and edge devices, its effect is not clear. In this work, we investigate the effect of ImageNet pretraining on increasingly small backbone architectures (ultra-small models, with < < 1M parameters) with respect to robustness in downstream object detection tasks in the infrared visual modality. Using scaling laws derived from standard object recognition architectures, we construct two ultra-small backbone families and systematically study their performance. Our experiments 1 1 1 The code will be available online after acceptance. on three different datasets reveal that while ImageNet pre-training is still useful, beyond a certain capacity threshold, it offers diminishing returns in terms of out-of-distribution detection robustness. Therefore, we advise practitioners to still use pre-training and, when possible avoid too small models as while they might work well for in-domain problems, they are brittle when working conditions are different.

Since the advent of deep learning, one key approach to add robustness to models is to use a pre-training phase
[
9
,
8
]
, in which a model is trained on a large body of data, different than the final task. With large models, this approach helps to bootstrap the model with useful and general features, which will then be refined in a second phase of fine-tuning, on a smaller dataset on the specific task and data distribution.
This approach is powerful because the expensive pre-training can be done once, and then the same model is fine-tuned for many different tasks. In addition, even when the data distribution and the task of the final model are different from the pre-training data, the final model still gains form it
[
18
,
44
]
.
This gain is mostly because deep learning models are trained with a non convex objective and they have millions of parameters to learn. Thus, during training, the optimization can find multiple local minima solutions. With pre-training, we make sure the fine-tuining phase starts from a good initialization in which the model has already seen many data points, such that the final solution will be better and more robust
[
11
]
.

While pre-training is well-known to help for large deep-learning models, we also know that for linear models and common losses (e.g. cross-entropy), pre-training is not needed
[
1
]
because the optimization is convex and we will obtain always the same solution, independently from the initialization.
In this work we analyse the effect of pre-training when using very small, but still non-linear models (ultra-small), in which the number of parameters is very low (
<1
M). Table
1
provide a summary of different ranges of computational resources and memory needed for different kind of applications and its associated models in the extended EfficientNet family. As normally the number of samples should be somewhere proportional to the number of data-points
[
16
]
, we expect that when the number of parameters is small enough, pre-training will stop to be useful. Thus, in this work, we characterize the effect of pre-training for performance generalization when reducing the model size. We believe that this is an important questions that has been overlooked by the vision community. In fact, by knowing in which situation and how much pre-training helps a small model to better generalize, it can help practitioners and industrial design to make a more appropriate choice in the trade-off between model size, performance and generalization.

More in detail, this study focus on object detection, which is one of the most fundamental tasks in computer vision
[
9
,
32
,
8
]
, because it allows the model to not only recognize and object, but also find its position in an image, which is very important for certain tasks, such as autonomous driving or robotics. Furthermore, we specialize our research to infrared data, as it is one of the most important modalities on embedded device, as it allows night vision while keeping most of the visual information needed for image understanding
[
36
]
.

The main contributions of our work can be summarized as follows:

•

(1)
We analyze for the first time the effect of RGB images (ImageNet) pre-training with ultra small infrared object detection models.

•

(2)
We evaluate two well-known families of convolutional models (EfficientNet and MobileNet), on three different infrared datasets, by scaling down the size of the models and testing their resilience to generalization with or without pre-training.

•

(3)
With the support of important ablations on the amount of data and resolution for the fine-tuning phase, our empirical evidence show that pre-training is important for the generalization of very small models, but when the size becomes too small (
<
100k params), pre-training has negative or no effect on generalization performance.

2
Related Work

Object Detection.
Unlike classification, which focuses only on identifying object categories, object detection requires both classification and precise localization within an image. Deep learning-based detectors are typically categorized into two-stage and one-stage methods. Two-stage detectors, such as Faster R-CNN
[
34
]
, employ a Region Proposal Network (RPN) to generate candidate regions, enabling accurate detection with reasonable efficiency. One-stage detectors, like YOLO
[
33
]
and FCOS
[
41
]
, eliminate the proposal step, resulting in faster inference. More recently, detectors incorporate language in their components, such as Grounding DINO
[
24
]
and YOLO-World
[
5
]
. Although this leads to an increase in performance, the efficiency is compromised, especially for edge devices. Therefore, different detectors suitable for edge devices have been explored, for instance, NanoDet
[
31
]
, which proposes a FCOS-style one-stage anchor-free detection model capable of having a good tradeoff between inference speed and memory for hardware-constrained edge devices.

Pretraining for Object detection.
Pretraining is a common practice in object detection, significantly boosting performance, especially for larger models
[
9
,
32
]
. It has been shown to accelerate convergence and enhance accuracy when transferring to detection tasks with limited training data
[
9
,
18
]
. Traditionally, ImageNet pretraining has been the standard, providing robust features that transfer well to various downstream tasks. Recent studies have explored self-supervised pretraining
[
3
,
12
]
to further improve downstream performance. However, these works predominantly focus on large or standard-scale models. The impact of pretraining on ultra-small models, particularly those with backbone size up to two orders of magnitude smaller than EfficientNet-B0
[
39
]
, remains underexplored. Additionally, the effect of pretraining on generalizing to out-of-domain detection tasks is less studied. In this work, we analyze supervised ImageNet pretraining for ultra-small convolutional networks in out-of-domain infrared object detection settings, highlighting the benefits and limitations in resource-constrained scenarios.

Out-of-distribution Robustness.

Robustness to distribution shifts remains a critical challenge in object detection, particularly in safety-critical applications. Prior works have studied domain adaptation
[
37
]
, data augmentation
[
7
,
45
]
, and self-training
[
48
]
to improve generalization across domains. Recent efforts focus on detecting out-of-distribution images
[
43
]
and improving model calibration under distribution shift
[
30
]
. While these approaches typically employ large backbone networks to enhance robustness, the behavior of ultrasmall models under OOD settings is underexplored. Our work investigates the OOD robustness of object detection models that are orders of magnitude smaller than conventional architectures, addressing the need for efficient yet robust detectors in resource-constrained deployment scenarios.

Efficient ConvNets.
Different methods were proposed to focus on efficiency and low-cost CNNs. Methods like MobileNetV1
[
14
]
leveraged depth-wise separable convolutions with adjustable complexity through width and resolution multipliers, while MobileNetV2
[
38
]
introduced inverted residual layers and linear bottlenecks to further preserve information flow and reduce computation. More recently, models such as MobileNetV3
[
13
]
and MnasNet
[
40
]
have employed Neural Architecture Search (NAS), explicitly incorporating latency as an optimization criterion. Alternative approaches, including ShuffleNet
[
47
]
and GhostNet
[
10
]
, introduced novel convolutional operations, such as channel shuffling, group convolutions, and Ghost modules, to significantly reduce parameters and computational cost. Additionally, transformer-based methods such as MobileViT
[
28
]
and TinyViT
[
42
]
have effectively integrated global context processing into compact architectures, demonstrating superior accuracy and transferability across multiple vision tasks.
Even if those model families are efficient and thought for embedded devices, they still contain millions of parameters and cannot be used directly on micro-controllers that are commonly used in industry (see Tab.
1
). For this reason, for our experiments, we extend two families of models (EfficientNet and MobileNetV3) to much smaller sizes down to the order of 10k parameters.

Method

3.1
Preliminaries

Object Detection.

The training set for OD is denoted as
\mathcal{D}=\{(x,Y)\}
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{W\times H\times C}
represents an image in the dataset, with dimensions
W\times H
and
C
channels. Subsequently, the OD model aims to identify
N
regions of interest within these images, denoted as
Y=\{(b_{i},c_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
. The top-left corner coordinates and the width and height of the object define each region of interest
b_{i}
. Additionally, a classification label
c_{i}
is assigned to each detected object, indicating its corresponding class within the dataset. In terms of optimization, the primary goal of this task is to maximize detection accuracy, often measured using the average precision (AP) metric across all classes. An OD is formally represented as the mapping
f_{\theta}:\mathbb{R}^{W\times H\times C}\to\hat{Y}
, where
\theta
denotes the parameter vector. To effectively train a detector, a differentiable surrogate for the AP metric, referred to as the detection cost function,
\mathcal{C}_{det}(\theta)
, is employed. The typical structure of such a cost function involves computing the average detection loss over dataset
\mathcal{D}
, denoted as
\mathcal{L}_{det}
, described as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{det}(\theta)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{(x,Y)\in\mathcal{D}}\mathcal{L}_{det}(f_{\theta}(x),Y).\end{split}

Model family generation.

We systematically construct ultra-small object detectors for non-RGB modalities by downscaling two backbone networks, EfficientNet-B0
[
39
]
and MobileNetV3-small
[
13
]
. Specifically, we reduce both the width and depth of these models up to seven levels in case of EfficientNet down EfficientNet-B0 and six levels down MobileNet-V3 small while maintaining input resolution, which is critical for object detection performance. Details of our downscaling procedure are provided in the supplementary material.

3.2
Proposed robustness evaluation pipeline

Figure
2
presents an overview of our framework. The pipeline comprises three main steps: (1) initializing the detector backbone either with ImageNet pre-training or random initialization, (2) training the detector on in-domain data, and (3) evaluating out-of-domain robustness to compare the effectiveness of ImageNet pre-training against random initialization.

Supervised ImageNet Pre-training.

Supervised pre-training on ImageNet
[
6
]
has been a standard approach to initialize convolutional neural networks for various downstream tasks. In this setting, models are trained on the ImageNet-1K dataset, which contains approximately 1.28 million training images across 1000 object categories. The objective is to minimize the cross-entropy loss between the predicted class probabilities and the ground truth labels:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\log\hat{y}_{i},

(2)

where
N
is the number of classes,
y_{i}
is the one-hot encoded ground truth label, and
\hat{y}_{i}
is the predicted softmax probability for class
i
.

The pre-training process results in models with weights that capture generic visual representations, which are transferable to downstream tasks such as object detection, including adaptation to different modalities. We adopt this standard supervised pre-training to initialize our Object Detector backbones to compare them against randomly initialized backbone weights.

Fine-tuning Object Detectors.

We study supervised object detection using data from an in-domain distribution
\mathcal{D}{in}={(x_{in},Y_{in})}
, where
x_{in}\in\mathbb{R}^{W_{in}\times H_{in}\times C}
denotes an image with spatial dimensions
W_{in}\times H_{in}
and
C
channels. Our goal is to evaluate the robustness of object detectors when tested on an out-of-domain distribution
\mathcal{D}_{out}
, comparing models initialized with random weights versus Imagenet pre-trained weights, and examining how model size affects this robustness.
We train two versions of each detector:

•

Random initialization:
All layers are initialized from scratch without any pre-training.

•

ImageNet pre-training:
Backbone networks are initialized with Imagenet pre-trained weights as described in Section
3.2
, while the rest of the detector is randomly initialized.

Both variants are trained using supervised in-domain data following the procedure in Section
3.1
. This ensures that the detectors learn representations useful for both in-domain and OOD Object Detection.

Robustness Evaluation.

We assess the relationship between model size and robustness of Imagenet pre-training on two different ways. First, we assess modality robustness where a detector is fine-tuned on RGB images and then evaluated on infrared data. The second evaluation involves a dataset with multiple cameras/views. In this case, the fine-tuning is performed on some viewpoints, while the evaluations is run on other viewpoints, not seen during fine-tuning.

(a) Datasets:
For the pretraining of the backbones we use
ImageNet
. ImageNet dataset consists of approximately 1.28 million training images and

For Object Detection, we explored two public RGB/IR benchmarking datasets: LLVIP and FLIR, as well as our inhouse Distech dataset which is fully in IR domain.
LLVIP:
The LLVIP dataset is a surveillance dataset composed of

FLIR ALIGNED:

For both these datasets, to assess robustness, we train our detectors with RGB domain images and test robustness on IR domain images using IR domain images as OOD test set in this case.
DISTECH:
The third dataset we conduct our robustness study is our in-house dataset known as Distech dataset. This dataset consists of images obtained from 30 overhead infrared domain cameras from multiple rooms. We split the data into trainval and test where the latter data is fully separate from trainval images in terms of rooms, the domains in our case. We use images from six cameras that is setup in a room entirely different from the rest of the cameras for test split and the images from rest the 24 cameras for the trainval split. We have in total 4177 images for training, 1045 images for validation and 1467 images in our OOD test set.

(b) ImageNet Pre-training Details:
For the ImageNet pretraining, we pretrain our EfficientNet model families as well as MobileNet-v3 model families using FFCV framework

]
. We train for
32

0.05

We adopt the single stage FCOS sytle object detector from Nanodet
[
31
]
for all our experiments. For LLVIP and FLIR datasets, we adopt a PANet

]

For data augmentation, we apply horizontal flip with a probability of

Modality Adaptation (RGB to Infrared):

In Fig.
3
we present generalization results on FLIR and LLVIP datasets with several ultra-small EfficientNet and MobileNet models. Here the task is to perform infrared detection with a model fine-tuned on RGB images of the same dataset. In this way, we make sure that the gap is only due to the different modality (RGB-Infrared) and no other changes of distributions are involved.
From those experiments, we see a clear trend in which larger models (B0 to B-3 and S to S-2) obtain a clear advantage from ImageNet pre-training on both datasets. This follows our intuition that pre-training helps until a certain model size. When the model becomes very small, pre-training does not help as the limited number of parameters cannot learn useful features from pre-training.

Domain Adaptation (different viewpoints):

In this experiment we evaluate the generalization performance of our models on Distech data with or without pre-training, when fine-tuning on a set of viewpoints, and testing on other, unseen viewpoints.
As in previous experiments, Fig.
6
reports the generalization performance with our two families of approaches (EfficientNet and MobileNet).
In this case, we cannot see a clear trend in performance when varying the model size. We believe this is due to the fact that in this case the domain gap from different point of view is smaller than a change of modality. Thus, when the domain gap is small, the effect of pre-training is not noticeable. A similar trend is also observable on the other datasets for in-domain data, where fine-tuning and evaluation are performed on the same data distribution (see supplementary material).

In conclusion, ImageNet pre-training effectively trades annotation effort for performance: even very small backbones equipped with these weights can rival or surpass larger models trained from scratch. When labels are scarce or deployment budgets are tight, combining a compact backbone with pre-training is the most efficient strategy. Only when plentiful RGB annotations are available does a randomly initialised model begin to close the gap, and even then, it generally falls several points short of its pre-trained counterpart.

The experiments show that enlarging the ImageNet crop pays dividends only when the detector is later trained and run on comparably large LLVIP frames. The absolute best scores are achieved with the 384/640 pairing (384-px pre-training, 640-px fine-tuning), but this setting quadruples the inference FLOPs compared to a 320-px baseline. To strike a more practical balance, we adopt 384-px crops during pre-training, while capping the LLVIP resolution at 480-px for both fine-tuning and inference. This 384 / 480 configuration retains nearly all of the accuracy gain delivered by high-resolution features yet keeps runtime cost well below that of the 640-px system, giving us the most favourable accuracy-per-compute trade-off.

Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the impact of pre-training in ultra-small convNets backbones for robust infrared object detection. We developed a family of ultra-small detectors based on scaling laws, which produce models able to run in edge devices with memory constraints.
Our results show that for modality generalization, when fine-tuning a model on rgb images and then evaluating on infrared images, ImageNet pre-training is valuable if the size of the model is higher than 100k parameters.
