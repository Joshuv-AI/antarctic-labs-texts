Tóth’s buses and the ‘‘detachment process’’
János Engländer

Abstract
This paper introduces the detachment process , a novel, time-inhomogeneous Markov process inspired by I. P. Tóth’s problem [ 11 ] concerning the number of ‘‘lonely passengers’’ (those without companions) when n n passengers are seated independently and uniformly in k k initially empty buses. Tóth showed that this number is stochastically non-decreasing in k k for fixed n n (see also Haslegrave’s work [ 4 ] ). The new model as dynamical version; main concepts: We extend Tóth’s model by treating the number of buses k k as a time parameter. Specifically, for a fixed number of passengers n n , the state of our Markov process at time k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 is exactly Tóth’s configuration ( n , k ) (n,k) . (We formally extend the process definition for all t ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ) t\in[1,\infty) .) These processes can be coupled for all n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 , and this larger coupled process is what we dub the detachment process . Our investigation focuses on properties related to detachment, clumping, the number of lonely passengers and of non-empty buses. The central notion is detachment , which occurs at time k k if every passenger occupies a distinct bus; we say the process is in a state of detachment at k k . A detachment time k k is when the process transitions from a non-detached state at k − 1 k-1 to a detached state at k k . 1.1
The
n
-detachment process

In this note we revisit I. P. Tóth’s model
[
11
]
and consider the number of buses as the time parameter; hence, for each
n\geq 1
we create a process for which its state at time
k\geq 1
is exactly Tóth’s model with parameters
n,k
.

Definition 1.1

(The
n
-detachment process)
.

We start with
X_{1}
which is the Tóth model with n passengers and
k=1
bus.
At time
k\geq 2
each passenger (sitting currently in one of
k-1
buses), independently, has the

chance to relocate to the
newly available
k
th bus. A bit more formally, we can say that the process is a measure-valued ‘‘occupancy process’’ on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\{1,2,...\}
and the mass on

is the number of passengers in bus number
z
.
We call
X_{1},X_{2},...
the
n-
detachment process
.

Convention:
We extend the definition of the
n
-detachment process from integer times to positive real times so that at time
t>1
the state of the process is the same as at time
\lfloor t\rfloor
. This way we avoid the frequent use of the integer value sign.

Remark 1.2
.

By a trivial inductive argument, the state

X_{k}
is equivalent to the Tóth model with
(n,k)
. That is, the one dimensional marginals of the
n
-detachment process are the Tóth models for the given
n
and
k=1,2,...
. (In terms of ‘‘occupancy problems,’’ we have the Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics with
n
balls and
k

\hfill\diamond

We continue with definitions.

Definition 1.3
.

A passenger is called
lonely
if there are no other passengers in the bus where the passenger is sitting. Of course, being lonely depends on time.
Let
L_{k}
denote the number of lonely passengers at time
k
; let

denote the number of nonempty buses in
X_{k}
, or the ‘‘support size’’ of
X_{k}
.

Definition 1.4

(Time of first and of permanent detachment)
.

Let
\tau^{(n)}
denote the ‘‘start of permanent detachment,’’ (
=
last detachment time) that is,

\tau^{(n)}:=\min\{k\geq 1\mid L_{k+j}=n,\ \forall j\geq 0\}.

The first detachment time is defined as

\hat{\tau}^{(n)}:=\min\{k\geq 1\mid L_{k}=n\}.

Although
\tau^{(n)}
is a priori
[0,\infty]
-valued, later we will prove that it is a.s. finite-valued (hence
\hat{\tau}^{(n)}
too).
Note that
\tau^{(n)}=\min\{k\geq 1\mid N_{k+j}=n,\ \forall j\geq 0\}
holds too.

Definition 1.5

(State of detachment vs. detachment time)
.

When
L_{k}=n
, we say the process is in a
state of detachment
. But
k
is called a

only when
L_{k-1}<n
and
L_{k}=n.

So, for example, if

k>\tau^{(n)}
then

is not a detachment time, but the process is in a state of detachment at
k
.

1.2
Coupling the processes for all parameters: the detachment process

One does not have to define the detachment process for each
n
separately, as they can be coupled in one ‘‘big’’ process. That process starts with infinitely many passengers, labeled by positive integers. The reader should recall that each passenger chooses the newly available bus independently, and this will also be true when we start with infinitely many passengers. This latter process will be called the
detachment process
.

It is clear that the sub-process formed by following the first
n
passengers only will then be the
n
-detachment process. In this case the random times
\tau^{(n)}
and
\hat{\tau}^{(n)}
are coupled in a monotone way, that is,

\tau^{(1)}\leq\tau^{(2)}\leq...
and

\hat{\tau}^{(1)}\leq\hat{\tau}^{(2)}\leq...

1.3
Comparison with classical occupancy problems

When one studies the law of
N_{k}
, the number of nonempty buses for a fixed
k
,
the model reduces to the classical occupancy problem. The behavior of
N_{k}
as both

n
and
k
tend to infinity is well understood in a variety of scaling regimes;
see the fundamental monograph
[
5
]
. That work also establishes
several limit theorems for the joint distribution of

(Y_{0},Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n}),

where
Y_{r}
denotes the number of buses containing exactly
r
passengers, under
a number of asymptotic assumptions. An interesting feature is that the treatment of
Y_{1}
—the number of ‘‘lonely’’
passengers—requires techniques different from those used for
Y_{r}
with

r\neq 1
. These issues, however, fall outside the scope of the present article,
and we will not pursue them here.

Our approach is different. We are interested in
process properties
and not solely one-dimensional marginal distributions. For instance, we will investigate the behavior of the times
k
when
L_{k}=n
occurs (the process is in a ‘‘state of detachment’’), and the behavior of
\tau^{(n)}
and
\hat{\tau}^{(n)}
, which cannot be determined by results on occupancy problems.

Rising and falling factorials, hypergeometric series:

In this paper

The falling factorial will also be denoted sometimes by

The

2.2
Single time detachment

Let
n\leq k
.
Denoting
(a)_{r}:=a^{\underline{r}}
, the probability that at
k

the process is in a
state of detachment
is clearly

\displaystyle\pi_{n,k}:=P(L_{k}=n)=\frac{(k)_{n}}{k^{n}},

(2)

while the probability that
k
is a
detachment time
is

\displaystyle p_{n,k}:=\frac{n(n-1)(k-2)_{n-2}}{k^{n}}.

(3)

To see why the latter identity holds, write

p_{n,k}=P(L_{k-1}<n,L_{k}=n)=\frac{(k)_{n}}{k^{n}}\cdot P(L_{k-1}<n\mid L_{k}=n).

Since in backward time the configuration at
k-1
is obtained from that at time
k
by sending all passengers in bus label
k
to the buses with labels less than
k
, but under the conditioning, in the
k
th bus there is at most one passenger, it follows that

\displaystyle p_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k)_{n}}{k^{n}}\cdot P(L_{k-1}<n,\ k^{th}\text{bus is non-empty at time }k\mid L_{k}=n)=\frac{(k)_{n}}{k^{n}}\cdot\frac{n(k-1)_{n-1}}{(k)_{n}}\cdot\frac{n-1}{k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{n(k-1)_{n-1}}{k^{n}}\cdot\frac{n-1}{k-1}=\frac{n(n-1)(k-2)_{n-2}}{k^{n}},

(4)

as claimed.

Bounding
\pi_{n,k}
is standard (‘‘birthday problem approximation’’). Recalling that for
k\geq n,

\pi_{n,k}=\frac{(k)_{n}}{k^{n}}=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{j}{k}\right);\ i.e.\ \log\left(\frac{(k)_{n}}{k^{n}}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\log\!\left(1-\frac{j}{k}\right),

and expanding
\log(1-x)=-x-\tfrac{x^{2}}{2}-\cdots
gives the bound

\log\left(\frac{(k)_{n}}{k^{n}}\right)<-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}j-\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}j^{2}.

Thus, we have

\log\left(\frac{(k)_{n}}{k^{n}}\right)<-\frac{n(n-1)}{2k}-\frac{n(n-1)(2n-1)}{12k^{2}}.

A similar computation (using that for
0<x<1,

\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}\frac{x^{m}}{m}\;\leq\;\sum_{m=3}^{\infty}\frac{x^{m}}{3}\;=\;\frac{x^{3}}{3(1-x)}
) gives the lower bound, and altogether we have

\displaystyle-\frac{n(n-1)}{2k}-\frac{n(n-1)(2n-1)}{12k^{2}}-\frac{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}{12\,k^{2}\,(k-n+1)}\;\leq\;\log\pi_{n,k}<-\frac{n(n-1)}{2k}-\frac{n(n-1)(2n-1)}{12k^{2}}.

(5)

Hence, for example, if
k=k(n)\ll n^{2}
and
n\to\infty
then
\pi_{n,k(n)}\to 0,
while if
k(n)\sim cn^{2}
then

\displaystyle-\log\pi_{n,k(n)}=\frac{1}{2c}+\mathcal{O}(1/n).

(6)

Note also that the product formula immediately shows that
\pi_{n,k}
is increasing in
k
.

Another consequence of (
5
) is the following result on the proportion of times spent in states of detachment.
Let
D^{n}_{k}
denote the number of times up to
k
when the
n
-detachment process is in a detached state:

D^{n}_{k}:=|\{i\in\{1,2,...,k\}:\ L_{i}=n\}|,

and let
\mathsf{R}^{n}_{k}
be the proportion of those times among all times
1,2,...,k
, that is,
\mathsf{R}^{n}_{k}:=D^{n}_{k}/k
.

Corollary 2.3

(Zero percent detachment until
o(n^{2})
)
.

If
k=k(n)=o(n^{2})
as
n\to\infty
then one has

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{R}^{n}_{k(n)}=0
in probability (even in
L^{1}
).

Note:
It follows from Theorem
3.2
of the next section that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{R}^{n}_{k(n)}=1
in probability if
k(n)\gg n^{2}.

Proof.

Let
A_{k}^{n}:=\{L_{k}=n
}. (Clearly, for
k<n,
the event
P^{(n)}(A)_{k}^{n}=0
.) Then

E\mathsf{R}^{n}_{k}=(1/k)ED_{k}^{n}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}P(A^{n}_{j})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi_{n,j},

and since
\pi_{n,j}
is increasing in
j
,
it is enough to show that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n,k(n)}=0
. But this follows from the assumption, as noted after (
5
).
∎

Let us now turn the asymptotic behavior of
p_{n,k(n)}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Consider a sequence
k_{n}\to\infty
with
k_{n}\geq n
.

(i)

For
any such sequence
, one has

p_{n,k_{n}}=\mathcal{O}(n^{-2}).

(ii)

If
k_{n}\sim c\,n^{2}
with
c>0
,
then

p_{n,k_{n}}\sim\frac{1}{c^{2}}\,e^{-1/(2c)}\,\frac{1}{n^{2}};

(iii)

If
k_{n}\gg n^{2}
,
then

p_{n,k_{n}}\sim\dfrac{n^{2}}{k_{n}^{2}}.

Proof.

Write

p_{n,k}=\frac{n(n-1)}{k^{2}}\prod_{j=2}^{n-1}\Bigl(1-\frac{j}{k}\Bigr),\qquad k\geq n.

Then

\log p_{n,k}=\log\left(\frac{n(n-1)}{k(k-1)}\right)+\log\pi_{n,k}.

By (
5
) it follows that
\dfrac{n(n-1)}{k(k-1)}e^{I_{1}}\leq p_{n,k}\leq\dfrac{n(n-1)}{k(k-1)}e^{I_{2}}
,
where

\displaystyle I_{1}:=-\frac{n(n-1)}{2k}-\frac{n(n-1)(2n-1)}{12k^{2}}-\frac{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}{12\,k^{2}\,(k-n+1)};\ I_{2}:=-\frac{n(n-1)}{2k}-\frac{n(n-1)(2n-1)}{12k^{2}}.

(7)

For (i), let
\ell_{n}:=\frac{n-1}{2k}.
Then

p_{n,k}\leq\dfrac{n(n-1)}{k(k-1)}e^{-\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2k}}=4(1+o(1))\ell_{n}^{2}e^{-n\ell_{n}}=16e^{-2}n^{-2}(1+o(1)),

using that
\max_{x>0}x^{2}e^{-nx}=4/(e^{2}n^{2})
.

Finally, (ii) and (iii) immediately follow from (
7
).
∎

2.3
Multi-step transitions and two-time detachment

It is important to observe that
for the
n
-detachment process, the
l
-step transition (
l\geq 1
) from time
k\geq 1
to time
k+l

is equivalent to the following: Every passenger independently joins one of the newly available buses (labeled
k+1,\dots,k+l
) with probability
l/(l+k).

To see why this equivalence is true, note that the configuration of passengers in the newly available buses is the same whatever the configuration on the first
k
buses was at time
k
. Therefore conditioning on the state of the process at
k
can be dropped when we
only consider the newly available buses
at time
k+l
. But without conditioning, we know that at time
k+l
the state of the process is precisely the Tóth model with
n
passengers and
k+l
buses, the restriction of which on buses labeled
k+1,\dots,k+l
is distributed according to the previous paragraph.

A useful application of this fact is as follows. Let
\varpi(n,k,l):=P^{(n)}(L_{k+l}=L_{k}=n)
be the probability that the process is at a detached state at time
k
as well as at time
k+l
, where
k,l\geq 1
and
k\geq n
.
Using the above description of the multi-step transitions, a straightforward computation (with the
\ell^{r}
and also the
k^{n}
terms canceling out) gives that for
0<k,l
,

\displaystyle\varpi(n,k,l)=\frac{(k)_{n}}{(k+l)^{n}}\sum_{r=0}^{n\wedge l}\binom{n}{r}\frac{(l)_{r}}{k^{r}}=\frac{(k)_{n}}{(k+l)^{n}}\,{}_{2}F_{0}(-n,-l;-;1/k),

(8)

where
{}_{2}F_{0}
is the generalized hypergeometric function.
(In the sum we can just as well write
\infty
as upper limit, as it terminates at
\min(n,l)
anyway.)

2.4
Concentration for the number of lonely passengers, WLLN

Stressing the total number of passengers in the notation, let now
L^{n}_{k}=L^{n}_{k(n)}
denote the count of lonely passengers for
n
passengers and
k=k(n)
buses, that is
L^{n}_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(\text{passenger i is lonely at time }k).
In this subsection, as a warm-up, we check when
L^{n}_{k}

is concentrated around its mean for
n\gg 1
and for that purpose we define

R_{n,k}:=\frac{\mathsf{Var}^{(n)}(L^{n}_{k})}{(E^{(n)}[L^{n}_{k}])^{2}}.

We are interested whether

\sigma(L^{n}_{k})=o(E^{(n)}[L^{n}_{k}])\ (\text{i.e.}\ R_{n,k}\to 0)
holds, in which case, by Chebyshev,

\displaystyle\lim_{n}P^{(n)}\left(\left|\frac{L^{n}_{k}}{E^{(n)}[L^{n}_{k}]}-1\right|>\varepsilon\right)=0

(9)

for all
\varepsilon>0.
Then
L^{n}_{k}

is concentrated around
E^{(n)}[L^{n}_{k}]=n(1-1/k)^{n-1},
and (since clearly
L^{n}_{k}\leq n
), the limit in (
9
) implies in particular the Weak Law of Large Numbers:

\displaystyle\lim_{n}P^{(n)}\left(\left|\frac{L^{n}_{k}-E^{(n)}[L^{n}_{k}]}{n}\right|>\varepsilon\right)=0,\ \forall\varepsilon>0.

(10)

Theorem 2.5

(Concentration around the mean;
n/\log n
scaling)
.

Let
n\to\infty.
Then

(i) the critical regime for
R_{n,k}\to 0
is
k(n)\sim\tfrac{n}{\alpha\log n}
, and then
R_{n,k}\to 0
if and only if
\alpha<1
; if on the other hand
k(n)\gg\frac{n}{\log n}
then
R_{n,k}\to 0
;

(ii)
c>1
and
k(n)\leq\dfrac{n}{c\log n}
implies that for any
m\geq 1
,

P^{(n)}(\exists\text{ at least m lonely passengers})=P^{(n)}(L^{n}_{k}\geq m)\leq\frac{1}{m\cdot n^{c-1+o(1)}},

as
n\to\infty
.

Remark 2.6

(Poisson approximation)
.

As the proof reveals (see the asymptotics in (

)), if
k(n)=\frac{n}{\log(cn)},\ c>0
(now constant is inside the logarithm!), then
M_{n,k}=k\cdot q_{n,k}\to\frac{1}{c}
, and this raises the hope that the law of
L_{n,k}
can be approximated by the Poisson distribution with parameter
1/c
, despite the lack of independence of the Bernoullis. This can indeed be done, as an application of the Chen-Stein method to the Birthday Problem with
n
persons and k days — see section 4.1 in
[
1
]
for the proof and error bounds.

Proof.

(of Theorem
2.5
)
First, with
X_{i}:=\mathds{1}\{\text{bus }i\text{ has exactly one passenger}\}\ i=1,\dots k,
and
q_{n,k}:=P^{(n)}(X_{1}=1)=\dfrac{n}{k}\left(1-\dfrac{1}{k}\right)^{n-1}

L^{n}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i},\qquad M_{n,k}:=E^{(n)}[L^{n}_{k}]=kq_{n,k}=n\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{n-1},

while for
i\neq j

A_{n,k}:=P^{(n)}(X_{i}=X_{j}=1)=\frac{n(n-1)}{k^{2}}\left(1-\frac{2}{k}\right)^{n-2}.

Hence

\displaystyle V_{n,k}:=\mathrm{Var}^{(n)}(L^{n}_{k})

\displaystyle=kp_{n,k}(1-p_{n,k})+k(k-1)\!\left[P^{(n)}(X_{i}=X_{j}=1)-p_{n,k}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=k\frac{n}{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{n}{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+k(k-1)\!\left\{\frac{n(n-1)}{k^{2}}\left(1-\frac{2}{k}\right)^{n-2}-\left[\frac{n}{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{n-1}\right]^{2}\right\},

\displaystyle V_{n,k}

Therefore,

R_{n,k}=\frac{1}{M_{n,k}}+\frac{k(k-1)\,A_{n,k}}{M_{n,k}^{2}}-1.

For the rest of the calculations, we will use that
k(n)\to\infty

\log(1-x)=-\sum_{j\geq 1}x^{j}/j

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{n-1}=\exp\left(-\frac{n-1}{k}-\mathcal{O}\!\Big(\frac{n}{k^{2}}\Big)\right);\ \ M_{n,k}=n\exp\left(-\frac{n-1}{k}-\mathcal{O}\!\Big(\frac{n}{k^{2}}\Big)\right);

(12)

\left(1-\frac{2}{k}\right)^{n-2}=\exp\left(-\frac{2(n-2)}{k}-\mathcal{O}\!\Big(\frac{n}{k^{2}}\Big)\right);\quad A_{n,k}=\frac{n(n-1)}{k^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{2(n-2)}{k}-\mathcal{O}\!\Big(\frac{n}{k^{2}}\Big)\right).

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\exp\left(\frac{n-1}{k}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n}{k^{2}}\right)\right)+\frac{k(k-1)\frac{n(n-1)}{k^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{2(n-2)}{k}-\mathcal{O}\!\Big(\frac{n}{k^{2}}\Big)\right)}{n^{2}\exp\left(-2\frac{n-1}{k}-\mathcal{O}\!\Big(\frac{n}{k^{2}}\Big)\right)}-1

Assume now that
k(n)=\dfrac{n}{\alpha\log n}
with
\alpha>0.
Then

\displaystyle R_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\exp\left((\alpha-1)\log n-1/k+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ +\exp\left(\frac{2}{k}+\log\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)+\log\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log^{2}n}{n}\right)\right)-1.

It follows that if
\alpha<1
, then
R_{n,k}\to 0
, while if
1\leq\alpha
, then
R_{n,k}\not\to 0.

It is also clear that if
k(n)\gg\dfrac{n}{\log n}
then
R_{n,k}\to 0
.

Finally (ii) follows immediately from the asymptotics of
M_{n,k}
in (

2.5
Minimum, maximum and range

Recall that
k
denotes both the time and the number of the newest bus that joins at that time. Let
m_{k}
and
M_{k}
denote the minimum and maximum bus numbers, respectively, among all nonempty buses at time
k
. That is,
m_{k}
and
M_{k}
are the bus numbers corresponding to the ‘‘leftmost’’ and ‘‘rightmost’’ occupied positions, assuming the buses arrive sequentially, always to the right. Let
R_{k}:=M_{k}-m_{k}
denote the ‘‘range’’ of non-empty buses.

Next, as another warm-up, we give some results about their large-time behavior. Since the large time limit of the point process scaled by
k
corresponds to sampling
n
points from the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
, the following result is hardly surprising. (But we think it is useful to record it.)

Theorem 2.7

(Beta limits)
.

Let
m_{k},M_{k}
and
R_{k}
be as in the previous paragraph.

1.

For
n\geq 1
fixed,

\lim_{k}\mathsf{Law}(m_{k}/k)=\mathsf{Beta}(1,n);\ \lim_{k}\mathsf{Law}(M_{k}/k)=\mathsf{Beta}(n,1),

that is,

\lim_{k\to\infty}P\left(\frac{m_{k}}{k}\leq x\right)=1-(1-x)^{n};\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}P\left(\frac{M_{k}}{k}\leq x\right)=x^{n}

for
x\in[0,1].

2.

The expectations and standard deviations satisfy

\displaystyle\lim_{k}E(m_{k}/k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1};\ \ \lim_{k}E(M_{k}/k)=\frac{n}{n+1};\ \ \lim_{k}E(R_{k}/k)=\frac{n-1}{n+1};

\displaystyle\lim_{k}\sigma(M_{k}/k)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k}\sigma(m_{k}/k)=\frac{1}{n+1}\sqrt{\frac{n}{n+2}}.

Proof.

Since the detachment process at the fix time
k
is exactly the Tóth model with parameters
n,k
, trivial computation yields that for
a=1/k,2/k,...,1,

P\left(\frac{m_{k}}{k}\leq a\right)=1-\left(\frac{k-ka}{k}\right)^{n}=1-(1-a)^{n};\quad P\left(\frac{M_{k}}{k}\leq a\right)=\left(\frac{ka}{k}\right)^{n}=a^{n}

Taking limits in
k,
and approximating
x
by fractions
i/k
, the first part follows.

The convergence of moments follows from the fact that
m_{k}/k,M_{k}/k\in[0,1];
this yields part 2.∎

3
First and last detachment times — asymptotic results

3.1
Permanent detachment time

We first consider the time of permanent detachment
\tau^{(n)}
, for which we have an explicit result.

Theorem 3.1

(Distribution of
\tau^{(n)}
)
.

The random variable
\tau^{(n)}

1.

is almost surely finite, with a tail asymptotical with
n(n-1)/k
as
k\to\infty
and thus has infinite expectation;

2.

has distribution function

\displaystyle F^{(n)}(k)=P(\tau^{(n)}\leq k)=\frac{\binom{k}{n}}{\binom{k+n-1}{n}},

(13)

using the convention
\binom{a}{b}:=0
when
a<b.

Proof.

It is easy to check that the second statement implies the first. Indeed, for fixed
n
and
k\to\infty,

n!\binom{k}{n}=k^{n}-\frac{1}{2}(n-1)nk^{n-1}+o(k^{n-1})
while

n!\binom{k+n-1}{n}=k^{n}+\frac{1}{2}(n-1)nk^{n-1}+o(k^{n-1})
, and so

\displaystyle P(\tau^{(n)}>k)=1-\frac{\binom{k}{n}}{\binom{k+n-1}{n}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{k^{n}-\frac{1}{2}(n-1)nk^{n-1}+o(k^{n-1})}{k^{n}+\frac{1}{2}(n-1)nk^{n-1}+o(k^{n-1})}=\frac{(n-1)nk^{n-1}+o(k^{n-1})}{k^{n}+(n-1)nk^{n-1}+o(k^{n-1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-1)n+o(1)}{k+(n-1)n+o(1)}=\frac{n(n-1)}{k}+o\!\left(\frac{1}{k}\right).

For the first statement, first recall that
\pi_{n,k}:=P(L_{k}=n)=\dfrac{(k)_{n}}{k^{n}}.

(Clearly,
\pi_{n,k}=0
when
k<n
.) Hence, for
k\geq n
,

\displaystyle P(\tau^{(n)}\leq k)

\displaystyle=\pi_{n,k}\prod_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\left(\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)^{n}+\frac{n}{i}\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)^{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi_{n,k}\prod_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\left(\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)^{n}\frac{n+i-1}{i-1}\right),

(14)

where, using independence, the product is the probability that after time
k
the ‘‘loneliness’’ of the passengers is never violated, that is, it never occurs for any time
i>k
that more than one of them jumps to the
i
th bus.
Considering the product only up to
k+N+1
, after a bit of algebra, one obtains that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\prod_{i=k+1}^{k+N+1}\left(\left(\frac{i-1}{i}\right)^{n}\frac{n+i-1}{i-1}\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{k}{k+N+1}\right)^{n}\frac{(n+k+N)!}{(k+N)!}\cdot\frac{(k-1)!}{(n+k-1)!}.

(15)

Multiplying by
\pi_{n,k}
,

\displaystyle A_{n,k,N}:=\pi_{n,k}\left(\frac{k}{k+N+1}\right)^{n}\frac{(n+k+N)!}{(k+N)!}\frac{(k-1)!}{(n+k-1)!}=\left(\frac{1}{k+N+1}\right)^{n}\frac{k!}{(k-n)!}\,\frac{(n+k+N)!}{(n+k-1)!}\,\frac{(k-1)!}{(k+N)!}.

(16)

Expressing with binomial coefficients,

\displaystyle A_{n,k,N}=\left(\frac{1}{k+N+1}\right)^{n}\frac{(n+k+N)!}{(k+N)!}\frac{\binom{k}{n}}{\binom{k+n-1}{n}}.

(17)

Thus, for
k\geq n
,

\displaystyle P(\tau^{(n)}\leq k)=\lim_{N\to\infty}A_{n,k,N}=\frac{\binom{k}{n}}{\binom{k+n-1}{n}}\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+N+1}\right)^{n}\frac{(n+k+N)!}{(k+N)!}.

(18)

Now use the fact that by Stirling’s formula,
if
a>0
is fix and
u\to\infty
then

\frac{(a+u)!}{u!}\sim u^{a}
to conclude that
the limit on the right hand side tends to one as
N\to\infty
(and
k,n
are fixed).
Therefore, for
k\geq n
,

\displaystyle P(\tau^{(n)}\leq k)=\frac{\binom{k}{n}}{\binom{k+n-1}{n}},

(19)

as claimed.
∎

Next, we show that
\tau^{(n)}
scales with
n^{2}
, more precisely, we prove that
\tau^{(n)}/n^{2}
has a limiting distribution as
n\to\infty
.

Recall that the reciprocal of an exponential variable is called
inverse exponential distribution
; we will denote this law by
\mathsf{IE}(\lambda
), when the original variable is
\mathsf{Exp}(\lambda)
-distributed.

Theorem 3.2
.

As
n\to\infty
, the law of

\tau^{(n)}/n^{2}
converges to
\mathsf{IE}(1)
. Thus, the limiting distribution function for positive
x
values is
F(x)=e^{-1/x}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Although the
\mathsf{IE}(1)
distribution has infinite mean, it has a unique mode which is
1/2
(see Figure
1
), hence the most likely value of
\tau^{(n)}
is around
n^{2}/2
for large

.

\hfill\diamond

:
The density of the limiting IE(1) law

Proof.

By (
19
), for
k\geq n
,

\displaystyle P(\tau^{(n)}\leq k)=\frac{k!/(k-n)!}{(k-1+n)!/(k-1)!}.

(20)

A little algebra yields the more useful form

\displaystyle P(\tau^{(n)}\leq k)=\left[\left(1+\frac{n}{k-1}\right)\left(1+\frac{n}{k-2}\right)...\left(1+\frac{n}{k-n+1}\right)\right]^{-1}.

(21)

In the rest of the proof we are going to assume that
k=k(n)
and
n=o(k)
, and use the elementary fact that

\displaystyle\log(d+1)-\log c\leq\sum_{c}^{d}\frac{1}{i}\leq\log d-\log(c-1),\ 1<c<d,

(22)

along with a Taylor expansion.
We thus have

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{n}{k-1}\right)\left(1+\frac{n}{k-2}\right)...\left(1+\frac{n}{k-n+1}\right)=

\displaystyle\exp\left[\frac{n}{k-1}+\frac{n}{k-2}+...+\frac{n}{k-n+1}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{3}}{(k-n+1)^{2}}\right)\right]=\exp\left(n\sum^{k-1}_{k-n+1}\frac{1}{i}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{3}}{(k-n+1)^{2}}\right)\right),

it follows that

3.2
The time of first detachment

Consider now the
first
detachment time
\hat{\tau}^{(n)}=\min\{k\geq 1\mid L_{k}=n\}.
The next theorem shows that the correct scaling for this time is between
n^{2}/\log n
and
n^{2}.

Theorem 3.4

(Scaling for the first detachment time)
.

Let
(k_{n})
be a sequence satisfying
k_{n}\geq n
.

(i)

Let
n^{2}=\mathcal{O}(k_{n})
. Then

\liminf_{n}P(\hat{\tau}^{(n)}\leq k_{n})\geq\liminf_{n}P(L_{k_{n}}=n)>0.

In particular,
\hat{\tau}^{(n)}/n^{2}
does not converge to infinity in law.

(ii)

Let
k_{n}\leq\dfrac{n^{2}}{c\log n},\ c>4
, or more generally, assume that
(n-1)^{2}/(2k_{n})-\log k_{n}\to\infty
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}P(\hat{\tau}^{(n)}\leq k_{n})=0,

and so for
k_{n}=o\left(\dfrac{n^{2}}{\log n}\right),
one has
\hat{\tau}^{(n)}/k_{n}\to\infty
in law.

(iii)

(large deviations) For
c>1
,

-\frac{1}{2c}-\,\frac{1}{6c^{2}}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P(\hat{\tau}^{(n)}\leq cn)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P(\hat{\tau}^{(n)}\leq cn)\leq-\frac{1}{2c}.

Proof.

The proof is based on (
5
).

(i)

The statement follows immediately from (
5
).

(ii)

By the union bound,

P(\hat{\tau}^{(n)}\leq k_{n})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{k_{n}}P(L_{k}=n)=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{n}}\frac{k!}{(k-n)!k^{n}}.

Using (
5
), one has

\displaystyle P(\hat{\tau}^{(n)}\leq k_{n})

\displaystyle<\sum_{k=1}^{k_{n}}\exp\{-(n-1)n/(2k)\}<k_{n}\exp\{-(n-1)^{2}/(2k_{n})\}

\displaystyle=\exp\{\log k_{n}-(n-1)^{2}/(2k_{n})\},

(24)

and the statement follows.

(iii)

The lower estimate follows from
P(\hat{\tau}^{(n)}\leq cn)\geq P(L_{cn}=n)
along with (
5
). The upper bound follows from (
(ii)
).

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

The final upper bound in (ii) was rough, but refining the estimate for the last sum provides minimal improvement. The primary issue is the overly crude union bound applied at the beginning of the argument in (ii).
\hfill\diamond

4
Scaling limit of times in a state of detachment — quadratic scaling

The following result establishes a scaling limit with quadratic (in
n
) scaling of times.

Theorem 4.1

(Scaling limit)
.

For
n\geq 1

define the
0-1
-valued (non-Markovian) stochastic process
X^{(n)}
on
[1,\infty)
by

X^{(n)}_{t}:=\mathds{1}(L_{tn^{2}}=n)=\mathds{1}(\text{state of detachment at time\ }tn^{2}),

and note that
X^{(n)}
is càdlàg by the way we extended the
n
-detachment process for real times. Then, as
n\to\infty
, the processes
X^{(n)}
have a limit in the sense of finite dimensional distributions (fidis) to a process with càdlàg paths.

Remark 4.2

(Lack of tightness)
.

For the laws of
\{X^{(n)},n\geq 1\}
one cannot hope to have tightness in the Skorokhod space
D([1,\infty))
, or even in
D([1,T])
(and thus convergence in the sense of the laws of the processes).
The intuitive explanation is as follows. Let
k_{1}=cn^{2}
and
k_{2}=dn^{2}
with fixed constants
1<c<d<T
, and define

l:=k_{2}-k_{1}=(d-c)n^{2}.

Consider

E_{n}(c,d):=P^{(n)}(L_{k_{2}}=n\mid L_{{k_{1}}}=n)=\left(\frac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\right)^{n}{}_{2}F_{0}\!\left(-n,\,-l;\,-;\,\frac{1}{k_{1}}\right).

Although the convergence

E_{n}(c,d)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{d-c}{2d^{2}}\right)

will be established in (
25
) below, the error bound in (
4.2
) is
not
uniform on
\{(c,d):1<c<d<T\}
, because
d-c
can be arbitrarily small.

The lack of tightness means that certain properties (such as for instance the first hitting time of
1
) of the limiting càdlàg process may be very different from those of all the approximating processes. Indeed, however large
n
is taken to be, the small scale fluctuations of the approximating processes will differ from those of the limiting one.

\hfill\diamond

Before turning to the proof we need some identities.

4.1
Collecting some basic identities

The following identities will be useful (and proven, if not shown before) in this section. They are collected here for convenience. Below
k_{1}=k_{1}(n)=cn^{2},k_{2}=k_{2}(n)=dn^{2},k_{3}=k_{3}(n)=hn^{2}
and
1\leq c<d<h.

(1a)

P^{(n)}(L_{k_{1}}=n)=\dfrac{(k_{1})_{n}}{k_{1}^{n}}

(1b)

\lim_{n\to\infty}P(L_{k_{1}}=n)=\exp(-1/(2c))

(2a)

P^{(n)}(L_{k_{1}}=n,L_{{k_{2}}}=n)=\dfrac{(k_{1})_{n}}{k_{2}^{n}}\,{}_{2}F_{0}(-n,k_{1}-k_{2};-;1/k_{1})

(2b)

\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{(n)}(L_{k_{2}(n)}=n,L_{{k_{1}(n)}}=n)=\exp\left(-1/(2c)-\dfrac{d-c}{2d^{2}}\right)

(3a)

P^{(n)}(L_{k_{2}}=n\mid L_{{k_{1}}}=n)=\left(\dfrac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\right)^{\!n}\,{}_{2}F_{0}\!\left(-n,\,-l;\,-;\,\frac{1}{k_{1}}\right)

(3b)

\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{(n)}(L_{k_{2}(n)}=n\mid L_{{k_{1}(n)}}=n)=\exp\left(-\dfrac{d-c}{2d^{2}}\right)

(4a)

P^{(n)}(L_{k_{1}}=L_{{k_{2}}}=L_{k_{3}}=n)=\dfrac{(k_{1})_{n}}{k_{2}^{n}}{}_{2}F_{0}\!\left(-n,\,k_{1}-k_{2};\,-;\,\dfrac{1}{k_{1}}\right)\,\left(\dfrac{k_{2}}{k_{3}}\right)^{\!n}{}_{2}F_{0}\!\left(-n,\,k_{2}-k_{3};\,-;\,\frac{1}{k_{2}}\right)

(4b)

\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{(n)}(L_{k_{1}}=L_{{k_{2}}}=L_{k_{3}}=n)=\exp\left\{-1/(2c)-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{d-c}{d^{2}}+\dfrac{h-d}{h^{2}}\right)\right\}

(5a)

P(L_{k_{1}}=L_{k_{3}}=n,L_{k_{2}}\neq n)=

\frac{(k_{1})_{n}}{k_{3}^{n}}\,\left[{}_{2}F_{0}(-n,k_{1}-k_{3};-;1/k_{1})-\,{}_{2}F_{0}\!\left(-n,\,k_{1}-k_{2};\,-;\,\frac{1}{k_{1}}\right)\,{}_{2}F_{0}\!\left(-n,\,k_{2}-k_{3};\,-;\,\frac{1}{k_{2}}\right)\right]

4.2
Convergence of the finite dimensional distributions

Proof.

The convergence of the one-dimensional distributions follows from (
6
). For the multidimensional distributions we use an inductive argument.
Let
v>1
and
0<t_{1}<t_{2}<...<t_{v}
and consider the events

E_{1},...,E_{v}
where each
E_{i}=E_{n,i}
equals either
\{L_{t_{i}n^{2}}=n\}
or
\{L_{t_{i}n^{2}}\neq n\}.
Since
X^{n}_{t}
can only take two values, it is sufficient to show that

P^{(n)}(E_{1}E_{2}...E_{v})
converges as
n\to\infty
. For that, we first assume that
E_{i}=E_{n,i}=\{L_{t_{i}n^{2}}=n\}
for all
i=1,...,v
. By the Markov property,

P^{(n)}(E_{1}E_{2}...E_{v})=P^{(n)}(E_{1})\cdot P^{(n)}(E_{2}\mid E_{1})\cdot P^{(n)}(E_{3}\mid E_{2})\cdot...\cdot P^{(n)}(E_{v}\mid E_{v-1}),

where we also used that given detachment at
t_{i}n^{2}
(
L_{t_{i}n^{2}}=n
) it is irrelevant in exactly which buses the passengers are located at
t_{i}n^{2}
, as far as detachment at the next time (
L_{t_{i+1}n^{2}}=n
) is concerned.
Therefore, to finish the argument in this case, it is sufficient to show the convergence of
P^{(n)}(E_{i+1}\mid E_{i})
.

Let
k_{1}<k_{2}.

Recall from Subsection
4.1

that

\displaystyle P^{(n)}(L_{k_{2}}=n\mid L_{{k_{1}}}=n)=\left(\frac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\right)^{\!n}\,{}_{2}F_{0}\!\left(-n,\,-l;\,-;\,\frac{1}{k_{1}}\right).

We now show that if
k_{1}=k_{1}(n)=cn^{2},k_{2}=k_{2}(n)=dn^{2}

with
0<c<d
then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{(n)}(L_{k_{2}(n)}=n\mid L_{{k_{1}(n)}}=n)=\exp\left(-\frac{d-c}{2d^{2}}\right).

(25)

Indeed, define

l=l(n):=k_{2}(n)-k_{1}(n)=(d-c)\,n^{2},

and

S_{n}:=\left(\frac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\right)^{n}{}_{2}F_{0}\!\left(-n,-l;\,-;\,\frac{1}{k_{1}}\right)=\left(\frac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\right)^{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}\frac{(l)_{r}}{k_{1}^{\,r}},

where
k_{1},k_{2},l
depend on
n
.
Letting
x:=\dfrac{l}{k_{1}}=\dfrac{d-c}{c}
,
we may rewrite the sum as

\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}\frac{(l)_{r}}{k_{1}^{\,r}}=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}x^{r}\frac{(l)_{r}}{l^{r}}=(1+x)^{n}\sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom{n}{r}\left(\frac{x}{1+x}\right)^{r}\left(\frac{1}{1+x}\right)^{n-r}\frac{(l)_{r}}{l^{r}}=(1+x)^{n}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{(l)_{R}}{l^{R}}\right],

where
R=R_{n}\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,\theta)
with success probability

\theta:=\frac{x}{1+x}=\left(\frac{d-c}{c}\right)\big/\left(\frac{d}{c}\right)=\frac{d-c}{d},

and
\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}
is the corresponding expectation.
Since
\dfrac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}=\dfrac{k_{1}-k_{2}}{k_{2}}+1=\dfrac{c-d}{d}+1=\dfrac{c}{d}=\dfrac{1}{1+x}
, it follows that

S_{n}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\dfrac{(l)_{R}}{l^{R}}\right].

Next, use an expansion for the logarithm when
r\leq n\wedge l
. Using the Lagrange remainder term (denoted by
L
here),

\log\frac{(l)_{r}}{l^{r}}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\log\!\left(1-\frac{j}{l}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\left[-\frac{j}{l}-L(j/l)\right]=-\frac{r(r-1)}{2l}-\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}L(j/l),

where
0\leq L(j/l)\leq\frac{1}{2(1-(j/l))^{2}}\left(\frac{j}{l}\right)^{2}=\frac{j^{2}}{2(l-j)^{2}},

that is,

\displaystyle\left|\log\frac{(l)_{r}}{l^{r}}+\frac{r(r-1)}{2l}\right|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{j^{2}}{2(l-j)^{2}}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{j^{2}}{2((c-d)n^{2}-n)^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{(r-1)r(2r-1)}{12((c-d)n^{2}-n)^{2}}\leq\frac{n^{3}}{6((c-d)n^{2}-n)^{2}}:=e(c,d,n).

(26)

(Clearly, for
c,d
fixed,
e(c,d,n)=\mathcal{O}(1/n).
)
Hence, for
r\leq l,

\exp\left\{-\frac{r(r-1)}{2l}-e(c,d,n)\right\}\leq\frac{(l)_{r}}{l^{r}}\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{r(r-1)}{2l}\right\}

and thus

\displaystyle\exp\left(-e(c,d,n)\right)\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(-\frac{R_{n}(R_{n}-1)}{2l}\right)\right]\leq S_{n}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\dfrac{(l)_{R}}{l^{R}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(-\frac{R_{n}(R_{n}-1)}{2l}\right)\right].

(27)

Since
R_{n}\sim\mathsf{Bin}\!\left(n,\frac{d-c}{d}\right)
, one can couple all
R_{n}
in the obvious way and then
one has
\lim_{n}\dfrac{R_{n}}{n}=\theta=\dfrac{d-c}{d}
a.s. by the Strong Law of Large Numbers.
Hence, almost surely,

\frac{R_{n}(R_{n}-1)}{2l}=\frac{R_{n}^{2}-R_{n}}{2(d-c)n^{2}}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{\theta^{2}}{2(d-c)}=\frac{d-c}{2d^{2}},

and this and (
27
), along with by bounded convergence implies that

\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{n}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{d-c}{2d^{2}}\right),

completing the proof of (
25
).

Returning now to the proof of the theorem, next, assume that
E_{i}=E_{n,i}=\{L_{t_{i}n^{2}}\neq n\}
for
exactly one
index
i
. For simplicity assume that this index is
i=1.
Then we are done by observing that

P^{(n)}(E_{1}E_{2}...E_{v})=P^{(n)}(E_{2}...E_{v})-P^{(n)}(E^{c}_{1}E_{2}...E_{v}).

Next, assume that
E_{i}=E_{n,i}=\{L_{t_{i}n^{2}}\neq n\}
for
exactly two
indices
i
; and suppose for simplicity that these are
i=1,2
. Similarly as before, we may write

P^{(n)}(E_{1}E_{2}...E_{v})=P^{(n)}(E_{3}...E_{v})-P^{(n)}(E^{c}_{1}E_{2}...E_{v})-P^{(n)}(E_{1}E^{c}_{2}...E_{v})-P^{(n)}(E^{c}_{1}E^{c}_{2}...E_{v}),

and we are done again because of the already proven cases. Continuing the argument inductively, we check that
P^{(n)}(E_{1}E_{2}...E_{v})
always has a limit as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Remark 4.3

(Transition from non-detached to detached)
.

It is worth recording also that if
k_{1}<k_{2}
then letting
l:=k_{2}-k_{1}>0

\displaystyle P^{(n)}(L_{k_{2}}=n\mid L_{{k_{1}}}\neq n)=:P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(B)-P(A^{c}B)}{P(A)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{n}(k_{2})-\pi(n,k_{1},k_{2}-k_{1})}{1-p_{n}(k_{1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{n}(k_{2})-\left(\frac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\right)^{\!n}\,{}_{2}F_{0}\!\left(-n,\,-l;\,-;\,\frac{1}{k_{1}}\right)}{1-p_{n}(k_{1})}.

(28)

Consequently, if
k_{1}=k_{1}(n)=cn^{2},k_{2}=k_{2}(n)=dn^{2}

with
0<c<d
then

\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{(n)}(L_{k_{2}}=n\mid L_{{k_{1}}}\neq n)=\frac{\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{2d}\right)-\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{1}{2c}-\dfrac{d-c}{2d^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp(-1/(2c))}.

4.3
Checking that the limit given by the fidis has a càdlàg version

We are going to show that the limiting process (in the sense of fidis) has a version with values in the Skorokhod-space of cadlag functions. For that it will be enough to check the sufficient condition (
11
) given in our Appendix B. The first half of it is that

\lim_{h\downarrow 0}P(X_{t+h}\neq X_{t})=0.

This is clearly satisfied since

\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{(n)}(L_{k_{2}(n)}=n\mid L_{{k_{1}(n)}}=n)=\exp\left(-\dfrac{d-c}{2d^{2}}\right),

and so for the limiting process
X
,

P(X_{c}=1\mid X_{d}=1)=\exp\left(-\dfrac{d-c}{2d^{2}}\right),\ 1\leq c<d,
which tends to one if
d\downarrow c.

Similarly, by Remark
4.3

if
k_{1}=k_{1}(n)=cn^{2},k_{2}=k_{2}(n)=dn^{2}

with
0<c<d
then

P(X_{c}=1\mid X_{d}=0)=\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{(n)}(L_{k_{2}}=n\mid L_{{k_{1}}}\neq n)=\frac{\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{2d}\right)-\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{1}{2c}-\dfrac{d-c}{2d^{2}}\right)}{1-\exp(-1/(2c))},

which tends to zero if
d\downarrow c.

Therefore, it only remains to check the second half, involving the probabilities

P(L_{k_{1}}=L_{k_{3}}=n,L_{k_{2}}\neq n)
and
P(L_{k_{1}}\neq n,L_{k_{2}}=n,L_{k_{3}}\neq n)
.

Checking the first probability.
Using Remark
4.3
, one has

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{(n)}(L_{k_{1}}=L_{k_{3}}=n,L_{k_{2}}\neq n)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}P(L_{k_{1}}=L_{k_{3}}=n)-\lim_{n\to\infty}P(L_{k_{1}}=L_{k_{2}}=L_{k_{3}}=n)

(29)

\displaystyle=e^{-1/(2c)}\left[\exp\left\{-\dfrac{h-c}{2h^{2}}\right\}-\exp\left\{-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{d-c}{d^{2}}+\dfrac{h-d}{h^{2}}\right)\right\}\right].

We first claim that for all
0<c<d<h,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P(L_{k_{1}}=L_{k_{3}}=n,L_{k_{2}}\neq n)<K(h-c)^{1+\alpha},

(30)

with
K=0.6
and
\alpha=1/2
, that is,

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}\!\left[e^{-\frac{h-c}{2h^{2}}}-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\frac{d-c}{d^{2}}+\frac{h-d}{h^{2}}\right)}\right]<0.6\cdot(h-c)^{3/2}.

(31)

(So
K,\alpha
do not depend on
T
.)
To verify (
31
), let

A:=\frac{h-c}{2h^{2}},\qquad B:=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\frac{d-c}{d^{2}}+\frac{h-d}{h^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\,(d-c)\!\left(\frac{1}{d^{2}}-\frac{1}{h^{2}}\right)+A.

Thus
B>A>0
and

e^{-A}-e^{-B}=\int_{A}^{B}e^{-t}\,dt\leq(B-A)\,e^{-A}\leq B-A.

Therefore, the lefthand side of (
31
) satisfies

e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}\!\left[e^{-A}-e^{-B}\right]\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}(B-A).

Now write
h=c+\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon:=h-c>0
, and parametrize

d=c+\tau\varepsilon
with
\tau\in(0,1)
. A direct calculation yields
that

B-A=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\,\tau(1-\tau)\,\bigl(2c+\varepsilon(1+\tau)\bigr)}{2\,(c+\tau\varepsilon)^{2}\,(c+\varepsilon)^{2}}.

Dividing by
(h-c)^{3/2}=\varepsilon^{3/2}
one obtains

\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}\!\left[e^{-A}-e^{-B}\right]}{(h-c)^{3/2}}\;\leq\;e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}\,\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}\,\tau(1-\tau)\,\bigl(2c+\varepsilon(1+\tau)\bigr)}{2\,(c+\tau\varepsilon)^{2}\,(c+\varepsilon)^{2}}=:\Phi(c,\varepsilon,\tau).

(32)

We need to bound
\Phi
uniformly over the domain
0<c<d<h
(equivalently, over

\varepsilon>0
,
\tau\in(0,1)
).
Use
\tau(1-\tau)\leq\tfrac{1}{4}
,
2c+\varepsilon(1+\tau)\leq 2(c+\varepsilon)
, and
\tau,\varepsilon>0,
to obtain

\Phi(c,\varepsilon,\tau)\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}\,\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{4}\cdot\frac{2(c+\varepsilon)}{2\,(c+\tau\varepsilon)^{2}(c+\varepsilon)^{2}}=e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}\,\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{4\,(c+\tau\varepsilon)^{2}(c+\varepsilon)}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}\,\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{4\,c^{2}(c+\varepsilon)}=:\Psi_{c}(\varepsilon).

Maximize
\Psi_{c}(\varepsilon)
over
\varepsilon>0
, by setting
x=\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, and writing

\Psi_{c}(\varepsilon)=\exp\left({-\dfrac{1}{2c}}\right)\,\dfrac{x}{4\,c^{2}(c+x^{2})}:

the maximum occurs at
x^{2}=c,
hence

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\Psi_{c}(\varepsilon)=e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{c}}{4\,c^{2}(c+c)}=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}}{8\,c^{5/2}}.

Since
\sup_{c>0}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}}{8\,c^{5/2}}<0.6
, (
32
) yields that

\sup_{0<c<d<h}\;\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}\!\left[e^{-A}-e^{-B}\right]}{(h-c)^{3/2}}\leq\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2c}}}{8\,c^{5/2}}<0.6,

and (
31
) is verified.

Checking the second probability.

We have

\displaystyle P^{(n)}(L_{k_{1}}\neq n,L_{k_{2}}=n,L_{k_{3}}\neq n)=P^{(n)}(L_{k_{1}}\neq n)P(L_{k_{2}}=n\mid L_{k_{1}}\neq n)P^{(n)}(L_{k_{3}}\neq n\mid L_{k_{2}}=n,L_{k_{1}}\neq n).

But the very last conditioning on
L_{k_{1}}\neq n
can be dropped, that is

\displaystyle P^{(n)}(L_{k_{1}}\neq n,L_{k_{2}}=n,L_{k_{3}}\neq n)=P^{(n)}(L_{k_{1}}\neq n)P^{(n)}(L_{k_{2}}=n\mid L_{k_{1}}\neq n)P^{(n)}(L_{k_{3}}\neq n\mid L_{k_{2}}=n),

(33)

because if at
k_{2}
the process is in a state of detachment then it does not matter where exactly the passengers are located at that time.
Hence, similarly to the first part, it follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}P^{(n)}(L_{k_{1}}\neq n,L_{k_{2}}=n,L_{k_{3}}\neq n)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left[\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{2d}\right)-\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{1}{2c}-\dfrac{d-c}{2d^{2}}\right)\right]\left[1-\exp\left(-\frac{h-d}{2h^{2}}\right)\right]=:G(c,d,h).

(34)

For
1\leq c<d<h\leq T
, we are going to show the existence of a constant
K(T)>0
such that

\displaystyle F(c,d,h):=\frac{G(c,d,h)}{(h-c)^{3/2}}\leq K(T).

(35)

(So
\alpha
does not depend on
T
but
K
does.)
First note that for
u,v\geq 0
,
e^{-u}-e^{-(u+v)}=e^{-u}(1-e^{-v})\leq v\,e^{-u}
, and so with

u=\frac{1}{2d},\quad v=\Bigl(\frac{1}{2c}-\frac{1}{2d}\Bigr)+\frac{d-c}{2d^{2}}=\frac{(d-c)(d+c)}{2cd^{2}},

we get

\displaystyle\bigl[e^{-\frac{1}{2d}}-e^{-\frac{1}{2c}-\frac{d-c}{2d^{2}}}\bigr]\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2d}}\cdot\frac{(d-c)(d+c)}{2cd^{2}}.

Since we also have
\bigl[1-e^{-\frac{h-d}{2h^{2}}}\bigr]\leq\frac{h-d}{2h^{2}},
it follows that

F(c,d,h)\;\leq\;e^{-\frac{1}{2d}}\,\frac{(d-c)(h-d)(d+c)}{4\,c\,d^{2}\,h^{2}}\,(h-c)^{-3/2}.

Note that
c,d,h>1,

e^{-\frac{1}{2d}}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2T}},

while

(d-c)(h-d)\leq\dfrac{(h-c)^{2}}{4}

and
d+c\leq 2h
. Thus

F(c,d,h)\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2T}}\,\frac{\frac{(h-c)^{2}}{4}\cdot 2h}{4\,c\,d^{2}\,h^{2}}\,(h-c)^{-3/2}=e^{-\frac{1}{2T}}\,\frac{(h-c)^{1/2}}{32\,c\,d^{2}\,h}\leq\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2T}}\cdot\sqrt{T}}{32}=:K(T),

which verifies (
35
).

5
Further scaling limit results

5.1
Detachment states in expectation — quadratic scaling with logarithmic correction

A subtle yet crucial distinction emerges in this subsection: while quadratic scaling is necessary to define the process’s scaling limit (cf. Theorem
4.1
), the behavior of the
expected number of detached states
demonstrates that sub-quadratic scaling is appropriate for this specific metric.

Let
X_{t}:=\mathds{1}(L_{t}=n)
for
t\geq n.
We recall from (
2
) that

\pi_{n,k}:=P(L_{k}=n)=\frac{(k)_{n}}{k^{n}},
and
define

\displaystyle e(n,k):=E^{(n)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k(n)}X_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k(n)}p_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{k(n)}P(L_{j}=n)=\sum_{j=1}^{k(n)}j^{-n}j(j-1)\dots(j-n+1)

(36)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=n}^{k(n)}j^{-n}j(j-1)\dots(j-n+1)=\sum_{j=n}^{k(n)}j^{-n}(j)_{n},

which is the expected number of detachment states up to
k(n)
.

The behavior of
e(n,k(n))
for large
n
and for various choices of
k(n)
is examined next.

Theorem 5.1

(Expected number of detachment states)
.

If

k(n)\sim\frac{n^{2}}{c\log n},\ n\gg 1,

and
c>4

then
e(n,k)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, i.e.
\sum_{j=1}^{k(n)}X_{j}\to 0
in
L^{1}
(cf. Theorem
3.4
(ii)), while
if
c<4
then
e(n,k)\to\infty.

Furthermore, if
y>0
and

\displaystyle k(n)=k(n,y):=\frac{n^{2}}{2\,(2\log n-2\log\log n+y)}\sim\frac{n^{2}}{4\log n},

(37)

then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}e(n,k(n))=\frac{e^{-y}}{8}=:c(y).

(38)

Corollary 5.2

(The critical time window)
.

Expanding
k(n,y)
as

\displaystyle k(n,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{4\log n}+\frac{n^{2}\log\log n}{4(\log n)^{2}}-\frac{n^{2}y}{8(\log n)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{2}(\log\log n)^{2}}{(\log n)^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{4\log n}\left\{1+\frac{\log\log n-y/2}{\log n}+\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{(\log\log n)}{(\log n)}\right]^{2}\right)\right\},

(39)

we conclude that for
c>1/8
and

\displaystyle k(n,c):=\frac{n^{2}}{4\log n}\left\{1+\frac{\log\log n+\log\sqrt{8c}}{\log n}+\mathcal{O}\left(\left[\frac{(\log\log n)}{(\log n)}\right]^{2}\right)\right\}

the relation
\lim_{n}e(n,k(n,c))=c
holds, that is one needs
k(n,c)
amount of time to have
c
detachment events on average with
n\gg 1
passengers. Loosely speaking, with
0<\mathrm{const}_{1}<\mathrm{const}_{2}
, the
critical time window
is about

\left[\frac{n^{2}}{4\log n}\left\{1+\frac{\log\log n+\mathrm{const}_{1}}{\log n}\right\},\frac{n^{2}}{4\log n}\left\{1+\frac{\log\log n+\mathrm{const}_{2}}{\log n}\right\}\right]

for large
n
, and the expected number of times in a state of detachment there is about

\frac{1}{8}\left(\exp(2\,\mathrm{const}_{2})-\exp(2\,\mathrm{const}_{1})\right)
. The size of the window around
n^{2}/(4\log n)
is

\sim\mathrm{const}\cdot\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)^{2}
.

Proof.

(of Theorem
5.1
:)

We first consider the
k(n)\sim\dfrac{n^{2}}{c\log n}
case.

Lower bound and
e(n,k)\to\infty
for
c<4
.

In order to analyze the behavior of

e(n,k)=\sum_{j=n}^{k(n)}j^{-n}j(j-1)\dots(j-n+1)=\sum_{j=n}^{k(n)}j^{-n}(j)_{n},

we first focus on the term
j^{-n}(j)_{n}
and will use that for
1<a<b
,

\int_{0}^{a}\log\!\left(1-\frac{x}{b}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x=\;-a\;-\;(b-a)\,\log\Bigl(1-\frac{a}{b}\Bigr),\qquad 0\leq a<b,

so for
n\geq 2
and
j\geq n,

0\geq F^{(n-1)}(j):=\int_{0}^{\,n-1}\log\Bigl(1-\frac{x}{j}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x\;=\;-(n-1)\;-\;(\,j-n+1\,)\,\log\Bigl(1-\frac{n-1}{j}\Bigr),\qquad j\geq n.

Hence, using monotonicity, and comparing the sum with the integral,

\log(j^{-n}(j)_{n})=\log(1-1/j)+...+\log(1-(n-2)/j)\geq F^{(n-1)}(j),\ j\geq n.

Extend the definition of
F^{(n-1)}
as

F^{(n-1)}(x):=\;-(n-1)\;-\;(\,x-n+1\,)\,\log\Bigl(1-\frac{n-1}{x}\Bigr)=-x\left[z\;+\;(\,1-z\,)\,\log\Bigl(1-z\Bigr)\right],\qquad x>n-1,

where
z:=\frac{n-1}{x},
and note that in
e(n,k)
the index starts at
j=n
.
Since
e^{F}
is strictly increasing in
j
, we can use a sum-integral comparison once again: for
k>n-1
,

e(n,k)\geq\sum_{j=n}^{k}e^{F^{(n-1)}(j)}\geq\int_{n-1}^{k}e^{F^{(n-1)}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Hence, it suffices to verify that if

I(n)\;=\;\int_{\,n-1}^{\,k(n)}\exp\!\big(F^{(n-1)}(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\text{with\ }k(n)\sim\frac{n^{2}}{c\log n},\quad 0<c<4,

then
\lim_{n\to\infty}I(n)=\infty.

First,
use the expansion on
0<z<1
:

z+(1-z)\log(1-z)\leq\frac{z^{2}}{2}+K_{0}z^{3},\ \text{as}\ z\to 0,

where
K_{0}
is an absolute constant (in fact
K_{0}=1/2
),
yielding for
x>n-1
that

F^{(n-1)}(x)\geq-\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{2x}-K_{0}\!\frac{(n-1)^{3}}{x^{2}},\ \text{as}\ n\to\infty,\ \frac{n}{x}\to 0,

hence

e^{F^{(n-1)}(x)}\geq\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{(n-1)^{2}}{2x}-\text{err}\Bigr),\ \text{as}\ \frac{n}{x}\to 0,

where
\text{err}\leq K_{0}\!\dfrac{(n-1)^{3}}{x^{2}}.

Next, we break the region of integration in
I(n)
to

[n-1,\,k(n)/2)
and
[k(n)/2,k(n)]
. Let us now consider the integral

I_{2}(n)\;:=\;\int_{k(n)/2}^{k(n)}\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{(n-1)^{2}}{2x}-\text{err}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Change variables and
let
C_{n}:=\tfrac{(n-1)^{2}}{2}
, write
u:=C_{n}/x
, hence
\mathrm{d}x=-C_{n}\,\mathrm{d}u/u^{2}
, and note that for the error term one has

\text{err}\leq 4K_{0}\,\dfrac{(n-1)^{3}}{k(n)^{2}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty.

Consequently, as
n\to\infty,

I_{2}(n)\geq\int_{k/2}^{k}e^{-(C_{n}/x)-\text{err}}\,\mathrm{d}x\sim\int_{k/2}^{k}e^{-C_{n}/x}\,\mathrm{d}x\sim C_{n}\int_{C_{n}/k}^{2C_{n}/k}\frac{e^{-u}}{u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u\;\sim\;\frac{k^{2}}{C_{n}}\,e^{-C_{n}/k},

where in the last step we used the asymptotics

\int_{x}^{2x}\frac{e^{-u}}{u^{2}}du\sim\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{2}},\ x\to\infty
, and that
C_{n}/k=C_{n}/k(n)\to\infty.

Similar calculation shows that with
c_{1}:=c/2
and
n\gg 1,

\displaystyle I_{1}(n)\;:=\;\int_{\,n-1}^{\,k(n)/2}\exp\!\big(F^{(n-1)}(x)\big)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\;\int^{k(n)/2}_{n-1}\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{(n-1)^{2}}{2x}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}x=C_{n}\int_{2C_{n}/k}^{(n-1)/2}\frac{e^{-u}}{u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=C_{n}\int_{(\frac{n-1}{n})^{2}c\log n+o(1)}^{(n-1)/2}\frac{e^{-u}}{u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u\leq C_{n}\int_{c_{1}\log n}^{(n-1)/2}\frac{e^{-u}}{u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u=\frac{n^{-c_{1}}}{c_{1}^{2}(\log n)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n^{-c_{1}}}{(\log n)^{3}}\right).

It follows that
I_{1}(n)=o(I_{2}(n)),
since
k(n)\sim\dfrac{n^{2}}{c\log n}
,
c_{1}=c/2
and so

I_{1}(n)/I_{2}(n)=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{(c/2)-c_{1}}\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{n^{4}}\right)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-2}).

Therefore

I(n)\;\sim\;I_{2}(n)\;\sim\;\frac{2\,k(n)^{2}}{n^{2}}\,\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{n^{2}}{2k(n)}\Bigr).

Now, if
a_{n}:=\frac{n^{2}}{2k(n)}\to\infty
then

I(n)\sim\dfrac{n^{2}}{2}\dfrac{e^{-a_{n}}}{a_{n}^{2}}

and since
k(n)\sim\dfrac{n^{2}}{c\log n}
,
a_{n}\sim\frac{c}{2}\log n
,
and
e^{-a_{n}}=\exp(-\frac{c}{2}\log n+o(1))
, yielding

\displaystyle I(n)\sim\frac{2n^{2}}{(c\log n)^{2}}\frac{1}{\exp(\frac{c}{2}\log n+o(1))},

(40)

and if
c\neq 4
then this implies that

\displaystyle\quad I(n)\;\sim\;\frac{2}{c^{2}}\,\frac{n^{\,2-\tfrac{c}{2}}}{(\log n)^{2}},\qquad n\to\infty.\quad

(41)

It follows that if
c<4
then
I(n)\to\infty
polynomially, and therefore
e(n,k)\to\infty
too.

Upper bound and
e(n,k)\to 0
for
c>4
.

After noticing that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=n}^{k(n)}j^{-n}j(j-1)\dots(j-n+1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=n}^{k(n)}\left(1-\frac{1}{j}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{j}\right)\dots\left(1-\frac{n-1}{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=n}^{k(n)}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{j}\left(1+2+\dots+n-1\right)\right\},

(42)

the rest of the argument is similar but much simpler than for the lower bound, leading again to the conclusion that the righthand side of (
5.1
) is asymptotical with the expression on the righthand side of (
41
), but for

it implies convergence to zero.
We leave the details to the reader.

Fine tuning for
k(n)\sim\dfrac{n^{2}}{4\log n}
.

We will use the same notation as before. Just like in the previous parts of the proof,
the relation (
40
) still holds, but now, as

, the asymptotic behavior depends on the
o(1)=a_{n}-2\log n=:b_{n}
term there, and we obtain that

\displaystyle I(n)\sim\frac{2}{(4\log n)^{2}}\frac{1}{e^{b_{n}}},

(43)

and this converges to a positive finite limit if and only if

and

we arrive at the choice

Remark 5.3

(Second moment formula)
.

Let, as before,
X_{i}:=\mathds{1}\{L_{i}=n\}
; let
M_{2}(n,k):=E\left(\sum_{1}^{k}X_{i}\right)^{2}
. The explicit formula for
M_{2}(n,k)
below might be of interest. By (

for
0<k,l
,

\displaystyle\pi(n,k,l)=P^{(n)}(L_{k+l}=L_{k}=n)=\frac{(k)_{n}}{(k+l)^{n}}\sum_{r=0}^{\min(n,l)}\binom{n}{r}\frac{(l)_{r}}{k^{r}}=\frac{(k)_{n}}{(k+l)^{n}}\,{}_{2}F_{0}(-n,-l;-;1/k),

hence

\displaystyle M_{2}(n,k):=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{k}\frac{(m)_{n}}{m^{\,n}}+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,l\geq 1\\

5.2
‘‘Almost detachment’’ — waiting for super-linear times

We now show the perhaps surprising result that although one has to wait roughly quadratic (in
n
) time for complete detachment,
any
super-linearly large time is sufficient for seeing ‘‘almost complete’’ detachment.

Theorem 5.4

(Almost detachment along any sequence of super-linear times)
.

Assume that
n\ll k=k(n).

Then, as
n\to\infty,

\displaystyle\frac{N_{k(n)}}{n}\to 1,\ \frac{L_{k(n)}}{n}\to 1\qquad\text{in probability}.

(44)

Proof.

(i) For the first convergence, since
N_{k}\leq n
for all
k\geq 1
, we only need to show that for
\varepsilon>0,

\lim_{n}P^{(n)}\left(N_{k(n)}<(1-\varepsilon)n\right)=0.

Using Stirling numbers of the second kind (see Appendix A), if

a_{n,j}:=k^{-n}\binom{k}{j}S(n,j)j!,\qquad 1\leq j\leq n,

then

\mathbb{P}\!\left(N_{k}\leq(1-\varepsilon)n\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{(1-\varepsilon)n}a_{n,j},

where the reason for having the factor
j!
is that we consider labeled buses.
Let
C:=n-N_{k}
and

Y:=\sum_{1\leq a<b\leq n}\mathds{1}_{\{\text{passengers $a$ and $b$ sit in the same bus}\}}.

If a bus has
r\geq 1
passengers, then its contribution to
C
is
r-1
, while its
contribution to
Y
is
\binom{r}{2}
. Since
\binom{r}{2}\geq r-1
for all

r\geq 1
(for
r=1
both vanish), we obtain

C\;\leq\;Y,
and hence

\mathbb{E}[C]\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}[Y]=\frac{n(n-1)}{2k}.

By Markov’s inequality, and since
n\ll k,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(N_{k}\leq(1-\varepsilon)n\right)=\mathbb{P}(C\geq\varepsilon n)\;\leq\;\frac{\mathbb{E}[C]}{\varepsilon n}\;\leq\;\frac{n(n-1)}{2\varepsilon nk}=\frac{n-1}{2\varepsilon k}\longrightarrow 0.

(ii) The second convergence follows from the first and the following simple observation:

N_{k}\geq L_{k};\quad n\geq L_{k}+2(N_{k}-L_{k})

(which must hold as non-empty buses without lonely passengers have at least two passengers),
that is,

n-(N_{k}-L_{k})\geq N_{k},

or

n/N_{k}-(N_{k}-L_{k})/N_{k}\geq 1.

To conclude the proof, note that if for some positive integer
r
,
\dfrac{N_{k}-L_{k}}{N_{k}}\geq\dfrac{1}{r}
(that is,
L_{k}/N_{k}\leq(r-1)/r
) then

N_{k}\leq\dfrac{r}{r+1}n.
But by (i), the probability of this latter event converges to zero. Hence
L_{k}/N_{k}\to 1
in probability, and so again by (i),
L_{k}/n\to 1
in probability too.
∎

6
Some results on the support size

In this section we analyze the size of the support at a fixed time (and importantly, not “up to that time”).
We recall that
N_{k}
denotes the size of the support (i.e. number of non-empty buses) at time
k
. We only give two results here, because the (limiting) distribution of the number of
empty buses
in various regimes has been investigated in
[
5
]
.

6.1
The tail of the support size with Stirling numbers

In the sequel the reader may want to consult Appendix A for material on Stirling numbers of the second kind, denoted by
S(a,b).

Lemma 6.1

(Tail of support size)
.

If
n\geq 2
then for
m\leq\min\{n,k\}
,

P^{(n)}(N_{k}\geq m)=\frac{k!}{(k-m)!}\left(k^{-m}+\sum_{u=m}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k^{u+1}}S(u,m-1)\right).

Proof.

For a fixed
k\geq 1,
we are going to use induction on
n
, by sitting the passengers one by one. Since each passenger chooses a bus uniformly and independently of the others, one has

\displaystyle P^{(n)}(N_{k}\geq m)=P^{(n-1)}(N_{k}\geq m)+P^{(n-1)}(N_{k}=m-1)\cdot\frac{k-m+1}{k}.

(45)

By the definition of
S(a,b)
, we may write

P^{(n-1)}(N_{k}=m-1)=k^{1-n}\binom{k}{m-1}(m-1)!\cdot S(n-1,m-1).

(As before, the factor
(m-1)!
appears because we have labeled buses.)
We thus obtained

P^{(n)}(N_{k}\geq m)=P^{(n-1)}(N_{k}\geq m)+k^{-n}\frac{k!}{(k-m)!}\cdot S(n-1,m-1),

and by induction, this leads to

\displaystyle P^{(n)}(N_{k}\geq m)

\displaystyle=P^{(m)}(N_{k}=m)+\frac{k!}{(k-m)!}\sum_{u=m}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k^{u+1}}S(u,m-1)

\displaystyle=k^{-m}\binom{k}{m}m!+\frac{k!}{(k-m)!}\sum_{u=m}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k^{u+1}}S(u,m-1),

as claimed.
∎

6.2
Support as birth process for fixed times

Recall that
N_{k}
denotes the support size at time
k
.

Theorem 6.2

(Distribution of support size)
.

For
n
passengers, the generating function of
N_{k}
equals

G_{n}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k-1}{j-1}\left(\frac{j}{k}\right)^{n-1}z^{\,j}(1-z)^{\,k-j}.

In particular,

\displaystyle{E}^{(n)}[N_{k}]

\displaystyle=G_{n}^{\prime}(1)=k-(k-1)\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{k}\bigr)^{n-1}=k\left[1-\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{n}\right],

\displaystyle{E}^{(n)}[N_{k}^{2}]

\displaystyle=k^{2}-(2k-1)(k-1)\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{n-1}+(k-1)(k-2)\left(1-\frac{2}{k}\right)^{\,n-1},

while

\mathsf{Var}^{(n)}(N_{k})=(k-1)\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{n-1}-(k-1)\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{2(n-1)}+(k-2)\left(1-\frac{2}{k}\right)^{n-1}\right].

Proof.

Fixing
k
and sitting down the passengers one by one, we see that
(N^{(n)}_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a pure birth chain on
\{1,2,\dots,k\}
with

\displaystyle P^{(n)}(N_{k+1}=m+1\mid N_{k}=m)=\frac{k-m}{k},\qquad P^{(n)}(N_{k+1}=m\mid N_{k}=m)=\frac{m}{k}.

(46)

Starting at
N_{1}=1
, for
n\geq 2
and
1\leq r\leq k
, the distribution is (see
[
3
]
)

\displaystyle P^{(n)}(N_{k}=r)=\binom{k-1}{r-1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(-1)^{\,r-j}\binom{r-1}{\,j-1\,}\left(\frac{j}{k}\right)^{n-1}.

(47)

(In
[
3
]
there is a slightly different but equivalent form.)
In fact, the generating function

G_{n}(z):=\mathbb{E}[z^{X_{n}}]

admits the closed form

G_{n}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k-1}{j-1}\left(\frac{j}{k}\right)^{n-1}z^{\,j}(1-z)^{\,k-j}.

We include a short proof for completeness, although this result is established (see
[
9
]
).
Using (
47
), (valid for
n\geq 2
and
1\leq r\leq k
), one has

G_{n}(z)=\sum_{r=1}^{k}P^{(n)}(X_{n}=r)\,z^{r}=\sum_{r=1}^{k}\binom{k-1}{r-1}z^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}(-1)^{\,r-j}\binom{r-1}{\,j-1\,}\left(\frac{j}{k}\right)^{n-1}.

Swapping the order of summation (note
j\leq r\leq k
):

G_{n}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{j}{k}\right)^{n-1}\sum_{r=j}^{k}\binom{k-1}{r-1}\binom{r-1}{\,j-1\,}(-1)^{\,r-j}z^{r}.

Using the identity

\binom{k-1}{r-1}\binom{r-1}{\,j-1\,}=\binom{k-1}{j-1}\binom{k-j}{\,r-j\,},

writing
r=j+m
with
m=0,\dots,k-j
:

\displaystyle G_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{j}{k}\right)^{n-1}\binom{k-1}{j-1}\sum_{m=0}^{k-j}\binom{k-j}{m}(-1)^{\,m}z^{\,j+m}

(48)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{j}{k}\right)^{n-1}\binom{k-1}{j-1}z^{\,j}\sum_{m=0}^{k-j}\binom{k-j}{m}(-1)^{\,m}z^{\,m},

(49)

and finally, recognizing the binomial expansion:

\sum_{m=0}^{k-j}\binom{k-j}{m}(-1)^{m}z^{m}=(1-z)^{\,k-j},

it follows that

G_{n}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k-1}{j-1}\left(\frac{j}{k}\right)^{n-1}z^{\,j}(1-z)^{\,k-j},

as claimed.
∎

7
Measuring clumping

7.1
Clumping

One possible way to quantify how much ‘‘detached’’ a configuration is to measure its ‘‘clumping.’’

Definition 7.1
.

Let
M_{k}=M_{1,k}
be the number of passengers in the bus of the passenger with label 1 at time
k
, including that passenger. Let
\tau_{1}^{\mathsf{lon}}:=\min\{k\geq\lceil n/2\rceil\mid M_{1,k}=1\}
, that is, the first time (not less than
n/2
) when the first passenger becomes lonely.
By Theorem
3.1
,
\tau_{1}^{\mathsf{lon}}<\infty
a.s.

The next result says that
M
behaves as a supermartingale between
\lceil n/2\rceil
and
\tau_{1}^{\mathsf{lon}}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

\{M(k\wedge\tau_{1}^{\mathsf{lon}})\}_{k\geq\lceil n/2\rceil}
is a supermartingale.

Proof.

The passenger we follow either relocates with probability
1/k
or stays with probability

(k-1)/k
, hence, when
n\leq 2k
and
a\geq 2
,

\displaystyle E^{(n)}(M_{k}\mid M_{k-1}=a,M_{k-2}=b_{k-2},...,M_{1}=b_{1})=E^{(n)}(M_{k}\mid M_{k-1}=a)

\displaystyle=(1/k)\mathsf{E}[1+\mathsf{Bin}(n-1,1/k)]+(k-1/k)\mathsf{E}[(a-\mathsf{Bin}(a-1,1/k)]

\displaystyle=(1/k)[1+(n-1)/k]+(k-1/k)[(a-(a-1)/k]\leq a,

since the inequality is equivalent to
n-1\leq(2k-1)(a-1).

(We do not have to consider
a=1
because of the stopping.)
∎

Definition 7.3

(Measuring clumping)
.

Let
W^{i}_{k}
be the number of passengers in bus number
i
for
1\leq i\leq k
at time
k
. Let
\widehat{M}_{k}:=\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}(W^{i}_{k})^{2}
.

Note that
\widehat{M}_{k}
measures how ‘‘clumped’’ the configuration is. Indeed if in a given bus, the passengers are re-distributed into a number (
>1
) of empty buses then
\widehat{M}_{k}
decreases. Hence
n\leq\widehat{M}_{k}\leq n^{2}
. Furthermore, since
\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}W^{i}_{k}=n
, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\widehat{M}_{k}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{k},

and equality corresponds to the case when
k
divides
n
and each bus has
n/k
passengers. Thus, altogether, one has

n^{2}\geq\widehat{M}_{k}\geq\max\left\{n,\frac{n^{2}}{k}\right\}.

Again, by Theorem
3.1
, with probability one, there exists a
k_{0}
such that
\widehat{M}_{k}=n,k\geq k_{0}
.

Let

\tau^{\mathsf{lon}}:=\tau_{1}^{\mathsf{lon}}\wedge\dots\wedge\tau_{n}^{\mathsf{lon}}

be the first time
k\geq n/2
that there exists at least one lonely passenger, where
\tau_{j}^{\mathsf{lon}}
is defined similarly as before.

Corollary 7.4
.

The process
\{\widehat{M}(k\wedge\tau^{\mathsf{lon}})\}_{k\geq\lceil n/2\rceil}
is a supermartingale.

Proof.

If
M_{i,k}
is defined similarly to
M_{1,k}
, then we have
\widehat{M}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i,k}
(since in a given bus, the number of travelers are counted as many times as that number is) and
M(i\wedge\tau_{i}^{\mathsf{lon}})
is a supermartingale starting with
\lceil n/2\rceil
by Lemma
7.2
, hence so is

M(i\wedge\tau_{i}^{\mathsf{lon}}\wedge\tau^{\mathsf{lon}})=M(i\wedge\tau^{\mathsf{lon}}).
This is then true for their sums as well.
∎

7.2
Relative clumping

Definition 7.5
.

We define the relative clumping (that is, relative to the number of passengers) by

\mathsf{RC}_{n,k}:=\log(n^{-1}\widehat{M}_{k})

and note that
\mathsf{RC}_{n,k}\in[0,\log n]
for all
k\geq 1
Furthermore,
\mathsf{RC}_{n,k}=0
if and only if
n\leq k
and the
n
balls are fully detached at
k
. On the other hand
\log n
is only attained when every passenger is in the same bus at
k
.

Next, we show that the tail of
\mathsf{RC}_{n,cn}
is (at least) exponentially small, uniformly in
n
.

Proposition 7.6

(Sharp drop of rel. clumping in linear time)
.

If
c>0
then

\max_{n\geq 1}P^{(n)}(\mathsf{RC}_{n,\lfloor cn\rfloor}>x)\leq(1+c^{-1})e^{-x},\quad x>0.

Proof.

Since
W_{k}^{i}
is
\mathsf{Bin}(n,1/k)
-distributed,
E^{(n)}(W_{k}^{i})^{2}=\dfrac{n}{k}\left(\dfrac{n-1}{k}+1\right)
. The statement then follows from the Markov-inequality.
∎

The proposition demonstrates that while complete “decumpling” (i.e., complete detachment) requires roughly a quadratic time interval, relative clumping significantly decreases from the initial
\log n
below a constant (dependent on
c
) already within
cn
time. To illustrate this phenomenon, we present Fig.
2
, which depicts a pronounced drop in relative clumping at the outset and, respectively, zoom in to reveal fluctuations within the initial 500 steps. These simulations were conducted using Chat GPT 5.

Remark 7.7
.

Since

Poisson number of passengers; a result on comparing binomials

When the number of passengers
\mathcal{N}
is not a fixed number
n
, but is random and Poisson distributed with parameter
\lambda>0
, the calculations and proofs are easier. In this case, the number of passengers in any given bus is obtained by ‘‘thinning’’ (or splitting) the Poisson distribution and thus it is itself Poisson distributed with parameter
\lambda/k
, and those passenger numbers are independent. For example,

P_{\lambda}(\text{full detachment at\ }k)=P_{\lambda}(L_{k}=\mathcal{N})=\left[e^{-\lambda/k}(1+\lambda/k)\right]^{k}=e^{-\lambda}(1+\lambda/k)^{k},

where
P_{\lambda}
refers to the law when the passenger number has parameter
\lambda.

In particular,

\lim_{k\to\infty}P_{\lambda}(\text{full detachment at\ }k)=1,

and a bit more generally, if
\lambda(k)=o(k)
, then
\lim_{k\to\infty}P_{\lambda}(\text{full detachment at\ }k)=1
still holds. However, for any
\lambda=\lambda(k):=ck,c>0
,

\lim_{k\to\infty}P_{\lambda}(\text{full detachment at\ }k)=0,

exponentially fast.

We next note that, in the case of a Poissonian passenger number (with a fixed
\lambda
) it is very easy to prove the monotonicity of the probability in Theorem
2.1
, without the sophistication of the proofs in
[
4
,
11
]
.
Indeed, using the independence of passenger numbers in the buses and the Poissonian thinning explained above, the probability of not having any bus with a single passenger is

(1-(\lambda/k)e^{-\lambda/k})^{k}.

Hence, it is enough to check that the function
f(x):=(1-xe^{-x})^{1/x}
is nonincreasing on the positive axis. Now, for
x>0,

\mathsf{sgn}(f^{\prime}(x))=-\mathsf{sgn}(\log x(x-1))\leq 0
, and we are done.

For the more general statement on stochastic dominance, let us first introduce the notation
\prec,\preceq
for (strict) stochastic dominance.

8.1
Comparing binomial distributions

We will also need the following (unpublished) theorem due to J. Najnudel
[
7
]
, which is of independent interest.

Lemma 8.1

(Najnudel’s theorem)
.

Let
X\sim\mathsf{Bin}(n,p)
and
Y\sim\mathsf{Bin}(m,q)
. For
Y\preceq X
to hold, it is necessary and sufficient that
n\geq m
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{n,p}(X=0)=(1-p)^{n}\leq(1-q)^{m}=\mathbf{P}^{m,q}(Y=0).

(50)

Note that necessity is trivial.
Since Najnudel’s proof is unpublished, we are going to provide here a relatively simple proof (different from Najnudel’s).

Proof.

First,
n<m
rules out the domination, as
X=m
is impossible. When
n=m
, domination occurs if and only if
p\geq q

(by representing
X,Y
as sums of i.i.d. Bernoulli variables). Hence, it is enough to assume that
m<n
and check the sufficiency of (
50
).

Let
\mathsf{PPP}(\lambda)
and
\mathsf{PPP}(\widehat{\lambda})
denote the Poisson point process on
[0,n)
with constant intensity
\lambda>0
and the Poisson point process won
[0,m)
with constant intensity
\widehat{\lambda}>0
, respectively. By ‘‘cell
i
’’ we will mean the interval
[i-1,i),i\geq 1
. These point processes induce binomial distributions with parameters
n,p
and
m,q
respectively if the ‘‘
i
th success’’ means that cell
i
is non-empty, and if
1-p=e^{-\lambda}
and
1-q=e^{-\widehat{\lambda}}
. Thus, the assumption in the lemma is tantamount to
e^{-n\lambda}\leq e^{-m\widehat{\lambda}}
,
that is
n\lambda\geq m\widehat{\lambda}
. We need to show that the last inequality implies the stochastic dominance in the statement.

Let us re-partition
[0,n)
into
m
equal intervals, each of length
n/m
(that is, into the sub-intervals
[0,n/m),[n/m,2n/m),...,[n-n/m,n)
, which yields cell-size
n/m
). This new partition with the same
\lambda
induces a different binomial distribution than before, namely the one with parameters
n
and
1-e^{-(n/m)\lambda}
. Since
n\lambda\geq m\widehat{\lambda}
, this last binomial distribution stochastically dominates the one with parameters
(m,q)=(m,1-e^{-\widehat{\lambda}})
. Therefore, to complete the proof, it is enough to show that with
\lambda
fixed, the number of non-empty cells in the
n
-partition of
[0,n)
stochastically dominates the number of non-empty cells in the
m
-partition of
[0,n)
.
This is accomplished by noting that
\mathsf{PPP}(\lambda)
on
[0,n)
can be realized by picking a Poisson(
\lambda n
) number of random points on
[0,n)
, one after the other, at each unit time, independently, such that each point’s location is uniformly distributed on
[0,n)
. The number of non-empty cells is going to be non-decreasing during this process up to time
T
which is Poisson(
\lambda n
)-distributed. That is, we have a pure birth process observed at a random time, and at that time we are interested in the number of non-empty cells. Finally, the statement follows from the fact that one birth rate dominates the other, and it is well known that this implies that the first birth process stochastically dominates the other at
each
given time. Indeed, if the current state of the birth process is
y
then the probability of birth is
1-y/n
resp.
1-y/m
. The proof is complete.
∎

8.2
Tóth’s theorem in the Poissonian case

With Najnudel’s theorem in our toolset, we can now verify Tóth’s theorem in the Poissonian setup.

Theorem 8.2

(Tóth’s theorem in the Poissonian case)
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
k_{2}>k_{1}.
Then

L_{k_{1}}\preceq L_{k_{2}}.

(51)

Proof.

In fact even
\prec
holds. By independence,

L_{k_{i}}
has distribution
\mathsf{Bin}(k_{i},\frac{\lambda}{k_{i}}e^{-\lambda/k_{i}}),
because in one cell the probability of receiving a single point is
\frac{\lambda}{k_{i}}\exp(-\lambda/k_{i})
. Since
k_{1}<k_{2}
, we may use Lemma
8.1
, for the stochastic dominance between binomials with

m=k_{1},n=k_{2}
,
q=(\lambda/k_{1})\exp(-\lambda/k_{1})
and
p=(\lambda/k_{2})\exp(-\lambda/k_{2})
. For the second condition to hold, introduce
u_{i}:=\lambda/k_{i}
, and the condition becomes that

f(u_{2})\geq f(u_{1}),
where
f(x):=(1-xe^{-x})^{1/x}
and as we have seen,
f
is non-increasing, and strictly decreasing for
0<x\neq 1
, finishing the proof.
∎

Appendix A: Stirling numbers of the second kind

In the paper, the following notion from combinatorics is used: A
Stirling number of the second kind
(or Stirling partition number) is the number of ways to partition a set of
a
objects into
b\leq a
non-empty subsets and is denoted by

S(a,b)
.
Here one considers
labeled
objects and
unlabeled
subsets.
Some useful known results on Stirling numbers of the second kind are collected below.

The following asymptotics is known
[
10
]
when
n/m=v>1
and
n\to\infty
:

\displaystyle S(n,m)\sim\alpha_{v}\cdot\beta_{v}^{n}\frac{m^{n}}{n^{m}}\gamma_{v}^{m}\binom{n}{m},

(52)

where
\alpha_{v},\beta_{v},\gamma_{v}
are explicit positive constants, depending only on
v
. For example, if
v=2
, then using that

\binom{2m}{m}\sim\frac{2^{2m}}{\sqrt{m\pi}},\ \text{as}\ m\to\infty,

one obtains that

S(2m,m)\sim k_{v}^{m}\cdot m^{m}(m\pi)^{-1/2},

with some
k_{v}>0.
Similar asymptotics can be obtained for
\binom{\lfloor cm\rfloor}{m}

for any
c>1
.

In
[
8
]
one finds the bounds:

\frac{1}{2}(b^{2}+b+2)b^{a-b-1}-1\leq S(a,b)\leq(1/2)\binom{a}{b}b^{a-b},

while a handy summation formula is

\displaystyle S(a+1,b+1)=\sum_{j=b}^{a}(b+1)^{a-j}S(j,b).

(53)

For further reading on Stirling numbers of the second kind, we recommend
[
8
]
.

Consider

. In the particular case when the

For a given

[0,m)

[t_{i-1},t_{i})_{i=1,...,v}
is called
\delta

t_{i}-t_{i-1}>\delta

A^{(n)}(m,\delta)
be the event that that there exists a
\delta

such that
X^{(n)}

[t_{i-1},t_{i}).

\delta

[0,m).
’’
Then the
0-1
-valued càdlàg processes
(X^{(n)},P^{(n)})
on
[0,\infty)
are tight if and only if for every
m>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}P^{(n)}(A^{(n)}(m,\delta))=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}P^{(n)}(X^{(n)}\ \text{is}\ \delta-\text{flat on\ }[0,m))=1.

Intuitively, the criterion requires that the paths
do not fluctuate too much
between zero and one. (Note that if

is càdlàg then

can be written as a disjoint union of half-open intervals of positive length, on each of which

is constant.)

[

,
\gamma
and

and

E^{(n)}|X^{(n)}_{t}|^{\gamma}\leq K,\ 0\leq t\leq T.

For

-valued processes, only the first inequality is relevant and it simply becomes

E^{(n)}|(X^{(n)}_{t}-X^{(n)}_{t_{1}})(X^{(n)}_{t_{2}}-X^{(n)}_{t})|\leq K|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{1+\alpha},\ 0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T,

\displaystyle P^{(n)}(X^{(n)}_{t}\neq X^{(n)}_{t_{1}},X^{(n)}_{t}\neq X^{(n)}_{t_{2}})\leq K|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{1+\alpha},\ 0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T,
