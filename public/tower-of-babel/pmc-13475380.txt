GLP-1–based incretin therapy is associated with lower incident Alzheimer’s disease and cardiorenal events in adults with documented neuropsychiatric, cognitive, or sensory risk
Authors: Karthik Murugadoss, A J Venkatakrishnan, Venky Soundararajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) develops over years, creating an opportunity for interventions that target modifiable risk factors before the onset of clinical dementia. Here we applied a federated electronic health record (EHR) system encompassing over 29 million de-identified patients toward a target-trial emulation framework involving 153,412 adults aged 50 years or older with at least one documented AD risk factor. New users of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1)–based incretin therapy were compared with new users of non-GLP-1 antidiabetic medications after a 12-month washout, with 1:1 propensity-score matching on 30 baseline variables and additional exact matching on index year of therapy initiation and 5-year age band. In the primary matched cohort, 28,901 patients per arm, incretin initiation was associated with lower first recorded AD diagnosis (hazard ratio [HR] 0.46, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.29–0.73, q = 0.003), all-cause dementia (HR 0.66 [0.55–0.79], q < 0.001), and all-cause mortality (HR 0.46 [0.37–0.58], q < 0.001), with directionally-lower Mild Cognitive Impairment (HR 0.76 [0.61–0.94], q = 0.135) and Alzheimer’s-related medication initiation (HR 0.82 [0.65–1.03], q = 0.217). The AD diagnosis mitigation signal was independently reproduced for semaglutide (HR 0.56; N = 23,675 per arm; q = 0.02), with the directionally consistent tirzepatide point estimate (HR 0.60) not reaching significance. GLP-1 RA initiation was also associated with substantially lower (all q < 0.001) incidence of heart failure (HR 0.50 [0.45–0.55]), cardiomyopathy (HR 0.38 [0.31–0.47]), major adverse cardiovascular events (HR 0.74 [0.67–0.82]), acute kidney injury (HR 0.67 [0.67–0.74]), and chronic kidney disease (HR 0.68 [0.62–0.75]). Negative-control outcomes showed no significant separation (all q > 0.45), including allergic rhinitis (HR 0.96 [0.87–1.07]), hemorrhoids (HR 1.07 [0.95–1.21]), and inguinal hernia (HR 1.39 [0.89–2.18]). Stricter two-code ICD definitions supported lower first recorded AD diagnosis in the incretin arm (HR 0.51 [0.29–0.89], q = 0.018) and lower first recorded all-cause dementia diagnosis (HR 0.68 [0.54–0.85], q = 0.003). In 12-month landmark analyses among semaglutide initiators, ≥5% weight-loss responders had lower 3-year AD cumulative incidence than non-responders after matching (0.07% vs. 0.40%; incidence ratio 5.76; P = .036), although this was not significant in the hazard-ratio model (HR 0.54, P = .39). Sustained-dose stratification showed no comparable gradient (high-dose vs. low-dose 0.30% vs. 0.14%; HR 2.77; P = .38), with ≤12 AD events per group. The weight-loss-specific pattern did not extend to all-cause dementia: weight-loss responders and non-responders had similar 3-year cumulative incidence (1.54% vs. 1.70%; HR 0.87, P = .53). Initiation of GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy in adults with documented AD risk factors was associated with lower recorded incidence of AD, dementia, mortality, and multiple cardiovascular and renal outcomes in this observational target-trial emulation. These findings support the hypothesis that earlier incretin therapy may contribute to AD risk modification through upstream cardiometabolic pathways, while prospective randomized prevention studies will be required to determine causality, underlying biological mechanisms, and optimal treatment timing.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and related dementias are projected to increase from 57.4 million cases globally in 2019 to 152.8 million by 2050 [1]. The two anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies approved recently, lecanemab and donanemab, slow cognitive decline by approximately 27%–35% over 18 months in patients with early symptomatic disease but carry a substantial burden of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, infusion logistics, and cost, and neither reverse established cognitive impairment [2, 3]. Therapeutic strategies that act earlier in the disease trajectory, including on metabolic and vascular pathways that contribute to neurodegeneration, remain an urgent unmet need.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is among the strongest modifiable risk factors for AD and all-cause dementia, with prospective cohort studies demonstrating a 50%–100% relative increase in dementia risk in people with type 2 diabetes compared to those without [4]. The biological link between systemic metabolic dysfunction and neurodegeneration is supported by evidence of cerebral insulin resistance, impaired glucose utilization in vulnerable brain regions, chronic neuroinflammation, blood–brain-barrier dysfunction, and accelerated cerebral small-vessel disease in patients with type 2 diabetes [5]. These observations have motivated the testing of glucose-lowering and weight-reducing therapies as candidate disease-modifying agents for AD.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have transformed metabolic medicine over the past decade. Initially approved for glycemic control in type 2 diabetes [6, 7], they have since demonstrated efficacy in chronic weight management [8, 9], reduction of major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with obesity and established cardiovascular disease but no diabetes [10], symptomatic improvement in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and obesity [11], and slowing of kidney disease progression in patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease [12]. The dual GLP-1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide co-agonist tirzepatide further improves outcomes in patients with obstructive sleep apnea [13]. The convergence of glycemic, weight-loss, cardiovascular, and renal benefits within a single drug class makes GLP-1 receptor agonists mechanistically attractive candidates for prevention of cognitive decline in populations whose dementia risk is partially shaped by metabolic disease.

Preclinical and early-phase clinical evidence has been consistent with a neuroprotective effect of GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure. Six months of liraglutide preserved cerebral glucose metabolism on 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography in patients with AD compared to placebo [14]. A pooled analysis of three randomized diabetes outcome trials of liraglutide and semaglutide together with two Danish nationwide registries reported a 53% lower rate of dementia diagnoses among GLP-1 receptor agonist users [15]. The ELAD phase 2 b randomized trial of 52-week subcutaneous liraglutide in 204 patients with mild-to-moderate AD, reported in 2025, did not reach its primary cerebral glucose metabolism endpoint but showed preservation of cortical brain volume on magnetic resonance imaging and a favorable cognitive trajectory [16].

The pivotal phase 3 EVOKE and EVOKE+ trials, designed to provide the definitive test of the GLP-1-receptor-agonist hypothesis in AD, randomized approximately 3800 patients with early symptomatic AD to oral semaglutide 14 mg daily or placebo over 2 years. There was no significant difference between groups on the primary Clinical Dementia Rating-Sum of Boxes endpoint at 104 weeks despite a significant reduction in cerebrospinal fluid phosphorylated-tau-181 in the semaglutide arm at 78 weeks. EVOKE and EVOKE+ therefore demonstrate a measurable effect of GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure on an established AD biomarker but no corresponding clinical benefit in a patient population with symptomatic disease already present at treatment initiation. An important unresolved question is whether GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure earlier in the disease trajectory, before a formal AD diagnosis, is associated with lower subsequent AD incidence in adults with established risk factors. Randomized primary-prevention trials of AD are difficult to execute at near-term scale because they require prolonged follow-up in large heterogenous at-risk populations. We therefore conducted an observational study to evaluate whether initiation of GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy was associated with subsequent AD-related outcomes in a risk-enriched population before diagnosis, rather than to evaluate disease modification after symptomatic AD has developed (Fig. 1).

Methods

Study design and cohort

We conducted a retrospective observational analysis on de-identified electronic health record data accessed through the nference Federated System [17–20]. We identified all adult patients aged 50 years or older with at least one documented AD risk factor recorded at any time before a candidate index date. The index date was defined as the patient’s first prescription, after a 12-month antidiabetic-medication washout, of one of eight study drugs (semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide, exenatide, metformin, an SGLT-2 inhibitor, or a DPP-4 inhibitor) [21]. Documented AD risk factors comprised any pre-index ICD-10 or ICD-9 diagnosis of depression, anxiety, sleep disorder, hearing loss, vision impairment, traumatic brain injury history, or memory complaints. These entry criteria were treated as heterogeneous AD-relevant risk or prodromal markers, not as equivalent biological risk strata. Depression and anxiety were included as baseline covariates in the propensity-score model, and prior antidepressant and antipsychotic exposure were also included; however, schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, bipolar disorder, severe or recurrent depression, mood-stabilizer exposure, and psychiatric-risk strata were not separately modeled or stratified in the primary analysis. Patients with any prior AD or related-dementia diagnosis at the index date were excluded. Patients with a documented history of type 1 diabetes mellitus, pancreatitis, thyroid cancer, or gastroparesis were also excluded, with these safety exclusions applied symmetrically across all arms. Patients were required to have at least three documented healthcare-activity points in the pre-index window to ensure minimal data completeness. Application of these criteria yielded 153,412 patients as the funnel-eligible analytic cohort.

Exposure groups and pairwise comparisons

Each patient was assigned a single exposure group based on which of the eight study drugs was the patient’s first qualifying prescription. Five individual GLP-1 receptor agonist groups (semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide, exenatide) and three individual non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic class groups (metformin, SGLT-2 inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors) were defined, together with two pooled groups: an all-GLP-1 receptor agonist group combining all five individual GLP-1 receptor agonists, and a non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic comparator combining the three non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic classes. Eight pairwise comparisons were pre-specified, restricted to those with sufficient sample size to support reliable propensity-score matching and event analysis: the class-level all-GLP-1 RA versus pooled non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic comparison; semaglutide versus each of metformin, SGLT-2 inhibitors, and DPP-4 inhibitors (three comparisons); tirzepatide versus each of metformin and SGLT-2 inhibitors (two comparisons; tirzepatide versus DPP-4 had too few eligible patients in the DPP-4 arm); each of semaglutide and tirzepatide versus the pooled non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic comparator (two comparisons).

Outcomes, prior-event filtering, and censoring

Five primary outcomes targeted AD and related cognitive endpoints: first incident AD diagnosis (ICD-10 G30 or F00, or ICD-9 331.0) [22]; first prescription of an AD-related medication (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, memantine, aducanumab, lecanemab, or donanemab); first all-cause dementia diagnosis using a broader ICD code set; first mild cognitive impairment (MCI) diagnosis (ICD-10 G31.84); and all-cause mortality. Four pre-specified negative-control outcomes (allergic rhinitis, hemorrhoids, inguinal hernia, cerumen impaction) were included as falsification endpoints with no plausible mechanistic link to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure [23]. Eleven cardiovascular endpoints and eleven renal endpoints were pre-specified to assess the cardiorenal association of GLP-1 receptor agonist initiation. Two sensitivity outcome definitions were assessed for each AD, all-cause dementia, and MCI endpoint: a second-confirmatory-ICD-code definition (requiring at least two ICD codes on separate encounter dates), and an augmented-curation classifier definition derived from unstructured clinical text with patient-level disease-presence probability of 0.80 or higher.

The analysis was restricted to incident events by excluding patients with the corresponding prevalent condition at the index date. Operationally, each outcome carried a pre-specified prevalent-disease definition (the same ICD code set used to ascertain the outcome itself, evaluated in the patient’s pre-index history), and patients flagged as prevalent on that outcome at the index date were removed from the at-risk denominator for that outcome. In addition, to mitigate delayed coding of comorbidities that are often documented at the first-T2DM-diagnosis encounter and that disproportionately coincide with first metformin or SGLT-2 prescription dates rather than later step-up GLP-1 initiation, any patient whose outcome event was recorded within the first 30 days after the index date was also treated as having a prevalent event and removed from the at-risk denominator for that outcome. A 30-day buffer period was selected a priori to minimize delayed documentation of prevalent disease immediately after treatment initiation, particularly around new diabetes diagnoses, while avoiding immortal-time bias through symmetric application across exposure groups. This issue is especially relevant when comorbidities are coded during the same clinical episode as a new diabetes diagnosis or first antidiabetic prescription, which may occur more often for metformin or sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitor initiation than for later step-up incretin therapy. Events recorded within 30 days after index were therefore treated as likely prevalent or delayed-documentation events and removed from the corresponding outcome-specific at-risk denominator, with the rule applied symmetrically across exposure arms. Because the buffer was applied after cohort entry and equally across arms, it was intended to reduce delayed prevalent-outcome capture rather than create exposure-immortal follow-up time. Specifically, when applied symmetrically across both arms of every comparison, this 30-day buffer does not introduce immortal-time bias [24]. Alternative buffer durations may be explored in future sensitivity analyzes.

The at-risk denominator therefore varies modestly across outcomes within the same matched cohort: for example, the 28,901-per-arm all-GLP-1 RA versus non-GLP-1 RA matched cohort retains 28,896 versus 28,885 patients at risk for incident AD but 28,543 versus 28,507 at risk for first AD-related medication initiation. For each outcome, patients were censored at the earliest of: the outcome event; death (for outcomes other than all-cause mortality); the patient’s last observed activity date (maximum date across medications, conditions, vital sign measurements, and laboratory values); or initiation of any drug from the comparator arm’s exposure class (switch censoring).

Propensity-score matching

For each of the eight pre-specified pairwise comparisons, a logistic-regression propensity-score model was fit on the funnel-eligible cohort using a 30-variable baseline covariate set: age at the index date; baseline body mass index; healthcare-utilization count in the 12 months before the index date; calendar year of the index date; sex; race; and 24 binary indicators of pre-index conditions and prior medication classes (Supplementary Table 1) [25]. Continuous covariates were median-imputed with a missing indicator and standardized; categorical covariates were one-hot encoded with the first level dropped; 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching was applied to the logit propensity score with a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit propensity score, using a greedy algorithm [26]. Exact matching was enforced on two auxiliary variables: calendar year of the index date, to remove residual calendar–year imbalance arising from staggered drug market entry; and 5-year age band at the index date, to remove residual age imbalance, which had been the single largest contributor to post-match imbalance under calendar-year-only exact matching and which is on the causal pathway for the AD and mortality endpoints. The 5-year age band was defined by 5-year bins from age 50–90 with a single open-ended band for age 90 or older. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were computed for every covariate before and after matching to assess balance [27].

Cognitive score-change matching

Cognitive instrument scores, including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), were extracted from clinical documents using a previously validated large language model (LLM)-extraction pipeline from unstructured clinical notes [28–34]. For the matched pre/post cognitive score-change analysis, a 1:1 propensity-score match was performed separately for each pairwise comparison and cognitive instrument, restricted to patients with at least one pre-index and at least one post-index score on that instrument in the 5-year follow-up window. The matching covariate set included the 30 baseline covariates plus two timing variables: time from the patient’s baseline score to the index date, and time from the index date to the patient’s latest post-index score on that instrument. Including the two timing variables ensures that matched pairs share both pre-index baseline timing and post-index outcome-observation timing, removing a known selection bias in matched score-change analyzes. The within-patient change score (Δ) was defined as the latest post-index score on a given instrument minus the closest pre-index score on the same instrument. Matched-pair differences (Δ in the index arm minus Δ in the comparator arm) were tested with a two-sided Welch t test [35].

Within-semaglutide weight-loss-band and dose-band analyzes

For the two within-semaglutide stratified analyzes, the cohort was restricted to semaglutide initiators who satisfied a 12-month landmark eligibility: alive at the index + 365-day landmark date; AD-free at the landmark; at least one baseline weight measurement in the 12 months preceding the index date; at least one weight measurement in the 12 months after the index date; and no other GLP-1 receptor agonist or anti-obesity medication prescribed during the landmark window. Maximum percent weight loss in the 12 months post-index was computed as the maximum percent reduction from the patient’s baseline weight (median of weight measurements in the 12 months pre-index) observed in any single post-index weight measurement during the landmark window. Maximum sustained semaglutide dose was computed as the highest semaglutide dose, in milligrams per week, observed across the patient’s semaglutide prescriptions during the landmark window. For the weight-loss-response analysis, patients were assigned to a responder band (5% or greater maximum body-weight loss) or a non-responder band (less than 5% loss). For the dose analysis, patients were assigned to a high-dose band (1.0 mg/week or greater) or a low-dose band (less than 1.0 mg/week). 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity-score matching was applied between the two bands within each stratification, using the same 30-variable baseline covariate set as the main matched cohort analysis. Exact calendar-year and age-band matching were not enforced in the within-semaglutide analyzes, as both bands within each stratification are exposed to the same drug. Post-landmark first AD diagnosis was the primary outcome, with censoring at the earliest of the outcome event; death; last observed activity; or initiation of any non-semaglutide GLP-1 receptor agonist, anti-obesity medication, or non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic medication.

Statistical analysis

For every pairwise comparison and outcome combination, a Cox proportional-hazards model with the treatment-arm indicator as the sole covariate was fit on the matched cohort, yielding the log hazard ratio (HR) and its standard error [36]. Raw two-sided P values were derived from the Wald test on the log HR. Q values (Benjamini–Hochberg [BH]–adjusted P values, BH q values) were computed under two pre-specified scopes [37]: for the five primary AD and related cognitive endpoints and the first negative-control phenotypes, the BH scope was per outcome across the 11 testable pairwise comparisons (Figs. 2, 3, and Supplementary Fig. 3); for the 11 cardiovascular and 11 renal endpoints, the BH scope was per comparison across the 22 cardiorenal endpoints (Figs. 4 and 5). Cells in which either arm contributed fewer than 11 events for a given outcome were excluded from the BH correction set, as the rank-based BH procedure is unstable in low-power cells and would distort the q value denominator for the remaining cells; such cells are annotated as “q = NA” in the figures and reported with the raw two-sided P value in body text. Kaplan–Meier at-risk tables on a 30-day grid out to 5 years were constructed per arm, giving per-bin number at risk, number of events, and number of censored patients; cumulative incidence at landmark time points (1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 years post-index date) was computed from the product-limit survival estimate with Greenwood standard errors [38]. All Kaplan–Meier panels display, per panel, the HR with 95% confidence interval, the raw two-sided P value, the BH-adjusted q value, and significance stars (q < .05, q < .01, q < .001); shaded bands denote 95% Greenwood confidence intervals (CIs); a light-gray treatment-duration interquartile-range band marks median follow-up; and a shared bottom legend shows arm labels with matched N per arm. For matched paired pre-/post-cognitive score-change analyzes, two-sided Welch t tests were applied to the matched-pair Δ distributions. Where the count of patients or events in any reported cell was fewer than 11, the cell count is reported as “<11” per the privacy convention adopted in this study, and statistical comparisons involving that cell are considered exploratory.

Analyses were interpreted according to a pre-specified inferential hierarchy. The primary analysis was the class-level propensity-matched comparison of all GLP-1 receptor agonist initiators versus pooled non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic medication initiators for the five AD-related primary outcomes. Supportive analyzes included drug-specific semaglutide and tirzepatide comparisons, cardiovascular and renal outcome analyzes, negative-control outcomes, and sensitivity outcome definitions using two-code-confirmed ICD definitions or augmented clinical-note curation. Exploratory analyzes included within-semaglutide weight-loss and dose stratifications, 2 × 2 weight-loss-by-dose cross-classifications, and matched cognitive instrument score-change analyzes. Exploratory analyzes were interpreted as hypothesis-generating, particularly when event counts were sparse or paired-score availability was limited.

Statistical analysis plan for study advancement

The analytic approach followed established pharmacoepidemiologic and biostatistical conventions throughout. The incident-user, active-comparator cohort design: new initiators of GLP-1 based incretin therapy compared with new initiators of non-GLP-1 antidiabetic medications after a 12-month washout, is the standard real-world-evidence paradigm for drug-effect estimation from routinely collected EHR data, with restriction to other antidiabetic initiators serving as the canonical active-comparator control for confounding by indication [21]. Propensity scores were estimated by logistic regression and matched 1:1 by greedy nearest-neighbor matching within a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit propensity score, with additional exact matching on calendar year of initiation and 5-year age band to remove residual calendar-time and age imbalance; balance was assessed by standardized mean differences before and after matching, following established causal-inference methodology [25–27]. Phenotypes and cognitive instrument scores, including the Mini-Mental State Examination and Montreal Cognitive Assessment, were ascertained from structured diagnosis and prescription codes and from unstructured clinical notes using a validated large language model extraction pipeline, consistent with evidence that adapted LLMs extract clinical phenotypes and cognitive measurements from notes at expert-comparable accuracy [28, 29, 33]. Incident events were counted only among at-risk patients free of the corresponding phenotype at baseline; HRs and cumulative-incidence curves were estimated by Cox proportional-hazards regression and the Kaplan–Meier product-limit estimator with Greenwood standard errors, and within-semaglutide landmark analyzes used rate ratios of 3-year cumulative incidence with Greenwood Z-tests across ordered weight-loss and dose bands to assess response- and dose-gradients [36, 38]. Immortal-time and reverse-causation concerns were addressed through incident-event restriction, symmetric baseline exclusions, a minimum pre-index observability requirement, and a 30-day post-index latency buffer applied symmetrically across arms, mirroring recognized corrections for time-related bias [24], while four pre-specified negative-control phenotypes provided a falsification test for residual confounding and differential ascertainment [23]. Continuous within-patient cognitive-score change was compared by Welch’s unequal-variance t test [35], and multiplicity across the cognitive, negative-control, and cardiorenal phenotype families was controlled by the BH false-discovery-rate procedure [37].

De-identification and HIPAA compliance certification

Prior to analysis, all EHR data were de-identified under an expert determination consistent with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule (45 CFR §164.514(b)(1)). The de-identification methodology [39, 40] employed a multilayered transformation approach to both structured and unstructured data fields. In structured data, direct identifiers including patient names and precise geographic locations were excluded entirely, while indirect identifiers underwent specific transformations: patient identifiers, medical record numbers, and accession numbers were replaced with one-way cryptographic hashes using confidential salts to preserve linkage across patient encounters; all dates were shifted backward by patient-specific random offsets (1–31 days) to preserve temporal relationships while obscuring exact event timing; ZIP codes were truncated to two-digit state-level resolution; and continuous variables including age, height, weight, and body mass index were thresholded to prevent identification of extreme values (e.g., ages 89 years or older transformed to “89+” and BMI over 40 transformed to “40+”). In clinical text, an ensemble de-identification system that combines attention-based deep learning with rule-based methods achieved an estimated >99% recall for personally identifiable information detection, with detected identifiers replaced by plausible fictional surrogates.

Data harmonization

To address heterogeneity in EHR data, we harmonized clinical variables including medications, anthropometric measurements, and diagnoses to standardized concepts. For medications, we first constructed a standardized drug concept database combining the nference knowledge graph with RxNorm hierarchies to capture ingredient, brand, and dose-specific information. Medication records were matched using a hierarchical approach prioritizing RxNorm codes when available, followed by ingredient-level matching, and finally natural language processing and pattern matching on free-text medication orders when structured codes were absent. For anthropometric measurements (height, weight, body mass index), we created a unified vocabulary from SNOMED and LOINC and matched EHR measurement descriptions using standardized text matching algorithms with abbreviation expansion and synonym resolution; ambiguous mappings were resolved using OpenAI GPT-4o with summary statistics as context, followed by manual verification. For diagnoses, we developed a hierarchical disease concept database from the knowledge graph and matched EHR diagnosis records by identifying the most specific common child concept in the hierarchy.

Code availability

The analysis code is not publicly available. Please contact the corresponding author for details. To support independent review and validation of the analytic logic, we provide expanded supplementary materials specifying the cohort-entry criteria, washout, exclusions, exposure definitions, outcome definitions, phenotype code lists, incident-event filtering, censoring rules, propensity-score matching variables and constraints, low-event handling, multiplicity-correction scopes, negative-control outcomes, and sensitivity-analysis specifications (Supplementary Table 4). These materials are intended to make the target-trial–emulation design and statistical workflow auditable without releasing patient-level records or proprietary source code.

Inferential hierarchy and interpretation framework

Results in this study are presented according to inferential tier: first the primary class-level Alzheimer’s-disease-related analysis, followed by supportive drug-specific, cardiorenal, negative-control, and sensitivity analyzes, and finally exploratory within-semaglutide and cognitive score–change analyzes.

Results

Cohort eligibility, derivation, and baseline characteristics

There were 153,412 adults aged 50 years or older who had a qualifying prescription for an antidiabetic medication, at least one documented pre-existing AD risk factor, and no pre-index diagnosis of type 1 diabetes, pancreatitis, thyroid cancer, or gastroparesis (Fig. 1a). The AD risk factors used for cohort eligibility were depression, anxiety, sleep disorder, hearing loss, vision impairment, traumatic brain injury history, or memory complaints. The eligible cohort included 59,156 GLP-1 receptor agonist initiators, comprising 34,563 semaglutide, 29,633 tirzepatide, 988 liraglutide, 596 dulaglutide, and 92 exenatide initiators, and 91,271 non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic medication initiators, comprising 62,772 metformin, 30,815 SGLT-2 inhibitor, and 2,765 DPP-4 inhibitor initiators (Figs. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Across eight pre-specified medication comparisons, 1:1 propensity-score matching was performed using a 30-variable baseline covariate set, with exact matching on calendar year of initiation and matching within 5-year age bands (Fig. 1a). The primary matched comparison included 28,901 GLP-1 RA initiators and 28,901 non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic medication initiators. In this matched cohort, mean age at the index date was 63.5 years in each arm, mean baseline body mass index was 34.7 versus 33.5 kg/m² (standardized mean difference [SMD]: 0.18), 63.5% versus 60.5% of patients were female, and both arms were approximately 85% White (Table 1). After matching, post-match absolute SMD was below 0.10 for all covariates except baseline heart failure (SMD: −0.13) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) (SMD: −0.12), each modestly enriched in the non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic arm (Figs. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2). Within the matched all-GLP-1 RA case arm of 28,901 patients, semaglutide and tirzepatide together accounted for 95.7% of initiators (17,418 [60.3%] and 10,223 [35.4%], respectively; Table 2). Among the 20,270 patients (70.1%) with a brand name resolvable from the electronic-prescription record, the four leading brands were Ozempic (n = 7367), Mounjaro (n = 3876), Wegovy (n = 3838), and Zepbound (n = 3759) (Table 3).

GLP-1–based incretin therapy initiation is associated with lower rates of first recorded Alzheimer’s disease, all-cause dementia, and all-cause mortality

The primary matched comparison showed coordinated lower HRs across neurological, cardiovascular, renal, and mortality outcomes, with the strongest cognitive association observed for first recorded AD diagnosis and the strongest cardiorenal associations observed for cardiomyopathy, heart failure, acute kidney injury, and chronic kidney disease (Fig. 1b). Specifically, among 28,901 propensity-matched initiators of GLP-1 based incretin therapy and 28,901 matched initiators of a non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic medication, the HR for first recorded AD diagnosis was 0.46 (95% CI 0.29–0.73; 30 vs. 67 events; q = .003) over follow-up extending up to 5 years from the index date (Fig. 2a, Table 1). Although AD event counts were relatively low (30 vs. 67 events), the association remained consistent across sensitivity analyzes using alternative outcome definitions.

The seven pre-index neuropsychiatric and sensory AD risk factors used to define cohort eligibility were well balanced after matching, including depression, anxiety, sleep disorder, hearing loss, vision impairment, traumatic brain injury history, and memory complaints, with all post-match absolute standardized mean differences below 0.10 (Fig. 2f). When using a more stringent definition requiring at least two ICD codes on separate dates, the HR for second-code-confirmed AD was 0.51 (95% CI 0.29–0.89; 23 vs. 49 events) (Supplementary Fig. 4). When AD was ascertained by AI-enabled curation of unstructured clinical text, the HR was directionally lower but attenuated and not statistically significant (HR 0.80, 95% CI 0.61–1.07; 92 vs. 114 events) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Similar directional trends were observed across the other four primary outcomes, with statistically significant associations for all-cause dementia and all-cause mortality and directionally lower rates of AD-related medication initiation and mild cognitive impairment. First all-cause dementia diagnosis showed a HR of 0.66 (95% CI 0.55–0.79; 206 vs. 304 events; q < .001) when considering at least one ICD code and 0.68 (95% CI 0.54–0.85; 128 vs. 185 events) when requiring two encounters with a corresponding ICD code (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 4). All-cause mortality showed a HR of 0.46 (95% CI 0.37–0.58; 112 vs. 244 events; q < .001) (Fig. 2c). First AD-related medication initiation (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, memantine, aducanumab, lecanemab, or donanemab) showed a directionally lower but non-significant association (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.65–1.03; 138 vs. 163 events; q = .217) (Fig. 2d). First MCI diagnosis was directionally lower using the one-code definition (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.61–0.94; 150 vs. 192 events; q = .13) but did not remain significant after multiplicity correction; the two-code confirmatory definition was also non-significant (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.62–1.11; 85 vs. 99 events) (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 4). Analysis of four pre-specified negative-control outcomes, allergic rhinitis, hemorrhoids, inguinal hernia, and cerumen impaction, showed no significant differences between the matched cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Notably, the cumulative-incidence curves are shown over 5 years, while the shaded gray interval denotes the interquartile range of recorded GLP-1 RA based incretin treatment duration, 1.5 to 12.3 months (Fig. 2a–c). Thus, much of the observed separation in AD, all-cause dementia, and mortality occurred beyond the typical recorded treatment-duration window, consistent with a persistent post-exposure or legacy-like association rather than only an on-treatment pharmacologic effect.

Semaglutide and tirzepatide show directionally consistent associations with lower recorded dementia-related outcomes

In the semaglutide versus non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic medications matched cohort (N = 23,675 per arm), the HRs were 0.56 (95% CI 0.36–0.87; 33 vs. 58 events; q = .02) for first recorded AD diagnosis, 0.78 (95% CI 0.64–0.95; 181 vs. 226 events; q = .03) for first recorded all-cause dementia diagnosis, and 0.54 (95% CI 0.42–0.69; 101 vs. 189 events; q < .001) for all-cause mortality. HRs for first AD-related medication initiation (0.99, 95% CI 0.78–1.27; 129vs.127 events; q = .95) and first MCI diagnosis (0.93, 95% CI 0.73–1.19; 131 vs. 137 events; q = .72) were not significantly different between these cohorts (Figs. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 6, Supplementary Table 2). Across semaglutide-anchored active-comparator analyzes, HRs for AD diagnosis were directionally below 1.0 versus metformin and SGLT-2 inhibitors and significant in the pooled non-GLP-1 RA comparator analysis, while all-cause dementia and all-cause mortality showed consistent reductions across multiple comparator rows (Fig. 3).

In the tirzepatide versus non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic medications matched cohort (N = 16,782 per arm), over a 2-year follow-up window the HRs were 0.33 (95% CI 0.14–0.79; <11 vs. 21 events; q = .04) for first recorded AD diagnosis, 0.52 (95% CI 0.37–0.73; 51 vs. 98 events; q < .001) for first recorded all-cause dementia diagnosis, 0.61 (95% CI 0.40–0.93; 36 vs. 59 events; q = .11) for first recorded MCI diagnosis, 0.64 (95% CI 0.40–1.04; 27 vs. 42 events; q = .22) for initiation of an AD-related medication, and 0.29 (95% CI 0.18–0.48; 21 vs. 72 events; q < .001) for all-cause mortality (Figs. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 7, Supplementary Table 3). Tirzepatide-anchored comparisons versus metformin and SGLT-2 inhibitors showed similar 2-year patterns for all-cause dementia and all-cause mortality, while AD diagnosis remained event-limited across tirzepatide rows (Fig. 3).

As tirzepatide entered clinical use later than semaglutide, tirzepatide-anchored comparisons had substantially shorter realized follow-up and fewer AD events. Accordingly, tirzepatide-specific estimates, particularly for AD, should be interpreted as directionally informative but less mature than the semaglutide estimates (Fig. 3).

GLP-1–based incretin therapy initiation is associated with lower incidence of heart failure, cardiomyopathy, and major adverse cardiovascular events

In the propensity-matched all GLP-1 based incretin therapy versus non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic medications cohort (N = 28,901 per arm), cardiovascular outcomes were assessed under an incident-event analysis protocol that excluded patients with documentation of the corresponding condition prior to the index date. Post-match cardiovascular burden covariates were generally well balanced, with the largest residual imbalance observed for heart failure, which remained modestly enriched in the comparator arm (SMD −0.124; Fig. 4l). The strongest separation was observed for heart failure (HR 0.50, 95% CI 0.45–0.55; 564 vs. 1035 events; q < .001; Fig. 4e) and cardiomyopathy (HR 0.38, 95% CI 0.31–0.47; 136 vs. 333 events; q < .001; Fig. 4i). The three-point major adverse cardiovascular event composite (all-cause death, acute coronary syndrome, or stroke) showed an HR of 0.74 (95% CI 0.67–0.82; 684 vs. 873 events; q < .001) (Fig. 4a).

Six of the remaining eight cardiovascular endpoints also demonstrated statistically significant separation after BH correction, with HRs as follows: cerebrovascular disease 0.87 (95% CI 0.77–0.99; q = .047; Fig. 4c), essential hypertension 0.74 (95% CI 0.67–0.82; q < .001; Fig. 4d), arrhythmias and conduction disorders 0.87 (95% CI 0.79–0.95; q = .004; Fig. 4f), peripheral vascular disease 0.85 (95% CI 0.77–0.95; q = .004; Fig. 4g), valvular and rheumatic heart disease 0.84 (95% CI 0.75–0.94; q = .004; Fig. 4h), and venous thromboembolism 0.80 (95% CI 0.69–0.93; q = .006; Fig. 4K). Ischemic heart disease (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.86–1.03; q = .236; Fig. 4b) and aortic disease (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.70–1.21; q = .598; Fig. 4j) occurred at similar rates in the matched cohorts. Notably, the cardiovascular cumulative-incidence curves are shown over 5 years, whereas the shaded gray interval denotes the interquartile range of recorded GLP-1 based incretin treatment duration, 1.5–12.3 months. The separation observed for several cardiovascular outcomes extended beyond this typical recorded treatment-duration window, consistent with a persistent post-exposure or legacy-like cardiovascular association rather than only an on-treatment pharmacologic effect (Fig. 4a, c–i, and k). The semaglutide-specific (Supplementary Fig. 8) and tirzepatide-specific (Supplementary Fig. 9) comparisons showed associations in the same direction and of similar magnitude for all endpoints with sufficient events to estimate.

These cardiovascular endpoints are to be reviewed as distal clinical correlates of systemic risk modification, and not as any evidence that cardiovascular improvement achieved upon GLP1RA therapy modifies local AD biology or direct pathological drivers thereof.

GLP-1–based incretin therapy initiation is associated with lower incidence of acute and chronic kidney disease, with no excess kidney failure or dialysis dependence

In the same propensity-matched cohort, 11 pre-specified renal outcomes were assessed under the same incident-event analysis, excluding patients with the corresponding prevalent renal condition at the index date. Post-match renal-burden covariates were well balanced, including chronic kidney disease and diabetic nephropathy/microvascular type 2 diabetes complications, with both post-match absolute standardized mean differences below 0.10 (Fig. 5l). Five renal endpoints were observed at significantly lower rates in the GLP-1 receptor agonist cohort after BH correction: acute kidney injury HR 0.67 (95% CI 0.60–0.74; 586 vs. 842 events; q < .001; Fig. 5a); CKD HR 0.68 (95% CI 0.62–0.75; 745 vs. 1038 events; q < .001; Fig. 5b); diabetic kidney disease HR 0.61 (95% CI 0.54–0.69; 394 vs. 639 events; q < .001; Fig. 5d); hypertensive kidney disease HR 0.62 (95% CI 0.52–0.73; 213 vs. 319 events; q < .001; Fig. 5e); and renal tubular or mineral metabolism disorders HR 0.61 (95% CI 0.46–0.81; 80 vs. 127 events; q = .002; Fig. 5k). Proteinuria or albuminuria showed a nominally lower HR in the GLP-1 receptor agonist cohort that remain significant after Benjamini-Hochberg correction (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.68–0.99; 207 vs. 242 events; q = .06; Fig. 5h). Incident kidney failure or dialysis dependence occurred at similar rates, with a HR of 0.79 (95% CI 0.50–1.25; 34 vs. 42 events; q = .37; Fig. 5c).

Notably, the renal cumulative–incidence curves are shown over 5 years, whereas the shaded gray interval denotes the interquartile range (IQR) of recorded GLP-1 based incretin treatment duration, 1.5–12.3 months. The separation observed for acute kidney injury, CKD, diabetic kidney disease, hypertensive kidney disease, and renal tubular or mineral metabolism disorders extended beyond this typical recorded treatment-duration window, consistent with a persistent post-exposure or legacy-like renal association rather than only an on-treatment pharmacologic effect (Fig. 5a, b, d, e, and k).

The remaining renal endpoints did not show significant separation, including glomerular, nephritic, or nephrotic disease (Fig. 5f), tubulointerstitial nephritis or pyelonephritis (Fig. 5g), cystic kidney disease (Fig. 5i), and kidney stone or obstructive uropathy (Fig. 5j). The semaglutide-specific (Supplementary Fig. 10) and tirzepatide-specific (Supplementary Fig. 11) comparisons showed similar trends for all renal outcomes with sufficient event counts for evaluation. Together, the renal analyzes showed lower rates of several early and intermediate renal phenotypes without evidence of an offsetting excess in kidney failure or dialysis dependence.

These renal endpoints are interpreted as distal clinical correlates of systemic risk modification, not as evidence that cardiorenal improvement directly modifies local AD biology.

Exploratory within-semaglutide analyzes suggest a numerical AD gradient by achieved weight loss but not by attained dose

Exploratory within-semaglutide analyzes suggested a numerical gradient by achieved weight loss but not by sustained dose: 3-year cumulative AD incidence was 0.07% among patients achieving at least 5% body-weight loss versus 0.40% among non-responders, whereas dose-stratified analyzes did not show a corresponding gradient (Fig. 1c). Because event counts were low and the Cox estimates were not statistically significant, these analyzes should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of mediation. Two parallel within-semaglutide stratified analyzes were performed, each restricted to semaglutide initiators meeting 12-month landmark eligibility, stratified by weight loss response into responders versus non-responders (≥5% vs. <5% weight loss; N = 30,546 landmark-eligible patients) or by sustained dose into high dose versus low dose (≥1.0 mg/week vs. <1.0 mg/week; N = 29,472 landmark-eligible patients) (see Methods). 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity-score matching was applied between the two groups within each stratification, yielding 12,127 matched patients per band for the weight loss-stratified analysis and 11,177 matched patients per band for the dose-stratified analysis. The Cox hazard ratio for post-landmark incident AD diagnosis was not significantly different between weight loss responders and non-responders (HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.13–2.20; P = .39), nor between the dose-stratified bands (HR 2.77, 95% CI 0.29–26.67; P = .38). However, the 3-year matched cumulative incidence diverged substantially between the weight-loss bands: 0.07% in responders versus 0.40% in non-responders, corresponding to a rate ratio of 5.76 (P = .036, Greenwood Z-test on the log cumulative-incidence ratio at the 3-year landmark). No comparable separation was observed for the dose stratification (high-dose vs low-dose 3-year cumulative incidence 0.30% vs. 0.14%; rate ratio 2.10, P = .41) (Table 4). These analyzes had limited statistical power because of low post-landmark event counts, with no more than 12 events combined across both bands in either stratification.

Because incident AD event counts within the landmark-eligible semaglutide cohort were sparse (no more than 12 combined events across both bands in either stratification), we repeated both the weight-loss and dose stratifications and the 2 × 2 cross-classification using the broader all-cause dementia endpoint, for which event counts are several-fold larger and the resulting estimates carry meaningfully more statistical power. Within-patient cognitive instrument score change is directionally consistent with the incident-disease analysis

A matched within-patient cognitive instrument score-change analysis at the 5-year landmark (matched n = 12 per arm) with both pre- and post-index MMSE or MoCA scores, gave directionally consistent point estimates (+1.75 points, 95% CI −0.51 to 4.01; P = .12) but also did not reach statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 12). For each comparison and cognitive instrument, a 1:1 propensity-score match was performed on the 30-variable baseline covariate set plus two timing variables (time from the patient’s baseline score to the index date, and time from the index date to the patient’s latest post-index score) so that matched pairs shared both pre-index baseline timing and post-index outcome-observation timing. The MMSE point estimate reported above corresponds to the all-GLP-1 RA versus non-GLP-1 RA antidiabetic medications matched comparison; the corresponding MoCA matched-pair difference for the same comparison was 0.00 points (95% CI −2.94 to 2.94; P = 1.00; matched n < 11 per arm). The semaglutide-specific matched comparisons were directionally null on both instruments (MMSE: +0.14 points, 95% CI −1.31 to 1.60; P = .83; matched n < 11; MoCA: −0.25 points, 95% CI −3.57 to 3.07; P = .87; matched n < 11) (Supplementary Fig. 12). The matched MMSE point estimate in the all-GLP-1 RA versus non-GLP-1 RA matched comparison is directionally consistent with the much larger incident-disease findings, but the matched cohort sizes for the score-change analysis are limited by paired-score availability across a 5-year follow-up window.

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of adults with AD risk factors, initiation of a GLP-1 receptor agonist was associated with lower incident diagnosis rates of AD, all-cause dementia, and mild cognitive impairment. Similar directional trends were observed in drug-specific analyzes of semaglutide and tirzepatide, although the tirzepatide-specific AD estimate was imprecise because of low event counts and shorter post-launch follow-up. These findings were corroborated by additional sensitivity analyzes that used alternative endpoint definitions including multiple ICD codes and AI-curated diagnoses extracted from clinical notes. Further, the lack of separation between the GLP-1 receptor agonist and control cohorts for four negative-control phenotypes suggest that the observed differences are less likely attributable to differential healthcare-contact rates or other generic ascertainment differences between the matched cohorts. In the same matched framework, incretin initiation was also associated with lower incidence of multiple cardiovascular and renal outcomes, providing multiorgan internal triangulation consistent with established cardiometabolic and kidney associations of GLP-1 based therapies and with prior real-world studies showing that incretin-associated benefits may vary by weight-loss response, attained dose, treatment persistence, and organ-specific outcome domain [19, 20, 41–43]. However, this triangulation should be interpreted as coherence across distal clinical outcomes, not as proof that GLP-1 receptor agonism, weight loss, or cardiorenal improvement directly modifies AD biology.

Beyond dementia-related phenotypes, the cardiorenal analyzes place the AD association in a broader biological and clinical context. The observed lower rates of heart failure, cardiomyopathy, major adverse cardiovascular events, acute kidney injury, CKD, diabetic kidney disease, and hypertensive kidney disease are directionally consistent with randomized and real-world evidence that GLP-1 based therapies can modify cardiovascular and renal risk biology [10, 12, 44]. These findings should not be interpreted as independent proof of neuroprotection, but they reduce the likelihood that the cognitive association is an isolated coding artifact. Rather, the parallel cognitive, cardiovascular, renal, mortality, and negative-control findings provide physiological triangulation across multiple distal clinical outcomes that is consistent with established cardiometabolic effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists. These observations strengthen the internal coherence of the overall findings but should not be interpreted as evidence that cardiovascular or renal improvements directly modify AD biology or represent the causal pathway underlying the observed cognitive associations. This interpretation is consistent with prior real-world dementia analyzes, including a nationwide target-trial emulation in type 2 diabetes in which semaglutide was associated with lower first-time AD diagnosis across antidiabetic comparator classes [22], a larger matched GLP-1 receptor agonist-versus-metformin study showing lower overall dementia, AD, and non-vascular dementia risk among GLP-1 receptor agonist initiators [45], and a CKD-focused analysis showing lower dementia and AD risk with GLP-1 receptor agonists versus DPP-4 inhibitors in patients with type 2 diabetes and stage 3 or later chronic kidney disease [46]. Because larger real-world dementia studies of GLP-1 receptor agonists have now been reported, the key distinction of this article is not absolute sample size alone, but the integration of a risk-enriched AD-diagnosis-naive cohort, semaglutide- and tirzepatide-specific analyzes, confirmatory structured and AI-curated note-derived outcome definitions, negative-control phenotypes, and cardiorenal endpoint evaluation within a single propensity-matched federated EHR framework.

These findings may help reconcile the apparent tension between real-world dementia-prevention signals and the neutral clinical readout of EVOKE and EVOKE+. EVOKE and EVOKE+ tested oral semaglutide 14 mg daily in patients with early symptomatic AD, including mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to AD, and did not show a significant effect on the primary Clinical Dementia Rating-Sum of Boxes endpoint over 104 weeks, despite evidence of a biological effect on cerebrospinal fluid phosphorylated-tau-181 [47]. In contrast, this article is evaluated a risk-enriched, AD-diagnosis-naive population at the time of incretin therapy initiation. This distinction is central: EVOKE asked whether semaglutide can slow measurable clinical decline after symptomatic AD has emerged, whereas the current analysis asks whether incretin therapy initiation is associated with lower subsequent recording of AD and related dementia outcomes before a formal diagnosis is present. The two results are therefore not mutually exclusive. Rather, they support a stage-dependent model in which incretin therapy may be more relevant upstream of established neurodegeneration, through modification of metabolic, vascular, inflammatory, and cardiorenal risk biology, than as a late disease-modifying intervention after amyloid, tau, synaptic, and functional injury are already clinically manifest.

Such interpretation should be viewed as one plausible explanation rather than the sole explanation for the neutral EVOKE/EVOKE+ clinical results. Other possibilities include insufficient central nervous system exposure at the tested dose or formulation, inadequate treatment duration for a slowly progressive neurodegenerative disorder, limited sensitivity of the clinical endpoint over the trial window, heterogeneity in participant biology or disease stage, or absence of meaningful clinical efficacy despite movement in selected biomarkers. Thus, EVOKE/EVOKE+ should be interpreted as an important boundary condition: they do not exclude a prevention-oriented incretin hypothesis, but they also do not establish that earlier treatment timing alone explains the neutral symptomatic-disease results.

This framing also clarifies the evidential hierarchy of the present study. The neurological, mortality, cardiovascular, and renal outcomes are clinically meaningful but remain distal clinical consequences; they do not identify the local biological event through which incretin exposure may alter AD risk, if the association is causal. Indeed, meta-analysis have indicated apparently distinct phenomena like liver fibrosis as associated with an approximately 30% increased risk of incident dementia, suggesting recent evidence of semaglutide associated liver stiffness reversal and liver fibrosis improvement may have possible intersections with dementia outcomes in AD patient subsets via mechanisms that we simply do not comprehend today [48]. A useful way to interpret the findings from this study is therefore as a stage-dependent conversion question rather than as evidence of symptomatic rescue: before a visible AD trace becomes clinically recorded, is there a still-viable Alzheimer-relevant physiological compartment that can be shifted toward more coherent output through upstream cardiometabolic, vascular, renal, inflammatory, or weight-linked remodeling? The present EHR data support this prevention-oriented hypothesis but do not establish direct neuroprotection, disease reversal, or local biological mechanism. Prospective studies with biomarker, imaging, cognitive, functional, and tissue-relevant readouts will be needed to distinguish causal mechanisms from downstream clinical association.

The temporal structure of the Kaplan-Meier curves also warrants careful interpretation for the primary matched incretin comparison (Fig. 2a–c). Although the recorded treatment-duration interquartile range was relatively short (1.5–12.3 months), outcome separation persisted across longer follow-up. Likewise, cardiovascular and renal outcome separation also extended beyond the typical recorded treatment-duration window (Fig. 4, cardiovascular outcomes; Fig. 5, renal outcomes). However, this pattern should not be interpreted as evidence of a post-exposure or legacy effect. EHR prescription records incompletely capture medication persistence, dispensing, adherence, treatment outside the health system, dose changes, discontinuation, and restart patterns. Accordingly, the sustained separation of the Kaplan–Meier curves is most appropriately interpreted as a persistent post-initiation association that requires prospective validation using more complete longitudinal exposure ascertainment.

This stage-dependent interpretation is further supported by a complementary clinical-note trajectory study in AD, in which decades of routine care documentation were transformed into standardized cognitive, functional, global severity, and neuropsychiatric trajectories using LLM-enabled curation [28]. In that analysis, 42,242 of 131,824 patients with structured AD diagnoses had at least one LLM-derived outcome assessment, and 341 initiated semaglutide after diagnosis. Semaglutide-treated patients showed more favorable paired MMSE and MoCA changes than matched controls, while longitudinal trajectories revealed substantial heterogeneity in the temporal ordering of cognitive versus functional decline. These findings suggest that future incretin trials in AD may need to account not only for diagnostic category, but also for disease phase, functional reserve, multimorbidity, APOE context, and whether cognitive or functional decline is the leading trajectory domain.

The present findings are also consistent with, but methodologically distinct from, recent real-world analyzes linking GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure to lower dementia or AD-related outcomes. In a nationwide target-trial-emulation study of patients with type 2 diabetes, semaglutide was associated with a 40%–70% lower risk of first-time AD diagnosis compared with other antidiabetic medications, including other GLP-1 receptor agonists, and was also associated with lower AD-related medication initiation [22]. A subsequent real-world target-trial–emulation analysis reported lower AD-related dementia incidence among semaglutide users compared with insulin, metformin, and older-generation GLP-1 receptor agonists [49]. Another large propensity-score–matched study reported lower overall dementia, AD, and non-vascular dementia among GLP-1 receptor agonist initiators compared with metformin initiators in type 2 diabetes [45]. A CKD-focused analysis further reported lower dementia and AD risk among GLP-1 receptor agonist users compared with DPP-4 inhibitor users in patients with type 2 diabetes and stage 3 or later CKD [46]. The current study extends this literature by specifically evaluating adults with documented pre-existing AD risk factors, including depression, anxiety, sleep disorder, hearing loss, vision impairment, traumatic brain injury history, or memory complaints. This provides insight into a plausible real-world implementation scenario in which patients at elevated risk of future AD are identified before a formal dementia diagnosis and considered for prevention-focused risk modification.

The main results of this study are consistent with multiple other real-world analyzes of the relationship between GLP-1 receptor agonists and dementia risk. For example, in a target trial emulation study of over 1.2 million patients with type 2 diabetes in the United States, including over 64,000 patients exposed to Semaglutide, Semaglutide was associated with significantly lower risk of AD-related dementia compared to multiple other classes of antidiabetic medications [49]. Another similar study of 1.1 million patients with type diabetes showed lower rates of new AD diagnoses and initiation of AD-related medications [22]. This article is complementary to and expands on these prior analyzes in several ways. For example, this study included patients taking Semaglutide or Tirzepatide, including pooled and individual analyzes, marking the first real-world evidence associating Tirzepatide with a reduced risk of AD and other cognitive impairment diagnoses compared to other non–GLP-1 receptor agonist antidiabetic medications. Further, this study specifically evaluated patients with pre-existing risk factors for AD, including depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, hearing loss, vision impairment, history of traumatic brain injury, or memory complaints. This provides useful insight into a plausible real-world implantation scenario in which patients at highest risk of developing AD are identified as candidates for potential neuroprotective interventions.

Recent direct comparisons of tirzepatide and semaglutide further highlight why agent-specific analyzes require caution. In a real-world study of adults with type 2 diabetes, tirzepatide initiation was associated with lower risk of mild cognitive impairment and dementia compared with semaglutide, while the AD estimate was directionally lower but less precise and did not clearly establish superiority [50]. This article differs in its comparator structure and clinical question. Rather than asking whether tirzepatide is superior to semaglutide among incretin initiators, the primary analysis asked whether GLP-1–based incretin therapy as a class is associated with lower subsequent dementia-related diagnoses compared with non–GLP-1 antidiabetic therapy in an AD risk-enriched cohort. In this framework, semaglutide showed a statistically supported AD association against non–GLP-1 comparators, whereas tirzepatide showed directionally consistent but statistically imprecise AD estimates and stronger all-cause dementia and mortality associations. Head-to-head semaglutide-versus-tirzepatide comparisons in the present dataset did not show significant differences in AD, all-cause dementia, mild cognitive impairment, or Alzheimer’s-related medication initiation. Thus, the most defensible interpretation is a class-level, hypothesis-generating incretin prevention signal with possible agent-specific heterogeneity that requires longer tirzepatide follow-up, systematic cognitive ascertainment, and prospective validation.

The within-semaglutide stratified analyzes were designed to explore whether the AD association varied by achieved weight loss or attained dose. When semaglutide initiators were divided by weight-loss response in the first 12 months after initiation, three-year cumulative incidence of AD was 0.07% in patients achieving 5% or greater weight loss versus 0.40% in non-responders, an approximately 5.8-fold gradient; when the same cohort was stratified by sustained dose, no comparable gradient was observed. Although both stratifications were underpowered to reach statistical significance at the available event counts and require replication, the contrasting patterns are compatible with a weight-linked or broader cardiometabolic explanatory axis rather than a simple relationship with attained medication dose. However, these exploratory analyses do not establish mediation, causality, or a specific biological mechanism. Instead, they suggest that achieved weight loss, or correlated improvements in metabolic, vascular, inflammatory, or other cardiometabolic factors, may contribute to the observed association with lower AD incidence. This interpretation is consistent with the established role of obesity and type 2 diabetes as upstream risk factors for dementia while remaining hypothesis-generating [4, 5].

Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, this is an observational study; despite propensity-score matching with exact matching on 5-year age band and calendar year of initiation, residual confounding remains possible. Post-match standardized mean differences remained modestly above 0.10 for baseline body mass index, which was higher in the incretin arm and would be expected, if anything, to bias against the observed association, and for heart failure and chronic kidney disease, each of which was modestly enriched in the comparator arm. Also, although the primary hazard ratio for AD was compelling, the absolute number of events was modest, with 30 events in the GLP-1 RA arm and 67 events in the comparator arm. Several supportive and exploratory analyzes were further limited by cells with fewer than 11 events. These sparse-event settings increase statistical fragility and reinforce that the findings should be interpreted as prevention-nomination signals rather than evidence that incretin therapy prevents AD.

The present analysis cannot localize the biological event through which incretin exposure may be associated with lower recorded AD incidence. Neurological, mortality, cardiovascular, and renal endpoints are clinically meaningful but distal; they do not adjudicate whether GLP-1 receptor agonism, achieved weight loss, vascular remodeling, renal improvement, inflammatory changes, or another correlated process directly modifies AD biology. The risk-enrichment criteria were clinically heterogeneous. Psychiatric diagnoses, sleep disorders, sensory impairment, traumatic brain injury, and memory complaints may differ in their relationship to AD risk, metabolic burden, treatment selection, healthcare contact, and dementia ascertainment. Although depression, anxiety, antidepressant exposure, and antipsychotic exposure were included in propensity-score adjustment, the study did not separately model schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, bipolar disorder, severe or recurrent depression, mood-stabilizer exposure, or psychiatric-risk strata. Residual confounding by psychiatric severity and psychotropic prescribing structure therefore remains possible. HPA-axis biology and cortisol dynamics were not directly measured in this EHR analysis. This is relevant because depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbance may involve altered stress physiology, which can influence metabolic risk, vascular burden, immune tone, cognitive vulnerability, treatment selection, healthcare contact, and dementia ascertainment. We do not claim that GLP-1 receptor agonists directly suppress cortisol secretion in humans. Rather, HPA-axis biology is an unmeasured layer that may link psychiatric-risk enrichment, antidiabetic prescribing structure, and later dementia-related outcomes. It is also possible that improvements in glycemic control, inflammation, weight, sleep, or psychological well-being during incretin therapy could secondarily affect stress physiology without implying direct cortisol suppression.

Another limitation is regarding unmeasured factors, including diabetes duration, glycemic control, socioeconomic status, education, family history, APOE genotype, amyloid or tau biomarker status, frailty, and lifestyle factors, that were not uniformly available in the EHR and may influence both treatment selection and dementia risk. Also, exposure was assigned from EHR prescription records, which identify treatment initiation but may incompletely capture medication dispensing, adherence, persistence, dose escalation, discontinuation, treatment obtained outside the health system, or restart patterns. Therefore, the study is strongest as a new-user initiation analysis, not as a definitive analysis of cumulative exposure, pharmacologic persistence, or dose-response biology. Even in this context, it must be noted that AI-assisted extraction and augmented curation of clinical notes may introduce potential misclassification. Although prior validation studies support the use of LLM-assisted extraction from unstructured clinical text, error rates can vary by phenotype, institution, documentation style, note density, and model threshold. In this study, AI-derived AD, dementia, and mild cognitive impairment definitions were used as sensitivity analyzes rather than as primary outcomes, and a patient-level disease-presence probability threshold of 0.80 or higher was applied. Nevertheless, false-positive and false-negative classification remains possible. Differential documentation, including greater note density, specialist evaluation, cognitive screening, or diagnostic workup in one treatment arm, could also bias AI-derived ascertainment. Therefore, the AI-curated sensitivity analyzes should be interpreted as supportive robustness checks rather than definitive adjudication of cognitive disease status.

Yet another limitation is that healthy-user and healthy-adherer bias may contribute to the all-cause mortality and cardiorenal findings; however, the null falsification results across four pre-specified negative-control phenotypes and the reproducibility of the AD and dementia associations under stricter outcome definitions and across drug-specific comparisons argue against generic ascertainment bias as the primary explanation.

Although the null negative-control outcomes reduce concern for generalized healthcare-contact or ascertainment bias, they cannot exclude AD-specific residual confounding. Unmeasured or incompletely captured factors such as socioeconomic status, access to newer GLP-1 receptor agonist therapies, insurance coverage, healthcare engagement, frailty, education, cognitive reserve, family history, APOE genotype, amyloid or tau biomarker status, lifestyle factors, and psychiatric severity may influence both treatment selection and subsequent AD ascertainment. Accordingly, the negative-control findings should be interpreted as supportive falsification evidence, not as definitive exclusion of confounding relevant to AD risk.

A further limitation is that cognitive outcomes were based primarily on recorded diagnoses and medication initiation rather than systematic adjudication of cognitive status, and EHR-based outcome timing should be interpreted as first recorded diagnosis rather than true biological onset. Moreover, follow-up was limited to 5 years from the index date, and the post-launch tirzepatide cohort accrued less follow-up than the semaglutide and metformin cohorts; longer follow-up will be needed to assess durability and to estimate tirzepatide-specific AD associations with greater precision. Also, Cox models summarize average relative hazards over follow-up and assume proportional hazards; this assumption was not formally tested (e.g., via Schoenfeld residuals), so hazard ratios should be interpreted alongside Kaplan-Meier curves and landmark cumulative-incidence estimates, particularly for endpoints in which curve separation may vary over time. Finally, the relatively small number of AD events, particularly within subgroup and weight-loss analyzes, limits precision of effect estimates. Accordingly, subgroup analyzes should be considered exploratory and require prospective validation. Likewise, the within-semaglutide weight-loss and dose stratifications and the matched cognitive instrument score-change analysis were exploratory, limited by low event counts or paired-score availability, and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Taken together, these findings support a testable hypothesis that earlier initiation of GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy may be associated with lower subsequent AD incidence within a broader framework of cardiometabolic risk modification. The full analytic workflow, covering cohort construction, exposure assignment, incident-event handling, propensity-score matching, multiplicity correction, and sensitivity analyzes, is documented in Supplementary Table 4 to support independent auditability and reviewer reproducibility, given that the underlying de-identified EHR data and analysis code cannot be publicly shared. They should not be interpreted as demonstrating disease prevention or direct neuroprotection. Instead, they motivate prospective randomized prevention trials together with biomarker-guided and mechanistic investigations designed to determine whether modification of upstream metabolic, vascular, or other cardiometabolic risk factors translates into clinically meaningful reductions in AD incidence, and to distinguish causal biological mechanisms from downstream clinical associations.
