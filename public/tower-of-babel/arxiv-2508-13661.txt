MACTAS: Self-Attention-Based Module for Inter-Agent Communication in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
Maciej Wojtala, Bogusz Stefańczyk, Dominik Bogucki, Łukasz Lepak, Jakub Strykowski, Paweł Wawrzyński

Abstract
Communication is essential for the collective execution of complex tasks by human agents, motivating interest in communication mechanisms for multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL). However, existing communication protocols in MARL are often complex and non-differentiable. In this work, we introduce a self-attention-based communication module that exchanges information between the agents in MARL. Our proposed approach is fully differentiable, allowing agents to learn to generate messages in a reward-driven manner. The module can be seamlessly integrated with any action-value function decomposition method and can be viewed as an extension of such decompositions. Notably, it includes a fixed number of trainable parameters, independent of the number of agents. Experimental results on the SMAC benchmark demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach, which achieves state-of-the-art performance on several maps.

Introduction

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL)
(
Ning and Xie 2024
;
Du and Ding 2021
;
Nguyen, Nguyen, and Nahavandi 2020
;
Oroojlooy and Hajinezhad 2023
;
Wong et al. 2023
)
promises efficient control in large-scale systems through the collective learning of many agents in dynamic environments. Multiple agents interact within a shared environment to learn optimal policies.
MARL is defined as the process in which agents optimize their behaviors based on the history of their interactions with the environment, i.e., on their experience. Research in this area aims to design methods that enable agents to learn quickly, i.e., from minimal experience. The challenges and complexities in using MARL for controlling multi-agent systems include coordination among agents, non-stationary environments due to evolving agent policies, and scalability issues. Advanced MARL approaches often involve sophisticated techniques such as decentralized training, communication protocols among agents, and hierarchical methods to manage multi-agent interactions as effectively as possible. Theoretical advancements and practical applications of MARL demonstrate its potential in diverse fields ranging from robotics
(
Orr and Dutta 2023
)
and autonomous driving
(
Yadav, Mishra, and Kim 2023
;
Schmidt et al. 2022
;
Althamary, Huang, and Lin 2019
)
to telecommunications
(
Li et al. 2022b
;
Feriani and Hossain 2021
)
.

3s5z_vs_3s6z scenario in the StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC).

The most prominent research topics in MARL are the action-value function decompositions
(
Wang et al. 2021
;
Liu, Zhu, and Chen 2023
)
, where agents share a joint reward according to their contribution, and inter-agent communication
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Guo, Shi, and Fan 2023
)
, where agents exchange information in order to coordinate their joint policy. The most challenging multi-agent environments are partially observable, usually via limiting agents’ information to their close proximity, and the successful achievement of their common goals is predicated upon coordination, which needs to involve some form of communication. In most research, the action-value function and inter-agent communication are issues addressed separately. Therefore, in this paper, we introduce communication into the very core of the action-value function decomposition.

The prevailing paradigm in cooperative MARL is
Centralized Training and Decentralized Execution
(CTDE), where the training algorithm utilizes the global state, but agents can only use their own local observations. We argue that in most challenging MARL tasks, this is insufficient. Moreover, in most challenging MARL tasks, some form of inter-agent communication is a necessity, which can be provided relatively easily (e.g., through WiFi). Therefore, we introduce a communication protocol based on collecting information from each agent in a central entity and broadcasting the combined information to all agents. Because information aggregation is based on attention, our proposed architecture requires a constant number of weights plus an embedding for each agent. It is easy to apply using readily available and efficient implementations of the Transformer encoder layer.

The contribution of this paper may be summarized as follows:

•

We introduce Multi-Agent Communication via Transformer on Agents’ States (MACTAS) module for inter-agent communication in MARL. This module allows the agent to share their state of consciousness.

•

We verify MACTAS experimentally on several challenging maps from SMAC, giving excellent results and establishing state-of-the-art in some cases.

2
Related Work

Single-Agent Reinforcement Learning (SARL)
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
)
addresses trial-and-error learning of sequential decision making under uncertainty. In Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)
(
Ning and Xie 2024
)
, a group of agents learns to act in the same dynamic environment, collectively modifying its state. There are various settings for MARL, depending on whether the agents operate synchronously or asynchronously, whether they have common or adverse goals, and other circumstances. In this paper, we focus on the synchronous and cooperative MARL, with a single reward value for all agents at every time instance.

MARL has been addressed by extending well-established SARL algorithms: Multi-Agent SAC
(
Wei et al. 2018
)
, Multi-Agent PPO
(
Yu et al. 2022
)
, Multi-Agent DDPG
(
Lowe et al. 2017
)
.

Generally, cooperative MARL adopts a centralized training with a decentralized execution (CTDE) paradigm, which suffers from the global action-value function, whose complexity grows exponentially with the number of agents
(
Kraemer and Banerjee 2016
)
. Therefore, action-value function decomposition is a fundamental problem in MARL. Existing decomposed architectures include modules assigned to agents. These modules are fed with agents’ observations and their previous actions and produce agents’ contributions to the architecture’s output. The best action for the agent is the one that produces the highest contribution. In VDN
(
Sunehag et al. 2018
)
, these contributions are just added. In QMIX
(
Rashid et al. 2018
)
, a neural module, the mixing network, produces its weighted sum with positive weights. In Weighted QMIX
(
Rashid et al. 2020
)
, the architecture is trained with a special weight assigned to the collective actions deemed the best. In QTRAN
(
Son et al. 2019
)
, an additional module fed with all the actions of all agents corrects the weighted sum of the agents’ contributions. In Qatten
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
, the mixing network is based on attention.
In CDS
(
Li et al. 2021
)
, a component of the mixing network optimizes the amount of exploration undertaken by the agents.
In DVD
(
Li et al. 2022a
)
, the internal state of the modules assigned to agents is also passed to the mixing networks.
In SHAQ
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
, the mixing network is enriched by several components based on Shapley value theory.
In QPLEX
(
Wang et al. 2021
)
, a carefully engineered
duplex dueling
of pairs of contributions of agents is introduced into the mixing network. In NA
2
Q
(
Liu, Zhu, and Chen 2023
)
, a similar duplex dueling is performed with the use of attention.

Another fundamental problem in the cooperative MARL is the coordination of agents’ activity with a certain communication protocol. A number of solutions have been proposed for that purpose, including CommNet
(
Sukhbaatar, Szlam, and Fergus 2016
)
which introduces differentiable communication by aggregating a learned broadcast vector from each agent, TarMAC
(
Das et al. 2019
)
uses signature-based soft attention to direct agent communication towards recipients, NDQ
(
Wang et al. 2019
)
applies two forms of regularization – mutual information maximization and message entropy minimization to optimize message content, GA-Comm
(
Liu et al. 2020
)
uses two-stage attention mechanism to limit number of messages exchanged between agents, IS
(
Kim, Park, and Sung 2021
)
encodes trajectory of future actions for each agent and shares them with others, in MAIC
(
Yuan et al. 2022
)
agents explicitly model their teammates and use it to bias their value function, DHCG
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
learns a dependency graph between agents and uses it to limit the number of communication edges, TEM
(
Guo, Shi, and Fan 2023
)
which uses a Transformer to simulate communication resembling email exchange, where agents only directly communicate with teammates nearby, but can forward messages from one to another, and ACE
(
Li et al. 2023
)
which proposes bidirectional action-dependent Q-learning.

In addition, there are solutions in which the agents communicate in natural language, including: TWOSOME
(
Tan et al. 2024
)
which uses an LLM to evaluate the probabilities of joint action and applies LoRa for efficient PPO training, and Verco
(
Li et al. 2024
)
which separates the output of the LLM into communication and actions, allowing LLM agents to exchange interpretable verbal messages.

In our setting, we consider communication methods that can be connected with an arbitrary action-value decomposition method. We also allow agents to send messages based only on their local information (current observation, historical observations, and historical actions). In this framework, the best results so far have been achieved by MAIC
(
Yuan et al. 2022
)
.

3.1
Problem definition

In this paper, we discuss the problem of MARL using the formalism of a cooperative Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (DecPOMDP) with communication. A DecPOMDP is defined by a tuple,
\langle\mathbb{N},\mathbb{S},\mathbb{A},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{R}\rangle
, where
\mathbb{N}=\{1,\dots,n\}
is a team of agents,
\mathbb{S}
is a space of environmental states,
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{A}^{1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{A}^{n}
is a space of team actions of the agents,
\mathcal{P}
is the state transition probability,
\mathcal{O}
is the observation function, and
\mathcal{R}
is the reward function. At time
t=1,2,\dots
each
i
-th agent makes an observation,
o^{i}_{t}=\mathcal{O}^{i}(s_{t})
, of the environment state
s_{t}
. Agents may exchange messages with others through a certain channel that may generally refine these messages with some processing.

Each agent maintains a representation,
\mathcal{I}^{i}_{t}
, of observations it has made, messages it has received, and actions it has taken. Based on thereof and a policy,
\pi^{i}
, the agent performs an action,
a^{i}_{t}\in\mathbb{A}^{i}
,
a^{i}_{t}\sim\pi^{i}(\cdot|\mathcal{I}^{i}_{t})
. The team action
a_{t}=[(a^{1}_{t}),\dots,(a^{n}_{t})]
impacts the next environment state
s_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})
and the reward
r_{t}=\mathcal{R}(s_{t+1})
is given collectively to all agents.

The goal of the agents’ learning is to optimize their team policy
\pi=[\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{n}]
and the message exchange protocol for the team to expect in each environment state
s
the highest sum of discounted rewards

\mathcal{V}^{\pi}(s)=E\left(\sum_{i\geq 0}\gamma^{i}r_{t+i}\Big|s_{t}=s,\text{policy in use}=\pi\right),

where
\gamma\in[0,1]
is the discount factor.

The goal of this paper is to establish a message exchange protocol that supports the above goal of learning.

3.2
MACTAS

Our proposed Multi-Agent Communication via Transformer on Agents States (MACTAS) architecture is presented in Fig.
2
. It is built on a decomposition of the action-value function. Each such decomposition includes the input modules assigned to the agents (
f^{i}
assigned to the
i
-th agent for
i=1,\dots,n
in Figure
2
). These modules are fed with previous actions of these agents,
a^{i}_{t-1}
, and their current observations,
o^{i}_{t}
. They produce agents’ contributions,
\widehat{Q}^{i}_{t}
, to future discounted rewards and perhaps some other vectors
(
Li et al. 2022a
)
. The outputs of the
f^{i}
modules are fed to the mixing network that produces the value of the action-value function,
\widehat{Q}_{t}
. The input module
f^{i}
usually comprises a feedforward part, denoted by MLP in Figure
2
, a recurrent part, denoted by GRU, and the output feedforward part, again denoted by MLP.

Intuitively, we understand the
f^{i}
module as the
consciousness
of the
i
-th agent. Based on what the agent is aware of, it determines its contribution to future discounted rewards, depending on the action it currently takes. In order to give a global view on the current state to the agent’s consciousness, we introduce a communication mechanism. We divide the
f^{i}
module after its recurrent part and feed its state
h^{i}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{h}}
to the module
g
where the states of consciousness of the agents are confronted. As a result, the
g
module produces increments,
z^{i}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{h}}
. The sum
z^{i}_{t}+h^{i}_{t}
is then passed to the second MLP part of the
f^{i}
module.

Consequently, the consciousness of each agent is based not only on its observations and previous actions, but also on observations and previous actions of all other agents.

The minimal requirement for the
g
module is as follows:

1.

It is fed with
n
vectors and produces
n
vectors, both of size
\dim(h^{i}_{t})
.
i
-th input vector corresponds to the
i
-th output vector.

2.

It is a layered structure and the layers include self-attention
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
.

Introducing
g
satisfying the above requirements in their basic form is enough to outperform MARL without communication by a large margin. However, what follows from our experiments is that usually the best performing form of the
g
module is a stack of Transformer (encoder) modules
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
, with the following settings:

1.

There are no positional embeddings. The agents’ embeddings are fed to
f^{i}
instead.

2.

There is no masking.

3.

We use a separate Adam optimizer
(
Kingma and Ba 2017
)
for the Transformer parameters; the rest of the architecture is trained with its originally proposed optimizer
(typically it is RMSProp:
Tieleman and Hinton 2012
).

4.

The weights of the output layer of the top Transformer module are initialized with zeros. Therefore, initially, this module simply passes the input forward.

Good performance of the Transformer in this role is an expected result. This architecture has been specifically optimized to transform a set of embeddings into another set of the same size, which is exactly what we require in the
g
module.

3.3
Implementation considerations

The basic approach to implement a multi-agent system with our proposed MACTAS module is to set up the Intermediate Processing Unit (IPU) on which the module is run. At each time instant, all
n
agents transmit their internal states
h^{i}_{t}
to the IPU. Then, the Transformer layers process all the input vectors and transmit the increments
z^{i}_{t}
back to the agents. The number of operations necessary to process the Transformer modules is
O(n^{2})
. However, they are very efficiently parallelizable. The total size of all the transmissions is
O(n)
, specifically, equal to
2n\dim(h^{i}_{t})
.

Usually in multi-agent systems, reducing the communication capacity to
O(n)
and offloading the communication processing burden to the IPU is very convenient. In these systems, the agents are naturally connected through a network and their computational capacity is limited (e.g., they are mobile and battery powered), contrary to that of the network infrastructure. Still, these are the agents that need to take on the processing of their observations and control.

In most inter-agent communication protocols, including MAIC
(
Yuan et al. 2022
)
, all pairs of agents need to be able to communicate, which translates into
O(n^{2})
messages. Technically, the parts of the agents that communicate with others could be moved to an IPU. That would require
O(n)
messages to and from the IPU. Still, natural parallelization of MACTAS is its noticeable advantage.

A distributed implementation of a multi-agent system with our proposed module is also possible. Then, each agent needs to take on the part of processing that consists of refining
h^{i}_{t}
and results in
z^{i}_{t}
. That requires exchange of messages with other agents as many times as many Transformer layers are stacked in MACTAS, each time with
O(n)
messages;
O(n^{2})
for all agents. In training, the agents need to aggregate increments in their shared weights.

In the distributed implementation, it may be the case that some of the agents are out of reach of others and do not exchange information with them. That only means that self-attention in MACTAS is forced to assign zero weight to unavailable transmissions from those unheard agents.

3.4
Exploration

Learning in a multi-agent system with decentralized execution is hampered by agents’ policies falling into suboptimal Nash equilibria. Let us consider a typical MARL setting where agents use the
\epsilon
-greedy strategy with
\epsilon
annealing and explore independently.
On average, only
n\epsilon
agents explore. In order to learn that a certain combination of agents’ actions is better than the current Nash equilibrium, at least two agents need to explore and try a better combination. However, this is unlikely. What is more likely is that only one agent explores, which leads to a combination worse than greedy. Note that this effect may be even stronger for a better approximation of the
Q
function, as it leaves less room for accidental switches of the greedy action combinations.

To counteract this effect, we propose a combination of
\epsilon
-greedy and Boltzmann exploration. Namely, each agent with probability
\epsilon
chooses independently a random action; however, with probability
1-\epsilon
it does not act greedily, but chooses from the top
k
of its actions (according to its local action-value function) using the Boltzmann strategy. Using
k=1
is equivalent to the pure
\epsilon
-greedy policy. Selecting a small
k
leads to exploring the region that is quite good according to the agent. We observe that
k=2
works well in practice. The temperature of the Boltzmann exploration controls the amount of extra exploration that we want to add. We plan to extend this idea and other exploration strategies for MARL in future work.

4
Experiments

In this section, we present the results of our proposed MACTAS communication protocol in the StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC:
Samvelyan et al. 2019
) environment. We compare it with the state-of-the-art MAIC
(
Yuan et al. 2022
)
, in combinations with 3 mixer algorithms: QMIX, QPLEX, VDN, and either MAIC or MACTAS communication protocol, on 6 maps. We present results of two ablation studies regarding the design of neural network connectivity and the exploration form. Additionally, in the Appendix, we report the sensitivity study of MACTAS to hyperparameters, lower-level SMAC settings, and analyses of the most interesting strategies that have emerged in training.

4.1
StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge

StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC) is a micromanagement benchmark where two opposite teams face each other in different scenarios (maps), one team being controlled by the RL agent and the other by a built-in algorithm. Maps differ by difficulty, mainly due to an imbalance in the number of controlled units. For our experiments, we selected 2 hard and 4 super-hard scenarios, which are widely benchmarked in the MARL community:

•

2c_vs_64zg
with 2 colossus units leveraging terrain advantage against 64 small enemy zerglings. Level: hard.

•

5m_vs_6m
with 5 cooperating marines and 6 enemy marines. Level: hard.

•

27m_vs_30m
big battle of 27 marines conquering 30 marines. Level: super-hard.

•

MMM2
scenario with a diverse units containing 1 medivac (medic), 2 marauders, and 7 marines fighting team with 1 more marauder and 1 more marine. Level: super-hard.

•

6h_vs_8z
with 6 shooting hydralisks against 8 malee zealots. Level: super-hard.

•

3s5z_vs_3s6z
scenario with 3 stalkers and 5 zealots against 3 stalkers and 6 zealots. Level: super-hard.

In order to verify versatility of MACTAS algorithm, we run experiments on a diverse set of hard and super-hard scenarios. Every map provides the opportunity to find a unique winning strategy. The agent requires learning basic skills such as navigating the battlefield, managing health and shield, or focusing the fire. All considered scenarios are imbalanced in favor of the enemies; therefore, simple strategies are not sufficient to win the episode.
Popular observed winning policies include crossing the obstacle on
2c_vs_64zg
, flanking on
27m_vs_30m
, ’kiting’ on
6h_vs_8z
, ’hit and run’ on
3s5z_vs_3s6z

(
Samvelyan et al. 2019
)
.

For MAIC communication, we use the same hyperparameters as in the original paper.

MACTAS hyperparameters

We notice that MACTAS is sensitive to communication hyperparameters depending on the scenario setup. The parameters are the number of stacked Transformer encoders and the dimension of their positional feedforward network block. We theorize that the depth and width of the communication channel create a trade-off between the ability to learn a complex joint policy (eg. kiting, hit-and-run), and the time it takes to learn an effective policy.

Both MACTAS and MAIC are agnostic to the value function decomposition algorithm, so we compare both algorithms in combination with commonly used mixers: VDN, QMIX, and QPLEX. Each combination named

4.3
Results

The basic results are presented in Fig.
3
. We compare MACTAS with MAIC on 6 maps and 3 mixers, which makes 18 combinations. If we consider the area under the learning curve, MACTAS performs better than MAIC in 9 cases, MAIC performs better in 3 cases and there are 6 draws. If we consider the final performance, MACTAS yields better results 6 times, MAIC yields better results 3 times, and there are 9 draws.

On the
2c_vs_64zg
map, all algorithms except MAIC+VDN achieve the same final performance. In terms of the area under the learning curve MACTAS performs similarly to MAIC for the QMIX and VDN mixers and better for the QPLEX mixer. MACTAS+QMIX and MAIC+QMIX give jointly the best results on this map.
On the
5m_vs_6m
map MACTAS+QPLEX achieves the same final result as MAIC+QPLEX but with a better area under the learning curve. MACTAS+QMIX gives the same final result as MAIC+QMIX, but has a worse area under the learning curve (MAIC+QMIX drops around two million steps). MACTAS+VDN achieves a better final result than MAIC+VDN with a similar area under the learning curve (MAIC+VDN starts to learn faster but is unable to go beyond
60\%
of the mean of the won battles). On this map MACTAS+QPLEX gives the best result.
We observe that on the
27m_vs_30m
map, our algorithm achieves a better area under the learning curve for all three mixers, and for QPLEX, it also achieves a better final performance. For the other mixers, the final performance is similar. We hypothesize that the good performance of our algorithm is the case because the number of parameters of our algorithm is independent of the number of agents, and thus our method scales well in environments with a large number of agents. Overall, MACTAS+QMIX gives the best result on this map.
On the
MMM2
map, MAIC performs better for all three mixers in terms of the final result. However, the areas under the learning curves are similar and for MACTAS+QMIX and for MAIC+QMIX, as our algorithm starts to learn faster. MAIC+QPLEX performs the best on this map. This is the only map on which a version of our algorithm does not give the best result.
On the
6h_vs_8z
map MACTAS performs better than MAIC for the QMIX mixer in terms of the area under the learning curve and the final result. MACTAS+VDN gives a similar final result as MAIC+VDN, but with a better area under the learning curve. MACTAS+QPLEX achieves a similar final result as MAIC+QPLEX, but with a worse area under the learning curve. Overall, MACTAS+QMIX and MAIC+QPLEX give jointly the best result on this map.
On the
3s5z_vs_3s6z
map, MACTAS+QMIX and MACTAS+VDN give better results in terms of both the area under the learning curve and the final result than MAIC+QMIX and MAIC+VDN, respectively. MACTAS+QPLEX gives a similar final result to MAIC+QPLEX, but with a worse area under the learning curve. MACTAS+VDN performs the best on this map.

4.5
Discussion

Our experiments reveal that MACTAS outperforms MAIC in
\frac{1}{2}
cases in terms of the area under the training curve and
\frac{1}{3}
cases in terms of the final performance. MAIC performs better than our method in
\frac{1}{6}
of the cases, and in the remaining cases, the performances are similar. Our method performs well with the QMIX mixer on all maps, while MAIC+QMIX struggles on the
\verb|6h_vs_8z|
and
\verb|3s5z_vs_3s6z|
maps. On these two maps, MAIC+QPLEX gives quite good results; however, its performance on the
\verb|2c_vs_64zg|
and
\verb|27m_vs_30m|
maps is much worse than all the other algorithms.
The existence of the residual connection around the communication module in MACTAS does not impact its performance on the maps requiring 2.5 million training steps, but is crucial on the
6h_vs_8z
and
3s5z_vs_3s6z
maps.
The specific exploration scheme introduced in Section
3.4
improves the performance of MACTAS in one case,
3s5z_vs_3s6z
, and is insignificant in others.
The absence of the dropout hampers performance; however, the exact value of the dropout parameter is not crucial when set in a reasonable interval (
5\%-20\%
). The performance of MACTAS is moderately sensitive to the shape of the communication module defined by the number of layers and the size of the hidden layer of the MLP contained there.

The modular nature of MACTAS is its great advantage. There are widespread variants of the Transformer code optimized for different machines. This makes the implementation and deployment of MACTAS in a given multi-agent system straightforward.

Limitations

A moderate sensitivity of the performance of our proposed communication module to its shape requires searching for its best setting in additional runs. This slows the deployment of MACTAS.

5
Conclusions and Future Work

In this paper, we have introduced MACTAS – a communication module for multi-agent reinforcement learning based on the Transformer encoder architecture. It is easy to implement and requires a fixed number of parameters, independent of the number of agents. It can be easily switched in any multi-agent action-value function architecture. We evaluated our proposed module in SMAC, the arguably most challenging MARL benchmark. The experimental results show that MACTAS+QMIX achieves SOTA results on the
27m_vs_30m
SMAC map and MACTAS+VDN on the
3s5z_vs_3s6z
map. MACTAS+QMIX achieves similar results as the best version of MAIC for the
\verb|2c_vs64_zg|
, and
\verb|6h_vs_8z|
maps, MACTAS+QPLEX achieves similar results as the best version of MAIC for the
5m_vs_6m
map. Only on the
\verb|MMM2|
map can we not level the MAIC results.

We observe that
\epsilon
-greedy exploration applied independently by agents in MARL leaves room for improvement since it condones the agents’ policies falling into suboptimal Nash equilibria. We addressed this issue by proposing a combination of
\epsilon
-greedy and Boltzmann exploration, which increased the performance of MACTAS+QMIX on the
3s5z_vs_3s6z
SMAC map. However, we believe that this issue requires further research and intend to undertake it in the future.

Appendix A
StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC)

As a benchmark for MACTAS, we chose the StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC)
(
Samvelyan et al. 2019
)
, which is a simplified version of the StarCraft II game. The official repository is available on
https://github.com/oxwhirl/smac
. SMAC uses PySc2 to communicate with the game engine
https://github.com/google-deepmind/pysc2
.
SMAC is characterized by decentralized management of each unit, and each unit is an independent agent. This feature allows for the testing of decentralized algorithms, including those originating from MARL. Benchmark scenarios are limited to combat with a limited number of units. Those scenarios do not account for the economic part of the game, the so-called “macro” part of the game – collecting resources and building structures. Resources in the game (gas and minerals) are required to build units and structures. Structures allow for the recruitment of units and their upgrading. The considered scenarios do not involve “worker” units, which collect resources. Instead, the scenarios focus on ’micro,’ the term used in the StarCraft II community for unit control during combat.
The challenge for a human player is multitasking between these two tasks – developing the game economy and controlling the fighting units. SMAC focuses only on combat, thereby ensuring this benchmark repeatability, as each iteration involves a fight between the same specification units, eliminating the uncertainty with unit upgrades and different strategies for the economic part of the game. In StarCraft II, there are three races, and our experiments covered controlling units of all of them. The way to run SMAC is described in our repository.

Appendix C
Tactics used by MACTAS

The SMAC challenge forces agents to learn various tactics, such as attacking from distance melee units (’kiting’) or using movement advantage (’hit and run’). In this section, we provide an overview of the tactics used by our model based on our understanding of StarCraft II. We observed differences in the behavior of our model depending on the random seed. The description of tactics is based on replays of our models for seed = 1.

C.1
2c_vs_64zg

Figure 9:
2c_vs_64zg replay.

In this scenario, agents control 2 colossi units. Colossi can move through hills between the high and the low ground. Against them, 64 enemy zerglings are deployed, which are fast ground units. Zerglings are most effective when they surround an opposite unit. In the scenario
2c_vs_64zg
, the colossi start on the hill, while the zerglings are split into two groups of 32 on the left and the right, both of them on the low ground. Figure
9
presents the following tactics: colossi attack the right group of zerglings and wait for the left group to climb the hill. Then, colossi use the ability to traverse the hill and attack the remaining zerglings, which are forced to run around the hill. In this way, the colossi avoid being surrounded and thereby defeated.

C.2
27m_vs_30m and 5m_vs_6m

Figure 10:
27m_vs_30m replay.

Figure 11:
5m_vs_6m replay.

The
27m_vs_30m
and the
5m_vs_6m
scenarios consist of marine units only. They are similar in the way that the opposing force numerically outnumbers the agents under control.
In Figure
10
, the agent’s units place themselves in a semicircle formation, also known as flanking, which maximizes the number of shooting units, while the enemies block each other. Units controlled by MACTAS focus fire on enemy units with lower health, in order to quickly reduce the number of opponents. Furthermore, units with low health retreat and continue fighting, while avoiding being shot. This behavior is presented in Figure
11
: Agents with low health retreat when others focus fire on one enemy unit. The winning strategy is similar for both
5m_vs_6m
and
27m_vs_30m
.
5m_vs_6m
is labeled as a hard benchmark, while
27m_vs_30m
is classified as super-hard. The percentage imbalance between units is higher in
5m_vs_6m
. However,
27m_vs_30m
has a larger number of units that need to cooperate.

C.3
MMM2

Figure 12:
MMM2 replay.

The MMM2 is a scenario with a diverse set of units containing 1 medivac (medic), 2 marauders, and 7 marines fighting a team with 1 more marauder and 1 more marine. Medivac cannot attack, but can heal units as long as it has energy. MACTAS ground units focus fire on enemy units and eliminate them one by one, and fall back when their health is low. The enemy medivac does not change healed unit, while the MACTAS’ medivac changes healed unit to the one with a critically low health or to the currently attacked unit. Units trained with MACTAS move medivac to the front line and focus enemy fire on it, and when its health level is low, retreat it. MACTAS’ medivac taking enemy fire secures outnumbered marines and marauders. Typically, human players try to save their medivacs because they are used for healing units between fights during a regular game. Only under certain conditions do they sacrifice their medivacs; for example, when they are breaking through an opponent’s line of defense to focus enemy fire on the medivacs and away from damage-dealing units. In this scenario, we can observe similar behavior in the MACTAS medivac control, which results in winning combat against an opponent who has a numerical advantage.

C.4
6h_vs_8z

Figure 13:
6h_vs_8z replay.

Agents control 6 range shooting units, hydralisks, while the enemy controls 8 melee zealots. In Figure
13
, we can see a tactic called “kiting” – hydralisks split into two groups and are attacking zealots from distances. Enemy zealots attack the nearest unit – our agents learned to move enough to change the focus of approaching units and then continue striking. Notably, the zealot units within this scenario are not upgraded with the “charge” ability, which would otherwise allow them to have a significant increase in movement speed and easily reach hydralisks.

C.5
3s5z_vs_3s6z

Figure 14:
3s5z_vs_3s6z replay.

MACTAS controls 3 stalkers and 5 zealots, while the enemy has one more zealot. The stalkers are fast, range-attacking units, but with weak defense. On the other hand, zealots have good defense, but need to get close to the opponent unit. The tactic in this scenario is interesting. Using one stalker as a decoy, MACTAS first defeats the enemy stalkers and then proceeds to eliminate the rest of the enemy force. One agent, a stalker, sacrifices himself and focuses a few enemy zealots on himself. In Figure
14
, we can see 5 enemy zealots following the stalker marked as “Local Player”. The remaining MACTAS’ 2 stalkers and 5 zealots attack 3 enemy stalkers and 1 zealot with an advantage. This move results in the elimination of enemy stalkers and the zealot. In the end, MACTAS’ units eliminate zealots lured by the sacrificed stalker.

We observe that the choice of the proper architecture is important; however, there is a certain amount of freedom in the choice of the architecture. For all mixers on the

shows the impact of the softmax temperature parameter on the proposed exploration scheme. We observe that this method is sensitive to this parameter. For the

0.25
achieving similar results to the

-greedy scheme, which is equivalent to choosing

-greedy exploration for the
0.33
