Equivalence of sub-Laplacians on Polarized groups
Antoni Kijowski, Sebastiano Nicolussi Golo, Ben Warhurst

Abstract
. We characterize smooth maps between sub-Riemannian Lie groups that commute with sub-Laplacians. We show they are sub-Riemannian conformal submersions. Our work clarifies the analysis initiated on Carnot groups in [ 1 ] . In particular, we show that the sub-Laplacian in a Carnot group determines the sub-Riemannian structure.

35B06, 53C17, 35H20, 53C30, 22F30, 22E25.

1.
Introduction

Laplace operators, defined as
\triangle u:=\operatorname{div}(\nabla u)
, depend on the choice of a metric and a measure.
The measure determines the divergence
\operatorname{div}
of vector fields.
The metric, Riemannian or sub-Riemannian, determines the gradient
\nabla u
(a vector field) in terms of the differential
\,\mathrm{d}u
(a differential form).

On a Lie group, we assume both the metric and the measure to be left invariant.
These conditions restrict the class of Laplace operators we obtain.
For example, on the abelian Lie group
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists just one Laplacian up to a change of coordinates.
In other words, if
\triangle_{1}
and
\triangle_{2}
are two Laplace operators determined by different choices of scalar product and Haar measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then there exists a linear isomorphism
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that, for every
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\triangle_{2}(u\circ F)=(\triangle_{1}u)\circ F
.

We are firstly interested in similar results for Carnot groups or, more generally, sub-Riemannian Lie groups.
We will describe sub-Riemannian Lie groups as tuples
(G,V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,\mathrm{vol}_{G})
,
where
G
is a Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
,

V=V(G)\subset\mathfrak{g}
is a vector space that is bracket generating in
\mathfrak{g}
,

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is a scalar product on
V
,
and
\mathrm{vol}_{G}
is a left-invarian Haar measure on
G
.
See Section
2.3
for details.
Typical examples of sub-Riemannian Lie groups are Riemannian Lie groups (when
V=\mathfrak{g}
) and Carnot groups (when
V
is the first layer of a stratification).

These data determine a (sub-)Laplace operator
\triangle_{G}u=\operatorname{div}_{G}(\nabla_{G}u)
,
where
\operatorname{div}_{G}
is the divergence defined by
\mathrm{vol}_{G}
and
\nabla_{G}u
is the horizontal gradient of
u
determined by
(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.
The operator
\triangle_{G}
is sub-elliptic, but not elliptic as soon as
V\neq\mathfrak{g}
.

The study of maps that commute with a Laplace operator is not new.
Helgason proved in
[
9
, p. 387]
that a diffeomorphism
F:G\to H
between Riemannian manifolds is an isometry if and only if it is a Laplacian commuting map, i.e.,
\triangle_{G}(u\circ F)=(\triangle_{H}u)\circ F
for all
u
of class
C^{2}
on
H
.
Watson extended this result in
[
18
]
: a smooth map
F:G\to H
is a Laplacian commuting map if and only if it is a harmonic Riemannian submersion, that is, an orthogonal submersion that is critical for a Dirichlet energy.
Goldberg and Ishihara gave a further generalization in
[
5
]
.
Fuglede
[
4
]
and Ishihara
[
10
]
independently showed that a smooth map
F:G\to H
between Riemannian manifolds is a Laplacian commuting map if and only if is is a harmonic morphism, that is, it pulls back harmonic functions to harmonic functions.
See also
[
6
,
8
,
16
,
17
]
, and further references in the bibliography of harmonic morphisms
[
7
]
.

We have a slightly different approach.
If
F:G\to H
is a smooth map between sub-Riemannian Lie groups, then the operator
u\mapsto\triangle_{G}(u\circ F)
is a differential operator
C^{2}(H)\to C^{0}(G)
of order two.
Thus, we can write
\triangle_{G}(u\circ F)=P_{2}u+P_{1}u+P_{0}u
, where each
P_{j}:C^{2}(H)\to C^{0}(G)
is a homogeneous differential operator of order
j
.
The following question arises:

Is
P_{2}
of the form
P_{2}u=\lambda^{2}\cdot(\triangle_{H}u)\circ F
, for some
\lambda:G\to(0,+\infty)
?

We show that this happens exactly when
F
is a conformal submersion, in which case the lower order terms
P_{1}
and
P_{0}
are determined.
A smooth map
F:G\to H
between sub-Riemannian Lie groups is a
conformal submersion
of factor
\lambda
if, for every
p
, the adjoint map between the dual spaces

(DF(p)|_{V(G)})^{*}:V(H)^{*}\to V(G)^{*}

is a homothetic embedding of factor
\lambda(p)
.
See Section
2.8
.

Theorem A
.

Let
G
and
H
be sub-Riemannian Lie groups, with
\Omega_{G}\subset G
and
\Omega_{H}\subset H
open.
Suppose that
F:\Omega_{G}\to\Omega_{H}
is a
C^{2}
-smooth map,
and that
\lambda:\Omega\to[0,+\infty)
,

b:\Omega\to V(H)
,
and
c:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
are continuous functions.

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

for all
u\in C^{2}(\Omega_{H})
,

\triangle_{G}(u\circ F)=\lambda^{2}\cdot(\triangle_{H}u)\circ F+\langle b,(\nabla_{H}u)\circ F\rangle_{H}+c\cdot(u\circ F).

(ii)

F
is a conformal submersion of factor
\lambda
,
c\equiv 0
and

b(p)=\mathrm{trace}_{G}(D^{2}F(p))-DF(p)[\nabla_{G}(\log\mu_{G})(p)]+\lambda(p)^{2}\nabla_{H}(\log\mu_{H})(F(p)).

In the case
\dim(G)=\dim(H)
and
\lambda>0
, then both conditions are equivalent to
F
being a conformal
C^{2}
diffeomorphism.
In particular, if
\lambda
is constant then
F
is a homothety;
if
\lambda\equiv 1
, then
F
is an isometry.

Proof.

Theorem

For the implication

satisfying even the apparently weaker conditions (

), or (

.
The form of the vector

.

Finally, we observe that conformal submersions between groups with the same (topological) dimension are diffeomorphisms and fall into the definition of conformal maps.

When
\lambda\equiv 1
, Theorem

gives a characterization of submetries of class
C^{2}
in terms of the sub-Laplacian.
When
\lambda\equiv 1
and
\dim(G)=\dim(H)
, Theorem

gives a characterization of isometries in terms of the sub-Laplacian.

In the particular case of Carnot groups we have the following corollary.
A
Carnot group
is a connected simply connected sub-Riemannian Lie group whose polarization
V(G)\subset\mathfrak{g}
is the first layer of a stratification of
\mathfrak{g}
.
See
[
12
,
2
]
for details.
The important feature of Carnot groups is that they support dilations, that is, homotheties for all positive factors
\lambda>0
.

Theorem B
.

Let
G
and
H
be sub-Riemannian Carnot groups.
If there exist a smooth map
F:G\to H
of class
C^{2}
and
\lambda>0

such that, for all
u\in C^{2}(H)
,

(1)

\triangle_{G}(u\circ F)=\lambda^{2}(\triangle_{H}u)\circ F

then
H
is a quotient of
G
, as Carnot groups.

If
\dim(G)=\dim(H)
, then
F
itself is the composition of a dilation, a left translation, and an (isometric) isomorphism of Carnot groups.
In particular,
G
and
H
are isomorphic as Carnot groups.

Theorem
B
is a particular case of Propositions
5.3
and
5.4
: see section
5.2
for their proofs.

Remark 1.1
.

The map
F
in Theorem
B
is in fact
C^{\infty}
smooth.
Indeed, if
u\in C^{\infty}(H)
and if
F
is
C^{2}
, then (
1
) implies that
u\circ F
is
C^{\infty}
smooth, by a standard bootstrap argument that uses the hypoellipticity of the sub-Laplacian.
Since
u
is arbitrary, we can take a system of coordinates in
H
and conclude that
F
is
C^{\infty}
smooth.
Such an argument cannot be used under the hypothesis of Theorem
A
.

Theorem
B
answers to a question from
[
1
]
.
In
[
1
, Chapter 16]
, it is asked whether two differential operators defined as sum of squares of vector fields on a Carnot group are equivalent.
More precisely, if
G
is a simply connected Lie group with stratified Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{s}V_{j}
, given two basis
X=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{r}\}
and
Y=\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r}\}
of
V_{1}
,
define
\triangle_{X}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\tilde{X}_{j}^{2}
and
\triangle_{Y}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\tilde{Y}_{j}^{2}
.
We use the tilde
\tilde{v}
to denote the left-invariant vector field with
\tilde{v}(1_{G})=v\in\mathfrak{g}
.
Thus, we wonder whether there exists a diffeomorphism
F_{0}:G\to G
such that
\triangle_{X}(u\circ F_{0})=(\triangle_{Y}u)\circ F_{0}
for all
u\in C^{2}(G)
.

In
[
1
, Chapter 16]
, it is shown that if
G
is a free Lie group, then such a diffeomorphism
F_{0}
always exists, and it is a Lie group automorphism.
It is also shown that in Heisenberg groups it may not exist.

Theorem
B
implies that such an diffeomorphism
F_{0}
exists if and only if the two sub-Riemannian Carnot groups given by the scalar products on
V_{1}
that make
X
and
Y
orthonormal, respectively, are isometric.
In particular, such an isomorphism
F_{0}
is itself an isometry and a Lie group automorphism.
However,
F_{0}
does not need to map the basis
X
to
Y
.
See also Corollary
5.1
.

In this way, we recover the results shown in
[
1
, Chapter 16]
for free groups.
Indeed, if
G
is a free Lie group, then every choice of two basis
X
and
Y
for
V_{1}
determines a Lie group automorphism
F_{0}:G\to G
that such that
(F_{0})_{*}X_{j}=Y_{j}
for all
j
.
Moreover, we give a complete description of all equivalence classes of sub-Laplacians in Heisenberg groups,
see Section
5.4
.

Finally, we notice that, since every Carnot group is a Carnot quotient of a free Carnot group, then every sub-Laplacian on a Carnot group is induced by the sub-Laplacian of a free group via a conformal submersion of factor 1, i.e., a submetry, which is the quotient map itself.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
General notations

If
M
is a smooth manifold, we denote by
TM
its tangent bundle
and by
\Gamma^{k}(TM)
the sections of
TM
of class
C^{k}
.
For
k=\infty
, we just write
\Gamma(TM):=\Gamma^{\infty}(TM)
.
If
F:M\to N
is a
C^{1}
map between smooth manifolds, we denote by
dF:TM\to TN
its standard differential-geometric differential.

2.2.
Lie groups

Given a Lie group
G
, we identify, as vector spaces, its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{Lie}(G)
with the tangent space
T_{1}G
of
G
at the identity
1_{G}
of
G
.
We denote by
L_{p}
the left translation by
p
.
If
v:E\to\mathfrak{g}
for some
E\subset G
, then
\tilde{v}:E\to TG
is the map

\tilde{v}(p):=dL_{p}[v]\in T_{p}G.

If
f
is a vector valued
C^{1}
-function defined in a neighborhood of
p\in G
, then

\tilde{v}f(p):=df(p)[\tilde{v}(p)]=\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}f(p\exp(tv)).

If
v\in\mathfrak{g}
, then
\tilde{v}
is the left-invariant vector field with value
v
at
1
.

2.3.
Polarized and Sub-Riemannian Lie groups

A
polarized Lie group
is a pair
(G,V)
where
G
is a connected Lie group and
V\subset\mathfrak{g}
is a bracket-generating subspace of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
G
. We call
V
a
polarization
of
G
and denote it by
V(G)
.
By
bracket-generating subspace
we mean a linear subspace that Lie generates the Lie algebra, i.e.,
\mathfrak{g}
is the smallest Lie algebra containing
V
.

Carnot groups are the stratified nilpotent examples.
An example where
\mathfrak{g}
is not nilpotent is
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
In this case the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
has the basis

X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\quad Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\quad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}

where

[X,Y]=Z\quad[Z,X]=2X\quad[Z,Y]=-2Y.

A two dimensional polarization is given by
V(G)={\rm span}\{X,Y\}
.

In general, the bundle of left translates

\tilde{V}(G):=\bigcup_{p\in G}dL_{p}(V)

forms a
horizontal subbundle
of
TG
, which is bracket generating.

A
sub-Riemannian Lie group
is a pair
(G,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G})
where
G
is a polarized Lie group and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G}
is a scalar product on the polarization
V(G)
.

2.4.

Let
G
and
H

\Omega\subset G
open.
The
Lie differential
of a smooth function

(2)

DF(p)[v]:=\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}F(p)^{-1}F(p\exp(tv)).

We will use square brackets

to highlight the (multi-)linear entry of a function.
In some cases we will drop the brackets and just juxtapose the vector as in

2.6.
Contact maps

Let
G
and
H
be polarized Lie groups and
\Omega\subset G
open.
A
C^{1}
-map
F:\Omega\to H
is a
contact map
if

DF(p)[V(G)]\subseteq V(H),\qquad\forall p\in\Omega.

Moreover by Proposition
(.a)
,
F
is a contact map if and only if
dF(p)[\tilde{V}(G)_{p}]\subseteq\tilde{V}(H)_{F(p)}
for all
p\in\Omega
.

Notice that, if
F:\Omega\to H
is a contact map of class
C^{2}
, then its second order Lie differential

D^{2}F(p)
at
p\in\Omega
preserves the polarizations, in the sense that

D^{2}F(p)[V(G),\mathfrak{g}]\subseteq V(H)
.
This follows immediately from (
3
).

If
G
and
H
are sub-Riemannian Lie groups, and if
F:\Omega\to H
is a contact map of class
C^{2}
, then the
trace
of the bilinear map
D^{2}F(p)|_{V(G)}:V(G)\times V(G)\to V(H)
is

\mathrm{trace}_{G}(D^{2}F(p)):=\sum_{i=1}^{r}D^{2}F(p)[X_{i},X_{i}],

for one (thus every) orthonormal basis
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
of
V(G)
.
The fact that the definition of
\mathrm{trace}_{G}(D^{2}F(p))
does not depend on the choice of the orthonormal basis of
V(G)
is a standard computation.
Indeed, if
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r}
is another orthonormal basis of
V(G)
,
then there exists a matrix
a\in\mathrm{GL}(r)
with
a^{T}=a^{-1}
and
Y_{i}=a_{i}^{j}X_{j}
(sum over
j
); hence

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}D^{2}F(p)[Y_{i},Y_{i}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}D^{2}F(p)[a_{i}^{j}X_{j},a_{i}^{k}X_{k}]=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{j}a_{i}^{k}D^{2}F(p)[X_{j},X_{k}]

\displaystyle\overset{a_{i}^{k}=(a^{T})^{i}_{k}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{j}(a^{T})^{i}_{k}D^{2}F(p)[X_{j},X_{k}]

\displaystyle\overset{a^{T}=a^{-1}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{j}(a^{-1})^{i}_{k}D^{2}F(p)[X_{j},X_{k}]

\displaystyle=\delta_{k}^{j}D^{2}F(p)[X_{j},X_{k}]=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}D^{2}F(p)[X_{\ell},X_{\ell}].

2.7.
Homothetic projections

Let
V,W
be Hilbert spaces.
The
transpose
of a linear map
L:V\to W
is the linear map
L^{T}:W\to V
such that

\langle L^{T}w,v\rangle_{V}=\langle w,Lv\rangle_{W}

for all
v\in V
and
w\in W
.

A linear map
L:V\to W
is a
homothetic projection
of factor
\lambda\geq 0
if

(6)

\langle L^{T}w_{1},L^{T}w_{2}\rangle_{V}=\lambda^{2}\langle w_{1},w_{2}\rangle_{W},

for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in W
,
i.e., the transpose
L^{T}:W\to V
is a
homothetic embedding
of factor
\lambda
.
Clearly, using the polarization identity, one can equivalently check (
6
) only for
w_{1}=w_{2}
.
It follows that, if
\lambda=0
then
L=0
, while if
\lambda>0
then
L
is surjective.
Moreover, we have the equivalent formulations in Proposition
2.4
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
V
and

(

(

(

, for every

(

To show

For

on the one hand, if

then, for all

,

\langle L^{T}w,v\rangle=\langle w,Lv\rangle=0
.
Thus,

.
On the other hand, if

, then, for all

.
Thus,

, and therefore

.

Next, we show

:
If

, then, for all

.
Therefore,

.

Finally, we show

:
if

, then there is

The last statement

Indeed, if

\langle Lv,L\bar{v}\rangle\overset{\eqref{eq6708401d}}{=}\lambda^{-2}\langle L^{T}Lv,L^{T}L\bar{v}\rangle\overset{\ref{lem6771bbb2_4}}{=}\lambda^{-2}\langle\lambda^{2}v,\lambda^{2}\bar{v}\rangle=\lambda^{2}\langle v,\bar{v}\rangle
,
which implies
(.e)
.
∎

2.8.
Conformal submersion of sub-Riemannian Lie groups

Let
G
and
H
be sub-Riemannian Lie groups, and
\Omega\subset G
open.
A
C^{1}
-conformal submersion
of factor
\lambda:\Omega\to[0,+\infty)
is a
C^{1}
contact map
F:\Omega\to H
,
such that, for every
p\in\Omega
, the restriction
DF(p)|_{V(G)}:V(G)\to V(H)
is a linear homothetic projection of factor
\lambda(p)
.

We stress that the use of the term “projection” is slightly abused, because
\lambda(p)
may be zero, in which case

.
However, if
\lambda(p)\neq 0
, then
DF(p)[V(G)]=V(H)
.
Since
V(H)
is bracket-generating,

is a smooth submersion on

, that is, the Lie differential
DF(p):\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{h}
is surjective for all

.

2.10.
Haar measure and modular function

Let
G
be a Lie group, let
\mathrm{vol}_{G}
denote a left-invariant Haar measure on
G
.
With a slight abuse of notation, we write
\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}
for the volume form that represents the measure
\mathrm{vol}_{G}
.

The measure
\mathrm{vol}_{G}
is left invariant in the sense that

(11)

\forall a\in G,\forall f\in L^{1}(\mathrm{vol}_{G}),\qquad\int_{G}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(y)=\int_{G}f(ax)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(x).

Equivalently, the volume form
\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}
is left invariant, in the sense that

\forall a,x\in G\qquad dL_{a}|_{x}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(ax)=\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(x).

The
modular function
of a Lie group
G
is the smooth group morphism
\mu_{G}:G\to(0,\infty)
given by

\mu_{G}(g):=\det(\operatorname{Ad}_{g}),

where
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is the adjoint representation of
G
.
More explicitly,
\operatorname{Ad}_{g}=DC_{g}
where
C_{g}(x)=gxg^{-1}
is the conjugation by
g
.
See
[
3
, Chapter 11]
for further details.

The role of the modular function can be described as follows.
If
g\in G
, then
R_{g}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}
is again a left-invariant volume form, because left and right translations commute with each other.
Therefore, there is a constant
\mu>0
such that
R_{g}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}=\mu\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}
:
clearly, such constant depends on
g
but not on the choice of
\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}
.
One can compute
\mu
and see that it is exactly
\mu_{G}(g)^{-1}
.
Indeed, since
C_{g}=L_{g}\circ R_{g^{-1}}
and
\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}
is left invariant, we have
C_{g}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}=R_{g^{-1}}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}
;
on the other hand,
C_{g}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}=\det(\operatorname{Ad}_{g})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}=\mu_{G}(g)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}
, because
C_{g}
is a group automorphism fixing
1_{G}
and pulling back a left-invariant volume form by an automorphism rescales it by the determinant of its differential at
1_{G}
.
In summary, for all
g\in G
,

(12)

C_{g}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}=\mu_{G}(g)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G},\qquad\text{and}\qquad R_{g}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}=\mu_{G}(g)^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}.

Moreover, for all
g\in G
and
f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathrm{vol}_{G})
,

(13)

\int_{G}f(xg)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(x)=\mu_{G}(g)\int_{G}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(x).

If
\mu_{G}\equiv 1
, then
G
is called
unimodular
.
Left-invariant Haar measures on unimodular Lie groups are also right invariant.

2.11.
Divergence

Let
G
be a Lie group, let
\mathrm{vol}_{G}
denote a left-invariant Haar measure on
G
and let
\Omega\subset G
be open.

The
Lie derivative
of a differential form
\omega
on
G
along a vector field
\tilde{X}\in\Gamma^{1}(T\Omega)
is the continuous differential form

\mathscr{L}_{\tilde{X}}\omega:=\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}(\Phi_{\tilde{X}}^{t})^{*}\omega,

where
\Phi_{\tilde{X}}
is the flow of
\tilde{X}
.

The
divergence
of a
C^{1}
vector field
\tilde{X}\in\Gamma^{1}(T\Omega)
is
the real-valued function
\operatorname{div}_{G}\tilde{X}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}

defined by the identity

(14)

\mathscr{L}_{\tilde{X}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}=(\operatorname{div}_{G}\tilde{X})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G},

where
\mathscr{L}
is the Lie derivative.
Notice that the divergence does not depend on the choice of a particular Haar measure of
G
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\Omega\subset G
be an open subset of
G
,
and
\tilde{X}\in\Gamma^{1}(T\Omega)
.
A continuous function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the divergence of
\tilde{X}
with respect to
\mathrm{vol}_{G}
, i.e.,
f=\operatorname{div}_{G}\tilde{X}
,
if and only if, for all
\phi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
,

(15)

\int_{\Omega}\phi\cdot f\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}=-\int_{\Omega}d\phi[\tilde{X}]\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}.

Proof.

We start with the following standard identity of differential forms (see
[
14
, Proposition 12.32]
) for
\tilde{X}\in\Gamma^{1}(T\Omega)
and
\phi\in C^{1}(\Omega)
:

(16)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\tilde{X}}(\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G})

\displaystyle=d\phi[\tilde{X}]\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}+\phi\cdot\mathscr{L}_{\tilde{X}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}

\displaystyle=d\phi[\tilde{X}]\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}+\phi\cdot(\operatorname{div}_{G}\tilde{X})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G},

where
\phi\in C^{1}(\Omega)
.
The lemma follows from the fact that, for all
\phi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)

(17)

\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{L}_{\tilde{X}}(\phi\mathrm{vol}_{G})=0.

To prove (
17
) given
\phi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
, one takes
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
such that
\psi=1
on a neighborhood of the support of
\phi
.
Then, the vector field
\tilde{Y}:=\psi\tilde{X}
has compact support in
\Omega
and thus it is complete.
Moreover, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that, if
|t|<\epsilon
, then
\Phi_{\tilde{Y}}^{t}(p)=\Phi_{\tilde{X}}^{t}(p)
for all
p
in the support of
\phi
.
Hence, for
|t|<\epsilon
, we have
(\Phi_{\tilde{X}}^{t})^{*}(\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G})=(\Phi_{\tilde{Y}}^{t})^{*}(\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G})
.
Since
\Phi_{\tilde{Y}}^{t}
is a orientation-preserving diffeomorphism
\Omega\to\Omega
, then (see
[
14
, Proposition 16.6]
)

\int_{\Omega}(\Phi_{\tilde{Y}}^{t})^{*}(\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G})=\int_{\Omega}\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}.

It follows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}\int_{\Omega}(\Phi_{\tilde{Y}}^{t})^{*}(\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G})=\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}\int_{\Omega}(\Phi_{\tilde{X}}^{t})^{*}(\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}(\Phi_{\tilde{X}}^{t})^{*}(\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G})=\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{L}_{\tilde{X}}(\phi\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}).

This proves (
17
) and thus the lemma.
∎

If
\tilde{X}\in\Gamma^{1}(T\Omega)
is a
C^{1}
vector field and
f\in C^{1}(\Omega)
, then (see (
16
) and
[
14
, Proposition 12.32]
)

(18)

\operatorname{div}_{G}(f\tilde{X})=df[\tilde{X}]+f\operatorname{div}_{G}(\tilde{X}).

Lemma 2.6
.

The divergence is left invariant, in the sense that,
for all
\tilde{X}\in\Gamma^{1}(TG)
and
a\in G
,

(19)

\operatorname{div}_{G}((L_{a})_{*}\tilde{X})=(\operatorname{div}_{G}\tilde{X})\circ L_{a^{-1}}.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{X}\in\Gamma^{1}(TG)
and
a\in G
.
Then we have:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\phi\cdot\operatorname{div}_{G}((L_{a})_{*}\tilde{X})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}

\displaystyle\overset{L.\penalty\ \ref{lem6771cc90}}{=}-\int_{\Omega}d\phi[(L_{a})_{*}\tilde{X}]\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}d\phi[dL_{a}|_{a^{-1}x}\tilde{X}(a^{-1}x)]\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}d(\phi\circ L_{a})|_{a^{-1}x}[\tilde{X}(a^{-1}x)]\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(x)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq6762c376}}{=}-\int_{a^{-1}\Omega}d(\phi\circ L_{a})|_{y}[\tilde{X}(y)]\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(y)

\displaystyle\overset{L.\penalty\ \ref{lem6771cc90}}{=}\int_{a^{-1}\Omega}(\phi\circ L_{a})(y)\operatorname{div}_{G}(\tilde{X})(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(y)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq6762c376}}{=}\int_{\Omega}(\phi\circ L_{a})(ax)\operatorname{div}_{G}(\tilde{X})(ax)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\phi(x)\operatorname{div}_{G}\tilde{X}(a^{-1}x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}(x).

∎

Lemma 2.7
.

If
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, then the divergence of the left-invariant vector field
\tilde{X}\in\Gamma(TG)
is the constant

(20)

\operatorname{div}_{G}\tilde{X}=-D(\log\mu_{G})[X]=-\mathrm{trace}(\mathrm{ad}_{X}).

In particular,
G
is unimodular if and only if
\operatorname{div}_{G}\tilde{X}=0
for all left-invariant vector fields
\tilde{X}
.

Proof.

If
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, then the flow of the left-invariant vector field
\tilde{X}
is

\Phi^{t}_{\tilde{X}}(g)=g\exp(tX)=R_{\exp(tX)}(g).

From (
12
) and the fact that
\mu_{G}
is a group morphism, it follows that

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}(\Phi^{t}_{\tilde{X}})^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}R_{\exp(tX)}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}\mu_{G}(\exp(tX))^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}=-D\mu_{G}(1_{G})[X]\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_{G}.

So, we have, for every

,

(21)

(\operatorname{div}_{G}\tilde{X})(p)=-D\mu_{G}(1_{G})[X].

.

) becomes

).

Finally, the fact that

follows from the Jacobi Formula for the derivative of the determinant.

2.12.
Sub-Laplacian of a sub-Riemannian Lie group

Let
G
be a sub-Riemannian Lie group,
\Omega\subset G
open and
\mathrm{vol}_{G}
a fixed Haar measure on
G
.
A
sub-Laplacian
on
G
is the operator
\triangle_{G}:C^{2}(\Omega)\to C^{0}(\Omega)
defined by

\triangle_{G}u=\operatorname{div}_{G}(\tilde{\nabla}_{G}u).

for all
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
If
X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
is an orthonormal basis of
V(G)
,
then, by (
4
), (
8
), (
18
), and (
20
),

(23)

\displaystyle\triangle_{G}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\tilde{X}_{i}^{2}u+\tilde{X}_{i}u\operatorname{div}_{G}\tilde{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}D^{2}u[X_{i},X_{i}]-\langle\nabla_{G}u,\nabla_{G}(\log\mu_{G})\rangle_{G}.

where
\mu_{G}
is the modular function.
If
G
is unimodular, then the above formula simplifies to the sum of squares

\triangle_{G}u=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\tilde{X}_{i}^{2}u.

Lemma 2.8
.

The sub-Laplacian is left invariant, in the sense that, for all
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
with

open, and all

,

(24)

\triangle_{G}(u\circ L_{g})=(\triangle_{G}u)\circ L_{g}.

\Omega\subset G
open,

and
g\in G
.
Set
h=g^{-1}
.
From (
10
), we obtain

(25)

\displaystyle(L_{h})_{*}(\tilde{\nabla}_{G}u)(p)

\displaystyle=dL_{h}\circ dL_{h^{-1}p}[(\nabla_{G}u)(h^{-1}p)]

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq67058b31}}{=}dL_{p}[\nabla_{G}(u\circ L_{h^{-1}})(p)]

\displaystyle=\tilde{\nabla}_{G}(u\circ L_{h^{-1}})(p).

Therefore, using the left invariance of the divergence, we obtain

\displaystyle(\triangle_{G}u)\circ L_{g}

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{div}_{G}(\tilde{\nabla}_{G}u))\circ L_{g}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq676300e5}}{=}\operatorname{div}_{G}((L_{h})_{*}\tilde{\nabla}_{G}u)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq676301bc}}{=}\operatorname{div}_{G}(\tilde{\nabla}_{G}(u\circ L_{g}))=\triangle_{G}(u\circ L_{g}).

∎

3.
The computation for conformal submersions

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
and
H
be sub-Riemannian Lie groups, and
\Omega\subset G
open.

F:\Omega\to H
be a
C^{2}
-smooth conformal submersion of factor
\lambda
between sub-Riemannian groups, as in
2.8
.
Define
b:\Omega\to V(H)
as

b(p):=\mathrm{trace}_{G}(D^{2}F(p))-DF(p)[\nabla_{G}(\log\mu_{G})(p)]+\lambda(p)^{2}\nabla_{H}(\log\mu_{H})(F(p)).

where
\mu_{G}
and
\mu_{H}
are the modular functions of
G
and
H
, respectively.

Then,
for all
u\in C^{\infty}(H)
and

,

(26)

\triangle_{G}(u\circ F)(p)=\lambda(p)^{2}\triangle_{H}u(F(p))+\langle b(p),\nabla_{H}u(F(p))\rangle_{H}.

Fix
p\in\Omega
and
u\in C^{2}(H)
.

X_{1},\dots,X_{r}
be an orthonormal basis of
V(G)

such that

\ker(DF(p))|_{V(G)}
.

DF(p)|_{V(G)}:V(G)\to V(H)

\lambda(p)
,

form an orthonormal basis of

by Proposition

.
It follows that

(27)

\sum_{i=1}^{r}D^{2}u(p)[DF(p)X_{i},DF(p)X_{i}]=\lambda(p)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}D^{2}u(p)[Y_{i},Y_{i}].

We can then compute:

\displaystyle\triangle_{G}(u\circ F)(p)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq670d6c55}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{r}D^{2}(u\circ F)(p)[X_{i},X_{i}]-\langle\nabla_{G}(u\circ F)(p),\nabla_{G}(\log\mu_{G})(p)\rangle_{G}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq670d7a23}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{r}D^{2}u(F(p))[DF(p)X_{i},DF(p)X_{i}]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i=1}^{r}Du(F(p))[D^{2}F(p)[X_{i},X_{i}]]-\langle\nabla_{G}(u\circ F)(p),\nabla_{G}(\log\mu_{G})(p)\rangle_{G}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq67683f08}}{=}\lambda(p)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}D^{2}u(p)[Y_{i},Y_{i}]

\displaystyle\qquad+Du(F(p))[\mathrm{trace}(D^{2}F(p))]-\langle\nabla_{G}(u\circ F)(p),\nabla_{G}(\log\mu_{G})(p)\rangle_{G}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq670d6c55}}{=}\lambda(p)^{2}\triangle_{H}u(F(p))

This concludes the proof, because

5.2.
Carnot groups and proof of Theorem
B

A
stratification
of a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a splitting
\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{s}V_{k}
such that
[V_{1},V_{k}]=V_{k+1}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,s-1\}
and
[V_{1},V_{s}]=\{0\}
.
A
Carnot group
is a connected simply connected sub-Riemannian Lie group
G
whose polarization
V(G)\subset\mathfrak{g}
is the first layer of a stratification of
\mathfrak{g}
, the Lie algebra of
G
.
See
[
12
,
2
]
for details.

The important feature of Carnot groups is that they support dilations,
that is, homotheties for all positive factors
\lambda>0
.
Indeed, for each
\lambda>0
, define
\delta_{\lambda}:G\to G
as the Lie group automorphism such that
(\delta_{\lambda})_{*}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra automorphism given by
(\delta_{\lambda})_{*}v=\lambda^{k}v
for all
v\in V_{k}
.

Homotheties between Carnot groups of the same dimension are in fact isomorphisms, see
[
13
]
and the proof of Proposition
5.3
below.
We don’t know a corresponding characterization of conformal submersions, but we
can characterize the pairs of Carnot groups between which there exist a conformal submersion:
the range group must be a quotient of the domain.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
G
and
H
be sub-Riemannian Carnot groups,
\Omega\subset G
open.
There exists a conformal submersion
F:\Omega\to H
of class
C^{1}
, if and only if there exists a Carnot morphism
F_{0}:G\to H
that is a conformal submersion.
In particular,
H
is a quotient of
G
by a dilation invariant normal subgroup.

If
\dim(G)=\dim(H)
and if
F
is a conformal submersion of constant factor
\lambda>0
, then, up to precomposing with a left translation,
F
itself is an isomorphism of Carnot groups.

Proof.

Suppose we have a
C^{1}
conformal submersion
F:\Omega\to H
of factor
\lambda:\Omega\to(0,+\infty)
.
Since
F
is a contact map of class
C^{1}
, it is Pansu differentiable at every point,
see for instance
[
2
]
.
The Pansu differential of
F
at a point
p
is the Lie group morphism
F_{0}:G\to H
such that

DF_{0}(1_{G})[v]=DF(p)[v]

for all
v\in V_{1}(G)
.
In particular,
F_{0}
is also a conformal submersion of factor
\lambda(p)
.

If
\dim(G)=\dim(H)
and if
F
is a conformal submersion of constant factor
\lambda>0
, then
\delta^{H}_{1/\lambda}\circ F:\Omega\to H
is an isometry, and thus it is the restriction of an isomorphism of Carnot groups by
[
13
]
.
∎

The case when
G=H
, we have the following characterization of homotheties and isometries in terms of the sub-Laplacian.

If

5.3.
Symplectic preparation to the Heisenberg group

The following discussion comes from
[
15
, Section 2.4]
.

Let
V
be a vector space of dimension
2n
.
A
symplectic form on
V
is a bilinear map
\omega:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
that is alternating and non-singular.
For a symplectic form
\omega
on
V
and a scalar product
g
on
V
,
define the operator
A_{\omega,g}\in\mathrm{GL}(V)
by

\omega(v,w)=g(v,A_{\omega,g}w)\qquad\forall v,w\in V.

The
symplectic spectrum of
g
with respect to
\omega
is the
n
-tuple

\vec{r}_{\omega}(g)=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})\in(0,+\infty)^{n}

such that
r_{1}\leq r_{2}\leq\dots\leq r_{n}
and such that
-r_{1}^{4},\dots,-r_{n}^{4}
are the eigenvalues of the operator
A_{\omega,g}^{2}
.

The symplectic spectrum is well defined, because
A_{\omega,g}^{2}
has at most
n
distinct eigenvalues and they are negative.
Indeed, notice that
A_{\omega,g}
is
g
-antisymmetric, that is,

g(v,A_{\omega,g}w)=-g(A_{\omega,g}v,w)
.
It follows that the eigenvalues of
A_{\omega,g}
are purely imaginary and thus

A_{\omega,g}^{2}
is
g
-symmetric, that is,

g(A_{\omega,g}^{2}v,w)=g(v,A_{\omega,g}^{2}w)
,
and it has negative eigenvalues.

It is a standard fact that any pair of symplectic forms
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
on
V
are equivalent, that is, there exists
\Psi\in\mathrm{GL}(V)
such that
\omega_{2}=\Psi^{*}\omega_{1}
,
see for instance
[
15
, Exercise 2.1.15]
.
The next lemma gives a complete description of equivalent classes of pairs
(\omega,g)
consisting of a symplectic form and scalar product.

Lemma 5.5

(
[
15
, Lemma 2.4.6]
)
.

Let
V
be a vector space,
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}
symplectic forms on
V
and
g_{1},g_{2}
scalar products on
V
.
The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

there exists a linear isomorphism
\Psi\in\mathrm{GL}(V)
such that
\omega_{2}=\Psi^{*}\omega_{1}
and
g_{2}=\Psi^{*}g_{1}
;

(ii)

\vec{r}_{\omega_{1}}(g_{1})=\vec{r}_{\omega_{2}}(g_{2})
.

Proof.

\ref{lem6768583e_1}\Rightarrow\ref{lem6768583e_2}
:
Set
A_{j}:=A_{\omega_{j},g_{j}}
.
Then, for all
v,w\in V
,

\displaystyle g_{1}(\Psi v,\Psi A_{2}w)

\displaystyle\overset{g_{2}=\Psi^{*}g_{1}}{=}g_{2}(v,A_{2}w)=\omega_{2}(v,w)

\displaystyle\overset{\omega_{2}=\Psi^{*}\omega_{1}}{=}\omega_{1}(\Psi v,\Psi w)=g_{1}(\Psi v,A_{1}\Psi w).

It follows that
\Psi A_{2}w=A_{1}\Psi w
for all
w\in V
, i.e.,
A_{2}=\Psi^{-1}A_{1}\Psi
.
Therefore,
A_{1}^{2}
and
A_{2}^{2}
have the same spectrum and thus
\vec{r}_{\omega_{1}}(g_{1})=\vec{r}_{\omega_{2}}(g_{2})
.

\ref{lem6768583e_2}\Rightarrow\ref{lem6768583e_1}
:
The statement follows from the following general observation.
If
\omega
is a symplectic form,
g
a scalar product and
A=A_{\omega,g}
,
then, since
A
is
g
-skew-symmetric, there exists a basis
\mathscr{B}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
of
V
that is orthonormal for
g
such that

AX_{i}=Y_{i}

and
AY_{i}=-r_{i}^{2}X_{i}
.
On this basis,
\omega
is determined by the relations
\omega(v,w)=g(v,Aw)
for
v,w\in\mathscr{B}
.
Therefore, in the hypothesis of
(ii)
, if we take this type of bases for both pairs
(\omega_{j},g_{j})
, then map that exchanges the bases is the required
\Psi
.
∎

5.4.
Example: Heisenberg groups

For a vector space
V
of dimension
2n
and a symplectic form
\omega
on
V
, define
\mathfrak{h}(\omega)
as the vector space
V\times\mathbb{R}
with Lie brackets

[(v,s),(w,t)]=(0,\omega(v,w)).

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}(\omega)
is a step-two stratified Lie algebra with layers
V_{1}=V
and
V_{2}=\mathbb{R}
.

We denote by
\mathbb{H}(\omega)
the corresponding connected and simply connected Lie group.
Using nilpotency and the BCH formula, we describe
\mathbb{H}(\omega)
as the manifold
V\times\mathbb{R}
with group operation

(v,s)*(w,t)=\left(v+w,s+t+\frac{1}{2}\omega(v,w)\right).

A linear map
\hat{\Psi}:\mathfrak{h}(\omega_{2})\to\mathfrak{h}(\omega_{1})
is an isomorphism of stratified Lie algebras if and only if there are
\Psi\in\mathrm{GL}(V)
and
\zeta_{\Psi}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

such that
\hat{\Psi}(v,t)=(\Psi v,\zeta_{\Psi}t)
, and

\Psi^{*}\omega_{1}=\zeta_{\Psi}\omega_{2}
.
Since symplectic forms are equivalent, every two
\mathfrak{h}(\omega_{2})
and
\mathfrak{h}(\omega_{1})
of same dimension are isomorphic as stratified Lie algebras for every pair of symplectic forms
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
.

Given a symplectic form
\omega
and a scalar product
g
on
V
, we define the
Heisenberg group

\mathbb{H}(\omega,g)
as the Carnot group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}(\omega)
, polarization
V
and scalar product
g
on
V
.

Theorem 5.6
.

Two Heisenberg groups
\mathbb{H}(\omega_{1},g_{1})

\mathbb{H}(\omega_{2},g_{2})

and

\triangle_{\mathbb{H}(\omega_{2},g_{2})}(u\circ F)=\lambda^{2}(\triangle_{\mathbb{H}(\omega_{1},g_{1})}u)\circ F,\qquad\forall u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{H}(\omega_{1},g_{1})),

if and only if (
37

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbb{H}(\omega_{1},g_{1})
and
\mathbb{H}(\omega_{2},g_{2})
are isometric.
Recall from
[
11
]
that isometries of nilpotent Lie groups are affine.
Thus, there is
\Psi\in\mathrm{GL}(V)
such that
\Psi^{*}g_{1}=g_{2}
,
\Psi^{*}\omega_{1}=\zeta_{\Psi}\omega_{2}
for some
\zeta_{\Psi}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.
By Lemma
5.5

\vec{r}_{\omega_{1}}(g_{1})=\vec{r}_{\zeta_{\Psi}\omega_{2}}(g_{2}).

It is an easy observation that, following the notation from Section
5.3
,

A_{\zeta_{\Psi}\omega_{2},g_{2}}=\zeta_{\Psi}A_{\omega_{2},g_{2}}
.
It follows that

\vec{r}_{\zeta_{\Psi}\omega_{2}}(g_{2})=\sqrt{|\zeta_{\Psi}|}\vec{r}_{\omega_{2}}(g_{2}),

which shows (
37
) with
\rho=\sqrt{|\zeta_{\Psi}|}>0
.

Suppose now that (
37
) holds.
We apply Lemma
5.5
again to obtain
\Psi\in\mathrm{GL}(V)
such that

\Psi^{*}g_{1}=g_{2}
and
\Psi^{*}\omega_{1}=\rho^{2}\omega_{2}
.
Then the map
\hat{\Psi}(v,t)=(\Psi v,\rho^{2}t)
is a Lie algebra automorphism
\mathfrak{h}(\omega_{2})\to\mathfrak{h}(\omega_{1})
which defines a Lie group automorphism
\mathbb{H}(\omega_{2},g_{2})\to\mathbb{H}(\omega_{1},g_{1})
that is an isometry.

The last part of the statement follows directly from Theorem
B
.
∎

5.5.
Example: Heisenberg groups in coordinates

It might be interesting to have a representation in coordinates of the Heisenberg groups and their sub-Laplacians.
Consider a basis
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
of a vector space
V
and define the symplectic form
\omega
by setting
\omega(X_{i},Y_{i})=1=-\omega(Y_{i},X_{i})
for all
i
, and zero otherwise.
For
\bar{r}\in(0,+\infty)^{n}
with

0<r_{1}\leq r_{2}\leq\dots\leq r_{n}<\infty
, define the scalar product
g_{\bar{r}}
such that

X_{1},\dots,X_{n},Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
is an orthogonal basis with

g_{\bar{r}}(X_{i},X_{i})=g_{\bar{r}}(Y_{i},Y_{i})=\frac{1}{r_{i}^{2}}.

Then, one can easily check that
\vec{r}_{\omega}(g_{\bar{r}})=\bar{r}
.
By Theorem
5.6
, if we assume
r_{1}=1
, then different vectors
\bar{r}
give non-isometric Carnot structures.

Using the BCH formula, we can reconstruct the Lie group
\mathbb{H}(\omega)
as
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
with group operation

(x,y,z)*(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})=\left(x+\bar{x},y+\bar{y},z+\bar{z}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}\bar{y}_{i}-\bar{x}_{i}y_{i}\right)\right),

where
x,y,\bar{x},\bar{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
z,\bar{z}\in\mathbb{R}
.

The left invariant vector fields are

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}-\frac{y_{i}}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z},

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}+\frac{x_{i}}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial z},

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}.

An orthonormal basis of
V
for
g_{\bar{r}}
is

\displaystyle\Xi_{i}

\displaystyle=r_{i}X_{i}\qquad\text{ if }i\in\{1,\dots,n\},

\displaystyle\Xi_{n+i}

\displaystyle=r_{i}Y_{i}\qquad\text{ if }i\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

We can express the sub-Laplacian
\triangle_{\bar{r}}
of
\mathbb{H}(\omega,g_{\bar{r}})
with formula (
23
) as

\displaystyle\triangle_{\bar{r}}=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\tilde{\Xi}_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}(\tilde{X}_{i}^{2}+\tilde{Y}_{i}^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y_{i}^{2}}+\frac{x_{i}^{2}+y_{i}^{y}}{4}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}+\left(x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}-y_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\right)\right).

In the latter expression, different choices of
\bar{r}
with
r_{1}=1
, give non-equivalent sub-Laplacians on
\mathbb{H}(\omega)\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
.
We have thus a simple explanation of the phenomena described in
[
1
, §16.3.1]
.
