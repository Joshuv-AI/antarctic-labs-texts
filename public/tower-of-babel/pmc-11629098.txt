Multiomics reveals tumor microenvironment remodeling in locally advanced gastric and gastroesophageal junction cancer following neoadjuvant immunotherapy and chemotherapy
Authors: Zhi Ji, Xia Wang, Jiaqi Xin, Lijun Ma, Duo Zuo, Hongli Li, Lan Su, Xinze Lv, Shaohua Ge, Le Zhang, Yong Liu, Yanhui Zhang, Tingting Ding, Ting Deng, Yi Ba, Rui Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerioperative chemotherapy is the standard of care for patients with locally advanced gastric and gastroesophageal junction cancer. Recent evidence demonstrated the addition of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitors enhanced therapeutic efficacy. However, the mechanisms of response and resistance remain largely undefined. A detailed multiomic investigation is essential to elucidate these mechanisms.MethodsWe performed whole-exome sequencing, whole-transcriptome sequencing, multiplex immunofluorescence and single-cell RNA sequencing on matched pretreatment and post-treatment samples from 30 patients enrolled in an investigator-initiated Phase 2 clinical trial (NCT04908566). All patients received neoadjuvant PD-1 inhibitors in combination with chemotherapy. A major pathologic response (MPR) was defined as the presence of no more than 10% residual viable tumor cells following treatment.ResultsBefore treatment, the positive ratio of CD3+T cells in both the tumor parenchyma and stroma was significantly higher in the non-MPR group compared with the MPR group (p=0.042 and p=0.013, respectively). Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was employed for feature gene selection and 13 genes were ultimately used to construct a predictive model for identifying MPR after surgery. The model exhibited a perfect area under curve (AUC) of 1.000 (95% CI: 1.000 to 1.000, p<0.001). Post-treatment analysis revealed a significant increase in CD3+T cells, CD8+T cells and NK cells in the tumor stroma of MPR patients. In the tumor parenchyma, aside from a marked increase in CD8+T cells and NK cells, a notable reduction in macrophage was also observed (all p<0.05). Importantly, forkheadbox protein 3 (FOXP3), the principal marker for regulatory T cells (Treg) cells, showed a significant decrease during treatment in MPR patients. FOXP3 expression in the non-MPR group was significantly higher than in the MPR group (p=0.0056) after treatment. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing analysis confirmed that nearly all Treg cells were derived from the non-MPR group.ConclusionsOur study highlights the critical role of dynamic changes within the tumor immune microenvironment in predicting the efficacy of neoadjuvant combined immunochemotherapy. We examined the disparities between MPR/non-MPR groups, shedding light on potential mechanisms of immune response and suppression. In addition to bolstering cytotoxic immune responses, specifically targeting Treg cells may be crucial for enhancing treatment outcomes.Keywords: Gastric Cancer, Neoadjuvant, Immunotherapy, Tumor microenvironment - TME

Key messages

The addition of immunotherapy to perioperative chemotherapy enhances therapeutic efficacy, but the mechanisms of response and resistance remain unclear. We performed multiomic investigation on matched pretreatment and post-treatment tissue samples from 30 patients with locally advanced gastric and gastroesophageal junction cancer. Our study highlights the critical role of dynamic changes within the tumor immune microenvironment in predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy. In addition to bolstering cytotoxic immune responses, specifically targeting regulatory T cell cells may be crucial for enhancing treatment outcomes.

Introduction

Gastric and gastroesophageal junction (G/GEJ) cancer is the fifth most common cancer and the fourth most lethal cancer worldwide.1 G/GEJ adenocarcinoma accounts for 95% of G/GEJ cancers. For patients with locally advanced G/GEJ adenocarcinoma, perioperative chemotherapy combined with surgical resection is recommended. This combined treatment approach maximizes systemic therapy while reducing the stage of locally advanced tumors and improving the eradication of residual disease and prevention of metastasis.2,4

In the FLOT4 study, patients were randomized to receive either the FLOT regimen (5-fluorouracil, oxaliplatin, and docetaxel) or the ECF/ECX regimen (epirubicin, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil/capecitabine) as perioperative treatment. The results suggest that the FLOT regimen can improve the R0 resection rate (78% vs 85%) and pathological complete response rate (pCR, 6% vs 16%). In terms of long-term prognosis, the FLOT regimen led to significant improvements in overall survival (OS, 35 vs 50 months) and disease-free survival (DFS, 18 vs 30 months).2 Moreover, the RESOLVE study conducted in China showed that the SOX (S1 and oxaliplatin) regimen during the perioperative period improved 3-year DFS compared with the XELOX (capecitabine and oxaliplatin) regimen as adjuvant therapy (62.02% vs 54.78%).5 Therefore, perioperative chemotherapy for G/GEJ adenocarcinoma can increase the R0 resection rate, reduce recurrence, and improve OS. However, the prognosis of patients with locally advanced G/GEJ cancer remains unsatisfactory.

In the past decade, immune checkpoint inhibitors that target programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) have been successful in the treatment of malignant tumors. Several Phase 3 clinical studies have confirmed the significant efficacy of chemotherapy combined with a PD-1 inhibitor in patients with advanced or metastatic G/GEJ cancer, and this regimen is also recommended as the standard first-line treatment.6,10 Phase 2 trials have also reported favorable antitumor activity with PD-1 inhibitors plus chemotherapy in patients with locally advanced G/GEJ cancer.11,14 The interim analysis of KEYNOTE-585, a Phase 3 trial aiming to explore the antitumor activity of neoadjuvant and adjuvant pembrolizumab (a PD-1 inhibitor) plus chemotherapy in patients with locally advanced G/GEJ adenocarcinoma, showed that perioperative pembrolizumab vs placebo improved the pCR rate (12.9% vs 2.0%, p<0.00001).15 Tumor regression after neoadjuvant therapy was significantly correlated with DFS and OS.16 The results of a study of 551 patients with G/GEJ cancer undergoing neoadjuvant treatment showed that, compared with patients with a tumor regression grade of 2 or 3, patients with a major pathological response (MPR) had a significant improvement in the 3-year OS rate (70.9% vs 48.8%).17 Overall, these studies demonstrated the efficacy of the addition of a PD-1 inhibitor for the treatment of locally advanced G/GEJ cancer, but the improvement in the pCR rate is very limited. Thus, patients who would benefit from PD-1 inhibitors combined with chemotherapy urgently need to be identified. However, biomarkers remain unclear and controversial. Moreover, the tumor immune microenvironment of locally advanced G/GEJ cancer and the effects of PD-1 inhibitors combined with chemotherapy are not fully understood. Therefore, comprehensive data analysis is needed to determine the underlying reasons and guide interventions to improve treatment efficacy.

In the present study, we integrated multiomics data obtained from whole-exome sequencing (WES), whole-transcriptome sequencing (WTS), multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) analysis and single-cell RNA sequencing. By comparing the genome and tumor microenvironment of MPR and non-MPR patients, we screened for effective predictive biomarkers of the response to neoadjuvant PD-1 inhibitors combined with chemotherapy. Moreover, we characterized the changes in the tumor genome and tumor microenvironment in patients before and after treatment to elucidate potential mechanisms of response and resistance.

Materials and methods

Data collection

This study included patients with locally advanced G/GEJ cancer from a Phase 2 clinical trial (NCT04908566) who had available and adequate primary tumor tissue samples for multiomics molecular analysis before and after neoadjuvant therapy. Tumor, node, metastases staging was determined by ultrasound gastroscopy and CT scan before treatment. The patients received four cycles of SOX combined with a PD-1 inhibitor, then underwent radical surgical resection and continued four cycles of SOX combined with a PD-1 inhibitor after surgery. The treatment outcome was assessed based on the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.1 criteria by two independent pathologists. MPR is defined as ≤10% viable tumor cells in the resected primary tumor, excluding resected lymph nodes.

We collected tumor biopsy tissues from patients before treatment for WES and WTS to profile gene expression. Furthermore, we conducted mIHC to analyze the tumor microenvironment. After radical surgical resection, surgical tissues were used to assess the MPR status, WTS was used to detect gene expression profiles, and mIHC analysis and single-cell RNA sequencing were used to analyze the tumor microenvironment and cellular heterogeneity (figure 1). The method for accessing the datasets is detailed in the Data Availability Statement.

WES and data analysis

Tissue DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s standard protocol. Fragments between 200 and 400 bp from the sheared tissue DNA were purified (Agencourt AMPure XP Kit, Beckman Coulter, California, USA), hybridized with capture probes baits, selected with magnetic beads, and amplified. Targeted sequencing of the whole-exome region was performed using capture-based methods.18,20 The quality and the size of the fragments were assessed by high sensitivity DNA kit using Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, California, USA). Indexed samples were sequenced on NextSeq 500 (Illumina, California, USA) with paired-end reads and an average sequencing depth of 1,000× for tissue samples.

Variant calling and annotation were performed using standardized pipelines following previously established methods (Burning Rock Biotech, Guangzhou, China).18 Tumor mutation burden (TMB) per patient was computed as a ratio between the total number of non-synonymous mutations detected and the total coding region size. The mutation count included non-synonymous single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels detected within the coding region and ±2 bp upstream or downstream region.

Copy number variations (CNVs) were analyzed based on the depth of coverage data of capture intervals. Coverage data were corrected against sequencing bias resulting from guanine cytosine (GC) content and probe design. The average coverage of all captured regions was used to normalize the coverage of different samples to comparable scales. The copy number was calculated based on the ratio between the depth of coverage in tumor samples and the average coverage of an adequate number (n>50) of samples without CNVs as references per capture interval. CNV is called if the coverage data of the gene region was quantitatively and statistically significant from its reference control. The limit of detection for CNVs is 1.5 for copy number deletion and 2.64 for copy number amplifications. Finally, amplifications or deletions at the genomic arm level were computed using GISTIC2.0 software.21

WTS and data analysis

The AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used to extract RNA from the FFPE tumor tissues. RNA quantity was assessed with a Qubit RNA HS assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Simultaneously, the LabChip GXII touch 24 system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) facilitated the evaluation of RNA quality. The criteria for RNA to be deemed suitable for library preparation included a minimum concentration of 4 ng/µL and a DV200 value of at least 40%. Following extraction, the raw sequencing reads were subjected to adapter trimming with Trimmomatic software. FastQC provided a thorough quality check of the reads. Subsequently, the cleaned reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh37 using STAR software and organized using SAMtools software.22 Read quantification was performed with HTSeq, resulting in detailed gene expression profiles.

Raw read counts were processed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using the “DESeq2” R package. Genes were considered upregulated or downregulated based on an adjusted p value<0.05, with a log2 (fold change) threshold >1 for upregulation and <−1 for downregulation.

For pathway enrichment analysis, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were analyzed using the “clusterProfiler” R package. The false discovery rate method was applied to adjust p values, with pathways considered significant if they had an adjusted p value<0.05. The enrichment results were visualized using bubble plots to illustrate key pathway changes.

mIHC assay and image analysis

Multispectral imaging, following mIHC staining protocols, was employed to detect tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Five micrometer-thick sections were prepared from FFPE tumor tissues. According to the instructions of the PANO 7-plex IHC Kit (Panovue, Beijing, China), these sections were subjected to epitope retrieval, endogenous peroxidase and protein blocking, antigen labeling, and signal amplification via tyramide (TSA). Two antibody panels were used to analyze the tumor microenvironment: the first panel targeted programmed cell death protein ligand 1 (PD-L1), PD-1, CD3, CD8, and Pan-CK; the second panel consisted of antibodies against CD68, CD163, CD56, and Pan-CK. After antigen labeling, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-labeled secondary antibodies at 37°C for 10 min. Each TSA development step involved microwave heat treatment. After labeling, the sections were counterstained for nuclei with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). The Mantra Quantitative Pathology Imaging System (PerkinElmer, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) was then used to acquire multispectral scans of the stained slides, and fluorescent spectra were collected at 20 nm intervals from 420 to 720 nm. These spectra were integrated into composite images. To construct a spectral library for unmixing, images from unstained and individually stained sections were analyzed to differentiate tissue autofluorescence from each fluorophore. Images were analyzed and quantified using the inForm software (PerkinElmer, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The quantities of different immune cell types within the tumor parenchyma and stroma were evaluated separately. The positive ratio was defined as the proportion of positively stained cells among all cells, while the density referred to the number of positive cells per unit area. The immune cell types evaluated included CD3+T cells, CD8+T cells, M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages, and natural killer (NK) cells. The markers associated with these immune cells include PD-1+, PD-L1+, CD3+, CD8+, CD56+, CD163+, and CD68+.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and data analysis

Isolating single cells

The surgically excised tissue samples were stored in MACS Tissue Storage Solution (Miltenyi Biotec) and sent to the laboratory (GeneChem, Shanghai, China) for sample preprocessing and analysis within 24 hours. The samples were washed with phosphate-buffered saline and minced on ice into small fragments (~1 mm³). Next, the tissue fragments were enzymatically digested in a solution containing 1 mg/mL collagenase I (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), 1 mg/mL collagenase II, 60 U/mL hyaluronidase (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 10 U/mL lyase, and 0.02 mg/mL DNase I (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) for 90 min at 37°C under constant agitation. Post-digestion, the cell suspensions were passed through 100 µm and 40 µm cell strainers and centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (Gibco) to remove any remaining erythrocytes. Following multiple washes with dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline containing sample buffer, the cells were stained, counted, and prepared for downstream single-cell RNA sequencing.

Single-cell RNA sequencing capture and library preparation

The BD Rhapsody system (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA) was employed to capture transcriptomic information from single cells. Individual cells were distributed across more than 200,000 microwells using a limited dilution approach. Saturating oligonucleotide barcoded beads were added, ensuring each microwell contained one bead paired with a single cell. Following cell lysis, the messenger RNA (mRNA) molecules hybridized to barcoded capture oligonucleotides on the beads. The beads were then collected into a single tube for reverse transcription and ExoI digestion. During complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, each cDNA molecule was tagged on the 5’ end (ie, the 3’ end of an mRNA transcript) with a unique molecular identifier and cell barcode indicating its cell of origin. Whole-transcriptome libraries were prepared using the BD Rhapsody single-cell whole-transcriptome amplification (WTA) workflow, which included random priming and extension (RPE), followed by RPE amplification PCR and WTA index PCR. The libraries were quantified using High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA) and High Sensitivity D1000 Reagents (Agilent Technologies) on a 4150 TapeStation System (Agilent Technologies) and a Qubit High Sensitivity DNA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Sequencing was subsequently performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, California, USA) on a 150 bp paired-end run.

Data alignment, quality control

Alignment and quantification of the 10x Genomics sequencing data were performed using the Cell Ranger pipeline (V.7.0.0, 10x Genomics, Pleasanton, California, USA), with GRCh38 as the reference genome. Scanpy (V.1.8.2) was subsequently employed to process the filtered_feature_bc_matrix for downstream analysis.23 Scrublet24 was applied to identify and exclude potential cell doublets. Cells expressing fewer than 200 genes, as well as genes detected in fewer than three cells, were removed. Cells with over 3,000 RNA counts or more than 10% mitochondrial reads were also filtered out. Following quality control, the data were normalized to 10,000 reads per cell, followed by log transformation via scanpy.pp.log1p(). Highly variable genes were selected with scanpy.pp.highly_variable_genes(), and PCA with 50 components was performed using scanpy.tl.pca to refine the data, mitigating batch effects across samples using the harmony integration function.25

Dimensionality reduction, clustering, and annotation

UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) was employed for data visualization with default settings. Cell clustering was conducted using the Louvain algorithm, which grouped cells according to their gene expression profiles. Cell type annotation was performed by leveraging lineage-specific markers, distinguishing T and NK cells based on their respective marker expression patterns. B cells and plasma B cells were similarly distinguished according to their distinct marker profiles. Epithelial, fibroblast, endothelial, and myeloid cells populations were identified based on their canonical gene expression signatures. T cells were further annotated into subcellular forms, namely, CD8 resting memory cells (CD8_Trm), CD8 effector memory T cells (CD8_Tem), CD8-activated T cells (CD8_Tac), CD4 resting memory T cells (CD4_Trm), CD4 central memory T cells (CD4_Tcm), CD4-exhausted T cells (CD4_Tex) and regulatory T cells (Tregs).

Differential gene expression and pseudotime analysis

The cellular identities of each cluster were established using the CellTypist tool (Teichmann Lab, UK), which assigns cell types based on characteristic marker gene expression.26 These predictions were subsequently employed to label the cell subsets across the clusters. Differential gene expression across individual clusters was statistically compared with all other cells using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. To further investigate DEGs in each cell type group, the functions scanpy.tl.rank_genes_groups and sc.tl.filter_rank_genes_groups were used with their default parameters. Thresholds were applied (min_in_group_fraction=0.25, max_out_group_fraction=0.5, and min_fold_change=0.5) to refine DEG identification. The top-ranked genes per cluster were identified, and an adjusted p value of <0.05 was considered to indicate significance. Additionally, pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted on T-cell subtypes using the partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) tool, which inferred connections between cells based on the single-cell topology.27

Enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted to compare the MPR and non-MPR groups. The analyses were performed using the ClusterProfiler package (V.3.18.1).28 In particular, cell-type-specific enrichment analyses were carried out for B cells, T cells, NK cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, and mast cells using the Scanpy.queries.enrich function (V.1.8.2).

Gene set variation analysis

To identify prominent pathways and cellular processes across different clusters, we employed gene set variation analysis (GSVA).29 The GSVA was executed using the corresponding R package, using a matrix of cluster-averaged log-transformed gene expression data.

ROC curve analysis

We used the pROC package to conduct ROC analysis, focusing on continuous variable markers such as the CD3+cell ratio, PD-1 expression, and TMB. The enrichment distribution of these markers was compared between the MPR and non-MPR groups, and the area under curve (AUC) values and predictive accuracy were calculated.

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis selected the 13 most predictive genes from the DEGs for the risk prediction model. The LASSO feature score is calculated as follows: LASSO feature score = (TSLP expression* 0.3442) + (CCL4L2 expression* 0.0256) + (CALML5 expression* 0.078) + (CYP3A4 expression* −0.0157) + (GRIN2A expression* −0.0277) + (CAMK2B expression* −0.2068) + (ADRB3 expression* −3.6284) + (WNT10A expression* −0.0862) + (SOST expression* −4.165) + (SFRP5 expression* −0.2707) + (RAC3 expression* −0.0893) + (APCDD1L expression* −0.198) + (CYP2D6 expression * −0.236).

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluations were conducted using the R programming environment (V.4.2.3). For categorical variables, group discrepancies were assessed either with the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test when applicable. Continuous variables across two or more groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study cohort comprised 30 patients diagnosed with locally advanced G/GEJ cancer, all of whom were enrolled in the investigator-initiated Phase 2 clinical trial (NCT04908566). Of them, 16 patients achieved an MPR following neoadjuvant treatment, while the remaining patients did not. The baseline characteristics of the patients are provided in table 1. Patient ages ranged from 48 to 70 years with a male predominance of 83.3%. Tumors were predominantly situated in the lower regions of the stomach, with the antrum being the most frequent site. Most tumors exhibited poor or poor-moderate differentiation. The cohort mostly consisted of adenocarcinomas, with a subset exhibiting signet-ring cell carcinoma. All patients completed the neoadjuvant therapy regimen and subsequently underwent radical surgical resection.

Standard clinical assessments, including age, gender, body mass index, tumor location, differentiation grade, and clinical stage, showed no significant differences between the MPR and non-MPR groups. All patients had microsatellite stable (MSS) tumors, and only one patient testing positive for EBV. Among the 19 patients who underwent human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) immunohistochemistry, none of the MPR group patients (n=5) exhibited HER2-positivity; In contrast, of the non-MPR group (n=14), 28.6% were 2+HER2-positive, 21.4% were 1+HER2-positive, and 50.0% were HER2-negative.

Differential tumor microenvironment features predict postoperative pathological response

We compared the genomic variation characteristics of tumor specimens from MPR and non-MPR patients before treatment (online supplemental figure S1A). First, there was no significant difference in the median TMB between the two groups (19.69 vs 20.19, p=1.000; online supplemental figure S1B). Second, mutations in the BLM gene were observed at a significantly higher frequency in MPR patients compared with non-MPR patients (36% vs 0%, p=0.045; online supplemental figure S1C). The BLM gene is crucial for maintaining DNA repair and genomic stability, processes that are directly linked to tumorigenesis and tumor progression. No other high-frequency mutated genes displayed significant differences in mutation frequencies between the two groups. Finally, we used GISTIC2.0 software to evaluate amplifications and deletions in the genome. The results showed a high degree of concordance between the MPR and non-MPR groups (online supplemental figure S1D).

mIHC staining was subsequently used to assess differences in the immune cells’ positive ratio between the groups. These immune cells included M1/M2 macrophages, CD3+T cells, PD-L1+cells, PD-1+cells, CD3+PD-1+ T cells, CD8+T cells, CD8+PD-1+ T cells and NK cells (ranked by the positive ratio of immune cell from high to low). The positive ratio of CD3+T cells in the tumor parenchyma and stroma was greater in the non-MPR group than in the MPR group (p=0.042 and p=0.013, respectively; figure 2A). The CD3+cell density results show a consistent trend where the absolute number of CD3+cells is higher in both the tumor parenchyma and stroma in the non-MPR group compared with the MPR group (p=0.09 and p=0.029, respectively; figure 2B). No significant changes were detected in other immune cell populations. The AUC for predicting the response of these tumors based on the positive ratio of CD3+cells in pretreatment samples was 0.765 (95% CI: 0.582 to 0.949) (p=0.008, figure 2F).

Transcriptomic analysis identified 856 DEGs between the MPR and non-MPR groups, with 529 genes upregulated and 327 genes downregulated in the MPR group (figure 2C, online supplemental table S2). In MPR patients, gene upregulation was observed in pathways involved in tumor cell proliferation, differentiation, and metastasis, including the calcium signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, and steroid metabolism pathway. Furthermore, genes involved in the cytochrome P450 pathway, which is closely associated with hormone synthesis and drug metabolism, also exhibited increased expression in the MPR group (figure 2D). Conversely, in non-MPR tumors, upregulated genes were significantly enriched in immune-related pathways. These pathways encompassed cytokine‒cytokine receptor interaction, chemokine signaling, interleukin-17, toll-like receptor, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and C-type lectin receptor pathways (figure 2E). These pathways are involved in the activation and regulation of the immune system, including the actions of cytokines and chemokines, as well as signal transduction through specific cell surface receptors. Additionally, no significant difference in baseline tumor purity was observed between the two groups, indicating that the observed enrichment differences were not driven by changes in the proportion of tumor cells (online supplemental figure S1E).

LASSO regression was employed to select the key feature genes from the pool of 856 DEGs, ultimately selecting 13 genes to construct a predictive model aimed at identifying patients who achieved MPR following surgery. The selected genes included TSLP, CCL4L2, CALML5, CYP3A4, GRIN2A, CAMK2B, ADRB3, WNT10A, SOST, SFRP5, RAC3, APCDD1L, and CYP2D6. The predictive model demonstrated an AUC of 1.000 (95% CI: 1.000 to 1.000, p<0.001; figure 2F).

Increased cytotoxic T/NK cells and decreased FOXP3 gene expression post-treatment correlated positively with pathological response

An assessment of immune microenvironment changes before and after treatment revealed more pronounced alterations in the positive ratio of immune cells in MPR patients compared with non-MPR patients. After treatment, a significant increase in the positive ratio of CD3+T cells, CD8+T cells, and NK cells was observed in the tumor stroma of MPR patients. In addition to the significant increase in the positive ratio of CD8+T cells and NK cells in the tumor parenchyma, a notable decrease in the positive ratio of M1 macrophages was also observed (all p<0.05; figure 3A,B). Similarly, a significant reduction in the number of M1 macrophages was observed in the tumor microenvironment of non-MPR patients (p=0.0017 in the tumor stroma, p=0.024 in the tumor parenchyma; figure 3A,B). A similar trend was observed for M2 macrophages in the tumor parenchyma (p=0.0025; figure 3A,B). While there was a significant upward trend in the positive ratio of CD8+T cells within the stroma, the changes observed in the tumor parenchyma did not reach statistical significance. After treatment, the density and positive ratio of CD8+T cells increased in the MPR group, ranking below CD3+T cells and M1 macrophages in total cell count. Additionally, both the density and positive ratio of various infiltrating immune cells in the MPR group’s immune microenvironment exceeded those in the non-MPR group, particularly within the tumor stroma. Although these differences did not reach statistical significance, they were in stark contrast to the pretreatment trends (online supplemental figure S2A).

In summary, although both groups showed an overall increase in immune cell infiltration post-treatment, the immune cells in the non-MPR group clearly failed to exert effective antitumor activity. To further assess the cytotoxic activity of NK cells and T cells on tumors, we compared the expression of cytotoxic molecules between the MPR and non-MPR groups. Notably, in MPR patients, post-treatment expression of CD8A (p=0.025), granzyme K (GZMK) (p=0.042), and granzyme H (GZMH) (p=0.02) was significantly elevated (figure 4A). These changes were not observed in the non-MPR group.

Given that tumor cells may employ various immunosuppressive mechanisms to evade the immune response during treatment, we also compared the changes in gene expression associated with the inhibition of antitumor immune responses before and after treatment. We evaluated PD-1 and PD-L1 expression levels using mIHC data and found no significant differences. Other immune checkpoint molecules, such as cytotoxic T lymphocyte associate protein 4 (CTLA-4), hepatitis A virus cellular receptor 2 (HAVCR2), lymphocyte activating 3 (LAG3), and T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), showed no significant upregulation either, as assessed by WTS data (online supplemental figure S2C,D). Following this, we assessed chemokine receptor expression and observed a substantial downregulation of receptors from the CCR and CXCR families in non-MPR patients post-treatment (figure 4B). This reduction in chemokine receptor expression typically correlates with diminished immune activity and decreased immune cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment, which may adversely affect therapeutic outcomes and patient prognosis. Notably, FOXP3, a key marker of Tregs, displayed a marked reduction in expression throughout the course of treatment in the MPR cohort. By the end of treatment, FOXP3 expression in the non-MPR group was significantly greater than that in the MPR group (figure 4C). This finding suggested that Tregs play a predominant role in immune suppression in the non-MPR group.

Single-cell analysis reveals tumor microenvironment remodeling post-treatment

To further elucidate the cellular heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment in post-treatment samples, we analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data from six samples, comprising three from the MPR group and three from the non-MPR group. Cell identities across clusters were annotated using CellTypist and canonical cell markers, leading to the classification of 9,287 cells into eight major subtypes (online supplemental figure S3A). Expression of representative marker genes was consistent across all major lineages, including COL1A1 in fibroblasts, PECAM1 in endothelial cells, AGR2 in epithelial cells, CD68 in myeloid cells, TPSAB1 in mast cells, CD79A in B cells, and CD3D in T cells and NK cells (online supplemental figure S3B). For visualization on the UMAP plot, cell clusters were color-coded and grouped according to their respective annotations. All cells were classified into the MPR and non-MPR groups (online supplemental figure S3C). In summary, we depicted the landscape of cellular clusters from six patients for further analysis. A comparison of the proportions of cell types between the MPR and non-MPR groups revealed statistically significant differences (p<0.001, online supplemental figure S3D). Although B cells, T cells, and NK cells were the major cell types in both groups, the proportions of T cells and NK cells were greater in the non-MPR group. Therefore, we further analyzed the proportions of T-cell subtypes.

We categorized a total of 1,390 cells into seven major cell subtypes: CD8_Trm, CD8_Tem, CD8_Tac, CD4_Trm, CD4_Tcm, CD4_Tex, and Treg (figure 5A). Furthermore, we divided all cells into the MPR and non-MPR groups (figure 5B). Consistent with the RNA sequencing results, the non-MPR group contained more than half of the Tregs that suppressed immune responses (figure 5C). The differential expression of the FOXP3 gene between the two groups confirmed this finding. The total proportions of CD4+_Tex cells and Tregs in the non-MPR group approached 45% of the total T-cell proportion. Conversely, the single-cell data suggested that CD4_Trm, CD8_Trm, and CD4_Tcm cells accounted for 90%, 99%, and 75% of T cells, respectively, in the MPR group (figure 5D). Pseudotime analysis revealed the differentiation trajectory of T cells within the tumor microenvironment (figure 5E). Cells are colored by their pseudotime score, ranging from 0 to 1, reflecting their progression through the differentiation process. The UMAP plot shows that CD8+T cells (CD8_Trm, CD8_Tem, and CD8_Tac) and CD4+T cells (CD4_Trm, CD4_Tex, and CD4_Tcm) follow distinct trajectories. CD8_Tac cells predominantly appeared at the beginning of the pseudotime trajectory, suggesting an early activation state, whereas Treg cells were located toward the end of the trajectory, indicating a more differentiated, regulatory role. The differentiation pathway analysis presented a simplified model of T-cell differentiation states using PAGA (figure 5F). The nodes represent different T-cell types (CD8_Tac, CD8_Tem, CD8_Trm, CD4_Trm, CD4_Tcm, CD4_Tex, and Treg), and the edges denote the transitions between these states. The pathway shows a clear progression from CD8_Tac cells (node 0) through various intermediate states to Treg cells (node 6), with CD4+ and CD8+ cells following separate but interconnected trajectories. The positioning of Treg cells at the end of the trajectory suggests their significant role in immune response regulation as tumors progress.

Discussion

Improving response efficacy and identifying robust biomarkers are major challenges in current immunotherapy. Despite the application of immunotherapy in the treatment of early-stage gastrointestinal tumors, many patients still struggle to benefit. The mechanisms underlying the response and resistance to immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy remain unclear. Although combination therapy can alter the immune microenvironment of “cold” tumors and activate immune responses, the current low response rate suggests that immune evasion mechanisms may significantly limit therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, comprehensive data analysis is needed to uncover the underlying reasons and guide interventions to improve the response rate in future treatments. In our study, we used multiomics data to analyze the dynamic changes in the tumor microenvironment of patients with locally advanced G/GEJ cancer receiving neoadjuvant PD-1 inhibitors combined with chemotherapy, aiming to investigate the responses of the immune system and tumor to treatment.

First, no significant differences in DNA molecular characteristics, including SNVs, TMB, or CNVs, were observed between the MPR and non-MPR groups. Likewise, the baseline immune microenvironment showed no substantial heterogeneity across the tumors. In all examined samples, the pretreatment immune microenvironment was predominantly characterized by infiltration of macrophages and CD3+T cells. While CD8+T cells and NK cells were also present, their overall infiltration levels remained relatively low. Despite more pronounced CD3+T cell infiltration in non-MPR patients, the infiltration levels of other immune cells did not significantly differ between the MPR and non-MPR patients. In previous studies on biomarkers of complete pathological response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy in colorectal cancer, CD8+T cell density was considered to be associated with the pathological response to PD-1 inhibitors in high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) colorectal cancer.30 In this study, we did not observe a difference in CD8+T cell infiltration between different response groups at baseline. This may be due to the absence of strong immune surveillance in proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) tumors.31 32 However, our results suggest that the baseline positive ratio of CD3+T cells may be a potential predictive marker for the efficacy of neoadjuvant PD-1 inhibitor combined with chemotherapy in locally advanced G/GEJ cancer.

Notably, we analyzed whole-transcriptome sequencing data and found that the expression of immune regulatory molecules in the tumor microenvironment differed significantly between non-MPR patients and MPR patients prior to treatment. Genes highly expressed in non-MPR patients were significantly enriched in pathways related to cytokines and chemokines. These molecules play crucial roles in modulating immune responses in the tumor microenvironment, promoting or inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis, and influencing treatment outcomes.33 34 Conversely, MPR patients exhibited upregulation in genes associated with pathways related to regulating tumor cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration. Hence, we speculate that tumor heterogeneity may lead to distinct immunoregulatory mechanisms at the transcriptomic level. Specifically, genes showing differential expression related to tumor proliferation, drug metabolism, and chemokines (such as TSLP, CCL4L2, CALML5, GRIN2A, CAMK2B, ADRB3, WNT10A, SOST, SFRP5, RAC3, APCDD1L, CYP3A4, and CYP2D6) may serve as better predictors of an MPR than PD-L1, TMB, and CD3+T cell density.

We integrated the changes in immune regulatory molecules and cellular composition during treatment, along with the final tumor immune microenvironment status post-treatment, to analyze the reasons for different responses to therapy. During treatment, MPR patients exhibited more significant changes in the density and positive ratio of immune cell compared with non-MPR patients, particularly regarding the elevation of cytotoxic immune cells (CD8+T cells and NK cells) and the reduction in macrophages. Similar trends have been observed in other tumors, such as in a study of the tumor microenvironment after neoadjuvant immunotherapy in non-small cell lung cancer, which yielded comparable results.35 36

We observed significant differences in the post-treatment reshaping of the tumor microenvironment between patients with varying pathological responses. MPR patients exhibited an effective immune response, while in non-MPR patients, immune cells, despite accumulating around the tumor, failed to mount a successful attack. The post-treatment immune cell count in the MPR group increased significantly, surpassing that of the non-MPR group, with a marked elevation in the expression of cytotoxic molecules. While the non-MPR group also exhibited an increase in immune cell numbers, the difference between pretreatment and post-treatment was not statistically significant. The immune cell subtype estimation by single-cell level analysis showed significantly more CD8+T cells and NK cells in the MPR group than in the non-MPR group. Additionally, we observed the downregulation of chemokine receptors, including the CCR family, CXCR family, CX3CR1, CXCL5, and CXCL6, in the non-MPR group. Previous studies have reported that the downregulation of these factors weakens immune activity during tumor immunotherapy and reduces immune cell penetration into the tumor microenvironment.37 38

Tregs, essential for maintaining immune system homeostasis and tolerance, play a pivotal role in tumor immune regulation and are a current research focus, especially as potential therapeutic targets.39 40 Post-treatment, FOXP3+gene expression was significantly higher in the non-MPR group compared with the MPR group, although the levels were similar between the two groups before treatment. During treatment, FOXP3+expression decreased in the MPR group, suggesting that, alongside the recruitment and activation of cytotoxic cells, there is a concurrent reduction in immune-suppressive cells. Single-cell post-treatment data further confirmed that Tregs were significantly more abundant in non-MPR patients. Additionally, CD4_Tex and Tregs together constituted nearly half of the total T-cell population in the non-MPR group.

Conversely, the single-cell data also suggested that CD4_Trm, CD8_Trm, and CD4_Tcm were maintained at relatively high levels in the MPR group. This finding implies that the quantity or activity of memory T cells is greater in this group. The immune system is in a more activated or protective state, and these immune memory T cells may play a crucial role in maintaining immune homeostasis, resisting infection, or controlling disease progression.41 Different types of B cells and plasma cells may play specific functions and roles in the progression of gastric adenocarcinoma.42 The single-cell data show that both the B-cell community and IgG+plasma cells, as well as IgA+plasma cells, are predominant in the MPR group (online supplemental figure S4). Therefore, reducing immune inhibitory cells or factors in the tumor microenvironment in the non-MPR group to restore immune attack as an adjunctive therapy may greatly improve the response to immunotherapy.

This study has three limitations. First, the sample size was small because obtaining tumor samples from patients was difficult. And, the study was constrained by the low incidence of MSI-H in patients with G/GEJ cancer, with all included patients being MSS, limiting the analysis of MSI-H cases. Second, all patients in this study received PD-1 inhibitors combined with chemotherapy. Therefore, the mechanisms revealed by our study are applicable only to new targets for specific therapies and need additional cohorts for validation. Third, due to the lack of pretreatment single-cell detection data, changes in the number of immune cells and changes in the expression of immune-related genes during treatment, we can rely only on bulk-sequencing RNA data for analysis.

Conclusion

Our study underscores the pivotal role of immune microenvironment dynamics in predicting the efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors combined with chemotherapy in patients with locally advanced G/GEJ cancer. One key finding of our research is the significant impact of Tregs on treatment outcomes. Although baseline immune cell infiltration did not consistently predict therapeutic efficacy, substantial changes during treatment, particularly increases in cytotoxic T and NK cells and decreases in Tregs, were strongly correlated with improved pathological responses. The greater post-treatment expression of FOXP3 and the downregulation of chemokine receptors in the non-MPR group suggest that Tregs play a crucial role in immune evasion mechanisms, limiting therapeutic effectiveness. These observations indicate that targeting Tregs, in addition to enhancing cytotoxic immune responses, may be essential for improving treatment outcomes.

Despite limitations such as the small sample size and the specificity of combining PD-1 inhibitors with chemotherapy, our study provides valuable insights into immune regulatory mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment. Future studies with larger samples and further validation are necessary. By integrating multiomics data, we identified potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets that could enhance the response rate and efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with G/GEJ cancer. The continued exploration of Treg-related pathways and their impact on therapeutic outcomes will be crucial for improving the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.
