Visual spatial relationship sensitive transformer for image captioning
Authors: Xianghua Piao, Dong Jin, Min Jung Kwon, Yeong Hyeon Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImage captioning is a cross-modal task that combines computer vision and natural language processing to generate natural language descriptions of visual content. Recent advances have explored the integration of both grid-based and region-based visual features to better capture relational and contextual information, such as interactions between objects and their surrounding environment. However, combining multiple types of features often results in spatial misalignment, which hinders the model’s ability to construct coherent visual-semantic relationships. To address these limitations, we propose a novel Spatial Alignment Positional Encoder(SAPE), which encodes spatial information across aligned grid-level and region-level features to construct a unified visual-spatial representation. In addition, we introduce two complementary enhancement modules: Group Normalization Multi-head Attention(GNMA) to capture global relational cues, and Convolution-based Feature Enhancement Attention(CFEA) to enrich local spatial details. To mitigate the degradation of positional signals during deep training, we further propose a Learnable Adaptive Positional Encoder(LAPE) that dynamically preserves position-sensitive information. These components are integrated into a unified transformer-based architecture named the Visual-Spatial Relationship Sensitive Transformer(VRST). Extensive experiments on the MSCOCO dataset demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach, achieving a CIDEr score of 141.9 on the Karpathy test split and 138.2 on the official evaluation server, surpassing several strong baselines.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering

Introduction

Image captioning involves translating an image into a precise and fluent sentence. With advancements in machine translation models, encoder-decoder architectures have gained popularity in the field of image captioning, driving significant progress. A major breakthrough occurred in 2015 with the introduction of Faster R-CNN1 as a feature extractor. This approach enabled the extraction of grid-based features containing semantic information from images2. This milestone spurred further research into image captioning, leveraging pre-trained models to extract meaningful features from images for training3.

Image captioning models can be divided into single-feature models and multi-feature models. Single-feature models have limited capacity to represent semantic content compared with multi-feature models4. For instance, when a model relies only on grid features extracted by partitioning an image into fixed-size grids and identifying key patterns within each grid, it may fail to capture detailed information about specific objects. By contrast, region features focus on the characteristics of individual objects or regions, which alleviates this limitation but often provide insufficient information about background context or semantic relationships. To overcome these shortcomings, researchers have actively explored multi-feature fusion in order to generate captions with richer semantic content. Nevertheless, this line of research faces two major challenges.

The first challenge is feature misalignment, which impedes accurate sentence generation by failing to capture the visual-spatial semantic relationships among objects in an image or between objects and the background. Feature alignment involves matching features, so they correspond within the same spatial domain. Luo et al.5 tackled this issue by employing a geometric alignment graph to align grid and region features using the bounding box information of objects in an image. Incorporating bounding box information into image captioning models enhances visual feature alignment and captures spatial relationships6,7. However, relying on geometric information such as bounding boxes can limit the model’s scalability and practicality8. To address this limitation, this paper proposes the Spatial Alignment Positional Encoder (SAPE), which facilitates the construction of visual-spatial semantic relationships. While high-dimensional features are more complex, they contain richer information compared to low-dimensional ones. SAPE aligns detailed features in high-dimensional space and integrates positional information. This enables the model to accurately identify aligned features in lower dimensions and capture contextual information enriched with semantic relationships. Furthermore, as model depth increases, sensitivity to positional information tends to diminish, weakening the model’s ability to capture visual-spatial semantic relationships9. To resolve this issue, the paper introduces Learnable Adaptive Positional Encoding (LAPE), which enhances the model’s sensitivity to positional information. LAPE enables the model to more effectively capture visual-spatial semantic relationships, improving its ability to generate semantically rich and contextually accurate sentences.

The second challenge lies in the over-reliance of image captioning models on multi-head self-attention during the encoder stage. This limits their ability to capture the relationships between objects and the background within an image effectively. While the repeated application of multi-head self-attention can help identify such relationships, it has limitations. For instance, Ji et al.10 demonstrated an effective approach by first applying multi-head attention separately to semantic and region features, then fusing them through a third multi-head attention step. However, this method focuses attention on a limited set of features based on their calculated importance, leading to the loss of information from certain regions when capturing global features. Enhancing global features is critical for generating high-quality captions that provide comprehensive semantic information. However, multi-head self-attention also struggles with modeling locality and translation invariance, making it less effective at representing local content and object-level relationships. While CNNs address locality and translation invariance in image processing, their limited receptive field restricts their ability to capture global features11. In this paper, we propose to replace multi-head self-attention with Group-Normalization Multi-head Attention to enhance global features and additionally use Convolution-based Feature Enhanced Attention (CFEA) to enhance local features in order to sufficiently capture contextual information of images.

The main goal of this research is to enhance image captioning quality by effectively capturing the semantic relationships between objects, as well as between objects and their background. To this end, this study proposes a Visual-Spatial Relationship Sensitive Transformer that applies a Spatial Alignment Positional Encoder (SAPE) and a Learnable Adaptive Positional Encoding (LAPE) to establish and maintain visual-spatial semantic relationships. It also applies a Group Normalisation Multi-Head Attention (GNMA) and a Convolution-Based Feature Enhancement Attention (CFEA) to enhance global and local features within the traditional Transformer architecture. And the validity of the proposed model is proven through extensive ablation studies and performance comparison experiments using the MSCOCO dataset. The main contributions are as follows:

Related work

With the advancement of deep learning models, various methods for image captioning have been proposed. Initially, statistical techniques12 were used, resulting in simple sentences with limited ability to represent the content of the images. Recently, encoder-decoder architecture-based models, which can more richly express the content of images, have become prevalent and can be broadly categorized into CNN-RNN-based models and Transformer-based models. Among them, research utilizing RNN-based models in the decoder stage to generate captions from visual features is still ongoing. However, due to their complex structure and challenges in parallel processing, these models are less efficient and thus less commonly studied compared to Transformer-based models.

It has been demonstrated that using visual features extracted by pre-trained models as input to the encoder in an encoder-decoder structured image captioning model helps represent meaningful embeddings, leading to widespread adoption of this approach in the research community13–17. Furthermore, since visual features such as grid, region, and segmentation are complementary, there has been a growing focus on leveraging multi-features to express richer semantic content7,18,10 . For example, Luo et al.5 proposed the Dual-Level Collaborative Transformer network (DLCT), which utilizes the complementary advantages of grid and region features and aligns multi-features using geometric information. Wu et al.19 introduced spatial alignment in the encoder stage to efficiently fuse grid and segmentation features. Zhang et al.4 proposed the Global Cross Encoder (GCE) in the encoder stage to establish aligned relationships between grid and region features. Additionally, they introduced Cross on Cross Attention (CoCA) in the decoder stage to further fuse visual and semantic features, enhancing the inference capability of the model. These studies highlight a prominent trend in the field, wherein researchers either incorporate geometric information into multi-head self-attention mechanisms or rely solely on models built with multi-head self-attention to achieve effective multi-feature fusion.

Various methods have been proposed to enhance visual position information in image captioning models. The most commonly used methods are Absolute Position Encoding (APE)20 and Relative Position Encoding (RPE)21,22. As RPE has been demonstrated to be more efficient in capturing relative distances between arbitrary positions, many studies have combined or modified it with attention mechanisms16,3,23–25. For example, Yang et al.15 addressed the issue of missing positional information in 2D space for visual features by proposing a coordinate-based spatial position encoding method. Similarly, Duan et al.26 aligned static global features and region features in spatial representation. Moreover, Hu et al.6 introduced Bi-Positional Attention, which combines APE and RPE to calculate geometric relationships between images and visual content. However, applying APE and RPE can lead to an imbalance in feature distribution, particularly due to the significant variation in object sizes within an image. To mitigate this issue, Hu et al.23 proposed Spatial-Enhanced Attention based on APE and RPE, which partially alleviated the problem but did not fully resolve it. Considering the potential impact of additional information, such as geometric positional data, on the scalability and usability of models, this paper introduces the Visual-Spatial Relationship Sensitive Transformer (VRST). The proposed VRST addresses the positional information issue without relying on supplementary data, ensuring a more scalable and practical approach. And it can effectively capture the relationship between object-object and object-background.

Proposed methodology

In this section, we introduce the proposed Visual-Spatial Relationship Sensitive Transformer (VRST) for image captioning, followed by a detailed explanation of its key components: Spatial Alignment Positional Encoder (SAPE), Learnable Adaptive Positional Encoder (LAPE), Group Normalized Multi-head Attention (GNMA), and Convolution-based Feature Enhancement Attention (CFEA). The overall architecture of the model is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Overview of the VRST architecture.

The proposed model takes a single image as input and extracts grid features and region features using two different feature extractors. Specifically, the grid features are extracted using Faster R-CNN1 with a ResNeXt-10127 backbone pretrained on the ImageNet28, while the region features are obtained from the VINVL20 detector trained on the Visual Genome29. Based on the Spatial Alignment Positional Encoder (SAPE), which aligns multi-features composed of grid and region features in high-dimensional space and assigns positional information, the model establishes the visual-spatial semantic relationship between and . Next, during the encoder stage, Group Normalized Multi-head Attention (GNMA) is applied to enhance global features, while Convolution-based Feature Enhancement Attention (CFEA) is used to improve local feature representation. Additionally, the Learnable Adaptive Positional Encoder (LAPE) provides positional information during training. The processed image features are then forwarded to the decoder. Finally, during the caption generation process, the decoder output is passed through a softmax function to compute the word probability distribution at each generation step t, where is the word to be generated and denotes the sequence of previously generated words.”

Spatial alignment positional encoder (SAPE)

In traditional transformer models, the Absolute Positional Encoder (APE) is commonly used to capture positional information. Given an image feature of dimension d, positional information is added to obtain the input representation , as shown in Eq. (1).

In this work, we employ a ResNext-10127 backbone to extract grid features and a VINVL-X15220 backbone to extract region features. Although both share the same embedding dimensionality, they capture complementary aspects of the image while also exhibiting fundamental differences. Grid features provide dense spatial coverage across the image lattice, whereas region features encode object-centric semantic information in an irregular manner. This complementarity makes them valuable for joint reasoning, but their heterogeneity inevitably causes spatial semantic misalignment when fused directly.

To address this issue, we propose the Spatial Aligned Positional Encoder (SAPE). SAPE projects both feature types into a unified embedding space through a shared MLP, explicitly establishing the correspondence between the dense spatial layout of grid features and the semantic representations of region features . The aligned features are then concatenated and combined with positional information to form the final unified representation , enabling consistent spatial semantic alignment and more effective feature fusion, as shown in Equation (2).

Learnable adaptive positional encoding (LAPE)

To address the issue of positional information decaying across layers in deep architectures, Chen et al.30 combined an Absolute Position Encoder (APE) with Relative Position Encoding (RPE). Specifically, they added APE before the Transformer encoder and introduced RPE within the encoder layers, leveraging learnable relative offsets. This combination effectively preserves positional awareness across depth. Inspired by this design, we propose Learnable Adaptive Position Encoding (LAPE), as illustrated in Fig. 2., which can be viewed as a lightweight multi-stage extension of learnable relative bias.

(a) Encoder of the proposed VRST model, (b) learnable adaptive positional encoding (LAPE) module architecture.

Specifically, LAPE does not inject positional signals in a single, one time manner within a single sub-module. Instead, it introduces a distinct set of parameters in each of the following three stages. The first stage is positioned at the input of the Transformer encoder, handling global-level feature representations. The second stage follows the attention module within the convolutional backbone network, focusing on enhancing local spatial features. The third stage operates during the fusion phase of convolutional and Transformer-based representations, playing a crucial role in aligning heterogeneous features prior to decoding. Specifically, our architecture incorporates three Transformer encoder layers, each internally utilizing an independent LAPE instance. That is, each layer possesses its own learnable relative position bias parameters, which are not shared across layers. Consequently, LAPE is instantiated five times throughout the entire model.

Each LAPE instance is assigned a unique set of learnable parameters, enabling it to adaptively respond to the structural and semantic properties of its corresponding feature stream. This stage-specific parameterization allows fine-grained control over how positional information is utilized across different network levels. Additionally, the positional offset term incorporates a dropout mechanism, suppressing over-reliance on fixed positional cues and encouraging the model to adaptively choose when to utilize or ignore positional information across different contexts.

Normalization is crucial for stabilizing feature distributions and mitigating the imbalance across different regions, which may otherwise hinder effective representation learning. Common normalization techniques include Layer Normalization31, Batch Normalization32, and Group Normalization33, each normalizing data along different dimensions. These methods have also shown effectiveness in tasks such as image captioning. For instance, DSNT23 introduces G-RMSNorm to enhance stability by selectively amplifying informative features.

However, traditional multi-head attention weights all spatial regions equally, which introduces a familiar bias whereby larger objects, by virtue of occupying more area, attract more attention and smaller objects are often missed. To alleviate this, we propose Group Normalization Multi-head Attention (GNMA), in which Group Normalization is directly applied to the value matrix V within the self-attention computation. Convolution-based feature enhancement attention (CFEA)

In the field of image processing, CNN-based attention mechanisms can be roughly divided into six types, including channel attention, spatial attention, channel-spatial attention, and others34. Among them, the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM)35 based on channel-spatial attention is widely used. This paper extends CBAM and proposes a Convolution-based Feature Enhancement Attention (CFEA) module to address the shortcomings of the standard multi-head attention mechanism in modeling local visual details. Although the self-attention mechanism has advantages in modeling long-range dependencies, it often fails to capture fine-grained information such as texture, edges, and spatial position. Therefore, we design a two-branch convolutional enhancement structure consisting of channel attention and spatial attention to supplement the modeling of local features before fusion.

Specifically, CFEA consists of two complementary convolutional pathways. The channel attention branch highlights informative feature channels using global average pooling and subsequent pointwise convolutions, while the spatial attention branch captures spatial dependencies with convolutions of larger receptive fields. The two branches operate in parallel, are fused to strengthen both channel relevance and spatial saliency, and their outputs are then aggregated into a locally enhanced feature map.

To ensure effective normalization of the fused features, we apply a parallel normalization strategy after the attention aggregation stage. Both Batch Normalization and Layer Normalization are used in parallel to capture their complementary advantages. BN is applied to normalize across the batch dimension, providing stable statistical signals for local activations and facilitating convergence32. LN, on the other hand, normalizes across the feature dimension within each individual sample, which is beneficial for harmonizing the outputs from heterogeneous attention branches and maintaining training stability31. This dual-path normalization ensures both local consistency and global representational alignment.

Finally, the outputs from both normalization paths are aggregated and passed through a MLP to re-balance the feature distribution and project the representation into a unified embedding space. This process improves the expressive capacity and compatibility of the fused features.

Experiments

Datasets and evaluation metrics

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed VRST, we conducted experiments using the widely recognized public dataset, MSCOCO. The MSCOCO dataset comprises 123,287 images, separated into 82,783 for training and 40,504 for testing, with each image labeled with five reference captions. Following the widely used split method37, we further divided the dataset into 113,287 training images, 5,000 validation images, and 5,000 testing images. To evaluate the quality of the generated captions, this paper adopts BLEU38, METEOR39, ROUGE-L40, CIDEr41, and SPICE42 as performance metrics. In the experiments, these metrics are abbreviated as B@n, M, R, C, S, respectively.

Discussion

To better interpret the qualitative effects of the four proposed modules, we first conducted a visual comparison of representative image captioning results, as illustrated in Figs. 4 and 5. These examples clearly demonstrate how each module contributes to sentence generation, helping produce captions that are not only more semantically accurate but also spatially well-aligned.

Qualitative comparison of generated captions with and without LAPE modules.

The SAPE module is particularly effective in aligning object positions and spatial relationships within generated captions. For instance, in the case of the airplane image, the baseline model incorrectly used the verb “sitting,” whereas the SAPE-enhanced version generated the more spatially appropriate term “parked.” Similarly, in the flower vase image, the SAPE module contributed to the phrase “on a table,” providing a clearer representation of the object’s relationship with its background. The GNMA module enhances the model’s ability to recognize group structures and interpret actions among objects. In the stuffed animal image, for example, GNMA accurately captures the phrase “in a pile” whereas the baseline provides only a generic spatial description. In the toddler example, GNMA successfully identifies both “two giraffes standing” enabling more precise interpretation of the interaction between the subject and surrounding objects. The CFEA module improves the representation of local details and background elements by incorporating convolution-based feature enhancement. In the kitchen scene, the baseline model fails to mention critical objects such as “pots and pans” while the CFEA-enhanced model explicitly identifies these elements, resulting in a more comprehensive description. Likewise, in an image showing two dogs playing, CFEA identified that the dogs were playing with a “frisbee” demonstrating the model’s heightened sensitivity to visual cues.

As Fig. 5 shows, when the LAPE module is not applied, the spatial relationship between the subject and background in the beach image is ambiguously described. In contrast, the LAPE1–3 models successfully generate spatially grounded expressions such as “on a beach near the ocean,” indicating that hierarchical positional encoding contributes to a more precise representation of scene structure. Comparison with other optimized positional encoding methods

To comprehensively validate the two proposed location modeling methods, LAPE and SAPE, we conducted comparative experiments against several representative location encoding methods as shown in Table 9. First, for LAPE, we compared it against APE, Relative Positional Encoding (RPE)53, and Rotary Positional Encoding (RoPE)54 within an architecture integrating LAPE, GNMA, and CFEA. Experimental results show that LAPE attains the highest scores. This demonstrates that LAPE’s learnable adaptive mechanism provides more flexible positional biases, thereby outperforming static position encoding and rotation-based position encoding in spatial reasoning capabilities. Second, for SAPE, we evaluated its performance against APE, RPE, RoPE, and Learnable Positional Encoding (Learnable PE)55 within an architecture integrating LAPE, GNMA, and CFEA. Results demonstrate that SAPE consistently outperforms these methods on most metrics. Similar to the comparison with LAPE, both RPE and RoPE performed below APE, further suggesting potential limitations when synergizing with the GNMA module. To validate the importance of the shared semantic space design in SAPE, we constructed a variant employing independent MLP projections for grid and region features. Experimental results demonstrated performance degradation in this variant, confirming the critical role of the shared embedding space in feature alignment.

In summary, although LAPE and SAPE differ in structure and application level, their common goal is to enhance the modeling capability of positional information. LAPE can be viewed as a lightweight, multi-stage extension for learning relative biases. It injects positional parameters at the Transformer input stage, after convolutional attention, and during the fusion stage between convolutions and Transformers. These parameters are independently instantiated within each Transformer layer, ensuring the effectiveness and adaptability of positional information across global, local, and cross-modal levels. Complementing this, SAPE fundamentally resolves spatial semantic misalignment issues during direct fusion of heterogeneous features by projecting grid and regional features into a unified embedding space via a shared MLP. Experimental results demonstrate that both methods outperform existing optimized location encoding approaches across various comparison settings, further validating the effectiveness of the proposed multi-level location information modeling strategy for image captioning tasks.

Limitations and future work

Despite the strong performance demonstrated by VRST, several limitations remain that warrant further investigation. First, the model occasionally fails in scenes containing ambiguous, occluded, or visually misleading inputs. As shown in Fig. 7, VRST sometimes produces syntactically correct captions that are semantically inaccurate. In Fig. 7a, for instance, a white liquid was incorrectly identified as milk, and the surrounding refrigerator context was disregarded. This type of error often arises in cluttered kitchen environments with occluded backgrounds, where substances such as breast milk or other visually similar liquids are misinterpreted as cow’s milk due to limited contextual cues. In Fig. 7b, a cow was misclassified as a dog, likely due to partial occlusion or complex natural surroundings. These failure cases are particularly evident in scenes requiring fine-grained spatial reasoning or involving uncommon object-background relationships. Such patterns suggest that the GNMA and CFEA modules may struggle to capture low-salience or ambiguous visual features, underscoring the need for more robust disambiguation mechanisms and improved contextual reasoning capabilities. Second, VRST relies on fixed visual backbone extractors, including ResNeXt101 and VINVL, which are typically trained on closed-set object categories. This reliance limits the model’s ability to generalize to unseen or out-of-distribution concepts. To overcome this limitation, future work may explore integrating open-vocabulary vision-language models, such as CLIP56 and BLIP-257 to enhance semantic generalization and adaptability to diverse visual contexts. Finally, the incorporation of multiple enhancement modules, including SAPE, GNMA, CFEA, and LAPE, adds architectural complexity and increases computational overhead. According to Hu et al.23, the DSNT benchmark model occupies 516.9MB, whereas the VRST model requires 565MB. Although the model remains deployable on high-end GPUs, its memory footprint may pose limitations in resource-constrained environments such as mobile or embedded systems. Future work should explore approaches such as knowledge distillation and quantization to reduce complexity while preserving performance.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed the Visual-Spatial Relationship Sensitive Transformer (VRST), a novel image captioning model designed to enhance spatial and semantic understanding by integrating both grid-based and region-based visual features. The model incorporates several key components: the Spatial Alignment Positional Encoder (SAPE) for aligning multi-scale visual features and embedding positional information; Group Normalization-based Multi-head Attention (GNMA) and Convolution-based Feature Enhancement Attention (CFEA) for enhancing global and local semantic representations; and the Learnable Adaptive Positional Encoder (LAPE) to mitigate positional information loss. These components work in synergy to improve alignment, attention mechanisms, and contextual reasoning.

Extensive experiments and ablation studies on the MSCOCO dataset demonstrate that VRST achieves consistently strong performance across various evaluation metrics, validating the contribution of each module. Despite these promising results, the model still relies on fixed visual feature extractors and introduces additional architectural complexity. As future work, we plan to investigate more adaptive feature encoding strategies, lightweight model variants, and scene-level reasoning techniques to further improve the model’s scalability, efficiency, and interpretability in diverse deployment scenarios.
