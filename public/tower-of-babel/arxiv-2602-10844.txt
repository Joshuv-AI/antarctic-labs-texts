Generalized Decidability via Brouwer Trees
Tom de Jong, Nicolai Kraus, Aref Mohammadzadeh, Fredrik Nordvall Forsberg

Abstract
. In the setting of constructive mathematics, we suggest and study a framework for decidability of properties, which allows for finer distinctions than just “decidable, semidecidable, or undecidable”. We work in homotopy type theory and use Brouwer ordinals to specify the level of decidability of a property. In this framework, we express the property that a proposition is α \alpha -decidable, for a Brouwer ordinal α \alpha , and show that it generalizes decidability and semidecidability. Further generalizing known results, we show that α \alpha -decidable propositions are closed under binary conjunction, and discuss for which α \alpha they are closed under binary disjunction. We prove that if each P ⁡ ( i ) P(i) is semidecidable, then the countable meet ∀ i ∈ ℕ . P ⁡ ( i ) \forall i\in\mathbb{N}.P(i) is ω 2 \omega^{2} -decidable, and similar results for countable joins and iterated quantifiers. We also discuss the relationship with countable choice. All our results are formalized in Cubical Agda.



1.
Introduction

Decidability and semidecidability are at the heart of computer science.
For example, it is decidable whether a natural number
p
is prime, and whether the pair
(p,p+2)
is a twin prime pair.
Given a natural number
n
, it is semidecidable whether there is a twin prime pair above
n
, i.e. whether there is a number
p>n
such that both
p
and
p+2
are prime.
A long-standing open problem in number theory, the famous
Twin Prime Conjecture
, asks whether there is a twin prime pair above
n
for every natural number
n
.
Despite significant efforts and successes
[
37
,
28
]
, the question remains unsolved.
1
1

1

For the avoidance of doubt, we do not make any progress on the Twin Prime Conjecture — it merely serves as an example of a proposition in the context of (generalized) decidability. Moreover, once the Twin Prime Conjecture gets solved, it becomes a decidable problem, which means that we cannot use it as an interesting example any longer.

Furthermore, there is no known algorithm that could semidecide the Twin Prime Conjecture.
While one could dovetail the searches and thus run the search for every
n
at some point, a positive result would require all searches to be successful, which (at least on an intuitive level) would take “too long.”
The current paper suggests and studies a framework in which the duration of such “algorithms” can be made precise and proved.

We work in constructive mathematics, where one commonly used definition of the statement “the property
P
is semidecidable” is that there exists a binary sequence
s:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbf{2}
such that
P
holds if and only if there exists an index
i
such that
s_{i}=1

[
30
,
3
]
.
Checking whether such a binary sequence contains a
1
corresponds to checking whether a given conatural number is finite.
Classically, the ordinal corresponding to the set of conatural numbers is
\omega+1
, and a semidecidable proposition is one of which a positive answer can be discovered in
\omega
-many steps, assuming this positive answer exists.

For the Twin Prime Conjecture, there are
\omega
-many semidecidable sub-problems, namely one for each possible input
n
, and each of these sub-problems lets us discover a positive answer within
\omega
steps (assuming this answer exists). Thus, one might expect (and we will prove) that the Twin Prime Conjecture’s ordinal is
\omega^{2}
.

Our general definition is that a proposition
P
is
\alpha
-decidable, for a given ordinal
\alpha
, if there exists an ordinal
x
such that
P\leftrightarrow x\geq\alpha
.
Naturally, this definition and everything we can prove only matches our expectation if we choose a suitable notion of ordinals which ensures, for example, that the
(\omega+1)
-decidable propositions coincide with the semidecidable ones.
For instance, if we take our set of ordinals to be given by some set of “syntactic” representations
[
7
,
31
,
34
]

such as Cantor Normal Forms (i.e. binary trees with a certain condition), then the inequality
x\geq\alpha
is always decidable, forcing
\alpha
-decidability to be identical to decidability.
Similarly, if we take ordinals to mean “sets with a wellfounded, transitive, and extensional relation”
[
29
,
1
,
19
,
18
,
35
,
13
]
in a constructive meta-theory, then the inequality
x\geq\omega+1
cannot be semidecidable.

A notion of ordinals that works for us are Brouwer trees
[
9
,
23
]
as previously formulated
[
27
]
in homotopy type theory, a setting that we adopt.
The type
\mathsf{Brw}
of such ordinals is, together with the relation
{}\leq{}:\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Prop}
, given by a quotient inductive-inductive construction
[
2
]
.
The constructors for
\mathsf{Brw}
are
\mathsf{zero}
,
\bsuc
, and
\blim
, constructing zero, successors, and limits of sequences, together with a path constructor to ensure that bisimilar sequences give equal limits, and constructors for the relation
\leq
to ensure that the expected equations holds.
An important design choice for the current work is that the constructor
\blim
only accepts
strictly
increasing sequences as its argument.
As a consequence, it is decidable whether a given ordinal is finite or infinite:
\mathsf{zero}
is finite,
\blim f
is always infinite, and
\bsuc\beta
is finite if and only if
\beta
is.
Similarly, it is semidecidable whether
\blim f\geq\omega+1
because the inequality holds if and only if we can find an index
i
such that
f_{i}
is infinite, which is equivalent to finding the digit
1
in a binary sequence.

Setting

We work in homotopy type theory (HoTT) as presented in the book
[
35
]
, using standard terminology and notation.
In particular, we write
\|A\|
for the propositional truncation, and
\exists x.\,P(x)
for
\|\Sigma x:X.\,P(x)\|
.
If
P
and
Q
are propositions (i.e., types with at most one element), then we write
P\land Q
instead of
P\times Q
, and
P\lor Q
instead of
\|P+Q\|
. We write
\mathsf{Prop}
for the subtype of propositions.
2
2

2

Everything can be assumed to take place in a fixed (e.g. the lowest) universe, and
\mathsf{Prop}
is a subtype of this fixed universe. The Agda formalization is universe-parametric. Propositional resizing is not assumed.

All the (small) types we consider are sets, i.e. have no interesting higher equalities.
Nevertheless, concepts from HoTT are required for our development as we build on the framework introduced in
[
25
,
27
]
,
which includes the set-truncated higher (a.k.a. quotient) inductive-inductive type
\mathsf{Brw}
of Brouwer trees and its relation
\leq
.
While no further higher inductive types are used (apart from in
Section

8.2
),
univalence is heavily relied on to establish the desired properties of
\mathsf{Brw}
in
[
27
]
.

In principle, it should be possible to carry out our work in a plain version of MLTT or another constructive foundation, as long as function extensionality holds, a version of propositional truncation is available, and the type
\mathsf{Brw}
with its relation
\leq
are assumed together with their properties. (Note that
Section

8.2
would require a similar additional assumption.)

Summary of main results

Defining an arbitrary proposition
P
to be
\alpha
-decidable
if
\exists x:\mathsf{Brw}.\,P\leftrightarrow x\geq\alpha
,
we first establish that this notion generalizes existing notions of decidability, in the sense that
P
is decidable if and only if it is
1
-decidable (if and only if it is
\omega
-decidable), and semidecidable if and only if it is
(\omega+1)
-decidable. We show that the set of
\alpha
-decidable propositions is closed under binary conjunctions, and the smaller set of
(\omega\cdot n+k)
-decidable propositions is closed under binary disjunctions.
We further study families
P:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
of semidecidable propositions and construct their
characteristic ordinal
, which allows us to determine that their countable meet is
\omega^{2}
-decidable, an instance of which is the twin prime conjecture.
The countable join of the family is only
(\omega\cdot 3)
-decidable, unless countable choice is assumed which, as is well-known, makes it semidecidable.
In a related vein, we study the notion of
Sierpiński-semidecidability
, an alternative to semidecidability that is closed under countable joins without the assumption of countable choice, and its relation to
\alpha
-decidability.
Using constructive taboos such as the Limited Principle of Omniscience or Markov’s principle, we are able to separate classes of
\alpha
-decidability, and studying the effect of quantifier alternations allows us to establish that the search for a counter-example to the twin prime conjecture, assuming it exists, is successful within
\omega^{2}+\omega
steps.

Related work

Our work lies at the boundary of
synthetic computability theory
, a subject pioneered by Bauer
[
3
]
and influenced by Rosolini
[
30
]
, Bridges and Richman
[
6
]
, and many others.
The approach of synthetic computability theory is to
not
fix a model of computation (such as Turing machines), but to use the notion of computability that is built into the logic one uses.
For example, in synthetic computability theory every function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is (tautologically) computable,
and a standard definition of the semidecidability says that
P
is semidecidable if there exists a binary sequence that contains the digit
1
if and only if
P
holds, which is the definition we are using.

The theory of
infinite time Turing machines
initiated by Hamkins and
Lewis
[
20
]
investigates a notion of Turing machine which can run
for infinite ordinal time. While there are similarities in motivation,
the resulting development is quite different from ours. Infinite time
Turing machines are a specific model of computation, couched in a
classical metatheory, whereas our notion of decidability is synthetic,
and developed constructively. It may be possible to describe our development
as a synthetic version of infinite time Turing machines that follows the approach
of constructive mathematics. However, the foundational differences mean that a
precise connection could only be made by interpreting our theory into an effective model, which, while of interest, is clearly beyond the scope of this paper.

Similarly,
Limit Computable Mathematics

[
17
,
21
]
is concerned with problems that can be solved in the limit of a possibly infinite sequence of “guesses”, i.e., by infinitely long decision procedures. This gives rise to constructive interpretations of classical proofs
[
4
]
, which our framework of ordinal decidability is not aiming to do.

Within constructive type theories, Brouwer tree ordinals have been investigated a number of times,
e.g. by Coquand, Hancock and Setzer
[
10
]
.
The most relevant work for us is the one by Kraus, Nordvall Forsberg, and Xu
[
27
]
, which suggests the version of Brouwer trees that are used in this paper.
Quotient inductive-inductive types were introduced in the HoTT book
[
35
]
and further studied by Altenkirch and collaborators
[
2
]
.

A preliminary variant of the definition of
\alpha
-decidability, and its relation to semidecidability, was presented in a talk at TYPES 2022
[
26
]
. The present paper develops and refines the theory suggested in that talk.

2.
Brouwer Ordinals

The notion of ordinals that this work is based on are the Brouwer ordinals introduced in
Kraus, Nordvall Forsberg, and Xu
[
25
,
27
]
.
In this section, we recall their constructions in two stages.
We first explain how the type
\mathsf{Brw}
with the relation
\leq
is constructed in
Section

2.1
, and recall the important properties in
Section

2.2
.
The reader is free to skip the first subsection as only the properties, but not the implementation, are referred to in later sections.

2.1.
Construction of Brouwer Ordinals

The type of Brouwer ordinals, denoted
\mathsf{Brw}
, is defined mutually with its order relation
\leq\,:\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Prop}
as a quotient inductive-inductive type
[
2
]
. This means the type and its relation are generated simultaneously by a set of point- and path-constructors.

The point constructors for the type
\mathsf{Brw}
are
\mathsf{zero}
,
\bsuc
, and
\blim
; the important thing to note here is that
\blim
constructs the limit of a
strictly increasing
sequence, meaning that the input of
\blim
is a function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Brw}
such that
\forall n.\,\bsuc(f_{n})\leq f_{n+1}
.
This “design choice” has important consequences with respect to the decidability of
\mathsf{Brw}
, which we will discuss in
Section

2.2
.
The path constructors of
\mathsf{Brw}
are a truncation constructor, ensuring that
\mathsf{Brw}
is a set, and a constructor
\mathsf{bisim}
, equating the limits of bisimilar sequences.
The constructors of the relation
\leq
ensure that
\mathsf{zero}
is minimal,
\bsuc
is monotone,
\blim
really constructs limits, and the relation is transitive and valued in propositions.

For this definition, one can define addition, multiplication, and exponentiation, with the usual properties.
Specific examples such as finite ordinals
n
, the least infinite ordinal
\omega
, and
\epsilon_{0}
can be defined straightforwardly.
The details are given in Kraus, Nordvall Forsberg, and Xu
[
27
]
.

It is worth remarking, and something we make use of repeatedly, that if we are proving a
property
of Brouwer ordinals, i.e. constructing an element of a proposition-valued type family over
\mathsf{Brw}
, then it suffices to consider the point constructors; the cases for the path constructors are all trivial since we are eliminating into a proposition.

2.2.
Properties of Brouwer Ordinals

The definition of
\mathsf{Brw}
and its relation
\leq
have several useful consequences.
With the following lemma, we recall the main properties that we need for the current paper from
[
27
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(

)
.

We have the following basic properties of
\mathsf{Brw}
and its relation
\leq
, with
\alpha<\beta
being defined as
\bsuc\,\alpha\leq\beta
.

(1)

Classification of ordinals:
Every ordinal is either zero, or a successor, or a limit.

(2)

Decidability of finiteness:

Related to the classification above, it is decidable whether a given ordinal
\alpha
is finite (i.e.
\alpha<\omega
) or infinite (i.e.
\alpha\geq\omega
).
If
\alpha
is finite, then we can explicitly find the natural number
n
such that the canonical embedding
\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Brw}
sends
n
to
\alpha
.
However, being able to decide
\alpha\leq\beta
and
\alpha=\beta
in general is a constructive taboo.

(3)

Properties of the relation:
The relation
\alpha<\beta
, defined to be
\alpha+1\leq\beta
, is transitive, wellfounded, and extensional.

The following properties characterize the relation
\leq
:

(4)

\bsuc\,\alpha\leq\bsuc\,\beta
if and only if
\alpha\leq\beta
.

(5)

If
\alpha<\blim f
then
\exists n.\alpha<f_{n}
.

(6)

If
\blim\,f\leq\bsuc\,\alpha
then
\blim f\leq\alpha
, and if
\alpha<\blim f
then
\bsuc\,\alpha<\blim f
.

(7)

\blim f\leq\blim g
implies that
f
is simulated by
g
. ∎

We omit the (known) proofs of these properties.
Key for the classification of ordinals is the choice of only allowing limits of strictly increasing sequences.
This also gives decidability of finiteness, because
\mathsf{zero}
is finite,
\blim f
is always infinite, and
\bsuc\beta
is finite if and only if
\beta
is.
Note that, in the formulation of the classification property, a limit ordinal is an ordinal
\alpha
such that there
exists
(
\|\Sigma\ldots\|
) a sequence
f
; asking for the sequence
f
itself would be too strong for the previous statement because of the constructor
\mathsf{bisim}
.

In the literature on ordinals, some authors consider zero a limit.
Our definition does not allow us to do this, but it often happens that we want to express that an ordinal is not a successor, i.e. either zero or a limit.
We write
\mathsf{Brw}^{zl}
for the subtype of
\mathsf{Brw}
consisting of zero and limits.

2.3.
Rounding to Limits

As a final preparation for the study of ordinal decidability, we note that we can
round
every Brouwer ordinal to a zero/limit.
We can round up or down, and we write
\upa,\dwa:\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Brw}^{zl}
for these two functions, defined in the obvious way:

\displaystyle\upa\mathsf{zero}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\mathsf{zero}\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\dwa\mathsf{zero}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\mathsf{zero}

\displaystyle\upa(\bsuc x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}x+\omega\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\dwa(\bsuc x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\dwa x

\displaystyle\upa(\blim f)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\blim f\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\dwa(\blim f)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\blim f

These definitions extend straightforwardly to the path constructors of
\mathsf{Brw}
.
The functions
\upa
and
\dwa
calculate zero/limits, which is automatic for most cases; for the successor cases, note that
\upa(\bsuc x)
is the limit of the sequence
\lambda n.x+n
, and
\dwa(\bsuc x)
is a limit by induction on
x
.

Lemma 2.2

(

)
.

The functions
\upa
and
\dwa
preserve
\leq
. ∎

Using the rounding down function
\dwa
, we can split every ordinal as the sum of an infinite (or zero) and a finite ordinal:

Lemma 2.3

(

)
.

Every
\alpha
can be written as
\alpha=\lambda+n
, where
\lambda
is zero or a limit, and
n
is finite (i.e., given by a natural number). ∎

Limit ordinals cannot distinguish between
\alpha
and
\upa\alpha
, in the following sense:

Lemma 2.4

(

)
.

For a zero/limit ordinal
\lambda:\mathsf{Brw}^{zl}
and any ordinal
\alpha:\mathsf{Brw}
, we have:

(1)

\alpha\leq\lambda\longleftrightarrow\upa\alpha\leq\lambda

(2)

\lambda\leq\alpha\longleftrightarrow\lambda\leq\dwa\alpha

(3)

\lambda<\alpha\longleftrightarrow\lambda<\upa\alpha

Based on the Brouwer ordinals introduced above, we define ordinal decidability:

Definition 3.1

(

\alpha
-Decidability)

Given
\alpha:\mathsf{Brw}

\alpha\mbox{-}\mathsf{decidable}\,P\;\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\;\exists y:\mathsf{Brw}.(P\leftrightarrow\alpha\leq y)

(

)

A proposition

\displaystyle P\lor\neg P\;

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\;1\mbox{-}\mathsf{decidable}\,P

\displaystyle\left(\exists(s:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbf{2}).(P\leftrightarrow\exists i.s_{i}=1)\right)\;

)

If

\displaystyle s^{\uparrow}_{0}\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}0

\displaystyle s^{\uparrow}_{n+1}\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\begin{cases}\omega&\text{if $n$ is minimal such that $s_{n}=1$}\\

Remark 3.4

(

)
.

We note two points:

(1)

For sufficiently low ordinals

Thus, the second part of

As one would intuitively expect, a proposition is decidable if and only if its evidence (or lack thereof) can be discovered in finitely many steps.
Since our abstract framework does not consider concrete algorithms, it also cannot distinguish between numbers of steps that only differ by a finite amount (cf.

For the further development, we do not use the terminology involving steps and refer to the technically simpler concept of

(2)

As we will see in the next section, it is sufficient to consider non-successor ordinals

; if we phrase

4.
Reduction to Limit Ordinals

As we do not use an explicit notion of algorithm but work with an abstract definition of
\alpha
-decidability,
and the complexity of a function is not an internal property of type theory, it is unsurprising that the framework cannot separate
1
-decidable from
n
-decidable propositions, for any positive natural number
n
.
We generalize this observation and show that we cannot distinguish between
{(\alpha+1)}
-decidability and
(\alpha+\omega)
-decidability; in other words, we do not lose anything if we restrict ourselves to
\lambda
-decidability for limit ordinals
\lambda
.

Lemma 4.1

(

)
.

Let
P
be a proposition and
\alpha
be an ordinal. Then,
P
is
(\alpha+1)
-decidable if and only if it is
(\alpha+2)
-decidable.

Proof.

By definition, the goal is to prove:

\Big(\exists u.(P\leftrightarrow(\alpha+1)\leq u)\Big)\leftrightarrow\Big(\exists v.(P\leftrightarrow(\alpha+2)\leq v)\Big).

Both directions from left to right and from right to left can be shown with the same strategy.
We only sketch the latter.
Assume
P
is
(\alpha+2)
-decidable.
As we are proving a proposition, we can assume that we are given
v
, and it suffices to consider the cases that
v
is zero, a successor, or a limit.
The first is trivial; in the second, if
v=v^{\prime}+1
, we choose
u
to be
v^{\prime}
and have
\alpha+2\leq v\leftrightarrow\alpha+1\leq v^{\prime}
by
Lemma

2.1
Item

4
; and, if
v
is a limit, we choose
u
to be
v
and apply
Lemma

2.1
Item

6
.
∎

By a straightforward induction, we have:

Corollary 4.2

(

)
.

For every ordinal
\alpha
and natural number
n
, a proposition
P
is
(\alpha+1)
-decidable if and only if it is
(\alpha+1+n)
-decidable. ∎

Remark 4.3

(

)
.

Note that
Lemma

4.1
cannot be formulated with
\alpha
instead of
\alpha+1
because, if
\alpha
is a limit ordinal (or zero), the statement will not hold in general. For example, we have seen that
(\omega+1)
-decidability is just semidecidability, while
\omega
-decidability is decidability.

To formalize the intuition that the hierarchy of ordinal decidability is indexed by limit ordinals,
our next goal is to show that
Corollary

4.2
holds even if
n
is replaced by
\omega
.

Theorem 4.4

(

)
.

For any ordinal
\beta
, a proposition
P
is
\beta
-decidable if and only if it is
\upa\beta
-decidable.

Proof.

If
\beta
is zero or a limit, there is nothing to do.
Using
Lemma

2.3
, in the final case
\beta=\lambda+n
for a limit (or zero)
\lambda
and a finite positive
n
, and by
Corollary

4.2
, we may assume
n=1
.
Therefore, our goal is to show:

\Big(\exists u.\,\,P\leftrightarrow\lambda+1\leq u\Big)\longleftrightarrow\Big(\exists v.\,\,P\leftrightarrow\lambda+\omega\leq v\Big)

From left to right, let us assume we are given
u
; using
Lemma

2.4
, we have
{\lambda+1\leq u}\leftrightarrow{\lambda+1\leq\upa u}\leftrightarrow{\upa(\lambda+1)\leq\upa u}\leftrightarrow{\lambda+\omega\leq\upa u}
, so we choose
v\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\upa u
.
From right to left, assume we are given
v
; we have
{\lambda+\omega\leq v}\leftrightarrow{\lambda+\omega\leq\upa v}\leftrightarrow{\lambda+1\leq\upa v}
, so we (again) choose
u\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\upa v
.
∎

As it turns out, we can phrase ordinal decidability purely in terms of zero/limits.

Definition 4.5

(

Limit-decidability)
.

For a proposition
P
and
\lambda:\mathsf{Brw}^{zl}
, we say that
P
is
\lambda
-limit-decidable if

\lambda\mbox{-}\mathsf{limit}\mbox{-}\mathsf{decidable}\,P\;\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\;\exists y:\mathsf{Brw}^{zl}.(P\leftrightarrow\lambda\leq y)

Theorem 4.6

(

)
.

For a proposition
P
and an ordinal
\beta
, we have that
P
is
\beta
-decidable if and only if it it
\upa\beta
-limit-decidable.

Proof.

Use
Theorem

4.4
, then, in the non-trivial direction, round down the witness and use
Lemma

2.4
Item

2
.
∎

5.
Closure under Binary Conjunctions

It is well-known that, if
P
and
Q
are decidable (or semidecidable), then so is
P\land Q
.
We generalize this statement to
\alpha
-decidability.
This means that, if there exists
x,y:\mathsf{Brw}
such that
P\leftrightarrow\alpha\leq x
and
Q\leftrightarrow\alpha\leq y
, then there exists
z
such that
{P\land Q}\leftrightarrow\alpha\leq z
.
This is in particular the case if
z
is the meet of
x
and
y
when we view
(\mathsf{Brw},\leq)
as a poset (i.e., the categorical product).
To avoid confusion with the poset of propositions (or the category of sets), and to support the intuition of ordinals as numbers, we refer to the meet of ordinals as their
minimum
:

Definition 5.1

(

Minimum)
.

We say that
\mu:\mathsf{Brw}
is the
minimum
of
\alpha
,
\beta:\mathsf{Brw}
if the property

(1)

\forall\gamma.(\alpha\geq\gamma\land\beta\geq\gamma)\leftrightarrow\mu\geq\gamma

is satisfied.

If the minimum of
\alpha
and
\beta
exists, then it is necessarily unique;
thus, stating that all minima exists is equivalent to giving a function that calculates them.
The current section is structured as follows:
In
Section

5.1
, we show that
\mathsf{Brw}
cannot constructively have binary minima in general; in
Section

5.2
, we show that binary minima exists for zero and limits; and in
Section

5.3
, we prove that, as a consequence,
\alpha
-decidable propositions are closed under binary conjunctions.

5.1.
Non-Constructability of Binary Minima

We cannot expect to be able to constructively calculate general binary minima:
if we could calculate the minimum of
\beta+1
and
\blim f
, then we could check whether this minimum is a successor or a limit, and thus decide if
\beta
is smaller than
\blim f
or not — but we know that
\beta<\blim f
is not decidable in general.
The precise argument that no such minimum function can be constructed is that it would imply
\mathsf{LPO}
, the
limited principle of omniscience
.
Recall that
\mathsf{LPO}
states that the existential quantification of any decidable proposition is again decidable, or, in other words, every semidecidable proposition is decidable: In any binary sequence, there either exists a
1
, or the sequence is constantly
0
.
This is a constructive taboo which is not derivable in type theory, so anything implying it cannot be derivable either.

Proposition 5.2

(

)
.

Assume that, for all ordinals
\alpha
and
\beta
, there exists a minimum.
Then,
\mathsf{LPO}
holds.

Proof.

Let
s
be a binary sequence; we aim to show that
s
is either constantly
0
or it contains the digit
1
.

We choose
\alpha\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\blim{s^{\uparrow}}
and
\beta\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\omega+1
, and assume that
\mu
with the property (
1
) exists.
If
\mu=0
, then
\gamma=1
gives an immediate contradiction.
If
\mu=\mu^{\prime}+1
, we consider the case
\gamma\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\omega+1
and get that
\blim{s^{\uparrow}}\geq\omega+1
(which is equivalent to
s
containing the digit
1
) is logically equivalent to
\mu^{\prime}\geq\omega
, which is decidable.
Finally, if
\mu=\blim t
for some sequence
t
, setting
\gamma
to
\blim t
implies
\omega+1\geq\blim t
and thus
\mu=\omega
. Therefore, the case
\gamma\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\omega+1
shows that
\blim{s^{\uparrow}}\geq\omega+1
leads to a contradiction, which forces
s
to be constantly
0
.
∎

5.2.
Construction of Binary Minima for Limits

While the above
Proposition

5.2

seems like bad news for the conjunction of
\alpha
-decidable propositions,
what helps us is that general minima are not required.
Using
Theorem

4.4
and
Theorem

4.6
, it suffices to define the minimum for zero and limits.

Theorem 5.3

(

)
.

We have binary minima for non-successors: For
\alpha,\beta:\mathsf{Brw}^{zl}
, there is
\mathsf{limMin}\,{\alpha}\,{\beta}:\mathsf{Brw}^{zl}
satisfying (
1
).

Due to the quotient-inductive-inductive construction of
\mathsf{Brw}
,
a rigorous proof of this theorem is more involved than it may seem on paper.
The goal of the current subsection is a sketch of our proof.
Additional type-theoretic difficulties and our solutions are discussed in
Section

9
.
The complete proof is contained in our Agda formalization (linked to above).

The basic strategy is to define the “minimum” function on all pairs of ordinals, i.e. as a function
\mathsf{limMin}:\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Brw}
. The function is correct (calculates minima) if the inputs are non-successors, but necessarily incorrect in general.
The point constructors of the function are given as

\displaystyle\mathsf{limMin}\,{\mathsf{zero}}\,{\beta}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{zero}

\displaystyle\mathsf{limMin}\,{(\bsuc\alpha)}\,{\beta}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{limMin}\,{\alpha}\,{\beta}

\displaystyle\mathsf{limMin}\,{(\blim f)}\,{\mathsf{zero}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{zero}

(2)

\displaystyle\mathsf{limMin}\,{(\blim f)}\,{(\bsuc\beta)}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{limMin}\,{(\blim f)}\,{\beta}

(3)

\displaystyle\mathsf{limMin}\,{(\blim\,f)}\,{(\blim\,g)}

\displaystyle=\blim\,(\lambda\,n.\,(\mathsf{limMin}\,{f_{n}}\,{g_{n}})+n),

which, in order to be accepted by Agda, can be expressed in a relational way (cf.
Section

9
).
We omit the proof that (
3
) does not distinguish between bisimilar sequences and refer to the formalization.
More interesting is the monotonicity of
\mathsf{limMin}
in both arguments, which is not only key for the well-definedness of (
3
), but also for the proof of
Theorem

5.3
:

Lemma 5.4

(

)
.

For all ordinals
\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}
and
\beta_{1}\leq\beta_{2}
, we have
\mathsf{limMin}\,{\alpha_{1}}\,{\beta_{1}}\leq\mathsf{limMin}\,{\alpha_{2}}\,{\beta_{2}}
.

Proof.

We do induction on the four ordinals.
The cases in which one or multiple are
\mathsf{zero}
or successor are easy; for example, if
\blim f\equiv\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}\equiv\bsuc\gamma
, the characterization of
\leq
implies
\blim f\leq\gamma
(cf.
Lemma

2.1
Item

6
), a case that is covered by the induction hypothesis.
The trickiest case occurs when all involved ordinals are limits.
Let us assume that
\alpha_{1}\equiv\blim f^{1}
,
\alpha_{2}\equiv\blim f^{2}
,
\beta_{1}\equiv\blim g^{1}
, and
\beta_{2}\equiv\blim g^{2}
.
The assumption now is that
f^{1}
and
g^{1}
are simulated by
f^{2}
and
g^{2}
, respectively, and we have to show that the sequence
\lambda n.\mathsf{limMin}\,{f^{1}_{n}}\,{g^{1}_{n}}+n
is simulated by the sequence
\lambda m.\mathsf{limMin}\,{f^{2}_{m}}\,{g^{2}_{m}}+m
. Therefore, let us fix
n
; from the assumption, we get
n_{1}
such that
f^{1}_{n}\leq g^{1}_{n_{1}}
, and we get
n_{2}
such that
f^{2}_{n}\leq g^{2}_{n_{2}}
.
We claim that
m\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\max(n,n_{1},n_{2})
fulfills the requirement.
We have
f^{1}_{n}\leq g^{1}_{n_{1}}\leq g^{1}_{m}
as well as
f^{2}_{n}\leq g^{2}_{n_{2}}\leq g^{2}_{m}
. By the induction hypothesis, together with
n\leq m
, we get

\mathsf{limMin}\,{f^{1}_{n}}\,{g^{1}_{n}}+n\leq\mathsf{limMin}\,{f^{2}_{m}}\,{g^{2}_{m}}+m
.
∎

The next major step in the construction is to establish the following properties:

Lemma 5.5

(

)
.

For all
\alpha,\beta:\mathsf{Brw}
, we have:

(1)

\mathsf{limMin}\,{\alpha}\,{\beta}\leq\alpha
,

(2)

\mathsf{limMin}\,{\alpha}\,{\beta}\leq\beta
,

(3)

\mathsf{limMin}\,{\alpha}\,{\alpha}=\dwa\alpha
.

Proof.

The property (
1
) is shown by an induction on
\alpha
and
\beta
, where the only interesting case is when both arguments are limits.
Thus, we want to prove
\mathsf{limMin}\,{(\blim f)}\,{(\blim g)}\leq\blim f
, i.e., for every
n:\mathbb{N}
, there exists
m:\mathbb{N}
such that
(\mathsf{limMin}\,{f_{n}}\,{g_{n})}+n\leq f_{m}
.
By the induction hypothesis, the left-hand side is below
f_{n}+n
, and choosing
m
to be
n+n
fulfills the requirement.

(
2
) is analogous.

Due to the above properties, to conclude property (
3
) it suffices to show
\dwa\alpha\leq\mathsf{limMin}\,{\alpha}\,{\alpha}
.
The least immediate case in the induction is
\alpha\equiv\blim f
.
We need to show that
f
is simulated by the sequence
h\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\lambda m.(\mathsf{limMin}\,{f_{m}}\,{f_{m}})+m
.
Let us fix
n:\mathbb{N}
, and write
f_{n}
as the sum of a zero/limit and a natural number as given by
Lemma

2.3
,
f_{n}=\dwa f_{n}+k
.
We then have:

\displaystyle f_{n}

\displaystyle=\dwa f_{n}+k

\displaystyle\leq\dwa f_{n+k}+(n+k)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{limMin}\,{f_{n+k}}\,{f_{n+k}}+(n+k)

\displaystyle=h_{n+k}.\qed

This allows us to show that we indeed have binary minima restricted to zero and limits:

Proof of
Theorem

5.3
.

We need to show that
\mathsf{limMin}
calculates limits for non-successor arguments
\alpha
and
\beta
, i.e., for every
\gamma:\mathsf{Brw}
, we need to prove

(\gamma\leq\alpha\land\gamma\leq\beta)\longleftrightarrow(\gamma\leq\mathsf{limMin}\,{\alpha}\,{\beta}).

The direction “
\leftarrow
” is a direct consequence of
Lemma

5.5
(
1
) and (
2
).
For the direction “
\rightarrow
,” note that we can restrict ourselves to the case that
\gamma
is zero or a limit due to

Lemma

2.4

Item

1
.
Then, the assumption, together with
Lemma

5.4
, implies
\mathsf{limMin}\,{\gamma}\,{\gamma}\leq\mathsf{limMin}\,{\alpha}\,{\beta}
; this suffices by (
3
) of the previous lemma.
∎

5.3.
Binary Conjunctions

The construction of minima makes it easy to show that
\alpha
-decidable propositions are closed under binary conjunctions:

Theorem 5.6

(

)
.

For any
\alpha:\mathsf{Brw}
, if
P
and
Q
are
\alpha
-decidable propositions, then so is
P\land Q
.

Proof.

As we are proving a proposition, we may assume that we are given
x
and
y
with
P\leftrightarrow\alpha\leq x
and
Q\leftrightarrow\alpha\leq y
.
By
Theorem

4.6
, we can assume that
\alpha
,
x
, and
y
are all limit ordinals (or zero).

Theorem

5.3
now yields
P\land Q\leftrightarrow{\alpha\leq\mathsf{limMin}\,{x}\,{y}}
.
∎

6.
Small Closures under Binary Disjunctions

Having shown that
\alpha
-decidable propositions are closed under binary conjunction, the next step is to study whether they are closed under binary disjunction.
This is the case for decidable and for semidecidable propositions (i.e., for
\alpha=\omega
and
\alpha=\omega\cdot 2
), but can unfortunately not be expected in full generality.
The reason is that no function with a property dual to the one of
\mathsf{limMin}
is definable.
To make precise what is and what is not possible, we define the following refined version of binary maxima:

Definition 6.1

(

Maximum)
.

Given
\alpha,\beta:\mathsf{Brw}
, and a property
P:\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Prop}
of ordinals,
we say that an ordinal
\nu:\mathsf{Brw}
is the maximum of
\alpha
and
\beta
with respect to ordinals satisfying
P
if:

\forall\gamma.P(\gamma)\to\left((\alpha\geq\gamma\lor\beta\geq\gamma)\leftrightarrow\nu\geq\gamma\right).

We show in
Section

6.1
that binary maxima cannot exist globally even for non-successors.
However, these maxima exist with respect to sufficiently small
\alpha
(
Section

6.2
), which means that such
\alpha
-decidable propositions are closed under binary disjunction (
Section

6.3
).

6.1.
Non-Constructability of Binary Maxima

From the assumption that binary maxima exist for arbitrary ordinals, we could derive a similar taboo as in
Proposition

5.2
; we do not demonstrate this here.
What is more interesting is that, unlike for minima, even restricting ourselves to non-successors cannot work:

Proposition 6.2

(

)
.

If binary maxima exist for non-successor ordinals, then
\mathsf{LPO}
holds.

Proof.

As binary maxima are
unique as soon as they exist, the assumption yields a function
{m:\mathsf{Brw}^{zl}\to\mathsf{Brw}^{zl}\to\mathsf{Brw}}
calculating maxima with trivial condition
P\equiv\lambda\delta.\mathbf{1}
.
Fixing
\alpha
and
\beta
in
Definition

6.1
with
\nu=m(\alpha,\beta)
, and setting
\gamma
first to
\alpha
, then to
\beta
, and finally to
m(\alpha,\beta)
gives
\alpha\leq m(\alpha,\beta)
,
\beta\leq m(\alpha,\beta)
, and
(m(\alpha,\beta)\leq\alpha)\lor(m(\alpha,\beta)\leq\beta)
. Putting these together, we get
\beta\leq\alpha\lor\alpha\leq\beta
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbf{2}
be a binary sequence.
In
Definition

3.3
, we defined the sequence
f^{\uparrow}
that “jumps” when a
1
occurs in
f
;
here, we want to “double jump,”
i.e., we replace
\omega
by
\omega\cdot 2
in
Definition

3.3
.
We refer to the resulting sequence as
f^{\Uparrow}
.

From the above, we get
\blim{f^{\Uparrow}}\leq\omega\cdot 2\lor\omega\cdot 2\leq\blim{f^{\Uparrow}}
.
If
\blim{f^{\Uparrow}}\leq\omega\cdot 2
, then
f_{k}=1
leads to a contradiction, so the sequence
f
is constantly
0
.
If
\omega\cdot 2\leq\blim{f^{\Uparrow}}
, then, by
Lemma

2.1
Item

6
, we have
\exists n.\omega\leq{f^{\Uparrow}}_{n}
. In this case, it cannot be the case that
f_{0},\ldots,f_{n-1}
are all
0
, so we can find a
1
by checking these finitely many binary values under the truncation monad.
∎

6.2.
Binary Maxima of Small Ordinals

Notwithstanding the above result,
we can calculate a function that has the universal property of the maximum with respect to ordinals
\alpha
of shape
\omega\cdot k
:

Theorem 6.3

(

)
.

Binary maxima of non-successor ordinals exist with respect to ordinals of the form
\omega\cdot k
.
Spelled out, this means that we can construct a function
\mathsf{limMax}:\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Brw}

such that, for all non-successors
\alpha
,
\beta
and for all
\gamma
such that
\exists k:\mathbb{N}.\gamma=\omega\cdot k
,
we have

(4)

(\alpha\geq\gamma\lor\beta\geq\gamma)\longleftrightarrow\mathsf{limMax}\,{\alpha}\,{\beta}\geq\gamma.

Proof sketch.

Much of the construction of
\mathsf{limMax}
is analogous to that of
\mathsf{limMin}
; it is given in detail in our formalization, and our approach to a technical difficulty related to the usage of a QIIT is discussed in
Section

9
.
The defining equations are

\displaystyle\mathsf{limMax}\,{\mathsf{zero}}\,{\beta}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\beta

\displaystyle\mathsf{limMax}\,{(\bsuc\alpha)}\,{\beta}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\mathsf{limMax}\,{\alpha}\,{\beta}

\displaystyle\mathsf{limMax}\,{(\blim f)}\,{\mathsf{zero}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\blim f

\displaystyle\mathsf{limMax}\,{(\blim f)}\,{(\bsuc\beta)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\mathsf{limMax}\,{(\blim f)}\,{\beta}

\displaystyle\mathsf{limMax}\,{(\blim f)}\,{(\blim g)}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\blim\left(\lambda\,n.(\mathsf{limMax}\,{f_{n}}\,{g_{n}})+n\right).

After we have defined
\mathsf{limMax}
satisfying the equations above, we need to show that it satisfies (
4
).
For the direction
\rightarrow
, it suffices to show
\alpha\leq\mathsf{limMax}\,{\alpha}\,{\beta}
and
\beta\leq\mathsf{limMax}\,{\alpha}\,{\beta}
, which is easy to prove by induction on
\alpha
and
\beta
.

In the assumption
\exists k:\mathbb{N}.\,\gamma=\omega\cdot k
, the natural number
k
is unique,
and we prove the direction
\leftarrow
by induction on
k
.
There is nothing to do for
k=0
.
Next, we assume that
\mathsf{limMax}\,{\alpha}\,{\beta}\geq\omega\cdot(k+1)
.
If one (or both) of
\alpha
,
\beta
are
\mathsf{zero}
, the conclusion is trivial; otherwise, assume
\alpha=\blim f
and
\beta=\blim g
.
By definition of
\mathsf{limMax}
and the property
Lemma

2.1
Item

5
, the assumption implies that there exists an
m:\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathsf{limMax}\,{f_{m}}\,{g_{m}}+m\geq\omega\cdot k
, and by property
Lemma

2.1
Item

6
, we get
\mathsf{limMax}\,{f_{m}}\,{g_{m}}\geq\omega\cdot k
.
The induction hypothesis implies

({f_{m}}\geq\omega\cdot k)\lor({g_{m}}\geq\omega\cdot k).

Because
\blim h\geq h_{m}+\omega
holds for any strictly increasing sequence, this gives

({\blim f}\geq\omega\cdot(k+1))\lor({\blim g}\geq\omega\cdot(k+1)).\qed

6.3.
Binary Disjunction

Although
\mathsf{limMax}
cannot prove general binary disjunction closure by
Proposition

6.2
, it can prove it for
\alpha
of the form
\alpha=\omega\cdot k+n
.
For
\omega\cdot 1
and
\omega\cdot 2
, this is the usual known closure for decidable and semidecidable propositions.

Theorem 6.4

(

)
.

For natural numbers
k
and
n
, the
(\omega\cdot k+n)
-decidable propositions are closed under binary disjunction.

Proof.

By
Theorem

4.4
, we may assume
n=0
.
If
P\leftrightarrow\omega\cdot k\leq x
and
Q\leftrightarrow\omega\cdot k\leq y
, then
we have
P\lor Q\leftrightarrow\omega\cdot k\leq\mathsf{limMax}\,{x}\,{y}

by
Theorem

6.3
.
∎

7.
Semidecidable Families and Quantifiers

Having studied binary meets and joins of
\alpha
-decidable propositions, we get back to the situation discussed in the introduction.
Assume we are given a family
P:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
of semidecidable propositions, for example the twin prime family (
n\mapsto
”there is a twin prime pair above
n
”).
In
Section

7.3
, we show that its infinite meet
\forall i.P_{i}
, which for the twin prime family corresponds to the twin prime conjecture, is
\omega^{2}
-decidable.
In
Section

7.4
, we show that its join
\exists i.P_{i}
is
(\omega\cdot 3)
-decidable, before presenting results for quantifier alterations in
Section

7.5
.

All these results critically depend on the construction of the
characteristic ordinal
of such a family
P
that we explain in
Section

7.1
.

Section

7.2
contains the simple but useful observation that such families can often be assumed to be increasing or decreasing.

7.1.
Characteristic Ordinals of Families of Semidecidable Propositions

Similar to how we combined the information given by two
\alpha
-decidable propositions in order to calculate a new proposition in
Sections

5
and
6
, we now need to express the information contained in a countable family
P
of propositions with a single ordinal.
We restrict ourselves to the case that every
P_{n}
is semidecidable.
A discussion of the more general case of
\alpha
-decidability can be found in
Section

10
.

Concretely, given a sequence
P:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
of semidecidable propositions, we want to construct a single ordinal
\Psi(P)
that, in a certain sense, measures how many of the propositions are true — we call it the
characteristic ordinal

\Psi(P)
of
P
.
It is approximated by
\Psi_{n}(P)
, an ordinal that measures how many of the propositions
P_{0},P_{1},\ldots,P_{n-1}
are true.
If these were assumed to be decidable, we could simply define
\Psi_{n}(P)
to be this number, but, as the
P_{i}
are only assumed to be semidecidable,
the definition of
\Psi_{n}(P)
is more involved.
Afterwards, we can take the limit of a construction involving
\Psi_{n}(P)
in order to define
\Psi(P)
.

Our strategy for the first part is as follows.
Assume we are given
n:\mathbb{N}
, together with a set

\{P_{0},P_{1},\dots,P_{n-1}\}
of propositions. We want to construct a function

\Psi_{n}:\left(\Pi_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}\mathsf{isSemiDec}(P_{i})\right)\to\mathsf{Brw}.

To do this, we take several steps:

(1)

First, we construct a similar function assuming we have access to the data contained in the semidecidability proofs.
Recall that
\mathsf{isSemiDec}(Q)
is defined to mean

\displaystyle\mathsf{isSemiDec}(Q)\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\exists s:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbf{2}.\,Q\leftrightarrow\exists i.s_{i}=1;

by its
data
, we mean the non-truncated version of the same type,

\displaystyle\mathsf{SemiDec}(Q)\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\Sigma s:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbf{2}.\,Q\leftrightarrow\exists i.s_{i}=1.

Concretely, we construct a function

\Psi^{+}_{n}:\left(\Pi_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}\mathsf{SemiDec}(P_{i})\right)\to\mathsf{Brw}.

(2)

In the second step, we then prove that
\Psi^{+}_{n}
is weakly constant, meaning that it maps any pair of inputs to equal outputs.
As the codomain is a set, this implies that
\Psi^{+}_{n}
factors through the propositional truncation
[
24
]
.
We therefore get:

\Psi^{*}_{n}:\left\|\Pi_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}\mathsf{SemiDec}(P_{i})\right\|\to\mathsf{Brw}.

(3)

Using
finite choice
, i.e. the
n
-fold application of the equivalence
\|A\times B\|\simeq\|A\|\times\|B\|
, we get the equivalence

\mathsf{FC}_{n}:\left(\Pi_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}\mathsf{isSemiDec}(P_{i})\right)\to\left\|\Pi_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}\mathsf{SemiDec}(P_{i})\right\|

Then,
\Psi_{n}
is the composition of
\Psi^{*}_{n}
with
\mathsf{FC}_{n}
.

The details for the first two steps are as follows.

Construction of
\Psi^{+}_{n}
:

Suppose, for each
i<n
, we are given a Boolean sequence
s^{i}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbf{2}
that semidecides
P_{i}
.
We replace each sequence
s^{i}
by an increasing sequence
t^{i}
that is defined by
t^{i}_{0}\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}s^{i}_{0}
and
t^{i}_{n+1}\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}t^{i}_{n}\lor s^{i}_{n+1}
; in other words,
t^{i}
is the “upwards normalization” of
s^{i}
.
Let
t:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be the pointwise sum of the
t^{i}
; i.e.,
t_{k}\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}t^{0}_{k}+t^{1}_{k}+\ldots+t^{n-1}_{k}
, where we implicitly convert
\mathsf{false}
to 0 and
\mathsf{true}
to 1.

The construction ensures that the sequence
t
is (weakly) increasing. We set

(5)

\Psi^{+}_{n}:=\,\blim(\lambda k.\omega\cdot t_{k}+k),

where the final “
{\!}+k
” ensures that the constructor takes a
strictly
increasing sequence as an argument.

Weak constancy of
\Psi^{+}_{n}
:

For the argument that
\Psi^{+}_{n}
is weakly constant, the following observation is useful:

Lemma 7.1

(

)
.

If
(s,q)
and
(s^{\prime},q^{\prime})
are semidecidability data for the same proposition
P
, then
s
and
s^{\prime}
are bisimilar, i.e., we have
\forall k.\,\exists m.\,s_{k}\leq s^{\prime}_{m}
and
\forall k.\,\exists m.\,s^{\prime}_{k}\leq s_{m}
.

Proof.

By symmetry, it suffices to show that
s
is simulated by
s^{\prime}
.
Let
k:\mathbb{N}
be given.
If
s_{k}=0
, the statement is trivial.
If
s_{k}=1
,
then
P
holds by
q
.
From
q^{\prime}
, we therefore get
\exists m.{s^{\prime}}_{m}=1
.
∎

Lemma 7.2

(

)
.

The function
\Psi^{+}_{n}
is weakly constant, i.e.
\forall s,\overline{s}.\,\Psi^{+}_{n}(s)=\Psi^{+}_{n}(\overline{s})
.

Proof.

Assume that, for each
i<n
, we have two sets of sequences
s^{i},{\overline{s}}^{i}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbf{2}
that both semidecide
P_{i}
.
Also, assume their normalized-up sequences are
t^{i}
,
{\overline{t}}^{i}
, and according to the second step of the previous construction, these two define functions
t,\overline{t}:\mathbb{N}\to\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
.
This gives us sequences
T,\overline{T}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Brw}
, defined by
T_{k}\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\omega\cdot t_{k}+k
and
\overline{T}_{k}\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\omega\cdot\overline{t}_{k}+k
. To prove that
\Psi^{+}_{n}
is weakly constant, we need to show that
T
simulates
\overline{T}
(and the symmetric statement, which is analogous).
Therefore, our goal is to show:

\forall k.\,\exists m.\,\omega\cdot t_{k}+k\leq\omega\cdot\overline{t}_{m}+m

For this, it suffices to show

\forall k.\,\exists m.\,t_{k}\leq\overline{t}_{m},

which is straightforward using
Lemma

7.1
.
∎

This completes the construction of
\Psi_{n}
.

Definition 7.3

(

Characteristic ordinal of a sequence)
.

Given a sequence of semidecidable propositions
P:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
,
the above construction gives us a sequence
n\mapsto\overline{\Psi_{n}}(P)
, where
\overline{\Psi_{n}}(P):\mathsf{Brw}
is the function applied to the assumed proof of semidecidability.
This sequence is (weakly) increasing by construction.
We define the
characteristic ordinal of
P
, written
\Psi(P)
, to be

\Psi(P)\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\blim(\lambda n.\,\overline{\Psi_{n}}(P)+n).

7.2.
Normalizing Families

The following observation tells us that we can assume that the family
P
is downwards (or upwards) closed, which will be a useful technical device:

Lemma 7.4

(

)
.

Let
P:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
be a family of propositions.

(1)

If, for some ordinal

, each
P_{i}
is
\alpha
-decidable, then we can construct a family

of
\alpha
-decidable propositions such that
\forall k.Q_{k+1}\to Q_{k}
, and such that
\forall k.P_{k}\leftrightarrow\forall k.Q_{k}
.

(2)

If, for some natural numbers

and

is
(\omega\cdot k+n)
-decidable,
then we can construct a family

.

In the first case, we define

.

.
In the second case, we set

.
∎

7.3.
Countable Meets of Semidecidable Propositions

Our first application of the characteristic ordinal
\Psi(P)
is the case of countable meets:

Theorem 7.5

(

)
.

Let
P:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
be a family of semidecidable propositions.
Then
\forall n.\,P_{n}
is
\omega^{2}
-decidable.

To give an example, the twin prime conjecture (cf. the introduction) is an instance of this theorem, and thus
\omega^{2}
-decidable.

In order to prove
Theorem

7.5
, we need several additional observations about the characteristic ordinal
\Psi(P)
and its approximations
\overline{\Psi_{n}}(P)
.
For the rest of this subsection, assume
P:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
is a given family of semidecidable propositions.

Lemma 7.6

(

Key Lemma)
.

We have that

\exists n.\,\omega\cdot 2\leq\overline{\Psi_{n}}(P)
if and only if
\exists m.\,P_{m}
.

Proof.

The following arguments rely heavily on the fact that the respective goals are propositions, which allows us to replace
\exists
by
\Sigma
several (but finitely many) times.

We first prove the left to right direction. We can assume that we are given an
n
such that
\omega\cdot 2\leq\overline{\Psi_{n}}(P)
.
If we unfold the latter expression using the notation and construction of
Section

7.1
, and especially recall (
5
),
this assumption becomes

\omega\cdot 2\leq\blim(\lambda k.\omega\cdot t_{k}+k).

Using
Lemma

2.1
Item

7
, this implies that there is
k
such that
\omega+1\leq\omega\cdot t_{k}+k
, which implies
t_{k}\geq 1
.
This allows us to check what the least
m
is such that
t_{m}\geq 1
, and the construction ensures that
P_{m}
holds.

For the right to left direction,
assume we have
m
such that
P_{m}
holds.
This implies that the sequence witnessing the semidecidability of
P_{m}
contains the digit
1
, and unfolding the construction, this implies

\overline{\Psi_{m+1}}(P)\geq\omega\cdot 2
.
∎

For a given natural number
k
, the sequence
\lambda i.\,P_{i+k}
is the sequence
P
without the first
k
entries.
By construction, we trivially have
\Psi(\lambda i.\,P_{i+k})\leq\Psi(P)
.
The other direction does not hold in general, but we have the following:

Lemma 7.7

(

)
.

For any natural number
k
, we have

\Psi(P)\geq\omega^{2}\textperiodcentered\;\rightarrow\;\Psi(\lambda i.\,P_{i+k})\geq\omega^{2}.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the lemma for
k=1
, as the general case follows by finite iteration.
By construction, for all natural numbers
n
, we have

(6)

\overline{\Psi_{n}}(P)\leq\overline{\Psi_{n}}(\lambda i.\,P_{i+1})+\omega.

We assume
\Psi(P)\geq\omega^{2}
.
For each
m
, by
Lemma

2.1
Item

7
applied to the index
m+1
, there exists an
n
such that
\overline{\Psi_{n}}(P)+n\geq\omega\cdot(m+1)
.
Combining this with (
6
) gives

{{\overline{\Psi_{n}}(\lambda i.\,P_{i+1})}+{\omega}+{n}}\geq{{\omega\cdot m}+\omega}
.
Using
Lemma

2.1
Item

6
and cancellation of addition with
\omega
(a consequence of
Lemma

2.1
(
6
)–(
7
)),
we get
\overline{\Psi_{n}}(\lambda i.\,P_{i+1})\geq\omega\times m
,
which means that the sequence
m\mapsto\omega\times m
is simulated by the sequence
n\mapsto\overline{\Psi_{n}}(\lambda i.\,P_{i+1})+n
as required.
∎

Next we observe that, if we know whether
P_{0}
to
P_{n}
hold, we can calculate explicitly what
\overline{\Psi_{n}}(P)
is.
We only require the following special case:

Lemma 7.8

(

)
.

Assume that
P
is downwards closed and
P_{n}
holds. Then, we have
\overline{\Psi_{n}}(P)=\omega\cdot(n+1)
.

Proof.

Since
P
is downwards closed, all
P_{i}
(with
i\leq n
) are true, and their corresponding sequences contain the digit
1
.
Tracking the definition of
\Psi
, we get the claimed equation.
∎

With the above lemmas at hand, we are ready to prove that countable meets of semidecidable propositions are
\omega^{2}
-decidable:

Proof of
Theorem

7.5
.

By
Lemma

7.4
, we can assume that the sequence
P:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
is downwards closed, i.e., that
P_{k+1}\to P_{k}
for each
k
.
We claim

(7)

\forall i.P_{i}\longleftrightarrow\Psi(P)\geq\omega^{2},

which shows that
\forall i.P_{i}
is
\omega^{2}
-decidable.

The direction “
\to
” is directly implied by
Lemma

7.8
.
For the direction “
\leftarrow
”, we assume
\Psi(P)\geq\omega^{2}
.
For an arbitrary natural number
n
, we need to show that
P_{n}
holds.
By
Lemma

7.7
,
we have
\Psi(\lambda i.\,P_{i+n})\geq\omega^{2}
,
and
Lemma

2.1
Item

7
implies that there exists a
j
such that

\overline{\Psi_{j}}(\lambda i.\,P_{i+n})+j\geq\omega\cdot 2
and therefore, by
Lemma

2.1
Item

6
,

\overline{\Psi_{j}}(\lambda i.\,P_{i+n})\geq\omega\cdot 2
.

Lemma

7.6
shows that there exists
l
such that
P_{l+n}
holds, and, as
P
is downwards closed, this means that
P_{n}
holds.
∎

Immediate consequences are the following:

Corollary 7.9

(

)
.

The countable meet of a family of decidable propositions is
\omega^{2}
-decidable.
The negation of a semidecidable proposition is also
\omega^{2}
-decidable.

Proof.

For the first statement, observe that every decidable proposition is also semidecidable; and for the second, note that the negation of a semidecidable proposition is true if and only if all digits in the sequence are
0
.
∎

7.4.
Countable Joins of Semidecidable Propositions

Having treated countable meets, the next case of interest are countable joins, in the following form:

Theorem 7.10

(

)
.

Let
P:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
be a family of semidecidable propositions. Then,
\exists n.\,P_{n}
is
(\omega\cdot 3)
-decidable.

Proof.

We show

\Psi(P)\geq\omega\cdot 3\,\leftrightarrow\,\exists n.\,P_{n}.

Note that the left-hand side is equivalent to
\exists k.\,\overline{\Psi_{k}}(P)\geq\omega\cdot 2
: Any sequence containing an element above
\omega\cdot 2
has a limit of at least
\omega\cdot 3
, and
Lemma

2.1
Item

7
together with
Lemma

2.1
Item

6
shows the other direction.
The statement is therefore given by
Lemma

7.6
.
∎

7.5.
Quantifier Alternations

Having discussed the decidability of
\forall i.\,P_{i}
and
\exists i.\,P_{i}
for semidecidable families,
a natural next step is to study more general formulas of the arithmetical hierarchy, i.e. multiple quantifiers and quantifier alterations.
We do not know whether the general case has a satisfactory solution;
here, we only present a special case that generalizes the decidability question of the search for counter-examples of relatively complicated problems.

As a preparation, we record the following observation that straightforwardly follows from the construction of the characteristic ordinal in
Section

7.1
.

Lemma 7.11

(

)
.

For two sequences of semidecidable propositions
P,Q:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
with
\forall n.\,P_{n}\to Q_{n}
, we have
\Psi(P)\leq\Psi(Q)
. ∎

This allows us to show:

Theorem 7.12

(

)
.

If
P:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
is a family of semidecidable propositions such that, for all
m
and
n
, we have
P(n,m)\to P(n,m+1)
, then
\exists m.\,\forall n.\,P(n,m)
is
(\omega^{2}+\omega)
-decidable.

Proof.

For every
m:\mathbb{N}
we have, by the construction of (
7
), that
\forall n.\,P(n,m)
is logically equivalent to
\Psi(\lambda n.\,P(n,m))\geq\omega^{2}
.
By the upwards closure assumption of the theorem statement and
Lemma

7.11
,
we get that the sequence

m\mapsto\Psi(\lambda n.\,P(n,m))+m

is increasing, and the summand “
{\!}+m
” ensures that it is
strictly
increasing.
If we write
\lambda
for the limit of this sequence, it is easy to observe that

\lambda\geq\omega^{2}+\omega\,\longleftrightarrow\,\exists m.\,\forall n.\,P(n,m),

which proves the theorem.
∎

As an instance of this, we see that the existence of a counterexample for the twin prime conjecture is
(\omega^{2}+\omega)
-decidable.
Indeed, if we write
P(n,m)
to mean that
{n\geq m}
implies that not both the numbers
n
and
n+2
are prime, then a counterexample to the twin prime conjecture consists of a number
m
such that
\forall n.\,P(n,m)
. Note that the proposition
P(n,m)
is decidable and therefore semidecidable, and that it is easy to check that
P(n,m)\to P(n,m+1)
holds.

8.
Ordinal Decidability and Countable Choice

In the previous section, we showed in
Theorem

7.10
that the countable join of semidecidable propositions is
(\omega\cdot 3)
-decidable.
Without further assumptions, it does not seem possible to show that this join is semidecidable, as such a closure property would imply
Escardó-Knapp choice

[
12
,
11
]
.
3
3

3

This should not be confused with a related principle also called Escardó-Knapp choice (EKC) as introduced by Andrew Swan in two talks from 2019
[
33
,
32
]
.

However, it is well-known that this closure property holds in the presence of
countable choice
, a principle which says that, for any type family
B:\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{U}
, we have

\left(\forall n:\mathbb{N}.\,\|B(n)\|\right)\,\to\,\|\Pi(n:\mathbb{N}).\,B(n)\|;

in other words, we can commute the propositional truncation with
\forall n
.
We sketch the argument in
Lemma

8.1
below.
In
Section

8.1
, we present further consequences of countable choice for the hierarchy of decidability.
Afterwards, in
Section

8.2
, we discuss
Sierpiński-semidecidability
, an alternative definition of semidecidability that is closed under countable joins without the assumption of a choice principle, and its connection with the notions studied in this paper.

8.1.
Consequences of Countable Choice

The following is Theorem 4 of Escardó and Knapp
[
12
]
.

Lemma 8.1

(

)
.

Assuming countable choice, the countable join of semidecidable propositions is semidecidable.

Proof sketch.

If there exists a binary sequence
s_{n}
for every natural number
n
, then countable choice implies that there exists a sequence
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbf{2}
that, when composed with any bijection
\mathbb{N}\cong\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
, gives a binary sequence that semidecides the infinite join.
∎

From the point of view of
Theorem

7.10
, the above lemma says that certain
(\omega\cdot 3)
-decidable propositions are semidecidable.
This result can be strengthened: assuming countable choice, not only
(\omega\cdot 3)
-decidable, but in fact all
(\omega\cdot k)
-decidable propositions are semidecidable.

The following auxiliary statement follows directly by unfolding and substituting:

Lemma 8.2

(

)
.

Let
\alpha,\beta:\mathsf{Brw}
be given. If, for all ordinals
x
, the proposition
x\geq\alpha
is
\beta
-decidable, then every
\alpha
-decidable proposition is
\beta
-decidable. ∎

This implies:

Theorem 8.3

(

)
.

Assuming countable choice, every
(\omega\cdot k)
-decidable proposition is semidecidable.

Proof.

We show by induction on
k
that, for all
x
, the proposition
x\geq\omega\cdot k
is semidecidable,
which implies the theorem by
Lemma

8.2
.
The claimed property is trivial for
k=0
(
0
-decidable propositions are true),
k=1
(
\omega
-decidable propositions are decidable), and
k=2
(
(\omega\cdot 2)
-decidable propositions are semidecidable).
Assume it holds for
k
.
We show by induction on
x
that
x\geq{{\omega\cdot k}+\omega}
is semidecidable.
The only interesting case is when
x
is a limit,
x=\blim f
. We have:

\displaystyle\phantom{\leftrightarrow\,\,}\blim f\geq\omega\cdot k+\omega

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\forall n.\,\exists m.\,f_{m}\geq\omega\cdot k+n

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\exists m.\,f_{m}\geq\omega\cdot k,

where the first step holds by
Lemma

2.1
Item

7
and the second because, if
f_{m}\geq{\omega\cdot k}
, then
f_{m+n}\geq{f_{m}+n}\geq{{\omega\cdot k}+n}
by strict monotonicity of
f
.
The last line is the countable join of propositions that are semidecidable by the induction hypothesis, making it semidecidable by
Lemma

8.1
.
∎

However, it is not the case that the whole hierarchy collapses, as
\omega^{2}
-decidable propositions are indeed distinguishable from
(\omega\cdot k)
-decidable ones.
Recall that
Markov’s principle
, written
\mathsf{MP}
, states that if the binary sequence
s:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbf{2}
is not constantly
0
, then there exists an index
i
such that
s_{i}=1
.
This is strictly weaker than
\mathsf{LPO}
.
In particular, there are models where both Countable Choice and
\mathsf{MP}
hold, but
\mathsf{LPO}
does not
[
22
]
, which means that the following result shows that Countable Choice is not sufficient to prove that
\omega^{2}
-decidable propositions are semidecidable.

Theorem 8.4

(

)
.

Assume every
\omega^{2}
-decidable proposition is semidecidable.
Then,
\mathsf{MP}
implies
\mathsf{LPO}
.

Proof.

\mathsf{LPO}
is equivalent to the statement that every semidecidable proposition is decidable; thus, assume
P
is semidecidable.
By
Corollary

7.9
,
\neg P
is
\omega^{2}
-decidable, and thus, by the assumption, it is semidecidable.
\mathsf{MP}
implies that a proposition is decidable when it and its negation are both semidecidable.
∎

Our next observation is that, under countable choice, every
\omega^{2}
-decidable proposition can be assumed to be a countable meet of semidecidable propositions:

Theorem 8.5

(

)
.

Let
P
be
\omega^{2}
-decidable. Assuming countable choice, there exists a family
Q:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
of semidecidable propositions such that
P\leftrightarrow\forall n.Q_{n}
.

Proof.

By assumption, there exists an
x
such that
P\leftrightarrow x\geq\omega^{2}
, and by
Theorem

4.6
, we can assume that
x
is a limit,
x=\blim f
for some
f
.
By the characterisation
Lemma

2.1
Item

7
, the expression
\blim f\geq\omega^{2}
is equivalent to
\forall k.\,\exists m.\,f_{m}\geq{\omega\cdot k}
, and since
\exists m.\,f_{m}\geq\omega\cdot k
is semidecidable by
Theorem

8.3
, this expression is of the claimed form.
∎

A consequence is that
\omega^{2}
-decidable propositions are closed under countable meets:

Theorem 8.6

(

)
.

Let
P:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
be a family of
\omega^{2}
-decidable propositions.
Assuming countable choice,
\forall n.\,P_{n}
is
\omega^{2}
-decidable.

Proof.

By the previous result, for each
n
, there exists a family
Q^{n}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
of semidecidable propositions such that
P_{n}\leftrightarrow\forall i.\,Q^{n}_{i}
.
Using countable choice, we can formulate this as the existence of a family
Q:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
.
Composed with any bijection
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\cong\mathbb{N}
, this gives a family
\overline{Q}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
of semidecidable propositions with the property
\forall n.\,P_{n}\leftrightarrow\forall n.\,\overline{Q}_{n}
. The right-hand side is
\omega^{2}
-decidable by
Theorem

7.5
.
∎

8.2.
Avoiding Countable Choice with Sierpiński Semidecidability

If one wants to study a notion of semidecidability that is closed under countable joins without the assumption of countable choice, one can consider the following:

Definition 8.7

(

Sierpiński type)
.

The
Sierpiński type
, denoted by
\mathcal{S}
, is the free
\omega
-complete partial order on the unit type
[
36
,
8
]
.
It can be implemented as a higher inductive type with the constructors
\top
,
\bot
, and
\bigvee:(\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{S})\to\mathcal{S}
, together with the expected equations.

Alternatively,
\mathcal{S}
can be defined as the initial
\sigma
-frame.
It is a sub-
\sigma
-frame of
\mathsf{Prop}
, and the canonical function
\mathcal{S}\to\mathsf{Prop}
is given by
s\mapsto s=\top
; see Escardó
[
15
]
for more details.
The induced notion of semidecidability has previously been used by Gilbert
[
16
]
and goes back to
[
14
, p. 53]
.

Definition 8.8

(

Sierpiński-semidecidability)
.

A proposition
P
is said to be
Sierpiński-semidecidable
if

\Sigma s:\mathcal{S}.\,P\leftrightarrow s=\top.

Note that even though formulated with
\Sigma
rather than
\exists
, the type of Sierpiński-semidecidability witnesses is still a proposition, since for
s,t:\mathcal{S}
, we have
s=t
if and only if
s=\top\leftrightarrow t=\top
.
The following is standard:

Lemma 8.9

(

)
.

Assuming countable choice, semidecidability and Sierpiński-semidecidability coincide. ∎

One direction holds without assuming countable choice:

Lemma 8.10

(

)
.

If a proposition
P
is semidecidable, then it is also Sierpiński-semidecidable.

Proof.

Given a binary sequence, we replace all occurrences of
0
by
\bot
, and
1
by
\top
.
The countable join of this sequence is the required element of
\mathcal{S}
.
∎

However, the following
shows that Sierpiński-semidecidability is weaker than semidecidability, because the analogous statement for semidecidability would imply Escardó-Knapp choice:

Proposition 8.11

(

)
.

If a proposition is
(\omega\cdot n+k)
-decidable, then it is Sierpiński-semidecidable.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the proposition
\alpha\geq\omega\cdot n+k
is Sierpiński-decidable.
By comparing the finite part of
\alpha
with
k
, this inequality can be seen to either be equivalent to
\alpha\geq\omega\cdot n
or to
\alpha\geq\omega\cdot(n+1)
, which means that we can freely assume
k=0
.
Therefore, it suffices to construct a function
s:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathcal{S}
, where we write
s\,n\,\alpha
as
s_{n}(\alpha)
, with the property

(8)

s_{n}(\alpha)=\top\,\longleftrightarrow\,\alpha\geq\omega\cdot n.

As the proposition
\alpha\geq\omega\cdot n
is decidable for
n=0
and
n=1
,
we can simply define
s_{n}(\alpha)
to be
\bot
or
\top
accordingly in these cases.
Otherwise, we do induction on
\alpha
:

\displaystyle s_{n+2}(\mathsf{zero})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\bot

\displaystyle s_{n+2}(\bsuc\beta)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}s_{n+2}(\beta)

\displaystyle s_{n+2}(\blim f)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vcentcolon\mspace{-1mu}\equiv}\bigvee_{i:\mathbb{N}}s_{n+1}(f_{i}).

It is easy to see that bisimilar sequences are mapped to equal values.
The property (
8
) follows from the construction of
\mathcal{S}
as the initial
\sigma
-frame; for example, we have

\displaystyle s_{n+2}(\blim f)=\top\;

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\;\left(\bigvee_{i:\mathbb{N}}s_{n+1}(f_{i})\right)=\top

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\;\exists i.\,s_{n+1}(f_{i})=\top

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\;\exists i.\,f_{i}\geq\omega\cdot(n+1)

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\;\blim f\geq\omega\cdot(n+2).\qed

9.
On the Agda Formalization

Overall, the formalization in Agda closely follows the paper development,
although we consider two techniques to be worth commenting on.

9.1.
A Relational Approach to Maps out of HITs

As discussed in
Section

5.2
, the construction of the

\mathsf{limMin}
function is subtle.
The point is that
\mathsf{Brw}
is a quotient inductive-inductive type, so
that, besides defining
\mathsf{limMin}
on point constructors, we also need
to define its action on the two path constructors for bisimilarity and
set truncation.

In order to get a well-defined map, it moreover becomes necessary to
simultaneously prove that
\mathsf{limMin}
is monotone, so that we must carry out a
mutually recursive construction.
The monotonicity proof of
\mathsf{limMin}
also requires dealing with path
constructors, and this becomes combinatorially involved, since
\mathsf{limMin}
takes
two arguments, so that monotonicity in both arguments leads us to consider four
arguments of type
\mathsf{Brw}
.
If two of these arguments are limits and two of them arise from the
\mathsf{bisim}

path constructor, then we must fill a square (as there are two interval
variables arising from the two path constructors). Similarly, if three, resp. four of these arguments arise from the
\mathsf{bisim}
constructor, then we must fill
a three-, resp. four-dimensional cube. Moreover, there are over three dozen
cases that involve path constructors, making this approach intractable.

It is natural to ask whether we cannot dispel of these path constructor cases by
appealing to the fact that type expressing monotonicity of
\mathsf{limMin}
is a
proposition, so that any cubes can always be filled.
The problem with this idea is that we could not get it to pass Agda’s
termination checker.

The alternative approach that we ended up adopting is to first define a

relation
, prove it to be single-valued and total, and to extract a
function only at the very end.
The idea that inspired this approach is that all our trouble is caused by path
constructors, so that we simply define a relation on point constructors only,
which, as a type family, automatically respects equality (and hence path
constructors).
Relational approaches to recursive functions have been considered before, see
e.g. Bove and Capretta
[
5
]
, and what we describe here may be taken as a
illustrative example of how it is beneficial for recursive functions
on higher inductive types too.

Specifically, we define, for
x,y,z:\mathsf{Brw}
, a type
\mathsf{limMin}[x,y]{\sim}z
and
simultaneously prove that this relation is monotone in both arguments, i.e. if
x\leq x^{\prime}
and
y\leq y^{\prime}
, then

(

9)

\mathsf{limMin}[x,y]{\sim}z\text{ and }\mathsf{limMin}[x^{\prime},y^{\prime}]{\sim}z^{\prime}\text{ implies }z\leq z^{\prime}.

The intended reading of
\mathsf{limMin}[x,y]{\sim}z
is that this type is inhabited precisely
when
z=\mathsf{limMin}\,{x}\,{y}
.

Now,
\mathsf{limMin}[x,y]{\sim}z
is defined as an inductive type family, where
would-be recursive function calls are recorded as hypotheses. For example, since
(
2
) specifies that

\mathsf{limMin}\,{(\blim\,f)}\,{(\bsuc\,y)}=\mathsf{limMin}\,{(\blim\,f)}\,{y},

we add a
constructor

lim-succ

\displaystyle:(f:\mathbb{N}\xrightarrow{<}\mathsf{Brw})\,(y,z:\mathsf{Brw})

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{limMin}[\blim f,y]{\sim}z\to\mathsf{limMin}[\blim f,\bsuc y]{\sim}z.

In total,
\mathsf{limMin}[x,y]{\sim}z
has only six constructors. We consider the cases
where the first argument is zero, a successor, or a limit. Only in the latter
case do we further case split on the second argument. Finally, we add a
truncation constructor that makes
\mathsf{limMin}[x,y]{\sim}z
proposition-valued.

As mentioned, we simultaneously prove that this relation is monotone and we note
that, in contrast to the direct functional approach discussed earlier, this does

not
require us to deal with any complicated path constructors.
This completes the mutually inductive part of the construction.

A convenient and direct consequence of monotonicity is that the relation is
single-valued: if
\mathsf{limMin}[x,y]{\sim}z
and
\mathsf{limMin}[x,y]{\sim}z^{\prime}
both hold, then

z=z^{\prime}
.
Hence, for any two
x,y:\mathsf{Brw}
, the type
\Sigma z:\mathsf{Brw}.\,\mathsf{limMin}[x,y]{\sim}z

is a proposition.

Totality of the relation expresses that we have an element of this type for any
x,y:\mathsf{Brw}
.
Thanks to the fact that the type is a proposition we can prove this inductively
by
only
considering point constructors for
x
and
y
, and this is
easy thanks to the constructors of
\mathsf{limMin}[x,y]{\sim}z
.

In summary, the type
\Sigma z:\mathsf{Brw}.\,\mathsf{limMin}[x,y]{\sim}z
is contractible for
every
x,y:\mathsf{Brw}
, and the first projection of the type yields a function

\mathsf{limMin}:\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Brw}\to\mathsf{Brw}
that moreover has the right computational
behaviour, e.g.

\mathsf{limMin}\,{(\blim\,f)}\,{(\bsuc\,y)}=\mathsf{limMin}\,{(\blim\,f)}\,{y}

holds definitionally, as well as all other expected equations. This is obviously convenient in the formalization.

9.2.
Properties of Maps out of Truncated Types

Recall that in
Section

7.1
we define a function out of a
truncated type via a weak constancy argument.
Abstracting away from specifics, the situation is as depicted in the following
commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\|A\|}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces|-|}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}

When proving a property of values of
g
, we may assume to have an actual
element
a
of
A
and prove the relevant property for
f\,a
instead.
Rather than repeating this setup for various properties, we state, prove and
appeal to a “meta”-lemma: for any proposition-valued family
P
over
B
,
we have
P(g\,t)
for all
t:\|A\|
, as soon as
P(f\,a)
holds for
all
a:A
.
One can similarly define and prove a binary version for families indexed over

B\times B
, which in the setting of
Section

7.1
allows
one to easily prove a monotonicity property for the map out of the truncated type.

In the formalization, this meta-lemma for the the characteristic ordinal
construction is called

characteristic-ordinal-up-to-reduction-lemma

(with its
binary version having a subscript
2
).

10.
Conclusions

We have introduced and studied the notion of
\alpha
-decidability, which generalizes existing notions.
We view this paper as the first step towards a theory of generalized decidability, and at the moment, many questions remain open.

An important problem is the question of upwards closure, which we have not discussed.
If
\alpha\leq\beta
, then the statement that
P
is
\alpha
-decidable should, at least intuitively, be weaker than the statement that
P
is
\beta
-decidable; phrased differently, an
\alpha
-decidable proposition could be expected to be
\beta
-decidable.
We do not expect that this is true in the stated full generality;
however, we are optimistic that it is true if
\alpha
and
\beta
are in Cantor Normal Form below
\epsilon_{0}
.
We have proofs for several special cases of this conjecture, such as an argument for the fact that every
(\omega\cdot k)
-decidable proposition is
(\omega\cdot(k+1))
-decidable, which generalizes the observation that every decidable proposition is semidecidable.
Similarly, we can show that every
\omega^{k}
-decidable proposition is
\omega^{k+1}
-decidable.

A further question is whether it is possible to calculate implications between propositions:
If
P
is
\alpha
-decidable and
Q
is
\beta
-decidable, what can be said about the proposition
P\to Q
?
The important special case that arises when
Q
is empty is partially answered by
Corollary

7.9
, which says that the negation of a semidecidable proposition is
\omega^{2}
-decidable.

In
Section

7
, we work with a family
P:\mathbb{N}\to\mathsf{Prop}
of semidecidable propositions.
While this might very well be the most interesting case, more general statements would involve families of
\alpha
-decidable propositions.
We do not know what can be said in the general case, but if one could, for example, show that taking countable join raises the “decidability level” by
\omega
, then this would give a more direct argument for
Theorem

7.12
.

A deeper but justified question is whether the type
\mathsf{Brw}
or Brouwer trees is the best possible notion of ordinals for this theory.
As discussed in
Section

8.2
, switching from binary sequences (semidecidability) to the initial
\sigma
-frame (Sierpiński-semidecidability) makes it possible to avoid the assumption of countable choice in certain situations.
We have not managed to formulate a variation of
\mathsf{Brw}
with the same property, but it is possible that such a variation exists.
