Influencing mechanism of miners’ safety citizenship behavior from the perspective of the theory of planned behavior
Authors: Jianping Yang, Jinxu Fang, Linzhi Xie, Qing Liu, Huilian Li, Caiyun Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Unsafe behaviors among miners constitute a key factor associated with the occurrence of safety accidents. Drawing on the theory of planned behavior (TPB), this study constructs a hypothetical model to investigate the factors associated with miners’ safety citizenship behavior (SCB). Data on subjective norms (SNs), perceived behavioral control (PBC), SCB intention (SCBI), SCB, and sense of safety responsibility (SSR) were collected through questionnaires, with a valid sample size of 1334. Structural equation modeling and multivariate statistical analysis were employed to test the proposed hypotheses. The findings show the following: (1) Personal factors are associated with behavioral attitudes, a sense of safety responsibility is related to subjective norms, and the organizational safety climate is significantly associated with perceived behavioral control. (2) Miners’ behavioral attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control are directly and positively associated with SCBI, and SCBI is related to actual SCB. Moreover, perceived behavioral control is directly and significantly associated with actual behavior. (3) The results of the mediation effect test indicate that all the hypothetical paths included in the research model are associated with significant mediating associations and that the corresponding mediating relationships of each path are effectively supported. (4) Multigroup analysis reveals that perceived behavioral control is not significantly associated with SCB among the Sichuan group. These results extend the application of TPB to the field of miner safety management. The proposed strategies can be implemented by strengthening safety training, enhancing skill training, conducting job-specific safety responsibility warning education, establishing a full-staff cogovernance norm system and optimizing operational conditions. These measures may be conducive to more positive behavioral attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control among miners, which could further contribute to stronger safety citizenship awareness, more active safety citizenship behavior, and fewer mine accidents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-50086-0.

Introduction

In recent years, coal mine safety has remained a crucial challenge in China. According to the National Mine Safety Administration, a total of 168 coal mine accidents occurred nationwide in 2022, resulting in 245 fatalities. In 2023, there were 443 fatalities, with the death rate per million tons substantially increasing to 0.094. The first quarter of 2024 alone had 17 accidents, resulting in 39 deaths. Miners’ unsafe behaviors are closely linked to the occurrence of coal mine accidents1. Typical examples include the fatal gas explosion accident at the Jinshangkou Coal Mine in Yongchuan District, Chongqing, and the extralarge collapse accident at the Xinjing Open-pit Coal Mine in Alxa League, Inner Mongolia, both in 2023. Inadequate individual safety behavioral competence is closely associated with miners’ unsafe behaviors2.

The individual safety behavior capacity of miners is manifested in two aspects: safety compliance behavior and safety citizenship behavior (SCB)3. Safety compliance behavior merely emphasizes whether workers adhere to various rules and regulations but overlooks the importance of guiding and encouraging workers to implement safety intentions and enthusiasm in their work practices. SCB refers to the voluntary individual actions taken by onsite personnel to ensure the safety performance of other team members and the project organization’s overall safety performance4. This includes, but is not limited to, management, voicing opinions, assisting colleagues, reporting safety violations, promoting workplace reforms, and demonstrating civic virtues5,6. In practice, SCB plays an important role in reducing casualties and economic losses during accidents. For example, at the Shenshuping No. 2 Mine in the Shanxi Coal Industry, miners exhibit proactive management by disabling faulty belt conveyors and promoting workplace reforms by installing spray dust suppression devices, leading to a reduction in fire risk and potential mechanical injuries. This case demonstrates the significant role of SCB in mine safety management.

According to the theory of planned behavior (TPB), behavioral intention directly affects the execution of behavior7. The theory explicitly identifies three core factors that are associated with individual behavioral intentions, namely, behavioral attitudes (BAs), subjective norms (SNs), and perceived behavioral control (PBC)7.

However, to the best of our knowledge, no empirical study has quantitatively explored the influencing factors of miners’ SCB in China, with relevant quantitative analyses remaining scarce in the context of coal mining. First, despite extensive SCB research in the construction, railway, aviation and textile sectors8,9 few studies have focused on coal mine safety management. The existing studies have focused mostly on miners’ compliant safety behavior, with insufficient attention given to proactive, altruistic SCB and its formation mechanism in high-risk underground scenarios. Second, while the TPB clarifies the links between BAs, SNs, PBC and behavioral intention, the relevant external influencing factors remain underexplored10. The existing TPB-based studies have mostly expanded the model with a single external variable and rarely systematically analyzed the effects of SC, SVs, SSR and organizational safety climate on miners’ SCB in a unified framework. To address these gaps, this study quantitatively investigates the factors associated with miners’ SCB and their interrelationships.

Theoretical framework and hypotheses

External factors

Given that the existing TPB-based studies in the coal mining sector have rarely integrated safety capability (SC), safety values (SVs), sense of safety responsibility (SSR) and organizational safety climate (OSC) into a single analytical framework and that the combined effects of these multidimensional antecedents on miners’ safety citizenship behavior (SCB) remain largely underexplored, this study incorporates the four aforementioned context-specific variables into the classic TPB framework to systematically examine their influencing mechanism on miners’ SCB.

Safety capability, safety values, and behavioral attitudes

Values, as the cognitive foundation of BAs, are closely associated with an individual’s safety decisions. The formation of safety values (SVs) can regulate behavior choices through cognitive frameworks. The implementation of these values requires practical capabilities. In the complex and high-risk working environment of coal mines, miners’ attitudes toward safety behaviors (e.g., operating in accordance with regulations and actively identifying potential hazards) are closely associated with their past experiences and their understanding of the importance of safety. When miners deeply recognize that safety behaviors can effectively safeguard their own and their colleagues’ lives or when they consider these behaviors necessary and worth adhering to, they will be more inclined to actively practice these behaviors. That is, miners’ own safety capability (SC)11 and their degree of identification with SVs12 are associated with their behavioral attitude and further associated with their willingness to engage in safety behaviors. Therefore, this study regards safety capability and safety values as key individual factors and proposes the following hypotheses:H1a: Miners’ SC is positively related to their BAs.H1b: Miners’ SVs are positively related to their BAs.

Sense of safety responsibility and subjective norms

SNs, which refer to the social pressures or expectations that individuals feel when making behavioral decisions, are associated with not only direct relationships such as those between relatives and friends but also the social environment and cultural context. The existence of SNs indicates that individuals tend to take into account the opinions and expectations of others when they make decisions, and this tendency is often significantly associated with their behavioral choices13. Workers with high levels of safety responsibility not only adhere strictly to the rules themselves but also actively intervene in others’ violations. As the main body of work-related injury accidents, although miners attach great importance to work safety, they often lack a clear understanding of their own safety responsibilities14. When the surrounding group shows a high level of emphasis on safety and miners themselves are aware of the practical effectiveness of safety behaviors in accident prevention and truly feel the responsibility to maintain their own and others’ safety, they will be more inclined to actively follow safety norms. This can effectively promote the implementation of safety behaviors at the SN level. Organization safety climate (OSC) and perceived behavioral control

PBC refers to individuals’ perception of the ease or difficulty of performing a specific behavior, including the perception and control of the required resources, opportunities, and capabilities7. In an environment with a strong organization safety climate, systematic training combined with advanced technical equipment can effectively enhance miners’ perception of control over safety behaviors.

Specifically, an organizational climate that emphasizes the supremacy of safety can enhance miners’ perceived behavioral control. It can help them believe that their safety behaviors will be supported and recognized by the organization, thus enabling them to more actively comply with safety regulations15. Organization safety climate enhances miners’ perceived behavioral control over safety behaviors, thereby increasing their likelihood of exhibiting behaviors that meet the standards of safety citizenship. Therefore, this study considers the organization safety climate as a key situational factor and proposes the following hypothesis:H3: OSC is positively related to miners’ PBC.

Theory of planned behavior

The TPB, initially developed by American social psychologist Ajzen7 and based on the extension of the theory of reasoned action, systematically explains the direct antecedents and determinants of individual behavior by introducing the variable PBC. The TPB suggests that an individual’s behavior is associated with his or her intention; the stronger the intention is, the greater the likelihood of the behavior being carried out. The formation and strength of this intention are jointly associated with BAs (positive or negative evaluation of the behavior), SNs (perceived social pressure and expectations), and PBC (assessment of one’s own ability and resource conditions), which in turn are related to their actual behavior. Owing to its ability to integrate psychological cognition and social factors, this theoretical framework has become a classic tool for explaining and predicting individual behavior.

The TPB has demonstrated broad applicability in the study of safety behaviors and is frequently integrated with psychological and organizational management theories to increase analytical depth. For instance, Ledesma et al.16 incorporated implicit attitude theory into the TPB framework, using “implicit safety associations” as a proxy variable for “ego depletion,” and quantified the association between unconscious cognition and behavior through the Implicit Association Test. In contrast, Erul et al.17 integrated social exchange theory, affective events theory, and the TPB to develop an integrated model in which “emotional solidarity” was employed to quantify associations related to community safety bonds.

Additionally, the TPB has been extended to areas such as SCB and unsafe behavior intervention. For example, Wang et al.18 reported that team safety stressors are significantly negatively associated with both proactive and prosocial safety behaviors, the two types of SCB. Furthermore, safety responsibility and affective commitment are associated with the mediating roles between team safety stressors and these two types of SCB. Its adaptability across scenarios and inclusiveness of multiple factors make it one of the core theories in behavioral research.

Safety behavior refers to the actions taken by employees when they perform their job tasks, where they voluntarily comply with the safety regulations set by the enterprise and demonstrate behaviors that are beneficial to their own and the surrounding environment’s safety19. Safety behavior research focuses on employees’ compliance with procedures and their proactive enhancement of safety. Geczik5 classified it into safety compliance (such as wearing protective equipment) and safety participation behaviors (such as participating in safety training).

Related studies have shown that psychological factors such as work–family conflict3 and psychological resilience20, organizational factors such as safety training21, leadership behavior22, and social factors such as team atmosphere and social support20 are associated with safety behavior. In terms of management practices, scholars have advocated for the use of measures such as shaping the safety culture, designing incentive mechanisms (e.g., combining rewards and deterrence), and providing psychological support to encourage employees to shift from passive compliance to active participation. This approach is related to remarkable results in high-risk industries such as chemical engineering and coal mining23.

The TPB established a systematic framework for research on miners’ SCB, which is associated with a comprehensive understanding and analysis of various factors related to miners’ SCB. This analytical framework is not only associated with the consideration of the relationship between individual BAs and the formation of intentions but also comprehensively associated with the consideration of the factors related to external environmental factors and personal capabilities. It is also associated with the analysis of the formation process of intentions and the exploration of the relationship between behavioral intentions and subsequent behaviors16. Methodology

This research was approved by the Academic and Ethics Review Committee of Architecture Engineering College, North China Institute of Science and Technology, with approval number ECAN-003–2024. Informed consent was obtained from all the subjects involved in the study, and all the experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

The research methodology involved the following key steps: First, on the basis of the theoretical framework and literature review, a hypothesis model with all the hypotheses was established. Second, specific questionnaires were designed according to the model and corresponding questionnaire design criteria. Third, this study employed a stratified random sampling technique. On the basis of a questionnaire survey, data on miners’ BAs, SNs, PBC, attitudes towards SCB, SCB, psychological sense of belonging, safety responsibility, and role breadth self-efficacy were collected. Fourth, structural equation modeling (SEM) and multivariate statistical analysis were used to analyze the collected data and proposed hypotheses.

Questionnaire design

The questionnaire comprised two parts. The first part contained sociodemographic information, such as sex, age, educational level, marital status, province where the mine is located, years of work experience, job position, and weekly working hours. In the second part, a five-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), was used to measure the proposed constructs. The measurement of subjective norms (SNs) focused on individuals’ perceptions of the expectations of significant others. Its theoretical basis was derived from the classic definition of subjective norms in the theory of planned behavior7, corresponding to questionnaire items SN1-SN3 (“I believe I should engage in safety citizenship behaviors”, “People whose opinions I value require me to engage in safety citizenship behaviors”, “I am responsible for practicing safety citizenship behaviors”). It primarily captured perceptions of social pressure at the individual level, and the items were designed with reference to relevant research by Liu et al.24.

Most measurement items in the second section referred to the existing literature. The sources of the BAs, SCBI, and SCB items were mainly from Liu et al.24. The design of the SC items was primarily based on those of Qin et al.25. The measurement instrument for SVs was developed mainly according to the methods of Griffin and Neal26. The scale design for organization safety climate was based on that from Huang et al.27. The sense of safety responsibility items were informed by the work of Morrison and Phelps28, while those for PBC were developed mainly according to Liu et al.24 and Curcuruto et al.29. Samples and data collection

A stratified, proportional, and random sampling process was used to select valid respondents. First, on the basis of the typicality and representativeness of the mining development characteristics in eastern, central, and western China, we divided the population into three strata by selecting the provinces of Shanxi, Sichuan, and Anhui. Shanxi, as China’s primary coal-producing region, has densely distributed mines with a long history of exploitation, exposing miners to persistent safety risks such as gas and water inrush. Moreover, the metal mines in Sichuan and nonmetallic mines in Anhui encompass diverse mining environments characterized by complex geological conditions and significantly varying operational scenarios. Miners in these three provinces represent a broad occupational group within high-risk industries. Furthermore, their safety perceptions, behavioral patterns, and emergency experiences collectively reflect the core influencing factors of SCB across different types of mines and operational environments. Second, within each of the three strata—Shanxi, Sichuan, and Anhui—we randomly selected representative mines to ensure that mines of various types and characteristics were included in the sample. Finally, within each selected mine, we further randomly sampled miners as the ultimate subjects of our investigation. Through this stratified random sampling process, we can focus on this group to analyze in depth the intrinsic motivations behind individual safety behaviors in high-risk industries, providing empirical evidence for the development of targeted safety management strategies.

From April 22, 2024, to July 8, 2024, a total of 1,651 questionnaires were distributed to random miners who had provided oral informed consent via an online survey. Invalid responses, such as incomplete questionnaires, unclear answers, and damaged questionnaires, were subsequently eliminated, and ultimately, 1,334 valid questionnaires (80.8%) were obtained. Furthermore, the sample is consistent with the demographic information of the total population. In addition to demographic characteristics such as age, sex, educational level, and marital status, the respondents also provided information such as job position, years of work experience, and weekly working hours, without any other personal or unit-related information.

To ensure accuracy and authenticity, the following measures were taken. First, the purpose and anonymity of the questionnaire were honestly conveyed to the respondents to make them feel comfortable and actively cooperative. Second, the questions were explained in a timely manner so that they could clearly express their thoughts. Finally, segmented surveys were conducted according to the miners’ job positions to ensure their broad participation.

Data analysis process

First, SPSS 25.0 was used for reliability and validity analysis. Second, SPSS 25.0 was used for common method bias (CMB) testing to exclude potential data bias problems. Descriptive statistical analysis was subsequently performed. AMOS 24.0 was used to construct an SEM. This study aims to explore the direct, indirect, and overall associations of miners’ individual factors, sense of safety responsibility, and organizational safety climate with BAs, SNs, and PBC. Accordingly, the paths through which these three types of psychological variables affect SCB through SCBI were analyzed. Finally, the research hypotheses were tested.

Variables and scale reliabilities.

This study evaluated the validity of the scale from both content validity and construct validity perspectives. The measurement items of this scale were developed on the basis of existing, well-established scales used domestically and internationally, aligned with our research objectives, and refined through repeated revisions by experts in the field. Consequently, the scale possesses good content validity.

With respect to construct validity, this study examined convergent and discriminant validity. Initially, the Kaiser‒Meyer‒Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were conducted. A KMO value greater than 0.5 is considered acceptable. As indicated in Table 3, the KMO value for this scale is 0.915, which exceeds 0.5. Furthermore, it passed Bartlett’s test of sphericity (Sig = 0.000 < 0.05), indicating that the data are suitable for factor analysis.

This study utilized confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the construct validity of the scale. Initial evaluations focused on convergent validity. Standardized factor loadings exceeding 0.5 are acceptable, whereas those surpassing 0.7 are considered optimal. The average variance extracted (AVE) should exceed 0.5, while the composite reliability (CR) should be greater than 0.7, with values above 0.8 being ideal. As shown in Table 2, the majority of items in this scale exhibit factor loadings greater than 0.7. Additionally, four items have factor loadings ranging from 0.6 to 0.7, indicating a substantial correlation between the indicators and their respective latent factors. Moreover, all the AVE values are above 0.5, whereas all the CR values exceed 0.8. Thus, the scale demonstrates strong convergent validity and high internal consistency.

This study verified discriminant validity by comparing the square root of the AVE with the corresponding correlation coefficients. If the square root of the AVE exceeds the correlation coefficient, the internal correlations among latent variables are stronger than their external correlations and exceed the correlations between different constructs. Hypotheses testing

This study constructed a theoretical model based on the TPB and utilized AMOS 24.0 software to test and fit the model. The chi-square test was employed to eliminate the potential influence of the estimated parameters in the hypothetical model on the model fit. Generally, a chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (chi-square/df) of less than 2 indicates an acceptable model fit. As shown in Table 5, the chi-square/df ratio was 1.70, which is less than 2, demonstrating a satisfactory fit. Additionally, goodness-of-fit indices (GFI) and adjusted GFI (AGFI) were used as preliminary measures of model fit, with values ranging from 0.8 to 1 indicating an acceptable fit. Furthermore, comparative fit (CFI) and incremental fit indices (IFI) were selected as indicators of incremental fit; values exceeding 0.9 suggest that the model is acceptable. The results revealed that all these indices exceeded 0.9, meeting the criteria. With respect to residual-based evaluation metrics, the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), which incorporates information about model degrees of freedom and residuals, served as a crucial indicator. An RMSEA value less than 0.05 signifies a good fit, whereas values between 0.05 and 0.08 indicate an acceptable fit. Here, the RMSEA value was 0.02, which is less than 0.05, further confirming the adequacy of the model fit.

Discussion

Relationships with external factors

The finding that SC and SVs are associated with BAs is consistent with that of Chen & Li31. A possible reason is that miners’ BAs are more strongly associated with their internalized, stable and risk-sensitive SVs in high-risk underground coal mining environments, which explains the larger effect size of SVs than that of SC32. In terms of sex and work experience, Deng et al.32 reported that the increasing working age of miners was connected to psychological factors such as SVs and competency factors such as SC and revealed the relationships among SC, SVs and BAs. Previous studies have also shown that SC is related to the operational skills and technical proficiency accumulated by miners through underground practices, whereas SVs represent an internalized cognitive orientation associated with risk perception, moral judgment and behavioral preference under high-risk pressure33. However, this pattern differs from the findings of Choudhry et al.34 in construction workers, where SC was more strongly related to safety attitudes than SVs were. Such differences may be attributed to contextual heterogeneity: construction sites are open and dynamic, associated with higher demands for workers’ immediate operational capacity, whereas underground coal mining involves highly standardized procedures and catastrophic irreversible risks, making SVs more central to associations with BAs.

The verified relationship between SSR and SNs in this study is consistent with that reported by Veljanovski35. The underlying mechanism can be explained as follows: SSR is related to awareness of accountability for operational safety consequences, which is associated with the activation of normative compliance willingness, whereas SNs are related to individuals’ perceived group safety expectations14. In underground coal mining, which involves intensive team-based collaboration and high interdependence in terms of miners’ safety at the same working face, individual violations may lead to catastrophic accidents; this strong risk interdependence explains why miners with a greater SSR are more likely to perceive and comply with SNs36, thus supporting the strong positive association between SSR and SNs identified in this study.

The findings concerning the association between OSC and PBC are consistent with those of previous studies indicating that underground coal mining involves persistent inherent hazards (water inrushes, fires, gas, and roof falls) and that miners’ perceived controllability of safe operations is closely related to enterprises’ safety investment, preemptive risk control and emergency support capacity, which are core carriers of OSC15. Moreover, a favorable safety climate is associated with reduced external constraints on safe operations and is related to greater individual perceived behavioral controllability influenced by advanced age, a low level of education, and long weekly working hours, which are associated with elevated PBC8,32.

Relationships with the TPB

All the traditional relationships proposed by the TPB have been confirmed in this study, which is consistent with previous findings while reflecting the unique characteristics of the mining industry.

With respect to the relationship between BAs and SCBI, underground coal mine safety citizenship behavior is extrarole proactive behavior beyond mandatory job requirements, which is distinct from post compliance bound by rigid institutional constraints37. The strict underground “three violations” accountability mechanism is related to the constrained basic compliance behaviors of miners and limited room for individual attitudes to link with extrarole SCBI, corresponding to the observed small effect size.

With respect to the association between SNs and SCBI, the coal mining industry widely implements team safety coguarantee and mentor-apprentice safety binding systems, where a single member’s violation is associated with joint safety liability for the whole team and bound individuals. This institutional design is closely related to the direct binding of group safety norms and miners’ individual interests and the strong association between SNs and SCBI38.

With respect to the relationships between H6 and H8, underground coal mining has strict safety management authority boundaries: miners can carry out only basic compliance operations within post authority, and safety citizenship behaviors (e.g., safety facility adjustment and hidden danger rectification) are associated with the required approval from teams and district teams. The controllability of individual behavior is closely related to enterprise approval processes and institutional support rather than subjective perception alone, corresponding to the observed small effect sizes.

Unlike the SCBI and SCB, underground coal mines are closed, interference-free fixed operation spaces with a continuous single-shift workflow and no fragmented interference. SCBI formed by miners before they enter the well is associated with unimpeded translation into actual SCB during operation24. Moreover, the immediate nature of underground risks is related to the onsite risk avoidance effect of safe citizenship behavior, which is further associated with higher conversion efficiency from intention to behavior.

Group differences

The association between PBC and SCB is not verified in the Sichuan group, possibly because of the characteristics of Sichuan miners. Mines in Sichuan are generally smaller in scale, with more complex production and geological conditions. In practice, miners’ safety behavior is largely constrained by technical specifications and safety regulations, which are associated with a substantial limitation of their space for independent decision-making and action. According to the TPB, when operational procedures and safety operations are highly standardized and institutionalized, the association between individual PBC and SCB is correspondingly weakened. In addition, local underground operations rely heavily on team collaboration and unified command, which are associated with limited room for individuals to regulate their behavior independently. This is associated with the further weakened direct association between PBC and SCB, ultimately rendering the path nonsignificant39.

It should be noted that several core constructs in this study exhibited relatively high mean values and limited variance. On the one hand, this pattern may be related to the high-risk and highly regulated nature of the mining industry, suggesting that safety is inherently a highly salient issue in the sample. On the other hand, the possibility of some socially desirable responses in safety-related self-report measures cannot be ruled out. Prior research has shown that when scores cluster near the upper end of a scale, the instrument’s ability to discriminate among high-scoring respondents is reduced40, and restricted variance may also affect the generalizability of the scores and lead to more conservative estimates of relationships among variables41.

Conclusions

All the research hypotheses proposed in this study have been verified and supported by empirical test results. Drawing on the TPB, this study examined the factors associated with miners’ SCB. The findings show the following: (1) Personal factors are associated with behavioral attitudes, a sense of safety responsibility is related to subjective norms, and the organizational safety climate is significantly associated with perceived behavioral control. (2) Miners’ behavioral attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control are directly and positively associated with SCBI, and SCBI is related to actual SCB. Moreover, perceived behavioral control is directly and significantly associated with actual behavior. (3) The results of the mediation effect test indicate that all the hypothetical paths included in the research model are associated with significant mediating associations and that the corresponding mediating relationships of each path are effectively supported. (4) The multigroup analysis revealed that perceived behavioral control is not significantly associated with SCB among the Sichuan group.

Theoretically, this study provides empirical evidence related to miners’ safety citizenship behavior. First, this study fills this research gap by examining the formation mechanism of miners’ SCB in high-risk underground coal mining settings, a topic that has been underexplored in the previous literature. Second, this study extends the TPB by clarifying how four context-specific antecedents (safety capability, safety values, sense of safety responsibility, and organizational safety climate) influence the core TPB constructs (behavioral attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control) and further drive miners’ safety citizenship behavioral intention and actual behavior. Third, the findings provide new insights into the applicable scope of the TPB in the mining context, which enhances our theoretical understanding of the formation logic of proactive prosocial safety behaviors in high-risk work settings and the effective extension path of the TPB in industry-specific occupational safety research.

These findings also have valuable practical implications for the safety management of miners. Management measures could be designed in line with the key paths identified in this study in mine enterprises. Given the relationships between safety capability and safety values on behavioral attitudes, enterprises could strengthen their job-related skill training, hazard identification, emergency drills, and accident case education. Given the association of a sense of safety responsibility with subjective norms, job responsibility, safety commitment, and mutual reminder mechanisms could be embedded in preshift meetings, job descriptions, and team management. Considering the link between the organizational safety climate and perceived behavioral control, enterprises could improve hazard reporting channels, increase feedback efficiency, ensure the provision of safety resources, and foster an organizational environment that supports proactive safety behavior. Since regional and operational contexts differ, enterprises should also adopt targeted management strategies. In particular, in contexts where perceived behavioral control cannot be directly translated into actual behavior, greater attention should be given to team coordination, onsite command, and standardized operational support to facilitate the transformation of safety intention into actual action.

This study has several limitations. First, the online questionnaire adopted in this study may introduce potential self-selection bias, as participants were likely to have greater safety concerns or higher education levels, which may to some extent affect the generalizability of the reported SCB levels. Second, the data were collected exclusively from coal miners in Anhui, Sichuan, and Shanxi provinces, which may limit the representativeness of the sample. Third, although a comparative single-factor CFA was used to assess CMV, more rigorous controls, such as the unmeasured latent method factor (ULMF) approach, were not applied, as model complexity and sample size constraints might hinder model identification and convergence; future research could adopt multisource data or advanced statistical controls to better address CMV and enhance the robustness of the results. Furthermore, the selection of external factors influencing the classic variables of the TPB may not have been fully comprehensive, as some potential determinants might have been overlooked.
