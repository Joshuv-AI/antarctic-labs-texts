Integrative bulk and single-cell transcriptomics identifies a HERC5-linked immune-attenuated risk program for pulmonary metastasis/relapse in osteosarcoma
Authors: Zhangfu Li, Baicheng Yang, Shiqing Liu, Shengji Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A nine-gene PM/R score was developed in the TARGET-OS discovery cohort.•A seven-gene implementation showed cross-cohort discrimination in GSE21257.•High-risk tumors showed reduced antigen presentation and immune activity.•HERC5 was linked to interferon and antigen-presentation programs.•Single-cell analysis suggested SPP1/MIF-centered communication axes.Keywords: Osteosarcoma, Pulmonary metastasis/relapse, HERC5, Antigen presentation, Single-cell RNA sequencing

Introduction

Osteosarcoma is one of the most common primary malignant bone tumors in children, adolescents and young adults, and metastatic progression remains the principal factor associated with poor clinical outcome [1,2]. Current treatment for high-grade osteosarcoma is based on multimodal therapy, including systemic chemotherapy and complete surgical resection, which has improved disease control in patients with localized tumors [3]. However, patients who present with metastatic disease or develop relapse still have unsatisfactory outcomes, and further survival improvement in these high-risk populations has remained limited [4]. The lung is the predominant site of osteosarcoma metastasis and recurrence, making pulmonary metastasis/relapse (PM/R) a central pattern of treatment failure [5]. Therefore, early identification of patients at high risk for PM/R is clinically important for risk-adapted surveillance, treatment stratification and individualized therapeutic decision-making.

Established clinicopathologic variables, including metastatic status at diagnosis, tumor size, tumor location and histologic response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, provide important prognostic information in osteosarcoma [6,7]. However, these factors do not fully capture the marked biological heterogeneity of osteosarcoma, a disease characterized by complex genomic alterations and diverse transcriptional states [8]. Recent multi-omics studies have further shown that osteosarcoma can be classified into molecular subtypes with distinct biological features and clinical outcomes, supporting the potential value of molecular stratification beyond conventional clinicopathologic assessment [9]. In this context, transcriptomic profiling may provide complementary information for identifying patients with a higher risk of pulmonary metastasis/relapse and for uncovering the biological programs associated with high-risk disease.

Several transcriptomic studies have attempted to construct gene signatures for osteosarcoma metastasis or prognosis using public bulk-expression datasets and computational feature-selection approaches. For example, co-expression network analysis combined with LASSO-based modeling has been used to identify gene signatures associated with lung metastasis and survival in osteosarcoma [10]. More recent studies have also developed metastasis-related or tumor microenvironment-based prognostic signatures and linked these models to immune infiltration or immunotherapy-related features [11,12]. However, many existing signatures primarily focus on overall survival, general metastasis or broad tumor microenvironment scores, and their biological interpretation often remains limited at the bulk-transcriptome level. In particular, fewer studies have specifically focused on pulmonary metastasis/relapse (PM/R) while simultaneously connecting risk prediction with immune-regulatory programs, candidate driver genes and single-cell microenvironmental context.

The osteosarcoma microenvironment is increasingly recognized as an active regulator of tumor progression rather than a passive background, with immune cells, stromal cells and vascular components collectively shaping metastatic behavior and therapeutic response [13]. Among these processes, antigen presentation, interferon-responsive signaling and cytotoxic lymphocyte activity are central to effective tumor immune recognition, whereas disruption of these programs may contribute to immune escape [14]. IFN-γ signaling activates JAK–STAT and downstream interferon-regulatory-factor programs that coordinate immune effector functions, including antigen-processing and antigen-presentation-related transcriptional responses [15]. HERC5, an interferon-inducible E3 ligase involved in ISGylation, has been implicated in innate immune regulation, but its relationship with antigen-presentation programs and PM/R-prone osteosarcoma remains insufficiently characterized [16].

Bulk transcriptomic signatures provide sample-level risk information but cannot fully resolve the cellular compartments and intercellular communication programs that contribute to high-risk tumor states. Single-cell RNA sequencing has enabled more detailed dissection of osteosarcoma heterogeneity, revealing diverse malignant, immune and stromal cell populations as well as immunosuppressive microenvironmental features in advanced or treatment-naïve osteosarcoma [17,18]. In addition, recent single-cell analyses have suggested that communication between tumor cells and microenvironmental components may be closely related to metastatic behavior in osteosarcoma [19]. Recent work has further emphasized that metastasis-initiating osteosarcoma subpopulations may establish paracrine interactions with lung and tumor cells during metastatic-niche formation [20]. Therefore, integrating bulk risk modeling with single-cell transcriptomic analysis may help clarify whether PM/R-associated signatures reflect tumor-intrinsic transcriptional states, immune-cell programs, stromal remodeling or ligand–receptor communication within the osteosarcoma microenvironment.

In this study, we developed an integrative transcriptomic and single-cell analytical framework to identify molecular programs associated with PM/R risk in osteosarcoma. Using TARGET-OS bulk RNA-seq data, we first constructed a compact PM/R-focused transcriptomic risk score and evaluated its cross-cohort performance in the GSE21257 external validation cohort. We then examined the clinical and prognostic relevance of this risk score in relation to PM/R burden, event-free survival and overall survival. To explore the biological context of high-risk disease, we integrated immune signature scoring, weighted gene co-expression network analysis, single-cell risk-score projection, virtual HERC5 knockout, pathway and transcription-factor activity inference, cell-cell communication analysis and computational drug-reversal screening. Through this framework, we aimed to develop a clinically relevant PM/R risk score and to nominate a HERC5-linked antigen-presentation and IRF/STAT-associated immune regulatory program underlying high-risk osteosarcoma.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This study was designed as a retrospective, public-dataset-based integrative transcriptomic and single-cell bioinformatics analysis of osteosarcoma. The overall aim was to develop a compact transcriptomic risk score for pulmonary metastasis/relapse (PM/R), evaluate its clinical and prognostic relevance, and explore the immune and microenvironmental programs associated with high-risk disease.

Bulk RNA-seq data from the TARGET-OS cohort were used as the discovery dataset. GDC STAR-count RNA-seq profiles and corresponding clinical annotations were obtained and matched for downstream analyses. In TARGET-OS, 88 RNA-seq samples were available; 87 samples with defined PM/R status were used for PM/R model development and risk-score analyses, and 84 samples with available survival information were included in event-free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS) analyses.

The GSE21257 microarray cohort was used as the primary external validation dataset. This cohort included 53 osteosarcoma samples with available 5-year metastasis information and was used to evaluate the cross-platform performance of the TARGET-OS-derived transcriptomic risk score. The GSE39055 microarray cohort, including 37 samples with recurrence and death information, was used only for exploratory validation because its endpoint structure differed from the primary PM/R definition.

Two public single-cell RNA-seq datasets were used for cellular and mechanistic extension analyses. GSE152048, containing 121,579 cells, was used to examine the distribution of the weighted risk score across major cell populations. GSE162454, containing 42,708 cells, was used to characterize HERC5 expression across osteosarcoma cell compartments and to perform virtual HERC5 knockout and cell-cell communication analyses.

Because all datasets used in this study were obtained from publicly available repositories and contained de-identified data, no additional patient recruitment, biospecimen collection or experimental intervention was performed. The study was therefore conducted as a secondary analysis of public data.

Clinical endpoints and risk-group definition

Pulmonary metastasis/relapse (PM/R) was used as the primary modeling endpoint. In TARGET-OS, PM/R-positive status was assigned when clinical annotations indicated lung/pulmonary metastatic disease at diagnosis, pulmonary relapse, or pulmonary metastasis documented during follow-up. Patients without pulmonary metastatic disease at diagnosis and without pulmonary relapse/metastasis during follow-up were classified as PM/R-negative. Non-pulmonary relapse or non-pulmonary metastatic events alone were not counted as PM/R unless pulmonary involvement was documented. Samples with missing PM/R status were excluded from model construction and PM/R classification analyses. Overall, 87 TARGET-OS samples with defined PM/R status were included in risk-score development and clinical association analyses.

The transcriptomic risk score was calculated for each sample using the final 9-gene model. For group-based analyses, patients were stratified into low-risk and high-risk groups according to the median risk score in the TARGET-OS cohort. This median-based cutoff generated 43 low-risk and 44 high-risk patients for comparisons of baseline clinical characteristics, PM/R burden, immune signatures and survival outcomes. Event-status summaries in Table 1 were available for all 87 patients, whereas time-to-event survival analyses were restricted to 84 patients with valid survival time.

Event-free survival (EFS) and overall survival (OS) were evaluated in TARGET-OS patients with available survival information. The corrected EFS event variable was derived from the TARGET-OS First Event annotation: non-censored First Event records were coded as EFS events, whereas censored or no-event records were coded as non-events. Under this corrected definition, 84 patients were included in the EFS analysis, with 41 EFS events. OS was defined using death as the event, and 84 patients with available OS information were included, with 29 death events.

For clinical translation analyses, age was selected as the primary clinical comparator for benchmarking against the transcriptomic risk score. Therefore, the clinical benchmark was specified as an age-only model rather than a multivariable clinical model. Predictive performance was compared among the age-only model, the transcriptomic risk-score model and the combined risk-score-plus-age model.

Bulk transcriptomic preprocessing and differential-expression analysis

For the TARGET-OS discovery cohort, GDC STAR-count RNA-seq data were processed at the gene level and matched with clinical annotations. Unstranded raw counts were used for DESeq2 differential-expression analysis. Genes were retained if they were annotated as protein-coding genes and had counts ≥10 in at least 10 samples. Raw count data were used for differential-expression analysis, whereas variance-stabilizing transformed (VST) expression values generated by DESeq2 were used for PCA and heatmap visualization. For machine-learning, WGCNA and related downstream analyses, the VST matrix was further standardized by gene-wise z-score transformation.

Differential-expression analysis was performed between PM/R-positive and PM/R-negative TARGET-OS tumors using DESeq2. Pulmonary metastasis/relapse status was used as the primary grouping variable. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Genes with an adjusted P value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 1 were considered differentially expressed. Under this threshold, 56 differentially expressed genes were identified, including 31 genes upregulated and 25 genes downregulated in PM/R-positive tumors. A less stringent DEG set using adjusted P < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change > 0.5 was also retained for enrichment and candidate-gene exploration when appropriate.

For microarray validation cohorts, processed GEO Series Matrix expression data were used rather than reprocessing raw CEL files. Platform annotations were obtained from GPL10295 for GSE21257 and GPL14951 for GSE39055. Probe identifiers were mapped to gene symbols using the platform Symbol annotation. When multiple probes mapped to the same gene symbol, probe-level expression values were collapsed by taking the mean expression value. For external validation, each GEO expression matrix was standardized by row-wise gene z-score transformation before risk-score calculation.

Gene-set enrichment and immune signature scoring

To interpret PM/R-associated transcriptomic programs, gene-set enrichment analysis was performed using ranked gene-level statistics from the TARGET-OS differential-expression analysis. Genes were ranked according to the direction and magnitude of association with PM/R status, and enrichment was assessed for Gene Ontology Biological Process gene sets using clusterProfiler-based preranked GSEA. Enriched terms were summarized to highlight major functional themes, including immune activation, interferon response, lymphocyte-mediated immunity, NK-cell-mediated immunity, translation, ribosome biogenesis, rRNA processing and mitochondrial translation.

To further quantify immune and functional states at the sample level, predefined gene signatures were scored for each TARGET-OS sample using single-sample gene-set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) implemented in the GSVA package, with Gaussian kernel estimation and absolute ranking disabled. The analyzed signatures included antigen presentation, macrophage, M1-like macrophage, IFN-γ response, immune checkpoint, T cell, CD8 T cell, NK cell, cytotoxicity, B cell, plasma cell, angiogenesis, proliferation and ribosome/translation programs. Signature scores were compared between low-risk and high-risk tumors to identify biological programs associated with the PM/R risk score.

For correlation analyses, continuous transcriptomic risk scores were correlated with immune and functional signature scores across TARGET-OS samples using Spearman correlation. Group differences between low- and high-risk tumors were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and false-discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P values were used to determine statistical significance. Signature-level results were visualized as mean score differences between risk groups and as correlations with the continuous risk score.

Machine-learning feature selection and risk-score construction

Machine-learning feature selection was performed to develop a compact transcriptomic risk score for PM/R classification. The machine-learning feature pool consisted of 100 protein-coding PM/R-associated genes meeting adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5 in the TARGET-OS differential-expression analysis. To reduce dependence on a single algorithm, multiple feature-selection approaches were applied, including LASSO, ElasticNet, RandomForest, support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) and XGBoost. ElasticNet was fitted using alpha = 0.5. RandomForest was trained with 1,000 trees and genes were ranked by MeanDecreaseGini. SVM-RFE was implemented with a radial-kernel SVM and 5-fold cross-validation. XGBoost was fitted using a binary logistic objective with AUC as the evaluation metric. These non-LASSO algorithms were used only for feature voting and stability assessment and were not combined into an ensemble predictor; the final predictive score was calculated solely from the LASSO model coefficients.

The final risk-score model was constructed using LASSO regression in the TARGET-OS cohort with the glmnet package. A binomial model was fitted with alpha = 1, and the penalty parameter was selected by 10-fold cross-validation using AUC as the optimization metric. The random seed was set to 20260526. Genes with non-zero coefficients in the final LASSO model were retained for risk-score calculation.

The final model contained nine genes: HERC5, GPR141, SLC45A4, LCN15, MXI1, UCK2, KRTAP13-2, KCNK6 and TRABD2A. The transcriptomic risk score for each sample was calculated as the weighted sum of the expression values of these genes multiplied by their corresponding LASSO coefficients:

Risk score = Σ βᵢ × expressionᵢ where βᵢ represents the LASSO coefficient for gene i. The expressionᵢ term represented gene-wise z-score-transformed VST expression in TARGET-OS and row-wise gene z-score-transformed expression within each external GEO cohort before applying the TARGET-OS-derived coefficients. Positive coefficients indicated that higher expression contributed to increased predicted PM/R risk, whereas negative coefficients indicated that higher expression contributed to lower predicted risk. The full coefficient set is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

For group-based analyses, patients were dichotomized into low-risk and high-risk groups using the median risk score in the TARGET-OS cohort as the cutoff. This cutoff was used consistently for clinical association, survival, immune signature and downstream mechanistic analyses.

External validation and robustness assessment

The predictive performance of the complete 9-gene transcriptomic risk score was first evaluated in the TARGET-OS discovery cohort, and a reduced implementation using the model genes available on the validation platform was assessed in GSE21257. Receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to assess discrimination for PM/R in TARGET-OS and for 5-year metastasis status in GSE21257. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used as the primary performance metric.

To apply the TARGET-OS-derived model to the external microarray cohort, the available model genes were matched between the discovery and validation platforms. Risk scores in GSE21257 were calculated using the same coefficient-based formula derived from TARGET-OS. The distribution of risk scores between metastasis-positive and metastasis-negative samples was compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Because of differences in platform, cohort composition and endpoint annotation, external validation was interpreted as cross-cohort assessment of transcriptomic signal transferability rather than as definitive clinical validation. Because not all nine model genes were available on the external microarray platform, the GSE21257 validation score was calculated using the seven available model genes while retaining their TARGET-OS-derived coefficients. The available genes were HERC5, GPR141, SLC45A4, MXI1, UCK2, KRTAP13-2 and KCNK6, whereas LCN15 and TRABD2A were unavailable. The same strategy was used for other external datasets, with risk scores calculated from the model genes available in each cohort.

To evaluate whether the observed model performance exceeded what could be expected from random gene selection, a random-gene benchmark analysis was performed. Random gene signatures of comparable size were repeatedly generated from the eligible gene pool, and their AUC distributions were compared with the observed 9-gene model AUC in both TARGET-OS and GSE21257. Empirical P values were calculated as (number of random signatures with AUC ≥ observed AUC + 1)/(number of random signatures + 1).

Leave-one-gene-out ablation analysis was further performed to estimate the contribution of individual model genes to external validation performance. In GSE21257, this analysis was restricted to the seven model genes available on the microarray platform. For each ablation run, one available gene was removed, the risk score was recalculated using the remaining available genes, and the resulting AUC in GSE21257 was compared with that of the full seven-gene external implementation. Genes whose removal caused a larger decrease in AUC were considered to contribute more strongly to the external predictive performance of the signature. Extended robustness and ablation results are provided in Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S4.

Clinical association, survival and calibration analyses

Clinical characteristics were compared between the low-risk and high-risk groups in the TARGET-OS cohort. Continuous variables were summarized as median with interquartile range and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using Fisher exact tests. The association between binary risk group and PM/R status was evaluated using Fisher exact tests, and odds ratios were calculated to quantify the enrichment of PM/R events in the high-risk group.

To assess the clinical predictive value of the transcriptomic risk score, ROC analysis was performed for three models: an age-only model, the transcriptomic risk-score model and a combined risk-score-plus-age model. Age was selected as the primary clinical comparator for this analysis. AUC values were calculated to compare the discrimination of these models for PM/R classification.

Survival analyses were performed for EFS and OS in TARGET-OS patients with available survival data. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to visualize survival differences between the low-risk and high-risk groups. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals for the association between risk group and survival outcomes. Additional Cox models were fitted using the continuous standardized risk score to evaluate the prognostic association of the risk score as a continuous variable.

Model calibration for PM/R prediction was assessed using a calibration plot comparing predicted probabilities with observed PM/R rates across prediction bins. The Brier score was calculated to quantify overall prediction error, with lower values indicating better agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Because calibration was assessed in the TARGET-OS discovery cohort, it was interpreted as apparent internal calibration rather than external or prospective clinical validation. The final calibration analysis was performed for the combined risk-score-plus-age model, matching the model displayed in Supplementary Fig. S4.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed in the TARGET-OS cohort to identify co-expression modules associated with PM/R risk and immune-related transcriptional programs. The input expression matrix consisted of gene-wise z-score-transformed TARGET-OS VST expression values. Candidate genes were selected from protein-coding PM/R-associated genes with adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5; because this set contained approximately 100 genes in the final analysis, all eligible genes were retained for network construction. An unsigned topological overlap matrix was used. The soft-thresholding power was automatically selected as the first power achieving a scale-free topology fit R² > 0.85, with a fallback value of 6 if this criterion was not reached. Modules were detected with a minimum module size of 30 and merged using a merge cut height of 0.25. Module-trait associations were evaluated using Pearson correlation.

For each module, the module eigengene was calculated as the first principal component of the expression matrix of genes assigned to that module. Module eigengenes were correlated with clinical and molecular traits, including PM/R status, continuous risk score, immune signature scores, antigen-presentation activity, T-cell and CD8 T-cell signatures, IFN-γ response and other functional programs. Pearson correlation analyses were used for module-trait associations, and P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

To prioritize robust module genes, we integrated multiple layers of evidence, including differential-expression results, module membership, gene-trait association and external validation in GSE21257. Module membership was defined as the correlation between individual gene expression and the corresponding module eigengene, whereas gene significance reflected the association between gene expression and PM/R-related traits. Strict candidate genes were required to show DEG evidence, belong to the main immune-related module, have an absolute module membership >0.55 and an absolute gene significance for PM/R >0.20, and show the same direction of association in GSE21257. Because not all candidates reached external FDR significance, external validation was interpreted as directional support rather than uniform statistical significance. The final strict candidate gene set is provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis and risk-score projection

Public scRNA-seq datasets were used to examine the cellular distribution of the PM/R risk signature and to explore HERC5-associated immune biology. Two osteosarcoma scRNA-seq cohorts were included: GSE152048 for cross-dataset assessment of risk-score distribution across cell populations, and GSE162454 for detailed HERC5 expression analysis, virtual knockout analysis and cell-cell communication inference.

For each scRNA-seq dataset, raw 10X count matrices were used to construct Seurat objects. Cells were retained if they had nFeature_RNA > 300, nFeature_RNA < 7,500 and mitochondrial transcript percentage <20%. Seurat objects were created with min.cells = 3 and min.features = 200. Expression values were normalized using the default LogNormalize method, 3,000 variable features were selected, and data were scaled before PCA. UMAP visualization was performed using the first 25 principal components. Clustering was performed at a resolution of 0.6.

HERC5 expression was summarized across annotated cell populations in GSE162454. For each cell type, the percentage of HERC5-expressing cells and the average normalized HERC5 expression level were calculated. HERC5-positive cells were also visualized on the UMAP embedding to show their distribution across the single-cell atlas. Major cell populations were annotated de novo by manual marker-gene module scoring at the cluster level, rather than by directly adopting the original author annotations. Marker sets included canonical lineage markers for osteoblastic tumor, myeloid/macrophage, osteoclast-like, lymphoid, plasma-cell, fibroblast/CAF, endothelial, pericyte/SMC and cycling compartments. Clusters were assigned to the cell type with the highest marker score, including osteoblastic tumor cells, myeloid/macrophage cells, osteoclast-like cells, T cells, B cells, plasma cells, fibroblast/CAF, endothelial cells, pericyte/SMC and cycling cells.

To project the bulk-derived PM/R risk model onto single-cell data, a weighted LASSO score was calculated for each cell using the genes available in the 9-gene model and their corresponding coefficients. The single-cell risk score was computed as the weighted sum of normalized gene expression values multiplied by the TARGET-OS-derived LASSO coefficients. Scores were then summarized by annotated cell type and by broader cell groups, including immune and tumor/stromal compartments. This analysis was used to assess whether the PM/R risk signature was preferentially distributed across specific cellular compartments rather than to define patient-level risk from single-cell data.

Virtual HERC5 knockout analysis

To further investigate HERC5-associated regulatory programs at the single-cell level, an in silico virtual knockout analysis was performed using scTenifoldKnk within the myeloid/macrophage subset of the GSE162454 scRNA-seq cohort. Raw RNA counts were used as input. Genes expressed in at least max(10 cells, 1% of cells) were retained. The virtual knockout was performed with HERC5 as the knockout target, 10 network repetitions, up to 500 sampled cells and three principal components, with the random seed set to 20260526. This analysis did not involve experimental HERC5 knockdown or knockout; instead, it simulated network perturbation from single-cell transcriptomic data. P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Predicted perturbed genes were prioritized using adjusted P < 0.05 or the top-ranked perturbation genes. HERC5 was selected for this analysis because it was included in the final 9-gene PM/R risk model, showed a negative coefficient in the risk-score formula, and was downregulated in PM/R-positive tumors.

Briefly, gene regulatory networks were reconstructed from the single-cell expression matrix, and a virtual HERC5 knockout was simulated by comparing the predicted network structure before and after HERC5 perturbation. Genes affected by the simulated knockout were ranked according to perturbation Z-scores and adjusted P values. Genes with larger perturbation scores were interpreted as being more strongly affected within the inferred HERC5-associated regulatory network.

The top perturbed genes were annotated according to their known immune-related functions, with particular attention to antigen presentation, myeloid activation, IFN-γ response and T-cell activation programs. Functional enrichment analysis was then performed on the perturbed gene list to determine whether HERC5-associated perturbations were concentrated in predefined immune programs. Enrichment results were summarized using overlap counts, odds ratios and FDR-adjusted P values.

Because scTenifoldKnk is a computational network-perturbation method, the virtual knockout results were interpreted as hypothesis-generating evidence for HERC5-associated regulatory relationships rather than experimental evidence of direct gene regulation or functional causality.

Pathway, transcription-factor and cell-cell communication inference

Pathway activity was computationally inferred in the TARGET-OS cohort using PROGENy through decoupleR to investigate signaling programs associated with the PM/R risk score. PROGENy estimates pathway activity from downstream transcriptional response genes rather than from direct biochemical measurements of pathway activation. Human PROGENy models with the top 500 responsive genes per pathway were used, and activity was estimated using multivariate linear modeling with a minimum gene-set size of 5. Inferred pathway activity scores were compared between low-risk and high-risk tumors, and continuous risk scores were correlated with inferred pathway activity using Spearman correlation. Particular attention was given to pathways showing consistent risk-group differences and risk-score correlations, including JAK-STAT and hypoxia-related signaling.

Transcription-factor (TF) activity was computationally inferred using DoRothEA through decoupleR. DoRothEA estimates TF activity from the expression of curated target-gene regulons and should therefore be interpreted as inferred regulatory activity rather than direct measurement of TF protein abundance, binding or activity. Human DoRothEA regulons with confidence levels A, B and C were used, and TF activity was estimated using univariate linear modeling with a minimum regulon size of 5. For each TARGET-OS sample, TF activity scores were estimated from bulk expression profiles. Differences in inferred TF activity between high-risk and low-risk tumors were assessed, and FDR adjustment was applied for multiple testing. To explore HERC5-associated regulatory programs, HERC5 expression was further correlated with inferred TF activity scores. IRF/STAT-related TFs and hypoxia-associated regulators were prioritized for interpretation because of their relevance to interferon signaling, antigen presentation and immune regulation.

Potential cell-cell communication patterns were inferred from the GSE162454 scRNA-seq cohort using CellChat. Annotated major cell populations were used as input cell groups, including osteoblastic tumor cells, myeloid/macrophage cells, osteoclast-like cells, T cells, B cells, plasma cells, fibroblast/CAF, endothelial cells, pericyte/SMC and cycling cells. CellChat estimates ligand-receptor communication probability from cell-type-level expression of curated ligand-receptor pairs; therefore, these results represent transcriptome-inferred communication potential rather than direct evidence of physical cell-cell contact, ligand secretion or functional receptor activation. To reduce computational imbalance, major cell types were downsampled where applicable before CellChat analysis. CellChatDB.human was used and restricted to the Secreted Signaling category. Communication probabilities were computed using the triMean method with population-size correction.

Focused communication analyses were performed to summarize dominant inferred signaling routes among osteoclast-like, myeloid/macrophage, cycling, endothelial and pericyte/SMC compartments. Ligand-receptor interactions involving SPP1, MIF, CD74, CD44, CXCR4, integrins and endothelial-related routes were highlighted because they represented prominent inferred communication axes in the single-cell analysis. CellChat results were interpreted as potential ligand-receptor communication inferred from transcriptomic co-expression patterns, rather than direct evidence of physical interactions or functional ligand activity.

Computational drug-reversal analysis

A computational drug-reversal analysis was performed to identify perturbagen signatures that may counteract PM/R-associated transcriptional programs. This analysis was designed as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating screen rather than as therapeutic validation. The results were used only to prioritize candidate perturbagen classes for future experimental testing and were not interpreted as evidence of drug efficacy or clinical actionability.

Two complementary gene sets were used for reversal analysis. High-risk upregulated genes were defined as genes with adjusted P < 0.05 and log2FC > 0.5, whereas protective downregulated genes were defined as genes with adjusted P < 0.05 and log2FC < −0.5. High-risk upregulated genes were queried against LINCS down signatures to identify perturbagens predicted to reverse high-risk programs, whereas protective downregulated genes were queried against LINCS up signatures to identify perturbagens predicted to restore protective programs.

Perturbagen enrichment was assessed using Enrichr resources, including LINCS_L1000_Chem_Pert_up, LINCS_L1000_Chem_Pert_down and DSigDB. Candidate perturbagens were ranked according to enrichment significance, and adjusted P values were calculated to account for multiple testing. Results were summarized at both the individual-perturbagen level and the mechanism-class level to identify recurrently represented perturbagen categories.

For interpretation, candidate perturbagens were grouped according to their reported target classes, including MEK/MAPK inhibitors, CDK inhibitors, FAK/PYK2 inhibitors, ERBB/EGFR inhibitors, SRC/ABL inhibitors, JAK inhibitors, ALK inhibitors, mTOR inhibitors and IKK/TBK1/NF-κB-related inhibitors. Mechanism classes were assigned using rule-based manual annotation of compound names and known target classes rather than automated database annotations. Because these results were derived entirely from computational signature-matching analyses, nominated compounds were considered candidate perturbagens for future experimental testing and were not interpreted as validated therapeutic options, treatment recommendations or evidence of efficacy in osteosarcoma.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and Fisher exact tests for categorical variables. Unless otherwise specified, all statistical tests were two-sided.

For classification analyses, ROC curves were used to evaluate model discrimination, and AUC values were reported as the primary performance metric. Calibration was assessed using apparent internal calibration plots and the Brier score in TARGET-OS; this analysis was descriptive and did not represent external calibration. For random-gene benchmark analyses, empirical P values were calculated by comparing the observed model AUC with the AUC distribution generated from random signatures.

Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models. HRs and 95% CIs were estimated for the association between risk group or continuous risk score and survival outcomes. EFS and OS analyses were restricted to TARGET-OS patients with available survival information. For EFS, non-censored First Event records were coded as events and censored/no-event records were coded as non-events.

For signature, pathway and TF activity analyses, differences between risk groups were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and correlations with continuous risk scores or HERC5 expression were assessed using Spearman correlation. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and FDR-adjusted P values were reported where appropriate. Statistical significance was generally defined as P < 0.05 or FDR < 0.05, depending on the analysis context.

For computational inference analyses, including scTenifoldKnk, PROGENy, DoRothEA, CellChat and drug-reversal analyses, results were interpreted as hypothesis-generating. These analyses were used to prioritize candidate regulatory programs, communication axes and perturbagens for future validation rather than to establish direct mechanistic causality.

Analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1. The main R packages used in this study included DESeq2 1.48.2, glmnet 4.1.10, pROC 1.19.0.1, survival 3.8.3, survminer 0.5.1, WGCNA 1.73, Seurat 5.3.0, CellChat 1.6.1, decoupleR 2.14.0, dorothea 1.20.0, clusterProfiler 4.16.0, GSVA 2.2.1, ggplot2 4.0.0, patchwork 1.3.2, data.table 1.17.8, xgboost 1.7.11.1, randomForest 4.7.1.2, e1071 1.7.16, caret 7.0.1 and enrichR 3.4.

Results

Transcriptomic alterations associated with pulmonary metastasis/relapse in osteosarcoma

We first characterized transcriptomic alterations associated with pulmonary metastasis/relapse (PM/R) in the TARGET-OS discovery cohort. Among primary osteosarcoma RNA-seq samples with available PM/R status, 56 were classified as No PM/R and 31 as PM/R (Fig. 1a). Thus, PM/R-positive cases represented tumors with documented pulmonary involvement either at diagnosis or during follow-up. Principal-component analysis based on variance-stabilized expression profiles showed broad transcriptomic heterogeneity across TARGET-OS tumors, with partial distributional differences between PM/R-positive and PM/R-negative samples (Fig. 1b).

Transcriptomic discovery of pulmonary metastasis/relapse-associated programs in osteosarcoma.

a, Overview of the TARGET-OS discovery cohort comparing primary osteosarcoma samples classified as PM/R-negative or PM/R-positive according to pulmonary involvement at diagnosis or during follow-up. b, Principal-component analysis of variance-stabilized RNA-seq expression profiles, colored by PM/R status. c, Volcano plot showing differentially expressed genes between PM/R-positive and PM/R-negative tumors, with significantly upregulated and downregulated genes highlighted according to the prespecified false-discovery rate and fold-change thresholds. d, Heatmap of representative top differentially expressed genes across TARGET-OS samples ordered by PM/R status. Values are row-scaled expression z-scores. e, Gene-set enrichment analysis of ranked differential-expression statistics, showing biological programs associated with the PM/R phenotype. Positive normalized enrichment scores indicate enrichment in PM/R-positive tumors, whereas negative scores indicate enrichment in PM/R-negative tumors. Differential expression was performed with DESeq2, and enrichment was assessed using preranked gene-set analysis with multiple-testing correction.

Differential-expression analysis identified a focused set of PM/R-associated genes. Using an adjusted P value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 1 as the prespecified threshold, 56 differentially expressed genes were detected, including 31 upregulated and 25 downregulated genes in PM/R-positive tumors (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Table S2). Representative genes with lower expression in PM/R-positive tumors included HERC5, CD38, CXCL9, GATA3 and IGF2, whereas MMP3, NTN1, MYC and SCARB1 were among the genes with higher expression in PM/R-positive tumors. A heatmap of representative top differentially expressed genes further illustrated coordinated expression differences between PM/R-positive and PM/R-negative tumors (Fig. 1d).

To explore the biological programs underlying these transcriptomic differences, we performed preranked gene-set enrichment analysis. PM/R-associated tumors showed enrichment of biosynthetic and translational programs, including mitochondrial gene expression, cytoplasmic translation, ribosome biogenesis, rRNA metabolism, ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis and rRNA processing. In contrast, PM/R-negative tumors were relatively enriched for immune-related programs, including response to type II interferon, lymphocyte-mediated immunity, immune-regulatory receptor signaling, positive regulation of leukocyte-mediated immunity, NK-cell-mediated immunity and adaptive immune response (Fig. 1e). Together, these findings indicate that PM/R in osteosarcoma is associated with a distinct transcriptomic state characterized by enhanced translational/ribosomal activity and reduced immune-related transcriptional programs.

Construction and external validation of a 9-gene transcriptomic PM/R risk score

We next sought to derive a compact transcriptomic score for PM/R classification. The machine-learning candidate pool comprised the 100 protein-coding genes passing adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 0.5 in the PM/R differential-expression analysis; this broader DEG threshold was used for feature selection, whereas the stricter |log2FC| > 1 set was used to summarize the most prominent DEGs. Multi-algorithm feature voting highlighted several recurrently selected genes, including HERC5, GATA3, SYT2, SP110, SAMD9L, PDE1B, LCP2, IGF2, GJA5 and ACSL5, suggesting that PM/R-related transcriptional variation was captured by a relatively stable set of candidate features (Fig. 2a).

Development, validation and robustness assessment of the PM/R risk model.

a, Consensus feature prioritization across multiple machine-learning algorithms, including penalized regression, random forest, support-vector-machine recursive feature elimination and gradient boosting. Genes selected by multiple methods were considered more stable candidates. b, Coefficients of the final transcriptomic risk model. Positive coefficients increase the predicted PM/R risk score, whereas negative coefficients decrease it. c, Receiver-operating-characteristic curves for PM/R prediction in the TARGET-OS discovery cohort and the independent GSE21257 validation cohort. In GSE21257, the validation score used seven of the nine model genes available on the microarray platform. d, Random-gene-set benchmarking comparing the observed model performance with empirical performance distributions obtained from randomly sampled gene sets. e, Leave-one-gene-out ablation analysis evaluating the contribution of each model gene to external validation performance. A larger decrease in AUC after gene removal indicates a stronger contribution to model robustness. AUC, area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve.

The final LASSO model retained nine genes to construct the PM/R risk score: HERC5, GPR141, SLC45A4, LCN15, MXI1, UCK2, KRTAP13-2, KCNK6 and TRABD2A. Thus, the reported 9-gene risk score corresponds to the LASSO model rather than an averaged or ensemble multi-algorithm predictor. Among these, LCN15, MXI1, UCK2 and KRTAP13-2 had positive coefficients and therefore contributed to higher predicted PM/R risk, whereas HERC5, GPR141, SLC45A4, KCNK6 and TRABD2A had negative coefficients and contributed to lower predicted risk (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Table S3).

The risk score showed strong discrimination in the TARGET-OS discovery cohort, with an apparent AUC of 0.922. To assess whether this signature retained predictive information across platforms and cohorts, we further evaluated it in the external GSE21257 cohort. Despite the smaller validation dataset and cross-platform differences, the score maintained moderate discriminatory performance for 5-year metastasis status, with an AUC of 0.728 (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Table S4). This GSE21257 score was calculated from the seven model genes available on the microarray platform while retaining the TARGET-OS-derived coefficients.

To examine whether the observed performance could be explained by random gene selection, we compared the model against randomly generated gene signatures. The observed risk score outperformed the random-gene benchmark in both TARGET-OS and GSE21257, with empirical P values of 0.001 and 0.023, respectively (Fig. 2d). Leave-one-gene-out ablation analysis in GSE21257 further showed that removal of GPR141, SLC45A4 or HERC5 produced the largest decreases in AUC, indicating that these genes made important contributions to the external validation performance (Fig. 2e). Together, these results support the discriminatory ability and cross-cohort transferability of the 9-gene transcriptomic score for identifying PM/R-prone osteosarcoma.

Clinical and prognostic relevance of the PM/R risk score

We then evaluated whether the transcriptomic risk score was associated with clinical PM/R burden and survival outcomes. In the TARGET-OS cohort, patients classified as high risk showed a higher frequency of lung metastasis at diagnosis, PM/R, any metastasis/relapse, death events and corrected EFS events, whereas age, sex and histologic response were broadly comparable between the two risk groups (Table 1). In particular, the corrected EFS event rate was higher in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group (29/44 [65.9%] vs. 12/43 [27.9%]). These event-status summaries used all 87 patients with defined PM/R status and risk groups, whereas the Kaplan-Meier and Cox survival analyses were restricted to 84 patients with valid survival time.

Consistent with the binary risk-group analysis, PM/R-positive tumors had significantly higher risk scores than PM/R-negative tumors (Wilcoxon P = 8.98 × 10⁻¹¹; Fig. 3a). When patients were stratified into low- and high-risk groups, PM/R events were markedly enriched in the high-risk group: 28 of 44 high-risk patients (63.6%) experienced PM/R compared with 3 of 43 low-risk patients (7.0%; Fisher P = 2.06 × 10⁻⁸; odds ratio = 22.3; Fig. 3b).

a, Distribution of the transcriptomic risk score according to PM/R status in TARGET-OS. b, Association between binary risk group and PM/R status, showing enrichment of PM/R events in the high-risk group. c, Discrimination performance of the risk score for PM/R prediction compared with an age-only model and a risk-score-plus-age model. d, e, Kaplan-Meier analyses of event-free survival (d) and overall survival (e) stratified by the risk-score group. For EFS, events were defined using non-censored First Event records. Hazard ratios were estimated using Cox proportional-hazards models, and P values were calculated using log-rank or Cox-model tests as indicated in the analysis output. f, Apparent internal calibration plot comparing predicted and observed PM/R probabilities for the combined risk-score-plus-age model. Error bars indicate approximate 95% confidence intervals for observed event rates within prediction bins.

Because age was selected as the primary clinical comparator for this analysis, we compared the risk score with an age-only model rather than a multivariable clinical model. The age-only model showed limited discrimination for PM/R (AUC = 0.537), whereas the transcriptomic risk score achieved substantially higher discrimination (AUC = 0.922). Adding age to the risk score did not materially improve the AUC (risk score + age AUC = 0.922; Fig. 3c).

Survival analysis further showed that the risk score stratified patient outcomes. Using the corrected EFS definition based on non-censored First Event records, the high-risk group had significantly worse EFS than the low-risk group (n = 84; events = 41; HR = 3.14, 95% CI, 1.60–6.16; Cox P = 8.76 × 10⁻⁴; Fig. 3d). The high-risk group also showed significantly poorer OS (n = 84; events = 29; HR = 3.75, 95% CI, 1.60–8.79; Cox P = 0.002; Fig. 3e). Apparent internal calibration analysis in the TARGET-OS cohort showed agreement between predicted and observed PM/R probabilities, with a Brier score of 0.107 (Fig. 3f), but this result should be interpreted descriptively rather than as external validation of clinical utility. Together, these results indicate that the PM/R risk score captures clinically meaningful metastatic and prognostic information beyond age alone.

High-risk tumors display reduced immune activity and antigen-presentation signatures

To investigate the biological context captured by the PM/R risk score, we next compared immune and functional signatures between the high- and low-risk groups. High-risk tumors showed a broad reduction in immune-related activity, with lower scores for antigen presentation, macrophage and M1-like macrophage signatures, IFN-γ response, immune checkpoint expression, T-cell, CD8 T-cell, NK-cell, B-cell, plasma-cell and cytotoxicity-related programs (Fig. 4a). In contrast, proliferation- and ribosome/translation-related signatures were not similarly decreased, suggesting that the high-risk state was characterized primarily by immune attenuation rather than a global loss of transcriptional activity.

Negative values indicate lower signature activity in high-risk tumors. b, Spearman correlations between the continuous risk score and immune-related signature scores. c, Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) module-trait association heatmap. Correlations and nominal P values are shown for selected modules and clinical or immune traits. The brown module showed positive associations with immune activity and negative associations with risk-related traits. d, Evidence matrix summarizing strict candidate genes supported by differential expression, module membership, gene-trait association and external validation evidence. Consistent with the group-wise comparison, continuous risk scores were negatively correlated with multiple immune signatures across TARGET-OS samples (Fig. 4b). The strongest inverse associations involved antigen presentation, macrophage and M1-like signatures, IFN-γ response, immune checkpoint activity, T-cell signatures and CD8 T-cell signatures. For example, antigen presentation, macrophage, M1-like and IFN-γ response signatures were all significantly lower in high-risk tumors and showed negative correlations with the risk score, supporting a coordinated immune-suppressed phenotype in PM/R-prone osteosarcoma.

These findings indicate that the transcriptomic risk score reflects not only metastatic or relapse status but also a distinct immune microenvironmental state. In particular, the consistent reduction in antigen-presentation and lymphocyte-associated programs suggests that reduced tumor-immune interaction-related transcriptional activity may be a major feature of high-risk osteosarcoma.

WGCNA identifies protective immune-module genes associated with lower PM/R risk

To further define gene modules underlying the immune-suppressed high-risk phenotype, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) in TARGET-OS. Among the identified modules, the brown module showed the strongest immune-related pattern. This module was positively correlated with antigen presentation, CD8 T-cell, IFN-γ response, T-cell and cytotoxicity signatures, whereas it was negatively associated with the continuous risk score and showed a negative trend with PM/R status (Fig. 4c; Supplementary Table S5B). These module-trait relationships suggest that loss of a coordinated immune transcriptional module may be linked to PM/R-prone osteosarcoma.

We then integrated differential expression, module membership, trait association and external validation evidence to prioritize robust candidate genes. This multi-evidence strategy identified ten strict candidate genes: CD38, TRIM22, GBP4, POU2F2, ACSL5, GJA5, SORL1, PDE1B, TNFSF13B and LCP2 (Fig. 4d; Supplementary Table S5). Most of these genes belonged to the brown immune module, with GJA5 assigned to the greenyellow module. These candidates were generally downregulated in PM/R-positive tumors and showed consistent directionality in GSE21257, supporting their association with a protective immune-related transcriptional state.

Cross-cohort visualization further confirmed that these candidates had negative TARGET-OS log2 fold changes and external validation support in GSE21257 (Fig. 4e). Notably, several of these genes, including CD38, LCP2, TNFSF13B, TRIM22 and GBP4, are closely related to immune activation, lymphocyte biology or interferon-associated responses. Together, the WGCNA results reinforce the observation that high-risk osteosarcoma is characterized by attenuation of immune and antigen-presentation programs, and they nominate a set of protective immune-module genes for downstream mechanistic interpretation.

Single-cell and virtual knockout analyses nominate HERC5-associated antigen-presentation biology

Given that HERC5 was included in the final risk model and was also downregulated in PM/R-positive tumors, we next focused on its potential cellular and regulatory context using public single-cell RNA-seq data. Using the marker-score-based cluster annotation described above, major cell populations in the GSE162454 osteosarcoma single-cell atlas included osteoblastic tumor cells, myeloid/macrophage cells, osteoclast-like cells, T cells, B cells, plasma cells, fibroblast/CAF, endothelial cells, pericyte/SMC and cycling cells (Fig. 5a). HERC5-positive cells were distributed across multiple compartments rather than being restricted to a single cell type (Fig. 5b). Quantitative cell-type analysis showed that HERC5 expression was most prominent in cycling cells, followed by T cells and myeloid/macrophage cells, with lower but detectable expression in osteoblastic tumor and stromal compartments (Fig. 5c; Supplementary Fig. S1a).

Predicted perturbation effects were concentrated in antigen-presentation and myeloid-associated genes, including CD74, HLA-DP/HLA-DR genes and C1QB. f, Enrichment of immune programs among HERC5-knockout-perturbed genes. The dashed line indicates the nominal FDR threshold of 0.05. FDR, false-discovery rate.

We then projected the 9-gene risk signature onto single-cell transcriptomes to explore its distribution across cellular compartments. In both GSE152048 and GSE162454, immune cell populations showed lower weighted LASSO scores overall, whereas tumor and stromal compartments tended to show relatively higher scores (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Table S6). This pattern suggests that the bulk-tumor risk score may partly reflect differences in the balance between immune and non-immune compartments, as well as transcriptional states within these compartments.

To further prioritize HERC5-associated downstream biology, we performed an in silico virtual HERC5 knockout analysis using scTenifoldKnk. The leading predicted perturbed genes included CD74, C1QB, HLA-DPB1, HLA-DPA1, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DRA, CCL3 and CCL3L1, many of which are involved in antigen presentation or myeloid immune programs (Fig. 5e; Supplementary Fig. S1b). Functional enrichment of the predicted perturbed genes showed significant enrichment of antigen-presentation genes, whereas myeloid activation showed a weaker, non-significant trend (Fig. 5f; Supplementary Fig. S1c).

These single-cell analyses support HERC5 as a candidate immune-associated node within the PM/R risk biology. Importantly, because the virtual knockout analysis is computational and was not experimentally validated in this study, these results should be interpreted as prioritizing a HERC5-linked antigen-presentation program rather than demonstrating direct functional causality.

Regulatory activity and cell-cell communication analyses connect HERC5-linked immune suppression with microenvironmental remodeling

To place the HERC5-associated immune program within a broader regulatory context, we inferred pathway and transcription-factor activities in TARGET-OS. PROGENy-inferred pathway activity analysis showed that high-risk tumors had lower inferred JAK-STAT activity and higher inferred hypoxia-related activity compared with low-risk tumors (Fig. 6a). The continuous risk score was also negatively correlated with inferred JAK-STAT activity and positively correlated with inferred hypoxia activity, suggesting that PM/R-prone tumors may be associated with reduced interferon/JAK-STAT transcriptional output and enhanced hypoxia-related transcriptional states.

Dominant inferred interactions included SPP1-integrin/CD44 and MIF-CD74-related axes. Point size indicates inferred communication probability, and colors indicate signaling pathway assignment.

Consistent with this pattern, DoRothEA-inferred transcription-factor activity analysis showed reduced inferred activity of several immune- and antigen-presentation-associated regulators in high-risk tumors, including IRF1, STAT2, RFX5 and IRF2, whereas hypoxia-associated regulators such as EPAS1 and HIF1A showed higher inferred activity (Fig. 6b). Correlation analysis further linked HERC5 expression to inferred IRF/STAT-related regulatory activity. HERC5 expression was positively correlated with inferred IRF9, STAT2, IRF1 and IRF2 activity, supporting a putative HERC5-linked regulatory program involving interferon-response and antigen-presentation biology (Fig. 6c).

We next used CellChat to explore transcriptome-inferred intercellular communication potential in the GSE162454 single-cell cohort. Focused communication analysis identified prominent inferred signaling routes among osteoclast-like, myeloid/macrophage, cycling, endothelial and pericyte/SMC compartments (Fig. 6d). At the ligand-receptor level, the dominant inferred interactions involved SPP1-integrin/CD44 routes, MIF-CD74/CD44 or MIF-CD74/CXCR4 axes, and NAMPT-related endothelial interactions (Fig. 6e). These patterns suggest that the HERC5-linked immune regulatory state may coexist with SPP1/MIF-centered microenvironmental communication potential, although they do not demonstrate physical ligand-receptor interactions.

Together, these regulatory and cell-cell communication analyses connect the PM/R risk score to reduced inferred JAK-STAT/IRF activity, increased inferred hypoxia-associated signaling and altered immune-stromal communication potential. Because PROGENy, DoRothEA and CellChat are inference-based approaches, these findings should be interpreted as computational evidence nominating regulatory and communication programs for further validation rather than direct proof of pathway activation, TF activity or physical ligand-receptor interactions.

Exploratory computational drug-reversal analysis identifies candidate perturbagens for risk-signature modulation

Finally, we performed an exploratory computational drug-reversal analysis to examine whether the PM/R-associated transcriptional programs matched known perturbagen signatures. This analysis was intended as a hypothesis-generating screen rather than therapeutic validation. Using genes upregulated in the high-risk state, the top-ranked computational matches included several MEK/MAPK-, CDK-, FAK/PYK2- and ERBB/EGFR-related perturbagens, such as AZD-8330, CGP-60474, alvocidib, PF-562271 and neratinib (Supplementary Fig. S3a).

We also analyzed perturbagens predicted to restore protective genes that were relatively lower in high-risk tumors. This complementary analysis prioritized candidate perturbagens targeting SRC/ABL, JAK, ALK, mTOR and IKK/TBK1/NF-κB-related pathways, including saracatinib, CYT387, NVP-TAE684, OSI-027 and TPCA-1 (Supplementary Fig. S3b). Mechanism-class summarization further showed that multiple kinase-related perturbagen categories were recurrently represented across the reversal results (Supplementary Fig. S3c).

In parallel, DSigDB-based drug-gene enrichment highlighted additional candidate signatures, including deferoxamine, ciclopirox and hypoxia/oxygen-related perturbation terms (Supplementary Fig. S3d). These findings suggest that PM/R-associated expression programs have computational reversal matches among perturbagens targeting kinase signaling, inflammatory signaling, cell-cycle regulation and hypoxia-related biology. However, these candidates should be interpreted only as computationally prioritized perturbagens for future experimental evaluation, not as validated therapeutic options or evidence of treatment efficacy in osteosarcoma.

Discussion

In this study, we developed an integrative transcriptomic and single-cell framework to characterize pulmonary metastasis/relapse (PM/R)-associated risk in osteosarcoma. Using the TARGET-OS cohort, we constructed a compact 9-gene transcriptomic risk score that showed high apparent discrimination for PM/R in the discovery cohort; a reduced seven-gene implementation retained moderate cross-cohort performance in GSE21257. Beyond classification performance, the risk score was closely associated with PM/R burden, event-free survival and overall survival, suggesting that it captured clinically meaningful high-risk disease biology. Mechanistically, high-risk tumors exhibited broad attenuation of immune-related transcriptional programs, particularly antigen presentation, IFN-γ response and lymphocyte-associated signatures. WGCNA, single-cell projection and virtual HERC5 knockout analyses further nominated a HERC5-linked antigen-presentation program, while pathway, transcription-factor and cell-cell communication inference connected this high-risk state to reduced JAK–STAT/IRF activity and altered SPP1/MIF-centered microenvironmental communication. Together, these findings suggest that PM/R-prone osteosarcoma may be characterized by a coordinated transcriptomic state involving metastatic risk, immune attenuation and microenvironmental remodeling.

The clinical relevance of the risk score lies in its focus on PM/R, which is one of the most consequential patterns of treatment failure in osteosarcoma. Although several clinical models have been developed to estimate the risk of pulmonary metastasis using readily available clinicopathologic or hematologic variables, their predictive performance is generally moderate and their biological interpretability remains limited [21,22]. In parallel, recent reviews have emphasized that osteosarcoma prognostic models are increasingly incorporating molecular and transcriptomic features, but many remain insufficiently validated across independent cohorts or are not tailored to specific failure patterns such as PM/R [23]. In this context, our 9-gene score may provide a transcriptomic complement to conventional clinical assessment by identifying tumors with higher PM/R burden and poorer EFS and OS. Importantly, the external validation in GSE21257 should be interpreted as evidence of cross-cohort signal transferability rather than definitive clinical validation, because the validation endpoint was 5-year metastasis and only seven of the nine model genes were available on the microarray platform.

Our study should also be interpreted in the context of previous transcriptomic risk models for osteosarcoma. Several studies have developed metastasis-related, microRNA–mRNA-based or immune-related prognostic signatures using public datasets and machine-learning or regularized regression approaches [[24], [25], [26]]. These studies collectively support the feasibility of molecular risk stratification in osteosarcoma, but most signatures were designed primarily for overall survival prediction or broad metastatic risk rather than a PM/R-focused endpoint. In addition, many models emphasize predictive performance while providing limited cellular or regulatory interpretation of the high-risk state. Compared with these studies, the present analysis has two main distinguishing features: first, the model was developed around PM/R, a clinically relevant failure pattern in osteosarcoma; second, the risk score was further linked to immune attenuation, antigen-presentation reduction, HERC5-associated IRF/STAT activity and single-cell communication programs. Thus, our work extends previous transcriptomic modeling efforts by integrating risk classification with biological interpretation of the PM/R-prone tumor state.

A major biological feature of the high-risk group in our analysis was the coordinated reduction of immune-related transcriptional programs, particularly antigen presentation, IFN-γ response, T-cell, CD8 T-cell, NK-cell and cytotoxicity-associated signatures. Recent reviews of the osteosarcoma immune microenvironment similarly emphasize that immune-cell composition, immune evasion and microenvironment modulation are closely related to metastatic progression, therapeutic resistance and immunotherapy development [27,28]. This finding is consistent with previous evidence that immune infiltration and PD-L1 expression are prognostically relevant in osteosarcoma, suggesting that variation in the immune microenvironment may influence disease behavior [29]. Immunogenomic analyses have also classified osteosarcoma into immune-high and immune-low subtypes, with immune-high tumors showing stronger HLA expression, immune-cell infiltration and more favorable prognostic features [30]. More broadly, impaired antigen presentation is a recognized mechanism of tumor immune evasion because loss or attenuation of MHC-related antigen-processing machinery can reduce tumor visibility to cytotoxic T cells [31]. Therefore, the immune-attenuated state observed in high-risk tumors in our study may reflect a reduced tumor-immune recognition program rather than a nonspecific decrease in transcriptional activity. The WGCNA results further supported this interpretation by identifying a protective immune-related module containing genes such as CD38, LCP2, TNFSF13B, GBP4 and TRIM22, which were generally lower in PM/R-positive tumors and directionally supported in the external cohort.

The HERC5-centered findings provide a potential link between the risk score and interferon-responsive antigen-presentation biology. HERC5 is an interferon-inducible HECT-type E3 ligase required for ISG15 conjugation in human cells, supporting its role as part of the broader interferon-stimulated gene program [32]. IFN-γ signaling can enhance antigen-processing and antigen-presentation machinery through the JAK/STAT1–IRF1 axis, while tumor-cell downregulation of antigen-presentation components is increasingly recognized as a mechanism of impaired immune surveillance [33,34]. In our analysis, HERC5 showed a negative coefficient in the PM/R risk score and was downregulated in PM/R-positive tumors, whereas in silico HERC5 knockout predicted perturbation of CD74 and multiple HLA class II-related genes. Consistently, high-risk tumors showed reduced JAK–STAT activity and lower inferred activity of IRF1, STAT2, RFX5 and IRF2, while HERC5 expression was positively associated with IRF/STAT-related transcription-factor activity. Although these computational results do not establish direct causality, they nominate a putative HERC5-linked antigen-presentation and interferon-regulatory program that may contribute to the immune-attenuated phenotype of PM/R-prone osteosarcoma. The involvement of HLA class II-related genes is also noteworthy because tumor or microenvironmental MHC-II expression has been associated with antitumor immune engagement and favorable outcomes across several cancers [35].

The single-cell communication analysis further suggests that PM/R-associated risk may involve not only immune attenuation but also broader microenvironmental remodeling. CellChat provides a framework for inferring ligand–receptor communication networks from single-cell transcriptomic data, but such results should be interpreted as computationally inferred signaling potential rather than direct evidence of physical cell-cell interactions [36]. In our analysis, prominent inferred interactions involved SPP1-integrin/CD44 routes and MIF-CD74/CD44 or MIF-CD74/CXCR4 axes, mainly connecting osteoclast-like, myeloid/macrophage, cycling, endothelial and pericyte/SMC compartments. This interpretation is supported by recent osteosarcoma single-cell work linking tumor-microenvironment communication programs to metastatic behavior [19]. This pattern is biologically plausible because SPP1, also known as osteopontin, can modulate tumor progression, angiogenesis, invasion and the tumor microenvironment through interactions with integrins and CD44 [37]. Similarly, the MIF-CD74 axis has been increasingly recognized as an immune- and inflammation-related signaling pathway involved in cancer progression and tumor-immune regulation [38]. Therefore, the inferred SPP1/MIF-centered communication programs may represent a microenvironmental context that coexists with HERC5-linked immune attenuation in high-risk osteosarcoma. However, these findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and require spatial or experimental validation to confirm the cellular proximity, ligand activity and functional consequences of these predicted interactions.

The computational drug-reversal analysis provides an additional hypothesis-generating layer for interpreting the PM/R-associated transcriptional state. By querying high-risk and protective gene sets against perturbation-signature resources, this analysis prioritized candidate perturbagens and mechanism classes related to MEK/MAPK, CDK, FAK/PYK2, ERBB/EGFR, SRC/ABL, JAK, ALK, mTOR and IKK/TBK1/NF-κB signaling. Such findings are biologically plausible because perturbation-signature matching has been widely used to connect disease-associated transcriptional programs with candidate small-molecule perturbagens [39,40]. In osteosarcoma, targeted therapeutic strategies, particularly kinase inhibitors, have been explored for relapsed or metastatic disease, although clinical benefits remain variable and no broadly effective targeted approach has been established [41]. Therefore, the drug-reversal results in this study should not be interpreted as therapeutic recommendations, predicted treatment sensitivity or evidence of efficacy. Instead, they provide a prioritized list of candidate perturbagen classes that may help guide future experimental studies of PM/R-associated transcriptional programs.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was based entirely on retrospective public datasets, and the sample size was limited because osteosarcoma is a rare malignancy. Although we performed cross-cohort validation in GSE21257, the validation endpoint was 5-year metastasis rather than the identical PM/R definition used in TARGET-OS, and only seven of the nine model genes were available on the microarray platform. In addition, the PM/R endpoint combined pulmonary metastasis present at diagnosis with pulmonary relapse/metastasis documented during follow-up; therefore, the model should not be interpreted as a purely prospective predictor of future pulmonary relapse among initially non-metastatic patients. Accordingly, the external validation should be regarded as supportive evidence of signal transferability rather than definitive clinical validation. Second, age was used as the primary clinical comparator, whereas other available or clinically important variables could not be comprehensively incorporated because of missingness, endpoint structure or limited sample size. Important prognostic factors such as tumor size, anatomical site, surgical margin, chemotherapy response, treatment protocol and metastasectomy status could not be comprehensively adjusted for. Similarly, calibration was evaluated internally in the discovery cohort and therefore requires confirmation in independent prospective datasets before clinical implementation. Third, although we followed principles consistent with transparent prediction-model reporting, the risk score still requires validation in larger, independent and preferably prospective cohorts before clinical application [42,43]. Finally, the mechanistic analyses, including WGCNA, scTenifoldKnk, PROGENy, DoRothEA, CellChat and drug-reversal screening, were computational and inference-based. These approaches are useful for nominating candidate regulatory programs and communication axes, but they cannot establish direct causality or functional activity without experimental, spatial or clinical validation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study developed a PM/R-focused transcriptomic risk score for osteosarcoma and linked high-risk disease to an immune-attenuated transcriptional state characterized by reduced antigen-presentation, IFN-γ response and lymphocyte-associated programs. By integrating bulk transcriptomics, external validation, WGCNA and single-cell inference, we further nominated HERC5 as a candidate immune-associated node connected to antigen-presentation and IRF/STAT-related regulatory activity. The inferred SPP1/MIF-centered communication patterns and drug-reversal results provide additional hypotheses regarding microenvironmental remodeling and potentially targetable transcriptional programs. Overall, these findings provide a computational framework for PM/R risk stratification and generate candidate biological hypotheses that warrant further validation in independent clinical cohorts and experimental models.
