Female Sex in Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Camilla Cocchi, Anna Vittoria Mattioli
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the recent paper by McGarvey et al.1 The authors should be commended for addressing an important and clinically relevant question: whether female sex should be considered an independent risk factor or a risk modifier for thromboembolic stroke in patients with atrial fibrillation.1

Using a large TriNetX-based cohort, the authors showed that female sex was not associated with a significantly increased risk of thromboembolic stroke among patients younger than 75 years, either in those without additional CHA2DS2-VA risk factors or in those with 1 additional risk factor.1 Conversely, among patients aged 75 years or older, female sex was associated with a statistically significant but modest increase in stroke risk. These findings support the concept that female sex does not uniformly increase thromboembolic risk across all patients with atrial fibrillation, but rather modifies risk in the presence of advanced age and greater comorbidity burden.2

This work has important implications for clinical decision-making. The inclusion of female sex in the CHA2DS2-VASc score has historically influenced anticoagulation decisions, particularly in women with otherwise low or intermediate risk.3 However, the findings by McGarvey et al1 reinforce the rationale behind the simplified CHA2DS2-VA score, suggesting that anticoagulation decisions should not be driven by sex alone, especially in younger women without additional risk factors. This may help reduce unnecessary anticoagulation exposure in low-risk female patients, while preserving appropriate treatment in older patients or those with multiple comorbidities.

Nevertheless, several aspects deserve further consideration. First, although the study benefits from a very large sample size, the absolute risk differences observed in older women were small. This distinction between statistical significance and clinical relevance is crucial when translating population-level findings into individualized treatment decisions. Second, the retrospective design and reliance on electronic health record data introduce potential limitations, including residual confounding, coding inaccuracies, incomplete assessment of anticoagulation adherence, and lack of information on important variables such as left atrial size, atrial fibrosis, atrial fibrillation burden, frailty, and hormonal status. These factors may be particularly relevant when evaluating sex-related differences in atrial remodeling and stroke risk.2,4

Importantly, the study also highlights the need to move beyond a binary interpretation of sex in cardiovascular risk stratification. Female patients with atrial fibrillation are heterogeneous, and their risk may be influenced by age, menopausal status, comorbidities, diagnostic delay, undertreatment, and differences in access to rhythm-control strategies.5 Future prospective studies should therefore investigate biological and clinical mechanisms underlying the increased stroke risk observed in older women, including atrial fibrosis, inflammatory pathways, anticoagulation quality, and sex-specific treatment patterns.

In conclusion, McGarvey et al1 provide valuable evidence supporting the view that female sex is best understood as a context-dependent risk modifier rather than a uniform risk factor in atrial fibrillation. Their findings encourage a more individualized approach to stroke prevention, particularly in women younger than 75 years without additional thromboembolic risk factors. Further prospective and mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify how sex, age, comorbidity, and atrial substrate interact to influence stroke risk in atrial fibrillation.
