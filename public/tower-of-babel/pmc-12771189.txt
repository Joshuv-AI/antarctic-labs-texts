Microstructural Engineering for Power Factor and ZT Optimization in Cu Alloyed n‑Type Bi2–x Sb x Te3 Thermoelectric Materials
Authors: Soo-Ho Jung, Jong Min Park, Linh Ba Vu, Seungki Jo, Jinhee Bae, Su-a Kwon, Jeong-Yun Sun, Young Heon Kim, Kyung Tae Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bismuth telluride (Bi2Te3) alloys are widely used thermoelectric (TE) materials for near-room-temperature energy conversion. The performance of TE devices in this range critically depends on n-type Bi2Te3 alloys for performance parity with that of their p-type counterparts. This study presents a strategy for tuning the thermoelectric properties of selenium-free n-type Bi2–x Sb x Te3 (BST) through controlled copper (Cu) alloying. Cu incorporation via ball milling enhanced the Seebeck coefficient but reduced electrical conductivity due to the formation of coarse Cu-rich precipitation. Subsequent melt-spinning recovered conductivity and promoted uniform Cu dispersion while inducing beneficial point defects and lowering lattice thermal conductivity. These combined effects shifted the peak ZT value toward room temperature, achieving better alignment with the operational range of the p-type materials. A four-pair TE device fabricated with 0.1 wt % Cu alloyed BST (melt-spun) demonstrated a 20% increase in output power at T hot = 450 K compared to the pristine sample. This work demonstrates the role of Cu alloying and microstructural engineering in optimizing n-type BST for efficient thermoelectric power generation.

Introduction

Thermoelectric (TE) materials have long been recognized for their ability to directly convert heat into electricity and vice versa, making them valuable in applications ranging from waste heat recovery. The performances of the TE materials are defined by the figure of merit, ZT. ZT is given by the relationship below, where S is the Seebeck coefficient, σ is the electrical conductivity, T is the absolute temperature, and κ is the thermal conductivity. 1 ZT=σS2κT

However, the relationships between these parameters are complex and often result in trade-offs. Therefore, the key to developing high-performance thermoelectric materials lies in achieving a balance among interdependent properties. Moreover, to achieve optimal efficiency and reliability in TE devices, precise matching of both the ZT values and retention of the chemical composition in the p- and n-type legs is required. In particular, balancing the peak ZT values between p- and n-type materials is crucial for the high performance of TE devices.

Among the TE materials, bismuth telluride (Bi2Te3) and its alloys stand out due to their exceptional thermoelectric properties near room temperature, which are critical for low-temperature energy harvesting applications. − Recently, a selenium (Se)-free n-type Bi2–x Sb x Te3 (BST) alloy has been proposed to achieve a peak ZT of 1.0. Mechanical deformation techniques, primarily through ball milling and spark plasma sintering, have been applied to the n-type alloy to induce a p- to n-type transition by controlling intrinsic point defects. However, the proposed BST alloy exhibits a high carrier concentration, which results in a ZT imbalance compared to p-type Bi2–x Sb x Te3. −

To address this challenge, various modification strategies, including dopant incorporation and nanostructuring, have been explored to fine-tune electrical and thermal transport properties. − Alloying or doping, in particular, has been extensively used to modify carrier concentration and scattering mechanisms, thereby influencing both electrical and thermal transport properties. − Among them, Cu modified TE materials have emerged as a particularly promising approach due to their ability to alter the band structure and carrier concentration. − However, the incorporation of Cu has shown inconsistent effects on TE performance, exhibiting variability in both p- and n-type materials. Consequently, further investigations are required to achieve consistent enhancements in the thermoelectric performance.

In this study, we present a novel approach to modifying the thermoelectric performance of Se-free n-type BST alloys through controlled Cu alloying. Our primary focus was to investigate the effect of Cu alloying in n-type BST and how the lattice-scale distribution of Cu atoms influences the thermoelectric properties of selenium-free n-type Bi2–x Sb x Te3 (BST). Initial Cu alloying via ball milling led to the formation of Cu rich secondary phases, which suppressed electrical conductivity but simultaneously enhanced the Seebeck coefficient, likely due to the formation of BiTe point defects that induce hole carriers. ,,

To improve the distribution of Cu within the BST matrix, a melt spinning process was employed, enabling rapid solidification and uniform Cu dispersion. Since melt spinning has already been shown to effectively introduce nanostructures and reduce lattice thermal conductivity in other materials, we employed the same technique in selenium-free n-type BST to modulate microstructure and enhance phonon scattering. , This process not only restored electrical conductivity but also reduced the lattice thermal conductivity by enhancing phonon scattering. As a result, Cu alloying combined with melt spinning process had multiple effects, including reduced electrical conductivity due to decreased carrier concentration, enhanced Seebeck coefficient, and reduced lattice thermal conductivity along with increased bipolar thermal conductivity approximately over 350 K. These effects optimized the power factor and shifted the ZT value closer to room temperature, helping to balance the performance between p- and n-type legs. A fabricated four-pair TE device using Cu alloyed BST demonstrated a 20% increase in output power at 450 K, validating the effectiveness of this approach.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of Cu Alloyed Bi1.7Sb0.3Te3 Powder

Bi, Sb, Te, and Cu shots were combined in the desired atomic weight percentages and subjected to planetary ball milling at 350 rpm for 8 h (Fritsch Monomill, Pulverisette, Germany). After synthesizing the Cu alloyed Bi1.7Sb0.3Te3 powder, it underwent heat treatment at 573 K for 1 h in a hydrogen atmosphere for reduction. The resulting pristine powder was either immediately sintered using the SPS method or cold compressed with a hand compressor for the melt spinning process.

Melt Spinning and SPS Process

Recovered pristine Bi1.7Sb0.3Te3 or Cu alloyed compressed pellets were put into the quartz tube with a 0.5 mm diameter nozzle in the melt spinning system. The molten materials were injected to the Cu wheel rotating speed of 1000 rpm. Recovered melt spun ribbon was pulverized and compacted under 50 MPa using SPS at 753 K for 5 min. Heating rate for the sintering was 100 K min–1.

Measurement of the Cu Alloyed Bi1.7Sb0.3Te3 Sample

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed on powder and bulk samples for the phase characterization (Rigaku, D/max 2500, Japan). Surface morphology was investigated by SEM analysis (FE-SEM, MIRA3 LM, TESCAN, Czech Republic). Microstructure and the detailed image of the dislocation was analyzed by TEM (JEOL, JEM-F200, Japan). Carrier concentration and carrier mobility were confirmed by Hall effect measurement (Ecopia, Model AHT55T3, South Korea). Electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficient were measured from 300 to 500 K by using SBA-458 (NETZSCH, Germany) in the Ar atmosphere. Thermal diffusion coefficient (D) was measured by the laser flash method (NETZSCH LFA 457, Germany). The specific heat (C p) was deduced by the Dulong-Petit limit. Density (ρ s) of the sample was measured by the Archimedes method. Total thermal conductivity was calculated from the measured data (κ = ρ s × C p × D).

Fabrication and Power Generation Measurement of the Four-Pair Device

Two n-type materials were chosen: pristine Bi1.7Sb0.3Te3 and melt spun Cu 0.1 wt % alloyed Bi1.7Sb0.3Te3 sample. For the counterpart, pristine Bi0.5Sb1.5Te3 was chosen, which was synthesized using the same process as that of pristine Bi1.7Sb0.3Te3. The sintered TE wafers were processed into the cuboids (legs) with dimensions of 2 × 2 × 4 mm3. The protective layer was subsequently applied to the TE legs via nickel electroless plating. Three solutions, designated LFS1, LFS2, and LFS3, were purchased from SAS and combined to attain a pH of 9. The precise mixing ratio and the coating process were regulated in accordance with the manual provided by SAS. For each device, four p-type legs and four n-type TE legs were used. Al2O3 plates were selected as the substrate for the device, and a Cu based circuit was printed on the substrate. The TE legs were attached to the substrate by SAC 305 paste and thermally treated in an Ar atmosphere at 523 K for 20 min. After the heat treatment, the device was soldered at both ends to affix the Cu wires, and the output power was subsequently measured by using a source meter. To provide the temperature differential, a hot plate and the circulator were employed.

Results and Discussion

It is well-known that Bi2–x Sb x Te3 alloys exhibit intrinsic p-type characteristics over a wide range of Sb content. Figure a,b shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the Cu alloyed samples. No noticeable change in crystal structure or formation of secondary phases was observed regardless of whether the melt spinning process was applied. In Figure c, the lattice parameters remain almost constant along both a- and c-axes, suggesting that the Cu concentration was too low to affect the lattice parameters, or Cu was substituted into the lattice as an antisite defect, such as CuBi. Cu particles were expected to be distributed within the BST grains in the form of relatively large precipitates when only the ball milling process was applied. However, with the addition of the melt spinning process, Cu was expected to be more uniformly distributed due to the melting and rapid quenching of the material. Differences in Cu morphology depending on the processing method were expected to influence thermoelectric properties, particularly phonon scattering. When an alloyed element is well dispersed within the crystal structure, it acts as an extrinsic point defect, reducing the lattice thermal conductivity. However, if the alloyed element forms relatively large particles, then the effect reduces. In contrast, in the case of Cu alloyed melt spun BST (MS-BST) samples, Figure d,e shows no visible Cu precipitates in the low-magnification TEM images. EDS analysis suggests that during the melt spinning process, the Cu was fully melted and uniformly dispersed. Figure shows the electrical transport properties and thermoelectric (TE) performance of Cu alloyed melt spun BST alloys. Electrical conductivity decreases with increasing Cu concentration while minimizing the reduction in the Seebeck coefficient, thereby optimizing the power factor to 2.7 × 10–3 W m–1 K–2. The Cu alloyed pristine BST alloy also exhibits similar trends in electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficients depending on the Cu concentration. However, as the Cu concentration increases, the power factor continues to decrease (Figure S3). This is because the enhancement in electrical conductivity observed with the melt spinning process does not occur in the pristine alloy.

In our previous study, the melt spinning process increased the carrier concentration by inducing V Te to the BST alloys, also increasing the grain size. Figure d presents the Hall measurement results, highlighting the effects of Cu on carrier concentration and mobility. Cu is known to substitute Bi/Sb sites BST alloys, which generate hole carriers and reduce the majority carrier concentration (electrons, in this case). ,,, The incorporation of Cu atoms into the lattice likely acts as a scattering center, reducing charge carrier mobility. The observed reduction in carrier mobility upon Cu alloying is likely due to increased ionized impurity scattering and local lattice distortion, which introduce alloy scattering centers. The decrease in carrier mobility upon Cu alloying can be understood using Matthiessen’s rule, which accounts for multiple scattering mechanisms: 3 1μ=1μI+1μAC where μI is the mobility for ionized impurity scattering and μ AC is acoustic phonon scattering. Cu induces an ionized impurity, and its contribution to μ I is given by 4 μI=64πε2(2kBT)3/2NIe3ms*f(LD) where ε is the dielectric constant, k B is the Boltzmann constant, N I is the ionized impurity concentration, and m s* is single valley effective mass. As N I, increases with Cu addition, μ I decreases.

Local lattice distortion is supported by moiré patterns observed in TEM images (Figure S2), induced by Cu incorporation. Notably, the melt-spun samples exhibit a slightly higher mobility compared to that of pristine samples, suggesting that rapid solidification leads to more uniform Cu dispersion and reduces lattice distortion. Both the carrier concentration and mobility decrease. Figure e shows the Pisarenko plot, demonstrating how the incorporation of Cu alters the effective mass (m d*) and consequently impacts the Seebeck coefficient. The Seebeck coefficient is described by the following equation based on the single parabolic band (SPB) model: 5 S=8π2kB2T3qh2md*(π3n)2/3 where k B is the Boltzmann constant, q is the carrier charge, h is the Planck constant, and m d* is the density of states effective mass. The plot reveals how Cu alloying influences m d*, affecting the Seebeck coefficient. The Cu alloyed pristine BST alloy also follows the same trends, depending on the Cu concentration. The melt spinning process increases both carrier concentration and mobility compared with pristine BST alloys. In conclusion, the combined effects of Cu alloying and the melt spinning process optimized the carrier concentration, thereby enhancing the power factor.

Thermal transport properties were subsequently examined to complete the evaluation of the thermoelectric performance. Figure illustrates the thermal transport properties and TE performance of the Cu alloyed melt spun samples. Cu alloyed samples exhibit reduced total thermal conductivity across the entire temperature range. The electronic thermal conductivity decreases proportionally with increasing Cu concentration. The Debye–Callaway model is often used to explain lattice thermal conductivity, considering various scattering mechanisms such as Umklapp phonon scattering, grain boundary scattering, and point defect scattering. The incorporation of Cu is expected to introduce additional point defects, enhancing scattering and reducing lattice thermal conductivity. However, as the majority carrier concentration decreases proportionally with increasing Cu content, bipolar thermal conductivity increases at higher temperatures. The equations and scattering mechanisms used for the calculations can be found in the Section 3 of the Supporting Information.

In the case of the Cu alloyed pristine samples, the process was carried out through mechanical milling, which resulted in nonuniform dispersion of Cu atoms within the lattice. As a result, the Cu precipitations failed to reduce the lattice thermal conductivity due to their nonuniform distribution and relatively large size. Moreover, bipolar thermal conductivity increases at higher temperatures as the majority carrier concentration decreases. This significantly increases the total thermal conductivity of the Cu alloyed n-type BSTs above 400 K (Figure S4).

As a result, decreased lattice thermal conductivity and increased bipolar thermal conductivity over 350 K shifts peak ZT of the Cu 0.1 wt % alloyed MS-BST sample near room temperature. The Cu 0.1 wt % alloyed MS-BST sample exhibits a peak ZT of 0.61 at 350 K, while the unalloyed BST pristine sample shows a slightly lower peak ZT of 0.59 at 450 K. Among the various Cu incorporation levels, 0.1 wt % Cu leads to the greatest improvement in ZT, accompanied by a 100 K shift toward room temperature. This enhancement in peak ZT is attributed to the combined effects of an optimized power factor and reduced lattice thermal conductivity achieved through the melt spinning process. Notably, the peak ZT for p-type BST alloys occurs at a similar temperature, effectively balancing the ZT values between p- and n-type BST alloys.

In contrast, the Cu 0.1 wt % alloyed pristine BST sample shows a peak ZT of 0.5 at 325 K. The increase in thermal conductivity at 350 K shifted the peak ZT closer to room temperature, but the power factor was not optimized, and the peak ZT value could not be sustained.

To evaluate the actual power generation performance of the sample with a shifted ZT peak, theoretical power output was calculated, and a four-pair device was fabricated. As shown in Figure a, the p-type legs were designed to have a peak ZT value similar to that of the n-type material for device fabrication. This was necessary because if the performance of the p-type material is significantly higher than that of the n-type, then the performance gap due to the lower ZT of the n-type material could obscure the actual device behavior. The peak ZT values of both the p-type material and the Cu 0.1 wt %-MS sample were observed at 350 K, whereas the pristine BST exhibited its peak ZT at 425 K. The maximum efficiency was calculated using the engineering ZT, which enables the prediction of maximum efficiency based solely on σ, S, and κ, without considering the device geometry. The average ZT values of the p- and n-type samples used in the device exhibited a greater discrepancy than the maximum efficiency, with approximately a 10 % difference in ZT avr values within the 300 to 350 K range. As a result, a noticeable difference in power generation capability was observed. The device consisted of four pairs of p- and n-type legs. Power generation was measured by maintaining the cold side temperature at 290 K while adjusting the hot side temperature to 350, 400, and 450 K. Across all temperature ranges, the Cu 0.1 wt %-MS sample outperformed the pristine sample in output power, with the device performance being approximately 20 % higher at T hot = 450 K. The calculated theoretical efficiency ratio of n-type samples is provided in the Supporting Information (Section S4), and the 20 % improvement of the device is somewhat high. Since the device was handmade, the difference could result from minor device imperfections. Nevertheless, considering that the midpoint temperature applied to the device remained below 375 K, the observed trend is consistent with the enhanced ZT value shown in Figures a and S6a.

Conclusions

In summary, this study presents a novel approach to enhancing the thermoelectric performance of selenium-free n-type BST alloys through controlled Cu alloying. By regulating the lattice-scale distribution of Cu within the BST matrix, we successfully optimized the power factor and achieved balanced ZT values between p- and n-type legs as a function of the temperature. The melt spinning process partially aided in the recovery of reduced electrical conductivity and enhanced phonon scattering by promoting the uniform dispersion of Cu atoms. As a result, the Cu 0.1 wt % alloyed sample exhibited a peak ZT near room temperature and delivered a 20 % increase in output power compared to the pristine sample when operated at 450 K. This work not only fills a gap in the existing literature by exploring the effects of Cu alloying in n-type BST but also provides a foundation for future research aimed at optimizing thermoelectric materials for real-world applications.
