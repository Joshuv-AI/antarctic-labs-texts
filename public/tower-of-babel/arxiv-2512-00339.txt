Global dynamics in a reaction-diffusion competition model with edge behavior
Kuiyue Liu and Shanshan Chen

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate a two-species competition model in a landscape consisting of a finite number of adjacent patches. For the two-patch scenario, by treating edge behavior at the interface as a strategy, it has been shown that there exists an ideal free distribution (IFD) strategy, which is a globally evolutionarily stable strategy. Specifically, when the resident species follows the IFD strategy and the mutant species does not, the mutant species is unable to invade the resident population. Building on this foundation, our work focuses on exploring the dynamics of the system when neither species can adopt the IFD strategy. We demonstrate that if the strategies of both species either exceed or fall below the IFD strategy, the mutant species can outcompete and eliminate the resident species, provided that its strategy is closer to the IFD strategy and its diffusion rates are equal to or slower than those of the resident species. Furthermore, if the strategies of the two species lie on opposite sides of the IFD strategy, the two species can coexist. This result is further extended to the case of an arbitrary but finite number of patches. Keywords : global dynamics; invasion analysis; reaction-diffusion; edge behavior MSC 2020 : 35K57, 37C65, 92D25, 92D40.

Discussion

We study a reaction-diffusion model for two-species competition in a fragmented habitat of
n
adjacent patches. The model incorporates habitat preference of individuals, which induces density discontinuities at interfaces. This model differs from established models, such as coupled ordinary differential equations (patch models) and classical reaction-diffusion models, which typically do not account for such discontinuities.

A central question in the study of two-species competition concerns the mechanisms that drive competitive exclusion or enable coexistence. Within a two-patch reaction-diffusion framework incorporating edge behavior,
[
40
]
demonstrated that when habitat preference is viewed as a strategy, the ideal free distribution (IFD) strategy is a global evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS). Our results extend this by proving that the IFD strategy is a global convergent stable strategy (CSS). Specifically, we show that a species with a strategy closer to the IFD can invade and outcompete the resident whose strategy is farther away, provided both strategies lie on the same side of the IFD. Conversely, two species can coexist if their strategies lie on opposite sides of the IFD. Moreover, this result is extended to an
n
-patch reaction-diffusion model incorporating edge behavior.

These findings are consistent with results from both coupled ordinary differential equations (patch models) and classical reaction-diffusion models, in which dispersal is typically treated as a strategy. Indeed, as demonstrated by
[
8
,
38
]
and
[
5
,
7
]
, the IFD strategy is also a global ESS in the frameworks of patch models and classical reaction-diffusion models, respectively. Furthermore, competitive outcomes (exclusion or coexistence) are also determined by whether species’ strategies reside on the same or opposite sides of the IFD, see
[
7
,
36
]
.
Therefore, across all three classes of models, the ideal free distribution (IFD) emerges as a universal principle that governs whether competition results in exclusion or coexistence.

Our analysis relies crucially on the monotonicity of the species steady state for the corresponding single-species model (
2.1
). According to Lemma
A.2
, this monotonicity in solutions to system (
2.1
) is guaranteed if either
\mathbf{p}\gg\overline{\mathbf{k}}
or
\overline{\mathbf{k}}\gg\mathbf{p}
holds. Biologically, this condition requires that the discontinuities in population density at all interfaces are either uniformly larger or uniformly smaller than the corresponding discontinuities in the carrying capacity
[
59
]
. A violation of this condition may lead to non-monotonic steady states, as demonstrated in Proposition
A.3
. Nevertheless, the non-monotonicity of steady states poses analytical challenges that remain to be explored in future work.

(B.7)

T_{t}(I)
is relatively compact in
X_{u}^{\alpha}\times X_{v}^{\alpha}
. This complete the proof.
∎

Finally, we consider the following eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\displaystyle-d_{i}(\phi_{i})_{xx}-c_{i}(x)\phi_{i}=\lambda\phi_{i}(x),\;\;\text{in }\Omega_{i},\;\;i=1,2,\cdots,n,

(C.33a)

\displaystyle\phi_{i+1}(x_{i}^{+})=p_{i}\phi_{i}(x_{i}^{-}),\quad d_{i+1}(\phi_{i+1})_{x}(x_{i}^{+})=d_{i}(\phi_{i})_{x}(x_{i}^{-}),

(C.33b)

\displaystyle(\phi_{1})_{x}(0^{+})=(\phi_{n})_{x}(L^{-})=0.

(C.33c)

Proposition C.9
.

Assume that
c_{i}(x)\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})
for all
i=1,\cdots,n
, and let
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\cdots,d_{n})
. Then the eigenvalue problem (
C.33
) admits a simple eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{d})
with a corresponding positive eigenfunction
\Phi^{*}
, and any other eigenvalue
\lambda\neq\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{d})
of (
C.33
) satisfies

\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)>\lambda_{1}
. Moreover,
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{d})
is continuous with respect to
\mathbf{d}
.

Proof.

Define

\Lambda:=\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\{\|c_{i}(x)\|_{C(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}+1\right\}.

For
\mathbf{g}\in G
, where
G
is defined in (
C.4
), we consider the following problem

\displaystyle\displaystyle-d_{i}(\phi_{i})_{xx}+[\Lambda-c_{i}]\phi_{i}=g_{i},\;\;\text{in}\;\;\Omega_{i},\;\;i=1,2,\cdots,n,

(C.34a)

\displaystyle\phi_{i+1}(x_{i}^{+})=p_{i}\phi_{i}(x_{i}^{-}),\quad d_{i+1}(\phi_{i+1})_{x}(x_{i}^{+})=d_{i}(\phi_{i})_{x}(x_{i}^{-}),

(C.34b)

\displaystyle(\phi_{1})_{x}(0^{+})=(\phi_{n})_{x}(L^{-})=0.

(C.34c)

Define

\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{u}\mathbf{u}:=(-d_{1}(u_{1})_{xx}+(\Lambda-c_{1}(x))u_{1},\cdots,-d_{n}(u_{n})_{xx}+(\Lambda-c_{n}(x))u_{n}),

(C.35)

and the domain of
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{u}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{u}):=\,\{

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{H}_{u}^{2}\cap\mathcal{H}_{u}^{1}:d_{i+1}(u_{i+1})_{x}(x_{i}^{+})=d_{i}(u_{i})_{x}(x_{i}^{-}),\,i=1,\cdots,n-1,

(C.36)

\displaystyle(u_{1})_{x}(0^{+})=(u_{n})_{x}(L^{-})=0\},

Then using arguments similar to those in the proof of Lemma
C.2
, we conclude that
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{u}
is symmetric and that (
C.34
) admits a unique solution
\Phi\in\mathcal{D}(\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{u})
. Moreover,
\Phi
satisfies

\|\Phi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{u}^{2}}\leq\mathcal{C}_{1}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{X_{u}}.

(C.37)

Here the constant
\mathcal{C}_{1}>0
depends on
d_{i}
,
p_{i}
,
1/d_{i}
and
1/p_{i}
(
i=1,\cdots,n
).
Since
\mathcal{H}_{u}^{2}\xhookrightarrow{c}C(\overline{\Omega}_{1})\times\cdots\times C(\overline{\Omega}_{n})
and
G\xhookrightarrow{c}X_{u}
, it follows from (
C.37
) that

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|\phi_{i}\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}\leq\mathcal{C}_{2}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{X_{u}}\leq\mathcal{C}_{3}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{G},

where the constants
\mathcal{C}_{2},\,\mathcal{C}_{3}>0
depends on
d_{i}
,
p_{i}
,
1/d_{i}
and
1/p_{i}
(
i=1,\cdots,n
). This combined with (
C.34a
) implies that
\Phi\in\mathcal{H}^{3}_{u}
and

\|\Phi\|_{\mathcal{H}_{u}^{3}}\leq\mathcal{C}_{4}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{X_{u}},

(C.38)

where the constant
\mathcal{C}_{4}>0
depends on
d_{i}
,
p_{i}
,
1/d_{i}
and
1/p_{i}
(
i=1,\cdots,n
).
The embedding

\mathcal{H}_{u}^{3}\xhookrightarrow{c}C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{1})\times\cdots\times C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{n})
combined with (
C.38
) implies that

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|\phi_{i}\|_{C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{i})}\leq\mathcal{C}_{5}\|\mathbf{g}\|_{G},

(C.39)

where the constant
\mathcal{C}_{5}
depends on
d_{i}
,
p_{i}
,
1/d_{i}
and
1/p_{i}
(
i=1,\cdots,n
).

For fixed
\mathbf{d}\gg\bm{0}
, define the linear operator
T(\mathbf{d}):G\to G
by
T(\mathbf{d})\mathbf{g}=\Phi
, where
\Phi
is the solution of (
C.34
) corresponding to
\mathbf{g}
. Since
\Phi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{1})\times\cdots\times C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{n})
and
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{1})\times\cdots\times C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{n})\xhookrightarrow{c}G
, we can deduce that
T(\mathbf{d})
is compact. Let

\mathcal{K}:=\{\mathbf{w}\in G:w_{i}(x)\geq 0\;\;\text{in}\;\;\Omega_{i}\}

be the positive cone in
G
. This cone has nonempty interior
\mathring{\mathcal{K}}
and satisfies
\mathcal{K}\cap(-\mathcal{K})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. By Theorem
B.5
, we obtain that, if
\mathbf{g}\in\mathcal{K}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
, then
\Phi\in\mathring{\mathcal{K}}
, which implies that
T(\mathbf{d})(\mathcal{K}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\})\subset\mathring{\mathcal{K}}
. Then it follows from the Krein-Rutman theorem (see e.g.,
[
16
, Theorem 1.2]
) that the spectral radius
r(\mathbf{d})
is a simple eigenvalue of
T(\mathbf{d})
with a corresponding positive eigenfunction
\mathbf{g}^{*}
.
This implies that

-d_{i}(\phi_{i}^{*})_{xx}+[\Lambda-c_{i}]\phi_{i}^{*}=\frac{\phi_{i}^{*}}{r(\mathbf{d})}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{i},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,n,

where
\mathbf{\Phi}^{*}=(\phi_{1}^{*},\cdots,\phi_{n}^{*})=T\mathbf{g}^{*}
is positive.
Consequently,
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{d})=1/r(\mathbf{d})-\Lambda
is a simple eigenvalue of (
C.33
) with a positive eigenfunction
\Phi^{*}
.
Then, using arguments similar to those in the proof of
[
16
, Theorem 1.4]
, we deduce that any eigenvalue
\lambda\neq\lambda_{1}
of (
C.33
) satisfies

\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)>\lambda_{1}
.

Finally, we will show that
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{d})
is continuous with respect to
\mathbf{d}
. By (
C.37
), the operator
T(\mathbf{d})
is also bounded and compact on
X_{u}
.
We first show that that
T(\mathbf{d})
is continuous in
L(X_{u})
, where
L(X_{u})
denotes the set of bounded linear operators on
X_{u}
. That is, for any sequence
\left\{\mathbf{d}^{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbf{d}^{k}=\mathbf{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\|T(\mathbf{d}^{k})-T(\mathbf{d})\|_{L(X_{u})}=0,

(C.40)

where
\|\cdot\|_{L(X_{u})}
is the operator norm in
L(X_{u})
.
Since the constant
\mathcal{C}_{4}
in (
C.37
) depends only on
d_{i}
and
1/d_{i}
(
i=1,\cdots,n
), we see from (
C.37
) that

\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{T(\mathbf{d}^{k})\mathbf{g}:\|\mathbf{g}\|_{X_{u}}\leq 1\right\}

is compact in
X_{u}
, and
\left\{\|T(\mathbf{d}^{k})\|_{L(X_{u})}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is bounded. Consequently,
\{T(\mathbf{d}^{k})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is collectively compact. Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{u}
is symmetric, it follows that
T(\mathbf{d}^{k})
is self-adjoint. Then it follow from

[
3
, Lemma 5.2 and Corollary 5.7]
that (
C.40
) holds if and only if

\lim_{k\to\infty}T(\mathbf{d}^{k})\mathbf{g}=T(\mathbf{d})\mathbf{g}\;\;\text{in}\;\;X_{u}\;\;\text{for any fixed}\;\;\mathbf{g}\in X_{u}.

(C.41)

Note that
\left\{\|T(\mathbf{d}^{k})\|_{L(X_{u})}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is bounded, and
G
is
dense in
X_{u}
. Therefore, to prove (
C.41
), it suffices to show that

\lim_{k\to\infty}T(\mathbf{d}^{k})\mathbf{g}=T(\mathbf{d})\mathbf{g}\;\;\text{in}\;\;G\;\;\text{for any fixed}\;\;\mathbf{g}\in G.

(C.42)

Now we construct an auxiliary function for further application. Let
\mathbf{g}=(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})\in G
. For any given
a>0
, we inductively
solve the following ODEs. For
i=1
:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\phi_{1}}{{\rm d}x}=\psi_{1},&x\in(0,x_{1}),\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\psi_{1}}{{\rm d}x}=\dfrac{1}{d_{1}}\left[(\Lambda-c_{1})\phi_{1}-g_{1}\right],&x\in(0,x_{1}),\\[10.0pt]
\phi_{1}(0^{+})=a,\;\;\psi_{1}(0^{+})=0.\end{cases}

(C.43)

For
i=2,\cdots,n
:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\phi_{i}}{{\rm d}x}=\psi_{i},&x\in(x_{i-1},x_{i}),\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\psi_{i}}{{\rm d}x}=\dfrac{1}{d_{i}}\left[(\Lambda-c_{i})\phi_{i}-g_{i}\right],&x\in(x_{i-1},x_{i}),\\[10.0pt]
\phi_{i}(x_{i-1}^{+})=p_{i-1}\phi_{i-1}(x_{i-1}^{-}),\;\;\psi_{i}(x_{i-1}^{+})=\dfrac{d_{i-1}}{d_{i}}\psi_{i-1}(x_{i-1}^{-}).\end{cases}

(C.44)

Define the auxiliary function
\mathcal{T}:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\mathcal{T}(a,\mathbf{d}):=\psi_{n}(L)
.

By the continuous dependence of solutions to ODEs on initial values and parameters, we see that
\mathcal{T}(a,\mathbf{d})
is continuous with respect to
(a,\mathbf{d})
.
Since (
C.34
) has a unique solution
\Phi=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{n})\in G
, it follows that
\mathcal{T}(a,\mathbf{d})=0
admits a unique solution
a(\mathbf{d})
for fixed
\mathbf{d}
. We now prove that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}a(\mathbf{d}^{k})=a(\mathbf{d}).

(C.45)

Since the constant
\mathcal{C}_{5}
in (
C.39
) depends only on
d_{i}
and
1/d_{i}
(
i=1,\cdots,n
), it follows from (
C.39
) that
\left\{a(\mathbf{d}^{k})\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is bounded. Consider any convergent subsequence
\{a(\mathbf{d}^{k_{j}})\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
with limit
a^{*}
. Since
\mathcal{T}(a(\mathbf{d}^{k_{j}}),\mathbf{d}^{k_{j}})=0
for all
j
and
\mathcal{T}(a,\mathbf{d})
is continuous with respect to
(a,\mathbf{d})
, taking the limit as
j\to\infty
yields
\mathcal{T}(a^{*},\mathbf{d})=0
. The uniqueness of the solution to
\mathcal{T}(a,\mathbf{d})=0
then implies that
a^{*}=a(\mathbf{d})
. Thus, (
C.45
) holds. Note that

\left(T(\mathbf{d})\mathbf{g},\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}T(\mathbf{d})\mathbf{g}}{{\rm d}x}\right)

is the solution of (
C.43
)-(
C.44
) for
a=a(\mathbf{d})
.
Similarly,

\left(T(\mathbf{d}^{k})\mathbf{g},\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}T(\mathbf{d}^{k})\mathbf{g}}{{\rm d}x}\right)

is the solution of (
C.43
)-(
C.44
) for
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{d}^{k}
and
a=a(\mathbf{d}^{k})
.
By the continuous dependence of solutions to ODEs on initial values and parameters, we see from (
C.45
) that (
C.42
) holds. Consequently,
T(\mathbf{d})
is continuous in
L(X_{u})
with respect to
\mathbf{d}
.

Note from
[
30
, Chapter III, Section 3.5]
that a simple and isolated eigenvalue depends continuously on the operator.
Since the spectral radius
r(\mathbf{d})
is a simple and isolated eigenvalue of
T(\mathbf{d})
on
G
(and hence
X_{u}
), it follows that

r(\mathbf{d})
is continuous with respect to
\mathbf{d}
, and consequently

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{d})
is continuous with respect to
\mathbf{d}
. This completes the proof.
∎
