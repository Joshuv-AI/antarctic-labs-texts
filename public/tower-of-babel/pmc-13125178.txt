Temporal effects of a botanical feed additive and experimental housing methods in broilers exposed to an acute feed withdrawal period prior to coccidiosis inoculation
Authors: Laney E. Froebel, Bailey Y. Watson, Michael J. Rincker, Ryan N. Dilger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, a commercial feed additive [Micro-Shield™ (MS); DPI Global, Porterville, CA] and experimental housing methods were evaluated for their temporal effects on endogenous antioxidants, growth performance, and intestinal health in broilers. The 30-d study (starter d 0-14, grower d 14-30) was conducted in a 2 × 4 factorial arrangement with factors including 1) housing method [2 levels; battery cages (BC) with raised-wire flooring or floor pens (FP) with fresh pine shavings] and 2) dietary treatment [4 levels; 1) control diet fed to birds not exposed to stressors (NC), 2) control diet fed to birds exposed to stressors (PC), 3) PC + 1,000 mg of MS/kg diet in the starter phase + 500 mg of MS/kg diet in the grower phase (MSL), and 4) 1,000 mg of MS/kg diet in the starter and grower phases (MSH)]. A total of 720 male Ross 708 chicks were assigned to 1 of 8 treatment groups: BC—NC, BC-PC, BC-MSL, BC-MSH, FP-NC, FP-PC, FP-MSL, or FP-MSH. Treatment groups raised in BC or FP were allotted to 10 or 8 replicates, respectively, each housing 10 birds. A multiple mild stressors challenge was imposed by birds undergoing a 12-h feed withdrawal prior to a coccidiosis vaccine challenge on study d 15, which was designated as 0 d post-inoculation (DPI). All data were analyzed by a 2-way ANOVA using SAS and effects were considered significant when P < 0.05. During the grower phase, the FP-NC treatment had the highest (P < 0.05) BWG compared with all other treatments. On 6 DPI, jejunal gene expression of IFN-γ, IL-1β, and IL-10 was highest (P < 0.05) in FP-MSH birds compared with all other treatments. On 9 DPI, there was a main effect of dietary treatment where the MSH treatment had higher (P < 0.05) serum catalase levels compared with its counterparts. These results suggest that MS supplementation, experimental housing method, and their interaction influence growth performance and overall health outcomes in experimental settings.

Introduction

Coccidiosis, caused by the Eimeria protozoan, remains one of the most costly and widespread diseases of the global poultry industry, with estimates reporting billions of dollars lost annually (Blake et al., 2020). The parasite invades and replicates in enterocytes, resulting in gastrointestinal tract damage, reduced nutrient absorption, overall compromised immune status, and poorer growth responses (Assis et al., 2010; Blake, 2015; Mesa-Pineda et al., 2021). While anticoccidial drugs and vaccines have been successful in mitigating the effects of disease, the emergence of drug-resistant Eimeria strains and increased consumer demand for antibiotic-free poultry products has created the need for alternative strategies to combat coccidiosis (Cervantes and McDougald, 2023).

In addition to coccidiosis, acute off-feed events can occur in commercial broiler production settings because of myriad factors including failures in feeding equipment or delayed feed delivery (Lacy, 2002). While this stressor can result in impaired growth performance, it has also been reported to potentially make broilers more susceptible to coccidiosis by decreasing intestinal mucus content (Thompson and Applegate, 2006). Further, in a study evaluating the synergistic effects of cyclically elevated ambient temperatures, feed withdrawal, and a coccidiosis vaccine challenge, broilers that were exposed to a 12-h feed withdrawal in combination with a 3 × vaccine challenge had similar concentrations of fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran, a marker of intestinal permeability, compared with broilers that received a 5 × vaccine challenge (Froebel et al., 2026b).

Saponins and polyphenols are plant-derived compounds that are recognized for their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties (Cheeke, 2000; Daglia, 2012; Islam et al., 2017; Oelschlager et al., 2019). Saponins have innate soap-like properties that can degrade Eimeria oocysts, leading to direct pathogen destruction (Francis et al., 2002). Additionally, the chemical structure of polyphenols can modulate permeability of Eimeria oocysts, resulting in degradation of the protozoan cell membrane (Idris et al., 2016). Several studies have reported that saponin and phenolic compounds can reduce oocyst shedding, decrease intestinal lesion severity, and modulate immune responses in Eimeria-challenged broilers, ultimately decreasing the severity of infection (Hassan et al., 2008; Bozkurt et al., 2016; Oelschlager et al., 2019). As such, these bioactive compounds have emerged as potential interventions to decrease the impact of coccidiosis infection in commercial broilers.

When evaluating mechanism of action of feed additives in research settings, the choice of housing method can influence experimental outcomes. Chickens raised in battery cages may be exposed to different levels of pathogen exposure and stress compared with birds raised in floor pen systems, which more closely mimics commercial production environments. These differences could ultimately impact the immune, gut health, and growth responses being evaluated. Therefore, it is important to assess how housing methods influence biological outcomes to better evaluate the broiler’s response to challenges such as Eimeria while consuming dietary additives. As such, the objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of saponin and polyphenol supplementation on temporal changes to antioxidant defenses, growth performance, and intestinal health in broilers exposed to a 12-h feed withdrawal prior to coccidiosis challenge either in battery cage or floor pen rearing systems.

Materials and methods

All animal care and experimental procedures were approved by the University of Illinois Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee prior to initiation of experiment.

Bird husbandry and experimental design

Ross 708 male broilers were obtained from a commercial hatchery (Hoover’s Hatchery, Rudd, IA) 2 d after hatch. Birds were weighed, selected, wingbanded, and assigned to 1 of 8 treatment groups in a completely randomized design. Birds were exposed to 1 h of darkness per day for the first 4 d of the study then underwent a step-down lighting program to reach 4 h of daily darkness on d 7, which remained constant for the remainder of the study. Birds were provided ad libitum access to feed and water for the duration of the study apart from the birds that were exposed to a 12-h feed withdrawal prior to the coccidiosis inoculation.

Multiple mild stressor challenge

To induce a multiple mild stressor challenge, some birds (FP-PC, BC-PC, FP-MSL, BC-MSL, FP-MSH, BC-MSH treatment groups) were exposed to a 12-h feed withdrawal on study d 14 prior to a coccidiosis vaccine challenge. On d 15, a validated vaccine challenge model was used to induce a coccidiosis infection, and the day of challenge was designated as day post-inoculation (DPI) 0. As such, chicks were orally gavaged with a vaccinator gun fitted with an oral gavage needle to administer 1 mL of either tap water (FP-NC and BC—NC treatment groups) or Eimeria oocysts (FP-PC, BC-PC, FP-MSL, BC-MSL, FP-MSH, BC-MSH treatment groups). The commercial coccidiosis vaccine (Coccivac®-B52, Merck Animal Health, Madison, NJ) contained live oocysts of E. acervulina, E. maxima, E. mivati, and E. tenella species.

The average body weight of all birds on d 14 was calculated and used to determine the dose for vaccine inoculation as previously described (Froebel et al., 2026a, 2026b). The dose was administered at 5 × the manufacturer’s recommendation (i.e., 1 dose per 40 g of body weight) using the average d 14 body weight of all birds. This dose was selected with the intention of imitating a mild coccidiosis infection and therefore reducing growth performance by 10-20%. In the current study, the average body weight was 450 g, and therefore, the required doses to achieve a 5 × challenge was calculated as 56.25 doses per bird using the following equation: [(450 g / 40 g) × 5].

Data and sample collection

Birds and feeders were weighed on study d 0, 14, and 30 to evaluate body weight gain (BWG), feed intake (FI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) to calculate growth performance parameters. Birds were checked twice daily to record mortality and culls, which were used to calculate mortality-corrected FI and FCR. Birds had variable feed intake on d 14 due to the 12-h feed withdrawal period; therefore, feed intake for this day was not included in data analysis. On study d 14, 18, 21, 24, 27, and 30 (i.e., −1, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 DPI), one bird per BC and FP was randomly chosen on each collection day and euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation to permit the collection of liver weights and blood and tissue samples.

The weights of sample birds were collected in addition to whole liver weights to allow for the calculation of relative liver weights. Blood was collected after euthanasia via cardiac puncture into evacuated serum tubes and allowed to clot at room temperature prior to being centrifuged at 2,250 rpm for 20 min at 4°C to obtain serum, which was stored at −80°C pending analysis. Jejunum tissue samples were collected at the midpoint of the jejunum (i.e., halfway between the end of the duodenal loop and Meckel’s diverticulum), snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C pending analysis.

Oocyst shedding

Oocyst shedding was evaluated by oocyst counts (i.e., oocysts per gram, OPG) in excreta samples on DPI 0, 6, and 15 from all BC and FP using the McMaster method (Levine et al., 1960). In both BC and FP, a clean plastic cup was used to collect approximately 5 g of fresh excreta from each individual housing unit, and excreta samples were pooled within replicate.

Gene expression

Jejunal tissues were collected on −1, 6, and 15 DPI to evaluate cytokine gene expression. Tissue weighing 50-100 mg was homogenized in 1 mL of Trizol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) for 2 min at 30 Hz (TissueLyser II, Qiagen, Valencia, CA). Manufacturer instructions were followed to complete RNA extraction from tissue, which was evaluated for quantity and purity using a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop ND-100, Nano-Drop Technologies, Wilmington, DE). Purity of extracted RNA was verified by evaluating 260/280 ratios, and all RNA samples fell within the range of 1.8-2.1. Extracted RNA was subsequently reverse-transcribed to create cDNA using a high-capacity reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA). As such, extracted RNA was run through a thermocycler for 10 min at 25°C, 120 min at 37°C, and 5 min at 85°C before being cooled to 4°C. Obtained cDNA was stored at −20°C until plating.

The Taqman Gene Expression Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) was utilized to obtain relative gene expression of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ; NM_205149.1), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β; XM_015297469.1), interleukin-10 (IL-10; NM_001004414.2), and interleukin-12 (IL-12; NM_213571.1). Amplification by RT-qPCR was achieved for target (IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-10, and IL-12) and reference (glyceraldehyde-3-hosphate dehydrogenase; GAPDH; NM_204305.1) genes. Synthesized cDNA was amplified with Taqman (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA) oligonucleotide probes containing 5′ fluorescent reporter dye and 3′ non-fluorescent quencher dye. Fluorescence was determined using the QuantStudio™ 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Forest City, CA) set to a maximum of 40 cycles. Gene expression of target genes was normalized through parallel amplification of endogenous threshold cycle method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001), and results were expressed as fold-change relative to birds in the control treatment group.

Antioxidant defenses and oxidative stress markers

Serum samples were collected on −1, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 DPI to evaluate catalase (CAT; Cat. No.: 707002), glutathione peroxidase (GPx; Cat. No.: 703102), malondialdehyde (MDA, Cat. No.: 700870), and superoxide dismutase (SOD; Cat. No.: 706002) using commercially available kits (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI). All assays were measured using a multi-mode microplate reader (Synergy HT; BioTek Instruments, Inc.) according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Cage was the experimental unit for all statistical analyses. Area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated for absolute and relative liver weights as well as serum CAT, GPx, MDA, and SOD levels using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC) to serve as summative, temporal measures in response to experimental treatment assignment. Additionally, all data were initially subjected to a 2-way ANOVA, with the model including fixed effects of housing method, dietary treatment assignment, and their interaction using the MIXED procedure of SAS. Results were considered significant when P < 0.05, and least-square means are reported. Differences among treatments were assessed using pairwise comparisons of least-square means (i.e., PDIFF option) without adjustment for multiple comparisons. Outliers were identified as having an absolute Studentized residual value of 3 or greater and were subsequently removed. For OPG data, NC treatments were not included in statistical analysis due to these treatment groups having zero variability in data.

Liver weights

Relative and absolute liver weights are reported in Table 4 and Supplementary Table 1, respectively. As organ weights normalized to BW inherently integrates the concept of allometric growth, relative liver weights are emphasized in the current manuscript. Discussion

Broilers are often exposed to a combination of environmental and immunological stressors in commercial production settings, namely acute off-feed events and avian coccidiosis. As the usage of antibiotic growth promoters continues to decline in the poultry industry, alternative feed additives are being evaluated for the ability to support broiler growth and overall health in practical contexts when facing immunological and environmental stressors. However, when evaluating the mechanism of action of nutritional additives in research settings, the choice of housing method may influence experimental outcomes. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the effects of dietary saponin and polyphenol supplementation on temporal changes to antioxidant defenses, growth performance, and intestinal health outcomes in broilers exposed to an acute off-feed event and coccidiosis challenge in both BC and FP rearing systems.

Dietary supplementation with saponins and polyphenols had no effect on broiler growth performance prior to the multiple mild stressors challenge. However, there was a main effect of housing method on FCR during the starter phase, with FP-raised birds exhibiting a higher FCR compared to birds housed in BC. This difference may be attributed to reduced physical activity in BC environments, which likely resulted in lower energy expenditure and subsequently improved FCR in these birds (Lay et al., 2011). During the grower phase, a main effect of housing method on FCR was again observed, though in this case, birds raised in FP demonstrated improved FCR. While the direct comparison of BC and FP housing methods for broilers is not well characterized in the current literature, Fouad et al. (2008) and Idrus et al. (2021) both reported that FP-housed birds had higher BW and FI compared with BC-housed birds. While only Fouad et al. (2008) reported improved FCR in FP-housed birds compared with BC-housed birds, this study allowed a larger floor space area for FP-housed birds than BC-housed birds, which is similar to the current study design. Further, Fouad et al. (2008) reported higher heterophil:lymphocyte ratios in BC-housed birds compared with FP-housed birds, indicating that nutrients may be partitioned toward sustaining a stress response rather than muscle accretion, ultimately resulting in diminished feed efficiency in these birds.

Additionally, during the grower period, FI was higher in the NC group compared with birds subjected to multiple stressors, which aligns with previous findings in using a multiple stressors challenge model (Froebel et al., 2026b). Notably, there was an interaction effect for BWG during the grower phase, with FP-NC birds exhibiting the greatest BWG and BC—NC birds showing intermediate performance. Overall, growth performance results followed anticipated patterns, where birds in the NC group had superior BWG and FI relative to birds exposed to environmental and immunological stressors.

While OPG counts are often evaluated in experimental settings to confirm biosecurity measures were maintained during the study, these counts are an insightful metric for assessing the severity of a coccidiosis infection as they can indicate the rate of Eimeria passage and recycling in the host. At peak infection on 6 DPI, no interactions or main effects were observed for OPG counts. However, by the recovery phase on 15 DPI, a main effect of housing method was reported, with birds housed in FP exhibiting higher OPG counts compared to those housed in BC. Although no differences were observed on 6 DPI, OPG counts had wider variability at this time-point, which may have masked true differences in oocyst shedding at peak infection. Ultimately, the finding of a main effect of housing method on 15 DPI was expected, as FP-housed birds were continuously exposed to excreta containing Eimeria oocysts, whereas BC-housed birds had more limited access to excreta. The reduced oocyst shedding observed in BC-housed broilers may contribute to decreased environmental cycling of Eimeria, potentially lowering the overall severity of coccidiosis infection in these birds (Hassan et al., 2008). The reduced OPG counts observed in BC-housed birds on 15 DPI coincided with lower gene expression of pro-inflammatory and regulatory cytokines in these birds, which will be discussed in greater detail later in the current manuscript.

As evidenced by OPG data in the current manuscript, evaluating both BC and FP housing methods is essential in broiler research because housing methods fundamentally influence bird exposure to stressors. Housing broilers in BC offers a controlled environment by removing the variability of litter exposure, making them valuable in isolating and evaluating specific mechanisms of action. However, this higher level of control in research settings does not allow for the consideration of the higher complexity in commercial production settings. In contrast, FP more closely mimic commercial production systems where birds are exposed to litter, and thus, fluctuating microenvironments. This can drastically influence broiler outcomes relating to intestinal health, immune function, and antioxidant defenses, and ultimately, how data are interpreted from feed additive trials.

As previously mentioned, liver weights normalized to BW allow for the evaluation of allometric growth, which can provide insight into how broilers allocate nutritional resources to meet metabolic and health demands during periods of stress (Dou et al., 2017; Richardson et al., 2025). Prior to induction of the challenge model, no differences in relative liver weights were observed at −1 DPI. However, by 3 DPI, both dietary treatment and housing method affected liver weight, with NC birds and those housed in BC exhibiting lower relative liver weights compared to their respective counterparts. The liver effectively functions as a secondary lymphoid organ and plays a critical role in initiating immune responses through the production of acute phase proteins and other immune mediators. Thus, increased relative liver weight in birds exposed to the stressors model at this time-point was expected (Klasing and Austic, 1984; Robinson et al., 2016). Birds housed in FP were continuously exposed to their own and pen mates’ excreta, whereas BC-housed birds were effectively physically separated from excreta due to use of raised-wire flooring. Although Eimeria oocysts would not have completed their endogenous life cycle by 3 DPI, the microbiological load present in litter may have contributed to enhanced immune stimulation in FP-housed birds (Song et al., 2022a, 2022b). At 9 and 15 DPI, birds receiving the botanical dietary intervention exhibited increased relative liver weights compared with NC and PC groups, suggesting a possible sustained immune response promoted by saponin and polyphenol supplementation (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2024). However, further investigation of additional immune mediators such as circulating acute phase proteins would aid in determining whether this localized response contributed to systemic effects in the host.

Cytokine production plays a critical role in the host response to coccidiosis by promoting immune cell infiltration at the site of intestinal infection, facilitating tissue repair, and supporting recovery of overall health and growth performance (Lillehoj, 1998; Lillehoj et al., 2004; Lu et al., 2023). In the present study, IL-12, a Th1 cytokine that bridges innate and adaptive immunity through stimulation of IFN-γ production by natural killer and T cells (Degen et al., 2004), had higher expression in the jejunal tissue of FP-housed birds on −1 DPI compared with BC-housed birds. This finding may indicate that continuous access to excreta elicited mild immune stimulation before Eimeria challenge in birds raised on litter, and ultimately, served as a confounding factor in downstream cytokine gene expression. However, because the relative fold change absolute difference for IL-12 at −1 DPI between BC and FP-housed birds was 0.630, it is unclear how meaningful this statistical difference is in terms of biological significance. On 6 DPI, IL-12 expression was higher in birds subjected to the stressor model, with the highest gene expression observed in MSH birds, suggesting that dietary supplementation with saponins and polyphenols may enhance cell-mediated immunity. As IFN-γ is essential for controlling Eimeria through macrophage activation and enhanced antigen presentation, its increased jejunal expression at peak infection is consistent with previous findings (Hong et al., 2006; Rochell et al., 2016; Oelschlager et al., 2019). Similarly, IL-1β, a proinflammatory cytokine that mediates leukocyte recruitment (Sheikh et al., 2005), was also elevated during this period, reflecting an active acute immunity response. Concurrent increases in IL-10, a regulatory cytokine that modulates inflammation, alongside IFN-γ and IL-1β expression at 6 DPI, suggests this counteractive mechanism was employed to limit tissue damage (He et al., 2012). The elevated jejunal expression of IL-10, IFN-γ, and IL-1β in FP-MSH birds indicates that both dietary supplementation and prolonged exposure to Eimeria oocysts in litter contributed to a heightened immune response. At 15 DPI, cytokine expression remained elevated in FP birds relative to BC-housed counterparts, suggesting sustained immune activation during the recovery phase.

In the current study, temporal changes in key antioxidant defense markers (i.e., CAT, GPx, and SOD) as well as MDA, a critical lipid peroxidation marker, were evaluated. The investigation of these markers across six time-points during the study provides insight into how oxidative stress manifests throughout the duration of a coccidiosis infection. Further, these data illustrate how the broiler antioxidant defense system responds to stressors across different dietary treatments and experimental housing methods. Because SOD, GPx, and CAT together comprise an integral innate antioxidant defense pathway in broilers (Surai et al., 2019), their levels over time may provide valuable insight into how a disruption to one constituent of the pathway may affect downstream antioxidant capacity and subsequent whole-body oxidative damage.

Across all antioxidant enzymes, time-dependent differences were evident regardless of dietary treatment, suggesting a physiological response to the imposed stressors. Differences were observed in SOD activity, which initiates the antioxidant cascade by catalyzing the dismutation of harmful superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide (Surai, 2016). The PC and MSH treatments had the highest SOD levels on 3 DPI, suggesting early activation of the endogenous antioxidant cascade. Considering that superoxide radicals are among the earliest reactive oxygen species produced during a coccidiosis infection, this early increase of SOD activity could indicate that both the coccidiosis challenge and the higher inclusion of saponins and polyphenols stimulated rapid SOD activation in response to increased immune cell respiratory burst activity (Allen and Fetterer, 2002; He et al., 2003). However, by 12 DPI, all challenged treatments had lower SOD compared with the NC treatment, suggesting potential suppression of SOD as the coccidiosis infection progressed (Georgieva et al., 2006) . As SOD is the first endogenous antioxidant in the SOD-CAT-GPx pathway, its early activation on 3 DPI would be expected to influence other antioxidant defenses further down the pathway cascade.

As complementary to the function of SOD, CAT is a catalyst for the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. We observed that BC-housed birds had higher CAT levels on −1 DPI compared with FP-housed birds, suggesting that BC-housed broilers had a higher antioxidant capacity prior to coccidiosis inoculation. Alternatively, these data may be reflective of differences in physiological stress state between the two housing methods. Ultimately, these effects could indicate a shifting balance between hydrogen peroxide production by SOD and broiler capacity for decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (Surai, 2016). Moreover, the NC treatment had the highest CAT levels on 6 DPI compared with the other dietary treatments, suggesting that the challenged treatments had a diminished antioxidant capacity at peak coccidiosis infection. However, on 9 DPI, the MSH treatment had the highest CAT levels compared with its counterparts, indicating that a higher inclusion of saponins and polyphenols may support a more robust counteractive response during heightened periods of oxidative stress. Despite these differences in time-dependent patterns of enzyme activity, CAT AUC values did not differ by dietary treatment or housing method, suggesting that total CAT response over the active infection and recovery periods remained relatively stable. However, a deeper analysis into the time-dependent patterns that were observed may be warranted.

The evaluation of GPx offers further insight into the broiler antioxidant status as GPx neutralizes peroxides generated from superoxide dismutation and lipid oxidation (Arthur, 2000). In the current study, an interactive effect was observed for GPx activity on 6 DPI where BC—NC birds had the highest GPx levels compared with all other treatments. This reduction of GPx in challenged treatment groups coincided with a reduction in CAT on 6 DPI, reinforcing that the broiler’s antioxidant capacity was likely diminished at peak coccidiosis infection. However, by 15 DPI, both MSL- and MSH-supplemented treatment groups had higher GPx levels compared with their counterparts, suggesting that saponin and polyphenol supplementation supported peroxide neutralization during the recovery phase for coccidiosis. One purported mechanism for increasing GPx levels involves activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 signaling pathway (Nrf2), which is a critical regulator for the broiler endogenous antioxidant defense system (Surai et al., 2019). As both polyphenols and saponins have been reported to induce Nrf2 expression, this may be the mechanism of action occurring in botanical supplemented birds on 15 DPI (Sun et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022). For GPx AUC values, the BC—NC treatment had the highest values whereas the FP-NC treatment had the lowest values, illustrating how experimental housing methods likely cause temporal changes to GPx activity in broilers.

As expected, MDA levels closely mirrored fluctuations in antioxidant enzyme activities, demonstrating the integrated nature of the broiler antioxidant systems. Because MDA is an end-product of lipid peroxidation, increases in MDA often occurs because of either increased oxidative load or insufficient antioxidant defenses in the host (Marnett and Hancock, 1999). The increased MDA levels in FP-housed birds on 12 DPI corresponded with lower GPx and SOD activity at the same time-point. These findings may indicate that reduced antioxidant activity predisposes birds to a higher accumulation of lipid peroxides (Oke et al., 2024). However, the MSH dietary treatment had the highest MDA levels on 9 DPI despite also having the highest CAT levels at this same time-point. These data reflect that even if saponin and polyphenol supplementation manages to enhance antioxidant potential, if oxidative stress loads exceed cellular capacity, lipid peroxidation may progress despite endogenous control mechanisms (Landete, 2013). Additionally, the MDA AUC values in BC-MSH birds were comparable to those reported for both BC—NC and FP-NC treatments, indicating the higher inclusion of saponins and polyphenols in BC-housed birds may have suppressed lipid peroxidation over the course of infection. Alternatively, FP-MSH birds had the highest MDA AUC values compared with all other treatment groups. In the current study, jejunal gene expression of IL-1β on 6 DPI followed a similar trend where BC-MSH birds had comparable IL-1β expression to NC treatment groups whereas FP-MSH birds had the highest. Previously, FP-housed birds were reported to have higher alpha diversity of ileal microbiota and enhanced peripheral and intestinal immune function compared with BC-housed birds (Song et al., 2022b). This finding is critical as increased prevalence of pathogenic bacteria in FP-housed birds could potentially increase reactive oxygen species production and ultimately induce lipid peroxidation (Kogut, 2019; Al Sulaiman et al., 2023; Mounir et al., 2025). Collectively, these data may reflect that the environmental complexity and increased immune activation in FP-housed birds as indicated by jejunal cytokine gene expression in the current study may override the antioxidant capacity of the botanical additive.

Conclusion

Overall, these data suggest dietary supplementation of saponins and polyphenols, as well as experimental housing methods, influence growth performance, immune, and antioxidant defense responses in broilers exposed to an acute 12-h feed withdrawal period prior to coccidiosis inoculation. Housing method influenced the efficiency of feed conversion during the starter phase, with birds raised in FP exhibiting a higher FCR compared with birds housed in BC, which may relate to differences in energy expenditure between the two housing methods. Additionally, housing method affected OPG counts where FP-housed birds had higher OPG counts on 15 DPI compared with BC-housed birds, indicating increased oocyst cycling in these birds. Increased oocyst cycling in FP-housed birds may have promoted a heighted immune response in these birds as evidenced by the FP-housed birds having elevated expression of IL-10, IFN- γ, and IL-1β in jejunal tissue compared with BC-housed birds on 15 DPI. Dietary supplementation with saponins and polyphenols also influenced broiler immune response as evidenced by increased relative liver weights compared with NC and PC birds at multiple time-points during the study. Collectively interpreting antioxidant defense results, an early increase in SOD levels on 3 DPI in challenged broilers was likely in response to rapid superoxide production caused by activation of the immune system and may have influenced endogenous antioxidant activity later in the challenge period. As broilers reached peak coccidiosis infection on 6 DPI, a reduction in CAT and GPx in challenged birds coincided with an increase in MDA, indicating that insufficient hydrogen peroxide detoxification promoted an increase in lipid peroxidation. Further, these data support the idea that the broiler antioxidant defense system should not be evaluated in individual segments, but rather collectively, as the activity of each enzyme influences, and is influenced by, early reactive oxygen species production and downstream oxidative damage. Additional research should continue to evaluate the collective response of broilers to environmental and immunological stress and further evaluate how mechanisms of action for both stressors and dietary interventions are influenced by experimental housing methods.
