Prognostic Significance of Secondary‐Type Mutations in NPM1‐Mutated Acute Myeloid Leukemia
Authors: Kun Yang, Hongmei Luo, Bing Xiang, Yang Dai, Yuping Gong, Jie Huang, Jianjun Li, Yongqian Jia, Yong Guo, Yuhuan Zheng, Hongbing Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT NPM1 is among the most frequently mutated genes in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), and NPM1‐mutated AML is generally associated with a favorable prognosis. However, substantial prognostic heterogeneity exists within this population. The prognostic significance of secondary‐type mutations (STMs) in NPM1‐mutated AML remains controversial. In this retrospective study of 179 patients with NPM1‐mutated AML, the prognostic effects of age, common comutations, and STMs were evaluated. Multivariate analysis revealed that age ≥ 60 years and the presence of FLT3‐ITD mutations were independently associated with poor overall survival (OS). Patients with NPM1/FLT3‐ITD/DNMT3A triple mutations had the shortest median OS among the molecular subgroups examined. The treatment response did not differ significantly between STM‐positive and STM‐negative patients. No statistically significant association between STM status and OS was observed in the overall cohort or in exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by ELN 2022 risk category and age. Sensitivity analyses accounting for stem cell transplantation and untreated patients yielded similar results. Given the limited number of STM‐positive patients, larger studies are needed to further clarify the prognostic significance of STMs in NPM1‐mutated AML.

Introduction

Mutations in myelodysplasia‐related genes in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), also known as secondary‐type mutations (STMs), include those in ASXL1, SF3B1, SRSF2, BCOR, STAG2, EZH2, U2AF1, and ZRSR2 [1, 2]. Previous studies have shown that STMs are associated with a history of myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) or MDS‐like clinical features and unfavorable outcomes [1, 3, 4, 5]. In 2022, the International Consensus Classification (ICC) of Myeloid Neoplasms and the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) risk classification incorporated these mutations into the classification of AML. The ICC classifies AML with mutations in these genes or RUNX1 as AML with myelodysplasia‐related gene mutations [6]. Similarly, the ELN 2022 risk classification places these mutations, together with RUNX1 mutations, in the adverse‐risk category [4].

NPM1 mutations are among the most prevalent molecular abnormalities in AML and are detected in approximately 30% of patients [7]. NPM1 mutations are rarely detected in clonal hematopoiesis. They are considered driver mutations that act as gatekeepers for AML and typically arise de novo [8, 9, 10]. Wild‐type NPM1 comprises 12 exons and encodes a widely expressed protein that undergoes nucleocytoplasmic shuttling. NPM1 mutations occur predominantly in exon 12, whereas mutations involving other exons are rare [7]. In 2016, the World Health Organization classified NPM1‐mutated AML as a distinct leukemia entity. Patients often exhibit characteristic biological and clinicopathological features, including a normal karyotype, high expression of HOX genes, low expression of CD34 and CD133, and persistent NPM1 mutations at relapse [11]. NPM1‐mutated AML is generally associated with a favorable response to chemotherapy and better outcomes, particularly in the absence of FLT3‐ITD mutations [12].

The prognostic impact of specific mutations may be influenced by the presence of co‐occurring mutations. For example, the favorable prognostic effect of NPM1 mutations may be attenuated by concomitant FLT3‐ITD mutations. Consequently, according to the ELN 2022 risk classification, patients with NPM1 mutations and concomitant FLT3‐ITD mutations are categorized as intermediate risk rather than favorable risk [4]. In addition to FLT3‐ITD, other frequent comutations, including NRAS, IDH1, IDH2, WT1, and DNMT3A, have been suggested to influence the prognosis of patients with NPM1‐mutated AML [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. The prognostic impact of STMs in NPM1‐mutated AML remains controversial [1, 2, 3, 18]. In this retrospective study, we investigated the prognostic impact of STMs in 179 patients with NPM1‐mutated AML through exploratory analyses of clinically relevant subgroups.

Subjects and Methods

Patients

Data from 179 patients with NPM1‐mutated AML and available cytogenetic and next‐generation sequencing data were collected at West China Hospital, Sichuan University, between 2012 and 2024. Patients aged < 14 years and those diagnosed with acute promyelocytic leukemia were excluded. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 2024(203)). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Review Committee because of the retrospective design and the use of anonymized clinical data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

STM‐positive AML was defined as the presence of at least one mutation in ASXL1, SF3B1, SRSF2, BCOR, STAG2, EZH2, U2AF1, ZRSR2, or RUNX1. AML was classified as de novo in patients without a history of antecedent hematological disease or prior leukemogenic therapy and as secondary AML in patients with a history of an antecedent hematological disorder, including MDS or a myeloproliferative neoplasm. Therapy‐related AML was defined as AML arising after previous chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunosuppressive therapy. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of diagnosis to death from any cause or the last follow‐up. Risk stratification and treatment response were assessed according to the ELN 2022 recommendations [4].

Next‐Generation Sequencing

All the samples were analyzed using an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) with targeted amplicon sequencing. For earlier NGS testing, only the presence and location of variant alleles were reported, without information on variant allele frequency (VAF). Most cases were sequenced using a 34‐gene panel, including ASXL1, BCOR, BCORL1, CALR, CBL, CEBPA, CSF3R, DNMT3A, ETV6, ETNK1, EZH2, FLT3, IDH1, IDH2, JAK2, KIT, KRAS, MPL, NF1, NPM1, NRAS, PHF6, PIGA, PTPN11, RUNX1, SETBP1, SF3B1, SRSF2, STAG2, TET2, TP53, U2AF1, WT1, and ZRSR2. A small subset of cases was sequenced using a 238‐gene panel. ASXL1, SF3B1, SRSF2, BCOR, STAG2, EZH2, U2AF1, ZRSR2, and RUNX1 were included in all sequencing panels.

Statistical Analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 and GraphPad Prism 10. Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables, whereas the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used for continuous variables. OS curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with OS. To account for the potential effect of stem cell transplantation (SCT) on survival, additional analyses were performed by censoring patients at the time of SCT and by including SCT as a time‐dependent covariate in the Cox regression model. A sensitivity analysis excluding patients who received no antileukemic treatment was also performed. Hazard ratios (HRs) are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). p values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Patient Characteristics

In this study, 179 patients with NPM1‐mutated AML (67 males and 112 females) were included. The clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis was 51 years (range, 14–84 years), and 132 patients (73.7%) were younger than 60 years. Most patients had de novo AML (174/179, 97.2%), whereas 5 patients (2.8%) had secondary AML, and none had therapy‐related AML. In accordance with the ELN 2022 criteria, 104 patients (58.1%) were classified as favorable risk, whereas 75 patients (41.9%) were classified as intermediate risk. Among the 179 patients, 26 (14.5%) were STM positive, and 153 (85.5%) were STM negative. Overall, 18 patients (10.1%) underwent SCT, including 3 of 26 (11.5%) STM‐positive patients and 15 of 153 (9.8%) STM‐negative patients. The most frequently comutated genes were FLT3‐ITD (75/179, 41.9%), DNMT3A (73/179, 40.8%), IDH1 (45/179, 25.1%), TET2 (37/179, 20.7%), IDH2 (36/179, 20.1%), NRAS (23/179, 12.8%), PTPN11 (17/179, 9.5%) and FLT3‐TKD (16/179, 8.9%) (Figure 1 and Table 1).

Secondary‐Type Mutations in NPM1 ‐Mutated AML

The clinical characteristics of the STM‐positive and STM‐negative patients are shown in Table 1. There was no significant difference in sex distribution between the two groups (p = 0.061). STM‐positive patients were older than STM‐negative patients at diagnosis (median age, 59 vs. 49 years; p = 0.010). STM‐positive patients also had lower leukocyte counts (p < 0.001) and bone marrow blast percentages (p = 0.007) than STM‐negative patients. Secondary AML was more frequent in STM‐positive patients than in STM‐negative patients (11.5% vs. 1.3%; p = 0.003). No significant differences in ELN 2022 risk category, platelet count, hemoglobin level, or treatment response were detected between the two groups.

Among the 26 STM‐positive patients, ASXL1 was the most frequently expressed gene in the STM (n = 9, 34.6%), followed by SRSF2 (n = 5, 19.2%) and RUNX1 (n = 5, 19.2%) (Figure 2a). Additional STMs included BCOR (n = 4, 15.4%), EZH2 (n = 3, 11.5%), STAG2 (n = 2, 7.7%), ZRSR2 (n = 2, 7.7%) and SF3B1 (n = 1, 3.8%). No U2AF1 mutations were detected, and five patients harbored two co‐occurring STMs. VAFs for both STMs and NPM1 were available for 18 patients. In 16 of these patients, the STM VAF was greater than or equal to the NPM1 VAF (Figure 2b and Table S1). Effects of Age and Comutation Patterns on Survival in Patients With NPM1 ‐Mutated AML

Potential prognostic factors for OS in patients with NPM1‐mutated AML were evaluated. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that age ≥ 60 years at diagnosis (HR, 2.330; 95% CI, 1.458–3.725; p < 0.001) and concomitant FLT3‐ITD mutation (HR, 1.662; 95% CI, 1.032–2.676; p = 0.037) were independently associated with poor OS (Table S2). Patients aged ≥ 60 years had a shorter median OS than those aged < 60 years (11.8 vs. 41.5 months; p < 0.001; Figure 3a), and patients with FLT3‐ITD mutations had a shorter median OS than those without FLT3‐ITD mutations (19.2 vs. 55.4 months; p = 0.004; Figure 3b).

Overall survival according to age and comutation status. K–M analyses of OS according to (a) age, (b) FLT3‐ITD status, (c) DNMT3A status, and (d) combined FLT3‐ITD and DNMT3A mutation status.

Although DNMT3A was not independently associated with OS in the multivariable analysis, it was among the most frequent comutations in our cohort, and the NPM1/FLT3‐ITD/DNMT3A triple‐mutated genotype has been associated with adverse outcomes in previous studies [15, 19]. Therefore, DNMT3A was further evaluated in the context of comutation patterns. There was no significant difference in OS between patients with and without DNMT3A mutations (median OS, 20.2 vs. 39.6 months; p = 0.080; Figure 3c). Patients were then stratified according to their combined FLT3‐ITD and DNMT3A mutation status. The median OS was 55.4 months (95% CI, NR‐NR) in patients with neither FLT3‐ITD nor DNMT3A mutations, 22.3 months (95% CI, 12.3–32.2) in those with FLT3‐ITD alone, 30.5 months (95% CI, 5.9–55.5) in those with DNMT3A alone, and 15.3 months (95% CI, 9.3–21.3) in patients with NPM1/FLT3‐ITD/DNMT3A triple mutations (overall p = 0.032; Figure 3d). Among the four molecular subgroups, the triple‐mutated group had the shortest median OS.

Impact of STMs on Outcomes

The prognostic impact of STMs was then evaluated in the overall cohort and clinically relevant subgroups. In our cohort, the response rates did not differ significantly between STM‐positive and STM‐negative patients (Table 1). In the overall cohort, the status of the STM was not significantly associated with OS. The median OS was 35.5 months for the STM‐positive patients and 26.6 months for the STM‐negative patients (log‐rank p = 0.861; Figure 4a), and Cox regression yielded an HR of 1.05 (95% CI, 0.59–1.89; p = 0.863) (Table S3). To account for the potential influence of SCT on survival, a sensitivity analysis was performed with patients censored at the date of SCT. The median OS was 35.5 months (95% CI, 1.4–69.5) in the STM‐positive patients and 26.2 months (95% CI, 17.8–34.6) in the STM‐negative patients, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.547; Figure S1).

Overall survival according to STM status. K–M analyses of OS according to STM status in (a) the overall cohort, (b) the favorable‐risk subgroup (ELN 2022), (c) the intermediate‐risk subgroup (ELN 2022), and (d) age‐defined groups.

Given the limited number of STM‐positive patients within individual subgroups, the ELN‐ and age‐stratified analyses were considered exploratory. STM status was not significantly associated with OS in the favorable‐risk subgroup (HR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.33–1.84; p = 0.564; Figure 4b and Table S3). In the intermediate‐risk subgroup, compared with STM‐negative patients, STM‐positive patients had a numerically shorter median OS (8.4 vs. 19.6 months; log‐rank p = 0.063; Figure 4c). Cox regression yielded an HR of 2.13 (95% CI, 0.94–4.80; p = 0.070) for STM‐positive versus STM‐negative patients. No statistically significant association between STM status and OS was observed within either age group (Figure 4d and Table S3). OS was longer in patients aged < 60 years than in those aged ≥ 60 years in both the STM‐positive (p = 0.034) and the STM‐negative groups (p < 0.001; Figure 4d). Given the small number of STM‐positive patients and the wide CIs, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously. HRs with 95% CIs and corresponding numbers of events for the overall cohort and subgroup analyses are summarized in Table S3. An additional sensitivity analysis excluding patients who received no antileukemic treatment revealed a broadly similar pattern of results (Figure S2).

Exploratory analyses were also performed within the STM‐positive population according to specific STM subtypes. No statistically significant difference in OS was observed between patients with ASXL1 mutations and those with other STMs (Figure S3). RUNX1 mutations were identified in 5 of 26 STM‐positive patients (19.2%), including one patient with a concurrent SF3B1 mutation, and no statistically significant difference in OS was observed between patients with RUNX1 mutations and those with other STMs (Figure S4).

Discussion

Our investigation revealed that patients with NPM1/FLT3‐ITD/DNMT3A triple mutations had the shortest median OS among the four molecular subgroups. Previous studies have also associated the NPM1/FLT3‐ITD/DNMT3A triple‐mutated genotype with poor prognosis [15, 19, 20]. These patients have been reported to be resistant to anthracycline‐based chemotherapy [21]. In contrast, increased sensitivity to quizartinib, a FLT3 inhibitor, and to ibrutinib, a BTK/TEC family kinase inhibitor, has been reported in AML with relevant molecular backgrounds [22, 23]. Yao et al. [19] reported that allogeneic hematopoietic SCT was associated with improved OS, event‐free survival and disease‐free survival in patients with NPM1/FLT3‐ITD/DNMT3A mutations. Garg et al. [24] reported that triple‐mutated AML was associated with an increased frequency of leukemia stem cells and upregulation of hepatic leukemia factor. In addition, Yang et al. [25] reported altered DNA methylation in DNMT3A/FLT3‐ITD‐mutated leukemia. These findings support the presence of a subgroup of NPM1‐mutated AML patients with relatively poor outcomes. However, the mechanisms underlying the adverse prognosis of this subgroup remain incompletely understood.

Several findings in our cohort were consistent with those of previous reports. First, the frequency of STMs was 14.5%, which is comparable to the 13.4%–19% reported in previous studies [1, 2, 3, 18]. STM‐positive patients were also older than STM‐negative patients at diagnosis (median age, 59 vs. 49 years), which is consistent with the findings of Eckardt et al. [1]. We also observed that the cooccurrences of STMs with TET2 mutations and STM VAFs were frequently greater than those of NPM1, which is in agreement with the findings of previous studies [3, 18]. However, several differences were also observed. ASXL1 was the most common type of STM in our cohort, whereas SRSF2 was the predominant type of STM in previous studies [1, 2, 3, 18]. Wright et al. [3] reported a greater proportion of male patients among those with STMs, whereas no significant difference in sex distribution was observed between STM‐positive and STM‐negative patients in our cohort. Eckardt et al. [1] reported lower leukocyte and platelet counts in STM‐positive patients. In our cohort, STM‐positive patients had lower leukocyte counts and bone marrow blast percentages, but the platelet count and hemoglobin level did not differ significantly between the two groups. Secondary AML was also more frequent in STM‐positive patients. These differences may reflect heterogeneity in patient populations and cohort characteristics across studies.

The impact of STMs on the prognosis of NPM1‐mutated AML remains controversial. Chan et al. [2] reported inferior outcomes in STM‐positive patients with favorable‐risk NPM1‐mutated AML. In contrast, Eckardt et al. [1] and Wright et al. [3] reported no significant adverse prognostic impact of STMs. Othman et al. [26] reported that myelodysplasia‐related gene mutations did not adversely affect outcomes in a large cohort of patients with NPM1‐mutated AML. In our cohort, STM status was not significantly associated with OS in the overall population or in the exploratory ELN‐ and age‐defined subgroups. Similar results were observed after patients were censored at the time of SCT and after patients who received no antileukemic treatment were excluded. The reasons for the differences among studies remain unclear, although heterogeneity in patient populations and treatment may contribute. Further studies are needed to clarify the prognostic significance of STMs in NPM1‐mutated AML.

Conclusion

In conclusion, age ≥ 60 years and the presence of FLT3‐ITD mutations were independently associated with poor OS in patients with NPM1‐mutated AML, and patients with NPM1/FLT3‐ITD/DNMT3A triple mutations had the shortest median OS. No statistically significant association between the status of the STM and treatment response or OS was observed. However, given the limited number of STM‐positive patients, particularly within individual subgroups, larger studies are needed to further clarify the prognostic significance of STMs in NPM1‐mutated AML.
