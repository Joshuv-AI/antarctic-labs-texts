Insights into plant‐part‐specific N2O production in roots and shoots of chicory (Cichorium intybus) using stable isotope labelling
Authors: Moritz Schroll, Maurice Maas, Steffen Greiner, Thomas Klintzsch, Frank Keppler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Nitrous oxide (N2O) contributes substantially to climate change and stratospheric ozone degradation, yet large uncertainties in its global budget point to overlooked sources. Growing evidence suggests plants produce N2O, but the mechanisms remain poorly constrained.For the first time, we resolved organ‐specific N2O formation in roots and shoots of aseptically cultivated chicory (Cichorium intybus) under light and dark conditions, combining 15N‐labelling (15NO3 −/15NH4 +) with position‐specific isotope analysis to identify N2O formation pathways.All cultures produced N2O under dark conditions, whereas illuminated shoots showed reduced production or net uptake, indicating photosynthetically driven suppression or internal consumption. Roots supplied with 15NO3 − exhibited relatively symmetric 15N enrichment at the α‐ and β‐positions of emitted N2O (compared to shoots), consistent with a reductive pathway that may involve nitric oxide as an intermediate. By contrast, shoots showed asymmetric intramolecular 15N enrichment exclusively under dark conditions with 15NO3 −, indicating a NO3 −‐dependent pathway mechanistically distinct from that operating in roots. No significant 15N incorporation was detected in any other shoot treatment.These organ‐specific, light‐dependent dynamics are absent from global N2O budgets and vegetation models. Our findings reveal unrecognized complexity in plant N2O exchange and provide a mechanistic framework for incorporating vegetation processes into future N2O budget assessments.

Introduction

Nitrous oxide (N2O) is the third most important anthropogenic greenhouse gas, contributing to climate forcing and likely emerging as the dominant ozone‐depleting substance emitted during the 21st century (Forster et al., 2007; Ravishankara et al., 2009). Its atmospheric concentration has increased by c. 22% compared to preindustrial levels, underscoring its growing impact on global climate and stratospheric ozone depletion (IPCC, 2014; Tian et al., 2020). Nitrogen, a vital macronutrient, plays a crucial role in plant growth and development. It is a fundamental component of Chl, amino acids, nucleic acids, and other secondary metabolites. Plants primarily acquire nitrogen in the forms of nitrate (NO3 −) and ammonium (NH4 +), which become available in the soil through natural processes such as nitrification and ammonification, as well as through fertilizers (Zayed et al., 2023). The microbial conversion of NO3 − and NH4 + to N2O through nitrification, nitrifier‐denitrification, and denitrification is responsible for two‐thirds of N2O emissions to the atmosphere (Tian et al., 2020).

Traditionally, studies on N2O cycling in terrestrial ecosystems have focussed on the exchange of N2O between soil surfaces and the atmosphere. While plants have primarily been recognized as conduits for N2O transfer in the soil–plant–atmosphere system, their own specific role in N2O formation and involved processes have surprisingly only recently been more in the focus of scientific interest. Already in the 1980s, Dean & Harper (1986) described the formation of N2O from two different plants species. Since then, an increasing number of studies observed N2O production and subsequent emission from vegetation including trees, plants, shrubs, herbs, cryptogamic covers, and grass (Dean & Harper, 1986; Chen et al., 1999; Goshima et al., 1999; Smart & Bloom, 2001; Hakata et al., 2003; Pihlatie et al., 2005; Bruhn et al., 2014; Lenhart et al., 2015, 2019; Machacova et al., 2016). However, there are contrasting findings, with several studies reporting N2O formation from the plants themselves, for example, when grown under sterile conditions (e.g. Lenhart et al., 2019) opposed to N2O production in plants attributed mainly to endophytic and phyllospheric bacteria (Bowatte et al., 2015; Qin et al., 2024). This contrasting evidence clearly shows that, even almost 40 yr after its first description, there is a clear lack of understanding of the underlying processes linked with plant N2O formation. This is further highlighted by the reported N2O emission rates from plants showing a large variability ranging from 10 to 6000 pmol g−1 h−1 (Lenhart et al., 2019; Qin et al., 2024). Nevertheless, recent studies by Lenhart et al. (2019) and Qin et al. (2024) estimate that plants contribute between 6% and 11% of global annual N2O emissions. These findings suggest that plant‐mediated N2O emissions are a ubiquitous process and highlight their potentially substantial role in the global N2O cycle. Despite this, the mechanisms of N2O formation in plants are mostly unclear and terrestrial vegetation has not yet been considered in global N2O budgets (Lenhart et al., 2019).

Previous studies have started to provide insight into the N2O formation mechanism using stable isotope techniques, since it allows for the differentiation of multiple sources due to their individual isotopic fingerprint (Toyoda et al., 2017). Position‐specific isotope analysis of N2O distinguishes the isotopic composition at the central (α) and terminal (β) nitrogen positions within the molecule. Because the two nitrogen atoms in N2O can originate from different precursors or reaction steps depending on the formation pathway, the position‐specific isotopic composition provides a powerful tool for mechanistic pathway attribution without requiring additional biochemical assays. Smart & Bloom (2001), by employing 15N‐labelled NO3 − as a tracer, demonstrated that enzymatic processes in plant foliage can generate N2O. Hakata et al. (2003) observed the emission of 15N‐labelled N2O from 16 different plant taxa grown aseptically in a 15N‐labelled NO3 − medium, concluding that NO3 − conversion to N2O is widespread in plants. Lenhart et al. (2019) were the first to report isotopic signatures of N2O released by plants, in addition to measuring the emission rates of 32 plant species from 22 families under controlled laboratory conditions. Emerging research suggests that plants possess the ability to denitrify (Timilsina et al., 2020, 2022a). For instance, mitochondria may serve as sites for N2O formation in plant cells, with evidence showing that plant N2O fluxes correlate positively with respiration rates and negatively with surface areas (Timilsina et al., 2022a). These relationships suggest that roots and mitochondria are integral to N2O production in natural conditions. Mechanistically, NO3 − serves as the primary precursor for plant‐derived N2O, being reduced via nitrate reductase to NO, which can subsequently be converted to N2O either via mitochondrial reduction under low‐O2 conditions or through the phytoglobin–NO cycle. The latter additionally serves to mitigate nitro‐oxidative stress under hypoxia (Timilsina et al., 2020).

Building on existing laboratory studies clearly identifying N2O formation from plants, two recent studies addressed the measurement of plant‐related N2O formation in the field and disentangling this production pathway from soil N2O fluxes using the stable isotope composition of N2O (Timilsina et al., 2022b; Xia et al., 2024). By comparing the isotopic signatures and emissions of N2O released from plants and soils, these studies unambiguously showed that plants are not merely passive conduits for N2O produced in soils but are themselves active sources contributing to atmospheric N2O fluxes. Moreover, there is further emerging evidence that N2O is not only produced by plants but that plants are also capable of reducing N2O fluxes, especially under photosynthetic activity (Schützenmeister et al., 2020). This finding has led to the hypothesis that N2O formation in plants may be more locally linked with the root systems (Timilsina et al., 2022b). However, currently, no study has systematically investigated the difference between N2O formation in different organs of plants such as the root and shoot.

To address this knowledge gap, we designed an isotope‐labelling study using chicory (Cichorium intybus) root and shoot cultures, cultivated under sterile conditions and guided by three specific hypotheses. First, we hypothesize that both roots and shoots are capable of producing N2O under conditions that minimize microbial contributions but that roots exhibit higher N2O fluxes per unit biomass than shoots owing to their higher respiratory activity and potentially lower internal oxygen availability. Second, we hypothesized that roots and shoots form N2O through mechanistically distinct pathways, reflected in different 15N enrichment patterns at the α‐ and β‐nitrogen positions of produced N2O. Third, building on previous evidence that photosynthetic activity reduces plant N2O emissions (Schützenmeister et al., 2020; Machacova et al., 2024), we hypothesized that light alters N2O exchange at the shoot organ level under sterile conditions.

Materials and Methods

Cultivation of root and shoot cultures of Cichorium intybus

Chicory (Cichorium intybus L.) was selected as the model organism for this study because its biology permits the independent cultivation of morphologically intact root and shoot cultures under fully sterile conditions (Kusch et al., 2009), enabling the attribution of N2O production to a single‐plant organ without the confounding influence of the other plant part or of associated soil microorganisms as well as considerable in‐house practical experience in the sterile cultivation. Chicory root cultures were established and cultivated following the methodology described by Kusch et al. (2009), resulting in hairy root cultures (HRCs) that were most comparable to young, fine roots. Root cultures were maintained in Erlenmeyer flasks filled with liquid medium and cultivated at a temperature of 25°C in a shaking incubator at 90 rpm in darkness. For chicory shoot cultures, chicory seeds were first treated with sodium hypochlorite and 0.01% Triton X‐100, followed by submersion in ethanol and subsequent rinsing with sterile water. Seeds were then germinated on Gamborg B5 medium (Duchefa Biochemie, Haarlem, the Netherlands) supplemented with 1% sucrose and 0.8% plant agar (Duchefa Biochemie) with a light phase (16 h) and a dark phase (8 h). Six weeks after germination, the seedlings were cut and the shoot segments were separately cultivated.

Experimental set‐up and conditions

The experimental set‐up of this study consisted of separately incubated chicory shoots and roots with the addition of 15N‐labelled NH4 + and NO3 − solutions. Stable isotope labelled solutions were prepared by dissolving 15N‐labelled ammonium chloride (NH4Cl, 15N ≥ 98%, Sigma‐Aldrich) and sodium nitrate (NaNO3, 15N ≥ 98%, Sigma‐Aldrich) in demineralized and autoclaved water. The final concentrations of NH4Cl and NaNO3 ranged between 27 and 34 mmol l−1. All incubated roots and shoot cultures as well as the respective controls were incubated in airtight 580 ml glass flasks (Weck, Öflingen, Germany) equipped with silicone stoppers (Saint‐Gobain Performance Plastics, Charny, France) for headspace sampling. Root cultures were incubated in liquid Gamborg B5 medium at 23°C and darkness. Accordingly, root controls consisted of the same liquid medium and glass flasks that were used for root cultures, but without the presence of the roots. Chicory shoots were incubated with a Gamborg B5 medium under both dark and light conditions to assess potential effects of light on N2O formation and isotopic composition. Control experiments consisted of the same medium without shoot cultures. A volume of 10 ml of the prepared 15N‐labelled solutions was added to the shoot and root cultures as well as controls using a laminar flow bench. Additionally, shoots and controls were incubated in the dark without the addition of 15N–NH4 + or 15N–NO3 − solutions (Table 1). Due to technical difficulties, no root and ‘shoot + light’ without added labelled compounds could be investigated. Following the addition of the labelled solutions, the glass flasks were transferred to a climate chamber with a temperature of 25°C. Dark experiments were carried out with the chamber lights switched off, whereas light conditions followed a day‐night cycle with a 16 h light phase and an 8 h dark phase. The intensity of photosynthetically active radiation was c. 90 μmol s−1 m−2.

Gas sampling

First, the internal pressure inside the glass flasks was measured using a handheld manometer (GHM Messtechnik GmbH, Erolzheim, Germany) to allow the calculation of the exact amount of substance of target gases. Subsequently, for the analysis of N2O mixing ratios and its isotopic composition, a plastic syringe (Plastipak, Plymouth, MI, USA) was used to extract 60 ml of sample gas, which was transferred into 20 ml or 40 ml glass vials equipped with silicone–polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) septa or butyl–PTFE septa. For the analysis of oxygen (O2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) mixing ratios, a 7 ml gas sample was injected into 3 ml exetainers (Labco Ltd, London, UK). To compensate for the changing pressure inside the glass flasks resulting from gas sampling, a cannula equipped with a 0.2 μm filter (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) was used. Gas samples were collected at three different time points: 6, 24, and 48 h after start of the incubation. In addition, four laboratory air samples were taken at the start of the experiment to determine the initial gas composition within the glass flasks.

Isotopes of N2O and definition of δ‐values

The four most common isotopologues of N2O are 14N14N16O, 14N14N18O, 14N15N16O, and 15N14N16O. The isotopomers 14N15N16O and 15N14N16O differ only in the position of the 15N atom within the molecule. When the 15N atom is located at the central nitrogen position, this configuration is referred to as 15Nα, whereas its placement at the terminal position is defined as 15Nβ. The mean of the isotopic ratios of 15Nα and 15Nβ is known as 15Nbulk–N2O (Toyoda & Yoshida, 1999). In this study, all stable nitrogen isotope ratios are expressed using the conventional ‘delta’ (δ) notation (Eqn 1), referring to the relative difference of the isotope ratio of a sample from the δ 15 Nair−N2 standard: (Eqn 1) δN15=N15N14sampleN15N14air−N2−1

Analysis of N2O mixing ratios and δ15N–N2O values

Analyses of N2O mixing ratios and its stable isotopes were performed using a Picarro G5131‐i cavity ring‐down spectroscope (CRDS; Picarro, Santa Clara, CA, USA). A Small Sample Introduction Module 2 (SSIM2; Picarro) was used to analyse small sample volumes ranging from 20 to 50 ml. Synthetic air (20.5% O2 in N2, ALPHAGAZ™ 1; Air Liquide, Paris, France) was used as carrier gas. Between each run, the system was evacuated and flushed with synthetic air to maintain accuracy. To ensure high‐quality analysis, two standard gases (RM2, δ15Nbulk–N2O: +7.3‰, δ15Nα–N2O: +15.8‰, δ15Nβ–N2O: −1.2‰; PIC14/2, δ15Nbulk–N2O: +4.5‰, δ15Nα–N2O: +15.9‰, δ15Nβ–N2O: −6.8‰) with known mixing ratios and isotopic composition were measured at the beginning and end of each measurement day for calibration and drift correction. CRDS instruments are known to be affected by cross‐sensitivities, where the occurrence and concentration of other gases can substantially influence the results of the analysis of the target gas (e.g. Harris et al., 2020). To prevent cross‐sensitivities with volatile organic compounds, a glass tube filled with Tenax© (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was placed in front of the inlet valve of the SSIM2 for the adsorption of volatile organic compounds. Additionally, the plastic syringe used for gas injection was partially filled the CO2 adsorbent Ascarite(II)© (Acros Organics, Geel, Belgium). Physical CO2 removal via Ascarite(II)© was applied to injected samples but was incomplete at high CO2 concentrations; for calibration standards introduced directly from tanks via the SSIM2, physical removal was not possible, so that in both cases, residual CO2 was additionally accounted for by spectroscopic correction. Corrections were applied for cross‐sensitivities to O2, CO2, CH4, and N2O concentration, as well as for effects of storage time, glass vials, and septa, following a similar approach of Havsteen et al. (2026), who developed a dedicated correction method for instruments of the same type (Supporting Information Methods S1–S3; Figs S1–S9). Although the applied corrections are non‐negligible, the underlying effects are systematic and could therefore be quantified and corrected. The accuracy of the corrected measurements was evaluated using the reference gases. The agreement between the corrected and assigned isotope values of the reference gases indicated that no substantial systematic bias remained after correction. The concentration ranges of interferants during the experiments and the magnitude of the corrections applied are described in the Methods S3. Analytical precision was assessed from repeated measurements of the working standard RM2 (Mohn et al., 2022) and ambient laboratory air, yielding 1σ values for RM2 of 0.5‰ for δ15Nbulk–N2O, 0.4‰ for δ15Nα–N2O, 0.8‰ for δ15Nβ–N2O, and 0.9‰ for δ15NSP–N2O, and for laboratory air of 0.4‰, 0.5‰, 0.4‰, and 0.6‰, respectively. Corresponding 3σ detection limits for RM2 were 1.4‰, 1.2‰, 2.4‰, and 2.7‰, and for laboratory air 1.2‰, 1.4‰, 1.2‰, and 1.8‰, respectively.

Analysis of CO2 and O2 mixing ratios

CO2 and O2 mixing ratios were determined using a GC‐2010 Plus gas chromatograph (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) coupled with a barrier discharge ionization detector (BID‐2010 Plus; Shimadzu). The set‐up included a packed stainless steel column with a length of 2 m, an inner diameter of 0.53 mm, and a carbon molecular sieve (ShinCarbon ST, 80/100 Mesh; Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) as the stationary phase. High‐purity helium (grade 6.0; Air Liquide) was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 5 ml min−1. Sample injection was performed using an autosampler (AOC‐20i, Shimadzu). As part of quality control, one reference standard was measured after every six to nine single measurements. Calibration was conducted using certified standard gas mixtures (CRYSTAL Gas Mixture; Air Liquide). The quantification of CO2 and O2 was determined by analysing multiple reference standards (CO2: 400 ppm–40% by volume, O2: 1–22% by volume), with each measurement performed in triplicate.

Mass balance approach for determination of isotopic composition of N2O

To characterize the isotopic composition of the N2O produced during each incubation, we applied a two‐component isotope mass balance approach. At time point t2, the total N2O in the flask headspace was treated as a mixture of the N2O present at the start of the incubation (t0) and the N2O produced by the plant tissue between t0 and t2. The isotopic composition of the produced N2O was calculated according to Eqn (2): (Eqn 2) δNproduced15=xit2xVHSxδNt215−xit0xVHSxδNt015xit2−xit0 where xi represents the mixing ratio of N2O and V HS represents the headspace volume at the respective time point. This calculation was applied identically to δ15Nbulk–N2O, δ15Nα, and δ15Nβ, yielding the position‐specific isotopic composition of produced N2O for each treatment. Values are reported with their associated SD from replicate measurements.

Statistical methods

The evaluation of measurement data from the Picarro G5131‐i was performed using R and Microsoft Excel. For the analysis of raw data from the Picarro G5131‐i, moving averages of the measurements over 100 s were calculated. For each measurement, a stable measurement period of at least 5 min was selected. Mixing ratios, production rates, and the isotopic change rate are presented as the arithmetic mean (n = 3 or 4) of independent replicates with their SDs. For some samples, results at specific time points were discarded due to analytical problems and instrument failure, including sample loss within the Picarro system and carry‐over effects from previous measurements. If fewer than three measurements were available for a given time point, no calculation of the SD was carried out. Additionally, the obtained data were used to determine the production rates of the gases N2O, O2, and CO2 for the shoot and root cultures as well as for the controls. For each incubated glass flask, an individual production rate was determined using linear regression. The arithmetic means of these production rates were then calculated for the same plant compartment and treatment. Furthermore, t‐tests were performed using sigmaplot (v.12.2.0.45, USA) to statistically support the observed findings. Please note that this study follows the recommendations of the American Statistical Association, ensuring that P‐values and other statistical parameters were not used as the sole basis for drawing conclusions (Wasserstein et al., 2019). Consequently, the term ‘statistically significant’ was deliberately avoided in the interpretation of the results.

Results

The incubations comprised a series of experiments in which shoot and root cultures of chicory were incubated under dark and light conditions with the addition of 15N‐labelled NO3 − and NH4 + to assess the changes in the mixing ratios of N2O, CO2, and O2 as well as the isotopic composition of N2O. The results in the first three subsections (Figs 1, 2, 3) present changes relative to the measurements at the start of the incubation. Furthermore, measured values are reported as absolute changes in the respective glass flasks, without considering the biomass of the different plant parts to ensure the comparability of flasks with plants and the respective controls, as the controls do not possess biomass. For the calculation of production rates, dry plant biomass was considered (Fig. 4). Chicory roots under dark conditions

N2O mixing ratios during the incubation of chicory roots increased by 38.8 ± 4.6 parts per billion by volume (ppbv) when supplemented with 15NO3 − (n = 3; Fig. 1a). The increase in N2O mixing ratios was accompanied by a strong shift in stable isotope values towards more positive values with δ15Nbulk–N2O values increasing by +64.7 ± 6.5‰ (n = 3), δ15Nα–N2O by +72.0 ± 7.6‰, δ15Nβ–N2O by +57.7 ± 5.4‰, and δ15NSP–N2O values by +14.3 ± 2.2‰ (n = 3; Fig. 1d–g). CO2 and O2 mixing ratios exhibited opposite trends, with CO2 increasing on average by 20.4 ± 0.7% (n = 3) while O2 mixing ratios decreased by 15.0 ± 1.1% (n = 3) (Fig. 1b,c).

When chicory roots were supplemented with 15NH4 +, N2O mixing ratios increased by 46.5 ± 8.4 ppbv (n = 3; Fig. 1a). Compared to the treatment with NO3 −, ‘root + 15NH4 +’ showed a less pronounced shift towards more positive stable isotope values with δ15Nbulk–N2O values increasing by +13.3 ± 1.3‰ (n = 3), δ15Nα–N2O by +17.1 ± 0.7‰, δ15Nβ–N2O by +9.6 ± 1.9‰, and δ15NSP–N2O values by +7.5 ± 1.4‰ (n = 3; Fig. 1d–g). The δ15NSP–N2O value of produced N2O reached 7.5 ± 1.4‰ at the end of the incubation period (Fig. 1g), lower than in the 15NO3 − treatment but still reflecting preferential 15N enrichment at the α‐position. During the incubation period, CO2 mixing ratios increased by 21.9 ± 1.2% (n = 3) while O2 mixing ratios decreased by 16.7 ± 1.0% (n = 3).

Controls containing ‘medium + 15NO3 −’ and ‘medium + 15NH4 +’ showed only minor increases in N2O mixing ratios (5.5 ppbv for ‘medium + 15NO3 −’ (n = 2) and 4.5 ppbv for ‘medium + 15NH4 +’ (n = 2), respectively) and negligible changes in stable isotope values as well as CO2 and O2 mixing ratios during the incubation.

Chicory shoots under dark conditions

N2O mixing ratios increased by 9.8 ± 2.8 ppbv when chicory shoots were incubated in the dark with 15NO3 − (n = 3; Fig. 2a). This increase was accompanied by a pronounced shift towards more positive δ15N values: +10.1 ± 1.4‰ for δ15Nbulk–N2O, +15.8 ± 1.1‰ for δ15Nα–N2O, +4.6 ± 2.5‰ for δ15Nβ–N2O, and for 11.2 ± 2.7‰ for δ15NSP–N2O (n = 3; Fig. 2b–d,g). CO2 mixing ratios increased by 3.28 ± 0.77% while O2 decreased by 3.91 ± 0.59% (n = 3; Fig. 2e,f).

When shoots were incubated in the dark with 15NH4 +, N2O mixing ratios elevated by 10.5 ± 1.3 ppbv (n = 4; Fig. 2a). Chicory shoots under light conditions

The N2O mixing ratio increased by 15.4 ppbv when shoots were incubated in the light with 15NO3 − (n = 2; Fig. 3a). This was accompanied by a pronounced shift towards more positive δ15N values. δ15Nbulk–N2O values increased by 14.4‰ (n = 2; Fig. 3d), δ15Nα–N2O values by 14.1 ± 1.5‰, δ15Nβ–N2O values by 4.5 ± 2.0‰ (n = 3; Fig. 3e,f), and δ15NSP–N2O values by 9.6‰ (n = 2; Fig. 3g). Under the same conditions, CO2 mixing ratios increased by 4.20% and 4.33% (n = 2; Fig. 3b), respectively, while O2 decreased by 0.34% and 3.83% (n = 2; Fig. 3c).

When shoots were supplied with 15NH4 + under light exposure, the N2O mixing ratio increased by 7.9 ± 1.6 ppbv (n = 3; Fig. 3a). Shifts towards more positive δ15Nbulk–N2O values were more modest (+2.7 ± 0.5‰; n = 3; Fig. 3d) and δ15Nα–N2O increased by 2.7 ± 0.5‰, δ15Nβ–N2O by 2.6 ± 0.3‰, and δ15NSP–N2O values by 0.1 ± 0.8‰ (n = 3; Fig. 3e–g). Controls (‘medium + 15NO3 − + light’ and ‘medium + 15NH4 + + light’; n = 3) showed only minor increases in N2O mixing ratios of 1.1 ± 0.6 ppbv and 6.8 ± 2.6 ppbv, respectively (Fig. 3a). Isotopic shifts in these controls were negligible for δ15Nbulk–N2O values (1.0 ± 0.4‰ and +1.5 ± 0.3‰), while δ15Nα–N2O/δ15Nβ–N2O values changed by < 3‰ and δ15NSP–N2O values changed by +3.7 ± 0.2‰ in the 15NO3 − control (n = 2; Fig. 3d–f,g). CO2 and O2 mixing ratios remained within ±0.4% of initial values.

Rates of N2O, CO2 production, and O2 uptake

Root cultures produced N2O with rates of 15.1 ± 4.1 pmol h−1 g−1 (n = 3) and 20.0 ± 5.1 pmol h−1 g−1 (n = 3, P = 0.89 when comparing these treatments) for NO3 − and NH4 + supplementation, respectively. Shoots incubated under dark conditions exhibited the highest N2O production rates when supplemented with either 15NO3 − (23.2 ± 3.7 pmol h−1 g−1, n = 3) or 15NH4 + (27.1 ± 6.0 pmol h−1 g−1, n = 4), while the ‘shoot + dark only’ treatment exhibited a production rate of 13.6 ± 2.8 pmol h−1 g−1 (n = 3, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4a). When shoots were incubated under light exposure, substantially lower N2O production rates were observed than those under dark conditions (P < 0.05). Specifically, the ‘shoot + 15NO3 − + light’ treatment yielded a production rate of 2.6 pmol h−1 g−1 (n = 2), while the ‘shoot + 15NH4 + + light’ treatment even showed a small net uptake of N2O, with a rate of −1.3 ± 0.8 pmol h−1 g−1 (n = 3).

Discussion

Plant‐mediated N2O cycling as an organ‐specific process: a revised conceptual framework

Our results demonstrate for the first time at the plant organ level that root and shoot N2O cycling operates through distinct biochemical pathways responding differently to NO3 −/NH4 + availability and light regime. This extends the current conceptual framework of plant roles in the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum: beyond soil‐derived N2O flux, plant‐mediated transport, and phyllosphere interactions, plants harbour an intrinsic, organ‐specific set of N2O production and consumption pathways that fundamentally alter how plant contributions to atmospheric N2O should be conceptualized and quantified (Fig. 6).

Conceptual scheme of the nitrous oxide (N2O) production, consumption, and transport processes across the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum. Processes established by prior literature are shown by black and white arrows; processes newly characterized at the organ level by this study, including the mechanistic distinction between root and shoot N2O formation pathways via isotope labelling (red arrows) and the light‐dependent source‐sink switch in shoots, are highlighted in orange and purple arrows.

Using sterile chicory cultures combining concentration measurement and 15N stable isotope labelling, we confirm that both roots and shoots independently produce and emit N2O without microbial influence. 15N enrichment in emitted N2O from root cultures supplied with 15N‐labelled NO3 −, accompanied with negligible isotopic shifts in medium‐only controls, provides strong evidence that plant tissue rather than microbial activity was the dominant source of labelled N2O under these sterile conditions. All medium‐only controls showed negligible changes in δ15N–N2O values, confirming that plant tissue drove observed isotopic enrichment in emitted N2O, though a slight increase in δ15N–N2O in the ‘medium + 15NO3 − + dark’ control may reflect a minor abiotic contribution. However, given the low changes in N2O mixing ratios in these controls compared to the plant treatments, this is not considered to have meaningfully affected our results.

Most current models treat microbial activity as the primary N2O source and represent plant contributions as passive conduit transport of soil‐derived N2O. Our results show that N2O dynamics in the soil–plant‐atmosphere continuum are more complex: N2O produced in roots and shoots of distinct biochemical origin may mix as it moves upwards through the plant, with some fraction potentially reduced within plant cells. These organ‐specific processes must be incorporated into future vegetation N2O emission models, which currently do not account for them (Lenhart et al., 2019; Qin et al., 2024).

Root N2O production: respiratory hypoxia and an NO3 −‐dependent reductive pathway

Root cultures showed clear 15N enrichment in N2O when supplied with 15N–NO3 − but not 15N–NH4 +, confirming that NO3 −, but not NH4 +, serves as the primary N2O precursor in roots, consistent with earlier work (Goshima et al., 1999; Lenhart et al., 2019). Crucially, the ‘root + 15N–NO3 −’ treatment was the only treatment where substantial amounts of N2O were produced and in which both δ15Nα–N2O and δ15Nβ–N2O increased in similar proportions (Fig. 5). This position‐specific enrichment was relatively symmetric; that is, it showed a smaller intramolecular difference than the ‘shoots + 15N–NO3 − + dark’ treatment. As one possible explanation, this pattern may reflect the isotopic fingerprint of N2O formation via nitric oxide as an intermediate: when two NO molecules, both originating from the same 15N labelled nitrate pool, combine to form N2O, 15N is distributed between the central (α) and terminal (β) nitrogen positions to a similar degree. We interpret this only as a relative comparison between organs and not as an assignment of a specific pathway, since the underlying δ15NSP–N2O value is a mass‐balance‐derived (the measured δ values remained near the calibrated range) and natural abundance site preference values (e.g. Sutka et al., 2006) might not apply in a 15N labelling experiment. The mass‐balance‐derived isotopic composition of produced N2O in the ‘root + 15NO3 −’ treatment reflects this clearly, with δ15Nbulk–N2O values of 635 ± 91‰, δ15Nα–N2O values of 716 ± 102‰ and δ15Nβ–N2O values of 557 ± 80‰, indicating near‐proportional enrichment at both positions. By contrast, the ‘root +15NH4 +’ treatment showed substantially lower source values across all metrics (δ15Nbulk–N2O: 117 ± 6‰, δ15Nα–N2O: 161 ± 16‰, δ15Nβ–N2O: 74 ± 6‰), consistent with limited incorporation of ammonium‐derived nitrogen into N2O via this pathway. The δ15NSP–N2O values further support a distinction between the two treatments. Total δ15NSP–N2O values were higher in the ‘root + 15NO3 −’ treatment (160 ± 23‰) than in the ‘root + 15NH4 +’ treatment (86 ± 20‰). The ‘root 15NH4 + treatment’ showed stronger relative enrichment at the α‐position compared to the β‐position. This suggests substrate‐dependent differences in 15N distribution within the plant and may be consistent with N2O formation via the phytoglobin–NO cycle and/or mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase in nitrate‐supplied roots (Timilsina et al., 2020).

The probable mechanistic driver of this pathway is root respiratory hypoxia. Elevated CO2 production and O2 consumption in root incubations (Fig. 4b,c) indicate high respiratory demand that progressively depletes internal O2. Under these low‐oxygen conditions, reduction in NO2 − to NH4 + by nitrite reductase is inhibited, favouring NO accumulation and its subsequent conversion to N2O. The correlation between N2O production and respiration rates in our data is consistent with the central mitochondrial role proposed by Timilsina et al. (2020, 2022a).

Shoot N2O production: oxygen‐sensitive pathway distinct from roots

The shoot data reveal a mechanistically informative divergence from expectations based on respiratory O2 depletion alone. Under dark conditions with NO3 −/NH4 + supplementation, shoots produced c. 23–27 pmol g−1 h−1, roughly 35% more N2O per unit biomass than roots under equivalent conditions, despite respiratory CO2 production and O2 consumption rates less than half those of roots (Fig. 4a–c). If N2O formation were simply a by‐product of respiratory O2 depletion, roots should outproduce shoots on a per‐biomass basis. The fact that shoots exceed root N2O production under conditions of comparatively low respiratory activity suggests that shoot N2O formation operates through a pathway governed by mechanisms distinct from mitochondrial hypoxic NO reduction.

A different N2O formation pathway in shoots compared to roots is also supported by the isotopic data. Both root + 15NO3 − and shoot + 15NO3 −+ dark treatments showed marked 15N enrichment in produced N2O, indicating that NO3 − serves as an N2O precursor in shoots as well as roots. Critically, however, the two treatments differed in the intramolecular distribution of 15N: roots supplemented with 15NO3 − showed relatively symmetric enrichment across the α‐ and β‐positions of N2O, whereas the shoot + 15NO3 − + dark treatment exhibited a markedly asymmetric pattern (mass‐balance‐derived δ15NSP–N2O values of 160 ± 23‰ and 429 ± 102‰, respectively; Fig. 5). We suggest that this asymmetry in site preference enrichment is indicative of a distinct N2O‐forming pathway in shoots compared to roots, while the dependence on NO3 − as substrate is retained in both organs.

By contrast, 15NH4 + was not detectably incorporated into N2O in shoots under dark conditions: the shoot + 15NH4 + + dark treatment (δ15Nbulk–N2O: 66 ± 10‰, δ15Nα–N2O: 119 ± 27‰, δ15Nβ–N2O: 14 ± 7‰, δ15NSP–N2O: 105 ± 33‰) was isotopically indistinguishable from the unlabelled shoot + dark control (δ15Nbulk–N2O: 64 ± 24‰, δ15Nα–N2O: 112 ± 28‰, δ15Nβ–N2O: 18 ± 21‰, δ15NSP–N2O: 94 ± 11‰), confirming that NH4 + does not serve as a significant N2O precursor in shoots. Because the shoot + dark‐only treatment received no 15N label, its δ15N values represent the natural isotopic baseline of shoot‐emitted N2O, reflecting fractionation during N2O formation from endogenous nitrogen pools rather than tracer incorporation. Medium‐only controls, including the medium + 15NH4 + control, showed negligible enrichment across all metrics, confirming that the observed isotopic signals originate from plant tissue activity rather than abiotic processes. Notably, the shoot + 15NH4 + + dark treatment remained at this isotopic baseline, indicating that the N2O emitted by shoots without 15NO3 − originates from endogenous, unlabelled nitrogen rather than from the supplied ammonium.

Photosynthetic suppression of shoot N2O formation and the emergence of net uptake

Photosynthetic activity suppressed shoot N2O production by 88% in the ‘NO3 − + light’ treatment and induced net N2O uptake in the ‘NH4 + + light’ treatment. This differential response, with more complete suppression under NH4 + than NO3 −, may reflect the distinct enzymatic pathways involved: NO3 −‐derived N2O production proceeds via well‐characterized reductive steps (NO3 − → NO2 − → NO → N2O), whereas N2O formation from NH4 + requires prior oxidation to NO2 − or NO, a pathway less directly connected to the major nitrogen assimilation routes and therefore potentially more sensitive to disruption by photosynthetically elevated O2 and enhanced nitrite reductase activity.

The suppression mechanism operates through at least three linked steps. First, photosynthesis elevates internal O2, disfavouring cytochrome c oxidase‐mediated NO reduction. Second, light enhances nitrite reductase activity, efficiently converting NO2 − to NH4 + and thereby eliminating a key N2O precursor. Third, an as‐yet uncharacterized plant enzymatic activity may convert produced N2O to N2 possibly explaining net uptake rather than simply reduced production; canonical bacterial N2O reductase (NosZ) is O2‐sensitive and therefore unlikely to account for this effect under photosynthetic conditions, but the occurrence of N2 emissions from plants and net N2O consumption in the light suggest a distinct, possibly O2‐tolerant consumption pathway in plant tissue. This is consistent with reports of N2 emissions from wheat supplied with labelled NO2 − (Vanecko & Varner, 1955), with the negative correlation between N2O consumption and CO2 respiration across plant taxa (Timilsina et al., 2020), and with Schützenmeister et al. (2020), who found up to 63% reduction in N2O production during photosynthesis in ash and beech saplings. Net N2O uptake by European beech under certain conditions (Machacova et al., 2024) further supports the existence of an active plant N2O consumption pathway.

A ‘shoot + light’ control without 15N label could not be included due to instrument failure, which also prevented additional measurements from being conducted. Despite these limitations, the suppression of N2O formation under illumination is supported by the consistently low 15N enrichment across all light treatments irrespective of substrate, indicating that minimal net N2O flux carries little 15N label and that the light response does not depend on any single treatment.

Implications for global N2O budgets: towards organ‐resolved, diel‐dynamic vegetation flux models

The findings presented here motivate concrete revisions to how plant‐mediated N2O is represented in global budgets, in two respects:

First, mechanistic resolution at the organ level. Currently, plant N2O production is conceptually represented as a single process. The results presented here require that representation to be split: roots operate a hypoxia‐associated, NO3 −‐dependent reductive pathway that may involve NO as an intermediate; shoots operate through a distinct, oxygen‐sensitive mechanism with a light‐regulated source‐sink switch.

Second, the diel dynamic. The night‐time source and daytime sink behaviour of shoots, indicated by the dark and light treatments, creates a strongly time‐structured plant N2O flux. Vegetation models that apply a single emission factor regardless of time of day or photosynthetic status will both misattribute the timing and potentially misestimate the magnitude of plant contributions. Integrating the light‐switch mechanism identified here into land‐surface models represents a necessary next step, though further studies under natural conditions are needed to better characterize the magnitude and environmental controls of this process.

The plants used in this study were cultivated under sterile conditions, substantially reducing potential microbial contributions to N2O production, although their complete exclusion cannot be guaranteed. In addition, the HRCs investigated here are most representative of young fine roots rather than mature roots of soil‐grown plants. Future studies should therefore extend this isotope‐based approach to intact plants in soil mesocosms. This will enable simultaneous partitioning of root, shoot, and soil N2O fluxes and provide a framework for evaluating the magnitude and environmental controls of plant‐derived N2O production under more natural conditions. By comparing sterile and nonsterile systems, the position‐specific isotope labelling approach demonstrated here could further be used to discriminate N2O of plant‐metabolic origin from that produced by associated microorganisms such as endophytes (Qin et al., 2024). This would allow the proportion of plant‐associated N2O attributable to the plant itself to be constrained, which we consider an important direction for future research.
