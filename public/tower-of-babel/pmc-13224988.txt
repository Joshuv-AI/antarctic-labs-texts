Sequential robotic-assisted thoracic surgery and transmanubrial approach for optimal resection of superior sulcus tumor: a case report
Authors: Rei Kobayashi, Eri Ota, Hironobu Yamaoka, Kana Nagayama, Ryoji Kawano, Yuichi Nakamura, Manabu Yamasaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuperior sulcus tumors invading the chest wall and major mediastinal vessels present significant surgical challenges. The transmanubrial approach (TMA) has been widely adopted for such tumors. However, the exposure of the dorsal side, particularly of the apex of the lung, can be limited when performing a TMA alone. This hybrid approach of TMA with robotic thoracic surgery improves visualization and resection safely. The clinical significance of this hybrid approach has received little attention. Here, we report a case of post-immunochemotherapy surgery using combined robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) and a TMA to achieve safe resection and vascular reconstruction of a tumor invading the right brachiocephalic vein.Case DescriptionA 59-year-old man was diagnosed with right upper lobe squamous cell carcinoma (cT4N0M0, stage IIIA) invading the anterior apex and mediastinal structures, including the right brachiocephalic vein. Following four cycles of immunochemotherapy (carboplatin, nab-paclitaxel, and pembrolizumab), significant tumor regression was observed, and post-immunochemotherapy surgery was planned. A RATS lobectomy was first performed, in the left lateral decubitus position, using the da Vinci Xi system with a five-port setup using port placement only, without mini-thoracotomy, which allowed detailed hilar and apical dorsal dissection. Subsequently, the patient was placed in a supine position, and the TMA was used to resect and reconstruct the invaded right brachiocephalic vein using a synthetic graft. The total operative time was 12 h and 8 min, and the total blood loss was 1,120 mL. The postoperative course was uneventful. The chest tube was removed on postoperative day 4, and no major complications such as prolonged air leak, pneumonia, or recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy were observed. And the patient was discharged on postoperative day 12. The pathological examination findings revealed that the staging was ypT2bN0M0 stage IIA. The patient has remained recurrence-free for 6 months following the surgery.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates that a hybrid approach combining RATS and the TMA is a safe and effective strategy for the resection of superior sulcus tumors with vascular invasion. RATS enhances surgical visualization and facilitates apical dissection, thereby optimizing safety and surgical outcomes when followed with the TMA.Keywords: Robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS), transmanubrial approach (TMA), superior sulcus tumor, case report, brachiocephalic vein reconstruction

Introduction

Background

Enbloc resection of superior sulcus tumors invading the apical region, superior vena cava (SVC), and bilateral brachiocephalic veins is challenging. Thoracic surgeons recognize the transmanubrial approach (TMA) as an optimal strategy for treating anterior superior sulcus tumors (1-3). However, in our several cases using the TMA, the operation was performed with poor visibility in the supine position because invasive tumors were large and obscured important thoracic structures. More specifically, in TMA, the phrenic, vagus, and recurrent laryngeal nerves are frequently difficult to identify as they lie behind or are obscured by the tumor. Furthermore, taping of the azygos vein is technically demanding, and complete dissection of right upper mediastinal lymph nodes can be challenging.

Rationale and knowledge gap

It remains unclear whether robotic-assisted surgery can complement these limitations of TMA.

However, robotic surgery offers excellent maneuverability in deep operative fields, making it particularly advantageous for apical dissection, especially from the dorsal side. In addition, RATS in the left lateral decubitus position facilitates dorsal manipulation even for large tumors, which we believe complements TMA performed in the supine position that provides excellent anterior visualization.

There are several reports about robotic surgery with superior sulcus tumor (4-6). And, to our knowledge, there are a few reports describing a combined TMA and robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) (7,8). However, we were unable to identify studies specifically showing that RATS performed first streamlines TMA. We also did not find prior descriptions of port-only RATS with post-TMA specimen retrieval that eliminates an intercostal utility thoracotomy (Figure 1). We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jovs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jovs-25-36/rc).

Case presentation

The study was approved by the Institutional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Itabashi Chuo Medical Center (No. 251028C) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report, accompanying images and the videos. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

A 59-year-old man underwent a computed tomography (CT) scan, which revealed a superior sulcus tumor measuring 70 mm × 44 mm × 85 mm in the right upper lobe. The tumor was extensively located adjacent to the mediastinum, the right brachiocephalic vein at the level of the bifurcation of the left brachiocephalic vein (Figure 2). After several examinations including enhanced CT, positron emission tomography (PET), and bronchoscopic biopsy, the patient was diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma [(SCC), cT4N0M0 stage IIIA] (Figures 3,4). We considered this case to be marginal with regards to curative resectability. In addition, this patient was on aspirin therapy for the prevention of recurrent stroke following a cerebral infarction. This patient has no particular family and psycho-social history. He was free of pain, and the physical examination demonstrated no remarkable findings.

The patient underwent four courses of immunochemotherapy with carboplatin (CBDCA) + nab-paclitaxel (PTX) + pembrolizumab. As a result of treatment, the CT revealed the tumor reduction was achieved, and a decision to proceed with post-immunochemotherapy surgery was made. Preoperative PET scans showed that the residual tumor was confined to the anterior region (Figures 3,4).

The patient initially had no neurological symptoms such as numbness or motor weakness of the right upper limb. After immunochemotherapy, the tumor had markedly decreased in size, and neither CT nor PET demonstrated findings suggestive of brachial plexus invasion. Therefore, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was not performed. However, we acknowledge that MRI might have provided a more accurate assessment.

After immunochemotherapy, the restaging was ycT2N0M0, corresponding to stage IB. In addition, we anticipated that the tumor could potentially require dissection around or even en bloc resection/reconstruction of the confluence of the bilateral brachiocephalic veins. If reconstruction was required, extensive dissection of apical region would have to be performed after heparinization was started for artificial graft reconstruction, which would then heighten the risk of bleeding. For this reason, we elected to begin the operation with a RATS approach, rather than TMA.

We performed RATS lobectomy using the da Vinci Xi system (Intuitive Surgical, Inc., Sunnyvale, California, USA). The surgical procedure was performed with five ports (four robotic arms and one assistant port without mini-thoracotomy), employing a fully robotic approach.

During RATS for a right upper lobectomy, we typically begin working from the dorsal side using the Dylewski RATS technique, which does not require a utility incision (9). In this case, we were able to perform the procedure in the following order: separation of the upper and lower lobes, resection of the A2 branch, separation of the upper and middle lobes, V1–3, A1+3, and upper lobe bronchus dissection.

The hilum was successfully dissected (V1–3, A1+3, right upper bronchus), while the apical lung region was fixed to the chest wall (Video 1).

RATS lobectomy of the right upper lobe and preparations for TMA. RATS, robotic-assisted thoracic surgery; TMA, transmanubrial approach.

We dissected and taped the azygos vein, vagus nerve, and phrenic nerve. The internal mammary vein and artery were identified, dissected, and divided in advance to prevent bleeding during the L-shaped sternal transection in the TMA procedure. We then resected the first intercostal space, and dissected the adhesions on the chest wall around the right upper lobe owing to inflammation following immunochemotherapy (Video 1). The surgical time for the RATS was 6 h and 55 min, with a blood loss volume of 220 mL. Subsequently, the patient was placed in a supine position, and right brachiocephalic vein resection and reconstruction with a synthetic graft were performed using the TMA (Video 2). The total surgical time was 12 h and 8 min, with a blood loss volume of 1,120 mL. Reconstruction of the right brachiocephalic veins was performed using a Y-shaped configuration of an 8-mm expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) vascular graft (W.L. Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, AZ, USA), anastomosed with continuous suturing using 5-0 ePTFE suture material (W.L. Gore & Associates). The length of the resected and reconstructed vein segment was approximately 85 mm.

Vascular reconstruction procedure following tumor resection. The right brachiocephalic vein was performed using a running suture technique under direct vision via the TMA. TMA, transmanubrial approach.

The bronchovascular structures required for right upper lobectomy were divided smoothly with the robotic approach; however, the subsequent adhesiolysis and dissection around the right brachiocephalic vessels required considerable time.

Dissection around the right brachiocephalic artery was also feasible under the TMA approach on macroscopic inspection without frozen section. Although there was a potential risk of microscopic positive margin in this site, we decided not to perform combined resection with brachiocephalic artery clamping, considering the patient’s history of cerebral infarction. Furthermore, because preoperative radiotherapy had not been administered, the option of postoperative radiation therapy remained available.

No intraoperative or postoperative complications occurred, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 12. The pathological examination findings revealed that the staging was ypT2bN0M0 stage IIA, and the tumor was classified as no major regression (one-third to two-thirds viable tumor remaining). However, post-immunochemotherapy CT demonstrated a marked reduction in tumor size from 70 mm × 44 mm × 85 mm to 35 mm × 25 mm × 34 mm, corresponding to approximately one-eighth of the original volume.

A total of 15 lymph nodes from six nodal stations were harvested and examined, confirming pathological N0. These included stations #2R, #4R, #10, #11s, #12u, and the subclavian region, none of which showed evidence of metastasis. Subcarinal lymph node dissection was not performed to avoid bronchial stump dehiscence. Importantly, none of these lymph nodes showed FDG uptake on preoperative PET imaging. The tumor was completely resected macroscopically; however, microscopic examination revealed pleural defects near the stump of the right brachiocephalic vein at the apex of the lung, corresponding to the area of dissection around the right brachiocephalic artery. But the others were considered not involved, including the chest wall. Therefore, additional postoperative radiation therapy (60 Gy) was administered to the area surrounding the right brachiocephalic artery. At 6 months postoperatively, the CT scan revealed no recurrence was observed (Table 1).

Discussion

Key findings

This surgical procedure was performed using RATS, followed by a TMA thoracotomy. This method has several benefits, which has rarely been described in the literature. This case report provides valuable insights into the potential role of robotic-assisted surgery in such thoracic tumors.

The RATS approach is less invasive than thoracotomy and is expected to result in less postoperative neuralgia, because this procedure can be completed using only 8–12-mm ports without a utility incision.

En bloc resection of apical lung cancer involving the chest wall requires a large thoracotomy (2,3). Instead of thoracotomy, we could undertake RATS for this operation to reduce postoperative pain using five ports and a minimum L-shaped manubrium incision, which could prevent shoulder immobility (7).

The RATS approach allows greater maneuverability in the apex area of the lung than thoracoscopic surgery (5). By itself, the TMA in a supine position has the following limitations: thoracic surgeons sometimes cannot identify important nerves, such as the vagus and phrenic nerves, from the viewpoint provided when using the TMA because they are not as familiar with this supine position viewpoint as they are when patients are positioned laterally. Moreover, a massive tumor often hides these nerves or the advanced lung cancer often involves them. Additionally, hemostasis cannot be achieved effectively after superior mediastinal lymph node dissection when using the supine TMA. The RATS approach in the lateral position may help overcome these limitations associated with the TMA in the supine position. RATS enabled preparation for the TMA through facilitating the taping of the azygos vein, vagus nerve, phrenic nerve, and the internal mammary vein and artery, which is occasionally more challenging in the supine position than in the lateral position.

This operation resulted in not large blood loss, especially during RATS, in the first half of the procedure. The intraoperative blood loss of more than 1,000 mL cannot generally be regarded as small. However, considering that this procedure required extensive adhesiolysis, was lengthy, included vascular reconstruction, and was performed under aspirin administration, we believe that the amount of blood loss was not excessive. Effective hemostasis achieved during RATS likely contributed to this favorable outcome.

Strengths and limitations

During surgery following immunochemotherapy for advanced lung cancer, adhesions to the surrounding tissues are frequently encountered. Distinguishing whether such adhesions represent true invasion by viable tumor cells or inflammatory adhesions/scarring secondary to treatment is often challenging. One site located cranial to the upper-lobe bronchus, which showed FDG uptake on PET before immunochemotherapy but not preoperatively, was submitted for intraoperative frozen-section analysis, and the result was negative. Although intraoperative frozen-section analysis may be useful for this purpose, evaluation can be difficult in cases with extensive chest wall adhesion, as in the present case. In this patient, FDG-PET positive lesions on post-immunochemotherapy were not located adjacent to the chest wall. Therefore, we considered the adhesion to represent post-treatment scar tissue and proceeded with dissection along the parietal pleural plane. Final pathological examination confirmed that no viable tumor cells were present at the chest wall dissection site.

The microscopic positive margin (R1) occurred in the area dissected via the TMA, rather than during the RATS portion, and therefore was not attributable to limitations of the robotic technique. The positive margin was located near the right brachiocephalic artery, where complex dissection was required, and this outcome had been anticipated to some extent. Although this could potentially have been avoided by performing resection and reconstruction of the right brachiocephalic artery in addition to the venous resection, we considered that the risk-benefit balance was unfavorable because of the patient’s history of cerebral infarction; therefore, arterial resection was not performed.

Comparison with similar research

Most of previous reports have described TMA or robotic surgery alone, but to our knowledge, only a few reports have combined the two techniques (7,8). We also did not find prior descriptions of port-only RATS with post-TMA specimen retrieval that eliminates an intercostal utility thoracotomy (Figure 1).

Explanations of findings

In this case, immunochemotherapy reduced the tumor size from 70 mm × 44 mm × 85 mm to 35 mm × 25 mm × 34 mm, and the FDG-avid region on PET also markedly decreased. Therefore, surgical resection was attempted.

Because residual tumor was suspected and inflammatory adhesions were anticipated around the SVC and brachiocephalic vein, TMA was considered necessary based on the preoperative imaging.

As demonstrated in the operative video, resection of the right brachiocephalic vein was ultimately required, and the TMA approach was essential to safely perform this procedure.

Implications and actions needed

This hybrid approach may be considered in selected patients. We consider that this approach may be appropriate when preoperative imaging suggests that hilar dissection can be performed safely. In contrast, when vascular dissection at the hilum is expected to be challenging—such as in cases with hilar tumor invasion or dense adhesions—intrapericardial taping of the pulmonary artery and/or pulmonary vein would be required, and an open approach may be preferable to RATS.

Furthermore, because lobectomy needs to be performed while the apex remains fixed, it may be advantageous if either the fissure between the upper and lower lobes or between the upper and middle lobes is well developed. Further studies are needed to confirm its safety and efficacy.

Conclusions

RATS, followed by TMA thoracotomy, has several benefits. Therefore, combining RATS with the TMA is a promising surgical procedure. RATS can enhance surgical visualization and facilitate apical dissection, thereby optimizing safety and surgical outcomes when followed with the TMA.

Patient perspective

This patient provided the following perspective: “Although it was a major surgery, I was pleased that my recovery was smooth and I was able to return to work earlier than I had expected.”
