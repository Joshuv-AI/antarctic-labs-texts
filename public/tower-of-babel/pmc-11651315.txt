How the Content of Interprofessional Safety Management Education Can Be Structured Through the Perception of Nursing Home Practitioners: A Mixed Methods Design
Authors: You Jung Hong, Dayeong Kim, Sung Ok Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to assess perceptions of safety management, with a particular focus on interprofessional influences, to determine priorities for safety management training needs and to explore target areas for strengthening the capacity of nursing home practitioners.DesignA mixed methods design was used.MethodsA qualitative‐driven mixed methods design was employed in two phases. In Phase 1, as a core component for integrating supplementary components, an inductive content analysis was performed to investigate perceptions of safety management and specific aspects of care related to it through semi‐structured interviews with nursing home practitioners. In Phase 2, a descriptive survey was conducted using items derived from the interviews conducted in the initial stage. The aim was to explore the educational needs of practitioners concerning safety management elements practised in nursing homes.ResultsIn the study, through qualitative interviews, practitioners' perceptions of interprofessional safety management were derived into 5 themes and 22 subthemes. Based on this, the results of a descriptive survey derived priorities through a paired t‐test, importance‐performance analysis, Borich's needs assessment model and the Locus for Focus model for safety management items performed by nursing home practitioners. Results from both phases of the study showed that practitioners recognise the importance of sharing the causes of safety accidents and that there is a high need for training on falls, dehydration, and hypoglycaemia management.Patient or Public ContributionNurses, care workers, physical therapists, and social workers employed in nursing homes took part in this study by participating in interviews and surveys.

Introduction

The global population of older individuals is on the rise, with projections indicating a significant increase in the number of people aged 60 or older. Estimates suggest that this demographic will grow from approximately 1.4 billion in 2030 to around 2.1 billion by 2050 (World Health Organization 2023). In recent years, the demand for nursing homes has increased significantly due to increasing life expectancy and the increasing prevalence of chronic diseases and disabilities among older people (Leutgeb et al. 2019). This surge in demand is due to the growing older population, many of whom are struggling to maintain independent living (De Meijer et al. 2013; Van Loon et al. 2023). Nursing homes play an important role by providing these individuals with necessary medical services and daily living support (Dale and Helton 2018).

Older individuals residing in nursing homes often experience a variety of physical and mental vulnerabilities, including physical disabilities, dependency, anxiety, depression, loneliness, and social problems; hence, they require comprehensive treatment (Luthfa and Pandin 2021). These vulnerabilities mean that older people are more likely to be exposed to various risks in their daily lives, which increases the likelihood of a variety of safety issues such as infections, falls, and medication errors (Brooke and Jackson 2020; Cappelli et al. 2020). Nursing home practitioners, including nurses, care workers, social workers, and physical therapists, deliver care that emphasises residents' physical, cognitive and social functions while addressing their complex health conditions (Lee, Park, and Chang 2024). These practitioners play a critical role in safeguarding resident safety, as they provide direct, face‐to‐face care and maintain the most frequent interactions with residents (Kim et al. 2022; Lee et al. 2020).

A variety of risk situations can occur in nursing homes, and all practitioners must know basic safety procedures and first aid methods (Choi, Kwon, and Chang 2021), but it is difficult to ensure consistent quality of care because practitioners in various occupations have different educational levels and backgrounds (Lee et al. 2020). Additionally, practitioners often find it difficult to recognise the roles or responsibilities of other professions and are unfamiliar with sharing specific knowledge, which can lead to poor quality of care for residents (Lovink et al. 2022). Therefore, nursing home practitioners must be equipped not only with specific knowledge but also with a cooperative attitude (Choi and Chang 2023; Choi et al. 2023). However, in a retrospective study to identify factors affecting the improvement of care in nursing homes, obstacles such as lack of competency, failure in teamwork and inadequate communication were identified in managing the safety of nursing home residents (Andersson et al. 2018).

Interprofessional education goes beyond simple knowledge transfer and plays an important role in increasing awareness of communication errors among practitioners (Gill et al. 2017). Additionally, interprofessional collaboration through core competencies such as professionalism, teamwork and interprofessional education can promote quality care and improve patient safety (Rayburn and Jenkins 2021). Research on interprofessional education has been more frequent in hospitals and clinics (Grimes and Guinan 2023; Schmidt et al. 2021), while reports in nursing home settings are relatively scarce. Although nursing home practitioners prioritise resident safety, addressing concerns such as maintaining comfort, managing dementia symptoms and controlling infections (Min and Yu 2023), research on the quality of care and patient safety in nursing homes remains limited overall (Wu and McIntyre 2021). Additionally, it has been reported that nursing home practitioners' knowledge about technical aspects of patient safety was high, but knowledge about sociocultural aspects such as cooperation was low (Dimitriadou et al. 2021). This demonstrates that research on the safety management education of nursing home interprofessional practitioners is needed.

The viewpoints of nursing home practitioners can provide insight regarding the most effective approaches for enhancing education in interprofessional safety management. Therefore, this study aimed to assess perceptions of safety management, particularly focusing on the impact of interprofessionalism, for determining the priorities for training requirements in safety management and exploring target areas for enhancing practitioners' capabilities in this field. The study employed a mixed methods approach that integrates quantitative and qualitative data to identify educational needs directly emerging from practice. This approach is expected to overcome the limitations inherent in single method studies, gain insights into multifaceted factors (Houben et al. 2022; Park et al. 2021) and devise the most effective strategies for bolstering safety management education.

Main Research Question

How do nursing home practitioners perceive resident safety management, and how do they apply safety management in their practice?

Supplementary Question

How do nursing home practitioners perceive the importance and performance level of safety management education, and what contents do they require?

Method

A qualitative‐driven mixed methods design (Morse 2016) was conducted in two phases (Figure 1). Morse (2016) said that mixed methods design consists of two components: a core component and a supplemental component. Phase 1, serving as the core component and theoretical foundation for integrating supplemental component, involved semi‐structured interviews with nursing home practitioners, including nurses, care workers, social workers and physical therapists. This phase focused on conducting an inductive content analysis to examine the participants' perceptions of safety management and the specific aspects of care associated with them. Phase 2 entailed conducting a descriptive survey utilising items derived from the Phase 1 interviews. The survey aimed to investigate the evaluation and educational requirements of practitioners regarding safety management components in nursing homes. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) developed by Tong, Sainsbury, and Craig (2007) was used to structure and organise this research report.

The mixed method design of this study.

Phase 1: Qualitative Study

In Phase 1, we focused on understanding how nursing home practitioners view and manage residents' safety through a qualitative approach. Qualitative research methods are valuable for deepening our understanding of why people behave as they do (Bryman 2012) and ‘are used to understand phenomena from the perspectives of those involved, to contextualize issues in their particular socio‐cultural‐political milieu, and sometimes to transform or change social conditions’ (Glesne 2016).

Participants and Data Collection

Using purposive sampling, 20 practitioners from four nursing homes in Seoul and Gyeonggi, Korea, were recruited. The study's data collection took place between August 22 and September 5, 2022. Due to concerns about COVID‐19 infections, the interviews were held online using Zoom. Each session lasted between 60 and 90 min. To ensure no parts of the interviews were overlooked and to enhance the accuracy of the data analysis, all sessions were recorded and subsequently transcribed. The interview questions included the following: We are interested in your experience of how safety management is generally carried out in nursing homes. Data Analysis

The analysis of the interview data regarding the content of safety management education for nursing home practitioners was carried out using inductive content analysis. This method, which aims to enhance knowledge and comprehension of a subject through the interpretation of interview data, involves a thorough review of the data, coding and the generation of codes (Lindgren, Lundman, and Graneheim 2020; Hsieh and Shannon 2005). This process led to the categorisation of meaningful content and the establishment of detailed categories. All researchers repeatedly read and coded the transcribed interviews to identify patterns. Any content that did not align with the established codes or was irrelevant to the research topic was removed. The final categorisation of content relevant to nursing home safety management education was determined after a careful review of the categories.

Trustworthiness

To enhance the validity of the analysis, all authors cross‐checked the coding and theme selection to ensure consistency. Additionally, we repeated participants' language by summarising their key points at the conclusion of each response to accurately capture their intent and perspectives throughout the discussion (Lincoln and Guba 1985).

Phase 2: Descriptive Survey

During Phase 2, a cross‐sectional design was employed to prioritise educational needs. This was done with the goal of establishing an educational programme aimed at enhancing interprofessional collaboration among nursing home practitioners.

Participants and Data Collection

In this phase, we engaged a diverse group of participants from four nursing homes in Seoul and Gyeonggi‐do, Korea, including nurses, care workers, social workers and physical therapists. The inclusion criteria set for nursing home practitioners were to have at least 1 year of experience and to be capable of articulately discussing safety management practices, as per Benner's (1982) guidelines. Data collection spanned from December 15, 2023, to January 10, 2024. The sample size was determined using G*Power version 3.1.9.4, selecting a paired t‐test with a significance level of 0.05, an effect size of 0.3 (Park and Song 2022) and a statistical power of 0.90. This analysis indicated a minimum requirement of 97 participants. Accounting for a potential 20% dropout rate, we distributed the survey to 120 participants, ultimately receiving 118 responses.

Instrument

The analysis tool consisted of 41 items designed to capture the safety management perceptions identified by nursing home practitioners. The items, consisting of 14 questions about interprofessional sharing and 27 questions to identify specific educational areas, were developed based on the insights gained from the interviews in Phase 1 of this study. To ensure accuracy and relevance, the items also underwent a content validity verification process. Ten experts—three nurses, three social workers, three physical therapists with over 10 years of nursing home experience, and one nursing professor—participated in evaluating whether the questions appropriately measured the intended content. Each item was assessed using a 4‐point Likert scale, where 1 denoted ‘not relevant’ and 4 denoted ‘highly relevant’. The content validity index was determined to be 0.94.

The survey consists of nine dimensions, including content, methods and reasons for sharing among practitioners, as well as areas like fall management, infection management, medication management, fluid‐electrolyte management and blood glucose management, for a total of 41 items. Each item was rated on a 5‐point Likert scale, from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high), assessing both importance and knowledge. Cronbach's α values were 0.917 for the importance and 0.962 for the performance of the questionnaire.

Data Analysis

In this study, we followed the processes of paired t‐test, importance‐performance analysis (IPA), Borich's needs assessment model and the Locus for Focus model (LF model) to determine the priorities of interprofessional education needs for safety management (Lee and Yang 2024).

To assess the gap between the perceived importance and actual performance of safety management education among nursing home practitioners, a paired t‐test was utilised. Next, the importance and performance perceptions regarding safety management items in nursing homes were analysed using means and standard deviations. The mean values of importance and performance perceptions were used to divide the matrices into four quadrants. In the IPA, the importance of educational elements is plotted on the vertical axis, while performance levels are plotted on the horizontal axis, creating a matrix from the average of importance and performance. Items falling into Quadrant 1 are regarded as strengths, indicating they are both important and performed well. Conversely, items in Quadrant 2 have high importance and low performance, so are recognised as items with high priority for improvement. Items in Quadrant 3 received less educational focus since they were seen as neither important nor performed well. Finally, items in Quadrant 4 were performed well but not considered very important.

Specifically, Borich's needs assessment model was performed to determine priorities for educational needs. The sum of the discrepancy scores between importance and performance for each item was calculated and a weight was assigned to importance, with higher scores indicating greater educational need. The Borich Needs Assessment formula is Needs=∑Importance−Performance×Importance¯N

However, since the Borich method only lists rankings according to demand values, the LF model was used to determine the relative priority of items. The LF model features two axes: the horizontal axis for importance and the vertical axis for the discrepancy between importance and performance. According to this model, items in Quadrant 1 (HH), where the gap between importance and performance is largest, are considered the most important areas for improvement (Lee and Jung 2021), while Quadrant 3 (LL) has the largest gap between importance and performance. Since the gap is below average, and importance is also below average, it is considered the lowest priority. The specific process for determining priorities followed the steps described in Table 1.

Nursing home practitioners' perceptions and implementation of safety management.

The participants consisted of five nurses, five care workers, four physical therapists and six social workers. The participants had an average age of 46.2 years. Among them, 4 were high school graduates and 16 were college graduates. Their clinical experience varied from 3 to 28 years, with an average of 13.9 years.

Theme 1: What Information to Share for Managing Safety?

Nursing home practitioners provide comprehensive care that includes prevention and intervention of safety incidents throughout the safety management process. Participants emphasised the importance of active communication and collaboration between practitioners regarding residents' individual characteristics and conditions. Among them, Theme 1 focuses on five sub‐themes on what information to share in the interprofessional area for safety management: identifying potential risk factors, understanding residents through underlying diseases, predicting abnormal changes, priority actions for practitioners and physical conditions causing abnormal symptoms. Participants paid attention to potential risk factors and emphasised identifying abnormal changes in residents and sharing them with practitioners.

Theme 2: How to Share the Information for Managing Safety?

Theme 2 focused on how information should be shared between practitioners in the area of safety management. Theme 2 consists of four sub‐themes: practitioner feedback post‐safety incident, developing a centralised collaboration and reporting system, monitoring risk factors and setting up regular observation routines for vulnerable residents.

Participants focused on sharing safety‐related information through daily observation of residents, as well as establishing feedback and reporting systems among practitioners. They emphasised securing the safety of residents and preventing secondary incidents through systematic management and emphasised ensuring that residents' conditions could be shared quickly and accurately.

Theme 3: Why Share Information for Managing Safety?

Theme 3 mentioned the reasons for the importance of sharing among practitioners in safety management. Participants emphasised the impact of sharing between practitioners on ensuring the safety of residents and on confirming that care was consistently provided in the process of preventing and intervening in safety incidents. Theme 3 had four sub‐themes: risk factor communication for collaboration, efficient risk situation response plans, knowledge sharing among practitioners for consistent care and importance of role division in risk situations.

Theme 4: Knowledge Mechanisms and Patterns

Theme 4 concerns the mechanisms and patterns that occur in safety management in nursing homes. Theme 4 consists of seven sub‐themes: dehydration and oedema mechanisms, identifying lifestyle patterns for detecting changes, linking underlying diseases with abnormal behaviours, physical changes from ageing, normalising daily life patterns, addressing physical vulnerabilities and bodily passageway management mechanisms.

Participants mentioned the types of safety incidents that can occur in nursing homes and how the safety management process occurs. They outlined preventive measures for safety incidents such as falls, dehydration, intestinal and airway obstructions and detailed response strategies. Additionally, the specific responsibilities of each practitioner in maintaining safety were clarified.

Theme 5: Knowledge Requirements

Theme 5 is in regard to areas that require education in the practical area of safety management. Theme 5 consists of two sub‐themes: safety incident response guidelines and optimal treatment methods in nursing homes. Participants emphasised the need for safety management education for practitioners and an accurate understanding of safety management methods while also speaking about the lack of confidence and anxiety that can result from an insufficiency of such knowledge. Additionally, they noted that although all practitioners must play a variety of roles in managing resident safety, there is a risk of inconsistent care due to differences in experience or knowledge levels. Consequently, all practitioners must be aware of the methods for preventing and intervening in safety incidents.

Results Narrative: Nursing Home Practitioners' Perceptions of Safety Management

This study was conducted using a mixed methods design consisting of Phases 1 and 2 to determine practitioners' perceptions of safety management education in nursing homes, identify the current level of performance and perceived importance and determine educational priorities. As a result of the study, practitioners in nursing homes recognised the importance of interprofessional collaboration and the need for education concerning it. Interprofessional education for safety management includes various methods to identify the individual characteristics of residents and to prevent and take action against safety incidents.

The highest priority content in interprofessional education for safety management was sharing the causes of safety incidents among practitioners. Practitioners emphasised that safety incidents can occur for a variety of reasons, and they therefore focused on minimising injuries to residents and preventing recurrences by closely sharing the causes of safety incidents to prevent individual caregivers from making hasty judgements.

Practitioners recognised the management of frequent or severe incidents such as falls, dehydration and hypoglycaemia as crucial to safety management in nursing homes. They aimed to ensure resident safety primarily through prevention activities. However, their awareness was mostly limited to assessing symptoms like ‘gait function and balance’ and ‘airway obstruction’. A significant disparity was noted between the achieved performance level and the perceived importance of these tasks, highlighting them as top priorities.

The participants suggested that despite being organised into different teams, all practitioners must collaborate closely to enhance residents' safety and care levels. However, the lack of detailed knowledge about each other's roles and the differences in professional backgrounds were noted as challenges. Additionally, the importance of sharing information on residents' characteristics and potential risk factors for effective prevention, clear role delineation, post‐incident feedback and establishing regular observation routines was underscored. Overall, the perceived performance levels of safety management tasks were lower than their importance, with nursing home practitioners showing a willingness to participate in safety management training, suggesting the need for strengthened practical training for practitioners.

Discussion

Safety incidents that occur in nursing homes occur in a variety of areas that can worsen residents' health outcomes, such as falls and medication errors. Residents of nursing homes have various chronic diseases and reduced physical function (Lee, Park, and Chang 2021; Buckinx et al. 2017), and, accordingly, study participants recognised that safety incidents can occur for a variety of reasons and that there is a need to look at safety management from multiple perspectives. From the perspective of nursing home practitioners, for improving the safety of residents, measures are taken to minimise errors by not only strengthening individual practitioners' capabilities, for example, improving their knowledge (Andersson et al. 2018), but also by considering organisational steps such as implementing a reporting system for providing a system‐wide perspective on safety issues (Bengtsson, Ekedahl, and Sjöström 2021).

The efficacy of education relies heavily on accurately assessing educational requirements (Ziwei et al. 2023). Therefore, this study combines the use of a paired t‐test, IPA, Borich's needs assessment model and the LF model to pinpoint key areas requiring enhancement. This approach contributes to the development of an educational programme that is reflective of current practice. The results of this study showed that the participants had high educational needs in managing falls, hypoglycaemia and dehydration. These are areas of safety incidents that frequently occur or are of high severity when they do occur in nursing homes (Choi, Kwon, and Chang 2021). Maintaining residents' physical comfort through consideration of individual physical characteristics is important for their safety management (Min and Yu 2023). Especially focusing on the top 1, 2 and 3 educational needs, practitioners expressed a high demand for blood sugar management. Residents with diabetes in nursing homes require an individualised approach because they are at high risk of complications, mainly due to iatrogenic hypoglycaemia and multiple comorbidities (McNabney et al. 2005), but it has been reported that optimal guidelines for preventing hypoglycaemia and managing blood sugar are lacking (Lega and Rochon 2022). Therefore, the results of this study suggest the need to develop an optimised training programme to strengthen the safety management capabilities of practitioners.

Accurately identifying the cause of a safety incident and sharing it among practitioners is important to prevent recurrence of incidents and improve safety. Identifying causes and sharing them among practitioners can provide insight into the basic factors that contribute to resident safety incidents as well as opportunities to promote learning from incidents and translate them into improvements (Ropero‐Padilla et al. 2022). Additionally, it provides a positive safety culture environment by promoting a shared understanding among practitioners and creating a proactive culture of incident reporting (Lindblad et al. 2020).

Despite the importance of understanding safety management from multiple perspectives, practitioners reported a lack of their own safety management capabilities. This means that providing optimal safety management that considers the complex needs and health status of residents must go beyond simple knowledge improvement to provide guidelines for cooperation between practitioners and safety management at the institutional level. Interprofessional education is a learning process that promotes collaborative care and safety by resolving issues of fragmentation of health service provision and separation between professionals (Olenick, Allen, and Smego 2010). Furthermore, it increases awareness of the capabilities of one's own occupation and supports gaining insight into the capabilities of other occupations (Haugland, Brenna, and Aanes 2019).

The results of this study show nursing home practitioners' educational needs and the necessity of strengthening interprofessional collaboration on falls, hypoglycaemia, hyperglycaemia, dehydration, airway obstructions and drug management, which can pose difficulties to nursing home practitioners in relation to safety management. To ensure the safety and improve the quality of life of nursing home residents, education based on safety management awareness and needs must be continuously provided, and related guidelines must be continuously followed. Additionally, practitioners at nursing homes must continuously review, update and implement policies and procedures related to safety incident prevention.

Limitations

This study was conducted to explore the content of interprofessional safety management education. Although a sample size for the survey was constructed and conducted in four nursing homes to encompass the diversity of nursing home settings, it may not fully represent such diversity.

Conclusion

This study investigated nursing home practitioners' perceptions of safety management and their priorities for interprofessional educational needs, offering valuable insights to guide improvements in safety management education. In conclusion, we recommend implementing a collaborative, interprofessional education approach within nursing home settings that reflects the specific needs of practitioners. This approach can better equip practitioners to address safety issues efficiently and effectively, thereby enhancing resident safety.
