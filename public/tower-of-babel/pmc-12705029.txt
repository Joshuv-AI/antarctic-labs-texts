Strengthening leprosy control through capacity building of allied health workers: A pre–post design
Authors: Reenaa Mohan, J Jenifer Florance Mary, Roghan Balaji, M Reveda, Pooja Krishnan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:The main hurdles to achieving early diagnosis and management of leprosy and its complications are lack of community awareness and the challenging social stigma. Healthcare workers play a key role in increasing community awareness and eliminating stigma about leprosy, and it is one of the important responsibilities. The objective of the study is to assess the knowledge of Allied Health Sciences (AHS) students about leprosy before and after the training program and to explore the stigma prevailing in the society about leprosy.Materials and Methods:It was a pre- and post-test mixed-method study involving both quantitative and qualitative components. Group discussion was conducted for 74 AHS students (lab technicians and nursing students). A paired t-test was used for pre and post-test analysis. Manual content analysis was done for the statements that emerged during the group discussion.Results:The mean change in overall score was 0.228, which was significant (t = 7.54, P < 0.01). There was an improvement in the knowledge score after training. The Pearson correlation coefficient between pre-test knowledge score and post-test scores was significant (r = 0.318, P = 0.04). The majority of them stated that leprosy patients will be isolated by the community as stigma remains a main challenge.Conclusion:Implementation of need-based health education and sensitization programs should be improved, and more emphasis needs to be placed on early case identification of leprosy.

Introduction

Leprosy, also known as Hansen’s disease, is one of the oldest known chronic infectious diseases.[1] Despite the decline in its prevalence, it remains a public health concern due to its associated morbidity and the deep-rooted social stigma.[2] In India, leprosy still carries significant social discrimination, often leading patients to be ostracized, which contributes to delays in seeking timely treatment.[3]

The National Leprosy Control Programme (NLCP) was launched in 1955 and later renamed as the National Leprosy Eradication Programme (NLEP) in 1983.[2] India achieved the elimination target of less than one case per 10,000 population in 2005. However, due to the disease’s long incubation period and persistent stigma, new cases continue to emerge, and achieving zero stigma remains a challenge.[34]

To address these issues, the World Health Organization (WHO) released a technical guide in 2020 for the early diagnosis, management of leprosy reactions, and prevention of disability.[5] This aligns with the global leprosy strategy (2021–2030) aiming for zero transmission, zero disability, and zero stigma.[6] Similarly, the NLEP’s operational guidelines (2020) emphasize capacity building of healthcare workers, recognizing that no health program can succeed without adequately trained staff.[47]

The main hurdles to achieving early diagnosis and management of leprosy and its complications are a lack of community awareness and the challenging social stigma. Healthcare workers play a key role in increasing community awareness and eliminating stigma about leprosy, and it is one of their important responsibilities. Keeping this in mind, as a part of this year’s leprosy day celebration under the theme “United for Dignity,” we planned to assess the knowledge level of allied healthcare workers about leprosy and train them based on NLEP guidelines. With this background, this study was planned with the following objectives to find out the knowledge about leprosy among the allied healthcare students. To assess the knowledge of Allied Health Sciences (AHS) students about leprosy before and after the training program and to explore the stigma prevailing in the society about leprosy.

Materials and Methods

The study was conducted in the two paramedical training institutes located in the field practice area of the Urban Health Training Centre (UHTC) under the Department of Community Medicine in a tertiary care teaching hospital in Puducherry, India. It was a pre- and post-test mixed-method study involving both quantitative and qualitative components [Figure 1]. A total of 74 AHS students (lab technicians and nursing students) who participated in the training program conducted at UHTC were included in the study. A purposive sampling technique was used to include all the students for the study.

Flow of the study

Training and data collection procedure

After obtaining institutional ethical clearance and consent from participants, pre-test data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire. The self-administered questionnaire included the demographic details and knowledge about leprosy containing both open and close-ended questions. A group discussion was done on leprosy and its associated stigma followed by educational training based on NLEP guidelines through group activity. A trained community medicine postgraduate conducted the educational training program. At the final stage, a post-test was conducted using the same questionnaire. The structure of the program is as follows:

Step 1: Baseline knowledge assessment using a pre-test questionnaire.

Step 2: Group activity which includes group discussion about leprosy and education training based on NLEP guidelines.

Step 3: End-line knowledge assessment using a post-test questionnaire to assess the knowledge improvement.

Group activity

Participants were divided into seven groups with 8–11 participants in each group. Each group was given a separate topic related to leprosy, which included causes, common symptoms, high-risk groups, mode of transmission, stigmatization of leprosy, handling the leprosy patients, and management of leprosy. The participants were given time for discussion and to jot down the points in charts using a sketch pen either in Tamil or English whichever language they were comfortable with, followed by a discussion. A representative from each group came forward and was asked to justify the points under their topic to the audience which was moderated by the Principal Investigator (PI). Training

Following the presentations from group members, a comprehensive, focused, need-based training was given by PI to the students, which emphasized filling the knowledge gap of the participants with the help of a flipbook, which included/covered more of a pictorial representation of the disease with topics like common symptoms, high-risk groups, mode of transmission, clinical and physical presentation, management, and the prevailing stigma. Other materials including audio-visual aids, charts, posters, and pamphlets were used to enrich their learning experience, and interactive sessions aided in the participatory learning of the students. Pamphlets with all the key messages on leprosy were also distributed to the students at the end of the session. Queries and questions were cleared at the end of the session by the PI.

Data analysis

The collected data were entered in Epi_Info (ver. 7.2.2.6; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, GA, USA, and WHO) software and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (ver. 24.0; IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA). Both the pre- and post-test data were compared to find the effect of the educational training. Paired t-test was used to test the statistical significance. If the P value is < 0.05, then the pre- and post-test differences were considered statistically significant. Manual content analysis was done for the qualitative components by analyzing the frequency of the items.

Results

Socio-demographic details of the participants

Among 74 AHS students, the mean age was 20 ± 2.5 (SD) years. All students agreed to participate in the study with a response rate of 100%. Among them, 60.8% were nursing students and 39.2% were laboratory technicians.

Knowledge about leprosy among the study participants

To assess the effectiveness of the educational training program among nursing students, knowledge was assessed using a pre-test followed by training and post-test [Table 2]. There was a 27.8% improvement in the score, which was significant (t = 7.54, P < 0.01). There was an improvement in the knowledge score after training. The Pearson correlation coefficient between pre-test knowledge score and post-test score was significant (r = 0.318, P = 0.04).

Discussion

It was found moderate level of knowledge about leprosy existed among the allied health care students which improved after the training program when compared to the baseline (27.8% improvement in score P value ≤ 0.01).

In our study, the level of awareness of the source of infection, its mode of transmission, and features of leprosy among the students were minimal. Similarly, the awareness about most people being immune to it was low among the participants. In contrast, a study done by Giri et al.[8] shows that students were aware of the mode and source of transmission. Our study result was comparable with the study done in India among the students in healthcare professionals in various cadres.[89101112] A study done by Mponda et al.[13] and Graciano-Machuca et al.[14] also found that there was a lack of knowledge about leprosy among the healthcare workers and students, which was similar to our study findings. Rajkumar et al.[15] conducted a study among nursing students toward leprosy and also showed that students who were trained in schools or colleges tend to have more knowledge on it than other students with suitable environment. These variations might contribute to the diversity in the level of knowledge among healthcare workers. Thus, the findings in our study could be attributed to the fact that there is less awareness among the students since this is still considered to be a hidden disease and not very commonly prevalent in the community.

As regards the treatment, the majority of the students were unaware of the fact that leprosy can be completely cured if proper case detection and management were given, yet it has been noted that they were aware of the availability of multidrug therapy (MDT) at government hospitals or government-accredited hospitals at free of cost. An identical research on medical students showed similar deficiencies in treatment knowledge.[816] Similarly, in an educational intervention study conducted by Niranjan et al.,[17] 40% of respondents reported that leprosy could be treated with MDT, which later increased to 80% after the intervention. Thus, imparting knowledge through education updates the prevailing knowledge and increases the awareness about leprosy.[18]

Added to that, the misconception toward leprosy elicited from the study before educational intervention showed that a need-based training to focus on the important aspects and cardinal features, and public health aspects needs to be emphasized. Also, after training the awareness level improved among the students (increased by 27.8%). Likewise, studies[1519] observed that there could be a definite improvement in knowledge and awareness after intensive training about leprosy, which supports our study findings. As recommended, necessary training and positive attitudes toward leprosy might increase the knowledge and changes can be observed as well.[81419]

Public health-related knowledge was found to be minimal among our study participants. This study showed that most of the students were also not aware of the NLEP, for which participants have been enlightened about recent advances in NLEP and explained the role of ASHA workers in the NLEP. This was supported by the study done by Jadhao et al.,[16] where 93.8% of students lacked knowledge regarding the NLEP, while in the study by Jain et al.,[9] 70.3% did not know about the existence of the NLEP. Similarly, a study done outside India showed that including national programs in the curriculum with stringent training through appropriate teaching and learning methods might derive the optimal benefit from the program.[20] Overall, education about leprosy and NLEP is considered highly among other priority interventions against the disease eradication.

On analysis of questions related to the assessment of the attitude of participants toward leprosy-affected patients, it was noted that the majority of the study participants had a negative attitude toward leprosy-affected patients. In this study, community isolation of leprosy patients was noted, they were treated as unwanted, and observed avoidance of homes with leprosy patients. Similar to our study, Jain et al.[9] showed that 86.5% of participants had never donated to leprosy patients, implying that an indicator for increasing social neglect of PWL could be due to neglect of public health aspects of the disease. Various studies among healthcare professionals concluded that after understanding the disease they exhibited sympathy and helpful attitude toward them.[1221] The personal attitude in integrating leprosy patients into their family, home, and community shows a negative attitude. Such attitude prevailing at the community level perceived by the students to which they belong could lead to the fact that social stigmas associated with leprosy play a major role.

Stigma, an ostracizing word, had many faces and was defined as “spoiled identity,”[22] which was reflected in our study’s reluctance to employ leprosy patients and beliefs linking leprosy to past sins or shame. These results were supported by the research done or literature published across the world.[142324] Stigma in leprosy is considered an enacted stigma that restricts the participation of the affected individuals, which later affects the quality of life.[25262728] However, there is a significant change in the attitude of the study participants toward these issues in the post-test evaluation. These changes can be attributed to the fact that there is a significant improvement in the knowledge of the study participants, and this has led to a change in attitude toward leprosy-affected patients. Thus, education about the disease would dissipate the unwanted stigma and might ensure a positive attitude toward the sufferers.[29] The target audiences must be emphasized on their role in the community and specific training to be given to create community awareness. In the end, stigmatization toward leprosy can be eradicated by the active participation of the target audience in the community to achieve “zero leprosy stigma and leprosy free status for the people in India.”

The major component of the NLEP program emphasizes training all healthcare workers in effective implementation of the framework and they are responsible for counseling the patients on leprosy and for creating awareness among the community which would pave the way toward the eradication of the disease. Also to effectively impart knowledge to eradicate the social stigma about leprosy.[27] Well-designed validated context-specific media and tools such as flipbooks and pamphlets help to address the issue. These tools are useful in improving the overall knowledge of patients and their families about leprosy and the steps taken to avoid discriminating against leprosy patients.[1216] It also helps paramedical professionals to counsel the patients regarding strict adherence to the MDT. Thus, it is important to amplify the awareness about leprosy and its social stigma among the target population.

This study has used interactive group activities to capture their emic perspective and to build confidence in them. Self-administered questionnaires were used, which might have resulted in recall and social desirability bias. Since the study was conducted in a short duration, with a small sample size, generalizability might not be possible.

Conclusion

Implementation of need-based health education and sensitization programs should be improved, and more emphasis needs to be placed on early case identification of leprosy. Prevailing consequences of stigma like discrimination, social exclusion, and isolation leading to a decreased quality of life and social status must be addressed. Social outcasts of leprosy patients can be avoided by bringing a change in the community by creating awareness. Advocacy, social, and livelihood support are needed. Further studies to identify the contrast between rural and urban and the effectiveness of IEC activities in improving community knowledge are recommended.
