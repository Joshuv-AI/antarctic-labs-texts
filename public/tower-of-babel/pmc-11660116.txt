Bioactive Properties of Phosphate-Modified Silicon Oxycarbide Protective Coatings: Morphology and Functional Evaluation
Authors: Magdalena Gawęda, Jakub Marchewka, Piotr Jeleń, Maciej Bik, Maciej Sowa, Wojciech Simka, Barbara Zagrajczuk, Zofia Kucia, Patryk Zając, Maciej Sitarz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents a study on the functional properties and morphology of coatings based on amorphous silicon oxycarbide modified with phosphate ions and comodified with aluminum and boron. The objective of this modification was to enhance the biocompatibility and bioactivity without affecting its protective properties. The comodification was aimed toward stabilization of phosphate in the structure. The coatings were prepared according to the typical procedure for polymer-derived ceramics: synthesized via the sol–gel method, deposited using the dip-coating technique, and subsequently pyrolyzed. Comprehensive analyses of the morphology, surface properties, corrosion resistance, and bioactivity were conducted to assess their functional performance. The coatings exhibited uniform and smooth surfaces, with phase separation observed in the boron-modified SiBPOC series. Surface wettability and free energy measurements demonstrated that SiPOC and SiBPOC coatings possessed moderate hydrophilicity and favorable surface free energy for cell adhesion and bone tissue mineralization. Corrosion resistance tests in Ringer’s solution revealed that SiBPOC coatings provided the highest protection against ion leaching, while SiAlPOC showed decreased resistance due to surface cracks. Bioactivity tests indicated calcium phosphate precipitation on the surface of all samples with higher hydroxyapatite formation on SiPOC and SiAlPOC coatings. In vitro tests using MG-63 osteoblast-like cells confirmed the biocompatibility of the coatings, with SiPOC and SiBPOC exhibiting the best combination of bioactivity, cell adhesion, and proliferation. These findings suggest that the phosphate- and boron-modified SiOC-based coatings are promising candidates for enhancing bone integration in orthopedic implants.Keywords: silicon oxycarbide, corrosion, biomaterials, amorphous coatings, polymer-derived ceramics

Introduction

The unique combination of mechanical properties, corrosion, and oxidation resistance, as well as thermal and chemical stability in silicon oxycarbide (SiOC)-based materials, including both amorphous and glass-crystalline forms, makes them a compelling and multifaceted option for medical applications. Elastic preparation techniques enable the production of SiOC in diverse forms, such as bulk, aerogel, coating, powder, nanoparticles, and fibers, with variable compositions, including structural carbon content, free carbon phase, and the presence of other ions. Through such manipulations, materials featuring improved or altogether novel properties may be developed.

SiOC exhibits exceptional resistance to harsh environmental conditions, including elevated temperatures, ion-rich environments, and abrasion. Additionally, SiOC can be synthesized through various methods, such as coating preparation and additive manufacturing. Due to these properties, SiOC shows great potential in tissue engineering, as it can endure conditions far more challenging than those found within the human body and can be fabricated into diverse physical forms. Its appeal is further supported by the growing number of recent publications exploring this field of study. According to the well-known William’s definition, a biomaterial is a substance designed to interact with the living body, tissue, or cells in a controlled manner and may be utilized as an independent element or part of a more complex system in therapeutic or diagnostic procedures in medicine and veterinary science.1 Biocompatibility refers to the material’s ability to exhibit specific functional properties while eliciting an acceptable host response.2 The more specialized term, cytocompatibility, is used to describe the neutral (or positive) interaction of the material with cells.3 Studies conducted on SiOC-based materials have demonstrated a lack of cytotoxicity, with only a slight reduction in cell viability compared to tissue culture polystyrene (TCPS) in some cases, which could be attributed to the nonpreferential surface topography.4−9 With regard to biomaterial properties, it is possible to take a step further and design bioactive materials that can facilitate tissue regeneration and foster a permanent bond with damaged tissue.10,11 Prior research, including our own,8,12,13 has clearly shown that SiOC-based coatings deposited on the titanium substrate are bioactive, with satisfactory mechanical and surface properties, and capable of inducing the precipitation of carbonate hydroxyapatite on the surface. Modifications with magnesium, boron, and calcium have been proposed to enhance cell proliferation, bone tissue mineralization, and tissue–material integration.3,14−16 However, manipulating the material’s composition to increase the free carbon phase content may result in an inert surface, making it suitable for contact with blood as an antithrombotic material.17

The objective of this study was to evaluate the fundamental functional properties of amorphous coatings based on SiOC modified with phosphate ions, as well as comodified with aluminum and boron.18 This evaluation included the examination of material’s morphology, wettability, surface free energy (SFE), corrosion resistance, bioactivity, and biocompatibility. The purpose of the modification was to enhance these properties. Effective protective coatings must be chemically stable and free from surface imperfections that could lead to corrosion, as their primary function is to prevent the release of substrate components into body fluids and avoid contact with corrosion products. The release of such compounds into the body can cause serious adverse effects, including metallosis, allergies, and ulcerations. A crucial factor in maintaining this protection is a strong bond with the substrate, such as a transition layer that ensures permanent adhesion.13 Additionally, the coating’s functional properties—bioactivity and biocompatibility—are linked to its composition, which influences key surface characteristics like wettability and SFE.19,20 Moderate hydrophilicity and relatively high SFE are essential for promoting protein adsorption, cell adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, and proper bone tissue mineralization.21−23 The selection of phosphate ions as a modification agent was a natural choice since they are a basic component of bioglass.11 The modification is aimed to increase biocompatibility and stimulate key proteins that regulate bone formation processes.24 However, the utilization of phosphate ions can be challenging due to the volatility of their precursors, necessitating supportive modification with aluminum and boron ions. This process facilitated the formation of [AlPO4] and [BPO4] structure units, which were easily introduced and permanently bonded into the SiOC network.25,26 The addition of boron ions may have auxiliary beneficial effects on the material’s properties as it regulates the ribonucleic acids of fibroblasts and promotes bone formation.27−29 Conversely, the presence of aluminum ions may reduce bioactivity.30−32

Methods and Materials

Samples’ Preparation

SiPOC, SiAlPOC, and SiBPOC amorphous coatings were prepared following the polymer-derived ceramics (PDC) concept. The detailed methodology was described in the previous study,18 which also included the justification for the selection of phosphate precursors. The sol–gel method was employed to prepare ladder-like polysilsesquioxanes (with varying molecular masses) as the preceramic polymer, which were modified with phosphate ions (series A), phosphate and aluminum ions (series C), and phosphate and boron ions (series E). (2-Diethylphosphatoethyl)triethoxysilane was used as the primary modifier and phosphate precursor. Aluminum and boron ions were introduced with aluminum tri-sec-butoxide and trimethyl borate, respectively. The concentrations of phosphate ions varied within the series, as shown in Table 1. As stated in the previous work,18 series B, D, and F were synthesized using triethyl phosphite, which has been shown to exhibit low effectiveness in incorporating phosphate into the SiOC matrix.

AISI 316L stainless steel plates, 1 mm thick and of varying dimensions depending on the type of examination (20 mm × 20 mm for electrochemical testing, a diameter of 10 mm for biocompatibility assessment, and 10 mm × 20 mm for other tests), were ground with sandpaper up to a gradation of 800 and then degreased in acetone and ethanol ultrasonic baths. The prepared preceramic polymer sols were deposited on the prepared plates by using a dip-coating technique. The samples were immersed in the sol for 60 s and withdrawn at a speed of 20 cm/min. The procedure was repeated three times for every sample with intervals of at least 20 min between repetitions to allow the layer to pre-dry. Further treatment of the samples was similar to xerogels:18 low-temperature drying up to 150 °C, followed by high-temperature pyrolysis (ceramization) at 800 °C in the protective argon atmosphere for 30 min, with a heating rate of 5 °C/min and free cooling.

Morphology Analysis

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging was conducted using Nova NanoSEM 200 (FEI Europe Company, Netherlands) and Phenom XL (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) microscopes, both equipped with an X-ray energy-dispersive module (EDS). The analyzed layers were not gold-sputtered prior to the measurements. Both secondary electron and backscattered electron detectors were used, with an accelerating voltage between 5 and 15 kV. To prepare cross-sections for determining the thickness and properties of the SiOC/metal interface, samples were hot-mounted in carbon-based conductive resin and polished using diamond paste up to a grain size of 1 μm, following the standard Struers procedure.

Contact angle (CA) measurements were performed using a DSA25E goniometer (Krüss, Germany) with water and diiodomethane as the polar and dispersive liquids, respectively. SFE was estimated using the Owens–Wendt–Rabel–Kaelble method based on 10 measurements per liquid using dedicated Advance software.

Corrosion Tests

Corrosion tests were performed on all types of SiOC-based coatings on AISI 316L steel substrates with uncoated steel used as the reference. A PARSTAT4000A potentiostat–galvanostat (Ametek, Princeton Applied Research, USA), controlled by dedicated Versa Studio version 2.60.6 software, was employed for the examination. A saturated calomel electrode (SCE) served as the reference electrode, while a platinum–titanium plate was used as the auxiliary electrode. The samples were exposed to a corrosive environment (Ringer’s solution with the composition: 8.6 g/dm3 NaCl, 0.3 g/dm3 KCl, and 0.48 g/dm3 CaCl2·6H2O; Fresenius Kabi, Poland) at 37 °C (the solution was preheated before measurements). The contact surface of the sample with the corrosive medium was delimited by pressing it against an O-ring seal with a diameter of 6 mm, resulting in a contact area with the solution of 0.283 cm2. Measurements were conducted in triplicate for each type of sample.

The experimental procedure consisted of (1) recording the open-circuit potential (EOC) for 3600 s; (2) electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements at EOC within the frequency range from 39.81 kHz to 10 mHz with an amplitude of 10 mV (for the steel reference sample) or 20 mV (for the coated samples), with 5 points per decade of frequency; and (3) potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) curves starting at −0.3 V vs EOC up to a current density (i) of 500 μA/cm2. The polarity direction was then reversed until the final potential reached −0.25 V versus the peak potential (at the time of polarity reversal). The potential scanning speed was set to 10 mV/min.

ZSimpWin version 3.60 software was used to fit the EIS results. The PDP curves were analyzed to determine the corrosion potential (Ecor) and the breakdown potential of the layer (Eb). The latter was determined near the point, where the current density (i) sharply increased above 50 μA/cm2. A tangent line was determined for the points measured between 50 and 150 μA/cm2. The breakdown potential was considered as the point of intersection of the tangent with the line for which i is 0. Due to the exclusion of some of the measurement results, the reported data were averaged from two measurements without specifying the standard deviation.

To assess the chemical stability and permeability of the coatings, the concentrations of ions released in simulated body fluids (SBFs), as described in Section 2.4, and deionized water were measured after 7 days using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer, specifically the Plasma 40 (PerkinElmer, USA).

Bioactivity and Biocompatibility Examination

The Kokubo test was conducted to evaluate the bioactivity of the coatings. A corrected simulated body fluid (c-SBF; Na+ 142.0 mM, K+ 5.0 mM, Mg2+ 1.5, Ca2+ 2.5 mM, Cl– 147.8 mM, HCO3– 4.2 mM, HPO42– 1.0 mM, SO42– 0.5 mM, pH = 7.40) was prepared in accordance with the composition outlined in ref (33). The materials were immersed vertically in sterile vessels containing c-SBF and maintained at a temperature of 36.6 °C for 28 days. Following the incubation period, the samples were analyzed using SEM and EDS to observe the presence of calcium phosphate precipitates exhibiting a characteristic cauliflower-like morphology of carbonate hydroxyapatite.34 The presence of these precipitates served as a strong indicator of the material’s ability to stimulate the regeneration of bone tissue.30,35−37

The samples prepared for biocompatibility tests were sterilized by using a SUN 12L autoclave (SUN Medical Instrument, China) at 121 °C in special sleeves containing 10 samples each. Sterile samples were subsequently placed in the wells of a 48-well TCPS plate (Nunc, Denmark). Prior to the addition of cell suspension to the materials, the plates underwent a second stage of sterilization: a 30 min exposure to 254 nm (UV-C) radiation. Human osteoblast-like cells MG-63 (ATCC, USA) were used in this study. The culture medium consisted of Eagle’s minimal essential medium (ATCC, USA) supplemented with 10 vol % Gibco fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cells were incubated in a HeraCell 150i incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were passaged at 70% confluence, and cells from passage 4 were used. To obtain a cell suspension, the surface of the culture bottle was washed with HyClone phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Cytiva, USA) after removing the medium, and the cells were separated from the bottom of the bottle with Gibco TrypLe Select solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). A culture medium that neutralizes the digestion action by Gibco TrypLe Select was then added, and the cells were centrifuged. Cells were counted in a Bürker chamber, and after centrifugation, the supernatant was removed. The medium was added in an amount necessary to obtain the required cell suspension density of 10,000 cells/ml. One milliliter of cell suspension was added to each well, resulting in a final density of 10,000 cells/well in the culture plate. Cultures were incubated in a Hera Cell 150i incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 3 and 7 days.

To observe the cell morphology, a CX41 optical microscope (Olympus) equipped with a fluorescent attachment was utilized. Before observation, the cells were stained with acridine orange at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. To each culture well was added 15 μL of dye just before observation. After 1 min, the samples were washed with a PBS solution and observed.

Viability PrestoBlue tests (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were performed after 3 and 7 days of culture. In each well of the 48-well plate, the culture medium was replaced with 500 μL of Gibco HBSS buffer (Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Then, 55.5 μL of PrestoBlue Cell Viability Reagent was added to each well, and the palate was incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The supernatants collected from the plate were transferred to a black 96-well OptiPlate microplate, and the fluorescence intensity was measured at 560/590 nm using a POLARstar Omega reader (BMG Labtech, Germany). The results were presented as average values with the standard deviation.

A cytotoxicity test was performed on 3 and 7 days of cultivation period using the Lonza ToxiLight assay (Lonza, USA). A 10 μL aliquot of supernatant was collected from each well of the culture plate and transferred to the protein-coated wells of the 96-well OptiPlate microplate. 50 μL of AK Detection Reagent (Lonza, USA) was added to each well of the white plate and incubated for 5 min in the dark with thorough mixing of the plate contents. The luminescence value was then measured by using a POLARstar Omega reader (BMG Labtech, Germany) to determine the number of dead cells. To determine the total number of cells, a complementary ToxiLight 100% Lysis Reagent Set assay (Lonza, USA) was performed, which enabled measurement of the total adenylate kinase level in the supernatant, providing information about both live and dead cells. Most supernatants were collected from the culture plate wells, leaving only 200 μL. Subsequently, 100 μL of 100% Cell Lysis Reagent (Lonza, USA) was added to all wells, and the plate was left for 10 min. After this time of the incubation period, 10 μL of the lysates was collected and transferred to a white 96-well OptiPlate microplate. 50 μL of AK Detection Reagent (Lonza, USA) was added to each well of the white plate and incubated in the dark for 5 min, and the luminescence value was measured.

Results and Discussion

Morphology of SiPOC, SiAlPOC, and SiBPOC Coatings’ Surfaces

The morphology of the coatings obtained is presented in Figures 1–3, showing the images of SiPOC (A series; Figure 1), SiAlPOC (C series; Figure 2), and SiBPOC (E series; Figure 3), both before (upper row) and after ceramization (bottom row). The as-deposited coatings exhibit a texture that replicates the surface of the steel substrate, including grooves from the polishing pretreatment. This texture is uniform for all of the samples and suggests that the coatings are relatively thin.

After undergoing high-temperature treatment, the surface morphology of the coatings exhibited significant differentiation among the specific series. In the case of SiPOC with lower concentrations of phosphate ions (samples A.1 and A.2; Figure 1), the surfaces retained their morphology from the polymer state. Conversely, the sample with higher dopant content (sample A.3; Figure 1) appeared smoother but exhibited visible cracks. This surface morphology resembled that of the SiAlPOC coatings (series C; Figure 2). Additionally, the SiBPOC coatings (series E; Figure 3) displayed a rough surface with multiple grooves similar to that of SiPOC, but phase separation was notably observed on the edges, particularly on sample E.2 (Figure 3). SEM observations were complemented with EDS analysis, confirming the desired elemental composition of the polymer-derived coatings, as previously reported from spectroscopic examination.18 However, caution is advised in interpreting the results presented in Table 2, given the semiquantitative nature of the analysis and the challenge in reliably detecting certain light elements, such as boron. Nonetheless, point EDS analysis of the precipitates on sample E.3 (Figure 4) revealed a relatively high concentration of chromium compared to other regions of the coating without precipitates, suggesting localized intense migration of chromium to the surface and subsequent precipitation in the form of oxide or spinel.38Table 2 reports an atomic concentration of phosphorus equal to zero for sample A.3. However, the presence of this element was confirmed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy in previous research.18 It is noteworthy that the concentration of phosphate increases within each respective series, as intended.

Cross-Sectional Analysis of the Coatings

Cross-sections of the coatings were prepared to assess elemental distribution and investigate interlayers potentially formed due to substrate oxidation during the high-temperature treatment. The thickness of the coatings was also estimated, though no discernible dependencies related to phosphate content or modifier type could be inferred from the observed differences (Table 3). SEM images of the samples, accompanied by EDS distribution maps indicating fundamental elements of the coatings (Si, P, Al, O, and C) and steel substrates (Fe, Cr, Ni, and Mn), are depicted in Figures 5–7. A distinct transition layer formed between the SiPOC-based coating and the substrate was observed, as anticipated. This layer results from a chemical reaction between the metal and polysilsesquioxane precursors since organosilicon compounds act as oxidants for the metal. Despite the application of an inert protective atmosphere during the high-temperature treatment, localized corrosion was induced, resulting in the formation of chromium oxides and (Mn,Cr)3O4.38,39 Unfortunately, due to relatively low concentrations of modifying ions (P, Al, and B) compared to other elements in the coating and substrate, their clear observation was challenging (Al detected in the map of sample A.1 was attributed to resin contamination).

A cross-section of the SiPOC sample (A.1, Figure 5) reveals a relatively thin coating, estimated to be about 560 nm thick. The interface layer between the coating and the metal substrate is clearly defined. The coating primarily consists of silicon, oxygen, and carbon, as expected. Chromium is evidently diffused within the coating, and the traces of nickel may also be observed. However, the presence of nickel should be interpreted cautiously, as its detection in cross-sections can result from sample preparation, where nickel is electrochemically deposited on the SiOC-based coatings to ensure their integrity during polishing of cross-sections. Across other coatings in the A series, the thickness increases with higher concentrations of phosphate ions.

The surface of the SiAlPOC coating displayed visible cracks but remained smooth, as evidenced by the cross-section of the C.1 coating (Figure 6). It exhibited a leveling effect, resulting in a very smooth outer surface that compensated for the roughness of the underlying substrate by varying thicknesses. The analysis of the coating revealed the presence of Si, O, and C as fundamental constituents, along with minor quantities of Al and Cr diffused from the steel substrate. Comparison of the coatings within the series, as detailed in Table 3, indicates that an increase in the phosphate content leads to a reduction in coating thickness, contrary to the trend observed in the SiPOC series.

SEM image and EDS maps of elements’ distribution for the C.1 SiAlPOC coating.

The analysis of the E.1 SiBPOC sample (Figure 7) indicates that the coating is analogous to those in the A series but thinner than those in the previously discussed C series. Additionally, EDS maps obtained during the analysis indicate similar chromium diffusion processes observed in the previous coatings.

The deposition of the coatings was conducted under identical conditions and parameters, allowing for a comparison of their effectiveness in obtaining the desired coatings. With three repetitions of the dip-coating process, most of the coatings had combined thicknesses ranging from 0.6 to 0.9 μm.

In summary, SEM observations reveal that the components of the coatings are distributed homogeneously, with no agglomerates observed in the cross-sections. Diffusion of chromium into the coatings was observed in all samples, and the presence of an interlayer was detected. Previous studies and literature suggest that this interlayer is spinel-based.38,39 Notably, all of the coatings demonstrate excellent adhesion to the substrate, as evidenced by their tight coverage and chemical bonding through the interlayer.

Wettability and SFE of the Coatings

The response of tissue to biomaterials is influenced by surface properties, such as wettability and SFE; therefore, these parameters are crucial for determining the suitability of biomaterial application.19 In the context of long-term use of orthopedic implants, achieving complete integration between the material and tissue is essential.20 Moderate hydrophilicity and relatively high SFE are considered as favorable surface properties for protein adsorption, cell adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, and proper bone tissue mineralization.21−23 However, optimal parameters for these surface properties reported in the literature vary significantly, with a water contact angle ranging between 57° and 70°40−43 and an SFE ranging from 40 to 120 mN/m.40 The CA for SiOC-based coatings containing only phosphate (series A) and phosphate with boron ions (series E) ranged between 51° and 56° (Figure 8a), indicating a desirable moderate hydrophilicity that remained constant at different phosphate concentrations. The SFE (Figure 8b) of these coatings is approximately 50 mN/m with a polar part of about 19 mN/m, and these values remain constant across the series, regardless of dopant concentrations. Conversely, for the SiAlPOC samples, both the CA and SFE depend on the concentration of phosphate ions. With increasing modifier content, the CA decreases significantly from nearly 80° (which is notably higher than the values observed for other samples) to just under 60° (Figure 8a). Concurrently, the SFE increases from 35 to 48 mN/m, primarily in its polar part (Figure 8b). Based on this data, it may be inferred that this group of samples exhibits lower affinity toward cells and may not favor their adhesion.

Corrosion Resistance of the SiPOC-Based Coatings

The primary purpose of the coatings is to protect the metallic substrate from the harsh conditions of human body fluids. The effectiveness of the coatings in this regard was evaluated by investigating their electrochemical corrosion resistance in Ringer’s solution. Initially, the changes in the stationery open-circuit potential EOC were observed under free corrosion conditions, which corresponded to the corrosion potential of the examined material. Figure 9 shows three exemplary EOC versus time plots. The reference steel sample demonstrated a steady increase in EOC, which eventually stabilized over time. This behavior is typical of surfaces of metals and alloys that passivate slowly under corroding conditions. The second type of plot represented the A and C series, which showed gradual deterioration of the coating. The observed potential’s oscillations had low amplitude with no significant increase in its value. The last presented plot was measured for the E series, and it indicated that the potential initially rose, dropped suddenly, and then rose again. This pattern was repeated throughout the experiment, and the measured amplitude reached hundreds of millivolts. This finding indicates that the coating is not entirely impermeable for the aggressive electrolyte. However, the rise after every drop indicates passivation of the surface after contact with Ringer’s solution.

Exemplary plots of the open-circuit potential for the coatings and reference steel showing three characteristic behaviors of the materials at free corrosion conditions (a). Electrical equivalent circuits depicting the corrosion process for the steel substrate (b) and imperfect coating (c).

After 1 h of sample immersion in Ringer’s solution, the EIS measurements were performed. The frequency range analyzed was restricted to 39.81 kHz–100 MHz due to the relatively large scattering of potential values obtained for all the sample measurement points at low frequencies, which required a longer acquisition time. The EIS data for uncoated steel was fitted using the R(QR) electrical circuit (Figure 9b), while the R(Q[R[QR)]) model for imperfect protective coating was applied to the SiOC-based layers (Figure 9c). Rs is the electrical resistance between the reference electrode and the surface of the sample. Rpo stands for the resistance of the electrolyte in the pores and defects of the layer, while Rct is the charge-transfer resistance due to the corrosion reaction of the steel substrate. Qc is the constant phase element (CPE), illustrating the dielectric properties of the entire layer, and Qdl represents another CPE modeling the electrical double layer of the sample.

Figure 10b illustrates the impedance spectra for the steel samples coated with SiPOC layers (A series) with the increasing phosphate ion content. For the subsequent samples A.1, A.2, and A.3, an increase in the values of both Qc and Rpo parameters was observed, suggesting a decrease in the number of defects present in the layers. The most resistant among the A series were A.2 samples, with the highest Rct value. Figure 10c presents the impedance spectra for SiAlPOC coatings (C series) with increasing phosphorus content. Similar to the results obtained for the A series samples, there was a trend of increasing charge-transfer resistance with increasing phosphate ion concentrations. However, in this case, a corresponding decrease in the Qc and Rpo values was noted. This can mean that with the increasing P content, the pores, cracks, and defects increasingly fill the microstructure of the coating. Nevertheless, the corrosion reaction is impeded by the thicker interlayer (Table 3). The addition of Al ions to the layers had a positive effect on the corrosion resistance of the system. The E series samples, coated with SiBPOC, also displayed a decrease in Qc value with the increasing phosphate ion content (Figure 10d). However, sample E.2 had the lowest value of both Rpo and Rct, indicating the highest concentration of structural defects among all samples from the E series (this was also the coating with the smallest thickness among the three—Table 3). Overall, the comodification of B ions in place of Al had a positive effect on the corrosion resistance of the system. It is noteworthy that the corrosion resistance of all A, C, and E series samples was higher than that of the reference steel sample, demonstrating the beneficial effect of the coatings on the material’s properties.

In the presented graphs, the differences in the determined breakdown potential (Eb) of the samples are visible and plotted in a linear scale. Conversely, the corrosion potential (Ecor) fluctuates within wider limits due to the high instability of the protective layers (Figure 9). A comparison of the phosphate-modified layers (A series) and aluminum-comodified coatings (C series) reveals that the breakdown occurred at much lower potentials, making these modifications unfavorable. The boron-phosphate-modified SiOC coatings (E series) yielded the best results, with the breakdown potential increasing to the values above 100 mV vs SCE. Notably, only the E.1 sample showed a breakdown potential higher than that of the steel substrate, indicating that the layers are not very resistant to corrosion in oxidizing environments. Furthermore, the thermal treatment in the surface preparation procedure adversely affects the substrate’s activity and deteriorates its resistance to oxidizing conditions, even if the layer protects against corrosion.46 This is indicated by Eb values, which, for most of the samples, are lower than those for the uncoated steel. Based on the examination carried out, the boron-phosphate-modified layers (especially E.1 and E.3) exhibit the best anticorrosion properties and show greater resistance to oxidizing conditions and free corrosion.

The primary purpose of the coatings is to provide isolation for the substrate from the environment, preventing corrosion, which can lead to the penetration of substrate elements into the body. Such penetration can cause several adverse effects, such as metallosis, allergies, and ulcerations. The effectiveness of this protective property was assessed by using EOS spectrometry of distilled water and SBF after immersing the samples in water for 7 days (Figure 13). SBF was chosen as both the solutions' simulating body conditions and the aggressive chloride-rich hostile environment. The results were compared with uncoated steel, with a focus on the main alloy components: iron, chromium, and nickel. The SiBOC (E series) coatings demonstrated the most stable and lowest results, which are in agreement with the corrosion resistance tests.

Bioactivity and Biocompatibility Examination

A series of in vitro tests were conducted to evaluate the bioactivity, biocompatibility, and cytotoxicity of the selected group of samples. The initial test performed to assess the applicability of the coatings for orthopedic implants was the Kokubo test.33 The samples were immersed in a c-SBF at 36.6 °C for 28 days. The precipitates formed on the surfaces were analyzed by SEM and EDS (Figure 14). This revealed the crystals of calcium phosphate with morphology characteristics for hydroxyapatite. However, their degree of surface coverage was higher for SiPOC (series A) and SiAlPOC (series C). Fewer agglomerates were observed on the surface of SiBPOC (series E), and those present were less developed. Minor quantities of chlorides (presumably sodium chloride) were also detected. Nevertheless, the results of the Kokubo test suggest that the coatings can be considered to be bioactive and capable of inducing bone tissue mineralization.

SEM and EDS analysis results after the so-called Kokubo test (for EDS, given in atom %, analysis considered only the detected SBF elements).

The subsequent step involved conducting the biocompatibility evaluations using osteoblast-like MG-63 cell culture. In the in situ examination, cells were directly seeded onto the SiOC-based coatings, along with TCPS as the control. Figure 15 illustrates the morphology of the cells after a 7-day incubation period. The results obtained for SiPOC (A.1) and SiBPOC (E.1) were comparable to those for the control: the surfaces were uniformly covered, and the cells appeared flattened. However, this was not the case for the SiAlPOC sample (C.1), which exhibited multiple visible voids on the surface. The difference in cell coverage might be attributed to surface properties, specifically the low SFE and its wettability.

MG-63 cell culture morphology after 7 days.

Quantitative evaluation of biocompatibility is depicted in Figure 16 as diagrams of proliferation (Figure 16a), cytotoxicity (Figure 16b), and cell viability (Figure 16c). Proliferation of osteoblast-like cells (Figure 16a) was described with the ToxiLight test and found to be comparable after 3 days for all the coatings while slightly lower for the control TCPS sample. After 7 days, the discrepancies between SiPOC-based coatings and TCPS were observed: the highest for control and the lowest for C.1 (SiAlPOC) and E.1 (SiBPOC). Cytotoxicity (also based on ToxiLight) was likewise assessed after 3 and 7 days (Figure 16b). After a short period, the control material had significantly lower cytotoxicity compared to the samples. However, after 7 days, the A.1 sample (SiPOC) exhibited the lowest cytotoxicity. The SiAlPOC, SiBPOC, and TCPS samples were statistically indistinguishable. The last evaluation was based on the PrestoBlue test and described the viability of the cells (Figure 16c). After 3 days, the results obtained for all the samples were comparable, excluding sample A.1 (SiPOC) with slightly lower fluorescence intensity, indicating lower viability of MG-63 cells. After a 7-day long exposure, values obtained for A.1 (SiPOC), C.1 (SiAlPOC), E.1 (SiBPOC), and TCPS were statistically identical. In summary, the SiPOC sample exhibited the highest biocompatibility and bioactivity, while the other samples demonstrated favorable properties compared to the reference material, especially after prolonged exposure.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive examination of the morphology and functional properties of SiOC-based coatings modified with phosphate ions and comodified with aluminum and boron. The topography of the coatings deposited using the dip-coating technique closely resembled that of the pretreated AISI steel substrate. With the exception of the SiAlPOC series and the SiPOC layer with the highest phosphate ion content, the obtained samples were uniform and smooth. The presence of boron ions slightly enhanced the formation of phase separation, as clearly depicted by SEM. The thickness of the coatings ranged from 600 to 900 nm, with the spinel-based interlayer present in most cases at approximately 250 nm.

Surface properties, including wettability and SFE, indicated favorable values for SiPOC and SiBPOC, which were independent of the dopant quantities. The estimated values of CA and SFE for SiAlPOC are strongly dependent on the composition, with the highest phosphate ion content yielding the desired range. Corrosion resistance examination strongly indicated the highest protective character of the SiBPOC coatings, as confirmed by the lowest leaching of metal ions into water and SBF. However, due to the presence of cracks, SiAlPOC coatings exhibited the lowest resistance to ion-rich media.

The majority of the analyzed samples induced precipitation of a calcium phosphate compound with morphology characteristics of hydroxyapatite, indicating the bioactivity of the materials. Further in vitro examination with osteoblast-like cells on selected samples proved their noncytotoxicity. Cells proliferated and developed properly on the surface of A.1, C.1, and D.1 samples, implying biocompatibility. However, as expected, the presence of aluminum ions negatively impacted cell adhesion.

In conclusion, the materials obtained from the SiPOC, SiAlPOC, and SiBPOC systems are bioactive, biocompatible, and corrosion-resistant. The most favorable properties were found for phosphate and boron ion-modified coatings, clearly indicating the direction of further investigation.
