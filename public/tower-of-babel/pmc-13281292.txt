Physical performance responses to match venue and outcome in elite soccer
Authors: Hadi Nobari, Okan Kamis, Ali Rıza Atıcı, Armin Gorouhi, Gizem Baskaya, Leandro Henrique Albuquerque Brandao, Gibson Moreira Praça
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIt is widely accepted that match venue influences technical, tactical, fitness, nutrition, the importance of the match, sleep, and psychological factors. This study investigates the impact of match venue (i.e., home vs. away) and match outcome (i.e., win, draw, or loss) on performance metrics among Turkish Super League teams. Utilizing a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) approach, we analyze performance indicators such as total distance covered, distance covered at different speed intervals, and number of sprint efforts (> 7 m·s⁻¹).ResultsConsidering the total distance covered, the GLM showed no significant effects of match venue [Wald (1,625) = 0.398; p = 0.528], match outcome [Wald (2,624) = 3.413; p = 0.181], and interaction between the factors [Wald (2,624) = 0.477; p = 0.788]. Significant effects were observed in the distance covered at speeds over 7 m·s⁻¹.ConclusionIn conclusion, the match venue does not impact physical performance. At the same time, winning teams display higher distances covered at higher speeds and perform more sprint efforts (> 7 m·s⁻¹), indicating that physically outperforming opponents is relevant for winning in elite soccer.Keywords: Match Venue, Match Outcome, Performance Metrics, Motor performance, Football

Introduction

Home advantage in soccer is recognized as one of the most critical factors in determining the outcome of a match [1]. It is widely accepted that match venue influences technical, tactical, fitness, nutrition, the importance of the match, sleep, and psychological factors [2], leading to better performances by the teams when playing at home than when playing away [3]. Several factors contribute to this advantage, such as crowd support, habituation to the pitch, travel fatigue, referee bias, and psychological factors [4, 5]. Therefore, investigating whether home advantage impacts players’ performance provides relevant information for practitioners planning upcoming home and away fixtures.

Soccer fans are often called the “12th man” and are an essential part of the sport as they form the basis of the stadium atmosphere [6]. Crowd support has always been cited as a significant factor in home advantage in soccer [7]. Many social and psychological factors involving fans are critical to home-field advantage. These include the proximity of the fans to the pitch and the constant loud and inspirational noise from them [8]. It was found that the noise of the fans made referees indecisive in their decision-making, and 15.5% fewer fouls were awarded against the home team [9]. Another study found a significant home bias in referees in 21 matches with spectators in the Italian Serie A compared to matches with no spectators [10]. Studies on referee bias in the context of home advantage generally indicate that referees tend to make more decisions against away teams [11]. Another study [12] examined the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and social pressure on player and referee performance and analysed matches in professional soccer leagues of 30 countries. It was found that before the pandemic, teams were, on average, 16% more likely to win at home than away matches, while during the pandemic, this difference decreased to 8%. Regarding referee bias, the authors found that before the pandemic, referees consistently made more decisions against away teams than home teams, a difference that disappeared during the pandemic [12]. On the other hand, when teams travel long distances for away matches, they may experience travel fatigue, which can negatively affect their performance. Another study showed that the distance travelled by away teams increases the expected number of goals in favor of the home team by 0.115 goals per thousand kilometres travelled [1].

While the initial research on match venue advantage in soccer focused on match outcome – i.e., the influence of such contextual factors on players’ winning probabilities – recent studies focused on understanding how the players’ and team’s performance are affected by match venue. For example, it has been shown that playing at home in the Spanish Second Division allowed players to achieve higher physical performance related to physical and technical variables [13]. On the other hand, another study in the Spanish Second Division B (the third level of the Spanish Soccer League) showed that away teams overperformed home teams in total distance (TD) and high-intensity accelerations [14]. Therefore, results seem to depend on the players’ and league’s level, and caution is required when extrapolating results from one league to another. The Turkish Super League, one of the top soccer leagues in Europe, provides an excellent opportunity to investigate this phenomenon, and there is little available data on this league. This research gap will be addressed in the current study.

In parallel with the growing interest in contextual factors such as match venue, recent research has highlighted a clear evolution in the physical demands of elite soccer, particularly in relation to high-intensity activities. Longitudinal data from top European leagues indicate that high-speed running (HSR), sprint distances, and the frequency of high-intensity efforts have increased across recent seasons. For example, a longitudinal analysis of Spanish LaLiga players from the 2019/20 to 2022/23 seasons reported significant increases in high-speed and sprint-related metrics, alongside a greater number of high-intensity actions [15]. These findings are consistent with earlier research showing that while TD covered has remained relatively stable, the intensity of match play has progressively increased. This shift toward more frequent and demanding high-speed actions underscores the importance of examining HSR and sprint performance when investigating contextual influences such as match venue and match outcome.

Considering these findings, this study aims to contribute to understanding the impact of match venue and match outcome on performance metrics among Turkish Super League teams. Utilizing a GLM approach, we strive to comprehensively analyse the performance indicators and their association with match venue and outcome. The findings of this study may have implications for coaches and teams in the Turkish Super League, as well as other soccer leagues, in developing strategies to optimize performance and maximize success.

Methods

A total of 19 teams were analyzed in this study in the 2022-23 Turkish Super League held in Türkiye. The study analyzed InStat data from all 19 teams (approximately 550 players) competing in the 2022/23 Turkish Super League, resulting in 626 match observations. Due to the aggregate commercial nature of the dataset, a strict minimum playing-time threshold was not applied; all tracked observations from official matches were included. All data were obtained from the InStat Sport (Instat, Moscow, Russia) video analysis system. InStat is a company that analyses individual and team performances in soccer. Numerous soccer teams have employed this method, which tracks the players’ performance indicators during the game as it provides the coaches with the data they need quickly and accurately [16]. The reliability of InStat data has been demonstrated in a previous study [17]. The total covered distance and displacement were analyzed across speed categories: less than 0.2 m·s⁻¹, 0.2–2 m·s⁻¹, 2.1–4 m·s⁻¹, 4.01–5.5 m·s⁻¹, 5.51–7 m·s⁻¹), and over 7 m·s⁻¹). Additionally, the analysis included the number of high-intensity sprints (5.51–7 m·s⁻¹) and maximum sprints (greater than 7 m·s⁻¹). The speed categories used in this study follow the default classification of the InStat Sport system: <0.2 m·s⁻¹ (standing), 0.2–2 m·s⁻¹ (walking), 2.1–4 m·s⁻¹ (low-speed running), 4.01–5.5 m·s⁻¹ (moderate-speed running), 5.51–7 m·s⁻¹ (high-speed running; HSR), and > 7 m·s⁻¹ (sprinting). The 5.51–7 m·s⁻¹ zone approximately corresponds to the commonly used HSR threshold of 19.8–25.2 km·h⁻¹, while the > 7 m·s⁻¹ zone aligns with the widely accepted sprint threshold of > 25.2 km·h⁻¹. Although these bands are more granular than some absolute thresholds reported in the literature, they allow a finer resolution of locomotor activity profiles. Our study does not involve direct experimentation, intervention and interaction with human participants The data analyzed in our research were obtained from previously recorded data by Turkish Soccer Federation, and were analyzed in aggregate form, without any identifying or sensitive personal information. The necessary permission to use the data was obtained from the Turkish Soccer Federation. The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Aksaray University Ethical Committee (Approval/Protocol Number: E-34183927-020-00001177579). Written informed consent was obtained from the participants. The data supporting this study’s findings are openly available in Figshare at 10.6084/m9.figshare.25211174.

Statistical analysis

Data are represented as means and standard deviation. To test the influence of match location (i.e., home vs. away) and match outcome (i.e., win, draw, or lose) on the observed variables, a GLM was used [18]. GLM shows advantages over the traditional ANOVA, especially when the assumptions for the analysis of variance are not met. Also, it is possible to adopt different probability distributions for the parameters of the dependent variables [19]. As the dependent variables are continuous, the identity link function tested two distributions for each variable: normal and gamma. The Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) was analyzed to check the model’s goodness of fit, and the model with the lowest AIC was assumed. Bonferroni’s post hoc was adopted when necessary, and the effect sizes were estimated using the standardized beta coefficients. The level of significance adopted was p = 0.05. All statistical procedures were performed using the software SPSS (v. 19.0).

Results

Data is represented in boxplots, and significant differences are highlighted in the figures. Whenever interactions between the factors were observed, Fig. 1 shows the data. Overall, match venue (home vs. away) showed no significant main effect on any of the physical performance variables analyzed, including total distance and all speed zones. In contrast, match outcome significantly influenced high-intensity performance variables, with winning teams covering greater distances at speeds > 7 m·s⁻¹ and performing more sprint efforts compared to drawing teams. Regarding interactions, only one significant interaction was observed for distance covered at very low speeds (< 0.2 m·s⁻¹), where teams losing at home showed higher values than those losing away. Considering the total distance covered (Fig. 2), the AIC indicated better goodness of fit of the Gamma model (AIC = 12604.94) over the standard model (AIC = 12608.00). The GLM showed no significant effects of match venue [Wald (1,625) = 0.398; p = 0.528], match outcome [Wald (2,624) = 3.413; p = 0.181], and interaction between the factors [Wald (2,624) = 0.477; p = 0.788].

Discussion

This study examines how Turkish Super League clubs’ performance indicators are affected by the match’s location (home vs. away) and result (win, draw, or defeat). Various performance metrics were examined using a generalized linear model (GLM) technique, including total distance covered, distance covered at distinct speed intervals, and maximum speed attained. Considering various speed thresholds, the results illustrate meaningful data on the distance soccer players’ movements during games. Few differences were observed between winning, losing, and drawing teams. Also, only some differences were reported between home and away teams. Both results are contrary to most available literature on this topic.

Our findings showed no significant effect of match venue, match outcome, or their interaction on the total distance covered and high-speed actions. These results differ from previously published studies, such as Praça et al., 2021 on young soccer players, who found a higher total distance covered in home matches rather than away matches [20]. Similarly, a study on a Portuguese professional soccer team considering the players’ performance separately due to their tactical role showed that match running performance was affected by the outcome and location of the match [21]. Home teams are expected to perform better than away ones due to different contextual factors, such as travel distance [22]. However, the present study focused on the Turkish Super League, where the country’s relatively smaller geographical size likely results in shorter travel distances compared to larger nations, such as Brazil [20]. Consequently, the reduced travel demands may attenuate fatigue-related effects on players’ performance, which could partly explain the limited influence of match venue observed in the present study. Also, it has been suggested that total distance is not necessarily related to successful outcomes in soccer [23]. Therefore, distances in different thresholds could be valuable information for understanding the effect of match venues on players’ physical performance. Additionally, it is important to acknowledge that unmodelled factors, such as opposition quality [24, 25] and environmental conditions [26], may have obscured the true effect of match venue and should therefore be considered in future research. For example, seasonal temperature variations, which are common in Türkiye, may have influenced players’ performance, potentially leading to reduced performance during summer matches and improved performance during winter. This represents an important avenue for future research.

Winning teams in the current study showed higher values of high-speed actions (over 7 m·s⁻¹) and lower values of low-speed actions (only when playing at home). The 7 m·s⁻¹ threshold corresponds to sprinting (> 25.2 km·h⁻¹). Winning teams showed significantly higher distance covered > 7 m·s⁻¹ and more sprint efforts (> 7 m·s⁻¹) than drawing teams. These sprint actions are tactically decisive in counterattacks, defensive recoveries, and runs behind the defense. Although positional data were unavailable, literature indicates that wide players typically accumulate the highest sprint volumes. This finding aligns with our previous work [27], where higher sprint outputs were linked to better physical readiness, especially when training continuity was maintained during breaks. This indicates that outperforming physically the opponent might be related to successful outcomes in elite soccer. These results align with previous literature, which suggests that high-speed distance [28] and sprint distance [29] were higher in winning teams. Outperforming the opponent physically might result in larger covered areas in the pitch and the creation of more goal-scoring opportunities, which explains the current results. For practitioners, it should be noteworthy to consider such variables in a multidimensional (i.e., physical, technical, and tactical) performance analysis model in soccer [30].

Although previous studies [21, 31] have frequently reported reduced physical performance (particularly high-speed running and sprinting) in away matches, our GLM analysis in the Turkish Super League showed no significant main effect of venue on total distance or any speed zone. This apparent contradiction can be explained by several league-specific factors. Türkiye’s relatively compact geography results in shorter average travel distances compared to larger countries (e.g., USA or Brazil), likely reducing travel-related fatigue. More importantly, our previous study using the exact same InStat dataset and identical speed thresholds [27] demonstrated that macro-level seasonal interruptions had a stronger influence on running performance than micro-level contextual factors such as venue. Specifically, physical performance (including high-speed running (5.51–7 m·s⁻¹) and accelerations) was better maintained or even improved after the planned 5-week FIFA World Cup break (when many players continued structured training) but declined noticeably after the unplanned 3-week pause caused by the 2023 earthquake in Türkiye. Our findings suggest that in the Turkish Super League, disruptions to training continuity may overshadow traditional home/away effects.

This study provides innovative insights into elite players’ physical performance and the influence of contextual factors such as match location and outcome on physical-related data. Despite its innovativeness, limitations should be acknowledged. First, it has been demonstrated that environmental constraints such as temperature and time of the match impact players’ performance [32, 33]. However, these variables were not possibly controlled in the current study as the researchers needed to define the match-play schedule. Also, variations in the training workload throughout a season were previously reported in the literature and might impact players’ performance [34]. However, we could not control the training load for the teams before the matches. Although mixed-effects models could explicitly account for the hierarchical structure of the data, the present study focused on population-level effects and model fit (AIC) and therefore adopted a GLM framework. However, the absence of random effects should be considered when interpreting the results. Finally, it should be acknowledged that players’ performance changes over the seasons [35–37]. Therefore, future studies should investigate evolutionary trends in the current data to expand its application.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the lack of significant effects of match venue and match outcome on most distance measures suggests that there may be other determinants of the distance players cover during soccer matches in the Turkish Super League. For example, player characteristics, tactical strategies, or environmental conditions may play a more influential role in shaping the distance-related performance of soccer players. Despite this, a few differences were observed between winning and losing teams, and physically outperforming opponents appears to be associated with a higher probability of success. This is particularly important when considering high-speed actions. Our findings contribute to understanding soccer’s physical demands and can inform training and match preparation strategies for coaches and sports scientists.

From a practical perspective, strength and conditioning coaches should consider systematically exposing players to high-speed running and high-acceleration actions during training sessions, as these variables appear to discriminate between winning and losing teams. Given their strong association with successful match outcomes, ensuring that players are adequately prepared to repeatedly perform such high-intensity actions may be critical for competitive performance. Notwithstanding, it should be acknowledged that most play-based training activities, particularly small-sided and conditioned games, are often constrained by limited space and time, which can reduce the opportunity for players to reach and sustain high running speeds or execute maximal accelerations [38]. As a result, these training formats may not provide sufficient stimulus to fully develop these capacities. Therefore, practitioners are encouraged to complement game-based approaches with more controlled and isolated training methods, such as linear sprint drills, resisted or assisted sprinting, and structured acceleration protocols. These approaches allow for greater control over intensity, volume, and recovery, ensuring that players are exposed to the specific mechanical and physiological demands associated with high-speed and high-acceleration efforts. Finally, although most distance-based metrics were not discriminatory in relation to match outcome, they still provide valuable information for practice design. Specifically, these data can be used to inform the planning and periodisation of training tasks, helping practitioners to align training loads with the physical demands of competition and to ensure appropriate exposure across different intensity domains.
