Slowly rotating Black Holes in DHOST Theories
Hugo Candan, Karim Noui, David Langlois

Abstract
We study slowly rotating black hole solutions within Degenerate Higher Order Scalar Tensor (DHOST) theories. Starting from a static, spherically symmetric metric solution of a DHOST theory, we employ the Hartle–Thorne ansatz to model a slowly rotating spacetime. We show that the differential equation governing the frame-dragging function ω \displaystyle\omega (which is supposed to depend on the radial coordinate only) is integrable for any DHOST theory allowing us to obtain its explicit form. We also consider angular dependence in ω \displaystyle\omega and show that regularity at the horizon and at infinity forbids it, as in General Relativity. As an illustration of the formalism introduced here, we study the slowly-rotating version of black hole solutions with primary hair obtained recently, examining the influence of the rotation on the Innermost Stable Circular Orbit (ISCO) and on the circular light trajectories in the equatorial plane.

General Relativity (with a cosmological constant) is the unique theory that satisfies the assumptions of Lovelock’s theorem in four dimensions

One of the simplest ways to construct alternatives to Einstein’s gravity is to assume that the metric field comes with a scalar field to mediate the gravitational interaction. Scalar–tensor theories have been extensively studied in recent years, leading to the classification of Degenerate Higher Order Scalar-Tensor (DHOST) theories

DHOST theories have been applied to cosmological and astrophysical contexts (see the review
[

In General Relativity (GR), black holes are known to have "no hair"

Slowly rotating solutions in DHOST theories

After a brief review of DHOST theories, we study their solutions for slowly rotating space-time following the Hartle-Thorne ansatz. Interestingly, we find an explicit integral formula for the first order term in the slowly rotating metric, for any (quadratic and cubic) DHOST theory.

\displaystyle X=-({1}/{2})\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi

2.2
The Hartle-Thorne ansatz for the metric

Many static and spherically symmetric solutions (black hole and more generally exotic compact objects) have been discovered (analytically and numerically) in the context of DHOST theories
[
35
,
52
,
12
,
53
,
11
,
49
,
18
,
14
]
. By contrast, very few rotating (axisymmetric) solutions have been reported in the literature. The first rotating solution found analytically was the stealth Kerr solution
[
11
,
14
]
where the metric is identical to the Kerr metric. From the stealth Kerr solution, new solutions were constructed
[
17
,
7
]
by applying a disformal transformation (see section
3.1
). Numerical solutions have also been found, for the cubic galileon theory
[
56
,
29
]
, and scalar-Gauss-Bonnet theories
[
38
,
37
,
26
]
.

Here, we study solutions that describe a rotating black hole space-time with a non-trivial scalar field profile at first order in the angular momentum
\displaystyle J
. Starting from an arbitrary static and spherically symmetric metric solution

\displaystyle g^{(0)}_{\mu\nu}
, we look
for a new solution of the form

g_{\mu\nu}=g^{(0)}_{\mu\nu}+J\,g^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}+{\cal O}(J^{2})\,.

(2)

Following the Hartle-Thorne ansatz, we assume the new metric to be of the form

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-h(r)\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}{f(r)}+r^{2}(\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\varphi^{2})-2\omega(r)r^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\varphi+{\cal O}(J^{2})\,,

(3)

where the function
\displaystyle\omega(r)
is implicitly proportional to
\displaystyle J
, and characterizes the frame dragging effect.

To our knowledge, this ansatz was first introduced by Lense and Thirring in 1918
[
47
]
, and was popularized later by Hartle and Thorne to describe the gravitational field of a rotating star within General Relativity
[
32
,
31
]
. One may wonder how general this ansatz is, in particular whether any stationary rotating spacetime reduces to this form at linear order in the angular momentum. The expression (
3
) was derived by Hartle using the specific symmetry of circular spacetimes
[
30
]
, but it actually also works for the non-circular solution found in
[
17
,
7
]
, suggesting that it might be more general. The possible generalization by adding a dependence of the function
\displaystyle\omega
on the angle
\displaystyle\theta
will be discussed in section
4
.

At this stage, we follow the usual procedure, which consists in, first, substituting the ansatz (
3
) into the equations of motion for the metric, namely

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mu\nu}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta S}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}=0\,,

(4)

and then in expanding these equations up to linear order in
\displaystyle\omega
. Notice that we do not need to take into account the equation of motion for the scalar field since it is a consequence of the metric equations of motion.

In theories invariant under the global shift symmetry
\displaystyle\phi\to\phi+
const, which will be discussed in section
3
, the scalar field can acquire a linear time dependence,

\phi=qt+\psi(r)\,,

(5)

without spoiling the stationarity of the metric, because all physical quantities in this case depend on the gradient of
\displaystyle\phi
and not
\displaystyle\phi
itself (see for instance
[
12
]
). In the most general case, where the theory is not shift-symmetric, the parameter
\displaystyle q
must vanish.

Let us recall that, in the absence of rotation (when
\displaystyle\omega=0
), the diagonal equations
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{tt}
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{rr}
together with the non-diagonal one
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{tr}
(or equivalently the equation of motion of the scalar field) enable us to determine the two metric components
\displaystyle h(r)
and
\displaystyle f(r)
and the scalar field
\displaystyle\psi(r)
. All the other equations can be shown to be redundant.

In the presence of a slow rotation, one can easily see that
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{tt}
,
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{rr}

and
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{tr}

remain unchanged compared to the case where
\displaystyle\omega=0
(they do not involve terms linear in
\displaystyle\omega
), and are thus satisfied by the metric components
\displaystyle h(r)
and
\displaystyle f(r)
of the static solution. Furthermore, as scalar quantities can only depend on even powers of the angular momentum
\displaystyle J
, the scalar field
\displaystyle\phi
also remains unchanged in the slow rotation approximation.

The difference with respect to the static case arises from the non-diagonal equation
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t\varphi}
, which is no longer trivial and yields a second order linear differential equation for
\displaystyle\omega(r)
, with coefficients depending on the static solution, i.e. on the functions
\displaystyle h(r)
,
\displaystyle f(r)
and
\displaystyle\psi(r)
. Hence
\displaystyle\omega(r)
can be obtained by solving directly
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t\varphi}=0
, but we will see that it is more convenient to consider a linear combination of
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t\varphi}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varphi\varphi}
which is straightforward to integrate.

Before discussing DHOST theories, let us recall that, for the Kerr metric in General Relativity, the function
\displaystyle\omega
is given by

\omega_{\mathrm{Kerr}}(r)=\frac{2J}{r^{3}}\,,

(6)

where
\displaystyle J
is the Arnowitt–Deser–Misner (ADM) angular momentum of the black hole
4
4

4

The expression (
6
) generalizes to

\omega(r)=\frac{2J}{r^{3}}+\frac{J\Lambda}{3}

for Kerr-(A)dS slowly rotating metric, where
\displaystyle\Lambda
is the cosmological constant
.

In the following, the integration constant appearing in
\displaystyle\omega(r)
when solving the equations will be expressed in term of
\displaystyle J
to match the asymptotic behavior of the Kerr solution, whenever possible.

2.4
Integrability of the equation from a conservation law

Let us now try to understand why equation (
10
) is so simple.
For that purpose, we first notice that under a change of the angular coordinates
\displaystyle\varphi\rightarrow\tilde{\varphi}={\varphi}+\omega_{0}t
where
\displaystyle\omega_{0}
is a constant, the Hartle-Thorne metric (
3
) becomes,

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}s^{2}=&-\Big[h(r)-r^{2}(\omega_{0}^{2}+2\omega_{0}\omega(r))\sin^{2}(\theta)\Big]\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}{f(r)}\\
&+r^{2}\left[\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\varphi}^{2}-2(\omega(r)+\omega_{0})\sin^{2}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\tilde{\varphi}\right]\,.\end{split}

(13)

At first order in
\displaystyle\omega(r)
and
\displaystyle\omega_{0}
, the line element above reduces to the original Hartle-Thorne line element where the function
\displaystyle\omega
has been shifted by a constant, i.e.
\displaystyle\omega(r)\rightarrow\omega(r)+\omega_{0}
. As a consequence, from the invariance under diffeomorphisms, this implies that if
\displaystyle\omega(r)
is a solution of the linearized equations, then
\displaystyle\omega(r)+\omega_{0}
is also a solution, because they are
related to the same metric written in two different coordinate systems (at linear order in
\displaystyle\omega
). This corresponds to a continuous symmetry in the space of solutions which can be exploited in order to obtain a “conserved” (i.e. a constant) quantity in the theory, which could be useful to integrate some of the equations.

However, at this stage, this symmetry is only true at linear order. Interestingly, we can make it exact by changing the initial Hartle-Thorne ansatz for the metric. Indeed, let us consider instead the following line element

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}s^{2}=&-h(r)\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}{f(r)}+r^{2}\left[\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}(\theta)(\mathrm{d}\varphi-\omega(r)\mathrm{d}t)^{2}\right]\\
=&-\left(h(r)-r^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta)\omega(r)^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}{f(r)}+r^{2}\left[\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\varphi^{2}-2\omega(r)\sin^{2}(\theta)\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\varphi\right]\end{split}

(14)

which differs from (
3
) by terms quadratic in
\displaystyle\omega^{2}
. Therefore, the equations of motion are unchanged at the linear level in
\displaystyle\omega
, which means that the two ansätze lead to the same slowly rotating solutions.
Furthermore, now, the change of the angular coordinate
\displaystyle\varphi\rightarrow\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi+\omega_{0}t
is exactly equivalent to the shift
\displaystyle\omega(r)\rightarrow\omega(r)+\omega_{0}
.
As we now show,
the conservation equation (
10
) is directly linked to this “shift-symmetry” (not to be confused with the shift-symmetry of the scalar field in the DHOST action).

First of all, substituting the ansatz (
14
) into the action and computing the equation of motion for
\displaystyle\omega
, we get

\frac{\delta S}{\delta\omega}=\frac{\delta S}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}\frac{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}{\delta\omega}=-\frac{2\sqrt{-g}}{h}(\mathcal{E}_{t\varphi}+\omega\mathcal{E}_{\varphi\varphi})\,,

(15)

where we have used the explicit expression of the inverse metric.
We recognize here the combination (
7
)
which leads directly to an integral expression for
\displaystyle\omega
.

Then an immediate consequence of the symmetry mentioned above is that the Lagrangian density
\displaystyle\cal L
does not depend directly on
\displaystyle\omega
but on its first and second derivatives. Hence, the equation for
\displaystyle\omega
is necessarily proportional to the total derivative of a quantity
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\delta S}{\delta\omega}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{J}}{\mathrm{d}r}\,,\qquad\mathcal{J}=\frac{\partial\cal L}{\partial\omega^{\prime}}-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(\frac{\partial\cal L}{\partial\omega^{\prime\prime}}\right)\,.

(16)

Finally, a straightforward calculation leads to

\mathcal{J}=\sin^{3}(\theta)Q(r)\omega^{\prime}(r)+\mathcal{O}(\omega^{3})\,,

(17)

where we have used the explicit expression of the determinant of the metric and
\displaystyle Q(r)
stands for the expression given in (
9
). In summary, the integrability of the equation for
\displaystyle\omega
is directly linked to a conservation law associated with the symmetry identified above.

Shift-symmetric theories

This section is devoted to study properties of slowly rotating solutions in shift symmetric DHOST theories. We recall that, in these theories, the functions entering in the action (

\displaystyle X

We start with a study of disformal transformations of slowly rotating metrics. Then, we explicitly compute

3.1
Disformal Transformations

In this section, we
consider
disformal transformations,

g_{\mu\nu}\;\longrightarrow\;\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=C(\phi,X)g_{\mu\nu}+D(\phi,X)\,\partial_{\mu}\phi\,\partial_{\nu}\phi\,,

(18)

where
\displaystyle C
and
\displaystyle D
are in general functions of
\displaystyle\phi
and
\displaystyle X
, constrained by the requirement that the disformal transformation is invertible and with a positive conformal factor
\displaystyle C

to keep the signature of the metric unchanged.
For shift-symmetric theories, the functions
\displaystyle C
and
\displaystyle D
are
supposed to depend on
\displaystyle X
only.

Disformal transformations induce transformations in the space of DHOST theories as follows:

\tilde{S}[\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu},\phi]=S[g_{\mu\nu},\phi]\,,

(19)

which means that, given a theory
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
for the metric
\displaystyle\tilde{g}
, one defines a new theory
\displaystyle S
for the metric
\displaystyle g
. When the disformal transformation is invertible, the two theories are classically equivalent in vacuum only while they differ when one takes into account (generic) coupling to matter.
In particular, their causal structure is different since light geodesics in one metric do not coincide with the light geodesics in the other metric, unless the transformation is purely conformal, i.e.
\displaystyle D=0
.

Let us now examine what the approximate slow-rotating metric becomes in a disformal transformation. We start with a DHOST action
\displaystyle S
and we consider a slowly rotating metric characterised by the functions
\displaystyle h(r)
,
\displaystyle f(r)
and
\displaystyle\omega(r)
. We have shown above that
\displaystyle\omega
satisfies the simple differential equation,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\mathrm{d}r}\;=\;\frac{k}{Q(r)}\,,

(20)

where
\displaystyle k
is an integration constant and
\displaystyle Q(r)
has been introduced in (
9
).
Performing a disformal transformation of the metric (
3
),
we find that the new metric
\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
can be written (at linear order in
\displaystyle\omega
) in the form (
3
)

\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}^{2}=-\tilde{h}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}^{2}}{\tilde{f}}+\tilde{r}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}-2\tilde{r}^{2}\tilde{\omega}\sin^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\varphi}\,+{\cal O}(\omega^{2})\,,

when using new coordinates
\displaystyle(\tilde{t},\tilde{r},\tilde{\varphi})
defined by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}=\mathrm{d}t-\frac{qD(r)\psi^{\prime}(r)}{C(r)h(r)-D(r)q^{2}}\mathrm{d}r\,,\quad\mathrm{d}\tilde{\varphi}=\mathrm{d}\varphi-\frac{qD(r)\psi^{\prime}(r)\omega(r)}{C(r)h(r)-D(r)q^{2}}\mathrm{d}r\,,\quad\tilde{r}=R(r)\equiv r\sqrt{C(r)}\,.

The new metric components are related to the old ones via the expressions

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{h}(\tilde{r})=Ch-q^{2}D\,,\qquad\frac{1}{\tilde{f}(\tilde{r})}=\frac{C}{R^{\prime 2}}\left(\frac{1}{f}+\frac{Dh\psi^{\prime 2}}{Ch-q^{2}D}\right)\,,\qquad\tilde{\omega}(\tilde{r})=\omega\,,

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle\tilde{r}

Let us make two comments: we have used the notation
\displaystyle C(r)
for
\displaystyle C(X(r))

\displaystyle D(r)

\displaystyle D(X(r))

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\tilde{r}

22
) so that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{t}=t+\mu(r)\,,\qquad\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi+\nu(r)\,,

(24)

where
\displaystyle\mu
and
\displaystyle\nu
are given in terms of integrals.
As a result,
\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}
satisfies an equation similar to (
20
),

\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\omega}}{\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}}\;=\;\frac{{k}}{\tilde{Q}(r)}\,,

(25)

where
\displaystyle\tilde{Q}
is the same function as in (
9
) but expressed in terms of the new functions
\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{i}
,
\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{i}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{i}
characterising the action
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
.
Of course, the two equations (
20
) and (
25
) must be equivalent, which leads to the consistency relation

R^{\prime}\frac{Q}{\tilde{Q}}=1\,.

(26)

This relation can be verified explicitly by using the expressions of
\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{i}
,
\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{i}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{i}
given in terms of
\displaystyle F_{i},A_{i}
and
\displaystyle B_{i}
, as we show in Appendix
A
.

In summary,
given a DHOST action
\displaystyle S
that admits a slowly rotating solution associated with the function
\displaystyle f
,
\displaystyle h
and
\displaystyle\omega
, then the disformally related action
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
admits also a slowly rotating solution associated to
\displaystyle\tilde{f}
,
\displaystyle\tilde{h}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}
related to the original functions by (
23
). In particular, the frame dragging functions are related by

\omega(r)=\tilde{\omega}\left(R(r)\right)\,,\quad\text{with}\quad R(r)=r\sqrt{C(r)}\,.

(27)

Expressing
\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}
in terms of
\displaystyle\omega
can be done only if the coordinate transformation
\displaystyle\tilde{r}=\sqrt{C(r)}r
is invertible, or equivalently
\displaystyle\tilde{r}
is a monotonous function of
\displaystyle r
.

3.2
Quadratic theories

In this subsection, we focus on quadratic shift-symmetric DHOST theories (
1
), which can be related to quadratic Horndeski theories via disformal transformations.

For quadratic
Horndeski theories, the expression of
\displaystyle Q
(
12
) reduces to the simple form

Q=\sqrt{\frac{f}{h}}(F_{2}-2XF_{2X})r^{4}\,.

(28)

This expression can be further simplified by taking into account
the equations of motion, as we now show. Due to the shift-symmetry of the theory, the equation of motion for the scalar field is a conservation equation, which means that it can be written as the divergence of a Noether current
\displaystyle J^{\mu}
[
12
]
,

\mathcal{E}_{\phi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta S}{\delta\phi}=0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\nabla_{\mu}J^{\mu}=0\,.

(29)

Furthermore, it was shown in
[
9
]
that
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}^{r}=qJ^{r}
, which necessarily implies
\displaystyle J^{r}=0
when
\displaystyle q\neq 0
. In the case where
\displaystyle q=0
, an argument using the physical necessity to keep
\displaystyle J_{\mu}J^{\mu}
finite on the horizon also gives
\displaystyle J^{r}=0
(see
[
34
]
for instance).

Finally, the combination

\mathcal{E}_{rr}+\frac{1}{fh}\mathcal{E}_{tt}-\frac{1}{f}\left(\psi^{\prime}+\frac{q^{2}}{fh\psi^{\prime}}\right)J^{r}=-\frac{2}{r}\sqrt{\frac{h}{f}}\penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(\sqrt{\frac{f}{h}}(F_{2}-2XF_{2X})\right)=0\,,

(30)

shows that the coefficient in front of
\displaystyle r^{4}
in the expression (
28
) of
\displaystyle Q
is constant.
As a consequence,
\displaystyle Q
is proportional to
\displaystyle r^{4}
, as in General Relativity, and
the function
\displaystyle\omega(r)
is identical to the one of Kerr (
6
) for all quadratic shift-symmetric Horndeski theories (even when
\displaystyle f(r)
and
\displaystyle h(r)
are different from the Schwarzschild metric coefficients). Hence,
we have

\omega(r)=\frac{2J}{r^{3}}\ .

(31)

This result can be
extrapolated
to other quadratic shift-symmetric DHOST theories by resorting to disformal transformations.
Indeed, given a slowly rotating metric
\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
defined in a Horndeski theory
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
, the slowly rotating metric

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}
in the DHOST theory
\displaystyle S
verifies

\displaystyle\displaystyle\omega(r)=k\int\frac{\sqrt{h/f}}{(F_{2}+2XA_{1})r^{4}}\mathrm{d}r\;=\;\frac{2J}{r^{3}}C(r)^{-3/2}\,,

(32)

according to (
11
) and (
9
) for the first equality, and to (
27
) and (
31
) for the second equality.
Notice that all the functions entering into the integral are viewed as functions of
\displaystyle r
and the integration constant has been fixed to zero.

In particular, the expression for
\displaystyle\omega

is the same as in General Relativity for shift-symmetric quadratic beyond Horndeski theories
[
28
]
which are related to Horndeski theories by a pure disformal transformation (i.e. with
\displaystyle C=1
). This is worth noting because many static solutions were found in this framework, see for instance
[
52
,
12
,
11
,
18
,
14
,
23
]
.

Otherwise, the function
\displaystyle\omega
is different from the Kerr expression. Nonetheless, if we want the disformal transformation to preserve the asymptotic flatness of the metric, we must impose that
\displaystyle C=1+o(1)
at infinity (when
\displaystyle r\rightarrow\infty
). In this case, the asymptotic behaviour of
\displaystyle\omega(r)
remains the same as in Kerr.

3.3
Circular time-like and light-like geodesics

As an application of the general formalism developed above, we consider the exact static black holes obtained recently
[
14
]
, in the context of specific shift-symmetric quadratic DHOST theories defined by the non-trivial functions

F_{0}=-\frac{2\alpha}{\lambda^{2}}\,X^{p},\quad F_{2}=1-2XA_{1},\quad A_{1}=-A_{2}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\,X^{p-1}\,,\quad A_{3}=-A_{4}=\frac{\alpha}{2}(2p-1)X^{p-2}\,,

(33)

and
\displaystyle A_{5}=0
,
where
\displaystyle\alpha
is a coupling constant and
\displaystyle p
some constant, conveniently chosen to be an integer or half-integer. Notice that these theories belong to the quadratic Beyond Horndeski theories.

The black holes obtained in these theories are interesting as they are the first solutions in DHOST theories characterized by a primary hair, i.e. a scalar charge that can be fixed independently of the mass of the black hole. This scalar parameter
\displaystyle q
is associated with the linear time dependence of the scalar field, namely

\phi(r,t)=qt+\psi(r)\,,

(34)

where the radial dependent part
\displaystyle\psi(r)
satisfies

\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}=\frac{q^{2}}{h(r)^{2}}\left[1-\frac{h(r)}{1+(r/\lambda)^{2}}\right]\,,

(35)

so that the scalar kinetic term is given by

X=\frac{q^{2}/2}{1+(r/\lambda)^{2}}\,,

(36)

which is regular and decays when going away from the black hole.

The explicit black hole metric depends on the value of
\displaystyle p
that defines the DHOST theory (
33
). For
\displaystyle p=1/2
, the metric is simply Schwarzschild, thus defining a stealth solution with a non trivial scalar profile. An interesting value is
\displaystyle p=2
, for which the metric is defined by

h(r)=f(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}+\xi_{2}\left(\frac{\pi/2-\arctan(r/\lambda)}{r/\lambda}+\frac{1}{1+(r/\lambda)^{2}}\right)\,,\qquad\xi_{2}\equiv\frac{3}{4}\alpha q^{4}\,.

(37)

From our discussion in the previous subsection, we know that slow-rotating version of these solutions, i.e. of the form (
3
), are such that
\displaystyle\omega(r)
is the same as in General Relativity.

It is then instructive to study time-like and light-like geodesics in the equatorial plane
\displaystyle\theta=\pi/2
. As a consequence of the time and angular invariance of the metric, one can introduce as usual the conserved energy and angular momentum

E=h\dot{t}+\omega r^{2}\dot{\varphi}\,,\qquad L=r^{2}\dot{\varphi}-\omega r^{2}\dot{t}\,,

(38)

where a dot denotes a derivative with respect to the geodesic parameter (the proper time
\displaystyle\tau
for a time-like geodesic and an affine parameter
\displaystyle\lambda
for a light-like geodesic). Inverting the above system to obtain
\displaystyle\dot{t}
and
\displaystyle\dot{\varphi}
in terms of
\displaystyle E
and
\displaystyle L
, and substituting in the expression
\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}=-\kappa
(with
\displaystyle\kappa=1
for time-like geodesics and
\displaystyle\kappa=0
for light-like ones), one gets, at first order in
\displaystyle\omega
, the radial equation

\frac{1}{2}\dot{r}^{2}+V_{\rm eff}(r)=0\,,

(39)

with the effective potential

V_{\rm eff}(r)=\frac{L^{2}f}{2r^{2}}-\frac{E^{2}f}{2h}+\frac{ELf}{h}\omega+\frac{\kappa}{2}f\,.

(40)

For massive particles, the innermost stable circular orbit is defined by the three conditions

{\rm ISCO:}\qquad V_{\rm eff}(r)=0\,,\qquad V^{\prime}_{\rm eff}(r)=0\,,\qquad V^{\prime\prime}_{\rm eff}(r)=0\qquad({\rm with}\ \kappa=1)\,.

(41)

The first two conditions define a circular orbit whereas the last one characterizes the onset of instability, i.e. the transition from stable circular orbits with
\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}_{\rm eff}(r)>0
to unstable ones with
\displaystyle V^{\prime\prime}_{\rm eff}(r)<0
.

As a first step, one can determine the ISCO as a function of
\displaystyle\xi_{2}
, for the non-rotating metric (i.e. J=0) (
37
). The result is plotted on Fig.
1
, together with the position of the BH horizon, which also depends on the parameter
\displaystyle\xi_{2}
(for the choice of parameter
\displaystyle\lambda=M
). We then compute the ISCO when a slow rotation, parametrized by
\displaystyle a=J/M
, is introduced.
In the limit
\displaystyle\xi_{2}\rightarrow 0
, one recovers the well-know results for Schwarzschild
\displaystyle r_{\rm ISCO}=6M
and for Kerr in the slow rotation limit:

\frac{\partial r^{-}_{\rm ISCO}}{\partial a}(\xi_{2}=0)=4\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\,,

(42)

where
\displaystyle r^{-}_{\rm ISCO}
denotes here the ISCO for retrograde orbits, i.e. orbiting the BH in the sense opposite to the BH rotation. The analogous expression for prograde orbits has the opposite sign. In other words, the ISCO radius increases with the BH rotation for retrograde orbits but decreases for prograde orbits.
One can notice that the position of the ISCO is most sensitive to the rotation when
\displaystyle\xi_{2}\simeq 0.9
, for the particular choice
\displaystyle\lambda=M
, as illustrated on Fig.

.

Let us now turn to massless particles and their circular geodesics in the equatorial plane (which are unstable).
The radial position of these orbits is obtained by solving

(43)

The analysis of the position of the circular light orbits is very similar to that of the ISCO and we have plotted the results on Fig.

4
On the angular dependence of the frame dragging function

So far, we have made the assumption that the frame dragging function
\displaystyle\omega
depends only on the radial coordinate
\displaystyle r
. In the context of General Relativity, such an assumption is well-justified because of the no-hair theorem
[
21
]
which ensures that the slowly rotating solution must correspond to the small angular momentum limit of the Kerr solution. Thus, there is no reason to expect
\displaystyle\omega
to depend on the angular coordinate
\displaystyle\theta
.

This argument is however no longer valid in modified theories of gravity, which admit hairy black hole solutions. Therefore, we study in this section the possibility that
\displaystyle\omega
depends on
\displaystyle\theta
. We start by computing the equation of motion for
\displaystyle\omega
. Then, we revisit the case of General Relativity and recall that regularity arguments prevent
\displaystyle\omega
to depend on
\displaystyle\theta
. Finally, we adapt the analysis to the specific case of DHOST theories and argue that
\displaystyle\omega
should not depend on
\displaystyle\theta
.

4.1
Equation of motion

Assuming a
\displaystyle\theta
-dependence for
\displaystyle\omega
leads to a partial differential equation, instead of the ordinary differential equation (
7
).
In order to compute this equation, we proceed as in section
2.4
since
the shift-symmetry argument remains valid
and finally obtain (see Appendix
B
for more details)

\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left[Q(r)\sin^{3}(\theta)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial r}\right]+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left[\hat{Q}(r)\sin^{3}(\theta)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\theta}\right]=0\,,

(44)

where
\displaystyle Q
is the same function as in (
9
) while
\displaystyle\hat{Q}
is defined by

\begin{split}\hat{Q}(r)=\frac{r^{2}}{\sqrt{hf}}\bigg[&\left(F_{2}+\frac{q^{2}}{h}A_{1}\right)+\frac{1}{2}f\psi^{\prime}X^{\prime}\left(F_{3X}-2\frac{q^{2}}{h}B_{6}\right)-\frac{q^{2}}{h}B_{2}\frac{(hX)^{\prime}}{h\psi^{\prime}}\bigg]\,,\end{split}

(45)

for DHOST theories
6
6

6

Similarly to
\displaystyle Q
, the expression (
45
) for
\displaystyle\hat{Q}
is obtained from

\hat{Q}(r)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{hf}}\bigg[\left(F_{2}+\frac{q^{2}}{h}A_{1}\right)r^{2}+f\psi^{\prime}\frac{q^{2}}{h}(3B_{3}+2B_{2})r+\frac{1}{2}f\psi^{\prime}X^{\prime}\left(F_{3X}-2\frac{q^{2}}{h}B_{6}\right)r^{2}-\frac{q^{2}}{h}B_{2}\frac{(hX)^{\prime}}{h\psi^{\prime}}r^{2}\bigg]\,,

after imposing the degeneracy condition
\displaystyle 3B_{3}+2B_{2}=0
.
.

To solve (
44
),
we proceed as in
[
32
]
and decompose
\displaystyle\omega(r,\theta)
on a basis of Legendre polynomials
7
7

7

The coefficient of
\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}\varphi
in the line element, which involves
\displaystyle\omega
, can be seen as an axial (or odd-parity) perturbation of the static and spherically symmetric background. Hence, it can be decomposed into the following basis of vectors on the 2-sphere,

V_{(l,m)}^{a}=\left(\frac{1}{\sin(\theta)}\frac{\partial Y_{l}^{m}}{\partial\varphi};-\sin(\theta)\frac{\partial Y_{l}^{m}}{\partial\theta}\right)\,.

Since we are considering an axisymmetric metric (whose coefficients do not depend on
\displaystyle\varphi
), only the modes
\displaystyle m=0
enter in the decomposition, hence

\omega(r,\theta)\sin^{2}(\theta)=\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}\omega_{l}(r)V_{(l,0)}^{\varphi}(\theta)=-\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}\omega_{l}(r)\sin(\theta)\frac{\partial P_{l}}{\partial\theta}\;.

,

\omega(r,\theta)\sin^{2}(\theta)=-\sum_{l=1}^{+\infty}\omega_{l}(r)\sin(\theta)\frac{\partial P_{l}}{\partial\theta}\,,

(46)

where
\displaystyle P_{l}
is the Legendre polynomial of order
\displaystyle l
evaluated at
\displaystyle\cos(\theta)
. Substituting (
46
) into (
44
) leads to the following ordinary differential equation for each mode
\displaystyle\omega_{l}
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(Q\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega_{l}}{\mathrm{d}r}\right)+\big(2-l(l+1)\big)\hat{Q}\,\omega_{l}=0\,.

(47)

For
\displaystyle l=1
, the second term in (
47
) vanishes and we recover the solution (
11
) discussed in the previous sections. For
\displaystyle l\geq 2
,
the analysis depends on the form of the functions
\displaystyle Q
and
\displaystyle\hat{Q}
. In the next three subsections, we discuss in turn three specific cases: General Relativity, quadratic DHOST theories of the form (
33
) and 4D-Einstein-Gauss-Bonnet (which is another particular case of DHOST theories).

4.2
The case of General Relativity revisited

As we mentioned above, the no-hair theorem
[
21
]
in General Relativity ensures that
\displaystyle\omega
does not depend on
\displaystyle\theta
.
Interestingly, one can recover this result from the differential equation (
47
), as discussed in
[
51
]
.

In General Relativity, the functions
\displaystyle Q
and
\displaystyle\hat{Q}
in (
47
) reduce to

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q(r)=r^{4}\,,\qquad\hat{Q}=\frac{r^{2}}{f(r)}\,,\qquad f(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}\,.

(48)

Expressing, for simplicity, the radial coordinate
\displaystyle r
in units of the Schwarzschild radius, which is equivalent to taking
\displaystyle 2M=1
, equation (
47
) reduces to

\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(r^{4}\omega_{l}^{\prime}\right)+\big(2-l(l+1)\big)r^{2}\omega_{l}=0\,.

(49)

For
\displaystyle l=1
, we obtain the solution corresponding to the Kerr metric (
6
) as expected. For
\displaystyle l\geq 2
, the solution is more involved but can be written in terms of special functions
[
51
]
.

For the present discussion, we simply need to study the asymptotic behaviour, at spatial infinity and near the horizon, of
\displaystyle\omega_{l}(r)
or, equivalently, of the function
\displaystyle y_{l}(r)=r^{2}\omega_{l}(r)
, which satisfies the simpler differential equation

r(r-1)\,y_{l}^{\prime\prime}+\left(\frac{2}{r}-l(l+1)\right)\,y_{l}\,=\,0\,.

(50)

The general solution of (
50
) can be written as

\displaystyle\displaystyle y_{l}(r)\;=\;c_{(1)}\,y_{l}^{(1)}(r)+c_{(2)}\,y_{l}^{(2)}(r)\,,

(51)

where
\displaystyle(y_{l}^{(1)},\,y_{l}^{(2)})
is some basis of solutions while
\displaystyle c_{(1)}
and
\displaystyle c_{(2)}
are constants (to be fixed with boundary conditions).
For
\displaystyle l=2
, the general solution is given explicitly by

y_{2}(r)=c_{(1)}\,r^{2}(r-1)+5c_{(2)}\,\left(4r^{2}-2r-\frac{2}{3}-\frac{1}{3r}+4r^{2}(r-1)\ln\left(\frac{r-1}{r}\right)\right).

(52)

At spatial infinity, the leading order of the two independent solutions
\displaystyle y_{l}^{(1)}
and
\displaystyle y_{l}^{(2)}
can be deduced from the equation

r^{2}\,y_{l}^{\prime\prime}-l(l+1)\,y_{l}\,{=}\,0\,,

(53)

where the coefficients in (
50
) have been replaced by their leading order expression at large
\displaystyle r
(see e.g. chapter 4.2 of
[
55
]
for details).
Since the general solution of (
53
) is simply given by a linear combination of
\displaystyle r^{l+1}
and
\displaystyle r^{-l}
, we can choose a basis
\displaystyle(y_{l}^{(1)},\,y_{l}^{(2)})
such that

y_{l}^{(1)}(r)=r^{l+1}(1+o(1))\,,\qquad y_{l}^{(2)}(r)=r^{-l}(1+o(1))\,.

(54)

As the solution should not diverge at spatial infinity in order to recover an asymptotically Minkowski metric, we have to fix
\displaystyle c_{(1)}=0
for every
\displaystyle l
in (
51
).

Let us now turn to the asymptotic behaviour at the horizon. In the case
\displaystyle l=2
, one sees that the solution
\displaystyle y_{l}^{(2)}
is pathological at the horizon (
\displaystyle r\rightarrow 1
)
because of the logarithm term: the solution is
continuous but not differentiable. As a consequence,
\displaystyle c_{(2)}
must also vanish if one imposes that the metric components is both continuous and differentiable.
The same conclusion applies for
\displaystyle l>2
, due to the presence of a similar logarithmic term leading to a pathological behaviour at the horizon (see
[
51
]
for instance). Hence, all modes
\displaystyle l\geq 2
must vanish and
\displaystyle\omega
cannot depend on
\displaystyle\theta
, as expected from the no-hair theorem.

4.3
Generalization to a class of quadratic DHOST theories

We now extend the analysis of the previous subsection to the family of quadratic shift-symmetric DHOST theories considered in section
3.3
, which admit interesting hairy black hole solutions
[
14
]
where the scalar field is of the form
\displaystyle\phi=qt+\psi(r)
while the metric is static and homogeneous, i.e.
\displaystyle f(r)=h(r)
.

We can easily compute the two functions entering the equation for
\displaystyle\omega
and we find
\displaystyle Q(r)=r^{4}
, as in General Relativity, while
\displaystyle\hat{Q}
is given by

\hat{Q}=\frac{r^{2}}{f}\left(F_{2}+\frac{q^{2}}{f}A_{1}\right)=\frac{r^{2}}{f}\left(1+f\psi^{\prime 2}A_{1}\right)\,.

(55)

The equation for the function
\displaystyle y_{l}(r)=r^{2}\omega_{l}(r)
now reads

\alpha_{2}(r)\,y_{l}^{\prime\prime}+\alpha_{0}(r)\,y_{l}=0\,,\qquad\alpha_{2}=r^{2}f\,,\quad\alpha_{0}=\big(2-l(l+1)\big)\left(1+f\psi^{\prime 2}A_{1}\right)-2f\,\,.

(56)

where the coefficients behave, at spatial infinity, as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{2}(r)=r^{2}(1+o(1))\,,\qquad\alpha_{0}(r)=-l(l+1)+o(1)\,,

(57)

assuming
8
8

8

The case
\displaystyle p=1
corresponds to a non-asymptotically flat space-time.

\displaystyle p>1
.
At spatial infinity, we thus recover the same asymptotic behaviour as in General Relativity, which implies that
one of the two branches of solutions has to be eliminated, say
\displaystyle c_{(1)}=0
.

At the horizon, by contrast, the behaviour of the solution differs from that in General Relativity.
Indeed,
near the horizon radius
\displaystyle r_{H}
(defined by
\displaystyle f(r_{H})=0
), the two functions
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}
behave as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{2H}\varepsilon(1+o(1))\,,\qquad\alpha_{0}=\frac{\alpha_{0H}}{\varepsilon}(1+o(1))\,,

(58)

where
\displaystyle\varepsilon=r-r_{H}
,
\displaystyle X_{H}=X(r_{H})
and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{2H}=r_{H}^{2}f^{\prime}(r_{H})\,,\qquad\alpha_{0H}=\alpha q^{2}\frac{X_{H}^{p-1}}{2f^{\prime}(r_{H})}(2-l(l+1))\,.

(59)

Hence, one can choose a basis of solutions (
51
) of (
56
) such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle y_{l}^{(1)}=\varepsilon^{n_{+}}(1+o(1))\,,\qquad y_{l}^{(2)}=\varepsilon^{n_{-}}(1+o(1))\,,

(60)

where the powers
\displaystyle n_{\pm}
are given by

n_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left[1\pm\left({1-4\frac{\alpha_{0H}}{\alpha_{2H}}}\right)^{1/2}\right]\,.

(61)

When
\displaystyle l=1
,
\displaystyle n_{+}=1
and
\displaystyle n_{-}=0
, thus the two branches are regular (continuous and differentiable) at the horizon. Hence, as in General Relativity, the mode
\displaystyle\omega_{1}
should be included.
For the modes
\displaystyle l\geq 2
, we must examine the near-horizon behaviour depending on the sign of
\displaystyle\alpha
.

If
\displaystyle\alpha>0
, we see that
\displaystyle n_{-}<0
, giving a diverging branch at the horizon, which must be eliminated. Provided that this branch is not the same as the one already eliminated at spatial infinity, we conclude that
\displaystyle y_{l}=0
for all
\displaystyle l\geq 2
. Moreover, except for some specific values of
\displaystyle\alpha
,
\displaystyle n_{+}
is not an integer and so the remaining branch at the horizon is not
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
. If we demand the solution to verify this property, we can eliminate both branches at the horizon.

If
\displaystyle\alpha<0
,
\displaystyle n_{\pm}
are either real or complex. When they are real, it is easy to see that
\displaystyle 0<n_{-}<n_{+}<1
, so both branches are continuous but not differentiable at the horizon, and must therefore be eliminated. When
\displaystyle n_{\pm}
are complex, the solutions are of the form,

y_{l}^{(1)}=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\penalty\ e^{i\Im(n_{+})\ln(\varepsilon)}(1+o(1))\,,\qquad y_{l}^{(2)}=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\penalty\ e^{-i\Im(n_{+})\ln(\varepsilon)}(1+o(1))\,,

(62)

where
\displaystyle\Im(n_{\pm})
denotes the imaginary part.
Both branches are continuous but not differentiable at the the horizon, so we can eliminate both again if we demand the solution to be differentiable.

In conclusion,
\displaystyle\omega
cannot depend on the angle
\displaystyle\theta
.

4.4
Four Dimensional Einstein-Gauss-Bonnet

In this section, we extend our analysis to the case of a four dimensional Einstein-Gauss-Bonnet theory, for which a static black hole solution is known
[
49
]
. A slowly rotating solution has already been found in
[
24
]
, and was studied in

. This theory belongs to the class of Horndeski theories, and is defined by the following non-trivial functions,

\begin{split}&F_{0}=8\alpha X^{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ F_{1}=8\alpha X\penalty\ \penalty\ ;\penalty\ \penalty\ F_{2}=1+4\alpha X\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ A_{1}=-A_{2}=-4\alpha\,,\\
&F_{3}=-4\alpha\ln\rvert X\lvert\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ 6B_{1}=-2B_{2}=3B_{3}=\frac{4\alpha}{X}\,,\end{split}

(63)

with
\displaystyle\alpha
being a coupling constant of dimension length squared. As this theory contains cubic terms in the second derivatives of the scalar field, it does not fall under the class of theories we have studied in the previous section
3.2
.

The static black hole solution is given by,

f(r)=h(r)=1+\frac{r^{2}}{2\alpha}\left(1-\sqrt{1+\frac{8\alpha M}{r^{3}}}\right)\,,

(64)

while the scalar field can be obtained from,

\psi^{\prime}(r)=\frac{\sqrt{f(r)}-1}{r\sqrt{f(r)}}\,,

(65)

where, for simplicity, we restrict ourselves to the case of
\displaystyle q=0
.

The slowly rotating function
\displaystyle\omega(r)
still satisfies the equation (
44
) where the two functions
\displaystyle Q
and
\displaystyle\hat{Q}
are now given by,

Q=r^{4}\sqrt{1+\frac{8\alpha M}{r^{3}}}\,,\qquad\hat{Q}=\frac{r^{2}}{f}\left[1+\frac{2\alpha}{r^{2}}\left(f-1-rf^{\prime}\right)\right]\,.

(66)

We start by considering the
\displaystyle l=1
mode, and an immediate calculation leads to the same solution as the one obtained in
[
24
]
,

\omega_{1}(r)=\frac{J}{2\alpha M}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{8\alpha M}{r^{3}}}-1\right)\,.

(67)

Notice that the two integration constants that show up when we integrate
\displaystyle\omega_{1}
are chosen such that
\displaystyle\omega_{1}
tends to the Kerr solution at spatial infinity.

In order to study the modes for
\displaystyle l>1
, we proceed as in the previous section. We first perform the change of variable
\displaystyle y_{l}=\sqrt{Q}\penalty\ \omega_{l}
so that the equation for
\displaystyle y_{l}
does not involve a first derivative term,

y_{l}^{\prime\prime}+\left[\frac{Q^{\prime 2}-2QQ^{\prime\prime}}{4Q^{2}}+\left(2-l(l+1)\right)\frac{\hat{Q}}{Q}\right]y_{l}=0\,.

(68)

At spatial infinity, the coefficient of
\displaystyle y_{l}
behaves as follows,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{Q^{\prime 2}-2QQ^{\prime\prime}}{4Q^{2}}+\left(2-l(l+1)\right)\frac{\hat{Q}}{Q}=-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^{2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)\,,

(69)

hence, the asymptotic behaviour is the same as in General Relativity (
53
) and we can eliminate the branch that diverges like like
\displaystyle r^{l+1}
.

At the horizon, located at radius
\displaystyle r_{H}=M+\sqrt{M^{2}-\alpha}
, the function
\displaystyle\hat{Q}
diverges (due to the presence of
\displaystyle f(r)
in the denominator) while
\displaystyle Q
and its derivatives are finite. Thus, the coefficient of
\displaystyle y_{l}
behaves as follows,

\displaystyle\displaystyle(2-l(l+1))\frac{\alpha_{H}}{\varepsilon}\,,\qquad\alpha_{H}=\frac{r_{H}^{4}-4\alpha Mr_{H}}{2r_{H}^{2}(r_{H}-M)(r_{H}^{2}+2\alpha)}\,,

(70)

where
\displaystyle\varepsilon=r-r_{H}
. Hence, the solution behaves as in General Relativity at the horizon with a logarithmic term
\displaystyle(r-r_{H})\ln(r-r_{H})
appearing in one of the two branches of solutions (for
\displaystyle l\neq 1
), making it non-differentiable. Hence, by requiring the solution to be differentiable, we can also eliminate a branch of solution.

We have no proof that the branch eliminated at infinity is not the same as the one eliminated at the horizon, in which case a regular branch of solution would remain. However, given that the theory (
63
) reduces to GR continuously in the limit
\displaystyle\alpha\rightarrow 0
, one can expect that the two branches eliminated at spatial infinity and at the horizon, respectively, are distinct, as in GR, at least for sufficiently small values of
\displaystyle\alpha
. This would confirm that
\displaystyle\omega
does not depend on the angle
\displaystyle\theta
.

5
Conclusion

We studied slowly rotating black-hole solutions within the framework of DHOST theories. Starting from any static, spherically symmetric background of a DHOST model, we derived a general integral expression for the frame-dragging function
\displaystyle\omega
. The possibility to obtain such a general result relies on an interesting "hidden" symmetry of the equations of motion, which leads to their integrability.
We then showed that this expression is consistent with disformal transformations and derived how
\displaystyle\omega
transforms under such transformations.
We illustrated our results with explicit examples and proved (using regularity conditions at infinity and at the horizon) that no solution for
\displaystyle\omega
with angular dependence can exist, as in General Relativity. In the context of the recently discovered black holes with primary hair, we analyzed the time-like and light-like circular geodesics and quantified how they deviate from the predictions of the Kerr solution, in the slow-rotation limit.

It would be interesting to investigate whether the method for computing slowly rotating solutions, along with the integrability property of the equations of motion, remains valid in other modified theories of gravity. We also aim to generalize our results to the case of slowly rotating stars.

Appendix A
Appendix: Disformal Transformations

In this appendix, we provide technical details on the effect of disformal transformations on slowly rotating spacetimes in DHOST theories.

We recall that a disformal transformation of the metric is defined by,

g_{\mu\nu}\;\longrightarrow\;\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=C(\phi,X)g_{\mu\nu}+D(\phi,X)\,\partial_{\mu}\phi\,\partial_{\nu}\phi\,,

(71)

where
\displaystyle C
and
\displaystyle D
are functions of
\displaystyle\phi
and
\displaystyle X
, constrained by the requirement that the disformal transformation is invertible. Moreover, we assume that the conformal factor
\displaystyle C
is a positive function to keep the signature of the metric unchanged. As we restrict ourselves to shift-symmetric theories, the functions
\displaystyle C
and
\displaystyle D
are also supposed to depend only on
\displaystyle X
.

Disformal transformations induce transformations in the space of DHOST theories as follows:

\tilde{S}[\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu},\phi]=S[g_{\mu\nu},\phi]\,,

(72)

which means that, given a theory
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
for the metric
\displaystyle\tilde{g}
, one defines a new theory
\displaystyle S
for the metric
\displaystyle g
. When the disformal transformation is invertible, the two theories are classically equivalent in vacuum only while they differ when one takes into account (generic) coupling to matter. Here, we are constructing slowly rotating solutions for scalar-tensor theories in vacuum and then, considering the action
\displaystyle S
or any disformally related action
\displaystyle\tilde{S}
must lead to equivalent metrics. Let us check that this is indeed the case.

We start with a DHOST action
\displaystyle S
and we consider a slowly rotating metric, characterized by the functions
\displaystyle h(r)
,
\displaystyle f(r)
and
\displaystyle\omega(r)
. We have shown that
\displaystyle\omega
satisfies the simple differential equation,

\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{\mathrm{d}r}\;=\;\frac{k}{Q(r)}\,,

(73)

where
\displaystyle k
is an integration constant and
\displaystyle Q(r)
has been introduced in (
9
) and can be decomposed as follows,

{Q}=({Q}_{2}+{Q}_{3})r^{4}+{Q}_{B}r^{3}\,,\,,

(74)

where the different components are defined by

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{f}{h}}(F_{2}+2XA_{1})\,,

(75)

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{3}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}f\sqrt{\frac{f}{h}}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}\frac{dX}{dr}(F_{3X}+4XB_{6}-2B_{2})\,,

(76)

\displaystyle\displaystyle Q_{B}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2f\sqrt{\frac{f}{h}}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}XB_{2}\,.

(77)

Notice that we have replaced
\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}
by the more explicit expression
\displaystyle\partial\phi/\partial r
to recall that we are taking the derivative of
\displaystyle\phi
at constant
\displaystyle t
(since this will become important later when we change coordinates).

Then, we perform a disformal transformation of the metric and, after a direct calculation, we can show that the metric
\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
still takes the form (
3
)

\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}^{2}=-\tilde{h}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}^{2}}{\tilde{f}}+\tilde{r}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}-2\tilde{r}^{2}\tilde{\omega}\sin^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{\varphi}\,,

(78)

where the new coordinates
\displaystyle(\tilde{t},\tilde{r},\tilde{\varphi})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}=\mathrm{d}t-\frac{qD(r)\psi^{\prime}(r)}{C(r)h(r)-D(r)q^{2}}\mathrm{d}r\,,\qquad\mathrm{d}\tilde{\varphi}=\mathrm{d}\varphi-\frac{qD(r)\psi^{\prime}(r)\omega(r)}{C(r)h(r)-D(r)q^{2}}\mathrm{d}r\,,\qquad\tilde{r}=R(r)\,,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{h}(\tilde{r})=Ch-q^{2}D\,,\qquad\frac{1}{\tilde{f}}(\tilde{r})=\frac{C}{R^{\prime 2}}\left(\frac{1}{f}+\frac{Dh\psi^{\prime 2}}{Ch-q^{2}D}\right)\,,\qquad\tilde{\omega}(\tilde{r})=\omega\,,

where the functions of the r.h.s. of the two identities are evaluated on
\displaystyle r
, viewed as a function of
\displaystyle\tilde{r}
.
Let us make two comments: we have used the notation
\displaystyle C(r)
for
\displaystyle C(X(r))
and
\displaystyle D(r)
for
\displaystyle D(X(r))
; as
\displaystyle C
is supposed to be positive, the coordinate
\displaystyle\tilde{r}
is well-defined. We can formally integrate the two differential equations in (
79

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{t}=t+\mu(r)\,,\qquad\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi+\nu(r)\,,

(81)

where
\displaystyle\mu
and
\displaystyle\nu
are given in terms of integrals.
As a result,
\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}
satisfies an equation similar to (

\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\omega}}{\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}}\;=\;\frac{\tilde{k}}{\tilde{Q}(r)}\,,

where
\displaystyle\tilde{k}

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}
