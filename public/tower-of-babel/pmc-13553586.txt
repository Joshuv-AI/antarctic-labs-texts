Elucidate the structural role of helper lipids in modulating hepatic expression following repeated intravenous administration of mRNA-LNPs
Authors: Jiahao Ge, Xiaochen Bo, Xinye Zhu, Mei Long, Yiqing Zhang, Mingxia Qiu, Qinggong Yuan, Michael Ott, Yuan Liu, Zhicong Zhao, Lei Huang, Qiang Xia, Taihua Yang, Siyuan Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are the most advanced non-viral platform for hepatic mRNA delivery, yet the functional role of helper phospholipids, particularly under repeated dosing regimens required for chronic protein replacement therapies, remains poorly understood. Here, we systematically investigated six helper phospholipids varying in alkyl chain length, headgroup, and unsaturation, evaluating LNP properties and in vivo efficacy after single and repeated intravenous administration (weekly, biweekly, or triweekly). DSPE-based LNPs consistently achieved the highest mRNA expression in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, DSPE's superior performance involves a two-step cooperative process: enhanced ApoE adsorption promoting cellular uptake and optimal pKa (6.6) for pH-responsive endosomal protonation. Notably, DSPC induced the highest IL-6 levels under the weekly dosing regimen, suggesting that vaccine-approved lipids may be suboptimal for low-inflammation therapies requiring frequent administration. Importantly, substituting DSPC with DSPE consistently enhanced expression across multiple ionizable lipid platforms (ALC-0315, MC3, and Lipid 5), and significantly improved PCSK9 gene editing efficiency as well as exogenous OTC protein expression. These findings redefine helper phospholipids as active regulators of repeated-dose mRNA delivery and identify DSPE as a promising candidate for chronic hepatic protein replacement.Keywords: mRNA therapeutics, Lipid nanoparticles, Helper phospholipids, Anti-PEG IgM

Introduction

The application of mRNA therapeutics in protein replacement therapies has garnered extensive interest, as mRNA enables rapid protein production, offers reduced immunogenicity and no risk of genotoxicity [1,2]. Preclinical research has extensively explored leveraging the liver as an in vivo bioreactor for mRNA-directed production of therapeutic proteins to address rare hepatic diseases, tumors, and other disorders [[3], [4], [5]]. Currently, several clinical trials are evaluating mRNA-based protein replacement strategies for diseases such as propionic acidemia (PA), methylmalonic acidemia (MMA) and ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency (OTCD) [[6], [7], [8]]. Despite its potential, the clinical translation of mRNA therapies remains largely limited by the lack of efficient delivery systems.

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) represent the most advanced non-viral platform for systemic mRNA delivery and exhibit intrinsic liver tropism via apolipoprotein E (ApoE) -mediated uptake by hepatocytes [9]. A standard LNP formulation comprises four key components: (1) an ionizable cationic lipid, responsible for mRNA encapsulation and endosomal escape; (2) a helper phospholipid, contributing to structural integrity; (3) cholesterol, enhancing membrane stability and fluidity; and (4) a polyethylene glycol (PEG)-conjugated lipid, modulating colloidal stability and pharmacokinetics [[10], [11], [12]]. While extensive efforts have focused on engineering novel ionizable lipids to enhance potency, the role of helper phospholipids remains underappreciated, despite their critical influence on membrane fluidity, phase behavior, and intracellular trafficking.

Helper phospholipids critically determine LNP physicochemical properties through variations in headgroup chemistry, alkyl chain length, and saturation [13,14]. These structural parameters modulate membrane fluidity, phase behavior, and nanoparticle stability [15]. While prior studies have examined the impact of helper phospholipids on the protein expression and biodistribution [16,17], these investigations have predominantly been employed under single-injection conditions, which do not reflect the requirements of chronic therapeutic applications. Repeated LNP administration can trigger cumulative innate and adaptive immune responses (including anti-PEG antibodies) [[18], [19], [20]]. However, how helper lipid structure influences these outcomes under different dosing intervals (weekly, biweekly, triweekly) remains unknown.

To address these gaps, we systematically investigated six representative helper phospholipids in mRNA-LNPs across single and repeated dosing schedules. We evaluated mRNA expression, liver enzyme levels, cytokine responses, and the generation of anti-PEG IgM and IgG antibodies. By correlating helper lipid structural features (alkyl chain length, headgroup identity, and unsaturation) with in vivo performance, we elucidated the pivotal role of helper lipids in LNP performance under repeated administration. Furthermore, we identified DSPE as a superior helper lipid for single and repeated administration, which exhibited enhanced mRNA expression in multiple ionizable lipid formulations. Replacing DSPC with DSPE significantly improved LNP performance in gene editing and protein replacement therapy. Collectively, our findings redefine helper phospholipids as active regulators of repeated-dose mRNA delivery and provide a rational basis for LNP design in chronic hepatic protein replacement.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ambion™ Nuclease-Free Water was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc (USA). Sodium citrate was from AVT (Shanghai) Pharmaceutical Tech Co., Ltd (China). Citric acid, sucrose, potassium dihydrogen phosphate and potassium phosphate dibasic trihydrate were purchased from NANJING CHEMICAL REAGENT CO., LTD (China). 1, 2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC), 1, 2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), 1, 2-dioctadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phophocholine (DSPC), 1, 2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DSPE), 1, 2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DPPC), 1, 2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DMPC), cholesterol, 1,2-dimyristoyl-rac-glycerol-3-methoxypolyethylene glycol-2000 (DMG-PEG2000) were purchased from Xi'an ruixi Biological Technology Co., LTD (China). Centrifugal Filter was from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Quant-iT™ RiboGreen™ RNA Reagent, 10% Triton X-100 and penicillin/streptomycin (Pen-Strep) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., LTD (USA). Britelite plus was from PerkinElmer (USA). D-Luciferin firefly Potassium Salt was from Maokang Biotechnology (China). Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) and Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco (Waltham, USA). BeyoAOF™ ITS Media Supplement was from Beyotime Biotech Inc (China).

Methods

Preparation of LNPs

Non-modified LNPs: LNPs were formulated by microfluidic mixing (INanoL, Micro&Nano Instrument Technology Co., LTD. China). Briefly, lipid 5, phospholipid, Chol, DMG-PEG2000 were dissolved in ethanol at the molar ratio of 50:10:38.5:1.5. LNPs were formulated by mixing the ethanol phase containing lipids with an aqueous phase containing mRNA at a volume ratio of 1:3. Then, the LNPs were diluted with phosphate solution, subsequently, ultrafiltered and concentrated by 100 KDa Amicon ultra-centrifugal filter. After that, the concentrated LNPs were dialyzed through 10% (w/v) sucrose solution. LNPs were eventually preserved in 10% sucrose solution and stored at 4 °C for further study.

Cy5-labeled LNPs: Chol in the standard lipid formulation was partially substituted with Cy5-Chol, wherein the modified cholesterol replaced 0.5% of the total Chol component. Subsequently, Cy5-Chol was combined with the remaining four lipid constituents and dissolved in ethanol. Concurrently, an aqueous phase containing mRNA was prepared. These two phases were then rapidly mixed using a microfluidic device to initiate LNPs.

Characterization of LNPs

Hydrodynamic diameter, polymer dispersity index (PDI) and zeta potential of the LNP nanoparticles were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS). The mRNA concentration of LNPs was measured by stunner (Unchained Labs). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) of LNP was quantified using Quant-iT™ RiboGreen™ RNA Reagent. Briefly, an equal volume of RiboGreen reagent was added to the diluted LNPs, then the fluorescence of the samples was measured using a microplate reader with excitation (Ex) and emission (Em) wavelengths setting to 485 nm and 530 nm, respectively. The difference between the total mRNA content and un-encapsulated mRNA was the amount of mRNA encapsulated in formulations. The morphology of LNPs was visualized by cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (Cryo-TEM; Glacios, Thermo Fisher Scientific, US).

Dissociation constant (pKa) detection of LNP

The zeta potentials of the LNPs were measured in storage buffers at various pH values (9.0, 8.0, 7.5, 7.0, 6.5, 6.0, 5.0, n = 3). The resulting pH–zeta potential data were fitted to a Boltzmann function using Origin software to generate the corresponding curve. The pH at which the zeta potential equaled zero was interpolated from the fitted curve and defined as the pKa of the LNPs.

Cell culture

AML12 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 1% BeyoAOFTM ITS Media Supplement and 40 ng/mL Dexamethasone. HepG2 cells and Hep1-6 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator.

In vitro mRNA uptake

HepG2, Hep1-6, and AML12 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were separately seeded in 24 well plates and incubated overnight. LNPs formulated with 6 phospholipids including Cy5-chol were mixed with the culture medium and incubated with cells at 37 °C. After incubating 3 h, cells were washed for fluorescence and then detached for flow cytometry.

LNP transfection in vitro

HepG2, Hep1-6, and AML12 cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 96 well plates and incubated overnight before treatment with different LNPs encapsulating with Luciferase mRNA. After incubating 24 h, Britelite plus Luciferase detection buffer was added to each well to quantify the Luciferase expression of each LNPs according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Endosome escape assay

Endosome escape of mRNA was visualized using fluorescence microscope. Cy5-labeled mRNA containing LNPs formulated with 6 phospholipids were mixed with the culture medium separately and incubated with cells at 37 °C. After incubating for 4h, cells were washed with PBS and stained with Lysotracker for 30 min at 37 °C. Then, cells were washed with PBS for 3 times followed by staining with Hochest for 10 min. After washing with PBS for 3 times, cells were imaged using microscope. The co-localization of Cy5-mRNA and lysotracker was quantified by Image J.

Inhibition of endosomal acidification

AML12 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. Prior to LNP transfection, the cells were pre-incubated for 30 min in AML12-specific medium supplemented with 10 nM bafilomycin A1 (Selleck Chemicals, USA). After this pretreatment, 50 ng of LNPs were added to each well. Following 24 h of incubation, Britelite plus Luciferase detection buffer was added to each well to quantify the Luciferase expression of each LNPs according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Protein corona composition

The mouse serum (500 μL) and lipid nanoparticle (LNP) suspension (500 μL) were mixed thoroughly and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 15000 rpm for 8 h. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed with 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After centrifugation at 15000 rpm for 30 min, the PBS was removed, and the pellet was retained. The resulting pellet was subjected to proteomic analysis for protein identification and quantification.

LNP transfection in vivo

Female BALB/c mice (6-8 weeks) were purchased from Shanghai ShengChang Biomedical Technology Co., LTD. Animal experiments followed the protocol approved by the Renji Hospital Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. BALB/c mice were administrated LNPs encapsulating Luciferase mRNA (0.25 mg/kg) through intravenous (i.v.) injection. 6 h and 24 h post-injection, D-luciferin Potassium Salt solution (150 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally injected to the mice. Luciferase expression at liver was monitored and quantified by Raycision IMAGEING 200 imaging system and manufacturer's software.

For ex vivo imaging, mice were euthanized at 6 h post-injection, and the major organs were dissected and collected. Organs were subjected to bioluminescence imaging using the same Raycision IMAGING 200 system.

Anti-PEG antibody response

The 96-well plates were coated with PEG lipid (1 mg/mL; PEG lipid in LNP used in this study, 50 μL per well). The coated 96-well plates were placed at 4 °C overnight. Then, the plates were washed with PBS and blocked by 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS)/PBS at 37 °C for 1 h. The serum samples diluted 10 times with 10% FBS/PBS were added to the 96-well plates (100 μL per well) and then the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Wash the plates with PBS three times and anti-mouse IgM-HRP or anti-mouse IgG-HRP (Abcam, UK) (1:500) was added (100 μL per well) to plates. Incubate them at 37 °C for 1 h. Wash the plates with PBS three times and then add TMB Chromogen Solution for ELISA (Beyotime, China) to the plates (100 μL per well). After avoiding light for 10 min, add stop solution for TMB substrate (Beyotime, China) to the plates (100 μL per well) and measure the absorbance at 450 nm.

Blood biochemistry and cytokine analysis

Blood samples (200 μL) were collected from BALB/c mice via retro-orbital venous puncture 6 h after each tail vein injection of Luciferase mRNA/LNP. The blood was allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to isolate serum. Serum AST and ALT levels were measured using biochemistry analyzer (Mindray, China). Serum TNF-α and IL-6 levels were quantified using mouse TNF-α ELISA kit and mouse IL-6 ELISA kit (MultiSciences, China), respectively.

Mouse serum PCSK9 level assay

Male C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks) were purchased from Shanghai ShengChang Biomedical Technology Co., LTD. Animal experiments followed the protocol approved by the Renji Hospital Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. On days 5, 8, and 14 after tail vein injection of 2 mg/kg PCSK9-targeting Cas9 mRNA/LNP, blood samples (200 μL) were collected from mice via retro-orbital puncture. The blood was allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain serum. Serum PCSK9 levels were measured using a mouse proprotein convertase 9 ELISA kit (YEASEA, China).

PCSK9 gene editing efficiency analysis

On day 14 after tail vein injection of 2 mg/kg PCSK9-targeting Cas9 mRNA/LNP, mouse liver tissues were collected and snap-frozen on dry ice. Total DNA was extracted from the liver tissues using tissue a genomic DNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, China) and used to prepare the Sanger sequencing reaction mixture. PCR amplification was performed according to the specified cycling conditions, including initial denaturation, cyclic reaction, and final extension. The primer sequences used were as follows:

F: TCTGAGGCTAGAGGACTGAG;

R: CTGCTGGATGACCAGTTGAG.

Following the reaction, the products were purified and loaded onto the sequencer according to standard protocols. After sequencing was completed, the data were analyzed using the ICE online tool (https://ice.editco.bio/#/) to calculate the PCSK9 gene editing efficiency.

Western blotting analysis

Mouse livers were collected at 24 h and on day 13 after the first injection of 2 mg/kg hOTC/LNP, as well as at 24 h and on day 13 after the second injection, and were snap-frozen on dry ice. To analyze ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) proteins from livers, liver tissues were lysed in radio immunoprecipitation assay lysate buffer (approximately 150 to 250 μL per 20 mg of tissue sample). The proteins in each sample were separated on SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels and analyzed by Western blotting. The antibodies used include mouse monoclonal antibody for human OTC (ThermoFisher, US, 1:2000), and rabbit polyclonal antibody for β-tubulin (Proteintech, US, 1: 5000). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG H&L and anti-mouse IgG H&L (Proteintech, US, 1:5000) secondary antibodies were used in Western blotting. Chemiluminescent reagents (Yamei, China) were used to visualize the protein signals under the LAS-3000 system (Fujifilm, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism. Data were presented as mean ± SD. Unpaired Student's t-test was used to analyze the statistical significance between two groups, while One-way ANOVA was used to analyze statistical differences among three or more groups. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All schemes were painted by using BioRender.

Results

The effect of phospholipid types on physicochemical properties of mRNA-LNPs

A standard LNP formulation composed of Lipid 5, DSPC, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG at a molar ratio of 50:10:38.5:1.5 was selected as the positive control. To systematically evaluate the role of helper phospholipids, we individually replaced DSPC with alternative phospholipids while keeping all other components constant. Specifically, DMPC (C14), DPPC (C16), and DSPC (C18) were used to investigate the effect of alkyl chain length, whereas DSPE, DOPC and DOPE were selected to assess the influence of headgroup identity (PC vs PE) and alkyl chain unsaturation. The physicochemical properties of the resulting LNPs were then characterized (Fig. 1A, Fig. S1).

Alkyl chain length significantly affected particle size. Substitution with the shorter-chain phospholipids DMPC and DPPC led to larger hydrodynamic diameters compared with DSPC-containing LNPs (DSPC: 90.1 ± 2.0 nm; DPPC: 112.9 ± 2.1 nm; DMPC: 97.2 ± 1.2 nm). In addition, DPPC-containing LNPs exhibited a modest but significant reduction in encapsulation efficiency relative to DSPC. By contrast, alkyl chain length had no measurable effect on polydispersity index (PDI) or zeta potential (Fig. 1B). Taken together, these findings indicated that alkyl chain length modulates LNP size and, to a lesser extent, encapsulation efficiency-likely through effects on lipid packing and membrane fluidity-while particle homogeneity and surface charge remain largely unaffected.

The degree of alkyl chain unsaturation also influenced particle size (Fig. 1C). LNPs formulated with saturated phospholipids (DSPC and DSPE) were significantly smaller (DSPC: 90.1 ± 2.0 nm; DSPE: 89.6 ± 2.4 nm) than those containing unsaturated phospholipids (DOPC: 103.0 ± 1.2 nm; DOPE: 116.4 ± 1.2 nm). However, unsaturation had minimal impact on PDI, zeta potential, or EE overall. Notably, DOPE-containing LNPs exhibited distinct physicochemical features compared to DOPC, including larger particle size (116.4 ± 1.2 nm, P < 0.0001), lower PDI (0.12 ± 0.02, P = 0.0486), and higher encapsulation efficiency (94.6 ± 1.9%, P = 0.0211).

Cryo-TEM analysis revealed pronounced differences in internal morphology depending on helper phospholipid composition (Fig. 1D and E). DOPC-containing LNPs were predominantly spherical and structurally homogeneous, with a minor fraction displaying concentric onion-like architectures. DOPE-containing LNPs primarily exhibited polyhedral morphologies. DSPC-based LNPs largely adopted multilamellar, onion-like internal structures, whereas DSPE-containing LNPs occasionally displayed a characteristic hemispherical morphology. In DPPC-formulated LNPs, one or two crescent-shaped voids were frequently observed. In contrast, DMPC promoted the formation of larger mRNA-deficient voids, generating vesicle-like compartments and occasionally producing a “snowman-like” morphology.

In addition, we determined the apparent pKa values of LNPs formulated with various helper lipids using a zeta potential-based titration method. As shown in Fig. S1, the DOPC-containing LNP exhibited the highest apparent pKa (7.8), whereas the DSPE-containing formulation yielded the lowest value (6.6). The remaining helper lipid formulations displayed intermediate pKa values ranging from 7.1 to 7.5.

Helper lipid structure differentially regulates LNP performance in vitro

We first evaluated cellular expression of LNPs formulated with six distinct helper phospholipids in AML12, Hep1-6, and HepG2 cells to assess LNP expression across hepatic cell types (Fig. 2A and Fig. S3A–B). DSPE-based LNPs consistently achieved the highest protein expression across all three cell lines. Among saturated PC phospholipids, shortening the alkyl chain progressively enhanced expression, with DMPC outperforming both DSPC (3.7-, 9.3-, and 3.7-fold in AML12, Hep1-6, and HepG2, respectively) and DPPC (1.5-, 2.1-, and 1.5-fold, respectively) (Fig. 2A and Fig. S3A). Correlation analysis revealed a strong inverse linear relationship between alkyl chain length and mRNA expression for saturated PC lipids (Fig. 2B and Fig. S3C).

The effects of unsaturation and headgroup identity on protein expression were analyzed individually (Fig. 2A and Fig. S3B). Within the PC series, unsaturated DOPC significantly enhanced expression compared with DSPC (30.5-fold in Hep1-6; 4.8-fold in HepG2). In contrast, within the PE series, saturated DSPE modestly outperformed DOPE (1.5-fold in AML12; 1.5-fold in Hep1-6). Under saturated conditions, PE-containing LNPs exhibited markedly higher expression than PC counterparts (3.8-fold in AML12; 49.4-fold in Hep1-6; 5.5-fold in HepG2) (Fig. 2A and Fig. S3B). However, when both phospholipids were unsaturated, the PE headgroup conferred a significant advantage over PC only in AML12 cells.

To explored the potential reasons behind LNP expression, we first investigated cellular uptake. Uptake efficiency varied substantially across formulations and cell lines (Fig. 2C–E and Fig. S3D–F). Overall, DOPC- and DSPE-containing LNPs exhibited consistently higher internalization, whereas DOPE-based LNPs showed the lowest uptake. Alkyl chain length exerted cell-type-dependent effects on uptake. In AML12 and HepG2 cells, DMPC significantly enhanced internalization compared to DSPC (AML12: 1.8-fold; HepG2: 1.4-fold) and DPPC (AML12: 1.7-fold; HepG2: 1.5-fold). In contrast, Hep1-6 cells preferentially internalized DSPC-containing LNPs over DMPC. The impact of unsaturation was strongly dependent on headgroup identity. Within the PC series, the presence of two double bonds (DOPC vs DSPC) markedly enhanced uptake across all three cell lines (4.8-fold in AML12 and Hep1-6; 3.6-fold in HepG2). Conversely, within the PE series, saturated DSPE exhibited higher uptake than unsaturated DOPE (3.6-fold in AML12; 3.3-fold in Hep1-6; 3.6-fold in HepG2). The cell line-dependent differences in uptake likely reflect variations in endocytic pathway activities [21], lysosomal pH [22], and protease capacity across hepatic cell types [23], underscoring the importance of using multiple cell models when evaluating helper lipid performance. To explore the molecular determinants of differential cellular uptake, we characterized the protein corona composition of the six LNP formulations, with particular focus on apolipoprotein E (ApoE), given its established role in mediating hepatocyte uptake via LDL receptor recognition. Notably, LNPs containing DOPC, DOPE, and DSPE exhibited relatively higher ApoE levels in the protein corona (Fig. 2F and Fig. S2). Correlation analysis revealed that ApoE abundance positively correlated with cellular uptake across the six formulations (Fig. 2G and Fig. S3G), suggesting that helper lipid structure may modulates ApoE adsorption, which in turn influences the extent of LNP internalization. This ApoE-dependent uptake pathway may partially account for the elevated cellular entry observed for DSPE- and DOPC-based LNPs.

Notably, despite higher cellular uptake of DOPC-containing LNPs compared with DSPE-based formulations, protein expression showed the opposite trend, with DOPC yielding lower expression. This discrepancy indicates that cellular internalization alone does not fully account for transfection efficiency. We therefore hypothesized that helper lipid structure may additionally influence endosomal escape, thereby modulating functional mRNA delivery. To test this hypothesis, endosomal escape was assessed in AML12 cells (Fig. 2H–I). DPPC-based LNPs showed significantly higher lysosomal retention than DSPC (P = 0.0181). In contrast, DOPE- and DSPE-containing LNPs exhibited lower lysosomal colocalization coefficients (r = 0.60 ± 0.02 and 0.55 ± 0.06, respectively), indicative of more efficient endosomal escape for PE-based LNP. Among the helper lipids, DSPE facilitated the greatest endosomal escape. However, these co-localization data alone provide correlative rather than causal evidence. To establish whether the observed escape efficiency is indeed driven by acid-dependent membrane destabilization, we performed a bafilomycin A1 experiment to functionally block endosomal acidification [24]. Notably, DSPE-LNPs exhibited the greatest sensitivity to endosomal acidification blockade among all six formulations, with expression reduced to levels significantly below those of DOPC-LNPs under bafilomycin A1 treatment (Fig. 2J). This finding is mechanistically consistent with DSPE's unique biophysical properties: its low apparent pKa (6.6) ensures extensive protonation only within the acidic endosomal lumen-a feature critical for ionizable lipid-mediated membrane destabilization and cytosolic release. Moreover, the conical geometry of the PE headgroup, which contrasts with the cylindrical shape of PC lipids, generates negative spontaneous curvature that thermodynamically favors the formation of fusogenic inverted hexagonal (HII) phases [25,26]. In contrast, DOPC-LNPs, with their high pKa (7.8), are already positively charged at physiological pH-driving strong cellular uptake-but lack the pH-responsive protonation gradient required for efficient endosomal escape, explaining their minimal sensitivity to bafilomycin A1 and their poor functional delivery despite high internalization.

Impact of helper lipids on LNP performance in vivo with a weekly dosing interval

To assess the impact of helper phospholipids under repeated administration, we intravenously administered Luciferase mRNA LNPs (1 mg/kg) to BALB/c mice at one-week interval for four consecutive doses (Fig. 3A). Following each injection, we evaluated in vivo Luciferase expression, anti-PEG IgM and IgG, liver enzymes (ALT, AST), and inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α).

To confirm whether LNPs containing different helper lipids are predominantly expressed in the liver following intravenous administration, we performed a single-dose injection and collected organs at 6 h post-administration for ex vivo imaging. The results confirm that luciferase expression is predominantly localized to the liver, with only minimal signals detected in the spleen and negligible signals in the lungs, heart, and kidneys across all helper lipid groups (Fig. S4).

Having established that all formulations direct expression primarily to the liver, we next compared their relative mRNA expression efficiency under repeated administration. The helper lipid markedly influenced mRNA expression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3B). Protein expression decreased with the repeated dosing of mRNA/LNP, which could be attributed to anti-PEG antibody, as previously reported [18]. Regarding alkyl chain length, a significant inverse correlation with protein expression was observed (R2 = 0.5808, P = 0.004; Fig. S5A–B). Specifically, DMPC achieved 5.2-fold and 3.2-fold greater expression than DSPC and DPPC, respectively, upon the first administration (Fig. 3C). This suggests that shorter alkyl chains favor mRNA expression. Following the second and third injections, DMPC and DPPC yielded similar expression intensities, both exhibiting higher bioluminescence signals than DSPC.

With respect to headgroup and saturation, we observed that the PE headgroup conferred more robust expression than the PC headgroup following the first and third doses (Fig. 3D). Across the first three injections, DSPE-formulated LNPs consistently produced the highest Luciferase expression among all groups. Relative to the standard DSPC formulation, DSPE enhanced expression by 20-fold (P = 0.0005), 14-fold (P = 0.0014), and 27-fold (P = 0.0008) after the first, second, and third administrations, respectively (Fig. 3D). The decline in expression after the fourth dose suggested possible anti-PEG antibody involvement. We therefore measured anti-PEG IgM/IgG levels.

IgM levels gradually increased with successive administrations, but showed no significant difference among the first three doses. However, after the final dose, longer alkyl chains (DSPC, DPPC) induced higher IgM levels than the shorter-chain DMPC (Fig. 3E), and DOPE elicited the highest anti-PEG IgM levels (Fig. 3F). No significant IgG levels were detected (Fig. S6A–B).

We next investigated the impact of helper lipid on serum ALT and AST levels. Interestingly, no significant elevation beyond the normal physiological range (ALT: 15-84 U/L; AST: 54-298 U/L for Balb/c mice) was observed after the first two rounds of administrations, whereas the third injection elicited the most pronounced ALT level, indicating a non-monotonic response profile that peaks at the third dose, followed by a decline. This pattern suggests that liver function markers may not be adequately captured by single-administration studies (Fig. 3G–H, Fig. S6C–D). From the perspective of helper lipid structure, a short alkyl chain (DMPC) demonstrated reduced ALT/AST responses compared with long alkyl chains (DPPC and DSPC) (Fig. 3G, Fig. S6C). Additionally, the PE headgroup combined with two double bonds (DOPE) exhibited lowest ALT compared to other combinations (Fig. 3H).

Moreover, inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α were assessed, as mRNA/LNP is a well-known inflammatory agent [20,27] (Fig. 3I–J, Fig. S8). Previous studies have demonstrated that ionizable lipids are the major source of inflammation [27], but we found that helper lipids also modulate the inflammatory response in this study. Under weekly administration, DSPC, which is approved for use in commercial mRNA vaccine products, exhibited the highest IL-6 levels among the helper lipids (Fig. 3I), suggesting that materials traditionally used in vaccines may not be suitable for therapies requiring low inflammatory responses and frequent dosing, such as protein replacement therapies. Additionally, we found that the inflammatory response was also cumulative, with higher IL-6 observed after multiple administrations, and that IL-6 levels were positively correlated with alkyl chain length across administrations (Fig. S7A–D). Furthermore, we found that both changing the headgroup from PC to PE and increasing double bond enabled reduced IL-6 level compared to DSPC across the dosing period (Fig. 3J). In contrast to IL-6, TNF-α exhibited the opposite trend, with TNF-α level generally decreased with repeated administration (Fig. S8). Moreover, the differences in TNF-α associated with alkyl chain length, headgroup, and saturation were less significant than those observed for IL-6. Nevertheless, higher alkyl chain length consistently correlated with higher TNF-α, consistent with the pattern observed for IL-6.

Impact of helper lipids on LNP performance under a biweekly dosing interval

In contrast to the weekly-interval regimen, prolonging the injection interval (Fig. 4A) enhanced mRNA expression after multiple administrations, with comparable bioluminescence intensity observed across doses. Consistent with the weekly dosing schedule, Luciferase expression exhibited a significant inverse correlation with helper lipid alkyl chain length (second: R2 = 0.4635, P = 0.0148; third: R2 = 0.4908, P = 0.0112; Fig. S9), indicating that shorter chains favor higher protein production. Additionally, DSPE-containing LNPs consistently produced the highest Luciferase expression during the second and third administrations (Fig. 4B–D). Relative to DSPC, DSPE enhanced expression by 5.1-fold (P = 0.0017), and 3.6-fold (P = 0.0008) after the second, and third doses, respectively (Fig. 4B–D). In addition, saturated (DS) helper lipids generally supported greater expression than their unsaturated (DO) counterparts at third and fourth administrations (Fig. 4D).

Regarding IgM, prolonging the dose interval reduced IgM production after the fourth dose, especially for DMPC, DPPC, and DSPC, which may explain the enhanced mRNA expression (Fig. 4E). For different headgroup and double bond combinations, although reduced IgM was observed, the IgM level of DOPC, DOPE, and DSPE still slightly increase as administration progressed (Fig. 4F), consistent with the gradually reduced Luciferase expression observed in Fig. 4D. Additionally, no significant differences in IgG were observed between groups (Fig. S10A–B).

Unlike weekly administration, ALT and AST levels were most pronounced after the second dose, indicating that the liver response is dependent on the administration interval (Fig. 4G–H, Fig. S10C–D). For the two-week interval, alkyl chain length exhibited no significant difference (Fig. 4G), whereas DOPE-based LNP maintained stable ALT levels compared with the significantly elevated ALT or AST observed for DOPC and DSPE (Fig. 4H). For inflammatory signals, IL-6 levels increased with alkyl chain length (Fig. 4I and Fig. S11), and helper lipids with saturated alkyl chains (DSPC and DSPE) promoted IL-6 production (Fig. 4J). In contrast, TNF-α levels were minimally affected by helper lipid composition, except that DPPC demonstrated significantly higher TNF-α levels than DSPC and DMPC (Fig. S12).

Impact of helper lipids on LNP performance under a triweekly dosing interval

The triweekly dosing interval demonstrated a similar expression profile to the biweekly interval (Fig. 4K–N). Specifically, longer alkyl chain length reduced protein translation (Fig. 4M), and DSPE/LNP showed the highest bioluminescence signal during the first three doses. However, this advantage diminished after the fourth injection (Fig. 4N). Interestingly, regarding anti-PEG antibody, minimal difference was observed in IgM (Fig. 4O–P), whereas IgG levels were robustly elicited after four cycles of dosing, a phenomenon not observed previously, indicating that a different antibody type is involved in anti-PEG clearance (Fig. S13A–B). Unlike IgM, IgG can engage Fcγ receptors on hepatic macrophages to facilitate LNP clearance [28]. However, since both DSPC- and DSPE-based LNPs elicited comparable IgG levels, this Fc-mediated mechanism alone does not fully explain the diminished DSPE advantage. It is more likely that antibody-mediated reduction in hepatic LNP exposure limits the intracellular endosomal escape benefit of DSPE.

For ALT/AST levels, the three-week interval showed a similar pattern to the two-week interval, with the second dose eliciting the most pronounced ALT/AST elevation (Fig. 4Q–R, Fig. S13C–D). Additionally, DOPE/LNP induced significantly higher ALT and AST levels, which differs from the pattern observed with weekly or two-week injections, indicating that the effect of helper lipids on ALT/AST levels depends on the dosing interval.

Unlike the more frequent dosing schedules, IL-6 production did not correlate with alkyl chain length under this regimen (Fig. 4S). Instead, IL-6 responses varied in a time-dependent manner: DMPC (C14) induced the highest levels after the second administration, whereas DSPC (C18) elicited the strongest response after the third, significantly exceeding other groups at that time point (Fig. 4S and T). Overall, IL-6 modulation appeared temporally dynamic rather than structurally governed. TNF-α levels were largely unaffected by helper lipid composition, with no significant intergroup differences detected. However, following the second administration, saturated lipids (DSPC and DSPE) exhibited a higher trend compared with unsaturated counterparts (DOPC and DOPE) (Fig. S13E and F).

Influence of dosing interval on LNP performance

To systematically evaluate the effect of dosing interval on LNP performance, percentage-changes relative to the first administration were analyzed for hepatic Luciferase expression, anti-PEG IgM and IgG across repeated dosing cycles. Weekly administration resulted in progressive expression declines after the second, third, and fourth doses for all formulations (Fig. 5A, Fig. S14A–C). In contrast, extending the interval to two or three weeks generally produced comparable or enhanced expression upon subsequent administrations, with the notable exception of DSPE, whose expression plateaued or slightly declined under longer intervals, possibly due to antibody-mediated reduction in hepatic LNP exposure that limits its intracellular endosomal escape advantage, along with potential PE-specific immune recognition. Regarding humoral responses, prolonging the dosing interval reduced anti-PEG IgM production across most formulations, with no consistent lipid-specific differences (Fig. 5B, Fig. S15A–C); weekly dosing facilitated the highest IgM levels. For IgG, longer intervals enhanced elicitation, an effect especially pronounced for DSPC, which showed the highest levels under the triweekly schedule (Fig. 5C, Fig. S15D–F).

Serum ALT and AST levels were influenced by both dosing interval and administration number. Weekly dosing exhibited cumulative ALT/AST elevation after four doses, whereas prolonging the interval alleviated this phenomenon (Fig. 5D, Fig. S16A-F, Fig. S17). IL-6 responses showed lipid- and interval-dependent patterns: prolonged intervals increased IL-6 levels, particularly under the 3-week schedule (Fig. 5E, Fig. S18A–C). Dosing every two weeks produced the lowest IL-6 levels; among these, DSPC, DMPC, DPPC, and DOPC LNPs showed reduced IL-6, whereas DOPE- and DSPE-formulations induced elevated IL-6. TNF-α levels decreased relative to the first dose under all conditions (Fig. 5F, Fig. S18D–F).

Impact of helper lipids on inter-parameter correlations

We next examined correlations among protein expression, anti-PEG responses, liver function markers, and inflammatory signals across different helper lipid formulations and dosing schedules (Fig. 5G–I). Under weekly dosing, Luciferase expression generally correlated negatively with IgM and IL-6, notably for DOPE, DSPE, and DMPC (IgM) and DMPC (IL-6) (Fig. S19A–D). Positive correlations with TNF-α were observed for DOPE and DPPC (Fig. S19E and F). IgM correlated negatively with TNF-α. The ALT/IL-6 relationship was lipid-dependent: positive for DSPE and DMPC (Fig. S19G), negative for DSPC (Fig. S19H). ALT correlated negatively with TNF-α, especially for DSPC (Fig. S19I).

Under biweekly dosing, the negative correlation between protein expression and IgM was substantially altered: only C18 lipids with PE headgroups (DOPE, DSPE) retained significant negative correlation; DSPC lipids showed weak negative correlation; shorter-chain DMPC lipid exhibited positive correlation (Fig. 5H). PE headgroups maintained a positive protein/TNF-α correlation, whereas PC headgroups showed an inverted (negative) correlation (Fig. S19J). At three-week intervals, most correlations disappeared, suggesting that the interplay among in vivo performance metrics is dependent on the dosing interval (Fig. 5I, Fig. S19K).

Effect of DSPE substitution on in vivo luciferase mRNA expression across different ionizable lipids and application in gene editing and protein replacement therapy

To determine whether the enhanced expression associated with DSPE depends on the ionizable lipid component, we systematically replaced DSPC with DSPE while maintaining a fixed formulation (Fig. 6A–B). Aligned with Lipid-5, substitution with DSPE significantly increased in vivo Luciferase mRNA expression for both ALC-0315 and MC3. Specifically, incorporation of DSPE into ALC-0315-based LNPs resulted in a 1.6-fold increase relative to DSPC (P = 0.0211, Fig. 6A). A more pronounced enhancement was observed for MC3-based formulations (3.1-fold, P = 0.0107, Fig. 6B). These results demonstrate that substitution of DSPC with DSPE consistently enhances mRNA expression in vivo, independent of the ionizable lipid used, indicating that the performance advantage conferred by DSPE is driven by its structural properties and is broadly applicable across LNP platforms.

To further investigate the performance of DSPE-based LNPs in gene editing and protein replacement therapy, we employed DSPE-based LNPs to deliver Cas9 mRNA/sgRNA for PCSK9 knockdown (Fig. 6C) and human OTC mRNA for exogenous OTC protein expression (Fig. 6F) in mice. At 14 days after gene editing treatment, both DSPC- and DSPE-based LNPs significantly reduced serum PCSK9 levels compared with the PBS group. DSPE reduced serum PCSK9 levels by 91.5%, 78.6%, and 99.4% relative to DSPC at days 5, 8, and 14 post-administrations, respectively (Fig. 6D). Meanwhile, the in vivo editing efficiency of PCSK9 achieved nearly 60% by DSPE-based LNPs, which was 4-fold higher than that of DSPC-based LNPs (P = 0.0329, Fig. 6E).

For protein replacement therapy, DSPE-based formulations consistently yielded higher human OTC expression compared to DSPC. Specifically, DSPE produced 6.2-fold (P = 0.0346) and 3.8-fold (P = 0.0034) increases in OTC protein levels relative to DSPC at 1 and 13 days post-first dose, respectively. Following the second dose, DSPE further achieved 11.2-fold (P = 0.0201) and 2.8-fold (P = 0.0035) higher OTC expression at the same time points (Fig. 6G–H).

Discussion

Since their successful application during the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic, LNPs have been extensively utilized in various disease treatments. However, most non-vaccine applications still rely on vaccine-derived formulations, which may be suboptimal for chronic protein replacement therapies requiring repeated administration to diseased hepatocytes. In this study, we systematically investigated how helper lipid structure affects LNP performance under single and repeated intravenous dosing, focusing on expression, anti-PEG antibodies, liver enzyme responses, and inflammatory cytokines (Fig. S20).

We found that protein expression is negatively correlated with alkyl chain length, consistent with previous studies [17], and that DSPE-based LNPs consistently achieve the highest expression across all dosing intervals. Although Guo et al. reported that DSPE showed no significant difference compared to helper lipids such as DOPC and DMPC in MC3-based LNPs [17], this discrepancy may be attributed to differences in preparation methods, which lead to different physicochemical properties and in vivo behavior [29]. DSPE's superior performance arises from a three-step cooperative mechanism: enhanced cellular uptake, pH-triggered protonation, and membrane destabilization. Upstream, DSPE-LNPs adsorb higher levels of ApoE compared to other formulations. This enhanced ApoE recruitment promotes hepatocyte uptake via LDL receptor recognition, providing an initial advantage in cellular internalization. However, uptake alone does not fully account for DSPE's functional superiority. The key differentiator lies in its pH-responsive membrane destabilization. The PE headgroup adopts a cone-shaped geometry, contrasting with the cylindrical shape of PC lipids. This conical structure generates negative spontaneous curvature that thermodynamically favors the formation of fusogenic inverted hexagonal (HII) phase which is a non-lamellar arrangement that inherently promotes membrane fusion [25,26]. Complementing this structural predisposition, DSPE-LNPs exhibit a low apparent pKa of 6.6, closely matching the acidic endosomal pH. This ensures that DSPE-LNPs remain largely neutral in circulation but become extensively protonated within endosomes, triggering electrostatic interactions with the anionic endosomal membrane and cooperative membrane disruption. To validate the causal role of endosomal acidification in DSPE's advantage, we performed a bafilomycin A1 experiment. Pharmacological blockade of endosomal acidification reduced DSPE-LNP expression to levels significantly below those of DOPC-LNPs. This finding proves that DSPE's functional superiority is specifically dependent on acid-triggered escape.

The lower hepatic expression of DSPC-containing LNPs after the first intravenous dose (Fig. 3B) is noteworthy given DSPC's widespread use in approved formulations. However, several studies have shown that DSPC is not universally optimal for intravenous hepatic delivery. Kauffman et al. systematically screened LNP formulations using Design of Experiment methodologies and found that replacing DSPC with DOPE as the helper phospholipid improved hepatic mRNA delivery potency [30]. Similarly, Guo et al. demonstrated that substituting the helper lipid DSPC in commercial LNPs with POPC resulted in a notable enhancement of mRNA delivery in hepatocytes [17]. These findings from independent groups collectively support our observation that DSPC, despite its widespread use, is not inherently the most effective helper phospholipid for intravenous hepatic mRNA delivery. Moreover, other studies have examined helper lipid effects in various contexts: Cheng et al. comprehensively reviewed the role of helper lipids in oligonucleotide delivery, emphasizing their influence on membrane fluidity and phase behavior [15]; Kawaguchi et al. recently demonstrated that helper lipid composition modulates both immunogenicity and reactogenicity of mRNA-LNP vaccines [31]; and Oguma et al. showed that phospholipid type affects LNP biodistribution to pancreatic islets [14]. Collectively, these studies support our finding that helper lipids are active determinants of LNP performance, though systematic comparisons under repeated dosing have been lacking prior to this work.

The advantage of DSPE over DSPC diminished with repeated injections under weekly dosing, likely due to anti-PEG IgM. However, prolonging the interval to two or three weeks improved expression for PC-headgroup lipids but not for PE-based LNPs, suggesting that additional immune responses (possibly against the PE headgroup itself) may contribute to the decline. However, this interpretation is currently inferred from correlative observations. Future studies will need to perform functional antibody assays (e.g., complement activation, neutralizing activity, or accelerated blood clearance in passive transfer experiments) to provide direct evidence.

The emergence of anti-PEG IgG under the triweekly regimen (Fig. S13A–B) is relevant for long-term therapy. Unlike IgM, IgG has a longer half-life and can engage Fcγ receptors on hepatic macrophages to facilitate LNP clearance [28]. For chronic protein replacement therapies requiring repeated administrations over extended periods, IgG-mediated clearance could pose a more sustained barrier than the transient IgM response. Under the triweekly regimen, both DSPC- and DSPE-based LNPs induced comparable IgG levels (Fig. S13A–B). Despite this, the expression advantage of DSPE over DSPC diminished after the fourth dose (Fig. 4N). This apparent paradox can be resolved by considering the nature of DSPE's advantage and the mechanism of IgG-mediated clearance. DSPE's functional benefit operates at the intracellular level-specifically, by enhancing endosomal escape. However, anti-PEG IgG reduces the total quantity of LNPs that reach hepatocytes through Fcγ receptor-mediated clearance. Once hepatic LNP exposure falls below a critical threshold due to this clearance, the intracellular endosomal escape advantage of DSPE becomes functionally limited-there is simply less LNP available for its superior escape machinery to act upon. In contrast, DSPC-based LNPs, which have intrinsically lower expression, experience a decline that is proportionally less noticeable; their baseline is already low, leaving less "room" for further reduction. Additionally, PE headgroups may elicit immune recognition beyond PEG, which could further compound clearance for PE-based formulations like DSPE.

Helper lipids also modulate IL-6 levels, though the effect is highly dependent on the dosing regimen. Under weekly administration, DSPC, which is used in commercial mRNA vaccines, elicited the highest IL-6 levels, consistent with previous research [31], indicating that vaccine-optimized lipids may be suboptimal for protein replacement requiring frequent dosing. IL-6 induction increased with alkyl chain length under weekly and biweekly schedules. However, this chain length-dependent pattern was not observed under the triweekly regimen, where IL-6 responses were temporally dynamic and varied across helper lipids (Fig. 4S–T), suggesting that the IL-6 levels induced by helper lipids are modulated by the dosing interval. This context-dependent nature of IL-6 induction should be considered when selecting helper lipids for chronic therapeutic applications [32].

The observed fluctuations in serum ALT and AST levels were also interval- and lipid-dependent, possibly reflecting unintended immune responses elicited by LNPs [20,33,34]. However, it is important to note that the measured ALT and AST levels remained largely within the normal physiological range for Balb/c mice (ALT: 15-84 U/L; AST: 54-298 U/L) [35]. Weekly dosing resulted in a cumulative ALT/AST elevation pattern that peaked at the third injection, while longer intervals reduced this response. Collectively, our data do not support the conclusion that these LNP formulations induced hepatotoxicity or liver damage, but rather suggest that they elicit transient, dose-interval-dependent fluctuations in liver enzyme levels.

Conclusions

Collectively, these findings establish helper phospholipids as key determinants of LNP performance under repeated administration, providing a rationale for their further mechanistic investigation and optimization in chronic hepatic protein replacement therapies. Future studies should perform functional antibody assays to directly confirm whether anti-PEG IgM contributes to the diminished advantage of PE-based LNPs under repeated dosing and systematically evaluate PE analogs-particularly DMPE (C14, PE), which combines the beneficial features of PE headgroups and shorter alkyl chains to define the optimal acyl chain composition for chronic delivery. Furthermore, the observed interval-dependent effects on liver enzyme responses and cytokine profiles highlight the need to tailor dosing schedules to specific helper lipid compositions in clinical translation ().
