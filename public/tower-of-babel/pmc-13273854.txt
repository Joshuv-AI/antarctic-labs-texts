Oncocytic adrenal adenoma: Diagnostic challenge of an incidentaloma; a case report
Authors: Mohamed Mehdi Kamoun, Zied Hadrich, Sahir Omrani, Rached Bayar, Ahlem Lahmar, Dhouha Bacha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adrenal oncocytic neoplasms (AONs) are rare non-functioning adrenal tumors with difficult preoperative diagnosis. We report a 40-year-old man with an 11 cm adrenal mass, negative hormonal workup, and imaging findings. The patient underwent open adrenalectomy. Histopathology showed oncocytic adenoma with borderline/uncertain malignant potential per Lin–Weiss–Bisceglia criteria (size >10 cm, necrosis). Postoperative course was uneventful, with no recurrence at 18 months. AONs should be considered in large adrenal incidentalomas; complete resection is essential for diagnosis, risk stratification, and follow-up.

Introduction

Adrenal oncocytic neoplasms are rare and usually non-functioning tumors, most often discovered incidentally during imaging. Despite their generally benign behavior, large tumors may pose a diagnostic dilemma due to their uncertain malignant potential and non-specific radiological features. In addition, large right-sided adrenal masses may occasionally mimic hepatic lesions on imaging, making accurate localization challenging.

We report the case of a right adrenal oncocytoma in a male patient.

Case report

A 40-year-old male with a recent diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus presented with complaints of right flank and lumbar pain of several weeks’ duration. There were no associated gastrointestinal, urinary, or systemic symptoms. Physical examination was unremarkable, with no palpable masses or signs of endocrine dysfunction.

Laboratory evaluation demonstrated a suppressed cortisol level following the 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test (<1.8 μg/dL). Plasma metanephrines, aldosterone-to-renin ratio, DHEA-S levels, and 24-h urinary catecholamines were all within normal limits. Additionally, tumor markers including CEA, CA-19-9, and AFP were normal. These findings were consistent with a non-functioning adrenal mass.

Abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) revealed a spontaneously hypodense, exophytic mass originating probably from segment VI of the liver. The lesion measured 10.5 cm in greatest dimension, showed mild hypervascularity in the arterial phase, and was hypovascular during the portal venous phase (Fig. 1). Abdominal MRI demonstrated a large heterogeneous right adrenal mass measuring 11 cm, predominantly hypointense on T2-weighted fat-suppressed images with multiple cystic areas. Chemical shift imaging showed no significant signal loss on opposed-phase images, suggesting the absence of intracellular lipid. (Fig. 2). Biochemical investigations were performed, including serum cortisol, plasma metanephrines, aldosterone-renin ratio, and androgens, all of which were within normal limits, confirming a non-functioning adrenal mass.Fig. 2MRI findings Red arrow: heterogeneous, T2-hypointense mass occupying the right adrenal fossa.

Under general anesthesia, an open transabdominal right adrenalectomy was performed through a Makuuchi incision. Intraoperative findings confirmed an 11 cm right adrenal mass without invasion of surrounding structures. The right hepatic lobe was mobilized, and a Kocher maneuver was carried out to facilitate exposure of the adrenal region. The right adrenal gland was dissected and removed en bloc, with careful identification, clipping, and division of the adrenal vein. No drains were placed. The operative time was approximately 120 minutes, and the estimated blood loss was less than 150 mL. (Fig. 3). Postoperative care included multimodal analgesia and prophylactic anticoagulation. Glycemic control was managed with an insulin correction protocol due to the patient's underlying diabetes. Oral intake was gradually resumed, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 3 in stable condition. No clinical features of adrenal insufficiency were observed during the postoperative period, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 3.Fig. 3Surgical specimen.

Follow-up imaging with CT scans was performed at 3, 6, 12, and 18 months postoperatively, demonstrating no evidence of local recurrence or metastatic disease. The patient remained disease-free throughout the follow-up period. Given the uncertain malignant potential of these tumors and the lack of standardized follow-up guidelines, surveillance will be continued for at least 5 years to monitor for late recurrence.

Discussion

Adrenal oncocytic neoplasms (AONs) are extremely rare tumors, first described by Kakimoto et al., in 1986, who reported the initial case of an adrenal oncocytic adenoma in a female patient.2 These tumors are characterized by their oncocytic cellular morphology, marked by abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and increased mitochondrial content.

A comprehensive literature review published in 2023 identified 286 reported cases of adrenal oncocytic adenomas worldwide, highlighting their rarity.3 The review also found that these tumors exhibit a marked female predominance, are predominantly non-functioning, and are frequently discovered incidentally during imaging for unrelated conditions. Most patients are asymptomatic, although some may present with non-specific symptoms such as abdominal or flank pain, as seen in our patient.

Due to their rarity and often indolent clinical course, preoperative diagnosis of AONs remains challenging. Imaging modalities such as CT and MRI can suggest adrenal origin and provide information about vascularity and size, but are generally not specific enough to differentiate AONs from other adrenal masses. The differential diagnoses considered included adrenocortical carcinoma, pheochromocytoma, metastatic adrenal involvement. Although the imaging characteristics raised suspicion for an oncocytic tumor, this diagnosis could not be confirmed preoperatively. Therefore, surgical resection is often indicated, especially in large tumors, to obtain a definitive diagnosis and to rule out malignancy. In this case, surgery was justified by size (>4 cm), atypical imaging, and need for definitive diagnosis.

An important educational aspect of this case is the initial radiologic misinterpretation of the lesion. On CT imaging, the large exophytic right adrenal mass was initially considered to be a probable hepatic segment VI lesion because of its size and close anatomical relationship with the liver. MRI played a crucial role in establishing the adrenal origin of the tumor and refining the diagnostic workup. This case highlights an underrecognized diagnostic pitfall in adrenal imaging, whereby large right-sided adrenal masses may mimic hepatic lesions on cross-sectional imaging. Awareness of this possibility is important to avoid misdiagnosis and to guide appropriate preoperative evaluation and surgical planning.

Preoperative adrenal biopsy was not performed, in accordance with the 2023 European Society of Endocrinology guidelines.4 Given the large size (11 cm) and heterogeneous appearance of the adrenal mass, surgical resection was considered the most appropriate management. Furthermore, adrenal biopsy is not recommended in patients without a history of extra-adrenal malignancy.

Histologically, the (LWB) system is the most widely accepted classification for oncocytic adrenal tumors, categorizing them as benign, of uncertain malignant potential, or malignant based on major and minor criteria.1 In our case, the tumor fulfilled two minor criteria—size >10 cm and focal necrosis—classifying it as having uncertain malignant potential.

There are no standard post-operative surveillance recommendations for AONs. The Lin-Weiss-Bisceglia criteria remain the gold standard. Recent reviews3 show excellent prognosis for borderline lesions, with recurrence rates <5%.Given AON with uncertain malignant potential, we opted for CT scan every 3 months for up to 5 years informed by clinical guidelines for adrenal cortical carcinoma follow-up5: Nevertheless, the planned surveillance schedule could not be fully implemented in clinical practice. Follow-up imaging was ultimately performed at 3, 6, 12, and 18 months after surgery and showed no signs of local recurrence or distant disease. However, limited evidence from systematic reviews suggests that in comparison to ACCs, AONs with BUMP have markedly better prognosis and lower rate of recurrence.6

Conclusion

Oncocytic adrenal neoplasms, though rare, should be considered when evaluating large adrenal masses with atypical features. Surgery remains essential to confirm the diagnosis and assess the risk of malignancy. In cases with uncertain malignant potential, as classified by the Lin–Weiss–Bisceglia system, a tailored follow-up is recommended despite the generally favorable prognosis.
