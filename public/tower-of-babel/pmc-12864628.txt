Integrating multi-omics data and machine learning to identify endocrine disrupting chemicals targeting key ccRCC-related genes
Authors: Mengzhuo Zheng, Debiao Wang, Bo Guan, Liyu Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is a highly aggressive and metastatic malignancy that poses a serious threat to patient health. While its development involves both genetic and environmental influences, the interactions between genetic susceptibility and endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) remain poorly understood. In this study, we integrated multi-omics datasets and applied machine learning approaches to identify EDCs and their associated target genes implicated in ccRCC pathogenesis. We analyzed bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing data and evaluated 101 machine learning algorithms to construct a robust prognostic model. This analysis identified 8 EDCs potentially involved in ccRCC: Diethylnitrosamine, Diethylstilbestrol, Resveratrol, 4,4’-diaminodiphenylmethane, Trichloroethylene, Arsenic, Lead, and 2,3,5-(triglutathion-S-yl)hydroquinone. 5 EDC-associated prognostic genes-BIRC5, CCND1, RRM2, CDH1, and TRIB3-were also identified. Pathway enrichment and immune infiltration analyses revealed intricate interactions between these genes and the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, cell-cell communication analysis revealed distinct signaling patterns among EDC-associated subpopulations, offering new insights into how EDCs may modulate gene expression and contribute to ccRCC progression. This study provides a foundation for future investigations into EDC-driven tumor biology and may guide the development of targeted therapies and preventative strategies against ccRCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04138-y.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is one of the top ten most common cancers worldwide, encompassing a heterogeneous group of tumors that originate from renal tubular epithelial cells [13]. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most prevalent subtype, accounting for approximately 75% of all RCC cases [8]. Although early-stage ccRCC is typically amenable to surgical resection or ablation with favorable outcomes, approximately one-third of patients eventually develop metastatic disease [16]. Advances in genomic sequencing have revealed several critical genetic alterations, including loss of chromosome 3p [34], and biallelic inactivation of the VHL gene [20], both of which are considered key drivers of ccRCC initiation and progression. Furthermore, the development of ccRCC is influenced by a multifaceted interplay of genetic predisposition, lifestyle choices, dietary patterns, occupational exposures, and environmental factors [7].

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) are hormone-like substances widely present in the environment that interfere with the synthesis, transport, metabolism, regulation, or degradation of endogenous hormones. By disrupting normal hormonal functions, EDCs can exert a wide range of adverse effects on development, reproduction, immune response, endocrine regulation, and nervous system function [14]. As major environmental pollutants, EDCs are commonly found in wastewater, textiles, industrial byproducts, and agricultural runoff. Due to their environmental persistence and atmospheric mobility, they can infiltrate human living environments, posing significant public health risks. EDCs exert their effects through three primary mechanisms. First, they can interact with hormone receptors, including nuclear receptors (e.g., estrogen receptor, androgen receptor, and progesterone receptor) and non-nuclear receptors (e.g., membrane receptors and neurotransmitter receptors). Studies have shown that EDCs not only engage classical hormone receptors but also modulate cellular functions via non-genomic steroid receptors, such as membrane estrogen receptors, and orphan receptors [11, 21]. Second, EDCs can interfere with enzyme-catalyzed pathways. For instance, some polychlorinated biphenyl (PCB) metabolites have been found to inhibit sulfotransferases, leading to elevated circulating estradiol levels and influencing disease development [2, 4]. Furthermore, EDCs can affect disease progression by regulating signaling pathways. For example, bisphenol A (BPA) and phthalates have been shown to induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a process closely associated with tumor invasion and metastasis [45]. Given these mechanisms, the identification, surveillance, and regulation of EDCs are essential to reduce their detrimental impact on human health and the environment.

In recent years, machine learning has emerged as a powerful tool in biomedical research, owing to its capacity to identify critical features and make accurate predictions from complex biomedical and bioinformatics datasets. These algorithms have been widely applied to disease prognosis and classification, providing researchers and clinicians with deeper insights into disease mechanisms and supporting the development of personalized treatment strategies. Notably, several robust RCC prognostic models based on large-scale cohort data have already been implemented in clinical settings [17, 24]. However, in the context of ccRCC, the intricate relationships among EDCs, gene expression, and disease progression remain poorly characterized. Addressing this gap is essential, as understanding how EDCs influence gene regulation and contribute to ccRCC initiation and development could inform the design of more effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

This study integrates bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing with machine learning to investigate how endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) influence gene expression and contribute to ccRCC. By evaluating 101 machine learning algorithms, we developed a robust prognostic model (RSF + CoxBoost) with strong generalizability (5-year AUC = 0.71). Unlike prior studies that rely mainly on bulk transcriptomics, our approach uniquely incorporates single-cell RNA-seq to identify EDC-related gene expression within specific cell types (e.g., epithelial cells, macrophages) and to reveal their metabolic heterogeneity and cell-cell communication via contact and secretory pathways. We further identified eight EDCs and five key prognostic genes (BIRC5, CCND1, RRM2, CDH1, TRIB3), and linked them to activation of JAK-STAT/MAPK signaling, enhanced tumor stemness, and immune remodeling (e.g., increased Tregs and M0 macrophages). These findings provide novel, mechanistic insights into how EDCs contribute to ccRCC across molecular and cellular scales. Given the subtle and multifactorial nature of EDC exposure, its biological impact is difficult to assess. Our integrative, cross-scale framework addresses this challenge by combining multi-omics data with rigorous algorithmic evaluation, thereby extending previous environmental cancer research and offering a foundation for future studies on EDC-related carcinogenesis and intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Bulk RNA-seq and ScRNA-seq data collection and pre-processing

RNA expression data from GSE15641 and GSE16449 were downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE15641 dataset consists of 23 normal samples and 32 ccRCC samples, with RNA expression profiles obtained using the GPL96 [HG-U133A] Affymetrix Human Genome U133A Array. The GSE16449 dataset contains 18 normal samples and 48 ccRCC samples, with RNA expression profiles generated using the GPL6480 Agilent-014850 Whole Human Genome Microarray 4 × 44 K G4112F. Gene expression and survival data for the TCGA-KIRC cohort were retrieved from the TCGA database (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga). Gene expression and survival data for the E-MTAB-1980 cohort were obtained from the EMBL-EBI database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/). To address batch effects in the GSE15641, GSE16449, and TCGA-KIRC datasets, the removeBatchEffect function from the Limma package was applied [28]. 2 ccRCC and 1 normal tissue single-cell RNA sequencing samples were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [32]. Single cell RNA-seq analysis was conducted using the “Seurat” R package [12]. To ensure data integrity, strict cell selection criteria were applied, particularly excluding cells with abnormally high expression levels or excessive sequencing depth. These criteria included a minimum RNA count (nCount_RNA) of 1000 to reduce the inclusion of cells with very low expression levels, and an upper limit for RNA feature counts (nFeature_RNA) set below 5000 to avoid cells with excessively high expression. To minimize the influence of mitochondrial RNA, a threshold for the mitochondrial RNA percentage (%MT) was set below 30%. Additionally, to ensure a comprehensive representation of the gene expression profile, the number of features (nFeature_RNA) was required to exceed 600. The filtered cell populations were subjected to dimensionality reduction clustering using UMAP, and the resulting clusters were annotated using marker genes specific to each cell population.

Identification of EDC-related differential genes and functional enrichment analysis

Differential expression analysis between tumor and normal groups was performed using the Limma package, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 and |logFC| >1 to identify and confirm differentially expressed genes(DEGs) that distinguish ccRCC from healthy controls. Gene ontology (GO) analysis was conducted using the “clusterProfiler” R package to elucidate the biological functions and pathways associated with commonly expressed genes, with a significance threshold of p.adj < 0.05. Genes with an “Inference Score > 45” were filtered from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) to identify key genes and chemicals relevant to ccRCC. The intersection of genes from the CTD and DEGs was used to identify common genes associated with environmental pollutants impacting ccRCC. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed using the “coxph” function in the R package “survival” with a p-value threshold of 0.01, to identify prognostic genes associated with the prognosis of ccRCC.

101 machine learning algorithms for prognostic model based on EDC-related genes

The TCGA-KIRC dataset was randomly divided into a training set and a test set at a 1:1 ratio. 10 machine learning algorithms were selected, including Lasso, Ridge, StepCox, CoxBoost, Random Survival Forests (RSF), Elastic Net (Enet), Partial Least Squares Regression for Cox models (plsRcox), Supervised Principal Components (SuperPC), Generalized Boosted Regression Models (GBM), and Survival Support Vector Machine (survival-SVM). To select variables and construct the prognostic model, 101 combinations of these algorithms were used, along with 10-fold cross-validation. The performance of each model was ranked and selected based on the average concordance index (C-index), and the best model was chosen as the EDC-associated Prognostic Score (EPS). The model’s predictive accuracy was estimated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Based on the median value of the EPS, the TCGA training set, test set, complete dataset and E-MTAB-1980 were classified into high-risk and low-risk groups. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curve analysis was performed using the “survminer” R package to assess the survival differences between the risk groups.

EDC-gene interaction analysis in ccRCC

To investigate the interactions between EDCs and key prognostic DEGs in ccRCC, we first performed a chemical-gene interaction analysis using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD), based on key DEGs. For the chemical-gene interaction search, we identified chemicals associated with the selected key genes. Subsequently, we downloaded data related to EDCs (https://endocrinedisruption.org) and filtered chemicals overlapping with EDCs to construct an EDCs-genes-ccRCC network.

Metabolic pathway and immune infiltration analysis

The PROGENy algorithm was used to infer the activity of 14 signaling and regulatory pathways in ccRCC, including androgen, EGFR, estrogen, hypoxia, JAK-STAT, MAPK, NF-kB, p53, PI3K, TGF-b, TNF-a, Trail, VEGF, and WNT [29]. The CIBERSORT algorithm was applied to deconvolute gene expression data and estimate the relative abundance of 22 immune cell types in ccRCC tumor and tissue samples [26]. Mantel tests were conducted to assess the correlation between prognostic EDC-related genes and tumor immune infiltration levels.

Quantification of tumor stemness index via one-class linear regression

The mRNAsi index was calculated using a one-class linear regression (OCLR) machine learning algorithm. The training dataset comprised RNA-seq expression profiles of human pluripotent stem cells (SC) and differentiated cells (e.g., ectoderm EB, mesoderm MESO) from the Progenitor Cell Biology Consortium (PCBC) (Synapse Project ID: syn2701943). Model training was implemented via the gelnet package: after mean-centering the expression matrix of the SC subset, a linear regression model was trained using SC samples as a single class with L1 regularization set to 0 and L2 to 1, generating gene-specific weights. For TCGA-KIRC samples, the expression profiles were intersected with the model’s gene set. The mRNAsi index was derived by computing the Spearman correlation between each sample’s expression vector and the gene weights, subsequently normalized to the [0,1] range via linear transformation. Comparative analysis and visualization of high-risk versus low-risk EPS groups were performed using ggplot2 package.

Identification of EDC-associated single cells

The “Scissor” method (Single-Cell Identification of Subpopulations with bulk Sample phenOtype coRrelation) was applied to identify cell subpopulations within the tumor microenvironment that are associated with specific phenotypes [33]. The EPS was used as a phenotype, and single cells were associated with microbial abundance based on correlation calculations. These cells were then classified into those positively and negatively correlated with EPS based on regression coefficients. Additionally, the scMetabolism R package was utilized to analyze the metabolic pathway features of microbes associated with EPS, providing insights into the metabolic activity of these microbes and their potential impact on the tumor microenvironment and overall disease progression [39].

Cell-cell communication analysis

The CellChat method was used to infer intercellular interactions between different Scissor + cell subpopulations, constructing a ligand-receptor crosstalk regulatory network [15]. Cell-cell Communications expressed in fewer than 10 cells in specific cell groups were filtered out. The netVisual function was applied to visualize the interaction patterns. When assessing signaling pathways with multiple ligand-receptor pairs, the netAnalysis_contribution function was used to calculate and visualize the contribution of each ligand-receptor pair to the overall signaling pathway.

qRT-PCR verification

We collected tumour and paracancer paired tissues from eight fresh ccRCC patients at Fuyang People’s Hospital. The tissues were ground with liquid nitrogen, and then 1 ml of TRIzol reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to extract total RNA. genomic DNA was removed from the RNA samples, and then reverse transcribed using the PrimeScript™ RT kit with gDNA Eraser (RR047A, Takara, Japan). Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR was performed using a SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Japan, RR820A) kit and LightCycler® 480 real-time PCR (Roche Life Sciences). The primer sequences were BIRC5- Forward : CCACTGAGAACGAGCCAGACTT; Reverse: GTATTACAGGCGTAAGCCACCG. CCND1- Forward : TCTACACCGACAACTCCATCCG; Reverse༚TCTGGCATTTTGGAGAGGAAGTG., CDH1- Forward : GCCTCCTGAAAAGAGAGTGGAAG; Reverse༚TGGCAGTGTCTCTCCAAATCCG., RRM2- Forward : CTGGCTCAAGAAACGAGGACTG; Reverse༚CTCTCCTCCGATGGTTTGTGTAC.

Data analysis and visualization

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.3). The non-parametric Wilcoxon test was employed for differential testing, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. All results were visualized using the “ggplot2” package.

Result

Identification of prognosis-related differentially expressed genes

To reduce the false-positive rate and improve the accuracy of identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we integrated the GSE15641, GSE16449, and TCGA-KIRC datasets and applied batch effect correction to the data (Fig.S1.a, b). By comparing the tumor group with the normal group, we identified a total of 967 significantly upregulated genes (p < 0.05 and logFC > 1) and 781 significantly downregulated genes (p < 0.05 and logFC < -1) (Fig. 1.a). Using an “Inference Score > 45” threshold, we identified 79 genes associated with environmental chemicals affecting ccRCC. A subsequent intersection analysis of these two gene sets led to the identification of 19 genes as differentially expressed under the influence of environmental chemicals. Further univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that 12 of these genes were significantly associated with the prognosis of ccRCC (p < 0.01). Specifically, TIMP1, BIRC5, CCNA2, RRM2, TRIB3, PLAU, F3, and COL1A1 were positively correlated with patient prognosis, while ABCB1, CTH, CCND1, and CDH1 were negatively correlated with patient prognosis (Fig. 1b).

Construction and evaluation of prognostic model based on 101 machine learning algorithms for EDC-related genes

Patients in the TCGA-KIRC dataset were randomly divided into a training set and a testing set in a 1:1 ratio. A total of 101 machine learning algorithms were employed to fit the prognostic model based on EDC-related genes. To mitigate overfitting and optimize feature selection, the study identified a random survival forest (RSF) model combined with the CoxBoost method, which selected five key EDC-related genes as prognostic features (Fig.2a). This model achieved the highest average concordance index (C-index) value of 0.684 (Fig. 2a). Based on these findings, the model was used to establish the EDC-associated Prognostic Score (EPS). The results demonstrated that the EPS had area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.63, 0.58, and 0.59 for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival predictions in the TCGA-KIRC dataset, respectively (Fig. 2b). In the E-MTAB-1980 dataset, the AUC values for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival were 0.86, 0.79, and 0.71, respectively, indicating that the model is effective in predicting long-term prognosis with strong generalizability (Fig. 2.c). By calculating the optimal cut-off point for EPS, patients in the TCGA-KIRC and E-MTAB-1980 datasets were classified into high-risk and low-risk groups. Patients with high EPS values had poorer prognoses, while those with low EPS values had better prognoses (Fig. 2d, e).

Differential functional analysis between high-risk and low-risk groups based on EDC-related genes

By calculating the optimal cut-off value for the EPS, patients in the TCGA-KIRC dataset were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups (Fig. 2d). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes between the two groups were primarily enriched in biological processes such as organelle fission, nuclear division, mitotic cell cycle phase transition, and chromosome segregation. In terms of cellular components, the genes were predominantly associated with chromosomal regions, spindle, and microtubules. In molecular functions, the genes were mainly enriched in activities like DNA-binding transcription activator activity, RNA polymerase II-specific DNA-binding transcription activator activity, and others, suggesting that EDCs may influence cell division and growth processes, subsequently affecting patient prognosis (Fig. 3a). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis also supported this conclusion, with pathways such as the cell cycle, DNA replication, and base excision repair being significantly enriched (Fig. 3.b). These results further emphasize the potential role of EDCs in influencing the progression of ccRCC through their impact on key cellular processes.

Chemical-gene interaction network of DEGs

To explore the relationship between EDCs and ccRCC, we constructed a chemical-gene-ccRCC (DCM-genes-ccRCC) interaction network (Fig. 4a). A total of 8 EDCs were found to influence the occurrence and prognosis of ccRCC by mediating five key genes (Fig. 4b-f). Specifically, Diethylnitrosamine and Diethylstilbestrol were shown to affect five critical genes—BIRC5, CCND1, RRM2, CDH1, and TRIB3—thereby facilitating the malignant progression of ccRCC (Table.S2). Among the genes, CCND1 was most influenced by EDCs, with several chemicals, including Diethylnitrosamine, Diethylstilbestrol, Resveratrol, 4,4’-diaminodiphenylmethane, Trichloroethylene, Arsenic, Lead, and 2,3,5-(triglutathion-S-yl)hydroquinone, all having an impact on its expression. This suggests that EDCs may associate with ccRCC progression through the regulation of key oncogenic genes.

Tumor immune microenvironment and signaling pathways associated with EDC-related genes

Using the PROGENy method, which is based on perturbation experiments, we calculated the activity of various signaling pathways. This approach revealed significant correlations between EDC-related genes and the disturbances in these pathways. Specifically, we found that JAK-STAT, Androgen, and MAPK pathways were significantly positively correlated with the EPS. In contrast, BEGF was significantly negatively correlated with EPS (Fig. 5.a). Significantly elevated mRNA stemness index (mRNAsi) scores were observed within the ccRCC EPS-high risk group, indicating enhanced cellular stemness characteristics within this subgroup, a finding that potentially underlies its association with a more aggressive disease phenotype and heightened risk of adverse clinical outcomes (Fig. 5.c). Further, we used the CIBERSORT algorithm to estimate immune cell infiltration in the samples. Compared to normal samples, ccRCC samples showed a significant increase in plasma cells, CD8 T cells, activated memory CD4 T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), gamma-delta T cells, resting NK cells, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, and activated dendritic cells. Conversely, naive B cells, resting memory CD4 T cells, activated NK cells, monocytes, and eosinophils were significantly reduced in ccRCC samples (Fig,5.d). Subsequently, we evaluated the relationship between EPS and the 22 immune cell subsets in ccRCC. Mantel test results demonstrated significant correlations between EDC-related genes and various immune cell types, including naive B cells, memory B cells, plasma cells, CD8 T cells, naive CD4 T cells, resting memory CD4 T cells, activated memory CD4 T cells, follicular helper T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), resting NK cells, activated NK cells, monocytes, M0 macrophages, M1 macrophages, activated dendritic cells, resting mast cells, activated mast cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils (Fig. 5.b). These findings suggest that EDC-related genes may play a critical role in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment in ccRCC.

Functional pathways and cell-cell communications of single-cell populations sensitive to EDCs

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were processed using the Seurat package for dimensionality reduction, cell clustering, and annotation. 9 distinct subpopulations were identified, including epithelial cells, monocytes, T cells, macrophages, NK cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, dendritic cells, and B cells (Fig. 6.a). The five genes mentioned above are associates with these cell subpopulations (Fig.S3). Using the EPS as a phenotypic trait, we applied the Scissor algorithm to identify phenotype-associated cells, resulting in 3,776 Scissor-positive and 4,479 Scissor-negative cells. These cells were predominantly enriched in epithelial, T, and endothelial cell populations (Fig. 6b, c). To explore their metabolic features, we used the scMetabolism package to assess pathway activity in Scissor+/- cells. In epithelial cells, Scissor + cells were enriched in ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, cytochrome P450-mediated xenobiotic metabolism, butanoate metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and various amino acid pathways. In macrophages, the pentose phosphate pathway and glycosphingolipid biosynthesis were prominent, while endothelial Scissor + cells showed activity in sphingolipid metabolism and glycosaminoglycan degradation (Fig. 6d). These results illustrate the metabolic diversity of EDC-responsive cell populations within the tumor microenvironment.

Further analysis of cell-cell communication revealed a complex network among EDC-positive subpopulations via three primary modalities: direct contact, ECM receptor interaction, and secreted signaling (Fig.S4.a-c). Epithelial cells showed broad contact-based signaling with endothelial cells, dendritic cells, and T cells, suggesting their central role in mediating direct immune interactions. Fibroblast–endothelial and fibroblast–dendritic interactions were particularly notable at the ECM level, potentially contributing to angiogenesis and immune evasion. Through secreted signaling, epithelial cells communicated extensively with endothelial cells, T cells, and monocytes, while fibroblasts also engaged actively with endothelial and dendritic cells. These findings underscore the heterogeneity and complexity of EDC-influenced intercellular communication and provide mechanistic insight into how EDCs shape the tumor microenvironment, offering new directions for therapeutic development.

Discussion

With industrialization, EDCs have become pervasive in the environment, originating from industrial by-products, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, building materials, textiles, and food packaging [35]. As exogenous agents, EDCs disrupt hormonal homeostasis by mimicking or interfering with the endocrine system. The kidney, as a key organ in metabolism and detoxification, is particularly vulnerable to EDC-induced toxicity. For instance, octylphenol has been shown to impair renal function by downregulating p53 and upregulating Bcl-2, leading to oxidative stress and apoptosis [18]. ccRCC, a common and highly metastatic kidney malignancy, is increasingly linked to environmental pollutants and occupational exposures [9]. Understanding how EDCs contribute to ccRCC pathogenesis and uncovering their molecular mechanisms is critical for developing targeted prevention and treatment strategies, with direct implications for reducing disease burden.

In this study, we integrated transcriptomic datasets and used differential expression analysis with univariate Cox regression to identify 12 EDC-related prognostic genes in ccRCC. Among 101 machine learning algorithms, Random Forest and CoxBoost yielded the optimal model, identifying five key genes: BIRC5, CCND1, RRM2, TRIB3, and CDH1. BIRC5, CCND1, RRM2, and TRIB3 were overexpressed in tumors, while CDH1 was underexpressed (Fig. S2). These trends were validated by qRT-PCR, immunohistochemistry, and/or Western blot [1, 23, 36, 41, 44]. Meanwhile, we verified BIRC5, CCND1, RRM2, and CDH1 with qRT-PCR in ccRCC and paired-normal tissues (Fig. S5). CCND1 encodes Cyclin D1, a key regulator of the G1/S cell cycle transition via CDK4/6 activation and RB phosphorylation, promoting E2F1-mediated transcription and tumor proliferation [25]. During the cell cycle regulation, CCND1 binds to and activates the G1-phase-specific kinases CDK4/6, forming the active cyclin D1-CDK4/6 complex. This complex phosphorylates the retinoblastoma protein (RB), leading to dissociation of the bound transcription factor E2F1. Liberated E2F1 initiates transcriptional programs that drive the transition from G1 to S phase, thereby accelerating cell proliferation and tumor occurrence [38]. BIRC5, located on 17q25.3, encodes survivin, a dual regulator of proliferation and apoptosis. It interacts with phosphorylated histone H3 at Thr-3 during mitosis, facilitating chromosome passenger complex (CPC) recruitment for chromosome segregation [42].Though the precise mechanisms in ccRCC remain unclear, BIRC5 likely promotes tumor progression via mitotic dysregulation. Silibinin has shown promise in ccRCC models by inhibiting BIRC5 and EGFR-ERK signaling [22]. The RRM2 gene encodes the M2 subunit of ribonucleotide reductase, which is essential for DNA synthesis by catalyzing the production of deoxyribonucleotides (dNTPs) to support DNA replication and repair [31]. RRM2, a subunit of ribonucleotide reductase, is essential for DNA synthesis. Its knockdown depletes dNTP pools, induces G0/G1 arrest, and impairs replication fidelity [47]. CDH1, frequently down-regulated in ccRCC, is epigenetically repressed via succinate-mediated inhibition of TET2, resulting in promoter hypermethylation [1]. TRIB3, a regulator of lipid metabolism, stabilizes PLIN2 by inhibiting TEB4-mediated degradation, enhancing lipid storage and ER homeostasis. Its silencing induces ER stress and apoptosis, implicating TRIB3 in ccRCC progression [23]. Similar with recent LRRC19-centric evidence, EDCs may repress the GPX3/LRRC19 axis and peroxisomal antioxidant capacity, limiting lipid-peroxide clearance and thereby promoting neutral-lipid accumulation in ccRCC [46]. Our research suggest that EDCs may promote tumorigenesis by disrupting genes critical to cell cycle and mitotic regulation (such as BIRC5, CCND1 and RRM2). The EDC-associated Prognostic Score (EPS) stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups enriched in GO terms related to nuclear division and cell cycle transitions. KEGG analysis also implicated cell cycle pathways, reinforcing the role of EDCs in driving abnormal proliferation and ccRCC pathogenesis.

In this study, we constructed an EDC-gene-ccRCC network that reveals how specific EDCs may contribute to ccRCC through molecular and cellular mechanisms. Among the eight identified EDCs, DEN, Arsenic, and Lead are of particular concern due to their widespread presence and strong biological effects. DEN, commonly found in air, alcohol, plastics, and smoked foods, is metabolically activated by cytochrome P450 enzymes into reactive electrophiles that induce oxidative stress, DNA alkylation, and formation of adducts like O6-ethylguanine, which promote mutagenesis and genomic instability [37]. Further studies have shown that DEN metabolites mediate DNA alkylation or oxidation, forming DNA adducts such as O6-ethylguanine and O4- and O2-ethylthymine adducts. These structural alterations enhance the mutagenic effects of DEN and may generate stable and heritable mutations in normal cells [10]. Arsenic accumulates in the kidneys, particularly in proximal tubules, where it generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), induces inflammation, and promotes fibrosis by overexpressing epigenetic regulators such as DNMT3A, DNMT3B, and MBD4 [3]. Lead, another heavy metal with extensive industrial use, also poses a significant public health concern. Lead is toxic to multiple organs, especially the cardiovascular system and kidneys [19]. Studies have shown that elevated blood lead levels are associated with increased methylation of lead-sensitive genes, such as lead dehydratase (ALAD) and p16, potentially leading to acute lead poisoning and related diseases [43]. Therefore, reducing the environmental pollution levels of these key EDCs is crucial for the prevention and intervention of ccRCC. We recommend that future efforts focus on monitoring and controlling these EDCs to minimize their potential harm to the kidneys and other organs. Effective control strategies targeting these environmental pollutants could be essential for reducing the incidence of ccRCC and improving public health outcomes.

Our integrative framework-combining bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing with machine learning-advances beyond prior studies that primarily rely on bulk transcriptomics. We identified strong associations between EDC-related genes and the activity of key signaling pathways, including JAK-STAT, androgen, and MAPK, as well as immune cell infiltration patterns, particularly regulatory T cells (Tregs) and M0 macrophages. These results suggest that EDCs may regulate gene expression and thereby influence pathway activation and immune cell differentiation. The JAK-STAT pathway, known to play a central role in both innate and adaptive immunity, is critical for CD4+ T cell differentiation through transcription factor activation [6]. Long-term exposure to specific EDCs may lead to a shift in the Th17/Treg cell balance mediated by the JAK-STAT pathway, with this effect showing a clear gender-dependent bias [40]. However, LRRC19-NF-κB-immune-activation axis provides a concrete avenue to counter EDC-driven immunosuppression in the kidney TME. Restoring LRRC19 signaling (pharmacologically or genetically) to re-engage NF-κB-dependent chemokine/costimulatory programs nominates LRRC19 and its downstream nodes as actionable immunotherapy targets [46]. These findings highlight the immunomodulatory potential of EDCs and suggest that they may reshape the tumor immune microenvironment, thereby influencing ccRCC pathogenesis.

Furthermore, this study highlights the metabolic heterogeneity and intercellular communication patterns of EDC-responsive cell subpopulations using single-cell analysis. We identified a notable presence of Scissor-positive cells among epithelial cells, T cells, and macrophages. Epithelial cells appear to participate in EDC-related metabolism by modulating cytochrome P450-mediated xenobiotic and hormone pathways, whereas macrophages are more involved in glycosphingolipid biosynthesis and sulfur metabolism. Independent studies have shown that EDCs regulate interleukin (IL) production, such as IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, and IL-8, thereby disrupting cellular communication [27]. Consistent with this, our analysis revealed that EDC-positive cells primarily communicate through direct cell-cell contact and secretory signaling, while extracellular matrix (ECM)-mediated signaling is less prominent. From a source-target perspective, EDC-positive epithelial cells engage broadly with diverse cell types, whereas EDC-positive macrophages primarily signal to endothelial cells, dendritic cells, and monocytes. Previous studies further support that EDCs alter specific signaling pathways - such as MAPK, PI3K/c-Src/FAK, p38 MAPK, and ROS - to mediate toxic effects, particularly reproductive toxicity [5, 30]. Together, our findings underscore the critical role of EDCs in modulating cellular signaling and metabolic pathways, particularly within immune and endocrine systems. This work provides new theoretical insights into the mechanistic basis of EDC-related disease pathogenesis and environmental toxicity.

In summary, this study presents the integrative framework combining bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing with systematic machine learning to dissect EDC-gene interactions in ccRCC. Compared to prior research that relied mainly on bulk transcriptomic data, our multi-omics approach offers three key advances: (1) incorporation of scRNA-seq enables the resolution of EDC-associated gene expression in distinct cell subpopulations and reveals metabolic heterogeneity and intercellular signaling patterns; (2) algorithmic rigor through evaluation of 101 machine learning models allowed the development of a robust EDC-associated Prognostic Score (EPS) with strong predictive performance across cohorts; and (3) mechanistic breadth was achieved by linking EDC exposure to tumor-related pathways such as JAK-STAT/MAPK signaling, stemness, and immune microenvironment remodeling. These contributions address a critical gap in current knowledge and establish a foundation for future research on environmental carcinogenesis. Our findings not only enhance understanding of EDC-driven tumor biology at the single-cell level but also provide a valuable reference for biomarker discovery, therapeutic targeting, and environmental health policy.

Conclusion

In this study, we found that EDC exposure plays an important role in the development of ccRCC.By identifying key genes and potential EDCs associated with ccRCC, we constructed a network of chemical-gene-phenotypic relationships that revealed eight EDCs that may be associated with ccRCC, including diethylnitrosamine (Diethylnitrosamine), hexestrol (Diethylstilbestrol), Resveratrol, 4,4’-diaminodiphenylmethane, Trichloroethylene, Arsenic, Lead, and 2,3,5-(triglutathione-S-yl) hydroquinone (2,3,5-(triglutathion-S-yl)hydroquinone).Using Random Forest and Coxboost algorithms, we identified BIRC5, CCND1, RRM2, CDH1, and TRIB3 as key prognostic genes associated with ccRCC and developed a prognostic model with robustness and generalisation capabilities. In addition, we observed significant correlations between these key genes and immune cells and identified potential response patterns to EDC at the single-cell subpopulation level. This study provides a theoretical basis for future research exploring the effects of environmental pollutant exposure on ccRCC and may provide a resource for information on clinical strategies for the prevention and treatment of ccRCC, as well as guide environmental health research.

Limitations

To advance this work, future studies should prioritize validating EDC exposure levels through prospective cohort designs with longitudinal biomonitoring (e.g., blood/urine EDC quantification), which would strengthen causal inference between specific chemicals and ccRCC progression. The prognostic model could be enhanced by integrating clinical variables (e.g., comorbidities, lifestyle factors) with multi-omics data to improve predictive accuracy. Expanding single-cell sequencing to larger cohorts, particularly rare cell subpopulations, would better capture tumor microenvironment heterogeneity. Crucially, further experimental validation (e.g., in vitro exposure models, gene editing) is needed to confirm mechanistic links between identified EDCs (e.g., Diethylnitrosamine, Arsenic) and dysregulation of key genes (BIRC5, CCND1) in ccRCC pathogenesis.
