Autonomy Matters: A Study on Personalization-Privacy Dilemma in LLM Agents Conference: ; DOI: ; CCS: Security and privacy Human and societal aspects of security and privacy CCS: Computing methodologies Discourse, dialogue and pragmatics CCS: Human-centered computing Human computer interaction (HCI)
Zhiping Zhang, Yi Evie Zhang, Freda Shi, Tianshi Li

Abstract
. Large Language Model (LLM) agents require personal information for personalization in order to better act on users’ behalf in daily tasks, but this raises privacy concerns and a personalization-privacy dilemma. Agent’s autonomy introduces both risks and opportunities, yet its effects remain unclear. To better understand this, we conducted a 3 × \times 3 between-subjects experiment ( N = 450 N=450 ) to study how agent’s autonomy level and personalization influence users’ privacy concerns, trust and willingness to use, as well as the underlying psychological processes. We find that personalization without considering users’ privacy preferences increases privacy concerns and decreases trust and willingness to use. Autonomy moderates these effects: Intermediate autonomy flattens the impact of personalization compared to No- and Full-autonomy conditions. Our results suggest that rather than aiming for perfect model alignment in output generation, balancing autonomy of agent’s action and user control offers a promising path to mitigate the personalization-privacy dilemma.

1.
Introduction

Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have led to the emergence of LLM agents such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT agent
(
OpenAI 2025a
)
, Microsoft AI agent
(
Microsoft 2025
)
, and Cursor
(
Cursor Documentation 2025
)
, which act on behalf of users in everyday tasks.
To better perform daily tasks with limited instruction, LLM agents require users’ personal data for personalization.
A common practice is connecting the LLM agents to external applications such as Gmail
(
OpenAI 2025a
;
Human AI Labs, Inc. 2024
)
, Notion Note
(
OpenAI 2025a
;
Anthropic 2025
)
and Slack
(
Human AI Labs, Inc. 2024
)
, where large amounts of up-to-date personal data can be accessed to personalize agents’ outputs to align with how users would perform through retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
(
OpenAI 2025a
)
.
Some systems also allow users to upload documents, such as chat histories
(
Mindverse AI 2025
;
WeClone 2025
)
, work files
(
Human AI Labs, Inc. 2024
;
Mindverse AI 2025
)
, or personal websites
(
Mindverse AI 2025
;
Human AI Labs, Inc. 2024
)
, to fine-tune the models.
For example, Personal AI allows both ways to personalize LLM agent and in the back allows to integrate the agents with these external platforms to perform tasks on behalf of users
(
Human AI Labs, Inc. 2024
)
.
However, personalization can raise privacy concerns, leading users to refuse personal information sharing
(
Awad and Krishnan 2006
)
. This phenomenon, known as the “Personalization-Privacy Paradox”, characterizes the tension between consumers’ desire for personalization due to the usefulness, and their concerns about privacy
(
Awad and Krishnan 2006
)
.
It has been widely documented in prior personalized AI systems with limited action space and autonomy, such as recommendation systems that only generate personalized content recommendation
(
Aguirre et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Karwatzki et al. 2017
)
.

The problems become more complex as the LLM agent, compared with non-agentic AI systems, are inherently endowed with more autonomy in the system actions.
They not only generate static content to populate a fixed, predefined interface, but also plan and execute actions on the fly, such as generating messages and automatically sending them to specific recipients through integration with real-world tools
(
Muthusamy et al. 2023
;
Talebirad and Nadiri 2023
)
.
Autonomy in AI agents refers to “the degree to which an AI system can operate independently of humans”
(
Kasirzadeh and Gabriel 2025
)
, which inherently relates to the degree of human control accompanying the agent.
The increased autonomy in LLM agents’ action introduces new privacy risks as the underlying LLMs remain inadequate at capturing individual privacy preferences
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
, and such vulnerability can extend to the agent’s interactions.
Recent studies on LLM agents in interpersonal communication contexts found that, beyond the conventional privacy concerns present in non-agentic AI system, such as concerns about data collection and misuse by organization, users also worry about the risk that agents might autonomously share or act upon sensitive information in ways that conflict with their own preferences
(
Goyal et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
.
With greater autonomy in the system’s actions, LLM agents may foster higher user trust by increasing the perceived capability, but at the same time this delegation of human control can increase concerns of potential privacy violations, thereby reducing trust
(
Puranam and Vanneste 2021
)
.

Although prior studies on personalized non-agentic AI systems have investigated different levels of user control and their influence on related constructs such as privacy concerns
(
Zhang et al. 2014
;
Shin et al. 2022
;
Jannach et al. 2016
)
, trust
(
Han and Ko 2025
;
Glikson and Woolley 2020
)
, and willingness to use
(
Fink et al. 2024
;
Möhlmann and Zalmanson 2017
;
Oh et al. 2025
)
, these controls are more focused on the input level, namely what personal information is used and how it is applied in generating personalized content
(
Zhang et al. 2014
)
.
This is corresponding to the limited autonomy of these systems in planning and taking actions.
For LLM agents, the effect of the personalization-privacy paradox remains to be investigated, and it is essential to look beyond static access control at the input level, to accommodate the dynamic and autonomous nature by placing user control over the agent actions in proper ways
(
Prunkl 2024
)
. In this study, we aim to address this gap by asking:

RQ:

How do different personalization and autonomy levels in LLM agents affect users’ privacy concerns, trust, and willingness to use them, and through what underlying psychological processes do these effects occur?

To answer this overall question, we conducted a 3
\times
3 between-subjects experiment (
N=450
) in which we manipulated personalization type and autonomy level in an LLM agent acting on behalf of users in an interpersonal communication scenario (a weekly professional update meeting or a family travel planning discussion).
Personalization was varied across three types: (1)
Basic personalization
, simulating common real-world practices where agents access user data from external platforms without consideration of user privacy preferences
(
Zhang et al. 2024c
)
; (2)
Privacy-aware personalization
, simulating an ideal case where personalization fully respects users’ privacy preferences, namely only including relevant yet non-privacy-violating personal information in the context;
As aligning LLMs with human privacy preferences is a major direction in HCI and AI research
(
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Teku et al. 2025
;
Sun and Zhao 2024
)
, this condition allowed us to examine the value of “perfect” alignment.
(3)
No personalization
, simulating the case where users refuse to share personal data.
Autonomy was manipulated following
Kasirzadeh and Gabriel 2025
: (1)
No autonomy
, which served as a baseline, making our results comparable to prior non-agentic AI systems
(
Aguirre et al. 2016
;
Cloarec 2020
)
, where the agent generates messages but always requires user confirmation to respond in the discussion; (2)
Full autonomy
, the other extreme, where the agent always responds automatically without user intervention; and (3)
Intermediate autonomy
, a middle level where the agent responds automatically by default but requests user confirmation when sensitive information is detected.
Each participant provided real personal data before the discussion session through a pre-survey, then experienced one assigned condition of the LLM agent in the discussion scenario, and finally completed a post-survey reporting their perceptions towards the LLM agents.

Our results highlight the important role of agent’s autonomy in understanding personalization-privacy dilemma in the context of LLM agents.
The results of linear mixed-effects regression showed that personalization without accounting for user privacy preferences raised privacy concerns and lowering trust and willingness to use.
However, compared to the No autonomy condition, under Intermediate autonomy these effects were attenuated, showing smaller increases in privacy concern and smaller decreases in trust and willingness to use.
The moderated mediation analysis helps explain this effect in terms of users’ underlying psychological processes: personalization influenced privacy concern, trust, and willingness to use through the mediators of perceived sensitivity, control, and usefulness.
These mediation effects were significant under No autonomy but became nonsignificant under Intermediate autonomy.
The potential reason is that Intermediate autonomy itself boosted users’ perceived control, thereby counteracting the decreases caused by personalization.
In other words, as Intermediate autonomy already enhanced users’ perceived control, personalization no longer had a strong indirect effect through these mediators.

Our results on Privacy-aware personalization (which produced privacy concern and trust levels similar to No personalization but the highest willingness to use) provide preliminary evidence of the value of aligning LLM agents with human privacy preferences, though achieving such perfect model alignment remains difficult in practice.
The moderating effects of Intermediate autonomy reveal a promising alternative that reducing privacy concerns and improving user trust and willingness to use by appropriately designing agent autonomy and user control.
Moreover, our findings suggest that while perfect alignment is hard to achieve, alternative mechanisms such as personalized delegation of autonomy offers a practical and complementary path to building trustworthy LLM agents that respect user privacy and mitigate concerns.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Personalization-Privacy Paradox in AI Systems

The
Personalization-Privacy Paradox
describes the complex and often contradictory relationship between a user’s desire for personalized and convenient digital experiences and their concern about individual privacy
(
Awad and Krishnan 2006
;
Cloarec 2020
)
. On one hand, users value the benefits of personalization, such as reduced information overload and increased satisfaction
(
Liang et al. 2006
)
. However, empirical evidence shows a reluctance among users to be profiled for these services even if they implicitly value customized outcomes
(
Awad and Krishnan 2006
)
. This reveals a complex psychological and behavioral tension rather than a simple transactional choice
(
Sheng et al. 2008
;
Treiblmaier and Pollach 2007
)
. To unravel its complexities, researchers have employed theoretical frameworks such as the privacy calculus model, which examines the trade-offs individuals make when disclosing personal information
(
Dinev and Hart 2006
)
.
A substantial body of research has sought to identify the factors that influence the Personalization-Privacy Paradox, with a particular emphasis on the core constructs of privacy concern, user trust, and willingness to use or disclose information
(
Kokolakis 2017
;
Ramzan et al. 2025
)
. These perceptions are influenced by various factors. For instance, studies show that while privacy concerns negatively impact the intention to adopt personalized services, this effect is highly dependent on situational factors
(
Sheng et al. 2008
)
. Demographic factors also play a role; prior research has noted that women and older people tend to value privacy more highly than men and younger people, respectively
(
Prince and Wallsten 2022
)
.

The intensity and complexity of the Personalization-Privacy Paradox have grown alongside technological innovation. While the core tension was present in early e-commerce, its scope and implications have been dramatically amplified by the proliferation of more pervasive and data-intensive AI systems. Research into this paradox has been predominantly situated in the context of AI systems with limited autonomy, such as recommendation systems that personalize advertisements, products, or social media feeds
(
Karwatzki et al. 2017
;
Sheng et al. 2008
;
Aguirre et al. 2016
;
Cloarec 2020
;
Liang et al. 2006
)
.

However, the emergence of Agentic AI, or LLM agents, introduces new dimensions to the paradox. Unlike traditional AI, agentic AI systems possess the autonomy to not only generate content but also plan and execute actions with minimal human intervention
(
Acharya et al. 2025
;
Murugesan 2025
)
, such as sending information or interfacing with other applications on a user’s behalf
(
Leong et al. 2024
)
. This heightened level of autonomy fundamentally alters the nature of the personalization-privacy calculus. These agents are often used for personal purposes and thus encapsulate sensitive personal information
(
Such et al. 2014
)
. The risk is no longer confined to how personal data is collected and used for content generation, but extends to how an agent might autonomously act upon sensitive information in ways that might contradict a user’s latent preferences or expectations. Therefore, this study aims to bridge this critical gap by investigating how different levels of personalization and, crucially, different levels of agent autonomy, collectively influence users’ privacy concerns, trust, and willingness to use these emerging systems.

2.2.
Autonomy in AI systems and User Controls

Human autonomy is an important concept in moral and political philosophy, defined as the capacity to live one’s life according to one’s own motives, free from manipulative or distorting external forces
(
Christman 2003
)
. In contrast, machine autonomy is conceptualized as the extent to which a system can operate in an independent and goal-directed way without user interference
(
Rijsdijk and Hultink 2009
)
. The rise of AI fundamentally transforms our relationship with technology by shifting agency and control from humans to machines, creating a direct tension with this principle
(
Murray et al. 2021
)
. Within HCI, this tension has been extensively studied as a relationship between user control and machine autonomy
(
Hardian et al. 2006
;
Rijsdijk and Hultink 2009
;
Kang and Lou 2022
;
Zanatto et al. 2024
)
. As AI systems become more pervasive in influencing or replacing human decision-making, this tension intensifies, posing significant ethical challenges to the conventional understanding of human agency
(
Dogruel et al. 2022
;
Calvo et al. 2020
;
Oh et al. 2025
)
.

Prior research provides empirical evidence showing that the balance between user control and system autonomy significantly impacts user perceptions. Studies have shown that an AI’s level of autonomy can influence users’ privacy concerns
(
Zhang et al. 2014
;
Shin et al. 2022
;
Jannach et al. 2016
;
Lippi et al. 2025
)
, their trust in the system
(
Han and Ko 2025
;
Glikson and Woolley 2020
)
, and their willingness to adopt the technology
(
Fink et al. 2024
;
Möhlmann and Zalmanson 2017
;
Oh et al. 2025
)
. However, this relationship is not linear. For example,
Fan and Liu 2022
found that algorithmic decision autonomy has an inverted U-shaped effect on consumer purchase decisions, where intermediate levels of autonomy yielded better outcomes than either low or full autonomy. This suggests that users do not simply prefer more autonomous systems; instead, research indicates they value a nuanced balance and often desire meaningful control over even highly capable systems
(
Fink et al. 2024
;
Rödel et al. 2014
)
.

While informative, these findings were established in an era where AI primarily functioned as a tool. The field is now undergoing a paradigm shift from AI as a tool to AI as an agent. LLMs are evolving from text generators into autonomous agentic systems capable of perceiving their environment, reasoning, planning, and executing complex, multi-step tasks with minimal human intervention
(
Ferrag et al. 2025
;
Acharya et al. 2025
;
Murugesan 2025
)
. Autonomy is the central, defining characteristic of these “agentic systems”
(
Kasirzadeh and Gabriel 2025
)
, leading researchers to propose frameworks that treat an agent’s level of autonomy as a deliberate design decision
(
Feng et al. 2025
;
Kasirzadeh and Gabriel 2025
)
. For instance,
Kasirzadeh and Gabriel 2025

(
Kasirzadeh and Gabriel 2025
)
defines agent autonomy as the capacity to perform actions without external direction or control and characterizes it across multiple levels, from “no autonomy” to “full autonomy.” Another framework by
Feng et al. 2025
defines five levels based on the required human involvement, with the user’s role shifting from an active “operator” at the lowest level to a passive “observer” at the highest. Although these frameworks use different approaches, they both conceptualize autonomy as a spectrum determined by the degree of the AI’s independent operation and the corresponding level of human involvement.

Crucially, this shift from tool to agent introduces a fundamentally different risk landscape. As
Prunkl 2024
argue, the threat from an AI tool (e.g., a personalized recommendation engine) comes from how a person or institution uses it to manipulate others. Addressing this risk requires limiting the context and purpose of its use. In contrast, the threat from an AI agent stems from the agent itself, as it can produce unpredictable and undesirable results. This distinction renders prior findings on AI autonomy potentially insufficient. Research on AI tools is inadequate for understanding the new dynamics of AI agents, where the stakes of independent action are dramatically higher. While we know that autonomy levels matter, it is unclear how user perceptions of trust, privacy, and control will adapt to systems that are no longer just making recommendations but are taking actions on their behalf. As researchers advocate that designing for autonomy is an ethical imperative for responsible AI
(
Calvo et al. 2020
;
Feng et al. 2025
)
, this study addresses this critical gap by using the newly established frameworks of agent autonomy to investigate how different levels of agentic action impact user perceptions.

3.
Hypotheses

This section introduces our experimental design space, defined by different types of personalization and levels of autonomy in LLM agents. Drawing on prior research and theoretical frameworks (e.g., the Technology Acceptance Model
(
Marangunić and Granić 2015
)
and the Privacy Calculus Model
(
Dinev and Hart 2006
)
), we also incorporate individual differences and the mediating mechanisms of perceived sensitivity, perceived control, and perceived usefulness, which together ground the hypotheses we propose.

3.1.
Personalization Types in LLM Agents

Personalization in LLMs adapts model outputs to user-specific data so that responses are more relevant and aligned with individual needs and expectations
(
Zhang et al. 2024c
;
Tseng et al. 2024
)
.
By tailoring the content, personalization can enhance user satisfaction and improve the quality of interaction
(
Zhang et al. 2024c
)
.
Our study focuses on user-level personalization, where an agent generates text output based on an individual user’s personal data
(
Zhang et al. 2024c
)
.
While personalization brings clear benefits, it also raises a central challenge: balancing the value of personalization with the risks of privacy violations
(
Pasch and Cha 2025
;
Yim 2023
)
.
To investigate this trade-off, we operationalize three types of personalization in LLM agents:

•

Basic Personalization:
The most common type of personalization in current LLM agents
(
OpenAI 2025a
;
Microsoft 2025
;
Cursor Documentation 2025
;
Anthropic 2025
)
, which we use as a baseline.
The agent can access and use user information from third-party applications to generate tailored responses, but it does not account for the user’s privacy preferences.

•

Privacy-Aware Personalization:
An idealized scenario in which the agent personalizes its output while fully respecting the user’s privacy preferences.
In this condition, the agent never discloses information the user wishes to withhold.
This condition allows us to examine the model alignment “best case” that the HCI and AI communities are actively pursuing
(
Gabriel 2020
;
Teku et al. 2025
;
Wu et al. 2023
)
.

•

No Personalization:
A general-purpose assistant condition
(
Zhang et al. 2024c
)
, where the agent has task knowledge but does not access or use any user-specific data, history, or preferences.

Users raise privacy concerns about personalization due to perceived risks
(
Toch et al. 2012
;
Awad and Krishnan 2006
)
, especially when LLM agents may disclose information that violates individual preferences
(
Leong et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
.
Relative to the Basic personalization condition, the No personalization and Privacy-aware personalization conditions avoid such risks, since the agent never shares information that users wish to keep private.
Thus, we hypothesize:

H1a:

Compared to Basic personalization, users interacting with an LLM agent under No personalization and Privacy-aware personalization will report lower privacy concerns.

Prior work shows personalization can enhance user trust
(
Komiak and Benbasat 2006
)
, but this gain is threatened by privacy concerns. Privacy assurance mechanisms can mitigate these concerns and preserve trust
(
Joinson et al. 2010
;
Bansal et al. 2015
)
.
We therefore hypothesize that an agent combining personalization with privacy awareness will be trusted most:

H1b:

Compared to Basic personalization, users interacting with an LLM agent under No personalization will report lower trust, but interacting with an LLM agent under Privacy-aware personalization will report higher trust.

Previous research has demonstrated that personalization enhances user experience and is more efficacious than non-personalized approaches
(
Li 2016
;
Toch et al. 2012
)
. A system that does not understand a user’s preferences is likely to be less efficient, potentially diminishing the user’s overall experience. However, the benefits of personalization are often counterbalanced by privacy concerns, which have been shown to negatively impact users’ intentions to use personalized services
(
Sheng et al. 2008
)
. This suggests that an ideal system would offer the advantages of a tailored experience while simultaneously respecting user privacy preferences. Such a system should, in theory, maximize a user’s willingness to engage. Based on this reasoning, we propose a hypothesis:

H1c:

Compared to Basic personalization, users interacting with an LLM agent under No personalization will report lower willingness to use, but interacting with an LLM agent under Privacy-aware personalization will report higher willingness to use.

3.2.
Autonomy Levels in LLM Agents

To investigate how an LLM agent’s autonomy influences user privacy concerns, trust and willingness to use, we operationalized the agent’s autonomy based on the framework introduced by
Kasirzadeh and Gabriel 2025
.
This framework defines the autonomy of AI agents as
“the capacity to perform actions without external direction or control”
and characterizes it across a multi-level spectrum. From this spectrum, we considered what constitutes meaningful interaction in our study context and selected three distinct and representative levels of agent autonomy as the experimental conditions:
two extremes (No Autonomy and Full Autonomy) and one middle condition (Intermediate Autonomy).

•

No Autonomy:

The LLM agent “is entirely dependent upon the principal for its ability to act and can only act in the manner the principal dictates” (In our context, the principal is the user in our study).

•

Full Autonomy:

The LLM agent “is able to perform all tasks without oversight or control”.

•

Intermediate Autonomy:

The LLM agent “can perform the majority of tasks independently, though it still relies upon input from the principal for critical determinations”.
In our study, we define critical determinations as situations involving potential privacy violations, reflecting current privacy-preserving design practices in which systems defer to humans when risks are detected
(
Bagdasarian et al. 2024
;
OpenAI 2025b
)
.

Prior research has established a strong link between user control and privacy
(
Landau 2015
;
Tene and Polonetsky 2012
;
Zhao et al. 2016
)
. The ability for users to manage their information flow and make explicit decisions on data sharing is an effective mechanism for reducing privacy risks and users’ privacy concerns
(
Zhang et al. 2014
;
Taylor et al. 2009
)
. In AI systems, solutions that grant users direct control over information release are therefore a critical focus for addressing privacy issues
(
Landau 2015
;
Meng et al. 2019
)
. As an LLM agent’s autonomy increases, the user’s direct control over its subsequent actions necessarily decreases, which can create uncertainty about how personal information is being used or shared by the agent, potentially heightening perceived privacy risks. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2a:

Users interacting with a high-autonomy LLM agent will report higher privacy concerns than those interacting with a low-autonomy agent.

While autonomy can offer advantages, prior research suggests that excessively high levels can be detrimental to user trust
(
Oh et al. 2025
)
. This negative reaction can be explained through the lens of psychological reactance. Existing work has noted that high AI autonomy can be perceived as a threat to a user’s freedom and personal agency, causing reactance towards the system
(
Dogruel et al. 2022
;
Oh et al. 2025
)
. Such psychological reactance is negatively associated with attitudes toward AI, including trust
(
Oh et al. 2025
)
. As system autonomy increases to the point where users feel a loss of control, it can increase perceived performance risks and failure severity
(
Lucia-Palacios and Pérez-López 2021
;
Rödel et al. 2014
)
. A highly autonomous agent, by acting without explicit user command for every step, may be perceived as overriding user agency. This potential loss of control can erode a user’s trust in the system’s reliability and intentions. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H2b:

Users interacting with a high-autonomy LLM agent will report lower trust than those interacting with a low-autonomy agent.

The relationship between autonomy and a user’s willingness to use a system is often non-linear
(
Fan and Liu 2022
;
Lucia-Palacios and Pérez-López 2021
)
. Prior work suggests that users prefer a balance, finding no-autonomy systems to be the least popular while favoring semi-autonomous approaches
(
Ball and Callaghan 2011
)
.
Fan and Liu 2022
also found this pattern in algorithmic purchase decisions, where middle autonomy resulted in better outcomes than either low or high autonomy. This indicates an inverted U-shaped effect, where willingness to use is highest at an intermediate level of autonomy. This preference can be understood as a trade-off. Increasing autonomy can boost convenience and perceived usefulness
(
Rijsdijk and Hultink 2009
;
Lucia-Palacios and Pérez-López 2021
)
. However, at very high levels, these benefits are often outweighed by drawbacks such as perceived intrusiveness and risk, making users reluctant to adopt highly autonomous products
(
Lucia-Palacios and Pérez-López 2021
;
Rödel et al. 2014
)
. These risks are amplified in agentic AI systems, which combine a high degree of autonomy with unpredictability
(
Prunkl 2024
)
. As user control diminishes, the perceived risk of undesirable outcomes (e.g., due to LLM hallucinations) can lower a user’s willingness to use the system. We therefore hypothesize:

H2c:

Users are more willing to use the LLM agent with intermediate autonomy than those with no autonomy or full autonomy.

Moreover, prior research has established a link between perceived autonomy and user attitudes, such as trust, usage intention, and privacy concern within the context of personalized services
(
Fink et al. 2024
;
Han and Ko 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2014
)
. This body of work indicates that personalization and autonomy often coexist and interact to shape a user’s overall perception of a system. Based on the interplay, we propose that the impact of one factor will depend on the level of the other. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3:

The effect of personalization will be influenced by an LLM agent’s autonomy level.

3.3.
Individual Differences

Previous research has demonstrated that individual differences play an important role in shaping people’s privacy concerns
(
Prince and Wallsten 2022
)
, trust
(
Riedl 2022
;
De Freitas et al. 2023
)
, and willingness to use AI systems
(
Weger et al. 2022
)
.
Building on these findings, our study examines how different sub-populations and individuals with varying traits (AI literacy, personal agency, and interpersonal agency) perceive the LLM agent.

3.3.1.
AI literacy.

A growing body of research indicates that AI literacy significantly shapes user attitudes toward artificial intelligence
(
Kaya et al. 2024a
;
Mantello et al. 2023
;
Schiavo et al. 2024
)
. For example, when individuals have a higher AI literacy, they are more likely to perceive the technology as both useful and easy to operate, which fosters greater acceptance
(
Schiavo et al. 2024
)
. Similarly, a person’s knowledge about AI has been identified as one of the most prominent individual factors shaping positive attitudes
(
Kaya et al. 2024b
)
. Individuals with greater AI literacy are more likely to make more informed judgments about the potential benefits and risks of these systems, which in turn influences their overall perceptions
(
Kaya et al. 2024a
)
. Therefore, we extend this line of inquiry to agentic AI and propose the following hypothesis:

H4a:

Individual AI literacy will influence user privacy concerns, trust and willingness to use the LLM agent.

3.3.2.
Personal Agency.

Personal agency refers to an individual’s sense of control over their own thoughts, actions, environment, and life course
(
Bandura 2001
)
. It is the belief in one’s ability to achieve desired outcomes through one’s own actions
(
Smith et al. 2000
)
. Previous studies have showed that psychological factors influence individuals’ attitudes toward AI
(
Ye et al. 2019
;
Kim et al. 2025
)
. For instance, people prefer actions that afford them more choice, and the capacity of AI to act without human input may be perceived as a threat to their agency
(
De Freitas et al. 2023
)
. Empirical evidence also suggests that individuals with a stronger sense of personal agency perceive a greater threat to their freedom as AI autonomy increases, which can lead to psychological reactance and, ultimately, more negative attitudes toward AI
(
Oh et al. 2025
)
. Thus, we hypothesize:

H4b:

Personal agency and interpersonal agency will influence user privacy concerns, trust and willingness to use the LLM agent.

3.3.3.
Interpersonal Agency.

In contrast, interpersonal agency is the ability to achieve desired goals through interactions with others (e.g., by expressing needs or behaving cooperatively)
(
Smith et al. 2000
)
. Unlike personal agency, which focuses on individual effort, interpersonal agency emphasizes how a person navigates and influences their social systems. Research has also noted that users’ psychological mechanisms in a social context influence their attitudes toward technologies. For example, Social Exchange Theory
(
Cropanzano and Mitchell 2005
)
, applied within the privacy calculus, indicates that individuals are willing to disclose information on social media in exchange for social connection, happiness, intimacy, convenience, or professional advancement
(
Cloarec et al. 2024
)
. Accordingly, users who have higher interpersonal agency may be more willing to adopt an LLM agent to achieve their goals. Hence, we hypothesize:

H4c:

Interpersonal agency will influence user privacy concerns, trust and willingness to use the LLM agent.

3.3.4.
Demographic.

Prior research has consistently demonstrated that demographic factors influence user perceptions related to technology and privacy. Specifically, variables such as gender, age, and education level have been shown to affect privacy concerns
(
Zukowski and Brown 2007
;
Prince and Wallsten 2022
)
, the willingness to use AI
(
Méndez-Suárez et al. 2023
;
Al Omari et al. 2024
)
, and trust in AI systems
(
Gillespie et al. 2021
;
Kozak and Fel 2024
;
Zhang and Dafoe 2019
)
. For instance, studies indicate that females and older individuals tend to exhibit higher levels of privacy concern than their male and younger counterparts, respectively
(
Prince and Wallsten 2022
)
. Conversely, higher levels of education have been associated with lower concerns regarding information privacy
(
Zukowski and Brown 2007
)
. Building on these findings, our study seeks to verify the influence of these demographic factors in the context of agentic AI. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4d:

Individual demographic differences (age, gender, and educational background) will influence user privacy concerns, trust and willingness to use the LLM agent.

3.4.
Mediation Effects

To explain the underlying psychological mechanisms driving user perceptions of the AI agent, our study draws upon two established theoretical frameworks: the Technology Acceptance Model
(
Marangunić and Granić 2015
)
and the Privacy Calculus Model
(
Dinev and Hart 2006
)
. Together, these models suggest that a user’s decision to adopt and trust a technology is the result of a rational trade-off between its perceived benefits and its perceived risks. We investigate three key mediators that underpin this trade-off: perceived sensitivity, perceived control, and perceived usefulness.

3.4.1.
Perceived Sensitivity.

In our study, we define perceived sensitivity as the extent to which a user believes the agent’s generated content or actions reveal sensitive personal information. This concept directly corresponds to the “perceived privacy risk” component of the Privacy Calculus Model, which is known to impact users’ privacy concerns, trust, and usage attitudes
(
Dinev and Hart 2006
)
. The level of personalization in our design determines the amount of sensitive information the agent can potentially reveal, while the level of autonomy influences the likelihood of an unsupervised disclosure. When an agent’s messages contain information a user deems sensitive, it acts as a clear signal of privacy risk. Such risk can be a direct antecedent to heightened privacy concerns, a loss of trust, and a decrease in willingness to use the agent.

H5a:

Personalization levels influence users’ privacy concerns, trust, and willingness to use through users’ perceptions of how sensitive the information included in the LLM agent’s messages is.

H6a:

Autonomy levels influence users’ privacy concerns, trust, and willingness to use through users’ perceptions of how sensitive the information included in the LLM agent’s messages is.

3.4.2.
Perceived Control.

Our experimental design influences two distinct facets of users’ perceived control. The personalization type affects a user’s perceived control over their data. Simultaneously, the agent’s autonomy level dictates the user’s control over its actions. We posit that perceived control is the mechanism through which our experimental manipulations influence user perceptions. When users feel they have greater control, they might perceive lower risk, which in turn reduces their privacy concerns
(
Dinev and Hart 2006
;
Zhang et al. 2014
)
. This sense of empowerment and predictability might also help build trust and increase their willingness to use the system
(
Fink et al. 2024
;
Han and Ko 2025
)
.

H5b:

Personalization levels influence users’ privacy concerns, trust, and willingness to use through users’ perceived control.

H6b:

Autonomy levels influence users’ privacy concerns, trust, and willingness to use through users’ perceived control.

3.4.3.
Perceived Usefulness.

Based on the Technology Acceptance Model, perceived usefulness is a primary driver of users’ attitudes and adoption intentions
(
Marangunić and Granić 2015
)
. In our study, usefulness is influenced in two ways: personalization affects the relevance and quality of the agent’s outputs, while autonomy influences its convenience and efficiency by changing the need for user intervention. This perception of usefulness provides the compelling “benefit” in the privacy calculus
(
Dinev and Hart 2006
)
, weighing against potential risks to positively influence the user’s attitude toward the technology. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H5c:

Personalization levels influence users’ privacy concerns, trust, and willingness to use through users’ perceived usefulness of the LLM agent.

H6c:

Autonomy levels influence users’ privacy concerns, trust, and willingness to use through users’ perceived usefulness of the LLM agent.

4.
Methodology

4.1.
Study Procedure

Figure 2
.

Overview of the study procedure with four steps: (1) Participants provided both non-sensitive and sensitive information for personalizing the LLM agent.
(2) Participants were introduced to one of the nine LLM agent conditions.
(3) Participants used the assigned LLM agent in the communication task. A sensitivity detection module monitored the agent’s generated messages and provided reminders based on autonomy levels (the Intermediate Autonomy condition is shown as an example).
(4) Participants reported their perceptions of the LLM agent.

We designed a 3 (personalization type)
\times
3 (autonomy level) between-subjects experiment to test our hypotheses.
Each participant was introduced to one of the nine LLM agent conditions and used the agent to act on their behalf in an online discussion scenario.
We included two representative scenarios to broaden participants’ reactions for exploratory purposes: one in a professional work context (a weekly update meeting with colleagues) and one in a personal daily-life context (a family travel plan discussion with relatives).
While we did not intend to systematically examine scenario effects, we treated scenario as a random factor, as detailed in
Section 5.1
.
Participants were randomly and evenly assigned to one of the two scenarios and one of the nine LLM agent conditions.
Each participant went through process with three main parts (a pre-survey to collect personal information for personalization, an agent interaction session, and a post-survey) as shown in
Figure 2
.
Each participant completed the study in four main parts (see
Figure 2
): (1) a pre-survey to collect personal information for personalization; (2) LLM agent introduction and task introduction; (3) Interaction session; and (4) a post-survey to report perception.

Step 1: Providing personal information for personalization.

In the pre-survey (see
Appendix B
), participants provided three pieces of non-sensitive information required for the assigned discussion scenario and three pieces of sensitive information that they explicitly indicated they did not want others in the scenario to know.
For example, in the weekly update meeting scenario, participants answered questions such as “What tasks did you complete in the past week?” (non-sensitive) and “What’s a work-related habit, difficulty, or situation you wouldn’t want your colleagues to know?” (sensitive).
We collected participants’ real personal information to ensure authentic reactions and perceptions.
This information was then integrated into the agent’s knowledge base for personalization during the interaction session.

Step 2: Introduction to the assigned LLM agent condition.

Participants were introduced to one of the nine LLM agent conditions, including information on what personal data the agent could access for the communication task, what actions the agent could perform during the discussion, and what controls the participant retained (e.g., whether and when the agent could automatically send responses).

Step 3: Using the LLM agent in the communication task.

After confirming that they understood what the agent is and what it can do, participants entered an online discussion session where the LLM agent acted on their behalf.
We implemented the study as a text-based chat to avoid confounding effects from multimodal interactions.
A sensitivity detection module (see
Section 4.2.4
) operated throughout the chat and provided reminders across all conditions.
Details of the agent designs and interaction mechanics are described in
Section 4.2
.

Step 4: Reporting perceptions of the LLM agent.

In a post-hoc survey (see
Section B.3
), participants reported their privacy concerns, willingness to use, and trust towards the agent they used. The survey also included Likert scales to measure their sense of Personal Agency, Interpersonal Agency, and AI literacy, followed by demographic questions. A full description of these measurements is provided in
Section 4.3
.

4.2.
Study Materials

The study was implemented on a Qualtrics

1

Qualtrics
is a website for building online survey study.
survey integrated with an LLM agent and discussion system.
The LLM agent conditions and scenarios were randomly and evenly assigned through the Qualtrics randomizer.

4.2.1.
Overview of the LLM Agent and Discussion System.

We built the LLM agent and the discussion system ourselves to ensure experimental control.
The discussion system included a chat interface and three agents: one agent that acted on behalf of users, driven by large language models (gpt-4o-mini and gpt-4o), and two role-playing agents that simulated other entities in a group discussion (two colleagues in the weekly update meeting scenario, or two relatives in the family travel plan scenario).
The responses of the two role-playing agents were largely scripted to ensure consistency across conditions (see
Appendix C
), with some parts generated by gpt-4o-mini based on participants’ personal information to enhance the realism of the discussion.
For example, the colleagues’ responses about their past week’s work were generated based on the participant’s job, since this information would be known to their colleagues in real world.
We also implemented a gpt-4o powered sensitivity detection module (see
Section 4.2.4
) to check if generated messages contained information participants had designated as private in their pre-survey.
In the following sections, we focus on how different personalization and autonomy conditions of the LLM agent were designed and manipulated.

4.2.2.
Manipulation of Personalization.

We manipulated personalization by controlling the amount of user knowledge that the LLM agent could access.
To simulate market practices where LLM agents access user data from connected third-party applications
(
OpenAI 2025a
;
Anthropic 2025
;
Human AI Labs, Inc. 2024
;
Cursor Documentation 2025
)
, we reformatted participants’ personal information from the pre-survey into
Notion Note
and
Google Calendar
formats and structured the data in JSON to feed into the LLM agent
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
.
Following prior work, we employed a “personalization via prompting” technique, which involves including specific user information as context within the prompts provided to the agent
(
Zhang et al. 2024c
)
.
Personal information collected in the Qualtrics pre-survey was sent to the LLM agent backend via API and included in the prompts under three conditions:

•

Basic Personalization (baseline):
The LLM agent had full access to all provided information of the participant.

•

Privacy-Aware Personalization:
The agent had access only to the participant’s non-sensitive information. Information explicitly marked as “not to be shared with other entities” was excluded and not passed to the agent, simulating an ideal condition in which the agent behaved in perfect alignment with the user’s privacy preferences.

•

No Personalization:
The agent had no access to any participant data, simulating a general-purpose assistant without personalization.

4.2.3.
Manipulation of Autonomy.

Following definitions of AI agent autonomy as the degree to which a system can operate independently of humans
(
Kasirzadeh and Gabriel 2025
)
, we manipulated this factor at three levels.

•

No Autonomy (baseline):
The LLM agent composed a message but always required the participant’s explicit approval or edits before sending it.

•

Full Autonomy:
The agent managed the entire conversational flow and sent messages automatically without requiring participant approval. Participants could only observe the discussion but had no opportunity to intervene.

•

Intermediate Autonomy:
The LLM agent generated and sent messages automatically by default.
However, when the sensitivity detection module (see
Section 4.2.4
) flagged a message as containing potentially sensitive information, the agent withheld the message and required the participant’s approval before sending.

4.2.4.
Sensitivity Detection.

The sensitivity detection module used an “LLM-as-a-judge” approach (powered by GPT-4o) to identify potentially sensitive information in generated responses,
without any personalization
to avoid confounding with the personalization manipulation.
This design was informed by LLM agent paradigms that leverage user control for “critical determinations”
(
Kasirzadeh and Gabriel 2025
)
.
For example, AirGapAgent
(
Bagdasarian et al. 2024
)
escalates to the user when information outside the approved set is requested, and OpenAI Operator
(
OpenAI 2025b
)
escalates to the user when predefined high-stakes domains are involved.
Our sensitivity detection followed the approach to evaluate sensitivity of LLM agent action introduced by
Shao et al. 2024
, where a separate model (distinct from the one driving the agent) extracted a list of potentially sensitive information in the task context and then judged whether the agent’s message included any of these elements.
Detailed prompts are provided in
Appendix D
.
This sensitivity detection module along all the conditions and the sensitive reminder UI was kept consistent across conditions.

4.3.
Measurement

After the interactive discussion session with the assigned LLM agent, participants completed a post-hoc survey measuring following variables to capture their perceptions of the agent as well as individual differences.

4.3.1.
Independent Variables.

In addition to the manipulated factors (personalization type and autonomy level of the LLM agent), we measured the following individual differences.

Personal Agency:

Personal agency was measured using a 8-item scale from
Smith et al. 2000
.
The eight questions are on a 4-point Likert scale, and a higher score means higher personal agency.
We define the personal agency value for each individual as the average rating of the eight questions.
The internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of all eight questions was 0.87 in our sample, which showed high reliability.

Interpersonal Agency:

Interpersonal agency was measured using a 5-item scale from
Smith et al. 2000
.
The five questions are on a 4-point Likert scale, and a higher score means higher interpersonal agency.
We define the interpersonal agency value for each individual as the average rating of the five questions.
The internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of all five questions was 0.79 in our sample, which showed high reliability.

AI Literacy:

AI literacy was measured using a 12-item scale from
Wang et al. 2023a
.
The 12 questions are on a 7-point Likert scale, and a higher score means higher personal agency (three items are in reverse form).
We define the AI literacy value for each individual as the average rating of the 12 questions.
The internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of all 12 questions was 0.81 in our sample, which showed high reliability.

Gender:

We provided four options for participants to select: “Male”, “Female”, “Non-binary / third gender”, and “Prefer not to say”.

Age:
We provided six options for participants to select their age: “18-24”, “25-34”, “35-44”, “45-54”, “55-64”, “64 or above”.

Education:
We provided seven options for participants to select: “Some school, no degree”, “High school graduate”, “Some college, no degree”, “Bachelor’s degree”, “Master’s degree”, “Professional degree”, and “Doctorate degree”.
We converted the seven options to integers 1 to 7, with 1 corresponding to “Some school, no degree” and 7 to “Doctorate degree”.

4.3.2.
Mediators.

The mediator variables in our experiment are perceived sensitivity, perceived control and perceived usefulness.

Perceived Sensitivity
:
The perceived information sensitivity is a binary variable with “Yes” and “No”.

Perceived Control
:
The perceived control was measured using a 4-item scale.
The four questions are on a 5-point Likert scale, and a higher score means higher personal agency.
We define the perceived control value for each individual as the average rating of the four questions.
The internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of all four questions was 0.95 in our sample, which showed high reliability.

Perceived Usefulness
:
The perceived usefulness was measured using a 4-item scale.
The four questions are on a 5-point Likert scale, and a higher score means higher personal agency.
We define the perceived usefulness value for each individual as the average rating of the four questions.
The internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of all four questions was 0.96 in our sample, which showed high reliability.

4.3.3.
Dependent Variables.

We have three dependent variables that associate with Personalization-Privacy Dilemma.

Privacy Concern
:
We asked participants to report their level of privacy concern on a 7-point Likert scale.

Trust
:
The perceived control was measured using a 12-item scale from
Jian et al. 2000
.
The twelve questions are on a 7-point Likert scale, and a higher score means higher personal agency.
We define the trust value for each individual as the average rating of the twelve questions.
The internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of all twelve questions was 0.92 in our sample, which showed high reliability.

Willingness to Use
:
We asked participants to report their level of willingness to use this LLM agent on a 7-point likert scale.

Participants

4.4.2.
Validation Checks

To eliminate uncertainty caused by the LLM that drove the agent and the sensitivity detection module, we conducted post-hoc validation checks on participants’ experiences to ensure the validity of our experimental manipulations in two aspects:
(1) We verified that all participants in the Basic personalization condition encountered at least one disclosure of their user-labeled sensitive information.
(2) We verified that all participants in the Intermediate autonomy condition received at least one
correct reminder
(a reminder triggered when sensitive information labeled by the participant was actually included in the agent’s message).

User-labeled Sensitive Information Coding.

To conduct such validation checks, we needed to detect whether participants’ pre-survey sensitive information items appeared in the LLM agent’s generated messages.
This required coding 450 participants
\times
5 agent-generated messages each.
We developed an LLM-based coding procedure with human calibration, a method increasingly used in large-scale annotation
(
Tai et al. 2024
)
.
A human coder from the research team manually coded 60 messages based on each participant’s three sensitive items (marking 1 if present, 0 if absent), yielding 180 coded instances.
We then designed a prompt (see
Appendix E
) for gpt-4o to perform the same task on the same set.
Inter-rater reliability between the human and LLM coder showed substantial agreement (Cohen’s
\kappa=0.894
).
After manual review of the few disagreements confirmed that the LLM’s reasoning was sound, we deemed the LLM coder reliable for coding the full dataset.

Validation Check (1): User-Defined Sensitive Information in the Agent Generated Messages.

In the Basic personalization condition, the agent’s generated responses contained at least one piece of user-defined sensitive information (Mean = 2.43, Min = 1, Max = 5), confirming that this manipulation allowed disclosure without privacy filtering.
In contrast, disclosures were rare in the No personalization (Mean = 0.02, Min = 0, Max = 1) and Privacy-aware personalization (Mean = 0.013, Min = 0, Max = 1) conditions.
Specifically, three participants in the No personalization condition and two participants in the Privacy-aware personalization condition were labeled by the LLM coder as having encountered one sensitive item in the chat, although theoretically these values should have been zero.
We then manually reviewed the model’s reasoning logs, the original chat transcripts, and the corresponding user-defined sensitive items, and found that none of these cases actually included user-defined sensitive information.
For example, when a participant had label one of their financial difficulties as a piece of sensitive information, the LLM coder mistakenly labeled non-sensitive budget discussions as sensitive due to overlap with the financial context.

Validation Check (2): Model-Driven Sensitive Reminders in the Interaction Session.

As introduced in
Section 4.2.4
, our chat system included a sensitivity detection module that provided reminders about potential privacy risks.

Since model judgments of sensitivity may differ from participants’ own labels, not all reminders could be guaranteed to be correct.
To ensure that participants in the Intermediate autonomy condition experienced at least one genuine “human takeover” moment, we required that they received at least one correct reminder.
We identified two types of errors:
Over-remind
(false positives), where a participant received a reminder for non-sensitive content, and
Miss-remind
(a false negative), where no reminder was shown for sensitive content.
Participants were excluded from the final dataset if they met one of two specific conditions: (a) they experienced an
Over-remind
event despite none of their generated messages containing any sensitive information, or (b) they experienced a
Miss-remind
without ever receiving a single correct sensitivity reminder.
This validation process was conducted iteratively. From our initial cohort of 450 participants, we identified and excluded 14 individuals (three due to the over-reminding criterion and 11 due to the miss-reminding criterion). These participants were replaced with new ones, and the validation process was repeated until all samples in the final dataset were confirmed to be valid.

4.5.
Methodological Limitations

Our method has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results.

First, our study examined two common daily contexts: a professional meeting and a personal trip discussion.
Although we did not intentionally study task effects, and the random effects of scenario were not significant in our analyses, the findings may not be fully generalizable to the broader spectrum of human-agent interactions.
Different contexts may shape users’ needs for personalization and their sensitivity to privacy in distinct ways.
Future research could investigate how these varying contextual factors influence user perceptions of LLM agents.

Second, we collected and used real participants’ personal data to personalize the LLM agents in order to ensure meaningful user perceptions of the task experience.
This approach introduces two sources of uncertainty:
(1) Each participant provided three pieces of sensitive information that they did not want other entities in the discussion to know.
However, we could not guarantee that all sensitive information would be revealed by the LLM agent during the discussion.
As a result, in the Basic personalization condition, participants may have encountered different amounts of sensitive information in the agent-generated messages.
To ensure the validity of the manipulation, we conducted pre-testing during system development and post-hoc checks of conversation logs to confirm that each participant in the Basic personalization condition encountered at least one instance of sensitive information in the generated messages.
(2) All conditions of the LLM agents included sensitivity checks for each generated response to avoid this becoming a confounding factor.
This meant it might be possible that participants in all three conditions have encountered the same number of sensitive notifications.
However, only in the Intermediate autonomy condition did the agent’s behavior (whether to automatically send or request confirmation) depend on the outcome of the sensitivity checks.
To ensure the validity of this manipulation, we conducted post-hoc checks of the conversation logs to confirm that each participant in the Intermediate autonomy condition received at least one confirmation request from the LLM agent.

5.1.

Each model included the main effects of personalization, autonomy, and their interaction, along with individual-difference covariates (AI literacy, personal agency, interpersonal agency, age, gender, and education background).
Including main and interaction effects in the same model allows us to directly test whether the influence of personalization depends on autonomy level while controlling for both factors simultaneously
(
Gelman and Hill 2007
)
.
A random intercept for scenario was included to account for heterogeneity between vignettes, as the influence of scenario itself was not the focus of this study.
During preprocessing, continuous covariates (AI literacy, personal agency, interpersonal agency) were z-standardized to improve interpretability and comparability of regression coefficients
(
Gelman 2008
)
.
Gender and education were dummy-coded as categorical variables.
For age, which was originally numeric, only centering was applied to mitigate collinearity
(
Harrison et al. 2018
)
.
Models were estimated with REML using the
lmerTest
package and achieved stable convergence.
We excluded participants who selected “Prefer not to say” for the education question, resulting in a final sample of 449 observations included in the mixed-effects models.
Model fit was assessed with AIC/BIC and explained variance (marginal and conditional

Specifically,
compared to Basic personalization, both No personalization and Privacy-aware personalization have significant negative effects on privacy concerns (supporting H1a) and significant positive effects on trust (supporting H1b) and willingness to use (supporting H1c).

For example, the coefficient for Privacy-aware personalization condition for the privacy concern is
-1.391
, indicating an estimated decrease of 1.391 points on the 7-point privacy concern scale when users interact with the LLM agent was personalized with consideration of individual privacy preferences, compared to Basic personalization without considering users’ privacy preferences.

5.3.
H2: The Effects of Autonomy Levels of LLM Agents Are Not Simply Linear

The effects of autonomy levels of LLM agents are not simply linear across higher or lower levels, and thus H2a and H2b are not supported. H2c is supported.

More specifically, as compared to No autonomy, LLM agents with Intermediate autonomy have significant negative effects on privacy concern and significant positive effects on trust.
In contrast, Full autonomy showed no significant effects, suggesting that interacting with LLM agents under No autonomy and Full autonomy led to similar levels of privacy concern and trust.
No significant differences in willingness to use were found across the three autonomy levels.

\times

Perceived usefulness.

Privacy-aware personalization had the highest proportion of participants perceiving the LLM agent as useful (89%), compared to No personalization (86%) and Basic personalization (81%).

H5a & H6a: Mediation Effects of Perceived Sensitivity.

As shown in

).
Correspondingly,

Thus,

By contrast, as shown in
Table 4
,

6.3.
H5b & H6b: Mediation Effects of Perceived Control.

Personalization also significantly increased perceived control (
a_{2no}=0.67
,
a_{2privacy}=0.66
in
Table 2
), which was negatively associated with privacy concern and positively associated with trust and willingness.
As shown in
Table 3
,
in the no autonomy condition, both No personalization and Privacy-aware personalization had significant indirect effects via control on all three outcomes (concern, trust, and willingness).
However, these mediation effects became nonsignificant in the intermediate autonomy condition.

For example, the indirect effect of No personalization on trust through perceived control dropped from 0.42 at no autonomy to 0.17 at intermediate autonomy, with a significant index of moderated mediation (–0.25 [–0.46, –0.05]).
This pattern suggests that intermediate autonomy moderates the mediating role of control, such that the pathway is only active under no autonomy.
H5b is partially supported.

In addition to moderating these mediation effects,
Table 4
shows that
intermediate autonomy directly increased perceived control (
a_{w2}=0.48
) and indirectly influenced all three outcomes through control (–0.23 on concern, +0.20 on trust, +0.21 on willingness).
H6b is thus supported.

As shown in
Table 2
, compared with basic personalization, privacy-aware personalization significantly increased perceived usefulness (
a_{3privacy}=0.46
), while no personalization did not (
a_{3no}=0.29
, n.s.).
Perceived usefulness was strongly associated with trust (

In the no autonomy condition, privacy-aware personalization significantly influenced trust and willingness via perceived usefulness, and no personalization significantly influenced willingness.
However, these indirect effects became nonsignificant in the intermediate autonomy condition, with significant indices of moderated mediation.
These findings indicate that

As shown in
Table 4
,

7.
Discussion

In this section, we draw on the study results to first discuss how LLM agent autonomy reshapes the Personalization-Privacy Dilemma (
Section 7.1
).
We then discuss how to design LLM agent autonomy for trustworthy LLM agent systems by focusing on how autonomy supports human control that aligns with user expectations to mitigate privacy concerns and build trust (
Section 7.2
), and how it can enable effective human oversight to mitigate privacy risks (
Section 7.3
).

7.1.
Personalization-Privacy Paradox in LLM Agents: Autonomy Matters!

Prior studies on the personalization-privacy paradox have largely focused on non-agentic AI systems such as recommender systems
(
Karwatzki et al. 2017
;
Sheng et al. 2008
;
Aguirre et al. 2016
;
Cloarec 2020
)
, where system actions are limited to generating content outputs and user privacy concerns primarily arise from institutional data collection
(
Prunkl 2024
)
.
Our study extends this paradigm to LLM agents, which differ from non-agentic systems by endowing varying levels of autonomy in agents’ actions.
It introduces novel privacy risks and privacy concerns.
On one hand, large language models are poor at aligning with individual privacy preferences
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
, and when autonomy is granted, these vulnerabilities can extend into the environments the agent interacts with
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
.
On the other hand, granting action autonomy inherently entails a relinquishing of human control, heightening users’ sensitivity to privacy risks.
Such concerns move beyond institutional data practices to
interpersonal and contextual risks
.
For example, an autonomous agent used for interpersonal communication may inadvertently disclose third-party information
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
;
Goyal et al. 2024
)
, while in professional settings it may raise concerns about confidentiality or reputational damage
(
Goyal et al. 2024
;
Leong et al. 2024
)
, thereby posing new challenges for user trust in agentic AI systems
(
Raheem and Hossain 2025
)
.
In our results, we observed a pattern of personalization effects (
Section 5.2
) similar to prior studies: personalization without considering user privacy preferences increased privacy concerns and reduced trust and willingness to use, whereas personalization that considered privacy preferences reduced privacy concerns and enhanced trust and willingness to use.

However, our findings also revealed that Intermediate autonomy of an LLM agent flattened the effects of personalization (
Section 5.4
), indicating that autonomy fundamentally shapes how acceptable personalization feels to users.
This suggests the central role of autonomy in addressing the personalization-privacy paradox for LLM agents, and that the effects of personalization cannot be understood in isolation from autonomy.
The HCI and AI communities have invested significant effort in
model alignment
, aiming to ensure that model outputs content aligns with human values
(
Gabriel 2020
)
, including privacy preferences
(
Shaikh et al. 2025
)
, thereby mitigating privacy concerns and fostering trust
(
Kirk et al. 2024
;
Shen et al. 2024
)
.

Our study suggests that, rather than solely pursuing perfect model-output alignment, balancing agent autonomy with user control offers a promising alternative for addressing the personalization-privacy dilemma, enabling users to benefit from more customized experiences without being deterred by growing privacy concerns.

This highlights a complementary axis of alignment beyond output content:
the alignment of autonomy
.
This concerns the boundaries of when agents should (and should not) act autonomously on behalf of users, and how users retain or exercise control.
In this sense, autonomy alignment becomes a key determinant of whether personalization is perceived as beneficial or risky.
Our results resonate with emerging views of multiple types of alignment in LLM agent systems
(
Goyal et al. 2024
)
, which extend beyond traditional output alignment to include autonomy and agency alignment.

7.2.
Designing LLM Agent Autonomy for Human Control and Trust

Delegate user control when
necessary
.

Interestingly, users interacting with Intermediate autonomy agents reported greater perceived control, lower privacy concerns, and higher trust and willingness compared to those with No autonomy agents (
Table 3
).
This seems paradoxical, as users in the No autonomy condition technically held more control: they always reviewed and confirmed whether to send the agent’s responses.
Similar patterns have been observed in prior work, where requiring constant user approval produced decision fatigue
(
Echterhoff et al. 2024
;
Ahmed and Pardaev 2025
)
and improper delegation design reduced sense of agency
(
Steyvers and Kumar 2024
;
Zanatto et al. 2024
)
, as responsibility was offloaded onto users rather than supported by the system.
In contrast, Intermediate autonomy delegated control only when potential privacy risks were detected, aligning better with users’ actual needs.
This suggests that effective autonomy design requires carefully selecting the “delegation moments” that align with user expectations and risk perceptions, rather than maximizing oversight in every step.
Moreover, these “delegation moments” could be further personalized: different contexts may involve different categories of risk (e.g., financial disclosure in family discussions versus reputational concerns in professional settings), and individuals vary in their sensitivity to these risks.
Our individual-differences results suggest that people with different traits (e.g., higher or lower AI literacy) may hold different boundaries of acceptable personalization and autonomy, highlighting the potential for autonomy design that adapts to both situational and personal factors.

Design LLM agent autonomy informed by the underlying psychological processes.

Current LLM agent designs of autonomy have explored multiple approaches but lack systematic guidelines.
For example, Operator asks users for confirmation when pre-defined sensitive domains such as finance or health are involved
(
OpenAI 2025b
)
, while Cursor asks users at the beginning of a session to choose the level of oversight they prefer (always confirm vs. execute automatically)
(
Cursor Documentation 2025
)
.
Our moderated mediation analysis (
Table 3
) reveal the psychological pathways through which the autonomy level and personalization collectively influence users’ trust, privacy concerns, and willingness, which can provide guidance for LLM agent design.
For example, if the design goal is to improve user’s willingness to use, perceived control and usefulness can be used as additional signals to guide how the autonomy and personalization should be co-designed, as we found these variables to be the significant mediators.

7.3.
Enhance Human Oversight for Mitigating Privacy Risks

In addition to designing agent autonomy to improve subjective perceptions as discussed in
Section 7.2
(e.g., reducing privacy concerns and increasing trust), we emphasize the importance of supporting effective human oversight of privacy leakage.
As shown in
5(a)
, only 62.7% of participants in Basic personalization condition recognized that the LLM agent’s responses contained information that they had just reported as sensitive and wanted to keep private. This means that even if the remaining 37.3% were given privacy control, they would fail to identify privacy leakage and such control mechanisms can fall short in practice.
Notably, the rate of sensitive-information recognition improved in the Intermediate autonomy condition compared with both Full and No autonomy. This further shows that Intermediate autonomy, which features a pre-selection mechanism that interrupts users only when sensitivity is likely, not only enhances subjective perceptions but also improves objective oversight efficacy.
In this way, Intermediate autonomy provides an opportunity to gather meaningful user feedback (e.g., through user oversight behaviors), which can then support model personalization approaches such as reinforcement learning.
Achieving perfect model alignment is far from straightforward, because privacy preferences are highly subjective
(
Lee and Weber 2025
)
, contextual
(
Zhang et al. 2024b
)
, dynamic and subject to change over time
(
Goldfarb and Tucker 2012
)
, and often difficult to elicit reliably
(
Zhang et al. 2024a
)
.
These autonomy-supported feedback approaches may therefore be more practical than aiming for static, perfect alignment with individual users, while still moving systems closer to what users expect.

8.
Conclusion

In this work, we take the first step to study how different personalization and autonomy levels in LLM agents affect users’ privacy concerns, trust, willingness to use, and the underlying psychological processes.
We conducted a 3
\times
3 between-participants experiment (
N=450
), randomly assigning each participant to one condition where an LLM agent acted on their behalf in an interpersonal communication scenario.
Our results show that personalization without considering users’ privacy preferences increases privacy concerns and decreases trust and willingness to use.
Unlike prior non-agentic AI systems, intermediate autonomy in an agent’s actions attenuates these personalization effects by moderating the mediators in the underlying psychological process.
Rather than relying solely on perfect model alignment, our findings point to a promising path through designing appropriate levels of autonomy in LLM agents that support user control, thereby mitigating privacy concerns and improving trust and willingness to use.
