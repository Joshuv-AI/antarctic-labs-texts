Small hole polarons in yellow phase δ \delta - CsPbI 3 \text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
Yun Liu

Abstract
A heterophase containing both the optically active α \alpha - CsPbI 3 \text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} and non-active δ \delta - CsPbI 3 \text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} has been demonstrated as an efficient white light emitter. This has challenged the conventional perspective that non-active phases of perovskites are undesirable in any perovskite-based optoelectronic devices. To understand the role that yellow phase δ \delta - CsPbI 3 \text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} plays in the light emission process, we performed a systematic computational study on its electronic and optical properties, which are relatively unexplored in the literature. Using the Fröhlich model we showed that both the electron and hole exhibit moderate coupling to longitudinal optical phonons. Explicit density functional theory calculations show that small hole polarons exist with a formation energy of − 96 -96 meV, corresponding to the contraction of the Pb-I bonds within a [ PbI 6 ​ ] \text{[}\text{PbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}\text{]} octahedron. Nudged elastic band calculations show that the hole polaron can hop into neighboring [ PbI 6 ​ ] \text{[}\text{PbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}\text{]} octahedral sites with a small activation barrier of 2.1 2.1 meV. Molecular dynamics simulations also show that the hole polaron exhibit periodic localization and delocalization behavior similar to carrier hopping with a characteristic lifetime of 0.3 0.3 ps. Our results have elucidated the role that δ \delta - CsPbI 3 \text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} play in the self-trapped emission in perovskite-based white light emitting diodes by supporting the presence of a localized small hole polaron.

,

,

The high optoelectronic performance of these perovskites-based devices rely on their crystal structures staying in the optically active black phases at ambient conditions.
During normal device operations, methylammonium lead iodides (
\text{MAPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
) are prone to chemical degradation upon exposure to moisture and oxygen, leading to the formation of undesirable side products such as
\text{PbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

,

,

.
Formamidinium (FA) and Cs-based compositions have improved chemical stability compared to their MA counterpart, but their optically active
\alpha

\delta
-phases respectively

As these

Therefore, various strategies such as encapsulation, surface passivation and chemical alloying have been developed to improve their phase stability
[
10

7
,
4
,

While conventional wisdom dictates that the presence of yellow phases need to be minimized whenever possible, recent evidence suggests that they might play an important role in many interesting light emission phenomena.
For example, intrinsic quantum confinement with the co-existence of
\alpha
and
\delta
-
\text{FAPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
has manifested as oscillatory absorption spectra

It was hypothesized that the
\delta

\alpha
-phases with a typical length scale of
10-20

The transformation into
\delta
-
\text{FAPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}

Light emission from these quantum confined regions can be potentially used as single-photon sources analogous to colloidal quantum dots
[
14
,
15
]
.

In addition, efficient and bright white light emitting diodes (LED) have been synthesized based on the heterophases of
\alpha
and
\delta
-
\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
[
16

17

.
The carrier injection and transport were dominated by the optically active
\alpha
-phase, with the charges ultimately injected into the
\delta
-phase where efficient radiative recombination occurred
via

The synergetic cooperation between the two phases are hypothesized to achieve the high efficiency and brightness observed in the white LED.
A more recent investigation has found that the heterophases are a mixture of
\gamma

\delta
-phases, whereby alloying Cl

.

Carrier self-trapping in semiconductors occurs when excited carriers and the locally distorted lattice form a quasi-particle called polaron
[
19
]
.
Polarons can be classified into two types based on the strength of the electron-phonon coupling.
Large polarons are formed due to the long-range interaction between electrons/holes and the longitudinal optical (LO) phonons.
In halide perovskites, large polarons are known to be responsible for the long carrier diffusion lengths
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
and enhanced effective masses
[
24
]
.
Their formation kinetics have been observed in pseudocubic
\text{CsPbBr}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, and first-principles calculation showed that the localized charge density associated with the hole polaron can extend over 8 unit cells in one direction
[
25
]
.

Stronger coupling between charge carriers and phonons leads to larger local lattice distortions that provide the driving force for small polarons to form.
The motion of small polarons have been observed in low dimensional perovskites and are characterized by incoherent phonon assisted hopping with much smaller carrier mobility
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
.
The existence and formation mechanism of small polarons in 3D perovskites are still not conclusive
[
30
]
.
For
\delta
-
\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, even the most basic optoelectronic properties are not understood, and it is therefore not clear what role it plays in the light emission processes in the heterophase devices.
Here in this work, we present a systematic investigation of the electronic structure of
\delta
-
\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
.
We utilized a combination of Fröhlich polaron model, density functional theory (DFT) and
ab initio
molecular dynamics (MD) calculations to show that small hole polarons are stable in
\delta
-
\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, which can potentially support carrier self-trapping and STE-mediated emissions.

We want to highlight the importance of dimensionality for localized carrier formation.
In 3D and 2D systems, the local lattice deformations arising from the strong coupling between carrier and phonons generate a potential barrier acting as a trap, and free carriers need to overcome this barrier to self-trap

38

While

\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}

The pseudo-1D electronic structure might favor easier formation of the self-trapped carriers in
\delta
-
\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
compared to its black phase counterpart.

The presence of small polarons can affect charge mobility in optoelectronic devices
[
56
,
57
]

We therefore performed nudged elastic band (NEB) calculations to better understand its hopping behavior and obtain its activation barrier.
As the hole polaron is mainly localized onto a single
\text{[}\text{PbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}\text{]}
octahedron, it can hop in two general directions.
One is along the crystallographic
b

\text{[}\text{PbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}\text{]}
octahedra are sharing edges along their square bipyramidal basal plane, labeled as path A in Fig.

The hopping distance is approximately the distance between the neighboring Pb atoms at
4.89
Å  with an energy barrier of
2.1

3

The hole polaron can also hop along path B whereby the neighboring octahedra are sharing edges in one of their square pyramids.
The hopping distance is approximately the same as path A at
4.81
Å  with a corresponding energy barrier of
1.5
meV.

Along both hopping paths, the hole polaron needs to migrate between two nearest neighbor I-I atoms.
The larger energetic barrier along path A is due to the smaller I-I distances at
4.40
Å  compared to that of
4.67
Å  along B.
However, hopping along path B would mean that the polaron need to change direction after each hop, so as to travel along the pseudo-1D

b

This means that for macroscopic transport, the polaron will hop along path A even though the energetic barrier is higher.
These energetic barriers are around 2 orders of magnitude smaller than some of the barriers observed in many oxides

58

The small hopping barrier also means that the presence of small localized hole polaron should not reduce the carrier mobility in

-

Conclusion

In conclusion, we systematically investigated charge localization and carrier self-trapping in
\delta
-
\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
.
The Fröhlich model predicts moderate coupling between the LO phonons and electrons and holes with the Fröhlich polaron constants of around
3.25-4.22
and Schultz radii of
\sim
20 Å  at
300
K.
Explicit DFT calculations using hybrid functionals and advanced structure searching methods show that small hole polaron is stable with a formation energy of
-96
meV.
NEB calculations show that the energetic barrier for hopping is only
2.1
meV, much smaller than typical values associated with small polarons, and can facilitate high carrier mobility in
\delta
-
\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
based optoelectronic devices.
MD simulations with the electron and holes in a triplet spin configuration further shows that the hole polaron exhibit characteristic hopping with a period of
0.3
ps.
Our results might have elucidated the role that
\delta
-
\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
play in the self-trapped emission in perovskite-based white LED by supporting the presence of a localized small hole polaron.
There are some reports suggesting that electron polaron might be stable in
\alpha
-
\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
clusters
[
60
]
, highlighting the possibility that STE might exist in the heterophases of
\delta
-
\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\alpha
-
\text{CsPbI}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, with the electron polaron and hole polaron localized onto the different perovskite phases.
To fully understand the nature of self-trapped emission in perovskite based white LEDs, a systematic study of the structural and electronic properties of the heterophases is needed.
