Modulatory roles of the vagus nerve and nicotine in bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in rats
Authors: Parisa Askarizadeh, Amirreza Dehghanian, Farzaneh Ketabchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary fibrosis is a chronic and progressive lung disease with limited therapeutic options. This study aimed to investigate the roles of the vagus nerve and nicotine in bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in rats.MethodsMale Sprague–Dawley rats were randomly assigned to five groups: Sham (SHAM; intratracheal [I.T.] saline), bleomycin (BLM; 2 mg/kg, I.T.), bleomycin with right cervical vagotomy (BLVR), bleomycin with nicotine (0.5 mg/kg/day, intraperitoneal [I.P.]) (BLNC), and bleomycin with vagotomy plus nicotine (BLVN).ResultsTwenty-four hours after bleomycin or saline administration, total white blood cell (WBC) counts and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) increased in all groups, with the BLVR group exhibiting significantly higher values than the BLM, BLNC, and BLVN groups. On day 14, NLR in the BLVR group and WBC counts in the BLM and BLVR groups remained elevated. Lung malondialdehyde levels were higher in the BLM and BLVR groups compared with the BLNC and BLVN groups. During mechanical ventilation, peak expiratory pressures were higher in the BLM, BLVR, and BLNC groups than in the SHAM group, whereas the BLVN group showed values comparable to the SHAM group. Lung compliance was lowest in the BLVR group but was preserved in the BLVN group. Histopathological examination confirmed reduced lung injury in the BLVN group compared with the BLM and BLVR groups.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the anti-inflammatory role of the vagus nerve and suggest that nicotine—particularly in combination with vagotomy—may confer protective effects against early-stage pulmonary fibrosis by mitigating inflammation, oxidative stress, and pulmonary dysfunction.

Introduction

Pulmonary fibrosis is a chronic, progressive restrictive lung disease characterized by fibroblast and myofibroblast proliferation, excessive extracellular matrix deposition, and consequent disruption of normal lung structure and function [1, 2]. Despite substantial progress in understanding its complex pathophysiology, effective therapeutic options remain limited. Current treatments, including anti-inflammatory agents, anti-fibrotic drugs, and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) inhibitors, provide only modest clinical benefits. As a result, the disease continues to carry a poor prognosis, with mortality typically occurring within 3–5 years after diagnosis [3, 4].

Over the past decade, the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway (CAP) has attracted considerable attention as an important mechanism involved in the regulation of inflammation [5, 6]. This has led to the hypothesis that vagal stimulation may serve as a potential therapeutic strategy for various inflammatory and autoimmune disorders [7]. However, its efficacy remains a subject of debate across different pathological conditions [8, 9]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that both electrical and pharmacological activation of the vagus nerve can exert protective and anti-inflammatory effects across a range of inflammatory disorders [10, 11]. These effects are not mediated by muscarinic receptors but rather rely on the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR) [12]. Vagal efferent stimulation leads to the release of acetylcholine (ACh), which binds to α7nAChRs expressed on inflammatory cells such as splenic macrophages, subsequently inhibiting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [13].

The lung is among the organs significantly regulated by the vagus nerve. Inflammatory cytokine receptors are expressed on the afferent nerve endings of the pulmonary parasympathetic system, allowing inflammatory signals to be transmitted to the central nervous system. In response, efferent vagal fibers stimulate postganglionic cholinergic neurons within the lung, leading to the release of ACh which acts on α7nAChRs expressed on alveolar macrophages and infiltrating leukocytes, ultimately suppressing the production of chemokines and pro-inflammatory cytokines [14–16]. While numerous studies support the anti-inflammatory and protective roles of the vagus nerve, contrasting evidence indicates that, under certain conditions, vagal activity may instead contribute to pulmonary inflammation [17–19]. These conflicting results may be attributed to variations in experimental models, including the type, severity, and duration of lung injury, the nature of the inflammatory stimuli, and the differential involvement of cholinergic receptor subtypes [6]. For instance, the role of the cholinergic nervous system in pulmonary fibrosis has not yet been fully elucidated; however, emerging evidence suggests that vagal signaling contributes to inflammation in this context. Both unilateral cervical vagotomy and knockout of α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (α7nAChRs) have been shown to attenuate lung fibrosis. In addition, stimulating α7nAChRs in cultured fibroblasts increases the expression of fibrogenic genes [20, 21]. These findings underscore the complex role of vagal modulation in lung injury and fibrosis, highlighting the need for further investigation. Muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, which are abundantly expressed in the lungs, are implicated in promoting fibroblast activation and fibrosis progression [22]. Thus, deletion of nicotinic receptors may enhance muscarinic signaling, while unilateral cervical vagotomy may reduce the effects of muscarinic receptors. These interactions suggest that the combined effects of vagotomy and nicotine administration may lead to distinct physiological outcomes—a hypothesis assessed in the present study. Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory effects of the vagus nerve may vary depending on the duration of the study, potentially differing between short-term and long-term responses. The present study aimed to investigate the effects of 14 days of unilateral cervical vagotomy, with or without daily administration of low-dose nicotine, in a rat model of bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis. Specifically, we evaluated total and differential white blood cell count (WBC), lipid peroxidation, systemic hemodynamics, respiratory mechanics, and histopathological alterations in lung tissue.

Material and methods

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Shiraz, Iran (approval code: IR.SUMS.AEC.1402.091). All animal experiments were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines and regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Forty male Sprague–Dawley rats weighing 220–300 g were obtained from the central animal facility of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences and housed in standard laboratory cages under controlled environmental conditions, including a temperature of 24–26 °C, relative humidity of 40–60%, and a 12:12-h light/dark cycle. Animals had ad libitum access to food and water. They were randomly assigned to one of the following five experimental groups: SHAM; bleomycin (BLM); right cervical vagotomy + bleomycin (BLVR); bleomycin + nicotine (BLNC); right cervical vagotomy + bleomycin + nicotine (BLVN). N = 8 in each group.

Protocol for the first day

Animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal (I.P.) administration of ketamine (60 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) and placed on a temperature-controlled surgical table to maintain normothermia throughout the procedure. Blood samples were obtained from the tail vein for determination of total and differential WBC, as well as for measurement of plasma malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration, a biomarker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress. The cervical skin was incised, and the connective tissues, neck muscles, and esophagus were carefully separated from the trachea while the animals maintained spontaneous breathing under an inspired oxygen fraction (FiO₂) of 50%. Bleomycin was administered intratracheally (I.T.) at a dose of 2 mg/kg in 1 mL of saline, delivered over 30 min while the animals were rotated through six different body positions. This dosing protocol was designed based on previous studies and pilot experiments to ensure uniform drug distribution between the left and right lungs [23, 24]. In the SHAM group, the cervical incision was performed as in the other groups; however, the vagus nerve was left intact, and an equivalent volume of saline was administered instead of bleomycin. Following the procedure, the muscle, fascia, and skin were sutured, and 4% lidocaine was applied to the surgical site to minimize postoperative pain. After the procedure, conscious animals were returned to the animal facility and housed individually in clean cages with ad libitum access to food and water for 14 days. All surgical procedures were performed under aseptic conditions. Daily protocol

Blood samples were collected from the tail vein at 3 h, 24 h, and on days 7 and 14 after bleomycin administration to assess total and differential WBC counts. Body weight was monitored daily throughout the experimental period. In the nicotine-treated groups, nicotine was administered I.P. (0.5 mg/kg) per day [25] (Fig. 1C). In the non-treated groups, an equivalent volume of saline was administered for the same duration.

Protocol for Day 14

Animals were anesthetized with an I.P. injection of sodium thiopental (SAS, Colombia) at a dose of 60 mg/kg, with additional doses administered as needed to maintain a surgical level of anesthesia. Body temperature was maintained within the normal physiological range throughout the procedure. Blood samples were collected, a tracheostomy was performed, and the femoral artery was cannulated using a heparinized PE50 catheter. The arterial catheter was connected to a data acquisition system (PowerLab, ML786, AD Instruments, Australia) to continuously monitor systemic blood pressure and heart rate. After a 10-min steady-state period, the animal was connected to a mechanical ventilator with 50% oxygen (FiO₂ = 0.5), tidal volume of 12 mL/kg and respiratory rate of 70 breaths per minute. Expiratory tubes were connected to pressure transducers (MLT0699, AD Instruments, Australia), and airway pressures were continuously recorded using the PowerLab system for 60 min. To prevent clot formation during lung compliance measurements, heparin (100 U, I.P.; Alborz Darou, Iran) was administered 10 min before the end of the experiment to prevent intravascular coagulation during measurement of lung compliance. Animals were then euthanized under deep anesthesia. Following thoracotomy, static lung compliance was measured. The lungs were then excised, and the middle lobe of the right lung along with a portion of the upper left lobe were snap-frozen at − 70 °C for subsequent MDA analysis. The lower lobe of the right lung and the lower left lung were fixed and submitted to the pathology department for histological evaluation using hematoxylin–eosin and Masson's trichrome staining (Fig. 1D).

Right cervical vagotomy

Animals were anesthetized with I.P. injections of ketamine (60 mg/kg, BREMER PHARMA, Germany) and xylazine (10 mg/kg, Bioveta, Czech). Body temperature was maintained within the normal physiological range throughout the procedure. A midline cervical incision was made, and the overlying fascia and muscles were carefully dissected. The right cervical vagus nerve was gently isolated from the adjacent carotid artery and transected, with approximately 0.5 cm removed from both proximal and distal ends [47]. The muscle layer and skin were then sutured and the surgical site was cleaned. To minimize postoperative pain, 4% lidocaine (Kharazmi, Iran) was applied to the sutured area. Bleomycin was administered 7 days after vagotomy.

White blood cell counting

A 10 µL blood sample was diluted with 190 µL of Marcano’s solution (Iran), and the total leukocyte count was determined using a hemocytometer under 10 × magnification with a light microscope (Olympus, Japan). Differential WBC counts were assessed by preparing blood smears stained with Wright’s solution. A total of 100 cells were examined under 100 × magnification, and the percentages of leukocyte subtypes were calculated [26]. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was subsequently computed.

Measurement of static lung compliance

Following a midline thoracotomy, the rib cages were gently retracted to prevent lung injury. A three-way stopcock was connected to the trachea, with one port attached to a pressure transducer and the other to a 50 mL air-filled syringe. Air was incrementally injected into the lungs in 2 mL steps over 5 s, followed by a 5-s equilibration period to allow airway pressure stabilization. Stabilized airway pressures were recorded at each step using the PowerLab system. Inflation continued until airway pressure reached approximately + 30 mmHg. The lungs were then deflated using the reverse procedure, removing 2 mL of air over 5 s at each step, followed by a 5-s equilibration period, until airway pressure returned to zero. Lung compliance and elastance (the inverse of compliance) curves were generated from the recorded pressures during both inflation and deflation phases [26, 27].

Measurement of tissue MDA levels

Tissue samples were stored at − 70 °C until the day of measurement. For homogenization, 50 mg of lung tissue was placed in a 2 mL tube containing 450 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and homogenized for 1 min. Next, 250 µL of 8% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, Sigma, Germany) was added to the homogenate. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. MDA levels in lung tissue were determined using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay. Briefly, 200 µL of each sample supernatant or standard solution (1,1,3,3-tetraethoxypropane; Merck, Germany) was mixed with 200 µL of a reagent mixture containing 0.25 N hydrochloric acid (HCl; Sigma, Germany), 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA; Sigma, Germany), and 0.8% thiobarbituric acid (TBA; Sigma, Germany). The mixture was incubated at 90 °C for 60 min, rapidly cooled on ice, and centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 10 min. Then, 100 µL of each reaction mixture was transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA) [26].

Preparation of lung histology

At the end of the experiment, the lower lobe of the right lung and the lower portion of the left lung were excised and fixed in 4% formalin for histological evaluation. Tissue processing and staining with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome were performed in the pathology department. Histological slides were assessed in a blinded manner by a pathologist using six pathological indices: perivascular hemorrhage, perivascular edema, alveolar hemorrhage, neutrophil infiltration, alveolar membrane thickening, and alveolar edema [28]. In addition, lung fibrosis was evaluated using the Ashcroft scoring model for Masson's trichrome staining [29].

Statistical analysis

All values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using SPSS version 23. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of data distribution. Normally distributed data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Parametric data within a period of time in each group were analyzed by repeated measured ANOVA with an appropriate post hoc test. Intergroup analysis was performed using a paired-t-test. Non-parametric data were analyzed using Kruskal–Wallis tests and Mann–Whitney U test. A significant difference was assumed when P < 0.05 and the confidence limits used were the 95% intervals.

In the BLM group, WBC counts increased at 3 h post-I.T. bleomycin injection (P = 0.040) and increased further at 24 h versus 0 h (P < 0.001) and 3 h (P = 0.004). At 7 and 14 d, counts remained above baseline (P = 0.04) but were lower than at 24 h (P = 0.003) and similar to 3 h (Fig. 2B).

In the BLVR group, WBC was unchanged at 3 h, increased at 24 h versus 0 h (P < 0.001) and 3 h (P = 0.003), declined on day 7 relative to 24 h (P = 0.01), but remained similar to 0 h and 3 h, and increased again on day 14 compared with 0 h (P < 0.001), 3 h (P = 0.01), and 7 d (P = 0.03), remaining comparable to 24 h (Fig. 2C).

In the BLNC group, WBC increased at 3 h (P < 0.001) and further at 24 h versus 0 h (P < 0.001). By day 7, counts declined, similar to 0 h but lower than 3 h (P = 0.03) and 24 h (P < 0.001). On day 14, WBC remained comparable to 0 h and 3 h, yet lower than 24 h (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2D).

In the BLVN group, WBC increased at 3 h versus 0 h (P = 0.01) and remained elevated at 24 h compared with 0 h (P = 0.014), with no difference from 3 h. At 7 d and 14 d, WBC showed no significant changes from earlier time points (Fig. 2E).

Changes in the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios

In the SHAM group, NLR increased at 24 h compared with baseline (P < 0.001). By day 7, it returned to baseline levels but was lower than at 3 h (P = 0.009) and 24 h (P < 0.001). At day 14, NLR decreased relative to 3 h (P = 0.002) and 24 h (P < 0.001), remaining comparable to baseline and day 7 (Fig. 2F).

In the BLM group, NLR increased at 3 h and 24 h compared with baseline (P < 0.001), with no difference between these two time points. By day 7, values were similar to baseline and 3 h but lower than 24 h (P < 0.001). At day 14, NLR decreased relative to 3 h and 24 h (P < 0.001) and remained comparable to baseline and day 7 (Fig. 2G).

In the BLVR group, NLR was unchanged between 0 and 3 h, increased at 24 h compared with 0 h (P < 0.001), declined by day 7 to levels comparable with 0 h and 3 h, but lower than 24 h (P < 0.001), and increased again by day 14 relative to 0 h and 7 d (P < 0.001), remaining similar to 3 h and 24 h (Fig. 2H).

In the BLNC group, NLR was elevated at 3 h and 24 h compared with baseline (P < 0.001), with no difference between these time points. By day 7, it returned to baseline but remained lower than 3 h and 24 h (P < 0.001). At day 14, NLR decreased relative to 3 h and 24 h (P < 0.001) and stayed comparable to baseline and day 7 (Fig. 2I).

In the BLVN group, NLR increased at 3 h versus baseline (P < 0.001) and peaked at 24 h (P < 0.001 vs. 0 h and 3 h). By day 7, values returned near baseline but remained lower than at 3 h and 24 h (P < 0.001), and by day 14, NLR declined further relative to 3 h and 24 h (P < 0.001), without significant differences from baseline or day 7 (Fig. 2J).

Comparison of changes in the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio

Baseline NLR values did not differ significantly among groups (Fig. 2K). At 3 h, NLR was higher in the BLNC group than in the SHAM group (P = 0.01). Conversely, the BLVN group showed lower values compared with the BLM (P = 0.009), BLVR (P = 0.005), and BLNC (P = 0.03) groups (Fig. 2L).

At 24 h, NLR in the BLM (P = 0.019) and BLVR (P = 0.005) groups were higher than in the SHAM group, with the BLVR group also exceeding the BLM group (P = 0.037). In the BLNC group, NLR was lower than in both the BLM and BLVR groups (P < 0.001). Similarly, in the BLVN group, NLR was lower than in the BLM (P = 0.006) and BLVR (P < 0.001) groups (Fig. 2M).

At 7 d, NLR was higher in the BLM group than in SHAM (P = 0.04), while BLVR (P = 0.01), BLNC (P < 0.001), and BLVN (P = 0.02) groups showed lower values than the BLM group. The values in the BLNC and BLVR groups did not differ significantly (P = 0.07) (Fig. 2N). At 14 d, NLR in the BLVR group exceeded both the SHAM and BLM groups (P < 0.001), whereas in the BLNC and BLVN groups were lower than the BLVR group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2O).

Maximum expiratory airway pressure (MEP) was higher in the BLM (P = 0.019), BLVR (P = 0.014), and BLNC (P = 0.018) groups compared with the SHAM group, with no other significant differences among groups (Fig. 4B). Vital capacity (VC) were lower in the BLM, BLVR, BLNC, and BLVN groups compared with the SHAM group (P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in VC among the BLM, BLVR, and BLNC groups. However, the BLVN group demonstrated a higher VC than the BLM (P = 0.016), BLVR (P < 0.001), and BLNC (P = 0.013) groups (Fig. 4C). A similar pattern was observed for total lung capacity (TLC), which was lower in the BLM, BLVR, BLNC, and BLVN groups compared with the SHAM group (P < 0.001). Furthermore, the BLVN group exhibited a higher TLC than the BLM (P = 0.016), BLVR (P < 0.001), and BLNC (P = 0.013) groups (Fig. 4D). As noted in the Methods, elastance is inversely related to lung compliance. Elastance in the BLM, BLVR, and BLNC groups were higher than that in the SHAM group (P < 0.001). In contrast, the BLVN group showed significantly lower elastance than the BLVR (P = 0.044) and BLNC (P = 0.001) groups (Fig. 4E).

Analysis of pressure–volume curves during deflation phase revealed several differences between groups (Fig. 4F). In the BLM group, the static lung compliance was lower than in the SHAM group at deflation volumes of 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 ml. The BLVR group showed lower compliance than the BLNC group at 6, 8, 10, and 12 ml, and were also less than the BLM, BLVN, and SHAM groups at 8, 10, and 12 ml. In the BLNC group, compliance was lower than in the SHAM group at 10, 12, and 14 ml. At 14 ml, compliance in the BLVN group was higher than in the BLM and BLNC groups, but at 12, 14, and 16 ml, it remained significantly lower than in the SHAM group (all comparisons, P < 0.05).

Discussion

Emerging evidence highlights the potential involvement of the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway in chronic inflammatory conditions [25, 26, 30–37], though conflicting reports suggest that parasympathetic activity may be pro-inflammatory under certain conditions [18–21, 38]. Nicotine has been reported to exert protective effects against pulmonary inflammation in various contexts, including asthma, pneumonia, acid-induced lung injury, and acute respiratory distress syndrome [25, 26, 36]. In the present study, right cervical vagotomy aggravated bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis, indicating a protective role of vagal signaling. Interestingly, co-administration of low-dose nicotine following vagotomy attenuated lung injury, as evidenced by improved lung structure and function, reduced inflammation, and lower oxidative stress, in both the acute (24 h) and chronic (14d) phases. These results suggest that stimulation of nicotinic receptors may partially compensate for the loss of vagal input and exert anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative effects via the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway.

The rise in WBCs within 24 h after I.T. administration of saline or bleomycin, followed by a decline by day 7, indicates the onset of an early acute inflammatory response that subsides during the first week. In the BLM group, WBC levels remained elevated at days 7 and 14 compared with baseline, indicating a sustained inflammatory response. Additionally, lung tissue MDA levels were significantly higher in the BLM group than in the SHAM group at day 14, reflecting persistent oxidative stress. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that bleomycin-induced inflammation may persist for several weeks [39, 40]. In the BLVR group, WBC levels were elevated during both 24 h and 7 days. These findings support the anti-inflammatory role of the vagus nerve in pulmonary fibrosis. Although studies on the role of the vagus nerve in lung fibrosis are limited, some have reported a protective effect of vagotomy, suggesting that activation of the α7nAChR may, under certain conditions, exacerbate lung injury [20, 21]. However, conflicting findings exist regarding the role of this receptor in modulating inflammatory diseases. For instance, a review article reported that α7nAChR expressed in alveolar macrophages and epithelial cells contributes to reducing lung injury and inflammation in chronic respiratory diseases, whereas inhibition of this receptor appears beneficial in malignancies. Thus, the function of α7nAChR may differ between inflammatory and anti-inflammatory conditions, depending on the severity and nature of lung injury [41]. The muscarinic receptor signaling also appears to play a role in fibrotic responses. Evidence indicates that acetylcholine promotes the proliferation of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts isolated from fibrotic lungs, an effect that is inhibited by muscarinic receptor antagonists [42]. Upregulation of muscarinic receptors has also been reported in various lung diseases [43], though their specific involvement in pulmonary fibrosis requires further investigation. In the present study, both the BLNC group and the BLVN group showed significantly lower WBC counts and tissue MDA levels compared to the BLM and BLVR groups, indicating reduced inflammation and oxidative stress. These findings are consistent with reports that nicotinic through activation of α7nAChR exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects in lung tissue [44].

The NLR is a widely used biomarker in inflammatory conditions, reflecting the balance between the innate immune response (neutrophils) and adaptive immunity (lymphocytes). An elevated NLR is associated with higher mortality rates in various chronic inflammatory diseases [45]. In the current study, a significant increase in NLR was observed in the BLM group 24 h after bleomycin administration consistent with previous studies [46]. The higher NLR observed in the BLVR group compared to the BLM group. In contrast, both the BLNC and BLVN groups showed reduced NLR values. Although histopathological scores in the BLNC and BLVN groups were higher than SHAM, they were notably lower than in the BLM and BLVR groups. Collectively, these observations indicate that vagus nerve—particularly through nicotinic receptors—may play a protective, anti-inflammatory role in bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis.

Hemodynamic assessments showed that MAP increased during mechanical ventilation in the SHAM, BLM, BLNC, and BLVN groups, whereas the BLVR group did not exhibit a significant change. HR remained stable across all groups under both resting and ventilated conditions. The lack of HR change following unilateral vagotomy is likely due to compensatory activity of the intact left vagus nerve [47]. The lower MAP observed in the BLVR group is unlikely to result from altered cardiac function but may instead reflect hypoxia-induced peripheral vasodilation secondary to severe lung injury. During mechanical ventilation, nicotine administration increased MAP in the BLNC group to levels comparable with the SHAM group, consistent with previous reports indicating that nicotine elevates blood pressure without significantly affecting HR [48]. In addition, the increased MAP observed during mechanical ventilation in the BLVN group may be attributed to the combined effects of nicotine and the reduced severity of lung injury in this group.

The right ventricular weight-to-body weight ratio (RVW/BW) was elevated in the BLVR group, likely reflecting increased pulmonary artery pressure secondary to bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis, which was further exacerbated by vagotomy. The RVW/BW ratio serves as an indirect indicator of pulmonary artery pressure, consistent with previous reports [24]. In the BLNC group, nicotine also increased the RVW/BW ratio, likely as a consequence of bleomycin-induced lung injury. Moreover, a direct effect of nicotine on cardiac remodeling—potentially mediated through α7nAChR activation and the development of pulmonary hypertension—has also been reported [49, 50].

Body weight loss was observed in all groups shortly after administration of bleomycin or saline, likely reflecting procedural stress. The BLNC group exhibited weight loss comparable to the BLM group, consistent with low-dose nicotine’s systemic effects on metabolic rate and appetite [51, 52]. This effect appeared independent of lung injury severity, as the BLNC group did not show aggravated pathology. In contrast, the BLVR group experienced less initial weight loss, potentially due to reduced α7nAChR-mediated metabolic stimulation [53]. The BLVN group did not display significant weight loss, suggesting that vagotomy may mitigate nicotine- or bleomycin-induced catabolic effects.

Mechanical ventilation revealed elevated mean expiratory airway pressure (MEP) in the BLM, BLVR, and BLNC groups compared with the SHAM group, whereas the BLVN group showed values comparable to SHAM. The increase in MEP likely reflects bleomycin-induced lung stiffness and small airway remodeling. Similar elevations of MEP in the BLM and BLNC groups suggest that bleomycin-induced injury may upregulate muscarinic acetylcholine receptor expression. Supporting this notion, enhanced expression of M2 and M3 receptors has been shown to promote fibroblast proliferation and airway smooth muscle remodeling in the lung [22]. Despite the presence of nicotine in the BLNC group, expiratory airway pressure was not reduced, in contrast to findings from previous studies [26]. In the BLVN group, vagotomy may have attenuated muscarinic receptor stimulation, thereby allowing nicotine to exert a more pronounced bronchodilatory effect [54]. Increased airway pressure is widely recognized as an indirect indicator of lung stiffness [55]. In fibrotic lungs, stiffened parenchyma necessitates greater pressure for expansion, increasing airway resistance during respiration. Our findings are consistent with this interpretation, as elevated expiratory pressures were accompanied by reductions in vital capacity (VC) and total lung capacity (TLC) in the BLM, BLVR, BLNC, and BLVN groups compared with SHAM. These observations align with previous findings by Schelegle et al., who reported that bleomycin administration rapidly decreases VC, TLC, and lung compliance [56].

The mean VC and TLC values in the BLVN group were higher than those in the BLM and BLVR groups, whereas MEP was lower. Since most pulmonary reflexes are mediated through parasympathetic pathways that generally induce airway constriction, vagotomy would be expected to decrease airway pressure [57]. However, the elevated pressure observed in the BLVR group—despite vagotomy—likely reflects fibrosis-related volume loss rather than the absence of parasympathetic modulation. These results suggest that, under fibrotic conditions, structural alterations in the lung exert a greater influence on airway pressure than autonomic regulation.

Lung compliance was significantly reduced in the BLM, BLVR, and BLNC groups compared with the SHAM group, indicating impaired pulmonary function following bleomycin exposure. Although compliance in the BLVN group was also lower than in the SHAM group, it remained higher than in the BLVR group. In contrast, lung elastance showed the opposite trend, with significant increases in the BLM, BLVR, and BLNC groups, whereas the BLVN group exhibited lower elastance than these groups. These findings suggest that vagotomy aggravates lung stiffness and inflammation, while its combination with nicotine mitigates these effects. Notably, in the BLNC group, lung compliance did not improve and, similar to the BLM group, was associated with increased elastance, possibly due to muscarinic receptor activation [22]. Therefore, vagotomy in the BLVN group may have reduced muscarinic signaling, allowing nicotine’s anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects to enhance lung mechanics.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that right cervical vagotomy exacerbates inflammation, oxidative stress, and lung injury, as well as impairs lung compliance and increases elastance during both the acute and chronic phases of bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis. While nicotine administration alone produced only modest improvements in these parameters, the combined intervention of vagotomy and nicotine significantly attenuated inflammatory responses and improved lung function. These findings underscore the anti-inflammatory role of the vagus nerve across different phases of lung injury and highlight the potential modulatory effects of nicotinic receptor activation. The discrepancies between the present findings and those of previous, limited studies on the vagus nerve’s role in pulmonary fibrosis may be due to variations in injury severity, experimental design, or study duration. Future research should explore the contribution of muscarinic receptors to fibrosis progression, as this may provide further insight into the complex neuroimmune interactions involved in pulmonary fibrogenesis and refine potential therapeutic strategies targeting cholinergic pathways.

Limitation of the study

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, only a few animals were assessed in the vagotomy + saline and nicotine + saline groups. As their results did not differ from those of the SHAM group and due to ethical considerations, the number of animals in these groups was not increased, and their data were not included in the paper. Second, due to methodological constraints, we did not evaluate molecular parameters such as Western blot, qPCR, or immunohistochemistry, α7nAChR-related signaling pathways, the effects of disease severity and duration, different nicotine doses, or the role of muscarinic receptors in pulmonary fibrosis. Third, our findings may not be directly extrapolated to human or clinical settings.
