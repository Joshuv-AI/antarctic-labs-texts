Recurrence in the chemotherapy regimen of bladder carcinoma originates from quiescent epidermoid-like cells
Authors: Dewang Zhou, Yuqing Li, Zeqin Yan, Jianxu Huang, Yishu Bao, Chunyang Gao, Caiting Tang, Xinchen Wu, Shang Wang, Daitian Tang, Haoxiang Zheng, Weihan Luo, Fei Li, Weibiao Ye, Zhaohua Ye, Xiqi Peng, Yujun Liu, Shimao Zhu, Yang Du, Jean Paul Thiery, Song Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer relapse upon chemotherapy remains the primary challenge in cancer treatment. By integrating high-resolution CRISPR/Cas9-based evolving lineage tracing with single-cell RNA sequencing in mice, we track the evolution of bladder carcinoma in the presence or absence of chemotherapy. We find that a cell population exhibiting an epidermoid-like cell state can survives chemotherapy. These epidermoid-like cells leave the quiescent state to initiate relapse, generating histologically distinct tumours through different evolutionary routes involving specific transcriptional changes. The plasticity of epidermoid-like cells and alterations in plasticity patterns during evolution shape the phenotypes of relapsed tumours. Integrated analyses reveal that Insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 5 is a key co-regulator of aggressive progression and squamous features in bladder carcinoma. Impeding insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 5 expression suppresses the chemoresistance and malignant behaviours of relapsed tumours. Our study deciphers key events and evolutionary dynamics during the natural progression and recurrence of bladder carcinoma. It offers a viable therapeutic approach to overcome the refractoriness of relapsed bladder carcinoma to conventional chemotherapy.

Introduction

Solid tumours usually partially or fully respond to first-line chemotherapy but often recur at different time points after treatment. Cancer relapse can be partially attributed to the pre-existence of a rare population of chemoresistant cancer cells in the primary tumour. Emerging evidence suggests that cells with de novo-induced resistance also fuel tumour recurrence1,2. Several mechanisms have been proposed to account for chemotherapy resistance in cancer, including adaptive mutability3–5, epigenetic remodelling6,7, changes in cell identity8–10, alterations in the cell cycle11,12, and rewiring of metabolism13,14. Chemorefractory quiescent cells can resume proliferation and even acquire increased malignant potential after the drug removal15–17. However, little is known about how tumour cells survive therapeutic pressure and the evolutionary dynamics of surviving tumour cells15–17.

Evolutionary principles govern cancer relapse18,19. This process involves defined phylogenetic signatures at each step and is initiated by the acquisition of therapeutic resistance to form relapsed tumours20,21. There is an urgent unmet need to decipher these mechanisms, which are responsible for the leading cause of mortality among cancer patients22–24. Exploring this process, particularly by real-time monitoring, has been challenging because recurrence is a complex and dynamic process23,24 involving multiple steps3,11 in residual cancer cells. Analogous to how cell fate mapping has improved our understanding of cell differentiation and organogenesis25,26, accurately reconstructed phylogenetic trees of primary and relapsed tumours can provide critical insights into cancer relapse by revealing the cell of origin and the evolutionary dynamics of seed cells that facilitate recurrence. Lineage tracing approaches enable the mapping of cell genealogies27–30, serving as an essential tool for investigating the evolutionary principles underlying biological processes such as cancer recurrence.

The effective treatment of non-muscle-invasive bladder carcinoma (NMIBC) is severely hampered by frequent recurrences31,32. Up to 70% of NMIBC patients who receive postoperative intravesical chemotherapy ultimately develop recurrent tumours, 20–30% of which progress aggressively and ultimately lead to death32. Therefore, NMIBC is an excellent experimental model for investigating the whole process of tumour relapse in the context of chemotherapy.

In this work, we leverage high-resolution CRISPR/Cas9-based evolving lineage tracing paired with single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) readouts for continuous monitoring of the evolutionary processes of bladder carcinoma (BLCA) with or without chemotherapy. The transcriptomic data, small proline-rich protein 3 (SPRR3)-driven GFP reporter assay results, and the evolutionary path of the natural progression of BLCA are integrated to gain insights into the origin and phenotype of chemoresistant cells. By combining phylogenetic and transcriptomic data, we identify distinct evolutionary patterns of BLCA relapse with tumour plasticity emerging as a key determinant of these patterns. We also identify insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 5 (IGFBP5) as a key co-regulator of cancer relapse, progression, and chemoresistance. In summary, we reconstruct the evolutionary landscape of the natural progression and relapse of BLCA and proposed abrogating IGFBP5 to overcome the refractoriness of BLCA to conventional chemotherapy and repress aggressive progression.

Results

Recapitulation of the bladder carcinoma relapse course under intravesical chemotherapy in an orthotopic mouse model

To study BLCA relapse in vivo in real time, we sought to establish a robust lineage-tracing model to record and explore recurrence. During this process, bladder carcinoma cells are expected to initially respond to intravesical chemotherapy, then become resistant, and ultimately regrow to generate relapsed tumours. First, we engineered two human low-grade NMIBC cell lines, MGH-U3 and RT4, using a previously described evolving lineage tracing system driven by CRISPR/Cas927,33 (Supplementary Fig. 1a–g). Specifically, the engineered cells contained (i) luciferase for live imaging to monitor therapeutic responses and tumour growth; (ii) Cas9-mCherry for generating heritable indels (insertion and deletion); (iii) several copies of the target site containing three cut sites for recording lineage information, which can be captured as expressed transcripts from a single-cell intact cDNA library; (iv) static 14-base-pair randomer barcodes positioned upstream of the target sites for distinguishing individual copies of these target sites; and (v) triple-sgRNAs to direct Cas9 to the target sites, thereby initiating lineage recording. Importantly, mismatches between the sgRNAs and the target sites were designed to reduce binding affinity, adjusting the lineage recording rate to enable long-term tracing.

Next, we investigated the response to intravesical chemotherapy using the two above-engineered lineage tracing (LT)-competent cell lines (“MGH-U3-LT” and “RT4-LT”) in an orthotopic xenograft model of BLCA (Fig. 1a). Briefly, engineered cell lines were surgically implanted into the lumen of the bladder of an immunodeficient mouse, where a small number of cells colonised directly onto the bladder mucosa, mimicking the undetectable residual tumour observed in human disease34. On day 3, the mice were treated once per week with either vehicle or chemotherapy for four cycles. Mice injected with MGH-U3-LT cells, hereafter referred to as “MGH-U3-LT mice”, were assessed by live luciferase-based imaging to monitor the kinetics of tumour growth. In the Gemcitabine group, the early bioluminescence signal was modest and localised to the primary site, consistent with engraftment. The signal rapidly decreased and even disappeared following four cycles of treatment (Fig. 1b). Treatment invariably led to the development of chemoresistance (reappearance of the bioluminescence signal) in some of the MGH-U3-LT mice (8/20), indicating the presence of minimal residual disease (MRD) (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Although the time to recurrence varied from mouse to mouse, comparable response rates in repeated trials were observed at the population level (Fig. 1b, right). The drug response profiles of RT4-LT mice were like those of MGH-U3-LT mice (Fig. 1c). Following Gemcitabine treatment, fourteen out of twenty RT4-LT tumours regressed and even disappeared without regrowth. Six RT4-LT tumours transiently reached MRD status and then regrew during or after treatment (Fig. 1c, right, and Supplementary Fig. 2b).

Histopathology analysis revealed that five out of the eight relapsed MGH-U3-LT tumours were urothelial carcinoma with squamous differentiation (UCSD; phenotype 1), whereas the three relapsed MGH-U3-LT tumours had mixed phenotypes (MP: UCSD + sarcomatoid carcinoma; phenotype 2), differing from urothelial carcinoma (UC) in vehicle-treated MGH-U3-LT tumours. Metastasis was found in two out of three mice bearing MP tumours (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Compared with the vehicle-treated RT4-LT tumours, the relapsed RT4-LT tumours all underwent squamous transition, as demonstrated by the presence of cellular bridges, which contrasted with the characteristic UC phenotype of the vehicle-treated tumours (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Collectively, these data suggest that intravesical Gemcitabine induces phenotypic switching in mice with relapsed bladder carcinoma.

To further assess whether phenotypic switching accompanies changes in cellular malignancy, we separately sorted carcinoma cells from distinct phenotypic variants of MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT tumours. We subsequently established tumour organoids corresponding to each variant (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). The results from MGH-U3-LT tumour organoids revealed that the MP tumour organoids grew significantly faster than the control tumour organoids did in vitro. Organoids derived from UCSD tumours and metastases were intermediate in size between those derived from MP tumours and vehicle-treated control tumour organoids. Organoids derived from metastatic tumour cells (Mets-MGH-U3-LT) grew faster than UCSD-MGH-U3 tumour organoids did (Fig. 1f). However, the size of the relapsed RT4-LT tumour organoids was comparable to that of the control organoids in vitro (Fig. 1g). To directly assess the malignant potential of cells derived from different tumour phenotypes, we further performed serial-dilution orthotopic transplantation assays. The results showed that MP-MGH-U3-LT and Mets-MGH-U3-LT exhibited a significantly higher tumour-initiating ability compared to UCSD-MGH-U3-LT and CTR-MGH-U3-LT. UCSD-MGH-U3-LT demonstrated greater tumour-initiating capacity than CTR-MGH-U3-LT (Supplementary Fig. 2g). Although MP-MGH-U3-LT and Mets-MGH-U3-LT displayed similar tumour-initiating frequencies, tumours derived from MP-MGH-U3-LT were consistently larger than those from Mets-MGH-U3-LT across a range of cell numbers (Supplementary Fig. 2h). The tumour-initiating capacity and tumour size were not significantly different across a range of cell numbers for CTR-RT4-LT and UCSD-RT4-LT cells (Supplementary Fig. 2i, j). Overall, these data show that relapsed MGH-U3-LT tumours are more malignant than vehicle-treated control tumours. Nevertheless, the malignant potential of RT4-LT tumours was not significantly different from that of control tumours, indicating that squamous differentiation described herein may not influence tumour malignancy. The effects of chemotherapy on tumours derived from different engineered cell lines warrant further study.

Multiple lineage transitions are induced during relapse

To gain further insights into the transcriptomic features of chemotherapy-treated relapsed tumours and vehicle-treated control tumours, we integrated the scRNA-seq data across MGH-U3-LT tumours. We detected 10 distinct subpopulations, including a squamous cell subpopulation characterised by the expression of SPRR3, LCE3D, S100A2, CRCT1, and SPRR2G, and a mesenchymal cell cluster characterised by the expression of RHOH, SNAI2, DGKI, CDH2, and IGFBP5 (Fig. 2a, b). These 10 subpopulations from MGH-U3-LT tumours could be categorised into four gene modules decomposed from all highly variable genes (Fig. 2c): the Leiden clusters in module 1 mainly consisted of the UCSD-MGH-U3 and metastatic tumours; module 2 was dominant in the shared Leiden cluster; the Leiden clusters in module 3 were derived from the CTR-MGH-U3 tumours; and the Leiden clusters in module 4 were mainly derived from the MP-MGH-U3 tumours (Fig. 2d, e). Consistent with the histopathological results, the CTR-MGH-U3 tumours presented luminal characteristics, as indicated by positive expression of PPARG, GATA3, GRHL2, UPK1B, and FOXA1, which are classical luminal-associated genes enriched in module 3 (Fig. 2f). Compared with module 3, module 1 exhibited a shift in the expression of basal and squamous lineage-specific genes, and module 4 exhibited a change in the expression of EMT/malignant progression lineage-specific genes. We aimed to integrate paired lineage and transcriptome data to predict fitness at single-cell resolution27,35 to elucidate mechanisms underlying increased malignancy of relapsed MGH-U3-LT tumours, as the growth advantage of this population can be assessed by its relative “fitness”36. First, we preprocessed the target site amplicon-seq and scRNA-seq data based on sequencing quality control (Supplementary Fig. 3a). We then inferred clonal populations called clonal tumours herein and reconstructed phylogenetic trees for the high-quality subset of clonal tumours with Cassiopeia37 (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Supplementary Fig. 3c shows the phylogeny and indel statuses summarised in the allele heatmap of a clonal tumour (UCSD_MGH-U3_4) with 591 cells. The tree revealed a complex and nuanced subclonal structure, wherein closely related cells shared more lineage alleles, as indicated by clade-specific allele patterns (Supplementary Fig. 3c, zoomed inlays). Indeed, phylogenetic and allelic distances were strongly correlated in this and other clonal tumours (Supplementary Fig. 3d and Supplementary Table 1).

With the resulting phylodynamic and transcriptome information, we identified fitness-associated gene programmes across all filtered clonal tumours (Supplementary Fig. 3e–g). We found that the MP-MGH-U3 tumours presented the highest fitness signature score. In contrast, the fitness signature scores of the shared Leiden cluster- and vehicle-treated MGH-U3-LT tumours were lower (Fig. 2g). Notably. However, the expression profiles of both the metastatic and UCSD-MGH-U3 tumours were enriched in gene module 1; the metastatic tumours presented higher fitness signature scores than the UCSD-MGH-U3 tumours did (Fig. 2g), explaining the difference observed in the tumour organoids. Moreover, the expression of a high-fitness signature was predictive of worse patient survival in the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) MIBC cohort (Fig. 2h).

We classified cells from RT4-LT tumours into six subpopulations, including the squamous and luminal-like subpopulations, based on marker gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). These six subpopulations from RT4-LT tumours were divided into two gene modules, derived from all highly variable genes (Supplementary Fig. 4c). The cell subpopulations (Leiden clusters 1, 2, 3, and 5) in gene module 1 were from the relapsed tumours. In contrast, cell subpopulations (Leiden clusters 0, 2 and 4) in gene module 2 were from vehicle-treated control tumours (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e). Interestingly, the gene expression profiles of the relapsed and vehicle-treated control RT4-LT tumours differed (Supplementary Fig. 4f). Specifically, the relapsed RT4-LT tumour lost the expression of FOXA1 and PPARG, which are involved in maintaining the luminal state of bladder carcinoma cells38, but upregulated the expression of squamous differentiation-associated genes (SPRR3, KRT14, and TGM1) and other luminal-like markers (GATA3, UPK2, UPK1B, and KRT20), indicating that the relapsed RT4-LT tumours maintained luminal characteristics despite undergoing the squamous transition. Control RT4-LT tumours expressed EMT-associated genes (CDH2, SNAI2, and VIM) and the luminal-like markers (PPARG, GRHL2, and FOXA1) (Supplementary Fig. 4f). Unexpectedly, chemotherapy slightly decreased the fitness of the RT4-LT relapsed tumours while maintaining relatively stable compared to MGH-U3-LT tumours, suggesting a potentially different evolutionary pattern from that of MGH-U3-LT tumours (Supplementary Fig. 4g). Taken together, these data suggest that chemotherapy-induced lineage transitions, particularly luminal-to-basal transition and EMT, increase the cellular fitness of carcinoma cells, thereby increasing the degree of tumour malignancy.

Squamous transition is a common mechanism for tumour relapse upon chemotherapy

To determine whether squamous transition is a preferred mechanism that contributes to tumour relapse upon chemotherapy, we leveraged transgenic Trp53flox/flox, Ptenflox/flox, and Akaluc-IRES-tdTomato mice to generate spontaneous bladder tumours that progressed from carcinoma in situ (CIS) to muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC)39,40. To initiate tumourigenesis, we delivered adenovirus under the KRT5 promoter (AdenoKRT5-Cre) into the bladder lumen via surgery (Supplementary Fig. 5a). This approach enabled the specific knockout of Trp53 and Pten in basal cells and the simultaneous introduction of Akaluc-IRES-tdTomato into basal bladder cells. Upon the initiation of tumourigenesis, as confirmed by bioluminescence imaging (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b), mice were treated with the same therapeutic regimen and schedule as in the mouse xenograft model. At the experimental endpoint, bladder tumours were collected for histopathological analysis, bulk RNA-seq and organoid culture (Supplementary Fig. 5a). One-third of the mice developed recurrence after four cycles of treatment (Supplementary Figs. 5b,c, 6c).

We next investigated whether chemotherapy also altered the phenotypes of the genetically engineered mouse model (GEMM) of bladder cancer and found that all the tumours (6/6) in the vehicle group exhibited UC characteristics. In contrast, the relapsed tumours presented MP histopathology (Supplementary Figs. 5d, 6c). We established two tumour organoid models derived from the CUUC-G cell line and the control CUUC cell line, respectively to explore further whether chemotherapy enhances tumour malignancy (Supplementary Fig. 6d). The organoids derived from relapsed carcinoma cells grew significantly faster than the organoids derived from the vehicle-treated control tumours (Supplementary Fig. 5e, f). Moreover, serial-dilution orthotopic transplantation assays further showed that CUUC-G cells derived from relapsed tumours exhibited enhanced tumour-initiating capacity and generated significantly larger tumours compared to their control counterparts. (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f).

Next, we performed transcriptomic analysis of vehicle-treated control and Gemcitabine-treated relapsed tumours. In line with the histopathology and tumour organoid results, the relapsed tumours presented increased expression of squamous differentiation-associated genes (e.g., Ivl, Crct1, and Sprr2e/h/g) and EMT-related genes (e.g., SNAI1, TWIST2, CDH2, and HIF1α) (Supplementary Fig. 5g, h). Further insights into the unique features of relapsed tumours were revealed by the Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO-BP) terms. Angiogenesis appeared at the top of this list, along with epithelial proliferation, EMT, skin development, and epidermis development signalling (Supplementary Fig. 6g), suggesting that chemotherapy induced squamous differentiation and aggressive progression in the GEMM of bladder carcinoma.

Together, our data demonstrate that chemotherapy induces squamous differentiation and improves the malignancy of carcinoma cells in the GEMM of bladder carcinoma, further supporting the results from orthotopic mouse models.

Relapsed tumours with distinct phenotypes were generated via different evolutionary patterns

Cell transformation could arise from random or structured evolutionary paths through transcriptional states. To explore how recurrent tumours were generated, we used a statistic termed “Evolutionary Coupling” (hereafter referred to as EvoCoupling) that extends a clonal coupling statistic29,41 to quantify the phylogenetic distance between pairs of cell states, which can be used to infer evolutionary trajectories. Previous results have suggested that differential evolutionary patterns between MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT tumours (Supplementary Fig. 4g). Therefore, we explored their evolutionary dynamics and differences by integrating all phylogenetic and scRNA-seq data across MGH-U3-LT tumours and RT4-LT tumours.

We leveraged phylogenetic EvoCoupling within each outfiltered clonal tumour to reveal different coupling patterns between cell states. In one representative UCSD-MGH-U3 clonal tumour (UCSD_MGH-U3_4), the squamous cells (Leiden cluster 10) and high-plasticity (HP) cell clusters 1 and 3 (Leiden clusters 4 and 11) clustered together, and HP cell clusters 1 and 3 (Leiden clusters 4 and 11) and HP cell cluster 2 (Leiden cluster 7) coupled together as well (Fig. 3a). For RT4-LT tumours, the coupling patterns between cell states were more straightforward than those for MGH-U3-LT tumours. In a representative vehicle-treated control clonal tumour (CTR_RT4_1), luminal-like cells (Leiden cluster 0) and progenitor cells (Leiden cluster 13) coupled together (Supplementary Fig. 8a). In one representative relapsed clonal tumour (UCSD_RT4_1), squamous cells (Leiden cluster 1 and Leiden cluster 6) were connected (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Interestingly, the squamous cells (Leiden cluster 1 and Leiden cluster 6) were independent of each other in another relapsed RT4-LT clonal tumour (UCSD_RT4_12), indicating that these cells self-renew rather than undergoing differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 8c). Importantly, there was no connection across Leiden clusters within MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT tumours (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Fig. 8a–c), suggesting that data integration did not influence each other and that their evolution was still independent. Notably, two other methods for inferring interstate relationships by indel information without phylogenetic trees (EditEvoCoupling) and local neighbourhoods on the tree (KNN-EvoCoupling) were utilised in these eight representative tumours and other clonal tumours (Supplementary Table 1). The consistency between phylogenetic EvoCoupling and EditEvoCoupling or KNN EvoCoupling was uncovered (Supplementary Figs. 7a–d and 8d–f), indicating that the inferred relationships between Leiden clusters are correct.

Hierarchical clustering of all candidate clonal tumours based on their phylogenetic EvoCouplings and cell state occupancy revealed that these clonal tumours could be categorised into three different groups (“Fates”; Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 7e, f). Fate 1 was predominantly characterised by Leiden clusters 1 and 6 in UCSD-RT4 tumours (Fig. 3c, left and Supplementary Fig. 7e, left), and couplings between cell states marked fate 2 observed in relapsed MGH-U3-LT tumours (Fig. 3c, middle, Supplementary Fig. 7e, middle). In contrast, fate 3 was dominated by CTR-RT4 and CTR-MGH-U3 tumours (Fig. 3c, right; Supplementary Fig. 7e, right), which is in line with earlier findings from our study, including histopathological and transcriptomic analyses.

Given that control clonal tumours share fate 3, whereas relapsed clonal RT4 and MGH-U3 tumours exhibit divergent fates, we thus hypothesised that the differences in tendencies towards fates drove the distinct evolutionary patterns. To test this hypothesis, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to state proportions and EvoCouplings. Independent cell states (Fate 1; Leiden clusters 1 and 6) and couplings between cell states (Fate 2; Leiden clusters 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, and 11) were the critical features distinguishing relapsed MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT tumours (Supplementary Fig. 7g), showing that squamous cells facilitate MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT tumour relapse through different modes.

To gain further insight into the evolutionary patterns that underlie these alternative fates, we utilised a statistical method called “Phylotime” to quantify the evolutionary distance between progenitor cells and their progeny at the single-cell level. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) of clonal-tumour EvoCouplings revealed that the CTR_RT4_1 tumour was similar to UCSD-RT4 clonal tumours but differed from CTR-MGH-U3 clonal tumours, although control clonal tumours shared fate 3 (Supplementary Fig. 7h); thus, we applied Phylotime in CTR-MGH-U3 clonal tumours and the CTR_RT4_1 tumour in the following study, respectively. Integrated Phylotime data of CTR-MGH-U3 clonal tumours evidenced that TH-positive cells (Leiden cluster 8) first differentiated into pre-squamous cells (Leiden cluster 12) and subsequently transited into either luminal-like cells (Leiden cluster 3) or squamous cells (Leiden cluster 10) during the natural progression of BLCA (Fig. 3d), further supporting the notion that pre-existing cell populations play a critical role in the formation of squamous cells.

For relapsed MGH-U3-LT tumours, the evolutionary trajectory was challenging to interpret because the Phylotimes of lower-malignancy UCSD-MGH-U3 tumours seem higher than those of MP-MGH-U3 tumours (Fig. 3e). To dissect further the evolutionary patterns of the two types of relapsed MGH-U3-LT tumours in fate 2, we integrated Phylotime data from UCSD-MGH-U3 and MP-MGH-U3 clonal tumours, respectively. We identified one route for the generation of UCSD tumours and two routes for the generation of MP tumours (Fig. 3f, g). Squamous cells evolved into HP cells (Leiden clusters 4, 7, and 11), ultimately leading to UCSD tumour formation (Fig. 3f). Alternatively, ELCs gave rise to MP tumours by evolving into high-fitness cell states such as mesenchymal cells (Leiden-cluster 5; route 1) or first differentiated into HP cells of the UCSD-MGH-U3 tumours (e.g., Leiden cluster 7) and subsequently progressed to form MP tumours (route 2). We also noticed that the formation of metastatic seeds is an early event during aggressive relapse (Fig. 3g), which is consistent with the findings of a previous study5. Notably, estimates of Phylotime were consistent with different metrics for approximating distances on the tree, using either the absolute number of mutations or the number of mutation-bearing edges, indicating that the inferred evolutionary paths were significant (Supplementary Fig. 7i–l).

To elucidate the transcriptional alterations underlying these different routes within MGH-U3-LT tumours, we integrated Phylotime data across the MP-MGH-U3 clonal tumours and CTR-MGH-U3 clonal tumours or UCSD-MGH-U3 clonal tumours, revealing distinct transcriptional changes along the Phylotime of each route (Fig. 3h). Specifically, the expression of EMT-associated markers, such as TWIST1, FN1, NR2F2, FOXC1, and RHOH, tended to increase along the evolutionary route of the MP tumour but was not observed along the other two evolutionary routes. Consistent with the results of the phylotime depicted in Fig. 3e, which demonstrated that the MP route could be completed more rapidly, the squamous cells entering the MP route escaped from cell cycle arrest faster than those entering the UCSD route (Fig. 3h). Moreover, squamous cells engaged in the MP route activated the WNT pathway and acquired stemness to enhance malignancy (Fig. 3h), which aligns with the findings of a previous study42.

Concerning the evolutionary patterns of RT4-LT tumours, progenitor cells (Leiden cluster 13) mostly evolved into luminal-like cells (Leiden cluster 0), and some squamous cells (Leiden clusters 1 and 6) during the natural progression of BLCA (Supplementary Fig. 8g). Although the transition between squamous cell subpopulations (Leiden clusters 1 and 6) was observed in the relapsed RT4-LT tumours, these squamous cells favoured proliferation/self-renew over further differentiation into other cell states (Supplementary Fig. 8h). Notably, estimates of Phylotime were consistent with different metrics for approximating distances on the tree, either in terms of the absolute number of mutations or the number of mutation-bearing edges, indicating that the inferred evolutionary paths are correct (Supplementary Fig. 8i, j).

Overall, our analyses reveal the evolutionary dynamics of BLCA in the presence or absence of chemotherapy (summarised in Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 8k). During the natural progression of BLCA, both luminal-like cells and squamous cells are generated in MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT tumours. During relapse processes, squamous cells, which serve as recurrent seeds, give rise to different phenotypic tumours through different evolutionary patterns. Specifically, squamous cells (Leiden clusters 1 and 6) prefer renewal rather than differentiation to generate relapsed UCSD-RT4 tumours with a luminal phenotype. In contrast, squamous cells (Leiden cluster 10) adopt branching evolutionary patterns with distinct transcriptional changes: one towards UCSD tumours and another towards MP tumours, similar to the findings on the clonal evolution of chemotherapy-resistant urothelial carcinoma5

Plasticity determines the evolutionary patterns of relapsed tumours

To deepen our understanding of the distinct evolutionary trajectories observed in relapsed MGH-U3-LT tumours and the different relapse patterns between MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT tumours, we investigated the dynamics of intratumoral transcriptional state diversity, as several studies have confirmed that such behaviour can promote aggressive progression6,43–47. MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT tumours widely varied in their transcriptional state profiles based on phenotype, with no single state dominating the transcriptional landscape (Supplementary Fig. 9a). In MGH-U3-LT tumours, low FitnessSignature scores were enriched for luminal-like, TH+ epithelial and squamous states. In contrast, increases in the fitness score were correlated with high-plasticity, enteric-like, and mesenchymal states. Conversely, RT4-LT tumours with low FitnessSignature scores showed enrichment of squamous cells. In contrast, increasing FitnessSignature scores correlated with luminal-like state predominance (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Transcriptomic heterogeneity analysis using Shannon’s Entropy Index46,48 revealed that relapsed MGH-U3-LT tumours had comparable levels of transcriptomic heterogeneity among themselves but demonstrated higher heterogeneity compared to CTR-MGH-U3 tumours (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Similarly, compared with their control counterparts, relapsed RT4-LT tumours presented increased transcriptomic heterogeneity, despite having lower FitnessSignature scores than CTR-RT4 tumours did (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Notably, MGH-U3-LT tumours exhibited higher levels of transcriptomic heterogeneity than their RT4-LT counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Together, these data suggest that tumour relapse is consistently associated with an increase in transcriptomic heterogeneity across different models.

We next asked how this intratumoral diversity is established and maintained. In principle, the diversity reflected by the entropy index can be achieved either through rare transitions and stable commitment to distinct states or through frequent transitions between these states. Lineage tracing is a uniquely valuable tool for distinguishing these two models, as it directly reveals the degree to which transcriptomic states are intermixed in subclonal lineages, thus providing a measure of effective plasticity. Using tumour phylogenies, we estimated the frequency of cellular state changes for each clonal tumour to create an empirical measurement of tree plasticity (hereafter referred to as the “EffectivePlasticity” score). We extended this measure to a single-cell statistic (“scEffectivePlasticity”) by averaging together the EffectivePlasticity scores for all the subclades that contained a particular cell33. We observed that cells exhibited consistently high scEffectivePlasticity during the evolutionary process of UCSD-MGH-U3 tumours. In contrast, scEffectivePlasticity showed a marked decreasing trend during the evolutionary process that generated MP-MGH-U3 tumours (Supplementary Fig. 9c). During the evolutionary process of UCSD-RT4 tumours, the scEffectivePlasticity score stayed relatively high compared with that of CTR-RT4 tumours, like UCSD-MGH-U3 tumours (Supplementary Fig. 9c). By assessing the natural evolution of BCLA in the absence of chemotherapy pressure, we observed consistent patterns of tumour plasticity dynamics across different cell lines: scEffectivePlasticity scores increased when squamous cells were generated. In contrast, scEffectivePlasticity scores decreased along evolutionary trajectories that evolved into non-squamous cells (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Importantly, this scEffectivePlasticity statistic was consistent with the results of alternative approaches that quantified adequate plasticity by comparing transcriptional states between cells with similar indel states (without relying on trees; Supplementary Fig. 9d–f) or those that assessed dissimilarity in gene expression profiles between nearest neighbours on the phylogenetic tree (Supplementary Fig. 9g–i).

Given that different evolutionary routes exhibit varying plasticity dynamics and graded differences in malignant potential, we next investigated the precise relationship between tumour plasticity and malignancy, as measured by the FitnessSignature scores. We found that tumours with high malignant potential typically exhibited elevated levels of tumour plasticity (Supplementary Fig. 9j, k), which aligns with previous studies indicating that tumour plasticity is a hallmark of tumour progression46,49. Notably, the average Effective Plasticity score was maximised when the Fitness Signature score fell within the intermediate regime, indicating a non-linear relationship between these metrics (Supplementary Fig. 9j, k). Taken together, these findings elucidate modes of tumour relapse, in which the loss of the squamous state was accompanied by lineage transition, generating high transcriptomic heterogeneity that permitted the selection of an increasingly stable state with increased fitness and ultimately resulted in the formation of MP-MGH-U3 tumours. Alternatively, UCSD tumours may be generated if their cell states, which evolve from squamous or progenitor-like cells, maintain relatively high plasticity without the selection of increasingly stable states (Supplementary Fig. 9l).

Chemoresistant carcinoma cells adopt an epidermoid-like cell state

Although clinical studies have indicated that the presence of squamous differentiation features in urothelial carcinoma is associated with lower chemosensitivity and a greater risk of recurrence50,51, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. To elucidate these mechanisms, we first investigated the transcriptomic profile of the squamous cell subpopulation derived from MGH-U3-LT tumours, as it exhibited a key role in cancer recurrence. We found that the majority of upregulated genes in this cell subpopulation were associated with the epidermal differentiation complex52,53 comprising CNFN, CRCT1, SPRR3, and SPRR2A/D/E/F/G (Supplementary Fig. 10a; Supplementary Dataset 1). Moreover, the squamous cell subpopulation exhibited greater chromatin accessibility at the locus of squamous-associated genes (e.g., SPRR3, SPRR2F, and S100A7) compared to other cell subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed a significant enrichment of resistance to drugs, keratinisation, and epidermal cell differentiation signalling signatures (Fig. 4a), indicating that carcinoma cells may acquire terminal epidermal differentiation characteristics responsible for chemoresistance. To clinically validate the presence of ELCs, we analysed the distribution of SPRR3, a specific marker for epidermal cell differentiation and keratinisation, by immunostaining five matched pairs of tumour samples collected from patients both before and after chemotherapy treatment (Supplementary Fig. 10c and Supplementary Table 2). SPRR3-positive cells were significantly enriched in post-chemotherapy tumours compared with pre-chemotherapy tumours. However, SPPR3 expression varied across pre-chemotherapy tumour tissues (Fig. 4b), suggesting that chemotherapy can promote ELCs formation. The standard of care for patients with locally advanced and metastatic is also evolving rapidly, with the antibody drug conjugate enfortumab vedotin (EV) in combination with pembrolizumab supplanting the use of Gemcitabine plus cisplatin54,55. Therefore, we further analysed the distribution of SPRR3 by immunostaining three matched pairs of localised MIBC samples collected from patients both before and after disitamab vedotin plus toripalimab treatment, a combination therapy also used to treat MIBC patients56,57 (Supplementary Table 2). The results also showed that SPRR3-positive cells were significantly enriched in post-immunotherapy tumours compared with pre-immunotherapy tumours, like the findings in our chemotherapy regimens (Fig. 4b).

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the formation of ELCs, we applied live-cell tracking to wild-type MGH-U3 bladder carcinoma cells that were transduced with a GFP reporter driven by the SPPR3 promoter (Fig. 4c). Weakly GFP-positive cells were observed as early as 24 h after Gemcitabine treatment. The expression further increased over time (Fig. 4d). Most of the surviving MGH-U3 cells were GFP positive in the presence of Gemcitabine (Fig. 4e), suggesting that chemoresistant carcinoma cells can be induced through lineage plasticity. Furthermore, we overexpressed SPRR3 in MGH-U3 cells and investigated the causal link to chemoresistance (Supplementary Fig. 10d). SPRR3-OE cells showed comparable proliferation ability but were less sensitive to Gemcitabine compared to their control counterparts, suggesting that SPRR3 is a mediator of chemoresistance (Supplementary Fig. 10e, f)

Given that the ELCs are involved in tumour relapse that showed by lineage dynamics, we next confirmed roles of ELCs in tumour relapse from experimental and clinical aspects. To explore this, we performed histological analysis and SPRR3 immunostaining across vehicle-treated control and corresponding relapsed tumours. We found that the relapsed tumours displayed significant keratin pearl areas, which were dark purple or pink with H&E staining and surrounded by hyperplasia of poorly differentiated keratinocyte-like cells; notably, keratin pearls were not observed in CTR-MGH-U3 tumours (Fig. 4f, top). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) showed that SPRR3 was specifically expressed in keratinised carcinoma cells; SPPR3+ cells were much more abundant in relapsed tumours than in vehicle-treated control tumours (Fig. 4f, bottom and Supplementary Fig. 10g), suggesting that some of the ELCs are a pre-existing rare cell population, further supporting the result of lineage tracing. Immunofluorescence of SPRR3 in RT4-LT tumours and those from the GEMM of bladder cancer supported the above results (Supplementary Fig. 10h–k). Overall, these data suggest that ELCs are involved in tumour relapse, as preceding states are maintained even in late-stage tumours during tumour progression58.

To clinically validate our experimental findings, we further performed SPRR3 immunostaining on eight matched pairs of tumour samples obtained from patients who were initially diagnosed with NMIBC and subsequently diagnosed with relapsed MIBC following chemotherapy treatment (Supplementary Fig. 10l and Supplementary Table 3). In line with preclinical observations, SPRR3 + cells were more enriched in relapsed MIBC than in corresponding primary NMIBC samples (Supplementary Fig. 10m–o). Moreover, survival analysis of a tissue microarray cohort of patients with bladder carcinoma revealed no significant association between SPRR3 expression and overall survival (OS), further suggesting that the ELCs is not correlated with tumour malignancy but only chemotherapy resistance (Supplementary Fig. 10p). Interestingly, we noticed that SPRR3 was upregulated across cell subpopulations of the relapsed RT4-LT tumours, which was different from its specific upregulation in the shared subpopulation of MGH-U3-LT tumours (Supplementary Fig. 10q, r); thus, this explains why the fitness score of the relapsed RT4-LT tumours was slightly lower than that of the control counterparts, as ELCs exhibited low fitness (Supplementary Fig. 4g). The different distributions of SPRR3 + cells certainly explained divergence in evolutionary patterns in MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT tumours, which warrants further study.

Overall, our findings elucidate the phenotypic traits and formation mechanisms underlying chemoresistant carcinoma cells, while deepening the biological understanding of squamous differentiation in bladder carcinoma by validating its association with reduced chemosensitivity and increased recurrence risk, as observed from both experimental and clinical aspects.

IGFBP5 is a coregulator of aggressive progression and squamous traits

The central challenge in studying tumour relapse lies in capturing key intermediate states and longitudinally reconstructing the continuum process from MRD to overt recurrence. Our lineage tracing approach is uniquely capable of high-resolution, real-time mapping of recurrence dynamics without inviolable assumptions (e.g., transcriptional similarity), thereby revealing otherwise missed bifurcation points and evolutionary principles related to relapse. We therefore sought to identify actionable targets for treating relapsed tumours based on their evolutionary dynamics, which we deciphered. First, we observed that the carcinoma cells that evolved from the key bifurcation point (progenitor-like cells) exhibit increased malignancy; such cells should contribute worse prognosis since they gave rise to sarcomatoid carcinoma and metastasis (Fig. 3i). Moreover, squamous traits were detectable in relapsed tumours (Fig. 1d, e and Supplementary Fig. 5d). Therefore, these observations highlight the value of targeting progenitor-like cells to repress aggressive progression and inhibiting squamous traits simultaneously to overcome chemoresistance.

To molecularly dissect potential coregulators of squamous traits and aggressive progression, we identified twenty five commonly upregulated genes among progenitor-like cells of MP tumours, relapsed tumours from a GEMM of bladder cancer, and surviving patient tumours after chemotherapy. In addition to PTGS2 and CEBPD, two known factors involved in resistance to chemotherapy in bladder cancer59,60—This shortlist included THBS2, IGFBP5, VCAN, ID1, IER3, and PFKFB3, which are known to correlate with aggressiveness, angiogenesis, and poor prognosis in bladder cancer61–66 (Supplementary Table 4). Notably, three of these twenty-five genes enriched in the aggressive progression were specifically involved in squamous differentiation (Fig. 5a). Prompted by these results, we further applied bulk RNA-seq to IGFBP5-deficient cells. Analysis of the transcriptomic profile revealed that IGFBP5 deficiency downregulated the expression of squamous differentiation-associated genes (IVL, SPRR2A/D/E/G, and TGM4/5) (Fig. 6h). Moreover, the genes downregulated following IGFBP5 abrogation were significantly enriched in epithelial cell proliferation, mesenchymal cell differentiation, and ameboid-type cell migration in addition to squamous differentiation, suggesting that downregulation of IGFBP5 suppresses tumour progression (Fig. 6i).

We then assessed the chemosensitivity of IGFBP5-KO carcinoma cells in vivo, given that the squamous differentiation fate is induced after they acquire chemoresistance. We established an orthotopic bladder cancer model by inoculating IGFBP5-sgScr and IGFBP5-KO MP-MGH-U3-LT cells into the mouse bladder. Intravesical chemotherapy was initiated on day 3 and administered once weekly for two consecutive cycles (Fig. 6j). Intravesical chemotherapy treatment delayed the growth of IGFBP5-sgScr MP-MGH-U3-LT tumours. Still, it exhibited a weaker suppression of tumour regrowth than IGFBP5 deficiency. Strikingly, all the mice bearing IGFBP5-deficient tumours demonstrated robust responses to chemotherapy (Fig. 6k, l). IGFBP5-KO MP-MGH-U3-LT cells also exhibited more sensitive to chemotherapy compared to their corresponding counterparts (Fig. 6m). Echoing our findings in MP-MGH-U3-LT tumours, Igfbp5-sgScr and Igfbp5-KO CUUC-G tumours, which were derived from chemoresistant relapsed tumours from the GEMM of bladder cancer; these results validated the crucial roles of IGFBP5 in promoting malignant progression and regulating squamous trait-mediated chemoresistance (Supplementary Fig. 12a–m).

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that genetic ablation of IGFBP5 has dual antitumour effects: it suppresses malignant progression by modulating key cellular processes, including epithelial cell proliferation, while simultaneously overcoming chemoresistance by inhibition of squamous differentiation programmes.

Discussion

Chemoresistance remains one of the significant challenges in cancer treatment15,67. Non-genetic mechanisms, particularly lineage transition, play crucial roles in this process15,67,68. However, our understanding of how tumour cells reprogramme themselves to survive chemotherapy and further unlock phenotypic plasticity, driving tumour relapses and likely increased malignancy, remains limited. Recent advances in lineage tracing technology have encouraged us to investigate the crucial but unanswered questions underlying these dynamic processes27,29,30,41,69. In this work, we integrated the CRISPR-based lineage tracer with single-cell RNA-seq and mouse models, alongside analysing matched pairs of clinical samples, to uncover the principles by which bladder carcinoma cells resist intravesical chemotherapy and eventually give rise to relapsed tumours with different phenotypes.

Histological variants are commonly observed in various cancers, particularly in recurrent and progressive tumours, such as neuroendocrine differentiation in prostate cancer70–72 and colorectal cancer73,74, as well as squamous differentiation in lung adenocarcinoma75,76 and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma77,78. Recently, bladder cancer has been reported to undergo semi-squamous differentiation, enabling it to resist cisplatin-based chemotherapy10. Preventing the emergence of these pathological subtypes, which are associated with poor prognosis and treatment resistance, is key to improving patient outcomes and extending survival. To achieve this critical yet unresolved goal, it is essential first to elucidate how cancer cells reprogramme themselves during treatment to evade killing by antitumour therapies. Our study highlights a specific cell state, the ELC state, which arises from the selection and expansion of a preexisting subpopulation and a de novo induction of such cells. Interestingly, cells in this state express unexpected markers that have previously not been implicated in semisquamous cells but are more closely related to those in a terminal squamous differentiation state (e.g., IVL, SPRR1A/B, and SPRR3). The enrichment of SPRR3 has been reported in chemoresistant bladder tumours that maintain a proliferative response59. In contrast, another study proposed the concept of a resistance continuum, suggesting that resistance develops through dynamic, stepwise cell-state transitions accompanied by a progressive increase in the potential for resistance79. Therefore, a possible explanation is that bladder carcinoma cells transition to the ELCs under the stress of chemotherapy and then to other states in the resistance continuum, such as the semisquamous differentiation state. Although further investigation is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms for this lineage conversion following chemotherapy, in which the microenvironment67,80, epigenetic reprogramming81–84, and metabolic plasticity85,86 may be involved, our findings highlight the importance of the ELCs in chemoresistance and cancer relapse in BLCA and, possibly, in epithelial cancers on a broader scale.

Second, if recurrent tumours are to be cured, studies are needed to elucidate how ELCs are involved in tumour relapse; namely, the evolutionary dynamics of recurrence in BLCA. We identified multiple distinct recurrence evolutionary patterns that exhibit different preferences in evolutionary trajectories, as well as graded differences in malignant potential and cellular plasticity. These preferences are orthogonal to the phenotypes of relapsed tumours. Notably, both branching evolution originating from ELCs and the formation of metastatic seeds are early events in the evolutionary history of MGH-U3-LT tumour relapse, which is consistent with a previous work that investigated how chemotherapy pressure directs the evolution of human urothelial carcinoma. However, the pattern of branching evolution was not observed in relapsed RT4-LT tumours. This may be because most collected human advanced urothelial carcinomas (88%) are metastatic urothelial carcinoma, which is more in line with the situation of the MGH-U3-LT model. The recurrence evolutionary pattern of RT4-LT tumours can be observed in other cancer types87,88, in which recurrence can proceed through several distinct patterns, including clonal expansion. In addition, our evolutionary patterns showed different relapse timing, consistent with the findings of a study in human diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL)19. In the above study, the authors indicated that relapses occurring in different timing frames exhibit distinct evolutionary dynamics, and that chemotherapy is more favourable for late relapse than for early relapse. In our study, relapsed MGH-U3-LT tumours exhibited two distinct relapse time frames with different evolutionary patterns: early relapse directly resulted in the MP tumour, and late relapse generated the UCSD tumour, as shown in Fig. 3e. Carcinoma cells exhibiting EMT are highly resistant to chemotherapy68, whereas basal-like bladder tumours are generally more sensitive to chemotherapy89. Given these reports and our findings, we speculate that the outcomes of chemotherapy may be superior for the UCSD tumour (late relapse) than for the MP tumour (early relapse), as the latter has a higher level of EMT but lower basal-like characteristics, as indicated by single-cell transcriptomic profiles (Fig. 2f). Collectively, our evolutionary models showed similarities with the human counterparts.

Third, we showed that the differential plasticity of ELCs between the RT4-LT and MGH-U3-LT models underlies their distinct evolutionary patterns, generating differential tumour phenotypes. Although chemotherapy efficacy varies across RT4 mice since individual mice vary in how they absorb, distribute, metabolise, and excrete drugs, consistent evolutionary dynamics and recurrent tumour phenotypes were observed in our study, indicating that cell-intrinsic factors underly plasticity differences. More importantly, differential outcomes were observed in two LT models, certainly explaining cell intrinsic factors primarily driving these plasticity differences. Our study also found that differences in alterations of plasticity patterns during evolution shaped the phenotypes of relapsed tumours. We guessed that IGFBP5 upregulation and differential chromatin accessibility biases cells toward the mesenchymal/MP route. The phenomenon is possibly conserved, since Igfbp5 upregulation was also observed in MP tumours of GEMM. In UCSD tumour evolution, we also observed consistent alterations in plasticity patterns (relative stability) across LT models. These phenomena indicate that the choice of evolutionary path is strictly governed by lineage-specific transcriptional programmes rather than being stochastic. However, the plasticity differences between the MGH-U3 model and GEMM may involve more complex mechanisms, given differences in cell origin, microenvironment, and subtypes of recurrent tumour phenotypes. Thus, whether extrinsic factors also contribute remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Our findings have several important clinical implications. First, the preexistence of the ELCs in the tumour mass suggests that there may be diagnostic opportunities for detecting the chemoresistant rare subpopulation within patients with BLCA before intravesical chemotherapy to predict which patients may benefit from chemotherapy. For the cohort of patients who cannot benefit from intravesical chemotherapy, additional adjuvant therapies (e.g., immune checkpoint inhibitors) following chemotherapy could be considered since the presence of the squamous feature in UC is associated with increased expression of PD-L138. Nevertheless, the efficacy of these combination strategies requires further validation through preclinical studies and clinical trials.

More importantly, the evolutionary dynamics we deciphered herein raise the possibility of therapeutic strategies to cure relapsed BLCA despite the lack of standard paradigms for treating relapsed tumours90–92. Our analysis identified a unique cell subpopulation (i.e., progenitor-like cells) originating from ELCs and resembling epithelial precursors during embryonic development that enables the transition into the mesenchymal state (evolutionary endpoint), which results in the formation of an MP tumour or transition into the enteric-like state, leading to metastasis. A recent study observed that a similar cell state could undergo differentiation into cells with distinct states and play a critical role in colorectal cancer metastasis73. Notably, suppression of IGFBP5, which is highly expressed in this subpopulation, simultaneously attenuated mesenchymal and squamous traits. These findings suggest that IGFBP5 may serve as a key target for differentiation therapy.

Cell lines

Animals

The Trp53flox/flox; Ptenflox/flox; Akaluc-IRES-tdTomato mice were generated by breeding Trp53flox/flox mice, Ptenflox/flox mice and Rosa26-LSL-Akaluc-IRES-tdTomato mice. For tumour transplantation experiments, 5–6-week-old NCG (NOD/ShiltJ-Prkdc-em26Cd52-Il2rg em26Cd22) mice, Balb/c Nude mice, and C57BL/6 J mice from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China) were utilised. Female mice were used in the recurrence experiments of NMIBC in the context of Gemcitabine because intravesical instillation was fundamentally incompatible for male mice. In other animal experiments, both female and male mice were used in this study. The animals were maintained and bred under a specific-pathogen-free environment at the Institute of Precision Medicine at the Shenzhen Luohu People’s Hospital Group. Adult animals were housed in a cage with a maximum of five mice unless specific requirements were needed. The light cycle was 12 h. The temperature of the animal rooms was 20–26 °C, and the humidity of the animal rooms was 40–70%.

Human tumour samples

Five matched pairs of pre-chemotherapy and post-chemotherapy tumour samples were acquired as paraffin-embedded tissue from Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University. Three matched pairs of pre-immunotherapy and post-immunotherapy (disitamab vedotin plus toripalimab) tumour samples were acquired as paraffin-embedded tissue from Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University. Eight matched tumour pairs of primary non-muscle-invasive bladder carcinoma (P-NMIBC) and relapsed muscle-invasive bladder carcinoma (R-MIBC) were acquired as paraffin-embedded tissue from Dongguan People’s Hospital.

Tumour formation and grafting

To induce spontaneous tumour formation in Trp53flox/flox; Ptenflox/flox; Akaluc-IRES-tdTomato mice, adenovirus-expressing Cre recombinase under the KRT5 promoter (AdenoKRT5-Cre; Hanheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China)) was surgically delivered to the bladder lumen as described previously39. In particular, the virus (25 μl) was mixed 1:1 (v/v) with DMEM (Gibco, 2263286) containing polybrene (10 μg/ml, MedChemExpress, HY-112735) and Evans Blue dye (0.02%, Sigma-Aldrich, 314-13-6). The diluted virus (5-10 μl) was injected into the bladder lumen. For the recurrence experiment exposed to chemotherapy using the genetically engineered mouse model (GEMM) of bladder cancer, intravesical Gemcitabine hydrochloride (240 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich, 122111-03-9) was administered in the seventh week after virus injection and repeated four times (once per week). After that, Gemcitabine hydrochloride was withdrawn, and the minimal residual disease (MRD) was allowed to grow.

For the xenograft recurrence experiment of NMIBC in the context of Gemcitabiine, 1 × 105 engineered bladder carcinoma cells, MGH-U3-LT or RT4-LT, were injected into the bladder lumen of NCG or BALB/c nude mice and maintained in the bladder lumen for at least 2 h. On the 3rd day after injection, mice were randomly assigned to the vehicle and the chemotherapy groups, then treated with the same therapeutic regimen as in GEMM. Following that, MRD was allowed to grow without treatment. At the experimental endpoint, tumours from xenograft models and GEMM of bladder cancer were both collected to be subject to histopathology analysis, generation of cell lines, and multi-omics sequencing.

To investigate IGFBP5 functions in vivo, 2 × 105 sgScr MP-MGH-U3-LT and sgIGFBP5 MP-MGH-U3-LT cells were orthotopically injected into the bladder wall of NCG mice, respectively. Similarly, sgScr CUUC-G and sgIgfbp5 CUUC-G cells were transplanted into C57BL/6 J mice in the same manner. Tumour growth was monitored once per week by bioluminescence imaging.

For the chemosensitivity assessment, mice injected with 2 × 105 sgScr MP-MGH-U3-LT and sgIGFBP5 MP-MGH-U3-LT cells were evenly assigned to four groups on the 3rd day: sgScr + vehicle, sgIGFBP5 + vehicle, sgScr + chemo, and sgIGFBP5 + chemo groups, which was based on signal values of bioluminescence imaging. Following that, mice were treated with either vehicle (Saline, Beyotime, ST341) or Gemcitabine hydrochloride (240 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich, 122111-03-9) once per week via intravesical installation. sgScr CUUC-G and sgIGFBP5 CUUC-G cells repeated the above experiment in the same manner in C57BL/6 J mice. Tumour growth and therapeutic response were monitored once per week by bioluminescence imaging. In all animal experiments, the maximal tumour burden did not exceed the threshold (2000 mm3) set up by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Shenzhen Luohu Hospital Group.

Limiting dilution assay

To evaluate the tumour-initiating ability across cell lines derived from different tumour phenotypes, a preset number of cells was orthotopically injected into the bladder wall of NCG mice, and then the tumorigenicity of cells was determined by bioluminescence imaging at 5 weeks after injection. Bladder carcinoma cells were diluted serially from 2 × 104 to 2 × 106 cells per ml, and 50 µl cell mixtures in PBS were injected. For cell lines derived from MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT tumours, 1 × 103 and 1 × 104 cells per injection were evaluated in 6 mice and 1×105 cells per injection was evaluated in 5 mice. For cell lines derived from transgenic mice, 1 × 103 and 1 × 104 cells per injection were evaluated in 7 mice and 1 × 105 cells per injection was evaluated in 6 mice.

Bioluminescence imaging

Mice were imaged with the AniView100 bioluminescent imaging system (Guangzhou Biolight, China) at the Shenzhen Luohu Hospital Group Medical Centre. Bioluminescence monitoring of tumour growth or therapeutic responses was performed once per week. Before imaging, mice were anesthetised with inhaled isoflurane and then injected intraperitoneally with 200 µL of D-Luciferin, Sodium Salt (Genomeditech, GM-040611) at a dose of 150 mg/kg body weight. For imaging AdenoKRT5-Cre; Trp53flox/flox; Ptenflox/lox; Akaluc-IRES-tdTomato mice, mice were anaesthetised with inhaled isoflurane and then injected intraperitoneally with 200 µL of AkaLumine hydrochloride (MedChemExpress, HY-112641A) at a dose of 5 mg/kg body weight. For each mouse, the bioluminescent signal was measured and calculated automatically using AniView100 Software; radiance units are photons/s/cm2 /steradian.

Immunohistochemistry and histopathology

All paraffin-embedded tumour tissues were sectioned with 5 μm thickness. For histopathology, sections were deparaffinated in Xylene (Aladdin, 1330-20-7) 5 min thrice, 100% ethanol (Aladdin, 64-17-5) 3 min twice, 95% ethanol 2 min, 70% ethanol 2 min, and 50% ethanol 2 min, and then were processed by Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining according to the standard protocol of Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) Stain Kit (Solarbio, G1120). For regular immunohistochemistry, slides were then probed with primary antibodies (1:100-1:200) for overnight at 4 °C. Next, a two-step detection kit (ZSGB-Bio, PV-9000) was used for IHC, and hematoxylin for nuclear staining. For multiplex immunohistochemistry (MIHC), primary antibodies were incubated sequentially according to the instructions of the 4-colour Multiplex IHC kit (Panovue, 10001100020). Briefly, deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking were performed. For each staining, after primary and secondary antibody incubation and rinse 3 times with PBS, fluorescein for different absorption wavelengths was added to the sections in 1:100 dilutions for 10 min at room temperature. Antibody complexes were removed by a second round of antigen retrieval, then repeated for the whole cycle for the next antibody. After the final cycle of staining, slides were stained with DAPI for 1 min at room temperature, then washed with distilled water and mounted after air-drying without dehydration.

Generation of cell lines from inducible bladder tumours in the transgenic mouse model

Tissue homogenisation and single-cell preparation

Single-cell suspensions of bladder tumours were prepared following the procedure below. Tumour tissues were washed using washing solution (PBS supplemented with 5% v/v penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), 1% w/v BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, 9048-46-8)) three times and minced with scissors on ice into small pieces. The pieces of tumour tissues were further rolling incubated in 10 mL of dissociation enzyme solution (DMEM medium with 100 μg/mL collagenase type I (Solarbio, C8140), 100 μg/mL collagenase type IV (Solarbio, C8160), 3% v/v penicillin/streptomycin, 20% v/v 0.25% trypsin (Gibco, 25200-072), 100 U DNase I (Solarbio, D8071), and 1% w/v BSA(Sigma-Aldrich, 9048-46-8)) for 2 h at 37 °C. Dissociated tissues were strained through a 40 μm filter (Corning, 352340) and then spun down at 350 g for 5 min. To remove red blood cells, the cell pellets were resuspended in 10 mL red blood cells Lysing Buffer (Solarbio, R1010) and incubated at room temperature for 3 min, followed by centrifugation. Cell pellets were resuspended with 6 ml washing solution and then strained through a 40 µm nylon filter-cap tube. Cells were fluorescence-sorted (identified by high GFP, mCherry and BFP fluorescence) to collect indicated carcinoma cells. Finally, the cells were thoroughly resuspended in PBS with 0.05% BSA, mixed with the Chromium Single Cell 3′ V3.1 Kit reagents (10X Genomics) according to the User Guide, and then loaded into droplet emulsions using the Chromium Controller, thus facilitating single-cell transcriptome analysis.

Immunoblotting

To collect proteins, collected cells were washed on the plate with cold PBS thrice, lysed in RIPA Buffer (Solarbio, R0010) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (MedChemExpress, HY-K0013), and collected by scraping. Collected cells were then lysed for 30 min on ice and then centrifuged to collect the supernatant. The protein concentration was determined by the BCA assay (Byeotime, P0012) against a bovine serum albumin standard curve. Protein (30 μg) of each sample was used for immunoblotting according to the standard protocol. Briefly, 30 ug protein was run on 10–15% Bis-Tris Gels for 45 min at 45 V and then for 1 h at 120 V in SDS Running Buffer. Protein was transferred onto the PVDF membrane (Millipore, IPVH00010) at 220 mA for 0.5–2 h according to the molecular weight of proteins. Membranes were blocked in blocking buffer (5% milk) for 1 h at room temperature before incubating with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. PVDF membranes containing proteins were washed three times in 0.1% Tween-20 in TBS before incubating with secondary antibody. The following primary antibodies and dilutions were used: primary antibodies (anti-CRISPR-Cas9 1:2000, Abcam, 7A9-3A3; anti-IGFBP5 1:1,000, Proteintech, 55205-1-AP; anti-beta actin, 1:5000, CST #3700; SPRR3 Rabbit Polyclonal Antibody 1:2000, HUABIO, ER1917-16), secondary antibodies were used at 1:10,000 (goat anti-rabbit HRP or goat anti-mouse HRP, CST #7074 or #91196). Membranes were imaged with a GelView 6000ProⅡ (Guangzhou Biolight, China).

For gene knockout, sgRNA sequences targeting mIgfbp5 or hIGFBP5 were synthesised by Guangzhou RiboBio Co., Ltd (Supplementary Dataset 3). sgRNAs were then subcloned into Lenti-CRIPSRv2 plasmid, respectively. The components of GFP-TargetSites and triple-sgRNA-BFP plasmid were synthesised by Guangzhou RiboBio Co., Ltd. and then were subcloned into the pll3.7 lentiviral system. Lenti-CRIPSRv2 and plasmid that express mCherry as the template to get Cas9-P2A-mCherry. Luciferase were amplified from PLV-CMV-luciferase-t2a-GFP. Engineered cell lines construction

Two lineage tracing-competent cell lines (hereafter, “MGH-U3-LT” and “RT4-LT”) were engineered as described in ref. 30 with minor modification. Briefly, for both MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT, the cells were first infected with luciferase-neomycin lentivirus; two days following infection, neomycin (+) cells were selected by treatment with 1000 µg/mL G-418 disulfate (MedChemExpress, 108321-42-2) for 12 days, and then single clones with stronger luciferase were selected and expanded. The strength of Luciferase was detected by the AniView100 bioluminescent imaging system. Next, the selected luciferase+ cells were infected with Cas9-P2A-mCherry lentivirus. Three days following infection, mCherry+ cells were collected by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) using the BD FACS Aria II. Following that, single-cell colonies were picked by cloning cylinders and seeded into 96-well plates. Their overexpression efficiency was further validated by western blotting. Next, the luciferase + ; mCherry+ cells were transduced with the GFP-TargetSite vector. After GFP-TargetSite transduction, mCherry + GFP + cells were collected by FACS. The above transduction-collection step was repeated for a total of three or four times to further maximise diversity of TargetSite (Supplementary Fig. 1). Finally, four days prior to implantation, luciferase + ; mCherry + ; GFP+ cells were transduced by tittered triple-sgRNA-BFP lentivirus; 48 hours following infection, mCherry + ; GFP + ; BFP + cells were collected by FACS and returned to cell culture until final preparation for implantation, thus producing lineage tracing-competent MGH-U3-LT and RT4-LT cell lines. Notably, the lineage information recording is initiated with the generation of lineage-tracing-competent cells. Since the same cell pool was used to establish the orthotopic mouse model across all experimental conditions (Vehicle or Gemcitabine), the onset of recording was consistent throughout the study. Furthermore, our data revealed a strong correlation between phylogenetic distance (i.e., the distance between two cells in the lineage tree) and allelic distance (the difference between their lineage alleles), indicating that mutations continue to accumulate until the experimental endpoint after transplantation (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). In summary, both the onset and duration of lineage recording were equivalent across all experimental conditions.

Organoid cultures

Sorted carcinoma cells (5 × 105 cells in total) were resuspended in 60% matrigel (Corning, 354234) mixed with organoid culture media (DMEM/F12 (Gibco, C11330500BT) supplemented with 1 x Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco, 15140-122), 1 x B27 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 17504-044), 1.56mM N-Acetylcysteine (Sigma-Aldrich, A9165-5G), 10 mM Nicotinamide (Sigma-Aldrich, 98-92-0), 500 nM A83-01 (MCE, HY-10432), 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (MCE, HY-P7109 or HY-P7067), 10 μM Y-27632 (MCE, HY-10071), 1x Glutamax (Gibco, 35050-061), 100 ng/mL FGF10 (MCE, HY-P7342AF or HY-P7170), 100 ng/mL R-spondin-1 (MCE, HY-P72784 or HY-P76012) and 100 ng/mL Noggin (MCE, HY-P70558 or HY-P70785)). The suspension was then plated as 250 μl droplet in the centre of one well of a pre-coated 6-well plate (Corning) with 60% Matrigel. The solidification of the drop was achieved by incubating the plate at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 30 minutes. Once the droplet solidified, 2 ml of organoid culture medium was added to the well, with media replacement performed every 3–4 days. We processed organoids for paraffin sectioning and Haematoxylin–eosin staining using standard protocols in the section of Immunohistochemistry and histopathology. Before organoid processing and embedding, we harvested organoids to remove the Matrigel, fixed them in 4% PFA (Solarbio, G2162) for 30 minutes at 4 °C, washed them in PBS three times, and placed them in 50 μl 3% Agarose (Sigma-Aldrich, A6013-10G) until solidification. To acquire dose–response curves in organoids for Gemcitabine, cell viability was assayed using CellTiter-Glo 3D (Promega, G9681) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Bulk RNA sequencing analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Three biological replicates were conducted for each condition. RNA-seq library was prepared using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, NR604-01). Adaptor ligation was performed using the VAHTS RNA Adaptors set1/set2 for Illumina, followed by paired-end 150 bp sequencing on a Novaseq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing reads underwent quality control and filtering via Fast with the parameters “--cut_front” and “--cut_tail” to generate high-quality clean reads. Transcript and gene expression levels were quantified using salmon and tximport, aligned to the GRCh38 or GRCm39 reference genomes, and annotated with GENCODE. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.44.0), with protein-coding genes deemed significant if they exhibited an absolute log2 fold change > 1 and an FDR < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.12.6).

scRNA-seq data analysis

Reads were mapped to the GRCh38 human transcriptome using Cell Ranger (v6.1.2) (10X Genomics), and TPM was computed for each gene in each filtered cell. Scanpy93 (v1.9.2) pipeline was leveraged to process and visualise scRNA-seq data. Genes expressed in fewer than three cells were removed, and cells with either < 3000 or more than 11,000 detected genes or > 0.2 mitochondrial fraction were excluded from further analysis. Datasets were integrated by the merge function in Scanpy, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on 2000 highly variable genes. UMAP was used to reduce dimensions based on the top 30 principal components. To identify differential expression genes (DEGs) of cell states of interest (i.e., ELCs and progenitor-like), the single-cell RNA count matrix was normalised to the median UMI count across cells and log-transformed. Then, DEGs were determined using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test in Scanpy, comparing each cell state of interest against all others with default parameters. The top 10 genes were selected from the DEGs of ELCs passing an FDR cutoff of 0.05 and a log2foldchange cutoff of 1, as displayed in Supplementary Fig. 10a.

Differential expression analysis of patient tumours

Bulk RNA-seq data were obtained from GEO datasets GSE124305, GSE48075, and GSE32894. Samples were classified into pre-chemotherapy and post-chemotherapy groups according to the original study’s annotations. The TPM expression matrix was log2-transformed (TPM + 1) for normalisation. To address technical batch effects between GEO datasets, we applied the removeBatchEffect function from the Limma package (v3.58.1). Then, differential expression analysis was performed using Limma’s linear modelling framework with empirical Bayes moderation. The threshold for significantly differentially expressed genes is an FDR of less than 0.05 and a log2 fold change of greater than 1. All analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.3).

scATAC-seq data analysis

scATAC-seq library was prepared using Chromium Single Cell ATAC Reagent (V1.1 chemistry) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Raw sequencing data (Fastq files) were aligned to the GRCh38 human genome using Cell Ranger ATAC v.2.1 (10X Genomics) to produce the count matrix. Subsequent processing was performed with Signac94 (v1.14.0) in R, first applying quality filtering: cells were retained only if they demonstrated < 2.5% blacklist region reads, > 20% peak region reads, transcription start site (TSS) enrichment scores > 2, nucleosome signal < 4, and total fragments in peaks between 2000-100,000. For chromatin accessibility analysis, we employed the following parameters: (1) the top 50 dimensions derived from singular value decomposition (SVD), and (2) association of chromatin accessible peaks within 2 kb upstream regions of each gene with corresponding gene annotations. Differential accessibility analysis was performed using MAST with the number of fragments in peak regions included as a latent variable.

Whole exome sequencing analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from CTR-MGH-U3-LT, UCSD-MGH-U3-LT, and MP-MGH-U3-LT using the EasyPure® Genomic DNA Kit (TransGen, EE101-12). Libraries were prepared by Novogene Co., Ltd. Libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform. For whole-exome sequencing (WES) analysis, raw sequencing reads were cleaned using the fastp (v0.19.7) tool and aligned to GRCh38 using BWA (v0.7.17) with default parameters. Following alignment, we conducted a series of data pre-processing steps as recommended by the Best Practices guidelines for somatic short variant discovery provided by the Genome Analysis Toolkit (v4.1.8.1). For the detection of somatic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (indels), we applied GATK’s mutect2 tool (v4.1.0.0). This caller can work with a tumour-normal pair of aligned samples (BAM files). The CTR-MGH-U3-LT served as the control for the other two samples. We applied stringent filtering criteria to identify variants, retaining only those meeting the following conditions: (1) variant allele frequency (VAF) > 10%, (2) location within protein-coding exons, and (3) absence in the matched control sample. Additional quality filters excluded variants that exhibited strand bias or occurred in homopolymer regions. Finally, the ANNOVAR tool was used to provide detailed annotations for the filtered variants, drawing information from multiple databases including refGene, cytoBand, Avsnp150, dbNSFP, COSMIC, and ClinVar.

Survival analysis with TCGA or UROMOL cohorts of bladder cancer

The fitness signature with only up-related genes was utilised in survival analysis. The bladder carcinoma (MIBC) cohort with RNA-seq data in TCGA was stratified into high-fitness and low-fitness groups based on the median expression of the sum of Fitness Signature genes. Subsequently, the R package survival (v3.7.0) was used for survival analysis, displaying the Kaplan–Meier curve along with a log-rank statistical test. For survival analyses based on IGFBP5 expression in the TCGA MIBC cohort or the UROMOL NMIBC cohort, patients were divided into high- and low-gene expression groups based on the optimal cutoff point, followed by survival analysis.

Lineage-tracing data analysis

The following analysis methods with a more detailed description, can be found in refs. 30 and 37, and the related code is available from https://github.com/mattjones315/KPTracer-release/tree/main/cassiopeia-kp. Here, we briefly introduce the ones used in this study.

Target site sequencing data processing and tree reconstruction with Cassiopeia

The raw sequencing data of the target site was pre-processed by Cassiopeia (v1.0.0) with default parameter settings33,37 for downstream phylogenetic tree reconstruction. Trees for each clonal population were reconstructed with Cassiopeia-Hybrid37. Briefly, Cassiopeia-Hybrid infers phylogenies by first splitting cells into clusters using a “greedy” criterion (Cassiopeia-Greedy) until a user-defined criterion is met, at which point each cluster of cells is reconstructed using a near-optimal Steiner-Tree maximum-parsimony algorithm (Cassiopeia-ILP). In most inferences, we used indel priors computed with Cassiopeia to select mutations using a Cassiopeia-Greedy algorithm, as well as to weight edges during the Steiner-Tree search with Cassiopeia-ILP. Generally, we used an LCA-based cutoff to transition between Cassiopeia-Greedy and Cassiopeia-ILP as previously described37.

Calling clonal populations and creating character matrices

Clonal populations/tumours were characterised as cell populations derived from a single progenitor clone, thereby harbouring identical sets of integrated barcodes (intBCs). Cassiopeia was leveraged to distinguish non-overlapping intbc sets and categorise cells through the utilisation of the “call-lineages” tool. Following the identification of intBC sets, clonal populations were subsequently determined. Specifically, (1) A “collection of intBC set” was defined as the intBCs observed in more than 20% of cells containing the top intBC (i.e., the most frequently detected intBC). (2) When cells had more than 25% of their UMIS of target site associated with the collection of intbc set, these cells were grouped into a “cell cluster” and the corresponding intBCs were removed from the intbc pool. (3) This above iterative process continued until either: (i) the inferred cell cluster was empty, indicating no further well-defined clusters, or (ii) clustered cells exceeded 98% of all cells determined by the intBC pool. (4) Next, intBCs presenting in more than 20% of cells in the same cell cluster were defined as a “clonal population intBC set”. Importantly, clusters exhibiting overlapping clonal population intBC sets were manually redefined to ensure accurate delineation of distinct clonal populations. (5) cells were assigned to a clonal population, if over 60% of their target site UMIs corresponded to a single clonal population intBC set. To obtain enough information for phylogenetic tree reconstruction, clonal populations with fewer than 90 cells were removed. Moreover, cut sites of individual clonal populations with over 80% missing information or mutations present in more than 98% of cells were excluded.

Preprocessing lineages with respect to states

In certain lineages, polytomies formed at the bottom of the tree due to the saturation of indels at the target sites. This could create an illusion of closer cellular relationships than what exists. To address this, a conservative approach was taken when interpreting cellular relationships in such non-bifurcating subclades. Specifically, states were assigned to each leaf in the tree, and then extra splits were introduced to each unique state in the tree if polytomies had more than one unique state.

Single-cell and mean EffectivePlasticity

EffectivePlasticity for individual clonal tumours was acquired by measuring a normalised parsimony score for cell identities at the leaves of the clonal tumour tree using the Fitch-Hartigan algorithm. Briefly, cells from Leiden clusters that accounted for less than 2.5% of the total tree size were first excluded. After that, cell identities (Leiden clusters) were assigned to the leaves of the phylogenetic tree. Then, the minimum number of times a transition from the ancestral cell to the required cell identities was measured. These parsimony scores were further normalised by the number of edges in the tree to get the EffectivePlasticity score. As every subtree contains a single cell, we thus calculate the average EffectivePlasticity of every subtree to represent the single cell EffectivePlasticity. The subtree is rooted at a node on the path from the root to a leaf. To measure the mean EffectivePlasticity of each Leiden cluster, cells were first stratified in each clonal tumour based on the Leiden cluster. Next, scores in each clonal tumour at the cluster level were averaged, thereby acquiring a distribution of Effective Plasticity scores for each Leiden cluster.

Measurement of the Allelic Effective plasticity score

The Allelic Effective Plasticity score provides an assessment of effective plasticity that does not rely on the phylogenetic tree. This score was acquired by quantifying the proportion of cells that are found in a different Leiden cluster than their closest relative, determined by modified edit distance. Notably, if a cell has multiple closest relatives, each relative’s contribution is normalised by the total number of equally close relatives this cell has.

Measurement of the L2 effective plasticity score

The L2 Effective Plasticity score provided an alternative tree-based metric that addressed random noise at the boundaries between Leiden clusters, rather than treating each cluster as a single point. Similarly with the Effective Plasticity score, nearest neighbours for each cell i were identified using the phylogenetic tree, and the neighbours located in a different Leiden cluster than cell i were considered. However, unlike the Effective Plasticity score, the L2 Effective Plasticity score specifically utilised an L2-distance in the x_umap latent space to quantify the distinctiveness of the neighbour. The L2 Effective Plasticity for each clonal tumour was then calculated as the average across all leaves within the clonal tumour.

Evolutionary coupling

Evolutionary Coupling refers to the normalised phylogenetic distance between any pair of variables on a tree. Specifically, the distance between pairs of states within a subset of the leaves of the phylogenetic tree was calculated and averaged. To generate background-normalised phylogenetic distances, these average distances were averaged by comparing them to a random background generated by shuffling the leaf assignments 2000 times. This process for obtaining a score is applied to all pairs of states in each clonal tumour that exceeds a 2.5% proportion threshold, retaining only cells in states that exceed this threshold. Interstate evolutionary couplings were further normalised by the matrix produced in the 2nd step. Finally, Evolutionary Couplings presented in this work were normalised by all the Evolutionary Couplings between states in a given clonal tumour.

K-nearest-neighbour (KNN) Coupling and Allelic Coupling

In this study, KNN Coupling and Allelic Coupling were used to assess whether these two Coupling results were consistent with that of Evolutionary Coupling. For Allelic Coupling, the distance between two cells at ith character was calculated based on modified edit distances (i refers to any possible value). To score KNN Coupling, the phylogenetic distance between leaves and the kth neighbour was calculated in the Evolutionary Coupling statistic (k = 10).

Hierarchical clustering of fates

Cell populations that accounted for less than 2.5% of the total tumour cells were initially filtered out to remove intrinsic noise from the clustering. Next, lineages were preprocessed to ensure that each leave of the phylogenetic tree with the same Leiden cluster was grouped. The number of mutated edges was used as the phylogenetic distance metric, following the principle that any edge with at least one mutation was assigned a weight of 1, and otherwise, the edge was assigned a weight of 0. After pre-processing, the evolutionary coupling for all pairs of states within each clonal tumour was measured. All vectors of evolutionary coupling were further concatenated together into a matrix. Evolutionary couplings were then converted to similarities by raising their values to an exponent. The cell state proportions were also added as another feature for clustering, corresponding to the coupling vector of each clonal tumour. After that, features were z-normalised, and then this transformed matrix was hierarchically clustered based on the Euclidean metric and the “ward” linkage method in the Python scipy package. Through this hierarchical clustering, three distinct groups, each representing a different fate, were identified. Three Fates were UMAP visualised on the cell state proportion concatenated matrix and Evolutionary Coupling. Key couplings were further pinpointed through PCA applied to the concatenated matrix of evolutionary coupling.

Aggregating Evolutionary Coupling across samples

To establish a consensus Evolutionary Coupling map across the clonal tumours from a specific fate or sample, we first calculated the average Evolutionary Coupling between individual state pairs in individual clonal tumours. Subsequently, we normalised these average Evolutionary Couplings by comparing them to the number of tumours. Notably, evolutionary patterns driven by a small proportion of cells were removed, and we only considered states that appeared in more than 2.5% of the total number of cells across all clonal tumours in a fate or sample.

Phylotime

Phylotime was defined as the distance between individual cells and their first ancestor with a particular state. To calculate the phylotime, we first defined the ground state, such as ELCs, depending on the analysed samples, and then inferred the sets of ancestral states using the Fitch-Hartigan bottom-up algorithm. Then, we quantified the branch length separating individual cells from their nearest ancestor as phylogenetic distances (i.e., the number of non-zero-length branches) in each clonal tumour via the Fitch-Hartigan bottom-up algorithm. Therefore, this Phylotime metric essentially reflects the proportion of the number of generations elapsed since the more recent ancestral node under maximum parsimony assumptions. The structure of phylogenetic trees is advantageous in modelling evolutionary divergence as it can explain homoplasy (i.e., same mutations occurring independently) and convergent phenotypic evolution events (i.e., reaching the same transcriptomic state separately), in contrast to pseudotime metrics inferred from scrna-seq. Briefly, the phylotime of each clonal tumour was normalised (0-1 scale). Next, phylotimes from targeted clonal tumours were merged by performing an average-based smoothing in X_umap latent space.

Phylotime differential expression

The Tradeseq package identified the changes in gene expression along Phylotime in each tumour sample95. Specifically, first VISION96 was applied to identify highly variable genes that were expressed in more than 10% of cells for each tumour sample. Second, fitted expression models and genes related to Phylotime were identified using the fitgam and associationTest functions of the Tradeseq package, respectively. The filtered criteria for significant genes were an FDR below 0.05 and a log2 fold change above 0.5. Third, the fitted expression models were leveraged to predict smoothed expression profiles, of which genes were subsequently clustered into the different segments of Phylotime, namely, from early to late. Finally, significant genes of interest were manually displayed.

Transcriptional gene module identification and enrichment

Transcriptional gene modules were identified using Hotspot (v0.9.0) via the following analytical pipeline. First, we performed autocorrelation analysis on the single-cell expression matrix containing all 2000 highly variable genes within the X_pca latent space, utilising the “danb” observation mode and the square root of the number of cells in the corresponding expression matrix. Genes with an FDR of less than 0.05 were retained for downstream clustering. Next, we calculated pairwise local autocorrelations with Hotspot and clustered genes using these pairwise statistics with the “create_modules” function from Hotspot (minimum gene threshold of 100, FDR threshold of 0.05, core_only = False). This procedure identified four distinct modules in MGH-U3-LT tumours and two different modules in RT4-LT tumours, which were used for downstream analysis. Each of the gene module scores was computed with VISION and then normalised to the range [0, 1] across all MGH-U3-LT or RT4-LT carcinoma cells.

Tree quality control and single-cell fitness inference

Before inferring single-cell fitness, we filtered out clonal tumours that had less than 10% unique indel states or less than 20% unexhausted target sites. Tumours with fewer than 90 cells were also removed for this analysis. In some trees containing exhausted lineages where all target sites were saturated with edits, we pre-processed branch lengths on each phylogeny by assigning a value of 0 to branches without mutations, separated nodes and 1 otherwise. Subsequently, we employed the ‘infer_fitness’ function of the jungle package (https://github.com/felixhorns/jungle) to generate fitness estimates for each phylogenetic leaf, normalised to other cells within the same tree.

Tumour fitness differential expression

To identify genes whose expression varied significantly with cellular fitness levels within each clonal tumour, a linear regression approach was employed. The expression level of each gene was regressed against the corresponding cell’s fitness score, while controlling for the number of detected genes per cell as a covariate. The analysis included only genes expressed in at least 10 cells. Using Julia’s GLM package (v1.3.7), we fitted linear models and assessed significance through likelihood ratio tests comparing the fitness-associated model against a reduced model that contained only the gene detection count covariate. We applied Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction to all p-values. To quantify expression differences, we calculated log₂fold change by comparing mean expression levels between cells in the highest and lowest fitness deciles (10th percentiles).

Meta-analysis and derivation of the fitness signature

The transcriptional fitness signature was generated by analysing differential expression patterns across individual clonal tumours using a majority-vote meta-analysis. This method prioritises genes according to their frequency of differential expression ( | log₂foldchange | > log₂(1.5) and FDR < 0.05) and the consistency of their regulation direction across tumour samples. The MetaVolcanoR R package (v1.0.1) was employed to perform the majority-vote analysis, calculating both metrics. Differentially expressed genes of fitness signature were identified if they exhibited differential expression in the same direction at least twice and had a frequency-to-consistency ratio exceeding 0.5.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests with a 95% confidence interval on the GraphPad Prism (v9.5.1). Data were all represented as mean ± SD or mean ± SEM, or otherwise illustrated in figure legends. The degree of statistical significance is denoted by exact P-values. The number of independent experiments, samples, or events is indicated in the figure legends. All attempts at replication in this study were successful. In general, the experiments were not randomised or performed by the investigator in a blinded manner, except where stated.
