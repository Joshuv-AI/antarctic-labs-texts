Background

The prevalence of multiple pregnancy is growing because of developments in assisted reproductive techniques. However, spontaneous multiple pregnancies are also seen, the incidence being 1 in 7000. Multiple pregnancies are associated with complications such as preterm labor, polyhydramnios, fetal neurological defects, structural defects, growth discordance. Certain complications are specific to monochorionic pregnancies such as twin reversed arterial perfusion (TRAP) sequence or acardiac twinning. This is found in 1% of monochorionic twins and 1 in 35,000 pregnancies [1].

This involves a normal fetus (donor or pump twin) that has features of heart failure and an acardiac fetus (recipient twin), that does not have a heart, is nonviable, has multiple congenital and chromosomal anomalies, lacks vital organs, and depends on the normal twin [1]. Even if the heart develops, either it is completely infantile or tubular. This case report presents spontaneous triplet pregnancy along with TRAP sequence.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old Asian female belonging to lower middle class, second gravida para one live one with one male child of 1-years-old delivered by lower segment cesarean section (LSCS) was booked at 24–25 weeks. It was a spontaneous conception. There was no history of ovulation induction, infertility treatment including artificial reproductive techniques or history of oral contraceptive use. There was no history of diabetes, hypertension or any surgical intervention in the past. There was no history of multiple gestation, genetic abnormality, diabetes or hypertension in her family. On examination uterus was corresponding to 26–28 week (more than period of gestation), multiple fetal parts were palpable. Two fetal heart sounds were auscultated. First trimester scan done at 13 weeks showed dichorionic triamniotic (DCTA) pregnancy. There were two regular intrauterine sacs. One embryo was seen in first sac and two embryos in second sac. First fetus was 13 weeks 5 days with nuchal translucency (NT) of 1.2, second was 12 weeks 6 days with NT of 1.6, and third was 10 weeks.

Ultrasonography was repeated at 24–25 weeks which showed two fetus of 24–25 weeks with normal liquor and weight of 751 and 711 g. Third fetus was 15 weeks with ectopia cardis, two chambered heart, deformed spine, diffuse subcutaneous edema. Its umbilical cord had two arteries and one vein. On Doppler imaging, umbilical arterial blood flow was toward the fetus (reversed arterial flow coming from normal twin going to abnormal fetus). Hence, diagnosis of TRAP sequence (acardiac twin) was made.

Fetal echocardiography for first and second fetus was normal. As there were no features of heart failure in the donor twin, expectant management was planned with intensive monitoring by twice weekly ultrasonography to look for any signs of cardiac decompensation.

After booking visit she came at 31 weeks with complaint of lower abdomen pain. There was no history of discharge or bleeding. On examination uterus was corresponding to 26–28 weeks (more than period of gestation), was relaxed and multiple fetal parts were palpable. Two fetal heart sounds were auscultated. Urine examination was negative for urine infection. On ultrasonography, first fetus was 30 weeks with cephalic presentation, liquor single pocket 3 cm, with weight of 1376 g and the second was 31 weeks with transverse presentation, liquor single pocket 4 cm, and weight of 1641 g. Third fetus was 22 weeks with diffuse subcutaneous edema, deformed spine, small skull vault, liquor single pocket 7.6 cm. Patient was given betamethasone 12 mg two doses 24 h apart and vaginal progesterone for 7 days.

At 34 weeks she developed preterm premature rupture of membranes.

Two healthy female babies of weight 2.1 kg and 1.6 kg were delivered by LSCS with Apgar score of 8/10 & 9/10. Acardiac triplet was of 500gm and had partially developed head, forehead, and ear. There was one upper limb, truncal maldevelopment, no lower limbs and ectopia cardis. There were two placentas. Weight of one was 250 g with one umbilical cord and other was 300 g, with two umbilical cords. Acardiac triplet had short cord.

Discussion

TRAP sequence is a rare but serious complication occurring specifically in monochorionic multiple pregnancies. Acardiac twin is caused due to large arterioarterial and venovenous anastomosis present on the surface of placenta, due to which all the blood supply from acardiac triplet comes and goes back to the normal twin rather than going to the placenta. An alternative hypothesis is cardiac dysmorphogenesis or primary defect in heart embryogenesis thus secondarily causing retrograde flow.

Normally oxygenated blood goes through umbilical vein and deoxygenated blood comes through umbilical artery. Owing to single and shared placenta arterial perfusion pressure of donor (normal) fetus exceeds than the recipient (acardiac) fetus. Thus deoxygenated (used arterial) blood from the donor fetus reaches the acardiac fetus through umbilical arteries because of reversed blood flow from its co-twin, hence the term twin reversed arterial perfusion. This deoxygenated blood reaches illiac vessels and aorta, thus insufficiently feeding lower part of the body. There is cardiomegaly and high output cardiac failure in normal fetus. If left untreated almost 50% of these normal fetuses may die in utero or shortly after birth. There may be polyhydramnios which may lead to preterm labor. Prognosis of pregnancy depends on size of acardiac fetus.

Diagnosis is made by Ultrasound. Detection of following features are suggestive of TRAP sequence—deformed twin with either no or rudimentary cardiac structure, cystic changes, craniofacial structures absence, hypoplastic thorax, dysmorphic abdominal organs, spine abnormalities, abdominal wall defects, dysmorphic abdominal organs, absent limbs, and abnormal vascular connections in umbilical cord and placenta. Doppler may show reversed flow in umbilical artery.

Treatment—expectant management with serial sonography and fetal echocardiography is done if the donor fetus does not have features of heart failure.

If diagnosed early in pregnancy, selective fetal reduction can be done by fetoscopic cord ligation of acardiac twin or by ligation of abdominal aorta by radiofrequency. Radiofrequency ablation is the least invasive procedure [4]. Invasive interventions are considered more successful if done in first trimester [5].

TRAP sequence in triplets is more complicated than in twin pregnancies. Venous connections (specially in monochorionic pregnancies) may affect all three fetus and may disturb hemodynamic balance [6–8]. Considering all these facts, conservative approach appears to be safer than invasive management [9, 10].

In our case, anomaly scanning showed normal pump fetus with no anomalies. Therefore, expectant management was decided with twice weekly ultrasound and doppler studies. Invasive procedures were not required as pump twin had no signs of cardiac failure or polyhydramnios and also because the acardiac twin did not grow much.

Conclusion

Triplet pregnancy with TRAP sequence is associated with higher risk of poor pregnancy outcome, including preterm labor and fetal death. Invasive procedures are not required for all multiple pregnancies. Few triplet pregnancies can be managed expectantly. Treatment should be individualized depending on size of acardiac twin, presence of heart failure in pump twin, and the degree of placental anastomosis.
