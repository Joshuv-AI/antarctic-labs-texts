Prevalence and predictors of critical COVID-19 pneumonia among patients with COVID-19 admitted at Nyarugenge major COVID-19 Referral Center in Rwanda
Authors: Mukantagengwa Marie Paul, Ndahimana Raphael, Nkeshimana Menelas, Abimana Deborah, Uwineza Jean Bonaventure, Dushimimana Pasteur, Murekatete Marie Beata, Muhizi Epaphrodite, Hafashimana Valens, Ndayisaba Prosper, Deborah Oluwaseun Shomuyiwa, Muhirwa Samuel, Rutaganda Eric
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronavirus Disease 2019(COVID-19) pneumonia, caused by the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2(SARS-CoV-2 virus), remains a significant global health challenge, contributing to millions of cases and deaths worldwide, including a substantial burden in Rwanda. Severe and critical cases often lead to respiratory failure, multi-organ dysfunction, and increased mortality. Despite global research, data on predictors of critical illness among hospitalized COVID-19 patients in Rwanda remain limited, highlighting the need for context-specific studies. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and identify risk factors for critical COVID-19 pneumonia among patients admitted to the Nyarugenge Major COVID-19 Referral Center in Rwanda.MethodsA mixed-study design combining retrospective and prospective designs was conducted from February to October 2021, and we included 320 patients aged 15 to 65 years admitted with severe or critical COVID-19 pneumonia. Data were collected on socio-demographic factors, vital signs, and clinical symptoms. Multivariate logistic regression was performed using Stata 17 to identify independent predictors of critical illness.ResultsOf the 320 patients, 30% presented with critical illness, while 70% had severe illness. Independent predictors of critical illness included oxygen saturation ≤ 90% {Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) = 5.83, 95% Confidence Interval(CI): 2.95–11.52, p < 0.001), respiratory rate > 20 beat per minute(bpm), (AOR = 2.34, 95% CI: 1.11–4.93, p < 0.05), and heart rate > 100 bpm (AOR = 2.49, 95% CI: 1.32–4.67, p < 0.01).ConclusionsCritical illness was prevalent in 30% of patients, with hypoxia, tachypnea, and tachycardia as significant predictors. These findings identify key clinical predictors of critical COVID-19 pneumonia and suggest that monitoring these indicators may help guide early intervention and optimize resource allocation in Rwanda and similar low- and middle-income settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12001-2.

Background

COVID-19, caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, has emerged as one of the most significant global health crises in recent history [1–3]. Declared a pandemic by the World Health Organization (WHO) on March 11, 2020, the disease had caused over 776.8 million cases and 7 million deaths globally by November 2024 [4].

Early data from Wuhan, China, involving 226 hospitalized COVID-19 patients, indicated that 85 patients (37.6%) required invasive mechanical ventilation, with 14 (6.2%) additionally receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). In addition, Noninvasive mechanical ventilation was administered to 20 patients (8.8%), while 24 (10.6%) underwent continuous renal replacement therapy [5]. As of April 9, 2020, 87 patients (38.5%) had died, and 15 (6.7%) remained hospitalized [5]. Additionally, a total of 1,190 confirmed COVID-19 patients were included in the analysis, of whom 261 (22%) developed severe illness after being admitted to the hospital [6].

Severe and critical COVID-19 pneumonia remain life-threatening complications that have strained healthcare systems globally, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where resources are limited [7]. Severe COVID-19 pneumonia is characterized by fever (>38.5 °C), dyspnea, hypoxia (SpO2 ≤ 90% on room air), and a respiratory rate ≥ 30 breaths per minute. Critical illness involves more advanced complications, including Fraction of inspired oxygen (Fio2) ≤ 300 mmHg, progressive lung involvement (>50% lesion progression within 24–48 h), and ARDS, often necessitating mechanical ventilation or advanced oxygen support [7].

LMICs, which account for 85% of the global population, face unique challenges in managing severe and critical COVID-19 pneumonia [8–10]. Limited access to Intensive Care Init (ICUs), mechanical ventilation, and advanced diagnostics contributes to higher mortality rates among hospitalized patients [10, 11]. According to the Africa Centers for Disease Prevention and Control (CDC), by March 2021, there were 77,463 reported COVID-19 deaths in sub-Saharan Africa and 24,302 COVID-19 deaths when South Africa and Lesotho were excluded, highlighting the disproportionate burden on resource-limited health systems [12]. Rwanda, part of this region, reported 20,186 cases and 280 deaths during the same period [13]. By the end of July 2024, the total number of confirmed COVID-19 cases had risen to 133,518, with 1,468 recorded deaths, highlighting proactive public health measures while also underscoring significant challenges in managing severe and critical COVID-19 cases [14].

While global studies have identified risk factors for severe COVID-19, including demographic factors such as advanced age, male sex, and obesity [15, 16], along with comorbidities like diabetes and hypertension [17–19], physiological indicators such as admission systolic blood pressure, respiratory rate, heart rate, oxygen saturation, and temperature [20–23], alongside key symptoms including shortness of breath, cough, chest pain, fever, and decreased level of consciousness, there remains a significant gap in data specific to low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [24, 25]. This gap is particularly evident in sub-Saharan Africa, where variations in healthcare infrastructure, population demographics, and disease dynamics may influence COVID-19 outcomes, emphasizing the need for region-specific data, particularly in Rwanda, where studies examining factors contributing to critical COVID-19 illness remain limited.

This study aims to assess the prevalence and identify risk factors for severe and critical COVID-19 pneumonia among patients admitted to the Nyarugenge Major COVID-19 Referral Center in Rwanda. By addressing this knowledge gap, the research seeks to generate context-specific data that can inform targeted interventions to enhance clinical decision-making, support resource allocation, and contribute to public health policy improvements and capacity-building efforts in similarly resource-constrained settings.

Methodology

Study design

This study employed both a retrospective and a prospective observational study. The retrospective component involved reviewing records of patients who had been admitted to Nyarugenge major COVID-19 referral center and were either discharged or had died. The prospective component included patients who were still attending the hospital during the study period and were assessed in real-time.

Study setting

The study was conducted at Nyarugenge District Hospital, Kigali City, Rwanda, which functioned as a major COVID-19 referral center with 120 beds, including 8 ICU beds, 24 beds for High Dependency Units (HDU), and 88 general ward beds. The study period spanned from February 1, 2021, to October 30, 2021, with the retrospective data collection covering the period until August 27, 2021, and prospective data collection starting from August 28, 2021, until the study’s conclusion.

Study population

The study included patients aged 15 to 65 years who were admitted with severe or critical COVID-19 pneumonia during the study period. Severe illness was defined by the presence of fever (>38.5 °C), dyspnea (difficulty breathing), and hypoxia (SpO2 ≤ 90% on room air) or a respiratory rate (RR) of ≥ 30 breaths per minute, as per Rwanda Biomedical Center guidelines [7]. Critical illness was characterized by respiratory failure, multi-organ dysfunction, or shock, often requiring advanced interventions such as mechanical ventilation, vasopressors, or ICU-level care. For pregnant women, critical illness was defined as SpO2 ≤ 95% [7].

Operational definition of variables

The dependent variable was either Severe illness of COVID-19, which was coded as 0 (defined by significant respiratory distress but without multi-organ failure), but Critical illness was coded as 1 (defined by severe respiratory failure, shock, and multi-organ dysfunction requiring intensive medical intervention). Independent variables were socio-demographic characteristics, laboratory results, Imaging results, and clinical findings as well.

Sample size used

The sample size was determined using the Taro Yamane (1967) formula, which is commonly applied when working with a finite and well-defined population. This method provides a simplified approach to estimating sample size when the total study population (N) is known, and a specific precision level (e) is desired. The formula is expressed as: [n = N/(1 + N(e)2], Where n is the required sample size, N is the total population, and e is the margin of error (set at 0.05 for 95% confidence). Based on approximately 1,600 total COVID-19 admissions at Nyarugenge Major COVID-19 Referral Center during the study period, the calculated sample size was: [n = 1600/(1 + 1600(0.05)2] = 320. This approach ensured adequate representation from a finite, well-defined hospital population.(Oakland, 1953).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

The study considered all patients aged 15 years up to 65 years old admitted to Nyarugenge district hospital for severe and critical COVID-19 infection during the study period. And, Patients or those to whom care were caregivers agreed to sign a consent for participation in the study (this was for a prospective group).

Exclusion criteria

Patient without clear discharge information in the database (dead, discharged home, counter-referred). And, Patients or those to whom care caregiver did not agree to sign a consent for participation in the study.

Sampling strategy

This study employed a systematic random sampling approach. Patients aged 15 to 65 years who were admitted to Nyarugenge District Hospital with severe or critical COVID-19 infection during the study period were assessed for eligibility based on the inclusion criteria. For the retrospective component (February 1 to August 27, 2021), data were obtained from hospital records and patient files. In the prospective phase (August 28 to October 31, 2021), similar data collection procedures were applied to patients who were still hospitalized. From a total of 1,600 admissions during the study period, the first patient was randomly selected, and thereafter every fifth patient was included to achieve the required sample size of 320 participants.

Data collection tool and procedures

A structured questionnaire was used to gather demographic information, clinical status, past medical history, laboratory and imaging findings, and discharge outcomes. Each patient was assigned a unique code to ensure confidentiality. For the retrospective data, patient records were reviewed, while for the prospective group, clinical information was gathered directly from patients and their caretakers, where applicable. The questionnaire used to collect data was developed by the study team, and it will be published as a supplementary (see supplementary file).

Reliability and validity

Data for this study were obtained from both paper-based and electronic medical records at Nyarugenge Major COVID-19 Referral Center. Retrospective data (February–August 2021) were extracted from hospital files and electronic databases, while prospective data (September–October 2021) were collected directly from patients during admission using a structured questionnaire developed from the Rwanda Biomedical Center (RBC) COVID-19 management guidelines and WHO case record forms. The tool was pre-tested on a small subset of records to ensure clarity and completeness, and data were cross-verified between paper and electronic sources to enhance accuracy. Two trained data collectors independently reviewed the files, with discrepancies resolved by the principal investigator. To minimize bias, systematic random sampling was applied, standardized case definitions were used, and regular data validation checks were conducted to ensure reliability and consistency.

Data processing and analysis

Data collected using the designed tool were analyzed using Stata version 18.5. Descriptive statistics, including the mean, median, and standard deviation, were calculated for continuous variables. The chi-square test (test of independence) was used to assess bivariate analysis of associations between categorical variables and poor discharge outcome as the dependent variable. Variables with significant associations in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis using logistic regression to identify relevant risk factors. No variables exhibited high or extreme multicollinearity. Statistical significance was determined using a p-value of < 0.05, a 95% confidence interval, and an odds ratio that did not cross 1.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the University of Rwanda’s College of Medicine and Health Sciences (Reference No. 289/CMHS IRB/2021), and permission for data collection was granted by the Rwanda Ministry of Health/National Health Research Committee (Reference No. NHRC/2021/PROT/047). In accordance with ethical guidelines, informed consent to participate was obtained directly from all participants aged 16 years and above. For participants younger than 16 years, informed assent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians as a minor age group. All patient data were treated with strict confidentiality, and records were anonymized for analysis.

Results

Socio-demographic factors associated with severity status among COVID-19 patients

Table 1 presents the distribution of socio-demographic factors associated with disease severity among COVID-19 patients (n = 320). A higher proportion of critically ill patients was observed among those aged > 50 years (34.9%) compared to those aged < 35 years (23.8%), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.182). Male patients had a slightly higher proportion of critical illness (34.1%) than female patients (25.7%), with no significant association observed (p = 0.102). Across provinces, the proportion of critically ill patients varied, with the Western Province recording the highest proportion (50.0%) and Kigali City the lowest (23.5%), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.067). Regarding medical insurance coverage, patients insured under Mutuelle de Santé had the highest proportion of critical illness (36.5%), while no critical illness was reported among those covered by Military Medical Insurance (MMI) or the Medical Insurance Scheme of the University of Rwanda (MUS/UR) (p = 0.207). Similarly, analysis by Ubudehe category showed that patients in Category B had a slightly higher proportion of critical illness (33.3%) compared to those in Category A (28.6%), though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.638).

Signs and symptoms associated with severity status among COVID-19 patients

Among 320 patients with COVID-19, the presence of certain signs and symptoms was significantly associated with critical illness. Patients presenting with shortness of breath had a higher proportion of critical illness (32.8%) compared to those without (19.4%, p = 0.033). Similarly, cough was more common among severely ill patients (75.3%) but still showed a significant association with critical illness (24.7% critical illness, p = 0.040). Fever was also linked to a lower proportion of critical illness (18.0% compared to 32.2% among those without fever, p = 0.044). The most significant association was observed for decreased level of consciousness, where 87.5% of affected patients were critically ill (p < 0.001). Other symptoms, including chest pain, hemoptysis, headache, weakness, nausea/vomiting, and agitation, did not demonstrate statistically significant associations with critical illness in this cohort. Vital signs associated with severity status among COVID-19 patients

Table 3 presents the vital signs associated with severity status among 320 Covid-19 cases. Patients with oxygen saturation ≤ 90% had a significantly higher proportion of critical illness (46.1%) compared to those with SpO₂ >90% (14.7%, p < 0.001). Similarly, a respiratory rate > 20 breaths per minute was associated with 38.8% critical illness, while those with a normal respiratory rate (12–20 bpm) had only 13.9% critical illness (p < 0.001). Heart rate > 100 bpm was also significantly associated with critical illness, observed in 41.3% of cases compared to 19.9% in the normal range (60–100 bpm, p < 0.001). Systolic blood pressure and temperature were not significantly associated with critical illness. However, a higher proportion of critically ill patients was observed with systolic Bood Pressure (BP) < 100 mmHg (44.4%) compared to the 100–139 mmHg range (27.6%, p = 0.122). Admission temperature variations showed no significant trend, with 50% critical illness for < 36 °C, 29.2% for 36–37.5 °C, and 27.8% for > 37.5 °C (p = 0.246).

Multivariate analysis of factors associated with severity status among COVID-19 patients

Table 4 shows factors associated with severity status among COVID-19 patients. After adjustment, patients with a respiratory rate above 20 breaths per minute had higher odds of severe disease (AOR = 2.34; 95% CI: 1.11–4.93; p = 0.024). Those with oxygen saturation ≤ 90% were also more likely to be severe (AOR = 5.83; 95% CI: 2.95–11.52; p < 0.001). An elevated heart rate (> 100 bpm) was significantly associated with severity (AOR = 2.49; 95% CI: 1.32–4.67; p = 0.004), while a low heart rate (< 60 bpm) was not (AOR = 3.39; 95% CI: 0.91–12.57; p = 0.067). Decreased level of consciousness had the strongest association with severity (AOR = 9.0; 95% CI: 8.2–19.76; p < 0.001). Shortness of breath, cough, and fever were not significantly associated with severe disease.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the prevalence and predictors of critical COVID-19 pneumonia among patients admitted to the Nyarugenge Major COVID-19 Referral Center in Rwanda. The Discussion focused on the significant Variables identified in analysis, which include admission respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, heart rate, decreased level of consciousness and fever. Of the 320 patients analyzed, 30% were categorized as having critical illness, while 70% experienced severe illness. These findings align with similar studies conducted in other resource-limited settings, including the African COVID-19 Critical Care Outcomes Study (ACCCOS) [26] and a multi-center cohort study in Libya [27], both of which reported significant proportions of critical COVID-19 cases. The ACCCOS study, for example, observed high critical illness rates with a mortality rate of 48.2% in critically ill patients, exceeding the global average [26].

Patients presenting with oxygen saturation ≤ 90% had significantly higher odds of developing critical COVID-19 severity compared to their counterparts. This finding aligns with a retrospective cohort study from China and research from treatment centers in Southern Ethiopia, both highlighting that lower oxygen saturation indicates lung compromise with reduced functional capacity [28, 29]. The SARS-CoV-2 virus’s strong affinity for lung tissue can lead to alveolar damage and impaired gas exchange, commonly observed in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [30]. Hypoxemia is widely recognized as a key marker of disease severity and a major determinant for ICU admission and the need for mechanical ventilation in COVID-19 patients [31].

Compared to the normal respiratory rate range of 12–20 breaths per minute, a respiratory rate exceeding 20 breaths per minute was significantly associated with critical illness. This finding is consistent with studies conducted at the Hospital Clinic of Barcelona and Taipei Medical University Hospital [32, 33], which emphasized that tachypnea reflects increased work of breathing and the body’s compensatory response to hypoxia, often preceding respiratory failure. These results highlight the importance of incorporating respiratory rate into early warning scores for the timely identification and management of deteriorating COVID-19 patients [34].

Finally, tachycardia (heart rate >100 bpm) has been identified as an independent predictor of critical COVID-19 illness. This finding is supported by consistent results from a systematic review and meta-analysis [35] and studies conducted in Wuhan, China [36], Niguarda Hospital, Italy [37], and University Hospital Dubrava in Zagreb, Croatia [38]. The link between elevated heart rate and critical illness can be attributed to the physiological stress response triggered by systemic inflammation, hypoxemia, and sepsis frequent complications in severe COVID-19 cases. Persistent tachycardia may indicate heightened sympathetic activation and cardiac strain, both of which have been associated with poor cardiovascular outcomes in hospitalized COVID-19 patients.

Overview of home quarantine in COVID-19

Home quarantine protocols emphasize self-isolation, ventilation, hygiene/disinfection, and symptom self-management to prevent transmission and ease health-system burden. These guidance frameworks presume patients remain at home unless they worsen, which in practice may contribute to delayed care-seeking and enrich hospital cohorts for more severe disease. In our setting, such dynamics could introduce selection bias, with milder community cases underrepresented and the proportion of critical pneumonia among admissions overestimated. Notably, the guidance also notes shorter disease courses among vaccinated individuals and more complicated courses in older/comorbid patients—factors that may further shape who reaches hospital care [39].

Overview of vaccination in COVID-19

COVID-19 vaccination reduces severe outcomes and hospitalization risk, though effectiveness has varied by variant—e.g., BNT162b2/ChAdOx1 reduced hospitalizations with Alpha but showed lower effectiveness with Delta—while vaccinated patients tend to experience shorter disease courses. In our analysis, unmeasured vaccination status could confound associations between candidate predictors and critical pneumonia (e.g., shifting the admitted case-mix toward unvaccinated or partially vaccinated individuals, and altering clinical/radiologic presentation in breakthroughs). Future work in our context should capture vaccine status and timing to adjust severity models appropriately [40].

Overview of viral lineage evolution in COVID-19

Comparative phylogenetic analyses of 74,075 genomes across continents show distinct variant clusters and continent-specific evolutionary patterns—even as Omicron lineages drove the global pandemic—indicating meaningful regional differences in mutation trajectories and lineage branching. Such shifts can plausibly influence clinical severity profiles and timing of diagnosis across study periods and sites, introducing time/variant confounding in severity estimates when variant data are unavailable [41].

Overview of clinically challenging or atypical COVID-19 cases

COVID-19 exhibits marked phenotypic heterogeneity, including multi-organ involvement and organ damage that may occur even without prominent respiratory symptoms; immune dysregulation and post-acute autoimmune phenomena further broaden presentations. This spectrum—ranging from cardiovascular and neurologic injury to immune and endothelial dysfunction—raises the risk of misclassification at the boundaries of moderate vs. critical pneumonia, especially when atypical or extra-pulmonary features predominate. Recognizing this heterogeneity is essential for refining severity definitions and interpretation [42–44].

Strengths and limitations

This study provides valuable context-specific evidence from a major COVID-19 referral center in Rwanda, addressing a critical gap in data on severe and critical illness in low-resource settings. The combined retrospective and prospective design enhanced data completeness and accuracy, while standardized national definitions ensured consistent classification of disease severity. Systematic random sampling improved representativeness and reduced selection bias. However, the study was limited to a single urban center, which may affect generalizability to rural or lower-tier facilities. Long-term outcomes after discharge were not assessed, and comorbidities such as HIV, tuberculosis and malnutrition, which are common in the region, were not evaluated despite their potential impact on disease progression. Retrospective data collection posed a risk of missing or incomplete information, though cross-validation with electronic records was performed. Despite these limitations, the study offers important insights to guide clinical care and health planning in similar resource-constrained settings.

Conclusion and recommendations

The study highlights key predictors of severe and critical COVID-19 pneumonia, including oxygen saturation ≤ 90%, respiratory rate > 20 breaths per minute, and tachycardia (HR > 100 bpm), essential for early detection and risk stratification. These clinical indicators support timely interventions such as oxygen therapy, close monitoring, and escalation of care. The importance of vital sign monitoring in routine triage, especially in resource-limited settings, is emphasized for prioritizing care.

Policy recommendations include implementing standardized triage protocols and developing national guidelines for early identification of respiratory distress. In clinical practice, there is a need to train healthcare workers on recognizing severe symptoms and ensuring routine monitoring of key clinical indicators. For resource allocation, prioritizing oxygen supplies, ICU beds, and ventilators, while expanding ICU capacity in referral hospitals, is critical. Further research should focus on conducting multicenter studies and exploring long-term outcomes of critical COVID-19 pneumonia patients. By implementing these strategies, healthcare systems in resource-limited settings, particularly in Rwanda, can enhance early detection, improve patient outcomes, and strengthen pandemic preparedness.
