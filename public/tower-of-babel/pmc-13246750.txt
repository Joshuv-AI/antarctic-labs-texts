Sex-specific increased reactivity of the PVT and prolonged PVT→CeA circuit engagement following psilocin administration
Authors: D P Effinger, J L Hoffman, S G Quadir, C S Rollison, D Toedt, M Echeveste Sanchez, M W High, C W Hodge, M A Herman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe psychedelic psilocybin has shown therapeutic potential, yet underlying neural mechanisms remain poorly understood. We investigated the impact of psilocin—the active metabolite of psilocybin—on basal activity and reactivity within the paraventricular nucleus of the thalamus (PVT) and PVT projections to central amygdala (CeA) in rats. Psilocin administration increased PVT c-Fos expression and selectively engaged PVT→CeA neurons in females, but not males. Psilocin enhanced PVT reactivity to an aversive air-puff stimulus, with effects primarily driven by passive responders. In PVT→CeA neurons, psilocin prevented time-dependent reductions in stimulus-evoked activity and maintained reactivity across timepoints in females but not males. The sustained engagement of PVT→CeA circuitry was driven by active responders. These findings identify sex-specific modulation of thalamic-limbic circuitry and behavior by psilocin, implicating PVT→CeA circuitry in the neural and behavioral effects of psychedelic compounds, advancing our understanding of how psychedelics modulate emotional brain circuits to further inform potential therapeutic mechanisms.Subject terms: Neural circuits, Stress and resilience

Introduction

Psychedelic compounds such as psilocybin, and it’s active metabolite psilocin1,2, have been shown to produce rapid-acting and persistent improvements in symptomology for a variety of different psychiatric disorders3–13. Despite observed clinical outcomes, little is known in terms of the neurobiological alterations underlying these persistent effects. Clinical imaging studies suggest that psilocybin promotes alterations in neural functional connectivity14–22, however less is known about the circuit-specific mechanisms underlying large-scale alterations in function.

The thalamus is a small structure within the diencephalon responsible for integrating signals from various brain regions within the central nervous system. Traditionally, the thalamus has been viewed as a passive relay station for sensory information to cortical regions, but has recently emerged as an integration hub for functional brain networks23. The thalamus has inputs from nearly all regions of the brain and outputs to regions critical for affective processes including cortex, striatum, and amygdala24–26. Though often thought of as a single brain region, the thalamus is comprised of several functionally distinct subnuclei27. The paraventricular nucleus of the thalamus (PVT) is a midline thalamic nucleus heavily involved in appetitive and aversive behaviors and is considered an integral hub within the limbic system governing emotional processing28,29 and goal directed actions to maintain homeostatic balance30. The PVT sends glutamatergic and peptidergic projections to various limbic regions31,32, including dense projections to the nucleus accumbens33,34, and to a lesser degree to the BNST34, central nucleus of the amygdala34,35, prefrontal cortex36, and hypothalamus32. The PVT has been shown to regulate threat responding, with projections to the central amygdala (CeA)37, specifically the lateral capsule of the CeA, which has been shown to regulate freezing behavior and immediate anxiogenic effects in conditioned fear assays38–40. Additionally, lesions of the PVT result in increased stress-induced c-Fos expression within CeA41, suggesting that the PVT may act as an upstream modulator of CeA reactivity to stressful stimuli.

Administration of the serotonergic psychedelic, lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) increases functional connectivity within the thalamus and promotes connectivity changes between the thalamus and sensory networks and striatum42. Previous work showed that administration of psilocin, the active metabolite of psilocybin, promoted acute increases in reactivity to an aversive air-puff in the central amygdala (CeA) in females but not males43. Given what is known about projections from PVT→CeA and their involvement in fear responding, the PVT may serve as an upstream mediator of previously observed effects. Recent studies have shown that psilocybin administration promotes increases in PVT c-Fos expression in mice44,45 and that chronic, low-dose administration of psilocybin strengthened cortical connections to the PVT46. Together these studies highlight the PVT as a critical region implicated in acute psychedelic drug action, however how psychedelics alter in-vivo functioning of the PVT remains poorly understood.

In the present study, we utilized in-vivo fiber photometry to assess the effects of psilocin administration on functional reactivity within the PVT and PVT→CeA circuitry. We found that psilocin administration produced increases in c-Fos expression in the PVT of females, but not males. We also found that psilocin produced acute increases in PVT reactivity in response to an aversive air-puff that persisted for 2 days following injection and was driven by passive responding females. Next, we utilized retrograde tracing and found increased c-Fos expression in PVT→CeA neurons following psilocin administration in females, but not males. Finally, using an intersectional viral approach, we found that psilocin administration reduced a time-dependent dampening of reactivity in PVT→CeA projections in females but not males and that this effect was driven by females employing active threat responding at baseline.

Results

The effects of psilocin administration on basal activity within the PVT

To assess the acute effects of psilocin administration on basal PVT activation, male and female Sprague Dawley rats received either vehicle or psilocin (2 mg/kg, s.c.) injection. Animals were then perfused 2hrs post injection for histological analysis. Changes in c-Fos expression were examined between groups. In males, there were no differences between the vehicle (mean: 101.3, SD: 15.4) and psilocin (mean: 119.4, SD: 71) treated animals (Fig. 1A). However, in females there was significantly higher cFos expression in psilocin (mean: 136.4, SD: 36.12) treated animals compared to vehicle control (mean: 59.92, SD: 30.08) group (t(8) = 3.636, p = 0.006; Fig. 1A). Interestingly, there were differences seen between vehicle treated males (mean: 101.3, SD: 15.4) and females (mean: 59.92, SD: 30.08) suggesting there may be sex differences in basal activation within the PVT (t(8) = 2.740, p = 0.02; Fig. 1B).

The effects of psilocin administration on PVT reactivity to an aversive air-puff stimulus

To assess the effects of psilocin on in-vivo PVT activity, we utilized jGCaMP7f-AAV9 to target neurons within the PVT utilizing an aversive air-puff stimulus in conjunction with behavioral fiber photometry (FP) assay to capture acute and prolonged changes in PVT reactivity (Fig. 1C). In vehicle-treated females, there were no significant differences in PVT reactivity traces, area under the curve (AUC), or peak point (PP) between baseline and injection. Additionally, no differences were seen in average max speed or time immobile following air-puff (Fig. 2A). In psilocin-treated females, there was a significant increase in reactivity following psilocin administration (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(2.860,14.30) = 4.106, p = 0.0284; Finjection(1.000, 5.000) = 11.97, p = 0.0180; Ftime(1.606, 8.030) = 19.45, p = 0.0011; Fig. 2B) with a significant increase in area under the curve (t(5) = 3.192, p = 0.0242; Fig. 2B) and peak point (t(5) = 3.574, p = 0.0160; Fig. 2B). The increase in PVT reactivity was associated with a decrease in max speed (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(9,117) = 5.212, p < 0.0001; Finjection(1, 13) = 5.181, p = 0.0404; Ftime(9,117) = 22.17, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2B) and an increase in time immobile in the first 3 seconds following air-puff (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(9,117) = 2.377, p = 0.0166; Finjection(1, 13) = 1.832, p = 0.1989; Ftime(9,117) = 11.79, p < 0.0001; Fig. 2B).

Next, we tested animals at 2- and 14-days post-injection to assess prolonged effects of psilocin administration on PVT reactivity in response to air-puff. At 2-days, there were no differences between baseline and the 2-day recording in PVT reactivity, max speed, or time immobile following air-puff in vehicle animals (Fig. 2C). In the psilocin treated animals, reactivity at 2-days remained elevated compared to baseline (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(2.325,11.62) = 2.063, p = 0.1677; Fsession(1.000, 5.000) = 9.546, p = 0.0272; Ftime(1.772,8.859) = 13.38, p = 0.0025; Fig. 2D). No differences were seen in max speed or time immobile following air-puff (Fig. 2D). At the 14-day follow up, there were no differences seen in vehicle treated animals for PVT reactivity, max speed, or time immobile following air-puff (Fig. 2E). In the psilocin group, signal was restored to baseline levels at the 14-day follow up, with no differences in max speed or time immobile (Fig. 2F). To determine if there were differences in PVT activity between vehicle and psilocin treated groups, a between groups comparison of calcium dynamics AUC across time was conducted. There were no significant differences between vehicle and psilocin groups across time (Fig. 2G). All together, these data suggest that psilocin administration in females produced increased basal activation of the PVT as well as acute increases in reactivity to the air-puff stimulus that persisted 2 days following administration but returned to baseline levels by 14 days.

Active vs. passive responding

Based on our previous findings demonstrating that baseline threat responding predicted drug sensitivity43,47, we divided groups by baseline behavioral responding to the air-puff into active and passive responding groups. Active responders were defined as animals that employed evasive locomotor behavior in response to the air-puff and actively moved away from the quadrant where the air-puff occurred (Fig. 3A), while passive responders were defined as rats that did not display enhanced locomotor behavior or actively try to escape the air-puff (Fig. 3B). At baseline, animals from both vehicle and psilocin groups had a roughly equal proportion of animals that displayed the active responding phenotype. On the day of injection, active responding was reduced in psilocin treated animals (Fig. 3C), with a significant difference between vehicle and psilocin treated groups (Fisher’s exact test: p = 0.0256). On the 2-day follow up, active responding in the psilocin-treated animals was increased such that levels were restored to baseline and were higher than levels in vehicle treated animals, however there were no significant differences seen between groups at the 2- and 14-day follow up (Fig. 3C). In the vehicle active responders, the proportion of animals employing an active responding strategy decreased across recording sessions. In the psilocin treated animals, active responding behavior was completely blocked on the day of injection but recovered to baseline levels that were higher than vehicle at the 2-day follow up recording with no significant differences between groups at 2- or 14-day follow up (Fig. 3D). In the passive responders, vehicle treated animals showed an increase in active responding on the day of injection, an effect not seen in psilocin treated animals (Fig. 3D). There were no differences in baseline PVT reactivity to the air-puff between active and passive responders for both vehicle and psilocin groups (Fig. 3E).

Increases in PVT reactivity with psilocin are driven by passive responders

We next assessed the differential effects of psilocin treatment between active and passive responders. In the vehicle active responders, there were no differences in PVT reactivity between baseline and injection (Fig. 4A), however there was a significant interaction in average max speed post air-puff, with significantly lower speeds at 2-seconds compared to baseline (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(9,54) = 2.292, p = 0.0293; Fsession(1, 6) = 0.3380, p = 0.5821; Ftime(9,54) = 21.42, p < 0.0001; Fig. 4A). In the vehicle passive responders, there were also no differences in PVT reactivity between baseline and injection. For average max speed, there was an interaction between treatment and time, with a significant increase in speed at 3 s post air-puff compared to baseline (2-way ANOVA: 2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(9,54) = 3.202, p = 0.0036; Fsession(1, 6) = 3.761, p = 0.1005; Ftime(9,54) = 30.45, p < 0.0001; Fig. 4B), which could be due to injection stress. In the psilocin active responders, no differences were seen in PVT reactivity between baseline and injection, however, psilocin treated animals showed a significant increase in AUC compared to baseline (p = 0.0120). There was a reduction in max speed (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(9,63) = 7.357, p < 0.0001; Fsession(1, 7) = 6.749, p = 0.0355; Ftime(9,63) = 16.49, p < 0.0001; Fig. 4C) and time immobile (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(9, 63) = 4.272, p = 0.0002; Fsession(1, 7) = 4.406, p = 0.074; Ftime(9, 63) = 5.909, p < 0.0001; Fig. 4C), which could also be impacted by injection stress, although in a manner different to what was observed in passive responders, where activity was increased. In the psilocin passive responders, there was a significant increase in PVT reactivity between baseline and injection (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(1.844, 3.688) = 3.462, p = 0.1427; Ftreatment(1.000, 2.000) = 20.13, p = 0.0462; Ftime(1.290, 2.580) = 10.75, p = 0.0571), with a significant increase seen in AUC (t(2) = 4.487, p = 0.0462, Fig. 4D) and no changes in max speed or time immobile. To determine if there were differences in PVT activity between vehicle and psilocin treated active and passive responders, a between groups comparison of calcium dynamics as measured by AUC across time was conducted. There were no significant differences between groups in the active or passive responders (Fig. 4E, F).

The effects of psilocin administration on basal activity within PVT→CeA circuitry

Next, to determine how psilocin alters activity within the PVT→CeA circuit, we utilized intracranial injection of retrobeads in the CeA to specifically examine the effects of psilocin on PVT neurons that project to the CeA (Fig. 5A). In males, there were no differences in the proportion of retrobead/cFos+ PVT neurons between vehicle (mean: 29.3, SD: 3.85) and psilocin (mean: 31.32, SD: 6.03) groups (Fig. 5B, C). However, in the female psilocin (mean: 48.27, SD: 17.98) group there was a greater proportion of cFos + /retrobead+ PVT neurons compared to vehicle (mean: 23.47, SD: 2.79) control (t(7) = 2.697, p = 0.03; Fig. 5B, C), suggesting increased activity of PVT→CeA projections in females but not males.

The effects of psilocin administration on reactivity of PVT→CeA circuitry in females

Next, we utilized an intersectional viral strategy to investigate how psilocin administration alters reactivity within this specific population of PVT→CeA neurons. To do this, we performed injections into the CeA with a retrograde Cre-recombinase virus (pENN.AAV.hSyn.Cre.WPRE.hGH) followed by injection of a Cre-dependent GCaMP (AAV9-pGP-AAV-syn-FLEX-jGCaMP7s-WPRE) and fiber cannula installation in the PVT (Fig. 5D, E).

In the vehicle treated females, we found that PVT→CeA reactivity was significantly reduced on the day of injection compared to baseline (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(29, 290) = 3.907, p < 0.0001; Finjection(1, 10) = 7.252, p = 0.0226; Ftime(29, 290) = 6.657, p < 0.0001; Fig. 6A). Area under the curve (AUC) (t(10) = 2.725, p = 0.0214) and peak point (PP) (t(10) = 2.661, p = 0.0239) were both significantly reduced (Fig. 6A). Additionally, no differences were seen in max speed or time immobile following air-puff (Fig. 6A). In the psilocin group, no differences were seen in PVT reactivity between baseline and injection (Fig. 6B), suggesting that psilocin treatment prevented a reduction in reactivity seen in vehicle control animals. Interestingly, no changes were seen in average max speed or time immobile following air-puff (Fig. 6B). At the 2-day follow up, vehicle females showed a persistent reduction in PVT→CeA reactivity compared to baseline (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(29, 290) = 2.637, p < 0.0001; Fsession(1, 10) = 2.933, p = 0.1175; Ftime(29, 290) = 7.084, p < 0.0001; Fig. 6C). However, no significant reductions in AUC and PP were observed, though there was a trend for PP (p = 0.0585, Fig. 6C). Additionally, there were no changes in average max speed or time immobile. In the psilocin group, no differences were seen in PVT→CeA reactivity at the 2-day follow up (Fig. 6D), suggesting a persistent prevention of reduction in signal reactivity seen in vehicle control. At the 14-day follow up, the vehicle group showed a sustained reduction in PVT→CeA reactivity compared to baseline (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(29,290) = 5.480, p < 0.0001; Fsession(1, 10) = 8.027, p = 0.0178; Ftime(29,290) = 6.454, p < 0.0001; Fig. 6E). Significant reductions were also observed in AUC (t(10) = 2.837, p = 0.0178) and PP (t(10) = 2.910, p = 0.0156; Fig. 6E) with no differences in average max speed or time immobile. In the psilocin group, there was a significant decrease in reactivity compared to baseline (2-way ANOVA: Finteraction(29, 290) = 3.568, p < 0.0001; Fsession(1, 10) = 6.321, p = 0.0307; Ftime(29, 290) = 12.53, p < 0.0001; Fig. 6F). There were also significant reductions observed in AUC (t(10) = 3.331, p = 0.0076) and PP (p = 0.0217; Fig. 6F). However, no changes in average max speed or time immobile were observed. To determine if there were differences in PVT activity between vehicle and psilocin treated females, a between groups comparison of calcium dynamics as measured by AUC across time was conducted. There were no significant differences found between groups across time (Fig. 6G). All together, these data suggest that psilocin delays time-dependent reductions in PVT reactivity to repeated aversive air-puff.

Psilocin-induced sustained reactivity of PVT-CeA circuitry is driven by baseline active responders in females

To assess the impact of baseline threat responding on changes in reactivity within the PVT→CeA circuit, females were split into active and passive groups for subsequent analysis. At baseline, 60% of animals in the vehicle group exhibited an active response to the air-puff. Similarly, 54.5% of the psilocin group exhibited an active response. No differences were seen between groups in the proportion of active responding across time (Fig. 7A). This relatively even split allowed for animals to be separated into active and passive responding groups for further analysis. Notably, in the passive responding group, psilocin treatment may have delayed a shift towards active responding following air-puff (Fig. 7B), however there were no significant differences between groups at any of the time points. In the active responders, vehicle treated females appeared to show a desensitization to the air-puff, exhibited by a decrease in the proportion of animals exhibiting an active response across time. Psilocin treated females did not show this same trend and appeared to maintain their responding strategy across time. No significant differences were seen between groups across time (Fig. 7B).

In the active responders, at baseline there were no differences seen in PVT→CeA reactivity, average max speed, or time immobile between groups (Fig. 7C). Similarly, in the passive responders, there were no differences in reactivity, max speed, or time immobile (Fig. 7D). On the day of injection, vehicle treated active responders exhibited blunted PVT→CeA reactivity compared to psilocin treated animals (Finteraction(29,319) = 2.495, p < 0.0001; Finjection(1, 11) = 3.852, p = 0.0755; Ftime(29, 319) = 14.27, p < 0.0001; Fig. 7E). In vehicle treated males, we found that PVT→CeA reactivity was not changed between baseline and injection. No differences were seen in max speed or time immobile (Fig. 8A). Discussion

In the current study we utilized immunohistochemistry and in-vivo fiber photometry (FP) to investigate the effects of the psychedelic psilocin on basal activity and reactivity of the PVT and PVT→CeA circuitry. Reactivity to the previously developed air-puff stimulus was used to assess changes in calcium dynamics in the presence of drug and at more prolonged time points. We found that psilocin administration produced increases in c-Fos expression and proportion of c-Fos + /retrobead+ cells within the PVT of females, but not males. Using a standard GCaMP approach in females, we found that psilocin administration increased PVT reactivity to the air-puff stimulus that persisted at a 2-day follow up recording, an effect not seen in vehicle controls. When animals were analyzed based on basal threat responding to air-puff, we found that the increased PVT reactivity was driven by females employing a passive responding strategy. Next, using an intersectional Cre-recombinase/Cre-GCaMP viral strategy, we found that psilocin prevented a decrease in reactivity seen in vehicle control on day of injection and sustained PVT→CeA activation at the 2-day follow up compared to a significantly reduced reactivity seen in vehicle control in females but not males. Notably, when females were split into groups based on baseline responding strategy, the sustained activation seen on day of injection in the psilocin treated group was driven by females employing an active baseline responding strategy.

The PVT is a significant hub within the central nervous system responding to stress28–30,49. Dysregulation of the PVT has been implicated in behaviors relating to psychiatric disorders such as major depressive disorder (MDD)50,51 and substance use disorder52–54. A hallmark of these disorders is a compromised ability to make emotionally and logically sound decisions55–59, including dysfunctional emotional regulation and responses to stress60–62. The bi-directional connections between cortical neurons and thalamic nuclei is referred to as the thalamocortical system63, which involves complex and functionally specific connections between these two regions64, and is involved in many cognitive processes such as decision making65. Dysregulation of thalamocortical connectivity is associated with disorders such as MDD66,67. When exposed to a stressor, animals employ either an active or passive coping strategy. In certain circumstances, it is more advantageous to freeze (passive) or flee (active). It has been shown that cortico-thalamic circuitry governs the decision to engage in active vs. passive responding to a threat68. From the PVT, there are divergent circuits mediating active vs. passive threat responding, with projections to the CeA orchestrating a passive response and projections to the nucleus accumbens (NAc) promoting an active response37. Previous findings showed that changes in reactivity within the CeA were primarily driven by animals employing an active threat responding strategy43, suggesting the possibility that threat responding circuitry may be implicated in psychedelic drug action. Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging studies in humans have illuminated changes in thalamic glucose metabolism, suggestive of increased activation, following administration of serotonergic psychedelics, including psilocybin16,69–71. Additionally, recent work highlighted strengthened cortical connections to the PVT following repeated, low-dose psychedelic administration in rats46. We found that global PVT reactivity to an aversive air-puff was increased with psilocin injection in females, and that this occurred in parallel with reductions in locomotor activity in response to the air-puff. This may suggest that PVT activity correlates with or is causal to reduces in threat responding. However, when we separated animals into active and passive threat responders based on baseline responding, we found that the increased PVT reactivity occurred in the passive responders, while reductions in threat responding occurred in the active responders. This could be due to limits of detecting reductions in threat responding in passive responders (i.e., a floor effect), or it could suggest that threat responding is not governed by global PVT activity, but by specific cell populations or discrete circuits. Together, our work contributes to our knowledge on the acute effects of psychedelic drug administration on reactivity within this key thalamic nucleus.

Here, we showed that psilocin induced acute increases in basal activity in PVT of females, but not males and increased reactivity to air-puff in the PVT of psilocin-treated females. Notably, vehicle injected females had lower basal activation compared to vehicle males, which could have contributed to greater increases in females, however similar sex-specific increases with psilocin were observed in PVT→CeA neurons despite similar basal activation levels. We have previously reported region-specific differences in basal reactivity to the aversive puff stimulus as well as psilocin effects in male and female rats. In a previous study we found no sex differences in basal reactivity to air-puff in the central amygdala (CeA) but females showed greater active responding to air-puff at baseline and displayed greater increases in basal activity and air-puff reactivity after acute psilocin, while males showed reductions in reactivity at later time points43. In another study, we found that males showed greater basal reactivity to the air-puff in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVN) compared to females, and while there were no differences in basal activity with acute psilocin, males showed increased PVN reactivity that was primarily driven by active responders47. Previous work has shown sex-differences in threat responding behavior, with females primarily employing an active threat response48,72. This may suggest that females have heightened activation of the PVT→NAc pathway, rather than PVT→CeA circuitry37. A previous study found divergent effects of dopamine receptor2 (DRD2) PVT projections to CeA or NAc during fear conditioning with opposing results on active or passive conditioned fear responses. in contrast to PVTDRD2→NAc neurons, activity of PVTDRD2→CeA neurons was associated with reduced avoidance behavior and increased freezing and optogenetic inhibition of PVTDRD2→CeA neurons increased active avoidance behavior and reduced freezing, suggesting that of PVTDRD2→CeA circuitry drives passive threat responding behavior37. Another study identified that corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) PVT neurons and upstream projections from ventromedial hypothalamus are bidirectionally modulated during approach avoidance conflict to food or predator odor exposure73. It has also been shown that female rats show compromised CRF receptor internalization, leading to heightened sensitivity to CRF signaling in females compare to males74. These studies highlight the potential for population- or circuit-specific effects and the possibility that different local or projection populations in PVT may explain why psilocin has differential effects on PVT reactivity between males and females. Another example is the neuropeptide, pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP), a modulator of stress responding75,76. Studies looking at PACAP expression within the PVT of mice showed greater expression in females than males77. Recent work has also shown that PACAP modulates 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT)2AR signaling via internalization of receptors in cell culture, with cerebrospinal administration of PACAP suppressing 5-HT2AR agonist induced head twitch responses78, a behavioral output thought to be involved in psychedelic drug action in rodents79–81. Given potential sex differences in PACAP expression and involvement of PACAP in 5-HT2AR activity, it is possible that local differences in PVT could be responsible for observed sex-specific effects of the 5-HT2AR partial agonist psilocin. However, future studies are needed to identify the specific signaling mechanisms involved in these effects. Future work should examine the effects of 5-HT2AR agonism on PVT reactivity through the use antagonist pretreatment or genetic knockdown.

The current study provides insight into the effects of psilocin administration on reactivity of PVT→CeA circuitry. Here, we showed that psilocin administration increased basal activation of PVT→CeA projecting neurons in females but not males. Additionally, we showed that psilocin prevented a reduction in activation of PVT→CeA neurons seen in vehicle group in response to repeated air-puffs. Previous findings in the lab demonstrated an acute increase in CeA reactivity of females but not males43. While this suggests that PVT→CeA circuitry may be an upstream mediator of the observed CeA effects, it is not the only circuit involved in threat responding behavior. Future work is needed to explore psilocin effects on upstream and downstream PVT circuitry and that employs manipulation of different circuits to determine how psychedelics impact reactivity to an aversive air-puff stimulus.

All together, these data provide valuable insight into the effects of the partial 5-HT2AR agonist psychedelic psilocin on PVT basal activity and reactivity to an aversive stimulus. Given the relevance of implicit threat and stress responding in the development of psychiatric disorders, studying PVT circuitry may provide key insights into better understanding the etiology of psychiatric disorders and potentially uncovering previously unknown mechanisms of action for possible therapeutic effects of psychedelics.

Methods

Animals

All experiments utilized male and female Sprague Dawley rats (200-400 g, ~8-weeks old at arrival, Envigo, Indianapolis, IN) that were group-housed prior to surgery followed by individual housing with nestlet enrichment in a humidity- and temperature-controlled (22 °C) vivarium on a 12 hr light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water.

Stereotaxic surgery

At ~9-weeks, male and female rats were anesthetized with isoflurane/oxygen (5% induction 2-3% maintenance), placed in a (KOPF) stereotaxic frame and intracranial surgery was performed using standard sterilization procedures according to approved protocols. For standard fiber photometry cohorts, rats were injected with pGP-AAV-syn-jGCaMP7f-WPRE; Addgene: 104488-AAV9) into the PVT (AP:−3.4 mm, ML:+/−0 mm, DV:−4.9 mm). For intersectional fiber photometry cohorts, 250 nl of retrograde-Cre recombinase (pENN.AAV.hSyn.Cre.WPRE.hGH; Addgene: 105553-AAVrg) was infused in the CeA (AP: −2 mm, ML: +/−3.9 mm, DV: −8 mm) at a rate of 100 nl/min using a 2.0 µL Hamilton Syringe (2.0Neuros Syringe, Model 7002 KH, 30 gauge). Immediately following CeA injections, a single injection of 400 nl of Cre-dependent GCaMP (AAV9-pGP-AAV-syn-FLEX-jGCaMP7s-WPRE; Addgene: 104491-AAV9) was injected into the PVT (AP: −3.4 mm, ML: +/−0 mm, DV: −5.2 mm). For all microinjections syringe was left in place for 5 minutes prior to removal. Following infusion, a mono-fiber cannula (Doric: MFC_200/230-0.37_6.5mm_ZF1.25_FLT) was installed at the PVT, 0.3 mm above injection site at coordinates (AP: −3.4 mm, ML: +/−0 mm, DV: −4.9 mm). Three screws were placed into the skull followed by application of Metabond dental cement to create a headcap and stabilize fiber implants. Animals were treated with 2.5 mg/kg Flunixin on the day of surgery and for two days following. All procedures were approved by the University of North Carolina Chapel Hill’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

Drug administration

Psilocin (Cayman Chemical) was dissolved in 2% glacial acetic acid and brought up to a 1 mg/ml dilution in 0.9% saline. On the day of injection animals received subcutaneous (s.c.) injections of either vehicle (2% glacial acetic acid in 0.9% saline) or psilocin (2 mg/kg) and were returned to their home cage for 30 min before being placed into open field box for a 10 min habituation prior to air-puff administration. For the c-Fos cohort, animals received injections 2 h prior to perfusion and collection of brain tissue for immunohistochemical analysis.

Retrograde tracing

At ~8-weeks of age, male and female Sprague Dawley rats were anesthetized with isoflurane/oxygen (5% induction 2-3% maintenance), placed in a stereotaxic frame (KOPF) and intracranial surgery was performed using standard sterilization procedures according to approved protocols. Rats received intracranial injections (300nL) of red retrograde beads (Lumafluor Inc.) into the CeA (AP: −2 mm, ML: +/−3.9 mm, DV: −8 mm). Seven days following intracranial retrobead surgery, animals were administered either vehicle or psilocin (2 mg/kg, s.c.) depending on group. Animals were then perfused 2 hr post injection and brains were collected for tissue processing.

Fiber photometry

To look for changes in PVT reactivity following psilocin administration, an air-puff stimulus was used in conjunction with in-vivo behavioral fiber photometry (FP). Baseline recordings were used to demonstrate changes in fluorescence (ΔF/F) prior to drug administration. Animals underwent FP recordings at ~12 weeks of age. All FP recordings were collected with the TDT RZ5 (Tucker-Davis Technologies, Alachua, FL) real time processor and Doric external components. Fiber implants were silica mono fiber cannula (Doric: MFC_200/230-0.37_6.5mm_ZF1.25_FLT) with a numerical aperture of 0.37 NA. Light output at the tip of the recording cable was confirmed and standardized to 50 μW on each recording day. Cohorts were split into two subgroups ran consecutively in order to control for time of day within each group and across subjects. On the day of recording, animals were habituated to the room for 1 hr after which they were placed in chambers for 10 min habituation and baseline recording. To test for acute effects, animals were given an injection of either vehicle or psilocin (2 mg/kg, s.c.) and placed in home cage for 20 min prior to 10 min habituation for a total of 30 min prior to the beginning of the air-puff session. Follow-up recordings were collected at 2- and 14-days post administration.

Air-puff stimulus

An air-puff stimulus was performed using house air supply under the control of a regulator to administer a short (500 ms) burst of 85 psi air by a Parker solenoid (Part #003-0868-900), powered by a custom 12 V electrical circuit box connected to the TDT RZ5 system to trigger solenoid opening and closing simultaneous to fiber recording timestamps through the Synapse program. Each session consisted of a 10 min habituation baseline period before the delivery of two air-puff administrations with a 5 min inter-stimulus interval (ISI). Air-puffs were delivered by positioning of silicone tubing connected to a metal rod lowered into the behavioral chamber from above and directed at the face of the animal. Tubing and rod were retracted and positioned above the behavioral chamber during baseline and between stimulus deliveries. Baseline fluorescence was calculated through a least-squares linear fitting of the 405 nm isosbestic control to the 465 nm GCaMP signal to create a resting GCaMP signal over the entire session not containing event-dependent fluctuations in GCaMP signal. Peri-event plots were then generated by averaging changes in fluorescence (ΔF/F) across two different air-puff trials to generate a mean change in reactivity. A minimum of two air-puffs were collected for each subject and averages were combined into corresponding groups to compare group means.

Locomotor behavior

Locomotor activity was assessed in a clear, plexiglass open field apparatus (50 cm × 50 cm) which was located within a sound dampened activity cabinet. Behavioral sessions were recorded and analyzed with ANY-maze video tracking system (Stoelting Co, IL, USA). Behavioral measurements were collected within two time periods: a 10-minute habituation prior to the onset of the air puff and then a 5-minute ISI immediately following exposure to the air puff. Analysis across time were calculated at 1 min resolution for the overall locomotor behaviors, and additionally at 1 s for the immediate post puff behavioral analysis to correspond with fiber photometry time points. Measures of locomotor activity included distance traveled (m), max speed (m/s), % time spent in the center zone, and time immobile (at 65% sensitivity, animal must remain immobile for at least 2 s). The center zone was defined within the tracking software ( ~ 7.6 cm × 7.6 cm; 45% of total area), and the time spent variable was calculated as (time spent in center zone/(total time spent in center + time spent in periphery)) × 100).

Perfusion

Transcardial perfusions were conducted in both retrograde tracing and fiber photometry experiments to prepare tissue for IHC processing. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane until rendered insensitive to toe-pinch. Subsequently, an incision was made in ventricle of the heart and the circulatory system was flushed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) followed by ~120 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (w/v). Brains were stored for 24 h in 4% paraformaldehyde (w/v) and then transferred to a 30% sucrose solution for storage until sectioning.

Immunohistochemistry

To measure changes in PVT activation, 40μm sections (AP coordinates −2.9 mm to −3.6 mm) were collected and prepared for immunohistochemistry (IHC) to stain for the protein c-Fos. Brain slices were rinsed in PBS, treated with a 50% methanol/PBS solution for 30 min, and exposed to 3% hydrogen peroxide for 5 min. After a blocking step with 0.3% Triton X-100 and 1% BSA at room temperature for 1 hr, slices were incubated with rabbit anti-cFos (1:3000, Millipore) at 4 °C for 24hrs. Following washes in TNT buffer (0.1% Tween-20 in TBS), slices were blocked with TNB buffer for 30 min, then incubated with goat anti-rabbit HRP (1:200, Abcam #ab6721) for 2hrs. Fluorescent labeling was achieved using tyramide-conjugated fluorescein (1:50, Akoya) in amplification diluent for 10 min at room temperature. Slices were washed, mounted with DAPI-containing antifade medium, and stored at 4 °C. Imaging was performed using a Keyence BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope. Imaging and quantification were performed by experimenters that were blind to the condition and sex of each animal. Images were taken at 20× magnification and stitched together using the BZ-X800 Analyzer program. Regions of interest were outlined and cell quantification was performed by manual counting using the FIJI multipoint counter tool.

Statistics and reproducibility

Fiber photometry signal collection by TDT RZ5 processor and TDT Synapse program and subsequent data analysis were performed with 500 ms time bins using Matlab (Version 2023a, The Mathworks, Inc.) and a custom Matlab script (10.5061/dryad.3ffbg79qr)43. For all comparisons of c-Fos/retrobead data, unpaired t-tests were used. For all comparisons between baseline and injection/follow-up session FP recordings, 2-way ANOVAs were used with Sidak multiple comparisons for any post-hoc analysis done following discovery of interaction. AUC and PP in control and experimental conditions were compared using paired t-tests. In all cases, p < 0.05 was the threshold for statistical significance and relative significance is reported as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. All data were graphed and statistically analyzed with Prism 10.0.3 (GraphPad Software). Subjects were excluded from data analysis if site verification revealed incorrect injection location and/or fiber placement outside of the PVT. Sample sizes for all experimental groups are listed within figure legends. Experimenters were blinded during manual cell counting and manual behavioral scoring. The experimenter was not blinded to condition during experimentation or during data analysis as these were all automated processes measuring changes in physiology and, therefore, did not involve any subjective interpretation during data collection. Sample sizes were determined based on previous work43.
