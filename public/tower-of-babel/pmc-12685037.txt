Woodlands of Antiquity: A millennium of dendrochronological data on forest exploitation and timber economy between the Alps and the Atlantic
Authors: Bernhard Muigg, Kristof Haneca, Willy Tegel, Martin Grünewald, Vincent Bernard, Niels Bleicher, François Blondel, Matthias Bolliger, Manuel Broich, Yann Couturier, Sjoerd van Daalen, Marta Domínguez-Delmás, Olivier Girardclos, Franz Herzig, Fabien Langenegger, Yannick Le Digol, Oliver Nelle, Kurt Nicolussi, Monika Oberhänsli, Christophe Perrault, Andreas Rzepecki, Yardeni Vorst, Julia Weidemüller, Thorsten Westphal, Andrea Seim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis study presents empirical evidence of the deforestation driven by the Roman Empire north of the Alps and offers a unique, robust, and high-resolution dataset to explore woodland exploitation across a full millennium. By analyzing timber, for centuries one of the primary construction materials, we were able to investigate forest history of the Roman period and compare it to times before and after the Roman occupation. The compiled dendrochronological dataset of 20397 absolutely dated woods bridges archaeological and environmental research by linking material culture with land-use change and ecological impact. This dataset deepens our understanding of the environmental and economic consequences of Roman imperial expansion, providing critical insights into the long-term interplay between human activity and forest dynamics in European Antiquity.

In the 1st century BCE, the Romans expanded north of the Alps, profoundly shaping societal organization, steering technological advancements, and influencing intellectual traditions in large parts of western and central Europe for more than half a millennium (ca. 1st century BCE–5th century CE). They introduced numerous innovations and socioeconomic advancements, including administrative and literary writing and groundbreaking developments in engineering, architecture, and horticulture (1–3). They brought new food plants to their northwestern provinces, including wine (Vitis vinifera subsp. vinifera), sweet chestnut (Castanea sativa), walnut (Juglans regia), and several fruit trees (e.g. Prunus persica) (3–6). Above all, the Roman presence marked an unprecedented anthropogenic transformation of landscapes, leaving enduring ecological and environmental imprints (7–9).

Despite the sophistication of Roman administration (10, 11), a central question remains: how did the ancient state apparatus manage the continuous demand for the fundamental resource of wood (for architecture, crafts, and fuel in domestic and public life) over centuries of population growth, increasing urban expansion, and military investments? Wood was indispensable to nearly every aspect of daily life. Yet the impact of logging activities to sustain and secure wood provision on the woodlands is only scarcely documented in written records (12, 13), limiting our current understanding of timber resource management in the Roman Era. This is particularly evident for the northwestern provinces, where the propagandistic depictions of Caesar (100–44 BCE) and Tacitus (ca. 58–120 CE) have molded the stereotypical image of dark and dense forests, which was mainly used to exaggerate military success or failure, but shaped perceptions of woodlands in Antiquity north of the Alps, even until modern times (14).

Given sociological factors such as demographic development (15, 16) and urbanization (17) during the Roman period, it is reasonable to assume geographically and temporally differentiated (over)exploitation of forest resources. (18) However, direct evidence of such practices is rare (19–21). In this context, woods from archaeological sites precisely dated through tree-ring analysis provide an invaluable proxy for reconstructing historical patterns of wood use, woodland exploitation, and timber procurement strategies (including long-distance transport) (22–24).

In this paper, we examine the intensity of and drivers behind woodland exploitation for timber, and the long-lasting impact on forest vegetation in the northwestern provinces of the Roman Empire (Fig. 1). Our consortium of dendroarchaeological laboratories assembled a unique dataset comprising over 20,000 tree-ring dated timbers from areas formerly occupied or influenced by the Roman Empire in present-day France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Switzerland, Germany, and Austria (SI Appendix, Table S1). We have divided the dataset into seven regional groups (Fig. 1A and Material and Methods), adhering to the Roman province structure at the time of Marcus Aurelius (121–180 CE), to assess spatiotemporal variation in wood utilization and resource availability. Our study spans the period 300 BCE to 700 CE to a) cover the full duration of Roman presence north and west of the Alps while b) including approximately two centuries before and after the Roman political, economic, and social dominance in the study area to illuminate the conditions prior to the Roman arrival and the developments during the transition from Late Antiquity to the Early Middle Ages.

Results

Tree Species Composition.

The dendroarchaeological dataset, including only absolutely dated woods, mainly from architectural elements, covers 15 taxa (SI Appendix, Table S2), and is dominated by angiosperm tree species (88.7%) when compared to gymnosperms (11.3%). For further spatiotemporal analysis, we focus only on the five most frequently represented (>100 occurrences) taxa: oak (Quercus spp., anatomically not distinguishable to the species level), beech (Fagus sylvatica), fir (Abies alba), spruce (Picea abies), and pine (Pinus sylvestris). Oak dominates the dataset, accounting for 86.5% of all samples (n = 17,638; Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1), indicating that it was the most used tree species north of the Alps. Beech, also an important angiosperm used in constructions, is represented by only 275 occurrences (1.3%; cf. SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The general dominance of oak indicates deliberate anthropogenic selection for timber (23–25), but does not allow to draw more detailed conclusions regarding spatial or temporal patterns in wood species selection for producing specific objects or feature types. Therefore, a comparison of species selection focused only on the more diverse group of gymnosperms.

Among them, fir is the most frequent species with 1709 samples (8.4%), followed by spruce (393 samples = 1.9%) and pine (196 samples = 1.0%). The majority (>90%) of conifer samples date before 250 CE (Fig. 2A), suggesting a sharp decline in their use for various purposes (Fig. 2B) during the 3rd century CE. Conifer provenance is restricted to their areas of natural distribution (Fig. 2D). The use of species outside their core regions implies deliberate transport and trade. After 240 CE, there is no evidence for the use of spruce outside its natural distribution. Similarly, the last archaeological find of fir timber outside its core region is observed in the 240 s (e.g., 240/241 in Metz-Grigy, Gallia Belgica) (26). In later periods, the importance of fir diminishes. The role of pine as construction timber, particularly for posts and boards, increases relatively among the conifer species after 350 CE (Fig. 2C). Spatial analysis reveals that the use of pine is mostly confined to northern Raetia (region SE). Chronologically, the Roman use of pine is largely restricted to the 1st century CE, but reemerges in post-Roman contexts (Fig. 2A). Some isolated use of pines in southern Germania Inferior, recorded from Cologne and a villa rustica of Kerpen-Manheim, are limited to the 1st and 2nd centuries CE, with the wood most probably transported to these locations (cf. Fig. 2D).

Functional Selection of Wood as Economic Indicators.

Conifer utilization evinces species-specific selection, such as for the production of stave vessels (n = 749, Fig. 2B) like barrels (27). Fir was used in 68.8% of these cases, followed by spruce (18.3%), and only 12.9% oak. Barrels were used as transport containers for various goods and were often traded outside their place of production. Furthermore, they were frequently repurposed as linings for water wells or cesspits. The disappearance of fir and spruce in the dataset after 240 CE might reflect a supraregional decline in long-distance trade in the northern provinces. As most barrel staves have no waney edge (i.e. last ring before felling), only an earliest possible felling date (a so-called terminus post quem date) can be estimated. Therefore, a certain time lag between tree felling and barrel construction must be considered, situating the disappearance of barrels broadly into the mid-3rd century. The decline of road maintenance on a section of the Via Claudia Augusta in the Northern Alps around the same time supports the interpretation of a decrease in long-range commercial activity (28). The reduced use of fir for timber and the diminished importance of spruce after 250 CE might further indicate regional shortages of coniferous timber (18).

Tree Age, Forest Stand Dynamics, and Forest Exploitation.

To assess the impact of forest exploitation on tree and stand age, individual tree-ring segment length (i.e., the number of years between the first year and the end year for each sample) was determined. Since dendrochronological samples do not always reflect the full tree age due to a) rings missing toward the bark and/or center of the tree (so-called pith) and b) an unknown stem height sampled (while the number of tree rings varies along the tree), we use the segment length as the minimal tree age (mTA). Those mTA vary among the tree species/taxa, reaching a maximum of 206 (pine), 245 (spruce), 299 (fir), 313 (beech), and 428 (oak) years.

We defined seven regional groups (Fig. 1A and Material and Methods), determined primarily by provincial borders at 161 CE. To ensure sufficient sample replication, smaller provincial datasets were combined. The region SW mainly consists of data from the provinces of Gallia Narbonensis and Gallia Aquitania, while the region SE includes Raetia and parts of Noricum (SI Appendix, Table S2). Results are only displayed for the four best-replicated regional groups SE, Gallia Belgica, Germania Inferior, and Germania Superior (Fig. 3). Marked variation in mTA across the entire study period reveals regional differences (SI Appendix, Supplementary Material S1 and Fig. S3), and several supraregional phenomena, such as a) generally increasing tree ages with the onset of Roman occupation (mostly mid to late 1st century BCE), probably due to the exploitation of previously unused woodlands, b) decreasing tree ages toward the end of the 2nd century CE, although the timing of the onset differs slightly between regions (abrupt around 200 CE in region SE and less pronounced in Germania Inferior from ca. 150 CE in contrast to slow decreases in Germania Superior and Gallia Belgica from ca. 150 CE and 200 CE, respectively), and c) post-Roman natural reforestation, expressed by increasing mTA after ca. 500 CE. The latter is more apparent in the regions SE, Gallia Belgica, Germania Inferior, and Germania Magna, compared to the Germania Superior, Gallia Lugdunensis, and the region SW (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The SE region stands out as the only region exhibiting signs of considerable early medieval forest exploitation, reflected in decreasing mean tree ages (from ca. 120 to 70 y) during the 7th century.

We defined trees exceeding double the dataset’s mean mTA (99.5 y) as “very old trees” (VOT). Such samples with mTA of 200+ y are expected to represent old-grown forest. VOTs are observed until ca. 200 CE (Fig. 3, cf. SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The marked decline of VOTs during the 3rd century suggests overexploitation or the degeneration of old-grown forests. Some areas, like the Black Forest, were not part of the Roman Empire anymore after the second half of the 3rd century, which might explain certain regional differences. The 4th century sees a phase of secondary woodland growth, with almost all felled trees aged below 200 y, and most even under 100 y. The general lack of data for the 5th century makes it difficult to establish the further chronological development of supraregional woodland succession. However, indirect evidence is provided by the reemergence of VOT from the 6th century onward (Fig. 3).

Archaeological Context and Construction Types.

The spatiotemporal occurrence of dendrochronological data reflects general patterns of woodland exploitation. However, disentangling the underlying drivers of these patterns requires integration with the archaeological context. In doing so, we used the data, for which the archaeological information and dendrochronological records have been provided (Material and Methods) and examined three specific categories: transport infrastructure (bridges (51.7%), roads/trackways (25.7%), wharfs (22.4%), and wheels (0.2%)), watercrafts (including ships (96.1%) and log boats (3.9%)) and the general distinction between fortified/military (11.3%) and civil/nonmilitary (88.7%) constructions—which can reveal the economic and social hierarchy fueling the demand for timber, and thereby steering the use of ancient woodland.

Transport Infrastructure.

Timing and intensity of investment in transport infrastructure varied across Roman provinces (Fig. 4A). Early, pre-Roman timbers related to infrastructure are recorded for Gallia Lugdunensis in the 3rd and 2nd centuries BCE (29). In Germania Superior and Gallia Belgica, such archaeological features were also observed, dating to the 2nd century BCE. Germania Inferior and Gallia Belgica display a steady transition into the Roman period during the 1st century BCE, while Germania Superior and the region SE show increased investments in transport infrastructure, recognizable by the accumulation of end years during the early and mid-1st century CE, respectively (Fig. 4A). The rise in transport infrastructure in the first century CE (37.7 % of the overall evidence) is most probably associated with Roman occupation. By mid-2nd century CE, investments in new infrastructure declined across most regions, and remained low thereafter (21.0% during the 2nd and 7.2% in the 3rd centuries). Only four regional groups (Gallia Belgica, Germania Inferior, Germania Superior, and the region SE) record any activities in the 4th century (still 10.1%), and evidence disappears almost completely (0.2%) in the 5th century CE. Post-Roman investments in transport infrastructure reappear in Germania Inferior, Germania Superior, and the region SE from the late 6th to 7th centuries (together 2.3%).

Watercraft.

Tree-ring dated archaeological ship timbers and log boats appear later than infrastructure, but show significant regional differences (Fig. 4B). Notably, regional allocation is limited with such mobile objects (cf. barrels). In Germania Inferior, the most data-rich region, an abrupt end of shipbuilding activity is observed in the early 3rd century CE (30). Simultaneously, the number of ship-related finds in Germania Superior also diminishes significantly, with only a few occurrences in the 4th century and one isolated date in the 5th century. The scarce ship data from Gallia Belgica and Gallia Lugdunensis cease around 200 CE as well. Evidence for the region SE is restricted to the 1st and early 2nd centuries CE. The region SW shows a different pattern with ship data clustering in the 4th and 7th centuries. (31) However, a supraregional decline becomes evident by the mid-3rd century, as 78.7% of watercraft-related timbers date before 250 CE (Fig. 4B).

Civil vs. Fortified/Military Constructions.

Dendrochronologically dated fortified structures are nearly absent prior to Roman conquest but rise sharply with Roman presence, although notable differences across regions emerge (SI Appendix, Supplementary material S1 and Fig. S5). For the pre-Roman period, dendrochronological evidence for civilian contexts is more abundant. Across all provinces, both categories show a pronounced decline between 200 and 250 CE. While archaeological timbers from civil contexts remain very scarce thereafter, military infrastructure works resurge around 370 CE, likely reflecting fortification efforts under Emperor Valentinian (321–375 CE) (32). However, this late phase of military constructions is mainly restricted to Germania Superior.

Discussion

Data Composition and Possible Biases.

Archaeological wood represents tangible, material evidence of timber use, enabling precise chronological and geographical comparisons. Therefore, data derived from dendroarchaeological records can be regarded as unbiased by propaganda or other possible politically motivated influences, in contrast to written records, which often exhibit a strong imperiocentric bias due to their predominantly Roman authorship. Therefore, dendrochronological datasets offer a more objective proxy of past human–environment interactions. However, certain biases inherent to the dataset must be acknowledged, particularly due to the selection of certain species for dendrochronological studies and the special preservation conditions of archaeological woods. The former bias implies that while some species, such as alder (Alnus sp.), are sometimes largely present in archaeological sites from the Roman Era, they are often not sampled due to our inability to date them by dendrochronology. Therefore, they are not represented by this dataset. The latter is due to the fact that most preserved timbers in the study area originate from waterlogged environments, which favor the survival of organic materials (33, 34). However, this also skews the dataset toward contexts related to water management and hydraulic engineering (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 and Supplementary Material S2).

General statements about spatiotemporal developments based on smaller subsets of data must be interpreted with caution, as a considerable number of dendrochronological data might only represent few constructions. For example, the 178 ship timbers from Germania Inferior (Fig. 4B) originate from only 13 different watercrafts. To mitigate this potential bias, timbers from the same tree —sharing a highly similar tree-ring pattern— were merged into a single series to prevent overrepresentation of individual trees and potential distorted interpretations.

The high number of timbers from water wells (n = 7260), on the other hand, provides critical insight into the development of water supply systems during Antiquity. The significant decline in occurrences of water wells in Late Antiquity can be linked to population decline and relocation of (rural) settlements —which form this period are less traceable by traditional archaeological field surveys and thus, not often excavated (35). At the same time, this decline may also contribute to the apparent reduction in coniferous timbers found during Late Antiquity. Additionally, building activities in later times have occasionally destroyed ancient structures, particularly in larger urban centers, which are underrepresented (30.9%) compared to rural (69.1%) contexts. Another bias of the dataset arises from uneven spatial coverage of dendroarchaeological research across the study area (Fig. 1). In this context, differences in geology and aspects of politics and research history must be considered, including soil type and conditions of preservation, economic activity and related frequency of archaeological excavation, the legal and administrative framework for development-led archaeology, and the timing and implementation of dendrochronology (SI Appendix, Supplementary Material S2). Despite these inevitable limitations, the dataset’s comprehensive scale and high-resolution chronology provides a representative picture of wood use in Roman times across different regions. By explicitly addressing these biases in our analysis, we ensure that interpretations remain robust and nuanced, offering critical insights into the environmental, demographic, and economic dimensions of Roman woodland exploitation.

Woodland Exploitation.

Tree species selection and woodland composition.

As the dataset exclusively consists of absolutely dated samples, the distribution of identified taxa is influenced by archaeologists’ and dendrochronologists’ selections and does not directly reflect the preferences for specific types of wood among ancient woodworkers. The same applies to age distribution, since samples with fewer rings, possibly from coppiced woodlands (21), are less likely to be sampled and dated. Furthermore, it has to be noted that woodland exploitation for fuelwood is not adequately recorded (SI Appendix, Supplementary Material S2). Despite these limitations, the large dataset provides indications for anthropogenic preferences, as also highlighted by studies from later periods (36). Oak was the most commonly used species, a pattern widely observed in European dendroarchaeology due to its excellent technological properties and high natural durability (33, 37). This strong reliance on oak in the northern provinces contrasts with Roman written sources, which more frequently mention softwood (12, 14). This discrepancy likely results from the pan-imperial perspective of Roman authors, which suggests a dominance of softwoods (in addition to fir, spruce, pine, and larch also cypress and cedar) over hardwoods in trade networks.

Fir is the most frequently used softwood in the study area, likely due to its mechanical qualities and local availability. Interestingly, a mismatch between the natural distribution of fir and spruce and the spatial distribution of their archaeological finds indicates significant timber transport (Fig. 2D). Wooden containers, particularly stave vessels, were predominantly made of coniferous wood (fir and spruce) until the mid-3rd century (33). After 240 CE, only five samples of conifers could be recorded. Then, oak becomes the primary species for barrels in Late Antiquity and early medieval times, with records mostly dating post-500 CE (27). The shift in used species and the end of conifer timber transport in the mid-3rd century indicates a lack of suitable trees available in the forest and likely reflects the depletion of accessible conifer stands (18) and a broader decline in long-distance trade. This observation aligns with other traded goods, proxy records, and the general research hypotheses about the 3rd-century economic and environmental crises (38, 39).

Regarding woodland composition, it has to be noted that the dataset is not representative of wood that was primarily used as fuel, which could, for example, explain the low proportion of beech wood in the dataset. (Proto-)industrial processes in pottery and glass production, brick and lime burning, as well as mining and metallurgy (40) required large volumes of wood and have impacted local woodlands, leaving environmental imprints on supraregional scales (39, 41). Other fuelwood-consuming activities, such as the widespread cremation rite (42), domestic woodcraft and heating (43) must be taken into consideration, as it is not covered by the dendrochronological dataset. Charcoal production sites serve as indicators for the presence of ancient forests and are frequently encountered in the archaeological record, but only occasionally dated (44). Their temporal distribution, based on radiocarbon dating, with pits and kilns as the most common type of charcoal production sites during the Late Iron Age and throughout the Roman era, follows similar intensity trends comparable to dendrochronologically recorded felling activity (45).

Minimal tree age and very old trees.

Tree age serves as an indicator for woodland exploitation and resource management, when available in large data quantities (46). This study uses individual segment lengths as a proxy for mTA. While most samples lack the complete pith-to-waney edge cross sections and precise tree age estimates are thus limited, the dataset’s scale still permits statements on spatiotemporal changes in the age structure of the forest stands that were exploited. However, based on mTA it has to be noted that the age of trees and forest stands is likely older. Notably, coppice-like short-rotation forest management systems, described as silva caedua by Roman authors (14), remain largely invisible to dendrocharchaeological methods (21).

The increased use of older trees corresponding with the onset of Roman occupation (Fig. 3) might be linked to the well-developed Roman administrative system, which enabled rapid infrastructure development and opened access to previously unexploited woodlands (34). For example, in Germania Magna, mTA decreases significantly within a few decades around the BCE/CE transition, coinciding with a significant rise in recorded woodland exploitation during the short period of Roman expansion attempts (cf. SI Appendix, Fig. S3). This demonstrates a) that with sufficient replication, segment length is a credible estimate for tree and stand age, and b) furthermore indicates a clear connection between woodland exploitation and stand age associated with historical events.

Written records suggest that the Romans expanded exploitation to available forest areas, even those outside their provinces and organized timber transport when profitable or necessary (12, 14, 47). This capability might somewhat dampen the detectable human impact on the age composition of forest stands, reflected in the general tree age. For a better understanding of the Roman timber economy, a focus on the occurrence of VOT is informative, as such trees take a long time to grow and therefore will not reemerge for a long time after intensive felling or overexploitation. Since there is no general definition of when a tree is considered “very old” (48, 49), we defined VOT as those with an mTA of 200+ y, corresponding to trees with more than twice the dataset’s mean mTA (99.5 y), and represented by only the top 5.5% of our dataset (n = 1117). This threshold is expected to capture relevant VOT that reflects the presence of old-grown forest stands. Our findings indicate a clear decline in VOT during the 3rd century CE, which is interpreted as a sign of overexploitation, most significantly visible in Germania Superior and the region SE. Despite the overall scarcity of data in Late Antiquity, the rise of VOT in the 6th century strongly suggests natural reforestation and the reemergence of old-grown forests during this period. Germination dates for VOT logged in the early medieval period accumulate mainly in the 4th century (Fig. 3 A–D). This apparent germination phase coincides with a period of less intense Roman land use. An earlier germination phase in the 3rd century BCE in Germania Inferior, Gallia Belgica, and Germania Superior (Fig. 3 A–C) might be connected with declined settlement activity known from the middle La Tène period and the Celtic migrations. The germination phase of VOT during the 1st c. BCE in Germania Superior and the region SE (Fig. 4 C and D) might be connected with the so-called Helvetier-Einöde (50). Post-Roman reforestation exhibits a supraregional phenomenon also recorded by other paleoecological archives like pollen and macroremains (51), which proves detectable through dendroarchaeological records.

Regional differences.

Spatiotemporally explicit variations in the results demonstrate the ability of the dataset to catch regional differences like the reinforcement of military structures in the 4th century, for which higher effort is only visible for Germania Superior, whereas for the other well-replicated border areas, the region SE and Germania Inferior, dendrochronological data do not display any expansion of military structures after 200 CE and the 220 s, respectively. This result provides a basis for future regional studies to build upon and interlink dendrochronological data, archaeological information and other ecological proxies. This will add more detail and address region-specific archaeological and paleoecological research questions. Promising examples of such integrative research have already been published, e.g. from Germania Inferior (52, 53).

Materials and Methods

Dendrochronological Data.

We compiled a dataset including a total of 20,397 absolutely dated tree-ring series from timbers, largely recovered from archaeological excavations in the area of the continental northwestern provinces of the Roman Empire (Fig. 1), and spanning the millennium between 300 BCE and 700 CE. This unique dataset was generated by 28 dendroarchaeological laboratories (SI Appendix, Table S1), who provided dendrochronological dates and metadata such as tree species, number of tree rings, and site location (see below).

Species identification for each sample was performed by the contributing laboratories and followed standard methods of taxonomical identification, based on microscopic analyses of wood anatomical features (54, 55).

Segment lengths (i.e., the number of years between start and end date of the tree-ring series) were used to estimate minimal tree ages (mTA). Trees with an mTA of 200+ y were considered very old trees (VOT).

Spatial Information.

In addition to the dendrochronological and dendrological data, spatially explicit information was collected for each sample, enabling to precisely map the spatial distribution (Fig. 1A). Samples were documented with excavation site coordinates, provided as World Geodetic System (WGS84) coordinates standardized to two decimal places, which offers an approximate accuracy of 1 kilometer, sufficient for the supralocal scope of this study. The geographical information associated with each wood sample made it possible to assign it to one of seven regional groups (SI Appendix, Table S2 and Supplementary Material S3).

They include Germania Superior, Germania Inferior, Gallia Belgica, and Gallia Lugdunensis, a region in the south–east (SE), comprising the provinces of Raetia and Noricum, and a southwestern region (SW), covering the area between the Pyrenees and the Alps. The seventh regional group Germania Magna covers an area beyond the eastern frontier, which only belonged to the Empire for a short period under Augustus in its western part, but remained under significant Roman cultural influence.

Archaeological Data.

The dataset includes archaeological information for the investigated and dendrochronologically dated wooden structures. This information varies in detail per lab and per project. For 14,147 analyzed wooden objects, information is available regarding the type of feature it belonged to, amounting to a total of ten feature types (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). General object information was available for 12,093 samples, yielding three object groups (construction timbers, wooden containers, and small objects). Further information on the individual construction element was compiled for 11,674 samples, resulting in 15 more specific categories (SI Appendix, Supplementary Material S3). The classification of individual construction elements largely followed the explanations and definitions of Volmer and Zimmermann (56).

Conclusion

Throughout European Antiquity, forests played a vital role in supporting human societies by providing essential resources for economic, military, and cultural development. The presented comprehensive corpus of precisely dated dendrochronological data offers a robust record of forest interventions and timber use across the northwestern provinces and adjacent areas shaped by Roman culture and economy.

This study applies dendroarchaeological analysis at a large spatial and temporal scale —spanning over a millennium— across multiple regions. The results reveal both supraregional trends in woodland exploitation and regionally specific trajectories, illustrating how forest utilization responded to historical, economic, and environmental shifts.

A marked intensification of the socioeconomic pressure on forests between the Alps and the Atlantic is apparent from the drastically increased dendroarchaeological evidence between 50 BCE and the early 3rd century CE, coinciding with the Roman conquest, expansion, and prosperity. The examples discussed in more detail underscore the method’s value in reconstructing long-term socioeconomic and environmental dynamics. Analysis of changes in tree ages over time suggests that the Roman conquest in the decades around the turn of the millennium opened access to new forest resources, but that the available forest resources declined from the end of the 2nd century onward, with regional variations in the timing and intensity of this reduction. Following the end of Roman hegemony, forests were able to recover and old stands and trees reemerging by the Early Middle Ages. This aligns with the occurrence of archaeological evidence showing a general economic decline in the 3rd century, reflected both in the declining investment in transport infrastructure, as well as in watercraft and goods traffic (barrels), likely linked to historically documented complex political crises. (57) The intensification of military investment in the 4th century, which is particularly evident in Germania Superior, is probably linked to Valentinian’s building program and demonstrates the regional visibility of historical events.

In addition to established dendrochronological methods, this study introduces the proxies mTA and the occurrence of VOT as indicators of forest age and human impact. These metrics allow for the identification of periods of intensified exploitation and provide insight into woodland recovery and degradation, even during phases with limited data availability, such as Late Antiquity.

The main contribution of this approach is the ability to pinpoint phases of regional intensification of exploitation efforts and evaluate supraregional developments in woodland structure and dynamics. Beyond these multicentennial and supraregional comparisons, this paper provides a spatial and chronologically explicit dataset for the study of the Roman society in western and central Europe, bridging archaeology and historical environmental research. By offering this new resource for interdisciplinary research, it contributes significantly to the study of European Antiquity and the close relationship between societies and their forested environments.
