Catheter-associated urinary tract infection and associated factors among patients admitted to intensive care unit at King Abdullah Hospital, Bisha, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Hassan A. Al-Amri, Lama A. Almalhan, Mushabab A. Alghamdi, Mutasim E. Ibrahim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCatheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI) lead to increased morbidity, mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and frequent antimicrobial use, potentially leading to development of multidrug-resistant pathogens. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of CAUTI and identify risk factors among patients in intensive care units (ICU) at King Abdullah Hospital (KAH), Bisha, Saudi Arabia.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional, hospital-based study was conducted between November 2023 and June 2024 among 327 patients admitted to the ICU at KAH and catheterized with a Foley catheter. Clinical data and general characteristics of the patients were gathered. Identification and antibiotic sensitivity testing of bacterial pathogens were performed as per standard laboratory methods. Univariate and multivariate logistic regressions identified factors associated with CAUTI. Results were presented as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), and p-values < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.ResultsA total of 327 adult patients were enrolled, with a CAUTI prevalence of 27.8% (n = 91). Klebsiella species were the most prevalent isolate (46.2%), followed by Escherichia coli (24.2%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (11.0%). Cotrimoxazole exhibited the highest resistance at 74.7%, followed by cefoxitin at 52.7% and piperacillin/tazobactam at 50.5%. Independent factors associated with CAUTI included advance age (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 4.34, p < .001), flank pain (aOR = 17.3, p < .001), catheter duration of more than 10 days (aOR = 18.0, p = .022), urinary incontinence (aOR = 18.0, p = .006), urine retention (aOR = 50.81, p < .001), patient with comorbidity (aOR = 37.07, p = .004), number of comorbidities (aOR = 32.07, p < .001), diabetes mellitus (aOR = 35.52, p < .001), hypertension (aOR = 25.02, p = .002), chronic renal diseases (aOR = 9.03, p = .008), cardiovascular diseases (aOR = 30.22, p < .001) and chronic pulmonary diseases (aOR = 15.31, p = .005).ConclusionThe prevalence of CAUTI was 27.8%. We identified several factors that affect the development of CAUTI in hospitalized patients. Understanding these risk factors helps identify effective interventions. Enhanced education on catheter management for healthcare providers and patients is crucial to reducing CAUTI.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Inserting indwelling urinary catheters is a routine procedure in intensive care units (ICU) [1]. Although indwelling catheters are necessary, they are one of the leading causes of urinary tract infections (UTI). It is estimated that 95% of UTIs in the ICU are linked to catheters [2]. Catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs) are the most common nosocomial infections, with over one million cases annually, accounting for more than 40% of hospital infections. CAUTI represents 80% of all nosocomial UTIs. This high incidence incurs significant medical costs, estimated between $420 million and $450 million annually in the United States [3]. Additionally, catheter-associated bacteremia costs about $2,900 per episode, with treatment expenses including antimicrobial therapy, extended hospital stays, physician consultations, and morbidity-related costs [3–5].

In Saudi Arabia, CAUTI represents a significant challenge in health care, in particular among hospitalized patients. The incidence rate of CAUTI is 8.18 per 1000 catheter days for adults in the ICU for the period spanning from 2004 to 2011 [6]. Despite the progress in medical technology and in the control of infections, CAUTI remains prevalent in hospitals, contributing to an increase in morbidity and health care costs. The surveillance data indicate that CAUTI rates in ICUs are worrying, with recent studies that highlight a persistent burden of infections relating to urinary catheters. A recent study has documented the rates of increased diseases associated with the device, including CAUTI from 2017 to 2020 in a Saudi Arabia hospital, underlining the continuous need for supervision in infection prevention strategies [7]. A multicenter study conducted in the Saudi Ministry of Health Hospital for two years confirmed the persistent prevalence of CAUTI, requesting immediate action to mitigate these infections [8]. A study conducted in Saudi Arabia in the ICUs of the 12 selected hospitals from 2013 to 2016 revealed that CAUTI was 28.4% of the total device-associated healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), and the mortality rate was 36.9% for CAUTI [9].

Assessing the current state of CAUTI is crucial for developing and evaluating strategies focused on its prevention and management. Moreover, the identification of risk factors is crucial for establishing the priority group requiring intervention. Like many other developing countries, Saudi Arabia has limited data on CAUTI rates in hospitals across various geographical regions. Understanding this information is crucial for patient health and helps health authorities identify areas or healthcare settings with high infection rates. With this knowledge, they can take appropriate action and implement immediate, effective control measures. While there have been few studies on CAUTI in Saudi Arabia, a recent investigation highlighted the need for increased interventional and epidemiological research to gain a deeper understanding of the causes and factors affecting the rate of CAUTI in the country [10]. However, information remains limited regarding CAUTI rates and their associated risk factors in many clinical settings in Saudi Arabia. To the best of our knowledge, there are no current studies in Bisha regarding CAUTI rates or the associated risk factors among ICU patients admitted to KAH in Bisha province, Saudi Arabia. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of CAUTIs and their associated risk factors among patients with indwelling urinary catheterizations throughout the ICU of KAH. By addressing risk factors and implementing effective prevention strategies, health professionals can reduce the incidence of these infections, thereby promoting better patient outcomes.

Methodology

Study design and setting

A retrospective cross-sectional hospital-based study was conducted between November 2023 and June 2024 at KAH, Bisha Province, Asir Region, Saudi Arabia. KAH is the main referral hospital in Bisha Province, Southwest Saudi Arabia. This hospital is a 400-bed tertiary care center that offers a variety of specialized services. The hospital has two different ICUs containing 28 beds, including a general ICU (16 beds), and a coronary care unit (CCU; 12 beds). These units are supported by a multi-disciplinary care team [11, 12].

Study population

The study population included all adult patients aged 18 years or older who experienced a new or recurrent admission to the ICU during the study period with a diagnosis other than UTI and had been catheterized with a Foley catheter. All patients have undergone short-term catheterization for less than 4 weeks. However, long-term catheterization is defined as having a catheter in place for more than 30 days [13]. The exclusion criteria included patients admitted with UTI and those transferred from other hospitals.

Therefore, the minimum sample size is 264. Given the exclusion of patients with incomplete information, we increased the initial sample size by 25% to reach a final sample of 330.

Data collection plan

We used a standardized data collection form to gather the required information from patients’ files and hospital databases. The extracted data included: (i) the patient’s demographic and general characteristics. (ii) clinical data of the patients, including type and duration of catheterization, indication of urinary catheterization, length of stay in ICU, source and cause of admission, symptoms, and type and number of comorbidities.

CAUTI was diagnosed based on a positive urine culture of ≥ 105 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL of one or two bacterial species in a patient with an indwelling urinary catheter in place for more than two consecutive days in an inpatient setting, accompanied by one of these four signs or symptoms: fever (>38.0 °C), suprapubic tenderness, costovertebral angle pain, or costovertebral angle tenderness [15, 16].

Identification and resistance patterns of bacterial pathogens

Preliminary isolation and identification of bacterial pathogens causing CAUTI were based on conventional microbiological methods. Bacterial identification was then confirmed using the VITEK II automated identification system (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Étoile, France). The susceptibility patterns of bacterial isolates were examined against different antibiotic classes using VITEK II cards (AST-N292) (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Étoile, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and breakpoints were determined following the policies of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [17]. The antibiotics that were tested include amikacin, amoxicillin/clavulanate, aztreonam, cefepime, cefoxitin, ceftazidime, ciprofloxacin, colistin, cotrimoxazole, gentamicin, imipenem, meropenem and piperacillin/tazobactam. American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) Klebsiella pneumoniae 700,603 and ATCC Escherichia coli 25,922 were used as quality control strains. The findings of antibiotic susceptibility were reported as resistant or susceptible.

Statistical analysis

The data were collected and entered into an Excel worksheet, then encoded and analyzed using IBM SPSS version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to describe the baseline characteristics and clinical information of the patients, presented in terms of numbers, frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations (SD). Risk factors associated with CAUTI were identified using both univariate and multivariate logistic regressions. The Pearson Chi-squared test is used to compare categorical data. In univariate logistic regression, factors associated with CAUTI were reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variables with p-values < 0.2 in the univariate model were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis [18]. The backward stepwise elimination method was used to obtain the final predictive factors independently associated with CAUTI. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to assess the model’s fit. The results were reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). All results with p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 327 adult patients examined for CAUTI were enrolled in the study. Among the subjects, 225 (68.8%) were male and 102 (31.2%) were female. The age of the patients ranged from 18 to 102 years, with an overall mean of 56.67 ± 23.13 years. Elderly patients comprised the largest demographic group, accounting for 44.6% (n = 146), followed by young adults at 30% (n = 98), and adults at 25.4% (n = 83). Most participants were married (85.3%, n = 279) and lived in the Bisha suburb (98.5%, n = 322) (Table 1).

Prevalence of CAUTI

The overall prevalence of CAUTI was 27.8% (n = 91). CAUTI was equally distributed among male (28.4%) and female (27.6%) patients(p = .87). The highest proportion of CAUTI was found among elderly patients (39.0%, n = 57) compared to adult (22.9%, n = 19) and young adult (15.3%, n = 15) patients (p < .001). Married patients had a significantly higher proportion of CAUTI (30.1%, n = 84) than single patients(14.6%, n = 7), (p = .027). Patients staying more than five days in the ICU had a higher proportion of CAUTI (38.0%, n = 35) compared to those who remained for five days or less (23.8%, n = 56), (p = .010).

Of 327 patients admitted to ICUs, 322 (98.5%) received a urethral catheter, while five (1.5%) received a suprapubic catheter. The catheterization duration exceeded 10 days in eight patients (2.4%) and ranged from 2 to 10 days in 319 patients (97.6%). The most common indications of catheterization were urinary incontinence (66.4%), followed by immobility (44.3%) and CNS disorders (8.9%) (Fig. 1).

Bacterial isolates and their drug resistance profiles

Among the 91 patients with CAUTI, Klebsiella species was the most prevalent pathogen at 46.2%, followed by Escherichia coli at 24.2%, Pseudomonas aeruginosa at 11.0%, Proteus mirabilis at 9.9%, and Acinetobacter species at 8.8%.

Bacterial pathogens exhibited the highest resistance rate to cotrimoxazole (74.7%), followed by cefoxitin at 52.7%, piperacillin/tazobactam at 50.5%, and ceftazidime at 49.5%. In contrast, Colistin showed the lowest resistance rate, 1.1%. The distribution of antibiotic resistance across various bacterial isolates is shown in Fig. 2.

Factors associated with CAUTI

Table 2 presents the factors associated with CAUTI among adult patients admitted to the ICU in King Abdullah Hospital, Bisha, Saudi Arabia. Married patients had more than double the odds of developing CAUTI compared to single patients (Crude OR = 2.5, 95% CI 1.09–5.85, p = .027). Patients aged 65 and older had more than three times the odds of having CAUTI compared with those younger than 65 years (cOR = 3.5, 95% CI 1.86–6.74, p = .001). Patients who stayed in the ICU for more than 5 days had twice the odds of having CAUTI compared to those who stayed for 5 days or less (cOR = 2.0, 95% CI 1.170–3.291, p = .010). Regarding signs and symptoms, patients with flank pain had eight times the odds of having CAUTI (cOR = 8.8, 95% CI 4.82–16.08, p < .001). Those with hematuria were more than four times more likely to have CAUTI (cOR = 4.5, 95% CI 1.42-14.0, p = .006). Patients with a catheter for more than 10 days had 20 times the odds of having CAUTI (cOR = 19.58, 95% CI 0.37- 161.54, p < .001) compared to those with a catheter for less than 10 days duration. With respect to indications for catheterization, patients with urine retention had 25 times the odds of having CAUTI (cOR = 25.0, 95% CI 5.61-111.06, p < .001). Moreover, those with urinary incontinence had more than three times the odds of having CAUTI (cOR = 3.8, 95% CI 2.02–7.05, < 0.001). In terms of comorbidities, patients with comorbidities were about twice as likely to have CAUTI (cOR = 2.3, 95% CI 1.2–4.6, p = .011), notably those with DM (cOR = 2.2, 95% CI 1.33–3.68, p = .002), hypertension (cOR = 2.43, 95% CI 1.4–4.26, p = .002), chronic renal disease (cOR = 2.0, 95% CI 1.03–3.72, p = .037), and cardiovascular diseases (cOR = 1.9, 95% CI 1.15–3.21, p = .013).

Discussion

Catheter-associated urinary tract infections are a serious healthcare-associated infection with potentially severe adverse events. This study reported the prevalence of CAUTI in 91 (27.8%) patients admitted to the ICU in KAH, Bisha, Saudi Arabia. Our result is slightly higher than the 22% prevalence reported in a recent study at a Tertiary Care Hospital in Hail province, the northern part of Saudi Arabia [14]. But our results were lower than the 42.2% reported from 2004 to 2011in the eastern region of the country [19]. Similarly, CAUTI rates have been reported at various levels in many countries. For example, the overall prevalence rate of CAUTI was 73.75% among ICU patients in a recent study from India [20]. The incidence of CAUTI was 27.8 per 1000 catheter days among inpatients in hospitals in Ethiopia [16]. Summary of device-associated infections data reported by hospitals participating in the US National Health Safety Network from January through December 2006 showed that the pooled average CAUTI rates ranged from 3.1 to 7.5 infections per 1,000 catheter days [21]. These variations may be influenced by diverse health practices and cultural contexts. A multinational study involving 623 ICUs across 37 countries highlighted significant factors contributing to CAUTI rates, particularly infection prevention protocols and staff training [22]. It is well known that variations in urological infection rates worldwide arise from disparities in access to healthcare resources and the use of advanced medical technology [4].

Like other studies, where most of the isolated pathogens were Gram-negative bacilli [20, 23, 24]. However, several antibiotic classes showed high resistance rates. Resistance to antimicrobial agents has been reported since their initial use and remains an increasingly alarming global issue [24–26]. A multi-center study in Saudi Arabia indicated high rates of antimicrobial-resistant pathogens from CAUTIs in the ICU, highlighting the complexities in treatment and increased healthcare costs [27].Therefore, understanding local resistance patterns of CAUTIs pathogens will give the required information to select empirical therapy with proper antibiotics.

In the univariate analysis, the study identified several factors correlated with CAUTI. The result showed that elderly patients were more likely to have CAUTI than adults and young adults. Similar findings have been reported in Saudi Arabia [1, 28, 29] and in other studies [13, 15, 30, 31]. Older adults are particularly vulnerable due to issues like urinary retention and cognitive impairment, complicating catheter management [32]. It’s generally accepted that several anatomical, physiological, and immunological factors contribute to the high incidence of infections in older individuals [33]. Anatomically, aging affects the urinary tract, making it more susceptible to infections. For example, bladder tissue senescence reduces muscle tone and neurogenic bladder conditions, complicating catheter use and promoting infection [3, 4]. Additionally, postmenopausal women experience decreased estrogen levels, leading to changes in vaginal flora and increased bacterial colonization risk [31]. In addition, elderly patients often have multiple comorbidities, like diabetes and chronic kidney disease, which impair immune function and increase infection vulnerability [34]. Long-term urinary catheters, frequently needed for these comorbidities, significantly increase the risk of infection by allowing bacteria direct access to the urinary tract [13].CAUTI can increase hospital stays, healthcare costs, and worse patient outcomes, including higher morbidity and mortality. Research data indicate that developing countries tend to have higher rates of CAUTIs than developed countries [22]. A cohort study from 2014 to 2022 across 67 Middle Eastern hospitals, including Saudi Arabia, found that poor adherence to best practices like proper catheter insertion and maintenance significantly increases CAUTI risk in ICU patients [35]. In Western countries, strict infection control protocols are usually enforced, emphasizing staff training and adherence to guidelines to lower CAUTI rates [22]. Therefore, best practices include proper catheter insertion techniques, maintaining sterile equipment, and regularly assessing the necessity of the catheter. However, healthcare professionals play a key role in implementing these practices, as their adherence to protocols directly impacts patient outcomes. Continued training and awareness campaigns can further improve compliance and help create a culture of safety [36]. ICU patients are typically in a compromised state due to underlying health conditions, invasive procedures, and the use of urinary drainage catheters. In this study, prolonged catheterization and ICU stays of more than 10 days were significantly associated with CAUTI. The extended duration of catheterization, especially in conjunction with prolonged ICU stays, is associated with an increased need for caution. A large cross-sectional study of six hospitals found that catheter duration is a critical determinant of infection rates across multiple medical care settings, reinforcing the need to promptly remove catheters when catheterization is no longer indicated [37]. Consistently, in a previous study from Saudi Arabia, Al-Hazmi suggested that the reduction of the duration of catheterization and the length of hospital stay of the patient positively impacts the reduction of catheter-related UTI [28]. Furthermore, the persistence of a urinary catheter beyond the required period exposes patients to a higher risk of infection due to biofilm formation on the catheter surface and increases the risk of bacterial colonization within the urinary collection bag [3, 23, 38]. To mitigate these risks, it is essential to implement preventive strategies such as the timely elimination of the catheter, using catheters covered with antimicrobials, and adherence to aseptic insertion techniques [39].

In the present study, we found that flank pain was a significant risk factor for CAUTI, which agrees with a previous study [4, 27]. Despite the extensive research on CAUTI, specific correlations with flank pain remain inadequately explored. A study pointed out the multifactorial nature of CAUTI risk factors, in which the pain in the flank can indicate an underlying urinary tract pathology that is often overlooked [37]. Therefore, there is a need for vigilant monitoring and flank pain evaluation in patients with internal catheters to mitigate the risks of CAUTI. Improved consciousness among medical care providers could lead to more specific prevention strategies, thus reducing the morbidity associated with CAUTI and improving patient outcomes [40].

In this study, we found that hematuria was significantly associated with CAUTI. Hematuria, characterized by blood in the urine, is increasingly recognized as a potential risk factor for CAUTI, which has been documented [3]. Blood can facilitate bacterial colonization and biofilm formation in catheters, thereby increasing susceptibility to infection. The underlying mechanisms imply several factors, including the altered integrity of the urinary tract and the inflammatory response triggered by hematuria, which can create an environment conducive to the growth of pathogens [41]. Therefore, the use of regular evaluations of the need for catheters in patients with hematuria can help mitigate the risk of CAUTI, promoting better patient outcomes [41].

We found that patients with urinary retention and incontinence were more likely to develop CAUTI than those with normal urination function. This agrees with findings from China [34, 38]. Stagnant urine creates an ideal environment for bacterial proliferation, which can rise through the catheter and lead to infection [37]. On the other hand, individuals with urinary incontinence often require intermittent or internal catheters to manage conservation and urinary incontinence, thus increasing their susceptibility to CAUTI. A catheter creates a direct path for pathogens to enter the urinary tract, and incontinence can break the skin or expose catheters to bacteria, increasing the risk of infection [38]. It has been noted that prolonged catheterization and poor hygiene practices observed in patients with urinary incontinence can increase the risk of CAUTI [38]. However, to mitigate the risk of CAUTI, prevention strategies include implementing rigorous aseptic techniques during catheter insertion, regularly re-evaluating the need to use the catheter, and educating the caregivers on the correct maintenance and catheter hygiene practices [37, 42].

In the present study, patients with three or more comorbidities were most likely to have CAUTI. It is generally recognized that patients with multiple comorbidities often have a more complex clinical profile, which influences the duration of internal catheters and the susceptibility to infections [37]. A multicenter study conducted in the United States involving patients with the placement of a new indwelling urethral catheter during hospitalization found that patients with more health problems reported higher rates of both infectious and non-infectious complications [43]. We found that DM and hypertension were significant risk factors for CAUTI, which agrees with many studies [20, 30, 34, 38, 40]. The physiological mechanisms contributing to this greater susceptibility include glucosuria, which creates a favorable environment for bacterial growth, and neuropathy, which can impair bladder function and increase the volume of residual urine [37]. In addition, the chronic hyperglycemic state observed in diabetic patients leads to alterations in metabolic function, which negatively affect the uroepithelial barrier and facilitate bacterial colonization [40]. In addition, the immune response in individuals with diabetes is often compromised, characterized by dysfunctional neutrophils and decreased cytokine responses. This deregulation can hinder the body’s ability to respond effectively to uropathogenic bacteria, contributing to the greater incidence of CAUTI in this population [37]. Furthermore, patients with hypertension often experience vascular changes, such as endothelial dysfunction, which can compromise defense mechanisms, thus increasing the risk of infection [44]. The combination of chronic hypertension and the presence of internal catheters can lead to a reduced immune response, further predisposing these patients to CAUTI. In addition, the use of catheters in hypertensive patients can promote bacterial colonization and the formation of Biofilm, critical factors in the pathogenesis of CAUTI. This is particularly relevant in the populations of patients with comorbidities, such as those in intensive care environments, in which the use of the catheter is frequent [15].

Chronic conditions such as diabetes, CRD, hypertension, and heart disease are explicitly associated with a higher incidence of CAUTI [20, 37]. Our study highlighted that the history of CRD increased the risk of CAUTI. The immunocompromised state resulting from the CRD and the interrupted urinary tract dynamics improves susceptibility to infections [4].

The co-occurrence of CAUTI with CVD is associated with a decline in overall patient outcomes. Such situations require reevaluation of standard patient management protocols, especially in intensive care settings, where patients are more vulnerable due to invasive procedures [37]. However, careful monitoring and managing urinary catheters in this population is fundamental [37]. Chronic pulmonary diseases (CPD), such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and asthma, significantly alter immune responses, thereby increasing the risk of UTI (anticipated infections) in patients with long-term catheters [37, 45].

The multivariate analysis identified numerous independent factors, including advanced age, flank pain, urinary incontinence, urinary retention, and the number of comorbidities, including DM, hypertension, CRD, CVD, and CPD, associated with CAUTI in our setting. By addressing these factors comprehensively, health professionals can develop enhanced strategies to reduce infection rates in critical patients [37, 40]. Identifying and dealing with risk factors associated with comorbidity can potentially mitigate the adverse results relating to catheterism, making health workers indispensable for customizing catheter management strategies based on comorbidity profiles to improve patient safety [42]. Evaluation and continuous adaptation of care practices can lead to significant improvements in patient safety and outcomes in the ICU.

Limitations

First, this study was conducted in a single hospital, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study focused on specific patient populations within the ICU; therefore, we did not assess the incidence of CAUTI in patients outside the ICU. Third, although we calculated the recommended sample size, larger datasets could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the risk factors associated with CAUTI in our context. Finally, only a limited number of bacterial isolates were collected for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, which increases the likelihood of bias in resistance patterns.

Conclusion

The present study found that the prevalence of CAUTI was 27.8%, and several factors influencing its development among hospitalized patients have been identified. This study presents findings on CAUTI within our clinical environment, providing insights to guide future local and national initiatives aimed at mitigating this infection. Nevertheless, understanding the risk factors associated with CAUTI helps identify effective interventions to prevent infection and reduce its incidence. Furthermore, enhanced education for healthcare providers and patients concerning catheter management and infection prevention is essential to mitigate these risks.
