The dietary inflammatory index is associated with the presence and severity of coronary artery disease
Authors: Niyaziaili Adili, Qian Zhao, Gulandanmu Aihemaiti, Jun-Kun Chu, Ju-Kun Zhang, Yi-Ning Yang, Xiao-Mei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic inflammation is a contributing factor to the onset and progression of coronary artery disease (CAD). In our study, we aimed to explore the correlation between the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) score and CAD severity.MethodsA case‒control study was performed comprising 214 patients exhibiting stable angina and a comparison group of 214 age- and sex-matched healthy individuals. A semiquantitative food frequency questionnaire was used to evaluate dietary intake, and coronary severity was determined by the Gensini score.ResultsParticipants with DIIs in the highest tertile had a greater propensity for CAD than those with DIIs in the lowest tertile, with an odds ratio (OR) of 3.50 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.79–6.85). After accounting for potential confounding factors, individuals in the highest DII tertile demonstrated an increased likelihood of severe CAD compared with those in the lowest tertile, with an OR of 4.09 (95% CI: 1.80–9.28, P trend = 0.001). Additionally, the propensity for severe coronary lesions increased linearly with increasing DII (P overall = 0.003, P nonlinear = 0.150).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that higher DIIs are related to an elevated likelihood and severity of CAD. Taken together, these findings suggest that an anti-inflammatory diet may be beneficial in managing CAD. However, well-designed prospective studies in different populations are still needed to validate these findings and explore the potential causal relationship between dietary inflammation and CAD.Keywords: Coronary artery disease, Diet, Inflammation, Dietary inflammatory index, Atherosclerosis

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a major global health concern, being a leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. Consequently, CVDs necessitate a substantial allocation of health care resources while simultaneously exerting a tremendous economic impact on affected individuals, families, and the broader society [2, 3]. Current evidence indicates that inflammation is the basis for the activation and progression of coronary atheroma and plaque instability and disruption [4–6], and the outcome is worse in patieanalysents with a more significant vascular inflammatory reaction [7]. Subsequent studies have shown that the benefits of statins are associated with the inhibition of inflammation in addition to the reduction of lipoprotein cholesterol [8]. The Canakinumab Anti-inflammatory Thrombosis Outcome Study (CANTOS) further demonstrated the role of anti-inflammatory therapy in inhibiting CVD progression independent of lipoprotein cholesterol [9].

Diet, a crucial modifiable factor, has the potential to significantly influence the regulation of chronic inflammation [10, 11]. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) was devised as a quantitative instrument for evaluating the inflammatory potential of a person’s dietary profile, considering the cumulative impact of diverse macro- and micronutrients, along with various food components. A higher DII is indicative of a diet with greater proinflammatory potential, whereas a lower DII corresponds to an anti-inflammatory dietary pattern [12]. Since its inception, the DII has gained significant popularity as a research tool for exploring the link between the inflammatory potential of diet and chronic noncommunicable diseases [13–20].

Previous studies have demonstrated positive associations between elevated DIIs and increased coronary artery disease (CAD) risk and related outcomes in diverse populations [21–23]. However, the relationship between DII and the extent of coronary lesions, as well as the role of diet-induced inflammation in CAD progression, remains unclear.

Against this backdrop, in the current case‒control study we sought to examine the link between DII scores and both the likelihood of CAD and the magnitude of coronary lesions among a Chinese population of stable angina patients. The study population comprises previously untreated individuals who were first diagnosed with stable angina and had no prior pharmacological intervention for coronary artery disease. This design allows for a more accurate assessment of the relationship between dietary inflammation and CAD, minimizing potential confounding factors related to medication use. Additionally, by analysing the differences in dietary nutrient and food group intake between patients with severe and mild coronary lesions, we aimed to provide scientific insights into the influence of specific dietary components on modulating inflammation and CAD.

Methods

Study participants

This retrospective case‒control study recruited 428 participants with first-time chest pain, including 214 patients with stable angina (CAD group) who were first diagnosed and had no prior pharmacological intervention for coronary artery disease and 214 healthy controls (control group). The control group was carefully matched in terms of age, sex and coronary angiography or coronary CTA to rule out CAD, as shown in Fig. 1. Participants from both the CAD group and the control group were enrolled consecutively from the Department of Cardiology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University from September 2019 to May 2021. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Human Ethics Committee of the hospital(K202101-20). Written informed consent was acquired from all subjects, who adhered to ethical principles.

Recruitment flowchart of study participants

Sample size was determined for matched case-control design using McNemar’s test. We assumed the highest DII tertile exposure rate of 25% in controls and an odds ratio of 2.5, which represents a clinically meaningful effect for dietary intervention strategies. With 80% power and two-sided α = 0.05, the calculated minimum was 182 matched pairs. To ensure adequate power, we recruited 214 pairs (428 participants). Post-hoc analysis based on observed data (OR = 2.74, 95% CI: 1.64–4.60) confirmed statistical power exceeded 90%.

All patients underwent coronary angiography or coronary CTA on the third day after admission. Based on WHO guidelines, CAD is diagnosed when a major coronary artery or its principal branches have more than 50% stenosis. Patients with severe liver or renal abnormalities, autoimmune system abnormalities, moderate to severe heart valve disease, chronic inflammation, acute infection, malignancy, or active bleeding were ruled out of the research.

On the day of admission, all participants underwent a standardized interview, which included gathering demographic information such as age and sex; lifestyle behaviours, including smoking habits; and dietary information pertaining to the past year. Additionally, medical history and medication usage data were documented. Anthropometric data, including weight, height, admission heart rate, admission systolic blood pressure (SBP), and admission diastolic blood pressure (DBP), were measured according to standardized routines. On the second day of hospitalization, fasting blood samples were obtained from participants to assess triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and fasting glucose concentrations. All blood biochemical tests were performed at the central laboratory of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University using a commercially available automated platform.

Collection of dietary data

A validated semiquantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [24] was used to evaluate dietary intake. Foods commonly consumed in Northwestern China included fruits, vegetables, meat, poultry, and cereals. The quantity and frequency of consumption were meticulously documented and converted into daily intake, with the following frequency classifications: daily, 4–6 times per week, 1–3 times per week, 1–3 times per month, not eaten or rarely eaten. The daily consumption of carbohydrates, proteins, cholesterol, fats, fibre, niacin, folic acid, vitamin A, vitamin D, vitamin E, vitamin B6, vitamin B12, β-carotene, iron (Fe), magnesium (Mg), and zinc (Zn) was evaluated by utilizing the Chinese Food Composition Table [25] to analyse the frequency and quantity of each food item ingested. For the dietary variables under consideration, the reference recall period was considered the one-year duration before the interview. The dietary information from the FFQ was used to compute the DIIs of all individuals. The DII was calculated with a scoring algorithm based on a review of 1943 articles assessing the impact of 45 food parameters on six inflammatory biomarkers (IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and C-reactive protein). However, due to regional dietary patterns in Northwestern China and FFQ limitations, only 16 parameters (e.g., protein, fiber, folic acid, vitamin E, iron, selenium) were assessable. These included key anti/proinflammatory nutrients listed in Table 4. Unavailable parameters (e.g., specific flavonoids or spices) were excluded. Different dietary components have additive and cumulative effects on inflammation. The specific steps for calculating the DII score were as follows: 1. The dietary intake data were converted into daily intake amounts using the 6th Edition of the Chinese Food Composition Table 2. The actual dietary intake data of the subjects were adjusted by comparison with a global standard dietary intake database to obtain Z scores for each food component or nutrient. The Z score was calculated as follows: Z = (actual intake of the food component - average intake level)/standard deviation. 3. The Z score was then transformed into a percentile value centred on zero; i.e., the Z score was converted to a percentile, doubled, and then subtracted by one. 4. A literature review was also conducted to determine the specific inflammatory effect score for each nutrient. 5. Individual-specific DIIs for each nutrient were calculated as follows: individual-specific DII = centred percentile value of the Z score × specific inflammatory effect score. 6. The individual-specific DIIs for all nutrients were summed to obtain the total DII for each subject [12]. In brief, the DII score positively correlated with the proinflammatory effect of diet, with increased scores indicating a stronger effect. Conversely, negative values reflect an anti-inflammatory effect, with larger negative values indicating a more potent effect.

Assessment of coronary artery disease severity

All coronary angiograms were obtained by skilled interventional cardiologists. Experienced cardiologists evaluated the severity of coronary lesions by employing the Gensini scoring system [26, 27]. The dietary information and DII scores of the participants were kept confidential. The Gensini score is determined by giving a score to each coronary stenosis based on the extent of luminal stenosis and the magnitude of the affected coronary artery. Among the 214 patients initially recruited in the CAD group, 18 were diagnosed via coronary CT angiography (CTA) and were excluded from the final analysis because the Gensini score could not be calculated. Among the 196 individuals in the CAD group, a subdivision was carried out into severe (n = 89) and mild (n = 107) coronary lesion groups based on the Gensini score, with a severe lesion defined as a score >20 and a mild lesion defined as a score ≤ 20.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Concurrently, continuous variables are reported as either means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges. Independent-samples t tests or Mann‒Whitney U tests were used to analyse the differences in demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, and DII scores between groups, while chi‒square tests were used to assess categorical variables. Given the matched case-control design, conditional logistic regression models were employed to compute odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for exploring the relationships between DII tertiles and CAD incidence, accounting for the matched pairs based on age and sex. The multivariable conditional logistic regression models incorporated additional covariates, including BMI, smoking habits, diabetes status, hypertension, lipid profile, and fasting glucose. For the analysis of coronary lesion severity among CAD patients, unconditional logistic regression was used as this analysis was conducted within the CAD group only and did not involve the matched control group. This approach allowed us to examine the association between DII scores and the severity of coronary lesions while adjusting for relevant confounders. Trend tests for DII scores were performed by treating the tertile categories as continuous variables. Additionally, the correlation between the DII score and the severity of CAD is presented in a restricted cubic spline plot. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance, and all analyses were conducted utilizing SPSS for Windows version 26.0 and R for Windows version 4.2.3.

Results

This study enrolled a total of 214 participants with CAD, and 214 healthy individuals without CAD. The CAD group was characterized by significantly greater DIIs, BMIs, TG levels, and prevalence of diabetes and smoking habits than the control group. Moreover, patients with CAD exhibited reduced HDL-C levels relative to controls. The DIIs were further categorized by tertile into tertile 1 (≤ −3.50), tertile 2 (−3.50 to −1.40), and tertile 3 (> −1.40), and the percentages of DIIs in these three categories were significantly different between the CAD and control groups. However, regarding age, sex, hypertension status, hypertensive medication, diabetes medication, SBP, DBP, heart rate, fasting glucose, TC, and LDL-C, we observed no significant differences between the patients with CAD and the controls. Discussion

In this study, we used a case‒control design to evaluate the correlation between DII scores and CAD and coronary severity in patients with stable angina who were first diagnosed and had no pharmacological intervention. We found that the DII score might act as a risk factor for CAD and the severity of coronary lesions. These results suggested the potential involvement of dietary inflammation in the progression of CAD.

In our investigation, a significant correlation was observed between higher DII scores and an elevated likelihood of CAD, supporting previous findings in various populations, including the US [28, 29], Middle Eastern [23, 30, 31], Australian [32], Italian [33], and Swedish [34] populations. Notably, our research differs from prior studies in that we recruited untreated patients experiencing their first chest pain, thereby minimizing potential pharmacological confounding factors on the association between DII scores and CAD incidence. Although inflammation has been previously linked to the severity of coronary artery disease [35, 36], research on the correlation between DII scores and the severity of CAD is lacking. Our investigation is the first to explore this association in patients with stable angina, providing valuable insights into the contribution of diet-induced inflammation to the progression of coronary atherosclerosis. We discovered that greater DIIs are significantly associated with more severe coronary lesions, implying that proinflammatory diets might exacerbate atherosclerotic processes.

The precise mechanisms underlying the association between a proinflammatory diet and an elevated risk of developing CAD are not yet fully understood. However, atherosclerosis, as the primary cause of CAD, is associated with the onset of chronic low-grade systemic inflammation induced by consuming a proinflammatory diet [37]. This inflammation has been established as a major contributor to the pathogenesis and progression of atherosclerosis [38]. Many clinical studies support the role of inflammation in CAD [9, 39–41]. All of these studies highlight the critical contribution of inflammation to the onset and progression of CAD, thereby revealing possible pathways to reduce CAD risk by improving inflammatory status.

There are several potential mechanisms by which a proinflammatory diet may contribute to CAD: (1) Endothelial dysfunction: Inflammatory mediators, including C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumour necrosis factor-α, can impair the function of the endothelial lining of blood vessels, leading to a reduction in nitric oxide bioavailability and vasoconstriction and an increase in the production of adhesion molecules that facilitate leukocyte attachment to the vessel wall [42, 43]. (2) Increased oxidative stress: Proinflammatory diets can potentially exacerbate the production of reactive oxygen species while simultaneously impairing the body’s antioxidant defence mechanisms. This imbalance eventually culminates in oxidative-mediated harm to critical biomolecules, including lipids, proteins, and DNA [44–47]. These processes contribute to the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis. (3) Lipid and lipoprotein modulation: Inflammatory cytokines can influence the metabolism and action of lipids and lipoproteins, leading to the accumulation of LDL particles in the arterial wall and a reduction in HDL concentrations [48, 49]. (4) Immune cell activation: Proinflammatory diets may stimulate the activation of immune cells, including macrophages and T lymphocytes, which can migrate into the vessel wall, release additional inflammatory mediators, and contribute to the formation and progression of atherosclerotic lesions [50, 51].

A higher DII indicates a dietary pattern characterized by greater consumption of proinflammatory nutrients, including saturated fat, cholesterol, and refined carbohydrates, and lower consumption of anti-inflammatory nutrients, such as dietary fibre, omega-3 fatty acids, and antioxidants [52]. Our analysis of dietary nutrient and food type intake revealed significant differences between the severe coronary lesion group and mild coronary lesion group. The severe coronary lesion group had lower intakes of protein, fibre, folic acid, vitamin E, iron, selenium, fresh fruit, and soybean products than did the mild coronary lesion group. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating inverse correlations between the intake of these nutrients and the risk of CAD [53, 54]. It has been suggested that these nutrients and food types might help reduce inflammation and oxidative stress [55, 56], thereby lowering the risk of atherosclerosis and subsequent CAD.

Our study employed different analytical approaches for different research questions. For the primary analysis examining the association between DII and CAD incidence, we used conditional logistic regression to appropriately account for the matched case-control design, which provides more efficient and unbiased estimates compared to unconditional models in matched data. For the secondary analysis examining the association between DII and coronary lesion severity within the CAD group, we used unconditional logistic regression, as this analysis did not involve the matched controls and focused solely on the CAD patients. This methodological approach ensures appropriate statistical handling of both the matched and unmatched components of our study. Several limitations in this study warrant consideration. First, because of the retrospective nature of the case‒control design, although exposure information was collected based on instructions for reporting the past year, it was not possible to determine the true chronology. The retrospective case-control design may have potential sources of bias, particularly the selection of prevalent cases, which might lead to biased results. Prospective cohort studies could help to determine the causal relationships between DII scores and CAD risk. Second, the evaluation of dietary consumption was based on self-reports from the FFQ, which are subject to reporting errors and inaccuracies, although the validity and reliability of the FFQ have been demonstrated. Objective measures of dietary intake and inflammation, such as inflammatory biomarkers, could provide more accurate information on the true association between diet-induced inflammation and CAD risk. Although we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, residual confounding may persist. There might be other unmeasured or poorly measured factors, such as physical activity, socioeconomic status, and genetic susceptibility, which could influence the associations observed in our study. Third, our DII score was derived from 16 of the original 45 food parameters due to the constraints of the FFQ. While this subset represents key pro- and anti-inflammatory nutrients, the inability to include all parameters might have introduced some degree of exposure misclassification bias. Besides, information on physical activity was not available in our dataset. As physical activity is a known protective factor for CAD and may correlate with healthier dietary patterns, its absence from our models could result in residual confounding. Future studies should aim to include objective or detailed measures of physical activity to better isolate the effect of dietary inflammation. Fourth, the single-centre nature of our investigation and the relatively small sample size might constrain the statistical power of our findings. To validate our results and explore potential effect modifications by factors such as age, sex, and ethnicity, larger studies and meta-analyses are necessary.

Conclusion

Our investigation found a significant association between elevated DIIs and increased odds of CAD, as well as severe coronary lesions. These findings suggest that an anti-inflammatory diet may be beneficial in managing CAD. However, given the case-control design of our study, we recommend further well-designed prospective studies in diverse populations to validate these findings and explore the potential causal relationship between dietary inflammation and CAD.
