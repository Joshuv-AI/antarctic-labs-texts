Catechol-O-methyltransferase connects dopamine homeostasis to redox signaling, metal homeostasis, and protein folding in schizophrenia
Authors: Sunil Jamuna Tripathi, Suwarna Chakraborty, Neil B. Wood, Dillon Hoopes, Sarah Barker, Edwin Vázquez-Rosa, Jiu An, Chunxuan Ma, Yuan Hou, Sudarshana M. Sharma, Feixiong Cheng, Bobby Thomas, Benjamin C. Orsburn, Stephen D. Fried, Solomon H. Snyder, Andrew A. Pieper, Bindu D. Paul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceDysregulated dopamine (DA) signaling and impaired redox homeostasis are central to the pathophysiology of schizophrenia (SCZ) and Parkinson’s disease (PD). Here, Tripathi and Chakraborty et al. demonstrate that loss of membrane-bound catechol-O-methyltransferase (MB-COMT), a key degrader of DA in the brain, disrupts cellular metal handling and redox-regulatory pathways and triggers widespread protein misfolding. Such disturbances are observed across both disorders, underscoring a critical role of MB-COMT in DA homeostasis in preventing neuropathology and identifying related pathways as potential therapeutic targets.

Discovered by Julius Axelrod in 1957, catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) is a key enzyme in the metabolism of catecholamines, including dopamine (DA), adrenaline (epinephrine), and noradrenaline (norepinephrine) and it influences neurotransmission, cognition, pain perception, substance use, neuropsychiatric impairment, and behavior (1–6). COMT is one of two primary enzymes responsible for degrading catecholamines and exists as two isoforms generated by different start codons: the membrane bound long form (MB-COMT) and the soluble short form (S-COMT) (7, 8). The COMT gene is organized with distinct upstream promoters that drive the production of separate mRNAs encoding the soluble and membrane-bound isoforms (8–11). The two isoforms are identical in sequence, except for an additional 50 amino acids in the N terminus of MB-COMT harboring a membrane anchoring domain (12). MB-COMT is the dominant isoform in the brain, exhibiting a higher affinity for dopamine than S-COMT and playing a unique role in DA inactivation due to its localization to neuronal cell bodies, axons, and dendrites (9, 13, 14). Notably, MB-COMT is most abundant in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and hippocampus, which emphasizes its important role in these brain regions that govern cognitive function (11).

In most neuronal synapses, the cessation of DA signaling is primarily regulated by reuptake through the DA transporter (DAT), especially in the striatum (15). However, DAT levels are relatively low in the PFC, a region critical for executive function and working memory, making MB-COMT essential for inactivating DA signaling in this area (16–19). COMT directly degrades DA into 3-methoxytyramine (3-MT), utilizing S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) as a methyl donor. COMT also catalyzes the methylation of 3,4-dihydroxyphenyl acetic acid (DOPAC), which is formed through monoamine oxidase (MAO) activity on DA, to produce homovanillic acid (HVA). HVA can additionally be generated from MAO activity on 3-MT, making it the primary product of DA degradation. Consequently, COMT-deficient mice exhibit elevated DA levels specifically in the PFC, while DA levels in other regions, such as the striatum, remain unchanged (20, 21).

Altered COMT activity and DA levels have several pathological consequences relevant to SCZ, a severe, progressive disorder associated with excessive DA signaling and marked by positive symptoms (hallucinations and delusions), negative symptoms (reduced motivation, diminished spontaneous speech, social withdrawal), and cognitive impairment (22–24). Convergent neuroanatomical and neuroimaging studies have established the dorsolateral PFC (DLPFC) as critical in SCZ pathophysiology (25–30), where COMT activity is higher than DAT activity. However, despite extensive research on patients globally, the molecular mechanisms underlying SCZ remain unclear. Epidemiological studies indicate that environmental factors play a significant role, while genetic research has identified a highly polygenic inheritance pattern, including polymorphisms in the COMT gene (2, 31).

The COMT Val158Met polymorphism (a G → A substitution at codon 158) produces a Met variant with reduced thermostability, resulting in significantly reduced MB-COMT enzymatic activity in the brain (14, 32, 33). Thus, in the PFC where DAT is sparse, Met158 carriers show relatively higher DA signaling due to decreased DA catabolism (14, 34). Notably, neuropsychological studies report that Val158 carriers exhibit poorer performance on executive function tests, including working memory, mental flexibility, attention control, problem solving, and response inhibition, relative to Met158 carriers (2, 35). These observations are consistent with the finding that genetic and pharmacologic decrease in COMT activity results in higher prefrontal dopamine transmission (15, 21, 36, 37). Interestingly, first episode psychosis patients who are Val carriers (Val/Val or Val/Met) show reduced executive function and smaller PFC volumes compared to Met/Met individuals experiencing a first psychosis (38). The Val158Met variant also interacts with environmental stressors to further modulate SCZ risk (39–42). In addition to the impact of COMT variations on DA signaling, recent evidence from postmortem human caudate tissue shows that decreased expression of the short form of the D2 dopamine receptor (DRD2) is a risk factor for SCZ (43).

COMT polymorphisms additionally affect individual responses to DA supplementation in Parkinson’s disease (PD). In PD, selective loss of dopaminergic neurons lowers DA levels, exacerbating disease progression (44). Symptoms of PD include bradykinesia, resting tremor, rigidity, and cognitive decline (45). COMT inhibitors are commonly used as adjunctive therapy with L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA) treatment to extend the residence time of DA in synapses, and, when centrally acting, can ameliorate cognitive deficits in PD patients (46–48). However, DA is highly susceptible to oxidation, a characteristic associated with neurodegeneration and neuropsychiatric disorders. Consequently, long-term DA-supplementation therapy can induce oxidative stress, potentially contributing to disease progression (49–53). Particularly when COMT activity is lower, elevated DA levels lead to increased production of 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetaldehyde (DOPAL) (Fig. 1A), an electrophile that can posttranslationally modify proteins and impair their function. Excess DA also causes oxidative damage when MAO deaminates free DA, generating oxidative stress-inducing hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). MAO further produces DOPAL, which is then converted to 3,4-dihydroxyphenylethanol (DOPET) or oxidized to DOPAC by alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) or acetaldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH). DA can also undergo spontaneous oxidation, leading to production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive o-quinones, such as DA o-quinone, aminochrome, and 5,6-dihydroxyindole. The toxic metabolites of DA, including dopamine semiquinones, may modify proteins, alter their activity, and elicit neurotoxicity (54–57).

Given the potential accumulation of these toxic metabolites in the brain due to reduced COMT activity, we characterized the biochemical consequences of MB-COMT deletion in cellular systems to provide insights into the signaling pathways potentially affected by diminished COMT activity in SCZ and during treatment of PD patients with DA-enhancing therapies.

Results

Elevated Oxidative Stress in MB-COMT −/− Cells.

Given that excess DA accumulates in the PFC due to reduced COMT activity and is oxidized into reactive metabolites that can damage cellular components, we assessed cellular stress responses and oxidative stress levels in PC12 cells lacking MB-COMT (MB-COMT −/− cells) (58). PC12 cells are a well-established model of DA biology due to their differentiated neuroendocrine phenotype and intact mechanisms for DA homeostasis, including tyrosine hydroxylase (TH; the rate limiting step in DA synthesis, conversion of L-tyrosine to L-DOPA) and DA receptors (59–64). The morphology, growth, and cellular appearance of the two cell lines were comparable under basal culture conditions. Notably, MB-COMT −/− cells display compromised DA metabolism, characterized by significantly reduced levels of the DA metabolites 3-MT and HVA, along with elevated levels of DOPAC (58).

To analyze the impact of MB-COMT on cellular viability in response to oxidative stress, we treated MB-COMT −/− and MB-COMT +/+ (WT) cells with H2O2 at various concentrations for 24 h and then measured their viability using the 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Fig. 1B). At 200 μM H2O2, the viability of MB-COMT −/− cells was significantly lower than that of WT cells. We also treated cells with increasing concentrations of DA, whose downstream oxidation products can be toxic, and measured cell viability using the MTT assay (Fig. 1C). Similar to treatment with H2O2, the viability of MB-COMT −/− cells was lower in comparison to WT cells. Additionally, oxidative stress levels were markedly higher in MB-COMT −/− cells both during basal conditions and after H2O2 exposure at sublethal concentrations, as assessed by the CellROX green assay (Fig. 1 D and E). Exposure of cells to 10 μM DA also resulted in higher levels of ROS in MB-COMT −/− cells as compared to WT controls (Fig. 1 F and G). We further observed elevated mitochondrial superoxide levels in response to exposure to H2O2 (Fig. 1 H and I) or DA (Fig. 1 J and K).

Finally, protein carbonylation, a marker of oxidative protein damage and impaired stress response (65), was significantly elevated in MB-COMT −/− cells under basal conditions and following H2O2 (Fig. 1 L and M) and DA exposure (Fig. 1 N and O), as measured through the 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) derivatization assay. In this assay, lysates are treated with DNPH, which reacts with carbonyl groups generated on oxidized amino acid side chains to yield stable 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone (DNP) adducts, which can be detected by immunoblotting with anti-DNP antibodies. Overall, the data show that dopamine is a highly potent oxidative stressor, on par or more potent than H2O2, and that the level of stress it imparts is exacerbated by the absence of MB-COMT.

Global Proteomic Analysis of MB-COMT −/− Cells.

To identify pathways affected by MB-COMT loss, we performed an unbiased proteomic comparison between MB-COMT −/− and WT cells using a direct data-independent acquisition (DIA) default workflow. In total, 3,219 proteins were quantified, and 949 were found to be differentially expressed between the two cell lines, with 863 being downregulated and 86 upregulated (P-value < 0.05) (Fig. 2A and Dataset S1), and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant alterations in pathways associated with neurodegeneration, most notably those related to PD and oxidative stress (Fig. 2B).

We next performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses for BP (processes that occur within a cell, an organism, or a population, such as “protein transport”; Fig. 2C), CC (subcellular structures or locations where gene products are active, such as “axon” and “synapse”;Fig. 2D), and MF (specific activities of a gene product, such as “GTP binding” or “glutathione transferase activity”; Fig. 2E). BP analysis revealed changes in protein transport, response to oxidative stress, protein folding, and synaptic vesicle endocytosis (Fig. 2C). CC terms showed enrichment for mitochondria, synapse, post synapse, and neuron projections (Fig. 2D). MF enriched pathways included protein binding, GTP binding, and metal ion binding, among others (Fig. 2E).

Proteins crucial for maintaining redox homeostasis were significantly disrupted in MB-COMT −/− cells. Notable changes included catalase 1 (Cat), Cu-Zn superoxide dismutase (Sod1), glutathione peroxidase 1 (Gpx1), transaldolase (Taldo1), thiosulfate sulfurtransferase (Tst, also called rhodanese) involved in cyanide and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) metabolism, and heme oxygenase 1 (Hmox-1). Key proteins involved in metal homeostasis, such as transferrin receptor (Tfrc), metallothionein 1 (Mt1), metallothionein 2 (Mt2), cystathionine-β-synthase-pair domain divalent metal cation transport mediator 2 (Cnnm2), aconitase-1 (Aco1), the mitochondrial iron-sulfur cluster assembly 1 homolog (Isca1), N-myc downstream-regulated gene 1 (Ndrg1) whose deficiency causes iron overload were also dysregulated in MB-COMT −/− cells, indicating iron dysregulation (Fig. 2F). Other proteins with altered expression included the copper transporting ATPase (Atp7a) and the cyp20a1 member of the cytochrome P450 superfamily of enzymes, suggesting dysregulation of copper homeostasis. Moreover, proteins involved in DA metabolism were also significantly dysregulated in MB-COMT −/− cells, including monoamine oxidase A (MaoA), mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase (Aldh2), and dopamine decarboxylase (Ddc). Overall, multiple pathways related to oxidative stress, synaptic signaling, neurotransmitter disposition, and metal handling that are implicated in SCZ were also found to be disrupted in MB-COMT −/− cells.

At the synapse, key proteins, including Anxa2 (annexin A2), Hpca (hippocalcin), Clic6 (chloride intracellular channel 6), and Stx12 (syntaxin-12), were significantly downregulated, whereas Rab-27A (ras-related protein Rab-27A), Dnm1 (dynamin-1), Stx1a (syntaxin-1A), and Stxbp2 (syntaxin-binding protein 2) were significantly upregulated. These alterations indicate dysregulation of vesicle recycling and endocytosis, disruption of calcium-dependent synaptic plasticity, and impaired function of GABAergic inhibitory synapses, collectively suggesting a synaptic imbalance. In addition to their roles in synaptic signaling, several of the identified proteins also influence redox homeostasis. For instance, Hpca, a calcium sensor, regulates mitochondrial calcium signaling and provides protection against oxidative stress and apoptosis and its downregulation in MB-COMT −/− cells may reflect a diminished capacity to buffer oxidative stress or it may reflect a secondary consequence of broader cellular dysfunction (66). Similarly, reduced expression of retinol binding protein 4 (Rbp4) may indicate altered retinoid metabolism and/or an adaptive response to oxidative stress, particularly under inflammatory conditions (67, 68). Given the association between elevated Rbp4 levels and ROS-generating inflammatory pathways, its downregulation could represent a compensatory mechanism aimed at limiting oxidative damage. Another key protein affected by MB-COMT deletion is Anxa2, which was also significantly downregulated. Anxa2 plays important roles in cellular antioxidant defense, and its depletion has been shown to induce ROS accumulation and promote oxidative stress–mediated cell death. Moreover, Anxa2 interacts with several redox regulators, including Peroxiredoxin 2, and contributes to the regulation of cellular stress responses (69, 70). In addition, we observed an upregulation of Rab-27A, a small GTPase involved in vesicle trafficking, exosome biogenesis, and secretion. Increased Rab-27A expression has been reported in several models of metabolic and oxidative stress and is associated with enhanced exosome production. These exosomes can transport signaling molecules, proteins, and other cargo that contribute to cellular adaptation and stress mitigation. For example, in stroke and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, elevated Rab-27A expression has been linked to increased exosomal secretion of the high-mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1) and modulation of NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2)-dependent signaling pathways that may ultimately influence cellular stress responses (71, 72). Collectively, these proteomics changes are consistent with disrupted redox homeostasis following MB-COMT deletion.

Last, we conducted western blot analysis to confirm the altered expression of key proteins involved in oxidative stress response in cells lacking MB-COMT. Levels of SOD1, catalase, HMOX1, TFRC, and HEBP1 were altered in MB-COMT −/− cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–G). Furthermore, expression of TST, a protein involved in detoxification of cyanide and clearance of the gasotransmitter, H2S, (73), was decreased in cells lacking MB-COMT, which could contribute to elevated oxidative stress (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G).

Redox Imbalance in Schizophrenia Patients.

To further explore the relationship between MB-COMT depletion and human SCZ, we compared our proteomic data with publicly available SCZ databases. In brief, differentially expressed proteins following MB-COMT deletion were matched to their human orthologs and then compared to genes with different transcript levels in the DLPFC of control and SCZ patients (GSE224683). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to assess potential alignment between the proteomic signature of MB-COMT deletion and genes dysregulated in SCZ. Notably, we observed a significant overlap in dysregulated proteins and genes associated with response to oxygen-containing compounds and transmission across chemical synapses, highlighting convergent disruption of redox-related and synaptic pathways in both datasets (Fig. 3 A and B and Dataset S2). In addition, pathways involved in nervous system development, synaptic function, amino acid metabolism, ubiquitination, and protein modification were identified as disrupted common pathways.

Because proteins ultimately execute cellular functions, we complemented the RNA-based comparisons by overlaying our results with human SCZ proteomic databases. We overlaid the protein list from MB-COMT −/− cells onto SCZ-associated proteins reported in a publicly available dataset generated from the DLPFC of normal and SCZ patients (74). Overlapping pathways were identified using enrichment analysis conducted via the Enrichr platform (2024 Web Release) (75), with the internal Reactome 2024 library. Pathway enrichment was considered significant when the Enrichr adjusted P-value was <0.05. “Shared pathways” were defined as Reactome terms that reached statistical significance independently in both datasets. Using this criterion, we identified pathways including response to stress, cellular responses to stimuli, transmission across chemical synapses, and integration of energy metabolism, among others (Fig. 3 C-E).

We next analyzed the expression of key proteins that were dysregulated in the DLPFC of SCZ patients (SI Appendix, Table S1) using western blot analysis. Consistent with our findings in the WT and MB-COMT −/− cell lines, we observed altered expression of the antioxidant enzymes catalase and SOD1 (Fig. 3 F and H). The levels of TST were also decreased in the DLPFC of SCZ patients, indicating a dysregulation of cyanide clearance and H2S metabolism, a messenger molecule in cognition and redox homeostasis that is dysregulated in neurodegeneration (Fig. 3 F and I) (76–82). Similarly, proteins involved in iron homeostasis, such as TFRC and HEBP1, exhibited increased expression in SCZ samples, indicating perturbation in iron homeostasis (Fig. 3 F, J, and K). Moreover, the direction of protein abundance change (either up or down) in SCZ was generally the same as the direction of change in MB-COMT −/− cells.

Loss of MB-COMT Promotes Protein Misfolding.

Because loss of MB-COMT resulted in elevated oxidative stress, which can promote protein misfolding and aggregation, we investigated whether disrupted DA or redox homeostasis induced alterations in protein folding, which may not be reflected as protein abundance differences. We utilized limited-proteolysis mass spectrometry (LiP-MS) to analyze proteome-wide structural changes in MB-COMT −/− cells. LiP-MS detects subtle alterations in protein structure that are reflected in changes in susceptibility to proteinase K, a protease with low sequence specificity but a preference to cleave at solvent-accessible positions (Fig. 4A) (83, 84). In these experiments, we identify both half-tryptic peptides (which represent locations where PK cleaved), and standard tryptic peptides (locations where PK did not cleave). We observed widespread changes in protein structure in the proteome of MB-COMT −/− cells as compared to WT controls under basal conditions. Fig. 4B shows a peptide volcano plot for this LiP-MS dataset, comprising 27,674 quantified peptides that were normalized for protein abundance and FDR-corrected on a per-protein basis (85) (cf. SI Appendix, Tables S2 and S3). We found that 1,508 (or 5.4%) of the peptides were above the threshold of significance to represent sites of structural alteration (>twofold effect size and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P-value < 0.05). The volcano plot shows a clear asymmetry, with half-tryptic peptides (red dots) being generally more abundant in MB-COMT −/− cells, which is suggestive that this proteome has more misfolded protein conformations that are more susceptible to proteolysis (86).

We next compared MB-COMT −/− cells to WT controls in the presence of the oxidant H2O2 (Fig. 4C) and DA (Fig. 4D). When stressed with H2O2, the structural differences between the cell lines’ proteomes was attenuated, with only 628 peptides (out of 29049, or 2.2%) showing significant changes in proteolytic susceptibility. And when stressed with DA, only 487 peptides (out of 28310, or 1.7%) showed significant changes. One interpretation of these data is that the high dose of DA generates oxidative stress that results in misfolding of a group of proteins that share a high degree of overlap with the proteins that misfold in the absence of MB-COMT. Hence, DA treatment does not result in many more proteins getting misfolded in the MB-COMT −/− background because the stress associated with these two effects are similar.

To robustly label a protein structurally perturbed by a treatment, we require the identification of two or more peptides with significant changes in proteolytic susceptibility. By this metric, 275 proteins were structurally altered in MB-COMT −/− cells compared to WT controls under basal conditions (Fig. 4H). This figure is larger than the number of proteins that exhibited altered folding in MB-COMT −/− cells exposed to oxidative stress by treatment with H2O2 or DA (104 and 80 respectively). Hence, these findings suggest that loss of MB-COMT in our cellular model imposes severe oxidative stress with broad consequences for protein structural integrity.

Upon DA treatment, the total number of altered proteins in WT cells (195) was similar to that in MB-COMT −/− cells (176), again suggesting potential redundancy in proteins affected by these effects (SI Appendix, Tables S2 and S3). On the other hand, upon H2O2 treatment, the total number of altered proteins in MB-COMT −/− cells (268) was significantly higher than in WT cells (95; SI Appendix, Table S2-S3). This suggests that the profile of oxidative damage imparted by H2O2 is distinct from (and possibly additive to) that associated with high DA and/or MB-COMT loss.

Analysis of gene ontology (GO) terms, Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), Molecular Function (MF), and KEGG analysis for MB-COMT −/− cells as compared to WT revealed changes in protein folding, redox homeostasis, and cysteine and methionine metabolic pathways (Fig. 4E). Treatment with H2O2 revealed changes in protein folding and refolding, synaptic transmission, protein chaperone activity, and metabolic pathways (Fig. 4F). Treatment with DA uncovered changes involving regulation of protein homo-oligomerization, astrocytic immune response, and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism (Fig. 4G). Overall, in response to either H2O2 or DA exposure, the number of proteins with altered structural perturbations was lower in WT cells as compared to MB-COMT −/− cells (Fig. 4H and SI Appendix, Fig. S2E and Table S2 and S3). Quantitative comparisons of MB-COMT −/− and WT cells treated with H2O2 or DA versus their respective controls, number of proteins with altered structural perturbations and protein abundances, are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–L.

We next inspected more closely the 275 proteins that exhibited altered protease sensitivity when MB-COMT −/− cells were compared to WT cells (Dataset S3). We further modeled some of the relevant structurally altered proteins using AlphaFold2 and labeled sites with inferred structural changes based on proteolytic susceptibility (Fig. 4 I–M and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Structurally altered proteins included those involved in iron handling and regulation, such as iron regulatory proteins 2 (IRP1 2) also known as aconitase 2 (Aco2), whose folding was altered in MB-COMT −/− cells as compared to WT cells under basal conditions. IRP2 is an essential RNA-binding protein that regulates levels of proteins involved in iron uptake, release, and storage, with IRP2 being notably expressed at higher levels than IRP1 in the brain (87). Loss of IRP2 causes motor, somatosensory, and executive dysfunction in mice (88). Additionally, both subunits of ferritin were hits and were among the most structurally altered proteins: 8 out of 14 peptides detected for ferritin heavy-chain (Fth1) and 13 out of 20 peptides detected for ferritin light-chain (Ftl1; Fig. 4I) showed changes in proteolytic susceptibility in cells lacking MB-COMT. Enzymes that are involved in detoxification of oxidative species also showed prominent alteration, suggesting functional engagement (e.g., catalase (9 out of 15 peptides altered), [Cu-Zn] Sod1 (4 out of 48 peptides altered), [Mn] Sod2 (2 out of 9 peptides altered)), and three homologues of peroxiredoxin (Prdx1, Prdx2, and Prdx3). At the organelle level, we found 9 proteins structurally altered in the lysosome (ninefold enriched) and 5 proteins altered in the peroxisome (eightfold enriched), suggesting activation of pathways for protein degradation.

We also noted the presence of structural alteration among proteins involved in the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS), specifically four subunits of the proteasome (Psma7, Psma5, Psmd5, and Psmb4). In addition to these hits, proteins involved in chaperone function were structurally altered. Dysregulation of the chaperone system leads to protein misfolding and toxicity (89). These included the mitochondrial chaperone Hsp60 (and its co-chaperonin Hsp10, and Hsp27 (Hspb1, Fig. 4J) which play important roles in proper protein folding and prevention of aggregation (90, 91). In addition, we observed structural changes in cathepsin B (Ctsb, Fig. 4K) and cystatin B (Cstb, Fig. 4L), two proteins involved in cellular proteostasis. Although neither protein functions as a classical molecular chaperone, both play critical roles in protein quality-control pathways that facilitate the clearance and regulation of misfolded proteins (92, 93). We also observed structural changes in progranulin (Grn, Fig. 4M) across groups. Grn functions as a key regulator of lysosomal homeostasis, facilitating the degradation of damaged and misfolded proteins and thereby contributing to cellular survival, proteostasis, and the regulation of inflammatory responses (94). Furthermore, growing evidence suggests a role of Grn in copathologies of frontotemporal dementia and schizophrenia (95, 96). In cells lacking MB-COMT, these proteins are on the whole more accessible to proteolysis, potentially due to oxidative damage promoting more open conformations (as indicated by the + signs labeling sites in Fig. 4 I–M). We also illustrate in SI Appendix, Fig. S3 the locations of sites with structural changes in cells treated with H2O2 or DA. In general, sites on these proteins are sometimes more susceptible, sometimes less susceptible to PK in MB-COMT −/− cells, reflecting how in the presence of treatments that causes oxidative damage, the effect of MB-COMT −/− is more context-dependent given that the genetic change and the treatments induce similar (partially overlapping) effects on protein folding.

Another protein that exhibited significant alteration (6 out of 23 peptides) was ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase isozyme L1 (Uchl1), a neuronal deubiquitinating enzyme that plays key roles in the ubiquitin–proteasome system, the cell’s machinery for protein quality control. Uchl1 is one of the most abundant proteins in neurons, making up 1 to 2% of total brain protein, and has been linked to neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease, PD, and Traumatic brain injury (97–100). More recently, higher levels of Uchl1 were detected in the plasma of treatment resistant SCZ subjects (101). Thus, loss of MB-COMT leads to changes in protein folding, degradation, and redox imbalance.

Finally, we investigated whether treatment with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) could reverse protein aggregation in MB-COMT −/− cells. Protein aggregation was assessed by measuring the accumulation of total ubiquitinated proteins in the detergent-insoluble fraction. WT and MB-COMT −/− cells were pretreated with NAC (3 mM) for 24 h and subsequently challenged with H₂O₂. Under basal conditions, MB-COMT −/− cells exhibited significantly greater protein aggregation than WT cells. H₂O₂ treatment further increased protein aggregation in both cell lines, with a more pronounced effect in MB-COMT −/− cells. Notably, NAC pretreatment significantly attenuated protein aggregation in MB-COMT −/− cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). These findings support the conclusion that oxidative stress contributes to the enhanced protein aggregation observed in MB-COMT −/− cells and that antioxidant treatment can reverse proteostasis disruption.

Discussion

DA signaling in the brain comprises a complex interplay of signaling cascades intertwined with its metabolism. DA is crucial for motor control, reward processing, cognitive function, and emotional regulation (102). However, the metabolism of DA places neurons under constant oxidative stress, making DA homeostasis and ROS mitigation vital for neuronal survival. Proper DA regulation is achieved through tight control of its biosynthesis, release, diffusion, reuptake, and catabolism. In the PFC, a region critical for executive cognition and working memory, DA degradation is especially important because synapses in this region exhibit minimal expression of DAT (103, 104).

In this study, we analyzed the consequences of depleting MB-COMT, the primary enzyme responsible for clearing DA in the PFC, using cells selectively lacking this enzyme. Our findings demonstrate that MB-COMT depletion leads to increased oxidative stress, as indicated by elevated ROS levels and oxidative damage, both of which are hallmarks of various neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric diseases (105–108). Furthermore, MB-COMT-deficient cells were more susceptible to oxidative stress-inducing agents, such as H2O2. These observations align with studies showing that inhibiting COMT by inhibitors such as tolcapone during DA-supplementation therapy for PD increases production of hydroxyl radicals (109).

We further demonstrate that treating cells with exogenous DA leads to increased levels of ROS, with greater effect in MB-COMT −/− cells compared to WT cells, though the specific proteins that are affected appear to overlap. This treatment models the effects of elevated DA that may occur in PD patients on DA supplementation therapy. The autooxidation of DA takes about 16 h and produces neurotoxic quinones and free radicals (54, 110). Quinone toxicity primarily arises from the ability to react with cellular proteins and small molecule thiols, such as glutathione (GSH) (57, 111). Proteins known to be modified by oxidized DA include parkin (57), DAT (112), TH (113), and the ferroptosis modulator Gpx4 (114). Other studies have shown that incubating PC12 cells with L-DOPA also induces cellular toxicity (115). Here, we show that selective depletion of MB-COMT causes redox imbalance and abnormal responses to oxidative stress in a cellular system characterized by aberrantly high levels of DOPAC and negligible levels of HVA (58).

Our unbiased proteomic comparison of MB-COMT −/− and WT cells revealed differentially expressed proteins, including those involved in redox balance, metal homeostasis, and synaptic function. Analysis of proteomic data revealed extensive dysregulation of iron homeostasis. Proteins involved in iron homeostasis, such as Aco1, ferritin, and transferrin, were differentially expressed. In addition to these proteins, amyloid precursor protein (APP) was upregulated in the MB-COMT −/− cells, indicating iron overload. APP is both regulated by iron (via an iron-responsive element or IRE in its mRNA) and regulates iron (by stabilizing ferroportin), forming a tightly coupled feedback system that becomes dysregulated in neurodegenerative disease (116, 117). Knocking out COMT increases cytosolic DA and enhances DA oxidation, generating ROS that liberate iron from ferritin and iron–sulfur clusters. The resulting rise in free iron provides a mechanistic basis for the IRE-dependent induction of APP. Thus, APP induction in WT cells likely reflects a compensatory response to iron dysregulation and oxidative stress. Interestingly, our LiP-MS study revealed a misfolding of the APP protein, which may affect iron trafficking, an aspect for future investigation.

MB-COMT −/− cells further exhibited a significant downregulation of thiosulfate sulfurtransferase (TST), a mitochondrial sulfurtransferase essential for sulfide oxidation, iron–sulfur cluster maintenance, cyanide detoxification, and redox homeostasis (118, 119). Reduced TST expression suggests impaired mitochondrial sulfur detoxification and destabilization of Fe-S-dependent respiratory enzymes, increasing oxidative stress. In COMT deficiency, this would likely amplify DA-driven ROS, promote protein misfolding, and worsen the proteotoxic stress observed in our dataset. An overlay of the proteomic profiles with publicly available databases for SCZ identified several overlaps, with oxidative stress response and transmission across chemical synapses emerging as key dysregulated pathways held in common. This finding was further validated by western blot analysis of both our cellular model and postmortem tissues from SCZ patients.

Last, oxidative stress further impairs proteostasis and protein folding, and we detected conformational alterations of proteins using LiP-MS in the cells lacking MB-COMT. LiP-MS analysis identified dysregulation of pathways involved in protein folding and refolding, cellular redox balance, response to ethanol, cytoskeletal architecture, and synapse formation. Altered folding of chaperone proteins was also observed in our study. Interestingly autoantibodies against chaperone proteins have been detected in SCZ (120, 121). Hsp27 functions in an ATP-independent manner to prevent aggregation of proteins and oxidative stress by promoting proper folding of enzymes such as glutathione S-transferase (91). Due to its-ATP-independent activity, Hsp27 is especially effective during energy deficient states (122). Prior studies have shown that the C-terminal domain of Hsp27 helps in targeting α-synuclein for degradation, while the N-terminal end prevents amyloid fibril nucleation (123). Therefore, treatments that mitigate oxidative stress and protein misfolding, or that normalize iron homeostasis, may have therapeutic potential in these conditions. One approach to jointly address these needs could involve normalizing MB-COMT levels or other aspects of DA homeostasis. In situations where increasing DA is therapeutically beneficial, such as DA-supplementation therapy for PD, adjunctive treatments that reduce oxidative stress and/or stimulate autophagy-mediated removal of misfolded proteins may also provide long-term benefits.

Materials and Methods

Cell Cultures and Reagents.

The PC12 MB-COMT −/− cells and their wild-type controls were previously described (58). These cell lines were generated using CRISPR–Cas9 gene-editing technology with three guide RNAs (gRNAs), as reported earlier and exhibit similar morphological characteristics under basal cell culture conditions (58). All chemicals were from Sigma unless mentioned otherwise. Cells were grown in DMEM, supplemented with 10% horse serum, 5% fetal bovine serum, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. MB-COMT −/− cells and their wild-type controls were treated with either different doses of oxidant hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), dopamine (DA), or N-Acetyl cysteine (NAC).

Additional details of reagents and methods are provided in SI Appendix.
