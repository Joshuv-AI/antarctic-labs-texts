The Weston Score in coronary artery calcification among patients with End-Stage Renal Disease
Authors: Jia Fu, Jin Liao, LiMan Long, HaoChen Yang, ShaXi OuYang, KangHan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the relationship between the Weston Score (WS) and the degree of stenosis on coronary angiography (CAG) in patients with End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD), and to investigate whether the WS for coronary artery calcification (CAC) can aid in the early diagnosis of coronary artery disease.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed chest computed tomography (CT) scans from 105 ESRD patients who underwent CAG between January 2021 and January 2025. The presence and extent of calcium in the main coronary arteries—the left main coronary artery (LMCA), left anterior descending (LAD), left circumflex (LCX), and right coronary artery (RCA)—were visually assessed. The WS (range 0–12) was calculated by summing the scores for each vessel, with a score of WS ≥ 7 indicating severe calcification. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess the correlation between the WS and the degree of coronary stenosis (< 50% vs. ≥50%). The predictive value of the WS for significant stenosis was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Agreement between the WS and CAG findings was assessed with the Kappa test. Clinical data were compared between groups based on the WS.ResultsAmong the 105 patients, the majority were male (79.05%), with a mean age of 63.44 ± 9.30 years. Unstable angina was the most common cardiovascular event (44.76%), and hypertension was the most frequent comorbidity (82.86%). CAG revealed that severe four-vessel disease (Grade IV) was most common (79.05%), with the LAD being the most severely affected vessel. WS assessment showed the highest calcification prevalence in the LAD (95.24%), with 69% classified as severe. The LMCA had the lowest calcification prevalence (42.86%), with mostly mild calcification. The LCX and RCA had intermediate prevalence, with the LCX having a higher proportion of moderate-to-severe calcification than the RCA. Significant coronary stenosis (≥ 50%) was present in 90.48% of patients, and 66.31% of these had a WS ≥ 5. All 10 patients with stenosis < 50% had a WS < 5. Spearman analysis confirmed significant positive correlations between the WS and stenosis degree for all coronary branches (all P < 0.05), with varying correlation strengths. ROC analysis indicated the highest diagnostic performance for the LMCA (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.878, P < 0.001), followed by the LCX (AUC = 0.737, P < 0.001). The LAD showed statistical significance (AUC = 0.683, P = 0.009), while the RCA had the weakest predictive ability (AUC = 0.608, P = 0.071). The combined WS for all four vessels predicted significant stenosis with an AUC of 0.902 (P < 0.001). The optimal diagnostic cutoff was a score of 5, yielding a sensitivity of 66.3% and specificity of 100%. Furthermore, patients in the WS ≥ 5 group had a lower mean age than those in the WS < 5 group (P = 0.034); dialysis vintage was significantly longer (P = 0.009); and levels of troponin I, creatinine, and serum calcium were all higher than those in the WS < 5 group (all P < 0.05).ConclusionWS is significantly positively associated with coronary stenosis severity in ESRD patients. It provides a reliable assessment for LMCA and LCX stenosis but has limited value for the RCA. A WS cutoff of ≥ 5 offers improved identification of coronary stenosis in these patients and can serve as a preliminary screening tool, although CAG remains essential for definitive diagnosis. Age, dialysis vintage, troponin I, creatinine, and serum calcium levels may be associated with CAC in ESRD.

Introduction

End-stage renal disease (ESRD), the final stage of chronic kidney disease, is often associated with complex and severe health issues, among which cardiovascular complications are a leading cause of mortality [1, 2]. Coronary artery lesions are particularly significant, and coronary artery calcification (CAC), as a major manifestation of these lesions, is closely linked to the occurrence and prognosis of cardiovascular events [3]. In ESRD patients, factors such as disordered calcium-phosphate metabolism and secondary hyperparathyroidism contribute to a notably higher incidence and severity of CAC compared to the general population [4]. Therefore, accurate assessment and timely intervention for CAC in ESRD patients are critically important.

In recent years, non-invasive techniques for assessing CAC have attracted considerable research attention internationally, particularly in the ESRD population. Although electrocardiogram-gated non-contrast chest CT (NCCT), the current clinical gold standard, is widely used in the general population, its applicability in ESRD patients faces significant challenges. This is primarily due to the necessity of iodinated contrast agents, which can exacerbate renal impairment through various mechanisms [5], thereby limiting its use in this population. With advances in medical imaging, chest computed tomography (CT) has been increasingly utilized for the detection and evaluation of CAC due to its non-invasive nature and efficiency [6]. Substantial evidence supports the reliable accuracy of non-contrast, non-gated conventional chest CT (NCCT) in detecting and quantitatively assessing CAC [7]. The Weston Score (WS), based on NCCT, offers an effective method for evaluating coronary calcification [8]. Multiple comparative studies have demonstrated a high concordance between WS and the gold standard electrocardiogram-gated CT [9, 10], establishing it as an ideal alternative for CAC assessment in ESRD patients. The WS scoring system quantifies calcification in the main coronary branches—including the left main, left anterior descending (LAD), left circumflex (LCX), and right coronary arteries—yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 12, which reflects the overall coronary calcification burden [11]. Compared to coronary angiography (CAG), the WS score is non-invasive, convenient, highly reproducible, and more suitable for long-term follow-up and monitoring in ESRD patients.

Although some studies have explored the application of the WS score in coronary artery disease [8], research focusing specifically on the ESRD population remains limited. ESRD patients exhibit unique pathophysiological characteristics; the mechanisms, progression, and relationship to cardiovascular events of coronary calcification in this group may differ from those in the general population. Thus, an in-depth investigation into the clinical value of the WS score for coronary calcification in ESRD patients is of significant importance for enhancing the understanding, prevention, and management of cardiovascular complications in this population.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

Patients hospitalized at Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital between January 2021 and January 2025 who underwent CAG were screened. The inclusion criteria were: [1] patients aged > 18 years; [2] patients meeting the KDIGO Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Evaluation and Management of Chronic Kidney Disease, with an estimated glomerular filtration rate persistently < 15 mL/min/1.73 m² or already receiving maintenance renal replacement therapy (hemodialysis/peritoneal dialysis); [3] patients with clinically suspected coronary artery disease requiring CAG and meeting at least one of the following criteria: typical angina symptoms (retrosternal crushing pain lasting ≥ 5 min), electrocardiogram showing myocardial ischemia changes (ST-segment depression ≥ 0.1 mV or T-wave inversion ≥ 0.2 mV), or echocardiography suggesting segmental wall motion abnormality; [4] patients who underwent non-contrast chest CT (NCCT) with a slice thickness ≤ 5 mm within 30 days prior to CAG; [5] patients with complete clinical medical records, including blood biochemical tests and follow-up data.

The exclusion criteria were: [1] patients aged < 18 years; [2] patients who did not undergo NCCT within 30 days before CAG; [3] patients with severe artifacts on chest CT images or an incomplete scanning range affecting coronary artery assessment; [4] patients with a history of coronary artery bypass grafting or percutaneous coronary intervention; [5] patients with moderate-to-severe valvular heart disease, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, congenital heart disease, or other serious cardiac structural abnormalities; [6] patients diagnosed with hematological diseases, autoimmune diseases, or other malignant tumors; [7] patients with missing clinical data.

Case screening was conducted strictly according to the study protocol by two attending physicians independently. Discrepant cases were adjudicated by a panel of experts via discussion. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital, and a waiver of informed consent was granted.

Patient grouping

According to the NCCT imaging characteristics and the WS visual scoring system, the 105 ESRD patients were divided into two groups: a WS < 7 group (n = 70, 66.67%) and a WS ≥ 7 group (n = 35, 33.33%). This grouping criterion was reviewed and confirmed by a multidisciplinary expert committee as consistent with the characteristics of vascular calcification in ESRD patients. The calcification severity in each major coronary artery branch (left main coronary artery [LMCA], LAD, LCX, and right coronary artery [RCA]) was further subcategorized, classified according to WS scores of 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively.

Based on the CAG results, the 105 patients were divided into two groups: a < 50% stenosis group (n = 10, 9.52%) and a ≥ 50% stenosis group (n = 95, 90.48%). This grouping scheme strictly adhered to current clinical assessment standards for coronary artery disease to ensure the relevance of the study findings to clinical practice. Stenosis severity was measured using a combination of visual estimation and quantitative coronary analysis (QCA), independently assessed by two experienced interventional cardiologists. Discrepant results were resolved through consensus.

Collection of clinical data

Clinical data collection included the following, obtained through the hospital’s electronic medical record system: [1] Demographic characteristics: sex, age, height, and body mass index (BMI); dialysis treatment parameters: dialysis modality (hemodialysis/peritoneal dialysis) and dialysis vintage (calculated from the initiation of dialysis); [2] Medical history: hypertension, diabetes, and history of stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke confirmed by cranial CT/Magnetic Resonance Imaging); [3] Laboratory parameters obtained within 24 h of admission: C-reactive protein, troponin I, B-type natriuretic peptide, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, uric acid, serum calcium, serum phosphorus, serum potassium, parathyroid hormone, total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and hemoglobin; [4] Types of cardiovascular events (classified according to European Society of Cardiology guidelines); electrocardiographic findings (e.g., ST segment and T wave changes, arrhythmias); and echocardiographic parameters (left ventricular ejection fraction, wall motion abnormalities, etc.). Data extraction was performed using electronic forms via dual independent entry. Key clinical indicators and imaging results were reviewed and confirmed by a senior physician, and discrepant data were reconciled by a third researcher.

Non-contrast chest CT examination

Chest CT data were acquired using a Philips Brilliance 16-slice spiral CT scanner. The scanning parameters were standardized as follows: tube voltage 120 kV, tube current 250 mA, slice thickness 5.00 mm, and pitch 5.0. The scanning range extended continuously from the lung apex to the lung base. All imaging data were retrospectively retrieved from the hospital’s Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) to ensure data integrity and traceability.

Image assessment was independently performed by two radiologists, each with over 10 years of experience in cardiovascular imaging. The evaluation process strictly followed a double-blind design, ensuring the physicians were unaware of the patient clinical data and CAG results. Calcification in the four major coronary arteries—namely, the LMCA, LAD, LCX, and RCA—was graded according to the internationally recognized WS visual scoring system: Score 0: No visually detectable calcification along the entire coronary artery. Score 1: Focal calcification, appearing as an isolated high-density focus (CT value ≥ 130 HU, extent ≤ 2 mm). Score 2: An intermediate state between Score 1 and Score 3 (calcification extent > 2 mm without forming a halo sign). Score 3: Diffuse calcification accompanied by a typical “halo sign” artifact (a low-density ring surrounding the calcified focus). The scores for each vascular branch were summed to obtain a total score (range 0–12). The final results required consensus through cross-verification by both readers. For cases with scoring discrepancies (approximately 12%), a third senior imaging expert was consulted for review. Inter-observer consistency was periodically assessed during the study (Kappa value ≥ 0.82), ensuring the reliability of the evaluations. Results

Patient baseline characteristics and vascular stenosis

This study finally enrolled 105 ESRD patients for clinical data analysis. The detailed results are shown in Table 1. The cohort included 83 males (79.05%) and 22 females (20.95%), with a mean age of 63.44 ± 9.30 years. According to the classification of admission cardiovascular events, there were 47 cases (44.76%) of unstable angina, 9 cases (8.57%) of acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, 25 cases (23.81%) of acute non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, and 24 cases (22.86%) of other cardiovascular events. Regarding comorbidities, 55 patients (52.38%) were smokers, 87 (82.86%) had hypertension, 43 (40.95%) had diabetes mellitus, and 25 (23.81%) had a history of cerebral infarction. CAG results demonstrated that the LAD had the highest proportion of severe stenosis (Grade IV, ≥ 75%) at 60.00%, while the proportion of mild stenosis (Grade I, < 25%) was 10.48%. The LMCA was predominantly affected by mild stenosis (Grade I, 70.48%), with the lowest proportion of severe stenosis (Grade IV, 5.71%). In the distributions of stenosis severity for the LCX and RCA, moderate-to-severe stenoses accounted for higher proportions, with severe stenosis rates of 44.76% for the LCX and 43.81% for the RCA. Overall, among the four coronary arteries, severe stenosis (Grade IV) was the most prevalent finding (79.05%), while mild stenosis (Grade I) accounted for only 5.71%. This indicates that the coronary artery disease in these ESRD patients is primarily characterized by severe stenosis, warranting particular clinical attention to the assessment and intervention of the LAD (Table 2; Fig. 3).

WS score distribution in ESRD patients

Based on chest CT results, WS scores were assigned to quantify CAC distribution across the major vessels in the 105 ESRD patients. The results revealed that the LAD had the highest calcification prevalence, observed in 100 out of 105 patients (95.24%). Among these, 69 patients (69.00%) exhibited severe calcification (score = 3), indicating that the LAD was the most frequently involved segment and also the most severely calcified. In contrast, the LMCA had the lowest calcification prevalence, present in only 45 patients (42.86%), with calcification being predominantly mild (score = 1, 24 patients, 53.33%). This suggests relative sparing of the LMCA with generally less severe calcification. The calcification prevalence in the LCX and RCA was intermediate, found in 69 (65.71%) and 55 (52.38%) patients, respectively. However, the proportion of moderate-to-severe calcification (score ≥ 2) was notably higher in the LCX than in the RCA, indicating differences in calcification severity between these two vessels despite their similar overall occurrence rates. Relationship between WS score and CAG stenosis severity

The relationship between CAC and stenosis severity was further analyzed using the WS scoring system. The results showed that among the 105 ESRD patients, 90.48% (n = 95) had significant coronary stenosis (≥ 50%), and among these, 66.31% (n = 63) had a WS score ≥ 5. In contrast, all 10 patients with stenosis < 50% had WS scores < 5 (Fig. 5A). Analysis of individual coronary branches revealed the following patterns: LMCA: Among the 60 patients with a WS score of 0, 56 (93.33%) had Grade I stenosis (< 25%) and 3 (5.00%) had Grade II stenosis (25–49%) (Fig. 5B). The proportion of severe stenosis (Grade IV, ≥ 75%) significantly increased with higher WS scores. For instance, 5 out of 15 patients (33.33%) with a WS score of 3 exhibited Grade IV stenosis. LAD: A clear gradient was observed between the WS score and stenosis severity (Fig. 5C). Among 69 patients with a WS score of 3, 50 (72.46%) had Grade IV stenosis, whereas only 1 out of 5 patients (20.00%) with a WS score of 0 had Grade IV stenosis. LCX Artery: The proportion of severe stenosis was significantly higher in the high WS score group. Specifically, 15 out of 25 patients (60.00%) with a WS score of 3 had Grade IV stenosis (Fig. 5D). RCA: Similarly, 13 out of 19 patients (68.42%) with a WS score of 3 had Grade IV stenosis (Fig. 5E). Subsequent Spearman rank correlation analysis demonstrated a statistically significant positive correlation between the WS score and the stenosis severity grade for each major coronary vessel (Fig. 6, all P < 0.05). This indicates a trend of increasing coronary stenosis severity with higher WS scores, confirming the clinical utility of the WS scoring system for evaluating coronary artery disease in ESRD patients. The strength of the correlation varied among the different vascular branches, which may be related to their distinct anatomical and hemodynamic characteristics. This finding suggests that the clinical interpretation of WS scores requires comprehensive judgement considering the specific vessel involved.

Diagnostic performance of the WS score for stenosis in individual coronary branches and the overall coronary tree

ROC curve analysis was performed to systematically evaluate the predictive value of the WS score for stenosis severity in each coronary artery branch. The results demonstrated that the WS score for the LMCA had the most notable diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.878 (P < 0.001), indicating a highly accurate predictive ability for LMCA stenosis. The WS score for the LCX artery also showed good diagnostic value, with an AUC of 0.737 (P < 0.001). The AUC for the LAD was 0.683 (P = 0.009); while its predictive performance was relatively limited, it remained statistically significant. In contrast, the predictive ability of the WS score for the RCA was the weakest, with an AUC of 0.608 (P = 0.071), which did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 7A-D). These results confirm that the predictive performance of the WS scoring system varies markedly across different coronary branches, being most reliable for the LMCA and LCX, while its value for the RCA is relatively limited. Furthermore, the diagnostic value of the total WS score for significant stenosis in the coronary tree was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 7E, the AUC for predicting significant coronary stenosis (≥ 50%) was 0.902 (P < 0.001), indicating good overall diagnostic performance. Analysis using the Youden index determined that a WS score cut-off value of 5 provided the best predictive performance, yielding a sensitivity of 66.3% and a specificity of 100%. A consistency check was performed using this optimal diagnostic cut-off value (WS ≥ 5). The results showed that the Kappa value for this new threshold (0.273) was markedly higher than that for the original threshold (0.1). Furthermore, using the WS ≥ 5 cut-off resulted in significantly fewer false negatives (32 cases) for identifying stenosis ≥ 50% compared to the original threshold (60 cases), while both thresholds produced 0 false positives for identifying stenosis < 50%. This suggests that the optimal diagnostic cut-off value (WS ≥ 5) has a superior ability to identify coronary stenosis in these patients and can serve as a reference for initial clinical screening. However, confirmation by CAG remains necessary for a definitive diagnosis. Details are provided in Table 4.

Comparison of clinical characteristics between groups stratified by the WS score diagnostic cut-off of 5

Patients were divided into a WS < 5 group (n = 42) and a WS ≥ 5 group (n = 63) according to the diagnostic cut-off value. A comparison of clinical characteristics between the two groups is summarized in Table 5. Regarding age, the mean age of patients in the WS ≥ 5 group (61.92 ± 9.77 years) was significantly lower than that in the WS < 5 group (65.71 ± 8.15 years) (P = 0.034), indicating a significant age difference between the groups and suggesting that age may be a factor influencing CAC in ESRD patients. For dialysis vintage, the median dialysis vintage in the WS ≥ 5 group (36.0 months) was significantly higher than that in the WS < 5 group (21.0 months) (P = 0.009), demonstrating a highly statistically significant difference and further suggesting that the duration of dialysis may be closely associated with the development and progression of CAC. The mean troponin I concentration was higher in the WS ≥ 5 group (3.67 ± 9.13 ng/mL) compared to the WS < 5 group (1.05 ± 3.22 ng/mL) (P = 0.041), suggesting that elevated troponin I levels may be related to the severity of CAC in ESRD patients. The mean serum creatinine level was higher in the WS ≥ 5 group (841.99 ± 286.60 µmol/L) than in the WS < 5 group (711.30 ± 290.62 µmol/L) (P = 0.026), indicating a statistically significant difference, which may reflect a connection between the degree of renal impairment and CAC. The mean serum calcium concentration was higher in the WS ≥ 5 group (2.22 ± 0.36 mmol/L) compared to the WS < 5 group (2.10 ± 0.20 mmol/L) (P = 0.032), suggesting that abnormal serum calcium levels might also be involved in the coronary calcification process in ESRD patients. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups for the following variables: sex, smoking history, hypertension, diabetes, history of cerebral infarction, C-reactive protein, hemoglobin, B-type natriuretic peptide, blood urea nitrogen, uric acid, serum phosphorus, serum potassium, parathyroid hormone, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, total cholesterol, and triglycerides.

Discussion

ESRD patients exhibit a significantly higher incidence and severity of CAC compared to the general population, attributable to factors such as calcium-phosphate metabolism imbalances associated with CKD-MBD, a chronic inflammatory state driven by uremic toxin accumulation, and aberrant molecular regulation driving the phenotypic switching of vascular smooth muscle cells to osteoblast-like cells [13–15]. This pathological alteration not only reduces vascular compliance and promotes left ventricular hypertrophy but is also strongly associated with cardiovascular events like myocardial infarction and arrhythmia, representing the leading cause of mortality in ESRD patients [16, 17]. Consequently, early identification and quantification of CAC are crucial for cardiovascular risk assessment and optimizing intervention strategies in this population. The current clinical standard for CAC assessment primarily relies on the Agatston score from electrocardiogram-gated CT [18]. However, the Agatston score faces limitations including the need for iodinated contrast, complex procedures, and high costs. NCCT, as a non-invasive, low-radiation imaging modality that requires no contrast agent, is already part of the routine examination protocol for ESRD patients. It allows for simultaneous detection and quantification of CAC, avoiding additional radiation exposure and financial burden associated with dedicated scans [19, 20]. The WS, a standardized semi-quantitative method for NCCT, offers advantages of operational simplicity and no requirement for specialized equipment [21]. Despite the convenience and safety profile making NCCT an ideal choice for CAC evaluation in ESRD patients, a validated visual scoring system tailored to the specific pathological features of this population is lacking. Existing literature predominantly focuses on the general population or patients with stable coronary artery disease. Given the distinct nature of vascular calcification in ESRD, the clinical application of visual scores carries significant uncertainty [22]. The present study explored the diagnostic value of the WS in ESRD patients and its association with coronary stenosis, providing experimental evidence to address this clinical dilemma, thereby offering a safe and feasible cardiovascular risk assessment tool for this high‑risk population.

The core findings of this study showed that coronary lesions in ESRD patients were predominantly severe stenosis, with vessel‑specific distribution and severity of CAC. The WS was significantly positively correlated with the degree of coronary stenosis, and its predictive performance varied across different coronary branches: it had higher predictive value for the left main coronary artery and left circumflex artery, but limited value for the right coronary artery. A WS cutoff of ≥ 5 was identified as the optimal preliminary screening threshold for coronary stenosis in this population. From an anatomical perspective, the right coronary artery exhibits significant individual variability, with greater variation in its course, branch angles, and vessel diameter than other coronary branches, leading to high heterogeneity in calcification distribution. Particularly at vessel turning points and bifurcations, the accuracy of calcification assessment is easily compromised [23–25]. Furthermore, the physiological curvature of the RCA and motion artifacts from cardiac contraction pose greater challenges for the identification and quantification of calcification features on CT images [26]. In addition, medial calcification is the main pathological feature in ESRD patients. This pattern of calcification results in an incomplete correspondence between the degree of calcification and the degree of luminal stenosis. Meanwhile, the resolution limitations of NCCT may miss tiny calcified foci, which is a major reason for the relatively low agreement between the WS and the gold standard of coronary angiography. Previous research in the general population suggested that a WS score ≥ 7 predicts severe coronary calcification [27]. Further analysis in this study revealed that a WS score ≥ 5 predicted coronary stenosis ≥ 50% with a sensitivity of 66.3% and a specificity of 100%, while significantly reducing the number of false-negative cases [32 vs. 60 with the original threshold (≥ 7)]. Overall agreement increased to a moderate level (Kappa = 0.273, P < 0.001), confirming its clinical application value within this specific score range.

Group analysis of ESRD patients using a WS score cut-off of 5 revealed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in several clinical indicators between the two groups. Patients with WS scores ≥ 5 were characterized by younger age, longer dialysis vintage, and higher levels of troponin I and serum creatinine. This suggests that long-term dialysis may accelerate CAC progression by promoting calcium-phosphate deposition, exacerbating the microinflammatory state, and inducing myocardial injury [28, 29]. Elevated serum calcium levels directly promote vascular calcification through multiple mechanisms, including: activation of calcium-sensing receptors in vascular smooth muscle cells promoting osteogenic differentiation; facilitation of hydroxyapatite crystal formation; inhibition of the activity of natural calcification inhibitors such as Matrix Gla Protein; and activation of pro-calcific gene expression via calcium-signaling pathways [30]. These abnormal changes collectively form a self-perpetuating vicious cycle: deteriorating renal function leads to calcium-phosphate metabolism disorders that promote vascular calcification, which in turn increases cardiovascular burden and further impairs renal function [31].

Nevertheless, several limitations of the WS scoring system for assessing CAC and stenosis severity warrant consideration. First, the WS itself has inherent sensitivity and specificity limitations, with insufficient ability to identify early, atypical calcified lesions and micro‑calcifications, potentially underestimating the actual degree of disease, and is affected by the specific calcification pattern in ESRD patients. Second, inherent drawbacks of NCCT imaging, such as limited scan resolution and cardiac motion artifacts, further affect the accuracy of the score. Third, the current WS scoring system does not incorporate vascular functional parameters or ESRD‑specific indicators such as calcium‑phosphate metabolism, making it difficult to fully reflect the impact of calcification on coronary physiological function and limiting individualized application of the score. Future research should focus on optimizing the scoring system, establishing novel assessment models incorporating three‑dimensional distribution characteristics of calcification, refining scoring standards adapted to the pathological features of ESRD, and combining multi‑modal examinations such as CT angiography and intravascular ultrasound to further improve the comprehensiveness and reliability of CAC evaluation, thereby promoting the widespread clinical application of the WS in ESRD patients.

In summary, the WS scoring system, by quantifying CAC distribution, effectively reflects the severity of coronary artery stenosis in ESRD patients, demonstrating particular clinical value for assessing lesions in the left main and LCX arteries. Combined with the screening cutoff of WS ≥ 5, it can effectively optimize cardiovascular risk stratification in ESRD patients, provide a reliable reference for early intervention, and is of great significance for improving cardiovascular outcomes and reducing mortality in this population.

CAG

Standard CAG was performed via transradial or transfemoral access by a specialized team of interventional cardiologists certified in national cardiovascular interventional therapy. Under the guidance of digital subtraction angiography, the radial or femoral artery was punctured using the Seldinger technique. After placing a 6 F arterial sheath, selective coronary cannulation was performed using Judkins catheters (JL4.0 for the left coronary artery, JR4.0 for the RCA). A non-ionic iodinated contrast agent (iodixanol, 320 mgI/mL) was injected at a flow rate of 4–6 mL/s, with a total volume of 8–12 mL per injection. Vessel opacification was achieved using multiple standard projections (6 views for the left coronary artery, 2 views for the RCA). Lesion assessment was independently conducted by two experienced interventional cardiologists. Stenosis severity was measured using QCA software (CAAS 5.11, Pie Medical Imaging). For vessels with multi-segmental lesions, the most severely stenotic segment was used for classification, according to the standards of the International Cardiovascular Interventional Society. The stenosis severity was graded as follows: Grade I: luminal stenosis < 25%; Grade II: luminal stenosis 25%–49%; Grade III: luminal stenosis 50%–74%; Grade IV: luminal stenosis ≥ 75%. A hemodynamically significant stenosis was defined as a luminal diameter stenosis ≥ 50% (i.e., Grade III and IV lesions), meeting the diagnostic criteria for obstructive coronary artery disease [32]. A blinded review process was implemented for imaging evaluation. All CT images and CAG results were independently assessed by two imaging specialists for consistency checking. Cases with discrepant assessments were adjudicated by a third senior interventional cardiologist, with the final result requiring consensus from the expert panel.

Statistical analysis

Based on pilot study data and previous literature, the expected sensitivity of the WS score for detecting significant coronary stenosis (≥ 50%) was set at 80%, with a margin of error of 10% and a confidence level of 95% (α = 0.05). The minimum sample size was calculated using the formula n = Z²_{α/2} × p(1-p)/E² (where Zɑ/2 = 1.96, p = 0.8, E = 0.1), yielding a result of 62 subjects. Accounting for a potential 20% rate of loss to follow-up or missing data, the target sample size was increased to 78. In this study, 105 patients were ultimately enrolled, meeting the analytical requirements.

Data analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics version 27.0. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median (Q1, Q3) and were compared using non-parametric tests. Categorical variables are described as frequency (percentage) and were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Inter-observer agreement for WS scoring was evaluated using the Kappa statistic, interpreted as follows: Kappa < 0.4 indicated poor agreement; 0.4 ≤ Kappa < 0.75 indicated moderate agreement; and Kappa ≥ 0.75 indicated good agreement. The diagnostic performance of the WS score for stratifying coronary stenosis risk was assessed by constructing a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The area under the curve (AUC) was interpreted as follows: 0.5–0.7, limited diagnostic value; 0.7–0.9, moderate diagnostic value; >0.9, high diagnostic value. The optimal diagnostic cut-off value was determined by maximizing the Youden index. The correlation between the WS score and the degree of coronary stenosis was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation, treating the WS score as an ordinal categorical variable. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For multiple comparisons, the significance level was adjusted using the Bonferroni correction to ensure the reliability of the findings.
