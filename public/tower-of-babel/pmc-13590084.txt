Combined supplementation with Bacillus subtilis and tea polyphenols alleviates high-fat diet-induced fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome in laying hens by regulating the gut–liver axis
Authors: Ming Qin, Cai Ma, Yuxin Zhang, Guodong Li, Zengguang Wang, Mingzhi Liang, Jingyu Wang, Xuefeng Zhang, Wenjing Huo, Ruili Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) poses a significant threat to the health and productivity of laying hens, characterized by hepatic steatosis and disrupted lipid metabolism. Bacillus subtilis (BS) and tea polyphenols (TP) have been reported to improve laying performance and modulate lipid metabolism. This study evaluated the effects of BS and TP supplementation on mitigating FLHS through physiology, microbiomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses. A total of 150 fifty-week-old laying hens were divided into three groups, with five replicates per group and ten hens per replicate: the control group (normal diet, ND), model group (high-fat diet, HFD), and treatment group (HFD + 300 mg/kg BS and 300 mg/kg TP, HFD+BS/TP). The results demonstrated that the addition of BS and TP effectively mitigated lipid metabolism disorders in laying hens consuming a high-fat diet. Supplementation with BS/TP altered the dysbiosis caused by the high-fat diet by reshaping gut microbial communities and decreasing the abundance of Collinsella and ASF356, thus restoring balance. Serum metabolomic analysis indicated that eight differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) involved in lipid metabolism were identified. Transcriptomic analysis identified six differentially expressed genes (DEGs) linked to lipid metabolism. Both DEMs and DEGs were primarily co-enriched in progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation. Overall, our findings showed that dietary supplementation with BS/TP could alleviate hepatic metabolic disturbances associated with FLHS in aged laying hens by modulating the gut-liver axis. This underscores the considerable potential of the BS/TP as an effective feed additive for preventing and alleviating the adverse effects of a high-fat diet.

Introduction

As laying hens progress into the later stages of their production cycle, both their performance and egg quality begin to deteriorate (Arulnathan et al., 2024). Importantly, the livers of these hens experience increased hepatic lipid accumulation as they age, leading to varying degrees of damage that can escalate to FLHS. This condition is characterized by the excessive buildup of lipids in the liver and abdomen, resulting in hepatic steatosis, liver rupture, inflammatory responses, and bleeding within the liver (Miao et al., 2021; Zhuang et al., 2019). Such complications lead to declines in egg production, fertilization rates, and hatchability (Zhang et al., 2018), ultimately impacting economic returns. The development of FLHS is linked to an imbalance in lipid metabolism caused by various factors, including nutritional intake, metabolic processes, environmental conditions, hormonal influences, and genetic predispositions, with disorders in lipid metabolism and hepatic lipid accumulation serving as the primary contributors (Feng et al., 2021). Despite this, cage systems continue to dominate the egg production sector, with approximately 74% of caged laying hens succumbing to FLHS, resulting in significant financial losses (Shini et al., 2019). Consequently, it is crucial to investigate strategies to alleviate liver damage in hens affected by FLHS to maintain consistent egg production and ensure quality throughout the laying period.

Natural dietary supplements, such as probiotics and TP, are increasingly recognized as promising options for enhancing the health and productivity of laying hens. Moreover, evidence suggests that combining polyphenols with probiotic bacteria may lead to enhanced health benefits, as reviewed by Banerjee and Dhar (2019). TP, the primary bioactive components found in tea, have been extensively studied for their various functional properties (Mithul et al., 2021). Their well-known antihyperlipidemic effects, along with documented antioxidant properties, liver-protective qualities, and anti-obesity effects, have garnered significant attention (Xing et al., 2019). Orita et al. (2025) reported that daily intake of green tea mitigated high-fat diet-induced non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) by inhibiting intestinal lipid absorption, modulating hepatic lipid metabolism, and regulating gut microbiota composition. Additionally, green tea polyphenols (GTPs) supplementation has shown to reduce obesity and serum lipid levels in broiler chickens by inhibiting fatty acid synthesis and promoting lipolysis (Huang et al., 2013). Research indicates that epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) can improve oleic acid-induced hepatic steatosis in the hepatocytes of laying hens through the MAPK pathway (Zhu et al., 2024). Notably, TP are considered highly safe, leave no residues, and align well with sustainable and environmentally friendly farming practices (Chen et al., 2008).

Probiotics positively influence the health of livestock and poultry by promoting a balanced gut microbiota, enhancing immune responses, and increasing nutrient absorption efficiency (Chowdhury et al., 2025). In recent years, researchers have increasingly focused on probiotics, primarily due to their potential roles in preventing and treating various diseases associated with lipid metabolism disorders. One notable strain within the Bacillus genus is BS, which offers numerous probiotic benefits. Guo et al. (2022) observed that incorporating B. subtilis into the diet decreased the risk of high-fat diets inducing fatty liver disease while also supporting the growth of grass carp. Additionally, earlier research indicated that B. subtilis C10 mitigates alcoholic liver injury by altering the composition of intestinal flora and liver metabolic pathways, thereby reducing oxidative damage and correcting abnormalities in lipid metabolism caused by alcohol exposure (Wang et al., 2024b). BS intervention has been shown to significantly reduce alcohol-induced acute liver injury in mice by modulating the enterohepatic axis (Wang et al., 2023). However, the beneficial regulatory effects of BS on FLHS and metabolic disorders in laying hens are seldom reported in the scientific literature.

Drawing from previous research, we discovered that BS and TP positively influence the remodeling of gut microbiota, enhance the integrity of the intestinal barrier, and improve lipid metabolism (Qin et al., 2024). Therefore, we speculated that the combination of BS and TP may yield synergistic beneficial effects on FLHS primarily by modulating intestinal microbiota, but this issue remains unreported and requires further investigation. In this study, we constructed a model of FLHS in aging laying hens to elucidate the ameliorative effects and potential mechanisms of co-dairy BS/TP supplementation on FLHS from the perspectives of gut microbiology and serum metabolomics, combined with hepatic gene transcription. This approach provides a scientific basis for developing natural feed additives for aging laying hens subjected to a high-fat diet.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and animal management

Bacillus subtilis used in this study was B. subtilis NBRC 13719 T, supplied by B&B KOREA Co., Ltd. Korea, with a viable count of 3 × 109 CFU/g. Tea polyphenols were obtained from Shanxi Dongyu Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. China, with 98.6% purity, which contains 79.6% catechins (45.2% epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG)), and were prepared by an aqueous extraction method using water as the solvent, followed by membrane separation concentration and resin adsorption purification. A total of 150 fifty-week-old Hy-line brown laying hens with an average laying rate of 80% were randomly assigned to three groups: normal diet (ND, control group), high-fat diet (HFD, model group), HFD supplemented with 300 mg/kg BS and 300 mg/kg TP (HFD+BS/TP, treatment group). Before diet preparation, BS and TP were premixed with a small amount of basal diet and then thoroughly mixed into the complete feed to reach final supplementation levels. Each group comprised five replicates, with ten hens per replicate. The laying hens were housed in a fully enclosed chicken facility featuring a one-story ladder cage system, which maintained a relative humidity of appropriately 65% throughout the trial period, allowing ad libitum access to water and feed. Following one week of feeding the normal diet, the experimental phase commenced and continued for five weeks. Daily records were maintained for laying rate, average daily feed intake (ADFI), average daily egg mass (ADEM) and feed conversion ratio (FCR). Sample collection

At the end of 5th week, one hen from each replicate was randomly selected after a 12-h overnight fast. Blood samples were collected between 08:00 and 09:00 to minimize circadian and feeding-related variation, and serum was separated by 3500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The hens were euthanized by severing the jugular vein to facilitate sample collection. Liver tissues were harvested, with a portion fixed in 4% neutral buffered paraformaldehyde for morphological observation and Oil Red O staining, while the remaining liver tissues were frozen promptly in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 °C. Cecal content samples were gathered on ice and preserved immediately in liquid nitrogen.

Histopathological identification of liver tissue

The fixed liver tissues were dehydrated and embedded in an OCT embedding medium. The tissues were cut into 8 μm sections using a slicer (LEICA 819, LEICA, Shanghai, China). The sections were then stained with Oil Red O solution in the dark and covered with a lid during the staining process. Next, the sections were sequentially immersed in 60% isopropanol for differentiation. After that, the sections were stained with hematoxylin solution. Finally, the sections were sealed with glycerin gelatin. The stained sections were examined under a microscope (NIKON ECLIPSE E100, Nikon, Japan) and analyzed using an image system (NIKON DS-U3, Nikon, Japan).

Biochemical assay of serum and liver

For hepatic lipid measurements, approximately 100 mg of frozen liver tissue was homogenized in ice-cold physiological saline at a ratio of [1:9, w/v]. The homogenates were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for the determination of triglyceride (TG, YJ033399), total cholesterol (TC, YJ036921), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, YJ036973) using commercial kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The results were normalized to protein concentration. Serum concentrations of progesterone (PROG, YJ47944), estradiol (E2, YJ33317), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH, YJ042764), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, YJ060767), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT, YJ060766) were determined using ELISA kits from Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The corresponding standard curves were provided in Supplementary Figure S1. Readings were obtained using a microplate reader (F50, Tecan Infinite, Biel, Switzerland).

Untargeted serum metabolic analysis with LC–MS

A volume of 120 μl of serum for each sample was placed into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube containing the internal standard. An ice-cold mixture of methanol and acetonitrile (2:1 v/v) was subsequently added to precipitate proteins. The tube was centrifuged, and the resulting supernatant was filtered through 0.22 μm microfilters before being transferred to LC vials. QC samples were prepared by combining aliquots from all 15 samples to create a pooled sample. The vials were stored at −80 °C until LC-MS analysis. An ACQUITY UHPLC system (Waters corporation, Milford, USA) coupled with an AB SCIEX Triple TOF 5600 System (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA) was utilized to analyze the metabolic profiles in both ESI positive and ESI negative ion modes. An ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) was used for both positive and negative modes. QC samples were injected at regular intervals throughout the analytical run to provide a dataset assessing repeatability. Metabolites were identified using Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) Data Processing Software, referencing public databases such as Human Metabolome Database (http://www.hmdb.ca/) and LIPID MAPS Structure Database (http://www.lipidmaps.org/)

16S rRNA sequencing and analysis

Cecal content samples were collected and sent to Shanghai OE Biotech Co., Ltd for 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Total genomic DNA was extracted using the MagPure Soil DNA LQ kit according to the manufacturer’s guidelines.The V3-V4 region was amplified via PCR with the following primers: 338F 5′-barcode–ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′ and 806R 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 250 bp paired-end reads. Sequencing library preparation and data processing were performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. The raw sequencing data were provided in FASTQ format. Subsequently, paired-end reads were preprocessed using Cutadapt software (Version 5.0) to identify and remove adapters. After trimming, low-quality sequences were filtered from the paired-end reads, which were then denoised, merged, and chimeras were detected and removed using DADA2 (Callahan et al., 2016) with the default QIIME2 (Rideout et al., 2019) parameters (https://qiime2.org/). Finally, the software generated the representative reads and the ASV abundance table. The representative read for each amplicon sequence variant (ASV) was selected using the QIIME2 package. All representative reads underwent annotation and were queried against the Silva database (Version 138) using the q2-feature-classifier with the default parameters.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from liver samples according to the manufacturer’ s specifications using the mirVana miRNA Isolation Kit (Ambion, USA). The integrity of the RNA was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA), and RNA samples exhibiting an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) of ≥ 7 and an A260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.2 were selected. For high-throughput RNA sequencing, strand-specific cDNA libraries were constructed with TruSeq Stranded mRNA LTSample Prep Kits in accordance with the manufacturer’ s instructions (Illumina, CA, USA) and subsequently sequenced on the Illumina HiSeqTM 2500 platform by OE biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw data were exported in FASTQ/FQ file format. Quality control and trimming were performed using Trimmomatic to eliminate adapter sequences and low-quality reads (Bolger et al., 2014). Clean reads were aligned to a reference genome using HISAT2. The fragments per kilobase per million (FPKM) values and read counts for each transcript (protein_coding) were analyzed using Cufflinks and HTSeq-count (Trapnell et al., 2010). DEGs were identified based on the FPKM values and read counts of each transcript using the DESeq R package.

Statistical analysis

The data are represented as means ± standard errors of the means (SEM). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted for statistical testing. The means were compared using Duncan’ s multiple range test, implemented in SAS 9.2 software (SAS Institute Inc., USA). The Spearman algorithm was utilized to analyze correlations among the gut microbiota genera, serum metabolites, and hepatic gene expressions. DEMs were selected based on a combination of a statistically significant threshold for variable influence on projection (VIP) values derived from the orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model and P-values from a two-tailed Student’s t-test on the normalized peak areas across different groups, where metabolites with VIP values greater than 1.0 and P-values less than 0.05 were classified as differential metabolites. To avoid overfitting, a 7-fold cross-validation and 200 response permutation tests were employed to assess the quality of the model. QIIME2 software facilitated the analysis of alpha and beta diversity. Microbial diversity in samples was assessed using alpha diversity metrics, including the Chao1 index and Shannon index. The unweighted Unifrac distance matrix generated by the R package (Version 3.5.1) was utilized for unweighted Unifrac Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) to evaluate beta diversity. Subsequently, the R package (Version 3.5.1) was applied to analyze significant differences between different groups using a t‐test statistical method. The linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) method was employed to compare the taxonomy abundance spectrum, with the Top 10 species selected based on relative abundance. Species detected in each sample were included for further analyses. Genes with a P value <0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1 were classified as DEGs. Heatmaps and volcano plots of DEGs were generated using DESeq software. To enrich the functional classification of DEGs, hierarchical clustering analysis was performed using R based on the Gene Ontology (GO) database and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg) (Kanehisa et al., 2008).

Dietary BS/TP mitigated hepatic lipid accumulation and liver damage in FLHS laying hens

The overall experimental design used in this study was illustrated in Fig. 1A. To evaluate the mitigating effect of BS/TP on hepatic lipid accumulation and injury in FLHS laying hens, we analyzed the histomorphology of liver tissues, hepatic lipid metabolism, and serum hormone levels. Phenotypic images revealed that the liver in the HFD group exhibited a yellowish-brown coloration and displayed greasy punctate spots. In contrast, the liver from the HFD+BS/TP group was characterized by a red hue, firmer consistency, and absence of greasy punctate spots (Fig. 1C). There was no significant difference in the size of the liver organs among the three groups (Fig. 1B). Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining showed marked mild hepatic steatosis in HFD laying hens, with circular cytoplasmic vacuoles of varying sizes, mild hepatic sinusoidal dilation, and occasional sinusoidal congestion. Conversely, this deterioration was significantly attenuated by the administration of BS/TP. Furthermore, results from Oil Red O staining indicated greater lipid accumulation in the hepatocytes of the HFD group, whereas the HFD+BS/TP showed a marked decrease in lipid accumulation. Supporting these observations, measurements of liver TG and HDL-C content aligned with the findings from H&E and Oil Red O staining (Fig. 1F-H). BS/TP effectively decreased the level of TG while increasing the HDL-C level compared to the HFD group (P < 0.05). Inclusion of BS/TP in the diet resulted in significantly lower AST activity compared to the HFD group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1D-E). The serum E2, FSH, and PROG levels of the ND group were 1.44, 1.34, and 1.67 times, respectively, that of the HFD group (Fig. 1J-K). In contrast, the serum E2, FSH, and PROG levels of the HFD+BS/TP group were obviously increased compared with the HFD group (P < 0.05). BS/TP reshaped the gut microbiota in FLHS laying hens

To identify which gut microbiota are related to the combined effect of BS/TP, a 16S rRNA analysis of the cecal contents was conducted. Microbial Venn diagram analysis of the ND, HFD, and HFD+BS/TP groups revealed 626 common ASVs, 665 ASVs specific to the ND group, 503 ASVs specific to the HFD group, and 526 ASVs specific to the HFD+BS/TP group (Fig. 2A, Table S1). According to the α-diversity index, the simpson (P = 0.13) and shannon (P = 0.36) indices in the HFD+BS/TP group increased slightly compared with the HFD group, but the differences were not significant (Fig. 2B-E). However, clear separations among the three groups were observed based on the weighted UniFrac PCoA (β-diversity) analysis, indicating overall structural changes in the intestinal microbiota after BS/TP supplementation (Fig. 2F). Phylum-level analysis showed a reduced abundance of Bacteroidota in HFD-fed laying hens compared with those in the ND group (Fig. 2G). Interestingly, BS/TP significantly increased the abundance of Proteobacteria and Spirochaetota compared with the HFD group, without any significant effect on Bacteroidota. At the genus level (Fig. 2H), the composition was mainly comprised of Bacteroides Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Fusobacterium, and [Ruminococcus]_torques_group in the ND, HFD, and HFD+BS/TP groups. The high-fat diet led to an increase in the relative abundance of Collinsella and ASF356 compared with the normal diet. However, this trend was alleviated by BS/TP supplementation (Fig. 2I-J). LEfSe analysis (with an LDA>2) was employed to identify bacterial taxa biomarkers associated with the ND, HFD, and HFD+BS/TP groups. A total of 12 distinct bacterial species were identified across the three experimental groups. Statistical analysis revealed five significant taxa in the ND group, three in the HFD group, and four in the HFD+BS/TP group (Fig. 2K). Specifically, dietary BS/TP supplementation resulted in a selective increase in the abundance of g_Clostridia_vadinBB60_group, g_Parabacteroides, and g_Mailhella compared. In contrast, the HFD group exhibited an enrichment of g_Slackia, g_Gallibacterium, and g_Oribacterium, among others. PICRUSt2 functional predictions revealed that the HFD+BS/TP group led to a significant increase in pathways associated with Shingolipid metabolism, Chemical carcinogenesis-reactive oxygen species, FoxO signaling pathway, degradation of flavonoids, and other glycan degradation, whereas pathway related to the phosphotransferase system (PTS) was significantly reduced (P < 0.05, Fig. 2L). The correlation between gut microbiota and liver lipid parameters was analyzed using pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 2M). The results showed that Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group was negatively correlated with AST. Parabacteroides and Clostridia_vadinBB60_group was negatively with TG. Fusobacterium and Lactobacillus was negatively related with HDL-C. BS/TP alters the serum metabolites induced in FLHS laying hens

To gain in-depth insights into the regulatory effect of BS/TP on the serum metabolic profile, an untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed using LC-MS/MS, identifying 923 annotated metabolites under positive ion mode and 926 annotated metabolites under negative ion mode (Table S2). Principal component analysis (PCA) score plots revealed clear separation and clustering of samples between the ND and HFD groups, as well as between the HFD and HFD+BS/TP groups (Fig. 3A and B). As shown in Fig. 3C, the OPLS-DA score scatter plot indicated significant differences between the HFD group and ND group, suggesting that the high-fat diet altered the serum metabolism of laying hens. Fig. 3D demonstrated that the BS/TP supplement exhibited significantly different clustering trends compared to the HFD group. Additionally, the permutation test (Fig. 3E and F) confirmed the stability of the OPLS-DA model, indicating good predictive performance without overfitting. A volcano plot was generated based on VIP values. Compared to the ND group, the HFD group had 189 upregulated and 189 downregulated DEMs. Similarly, the HFD+BS/TP group showed 143 significantly upregulated and 76 downregulated DEMs compared to the HFD group (Fig. 3G and H, Table S3). Subsequently, heat maps were generated for the top 50 DEMs among the three groups (Fig. 3I and J). Discussion

FLHS, a prevalent metabolic disorder, demonstrates a high incidence during the laying phase, with its occurrence increasing as hens age. This condition is closely associated with reduced egg production in laying hens, resulting in significant economic losses for the poultry industry (Wang et al., 2025). Therefore, the development of therapeutic strategies to avert these negative outcomes is crucial. Notably, a high-fat diet has been linked to an increased prevalence of FLHS (Rozenboim et al., 2016). Among various green feed additives, probiotics and TP are two bioactive compounds that show promise in regulating lipid metabolism. Previous research has indicated that probiotics possess substantial potential for enhancing laying performance, addressing metabolic disorders, managing fatty liver disease, and regulating gut microbiota affected by high-fat diet (Li et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a, 2025b). As an alternative and complementary therapeutic strategy, active components derived from tea extract have demonstrated significant effect on lipid metabolism regulation and the alleviation of metabolic disorders associated with obesity (Cho et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2023; Rocha et al., 2016). Zhu et al. (2024) revealed that EGCG produces multifaceted effects on chicken hepatocytes, particularly in counteracting OA-induced hepatic steatosis. It effectively modulates lipid metabolism, reduces oxidative stress and inflammation, minimizes DNA damage, and influences autophagy and apoptosis. EGCG shows considerable promise in mitigating the adverse effects triggered by OA, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic agent for addressing lipid-related disorders. Mechanistically, EGCG exerts these beneficial effects through the modulation of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway. However, the limited number of studies investigating the synergistic effects of BS and TP, along with the unknown underlying mechanisms, underscores the necessity of this study.

In this study, our findings demonstrated that BS/TP supplementation resulted in a significant reduction of 48.89% in TG and a noteworthy increase of 51.13% in HDL-C. When hepatocytes are damaged, AST enters the bloodstream through the hepatocyte membrane, leading to elevated serum AST levels (Wang et al., 2024a). AST levels increased significantly in the group subjected to a high-fat diet, indicating liver damage; dietary supplementation with BS/TP alleviated this effect. Furthermore, BS/TP effectively reduced fat accumulation in liver tissue induced by the high-fat diet. Therefore, BS/TP may provide protective benefits against the detrimental effects of a high-fat diet on fatty hepatitis and fat accumulation. Nevertheless, the mechanisms and pathways through which BS/TP exerts its effects warrant further exploration.

In our investigation, serum biochemical markers indicated that BS/TP significantly elevated FSH, PROG, and E2 content, which may aid in restoring the liver’s capacity to export lipid. Estrogen plays a crucial protective role in the metabolism of hepatic lipid. By binding to strogen receptor alpha (ERα) in hepatocytes, estrogen inhibits the expression of Srebp-1c and its downstream lipogenic enzymes, fatty acid synthase (FAS) and acetyl-coA carboxylase (ACC), thus reducing hepatic de novo lipogenesis (Lee et al., 2019). During the late laying period, hens exhibit decreased estrogen levels alongside excessive accumulation of hepatic lipids, suggesting that diminished estrogen secretion due to ovarian regression is a significant factor in the development of fatty liver syndrome. Treatment with genistein, a phytoestrogen, increased serum estrogen levels and alleviated FLHS (Lv et al., 2018).

The relationship between hepatic metabolic pathways and ovarian steroidogenesis may be explained by a potential gut–liver–ovarian axis. Although steroidogenesis mainly occurs in steroidogenic tissues such as the ovary, the liver is central to cholesterol homeostasis, lipoprotein metabolism, bile acid production, and systemic steroid metabolite clearance. Therefore, hepatic metabolic status can indirectly influence the substrate pool and endocrine environment required for ovarian steroidogenesis. In the present study, BS/TP supplementation increased hepatic HDL-C-related cholesterol content and improved hepatic lipid accumulation, suggesting an improvement in hepatic lipid handling. Since cholesterol is the essential precursor for steroid hormone synthesis, improved hepatic cholesterol and lipoprotein metabolism may contribute to maintaining the circulating substrate availability required by ovarian steroidogenic tissues. In addition, bile acids synthesized and modified in the liver can regulate intestinal lipid absorption and gut microbiota composition, thereby affecting circulating lipid and steroid-related metabolites. Thus, the BS/TP-induced changes in gut microbiota, serum metabolites, and hepatic gene expression observed in this study may collectively contribute to the improved systemic endocrine status, as reflected by increased serum E2, FSH, and PROG levels.

Metabolic balance is primarily governed by the gut microbiota through three main pathways: altering microbial composition, modulating microbial metabolites, and enhancing intestinal barrier function (Bock et al., 2024). Emerging evidence underscores the essential role of gut microbiota in fatty liver disease (Fang et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025). Additionally, previous studies have shown that the combination of these two additives is more effective than a single drug in improving lipid metabolism by targeting intestinal inflammation (Li et al., 2015). In our earlier research, the regulation of gut microbiota in laying hens by BS, TP, the BS/TP co-dietary demonstrated that, compared to other groups, the BS/TP group synergistically stimulated the growth of Bacteroides while inhibiting the relative abundance of potentially_pathogenic phenotype (Qin et al., 2024). Consistent with these findings, BS/TP significantly modified the structure of the intestinal microbiota in laying hens. Specifically, BS/TP markedly reduced the abundance of harmful bacteria, including Collinsella and ASF356. This reduction is significant because Collinsella aerofaciens promotes systemic inflammation and fat accumulation by metabolizing bile acids and producing ammonia; its abundance correlates positively with triglyceride and total cholesterol levels in patients with NAFLD (Iqbal et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2022). Similarly, ASF356 (family Lachnospiraceae) has been recognized as a microbial signature associated with high-fat diet consumption, showing positive correlations with body weight, leptin levels, and serum lipid parameters such as TC and TG (Portela et al., 2023). Therefore, the reduction of Collinsella and ASF356 induced by BS/TP is closely linked to obesity and lipid metabolism, effectively mitigating fat accumulation and lipid metabolism disorders. Results from Spearman correlation analysis between serum metabolites and gut microbiota revealed that changes in the abundance of Oribacterium and Fournierella due to BS/TP supplement were closely associated with glycerophospholipid metabolism and the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids. Collectively, BS/TP supplementation can effectively rectify gut microbiota imbalances induced by a high-fat diet.

The liver plays a pivotal role in lipid metabolism; however, prolonged fat accumulation can lead to excessive TG synthesis and fatty liver disease (Huang et al., 2025; Dukewich et al., 2025). The PI3K/Akt signaling pathway is essential for governing cellular processes such as glucose uptake, protein synthesis, lipid metabolism, and cell survival. This pathway plays a key role in sustaining energy balance by enhancing insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism while preventing excessive fat storage (Randeni et al., 2025). Transcriptomic analysis of liver tissue indicated that BS/TP modified gene expression levels in vivo. These genes are primarily involved in metabolic pathways, including the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and galactose metabolism. Key genes include those related to bile acid homeostasis, such as Ugt2a2, and those associated with steroid hormone metabolism, such as Hsd17b2 and Hsd11b2. The expression of various Ugt isoforms is linked to fatty liver and occurs following fasting and obesity, both of which correspond to increased hepatic TG content (Xu et al., 2012). Ugt2a2 facilitates the glucuronidation of primary bile acids, promoting their excretion and reducing hepatocyte damage (Perreault et al., 2013). Treatment with BS/TP lead to the downregulation of Ugt2a2, suggesting that this combined intervention effectively decreases the hepatic bile acid burden. The upregulation of Hsd17b2 and Hsd11b2 due to BS/TP treatment represents a significant protective mechanism against hormone-induced lipogenesis. Hsd17b2 catalyzes the NAD⁺-dependent oxidation of active estrogens and androgens—converting E2 to estrone (E1) and testosterone to androstenedione—thereby diminishing local sex steroid signaling (Jin et al., 2025). Hsd11b2 acts as a high-affinity dehydrogenase that converts cortisol to cortisone, preventing inappropriate activation of glucocorticoid receptor (Milagro et al., 2007). A previous study found that Hsd11b2 mRNA levels were lower in obese individuals compared to those of normal weight, while weight loss reversed this trend (Podraza et al., 2024). This mechanism aligns well with the established roles of TP and probiotics in improving metabolic dysregulation. Resveratrol, a polyphenolic compound, has been shown to reverse the abnormalities of the limbic–hypothalamic–pituitary adrenal (LHPA) axis characteristic of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) by increasing corticoliberin production and regulating tissue glucocorticoid (Hsd11b1 or Hsd11b2) metabolism (Tseilikman et al., 2023). In rats treated with glucocorticoids (dexamethasone), the administration of probiotics significantly improved glucocorticoid-induced hepatic steatosis and histological abnormalities (Inceu et al., 2023). These findings suggest that BS/TP may alleviate fatty liver disease by optimizing the enterohepatic circulation through decreased bile acid glucuronidation while simultaneously accelerating the metabolic clearance of estrogen and cortisol. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the causal roles of these candidate genes require further functional validation. Our research group has designed follow-up studies using gene overexpression, RNA interference/knockdown, and gene-editing approaches in hepatocyte-based cellular models and in laying-hen models to determine whether these genes directly regulate hepatic lipid accumulation, metabolite transport, and FLHS development. These studies will provide deeper mechanistic evidence for the regulatory network identified in the present multi-omics analysis.

Additionally, the results from the LC-MS/MS revealed that serum metabolites significantly influenced by BS/TP were predominantly associated with glycerophospholipid metabolism, purine metabolism, and insulin signaling pathway, with the purine metabolism pathway exhibiting highest concentration of metabolites. Notably, a prior investigation conducted by Liu et al. (2025) demonstrated that the combination of traditional Chinese medicine and probiotics effectively regulated hepatic steatosis and metabolic disorders in mice fed high-fat diet by markedly enriching purine metabolites such as guanosine, guanosinemonophosphate (GMP), and guanine, as well as upregulating genes related to purine metabolism, including Cry1 and Ppard. Moreover, an integrated multi-omics analysis indicated a relationship between these altered microbial communities and DEMs. Consequently, the modulation of purine metabolism by BS/TP may represent one mechanism through which it alleviates lipid metabolism disorders induced by a high-fat diet. Research by Zhu et al. (2022) and Pan et al. (2022) indicated that treatment with polyphenols and postbiotics mitigated HFD-induced obesity by altering gut microbiota composition and enhancing lipid metabolism regulation through the glycerophospholipid metabolism signaling pathway. Furthermore, correlation analysis between serum metabolites and differentially expressed hepatic genes revealed that the differential metabolites affected by BS/TP treatment were closely associated with genes annotated to progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation, histidine metabolism, and steroid hormone biosynthesis. Importantly, because transcriptomic analysis was performed in liver tissue rather than ovarian tissue, these pathway annotations should not be interpreted as direct evidence that hepatic genes operate within the ovary. Instead, the enrichment of steroid hormone biosynthesis and progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation-related pathways may indicate a systemic metabolic connection between hepatic lipid/steroid metabolism and reproductive endocrine status. In this context, the hepatic transporter-related genes Slc22a6-a, Slc22a7, and Slc47a1 may be relevant because members of solute carrier transporter families participate in the transport and clearance of endogenous metabolites, including lipid-derived and steroid-related molecules. The altered expression of these genes after BS/TP supplementation suggests that hepatic metabolite transport capacity may have been modified, which could influence the circulating availability of metabolites associated with ovarian steroidogenesis. Nevertheless, this interpretation remains associative. Direct evidence from ovarian tissue, such as the expression of steroidogenic genes including StAR, CYP11A1, HSD3B, CYP19A1, and ovarian steroid concentrations, is required to confirm whether BS/TP directly regulates ovarian steroidogenesis. Therefore, our integrated microbiome–metabolome–transcriptome analysis supports the hypothesis that BS/TP alleviates HFD-induced metabolic disturbance through a gut–liver axis and may secondarily improve systemic reproductive endocrine status. Future studies should include ovarian transcriptomics, targeted steroid metabolomics, and functional validation of hepatic metabolite transporters to clarify the causal relationship between hepatic metabolism and ovarian steroidogenesis.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Although 150 laying hens were included in the feeding trial, the dietary treatment effect was evaluated using five independent replicates per treatment, with ten hens per replicate. For terminal phenotypic measurements and the 16S rRNA sequencing, untargeted metabolomics, and transcriptomic analyses, one hen was randomly selected from each replicate; thus, the effective number of independent biological samples was five per treatment. This sampling strategy avoided pseudoreplication; however, the modest number of independent replicates may have limited the statistical power to detect small-to-moderate effects and reduced the precision of estimated effect sizes. Therefore, nonsignificant results should not be interpreted as evidence of no biological effect, and the multi-omics signatures and correlation networks identified in this study should be considered associative and hypothesis-generating. The consistency of the observed changes across liver phenotype, biochemical indices, gut microbiota, serum metabolites, and hepatic gene expression supports the biological relevance of the findings; nevertheless, larger independently replicated trials, targeted metabolomic validation, and functional studies of candidate genes are required to confirm the reproducibility and causal mechanisms of BS/TP-mediated protection against FLHS.

Conclusion

This study elucidated the protective effect and underlying mechanism of BS/TP on hepatic lipid metabolism disorder induced by high-fat diets via the “gut-liver axis” . Specifically, dietary supplementation with BS/TP altered the composition of the gut microbiota and enhanced the production of Isoferulic acid 3-sulfate and 1E,4Z,6a,8b,10a)-8-(2-Methylbutanoyloxy)-10,15-dihydroxy-3-oxo-1,4,11(13)-germacratrien-12,6-olide. Further analysis indicated that it modulated the expression of key genes, including Ugt2a2 and Hsd11b2, thereby mitigating hepatic damage in FLHS laying hens. In conclusion, this research identified the BS/TP combination as a functional feed additive and proposed a novel strategy for preventing liver diseases in poultry.
