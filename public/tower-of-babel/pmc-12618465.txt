Sandy soils compatibility and least limiting water range under organic, irrigated and perennials management
Authors: David Gabriel Campos Pereira, Rafaela Watanabe, Jhuliendri Bortoluzzi Genova, Vanessa Maria de Souza Barros, Sâmia Paiva de Oliveira, Maria Eugenia Ortiz Escobar, Júlio César Lima Neves, Getulio Coutinho Figueiredo, Álvaro Pires da Silva, Teogenes Senna de Oliveira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgricultural systems have advanced, but challenges remain in improving efficiency while maintaining soil quality in irrigated areas. This study evaluated compaction, compressibility, and the least limiting water range (LLWR) in sandy soils cultivated with West Indian cherry (Malpighia punicifolia L.) in Northeastern Brazil. Soil quality was assessed in three organic management areas: one (A1) and six (A6) years under center pivot irrigation, and ten (A10) years under microsprinklers. Samples were collected at depths of 0.0–0.10 m and 0.20–0.30 m in rows (R) and interrows (I) to analyze compaction degree (DC), maximum density (Bdmax), critical moisture (WCcrit), preconsolidation pressure (σp), compression index (Cc), LLWR, and total organic carbon (TOC). Higher DC was observed in A1R (99%), A1I (92%), and A6I (97%) at 0.0–0.10 m, and A10I (92%) at 0.20–0.30 m. LLWR was smaller in the interrows of A1 and A6, and in the rows of A10 at both depths. Increased surface organic matter reduced penetration resistance and improved water availability. LLWR proved more sensitive than DC and σp in detecting soil degradation. The study highlighted differences in soil properties between center pivot and microsprinkler irrigation systems and their combined impacts on soil structure.Keywords: Sandy soils, Biodynamic agriculture, Soil management, Compaction susceptibility, Soil physical indicators

Introduction

Agricultural production has been challenged by various factors, with water deficit being one of the most limiting factors for agricultural productivity1,2. This water deficit can be caused by low local rainfall combined with high crop evapotranspiration. To overcome such adversities, irrigation has been widely used, bringing significant benefits to agriculture3, especially in arid and semi-arid regions. Irrigation is a well-established technology in developed countries and is becoming increasingly common in developing countries. Compared to rainfed areas, irrigated lands aim to maintain soil moisture at satisfactory levels according to the crops’ water requirements4.

In Brazil, there is an extensive irrigated area on sandy soils, particularly in the Northeast5. These soils are generally less prone to compaction and have lower elasticity and compressibility6. However, under certain conditions of use such as with pivot or localized irrigation—these soils can become susceptible to plastic deformation due to the constant pressure from agricultural machinery and frequent higher soil moisture, which induces significant physical changes7. These changes are also influenced by soil organic matter, density, and structure8,9.

The Brazilian semi-arid region is notable for the heterogeneity of its soils, due to the region’s chemical, physical, and mineralogical diversity10. These characteristics, combined with the high incidence of solar radiation, create favorable conditions for implementing irrigation in these areas. However, considering that water is a scarce resource in the region, and the irrigation infrastructure (dams, canals, pumping stations, etc.) is permanent and involves high economic investment, efficient soil management must be considered essential to ensure long-term agricultural production.

Compaction is recognized as one of the main degradation processes in agricultural systems11, and its prevention is already regulated by specific legislation in some European Union countries12. The occurrence of compaction, both in the rows and between the rows, caused by mechanization, is well documented in the literature13,14, especially between the rows, where mechanical pressure is more pronounced an issue that is exacerbated in pivot-irrigated crops. The use of perennial crops favors greater exploitation and exposure of the soil by the roots over the years, which, combined with inadequate management, can increase susceptibility to erosion between the rows, limiting the development of spontaneous plants15.

Pivot-irrigated areas experience greater intensity of land use due to more frequent wetting and drying cycles, resulting in more compaction and lower soil stability compared to microsprinkler-irrigated areas, which maintain more stable and resilient conditions over time. In this context, this study was conducted based on the following hypotheses: (i) the physical quality of the soil is essential for assessing the sustainability of irrigated agricultural systems and could be compromised by land use; and (ii) the comprehensive behavior of the soil can serve as an indicator of the management quality adopted. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to evaluate soil compaction, compressibility, and the least limiting water range (LLWR) in irrigated sandy soils cropped with West Indian cherry (Malpighia punicifolia L.) in the Ibiapaba region, Northeastern Brazil.

Material and methods

The study area is located in the Ibiapaba region, State of Ceará, Northeastern Brazil (3°51′12″ S; 41°05′10″ W), at an altitude of 850 m, with a hot semi-arid (BSh) climate according to Köppen’s classification. The average annual temperature and rainfall are 28 °C and 670 mm yr⁻1, respectively16, with a rainy season ranging from January to May and a dry season from July to November. The natural vegetation is known as Carrasco17 and is characterized by short-statured individuals (ranging between 3 and 4 m in height), composed of native and transitional species from the Cerrado and Caatinga biomes.

The area was cultivated between 1991 and 1997 with watermelons and strawberries under conventional management (intensive fertilization and pesticide application to control pathogens, insects, and weeds). Oxisols are the main soil classes found in the area18, equivalent to Ferralsols according to the FAO classification19.

For this research, three areas cultivated with West Indian cherry (Malpighia punicifolia L.) under organic soil management were selected: one (A1) and six years (A6) after the implementation of a center pivot irrigation system, and ten years (A10) after the implementation of drip irrigation (Fig. 1). The fertilization consisted of using green manure in the interrow spaces, composed of sunn hemp (Crotalaria juncea L.) and 20 kg ha⁻1 of organic compost. Weed management between the rows is performed with a brush cutter coupled to a tractor with lateral displacement, which allows the decomposition of biomass close to the West Indian cherry crops. In the rows, weeding is done manually. The farm is currently certified by the Biodynamic Institute (IBD—http://ibd.com.br) and Demeter (http://www.demeter-usa.org/). More details about the farm can be found at: https://www.portalnutrilite.com.br/nossas-fazendas/.

Before these species flowered, mechanical mowing of the interrow spaces was carried out, with the green matter being spread in the rows21. Results

Possible influence of soil granulometric composition

The comparative analysis of results from rows and interrows within specified areas (A1, A6, or A10) revealed no significant differences (p < 0.05) in the content of granulometric fractions, indicating that soil granulometric composition did not impact the outcomes of this study. Exceptions were noted in the silt content (A6R vs A6I at a depth of 0.0–0.10 m; A6R vs A6I and A10R vs A10I at 0.20–0.30 m depth) and coarse sand (CS) content (A1R vs A1I), where higher levels were found in A1I and A6R compared to A1R and A6I, respectively.

Conversely, when comparing rows and interrows across different areas, notable differences emerged. Specifically, A1 and A6 exhibited higher values relative to A10 across both rows and interrows at depths of 0.00–0.10 m and 0.10–0.20 m for clay, silt, and coarse sand (CS). The sole exception was fine sand (FS), which presented higher levels in A10R. These disparities among areas regarding sand fractions were not evident when total sand (TS) was evaluated, as no significant differences were detected (refer to Table 2). Despite these minor variations in granulometric fraction contents for A1 and A6 at two depths, and for A10 at 0.00–0.10 m, the textures were classified as sandy loam for both A1 and A6, while A10 at 0.10–0.20 m was categorized as loamy sand. The texture classifications of the assessed areas were deemed to be very similar.

Soil compaction and compressibility

The average values for DC varied from 75 to 99% and from 75 to 92% at 0.00 to 0.10 m and 0.20 to 0.30 m depths, respectively (Table 2). A1R, A1I, A6R, and A6I had higher mean values for DC at 0.00 to 0.10 m depth compared to A10R and A10I (Table 2). However, statistically, there were no differences for DC (p > 0.05) when comparing the evaluated areas at 0.00–0.10 m and 0.20–0.30 m depths. The only exception among the comparisons occurred for A6R vs A10R (p < 0.05) at 0.20–0.30 m depth, with higher DC values at A10R (87%). There were comparative trends for A1R vs A6R (p < 0.015), A1R vs A10R (p < 0.15), and A6R vs A6I (p < 0.20) at 0.00–0.10 m depth, and for A6R vs A6I (p < 0.10) at 0.20–0.30 m depth. The highest DC values were for A1R (99%), much alike the ones for A6I (97%) (Table 2) at the first depth.

Maximum bulk density (Bdmax) and critical water content (WCcrit) showed statistical differences (p < 0.05) for all comparisons. The highest means for Bdmax were observed in A1I and A6I, while WCCrit had a different behavior, being higher in the rows. On the other hand, differences were observed at A10 for Bdmax and WCCrit in comparison to A1 and A6. The means were higher for Bdmax at 0.00–0.10 m depth, whilst for WCCrit, they were higher at 0.20–0.30 m depth. WCCrit in the areas under evaluation was higher than the water content at field capacity (FC), represented by the gravimetric moisture content (Mg) of the samples equilibrated at − 0.01 MPa (Table 2).

It was not possible to estimate the compression index (Cc) and preconsolidation pressure (σp) in A6I (0.00–0.10 m depth), since all soil samples showed an elevated m value (m is the inflexion point from the compression curve restricted to the logarithmic of the maximum pressure applied, m ≤ log 1200 kPa = 3.08) due to low variation in the void index (eo), between the beginning and end of the uniaxial compaction test. As a result, it was not possible to estimate m, leading to an overestimation of σp. A6I showed high Bd, which can be associated with these results and had a smaller soil compression curve. In general, the rows had higher values for Cc (p < 0.05) at both depths when compared to A1I and A6I, except at A10, which did not present significant difference (Table 2). The pressure curves (Fig. 2) were normalized to eliminate the initial bulk density effect42,43, showing less variation of bulk density (Bd) at A10I when applied pressure, as well as A1I and A6I at 0.00–0.10 m depth. Moreover, rows at the two depths and all evaluated areas showed a higher reduction of Bd.

The σp means varied between 101 to 205 kPa for the evaluated areas. Despite the distinct response of Bd in regard to applied load, the average values for σp in rows and interrow spaces had no significant difference (p < 0.05) or trends (p < 0.20). These results may be associated with higher TOC values in the rows (p < 0.05) (Table 2) resulting from the addition of organic composts and palm straw over time.

Least limiting water range

The least limiting water range (LLWR) was limited by the water content between PR and FC (Fig. 3) for all areas. The amplitude of LLWR in A1 and A6 was greater in rows in comparison to the interrows. Higher resistance to penetration in the interrows (Fig. 3) provided greater values of Bd (p < 0.05) and lower contents of TOC (p < 0.05) (Table 2), contrary to rows. Lower value of LLWR was observed in the inter-rows, even presenting higher value for critical density (Bdcrit) compared to the rows (Fig. 2).

The LLWR values were similar for A1 and A6 in rows; on the other hand, A1 was higher than A6 at both depths for interrows (Fig. 3).

A10 showed field capacity (θFC) and permanent wilting point (θPWP) similar at both depths of rows and interrows. However, penetration resistance (θPR) was lower for interrows at 0.00–0.10 m, influencing the higher amplitude of LLWR for interrows at A10. This was not observed in the upper layer of A10I, where LLWR was greater in A10R, despite the larger number of samples above the critical density (Bdcrit) (Fig. 4). LLWR at 0.20–0.30 m was lower than the superficial depth, and higher than rows in A1 and A6. In contrast, both depths showed high Cc and Bd (p < 0.05) at interrows for A1 and A6, while Bdmax was small (p < 0.05). On the other hand, LLWR amplitude of rows from A1 and A6 were higher than A10 for both depths, in contrast when considering that A1 and A6 had higher Bd (p < 0.05) in their rows compared to A10R.

Discussion

Granulometric composition influence on soil quality

Although the natural properties of the soil vary, the differences observed in soils managed for agricultural production tend to accentuate when a comprehensive set of practices is implemented. However, some soil properties, such as granulometric composition, are more resistant to changes. The relative uniformity of the granulometric fractions (p > 0.05) at 0.00–0.10 m observed for A1, A6, and A10, both in rows and interrows within an area or between areas, stands out from the number of differences observed at 0.20–0.30 m (Table 2), as twice as many significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed between different situations and granulometric fractions (15 cases). These results may be associated with the effects of time and irrigation types, as they occurred more frequently in A6, an extreme situation in terms of management, when compared to A1, which has a shorter pivot irrigation management time, even when compared to A10. For A10, despite having a longer management time, micro-irrigation, a localized irrigation method, does not promote much alteration due to its limited area of operation and smaller water volumes compared to central pivot irrigation. Moreover, there is the effect in rows and interrows, as fewer differential cases (p > 0.05) occurred within the same areas, comparatively to the higher number of significant comparisons that occurred in the interrows of different areas, where direct irrigation management is more pronounced.

Changes in granulometric fractions resulting from irrigation methods have been widely discussed in other research papers. These changes occur through processes such as clay eluviation in the upper soil layers, influenced by factors such as the volume of water applied over time (water intensity) and the quality of irrigation water44. Each irrigation system presents a distinct water application efficiency associated with varying water quantities. Notably, localized irrigation methods (such as drip irrigation or micro-irrigation) demonstrate higher efficiency compared to central pivot systems, potentially leading to water savings of up to 75%45.

In certain cases, it is relevant to consider the quality of water and its impact on the physical and chemical behavior of the soil. However, this consideration does not apply to the present study, as the quality of irrigation water is well-established. This is evidenced by regular water quality monitoring conducted by the Companhia de Gestão dos Recursos Hídricos do Estado do Ceará (COGERH-CE). The water quality from the Jaburu I dam (http://www.hidro.ce.gov.br) that supplies the farm provides irrigation-grade water with commendable quality. It is classified as suitable for irrigation, presenting a low risk of salinization. This conclusion is supported by low concentrations of nutrients, a low count of cyanobacteria, and high water transparency. Regarding salinity risk, it was classified as moderate (C2) in all evaluations by COGERH; and regarding sodicity, it was classified as low risk (S1) throughout the period46. Variations in ion levels between the dry and wet seasons should be considered due to the high evapotranspiration in the region and the dilution effects during the wet seasons.

However, it is essential to recognize the variation in ion content between the dry and wet seasons due to the high evapotranspiration rates in the region and dilution effects during the wet periods. In irrigation systems that use substantial water volumes such as in central pivot irrigation there is a potential for greater destabilization of the clay fraction due to salt accumulation45,47. This is particularly relevant in soils with lower clay content and, consequently, lower buffering capacity.

In A6, the clay content in the first layer (0.00–0.10 m) is lower than in the second (0.20–0.30 m), both in rows and interrows. These results for A6 may be due to the larger water volumes and higher irrigation intensity employed via central pivot (A1 and A6), when compared to the micro-irrigation areas (A10). Even in the early stages of the crop, when direct irrigation occurs on the rows, this cumulative effect may not be evident due to the application of a mulch layer made from the leaves of Copernicia prunifera (Miller) H. E. Moore, a common practice throughout the crop’s life cycle in the area studied. These reductions in clay content for A6 are not accompanied by larger proportions of sand in the interrows.

A significant difference was observed for the coarse sand fraction in A1 and for the silt content in A6 and A10 (p < 0.05). These variations suggest that management may have impacted these soil fractions differently, especially in the interrows, which more directly reflect the effects of agricultural practices over time. Notably, silt content was the granulometric fraction with the most statistical differences, particularly at the second depth. However, these results should be interpreted with caution, as the determination of silt was made indirectly, which may be subject to cumulative errors from the estimates of other granulometric fractions48.

Reports in other articles point to issues related to inefficient dispersion, which may present a false proportion of the silt and sand fractions in soils with high Fe and Al oxide content49. However, these articles do not address such methodologies in sandy soils. Thus, the consideration of clay and sand classes allowed for the elimination of all natural differences and their relation to the results presented, being associated with the physical-hydric behavior of the soil evaluated under different management systems, as addressed in the following sections. Another aspect is that the behavior is certainly dictated, among other factors, by the common interaction of granulometric fractions reflected in texture classes. The dominant texture class in A1, A6, and A10 is sandy loam, except for the depth of 0.20–0.30 m in A10, where it is classified as loamy sand, thus similar texture classes.

Soil compaction and compressibility

Interrows in any agricultural area usually present high soil compaction values, since they’re subjected to higher pressures due to trampling and machine traffic50. Reorganization of soil particles through surface mechanization in order to control weeds or establish cover plants with the use of machines51, as well as higher amplitudes of wetting and drying cycles52, can also lead to compaction, as was evaluated. Wheel traffic from heavy vehicles, i.e., central pivot wheels, can compact soils to varying degrees through the plant’s root zone, often causing increased mechanical resistance and decreased permeability to air and water. These circumstances can prevent root elongation and significantly reduce plant growth and yield53,54.

However, this phenomenon was not observed in the case of DC when most comparisons were considered, with no statistical significance (p > 0.05). DC, which represents the ratio between soil bulk density (Bd) and the maximum soil bulk density obtained through the Proctor test, is frequently employed to eliminate granulometric disparities across the examined scenarios in the current study24,55, despite exhibiting robust repeatability56.

For a few scenarios, the expected higher DC of interrows occurred for A6 at 0.00–0.10 m (p < 0.20) and at 0.20–0.30 m (p < 0.10), emphasizing the effects management has, rather than the natural granulometric differences. On the other hand, DC of rows at A1 (p < 0.10 or p < 0.15) at 0.00–0.10 m, as well as A10 at 0.20–0.30 m depth (p < 0.05) had higher values (Table 2), which seems to be contradictory, despite being a trend. Higher DC at rows may be linked to the spatial remnants of operations on these soil layers with the use of machines for cultural treatments or even orchard replacement when the West cherry Indian was established.

Nevertheless, even considering that DC mitigates the impact of soil texture on comparisons, the absence of observed significant differences (p < 0.05) does not appear to accurately reflect the effects of handling. This is because, when analyzing Bd, it was noted that this variable exhibits the highest variability (p < 0.05) for interrows in A1, A6, and A10 across most comparisons and depths. The exceptions were found at depth of 0.20–0.30 m in interrows comparisons between A1 and A6 (A1R > A6R, p < 0.05), and the tendency (p < 0.10) between rows and interrows in A1 (A1R > A1I). An increase in Bd leads to enhanced load-bearing capacity of the soil57–59, thus preventing the transmission of compaction to deeper layers when the soil resistance to compaction is higher60. However, Bennett et al.51 identified machine handling effects on soil compaction down to a depth of 0.8 m.

Critical water content (WCcrit), as well as Bdmax, both derived from the Proctor test, also exhibited significant differences across all evaluated conditions and depths. The highest WCcrit values were observed in rows, and the highest Bdmax values in interrows, at both depths in A1 and A6 (p < 0.05), a trend not seen in A10. Regardless of soil texture, some authors associate Bdmax with elevated levels of total organic carbon (TOC)61,62, which could also be connected to larger deposits of organic matter (Elom), carbon from the light fraction (ECLom), and rows, all of which were higher in A1, A6, and A10, both at depths of 0.00–0.10 m and 0.20–0.30 m (p < 0.05), directly correlated with different management practices.

Once again, A10 was the only exception, where the highest values for WCcrit occurred in interrows and at a depth of 0.10–0.20 m. According to Libohova et al.63, organic matter influences the increase in water retention and reduction in soil density when applied to the soil in a function through: (i) low specific weight of the organic matter; (ii) increased water retention in the soil due to the water’s affinity with the specific surface of organic matter; and (iii) an energy dissipation due to the damping effect by organic matter. The low increase in TOC between interrows may have helped reduce Bdmax in this region. Zhao et al.64 stated that the increase in C and organic matter content is associated with an increase in the binding forces between soil particles, resulting in a greater compaction resistance. It is suggested, therefore, to adopt practices that promote increments of organic matter content in the interrows, in order to increase WCcrit, where the degradation of soil structure through machine traffic is usually greater.

The elevated WCcrit value in comparison to Mg might be linked to the accumulation of organic materials in the soil. This accumulation can increase the WCcrit of soil compaction, as highlighted by Serra-Wittling et al.45 and de Moraes et al.65. In terms of the same applied load, Bd exhibited higher values in crop interrows. This trend could be attributed to the higher levels of TOC and EClom content in these interrow areas. Similar findings have been reported in other studies, such as Aragón et al.61, where an increase in TOC levels led to a reduction in Bdmax and an increase in WCcrit.

The behavior of Bd, Bdmax, and WCcrit more accurately captured the distinctions between crop rows and interrows, in contrast to the differences observed in DC and irrigation methods: central pivot (A1 and A6) or microsprinkler (A10). Crop rows receive regular applications of organic compounds, along with deposits of leaves, branches, and fruit, forming plant litter on the soil surface. Additionally, irrigation contributes humidity, fostering a stable pedoenvironment that promotes microbial activity, the integration of organic matter into the soil, and the growth of superficial roots. These factors collectively contribute to a more stable soil physical condition. The behavior of Bd, Bdmax, and WCcrit more accurately captured the distinctions between crop rows and interrows, in contrast to the differences observed in DC and irrigation methods: central pivot (A1 and A6) or microsprinkler (A10).

In the case of interrows, an additional challenge is represented by machine traffic, which applies pressure on the soil surface. Additionally, central pivot irrigation covers the entire area, resulting in higher soil moisture levels. Regarding the causes of soil erosion in fields irrigated by central pivot66, found that furrow incision was frequently linked to central pivot wheel tracks, which were identified as the direct cause of furrow development in at least 51 central pivot field images. These tracks can suffer severe compaction if the lateral tower is moving over saturated or puddled soil, creating deep circular channels that intercept runoff within the irrigated fields67. This effect further exacerbates soil structure degradation, reinforcing the need for proper water management and controlled traffic techniques to mitigate compaction and erosion in irrigated systems. For example, water-induced soil erosion selectively removes finer particles and organic carbon from the soil, reducing the soil’s water retention capacity and increasing surface runoff susceptibility68. As demonstrated by Batista et al.66, soil erosion can reduce the fraction of rainfall effectively available to crops, which in turn increases irrigation requirements and reinforces a feedback mechanism between water use and erosion.

This elevated moisture makes soil deformations more perceptible through several parameters. However, this dynamic does not apply to areas irrigated by microsprinkler, where the moisture range is limited, particularly in spaces between rows. On the contrary, microsprinkler irrigation does not support the rapid growth of cover crops in the interrows, which brings a series of positive benefits. This discrepancy is already evident in areas irrigated by central pivot. This specific circumstance is responsible for the lower values of the variable observed earlier.

The compaction curves presented in Fig. 4 underwent normalization to mitigate the impact of initial sample density, as recommended by Oliveira et al.42. Among the scenarios, A10 exhibited the least decline in Bd concerning the applied load. Broadly, the upper layer of interrow spaces in A1 and A6 showed minor increases in Bd due to applied loads, unlike the trend observed in crop rows, where these increments were more pronounced. This discrepancy suggests that the management practices employed in interrow spaces led to an increase in soil density while decreasing pore space, consequently resulting in reduced soil vulnerability to compaction. Elevated values of Cc in crop rows correlate with higher eo and lower Bd. A greater Cc value signifies heightened susceptibility to compaction; denser soils undergo less deformation under external pressure, leading to lower Cc values. This finding aligns with research by Figueiredo et al.26 and Suzuki et al.69.

The average values for σp ranged from 101 to 205 kPa for the evaluated situations, and they are within the range of pressure applied by tractors, with a tendency for machine usage increasing, which frequently surpasses 200 kPa24. These values are less than the 274 kPa observed by Lima et al.70 in Oxisols cultivated with citrus, but close to Oxisols under no-tillage and conventional tillage25. The lack of significant difference between the mean σp values in the crop rows and interrow spaces may be attributed to the higher total organic carbon (TOC) content in the rows, which resulted from the addition of organic compost and palm straw. The organic matter enhances the stability of soil aggregates, contributing to the preservation of soil structure, increased load-bearing capacity, and improved resistance to soil compaction71.

The elevated value of the parameter m found in all soil samples from A6I can be attributed to the substantial soil bulk density of the analyzed samples. This high bulk density led to a flattened curvature in the soil compaction curve, as explained by Gregory et al.72 and Silva et al.25. This distinctive behavior can be understood in the context that the applied load on the soil surpasses the values of σp. Consequently, this situation has the potential to lead to increased compaction, as indicated by Dec et al.73 and Dias Junior and Pierce74. Furthermore, the LLWR (discussed in the subsequent part of the analysis) in A1, A6, and A10 experiences reduction with the escalation of soil bulk density. It is crucial to recognize the significance of controlling and reducing machine traffic, especially in relation to the load-bearing capacity of the soil. Exceeding this capacity should be avoided to maintain the soil structural integrity and long-term sustainability.

Least limiting water range

The amplitude of the LLWR was greater in rows than in the interrows for A1 and A6, which can be explained by the absence of traffic and higher organic matter content in rows (i.e., TOC, ELom, and ECLom), less pressure on soil particles through surface mechanization50,75, as well as a smaller amplitude of wetting and drying cycles due to a decrease in macropores76. Furthermore, the results for A1 and A6 presented the cumulative time of absence of these same effects as observed in rows when considering the reduction of LLWR between A1 and A6 in the interrows. The interrows of A1I and A6I had a narrower LLWR compared to A10I, suggesting that microsprinkler irrigation at A10 (which did not irrigate the interrows) promotes better physical conditions in interrows, since they had been drier, withstanding higher surface pressure than A1 and A6. The interrows in A1 and A6, which were frequently irrigated, had higher soil moisture levels, which led to less physical resistance to deformation caused by machinery and soil compaction.

Some of the evaluated soil properties, especially at a depth of 0.00–0.10 m (Cc, Bd, and DC), showed better physical conditions for A10, where the LLWR was wider (p < 0.05). The increase in water content in the interrow spaces due to irrigation increases susceptibility to compaction and the detrimental effects of machine traffic on soil structure, mainly in A1I and A6I, which displayed low values for WCcrit, making them more susceptible to compaction at lower water contents. Machine traffic should therefore be controlled differently in areas irrigated by a central pivot compared to those under localized irrigation. Furthermore, the presence of narrow LLWR ranges in the interrows of A1I and A6I indicates soil degradation, limiting the regions exploitable by plant roots. Despite water availability, these spaces experience physical constraints that impede root development and growth, considerably diminishing crop water utilization. Particularly in humid conditions, machine traffic leads to a reduction in macropores, hampering gas exchange and constraining root growth76.

It is important to emphasize that A10I contains a larger number of samples surpassing the critical soil bulk density (Bdcrit) (as illustrated in Fig. 3). This occurrence could be attributed to the presence of samples exhibiting low values for soil bulk density. However, when compared within the same density range (1.2 to 1.8 kg dm-3), the LLWR remains consistent between rows and interrow spaces with a 10-year cultivation history. This demonstrates that in areas A1 and A6, there is a need to review water content management during mechanized operations. The LLWR was observed to be lower than the water content situated between PWP and PA, with θPR (presumably representing a parameter related to water content) being the most constraining factor. This observation aligns with findings from other researchers62,77–79. According to Pacheco and Cantalice79, in management systems that enhance the structural quality of the soil, the LLWR is numerically equivalent to the water availability between FC and PWP.

In crop rows, mitigating the adverse impacts of high penetrative resistance may involve increasing irrigation frequency to maintain elevated soil moisture content during periods of less restrictive conditions80. However, a frequent watering approach may introduce challenges such as diminished irrigation efficiency, heightened nutrient leaching, heightened soil compaction susceptibility, and even potential clay eluviation. In interrow spaces where LLWR reached zero or near-zero values, remedial measures should be considered. These measures could encompass subsoiling between crop rows, combined with controlling machine traffic. This strategy aims to sustain the porosity created by subsoiling over time. Despite the fact that the interrows in A6 were scarified three months prior to harvest, the LLWR remained notably narrow, indicating that the effects of scarification were short-lived.

As indicated by the findings of Medeiros et al.81, implementing subsoiling between crop rows in an orange orchard led to a reduction in soil bulk density, penetrative resistance, and compaction degree, ultimately resulting in increased fruit count and production. Nevertheless, employing techniques such as subsoiling for soil management in orchards requires extensive and long-term experimentation to ascertain their significance in fruit cultivation82.

Landscape implications, potentialities and limitations

Sandy soils (especially those with sandy textures: > 70% sand and < 15% clay) represent great territorial extensions throughout the world. These soil types cover approximately 900 million hectares83 and are prevalent across various geographical environments, influenced by factors such as source materials and climate84. Once underestimated, they now constitute a significant agricultural frontier in Brazil. The utilization of these soils for technical agriculture gained prominence with the advent of technologies that address challenges like water scarcity (through irrigation) and low CTC (cation exchange capacity)85. Sandy soils offer several advantages for agriculture. They are easily mechanized due to their relatively flat or gently sloping terrain, which facilitates soil preparation such as constructing beds and ridges as needed. However, their low clay content reduces water storage capacity and nutrient retention, which can be seen as a drawback. On the other hand, this characteristic promotes rapid nutrient availability to plants, as the lower clay presence reduces the adsorption of elements like phosphorus (P)86.

Sandy soils have increasingly become an alternative for global agricultural production, not only due to the characteristics mentioned above but also due to the decrease in the global availability of arable land87, whether because of the limited area exploited by agriculture or the constant degradation of agricultural land, which allows soils that were previously unexplored to be cultivated today. In Brazil, extensive areas of these soils support a range of perennial, semiannual, and annual crops. Notably, coffee and fruit cultivation represent prominent perennial practices, while soybeans and cotton, along with other annual crops, also find their place88,89.

These agricultural activities, often supported by diverse irrigation methods, have historically faced limitations due to water scarcity a challenge that persists today and is expected to be compounded by ongoing climate change87–89. Consequently, the imperative to optimize soil management, water utilization, and capital investment has never been more critical. Research in these areas is essential, and studies like the present one contribute significantly to comprehending the behaviors of these soils. It is worth noting that the insights gained extend beyond the conditions outlined in this study; they also hold relevance for other soil categories exhibiting characteristics akin to sandy soils.

The significance of economic and technical considerations regarding sandy soils frequently spurs the creation of studies like this. Understanding the physical, chemical, and biological properties of these soil types is pivotal, particularly when coupled with intensive organic matter utilization a method that can enhance soil fertility (increasing nutrient availability, cation exchange capacity, and reducing nutrient leaching) while addressing physical challenges such as low water retention and, consequently, limited biological activity. The worldwide organic agricultural area has expanded significantly in recent years, not only due to environmental concerns (contamination of natural resources) and human health issues (minimizing food contamination through the reduction of chemical inputs used for insect, pathogen, and weed control) but also due to the potential for better management of organic waste from agricultural or industrial sources, especially when applied as a conservationist approach for these soils90.

Studies such as this one, where field situations are assessed, may initially appear to present case studies that lack certain experimental statistical foundations such as randomization, repetition, and local control. Nevertheless, they offer a valuable opportunity to connect meticulous and systematic research with the situations under evaluation. This holds true as long as specific conditions are met and understood for selecting and investigating these scenarios: (i) systematic monitoring of information related to diverse cultivation areas and the property as a whole, even if this information was not directly utilized in the study; (ii) the potential for a thorough characterization of the historical management practices across different areas and the entire property; (iii) the consistent application of operational techniques and cultural practices within the cultivation areas; and (iv) active technical supervision and involvement of individuals possessing suitable education aligned with the technological demands of plantation management. These conditions have all been carefully observed and deemed appropriate within the context of the present study.

The identification of research topics must consider the experimental planning that will be used and begin with the evaluation of pre-established conditions and a clear definition of objectives and hypotheses that will be evaluated, or, at the very least, formulation of questions that need to be answered. Reproducibility in evaluated situations (in this case: land use intensity–fruit cultivation, irrigation types–localized or sprinkler, crop type–acerola; uniform cultural practices at the different researched areas, etc.) is crucial when studying the behavior of soil processes that are widely present in natural settings (research on specific/particular situations is not recommended, unless economically or technically justified). Furthermore, sandy soils, due to their lower reactivity and more homogeneous properties, provide an interesting research environment, as controlled simulations are often challenging. Thus, conducting field studies under systematic management practices is particularly useful for these less interactive pedoenvironments.

The results highlight the distinction between two types of irrigation (pivot and microsprinkler) and the consequences of their use in conjunction with agricultural machinery and perennial crops. Specifically, they underscore the cumulative effects of cultivation due to the lack of periodic elimination of soil compaction. Additionally, they demonstrate the spatially differentiated susceptibility of highly uniform soils (with low-reactive granulometry) to pressure-induced compaction caused by machinery traffic during cultivation. This comparative and differential pressure distribution occurs both within and between crop rows, emphasizing the complexity and the need for integrated management to mitigate negative impacts on agricultural environments.

The highest penetration resistance observed, as well as the lowest LLWR, in areas irrigated via central pivot, could be minimized by increasing irrigation frequency to maintain higher soil water content when machinery is not in use. However, frequent irrigation, as a tool for reducing mechanical resistance, may decrease irrigation efficiency, increase the crop’s water footprint, enhance nutrient leaching, and reduce farm profitability. Moreover, considering that the study area is in a water-scarce region, this approach is not recommended.

One significant limitation of this study stands out: the lack of in-depth analysis on the relationships between certain evaluated variables and those linked to organic matter, particularly regarding its fractionation and overall presence. This is important because organic matter and clay content significantly influence the plasticity of the soil. Higher organic matter content tends to reduce compaction risk, acting as an energy dissipator, enhancing cation exchange capacity, influencing nutrient dynamics, and decreasing soil bulk density45,71,91. Long-term studies monitoring organic matter fluctuations are therefore essential.

To address the challenges posed by central pivot irrigation, such as high application rates and potential compaction caused by wheel traffic, it is essential to implement soil conservation practices that promote soil cover and water infiltration, preventing harmful runoff concentration during irrigation. Techniques such as no-tillage systems, broad-base terraces, retention basins, and contour channels have been recommended92,93. Additionally, the proper design and management of pivot irrigation systems are just as important as soil management in preventing runoff and erosion94. When well managed, these systems can greatly improve both soil and surface water quality95. By integrating soil conservation techniques with efficient irrigation management, future research can enhance soil and water management strategies, promoting more sustainable and resilient agricultural systems66.

Conclusions

The least limiting water range demonstrated greater sensitivity in identifying variations in the impact of management practices on soil structural quality compared to load-bearing capacity or compaction metrics. This underscores its importance as an indicator of soil physical health, particularly in sandy soils, where structural changes significantly affect water retention and root growth.

Soil in the interrows, subjected to one and six years of cultivation, exhibited greater structural degradation compared to the planting rows due to machinery traffic and higher moisture content caused by central pivot irrigation. While this irrigation method can increase soil compaction and reduce the least limiting water range, it also enhances load-bearing capacity, highlighting the need for appropriate management strategies to balance these effects. The study findings indicate that adopting localized irrigation systems, such as microsprinklers or drip irrigation, can minimize soil structural degradation in irrigated orchards by optimizing water distribution and reducing compaction.

Future research should focus on long-term assessments of soil physical and hydraulic properties under different irrigation regimes and management strategies. The integration of conservation techniques and localized irrigation may provide valuable insights for optimizing water use and soil conservation in sandy agricultural soils. The findings of this study reinforce the importance of management practices that consider the interaction between irrigation and soil structural quality to ensure long-term agricultural sustainability and productivity.
