Twelve-Year Incidence, Causes, and Determinants of Visual Impairment and Visual Improvement in a Multiethnic Asian Population: The Singapore Epidemiology of Eye Diseases Study
Authors: Gabriel Dawei Yang, Can Can Xue, Miao Li Chee, Zhi Da Soh, Qingsheng Peng, Jocelyn Hui Lin Goh, Zhi Wei Lim, Miao Ling Chee, Hongyu Wei, Yanyan Chen, Charumathi Sabanayagam, Ching-Yu Cheng, Yih Chung Tham

ABSTRACT

By 2050, an estimated 474 million people worldwide are projected to have moderate to severe vision impairment (VI),1 highlighting a substantial and growing health care burden. However, prevalence estimates alone do not capture the dynamic nature of VI, including its onset, progression, and potential for improvement. Longitudinal population-based studies are therefore essential to better understand the incidence and temporal changes of VI.

To date, most longitudinal evidence on VI has been derived from Western populations,2, 3, 4, 5 with relatively limited data from Asia. This gap is significant, as Asia accounts for nearly 60% of the global population and is undergoing rapid demographic and epidemiological transitions, including population ageing, increased life expectancy, and a rising burden of myopia and age-related eye diseases.6,7 Furthermore, multiethnic Asian populations remain underrepresented in existing literature, and very few studies have concurrently examined both incident VI and visual improvement over time.

To address these gaps, we investigated the 12-year incidence of VI and visual improvement, identified associated risk factors, and determined the primary causes of VI in the Singapore Epidemiology of Eye Diseases (SEED) study, a large, multiethnic Asian population-based cohort.

Materials and Methods

Study Populations

The design and methodology of the SEED study have been described previously.8, 9, 10, 11, 12 All study procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the Singapore Eye Research Institute institutional review board. All subjects provided written informed consent.

Briefly, baseline recruitment used age-stratified random sampling to enroll 10 033 adults aged 40–80 years (3280 Malays, 3400 Indians, 3353 Chinese) between 2004 and 2011. Participants were invited for follow-up at 6 years (SEED-2) and 12 years (SEED-3). At SEED-2, 6762 individuals participated (78.79% response), and at SEED-3, 4495 participated (76.96% response). An additional 28 participants who missed the SEED-2 examination but returned for SEED-3 (25 Malays and 3 Indians) were included, resulting in a total of 4523 participants in the current analyses. The mean follow-up duration was 11.61 ± 1.05 years. Attrition at SEED-3 was mainly due to death (n = 537), severe cognitive or mobility impairment (n = 337), relocation (n = 44), institutionalization (n = 3), and refusal (n = 1346) (Fig 1).

Study inclusion and exclusion flowchart. SEED = Singapore Epidemiology of Eye Diseases study. Twenty-eight participants who missed SEED-2 but attended SEED-3 (25 Malays and 3 Indians) were included in the current analyses but were not included in the study flowchart or the calculation of the final response rate.

Definition

In this 12-year follow-up study, we examined both the incidence of VI and visual improvement based on best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and presenting visual acuity (PVA). For each study individual, VI was defined based on the better-seeing eye. Detailed assessment methods were described in the prior cohort profile paper,12 with key definitions provided below.

Visual Impairment

According to the United States (US) definition,13,14 VI was defined as visual acuity (VA) < 20/40; low vision was defined as VA < 20/40 but ≥20/200, and blindness was defined as VA < 20/200. At baseline, an eye was considered at risk of developing VI if VA was ≥ 20/40, and at risk of developing blindness if VA was ≥ 20/200.3,5 Persistent VI is defined as visual regression or no improvement over the follow-up. These definitions were consistent with those used in our previous 6-year report.15

Visual Improvement

Visual improvement was also assessed based on the better-seeing eye.3 Improvement was determined as a shift (from baseline) to a less severe VI category at follow-up visit, for example.

•From blindness to low vision or no VI, or

From blindness to low vision or no VI, or

•From low vision to no VI.

From low vision to no VI.

Primary Causes of Visual Impairment

At the eye level, the examining ophthalmologist determined the principal cause based on the relative severity and the condition most likely to account for the visual loss. When multiple abnormalities were present for the same eye, priority of choice was given to the most plausible and treatable pathology.16 This definition was consistent with past VI paper series of the SEED study.16

Other Measurements and Systemic Assessments

Data were collected using an interviewer-administered questionnaire covering subject demographics, medical history, lifestyle risk factors (such as current smoking status and alcohol intake), and socioeconomic status (including education level, type of housing, and monthly income). History of diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined by nonfasting glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥6.5%, diabetic medication use, or self-reported diabetes. Hyperlipidemia was defined as total cholesterol ≥6.2 mmol/L or lipid-lowering medication use. Hypertension was defined by systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, antihypertensive medication use, or self-reported hypertension. History of cardiovascular disease (CVD) included self-reported angina, stroke, or myocardial infarction. History of chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (calculated using the CKD-Epidemiology Collaboration equation).17,18 Body mass index was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m2). Education level was categorized as either having received formal education (defined as primary education or higher) or no formal education. Housing type was classified into three categories: 1- to 2-room public flats, 3- to 4-room public flats, or 5-room public flats and better. Monthly income was categorized as either ˂SGD$2000 or ≥SGD 2000.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.5.1. Independent t-test and Pearson chi-square test were used for continuous and categorical data, respectively, to compare between subjects with and without incident VI and visual improvement. Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. One-way analysis of variance and chi-square tests were performed for continuous variables and categorical variables, respectively, to compare subject characteristics among three ethnicities.

Incidences of VI and rate of visual improvement were evaluated and standardized to the Singapore Population Census 2020.19 These analyses were stratified by baseline age group (40–49, 50–59, 60–69, and ≥70 years), ethnicity, and sex. The modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimation20 was used to evaluate the associations between baseline demographic, systemic, and socioeconomic factors with incident best-corrected and presenting VI, as well as visual improvement over 12 years. Age was included in all regression models as a continuous variable to maximize statistical power and minimize residual confounding. For ease of interpretation, relative risks (RRs) were presented per 10-year increase in age by scaling the corresponding regression coefficient.

Results

A total of 4523 eligible participants were included in the current analysis. Compared to included participants, excluded participants were more likely to be older, current smokers, without formal education, lower income, reside in smaller housing, and have a higher prevalence of DM, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, CVD, and CKD (all P < 0.001). In addition, the prevalence of baseline VI was higher among excluded participants, indicating poorer baseline visual status. (Supplementary Table 1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

As shown in Table 1, 4433 and 3892 participants were eligible for the analyses of incident best-corrected VI and presenting VI, respectively. Compared to those without incident best-corrected VI, affected participants were generally older and more likely to have DM, hypertension, CVD, CKD, smaller housing, lower monthly income, and no formal education (all P ≤ 0.008). Similar baseline differences were observed for presenting VI.

Following adjustment for age, gender, ethnicity, history of DM, hyperlipidemia, hypertension and CKD, type of housing, monthly income, and education level, older age (per decade; RR = 2.06, 95% CI, 1.55–2.74), Malay ethnicity (Chinese as reference: RR = 2.12, 95% CI, 1.26 – 3.59), history of CKD (RR = 1.70, 95% CI, 1.09–2.67), history of DM (RR = 1.90, 95% CI, 1.27–2.86), lower income (RR = 2.52, 95% CI, 1.20–5.30), and lack of formal education (RR = 2.14, 95% CI, 1.37–3.34) were significantly associated with incident best corrected VI (all P ≤ 0.02). To further quantify the relative contribution of these significant baseline factors, PAFs were calculated (Table 4). Discussion

In this 12-year, population-based, multiethnic Asian cohort, the age-standardized incidence of best-corrected VI was 3.5% and presenting VI was 8.1%. Cataract and refractive error were the predominant causes of incident VI. Socioeconomic disadvantages, including lower income, lack of formal education, and smaller housing type, were consistently associated with higher risk of incident VI. Beyond incidence, this decade-long longitudinal design enabled characterization of VI trajectories over time, including progression, persistence, and visual improvement among individuals with baseline impairment. Notably, a substantial proportion experienced visual improvement over 12 years, highlighting the reversibility of a large component of VI cases. These findings carry significant implications for health system planning in multiethnic Asian populations, particularly in the context of rapidly ageing societies.

Several longitudinal studies have examined long-term visual outcomes in older populations (Supplementary Table 4, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). The Beaver Dam Eye Study (BDMS)5 and the Blue Mountains Eye Study,3 both conducted in predominantly Caucasian cohorts, reported 15-year crude incidences of best-corrected VI of 8.3% and 5.2%, respectively. The Andhra Pradesh Eye Disease Study,22 the only comparable long-term study in Asia, reported 15-year crude incidences of best-corrected VI of 10.6%. Although the SEED cohort showed overall lower incidence estimates after age standardization, a notable finding was the marked discrepancy between incident best-corrected VI (3.5%) and presenting VI (8.1%) in the SEED cohort. This difference indicates the burden of uncorrected refractive error and other treatable conditions. Of note, unlike AMD, which is the leading cause of VI in both Blue Mountains Eye Study5 and Andhra Pradesh Eye Disease Study,22 its contribution as cause of incident VI in SEED was minimal. Such discrepancies likely reflect both ethnic and environmental differences in AMD susceptibility,23,24 as well as the potential masking effect of untreated cataract in our cohort, which may obscure the diagnosis of underlying retinal pathology.

Compared with our previous findings in the 6-year SEED follow-up study,15 history of CVD and smoking were no longer significantly associated with incident VI, whereas older age, low income, and lack of formal education remained robustly associated. Although age is consistently identified as the dominant driver of VI across longitudinal studies,3,5,22 low income contributed the largest PAF in our cohort (61% for best-corrected VI and 41% for presenting VI). This finding underscores the central role of socioeconomic barriers, particularly affordability and access to eye care as key targets for interventions. Furthermore, consistent patterns emerged across both SEED 6-year and 12-year followups: women continued to exhibit a higher incidence of VI than men, and Malays consistently demonstrated the highest VI incidence across all definitions, even after adjustment for socioeconomic and clinical covariates. Prior literature suggests that gender-related differences in health-seeking behavior and the prioritization of personal health may partly explain the higher burden of VI among women,25 whereas cultural norms, genetic susceptibility, and variations in care utilization may underlie ethnic disparities.26,27 Although age and ethnicity are nonmodifiable factors, they remain important for identifying populations at higher risk of VI and to be prioritized for vision screening. Together with the prominent contribution of socioeconomic factors, these findings underscore the need for further research into care-seeking behavior and barriers to eye care utilization to support targeted and equitable eye care strategies.

Our study further adds to the literature by quantifying the 12-year incidence of visual improvement, an outcome rarely evaluated in population-based cohorts. We observed substantial visual improvement rates (BCVA-defined: 52.0% and PVA-defined: 74.5%) among those with VI at baseline. Socioeconomically advantaged individuals were more likely to experience improvement in presenting vision. Despite these gains, a considerable proportion of participants remained visually impaired at follow-up, predominantly due to treatable causes such as cataract and uncorrected refractive error. The persistence of VI attributable to these avoidable conditions reinforces their continued public health importance. Notably, a substantial subgroup presented with presenting but not best-corrected VI, indicating that uncorrected refractive error contributed significantly to VI and highlighting opportunities to strengthen refractive services and improve spectacle coverage. Overall, the burden of avoidable VI remains appreciable, underscoring the need to enhance service delivery and improve access to cataract surgery and refractive correction.28,29

Based on population projections, the number of Singapore residents aged over 65 years is expected to reach approximately 3 million by 2050, comprising nearly one-third of the population.30,31 Given this demographic shift, our findings underscore a growing public health burden of VI if current incidence patterns persist. Importantly, the consequences of this rising burden may extend well beyond vision loss, as VI is a recognised risk factor for many other conditions, such as dementia32,33 and traumatic injuries.34,35 Accordingly, improving public awareness, affordability, and access to eye care, particularly for socioeconomically disadvantaged and marginalized groups, may confer broad benefits, mitigating not only visual morbidity but also wider economic, physical, and mental health burdens.36

Together with prior epidemiological studies, our findings provide unique long-term, multiethnic evidence on both incident VI and visual improvement. Despite the importance of such evidence for vision and blindness prevention, reporting metrics and terminologies across studies are often inconsistent and contradictory at times. For instance, the terms binocular, monocular, bilateral, and unilateral are sometimes used interchangeably,4,5,37,38 despite each representing distinct analytical approaches with different clinical and epidemiologic implications. Likewise, comparability across studies is limited because some studies only reported the incidence of low vision and/or blindness,2,3,38 while others reported VI,5,15,22 which is a broader category encompassing both low vision and blindness. Furthermore, even within established cohorts such as the BDES, the definition of visual improvement has evolved over time, from being based on the worse-seeing eye2,38 to the better-seeing eye,3 further complicating cross-study comparison and interpretation. Altogether, these inconsistencies suggest the need for a harmonized framework and standardized nomenclature in population-based ophthalmic epidemiology to enable consistent reporting, facilitate meaningful cross-study comparisons, and support global benchmarking of eye health outcomes.

Strengths of our study include the large, population-based cohort with a wide age distribution and a long 12-year follow-up, providing robust longitudinal findings that are rarely available in ophthalmic epidemiology. Second, this is the only multiethnic Asian cohort study, encompassing the three major Asian ethnicities, namely Malays, Indians, and Chinese, thereby enabling insightful evaluation of ethnic differences in VI and visual improvement. Third, the longitudinal design allowed us to characterize the trajectories of VI over time, including progression, persistence, and visual improvement among individuals with baseline VI, providing an even more holistic understanding of the VI trends and patterns in this population. Finally, the use of consistent, standardized protocols for VA and other systemic and socioeconomic measurements across all study visits enhances the robustness and comparability of our findings.

Nevertheless, several study limitations should be considered. First, the true incidence of VI may have been underestimated, as individuals who did not return for follow-up were older, more likely to have comorbidities, and lower socioeconomic status, and thus may have been more likely to have incident VI or to remain visually impaired. Second, visual field deficit was not included as a criterion in our definition of VI. This may potentially result in slight underestimation of the incidence of VI due to glaucoma.

In conclusion, this study provides robust estimates of the 12-year incidence of VI and visual improvement in a multiethnic Asian population. Our findings offer important insights into the population-level burden and trajectory of vision loss, demonstrating that long-term incidence of VI is largely driven by treatable causes and patterned by socioeconomic disadvantage. Taken together, these findings indicate that a substantial proportion of VI is not only preventable but also reversible. As populations age rapidly, these results can inform health system planning and support more efficient allocation of resources to eye care priorities, ultimately promoting healthier vision and reducing preventable visual impairment.
