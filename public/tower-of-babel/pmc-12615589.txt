Urea and potassium nitrate synergy in sesame cultivation: boosting growth, yield, and antioxidant resilience through tailored nitrogen fertilization
Authors: Khalil M Saad-Allah, Amr Elkelish, Abdelghafar M Abu-Elsaoud, Sami Asir Al-Robai, Dorya Essa, Arfang Badji, Badriah Alammari, Walaa A Abo-Shanab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of different nitrogen sources on sesame (Sesamum indicum L.) growth, yield, and physiological parameters to identify optimal fertilization strategies for maximizing production. Sesame is a valuable seed plant known for its oil, protein, and antioxidant content. A two-season field experiment (2021 and 2022) was conducted to investigate the impact of eight nitrogen sources, applied at 126 kg N/ha, including two organic sources (urea and thiourea), four nitrate salts (sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium nitrates), and two ammonium salts (sulfate and citrate). The experiment followed a completely randomized block design with three replicates. Among the nitrogen sources, urea exhibited superior performance in promoting vegetative growth, nitrogenous compounds, and seed yield. K nitrate emerged as the leading source for enhancing K+ and NO3− concentrations, capsules number, and oil content. Implementing Na nitrate increased catalase and ascorbate peroxidase activities, while urea enhanced glutathione reductase activity, and Mg nitrate stimulated glutathione-S-transferase activity. Moreover, Mg nitrate demonstrated the highest efficacy in improving Chl b levels and photosystem II photochemical efficiency. Consequently, K nitrate and urea can be considered optimal fertilizers for maximizing sesame production. Although urea resulted in the greatest vegetative growth of sesame, ammonium salts (citrate and sulfate) exhibited the second-highest effectiveness in enhancing most of the evaluated parameters. Compared to the least effective nitrogen source, urea significantly increased Chl b (57.1% vs. K nitrate), carotenoids (58.8% vs. Mg nitrate), amino acids (31.6% vs. K nitrate), nitrogen content (208.8% vs. ammonium citrate), seed yield (62.8% vs. thiourea), and oil production (41.1% vs. ammonium citrate). These results demonstrate that urea and K nitrate are optimal nitrogen sources for maximizing sesame growth and yield, providing farmers with targeted fertilization strategies to enhance productivity and sustainable nutrient management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23537-3.Keywords: Antioxidant enzymes, Fertilizer optimization, Nitrogen use efficiency, Oilseed crops, Photosynthetic performance, Yield componentsSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Sesame (Sesamum indicum L.) is one of the most economically and nutritionally significant oilseed crops in the Pedaliaceae family, valued for its high-quality oil, protein content, and antioxidant properties1. Sesame seeds are a rich source of essential fatty acids, proteins, carbohydrates, and key micronutrients, including vitamin E and lignans such as sesamin and sesamolin2–4. These bioactive compounds contribute to the nutraceutical properties of sesame, offering protection against chronic conditions such as hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, cancer, and neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s disease5,6. Additionally, sesame’s dermatological benefits further enhance its value as a functional food. Additionally, the global demand for sesame has surged in recent decades, due to its high-quality oil, protein content, and antioxidant properties7.

As a versatile crop, sesame thrives in diverse climatic and edaphic conditions, making it a resilient agricultural commodity. Beyond its nutritional benefits, sesame holds significant economic importance due to its applications in medicine, biodiesel production, and the cosmetics industry. Notably, sesame seeds contain three times more calcium than an equivalent volume of milk, underscoring their role as a dietary supplement for bone health8. Furthermore, the byproducts of sesame oil extraction, particularly defatted sesame cake, serve as an unconventional yet valuable protein source, enriched with beneficial phytochemicals7. These bioactive compounds enhance the functional properties of sesame byproducts, positioning them as sustainable ingredients for fortified foods and animal feed. Consequently, sesame is not only a vital oilseed crop but also a multifaceted resource with far-reaching industrial and nutritional applications.

As global demand for nutrient-dense crops increases, optimizing sesame cultivation practices, particularly nitrogen (N) fertilization, has become essential for enhancing yield, oil quality, and overall crop sustainability9–12. Nitrogen is a key macronutrient influencing plant growth, seed development, and oil production in oilseed crops13. Studies have demonstrated that sesame responds positively to nitrogen fertilization, with optimal application rates typically ranging between 46 and 100 kg N/ha, depending on cultivar-specific requirements1,14. However, maximizing nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) in sesame cultivation depends not only on the application rate but also on the fertilizer source, timing, and method of application15. Common nitrogen fertilizers, such as urea, ammonium nitrate, and ammonium sulfate, exhibit varying effects on sesame growth and yield, with urea being widely adopted due to its cost-effectiveness and efficiency1,16. For instance, split applications, where nitrogen is applied in two to three doses, including pre-emergence and during flowering, have been shown to significantly improve sesame productivity1,17,18.

Beyond yield enhancement, nitrogen fertilization influences soil microbial dynamics, particularly in sesame rhizosphere communities. Wang et al.19 found that a balanced nitrate-ammonium ratio promotes beneficial bacterial populations while suppressing pathogenic microbes, thereby improving soil health and crop resilience. Additionally, calcium nitrate has been reported to enhance nutrient uptake, stress tolerance, and overall plant vigor in sesame20,21. Despite its benefits, excessive or mismanaged nitrogen fertilization poses serious environmental risks, including groundwater contamination through leaching, atmospheric pollution via volatilization, and soil degradation due to salinization and pH imbalances22. Sustainable nitrogen management strategies are thus critical to minimizing these adverse effects while maintaining high crop productivity23.

The choice of nitrogen (N) fertilizer significantly influences sesame productivity and environmental sustainability. Urea, the most widely used N source, promotes growth at 80 kg/ha but contributes to NH3 volatilization, while calcium nitrate enhances rhizosphere microbiota and reduces N losses19. Ammonium sulfate lowers soil pH, improving micronutrient availability but increasing acidification risks in sensitive regions24. Controlled-release N fertilizers mitigate leaching by 30–50% compared to conventional urea, addressing groundwater contamination concerns25. However, excessive N application (> 100 kg/ha) elevates N2O emissions by 1.2–2.5-fold, exacerbating climate impacts26. Precision fertilization using sensor-based recommendations optimizes N use efficiency (NUE) in crop plants by 25–40%, balancing yield and ecological footprints27.

Despite extensive research on single nitrogen (N) sources in sesame cultivation, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding the synergistic effects of combined N formulations, particularly urea and nitrate nitrogen, on growth, physiological responses, yield quality, and oil content of sesame. Current literature lacks systematic investigation into how these N combinations influence critical physiological processes including redox homeostasis, antioxidant defense systems, photosynthetic efficiency, and mineral composition, while the optimal N formulation for simultaneously enhancing vegetative growth and seed yield remains undetermined. Addressing these gaps, the present study aims to identify the most effective N form for sustaining both vegetative growth and seed yield, elucidate the physiological mechanisms underlying N source effects on photosynthetic pigments, antioxidant potential, and mineral status, and evaluate the impact on oil content and overall productivity. By examining these interconnected parameters, this study could provide a holistic understanding of N management in sesame, bridging the gap between physiological responses and long-term agronomic outcomes. The findings will establish evidence-based recommendations for N fertilization strategies that optimize both crop performance and environmental sustainability in sesame production systems.

Materials and methods

Plant material and experiment setting

The seeds of sesame (Sesamum indicum L., cv Giza 32) were kindly provided by the Agricultural Research Center, Giza, Egypt. The trial was performed in the summers of 2021 and 2022 at the Research Farm of the Faculty of Science, Tanta, Egypt (30.8015º N, 30.9926º E) (supplementary Figure S1). The soil at the designated study site exhibited a clayey nature for the two seasons. Table 1 provides an overview of the physio-chemical properties of the utilized soil throughout the two seasons. The physio-chemical properties of the soil were analyzed according to the approach described by Alvarenga et al.28. The experimental site experienced semi-arid weather with negligible summer precipitation (< 0.001 mm), average day and night temperatures of 36.9/16.3 °C, relative humidity of 57–62%, wind speed of 1.2 m s− 1, and solar radiation of 28.4 MJ m− 2 d− 1. These parameters were continuously monitored using a calibrated weather station positioned at the experimental site, with data recorded at 30-min intervals throughout both growing seasons.

In each season, the experiment was conducted using a randomized complete block design (RCBD). The experimental area comprised 24 blocks (3 replications × 8 treatments), with each treatment randomly assigned within blocks. Individual plot size was 9 m2 (3 × 3 m), separated by 1 m spacing, resulting in a total experimental area of 23 × 15 m29. Wheat was the preceding crop grown in the study soil in both seasons. For soil preparation, a mounted spinning plough with 24 L-shaped blades that are positioned on the complete tractor width was utilized to execute minimal tillage at a depth of 0.15 m on the soil at the study site. The other agricultural practices were carried out following EMALR’s (the Egyptian Ministry of Agriculture and Land Reclamation) recommendations for sesame cultivation.

The sesame seeds were properly cleaned with tap water, surface-sterilized for 5 min with 0.1% (w/v) HgCl2, and then extensively rinsed 5 times with deionized water before sowing. On May 15 for the first season and May 17 for the second, approximately 10 sesame seeds were planted in each hole, with the holes being spaced 30 cm between and the rows being spaced 50 cm separated. The plots were watered according to the recommended field capacity (275 L/m2), and seedlings were pruned to two per hole after the complete seedling establishment (15 days after the planting date). Twenty-day-old seedlings were exposed to eight different nitrogen sources with varying levels of available nitrogen: sodium nitrate (16.0%), potassium nitrate (13.9%), calcium nitrate (15.5%), magnesium nitrate (11.0%), ammonium sulfate (21.0%), ammonium citrate (17.3%) (as inorganic nitrogen sources), urea (46.0%) and thiourea (36.8%) (as organic nitrogen sources), with each nitrogen source uniformly applied as granular fertilizer via manual surface broadcasting at 9 O’clock local time, following complete dew evaporation to ensure optimal soil incorporation and minimize volatilization losses, with the rate of 42.0 kg N/ha (supplementary Figure S2). The fertilizer ddition was performed when the soil reaches 40% field capacity. Fertilizer applications were conducted when soil moisture reached 40% field capacity, as determined by gravimetric analysis of surface soil samples (0–20 cm depth) collected immediately prior to treatment. The different nitrogen formulations were applied at the same rate when the plants reached 45 and 65 days old. Consequently, each plot received 42 kg nitrogen/ha, resulting in a cumulative total of 126 kg of nitrogen/ha throughout the three doses (Table S1). The plots were irrigated with the corresponding field capacity whenever needed (when it reaches 40%) in each season, without any other supplemental nutrients applied throughout the entire growing season, ensuring that all observed plant responses could be only attributed to the experimental nitrogen treatments. When sesame attained the age of 70 days, plants were manually harvested to evaluate growth criteria, pigment content, nitrogenous compounds, antioxidant potential, and ionic concentration. Fresh samples were washed with tap water followed by distilled water, pulverized in liquid nitrogen using mortar and pestle, and kept at -80 °C until use. The oven-dried samples (at 70 °C) were finely powdered using an electric mixer, sieved through a 0.2 mm sieve, and then kept in paper bags until analysis.

Growth criteria assessment

The plants were collected from the soil and washed with distilled water, and then the plant heights (cm/plant) were measured using a graduated tap. Leaf relative water content measurements (%) were performed based on leaf samples’ fresh, turgid, and dry weights.

Photosynthetic pigments and chemical efficiency of photosystem II (Fv/Fm)

The photosynthetic pigments (Chl a, Chl b, and carotenoid) of sesame leaves were determined following the method of Metzner et al.30. The liquid nitrogen-homogenized leaf samples were extracted in 80% pre-chilled acetone, measured at 663, 644, and 452 nm, and the pigments were expressed as mg.g− 1 fresh mass (FM). The photosynthetic efficiency (Fv/Fm) of photosystem II, as determined by chlorophyll fluorescence, was quantified in the fifth fully expanded upper leaf using a digital fluorometer (OS-30 P, Hudson, USA) according to Saad-Allah and Ragab31.

Nitrogenous compounds

According to Bradford32, sesame leaf powders’ total soluble protein concentration was assessed using a calibration curve created using bovine serum albumin as a reference protein.

The amount of total free amino acids was calculated using the ninhydrin approach according to the method of Lee and Takahashi33 based on a clibaration curve contructed by glycine.

Using a standard curve created from proline, the concentration of free proline was measured with the adoption of the ninhydrin-acid reagent as described by Bates et al.34.

The quantity of glycinebetaine (GB) was assessed by reacting with iodine to generate the betaine-periodide complex at low temperature in an acidic medium, as reported by Grieve and Grattan35, using the periodide technique and a standard curve made from GB. All the estimated nitrogenous compounds were reported as mg.g− 1 dry matter (DM).

Estimation of antioxidant compounds

Flavonoids determination in the dry-powdered sesame leaf tissues was performed using the aluminum chloride protocol proposed by Chang et al.36 using quercetin as a standard flavonoid for constructing the standard curve.

Total phenolic compound content was quantified following Jindal and Singh’s method37 using Folin-Ciocalteu’s reagent and gallic acid as standard phenol. The content of total flavonoids and phenols in sesame leaves was expressed as mg.g− 1 DM.

Antioxidant enzymes assay

Liquid nitrogen-grinded sesame fresh leaves were extracted for antioxidant enzymes in 50 mM cold phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 0.1 mM EDTA. Homogenates were centrifuged under cooling at 7000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant was used as a source for assaying the following enzymes:

Catalase (CAT) activity was determined by the Havir and McHale method38 using K-phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) following the decline in H2O2 (20 mM) absorbance at 240 nm.

Guaiacol peroxidase (POD) activity was estimated by the technique proposed by Kato and Shimizu39 depending on the rate of guaiacol (7.2 mM) oxidation by H2O2 (11.8 mM) in the presence of phosphate buffer (pH 5.8). The increase in the samples’ absorbance due to the formation of tetraguaiacol was measured at 470 nm and employed in calculating POD activity.

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was determined by recording the rate of H2O2-dependent decline at 290 nm, which is caused by ascorbate oxidation in a reaction mixture consisting of ascorbic acid (0.5 mM acid), H2O2 (0.25 mM), and EDTA (0.2 mM) in 5 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0)40.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined using a solution of L-methionine (13 mM), nitroblue tetrazolium (75 µM), EDTA (100 µM) and riboflavin (2 µM). A 30 W fluorescent bulb was used to illuminate reaction vessels for 15 min, after which the light was turned off. Formazan, a by-product of nitroblue tetrazolium reduction, was measured at 560 nm41.

The decline in NADPH absorbance at 340 in a reaction mixture of 25 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 0.5 mM oxidized glutathione (GSSG), and 120 µM NADPH was employed for determining the activity of glutathione reductase (GR)42.

According to Habig et al.43, reduced glutathione (GSH) and 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (CDNB) conjugate to create an adduct, which can be employed to monitor the activity of glutathione S-transferase (GST) at 340 nm. GST reaction mixture included 0.75 mM CDNB, 30 mM GSH, and 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.2).

The measured enzyme activities were reported as µM g− 1 FM/min− 1 based on the following extinction coefficients (in mM− 1 cm− 1): 43.6 for CAT, 26.6 for POD, 2.8 for APX, 21.1 for SOD, 6.22 for GR, and 9.6 for GST.

Minerals analysis

By following the procedures of Allen et al.44, wet acid digestion was employed to determine the mineral components of sesame leaves using a mixture of HNO3 and H2O2 (5:3 v/v). For determining nitrogen (N) concentration, the acid digestate was neutralized, and the neutral solution was then reacted with Rochelle reagent, sodium phenate, sodium nitroprusside, and sodium hypochlorite for 15 min. at 37 °C. The optical density was measured at 630, and nitrogen content (mg.g− 1 DM) was assessed from a standard curve utilizing ammonium chloride44. The potassium (K) concentration was determined by an inductively coupled plasma-optical spectrophotometer (Polyscan 61 E, Thermo Jarrell-Ash Corp., Franklin, MA, USA).

The colorimetric approach developed by Cataldo et al.45 was used to assess the nitrate (NO3−) content of sesame leaf powders. The water-based extract of leaf powders was properly mixed with salicylic acid and NaOH. After cooling, the absorbance was measured at 410 nm and the concentration of NO3− (µmol.g− 1 DM) was determined with the aid of a standard curve produced by KNO3. Ammonium (NH4+) ion concentration in the leaf aqueous extracts was measured calorimetrically using the Nessler reagent as described by Molins-Legua et al.46. The leaf extracts were combined with Na-K tartrate solution and Nessler reagent for 5 min.; then the absorbance was measured at 425 nm. NH4+ concentration (µmol.g− 1 DM) was calculated with the aid of an NH4Cl-based standard curve.

Yield analysis

The number of capsules per plant (capsule.plant− 1) and the total seed yield per ha (kg seeds.ha− 1) were computed at the time of harvest, following the full development of the capsules. To determine the seed oil content (kg.ha− 1), dried sesame seeds were pulverized and put through a 6-hour Soxhlet extraction process using n-hexane as a solvent. Following removing the n-hexane under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator at 40 °C, the oil content was determined as g.m− 2.

Statistical analysis

To investigate the effects of the different fertilizer’s formulation under the assumption of randomness, a comprehensive analysis was conducted by aggregating and examining experimental findings spanning two consecutive seasons. Prior to combined analysis, we rigorously validated parametric assumptions using Hartley’s Fmax test for variance homogeneity (Fmax = 3.21, P > 0.05), confirming comparable error variances between years. A two-way ANOVA incorporating year × treatment interactions revealed non-significant effects for most traits (P > 0.25), justifying data pooling across the 2021–2022 growing seasons (n = 3 replicates year− 1). The combined dataset was analyzed via mixed-effects ANOVA using CoStat software (CoHort, V. 6.311), with experimental blocks treated as random effects and treatments as fixed factors. Post hoc comparisons employed Fisher’s LSD test (P ≤ 0.05) only after confirming model assumptions through: (1) residual normality (Shapiro-Wilk, W ≥ 0.93), and (2) homoscedasticity (Levene’s test, P = 0.18). Inter-variable relationships were quantified using Pearson correlation matrices using GraphPad Prism software (v 8.3.0), with Bonferroni-adjusted significance thresholds to control for multiple comparisons.

Results

Growth criteria

Figure 1 depicts how sesame (Sesamum indicum L. cv Giza 32) plants responded to eight different nitrogen sources (Na nitrate, K nitrate, Ca nitrate, Mg nitrate, ammonium sulfate, ammonium citrate, urea, and thiourea) in terms of shoot height and leaf relative water content. The administered nitrogen formulations exhibited a significant (P < 0.01) impact on the investigated growth traits of sesame.

Effect of different nitrogen sources on growth criteria of sesame plants. (A) shoot height, and (B) leaf relative water content. Values represent means ± standard error (SE) of 3 replicates. Bars sharing the same lowercase letter within each panel are not statistically different according to Fisher’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) test (P < 0.05).

Urea and Mg nitrate exhibited the highest sesame shoot heights (133.4 and 126.6 cm). Other nitrate, sulfate and ammonium salts of nitrogen fertilizers demonstrated similar effects on shoot height, with Ca nitrate yielding the lowest (103.8 cm). Thus, compared to the Ca nitrate treatment, shoot height increased by 28.52% with urea and 21.97% with Mg nitrate. The maximum leaf relative water content (RWC) was established by Ca nitrate (91.6%), Na nitrate (88.9%), and ammonium citrate (87.9%). K nitrate resulted in the least RWC (74.4%) of all nitrogen sources. Consequently, RWC increased by 23.12, 19.45, and 18.15% with Ca nitrate, Na nitrate, and ammonium citrate, respectively, compared to the K nitrate treatment.

Leaf pigments and photosynthetic activity (Fv/Fm)

The data illustrated in Fig. 2 highlights the response of sesame leaves to various nitrogen formulations in terms of photosynthetic pigments (Chl a, Chl b, and carotenoids) and photosynthetic activity. The administered nitrogen formulations non-significantly (P > 0.05) affected Chl a, significantly (P < 0.05) affected Chl b, but highly significant (P < 0.01) affected carotenoids and photosynthetic activity.

Although statistical analysis indicated no significant difference (P = 0.289) among treatments for Chl a content, urea achieved the highest value (0.80 mg.g− 1 FM), while sodium nitrate yielded the lowest (0.68 mg.g− 1 FM). Furthermore, the two organic nitrogen formulations, urea, thiourea, and Mg nitrate, exhibited similar significant (P = 0.022) promoting influence on Chl b, as they yielded the maximum content. As a result, urea increased Chl a and Chl b by 17.6 and 57.1%, respectively, as compared to Na nitrate and Mg nitrate. Additionally, urea and Ca nitrate provoked the highest carotenoid content (0.27 mg.g− 1 FM), but Mg nitrate provoked the lowest (0.17 mg.g− 1 FM). So, relative to Mg nitrate, application of urea and Ca nitrate high significantly (P = 0.008) resulted in a 58.82% increase in carotenoid content (P = 0.008). Regarding the photosynthetic activity of photosystem II (Fv/Fm), urea and Mg nitrate resulted in the highest (0.795) activity. However, the least activity (0.777) was reported when Na nitrate and K nitrate were used as nitrogen sources. So, the results showed that, though highly significant (P < 0.001) change in Fv/Fm with various nitrogen formulations, photosynthetic activity non-significantly affected, with urea and Mg nitrate offering only 2.32% increase over K and Na nitrate.

Nitrogenous compounds

In the present investigation, the administered nitrogen sources differed in their effects on soluble proteins, free amino acids (FAA), free proline, and glycine betaine (GB) (Fig. 3). Variations in nitrogen sources highly significantly (P < 0.01) influenced protein, proline, and GB contents in sesame leaves. In contrast, FAA content was significantly impacted (P < 0.05).

Effect of different nitrogen sources on nitrogenous compounds in sesame plants (A) total soluble proteins, (B) free amino acids, (C) proline content, and (D) glycinebetaine content. Values represent means ± standard error (SE) of 3 replicates. The highest protein level (31.75 mg.g− 1 DM) in the present investigation was triggered by nitrogen incorporation in thiourea; however, all other used forms of nitrogen produced a relatively similar impact on the protein. Accordingly, protein level under thiourea treatment exceeded that of Na nitrate (the least protein level) by 68.34%. In terms of FAA, urea and Ca nitrate triggered the highest content (28.86 and 26.25 mg.g− 1 DM, respectively), whereas the other forms of nitrogen showed, particularly K nitrate, ammonium sulfate, ammonium citrate, and thiourea, were statistically similar in their effect on FAA, as indicated by their grouping in the same homogenous subset (b). Compared to K nitrate (21.93 mg.g− 1 DM), urea and Ca nitrate application increased FAA content by 31.60 and 19.70%, respectively. Proline, on the other hand, displayed a maximum concentration (10.90 mg.g− 1 DM) when sesame plants were fertigated with urea and ammonium sulfate. However, compared to the other nitrogen sources, ammonium citrate fertigation of sesame showed the least proline accumulation (9.20 mg.g− 1 DM). Therefore, proline content was 18.48% higher under urea and ammonium sulfate treatments than under ammonium citrate. Additionally, our findings demonstrated that the quaternary ammonium compound GB reached its highest concentration when urea fertilizer was applied to sesame (2.04 mg g− 1 DM). In contrast, applying ammonium sulfate as a fertilizer achieved the lowest content (1.94 mg.g− 1 DM). Hence, urea fertigation significantly (P < 0.01) triggered 5.15% increment in GB accumulation compared to ammonium sulfate fertigation. Overall, our findings showed that nitrogen administration in the form of urea was most advantageous for nitrogen assimilation in sesame plants.

Antioxidant molecules

The prospective variation in the oxidative status of sesame brought on by the change in the formulation of nitrogen fertilizers applied is represented in Fig. 4. The results of the statistical analysis showed that the different applied forms of nitrogen sources have a highly significant impact on the total amount of flavonoids and phenols in sesame leaves. Sesame showed the maximum quantity of flavonoids when urea, Ca nitrate, and ammonium sulfate were employed as nitrogen sources (4.70–4.75 mg g− 1 DM). The lowest flavonoid content, on the other hand, was produced by fertigating sesame with thiourea and Na nitrate (3.77–3.85 mg g− 1 DM). All in all, ammonium sulfate, Ca nitrate, and urea yielded increases in flavonoid content of 26.09, 25.83, and 24.61%, respectively, compared to thiourea.

The various nitrogen sources employed in this experiment were also shown to affect the content of phenolic compounds, a class of secondary metabolites known to contribute to antioxidant defense mechanisms in plants. In sesame leaves, ammonium sulfate led to the build-up of a significant amount of phenols (11.70 mg g− 1 DM), followed by Mg nitrate (11.51 mg g− 1 DM). On the other hand, K nitrate, among the applied nitrogen sources, accumulated the lowest amount of phenols (8.23 mg g− 1 DM). Accordingly, compared to Mg nitrate, ammonium sulfate and Mg nitrate provoked 42.16, and 39.85, respectively, overaccumulation of phenols.

So, considering the preceding data, a clear and significant positive correlation was observed between the effects of nitrogen sources on flavonoid and phenol content. Treatments that enhanced flavonoid accumulation, specifically ammonium sulfate, and Ca nitrate, concurrently promoted the highest phenolic compound levels. This consistent pattern across treatments indicates that nitrogen source manipulation coordinately regulates the biosynthesis of these key phenol and flavonoid antioxidants in sesame. Therefore, the response is not isolated but represents a correlated enhancement of the plant’s secondary metabolism.

Antioxidant enzymes

The type of nitrogen source applied to sesame plants significantly affected the antioxidant enzymes’ activity (Fig. 5). Following the incorporation of different nitrogen formulations, highly significant variations (P < 0.01) in the activities of catalase (CAT), guaiacol peroxidase (POD), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione reductase (GR), and glutathione-S-transferase (GST) were determined.

The data revealed that nitrogen formulations high significantly (P < 0.01) affected CAT activity in sesame plants. The plants treated with Na and K nitrates exhibited the highest CAT activity (13.84 and 12.60 µM g− 1 FM/min− 1, respectively), whereas those treated with Ca nitrate and thiourea possessed the lowest CAT activity (7.48 µM g− 1 FM/min− 1). Accordingly, Na and K nitrate enhanced CAT activity by 85.03 and 68.45%, respectively, over the activity reported by Ca nitrate and thiourea. In terms of POD activity, there were highly significant differences (P < 0.001) among the administered nitrogen forms. The most pronounced POD activity was achieved by Ca nitrate (9.73 µM.g− 1 FM/min− 1) and ammonium sulfate (9.68 µM g− 1 FM/min− 1), whilst the lowest activities (4.34 and 4.88 µM.g− 1 FM/min− 1) were attained by Mg and Na nitrates, respectively. In this regrd, the potency of Ca nitrate and ammonium sulfate as activators of POD activity was evident, yielding increases of 124.19 and 123.04%, respectively, relative to Mg nitrate. The variability in SOD activity with the nitrogen form was highly significant (P < 0.001) throughout the current investigation. Ammonium sulfate and thiourea were the highest contributors to SOD activity (0.100 and 0.097 µM g− 1 FM/min− 1, respectively). When Na nitrate was used as nitrogen fertilizer, the lowest SOD activity (0.053 µM g− 1 FM/min− 1) was asserted. Markedly, a noticeable discrepancy in SOD activity was monitored, with ammonium sulfate and thiourea higher than Na nitrate activity by 88.68 and 83.02%, respectively.

In response to the implementation of nitrogen fertilizer, APX activity also significantly varied (P = 0.000). Na nitrate resulted in the greatest APX activity (0.16 µM g− 1 FM/min− 1), followed by ammonium sulfate (0.14 µM.g− 1 FM/min− 1). Contrarily, urea as an organic form of nitrogen resulted in the lowest APX activity (0.04 µM g− 1 FM/min− 1). Notably, a pronounced disparity in APX activity was observed, with Na nitrate and ammonium sulfate exceeding the urea activity by 300.00 and 250.00%, respectively. Likewise, highly significant (P < 0.001) variations in GR ctivity as influenced by the applied nitrogen sources was attained. The urea application resulted in the highest GR activity (9.27 µM.g− 1 FM/min− 1), whereas thiourea produced the lowest (4.65 µM g− 1 FM/min− 1) activity. Accordingly, a significant difference in GR activity was found between the nitrogen sources. The activity level under urea treatment was 99.35% higher than that of the thiourea treatment. A highly significant variation (P < 0.001) in GST activity was observed across the different nitrogen sources. Ca nitrate was the most effective form of nitrogen fertilizers in stimulating GST activity (0.030 µM.g− 1 FM/min− 1), whereas ammonium citrate and sulfate resulted in the least activities of GST (0.010 and 0.011 µM g− 1 FM/min− 1, respectively). This result demonstrates the pronounced superiority of Ca nitrate in ctivating GST. This teratment enhanced GST activity by 200.00% compared to ammonium citrate and by 172.72% compared to ammonium sulfate.

The overall assessment of enzyme activities response to the applied nitrogen sources indicates that no single nitrogen source optimized all enzymes; however, ammonium sulfate consistently enhanced multiple key enzymes (POD, SOD, APX), marking it as a highly effective treatment. Furthermore, specific sources excelled in activating enzymes: Ca nitrate for POD and GST, urea for GR, and Na and K nitrate for CAT. This demonstrates that the antioxidant response is highly specific to the nitrogen form applied.

Ionic content

The ionic content of sesame plants was shown to be significantly impacted by the type of nitrogen fertilizer employed in the present investigation (Fig. 6). Changes in nitrogen fertilizer formulation led to highly significant variations (P < 0.01) in nitrogen (N), potassium (K), and ammonium (NH4+) concentrations, but only a significant (P < 0.05) variation in the concentration of nitrate (NO3−). The maximum N content (6.30 mg.g− 1 DM) was attained when urea was administered as a fertilizer, whereas the lowest N content (2.04 mg.g− 1 DM) was attained following the usage of ammonium citrate as a nitrogen fertilizer. This result demonstrates the superior efficacy of urea, which increased the nitrogen content by 208.82% compared to ammonium citrate. In addition, the concentration of K significantly varied in response to the type of nitrogen fertilizer incorporated. When K nitrate was used as a nitrogen fertilizer, the concentration of K was the most prominent (11.57 mg.g− 1 DM), but when Mg nitrate was used, the concentration of K was at its lowest value (6.35 mg.g− 1 DM). This result underscores a significant nutritional interaction, whereby K nitrate fertilization increased sesame K concentration by 82.20% compared to Mg nitrate.

Also, the concentration of NH4+ was found to be a function of the applied nitrogen fertilizer. Notably, thiourea application boosted NH4+ accumulation (3.16 mg g− 1 DM) in sesame leaves, whereas Ca nitrate administration resulted in the lowest concentration of NH4+ (0.83 mg.g− 1 DM). This prominent difference highlights pronounced effect of thiourea on NH4+ accumulation, yielding a concentration 280.72% greater than that resulting from Ca nitrate fertilization. However, compared to the other ions under investigation, the NO3− concentration was the least responsive to the different forms of nitrogen fertilizers employed. Sesame plants treated with K nitrate demonstrated the highest NO3− (2.23 mg.g− 1 DM) levels, whereas plants treated with Ca nitrate exhibited the lowest level (1.54 mg.g− 1 DM). This result indicates a clear differential effect, with K nitrate fertilization leading to a 44.81% greater NO3− accumulation compared to Ca nitrate.

Yield attributes

According to the data in Fig. 7, the application of different nitrogen formulations experienced highly significant (P < 0.01) effects in the evaluated sesame yield components, comprising the number of capsules per plant, the number of seeds produced per plant, and the oil content. Applying K nitrate as fertilizer brought about the highest number of capsules (32.0) produced per individual plant. Urea was the second-best fertilizer that yielded the greatest number of capsules (30.6). Ammonium citrate, however, resulted in the least number of capsules (20.0) per individual sesame plant. This result demonstrates a substantial agronomic advantage of K nitrate and urea, which increased capsule number per plant by 60.0% and 53.0%, respectively, over the capsule yield achieved with ammonium citrate.

Sesame fertigation with urea offered the highest value (663.56 kg.ha− 1), followed by K nitrate (556.70 kg.ha− 1), regarding the seed yield. However, thiourea application resulted in the lowest yield of seeds (407.49 kg.ha− 1) among the investigated nitrogen formulations. In conclusion, the superior performance of urea and K nitrate is quantified by seed yield increases of 62.84 and 36.62%, respectively, over thiourea, confirming urea as the optimal choice for maximizing sesame seed yield. As a result, urea can be considered the best-performing nitrogen form that could be incorporated as a fertilizer to achieve optimum sesame seed productivity.

As sesame is an oily crop, it was crucial to contrast the different nitrogen formulations regarding oil production. The various nitrogen sources high significantly (P < 0.001) affected the oil content of sesme seeds. K nitrate and urea demonstrated the maximum oil concentration in sesame seeds yielding 18.81 and 18.25 kg ha− 1, respectively. However, after sesame fertigation with ammonium citrate, the least oil content (12.93 kg.ha− 1) was recorded. This result highlights a substantial increase in oil yield of 45.48% with K nitrate and 41.14% with urea, confirming their significant advantage over ammonium citrate.

In conclusion, based on the assessed yield and oil-related traits, urea emerged as the most effective nitrogen source overall. While K nitrate was superior for the number of capsules per plant and shared the highest oil concentration, urea was the best treatment for the most critical agronomic parameter; seed yield (663.56 kg·ha− 1), while also performing as a close second in capsule number and oil production. Therefore, urea is identified as the optimal nitrogen formulation for maximizing overall productivity and oil yield in sesame.

Discussion

The choice of nitrogen sources significantly influences sesame plants’ growth, yield, antioxidant status, and oil content. Various nitrogen forms, such as ammonium, nitrate, and urea, impact plant physiology differently. Ammonium, readily taken up by plants, can enhance vegetative growth but may lead to nitrogen toxicity if over-applied. Nitrate, on the other hand, promotes leaf development and photosynthesis efficiency, potentially increasing yield. Urea, a cost-effective nitrogen source, supports growth and yield by providing a balanced nitrogen supply. The antioxidant status of sesame plants is crucial for stress tolerance and overall health. Nitrogen sources can influence the antioxidant capacity of plants, with balanced nitrogen levels promoting the production of antioxidants like phenolic compounds and flavonoids. The oil content in sesame seeds, essential for their commercial value, is influenced by nitrogen availability. Optimal nitrogen supply can enhance seed oil accumulation, producing higher oil content and quality. Balancing the use of different nitrogen sources is essential to maximize growth, yield, antioxidant status, and oil content in sesame plants. Proper management strategies tailored to the specific needs of sesame cultivation can optimize these factors and improve overall crop productivity.

While nitrogen fertilizers play a crucial role in modern agriculture, the environmental impacts of nitrogen fertilizers, such as nitrogen leaching and greenhouse gas emissions, are significant considerations. Urea can contribute to nitrogen leaching, which leads to groundwater contamination and eutrophication of water bodies47. Thiourea, a less common nitrogen fertilizer, also poses similar risks of nitrogen leaching if not managed effectively. Nitrate fertilizers are more water-soluble, increasing the potential for nitrogen leaching into groundwater if overapplied or in areas with high precipitation. Moreover, these water-soluble fertilizers can contribute to nitrous oxide emissions, a potent greenhouse gas, through nitrification and denitrification in soil48. Ammonium sulfate and ammonium citrate, which contain ammonium nitrogen, are prone to ammonia volatilization. Hence, excessive ammonia emissions can lead to air pollution and acid rain formation, impacting human health and ecosystems49. To mitigate these environmental impacts, best management practices such as precision agriculture, split applications, and nitrification inhibitors can help optimize nitrogen fertilizer use efficiency, reduce nitrogen losses, and minimize greenhouse gas emissions. Additionally, sustainable farming practices like crop rotation, cover cropping, and agroforestry can enhance soil health, promote nutrient cycling, and reduce the reliance on synthetic nitrogen fertilizers, fostering more environmentally friendly agricultural systems.

The results of the current investigation established that the different nitrogen sources that were implemented impacted sesame growth as measured by shoot height and relative water. This may be because nitrogen increases the number and length of internodes, which increases plant height50. This study also clarifies the effectiveness of urea and Mg and Ca nitrates as essential nitrogen sources for sesame. In harmony with our results, Jan and Khan51 reported that nitrate promotes growth more than ammonium, but their effects vary by species, habitat, and soil condition. Furthermore, Li et al.52 showed that urea and calcium nitrate promoted maize and wheat growth by offering the highest WUEs and reduced nitrogen loss. In this study, the improved sesame growth and hydraulic status after the receipt of Mg nitrate could be attributable to the increased chlorophyll synthesis, enzymatic activities, and improved nitrogen assimilation. Urea may also save plant energy as organic nitrogen (is rapidly hydrolyzed to ammonium (NH4+) by soil urease enzymes, and its physiological effects are therefore primarily mediated through ammonium assimilation) is rapidly absorbed into amino acids and proteins.

The observed variations in leaf relative water content under different nitrogen treatments can be attributed to the distinct roles of the accompanying cations (Na, K, Ca, and NH4) in plant water regulation. Our data shows that the highest RWC was achieved with Ca nitrate (91.6%), Na nitrate (88.9%), and ammonium citrate (87.9%), while K nitrate resulted in the lowest RWC (74.4%). The superior performance of Ca nitrate can be explained by the crucial role of calcium in maintaining cell wall and membrane integrity. Calcium stabilizes cell membranes, which can reduce electrolyte leakage and improve the ability of the plant to retain water, thus leading to a higher RWC53. While high concentrations of Na are generally toxic to plants, at lower concentrations, it can act as an osmoticum, helping to maintain cell turgor and water uptake. This could explain the relatively high RWC observed in the Na nitrate treatment54. Furthermore, the high RWC in ammonium citrate treatment could be linked to its rapid assimilation by plants than nitrate. This more efficient assimilation could lead to improved overall plant health and, consequently, better water status. Conversely, the significantly lower RWC in the K nitrate treatment may be due to nutritional imbalance, potentially antagonizing the uptake of other essential cations (e.g. Ca and Mg), which are vital for membrane stability and chlorophyll function. This imbalance could have impaired the plant ability to regulate water effectively, leading to the observed lower RWC55.

The differential response of photosynthetic pigments to nitrogen sources underscores the critical role of nitrogen form in regulating chloroplast function. While Chl a content did not vary significantly across treatments, the highest value under urea fertilization aligns with its role as efficient nitrogen source that supports chlorophyll biosynthesis56. The significant promotion of Chl b by urea and thiourea, organic forms that facilitate gradual nitrogen release, suggest enhanced synthesis of light-harvesting complexes, consistent with findings by Zhang et al.57 in potato. Urea, as an organic source, may be more efficiently assimilated into the amino acids (like glutamate) that serve as precursors for chlorophyll biosynthesis, thereby promoting pigment accumulation58. Furthermore, the inclusion of Mg nitrate among the top performers for Chl b is expected, as the Mg ion is the central component of the chlorophyll molecule, and its availability is critical for synthesis.

The impact of nitrogen sources extended to carotenoids, essential pigments for light harvesting and photoprotection. Urea and Ca nitrate treatments yielded the highest carotenoid content; however, the lowest content found with Mg nitrate. The positive effect of urea aligns with the concept that efficient nitrogen metabolism supports the synthesis of all nitrogen-containing compounds59, including the enzymes required for the carotenoid biosynthesis pathway. The strong performance of Ca nitrate may be linked to the role of Ca as a secondary messenger in signaling pathways that regulate plant responses to light and stress60, which could involve modulating carotenoid levels for photoprotection. The maximum quantum efficiency of PSII, measured as Fv/Fm, showed a significant response to the different nitrogen treatments. However, the absolute differences were small. Urea and Mg nitrate treatments resulted in the highest Fv/Fm value, a modest increase over the lowest activity observed with Na nitrate and K nitrate. This slight improvement with urea and Mg nitrate suggests a more efficient use of light energy in the primary photochemical reactions. This can be associated with the role of urea in promoting chlorophyll and carotenoid levels, ensure efficient light capture, while sufficient Mg (from Mg nitrate) is essential for the structural and functional integrity of PSII reaction centers61. The slightly lower Fv/Fm values in the Na nitrate and K nitrate treatments could indicate an indirect stress or ionic imbalance that minimally impaired the efficiency of the photosynthetic machinery.

When various nitrogen forms were applied to sesame, soluble proteins, amino acids, proline, and glycine betaine (GB) were increased, with urea superior in protein and GB concentrations. Urea is a form of organic nitrogen and is an essential nitrogen source for many metabolic processes, including the biosynthesis of proteins and amino acids, nucleic acid metabolism, enzyme activity, the formation of various organs and organelles, etc62. These results are in harmony with those of Pal et al.63 in chickpeas, Witte et al.64 in potatoes, and Ochieng’ et al.65 in maize. Also, the enhanced nitrogen metabolism of Ca and Mg nitrates may be explained by the fact that nitrate treatment results in soil alkalinization and improves the uptake of essential cations onto the surface of the soil exchange complex65. As a result, the mutually beneficial synergistic action of nitrogen and other cations is enhanced, causing an increased nitrogen metabolism, producing proteins, amino acids, and GB.

Our study found that sesame plants fertilized with ammonium sulfate and urea accumulated significantly higher concentrations of antioxidant components, including flavonoids and phenols, than those treated with other nitrogen sources. The observed enhancement in phenolic and flavonoid contents under ammonium sulfate and urea treatments can be attributed to specific nitrogen-form-mediated physiological mechanisms. Urea, upon rapid hydrolysis to ammonium, and direct ammonium application via ammonium sulfate, are efficiently assimilated into amino acids. This process provides increased carbon skeletons and energy (ATP/NADPH) from the TCA cycle, which subsequently fuels the phenylpropanoid pathway66,67. Specifically, nitrogen assimilation shifts carbon flux towards the production of secondary metabolites, including phenolic compounds and flavonoids, by enhancing the activity of key enzymes like phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), which uses phenylalanine, a primary product of nitrogen assimilation, as its substrate67. This mechanistic link between efficient ammonium assimilation and secondary metabolism is well-supported; for instance, Machado et al.68 documented that ammonium sulfate outperformed other nitrogen sources in increasing total phenol content, correlating this with higher nutrient uptake efficiency and precursor availability. Similarly, the study by Mirzamohammadi et al.66 confirmed that the phenolic increase in urea-fertilized plants is not merely correlated with, but is a direct physiological consequence of, enhanced nitrogen availability and assimilation, which redirects carbon allocation towards defensive secondary compounds.

Compared to other fertilizers, Na nitrate increased CAT and APX activities, while ammonium sulfate increased SOD, urea increased GR, and Mg nitrate increased GST. In addition to supporting proper plant growth, nitrogen has been reported to preserve plants’ oxidative balance69. According to Vijayan et al.70, nitrate uptake promotes cells to produce more ATP, and the reduction of NO3− into NH4+ is the key mechanism for keeping pH homeostasis during hypoxia. This suggests nitrogen minimizes plants’ free radicals and antioxidant enzyme consumption, sustaining oxidation balance. Appropriate nitrogen source administration has been reported to boost antioxidant enzyme activity and decrease maize leaf senescence substantially71. Additionally, appropriate nitrogen fertilizer application can boost antioxidant enzyme activity by promoting gene expression in their synthesis72. The increased activity of antioxidant enzymes and induced soluble proteins, on the other hand, were attributed by Maleva et al.73 to their involvement in enhancing plant tolerance to the oxidative stress brought on by urea. The expression and activity of antioxidant enzymes, including POD, CAT, and SOD were reported to be effectively correlated with nitrogen availability in plant leaves74. Samuel et al.75 demonstrated that urea and nitrate fertilizers caused increased intracellular H2O2 levels and antioxidant enzyme activity. The concomitant increase in GR and decrease in APX activity under urea treatment could be attributed to urea assimilation intracellularly generates ammonium ions, potentially imposing a mild metabolic stress that disrupts the redox equilibrium. The observed increase in GR activity is likely a compensatory response for maintaining the reduced glutathione (GSH) pool, essential for the ascorbate-glutathione (AsA-GSH) cycle and ROS detoxification. Conversely, the decline in APX may indicate a strategic shift in the antioxidant pathway, favoring glutathione-mediated detoxification over the ascorbate-dependent system, possibly due to substrate limitation or post-translational regulation under these specific conditions. This altered enzymatic profile suggests a nitrogen source-specific reconfiguration of the antioxidant network.

So, considering the obtained data, evidence of nitrogen-source-specific induction of distinct detoxification pathways. The high CAT and APX activities under nitrate treatments (Na and K nitrate) suggest a primary role in H2O2 scavenging, likely due to nitrate-induced oxidative stress. Conversely, the pronounced stimulation of GR and GST by urea and Ca nitrate, respectively, indicates a shift toward glutathione-mediated detoxification and regeneration. The decline in APX activity coupled with GR activation under urea suggests a strategic reallocation within the ascorbate-glutathione cycle, prioritizing glutathione reductase activity to maintain redox homeostasis. These responses reflect tailored antioxidant strategies dependent on the metabolic and oxidative challenges imposed by each nitrogen form.

Total nitrogen (N) was more prominent after using urea and Ca nitrate, while potassium (K+) increased by K nitrate and ammonium citrate administration. Thiourea was leading in increasing NH4+ concentration, whereas K nitrate was leading in increasing NO3− concentration, and ammonium citrate occupied the second position owing to both ions’ concentration. The increased total nitrogen content within plant tissues has been reported in spinach76, rice77, and maize78. Irshad et al.78 investigated the higher nitrogen accumulation following urea administration because urea-N is more readily available to plants than other nitrogen sources. The addition of K nitrate as a fertilizer has been reported to increase K concentration in many plant species, including bell pepper79, barley80, and citrus81. The accumulation of K+ has been described as a passive process; it doesn’t involve an additional energy cost81, allowing the plant to keep growing, as demonstrated in our experiment after fertilization of sesame with K nitrate. Thiourea improves plant development by increasing leaf nitric oxide production82. Furthermore, the NH4+-increasing effect of thiourea treatment might be attributed to the decreased mineralization of nitrogen or the stabilizing influence of sulfur in its composition. K nitrate may cause higher NO3− concentrations due to enhanced rhizosphere-to-leaf absorption and mobility and reduced nitrate reductase activity. The variation in NH4+ and nitrate NO3− content is a direct function of the chemical form of nitrogen applied. Treatments like thiourea and urea are likely to release or be metabolized to ammonium, leading to its accumulation. Conversely, nitrate sources (e.g., K, Na, Ca nitrate) directly supply NO₃⁻, necessitating its assimilation by nitrate reductase. The differences in accumulation between nitrate sources themselves (e.g., K vs. Ca nitrate) further suggest that the accompanying cation (Na, K, Ca, and Mg) can significantly influence the uptake and/or assimilation efficiency of the nitrate ion.

Sesame seed yield depends largely on the number per plant. Our study demonstrated that K nitrate and urea boosted capsule production and seed yield per plant. In accordance with our findings, Qiqige et al.83 reported that nitrate-nitrogen-treated potato plants produced more tubers than ammonium-nitrogen-treated plants. Although ammonium assimilation is easier than nitrate assimilation, the overabundance of ammonium can diminish photochemical efficiency and photon efficacy in photosynthesis. Alternatively, nitrate-nitrogen can boost light energy and energy transformation efficiency, improving yield performance84. Nonetheless, urea fertilization resulted in better maize yield than the other nitrogen fertilization methods, according to Li et al.52. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that urea significantly improves the uptake of other nutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, Na, Fe, Cu, Mn, and Zn), as well as the interaction of nitrogen with these nutrients, leading to better yield85. Many features, including the oil and protein content, can detect sesame seed quality. According to Couch et al.86, 31–66% of the nitrogen consumed by the plant is remobilized to capsules and seeds since oil and protein are largely composed of nitrogen. The enhanced oil concentration of oily crops by urea application has been reported by Salih (2013)87 and Kakabouki et al.88.

Accordingly, the form of nitrogen directly influences sesame seed quality by regulating the remobilization of assimilated nitrogen into seed storage compounds (oil and protein). Our results confirm that urea application significantly enhances oil concentration, aligning with the established role of nitrogen in seed filling. This principle, that targeted nutrient management dictates physiological outcomes, is reinforced by advances in oilseed agriculture, where precision fertilization strategies in crops like soybean and camelina have improved yield and quality under stress89,90. Thus, nitrogen source is a critical determinant of resource allocation, ultimately shaping both crop growth and end-product quality.

Conclusion

Nitrogen fertilizers are widely used to boost plant growth and productivity to maintain food security for the growing world population. This study showed that differences in growth rate, photosynthetic pigments, nitrogen assimilation, redox homeostasis, ionic content, and yield attributes of sesame were affected by the different nitrogen forms, and this could be attributed to differences in mechanisms underlying nitrogen assimilation and energy costs of the supplemented nitrogen source. Based on the study results, it was evident that urea and magnesium nitrate emerge as the most favorable nitrogen sources for enhancing sesame growth, chlorophyll content, photosynthetic activity, and productivity. Despite exhibiting notable leaf relative water content and carotenoid levels, Ca nitrate lagged behind in promoting sesame growth and yield. Urea exhibited remarkable influence on Chl a, carotenoids, flavonoids, and oil content, making it a versatile choice for sesame fertigation. Furthermore, urea and K nitrate proved pivotal in maximizing seed yield and oil content, emphasizing their superiority in ensuring optimal sesame productivity. In conclusion, for optimal sesame growth, development, and seed yield, a combination of urea and K nitrate as fertilizers is recommended to achieve sustainable and enhanced sesame cultivation outcomes. However, while this study was conducted in semi-arid conditions, the impact of various nitrogen fertilizers on sesame plants’ growth, yield, and physiological parameters could vary across different agroecological zones, raising an essential consideration for future investigations. Factors such as soil types, climate conditions, and management practices can significantly influence the study results. Furthermore, our study demonstrates the efficacy of specific nitrogen forms, a key limitation is that the underlying molecular mechanisms driving the observed physiological changes were not elucidated. Future research employing transcriptomic and proteomic analyses is recommended to identify the genetic and enzymatic pathways responsible for the differential responses to nitrogen sources. From a practical standpoint, the recommended application of urea and K nitrate offers a strategy for enhancing sesame productivity and oil yield, which could improve economic returns for farmers. Further field trials are necessary to validate these results across diverse growing seasons and agro-climatic conditions to articulate robust and sustainable nitrogen management protocols.
