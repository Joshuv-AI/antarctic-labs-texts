Generation of G9 genotype porcine rotavirus using reverse genetics system and its application for antiviral screen and vaccine development
Authors: Nan Han, Ran Tao, Xi Cheng, Hui Deng, Jianxin Wang, Xianyu Bian, Jinzhu Zhou, Xuejiao Zhu, Min Sun, Xuehan Zhang, Mi Hu, Rongli Guo, Wei Wang, Siyuan Ding, Bin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGroup A rotaviruses (RVs) continue to be one of the most important pathogens causing severe acute gastroenteritis in infants and young animals worldwide. Recently, the prevalence of porcine RV (PoRV) from pig farms has strikingly increased, adversely affecting the swine industry, particularly with the G9 genotype of PoRV VP7 emerging as the predominant genotype spreading in China. Current vaccines against PoRV fail to provide sufficient protective immunity, necessitating urgent development of effective vaccines and antiviral drugs against PoRV. Here, we successfully established and improved the entirely plasmid-based reverse genetics (RG) system to rescue a G9 genotype of recombinant PoRV AHFY2022 strain (G9P[23]). Using the improved RG system, we rescued recombinant AHFY2022 harboring the fluorescent UnaG protein or nano-luciferase (NLuc) reporter within gene segment 7 that encodes non-structural protein 3 (NSP3). Furthermore, we adopted the UnaG reporter virus to screen anti-PoRV drugs and identified two promising antiviral drugs, C8 and C9. Moreover, we generated the recombinant PoRV (rAHFY2022-G5-VP7) containing G5 genotype of VP7 from PoRV-positive samples in the backbone of AHFY2022 strain. The reassortant strain exhibited efficient replication and genetic stability. Mouse models were utilized to evaluate the immune responses elicited by rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 strain in vivo, revealing similar neutralizing antibodies and cellular immune response compared to the parental AHFY2022 strain in mice. Together, this study provides an important tool for screening potential anti-PoRV drugs and developing novel vaccines against prevalent PoRV strains.Keywords: Reverse genetics, Reporter virus, Porcine rotavirus (PoRV), Antivirals, Vaccine development

Introduction

Rotaviruses (RVs) are the major pathogens causing acute diarrhea in children, piglets, calves, and other young animals, which severely damage human health and animal husbandry industry worldwide (Troeger et al., 2018; Vlasova et al., 2017). RVs are double-stranded ribonucleic acid (dsRNA) viruses belonging to the genus Rotavirus in the family of Sedoreoviridae. RV genome consists of 11 segments encoding 6 structural proteins (VP1–VP4, VP6 and VP7) and 5 non-structural proteins (NSP1–NSP5/6). RVs were classified into 11 groups (A–D, and F–L) based on the antigenic characterization of the VP6 gene (Matthijnssens et al., 2012). Group A RV (RVA) is the primary causative agent of RV-associated diarrhea due to its high prevalence and pathogenicity. A dual typing system based on the outer capsid proteins VP7 and VP4, known as G and P genotype, was established (Matthijnssens et al., 2008).

Porcine rotavirus (PoRV), first isolated in 1976, causes acute gastroenteritis and has spread in pig farms all over the world, resulting in huge economic losses (Vlasova et al., 2017; Woode et al., 1976). Previous studies demonstrated that the prevalence rates of group A PoRV ranging from 3.3% to 67.3% (Vlasova et al., 2017). In 2018, PoRV was detected at a low prevalence rate of 0.53% among 168 farms in China (Zhang et al., 2019). However, a more recent study demonstrated that the positive detection rate of PoRV in clinical samples was up to 44% from 2075 farms in China in 2022 (Song et al., 2024). Another report showed that the overall positive rate of PoRV in pigs was as high as 51.15%, with 86.52% of the tested pig farms in 23 provinces across China confirming PoRV presence during the same year (Qiao et al., 2024). It is important to pay attention to the high prevalence of PoRV in pigs in recent years. Epidemiological investigations have identified the G9 genotype as the predominant genotype circulating in pigs, followed by the G5 genotype (Pan et al., 2025; Qiao et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024; Tao et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). Besides, co-infections of PoRV with porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) were notably common in pig farms (Hou et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2024). A recent study indicated that PoRV infection promoted PEDV infection, thereby complicating pig disease prevention and control strategies in swine (Liu et al., 2024a). Currently, only one live attenuated trivalent diarrhea vaccine (PEDV-transmissible gastroenteritis virus [TGEV]-PoRV), which includes the G5 genotype of the PoRV strain NX, is licensed for the prevention of PoRV infection in China. However, current vaccines cannot provide sufficient efficacy against the diverse genotypes of PoRV infection circulating in pigs (Park et al., 2019; Vlasova et al., 2017). Although some drugs, such as JIB-04, Portulaca oleracea L. polysaccharide, and bavachin, had shown potential anti-PoRV activities (Jung et al., 2024; Son et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023), there remains a lack of effective specific treatments for PoRV infection (Ghonaim et al., 2025). Thus, the development of more effective vaccines or antiviral therapies is necessary to prevent PoRV infections.

An entirely plasmid-based reverse genetics (RG) system was established in 2017 (Kanai et al., 2017). Subsequently, a series of recombinant RV strains were successfully rescued (Diebold et al., 2022; Hamajima et al., 2022; Kanda et al., 2022; Kawagishi et al., 2020; Komoto et al., 2019; Qin et al., 2024; Sanchez-Tacuba et al., 2020; Snyder et al., 2024). Several studies demonstrated that the successful generation of recombinant RVs harboring foreign proteins such as fluorescent (UnaG, GFP, RFP, mCherry and ZsGreen) or bioluminescent (NLuc) reporter proteins (Kanai et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2022; Philip and Patton, 2020; Qin et al., 2024). Some studies reported that reassortant RV strains were rescued by replacing genome segments with those from different strains using the RG system (Kanai et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024b; Valusenko-Mehrkens et al., 2023). These findings highlight that the RG system is a powerful technological platform for exploring viral infection, pathogenesis and developing novel vaccines. However, an RG system based on G9 genotype of PoRV has not yet been reported. Considering the high prevalence of G9 genotype PoRV in pigs, establishing an RG system of G9 genotype PoRV is essential for both basic and applied research.

In this study, we successfully rescued a recombinant G9 genotype of PoRV AHFY2022 strain using the established RG system. Furthermore, we generated two reporter viruses expressing fluorescent proteins (UnaG or NLuc) by using the system and utilized the UnaG reporter virus for antiviral drugs screening. Besides, a reassortant virus harboring G5 genotype of VP7 gene from clinical samples was also rescued using the backbone of rAHFY2022. Mouse experiments showed that the G5 genotype virus could induce robust immune responses, similar to those elicited by the wild type strain. In summary, this study provided an important platform to discover potential antiviral therapies and rapidly develop effective vaccines against prevalent PoRV based on the established RG system of G9 genotype PoRV.

Results

Establishment of a reverse genetics system for G9 genotype PoRV strain AHFY2022

The prevalence of PoRV on pig farms has been gradually increasing in recent years, with G9 emerging as the predominant genotype of PoRV circulating in Chinese pig farms (Table 1). Thus, it is urgent to develop novel antiviral therapies or vaccines against PoRV in pigs. The RG system is an important approach to address the issues. Given the high prevalence of G9 genotype PoRV, we attempted to establish an RG system based on the G9 genotype strain AHFY2022 (G9P[23]). First, the 11 full-length cDNA segments of AHFY2022 strain were cloned in the pT7 vector. According to the methods of Kanai and Komoto (Kanai et al., 2017; Komoto et al., 2018), we cotransfected BHK-T7 cells with the 11 plasmids along with 3 helper plasmids (pCAG-D1R, pCAG-D12L, and pCAG-FAST-p10). After 24 h post transfection, MA104 cells were added to support virus replication. 72 h later, the cells were collected, subjected to three rounds of freezing and thawing, and the supernatants were incubated with MA104 cells. After 2–3 days, a typical cytopathic effect (CPE) was observed in MA104 cells, indicating that the recombinant virus (rAHFY2022-WT) was successfully rescued (data not shown). Nonetheless, the efficiency of generation recombinant viruses was not high as we expected. To enhance the efficiency of generation recombinant viruses, we added three additional helper plasmids (C3P3-G1, pCAGGS-HA-NSP2, and pCAGGS-HA-NSP5) to the transfection pool. Although it is widely recognized that trypsin addition enhances RV infectivity, previous studies have found that pancreatin treatment can better improve RV infection (Makabe et al., 1985; Sanekata et al., 1996; Sato et al., 1995). Consistent with these findings, we found that the addition of pancreatin significantly promoted RV replication. The rescue efficiency and viral titers of the recombinant RV was significantly increased using the optimized RG system compared to Komoto's method (Fig. 1A and B). To confirm that the rAHFY2022-WT strain was derived from infectious clones rather than from the parental AHFY2022-WT strain, we introduced a genetic marker into the pT7-NSP1-AHFY2022 plasmid. A synonymous mutation (T to C, maintaining the Phe280 residue) was introduced at nucleotide position 872, creating a unique EcoRI site (as a genetic marker) in the NSP1 gene. Because NSP1 has been reported to be dispensable for rotavirus replication in vitro (Hou et al., 2021), and the introduction of such silent mutations is widely utilized in rotavirus reverse genetics systems without altering viral phenotypes (Kanai et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2024b), this specific modification is not expected to affect the biological characteristics or replication efficiency of the recombinant virus. As shown in Fig. 1C, sequence analysis indicated that the rAHFY2022-WT strain carried the introduced mutation in the NSP1 gene, whereas the wild-type strain did not. Furthermore, the PCR amplicon from rAHFY2022-WT could be digested by EcoRI restriction endonuclease, producing two smaller fragments, whereas the wild-type AHFY2022-WT remained undigested (Fig. 1C). Sequencing of the virus from passages 3 to 7 showed no changes in the genetic marker, indicating its stability (Fig. 1C). Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis of dsRNA genomes extracted from rAHFY2022-WT virions displayed migration patterns identical to those of the parental AHFY2022 strain (Fig. 1D). As indicated in Fig. 1E and F, VP6 protein expression was detectable in both rAHFY2022-WT and AHFY2022-WT using IFA and Western blot assays. Additionally, the growth curves analyses revealed that the replication kinetics of rAHFY2022-WT were slightly lower than those of the wild-type AHFY2022-WT, with the highest viral titer reached at 36 h (Fig. 1G). Plaque size measurements revealed that both rAHFY2022-WT and AHFY2022-WT formed similar plaque sizes in MA104 cells (Fig. 1H). These data demonstrated that the replication characteristics of rAHFY2022-WT are roughly comparable to those of the parental wild type AHFY2022. Together, we successfully rescued a G9 genotype PoRV AHFY2022 strain based on the optimized RG system.

Generation of recombinant PoRV expressing fluorescent protein UnaG

The reporter virus expressing foreign fluorescent proteins is an important tool for exploring viral replication processes or screening potential antiviral drugs. Previous studies demonstrated that recombinant RV expressing the fluorescent protein UnaG fused to NSP3 exhibited higher fluorescence levels compared to those with UnaG fused to NSP1. We attempted to engineer a recombinant G9 genotype PoRV expressing foreign fluorescent proteins using the established optimized RG system. As shown in Fig. 2A, the fluorescent gene UnaG was fused to the C terminus of NSP3 ORF in the plasmid pT7-NSP3-AHFY2022. An autocleavage peptide sequence was introduced between NSP3 and UnaG ORF. To facilitate the detection of UnaG expression, a 3 × Flag tag was fused to the N terminus of UnaG. Then the reporter virus rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG was successfully rescued by using the RG system. The typical CPE and UnaG fluorescent signals were obviously observed in MA104 cells infected with rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG when compared to mock-infected cells (Fig. 2B). In addition, gel electrophoresis analysis showed that the genome segment 7 of rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG migrated slower than that of the wild type virus due to the introduction of the UnaG gene (Fig. 2C, red asterisk). As indicated in Fig. 2D, IFA revealed that the expression levels of VP6 in rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG were comparable to that of rAHFY2022-WT and AHFY2022-WT. However, the Western blot assay indicated that VP6 expression levels in rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG were slightly lower than those observed in the wild type strain (Fig. 2E). UnaG expression was exclusively observed in cells infected with UnaG reporter virus (Fig. 2E). One-step growth experiments demonstrated that the replication of rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG was slightly lower than that of rAHFY2022-WT, with the highest viral titer reached at 36 h (Fig. 2F), although both produced similar plaque sizes (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, we serially sub-cultured rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG at low multiplicity of infection to evaluate the genetic stability of UnaG gene. As shown in Fig. 2H, no differences were observed in the pattern of the reporter viral dsRNA within 7 passages (Fig. 2H, red arrow). Taken together, these results demonstrated that the reporter virus rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG was successfully recovered using the RG system.

Recovery of a PoRV reporter virus expressing Nano-Luciferase (NLuc)

In addition to rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG, the pT7-NSP3-NLuc plasmid expressing the NLuc reporter in the PoRV gene segment 7 was constructed, which was analogous to pT7-NSP3-UnaG (Fig. 3A). Then the rAHFY2022-NSP3-NLuc was generated using the RG system. As indicated in Fig. 3B, the luciferase activity of rAHFY2022-NSP3-NLuc infected cells was much higher than that in wild type virus or mock-infected cells (Fig. 3B). Additionally, RNA gel electrophoresis revealed that the genome fragment 7 of rAHFY2022-NSP3-NLuc RV migrated more slowly compared to the wild-type due to the insertion of the NLuc gene (Fig. 3C). IFA and Western blot assays indicated that specific fluorescence signals or bands against VP6 could be detected in both rAHFY2022-NSP3-NLuc and rAHFY2022-WT infected cells (Fig. 3D and E). And NLuc expression was detectable in MA104 cells infected with rAHFY2022-NSP3-NLuc, while it was absent in cells infected with the wild type virus or mock-infected cells (Fig. 3E). Although the plaque sizes of both rAHFY2022-NSP3-NLuc and rAHFY2022-WT were comparable (Fig. 3G), the replication efficiency of rAHFY2022-NSP3-NLuc was significantly lower than that of rAHFY2022-WT, with the highest viral titer reached at 36 h (Fig. 3F), The Western blot assays indicated that the VP6 expression levels in rAHFY2022-NSP3-NLuc were slightly lower than those observed in the wild type strain (Fig. 3E). These findings suggested that the expression of the luciferase gene significantly impaired RV replication, consistent with previous studies (Kanai et al., 2017; Komoto et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2022). Importantly, luminescence was still highly detectable even after 7 passages, and no significance in luciferase activity were note throughout these passages (Fig. 3H). Moreover, RNA electrophoresis showed that the genome segment 7 of rAHFY2022-NSP3-NLuc exhibited genetic stability across all seven passages (Fig. 3I). These results indicated that the reporter virus rAHFY2022-NSP3-NLuc, characterized by robust luciferase activity, was successfully rescued by using the RG system.

Application of reporter viruses for antiviral drugs screen

Currently, there are no efficient antiviral drugs available for PoRV infection in pigs. Thus, developing efficient tools for screening potential anti-PoRV drugs is essential. In this study, we aimed to utilize the recovered reporter viruses to efficiently screen for potential anti-PoRV drugs. Considering the limitations previously observed with the NLuc reporter virus, we selected the rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG strain as the primary tool for antiviral screening to obtain more reliable data. Ten small molecules (C1 to C10), belonging to phenylpropionic acid derivatives, 1,2,3,4-tetrahydroisoquinoline derivatives, and oleanolic acid derivatives respectively, were synthesized and stored in our laboratory (Supplementary Table S1). Previous studies had demonstrated that some derivatives of these three compound classes exhibit potential anti-inflammatory or antiviral activities (Jannus et al., 2024; Khwaza et al., 2018; Kinra et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022). However, it remains unclear whether these compounds inhibit PoRV replication. To evaluate the antiviral potential of this compound library (C1 to C10) against PoRV, we performed a drug screening assay referring to relevant literature, utilizing a 6 h pretreatment protocol (Qin et al., 2024). MA104 cells seeded in 48-well plates were pre-treated with the ten small molecules (10 μM each) for 6 h at 37 °C prior to infection with rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG at an MOI of 0.01. At 8 h post infection (hpi), the cells were imaged to analyze the relative fold of fluorescence intensity. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, the screening results demonstrated that compounds C8 and C9, belonging to the class of oleanolic acid derivatives, significantly decreased the percentages of UnaG-positive cells. Based on these results, we focused our subsequent evaluations on compounds C8 and C9 (Fig. 4A). To further validate the pretreatment efficacy and address potential pharmacokinetic variations, we compared the anti-PoRV effects of 1-h and 6-h pretreatments using C8 and C9. Our results indicated that the 6-h pretreatment yielded slightly superior anti-viral activity compared to the conventional 1-h protocol (Supplementary Fig. S2). Therefore, the 6 h pretreatment duration was adopted for all subsequent experiments. The cell viability assay showed that the 50% cytotoxic concentration (CC50) for C8 and C9 was 103.3 μM and 39.2 μM, respectively (Fig. 4B). As indicated in Fig. 4C and D, treatment with either C8 or C9 treatment significantly inhibited green fluorescence intensity in reporter viruses infected MA104 cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4C), with the 50% of maximal effect (EC50) values for C8 and C9 being 3.2 μM and 2.2 μM (Fig. 4D), respectively. And the selectivity index (SI) value for C8 (SI = 32.3) was higher than that for C9 (SI = 17.8), suggesting that both C8 and C9 were promising potential candidates for anti-PoRV therapies. Consistent with the results of reporter viruses, RT-qPCR, 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) assays and Western blot assays showed that C8 or C9 treatment also significantly inhibit AHFY2022-WT replication at indicated concentrations (Fig. 4E, F and G). Furthermore, we explored the potential antiviral mechanisms of C8 and C9 by analyzing their effects on various stages of the PoRV infection cycle (Supplementary Fig. S3). Our findings indicated that C8 restricted PoRV infection at the binding and replication stages, while C9 inhibited PoRV propagation across nearly all the stages of the replication cycle (Fig. 4H–K). Notably, C9 predominantly suppressed PoRV infection during early stages compared to later stages. Previous studies revealed that oleanolic acid derivatives inhibited influenza virus replication by interrupting the interactions between viral proteins and receptors during the entry stage (Meng et al., 2019). To further investigate the interactions between C9 and viral outer capsid proteins VP4 and VP7, we employed AlphaFold 3 modeling. As indicated in Fig. 4L and N, the docking analysis showed that C9 could bind to VP4 prior to proteolytic cleavage (binding energy: −8.7 kcal/mol) and VP7 (binding energy: −8.3 kcal/mol), primarily targeting the VP5∗ subunit of VP4 and the β-barrel domain of VP7, respectively. The key residues on VP4 or VP7 protein that are essential for C9 binding were identified. C9 docking to VP4 protein is mediated by hydrogen bonds with Ser583, Leu575, and Asp593, and hydrophobic interactions with Arg582, Ile581, Ile590, Leu575, and Val588. It also forms a carbon-hydrogen bond with Pro579 and maintains van der Waals contacts with surrounding amino acids. C9 forms hydrogen bonds with Ser153, Asp270, and Asp231 of VP7, establishes hydrophobic interactions with Pro275, Val230, and Leu150. Additionally, it creates a carbon-hydrogen bond with Asp231, engages in electrostatic interaction with Glu180, exhibits steric repulsion with Thr272, and maintains van der Waals forces with surrounding amino acid residues. We further analyzed the direct interactions between C9 and VP4/VP7 proteins using biolayer interferometry (BLI) assays. Our results indicated that C9 could bind to both VP4 and VP7 proteins in a dose-dependent manner, with KD values of 10.8 μΜ and 33.6 μΜ, respectively (Fig. 4M and O). Notably, VP4 exhibited a higher binding affinity for C9 compared to VP7 protein, consistent with the predictions made by AlphaFold 3. Additionally, we also investigated the regulatory effects of C8 and C9 on inflammatory responses induced by RV. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S4, both C8 and C9 markedly downregulated the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-1β in PoRV infected cells. These results indicated that both C8 and C9 can exert their anti-PoRV activity through multiple mechanisms. Overall, these findings suggested that the UnaG reporter virus can be effectively utilized for high-throughput screenings of potential antiviral drugs.

Generation of a G5 genotype VP7 monoreassortant virus from PoRV clinical samples in the backbone of AHFY2022 strain

PoRV genotypes co-circulate in pig populations, with their distribution becoming increasingly complex in recent years. Although G9 is currently the predominant genotype in China, our epidemiological data (Table 1) indicate that the G5 genotype has emerged as the second most prevalent strain, highlighting its widespread transmission and the urgent clinical need for multivalent vaccines covering both major genotypes. However, traditional isolation of prevalent field strains is often challenging. To rapidly obtain the VP7 gene of the diarrhea-causing G5 strain, we directly amplified the full-length G5-VP7 segment from clinical samples and inserted it into the pT7 vector. Utilizing the RV reverse genetics (RG) system, we used the rAHFY2022-WT (G9) as a backbone to recover the recombinant monoreassortant virus rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 by replacing its VP7 gene with the G5 genotype VP7 gene (Fig. 5A). This approach demonstrates the robust utility of our RG system as a rapid platform for generating vaccine candidates directly from refractory clinical samples. As shown in Fig. 5B, the recombinant strain rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 exhibited more severe CPE compared to the wild-type strain rAHFY2022-WT. RT-PCR analysis revealed that the presence of specific bands corresponding to the conserved regions of VP7 gene in cells infected with either rAHFY2022-WT or rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 infected-cells. Notably, bands targeting the G5 genotype VP7 gene were exclusively detected in rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 infected-cells (Fig. 5C). These findings indicated that we successfully generated the reassortant virus rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 strain. RNA gel electrophoresis demonstrated that rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 contained typical 11 genome segments, which was similar with that of rAHFY2022-WT (Fig. 5D). Western blot analysis showed that both rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 and rAHFY2022-WT exhibited comparable expression levels of VP6 and VP7 proteins (Fig. 5E). The replication kinetics of rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 were slightly higher than those of rAHFY2022-WT, with the highest viral titer reached at 36 h (Fig. 5F), while the plaque sizes formed by the two recombinant viruses were similar (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, RNA gel analysis showed that there were no obvious differences in the genome segments patterns of rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 across several passages, revealing the genetic stability of the reassortant virus (Fig. 5H). In conclusion, a reassortant virus encoding G5 genotype VP7 within the backbone of rAHFY2022-WT was successfully generated by using the RG system.

The reassortant virus rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 inducing robust immune responses in mice

The mice experiment was performed to investigate the immunogenicity of the wild type AHFY2022-WT strain and the reassortant virus rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 strain in vivo. We first analyzed the growth kinetics of the AHFY2022-WT and rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 in MA104 cells. As indicated in Supplementary Fig. S5, both strains exhibited similar growth kinetics in vitro, with the highest viral titer reached at 36 h. As shown in Fig. 6A, four groups of adult Balb/c mice (male, four weeks old, n = 6/group) were orally inoculated with PBS, AHFY2022-WT, rAHFY2022-G5-VP7, or AHFY2022-WT + rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 viruses (dose: 105.5TCID50/mouse) at 2-week intervals (at 0, 14, and 28 days post-infection (dpi)). Because PoRV exhibits lower tropism in mice than in its natural host, this three-dose vaccination regimen (one prime and two boosts) was specifically employed to maximize the in vivo immune efficacy. The indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (iELISAs), as described previously (Tang et al., 2024), were used to evaluate levels of the serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody against VP4 or VP7 protein induced by the mice following AHFY2022-WT or/and rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 infections. As shown in Fig. 6B, all the levels of VP4∗P[23]-specific IgG in serum collected from mice orally infected with the viruses were gradually increased compared with PBS group until 28 dpi. Notably, the serum IgG levels in the AHFY2022-WT + rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 group were significantly higher than those in the other viral groups, which indicated that the combination of these two viruses could induce a substantial immune response against PoRV in mice. However, primary infection with AHFY2022-WT or/and rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 induced a weak immune response against VP7 protein, particularly the G9 genotype of VP7. This weak primary response highlighted the necessity of our consecutive booster strategy. After booster infections at 14 and 28 dpi, the serum IgG levels against G9 genotype of VP7 increased significantly, with the second boost (the third vaccination) yielding a markedly stronger response than the first. In contrast, the serum IgG levels against G5 genotype of VP7 gradually decreased by 35 dpi. Additionally, it was observed that AHFY2022-WT could induce serum IgG levels against G5 genotype of VP7 comparable to those induced by rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 in mice. Furthermore, the fecal IgA levels against VP4 or VP7 protein in mice were also analyzed, as these are important for clearance and protection from RV infection. As shown in Fig. 6C, all primary infections with viruses could significantly elevate the fecal IgA levels at 14 dpi compared with PBS group, whereas the IgA levels were quickly declined after 14 dpi. Similar to the serum IgG results, the AHFY2022-WT + rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 group still exhibited the highest levels of fecal IgA among all groups. Moreover, we further analyzed the heterologous or homologous neutralizing activity of the serum IgG in mice against G9 or G5 genotype of PoRV. As shown in Fig. 6D, all the serum from PoRV infected mice exhibited varying degrees of neutralization against AHFY2022-WT or rAHFY2022-G5-VP7. The combination of AHFY2022-WT and rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 induced higher and more concentrated neutralizing antibody (NAb) titers compared to single-virus inoculation, likely due to the bivalent of the PoRV inoculum. The serum NAb titers for mice inoculated with rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 exceeded 26 against rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 strain. Moreover, anti-AHFY2022-WT or anti-rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 serum could cross neutralize the G9 or G5 genotype of PoRV. Importantly, the serum NAb collected from the mice infected with AHFY2022-WT or rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 could also neutralize the isolated PoRV strains NJ2012 (G9P[7]) and NJHS2024 (G5P[23]) to some extent. Collectively, these findings suggested that the G5 genotype of reassortant rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 could induce robust humoral immune responses, which were similar with the parental strain AHFY2022-WT.

Besides, we evaluated the cellular immune responses induced by the rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 at 35 dpi (Fig. 6E). Splenocytes were isolated, and T lymphocytes was proliferation was assessed under concanavalin A (ConA) stimulation. Mice inoculated with both AHFY2022-WT and rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 exhibited a significantly higher lymphocytes count compared to those inoculated with AHFY2022-WT, rAHFY2022-G5-VP7, or PBS (Fig. 6E). We observed that lymphocyte proliferation index for the rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 group was higher than that of AHFY2022-WT group (Fig. 6E). After 72 h post stimulation, ELISA revealed significantly elevated secretion levels of the interferon γ (IFN-γ) and interleukin 4 (IL-4) secretion levels in the supernatant from AHFY2022-WT or/and rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 groups compared to the PBS group. In addition, we also analyzed the percentages of CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells in splenic lymphocytes and found elevated percentages across all viral inoculated groups compared to the PBS group. Finally, the percentage of splenic B lymphocyte subsets were analyzed at 35 dpi. It was demonstrated that mice inoculated with the viruses could stimulate higher percentages of B lymphocytes than those in PBS group mice, with rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 induced more B cells than AHFY2022-WT in mice (Fig. 6E). In summary, the reassortant virus rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 strain could induce the functional humoral and cellular immune responses against PoRV infection, similar to the parental AHFY2022-WT strain.

Discussion

Recent studies have revealed that the prevalence rates of PoRV in pig farms have been gradually increased over recent years. The complexity of genotype combinations and coinfection with other enteric pathogens further complicate efforts to prevent the spread of PoRV among pigs. It should be noted that G9 had become the dominant genotype of VP7 gene detected in PoRV positive samples. Thus, it is urgent to develop effective anti-PoRV drugs and vaccines to prevent and control PoRV infection. The RG system is an important tool to address these problems. Although multiple RV strains including G5 genotype of PoRV OSU strain had been successfully recovered (Snyder et al., 2024), the RG system based on G9 genotype of PoRV has yet to be established. To address this critical gap, our current study aimed to pioneer a robust RG platform specifically tailored for a circulating G9 PoRV field isolate.

Since the RG system of RV had been established at 2017 (Kanai et al., 2017), many groups attempted to optimize the protocols to enhance the rescue efficiency (Komoto et al., 2018; Philip et al., 2019; Sanchez-Tacuba et al., 2020). We successfully recovered the recombinant PoRV AHFY2022 strain using these optimized measures (data not shown). However, the rescue efficiency did not meet our expectations. Given the transfection efficiency of the BHK-T7 cell line used in this study is nearly 100% (data not shown), we further optimized other protocols of the RG system. Previous studies demonstrated that the expression levels of NSP2 and NSP5 genes, the important components of viral viroplasm (Patton et al., 2006), were critical for generation recombinant RV. Thus, we added the eukaryotic expression plasmids of NSP2 and NSP5 (pCAGGS-HA-NSP2 and pCAGGS-HA-NSP5) in the system to enhance the expression levels of these two genes directly, finally promoting PoRV primary replication efficiency (Fig. 1A). Besides, previous reports revealed that pancreatin was commonly used in RV isolation and infection(Makabe et al., 1985; Sanekata et al., 1996; Sato et al., 1995). Our findings corroborated this, as pancreatin treatment led to the formation of more typical CPE in PoRV infected cells compared to trypsin treatment, indicating that pancreatin could increase RV infection efficiency. Thus, we also added pancreatin in BHK-T7 and MA104 coculture system to increase viral particle yield. The rescued efficiency was significantly increased based on the above optimized measures. Although the AHFY2022 strain was a new field isolate (Wang et al., 2024), the recombinant rAHFY2022-WT strain was successfully rescued using the optimized system. In fact, the major novelties and contributions of our study resulting from these optimizations are three-fold. Firstly, we established the first RG system specifically targeted at a G9 genotype PoRV, addressing a critical gap and providing an essential platform for studying the pathogenicity and evaluating antiviral strategies against this predominant circulating strain. Secondly, by systematically optimizing the protocol—particularly the supplementation of NSP2/NSP5 and the use of pancreatin—we significantly improved the rescue efficiency. Thirdly, we demonstrated the translational potential of this optimized system by rapidly generating a cross-genotype recombinant vaccine candidate (rAHFY2022-G5-VP7) with validated in vivo immunogenicity.

Previous studies reported that the segment 7 (NSP3) ORF could be used as an expression platform for producing foreign proteins, such as fluorescent proteins or other viral proteins (Kawagishi et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Philip et al., 2023; Philip and Patton, 2020). In this study, we successfully generated the recombinant reporter virus expressing UnaG or NLuc. However, it should be noted that the replication kinetics of the recombinant reporter virus rAHFY2022-NSP3-NLuc was significantly impaired compared to that of the reporter virus rAHFY2022-NSP3-UnaG, suggesting that the foreign gene NLuc adversely affects RV replication than UnaG does. To further minimize the impact of foreign inserts on viral fitness, future optimizations should remove the 3 × Flag tag. Furthermore, we hypothesize that the severe replication attenuation observed with the NLuc insertion stems from packaging constraints (Hatazawa et al., 2021; Kanai et al., 2017; Komoto et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2022). Because the 11 dsRNA segments are tightly packed within the restricted viral capsid, increased segment length likely disrupts the optimal spatial arrangement of progeny virions. Future designs could mitigate this by deleting endogenous viral sequences (e.g., within the NSP1 segment) to balance overall segment size (Kanai et al., 2019). Given these limitations, we selected the more robust UnaG reporter virus for antiviral screening. Unlike traditional anti-VP6 immunological assays, direct fluorescence quantification enables highly efficient, scalable high-throughput screening (Kanai et al., 2017; Qin et al., 2024). Despite slightly impaired replication kinetics compared to the wild-type (WT) strain, our UnaG platform proved highly reliable. Using this system, we successfully identified two anti-PoRV compounds, C8 and C9, whose activities and mechanisms were rigorously validated in the WT strain. These two compounds belong to the class of oleanolic acid derivatives. Although it had been reported that oleanolic acid derivatives possess antiviral and anti-inflammatory activities (Jannus et al., 2024; Khwaza et al., 2018), their role in restricting RV replication remains unknown. Our studies revealed that C8 targeted both binding and replication stages of PoRV infection, while C9 exerted on all the stages of viral infection cycle. We focused on C9 due to its higher antiviral activity compared to C8 and its significant antiviral efficacy predominantly during the early infection stage. Notably, we observed a discrepancy in the dose-response of C9 between different assays at 10–20 μM. Specifically, while the luminescence signal and NSP5 expression were drastically reduced at these concentrations, the infectious viral titer determined by the plaque assay maintained a relatively higher level (Fig. 4). This difference can be largely attributed to the distinct sensitivities of the assays and the specific antiviral mechanism of C9. The luminescence reporter and NSP5 expression are highly sensitive indicators of early viral gene expression and intracellular protein synthesis in the monolayer cells. In contrast, the plaque assay measures the titer of fully assembled, infectious progeny virions released into the supernatant. Because C9 predominantly targets the early infection stages, the acute suppression of early intracellular protein synthesis may not scale proportionally with the reduction in the final production of infectious progeny virions. Previous studies demonstrated that oleanolic acid derivatives acted as inhibitors of influenza virus entry by interrupting the interaction between viral proteins and receptors or by binding with hemagglutinin (Han et al., 2017; Hassan Mir et al., 2021). Through BLI assays, we found that the binding affinity of C9 for the viral outer structural protein VP4 is approximately three times higher than that for VP7, and the interaction between C9 and VP4 is relatively stable and resistant to dissociation. Specifically regarding VP4, C9 primarily targets the foot domain of VP5∗ (aa 510–776). We hypothesize that the binding of C9 to the VP5∗ foot domain may introduce steric hindrance or induce conformational locking, which prevents VP5∗ from correctly inserting into the lipid bilayer. This subsequently affects calcium ion permeation and ultimately disrupts the viral entry process (de Sautu et al., 2024). Therefore, we preliminarily speculate that binding to VP4 plays the primary role in its antiviral process. Although we attempted to express and purify the recombinant proteins with the predicted mutant binding sites, we were unable to achieve sufficient purity for BLI analysis to verify the binding sites between C9 and viral proteins. Thus, further studies were needed to verify the above hypotheses and evaluate the antiviral effectiveness in animal models. Moreover, this study revealed that both C8 and C9 exhibited significant anti-inflammatory activity in PoRV infected cells, which provided potential applications against acute gastroenteritis caused by PoRV infection. Collectively, this study provided a strategy for high-throughput screen anti-PoRV drugs. In addition to reporter viruses, recent studies reported that the recombinant viruses harboring the S1 gene of SARS-CoV-2 or VP1 gene or P domain of human norovirus were successfully recovered (Kawagishi et al., 2023; Philip et al., 2023), indicating that the potential utility of RVs as vaccine platforms for other enteric pathogens. For PoRV, recovering recombinant virus that express the antigenic epitopes from other genotypes PoRV within NSP3 gene could facilitate prevention of diverse genotypes PoRV (Kanai and Kobayashi, 2021; Uprety et al., 2021). Additionally, recombinant PoRV could be served as a vector expression the immunogenic antigen genes from other important enteric viruses, such as PEDV or Porcine deltacoronavirus (PDCoV), providing protection for pigs against multiple enteric pathogens infections simultaneously.

Interestingly, the reassortant rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 exhibited higher in vitro replication efficiency than the parental rAHFY2022-WT (G9) strain in MA104 cells. This advantage may stem from structural differences between the G5 and G9 VP7 glycoproteins, which could enhance viral attachment and cell entry via receptors like integrins (Graham et al., 2003; López and Arias, 2004), or improve late-stage assembly. However, this in vitro advantage does not contradict G9's epidemiological predominance. Recent evidence shows G5 and G9 strains exhibit comparable replication and pathogenicity in vivo (Liu et al., 2024b), suggesting the cellular replication advantage is mitigated by the complex mucosal environment of the host intestine. Ultimately, field prevalence is driven by multifactorial ecological determinants—such as herd immunity, immune evasion, cross-species transmission, and viral recombination—rather than mere replication kinetics. Future studies integrating these factors are needed to fully elucidate G9's widespread dominance. Mice experiments showed that rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 could induce robust humoral and cellular immune responses against PoRV, particularly concerning the isolated strains (NJ2012 (G9P[7]) and NJHS(G5P[23])), similar to the parental strain. However, notable differences were observed in the NAb levels against homotypic strains, especially G9 genotype strains. The NAb titers from anti-AHFY2022-WT serum against NJ2012 strain were much lower than those of AHFY2022-WT, which suggested that the difference of VP4 genotype also influence cross-protection among heterotypic strains. Although some weak cross-neutralization between G5 and G9 genotype, our previous animal experiments showed that piglets vaccinated with G5 genotype PoRV could not resist G9 genotype PoRV challenge (data not shown). These results further suggested that G5 genotype PoRV could not provide complete cross protection against G9 genotype PoRV infection in pigs. Besides, it is interesting to observe that rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 could induce more B lymphocytes than AHFY2022-WT. Given that the only difference between the two strains was the genotype of VP7 genotype, which suggested that VP7 dominated the proportion of B lymphocytes. A previous study reported that VP7 protein initiates intestinal B-cell activation during RV infection (Blutt et al., 2004). Our study indicated that the fecal IgA levels of rAHFY2022-G5-VP7 group were higher than those in AHFY2022-WT group (Fig. 6C), consistent with the observations in the previous study. Further studies are warranted to investigate the key amino acid residues determining the activation of B cells based on the sequence alignment, which supported the development of more efficient PoRV vaccines. The serum NAb titers from the mice against PoRV were not as high as we expected, probably resulting from the species difference of RV. Thus, further study is needed to evaluate the efficiency of passive immune protection of the reassortant virus in piglet models. Furthermore, this strategy could be applied to produce vaccine candidate library by modifying existing safe and effective licensed PoRV vaccines (Fukuda et al., 2024). In summary, these findings provided the potential utility of rapid development novel vaccine candidates matching current circulating genotype of PoRV by using RG system (Desselberger, 2020).

Despite the successful establishment of the optimized RG system and its preliminary applications, several limitations in this study should be acknowledged. First, although the BLI assay and molecular docking provided evidence for the direct interaction between compound C9 and viral structural proteins (VP4 and VP7), the precise binding residues predicted by AlphaFold 3 were not functionally validated through site-directed mutagenesis due to technical challenges in obtaining highly purified mutant proteins. Further structural biology approaches, such as cryo-electron microscopy, are required to elucidate the definitive binding mechanisms. Second, the immunogenicity of the cross-genotype reassortant vaccine candidate (rAHFY2022-G5-VP7) was exclusively evaluated in a murine model. Given the restricted tropism of PoRV in mice and species-specific immune variations, the actual cross-protective efficacy and neutralizing capabilities must be rigorously assessed in piglets—the natural host—through viral challenge experiments in future studies. Finally, the insertion of the NLuc gene significantly attenuated viral replication, highlighting the restricted packaging capacity of the rotavirus capsid. Further optimization of the RG platform, such as compensatory truncation of endogenous viral genes, is necessary to improve the viral fitness of reporter strains.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully established and optimized the RG system based on the G9 genotype of PoRV strain AHFY2022. We further generated the recombinant reporter virus expression UnaG and NLuc, respectively. And the UnaG reporter virus was successfully applied for screening anti-PoRV drugs, leading to the identification of two potential anti-PoRV candidates. In addition, a reassortant virus harboring G5 genotype VP7 gene, cloned from clinical samples, was generated. The reassortant virus could stimulate robust immune response in mice. Together, these findings offered an important strategy for discovering potential anti-PoRV drugs and developing novel vaccines against PoRV circulation in pigs.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and viruses

MA104 (Cercopithecus aethiops embryo kidney epithelial cells) cells were grown in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; HyClone, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (OPCEL, Inner Mongolia, China). BHK-21 (Baby hamster kidney cells) cells stably expressing T7 RNA polymerase (BHK-T7) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, BasalMedia, China) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (OPCEL, Inner Mongolia, China). All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.

PoRV strain AHFY2022 (G9P[23]; GenBank: OQ979280.1–OQ979290.1), PoRV strain NJ2012 (G9P[7]; GenBank: MT874983–MT874993), and PoRV strain NJHS2024 (G5P[23]) were isolated and maintained in our laboratory (Wang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2021). All the RV strains used in this study were propagated in MA104 cells.

Antibodies and reagents

PoRV VP6 rabbit polyclonal antibody (pAb) and VP7 monoclonal antibody (mAb) were prepared in our laboratory (Tao et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2021). Rabbit pAbs against GAPDH and Flag were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). The mAb against NLuc was purchased from Promega (Madison, USA). Pancreatin from porcine pancreas were purchased from Sigma (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Ten different kinds of small molecules were synthesized and stored in our laboratory, whose biochemical structure and category information were presented in Supplementary Table S1. All the small molecules were dissolved in sterile dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to a concentration of 1 mM.

Plasmid construction

The pT7 plasmids (simian SA11 strain) and three helper plasmids (pCAG-D1R, pCAG-D12L, and pCAG-FAST-p10) were kindly provided by Takeshi Kobayashi through the Addgene (Kanai et al., 2017). The helper plasmid C3P3-G1, which expresses both the African swine fever virus NP868R capping enzyme and the T7 DNA-dependent RNA polymerase, was kindly provided by Dr. Siyuan Ding (Sanchez-Tacuba et al., 2020). The eukaryotic plasmids (pCAGGS-HA-NSP2 and pCAGGS-HA-NSP5) expressing the PoRV NSP2 and NSP5 proteins were constructed in our lab. To construct the plasmids used for the reverse genetics system of PoRV strain AHFY2022, the 11 gene segments were inserted into the pT7 vector separately. The resulting plasmids were named pT7-VP1-AHFY2022, pT7-VP2- AHFY2022, pT7-VP3-AHFY2022, pT7-VP4-AHFY2022, pT7-VP6-AHFY2022, pT7-VP7- AHFY2022, pT7-NSP1-AHFY2022, pT7-NSP2-AHFY2022, pT7-NSP3-AHFY2022, pT7-NSP4- AHFY2022, and pT7-NSP5-AHFY2022. The plasmids pT7-NSP3-UnaG-AHFY2022 and pT7-NSP3-NLuc-AHFY2022 were synthesized by BioCorp (General Biol, China), which encode UnaG and NLuc genes respectively as described previously (Liu et al., 2022; Philip et al., 2019). The pT7-VP7-G5 plasmid, containing a G5 genotype of VP7 segment, was constructed based on a G5 genotype of PoRV positive sample from a pig farm. All plasmid sequences were confirmed by DNA sequencing. The primer sequences used for plasmid construction will be provided on request.

Generation of recombinant rotaviruses

Recombinant RVs were generated as previously described with some modifications (Kanai et al., 2017; Komoto et al., 2018; Sanchez-Tacuba et al., 2020). Briefly, BHK-T7 cells were transfected with AHFY2022 pT7 plasmids (1200 ng for pT7-NSP2-AHFY2022 and pT7-NSP5-AHFY2022; 400 ng for the remaining pT7 plasmids), four helper plasmids (400 ng for pCAG-D1R, pCAG-D12L and C3P3-G1; 15 ng for pCAG-FAST-p10) and NSP2 and NSP5 expression plasmids (400 ng for pCAGGS-HA-NSP2 and pCAGGS-HA-NSP5) by using TransIT-LT1 transfection reagent (Mirus Bio). After 24 h post transfection, BHK-T7 cells were washed with PBS for twice and replaced with FBS-free medium. 24 h later, 2 × 104 MA104 cells in 200 μL of serum-free medium were added to the well, along with 0.015 mg/mL of pancreatin (Sigma, USA). Then, MA104 and BHK-T7 cells were cocultured for 48 h, after which they were frozen and thawed three times. The lysate was centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris, and then were activated with 15 μg/mL of trypsin. 500 μL of the supernatant was transferred onto MA104 monolayers in 12-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After absorption, MA104 cells replaced with FBS-free medium containing 0.015 mg/mL of pancreatin for 3 days or continued to be passaged until visible cytopathic lesions appeared. We defined viruses as successfully rescued when significant cytopathic lesions were observed in MA104 cells infected with the corresponding RV rescued passages 1–3.

RNA gels

Viral dsRNA was extracted with RNA isolation kit (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol and then mixed with Loading Buffer (Yeasen, China). Samples were subjected to PAGE (7.5%) for 3 h at 180V and then stained with prepared staining solution for 30 min and visualized by the gel Image system (Tanon, China).

Immunofluorescence analysis

MA104 cells were infected with virus at an MOI of 0.1. After 16 hpi, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were fixed with pre-cooled ethanol for 15 min. Then the fixed cells were washed with PBS for three times and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature. Then the cells were incubated with anti-VP6 monoclonal antibody (prepared in our laboratory, 1:2000) for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by incubation with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG fluorescent secondary antibody (1:500) (BOSTER, China) for 40 min at 37 °C. The nuclei were stained with 4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 min at room temperature (RT) and washed with PBS for three times. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX-15, Japan).

Western blotting analysis

Cells were lysed with WB Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, China) for 20 min on ice. Cell lysates were subjected to SDS-PAGE analysis and transferred to nitrocellulose (NC) membranes (Pall Corporation, USA). The membranes were probed with specific primary antibodies and secondary antibodies. The membranes were imaged and analyzed using Tanon imaging system (Tanon, China).

Multiple-step virus replication

Monolayers of MA104 cells in 24-well plates were incubated with activated WT or recombinant PoRV at an MOI of 0.01 for 1 h, then washed twice with PBS, and then cultured in MEM supplemented with 0.5 μg/mL trypsin. The virus samples were harvested every 12 hpi up to 48 h (with three replicates for each time point). The infected cells were frozen and thawed twice before the determination of virus titers. The viral titers were calculated as 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID50). And the TCID50 values were analyzed based on the Reed and Muench method.

Plaque assay

The monolayers of MA104 cells in 12-well plates were incubated with trypsin-activated WT or recombinant PoRV for 1 h, washed with PBS for twice time, and then were covered with 1.1% agarose (Sigma, USA) in FBS-free medium supplemented with 0.5 μg/mL trypsin, 1% penicillin-streptomycin antibiotic and 100 μg/mL DEAE-Dextran (Standard, Shanghai, China). Cultures for plaque assay were incubated at 37 °C for 3–4 days and then stained with 0.41 mg/mL Neutral Red (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to visualize plaques.

Luciferase assay

MA104 cells were infected with PoRV at an MOI of 0.01 for 12 h. The infected cells were frozen and thawed three times and then were added Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay Reagent (Promega, USA) according to manufacturer's instructions. The NLuc activity in cell lysates was quantified using the Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay system (Promega, USA).

Cytotoxicity assay

MA104 cells were treated with the small molecules for 24 h. The viability of the MA104 cells was assessed with the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)

The total RNA of cells infected with PoRV was extracted by using FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, China) and then was reverse transcribed to cDNA using a HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) experiments were performed by using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and analyzed on the Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, USA). The primers used in this study were as follows: RV-NSP5-F: 5′-CTGCTTCAAACGATCCACTCAC-3′, RV-NSP5-R: 5′-TGAATCCATAGACA- CGCC-3′; GAPDH-F:5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′, GAPDH-R:5′-GGCT- GTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′; VP7-395-F: 5′-GGCTTTAAAAGAGAGAATTTC CGTCTGG-3′, VP7-395-R: 5′-ACTGATCCTGTTGGCCATCCTTT-3′; G5-VP7-CDS-F: 5′-GCGGTTAGCTCCTTTTAATGTATGGTATTGAATATACCACAG-3′, G5-VP7-CDS-R: 5′-TCTAACCTAAGTTATACCTAGACTCGATAATAAAAAGC-3′. The data were presented as the fold-change normalized to housekeeping gene GAPDH based on the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Molecular docking

The structure of the VP7/VP4 protein of AHFY2022 strain was predicted using the AlphaFold v3 server (https://alphafoldserver.com/). The PROCHECK tool from the SAVES v6.0 online platform (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/) was used to generate a Ramachandran plot for model validation. After that, the model with the highest quality score was selected for subsequent docking analysis. Molecular docking analysis between the protein and C9 was conducted using the CB-Dock2 platform. CB-Dock2 was used to calculate the binding affinity energy (kcal/mol), and the optimal docking model with the lowest energy was derived. Finally, PyMol v2.6 visualization software was used to analyze the interaction details between the protein and C9, and generate 3D interaction diagrams, while Discovery Studio (DS) 2021 was employed to generate 2D interaction diagrams.

Biolayer interferometry (BLI) assays

The recombinant viral outer proteins (VP4/VP7) of AHFY2022-WT strain were expressed and purified as described previously (Tang et al., 2024). The binding kinetics were analyzed using the Octet RED96 system, with assays conducted in black 96-well plates and a kinetic buffer (PBS). The total working volume for each well was 200 μL. Prior to each assay, NTA biosensor tips were pre-immersed in 200 μL of PBS for 10 min at RT. Data were analyzed using ForteBio software, which was globally fitted using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to determine the dissociation constant (KD). For VP4/VP7 proteins (100 μg/mL), NTA biosensors were loaded for 300 s at RT. Following a baseline period of 120 s, the sensors were exposed to increasing concentrations of C9 (6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 μM) for 300 s of association, followed by a 300 s dissociation period in the kinetic buffer.

Mouse experiment

Four-week-old Balb/c mice (Cavens Laboratory, Changzhou, China) were randomized into four groups (PBS, AHFY2022-WT, rAHFY2022-G5-VP7, or AHFY2022-WT + rAHFY2022-G5-VP7) and immunized with the same dose of viruses via oral gavage (105.5 TCID50/mouse). Two booster vaccinations with the same dose were administered to all groups 14 days after the initial vaccination. Peripheral blood and feces were collected at 0, 14, 28, and 35 days post immunization to evaluate serum and fecal antibody titers, respectively. At 35 dpi, all mice were humanely culled, and the blood and spleen were collected to analyze the cellular immune responses as described previously (Li et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024).

Indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent (iELISA) assay

The iELISA assay was performed as described previously (Tang et al., 2024). Briefly, The ELISA plate was coated with 1 μg/mL of VP7 or VP4 protein in coating buffer and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The plates were then blocked with PBST containing 5% (wt/vol) skim milk at RT for 2 h. The mouse sera or diluted fecal suspension was diluted 1:100 in PBST and added to the wells (100 μL/well), and then were incubated at RT for 1 h. The amount of serum IgG or fecal IgA was detected with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (Proteintech, China) or HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgA antibody (Abcam, China). After washed with PBST, 100 μL of 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine substrate (Solarbio, China) was added to the wells and incubated for 10 min at RT. The reaction was stopped with sulfuric acid (2 M, 100 μL/well), and the optical density (OD) at a wavelength of 450 nm (OD450) was measured by using the BioTek Cytation 7 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (BioTek, USA).

Virus neutralizing assay

Neutralization assays were performed as previously described (Tang et al., 2024). In brief, the serum samples were twofold serially diluted with medium, mixed with 200 TCID50/100 μL virus, and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Monolayers of MA104 cells in 96-well plates were incubated with the virus-serum mixture and were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Then cells were washed with PBS and replaced with MEM supplemented with 1 μg/mL trypsin. After incubation for 3–5 days, the neutralizing titers were calculated as the reciprocal of the highest dilution causing complete neutralization based on the CPE.

Lymphocyte proliferation assay

Splenocytes collected from mice in this study were seeded in 96-well plates with 2 × 105 cells/well and stimulated with 10 μg/mL ConA (Sigma, USA). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for approximately 72 h, and the supernatants were collected for cytokine detection. Lymphocyte proliferation was then evaluated with the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) (Solarbio, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Splenocyte stimulation is expressed as the stimulation index (S.I.), which was calculated as the ratio of the average OD450 value of the stimulated wells to that of the unstimulated wells.

Cytokine detection

The supernatants collected from mice were used for cytokine detection. The concentrations of IFN-γ and IL-4 in the supernatant of the splenic lymphocytes were determined with an IFN-γ ELISA kit (MeiMian, China) and an IL-4 ELISA kit (MeiMian, China), respectively, according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentrations of IFN-γ and IL-4 in the samples were calculated based on standard curves.

T-cell analysis

Mice were euthanized at 35 dpi and their splenocytes were isolated. The harvested cells (1 × 106) were resuspended in 300 μL of PBS containing anti-mouse antibodies (Biolegend, USA) and incubated at 4 °C in dark for 30 min. The cell pellets were washed with PBS and resuspended in 200 μL of fluorescence preservation solution (0.01 M PBS, pH 7.4, 2% glucose, 1% formaldehyde, and 0.1% NaN3). The percentages of the specific cells (CD3+CD4+ T-cells and CD3+CD8+ T-cells) were determined and analyzed by using Flow cytometry (BD, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using the GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad, USA) Statistical comparisons were performed using Student's t-test or ANOVA test, and were presented as the means ± standard deviation (SD). Differences were considered significant indicated as: ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001. All experiments were repeated at least three times.
