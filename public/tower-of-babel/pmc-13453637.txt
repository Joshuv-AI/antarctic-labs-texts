Surgical aortic valve replacement after transcatheter aortic valve replacement: How an orthopedic device can help cardiac surgeons
Authors: Patrick D Rudersdorf, William M Whited
Published: 2026

Excalibur (Arthrex Corporation).

Central Message

A preferred technique for TAVR explantation does not exist. The Excalibur shaver (Arthrex Corporation) is another tool to add to the surgeon's armamentarium for this challenging operation.

Surgical aortic valve replacement after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), is the largest growing surgical procedure in cardiac surgery with a reported mortality of 13% to 17%.1 Currently, no preferred technique for TAVR explantation exists. Published case reports and technique videos exist, and the Heat Valve Collaboratory offers an excellent synopsis with the goals of improving outcomes in TAVR explantation.2, 3, 4 Here we present a novel approach utilizing an orthopedic shaver device, Excalibur (Arthrex Corporation), to explant a balloon expandable TAVR implanted 3 years prior.

Background

The Excalibur is a device used for precise shaving of bone and soft tissue, typically in an arthroscopic operation. The Excalibur is paired with a high speed (max 8000 rpm) ergonomic handpiece that has the capability of simultaneously debriding bone and suctioning the debris.

Cardiac surgeons have sought to simplify calcium debridement for decades. Current debridement techniques include the combined use of rongeurs, forceps, and a 15-blade scalpel and little has changed regarding this technique. Well described in the literature is the use of the SONOPET (R) Ultrasonic Aspirator (Stryker Corporation) for aortic stenosis. This has been an off-label use for decades and has been the only instrument that provides mechanical debridement for calcified tissue in the heart. However, dangers exist with the use of this mechanical debridement for important reasons: the tip on the CUSA is not protected, calcified and healthy tissues are morcellated having the unintended consequence of seriously damaging healthy tissue in proximity to the calcification, and the device does not reliably eliminate the calcific debris from the surgical field, thus exposing the patient to risk of systemic embolization. For these reasons, some surgeons have limited the use of the CUSA or eliminated it from their practice entirely.

The ideal instrument for mechanical debridement of calcified tissue in the heart could have these properties, among others: mechanical debridement of only calcified tissue, production of no damage to surrounding structures, and aspiration of the calcific debris to eliminate risk of systemic embolization. The unique design of the Excalibur is such that many of these are met. Specifically, only calcified bone is debrided, healthy tissue is spared, and the wide mouth of the 4.0-mm Excalibur allows calcium debris to be concomitantly aspirated. In addition, the portion actively involved with mechanical debridement is only 180° and is not present at the tip. As such, this design is inherently protective of surrounding structures, minimizing collateral damage. Given these advantageous properties, Excalibur is an important tool in a surgeon's toolbox.

Technique

Midline sternotomy and exposure was in standard technique. Unlike other reports, we began with excision of the native aortic valve leaflets. Using the Excalibur 4.0 mm × 13 cm programmed at 8000 rpm, continuous settings, and maximal suction, the native aortic valve was mechanically debrided with the mouth of the Excalibur directed toward the TAVR framework. Ample irrigation along the shaft of the Excalibur during use is prudent (see Videos 1 and 2 and Figures E1 and E2).

The native aortic valve leaflets were easily debrided thus exposing the TAVR framework circumferentially (Figure E3). In addition to complete debridement of the native aortic valve leaflets, the shaver has the added benefit of disrupting large chunks of calcium, thus making its removal with a pituitary rongeur very easy. Finally, the in-folding technique was employed to easily separate the TAVR framework and skirt from the surrounding structures. A 15 blade was used to sharply divide the subannular tissue along the TAVR skirt (Figure E4). The TAVR was explanted in less than 20 minutes.

Mechanical debridement to level of native aortic valve annulus.

Use of 15-blade to separate sub-annular tissue from TAVR skirt.

The residual calcium present on the native aortic valve annulus was further debrided using standard technique as well as the Excalibur (Figure E5). The aortic root and left ventricular outflow tract were copiously irrigated and a 29-mm Edwards Inspiris valve was implanted. The aortotomy was closed, and the patient was weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass. On transesophageal echocardiogram assessment, the SAVR had no paravalvular leak. Total cardiopulmonary bypass time was 115 minutes; aortic crossclamp time was 103 minutes. At 1-year of follow-up, the patient is alive with single-digit aortic valve gradients.

Discussion

No standard technique exists for SAVR after TAVR, especially because TAVR platforms differ. Regardless of TAVR platform, SAVR after TAVR is fraught with complications. The separation and subsequent explantation of the TAVR from adherent structures is technically demanding. This portion of the operation can result in various scenarios of collateral damage necessitating more than the name “SAVR after TAVR” implies. Collateral damage is not always avoidable given the nature of TAVR and the unique pathology it produces within the native annulus. By removing the native aortic valve before explantation, we believe this technique may prevent this additional damage. The Excalibur is uniquely designed for this operation because all the mechanical debridement is directed away from critical structures and the smooth, rounded tip of the Excalibur's design prevents injury to surrounding structures.

Initial removal of the native aortic valve leaflets resulted in important observations. First, this produced ample room in the sinuses of Valsalva such that the boundaries of the TAVR framework, the TAVR skirt, and native tissue could be completely defined. Next, the blunt tip of the Excalibur allowed a protective separation from the native aorta and the native aortic valve leaflets such that the native aortic root was unharmed. TAVR explant at the level of the native annulus was significantly easier without the presence of the native aortic valve leaflets. Finally, the TAVR served as a barrier that prevented debris from entering the cavity of the left ventricle.

This technique and instrumentation add to surgeons’ armamentarium and the TAVR explant literature. This technique differentiates itself from others by debriding the native aortic valve first, followed by TAVR explant.
