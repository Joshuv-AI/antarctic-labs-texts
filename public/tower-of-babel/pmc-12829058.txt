Validity and measurement invariance of the cognitive and affective mindfulness scale-revised (CAMS-R) among nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses
Authors: Zhiguang Fan, Yi Luo, Jing Cai, Hong Zou, Xiaoxi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis present study aimed to evaluate the reliability, validity, and measurement invariance of the Cognitive and Affective Mindfulness Scale-Revised (CAMS-R) among nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses.MethodA sample of 165 nursing postgraduates, 394 nursing interns, and 540 registered nurses completed the survey, using CAMS-R, Workplace Well-Being Scale (WWB), Occupational Disidentification Scale (QDS), Stigma Consciousness Questionnaire (SCQ) and Intent to Leave Scale (ILS). The collected data were used for item analysis, exploratory factor analysis (EFA), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), correlation analysis, and measurement invariance testing and Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω coefficients were calculated.ResultsThe Chinese version of the CAMS-R consisted of 10 items across three dimensions: acceptance, attention, and awareness. CFA results indicated that the fit indices of the bi-factor model were superior to those of other models. Additionally, the Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω coefficients of the overall CAMS-R scale and each dimension ranged from 0.73 to 0.84. Results of criterion validity testing showed that CAMS-R was significantly positively correlated with WWB and significantly negatively correlated with QDS, SCQ, and ILS. Meanwhile, the measurement invariance of CAMS-R in nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses was not established.ConclusionsThe Chinese version of the CAMS-R is a reliable and effective tool for assessing mindfulness among nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses; however, potential group differences should be interpreted with cautious clarification. The results of this study suggest that nursing educators can use the CAMS-R to examine the relationship between mindfulness and nurses’ occupational behavior and well-being, and evaluate the intervention effects of mindfulness.Keywords: Cognitive and affective mindfulness, Nurse, Measurement invariance, Reliability, Validity

Globally, nurses are regularly exposed to high-stress environments such as long working hours, low income, high job demands, limited promotion opportunities, unfair treatment, denigration and discrimination, and workplace violence [1, 2]. These stresses pose a serious threat to nurses’ health, emotional stability, social interactions and health-promoting behaviors, and may increase occupational risks, or even induce substance abuse, self-harm, and suicide [3]. The problem is not confined to nurses, nursing postgraduates are also affected by serious mental health problems, of which sleep disorders, anxiety, depression, and burnout are most common [4]. The main sources of their stress include the learning of professional knowledge and skills, financial issues, and future career choices [5]. For nursing interns, they experience stress and anxiety due to factors such as career decision dilemmas, competitive pressures for employment, graduation, licensure examinations, and low self-confidence in clinical competence [6]. Although there are differences in the sources and levels of stress among nurses, nursing postgraduates, and nursing interns, all three groups are in high-stress environments.

Currently, many intervention methods have been developed and validated in clinical practice, such as self-compassion training, emotion regulation and relaxation interventions, cognitive behavior therapy, and digital mental health interventions [7, 8]. Among these, mindfulness-based psychoeducation, lectures, received stress management and resiliency training, and meditation programs have received increasing attention from researchers [9]. Mindfulness, as a psychological resource, enhances nurses’ ability to maintain inner calm and relaxation, cope with stressful life events, and have positive interactions with patients [10]. Mindfulness is generally defined as the process of regulating attention on immediate experiences, maintaining awareness of body sensations, affective valence, cognitive and emotional phenomena, and the external environment with an orientation of curiosity, openness, acceptance, and equanimous attitude [11, 12].

In recent years, there has been a remarkable growth in the research and practice concerning workplace mindfulness, especially attention paid to the positive value of mindfulness at the individual and organizational levels [13]. In the case of nurses, mindfulness training is a promising intervention that can effectively improve self-compassion, well-being, and life satisfaction, while reducing burnout, and perceived stress at the same time [14, 15]. Even a 3-min mindfulness practice is sufficient to lead to positive results, benefiting individuals’ health and career development [16]. Furthermore, implementing mindfulness interventions among nurses helps reduce compassion fatigue while enhancing compassion satisfaction and work engagement, thereby boosting patients’ well-being [17, 18]. A systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that different types of mindfulness interventions can significantly mitigate depression, stress, and death anxiety among nurses both in the short and long term, and improve resilience and sleep quality [19].

Mindfulness is composed of multiple dimensions, both distinct and overlapping, and when influenced by Buddhist perspectives, its definition becomes increasingly complex and diverse [20, 21]. Currently, scholars have developed many scales assessing mindfulness based on different perspectives or research purposes [22]. The most commonly used and validated scales include the Cognitive and Affective Mindfulness Scale-Revised (CAMS-R), Freiburg Mindfulness Inventory (FMI), the Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS), the Southampton Mindfulness Questionnaire (SMQ), the Kentucky Inventory of Mindfulness Scale (KIMS), the Toronto Mindfulness Scale (TMS), and the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ) [23].

The main focus of this study is examining the reliability and validity of the CAMS-R among nurses for the following three reasons. First, most researchers regard mindfulness as a transient or temporary state (state mindfulness) or a stable individual difference (trait mindfulness) [24]. The CAMS-R conceptualizes mindfulness as a response tendency that exhibits stability across different contexts, but can also be altered via intervention [12]. The CAMS-R, therefore, can be used to analyze individual differences in mindfulness and its antecedent and outcome variables, as well as to explore the instantaneous experience and intervention effects of mindfulness.

Second, certain scales (e.g., FMI, and KIMS) contain many specialized terms and are more applicable to people with meditation experience or familiar with Buddhist concepts [22, 25]. This study mainly focused on the broad benefits of mindfulness on nurses (especially its positive effects on career development) rather than individuals familiar with mindfulness training principles and methods. As indicated in a review study, the CAMS-R presented a higher correlation than other mindfulness scales with reactions on distressing inner experiences, psychological symptoms, neuroticism, and difficulties in emotion regulation, and was more suitable for clinical practice and assessment of intervention effects [26]. Third, CAMS-R has been used as an assessment tool for measuring mindfulness among nurses, but to date no previous studies have explored the validity of the scale among nurses [27]. For example, Pérez-Fuentes et al. employed the CAMS-R to examine the relationship between mindfulness, cognitive and affective empathy, and engagement among nurses [28].

Initially, in order to capture a broad conceptualization of mindfulness, Feldman and his colleagues developed an 18-item Cognitive and Affective Mindfulness Scale (CAMS). Despite the fact that CAMS proves to be an effective tool for assessing mindfulness, the CAMS has issues such as low internal consistency, lack of evaluation of present-focused dimensions, and instability of factor loadings in some items [29]. As a result, Feldman et al. redeveloped a 12-item Cognitive and Affective Mindfulness Scale-Revised (CAMS-R) [12]. In CAMS-R, mindfulness is conceptualized as a multidimensional construct comprised of four parts: attention, present focus, awareness, and acceptance. Moreover, given that item 2 and item 7 assessing worry and rumination towards future or past respectively may exhibit strong correlations with other constructs, Feldman et al. deleted the above two items and constructed a 10-item version of the CAMS-R. Results showed that both the 12-item and 10-item scales exhibited acceptable psychometric characteristics, and there was no significant difference between the two scales. The CAMS-R has been widely used due to its simplicity, use of clear everyday language (not merely intended for participants in mindfulness training), and comprehensive conceptual coverage [30].

The CAMS-R has been currently revised into different languages, and its validity has been examined in various cultural contexts. Currently, the CAMS-R has been revised into versions in different languages, and its validity has been validated among university students or community populations in Italy, Turkey, Indonesia, Arabic countries, and Hong Kong [31–35]. Meanwhile, some researchers examined the reliability and validity of the CAMS-R in the medical and healthcare fields [36]. For example, Snyder et al. found that the CAMS-R exhibited good construct validity, internal consistency reliability, and predictive validity in cancer patients, as well as measurement invariance in populations of different genders, cancer types, and stages [37].

Although the CAMS-R is recognized as an effective tool for assessing mindfulness willingness and capacity, controversy still exists about its factor structure. When developing the initial scale, Feldman et al. pointed out that there was a medium to large correlation between the four dimensions of the CAMS-R, and the internal consistency level of each dimension is relatively low [12]. Therefore, Feldman et al. constructed a second-order four-factor model and suggested computing the total scores of all dimensions rather than separate scores for each dimension. In subsequent studies, some inconsistent results were yielded, suggesting that the factor structure of the CAMS-R needs to be reexamined with caution. For example, in the study of Chan et al., the four-factor structure of mindfulness was explored and verified, but item 10 (initially belonging to the Acceptance dimension) and item 11 (initially belong to the Present-focus dimension) were instead classified under the Awareness and Attention dimension respectively [35]. In Huang et al.’s study, it was found that after the exclusion of three items (item 2, item 6, and item 7) and the Present-focus dimension, the CAMS-R exhibited acceptable psychometric properties in people living with HIV in Myanmar [38].

According to the analysis of the extant literature, the following four aspects can be considered to further promote the in-depth research on CAMS-R psychometric research. First, the Chinese version of CAMS-R is revised based on Hong Kong university students and written in traditional Chinese characters. China’s mainland and Hong Kong differ greatly in the political system, social culture, education system and economic development, which may limit the scope of use of the Chinese version of CAMS-R. Second, in the field of medical and healthcare, extant studies have only explored the reliability and validity of the CAMS-R in patients, and the empirical evidence from nurses is still insufficient. Third, as potential talents in the nursing industry, nursing postgraduates are mainly engaged in coursework at present despite the prior experience of clinical internships or short-term work. Nursing interns are engaged in basic nursing operations under the supervision and guidance of nurses or teachers. Nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses differ greatly in terms of work responsibilities, sources of stress, and attitudes towards their careers, and thus may affect the understanding of scale items [39]. Fourth, in order to better reveal the factor structure of the scale, while verifying the second-order four-factor model, it is necessary to further investigate the plausibility of other factor structures (e.g., bi-factor model).

Based on the considerations above, this study aimed to examine the reliability, validity, measurement invariance of the 10-item CAMS-R among nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses, and its effects on occupational behavior and well-being. In the development of the original scale, Feldman et al. emphasized that items 2 and 7 were weakly correlated with other items and strongly correlated with the construct of worry and rumination, possibly because these two items measure the outcome of mindfulness rather than the process [12]. Meanwhile, compared with the 12-item version, the 10-item CAMS-R demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties as well and is more concise. Therefore, Feldman et al. recommended using the 10-item scale in relevant studies. In addition, considering the controversy surrounding the factor structure of the CAMS-R, particularly the conceptual overlap between the present focus and attention dimensions, one aim of the study was to test whether these two dimensions could be empirically distinguished among nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses. As a result, this study mainly focused on the reliability and validity of the 10-item CAMS-R. Results of this study provide empirical evidence for explaining the effectiveness of the CAMS-R, and serve as instrumental support for the evaluation of the effectiveness of mindfulness interventions. Furthermore, this study may provide a theoretical reference for improving nurses’ mindfulness, occupational well-being, and professional identity, and reducing occupational stigma and turnover intentions.

Methods

Aim of the study

The purpose of this study was to investigate the reliability, validity and measurement invariance of the Chinese version of the CAMS-R among nursing postgraduates, nursing interns and registered nurses.

Design and setting

In factor analysis, an insufficient sample size may lead to unstable factors and decreased generalizability. Typically, the minimum sample size should be 5 times or even 10 times the number of items [40, 41]. This study uses the 10-times rule to determine the minimal sample size, and thus the number of nursing postgraduates, nursing interns and registered nurses should all exceed 100. The researchers reached out to nursing teachers from five universities in three provinces in China, and after obtaining consent, the teachers sent the survey link to the nursing postgraduates. All nursing postgraduates have participated clinical internships during their undergraduate or graduate studies, some nursing postgraduates used to be nurses (26 individuals), and a small number of nursing postgraduates are currently employed nurses (8individuals). Considering that all the nursing postgraduates in this study are full-time students, even the employed nurses (8 individuals) have part-time or short-term contracts with hospitals. Therefore, this study still defines this group as nursing postgraduates. In addition, the survey of the nursing interns and registered nurses is completed in three channels: (1) Communication with the management of the university-affiliated hospitals and the internship hospitals, who assist in sending the survey link to the nurses; (2) the researchers conducted phone calls with graduates by phone and invited them to participate in this survey; (3) Through the snowball sampling method, participants were asked to recommend potential participants who met the research criteria and provide corresponding contact information. After the survey, the researchers reviewed the data thoroughly, rephrased all items in the same direction, and deleted the data entries that selected the same options.

Measures

In addition to the CAMS-R, participants also filled out a sociodemographic information questionnaire, including age, years of professional experience, gender, place of residence, ethnicity, degree of education, marital status, and professional title. In addition, to examine the criterion validity of the CAMS-R, participants also filled in the Workplace Well-Being Scale (WWB), Occupational Disidentification Scale (QDS), Stigma Consciousness Questionnaire (SCQ), and Intent to Leave Scale (ILS).

Workplace well-being scale (WWB)

WWB was developed by Zheng et al. in 2015 to assess employees’ satisfaction with their jobs and the meaning and value they feel from work [42]. WWB comprises 6 items, including a sample item “In general, I feel fairly satisfied with my present job”. The scale is scored on a 7-point scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for WWB in the three sample populations were 0.946, 0.964, and 0.938, respectively; the McDonald’s ω coefficients were 0.946, 0.964, and 0.939, respectively.

Occupational disidentification scale (QDS)

To measure the degree of employees’ denial and detachment from their professional identity, Schaubroeck et al. developed the QDS based on the organizational disidentification scale [43]. The QDS consists of 3 items, a sample item is “I feel embarrassed by my job”. The scale is scored on a 7-point scale, and participants need to choose from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for QDS in the three sample populations were 0.803, 0.860, and 0.823, respectively; the McDonald’s ω coefficients were 0.805, 0.862, and 0.829, respectively.

Stigma consciousness questionnaire (SCQ)

The SCQ was used to capture the stigmatizing attitudes perceived by employees in the workplace [44]. The SCQ is a unidimensional structure consisting of 6 items, with a sample item being “My being a nurse influences how people who are not nurse act with me”. The scale is scored on a 7-point scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for SCQ in the three sample populations were 0.916, 0.919, and 0.881, respectively; the McDonald’s ω coefficients were 0.918, 0.919, and 0.882, respectively.

Intent to leave scale (ILS)

The ILS is widely used to assess the intentions of nurses, doctors, service workers, and other employees to leave their current positions in search of new job opportunities [2, 45]. The scale includes 4 items, such as “How long do you intend to remain with this organization?” The ILS is scored on a 5-point scale, and participants rate the frequency of each item on a scale from “never” to “constantly”. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients for ILS in the three sample populations were 0.836, 0.820, and 0.829, respectively; the McDonald’s ω coefficients were 0.839, 0.856, and 0.831, respectively.

Scale adjustment

The Chinese version of the CAMS-R was revised based on the English version and the Traditional Chinese version. First, an Associate Professor of Psychology and a psychology graduate student studying at a university in Hong Kong were responsible for adapting the Traditional Chinese version into Simplified Chinese. Additionally, one English language teacher and one PhD in psychology were invited to translate the English version of the scale into Chinese. Next, the researchers compared all the translated versions and examined whether the Chinese version of the scale aligned with the original content and whether its language style adhered to the expression conventions in mainland China. This led to the formation of the initial Chinese version of the scale. Following this, another English teacher who had not previously encountered the CAMS-R conducted a back-translation of the Chinese version into English, which was then compared with the original scale. The researchers also invited an Associate Professor of Nursing, a PhD in Nursing, and two Associate Professors of Psychology to evaluate the content of the scale. Furthermore, 11 clinical nurses were invited to participate in a pre-survey, focusing on assessing whether the scale’s items were clear, concise, and easy to understand. Based on expert feedback and the pre-survey results, the researchers made slight adjustments to certain items.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 24.0, and the mean, standard deviation, kurtosis, and skewness of each item were calculated. In addition, to investigate whether the data are suitable for factor analysis, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value was calculated. A KMO value greater than the 0.80 threshold indicates strong correlations among variables, suggesting that the data are suitable for factor analysis [46]. On this basis, all data (n = 1099) were randomly divided into two groups (equal numbers of nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses) and performed EFA and CFA respectively [47]. In EFA, the maximum likelihood method was used for factor extraction based on eigenvalues greater than 1, and the Promax method was used for factor rotation [48].

Based on the EFA results and theoretical frameworks, five models were constructed in CFA for comparison. These five models are: (1) first-order three-factor model, the factor structure explored by EFA; (2) first-order four-factor model, the factor structure constructed in the original study; (3) second-order four-factor model, specified by adding a higher-order latent variable to the four first-order dimensions, consistent with the factor structure recommended in the original study; (4) second-order three-factor model, specified by adding a higher-order latent variable to the three dimensions explored; (5) bi-factor model, constructed by adding a general factor on the basis of the first-order three-factor model to explain the common variation across all items [49]. RMSEA and SRMR less than 0.08, and CFI and TLI greater than 0.90 are used as criteria for judging whether the model is a good fit [50]. If a model demonstrates a better fit index and a lower AIC value, it is regarded as the optimal model [51].

In order to explain the predictive effect of cognitive and affective mindfulness on nurses’ occupational behavior and well-being, the correlation between the CAMS-R and the WWB, QDS, SCQ, and ILS was calculated using Pearson correlation analysis. Additionally, Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s ω coefficients above the recommended 0.70 threshold were used as the criteria for good CAMS-R reliability [52]. McDonald’s ω coefficients were calculated using the plugin developed by Hayes (www.afhayes.com). Meanwhile, in order to investigate the measurement invariance of the CAMS-R in nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses, the study sequentially imposed equality constraints on factor loadings, indicator intercepts, and error variances based on the configural invariance model (M1) and the following weak invariance model (M2), strong invariance model (M3) being constructed. The CFI of the above four models were compared in turn, and if ∆CFI < -0.01 (i.e., the CFI decreases by more than 0.01), it indicates that the CAMS-R has a measured invariance among nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and nurses [53]. If the threshold value of -0.01 is exceeded, the imposition of further constraints is discontinued, and conclusions are drawn accordingly.

Descriptive statistical analysis (see Table 2) showed that the absolute value of kurtosis of each item was between 0.18 ~ 0.61 and the absolute value of skewness was between 0.04 ~ 0.26. The absolute values of kurtosis and skewness close to 0 indicate that the data do not deviate from the assumption of normal distribution. Results of the independent sample t-test (see Table 2) showed that the scores of each item in the high group of the CAMS-R were significantly higher than those of the low group (t = 14.03 ~ 29.58, all ps < .01). Furthermore, each item in the CAMS-R was significantly correlated with the total score (all ps < .01), and the r value was between 0.49 ~ 0.71.

Exploratory factor analysis

In Sample 1 (n = 549), the KMO value was 0.83, and Bartlett’s test was significant (χ² = 1674.67, df = 45, p < .001), indicating that the data was suitable for factor analysis. The EFA results (see Table 3) showed that three factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, with the percentages of variance explained being 38.91%, 13.76%, and 11.11%, respectively, and the cumulative percentage of variance explained being 63.78%. Differing from the original scale, item 11 did not form an independent factor, but was attributed to the Attention dimension. The factor load value of each item was between 0.54 and 0.84 and communality 0.33 ~ 0.64.

Correlation between CAMS-R and occupational psychology and behavior

Results of Pearson correlation analysis showed that (see Table 5), the total score of CAMS-R and the scores of each dimension were significantly positively correlated with WWB (r = 0.22 ~ 0.37, all ps < .01); the total score of CAMS-R and Acceptance, and Attention dimensions were significantly negatively correlated with QDS, SCQ, and ILS (r=-0.10~-0.32, all ps < .01). Furthermore, the Awareness dimension only had a significant negative correlation with ILS (r=-0.07, p = .01), and was not significantly correlated with QDS (r=-0.04, p = .15) and SCQ (r = -0.04, p = .17).

Reliability test

The reliability test results showed that the Cronbach’s α coefficients of the total CAMS-R scale, Acceptance, Attention, and Awareness dimensions were 0.84, 0.80, 0.75, and 0.73, respectively, and the McDonald’s ω coefficients were 0.84, 0.81, 0.76, and 0.73, respectively.

Measurement invariance test

The study examined the fit indices of the bi-factor model among nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses respectively. The results showed that the fit indices of CAMS-R in nursing postgraduate students were χ2 = 54.73, df = 23, χ2/df = 2.38, RMSEA = 0.092, CFI = 0.933, TLI = 0.868, SRMR = 0.057. The fit indices of CAMS-R in nursing interns were χ2 = 52.24, df = 23, χ2/df = 2.27, RMSEA = 0.057, CFI = 0.980, TLI = 0.960, SRMR = 0.033. The fit indices of CAMS-R in nurses were χ2 = 40.98, df = 23, χ2/df = 1.78, RMSEA = 0.038, CFI = 0.989, TLI = 0.978, SRMR = 0.027. Except for some fit indices that did not meet the criteria in nursing postgraduates, the bi-factor model of the CAMS-R demonstrated good fit among nursing interns and registered nurses, indicating that it was appropriate to proceed with measurement invariance testing. The measurement invariance test (see Table 6) revealed that the change in CFI (∆CFI = -0.015) between Model 3 and Model 2 exceeded the threshold of -0.01. Therefore, the strong invariance model (i.e., intercept invariance) of the CAMS-R was not supported.

Discussion

Stress is common among nurses due to factors such as inadequate compensation, staff shortages, frequent night shifts, limited opportunities for career advancement, workplace bullying, high patient demands, and exposure to patient suffering [54]. Additionally, nursing postgraduates and interns need to deal with multiple pressures such as coursework, academic research, clinical internship, and lack of stable income, and hence often face disproportionately high levels of stress [55]. Mindfulness training is considered highly beneficial in reducing stress, burnout, and emotional distress among nurses, and improving resilience, job satisfaction, and empathy [56, 57]. To better evaluate the effects of mindfulness interventions in nursing education and healthcare settings, it’s essential to test the reliability and validity of the Cognitive and affective mindfulness scale-revised (CAMS-R) among nurses and nursing postgraduates. This study found that the 10 items of the CAMS-R demonstrated good discriminant ability, and the scale exhibited good construct validity, criterion validity, and reliability, making it a reliable and effective tool.

Through exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis, this study identified a three-factor structure. Feldman et al. conducted an EFA on the 12-item CAMS-R during the development of the original scale, and verified the validity of the four-factor model in the 12-item and 10-item scales [12]. In contrast, in this present study a three-factor structure was explored in the 10-item CAMS-R, and it was found that the second-order three-factor model was superior to the first-order four-factor model and the second-order four-factor model. This study draws a conclusion that is inconsistent with Feldman et al.’s research, yet is supported by other empirical evidence, especially from Asian countries [38]. For example, in the study of Chan et al., it was found that item 11 belonged to the Attention dimension [35]. The results of this study suggest that some conceptual overlap exists between Present focus and Attention, and the CAMS-R seems unable to completely and accurately distinguish between the two.

This result may be attributed to the complexity of the mindfulness construct [58]. Although previous studies have developed various scales based on different understandings of mindfulness, most researchers have followed the two-component model of mindfulness proposed by Bishop et al., which was also adopted in the development of the CAMS-R [59]. The two-component model conceptualizes mindfulness as consisting of two fundamental components: self-regulation of attention and orientation to experience [60]. Specifically, self-regulation of attention refers to the capacity to focus attention on the present moment and maintain sustained attention over time; orientation to experience describes an individual’s attitude of curiosity, openness, and acceptance toward present experiences [61]. The Present-focus and Attention dimensions of the CAMS-R collectively reflect the self-regulation of Attention component and present a strong correlation, likely to cause confusion. Therefore, future research could consider distinguishing more finely between Present-focus and Attention by proposing operational definitions and developing new items. Moreover, the first-order four-factor model and the second-order four-factor model of the 10-item CAMS-R both demonstrated acceptable fit indices. To avoid purely data-driven decisions and to maintain theoretical and methodological rigor, researchers may as well consider retaining the Present-focus dimension (including only item 11).

In the original scale, mindfulness was considered to be composed of one second-order latent factor and four first-order latent factors, and a higher-order factor model was constructed. To better explain the common variance (general factor) and unique characteristics (special factor) of each item in the CAMS-R, this study constructed a bi-factor model [62]. The bi-factor model and the higher-order model have nested relations, and they are equivalent only when the loadings satisfy the proportion constraint [63]. In most psychometric studies, the bi-factor model is found to more accurately reflect psychological constructs and is often more appropriate than the higher-order model [64]. This study reached consistent conclusions, the fit indices of the bi-factor model of the CAMS-R superior to those of the higher-order model.

The results of the measurement invariance testing indicate that the configural and weak invariance of the CAMS-R across the three sample groups were supported by the data. However, the ∆CFI for strong invariance is -0.015, exceeding the threshold. In other words, the CAMS-R is not invariant among nurses at different career development stages. Currently, testing the invariance of mindfulness-related scales across different groups is a heated topic in the field of mindfulness research, but conclusions from various studies are inconsistent [65]. For instance, Levin-Aspenson et al. found that all dimensions of the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ) met configural and weak invariance, while only certain dimensions satisfied strong invariance [66]. Johnson et al.’s research results showed that people of different age groups have different frames of reference and varying understandings of some items in the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ-15) [67]. Krägeloh et al.’s study revealed response shifts in the 37-item Comprehensive Inventory of Mindfulness Experiences (CHIME) among populations with different levels of mindfulness experience [68].

The findings of this study are consistent with research results of Johnson et al. and Krägeloh et al. Since the weak invariance model of the CAMS-R across different groups was supported, this indicates that the strength of the relationship between the items and the latent constructs is equal. However, the lack of strong invariance (i.e., unequal item intercepts) suggests that participants in different groups may have systematic biases in their responses to the scale, which could confound the group comparison results [53]. In other words, due to the influence of prior knowledge, education level, response style, or work experience, nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses may interpret some items of the CAMS-R with bias [69]. Therefore, based on the analysis of the current sample population, it can be concluded that the CAMS-R should not be used for comparing differences between nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses, or that caution should be exercised when interpreting group differences. The potential impact of measurement non-invariance on the results should be clearly stated. Nevertheless, the CAMS-R remains an effective tool for exploring the relationship between mindfulness and other variables, such as through correlation analysis, regression analysis, or structural equation modeling.

In the criterion validity testing, it was found that the total score of the CAMS-R, as well as the acceptance and attention dimensions, had a significant positive correlation with work well-being, and a significant negative correlation with occupational disidentification, occupational stigma, and intention to leave. However, although the awareness dimension showed a significant negative correlation with intention to leave, the correlation coefficient was only − 0.07. This result can be interpreted from three perspectives. First, the awareness dimension demonstrates characteristics different from those of the acceptance and attention dimensions, supporting the view that mindfulness is a multidimensional concept and indicating that the CAMS-R is capable of capturing the differences among the various dimensions of mindfulness [70]. Second, researchers have currently designed mindfulness intervention methods with varying formats, approaches, durations, and content [71]. The results of this study suggest that intervention methods focusing on different components of mindfulness may lead to significantly different practical effects. Unlike the acceptance and attention dimensions, the awareness dimension primarily reflects an individual’s ability to describe, keep track of, and notice their current experiences. In intervention practice, to enhance nurses’ professional identity, reduce occupational stigma, and lower turnover intention, efforts should focus on strengthening their ability to regulate attention and cultivate an attitude of acceptance or nonjudgment toward their experiences. Third, statistical significance does not equate to practical significance. In general, despite small or even trivial effect sizes, statistical significance may still be observed with larger sample sizes [72]. Therefore, the correlation between the awareness dimension and intention to leave may be a statistical correlation influenced by the sample size.

This study has some theoretical value. Based on the analysis of the extant literature, this study is the first known academic attempt to verify the psychometric characteristics, factor structure and measurement invariance of the CAMS-R among nursing postgraduates, nursing interns and registered nurses. This study not only enriches the literature on mindfulness in workplace settings but also contributes to a better understanding of the factor structure of the CAMS-R, providing empirical evidence to address the ongoing debates regarding the construct structure. Additionally, the exploration of the bi-factor model in this study aligns with the view that mindfulness is a multidimensional structure and conducive to better understanding the relationships between different dimensions, providing a foundation for exploring the relationship between mindfulness and outcome variables [73]. Another theoretical contribution of this study lies in the examination of the measurement invariance of the CAMS-R among nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses, which provides psychometric evidence for the comparison and interpretation of group differences.

In addition to theoretical contributions, this study also has some practical implications. First, this study offers instrument support for the evaluation of the effectiveness of mindfulness interventions in nursing education and healthcare practice fields. At present, various intervention methods based on mindfulness have been applied in medical and healthcare sector, especially in stress management and burnout prevention [74]. Meanwhile, some researchers have made attempts to integrate mindfulness training into the cultivation of nursing talents, designing different forms of intervention strategies (e.g., reflective mindfulness training, and web-based mindfulness interventions), and offering related courses or lectures [75, 76]. This study validated the reliability and validity of the CAMS-R among nursing students and registered nurses, and could be an effective instrument for assessing changes in mindfulness, tracking the long-term effects of intervention, and comparing different intervention methods.

Second, the exploration of the relationship between mindfulness and occupational psychology and behavior of this study aids in a better understanding of the potential value of mindfulness for nursing students and nurses. Cultivating mindfulness among nurses not only helps to improve occupational identity, reduce occupational stigma, and decrease turnover intentions, but also increase job satisfaction. Not only can nurses benefit from participating in mindfulness interventions, these interventions can also influence emotional states, satisfaction, engagement, and vitality during work, thereby enhancing patient well-being. Additionally, the nursing industry faces serious issues such as staff shortages and high turnover rates, imposing negative effects on healthcare costs and nursing quality [77] . Findings of this study suggest that implementing mindfulness interventions for nursing students, interns, and nurses at different stages of their professional development could facilitate in attracting more nursing students to pursue careers in the nursing profession in the future. Furthermore, mindfulness interventions may help stabilize the nursing workforce, reduce the loss of skilled professionals, and ultimately contribute to the sustainable development of the profession.

Limitations and future research

Although the validation results indicate that the CAMS-R demonstrates acceptable reliability and validity, some limitations should be addressed in future research. First, the representativeness of the sample is limited, as the study recruited only 165 nursing postgrads. Furthermore, the convenience and snowball sampling methods may constrain the generalizability of the findings. The use of convenience sampling in this study may systematically bias the sample towards the most easily accessible participants, potentially overlooking other groups, such as nurses from community hospitals or those with longer work experience. Additionally, snowball sampling relies heavily on initial recommenders, and participants tend to refer others who share similar views, which could lead to higher homogeneity in the sample. Although there are these limitations, the nurses and nursing interns in this study were from nearly 30 hospitals across 15 provinces in China, and nursing postgraduates were from five universities in three provinces. Therefore, the sample is quite representative and reflects the main characteristics of nurses across the country. Future research could consider further validating the CAMS-R among broader populations, such as undergraduate and vocational nursing students, to provide stronger measurement support for implementing mindfulness interventions in nursing education. Moreover, it’s suggested that the instrument administered to a larger and more diverse sample of nurses—varying in geographic region, hospital level, professional rank, and years of experience—to enhance the robustness of the conclusions.

Second, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, it is difficult to explain the causal relationship between the CAMS-R and nurses’ professional identity and behavior. In future studies, longitudinal tracking is needed to better illustrate the predictive validity and test-retest reliability of the CAMS-R. Third, while this study explores the factor structure of CAMS-R and finds that the bi-factor model is superior to other models, this does not mean that the controversy over the factor structure of the CAMS-R has been completely resolved. In subsequent research, the factor structure of the CAMS-R in diverse populations from different cultural backgrounds still needs to be examined.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that the 10-item Chinese version of the cognitive and affective mindfulness scale-revised (CAMS-R) is a reliable and valid instrument for measuring mindfulness levels among nursing postgraduates, nursing interns, and registered nurses. This research has yielded a deeper understanding of the scale’s cross-cultural applicability, underlying factor structure, and its relevance to nurses’ career development and occupational behavior. Beyond enriching the growing body of literature on mindfulness in workplace settings, the study also offers instrumental value for evaluating the effectiveness of mindfulness-based interventions in nursing education and psychological support services for nurses.
