Direct SERS Detection of Folic Acid Using Modified Graphene Oxide–Gold Nanobowl Composites
Authors: Krystian Pupel, Kacper Jędrzejewski, Mateusz Kasztelan, Sylwia Żołądek, Barbara Pałys
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGold nanobowls (AuNBs) synthesized by the template-free method were deposited on graphene oxide (GO) to obtain an ultrasensitive surface enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) platform for folic acid (FA) detection. GO was conditioned in aqueous solutions at various pH values to optimize the adsorption of the FA molecule and the intensity of the SERS signal. It was found that the conditioning procedure influences the orientation of FA on the SERS supports and the quality of the spectra in result. The limit of detection (LOD) for FA was equal to 10–12 M for the best combination of GO conditioned at pH = 1 and AuNBs (GO-1/AuNBs). The composites of AuNBs with GO conditioned in solutions of higher pH were less efficient. The LOD values were equal to 10–8 M for AuNBs deposited on GO conditioned at pH = 5.6 (GO-5.6/AuNBs) and 10–7 M for AuNBs deposited on GO conditioned at pH = 13 (GO-13/AuNBs). The high sensitivity of the GO-1/AuNBs composite has been explained by the high adsorption of FA and the molecular orientation with a p-aminobenzoic ring attached to the surface. We suppose that such an orientation fosters efficient charge transfer between the adsorbed molecule and the support, which in turn enhances the SERS intensity by the chemical effect in addition to the electromagnetic enhancement yield by AuNBs. The adsorbed FA molecules attain the random conformation or adsorption through the pteridine ring on the supports with GO conditioned at higher pH. Such orientation results in less efficiency in obtaining the enhancement through the chemical mechanism.

Introduction

Folic acid (FA) is an organic compound that belongs to the vitamin B group. It is not synthesized by the human body and, therefore, must be obtained from food.1 FA takes part in many important processes ongoing in the human organism, such as nucleic acid synthesis, red blood cell production, or cell division.2,3 Its deficiency can lead to many diseases, such as megaloblastic anemia, an increased risk of cancer, or leukopenia. Folic acid is essential during fetal development.4 Thus, detection of FA is pivotally important due to complications caused by its deficiency.

So far, many methods for the detection of folic acid have been published, such as chromatographic, electrochemical, or spectrophotometric methods and surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS).5−8 Despite their high sensitivity, these methods are rather time-consuming or expensive. Furthermore, the efficiency of the assay may also be hindered by interfering species. The great advantages of surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) are the fingerprint like analytical signal and the sensitivity enabling single-molecule detection in optimized conditions.9,10 The primary mechanism causing the enhancement of Raman spectra is the large electromagnetic field produced on the surface of metallic nanoparticles as a result of the enhancement of surface plasmons.11 This effect can be maximized by the choice of the appropriate plasmonic material, the shape and size of the nanoparticle, and the distance between the nanostructures, enabling the formation of hotspots.12,13 The self-assembly process can be used to design the second-generation hot spots for very sensitive and homogeneous SERS supports.14

Gold nanobowls (AuNBs) were recently synthesized by our group.15 These nanostructures are characterized by broad, strong plasmonic absorption, giving a strong SERS enhancement. The enhancement factors for AuNBs are higher compared to the spherical gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) and gold nanourchins (AuNUs).15 For this reason, we chose AuNBs to design a novel SERS assay for FA determination.

Another important contribution to the SERS enhancement comes from the so-called chemical effect, which implies charge transfer between the chemisorbed molecule and the support of the laser light upon absorption. This mechanism resembles the resonance Raman effect.16 The chemical enhancement is a quantitatively smaller factor compared to the electromagnetic effect, although it might improve the selectivity of SERS assays as it requires the chemisorption of the molecule on the SERS active medium. The chemical enhancement may operate in parallel to the electromagnetic enhancement. Some semiconductive or graphene family materials are able to enhance Raman intensity mainly through the chemical mechanism.17,18

The obstacles hampering the wide use of SERS in chemical analysis are the repeatability and long-term stability of SERS supports. It is generally accepted that silver nanostructures yield large values of the enhancement factors, although their long-term stability is lower compared to gold nanoparticles. The design of stable and homogeneous substrates is still a challenge for SERS applications. The combination of graphene materials with noble metal nanoparticles is one of the possibilities for improving the stability.19 Graphene oxide (GO) wrapped gold nanostars were proposed as highly stable probes for Raman imaging.20

GO is an easy-to-work-with material that can be modified via chemical reactions. The presence of numerous oxygen groups on the surface enables modifications that have an influence on the SERS enhancement. Recently, we have shown that the simple treatment of GO with a 3% ammonia solution significantly improves the interaction with gold nanoparticles and the SERS efficiency in turn.21 Conditioning of GO in basic solution also influences the SERS properties of silver nanoparticles.22

SERS has been studied as a possible tool for FA detection. Yang et al. published a label-free substrate for detection of FA using the sandwich structure containing a copper film and silver nanoparticles. In this sandwich structure, the bottom layer is composed of a copper foil decorated with silver nanoparticles synthesized by the galvanic displacement reaction, and the top layer is constituted by silver nanoparticles.23 The plasmonic enhancement was optimized by the deposition of silver nanostructures. The limit of detection was as low as 1 × 10–10 M under optimized conditions.

Another approach was the thermal reorganization of silver nanoparticles in freestanding, flexible chitosan-functionalized reduced graphene oxide thick films, which was used to obtain a homogeneous probe for folic acid sensing.24 Spectra of FA were obtained at micromolar concentrations.

The combination of GO, the cationic polymer, and silver nanoparticles was used to enrich the adsorption of folic acid. The SERS spectra were recorded at a concentration of 9 × 10–9 M.25

Wu et al. proposed an indirect SERS assay of FA using gold and silver nanoparticle heterodimers with so-called SERS reporters and antibodies as ligands. The antigen–antibody immunoreaction caused the diminishing intensity of the reporter molecule bands in the presence of FA molecules.26 The immunoreaction gives excellent sensitivity and selectivity, although the synthetic route to obtain the nanoparticles modified by both the Raman reporter molecule and the antibody is not straightforward.

Recently, the electrochemically generated SERS substrates were proposed for FA detection,27 giving detection LOD equal to 7.3 × 10–7 M.

Here, we propose the simple direct SERS substrate composed of AuNBs deposited on GO for the detection of FA. In order to influence the GO adsorption, it was modified using sodium chloride solutions with pH = 1 and pH = 5.6, as well as a 3% sodium hydroxide solution. We demonstrate that the orientation of the FA molecule on the SERS support is dependent on the GO pretreatment. Up to our knowledge, the correlation between the pretreatment of GO and the influence on the adsorption of analyte molecules on the gold nanoparticles–GO hybrid supports was not reported. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the orientation is correlated with the intensity of the SERS signal.

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Reagents

All reagents were available commercially and were used as received. Tetrachloroauric acid trihydrate, (HAuCl4·3H2O, >99.9%), silver nitrate (AgNO3, 99.9999%), sodium citrate dihydrate, HOC(COONa)-(CH2COONa)2·2H2O (>99.9%), sodium borohydride (powder, NaBH4, 98%), rhodamine 6G (R6G, 99%), and graphite powder (1–2 μm) were purchased from Merck. Nitrogen gas (purity 99.999%) was purchased from Air Products (Poland). Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 95%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 35–38%), and sodium chloride (NaCl) were purchased from Avantor Performance Materials (Poland). Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%), ammonia solution (NH4OH, 25%), and potassium permanganate were purchased from Chempur (Poland). Potassium bromide (KBr, spectroscopic grade) was purchased from Pike Technologies. All solutions were prepared using distilled water (Milli-Q, 18.2 MΩ·cm). Solutions for nanoparticle synthesis were deoxygenated by being bubbled with nitrogen gas.

Synthesis of GO and its Conditioning

Graphene oxide was synthesized using a modified Hummers–Offeman method.28 Briefly, 6 g of graphite powder and 150 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid were mixed while being cooled in an ice bath. Afterward, 21 g of potassium permanganate was added, and the mixture was stirred for 2 h. Next, 150 mL of distilled water was added slowly; the average rate was 0.5 mL/s. Subsequently, 35 mL of hydrogen peroxide was added. Prepared GO was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 0.5 h, washed with distilled water four times, and left to dry on air.

The GO was conditioned in aqueous solutions containing 0.1 M HCl, 0.2 M NaCl, and 3% NaOH. The pH values of these solutions were equal to 1.0, 5.6, and 13.0, respectively (measured by the pH-meter ELMETRON, Poland). The temperature in the lab was equal to 22 °C during the whole conditioning procedure. No further temperature control was applied.

Three samples of 20 mg of GO were mixed with 3 mL of each solution separately, sonicated for 30 min, and mixed with a magnetic stirrer (700 rpm) overnight. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 30 min, washed with distilled water, left to dry in air, and subsequently stored in an airtight container. The conditioned GOs were labeled GO-1 (GO conditioned at pH = 1), GO-5.6 (GO conditioned at pH = 5.6), and GO-13 (GO conditioned in the 3% NaOH).

Synthesis of AuNBs

The synthesis of AuNBs has been described previously.15 Briefly: 15 mL of a 6 mM solution of sodium citrate was mixed with 3 mL of a 4.3 mM solution of HAuCl4 and 2 mL of 7.8 mM AgNO3. Subsequently, the solution was mixed for 3 min under the flow of nitrogen gas. Separately, a solution of 25 mL of 30% H2O2 and 1.5 mL of 0.03 M NaBH4 was prepared and deoxygenated by using nitrogen gas. After deoxygenation, the solution was added to the mixture of metal precursors and mixed for 1 min. Subsequently, 15 mL of 6 mM solution of sodium citrate was added to the mixture and stirred for 2 h. The resulting solution was centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 20 min and washed three times with distilled water. The synthesis was carried out in the lab, where the temperature was kept at 22 °C. No additional temperature control procedures were used.

Preparation of SERS Substrates

SERS substrates were prepared by using a layer-by-layer method on ITO glass. The procedure of the support illustration is illustrated in Scheme 1. First, ITO glass was cut into small pieces and cleaned: washed with detergent, rinsed with distilled water, and then sonicated in ethanol for 10 min. Such prepared ITO plates were left to dry and used further to prepare the SERS substrate.

Preparation of the SERS Support

The aqueous dispersion of GO-1, GO-5.6, or GO-13 was prepared. For all types of GO, the concentration was equal to 0.2 mg/mL. The dispersions were sonicated in an ultrasonic bath for 30 min. The ITO plates were put into the sonicated dispersions to allow for the adsorption of GO. The ITO plates were kept in the GO dispersions for 24 h. Then, they were taken out of the solution and left to dry. Next, they were immersed in the AuNBs colloidal solution for 24 h, taken out again, and air-dried. The AuNBs support without the GO layer was obtained by immersing the clean ITO plate into the colloidal solution of AuNBs for 24 h.

To perform SERS experiments, 40 μL of water solutions of R6G or folic acid with various concentrations was drop-casted on the substrates and left to dry before the measurement.

Instrumentation

Infrared spectra were obtained using a Nicolet iS50 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) with a DTGS detector. The spectra of various types of GO were recorded in KBr pellets. The portion of dried GO–ca. 1 mg was mixed with 230 mg of KBr in an agate mortar. The pellet was formed by using the SPECAC hydraulic press. Typically, 64 scans were used for the sample, as well as the background spectrum. The KBr pellet without the sample was used to collect the background spectrum.

Raman spectra were recorded using the DXR Raman microscope by Thermo Scientific equipped with a 50×/0.50 NA objective. To keep the spectra comparable, we used a 1 mW laser power in all experiments shown in this work. Typical exposure times were equal to 2 s, and 5 scans were averaged for a single spectrum. In Raman measurements, a mapping technique was used to automate the performance of a larger number of measurements and to collect spectra from an area that best reflected the trend on the surface of the entire sample. For this purpose, a 7 × 7 grid was defined, and the results obtained were averaged. Therefore, each Raman spectrum presented in this work is the average of 49 spectra obtained by mapping. The red laser (633 nm) line was used as the source of excitation.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of GO, GO-1, GO-5.6, and GO-13

GO, due to the abundance of the polar oxygen functionalities, gives strong infrared spectra. The possible effects of the conditioning of GO in the aqueous solutions containing H3O+, Na+ ions, and Cl– ions were studied by infrared and Raman spectra. Figure 2 compares the infrared spectra of the pristine GO with the GO samples after the conditioning in the solutions having different pH. Among the spectral differences, the gradual diminishing of bands in the 1300–1100 cm–1 range and the disappearance of 851 cm–1 is visible in the spectra of GO-1, GO-5.6, and GO-13. The modes contributing to this spectral range include C–O and C–O–C motions of phenolic, carboxylic, ether, and epoxide groups.29−31 The bands at 1226 and 851 cm–1 correspond to the asymmetric and symmetric C–O–C modes of the epoxide groups.15,21 The diminishing of these bands indicates thus the opening of epoxide groups upon conditioning of GO in aqueous solutions with a wide range of pH values. The epoxide bands are entirely removed in the spectrum of GO-13, which agrees with our previous observations for GO conditioned in ammonia solution.15,21 The opening of epoxide rings at basic solutions stays in agreement with the report of Taniguchi et al., who have demonstrated that the epoxide ring opening in GO is very effective in basic solutions, while in acidic conditions, the epoxide rings are relatively stable.32 Taniguchi et al. have observed that the epoxide ring opening is reversible when the pH is turned back to pH = 3. However, if GO is conditioned in the basic solution for a longer time than 12 h, the ring opening is followed by the removal of oxygen from the GO surface—thereby, partial reduction of GO occurs. We condition the GO samples overnight. Therefore, we suppose that GO undergoes partial reduction—as described by Taniguchi et al. The partial reduction of GO might be a source of new defects in the GO structure. The influence of defects on infrared spectra was discussed in the literature.29,30 The theoretical calculations on models of GO defects showed that both OH stretching and OH bending modes are affected by the lattice vacancies.30

Infrared spectra of GO, GO-1, GO-5.6, and GO-13 in the 4000–500 cm–1 range (left) and enlarged fingerprint range (right).

Spectra shown in Figure 2 indicate that some epoxide rings open, even in acidic solution. We suppose that this may be the effect of the prolonged conditioning, as we condition the GO samples overnight (see Experimental Section).

Another difference between the spectra is diminishing the band at 1730 cm–1—coming from the carboxylic (COOH) groups of GO.33 This band disappears completely for GO-13, indicating that all carboxylic groups are dissociated after prolonged conditioning in the basic solution. The bands due to the COO– groups are expected at ca. 1630 and 1370 cm–1, which are typical frequencies of salts of carboxylic acid in infrared spectra.34 In the case of the GO sample, these bands overlap with OH bending and C–O stretching of other groups of GO. The general observation is that the differences between the pristine GO and GO-13 are most visible. The epoxide groups are removed, and all COOH groups are transformed in the COO– group, while the spectra of GO-1 and GO-5.6 indicate partial opening of epoxides and partial dissociation of carboxylic groups. The presence of COO– groups suggests that the chemical properties of GO-13 resemble salts of carboxylic acid. GO is a weak acid,35 while NaOH is a strong base. Thus, aqueous solutions of GO-13 should be basic due to the hydrolysis. To verify that, we dissolved GO-13 in water at the concentration equal to 0.2 mg/mL and measured pH. The pH value was equal to 9.86. We prepared the analogic 0.2 mg/mL solutions of GO-5.6 and GO-1. The measured pH values were equal to 5.75 and 5.43, respectively. The solutions were prepared with freshly distilled water in order to avoid the influence of dissolved CO2 on the pH of the solution.

The pH = 9.86 measured for the dissolved GO-13 confirms that dried GO-13 contains COO– groups, not COOH, which was observed in infrared spectra.

Raman spectra of graphene-related materials are extensively used to estimate the number and type of structural defects.36 Raman spectra of GO, GO-1, GO-5.6, and GO-13 look very similar to each other at first glance (Figure 3). Two typical bands for graphene-related materials—the so-called G band and D band are observed. The G band near 1580 cm–1 exists in spectra of all sp2 carbon materials and corresponds to the areas of ordered honeycomb-like carbon plains. The D band originates from defected or fragmented graphene planes. The intensity ratio between the D and G bands is a popular measure of defect concentration. According to the Tuinstra’s model, the ID/IG ratio is reversibly proportional to the size of crystalline domains in carbon materials.37 In the samples studied here, the ID/IG values change in the row: GO (2.09), GO-1 (2.76), GO-5.6 (2.80), and GO-13 (2.99), indicating that the conditioning in the aqueous solution creates new defects in the GO structure and the basic solution creates most of the defects.

Raman spectra of GO, GO-1, GO-5.6, and GO-13 (left). The example deconvolution of the Raman GO spectrum (right).

The bands shown in Figure 3 are very broad, indicating that more than two bands contribute to the range between 1000 and 1800 cm–1. Similar observations were reported for other GO or reduced GO materials.38,39 Following previous works, we fitted the concerned range with 5 Gaussian–Lorentzian peaks. Figure 3 (right) presents the fit result for the pristine GO. The five components are marked as D*, D, D”, G, and D’. The fit results for the remaining GOs can be found in Figures S1–S4. According to Claramunt et al.,38 the position of the D* band is red-shifted upon increasing the content of oxygen, while the position of the D” band is blue-shifted. Changes in the D* and D” positions upon the conditioning indicate that the oxygen content decreases in the row: GO> GO-1> GO-5.6> GO-13 (Table S1). Such a result stays in agreement with the infrared spectra. The intensity of the ID” band normalized to the G band (ID”/IG) is correlated with the crystallite size according to literature reports.38,40,41 The bigger the value of ID”/IG, the smaller is the crystallite size. This parameter changes in the row GO (0.31) < GO-1 (0.68) < GO-5.6 (0.69) < GO-13 (0.78)—similarly to the ID/IG ratio (Table S2). This observation suggests that the GO-13 sample is the most defective.38 Another parameter differentiating the studied samples is the intensity of the D’ band normalized to the G band intensity (ID’/IG), which is highest for the GO-1 samples. The origin of D’ is not clearly elucidated band. Some authors attribute it to a disorder-induced phonon mode due to crystal defects.42,43

The position of the G band is often correlated with the oxygen content in the sample, although the position of the G band can also be affected by the adsorption of electron donation or electron-withdrawing ions or molecules.44,45 In the series of studied samples, the position of the G band in pristine GO (1597 cm–1) is significantly blue-shifted with respect to the conditioned samples (ca. 1580 cm–1), which stays in agreement with the diminishing content of oxygen.

In summary, the infrared and Raman spectra of the GO samples indicate that GO-13 is the most defective sample, which contains carboxylic groups in the form of a salt (COO–). The GO-1 sample is less defective in general. Unlike GO-13, it contains the carboxylic groups in the acidic form (COOH).

Deposition of AuNBs on the GO Layers

The pristine GO and the GO conditioned in aqueous solutions (GO-1, GO-5.6, and GO-13) were adsorbed on ITO glass plates (as described in the experimental section). Subsequently, the layers were put into the colloidal solution of AuNBs in order to adsorb the nanoparticles on the GO. SERS Studies, R6G and FA Detection

The GO materials were deposited on a solid substrate. The ITO glass was used as a support. In the next step, AuNBs were deposited. All GO–AuNB combinations were tested as possible SERS supports. We used the Rhodamine 6G (R6G–structure in Figure S5) dye, which is a popular SERS probe, and the data are easily comparable with the results of other authors.46−49 The spectra of FA were studied after characterization with R6G. Figure 5 compares the SERS spectra of the R6G at 10–7 M concentration on bare AuNBs layers—deposited directly on ITO and on the layers composed of GO-1, GO-5.6, and GO-13 with AuNBs on top.

SERS spectra of R6G (10–7 M) on bare AuNBs, GO-1/AuNBs, GO-5.6/AuNBs, and GO-13/AuNBs.

The spectra were averaged from 49 measurements on different spots to minimize random experimental errors. All spectra presented in Figure 5 present high intensity of SERS signal confirming very high SERS efficiency of AuNBs. The R6G bands are very strong, covering the GO bands, which are visible in the SERS spectra of bare supports (Figure S6).

The spectrum of AuNBs and GO-1 shows the highest intensity, being ca. three times higher compared to bare AuNBs, suggesting that GO-1 very efficiently improves the SERS enhancement of AuNBs. Very high quality spectra of R6G can be obtained at the concentration of R6G as low as 10–12 M (Figure S7), proving high sensitivity of GO-1/AuNBs in comparison to other hybrid gold nanomaterials. Comparison of the detection limits for the R6G molecule reported for several SERS supports is shown in Table S3.

The spectra of R6G on bare GO-1, GO-5.6, or GO-13 layers without AuNBs have rather low intensity. We were able to detect clear R6G peaks starting from the concentration of 10–4 M (Figure S8). The spectra of R6G at the lower concentration range on GO were reported by other authors50 by application of the 514.5 nm laser line being in resonance with the molecular absorption of R6G. We apply the 633 nm laser line, which falls out of the molecular resonance of R6G. The weak intensity of R6G on bare GO layers suggests that GO-1, GO-5.6, or GO-13 themselves make a rather small contribution to the enhancement of Raman spectra, but they do influence the properties of AuNBs. To investigate the possible influence of GO on AuNBs, we compared the UV–vis spectra of AuNBs dispersion with spectra of mixtures of the AuNBs and GOs dispersed in water (Figure S9). The maximum of the gold plasmon band is shifted from 556 nm in the spectrum of AuNBs to ca. 545 nm in the spectra of all mixture dispersions. The plasmonic bands in mixtures are also broader. These changes in UV–vis spectra suggest that the interaction with GO might influence the plasmonic band of gold.

The well-known feature of SERS is that relative band intensities depend on the orientation of the adsorbed molecule with respect to the support.51,52 Despite the differences in the general intensity of the spectrum, the relative intensities of bands in the R6G spectra remain the same independent of the type of support. The overall spectral shape is similar to reports of other authors, who suggested the random orientation of R6G in their systems.53,54 The positions of R6G bands do not depend on the support as well. As visible in Table 1, all parts of the R6G are represented in the observed SERS spectra, which stays in agreement with the random orientation of R6G molecules on the studied supports.

The FA spectra show a different behavior. Figure 6 shows typical SERS spectra of FA on GO-1/AuNBs, GO-5.6/AuNBs, and GO-13/AuNBs. The relative standard deviation (RSD) values for the 1170 cm–1 band of FA were equal to 8.9%, 8.5%, and 9.1% for GO-1/AuNBs, GO-5.6/AuNBs, and GO-13/AuNBs, respectively, indicating rather homogeneous SERS enhancement. As illustrated in Figure 6, the relative band intensities in the FA SERS spectrum depend significantly on the type of support. A possible explanation is the different orientations of the FA molecule with respect to the support. The dependence of the relative intensities of the bands on the molecular orientation is a fundamental property of SERS spectra. The molecular oscillations perpendicular to the surface give rise to the strongest bands in the spectrum as a consequence of the electromagnetic enhancement.56 Furthermore, modes involving part of the molecule interacting directly with the surface will be enhanced by the chemical mechanism.12,13

The most intense bands in FA spectra on GO-1/AuNBs and GO-5.6/AuNBs (Figure 6) form characteristic triplet with components at 1617, 1587, and 1561 cm–1 for GO-1/AuNBs and 1626, 1593, and 1576 cm–1 for GO-5.6/AuNBs. The shifts in band positions suggest a difference in the strength of the interaction between FA molecules with GO-1/AuNBs and GO-5.6/AuNBs. The bands at 1617, 1587, 1626, and 1593 cm–1 correspond to PABA modes. The band observed at 1561 cm–1 for GO-1/AuNBs or 1576 cm–1 for GO-5.6/AuNBs and GO-13/AuNBs corresponds to the mixed contributions of PABA and PT.

Further differences between the spectra in Figure 6 involve bands at 1514 cm–1 that are visible clearly for GO-1/AuNBs and GO-5.6/AuNBs, while the band at 1484 cm–1 is visible in the spectrum on GO-13/AuNBs. The mode at 1514 cm–1 corresponds to the PT, while the 1484 cm–1 band involves the PABA motion, suggesting that the PABA part of the FA molecule is directly attached to the GO-1/AuNBs support, while the PT ring is attached to GO-5.6/AuNBs and GO-13/AuNBs. Another indication for such molecular attachment is the 1337 cm–1 band of the PT ring, which is very strong in the spectrum at GO-13/AuNBs, has middle intensity at GO-5.6/AuNBs, and is rather weak at GO-1/AuNBs. The modes of PT at 1183 cm–1 are visible in all three spectra, while the 1170 cm–1 mode of PABA is very intense in the spectrum at GO-1/AuNBs.

In summary, the strongest bands in the FA spectrum of GO-1/AuNBs involve PABA modes. The spectrum of FA on GO-5.6/AuNBs has strong bands of both PABA and PT, while in the spectrum on GO-13/AuNBs, the strongest modes are due to PT motions. Consequently, we suppose that the FA molecule is oriented with the PABA ring toward the GO-1/AuNB surface; on the GO-5.6/AuNBs, it attains random orientation, while in the case of GO-13/AuNBs, the PT ring interacts directly with the support.

Castillo et al.8,57 have shown that FA molecules adsorb primarily through the nitrogen atoms of the pteridine ring on silver or gold nanopillar structures, which were prepared by reactive ion etching, cleaning, and further evaporating the gold or silver layer. We observed a similar orientation of FA on the GO-13/AuNBs, while on GO-5.6/AuNBs and GO-1/AuNBs, the orientation was different. This observation suggests that the presence of the GO layers influences the molecular orientation of the FA molecules. Such influence could be rationalized by the different surface compositions of GO layers, which differ by the content of polar and charged surface groups and by the content of defects in GO.

The molecular orientation with the PABA ring adhering to the support might foster the charge transfer possibility between the adsorbed molecule and the support, as the shape of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of the FA molecule is localized on the PABA part.59 It is difficult to prove the charge transfer by UV–vis spectra of the SERS supports as the possible CT band overlaps with plasmonic absorption. However, the UV–vis spectra of the SERS supports studied here show slight differences upon drop-casting the FA solution on them (see Supporting Information).

The infrared and Raman characterization showed that GO-1/AuNBs contain more nonhexagonal rings compared to GO-5.6/AuNBs and GO-13/AuNBs. Another difference is the presence of epoxides and nondissociated COOH groups. The nonhexagonal and the different surface compositions could thus be factors that influence the molecular orientation of FA on the support. However, the exact mechanism of such influence requires further studies.

The general intensity of the FA SERS spectra on the GO-1/AuNBs support is strongly dependent on the concentration, as illustrated in Figure 7. The characteristic bands of FA can be clearly detected at the concentration of 10–12 M. The Raman intensity versus logarithm values of the analyte concentration plot is linear in the range of 10–12 to 10–8 M. It is the lowest detection limit reported for FA direct SERS assays to our knowledge. Table 3 compares the LOD values reported for the SERS detection of folic acid.

Discussion of the Enhancement Mechanism

The greatest contribution to the SERS enhancement in the three studied supports comes from the plasmon resonance of the AuNBs. Such a conclusion is supported by the observation that the enhancement of Raman spectra on GO-1, GO-5.6 and GO-13 layers without AuNBs is very weak (Figure S8). However, the layers of GOs are important as modifiers of AuNBs. It should also be noted that the shape of AuNBs—with empty cavities and sharp edges, suggests that the electromagnetic field formed upon the plasmonic resonance is strong. So, AuNBs are supposed to give strong electromagnetic enhancement, and generally, it is observed.

Interaction between AuNBs and GOs causes the blue-shift of the maximum plasmonic peak (visible in the UV–vis spectra, Figure S9). Furthermore, AuNBs deposited on GOs form aggregates visible in SEM images of the support—hotspots can be formed between neighboring gold nanoparticles.

The differences between GO-1/AuNBs, GO-5.6/AuNBs, and GO-13/AuNBs are significant, as the detection limits for FA are equal to 10–12 M, 10–8 M, and 10–7 M for GO-1/AuNBs, GO-5.6/AuNBs, and GO-13/AuNBs, respectively. The spectra of FA also show different relative intensities depending on the support, which we attribute to various molecular orientations of FA on the three studied supports. The spectrum of FA on GO-1/AuNBs shows strong peaks corresponding to the PABA part of the FA molecule. We concluded that the FA molecule attains the orientation with the PABA ring oriented toward the surface. The reason why orientation is affected by the pretreatment of GO is correlated with different surface compositions and the various amounts of defects in studied GOs. Such structural differences were indicated by the infrared and Raman spectra of GOs. We suppose that the orientation with the PABA ring close to the surface enables charge transfer between the support and the FA molecule. Such charge transfer could give an extra enhancement above the electromagnetic contribution of AuNBs. Such an extra enhancement could explain the superior intensity of the FA spectrum on GO-1/AuNBs in comparison to FA on GO-5.6/AuNBs and GO-13/AuNBs. The selective enhancement of modes involved in the electronic transition is a typical feature of the resonance Raman spectra. Therefore, the charge transfer mechanism of the enhancement of the spectra is probable.

Conclusions

We propose a simple SERS support for the direct detection of FA, which allows the detection of FA at a concentration equal to 10–12 M. The high sensitivity is reached by depositing the AuNBs on the GO preconditioned in aqueous solution at pH = 1 (GO-1). The GO precondition influences the preferential molecular orientation of the FA adsorbed on the SERS support. Analysis of SERS spectra suggests that the FA molecule is oriented toward the support. We suppose that such orientation is beneficial for the chemical SERS enhancement. Such a simple conditioning procedure opens the possibility of improving the sensitivity of SERS by controlling the molecular orientation of analyte molecules, although the mechanism responsible for the orientation changes requires further studies.
