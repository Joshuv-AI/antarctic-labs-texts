Constructing water-resistant phase-pure alloy carbide for boosting syngas to olefins
Authors: Ling Li, Heng Liang, Yuhan Peng, Linkai Wang, Ruoting Shan, Jida Wang, Xiang-Kui Gu, Yanfei Xu, Mingyue Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFischer–Tropsch synthesis offers a promising route for sustainable olefins production. However, a persistent challenge lies in the presence of water—a natural byproduct that oxidizes the active iron carbide phase and triggers the competing water-gas shift side reaction, ultimately capping olefins yield below 37%. In this study, we propose a strategy to tailor the adsorption and reaction behaviors of water by constructing phase-pure alloy carbide. Substituting cobalt into the Fe5C2 lattice rearranges the adsorption geometry of water molecules on metal carbide, making water more prone to desorb rather than dissociate. This key change endows the catalyst with superior resistance to oxidation by water vapor and low water-gas shift reactivity. Thus, near-complete carbon monoxide conversion (99%) and an unprecedented olefins yield of 47% are achieved under a mild reaction temperature of 280 °C, opening new avenues for efficient olefins synthesis.Subject terms: Heterogeneous catalysis, Chemical engineering, Catalyst synthesis

Introduction

Olefins are important chemical feedstocks in industry and are generally produced from the depleting petroleum resources, necessitating the development of alternative production routes. Since syngas (CO and H2) is easily derived from sustainable biomass, abundant natural gas, and coal, the direct conversion of syngas to olefins represents a highly promising pathway and has garnered considerable attentions1,2. Two main routes for converting syngas to olefins have been developed, both achieving substantial progress in recent years. In 2016, Bao et al.3 proposed the oxide-zeolite (OXZEO) route, achieving 80% selectivity for light olefins (C2–C4 olefins) in hydrocarbons and 17% CO conversion over the ZnCrOx–SAPO-34 catalyst. Recently, they further broke the activity-selectivity trade-off by incorporating germanium-substituted AlPO-18 within the OXZEO system, obtaining 83% selectivity for light olefins in hydrocarbons at a high CO conversion of 85%4.

Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) is another classic route for syngas conversion, which not only enables the synthesis of light olefins5 but also facilitates the production of valuable C5+ olefins6. For example, the Fe/α-Al2O3 catalyst exhibited 53% selectivity for light olefins among hydrocarbons at 80% CO conversion7, while the CoMn catalyst achieved 61% selectivity for light olefins among hydrocarbons at 32% CO conversion8. Additionally, linear α-olefins (LAOs) with carbon numbers ranging from C2 to C10 could be synthesized over the Mn-χ-Fe5C2 catalyst with 55% selectivity in hydrocarbons at a CO conversion of 53%9.

However, the aforementioned olefins selectivity refers exclusively to their content within hydrocarbon products and excludes the CO2 by-product. In reality, during the conversion of syngas, water molecules generated in the FTS reaction easily trigger the water-gas shift (WGS) side reaction (Fig. 1a), resulting in the production of abundant CO2. The enhancement in FTS activity will lead to the formation of more water during reaction, thereby intensifying the WGS side reaction. Thus, low CO2 selectivity can be obtained at relatively low CO conversion levels10–14 (circled region in blue, Fig. 1b), but higher CO conversion ( > 50%) is generally accompanied with high CO2 selectivity of >40%5,7,15–17 (circled region in red, Fig. 1b). This intrinsic coupling between the FTS main reaction and the WGS side reaction generally limits the yield of total hydrocarbons to less than 50% (Supplementary Table 1).

In recent years, two strategies have been developed to suppress the formation of CO2 by-product on the FTS catalysts during syngas conversion. One strategy involves rendering the catalyst surface hydrophobic, which suppresses the WGS side reaction by inhibiting the adsorption of water molecules on catalyst18. For example, in our previous work, the hydrophobic FeMn@Si-c catalyst exhibited a low CO2 selectivity of only 13% at CO conversion of 56%19. However, the hydrophobic layer coated on catalyst impedes the mass transfer during reaction and limits the catalytic activity, resulting in relatively low CO conversion of 50 ~ 60% even at high reaction temperature of 320 °C19–21 (circled region in orange, Fig. 1b). Traditional iron-based FTS catalysts typically consist of a mixture of iron carbides and Fe3O4, where the iron carbides serve as the active phase for the FTS reaction, while Fe3O4 exhibits high activity for the WGS reaction22–24. Thus, another strategy is to synthesize phase-pure iron carbides as a catalyst. For example, the CO2 selectivity over the Mn-χ-Fe5C2 catalyst could be suppressed to 21% at CO conversion of 53%9. Nevertheless, when CO conversion is high and more water molecules will be generated during reaction, the WGS side reaction still occurs inevitably on these phase-pure Fe5C2 or Fe2C catalysts, leading to high CO2 selectivity of >30%9,13,14 (circled region in black, Fig. 1b). Effectively suppressing the formation of CO2 by-product at high CO conversion remains very challenging, which limits the achievable yield of hydrocarbon products. Thus, the olefins yield during syngas conversion is generally below 37% (Supplementary Table 2).

In this work, we show that constructing a phase-pure alloy carbide provides an effective strategy for suppressing water-induced oxidation and boosting olefins production in the FTS reaction. It is found that Co doping rearranges the adsorption geometry of water molecules on the carbide surface, making water preferentially desorb rather than dissociate, which endows the catalyst with superior resistance to steam oxidation and markedly reduced WGS activity. As a result, near-complete CO conversion (99%) and an olefins yield of 47% are achieved, demonstrating that controlling water behavior through alloy carbide formation opens a practical pathway for efficient syngas-to-olefins conversion.

Results

Catalytic performance for syngas to olefins

We present a Fe[Co]Na catalyst composed of a novel (Fe[Co])5C2 phase, where Co atoms are doped into the lattice of Fe5C2 phase (Supplementary Fig. 1). This unique phase possesses much lower intrinsic affinity for water molecules than traditional iron carbides, endowing catalyst with high FTS activity while maintaining low WGS activity (Fig. 1a). At CO conversion of 99%, the CO2 selectivity over Fe[Co]Na could be suppressed to less than 25%, thereby achieving an excellent yield of >70% for hydrocarbons (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, the hydrocarbon products were predominantly high-value olefins (Supplementary Fig. 2). Consequently, this Fe[Co]Na catalyst exhibited the highest yield for olefins at mild reaction temperature of 280 °C (Fig. 1c), in comparison with the state-of-the-art catalysts from the literatures3–5,7–9,11,14,19,25–28. Additionally, it exhibited robust catalytic performance across a broad range of reaction temperatures and space velocities (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Upon the introduction of syngas, this catalyst rapidly reached a steady state within 10 hours and demonstrated good stability during 150 hours of continuous operation (Fig. 1d). All the above catalytic results suggested that the Fe[Co]Na catalyst performed well in the efficient conversion of syngas, achieving high olefin yield while simultaneously reducing CO2 production.

To reveal the underlying reason for the superior catalytic performance of the Fe[Co]Na catalyst, the structure and performance of pure Fe, FeNa, Fe[Co]Na, and pure Co catalysts prepared by the same method were compared. The pure Fe catalyst exhibited very low catalytic activity, with a CO conversion of only 26.1% (Supplementary Table 3). Although the addition of Na slightly suppressed the reduction of iron species (Supplementary Fig. 5), it enhanced the adsorption ability of catalyst for CO (Supplementary Fig. 6), which promoted the formation of the active iron carbides phase (Fig. 2a) and consequently significantly improved the catalytic activity. However, iron carbides were readily oxidized by water molecules generated during reaction, leading to the transformation of iron species into a mixed phase consisting of Fe3O4 and iron carbides (Supplementary Fig. 7). Since Fe3O4 is very active for the WGS reaction, the CO2 formation rate over the FeNa catalyst was high (Fig. 2b). Regarding the pure Co catalyst, it transformed into a mixture of Co2C phase and metallic Co phase during high-temperature reaction. The Co2C phase possesses very high WGS reactivity8,10, also resulting in a high CO2 formation rate over this catalyst. In contrast, the Fe[Co]Na catalyst did not form the Co2C phase, and its iron carbides phase remained stable during 150 hours of continuous reaction (Fig. 2a), endowing this catalyst with much higher formation rate for hydrocarbons (Supplementary Fig. 8) while lower formation rate for CO2 (Fig. 2b). To further compare the catalytic performance within the kinetic regime, we also performed another set of experiments on both Fe[Co]Na catalyst and conventional FeNa catalyst after thoroughly diluting them with inert quartz sand (50 mg of catalyst diluted with 500 mg of quartz sand). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9a, when the space velocity increased from 125,00 to 25,000, then to 50,000 mL·h−1·g−1, the CO conversion decreased from 60.3% to 29.0%, then to 14.4%. The calculated CO consumption rates at these three space velocities were 97.22, 93.51, and 92.87 mmolCO·h−1·gcatalyst−1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9b). With the increase of space velocity, the fluctuation in catalytic activity was less than 5%. Moreover, at high space velocity, the CO conversion was 14.4%, which was below the 15% threshold for differential reactor operation. Thus, the data acquired at a space velocity of 50,000 mL·h−1·g−1 using thoroughly diluted catalyst fall within the kinetic regime and can be used for the comparison of intrinsic catalytic activity. Then, using the same diluted bed configuration and a space velocity of 50,000 mL·h−1·g−1, we further compared the performance of the Fe[Co]Na and FeNa catalysts. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9c, at the same reaction conditions, the Fe[Co]Na catalyst exhibited much higher CO conversion and lower CO2 selectivity than the conventional FeNa catalyst. In addition, the formation rate of hydrocarbons over the Fe[Co]Na catalyst was 1.7 times that over the conventional FeNa catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 9d). These data clearly demonstrated that under kinetically controlled conditions, Co modification intrinsically enhanced the FTS activity and suppressed CO2 formation, which was consistent with the above conclusion. Moreover, it was the unique structure of the Fe[Co]Na catalyst, rather than the bimetallic Fe-Co system, that played the key role in modulating the catalytic performance (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11).

The above results demonstrated that doping Co atoms into the iron-based catalyst significantly impacted the FTS and WGS activities. To gain deeper insights into this difference, we employed in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) to further observe the evolution of surface species on the catalyst during syngas conversion. The doublet bands appearing at 2177 and 2115 cm−1 were attributed to the vibration of gaseous CO on catalyst29. Upon exposure to the syngas atmosphere, several bands at 2848 to 2969 cm−1 emerged on the FeNa catalyst, and their intensity gradually increased (Fig. 2d), which was attributed to the gradual formation of CHx intermediate species on the catalyst surface27. Simultaneously, the band corresponding to gaseous CO230 at 2347 cm−1 also gradually intensified (Supplementary Fig. 12), indicating that water molecules produced during the reaction readily triggered the WGS reaction on the FeNa catalyst. Furthermore, as the reaction progressed and more water accumulated, a broad signal at 3200 to 3600 cm−1 appeared because of the adsorption of the unreacted water on catalyst surface5. In contrast, after exposure to the syngas atmosphere, only prominent bands related to the CHx species were observed on the Fe[Co]Na catalyst, with no detectable signals for water or CO2 (Fig. 2e), suggesting that water molecules generated on this catalyst readily desorbed and were less likely to initiate the WGS reaction to produce CO2.

Structural characterization

To elucidate the reason for the different catalytic behaviors mentioned above, we performed detailed structural characterization on the catalysts. After the reaction, the EDS mapping of Fe[Co]Na catalyst revealed overlapping distribution regions for Fe, Co, C, and Na elements (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14), indicating that all these elements were homogeneously distributed within the spent catalyst. The specific phase composition of iron species in the catalyst was analyzed using Mössbauer spectroscopy (Supplementary Table 4). It was found that the iron species in the FeNa catalyst were converted into a mixture of 41.3% Fe3O4 phase, 45.2% Fe5C2 phase and 13.5% Fe3C phase during reaction (Supplementary Fig. 15), while those in the Fe[Co]Na catalyst were transformed exclusively into the Fe5C2 phase (Fig. 3a), displaying the good stability of metal carbide in the Fe[Co]Na catalyst. Only diffraction peaks associated with Fe5C2 phase were detected in the XRD pattern of spent Fe[Co]Na catalyst, with no characteristic diffraction peak corresponding to Co species observed (Supplementary Fig. 16). Furthermore, the XPS spectra suggested the absence of cobalt oxide (Supplementary Fig. 17). Especially, compared to the standard peaks, the diffraction peaks of Fe5C2 in the spent Fe[Co]Na catalyst shifted to higher angles (Fig. 3b), which implied that Co atoms were substituted into the Fe5C2 lattice, forming an alloy carbide phase and resulting in lattice contraction.

To further confirm the formation of this alloy carbide phase, we analyzed the chemical and coordination environments of Co species using X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS). Compared to Co foil, the absorption edge in the X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) of the spent Fe[Co]Na catalyst slightly shifted to higher energy (Fig. 3c), indicating that the Co species were not present as metallic Co. In the Fourier transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS), the first-shell peaks of Co3O4 and Co foil appeared at approximately 1.5 Å and 2.2 Å, corresponding to Co–O and Co–Co bonds, respectively (Fig. 3d). In contrast, the first-shell peak of the spent Fe[Co]Na catalyst appeared at around 1.3 Å, which was attributed to the Co–C bond31. Additionally, the spent Fe[Co]Na catalyst exhibited a peak shape similar to that of Fe5C2 (Supplementary Fig. 18), further confirming that Co atoms substituted for some Fe atoms in the Fe5C2 structure, forming an alloy carbide. The wavelet transform (WT)-EXAFS also provided insights into the structure of alloy carbide, revealing that the coordination environment of Co in the spent Fe[Co]Na catalyst differed significantly from those in Co3O4 and Co foil (Fig. 3e). The aforementioned characterizations, including Mössbauer spectroscopy, XRD, TEM, and XAFS, collectively demonstrated that Co atoms were substituted into the Fe5C2 lattice in the spent Fe[Co]Na catalyst, forming a new (Fe[Co])5C2 alloy carbide phase.

The stability of metal carbides in the catalyst was studied via temperature-programmed in situ XRD technology. Before test, the iron species in the FeNa and Fe[Co]Na catalysts were transformed into metal carbides via reduction in H2 at 400 °C and carburization in syngas at 320 °C. Subsequently, both catalysts were exposed to the CO + H2O atmosphere from 200 to 480 °C to assess the antioxidant ability of metal carbides on water vapor. For the FeNa catalyst, upon increasing the temperature to 260 °C, the Fe3O4 phase appeared (Supplementary Fig. 19). Moreover, the peak intensity of the Fe3O4 phase enhanced, while that of the Fe5C2 phase weakened during the increase of temperature (Fig. 3f), suggesting that Fe5C2 was gradually oxidized into Fe3O4 by H2O. In contrast, the alloy carbide in the Fe[Co]Na catalyst maintained its structure up to 320 °C (Fig. 3g). Further increasing temperature led to the gradual oxidation of alloy carbide into CoFe2O4 (Supplementary Fig. 20). The transformation temperature of metal carbides into metal oxides increased obviously from 260 °C of Fe5C2 to 340 °C of (Fe[Co])5C2 (Fig. 3h), suggesting that the alloy carbide possessed much higher antioxidant ability than the single iron carbide. Thus, after syngas conversion, the iron species in the FeNa catalyst were converted into a mixture of Fe3O4 and Fe5C2 phases (Fig. 2a), while the (Fe[Co])5C2 phase in the Fe[Co]Na catalyst remained stable under wide reaction conditions (Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22).

Theoretical analysis

Since water molecules play important roles in inducing the phase evolution of the catalyst and causing the formation of CO2 by-product during the syngas conversion process (Supplementary Fig. 23), further insights into water molecules' interaction with Fe5C2 and (Fe[Co])5C2 are highly needed. We investigated the adsorption and dissociation of water molecules via density function theory (DFT) calculations and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using the trained neural network potentials (NNPs). Firstly, NNPs were trained using five different pure and water containing Fe5C2 and (Fe[Co])5C2 structures (Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25), and the suitability of the iteratively trained NNPs for the studied systems was systematically validated (Supplementary Fig. 26). MD simulations revealed that the substitution of Co into the Fe5C2 lattice altered the interfacial water structure, transforming the two-peak water density near the surface to a single peak (Supplementary Fig. 27), indicating changes in water adsorption and dissociation behaviors. To further differentiate the impact of Co doping, we analyzed the radial distribution function (RDF) of the interactions between Fe and Fe/Co, Fe and C, O and H in water, Fe/Co and the O in water, and Fe/Co and the H in water (Supplementary Fig. 28 and Fig. 4a), which revealed that Co substitution strengthened the interaction between the surface of metal carbide and the H atom in water while weakened the interaction with the O atom in water. To further analyze the changes in the orientation of water molecules, we examined the distribution of surface water molecule normal vectors and the surface angle (Fig. 4b). After the substitution of Co into the Fe5C2 lattice, more water molecules presented with angle <0°, indicating the reorientation in configuration from water-up (Fig. 4c) to water-down (Fig. 4d). The above results suggested that the formation of alloy carbide fundamentally altered the interaction mode between water molecules and the metal carbide surface.

To shed light on the adsorption and dissociation behaviors of water molecules on metal carbide, DFT calculations were further performed (Fig. 4e). After the incorporation of Co, the adsorption energy of the water molecule weakened from −0.83 eV to −0.54 eV, and the dissociation energy barrier of the water molecule increased from 0.35 eV to 0.63 eV. As a result, the energy barrier of water desorption was lower than the energy barrier of water dissociation on the surface of (Fe[Co])5C2, while the energy barrier of water desorption was much higher than the energy barrier of water dissociation on the surface of Fe5C2. This suggested that water molecules were more likely to desorb rather than dissociate on the alloy carbide, while water molecules were more likely to dissociate rather than desorb on the single iron carbide. To further investigate the reason for this phenomenon, we analyzed the changes in the intermediate structures. On the surface of Fe5C2, water molecules dissociated directly, and the transition-state intermediate presented the water-up configuration (Supplementary Fig. 29). However, on the surface of (Fe[Co])5C2, the transition-state structure of the directly dissociating water molecule presented the water-down configuration (Supplementary Fig. 30). To correlate these structural differences with bonding characteristics, we calculated the -ICOHP values between Fe atom and the O atom in adsorbed water molecule (Fig. 4f), and those between OH and H in the water molecule at dissociation transition state (Fig. 4g). The -ICOHP values on Fe5C2 were 0.75 eV and 1.72 eV, and both higher than those on (Fe[Co])5C2, which were 0.62 eV and 1.43 eV, respectively. These results suggested that doping Co into the lattice of Fe5C2 weakened the interaction strength between Fe and water molecules, thereby reducing the adsorption energy of water. Furthermore, the decreased bonding strength of the OH–H bond in the water molecule at the dissociation transition state led to a higher transition-state energy, thereby enhancing the overall energy barrier for water dissociation.

Combining the results of MD simulations and DFT calculations, the formation of alloy carbide reoriented the interaction mode between water molecules and the surface of metal carbide, thereby altering the adsorption and dissociation behaviors of water molecules. As a result, water molecules are more likely to desorb rather than dissociate on the alloy carbide, while the opposite is true on the single iron carbide, which is consistent with the in situ DRIFTS spectra (Fig. 2d, e). The different adsorption behaviors of water vapor and water droplets on the FeNa and Fe[Co]Na catalysts also experimentally verified the results of the above theoretical analysis (Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32). Thus, the Fe[Co]Na catalyst possessed much higher antioxidant ability on water vapor (Fig. 3f–h) and a lower formation rate of CO2 during syngas conversion (Fig. 2b) than the traditional FeNa catalyst.

Synergistic effect between cobalt and sodium

Beyond Co doping, the Na promoter also played an important role in the formation and stabilization of the carbide phase. To illustrate the effect of the Na promoter, we prepared FeCoNa catalysts with a fixed Co/Fe ratio but varying Na contents (0.0 ~ 1.2 wt.%). The XRD pattern of the spent FeCo 0.0 wt.% Na catalyst showed clear diffraction peaks of Fe3O4 and residual CoFe alloy, with little alloy carbide detected (Supplementary Fig. 33a), suggesting that without Na, the metal species were predominantly oxidized into metallic oxide under the reaction conditions. At 0.4 wt.% Na, the Fe3O4 peaks disappeared, indicating that the water‑induced oxidation was suppressed. However, small peaks of un‑carburized CoFe alloy remained, showing that the carburization of metal species was still incomplete. At 1.2 wt.% Na, the catalyst was fully converted into pure (Fe[Co])5C2 alloy carbide with no detectable Fe3O4 or CoFe alloy. Therefore, the minimum Na content to prevent oxidation was 0.4 wt.%, whereas complete carburization required 1.2 wt.%. Our main catalyst Fe[Co]Na contains 1.2 wt.% Na, ensuring the pure carbide phase that we believe underpins both high activity and low CO2 selectivity.

During the FTS reaction, iron-based catalysts are simultaneously subjected to two opposing reactions: carburization by CO (forming iron carbides, the FTS active phase) and oxidation by H2O (a byproduct, which converts iron species to Fe3O4). Conventional iron-based catalysts therefore typically end up as a mixture of Fe3O4 and iron carbides. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 33b, increasing Na content significantly enhanced the CO adsorption capacity of the catalyst, which could lower the energy barrier for CO dissociation and promote the carburization process. Hence, we proposed the kinetic role of Co and Na in the Fe[Co]Na catalyst for the formation and stabilization of the carbide phase. In brief, Co mitigated the oxidation process by altering the adsorption and dissociation behavior of water, while Na promoted the carburization process by strengthening CO adsorption and accelerating carbide formation. The synergistic effect between Co and Na shifted the kinetic competition between carburization and oxidation toward the carburization side, thereby enabling the formation of a phase-pure alloy carbide that was resistant to steam oxidation. This synergy explained why the Fe[Co]Na catalyst, in which Na drove the efficient carburization and Co suppressed the water‑induced oxidation, exhibited both high intrinsic activity and low CO2 selectivity, whereas neither Co alone nor Na alone could achieve such a stable pure carbide phase.

Consistent with the phase evolution, the catalytic performance correlated systematically with Na content. As the increase of Na content from 0.0 wt.% to 1.2 wt.%, the hydrocarbons formation rate increased from 29.68 to 33.02 mmolcarbon·h−1·gcatalyst−1, while the CO2 formation rate decreased from 15.50 to 12.90 mmolcarbon·h−1·gcatalyst−1 (Supplementary Fig. 33c). These trends confirmed that stabilizing the pure carbide phase and reducing Fe3O4 phase not only enhanced the hydrocarbons productivity but also suppressed the CO2 formation, which was consistent with the above conclusion.

Regarding hydrocarbons distribution, the Na promoter increased olefins selectivity from 33.6% over the pure Fe catalyst to 72.7% over the FeNa catalyst, while doping Co into the lattice of the Fe5C2 phase decreased olefins selectivity to 69.9% over the Fe[Co]Na catalyst (Fig. 2c). To further understand the effects of Na and Co on olefins selectivity, we conducted the C2H4 pulse hydrogenation experiment. The R value, defined as the ratio of the integrated peak area of C2H4/C2H6, quantified the resistance ability of the catalyst for olefins hydrogenation. The R values for the pure Fe, FeNa, and Fe[Co]Na catalysts were 5.7, 15.8, and 9.5, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 34), indicating that Na suppressed the secondary hydrogenation of olefins, while the doping of Co enhanced olefins hydrogenation. As the Co/Fe molar ratio in the Fe[Co]Na catalyst increased from 0 to 1 (Supplementary Table 5), the olefins selectivity progressively decreased to 59.9% (Supplementary Fig. 35), further demonstrating that excessive incorporation of Co was detrimental to olefins formation. The synergistic cooperation between Co and Na enabled the Fe[Co]Na catalyst to exhibit excellent performance in the syngas-to-olefins conversion.

Discussion

In summary, we proposed a novel strategy to boost olefins production in the FTS reaction by constructing a phase-pure alloy carbide. Doping Co into the lattice of Fe5C2 altered the adsorption configuration of water molecules on the surface of metal carbide, which significantly changed the energy barriers for both water desorption and dissociation. Consequently, on the alloy carbide, water molecules preferentially desorbed rather than dissociated, while on the single iron carbide, dissociation was favored over desorption. This behavior endowed the Fe[Co]Na catalyst with superior antioxidant ability on water vapor and lower WGS reactivity compared to the conventional catalysts, thus achieving a single-pass olefins yield from syngas of up to 47.6%. Considering that many hydrogenation reactions are affected by water molecules, this new approach of constructing water-resistant alloy carbide to modulate the adsorption and reaction behaviors of water can guide the design of more efficient catalysts in the future.

Methods

Catalyst preparation

The chemical materials used for catalyst preparation were purchased from the Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., and all of these chemical materials were used directly without further purification.

Synthesis of Fe[Co]Na

An appropriate amount of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (AR) and Co(NO3)2·6H2O (AR), with a total amount of iron and cobalt of 50 mmol, were dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water. Additionally, 7.95 g of Na2CO3 (AR) was dissolved in another 50 mL of deionized water. Both solutions were simultaneously added to 100 mL of deionized water using a peristaltic pump. The co-precipitation process was sustained at 80 °C with continuous heating and stirring. After aging for 30 min, the precipitate was washed with 200 mL of deionized water, dried at 80 °C for 12 hours, and calcined in air at 450 °C for 4 hours. The Co/Fe molar ratio in the catalyst was 0.5 unless otherwise noted.

Synthesis of FeNa

The FeNa catalyst was prepared by similar procedures to Fe[Co]Na. 20.2 g of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O was dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water. Additionally, 7.95 g of Na2CO3 was dissolved in another 50 mL of deionized water. Both solutions were simultaneously added to 100 mL of deionized water using a peristaltic pump. The co-precipitation process was sustained at 80 °C with continuous heating and stirring. After aging for 30 min, the precipitate was washed with 200 mL of deionized water, dried at 80 °C for 12 hours, and calcined in air at 450 °C for 4 hours.

Synthesis of Fe2O3

The pure Fe catalyst was prepared by a similar procedure to FeNa. 20.2 g of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O was dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water. Additionally, 7.95 g of Na2CO3 was dissolved in another 50 mL of deionized water. Both solutions were simultaneously added to 100 mL of deionized water using a peristaltic pump. The co-precipitation process was sustained at 80 °C with continuous heating and stirring. After aging for 30 min, the precipitate was washed with 200 mL of deionized water for 6 times, dried at 80 °C for 12 hours, and calcined in air at 450 °C for 4 hours.

Synthesis of Co3O4

The pure Co catalyst was prepared by a similar procedure to Fe2O3. 14.6 g of Co(NO3)2·6H2O was dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water. Additionally, 7.95 g of Na2CO3 was dissolved in another 50 mL of deionized water. Both solutions were simultaneously added to 100 mL of deionized water using a peristaltic pump. The co-precipitation process was sustained at 80 °C with continuous heating and stirring. After aging for 30 min, the precipitate was washed with 200 mL of deionized water for 6 times, dried at 80 °C for 12 hours, and calcined in air at 450 °C for 4 hours.

Synthesis of metallic Fe and FeCo alloy

The metallic Fe and FeCo alloy in Fig. 3b were prepared by reducing the relevant catalysts in pure H2 at 400 °C and 0.1 MPa for 10 hours. After reduction, the relevant catalysts were cooled to room temperature in the H2 atmosphere, followed by passivation in the 1%O2/99%N2 gas at room temperature and 0.1 MPa for 30 min. This passivation treatment prevented the spontaneous combustion of the reduced catalyst in air.

Catalyst characterization

The morphology and elemental distribution of the catalyst were characterized by scanning electron microscope (SEM) on a MIRA3 TESCAN microscope equipped with an Aztec Energy X-Max 20 energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector. Transmission electron microscope (TEM) and corresponding EDS elemental mapping images were obtained on a FEI Talos F200x with 200 kV accelerating voltage. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was tested on the Escalab Xi+ instrument.

The adsorption and desorption isotherms of water vapor on the catalyst were tested on a BSD-VVS Vacuum Vapor/Gas Sorption Analyzer. Typically, 120 mg of catalyst sample was pretreated under vacuum at 200 °C for 3 hours. After cooling to 25 °C, the adsorption and desorption tests of water vapor were performed via the vacuum-static-weighing method. Water-droplet contact angle was tested on a JC2000C contact angle goniometer.

Mössbauer spectrum of the catalyst was obtained on wissel Mössbauer spectrometer. X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the catalyst was obtained using an Ultima IV-185 diffractometer with a Cu Kα radiation source. The measurements were conducted over a 2θ range of 5° to 80°, with a scanning step of 0.02° and a scanning rate of 5°/min. The phases of the catalyst were identified based on the JCPDS standard cards. The elemental composition of the catalyst was determined by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) on an Agilent 5110 instrument after the sample had been digested with aqua regia.

In situ XRD patterns of the catalyst in the CO + H2O atmosphere were recorded on an Ultima IV-185 diffractometer equipped with an HT-XRD-900 reactor chamber (Beijing Scistar Technology Co., Ltd.). Before the test, the iron species in the catalyst were transformed into metal carbides via reduction in pure H2 at 400 °C, 0.1 MPa for 10 hours and carburization in syngas at 320 °C, 0.1 MPa for 10 hours. About 100 mg of the powder sample was packed into a ceramic sample stage in the reactor chamber. The 31.6%CO/68.4%N2 gas with a flow rate of 20 mL/min was switched into the reactor chamber, and H2O was introduced into the reactor chamber by passing this gas through a glass wash bottle containing water at 60 °C. The temperature was ramped from room temperature to 480 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min. During the heating process, the XRD patterns of the catalyst with the 2θ range of 29° ~ 79° were collected continuously, and the scanning step and rate were 0.02° and 10°/min, respectively. The temperature was held for 10 min at each point before collecting data.

In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was tested on a NICOLET iS50 FTIR Spectrometer. About 50 mg of powder catalyst uniformly mixed with 100 mg of powder CaF2 was placed into the sample cell. Prior to each measurement, the sample underwent a pretreatment procedure consisting of in situ heating to 320 °C under Ar flow (30 mL/min), then holding at 320 °C for 2 hours, followed by reduction in pure H2 (30 mL/min) at 400 °C for 2 hours. The heating rate was 10 °C/min. Subsequently, after cooling to and holding at 280 °C under Ar flow (30 mL/min), the background spectrum was acquired. The gas was then switched to syngas (H2/CO = 2:1, 30 mL/min), and in situ DRIFTS spectra were collected.

Co K-edge analysis was conducted using Si(111) crystal monochromators at the BL11B beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) in Shanghai, China. Prior to the analysis, samples were compressed into sheets measuring 1 cm in diameter and sealed with Kapton tape. The X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra were acquired at room temperature using a 4-channel Silicon Drift Detector (SDD) Bruker 5040. The Co K-edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra of both the specific sample and standard samples (Co foil and Co3O4) were recorded in transmission mode. Two scans of each specific sample showed negligible changes in both the line shape and peak position of the Co K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra. The resulting spectra were analyzed and processed using the software programs Athena and Artemis.

Carbon monoxide temperature-programmed desorption (CO-TPD) tests were conducted on a Micromeritics AutoChem 2920 instrument. Prior to each test, 100 mg of sample was packed into the quartz tube and reduced in H2 at 400 °C for 2 hours. Then, the sample was cooled to room temperature and purged with Ar for 30 min. Next, saturated adsorption of CO on the sample was achieved by introducing 10%CO/90%Ar into the system for 1 hours. Subsequently, the sample was flushed with Ar for 1 hours to remove the physically adsorbed CO. Finally, the TPD experiment was carried out in a constant flow of Ar (30 mL/min) by increasing the temperature to 800 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The desorbed CO during the heating process was detected by a thermal conductivity detector (TCD).

Pulse transient hydrogenation experiment of C2H4 was performed on a Micromeritics Autochem 2920 instrument. Prior to each test, 100 mg of sample was packed into the quartz tube and reduced in H2 at 400 °C for 2 hours. Then, the reactor was cooled down to 280 °C in an H2 atmosphere. Subsequently, the 5%C2H4/95%Ar gas was pulsed into the system at a flow rate of 50 mL/min to complete the pulse transient hydrogenation. The signals of C2H4 (m/z = 28) and C2H6 (m/z = 30) were detected by a quadrupole mass spectrometer (MS, Hidden, QIC-20) detector. The integrated peak area ratio of C2H4/C2H6 was calculated and denoted as R, which displayed the resistance ability of the catalyst for olefin hydrogenation.

Catalytic test

The syngas conversion reaction was performed in a stainless fixed-bed reactor (inner diameter = 10 mm, length = 40 cm). Typically, 0.5 g of catalyst diluted with 0.5 g of quartz sand was used. Prior to each reaction, the catalyst was reduced in pure H2 at 400 °C and 0.1 MPa for 10 hours. Then, the syngas conversion reaction was conducted at 280 °C, 3.0 MPa, 3600 mL·h−1·g−1 for 30 hours, unless otherwise stated. The H2/CO ratio in syngas was 2:1. The reaction products were detected by two gas chromatographs (FULI GC 97). One gas chromatograph was equipped with a TCD and a flame ionization detector (FID). Porapak Q and 5 A MolSieve packed column were connected to the TCD, while the RB-PLOT Al2O3 capillary column was connected to the FID. The other gas chromatograph was equipped with an RB-5 capillary column and an FID. After reaction, the spent catalyst was transferred into a small glass bottle and then inflated with N2. The bottle was put into a Teflon flask deoxygenated by Fe powder and then vacuumed and filled with N2. The carbon balance calculated was above 95%. The CO conversion and product selectivity were calculated on a carbon-atom basis. The catalytic data at the stable state after 12 hours of reaction were collected and used for comparison.

Computational methods

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations and ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were performed using the Vienna ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP, version 6.3.0)32. In these simulations, the interaction between atomic nuclei and electrons was described using the projector augmented wave (PAW) method33. The exchange-correlation interaction was described using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional34 within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)35. The cutoff energy for the Kohn-Sham orbital expansion was set to 400 eV. During geometry optimization, the convergence criteria were set to a force less than 0.02 eV/Å. The crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) was analyzed by the LOBSTER program36.

In the AIMD simulations, a time step of 1 fs was used, and the simulation time for each structure was 5 ps. During the simulations, the system temperature was maintained at 330 K, which is 30 K higher than room temperature, to approximate the nuclear quantum effects of water at room temperature37. In these simulations, Fe5C2 and (Fe[Co])5C2 were trained using five different structures, as shown in Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25.

All molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using the LAMMPS software package38. The simulations were carried out in the canonical ensemble (NVT), and the Nose-Hoover thermostat was used for temperature control39. The equations of motion were numerically integrated using the Verlet algorithm40, with a time step of 1 fs. Unless otherwise specified, the interlayer distance of Fe5C2 and (Fe[Co])5C2 was set to 1.50 nm.

The training of neural network potentials (NNPs) was carried out using the DeePMD-kit software package41. The embedding network size was set to (25, 50, 100), and the fitting network size was set to (60, 60, 60). The cutoff radius was set to 6 Å, and the smoothing parameter rcut_smth was set to 0.5 Å. The initial learning rate was set to 0.001 and was gradually decayed to 10−8 over 10−6 steps during the deep neural network training process. To accelerate convergence, the weight factors for energy and force in the loss function were adjusted from 0.02 to 1 and from 1000 to 1, respectively, during the optimization process.

To obtain more accurate and robust NNPs, four independent NNP models were trained in parallel. These models were then employed to perform MD simulations on Fe5C2 and (Fe[Co])5C2 systems with different water concentrations (1 g/cm3 and 0.5 g/cm3, respectively), as well as on isolated water molecules. From the generated trajectories, structures exhibiting significant discrepancies in predicted energies among the four models were identified. These high-error configurations were recalculated using DFT, and the resulting energies and forces were added to the training dataset. The NNPs were retrained using the updated dataset, and this iterative procedure continued until the force prediction error across all four models was reduced below 0.02 eV/Å. This active-learning strategy has been successfully employed in our previous work42. The simulation temperature range was from 300 K to 400 K. Based on the iterated potential functions, MD simulation verification was performed for the Fe5C2-water and (Fe[Co])5C2-water systems; the results were shown in Supplementary Fig. 26. For the former, the RMSE and R2 were 0.63 meV/atom and 0.9967, respectively, and for the latter, they were 0.56 meV/atom and 0.9969. This demonstrates that the iteratively trained potential functions can effectively reproduce the relevant energy information in the systems. The scripts for theoretical analysis in this work were provided in Supplementary Code 1.
