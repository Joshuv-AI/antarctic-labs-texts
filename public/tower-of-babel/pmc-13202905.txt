Feeding practices of perinatal Sars-CoV-2-positive parents and their infants: a retrospective review and survey at a large US hospital system
Authors: Heidi Szugye, Jameson A Mitchell, Wei Liu, Anika Kumar, Suet Kam Lam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBirthing parents with confirmed Sars-CoV-2 infection around the time of delivery may differ in their birthing experience and infant feeding decisions. This study aimed to explore characteristics, feeding practices, and experiences of parents who tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 around delivery.MethodsAdult parents at time of delivery who delivered infants (gestational age ≥ 35 weeks) between March 1, 2020, and January 1, 2022, who tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 within seven days prior to or following delivery were identified. Inpatient birthing parent and infant characteristics and feeding choices were retrospectively reviewed from the electronic medical record. Surveys were sent electronically to collect additional information on feeding decisions and experiences.ResultsOf the 72 eligible dyads, 52.8% (n = 38) were exclusively breastfed and 25.0% (n = 18) received human milk or were breastfeeding and formula feeding on day of discharge. Two infants (2.8%) tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 at 24 h of life. Five parents completed a survey reporting challenges of low milk production, lack of breastfeeding support, a need to wear personal protective equipment, birth parent COVID-19 symptoms, conflicting advice from healthcare providers, access to healthcare providers, adequate birth parent nutrition, and formula attainment. Parental feelings of guilt, stress, inadequacy, regret, loneliness, and desperation in relation to feeding choices were also reported.ConclusionSars-CoV-2-positive parents face unique challenges that influence their feeding choices and experiences that healthcare providers should be aware of and ensure appropriate support.Keywords: Sars-CoV-2, COVID-19, Breastfeeding, Human milk, Birthing experience, Infant feeding practices, Breastfeeding support, Maternal COVID-19

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has posed significant challenges to infant-maternal health, particularly in the context of breastfeeding practices and the potential transmission of the virus from parent to child. Prior to the pandemic, guidelines and research emphasized the benefits breastfeeding provides to both parents and infants [1, 2]. However, the pandemic introduced unique challenges to breastfeeding practices, prompting the development of constantly evolving recommendations. These guidelines aimed to balance the benefits of providing human milk—which offers immune protection and other advantages—to infants, against the initially unknown risks of Sars-CoV-2 transmission through direct contact or human milk [3].

Several studies have explored the characteristics of newborns born to Sars-CoV-2-positive parents, shedding light on the incidence (4%) and transmission rates of Sars-CoV-2 in newborn nurseries (2.7% of infants born vaginally and 5.3% born via cesarean delivery) [4, 5]. Furthermore, comprehensive literature reviews have been conducted to provide clinical guidance on breastfeeding during the pandemic, including infection prevention and control measures [6]. Moreover, studies on the detection of Sars-CoV-2 in human milk and the persistence of antibodies in maternal and infant populations following Sars-CoV-2 infection or vaccination highlight the potential immune protection conveyed through breastfeeding and maternal vaccination [7, 8].

The potential ramifications of Sars-CoV-2 positivity on breastfeeding in the postpartum care setting have not been adequately described. There is growing concern about the psychological impact of the pandemic on pregnant people and breastfeeding parents, with studies examining mental health outcomes and social support during this period [9, 10]. If breastfeeding parents who test positive for Sars-CoV-2 experience more breastfeeding challenges and negative experiences, they may be at heightened risk of developing mental health disorders. To our knowledge, at the time this manuscript was written, only five other studies with 15 (US), 25 (UK), 14 (Turkey), 90 (Romania), and 63 (India) participants, respectively, have studied the breastfeeding practices and experiences of parents diagnosed with COVID-19 around the time of delivery [4, 8, 11–14]. Adding to the growing body of research on breastfeeding practices and maternal-infant health during the pandemic, this study aimed to explore the characteristics, feeding practices, and experiences of birthing parents who tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 in a US tertiary care hospital postpartum unit around the time of delivery.

Methods

A retrospective chart review and parent-completed survey were conducted to explore breastfeeding practices and experiences of parents who tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 around the time of delivery. The study was designed retrospectively given the rapidly evolving and unprecedented nature of the pandemic and to avoid influence on feeding practice choices and experiences of the study participants. The study was initially approved by the institution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB) on April 23, 2021, with subsequent amendments made to expand the study size.

This study took place at two large Midwestern US suburban birthing hospitals. Combined, they deliver close to 10,000 live infants a year (about half of all infants born in the greater metropolitan area and one of the hospitals delivering the second largest number per year in the state). Both hospitals have level 1 normal newborn nurseries and level III Neonatal Intensive Care Units (NICUs). Both hospitals have received Baby Friendly Designation from Baby Friendly USA and the International Board of Certified Lactation Consultant (IBCLC) Care Award for their commitment to supporting breastfeeding families. At the time of the study, IIBCLCs were available seven days a week in-hospital during daytime hours, with virtual visits offered to those who were Sars-CoV-2-positive early in the pandemic. At the time of delivery, our patients are asked if they plan to breastfeed or breast milk feed. All patients who intend to provide any breast milk feeding or direct breastfeeding receive one lactation consultant visit in the first 24 h. One-on-one IBCLC appointments and support groups were available to patients on discharge. Feeding type at discharge was determined using electronic medical record (EMR)-documented in-hospital feedings on the calendar day of discharge, reflecting both the infant’s feeding status at discharge and the immediate planned home feeding regimen until follow-up (always scheduled with pediatrician within one to two days of discharge). Based on these records, feeding type at discharge was categorized as: 1) Human milk, defined as exclusive human milk feeding (direct breastfeeding and/or expressed breast milk); 2) Formula, defined as exclusive formula feeding; 3) Both, defined as a combination of human milk and formula feedings.

Infectious precautions were standard across hospitals and evolved as transmission evidence became available. Initially Sars-CoV-2-positive patients, staff, and visitors required no masking but quickly within the first months of the pandemic transitioned from contact/droplet precautions with N95 use for aerosol-generating procedures only, to N95 masking as more evidence became available on transmission of Sars-CoV-2. Visitors were limited to one asymptomatic and Sars-CoV-2 negative visitor at a time during most of the pandemic. All patients were provided an isolette in their room for their infant if the lactating parent was Sars-CoV-2 positive and shared medical decision making based on current transmission evidence was shared. Complete separation in separate rooms was not mandated when the infant was asymptomatic. Parents were given the option to breastfeed their infant directly using contact/droplet precautions or providing pumped breast milk to baby via support person in the room, counseling on known risks, benefits, evidence at that time. Throughout this time period, all parents were tested for Sars-CoV-2 on time of admission to labor and delivery, infants tested at 24 h of life, with repeat infant testing at 14 h of life ordered prior to discharge.

Eighty-eight parents were initially identified via an Electronic Medical Record (EMR) search request who delivered between March 1, 2020, and January 1, 2022, at our healthcare system’s two largest birthing hospitals who tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 within seven days before or after delivery. Data was verified with manual chart review to confirm inclusion criteria were met. This represented about 0.5% of all infants (n = 17,910, approximately) who were born during that period. Although this is a small percentage of overall births, sample size was dependent on Sars-CoV-2 positivity rates at time of birth. Thus, we utilized convenience sampling methods.

Sixteen participants (Fig. 1) were excluded for the following reasons: birthing parent’s preferred language was not English (n = 2), birth parent age < 18 (n = 1), gestational age < 35 weeks at delivery (n = 6), NICU admission (n = 4), or gestational age < 35 weeks and NICU admission (n = 3).

Selection of study participants

The medical charts of all Sars-CoV 2-positive parents and their infants were extracted for demographic characteristics, as well as feeding choices. In addition, a 32-question survey was developed by the authors consisting of multiple choice and free text questions about intended and actual feeding practices and decisions. The survey questions were based on the Center for Disease Control (CDC)’s validated Infant Feeding Survey [15]. A copy of the survey can be found in the Supplemental Materials.

Between April 2021 and March of 2022, a chart review of in-hospital breastfeeding rates and parent/infant dyad characteristics were conducted on those patients who tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 seven days prior or after day of delivery (n = 72) between March 1, 2020, and January 1, 2022. These patients were eligible for participation in an electronic survey about their breastfeeding experience in the hospital and upon discharge. After obtaining consent via an opt-out option from birthing parents’ primary obstetricians (IRB stipulation), birthing parents were sent a letter via MyChart Epic Systems EMR (or postal mail if MyChart was not enabled) in January of 2023 informing them of the study. Birthing parents were then contacted via telephone by a co-investigator to further discuss risks, benefits, and alternatives to participating in the study. If the birthing parent agreed, an email was sent to them to request signed consent for the study using DocuSign®. Consented birthing parents received a link to an anonymous REDCap survey. All data was stored in institutional REDCap.

Continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as frequency and percent. For comparisons of birth parent and infant characteristics by feeding type at discharge, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was used for continuous/ordinal characteristics; the Pearson χ2 test or the Fisher exact test was used for categorical characteristics, as appropriate. For variables in which the overall group comparison was statistically significant at the level of 0.05, pairwise tests were performed among all feeding groups with a Bonferroni multiple comparison adjustment to the significance criteria (α = 0.05/3 = 0.017). SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) was used for all analyses.

Results

Of the 72 eligible dyads, 52.8% (n = 38) were exclusively breastfed and 25.0% (n = 18) received human milk or were breastfeeding and formula feeding on day of discharge. In the study sample, exclusive breastfeeding rates at both hospital nurseries at discharge were 81.3% in 2020 and 76.8% in 2021. Among the remainder, 22.2% (n = 16) were formula fed. The percent of exclusive and mixed breastfeeding at discharge showed no significant change over birth years (2020 vs ≥ 2021: 81.3 vs 76.8%, p > 0.99).

Table 1 includes parental demographic data for all participants overall and by feeding type. The median age of the birth parents was 28 years. Fifty-eight percent of parents self-identified as White, 32.8% Black, 14.9% Hispanic or Latino, and 9.0% American Indian or Alaska Native. Nearly half of the parents (47.1%) reported having Medicaid insurance. Forty-seven percent reported this was their first liveborn infant, 23.6% second, 13.9% third, and 15.3% greater than or equal to fourth. The majority of infants (69.4%) were born via vaginal delivery, followed by 27.8% cesarean delivery, and 2.8% via assisted vaginal delivery. Six parents (8.3%) were diagnosed with prolonged rupture of membranes (≥ 18 h). Nine parents (15%) reported a cough, and the rest reported no COVID-19 symptoms. The median length of birth parent hospitalization was three days. Birth parents who exclusively formula-fed their babies at discharge had a higher prevalence of Medicaid insurance compared to those who exclusively breastfed (68.8 vs 31.4%, p = 0.025). There were no statistically significant differences in other birthing parent factors amongst the feeding type groups.

Table 2 includes infant demographic data for all participants overall and by feeding type at discharge. Twenty-two percent of infants were born in 2020, while 77.8% were born in later years. The median gestational age was 39 weeks, and 51.4% were female-assigned at birth. The median 1-min APGAR was eight, and the 5-min APGAR was nine. The median birth weight was 3.2 kg and the median percent weight loss at discharge was 5.0%. Among all the infants, 2.8% (n = 2) of infants tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 virus and 91.7% (n = 66) tested negative at 24 h of life. No infants tested positive at 14 days of life; however, the majority (79.2%) had no reported results or refused testing. The median number of documented IBCLC visits was higher for those providing exclusive or mixed breastfeeding, as compared to those who exclusively formula fed (2 vs 2 vs 0, p < 0.001). There were no statistically significant differences in other infant factors amongst the feeding type groups.

On average, surveyed birth parents tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 one day before delivery (range of two days). No surveyed birth parents had COVID-19 symptoms at the time of Sars-CoV-2 testing likely due to routine testing being done on admission to labor and delivery regardless of presence of symptoms or not. None of these infants in our sample tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 at seven and 14 days, despite three out of five infants having exposure to an additional household member with Sars-CoV-2 infection.

All survey participants had a feeding plan before delivery: half intended to exclusively breastfeed, and half intended to provide formula and human milk to their infants. All of the participants initiated their feeding plan/intentions. Three infants were not breastfed after discharge. One infant exclusively fed human milk for four weeks and one infant breastfed for 48 weeks with solid introduction at 28 weeks. Two birthing parents chose to stop breastfeeding for personal reasons, one for low supply, and one parent due to inconsistent information about feeding practices and choices.

All survey participants received a lactation consultation visit in the hospital; half were conducted in-person and half via phone due to Sars-CoV-2-positive status. All survey participants cited in-hospital challenges, with half reporting supply issues and half reporting a lack of support and challenges reported by individual participants. Challenges borne by survey participants included: wearing personal protective equipment (PPE), breastfeeding in the setting of own parental COVID-19 symptoms, and receiving inconsistent information about feeding choices and practices from hospital staff. After discharge, half of the survey participants continued to experience low supply. Other post-hospital challenges included: difficulty accessing healthcare providers, parental COVID-19 symptoms, adequate parental nutrition, formula attainment, and inconsistent information. Survey participants also reported feelings of guilt, stress, inadequacy, regret, loneliness, and desperation. Suggestions to improve care included: hospital personnel being more present, ensuring the parent feels seen and heard, and utilizing online support materials.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the feeding plans, choices, and experiences of parents who tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 at the time of delivery. Of the 72 Sars-CoV-2-positive birthing parent/infant dyads reviewed, 77.8% (n= 56) were feeding breast milk (with or without formula) at discharge. This was similar to institution-wide breastfeeding rates at discharge (81.3% in 2020 and 76.8% in 2021), although our institution-wide data did not exclude parents age < 18 years and non-English speaking parents. Other studies have reported breastfeeding rates for Sars-CoV-2-positive parents of about half their matched Sars-CoV-2 negative counterparts [4, 16].

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) first provided guidance on the management of infants born to parents with suspected or confirmed Sars-CoV-2 positivity on April 2, 2020, advising separation given unknown transmission modality [17]. This was subsequently updated in November 2022 based on research showing that only 2–3% of infants contracted COVID-19 when exposed around the time of delivery with no attributable infant deaths [14, 15]. Furthermore, Rostomian, et al. found that infant separation from their parents in the hospital setting led to shorter durations of any human milk feeding [18]. Similarly, in our study, only two infants (2.8%) tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 at 24 h of life, though this is limited by the number of infants who obtained testing at the appropriate times. There was also no statistically significant difference amongst those who tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 and feeding choice. Although these studies have small sample sizes, the lack of transmission in all the participants reflects the low transmission rate reported in literature [7, 8]. Interestingly, all survey participants’ infants were born between November 10, 2021, and December 30, 2021, about two years into the start of the pandemic when restrictions were less strict, and parents still reported pandemic-specific effects on feeding experiences.

A large survey completed by Brown, et al. found that more than 70% of breastfeeding parents stopped breastfeeding because of a lack of support during the pandemic [11]. Only 13% of those surveyed reported feeling ready to do so [11]. The most common reasons cited for early cessation were insufficient professional support, latch difficulties, exhaustion, insufficient milk, and pain [11]. Twenty-five (2.0%) of these study participants endorsed symptoms of COVID‐19, of which 16.0% were told they needed to separate themselves from their baby [11]. Warner, et al.’s June 2022 literature review of 5,123 newborns born to Sars-CoV-2-positive parents across the globe revealed 70% chose to breastfeed despite Sars-CoV-2 positivity [19]. Our data showed all surveyed birthing parents (n = 4) planned to provide human milk to their infant prior to delivery, and 75% were giving human milk at time of discharge.

Our survey participants’ reporting of stress is comparable to the study conducted by Mayopolous, et al., who reported an increased acute stress response in Sars-CoV-2-exposed communities, compared to matched pre-pandemic populations [20, 21]. This increased stress correlated with more childbirth-related posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), less bonding with infant, and more breastfeeding challenges. In addition, the incidence of PTSD was found to be twice as high in Sars-CoV-2-positive parents postpartum [20, 21]. This population was also more likely to have visitor restrictions and separation of infant and parent [20, 21]. Matyas, et al. also found that parents who described a negative hospital experience were 2.4 times more likely to not continue breastfeeding [13]. Overlapping experiential themes included feelings of guilt, stress, anxiety, and isolation. These feelings may further exacerbate depression and anxiety symptoms reported in postpartum parents since the Sars-CoV-2 outbreak [22, 23].

Overlapping challenges expressed in the Asci survey study and our study include: having to wear PPE, such as masks, while breastfeeding/trying to bond with one’s baby; trying to breastfeed in the setting of birth parent COVID-19 symptoms; and conflicting advice and information given by healthcare providers [12]. Unique to our study, compared to the Asci study, were reported challenges with supply, healthcare provider access difficulties, adequate birth parent nutrition, and formula attainment. More specifically, in late 2021 the FDA received reports of infants who had consumed formula made at a specific plant who later became ill with Cronobacter sakazakii or Salmonella, prompting further investigation, recalls, and ultimately a formula shortage by early 2022.

There are several limitations in this retrospective chart review and survey study. The biggest limitation of our study was the low survey response rate (7%), which can lead to self-selection and non-respondent bias as well as reduced generalizability We suspect the low response rate was due to the complex, multi-step consent process required by our institution’s IRB to obtain consent from both the obstetric clinician and multiple steps in obtaining patient consent electronically. In addition, all survey participants identified as White/Caucasian and had private insurance coverage, which could symbolize a difference in access to internet and phones. This is especially problematic, given that racial and ethnic minorities report more barriers to breastfeeding than white patients [24]. Ideally all key subgroups would be represented so further subgroup analyses could be completed. In addition, given the time that had passed since their early breastfeeding experiences, recall bias could contribute to the respondents’ answers. Lastly, post-hospital lactation care was not measured and infant testing for Sars-CoV-2 was low.

Our findings add to the growing body of evidence that the pandemic may have negatively impacted the breastfeeding experiences of families and potential solutions for the future. Specifically, our results illuminate the perspectives of birthing parents who tested positive for Sars-CoV-2 at the time of delivery regarding their breastfeeding plans and ultimate practices. Clinicians and healthcare workers should be aware that this patient population may be at increased risk for breastfeeding challenges and difficult emotional experiences during the postpartum period. Caregivers should ensure frequent in-person breastfeeding support, with careful attention to birth parent symptomatic care. In addition, caregivers should work to develop standardized policies and procedures with corresponding patient handouts to ensure consistent and up-to-date information is provided to the patient.

Lastly, this population may be at risk for negative emotions surrounding the breastfeeding experience, which could potentially lead to earlier breastfeeding cessation, increased postpartum mood disorders, and a negative influence on future breastfeeding experiences. Offering ongoing psychological support may be beneficial.

Conclusion

Sars-CoV-2-positive parents face unique challenges that influence their feeding choices and experiences. Healthcare providers should be aware of this and ensure appropriate and tailored communication and support. Future research is warranted to understand how the pandemic’s impact on feeding choices and experiences may influence future postpartum feeding decisions, patient experiences, and breastfeeding outcomes, especially with the development of standardized care practices.
