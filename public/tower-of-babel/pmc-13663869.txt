Nursing Process Competency Among Nursing Interns and Its Associated Psychological, Cognitive, and Environmental/Social Factors: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Xinxue Xi, Jing Le, Chenxi Zhou, Keke Zhang, Tieying Zeng, Ying Wang, Jing Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNursing process competency is essential for clinical reasoning and the delivery of systematic and individualised nursing care. However, evidence remains limited regarding the level of this competency among nursing interns and the psychological, cognitive, and environmental and social factors associated with it.Aim(s)Guided by the Clinical Learning Environment Model, this study aimed to describe the level of nursing process competency among nursing interns and identify the psychological, cognitive, environmental and social factors associated with this competency.MethodsA single‐centre, hospital‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted in a tertiary teaching hospital in China. A self‐selected non‐probability sample of 348 final‐year nursing interns completed a structured online survey. The survey included the Competency of Nursing Process Questionnaire, the Belongingness Scale–Clinical Placement Experience, the Academic Support in the Practicum Scale, and investigator‐developed indicators of cognitive and skill‐related and environmental and social factors. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, bivariate analyses, and multiple linear regression, with all prespecified variables entered simultaneously into the regression model.ResultsThe mean nursing process competency score was 81.35 (SD = 12.22; observed range: 26–105), with diagnosis and planning having the lowest item‐level mean scores. The fully adjusted regression model was statistically significant, F(27, 320) = 30.51, p < 0.001, and explained 72.0% of the variance in nursing process competency (adjusted R2 = 0.697). Clinical belongingness showed the strongest positive mutually adjusted association with competency (β = 0.732, p < 0.001; semi‐partial R2 = 0.2742), followed by self‐rated theoretical nursing knowledge (β = 0.137, p = 0.009; semi‐partial R2 = 0.0061). Perceived support from physicians showed a smaller positive association in the primary model (β = 0.115, p = 0.048; semi‐partial R2 = 0.0034), but this association was not statistically significant in the sensitivity analysis (p = 0.058) and was therefore interpreted as exploratory.ConclusionClinical belongingness was the factor most strongly associated with nursing process competency, highlighting the potential relevance of interns' sense of acceptance and connection within the clinical learning environment. Self‐rated theoretical nursing knowledge was also associated with competency after mutual adjustment. The smaller association involving perceived support from physicians was not robust to sensitivity analysis and requires further investigation. Given the cross‐sectional design, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships.Implications for Nursing PractiseThe findings suggest that nurse educators, preceptors, and clinical managers should consider structured orientation, inclusive team communication, continuity of preceptorship, and regular feedback to foster interns' clinical belongingness, alongside supervised opportunities to apply theoretical knowledge during assessment, diagnosis, and care planning. These strategies should be prospectively evaluated before their effectiveness is assumed.Reporting MethodThis study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement and its checklist for cross‐sectional studies.No Patient or Public ContributionNursing interns participated only as survey respondents and were not involved in the study design, conduct, data analysis, interpretation, reporting, or dissemination.Keywords: clinical belongingness, clinical learning environment, cross‐sectional study, nursing education, nursing interns, nursing process competency, theoretical nursing knowledge

Introduction

The clinical practicum stands as a cornerstone in nursing education, establishing a connection between theoretical knowledge and practical implementation while promoting the professional socialisation of nursing interns (Chen et al. 2020). Throughout this phase, the cultivation of core clinical competencies directly impacts patient safety and the quality of care rendered (Xu et al. 2025). Among these competencies, the proficiency in effectively applying the nursing process, which includes assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation, holds a central position (Shahzeydi et al. 2024). This systematic methodology is essential for clinical reasoning and guarantees the provision of scientific, individualised patient care, thereby serving as a critical indicator of educational achievements and preparedness for professional practise (Leal et al. 2024).

Despite its acknowledged significance, nursing interns often face difficulties in consistently implementing the nursing process, particularly in intricate clinical scenarios that necessitate subtle assessment and evaluation (Shahzeydi et al. 2024). This inconsistency in performance implies that the determinants of competency transcend mere theoretical knowledge acquisition (Chen et al. 2020). Previous research has conventionally explored this issue from disparate perspectives: a cognitive approach concentrating on knowledge retention and critical thinking (Zlamal et al. 2023); a psychological perspective scrutinising self‐efficacy and anxiety (Yu et al. 2021); and an environmental approach investigating instructor support and clinical resources (Thomsen et al. 2024). Nevertheless, this fragmented perspective limits a comprehensive understanding of nursing process competency because psychological, cognitive, and environmental/social factors are rarely examined simultaneously within the clinical learning context.

Background

Evidence remains limited regarding the overall level of nursing process competency among nursing interns and the psychological, cognitive, and environmental/social factors associated with this competency. To provide a coherent basis for examining these factors, the Clinical Learning Environment Model may offer a useful organising framework (Flott and Linden 2016; Yu et al. 2021). Consistent with situated learning theory, this framework views professional development as occurring through participation in clinical practise and interaction within a community of practise, including ‘legitimate peripheral participation’ (Lave and Wenger 1991). From this perspective, competence develops within a clinical learning context involving affective belonging (Singer et al. 2022), supportive supervision and team practise (Thomsen et al. 2024), and learners' knowledge integration and self‐regulation (Seo and Eom 2021). This perspective supports the simultaneous consideration of learner‐related and contextual factors associated with nursing process competency. For example, a pilot study of a novel clinical practise model highlighted the importance of supporting students' well‐being, strengthening their sense of belonging, and improving their relationships with healthcare staff during the transition from university to the workplace (Thomsen et al. 2024).

Drawing on the Clinical Learning Environment Model and previous literature (Flott and Linden 2016; Wille et al. 2025), the potential factors associated with nursing process competency were organised into three conceptual domains for the present study: the psychological domain, conceptualised as the Perceptual and Belief System; the cognitive domain, conceptualised as the Individual Knowledge Reserve; and the environmental/social domain, conceptualised as the Context of Interaction (Figure 1). These domains were used to structure the selection and analysis of variables rather than to specify causal pathways among them.

Conceptual framework of factors associated with nursing process competency among nursing interns. Psychological, cognitive, and skill‐related, and environmental/social factors were examined as potential correlates of nursing process competency. Sociodemographic and health‐related characteristics were included as covariates. The connections represent statistical associations and do not imply causal relationships.

The psychological domain (Perceptual and Belief System) encompasses learners' emotional, attitudinal, belief‐related, and motivational states (Yu et al. 2021). Clinical belongingness refers to an individual's emotional connection to and sense of acceptance within the clinical practise community and represents an important psychological experience during clinical learning (Singer et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2025). Self‐efficacy in applying the nursing process reflects learners' confidence in their ability to complete nursing process activities successfully (Yu et al. 2021). Together, these factors reflect how nursing interns perceive themselves and their place within the clinical learning context.

The cognitive domain (Individual Knowledge Reserve) concerns the knowledge and learner‐level capabilities required to understand clinical situations, make judgements, and apply the nursing process (da Silva et al. 2023). It includes theoretical nursing knowledge, referring to the mastery of fundamental and specialised nursing concepts (Chen et al. 2020), and nursing skill proficiency, which supports the application of theoretical knowledge in clinical practise (Abd elrahman et al. 2021). Critical thinking, involving analysis, evaluation, and judgement, is essential for clinical reasoning and informed decision‐making (Zlamal et al. 2023). Knowledge related to the five stages of the nursing process—assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation—is also directly relevant to nursing process competency (Shahzeydi et al. 2024). Interpersonal communication skills have both learner‐related and contextual components; in the present study, they were considered a learner‐level capability because they support information gathering, clinical judgement, and communication with patients and healthcare professionals (Leal et al. 2024).

The environmental/social domain (Context of Interaction) comprises contextual factors related to the clinical setting, social interactions, organisational culture, learning resources, and support from others (Hammad et al. 2024; Thomsen et al. 2024). Perceived academic support reflects students' perceptions of the support provided by educational institutions, clinical placement institutions, and instructors. Although this construct is assessed through students' perceptions, its referents are external sources of support; it was therefore included in the environmental/social domain (Zhu et al. 2025). Preceptor teaching style represents instructional behaviours and interaction patterns within the clinical learning environment (Singer et al. 2024). Support from clinical nurses reflects the guidance, acceptance, and assistance provided to interns when they apply the nursing process (Prathibha et al. 2024), whereas support from head nurses includes encouragement, authorisation, and access to organisational resources (Li et al. 2023). Support from physicians reflects the attitudes and cooperativeness of interprofessional team members (Leal et al. 2024). Trust and cooperation from patients and their families constitute additional contextual conditions under which interns implement the nursing process (Martínez‐Linares et al. 2019). Perceived collaboration among nurses reflects the social and collaborative characteristics of the clinical workplace (Leal et al. 2024). Formal learning opportunities, including participation in and organisation of holistic nursing ward rounds, represent structured learning resources provided by clinical institutions (Wang et al. 2025).

In summary, the Clinical Learning Environment Model was used as an integrated framework to organise the simultaneous examination of psychological, cognitive, and environmental/social factors related to nursing process competency. Clarifying these factors may help nurse educators and clinical managers identify priorities for strengthening clinical learning support and improving nursing process education. Accordingly, this study aimed to describe the overall and stage‐specific levels of nursing process competency among nursing interns and identify the psychological, cognitive, and environmental/social factors associated with this competency. To address these objectives, the following research questions were formulated:

RQ1. What are the overall and stage‐specific levels of nursing process competency among nursing interns, including assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation?

RQ2. Which psychological, cognitive, and environmental/social factors are associated with nursing process competency after adjustment for relevant sociodemographic and health‐related characteristics?

Methods

Study Setting and Design

A single‐centre, hospital‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted to describe the level of nursing process competency among nursing interns and examine the factors associated with this competency. The conceptual framework shown in Figure 1 positioned nursing process competency as the study outcome. Potential associated factors were organised into three analytical categories corresponding to the conceptual domains: (a) psychological factors, including clinical belongingness and self‐rated confidence in applying the nursing process; (b) cognitive and skill‐related factors, including self‐rated theoretical nursing knowledge, self‐rated nursing skill proficiency, self‐rated knowledge of the nursing process, self‐rated critical thinking ability, and self‐rated interpersonal communication skills; and (c) environmental and social factors, including perceived academic support, preceptor teaching style, perceived support from clinical nurses, head nurses, and physicians, perceived trust and cooperation from patients and their families, perceived collaboration among nurses, and formal learning opportunities, represented by participation in and organisation of holistic nursing ward rounds. Sociodemographic and health‐related characteristics, including age, gender, self‐rated physical and mental health status, and educational level, were treated as covariates. This framework enabled the simultaneous examination of learner‐related and contextual factors associated with nursing process competency and did not specify causal pathways among these factors.

The study was conducted at a large tertiary teaching hospital in Wuhan, China, which serves as a national medical centre. The hospital has more than 6000 inpatient beds and approximately 4500 nurses, of whom approximately half are certified by the hospital as clinical preceptors. Each year, the hospital receives approximately 350–400 nursing interns from 15 universities. During their clinical placements, each intern is assigned to a designated clinical preceptor.

Participants and Sampling

A non‐probability, single‐centre sampling approach with a census invitation at the study site was used. All nursing interns undertaking clinical placements at the study hospital during the data collection period were screened for eligibility and invited to participate if eligible. Because participation was voluntary, the final respondent sample was self‐selected. The eligibility criteria were: (a) being final‐year nursing interns; (b) having completed at least 4 months of clinical practicum at the study hospital; and (c) having no prior formal employment in healthcare. Eligible interns voluntarily decided whether to participate and provided informed consent before completing the survey.

An a priori power analysis was conducted to estimate the minimum number of completed cases required for the planned multiple linear regression analysis, rather than to establish the population representativeness of the sample. Using G*Power 3.1, a fixed‐model multiple regression analysis testing the deviation of R2 from zero was specified with 27 coefficient parameters, reflecting the 21 prespecified explanatory variables after dummy coding of multicategory variables, α = 0.05, a statistical power of 0.95, and a medium effect size of f2 = 0.15. The minimum required analytical sample was 249 participants. To allow for an anticipated 20% of non‐response or incomplete questionnaires, the target number of completed responses was increased to 312 (249/[1–0.20]). All 392 eligible interns were invited, and 348 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 88.8%. The final sample exceeded the minimum analytical requirement. However, the power calculation addressed the adequacy of the sample for the planned regression analysis and did not eliminate the potential for self‐selection bias or confer population representativeness on the non‐probability sample.

Measurements

Data were collected using validated Chinese‐language instruments and a set of investigator‐developed single‐item ratings. The validated instruments were used to assess nursing process competency, clinical belongingness, and perceived academic support. The remaining factors within the psychological, cognitive, and skill‐related, and environmental and social categories were assessed as individual self‐reported indicators rather than as validated multi‐item scales.

Sociodemographic and Health‐Related Characteristics

An investigator‐developed questionnaire was used to collect participants' age, gender, self‐rated physical health, self‐rated mental health, and educational level. The response categories for these variables are provided in Table S1. These variables were treated as participant characteristics and potential covariates rather than as components of a psychometric scale.

Psychological Factors

Psychological factors included clinical belongingness and self‐rated confidence in applying the nursing process.

Clinical belongingness was assessed using the Chinese version of the Belongingness Scale–Clinical Placement Experience (BES‐CPE), culturally adapted and validated by Wang and Jin (2020). The Chinese version contains 26 items. Twenty‐five items are assigned to three subscales: Esteem (11 items), Connectedness (6 items), and Efficacy (8 items); one additional item is retained only in the calculation of the total score. Each item is rated on a five‐point Likert scale, with negatively worded items reverse‐scored. Total scores range from 26 to 130, with higher scores indicating a stronger sense of belongingness during clinical placement. In the Chinese validation study, Cronbach's α was 0.889 for the total scale and ranged from 0.751 to 0.856 for the three subscales. In the present study, Cronbach's α was 0.942 for the total Chinese BES‐CPE and 0.960, 0.599, and 0.792 for the Esteem, Connectedness, and Efficacy subscales, respectively.

Bandura conceptualised self‐efficacy as an individual's belief in their capability to perform the actions required to achieve a specified outcome and recommended that self‐efficacy be assessed in relation to clearly defined, domain‐specific tasks (Bandura 2006). Guided by this task‐specific approach and by the 0–10 confidence‐rating format used in previous nursing self‐efficacy research (Kivilahti et al. 2024), confidence in applying the nursing process in the present study was assessed using a single investigator‐developed item: ‘On a scale from 0 to 10, how confident are you that you can effectively apply the nursing process when providing patient care?’ Responses ranged from 0 (‘not at all confident’) to 10 (‘completely confident’), with higher scores indicating greater self‐rated confidence. Because this single item had not undergone formal psychometric validation, it was interpreted as self‐rated confidence in applying the nursing process rather than as a validated measure of self‐efficacy; internal‐consistency reliability was therefore not applicable.

Cognitive and Skill‐Related Factors

Five cognitive and skill‐related constructs were assessed separately using investigator‐developed single‐item numerical ratings: self‐rated theoretical nursing knowledge, self‐rated nursing skill proficiency, self‐rated knowledge of the nursing process, self‐rated critical thinking ability, and self‐rated interpersonal communication skills. Responses were recorded on 0–10 scales, with higher scores indicating higher self‐perceived levels of the corresponding attributes. The exact wording and response anchors for each indicator are provided in Table S1.

The selection of these constructs and the content of the items were informed by the study's conceptual framework and relevant literature (Alkhelaiwi et al. 2024; Immonen et al. 2019). Because each indicator represented a narrowly defined construct and was analysed separately, the items were not combined to form a composite cognitive and skill‐related scale, and no total score was calculated. These investigator‐developed single‐item ratings had not undergone formal psychometric validation and were therefore treated as exploratory self‐reported indicators. Internal‐consistency reliability was not applicable to single‐item measures.

Environmental and Social Factors

Perceived academic support was classified as an environmental and social factor because it represents interns' perceptions of support provided by educational institutions, clinical placement institutions, preceptors, and clinical facilitators. It was measured using the Chinese version of the Academic Support in the Practicum Scale, originally developed by Arribas‐Marín et al. (2017) and culturally adapted and validated by Li et al. (2018). The Chinese version contains 23 items across four dimensions: peer support (6 items), academic institution support (6 items), preceptor support (5 items), and clinical facilitator support (6 items). In the Chinese validation study, the scale demonstrated satisfactory construct validity and internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α of 0.958 for the total scale. In the present study, the items were rated using an 11‐point scale (ranging from 0 to 10), with higher scores indicating greater perceived academic support. For the Academic Support in the Practicum Scale, Cronbach's α was 0.982 for the total scale and 0.958, 0.942, 0.961, and 0.962 for the Peer Support, Preceptor Support, Academic Institution Support, and Clinical Facilitator Support subscales, respectively.

The remaining environmental and social factors were assessed using investigator‐developed indicators. A single‐item format was used for narrowly defined aspects of the clinical learning environment, consistent with methodological guidance on single‐item measures (Bowling 2005). Preceptor teaching style was recorded using four categories: supportive and mentoring, rigorous and authoritative, humorous and energetic, and inquiry‐based and heuristic. Perceived support from clinical nurses, head nurses, and physicians; perceived trust and cooperation from patients and their families; and perceived collaboration among nurses were each rated separately on a 0–10 scale using construct‐appropriate anchors. Exact item wording and response anchors are provided in Table S1.

Participation in and organisation of holistic nursing ward rounds were recorded as separate cumulative counts from the beginning of clinical placement to survey completion. No composite score was calculated. As these investigator‐developed indicators were assessed separately and had not undergone formal psychometric validation, they were treated as exploratory self‐reported measures, and internal‐consistency reliability was not applicable.

The Chinese Version of the Competency of Nursing Process Questionnaire (C‐ CNPQ )

The CNPQ, developed by Koy et al. (2023) and translated into Chinese by Wang et al. (2024), was used to assess nursing process competency among nursing interns. This 21‐item scale assesses competency across five stages: Assessment (4 items), Diagnosis (5 items), Planning (5 items), Intervention (corresponding to the implementation stage; 4 items), and Evaluation (3 items). Responses range from 1 = ‘inability to perform’ to 5 = ‘ability to perform independently’, with total scores ranging from 21 to 105; higher scores indicate greater nursing process competency. The scale exhibits robust psychometric characteristics (Cronbach's α = 0.978; subscale α = 0.898–0.956; test–retest reliability = 0.783), and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) verified its five‐factor structure (χ2/df = 2.05, CFI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.04). In this study, the overall Cronbach's α was 0.986 (subscales: α = 0.938–0.951).

Data Collection

Data were collected between January and March 2025 after ethical approval had been obtained from the institutional review board of the study hospital. The questionnaire was administered through Questionnaire Star (Wenjuanxing; https://www.wjx.cn/), a widely used online survey platform in China, which generated a survey link and QR code. Before formal data collection, three nursing interns pilot‐tested the online questionnaire to assess the clarity of the instructions and investigator‐developed items, the sequence and display of questions, the functionality of the link and QR code, and the time required for completion. Their feedback was used to refine the instructions, item sequence, and wording of the investigator‐developed questions without changing the content or scoring of the validated instruments. The three interns who participated in the pilot testing were not included in the final analytical sample. The final survey required approximately 10 min to complete.

Nursing managers and clinical preceptors were informed about the study and assisted in distributing the recruitment notice to eligible interns. The notice contained the participant information sheet, survey link, and QR code. Their role was limited to distributing the recruitment information, and participation was voluntary. Potential participants accessed the survey by clicking the link or scanning the QR code. The first page presented the electronic informed consent form, and only those who selected ‘I agree’ were permitted to proceed. Participants were informed of the study purpose, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality protections, and their right to discontinue the survey before submission.

All questionnaire items were set as required fields to prevent item‐level missing data in submitted questionnaires. The survey was configured not to collect names, student numbers, or other direct personal identifiers. Of the 392 eligible interns invited, 348 submitted complete questionnaires and were included in the analysis, yielding a response rate of 88.8%.

Data Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0. All tests were two‐tailed, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were checked for out‐of‐range values, coding errors, missing data, and potentially influential observations before analysis. No missing data were identified among the 348 included questionnaires; therefore, no imputation procedures were required or performed, and all 348 respondents were included in every analysis. Internal consistency was evaluated for the multi‐item scales using Cronbach's α. Internal‐consistency reliability was not applicable to the investigator‐developed single‐item indicators.

Continuous variables were summarised using means and standard deviations (SDs) or medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate, whereas categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages. The distributions of continuous variables were examined using graphical methods. Total and subscale scores for nursing process competency were calculated according to the scoring instructions. Because the five subscales contained different numbers of items, item‐level mean scores were also calculated to facilitate descriptive comparisons across the assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation stages.

The investigator‐developed 0–10 numerical rating indicators were analysed using their original scores. Although these single‐item ratings were ordinal at the measurement level, they were treated as approximately continuous variables because they comprised 11 ordered response options and represented numerical assessments of narrowly defined constructs (Harpe 2015). The original scores were retained to avoid the loss of information associated with categorisation.

Bivariate analyses were conducted to examine associations between the explanatory variables and the total nursing process competency score. Pearson's correlation was used for approximately normally distributed continuous scale scores with linear relationships, whereas Spearman's rank correlation was used for the 0–10 numerical rating indicators, count variables, and continuous variables with skewed distributions or marked ceiling effects. Differences in competency scores across categorical variables were examined using independent‐samples t‐tests or one‐way analysis of variance, with Welch's correction applied when the homogeneity‐of‐variance assumption was not met.

A multiple linear regression model was fitted with the total nursing process competency score as the outcome variable. Variables prespecified on the basis of the Clinical Learning Environment Model and previous literature were entered simultaneously using the enter method, rather than being selected solely according to their statistical significance in the bivariate analyses. The original 0–10 scores were entered as approximately continuous explanatory variables. Age was retained as a continuous variable, whereas nominal and ordinal categorical variables were dummy‐coded using prespecified reference categories. The cumulative numbers of holistic nursing ward rounds in which participants had participated and which they had organised were entered using their original counts when their relationships with the outcome were approximately linear; alternative transformations were considered in sensitivity analyses when marked skewness or non‐linearity was observed.

Regression results were reported as unstandardized coefficients (B), standard errors, standardised coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, and p‐values. Overall model performance was evaluated using the F statistic, R2, and adjusted R2. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with a VIF > 10 indicating severe multicollinearity (Kim 2019). Linearity and homoscedasticity were examined using plots of standardised residuals against standardised predicted values; residual normality was assessed using Q–Q plots; independence was evaluated using the Durbin–Watson statistic; and potentially influential observations were examined using Cook's distance.

Bivariate Associations With Nursing Process Competency

Among the categorical characteristics, nursing process competency differed significantly according to self‐rated physical health, F(2, 345) = 11.46, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.062, and self‐rated mental health, F(2, 345) = 9.75, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.053. Tukey‐adjusted post hoc comparisons indicated that interns reporting healthy physical or mental health had higher competency scores than those reporting fair health. No other pairwise comparisons among the physical‐ or mental‐health categories were statistically significant. No significant differences were observed according to gender, educational level, or preceptor teaching style (Table 3).

Factors Associated With Nursing Process Competency After Mutual Adjustment

All explanatory variables specified a priori on the basis of the Clinical Learning Environment Model and previous literature, together with sociodemographic and health‐related covariates, were entered simultaneously into a multiple linear regression model using the enter method to estimate their mutually adjusted associations with the total nursing process competency score. The fully adjusted multiple linear regression model was statistically significant, F(27, 320) = 30.51, p < 0.001, and explained 72.0% of the variance in nursing process competency (R2 = 0.720; adjusted R2 = 0.697).

Inspection of the standardised residual‐versus‐predicted‐value plot and Q–Q plot did not indicate severe violations of linearity, homoscedasticity, or residual normality. The Durbin–Watson statistic was 1.99, the maximum VIF was 3.83, the minimum tolerance was 0.261, and the maximum Cook's distance was 0.096, indicating no severe multicollinearity, residual dependence, or highly influential observations.

Clinical belongingness showed the strongest positive mutually adjusted association with nursing process competency (B = 0.673, 95% CI [0.598, 0.748], β = 0.732, p < 0.001; semi‐partial R2 = 0.2742), uniquely accounting for 27.42% of the outcome variance. Self‐rated theoretical nursing knowledge was also positively associated with competency (B = 0.987, 95% CI [0.250, 1.725], β = 0.137, p = 0.009; semi‐partial R2 = 0.0061), uniquely accounting for 0.61% of the outcome variance. Perceived support from physicians showed a smaller, borderline positive association (B = 0.738, 95% CI [0.007, 1.469], β = 0.115, p = 0.048; semi‐partial R2 = 0.0034), uniquely accounting for 0.34% of the outcome variance. None of the remaining variables were associated with competency after mutual adjustment in the fully adjusted model (Table 5).

Discussion

This study described nursing process competency among nursing interns and examined its associations with psychological, cognitive, and environmental/social factors within the Clinical Learning Environment Model. Overall, the interns reported relatively high nursing process competency, although item‐level mean scores were slightly lower for the diagnosis and planning stages than for the other stages. In the fully adjusted model, clinical belongingness showed the strongest positive association with nursing process competency, while self‐rated theoretical nursing knowledge demonstrated a smaller positive mutually adjusted association. Physician support showed a modest association in the primary analysis, but this association was not maintained in the sensitivity analysis. These findings highlight the relevance of both interns' experiences within the clinical learning environment and their theoretical knowledge to nursing process competency. However, because of the cross‐sectional design, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships.

Nursing Process Competency Across Stages

Competency ratings were broadly similar across the five stages of the nursing process; diagnosis and planning were rated relatively lower, while implementation received the highest rating. Given the small differences between stages and the absence of established cut‐off values (Koy et al. 2023), these findings do not support characterising diagnosis and planning as substantial competency deficits or the overall pattern as structurally imbalanced. Rather, they represent modest stage‐specific differences that may warrant educational attention.

This pattern is partly consistent with prior studies showing stage‐specific variation in nursing process performance among nursing students and interns, with several studies identifying difficulties in the reasoning‐intensive stages of diagnosis and related planning tasks, although other studies report greater problems in assessment or evaluation (Basit and Korkmaz 2021; Shahzeydi et al. 2024). However, direct comparison across studies should be made cautiously because of differences in measurement instruments, educational programmes and clinical placement contexts. One possible explanation relates to both the cognitive demands of these stages and the learning opportunities available during clinical placement. Diagnosis and planning require interns to synthesise assessment findings, identify and prioritise patient problems, and translate clinical judgements into individualised goals and interventions (Hong et al. 2021; Smith et al. 2022). By contrast, implementation activities are often more observable, structured and frequently practised, potentially providing interns with more opportunities for supervised performance and feedback. However, because stage‐specific learning opportunities and clinical autonomy were not directly assessed, this explanation remains tentative.

Clinical Belongingness

Clinical belongingness showed the strongest adjusted association with nursing process competency. Its semi‐partial R2 of 0.2742 indicated that it uniquely accounted for 27.42% of the outcome variance after adjustment, substantially more than the other principal terms. Although previous research has linked clinical belongingness with students' engagement in clinical learning, professional identity and perceived practise readiness, direct evidence examining its association with nursing process competency remains limited (Singer et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2024; Nease et al. 2026). The present study therefore adds preliminary evidence that clinical belongingness may be relevant to interns' self‐perceived ability to apply the nursing process.

This association is consistent with situated learning perspectives, which conceptualise competence development as participation within a community of practise rather than solely as knowledge acquisition (Lave and Wenger 1991). Interns who feel accepted and respected may be more willing to seek clarification, express uncertainty, discuss clinical judgements and obtain feedback, which may increase their opportunities to participate in the nursing process (Singer et al. 2024; Squire et al. 2024). However, these processes were not directly examined and should be regarded as explanatory possibilities rather than established mechanisms. Moreover, the cross‐sectional design in the present study precludes determining the direction of this association. Belongingness may support competency development, but interns who perceive themselves as more competent may also participate more actively and consequently experience greater belongingness. The association should therefore be interpreted as preliminary and non‐directional.

Theoretical Nursing Knowledge

Self‐rated theoretical nursing knowledge remained positively associated with nursing process competency in the adjusted model, although its unique contribution was comparatively small (semi‐partial R2 = 0.0061; 0.61% of the outcome variance). This association is conceptually consistent with previous research indicating that clinical reasoning relies on the integration of theoretical knowledge with patient information and clinical experience (Hong et al. 2021; Leal et al. 2024). Applying the nursing process requires interns to retrieve, integrate, and apply knowledge when interpreting patient information, formulating nursing diagnoses, and selecting appropriate interventions. A stronger theoretical foundation may therefore help interns connect individual nursing activities with their underlying clinical rationale. However, because theoretical nursing knowledge was measured using a single self‐rating item, the observed association reflects perceived knowledge rather than objectively assessed knowledge mastery.

Conceptually, theoretical knowledge may be especially relevant to diagnosis and planning, which require interns to analyse patient information, prioritise problems and formulate individualised care plans (Hashemiparast et al. 2019; Hong et al. 2021). However, the present study did not examine stage‐specific associations between theoretical knowledge and nursing process competency; this explanation therefore remains tentative. Taken together, these findings support closer integration of theoretical learning with the application of the nursing process in clinical education.

Environmental and Social Factors

Several environmental/social indicators were positively associated with nursing process competency in the bivariate analyses, but most did not retain associations after simultaneous adjustment. This pattern does not establish that these contextual factors are unrelated to competency. Rather, the attenuation of their associations may partly reflect shared variance among perceptions of support, clinical belongingness and other explanatory variables. Because mediation and interaction effects were not examined, however, the relationships among these factors cannot be determined from the present analysis.

Physician support showed a modest positive association with nursing process competency in the primary model, with a small unique contribution (semi‐partial R2 = 0.0034; 0.34% of the outcome variance). Supportive interprofessional interactions may help interns clarify patient conditions, understand treatment decisions and integrate medical and nursing information during assessment and care planning (Degu et al. 2023; Leal et al. 2024). However, the association was not retained in the sensitivity model in which the ward‐round count variables were log‐transformed and should therefore be considered exploratory. The present findings are insufficient to identify physician support as a specific intervention target, but its relationship with nursing process competency warrants further investigation.

Strengths and Limitations

This study used a theory‐informed framework to examine psychological, cognitive, and environmental/social factors simultaneously in relation to nursing process competency. The principal multi‐item scales demonstrated high internal consistency at the total‐scale level, and the high response rate limited the extent of non‐response within the participating hospital. The analysis also included variables prespecified from the theoretical framework and previous literature, together with sensitivity analyses addressing the skewness of the ward‐round count variables.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes conclusions regarding temporality or causality, and the Clinical Learning Environment Model was used to organise the explanatory variables rather than to test causal pathways among its dimensions. Second, participants comprised a self‐selected convenience sample from a single tertiary teaching hospital. Although most eligible interns participated, selection bias remains possible, and the findings may not be generalisable to other settings. Because eligibility required at least 4 months of placement and exact placement duration was not modelled, the observed belongingness level may not represent students earlier in placement.

Third, the outcome and most explanatory variables were self‐reported. Common‐method variance and social desirability may have contributed to the observed associations and should be considered when interpreting the high proportion of variance explained by the model (Podsakoff and Organ 1986; van de Mortel 2008). The cumulative ward‐round counts were also subject to potential recall error. Future studies should incorporate objective or multi‐source competency assessments.

Fourth, several cognitive and environmental/social factors were assessed using investigator‐developed single‐item indicators that had not undergone formal psychometric validation. Self‐rated confidence in applying the nursing process should therefore not be interpreted as equivalent to self‐efficacy measured using a validated multi‐item scale. The comparatively low internal consistency of the connectedness subscale also limits subscale‐specific interpretation.

Fifth, the regression model simultaneously included 27 coefficient parameters, several of which represented conceptually related perceptions. Consequently, small coefficients and nominal p‐values may be sensitive to model specification even though no severe multicollinearity was detected. The adjusted estimates, particularly that for physician support, should therefore be interpreted as exploratory until replicated in independent samples.

Finally, potentially relevant contextual characteristics, including student‐to‐preceptor ratios, continuity and quality of supervision, and ward‐level learning conditions, were not measured (Abuosi et al. 2022; Thomsen et al. 2024). Clustering of interns within wards or preceptors was also not modelled. Multicentre, longitudinal and multilevel studies incorporating these contextual factors and objective competency assessments are needed to examine the direction, stability and generalisability of the observed associations.

Implications for Nursing Education and Clinical Placements

The findings identify two provisional priorities for clinical education: fostering interns' sense of belonging and strengthening the integration of theoretical knowledge with nursing process application. Nursing educators, preceptors and clinical managers could consider structured orientation, clear role expectations, continuity in preceptorship, inclusive communication and regular feedback, as belongingness is shaped by environmental, relational and experiential factors (Singer et al. 2022; Wakefield et al. 2023). Progressive involvement in patient assessment, care planning and supervised clinical decision‐making may also provide interns with opportunities to participate meaningfully in the clinical team while applying the nursing process (Onay et al. 2024; Shahzeydi et al. 2024).

Additional educational attention could be directed towards diagnosis and planning without treating these stages in isolation from the wider nursing process. Case‐based teaching, nursing process–based simulation, supervised care‐plan development and guided reflection may help interns interpret assessment findings, prioritise nursing diagnoses and connect theoretical knowledge with individualised goals and interventions (Chang et al. 2021; Sümen and Adibelli 2024). Academic educators and clinical preceptors could jointly reinforce the clinical rationale underlying each stage rather than emphasising task completion alone. These strategies require prospective evaluation before their effectiveness in strengthening nursing process competency can be established.

Conclusion

Nursing interns reported generally high nursing process competency, with relatively small differences across the five stages and slightly lower item‐level scores for diagnosis and planning. Clinical belongingness showed the strongest mutually adjusted association with nursing process competency, while self‐rated theoretical nursing knowledge demonstrated a smaller positive association. The association involving physician support was not robust across sensitivity analyses and requires further examination.

These findings highlight the potential relevance of fostering interns' acceptance, connection, and meaningful participation within the clinical learning environment while strengthening the integration of theoretical knowledge with clinical reasoning. However, the cross‐sectional, single‐centre, and self‐reported nature of the study limits causal interpretation. Longitudinal, multicentre, and intervention studies incorporating objective competency assessments are needed to determine whether changes in the clinical learning environment lead to improvements in nursing process competency.
