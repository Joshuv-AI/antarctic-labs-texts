Is External Information Useful for Data Fusion? An Evaluation before Acquisition
Guorong Dai, Lingxuan Shao, Jinbo Chen

Abstract
We consider a general statistical estimation problem involving a finite-dimensional target parameter vector. Beyond an internal data set drawn from the population distribution, external information, such as additional individual data or summary statistics, can potentially improve the estimation when incorporated via appropriate data fusion techniques. However, since acquiring external information often incurs costs, it is desirable to assess its utility beforehand using only the internal data. To address this need, we introduce a utility measure based on estimation efficiency, defined as the ratio of semiparametric efficiency bounds for estimating the target parameters with versus without incorporating the external information. It quantifies the maximum potential efficiency improvement offered by the external information, independent of specific estimation methods. To enable inference on this measure before acquiring the external information, we propose a general approach for constructing its estimators using only the internal data, adopting the efficient influence function methodology. Several concrete examples, where the target parameters and external information take various forms, are explored, demonstrating the versatility of our general framework. For each example, we construct point and interval estimators for the proposed measure and establish their asymptotic properties. Simulation studies confirm the finite-sample performance of our approach, while a real data application highlights its practical value. In scientific research and business applications, our framework significantly empowers cost-effective decision making regarding acquisition of external information.

Introduction

Consider a general statistical estimation problem, where the target is a finite-dimensional parameter vector
\mu_{0}\equiv\mu(F_{Z})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is a functional of the distribution function
F_{Z}
of a random vector
Z
. Suppose an internal data set
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}:=\{Z_{i}:i=1,\ldots,n\}
is available, providing
n
independent copies of
Z
, allowing valid estimation of
\mu_{0}
under suitable conditions. Additionally, external information concerning
F_{Z}
, independent of
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
, may also be obtainable. Examples include additional individual observations or summary statistics for components of
Z
. This external information, denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
, has potential to enhance the estimation of
\mu_{0}
beyond what can be achieved with
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
alone, as demonstrated in
Chakrabortty et al. 2022
,
Hu et al. 2022
,
Zhang and Bradic 2022
and other data fusion literature. However, acquiring external information often incurs costs in practice. This situation raises an important question: are the potential benefits of incorporating external information worth the cost? Before making a payment decision, it is preferable to evaluate its utility for estimation using the currently available data
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
. This problem is common in scientific research and business applications. To illustrate this, consider the following example, which will be explored in detail in Section
4
:

Example 1

Suppose
Z\equiv(Y,X)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
consists of a response variable and a set of covariates. The goal is to estimate the mean response
\mu_{0}\equiv\mathbb{E}(Y)
. Below are three cases involving different types of external information that may be available in addition to the internal data
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}\equiv\{(Y_{i},X_{i}):i=1,\dots,100\}
:

(i)

An online database offers massive covariate data, almost fully capturing the true distribution
F_{X}
of
X
, at a certain price.

(ii)

Under a fixed budget, it is possible to collect either 20 additional observations of
Y
or 100 additional observations of
X
. This situation, where response data are more expensive than covariate data, aligns with the “semi-supervised” setting discussed in recent literature
(
Chakrabortty et al. 2022
;
Zhang and Bradic 2022
)
.

(iii)

Again under budget constraints, one may choose between 100 additional individual observations for
X
or the average of 500 additional observations for
X
. Such scenarios are common in practice, as summary statistics are often easier to obtain than individual-level data
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
.

In Example
1
and similar scenarios where
\mu_{0}
represents other functionals of
F_{Z}
, it is critical to assess and compare the utility of various types of external information for estimating
\mu_{0}
. Motivated by this great practical importance, we aim to quantify external information utility and perform valid statistical inference on it using the internal data
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
.

1.1
Basic idea

Using the internal data
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
, we can typically construct consistent and asymptotically normal estimators for common parameters, ensuring valid inference. The primary advantage of incorporating the external information
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
is its potential to enhance inference efficiency. Once
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
is obtained, it can be integrated with
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
to estimate
\mu_{0}
through a variety of data fusion methods, given the rapid advancements in this field. However, it is impractical to evaluate each method and compare them to estimators relying solely on internal data. This challenge motives us to characterize the utility of
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
as its intrinsic property—independent of any specific data fusion methodology. Specifically, within a broad class of reasonable and commonly used estimators for
\mu_{0}
, we compare the optimal efficiency attainable in two scenarios: with and without the incorporation of
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
. Such a comparison quantifies the maximum potential utility of
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
for estimating
\mu_{0}
, agnostic to specific algorithms employed for estimation. This conceptual framework will be formalized in Section
2
.

1.2
Existing relevant works and our contributions

As one of the most compelling research areas in statistics, data fusion has garnered significant attention in the past few decades. Comprehensive reviews of this field can be found in
Hu et al. 2022
and
Yu and Zhang 2024
. Most existing studies on data fusion assume both the internal data
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
and the external information
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
are already available, concentrating on how to utilize them effectively in estimation problems. This essentially differs from our objective, which is to evaluate the intrinsic, algorithm-agnostic utility of
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
before its acquisition. To our knowledge, this has not been studied in the literature.

Specifically, in the context of estimating a generic functional
\mu_{0}\equiv\mu(F_{Z})
of the population distribution
F_{Z}
, we introduce in Section
2.2
a general algorithm-agnostic utility measure, denoted by
\theta_{0}\in(0,1\,]
, for various types of external information. Following the approach outlined in Section
1.1
, this measure is defined as the ratio of the semiparametric efficiency bounds
(
Tsiatis 2007
)
associated with estimation based solely on internal data versus estimation incorporating both internal data and external information. The complement
(1-\theta_{0})
quantifies the maximum potential efficiency improvement achievable by incorporating the external information
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
into the estimation. Next, we consider statistical inference for
\theta_{0}
using only the internal data
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}\equiv\{Z_{i}:i=1,\ldots,n\}
, which can be performed prior to acquiring
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
. To avoid model misspecification and ensure broad applicability, we develop in Section
3
a fully nonparametric approach for estimating
\theta_{0}
. The efficient influence function method
(
Hines et al. 2022
)
is adopted to address challenges posed by potentially complex nuisance functions, ensuring tractable asymptotic properties of our estimators.

As illustrations of our general framework, we thoroughly analyze Example
1
(Section
4
), as well as two more complex cases where the target
\mu_{0}
is a nonlinear or vector-valued functional of
F_{Z}
(Appendices
A
–
B
). In these examples with various types of external information, we specify point and interval estimators for the utility measure
\theta_{0}
, deriving their asymptotic properties including convergence rates and coverage guarantees. All the theoretical results are validated through comprehensive numerical studies. Furthermore, in Section
6
, we discuss scenarios where
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
represents possibly biased information regarding
F_{Z}
, providing guidance on interpreting our framework in such cases.

2
Problem formulation

We now formalize the framework outlined in Section
1.1
. Suppose the population distribution
F_{Z}\in\mathcal{F}
for some semiparametric model
\mathcal{F}
, and the functional
\mu
in the definition of the target
\mu_{0}\equiv\mu(F_{Z})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is pathwise differentiable on
\mathcal{F}
at
F_{Z}
. As is common in standard estimation problems, we restrict attention to the class of regular and asymptotically linear estimators for
\mu_{0}
, which encompasses most reasonable and widely used estimators
(
Tsiatis 2007
;
Hu et al. 2022
)
. Regularity and asymptotic linearity of an estimator
\overline{\mu}
for
\mu_{0}
ensure that its limiting behaviors do not depend on the particular data generating process too heavily, and
\{\hbox{ACov}(\overline{\mu})/n\}^{-1/2}(\overline{\mu}-\mu_{0})
converges in distribution to a standard normal random vector as
n\to\infty
, where the asymptotic covariance matrix
\hbox{ACov}(\overline{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
of
n^{1/2}\,\overline{\mu}
is finite and non-singular. These properties underpin standard inference procedures, such as confidence interval construction and hypothesis testing, with the asymptotic efficiency of inference governed by
\hbox{ACov}(\overline{\mu})
.

2.1
Internal-data-only and data-fusion efficiency bounds

To assess the optimal efficiency attainable with and without external information, we consider the
corresponding semiparametric efficiency bounds, denoted by
\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\Theta_{\hbox{DF}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, for estimating
\mu_{0}
based on (i) the internal data
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
alone and (ii) both
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
and the external information
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
, respectively. These bounds represent meaningful lower limits (in the sense of matrix definiteness) of
\{\hbox{ACov}(\overline{\mu}):\overline{\mu}\in\mathcal{U}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}\}
and
\{\hbox{ACov}(\overline{\mu}):\overline{\mu}\in\mathcal{U}_{\hbox{DF}}\}
, where
\mathcal{U}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{\hbox{DF}}
are the sets of all regular and asymptotically linear estimators for
\mu_{0}
in the respective settings. The internal-data-only efficiency bound
\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
is the supremum of the Cramér-Rao bounds over all parametric submodels of the semiparametric model
\mathcal{F}
. The data-fusion efficiency bound
\Theta_{\hbox{DF}}
can be derived analogously from a “smaller” semiparametric model
\mathcal{F}_{\hbox{DF}}\subset\mathcal{F}
, reflecting additional restrictions imposed by the external information
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
. For instance, in Example
1
(i), knowledge of the marginal distribution
F_{X}
reduces the fully unrestricted nonparametric model
\mathcal{F}
to a subset
\mathcal{F}_{\hbox{DF}}
, where
F_{X}
is fixed and only the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
remains unrestricted. Consequently,
\mathcal{F}_{\hbox{DF}}
contains no more parametric submodels than
\mathcal{F}
, ensuring that
\Theta_{\hbox{DF}}
is “no greater than”
\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}
, in the sense that

\displaystyle(\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}-\Theta_{\hbox{DF}})\hbox{ is positive semi-definite}.

(1)

A crucial observation is that under given semiparametric models
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\hbox{DF}}
, the efficiency bounds
\{\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}},\Theta_{\hbox{DF}}\}
are determined solely by the population distribution
F_{Z}
, not depending on any specific estimation method. More formally,

\{\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}},\Theta_{\hbox{DF}}\}
are functionals of
F_{Z}
, independent of

specific estimation methods used for the target
\mu_{0}
.

(2)

We have briefly introduced the concept of the semiparametric efficiency bound in scenarios with and without external information, establishing the foundation for efficiency comparison between the two cases. For a more in-depth and comprehensive treatment of semiparametric theory in the internal-data-only setting, interested readers are referred to
Tsiatis 2007
, which includes methods for deriving efficiency bounds across various estimation problems. More recently,
Hu et al. 2022
extended this theory to incorporate additional summary statistics. Furthermore, when the external information
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
consists of additional individual data, semiparametric theory for the combined sample structure
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
(such as
\{(Y_{i},X_{i}):i=1,\ldots,n\}\cup\{X_{i}:i=n+1,\ldots,n+N\}
in Example
1
(iii)) has been developed in
Tsiatis 2007
. For most common parameters and various types of external information, including those considered in the examples of Section
4
and Appendices
A
–
B
, the efficiency bounds
\{\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}},\Theta_{\hbox{DF}}\}
have already been derived in the existing literature on semiparametric theory and data fusion. Our framework enables direct applications of any established results on semiparametric efficiency bounds. Thus, we treat the forms of
\{\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}},\Theta_{\hbox{DF}}\}
as known within our general approach, and will specify them when studying concrete examples.

2.2
A utility measure for external information

When the target
\mu_{0}
is a vector, directly comparing the two efficiency bounds
\{\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}},\Theta_{\hbox{DF}}\}
is unlikely to yield simple and interpretable measures. In this case, a practical strategy is to transform the matrices into scalars using the trace operation
\mathrm{Tr}(\cdot)
. Due to the relationship in (
1
), the linearity of the trace ensures

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}})-\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\hbox{DF}})=\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}}-\Theta_{\hbox{DF}})\geq 0.

(3)

Additionally, we assume
\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\hbox{DF}})>0
to exclude the degenerate case where
\Theta_{\hbox{DF}}
is a zero matrix, indicating
\mu_{0}
can be estimated deterministically. This, along with the monotonicity in (
3
), implies
\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}})\geq\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\hbox{DF}})>0
. Then, our utility measure for external information is defined as the ratio of the two traces, denoted by
\theta_{10}
and
\theta_{20}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\theta_{0}\equiv\theta_{10}\,/\,\theta_{20}:=\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\hbox{DF}})\,/\,\mathrm{Tr}(\Theta_{\hbox{\rm{IN}}})\in(0,1\,].

(4)

For the external information
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
, the measure
\theta_{0}
quantifies its maximum potential contribution to the estimation, as the complement
(1-\theta_{0})=(\theta_{20}-\theta_{10})/\theta_{20}\in[\,0,1)
represents the improvement in the best achievable (componentwise) efficiency gained by incorporating
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
. The extreme case of
\theta_{0}=1
indicates
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
provides no additional efficiency benefit. Furthermore, as a ratio within
(0,1]
,
\theta_{0}
is invariant to the scale and units of measurement, facilitating comparisons across different types of external information.

Besides its explicit interpretation in quantifying external information utility, the measure
\theta_{0}
in (
4
) also possesses two critical properties for our analysis, which are derived from the fact in (
2
):

(i)

It is algorithm-agnostic, defined independently of specific estimation methods used in either the internal-data-only or data-fusion setting. Formally,
\theta_{0}
is a functional of
F_{Z}
, which represents an intrinsic characteristic of the population distribution, and thereby allows for meaningful statistical inference.

(ii)

As a functional of
F_{Z}
, it can be estimated from the internal data
\{Z_{i}:i=1,\ldots,n\}
, enabling evaluations of potential utility for external information before its acquisition.

We now turn to estimating the utility measure
\theta_{0}
in (
4
)
using the internal data
\{Z_{i}:i=1,\ldots,n\}
. To avoid model misspecification and ensure braod applicability, our analysis adopts a nonparametric framework. Since
\theta_{10}\equiv\theta_{1}(F_{Z})
and
\theta_{20}\equiv\theta_{2}(F_{Z})
may be complex functionals of
F_{Z}
, the resulting form of
\theta_{0}\equiv\theta(F_{Z})
can be intricate, often involving challenging nuisance functions. To address this complexity, we adopt the efficient influence function method. As demonstrated by
Hines et al. 2022
, this approach effectively mitigates the impact of nuisance estimation and, under suitable conditions, yields point estimators that achieve the optimal efficiency for
\theta_{0}
in a nonparametric setting. We begin by clarifying some relevant concepts.

Definition 1

Suppose a scalar-valued functional
F\mapsto\vartheta(F)
is Gâteaux differentiable on a nonparametric model at
F_{Z}
, and

\mathrm{Var}\{\dot{\vartheta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z}-F_{Z})\}\in(0,\infty)
. In this case,
\dot{\vartheta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z}-F_{Z})
is referred to as the “nonparametric efficient influence function” of
\vartheta_{0}\equiv\vartheta(F_{Z})
. If an estimator
\widehat{\vartheta}
based on
\{Z_{i}:i=1,\ldots,n\}
satisfies
\widehat{\vartheta}-\vartheta_{0}=n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\dot{\vartheta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z_{i}}-F_{Z})+o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
, then
\widehat{\vartheta}
is called a “nonparametric efficient estimator” for
\vartheta_{0}
.

Our goal is to derive a nonparametric efficient estimator for
\theta_{0}\equiv\theta_{10}/\theta_{20}
. As indicated by the next proposition, achieving this goal reduces to constructing nonparametric efficient estimators for
\theta_{10}
and
\theta_{20}
individually.

Proposition 1

For
\theta_{0}\equiv\theta(F_{Z})
defined in (
4
), its nonparametric efficient influence function is given by

\dot{\theta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z}-F_{Z})=\dot{\theta}_{1}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z}-F_{Z})/\theta_{20}-\theta_{10}\dot{\theta}_{2}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z}-F_{Z})/\theta_{20}^{2}.

Let
\{\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2}\}
be estimators for
\{\theta_{10}\equiv\theta_{1}(F_{Z}),\theta_{20}\equiv\theta_{2}(F_{Z})\}
, constructed from
\{Z_{i}:i=1,\ldots,n\}
. If
\mathrm{Var}\{\dot{\theta}_{j}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z}-F_{Z})\}<\infty
, and
\widehat{\theta}_{j}-\theta_{j0}=n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\dot{\theta}_{j}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z_{i}}-F_{Z})+O_{p}(r_{j})
for some positive series
r_{j}\equiv r_{j}(n)\to 0
(
j=1,2
), then the ratio estimator
\widehat{\theta}:=\widehat{\theta}_{1}/\widehat{\theta}_{2}
satisfies

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}-\theta_{0}=n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\dot{\theta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z_{i}}-F_{Z})+O_{p}(r_{1}+r_{2}+n^{-1}).

To estimate
\theta_{j0}\equiv\theta_{j}(F_{Z})
, we apply the estimation equation method from
Hines et al. 2022
, solving
n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\dot{\theta}_{j}(\widehat{F}_{Z};\delta_{Z_{i}}-\widehat{F}_{Z})=0
to obtain an estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{j}\equiv\theta_{j}(\widehat{F}_{Z})
(
j=1,2
), where
\widehat{F}_{Z}
is an estimator for
F_{Z}
. This procedure is computationally straightforward because
\theta_{j0}
is defined as the trace of an efficiency bound, corresponding to a covariance matrix. This implies
\theta_{j0}\equiv\mathbb{E}\{\psi_{j}(Z;F_{Z})\}\equiv\int\psi_{j}(z;F_{Z})dF_{Z}(z)
for some functional
\psi_{j}(\cdot\,;\cdot)
. Consequently, its Gâteaux derivative has the form

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{j}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z}-F_{Z})=\phi_{j}(Z;F_{Z})+\psi_{j}(Z;F_{Z})-\theta_{j}(F_{Z}),

with
\phi_{j}(Z;F_{Z}):=(\partial/\partial t)\{\hbox{$\int$}\psi_{j}(z;F_{Z}+t(\delta_{Z}-F_{Z}))dF_{Z}(z)\}\mid_{t=0}
. This leads to the closed-form expression of
\widehat{\theta}_{j}

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{j}\equiv\theta_{j}(\widehat{F}_{Z})=n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\{\phi_{j}(Z_{i};\widehat{F}_{Z})+\psi_{j}(Z_{i};\widehat{F}_{Z})\}.

In concrete examples such as those in Section
4
and Appendices
A
–
B

to be any reasonable estimator for

, such that

(i)

;

(ii)

characteristics of the conditional distributions, such as means and distribution functions, are estimated using flexible regression or machine learning methods that satisfy certain high-level convergence rate conditions;

(iii)

the marginal and conditional density functions are estimated using kernel smoothing.

4
Example: Mean response estimation with external covariate information

To illustrate our general framework, we analyze a concrete example: mean response estimation with external covariate information, where
Z\equiv(Y,X)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and the objective is to estimate
\mu_{0}\equiv\mathbb{E}(Y)
within a nonparametric model. Using only the internal data
\{(Y_{i},X_{i}):i=1,\ldots,n\}
, the efficiency bound for
\mu_{0}
is given by

\displaystyle\theta_{20}\equiv\mathbb{E}\{(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}\},

(5)

as shown by
Tsiatis 2007
. We assess the utility of two types of external covariate information: individual data or a summary statistic, that is,

N
independent copies
\{X_{i}:i=n+1,\ldots,n+N\}
of
X
, or

(6)

\displaystyle\hbox{their average $N^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+N}$}X_{i}$}.

(7)

Define the fixed constant
\nu:=n/(n+N)\in[\,0,1)
, which is known prior to acquiring the external information. When
\nu=0
, corresponding to
N=\infty
, the external information in (
6
) and (
7
) represents the distribution and expectation of
X
, respectively. Incorporating this information,
Zhang and Bradic 2022
and
Hu et al. 2022
demonstrated that for estimating
\mu_{0}
, the data-fusion efficiency bound is given by

\displaystyle\theta_{10}\equiv\mathbb{E}[(1-\nu)\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}+\nu(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}],

(8)

where

equals
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X)
in case (
6
) and
\beta_{0}^{\rm T}\overrightarrow{X}
in case (
7
), with
\beta_{0}:=\mathbb{E}(\overrightarrow{X}\overrightarrow{X}^{\rm T})^{-1}\mathbb{E}(\overrightarrow{X}Y)
.
We proceed with the general formula (
8
) of
\theta_{10}
, where

, and analyze the two cases, (
6
) and (
7
), simultaneously. Combining (
5
) and (
8
), the utility measure
\theta_{0}
defined in (
4
) takes the form

\displaystyle\theta_{0}\equiv(1-\nu)\mathbb{E}[\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}]\,\big/\,\mathbb{E}\{(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}\}+\nu.

(9)

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{1}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z}-F_{Z})=(1-\nu)\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}+\nu(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}-\theta_{10},

Using the results in Proposition

\{(Y_{i},X_{i}):i=1,\ldots,n\}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{1}\equiv n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}[(1-\nu)\{Y_{i}-\widehat{g}(X_{i})\}^{2}+\nu(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu})^{2}],\quad\widehat{\theta}_{2}\equiv n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu})^{2},

(10)

where
\widehat{\mu}:=n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}Y_{i}
. To construct
\{\widehat{g}(X_{i}):i=1,\ldots,n\}
, we adopt the cross-fitting strategy
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
. Specifically, for a fixed integer
M\geq 2
, we randomly divide the index set
\mathcal{I}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
into
M
disjoint subsets
\{\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{M}\}
of sizes as even as possible, and set

\displaystyle\widehat{g}(X_{i})\equiv\hbox{$\sum_{m=1}^{M}$}\widehat{g}_{m}(X_{i})\mathds{1}(i\in\mathcal{I}_{m}),

(11)

where
\widehat{g}_{m}
is an estimator for
g
constructed using the data
\{(Y_{i},X_{i}):i\in\mathcal{I}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{m}\}
. The cross-fitting procedure removes the dependence between
\widehat{g}
and
X_{i}
in
\widehat{g}(X_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. It simplifies the control of remainder terms in the expansion of
\widehat{\theta}_{1}
, while not altering the influence function thereof. This approach allows for the use of flexible regression or machine learning methods, with potentially intractable first-order properties, to construct
\widehat{g}_{m}
. Therefore, we leave the form of
\widehat{g}_{m}
unspecified and only impose high-level convergence rate conditions; see Assumption
1
. In the numerical studies presented in Section
5
, various methods will be employed to construct
\widehat{g}_{m}
. Finally, the point estimator for
\theta_{0}
in (
9
) is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}\equiv\widehat{\theta}_{1}/\widehat{\theta}_{2}=(1-\nu)\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\{Y_{i}-\widehat{g}(X_{i})\}^{2}/\{\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu})^{2}\}+\nu.

(12)

In the following, we present asymptotic properties of
\widehat{\theta}
after specifying necessary regularity conditions.

Assumption 1

For
m=1,\ldots,M
and some positive series
r_{g,2},r_{g,4}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, the estimator
\widehat{g}_{m}
in (
11
) satisfies

\displaystyle\hbox{$\int$}\{\widehat{g}_{m}(x)-g(x)\}^{2}dF_{X}(x)=O_{p}(r_{g,2}^{2}),\ \hbox{$\int$}\{\widehat{g}_{m}(x)-g(x)\}^{4}dF_{X}(x)=O_{p}(r_{g,4}^{4}).

Theorem 1

Suppose Assumption
1
holds and there exists some constant
c>0
such that
\mathbb{E}[\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}\mid X]+\mathbb{E}(Y^{4})<c
almost surely.
Then, our estimator
\widehat{\theta}
in (
12
) satisfies

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}-\theta_{0}=n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\dot{\theta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z_{i}}-F_{Z})+O_{p}(r_{g,2}^{2}+(r_{g,2}+r_{g,4}^{2})/n^{1/2}+n^{-1}),

(13)

with
\dot{\theta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z_{i}}-F_{Z})
specified in Proposition
2
. It follows that:

(a)

\widehat{\theta}
attains the following convergence rate:

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}-\theta_{0}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}+r_{g,2}^{2}+(r_{g,2}+r_{g,4}^{2})/n^{1/2}+n^{-1})=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}+r_{g,2}^{2});

(14)

(b)

if
r_{g,2}^{2}+(r_{g,2}+r_{g,4}^{2})/n^{1/2}=o(n^{-1/2})
and
\mathbb{P}\{\dot{\theta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z}-F_{Z})=0\}<1
, then
\widehat{\theta}
is nonparametric efficient, that is,
\widehat{\theta}-\theta_{0}=n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\dot{\theta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z_{i}}-F_{Z})+o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
;

(c)

if
\mathbb{P}\{g(X)=\mu_{0}\}=1
, then
\mathbb{P}\{\dot{\theta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z}-F_{Z})=0\}=1
and the rate in (
14
) is improved to

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}-\theta_{0}=O_{p}(r_{g,2}^{2}+(r_{g,2}+r_{g,4}^{2})/n^{1/2}+n^{-1}).

(15)

Remark 1
(Discussion of the rate conditions on
\widehat{g}_{m}
)

Theorem
1
(b) requires the following conditions:

\displaystyle\hbox{$\int$}\{\widehat{g}_{m}(x)-g(x)\}^{2}dF_{X}(x)=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

(16)

\displaystyle\hbox{$\int$}\{\widehat{g}_{m}(x)-g(x)\}^{4}dF_{X}(x)=o_{p}(1).

(17)

It is evident that under (
16
), condition (
17
) holds, for instance, as long as
\{\widehat{g}_{m}(X),\,g(X)\}
are almost surely bounded. We now address condition (
16
) in the two cases separately:

(a)

In case (
6
) with
g(X)\equiv\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X)
, the estimator
\widehat{g}_{m}
can be constructed using various regression or machine learning algorithms targeting the conditional mean
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X)
. In the context of estimation problems involving nuisance functions, such as those in
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
, the high-level condition (
16
) is often imposed as the general minimum convergence rate requirement for flexible nuisance estimators with unspecified forms.

(b)

In case (
7
) with
g(X)\equiv\beta_{0}^{\rm T}\overrightarrow{X}
, we set the nuisance estimator
\widehat{g}_{m}(X_{i})\equiv\widehat{\beta}_{m}^{\,{\rm T}}\,\overrightarrow{X}_{i}
in (
11
), where
\widehat{\beta}_{m}
is an estimator for
\beta_{0}\equiv\mathbb{E}(\overrightarrow{X}\overrightarrow{X}^{\rm T})^{-1}\mathbb{E}(\overrightarrow{X}Y)
, constructed using the data
\{(Y_{i},X_{i}):i\in\mathcal{I}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{m}\}
. For this setting, condition (
16
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\hbox{$\int$}(\widehat{\beta}_{m}^{\,{\rm T}}\,\overrightarrow{x}-\beta_{0}^{\rm T}\,\overrightarrow{x})^{2}dF_{X}(x)=(\widehat{\beta}_{m}-\beta_{0})^{\rm T}\mathbb{E}(\overrightarrow{X}\overrightarrow{X}^{\rm T})(\widehat{\beta}_{m}-\beta_{0})=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

Therefore, if
\|\mathbb{E}(\overrightarrow{X}\overrightarrow{X}^{\rm T})\|<c
for some constant
c>0
, a sufficient condition for (
16
) is

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\beta}_{m}-\beta_{0}\|^{2}=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(18)

When the dimension
p
of
X
is fixed, (
18
) is satisfied by the ordinary least squares estimator under standard conditions. In high-dimensional settings with a diverging number of covariates in
X
, a reasonable choice for
\widehat{\beta}_{m}
is the Lasso estimator. For such
\widehat{\beta}_{m}
, (
18
) is typically equivalent to a sparsity assumption—the number of nonzero components in the vector
\beta_{0}
is of order
o(n^{1/2}/\log p)

(
Wainwright 2019
)
.

Remark 2
(Interpretation of Theorem
1
(c))

The condition
\mathbb{P}\{g(X)=\mu_{0}\}=1
is equivalent to the utility measure
\theta_{0}\equiv(1-\nu)\mathbb{E}[\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}]\,\big/\,\mathbb{E}\{(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}\}+\nu=1
. This implies incorporating the external information does not provide any efficiency benefit for estimating
\mu_{0}\equiv\mathbb{E}(Y)
. In this scenario, the influence function
\dot{\theta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z}-F_{Z})
in the expansion (
13
) vanishes, leading to the improved rate in (
15
).

4.2
Confidence interval construction

Theorem
1
(c) indicates if
\mathbb{P}\{g(X)=\mu_{0}\}=1
, meaning
\theta_{0}=1
, the first-order term in the expansion (
13
),
n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\dot{\theta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z_{i}}-F_{Z})
, is almost surely zero. In this case, the limiting behavior of
(\widehat{\theta}-\theta_{0})
is governed by the higher-order terms, which include errors from estimating the nuisance function
g
. Consequently, the asymptotic distribution of
\widehat{\theta}
differs depending on whether
\theta_{0}\in(0,1)
or
\theta_{0}=1
. Additionally, when
g(X)
represents a nonparametric or high-dimensional model, these higher-order terms often exhibit intractable limiting behaviors. In practice, we generally do not know if
\mathbb{P}\{g(X)=\mu_{0}\}=1
, preventing
\widehat{\theta}
from being used to construct confidence intervals that are valid for all
\theta_{0}\in(0,1]
. This issue motivates us to propose an alternative estimator with a consistently well-behaved asymptotic distribution.

The term
n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\dot{\theta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z_{i}}-F_{Z})
in (
13
) corresponds to the errors in approximating the expectations
\mathbb{E}[\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}]
and
\mathbb{E}\{(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}\}
(as if
g
and
\mu_{0}
were known), as demonstrated in the Supplementary Material. Specifically, these errors are given by

\displaystyle n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\{Y_{i}-g(X_{i})\}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}]~\hbox{and}~n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}(Y_{i}-\mu_{0})^{2}-\mathbb{E}\{(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}\}.

(19)

Whenever
\mathbb{P}\{g(X)=\mu_{0}\}=1
, the two terms in (
19
) are almost surely identical and cancel out, resulting in
\mathbb{P}\{n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}\dot{\theta}(F_{Z};\delta_{Z_{i}}-F_{Z})=0\}=1
. To avoid this vanishing first-order term issue, our strategy is to approximate the two expectations using different halves of the available data. Specifically, we propose an alternative estimator:

\displaystyle\textstyle\widetilde{\theta}\equiv(1-\nu)\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{\widetilde{n}}$}\{Y_{i}-\widehat{g}(X_{i})\}^{2}\,\big/\,\{\hbox{$\sum_{i=\widetilde{n}+1}^{n}$}(Y_{i}-\widehat{\mu})^{2}\}+\nu~\hbox{with}~\widetilde{n}:=\lceil n/2\rceil,

(20)

where
\{\widehat{g}(X_{i}):i=1,\ldots,\widetilde{n}\}
are constructed as in (
11
), and
\widehat{\mu}\equiv n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}Y_{i}
. The error terms of
\widetilde{\theta}
, corresponding to those in (
19
), are now

\displaystyle\widetilde{n}^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{\widetilde{n}}$}\{Y_{i}-g(X_{i})\}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}]~\hbox{and}~(n-\widetilde{n})^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=\widetilde{n}+1}^{n}$}(Y_{i}-\mu_{0})^{2}-\mathbb{E}\{(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}\},

which are unequal and do not cancel out, regardless of whether
\mathbb{P}\{g(X)=\mu_{0}\}=1
. As a result, under suitable conditions, we can ensure these error terms dominate the higher-order terms in the expansion of
(\widetilde{\theta}-\theta_{0})
. This allows for an explicit asymptotic distribution of
\widetilde{\theta}
, enabling the construction of valid confidence intervals for
\theta_{0}
.

Theorem 2

Suppose Assumption
1
holds with
n^{1/2}r_{g,2}^{2}+r_{g,4}=o(1)
, and there exist some positive constants
\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
such that
\mathbb{E}[\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}\mid X]+\mathbb{E}(|Y|^{4+c_{1}})<c_{2}
almost surely. If

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}:=2(1-\nu)^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}[\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}]\,/\,\theta_{20}^{\,2}+2(\theta_{10}-\nu\theta_{20})^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}\{(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}\}\,/\,\theta_{20}^{\,4}>c

(21)

for some constant
c>0
, then we have the following conclusions:

(a)

the estimator
\widetilde{\theta}
in (
20
) satisfies
n^{1/2}(\widetilde{\theta}-\theta_{0})/\gamma\,\xrightarrow{w}\,\hbox{Normal}(0,1)
;

(b)

for any predetermined confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the interval

\displaystyle\mathrm{CI}_{\alpha}\!:=\![\,\widetilde{\theta}-\widehat{\gamma}\,u_{(1+\alpha)/2}/n^{1/2},\,\widetilde{\theta}+\widehat{\gamma}\,u_{(1+\alpha)/2}/n^{1/2}\,]\hbox{ satisfies }\hbox{$\lim_{\,n\to\infty}$}\,\mathbb{P}(\theta_{0}\in\mathrm{CI}_{\alpha})=\alpha,

(22)

where

\widehat{\gamma}^{\,2}:=2(1-\nu)^{2}\,\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}[\{Y-\widehat{g}(X)\}^{2}]\,/\,\widehat{\theta}_{2}^{\,2}+2(\widehat{\theta}_{1}-\nu\widehat{\theta}_{2})^{2}\,\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\{(Y-\widehat{\mu})^{2}\}\,/\,\widehat{\theta}_{2}^{\,4},

with
\{\widehat{\theta}_{1},\widehat{\theta}_{2}\}
as in (
10
),
\{\widehat{g}(X_{i}):i=1,\ldots,n\}
as in (
11
) and
\widehat{\mu}\equiv n^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=1}^{n}$}Y_{i}
.

As indicated by (
21
), as long as one of the two variances,
\mathrm{Var}[\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}]
and
\mathrm{Var}\{(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}\}
, is positive, the asymptotic variance
\gamma^{2}
of
\widetilde{\theta}
has a positive lower bound. This ensures the asymptotic normality result stated in Theorem
2
(a). Moreover, with a consistent estimate
\widehat{\gamma}
for
\gamma
, we provide in Theorem
2
(b) an asymptotically valid confidence interval
\mathrm{CI}_{\alpha}
for
\theta_{0}
.

5
Numerical studies

In this section, we evaluate the numerical performance of the method introduced in Section
4
. When the nuisance function
g(X)\equiv\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X)
, we estimate it using the Super Learner algorithm
(
van der Laan et al. 2007
)
, which combines estimators from ordinary least squares regression, additive models
(
Hastie and Tibshirani 1990
)
and neural networks
(
Ripley 2007
)
into an optimal convex combination. The weights for the combination are determined by minimizing the squared prediction error using ten-fold cross-validation. These algorithms are implemented using the
R
packages
SuperLearner
,
glm
,
gam
and
nnet
with all arguments set to the default values. For the case where
g(X)\equiv\beta_{0}^{\rm T}\overrightarrow{X}
, we approximate the parameter vector
\beta_{0}\equiv\mathbb{E}(\overrightarrow{X}\overrightarrow{X}^{\rm T})^{-1}\mathbb{E}(\overrightarrow{X}Y)
using the ordinary least squares estimate. In the cross-fitting procedure (
11
), we set the number of folds to
M=5
.

Since the utility measure
\theta_{0}
lies within the interval
(0,1]
, it is practical to truncate its estimators. For a general interval
\mathcal{L}:=[\ell_{1},\ell_{2}]
with
\ell_{1}\leq\ell_{2}
, which reduces to a single value if
\ell_{1}=\ell_{2}
, we define the truncating function as

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{L}):=[\,\ell_{1}\mathds{1}(0<\ell_{1}<1)+\mathds{1}(\ell_{1}\geq 1),\,\ell_{2}\mathds{1}(0<\ell_{2}<1)+\mathds{1}(\ell_{2}\geq 1)\,].

(23)

We apply this truncating function to the point estimator
\widehat{\theta}
and confidence interval
\mathrm{CI}_{\alpha}
, which are computed as in (
12
) and (
22
). In all numerical studies, when referring to
\widehat{\theta}
and
\mathrm{CI}_{\alpha}
, we assume they have been truncated by the function
H(\cdot)
.

), where we set the coefficient

. All results are summarized over

For the utility measure
\theta_{0}

and

5.2
A real data example

We now apply our the method from Section
4
to data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (
https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/continuousnhanes/default.aspx?BeginYear=2015
), including records of systolic blood pressure (
Y\in\mathbb{R}
), age and body mass index (
X\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
) for
n=6550
individuals. These internal data allow for estimating the population’s average systolic blood pressure,
\mathbb{E}(Y)
, a key public health metric. To improve estimation precision, the most effective approach is to collect additional observations for
Y
. However, this can be costly, as measuring blood pressure often requires multiple readings using specialized instruments over an extended period. In contrast, individual data and summary statistics of
X
, though less effective, are easier to obtain from demographic or healthcare databases. Therefore, we consider three types of external information:

(i)
\{Y_{i}:i=n+1,\ldots,n+N\}
, (ii)
\{X_{i}:i=n+1,\ldots,n+N\}
, (iii)
N^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+N}$}X_{i}
,

(25)

and evaluate their utility for estimating
\mathbb{E}(Y)
using the internal data
\{(Y_{i},X_{i}):i=1,\ldots,n\}
. Comparing their effectiveness with acquisition costs helps determine the most worthwhile option.

The utility of (i)–(iii) in (
25
) is quantified by
(1-\theta_{0})
, with
\theta_{0}
defined in (
4
). This parameter represents the potential efficiency gain from incorporating external information. The utility of (i) is
1-\theta_{0}\equiv 1-\nu\equiv 1-n/(n+N)
, serving as a benchmark. For (ii) and (iii), we focus on
\theta_{0}^{*}:=(1-\theta_{0})/(1-\nu)\equiv 1-\mathbb{E}[\{Y-g(X)\}^{2}]\,/\,\mathbb{E}\{(Y-\mu_{0})^{2}\}
, with
\theta_{0}
specified in (
9
), which measures their relative utility compared to (i). Since
\theta_{0}^{*}
is a linear transformation of
\theta_{0}
, its point and interval estimators follow from
\widehat{\theta}
and
\mathrm{CI}_{\alpha}\equiv[L_{\alpha},U_{\alpha}]
in (
12
) and (
22
), given by
\widehat{\theta}^{\,*}:=(1-\widehat{\theta})/(1-\nu)
and
\mathrm{CI}_{\alpha}^{*}:=[\,(1-U_{\alpha})/(1-\nu),\,(1-L_{\alpha})/(1-\nu)\,]
. Applying the inference method from Section
4
, we obtain:

•

for (ii),
\widehat{\theta}^{\,*}=0.2864
and
\mathrm{CI}_{0.95}^{*}=[\,0.2128,0.3288\,]
;

•

for (iii),
\widehat{\theta}^{\,*}=0.2580
and
\mathrm{CI}_{0.95}^{*}=[\,0.1799,0.3001\,]
.

The close point estimates and overlapping confidence intervals indicate that the two types of external information offer similar utility. This suggests the conditional mean model
\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X)
is likely close to linear. Given the summary statistic (iii) is typically easier to obtain than the individual data (ii), (iii) is the more cost-effective choice. Between (i) and (iii), the decision depends on their respective costs and specific budget constraints.

6
Discussion

6.1
Additional examples

For external information
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
in various forms, we developed a general framework to assess its utility for estimating a generic functional
\mu_{0}\equiv\mu(F_{0})
of the population distribution
F_{Z}
prior to its acquisition. Beyond the practically important example in Section
4
, Appendices
A
–
B
examine two additional cases that are both practically relevant and technically more involved. There,
\mu_{0}
corresponds to either a response quantile or the parameter vector of a multiple linear model, making it a nonlinear or vector-valued functional of
F_{Z}
, while
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
consists of either individual covariate data or univariate regression parameter estimates. For these settings, we specify the utility measure
\theta_{0}
in (
4
) and establish theoretical properties for its point and interval estimators, constructed using the approach from Section
3
. The corresponding numerical studies appear in Appendix
C
. These examples further demonstrate the versatility of our framework.

6.2
Possibly biased external information

While our primary focus has been on cases where the external information
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
accurately reflects characteristics of the population distribution
F_{Z}
, the utility measure
\theta_{0}
in (
4
) also has meaningful interpretations when
\mathcal{D}_{\hbox{EX}}
represents possibly biased information concerning
F_{Z}
. To illustrate this, recall the mean response estimation problem from Section
4
, where the target parameter is
\mathbb{E}(Y)
, and the internal data set
\{(Y_{i},X_{i}):i=1,\ldots,n\}
consists of
n
independent copies of the pair
(Y,X)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The external information is now
\widetilde{\xi}:=N^{-1}\hbox{$\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+N}$}(X_{i}+h)
, where
h\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is an unobserved constant vector. Suppose there exists an unknown index set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{J}:=\{1,\ldots,p\}
such that
h_{[j]}=0
for
j\in\mathcal{A}
and
h_{[j]}\neq 0
for
j\in\mathcal{J}\backslash\mathcal{A}
. This indicates
\widetilde{\xi}
is a partially biased estimate for
\mathbb{E}(X)
. In this scenario, directly integrating
\widetilde{\xi}
may lead to biased estimation. As shown in
Hu et al. 2022
, the oracle case occurs when
\mathcal{A}

6.3
Potential extension

Thus far, we have been concentrated on the potential efficiency benefits of external information, primarily due to the simplicity of its quantification. However, more broadly, incorporating external information can also improve other aspects of inference. For example, in the context of treatment effect estimation based on observational studies,
Chakrabortty and Dai 2022
demonstrated that including additional individual data of treatment indicators and baseline covariates can enhance robustness compared to estimators relying solely on the internal data. Future research will aim to quantify the robustness benefits of external information in causal inference and other problems involving model misspecification, thereby expanding the scope and applicability of our framework.
