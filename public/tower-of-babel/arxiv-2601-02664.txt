Powerful Fibonacci polynomials over finite fields
Graeme Bates, Ryan Jesubalan, Seewoo Lee, Jane Lu, Hyewon Shim

Abstract
Bugeaud, Mignotte, and Siksek proved that the only perfect powers in Fibonacci sequence are 0, 1, 8, and 144. In this paper, we study the polynomial analogue of the problem. Especially, we give a complete characterization of the Fibonacci polynomials that are perfect powers or powerful over finite fields, where there are infinitely many of them. We also give similar characterizations for some of Horadam’s generalized Lucas polynomial sequences, which include Fibonacci, Lucas, Chebyshev, and Jacobsthal polynomials.



1   Introduction

Fibonacci numbers
F_{n}
and Lucas numbers
L_{n}
are among the most widely studied objects in mathematics. They are defined recursively by

\displaystyle F_{0}=0,

\displaystyle\quad F_{1}=1,\quad F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}\quad(n\geq 2),

\displaystyle L_{0}=2,

\displaystyle\quad L_{1}=1,\quad L_{n}=L_{n-1}+L_{n-2}\quad(n\geq 2).

Many authors have studied various arithmetic properties of these sequences.
For example, Brillhart–Montgomery–Silverman
[
3
]
studied factorizations of Fibonacci and Lucas numbers for small
n
. Dubner–Keller
[
7
]
studied Fibonacci and Lucas numbers that are prime, and it remains open whether there are infinitely many such numbers.
Some works are also interested in determining all perfect powers in the Fibonacci and Lucas sequences.
For instance, Cohn
[
5
]
gave an elementary proof showing that
F_{0}=0
,
F_{1}=F_{2}=1
, and
F_{12}=144
are the only squares.
Bugeaud, Mignotte, and Siksek
[
4
]
showed that
0
,
1
,
8
, and
144
are the only perfect powers appearing in the Fibonacci sequence (see Section
2.1
).

An analogue of this problem can be formulated for
Fibonacci polynomials

F_{n}(T)\in\mathbb{Z}[T]
, the polynomial analogues of Fibonacci numbers, defined by

F_{0}(T)=0,\quad F_{1}(T)=1,\quad F_{n}(T)=TF_{n-1}(T)+F_{n-2}(T).

(1)

For example, several authors have studied the irreducibility of Fibonacci polynomials. Webb and Parberry showed that
F_{n}(T)
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
if and only if
n
is prime
[
20
]
.
Bergum and Hoggatt considered the analogous problem for Lucas polynomials and their generalizations in
[
1
]
.
In
[
11
]
, Kitayama and Shiomi studied irreducibility over finite fields, giving necessary and sufficient conditions for these polynomials to be irreducible over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

In this paper, we study Fibonacci polynomials over finite fields that are perfect powers, analogous to the work of Cohn
[
5
]
and Bugeaud–Mignotte–Siksek
[
4
]
.
In particular, we give a complete characterization of perfect powers.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
j>1
. Let
p
be an odd prime number coprime to
2j
,
q
be a power of
p
, and let
a=\mathrm{ord}_{2j}(p)
denote the order of
p
in
\left(\mathbb{Z}/2j\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times}
. For
n>0
,
F_{n}(T)
is a perfect
j
-th power in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
if and only if
n=p^{ak}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Similar result is available for
p=2
as well (Theorem
3.11
).
Moreover, we give a characterization of Fibonacci polynomials that are
powerful
, i.e. multiplicities of each irreducible factor are at least two.
1
1

1

Although we will mostly focus on nonconstant polynomials, constant polynomials will also be considered powerful, since they have no non-unit irreducible prime factors.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
p>3
be a prime and
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field of characteristic
p
.
Then
F_{n}(T)
is powerful in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
if and only if
p\mid n
.

Similar result holds for
p=2
and
p=3
as well (Corollary
3.13
and
3.12
).

These results contrast to the case of the original Fibonacci sequence.
There are only finitely many Fibonacci numbers that are perfect powers or powerful, where the second assertion is true under the ABC conjecture
[
17
,
22
]
.

In fact, we prove similar characterizations for certain
generalized Lucas polynomial sequences
of Horadam
[
9
]
, which generalize both Fibonacci polynomials and Lucas polynomials (see Definition
2.3
).
The proofs of these characterizations are based on the polynomial analogue of Binet’s formula and the discriminant formula of Florez, Higuita, and Ramirez
[
8
]
, except that certain small characteristic cases need separate considerations.
We also provide explicit factorizations of these polynomials for certain
p
’s, where the accompanying code can be found in
github.com/seewoo5/sage-function-field
.

2   Preliminaries

2.1   Diophantine equations with Fibonacci numbers

Diophantine equations involving Fibonacci numbers have been widely studied.
One such equation asks to find all perfect powers in the Fibonacci sequence, i.e. solutions for the equation

F_{n}=y^{p}

(2)

for prime
p
.
Cohn
[
5
]
proved that the only solutions for
p=2
are
F_{0}=0^{2},F_{1}=F_{2}=1^{2}
and
F_{12}=12^{2}
by only using elementary arguments, and a similar method was used to find all squares in the Lucas sequence
[
6
]
.
Subsequent papers studied (
2
) for larger
p
, such as
p=3

[
13
,
15
]
,
p=5

[
14
]
, and
p<5.1\cdot 10^{17}

[
16
]
.
In
[
4
]
, Bugeaud, Mignotte, and Siksek finally proved that these are all such numbers, using the modular method
[
21
,
19
]
and Baker’s theorem of linear forms
[
18
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
4
, Theorem 1, Theorem 2]
)
.

The only perfect powers in the Fibonacci sequence are
F_{0}=0,F_{1}=1,F_{2}=1,F_{6}=8,
and
F_{12}=144
.
Also, the only perfect powers in the Lucas sequence are
L_{1}=1
and
L_{3}=4
.

More generally, one can ask to characterize Fibonacci numbers that are
powerful
, i.e. the multiplicities of each prime factors are at least two.
Assuming ABC conjecture, it was shown that there are only finitely many powerful numbers in Fibonacci sequence
[
17
,
22
]
.

2.2   Fibonacci polynomials

Fibonacci polynomials (over any ring) are polynomial analogues of Fibonacci numbers, which are recursively defined as in (
1
).
The first few Fibonacci polynomials are:

F_{0}(T)=0,\quad F_{1}(T)=1,\quad F_{2}(T)=T,\quad F_{3}(T)=T^{2}+1,\quad F_{4}(T)=T^{3}+2T,\quad\dots

These polynomials satisfy the following analogue of the
Binet’s formula
.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
11
, Lemma 3.1]
)
.

Let
\alpha(T),\beta(T)
be the roots of
X^{2}-TX-1
.
Then

F_{n}(T)=\frac{\alpha(T)^{n}-\beta(T)^{n}}{\alpha(T)-\beta(T)}.

(3)

Over a characteristic
\neq 2
ring, we can write

\alpha(T)=\frac{T+\sqrt{T^{2}+4}}{2},\quad\beta(T)=\frac{T-\sqrt{T^{2}+4}}{2}

and

F_{n}(T)=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}T+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{T^{2}+4}\right)^{n}-\left(\frac{1}{2}T-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{T^{2}+4}\right)^{n}}{\sqrt{T^{2}+4}}\text{.}

(4)

It is known that the Fibonacci polynomial factors as
[
12
, p. 478]

F_{n}(T)=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(T-2i\cos\left(\frac{k\pi}{n}\right)\right)=\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(T-i(\zeta_{2n}^{k}+\zeta_{2n}^{-k}))

(5)

for
n\geq 2
, so the zeros of
F_{n}(T)
in
\mathbb{C}
are given by

2i\cos\left(\frac{k\pi}{n}\right)=i(\zeta_{2n}^{k}+\zeta_{2n}^{-k}),\quad 1\leq k\leq n-1

(6)

which are all algebraic integers.

2.3   Lucas polynomial sequence

We can consider more general families of polynomials by replacing the coefficient polynomials and initial terms in (
1
) by others, which are studied by Horadam
[
9
]
.

Definition 2.3

(
[
9
, Horadam]
)
.

Let
f(T)
and
g(T)
be monomials of degree 0 or 1 (over some ring R).
Define the following recurrence relation:

\mathcal{W}_{n}(T)=f(T)\mathcal{W}_{n-1}(T)+g(T)\mathcal{W}_{n-2}(T)\text{ for }n>1\text{,}

(7)

letting
\mathcal{W}_{0}(T)
be a constant in
R
and
\mathcal{W}_{1}(T)\in R[T]
be a monomial of degree 0 or 1.

Let
W_{n}(T)
and
w_{n}(T)
be polynomial sequences satisfying the recurrence relation of
\mathcal{W}_{n}(T)
and the initial conditions:

\displaystyle W_{0}(T)=0,

\displaystyle\quad W_{1}(T)=1,

\displaystyle w_{0}(T)=2,

\displaystyle\quad w_{1}(T)=f(T)\text{.}

These families are also called as
Fibonacci-type
and
Lucas-type
, respectively (which are denoted as
\mathcal{F}_{n}(T)
and
\mathcal{L}_{n}(T)
in
[
8
]
). For the remainder of this section, let
f(T)
and
g(T)
be monomials of degree
0
or
1
in
\mathbb{Q}[T]
of choice (for the most of the cases, they will be in
\mathbb{Z}[T]
) and let the sequences
W_{n}(T)
and
w_{n}(T)
be determined by
f(T)
and
g(T)
.

Continuing,
[
9
]
observes the
Binet forms
:

\displaystyle W_{n}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(T)^{n}-\beta(T)^{n}}{\Delta(T)}

(8)

\displaystyle w_{n}(T)

\displaystyle=\alpha(T)^{n}+\beta(T)^{n}

(9)

where

\displaystyle\alpha(T)

\displaystyle:=\frac{f(T)+\sqrt{f(T)^{2}+4g(T)}}{2}

(10)

\displaystyle\beta(T)

\displaystyle:=\frac{f(T)-\sqrt{f(T)^{2}+4g(T)}}{2}

(11)

\displaystyle\Delta(T)

\displaystyle:=\alpha(T)-\beta(T)=\sqrt{f(T)^{2}+4g(T)}\text{.}

(12)

When we work over characteristic 2, the equations (
10
) and (
11
) need to be replaced by
\alpha(T)=f(T)
and
\beta(T)=0
.

This recurrence relation generalizes many of the known polynomial sequences.
For example, when
f(T)=2T
and
g(T)=-1
(resp.
f(T)=1
and
g(T)=2T
), the polynomial
W_{n}(T)
becomes the Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind
U_{n-1}(T)
(resp. Jacobsthal polynomial
J_{n}(T)

[
10
]
).
When
f(T)=T
and
g(T)=1
, the polynomial
w_{n}(T)
becomes the
Lucas polynomial

L_{n}(T)
, i.e.

L_{0}(T)=2,\quad L_{1}(T)=T,\quad L_{n}(T)=TL_{n-1}(T)+L_{n-2}(T)

(13)

which is the polynomial analogue of the Lucas numbers.
See also
[
9
, Table 1]
for more examples.

3   Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
and
3.11
, completely characterizing Fibonacci polynomials over finite fields that are perfect powers. For the remainder of this paper, let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
denote a finite field of characteristic
p
. Also, we will mainly focus on nonconstant polynomials.

3.1   Factorization over finite fields

We first find the factorization of
F_{n}(T)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we have

F_{p^{k}}(T)=(T^{2}+4)^{\frac{p^{k}-1}{2}}

(14)

when
p
is odd, and

F_{2^{k}}(T)=T^{2^{k}-1}

(15)

when
p=2
.

Proof.

When
k=0
, this is trivial. Assume
k\geq 1
.
When
p
is odd, by (
4
)

F_{p^{k}}(T)=\frac{\alpha(T)^{p^{k}}-\beta(T)^{p^{k}}}{\alpha(T)-\beta(T)}=\frac{(\alpha(T)-\beta(T))^{p^{k}}}{\alpha(T)-\beta(T)}=(\alpha(T)-\beta(T))^{p^{k}-1}=(T^{2}+4)^{\frac{p^{k}-1}{2}}.

For
p=2
, we still have
(\alpha(T)-\beta(T))^{2}=(\alpha(T)+\beta(T))^{2}=T^{2}
and
\alpha(T)-\beta(T)=T
, so by (
3
)

F_{2^{k}}(T)=\frac{\alpha(T)^{2^{k}}-\beta(T)^{2^{k}}}{\alpha(T)-\beta(T)}=(\alpha(T)-\beta(T))^{2^{k}-1}=T^{2^{k}-1}.

Note that we are working over characteristic
p
, so
(\alpha-\beta)^{p^{k}}=\alpha^{p^{k}}-\beta^{p^{k}}
holds for any
k\geq 1
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Write
n=p^{k}m
where
m
is coprime to
p
. Then
F_{n}(T)
factors over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
as

F_{p^{k}m}(T)=F_{p^{k}}(T)F_{m}(T)^{p^{k}}.

(16)

Proof.

By (
3
),

\displaystyle F_{n}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(T)^{p^{k}m}-\beta(T)^{p^{k}m}}{\alpha(T)-\beta(T)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\alpha(T)^{m}-\beta(T)^{m})^{p^{k}}}{\alpha(T)-\beta(T)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\alpha(T)-\beta(T))^{p^{k}}}{\alpha(T)-\beta(T)}\left(\frac{\alpha(T)^{m}-\beta(T)^{m}}{\alpha(T)-\beta(T)}\right)^{p^{k}}

\displaystyle=F_{p^{k}}(T)F_{m}(T)^{p^{k}}.

∎

By Lemma
3.1
and
3.2
, we get:

Proposition 3.3
.

Write
n=p^{k}m
where
m
is coprime to
p
. Then
F_{n}(T)
factors over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
as

F_{p^{k}m}(T)=(T^{2}+4)^{\frac{p^{k}-1}{2}}F_{m}(T)^{p^{k}}.

(17)

When
p=2
, the above factorization becomes

F_{2^{k}m}(T)=T^{2^{k}-1}F_{m}(T)^{2^{k}}.

(18)

3.2   Square-free factor

By Proposition
3.3
, it is enough to understand the factorization of
F_{m}(T)
for
p\nmid m
.
For odd
p
, the following shows that they are square-free.

Proposition 3.4
.

Assume that
p
is odd and
p\nmid m
. Then
F_{m}(T)
is square-free over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

We provide two different proofs of Proposition
3.4
.
The first proof is based on the discriminant formula of Florez–Higuita–Ramirez
[
8
]
.

First proof of Proposition
3.4
.

Recall that a polynomial over a field has no multiple root in an algebraic closure if and only if the distriminant is nonzero.
The discriminant of
F_{m}(T)
is given by
[
8
, Theorem 4]

\mathrm{Disc}(F_{m})=(-1)^{\frac{(m-2)(m-1)}{2}}2^{m-1}m^{m-3}

and it is nonzero in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
when
2\neq p\nmid m
, so
F_{m}(T)
is square-free.
∎

The second proof directly shows that the zeros (
6
) are all distinct modulo
p
(i.e. distinct in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
).

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
n>1
and
p\nmid n
. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
with
0<|k|<n
,

\zeta_{2n}^{k}\not\equiv 1\pmod{p},

i.e.
\zeta_{2n}^{k}-1
is nonzero in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.

Proof.

Let:

f(T):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}-n<k\leq n\\
k\neq 0,\text{ }k\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}(T-\zeta_{2n}^{k})\text{.}

When
k=n
, we have
\zeta_{2n}^{n}=-1\not\equiv 1\pmod{p}
. Suppose
\zeta_{2n}^{k}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
for some
k
such that
0<|k|<n
, then
f(1)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
.
Notice since:

\displaystyle T^{2n}-1

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}-n<k\leq n\\
k\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}(T-\zeta_{2n}^{k})=(T-1)f(T)\text{,}

we have:

\displaystyle f(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{T^{2n}-1}{T-1}=\underbrace{T^{2n-1}+T^{2n-2}+\dots+T+1}_{2n\text{ terms}}\text{.}

From this we get
f(1)=2n
and
f(1)\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, contradicting
p\nmid n
.
∎

Second proof of Proposition
3.4
.

If
m=1
, then
F_{1}(T)=1
is square-free by definition. Assume
m>1
.
Suppose
F_{m}(T)
is not square-free, so that it has a multiple zero in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.
By (
6
), there exist distinct
k,l\in\{1,2,\dots,m-1\}
such that
\zeta_{2m}^{k}+\zeta_{2m}^{-k}=\zeta_{2m}^{l}+\zeta_{2m}^{-l}
in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.
Then
(\zeta_{2m}^{k}-\zeta_{2m}^{l})(\zeta_{2m}^{k+l}-1)=0
, hence
\zeta_{2m}^{k}=\zeta_{2m}^{l}
or
\zeta_{2m}^{k}=\zeta_{2m}^{-l}
.
By Lemma
3.5
, we have
k\equiv l\pmod{2m}
or
k\equiv-l\pmod{2m}
, which are both impossible.
∎

Next, we show that
F_{m}(T)
for
p\nmid m
is always coprime to
T^{2}+4
, hence
(T^{2}+4)^{\frac{p^{k}-1}{2}}
and
F_{m}(T)^{p^{k}}
in (
17
) do not share common factors. Knowing that the recurrence relation of the Fibonacci polynomials is
Fibonacci-type
, we can apply the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6

(
[
8
, Lemma 19]
)
.

For
n\geq 0
, we have

W_{n}\pmod{f^{2}+4g}=\begin{cases}n(-g)^{(n-1)/2}&2\nmid n\\
(-1)^{(n+2)/2}(nfg^{(n-2)/2})/2&2\mid n\end{cases},

(19)

where
f
and
g
are the coefficient polynomials in (
7
).

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
p
be an odd prime. We have

F_{n}(T)\pmod{T^{2}+4}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}n&2\nmid n\\
(-1)^{\frac{n}{2}+1}\frac{nT}{2}&2\mid n\end{cases}

(20)

over a field of characteristic
p
.

Proof.

This is a special case of Lemma
3.6
, when
f(T)=T
and
g(T)=1
.
Note that 2 is invertible in a field of odd characteristic.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

Suppose
p\nmid m
. Then,
T^{2}+4
and
F_{m}(T)
are coprime in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
.

Proof.

Assume
p
is an odd prime.
Suppose
T^{2}+4
and
F_{m}(T)
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
share a factor. Then, there must exist some common zero of the polynomial, which we will denote as
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.
Then
\alpha^{2}+4=0
and
F_{m}(\alpha)=0
.
However, such
\alpha
cannot exist by Corollary
3.7
; if
m
is even, then
F_{m}(\alpha)=(-1)^{\frac{m}{2}+1}\frac{m}{2}\alpha
and it is nonzero since
m\neq 0
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and the case where
m
is odd follows similarly.

For
p=2
,
F_{n}(0)=F_{n-2}(0)
for all
n\geq 2
, so
F_{m}(0)=F_{1}(0)=1
for all odd
m
. Hence
T^{2}+4=T^{2}
and
F_{m}(T)
are coprime for odd
m
.
∎

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
p=2
. For
n>0
,
F_{n}(T)
is square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
if and only if
n
is odd.

Proof.

This immediately follows from
[
11
, Lemma 3.5]
; we have
F_{2k}(T)=TF_{k}(T)^{2}
and
F_{2k+1}(T)=(F_{k+1}(T)+F_{k}(T))^{2}
, which can be proven by (
3
).
∎

Finally, before proceeding to the main theorem, we note the following result.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
p
be an odd prime number. If
j>1
and
p\mid j
, then there exists no
n
such that
F_{n}(T)
is a perfect
j
-th power in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
.

Proof.

Suppose
j>1
and
p\mid j
. Assume there exists some
n=p^{k}m
with
k\geq 0
and
p\nmid m
such that
F_{n}(T)
is a perfect
j
-th power in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
.
If
k=0
, then
p\nmid m
and
F_{n}(T)=F_{m}(T)
is square-free by Proposition
3.4
, which cannot be a perfect
j
-th power for
j>1
, so
k\geq 1
.
By Lemma
3.2
, we have
F_{p^{k}m}(T)=(T^{2}+4)^{\frac{p^{k}-1}{2}}F_{m}(T)^{p^{k}}
, where
F_{m}(T)
is coprime to
T^{2}+4
by Corollary
3.8
.
Then
j
should divide the exponent
\frac{p^{k}-1}{2}
, which contradicts to
p\mid j
.
∎

3.3   Proof of the main theorem

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

(\Leftarrow)
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary. By Lemma
3.1
,
F_{p^{ak}}(T)=(T^{2}+4)^{\frac{p^{ak}-1}{2}}
, and by our assumption,
p^{a}\equiv 1\pmod{2j}
and thus
\frac{p^{ak}-1}{2}\equiv 0\pmod{j}
. Therefore,
F_{n}(T)
where
n=p^{ak}
is a perfect
j
-th power.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose
F_{n}(T)
is a perfect
j
-th power in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
and write
n=p^{ak+b}m
, where
p
and
m
are coprime (so
p\nmid m
),
k\geq 0
, and
0\leq b<a
. By Proposition
3.3
, we have

F_{p^{ak+b}m}(T)=(T^{2}+4)^{\frac{p^{ak+b}-1}{2}}\left(F_{m}(T)\right)^{p^{ak+b}}\text{.}

Suppose
b>0
. Then,
p^{ak+b}=p^{ak}p^{b}\equiv p^{b}\pmod{2j}
, where
p^{b}\not\equiv 1\pmod{2j}
by the assumption that
0<b<a
. This implies that
\frac{p^{ak+b}-1}{2}\not\equiv 0\pmod{j}
, so
(T^{2}+4)^{\frac{p^{ak+b}-1}{2}}
is not a perfect
j
-th power.
Since
T^{2}+4
and
F_{m}(T)
are coprime by Corollary
3.8
, we get a contradiction and we should have
b=0
.
We can rewrite
F_{n}(T)
as

F_{p^{ak}m}(T)=(T^{2}+4)^{\frac{p^{ak}-1}{2}}\left(F_{m}(T)\right)^{p^{ak}}\text{,}

where
(T^{2}+4)^{\frac{p^{ak}-1}{2}}
is a perfect
j
-th power and
F_{m}(T)
is square-free by Proposition
3.4
. If
m\neq 1
, then
F_{m}(T)\neq 1
and by Corollary
3.8
, the exponent
p^{ak}
needs to be a multiple of
j>1
, which contradicts to
\gcd(p,2j)=1
.
This implies
m=1
and we have
n=p^{ak}
.
∎

When
p=2
, we already showed that
F_{n}(T)
for
n>0
is square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
if and only if
n
is odd. We can further determine higher powers in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
j>2
be an odd number and
a=\mathrm{ord}_{j}(2)
be the order of
2
in
(\mathbb{Z}/j\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
.
Let
q
be a power of
2
.
Then
F_{n}(T)
is a perfect
j
-th power in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
if and only if
n=2^{ak}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

If
n=2^{ak}
, then
2^{a}\equiv 1\pmod{j}
and (
15
) shows that
F_{n}(T)=T^{2^{ak}-1}=(T^{\frac{2^{ak}-1}{j}})^{j}
is a perfect
j
-th power.
Conversely, assume that
n=2^{s}\cdot m
for
s\geq 0
and odd
m
.
From (
1
), one can easily check that
F_{m}(0)=1
for all odd
m
.
In particular,
\gcd(T,F_{m}(T))=1
.
If
m>1
, then by (
18
)
F_{n}(T)
cannot be a perfect
j
-th power.
Hence
m=1
and
F_{n}(T)=F_{2^{s}}(T)=T^{2^{s}-1}
, which is a perfect
j
-th power if and only if
2^{s}\equiv 1\pmod{j}\Leftrightarrow s\equiv 0\pmod{a}
.
∎

Similarly, we can also prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

(\Rightarrow)
If
p\nmid n
, then
F_{n}(T)
is square-free and cannot be a powerful polynomial.

(\Leftarrow)
If
n=p^{k}m
with
k\geq 1
, then
F_{n}(T)
is powerful by (
17
).
Here we use
p>3
to guarantee
\frac{p^{k}-1}{2}>1
.
∎

We need extra care for
p=3
, since we can have
\frac{p^{k}-1}{2}=1
when
k=1
.

Corollary 3.12
.

For

,
F_{n}(T)
is powerful over

if and only if
9\mid n
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Theorem
1.2
, except that we need
k\geq 2
to have
\frac{3^{k}-1}{2}>1
.
∎

Similar characterization is also available for
p=2
.

Corollary 3.13
.

For
p=2
,
F_{n}(T)
is powerful over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if and only if
n\geq 3
is odd or a multiple of
4
.

Proof.

By (

) and
[
11
, Lemma 3.5]
, when
n=2^{k}m
with odd

, we have

F_{n}(T)=T^{2^{k}-1}F_{m}(T)^{2^{k}}=T^{2^{k}-1}(F_{(m+1)/2}(T)+F_{(m-1)/2}(T))^{2^{k+1}}.

If
k=0
and
n=m\geq 3
is odd, then
F_{n}(T)=(F_{(m+1)/2}(T)+F_{(m-1)/2}(T))^{2}
is a square of a non-constant polynomial, hence powerful.
Similarly,
F_{n}(T)
is powerful when
k\geq 2
.
However, if
k=1
, then
F_{n}(T)=TF_{m}(T)^{2}
is not powerful, since
T
and
F_{m}(T)
are coprime and
F_{n}(T)
is divisible by

only once.
∎

4   Generalization

In this section, we generalize the results for the Fibonacci polynomials to
W_{n}(T)

with
\deg f(T)=1
and
\deg g(T)=0
, including the Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind (Example
4.13
). We also find all the polynomials in the Lucas-type sequence
w_{n}(T)
which are perfect powers.

First, we need the following factorization lemmas, similar to Lemma
3.1
and
3.2
.
We skip the details since the proofs are similar.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
p
be a prime and
n=p^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
W_{n}(T)=\Delta(T)^{n-1}
and
w_{n}(T)=f(T)^{n}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
n=p^{k}m
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,
W_{n}(T)=W_{p^{k}}(T)W_{m}(T)^{p^{k}}=\Delta(T)^{p^{k}-1}W_{m}(T)^{p^{k}}
and
w_{n}(T)=w_{m}(T)^{p^{k}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
.
In particular,
w_{n}(T)
is a perfect power if and only if it is powerful.

We also have the following coprimality result analogous to Corollary
3.8
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose
p
is an odd prime and
p\nmid m\cdot\mathrm{lc}(f)\cdot g
. Then
f(T)^{2}+4g(T)
and
W_{m}(T)
are coprime in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
.

Proof.

Suppose
f(T)^{2}+4g(T)
and
W_{m}(T)
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
share a factor. Then, there must exist some common zero of the polynomial, which we will denote as
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.
Then
f(\alpha)^{2}+4g(\alpha)=0
and
W_{m}(\alpha)=0
.
However, such
\alpha
cannot exist by Lemma
3.6
.
∎

We will now let
\deg f=1
and
\deg g=0
in the recursive definition of
W_{n}(T)
and
w_{n}(T)
(see Definition
2.3
). Using the discriminant formulas in
[
8
]
, we can show the following results analogous to the case of Fibonacci polynomials.

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose
p
is odd and
p\nmid m\cdot\mathrm{lc}(f)\cdot g
, where
\mathrm{lc}(f)
is the leading coefficient of
f(T)
. Then,
W_{m}(T)
and
w_{m}(T)
are square-free over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Proof.

This follows from the discriminant formulas for
W_{m}
and
w_{m}

[
8
, Theorem 4, Theorem 5]
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Disc}(W_{m})

\displaystyle=(-g)^{\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{2}}2^{m-1}m^{m-3}\mathrm{lc}(f)^{(m-1)(m-2)},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Disc}(w_{m})

\displaystyle=(-g)^{\frac{m(m-1)}{2}}2^{m-1}m^{m}\mathrm{lc}(f)^{m(m-1)}\text{.}

In particular, the discriminants are nonzero for
p\nmid m\cdot\mathrm{lc}(f)\cdot g
, and
W_{m}
and
w_{m}
are thus square-free over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
p
be an odd prime number and assume
p\nmid\mathrm{lc}(f)\cdot g
. If
j>1
and
p\mid j
, then there exists no
n
such that
W_{n}(T)
is a perfect
j
-th power in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
.

Proof.

One can proceed as in the proof of Theorem
3.10
.
Suppose
W_{p^{k}m}(T)
is a perfect
j
-th power for some
k\geq 0
and
m
not divisible by
p
.
If
k=0
,
W_{p^{k}m}(T)=W_{m}(T)
is square-free by Proposition
4.4
, which cannot be a perfect
j
-th power for
j>1
.
Hence
k\geq 1
, and Lemma
4.2
gives
W_{p^{k}m}(T)=(f(T)^{2}+4g(T))^{\frac{p^{k}-1}{2}}W_{m}(T)^{p^{k}}
, where the coprimality of
f^{2}+4g
and
W_{m}
(Proposition
4.3
) implies that the exponent
\frac{p^{k}-1}{2}
has to be a multiple of
j
.
This contradicts to
p\mid j
.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
j>1
. Let
p
be an odd prime number coprime to
2j
, and let
a=\mathrm{ord}_{2j}(p)
denote the order of
p
in
\left(\mathbb{Z}/2j\mathbb{Z}\right)^{\times}
. Suppose
p\nmid\mathrm{lc}(f)\cdot g
. For
n>0
,
W_{n}(T)
is a perfect
j
-th power in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
if and only if
n=p^{ak}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary. By Lemma
4.1
,
W_{p^{ak}}(T)=\Delta(T)^{p^{ak}-1}=\left(f(T)^{2}+4g(T)\right)^{\frac{p^{ak}-1}{2}}
. Because
p^{a}\equiv 1\pmod{2j}
and thus
\frac{p^{ak}-1}{2}\equiv 0\mod j
,
W_{p^{ak}}(T)
is a perfect
j
-th power.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose
W_{n}(T)
is a perfect
j
-th power in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
and write
n=p^{ak+b}m
, where
p
and
m
are coprime (so
p\nmid m
),
k\geq 0
, and
0\leq b<a
. With Lemma
4.2
, we may write
W_{n}(T)
as follows:

\displaystyle W_{p^{ak+b}m}(T)

\displaystyle=W_{p^{ak+b}}(T)\left(W_{m}(T)\right)^{p^{ak+b}}=\left(f(T)^{2}+4g(T)\right)^{\frac{p^{ak+b}-1}{2}}\left(W_{m}(T)\right)^{p^{ak+b}}

Suppose
b>0
. Then,
p^{ak+b}\not\equiv 1\pmod{2j}
by the assumption
b<a
and thus
\frac{p^{ak+b}-1}{2}\not\equiv 0\pmod{j}
. So
W_{p^{ak+b}}(T)
is not a perfect
j
-th power. Then,
W_{p^{ak+b}m}(T)
is not a perfect
j
-th power since
W_{p^{ak+b}}(T)
and
W_{m}(T)
are coprime by Proposition
4.3
. We thus let
b=0
and rewrite
W_{n}(T)
as follows:

W_{p^{ak}m}(T)=W_{p^{ak}}(T)\left(W_{m}(T)\right)^{p^{ak}}\text{,}

where
W_{p^{ak}}(T)
is a perfect
j
-th power and
W_{m}(T)
is square-free by Proposition
4.4
. Then, if
m\neq 1
, then
W_{m}(T)\neq 1
and by Proposition
4.3

j>1
must divide
p^{ak}
, which is a contradiction to
\gcd(p,2j)=1
. We thus have
m=1
and
n=p^{ak}
.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

Suppose
p>3
and assume
p\nmid\mathrm{lc}(f)\cdot g
.
Then
W_{n}(T)
is powerful in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[T]
if and only if
n
is a multiple of
p
.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
If
p\nmid n
, then
W_{n}(T)
is square-free and cannot be a powerful polynomial.

(\Leftarrow)
If
n=p^{k}m
with
k\geq 1
, then
W_{n}(T)
is powerful by Lemma
4.2
.
∎

When
p=2
, we have the following results, generalizing Theorem
3.11
and Corollary
3.13
.

Lemma 4.8
.

For each
n
, there exist integers
\{c_{n,k}\}_{0\leq k\leq\frac{n-1}{2}}
such that

W_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}c_{n,k}f^{n-1-2k}g^{k}

(21)

where
c_{n,(n-1)/2}=1
for odd

.
In particular,
f\mid W_{n}
for even
n
.
When
n
is odd and

\gcd(W_{n},f)=1
.

Proof.

The equation (
21

, and the following claims immediately follow from it.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
q
be a power of 2 and

1.

W_{2n}(T)=f(T)W_{n}(T)^{2}

W_{2n+1}(T)=(W_{n+1}(T)+gW_{n}(T))^{2}

Proof.

The proof for

in
[

generalizes.
The first claim follows from the Binet form (

) and

For the second claim, we have

(here we used

), and the result follows.
