Association between total bilirubin levels and the risk of acute kidney injury: a large hospital- based cohort study
Authors: Youlu Zhao, Shuo Xue, Damin Xu, Lingyi Xu, Qingqing Zhou, Linger Tang, Yuhui Zhang, Jicheng Lv, Li Yang, Jinwei Wang, Xizi Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a common hospital-acquired condition linked to oxidative stress and poor outcomes. Bilirubin exerts dual, level-dependent effects, acting as an antioxidant within mildly elevated levels yet becoming cytotoxic when markedly elevated. While moderate bilirubin elevation shows long-term kidney benefits, its association with AKI remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included adult patients admitted between 2018 - 2020 with total bilirubin (TBIL) below 1.5 times the upper limit of normal (<30 μmol/L). Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a prevalent and severe complication among hospitalized patients [1], and its pathogenesis is closely linked to oxidative stress. This connection highlights the potential protective role of endogenous antioxidants [2]. Bilirubin, the end product of heme metabolism, is a potent endogenous antioxidant. It has been shown to confer protective effects when present at normal to mildly elevated levels [3].

Evidence from experimental models confirms this beneficial role; for instance, hyaluronic acid–coated bilirubin nanoparticles have been shown to attenuate ischemia–reperfusion–induced AKI in rats [4], and bilirubin administration has been demonstrated to attenuate the progression of diabetic kidney disease in rats [5]. Consistent with these experimental findings, observational studies in humans have linked mildly elevated bilirubin levels with improved long-term kidney outcomes in patients with chronic conditions such as chronic kidney disease [6], diabetes [7], and hyperuricemia [8].

In contrast, severely elevated total bilirubin (TBIL)levels—typically exceeding 1.5 times the upper limit of normal [9]—are a well-recognized risk factor for AKI and have been consistently associated with adverse renal outcomes [8]. This transition is attributed to a shift in the biological effects of bilirubin, whereby its protective antioxidant properties give way to direct cytotoxicity, particularly through injury to renal tubular cells [10, 11]. This duality raises a clinically relevant question of whether the long-term kidney benefit associated with subtoxic bilirubin levels extends to a reduced risk of AKI in the acute, in-hospital setting [10]. To address this gap, this study specifically investigates the association between TBIL levels within this range and the development of in-hospital AKI, thereby exploring its complex role during acute hospitalization.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Peking University First Hospital. We enrolled adult patients admitted between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2020, who had a TBIL level below 1.5 times the upper limit of normal (<30 μmol/L) measured within 24 hours of admission. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe liver diseases, including liver cirrhosis and biliary tract disease; (2) haemochromatosis or Gilbert’s syndrome; (3) progressive malignancies; (4) fewer than two serum creatinine (SCr) measurements; (5) end-stage kidney disease or maintenance dialysis; (6) nephrectomy or kidney transplantation during hospitalization; (7) highest SCr < 53 μmol/L [12, 13] from 90 days before admission until discharge; (8) initial SCr > 353.6 μmol/L at admission; (9) death or AKI within 24 hours of admission. Only the first admission was considered for patients with multiple hospitalizations. The selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

Data collection

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from the electronic medical records in the clinical data warehouse of Peking University First Hospital. The dataset included demographic characteristics, pre-existing comorbidities, concomitant medications, laboratory test results, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, and surgical information. Baseline was defined as the first 24 hours after hospital admission, during which baseline laboratory values, comorbidities, and early clinical exposures were collected.

Study endpoints

Patients were followed until discharge or 90 days after admission, whichever came first. The primary endpoint was in-hospital AKI, defined by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) SCr criteria [14]: SCr increase ≥ 26.5 μmol/L within 48 hours or ≥1.5 times the baseline within 7 days. The secondary endpoint was in-hospital mortality.

Statistical analysis

TBIL was additionally dichotomized at the cohort median to define low- and high-TBIL groups for between-group comparisons. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the chi-square test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (25th–75th percentile) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) within Cox regression models were used to examine the potential nonlinear association between TBIL levels, treated as a continuous variable, and the risk of in-hospital AKI. The optimal number of knots was determined based on the lowest Akaike Information Criterion. Covariates were selected based on previous literature [15–17] and clinical relevance, including demographic characteristics (age and sex), laboratory tests including estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), serum albumin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), hemoglobin, and white blood cell (WBC); comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, lung disease), treatment-related factors (surgery, contrast exposure, nephrotoxic drugs, hepatotoxic drugs). The eGFR was derived from admission SCr according to the 2009 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [18].

Cox regression was performed to evaluate the association between TBIL, modeled as a continuous variable, and the hazards of in-hospital AKI and mortality using sequentially adjusted models. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 incorporated demographic and eGFR adjustments; Model 3 added adjustments for serum albumin; and the fully adjusted Model 4 included additional laboratory tests, comorbidities and treatment-related factors. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modifications by age (<75 vs. ≥75 years old), sex (female vs. male), eGFR (≥60 vs. <60 mL/min/1.73 m2), diabetes mellitus (no vs. yes), WBC (<10 vs. ≥10 × 109/L), abnormal liver function (no vs. yes), direct bilirubin (DBIL)/indirect bilirubin (IBIL) ratio < 0.2 (no vs. yes), and serum albumin (≥35 vs. <35 g/L). Abnormal liver function was defined as ALT > 40 IU/L or AST > 35 IU/L, based on the laboratory’s upper limits of normal. A DBIL/IBIL ratio cutoff of 0.2 was applied, consistent with the institutional reference range (0.2–0.4) and previous reports [19]. For sensitivity analysis, TBIL was analyzed as a continuous variable, and the association with in-hospital AKI was re-examined using Cox proportional hazards models with the same covariate adjustments as in the primary analysis.

A post hoc power analysis was performed to assess the statistical power of the study. Given the overall large sample size (n = 29,261), the study had statistical power of >99% to detect a hazard ratio of 1.05 per 1 μmol/L increase in serum TBIL for the primary outcome of in-hospital AKI (n = 785), at a two-sided α = 0.05. For the secondary outcome of in-hospital mortality (n = 79, mortality rate 0.27%), the corresponding statistical power was 59%.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The key R packages utilized included survival, rms, ggplot2, and dplyr. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 29,261 patients were included in the study (Fig. 1). The median age of the overall cohort was 60 (interquartile range 46—70) years, and 55.11% were male. The mean serum TBIL level in the overall cohort was 12.87 ± 5.06 μmol/L. Using the median TBIL value of 12.1 μmol/L as the cutoff, patients were categorized into high- and low-TBIL groups. Compared to the low-TBIL group, patients in the high-TBIL group were significantly older (median 62 vs. 57 years) and had a higher proportion of males (66.1% vs. 43.9%).

The high-TBIL group demonstrated significantly higher levels of serum albumin, AST, ALT, and hemoglobin, but lower WBC counts (all p < 0.001). This group also had a higher prevalence of reduced kidney function (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 : 27.4% vs. 26.3%) and comorbidities including hypertension and coronary heart disease (both p < 0.001). In addition, these patients experienced greater exposure to surgery, contrast, nephrotoxic drugs, and hepatotoxic drugs (all p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Association between total bilirubin and in-hospital AKI

The overall incidence of in-hospital AKI was 2.68% (785/29,261). Unadjusted RCS analysis revealed a U-shaped relationship between TBIL and the risk of in-hospital AKI (P-overall = 0.004, P-nonlinear = 0.002), with the lowest risk observed within the TBIL range of 10.1–12.1 μmol/L (Fig. 2A). However, this nonlinear pattern was no longer evident after adjusting for all covariates (p-overall = 0.001, p-nonlinear = 0.09) (Fig. 2B).

Restricted cubic spline curves showing the association between serum total bilirubin levels and the hazard ratio for in-hospital AKI (A) Unadjusted model. (B) Fully adjusted model controlling for age, sex, laboratory tests (eGFR, serum albumin, ALT, AST, hemoglobin, WBC), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, lung disease), surgical procedures, contrast exposure, nephrotoxic drugs, and hepatotoxic drugs

When TBIL was analyzed as an ordinal variable by quartiles in the Cox model, no significant association with in-hospital AKI was observed in the unadjusted model (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.92–1.05) or after adjustment for age, sex, and eGFR (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.95–1.09) (Table 2). Notably, further adjustment for serum albumin yielded a significant positive association (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.07–1.22), which remained consistent in the fully adjusted model (Model 4, HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.06–1.22). Additionally, a positive association between TBIL and AKI was also observed in a model that did not include albumin (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.16), and a moderate positive correlation between TBIL and serum albumin was noted (Spearman r = 0.332, p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Association between total bilirubin and in-hospital mortality

The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 0.27% (79/29,261). When causes of death were categorized and compared between high-TBIL and low-TBIL groups (detailed in Supplementary Table 1), cardiovascular disease and sepsis emerged as the most common causes in both groups. Patients with higher TBIL levels had numerically higher proportions of cardiovascular deaths (40.0% vs. 30.8%) and sepsis-related deaths (27.5% vs. 23.1%), whereas deaths due to respiratory diseases were less frequent in this group (10.0% vs. 23.1%). Overall, no statistically significant difference in the distribution of causes of death was observed between the two groups (overall p = 0.46).

In the Cox regression analysis (Table 2), TBIL was significantly associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality in the unadjusted model (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.04–1.50). After adjustment for age, sex and eGFR (Model 2), the association attenuated and became non-significant (HR 1.18, 95% CI 0.97–1.43). Further adjustment for serum albumin (Model 3) revealed a strengthened positive association (HR 1.40, 95% CI 1.15–1.71), which remained significant in the fully adjusted model (Model 4, HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.02–1.54).

Discussion

Based on a large cohort of 29,261 hospitalized patients, this study demonstrated that even within the subtoxic range (<1.5 times the upper limit of normal), elevated TBIL is independently associated with increased risks of in-hospital AKI and mortality. While RCS initially suggested a U-shaped relationship between TBIL and in-hospital AKI risk, the nonlinear component was attenuated after multivariable adjustment.

The dual biological nature of bilirubin complicates its clinical interpretation. Although mild elevations may confer antioxidant benefits, our findings suggest that, in hospitalized patients, TBIL levels within this subtoxic range remain positively associated with the risk of in-hospital AKI. This contrasts with previous studies on bilirubin and AKI, which had largely focused on contrast-induced acute kidney injury (CI-AKI) and consistently reported protective effects. For example, in a cohort of 544 patients undergoing coronary intervention, those with normal (8.55—11.97 μmol/L) or low TBIL (≤8.55 μmol/L) had significantly higher risks of CI-AKI than those with high TBIL (>11.97 μmol/L) (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 4.83 and 11.82, respectively) [20]; similarly, another study reported significantly lower TBIL levels in patients with CI-AKI (8.4 ± 5.1 vs. 11.0 ± 3.4 μmol/L; p < 0.001) [21].

Several methodological differences may account for this discrepancy, including variations in study populations, TBIL ranges, and covariate adjustment. Unlike CI-AKI, which is predominantly driven by oxidative stress following contrast exposure [22, 23], our study encompassed a broader spectrum of in-hospital AKI with multifactorial pathophysiology. This difference is compounded by the distinct TBIL ranges across studies: patients in the two CI-AKI investigations had lower TBIL levels (median estimated 9–11.5 μmol/L) than those in our cohort (median 12.1 μmol/L). In this more complex clinical context where AKI etiologies extend beyond oxidative stress, the antioxidant effect of bilirubin may become less prominent, giving way to its direct cytotoxicity [24] or its link to subclinical cholestasis and inflammation [1]. Notably, our study employed a more comprehensive adjustment approach by including serum albumin and liver enzymes, which were not fully accounted for in previous CI-AKI studies [20, 21].

Interestingly, in the Cox regression analysis evaluating the association between TBIL and AKI, the crude comparison showed an opposite direction to the adjusted association. This pattern often occurs when a confounder is associated with the exposure and the outcome in opposite directions. In our dataset, serum albumin emerged as such a confounder. Albumin is a well-established protective factor for AKI [25] and was positively correlated with TBIL, thereby masking the underlying adverse association in unadjusted comparisons. After adjustment for albumin and other confounders, the independent adverse association between TBIL and AKI became apparent. This interpretation is further supported by the role of albumin as a marker of baseline health status— a common upstream determinant of both bilirubin metabolism and renal susceptibility [26, 27]. Accordingly, adjustment for albumin was necessary to clarify the independent association between TBIL and AKI, a conclusion reinforced by our additional analyses showing that the positive association between TBIL and AKI was present even without adjustment for albumin but became slightly stronger after its inclusion.

Beyond its association with AKI, higher TBIL levels were also associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality, consistent with previous reports in critically ill populations. In a large Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III)–based cohort, patients with TBIL ≥ 34.2 μmol/L had a significantly higher risk of in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR 1.654, 95% CI 1.31–2.09) [28]. Supporting evidence from the general population (the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, NHANES) further links higher TBIL to increased all-cause mortality (HR 1.59, 95% CI, 1.46–1.72) [29]. Regarding causes of death, although the overall distribution did not differ significantly between TBIL groups in our cohort, patients with higher TBIL (≥12 μmol/L) had numerically higher proportions of cardiovascular- and sepsis-related deaths. This pattern aligns with prior evidence linking elevated bilirubin to increased cardiovascular mortality after acute myocardial infarction [30] and to higher mortality in sepsis [31]. Given that TBIL is routinely measured at admission, its elevation may facilitate risk stratification for in-hospital AKI and mortality, enabling early identification and timely intervention.

This large-scale study provides the first evidence from a general hospitalized population that TBIL, even within the mildly elevated range, is independently associated with an increased risk of in-hospital AKI. RCS analysis further suggested a positive association between TBIL levels and AKI risk. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the observational study design precludes causal inference between TBIL and outcomes. Second, despite comprehensive adjustment for confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured factors may still exist. Third, the reliance on a single TBIL measurement at admission limits our understanding of how dynamic changes in bilirubin levels might influence AKI risk over time. Fourth, despite the large sample size, the relatively low number of in-hospital death events may have limited statistical power to detect modest associations between TBIL and mortality. Fifth, AKI was defined using the SCr criterion only as urine output data were not consistently available outside ICU settings [32]. Future research should incorporate longitudinal monitoring of bilirubin and kidney function to elucidate the temporal dynamics and underlying mechanisms of bilirubin in AKI pathogenesis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this large-scale study demonstrated that higher TBIL, even within the subtoxic range, was independently associated with increased risks of in-hospital AKI and mortality in a general hospitalized population. Importantly, serum albumin was identified as a critical confounder of this relationship. These findings highlight the context-dependent nature of bilirubin’s biological effects and support its potential utility as a marker for AKI risk stratification.
