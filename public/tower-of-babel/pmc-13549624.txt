Two-Staged Resection of an Infantile Spinal Epidural Teratoma With Intrathoracic Extension: Surgical Case Report With Literature Review
Authors: Tae-Kyun Kim, Yasuhiro Kuroda, Mai Watakabe, Young-Soo Park, Ryota Sasaki, Kenta Nakase, Yudai Morisaki, Ryuta Matsuoka, Shohei Yokoyama, Masashi Kotsugi, Yasuhiro Takeshima, Shuichi Yamada, Fumihiko Nishimura, Ichiro Nakagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES:Spinal epidural teratoma (SET) is a rare spinal tumor, particularly in the pediatric population. Because of its rarity and potential for extensive growth into both intraspinal and extraspinal compartments, surgical strategies are not well-standardized. This study aims to describe a successful 2-stage surgical approach for an extensive infantile SET and review the existing literature.METHODS:A 1-month-old male infant presented with a large SET extending from T4 to L2 and into the posterior mediastinum. A two-stage surgical approach was performed to achieve gross total resection (GTR). The intraspinal tumor was removed using a split-spinous process laminotomy from T7 to L3. This technique used the natural flexibility of the infant's vertebral arches to create an “open-door” access without detaching the paravertebral muscles. The second surgery was performed 1 month later for the remnant tumor located inside the posterior mediastinal space. The intrathoracic tumor was removed using a thoracoscope with only three 5-mm ports and a small incision for specimen extraction.RESULTS:Histopathology confirmed a mature teratoma. Postoperative MRI demonstrated GTR. The patient remained neurologically intact and was discharged without respiratory or surgical complications. At the 16-month follow-up, there was no evidence of recurrence or spinal deformity. A literature review identified only 10 previous cases of pediatric SET, highlighting a male predominance and a high frequency of multilevel extension. Paraparesis was common in most cases, whereas 2 infants presented with dyspnea because of mediastinal compression. Histologically, all except 1 case were mature teratomas, and all cases treated with GTR remained recurrence-free.CONCLUSION:GTR is the gold standard for pediatric SET because of its histologically mature nature. A staged approach with “open-door” split-spinous process laminotomy and thoracoscopic surgery was an effective strategy. Reducing surgical stress is essential in fragile infants with extensive SETs.

Teratoma is a type of germ cell tumor that contains tissues from 1 or more of the 3 embryonic layers (ectoderm, endoderm, and mesoderm) and is defined as mature, immature, or malignant based on how well-differentiated the cells are.1 Spinal teratoma is an extremely rare tumor of the central nervous system, comprising only 0.15%-0.18% of all spinal tumors and 5%-10% of the pediatric population.2,3 Spinal tumors are usually located in epidural, intradural, or intramedullary regions1,4; however, spinal epidural teratomas (SETs), especially in infants and children, are extremely rare2,3; only 10 cases of pediatric SET have been previously reported.

As with other spinal tumors, surgical tumor resection is the standard treatment for SET to both pathologically confirm the diagnosis and decompress the spinal cord from the tumor.4-7 Additional resection would be required if there is intrathoracic extension. Nevertheless, there are few papers written about surgical details of SET resection. In this study, we report an infantile SET which extended into the thoracic cavity and was totally removed through a 2-stage surgical strategy in our institution.

One of the aims of this study is to introduce our surgical procedures and concerns of what we have done to our patient as a technical note. Another aim is to review and summarize about rare pediatric SET and contribute further information to its research. This study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (No. 3728), and parental consent was obtained for the underage patient.

ILLUSTRATIVE CASE

A 1-month-old infant boy grew healthy until his parents found a bump on his right back. A spinal MRI found a large intraspinal tumor locating from the T4 to L2 level, compressing the spinal cord to the left (Figure 1A-1C). A partially cystic, multilobulated mass lesion with bony fragments was also extending from the T10 intervertebral foramina paravertebrally into the posterior mediastinum (Figure 1D), which was compressing the right lung as well. However, the patient had no motor or sensory deficits, abnormal reflexes, respiratory disorders, and vesicorectal disorders. Spinal radiograph demonstrated no scoliosis nor spinal dysraphism.

The pediatric surgery team first made a biopsy for diagnosis from the mass inside the thoracic cavity. As the histopathology came up with a relatively well-differentiated mature teratoma, surgical resection instead of chemotherapy or radiotherapy was soon scheduled for its treatment. Two surgeries with different approaches were planned to totally remove the multilobulated tumor. Resection of the intraspinal lesion was first performed to immediately decompress the spinal cord before the patient presented any neurological symptoms. The extraspinal and intrathoracic mass was then removed intra- and extrathoracically using a thoracoscope 1 month later.

Under general anesthesia, the patient was set prone after preparation of motor evoked potential monitoring. The L3 level was preoperatively checked using portable X-ray for orientation to approach the tumor from the caudal end. A microscope was used for tumor removal. Spinous processes of L3 to T7 were exposed and split in the middle without detaching the paravertebral muscles from the arch. As the middle of spinous processes was relatively soft like cartilage, a splitting procedure was performed using a surgical knife and an ultrasound scalpel. An ultrasound scalpel was used when the process was too hard to split using a surgical knife (Figure 2A and 2B). The arch was flexible enough to open-door the spinal canal with a spreader device and silk sutures (Figure 2C), without making bilateral gutters. The intraspinal tumor was mostly composed of fat tissue which easily detached from the dura by itself (Figure 2D). The tumor cephalad to T4-T6 was also pulled out easily from the spinal canal, allowing us to avoid the exposure of T4-T6. The totally intact dura demonstrated that the tumor was SET. A bony fragment inside the tumor sticking inside the spinal canal was debulked with an ultrasound aspirator (SONOPET®; Stryker Corp.). Only the nerve root of T10 needed resection to disconnect the tumor from the extraspinal part. After removing the tumor, the spinous processes were just sutured and closed tightly again. No positive reactions or deterioration was seen in motor evoked potential during the surgery. The postoperative skin incision was only 12 cm long, which healed within 1 week without infection nor cerebrospinal fluid leak. The patient had no new neurological symptoms after the surgery and showed complete decompression of the spinal cord through postoperative spinal MRI. The pathology of the tumor was confirmed as mature teratoma again, composed of all 3 germinal elements: bone, cartilage, adipose tissue, neural tissue, keratin tissue, and intestinal epithelium (Figure 3A-3C).

One month later from the primary operation, a combination approach from both inside and outside the thoracic cavity was planned to remove the remnant tumor in the intrathoracic and extraspinal space. The patient was fixed to a lateral position right side up to insert a thoracoscope (Karl Stortz, Inc.) laterally toward the intrathoracic tumor. The lateral position was also intended to cut out the extraspinal cystic lesion from the back. Under 1-lung ventilation, three 5-mm ports were inserted into the thoracic cavity for using the thoracoscope and forceps. An air pressure of 6 mm Hg was maintained to compress the right lung during tumor resection. The intrathoracic tumor was also composed mostly of fat tissue, locating at the T10-11 level in the posterior mediastinum near the inferior vena cava (Figure 4A). During resection between the tumor and pleura all the way around, the tumor showed no adhesion with the lung nor inferior vena cava. The lesion was finally removed by disconnecting the neck from the extrathoracic part of the tumor and was taken out intercostally.

A new skin incision was added vertically to the previous middle incision just above the extraspinal cystic lesion remained in the right back (Figure 4B). The membrane of the cystic lesion was thick enough not to rupture during dissection from the muscles. Although the neck of the membrane continued deep inside into the intrathoracic space, the translucent light from the thoracoscope led us to the correct direction. The extraspinal lesion was totally removed by connecting the intra- and extrathoracic spaces as a tunnel. The tunnel was closed only by suturing the extrathoracic muscles tightly. The patient was discharged on day 10 postoperatively without any surgical complications including respiratory functions, wound troubles, and cerebrospinal fluid leaks. No adjuvant therapy was scheduled. Spinal MRI showed gross total removal with no recurrence at 3 months and 16 months, postoperatively (Figure 5A and 5B). Spinal deformities were also negative on spinal X-rays and computed tomography scans (Figure 5C).

LITERATURE REVIEW

Review of SET was performed by searching combinations of the following terms through PubMed: “spinal,” “intraspinal,” “epidural,” “extradural,” “intradural,” “intramedullary,” “intradural-extramedullary,” “conus medullaris,” and “teratoma.” Non-English reports were excluded.8 Extraspinal paravertebral teratomas, sacrococcygeal teratomas, and teratomas associated with other anomalies that needed dura incision with an ambiguous description of tumor location were excluded.7,9 Cases without pathological confirmation and cases above 18 years old were also excluded to investigate about pediatric SETs.6,10,11

DISCUSSION

Only 9 of 185 papers included 10 cases of pediatric SET between 1948 and 2025. The 11 cases (including our case) are shown in Table. All cases were younger than 6 years (range: 3 days to 6 years), with more than half being younger than 1-year-old (54.5%). Male predominance (male: 9 cases, female: 2 cases) was observed. Nine cases (81.8%) showed intraspinal extradural extension for more than 3 levels, and 7 cases (63.6%) showed extraspinal extension. Paraparesis of lower extremities and sensation disorder were the most common symptoms because of spinal cord compression, frequently in cases which the tumors were located widely in the thoracic and lumbar levels.1-3,12-15 Dyspnea associated with cyanosis because of intercostal retractions when crying was seen in 2 infants with large tumors extending bilaterally into the mediastinal space.2,13 Although our case was negative with neurological symptoms, age and clinical courses in the literature indicate that some kinds of symptoms might have occurred to our patient in no time. It would be very important, or maybe indispensable, to make early decisions for surgical resection to avoid unfavorable outcomes.

Teratoma is usually composed of tissues differentiated from the 3 germinal layers, mostly of not only adipose tissue and fibromatous tissue but also neural tissue, hair, bone, cartilage, smooth muscle, mucus glands, respiratory epithelium, and gastrointestinal epithelium.1,14,18 Although these components can help make diagnosis of teratoma using computed tomography scans and MRI,19 definite diagnosis becomes quite difficult when the teratoma is not mature enough to see these components. Occasionally, SET can present as intramedullary tumors (eg, astrocytoma, ependymoma, and ganglioma) or intradural extramedullary tumors (eg, dermoid and epidermoid tumor, neurofibroma, schwannoma, and meningioma).4,16 Large series in the literature have reported that neuroblastoma and Ewing's sarcoma are the most common spinal tumors of the epidural space in children.13,15,16 Thus, tissue biopsy becomes necessary to confirm both the diagnosis and histopathology of intraspinal tumors.

Among the 11 pediatric SET cases, all except 1 case were mature teratomas. Yoshioka et al2 reported an extensive SET which showed grade I immature teratoma with incompletely differentiated neuroepithelial tissue (<10%) in the posterior mediastinal region. However, the histology of the intraspinal part was mature without immature components. The high tendency of mature tissues in SET indicates that SET acts almost as a benign tumor. This fact gives us a high chance to fully cure patients with SET by gross total resection (GTR).20 All cases that underwent GTR showed no recurrence of the teratoma.1,3,14-17

GTR becomes the gold standard for pediatric SETs according to its mature histopathology.18,20 Multistage surgeries might be required if SET shows extraspinal paravertebral extension. The spinal canal also needs quite an expansion to expose these large tumors, which makes it highly invasive to the children. Although the strategy could be highly invasive and unavoidable, we concerned about how to minimize the intraoperative damage and postoperative complications.

Conventional laminectomies are known to cause postoperative spinal instability and deformity, especially in pediatric patients.21-23 Laminotomy is rather recommended to prevent these sequelae.13 However, postoperative scoliosis can be seen in wide laminotomies, where paravertebral muscles and ligaments have extensive damage as well.24,25 In our case, we attempted to just split and open-door the spinous processes and arches without dissecting the paravertebral muscles from the arches and performed it one by one from L3. Although we had to expose the spinal canal to T7 at least, we were able to avoid splitting T4-T6, where the tumor naturally detached from the dura halfway through the operation. Skin incision was shortened as well, whereas most of the cases in the literature involved opening the full length of the spinal canal to expose the whole intraspinal tumor. The spinous processes and arches were also easily reconstructed by only suturing the vertebral muscles. However, further follow-up will be needed to evaluate whether our procedures had benefit to prevent postoperative scoliosis in the future.

Multistage surgeries are considered when SET extends to the extraspinal paravertebral space, especially into the mediastinal area.13 The primary reason is to accomplish GTR using several approaches from different directions. Prone position will be best for removing the intraspinal part through laminectomy or laminotomy; however, lateral position will be much better for approaching to the tumor inside the posterior mediastinal space with a thoracoscope. Generally, intraspinal surgery would go first to release the spinal cord from tumor compression. However, thoracotomy becomes urgent when dyspnea with cyanosis is observed.2,13

Another reason for multistage surgeries is to divide the whole process of GTR into short spans to reduce the stress of anesthesia, blood loss, and body damage, such as skin incision, muscle damage, skeletal damage, and lung damage. It would also give time for fragile infants to recover their damages part by part until the next operation.

Chemotherapy and radiotherapy are not common for mature teratomas.18,20 So far with the mature histopathology of SET, adjuvant therapy was also not performed in most of the previous cases. Baysefer et al16 performed adjuvant therapy to both 2 cases although there were no details about the reason or regimen. Tumor resection was repeated when tumor recurrence was observed in 3 reports.2,12,13

Several limitations are noticeable in our study. Pediatric SET is too rare to declare about its characteristics and mature histopathology with only small numbers of case reports. Adjuvant therapy might be concerned if the tumor becomes immature.18 Although GTR is required, it is not always achievable if the tumor has significant adhesions with the surrounding structures, such as nerve roots and blood vessels. In contrast to our procedures, fully opening the spinal canal would be appropriate not only for exposing the whole tumor but for the whole surgical field. Our surgical method of splitting the spinous process is applicable only in very young infants whose spinous arches are still soft and flexible enough to open-door the spinal canal. Conventional laminotomy might have been chosen for older patients. With the extending nature of SET, most of the reports performed multistage surgeries. However, it is a controversial issue that depends on the patient's condition and whether it could be better for the patient to perform tumor removal in a single operation.

CONCLUSION

SET in the pediatric population is an extremely rare entity that often presents as a large, multicompartmental mass. As SET appears to be a histologically mature tumor, GTR is the gold standard for achieving a complete cure. Our case demonstrated that a 2-staged approach, using “open-door” split-spinous process laminotomy and thoracoscopic surgery, can achieve GTR while minimizing surgical stress in infants. Early surgical intervention and tailored operative strategies are essential to prevent neurological deterioration and ensure favorable outcomes with this rare spinal tumor.
