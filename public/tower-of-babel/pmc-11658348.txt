Background

An impacted tooth fails to erupt into the oral cavity within the expected time frame of normal development [1]. The most commonly impacted teeth are third molars and canines [1]. Supernumerary teeth, defined as extra teeth beyond the normal series of dentition, can appear anywhere in the dental arch [2–4]. They may present as solitary or multiple, unilateral or bilateral, and may be erupted or impacted [2, 5]. Supernumerary teeth are typically found in the maxillary incisor region, mandibular premolar region, and distal to the maxillary and mandibular third molars [4, 6]. Rarely, they can occur in locations outside the dental arches, such as the nasal cavity, inferior nasal conchae, maxillary sinus, ethmoid sinus, and skull [4]. To our knowledge, only two cases of supernumerary teeth in the horizontal plate of the palatine bone have been reported globally in the existing literature [4]. This case represents the third known instance.

The prevalence of supernumerary teeth in permanent dentition ranges from 1.5 to 3.5%, while in deciduous dentition, it ranges from 0.3–0.8%.4,7 The occurrence of supernumerary teeth by location, in descending order of frequency, is as follows: in the maxilla, 21 > 11 > 12 > 18 > 28, and in the mandible, 34 > 44 > 35 > 45 > 42.6

The exact etiology of supernumerary teeth remains unclear, with several theories proposed, including phylogenetic theory suggesting that it is due to regression of the anthropoids that had more teeth, autonomic recessive inheritance, X-chromosome linkage, abnormal responses to local trauma, tooth bud dichotomy and hyperactivity of the dental lamina [2, 4–6]. These factors may be influenced by genetic, epigenetic, and environmental elements.

We report a rare case of an impacted supernumerary tooth in the horizontal plate of the palatine bone. The tooth was successfully managed through surgical extraction and no complications were observed during the one-year follow-up period.

Case presentation

A 10-year-old boy presented to the Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery outpatient department (OPD), accompanied by his parents, with a chief complaint of a bony hard swelling on the palate that had persisted for the last three years. The swelling was not associated with pain or any other discomfort. There was no significant medical or family history, and no genetic abnormalities were noted. Clinical examination revealed an erupted supernumerary tooth palatal to the maxillary right central incisor, as well as a bony hard swelling on the midline of the posterior hard palate. The swelling was non-tender, and the overlying mucosa appeared healthy. A non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) scan of the face (Made in Japan, Hitachi Scenaria 128 slice CT, Serial axial scans with multiplanar reconstruction in bone and soft tissue windows of slice thickness 0.6 mm) confirmed the presence of an impacted supernumerary tooth in the middle of the horizontal plate of the palatine bone, with the crown oriented posteriorly and the root anteriorly (Fig. 1). The decision was made to operate under general anesthesia.

Axial, coronal, and sagittal sections of a non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) scan of the face, showing an impacted supernumerary tooth in the horizontal part of the palatine bone, indicated by a red arrow

A comprehensive pre-anesthetic evaluation was conducted to identify health conditions, assess anesthesia-related risks, and optimize patient care. The assessment included:

Medical History and Physical Examination: This included reviewing the patient’s past medical and surgical history, current medications and allergies and any history of smoking, alcohol or substance use.

Airway Assessment: This included evaluating the Mallampati score, thyromental distance, neck flexibility, jaw mobility, and the oral and nasal anatomy for any obstructions.

Cardiovascular Evaluation: Vital signs and an electrocardiogram (ECG) were performed to assess cardiac function.

Respiratory System Assessment: A chest X-ray and pulmonary function tests were conducted as part of the respiratory evaluation.

Laboratory Investigations: Complete blood count (CBC) to assess hemoglobin levels, white blood cell count, and platelets, along with renal and liver function tests, were performed.

All reports and findings were within normal limits, indicating that the patient was fit for anesthesia.

Potential surgical risks, including bleeding, palatine bone fracture, oroantral communication, infection, and wound dehiscence, were explained to the patient’s parents. The risks of leaving the impacted tooth untreated may lead to cyst formation, infection, pain, pus discharge and oroantral fistula, were also explained to them. The patient’s family consented to proceed with the surgical treatment. After obtaining written and informed consent, the patient was scheduled for surgery.

The patient was prepared for surgery with standard preoperative measures. After positioning the patient supine, oro-tracheal intubation was performed, parts were prepared, and a throat pack was placed. A palatal crevicular incision was made from the right second molar to the incisive papilla, and a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap was raised. The impacted supernumerary tooth in the horizontal plate of the palatine bone was located and exposed. The overlying bone was removed using a micromotor straight handpiece (NSK) with a number 8 round bur. The impacted tooth was extracted in its entirety (Figs. 2 and 3). A blunt instrument was used to assess for oroantral communication and sound palatal bone was confirmed in the surgical extraction site. The erupted supernumerary tooth palatal to the right maxillary central incisor was also extracted. Hemostasis was achieved and copious irrigation with normal saline was done. The flap was closed primarily using 3 − 0 Vicryl sutures.

Intraoperative view showing the extracted supernumerary tooth from the horizontal plate of the palatine bone

Medications were prescribed for five days as follows: Amoxicillin 500 mg every 8 h, Ornidazole 500 mg every 12 h, and Paracetamol 500 mg every 6 h. Postoperative care included intraoral irrigation with 0.12% chlorhexidine twice daily for one week.

The healing process was uneventful, and no complications were noted during the one-year follow-up.

Discussion and conclusions

Supernumerary teeth are among the most common anomalies in odontogenesis, occurring more frequently in permanent dentition than in deciduous dentition [4, 7]. Supernumerary teeth are also more common in the Asian population than in individuals of European descent [6]. In the present case, supernumerary teeth were associated with permanent dentition.

Supernumerary teeth may occur in isolation or as part of syndromes such as Gardner’s syndrome, cleidocranial dysplasia, Down’s syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis, etc [3, 5, 6]. In the present case, no syndromic association was noted.

Approximately 76–86% of cases have a single supernumerary tooth, with 12–23% have two supernumerary teeth and cases with three or more supernumerary teeth are rare [2, 6, 8]. In this case, the patient had two supernumerary teeth.

Supernumerary teeth can cause complications such as ectopic eruption, delayed eruption, impaction, root resorption of adjacent teeth, crowding, spacing, food retention leading to poor oral hygiene, odontogenic cyst formation and aesthetic concerns [3, 5, 8].

Supernumerary teeth are more commonly observed in males, with a male-to-female ratio of 2:1 [8, 9]. In the present case, the patient was male.

Morphologically, supernumerary teeth are categorized into four types: conical, tuberculate, supplemental, and odontomas [2, 5, 9]. Approximately 74% of supernumerary teeth are impacted, 80% exhibit normal orientation, and 53% are normal size [6]. The conical type, accounting for 75% of supernumerary teeth, typically has well-developed roots [6]. In this case, both impacted supernumerary teeth exhibited a conical crown with well-developed roots. One of the teeth was impacted with the crown facing posteriorly and the root anteriorly, while the other erupted with a normal orientation, consistent with reports in the literature.

Most supernumerary teeth are asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally on radiographs [7, 9]. While plain radiographs can be used for screening, computed tomography (CT) is preferred for accurate diagnosis and surgical planning due to its ability to precisely locate the supernumerary tooth and assess its size and orientation [7, 10]. Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) is also a useful tool for accurately locating supernumerary teeth and aiding in surgical planning [9]. Compared to traditional NCCT imaging, CBCT offers lower ionizing radiation exposure while still providing sufficient information for dental pathology assessment. However, the overall image quality achieved with NCCT is superior to that of CBCT, primarily due to the lack of soft tissue differentiation in CBCT [11, 12]. In the present case, the patient presented with bony swelling on the posterior hard palate and an NCCT scan of the face was done to determine the details of the pathology.

The primary treatment for supernumerary teeth is extraction [7–9]. However, if the teeth are located near vital structures or intracranially and are asymptomatic, close monitoring with periodic radiographs is recommended rather than extraction [7]. The optimal timing for surgical extraction is still debated [9]. Some authors suggest extraction when the roots of adjacent teeth are fully formed, others recommend extraction when half of the adjacent teeth’s crowns are formed, while others advocate for removal only if complications arise [9]. In this case, the impacted supernumerary tooth in the horizontal plate of the palatine bone, which was not associated with the adjacent teeth, was surgically extracted. while the erupted supernumerary tooth, where the adjacent central incisor root was completely formed, was extracted non-surgically.

In cases of swelling in the posterior hard palate, an impacted supernumerary tooth should be considered in the differential diagnosis. While extraction is the preferred treatment, if supernumerary teeth are located near vital structures and are asymptomatic, close observation with periodic radiographs is an acceptable management option.
