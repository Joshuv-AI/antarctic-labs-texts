A systematic approach to MR spectroscopy of the brain: Indications, protocol, metabolites, and spectral analysis in neurologic diseases
Authors: Shadman M. Rahman, Allison Peng, Kristen Si, Carys L. Kenny-Howell, Mantej Singh, Muhammad Ahsan Asif, Saumik Z. Rahman, Saeed Rahmani, Luca Pasquini, Mehmet Adin, Elizabeth Schrickel, Long H. Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) is a noninvasive imaging technique that complements conventional magnetic resonance imaging by providing in vivo metabolic and biochemical characterization of brain tissue. By quantifying metabolites such as N-acetylaspartate, choline, creatine, lactate, and myo-inositol (myo-Ins), MRS offers insight into neuronal integrity, membrane turnover, energy metabolism, inflammation, and necrosis, features that help distinguish between pathologies with greater specificity than anatomic imaging alone.This review presents a clinically focused, systematic approach to brain MRS, addressing indications, acquisition strategies, spectral interpretation, and characteristic metabolic patterns across a broad spectrum of neuropathologies. Key technical factors influencing spectral quality and reproducibility, such as voxel selection, field strength, echo time, and sequence choice, are highlighted. Metabolic signatures of neoplastic, infectious, inflammatory, demyelinating, metabolic, and neurodegenerative disorders are reviewed with an emphasis on practical differentiation in the setting of overlapping imaging appearances. Recognizing the limited specificity of isolated metabolite abnormalities, this review emphasizes integration of MRS findings with clinical context and conventional MRI. A structured interpretive framework is proposed to promote consistent analysis and enhance diagnostic confidence, reinforcing the role of MRS as a valuable adjunct in contemporary neuroradiologic practice.

Introduction

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) is a noninvasive imaging technique that complements conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) by providing metabolic and biochemical characterization of brain tissue. 1 By quantifying metabolites such as N-acetylaspartate (NAA), choline (Cho), creatine (Cr), lactate, and myo-Ins, MRS offers insight into neuronal integrity, membrane turnover, energy metabolism, and gliosis, thereby enhancing lesion characterization beyond anatomic imaging alone. 2

In neuroradiology, MRS serves as a valuable adjunct in the evaluation of neoplastic, infectious, inflammatory, demyelinating, metabolic, and neurodegenerative disorders.1,3 Characteristic metabolic patterns assist in tumor grading, differentiation of recurrent neoplasm from treatment-related change, identification of demyelinating lesions, and characterization of infectious and metabolic encephalopathies. 3 However, individual metabolite abnormalities often lack disease specificity, necessitating interpretation within the broader context of lesion location, conventional MRI findings, and clinical presentation. 1

Despite its clinical utility, MRS remains technically demanding. Spectral quality is highly dependent on magnetic field homogeneity, voxel placement, and acquisition parameters while variability in protocols across vendors limits standardization and reproducibility.1,2,4 Interpretation also requires familiarity with artifacts and overlapping metabolite peaks, underscoring the importance of a structured and systematic approach.5–7

This review presents a clinically focused framework for brain MRS, including indications, acquisition strategies, spectral interpretation, and characteristic metabolic profiles across a spectrum of neurologic diseases. Emphasis is placed on practical differentiation of overlapping imaging appearances and integration of spectroscopic findings with conventional MRI to improve diagnostic confidence in contemporary neuroradiologic practice.

Review methodology

This is a narrative review and was not registered or conducted as a formal systematic review or meta-analysis. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE through April 2026 using combinations of terms including “magnetic resonance spectroscopy,” “MR spectroscopy,” “MRS,” “proton spectroscopy,” “single-voxel spectroscopy,” “multi-voxel spectroscopy,” “chemical shift imaging,” and brain-region/pathology terms (brain tumor, glioma, glioblastoma, metastasis, abscess, encephalitis, demyelinating disease, multiple sclerosis, mitochondrial disease, leukodystrophy, neurodegenerative disease, dementia, and stroke). Metabolite-specific terms (N-acetylaspartate, choline, creatine, lactate, myo-inositol, lipid peak, and glutamate/glutamine) and technical terms (echo time, voxel selection, field strength, 3T, 7T, PRESS, STEAM, spectral quality, and quantification) were also incorporated. Inclusion was emphasized for studies establishing diagnostic metabolic patterns, technical acquisition parameters, and clinical differentiation of overlapping pathologies. Society guidelines and consensus statements on MRS acquisition and reporting, along with existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses, were prioritized where available. Reference lists of key sources were manually searched to identify foundational and recent literature.

To present each pathology category in a consistent way, we describe (i) the characteristic metabolic pattern and underlying biochemical basis; (ii) the clinical scenario(s) and differential diagnoses in which MRS adds value; (iii) key technical acquisition considerations (voxel placement, echo time, and field strength) relevant to that pathology; (iv) diagnostic performance or distinguishing metabolic thresholds where available, with attention to overlap with mimics; (v) recognized pitfalls, artifacts, and limitations affecting interpretation; and (vi) practical integration points with conventional MRI and clinical context.

Indications for MRS

MRS is primarily used as an adjunct to conventional MRI imaging, especially when conventional MRI is inconclusive or if additional metabolic information could support a more definitive diagnosis and guide clinical management. 4 Common indications for MRS include:• Differentiation of brain tumors from non-neoplastic lesions: MRS is particularly helpful in distinguishing tumors from non-neoplasms such as demyelination, infection, radiation injury, or other cases where MRI can be indeterminate.3,4• Assessment of tumor grading: MRS can be utilized to differentiate between high-grade from low-grade gliomas by providing a metabolic profile that gives insight into tumor aggressiveness, aiding in therapeutic and surgical decision-making.3,4• Differentiation of recurrent tumor from treatment-related changes: Differentiates tumor recurrence from post-treatment effects that may arise, such as radiation necrosis (Figure 1).3,8• Evaluation of metabolic and neurodegenerative disorders: In metabolic derangements such as Canavan’s disease and Leigh’s disease, MRS can characterize metabolic patterns and more specifically classify neurodegenerative disorders with markers that would not be seen on conventional MRI.9,10• Investigation of inflammatory and infectious brain lesions: Assists in differentiating abscesses from neoplasms of the brain based on their unique metabolic profiles.2,8

MRS in relation to conventional MRI and perfusion MRI

MRS shows moderate diagnostic performance for brain tumor evaluation (sensitivity ∼80% and specificity 78%), with utility highly dependent on clinical context. 11 In indeterminate cases, MRS improves diagnostic accuracy by approximately 15% but may be misleading in a minority of cases. 12 As a stand-alone modality, a Cho/Cr ratio ≥1.78 predicts malignancy with 80% sensitivity and 73% specificity, outperforming perfusion MRI alone. Protocol for MRS

Optimized MRS protocols are essential for accurate metabolite quantification and reliable differentiation of intracranial pathologies that may appear similar on conventional MRI.

Patient preparation and positioning

Patients are positioned supine with head immobilization to minimize motion artifacts; limiting caffeine and alcohol intake prior to prolonged acquisitions, particularly with multivoxel spectroscopy (MVS), is recommended. 15

Scanner selection and field strength considerations

MRS may be performed on 1.5 T, 3T, or higher field strength scanners, with 3T preferred due to superior signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and spectral resolution. Ultra-high-field systems (≥7T) further improve SNR, spectral separation, scan efficiency, and metabolite quantification, particularly at shorter echo times (TEs). 16

Imaging sequences and parameters

Following routine MRI, spectra are acquired from a selected volume of interest using either single-voxel spectroscopy (SVS) or MVS. SVS provides higher spectral quality, shorter acquisition times, and more reliable metabolite quantification, making it well-suited for small lesions or diffuse parenchymal processes. In contrast, MVS allows assessment of spatial heterogeneity and is preferred for larger or multifocal lesions, despite reduced quantitative accuracy due to voxel bleeding and signal mixing (Figure 2).17–19 Spectra are typically obtained from both abnormal tissue and contralateral normal brain for comparison.10,17Figure 2.Left: Single-voxel spectroscopy. Water suppression is required for adequate visualization of metabolite peaks and is most commonly achieved using chemical shift selective (CHESS) techniques, with alternative approaches including VAPOR and WET. 18 Spectral acquisition is most often performed using either point-resolved spectroscopy (PRESS) or stimulated echo acquisition mode (STEAM). PRESS offers higher SNR and is preferred for metabolites detected at longer TEs, including NAA, Cho, and creatine, whereas STEAM enables shorter TEs and improved voxel localization at the expense of reduced SNR, facilitating detection of metabolites such as lipids, myo-Ins, glutamate, and glutamine.18–20

Advanced and emerging techniques

Limitations of routine MRS include low SNR and spectral overlap. Advanced approaches, including ultra-high-field imaging, parallel imaging with compressed sensing, and automated shimming and voxel selection, improve spectral quality, acquisition efficiency, and metabolite separation are likely to further enhance clinical applicability.5,21 A technique such as Linear Tangent Space Alignment allows for improved spectral quantification accuracy, improving the reliability of metabolite ratio measurements. 22 Mescher-Garwood point-resolved spectroscopy (MEGA-PRESS) helps reduce spectral overlap through a technique called J-difference editing, which utilizes the J-coupling properties of target metabolites to isolate their signals from any overlapping peaks. 23 It is worth noting the limited availability of MEGA-PRESS in clinical practice due to vendor implementation heterogeneity, post-processing requirements, and expertise. 23 Emerging techniques include AI-assisted spectral interpretation, automated metabolite quantification, radiomics, MRS integration with multiparametric MRI, and 7T spectroscopy; though these are not used routinely in clinical practice.23,24

MRS quality assurance and artifacts

MRS is susceptible to multiple technical artifacts arising from field inhomogeneity, localization errors, and imperfect signal processing, all of which can significantly impact metabolite identification and quantification. Understanding these artifacts is essential for proper spectrum acquisition, interpretation, and avoidance of diagnostic pitfalls.

Poor shimming

Shimming involves iterative optimization of magnetic field homogeneity within the spectroscopy voxel. While first-order shimming is automated on clinical systems, manual second-order adjustments may sometimes be necessary. Incomplete water suppression

Because tissue metabolite concentrations are low, effective water suppression is a critical component of most MRS examinations. Inadequate water suppression can overwhelm the metabolite signal, rendering spectra poorly visible or obscured by noise 25 (Figure 3).

Phasing errors

In MRS, the time-domain signal is acquired as a free induction decay (FID). Improper phasing causes mixing of absorption and dispersion components, resulting in broadened and distorted spectral peaks. Phasing corrects this by optimizing the spectrum to the absorption mode using zero-order and, when necessary, first-order adjustments; although largely automated, manual refinement may occasionally be required 25 (Figure 3).

Chemical shift displacement

Chemical shift displacement artifact arises from frequency-dependent spatial misregistration of metabolites during voxel localization. Differences in chemical shift produce spatial offsets between metabolite signals, reducing true voxel overlap, particularly at higher magnetic field strengths. This effect is most pronounced in single-voxel spectroscopy and can be minimized through the use of higher-bandwidth RF pulses and stronger localization gradients 25 (Figure 3).

MVS contamination

Spectral contamination occurs when metabolite signals are incorrectly assigned to adjacent voxels. Limited spatial sampling in typical MRS grids results in truncation of the acquired signal, causing metabolite peaks to spread into neighboring voxels according to the point spread function. Although this artifact cannot be completely eliminated, it may be reduced through digital filtering and increased spatial resolution 25 (Figure 3).

The data quality can be assessed by visual inspection to allow the identification of artifacts, distortions of spectroscopic curves, and signal-to-noise ratio disturbances that could invalidate the findings. A final step of automatic post-processing has yielded information in a user-friendly format for daily use by radiologists. The reliability and reproducibility of the predictions can be assessed by processing with 6 LCModel software, a user-independent, frequency-domain tool for fitting 1H-MRS obtained in vivo. 27

MRS peaks/metabolites

The following section describes important brain metabolites that can be identified on acquired MRS spectra and their respective functional roles.

N-acetyl aspartic acid (NAA)

NAA is a healthy neuronal marker, with a prominent signal at 2.01 ppm. A key secondary signal to be aware of is from N-acetylaspartylglutamate (NAAG), which resonates at approximately 2.04–2.05 ppm, overlapping extensively with the NAA singlet. At standard clinical field strengths (1.5 T and 3T), NAA and NAAG cannot be reliably separated; thus, the observed peak is typically reported as “total NAA” (tNAA). 28 Separation of NAA from NAAG is feasible at 7T or with MEGA-PRESS. 29 Distinction between NAA and NAAG has clinical relevance as certain pathologies, such as TBI, have shown the reduction in tNAA reflect decreased NAAG rather than NAA itself. 26 Decreased NAA is associated with neuronal and axonal loss or dysfunction due to various pathologies, including tumors, stroke, and multiple sclerosis. Increased NAA is characteristically seen with Canavan disease, though it may also be seen in Salla disease and Pelizaeus-Merzbacher disease.18,20

Choline (Cho)

Cho is a marker of cellular membrane turnover, with a peak at 3.20 ppm. Phosphocholine at 3.22 ppm, glycerophosphocholine at 3.23 ppm, and free choline at 3.21 ppm are major contributors to the Cho signal. At clinical field strengths, these three compounds are unresolved and appear as a single peak. 30 As a biomarker for cell proliferation, Cho levels can be prognostic, correlating with the degree of malignancy associated with tumors. They are also increased in processes involving membrane degradation, such as demyelinating disease, infarction, or inflammation.18,20

Creatine (Cr)

Cr is a marker of intracellular metabolism, with a peak at 3.03 ppm. A secondary resonance is seen at 3.9 ppm, arising from the CH2 protons of the creatine/phosphocreatine molecule. Total creatine quantification ideally incorporates both 3.03 and 3.9 ppm resonances. 31 The 3.9 ppm peak is often less utilized clinically as it can be affected by incomplete water suppression artifacts. 32 Cr is considered a stable metabolite, with variations indicating pathology. Decreased Cr in the brain may reflect chronic liver disease. In brain tumors, findings related to Cr are mixed, with decreased Cr implicated as a feature in high-grade glioma but a favorable prognostic indicator in low-grade glioma.18,20

Lactate (Lac)

Lactate (Lac) is a marker of anaerobic glycolysis, appearing as a characteristic doublet at 1.33 ppm. It is typically absent under normal conditions but increases in states of anaerobic metabolism such as cerebral hypoxia, ischemia, seizures, mitochondrial disease, and acute inflammation (Figure 4). 18 Lactate can be distinguished from lipids, which also resonate near 1.3 ppm, by its J-coupled doublet (coupling constant ∼7 Hz) compared with the broad singlet of lipids. At intermediate echo times (135–144 ms), the lactate doublet inverts, while lipid peaks remain upright, facilitating differentiation.33,34 High TE (288 ms) has lower signal-to-noise ratio and is useful to confirm lactate presence by nulling out lipid peaks, above the baseline. Myo-inositol (myo-Ins)

Myo-Ins is a glial marker almost exclusively found in astrocytes, with a peak at 3.56 ppm at short echo times. Elevated myo-Ins is observed with increased glial cell proliferation (i.e., low-grade gliomas) or hypertrophy, indicating inflammation.

Lipids

Lipids are constituent parts of cellular membranes that serve as markers of membrane breakdown, with peaks at 1.3 ppm and 0.9 ppm. Under normal conditions, such peaks are absent. However, they may appear due to voxel contamination from surrounding fatty tissue or disease processes involving membrane degradation or necrosis, as observed in metastases, lymphomas, or primary malignant tumors. 18

Valine (Val), leucine (Leu), and isoleucine (Ile)

These branched-chain amino acids are produced by bacterial metabolism within abscess cavities. The 0.9 ppm peak, representing the methyl groups of Val, Leu, and Ile, is typically detected using intermediate echo times around 136 ms, where it appears as an inverted peak due to J-coupling effects. Glutamate (Glu) and glutamine (Gln)

Glu and Gln are both markers of brain metabolism, with peaks at 3.6–3.8 ppm at short echo times and closely grouped multiplets between 2.1 and 2.4 ppm. These peaks are difficult to separate, and Glu and Gln are generally measured together as Glx. Variation in Glx has been implicated in a range of pathologies, including depression, cancer, hepatic encephalopathy, and multiple sclerosis, among others.18,20

2-Hydroxyglutarate (2-HG)

2-HG has two primary resonances on MRS: a multiplet at 2.25 ppm and another at 4.02 ppm. Detection can be challenging due to spectral overlap with glutamate, glutamine, and GABA at the 2.25 ppm region. 38 2-HG serves as a highly specific biomarker for isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-mutant gliomas. 36

It is worth noting that this is not an exhaustive catalogue of metabolites detectable by MR spectroscopy. Pathology overview

It is important to recognize that many neurologic diseases, even across distinct etiologic categories, share a similar MRS signature, such as reduced NAA and elevated Cho, limiting the specificity of these findings in isolation. 40 Additionally, certain metabolite ratios that can be utilized to aid in diagnosis has its limitations as there is no universally standardized threshold and should thus be evaluated in the context of local protocols. As a result, MRS should not be viewed as a stand-alone diagnostic method, but rather as a complementary problem-solving tool. Accurate interpretation requires integration of the patient’s clinical presentation, symptom chronology, and detailed anatomic MRI features. Only by synthesizing metabolic data with clinical and structural imaging context can MRS meaningfully narrow the differential diagnosis and enhance diagnostic confidence.

MRS in diagnosing brain tumors

Definitive diagnosis

Differing patterns of metabolite abnormality aid in distinguishing tumors from other major categories of brain pathology (Table 3). Brain tumors demonstrate elevated Cho with reduced NAA, resulting in a dominant Cho peak. This reverses the normal tissue metabolite pattern where the NAA peak is largest. 41 Primary brain neoplasia, that is, gliomas, demonstrate an elevated Cho/Cr ratio. 42 Myo-Ins and lactate are also elevated in glioma, while NAA levels are decreased. 39 In comparison to gliomas, primary CNS lymphoma produces a less elevated Cho/NAA ratio and is not typically associated with increased myo-Ins. 3 In CNS lymphomas, it is common to observe a pattern of increased choline levels, decreased NAA, and increased lipids even in the solid tumor component without necrosis, which helps the differential diagnosis with glial lesions, in which lipid peaks are usually observed in the necrotic component. 43 Compared to primary brain tumors, metastatic lesions often have increased lipids. 2 Compared to Grading of gliomas

Elevated Cho/NAA (>2.20; TE 135 ms, 1.5 T, 3D multivoxel PRESS) and Cho/Cr ratios favor high-grade over low-grade glioma (Figure 6)1,45; the most cited Cho/Cr threshold is > 1.75–1.78 (TE 144 ms, 1.5 T, and multivoxel CSI PRESS). 46 High levels of myo-Ins are observed in low-grade gliomas. 47 Longitudinal increases in these ratios signal malignant progression. Lactate indicates necrosis; a Lac/NAA ratio >0.25 (TE 144 ms, 1.5 T, multivoxel PRESS) signals higher-grade tumors,1,48 which typically display overlapping lipid/lactate peaks at 1.3 ppm. 49 2-HG at 2.25 ppm is highly specific for IDH-mutant gliomas, correlating with better prognosis compared to IDH-wildtype tumors. 50 Three major detection strategies exist: short TE PRESS (TE 30 ms) offers routine clinical balance (88.9% sensitivity and 97% specificity); intermediate TE PRESS (TE 97 ms) is highly confirmatory (100% specificity and 51.2% sensitivity); and MEGA-PRESS targets the 4.02 ppm multiplet (86.7% specificity and 72.2% sensitivity) but has a 17.5% artifact-driven non-diagnostic rate. 38 Advanced methods like MEGA-PRESS can also track cystathionine (2.72 ppm) and glycine (3.55 ppm) for subtype identification and treatment monitoring. 51 Figure 6.High-grade glioma. Tumor vs treatment response

While both radiation-induced injury (pseudoprogression) and tumor progression can present as worsening contrast enhancement and FLAIR hyperintensity, tumor progression is associated with increased Cho/NAA (>1.8) and Cho/Cr (>1.79) (measured at TE 144 ms; 1.5 T; multivoxel CSI PRESS) 52 (Figure 1). When considered alongside the clinical presentation and lesion location outside the radiation field, these findings favor tumor progression, with MRS demonstrating high sensitivity (91%) and specificity (95%) for differentiating tumor progression from pseudoprogression. 4

MRS in infectious pathologies

Pyogenic abscesses

Pyogenic abscesses produce abnormal metabolites absent in normal brain tissue: cytosolic amino acids (valine, leucine, and isoleucine at 0.9 ppm), acetate (1.92 ppm), and succinate (2.4 ppm).10,53 Cytosolic amino acids mark neutrophil-mediated proteolysis, 54 explicitly excluding necrotic brain tumors that mimic abscesses on structural imaging (Figure 5).10,53 Acetate and succinate reflect anaerobic microbial metabolism.55,56 Absent NAA, Cho, and Cr peaks further differentiate abscesses from neoplasms due to a lack of membrane-bound structures. 55 Elevated lipid/lactate peaks occur but are less specific.10,53 Post-antibiotic treatment, a singular lactate peak typically persists. 57 Single-voxel PRESS at intermediate TE (135–144 ms) within the cavity at 1.5 T or 3T remains optimal, leveraging J-coupling inversions of amino acids and lactate to definitively separate abscess from necrotic tumor. 56

Tuberculoid abscesses

Tuberculoid abscesses present with lactate and lipid peaks on MRS, reflecting caseous anaerobic metabolism.53,58 While these findings are shared with pyogenic abscesses, tuberculoid abscesses lack amino acid peaks due to low levels of proteolytic activity.58,59 As with pyogenic abscesses, tuberculoid abscesses lack NAA, Cho, and Cr peaks, which differentiate them from tumors.55,59 The optimal protocol for suspected tuberculous abscess is single-voxel PRESS at short TE (20–30 ms) at 1.5 T or 3T. A dual-TE approach can also be utilized and is valuable as short TE can maximize lipid detection, while intermediate TE (135 ms) allows for assessment for amino acid inversion at 0.9ppm—if amino acids are present and invert, this would rather suggest a diagnosis of pyogenic abscess.58,60

Fungal abscesses

Fungal abscesses are characterized on MRS by a trehalose peak between 3.6 and 3.8 ppm, which is a highly specific marker for common fungi species (e.g., Cryptococcus, Aspergillus, and Candida).10,61 Elevated amino acid, lactate, and lipid peaks can be observed but are not specific to fungal abscesses.21,53 Decrease in lipid and lactate peaks can be an indicator of a positive response to therapy.10,62 The optimal MRS protocol includes single-voxel PRESS at short TE (20–30 ms) at 1.5 T or 3T, with a dual-TE approach of adding intermediate TE (135 ms), which allows for assessment of amino acid inversion-redirecting toward pyogenic etiology. 60 Voxel placement is preferred within the abscess cavity. 60

Parasitic lesions

Neurocysticercosis (NCC) and other parasitic infections have elevated lactate with lipid peaks in degenerating cysts. 21 NCC has a characteristic pattern of elevated succinate, without elevated acetate. 68 The elevated succinate peak has been associated with the aerobic metabolism of scolices. 69 Amino acid peaks have also been found in NCC. 69 This allows differentiation from brain abscesses, which also present with a succinate peak, but typically alongside elevated acetate.10,53 The recommended protocol for acquisition is intermediate TE (135 ms) with the PRESS pulse sequence at 1.5 T 70 (Figure 10).Figure 10.Proposed diagnostic algorithm for interpreting brain MRS spectra. MRS in inflammatory and demyelinating disorders

Multiple sclerosis (MS)

MS typically has elevated Cho, reflecting increased membrane turnover and inflammation. Myo-Ins is also elevated, which is a marker for gliosis and glial proliferation. Creatine levels are elevated with reduced NAA levels, which reflect neuronal or axonal injury and dysfunction and thus serves as a marker for disease activity (Figure 11).10,71 However, these patterns are not highly specific for MS but rather broadly represent patterns seen in inflammatory and demyelinating diseases. In chronic MS lesions, Cho levels are positively correlated with lesion size, indicating the role of chronic inflammation in lesion progression. 72 Longitudinal changes in NAA have been used to evaluate treatment response and potentially differentiate from gliomas.73,74 The recommended protocol for MS includes a dual-TE approach with short TE (30 ms) and intermediate TE (135 ms) with the PRESS pulse sequence. 75 Voxel placement should be centered on the corpus callosum. 75 Figure 11.Demyelination (multiple sclerosis). Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)

ADEM is marked by a characteristic acute elevation of lipids, which decreases over time, which can help differentiate between other demyelinating disorders.76,77 ADEM also presents with elevated Cho, elevated lactate, and reduced NAA, which are non-specific markers of demyelination.21,63 As ADEM typically presents as a monophasic disorder with rare relapses, abnormal findings on MRS, such as reduced NAA and elevated lactate, are typically found to be resolved upon later evaluations.78,79

Other inflammatory pathologies

Other causes of neurological inflammation, such as HIV-associated neuroinflammation, hepatitis C-related neuroinflammation, and chronic pain syndromes, are also detected by MRS. Typical markers include elevated myo-Ins and Cho, along with varying decreases in NAA based on the level of neuronal injury.80,81

For inflammatory and demyelinating disorders, short TE (30 ms) is preferred for detecting the metabolites relevant to the pathology such as myo-Ins for gliosis and lipids for myelin breakdown. Intermediate TE (135 ms) can provide clearer NAA quantification for assessing neuronal damage and improved lactate detection. 75

MRS in inborn errors and metabolic disorders

Canavan disease

Canavan disease demonstrates a characteristic, markedly elevated NAA peak, often dominating the spectrum, with increased NAA/Cr and NAA/Cho ratios due to aspartoacylase deficiency.8,82 This finding distinguishes it from other disorders with apparent N-acetyl elevation. In Salla disease, the increased N-acetyl signal reflects accumulation of N-acetylneuraminic acid (NANA) rather than NAA, while Pelizaeus-Merzbacher disease shows increased total NAA from altered tissue composition rather than true metabolic accumulation. 83 In contrast, most disorders associated with neuronal loss demonstrate reduced NAA. These findings are best appreciated in white matter using SVS or MVS.84,85

Maple syrup urine disease (MSUD)

MSUD has a broad peak complex at 0.9 ppm, which inverts at intermediate TE due to the branched-chain keto acids (BCKAs) and branched-chain amino acids (BCAA). This finding is characteristic and highly suggestive of MSUD. 86 Reports have shown, however, that slightly elevated BCAA and BCKA may remain in patients after recovery, if extensive signal averaging is used. 87 The NAA/Cho and NAA/Cr ratios are decreased in the disease, which are less specific findings. These findings are prone to reverse after treatment.56,86

MRS in urea cycle disorders

Urea cycle disorders arise from the enzyme defects that impair the conversion of ammonia to urea, causing excessive accumulation of ammonia and glutamine in the body. MRS typically demonstrates elevated glutamate/glutamine (Glu/Gln) peaks between 2.0–2.5 ppm and 3.6–3.8 ppm during episodes of hyperammonemia. 6 Hyperammonemia can also produce a lactate doublet at 1.3 ppm as metabolic dysfunction leads to a shift toward anaerobic glycolysis. 6 Chronic hyperammonemia is generally associated with reduced myo-Ins and Cho levels.6,88

For inborn errors of metabolism and metabolic disorders, the recommended protocol consists of single-voxel PRESS at short TE (35 ms), at 1.5 T or 3T, with a dual-TE approach of adding longer TE (144 ms at 1.5 T or 288 ms at 3T) to account for pathognomonic signatures optimally detected at different TEs.89,90 Commonly sampled locations for voxel placement include deep gray nuclei, occipital and midline parietal cortices, and relevant white matter areas. 90

Neurodegenerative disorders

Alzheimer’s disease (AD)

MRS can detect metabolic changes of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) before clinical cognitive impairment, including ∼11% of cases with hippocampal sparing and normal MRI.91,92 AD shows reduced NAA and early myo-Ins elevation, correlating with amyloid-β pathology, while Cho changes vary by stage and region.91,92 A reduced NAA/myo-Ins ratio (<1.69) predicts progression and cognitive decline. 93 The combination of reduced NAA/Cr with elevated myo-Ins/Cr and Cho/Cr is relatively specific for AD, aiding differentiation from vascular dementia, frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). Vascular dementia primarily affects white matter with preserved cortical myo-Ins; FTD shows frontal cortical predominance; DLB preserves posterior cingulate NAA/Cr despite overlapping hippocampal and white matter abnormalities. 91

The optimal protocol for acquisition consists of SVS PRESS with TE 30 ms, at 3T. Voxel placement is preferred in the posterior cingulate gyrus and in the hippocampus. 94 Reduced NAA/Cr ratios have been shown in the posterior cingulate gyrus, medial temporal lobe, occipital lobe, parietal lobe, and frontal lobe. 95

Parkinson’s disease (PD)

In PD, NAA/Cr and myo-Ins/Cr ratios are reduced. Cr levels are particularly elevated in the substantia nigra, thalamus, striatum, or sensorimotor cortex, reflecting neuronal dysfunction, glial activation, and altered energy metabolism. Differing patterns of metabolite derangement on MRS are associated with PD subtypes based upon non-motor symptoms. 96 Elevated Cho/Cr is seen in sleep disorders while altered mI/Cr is seen in gastrointestinal dysfunction. MRS findings precede the structural changes observed on conventional MRI and metabolite abnormalities become more pronounced with disease progression, which can aid in early diagnosis and disease monitoring.96,97

Optimal protocol for acquisition includes SVS PRESS with short TE (30 ms) at 3T, with voxel placement preferred in the putamen.98,99

Huntington’s disease (HD)

MRS findings in HD include reduced NAA and total NAA in the putamen and caudate nucleus. Lower creatine levels are also seen at these sites. In patients with premanifest HD (prior to the onset of clear motor symptoms), reductions in NAA, glutamate, and Cho can be seen in the frontal cortex.40,100

The recommended protocol includes SVS PRESS with short TE (30 ms) at 3T, with voxel placement preferred in the putamen. The caudate nucleus can also be informative. 100

a. Visually inspect Hunter’s angle: Hunter’s angle serves as a rapid visual screening tool for spectral normality. It refers to the normal descending pattern formed by three major metabolite peaks: NAA (2.0 ppm), Cr (3.0 ppm), and Cho (3.2 ppm). 1 After connecting the peaks for these three metabolites, a normal downward slope from NAA to Cho implies preserved neuronal integrity; flattening or reversal suggests pathology (e.g., tumor, demyelination, metabolic disorders, and neurodegeneration) 1 (Figure 12).b. Conclusion

MRS serves as a versatile and informative adjunct to conventional neuroimaging, offering biochemical insights that impact diagnostic assessment across a wide spectrum of neuropathologies. MRS has broad utility in neoplastic, infectious, inflammatory, metabolic, as well as neurodegenerative processes. The greatest value of MRS arises from integrating metabolic signatures with other clinical and imaging information. Spectral profiles alone often do not offer high specificity for individual diseases—yet their combination with conventional MRI and perfusion imaging can provide definitive diagnosis and disease monitoring in a variety of scenarios. Technical optimization and systematic evaluation are key to meaningful application of MRS. Further development of automated shimming, advanced quantification software, and ultra-high-field systems are expected to enhance the accessibility and utility of brain spectroscopy. MRS stands poised to be an integral and increasingly leveraged component of modern neuroradiologic practice.
