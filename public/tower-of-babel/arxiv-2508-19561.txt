Energy-Equidistributed Moving Sampling Physics-informed Neural Networks for Solving Conservative Partial Differential Equations
Qinjiao Gao, Longzhe Xu, Dongjiang Wang, Ran Zhang

Abstract
This paper presents a novel Energy-Equidistributed adaptive sampling framework for multi-dimensional conservative PDEs, introducing both location-based and velocity-based formulations of Energy-Equidistributed moving mesh PDEs (EMMPDEs). The framework utilizes the energy density function as the monitor function, ensuring that mesh adaptation dynamically tracks energy evolution during temporal integration. These theoretical developments are integrated with deep neural networks to establish the Energy-Equidistributed Moving Sampling Physics-Informed Neural Networks (EEMS-PINNs), which integrate physics-informed learning with energy-adaptive mesh optimization. Extensive numerical experiments demonstrate that EEMS-PINNs effectively maintain solution accuracy in long-time simulations while preserving conserved energy. The framework’s robustness is further evidenced by its stable performance in non-conservative systems. The code for this paper can be found at https://github.com/sufe-Ran-Zhang/EMMPDE .

This loss function is computed at residual collocation points distributed throughout the computational domain, analogous to the grid points used in conventional numerical discretizations. Alternatively,

The selection of monitor functions has been a fundamental yet challenging aspect of moving mesh methods, as it directly governs mesh concentration and critically impacts solution accuracy

Preliminary

2.1
Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)

By encoding the governing PDEs directly into the neural network’s loss function through automatic differentiation, PINNs enforce physical consistency while maintaining the flexibility and approximation power of modern machine learning architectures. This paradigm shift enables the solution of PDE systems without relying on traditional discretization methods, while preserving fundamental physical principles through mathematical constraints embedded in the optimization process.

Consider the PDEs of the form

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{t}+\mathcal{N}[\bm{u}](\bm{x},t)=\bm{f}(\bm{x},t),\ \bm{u}=\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\ \bm{x}\in\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t\in[0,T],

(2.1)

\displaystyle\bm{u}(\bm{x},0)=\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{x}),\quad x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle\bm{u}\left(\bm{x},t\right)=\bm{u}_{b}\left(\bm{x},t\right),\ (\bm{x},t)\in\partial\varOmega\times\left[0,T\right],

where

\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{x})
is the initial condition,
\bm{u}_{b}(\bm{x},t)
represents boundary conditions and
f(\bm{x},t)
is the corresponding right hand side, and

\mathcal{N}[\cdot]
denotes a nonlinear differential operator up to
p
-order acting on the unknown solution
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)
respect to the spatial variable
\bm{x}
.

Typically, PINNs employ a fully connected neural network
\widehat{\bm{u}}\left(\bm{x},t;\Theta\right)
to approximate the solution of the function
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)
, where
\Theta
represents the parameter set of the neural network. A deep neural network (DNN)
\widehat{\bm{u}}\left(\bm{x},t;\Theta\right)
possessing
L-1
hidden layers with widths
l_{k}
(
1\leq k\leq L-1
), and an input and output layer is denoted as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\bm{z}^{(0)}=(\bm{x},t),\\
&\bm{z}^{(k)}=\mathscr{L}_{k}(\bm{z}^{(k-1)})=\phi(W^{\left(k\right)}\bm{z}^{(k-1)}+b^{\left(k\right)}),\ k=1,\cdots,L-1,\\
&\widehat{\bm{u}}\left(\bm{x},t;\Theta\right):=\mathscr{L}_{L}(\bm{z}^{(L-1)})=W^{\left(L\right)}\bm{z}^{(L-1)}+b^{\left(L\right)},\end{split}

(2.2)

where
\mathscr{L}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{l_{k-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{l_{k}}
denotes the mapping of the
k
-th layer with
l_{0}=d+1
and
l_{L}=m
.
\phi(\cdot)
denotes the activation function, which allows a neural network to map nonlinear relationship.
A critical requirement for the activation function in PINNs is at least twice continuously differentiable, as this regularity is essential for properly computing the differential operators. The hyperbolic tangent function,
tanh(\cdot)
, is frequently employed due to its smoothness and bounded gradient properties. The complete set of trainable parameters is denoted by
\Theta=\{(W^{\left(k\right)},b^{\left(k\right)})\}_{k=1}^{L}
, where
W^{\left(k\right)}
and
b^{\left(k\right)}
represent the weight matrices and bias vectors for each of the
L
hidden layers, respectively.

In PINNs, the neural network solution approximant
\widehat{\bm{u}}(\bm{x},t;\Theta)
generates the following fundamental residual quantities that measure the discrepancy from the exact solution of the PDE system (
2.1
):

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{p}(\bm{x},t;\Theta):=\widehat{\bm{u}}_{t}\left(\bm{x},t;\Theta\right)+\mathcal{N}[\widehat{\bm{u}}\left(\bm{x},t;\Theta\right)]-\bm{f}(\bm{x},t),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{i}(\bm{x};\Theta):=\widehat{\bm{u}}(\bm{x},0)-\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle\bm{r}_{b}(\bm{x},t;\Theta):=\widehat{\bm{u}}\left(\bm{x},t\right)-\bm{u}_{b}\left(\bm{x},t\right).

The minimization of these residuals during training ensures that the neural network approximant simultaneously satisfies the governing PDE, initial conditions, and boundary constraints. To discrete the loss function defined by the residuals, we define the training dataset as
\mathcal{X}
, which includes the sampling points
\mathcal{X}_{i}=\{(\bm{x}_{i}^{n},t_{i}^{n}=0)\}_{n=1}^{N_{i}}
in the initial domain, the sampling points
\mathcal{X}_{b}=\{(\bm{x}^{n}_{b},t^{n}_{b})\}_{n=1}^{N_{b}}

in the boundary domain, and collocation points
\mathcal{X}_{p}=\{(\bm{x}^{n}_{p},t^{n}_{p})\}_{n=1}^{N_{p}}
in the equation domain. During training, these points are typically distributed uniformly across the spatiotemporal domain.

Then PINNs are aimed at finding
\widehat{\bm{u}}\left(\bm{x},t;\Theta\right)
by minimizing the empirical
L_{2}
loss function

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}\left(\Theta;\mathcal{X}\right)=\lambda_{p}\mathcal{L}_{p}\left(\Theta;\mathcal{X}_{p}\right)+\lambda_{b}\mathcal{L}_{b}\left(\Theta;\mathcal{X}_{b}\right)+\lambda_{i}\mathcal{L}_{i}\left(\Theta;\mathcal{X}_{i}\right),\end{split}

(2.4)

where

\mathcal{L}_{p}\left(\Theta;\mathcal{X}_{p}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{p}}{|r_{p}\left(\bm{x}^{n}_{p},t^{n}_{p};\Theta\right)|^{2}},\ \mathcal{L}_{b}\left(\Theta;\mathcal{X}_{b}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}{|r_{b}\left(\bm{x}_{b}^{n};\Theta\right)|^{2}},\ \mathcal{L}_{i}\left(\Theta;\mathcal{X}_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{i}}{|r_{i}\left(\bm{x}_{i}^{n},t_{i}^{n};\Theta\right)|^{2}}.

(2.5)

Here
\lambda_{f}
,
\lambda_{b}
, and
\lambda_{i}
denote the positive scalar weights.
Through gradient-based optimization of the loss function, PINNs learn parameters
\Theta\text{ that produce }\widehat{\bm{u}}(\bm{x},t)
converging to the PDE solution
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)
.

2.2
Conservative PDEs

In this paper, we consider the energy-conservative PDEs
[
9
]
of the following general form

\bm{u}_{t}=S\frac{\delta H}{\delta{\bm{u}}}[\bm{u}]+\bm{f}(\bm{x},t),\quad\bm{x}\in\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t\in[0,T],

(2.6)

where
S
denotes a skew-symmetric operator with respect to the
L^{2}
inner product, and the energy functional
H[\bm{u}]
is given by

H[\bm{u}](t)=\int_{\Omega}\omega(\bm{x};\bm{u}^{(\alpha)})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(2.7)

i.e., the integral of the energy density function
\omega
over the spatial domain with

\bm{u}^{(\alpha)}=\left\{\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}\bm{u}}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}}:|\alpha|\leq p\right\},\quad\alpha\in\left(\mathbb{Z}^{+}\right)^{d}.

\frac{\delta H}{\delta\bm{u}}[\bm{u}]
represents the variational derivative of
H
defined by

\left\langle\frac{\delta{H}}{\delta\bm{u}}[\bm{u}],\bm{v}\right\rangle_{L_{2}}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{~d}\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}{H}[\bm{u}+\epsilon\bm{v}],\quad\forall\bm{v}\in H^{p}(\Omega).

Under zero forcing (
f(\bm{x},t)\equiv 0
) with compatible boundary conditions (e.g., homogeneous Dirichlet or periodic constraints), the PDE system (
2.6
) reduces to a proper Hamiltonian formulation that exactly preserves the Hamiltonian integral
H

[
3
,
6
]
. This follows from the skew-symmetry of
S
:

\frac{dH}{dt}=\left\langle\frac{\delta H}{\delta{u}}[\bm{u}],\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial t}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\left\langle\frac{\delta H}{\delta\bm{u}}[\bm{u}],S\frac{\delta H}{\delta\bm{u}}[\bm{u}]\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=0.

(2.8)

In our theoretical analysis, we will focus primarily on the integral-preserving Hamiltonian formulation, where the conservation properties hold exactly. For the numerical experiments, we will consider both the energy-conservative case and the generalized PDE form (
2.6
) with non-zero forcing terms, thereby demonstrating the robustness of our methodology across different physical scenarios.

Example 2.1
.

Consider the following nonlinear wave (NLW) equation as an illustrative example. For any given smooth function
G
, we have

\displaystyle u_{tt}-\Delta u+G^{\prime}(u)=0,\quad(\bm{x},t)\in\Omega\times[0,T],

(2.9)

\displaystyle u(\bm{x},0)=u_{0}(\bm{x}),\ u_{t}(\bm{x},0)=u_{1}(\bm{x}),\quad x\in\Omega,

\displaystyle u(\bm{x},t)=u_{b}(\bm{x},t),\quad(\bm{x},t)\in\partial\Omega\times[0,T].

By introducing
u_{t}=v
, we have
\bm{u}=(u,v)
. Then the equation can be transformed as a first-order system of PDEs

\bm{u}_{t}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-\Delta u+G^{\prime}(u)\\
v\end{array}\right],

which has a first integral

H[\bm{u}](t)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+G(u)\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

It can be written into the form (
2.6
) by noting that

\frac{\delta H}{\delta u}[u]=-\Delta u+G^{\prime}(u),\quad\frac{\delta H}{\delta v}[v]=v.

3
Energy-Equidistributed moving mesh strategies

In this central part of the paper, building upon our previous work on Energy-Equidistributed Principles (EEPs)
[
12
]
, we first extend these concepts to multi-dimensional conservative PDEs by developing two key formulations: a location-based Energy-Equidistributed Moving Sampling PDE (EMMPDE) derived through variational methods in computational space
[
4
]
, and then a modified velocity-based EMMPDE designed for enhanced dynamic mesh adaptation. Leveraging this theoretical foundation, we introduce Energy-Equidistributed Moving Sampling PINNs (EEMS-PINNs), a novel deep learning framework that integrates our energy-based adaptive sampling strategy with PINNs.

3.1
Energy-Equidistributed Principles (EEPs)

Conceptually, MMPDE generates a moving mesh that continuously maps a suitable computational space
\Omega_{C}
into the physical domain
\Omega
. The mapping is defined by a bijective coordinate transformation from the computational coordinate
\bm{\xi}\in\Omega_{C}
to the physical coordinate
\bm{x}\in\Omega
through the relation:

\bm{x}=\bm{x}(\bm{\xi},t).

(3.1)

The moving mesh methodology fundamentally relies on the equidistribution principle. For multi-dimensional problems, this approach naturally extends to a variational formulation, where the coordinate transformation (
3.1
) is derived as the minimizer of a mesh functional. The resulting moving mesh PDE system then emerges as the Euler-Lagrange equations of this optimization problem. This variational perspective provides a unified framework for generating optimally adapted meshes while maintaining desirable geometric characteristics across multiple dimensions, such as uniformity, alignment, and orthogonality.

In moving mesh methods based on equidistribution, the monitor function bridges the physical solution and the MMPDEs. This function is designed to take larger values in regions where the physical solution requires higher mesh resolution, such as near singularities or sharp gradients like shock waves in the Burgers equation.

While this approach effectively resolves fine-scale features, it inherently overlooks the conservation of energy, a fundamental property of Hamiltonian systems.
To address this limitation, our proposed Energy-Equidistributed Principles introduce the use of the energy density function as the monitor function. This innovation ensures that mesh adaptation not only tracks spatial solution features but also preserves the Hamiltonian system’s energy dynamics throughout temporal evolution. For clarity, we first reformulate the one-dimensional formulation in
[
12
]
through a variational framework before extending to multidimensional cases.

1D case

Without loss of generality, we set
\Omega_{C}=\Omega=[0,1]
and the boundary conditions for the coordinate mapping are explicitly enforced as:

x(0,t)=0,\ x(1,t)=1,\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

(3.2)

Assuming that
{u}(x)
is the solution of of Equ.(
2.6
) with initial condition
{u}_{0}(x)
, then the total energy remains invariant over time, satisfying
H(t)=H_{0}
for all
t>0
, where the initial energy
H_{0}
is given as

H_{0}\doteq H(0)=\int^{1}_{0}\omega({x};{u}_{0}^{(\alpha)})\ \mathrm{d}x.

Then motivated by the equidistribution principle, we start from the uniform distribution samples
\xi_{j}=\frac{j}{N+1}\ (j=0,1,\cdots,N)
, and intend to find a mesh
x_{j}=x(\xi_{j},t)\in[0,1]
such that

H_{j}(t)\doteq\int^{x_{j+1}}_{x_{j}}\omega({x};{u}^{(\alpha)})\ \mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{N}H(t),\ \ j=0,1,\cdots,N-1.

(3.3)

This Energy-Equidistributed Principle ensures preservation of the Hamiltonian structure for each semi-discrete ODE system by employing the energy density function
\omega
as the monitor function.
Rewrite (
3.3
), we have

\int^{x(\xi_{j},t)}_{0}\omega({x};{u}^{(\alpha)})\ \mathrm{d}x=\xi_{j}H_{0},\ \ j=1,2,\cdots,N-1.

(3.4)

Consequently, for the continuous transformation of the mesh,
x=x(\xi,t)
, the Energy-Equidistributed Principle then goes to:

\int^{x(\xi,~t)}_{0}\omega({x};{u}^{(\alpha)})\ \mathrm{d}x=\xi H_{0},\ \ \xi\in(0,1).

(3.5)

By computing the first and second derivatives of Equ. (
3.5
) with respect to the computational coordinate
\xi
, we derive the following two commonly used one-dimensional EEPs:

\displaystyle\omega({x};{u}^{(\alpha)})\frac{\partial x}{\partial\xi}=H_{0},

(3.6a)

\displaystyle E(x(\xi,t),t)\doteq\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\left(\omega({x};{u}^{(\alpha)})\frac{\partial x}{\partial\xi}\right)=0.

(3.6b)

Through the calculus of variations
[
17
]
, we can obtain that Equ. (
3.6b
) is exactly the Euler-Lagrange (E-L) equation of the variational problem

\mathcal{I}[u](t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\omega(x;{u}^{(\alpha)})\frac{\partial x}{\partial\xi}\right)^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\xi

(3.7)

with respect to the coordinate mapping
x(\xi,t)
.

On the other hand, the function approximation perspective in adaptive mesh generation, motivated by Ren and Wang
[
27
]
, focuses on constructing coordinate transformations where solutions exhibit improved regularity properties in computational space. Thus,
[
4
]
propose to look for the mesh map
x=x(\xi,t)
that minimizes a gradient-dependent functional:

\min_{x}\int_{0}^{1}\omega({x}(\xi,t);{u}^{(\alpha)})\ \mathrm{d}\xi\ \ \text{with}\ \omega=\sqrt{1+u_{x}^{2}x_{\xi}^{2}}.

(3.8)

This variational formulation leads to the Euler-Lagrange equation:

\left(\frac{u_{x}^{2}}{\sqrt{1+u_{x}^{2}x_{\xi}^{2}}}x_{\xi}\right)_{\xi}=\frac{u_{x}u_{xx}x_{\xi}^{2}}{\sqrt{1+u_{x}^{2}x_{\xi}^{2}}}.

(3.9)

The direct numerical solution of Equ. (
3.9
) becomes computationally intractable due to the strongly nonlinear source term. A simplified formulation emerges by setting the right-hand side of (
3.9
) to zero and replacing
u_{x}
with the regularized quantity
u_{x}x_{\xi}
, which reduces to the Energy-Equidistribution Principle Equ. (
3.6b
) with the particular monitor function
\omega=\sqrt{1+u_{x}^{2}x_{\xi}^{2}}
. This simplified approach maintains essential mesh adaptation properties while improving numerical feasibility. As the generalization to Equ. (
3.8
), the variational problem can be formulated as minimizing the energy integral

\min_{x}H(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\omega(x(\xi,t);u^{(\alpha)})\ \mathrm{d}\xi,

(3.10)

where
\omega
represents the energy density function associated with the conserved quantity
H
.

2D case

We now extend the aforementioned methodology to multi-dimensional conservative PDE systems. While we present the theory specifically for the two-dimensional case (
d=2
) for clarity, all results generalize naturally to higher dimensions. Given a scalar energy density function
\omega
, we construct the symmetric positive definite matrix-valued monitor functions

G_{k}=\omega(\bm{x})I_{d}
(
k=1,2,\cdots,d
), where
I_{d}
denotes the
d
-dimensional identity matrix. The variational formulations (
3.7
) and (
3.10
) extend naturally to higher dimensions, providing the theoretical basis for conservative mesh adaptation in multi-dimensional problems.

Specifically, the multidimensional generalization of the variational functional (
3.7
) takes the form:

\mathcal{I}[\bm{u}](t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{C}}\left[\nabla x_{1}^{T}G_{1}\nabla x_{1}+\nabla x_{2}^{T}G_{2}\nabla x_{2}\right]\ \mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2},

(3.11)

where
\nabla=\left(\partial_{\xi_{1}},\partial_{\xi_{2}}\right)^{T}
. The multi-dimensional counterpart of (
3.10
) is simply minimizing the Hamiltonian energy (
2.7
):

\min_{\bm{x}}\int_{\Omega_{C}}\omega(\bm{x};\bm{u}^{(\alpha)})\ \mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}.

(3.12)

Thus, we can naturally extend the Euler-Lagrange equation (
3.6b
) to higher dimensions, and get the Energy-Equidistribution Principle in the two-dimensional case:

\displaystyle E_{1}\doteq\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{1}}\left(G_{1}\frac{\partial x_{1}}{\partial\xi_{1}}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}\left(G_{1}\frac{\partial x_{1}}{\partial\xi_{2}}\right)=0,

(3.13)

\displaystyle E_{2}\doteq\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{1}}\left(G_{2}\frac{\partial x_{2}}{\partial\xi_{1}}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{2}}\left(G_{2}\frac{\partial x_{2}}{\partial\xi_{2}}\right)=0.

We have introduced a mesh adaptation strategy based on a computational-space variational formulation (as opposed to physical-space approaches), following the framework developed in
[
4
]
.
By operating in the computational domain rather than the physical space, this strategy avoids the numerical complexities inherent in solving the Euler-Lagrange equations (
3.13
) on intricate domains.

Huang
[
16
]
introduced a class of robust MMPDEs derived from gradient flow formulations of variational principles to enable dynamic mesh adaptation for time-dependent solutions. The standard implementation for solving (
3.13
) employs the location-based EMMPDE

\bm{x}_{t}=-\frac{1}{\tau}\bm{E},

(3.14)

where
\tau>0
is a relaxation parameter controlling mesh movement timescales and

\bm{E}=\left(E_{1},E_{2}\right)^{T}
represents the variational derivatives of the energy functional. The mesh evolves according to this system from an initial configuration toward equilibrium, producing an optimized mesh distribution according to the specified energy-based monitor function while maintaining numerical stability through the gradient flow structure.

For the mesh evolution over time step
\tau
, when
\bm{x}(\bm{\xi},t+\tau)
approaches steady state, the equilibrium condition
\bm{E}(\bm{x}(\bm{\xi},t+\tau),t+\tau)=0
holds.
Suppose
\bm{E}
is smooth enough, a modified EMMPDE can be derived through first-order Taylor expansion of the steady state condition at time
t
:

0=\bm{E}(\bm{x}(\bm{\xi},t+\tau),t+\tau)=\bm{E}(\bm{x}(\bm{\xi},t),t)+\tau\left[A\bm{x}_{t}(\bm{\xi},t)+\bm{E}_{t}(\bm{x}(\bm{\xi},t),t)\right],

where the Jacobian matrix
A
is given by:

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(E_{1}\right)_{x_{1}}&\left(E_{1}\right)_{x_{2}}\\
\left(E_{2}\right)_{x_{1}}&\left(E_{2}\right)_{x_{2}}\end{array}\right).

Consequently, we have the following velocity-based EMMPDE

\bm{x}_{t}=-A^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\tau}\bm{E}+\bm{E}_{t}\right).

(3.15)

The variational strategy EMMPDE (
3.14
) exemplifies location-based mesh movement by directly controlling mesh point positions. In contrast, EMMPDE (
3.15
) incorporates a velocity-based approach through the term

\bm{E}_{t}
, targeting mesh velocity explicitly and deriving point locations by integrating the velocity field. For further modifications and extensions of MMPDE-based methods, we refer to
[
16
]
.

Remark 3.1
.

The selection of an appropriate monitor function
\omega
in MMPDEs remains a challenging and problem-dependent aspect of mesh adaptation, with common choices including solution-derived quantities such as the generalized arc-length measure
\omega=\sqrt{1+c^{2}\left|\nabla_{\bm{x}}\bm{u}(\bm{x})\right|^{2}}
or alternatively
\omega=\sqrt{1+c^{2}\left|\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}\bm{u}(\bm{x}(\bm{\xi}))\right|^{2}}
,
vorticity-based functions for fluid flows, or curvature-based monitors for geometric features
[
2
]
. In contrast, Energy-Equidistributed MMPDEs employ the energy density function as the monitor to guarantee exact energy equidistribution among mesh points. A key requirement for all monitor functions is maintaining strict positivity, which can be ensured through changing
\omega
into
\omega+C
using a sufficiently large positive constant
C
.

3.2
Energy-Equidistributed Moving Sampling PINNs (EEMS-PINNs)

Conventional implementations of moving mesh methods typically solve the coupled system of the underlying PDE and mesh equations either simultaneously or alternately using standard discretization approaches such as finite difference, finite element, or kernel-based methods. Building on the framework of
[
35
]
, we propose a novel alternating learning paradigm where PINNs iteratively solve both the EMMPDE and the underlying physical PDE, termed Energy-Equidistributed Moving Sampling PINNs (EEMS-PINNs). The framework first trains an initial PINNs to approximate the solution, which then informs the construction of the energy-based monitor function for the EMMPDE. This adaptive mesh generation guides subsequent PINNs training through optimized collocation points, accelerating loss convergence while preserving the Hamiltonian structure of the system. Numerical experiments in Section
4
demonstrate that the Energy-Equidistributed principle provides such effective guidance that optimal meshes often emerge after just one iteration, eliminating the need for repeated refinements.

Following the initialization procedure described in Section
2
, we begin with a set of uniformly distributed collocation points across the computational domain:
\mathcal{X}^{ini}=\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{p}\cup\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{i}\cup\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{b}
, with
\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{p}=\{(\bm{\xi}^{n}_{p},t^{n}_{p})\}_{n=1}^{N_{p}}
,

\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{i}=\{(\bm{\xi}^{n}_{i},t^{n}_{i}=0)\}_{n=1}^{N_{i}}
and

\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{b}=\{(\bm{\xi}^{n}_{b},t^{n}_{b})\}_{n=1}^{N_{b}}
. Using the nonlinear wave equation (
2.9
) as our model system, we pre-train an approximation of the solution using PINN with a fully connected multilayer network architecture
\left(\hat{{u}},\hat{{v}}\right)\doteq\widehat{\bm{u}}(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta_{H})
defined in (
2.2
). The PDE residuals are formulated as two coupled components:

\displaystyle{\displaystyle r}_{p,1}(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta_{H})=\widehat{u}_{t}\left(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta_{H}\right)-\widehat{v}\left(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta_{H}\right),

(3.16)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle r}_{p,2}(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta_{H})=\widehat{v}_{t}\left(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta_{H}\right)-\Delta\widehat{u}\left(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta_{H}\right)+G^{\prime}(\widehat{u}\left(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta_{H}\right)).

The optimal parameter
\Theta^{*}_{H}
of the output
\left(\hat{{u}},\hat{{v}}\right)
can be learned by minimizing the following loss function

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{H}\left(\Theta_{H};\mathcal{X}^{ini}\right)=&\lambda_{p,1}\mathcal{L}_{p,1}\left(\Theta_{H};\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{p}\right)+\lambda_{p,2}\mathcal{L}_{p,2}\left(\Theta_{H};\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{p}\right)+\lambda_{b}\mathcal{L}_{b}\left(\Theta_{H};\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{b}\right)+\\
&\lambda_{i,1}\mathcal{L}_{i,1}\left(\Theta_{H};\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{i}\right)+\lambda_{i,2}\mathcal{L}_{i,2}\left(\Theta_{H};\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{i}\right),\end{split}

(3.17)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}_{p,1}\left(\Theta_{H};\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{p}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{p}}{|r_{p,1}\left(\bm{\xi}^{n}_{p},t^{n}_{p};\Theta_{H}\right)|^{2}},\ \mathcal{L}_{p,1}\left(\Theta_{H};\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{p}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{p}}{|r_{p,1}\left(\bm{\xi}^{n}_{p},t^{n}_{p};\Theta_{H}\right)|^{2}},\\
&\mathcal{L}_{i,1}\left(\Theta_{H};\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{i}}{|\hat{u}(\bm{\xi}_{i}^{n},0)-u_{0}(\bm{\xi}_{i}^{n})|^{2}},\ \mathcal{L}_{i,2}\left(\Theta_{H};\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{i}}{|\hat{v}(\bm{\xi}_{i}^{n},0)-u_{1}(\bm{\xi}_{i}^{n})|^{2}},\\
&\mathcal{L}_{b}\left(\Theta_{H};\mathcal{X}^{ini}_{b}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{b}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}{|\hat{u}(\bm{\xi}_{b}^{n},t_{b}^{n})-u_{b}(\bm{\xi}_{b}^{n},t_{b}^{n})|^{2}}.\end{split}

(3.18)

The pre-trained PINN solution
(\hat{u},\hat{v})
provides essential prior information for the Energy-Equidistributed moving sampling method, specifically enabling the construction of the energy density monitor function:

\omega(\bm{\xi};\hat{u},\hat{v})=\frac{1}{2}\hat{v}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\hat{u}|^{2}+G(\hat{u}).

With this physics-informed
\omega
and and initial collocation points
\mathcal{X}^{ini}
, we then learn the adaptive coordinate transformation

\bm{x}=\bm{x}(\bm{\xi},t)
through the EMMPDE deep neural network
\hat{\bm{x}}(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta_{E})
with
\Theta_{E}
being the EMMPDE network parameter.

To enforce the geometric boundary condition
\bm{x}(\bm{\xi},t)=\bm{\xi}
for
\bm{\xi}\in\partial\Omega
, during mesh adaptation learning, we design a structured neural network architecture inspired by
[
30
]
. The boundary-constrained coordinate transformation is formulated as:

\hat{\bm{x}}^{bc}(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta_{E})=\bm{\xi}+\psi(\bm{\xi})\hat{\bm{x}}(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta_{E}),

(3.19)

where
\psi
is the normalizer function satisfying
\psi=0
on
\partial\Omega
. The normalizer function
\psi(\bm{\xi})
can be constructed using the signed distance function to the boundary
\partial\Omega

[
30
]
. In the 1D case with
\Omega=[0,1]
, where boundary conditions (
3.2
) require
\xi=0
and
\xi=1
, we consider three common normalizer constructions:

\displaystyle\psi_{A}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\xi(1-\xi)\quad\text{(Product form)}

\displaystyle\psi_{B}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\xi+(1-\xi)-\sqrt{\xi^{2}+(1-\xi)^{2}}\quad\text{(R-equivalence)}

\displaystyle\psi_{C}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi(1-\xi)}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+(1-\xi)^{2}}}\quad\text{(Normalized product)}

where
\psi^{(1)}(\xi)=\xi
and
\psi^{(2)}(\xi)=1-\xi
represent the signed distance functions to each boundary. These constructions satisfy the essential properties:

\psi(0)=\psi(1)=0
(boundary annihilation) and

\psi(\xi)>0
for
\xi\in(0,1)
(interior positivity). For multi-dimensional domains, the construction generalizes via R-functions:

\psi(\bm{\xi})=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\psi_{k}(\xi_{k})

(3.20)

where
\psi_{k}
are 1D normalizers for each dimension.

The optimal boundary-constrained mesh
\hat{\bm{x}}_{t}^{bc}(\bm{\xi},t;\Theta^{*}_{E})
is learned by minimizing the EMMPDE loss function

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{L}_{E}\left(\Theta;\mathcal{X}^{ini}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}{\left|\hat{\bm{x}}_{t}^{bc}(\bm{\xi}_{p}^{n},t_{p}^{n})-\frac{1}{\tau}E(\hat{\bm{x}}^{bc}(\bm{\xi}_{p}^{n},t_{p}^{n}),t_{p}^{n})\right|^{2}}.\ \end{split}

(3.21)

This formulation naturally extends to alternative EMMPDE variants like (
3.15
) through appropriate modification.

In the last step, the EEMS-PINN framework completes its adaptive cycle by feeding the optimized sampling points
\mathcal{X}^{new}=\hat{\bm{x}}^{bc}(\mathcal{X}^{ini};\Theta_{E}^{*})
back into the physics-informed neural network. These energy-adapted collocation points, now concentrated in regions of high dynamical activity, enable the PINN to produce an improved solution approximation
\widehat{\bm{u}}(\bm{x},t;\Theta_{H}^{*})
through minimization of the loss function (
3.17
). Numerical experiments in Section
4
demonstrate that the Energy-Equidistributed principle provides such effective guidance that optimal meshes often emerge after just one or two iterations for most benchmark problems.

To rigorously validate the energy conservation law during network training, we implement a numerical quadrature scheme that evaluates the energy functional
H
using the PINN solution approximation
\widehat{\bm{u}}(\bm{x},t;\Theta_{H}^{*})
. For any given time snapshot
t^{*}
, we define the quadrature point set:

\mathcal{X}^{t^{*}}=\{(\bm{x},t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]\,|\,t=t^{*}\}.

The discrete energy approximation is then computed as:

H_{d}[\widehat{\bm{u}}](t^{*})=\sum_{(\bm{x}_{n},t^{*})\in\mathcal{X}^{t^{*}}}\alpha_{n}w(\bm{x}_{n};\widehat{\bm{u}}^{(\alpha)}),

(3.22)

where

\bm{x}_{n}
are quadrature points
and
\alpha_{n}
are corresponding quadrature weights.
The relative energy error metric:

\Delta H_{d}(t^{*})=\frac{|H_{d}(t^{*})-H_{d,0}|}{H_{d,0}}

(3.23)

quantifies conservation law preservation, where
H_{d,0}
is the reference energy computed using the exact initial condition
\bm{u}_{0}(\bm{x})
.

The EEMS-PINN framework establishes a self-consistent iterative cycle that couples neural network approximation with variational mesh adaptation. As detailed in Algorithm 1 and visualized in Figure
1
, each iteration performs three fundamental operations. This adaptive process automatically achieves optimal point concentration in high-energy regions while maintaining the Hamiltonian structure.

Numerical Experiments

In this section, the numerical experiments will validate the effectiveness of our Energy-Equidistributed Moving Sampling PINNs (EEMS-PINNs) through systematic comparisons with standard PINNs
[
25
]
and Adaptive Point Movement PINNs (WAM-PINNs)
[
14
]
on both 1D and 2D test cases. In the WAM-PINNs framework, collocation points are initially distributed according to a prior probability density
p_{0}(\bm{x})
, typically chosen as a uniform distribution over the computational domain. The adaptive sampling process then dynamically adjusts the point locations through resampling from the modified probability density:

p(\bm{x})=\frac{w^{k}(\bm{x})}{\mathbb{E}[w^{k}(\bm{x})]},\quad\text{with}\ w(\bm{x})=\sqrt{1+|\nabla{u}|^{2}},

(4.1)

where the gradient
\nabla{u}=(\partial_{x_{1}},\partial_{x_{2}},\dots,\partial_{x_{d}},\partial_{t})^{T}
includes both spatial and temporal variables of the PDE system and the hyperparameter exponent
k\geq 1
controls the degree of point concentration.
The normalization constant
\mathbb{E}[w^{k}(\bm{x})]
is estimated numerically via Monte Carlo integration of the monitor function over the domain.

For PINN and WAM-PINN, collocation points are initially generated through uniform random sampling across the entire computational domain. In contrast, EEMS-PINN employs a structured initialization with points arranged on a uniform space-time grid before undergoing energy-based adaptation.
The neural network architecture throughout this study employs the deep fully-connected structure defined in Equ.(
2.2
), with Tanh activation functions in all hidden layers to ensure
C^{2}
continuity required for accurate derivative computations in the PDE residuals. For the loss function of the underlying conservative PDE, we maintain uniform weighting coefficients (
\lambda_{p}=\lambda_{b}=\lambda_{i}=1
) across all constraint terms - governing equations, boundary conditions, and initial conditions - ensuring balanced enforcement of all physical constraints during optimization.

Our optimization approach combines the benefits of Adam and L-BFGS algorithms in a two-phase training strategy. Initially, we employ the Adam optimizer for a predetermined number of iterations to navigate the parameter space and avoid poor local minima, following the recommendations in
[
21
]
. Subsequently, we switch to the L-BFGS optimizer to exploit its superlinear convergence properties near local optima, significantly accelerating the final stages of training. 5
Conclusions

In this paper, we have introduced a novel Energy-Equidistributed moving mesh strategy for conservative PDEs, grounded in the EEP to rigorously preserve energy during adaptive mesh refinement. We derived the EMMPDEs that dynamically concentrate collocation points in regions of high energy variation, and integrate this framework with PINNs to develop EEMS-PINNs. Numerical experiments demonstrate clear advantages of the proposed method over gradient-based adaptive sampling approaches. The energy-driven framework rigorously enforces conservation laws while significantly improving solution accuracy. Performance remains robust across both conservative and non-conservative systems, maintaining stability throughout long-time simulations.

This research opens three key directions for further investigation. First, we will conduct rigorous theoretical analysis in EMMPDE formulation. Second, we plan to extend the Energy-Equidistributed framework to high-dimensional conservative systems, with targeted applications in image processing and turbulent fluid simulations. Finally, we will address the geometric challenges of implementing EEMS-PINNs on curved manifolds
[
18
]
by developing extrinsic sampling strategies that respect both the Hamiltonian dynamics and surface topology.
