Nonlinear Interaction Patterns in Health Literacy Identified through Explainable Artificial Intelligence: A Focus on Age and Education
Authors: Nari Kim, Nam-Ju Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to identify predictors of health literacy (HL), focusing on nonlinear relationships and interaction effects in a nationally representative population.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed data from 8,630 Korean adults participating in the Korea Health Panel Survey. HL was assessed using the Korean version of the European Health Literacy Survey Questionnaire 16-item (HLS-EU-Q16) and categorized as sufficient or insufficient. An extreme gradient boosting algorithm (XGBoost) was applied, incorporating survey weights. Model performance was evaluated using standard metrics, including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and Brier score. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values were calculated to quantify individual feature importance and identify interaction effects among 69 features.ResultsThe XGBoost model achieved good discrimination (AUC = 0.840) and calibration (Brier score = 0.161). Age (24.4%) and education level (19.0%) were the most influential predictors. SHAP interaction analysis identified a meaningful interaction between age and education level (mean |interaction value| = 0.078), with interaction plots indicating positive patterns among adults aged 60–80 with lower educational attainment. The contribution of online health information use varied by age, showing a negative association among younger adults but a positive association among older adults.ConclusionsHL is shaped by nonlinear and interactive effects across sociodemographic and health-related factors. Explainable machine learning approaches can facilitate the identification of high-priority populations and support the development of tailored, patient-centered educational strategies to improve HL and promote engagement with care.Keywords: Health Literacy, Patient Education as Topic, Machine Learning, Aged, Educational Status

I. Introduction

Patient-centeredness has become a core value in modern healthcare. As healthcare systems grow more complex and chronic disease management becomes increasingly prominent, a patient-centered approach is essential. Achieving this goal requires patients to have adequate health literacy (HL), defined as the ability to find, understand, appraise, and use information and services to protect and improve their health and well-being [1,2].

Patients with limited HL often have a poorer understanding of disease management [3], may conceal their problems due to shame or stigma [4], and tend to participate less in preventive healthcare services [5]. These barriers impede communication and shared decision-making. Addressing these challenges requires identifying and understanding the factors that influence HL, enabling healthcare providers to better support patients with varying HL levels.

Research has identified various sociodemographic (e.g., age, education) [6,7], health-related (e.g., number of chronic diseases, body mass index [BMI]) [8,9], and environmental factors (e.g., regional differences) [10] as predictors of HL. However, HL is inherently multidimensional and shaped by multiple interacting influences [11]. The mechanisms through which these factors interact to affect HL remain poorly understood. For instance, Dauletkaliyeva et al. [10] found that majoring in engineering, being 19 or older, and being male did not individually influence HL, however, the combination of these characteristics was associated with significantly higher HL scores. Understanding these complex interactions can inform the development of tailored, patient-centered education and counseling strategies.

Machine learning (ML) offers new opportunities to explore the nonlinear relationships and interaction effects that shape HL. Unlike traditional statistical approaches, which require pre-specified interaction terms and rely on distributional assumptions, ML algorithms can automatically detect complex patterns. Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), a decision tree–based ensemble algorithm, incorporates regularization to mitigate overfitting while maintaining high predictive accuracy and computational efficiency [12]. However, the complexity of ML prediction processes often limits interpretability. To address this challenge, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), an explainable artificial intelligence technique, quantifies each variable’s contribution to model predictions [13,14]. This study applies an XGBoost–SHAP framework to systematically identify predictors of HL and their interactions. The findings aim to provide an evidence base for developing patient-centered education and counseling strategies that enhance patients’ engagement in their own care.

II. Methods

1. Setting and Sample

This cross-sectional study used data from the Korea Health Panel Survey (KHPS) Version 2.2 (2019–2022), a nationally representative annual survey conducted jointly by the Korea Institute for Health and Social Affairs and the National Health Insurance Service. This analysis used the 2021 single-year dataset, corresponding to the first year in which HL was measured. The 2021 KHPS wave, conducted from January 1 to December 31, included 5,907 households and 13,799 individuals with corresponding medical utilization data. After excluding respondents aged 19 and under and those with missing or “don’t know” responses to one or more HL items, the final analytic sample comprised 8,630 individuals.

2. Predictor Variables and Outcome

The selection and classification of predictor variables were based on prior studies [11,15,16]. Predictors were grouped into three domains: sociodemographic, health-related, and environmental factors. Sociodemographic variables included sex, age, education, marital status (presence of spouse), cohabitation with family, economic activity, type of health insurance, total household income, and private health insurance enrollment. Health-related variables included self-rated health, BMI, number of chronic diseases, health-related quality of life (HRQOL), mental health status (perceived stress, depressive symptoms, anxiety, suicidal ideation), health behaviors (physical activity, lifetime smoking, alcohol use), medication adherence, participation in health screenings, number of healthcare visits (emergency, inpatient, outpatient), and sources of health information (mass media, interactive communication, online platforms, other, or none). Environmental variables included residential region (17 provinces, urban or rural) and unmet medical needs. Detailed definitions are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

The outcome variable, HL, was assessed using the Korean version of the 16-item European Health Literacy Survey Questionnaire (HLS-EU-Q16) [17,18]. The KHPS research team reviewed and refined the translated instrument before its use. The questionnaire comprises 16 items, including seven related to healthcare, five related to disease prevention, and four related to health promotion, each rated on a 5-point scale: “very difficult,” “fairly difficult,” “fairly easy,” “very easy,” and “don’t know.” In accordance with the HLS-EU-Q16 scoring guidelines, “very difficult” and “fairly difficult” responses were scored as 0, whereas “fairly easy” and “very easy” responses were scored as 1 [17]. Respondents who selected “don’t know” for any item were excluded from the analysis. Total scores were calculated by summing all 16 items, yielding a range of 0 to 16. Scores of 0–12 were classified as insufficient HL, and scores of 13–16 were classified as sufficient HL [19].

3. Data Analysis

The dataset (N = 8,630) contained no missing values and was preprocessed by one-hot encoding nominal variables, converting ordinal variables to numeric form (e.g., education coded as 1–5), and retaining continuous variables, thereby expanding the number of features from 29 to 69. Survey weights (I_WGC) provided by the KHPS were applied. Descriptive statistics and group comparisons according to HL level were conducted using the Rao–Scott chi-square test and the design-based t-test, incorporating survey weights. To examine correlations among continuous and ordinal predictors, Spearman rank correlation coefficients were calculated. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

For model development, the dataset was randomly divided into a training set (n = 6,904) and a test set (n = 1,726) at an 8:2 ratio. To preserve the outcome distribution across the two datasets, stratified random splitting was performed using the createDataPartition() function from the caret package. The model was trained on the training set and evaluated on the test set, with sufficient HL defined as the positive class and insufficient HL as the negative class. To validate the choice of algorithm, XGBoost was compared with random forest and logistic regression. All three models incorporated survey weights. XGBoost and random forest were optimized using five-fold cross-validation with grid search. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and the Brier score. All performance metrics were calculated on the unweighted test set.

SHAP estimates each feature’s marginal contribution to model predictions across all possible feature combinations, enabling consistent and quantitative assessment of feature importance [13]. The TreeSHAP algorithm was applied to interpret the trained model [14]. Feature importance was assessed using mean absolute SHAP values, expressed as a percentage of the total absolute contribution across all features. The top eight features were visualized using SHAP dependence plots which illustrate the relationship between each feature’s value and its SHAP value—the sum of its main effect and all pairwise interaction effects [14].

To assess pairwise interactions, a SHAP interaction matrix was generated for all feature pairs using the shap.prep.interaction() function in the SHAPforxgboost package. In the resulting matrix, diagonal elements represent main effects, whereas off-diagonal elements represent pairwise interaction effects [14]. The three feature pairs with the highest mean absolute SHAP interaction values were selected for visualization using ggplot2. The decomposition of SHAP values into main and interaction effects for the top eight features is summarized in Supplementary Table S2, and Supplementary Figure S1 illustrates this decomposition for the three selected feature pairs. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Development Core Team). The overall model development and analysis process is illustrated in Figure 1.

2. Model Performance

XGBoost, random forest, and logistic regression demonstrated comparable overall performance, with XGBoost showing slightly higher discrimination (AUC: 0.840 vs. 0.836 vs. 0.833) and better calibration (Brier score: 0.161 vs. 0.163 vs. 0.165) (Figure 2, Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). The optimized XGBoost model achieved an accuracy of 0.773, sensitivity of 0.826, specificity of 0.723, and an F1-score of 0.780.

3. SHAP Feature Importance

The top eight predictors accounted for over 70% of the total SHAP values. Age was the most influential factor (24.38%), followed by education level (19.03%). Online health information use contributed 6.07%, while having no search experience contributed 5.95%. Self-rated health and HRQOL accounted for 4.29% and 4.24%, respectively, and the number of chronic diseases and total household income contributed 3.47% and 3.20%. 4. SHAP Dependence Plots

SHAP dependence plots for the eight most influential features are presented in Figure 4. SHAP values steadily declined with increasing age, with a sharp drop after 60 (Figure 4A), indicating that older age is strongly associated with insufficient HL in a nonlinear manner. For education level, higher attainment corresponded to a greater likelihood of sufficient HL. SHAP values were negative for individuals with middle school education or less (levels 1–2), reflecting insufficient HL, but became positive from high school onward (levels 3–5), indicating stronger associations with sufficient HL (Figure 4B). Online health information use showed a marked impact. Participants who reported searching for health information online exhibited positive SHAP values and were more likely to have sufficient HL (Figure 4C). In contrast, those with no prior online search experience had negative SHAP values, indicating insufficient HL (Figure 4D). Self-rated health followed a similar pattern, with positive self-ratings associated with higher SHAP values and sufficient HL, whereas negative self-ratings aligned with insufficient HL (Figure 4E). HRQOL demonstrated a nonlinear relationship with HL. Moderate to moderately high HRQOL levels (approximately 0.5–0.75) were associated with sharp increases in SHAP values and sufficient HL, whereas the association weakened at both lower and very high HRQOL levels (Figure 4F). As the number of chronic diseases increased, SHAP values became increasingly negative, suggesting poorer HL (Figure 4G). For total household income, SHAP values increased up to approximately 400 million KRW, indicating a positive association with sufficient HL, and then gradually declined beyond this level, revealing a nonlinear trend (Figure 4H).

5. SHAP Interaction Effects

SHAP interaction plots for the three most influential feature pairs are presented in Figure 5. Three feature pairs—age × education level, age × online health information use, and education level × online health information use—showed direction reversals across subgroups. The strongest interaction was observed between age and education level (mean |interaction value| = 0.078), particularly among individuals aged 60 years and older (Figure 5A). Within this age group, individuals with lower educational attainment (levels 1–2) exhibited positive SHAP interaction values, suggesting that the combination of older age and lower education was associated with relatively higher HL. In contrast, for older adults with higher education levels (levels 3–5), suggesting minimal interaction between the two factors in this subgroup. The interaction between age and online health information use (mean |interaction value| = 0.022) varied across age groups. Among respondents in their twenties, negative interaction values indicated that online health information use was associated with lower HL (Figure 5B). Conversely, among adults aged 60 years and older, the interaction values were positive, suggesting that online health information use was associated with higher HL in older populations. Similarly, the interaction between education level and online health information use (mean |interaction value| = 0.022) was positive among individuals with lower education levels (levels 1–2), indicating that online engagement was associated with higher HL in this group. In contrast, individuals with higher education levels (levels 3–5) showed negative interaction values, suggesting that online health information use was associated with lower HL in this subgroup (Figure 5C).

IV. Discussion

This study advances the understanding of factors associated with HL by applying explainable machine learning to nationwide, population-based data. By incorporating survey weights during model training based on the KHPS’s probability-proportional stratified sampling, the study enhances national representativeness and strengthens generalizability. The XGBoost–SHAP analysis quantified and visualized nonlinear relationships and interaction effects among HL predictors, thereby improving the interpretability of complex patterns. These findings identify key predictors and interaction patterns that can inform tailored patient education strategies and support targeted interventions. Furthermore, the results may guide the future development of clinical decision support tools for HL assessment.

1. Research Implications

The SHAP interaction analysis revealed several noteworthy patterns. Consistent with prior studies, older age and lower educational attainment were generally associated with lower HL [6,9], which the dependence analysis confirmed. However, the interaction analysis uncovered an atypical pattern among individuals aged 60–80 with lower education levels. This group corresponds to adults at or beyond the legal retirement age in Korea [20]. Previous research has shown that retirement is associated with positive behavioral changes such as reduced smoking and alcohol use, weight loss, and increased physical activity [21,22]. Although lower-educated individuals may have had limited exposure to health information earlier in life, this stage may represent a turning point where their motivation to learn about health increases. In contrast, highly educated individuals may already possess sufficient HL, resulting in smaller age-related gains.

Our findings extend previous research on online health information use and HL [23,24] by showing that this relationship is not uniformly positive and varies significantly by age. Among younger adults in their early twenties, online health information use was associated with lower HL, possibly due to information overload and difficulty filtering the large volume of available content. Conversely, for those aged 60 and older, online health information use was positively associated with HL, likely reflecting more deliberate and goaloriented information-seeking. Older adults who overcome digital barriers tend to engage in intentional, systematic learning processes that strengthen HL [25].

The SHAP dependence analysis also highlighted the nonlinear effects of continuous variables. HL declined sharply after age 60, likely reflecting cognitive decline, reduced digital access, and deteriorating physical function. Both HRQOL and household income exhibited nonlinear relationships: at lower levels, both were negatively associated with HL, but once a threshold was reached, their effects became positive, although with diminishing returns at higher levels. This suggests that individuals lacking adequate health functioning or financial resources may struggle to engage with health information, but once these needs are met, their motivation and ability to manage health improve before plateauing. These nonlinear patterns underscore the methodological contribution of this study. XGBoost has a strong capacity to capture nonlinear relationships and identify complex interactions among predictors without requiring their prior specification. Although the models performed similarly, linearity assumptions of logistic regression limit its ability to detect such patterns unless polynomial or interaction terms are explicitly specified.

Finally, this study underscores the importance of health-related variables in understanding HL. While prior research has primarily focused on sociodemographic factors [6,7], our results show that, beyond age and education, health-related variables—such as online health information use, prior information-seeking experience, self-rated health, HRQOL, and chronic disease burden—are also significant. Thus, HL is shaped not only by socioeconomic context but also by personal health perceptions, behaviors, and engagement patterns.

2. Implications for Practice

For older adults aged 60–80 with lower educational attainment, the positive interaction observed during this transitional period suggests opportunities for targeted interventions. Traditional approaches often rely on simplified, one-directional communication [4,26]; however, empowerment-focused strategies may be more effective in sustaining motivation. In age-differentiated digital health interventions, systems should be designed to address the distinct needs of different age groups. For older adults aged 60 years and older, interventions should begin with foundational digital literacy training, including smartphone use, application installation, and basic internet searches. Given that limited digital literacy remains a major barrier [27], healthcare providers can demonstrate these steps directly on patients’ own devices to foster confidence and promote independence. In contrast, for younger adults, particularly those in their early twenties, the focus should shift from increasing the frequency of information-seeking to strengthening critical appraisal skills. Frameworks such as the CRAAP test (currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, and purpose) may be useful for evaluating the credibility of online health information [28].

To enhance behavioral engagement, interventions should extend beyond information provision to actively cultivate health-related attitudes and behaviors. Providers can design learning-oriented interactions that connect health information to patients’ daily routines and lived experiences [1,29]. For example, counseling sessions may incorporate the teach-back method to confirm understanding, followed by collaborative goal-setting to establish concrete and achievable behavior changes. Self-monitoring tools, such as paper-based logs or mobile health trackers, can help patients visualize progress, while regular feedback during follow-up visits can reinforce positive behaviors and address emerging barriers [30].

3. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional study design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between predictors and HL; therefore, all identified associations should be interpreted with caution. Longitudinal studies are therefore needed to clarify temporal relationships. Second, the data were collected in 2021 during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have inflated the observed effects of online engagement. Future studies should compare data collected before and after the pandemic to determine whether these patterns persist. Finally, the study was conducted in South Korea, a relatively homogeneous society in terms of ethnicity and language. Consequently, cultural and linguistic diversity in HL could not be adequately assessed. Cross-national studies are needed to examine how HL predictors and their interactions differ across varied sociocultural and healthcare contexts.

Nonetheless, this study identified complex, nonlinear interactions among predictors of HL using explainable machine learning approaches. The unexpected positive patterns observed among older adults with lower educational attainment, together with the age-dependent effects of online health information use, underscore the need for tailored, age-stratified interventions. These findings support the development of patient-centered educational strategies that strengthen patients’ engagement in their own care.
