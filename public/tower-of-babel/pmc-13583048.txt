Combined Application of a Helix‐Fixation Leadless Pacemaker and a Subcutaneous Implantable Cardioverter‐Defibrillator in a Dialysis Patient: A Case Report
Authors: ZhaoWei Zhou, Wenbo Zhang, Jie Pang, Ying Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCombined use of S‐ICD and LP provides an effective alternative for patients requiring defibrillation and pacing while avoiding transvenous leads. This strategy is particularly valuable for patients with a high risk of infection, though careful consideration must be given to minimizing potential device–device interactions.Keywords: helix‐fixation Leadless pacemaker, hemodialysis, sinus arrest, subcutaneous implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator, ventricular tachycardia

Introduction

Leadless pacemakers (LPs) and subcutaneous implantable cardioverter‐defibrillators (S‐ICDs) can reduce transvenous implantation‐related infections and lead‐associated complications in patients at high risk of infection or with limited venous access. Their combined use provides a strategy entirely free of transvenous leads for patients requiring both pacing and defibrillation. However, the risk of inappropriate shocks due to cross‐sensing must be carefully managed. Here, we report a patient with ischemic cardiomyopathy and uremia who was receiving hemodialysis. The patient had an existing S‐ICD and underwent implantation of a helix‐fixation leadless pacemaker for symptomatic bradycardia. This case aims to provide clinical experience for the management of similar patients through cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) selection and perioperative screening.

Case Presentation

A 56‐year‐old male patient presented to a local hospital on March 31, 2024, with recurrent chest tightness and shortness of breath. He had a 5‐year history of chronic heart failure, with a lowest left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 22%. He had been receiving thrice‐weekly hemodialysis via a functional left forearm arteriovenous fistula for 10 years. He also had a history of Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and hypertension (HTN).

On April 1, 2024, the coronary angiography (CAG) revealed 80% stenosis in the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) and chronic total occlusion of the mid‐LAD, 70% stenosis in the distal left circumflex artery, and 70% stenosis in the mid right coronary artery. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was subsequently performed with the implantation of two stents in the LAD. A diagnosis of ischemic cardiomyopathy was made. After discharge, the patient continued guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT), including dapagliflozin (20 mg daily), metoprolol succinate extended ‐release (23.75 mg daily), enteric‐coated aspirin (100 mg daily), clopidogrel bisulfate (75 mg daily), atorvastatin calcium (20 mg daily), and sacubitril/valsartan (50 mg twice daily). The patient had paroxysmal atrial flutter with both high stroke and bleeding risks. After thorough discussion with the family, anticoagulation was declined.

On April 7, 2024, the patient was readmitted due to sudden syncope at home. His serum potassium level was 3.34 mmol/L. Electrocardiography (ECG) revealed ventricular tachycardia at 212 bpm (Figure 1). Sinus rhythm was restored after electrical cardioversion, and the patient was subsequently transferred to our hospital.

Methods

Electrocardiography on admission demonstrated sinus rhythm with a QRS duration of 128 ms. Ultrasound cardiac echocardiography (UCG) revealed global cardiac enlargement, with a left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter (LVEDD) of 94 mm, an LVEF of 23%, reduced right ventricular function, moderate pulmonary hypertension, and moderate mitral and tricuspid regurgitation. Holter monitoring showed sinus rhythm with an average heart rate of 71 bpm, frequent premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), and episodes of non‐sustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT). The admission diagnoses included ischemic cardiomyopathy status post‐PCI, frequent PVCs, and paroxysmal VT, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional Class III‐IV heart failure, paroxysmal atrial flutter, diabetic neuropathy, maintenance hemodialysis, and hypertension.

The LAD had been completely revascularized with post‐procedural Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) flow Grade 3. The patient had no chest pain or acute ST‐segment changes, providing no evidence of ischemia. Given the extremely high risk of repeat invasive examinations or stress testing, no routine ischemic evaluation was performed before the procedure. The patient met the Class I indications for ICD implantation [1]. At the time, the electrocardiogram showed no left bundle branch block (LBBB), a QRS duration of less than 130 ms, there was no indication for Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy (CRT), and anti‐bradycardia pacing was not required. However, due to long‐term hemodialysis and concomitant T2DM, the patient was considered at high risk for CIEDs‐related infection [2]. After thorough discussion with the patient and his family, and after obtaining informed consent, S‐ICD implantation was chosen [1].

On April 11, 2024, the patient underwent implantation of an S‐ICD (EMBLEM A209, Boston Scientific). The intraoperative PRAETORIAN score was 30 [3], and no defibrillation threshold (DFT) testing was performed. Postoperatively, the SMART Pass function of the S‐ICD was activated during device programming [4]. The S‐ICD was programmed with a conditional shock zone at 200 bpm and a shock zone at 230 bpm. The patient recovered well after surgery. Pre‐discharge Holter demonstrated sinus rhythm with an average heart rate of 69 bpm and frequent PVCs.

On July 29, 2024 (three months post S‐ICD implantation), the patient experienced multiple ICD shocks during hemodialysis. Device interrogation revealed a total of 24 VT/ventricular fibrillation (VF) episodes, all of which were appropriately detected (Figure 2). Oversensing was noted. The sensing vector was therefore changed from the primary vector to the secondary vector. His antiarrhythmic medication was adjusted.

Successful termination of ventricular fibrillation with a single 80 J shock.

On December 9, 2024, the patient experienced amaurosis during hemodialysis and was readmitted to the hospital. His serum potassium was 3.55 mmol/L, and his serum calcium was 2.1 mmol/L. An ECG demonstrated marked sinus bradycardia, sinus arrest, and ventricular escape rhythm (Figure 3). DCG showed sinus bradycardia with an average heart rate of 55 bpm, a minimum heart rate of 49 bpm, and a maximum heart rate of 64 bpm. Follow‐up UCG revealed global cardiac enlargement, with an LVEDD of 72 mm and an LVEF of 36%, reduced right ventricular function, mild pulmonary hypertension, and mild mitral and tricuspid regurgitation.

Electrocardiogram demonstrating sinus arrest with ventricular escape rhythm (33 bpm).

The patient was diagnosed with sick sinus syndrome (SSS) and met the Class Ic indication criteria for anti‐bradycardia pacing therapy. Given the absence of suitable venous access, the patient was considered an appropriate candidate for leadless pacemaker implantation [5].

On December 12, 2024, the patient underwent implantation of a helix‐fixation leadless pacemaker (Aveir VR, Abbott Medical, USA). Preoperative device interrogation confirmed normal sensing in all three S‐ICD sensing vectors. Intravenous cefuroxime (0.25 g) was administered 30 min before the incision, with the dose adjusted for end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) and continued every 12 h for 48 h postoperatively. Under femoral venous access and systemic heparinization, right ventriculography was performed to delineate the tricuspid valve and right ventricular apex. The LP was delivered to the lower right ventricular septum via a deflectable sheath, and final positioning was confirmed by contrast injection in RAO 30°and LAO 45°projections. Adequate safety distances from the apex and tricuspid valve were verified.

Pre‐fixation mapping was then performed. A marked injury current elevation was observed, with an impedance of 360 Ω, sensing amplitude of 3.7 mV, and pacing threshold of 1.25 V@0.4 ms. S‐ICD sensing was subsequently assessed under different LP pacing conditions. During low‐output pacing (1.5 V@0.4 ms) at 100 bpm, no oversensing was observed in any of the three S‐ICD sensing vectors. We repeated the S‐ICD sensing assessment during high‐output pacing (Figure 4A). Based on these findings, we decided to deploy the LP at this position. During screw‐in fixation, the impedance elevation and injury current remained stable and satisfactory.

S‐ICD waveform screening. (A) Occasional pacing‐spike oversensing was observed in the primary and alternate sensing vectors. (B) Occasional pacing‐spike oversensing was observed in the primary and secondary sensing vectors. (C) No oversensing was observed in any of the three S‐ICD sensing vectors.

Following the stability test, repeat electrical measurements showed a pacing threshold of 1.0 V, a sensing amplitude of 7.5 mV, and an impedance of 560 Ω. During low‐output pacing (1.5 V@0.4 ms) at 100 bpm, no oversensing was observed. We repeated S‐ICD sensing during high‐output pacing (Figure 4B). The LP was therefore released successfully. The introducer sheath was removed, and a figure‐of‐eight stitch was placed. The position of the two devices was optimal on fluoroscopy projections (Figure 5). The total procedure time was 1.5 h.

Postoperative chest radiographs following LP implantation: (A) Posteroanterior view. (B) Left lateral view.

Postoperative UCG showed no pericardial effusion or procedure‐related complications. The patient received regular hemodialysis after the procedure.

Conclusion and Results

Postoperative S‐ICD interrogation confirmed normal sensing in all three vectors during pacing (2.5 V@0.4 ms) at 80 bpm (Figure 4C) under different body positions (supine, sitting, standing). Ultimately, the S‐ICD was programmed to use the secondary sensing vector, while the LP was programmed for bipolar pacing with an output of 2.5 V@0.4 ms and a lower pacing rate of 45 bpm. No inappropriate detection or aborted therapy occurred during pacing testing.

During pacemaker clinic follow‐up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post‐implantation, LP pacing parameters remained stable, ventricular pacing burden was < 1%, and no abnormal S‐ICD sensing events were detected. At 1‐year follow‐up, the patient's functional status had improved to NYHA Class II, and device interrogation revealed remaining battery capacities of 94% for the LP and 53% for the S‐ICD. No remote monitoring was utilized.

Discussion

Traditional transvenous CIEDs are associated with long‐term complications [6], including lead wear, pocket hematoma, and lead‐related infection. Among these, hemodialysis‐dependent ESKD has been repeatedly confirmed as one of the strongest independent predictors of lead‐related infection. This patient had a history of long‐term hemodialysis, ischemic cardiomyopathy, paroxysmal ventricular tachycardia, and T2DM. He was therefore at high risk for CIEDs infection. For such patients, device selection should consider individualized alternative strategies based on the 2025 Appropriate Use Criteria for CIEDs [7]. In patients without a bradycardia pacing indication, a subcutaneous or extravascular ICD can completely avoid transvenous leads. When a pacing indication exists, LP becomes a more appropriate option. At the time of his initial presentation, there was no indication for CRT or anti‐bradycardia pacing. S‐ICD implantation was a Class 1 indication for secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death (SCD) [1].

DFT testing is routinely recommended during S‐ICD implantation. However, results from the 2024 PRAETORIAN‐DFT study [3] demonstrated that a high PRAETORIAN score is an independent predictor of DFT failure. In patients with a PRAETORIAN score < 90, the success rate of a 65 J DFT test was 99%, while the success rate at 80 J reached 100%. In the present case, the patient had severely impaired cardiac function (LVEF 23%) and a PRAETORIAN score of 30 during S‐ICD implantation; therefore, intraoperative DFT testing was not performed. Subsequent follow‐up confirmed that the S‐ICD was able to accurately detect and successfully terminate VT/VF episodes.

Eight months after S‐ICD implantation, the patient developed sinus arrest with a ventricular escape rhythm and subsequently required anti‐bradycardia pacing. Investigation of the event found no acute electrolyte disturbance. Holter monitoring recorded significant sinus bradycardia throughout the day and night, even on days without hemodialysis. The patient required long‐term low‐dose oral beta‐blocker therapy to prevent malignant arrhythmias. Moreover, this medication was a guideline‐recommended, essential therapy and could not be discontinued. Therefore, permanent pacing was a Class I indication [5]. Because the S‐ICD lacks pacing capability, and considering the patient's high infection risk and lack of suitable venous access, LP implantation was considered the preferred strategy [7]. In addition, the patient had ischemic cardiomyopathy with global cardiac enlargement. Unlike passive‐fixation single‐chamber LP [8], the helix‐fixation LP allows noninvasive mapping [9] before active screw deployment into the right ventricular endocardium. By assessing the injury current elevation and predicted pacing parameters, an optimal ventricular pacing site can be identified, thereby reducing myocardial injury and procedure‐related complications. Furthermore, the helix‐fixation LP system offers a long‐life battery [10], retrievability [11], and the potential for future upgrade to a dual‐chamber leadless pacing system [12]. At that time, no atrial LP was available, and the patient was expected to have a low pacing burden. Therefore, the Aveir VR was chosen for implantation.

The S‐ICD and LP are two independent devices without direct communication or data transmission capabilities. Implanting a pacemaker in a patient with an existing S‐ICD may increase the risk of inappropriate shocks (IAS), as pacing spikes, paced wide QRS complexes, and T waves can be sensed by the S‐ICD, leading to double or triple counting. Consequently, the system may then misclassify these signals and deliver an IAS [13]. In 2023, Giuseppe M. Calvagna et al. retrospectively analyzed 13 high‐infection‐risk patients who underwent combined implantation of an S‐ICD and an LP (Micra, Medtronic) between 2018 and 2022 [14]. The LP lower rate was programmed to 40 bpm, resulting in a ventricular pacing burden of 5%. During a mean follow‐up of 35 months, no cases of S‐ICD double counting or undersensing were observed, and no device‐related complications or infections occurred. These findings demonstrated that combined implantation of an S‐ICD and an LP is both safe and feasible. More recently, a single‐center Canadian study [15] analyzed 11 patients who underwent combined S‐ICD and LP therapy between 2013 and 2024. Among them, five patients had a pre‐existing S‐ICD before LP implantation, five underwent LP implantation first, and one underwent simultaneous implantation. The implanted LP systems included Micra VR (8 patients), Micra AV (2 patients), and Aveir VR (1 patient). Five patients (45.5%) were pacing dependent. Over a mean follow‐up of 32.9 ± 19.2 months, S‐ICD oversensing events occurred in five patients, but no inappropriate shocks were reported. In patients who underwent LP implantation before S‐ICD implantation, no inappropriate sensing occurred during DFT testing. These findings further support that combined S‐ICD and LP therapy does not increase the risk of IAS. Nevertheless, closer monitoring for oversensing may be warranted in pacing‐dependent patients.

The major factors influencing S‐ICD oversensing include the implantation site of the LP, pacing rate, and high‐output pacing. Nieves et al. reported simultaneous implantation of an LP and an S‐ICD in a single procedure [16], utilizing intraoperative screening to ensure device‐device compatibility. In our case, although the implantation was staged rather than simultaneous, we adopted a similar principle of intraoperative testing. S‐ICD vector screening was performed simultaneously during the helix‐fixation LP implantation procedure. Pre‐fixation mapping of the LP was conducted, and after confirming satisfactory injury current, impedance, sensing amplitude, and pacing threshold, high‐rate pacing tests under both high‐ and low‐output settings were performed to identify the optimal implantation site free of S‐ICD cross‐sensing. After screw fixation of the helix‐fixation LP, repeat high‐rate pacing tests under both high‐ and low‐output conditions were conducted to further confirm the absence of S‐ICD oversensing. As the patient was not pacing‐dependent, the helix‐fixation LP only required a low backup pacing rate of 40–45 bpm. Before the implantation of the LP, since the primary vector observed a double‐counting phenomenon and the alternate vector had an excessively low amplitude at the intrinsic rhythm, the secondary vector was programmed. During the LP implantation process, there was no oversensing at low bipolar output (2.5 V@0.4 ms). 2.5 V@0.4 ms is the working voltage of LP, so we chose Secondary as the programmed vector. When increasing the LP output voltage to 6.0 V@0.4 ms, over‐sensing was observed. This indicates that the preferred working voltage for LP should be below 6.0 V@0.4 ms.

In this case, no procedure‐related complications occurred during the 1‐year follow‐up period. Device interrogation demonstrated stable pacing parameters without pacing or sensing abnormalities. Nevertheless, a longer‐term follow‐up remains necessary. This study is limited by its single‐case nature. The patient was not pacemaker‐dependent, and only a single‐chamber ventricular LP was available at that time. VVI pacing does not provide atrioventricular synchrony. If the pacing burden increases significantly in the future, the addition of an atrial LP may need to be considered.

This combined strategy may be considered in selected patients. Specifically, a leadless pacemaker presents a viable option for those at high infection risk and with limited venous access who require both S‐ICD and bradycardia pacing. However, meticulous intraoperative screening for device‐device interactions is essential. Ultimately, this approach requires further evaluation in larger studies.
