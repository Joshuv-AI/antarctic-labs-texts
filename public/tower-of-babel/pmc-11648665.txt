Fluctuating charge-density-wave correlations in the three-band Hubbard model
Authors: Peizhi Mai, Benjamin Cohen-Stead, Thomas A. Maier, Steven Johnston
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe high-temperature superconducting cuprates feature modulations in the spin and charge density, which intertwine with each other and superconductivity. The charge density modulations have been observed in almost all cuprate families; however, their behavior varies widely across different families of materials and temperature ranges. We present the observation of combined fluctuating spin and charge density modulations in large-scale quantum Monte Carlo calculations for the three-band Hubbard model. We find that the model’s charge modulations are decoupled from the spin modulations, with a charge incommensurability that decreases with hole doping. This behavior is quantitatively consistent with high-temperature experimental measurements on a broad range of cuprate families and contrasts with the modulations observed in the single-band Hubbard model.

Understanding the anomalous normal-state of the high-temperature (high-Tc) cuprates is one of the most fascinating and unresolved problems in condensed matter physics (1). Strong electron–electron interactions govern this state, producing a highly correlated electron liquid that defies effective single-particle descriptions. In particular, underdoped cuprates exhibit intertwined order at low temperature T (1, 2), where combined unidirectional spin- and charge-density-waves (i.e., stripes) that can compete or cooperate with superconductivity in nontrivial ways. For example, stripe order can compete with superconductivity (3, 4) or drive the formation of pair density waves, depending on the material (5–8). Charge-density-waves, associated with the charge component of the stripes (9), have also observed across nearly all families of cuprates (10–12), sometimes extending up to as high as room temperature. Thus, understanding the origin of these spin and charge correlations is crucial to understanding the cuprate normal state and unraveling the mystery of cuprate superconductivity.

The single-band Hubbard model is widely believed to be the low-energy effective model for the high-Tc cuprates (13, 14). The last decade has seen impressive advances in obtaining reliable numerical solutions to this model (15). For example, several numerical methods have obtained direct evidence for fluctuating (16–18) or static (19–21) stripe order in its strong coupling regime relevant to the cuprates. The calculations have also produced a general picture where the energy differences between competing metastable states are minimal, rendering the ground state highly sensitive to model parameters (19, 22, 23). At finite-T, determinant quantum Monte Carlo (DQMC) and minimally entangled typical thermal states (METTS) have demonstrated an interlocking of spin and charge stripes in the single-band model (16, 17, 20), aligning with the Yamada relation (24) and consistent with experimental observations in doped La-based cuprates at low temperature (10, 24–29). However, these simulations are incompatible with observations of a decreasing wave vector for the charge modulations with increasing hole-density in YBa2Cu3O6+δ (YBCO) (30–36), (Bi,Pb)2.12Sr1.88CuO6+δ (Bi2201) (37) and HgBa2CuO4+δ (Hg1201) (38). It has recently been proposed that this decrease may be a universal behavior of the high-temperature charge fluctuations across all cuprates (10).

Due to its coarse-grained nature, the single-band Hubbard model has limited ability to encapsulate specific-material characteristics among different cuprate families. It also cannot capture the crucial hybridization between the Cu and O orbitals, which govern excitations across the charge-transfer gap. These effects are instead described by the three-band Hubbard (or Emery) model, which explicitly includes the Cu 3d and O 2p degrees of freedom (41). Cluster dynamical mean-field theory and (DCA) studies of this model have observed a superconducting transition (42–45) with a transition temperature Tc whose dependence on the charge transfer gap aligns with experimental data across various cuprates. Recent density matrix renormalization group studies on two-leg three-band ladders have also found evidence for a pair density wave superconducting ground state (46, 47). Nonetheless, it remains an open question to what extent the spin and charge stripes in the three-band model are consistent with the experimental observations in the cuprates. So far, finite-T DQMC calculations have observed fluctuating spin stripes (48) but reported no corresponding charge component. Similarly, large-scale two-dimensional tensor network calculations have only detected a weak charge modulation that appears to vanish in the thermodynamic limit (49). Whether the three-band model supports a charge modulation in addition to the observed spin stripes remains an open question.

Here, we present a detailed DQMC study of the fluctuating spin- and charge-correlations in the doped three-band Hubbard model. Advances in algorithms and computational power have allowed us to perform simulations at lower temperatures than the previous DQMC study (48). We find evidence for fluctuating charge stripes in addition to the previously observed spin stripes, in both momentum and real space, for a range of hole densities and charge-transfer energies. Notably, we find that the incommensurability of the charge correlations decreases with hole doping, opposite to the trend found for the single-band model but consistent with the high-T behavior observed across different cuprate families (10). In contrast, for the electron-doped case, we find that the density-dependent charge correlations in the three-band model are very similar to those observed in the single-band model.

Results

Fig. 1 summarizes our main results. Our DQMC simulations reveal the presence of decoupled fluctuating spin and charge modulations in the three-band Hubbard model. The former are consistent with prior simulations (48). In real space, these correlations manifest as short-range correlations with a very short correlation length. In momentum space, the spin correlations appear as Lorentzian peaks centered around q=(0.5±δs,0.5) in units of 2π/a, as sketched in Fig. 1A. Similarly, the charge correlations produce peaks in the charge susceptibility at q=(±δc,0), as illustrated in Fig. 1B. If the two components form an intertwined stripe, then one expects δc=2δs. [Note, our rectangular cluster geometry frustrates the equivalent peaks at (0.5,0.5±δs) and (0,±δc) that appear in simulations on square lattices (48).]

Our simulations show that the incommensurability of the spin modulations δs increases with hole doping, as shown in Fig. 1C. This behavior is consistent with prior results (48) and in quantitative agreement with experimental observations on a subset of cuprates. In contrast, the charge incommensurability δc decreases with hole doping as shown in Fig. 1D for representative values of the bare charge transfer energy ΔCT=1.5 and 3 eV. The results for ΔCT=3 eV are in excellent agreement with the wavevectors extracted from X-ray scattering measurements on Bi2201 (37) and Hg1201 (38) while the smaller value of ΔCT agrees fairly well with the qualitative behavior observed in YBCO (30–36). The opposite doping dependence of δs and δc indicate that the spin and charge modulations are not intertwined at this temperature. The behavior of the charge modulations presented here is consistent with the universal high-temperature behaviors in experiment (10) and in contrast to the predictions of the single-band Hubbard model (17, 50). We will now present the various measured quantities that lead to these results. Throughout, we work in hole language, where the number of excess holes in the unit cell is ρ=⟨n⟩−1.

Fig. 2 plots the static Cu–Cu staggered spin χstag,s(r,ω=0) and charge χc(r,ω=0) susceptibilities in real space. [Here, we include a ri-dependent staggered phase factor exp(i(π,π)·rj) to the spin correlations only, so that regions with the same blue or red color represent short-range antiferromagnetic domains but with a relative π-phase shift.] Results are shown for several hole doping densities and obtained on 16×4 clusters with an onsite Hubbard interaction Ud=6eV on the Cu 3d-orbitals, a fixed charge-transfer energy ΔCT=3 eV, and inverse temperature β = 14 eV−1. A clear unidirectional spin stripe is observed in Fig. 2A for ρ>0.1 holes per unit cell, consistent with prior results (48). The charge stripe pattern is also apparent in Fig. 2B; however, its overall amplitude and correlation length are much smaller than those for the spin correlations. This observation suggests that the charge modulations have a lower temperature scale compared with the spin modulations, consistent with predictions for the single-band model (17, 50) and the weak signals previously reported in the three-band model (49).

Fig. 3 A and D plot the results as a function of temperature with a fixed hole-doping ρ=1/8 and ΔCT=3eV. In this case, a double peak structure appears in the Cu–Cu spin correlations as the temperature is lowered, indicative of the formation of fluctuating spin stripe correlations. The formation of charge modulations is also apparent in Fig. 3D as the system cools down. While the amplitude of the peaks is smaller than their spin counterpart, the charge peaks can be discerned directly at lower temperatures without the need for curve fitting.

Fig. 3 B and E examine the hole-density dependence of the correlations at fixed ΔCT=3 eV and β = 14 eV1. Here, the spin correlations evolve from a single antiferromagnetic peak centered at (0.5,0.5) to incommensurate peaks at (0.5±δs,0.5) with increasing hole-doping, again consistent with previous studies (48) and the single-band Hubbard model (16, 17, 50). In the single-band case, however, the charge incommensurability δc is increases commensurately with the spin correlations with hole doping (17, 50) (reproduced in Fig. 4C), consistent with an intertwined stripe. In contrast, we find that δc decreases with hole doping ρ in the three-band model, as shown in Fig. 3E and summarized in Fig. 1C. Thus, there appears to be a fundamental difference between the spin- and charge-correlations of the two models at these temperatures, even though the single-band model correctly captures the pairing correlations of the three-band model (44).

The charge transfer energy ΔCT is a key parameter controlling the property variations across different families of cuprates (51–53). Therefore, Fig. 3 C and F explore the evolution of the spin and charge correlations, respectively, as a function of ΔCT at 1/8-hole doping and β = 14 eV1. Decreasing ΔCT enhances the charge susceptibility while suppressing the spin susceptibility, which also suggests the decoupling between charge and spin fluctuations at high temperatures. The double-peak stripe structure in the charge susceptibility persists as a function of ΔCT and is enhanced relative to a central q=0 peak as ΔCT decreases. (The latter also develops as ΔCT decreases due to the transfer of holes from the O to Cu orbitals.) Interestingly, we also observe that the charge correlations have a nonmonotonic dependence on the charge transfer energy, and appear to be strongest for ΔCT=2 eV. The overall dependence on ΔCT indicates that the charge correlations observed here are robust over a wide range of charge transfer values, consistent with the ubiquitous presence of (CDW) correlations across different cuprates (54). The average value of the Fermion sign increases significantly as ΔCT decreases, allowing us to reach lower temperatures. For example, SI Appendix show results down to β = 25 eV−1 for ΔCT=1.5 eV, where the charge correlations become even more apparent. Therefore, we expect that these correlations will persist at lower temperatures.

So far, we have focused on the Cu–Cu charge correlations, where the strongest signals are observed. The interorbital charge correlations (SI Appendix) are significantly weaker than the diagonal intraorbital ones, presumably because we have neglected any Cu–O interactions. Fig. 4 A and B summarize the intraorbital Ox-Ox and Oy-Oy charge correlations, respectively, along the (qx,0) direction as a function of hole-doping ρ while fixing β = 14 eV1 and ΔCT=3 eV. On a square lattice, one would expect the Ox-Ox and Oy-Oy correlations to differ when taken along the same momentum cut but to be identical for cuts rotated by π/2. Our 16×4 clusters introduce a slight anisotropy in the oxygen occupations (SI Appendix) and corresponding density–density correlations. Comparing to the Cu–Cu correlation in Fig. 3E, the Ox-Ox and Oy-Oy correlations shown in Fig. 4 A and B bear a closer resemblance to the charge correlations obtained for single-band model as shown in Fig. 4C. In particular, the Oy-Oy correlations are similar, with double peaks appearing and separating from each other as the doping increases. However, in real space, neither Ox-Ox nor Oy-Oy show a clear unidirectional stripe pattern (SI Appendix), indicating that the O correlations are still weak at this temperature.

Discussion

The doping evolution of the Cu–Cu spin and charge modulations are qualitatively, and in some cases quantitatively, consistent with observations on a subset of the high-Tc cuprates. In particular, the evolution of the charge modulations with doping is in excellent agreement with many Cu L-edge resonant X-ray scattering probes, as summarized in Fig. 1D, which are sensitive to the Cu charge density.

Our results support the proposal that the high-T charge correlations behave very differently from the low-T intertwined stripe order that has been observed in several cuprate families (10). While we can observe robust charge correlations down to β=25eV−1(T=580K) in some cases, it is unclear how the high-T correlations connect to the low-T correlations, if at all. At our simulation temperatures, there are a significant number of holes thermally excited across the charge transfer gap. The three-band Hubbard model can capture this charge transfer, while the single-band model cannot, leading to qualitatively different behavior. One possibility is that the freezing out of the charge transfer excitations will lock the charge modulations into the spin modulations, resulting in an intertwined order consistent with the single-band model. Constrained path quantum Monte Carlo calculations and other methods capable of bridging the high- and low-temperature regimes may be able to shed additional light on this problem.

Another outstanding issue is the relative strength of the spin and charge correlations. The single-band Hubbard model predicts (17, 50) that the spin modulations are significantly stronger than the charge modulations, while the situation is reversed in the actual materials (3, 4, 54). Our calculations show that the three-band model has a similar inverted hierarchy, consistent with the vanishing of charge correlations also inferred from 2D tensor network calculations (49). Accounting for this discrepancy may require the inclusion of additional (possibly density dependent) hopping pathways (55, 56), additional O 2p and Cu 3d–O 2p Coulomb interactions, or other couplings like the electron–phonon coupling (57, 58).

Methods

The Model.

We studied the three-band Hubbard model for the CuO2 plane. The Hamiltonian is[1]H=∑i,σ(ϵd−μ)di,σ†di,σ+∑i,α,σ(ϵp−μ)pi,α,σ†pi,α,σ+tpd∑i,j,αPi,jαdi,σ†pj,α,σ+H.c.+tpp∑j,j′,α,α′Qj,j′α,α′pj,α,σ†pj′,α′,σ+H.c.+Ud∑idi,↑†di,↑di,↓†di,↓.

Here, di,σ† (di,σ) creates (annihilates) a spin σ (=↑,↓) hole in the copper dx2−y2 orbital at site i; pi,α,σ† (pi,α,σ) creates (annihilates) a spin σ hole in the oxygen pα (α=x,y) orbital at site j; tpd and tpp are the magnitude of the nearest neighbor Cu–O and O–O hopping, respectively; Pi,jα and Qj,j′α,α′ are the associated phase factors; ϵd and ϵp are the Cu and O site energies; μ is the chemical potential; and Udd is the on-site Cu Hubbard interaction. The orbital phase convention given by Pi,jα and Qj,j′α,α′ is sketched in figure 1 of ref. 45. We only consider the effect of on-site interaction in the Cu dx2−y2 orbital since including the on-site oxygen repulsion has a minor effect on the results but leads to a more severe sign problem (44, 45, 59).

Throughout, we fix tpd=1.13, tpp=0.49, ϵd=0, and Udd=6 (all in units of eV) and adjust the charge transfer Δ=ϵp−ϵd by changing the value of ϵp. The value of μ is chosen to set the cluster’s filling. Additional results for other values of tpp, different cluster sizes, and inverse temperatures are provided in SI Appendix.

Determinant Quantum Monte Carlo.

We solved Eq. 1 using unbiased DQMC simulations, as implemented in the SmoQyDQMC.jl package (60). We tuned the chemical potential μ for each parameter set to obtain the desired density within an accuracy of O(10−4). We performed 8,000 warm-up sweeps for each Markov chain, followed by 80,000 measurement sweeps. Both equal- and unequal-time measurements were conducted once per sweep. We varied the number of Markov chains between 2,500 and 12,600 for each parameter set, depending on the severity of the sign problem and the intensity of the stripe pattern. The details of the average sign are provided in SI Appendix.

Measurements.

A previous DQMC study of spin-stripes in the three-band Hubbard model found that they are fluctuating in nature at high temperatures (48), with short-range spatial correlations that are fluctuating in time. In general, the zero-frequency susceptibility (ω=0) is more sensitive to fluctuating short-range order compared to equal-time correlation functions (τ=0) because the former integrates over temporal fluctuations (17, 50, 61).
