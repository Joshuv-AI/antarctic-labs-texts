Locally Recurrent Rectal Cancer in the Molecular Era: A Scoping Review of Biomarkers of Oncologic Outcome following Salvage Surgery
Authors: Jennifer K Vu, Evonne Younan, Sascha Karunaratne, Sarah Sutherland, Paul A Sutton, Y Nancy You, Oliver Peacock, Daniel Steffens, Michael J Solomon, Kilian G M Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadical salvage surgery offers the only chance of cure for patients with locally recurrent rectal cancer (LRRC), yet up to 50% develop disease recurrence after surgery. Identification of those at highest risk of disease recurrence may improve selection for surgery and intensification of neoadjuvant treatment. This scoping review aimed to identify molecular determinants of oncologic outcomes following surgery for LRRC.Materials and MethodsThis study was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews. MEDLINE, Embase, CENTRAL, and Scopus databases were searched for articles published between 2005 and 2025 that reported associations between blood, tissue, or other molecular factors and postoperative oncologic outcomes (recurrence and survival) in patients undergoing treatment for LRRC.ResultsThe search identified 2799 articles, of which 17 were included. Sample size varied from 21 to 213 patients. Locoregional recurrence rates after surgery ranged from 15% to 52% (various timepoints between 3 and 5 years), while 5-year overall survival ranged from 22% to 53%. Elevated carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) was associated with shorter overall survival in 11 studies, and poorer relapse-free survival in 1 study. Elevated serum carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) was associated with shorter overall survival and disease-specific survival in two studies. No studies evaluating contemporary biomarkers (mutational profile, microsatellite instability, or circulating-tumour DNA) were identified.ConclusionsEvidence evaluating molecular biomarkers in LRRC is limited and has focused on traditional serum biomarkers such as CEA and CA19-9. Contemporary colorectal cancer biomarkers including mutational profile and ctDNA are yet to be studied in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1245/s10434-026-20116-2.Keywords: Molecular biomarkers, Oncologic outcomes, Surgery, Locally recurrent rectal cancer

Exenterative salvage surgery offers durable disease control and the only chance of cure for patients with locally recurrent rectal cancer (LRRC) and is therefore considered the standard of care. Although long-term survival is now achievable in up to 55% of selected patients, the risk of subsequent disease recurrence is considerable, reported to be as high as 55% in contemporary series.1–9 Early recurrence is particularly devastating, given the magnitude of pelvic exenteration surgery, which is associated with a prolonged recovery and significant functional implications.1,10–12 This morbidity is justified by the reasonable chance of long-term disease control or cure (achieved in 45–55% of selected patients in contemporary series), yet predicting early failure, and identifying patients who may undergo potentially futile surgery, is difficult.3,8,13,14 Furthermore, whether intensification of multimodal therapy by integration of re-irradiation, induction chemotherapy, and adjuvant chemotherapy or targeted biological therapy may improve oncologic outcomes remains unclear. Improved risk stratification to identify patients at greatest risk of recurrence following salvage surgery may provide an opportunity to improve patient selection and ensure that (neo)adjuvant therapies are directed toward patients most likely to benefit, thereby improving oncologic outcomes.

Despite recent advances in understanding molecular determinants of outcomes in colorectal cancer more generally, and particularly in patients with stage IV disease, there has been comparatively little work done evaluating prognostic and predictive biomarkers in patients with LRRC.15,16 Studies in primary and locally advanced rectal cancer have explored potential biomarkers such as KRAS, BRAF, TP53, microsatellite instability, and ctDNA to predict therapy response and prognosis, however such studies have typically excluded patients with LRRC.17,18

The objective of this scoping review was to identify all previously evaluated molecular determinants and biomarkers predictive of oncologic outcomes or response to treatment in patients with LRRC. Given the limited and heterogeneous evidence in this area, a scoping review methodology was selected to systematically map the existing literature and identify gaps to inform future research.

Materials and Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) checklist and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews.19,20

Protocol and Registration

The review protocol was registered with the Open Sciences Framework on 13 October 2025 (https://osf.io/a36qb/overview).

Eligibility Criteria

The population/concept/context (PCC) framework was used to develop the search strategy. The population was patients who had undergone surgery for LRRC with curative intent. The concept was molecular factors (e.g., immunophenotypic, genetic, epigenetic, proteomic, metabolomic, transcriptomic, or tumour microenvironment markers) associated with oncologic outcomes (e.g., survival, disease recurrence). The context was curative-intent management in any clinical or research setting, with no geographic restrictions. Articles were included if they contained data relating to associations between blood, tumour tissue, saliva, stool, peritoneal fluid, urine, or other molecular tumour factors and oncologic outcomes.

Databases were interrogated for articles published from 1 January 2005 to 1 October 2025. The search was limited to studies published after 1 January 2005 to ensure results reflect the contemporary understanding of molecular medicine and techniques. No language restrictions were applied, and efforts were made to translate any non-English studies to English. The following study types were included: randomized trials, cohort studies (retrospective and prospective), qualitative studies, abstracts, and published conference proceedings. Case reports were excluded.

Information Sources

A systematic search was conducted across four databases: MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Scopus.

Search

A three-step strategy was used, beginning with an initial limited search of MEDLINE and Embase via Ovid based on the population/concept/context described above to identify relevant articles. The text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, and the index terms used to describe the articles, were used to develop a full search strategy in consultation with a University of Sydney medical librarian for Medline, Embase, CENTRAL, and Scopus. The reference lists of all included studies were screened to identify additional relevant studies, and a gray literature search was conducted using identified sources and those cited in related systematic reviews of a similar topic. If a full text was not found, the authors were to be contacted, however this was not required during this review. The search strategy (included as Supplementary Information), including all identified keywords and index terms, was adapted for each included database and/or information source.

Selection of Sources of Evidence

Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts against the inclusion criteria. Two reviewers (J.V., E.Y.) independently assessed full texts against the inclusion criteria for eligibility. Reasons for exclusion were recorded. Disagreements were resolved by involving a third reviewer (K.G.M.B.). Two reviewers reviewed the extracted data. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with an additional reviewer. Studies that were published in duplicate were only included once, but all versions were to be considered for maximal data extraction.

Data Charting Process

Citations identified through the search were imported into Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for screening.21 The study selection process was documented and presented in a PRISMA flow diagram.20 Data charting from the included sources of evidence was done using forms that have been tested by the team before their use and was done independently by two reviewers.

Data Items

For each study, the data items extracted were study design, sample size, patient demographics (sex, median age), treatment characteristics (proportion of patients undergoing curative intent surgery, neoadjuvant radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy, exenterative surgery, or multivisceral resection), R0 resection rate, oncological outcomes (local or locoregional recurrence rate, overall survival, any other reported oncological outcomes), and biomarker data (biomarker/s evaluated, time point(s) at which biomarkers were evaluated, association with reported oncological outcomes).

Quality Assessment

A formal quality assessment was not conducted because this scoping review was designed to describe the available literature rather than to evaluate study quality.

Results

The search identified 2799 articles. After removal of duplicates, studies were screened against the inclusion and exclusion criteria, resulting in 17 articles being included in the final review (Fig. 1). Sixteen of 17 studies were retrospective cohort studies, and 1 was a protocol for an ongoing prospective cohort study.22 Included study characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Sample size ranged from 21 to 213 patients,23,24 and median patient age ranged from 50 to 65 years (range 19–91 years).24,25 There was considerable variation in the rate of neoadjuvant radiotherapy (10–100% of patients) and neoadjuvant chemotherapy (0.6–44%) between studies.26,27 Rate of exenteration or multivisceral resection ranged from 6% to 75%.25,29 The rate of curative-intent surgery ranged from 60% to 100%,23,28,30–33 and R0 resection was achieved in 48–100% of cases.23,25 Locoregional recurrence rates following curative-intent surgery for LRRC ranged from 15% to 52%,27,32 with the length of follow-up varying from 3 to 5 years. The 5-year overall survival varied from 22% to 52%.24,29 Fifteen studies (88%) evaluated serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), two studies evaluated serum CEA doubling time (CEA-DT), two studies evaluated serum carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), and three studies evaluated other biomarkers (Table 1). Of the two studies that evaluated CA19-9, one included patients with distant disease (liver and paraaortic lymph node metastases but not peritoneal or lung disease).24,31

CEA

Elevated serum CEA was most commonly defined as ≥ 5 ng/ml (in six studies) and ≥ 10 ng/ml (in three studies). The timepoint of CEA measurement was at the time of diagnosis of, or prior to, surgery for LRRC in all included studies. Eleven studies assessed the association between CEA and overall survival (OS), of which ten studies reported that an elevated serum CEA was a negative predictor of OS (Table 2). One study reported no association between serum CEA and OS, however this multivariate analysis also included patients who did not undergo salvage surgery for LRRC.34 Short CEA-DT was reported to be a negative independent predictor of OS in one study (CEA-DT < 150 days; OR 0.268, 95% CI 0.084–0.861, p = 0.0270),35 and of recurrence-free survival (RFS) in another (CEA ≤ 210 days; HR 0.32, 95% CI 0.12–0.87; p = 0.026).32

Other Serum Biomarkers

Elevated serum CA19-9 at the time of diagnosis of LRRC was associated with a shorter OS (HR = 1.58, 95% CI 1.17–2.13, p = 0.003), DSS (p = 0.002), and cancer-specific survival (CSS; p = 0.03) (Table 3).24,31 The ongoing prospective study by Hashimoto and colleagues is the first study to assess the association between circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) after surgery for LRRC and minimal residual disease (MRD), with results pending at the time of this review.22

Tumour Tissue-Based Biomarkers

Two studies that assessed tumour tissue-based markers obtained intraoperatively were identified. Nagata and colleagues reported that the presence of both CD133 and CD144 on tumour tissue immunohistochemistry negatively predicted OS on Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, without including CEA as a confounder, and neither marker alone was significantly associated with OS.37 Bedrosian and colleagues reported no association between tumour tissue staining for p53, Ki-67, or Bcl-2 and either OS or DSS (Table 3).28

No studies evaluating stool, saliva, urine, peritoneal fluid, or other biomarkers were identified.

Discussion

This scoping review demonstrates that the current evidence base for molecular biomarkers in patients undergoing curative intent surgical treatment for LRRC is limited. Among the 17 included studies, only a small number of conventional biomarkers were evaluated, most commonly CEA. Elevated serum CEA is associated with poorer overall and recurrence-free survival in patients undergoing surgery for LRRC. Serum CEA-DT, elevated serum CA19-9, and the presence of both CD133 and CD44 in tumour tissue have also been identified as poor prognostic markers. However, it is important to emphasize that these findings do not reflect analyses designed to evaluate biomarker performance. In almost all studies, biomarkers were included as covariates within multivariable models designed to identify clinical predictors of oncologic outcome, rather than being assessed as candidate prognostic markers with appropriate measures of predictive performance such as discrimination, calibration, or incremental prognostic value. Consequently, the clinical utility of the identified biomarkers for risk stratification is uncertain. Importantly, none of the molecular biomarkers used routinely in contemporary care of primary and metastatic colorectal cancer patients (such as KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, mismatch repair deficiency, microsatellite instability, or ctDNA) have been evaluated in the LRRC population, and no DNA or RNA sequencing data were identified. There is also little evidence of ongoing work in this area, with the exception of a prospective cohort study evaluating ctDNA by Hashimoto and colleagues.22 Collectively, these findings highlight not only the narrow scope of biomarkers studied to date but also the absence of dedicated biomarker-driven research in this field.

Despite major advances in surgical technique that have transformed the management of LRRC from predominantly palliative to offering the potential for long-term survival, distant recurrence remains the most common pattern of failure and the main limitation to durable oncologic control.7 Improvements in preoperative imaging, centralization to high-volume centers, and advances in surgical technique over recent decades have increased rates of R0 resection and improved perioperative outcomes. However, in an era where en bloc pelvic bone and neurovascular resection is now performed routinely at specialized centres, it is unlikely that even more radical “higher and wider” resections will translate to meaningful reductions in the risk of systemic relapse. There is also wide variation and ongoing uncertainty around the role of induction chemotherapy, re-irradiation, and intraoperative radiotherapy as part of multimodal treatment of LRRC. Despite routine use of induction chemotherapy in some centers, available data have not demonstrated improvements in R0 resection, nor disease-free or overall survival rates with this approach.13,38 Re-recurrence rates (local and distant) remain as high as 50–55% even after R0 resection.1,4,7,8 Without improved risk stratification to identify patients at greatest risk of disease recurrence, inform biologically driven (neo)adjuvant strategies, and measure therapeutic response to those neoadjuvant treatments, it is unlikely that long-term oncologic outcomes in LRRC will substantially improve beyond current benchmarks.39,40

Although prognostic and predictive biomarkers have been increasingly characterized in colorectal cancer more broadly (Table 4),41–43 LRRC likely represents a biologically and clinically distinct entity from primary rectal cancer, and findings for primary rectal cancer may not necessarily be applicable to LRRC.16,17 Whether, for example, the established association between RAS/RAF mutations and early treatment failure in advanced primary rectal cancer also applies to LRRC should be specifically evaluated rather than extrapolated.44,45 Patients with LRRC, by definition, have had prior treatment exposures, have altered patho-anatomy, and require beyond-total mesorectal excision (TME) surgery for complete excision. The different patterns of failure in LRRC and the fact that these cancers have failed prior therapy support the concept of a fundamentally different disease process compared with treatment-naive primary tumours.7 Consequently, conventional frameworks such as TNM staging are not applicable or validated in a LRRC population and therefore may offer limited prognostic discrimination. Two recent large, contemporary series have demonstrated the difficulty in reliably predicting long-term oncologic outcomes following surgery for LRRC.46,47 The findings of this scoping review are consistent with a previous review by Gould and colleagues that focused on traditional pathological predictors rather than molecular biomarkers, yet also identified no studies evaluating molecular determinants of outcomes in LRRC.10

These findings highlight a clear need to study LRRC as a distinct disease entity and to evaluate prognostic and predictive biomarkers specifically within this population. Potentially resectable LRRC remains an uncommon presentation, and this group of patients have historically been excluded from clinical trials as they fall outside the more conventional cohorts of either advanced primary rectal cancer or metastatic disease. LRRC case volumes are likely to be relatively low, even at high-volume units, and meaningful progress in biomarker discovery in the form of de novo biomarker studies will therefore require coordinated collaboration between centres. Dedicated translational research programs focused on LRRC are required, ideally utilizing established biomarker discovery roadmaps, with prospective biospecimen collection that is coordinated across centers that specialize in LRRC.48 This is the most realistic framework for systematic evaluation of molecular biomarkers and the development of biologically informed treatment strategies for patients with LRRC. Table 4 demonstrates the extensive biomarker work conducted in other colorectal cancer populations (particular the metastatic setting) and may serve as a list as candidate biomarkers for evaluation in de novo studies of patients with LRRC.41–43

The strengths of this review include its novel focus on molecular biomarkers specifically in the LRRC population and its comprehensive inclusion of all study designs from the past two decades, enabling identification of the scarce evidence on prognostic and predictive biomarkers in LRRC. Limitations of the scoping review design include limited assessment of study quality and heterogeneity across studies, particularly relating to patient population, neoadjuvant therapy use, extent of surgery, and R0 resection rates. This therefore limited comparability between studies and precluded formal meta-analysis. While this scoping review maps the existing evidence, the conclusions are hypothesis-generating and highlight the need for prospective, standardized, multicenter studies in this rare population.

Conclusions

There is currently limited evidence evaluating molecular biomarkers of oncologic outcome in patients undergoing curative intent treatment for LRRC. Most available studies are retrospective and have demonstrated association between conventional markers such as serum CEA and CA19-9 and oncologic outcomes, but these have not been formally evaluated as prognostic biomarkers. The prognostic performance and clinical utility of these markers in LRRC therefore remains uncertain. Contemporary colorectal cancer biomarkers including tumour mutational profile and ctDNA are yet to be studied in this population, and no DNA or RNA sequencing data in LRRC are available. LRRC represents a biologically distinct, more challenging disease entity that carries a high risk of re-recurrence, which justifies de novo biomarker studies in this group. Biomarker-driven risk stratification may guide treatment selection and the development of personalized multimodal treatment strategies.
