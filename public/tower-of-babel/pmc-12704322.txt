Yersinia pestis transmission risk associated with Himalayan marmots (Marmota himalayana) of the Qinghai-Tibet plateau
Authors: Zihang Wang, Jiarong Ren, Haoqiang Ji, Shihao Li, Xinyue Fang, Dongsheng Ren, Yue Wu, Bang Fu, Chunmin Li, Ying Liang, Xiaobo Liu, Liang Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The Himalayan marmot is the primary plague (Yersinia pestis) reservoir on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, driving human transmission in high altitudes. Effective control requires predicting climate, environment, and human activity-driven marmot density changes and multi-dimensional risk assessment. This study used a random forest model to analyze these factors' impact on marmot density and employed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to create a risk framework. Findings show climate remains the dominant driver of marmot density under current conservation policies. In risk assessment, host density and plague detection rate were primary risk sources. Among human factors, human density showed stronger risk correlation than other disturbance indicators. This provides a comprehensive, multi-factor risk assessment method for plateau plague control, improving zoonotic disease prevention systems.

Introduction

As the ‘third pole ‘of the earth, the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP) has a unique and complete ecosystem. It is not only the highest biodiversity enrichment area in the world, but also one of the world's biodiversity hotspots [1]. Its magnificent natural landscape and unique geographical environment continue to attract a large number of tourists through self-driving tours, hiking and other ways to cross-regional tours [2]. However, the frequency of human contact with plague natural foci has increased significantly, and the resulting public health security issues need urgent attention.

It is worth noting that the QTP, as the largest natural plague focus in China, continues to be active at a high level. In recent decades, the region has become the main focus of human plague cases in China [3,4]. According to data from the China Public Health Emergency Database, more than 30 human plague cases were reported within the provinces of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau between 2005 and 2024, accounting for over 50 % of the national total during this period. Plague, an ancient zoonotic infectious disease that has caused global disasters [5], has formed a stable natural focus cycle in the QTP ecosystem. Among them, Himalayan Marmots, as the main storage host, together with its surface parasitic fleas (such as Callopsylla dolabris, Oropsylla silantiewi) constitute the core transmission chain of Yersinia pestis (the plague bacterium) [6]. Historical epidemics reveal that over 70 % of human plague infections in this area stem from contact with Himalayan marmots [7]. Transmission routes involve direct contact with infected marmot carcasses or consumption of their meat [8], as well as indirect transmission through infected livestock like Tibetan sheep [9,10].

Notably, over the past two decades, the intensity of human activities in the QTP region has exhibited a sustained upward trend. The Human Footprint Index (HFI)-a dimensionless composite indicator based on eight-dimensional datasets, including built environment, population density, nighttime lights, cropland, pasture, roads, railways, and navigable waterways [11]-reveals a significant escalation of anthropogenic pressure in this area. With the rapid development of plateau tourism, incoming visitors generally lack awareness of zoonotic exposure prevention, compounded by traditional hunting practices in certain regions [12], resulting in a geometric escalation of cross-species transmission risks for zoonotic diseases. On the other hand, the habitats and behavioral patterns of Himalayan marmots are particularly vulnerable to anthropogenic disturbances [13,14], with studies indicating increased proximity of burrows to roads under such disturbances [15].

Previous studies primarily assessed plague risk by predicting changes in the distribution of Himalayan marmots, the primary host of Y. pestis. Yuan et al. [16]previously analyzed the dynamic patterns of plague on the QTP based on temporal scales. Subsequently, Qian et al. [17] utilized ecological niche models to predict the distribution patterns of Himalayan marmots, thereby inferring plague risk levels. Furthermore, Gao et al. [18]predicted the natural habitat distribution of Himalayan marmots using satellite data. However, none of these studies has addressed the critical pathway of Y. pestis transmission to humans through quantitative analysis.

Against these backdrops, establishing a multifactorial risk assessment model for plague transmission holds critical practical significance. Machine learning methods are increasingly being applied in disease ecology, as they are well-suited to handle complexity and nonlinearity. Whether predicting infectious disease dynamics [19] or exploring host-pathogen interactions [20], these methods demonstrate strong performance. Random forests are a versatile machine learning technique that involves repeatedly sampling from the original dataset to create multiple decision trees. These trees are then aggregated to form a final decision, making it suitable for addressing complex prediction and regression problems [21].This study employs a Random forest model to investigate the effects of climatic factors, environmental factors, and human activities on marmot density. Marmot density is incorporated as a component of the risk framework, and Principal Component Analysis (PCA)—a dimensionality reduction method that maximizes the explained variance across all variables [22]—is applied to conduct multi-dimensional quantitative analysis of multiple factors potentially influencing the transmission risk of Y. pestis. Materials

Study location and subjects

The QTP, with an average elevation over 4000 m [23], is the world's highest and most extensive plateau. It spans the Tibet Autonomous Region and several other provinces in southwestern China (26°00′-39°47′ N, 73°19′-104°47′ E) [24].The endemic Himalayan marmot, which serves as the primary host of the high-risk pathogen Y. pestis, is widely distributed across the entire plateau. Although the QTP is recognized as China's largest natural plague focus, it also harbors vast undeveloped natural resources. These include diverse landforms such as marshes, forests, grasslands, deserts, rock formations, and glaciers. These distinctive geological features attract significant numbers of tourists for exploration. Over the past two decades, development initiatives on the plateau have significantly boosted tourism. In 2017, the Qinghai-Tibet Highway experienced a severe traffic jam stretching over 200 km, leaving tourists stranded for up to 90 h. By 2023, Tibet alone received 55.1697 million tourist visits [25]. The influx of such a large number of non-local visitors is likely to have increased the population's risk of plague infection. To address this emerging threat, our study aims to investigate the likelihood of Y. pestis infection at the county scale in order to develop targeted prevention strategies.

Mainly from China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), PubMed, Web of Science and other literature, GBIF and other open databases and field visits to collect Himalayan marmot coordinate points. Human footprint index, human density and livestock density

Human pressure factors-including urbanization [27], transportation networks [28], and land-use changes [29]-collectively alter human exposure probabilities to disease vectors and pathogen transmission risks. HFI, through its interconnected mechanistic pathways [30], ultimately serves as a robust metric for quantifying human-induced environmental pressures.

While HFI incorporates both human population density and livestock presence, human density remains a critical determinant of human infection risk. Notably, livestock husbandry constitutes the primary income source for QTP residents. Epidemiological investigations in endemic areas have identified Y. pestis in cattle and sheep [31], with traceability studies confirming transmission routes through licking rodent carcasses or direct contact with marmots.

The statistical analysis performed in R showed no significant multicollinearity (all correlation coefficients <0.7) among human density, livestock distribution, and HFI [32]. Therefore, all three variables were deemed suitable for inclusion in the same model. HFI was obtained from the Scientific Data repository (2000−2020). Population density data were sourced from the WorldPop open-access datasets, covering the period 2000–2020. Livestock distribution data were derived from the Gridded Livestock of the World (GLW 4: Gridded Livestock Density, Global-2020- 10 km resolution).

Minimum distance to roads

Road construction accelerates human mobility and elevates the likelihood of human exposure to disease vectors [33]. While linear infrastructure such as roads and powerlines is critical for socioeconomic development, it can negatively impact wildlife populations across multi-kilometer zones in adjacent environments [34]. Tourists or local residents encountering Himalayan marmots may interact with or illegally hunt these animals, significantly increasing Y. pestis transmission risks [8]. We incorporated road proximity as a predictive variable, sourced from the open-access OpenStreetMap database (2020). Using ArcGIS 10.8, the “Near” tool within the “Proximity” analysis module calculated minimum distances (km) between marmot presence points and roads.

Notably, railways on the QTP were excluded due to their limited distribution and operational isolation from wildlife habitats.

Marmot density, detection rate of Y. pestis and historical human plague occurrence place

Suitable habitats for Himalayan marmots exist across all counties of the QTP [35]. As real-time data on Himalayan marmot density and detection rate of Y. pestis is unavailable, we gathered information at the county level from literature published after 2000 (Table S1). However, there are some missing values, which we supplement by model or mean. The historical human plague occurrence site is derived from the open literature for the verification of the final results.

Climate factors and environmental variables

Climate and human-induced changes can potentially affect parasite transmission in wildlife host-parasite systems [36]. The Himalayan marmot, a main plague bacterium host on the QTP, has ticks and fleas on its body that also carry the bacteria [37]. Climate factors like temperature, precipitation and humidity influence host animals' and parasites' reproduction and development through various mechanisms [38]. Climate data for the period 2000–2020 were obtained from publicly available datasets released by meteorological research institutes [39].

As a keystone species in QTP ecosystems, Himalayan marmots are gregarious rodents that rely on cold-adapted herbaceous plants for food [40]. They hibernate from November to March, with over-winter survival directly linked to food abundance and quality, making temperature and humidity crucial for their survival [41,42]. Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), an indicator for qualitatively and quantitatively assessing vegetation cover and growth vigor, was selected as a factor influencing Himalayan marmot density. The data, covering the 20-year period from 2000 to 2020, were sourced from the EARTHDATA [43].

Statistical analysis

Random forest-based prediction of marmot density

Marmot density is an important factor in the spread of plague. Based on literature-recorded Himalayan marmot density at the county level and the corresponding survey years, we extracted the average climate data for each county in the surveyed years. Strong correlations among data can introduce analytical bias and distort variable importance [44]. We analyzed data correlations using the R Package “Corrplot”, considering a correlation coefficient > 0.7 as an indicator of high correlation between variables [32]. We finally selected four climate indicators: Diurnal temperature range (Dtr), Potential evapotranspiration (Pet), and Daily mean temperature (Tmp). For human impact, we used the HFI. Environmental variables, we used the NDVI.

We built a predictive model with the random forest algorithm, set up evaluation metrics, calculated variable importance, drew variable-response curves, and automatically selected the optimal mtry parameter. This helped explore the quantitative relationship between Himalayan marmot density and these factors (Table S2).

For the marmot density data, we used models to analyze the factors influencing density changes and then predicted the 2020 marmot density in counties across the QTP based on the three most important factors (Table S3). Given the varying spatial scales (provincial vs. prefecture vs. county-level) of the reported detection rate of Y. pestis in the literature, we implemented a data harmonization procedure. We first computed the mean detection rate for each province based on all available records. This provincial mean value was then applied to every county within that province to ensure spatial consistency for subsequent analysis and mapping.

Risk assessment framework for Y. pestis transmission

For the risk assessment framework, we employed a cross-sectional analysis with 2020 as the reference point, and all collected data were gathered through 2020. All distance metrics were converted to kilometers (km) and area measurements to square kilometers (km2). Given significant scale disparities among variables, z-score standardization was implemented in R to eliminate dimensional bias. PCA systematically determined variable weightings in transmission risk.

The risk assessment framework includes point risk value and surface risk value (county level). Point risk indices integrated: Localized HFI, Population density, Livestock density, detection rate of Y. pestis, Minimum distance to roads.

County-level risk indices integrated: County-averaged HFI, Population density, Livestock density, Marmot density (random forest model prediction), Detection rate of Y. pestis (mean value). Since the detection rate of Y. pestis is calculated at the provincial level, direct principal component analysis would significantly diminish the weight of this factor. Therefore, at the county level, we employed hierarchical principal component analysis, dividing the factors into two tiers: Biological Factors (Marmot density, Detection rate of Y. pestis) and Human Activity Factors (County-averaged HFI, Population density, Livestock density). Both tiers were assigned a weight of 0.5. Principal component analysis was performed separately for each tier to determine the weight of each factor, and the risk value was ultimately calculated.

Final risk values were calculated hierarchically (point/county) using PCA-derived weights, enabling spatially explicit plague transmission risk stratification, and the prediction results were compared using historical plague areas and county levels (Table S4).

Discussion

Analysis of influencing factors of marmot density

As a burrow-dwelling and hibernating rodent species, human disturbances and climate change can directly impact Himalayan marmot population reproduction. Human activities can disrupt marmot behavior and distribution [45], while climate change and anthropogenic factors jointly influence vegetation growth on QTP – essential resources critical for marmot survival [46].

Model analysis shows marmot population density peaks when Daily mean temperatures are −4 °C to −1 °C and Daily temperature ranges reach 14 °C. Densities drop with sustained warming, stabilizing beyond a threshold. Their optimal habitat is above 3000 meters [47], where low temperatures (often below 0 °C) and high daily temperature ranges (>10 °C) prevail [48], helping meet their metabolic requirements and lower heat stress risks [49].Their activity is regulated by light-dark cycles, with foraging and social behavior limited to daylight to avoid night-time cold [50]. These marmots are highly adapted to cold, high-temperature-fluctuation environments, but warming may shrink their suitable habitat and impact population dynamics.

Potential evapotranspiration defines regional aridity regimes. Over recent decades, most areas of the QTP have experienced increasing humidity alongside declining Pet value [51]. As the world's largest cryospheric freshwater reservoir, glacial meltwater permeates nearly all unconsolidated terrain across QTP [52].The variable response curve indicates that higher daily Pet values correspond to increased aridity in the region, which is associated with lower marmot densities. Accelerated glacial wastage driven by global warming elevates regional humidity levels, while substantial nocturnal dew deposition events–triggered by extreme diurnal temperature fluctuations–collectively establish critical hydrological foundations for vegetation development. However, the rise in humidity levels enhances the survival rate of ectoparasites, such as fleas [53], which inevitably raises the risk of Y. pestis transmission.

HFI dynamics exhibit direct epidemiological relevance, as land-use transitions often correlate with shifts in vector-borne disease burdens and public health demands [30]. Ecologically intact areas are classified at HFI < 4 [54], while HFI > 12 denotes intense anthropogenic pressure [55]. Marmot densities demonstrate significant declines in regions where HFI exceeds 8. Over the past two decades, plateau-wide HFI escalation reflects accelerated conversion of natural ecosystems to croplands, pastures, and infrastructure [56], directly degrading marmot habitats (Table 1). NDVI is used to measure the abundance and distribution of vegetation [58,59]. Marmot population density exhibits a non-monotonic relationship with NDVI. When NDVI is too low, scarce forage resources cannot support livestock development, leading to reduced anthropogenic disturbance (grazing). Consequently, marmot density becomes relatively high. As NDVI increases, suitable vegetation attracts greater grazing activity, creating resource competition between livestock operations and marmots [60]. Simultaneously, denser vegetation may intensify intraspecific competition among marmots and attract more predators, collectively suppressing population growth. When NDVI reaches approximately 0.32, moderate grazing pressure and marmot intraspecific competition achieve a dynamic equilibrium, resulting in peak population density. However, when NDVI surpasses this equilibrium point and continues to rise, higher-quality vegetation typically induces stronger grazing pressure (overgrazing), causing marmot density to gradually decline. Not until NDVI exceeds 0.5 does vegetation productivity become sufficient to simultaneously meet the requirements for both livestock grazing and marmot survival, at which point marmot density begins to gradually rise again.

Analysis of risk assessment framework

As a core host of Y. pestis, the Himalayan marmot's population density regulation is key for plague prevention. Yet, as a keystone species in cold meadow ecosystems, it plays crucial roles like energy transfer [61] and promoting plant growth [62]. Large-scale real-time monitoring will consume a lot of manpower and material resources, so multi-dimensional risk assessment can provide a solid foundation for preventing the spread of plague.

Pathogen transmission is a complex process that responds to multiple facets of environmental change [63]. The risk of human exposure to infectious vectors is jointly influenced by climatic conditions, land use patterns, ecosystem dynamics, and population mobility; while the transmission efficacy of vectors depends on three measurable parameters: the spatiotemporal distribution density of vector populations, the contact probability between hosts and vectors, and the pathogen transmission efficacy of vector carriers [30].

Humans, as the ultimate infection target of Y. pestis, account for a significant weight in both point-based and country-based risk assessments due to human population density. The QTP has an area of 2.5 million square kilometers, accounting for 26 % of China's land area, yet its resident population is only about 10 million, with a density of 4 people per square kilometer. However, a large number of tourists visit annually [64]. Many tourists, unaware of the risks, interact with marmots through feeding and touching, creating a significant exposure source. This characteristic of “low permanent resident population density combined with high mobile exposure intensity” may further increase the weight of human density in risk transmission under real-world scenarios.

Higher detection rates of Y. pestis and increased marmot density both constitute critical factors in plague transmission risk: the former significantly enhances the pathogen transmission efficacy of vector hosts, while the latter elevates the probability of human exposure. However, this study found that the population density of Himalayan marmots peaked under a mean daily temperature range of −4 °C to −1 °C, coupled with a diurnal temperature variation of 14 °C. In contrast, the primary flea species parasitizing these marmots require warmer conditions (around 20–30 °C) and high humidity for development [65], and the replication of Yersinia pestis within fleas is also highly temperature-sensitive [66].

This ecological contrast means that high marmot density does not automatically translate into high transmission risk, even though burrow microclimates are notably warmer than the external environment [67]. Conversely, elevated risk can arise in areas with low marmot density where microclimatic conditions facilitate flea development and pathogen propagation—particularly when compounded by human activity. These findings are consistent with the spatial risk patterns identified in our analysis. Furthermore, in point-based risk assessments, HFI demonstrates substantial weighting—this metric not only quantifies anthropogenic pressure but also partially reflects the probability of human-marmot contact within grid cells where marmots are present.

This study, combining point-based and county-level analyses, offers a comprehensive evaluation of Y. pestis transmission risks in Himalayan marmot habitats. At the point scale, human density weighs the most, with a median relative risk value of 0.15. At the county level, the human density still carries the most weight, while livestock carries the least, and the median relative risk value rises to 0.44. Risk assessments at different scales show significant differences. Point-based assessments can capture local high-risk clusters, while county-level assessments, though enhancing model stability, may reduce spatial heterogeneity in extreme risks. In the study area, most county-level risk averages fall between 0.4 and 0.6, close to the theoretical median of 0.5. This central tendency masks the spatial distribution of high-risk points. County-level averaging reduces extreme value interference and meets the “relatively homogeneous risk” requirement for regional public health resource allocation. Based on the comparison between counties with historical human plague occurrences and predicted county-level risk values, most outbreak locations showed predicted relative risk values higher than the median risk value, indicating the high practical significance of this research.

Further analysis shows that plague transmission risks are influenced by multiple factors. Urbanization alters human-marmot contact interfaces, tourism creates new transmission pathways, and climate change affects plague-bearing areas by altering the ecological niches of vector organisms [68]. These findings suggest that in ecologically fragile areas like the QTP, zoonotic disease prevention requires a multi-dimensional risk assessment system covering natural ecology, human activities, and socio-economy. Behavioral interventions and real-time monitoring in tourist hotspots are particularly important.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Himalayan marmot density and detection rates of Y. pestis were obtained from literature and supplemented with technical methods rather than field surveys. On the other hand, the specific number of non-residents in each county remains unknown. The risk estimation is based solely on the local population size without accounting for the impact of transient individuals.

Conclusions

This research, via a random forest model, reveals that climate, human activities and environment affect Himalayan marmot density. Temperature and humidity indirectly regulate marmot density by impacting plateau vegetation and species' ecological niches. Human activities directly alter habitat landscapes and marmot survival space. A combined point and county-level assessment shows that in transmission risks, human density is the most important factor. Detection rate of Y. pestis and HFI were closely followed in point risk assessment. Detection rate of Y. pestis and marmot density were closely followed in county risk assessment. The study constructs a multi-source variable-based risk assessment model to provide spatial decision-making support and scientific evidence for Y. pestis prevention on the QTP.
