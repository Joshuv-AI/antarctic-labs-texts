Bio-inspired antioxidant stabilization for efficient tin-lead and all-perovskite tandem solar cells
Authors: Yiting Jiang, Tingfeng Lei, Chengda Ge, Yidan An, Nan Zhang, Yunfan Wang, Lingyi Ke, Zhiqiang Guan, Chun-To Wong, Danning Lyu, Zhihui Liu, Jingdong Luo, Sai-Wing Tsang, Zaiwei Wang, Alex K.-Y. Jen, Hin-Lap Yip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of high-performance narrow-bandgap tin-lead (Sn-Pb) perovskite solar cells (PSCs) is fundamental to surpassing the Shockley-Queisser limit via all-perovskite tandem configurations. Nevertheless, the vulnerability of Sn2+ to oxidation in Sn-Pb perovskite films and devices remains a formidable obstacle to achieving superior film quality and competitive power conversion efficiency (PCE). Here, we introduce a bio-inspired antioxidant engineering strategy employing gallic acid (GA) as a dopant and tannic acid (TA) as a surface passivator to separately stabilize the perovskite bulk and interface. GA, a small antioxidant molecule, localizes at the grain boundaries to impart oxidation resistance and suppress the formation of excess SnI2 impurities. TA, with its larger molecular framework, resides at the film surface to form a robust passivation layer that hinders oxygen intrusion while establishing a dipole that facilitates interfacial charge transfer. The dual-molecule synergy significantly enhances film oxidative stability against both intrinsic (precursor degradation) and extrinsic (neutral oxygen and superoxide) stimuli. Consequently, the Sn-Pb PSCs achieve a champion PCE of 23.46%, enabling a remarkable 29.95% (certified 29.44%) efficiency in monolithic all-perovskite tandems.

Introduction

Metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have emerged as leading candidates for next-generation photovoltaics due to their stellar optoelectronic properties and the rapid increase in power conversion efficiency (PCE)1–3. Single-junction PSCs have now achieved certified efficiencies exceeding 27%, approaching the Shockley-Queisser (S-Q) limit4–6. All-perovskite tandem solar cells (TSCs), composed of multiple light absorbers with different bandgaps, have attracted extensive attention for their potential to surpass the S-Q limit and maximize light-harvesting across a broader solar spectrum with reduced thermalization losses7–10. However, mixed tin-lead (Sn-Pb) narrow-bandgap (NBG) PSCs, serving as the bottom subcells to capture long-wavelength photons, constitute the primary bottleneck in advancing tandem performance due to the greater susceptibility of Sn-containing perovskites to degradation compared with their pure Pb-based counterparts11,12.

Although the PCE of Sn-Pb PSCs has been boosted to over 24%13–16, it still lags significantly behind its theoretical limit and suffers from severe instability. This instability fundamentally originates from the labile nature of Sn2+, which is highly prone to oxidation into Sn4+ driven by a combination of intrinsic chemical environments and extrinsic environmental stressors17. Intrinsic factors stem from the inherent chemical makeup and film fabrication processes: raw precursor materials inevitably contain trace amounts of Sn4+ impurities, and standard coordinating solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) directly oxidize Sn2+ during film annealing18–21. Conversely, extrinsic factors involve external stimuli encountered during device storage and operation—primarily neutral oxygen (O2) from the ambient atmosphere and highly reactive superoxide radicals (O2⁻) generated under simultaneous light and oxygen exposure22. Because Sn-Pb perovskite films and devices are subjected to these stressors throughout fabrication and operation, developing effective strategies to suppress oxidation from both intrinsic and extrinsic sources is critical.

Extensive efforts have been devoted to enhancing the intrinsic stability of Sn-Pb perovskites. Additives such as tin fluoride (SnF2)23,24, various antioxidants25–28, and reducing agents29–32 have been shown to reduce Sn4+ content in precursor solutions and films. Moreover, employing an N,N′-dimethylpropyleneurea (DMPU)-based solvent system has been shown to profoundly inhibit Sn2+ oxidation33. Furthermore, degradation induced by simultaneous exposure to air and irradiation demands critical attention, as the generated O2⁻ accelerates the decomposition of perovskite components34. In parallel, naturally occurring polyphenols such as gallic acid (GA) have been explored as effective antioxidants in pure Sn-based perovskites35, while tannic acid (TA) has been used as a passivating interfacial layer to stabilize Pb-based PSCs36. Although these individual applications highlight the remarkable potential of plant-derived molecules, addressing the multifaceted degradation pathways of mixed Sn-Pb NBG perovskites requires a more integrated approach.

To achieve this comprehensive protection, we harness the synergistic potential of GA and TA by mimicking the evolutionary defense mechanisms of vascular plants, where hydrolyzable tannins naturally shield tissues from oxidative degradation37. In biological systems, these polyphenols utilize dense arrays of vicinal phenolic hydroxyl (Ph-OH) groups to chelate transition metal ions, preventing their oxidation by altering their redox potential38,39. By translating this biological efficacy to photovoltaics, we emulate this protective chelation behavior to thermodynamically stabilize vulnerable Sn2+ centers against oxidation.

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, we execute this via a spatially decoupled dual-protection strategy. In the precursor phase, GA acts as a primary sacrificial electron donor, facilitating the in situ reduction of Sn4+ back to Sn2+ to purify the bulk lattice at its source. Subsequently, TA is applied as a post-treatment surface layer, creating a robust interfacial shield. Crucially, both GA and TA possess ortho-dihydroxyl (catechol) motifs that enable strong bidentate coordination with Sn species. While the smaller GA molecule utilizes this property to anchor within grain boundaries and promote bulk lattice integrity, the bulkier TA leverages its high density of these motifs to form a multifunctional interfacial layer on the perovskite surface. We hypothesize that this geometric “locking” at the surface serves a dual purpose: providing a dense kinetic barrier against oxygen ingress, while simultaneously coordinating with existing surface Sn4+ to modulate deep-level trap states. Spatially deploying this synergistic strategy endows the NBG Sn-Pb perovskite films with robust resistance against both intrinsic chemical oxidation and extrinsic environmental stressors. Consequently, the resulting single-junction devices achieve a champion PCE of 23.46%, which further enables a remarkable PCE of 29.95% (certified 29.44%) for monolithic all-perovskite tandem devices. Overall, this work provides a comprehensive pathway to mitigate oxidation in Sn-Pb perovskites, highlighting their immense potential for advanced photovoltaic technologies.

Results

To experimentally validate this bio-inspired, spatially decoupled protection strategy, we first examined the structural properties and chemical interactions of GA and TA within the Sn-Pb perovskite system. Both naturally occurring polyphenols feature abundant carboxyl (-COOH) and Ph-OH groups (Supplementary Fig. 1), providing an ideal platform for targeted antioxidant intervention without compromising delicate crystallization dynamics. Building on their distinct molecular sizes, we systematically investigated their individual and synergistic roles: the smaller GA was introduced into the precursor solution as a bulk additive, while the larger TA was spin-coated onto the perovskite surface as a capping layer. For comparison, pristine films and devices served as controls, whereas those subjected to GA doping, TA post-treatment, and the combined dual-treatment are denoted as GA-D, TA-P, and GA-D & TA-P, respectively.

Suppression of oxidation induced by intrinsic factors in NBG Sn-Pb perovskites

The presence of Sn4+ in Sn-related raw materials (i.e., SnI2) is virtually inevitable, raising concerns about the device performance of Sn-based PSCs. Hence, we first carried out X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to quantify the Sn4+ content in the Sn-Pb perovskite films with and without GA or TA processing (Fig. 1b–e). The proportion of Sn4+ was calculated to be 30.9%, 19.6%, 17.3%, and 8.1% for the control, GA-D, TA-P, and GA-D & TA-P films, respectively. This progressive reduction of Sn4+ content demonstrates that GA and TA are effective in mitigating undesired Sn4+ in raw materials to produce high-quality Sn-Pb perovskite films.

To understand the chemical origins of this reduction and the protective mechanism, we employed proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) to analyze the interaction between GA/TA and SnI2 in a DMSO-d6 solution. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1a–c, the characteristic peaks at 8.86 ppm and 9.21 ppm correspond to the Ph-OH protons at different positions on the GA benzene ring. Upon the addition of SnI2, these peaks decreased in intensity and broadened. This broadening is indicative of a dynamic chemical exchange on the NMR timescale, driven by the strong Lewis acid-base coordination between the hydroxyl oxygen lone pairs and the Sn2+ center. Furthermore, the peak at 12.25 ppm, corresponding to the acidic -COOH group, vanished completely in the GA+SnI2 sample. This disappearance suggests deprotonation and the formation of a strong Sn-carboxylate complex. Together, this strong coordination effectively shields the Sn2+ ions, providing direct evidence for the suppression of Sn2+ oxidation in the raw materials. The larger TA molecule, which possesses similar Ph-OH groups, exhibits an identical coordination effect; the two Ph-OH peaks at 9.32 and 10.00 ppm both disappear in the TA+SnI2 sample due to complexation with the Sn center (Supplementary Fig. 1d–f).

Mixed solvents of N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF) and DMSO are typically used solvents for preparing perovskite precursor solutions, yet DMSO has been proposed to act as an intrinsic oxidizing agent for Sn2+20. To verify this hypothesis and test the protective limits of our additives, pristine SnI2 and perovskite solutes were separately dissolved in pure DMSO, with DMSO alone serving as the control. These solutions were then heated at 100 °C for several hours to accelerate the oxidation process. We recorded the color changes in these solutions and observed a shift from an initial pale yellow to orange-red (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating the oxidation of Sn2+ to Sn4+. We further analyzed the composition of these solutions with 1H NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 3). The newly observed peak at 1.97 ppm corresponds to the methyl protons of dimethylsulfide (DMS), confirming that an irreversible redox reaction occurred between Sn2+ and DMSO after heating33. The evolution of transmittance spectra of the SnI2 or Sn-Pb perovskite solutions further validates the oxidation behavior of DMSO. As shown in Fig. 1f–i, redshifts of the absorption edge were observed after these fresh solutions were heated at 100 °C for several hours. This is a hallmark of Sn2+ oxidation, implying the formation of [SnI6]2- complexes. Intriguingly, upon the introduction of GA or TA, the absorption edge of the oxidized solutions approaches reverted toward that of the pristine solution, with the solution color returning to its initial state (Supplementary Fig. 2). This indicates that the Sn2+ oxidation induced by DMSO can be effectively inhibited via the incorporation of GA and TA.

Suppression of oxidation induced by extrinsic factors in NBG Sn-Pb perovskites

The susceptibility of Sn-containing perovskites to ambient oxygen hinders the preparation of high-quality perovskite films40. To visualize the oxidation behavior of Sn-Pb perovskite in an ambient atmosphere, we directly placed the prepared precursor in air to observe its color change. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, after exposure to air for 30 minutes, the precursor solution turned red from the initial yellow color, indicating that Sn2+ was oxidized to Sn4+. When excess GA (Supplementary Fig. 4a) or TA (Supplementary Fig. 4b) was added to the oxidized solution, its color turned light orange. The subsequent addition of DMF to dissolve the excess GA/TA caused the solution to revert to its original translucent yellow color. These phenomena provide compelling evidence that GA and TA, via their Ph-OH groups, have a strong capability to reduce Sn4+ to Sn2+38,39.

To evaluate the impact of neutral oxygen on Sn-Pb perovskite films, we placed the fabricated films in dry air and recorded the evolution of their ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra over time (Fig. 2a–d). The absorption edge of the control film gradually blueshifted with increasing exposure time to dry air. In comparison, these shifts were markedly attenuated for the GA- or TA-treated samples. We further performed confocal photoluminescence (PL) mapping on these films exposed to dry air for 120 min to investigate the effect of oxidation on carrier recombination behavior. In environmental operational conditions, oxygen is not the only factor impairing the performance of Sn-containing PSCs; other extrinsic factors, such as light exposure, are also frequently present. Previous studies have demonstrated that the combined effects of light and oxygen cause more severe damage to Sn-containing PSCs than oxygen alone, primarily due to the generation of O2–22. Under illumination and oxygen exposure, photogenerated electrons transfer to oxygen molecules adsorbed on the perovskite surface to form O2–. Therefore, we evaluated the effects of light/oxygen on Sn-Pb perovskite films by measuring the O2– yield.

In our experiment, the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photochemical reduction method was introduced to detect the O2– yield41,42. The Sn-Pb perovskite films were immersed in anhydrous ethyl acetate containing dissolved NBT, where the solvent does not damage the perovskite films but allows the free O2– to be captured. A controlled-degradation environment was also established, with a stable oxygen flow rate (10 mL/min) and a constant light intensity. The concentration of O2– generated at the perovskite surface is reflected in the intensity of the characteristic absorption peak at 560 nm (Fig. 2i, j and Supplementary Fig. 5). The control film exhibited gradually increasing absorbance within the range of 450-700 nm as exposure time to light and oxygen increased, indicating sustained O2– generation. In contrast, the GA- or TA-treated film showed significantly reduced absorbance at 560 nm, with the GA-D & TA-P film presenting the lowest absorbance after a 15 min fast photooxidation process (Fig. 2k), suggesting a much lower O2– yield. This is attributed to the ability of the antioxidants GA and TA to act as oxygen-trapping agents, thereby alleviating the attack of O2– on the Sn-Pb perovskites. We further exposed the Sn-Pb perovskite films to continuous irradiation/air atmosphere for 30 min to record the evolution of their PL spectra (Supplementary Fig. 6). Comparison of the evolution in extracted full width at half maximum (FWHM) of PL spectra revealed that the GA-D & TA-P film exhibited significantly smaller changes in FWHM than the control group (Fig. 2l), primarily due to the suppression of O2– generation under photo/oxygen exposure via the collaborative efforts of GA and TA.

To elucidate the specific chemical interactions between these polyphenols and the Sn-Pb perovskite framework, we performed Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Given that both GA and TA possess abundant Ph-OH and -COOH groups, they are highly capable of forming strong hydrogen bonds and coordinating with metal cations. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7a–d, the FTIR spectra of pure GA powder and GA dissolved in DMF exhibited no discernible vibrational shifts. In contrast, the -OH, -C=O, and Ph-O stretching vibrations of pure TA powder all shifted toward higher wavenumbers upon dissolution in DMF or isopropanol (IPA). This shift is attributed to the inherent self-assembling behavior of TA molecules via intermolecular hydrogen bonding36, which facilitates the formation of a dense, oxygen-blocking protective layer on the perovskite surface.

Furthermore, when GA was mixed with SnI2, the -C=O peak shifted from 1611 cm−1 to 1599 cm−1, indicating a strong Lewis acid-base interaction with undercoordinated Sn2+. Similar shifts in the -OH and Ph-O vibrations were observed upon mixing GA with SnI2 or PbI2 (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f), confirming the coordination between GA and the metal cations. For TA mixed with SnI2 or PbI2 in DMF (Supplementary Fig. 7g, h), the shift in the -C=O vibration was negligible, likely due to the extensive involvement of -C=O groups in the aforementioned hydrogen bonding network. Conversely, the -OH vibrational peak underwent a profound shape change alongside a slight shift in the Ph-O vibration. This suggests that the pendant Ph-OH groups in TA actively coordinate with the metal cations, thereby stabilizing the Sn-Pb perovskite films.

Based on the above analysis, we conclude that the -C=O group in GA functions as a Lewis base to coordinate with Sn2+, reinforcing the structural integrity of the Sn-Pb perovskite. The abundant Ph-OH groups also serve as reducing agents, thereby minimizing Sn4+ content in both the precursor solution and the resulting perovskite films. The significant steric hindrance of the bulkier TA, coupled with the dense nature of the underlying Sn-Pb perovskite layer, limits their diffusion into the film interior. Instead, the formation of an extensive hydrogen bonding network via its numerous Ph-OH and -C=O groups protects the perovskite layer against oxidative degradation induced by environmental oxygen and O2–. Moreover, the pendant Ph-OH groups interact with the undercoordinated surface metal cations and coordinate with surface Sn4+ to form stable five-membered chelate rings. Consequently, the synergistic combination of GA and TA effectively suppresses both intrinsic and extrinsic oxidation pathways, promoting the formation of high-quality Sn-Pb perovskite films with substantially reduced defect densities.

Suppressed non-radiative recombination and improved carrier transportation

To investigate the impact of GA and TA on the crystallinity of the Sn-Pb perovskites, we conducted X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8a, alongside the strong diffraction peaks corresponding to the (100) and (200) planes, an impurity peak assigned to SnI2/PbI2 appeared at around 12.9° in all four films. Notably, the intensity of this impurity peak gradually decreased and eventually disappeared completely as the GA doping concentration increased from 0.1 to 0.5 (Supplementary Fig. 8b). This attenuation in the XRD patterns correlates strongly with the progressive diminishing of bright features at the grain boundaries, as observed in the top-view scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 8c-e). In contrast, the TA-P film exhibited an impurity peak intensity comparable to that of the control. This discrepancy is attributed to the relatively small molecular size of GA, coupled with lattice exclusion effects, facilitating its expulsion from the growing perovskite lattice and subsequent accumulation at grain boundaries, where it interacts effectively with localized metal cations, corroborating our FTIR results.

To determine the composition of the bright grain-boundary precipitates observed in the SEM images (Fig. 3a-d), we performed SEM-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS). Interestingly, F atoms were undetectable in these randomly selected bright precipitates (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 1). This confirms that the precipitates are not SnF2, as reported in previous literature43–45, but are most likely SnI2 or PbI246. Theoretically, the stoichiometric ratio of Sn: Pb: I in the Sn-Pb perovskite is 0.5: 0.5: 3. However, based on the atomic percentages extracted from Supplementary Table 1, the relative proportion of Sn in these bright features significantly exceeds that of Pb or I, indicating that the precipitates are Sn-rich domains, most likely corresponding to SnI2. These Sn-rich regions typically exhibit iodine vacancy enrichment, which readily promotes the generation of superoxide species22. Compared to the control and TA-P films, the density of these bright features was significantly reduced for the GA-D and GA-D & TA-P samples. This suggests that the introduction of GA facilitates the removal of excess SnI2 at grain boundaries and inhibits the formation of O2–, which aligns with our XRD results and the observed decrease in O2– yield. Finally, atomic force microscopy (AFM) was utilized to evaluate the surface morphology (Supplementary Fig. 10). The root-mean-square (RMS) roughness values were 33.0 nm, 23.6 nm, 27.9 nm, and 21.3 nm for the control, GA-D, TA-P, and GA-D & TA-P films, respectively. Surface potential is another crucial metric for evaluating the effects of GA doping and TA passivation on the Sn-Pb perovskite films. As shown in Fig. 3e-h, Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) measurements were performed to determine the average contact potential difference (CPDAVG) of the four samples. The CPDAVG values for the control, GA-D, TA-P, and GA-D & TA-P films are 132, 184, 189, and 223 mV, respectively. As CPD is inversely related to the work function50, these increasing values indicate a progressive reduction in the work function across the treated samples. Consequently, the GA-D & TA-P perovskite film possesses the lowest work function and displays a more n-type character than the other three films51. This n-type shift effectively compensates for the undesired intrinsic p-type self-doping and enhances electron transport between the perovskite film and the ETL.

To further investigate the charge-carrier dynamics of the Sn-Pb perovskite films following GA and TA processing, we conducted steady-state PL and time-resolved PL (TRPL) measurements. As shown in Fig. 3i, the GA-D & TA-P film exhibits the strongest PL intensity among the four samples, indicating effectively suppressed non-radiative recombination. Additionally, the GA-doped films (GA-D and GA-D & TA-P) both exhibited a slight blueshift in their PL peaks from 1011 nm to 1007 nm compared to the control and TA-P films. This blueshift suggests that the reduction of shallow trap states in the perovskite bulk suppresses sub-bandgap emission, thereby facilitating direct radiative recombination from the intrinsic band-edge transition48. To determine the specific electronic band structure of these Sn-Pb perovskite films, we performed ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) measurements (Supplementary Fig. 11). By combining these results with the optical bandgap of 1.25 eV calculated from the UV-vis absorption spectra and Tauc plots (Supplementary Fig. 12), we constructed the energy level diagram depicted in Fig. 3k. After doping with GA, the Sn-Pb perovskite presents a shallower Fermi level (Ef), indicating a more n-type behavior that is in full agreement with the KPFM results. Post-treatment with TA further enhances field-effect passivation by inducing a surface dipole on the Sn-Pb perovskite52, as evidenced by a downshift in the vacuum level (EVAC). Consequently, the combination of n-type doping and surface dipoles promotes electron extraction to the ETL and mitigates hole accumulation at the perovskite/ETL interface in the GA-D & TA-P Sn-Pb perovskite sample (Supplementary Fig. 13). This synergistic effect enables highly effective charge transfer and significantly inhibits carrier recombination.

Performance of NBG Sn-Pb PSCs and all-perovskite tandem devices

We subsequently fabricated the NBG Sn-Pb PSCs utilizing a device configuration of glass/indium tin oxide (ITO)/poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS)/perovskite/C60/bathocuproine (BCP)/silver (Ag). We first optimized the GA doping concentration to 0.1 mg/mL (Supplementary Fig. 14a) and the TA post-treatment concentration to 0.5 mg/mL (Supplementary Fig. 14b). To achieve optimal device performance, a binary post-treatment strategy involving both EDAI2 and TA was implemented. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 14c, the synergistic use of EDAI2 and TA outperforms individual treatments. Given the established nature of EDAI2, this work primarily focuses on the under-explored mechanisms of TA and its interaction with the Sn-Pb perovskite. Next, we evaluated the photovoltaic performance of the Sn-Pb PSCs processed with GA/TA. The statistical distribution and corresponding photovoltaic parameters are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Table 3, with the representative current-voltage (J–V) curves shown in Fig. 4a and their corresponding parameters detailed in Supplementary Table 4. The control devices exhibited an average PCE of 19.10%, with a short-circuit current density (Jsc) of 32.73 mA/cm2, an open-circuit voltage (Voc) of 0.810 V, and a fill factor (FF) of 72.13%. Upon doping with GA or post-treating with TA, the devices displayed improved Voc and FF. Consequently, the dual-treated GA-D & TA-P PSCs yielded an average PCE of 22.92% with an enhanced Voc of 0.868 V and an FF of 80.14%. Notably, the best-performing GA-D & TA-P PSC achieved a highest PCE of 23.46% (22.61%), along with a Jsc of 33.14 (33.08) mA/cm2, a Voc of 0.869 (0.872) V, and an FF of 81.48% (78.39%) under reverse (forward) scans (Fig. 4b). To validate these results, we calculated the integrated Jsc obtained from the external quantum efficiency (EQE) spectrum. These values were 31.81 and 32.24 mA/cm2 for the control and GA-D & TA-P devices, respectively, which is in good agreement with the values extracted from the J–V measurements (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 16). The slight increase in Jsc for the target device is attributed to the fact that under short-circuit conditions, the strong built-in electric field already enables a high charge collection efficiency in the control sample, approaching the theoretical limit for a 1.25 eV bandgap. In contrast, the synergistic action of GA and TA primarily manifests in the enhancement of Voc and FF. Because these parameters are determined under forward-bias conditions where the internal electric field is weakened, charge carriers are more susceptible to interfacial losses. By improving interfacial charge transport and passivating defects, the GA and TA treatment effectively suppresses non-radiative recombination in this forward-bias regime, leading to the significant improvements observed in Voc and FF. Beyond initial performance, we further monitored the shelf stability of unencapsulated devices in a glovebox filled with N2. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 17b, the efficiency of the control device decreased to only 72.1% of its initial value after storage for ~1300 h, while the GA-D & TA-P device maintained 92.5% of its initial efficiency for over 3000 h of storage. To evaluate their oxygen resistance, we recorded the PCE decay of both unencapsulated control and target devices stored in dry air (~2% RH) conditions. Benefiting from its strong antioxidant properties, the GA-D & TA-P device experienced a PCE decay of only 10.6% over 90 h, while the efficiency of the control device decreased to 76.5% during the same period (Supplementary Fig. 17c). To further assess the robust nature of the GA/TA-modified films, we conducted supplementary stability tests under accelerated aging conditions (80 °C or 65% RH). As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 17d, the GA-D & TA-P device maintained higher efficiency retention for almost 500 min of continuous heating at 80°C. SEM images (Supplementary Fig. 17e-f) reveal that GA/TA effectively suppresses the precipitation of metal salts at grain boundaries, thereby improving the thermal resistance of the perovskite structure. However, under high-humidity conditions (65% RH), both the control and GA-D & TA-P unencapsulated devices experienced a rapid decline in performance within 400 min (Supplementary Fig. 17g-i). Future efforts are still urgently needed to overcome the hydrothermal stability bottleneck for Sn-Pb PSCs.

To elucidate the origins of the improved photovoltaic performance in the GA- and TA-treated devices, several device characterizations were conducted. Supplementary Fig. 18a shows the dark J–V curves, revealing lower dark saturation current densities in the GA-D & TA-P devices compared to the control. This suggests reduced leakage current density and restrained charge-carrier recombination. We also carried out space-charge-limited current (SCLC) measurements to quantitatively estimate the internal defect density (Supplementary Note 2). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 19, both hole-only and electron-only devices demonstrated reduced defect density after doping with GA and post-treatment with TA. Additionally, the lower EQE response below the bandgap (Supplementary Fig. 18b) and a smaller Urbach energy calculated from the EQE spectra (Supplementary Fig. 18c) of the GA-D & TA-P PSCs indicate fewer sub-bandgap states and reduced trap-assisted Shockley-Read-Hall (SRH) recombination53–55, thus contributing to the enhanced Voc and FF.

To further quantify non-radiative energy losses and validate the passivation effects of GA/TA on the Sn-Pb PSCs, we employed PLQY measurements to estimate the quasi-Fermi level splitting (QFLS) of the films (Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Table 5). The control film exhibited an average PLQY of 1.05%, which is substantially lower than that of the GA-D & TA-P sample (3.94%), a finding consistent with the PL & TRPL results. To probe interfacial recombination kinetics, we further assessed the PLQY of perovskite films coated with the ETL (C60), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 20a. Compared to the neat perovskite films, the samples covered with C60 exhibited significantly reduced PLQY values, indicating severe interfacial energy loss and carrier quenching at the perovskite/ETL interface56. However, the GA-D & TA-P film with C60 maintained a PLQY approximately five times higher than that of the C60-coated control sample. This retention of luminescence verifies the capability of these multifunctional molecules to effectively impede interfacial non-radiative recombination10. Consequently, the calculated QFLS values for the GA-D & TA-P samples (both neat and C60-coated) were notably higher than those of the control, correlating well with the enhanced Voc and FF observed in the corresponding PSCs (Supplementary Fig. 20b).

We further conducted electroluminescence EQE (EQEEL) measurements (Supplementary Note 4) to quantify the Voc losses stemming from non-radiative recombination (∆Voc, non-rad) in the Sn-Pb PSCs. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 20c, the ∆Voc, non-rad was derived from the EQEEL value at an injection current density equivalent to the Jsc of the device. Accordingly, the calculated ∆Voc, non-rad for the control device is 0.165 V, whereas the GA-D & TA-P device exhibits a significantly reduced loss of 0.118 V (Supplementary Fig. 20d). This reduction confirms the superior passivation capability of these two molecules in effectively suppressing non-radiative recombination in both the perovskite bulk and at the interface.

To demonstrate the potential of the GA and TA treatment for multijunction applications, we fabricated monolithic all-perovskite tandem solar cells comprising modified NBG perovskite subcells and 1.79 eV FA0.8MA0.1Cs0.1Pb(I0.6Br0.4)3-based wide-bandgap (WBG) subcells57. Figure 4d presents a cross-sectional SEM image of the tandem solar cell, which features an architecture of glass/ITO/(4-(7H-dibenzo[c,g]carbazol-7-yl)butyl) phosphonic acid (4PADCB)/WBG perovskite/C60/atomic layer deposition (ALD)-SnOx/ITO/PEDOT:PSS/NBG perovskite/C60/BCP/Ag. The standalone 1.79 eV WBG PSC used in the tandem stack displays a PCE of 20.22% under reverse scan and an EQE-integrated Jsc of 17.91 mA/cm2 (Supplementary Fig. 21). As shown in Fig. 4e, the Voc and FF of the GA-D & TA-P-based tandem devices are higher than those of the control devices. Consequently, our best-performing GA-D & TA-P tandem solar cell achieves an impressive PCE of 29.95% (29.44%), with a Jsc of 16.48 (16.48) mA/cm2, a Voc of 2.203 (2.203) V, and an FF of 82.48% (81.10%) under reverse (forward) scan (Fig. 4f). We also obtained an independently certified PCE of 29.44% under reverse scan from a third-party laboratory (Supplementary Fig. 22).

To confirm the tandem device performance, we evaluated the spectral response and operational stability. The EQE-integrated Jsc values for the WBG and NBG subcells in the tandem devices are 16.23 mA/cm2 and 16.13 mA/cm2, respectively, matching well with the photocurrent density obtained from the J–V characteristics (Fig. 4g). The corresponding steady-state PCE of the GA-D & TA-P tandem device is 29.17% when measured at a bias of 1.94 V (Fig. 4h). Finally, we monitored their operational stability under AM 1.5 G illumination at ~40 ± 5 °C by tracking the maximum power points (MPPs) over time, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 23. The control and GA-D & TA-P-based tandem devices retained 66% and 80% of their initial PCEs, respectively, after continuous operation for almost 280 h.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a bio-inspired antioxidant strategy that integrates two polyphenolic molecules featuring Ph-OH and -C=O functional groups to realize high-performance NBG Sn-Pb PSCs and all-perovskite tandem devices. The smaller molecule, GA, when incorporated into the perovskite precursor, effectively suppresses Sn2+ oxidation and eliminates residual SnI2 at the grain boundaries. Meanwhile, the larger molecule, TA, applied via a post-treatment process, forms a protective interfacial layer that not only mitigates oxygen-induced degradation but also provides field-effect passivation to enhance electron transport and prevent non-radiative carrier recombination. Benefiting from this synergistic antioxidant approach, both intrinsic stresses (originating from raw materials and solvents) and extrinsic stresses (neutral oxygen and superoxide radicals) are significantly alleviated within the mixed Sn-Pb perovskite films. As a result, the dual-treated GA-D & TA-P devices delivered a champion PCE of 23.46% for single-junction Sn-Pb PSCs and a remarkable 29.95% (independently certified at 29.44%) efficiency for all-perovskite tandem solar cells. These findings highlight the immense potential of multifunctional, naturally derived molecules to simultaneously stabilize and optimize both the bulk and the interfaces of hybrid Sn-Pb PSCs, offering a promising pathway toward durable, high-efficiency all-perovskite tandem photovoltaics.

Methods

Materials

All chemicals were used as received without further purification, including tin (Ⅱ) iodide (SnI2, 99.999%, Yingkou Shangneng Photoelectric material Co., Ltd.), lead iodide (PbI2, 99.999%, TCI), lead bromide (PbBr2, 99.9%, TCI), formamidinium iodide (FAI, >98%, Greatcell), methylammonium iodide (MAI, 99.9%, Aladdin), cesium iodide (CsI, 99.9%, Aladdin), ethylenediammonium diiodide (EDAI2, >98%, Xi’an Polymer Light Technology Corp.), tannic acid (TA, Aladdin), gallic acid (GA, 99% Aladdin), tin (Ⅱ) fluorine (SnF2, 99%, Sigma Aldrich), Phenylethylammonium acetate (PEAAc, Xi’an Polymer Light Technology), fullerene (C60, Xi’an Polymer Light Technology Corp.), bathocuproine (BCP, 99.9%, Xi’an Polymer Light Technology Corp.), poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS, Clevious PVP AI 4083). Dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.99%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, 99.5%), chlorobenzene (CB, 99.9%), and isopropanol (IPA, 99.5%) were purchased from J&K Scientific.

Precursor preparation

The NBG FA0.6MA0.3Cs0.1Sn0.5Pb0.5I3 perovskite precursor

The 1.8 M Sn-Pb perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving SnF2 (14.1 mg), CsI (46.8 mg), MAI (85.8 mg), FAI (185.7 mg), SnI2 (335.3 mg) and PbI2 (414.9 mg) in a mixed solvent (1 mL) of DMF and DMSO at a volume ratio of 3:1. For GA modified precursor, 0.1 mg (0.06 mol%) GA was added into the perovskite precursor solution. All perovskite precursors were filtered through 0.22 μm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) filters before use. For a post-treatment solution, 0.5 mg TA and 0.5 mg EDAI2 were dissolved in 1 mL IPA, respectively. And then they were mixed at a 1:1 volume ratio after filtering through a 0.22 μm PTFE filter.

The WBG FA0.8MA0.1Cs0.1Pb(I0.6Br0.4)3 perovskite precursor

The 1.2 M perovskite precursor solution was prepared by dissolving MAI (19.1 mg), CsI (31.2 mg), FAI (165.1 mg), PbI2 (221.3 mg), and PbBr2 (264.2 mg) in a mixed solvent (1 mL) of DMF and DMSO at a volume ratio of 4:1. It should be noted that 2.5 mol% PEAAc was added into the precursor before use57.

Device fabrication

Fabrication of single-junction NBG PSCs

The patterned transparent conductive oxide substrates (ITO) were cleaned sequentially with detergent, deionized water, and IPA, respectively. The ITO substrates were then dried in an oven and treated with O2 plasma for 25 min before use. The hole transporting layer was prepared by spin-coating PEDOT:PSS onto ITO substrates at 5000 rpm for 30 s followed by annealing at 150 °C for 20 min. After cooling, the substrates were transferred into a glovebox filled with N2. For the NBG Sn-Pb mixed perovskite deposition, 70 μL perovskite precursor solution was spin-coated onto the PEDOT:PSS layer with a two-step procedure, i.e., 1000 rpm for 10 s followed by 5000 rpm for 40 s. During the process, 300 μL of CB as the antisolvent was dripped onto the substrates 20 s before the end of the second step. The as-prepared films were then transferred onto a hotplate and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. The passivation layer was spin-coated onto the cooled perovskite films at 5000 rpm for 30 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. Finally, the samples were transferred into a vacuum chamber for thermal evaporation, where C60 (25 nm), BCP (6 nm), and Ag (100 nm) were sequentially deposited.

Fabrication of monolithic all-perovskite tandem devices

The patterned ITO substrates were cleaned as described above. 4PADCB dissolved in IPA (1 mg/mL) was spin-coated onto the ITO substrates at 3000 rpm for 30 s in the N2-filled glovebox, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 10 min. After cooling, the WBG perovskite film was prepared using a two-step spin-coating process: 1000 rpm for 10 s, followed by 4000 rpm for 40 s. 300 μL of CB as the antisolvent was dripped onto the substrates 15 s before the end of the second step. The as-prepared films were then transferred onto a hotplate and annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. The passivation layer was spin-coated onto the cooled perovskite films at 5000 rpm for 30 s followed by annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. Then, the substrates were transferred into a vacuum chamber for C60 (20 nm) deposition. Next, the samples were transferred into the atomic layer deposition (ALD) chamber to deposit a 20 nm SnO2 layer under a constant substrate temperature of 80 °C, followed by sputtering 20 nm ITO through the magnetron sputtering system. After finishing this process, PEDOT:PSS diluted with IPA (1:1, v:v) was spin-coated onto the substrates at 5000 rpm for 40 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 1 0 min. Then, the substrates were transferred into the glovebox for the preparation of NBG perovskite films as mentioned above. Finally, C60 (25 nm), BCP (6 nm), and Ag (100 nm) were sequentially deposited to complete the tandem device fabrication.

Film characterizations

The XRD patterns were obtained using a D2 Phaser instrument with Cu Kα (λ = 1.5418 Å) radiation. The top-view SEM images were obtained through Apreo 2 S (Thermoscientific). AFM and KPFM were measured using a Dimension Icon AFM (Bruker) in tapping mode. XPS and UPS were conducted on a VG ESCALAB 220i XL surface analysis system with a He-discharge lamp (hν = 21.22 eV) and a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray gun (hν = 1486.60 eV), respectively. FTIR spectra were characterized by PerkinElmer. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker AVANCE Ⅲ 400-MHz spectrometers. UV-vis absorption spectroscopy was measured using a Lambda 1050 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Inc.). PL and TRPL measurements were performed with FLS1000 (Edinburgh Inc.). PL mappings were obtained using a WITec RAMAN alpha 300 R with a pulsed laser at 532 nm. PLQY measurements were performed using an integrating sphere to estimate the absolute quantum yields and a 665 nm continuous laser to excite the samples.

Device characterizations

J–V characteristics were collected using a Keithley 2400 source meter under simulated AM 1.5 G solar illumination (Enlitech, SS-F5) at 100 mW/cm2 (1 sun) in an N2-filled glovebox. The light intensity was calibrated with a silicon solar cell (KG-2 filter) from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory. The measured area is fixed to be 0.049 cm2 with a metal mask. EQE spectra were performed from 300 to 1100 nm with a QE system (QE-R, Enlitech) under dark conditions. The corresponding Urbach energies were assessed by fitting the slope within the low-energy region. EQE spectra of the all-perovskite tandem devices were measured using the same system as for single-junction devices. To isolate the response of individual subcells, appropriate bias lights and filters were employed. For the measurement of the top subcell, a constant bias light was passed through an 800 nm long-pass filter to ensure the bottom subcell was not current-limiting. For the bottom subcell, the top subcell was saturated using a bias light with a 550 nm short-pass filter. The sub-bandgap EQE (s-EQE) spectra were monitored using a home-built setup. During the measurements, light emitted from a 1000 W xenon arc lamp (Newport) is directed through a monochromator (Zolix) and an optical chopper (ThorLabs) before being focused on the device area. The resulting photocurrent was amplified using a current amplifier (Standard Research SR570) and subquently collected by a lock-in amplifier (Standard Research SR830). The intensity of the light source was determined using calibrated silicon and germanium detectors (ThorLabs). The dark J–V and SCLC measurements were conducted with a Keithley 2400 source meter under dark conditions. EL measurements were performed using Enlitech REPS with a bias range of 0.95 V to 1.55 V. The stabilized power output was monitored by setting the bias voltage to the MPP and then tracking the current density. MPP tracking was conducted under a multicolor LED solar simulator (Guangzhou Crysco Equipment Co. Ltd), which replicates the AM 1.5 G spectrum at a temperature of 40 °C in a nitrogen-filled golvebox.
