Continuous-flow photogeneration of nitrosobenzaldehydes enables indazolone formation
Authors: Nicolas Leclercq, Diana V. Silva-Brenes, Jean-Christophe M. Monbaliu, Marcus Baumann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
1,2-Dihydro-3H-indazol-3-ones (indazolones) are nitrogen-containing heterocycles with broad biological relevance, yet their synthesis often relies on harsh conditions or expensive metal catalysts. Herein, we report a metal-free continuous-flow photochemical protocol that enables rapid access to indazolone cores under mild and sustainable conditions. Direct irradiation of o-nitrobenzyl alcohols in ethanol at 365 nm generates o-nitrosobenzaldehydes in situ, which subsequently undergo condensation-cyclization with primary amines to afford indazolones. UV-visible spectroscopy combined with conceptual DFT analysis suggests that reaction efficiency is governed by wavelength-dependent substrate absorption: weak absorbers generate nitroso intermediates inefficiently, whereas strongly absorbing substrates undergo competitive photodegradation. By matching the irradiation wavelength to the absorption properties of individual substrates, the accessible chemical space can be extended beyond that obtained under fixed-wavelength conditions. Conceptual DFT further rationalizes substituent-dependent electronic effects, clarifying how photophysical and polar reactivity factors jointly control productive indazolone formation. The reaction scalability is assessed with commercial flow reactors.

Introduction

1,2-Dihydro-3H-indazol-3-one derivatives (indazolones) are valuable nitrogen-containing heterocycles that have attracted sustained attention due to their diverse properties, including anti-inflammatory1,2, anticancer3–5, and antimicrobial activities (Fig. 1a, compounds 4-I−IV)6. Beyond their role as pharmacophores in drug discovery, indazolones also serve as versatile building blocks in synthetic chemistry7. Despite this broad interest, access to libraries of functionalized indazolones remains limited as their preparation often relies on demanding or poorly generalizable methods8.Fig. 1Overview of the applications and synthetic protocols targeting indazolone.a Structures of biologically active compounds containing indazolone moieties. b Previously reported chemical synthesis of indazolone derivatives, presenting both advantages and drawbacks for each method. c Previously reported photochemical methods for the synthesis of indazolone derivatives through the transient formation of o-nitrosobenzaldehyde. d General scheme of the work reported in this paper adapting the photochemical synthesis of indazolone derivative to a scalable, continuous-flow system.

Conventional approaches to indazolone cores typically involve cyclization or rearrangement strategies that require transition-metal catalysis9 or harsh reaction conditions, which can compromise functional group tolerance and late-stage functionalization (Fig. 1b)7,10–13. Alternative photochemical protocols have been recently reported under batch conditions; however, these protocols often suffer from long irradiation times and limited efficiency, hindering their translation to larger scale (Fig. 1c)8,14,15. In this context, continuous-flow processing offers distinct advantages, particularly when combined with photochemistry and when reactive intermediates are involved, by enabling improved irradiation control and enhanced reproducibility (Fig. 1d)16–22.

Herein, we report a metal-free photochemical protocol implemented under continuous-flow conditions for the rapid synthesis of indazolone scaffolds. Under controlled irradiation, readily available o-nitrobenzyl alcohols 1 undergo in situ photoconversion to o-nitrosobenzaldehydes 223 (Fig. 1d, see also SI Section 1.3., Fig. S2), which subsequently react with primary amines 3 in a condensation-cyclization sequence to afford indazolones 4. This approach leverages flow chemistry for the controlled generation and in situ consumption of reactive intermediates24–26.

In addition to developing a synthetic protocol, we sought to rationalize its scope and limitations using spectroscopic and computational tools. UV-visible absorption spectroscopy was used to correlate substrate absorption features with productive reactivity under fixed irradiation conditions (365 nm), while conceptual density functional theory (cDFT) was used to assess how electronic substituent effects modulate the reactivity of key intermediates. Together, these complementary analyses offer mechanistic insight into the factors governing indazolone formation and establish a rational basis for selecting effective reaction conditions. The scalability of the protocol is then demonstrated with commercial flow reactors.

Results and discussion

Reaction optimization

This investigation started with the batch synthesis of N-cyclohexylindazolone (4a) using conditions reported in the literature (0.5 mmol of 1a, 2 equiv. of 3a, 365 nm irradiation at 3 ×18 W for 24 h), reported to afford 4a in 71% yield10. Following this precedent, a mixture of o-nitrobenzyl alcohol (1a, 1 equiv.) and cyclohexylamine (3a, 2 equiv.) was initially prepared in a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). However, the limited solubility of 1a resulted in precipitation, thus preventing direct translation to continuous-flow conditions. To address this limitation, a 70:30 (v/v) PBS/ethanol mixture was employed, affording complete dissolution of both reagents. The resulting solution was processed in a commercial lab-scale flow reactor (Vapourtec E-Series) equipped with a 365 nm UV lamp (See SI, Scheme S1). A brief solvent screening was then conducted to improve reaction efficiency. Increasing the ionic strength of the buffer proved detrimental (Table 1, Entry 2), while replacing PBS with non-buffered medium led to a slightly decreased yield (Table 1, Entry 3). In contrast, performing the reaction in pure ethanol resulted in near-complete conversion with high selectivity (Table 1, Entry 4). On this basis, subsequent optimization studies were conducted in ethanol. Reaction parameters were next examined using a one-factor-at-a-time approach. Increasing the concentration of 1a led to a reduced conversion and lower yield (Table 2, Entry 2-3), while decreasing the residence below 8 min resulted in incomplete conversion (Table 2, Entries 7-8). In contrast, reducing the residence time from 10 to 8 minutes had no measurable impact on the yield (Table 2, Entry 6). Next, reducing the excess amine 3a to 1.2 equiv. had no adverse effect (Table 2, Entries 4-5). The influence of lamp power was also evaluated. Reducing the power to 20 Watt decreased the yield, whereas increasing it to 80 Watt gave complete conversion (Table 2, Entries, 9-10). While higher lamp power marginally improved conversion, excessive irradiation was therefore avoided to minimize competing secondary photochemical processes, especially for halogenated substrates. Extension of the scope with substituted aryl derivatives 1a-h

Using the optimized photo-flow conditions established for model alcohol 1a, a series of substituted o-nitrobenzyl alcohols (1b-h) was evaluated with cyclohexylamine (3a) under continuous -low conditions. Under these conditions, a small library of indazolones (4b-4h) was obtained with yields ranging from low to high, depending on the nature of the aryl substitution pattern (Fig. 2). Good performances were observed for substrates 1a, 1b, 1d, 1f-h, whereas no indazolone formation was detected with derivatives 1c and 1e. For several substrates (notably, 1c, 1e, 1f) extensive degradation was observed, with little to no recovery of the starting o-nitrobenzyl alcohol. Additionally, dehalogenation was detected for halogenated compounds 4g and 4h. To determine whether this photoreduction originated from the starting o-nitrobenzyl alcohol or from the indazolone product, both 1h and isolated 4h were independently subjected to the optimized reaction conditions (Table 2, Entry 12). To assess the influence of electromodulating substituents on productive indazolone formation, the yields obtained from substrates 1a-h were plotted against the corresponding Hammett σ substituent constants (Fig. 4). The use of Hammett σ values allows to rank the electron withdrawing or donating properties of the substituents used28, thus enabling comparison of substituent effects on conversion. Because the substituents concomitantly affect multiple parameters in photochemical process (e.g. absorption wavelength, degradation pathways), these correlations are interpreted qualitatively rather than as true kinetic parameters. Overall, Fig. 4 indicates that the transformation tolerates electron-withdrawing (EWG) and electron-neutral substituents, whereas substrates bearing strong electron-donating groups (EDG) fail to afford the desired indazolones. Halogenated compound 4h is a notable outlier due to partial dehalogenation into 4a.Fig. 4Yield (%) of indazolone versus Hammett sigma constants of the substituents.NMR yields were calculated using trimethoxybenzene as an internal standard.

Aside from modulating the electron structure of the aromatic ring, substituents influence the absorption features of the substrate and, consequently, the efficiency of photochemical nitroso generation. To evaluate this effect, UV-visible absorption spectra were taken for all investigated o-nitrobenzyl alcohols (See SI Section 1.2., Fig. S1). Consistent with the established photophysical trends, EWG substituents induce hypsochromic shifts, whereas EDG substituents induce bathochromic shifts. Plotting the normalized absorbance at the experimental irradiation wavelength (365 nm) against Hammett σ constants confirms that EDG-substituted substrates, such as 1c and 1e, have absorption maxima that are closer to this wavelength than their EWG counterparts (See SI Section 1.4., Fig. S3). We hypothesize that excessive absorption promotes competing photochemical pathways, leading to substrate degradation. Our results indeed show a correlation between low indazolone yield and substrates with absorption maxima near 365 nm (See SI Section 1.4., Fig. S4). On the EWG side of the Hammett scale, diminished absorption limits productive nitroso photogeneration, thus resulting in incomplete conversion and recovery of starting material. Substrates lacking strong EDG or EWG substituents such as 1a (R = H) and the fluorinated derivative 1b display a favorable balance between sufficient absorption and photostability, making them particularly well suited for this transformation. Finally, the anomalously low yield observed for compound 4h is attributed to competing photodehalogenation (Fig. 3), which such chlorinated derivatives are prone to undergo.

Impact of the irradiation wavelength

Guided by the structure-absorption-photoreactivity relationships established above, we next examined whether adjusting the irradiation wavelength could mitigate photodegradation while maintaining productive nitroso photogeneration. Substrate 1e was selected as proof-of concept, as only extensive substrate degradation was observed at 365 nm (Fig. 2). The UV-visible absorption spectrum of 1e shows a steep decrease in absorbance at wavelengths above 365 nm (See SI Section 1.2., Fig. S1). Consistent with this observation, increasing the wavelength to 400 nm enabled the formation of indazolone 4e, albeit in modest yields (Table 3, Entry 3). Further red-shifting of the irradiation wavelength resulted in progressively lower conversion and yield (Table 3, Entries 4-5). Across this series, an overall reduction in substrate degradation was observed while the wavelength increased, supporting the notion that wavelength tuning can partially decouple productive excitation from unproductive, competing photochemical pathways.Table 3Scope of wavelengthsa1H-NMR yields were calculated using trimethoxybenzene as an internal standard. Conceptual density functional theory (CDFT) analysis

To further elucidate how electronic substituent effects influence the reactivity of the nitroso intermediates, a conceptual Density Functional Theory (cDFT) analysis was performed on o-nitrosobenzaldehyde intermediates (2a-h) derived from substrates 1a-h. Global and local electrophilicity indices as well as electrostatic potential (ESP) maps were computed29. Details are listed in the Supporting Information (See SI, Section 2.1.).

Electrostatic Surface Potential (ESP) maps of the optimized intermediates reveal two distinct electrophilic regions corresponding to the nitroso and aldehyde moieties, respectively (Fig. 5). Local electronic descriptors (See SI, Section 2.2.) show that the nitroso moiety (ωN) is consistently more electrophilic than the aldehyde group (ωC), supporting nucleophilic attack at the nitroso nitrogen as the dominant pathway. A strong correlation between the ωN and Brown’s substituent constants (σp+, See SI, Figure S8) highlights even more the strong electrophilicity of the nitroso moiety, which can be described as having a pseudo-cationic character. A second observation concerns the magnitude of the effect exerted by aryl substitution on both reactive centers. The local electrophilicity at the nitroso center varies significantly with aryl substitution (0.94 < ωN < 1.18), whereas the aldehyde carbon displays much smaller variations (0.07 < ωC < 0.12). The fact that NO is the most reactive center and also the most sensitive to modulation is reflected in the large yield variations observed with different substituents.Fig. 5Electrostatic potential (ESP) surface plots of the intermediate o–nitrosobenzaldehydes 2.Regions of negative electrostatic potential (electron-rich) are indicated in red, while regions of positive electrostatic potential (electron-deficient) are highlighted in blue. The combined use of UV-visible spectroscopy and cDFT analysis provides a global description of the factors governing indazolone formation under photochemical conditions. Efficient reaction requires sufficient substrate absorption at the irradiation wavelength to enable nitroso generation, while avoiding excessive absorption that promotes degradation. For strongly electron-rich substrates, photodegradation resulting from excessive absorption precedes and dominates over polar reactivity considerations, whereas for less strongly donating systems, electrophilicity becomes the limiting factor. Once the nitroso intermediate is formed, its electrophilicity, which is modulated by aryl substitution, controls its ability to undergo nucleophilic attack by amine 3, thereby determining productive indazolone formation.

Long run and scalability assessment

In order to assess the temporal stability of the reaction, a long run experiment was carried out under the optimized conditions (Table 2, Entry 12). The reaction was monitored every hour via 1H qNMR analysis. Over more than 6 h of continuous reaction, 3.60 g of 4a was isolated (86.2% isolated yield), while the conversion and yield, determined by qNMR, remained stable at 95% and 91%, respectively (Fig. 7a). For such long runs, a decrease in yield was observed when using denatured ethanol. In addition, the scalability of the process was evaluated by transposing it to a Corning® Advanced-FlowTM Lab Photo Reactor. By decreasing the residence time to 1 min 22 s, a space-time yield (STY) of up to 409.6 g.L−1.h−1 was achieved, while maintaining both conversion and yield above 90% (Fig. 7b). For comparison, the Vapourtec setup under the optimized conditions (Table 2, Entry 12) afforded a STY of 77.8 g.L−1.h−1, whereas the same photochemical reaction reported by Kurth et al. displayed an STY of 0.32 g.L−1.h−1 14. More importantly, the scalability of reactions performed in Corning® Advanced-FlowTM Reactor technology is well established up to industrial scale, ensuring transposition of these conditions to production scales30,31.

Given that the two flow photochemical setups led to markedly different outcomes, and since lamp power had previously been identified as a key parameter influencing the reaction behavior, actinometric measurements were performed in order to quantify the effective photon flux delivered by each reactor. Solutions of (E)-azobenzene (80 mM) were passed through the reactors and irradiated at 365 nm under varying lamp intensities. The (E) → (Z) photoisomerization was monitored through 1H NMR spectroscopy. Due to fast reaction kinetics, the lamps could not be operated at full power. However, plotting the measured conversion versus the light power revealed a linear relationship, indicating that the photon flux increased proportionally with the lamp power for both photoreactors (See SI, Section 2.1.). By extrapolating our data and by using the 0.12 quantum yield of the isomerization at 365 nm already reported in a similar research case32, the photon fluxes could be calculated. Based on our experiments, the Vapourtec R-Series reactor at 40 W and the Corning® Advanced-FlowTM Lab Photo Reactor at 100% power are estimated to have photon fluxes of 2.57 × 10-5 and 3.74 × 10-5 mol photons·s−1, respectively. The higher space-time yield reached in the Corning® Lab Photo Reactor can be partly rationalized by its higher photon flux, which irradiates a reduced reactor volume compared to the Vapourtec equipment, combined with superior mass-transfer (mixing) of Corning reactors.

Conclusion

This project successfully demonstrated the synthesis of indazolone derivatives via a metal-free photochemical strategy operating in ethanol under short residence times and conducted under continuous-flow conditions. By leveraging the in situ generation of reactive o-nitrosobenzaldehydes from o-nitrobenzyl alcohols under 365 nm LED irradiation, followed by cyclization with primary amines, various nitrogen-containing heterocycles were obtained in modest to excellent yields. Beyond reducing reaction times, the continuous-flow format proved essential for controlling photon flux, maintaining steady-state conditions over extended operation, and enabling transposition of reaction conditions between photoreactor platforms. These aspects are difficult to decouple or systematically optimize under batch conditions, particularly for reactions involving short-lived nitroso intermediates.

Electron-poor and electron-neutral substrates proved well suited to the optimized conditions, while electron-rich o-nitrobenzyl alcohols failed to generate the desired indazolone products. Electron-donating substituents led to extensive decomposition of the starting material, which may arise from the high absorptivity of these compounds at 365 nm. UV-Vis analysis indicated that irradiation at longer wavelengths (400–425 nm) enables activation of an electron-rich substrate (1e) while decreasing decomposition by selecting an irradiation wavelength where the substrate sufficiently absorbs light to trigger the reaction without promoting unwanted secondary reactions. In general, the results were rationalized by comparing the absorption maxima (λmax) of the substrates; however, a more rigorous understanding would require quantitative comparison of their molar absorptivities (ε). Although not explored in this work, energy-transfer photocatalysis remains an attractive alternative to decouple excitation from substrate absorption, which may in turn result in better conversion while minimizing substrate degradation. Complementary DFT analysis further explains this poor yield as a consequence of the low electrophilicity of the intermediate, hindering the subsequent nucleophilic attack by the amine.

Overall, this work highlights how modern synthetic chemistry, through the synergy of photochemistry, flow processing, and theoretical insight, can offer both efficiency and sustainability in the development of green and readily scalable routes.

Methods

General methods

Optimization of continuous-flow reactions were carried out on a Vapourtec E-Series system equipped with peristaltic pumps and a static back-pressure regulator (75 psi, 5.2 bar). The long run and actinometry tests were performed in a Vapourtec R-Series system equipped with high-pressure piston pumps and a static back pressure regulator (75 psi, 5.2 bar). For photochemical experiments, the UV-150 module from Vapourtec was mainly used in combination with a high-power double-sided LED (365 nm, purchased from Vapourtec) regulated between 20 W and 80 W. The system was cooled to 23–28 °C by passing a stream of compressed air through the reactor unit. No optical filters were used. A 20 mm distance separated the lamp from the reactor. The coil was placed inside the reactor that included a reflecting medium, guaranteeing efficient irradiation. Reactor coils were made of perfluoroalkoxy polymer (PFA) tubing with a 1/16-inch external diameter (1 mm internal diameter) and a volume of 10 mL.

Additional UV light sources used for the synthesis of 4e are either single-sided UV lamps with a wavelength of 385 nm, 400 nm, or 425 nm, or a double-sided UV lamp of 450 nm. All lamps were purchased from Vapourtec. All single-sided lamp powers were set at 40 W. The double-sided 450 nm lamp had a fixed power of 54 W.

The scalability assessment for the continuous-flow synthesis of 4a was carried out in a Corning® Advanced-FlowTM (AFR) Lab Photo Reactor system equipped with 2 LED panels. An 18 mm distance separated both lamps from the reaction plate. The reactor used was a Corning glass plate with 2.7 mL of internal volume. The LED panels were cooled around 25 °C using a thermostat using a 1:1 mixture of ethanol and water as heat transfer fluids. A second thermostat was used to control the reaction temperature by circulating a 7 wt% ethylene glycol in water solution through the service channels of the Corning Lab Reactor module. The reaction feed solution was pumped using an Asia Syringe Pump equipped with two syringes of 2.5 mL and 5.0 mL, respectively.

Unless otherwise stated, all solvents were purchased from Fisher Scientific or Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. Similarly, unless specified otherwise, all reagents were obtained from Fluorochem, Sigma-Aldrich, or TCI. Reagent quality was assessed by NMR analysis prior to use, and all materials were employed as received.

General procedure used for optimization of the synthesis of indazolone derivatives 4

To a 50 mL volumetric flask, 20 mL of non-denatured ethanol, 2-nitrobenzyl alcohol 1 (2.5 mmol, 1 equiv.) and amine derivatives 3 (3 mmol, 1.2–1.5 equiv.) were added. The volume was completed using ethanol. The reaction solution was passed through a Vapourtec E-Series reactor (10 mL of internal volume) at a flow rate of 1.25 mL.min−1 (8 min of residence time). The solution was irradiated within the flow reactor by a 40 W 365 nm UV lamp. After waiting 20 min (2.5 residence time) for the reaction to reach steady-state, the solution was collected for 16 min (20 mL, 1 mmol of product expected). The collected solution was washed with 1 M HCl and extracted using dichloromethane. The organic layers were dried using anhydrous sodium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated under vacuum. Then the crude reaction mixture was purified by chromatography on silica and eluted with a gradient of dichloromethane/methanol or cyclohexane/ethyl acetate (based on TLC tests) to obtain the desired product.

Long run synthesis of 4a

To a 500 mL volumetric flask, 200 mL of non-denatured ethanol, 2-nitrobenzyl alcohol 1a (3.84 g, 25.11 mmol, 1 equiv.) and cyclohexylamine 3a (3.11 g, 31.47 mmol, 1.2 equiv.) was added. The volume was completed using ethanol. The reaction solution was passed through a Vapourtec R-Series reactor (10 mL of internal volume) at a flow rate of 1.25 mL.min−1 (8 min of residence time). The solution was illuminated within the flow reactor by a 40 W 365 nm UV lamp. Samples were taken every hour and analyzed through 1H qNMR. The collected solution (385.2 mL, rest was used for analysis) was washed with 1 M HCl and extracted using dichloromethane. The organic layers were dried using sodium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated under vacuum. Then, the crude reaction mixture was purified by chromatography on silica and eluted with a gradient of dichloromethane/ethanol (dichloromethane → dichloromethane/ethanol 4:1). After drying, 4.74 g (16.67 mmol, 86.2% yield) of the title compound was obtained as a dark oil. For details, see SI section 1.5.

Proof-of-scalability in the Corning® lab photo reactor

To a 100 mL volumetric flask, 50 mL of non-denatured ethanol, 2-nitrobenzyl alcohol 1a (741 mg, 4.84 mmol, 1 equiv.) and cyclohexylamine 3a (596 mg, 6.01 mmol, 1.2 equiv.) was added. The volume was completed using ethanol. The reaction solution was passed through the Corning AFR reactor (2.7 mL of internal volume) at a flow rate of 0.338 to 2.7 mL.min−1 (0.5 to 4 min of residence time). The solution was illuminated within the flow reactor by two 9.2 W 365 nm UV lamps (100% power). The collected solution was washed with 1 M HCl and extracted using dichloromethane. The organic layers were dried using sodium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated under vacuum. For details, see SI section 1.6.
