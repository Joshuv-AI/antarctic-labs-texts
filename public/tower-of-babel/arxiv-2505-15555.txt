Robust Activity Detection for Massive Random Access
Xinjue Wang, Esa Ollila, and Sergiy A. Vorobyov

Abstract
Massive machine-type communications (mMTC) are fundamental to the Internet of Things (IoT) framework in future wireless networks, involving the connection of a vast number of devices with sporadic transmission patterns. Traditional device activity detection (AD) methods are typically developed for Gaussian noise, but their performance may deteriorate when these conditions are not met, particularly in the presence of heavy-tailed impulsive noise. In this paper, we propose robust statistical techniques for AD that do not rely on the Gaussian assumption and replace the Gaussian loss function with robust loss functions that can effectively mitigate the impact of heavy-tailed noise and outliers. First, we prove that the coordinate-wise (conditional) objective function is geodesically convex and derive a fixed-point (FP) algorithm for minimizing it, along with convergence guarantees. Building on the FP algorithm, we propose two robust algorithms for solving the full (unconditional) objective function: a coordinate-wise optimization algorithm (RCWO) and a greedy covariance learning-based matching pursuit algorithm (RCL-MP). Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed methods significantly outperform existing algorithms in scenarios with non-Gaussian noise, achieving higher detection accuracy and robustness.

Massive machine-type communications (mMTC) represent a cornerstone of future wireless communication networks, particularly within the Internet of Things (IoT) framework

This paradigm involves connecting a vast number of devices such as sensors, machines, and robots, leading to immense data traffic.

Conventional wireless systems rely on grant-based random access protocols where devices request transmission opportunities using a limited pool of orthogonal pilot sequences.
When transmitting data, an active device randomly selects one of these sequences and sends it as a resource allocation request.
However, as the number of simultaneously active devices exceeds the number of available orthogonal sequences, inevitable collisions occur.

I-A

Main Contributions

Under Gaussian channel and noise, the device activity detection problem can be formulated as an MLE problem, in which the sample covariance matrix (SCM) of received signals is a sufficient statistic
[
42
]
.
The resulting (Gaussian) negative log-likelihood function (LLF) is non-robust as it fails to down-weight data points with large generalized (Mahalanobis) distances.
To address this, we propose using robust loss function that enhance robustness against non-Gaussian noise.
However, this approach results in an objective function that lacks a closed-form solution, even in the conditional case, where other parameters are fixed, and the objective is solved for a single remaining parameter only.
We show that the coordinate-wise (conditional) objective function is geodesically convex and derive an efficient fixed-point (FP) algorithm for minimizing it, along with convergence guarantees.
Building on the FP algorithm, we develop two robust AD methods: robust coordinatewise optimization (RCWO) algorithm and robust covariance learning-based matching pursuit algorithm (RCL-MP).

The proposed methods are then evaluated in extensive numerical experiments under various synthetic and realistic heavy-tailed noise conditions.
The results show that the proposed methods consistently outperform Gaussian-based counterparts in detection performance.
Furthermore, the proposed RCL-MP algorithm achieves computational efficiency comparable to Gaussian-based CWO, making it practical for large-scale deployments. The RCWO algorithm on the other hand achieves better performance than the greedy RCL-MP algorithm when the number of active devices are large.

Our proposed work is closely related to but distinct from previous studies. In
[
43
]
, the authors employ low-dimensional approximation techniques, such as principal component analysis (PCA) of the channel, to enhance activity detection under highly varying channel conditions. In contrast, we focus on environments with non-Gaussian noise and fixed channel.
Effort addressing non-Gaussian noise scenarios has been lacking in mMTC literature and the present work aims to fill this gap. We note that the authors in
[
44
]
proposed a greedy CL-MP algorithm assuming Gaussian data. Our RCL-MP algorithm is a non-trivial extension of this work, making the method robust to outliers and severe noise conditions.

II
System Model

We consider an uplink single-cell massive random access scenario with
N
single-antenna machine type devices (MTDs) communicating with a base station (BS) equipped with
M
antennas.
For the purpose of device identification, each device
n
is preassigned a unique signature sequence
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{n}=(a_{n1},\ldots,a_{nL})
of length
L
, which is known at the BS.
We also assume that the user traffic is sporadic i.e., only
K\ll N
devices are active during each coherence interval.
The objective is to detect which subset of devices is active based on the received signal at the BS.
In massive MTC scenarios, also the number of devices is greater than the pilot length (i.e.,
N>L
or
N\gg L
).

The generative signal model can be written as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{Y}}={\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{X}}+{\boldsymbol{E}},

(1)

where
{\boldsymbol{Y}}=({\boldsymbol{y}}_{1}\ \cdots\ {\boldsymbol{y}}_{M})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{L\times M}
denotes the received signal matrix over
L
signal dimensions (symbols) and
M
antennas, the columns of
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
are independent due to independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) channel coefficients over different antennas,
{\boldsymbol{A}}=({\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}\ \cdots\ {\boldsymbol{a}}_{N})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{L\times N}
is the sensing matrix collecting pilot sequences of devices (known over-complete pilot matrix),

{\boldsymbol{X}}=({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\ \cdots\ {\boldsymbol{x}}_{N})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N\times M}
is the unobserved channel matrix with

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}=\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i},\ \text{for}\ i=1,\ldots,N

(2)

modeling the channel vector between the
i
-th device and the BS, where
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}\sim{\mathbb{C}}{\mathcal{N}}_{M}({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{I}})
is the Rayleigh fading component, and
\gamma_{i}
is the scaling component defined as

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}=\alpha_{i}\varrho_{i}\beta_{i},

(3)

where
\alpha_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is an indicator function of device activity (
=1
when device is active and
=0
otherwise),
\varrho_{i}
is the device’s uplink transmission power, and
\beta_{i}>0
is the large-scale fading component (LSFC) accounting for path-loss and shadowing.
Since only
K\ll N
devices are active, the signal matrix
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is
K
-rowsparse, i.e., at most
K
rows of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
contain non-zero entries.
The rowsupport of
{\boldsymbol{X}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N\times M}
is the index set of rows containing non-zero elements

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}=\textrm{supp}({\boldsymbol{X}})=\{i\in[\![N]\!]:x_{ij}\not=0\textrm{ for some }j\in[\![M]\!]\}.

(4)

Thus,
{\mathcal{M}}
collects the indices of the active devices,
{\mathcal{M}}=\{i\in\llbracket N\rrbracket:\alpha_{i}=1\}
.
The objective of activity detection is thus to identify the support set
{\mathcal{M}}
, given the received signal
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
, the pilot matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, and the noise level
\sigma^{2}
.

Further assuming that the noise elements are independent and identically circular Gaussian distributed,
e_{lm}\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with known variance
\sigma^{2}>0
, one has that

{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m}\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}_{L}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
are i.i.d with
L\times L
positive definite Hermitian (PDH) covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\text{cov}({\boldsymbol{y}}_{m})
given by
[
1
]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}={\boldsymbol{A}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}+\sigma^{2}{\boldsymbol{I}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\gamma_{i}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}+\sigma^{2}{\boldsymbol{I}},

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{N})^{\top}
.

Since
{\mathcal{M}}=\textrm{supp}({\boldsymbol{X}})=\textrm{supp}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
,
covariance learning
(CL)-based support recovery algorithms can be constructed by minimizing the Gaussian negative log-likelihood function (LLF) of the data
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
defined by (after scaling by

and ignoring additive constants)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m}+\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|.

(6)

CL-based methods treat
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
as a set of deterministic but unknown parameters and model the received signal
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
based on the Gaussianity of both the source signal
{\boldsymbol{X}}
and the noise
{\boldsymbol{E}}
.
This distribution assumption formulates the problem as a maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) problem, making it tractable for analysis and implementation.
Despite the inherent non-convexity of the optimization problem in (
6
), which arises from the convexity of
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m}
and the concavity of
\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|
, several efficient CL methods have been developed to solve it, such as SBL
[
15
]
, M-SBL
[
16
]
, variants of CWOs
[
45
,
6
,
46
]
, and greedy methods
[
17
,
44
]
.
Especially, component-wise algorithms
[
45
,
6
,
46
,
17
]
iteratively update the variable associated with each device, providing closed-form solutions for subproblems.
This reduces the computational complexity and enhances the algorithm efficiency
[
42
]
.
Additionally, CL algorithms have better performance with a large number of antennas
M
and a smaller pilot length
L
compared to AMP methods in low-latency mMTC scenarios
[
46
]
.

In the aforementioned methods, Gaussianity is widely assumed.
However, real-world scenarios frequently deviate from Gaussian assumptions due to the presence of outliers (resulting in heavy-tailed distributions of received signals), imperfect or outdated channel state information or changes in device mobility and environmental conditions. In such cases, the traditional Gaussian assumption becomes invalid, leading to potential issues in estimation accuracy.
Consequently, robust methods are required to effectively address situations where the Gaussianity is not valid.

III
Robust objective function for activity detection

Our goal is robust detection of active devices.
Non-robustness of the solution to Gaussian negative LLF (
6
) stems from the property that
large (squared) Mahalanobis distances
\{{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
are not down-weighted
and can thus have an unduly large influence in the obtained solution.
A commonly used approach in robust statistics
[
47
,
27
]
to reduce the impact of such outlying observations is to downweight them via robust loss function, which is defined as follows. First, recall that a function
\rho(t)
is said to be geodesically convex in
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
if
r(x)=\rho(e^{x})
is convex in
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 1

(loss function)
.

A geodesically convex function
\rho(t)
that is continuous in
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
, non-decreasing and differentiable, is called a loss function,
and its first derivative

\displaystyle u(t)=\rho^{\prime}(t)\geq 0

(7)

is called a weight function.

Then, a more general version of (
6
) is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{bM}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\rho\left({\boldsymbol{y}}_{m}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{y}}_{m}\right)+\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|,

(8)

where
\rho
is a loss function in sense of Definition
1
.
This robust loss function generalizes the Gaussian MLE in (
6
) by applying
\rho
to the Mahalanobis distance, reducing the impact of outliers and handling non-Gaussian noise effectively.

The term
b
is a consistency factor defined as

\displaystyle b=\mathsf{E}[\psi({\boldsymbol{y}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{y}})]/L,\ {\boldsymbol{y}}\sim\mathbb{C}\mathcal{N}_{L}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),

(9)

where
\psi(t)=t\rho^{\prime}(t)
.
This consistency factor is used in M-estimation for obtaining consistency of the obtained estimator to covariance matrix when data is Gaussian-distributed
[
47
,
27
]
.

There are several loss functions
\rho
that can be used in (
8
) such as

Gaussian loss

\rho(t)=t
. Its weight function is
u(t)=1
, and consistency factor is
b=1
. Hence the negative LLF (
8
) reduces to (
6
). Gaussian loss is however non-robust. A robust loss function has the property that the weight function descends to zero as
t
increases. Below we discuss two popular examples of robust loss functions that can be used instead of the Gaussian loss.

1). Huber’s loss

[
48
]
is based on a weight function

\displaystyle u(t;c)=\begin{cases}1,&\ \mbox{for}\ t\leq c^{2},\\
c^{2}/t,&\ \mbox{for}\ t>c^{2},\end{cases}

(10)

where
c>0
is a tuning constant that controls robustness (how heavily one down weights large distances). The loss function
\rho
corresponding to Huber’s M-estimator is
[
49
]
:

\rho(t;c)=\begin{cases}t&\ \mbox{for}\ t\leqslant c^{2},\\
c^{2}\big(\log(t/c^{2})+1\big)&\ \mbox{for}\ t>c^{2}.\end{cases}

(11)

As
t={\boldsymbol{y}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{y}}\sim(1/2)\chi^{2}_{2L}
when
{\boldsymbol{y}}\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}_{L}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, it is common to choose
c^{2}

as the
q
th quantile of
(1/2)\chi^{2}_{2L}
-distribution, i.e., verifying
2c^{2}=F^{-1}(q;\chi^{2}_{2L})
for some
q\in(0,1)
, where
F(\cdot;\chi^{2}_{2L})
designates the cumulative density function (cdf) of
\chi^{2}_{2L}
-distribution.
We regard
q\in(0,1)
as a loss parameter which can be chosen by design, and use
q=0.9
as our default choice.
For
q\to 1
, Huber’s loss equals Gaussian loss.
The scaling constant
b>0
can be expressed in closed form as

b=F_{\chi^{2}_{2(L+1)}}(2c^{2})+c^{2}(1-F_{\chi^{2}_{2L}}(2c^{2}))/L.

(12)

2). t-loss

[
27
, Sec. 4.4.1]
is defined as

\rho(t;\nu)=\frac{\nu+2L}{2}\log(\nu+2t).

(13)

It originates as being the ML-loss function for the circular complex
L
-variate
t
-distribution (MVT) with
\nu
degrees of freedom.
The
\nu
parameter in (
13
) is viewed as a loss parameter which can be chosen by design.
The case
\nu=1
corresponds to the Cauchy loss, while the case
\nu\to\infty
yields the Gaussian loss.
A large
\nu
indicates a strong degree of belief in the Gaussianity assumption.
The respective weight function is

\displaystyle u(t;\nu)=\frac{\nu+2L}{\nu+2t}.

(14)

In this case, the consistency factor
b
in (
9
) is not as easy to derive in closed form, but can always be computed using numerical integration.

In developing a practical algorithm for computing the signal powers, we first consider the simpler case, the conditional objective function where all device powers
\{\gamma_{j}\}
for

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\gamma\mid\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\backslash i})=\frac{1}{bM}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\rho(s_{m,i}(\gamma))+\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\backslash i}+\gamma{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}|,

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\backslash i}=\sum_{j\not=i}\gamma_{j}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}+\sigma^{2}{\boldsymbol{I}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}-\gamma_{i}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}

Denoting

We develop a Robust CL-based Matching Pursuit (RCL-MP) algorithm, following the generic matching pursuit strategy outlined in

Unlike the RCWO updating cyclically, RCL-MP selects the most influential signal index

\epsilon_{i}
at each iteration and adds it to the support set.
This method updates the parameters by solving the optimization problem

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}=\min_{\gamma\geq 0}{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\gamma)

(34)

for atoms
i
in the complement of the current support set
{\mathcal{M}}^{(k)}
, and adds the atom
i
to this that minimum value of error
\epsilon_{i}
(i.e.,
i=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{j}\epsilon_{j}

Initialization.
We begin with the following settings.

•

The iteration index is set to
k=0
and the initial power vector is set to
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}_{N\times 1}

•

The support set
\mathcal{M}^{(k)}

.

•

The initial covariance matrix is set to

1) Compute

For each device

•

Numerical Experiment

This section validates the performance of our proposed RCWO and RCL-MP algorithms, comparing them with common algorithms for the AD problem under a variety of settings.
We use the following performance metrics for this evaluation: the (empirical) probability of exact recovery (
P_{\textrm{ER}}
), the probability of missed detection (
P_{\textrm{MD}}
), and the probability of false alarm (
P_{\textrm{FA}}
).
Exact recovery occurs when all active devices are correctly detected. We define the (empirical) probability of exact recovery (
P_{\textrm{ER}}
) as

\displaystyle P_{\textrm{ER}}=\frac{1}{N_{\textup{sim}}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\textup{sim}}}\mathds{1}({\mathcal{M}}_{n}={\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}),

(38)

where
N_{\textup{sim}}
is the number of Monte Carlo trials,
\mathds{1}(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function,
{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}
is the true set of active devices, and
{\mathcal{M}}_{n}
is the estimated support for the
n
-th Monte Carlo trial.
Missed detection occurs when an active device is incorrectly identified as inactive. The (empirical) probability of missed detection (
P_{\textrm{MD}}
) is defined the ratio of missed devices relative to the total number of active devices
averaged over MC trials:

\displaystyle P_{\textrm{MD}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\textup{sim}}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\textup{sim}}}\dfrac{|{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}\setminus{\mathcal{M}}_{n}|}{|{\mathcal{M}}^{\circ}|}.

(39)

Since we select exactly
K
devices as active in our simulations (where
K=|\mathcal{M}^{\circ}|
is the true number of active devices), the number of false alarms equals the number of misdetections,
thus,
P_{\textrm{FA}}=P_{\textrm{MD}}
(see also
[
13
]
).

VI
Conclusion

This paper presented robust algorithms for device AD in massive mMTC under non-Gaussian noise conditions.
By replacing the Gaussian loss function with robust alternatives, we addressed the limitations of existing methods in handling heavy-tailed noise and outliers.
The proposed methods, supported by a FP algorithm with proven convergence, demonstrated superior performance in extensive numerical experiments, particularly in challenging impulsive noise environments.
The numerical experiments confirmed the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed approaches in improving detection accuracy under diverse noise conditions, while maintaining practical computational efficiency.
These findings make the proposed methods promising solutions for real-world mMTC systems where accurate activity detection is critical.
Future work may explore extending these methods to more complex network settings, such as cell-free architectures, and addressing real-time implementation challenges in large-scale systems.

Proof of Theorem
1

Recall the conditional objective function

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\gamma)=\frac{1}{bM}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\rho(s_{m,i}(\gamma))+\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\backslash i}+\gamma{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}|.

Our objective is to show that
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\gamma)
is geodesically convex in
\gamma\geq 0
, meaning that
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(e^{x})
is convex in
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
It suffices to show that
\rho(s_{m,i}(\gamma))
and the 2nd term of
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\gamma)
and are geodesically convex.
Since the sum of geodesically convex functions is still geodesically convex, the entire objective function
{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\gamma)
is geodesically convex in
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
.

Let

\displaystyle h(\gamma)=\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\backslash i}+\gamma{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}|.

(42)

Using the matrix determinant lemma
3
3

3

The matrix determinant lemma for a rank-one update states that for an invertible matrix
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and vectors
{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}
, the determinant of
{\boldsymbol{A}}+{\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}

|{\boldsymbol{A}}+{\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}|=(1+{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}{\boldsymbol{A}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{u}})|{\boldsymbol{A}}|
.
and the shorthand notation

, we have

\displaystyle h(e^{x})

\displaystyle=\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\backslash i}+e^{x}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}|

\displaystyle=\log\left((1+e^{x}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}^{{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{H}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\backslash i}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i})|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\backslash i}|\right)

\displaystyle=\log(1+d_{i}e^{x})+\log|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\backslash i}|.

(43)

The second derivative of

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}(e^{x})=\frac{d_{i}e^{x}}{(1+d_{i}e^{x})^{2}}\geq 0,

(44)

which shows that

is convex in

is geodesically convex in
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

From the known expression for

\displaystyle s_{m,i}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=c_{m}-\frac{\gamma b_{m}^{2}}{1+\gamma d_{i}},

This implies geodesic convexity of

Thus,

\displaystyle Q_{i}(\gamma\mid\gamma^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle\geq{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\gamma),

(49a)

\displaystyle Q_{i}(\gamma^{(\ell)}\mid\gamma^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\gamma^{(\ell)}).

with respect to

, we have

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}_{i}(\gamma\mid\gamma^{(\ell)})=\frac{1}{bM}\sum_{m=1}^{M}u_{m,i}(\gamma^{(\ell)})\frac{\partial s_{m,i}(\gamma)}{\partial\gamma}+\frac{d_{i}}{1+\gamma d_{i}}.

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}

\displaystyle Q_{i}(\gamma^{(\ell+1)}\mid\gamma^{(\ell)})\leq Q_{i}(\gamma^{(\ell)}\mid\gamma^{(\ell)}).

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\gamma^{(\ell+1)})\leq Q_{i}(\gamma^{(\ell+1)}\mid\gamma^{(\ell)})\leq Q_{i}(\gamma^{(\ell)}\mid\gamma^{(\ell)})={\mathcal{L}}_{i}(\gamma^{(\ell)}).
