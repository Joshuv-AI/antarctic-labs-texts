A modified LIFT approach of offsetting sphincter muscle plication aimed at decreasing recurrence rates: a single-center retrospective review
Authors: A. Troester, J. Frebault, E. Von Der Marwitz, E. Arsoniadis, S. M. Goldberg, P. Goffredo, C. Jahansouz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTranssphincteric anal fistula is a common and challenging diagnosis for patients and surgeons alike. Ligation of the intersphincteric fistula tract (LIFT) following non-cutting seton placement represents an established definitive treatment with many technical variations. Unfortunately, up to 53% of attempted LIFTs fail. We aim to describe a modified LIFT approach and evaluate outcomes at our institution.MethodsThirty-two patients from 2021 to 2024 underwent the modified LIFT approach which included the offsetting of the transected fistula ends with interposing muscle plication, along with closure of the internal opening, and wide excision of the external opening. Retrospective chart review was performed to assess rates of primary wound healing, complications, recurrence, and incontinence. Recurrences were grouped into three types: type I, sinus tract or recurrent abscess without an internal opening; type II, conversion to an intersphincteric fistula; and type III, transsphincteric recurrence.ResultsThe majority of patients were male (69%) with a mean age of 44 years and mean BMI 31.6 kg/m2. Median operative time was 88 min. Primary healing rate was 94%. Median healing period was 7 (range 4–16) weeks. Of the 10 recurrences, (5 type I, 5 type II, and 0 type III), median time to recurrence was 6 months after the primary wound healed. No patients experienced any postoperative incontinence or urinary retention.ConclusionsIn this cohort of patients with transsphincteric fistulas undergoing the LIFT procedure, the addition of offsetting muscle plication effectively limited transsphincteric recurrence. Further investigation is warranted to directly compare this LIFT adaptation to previously published literature.

Introduction

Anal fistulas, defined as abnormal pathologic tracts between the anal canal and perianal skin, develop in approximately one-third of patients after acute anorectal abscess treatment with a male predominance and a mean age at presentation of 40 years [1]. They are classified both relative to the internal and external anal sphincters and as either simple or complex, with complex fistulas being transsphincteric involving > 30% of the external sphincter, suprasphincteric, extrasphincteric, or horseshoe fistulas and those associated with concomitant processes such as inflammatory bowel disease or malignancy [2, 3]. The management of complex anal fistulas comprises a vast array of surgical approaches, including endorectal advancement flaps, ligation of the intersphincteric fistula tract (LIFT), and novel therapies such as the fistula tract laser closure. These techniques are typically preceded by placement of a draining seton for acute infection control.

First described in 2007, the LIFT is primarily indicated for transsphincteric fistulas, and initially reported a 94% success rate with no impact on continence [4]. Despite the promising results from this early case-series, subsequent systematic reviews have revealed a pooled failure rate as high as 30–40%, indicating the need for technical improvement to foster durable long-term outcomes [5–9]. Since the original LIFT description by Rojanasakul at least six technical variations have been published with success rates ranging from 47% to 95% [8]. Aimed at further enhancing healing rates while preventing recurrence and limiting incontinence, these technical adaptations include the LIFT-plug [10], BioLIFT [11], LIFT without excision of the intersphincteric tract [12], LIFT-plus [13], LIFT-plus with intraoperative seton placement [14], and LIFT-plus with advancement flap [15]. The “plus” in LIFT-plus signifies the coring-out of the fistula tract from the external opening of the fistula tract to the lateral border of the external sphincter. Despite the multitude of adaptations, all variations leave the ligated fistula tract ends in close proximity within the intersphincteric space supporting a path of least resistance should the transsphincteric fistula recur.

We hypothesize that offsetting the ligated fistula ends with an interposed sphincter muscle plication—thereby maximizing the distance apart—could decrease transsphincteric recurrence rates. To our knowledge, this additional variation has not been previously reported in the literature. Thus, we aim describe the novel modification to the LIFT procedure and describe our single-institution experience.

Methods

Study population

A total of 32 adults were diagnosed with a transsphincteric anal fistula and underwent the LIFT procedure from August 2021 through December 2024 at the University of Minnesota Medical Center. Patients eligible for the LIFT procedure included those with a transsphincteric fistula that involved a substantial portion of external sphincter, where performing a fistulotomy would pose a high risk of incontinence. All patients initially underwent non-cutting seton placement to control the acute fistula followed by an endoanal ultrasound approximately 6–8 weeks later. Endoanal ultrasound confirmed the extent of muscle involvement and the absence of additional tracts. All procedures were performed by one of three board certified colorectal surgeons. The inclusion criterion was a 4-week minimum follow-up period to assess healing. The exclusion criterion was patients diagnosed with perianal Crohn’s disease. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Minnesota.

Operative technique

Patients were placed in the prone jackknife position with their buttocks taped widely apart. General anesthesia or monitored anesthesia care plus local anesthetic was used according to anesthesiologist preference. Bowel preparation was administered in the form of enemas or oral drinkable solution. All patients received appropriate preoperative intravenous antibiotics. In all cases, a previously placed seton identified the fistula tract. The seton was removed and the fistula tract was cannulated with a Parks fistula probe (Fig. 1). A 2–3 cm curvilinear incision was made at the intersphincteric groove and dissection was carried down to and around the fistula tract with a lone-star retractor aiding the exposure. The fistula tract in the intersphincteric space was encircled on either side with 2–0 Vicryl sutures (Fig. 2) and the fistula probe was removed. The Vicryl sutures were tied and the fistula tract was excised sharply (Fig. 3). Additional figure-of-eight absorbable sutures were placed to reinforce the edges of the transected fistula tract. The closure sites on the internal and external muscle were tested with gentle irrigation of dilute peroxide without evidence of leakage into the intersphincteric space. Then, the divided edges of the fistula tract were maximally offset in the intersphincteric space with absorbable sutures (Figs. 4 and 5). This represents a key additional step not previously described in modifications to the LIFT procedure. The internal opening on the mucosal side within the anal canal was closed to reduce intersphincteric recurrence risk. The intersphincteric space was closed in layers with 3–0 Vicryl suture for muscle and 3–0 Monocryl suture for skin. Postoperative management

All procedures were performed on an outpatient basis. The majority of patients were discharged with a multimodal pain medication regimen and 7 days of metronidazole (500 mg TID) and instructed to perform sitz baths three times daily until the wound healed. Routine follow-up was conducted to monitor for primary wound healing, complications, recurrence, and incontinence at 2–4 weeks, 6–8 weeks, and as needed thereafter. Primary wound healing was defined as healing of both the original external opening of the fistula and the surgical site and was documented with photos in the electronic medical record. Continence evaluation was based on clinical symptoms. Similar to published literature, failures were classified into three types [16, 17]. Type I failures characterized sinus tracts or recurrent abscesses without an internal opening. Type II failures represented a conversion to an intersphincteric fistula. Type III failures were complete failures extending from the previous internal opening to one or more external skin openings. It was possible for patients to have documented primary wound healing followed by remote presentation of symptoms and a workup demonstrating one of the described failure types.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized with mean and standard deviation. Temporal variables were described with a median and range, and categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages.

Results

From August 2021 to December 2024, 32 LIFT procedures were performed on patients with a transsphincteric anal fistula. The cohort was predominantly male (69%) with a mean age of 44 years and a mean BMI of 31.6 kg/m2. On average, patients underwent 1.7 prior anorectal procedures (incision & drainage, seton placement, or fistulotomy) and had a seton in place for a range of 11 to 112 weeks (median 22 weeks; Table 1). Median operating room time was 88 min. There were no intraoperative complications. Patients were followed for an average of 18 weeks (range 4–68 weeks). Over two-thirds of patients (11/32) had follow-up of at least 8 weeks, and 28% (n = 9) had follow-up beyond 6 months. The primary wound healing rate was 94% (30/32 patients). Median healing period was 7 (range 4–16) weeks. No patient experienced postoperative incontinence. Of the 10 failures, none were transsphincteric recurrences and failure occurred at a median of 6 months (range 3–13 months; Table 1). Comparing patients who experienced a failure with those who did not, there were no differences in preoperative BMI (31.5 vs. 31.3 kg/m2) or history of tobacco use (1 patient vs. 3 patients).

Failures

Ten patients (31%) had postoperative failures with varying degrees of severity (Fig. 7). Five of these patients had a type I recurrence. Three patients had a symptomatic sinus tract that extended to, but did not involve, the lateral border of the sphincter complex. All three were successfully treated with lay-open fistulotomy and curettage. These procedures occurred at 3, 7, and 13 months post LIFT. The patient who underwent lay-open fistulotomy and curettage at 7 months had a non-healing primary wound while the other two patients had complete primary wound healing on clinical exam with subsequent development of the type I recurrence. One patient had a recurrent abscess form at 11 months post LIFT in the site of the previous external opening which was successfully treated with an incision and drainage procedure in clinic. Finally, one patient had a complex initial fistula tract extending to the anterior scrotum. After the LIFT, two procedures were performed to first completely excise the remaining tract to the scrotum, and subsequently to debride/marsupialize a proximal portion of the tract that was causing pain and discharge. Discussion

Of the 32 LIFT procedures performed in our series, the primary wound healing rate was 94%. The median healing period was 7 (range 4–16) weeks. There were 10 failures; 5 were type I, 5 type II, and 0 type III with a median time to failure of 6 months. No patient suffered from postoperative incontinence.

Four previous reviews have published pooled success rates for the LIFT procedure ranging from 47% to 95% [5–7, 9]. However, only one review critically appraised each LIFT modification individually [8]. Primary healing rates were 61–94% for the classic LIFT, 69–94% for the BioLIFT, 47–94% for the LIFT without excision, 77–85% for the LIFT-plus, 51% for the LIFT-plus with advancement flap, 95% for the LIFT-plug, and 95% for the LIFT-plus with intraoperative seton. Caution should be applied when interpreting the LIFT-plug and LIFT-plus with intraoperative seton healing rates as these were based on single-institution series of 21 patients each.

Long-term follow-up in this patient population reveals diminished success over time, as highlighted by failures in 31% of patients in our series despite 30 patients demonstrating upfront complete healing. Madbouly et al. had similar observations in a randomized controlled trial comparing LIFT to mucosal advancement flap, finding that after complete healing in 33 out of 35 patients undergoing LIFT, 7 (21%) developed recurrence at a mean of 18 weeks [18]. While technical factors undoubtedly contribute—and are controlled by the surgeon—multiple operative modifications have reported similar long-term recurrence rates [13, 18–20]. Therefore, determining which patient-driven factors place an individual at higher risk of recurrence warrants critical evaluation to limit subsequent disease severity. While there is a paucity of literature examining these factors, higher preoperative BMI [21] has been associated with higher rates of LIFT failure. However, in our series there was no difference in preoperative BMI between patients who did and did not experience a postoperative failure (31.5 vs. 31.3 kg/m2), an observation potentially limited by sample size. Along similar lines of thinking, other authors have reported higher failure rates in patients with preoperative tobacco use; however, no statistical comparisons could be made given the small incidences [19]. Finally, although postoperative perianal hygiene plays a critical role in wound healing and recurrence, it is inadequately captured through both prospective and retrospective analysis.

Certain disease-specific factors—namely longer fistula tract length—have also been associated with LIFT failure [17]. However, the presence of a seton at the time of LIFT has not been correlated with subsequent failure (p = 0.4) [20]. In their series, Aboulian et al. observed persistent symptoms in 4 of 17 patients with a seton in place at time of LIFT compared to 4 of 9 patients without a seton [20]. Those without a seton had undergone either a fibrin plug, glue, or mucosal advancement flap procedure. In our cohort, all of the patients had at least one seton in place, which was present for a median of 22 weeks (range 11–112 weeks). Typically, setons serve as a means of controlling acute infection; however, one series published by Shanwani et al. examined 45 patients who underwent the LIFT-plus operation without a preoperative seton and reported an 18% recurrence rate [13]. Although potentially counterintuitive, these results demonstrate that in the absence of an active infection, LIFT may be attempted without prior seton placement. Additionally, in our experience, multiple prior anorectal operations increase the technical difficulty of the definitive LIFT surgery as a result of the cycle of uncontrolled infection and inflammation followed by fibrosis of the tract and surrounding tissues. Serving as a proxy for this observation, the median operative time in our cohort was substantially longer than in the reported literature (88 vs. 39 min) [7]. Specifically, in the series by Shanwani et al. where 45 patients underwent the LIFT-plus operation as their first anorectal surgery, operative time averaged 68 min [13]. Along similar lines, Abcarian et al. reported a drop in healing rate from 90% to 65% in patients who had zero prior operations compared to those undergoing two or more, but failed to report whether this achieved statistical significance [19]. Although these single-institution series provide insight as to possible risk factors, larger prospective studies are needed to elucidate a potential correlation between the aforementioned characteristics and success or failure of the LIFT procedure.

Limitations to this study include the retrospective single-institution design, which lacks adequate power to determine differences in postoperative outcomes based on patient-specific preoperative variables. Patients may have followed up elsewhere potentially underestimating recurrence. Although each patient had a minimum of 4 weeks of postoperative follow-up, patients who recently underwent surgery lack long-term outcome data. Additionally, some patients with longer-term outcomes documented had a lapse in follow-up before re-presenting to clinic, which complicates understanding of new vs. recurrent disease. While the cohort size of 32 is relatively small, this is in line with other descriptions of LIFT modifications published in the literature [8]. Despite comparison to other published LIFT modifications, true advantages or disadvantages of each specific LIFT technique cannot be elucidated until larger prospective, randomized studies have been performed.

Conclusions

In this single-institution retrospective review, we describe a novel LIFT modification and examine its success in a series of 32 patients. The technique involves the additional step of offsetting the ligated fistula ends with an interposed sphincter muscle plication. While it does not represent a radical shift in operative technique, our modification to the LIFT procedure is safe, technically feasible, and limits transsphincteric recurrence. Additional detailed investigation is needed to directly compare this LIFT adaptation with existing literature.
