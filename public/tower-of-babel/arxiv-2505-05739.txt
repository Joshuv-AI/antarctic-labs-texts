Static and dynamic theory of polarization under internal and directing electric fields: Fixed-charge and fixed-potential conditions
Akira Onuki

Abstract
We present a continuum theory on statics and dynamics of polar fluids, where the orientational polarization 𝒑 1 {\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1} and the induced polarization 𝒑 2 {\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2} are governed by the Onsager directing field 𝑬 d {\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{d} and the Lorentz internal field 𝑭 F , respectively. We start with a dielectric free energy functional ℱ \cal F with a cross term ∝ ∫ d ​ 𝒓 ​ 𝒑 1 ⋅ 𝒑 2 \propto\int\hskip-1.42262ptd{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}~{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2} , which was proposed by Felderhof [ [ J. Phys. C: Solid State Phys. 12 , 2423 (1979) ] ] . With this cross-coupling, our theory can yield the theoretical results by Onsager and Kirkwood. We also present dynamic equations using the functional derivatives δ ​ ℱ / δ ​ 𝒑 i \delta{\cal F}/\delta{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{i} to calculate the space-time correlations of 𝒑 i {\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{i} . We then obtain analytic expressions for various frequency-dependent quantities including the Debye formula. We find that the fluctuations of the total polarization drastically depend on whether we fix the electrode charge or the applied potential difference between parallel metal electrodes. In the latter fixed-potential condition, we obtain a nonlocal (long-range) polarization correlation inversely proportional to the cell volume V V , which is crucial to understand the dielectric response. It is produced by nonlocal charge fluctuations on the electrode surfaces and is sensitive to the potential drops in the Stern layers in small systems. These nonlocal correlations in the bulk and on the surfaces are closely related due to the global constraint of fixed potential difference. We also add some results in other boundary conditions including the periodic one, where nonlocal correlations also appear.

II
Polarization in linear regime

In the continuum approach,
we treat a nearly incompressible, one-component
polar liquid with a
permanent dipole moment
\mu_{0}
.
Electric charges are present
only one the electrode surfaces
(which will be supposed in the fluid in Sec.V). There is no chemical
reaction in the fluid and on the surfaces.
The net polarization
p
consists
of orientational, atomic,
and electronic contributions
[
7
,
8
,
6
,
3
]
.
The orientational one
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}
is dominant at low frequencies
in highly polar fluids.
The sum of the atomic and electronic ones
is written as
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}

and is called the induced polarization.
The Maxwell electric field
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}=-\nabla\Phi

is produced by
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}+{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}
and the
electrode charges, where
\Phi
is the electric potential.
The electric induction is
given by
{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}+4\pi{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}

in the cgs units.

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\delta{\cal F}/\delta{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}=a_{11}{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}+a_{12}{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},

(5)

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\delta{\cal F}/\delta{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}=a_{22}{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}+a_{12}{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}-{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\chi_{1}={\sum}_{j}a^{1j}=(a_{22}-a_{12})/(a_{11}a_{22}-a_{12}^{2}),

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\chi_{2}={\sum}_{j}a^{2j}=(a_{11}-a_{12})/(a_{11}a_{22}-a_{12}^{2}).

\langle{{M}_{i\alpha}^{\rm tot}{M}_{j\beta}^{\rm tot}}\rangle

Some discussions on their dynamics

The case of fixed

(i) First, setting

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76219pt{\langle{M^{\rm tot}_{i\alpha}M^{\rm tot}_{j\beta}}\rangle}/{Vk_{B}T}=\chi_{\perp}^{ij}(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\delta_{\alpha z}\delta_{\beta z})+\chi_{\parallel}^{ij}\delta_{\alpha z}\delta_{\beta z},

\displaystyle\hskip-22.76219pt{\langle{M^{\rm tot}_{\alpha}M^{\rm tot}_{\beta}}\rangle}/{Vk_{B}T}=\chi(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\delta_{\alpha z}\delta_{\beta z})+\frac{\chi}{\epsilon}\delta_{\alpha z}\delta_{\beta z},

(53)

For

\int\hskip-2.84526ptd{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{\perp}^{\prime}\nabla_{\alpha}\nabla_{\beta}\psi(|{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}^{\prime}|)\cong-\delta(z-z^{\prime})\delta_{\alpha z}\delta_{\beta z},

(54)

where we integrate over
{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}^{\prime}_{\perp}=(x^{\prime}.y^{\prime})
and

\psi(r)

1/4\pi r
.
Here, Eq.(54) is obtained from
\int\hskip-2.84526ptd{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{\perp}^{\prime}\nabla_{z}\psi(|{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}^{\prime}|)\cong-(z-z^{\prime})/2|z-z^{\prime}|

Here, the dipolar interaction suppresses
the

.
In this case, no nonlocal correlation appears

We can derive Eq.(53)

,

(55)

Here,
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}})={\mbox{\boldmath$M$}}^{\rm tot}

\propto\exp(-{\cal F}_{\rm tot}/k_{B}T)

\displaystyle{\langle{M^{\rm tot}_{i\alpha}M^{\rm tot}_{j\beta}}\rangle}/{Vk_{B}T}=\chi_{\perp}^{ij}\delta_{\alpha\beta},

\displaystyle{\sum}_{\alpha}G_{\alpha\alpha}({\mbox{\boldmath$r$}})={\langle{{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$r$}})\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}})}\rangle}/{k_{B}T}

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi}{\epsilon}(2\epsilon+1)\delta({\mbox{\boldmath$r$}})+\frac{\chi(\epsilon-1)}{\epsilon V}~~({\rm periodic}),

(57)

where the first term

(iii) Third, Sprik’s group

(58)

V
Solvation free energy with a charge density

Marcus
[
7
]
studied the electron
transfer kinetics in a polar solvent, where
the solvent polarization around ions was assumed to obey the
classical electrostatics. In this approach, various
chemical reactions in a polar solvent have been
studied
[
10
,
9
,
11
,
12
,
30
,
26
,
85
,
84
]
.
Marcus wrote the fastest
electronic polarization as
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{e}

and the sum of the slower orientational
and atomic ones as
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{u}
. He then expressed
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{e}

in terms of
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{u}

and the
bare electric field

{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}
produced by
the solute charge density
\rho_{\rm s}
and the electrode charges
in local equilibrium.
In these papers, however,
the Lorentz term in the free energy
has been missing. Hence, it is included
in this section.
In our theory,
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}
denotes the orientational polarization
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}
the sum of the atomic and electronic ones.
The relations here
will be used in Secs.VI-VIII.

The bare field
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}

does not include the polarization contribution and
is determined by
[
7
,
8
]

{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}=-\nabla\Phi_{\rm bare},~~~\nabla\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}=-\nabla^{2}\Phi_{\rm bare}=4\pi{\rho}_{\rm s},

(60)

where
\Phi_{\rm bare}
is the bare
potential and the electrode charges
appear in the boundary conditions.

We divide
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$r$}})
into
the longitudinal part
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{i{\parallel}}({\mbox{\boldmath$r$}})

and the transverse part
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{i{\perp}}({\mbox{\boldmath$r$}})
, where their Fourier components are

{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}}_{i{\parallel}}({\mbox{\boldmath$q$}})

and
{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}}_{i{\perp}}({\mbox{\boldmath$q$}})
, respectively
(see below Eq.(35)).
If
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{i}
depends only on
z
,
we have
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{i\parallel}=(0,0,{p}_{iz})
.
From
\nabla\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}=4\pi\rho_{\rm sol}
we find

{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}={\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}+4\pi{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{\parallel}={\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}-4\pi{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{\perp},

(61)

where
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{\parallel}={\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{\perp}={\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1\parallel}+{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2\parallel}
. The fields
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}
and
E
are
longitudinal.
Thus,
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}={\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}
for
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{\perp}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}
.

Minimization of
{\cal F}
with respect
to
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}
at fixed
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}
and
Q_{0}
is attained
at
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}={\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}^{\rm eq}
as in Eqs.(6) and (9).
Using Eq.(61) we rewrite this
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}^{\rm eq}

in terms of
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}

and
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}
as

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}^{\rm eq}=\frac{\epsilon_{\infty}-1}{4\pi\epsilon_{\infty}}\Big[{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-\frac{8\pi}{3}{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1\parallel}\Big]+\frac{\epsilon_{\infty}-1}{3}{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1\perp}.

(62)

Then,
{\cal F}

at
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}={\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}^{\rm eq}

is written as

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt{\cal F}_{\rm s}=\int_{V}\hskip-2.84526ptd{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}\Big[\frac{|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}|^{2}}{8\pi\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2\chi_{\parallel}^{11}}\Big|{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1{\parallel}}-{{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}}_{1}^{\rm eq}\Big|^{2}+\frac{|{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1\perp}|^{2}}{2\chi_{\perp}^{11}}\Big],

(63)

where
\chi_{\perp}^{11}
and
\chi_{\parallel}^{11}

are given in Eqs.(39) and (43) and

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}^{\rm eq}=\frac{\chi_{1}}{\epsilon}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}.=\frac{3(\epsilon-\epsilon_{\infty})}{4\pi(\epsilon_{\infty}+2)\epsilon}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}.

(64)

Here,
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1{\parallel}}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}

evolve slowly, to which

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1\perp}
is uncoupled in
the linear order.

We are interested in the thermal fluctuation of

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}
around

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}^{\rm eq}
in Eq.(62), so we consider its deviation,

{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}-{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}^{\rm eq},

(65)

which will be important in the next section.
Retaining
\xi

we express
E
in Eq.(61) and
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}+{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}
as

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\infty}}\Big[{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-\frac{4\pi}{3}(\epsilon_{\infty}+2){\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1\parallel}\Big]-4\pi{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{\parallel},

(66)

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}=\frac{\epsilon_{\infty}-1}{4\pi\epsilon_{\infty}}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}+\frac{\epsilon_{\infty}+2}{3\epsilon_{\infty}}\Big({\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1\parallel}+\epsilon_{\infty}{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1\perp}\Big)+{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}.

(67)

The free energy
increase due to
\xi
is calculated as

{\cal F}_{\xi}={\cal F}-{\cal F}_{\rm s}=\frac{1}{2}{a_{22}}\int_{V}d{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}\Big[\epsilon_{\infty}|{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{{\parallel}}|^{2}+|{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{{\perp}}|^{2}\Big],

(68)

where
{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{\parallel}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{\perp}
are
the longitudinal and transverse parts of
\xi
, respectively.
Thus,

\int d{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}\langle{\xi_{\alpha}({\mbox{\boldmath$r$}})p_{1\beta}({\mbox{\boldmath$0$}})}\rangle=0
,
so
\xi
is
orthogonal
to
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}
.
From the above
{\cal F}_{\xi}
we obtain
the variances of the Fourier components

{\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}}({\mbox{\boldmath$q$}})

of
{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$r$}})
:

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\frac{\langle{{\hat{\xi}}_{\alpha}({\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}){\hat{\xi}}_{\beta}({\mbox{\boldmath$q$}})^{*}}\rangle}{Vk_{B}T}=\frac{\epsilon_{\infty}-1}{4\pi}\Big[(\delta_{\alpha\beta}-{\hat{q}}_{\alpha}{\hat{q}}_{\beta})+\frac{{\hat{q}}_{\alpha}{\hat{q}}_{\beta}}{\epsilon_{\infty}}\Big].

(69)

In
E
in Eq.(66), the last term

-4\pi{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{\parallel}

serves as a rapidly varying random noise
(see Eqs.(74) and (75)).

Lee and Hynes
[
8
]
presented the solvation
free energy
{\cal F}_{\rm s}

in their Eq.(2.4), which is
equivalent to that of Marcus
[
7
]
.
Let us rewrite the second term in Eq.(63) as

\frac{1}{2\chi_{\parallel}^{11}}\Big|{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1{\parallel}}-{{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}}_{1}^{\rm eq}\Big|^{2}=\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon_{\infty}}{8\pi\epsilon\epsilon_{\infty}}\Big|{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-\frac{\epsilon_{\infty}+2}{3}\cdot\frac{4\pi\epsilon{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1\parallel}}{\epsilon-\epsilon_{\infty}}\Big|^{2},

where use is made of Eq.(39).
We can see that the factor
(\epsilon_{\infty}+2)/3
in the above
expression does not appear in Lee-Hynes’
{\cal F}_{\rm s}
.
In Appendix C, we will give more
comments on the previous linear theories.

VI
Polarization dynamics

VI.1
Dynamic equations

We now investigate the linear
dynamics of the time-dependent
polarizations
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{i}({\mbox{\boldmath$r$}},t)

at long wavelengths, where the translational motions are negligible.
The temperature
T
and the dipole density
n
are
homogeneous constants.
We set up simple relaxation equations
[
88
,
74

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}=-L_{1}\frac{\delta}{\delta{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}}{\cal F}=\frac{\chi_{1}}{\tau_{\rm L}\epsilon}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}-\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm L}}{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1{\parallel}}-\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm D}}{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1\perp}

\displaystyle\hskip 5.69054pt-({4\pi}/{3})L_{1}(2{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{\parallel}-{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{\perp}),

(70)

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}=-L_{2}\frac{\delta}{\delta{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}}{\cal F}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm f}}(\epsilon_{\infty}{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{\parallel}+{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{\perp}),

where
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are kinetic coefficients
with
L_{1}\ll L_{2}
and

at fixed

.
We should replace
{\cal F}
by

\tilde{\cal F}
in Eq.(15) at fixed

to
obtain the same equations (see below Eq.(16)).
The right hand sides are expressed in terms of

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}
,
\xi
, and
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}

and

In Eqs.(70) and (71)

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm f}=(\epsilon_{\infty}-1)/(4\pi L_{2}).

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1\parallel}

\xi

is the
Debye relaxation time in Eqs.(1) and (89) below.

,

In the limit

, we
are led to
{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}-{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}^{\rm eq}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}

The relaxation equation (67) is

Using Eqs.(13), (64), and (66),

We should also generalize Eqs.(70) and (71)

VII
Dynamical linear response

VII.1
Frequency-dependent dielectric functions

In this subsection,
we examine the dielectric response
to an applied field oscillating with frequency
\omega

in the geometry of parallel metal plates.
The deviations are the statistical averages
and are written in complex numbers
depending on time as
e^{i\omega t}
,
where their real parts have their physical meaning.

The vector deviations are written as

{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{i}=p_{i}^{*}{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{z},~~~{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}=p^{*}{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{z},~~~{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}=E_{0}^{*}{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{z},~~{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}=E^{*}{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{z},

(85)

where
{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{z}

is the unit vector along the
z
axis.
Here,
p_{i}^{*}
and
E^{*}
are homogeneous in the bulk
but depend on
z
near the walls.
We relate
{E}_{0}^{*}
and
{E}^{*}
as

E_{0}^{*}=\epsilon^{*}(\omega)E^{*}=4\pi{\bar{\sigma}}_{0}\propto e^{i\omega t}.

(86)

where
\epsilon^{*}(\omega)=1+4\pi\chi^{*}(\omega)
is
the frequency-dependent dielectric constant.
We define the frequency-dependent
dielectric susceptibilities for
E
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}_{0}
as

\chi_{i}^{*}(\omega)=p_{i}^{*}/E^{*},~~\alpha_{i}^{*}(\omega)=p_{i}^{*}/E_{0}^{*}.

(87)

Their sums are the net susceptibilities

\chi^{*}(\omega)=p^{*}/E={\sum}_{i}\chi_{i}^{*}(\omega)
and

\alpha^{*}(\omega)=p^{*}/E_{0}={\sum}_{i}\alpha_{i}^{*}(\omega)
, where

\alpha_{i}^{*}(\omega)={\chi_{i}^{*}(\omega)}/{\epsilon^{*}(\omega)},~~\alpha^{*}(\omega)=[1-1/\epsilon^{*}(\omega)]/4\pi.

(88)

In the present situation,
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{i}

are longitudinal (or
{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{i\perp}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}
).
Setting
{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}
,
we have
(1+i\omega\tau_{\rm L})\alpha_{1}^{*}=\chi_{1}/\epsilon
from Eq.(70) and
\alpha_{2}^{*}=(1-1/\epsilon_{\infty})(1-8\pi\alpha_{1}^{*}/3)/4\pi

from Eq.(62). Then, we obtain
all the susceptibilities as

\displaystyle\hskip-34.1433pt\epsilon^{*}(\omega)=\epsilon\frac{1+i\omega\tau_{\rm L}}{1+i\omega\tau_{\rm D}}=\epsilon_{\infty}+\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon_{\infty}}{1+i\omega\tau_{\rm D}},

(89)

\displaystyle\hskip-34.1433pt\chi_{1}^{*}(\omega)=\frac{\chi_{1}}{1+i\omega\tau_{\rm D}}=\frac{3}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{\epsilon^{*}(\omega)-\epsilon_{\infty}}{\epsilon_{\infty}+2},

(90)

\displaystyle\hskip-34.1433pt\chi_{2}^{*}(\omega)=\frac{\epsilon_{\infty}-1}{4\pi}\Big[1+\frac{4\pi}{3}\chi_{1}^{*}(\omega)\Big],

(91)

\displaystyle\hskip-34.1433pt\alpha^{*}(\omega)=\frac{\epsilon_{\infty}-1}{4\pi\epsilon_{\infty}}+\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon_{\infty}}{4\pi\epsilon\epsilon_{\infty}(1+i\omega\tau_{\rm L})},

(92)

\displaystyle\hskip-34.1433pt\alpha_{1}^{*}(\omega)=\frac{\chi_{1}}{\epsilon(1+i\omega\tau_{\rm L})}=\frac{3}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon_{\infty}}{(\epsilon_{\infty}+2){\epsilon(1+i\omega\tau_{\rm L})}},

(93)

\displaystyle\hskip-34.1433pt\alpha_{2}^{*}(\omega)=\frac{\epsilon_{\infty}-1}{4\pi\epsilon\epsilon_{\infty}}\Big[\epsilon-\frac{8\pi\chi_{1}}{3(1+i\omega\tau_{\rm L})}\Big],

(94)

which hold for
\omega\tau_{\rm f}\ll 1
. Note that
Eq.(89) is the Debye formula (1).
Here,
\epsilon^{*}(\omega)
and
\chi_{i}^{*}(\omega)

are characterized by
\tau_{\rm D}
, while

\alpha^{*}(\omega)
and
\alpha_{i}^{*}(\omega)
by
\tau_{\rm L}
.
See their relaxation functions
in Eqs.(97)-(102) below.

For
\epsilon\gg\epsilon_{\infty}
and
\epsilon_{\infty}-1\gtrsim 1
we notice that

\epsilon_{\infty}
has the meaning of the high-frequency
dielectric constant in the frequency
range
\tau_{\rm L}^{-1}\ll\omega\ll\tau_{\rm f}^{-1}
, where
|{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}|\gg|{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}|
and

\chi^{*}(\omega)\cong\chi_{2}^{*}(\omega)\cong(\epsilon_{\infty}-1)/4\pi.

(95)

See the sentences below Eq.(13) on the static limit
\chi_{2}
.

In addition, we note that Eq.(70) and (71)
can be solved for general
\omega
under Eq.(85).
For example, in the case

L_{1}/L_{2}\ll 1
,
\epsilon^{*}(\omega)
and
\alpha^{*}(\omega)

are calculated as

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\epsilon^{*}(\omega)-1=\frac{\epsilon_{\infty}-1}{1+i\omega\tau_{\rm f}}+\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon_{\infty}}{1+i\omega\tau_{\rm D}},

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt4\pi\alpha^{*}(\omega)=\frac{\epsilon_{\infty}-1}{\epsilon_{\infty}+i\omega\tau_{\rm f}}+\frac{\epsilon-\epsilon_{\infty}}{\epsilon\epsilon_{\infty}(1+i\omega\tau_{\rm L})},

(96)

where the first terms involve
\tau_{\rm f}
.

(112)

IX
Summary and remarks

We have studied statics and dynamics of dielectric fluids
in the linear regime in the continuum theory.
We have assumed
the fixed-charge and fixed-potential
conditions in a
L\times L\times H
cell with
H\ll L
.
We have also revealed consequences for the
total polarizations in other boundary conditions.
We have found nonlocal polarization correlations,
which are essential in electrostatic systems.
Our main results are summarized below.

(i) In Sec.IIA, we have started with Felderhof’s
dielectric free energy density
f
in Eq.(3)
[
6
]
.
It contains the Lorentz term
\propto{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{1}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}_{2}

From this

we have obtained the susceptibilities
\chi_{1}
and

in Eq.(13), which also follow

[
2
]
.
In Sec,IIB, we have examined the effect of the

in Eq.(2).
In Sec.IIC, we have introduced the directing field

governing

and the orientational susceptibility
\chi_{d}

by

(ii) In Sec.III, we have calculated

,

(iii) In Sec.IV, we have calculated the

(iv) In Sec.V, we have examined the solvation

(vi) In Sec.VII,

(vii) In Sec.VIII,

Appendix A:
Thin-film model of Stern layers

Here, we treat a Stern layer as a thin film with a
low dielectric constant
\epsilon_{s}(\ll\epsilon)
in the region

0<z<d~(\ll H)
.
In the film, the surface charge density
{\sigma}_{0}

at
z=0
induces a polarization
p_{s}
along the
z
axis,
where the electric field is given by

E_{s}=4\pi({\sigma}_{0}-p_{s})
.
We then propose the dielectric free energy density in the film as

f_{s}=2\pi({\sigma}_{0}-p_{s})^{2}+p_{s}^{2}/2\chi_{s},

(A1)

where
\chi_{s}=(\epsilon_{s}-1)/4\pi
.
For given
{\sigma}_{0}
, minimization of
f_{s}

with respect to
p_{s}
yields

p_{s}/\chi_{s}=E_{s}=4\pi{{\sigma}}_{0}/\epsilon_{s},~~f_{s}=2\pi{{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}/\epsilon_{s}.

(A2)

The surface capacitance is then
given by
C_{0}=\sigma_{0}/dE_{s}=\epsilon_{s}/4\pi d
. If the dielectric constant varies
smoothly as

\epsilon=\epsilon(z)
,
C_{0}
is the
z
-integral
of
E_{s}(z)
in the layer
and
\Phi_{a}
is given by
[
69
]

\Phi_{a}=4\pi\sigma_{0}\int_{0}^{H}dz{\epsilon(z)}^{-1}.

The three-dimensional
electric potential
can be calculated if use is made of the Fourier transformation
in the
xy
plane
[
81
,
41
]
.
Note that the charge-free polarization and electric field
[
62
]
,

p_{\rm int}
and
E_{\rm int}(=-4\pi p_{\rm int})
,
do not contribute to
f_{s}
.

The integral of
f_{s}
in the film
0<z<d
is given by

d\int d{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{\perp}f_{s}=L^{2}{\bar{\sigma}}_{0}^{2}/2C_{0}+\int d{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}_{\perp}({\sigma}^{\rm inh}_{0})^{2}/2C_{0},

(A3)

where
\sigma_{0}^{\rm inh}=\sigma_{0}-{\bar{\sigma}}_{0}

is the inhomogeneous part of
\sigma_{0}
and
the first term gives the surface free energy
in Eq.(133). For
C_{H}=C_{0}
, Eq.(19) gives the surface electric length,

\ell_{\rm w}=2d(\epsilon/\epsilon_{s}-1)\cong 2d\epsilon/\epsilon_{s}\gg 2d.

(A4)

There should be Coulombic repulsion
among the surface charges, which is
screened by the polarization deviation near the surface.
Thus, we have an additional surface free energy density,
written as
(\sigma_{0}^{\rm inh})^{2}/2D_{0}
at long wavelengths.
Then, the short-range variance
S_{0}
of
{\sigma}^{\rm inh}_{0}

in Eq.(138) is given by

S_{0}=(1/C_{0}+1/D_{0})^{-1}.

(A5)

We also propose a dynamic equation for
p_{s}
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{s}=-L_{s}\frac{\partial f_{s}}{\partial p_{s}}=-L_{s}\Big[\frac{\epsilon_{s}}{\chi_{s}}p_{s}-4\pi{{\sigma}}_{0}\Big],

(A6)

where
L_{s}
is a kinetic coefficient and
the relaxation time of
{p}_{s}
is
\tau_{s}=\chi_{s}/\epsilon_{s}L_{s}
.
For
{\bar{\sigma}}_{0}\propto e^{i\omega t}
,
the lateral averages of
{p}_{s}
and

{E}_{s}
in the film are given by

{\bar{p}}_{s}={4\pi\chi_{s}{\bar{\sigma}}_{0}}/{\epsilon_{s}(1+i\omega\tau_{s})},~~~{\bar{E}}_{s}={4\pi}{\bar{\sigma}}_{0}/{\epsilon_{s}^{*}(\omega)}.

(A7)

We define the frequency-dependent film dielectric constant
and surface capacitance by

\epsilon_{s}^{*}(\omega)=1+\frac{\epsilon_{s}-1}{1+i\omega\tau_{s}\epsilon_{s}},~~C^{*}(\omega)=\frac{1}{4\pi d}\epsilon_{s}^{*}(\omega).

(A8)

The induced potential drop at
z=0

is given by
d{\bar{E}}_{s}={\bar{\sigma}}_{0}/C^{*}(\omega)
,
Thus, we find Eq.(145).
