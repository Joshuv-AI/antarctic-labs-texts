A case of gender-affirming mastectomy in a transmasculine patient during treatment with isotretinoin
Authors: Alicia Mizes, David J Field, Paul Blackcloud
Published: 2026

Introduction

Hormones play a significant role in the pathogenesis of acne vulgaris, especially for transgender patients taking testosterone.1, 2, 3 There are limited evidence-based guidelines for treating acne in patients receiving testosterone therapy, and acne severity may warrant escalation to isotretinoin (13-cis-retinoic acid). Concurrently, many patients also desire gender-affirming surgeries after starting testosterone.4 Historically, discontinuing isotretinoin for 6 months prior to excisional procedures has been advised; in more recent years, this recommendation has been called into question.2,3,5 We described a transmasculine patient on testosterone taking isotretinoin who, after a discussion involving potential risks of poor wound healing with surgery, opted to continue acne treatment and undergo gender-affirming mastectomy with a successful outcome.

Case report

A 22-year-old transmasculine patient presented to dermatology for worsening acne since starting testosterone therapy over one year prior (currently testosterone 1.62% gel 40.5 mg topically daily). His contributory medical history included lifelong acne, which previously failed to improve with topical tretinoin and oral minocycline. Physical examination showed numerous inflammatory papules and a few open and closed comedones involving the cheeks, forehead, and chin, with more severe inflammatory acne of the chest and back, with excoriations and early scarring (Fig 1, A). He started a topical regimen of a benzoyl peroxide wash and tretinoin 0.025% cream, in addition to systemic treatment with doxycycline 100 mg twice daily, which he had difficulty tolerating due to nausea and vomiting. At his 3-month follow-up appointment, he endorsed using the tretinoin cream, but not the benzoyl peroxide wash, and was only able to tolerate doxycycline once daily. Given persistent acne (Fig 1, B), the decision was made to start isotretinoin at 30 mg daily, which after 1 month was increased to 60 mg daily (1 mg/kg/d). Three months into treatment with isotretinoin, he reported plans to undergo an elective mastectomy as part of gender-affirming care. After extensive discussion of the potential risks of poor wound healing and through shared decision-making that aligned with his goals of continuing to treat his acne while undergoing surgery, the patient chose to continue isotretinoin at 60 mg daily and underwent a bilateral simple mastectomy with free nipple grafting during his third month of isotretinoin. His postoperative course included a small seroma of the right side of the chest requiring drainage twice, but he otherwise had no postoperative complications or delayed wound healing and continued to tolerate isotretinoin well. Two months after surgery, isotretinoin was increased to 80 mg daily. He had complete clearance of his acne after 7 months of isotretinoin treatment and a cumulative dose of 232.5 mg/kg (Fig 1, C). Six months after his surgery and one month after stopping isotretinoin, he continues to be free of keloid formation and hypertrophic scarring (Fig 2, A-C).

Case discussion

Many transmasculine persons who take testosterone experience acne within the first 2 years of treatment. One cohort study of 988 transmasculine patients found the prevalence of acne increased from 6.3% to 31.1% after initiating testosterone.4 Another study found facial acne increased from 35% to 82.4% and chest and back acne increased from 15% to 88.2% after 6 months of treatment.6 Initial treatment of mild acne consists of topical retinoids, antibiotics, and antiandrogens, while treatment of moderate acne includes oral antibiotics. Spironolactone is typically avoided in transmasculine patients due to potential feminizing effects, including breast tissue development and body fat redistribution that may cause gender dysphoria.4 Similar to cisgender patients, transmasculine patients with severe acne should be considered candidates for isotretinoin. Previous case series report isotretinoin initiation about 6 months after initiation of testosterone, with a treatment duration of 3.5 to 9 months.6 Transmasculine patients may also pursue gender-affirming mastectomy and gender-affirming genital surgery, including metoidioplasty, phalloplasty, hysterectomy, and/or oophorectomy, and procedure timing should be considered when prescribing isotretinoin.4

Current guidelines recommend discontinuing isotretinoin 6 months prior to elective surgeries and cosmetic procedures, which includes dermabrasion, chemical peels, and laser procedures.5,7 The concept that isotretinoin causes poor wound healing, hypertrophic scarring, and keloids is largely based on 3 case series from the mid-1980s that described 10 patients with delayed healing and keloid formation after mechanical dermabrasion and 1 after argon laser treatment.5,7 A systematic review with consensus recommendations from 2017 that reviewed 32 relevant publications with 1485 procedures found insufficient evidence to support delaying manual dermabrasion, superficial chemical peels, cutaneous surgery, laser hair removal, and fractional ablative and nonablative procedures for patients currently taking or having recently completed isotretinoin treatment.5,7 The cutaneous surgeries performed on patients receiving isotretinoin included blepharoplasties, liposuctions, fat transfers, face-lifts, skin biopsies, subcisions, excisions, and punch elevations of scars. Only mechanical dermabrasion and fully ablative laser procedures were not recommended given reports of keloid formation and hypertrophic scarring.5

To the best of our knowledge, there are no previously reported cases of transmasculine individuals taking isotretinoin who have concurrently undergone gender-affirming surgery. Only 1 study reports 2 cis women who began Roaccutane about 2 months prior to bilateral reduction mammoplasties; 1 continued Roaccutane 4.5 months postoperatively and the other 16 months postoperatively, and both healed well.8

Transgender patients may report that acne worsens their mental health and quality of life due to anticipation of social rejection and avoidance of social interactions, dissatisfaction with their body appearance, and rejection and discrimination by others.9 Although the timeline of utilizing isotretinoin can be complicated by the timing of gender-affirming surgeries, as current guidelines suggest the discontinuation of isotretinoin at least 6 months prior, we advocate that the significant impact of severe acne on mental health and gender dysphoria should be considered when deciding treatment. Here, we presented a 22-year-old transmasculine patient who decided to continue isotretinoin through his scheduled gender-affirming mastectomy after a shared decision-making discussion during which we shared the existing literature influencing guidelines on isotretinoin cessation. Our patient’s acne caused him considerable discomfort and contributed to gender dysphoria, and he desired quick improvements but also did not want to delay his gender-affirming care. He recovered well postoperatively without significant complications and healed without hypertrophic scarring or keloid formation. Consequently, we suggested that health care providers can cautiously consider continuing isotretinoin for patients getting gender-affirming mastectomies without other risk factors after thorough counseling.
