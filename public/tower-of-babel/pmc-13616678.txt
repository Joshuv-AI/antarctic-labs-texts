Combined vitamins D3 and K2 as potential modulators of multiple myeloma cell pathophysiology: transcriptomic and epigenetic effects under standard culture and bone marrow-like conditions
Authors: Karolina Łuczkowska, Patrycja Stodolak, Piotr Kulig, Bogusław Machaliński
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMultiple myeloma is an incurable hematological malignancy that requires extensive research to understand its biology and identify novel therapeutic strategies. Hypoxia within the tumor microenvironment affects numerous biological processes, including enhancing cellular stress resistance, promoting angiogenesis, shifting metabolism toward anaerobic glycolysis, and modulating gene expression and epigenetic modifications.MethodsIn this study, we investigated the effects of combined vitamin D3 and vitamin K2 treatment on MM cells cultured under standard and bone marrow-like conditions, emphasizing the critical role of oxygen levels in cell culture. Transcriptomic (mRNA sequencing) and DNA methylation analyses were performed exclusively for the combined vitamin D3 and vitamin K2 treatment to characterize the molecular changes associated with this intervention.ResultsTranscriptomic and DNA methylation analyses comparing cells treated with the combined vitamin D3 and vitamin K2 regimen with untreated controls (adjusted p<0.05) identified 16 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 83 differentially methylated positions (DMPs) in U266 cells, and 4,441 DEGs and 26 DMPs in MM1S cells under bone marrow-like conditions. Under normoxic conditions, 444 DEGs and 5,269 DMPs were identified in U266 cells, whereas 4,575 DEGs and 8,710 DMPs were detected in MM1S cells. These findings demonstrate that the molecular response to the combined vitamin treatment differed between MM cell lines and oxygen conditions, with a particularly restricted transcriptional response in U266 cells and reduced DNA methylation changes under hypoxia. These findings suggest cell line-specific adaptation mechanisms to low oxygen conditions, similar to those occurring in the tumor microenvironment in vivo. Focusing on the effects of vitamin treatment under hypoxia, we identified several genes, including ABCA1, ADAMTS9, and RUNX1T1, that may contribute to the antitumor mechanisms of VD and VK. Notably, hypermethylation of the MCOLN3 gene correlated with reduced expression, identifying MCOLN3 as a potential target for further investigation into the molecular effects of vitamin D3 and vitamin K2.DiscussionOverall, these findings demonstrate that oxygen availability influences the antitumor effects of combined vitamin D3 and vitamin K2 treatment and reveal molecular changes that may contribute to their mechanism of action. They also highlight the importance of considering physiological oxygen conditions in in vitro studies of multiple myeloma.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is an incurable hematologic malignancy derived from the malignant plasma cells of MM residing in the bone marrow. It is classified as a chronic and progressive disease, characterized by alternating periods of remission and relapse, with clinical and molecular pathogenesis marked by substantial heterogeneity (1–4). Despite the introduction of novel therapies, including proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulatory drugs, and monoclonal antibodies, MM remains a disease with a high risk of relapse and resistance to treatment. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore new therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating the intracellular survival pathways of MM cells and their interactions with the bone marrow microenvironment (2).

The molecular basis of MM encompasses both genetic and epigenetic alterations that jointly drive malignant transformation, disease progression, and drug resistance. The most common cytogenetic abnormalities include translocations involving the IGH region (14q32), RB1 deletions, 1q gain, hyperdiploidy, and mutations in NRAS, KRAS, and TP53 (8, 9). Particularly unfavorable prognostic factors are represented by translocations t (4,14)(p16;q32), t (14,16), t (14,20), chromosome 1 abnormalities, and 17p deletion involving the TP53 gene (5–8). Alongside these alterations, increasing attention has been directed toward epigenetic dysregulation, which significantly shapes disease biology and may represent a promising therapeutic target (9–11).

An essential factor in the pathophysiology of MM is the bone marrow microenvironment, where malignant cells reside and adapt to local conditions. Of particular importance is hypoxia, a physiological hallmark of the hematopoietic niche. By regulating bone marrow cell function, hypoxia promotes metabolic changes, enhanced proliferation, survival of malignant plasma cells, and epigenetic modifications (12–14). Stabilization of the transcription factor HIF-1α triggers adaptive molecular pathways while simultaneously inducing epigenomic remodeling, further contributing to the complexity of disease progression. It is noteworthy that most in vitro studies are conducted under normoxic conditions (21% O2), which fail to accurately reflect the hypoxic environment of the bone marrow (15–18). Because hypoxia profoundly influences gene expression, epigenetic regulation, and intracellular signaling pathways, it may also modify the cellular response to compounds with regulatory activity, including vitamins with potential antitumor properties.

Metabolic and environmental factors, such as vitamins with potential modulatory activity, also deserve particular attention. Vitamin D (VD), a prohormone activated in the liver and kidneys, exerts its effects via the nuclear receptor VDR, regulating the expression of numerous genes (19). Although traditionally associated with calcium–phosphate homeostasis and bone metabolism, an increasing number of studies highlight its pleiotropic, so-called nonclassical actions (20). These include the regulation of immune responses and modulation of tumor-related processes. VD deficiency, frequently observed in MM patients, is associated with poor prognosis and higher mortality (21–23). Studies have shown that VD can inhibit cell viability, induce differentiation, and influence the epigenome by modulating DNA methylation, histone acetylation, and microRNA expression (24–26).

Vitamin K (VK), particularly in the form of menaquinone-7 (MK-7), has also attracted growing interest. Beyond its well-established role in blood coagulation and bone metabolism, VK exhibits antitumor properties, including the induction of apoptosis, inhibition of proliferation, and epigenomic regulation through promoter methylation and histone acetylation (27–31). Among different VK isoforms, MK-7 is characterized by high bioavailability and a longer half-life, which may contribute to its sustained biological activity. Previous studies have indicated that VK derivatives may inhibit tumor cell growth and induce apoptosis in several cancer models. However, the biological effects of VK/MK-7 in multiple myeloma remain insufficiently explored, and its potential role in regulating malignant plasma cell biology requires further investigation. Although its antitumor effects are less extensively documented compared to VD, accumulating evidence suggests that the combined activity of both vitamins may exert synergistic effects. In clinical practice, vitamin K2 is frequently considered as a complementary supplementation to vitamin D3, particularly due to the synergistic roles of both vitamins in calcium homeostasis and bone metabolism. This combination may be of particular relevance in multiple myeloma patients, who are at increased risk of skeletal complications due to disease-associated bone remodeling abnormalities. This synergy, well established in bone and cardiovascular physiology, may also contribute to the modulation of molecular and epigenetic plasticity in malignant cells. Considering the complementary biological functions of both vitamins, combined VD and VK treatment may represent a promising approach for modulating malignant plasma cell behavior and the bone marrow microenvironment.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects of combined vitamin D3 and vitamin K2 treatment in multiple myeloma cells under both standard and bone marrow-like conditions, providing new insights into how oxygen availability influences the molecular and biological responses to vitamin-based therapy. The primary aim of this study was to investigate the effects of combined vitamin D3 and vitamin K2 treatment on multiple myeloma cells under standard and bone marrow-like conditions and to determine whether oxygen availability influences the biological response to vitamin treatment. The secondary aims were to: (i) characterize transcriptomic and DNA methylation changes associated with combined vitamin D3 and vitamin K2 treatment; (ii) identify molecular pathways and candidate genes potentially involved in the antitumor effects of vitamin treatment; and (iii) evaluate the importance of applying physiologically relevant oxygen conditions in in vitro studies of multiple myeloma. We hypothesized that combined vitamin D3 and vitamin K2 treatment would exert antitumor effects on MM cells and that the magnitude and molecular characteristics of this response would be modulated by oxygen availability.

Materials and methods

Cell culture general course of the experiment

In this study, the U266 and MM1S human multiple myeloma cell lines (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were utilized. The cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (ATCC, cat. no. 30-2001), supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 4500 mg/L glucose, and 1500 mg/L sodium bicarbonate, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. To create a complete growth medium, fetal bovine serum was added to reach a final concentration of 15%. The culture medium was refreshed every three days. The concentrations of vitamins 25(OH)D (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and K2MK-7 (Sigma-Aldrich) were determined experimentally using the Alamar Blue assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the results are presented in subsection 3.1. After testing various concentrations, myeloma cells were incubated for 48 hours with the selected doses of 10-6M 25(OH)D and 2.5×10-7M K2MK-7 for subsequent experiments. In addition, the selected dose of vitamins is not toxic to fibroblasts (see subsection 3.1). This approach was adopted to ensure that the selected doses exerted biological effects on malignant cells while remaining non-toxic to non-malignant cells, thereby reflecting concentrations with greater potential translational and clinical relevance rather than maximizing cytotoxicity. Cell cultures were maintained both under standard conditions (~14.5 PSI and 21% O2) and under conditions similar to the natural environment in which multiple myeloma occurs, i.e., bone marrow-like low-pressure/hypoxic conditions (1 PSI and 6% O2). These parameters were selected to mimic the physiological environment of the bone marrow, where multiple myeloma cells reside. To establish bone marrow-like conditions, cells were cultured under reduced oxygen and pressure conditions using an Avatar system (Xcell Biosciences; San Francisco, CA, USA). The study consisted of the following research groups: a) myeloma cells incubated with 25(OH)D; b) myeloma cells incubated with K2MK-7; c) myeloma cells incubated simultaneously with 25(OH)D and K2MK-7. The control group consisted of myeloma cells not treated with vitamins. Both vitamins were initially dissolved in ethanol and subsequently diluted in culture medium to the desired working concentrations. A vehicle control containing the corresponding concentration of ethanol (maximum final concentration: 0.01% v/v) was included in all experiments to exclude potential solvent-related effects. Cells from each research group and the control group were assessed for cell viability. However, for mRNA-seq and methylation array analysis, only the control group and the group simultaneously treated with 25(OH)D and K2MK-7 were selected. The 25(OH)D form was chosen for this study because it is significantly more chemically stable than the 1,25(OH)2D form, which has a very short half-life and is easily degraded. This short half-life, combined with possible catabolic mechanisms (e.g., via the CYP24A1 enzyme found in U266 and MM1S cells), may limit its sustained activity in cell cultures. The 25(OH)D form appears to be more suitable for obtaining reproducible and reliable results in cell cultures.

Assessment of cell viability over time

Cell viability over time was assessed using the Alamar Blue assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The study aimed to demonstrate the long-term effects of a single incubation with vitamins on myeloma cells. The experiment was performed in three biological replicates for each cell line, with six technical replicates per biological replicate. Therefore, cells after 48 hours of vitamin incubation and a control group were seeded into 96-well plates (15000 cells/well). Changes in cell viability were assessed on days 1, 3, 6, and 8 of culture.

DNA extraction and bisulfate conversion

Genomic DNA was extracted from three independent cell culture replicates for each experimental group using the PureLink Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. DNA yield and integrity were evaluated with the TapeStation 4510 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) using the Genomic DNA ScreenTape kit (Agilent Technologies). All DNA samples exhibited a DNA Integrity Number (DIN) of 9 or higher, indicating excellent quality. For bisulfite conversion, 500 ng of DNA per sample was processed using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) according to the supplier’s instructions.

RNA isolation

Total RNA was isolated from three biological replicates per group, each derived from 1 × 106 cells. The extraction was carried out using the mirVana miRNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and integrity were evaluated using the TapeStation 4510 system in combination with the RNA ScreenTape kit (Agilent Technologies). All samples achieved RNA Integrity Numbers (RINs) of 9 or higher, indicating excellent RNA quality.

Methylation arrays

DNA methylation profiling was performed using the Infinium MethylationEPIC v2.0 BeadChip Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Each experimental group included three independent biological replicates (n=3). After bisulfite treatment, DNA was amplified for 24 hours, enzymatically fragmented, and precipitated to prepare it for hybridization. Samples were hybridized to the BeadChip at 48 C for 20 hours. Following hybridization, non-specifically bound DNA was removed through washing steps. Primer extension was then carried out using labeled nucleotides, and the arrays were stained. Final detection was conducted on a NextSeq 550 system (Illumina).

mRNAseq

Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep Kit and IDT for Illumina RNA UD Indexes Set A (Illumina), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Each experimental group included three independent biological replicates (n=3). For each sample, 1000 ng of total RNA was used. RNA was first purified using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and fragmented for cDNA synthesis. Reverse transcription was performed with Actinomycin D to enhance strand specificity by inhibiting DNA-dependent synthesis. The second strand was then synthesized, followed by A-tailing and adapter ligation. Libraries were purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), indexed, and amplified to form clusters during sequencing (10 PCR cycles: 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s). After a second purification, library quality and concentration were assessed using the TapeStation system and D1000 ScreenTape (Agilent Technologies). Libraries were diluted to 1.3 nM, pooled, denatured with 0.2 N NaOH, and sequenced at 1.3 pM on a NextSeq 550 (Illumina) platform using the High Output Kit v2.5 (150 cycles, Illumina).

Western blot

VDR protein levels were assessed using automated Western blot analysis. Cell lysates from the U266 and MM1S lines were obtained from three biological replicates and assayed using the Bradford method. The analysis was performed using the 12–230 kDa Fluorescence Separation Module (Bio-techne), EZ Standard Pack 1 12–230 kDa (Bio-techne), primary anti-VDR rat monoclonal (9A7) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a concentration of 1:20, and anti-GAPDH mouse monoclonal, Invitrogen GAPDH monoclonal anti-mouse (1D4) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a concentration of 1:20; appropriate secondary antibody anti-rat (Thermo Fisher Scientific), anti-mouse secondary antibody (Bio-techne)) and at a concentration of 1:200. The amount of protein used per capillary was 0.62 mg/mL. Samples were denatured according to the manufacturer’s instructions before analysis. The separation1 and analysis ware performed using a Jess apparatus (Bio-techne). Densitometric analysis of western blot data was performed using the dedicated Compass for Simple Western software associated with the Jess system. Integrated optical density (IOD-area under the peak) values were obtained automatically by the software, with background corrected and target protein quantification normalized to GAPDH.

qRT-PCR

The qRT-PCR reaction was carried out in two stages: 1) reverse transcription of 0.1 µg mRNA using the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by 2) the qRT-PCR amplification step. For each sample, the reaction mixture contained 5 μL SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), 1 μL of cDNA template, 1.8 μL of specific primers (0.4 μL forward and 0.4 μL reverse), and 3.2 μL of nuclease-free water. To validate the constitutive gene and obtain the most reliable results, two genes, beta-2-microglobulin (BMG) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), were used. The PCR reactions were carried out using a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). Primers were designed with BLAST PRIMER and synthesized by the Laboratory of DNA Sequencing and Oligonucleotide Synthesis, Institute of Biochemistry and Biophysics, Polish Academy of Sciences, Warsaw, Poland. The primers used had the following sequences: BMG F 5’-AATGCGGCATCTTCAAACCT-3’; R: 5’-TGACTTTGTCACAGCCCAAGA-3’; GAPDH F: 5’-GAGTCCACTGGCGTCTTCAC -3’, R: 5’-GGTTCACACCCATGACGAAC-3’, VDR F: 5’ GACCTCACAGAAGAGCACCC 3’, R: 5’-CGTTCCGGTCAAAGTCTCCA-3’.

Bioinformatic analysis

Sequencing reads were demultiplexed using Illumina bcl2fastq (v2.20) to generate FASTQ files. Quality control was performed with FastQC, and Cutadapt (32) was used to trim adapters and low-quality reads. Processed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using STAR (v2.5.2b) (33) with the corresponding GTF (34) file from Ensembl. Gene-level counts were obtained using featureCounts (35) and imported into R for TPM normalization. Genes with low median expression were excluded, and differentially expressed genes were defined as those with a fold change >2 or <−2 between study and control groups. To identify functional biological pathways associated with observed gene expression changes, enrichment analysis was performed using two complementary approaches: Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and pathfindR. GSEA was used to assess which gene sets are statistically enriched across the entire ranked list of genes, applying a significance threshold of padj. < 0.05. In contrast, pathfindR was employed to identify active subnetworks within the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network among statistically significant (padj. < 0.05) genes, enabling detection of biologically related gene clusters and their associated pathways. Combining these approaches provided both a global overview of potentially enriched pathways and detailed insights into gene interactions and modules relevant to the biological context under study.

Raw data were generated on Infinium EPICv2 bead arrays, and a corresponding CSV sample sheet was prepared to map each sample to its respective group. All IDAT files and the sample sheet were placed in a single directory in preparation for downstream processing in R (v.4.4) with relevant libraries. Quality assessment and preliminary data handling were carried out using methods available in the minfi package (36, 37). First, the RGChannelSet was created by reading the IDAT files, and metadata were retrieved via phenotypic data extraction from the sample sheet. Background correction and normalization were then executed with the Noob procedure, which helps to correct potential technical artifacts and variability in the fluorescence signals (38). The EPICv2 annotation was assigned to ensure accurate probe localization, and suboptimal probes were removed based on standard criteria to preserve data integrity. Once a MethylSet containing beta-values was obtained, additional QC filtering was performed with ChAMP, which enabled comprehensive analyses and facilitated the detection and removal of potential outliers or artifacts (39). Batch-associated variation was subsequently addressed via ComBat, which normalizes data across experimental batches (40). Beta-values were first converted to M-values, and batch correction was performed on M-values, as recommended for statistical analyses of DNA methylation data (41). The data were then reconverted to beta-values. Quality metrics were rechecked post-ComBat to confirm that normalization and adjustment did not introduce new biases. Differentially methylated positions (DMPs) were identified for each pairwise contrast, and an appropriate false discovery rate (FDR) threshold was set to control for multiple testing. A variety of visualization approaches were implemented, including Manhattan-like plots (with CpGs ranked by effect size), donut charts illustrating proportions of hyper- versus hypomethylated CpGs, and bar plots summarizing distributions. Annotations were conducted by referencing the EnsDb.Hsapiens.v75 database (42), enabling the identification of implicated genes. Lists of significantly hypermethylated and hypomethylated genes were subsequently generated for functional enrichment. Gene Ontology (GO) analyses were then conducted via the clusterProfiler package (43). Separate hypermethylated and hypomethylated gene lists were mapped to Entrez identifiers and subjected to over-representation analysis (ORA) for sets of hypermethylated and hypomethylated genes, and GSEA. For the GSEA, beta-based or M-based metrics were converted into a ranked score and ordered. Separate hypermethylated and hypomethylated gene lists were mapped to Entrez identifiers and subjected to GO over-representation analyses, addressing categories from Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component. Visualization functions (e.g., dot plots, emap plots) were accessed through enrichplot, with additional customization provided by ggplot2-related libraries (44).

To explore potential epigenetic regulation of transcriptional changes, an integrative analysis was performed by overlaying RNA-seq results with DNA methylation data obtained from the same biological samples. First, differential gene expression analysis was carried out using normalized RNA-seq data. Genes with a fold change >2 or <−2 and adjusted p < 0.05 were classified as significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The list of DEGs was then used to query the DNA methylation dataset generated using the Infinium MethylationEPIC v2.0 BeadChip array. For each DEG, the methylation status of associated CpG sites—particularly those located in gene promoter regions—was extracted. CpG probes corresponding to DEGs were evaluated for differential methylation using the limma package, with significance defined as adjusted p < 0.05 and |Δβ| > 0.2. The analysis aimed to identify genes that were both transcriptionally altered and showed evidence of DNA methylation changes. Particular attention was given to inverse relationships, such as promoter hypermethylation coinciding with downregulation of gene expression, or hypomethylation with upregulation, suggesting potential epigenetic regulation.

The overlap between DEGs and differentially methylated genes (DMGs) was visualized using heatmaps. These integrative results were further subjected to functional enrichment analysis to assess the biological relevance of epigenetically regulated transcriptional changes.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1. GraphPad Software, USA). For statistical analysis of Western blot and qRT-PCR results, comparisons between two independent cell lines were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test with Welch’s correction to account for unequal variances. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and statistical significance was set at p<0.05. For the analysis of cell viability, a two-way ANOVA with FDR correction followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. Adjusted p-values (p.adj<0.05) were considered statistically significant.

Results

Selecting the dose of vitamins

Assessment of vitamin cytotoxicity was performed using the AlamarBlue assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Vitamins were tested at four concentrations (10-7 M, 2.5×10-7 M, 10-6 M, and 2.5×10-6 M) and incubated with multiple myeloma cell lines (U266 and MM1S) as well as human fibroblast cells (line NHDF; ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) for 48 hours under both standard and bone marrow-like conditions. The obtained results were used to determine the appropriate vitamin concentrations for subsequent stages of the experiment. The main objective was to establish a dose that would not be toxic to fibroblast cells. For vitamin D, cytotoxicity toward fibroblasts was observed at a concentration of 2.5×10-6 M. Therefore, a lower concentration (10-6 M) was selected for further experiments, as it did not exhibit toxic effects on fibroblasts but still reduced cell viability of both multiple myeloma cell lines under both oxygen conditions. The selected concentration of vitamin K was also intended to be non-toxic to fibroblasts; however, no cytotoxicity toward fibroblasts was observed at any of the tested doses. Nevertheless, concentrations of 10-6 M and 2.5×10-6 M were found to be highly cytotoxic to myeloma cells, resulting in 70–80% cell death. Therefore, the concentration of 2.5×10-7 M was chosen, which induced approximately 55% cell death in the MM1S line. A higher level of cell mortality in the tested samples could significantly interfere with data interpretation, leading to misrepresentation of the actual effects of vitamin K. Excessive apoptosis or necrosis may induce secondary alterations in the cellular transcriptome and proteome that are not directly related to the primary mechanism of vitamin K action. Such effects could obscure key signaling or metabolic pathways through which vitamin K exerts its cellular functions, such as the regulation of γ-carboxylation–dependent gene expression or modulation of oxidative stress. Consequently, the observed outcomes might reflect the consequences of cell death rather than the specific biological activity of the vitamin itself.

An additional challenge in experimental design was the selection of appropriate vitamin concentrations under conditions involving two distinct multiple myeloma cell lines cultured both in normoxia and hypoxia. The diverse metabolic backgrounds and differential sensitivity of the cell lines to oxygen availability and nutrient composition suggested that the optimal vitamin concentrations might vary between conditions. However, our aim was to apply uniform concentrations across all experimental variants to enable direct comparison of the results and to evaluate the influence of oxygen conditions on cellular responses to vitamin supplementation. The need to maintain this consistency represented a significant constraint in the experimental design.

Analysis of vitamin D receptor expression in U266 and MM1S cells

This chapter presents the expression level of the vitamin D receptor (VDR) in the cell lines used in the experiment (U266 and MM1S). During bioinformatic analysis of mRNAseq and methylation arrays, substantial differences in the numbers of DEGs and DMPs identified between the cell lines were observed, as described in the respective sections of the Results. Therefore, the following steps were taken to clarify these differences: automated western blot analysis to visualize the semi-quantitative expression of the vitamin D receptor (VDR) and qRT-PCR to assess VDR gene expression were performed. The results clearly indicate differences in VDR levels between cell lines. Significantly less of this protein was observed in the U266 line, which would correlate with significantly less data obtained from large-scale analyses. These changes may represent an individual characteristic, but paradoxically, they allow for a broader perspective on the effects of vitamins, and vitamin D in particular. Comparison of the influence of atmospheric and hypoxic conditions on the obtained results of the analyses

Changes in cell viability

The analysis of cell viability showed noticeable differences between both myeloma cell lines depending on oxygen availability and the applied compounds.

For U266 cell line, under standard conditions, two-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of time (F (3,32)=983.2, p<0.0001) and a significant overall effect of treatment (F (3,32)=2.907, p=0.0497), whereas the interaction between time and treatment was not significant (F (9,32)=1.738, p=0.1208). Under standard conditions, a gradual increase in cell viability was observed over time in all groups of the U266 line. The control group showed the highest cell viability up to day 6. On the 8th day of culture, cell viability in the treatment groups approached the level observed in the control group; however, the differences were not statistically significant.

In bone marrow-like conditions, U266 cells demonstrated a gradual increase in cell viability over time, and overall cell viability values were significantly different from those observed under standard conditions. Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of time (F (3,32)=1317, p<0.0001), treatment (F (3,32)=27.68, p<0.0001), and a significant interaction between time and treatment (F (9,32)=6.853, p<0.0001). Post hoc Tukey analysis indicated that at early time points (days 3 and 6), the cell viability rate of K2MK-7-treated cells was slightly higher than the control, but these differences were not statistically significant. On day 8, the control group showed the highest cell viability, whereas treatment with K2MK-7 (p.adj=0.007); 25(OH)D (p.adj<0.0001) or in combination with 25(OH)D and K2MK-7 (p.adj<0.0001) resulted in a statistically significant decrease in cell viability compared with the control. The reductions in the Alamar Blue signal were 12%, 20%, and 27%, respectively.

For MM1S cell line, under standard conditions, two-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of time (F (3,31)=597.4, p<0.0001), a significant overall effect of treatment (F (3,31)=95.99, p<0.0001), as well as the interaction between time and treatment was significant (F (9,31)=13.43, p<0.0001). However, in the MM1S line, the cell viability pattern differed from that observed in U266 cells. Under standard conditions, MM1S cells showed a gradual increase in cell viability over time, reaching the highest values on day 8 (post hoc Tukey p.adj<0.0001). Throughout the experiment, the control group maintained the highest cell viability level, while on day 8 of cell culture, treatment with 25(OH)D (p.adj<0.0001), K2MK-7 (p.adj<0.0001), or their combination (p.adj<0.0001) led to a reduction in cell viability (sequentially by 35% and 28%), with the most pronounced decrease observed after combined treatment (44%). Under bone marrow-like conditions, MM1S cells also demonstrated a time-dependent increase in cell viability. Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of time (F (3,29)=110.8, p<0.0001), treatment (F (3,29)=13.52, p<0.0001), and a significant interaction between time and treatment (F (9,29)=3.966, p=0.0022). On days 3 and 6, cell viability in the treated groups was similar or slightly lower than that in, while on day 8 the control group again showed the highest cell viability level. Treatment with 25(OH)D (p.adj=0.0016), K2MK-7 (p.adj=0.044), and particularly their combination (p.adj<0.0001) resulted in reductions in the Alamar Blue signal of 34%, 25%, and 51%, respectively. The inhibitory effect was more pronounced than under standard conditions for the last combined treatment group.

In summary, the obtained results differ significantly between pressure and oxygen concentration conditions, with the observed trend indicating stronger effects of the same vitamin doses under bone marrow-like conditions. This is particularly noticeable when analyzing the U266 cell line, where no significant differences were observed after 8 days of cell culture under standard conditions compared to bone marrow-like conditions, where cell viability decreased by 27% after the combined use of both vitamins. Detailed cell viability rate data for each time interval and both cell lines are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Figure 4S).

Comparisons of the same treatments between different oxygen conditions revealed significant differences for 25(OH)D (p.adj=0.0047 hypoxia vs. atmosphere) and 25(OH)D+K2MK-7 (p.adj<0.0001 hypoxia vs. atmosphere), indicating a stronger effect of these compounds under bone marrow-like conditions, indicating that oxygen conditions strongly influenced the response, with the combined treatment 25(OH)D+K2MK-7 reducing cell viability by ~27% more relative to the control under hypoxia than under atmospheric oxygen. Statistical analysis of the MM1S cell line revealed a significant effect of oxygen conditions and applied treatments on the analyzed parameter. Two-way ANOVA showed no significant interaction between oxygen conditions and treatment (F (3,15)=0.7551, p=0.5364), indicating that the effect of the compounds did not depend on oxygen availability. However, significant main effects were observed for both oxygen conditions (F (1,15)=53.80, p<0.0001) and treatment (F (3,15)=33.63, p<0.0001). The mean predicted value under hypoxia was 487.5, compared to 685.6 under standard conditions, with a mean difference of −198.1 (95% CI: −255.7 to −140.6), indicating a significant reduction in cell viability under hypoxia. Post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparisons between the same treatments across different oxygen conditions showed significant differences for several groups, including control (p.adj=0.0068 hypoxia vs. atmosphere) and 25(OH)D+K2MK-7 (p.adj=0.0296 hypoxia vs. atmosphere), indicating that oxygen conditions strongly influenced the response, with the combined treatment 25(OH)D+K2MK-7 reducing cell viability by 7.3% more relative to the control under hypoxia than under atmospheric oxygen.

DNA methylation changes

Comprehensive bioinformatics analysis demonstrated that oxygen availability significantly affects DNA methylation profiles in myeloma cell lines (U266 and MM1S). Differential methylation level was determined using the following thresholds: p.adj<0.05, with a beta delta value>0.2, where 1 means full methylation of the gene and 0 means no methylation of the gene. Preliminary analyses comparing control cells cultured under bone marrow-likeand standard conditions revealed notable differences in DNA methylation. In the untreated MM1S cell line under bone marrow-like conditions vs. standard conditions, 103 genes were hypomethylated and 20 genes hypermethylated. In contrast, the U266 cell line showed fewer methylation changes, with only 1 gene hypomethylated and 45 genes hypermethylated. In MM1S cells exposed to hypoxia and treated with vitamins D and K, 4 hypomethylated and 22 hypermethylated genes were identified compared to control cells. These changes occurred predominantly within promoter regions (TSS200 and TSS1500). By contrast, under normoxic conditions, vitamin treatment resulted in markedly different outcomes: 8404 hypermethylated and 306 hypomethylated sites relative to control. In this setting, hypermethylation affected more than 17% of TSS200 and TSS1500 regions, while hypomethylation accounted for nearly 30%. A similar trend was observed in the U266 cell line. Under hypoxia, treatment with vitamins D and K resulted in lack of hypomethylation changes and 83 hypermethylated genes compared to control. These alterations were also enriched in promoter regions, with more than 12% of hypermethylated sites mapping to TSS200 and TSS1500. In contrast, analysis under normoxic conditions revealed 5151 hypermethylated and 118 hypomethylated sites following vitamin treatment relative to control. Among these, hypermethylation was observed in nearly 15% of TSS200 and TSS1500 regions, while hypomethylation was detected in over 30%. Obtaining such extreme results led to the identification of meaningful differences depending on oxygen content and pressure, which significantly influenced the obtained functional results. Heatmaps present genes with the highest variability identified in cells cultured under hypoxia, along with their corresponding beta values obtained for the same genes under normoxic conditions (p.adj<0.05) (Supplementary Figure 1). Following vitamin D incubation, only a few dozen genes exhibited methylation changes under hypoxia, whereas several thousand genes showed significant alterations under normoxia. Consequently, the majority of genes altered under normoxic conditions do not display any changes under hypoxia. Therefore, generating heatmaps based on the highest-variability genes identified in normoxia would not be meaningful, as these genes do not undergo significant changes in hypoxia. Moreover, for the small subset of genes that did exhibit changes in hypoxia, the beta values under normoxia differ by at least 20%. This indicates that even the few genes altered in hypoxia have substantially different methylation profiles compared to normoxia, which may significantly impact data interpretation and downstream functional analyses. In summary, bone marrow-like conditions result in a markedly reduced number of methylation changes compared to normoxia, and the differences in beta values for the altered genes highlight the importance of considering oxygen conditions when interpreting results and conducting functional analyses.

Global transcriptome analysis

Bioinformatics data analysis revealed significant differences in gene expression depending on the oxygen conditions used for culturing myeloma cell lines (U266 and MM1S). For all subsequent analyses, the following criteria were applied to qualify gene expression changes: p.adj<0.05; fold change>2 for upregulated genes; and fold change<-2 for downregulated genes. Initial comparisons between control cells cultured under bone marrow-like and standard conditions revealed marked transcriptional differences. In the MM1S cell line, 235 genes were downregulated and 99 were upregulated. In contrast, the U266 cell line exhibited fewer differentially expressed genes, with 135 upregulated and only 10 downregulated.

Notably, the changes induced by vitamin treatment varied significantly depending on oxygen concentration and pressure. When comparing MM1S cells treated with vitamins D and K under bone marrow-like conditions vs. control, 2177 genes were found to be upregulated and 2264 downregulated. In the U266 line, only 4 genes were down- and 12 upregulated. Contrary results, although the differences were not as large as those observed for DNA methylation, were observed after incubation of myeloma cells with vitamins under standard conditions. The MMS1 line showed 2422 downregulated and 2153 upregulated genes, while the U266 line showed 253 downregulated and 191 upregulated genes. In the case of the MM1S cell line, the largest differences in gene expression levels comparing hypoxia vs. normoxia were observed for the PFKFB4, CA9, MIR210HG, and TNS1 genes (padj <0.05, fold change log2FC>2). For comparison, in the case of the U266 line, the highest expression variability was identified for the HK2 (log2FC=0.73, padj=0.0020) and DDIT3 (log2FC=−0.81; padj=0.0047) genes. Table (Supplementary Figure 2) with selected genes with the highest variability and heatmaps for the most important processes (A) cell development; B) cellular response to endogenous stimulus; C) regulation of apoptotic process; D) regulation of cell population proliferation) (Supplementary Figure 3) are presented in the supplementary.

Overall, the results indicate significant differences depending on oxygen levels and pressure. Therefore, to optimize experimental procedures and obtain more reliable data, it is important to consider conducting studies under conditions as close to physiological as possible. This is particularly important for cancer cells, as their in vivo development environment differs significantly from standard in vitro culture conditions.

Functional analysis of the effect of vitamins on DNA methylation and global gene expression

In this study, our main objective was to determine the effects of vitamins D and K under conditions that reflect the in vivo microenvironment of multiple myeloma. Therefore, detailed analyses of gene expression, DNA methylation, and functional enrichment were performed and described for physiological oxygen conditions (hypoxia). Results obtained under normoxia were used as a reference for data interpretation and to highlight the importance of employing physiologically conditions in experimental settings. For clarity, part of the functional analyses from normoxia are presented in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Figure 1, S2, S3). This approach allows the narrative to focus on the most biologically relevant findings while ensuring the completeness of the analysis.

Integrative analysis

An integrative analysis comparing methylation and transcription profiles revealed only limited changes following incubation with vitamins under bone marrow-like conditions. The objective of the analysis was to identify statistically significant alterations, based on the assumption that hypermethylation suppresses gene expression, whereas hypomethylation enhances it (Figure 4). The analysis criteria were as follows: fold change expression>2; beta value>0.2, padj<0.05. Within this framework, only two genes, MCOLN3 and CLSTN2, exhibited a concurrent increase in methylation and decreased their expression. Notably, hypermethylation of the MCOLN3 gene was detected in the promoter region (TSS1500), which increases the likelihood of a direct impact of this modification on its expression levels. The MCOLN3 gene encodes the protein mucolipin-3, which functions as an ion channel primarily located in lysosomes and endosomes. It is involved in endosomal transport and autophagy, facilitating the fusion of endosomes with lysosomes and the degradation of cellular components. Dysregulation of its function may lead to disturbances in calcium signaling and degradative processes, thereby promoting tumorigenic transformation. Through the modulation of autophagy and interaction with key pathways such as mTOR, MCOLN3 may support the adaptation of cancer cells to conditions of metabolic stress and hypoxia, enhancing their survival and tumor progression. Increasing evidence also suggests that MCOLN3 may play an important role in the pathogenesis of multiple myeloma. Studies have shown that its expression is significantly elevated in patients with both newly diagnosed and relapsed or refractory myeloma, compared with healthy individuals and those in remission. In contrast, the integrative analysis performed after incubation of myeloma cells with vitamins under normoxic conditions revealed a markedly higher number of correlations using the same analytical criteria. Specifically, for the MM1S cell line, 312 associations were identified, of which 148 were located in promoter regions (TSS200 and TSS1500) as well as in the 5′UTR. For the U266 cell line, 26 correlations were identified, including 10 in promoter regions and the 5′UTR.

Our study revealed clear differences between standard and bone marrow-like conditions at multiple levels, including DNA methylation, gene expression, enrichment analyses, and integrative comparisons, even though the vitamin dose and incubation time were the same. Under normoxia, more correlations and changes in promoter regions were observed, while hypoxia resulted in fewer detectable alterations. These results suggest that oxygen levels strongly influence cellular responses, affecting transcriptional and epigenetic regulation as well as pathway activity. This emphasizes the importance of maintaining culture conditions that closely resemble the physiological environment to ensure that in vitro findings are reliable and reflective of in vivo biology.

Discussion

Multiple myeloma still remains incurable yet due to modern treatment strategies the prognosis and clinical outcomes have significantly improved. Nonetheless, despite the recent progress, there is room for improvement and further preclinical and clinical research. The aim of this study was to investigate the effect of VD and VK upon MM cells under standard and bone marrow-like conditions to unveil molecular and epigenetic aspects associated with MM cells cell viability and survival.

Bone marrow microenvironment is crucial for understanding the biology of MM, mechanisms of myelomagenesis as well as disease progression, relapse and development resistance to anti-MM treatment (137). Although there were multiple preclinical studies being conducted, many were conducted under atmospheric oxygen pressure and failed to recognize the hypoxic environment which is a hallmark of MM niche (138). In this study, we demonstrated that gene expression, methylation profile, as well as proliferative potential of MM cells differed between standard and bone marrow-like conditions. This finding is crucial for the design of future in-vitro MM studies. Having considered that hypoxia better represents MM microenvironment and affects epigenetic alterations, gene expression and proliferative potential, results obtained in standard oxygen conditions may be inaccurate. Therefore, due to the abovementioned rationale, in in-vitro MM studies bone marrow-like conditions should be preferred, and perhaps in the future, constitute a new standard in study design and methodology in MM research.

VD is not a classical vitamin. From the standpoint of its molecular mechanism of action, it should be rather considered as a prohormone synthesized in the skin upon exposure to ultraviolet light in addition to dietary intake (139). Activation of VD requires two hydroxylations. The first, in which 25(OH)D3 is synthesized, occurs in the liver. Subsequently, 25(OH)D3 (calcidiol) is subjected to the second hydroxylation in the kidneys, mainly in the proximal tubule. The final product of these reactions is 1,25(OH)2D3 (calcitriol), being the most biologically active form of VD (139). The classical role of VD is the regulation of calcium and phosphate homeostasis, and it is therefore crucial for bone metabolism and health.

The role of VD in calcium metabolism is very well-established. Nonetheless, so-called non-classical actions, i.e., not directly related to the regulation of calcium-phosphate metabolism and bone health, are the subject of intensive ongoing research. In the first place, one should consider that VDR is expressed in most human cells (140). Therefore, it may be assumed that VD influences the metabolism and function of various cell types via regulating gene expression, exhibiting numerous pleiotropic effects. Besides genomic action mediated by VDR, VD also exhibits non-genomic effects, believed to be mediated by membrane receptors (141, 142). Non-genomic actions are predominantly associated with activating various signaling molecules, the subsequent generation of second messengers, and the opening of ion channels (143). To highlight the rationale for investigating VD in MM, it should be mentioned that VDR is also expressed on MM cells. In the present study, the expression of VDR in U266 and MM1S cells was confirmed at both the mRNA and protein levels using qRT-PCR and automated Western blot analysis, respectively (Figure 1). Differences in VDR expression levels and the activity of VDR-dependent signaling pathways between MM cell lines may partially contribute to their distinct responses to combined vitamin D3 and vitamin K2 treatment. In addition, the divergent transcriptomic and DNA methylation profiles observed between U266 and MM1S cells may reflect cell-line-specific differences in baseline epigenetic states, chromatin accessibility, and other regulatory mechanisms affecting gene expression.

In the first step of our experiment, U266 and MM1 cell lines were cultured with VD under standard and bone marrow-like conditions. In these two very different environments, it was observed that VD affects the cell viability potential of MM cells. More precisely, VD decreases the cell viability rate of MM cells both in standard and bone marrow-like conditions. Although differences in proliferative potential, methylation profiles, and gene expression patterns were observed between the U266 and MM1S cell lines, these differences may reflect cell-line-specific molecular characteristics, potentially including differences in genetic and epigenetic backgrounds. A deeper analysis of methylation and gene expression profile revealed the alterations in genes associated with cancer development and progression, not only in MM but also in solid tumors. Table 1 presents the methylation profiles of selected genes, revealing similar patterns between both (U266 and MM1S) investigated cell lines. The identified genes are highly associated with cancer development. Previously documented evidence regarding their involvement in multiple myeloma primarily applies to the ATM (57, 58), FOXO1 (55, 56), TOX (67, 68), LPAR3 (69–71) genes. Nevertheless, the other genes analyzed such as BCL11A (75–77), MECOM (72–74), BCL2L2 (65, 66), ARHGEF10 (54), FOXN3 (51–53) may also play important roles, although their significance in multiple myeloma has not yet been established. In other malignancies, these genes have been associated with disease progression and metastasis, suggesting their potential functional relevance. Nonetheless, their functional importance in MM requires additional studies. Interestingly, genes commonly recognized as oncogenes (including TOX (67, 68), LPAR3, BCL2L2 (65, 66), MECOM (72–74), and BCL11A (75–77) were hypermethylated in the aftermath of vitamin treatment.

Another important finding was the overrepresentation of the IL-17 signaling pathway, after vitamin treatment which, although showing a high fold enrichment (16.83), was observed only in the U266 cell line. From the perspective of MM, IL-17 has been reported to act as a potential growth-promoting factor for MM cells. Moreover, this pathway may influence the bone marrow microenvironment, potentially supporting proliferation and survival of malignant cells, contributing to promote disease progression, and modulating the inflammatory response (144). Having considered the anti-MM activity of VD, the overexpression of IL-17 related signaling might represent a potential cellular response to the effects of vitamin treatment. Similar observation was made in MM patients achieving deep and long-lasting response to lenalidomide (145) in whom increased activity of the IL-17 axis was proposed as a potential mechanism that might enhance IL-17-related signaling in MM cells and potentially counteract the anti-MM effects of lenalidomide. Although in the context of this study the role of IL-17 signaling remains unclear, this observation may provide further support for investigating the potential role of IL-17 signaling in MM.

The above observations from this experiment alongside other in vitro research (146), including these conducted by our team (9, 10, 20), and human studies (147–150) strongly suggests the rationale for restoring the VD status in MM patients to boost the immune system, enhance the efficacy of anti-MM treatment regimen and improve overall prognosis.

Conclusions

The conducted studies demonstrated both the effects of vitamins D and K on myeloma cells and the importance of maintaining oxygen conditions during cell culture and throughout the experiment to better reflect their natural microenvironment.

Hypoxia within the tumor microenvironment affects numerous biological processes, including enhancing cellular stress resistance, promoting angiogenesis, shifting metabolism toward anaerobic glycolysis, and influencing gene expression and epigenetic modifications. The results of our in vitro analyses suggest that the limited number of changes observed under bone marrow-like conditions may partially reflect adaptive cellular responses to low oxygen levels, including metabolic, transcriptional, and epigenetic changes, which may influence the response of cancer cells to treatment. This emphasizes the importance of conducting experiments under conditions that closely mimic physiological environments to ensure that the results are biologically relevant and more accurately reflect the actual behavior of cancer cells.

Focusing on the results obtained after incubation with vitamins under bone marrow-like conditions, a number of genes, such as ABCA1, ADAMTS9, RUNX1T1, showed pronounced expression changes specifically in the MM1S cell line. These findings should therefore be considered cell-line-specific and may represent potential candidates for further investigation rather than generalizable mechanisms of vitamin action in multiple myeloma. Notably, one of the most intriguing findings is the observed association between hypermethylation of the MCOLN3 gene and a reduction in its expression. This may suggest a potential pathway associated with the antitumor effects of vitamins D and K but may also provide a potential direction for future gene-targeted therapies.

Study limitations

We acknowledge that this study has certain limitations. An additional limitation of the omics component is that transcriptomic and methylation analyses were performed only for the combined 25(OH)D+K2MK-7 treatment group and the corresponding control group. Although the effects of each vitamin individually were assessed in the cell viability experiments, the individual transcriptomic and epigenetic contributions of 25(OH)D and K2MK-7 could not be distinguished in the omics analyses. The experimental design did not include a wide range of vitamin concentrations or incubation intervals, which could have better revealed the potential effects of the vitamins, particularly under bone marrow-like conditions. Another limitation is that VDR expression was not assessed under bone marrow-like conditions. Therefore, we cannot determine whether the different cellular responses observed under these conditions, particularly the weaker transcriptional response in U266 cells, are associated with changes in VDR expression. Further studies assessing VDR expression under bone marrow-like conditions are warranted to clarify this potential mechanism. Furthermore, the analysis was limited to two cell lines, and expanding the study to include myeloma lines with diverse genetic backgrounds would allow for a more comprehensive understanding of the observed effects. In particular, the markedly reduced VDR expression observed in the U266 cell line represents a cell-line-specific finding that may contribute to the distinct responses observed between MM cell lines. This observation further highlights the genetic and epigenetic heterogeneity among MM cells and reinforces the need to use multiple myeloma cell lines with diverse molecular backgrounds in future in vitro studies. As an in vitro cell-line-based study, the translational relevance of the findings remains to be validated in primary patient-derived samples and in vivo models. Future research should address these aspects to confirm and expand upon our findings. Nonetheless, the main goal of the study—to demonstrate differences arising from varying oxygen concentrations and pressures—was achieved. Significant differences were observed, clearly confirming the importance of replicating a tissue’s natural microenvironment in in vitro research.
