The MdERF1B-miR482c-MdCCR4-MdTLP1b module regulates apple resistance to Colletotrichum gloeosporioides
Authors: Baodong Zhang, Jialin Cong, Zongshan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlomerella leaf spot (GLS), incited by Colletotrichum gloeosporioides, poses a significant threat to apple (Malus domestica) production. microRNAs (miRNAs) are critical regulators of plant–pathogen crosstalk. However, the underlying mechanism through which miR482 regulates apple resistance to GLS remains unclear. Here, we show that overexpression of miR482c compromises resistance against C. gloeosporioides, while silencing miR482c confers resistance to C. gloeosporioides. 5′RACE assays in apple and co-expression assays in Nicotiana benthamiana confirmed that miR482c targets and cleaves the MdCCR4 mRNA. Subsequent research revealed that overexpression of MdCCR4 significantly enhances apple resistance to C. gloeosporioides. Notably, MdCCR4 physically interacts with the thaumatin-like protein MdTLP1b, which also positively regulates resistance to C. gloeosporioides. Additionally, MdERF1B was identified as an upstream regulator of miR482c through bioinformatics and Y1H screening, and was further confirmed to negatively regulate miR482c transcription by directly binding to its promoter. Meanwhile, MdERF1B overexpression enhances resistance to C. gloeosporioides, while silencing MdERF1B compromises resistance to C. gloeosporioides. Collectively, our findings indicate that the MdERF1B-miR482c-MdCCR4-MdTLP1b regulatory network is pivotal for apple defense against C. gloeosporioides, highlighting promising candidates for the genetic improvement of disease resistance.

Introduction

Apple is a globally vital deciduous fruit crop, but its cultivation is frequently plagued by fungal diseases such as Alternaria leaf spot, Valsa canker, ring rot, and Glomerella leaf spot (GLS) [1–3].

GLS, incited by Colletotrichum gloeosporioides, stands out as a major threat, with outbreaks exacerbated by high temperature and humidity [4, 5]. Currently, control of GLS depends heavily on chemical treatments, raising concerns about environmental safety and public health. Thus, breeding resistant cultivars represents a more sustainable strategy. However, developing such cultivars requires a deep understanding of the molecular interplay between apple and C. gloeosporioides.

Noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) serve as critical regulatory molecules that facilitate plant adaptation to fluctuating environmental stress [6]. In particular, microRNAs (miRNAs) fine-tune plant immunity by dampening the expression of defense-associated genes at the post-transcriptional level [7]. miR482, an miRNA closely associated with disease resistance, primarily participates in the interaction between plants and pathogens by negatively regulating NLR genes [8]. In potato, overexpression (OE) of miR482e triggers secondary tasiRNAs by targeting NBS-LRR, promotes sensitivity to Verticillium dahliae [9]. Similarly, in tomato, OE of miR482c suppresses NBS-LRR target genes and antioxidant enzyme activities, leading to reduced ROS scavenging capacity and exacerbated membrane lipid peroxidation, which ultimately enhances plant susceptibility to Phytophthora infestans [10]. Furthermore, multiregulatory analysis shows that poplar miR482a triggers phasiRNAs by targeting PHA7, thereby reducing ROS accumulation and enhancing susceptibility to the canker pathogen Lonsdalea populi [11]. Similarly, in apple, miR482b promotes susceptibility to Alternaria alternata f. sp. mali by targeting MdTNL1 and initiating phasiRNA biogenesis to further repress immune responses [12]. Despite the well-documented negative regulatory role of the miR482 family across various species, the distinct contribution and underlying mechanism of miR482c in response to C. gloeosporioides represent a knowledge gap.

Plants orchestrate a robust immune response against pathogens by harnessing the induced upregulation of numerous defense-related genes [13]. Notably, pathogenesis-related (PR) genes constitute a major part of these genes, a group closely associated with systemic acquired resistance [14]. So far, researchers have identified 17 distinct PR gene families, such as thaumatin-like proteins [15]. Thaumatin-like proteins (TLPs), which constitute the PR5 family among the 17 identified PR proteins, combat fungal pathogens by suppressing both mycelial development and spore germination [16]. Heterologous expression of the rice Ostlp gene conferred resistance to cassava against infection by C. gloeosporioides f. sp. manihotis [17]. In wheat, by activating defense-associated enzymes and reactive oxygen species pathways, the OE of TaTLP1 in wheat confers significant resistance against Bipolaris sorokiniana and Puccinia triticina [18]. In potato, OE of CsTLP significantly improved tolerance to Macrophomina phaseolina and P. infestans infection by activating intrinsic defense pathways involving the upregulation of StPAL and StLOX genes [19]. Notably, PR5 family proteins frequently serve as targets for pathogen-secreted effectors to modulate host immunity. Specifically, the interaction of PevD1 (a V. dahliae effector) with GhPR5 impairs antifungal activity in cotton [20]. Similarly, PR5 osmotin binds to BcIEB1 to modulate plant immunity against Botrytis cinerea [21]. In parallel with these findings, the effector BEC1054 from Blumeria graminis has also been reported to interact with PR5 proteins [22], further illustrating the prevalence of this pathogen strategy. Extensive studies have highlighted the significance of TLPs in fungal defense and their manipulation by effectors. However, the specific role of TLP1b in the resistance of apple (Malus domestica) against C. gloeosporioides remains largely unexplored.

Transcription factors (TFs) orchestrate complex biological networks by precisely modulating gene expression [23]. Among them, the ethylene response factor (ERF) family stands out as a critical class of transcription factors that exerts a pivotal function in regulating plant immunity and defense responses against pathogens [24]. Mechanistically, ERFs counteract pathogen infection through regulating the expression of PR genes as well as those involved in the biosynthesis of physical barriers and secondary metabolites [25]. For instance, OE of MdERF11 boosts the transcription of salicylic acid-related genes (EDS1, PAD4) and PR genes (PR1, PR5), thereby enhancing resistance to apple ring rot [26]. Likewise, by regulating hormone signaling (salicylic acid and jasmonic acid), ectopic expression of MdERF100 in Arabidopsis significantly improves defense against powdery mildew [27]. Furthermore, MdERF114 strengthens resistance against F. solani by promoting physical barrier fortification via PRX63-mediated lignin deposition [28]. Thus, uncovering the precise mechanisms of specific ERF members during the apple–C. gloeosporioides interaction is a crucial research priority.

Our previous studies have demonstrated that miR482c is induced by C. gloeosporioides. In this study, we identified a novel regulatory module, MdERF1B-miR482c-MdCCR4-MdTLP1b, that orchestrates apple resistance to C. gloeosporioides. Our findings reveal that miR482c compromises resistance against GLS by post-transcriptionally silencing the protein kinase gene MdCCR4. Notably, MdCCR4 was found to physically interact with the thaumatin-like protein MdTLP1b, and both proteins positively regulate the immune to C. gloeosporioides. In addition, we show that the nucleus-localized transcription factor MdERF1B positively regulates defense against C. gloeosporioides and represses miR482c transcription by binding its promoter. Collectively, our findings elucidate the hierarchical regulation of the MdERF1B-miR482c-MdCCR4-MdTLP1b cascade, thus deepening our understanding of the apple–pathogen pathosystem and facilitating the genetic improvement of GLS resistance in breeding.

Results

miR482c directly cleaves protein kinase MdCCR4 mRNA

As a deeply conserved lineage, miR482 is known to exist in 26 plant species, including tomato, grape, and apple (Fig. S1). miR482c was upregulated in response to C. gloeosporioides in the susceptible varieties ‘Royal Gala’ (‘GL-3’) through sRNA-seq in our previous study [29]. To understand the characteristics of the apple miR482 family, all mature sequences of apple miR482 from the miRBase database were downloaded and subjected to multiple sequence alignment, with the results shown in Fig. S2A. The alignment results showed significant sequence divergence among members of the apple miR482 family, while miR482b and miR482c exhibited high conservation. To investigate the genomic context of miR482c, we aligned its precursor sequence against the apple reference genome. Genomic mapping anchored the miR482c precursor to the 2 405 018–2 405 158 region of chromosome 13. Subsequent analysis of RNA secondary structure confirmed its ability to form a canonical stem-loop, exhibiting a minimal free energy of −52.9 kcal/mol (Fig. S2C). Following the retrieval of the miR482c promoter from the GDR database, putative cis-elements were predicted using the PlantCARE website. The presence of specific binding sites implies that miR482c expression is subject to regulation by phytohormones like abscisic acid, gibberellin, and salicylic acid, as well as general defense pathways (Fig. S2D).

We employed the psRNATarget online tool (https://www.zhaolab.org/psRNATarget/) applying standard settings to screen for candidate targets of miR482c. Serine/threonine protein kinase MdCCR4 are predicted to be target genes of miR482c. To verify this prediction, 5′ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) assay was performed using total RNA extracted from apple leaves through HiScript-TS 5′/3′ RACE Kit. It was found that the 619–620 base site of MdCCR4 can be cleaved by miR482c, suggesting that miR482c directly cleaves protein kinase MdCCR4 mRNA. To further verify whether miR482c could interact with MdCCR4, MdCCR4 was co-injected with 35S:miR482c into N. benthamiana. 35S:GUS was utilized as the positive control, while 35S:miR482c acted as the negative control (Fig. 1B). Histochemical GUS staining results exhibited no visible color in N. benthamiana expressing only 35S:miR482c. In contrast, leaves transformed with 35S:GUS or 35S:MdCCR4-GUS alone displayed intense blue staining. After co-injecting and expressing 35S:miR482c and 35S:MdCCR4-GUS in N. benthamiana, the leaves of tobacco plants co-expressing the genes showed significantly lighter pigmentation compared to those expressing only 35S:GUS or 35S:MdCCR4-GUS (Fig. 1C). Quantitative GUS fluorescence activity was detected in these plants, supporting the results of GUS histochemical staining (Fig. 1D). In conclusion, these results confirm that miR482c can target and cleave MdCCR4 transcripts through complementary base pairing.

To verify whether miR482c and MdCCR4 respond to C. gloeosporioides infection, their expression profiles were monitored in ‘GL-3’ and ‘FJ’ leaves. RT-quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays revealed a marked induction of both pre-miR482c and mature miR482c at 24 and 72 HPI in the leaves of ‘GL-3’ (Figs 1E and S3A), while MdCCR4 expression levels were markedly downregulated (Fig. 1E). In contrast, the ‘FJ’ cultivar exhibited a significant downregulation of pre-miR482c and miR482c (Figs 1F and S3B), which coincided with a robust upregulation of MdCCR4 transcripts at 24 and 72 HPI (Fig. 1F).

miR482c negatively regulates resistance to GLS in apples

Given that C. gloeosporioides infection triggers miR482c accumulation, we proposed that this miRNA is integral to the resistance response in apple. To verify this hypothesis, vectors for miR482c OE and silencing were constructed, as shown in Fig. S4. Transient transformation of apple leaves was performed with these vectors using Agrobacterium-mediated method, with OE-miR482c overexpressed in ‘FJ’ leaves and STTM-miR482c introduced into ‘GL-3’ leaves. At 4 days postinfiltration, the transcript abundance of miR482c was quantified via RT-qPCR. RT-qPCR analysis revealed a nearly 19-fold elevation of miR482c expression levels in OE-miR482c plants (Fig. 2A) and a 69% reduction in STTM-miR482c plants (Fig. 2D), indicating the successful generation of transient miR482c OE and silenced leaves.

Subsequently, C. gloeosporioides (with a concentration of approximately 1 × 106 conidia·ml−1) inoculation was carried out on ‘FJ’ leaves, as well as those expressing OE-miR482c or the empty vector (EV), to assess the function of miR482. The changes in lesions were observed 3 days after inoculation, the typical symptoms are shown in Fig. 2B. Exacerbated disease phenotypes were observed in miR482c-overexpressing plants, accompanied by a marked elevation in the disease index. Meanwhile, RT-qPCR detection revealed that the biomass of miR482c-overexpressing plants also significantly increased compared to EV plants (Fig. 2C). We continued to analyze the phenotypic changes in STTM-miR482c plants after inoculation with C. gloeosporioides. Compared to EV and ‘GL-3’ plants, STTM-miR482c plants exhibited milder disease symptoms and a smaller disease index at 3 d after infection by C. gloeosporioides (Fig. 2E). A significant decrease in biomass was detected in STTM-miR482c leaves compared to EV leaves using RT-qPCR (Fig. 2F). In addition, because the ‘GL-3’ is exclusively maintained as tissue-cultured plantlets in our facility, we further injected the OE-miR482c and STTM-miR482c vectors into ‘FJ’ fruits and inoculated them with C. gloeosporioides. The size of fruit necrotic lesions was observed 3 days after inoculation. As shown in Fig. 2G, fruits overexpressing miR482c exhibited longer lesions, whereas fruits with silenced miR482c showed smaller lesions. Collectively, these findings suggest that miR482c attenuates defense against C. gloeosporioides.

According to previous research reports [8], miR482 typically targets and cleaves NLR genes in response to pathogen infection. In this study, we investigated the transcript abundance of PR genes, given that NLR receptors facilitate their regulation by activating transcription factors like WRKY [30, 31]. Particularly, the transcript abundance of MdPR1, MdPR5, and MdPR10 was quantified in OE-miR482c and STTM-miR482c plants via RT-qPCR. As Fig. S5 shown, expression levels of MdPR1, MdPR5, and MdPR10 in OE-miR482c plants were apparently reduced, while they exhibited an opposite expression trend in STTM-miR482c plants. Taken together, our findings establish that the OE of miR482c downregulates MdPR1, MdPR5, and MdPR10, consequently facilitating C. gloeosporioides infection.

MdCCR4 positively regulates tolerance of apple to C. gloeosporioides

To further understand how MdCCR4 contributes to apple defense against C. gloeosporioides, we cloned the complete coding sequence (CDS) of MdCCR4 into the pRI101-GFP plasmid (Fig. S6A). Subsequently, the plasmid transiently expressed in ‘GL-3’ through Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration, with WT and EV plants serving as controls. RT-qPCR analysis verified robust MdCCR4 expression 4 days after transformation (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, the successful translation and accumulation of the MdCCR4 protein in the transiently transformed leaves were confirmed by western blot analysis using an anti-GFP antibody (Fig. S7). Following inoculation with C. gloeosporioides, the OE-MdCCR4 plants displayed enhanced resistance, characterized by smaller lesion areas and a reduced disease index compared to the severe browning observed in the control plants (‘GL-3’ and EV) (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, analysis of fungal growth revealed a marked reduction in the biomass of C. gloeosporioides in the OE-MdCCR4 plants compared to controls (Fig. 3C).

To investigate the loss-of-function phenotype, we silenced endogenous MdCCR4 using an RNA interference (RNAi) strategy (Fig. S6B). We performed Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration to deliver the RNAi-MdCCR4 construct into ‘FJ’ leaves. Silencing efficiency of MdCCR4 in the transiently transformed leaves was confirmed by RT-qPCR (Fig. 3D). To determine how the downregulation of MdCCR4 influences disease resistance, we inoculated these leaves with C. gloeosporioides. In sharp contrast to the OE-MdCCR4 plants, RNAi-MdCCR4 leaves displayed rapidly expanding brown spots and a significantly higher disease index compared to ‘FJ’ and EV leaves (Fig. 3E). Consistent with these severe symptoms, fungal biomass increased meaningfully in RNAi-MdCCR4 leaves (Fig. 3F). Taken together, these data indicate that MdCCR4 positively regulates apple resistance to C. gloeosporioides.

MdCCR4 physically interacts with MdTLP1b

Having established that MdCCR4 promotes apple defense, we next investigated its downstream mechanisms. Sequence annotation identified MdCCR4 as a putative serine/threonine protein kinase (Fig. 1A). Subcellular localization assays using MdCCR4-GFP in N. benthamiana revealed specific signal accumulation at both the plasma membrane and the nucleus (Fig. S8). To identify interacting partners within these compartments, we utilized an AI-driven virtual screening approach as a targeted alternative to conventional library screening. Through Hdock screening and further AlphaFold3 prediction screening, we selected the MD08G1011900 protein of interest from the top 10 scoring proteins as our research subject (Table S1). To determine the gene sequence, PCR amplification was performed using apple cDNA as the template to obtain the TLP1b (LOC103410424) gene. Sequence analysis revealed that MdTLP1b possesses a coding region of 966 bp, corresponding to 321 amino acid residues.

To verify interplay between MdCCR4 and MdTLP1b, A Y2H assay was implemented. MdCCR4 was inserted into pGBKT7 (pGBKT7-MdCCR4) and MdTLP1b was inserted into pGADT7 vector (pGADT7-MdTLP1b), respectively. Next, plasmids were simultaneously introduced into Y2HGold yeast cells. Protein–protein interplay in Y2H cell was assessed by monitoring cell viability and chromogenic reactions. Yeast strains co-expressing pGBKT7-MdCCR4 and pGADT7-MdTLP1b exhibited normal growth on the selective medium (SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade). Furthermore, these colonies developed a blue color in the presence of X-α-Gal (Fig. 4B). These data provide evidence for the physical binding of MdCCR4 to MdTLP1b.

To substantiate the physical association between MdCCR4 and MdTLP1b in vivo, we utilized both bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay. For BiFC, Nicotiana benthamiana leaves co-expressing MdCCR4 and MdTLP1b displayed strong nuclear YFP fluorescence. In contrast, the negative control groups failed to exhibit any fluorescent signal (Fig. 4C). This interaction was further corroborated by LCI assays. Strong luminescence was exclusively detected in leaf regions co-expressing nLUC-MdCCR4 and cLUC-MdTLP1b, whereas control areas remained nonluminescent. Subsequent quantification revealed markedly higher luciferase activity in samples co-expressing nLUC-MdCCR4 and cLUC-MdTLP1b compared to controls. These data collectively confirm that MdCCR4 physically interacts with MdTLP1b in plant cells.

We further substantiated this interaction in a pull-down approach. Recombinant MdTLP1b-HIS and MdCCR4-GST plasmids were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) following IPTG induction. Subsequently, MdTLP1b-HIS was mixed with MdCCR4-GST or a GST negative control, followed by capture on Ni-NTA beads. The assay revealed that MdTLP1b-HIS was specifically pulled down by MdCCR4-GST, but not by the GST control. In summary, our data from both cellular and cell-free systems confirm the direct interaction between MdCCR4 and MdTLP1b.

The overexpression of MdTLP1b enhances apple resistance to C. gloeosporioides

Considering the established physical interplay between MdCCR4 and MdTLP1b, we investigated whether MdTLP1b also contributes to apple immunity against C. gloeosporioides. We first monitored MdTLP1b transcript levels in resistant and susceptible varieties using RT-qPCR. Notably, C. gloeosporioides infection triggers a rapid accumulation of MdTLP1b mRNA specifically in the resistant ‘FJ’ cultivar (Fig. S9), implying MdTLP1b is crucial for disease resistance. To assess the validity of this prediction, we generated OE and RNAi silencing constructs targeting MdTLP1b. These vectors were transiently delivered into ‘GL-3’ and ‘FJ’ leaves, respectively, via Agrobacterium-mediated method. Subsequent RT-qPCR validation 4 days postinfiltration confirmed effective manipulation of gene expression: OE-MdTLP1b leaves displayed markedly elevated transcript levels relative to EV controls (Fig. 5A), whereas the RNAi-MdTLP1b samples showed substantially suppressed MdTLP1b transcript abundance (Fig. 5E). Consistently, western blot analysis further verified that the MdTLP1b fusion protein was successfully expressed in these OE-MdTLP1b leaves at the translational level (Fig. S10).

Next, inoculation with C. gloeosporioides was performed on the transiently transformed leaves to assess disease resistance. Overexpression of MdTLP1b significantly attenuated disease symptoms and decreased the disease index relative to controls (Fig. 5B and C). Conversely, silencing MdTLP1b in the resistant ‘FJ’ cultivar compromised its resistance, leading to more severe lesions and a higher disease index than in the ‘FJ’ and EV controls (Fig. 5F and G). Consistent with the phenotypic observations, the OE-MdTLP1b leaves exhibited significantly inhibited C. gloeosporioides growth, confirmed by a reduction in fungal biomass quantified via RT-qPCR (Fig. 5D) but promoted C. gloeosporioides growth in the RNAi-MdTLP1b leaves (Fig. 5H). Collectively, our data demonstrate that MdTLP1b positively regulates apple immunity against C. gloeosporioides.

Given that MdTLP1b physically associates with MdCCR4 and positively regulates apple immunity against C. gloeosporioides, we explored whether MdCCR4 influences MdTLP1b transcription. In apple leaves overexpressing MdCCR4, we observed a marked elevation in MdTLP1b transcript abundance compared to vector controls. Conversely, silencing MdCCR4 in the resistant cultivar ‘FJ’ led to a significant downregulation of MdTLP1b expression relative to the controls (Fig. S19E). Collectively, these findings provide evidence that MdCCR4 promotes MdTLP1b expression, implying that MdTLP1b functions downstream of MdCCR4 to confer resistance against C. gloeosporioides.

MdERF1B represses miR482c promoter activity

Similar to protein-coding genes, MIR gene transcription is controlled by transcription factors and specific DNA motifs within their promoter regions [32]. To investigate the transcriptional regulation of miR482c, we cloned a 2.0-kb promoter region upstream of its precursor sequence (Fig. S11). Using a GUS reporter assay in ‘GL-3’ leaves, we evaluated promoter activity in response to C. gloeosporioides infection. Histochemical staining revealed intense blue signals in infected leaves compared to uninoculated controls (Fig. S12A). Quantitative assays corroborated these findings, showing significantly elevated GUS activity upon infection (Fig. S12B), indicating that the miR482c promoter is induced upon C. gloeosporioides infection. Furthermore, to identify potential transcription factors (TFs) that regulate miR482c expression during C. gloeosporioides infection, we predicted TF binding sites in the miR482c promoter using PlantTFDB (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/prediction.php). The results showed that members of 25 TF families potentially bind to this region and participate in the regulation of miR482c (Fig. S13).

Considering the established contribution of ERFs to apple immunity against C. gloeosporioides, relevant ERF transcription factors were selected as the research subjects. Subsequently, to identify specific ERF transcription factors associate with the miR482c promoter, we employed a yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) screening system. Y1HGold strain was simultaneously transformed with the bait plasmid (PromiR482c-pAbAi) and various ERF prey constructs (Fig.S14). For quality control, the interaction between P53-pAbAi and Rec53-pGADT7 was used as a positive standard, while the empty pGADT7 vector paired with the bait served as a negative check. Screening results revealed that yeast cells carrying MdERF1B-pGADT7 exclusively survived on the selective medium (SD/-Leu supplemented with 500 ng/ml AbA), whereas cells expressing other ERF candidates failed to proliferate (Fig. 6A). This result indicated that MdERF1B bound the miR482c promoter in yeast.

To further validate the binding of MdERF1B to the miR482c promoter in vivo, we performed a ChIP-qPCR assay. As shown in Fig. 6C, in the negative control region (C1), no significant difference in DNA enrichment was observed between the GFP-antibody and the No-antibody control. However, in the specific promoter fragment (C2) harboring the binding motif, the enrichment level using the GFP-antibody was significantly higher than that of the No-antibody control. It became apparent that MdERF1B associated with miR482c promoter in apple calli. Dual-luciferase reporter analysis was carried out in N. benthamiana to verify the regulation of miR482c by MdERF1B. Specifically, we co-infiltrated the promiR482c:LUC reporter alongside the 35S:MdERF1B effector or an EV control. We noticed a substantial attenuation of luminescence intensity in leaves harboring MdERF1B compared to the control. Consistent with this observation, quantitative assays demonstrated a significantly lower LUC/REN ratio in leaves co-expressing MdERF1B compared to the control (Fig. 6B), signifying the transcription of miR482c is inhibited by MdERF1B in N. benthamiana.

To further verify MdERF1B directly binding to miR482c promoter, we employed an electrophoretic mobility shift (EMSA) to verify this interaction. EMSA results presented in Fig. 6D revealed that the MdERF1B-GST fusion protein formed a stable DNA-protein complex with the biotinylated wild-type probe, resulting in a retarded band, whereas the GST control did not. Crucially, this interaction proved to be specific: the shifted signal was gradually diminished by adding an excess of unlabeled cold probes but was undetectable when using a mutant probe lacking the core GC-rich motif. Our analysis revealed that MdERF1B is associated with the miR482c promoter. In addition, we verified through molecular docking that MdERF1B associated with miR482c promoter (Fig. S15).

Finally, we quantified the transcript abundance of miR482c in the OE-MdERF1B and RNAi-MdERF1B plants via RT-qPCR. The results revealed a distinct inverse correlation: miR482c expression was significantly downregulated in the OE-MdERF1B leaves, whereas it was markedly elevated in the RNAi-MdERF1B leaves (Fig. 6E). Combined with the results above, we conclude that MdERF1B directly targets the miR482c promoter to negatively regulate its transcription.

The transcription factor MdERF1B enhances resistance to C. gloeosporioides infection

To examine the intracellular distribution of MdERF1B, we cloned its complete open reading frame (lacking the stop codon) into the PRI101-GFP plasmid. Following transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves, confocal imaging showed that the free GFP control displayed a diffuse signal throughout the cell. In contrast, the MdERF1B-GFP fusion protein accumulated specifically within the nucleus, overlapping perfectly with the nuclear marker (Fig. 7A). These observations establish that MdERF1B resides in the nucleus.

Transcriptional activation activity of MdERF1B assessed by a dual-luciferase reporter system in N. benthamiana leaves. D. Quantitative real-time PCR was utilized to determine the expression level of MdERF1B across ‘GL-3’, EV, OE-MdERF1B, and RNAi-MdERF1B leaves. Phenotypes (E) analysis of plants overexpressing MdERF1B (OE-MdERF1B), silencing MdERF1B (RNAi-MdERF1B), and control plants (‘GL-3’ and EV) was observed, and the disease index (F) of these plants were measured at 72 HPI. G. Quantification of C. gloeosporioides relative biomass in these plants was monitored through RT-qPCR analysis.

To assess the transactivation potential of MdERF1B, we employed a yeast-based system. The CDS of MdERF1B was cloned into pGBKT7 to generate the GAL4BD-MdERF1B fusion construct, with empty pGBKT7 and VP16 serving as negative and positive controls, respectively. We observed that yeast cells harboring GAL4BD-MdERF1B were able to proliferate on triple-dropout medium (SD/-Trp/-Ade/-His) and developed a blue color upon X-α-Gal treatment (Fig. 7B). These phenotypes confirm that MdERF1B possesses intrinsic transcriptional activation ability. Dual-luciferase reporter analysis was employed in N. benthamiana leaves to confirm the transcriptional activity in plant cells. The schematic diagrams of the constructed vectors are presented in Fig. S16. Compared to the negative control, leaves expressing pBD-MdERF1B exhibited significantly stronger LUC intensity. This was corroborated by quantitative analysis, which revealed a significantly elevated LUC/REN ratio in the pBD-MdERF1B expressing tissues (Fig. 7C), confirming that MdERF1B possesses transcriptional activation activity in vivo.

To explore potential function of MdERF1B in GLS resistance, we first sought to understand the contribution of MdERF1B to GLS immunity by comparing its mRNA levels between resistant and susceptible apple varieties via RT-qPCR. Notably, infection with C. gloeosporioides triggered a distinct accumulation of MdERF1B transcripts in the resistant variety ‘FJ’ (Fig. S17). We next constructed MdERF1B OE and RNAi vectors and transiently delivered into ‘GL-3’ leaves mediated by Agrobacterium. In contrast to ‘GL-3’ and EV controls, transcript levels of MdERF1B were markedly elevated in the OE-MdERF1B leaves but substantially attenuated in the RNAi-MdERF1B leaves, as verified by RT-qPCR (Fig. 7D). Moreover, immunoblotting results confirmed the specific accumulation of the MdERF1B protein in the OE-MdERF1B apple leaves (Fig. S18). Subsequently, C. gloeosporioides inoculation was carried out on these treated leaves. The RNAi-MdERF1B leaves phenotypically developed aggravated disease symptoms characterized by expansive lesion areas, whereas the OE-MdERF1B leaves displayed significantly alleviated symptoms relative to the controls (Fig. 7E). These visual observations were substantiated by the disease index, which was notably elevated in the RNAi-MdERF1B leaves but suppressed in the OE-MdERF1B leaves (Fig. 7F). A similar pattern was observed regarding fungal growth: OE-MdERF1B plants harbored significantly less pathogen DNA, while RNAi-MdERF1B plants supported greater fungal colonization (Fig. 7G). Current findings indicate that MdERF1B contributes significantly to the defense of apple against C. gloeosporioides.

To validate the sequential regulatory cascade of the MdERF1B-miR482c-MdCCR4-MdTLP1b module, we assessed the transcript abundance of these downstream targets in transiently transformed apple leaves. RT-qPCR results showed that the transient OE of MdERF1B significantly upregulated the expression of MdCCR4 and MdTLP1b, whereas their expression was markedly reduced in RNAi-MdERF1B leaves (Fig. S19A and C). Consistent with the negative regulation of miR482c by MdERF1B, the transient OE of miR482c significantly repressed the transcription of MdCCR4 and MdTLP1b. In contrast, silencing miR482c (STTM-miR482c) resulted in the accumulation of both MdCCR4 and MdTLP1b transcripts (Fig. S19B and D). Coupled with our earlier observation that MdCCR4 positively dictates MdTLP1b expression (Fig. S19E), these findings suggested that upon C. gloeosporioides infection, MdERF1B promoted the expression of MdCCR4 by repressing the transcription of miR482c and ultimately led to the accumulation of MdTLP1b in apple, thereby promoting a robust defense response against the fungal pathogen.

Discussion

Glomerella leaf spot (GLS), primarily incited by Colletotrichum species such as C. gloeosporioides and C. fructicola, is a highly destructive fungal diseases that severely impacts apple (M. domestica) cultivation [33, 34]. The disease leads to severe defoliation and fruit necrosis, resulting in substantial economic losses [35]. Recent studies have demonstrated that the resistance to C. gloeosporioides can be regulated by multiple immune pathways. In the realm of transcriptional regulation, emerging studies have highlighted the contrasting functions of WRKY transcription factors in modulating defense against C. gloeosporioides. For example, MdWRKY100 confers tolerance to C. gloeosporioides via the activation of SA-related genes MdPAL1 and resistance gene MdRPM1, while VQ protein MdVQ37 physically interacts with MdWRKY100 to repress its transcriptional activity [36, 37]. Conversely, MdWRKY17 attenuates resistance to C. gloeosporioides by activating the salicylic acid degradation gene MdDMR6, leading to a reduction in SA levels and promoting C. gloeosporioides infection. Similarly, MdWRKY71 upregulates the SA degradation genes MdDLO1, thereby enhancing susceptibility to C. gloeosporioides [38, 39]. In addition, OE of MdIPT8 confer resistance by elevating endogenous cytokinin levels [40], while miR7125-MdARF1 module improves apple defense against C. gloeosporioides through promoting MdMYB46-mediated lignin synthesis [41]. The m6A reader protein MhYTP2 negatively modulates tolerance to C. gloeosporioides via physical association with the MdRGA2L transcript and the acceleration of its decay [42]. Exogenous chitosan enhances apple resistance to C. gloeosporioides by activating antioxidant defenses and salicylic acid-mediated immunity [43]. However, the molecular mechanisms by which ethylene response factors (ERFs) coordinate with miRNAs to regulate defense responses against C. gloeosporioides have not been fully characterized. In this study, we identified the MdERF1B-miR482c-MdCCR4-MdTLP1b module as a key regulator of apple resistance to C. gloeosporioides (Fig. 8). We demonstrate that MdERF1B inhibits the expression of miR482c, thereby relieving the repression of MdCCR4. Subsequently, accumulated MdCCR4 interacts with MdTLP1b to positively regulate the immune response (Fig. 4). Collectively, this multilevel cascade offers new insights into apple–pathogen interactions and valuable targets for resistance breeding.

The working model of the MdERF1B-miR482c-MdCCR4-MdTLP1b module in apple in response to C. gloeosporioides infection.

A growing body of evidence has shown that microRNAs (miRNAs) fine-tune plant immune responses via transcript degradation or translational inhibition [44, 45]. The miR482 family, highly conserved across plant species, is canonically known to target NBS-LRR transcript [8]. For instance, the elevated levels of miR482 in tomato and potato negatively regulate NBS-LRR targets, resulting in impaired immunity against P. infestans and Verticillium dahliae, respectively [9, 10]. Consistent with these reports, this study reveals that miR482c functions to suppress the apple defense response to C. gloeosporioides; OE of miR482c significantly aggravated disease symptoms, while silencing it enhanced resistance (Fig. 2). However, unlike previous studies that predominantly identified NBS-LRRs as targets, we validated a serine/threonine protein kinase, MdCCR4, as a direct target of miR482c via 5′ RACE and co-expression assays in N. benthamiana (Fig. 1). This finding represents an expansion of the targeting diversity of the miR482 family beyond the canonical NBS-LRR repertoire. In apple, it may also reflect functional diversification adapted to specific pathogen pressures, where miR482c modulates plant immunity not only by regulating R genes but also by dampening kinase-mediated signaling cascades. By cleaving MdCCR4, which physically interacts with the thaumatin-like protein MdTLP1b to activate downstream defense responses (Fig. 4), miR482c adds a novel post-transcriptional regulatory layer to the apple-C. gloeosporioides pathosystem. The pathogen-induced upregulation of miR482c in the susceptible cultivar ‘GL-3’ likely reflects a virulence strategy employed by C. gloeosporioides to subvert host surveillance by targeting MdCCR4 (Fig. 1). This broadens our understanding of miR482’s versatile roles in plant-pathogen interactions and underscores species-specific adaptations in woody perennials.

Serine–threonine kinases are pivotal in mediating signal transduction and triggering plant immune responses [46]. A classic example is the tomato Pto kinase, which mediates defense signaling not only by phosphorylating the downstream target Pti1 but also by interacting with the ERF transcription factor Pti4 to directly induce defense gene expression [47–49]. Similarly, in tobacco, NrSTK has been characterized as a defense enhancer against black shank, whose biological function requires the presence of a conserved ATP-binding site as well as a motif essential for serine/threonine kinase activity [50]. Similarly, the introduction of the cotton kinase GbSTK into Arabidopsis significantly improved plant tolerance to both V. dahliae and oxidative stress through the activation of immune signaling and the maintenance of ROS homeostasis [51]. In our study, the result shows that OE of MdCCR4 outstandingly confer tolerance to C. gloeosporioides (Fig. 3). In an effort to elucidate the underlying mechanism, we searched for MdCCR4-binding proteins and identified MdTLP1b, a thaumatin-like protein. We verified that MdCCR4 physically binds to MdTLP1b in dual assays (in vivo and in vitro) (Fig. 4), and further demonstrated that the abundance of MdCCR4 positively modulates the transcript levels of MdTLP1b (Fig. S19E). These findings align with recent work in walnut (Juglans regia), where the JrCCR4–JrTLP1b interaction was shown to promote anthracnose resistance [52]. To explore the potential for a conserved evolutionary strategy, multiple sequence alignments were performed, revealing high amino acid sequence identity and conserved functional domains between the apple and walnut orthologs (Fig. S20). Collectively, our results underscore that the CCR4–TLP1b interaction represents a conserved defense mechanism across diverse woody plant species.

Thaumatin-like proteins (TLPs), intrinsic members of the PR-5 family, serve as cornerstone components of plant innate immunity, typically functioning by disrupting fungal membrane integrity to inhibit spore germination and mycelial expansion [16, 53]. Aligning with extensive documentation of TLP-mediated resistance in diverse crop species—including rice, wheat, and potato [17, 18, 54], our investigation found that MdTLP1b acts as a critical positive regulator of defense against C. gloeosporioides in apple (Fig. 5). Specifically, MdTLP1b transcription is strongly induced upon infection in the resistant cultivar ‘FJ’ (Fig. S9B) and functional validation assays further found that OE of MdTLP1b conferred resistance to C. gloeosporioides, whereas RNAi-mediated silencing effectively attenuated the native resistance of ‘FJ’ plants (Fig. 5). Furthermore, a protein sequence alignment revealed that MdTLP1b shares high-sequence identity with the walnut ortholog JrTLP1b (Fig. S20B). These findings strikingly recapitulate the functional characteristics of JrTLP1b in walnut, highlighting a conserved antifungal mechanism for TLP1b orthologs in woody perennials combating anthracnose pathogens [52].

The miRNA-target modules exert a vital function in orchestrating plant growth and development [45], and their activities are often fine-tuned by upstream transcription factors (TFs). For instance, the formation of vascular tissues in Populus is orchestrated by the PtoTCP20-miR396d-PtoGRF15 signaling module [55], while in apple, modules such as MdMYB16-miR7125-MdCCR [56] and MdBBX22-miR858-MdMYBs [57] coordinate the homeostasis of secondary metabolites. The expression of miRNA-target regulatory circuits during infection is fine-tuned by resistance (R) genes in addition to specific TFs. For instance, the over-accumulation of the R protein SNC1 leads to a global repression of miRNA biogenesis to amplify immune responses [58]. Furthermore, in pear, the transcription factor PcMYB44 associated with PcMIR397 promoter to impair its activity, resulting in relieving the post-transcriptional silencing of laccase genes to promote lignification and broad-spectrum fungal resistance [59]. Similarly, in apple roots, MdERF61 was found to bind specific GCC-boxes within the mdm-miR397b promoter to inhibit its transcription, which subsequently enhances MdLAC7b-mediated lignin deposition and confers resistance against Fusarium solani [60]. Consistent with these findings, we identified a novel regulatory module, miR482c-MdCCR4-MdTLP1b, that is regulated by MdERF1B (Fig. 8). We demonstrated that MdERF1B negatively regulates the transcriptional level of miR482c (Fig. 6), while it also positively modulates apple defense against C. gloeosporioides (Fig. 7D–G).

Overall, this study substantiates that the MdERF1B-miR482c-MdCCR4-MdTLP1b module orchestrates apple resistance to C. gloeosporioides. Specifically, miR482c acts as a negative regulator by post-transcriptionally silencing MdCCR4, a serine/threonine protein kinase gene. We determined that MdCCR4 physically interacts with the thaumatin-like protein MdTLP1b, and both components are essential for defense against C. gloeosporioides. Moreover, the transcription factor MdERF1B represses miR482c transcription via a direct interaction with its promoter, while upregulation of MdERF1B improves resistance to C. gloeosporioides.

Materials and methods

Pathogen cultures and plant material

C. gloeosporioides W16 strain was incubated on PDA medium under 28°C. After making scratch lines 3 d later (to promote conidia production), the culture medium was returned to the incubator for continued cultivation. After continued cultivation for 4 d, the red conidia were harvested using ddH2O, followed by straining through four layers of filter to yield a spore suspension. Prior to the inoculation assays, the density of conidial suspension was standardized to 1 × 106 conidia·ml−1.

In this study, laboratory-preserved M. domestica plantlets, specifically the ‘Royal Gala’ (‘GL-3’) and ‘Fuji’ (‘FJ’) cultivars, were utilized as experimental material. They were propagated on MS within a controlled growth chamber maintained at 25°C with a relative humidity of 80%. The plants were subjected to a 16-h photoperiod with a photon flux density of 120 μmol m−2 s−1.

Seedlings of N. benthamiana were grown in a soil mixture consisting of equal parts of loam, vermiculite, and sterile perlite. The plants were kept in a growth chamber at 25°C with 80% relative humidity.

Bioinformatics analysis

A genomic fragment of 2 kb located 5′ to the pre-miR482c was downloaded from GDR. For the prediction of putative cis-acting regulatory elements, the sequence was processed using PlantCARE. Subsequently, visualization of these identified motifs was performed with TBtools [61]. Additionally, potential transcription factors (TFs) associating with the miR482c promoter were screened via the ‘Binding Site Prediction’ module in PlantRegMap (http://plantregmap.gao-lab.org/).

GUS assay

GUS expression levels were assessed through both histochemical staining and quantitative enzyme activity assays, in accordance with the methods outlined by Jefferson et al. [62] and Anand et al. [63] with minor modifications. For histochemical analysis, the infiltrated leaves were immersed in GUS staining buffer and kept at 37°C. Subsequently, the samples were decolorized using 75% ethanol to completely remove chlorophyll and enhance the visualization of the blue staining.

Spectrophotometric analysis was employed to determine the GUS activity with the aid of a specific assay kit provided by Solarbio Life Sciences (Beijing, China). The assay was conducted in full compliance with the manufacturer’s protocols. The amount of enzyme catalyzing the formation of 1 nmol p-nitrophenol per hour per mg protein was considered as one unit of activity. For each construct, nine independent leaves were infiltrated to ensure statistical reliability.

5′ RACE assay

To verify the targeting association between miR482c and MdCCR4, the RACE assay was conducted by utilizing the HiScript-TS 5′/3′ RACE Kit as per the instructions provided by the manufacturer. In short, 5′-RACE-ready cDNA was carried out using the supplied 5′ TS Oligo and enzyme mix. Subsequent nested PCR amplification was conducted employing gene-specific outer and inner primer pairs to amplify the cleaved MdCCR4 transcripts (Table. S2). The obtained PCR products were purified and ligated into a pTOPO-Blunt vector. Finally, randomly selected positive clones were analyzed via Sanger sequencing to map the precise cleavage sites.

Total RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from approximately 100 mg of apple leaf tissue employing a column-based purification method [29]. Specifically, the leaf samples were homogenized in 1 ml of lysis buffer supplemented with 150 μl of 10% N-lauroylsarcosine. The homogenate was incubated at 70°C for 10 min, cooled on ice for 5 min, and subsequently centrifuged at 13 000 rpm for 15 min to remove cellular debris. We added 300 μl of pure ethanol to the 600 μl clarified supernatant before transferring the mixture to a spin column. Finally, the RNA was collected in 30–50 μl of RNase-free water after washing.

Subsequently, total RNA was converted into first-strand cDNA utilizing the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme) as per the provided instructions. The RT-qPCR experiments were conducted on a CFX Connect™ Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Finally, relative gene expression levels were quantified using the comparative 2−ΔΔct method [64].

To determine the relative fungal biomass, we followed the protocol of Jiang et al. [65] (slightly modified). Genomic DNA extraction from the infected leaves (72 HPI) was carried out with a Kit provided by Sangon Biotech. Data normalization was achieved by using C. gloeosporioides β-tubulin gene and M. domestica MdActin gene as internal standards.

Agrobacterium-mediated transient transformation

Transformation of Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 was achieved via the heat-shock method. Confirmed colonies were cultured in liquid LB broth and cultures were shaken at 180 rpm (28°C) until an OD600 of 0.6–1.0 was attained. The harvested cells were resuspended in infiltration buffer. Leaves from 5-week-old tissue-cultured apple plantlets were selected as explants for the transient assay. Infiltration of the Agrobacterium suspension was performed using a syringe without a needle. For each construct, 20 independent plantlets were utilized. Following infiltration, the plantlets were maintained at 25 ± 1°C with a 16-h photoperiod before subsequent analysis.

For the N. benthamiana assay, the bacterial suspension was standardized to an OD600 of 0.8–1.0. Infiltration was carried out on the undersides of leaves from 6-week-old N. benthamiana utilizing a syringe without a needle. Postinfiltration, the plants were kept in a climate chamber at 25 ± 1°C.

Y1H assay

The recombinant pAbAi vector harboring the miR482c promoter was transformed into 50 μl of Y1Hgold yeast cells. Post-transformation, the yeast cells were plated on SD/-Ura and cultured for 3–5 d to screen for positive colonies. Meanwhile, the open reading frames (ORFs) of Rap2.12, Rap2.3, ERF9, ERF4, ERF86, and ERF1B were integrated into the pGADT7 vector to yield Rap2.12-pGADT7, Rap2.3-pGADT7, ERF9-pGADT7, ERF4-pGADT7, ERF86-pGADT7, and ERF1B-pGADT7, respectively. Subsequently, these recombinant plasmids were individually mobilized into the miR482c-promoter-containing Y1H yeast strains. The transformed cells were then cultured on SD/-Leu supplemented with AbA to screen for specific protein–DNA interactions. As a negative control, empty pGADT7 was also transformed into the bait Y1H strains.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

MdERF1B ORF was ligated into the pGEX-6p-1 to yield recombinant plasmid GST-MdERF1B. Verified positive clones were cultured in LB medium at 37°C for 14 h, followed by culture expansion. When the cell density hit 0.4–0.5, we added 1 mM IPTG to induce protein expression and kept the culture at 28°C for 6 h. The harvested cells were resuspended in PBS (15 ml) and sonicated for 20 min. The lysate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was processed with a GST Fusion Protein Purification Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) to obtain the target protein. Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) provided the 5′-biotinylated probes used for EMSA.

The LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA kit was employed to conduct the EMSA, following procedures outlined by the manufacturer. First, pre-electrophoresis was performed at 100 V to eliminate impurities. For the binding assay, the purified MdERF1B-GST protein was incubated with unlabeled competitor probes in the binding buffer for 10 min at room temperature, followed by the addition of biotin-labeled probes for an additional 20 min. The reaction mixtures (10 μl) were loaded and separated by electrophoresis. Subsequently, the protein–DNA complexes were transferred onto a nylon membrane. The nylon membrane was cross-linked using UV irradiation (120 mJ/cm2) for 3 min on both the front and back sides. After blocking for 15 min, the membrane was incubated with a streptavidin-conjugated HRP antibody (1:2000 dilution) for 15 min at room temperature. Following four washing steps (5 min each), the signals were visualized using ECL reagents.

ChIP-qPCR

We followed the protocol of Li et al. [66] to conduct ChIP assays. The ORF of MdERF1B was ligated into the pRI101 to yield the 35S:GFP-MdERF1B. Apple calli were then transformed with this plasmid, while control experiments utilized transgenic calli carrying the empty pRI101-GFP vector (35S:GFP). For the ChIP analysis, we employed the Biyoung ChIP Assay Kit based on the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, cross-linked chromatin was isolated from the transgenic calli and immunoprecipitated using an anti-GFP antibody to isolate DNA fragments bound to the GFP–ERF1B fusion protein. The recovered DNA was subsequently analyzed by quantitative PCR (qPCR). ChIP-qPCR assays were conducted on a CFX Connect™ Real-Time System using SYBR Premix Ex Taq reagent. The enrichment of DNA fragments was calculated relative to the input DNA, and data were normalized to the control samples.

Dual LUC

To analysis transcriptional regulation of miR482c by MdERF1B, miR482c promoter was ligated into pGreenII 0800-LUC (PromiR482c-LUC) to generate reporter, carrying Renilla luciferase (REN) gene for normalization. Simultaneously, the MdERF1B coding sequence was integrated into pGreenII 62-SK (35S:MdERF1B) to yield effector, while 35S functioned as the negative control. Subsequently, reporter and effector were transferred into A. tumefaciens GV3101 cells carrying pSoup and injected into N. benthamiana leaves. At 48 h postinfiltration, luminescence was visualized using a Tanon 5200 Multi CCD system. The enzyme activities of Firefly and RENs were quantified using a Kit provided by Vazyme, and the relative binding activity was determined by normalizing LUC values to REN.

To assess the transcriptional activity of MdERF1B in N. benthamiana, the ORF of MdERF1B was ligated into the pBD vector (modified from pGreenII 62-SK) to generate the pBD-MdERF1B effector. For controls, pBD-VP16 functioned as the positive standard, whereas the empty pBD vector was used as the negative. Following verification, effector and reporter strains were co-infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves. At 48 h postinfiltration, luminescence imaging and relative activity assays were conducted as described above.

Y2H assay

To construct the bait and prey vectors, ORF of MdCCR4 and MdTLP1b were ligated into pGBKT7 and pGADT7, respectively. The resulting plasmids were co-introduced into Y2HGold yeast cells via the standard polyethylene glycol/lithium acetate protocol. For the controls, pGBKT7-53/pGADT7-T was used as the positive standard, while pGBKT7-lam/pGADT7-T functioned as the negative. Initially, transformants were screened on SD/-Leu/-Trp. To verify protein–protein interactions, yeast cells were transferred to SD/-Ade/-His/-Leu/-Trp containing X-α-gal and cultured at 29°C to observe blue colony formation.

Pull-down assay

To generate MdTLP1b-HIS and MdCCR4-GST vectors, the MdTLP1b CDS (lacking the stop codon) and MdCCR4 CDS (lacking the signal peptide and stop codon) were ligated into pET-32a-c and pGEX-6p-1, respectively. These plasmids were then transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) and Rosetta (DE3) cells, and protein expression was induced using IPTG. After induction, the recombinant proteins were purified using the corresponding His- and GST-tag purification kits (Beyotime Biotech). For pull-down assay, the purified MdTLP1b-HIS and MdCCR4-GST proteins were co-cultured at 4°C for about 2 h. Subsequently, the protein mixture was incubated with His-tag purification resin (Beyotime Biotech) for another 2 h at 4°C. After washing five times to remove unbound proteins, the bound proteins were eluted and subjected to Western blotting analysis using anti-HIS and anti-GST antibodies.

Firefly luciferase complementation imaging assay

We carried out LCI assay following the method established by Zhao et al. [67]. The ORF of MdCCR4 and MdTLP1b was ligated into pCAMBIA 1300-nLUC and pCAMBIA 1300-cLUC to generate nLUC-MdCCR4 and cLUC-MdTLP1b. Using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, we delivered these constructs into the leaves of N. benthamiana. Prior to infiltration, various combinations of the transformed Agrobacterium strains were suspended in MES buffer at an OD600 of 0.8–1.0. After being kept in the dark for 24 h postinfiltration, the plants were moved to a growth chamber and cultured under light conditions for a further 48 h. To detect the interaction, samples were treated with a 1 mM solution of D-luciferin (Promega), subsequently luminescence imaging and relative activity assays were conducted as described above.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay

To construct BiFC vectors, MdCCR4 and MdTLP1b were integrated into pEarleyGate201-YN and pEarleyGate202-YC, producing YN-MdCCR4 and YC-MdTLP1b, respectively. Following sequence confirmation, these plasmids were transferred into A. tumefaciens GV3101 strain. The harvested cells were suspended using infiltration buffer to achieve an optical density of 1.5. Suspensions of the two strains were combined and co-injected into the leaves of N. benthamiana. At 48 hours postinfiltration, confocal images of YFP fluorescence were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 system (Leica, Germany).

Subcellular localization

To generate the MdERF1B-GFP and MdCCR4-GFP fusion construct, the MdERF1B and MdCCR4 coding region (with the stop codon removed) were integrated into pRI101-GFP, whereas empty pRI101-GFP was utilized as negative control. Following verification, plasmids were mobilized into A. tumefaciens (GV3101) by heat shock. For transient expression, Agrobacterium strains harboring MdERF1B-GFP or the EV were individually mixed with a nuclear marker (mCherry) and co-infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. GFP and mCherry fluorescence signals were subsequently captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Leica, Germany).

Analysis of transcriptional activation

To analyze the transcriptional activity of MdERF1B, the ORF of MdERF1B was ligated into the pGBKT7 to generate the pGBKT7-MdERF1B. As negative and positive controls, the empty pGBKT7 vector and pGBKT7-VP16 were selected, respectively. The constructs were transformed into the yeast strain Y2HGold. The transformed yeast cells were initially selected on SD/-Trp medium. Subsequently, the yeast cultures were cultured in SD/-Trp/-Ade/-His and SD/-Trp/-His/-Ade plates supplemented with X-α-Gal (CX11922, Coolaber, Beijing, China). The plates were incubated at 30°C for 3 d, and the growth status and α-galactosidase activity (blue color development) were photographed.

AI-based virtual screening for MdCCR4-interacting proteins

To identify potential interacting partners of the serine/threonine protein kinase MdCCR4, an AI-based virtual screening was performed following the method established by Li et al. [68]. Briefly, the 3D structure of the serine/threonine kinase MdCCR4 was modeled using AlphaFold. Subsequently, AI-driven virtual screening for MdCCR4-interacting proteins was performed via HDOCK. Proteins with a docking score below −300 were further subjected to AlphaFold-based interaction prediction. Finally, proteins with an ipTM + pTM score greater than 0.75 were selected as potential candidate proteins.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Prism, San Diego, CA, USA). All assays were performed three times independently, and values are shown as mean ± SD. Differences were assessed by one-way ANOVA (Tukey’s test). Asterisks indicate significance: P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), and P < 0.001 (***).
