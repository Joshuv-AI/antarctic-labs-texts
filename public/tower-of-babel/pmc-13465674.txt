Epigenetic vulnerability in endocrine disruption: bridging mechanisms, models, and environmental inequities
Authors: Fernando Lizcano, María Camila Ramírez-Bohorquez, María Camila Ballesteros-Garcia, Valentina Vargas, Sebastián Cardenas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This narrative mini-review examines how endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) induce persistent epigenetic alterations that shape endocrine, metabolic, and developmental trajectories across the lifespan. It addresses the emerging gap in integrating mechanistic epigenetic evidence with population-level vulnerability, particularly in underrepresented regions such as Latin America. These effects are especially critical during sensitive windows—including fetal development, childhood, and puberty—when DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNA networks establish long-term molecular programming. While animal models have provided key mechanistic insights, their translational limitations highlight the need for human-relevant systems capable of capturing endocrine-specific and epigenetic endpoints under environmentally realistic conditions. We propose that epigenetic programming represents a central biological interface linking EDC exposure to context-dependent vulnerability. New Approach Methodologies (NAMs)—including organoids, micro-physiological systems, and computational models—offer a translational bridge aligned with Adverse Outcome Pathway frameworks. Importantly, susceptibility to endocrine disruption is shaped not only by biological factors but also by environmental, nutritional, and socioeconomic determinants. Evidence from Latin American populations—including altitude gradients, nutritional transitions, and structural inequalities—illustrates how these contextual factors interact with epigenetic mechanisms to influence metabolic and endocrine outcomes. This integrative perspective supports the development of more equitable and context-sensitive approaches to endocrine toxicology.

Introduction

This narrative review was based on a structured literature search conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, focusing on studies published between 2015 and 2025. Keywords included “endocrine-disrupting chemicals”, “epigenetics”, “DNA methylation”, “histone modification”, “non-coding RNA”, “organoids”, and “Latin America”. Priority was given to recent human studies, multi-omics research, and mechanistic experimental models. Additional seminal references were included to contextualize foundational concepts.

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) are exogenous substances capable of altering hormonal signaling and producing long-lasting effects on endocrine, metabolic, and developmental health [1]. Growing evidence demonstrates that many EDC-associated outcomes are mediated through epigenetic mechanisms—including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNA regulation—which shape gene expression patterns during critical windows of biological plasticity [2–4]. These mechanisms contribute not only to individual susceptibility but may also support intergenerational and transgenerational transmission of risk [5, 6].

Although traditional toxicology has relied heavily on animal models, these approaches have limited capacity to capture endocrine-specific perturbations, low-dose effects, and complex mixture exposures. Recent advances in New Approach Methodologies (NAMs), such as organoids and micro-physiological systems [7], provide human-relevant platforms for assessing epigenetic and hormonal endpoints within Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) frameworks [8–11].

Yet the biological impact of EDCs cannot be fully understood without accounting for environmental and social determinants that shape exposure and vulnerability. In Latin America, factors such as poverty, dietary transitions, limited regulatory infrastructure, and geographical conditions—including altitude—intersect with biological susceptibility, influencing metabolic and endocrine outcomes at the population level [12–14].

This mini-review integrates epigenetic mechanisms, NAM-based evidence, and population-specific determinants to provide a contextualized perspective on endocrine disruption. (Fig. 1). We propose that epigenetic mechanisms function as the central biological interface through which chemical exposures and socio-environmental modifiers converge. Within this framework, human-relevant New Approach Methodologies offer a translational bridge capable of capturing context-dependent vulnerability and informing more equitable regulatory and public health strategies.

Core epigenetic mechanisms affected by endocrine-disrupting chemicals

EDCs alter gene expression through multiple epigenetic pathways that regulate chromatin architecture and cellular identity. The most widely studied mechanisms include DNA methylation, histone post-translational modifications, and non-coding RNA networks. These systems interact to coordinate developmental and endocrine homeostasis, and their disruption can generate persistent molecular signatures that extend across the lifespan and, in some cases, to subsequent generations [15–18].

DNA methylation

DNA methylation is the most consistently affected epigenetic mark in EDC research. Methylation at CpG dinucleotides, regulated by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), plays a central role in genomic imprinting, tissue-specific gene expression, and endocrine pathway regulation [19]. Multiple EDCs—including BPA, phthalates, polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), and dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT) derivatives—induce locus-specific hypo- or hypermethylation during sensitive developmental Windows [20]. Alterations in promoter methylation of genes involved in glucose metabolism, adipogenesis, and neuroendocrine maturation have been documented in human studies, primarily in peripheral tissues such as blood and placenta [21–24]. These findings have been associated with metabolic dysfunction, reproductive alterations, and changes in pubertal timing. While such observations provide population-level evidence of epigenetic disruption, mechanistic insights into tissue-specific chromatin remodeling are largely derived from experimental models. Notably, methylation patterns established early in life may exhibit relative stability, supporting the possibility of intergenerational transmission through incomplete epigenetic reprogramming [25–28].

Histone modifications

Histone post-translational modifications (PTMs), including acetylation, methylation, and ubiquitination, fine-tune chromatin accessibility and transcriptional responsiveness to hormonal signals [29]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) can perturb the activity of histone acetyltransferases, deacetylases, and methyltransferases, leading to altered expression of endocrine-responsive genes [4]. Mechanistic investigations have highlighted the role of histone demethylases of the KDM4 family (e.g., KDM4A and KDM4C), which regulate H3K9 and H3K36 methylation states and influence adipocyte differentiation, insulin sensitivity, and cell proliferation [30, 31]. Dysregulation of KDM4C has been linked to hormone-sensitive cancers, while aberrant KDM4A activity contributes to metabolic reprogramming in obesity [32]. Our own experimental studies, together with those of other groups, suggest that histone demethylases represent a key mechanistic intersection between endocrine disruption, metabolic regulation, and cancer susceptibility [33].

Similarly, EID1, a transcriptional coregulator involved in chromatin remodeling, has been shown in experimental systems to modulate lipid metabolism and adipocyte function and may interact with environmental exposures to influence metabolic outcomes [34].

Non-coding RNAs

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and other regulatory RNA species respond dynamically to environmental cues and are key mediators of EDC effects [35]. EDCs can alter miRNA expression profiles that regulate steroidogenesis, hypothalamic development, pubertal timing, and metabolic pathways. Several miRNAs involved in insulin signaling, adipogenesis, and gonadal development exhibit altered expression following exposure to BPA, phthalates, PFAS, or PCBs, as demonstrated primarily in experimental models, with emerging supportive evidence from human observational studies [36]. Because miRNAs orchestrate coordinated gene networks, their dysregulation can amplify the biological impact of low-dose exposures. lncRNAs, although less studied, have also emerged as regulators of hormone receptors, epigenetic enzymes, and chromatin looping, suggesting a broader RNA-based dimension of endocrine disruption [37, 38].

Interactions and transgenerational effects

These epigenetic mechanisms do not operate in isolation. DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs interact to form an integrated regulatory system. EDC-induced perturbations can propagate through feedback loops, shaping chromatin memory and establishing persistent transcriptional states. Such multigenerational effects have been consistently demonstrated in animal models, whereas evidence in human populations remains limited and largely indirect [39]. Although human evidence remains more limited, emerging methylome and small-RNA datasets suggest that similar patterns may occur, especially when exposure occurs during prenatal or early-life windows [40].

Across the literature, it is critical to distinguish between mechanistic evidence derived from experimental systems and associative evidence from human studies, as both provide complementary but distinct insights into epigenetic disruption. Recent advances in epigenetic epidemiology have enabled the application of epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS), RNA sequencing, and integrative multi-omics approaches to characterize EDC-induced molecular signatures. However, interpretation remains constrained by tissue specificity, temporal variability, and the use of surrogate tissues in human studies.

Research over the past decade has substantially expanded the catalogue of EDCs with documented epigenetic effects in humans and experimental models, although the strength and mechanistic depth of evidence vary across chemical classes. Epigenetic and Endocrine effects of major endocrine-disrupting chemicals

Bisphenols (BPA, BPS, BPF)

Bisphenol A (BPA) remains the most extensively studied EDC with respect to epigenetic outcomes. BPA exposure induces alterations in DNA methylation of genes involved in steroidogenesis, glucose metabolism, adipogenesis, and neurodevelopment. Hypomethylation of imprinted genes (e.g., IGF2/H19), altered methylation of estrogen-responsive loci, and methylation drift in adipose tissue have been reported in both human epidemiological studies – primarily using peripheral tissues- and controlled experimental models [24, 55–57].

Replacement bisphenols such as BPS and BPF, marketed as safer alternatives, exhibit comparable or even stronger endocrine and epigenetic activity, including histone acetylation changes and dysregulation of miRNAs involved in metabolic programming. Recent biomonitoring studies highlight the growing ubiquity of BPS/BPF, raising concern that “regrettable substitutions” may perpetuate epigenetic risk [58, 59].

Phthalates

Phthalate metabolites modulate epigenetic programming during fetal and postnatal development, with demonstrated effects on DNA methylation, histone marks, and miRNA expression in tissues related to reproductive maturation, adiposity, and neuroendocrine regulation. Several cohort studies have documented associations between prenatal phthalate exposure and altered methylation patterns in genes implicated in pubertal timing, thyroid signaling, insulin pathways, and metabolic homeostasis [23, 27, 60].

Complementary mechanistic studies in experimental systems demonstrates that phthalates can modify KDM activity and histone acetylation in reproductive tissues [61].

Organochlorines (DDT/DDE, PCBs)

Although banned or restricted in many regions, DDT/DDE and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) persist in the environment and continue to exert epigenetic effects. Animal studies demonstrate that neonatal exposure to DDT accelerates puberty and modifies histone acetylation in hypothalamic genes regulating reproductive maturation. In contrast, human studies primarily report association between PCBs/DDE exposure and DNA methylation patterns in metabolic and reproductive pathways induce miRNA dysregulation in the medial preoptic area and alter methylation of genes involved in neuroendocrine development [62–64].

Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS)

PFAS are emerging contaminants with endocrine and metabolic effects increasingly linked to epigenetic dysregulation. Epidemiological studies have associated prenatal and early-life PFAS exposure with altered DNA methylation patterns in genes regulating thyroid function, lipid metabolism and immune responses. Experimental systems further demonstrate that PFAS can modulate histone acetylation, inhibit histone deacetylases activity, and disrupt miRNAs networks [65, 66].

Several PFAS compounds exhibit long biological half-lives, raising concern that sustained epigenetic alterations may contribute to multigenerational health effects [67].

Flame retardants and other emerging EDCs

Brominated flame retardants (BFRs) have been shown in experimental models to alter DNA methylation and histone acetylation patterns in neural and reproductive tissues. In human studies, these compounds have been associated with altered neurodevelopment and endocrine otucomes [68, 69]. Organophosphate esters (OPEs), increasingly used as PBDE replacements, have likewise been associated with differential methylation of genes linked to neuroendocrine signaling, oxidative stress, and inflammatory pathways in both experimental and human studies [70–73].

Herbicides and fungicides—including glyphosate and azoles—are under investigation for potential epigenetic effects via interference with one-carbon metabolism, chromatin remodeling, and steroidogenic pathways [68, 74, 75].

Integrative perspective

Across these diverse chemical classes, a consistent pattern emerges:

• EDCs preferentially disrupt epigenetic programming during sensitive windows (prenatal life, early childhood, puberty).

• Low-dose exposures and mixture effects can provoke measurable epigenetic changes, challenging conventional toxicology thresholds.

• Several EDC-induced epigenetic marks show persistence, raising questions about transgenerational inheritance and long-term disease risk.

• These findings underscore the need for human-relevant mechanistic tools—such as NAMs—to capture the full spectrum of endocrine and epigenetic disruption.

Research strategies: an integrated framework from molecular models to community engagement

Addressing the health risks posed by EDCs—particularly those imperceptible to human senses and often embedded in the materials and products of everyday life—requires a paradigm shift in the way environmental health research is conceived and implemented. Over the past two decades, it has become evident that neither basic laboratory science, conducted in isolation, nor fragmented, community-level interventions alone can achieve sustained reductions in harmful exposures or their associated disease burden [76]. In the context of pronounced disparities between the Global North and South—differences that encompass industrial practices, regulatory enforcement, patterns of exposure, and public awareness—there is a pressing need for research strategies that are both scientifically robust and socially attuned [77, 78].

Here, we outline an integrated, iterative, and bidirectional research framework designed to bridge the gap between molecular-level discovery and real-world population health. This approach seeks to transform mechanistic insights into actionable strategies that can be implemented across diverse sociocultural and regulatory landscapes, while also ensuring that knowledge flows back from communities to refine and adapt scientific inquiry [79].

The starting point of this framework is the recognition that in most societies—particularly in resource-constrained settings—there is limited public awareness of environmental risks that do not manifest as acute or visible harm. EDCs, often present in plastics, personal care products, household cleaners, and processed foods, can accumulate in the body and exert effects on endocrine and developmental systems over prolonged periods, frequently below the threshold of consumer detection. The first strategic priority, therefore, is the generation of rigorous, context-specific evidence on these risks, employing methods that range from analytical chemistry and exposomics to in-depth mechanistic studies of toxicological pathways [80].

Within this integrative framework, the role of experimental models becomes pivotal. Translating complex exposures into measurable biological outcomes depends on models that reflect human physiology with fidelity and ethical responsibility. This is precisely the promise of New Approach Methodologies (NAMs), particularly organoid and micro-physiological systems, which are redefining how endocrine disruption is studied.

New approach methodologies (NAMs) for epigenetic and endocrine toxicology

Traditional toxicological testing has relied heavily on animal models and high-dose exposures, which are often poorly suited to detect subtle, low-dose, non-monotonic, and mixture effects on endocrine and epigenetic endpoints [81, 82]. These approaches may fail to capture species-specific receptor sensitivity, chromatin dynamics, and developmental windows that are central to endocrine disruption [83].

New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) have emerged to address these limitations by providing human-relevant, mechanistically informed platforms that can be aligned with Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) frameworks and the ethical principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) [8, 9]. Organoids, micro-physiological systems, and computational toxicology platforms enable interrogation of chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNA responses under environmentally realistic exposure conditions [84].

However, despite their transformative potential, NAMs also face important conceptual and practical limitations. First, many organoid systems lack full vascular, immune, and endocrine axis integration, restricting their capacity to model systemic cross-talk and long-term developmental programming [7]. Second, inter-laboratory reproducibility and standardization remain significant challenges, particularly for epigenomic readouts that are sensitive to culture conditions and passage effects [85, 86]. Third, while NAMs are well suited for mechanistic interrogation, their quantitative extrapolation to population-level risk assessment still depends on robust exposure data and validated physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models [87]. Finally, regulatory acceptance of NAM-derived endpoints in endocrine toxicology is evolving and requires harmonization across agencies and jurisdictions [80].

Thus, while NAMs constitute a major advance in mechanistic and human-relevant toxicology, their translational value depends on integration with epidemiological evidence capable of capturing real-world exposure variability and long-term outcomes. This integration requires the identification of shared molecular signatures across systems, enabling meaningful cross-validation between experimental models and human cohort studies and strengthening population-level risk assessment.

Organoids and 3D culture systems

Organoids derived from human stem or progenitor cells recapitulate key features of endocrine and metabolic tissues, including cellular heterogeneity, tissue architecture, and hormonal responsiveness. Organoid models of pancreas, adipose tissue, liver, thyroid, gonads, and hypothalamus–pituitary axes now allow direct assessment of: - hormone synthesis and secretion, - receptor signaling and feedback regulation, - epigenetic remodeling in response to EDC exposure [88].

Chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation profiles, histone modifications, and non-coding RNA expression can be interrogated in organoids exposed to environmentally relevant concentrations of EDCs, providing an integrated view of early molecular events that precede functional endocrine alterations. Because organoids can be generated from genetically diverse donors, they also offer an opportunity to explore interindividual variability in epigenetic susceptibility [85, 89, 90].

Micro-physiological systems and multi-organ platforms

Micro-physiological systems (MPS) and “organ-on-a-chip” technologies extend the organoid concept by recreating dynamic microenvironments, including fluid flow, mechanical forces, and controlled gradients of nutrients or oxygen. Multi-organ platforms linking liver, adipose tissue, reproductive organs, and brain-relevant structures enable the study of: -biotransformation and metabolite formation of EDCs, - endocrine crosstalk (e.g. liver–adipose–pancreas, HPT/HPA axis), - systemic epigenetic responses across tissues [84].

These systems are particularly well suited to investigate non-monotonic dose–response relationships and delayed effects, which are challenging to capture in traditional assays. Integration with high-throughput epigenomic profiling (ATAC-seq, ChIP-seq, methylome, small-RNA sequencing) allows mapping of EDC-induced changes at regulatory elements and gene networks.

Computational models and PBPK-integrated approaches

In silico tools and physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models complement experimental NAMs by simulating internal doses, tissue distribution, and mixture interactions. When coupled with epigenetic and transcriptomic data from organoids or MPS, these models can: - link external exposure to target tissue concentrations, - estimate population variability (age, sex, pregnancy, comorbidities), - support quantitative AOPs that incorporate epigenetic key events [91]. Machine learning and network-based approaches also facilitate identification of epigenetic signatures predictive of endocrine outcomes, enabling prioritization of chemicals for regulatory scrutiny and guiding safer-by-design strategies [87, 92].

Relevance for vulnerable and under-studied populations

For regions with limited toxicology infrastructure, such as many areas in Latin America, NAMs offer the possibility of developing scalable, cost-effective platforms that can be adapted to local research priorities. Organoids derived from donors belonging to specific ethnic, geographic, or socioeconomic backgrounds could help characterize context-specific epigenetic vulnerability to EDCs, including interactions with altitude, undernutrition, or obesogenic environments.

By embedding NAMs within epidemiological and public health frameworks, it becomes possible to bridge molecular epigenetic mechanisms with population-level risk, thus moving from descriptive associations to mechanistically informed and context-sensitive strategies for regulation and prevention (Fig. 2).

Despite their transformative potential, NAMs face important limitations. Organoid systems may lack full immune and vascular integration, inter-laboratory standardization remains a challenge, and long-term multigenerational modeling is still technically constrained. Computational models depend heavily on high-quality exposure data, which are often scarce in low- and middle-income regions. Addressing these limitations will be essential to ensure that NAM-based endocrine toxicology achieves both scientific robustness and regulatory acceptance.

While these approaches provide valuable mechanistic insight, their translation to real-world populations requires validation in epidemiological studies that account for exposure heterogeneity, cumulative mixture burden, and long-term developmental trajectories. Advanced statistical approaches—including weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression, Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR), and exposomic frameworks—are increasingly applied to characterize complex chemical mixtures and their interactions with epigenetic and endocrine endpoints, facilitating integration between mechanistic models and population-level risk assessment [93].

Social, nutritional, and environmental determinants of EDC impact in Latin America

The biological effects of EDCs cannot be fully understood without considering the social, nutritional, and environmental contexts in which exposure occurs. Unlike high-income regions, where regulatory oversight, research capacity, and exposure monitoring systems are more developed, many Latin American populations experience simultaneous burdens of poverty, informal industrial processes, urban pollution, nutritional transitions, and limited health surveillance. These conditions create a landscape in which EDC exposure is both more frequent and more biologically consequential, amplifying epigenetic vulnerability across the life course [94].

Socioeconomic inequality and differential exposure

Socioeconomic disadvantage increases contact with EDCs through multiple pathways: informal recycling of plastics and electronic waste, unregulated pesticide use in agricultural regions, poorly controlled industrial emissions, and reliance on low-cost consumer products containing bisphenols, phthalates, PFAS, and flame retardants. In many low-income communities, food storage practices, overcrowded housing, and restricted access to regulated products compound exposure. These structural determinants establish a social gradient of endocrine risk, wherein disadvantaged groups accumulate higher chemical burdens during critical developmental windows [95–98].

Nutritional transitions and metabolic susceptibility

Latin America has undergone a rapid nutritional shift characterized by increased consumption of ultra-processed foods, disruptions in traditional dietary patterns, and rising prevalence of obesity in children and adolescents [99]. Nutritional status modulates endocrine and epigenetic responses to EDCs by influencing one-carbon metabolism, adipose tissue inflammation, and insulin signaling. Undernutrition and obesity—often coexisting within the same communities—may both potentiate EDC-induced epigenetic dysregulation, particularly at loci involved in adipogenesis, pubertal timing, and hypothalamic development. These interactions underscore the need to situate molecular findings within the broader context of food systems and dietary inequities [27, 28, 100].

Geographical and environmental modifiers: evidence from altitude

Geographical factors also shape endocrine and metabolic outcomes in the region. Our own work, based on ecological analyses in human populations, demonstrates that childhood obesity prevalence decreases with increasing altitude in Colombia, indicating that environmental context influences metabolic regulation independently of lifestyle factors [101]. Extending these observations, studies conducted in two Andean populations revealed an inverse ecological association between altitude and type 2 diabetes mellitus, suggesting that chronic exposure to hypobaric hypoxia may promote adaptive responses involving mitochondrial efficiency, insulin sensitivity, and potentially epigenetic modulation of energy metabolism [102–105]. These findings illustrate how environmental gradients, such as altitude, interact with biological pathways that are also sensitive to EDCs, opening the possibility that geographic context modifies the epigenetic impact of chemical exposure [106, 107].

Regulatory gaps and limited research capacity

Many countries in Latin America lack comprehensive regulatory frameworks for EDCs, modern analytical infrastructure, or region-specific toxicological testing protocols. Surveillance systems for reproductive and developmental endpoints remain limited, and few laboratories have the capacity to evaluate epigenetic biomarkers. These gaps create blind spots in risk assessment, particularly regarding mixture exposures, low-dose effects, and susceptible subpopulations. The scarcity of local biomonitoring data reinforces global inequities in EDC research, limiting the ability to develop context-adapted interventions.

Toward context-specific epigenetic toxicology

Reducing the burden of endocrine disruption in Latin America requires research strategies that recognize the convergence of molecular, environmental, and social determinants. Although NAMs—such as organoids, micro-physiological systems, and computational approaches—represent transformative tools for studying human-relevant endocrine and epigenetic endpoints, their true impact emerges when they are embedded within a broader, context-sensitive framework. Integrating mechanistic data with exposomic profiles, nutritional and geographic modifiers, and community knowledge enables the identification of population-specific epigenetic vulnerability and the design of interventions that reflect local realities (Fig. 3).

Community engagement and environmental health literacy.

Co-production of knowledge with affected communities improves risk communication, supports behavioral interventions, and informs culturally appropriate policy design. Community report-back initiatives can help translate complex epigenetic findings into actionable public health strategies.

Taken together, these priorities define a framework for a more equitable and mechanistically informed understanding of endocrine disruption, bridging molecular epigenetics with real-world vulnerability in Latin America. Within this framework, genetic ancestry and population structure may further shape epigenetic responses to EDC exposure, modulating susceptibility through gene–environment interactions that remain insufficiently characterized.

Limitations

Current evidence is limited by heterogeneity in study design, variability in exposure assessment, reliance on surrogate tissues in human epigenetic studies, and limited longitudinal data in Latin American populations.

Conclusion

Epigenetic mechanisms constitute a central interface through which environmental exposures are translated into endocrine, metabolic, and developmental disease risk. The convergence of EDC, contextual determinants, and molecular programming highlights the need to move beyond reductionist toxicology toward integrative and population-informed models of vulnerability. In this context, the combined use of NAMs, multi-omics platforms, and epidemiological validation will be essential to strengthen translational relevance and improve population-level risk assessment. Importantly, incorporating regional determinants such as nutritional transitions, altitude, ancestry, and socioeconomic inequities may help explain differences in susceptibility across populations. Advancing this framework will be critical for the development of more precise, equitable, and clinically relevant endocrine toxicology.
