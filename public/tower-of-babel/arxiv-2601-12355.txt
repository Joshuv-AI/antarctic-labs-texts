LB-MCTS: Synergizing Large Language Models and Bayesian Optimization for Efficient CASH
Beicheng Xu, Weitong Qian, Lingching Tung, Yupeng Lu, Bin Cui

Abstract
To lower the expertise barrier in machine learning, the AutoML community has focused on the CASH problem—a fundamental challenge that automates the process of algorithm selection and hyperparameter tuning. While traditional methods like Bayesian Optimization (BO) struggle with cold-start issues, Large Language Models (LLMs) can mitigate these via semantic priors. However, existing LLM-based optimizers generalize poorly to the high-dimensional, structured CASH space. We propose LB-MCTS, a framework synergizing LLMs and BO within a Monte Carlo Tree Search structure. It maximizes LLM reasoning with Selective Tuning Memory (STM) and explicit exploration-exploitation trade-off. It combines the strengths of both paradigms by dynamically shifting from LLM-driven to BO-driven proposals as data accumulates. Experiments on 104 AMLB datasets demonstrate the superiority of LB-MCTS over the competitive baselines.

Introduction

In recent years, machine learning has advanced in diverse domains, such as computer vision
(
16
;
26
)
, and recommendation systems
(
52
;
47
)
.
Despite these advancements, developing tailored solutions with strong performance remains a knowledge-intensive task, requiring careful selection of suitable ML algorithms and hyperparameter tuning
To lower barriers and streamline deployment, the AutoML community has introduced the Combined Algorithm Selection and Hyperparameter Optimization (CASH) problem
(
48
)
and proposed several methods
(
21
;
17
)
to automate this optimization process.
Among them, the dominant approach is Bayesian Optimization (BO), which builds surrogate models of the performance landscape to guide iterative evaluations.
However, by treating configurations as abstract numeric vectors, pure BO solvers fail to incorporate prior knowledge, necessitating extensive evaluations to explore high-dimensional and conditional search spaces
(
10
)
.

Convergence of LLM-based methods on 104 datasets.

Recently, the reasoning and in-context learning (ICL) capabilities of large language models (LLMs) have made them attractive for black-box optimization
(
25
;
55
)
.
LLMs can serve as context-aware “experts” with domain knowledge, alleviating the limitations of BO.
Early works directly treat the LLM as a standalone optimizer, iteratively prompting it to obtain candidates
(
55
)
.
However, pure LLM optimizer suffers from inherent limitations and is unreliable in many practical scenarios
(
19
)
. This is because it does not fit a surrogate model (nor quantify uncertainty) and struggles to utilize the full optimization history with a limited context window.
Therefore, attempts have been made to integrate LLMs with BO
(
1
;
7
)
, e.g., using LLMs to generate warm-starts or candidates for BO, or interleaving them with BO for alternating optimization. Such synergy has yielded improved performance.

However, all existing LLM-based optimizers remain confined to unstructured and low-dimensional problems, leaving the highly structured and high-dimensional CASH problem unexplored. Tackling CASH with LLMs presents three critical challenges that current approaches fail to address:

C1
:
Efficient context utilization
.
CASH features a hierarchical search space (i.e., specific hyperparameters active only for specific algorithms) where the history of one algorithm (e.g., SVM) provides negligible guidance for another (e.g., XGBoost). Yet, existing methods indiscriminately populate prompts with the best, recent, or entire optimization history. This approach introduces cross-algorithm noise, leading to low context utilization and high token costs.

C2
:
Exploration-exploitation trade-off
.
Success in CASH demands a dual-level exploration-exploitation balance: selecting between distinct algorithms, and navigating between promising vs. unexplored regions within each algorithm’s subspace.
However, existing LLM-based methods lack an explicit trade-off mechanism, leading to premature convergence and local optima.

C3
:
Effective synergy with BO
.
Combining the semantic reasoning of LLMs with the numerical precision of BO is key to efficient optimization. However, current methods either use static integration strategies without dynamic adaptation or rely on rigid heuristic thresholds to switch between LLM and BO, which generalizes poorly.

Figure

1
compares LB-MCTS with four existing LLM-based optimizers by plotting the average best validation performance (normalized per dataset) across iterations.
While existing methods plateau early, LB-MCTS continues to improve and reaches the highest final performance.

In this paper, we propose LB-MCTS, a new framework that synergizes LLMs and BO to solve the CASH problem.
The contributions are summarized as follows:
(i) To tackle
C1
,
We propose a Selective Tuning Memory (STM) mechanism that retrieves similar and high-quality experiences specific to the selected algorithm. This allows the LLM and BO to optimize in a focused space with noise-free guidance.
(ii) To deal with
C2
, LB-MCTS leverages Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS).
The tree structure explicitly handles the hierarchical trade-off: the root node balances selection among algorithms, while subtrees manage exploration and exploitation within each algorithm’s subspace.
(iii) For
C3
, it structurally combines the optimization trajectories of BO and LLM and dynamically switches between them for configuration proposal. This achieves more sample-efficient optimization than either component alone.
(iv) Empirical studies on 104 datasets from the AutoML Benchmark demonstrate that LB-MCTS outperforms both BO-based and LLM-based state-of-the-art baselines.

Preliminary and Related Work

2.1
CASH Problem and Traditional Methods

We first introduce the basic notations for the CASH problem.
Let
\mathcal{A}=\{A^{1},\dots,A^{K}\}
be a set of
K
candidate algorithms, where each algorithm
A^{i}
is associated with a specific hyperparameter space
\Lambda^{i}
.
Given a dataset partitioned into training and validation sets (
\mathcal{D}_{train},\mathcal{D}_{val}
), the CASH problem aims to jointly identify the optimal algorithm
A^{*}
and its configuration
\lambda^{*}
to maximize the validation metric
\mathcal{F}
:

A^{*}_{\bm{\lambda}^{*}}=\operatorname*{argmax}_{A^{i}\in\mathcal{A},\bm{\lambda}\in\Lambda^{i}}\mathcal{F}(A^{i}(\bm{\lambda},\mathcal{D}_{train}),\mathcal{D}_{val}).

(1)

The CASH problem is first introduced by Auto-WEKA
(
48
)
and solved by Bayesian Optimization (BO)
(
46
;
20
;
2
)
.
BO is a popular algorithm framework that solves black-box problems through looping over three steps:
1) It fits a surrogate
f
on the observed data
D=\{(\bm{\lambda}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n-1}
;
2) BO selects the next configuration
\bm{x}_{n}
by maximizing an acquisition function
\alpha
:
\bm{\lambda}_{n}=\arg\max_{\bm{\lambda}}\alpha(\bm{\lambda};f)
;
3) BO evaluates
\bm{\lambda}_{n}
to obtain the performance
y_{n}
and update
D\leftarrow D\cup\{(\bm{\lambda}_{n},y_{n})\}
. Many other leading AutoML Systems like Auto-sklearn
(
11
)
, LightAutoML
(
49
)
, and H2O
(
27
)
also adopt BO as the core solver for CASH.
Another line of work improves BO by considering the diversity of models evaluated at each iteration
(
43
;
39
)
.
In addition, genetic programming
(
36
)
and the multi-armed bandits (MAB) based methods
(
28
;
30
)
are also used to address the CASH problem.

2.2
LLM-based Optimizer

Recently, researchers have explored LLMs as standalone alternatives to traditional optimizers with their emergent reasoning capabilities. These approaches have been applied to diverse tasks, including chemistry
(
33
)
, hyperparameter optimization
(
31
)
, feature engineering
(
18
)
, and molecular optimization
(
35
)
.
Typical methods like LLAMBO
(
31
)
and OPRO
(
55
)
use the ICL capabilities of LLMs to iteratively propose solutions.
Despite their innovation, these methods are primarily validated on low-dimensional black-box optimization (usually with dimensionality
D<10
).
This confinement stems from the lack of mathematical guarantees offered by traditional optimizers such as BO.
Additionally, constant LLM querying is economically demanding, with methods like LLAMBO requiring dozens of API calls per configuration.

In response to the limitations of pure LLM optimizers, hybrid approaches have emerged that combine LLMs and BO
(
41
;
50
)
.
BOPRO
(
1
)
uses BO to optimize prompt instructions for the LLM, whereas ADO-LLM
(
56
)
employs the LLM to generate candidates for BO.
SLLMBO
(
34
)
alternates between LLM-based parameter exploitation and TPE
(
2
)
in a fixed 50:50 ratio.
BORA
(
7
)
, which is most closely related to our approach, dynamically alternates between LLM-based search and GP-based BO using a heuristic variance-based policy.
Although these hybrid approaches improve upon pure LLM optimizers, they are still primarily restricted to low-dimensional settings (
D<20
) with flat, unstructured parameter spaces.
Naively extending them to the highly structured, high-dimensional CASH setting is ineffective due to C1-C3.
Overall, effectively synergizing LLMs and BO for the CASH problem remains an open challenge.

2.3
Monte-Carlo Tree Search

Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) is a sampling-based algorithm for tree-structured search spaces
(
24
)
.
It incrementally builds a search tree by balancing exploration (trying new actions) and exploitation (refining promising actions)
(
6
;
13
)
.
Each MCTS iteration consists of four phases:
1)
Selection:
Starting from the root, MCTS traverses the tree to select the node for expansion.
Modern approaches (e.g., AlphaGo Zero
(
45
)
) employ PUCT
(
42
)
for selection.
For a parent node
s
, the next child node
s_{i}
is selected from the set of children
\mathcal{C}(s)
to maximize:

\arg\max_{s_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(s)}\left\{Q(s_{i})+c_{puct}\cdot P(s_{i})\cdot\frac{\sqrt{N_{s}}}{1+N_{s_{i}}}\right\},

(2)

where
Q(s_{i})
is the mean value of
s_{i}
,
P(s_{i})
is the prior probability of visiting
s_{i}
, and
N_{s}
(
N_{s_{i}}
) denotes the visit count of the parent (child). The constant
c_{puct}
balances exploitation and exploration.
2)
Expansion:
Expand the node by adding a new child node with an untried action.
3)
Playout:
Conduct a complete simulation to a terminal node to yield a reward value.
4)
Backpropagation:
Update node statistics by propagating the reward back up the path.

Motivated by this, AlphaD3M
(
9
)
and MOSAIC
(
40
)
build upon MCTS to explore the pipeline search space.
ML-master
(
32
)
utilizes MCTS for code generation task.

3.1
Tree Structured Search for CASH

We model CASH problem as a sequential decision-making process. As shown in
Figure

2
, the search space is organized into a tree structure containing three types of nodes:

•

CASH Node
(Root
s_{\text{root}}
): represents the initial state of the CASH problem. It encompasses all candidate ML algorithms and their respective hyperparameter spaces.

•

Algo Nodes
(
s_{\text{algo}}
): immediate children of the root. Each represents the hyperparameter optimization problem for a specific algorithm
A^{i}
in the subspace
\Lambda^{i}
.

•

HP Nodes
(
s_{\text{hp}}
): all descendants of an Algo Nodes. Each HP node represents a concrete configuration
\bm{\lambda}_{s_{\text{hp}}}
of the algorithm and its validation metric
y_{s_{\text{hp}}}
.

Each node
s
maintains three key statistics: the visit count
N_{s}
, the cumulative reward
R_{s}
, and the subtree best performance
y^{\max}_{s}
, i.e., the best validation metric observed so far in the subtree rooted at
s
.
After that, we define actions as transitions between nodes, yielding three action types:

•

Algorithm Selection Action
(
s_{\text{root}}\xrightarrow{a_{\text{sel}}}s_{algo}
): select an algorithm
A^{i}\in\mathcal{A}
to be optimized, constraining the search to its specific hyperparameter subspace.

•

Initialization Action
(
s_{\text{algo}}\xrightarrow{a_{\text{init}}}s_{\text{hp}}
):
generates the initial configuration
\bm{\lambda}_{0}
for the selected algorithm, serving as an initialization of an optimization trajectory.

•

Optimization Action
(
s_{\text{hp}}\xrightarrow{a_{\text{opt}}}s_{\text{hp}}^{\prime}
):
proposes a new configuration
\bm{\lambda}_{t+1}
based on a basic configuration, aiming to refine the performance.

Then we define an
optimization trajectory

\mathcal{T}
as a sequence of nodes and actions originating from the root.

The trajectory structurally captures the process of choosing an algorithm, initializing a configuration, and iteratively refining its hyperparameter values.
Consequently, the CASH problem is reformulated as a
sequential decision-making process
, where the objective is to find the optimal trajectory that maximizes the validation performance of the final configuration.
The process can be iteratively solved by MCTS introduced in
Section

2.3
.
In our setting,
Expansion represents executing an action (
a_{\text{init}}
or
a_{\text{opt}}
) to propose a configuration via LLM/BO.
Playout evaluates the proposed configuration to obtain its validation metric
y_{new}
and computes a binary reward
r=\mathbb{I}(y_{new}>y^{\max}_{s_{\text{root}}})
, which signals whether the new trial improves upon the global optimum.
Finally, Backpropagation propagates these values up the trajectory: for each ancestral node
s
, it updates the visit count (
N_{s}\leftarrow N_{s}+1
), the cumulative reward (
R_{s}\leftarrow R_{s}+r
), and the subtree best (
y^{\max}_{s}\leftarrow\max(y^{\max}_{s},y_{new})
) to guide future iterations.

Surrogate model for each algorithm.

We maintain a separate Gaussian Process (GP)
(
53
)
surrogate model
f_{i}
for each algorithm
A^{i}
.
Importantly,
f_{i}
is trained on the local dataset
\mathcal{D}_{i}=\{(\bm{\lambda}_{s^{k}_{\text{hp}}},y_{s^{k}_{\text{hp}}})\}_{k=1}^{N_{i}}
, comprising all
N_{i}
configurations and their performance collected within the subtree rooted at
s_{\text{algo}}^{i}
.
This per-algorithm modeling exploits disjoint hyperparameter spaces to reduce dimensionality, enhancing precision compared to a single high-dimensional global surrogate
(
28
)
.
We define the GP covariance function
k(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})
as a mixed kernel:

k(\bm{\lambda},\bm{\lambda}^{\prime})=k_{\nu}(\bm{\lambda}_{cont},\bm{\lambda}_{cont}^{\prime})\times k_{cat}(\bm{\lambda}_{cat},\bm{\lambda}_{cat}^{\prime}),

(3)

where
k_{\nu}
is a Matérn-
5/2
kernel on continuous dimensions, and
k_{cat}
is a Hamming kernel for categorical similarity.

3.2
Algorithm Selection

In each MCTS iteration, the Selection phase starts at
s_{\text{root}}
and chooses one of its
K
child Algo node, which directly corresponds to selecting an algorithm
A^{i}\in\mathcal{A}
for CASH. Concretely, we utilize PUCT in
Equation

2
and the estimated mean value of each Algo node
s_{\text{algo}}^{i}
is

Q(s_{\text{algo}}^{i})={R_{s_{\text{algo}}^{i}}}/{N_{s_{\text{algo}}^{i}}}+\tilde{y}^{\max}_{s_{\text{algo}}^{i}},

(4)

where the first term is the average reward of node
s_{\text{algo}}^{i}
, and the second term is the normalized best validation performance in its subtree (min–max normalized using the global range of observed metric scores).

Q(s_{\text{algo}}^{i})
favors algorithms that not only yield high absolute performance (via
\tilde{y}^{\max}
) but also demonstrate a high potential for continuous improvement (via the average reward).

Algorithm Prior.
Inspired by AlphaGo Zero
(
45
)
, which uses a policy network to guide the search, we construct a selection prior
P(s_{\text{algo}}^{i})
of each algorithm. Instead of a neural policy, we estimate the algorithm quality with surrogates introduced in
Section

3.1
. Specifically, for each algorithm
A^{i}
, we uniformly sample
n_{s}
configurations from
\Lambda^{i}
, predict their performance using the corresponding surrogate
f_{i}
, and compute the normalized mean predicted score
\bar{v}_{i}
. The prior distribution is:

P(s_{\text{algo}}^{i})=\frac{\exp(\bar{v}_{i})}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp(\bar{v}_{k})}.

3.3
Hyperparameter Optimization

Once an algorithm
A^{i}
is selected at the root, the remaining task reduces to an algorithm-specific HPO subproblem: finding
\bm{\lambda}\in\Lambda^{i}
with best performance.
During subsequent MCTS phases,
LB-MCTS integrates two complementary configuration proposers: a BO proposer for quantitative search, and an LLM proposer for qualitative proposals.

3.3.1
BO proposer

The BO proposer directly generates a new node by selecting the best candidate from a sampled pool.

Candidate Generation.
We employ two sampling strategies to generate a pool of candidate configurations
\mathcal{P}
.
(i) Random sampling draws candidates randomly from the search space
\Lambda^{i}
.
(ii) Local sampling treats every HP node
s_{\text{hp}}
in the subtree of
s_{\text{algo}}^{i}
as a basic configuration and samples candidates from its neighborhood (i.e., via small perturbations of configuration in
s_{\text{hp}}
).

Selection & Expansion.
We then rank all candidates in
\mathcal{P}
using the Expected Improvement (EI)
(
23
)
acquisition function based on the surrogate
f_{i}
, and select the candidate
\bm{\lambda}_{new}
with the highest score.
The expansion type depends on the source of
\bm{\lambda}_{new}
:
If it originates from random sampling, it is treated as an initialization action (e.g.,
s^{1}_{\text{algo}}\xrightarrow[\text{\tiny BO random search}]{a_{\text{init}}}s^{2}_{\text{hp}}
), expanding directly from the Algo node
s_{\text{algo}}^{i}
;
If it originates from local sampling, it is treated as an optimization action (e.g.,
s^{2}_{\text{hp}}\xrightarrow[\text{\tiny BO local search}]{a_{\text{opt}}}s^{5}_{\text{hp}}
), expanding as a child of the basic configuration node.

3.3.2
LLM proposer

Following the algorithm selection, the LLM proposer continues the Selection phase using PUCT until a leaf HP node is reached.
It then expands by querying the LLM to generate a new configuration, as illustrated in
Figure

3
.

Selection.
The LLM proposer extends the Selection by recursively applying the PUCT criterion (
Equation

2
) within the chosen algorithm subtree. The traversal proceeds until it reaches a leaf node—a node that the LLM has not fully expanded, e.g.,
s^{3}_{\text{hp}}
in
Figure

2
. Unlike the algorithm level, we assign a uniform prior
P(s_{\text{hp}})
to all child nodes here.

Expansion via ICL.

Given a selected leaf node
s_{\text{base}}
with a basic configuration
\bm{\lambda}_{\text{base}}
, the LLM proposer executes Expansion by querying the LLM to propose a new configuration through adjusting
\bm{\lambda}_{\text{base}}
. We construct a structured prompt comprising four key components:
(i) the
task description

\psi
(dataset description and optimization metric),
(ii) the
Selective Tuning Memory

\mathcal{M}_{i}(s_{\text{base}})
, containing informative historical trials from algorithm
A^{i}
,
(iii) the basic configuration
\bm{\lambda}_{\text{base}}
, and
(iv) an optimization directive
d\in\{\textsc{Warmup},\textsc{Exploration},\textsc{Exploitation}\}
.
Note that (ii) and (iii) are empty for a warmup expansion when
s
is an Algo Node.
Formally, the LLM generates a reasoning chain
\mathcal{R}
followed by a new configuration
\bm{\lambda}_{\text{new}}
:

(\mathcal{R},\bm{\lambda}_{\text{new}})=\mathrm{LLM}\big(\psi,\mathcal{M}_{i}(s_{\text{base}}),\bm{\lambda}_{\text{base}},d\big).

(5)

The output
\bm{\lambda}_{\text{new}}
is added as a new child node
s^{\text{new}}_{\text{hp}}
of
s_{\text{base}}
.
Crucially, the directive
d
explicitly governs the search mode:
Warmup
provides a high-quality initial point when expanding an Algo node (e.g.,
s^{1}_{\text{algo}}\xrightarrow[\text{\tiny LLM warmup}]{\tiny a_{\text{init}}}s^{1}_{\text{hp}}
).

Exploitation
instructs the LLM to locally refine
\bm{\lambda}_{\text{base}}
for immediate gains (e.g.,
s^{2}_{\text{hp}}\xrightarrow[\text{\tiny LLM exploitation}]{\tiny a_{\text{opt}}}s^{4}_{\text{hp}}
); and
Exploration
encourages bold adjustments to probe unknown regions (e.g.,
s^{2}_{\text{hp}}\xrightarrow[\text{\tiny LLM exploration}]{\tiny a_{\text{opt}}}s^{3}_{\text{hp}}
).
An Algo node is regarded as a leaf before it has generated
n_{\text{w}}=3
initial configurations via
Warmup
.
An HP node is considered fully expanded by the LLM only after both
Exploration
and
Exploitation
have been executed once; otherwise, it remains a leaf.
Detailed prompt template and example are shown in Appendix
B.1
and
B.3
.

Playout and reflection.

Upon generation, the new configuration
\bm{\lambda}_{\text{new}}
is evaluated on the training/validation split to obtain the performance metric
y_{\text{new}}
. To bridge the gap between numerical rewards and the LLM’s semantic reasoning, we subsequently incorporate a reflection mechanism inspired by Reflexion
(
44
)
. We prompt LLM to analyze the evaluation result by comparing it against the performance of
\bm{\lambda}_{\text{base}}
and configurations in memory
\mathcal{M}_{i}(s_{\text{base}})
:

\rho_{\text{new}}=\mathrm{LLM}\big(\psi,\mathcal{M}_{i}(s_{\text{base}}),\bm{\lambda}_{\text{base}},y_{\text{base}},\bm{\lambda}_{\text{new}},y_{\text{new}}\big).

(6)

where
\rho_{\text{new}}
is a textual summary of what worked or failed and how to adjust future configurations.
Detailed prompt template and example are shown in Appendix
B.2
and
B.3
.
This reflection is stored in the corresponding new HP node
s^{\text{new}}_{\text{hp}}
and provides instructions for future expansion.

Selective Tuning Memory.

Efficiently utilizing optimization history is crucial for ICL.
Therefore, we propose the Selective Tuning Memory (STM) mechanism.
In the CASH setting, mixing history from different algorithms introduces noise. We therefore first isolate the optimization history so that the memory
\mathcal{M}_{i}(s_{\text{base}})
for
s_{\text{base}}
contains only trials from the same algorithm
A^{i}
.
After that, we view each transition in the MCTS subtree—from a parent node
s_{p}
to a child
s_{c}
—as an “optimization attempt”
e=(\bm{\lambda}_{p},y_{p},\bm{\lambda}_{c},y_{c},\rho_{c})
, capturing the modification from the parent configuration
\bm{\lambda}_{p}
to the child
\bm{\lambda}_{c}
, their performances, and reflection
\rho_{c}
. (For BO-generated nodes, we construct a textual summary of the parameter changes and performance delta).

Given an HP node with configuration
\bm{\lambda}_{\text{base}}
to be expanded, our goal is to retrieve historical attempts that are both relevant (similar starting point) and instructive (high resulting performance
y_{c}
).
The similarity between a historical attempt’s starting configuration
\bm{\lambda}_{p}
and
\bm{\lambda}_{\text{base}}
can be measured using the algorithm’s GP covariance kernel
k(\bm{\lambda}_{p},\bm{\lambda}_{\text{base}})
in
Equation

3
, which jointly captures continuous-categorical similarity. To balance relevance and performance, we compute the Pareto frontier over all historical attempts in the algorithm’s subtree based on
(k(\cdot,\bm{\lambda}_{\text{base}}),y_{c})
, selecting the non-dominated attempts as the
Global Memory

\mathcal{M}_{\text{global}}
.
Complementing this, we include the
Local Memory

\mathcal{M}_{\text{local}}
, comprising the sequence of attempts along the ancestral path from the algorithm root to
s_{\text{base}}
. This preserves the optimization trajectory context. The final memory is the union
\mathcal{M}_{i}(s_{\text{base}})=\mathcal{M}_{\text{global}}\cup\mathcal{M}_{\text{local}}
, providing both high-quality, relevant references and coherent trajectory awareness.

3.3.3
Dynamic Proposer Selection

To synergize the complementary strengths of semantic reasoning and quantitative modeling, LB-MCTS dynamically selects either the LLM or BO proposer at each HPO step.
Our insight is that BO becomes increasingly reliable as data accumulates and the surrogate model matures, whereas LLMs may excel in the early stages with prior knowledge and semantic understanding.
We maintain a dynamic probability
p^{i}_{\text{BO}}
of selecting BO for each algorithm.

Specifically, we assess the reliability of the BO surrogate
f_{i}
for the algorithm
A^{i}
by measuring its generalization capability.
Given the algorithm-specific dataset, we perform
k
-fold cross-validation.
We train
f_{i}
on training folds and compute the Kendall’s rank correlation coefficient (
\tau^{i}
) between predicted and true rankings on validation folds to quantify how well
f_{i}
can capture the relative order of configurations.
This correlation is rescaled to
[0,1]
as a probability:
p^{i}_{\text{BO}}=\text{max}(\epsilon,\frac{\tau^{i}+1}{2})
, where a small constant
\epsilon>0
(e.g.,
0.05
) ensures the BO proposer remains active.
We initialize the BO probability
p^{i}_{\text{BO}}
to 0 and update it every
k
observations (e.g.,
k=5
).
In each iteration, we select the BO proposer with probability
p^{i}_{\text{BO}}
and the LLM proposer with probability
1-p^{i}_{\text{BO}}
. This mechanism ensures a smooth transition from early LLM-driven search to rigorous BO-driven search as the surrogate model becomes accurate.

3.4
Algorithm Summary

The procedure of LB-MCTS is summarized in Appendix
A.1

We run MCTS iteratively over the CASH tree:
1)
Algorithm Selection
: The root selects an Algo node with PUCT.
2)
Proposer Selection
: For the chosen algorithm, we estimate the GP surrogate’s quality to select the BO or LLM proposer.
3)
Proposal
: The BO proposer samples candidates and returns the best one with EI; the LLM proposer extends PUCT to select a base node and prompts LLM with a specific directive to generate a new configuration.
4)
Playout & Reflection
: The new configuration is evaluated, followed by a reflection, and a new HP node is added.
5)
Backpropagation
: The result is propagated to update ancestral node statistics.
Repeating until the budget is exhausted, LB-MCTS outputs the algorithm–configuration
(A^{*},\bm{\lambda}^{*})
with the highest validation performance observed.

3.5
Discussion

Difference with Previous Methods.

(i) Compared with prior LLM-based optimizers, LB-MCTS is designed for the structured CASH setting, while still generalizing to standard unstructured optimization, where the tree degenerates to an Algo node branch.
(ii) Compared to existing LLM–BO hybrids (e.g., BORA, SLLMBO), which feed BO-evaluated trials to LLM as an unstructured history, LB-MCTS uses MCTS to structurally fuse BO’s search trajectories with the LLM’s optimization trajectories. This exposes richer, path-aware semantic information to LLM than a flat list of past trials.
(iii) Compared with MCTS-based AutoML methods (e.g., AlphaD3M, Mosaic), which use MCTS only to choose pipeline algorithms, LB-MCTS builds a tree that jointly covers algorithm selection and fine-grained HPO trajectories, naturally facilitating LLM integration.

Convergence Analysis
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose LB-MCTS, a framework that synergizes LLMs and BO via Monte Carlo Tree Search to solve the CASH problem. LB-MCTS introduces three key components: a tree structure to manage hierarchical exploration-exploitation trade-offs, a Selective Tuning Memory (STM) to enhance LLM reasoning, and a dynamic switching mechanism to optimally combine LLM and BO proposers.
Experiments on 104 AMLB datasets show that LB-MCTS outperforms BO-based, LLM-based, and hybrid baselines.

Proof of Theorem
3.1

Proof.

The proof relies on the hierarchical decoupling of the search space and the probabilistic interleaving of the optimizers.

1. Algorithm Space Coverage (MCTS Level).

At the root level, LB-MCTS employs the PUCT algorithm. A known property of UCT-based bandits is that as the total visits
N\to\infty
, the exploration term ensures the visit count of every arm (algorithm
A^{k}
) goes to infinity (
N_{A^{k}}\to\infty
). Thus, the algorithm
A^{*}
containing the global optimum will be selected infinitely often.

2. Subspace Convergence (Proposer Level).

Consider the subsequence of iterations where the optimal algorithm
A^{*}
is selected. In this subspace, LB-MCTS selects the BO proposer with probability
P_{\text{BO}}\geq\epsilon
. By the Borel-Cantelli lemma, as
N_{A^{*}}\to\infty
, the number of BO steps also approaches infinity almost surely.
Existing literature guarantees that BO (specifically utilizing Gaussian Processes or including random search components) creates a dense sequence of samples that converges to the global optimum of the subspace
(
4
;
20
)
.

3. Non-interference of LLM.

The LLM proposer operates as a heuristic policy. While it accelerates the search by prioritizing semantically promising regions based on ICL, it does not restrict the search space accessible to BO. Since the BO component is guaranteed to execute infinitely often, any potential stagnation or local optimality from the LLM is eventually corrected by the BO’s global exploration.

Conclusion.
Since LB-MCTS visits the optimal algorithm subspace infinitely often and performs consistent BO search within that subspace infinitely often, it converges to the global optimum
\bm{\lambda}^{*}
almost surely.
∎

Theoretical-Empirical Alignment.

Our experimental observations on 104 datasets (
Figure

7
) directly validate the conditions required for this convergence.

•

Safety Guardrail:
While
P_{\text{BO}}
starts low (
<0.3
for
N<25
) to leverage semantic priors, the
\epsilon
-clipping ensures that the theoretical “anchor” of BO is never deactivated, even when LLM heuristics dominate the early search.

•

Transition to Rigor:
As evaluations grow, we observe
P_{\text{BO}}
rising to
\approx 0.5
at
N\approx 50
and stabilizing between
0.6
and
0.8
after
N\approx 100
. This empirical trend shows that the system naturally shifts toward the theoretically-grounded BO regime as the surrogate matures, fulfilling the convergence criteria with increasing confidence.

•

Global Correctness:
The fact that the LLM is selected in only 107 out of 300 iterations on average ensures that the majority of the budget is managed by the convergent BO process, while the LLM purely serves as an acceleration heuristic that does not compromise the global search density.

C.3
Implementation Details of Baselines

We adhered to the open-source versions or the methodologies outlined in the original papers for all other baseline implementations.
We implement the BO components of all baselines with Openbox 0.8.1
(
22
)
, an open-source toolkit designed for black-box optimization.
We employ GPT-4o-mini
(
38
)
, OpenAI’s cost-efficient model, as the backbone for all LLM-based optimizers.
Since LLM-based methods rely on warm-starting and prior work typically uses only a few initial configurations (e.g.,
\sim
5 in LLAMBO and BORA) on much simpler tasks, we standardize and strengthen warm-starting for our harder CASH setting: at initialization, the LLM generates 3 seed configurations for
each
algorithm to improve early coverage of the search space.
All the experiments are
conducted on a machine with 24 ‘AMD EPYC 7702P’ CPU cores and 80G memory.

•

SMAC
(
20
)
.
We implement SMAC as a standard BO-based CASH optimizer using a random-forest surrogate. The optimizer treats CASH as a structured configuration space with conditional hyperparameters, and iteratively proposes one configuration per evaluation.

•

OptDivBO
(
39
)
.

OptDivBO enhances standard BO by encouraging diversity in the proposed models through ensemble selection. Following the original paper, we fix the diversity trade-off parameter
\tau
at 0.2 and set the ensemble size for post-hoc selection to 25.

•

Rising Bandit(RB)
(
28
)
.

RB addresses the CASH problem by treating algorithm selection as a multi-armed bandit problem.
If the upper bound of the reward of an arm
k
(algorithm
A^{k}
) is less than or equal to the lower bound of another arm in the candidate set, then the arm
k
will be eliminated from the candidate set.
The parameter
C
for computing the smooth growth rate is set to 7.

•

MOSAIC
(
40
)
.

MOSAIC uses a tree policy for algorithm selection and BO for HPO. Algorithm selection is guided by a UCB-style criterion with exploration constant
C_{\text{ucb}}=1.3
, and tree expansion is controlled by progressive widening with coefficient
PW=0.6
. For the BO component, MOSAIC fits a
single global
random-forest surrogate over the entire CASH space using all observed trials (rather than per-algorithm surrogates), and proposes configurations under the selected algorithm accordingly.

•

OPRO
(
55
)
.

OPRO treats LLM as a standalone optimizer. In each iteration, we prompt the LLM with the task description and a truncated history containing only the top-20 best-performing trials found so far. The LLM is instructed to identify patterns from these high-quality examples and propose a single new configuration for evaluation.

•

LLAMBO
(
31
)
.

LLAMBO replaces traditional BO components with LLM . Following the official implementation, we include the entire optimization history in the prompt (subject to context limits).
In each round, the LLM is tasked to generate candidates that improve upon the current best score.
The method generates 20 candidate configurations per iteration. It then randomly shuffles the order of historical examples 10 times to aggregate the predictions.
Consequently, each optimization round necessitates a total of 30 API calls.

•

BOPRO
(
1
)
.

BOPRO uses BO to optimize the prompt instructions themselves. For the in-context learning component, the prompt includes a set of 8 historical examples. In each iteration, the BO-selected instruction template guides the LLM to reason over these examples and generate a new hyperparameter configuration.

•

BORA
(
7
)
.

BORA dynamically switches between an LLM and a GP-BO proposer based on posterior variance. When the LLM is active, we follow the original paper’s setting by including the full optimization history in the prompt to maximize context awareness.

C.8
Ablation Study: Exploration and Exploitation Strategy

This appendix extends the ablation study on LB-MCTS’s exploration and exploitation strategies by investigating their impact on the distribution of proposed configurations.
We first analyze the
diversity of proposed configurations
of three strategies.
Unlike our previous analysis of ensemble diversity, which considered the predictive differences between models on the validation set, here we quantify diversity directly at the level of the proposed hyperparameter configurations themselves. This allows for a more direct behavioral analysis of how different strategies shape the search space.
Specifically, the diversity score is calculated independently for each algorithm. For a given algorithm
A^{i}
with a hyperparameter space
\Lambda^{i}
, the overall diversity score
D(A^{i})
is defined as the mean of the normalized diversity scores of all its hyperparameters:

D(A^{i})=\frac{1}{|\Lambda^{i}|}\sum_{p\in\Lambda^{i}}S(p)
,
where
S(p)
denotes the normalized diversity score for a specific parameter
p
. The calculation of
S(p)
depends on the parameter type (numeric or categorical).

•

Numeric Parameters.
For continuous or integer parameters (e.g., learning rate, max depth), we normalize sampled values to
x^{\prime}\in[0,1]
using linear or log-domain min-max scaling as appropriate. The diversity score
S_{num}(p)
is then defined as the standard deviation of these normalized samples:

S_{num}(p)=\text{std}(\{x^{\prime}_{i}\})
.

•

Categorical Parameters.

We measure categorical diversity using normalized Shannon entropy:

S_{cat}(p)=\frac{-\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_{k}\ln(q_{k})}{\ln(K)}
,
where
q_{k}
is the empirical probability of category
k
among
K
possibilities. This ensures
S_{cat}(p)\in[0,1]
, with 1 representing a perfectly uniform distribution (maximum diversity).

Figure

14(a)
presents the heatmap of diversity scores by algorithm across all 10 datasets.
Note that diversity is quantified per algorithm rather than across algorithms, preventing bias from different hyperparameter dimensionalities.
We can observe that
The
Exploration
Only strategy (middle row) exhibits the highest diversity across almost all algorithms, indicating a broad but potentially inefficient search that mimics random sampling.
The
Exploitation
Only strategy (bottom row) shows the lowest diversity, suggesting rapid convergence to a narrow region of the hyperparameter space, carrying the risk of getting trapped in local optima.
LB-MCTS (top row) maintains an intermediate diversity level. This confirms that our method effectively balances the trade-off, exploring sufficient configuration space before focusing resources on promising regions.

visualizes the evaluated configurations for the AdaBoost algorithm on the “qsar-biodeg” dataset using t-SNE, with LLM-proposed points marked as stars and warmup/BO proposals as circles.
Under the

Conversely, the

In contrast, LB-MCTS (left) generates a distribution that forms distinct, high-performing clusters (dark green stars) while retaining separation between them. This demonstrates that LB-MCTS successfully guides the search to exploit promising modes deeply while periodically exploring new regions to escape stagnation.

C.10
Ablation Study: Selective Tuning Memory

In this appendix, we validate the effectiveness of our Selective Tuning Memory (STM) mechanism. Unlike prior methods that mix history from different algorithms, a key prerequisite of our framework is the isolation of optimization history by algorithm to prevent cross-algorithm noise.
Building on this cleaner, algorithm-specific history, we adapt and thereby strengthen retrieval strategies from existing LLM-based optimizers for fair comparison:
(1) All: including the entire history of the current algorithm, mimicking the strategy of BORA and LLAMBO;
(2) Top-
k
: retrieving only the best-performing trials, mimicking OPRO (here we set
k=20
to match the context length of OPRO).
Next, we ablate the components of our STM design:
(3) Global Only: retaining only the Pareto-selected global memory;
(4) Local Only: retaining only the ancestral trajectory.
Note that
all variants operate on per-algorithm histories (no cross-algorithm mixing)
, so differences come purely from the retrieval strategy within each algorithm.
Finally, (5) we include a lower bound baseline, No Memory, which provides zero historical trials.

Figure
16
plots the normalized performance averaged over 10 datasets (detailed in Appendix
C.7
). Five observations can be concluded:
(i) LB-MCTS (full STM) outperforms all variants, achieving the
best final average rank of 1.80
.
(ii) We observe that strategies relying solely on high-performing samples—Global Only (Rank 2.30) and Top-
k
(Rank 2.80)—perform reasonably well but are suboptimal. This indicates that while knowing “where the best solutions are” is useful, lacking the context of “how we got here” (trajectory) limits the LLM’s reasoning ability.
In particular, trajectory information may help the LLM (a) avoid repeatedly moving back into regions that have already been explored and found unpromising, and (b) infer trends between parameter changes and performance changes—analogous to a coarse, history-based gradient signal—both of which are absent when only Global Memory are shown.
(iii) Global Only outperforms Top-
k
, confirming that retrieving trials based on the Pareto frontier of similarity and performance provides more relevant guidance to the current expansion task than simply selecting based on raw performance alone.
(iv) All (Rank 3.60) exposes the entire history and therefore includes the trajectory, but suffers from severe context pollution—many mediocre or redundant trials dilute the useful signal and consume the prompt budget.
(v) Finally, Only Local (Rank 4.30) and No Memory (Rank 5.40) yield the poorest results, underscoring that without a set of high-quality global references, the search struggles to efficiently locate optimal regions.
