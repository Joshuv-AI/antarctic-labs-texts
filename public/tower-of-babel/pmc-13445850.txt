Effectiveness without safety: a response to Duggan, Hardy, and Waller (2026)
Authors: Laurence Cobbaert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis commentary responds to Duggan, Hardy, and Waller’s systematic review and meta-analysis of cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) for outpatients with anorexia nervosa (AN). Although the review synthesises evidence of clinical benefit, it does not define, extract, or discuss iatrogenic harm or reasons for dropout. This omission limits what can be inferred from the review’s conclusions. Effectiveness and safety are distinct evidentiary domains: improvement in weight or eating disorder (ED) symptoms does not demonstrate absence of iatrogenic harm, especially when clinical trials have not systematically defined, monitored, or reported treatment-related risks. Evidence-based care requires not only evidence of effectiveness, but also evidence about safety. This commentary argues that evidence syntheses may produce an unduly favourable account of treatment impact when they quantify and emphasise benefit without equivalent attention to iatrogenesis, weakening informed consent and obscuring patient-reported experiences that may not be captured by symptom or weight outcomes alone. The paper considers methodological, clinical, ethical, epistemic, and intersectional implications of this asymmetry. It recommends that future ED clinical trials and systematic reviews evaluate benefit and harm together, undertake balanced assessments of benefits versus risks, use clearer harm definitions and patient-reported measures, report reasons for dropout, assess longer-term outcomes, and involve lived experience contributors substantively in study design, interpretation, and translation.Keywords: Anorexia nervosa, Cognitive-behavioural therapy, Treatment-related harm, Adverse events, Unwanted events, Patient experience, Epistemic justice, Relapse, Systematic review

Introduction

Background

Duggan, Hardy, and Waller present a systematic review and meta-analysis of cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) for outpatients with anorexia nervosa (AN) [1]. The review identified 26 studies and reported pre-to-post effects for weight, eating disorder (ED) symptoms, depression, anxiety, and quality of life, although the number of studies contributing data varied substantially by outcome [1]. Furthermore, across studies with calculable allocation and completer data, dropout was approximately 29.6% in aggregate and 31.5% as an unweighted study-level mean, with a median of 33.3% [1]. However, participants’ reasons for dropping out were not extracted or discussed, limiting interpretation of whether dropout reflected accessibility constraints, iatrogenic harm, or other acceptability-related concerns [1]. Duggan et al. concluded that the findings present an optimistic picture and that clinicians can expect good outcomes regardless of starting weight, age, or illness duration [1].

These conclusions require caution. CBT evidence claims are valid only within the populations, outcomes, and service contexts represented in the clinical trials, and should not be extended uncritically to under-represented groups, including trans and gender diverse individuals, neurodivergent people, and Indigenous, First Nations, and Māori communities [2]. Additionally, ED treatment benefit is not evidence of safety when iatrogenic harm remains underdefined, undermeasured, and underreported, and when patient reports of iatrogenesis are dismissed as resistance, non-compliance, lack of insight, or treatment-interfering behaviour [3].

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses matter because they can influence how treatments are perceived, recommended, funded, prescribed, delivered, and discussed across clinical, policy, and research settings [1, 4–6]. Findings of benefit therefore require careful interpretation: improvement in ED symptoms or weight does not, on its own, establish that a treatment is safe, adequately personalised, culturally valid, or appropriate for all groups to whom the findings may later be applied [4–12].

This distinction is especially important for AN. ED outcome research shows that remission, recovery, relapse, chronicity, and mortality are difficult to interpret because definitions, samples, treatment contexts, and follow-up periods vary across studies [5, 8, 9]. In Duggan et al.‘s review, follow-up meta-analyses were based on only 13 of 26 included studies for weight and 12 of 26 studies for ED symptoms; no follow-up analyses were reported for depression, anxiety, quality of life, adverse effects, negative treatment experiences, or iatrogenic harm [1]. Their conclusion that clinicians can broadly expect good outcomes therefore risks overstating what the available evidence can support [1, 5, 7–9].

The absence of harm-related outcomes is not a minor methodological detail. Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are designed to determine beneficial intervention effects, but clinical trials should also explicitly collect safety data about potential harmful effects so that the benefit-risk balance upon which the construct of ‘evidence-based care’ relies can be applied [4, 10]. Duggan et al. do not define, extract, or discuss iatrogenic harm, and they do not identify the absence of harm-related data from their included studies as a limitation [1]. In psychotherapy research, iatrogenic harm is often inconsistently defined, under-monitored, and underreported, creating an asymmetrical evidence base in which benefit is consistently prioritised and emphasised while iatrogenic harm remains comparatively invisible [3, 7, 11–20]. Such asymmetry may produce an unduly favourable picture of CBT by supporting expectations of benefit that are not balanced by equivalent evidence about safety and sustainability [3, 4, 7, 10–20].

Aim and scope of this commentary

This commentary examines what can and cannot be inferred when an effectiveness-focused review of CBT for AN evaluates measured benefit without a parallel safety data framework for identifying, extracting, or interpreting iatrogenic harm [1, 3–20]. It focuses on Duggan et al. as a recent synthesis of CBT for outpatients with AN, while situating that review within broader evidence on psychotherapy safety, ED outcomes, limited follow-up, relapse, dropout meaning, generalisability, adverse therapeutic processes, and lived-experience-led accounts of harm [1–25].

The central argument is that Duggan et al.’s review provides an incomplete account of treatment impact because measured benefit is synthesised while iatrogenic harm remains undefined, unextracted, and undiscussed [1]. This matters because an evidence base that quantifies benefit without equivalent attention to iatrogenic harm can produce an unduly favourable picture of treatment impact, weaken informed consent, and obscure patient-reported harms that may not be captured by ED-specific symptom or weight outcomes alone [3–20]. This commentary therefore examines the methodological, clinical, and ethical implications of evaluating CBT for AN through effectiveness outcomes alone, and argues that iatrogenic harm should be treated as a core outcome domain in both primary psychotherapy research and evidence synthesis.

Effectiveness is not safety

Benefit, iatrogenic harm, and safety inference

Effectiveness and safety are related but distinct evidentiary domains: improvement on intended outcomes does not demonstrate absence of iatrogenic harm [3, 4, 11, 12, 18]. Psychotherapy should not be assumed benign because it is non-pharmacological. Berk and Parker argued that psychotherapy, like any potent intervention, can help and harm, while Scott and Young argued that psychotherapies should be assessed for both benefit and harm [11, 12]. Junqueira et al. and Phillips et al. similarly emphasise that explicit data on iatrogenic harm in health research, including behavioural interventions, are necessary for interpreting intervention effects, communicating risk (e.g., health advertising and informed consent), and supporting person-centred clinical decision-making [4, 10]. Without such safety data, clinicians and patients receive an incomplete account of intervention impact, because benefit is quantified while potential risks of harm are left outside the evidentiary frame.

Psychotherapy literature also cautions against interpreting non-reporting as safety [13–20]. Reviews show that iatrogenic harm is inconsistently defined and rarely embedded in protocols or clinical trials [13–16]. Recent research further stresses that ‘no evidence of iatrogenic harm’ is not equivalent to ‘evidence of no iatrogenic harm’ [17]. Narrative and theoretical work describes negative events, delayed harm, and the role of language, labels, belief systems, and treatment culture in producing iatrogenic effects [18–20].

Definition and conceptual precision

Conceptual clarity is essential because unwanted events, adverse effects, treatment-related harm, iatrogenic harm, negative treatment experiences, dropout, non-response, relapse, inclusivity, accessibility, and acceptability are not interchangeable terms or concepts [3, 4, 7, 10, 13, 20]. Treatment-related harm usually refers to psychologically, relationally, culturally, epistemically, or physically significant harm plausibly associated with treatment, therapist behaviour, treatment processes, language, labelling, or broader care environment or service context [3, 11, 15, 18–21]. Iatrogenic harm is specifically most often used for harm directly produced or intensified by treatment or healthcare practices, including harms that may be delayed, cumulative, identity-based1, or relational rather than immediately visible as ED-specific symptom deterioration [3, 18–21].

These distinctions are not merely semantic. Dropout, non-response, relapse, patient-reported dissatisfaction, negative treatment experiences, adverse effects, treatment-related harm, and iatrogenic harm each have different methodological and clinical implications, and should not be treated as interchangeable. Dropout, for example, may reflect practical barriers, ambivalence, poor therapeutic fit, misattunement, treatment-related or iatrogenic harm, or unrelated life circumstances; it cannot be assumed to indicate either treatment failure or treatment harm without further assessment [12, 21, 26]. Similarly, harm cannot be evaluated if it is not clearly defined, actively elicited, and distinguished from symptom persistence that is not attributable to treatment processes [4, 13, 27, 28]. Measures such as the Psychotherapy Side Effects Scale and the Positive and Negative Experiences of Psychotherapy scale show that treatment-related harm can be operationalised, although such measures are not yet routinely used in ED clinical trials or reviews [27, 28].

Why Duggan et al.'s omission matters

What the review can and cannot show

Duggan et al. provide a synthesis of CBT effectiveness for outpatient AN, using weight and ED symptoms as primary outcomes and depression, anxiety, and quality of life as secondary outcomes [1]. These outcomes are clinically important, but they do not measure the full impact of treatment [1, 5, 7]. The review’s eligibility criteria required pre-to-post treatment scores for weight or ED symptoms, thereby centring measurable clinical change as the basis for inclusion [1]. However, safety-related outcomes, including treatment-related or iatrogenic harm, unwanted events, adverse effects, negative treatment experiences, reasons for dropout, relapse rates, and cultural safety, were not extracted as outcome domains, and their absence was not identified as a limitation [1]. Although Duggan et al. considered dropout as a post hoc moderator of treatment effects, dropout was not examined as a patient-experienced outcome, an indicator of treatment acceptability, a possible marker of therapeutic misattunement, or a potential signal of treatment-related or iatrogenic harm [1, 26].

The review’s limitations discuss English-language restriction, single-reviewer conduct, limited reporting of minimum outcome data, and heterogeneity [1]. However, the absence of explicit safety data, including treatment-related and iatrogenic harm, is not identified as a limitation [1]. This is problematic because the review may validly synthesise measured ED-specific clinical change, but it cannot establish safety if harm-related outcomes were not defined, monitored, extracted, or discussed [1, 4, 7, 11].

The same interpretive caution applies to generalisability. Duggan et al.’s conclusion that clinicians can expect good outcomes should be understood as bounded by the populations, outcome measures, and service contexts represented in the included studies, particularly given the significant underrepresentation of trans and gender diverse individuals, neurodivergent people, and racialised communities in ED clinical trials [1–3]. It should not be read as universal evidence that CBT is effective, safe, acceptable, adequately personalised, or culturally appropriate for all outpatients with AN [1–3]. This distinction is especially important when effectiveness conclusions may be translated into service expectations, referral pathways, treatment recommendations, healthcare advertising, and patient-facing claims about what CBT can be expected to achieve. Read broadly, such conclusions may also imply a one-size-fits-all model of CBT for AN, despite evidence-based practice requiring attention to patient preferences and values, clinical expertise, context, and the limits of the populations represented in the evidence base [1, 2, 34].

Iatrogenesis monitoring and follow-up

The omission is consequential because iatrogenesis monitoring in AN psychotherapy clinical trials, including CBT, is already limited [7]. Kinnaird et al. found substantial inconsistency in definitions, monitoring, and reporting of unwanted events, and many clinical trials provided insufficient information to determine whether unwanted events were assessed; for example, only one out of 23 included studies discussed patient-reported satisfaction with psychological treatment for AN [7]. For systematic reviews, this means that the absence of safety data about iatrogenic harm should not be treated as reassuring or reflecting optimism; it should be interpreted as uncertainty and reported as such [4, 7, 13]. ‘No iatrogenic harm reported’ is not equivalent to ‘harms were systematically monitored and not found’ [4, 7, 13]. Where primary studies do not provide consistent safety data related to iatrogenic harm, a review cannot determine whether harms were absent, unmeasured, unreported, or measured in ways that were not extractable.

Limited follow-up in clinical trials compounds this problem. Duggan et al.‘s follow-up analyses were restricted to weight and ED symptoms and were based on approximately half of the included studies [1]. Yet iatrogenic harm in psychological treatment may emerge during treatment, after termination, or through longer-term changes in trust, self-understanding, help-seeking, relationships, identity, or service engagement [3, 11, 13–25]. Lived experience-led research illustrates how cumulative iatrogenic harm can become visible later as avoidance of future healthcare rather than immediate and straightforward ED symptom deterioration [2, 3, 17, 22, 25].

Broader ED clinical research reinforces the need for caution [5, 6, 8, 9]. Miskovic-Wheatley et al. found that ED clinical research remains difficult to interpret because definitions of remission, recovery, relapse, and diagnostic crossover vary, and they recommended focused long-term follow-up research, particularly for AN [8]. Danielsen et al. found that symptoms improved after inpatient ED treatment for most participants, but only about one-third were in remission at follow-up [9]. Solmi et al. similarly found that, even following evidence-based treatments, rates of successful ED treatment remain limited, with low remission, incomplete or partial recovery, relapse risk, and high mortality, particularly for AN [5].

Ethical and epistemic implications

Distress, treatment burden, and iatrogenic harm

AN treatment may involve distressing experiences related to behavioural change, nutritional rehabilitation, medical monitoring, and, in some circumstances, compulsory intervention [32, 33]. An iatrogenesis-informed approach should therefore not equate all distress, discomfort, or treatment burden during ED treatment with iatrogenic harm [32–34]. However, behavioural change or treatment retention should not automatically be interpreted as genuine, sustainable therapeutic progress, nor as evidence that iatrogenic harm is absent. Such behavioural change may reflect meaningful recovery-oriented engagement, but it may also reflect limited alternatives, fear of autonomy loss, including inpatient admission and compulsory enteral or parenteral feeding, fear of being labelled non-compliant or failing treatment, or behavioural compliance shaped by fear of therapist rejection, anger, withdrawal of care, or other trauma-related appeasement responses [3, 22, 23, 29–33]. Tan and colleagues’ work on control, compulsory treatment, and decision-making capacity is important because it shows that treatment may be experienced as coercive or autonomy-limiting while still being clinically complex, particularly where starvation and capacity intersect [32, 33].

Evidence-based practice requires integration of research evidence, clinical expertise, and patient values, but it also fundamentally requires sufficient evidence to evaluate whether expected benefits outweigh possible risks of harm [4, 12, 34]. RCTs and evidence syntheses therefore need to assess benefit and harm together, because treatment decisions rely on accurate knowledge of both domains, and poor harms reporting can create a false impression of safety [4, 12, 17]. In the context of EDs, this means evaluating not only whether an intervention improves ED symptoms or weight, but also how it is delivered, whether informed consent is respected, and whether the person experiences care as safe, adequately personalised, attuned, culturally congruent, meaningful, and sustainable [2, 3, 22, 23, 29–35]. It also requires attention to the possibility that the same intervention may not cause iatrogenic harm in one demographic or environmental context, may be burdensome but ethically justified in another, and may be harmful in another [2, 3].

Harm reduction debates in the context of severe and longstanding AN illustrate this balance. Bianchi et al. argue that harm reduction may be ethically defensible for some people with severe and enduring AN when full symptom remission is unlikely, the person has capacity, and the aim is to reduce medical risk while preserving quality of life [36]. Birch et al. caution that harm reduction may be part of the journey but should not become managed decline or a substitute for hope and recovery-oriented care where such care remains possible [37]. Yager similarly warns that repeated, unrealistic recovery demands after multiple unsuccessful experiences of treatment may generate shame and iatrogenic harm [38]. Recent ED research also suggests that harm reduction may be relevant during treatment delay, treatment ambivalence, and ongoing risk during active treatment [39].

Patient experience and epistemic justice

The under-recognition of treatment-related and iatrogenic harm has epistemic justice and accountability implications [3, 19, 26, 29, 30]. Qualitative and lived experience-led ED research describes treatment-related trauma, misattunement, cultural incongruence, invalidation, severe mental health deterioration beyond ED symptoms alone, loss of hope, internalised shame, neglect, dignity violations, and iatrogenic harm associated with long-standing consequences, such as long-term avoidance of healthcare services altogether, that may not be captured by standard clinical trial outcomes and may occur even when treatment improves weight or ED symptoms2 [22–25, 29–31, 35, 40–42]. Across this scholarship, people who report iatrogenic harm describe being met with defensiveness, denial, and dismissiveness, or having their accounts reframed as resistance, defiance, manipulation, exaggeration, attention-seeking, being difficult, non-compliance, lack of insight, lack of motivation to recover, or treatment-interfering behaviour, rather than with acknowledgement, compassion, accountability, and opportunities for repair-oriented practice and healing [3, 29–31, 35, 40].

Duggan et al.’s review illustrates how these concerns can arise at the level of evidence synthesis: measured benefits are extracted and made visible, while treatment-related and iatrogenic harm remain outside the review’s analytic frame [1]. When evidence syntheses make benefit visible but leave treatment-related and iatrogenic harm unexamined and unacknowledged, ED clinicians and services may receive an incomplete picture of treatment impact and may be less equipped to understand what iatrogenic harm is, how it can present, what consequences it may have, how to identify or mitigate it, and how to respond adequately when it happens [1, 3, 7, 13–17]. This can make patient reports of iatrogenic harm more likely to be minimised, disbelieved, misinterpreted, or dismissed rather than recognised as clinically valid and epistemically legitimate [3, 22, 29, 30, 35, 40].

Intersectionality

These concerns are amplified for marginalised groups because evidence generated from narrow clinical samples, or from samples whose demographic and identity characteristics are insufficiently described, cannot be assumed to establish safety, relevance, acceptability, or overall benefit for people whose identities, communication styles, cultural, racial, or ethnic contexts, body types, or support needs are under-represented in the evidence base [2, 3, 17]. CBT-related evidence should therefore not be universalised as if it was broadly applicable to neurodivergent people, trans and gender diverse people, Indigenous, First Nations, Māori, or other culturally, ethnically, or racially minoritised communities unless those groups are represented and subgroup-specific outcomes, harms, acceptability, and treatment experiences are collaboratively assessed [2, 3, 17, 43–45].

Work on Indigenous perspectives, neurodiversity-affirming psychotherapy, and gender-affirming approaches in psychotherapy shows how harm can arise when treatment models, such as CBT and exposure therapy, rely on culturally incongruent, including Eurocentric, social norms and assumptions, neuronormative expectations, identity pathologisation, withholding or delaying gender-affirming care, or coercive institutional power [43–45]. These are not peripheral concerns: they shape whether treatment is inclusive, attuned, accessible, safe, and ethically defensible for the individuals who receive it [2, 3, 17].

For example, Spadaccini and Schweizer describe inpatient ED care for a young trans man in which distress about puberty was pathologised as an ED-related “irrational fear” rather than recognised as gender dysphoria; his wish for gender-affirming care was treated as unrealistic and symptomatic of psychiatric pathology; and exposure-based reasoning was used to make him “face” pubertal development while gender-affirming care was withheld, despite his disordered eating behaviours functioning to prevent puberty and reduce gender dysphoria [45]. In this context, exposure became entangled with gender identity conversion practices by treating pubertal development as something to be imposed rather than as something requiring gender-affirming assessment and care, producing severe and enduring psychological harm as well as prolonged disengagement from and loss of trust towards healthcare providers and services [45]. This example illustrates how clinical trials of exposure-based or behavioural interventions may fail to capture significant risks of iatrogenic harm when they do not assess intersectionality, identity-related factors, cultural safety, and iatrogenic harm [2, 3, 7, 17, 45].

Elwyn similarly describes ED treatment, including outpatient CBT, that was not gender-affirming, strengths-based, or neurodiversity-affirming, despite neurodivergence, LGBTIQ+ identity, stigma, systemic discrimination, and identity-based violence being central to the development and maintenance of their AN [35]. In that account, clinicians’ conceptualisations of AN erased and invalidated the meaning of Elwyn’s personal lived experiences, including by reframing gender dysphoria-related disordered eating and autistic communication differences or movement as illness-driven resistance, regression, avoidance, or attempts to undermine treatment [35].

Implications for future clinical trials and reviews

Future clinical trials in the context of EDs, including CBT, should evaluate treatment benefit and risks of harm together [3, 4, 7, 10–21]. Iatrogenic harm monitoring should not be limited to weight loss or worsening ED symptoms; it should also assess patient-reported satisfaction with treatment, unwanted events, adverse effects, relational rupture, coercion, cultural incongruence, identity-related harms, reasons for dropout, treatment inclusivity and acceptability, relapse, and delayed harm at follow-up [3, 7, 21–25, 29–31].

The Positive and Negative Experiences of Psychotherapy scale offers one example of how this gap could begin to be addressed because it assesses both positive and negative psychotherapy experiences [28]. It asks patients whether specific positive or negative experiences occurred, whether they attributed them to therapy, and includes negative-experience domains such as symptom escalation, emotional distress, low-quality therapy or therapeutic alliance, stigma, loss of trust in the therapist, and deterioration in self-esteem, daily functioning, or relational functioning [28]. Because the scale was not developed specifically for ED treatment contexts, it would likely require adaptation and validation to capture ED-specific risks of harm related to weight stigma, nutritional rehabilitation, coercion, cultural incongruence, identity-related harm, relapse, and avoidance of healthcare services [3, 7, 22–25, 28–31]. Such adapted measures could help CBT clinical trials move beyond ED symptom-only assessment by systematically eliciting patient-reported experiences of and satisfaction with treatment and making iatrogenic harm more visible [3, 7, 28].

Clinical trials should also assess longer-term outcomes, because end-of-treatment improvement cannot establish sustained recovery, absence of later emerging iatrogenic harm, or overall treatment value [5, 7–9, 34]. Follow-up should be long enough to detect relapse, loss of trust in healthcare services, identity-related harm, service avoidance, and other consequences that may not be visible at discharge or immediately after treatment completion.

Future systematic reviews should extract whether primary studies monitored iatrogenic harm, how it was defined, which measures were used to assess it, whether assessment occurred during treatment, at follow-up, or both, whether patient-reported harms were elicited, and whether reasons for participants’ dropout were analysed [4, 7, 10, 13, 21, 28]. Where these data are absent, reviews should identify that absence as an interpretive limitation rather than implying safety [3, 4, 7, 13, 17]. Reviews should also assess whether included samples represent the entirety of the demographic groups to whom findings are later generalised, including neurodivergent, trans and gender diverse, Indigenous, First Nations, and Māori patients [2, 43–48]. Where such groups are absent, under-represented, or not described, reviews should avoid implying that average effects establish safety, relevance, acceptability, cultural validity, or suitability for those populations.

Lived experience involvement in ED-related clinical trials of psychotherapies, including CBT, can strengthen outcome selection, interpretation, translational value, and real-world relevance, but it must be substantive rather than tokenistic [46–50]. The purpose of co-design is not only to make research more acceptable, but to improve the questions asked, outcomes selected, harms anticipated, findings interpreted, and translation of results into care that is meaningful and safe to those most affected [46–50]. In CBT clinical trials and reviews, lived experience contributors may help identify treatment-related harms, participation barriers, reasons for dropout, culturally or identity-specific concerns, and specific practices that are meaningful, acceptable, and sustainable beyond ED symptoms or weight outcomes alone [46–49].

Meaningful lived experience partnership also requires attention to process and power. Elwyn et al. argue that ethical partnership in ED research requires recognising lived experience as expertise, sharing power and decision-making, supporting autonomy and dignity of risk, clarifying roles and disclosure choices, providing fair remuneration and authorship, building emotionally safe ways of working, practising reflexivity and transparency, and ensuring mutual benefit and capacity building [46]. Maguire et al. conceptualise co-production in ED research as a power-sharing approach in which lived experience, clinical, and research collaborators collectively shape priority setting, research questions, study design, interpretation, and translation, rather than being consulted only after the agenda has been set [47].

Furthermore, Miskovic-Wheatley et al. show that although lived experience involvement in ED research is increasing, implementation remains uneven, with variable reporting, limited reflexivity, power imbalances, difficulty assessing representativeness, and a need for clearer guidance [48]. They also emphasise that lived experience involvement can improve outcome selection, identify overlooked iatrogenic harm, strengthen iatrogenic harm assessment and reporting, and ensure that patient-relevant experiences are not excluded from knowledge production and evidence generation [48]. Musić et al. likewise argue that lived experience involvement can identify overlooked negative outcomes, highlight barriers to participation, improve the sensitivity and comprehensibility of research procedures, provide insight into findings, increase trust, and support real-world significance [49].

Conclusions

Duggan et al. provide useful evidence about measured benefits of outpatient CBT in the context of AN, but their review cannot establish safety, sustained recovery, absence of iatrogenic harm, or overall treatment value because these domains were not systematically assessed [1]. The absence of a harm framework is not merely a missing topic; it is a critical and consequential methodological limitation that should be acknowledged when interpreting the review’s conclusions [1, 3, 4, 7, 12, 13, 17].

This commentary does not negate evidence of CBT benefit [1, 5, 6]. It clarifies that effectiveness claims are partial when they rely on an asymmetrical evidence base in which benefits are measured more consistently than risks of harm [3, 4, 7, 11–21]. Future clinical trials and reviews should evaluate benefit, treatment-related and iatrogenic harm, follow-up, relapse, acceptability, and patient experience together so that ED treatment evidence can provide a more balanced account of treatment impact and better support safe, ethical, culturally responsive, and person-centred care.
