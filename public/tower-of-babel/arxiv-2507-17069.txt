The Generalized Matrix Separation Problem: Algorithms
Xuemei Chen and Owen Deen

Abstract
When given a generalized matrix separation problem, which aims to recover a low rank matrix L 0 L_{0} and a sparse matrix S 0 S_{0} from M 0 = L 0 + H ​ S 0 M_{0}=L_{0}+HS_{0} , the work [ 7 ] proposes a novel convex optimization problem whose objective function is the sum of the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm and nuclear norm. In this paper we detail the iterative algorithms and its associated computations for solving this convex optimization problem. We present various efficient implementation strategies, with attention to practical cases where H H is circulant, separable, or block structured. Notably, we propose a preconditioning technique that drastically improved the performance of our algorithms in terms of efficiency, accuracy, and robustness. While this paper serves as an illustrative algorithm implementation manual, we also provide theoretical guarantee for our preconditioning strategy. Numerical results illustrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach.

Introduction

Given a known matrix
M_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we aim to decompose it as the sum of a low-rank matrix
L_{0}
and a
filtered
sparse matrix
S_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times n}
:

M_{0}=L_{0}+HS_{0}.

(1)

As shown in (
1
), the sparse matrix component is filtered or masked by a known linear transformation
H\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times p}
, and the product
HS_{0}
is not necessarily sparse.

The separation problem (
1
) was first raised in
[
7
]
, which also proposes the following convex optimization problem to recover
L_{0}
and
S_{0}
, given that
M_{0}
and
H
are known:

(\hat{S},\hat{L})=\argmin_{S,L}\lambda\|S\|_{1}+\|L\|_{*},\quad\text{subject to }L+HS=M_{0},

(2)

where
\|\cdot\|_{*}
is the nuclear norm, a convex relaxation of the matrix rank, and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is the vectorized
\ell_{1}
-norm, a convex relaxation of sparsity.

When
H
is the identity matrix, the problem reduces to the standard robust PCA
[
6
,
5
]
formulation:

(\hat{S},\hat{L})=\argmin_{S,L}\lambda\|S\|_{1}+\|L\|_{*},\quad\text{subject to }L+S=M_{0}.

(3)

There are many algorithms for solving the robust PCA problem (
3
). The alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)
[
1
]
algorithm is a popular choice
[
18
,
19
]
. A nonconvex algorithm based on alternating projections is presented in
[
14
]
, and an accelerated version that significantly improves accuracy is proposed in
[
2
]
. The work in
[
3
]
proposes another nonconvex algorithm using CUR decomposition.

The matrix
H
can be of any shape and may be singular. However, we generally require
H
to behave well enough that it is injective on
\Omega(S_{0})
, the set of all
p\times n
matrices whose support lies within the support of
S_{0}
. We refer the reader to
[
7
]
for more details.

The convex optimization problem (
2
) is novel, and in this paper, we address the iterative steps for solving (
2
) using ADMM in detail. ADMM was introduced in
[
11
]
and
[
9
]
, and gained popularity due to its effectiveness in large-scale data analysis and its applications in signal processing and machine learning. We refer the reader to
[
1
]
for an excellent review.

The contributions of this paper are twofold:

•

We provide detailed and efficient algorithms for solving (
2
), along with implementable pseudocode. Practical special cases, such as circulant
H
, are addressed. Moreover, Section
5
examines the case where
M
arises from a tensor, which introduces memory and computational challenges. To address this, we focus our computations on separable filters that require fewer bits to store and allow for faster application to data.

•

We propose a preconditioning technique that significantly improves performance in terms of both efficiency and accuracy. Theorem
4.3
provides a theoretical guarantee for using the preconditioned filter under certain incoherence conditions on
H
,
L_{0}
, and
S_{0}
. Based on this technique,
we recommend Algorithm
5
for the general matrix separation problem
and Algorithm
10
for the specialized 3D case. Additionally, we provide parameter choices based on extensive numerical experiments.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2
introduces the notation and necessary preliminaries. Section
3
details the mathematical framework for the matrix separation problem, followed by the preconditioning technique in Section
4
and its extension to video data in Section
5
. Extensive numerical results are presented in Section
6
. Section
6.1
uses four experiments to demonstrate the superior performance of the preconditioning technique. Section
6.2
explores the maximum rank and sparsity allowed with two different choices of
H
and three sparsity models. Section
6.3
applies our algorithm to a simultaneous background removal and deblurring problem on real video data. We end with a discussion including future work.

We use lowercase letters such as

Let
{\mathbb{F}}

{\mathbb{R}}

\ell_{p}

For a matrix

•

The

\|X\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_{ij}|^{2}}
.

•

Both the

are based on its vectorization. That is,

\|X\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_{ij}|
, and
\|X\|_{\infty}=\max_{i,j}|x_{ij}|
.

•

The
nuclear norm
of

is
\|X\|_{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}
, where

.

•

The
spectral norm
of
X
is denoted by
\|X\|
. It is the largest singular value of
X
.

The transpose of
X
is denoted by
X^{\top}
, and its conjugate transpose by
X^{*}
. For a matrix
X=(x_{ij})\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
, the vector of diagonal elements is defined as
{\rm{diag}}(X)=[x_{11},x_{22},\ldots,x_{nn}]^{\top}
. Conversely, given a vector
v
,

is the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are given by

. We let

denote the

The

and defined elementwise as

. Likewise, we use

For a vector

Definition 2.1

(Soft Thresholding Operator)
.

The
soft thresholding operator
with parameter
\lambda
, denoted by

, is a mapping from
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For any

, the operator is defined as

(S_{\lambda}(y))_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{i}-\lambda&\text{if }y_{i}>\lambda,\\
y_{i}+\lambda&\text{if }y_{i}<-\lambda,\\

It is well known that

S_{\lambda}(a)=\argmin_{b\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\lambda|b|+\frac{1}{2}(b-a)^{2}\right).

(4)

For a matrix
M
,
S_{\lambda}(M)
is to apply soft thresholding to each entry of the matrix
M
.

Definition 2.2

(Singular value thresholding operator)
.

Given a matrix
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we denote
D_{\rho}(Y)
as the

D_{\rho}(Y)=US_{\rho}(\Sigma)V^{\top},\text{ where }Y=U\Sigma V^{\top}\text{ is any singular value decomposition of }Y.

Analogous to (
4
), the following result is well established.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
4
]
)
.

For
\lambda>0
and
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, then

D_{\lambda}(Y)=\argmin_{X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}}\left(\lambda||X||_{*}+\frac{1}{2}||X-Y||^{2}_{F}\right).

2.1
Properties of the Kronecker Product

For
A\in{\mathbb{F}}^{m\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{F}^{p\times q}
, the
Kronecker product

A\otimes B
is an
mp\times nq
matrix defined by

A\otimes B=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}\cdot B&a_{12}\cdot B&\cdots&a_{1n}\cdot B\\
a_{21}\cdot B&a_{22}\cdot B&\cdots&a_{2n}\cdot B\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{m1}\cdot B&a_{m2}\cdot B&\cdots&a_{mn}\cdot B\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Let
X\in{\mathbb{F}}^{q\times n}
, then

(A\otimes B)\vecc(X)=\vecc(BXA^{\top}).

(5)

Below are well-known properties of the Kronecker product.

1.

Mixed-product property:
If there exists matrices
C,D
that can multiply with
A
and
B
respectively, then

(A\otimes B)\cdot(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD).

2.

Inverse of a Kronecker product:
If
A
and
B
are invertible, then
(A\otimes B)^{-1}=A^{-1}\otimes B^{-1}
.

3.

Transpose of a Kronecker product:

\displaystyle\text{Transpose: }(A\otimes B)^{\top}=A^{\top}\otimes B^{\top}.

\displaystyle\text{Conjugate Transpose: }(A\otimes B)^{*}=A^{*}\otimes B^{*}.

The following lemma is a straightforward computation.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times m}
be diagonal matrices. Then

\im_{m,n}({\rm{diag}}(A\otimes B))={\rm{diag}}(B)[{\rm{diag}}(A)]^{\top}

(6)

Proof.

Let
{\rm{diag}}(A)=[a_{1},a_{2}\cdots,a_{n}]^{\top}
and
{\rm{diag}}(B)=[b_{1},b_{2}\cdots,b_{m}]^{\top}
.
We have

{\rm{diag}}(A\otimes B)=[a_{1}b_{1},\cdots,a_{1}b_{m},\cdots,a_{n}b_{1},\cdots,a_{n}b_{m}]^{\top}.

By definition of
\im(\cdot)
,

\displaystyle\im_{m,n}({\rm{diag}}(A\otimes B))=\im_{m,n}\left(\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}B\\
&a_{2}B\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&a_{n}B\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}b_{1}&\cdots&a_{n}b_{1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{1}b_{m}&\cdots&a_{n}b_{m}\end{bmatrix},

which is exactly the right hand side of (
6
).
∎

2.2
Circulant Matrix and Fourier Transform

Definition 2.5
.

A
circulant matrix
is a square matrix in which each column (or row) is a cyclic permutation of the first column (or row) vector. More formally, if the first column of the circulant matrix is given by the vector
c=[c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}]^{\top}
, then the circulant matrix
C
is defined as

C=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&c_{n}&c_{n-1}&\cdots&c_{2}\\
c_{2}&c_{1}&c_{n}&\cdots&c_{3}\\
c_{3}&c_{2}&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{4}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{n}&c_{n-1}&c_{n-2}&\cdots&c_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

Let
F_{n}
be the
n\times n
Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) matrix, defined by

F_{n}[j,k]=\omega^{jk},\quad\text{for $j,k=0,1,\cdots,n-1$},

where
\omega=e^{-2\pi i/n}
. Then
FF^{*}=nI_{n}
.

If
c
is the first column of a circulant matrix
C
, then it is well known that

FCx=(Fc)\odot(Fx)={\rm{diag}}(Fc)Fx.

In other words,
C
can be diagonalized by the DFT:

F_{n}CF_{n}^{*}=n{\rm{diag}}(F_{n}c).

(7)

Furthermore,

F_{n}C^{*}CF_{n}^{*}=\frac{1}{n}F_{n}C^{*}F_{n}^{*}F_{n}CF_{n}^{*}=\frac{1}{n}(nD)^{*}(nD)=nD^{*}D,\quad\text{where }D={\rm{diag}}(F_{n}c).

(8)

2.3
ADMM and LASSO

Given convex functions
f
and
g
, ADMM seeks to solve the problem

\min_{x,z}\{f(x)+g(z)\},\quad\text{subject to }Ax+Bz=c.

(9)

The algorithm is given below with an appropriately chosen parameter
\rho

[
1
, (3.5)–(3.7)]
:

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{x}\left(f(x)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|Ax+Bz^{k}-c+u^{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle z^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{z}\left(g(z)+\frac{\rho}{2}\|Ax^{k+1}+Bz-c+u^{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle u^{k+1}

\displaystyle=u^{k}+Ax^{k+1}+Bz^{k+1}-c.

(12)

ADMM has found applications in a variety of settings, including model fitting and classification, as well as signal and image processing. Part of its appeal is that the algorithm’s updates lend themselves to parallel implementation.

The Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)
[
16
]
solves the following optimization problem:

\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times l}}\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|x\|_{1},

(13)

where
\lambda>0
balances data fitting with regularization. If
\lambda
is close to 0, then the solution
x
will be similar to the ordinary least squares solution
[
12
]
.

We recast (
13
) as

(\hat{x},\hat{z})=\argmin_{x,z}\frac{1}{2}\|Ax-b\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|z\|_{1},\qquad\text{subject to }x-z=0,

which is a special case of ADMM. Then, (
10
)–(
12
) become

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle=(A^{\top}A+\rho I)^{-1}(A^{\top}b+\rho(z^{k}-u^{k}))

(14)

\displaystyle z^{k+1}

\displaystyle=S_{\lambda/\rho}(x^{k+1}+u^{k})

(15)

\displaystyle u^{k+1}

\displaystyle=u^{k}+x^{k+1}-z^{k+1}.

(16)

The first step (
14
) is to solve the linear system:

\displaystyle(A^{\top}A+\rho I)x^{k+1}=A^{\top}b+\rho(z^{k}-u^{k}).

(17)

Since the same coefficient matrix,
A^{\top}A+\rho I
, is used repeatedly, we gain efficiency by pre-factorizing it.

For example, we can precompute the SVD of
A^{\top}A
as
A^{\top}A=V\Sigma V^{\top}
, where
V
is a square matrix. Then,

A^{\top}A+\rho I=V(\Sigma+\rho I)V^{\top},

and the solution to
(A^{\top}A+\rho I)x=r
is

x=V(\Sigma+\rho I)^{-1}V^{\top}r.

This is efficient to compute since
\Sigma+\rho I
is diagonal. This leads to Algorithm
1

14
)–(

).

3
Iterative Steps for the Generalized Matrix Separation Problem

The convex optimization problem (
2
) is well suited to be solved by ADMM since the objective function is a sum of two convex functions, allowing it to be reduced to solving two simpler subproblems.
Using the scaled form (3.5)–(3.7) of
[
1
]
, our three steps are:

\displaystyle L^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{L}\left(\|L\|_{*}+\rho/2\|L+HS^{k}-M+U^{k}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle S^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{S}\left(\lambda\|S\|_{1}+\rho/2\|L^{k+1}+HS-M+U^{k}\|_{F}^{2}\right)

(20)

\displaystyle U^{k+1}

\displaystyle=U^{k}+L^{k+1}+HS^{k+1}-M.

(21)

Note that the nuclear norm and
\ell_{1}
-norm terms are handled independently, allowing each subproblem to be solved efficiently.
The dual variable is then updated using the residual of the constraint
L+HS=M_{0}
.

The solution to (
19
) is exact and given by
L^{k+1}=D_{\rho^{-1}}(-HS^{k}+M-U^{k})
according to Theorem
2.3
.

The problem (
20
) does not have a direct solution. Rewriting (
20
), we express the update for
S^{k+1}
as

S^{k+1}=\argmin_{S}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\rho}\|S\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\|HS-P\|_{F}^{2}\right),

(22)

where
P=-L^{k+1}+M-U^{k}
. This now takes the form of a LASSO problem as detailed in Section
2.3
. Algorithm
4
summarizes these three steps.

4
Preconditioning of
H

The convergence rate of ADMM (equations (
10
)–(
12
)) depends on the properties of the objective functions and constraints. The work
[
8
]
demonstrates a linear convergence rate of ADMM and its generalized version under various conditions, provided that at least one of the two objective functions is strongly convex. A linear convergence rate was also proven in
[
15
]
using a dynamical system approach, where strong convexity of either
f
or
g
is again assumed.

For a differentiable function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
, it is
strongly convex
with a constant
\alpha>0
if

\langle x-y,\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\rangle\geq\alpha\|x-y\|_{2}^{2},\quad\text{for all }x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

(23)

This definition can be generalized to non-smooth convex functions where the gradient is replaced by the subgradient.

It is easy to verify that
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|Hx-b\|_{2}^{2}
is strongly convex when
H
has full column rank. Given
\nabla f(x)=H^{\top}(Hx-b)
, we can compute that (
23
) holds with
\alpha
being the smallest singular value of
H^{\top}H
.

The results in
[
8
]
or
[
15
]
do not apply to (
2
) since neither
\|\cdot\|_{*}
nor
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is strongly convex. However, they do apply to the LASSO problem because one of the objective functions is
\frac{1}{2}\|Hx-b\|_{2}^{2}
, which is strongly convex as mentioned above. The convergence of (
20
) (LASSO) greatly affects the convergence of Algorithm
4
. In particular, using
[
8
, Corollary 3.6]
, we see that the convergence rate of Algorithm
1
depends on the condition number of
H
(the ratio of the largest to smallest singular value of
H
).
This motivates our preconditioning technique which we introduce below.

Let
U_{H}\Sigma_{H}V_{H}^{\top}
be the SVD of
H
, where
U_{H}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times{\rm{rank}}(H)},V_{H}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p\times{\rm{rank}}(H)}
. If we left multiply the constraint equation (
1
) with the positive semidefinite matrix
C=U_{H}\Sigma_{H}^{-1}U_{H}^{\top}
, then we have

CM_{0}=CHS_{0}+CL_{0}.

(24)

The new filter is
\tilde{H}=CH=U_{H}V_{H}^{\top}
. Like
H
, the preconditioned
\tilde{H}
is not necessarily full row rank or full column rank, but
\tilde{H}
has condition number 1 (when restricted onto row space of
H
).

The idea is to employ Algorithm
4
with inputs
CM_{0}
and
\tilde{H}
(instead of
M_{0}
and
H
), which will return approximations of
S_{0}
and
CL_{0}
. Since
C
is not necessarily invertible, recovery of
L_{0}
(if desired) can be achieved using the constraint (
1
).

To summarize, under this preconditioning strategy, the recovered sparse and low-rank components
\hat{S}_{c}
and
\hat{L}_{c}
are computed as

\begin{cases}(\hat{S}_{c},\hat{Y})=\displaystyle\argmin_{S,Y}\gamma\|S\|_{1}+\|Y\|_{*},\quad\text{subject to }Y+\tilde{H}S=CM_{0},\\
\hat{L}_{c}=M_{0}-H\hat{S}_{c}.\end{cases}

(25)

The preconditioning steps are explicitly listed in Algorithm
5
for completeness.

In this section, we discuss the details of applying Algorithm

Let

be a three-dimensional tensor. We denote its

We define the

1

Even for a small video, the filter

Definition 5.1
.

For matrices

\displaystyle H\mat({\mathcal{X}})=(G_{2}\otimes G_{1})\mat({\mathcal{X}})=\mat(\mathfrak{B}_{G_{1},G_{2}^{\top}}({\mathcal{X}})).

(34)

Definition 5.2
.

Let

\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{F}^{m_{1}\times m_{2}\times K}

\mathfrak{D}_{Y}(\mathcal{X})[:,:,k]=\mathcal{X}[:,:,k]\oslash Y,\quad\text{for }k=1,2,\dots,K,

We consider the following optimization problem:

\hat{\mathcal{X}}=\argmin_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left(\lambda\|\mat({\mathcal{X}})\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\|H\mat({\mathcal{X}})-\mat(\mathcal{B})\|_{F}^{2}\right),

(36)

where we define
D_{i}=\mathrm{diag}(F_{n_{i}}E_{i}[:,1])
and
L_{i}:=I_{k_{i}}\otimes D_{i}^{*}D_{i}
.
Consequently, the expression for
G_{2}^{\top}G_{2}\otimes G_{1}^{\top}G_{1}
becomes:

\displaystyle(F_{B_{2}}\otimes F_{B_{1}})(G_{2}^{\top}G_{2}\otimes G_{1}^{\top}G_{1})(F_{B_{2}}\otimes F_{B_{1}})^{*}=n_{1}n_{2}(L_{2}\otimes L_{1}).

(46)

We apply
F_{B_{2}}\otimes F_{B_{1}}
to both sides of equation (
40
) (where
R
denotes the right-hand side):

\displaystyle(F_{B_{2}}\otimes F_{B_{1}})(G_{2}^{\top}G_{2}\otimes G_{1}^{\top}G_{1}+\rho I_{m_{1}m_{2}})\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}(F_{B_{2}}\otimes F_{B_{1}})^{*}(F_{B_{2}}\otimes F_{B_{1}})X=(F_{B_{2}}\otimes F_{B_{1}})R,

(47)

where we use the property
(F_{B_{2}}\otimes F_{B_{1}})^{*}(F_{B_{2}}\otimes F_{B_{1}})=(n_{1}n_{2})I_{m_{1}m_{2}}
.

Incorporating the diagonalization result from (
46
), the system simplifies to

\displaystyle(L_{2}\otimes L_{1}+\rho)(F_{B_{2}}\otimes F_{B_{1}})X=(F_{B_{2}}\otimes F_{B_{1}})R,

(48)

which can be solved efficiently via FFT since
L_{2}\otimes L_{1}
is diagonal. We can simplify
L_{2}\otimes L_{1}
even further. By Lemma
2.4
,

\displaystyle L:=\im_{m_{1},m_{2}}({\rm{diag}}(L_{2}\otimes L_{1}))={\rm{diag}}(L_{1})[{\rm{diag}}(L_{2})]^{\top}.

Let
d_{i}={\rm{diag}}(D_{i}^{*}D_{i})
, which is a vector of length
n_{i}
. Then,

L_{i}=I_{k_{i}}\otimes{\rm{diag}}(d_{i}),\text{ and }{\rm{diag}}(L_{i})^{\top}=\underbrace{[d_{i}^{\top},d_{i}^{\top},\cdots,d_{i}^{\top}]}_{\text{$k_{i}$ times}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle L=\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}\\
d_{1}\\
\vdots\\
d_{1}\end{bmatrix}[d_{2}^{\top},d_{2}^{\top},\cdots,d_{2}^{\top}]=\begin{bmatrix}d_{1}d_{2}^{\top}&\cdots&d_{1}d_{2}^{\top}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
d_{1}d_{2}^{\top}&\cdots&d_{1}d_{2}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}=J_{k_{1}\times k_{2}}\otimes d_{1}d_{2}^{\top}=L.

Following a similar argument used to derive (
43
), the solution to
(H^{*}H+\rho I)X=R
is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}=\mathfrak{B}_{F_{B_{1}}^{-1},(F_{B_{2}}^{-1})\top}(\mathfrak{D}_{L+\rho}(\mathfrak{B}_{F_{B_{1}},F_{B_{2}}^{\top}}(\mathcal{R}))).

(49)

Note that when the
G_{i}
’s are circulant,

corresponds exactly to the 2D Fourier transform.

7
Discussion

In this paper, we presented a comprehensive framework for solving the generalized matrix separation problem. Through ADMM and utilizing efficient algorithmic strategies for various structures of the filtering matrix
H
, we demonstrated significant improvements in both computational efficiency and recovery accuracy. We introduced a preconditioning method that improves convergence and is supported by theoretical guarantees under standard incoherence assumptions.

Our numerical experiments confirm the robustness and scalability of the proposed algorithms across a variety of scenarios, including applications to video data for simultaneous background removal and deblurring. The ability of our methods to efficiently handle circulant, separable, and block-structured filters shows their practical relevance in real-world problems. Future work may consider extensions to adaptive or time-varying filters and aim to establish stronger recovery guarantees under broader conditions.
