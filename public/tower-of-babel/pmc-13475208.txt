Foreign body–associated maxillary sinus aspergillosis caused by Endomethasone extrusion: diagnostic challenges, biological mechanisms, and surgical management
Authors: Amanurrahman Zubair Ahemad, Tarun Kumar Singh, Aakash Gupta, Ekta Choudhary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extrusion of endodontic sealers into the maxillary sinus is an uncommon but clinically significant complication, particularly when biologically active zinc oxide–eugenol–based materials are involved. Their persistence within the sinus may impair mucociliary clearance and sustain chronic inflammation. This report describes a 25-year-old woman with persistent unilateral maxillary pain following root canal treatment and subsequent extraction of the involved tooth. Cone-beam computed tomography revealed a well-defined radiopaque foreign body within the maxillary sinus associated with mucosal thickening. Conservative therapy failed to relieve symptoms, and surgical removal was performed using a Caldwell–Luc approach. Histopathological examination demonstrated septate fungal hyphae with acute-angle branching consistent with Aspergillus species in association with a foreign body reaction and chronic inflammatory changes. Complete symptom resolution was achieved and maintained at one-year follow-up. This case highlights the diagnostic value of CBCT in post-treatment persistent pain, the biological behaviour of extruded Endomethasone within the sinus, and the need for early surgical intervention in symptomatic patients. Preventive strategies based on preoperative anatomical assessment and controlled obturation are also discussed. This case defines a transition from odontogenic to biomaterial-induced sinonasal disease and proposes CBCT-based criteria for early surgical intervention.

Introduction

Endodontic treatment of maxillary posterior teeth is anatomically demanding because the root apices may lie in close proximity to, or occasionally project into, the maxillary sinus as a result of sinus pneumatisation and thinning of the sinus floor.1 In such situations, over-instrumentation or excessive obturation pressure may displace endodontic materials beyond the apical foramen and into the sinus cavity.2 Although uncommon, this complication is clinically important because the resulting pathology may extend beyond the periapical tissues and present as persistent sinonasal symptoms rather than conventional odontogenic pain. Contact between obturation materials and the Schneiderian membrane can impair mucociliary function, provoke acute or chronic inflammation, and produce persistent mucosal thickening.3 The biological response depends not only on the composition of the sealer but also on the tissue environment in which it is deposited. Zinc oxide-eugenol (ZOE)-based sealers such as Endomethasone are widely used because of their antimicrobial activity, sealing properties, and handling characteristics; however, eugenol may exert cytotoxic and neurotoxic effects when extruded beyond the root canal system. In addition, the zinc component of these materials has been associated with fungal colonisation of the maxillary sinus, particularly aspergillosis.17 Nevertheless, the tissue response to corticosteroid-containing ZOE sealers within the sinus and the factors that should prompt surgical removal remain incompletely defined. Diagnosis may be delayed because conventional periapical radiographs can identify overextension but cannot reliably determine the three-dimensional location of the material or the extent of associated sinus disease. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) is therefore valuable for precise localisation, assessment of Schneiderian membrane changes, exclusion of alternative odontogenic causes, and surgical planning. Management remains controversial.18 Observation may be appropriate for asymptomatic patients with inert material, whereas non-resorbable ZOE-based sealers may continue to sustain inflammation and, in symptomatic cases, may require surgical removal.19 The persistence of maxillary pain after extraction challenges the conventional assumption that post-endodontic symptoms are necessarily odontogenic. When endodontic biomaterials are displaced into the maxillary sinus, the disease process shifts from a confined periapical environment to the respiratory mucosa, where the foreign body may act as an independent driver of chronic inflammation. The present report introduces a tissue-specific disease model for biomaterial-induced maxillary sinusitis and proposes a cone-beam CT–based diagnostic pathway to enable early identification and definitive surgical management in patients with persistent post-endodontic pain.

Case report and management

Patient information

A 25-year-old woman was referred to our tertiary care centre with persistent pain in the left maxillary posterior region of approximately six months’ duration. Her medical history was non-contributory, and she reported no previous sinonasal disease, facial trauma, or systemic illness.

The dental history revealed that root canal treatment of the left maxillary first molar was performed in June 2025 at a private clinic for deep caries with pulpal involvement. The patient reported no reduction in pain after the procedure. Owing to persistent symptoms, the treated tooth was extracted 1 month later; however, the pain persisted in the same region with minimal improvement. The absence of symptomatic relief after extraction suggested that the primary source of pain was no longer odontogenic and warranted further radiological evaluation.

Clinical findings

Clinical examination demonstrated a healed extraction site with no evidence of oroantral communication, swelling, or local tenderness. Adjacent teeth were non-tender to percussion and responded normally to vitality testing. No nasal obstruction, purulent discharge, or facial swelling was noted.

Radiological assessment

Periapical radiography

Intraoral periapical radiography revealed a well-defined radiopaque mass superior to the extraction socket of the left maxillary first molar, apparently located within the maxillary sinus. The two-dimensional image did not permit accurate localisation or assessment of the relationship between the foreign body and the sinus walls.

Cone-beam computed tomography findings

A limited field-of-view CBCT scan of the left posterior maxilla was obtained for three-dimensional assessment. Imaging demonstrated a well-defined hyperdense mass within the inferior aspect of the left maxillary sinus in close proximity to the sinus floor at the site corresponding to the previously treated tooth (Fig. 1). Quantitative analysis performed using a standardised region of interest yielded a mean grey value corresponding to approximately 1006 HU (HU-equivalent). This radiodensity was comparable to adjacent cortical bone and within the reported range for highly radiopaque zinc oxide–eugenol–based endodontic sealers, thereby supporting identification as extruded obturation material rather than an antrolith, fungal concretion, or metallic foreign body. Although CBCT grey values are not absolute Hounsfield units, the marked hyperdensity relative to surrounding cancellous bone and sinus contents provided reliable material characterisation.

The displaced material was located in the dependent portion of the sinus without evidence of bony wall perforation. The Schneiderian membrane showed localised mucosal thickening of approximately 2.6 mm adjacent to the foreign body, whereas the remaining sinus mucosa appeared normal. The ostiomeatal complex was patent, and no air–fluid level or radiological features of acute sinusitis were identified.

Treatment planning and surgical management

Initial conservative management with systemic antibiotics, analgesics, and nasal decongestants failed to produce symptomatic improvement, confirming that the retained foreign body was the primary source of the disease.

Surgical removal was therefore planned. Under local anaesthesia, a mucoperiosteal flap was elevated in the left canine fossa region, and a bony window was created to access the maxillary sinus. On entering the sinus, a well-defined solid mass corresponding to the extruded endodontic material was identified at the sinus floor and was removed in toto (Fig. 2). The adjacent sinus mucosa appeared thickened and inflamed and was carefully curetted. The sinus cavity was irrigated with sterile saline, and the surgical site was closed primarily. Histopathological findings

Microscopic examination revealed respiratory sinus mucosa with a dense chronic inflammatory infiltrate composed predominantly of lymphocytes and plasma cells with scattered eosinophils. Numerous multinucleated giant cells were present, indicating a foreign body–type granulomatous reaction. Within the inflammatory stroma, multiple septate fungal hyphae showing acute-angle (approximately 45°) dichotomous branching were identified, consistent with Aspergillus species (Fig. 3). Postoperative course and follow-up

The postoperative period was uneventful. The patient reported complete resolution of pain within the first postoperative week. Clinical and radiological follow-up at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year demonstrated normal healing, with no recurrence of symptoms. A postoperative CBCT scan showed complete removal of the foreign body and resolution of the sinus mucosal thickening, with restoration of normal sinus aeration (Fig. 4). Discussion

Persistent unilateral maxillary pain after extraction of the treated tooth was the principal diagnostic clue in this case because it indicated that the source of symptoms was no longer confined to the tooth or periapical tissues.1 Although extraction eliminated the potential intraradicular source of infection, pain continued until the material retained within the maxillary sinus was surgically removed. This clinical sequence supports the view that an extruded endodontic sealer may become an independent sinonasal pathological stimulus capable of sustaining inflammation after the original odontogenic focus has been eliminated.18,20 The biological response to an extruded sealer depends not only on its composition but also on the anatomical environment in which it is deposited. Small quantities of zinc oxide–eugenol (ZOE)-based material within periapical tissues may provoke a localised inflammatory response followed by fibrosis or repair.20,21 In contrast, the maxillary sinus is an aerated cavity lined by ciliated respiratory epithelium and dependent on effective mucociliary transport and ostial drainage. A non-resorbable foreign body in this environment may impair mucus clearance, irritate the Schneiderian membrane, and produce persistent focal mucosal inflammation.18,22 Endomethasone may be particularly relevant because eugenol can exert cytotoxic effects on fibroblasts and other viable cells when released outside the root canal system, while the persistent solid material may elicit a foreign-body reaction. Although the corticosteroid component may transiently suppress acute inflammation, it cannot prevent a chronic tissue response when the material remains in a non-physiological site.23 In the present case, chronic inflammatory infiltration and multinucleated giant cells demonstrated an established foreign-body reaction; therefore, the earlier interpretation that there was no granulomatous response was not appropriate. Histopathological examination additionally revealed septate fungal hyphae with acute-angle branching, morphologically consistent with Aspergillus species. Because fungal culture, special staining, or molecular identification was not reported, these findings should be interpreted as Aspergillus-associated non-invasive fungal sinusitis rather than definitive species-level confirmation.24 Zinc-containing endodontic materials have previously been associated with maxillary sinus fungus balls, and zinc may support fungal metabolism and growth. 7,13,25

The retained sealer may also provide a surface for fungal adherence, while impaired mucociliary clearance facilitates accumulation of fungal elements and inflammatory debris. This combined foreign-body and fungal mechanism provides a biologically plausible explanation for the persistence of symptoms despite extraction of the involved tooth. CBCT was central to diagnosis and treatment planning.26 Conventional periapical radiography demonstrated a radiopaque structure superior to the extraction site but could not reliably establish its precise location or relationship to the sinus floor and adjacent mucosa. CBCT confirmed that the material was situated within the dependent inferior portion of the sinus, demonstrated focal Schneiderian membrane thickening, and excluded retained roots, bony perforation, and other evident odontogenic causes. The marked radiopacity supported identification of an endodontic foreign body; however, CBCT grey values are device- and acquisition-dependent and should not be interpreted as absolute Hounsfield units or used alone to determine material composition. CBCT also assisted surgical planning by defining the accessibility of the lesion and documenting postoperative removal and restoration of sinus aeration.27 Management of endodontic materials displaced into the maxillary sinus must be individualised. Observation may be reasonable for an asymptomatic patient with a small, inert foreign body and no mucosal disease, whereas persistent unilateral pain, a non-resorbable ZOE-based sealer, associated mucosal thickening, fungal involvement, or failure of conservative treatment favours surgical removal. Endoscopic transnasal surgery is commonly preferred because it preserves physiological drainage and generally causes less morbidity; however, the Caldwell–Luc approach provided direct access to the relatively large, solid material located in the inferior sinus and permitted complete retrieval together with sampling of the inflamed mucosa.28 The favourable one-year clinical and radiological outcome supports the appropriateness of this approach in the present anatomical setting, although it should not be interpreted as universally superior to endoscopic management. Prevention remains essential and depends on accurate working-length determination, controlled sealer placement, avoidance of excessive obturation pressure, and careful assessment of root–sinus proximity.29 Preoperative CBCT should not be used routinely for every endodontic procedure, but it may be justified when conventional imaging suggests an unusually close relationship between posterior tooth roots and the sinus or when persistent symptoms remain unexplained. This case therefore emphasises that pain continuing after endodontic treatment and extraction should prompt evaluation for an intrasinus foreign body and that timely three-dimensional imaging and definitive removal may prevent prolonged morbidity and repeated unnecessary dental intervention.

Conclusion and clinical implications

Extruded ZOE based sealer within the maxillary sinus should be regarded not only as a mechanical foreign body but also as a biologically active substrate capable of promoting fungal colonisation. The present case demonstrates that persistent post-endodontic maxillary pain may represent foreign body–associated aspergillosis rather than odontogenic disease. CBCT enables early recognition of this entity and facilitates timely surgical intervention, resulting in definitive resolution.
