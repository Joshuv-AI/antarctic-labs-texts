Loss Compensation and Coherent Virtual Absorption via Virtual Complex Frequency Wave Excitation
Authors: Sheng Zhang, Zhi Hong Hang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTComplex frequency waves (CFWs), exhibiting extraordinary propagation properties including virtual gain/loss, have attracted increasing research attention. However, the experimental excitation of CFWs at high frequencies, especially in microwave and optical regimes, remains a big challenge. Here, by establishing a mathematical bridge between real and complex frequencies, this work presents a general method for CFW excitation, termed virtual CFW excitation, enabling us to obtain all electromagnetic responses for complex frequencies, including wavevector, electric/magnetic field, and transmission/reflection coefficient, even in the presence of intrinsic noise. Specifically, using virtual CFW excitation can offset the effect caused by material losses and the lost field information of both a point source and surface plasmon polaritons can be successfully recovered. Moreover, coherent virtual absorption is verified by proof‐of‐principle microwave transmission‐line experiments. Our finding provides an effective tool to explore phenomena of complex frequencies, mitigating experimental difficulties and promoting the future development of optical devices.

Introduction

Over recent decades, metamaterials have received great research attention owing to their excellent abilities for manipulating electromagnetic (EM) waves, where significant phenomena have been discovered, such as negative refraction [1, 2, 3], perfect tunneling [4, 5, 6], invisibility cloaking [7, 8, 9, 10, 11], and abnormal reflection/transmission [12, 13, 14]. Nevertheless, inherent material losses [15], stemming from material absorption and scattering, hinder the practical applications of metamaterials. For instance, suffering from metal loss, the actual resolution of the superlens is substantially reduced [16]. Thus, alleviating performance downgrade caused by material losses becomes an urgent task for the metamaterial community. Several strategies, including adopting low‐loss materials [17, 18] and optimizing structure geometries [19], have been developed, but remain constrained to specific scenarios. An alternative strategy is to incorporate gain material [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25], yet leading to undesirable effects such as instabilities and extra noises [26, 27]. Interestingly, the excitation of complex frequency waves (CFWs) with engineered temporal profiles has been theoretically proposed to recover the lost sub‐wavelength information caused by material loss without introducing noise [28, 29], indicating that the implementation of CFW offers a better solution than introducing gain. Distinct from EM wave behaviors in the real frequency domain [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36], CFWs exhibit unique propagation characteristics [37, 38], that is, virtual loss and virtual gain [38, 39, 40], which further lead to intriguing theoretical predictions, such as coherent virtual absorption [41, 42, 43] and virtual parity–time symmetry [44]. Consequently, the experimental realization and measurement of CFWs become vital, which facilitate the exploration of their potential applications. Time‐domain manipulations of acoustic waves to achieve CFW have been demonstrated [45, 46], where the sound imaging resolution can be enhanced by CFW with optimized temporal decay [46]. However, the direct time‐domain excitation of CFWs at high frequencies, particularly in microwave and optical regimes, remains a big challenge due to hardware limitations. Recent advances to realize CFWs by synthesizing multiple real‐frequency waves have initially demonstrated the success of offsetting material loss [47, 48, 49]. Thus, developing more strategies for high‐frequency CFW excitations will favor the development of future applications.

In this study, we propose a general method for CFW excitation. As illustrated in Figure 1, the EM responses for complex frequencies, including the wavevector, electric/magnetic field, and transmission/reflection coefficient, can be obtained from real frequencies through the introduction of a mathematical bridge function F(ω), termed the virtual CFW excitation. Specifically, we demonstrate that the propagation losses of both a point source and surface plasmon polaritons in the presence of material loss are successfully offset. In addition, we have unveiled the physical mechanism of coherent virtual absorption, eventually confirmed in proof‐of‐principle microwave experiments.

Results and Discussions

Loss Compensation for a Point Source in Lossy Media

We assume eiωt time dependence, where ω represents real angular frequency. For a conducting medium with electric conductivity σ, the corresponding complex permittivity can be given by ϵ˜=ϵr−iσωϵ0 [50], where ϵr and ϵ0 denote the real part of relative permittivity and permittivity of free space, respectively. The complex wavevector of EM waves propagating in such a medium can be expressed as follows: (1a) k˜=ωcϵr−iσωϵ0, where k˜ and c are the complex wavevector and the speed of light in free space, respectively. From Equation 1a, it is found that the non‐zero conductivity introduces a negative imaginary part to the wavevector, resulting in the propagation loss of EM waves. This physical interpretation relies on the assumption that the frequency is purely real values. If complex frequencies are considered, wavevector could become a real value, given by the following equation: (1b) k=ω˜cϵr−iσω˜ϵ0, where k and ω˜ are the real wavevector and the complex angular frequency, respectively. Solving Equation 1b (see Supporting Information S1) enables that complex frequency f˜=fr+ifi must satisfy the following equation: (2) fi=σ4πϵ0ϵr, here fi is solely determined by the ratio σϵr, indicating that broadband lossless propagation in lossy media is possible if a complex frequency excitation is allowed.

For simplicity, a material with ϵr=1 and σ=0.1S/m is considered. By evaluating Equation 1a, we plot the real and imaginary components of the wavevector for real frequencies, as shown in Figure 2a. The results reveal the presence of a negative imaginary component in the wavevector, corresponding to the attenuation of EM waves. This attenuation can be compensated if a complex frequency with an imaginary component of 0.899GHz is excited. Calculating Equation 1b, the corresponding wavevectors under complex frequency are depicted as blue and red circles in Figure 2b, it is found that wavevectors are purely real values. To further establish a connection between real and complex frequencies, we propose a mathematical framework that enables virtual CFW excitation, allowing the wavevector at complex frequencies to be derived directly from its real‐frequency counterpart. Specifically, the dependence of the complex wavevector on frequency can be represented by a polynomial expansion (3) k˜(f)=∑i=0naifi, where n=2 and ai denotes the coefficient of the polynomial. The excellent agreement between analytical results, the polynomial and using the finite element method (FEM) implemented in COMSOL Multiphysics can be found in Figure 2a. By substituting real frequencies with complex frequencies in Equation (3), as shown in Figure 2b, we can retrieve the wavevectors for complex frequency, which align perfectly with the analytical expression of Equation 1b. This finding confirms that the proposed virtual CFW excitation provides an effective mathematical bridge linking real and complex frequency domains to obtain EM responses from real frequency counterparts.

Furthermore, we proceed to demonstrate that the lost electric field information caused by material loss can be restored. Figure 3a shows a point source radiating in a lossless medium. When material loss of σ=0.1S/m is introduced, as shown in Figure 3b, the corresponding electric field Ez decays quickly away from the source. Using the Ez‐distributions for the lossy medium over real frequency range from 10 to 12 GHz with a spacing of 0.0025 GHz, we can build a bridge function, that is, Ezx0,y0,f=∑i=0naix0,y0fi, where x0,y0 refers to the spatial position. By inserting the complex frequencies with an imaginary part of 0.899GHz into the polynomial, the Ez‐distributions at different complex frequencies can be obtained, as shown in Figure 3c,d, the lost electric fields are recovered. Moreover, to identify the recovery of the electric field, we introduce the correlation coefficient. As displayed in Figure 3f, the correlation coefficient is only 85% between field distributions of a lossless medium and a lossy medium at the same real frequencies, whereas it exceeds 97.5% under virtual CFW excitation, validating the effectiveness of our proposal for field recovery. Even if a random noise is added to conductivity (0.08∼0.12S/m, while maintaining a 0.1S/m average value), a similar undistorted Ez‐distribution at complex frequency is displayed in Figure 3e with an above 95% correlation coefficient. The robustness is thus verified, and more details for random noise can be found in Supporting Information S1.

Loss Compensation of Surface Plasmon Polariton

The virtual CFW excitation can also be utilized to compensate for the propagation loss of surface plasmon polariton (SPP), as a demonstration to recover evanescent magnetic fields. The surface plasmon polariton, supporting only transverse electric mode with magnetic field polarized out of the plane, is confined at the interface between a dielectric and metal [51, 52, 53], exhibiting significant potentials for energy transfer [54, 55, 56, 57], and biosensors [58, 59, 60]. Here, we consider the SPPs propagate along the interface between air (ϵd=1) and a lossy metal with ϵm=−2−iσωϵ0 and the corresponding dispersion relation is given by k˜spp=ωcϵdϵmϵd+ϵm [52]. Because of the wavevector mismatch between SPPs and free space waves, total internal reflection [61] is used to excite the SPPs by an isosceles‐right‐triangle‐shaped prism with permittivity ϵprism=4, as shown in Figure 4a. When the conductivity is set as σ=0S/m, the SPP propagates along the interface between the lossless metal and air without dissipation, as shown in Figure 4a, we highlight the corresponding Hz‐distribution within the red wireframe in Figure 4c. When lossy metal with σ=0.05S/m is considered, as shown in Figure 4b–d, Hz quickly decays along the propagation direction. Using virtual CFW excitation, we can also construct a polynomial to depict the magnetic field for real frequencies, that is, Hzx0,y0,f=∑i=0naix0,y0fi. The imaginary part of complex frequency can be derived as fi≈0.22315GHz by solving Imk˜spp=0, implying that when a complex frequency wave is excited, the propagation loss can be eliminated. Employing virtual CFW excitation by inserting complex frequencies into polynomial, as shown in Figure 4e,f, lossless propagation of SPPs can be observed along the interface for different complex frequencies. More investigations for SPPs at an interface between air and dispersive metal can be found in Supporting Information S1.

Experimental Realization of Coherent Virtual Absorption

Transmission and reflection are also important characteristics for EM waves, which are associated with EM absorption. A typical example of complex frequency is coherent virtual absorption [41, 42], whereas its experimental realization is still a big challenge due to the lack of experimental techniques for complex frequency excitations in microwave and optical regimes. Here, using virtual CFW excitation, coherent virtual absorption is experimentally realized through a microwave transmission line, which is, to the best of our knowledge, the first experimental verification in optics. The transmission line is displayed in Figure 5a, composed of the copper microstrip line and patch printed on FR‐4 substrate (relative permittivity ϵr=4.5) with the metallic reflector. A resistor is integrated into the gap between the microstrip line and the metallic patch, where the geometry parameters are p = 50 mm, l = 20 mm, g = 1 mm, and w = 2.8 mm, and the thickness of the FR‐4 substrate is t = 1.5 mm. The coupled mode theory (CMT) [62, 63, 64] analysis of such a transmission line can be found in Supporting Information S1.

Simulated by the finite difference time domain method (CST Microwave Studio), the transmission coefficients for different resistors are displayed in Figure 5b, from which the resonant frequency is found at f0=1.6GHz, whereas the non‐radiation loss Γ can be controlled by tuning the integrated resistors. As shown in Figure 5c, it is found that non‐radiation loss Γ is a linear dependence on the resistance of the integrated resistor R as Γ=0.019R, where the radiation loss γ is nearly a constant (∼0.45 GHz). Two counter‐propagating CFWs are required to induce coherent virtual absorption with t+r=0, whose frequency should satisfy f˜=f0−i(γ−Γ) (see Supporting Information S1). Similarly, we can also build a polynomial to describe the dependence of t+r on real frequencies, that is, t+r=∑i=0naifi. By introducing the complex frequency f˜ into the polynomial, we can obtain |t+r| as a function of complex frequencies for different resistors, as shown in Figure 5d, |t+r| reach zero at the desired complex frequencies, indicating that coherent virtual absorption occurs. It is worth noting that for some electromagnetic responses with strong dispersions, the polynomial fitting may fail and other fitting functions, that is, rational fitting or Lorentzian fitting, shall be considered, more details can be found in Supporting Information S1.

Finally, coherent virtual absorption is visualized by our proof‐of‐principle experiments. As depicted in Figure 6a, the two ports of the experimental sample are connected to the vector network analyzer (Keysight E5071C) for transmission/reflection coefficient measurements. Different resistances, that is, R=8.6Ω,10.2Ω,12.5Ω,16.6Ω,22.6Ω, can be integrated into the transmission line. By implementing virtual CFW excitation for measured transmission and reflection coefficients, we can obtain |t+r| as a function of the real and imaginary parts of the complex frequency, as shown in Figure 6b, for R=12.5Ω, coherent virtual absorption occurs at f˜=1.665−0.194iGHz with a minimal value of |t+r|. Here, we note that the real part of the frequency deviates slightly from the theoretical prediction due to the imperfect fabrication of the sample. Moreover, |t+r| for R=12.5Ω is plotted in a green line of Figure 6c, and similar spectra can be found for other integrated resistances, indicating the success of demonstrating coherent virtual absorption. Figure 6d displays the experimentally extracted radiation loss, non‐radiation loss, and imaginary part of complex frequency as a function of resistances, in accord with our theoretical prediction. The effects of amplitude and phase imbalance between two inputs can be found in Supporting Information S1.

Conclusions

In summary, we have successfully proposed a virtual CFW excitation to study the properties of CFWs. Through constructing a mathematical bridge between real and complex frequencies, any EM responses for complex frequencies can be obtained from the real frequency domain. We demonstrate that CFWs can compensate for the propagation loss of both point sources and the SPPs even in the presence of noise. Finally, the coherent virtual absorption is experimentally demonstrated. Although our work is only verified in microwave experiments, the method is general and can also be extended to the optical regime, which will open up more opportunities to investigate wave physics in the complex frequency domain.
