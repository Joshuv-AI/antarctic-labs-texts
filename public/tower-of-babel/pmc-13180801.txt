Association of Anemia with Mortality and Functional Prognosis in Patients with First-Ever Ischemic Stroke: A Retrospective Cohort Study in Tunisia
Authors: Houssem Slimen, Anis Hassine, Arwa Rekik, Emna Jarrar, Amal Hajkacem, Yosra Dhaha, Amina Bouatay, Sana Ben Amor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThere is significant debate surrounding whether anemia should be considered a prognostic factor for stroke. The aim of this study was to assess the impact of anemia on the outcomes after the first ischemic stroke (IS), based on the Hemoglobin (Hb) levels and IS etiology.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study in our department of Neurology including patients presenting with a first ever IS admitted in our Neurovascular unit between January 2023 and December 2023. Vascular risk factors and clinical presentations were documented. Mortality and outcomes (modified Rankin scores= mRS) were evaluated at three and six months. All parameters assessed were compared between anemic and non-anemic patients. Then, we examined the association between stroke outcomes, severity, etiology and type of anemia.ResultsWe included 549 patients with a mean age of 63.7±12 years. Anemic patients (27.9%) were older (p< 0.001) and were less likely to be smokers, consistent with the known effect of smoking on increasing Hb levels. On multivariate analysis, anemia was associated with more severe clinical presentations at onset (p=0.001) and with increased mRS during follow-up. Independent of the severity and type of anemia, patients diagnosed with anemia in both the acute phase and at six months had a significantly higher mortality rate, respectively, p=0.02 and p=0.04.ConclusionAnemia is a common biological finding among IS patients and is associated with more severe clinical presentations, poorer functional outcomes, and increased mortality, suggesting a potential role as a prognostic marker that warrants further investigation.Keywords: ischemic stroke, anemia, mortality, outcomes

Introduction

Anemia is a prevalent hematologic condition whose frequency increases with age.1 Literature findings have already highlighted the link between anemia and several diseases. It could be an independent predictor of mortality in patients with heart failure and chronic kidney failure.2,3 Moreover, anemia is frequent in patients with cardiovascular disease, but the connection between those two diseases is not well-established.4

Anemia is found in up to 40% of patients with ischemic stroke (IS).5 The question arises whether it is merely a comorbidity, as the frequency of these two diseases increases with age, or if there is an association between anemia and IS. In a systematic review of the literature, the link between Hemoglobin (Hb) levels at admission and functional outcomes after IS was found to be heterogeneous.5 Some studies suggest that anemia could affect disability and mortality during the acute phase of IS through several mechanisms.5,6 However, in the Cochrane Database systematic review published in 2021, authors conclude that there is no evidence giving blood transfusions to patients with lower blood counts (hemoglobin levels of 7.0 g/dL to 8.0 g/dL) compared to higher blood counts (9.0 g/dL to 10.0 g/dL) affects risks of death, myocardial infarction and stroke.7

On the other hand, recent reports indicate a significant association between anemia and the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at three months after endovascular treatment and early neurologic deterioration after intravenous thrombolysis.8,9 This association may be partially explained by the role of erythropoietin and other anemia parameters in the quality of collateral status.10 Moreover, anemia can have a direct impact on the ischemic penumbra, as decreased oxygen-carrying capacity (CaO2) may hinder collateral blood flow in the case of large vessel occlusion, potentially leading to greater infarct severity and early neurological deterioration.11

Yet, several challenges are still ahead to understand the relationship between serum Hb levels and the outcomes following IS in adults. Furthermore, it is important to note that previous studies did not document outcomes based on the severity of anemia and stroke subtypes classification. Previous studies have not provided a comprehensive overview of the optimal target Hb levels or established uniform criteria for blood cell transfusion. For all those reasons, new studies are essential, particularly in low-income countries where recanalization treatment is not being routinely used.

In this study, we aim to assess the impact of anemia on outcomes in patients experiencing their first-ever IS, thereby minimizing confounding from prior disability, based on Hb levels and stroke subtypes.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

Our study was conducted between January 2023 and December 2023 in the neurovascular stroke unit of the Neurology department at Sahloul Hospital in Sousse, Tunisia. We included all patients admitted on the first day of their first-ever IS diagnosis according to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) criteria.12,13

Anemia was defined based on WHO criteria.14 Anemic patients were divided into three subgroups according to the type of anemia. Normochromic normocytic anemia (NNA) was defined as a Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV) of 80–100 fL and a Mean Corpuscular Hb (MCH) of 27–32 pg. Hypochromic microcytic anemia (HMA) was defined as an MCV of <80 and macrocytic anemia (MA) as an MCV of >100. Based on the severity of this condition, we divided patients into two subgroups: mild anemia with Hb ranging from 10 to 12 g/dl and moderate to severe anemia if Hb level was below 10.0g/dL. Given the small sample size of the subgroups HMA and MA, they were grouped in the analytical study.

Neurological examination findings on the first day of IS, functional status at three and six months, death rates at the acute phase and six months, and the frequency of chosen comorbidities were compared between two groups of patients: those with anemia and those with normal Hb serum level. These parameters were also compared between the subgroups of anemic patients according to the type and severity of anemia.

Functional outcome at three and six months was assessed with mRS: a score equal to or greater than 3 was indicative of a poor functional outcome, whereas a score less than 3 was indicative of a good functional outcome.

Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee, Sahloul Hospital, Sousse, Tunisia. January 2023.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using Statistica 24 software. Nonparametric values were compared using the Chi2 test and U Mann–Whitney test, while parametric data analysis relied on the t-Student test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the comparison of means across multiple (>2) independent series, the Snedecor F-test was employed for parametric analysis of variance (ANOVA one-way).

The association study was conducted through the calculation of the odds ratio (OR) and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (95% CI). The significance threshold retained for all statistics was p < 0.05. These OR were adjusted by multivariate analysis using binary logistic regression for potential confounders with p < 0.25. A significance level of 0.05 was applied to all statistical tests.

Results

From January to December 2023, 549 consecutive patients with first-ever IS were first enrolled in the study. Seventy-four patients were excluded due to interrupted follow-up. The mean age was 63.7±12 years. Our population included 327 men (59.6%) and 222 women (40.4%) (sex ratio=1.47). Hypertension (66.1%) was the most common vascular risk factor. Based on the TOAST classification, the most common etiology of IS was large vessel atherosclerosis (36.2%) followed by emboligenic heart disease (29.1%) and small vessel disease (9.3%). Following adjustment for variables that showed significant differences between patients with and without anemia in univariate analysis (age, smoking, TACS), the multivariate analysis revealed that patients with anemia experienced more severe strokes (score of NIHSS at the acute phase) in comparison to those without anemia (OR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.1–2.43). For the analysis of 7-day and 180-day mortality, the final multivariate model included anemia, age, and NIHSS score because of the limited number of events, in order to simplify the model and ensure statistical validity. Mortality rates at the acute phase and six months were higher among patients with anemia with a statistically significant difference (OR = 2.2, 95% CI: 1.14–4.62; OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1–3.92) (Table 4). Other significant independent predictors of both short- and long-term mortality, as identified in the multivariate analysis, included advanced age and the severity of stroke, as indicated by the NIHSS score. Comparison of death rates and severity of IS between different types of anemia and according to Hb level did not show statistically significant differences (Tables 5 and 6).

After adjusting for confounding factors between patients with and without anemia, the multivariate analysis showed that functional independence at three and six months was less frequent in anemic patients (OR = 2.71, 95% CI: 1.73–4.22; OR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.51–3.67) (Table 4). However, we did not show any statistically significant differences regarding the correlation between the different anemia types and functional outcomes at 3 and 6 months (Tables 5 and 6). There was no disparity in the recurrence rate of stroke between the two groups (p = 0.79).

Discussion

The main finding of the present study was that patients with anemia presented with more severe strokes at onset, greater disability at both short- and long-term follow-up, and higher mortality rates at 7 days and 6 months compared with non-anemic patients, independent of stroke severity or subtype. This may be partly explained by the higher proportion of TACS among anemic patients. The observation that anemic patients had a significantly higher rate of TACS (15% vs 7.8%, p = 0.01) supports the hypothesis of impaired collateral circulation. It has been suggested that lower Hb levels may compromise collateral circulation, leading to larger and more severe infarcts.10 In addition, reduced Hb concentration may increase infarct size by impairing neuronal survival in the ischemic penumbra through a reduction in arterial oxygen content (CaO2).6 Moreover, red blood cells may promote thrombus formation and enhance thrombus stability.15 These mechanisms could explain the higher NIHSS scores at admission and the increased mortality observed at 7 days.

Anemia, defined based on the admission Hb level, was identified in 27.9% of our patients, particularly in those aged over 65 years. Dubyk et al reported a higher prevalence of anemia in elderly patients, who experienced their first stroke compared to the population data.16 Evidence has accumulated that anemia is common in older persons and increases vulnerability to adverse outcomes, including early mortality.17 As expected, smoking was more frequent among non-anemic patients, which is consistent with the known association between smoking and secondary polycythemia. Despite this higher burden of smoking-related vascular risk in the non-anemic group, outcomes remained worse in anemic patients, further supporting the potential independent impact of anemia on stroke severity and prognosis. The relatively high proportion of “undefined” anemia in our cohort may reflect the retrospective nature of the study and the limited etiological workup often performed in the acute phase of stroke.

In this study, mortality rates at six months were higher among patients with anemia with a statistically significant difference. We conclude that anemia was an independent predictive factor of mortality at six months, in line with the results of prior studies. Abedi V. et al conclude that age, Hb levels, and body mass index are the top three associated factors with long-term mortality after IS.18 Nam H.S. et al showed in the multivariate logistic regression analysis that age, myocardial infarction, anemia, and metastatic cancer were independently associated with 6-month mortality in patients after reperfusion therapy.19 The higher rate of mortality at six months could be related to comorbid conditions classically associated, such as heart failure and chronic kidney failure.2,3 On the other hand, blood transfusion in anemic patients with or without hemorrhagic complications was considered a powerful predictor of worse outcomes and could increase mortality.15,17,20

We did not observe any statistically significant difference between the different types and severity of anemia regarding functional outcomes and mortality. Mortality rates in the acute phase as well as at six months were comparable across the different subgroups of anemic patients. The death rate in patients with moderate to severe anemia was higher than in those with mild anemia [7-day: 8% (mild) vs 11.3% (moderate/severe), p = 0.5; 6-month: 11.1% (mild) vs 17% (moderate/severe), p = 0.33], but the difference was not statistically significant. Although a trend toward worse outcomes with lower Hb levels was observed, the lack of statistical significance is likely attributable to a type II error due to insufficient statistical power, related to the small sample size in the moderate/severe anemia group (n = 53), which limits the ability to detect clinically meaningful differences. Only a limited number of studies have explored the association between Hb levels and stroke outcomes.5,21–23 These studies suggest that the relationship between Hb levels and poor outcomes is not linear, with increased risk observed at both low and high Hb concentrations. Anemia represents a heterogeneous condition, and most studies have not examined how its specific characteristics influence outcomes after acute ischemic stroke.21–23 Millionis et al reported that the highest mortality rates during both the acute and late phases of stroke were observed in patients with the lowest Hb levels on admission.6 We acknowledge this limitation in statistical power and recommend that future studies with larger cohorts confirm this potential dose–response relationship.

Our current study is the first in the Tunisian population to investigate the impact of anemia on mortality and functional outcome during a first-ever IS. Our findings indicate the crucial importance of closely monitoring patients with anemia on admission. It is essential to identify anemia in IS patients upon admission and initiate an etiological diagnostic approach to identify reversible causes since patients suffering from IS, even those with mild anemia, present an elevated risk of mortality and an unfavorable functional outcome. Even though we did not observe a statistically significant difference according to Hb level, anemia was associated with poorer outcomes in our cohort and may therefore represent a marker of disease severity and frailty. However, the benefit of red blood cell transfusion in patients with acute IS remains uncertain, and current evidence does not support routine transfusion outside established guideline-based indications.7 We recommend an intensified rehabilitation program for anemic patients suffering from IS to minimize physical disability in the long-term.

There were some limitations to our study. First, the data were derived from a single hospital database. Second, a notable proportion of patients were lost to follow-up, which may have influenced the assessment of long-term outcomes. Finally, different etiologies of anemia were grouped together for statistical analysis due to the limited number of patients in each subgroup, although these conditions may have distinct biological and thrombotic implications.

Conclusion

Anemia is an independent robust predictor of mortality and poor outcomes in patients with acute IS. These findings highlight the potential prognostic importance of anemia in acute stroke and underline the need for further prospective studies to better understand its clinical implications.
