Minority stress mediates associations of sexual minority state policies and tobacco use among US sexual minority young adults
Authors: Katelyn F Romm, Erin A Vogel, Christina Dyar, Laurie A Drabble, Patricia A Cavazos-Rehg, Carla J Berg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:State policies surrounding sexual minority (SM) rights are associated with tobacco use among SM individuals. Research is scant regarding the role of distinct SM policy categories on SM young adults’ (SMYAs) tobacco use and mechanisms explaining these associations.Methods:We analyzed 2023 survey data from 1100 SMYAs (ages 18–34; 14.2 % gender minority; 66.1 % bisexual+, 29.1 % monosexual; 53.2 % racial/ethnic minority) with representation across 45 US states and DC. Regression-based models examined: 1) direct associations of residing in states with negative and limited (vs. comprehensive) SM state policies with respect to 7 policy categories (relationship/parent recognition, nondiscrimination, religious exemptions, LGBTQ youth, healthcare, criminal justice, gender identity documents) with minority stress (mental health, internalized stigma, community connectedness); 2) direct associations of policy categories and minority stress with tobacco use (past-month cigarette, e-cigarette, any tobacco use, number of products used); and 3) indirect associations of policy categories with tobacco use through minority stress.Results:Relative to residing in states with comprehensive policies, residing in states with limited relationship/parent recognition policies indirectly predicted higher odds of e-cigarette use through mental health; weaker nondiscrimination policies indirectly predicted using more tobacco products through internalized stigma; and negative healthcare policies indirectly predicted higher odds of cigarette and any tobacco use through community connectedness.Conclusions:These novel findings regarding associations among distinct SM policy categories, minority stress mediators, and tobacco use outcomes warrant further examination to better understand these distinct mechanisms, ultimately to inform SM-related policy and advocacy efforts, as well as tobacco prevention and cessation efforts.Keywords: Tobacco use, Sexual minority, Young adults, Policy, Minority stress

Introduction

Young adulthood is characterized by increased independence, financial and educational changes, and increased risk for tobacco use (Arnett, 2005, 2014; Tucker et al., 2005). Despite decreased cigarette use prevalence, tobacco use remains high among young adults (YAs), largely due to the proliferation of new nicotine and tobacco products (e. g., e-cigarettes) (Cornelius et al., 2022; Dai and Leventhal, 2019; USDHHS.. 2021). Sexual minority YAs (SMYAs; i.e., bisexual, gay/− lesbian, or another non-heterosexual identity) show disparate tobacco use rates and more harmful use patterns, such as polytobacco use (i.e., use of 2 or more products during the same time period) than their heterosexual peers; Li et al., (2018); Romm et al., (2022); Wheldon et al. (2018).

Minority stress theory suggests that SMYAs face hostile stress related to their minoritized identity and may use tobacco to cope with these experiences (Blosnich et al., 2013; Evans-Polce et al., 2020; Gordon et al., 2020; Krueger, Fish, et al., 2020; McCabe et al., 2017). Minority stress occurs at the psychological (e.g., mental health), social (e.g., less community support), and structural (e.g., policy context) levels, and these contexts impact one another (Meyer, 2003). Prior work has largely focused on psychological and social minority stress and suggests that SM (vs. heterosexual) individuals report greater mental health symptoms (Blosnich et al., 2013), internalized stigma (i.e., internalization of negative societal views of one’s sexual identity; Hatzenbuehler, 2009) (Blosnich et al., 2013), and less community support (Krueger, Braymiller, et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2020), which are associated with elevated tobacco use rates.

Regarding structural minority stress, SM-related policies largely occur at the state level (Cramer et al., 2017), and fall into 7 categories related to: relationship/parent recognition, nondiscrimination, religious exemptions, LGBTQ youth, healthcare, criminal justice, and gender identity documents. These policy categories may be protective (e.g., nondiscrimination policies) or harmful (e.g., religious exemptions from nondiscrimination policies) for SM individuals. Notably, weaker SM policy protections promote societal stigma toward SM individuals (Ofosu et al., 2019; Roy et al., 2023), which is associated with greater psychological and social minority stress (Badgett, 2011; Everett et al., 2016; Hatzenbuehler et al., 2009, 2010; Rothblum et al., 2011; Watson et al., 2021). For instance, weaker relationship/parent recognition policies (e.g., nondiscrimination policies for SM parents adopting) and LGBTQ youth policies (e.g., school nondiscrimination policies) are associated with mental health symptoms (Everett et al., 2016; Goldberg and Smith, 2011) and less SM community support (Watson et al., 2021), respectively.

SM state policies are also associated with substance use among SM individuals, with protective policies predicting decreased odds of substance use (Drabble et al., 2021; Drabble, Munroe, et al., 2022; Greene et al., 2021) including tobacco use (Titus et al., 2021), and harmful policies predicting increased odds of use (Pachankis et al., 2014). This prior work has examined structural minority stress in isolation from psychological and social minority stress and has aggregated across distinct policy categories (e.g., relationship/parent recognition, nondiscrimination, healthcare policies) that may have different impacts (Drabble et al., 2021; Drabble, Munroe, et al., 2022; Pachankis et al., 2014; Titus et al., 2021).

Identifying the distinct SM policy categories that most strongly influence SMYAs’ tobacco use and the minority stress mechanisms through which these associations occur is crucial to informing advocacy and intervention efforts to promote health equity. Because cigarettes and e-cigarettes are the most commonly used tobacco products among SMYAs (SAMHSA, 2023), we assessed past-month cigarette use, e-cigarette use, any tobacco use (to capture the wider range of products on the market), and the total number of products used among those reporting current use given greater health consequences associated with polytobacco use (Ali et al., 2016). We examined: 1) these 7 SM policy categories in relation to psychological (i.e., mental health symptoms, internalized stigma) and social (i.e., community connectedness [feeling part of the SM community, having SM peers; Goldbach et al., 2015]) minority stress; 2) policy categories and minority stress in relation to tobacco use behaviors (i.e., past-month [current] cigarette, e-cigarette, any tobacco use, number of products used); and 3) indirect associations between policy categories and tobacco use through minority stress. We hypothesized that weaker policy protections are associated with greater minority stress, higher odds of past-month tobacco use, and more tobacco products used. Distinct indirect associations between policy categories and tobacco use outcomes via minority stress have not been previously examined; thus, these analyses are exploratory and are intended to facilitate future research disaggregating the role of specific SM policy categories on tobacco use and the minority stress mechanisms through which these associations occur.

Methods

Participants and procedures

The current study analyzed baseline survey data among YAs (aged 18–34) in the Cannabis Regulation, Marketing, & Appeal (CARMA) study, designed to examine sociocontextual correlates of cannabis use. This longitudinal study launched in June-November 2023, involves survey assessments every 6 months for 2 years, and was approved by the George Washington University Institutional Review Board.

Study ads were posted on Facebook and targeted individuals by using indicators reflecting those eligible (i.e., ages 18–34, US residents, English speaking). Purposive, quota-based sampling ensured ~50 % of the sample reported current cannabis use, roughly equal proportions reported male and female sex, and ~40 % identified as a racial and/or ethnic minority. After clicking on an ad, individuals were sent a message via chatbot on Facebook Messenger with a study description and screening questions assessing their age, country/state of residence, race/ethnicity, sex, sexual orientation, and cannabis use status. Those deemed preliminarily eligible were sent a link to the full study description and consent form in Alchemer (i.e., an online survey platform), screened to confirm eligibility, and were administered the baseline survey. Upon completion, participants confirmed their participation in the study a week later by responding to an emailed survey link. Regarding fraud prevention, the chatbot prescreening was crucial to ensuring each individual prescreened had a Facebook account, which dramatically reduced bot activity and duplicate responses. Additional fraud prevention efforts included withholding details of eligibility criteria before screening, using the one-week follow-up period to examine data validity (e.g., duplicate IP addresses, e-mail addresses, phone numbers; illogical responses; survey completion time), and confirming validity of contact information before providing incentives (Heffner et al., 2021; Pratt-Chapman et al., 2021).

Of the 18,426 Facebook profiles who clicked ads, 6908 (37.5 %) completed the Chatbot pre-screening, and 6128 (88.7 % of those who completed the pre-screening) were preliminarily eligible and were provided screener and confirmation links. Of the 5081 (82.9 %) who consented, 129 (2.2 %) were excluded due to: (a) ineligibility (n=14) or (b) not completing the screening (n=115). Among the remaining 5672 individuals, 974 (17.2 %) provided only partial survey data. Of the 4698 who completed the baseline survey, 313 (6.7 %) were not sent confirmation links because they did not provide valid contact information. Of the 4385 provided confirmation links, 4031 (91.9 %) confirmed participation, were fully enrolled, and received a $10 Amazon e-gift card incentive. The current analyses were restricted to 1100 participants identifying as SM (i.e., gay/lesbian, bisexual, or another non-heterosexual identity).

Measures

Sample selection variable: sexual orientation

Participants were asked “How would you describe your sexual orientation (Please choose the option that best applies): straight, bisexual, gay/lesbian, other-please specify, prefer not to answer.” Those who reported bisexual, gay/lesbian, or another sexual orientation (e.g., queer, pansexual) were included in the current analyses. Those who reported straight (N=2844), prefer not to answer (N=82), or that they were unsure (N=5) were excluded.

Tobacco use outcomes

Participants reported the number of days in the past 30 they used cigarettes, e-cigarettes, cigars, hookah, smokeless tobacco (SLT) or snus, and tobacco-free nicotine pouches (0–30 days). Responses for each product were dichotomized (0: 0 days; 1: ≥1 day). We constructed 4 outcome variables: any vs. no current cigarette use, e-cigarette use, tobacco use (i.e., all products aggregated), and total number of products used (among those reporting current use; 1–6 products). Tobacco products other than cigarettes and e-cigarettes were not examined as individual outcomes due to low use prevalence.

Primary predictor: SM state policy categories

Participants reported their state of residence, which was linked to SM state policy variables derived from the Movement Advancement Project’s (MAP) SM state policy coding system in Fall 2023 (Movement Advancement Project MAP, 2024). Seven SM state policy variables were created to reflect the 7 categories of policies covered by MAP: (1) relationship/parent recognition (e.g., nondiscrimination protections for SM parents adopting); (2) nondiscrimination (e.g., employment nondiscrimination laws); (3) religious exemptions from nondiscrimination policies (e.g., for medical professionals); (4) LGBTQ youth (e.g., school nondiscrimination policies); (5) healthcare (e.g., private health insurance nondiscrimination laws); (6) criminal justice (e.g., hate crime laws); and (7) gender identity documents (e.g., laws surrounding gender markers on identity documents). For each state, MAP assigns each of the 7 policy categories an overall score based on the presence or absence of multiple harmful/discriminatory policies and/or inclusive/protective policies. Scores correspond to one of 5 categories – negative: <0 indicating only harmful/discriminatory policies; low: 0–24.9 % of total possible points; fair: 25–49.9 %; medium: 50–74.9 %; and high: 75–100 %. Thus, each policy could have a possible score of 0 (negative)-4 (comprehensive).

Consistent with prior work assessing SM state policy in relation to substance use and health (Drabble et al., 2021; Drabble, Munroe, et al., 2022; Gonzales and Ehrenfeld, 2018), our preliminary analyses, and to prevent reducing statistical power, we aggregated across certain categories of SM state policy variables. Our preliminary analyses using the 5-level policy variables (i.e., negative, low, fair, medium, high) indicated that low, fair, and medium policies were similarly associated with minority stress mediators and tobacco use and thus, were aggregated for power and parsimony. Note that relationship/parent recognition policies do not contain harmful/discriminatory policies; thus, states were assigned a code of limited versus comprehensive. Religious exemption policies do not contain protective policies and thus, states were assigned a code of negative versus no policies (i.e., absence of all negative policies). Additional details regarding data and coding can be found elsewhere (Movement Advancement Project MAP, 2024). See Supplementary Table 1 for additional information on SM state policy coding.

Minority stress mediators

Mental health symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire – 4 item (PHQ-4), including 2 items assessing depressive and anxiety symptoms, respectively, in the past 2 weeks (0=not at all-3=nearly every day; scores summed, range 0–12; α=.88) (Löwe et al., 2010). Internalized stigma was assessed using the Internalized Sexual Stigma Scale, containing 9 items assessing endorsement of sexual identity stigma as part of one’s self-concept (1=strongly disagree-5=strongly agree; scores averaged; α=.88) (Herek and Glunt, 2001). SM community connectedness was assessed with 5 items asking participants to indicate whether they feel part of the queer community, know others who are queer, have peers who are queer, feel part of a community group for being queer, and have been to a community center for queer individuals (0=no, 1=yes; scores summed, range 0–5; α=.70) (Goldbach et al., 2015).

Covariates

Covariates included participant self-report of age, gender identity (i. e., “Recognizing that gender identity can be complex, which gender best applies to you?”; cisgender man, cisgender woman, gender minority), sexual identity (monosexual, bisexual+, another sexual identity), race (White, Black, Asian, another race), ethnicity (non-Hispanic, Hispanic), employment (full-time, part-time, student, unemployed), and past-month cannabis use (no/yes). We also controlled for state-level tobacco policy using American Lung Association’s state-level tobacco grades (American Lung Association ALA, 2024), which assign a grade of A (excellent), B, C, D, or F (inadequate) to each state with regard to: prevention/cessation funding, smokefree laws, excise taxes, access to cessation, and flavored product restrictions. State grades were reverse coded (F=0-A=4) and scores were averaged across policies.

Data analysis

Descriptive and bivariate statistics were conducted with SPSS v28 and primary analyses were conducted with Mplus 8.8. First, descriptive statistics characterized participants and examined response distributions. Next, bivariate analyses (i.e., Pearson’s correlations, independent-samples t-tests, one-way ANOVAs, Chi-square tests) examined associations between covariates (i.e., age, gender identity, sexual identity, race, ethnicity, employment, current cannabis use, state tobacco control policies) and SM policy categories (i.e., relationship/parent recognition, nondiscrimination, religious exemptions, LGBTQ youth, healthcare, criminal justice, gender identity documents) with minority stress (i.e., mental health symptoms, internalized stigma, community connectedness) and tobacco use (i.e., current cigarette, e-cigarette, any tobacco use, total number of products used), as well as associations among minority stress and tobacco use. Prior to running regression-based models, we tested for multicollinearity of SM state policy categories by examining tolerance (>.2) and VIF (<5) (Kim, 2019), conducting sensitivity analyses that examined separate models for each SM state policy category, and testing for suppression by comparing findings from primary analyses to bivariate analyses. Together, preliminary analyses indicated that multicollinearity was not present. Finally, regression-based models were conducted separately for each tobacco use outcome. Each of the 4 regression-based models examined: 1) direct associations of SM state policy categories with minority stress and tobacco use; 2) direct associations of minority stress with tobacco use; and 3) indirect associations of SM state policies on tobacco use via minority stress using the MODEL INDIRECT command (see Fig. 1) (Muthen and Muthen, 2017). All models accounted for the non-independence of observations (i.e., participants residing within the same state) by including participants’ state of residence as a cluster variable, which provides robust standard errors (Muthen and Muthen, 2017). Models controlled for all covariates. Acceptable model fit values are CFI values of ≥.90 and RMSEA and SRMR values of ≤.08 (Kline, 2015).

Conceptual model depicting direct effects of: covariates > minority stress factors; covariates > tobacco use outcome; SM state policy categories > minority stress factors; SM state policy categories > tobacco use outcome; minority stress factors > tobacco use outcome and indirect effects of: SM state policy categories on tobacco use outcome through minority stress mediators. Solid arrows depict direct effects, dashed arrows depict indirect effects. One model was conducted for each tobacco use outcome, separately.

Results

Participant characteristics

Shown in Table 1, in this sample of YAs (representing 58.8 % past-month cannabis use), participants were 25.34 (SD=4.85) years old on average, 53.7 % identified as cisgender women, 32.1 % cisgender men, and 14.2 % gender minority; and 66.1 % identified as bisexual+, 29.1 % monosexual, and 4.8 % another sexual identity. Regarding race and ethnicity, 65.3 % identified as White, 14.7 % Black, 9.2 % Asian, 10.8 % another race, and 18.5 % Hispanic. Additionally, 31.2 % were students, 29.8 % employed full-time, 24.5 % unemployed, and 14.5 % employed part-time. Participants residing in states with negative policies ranged from 3.9 % (weaker nondiscrimination) to 60.4 % (religious exemptions), those in states with limited policy protections ranged from 32.3 % (healthcare) to 66.6 % (relationship/parent recognition), and those in states with comprehensive policy protections ranged from 29.1 % (criminal justice) to 54.8 % (nondiscrimination). See Supplementary Table 2 for additional information on SM policy scores by state. Regarding current tobacco use (Table 2), 23.6% reported cigarette use, 30.2 % e-cigarette use, and 40.6 % any tobacco use. Participants reporting current use used between 2 and 3 products on average (M=2.07[SD=1.25]).

Discussion

The current study elucidates specific SM state policy categories associated with SMYAs’ tobacco use behaviors, as well as minority stress mediators of these associations. SM state policy categories showed distinct associations with tobacco use in expected directions (i.e., negative vs. comprehensive policy protections were associated with higher odds of use). Negative healthcare policies predicted cigarette, e-cigarette, and any tobacco use and negative LGBTQ youth policies predicted e-cigarette and tobacco use relative to comprehensive policy protections. Weaker relationship/parent recognition policies were associated with e-cigarette use and weaker nondiscrimination policies were associated with cigarette use and using more products. These associations occurred after accounting for state tobacco policies, which are highly correlated with reduced tobacco use (Mader et al., 2016), and it is worth noting that at least some SM state policy categories appear to influence risk even when accounting for highly effective tobacco policies. With the exception of relationship/parent recognition, differences between limited and comprehensive policies were not significant, which may suggest that residing in states with even limited SM policy protections may be protective for SMYAs, although this warrants further examination.

Findings emphasize the importance of disaggregating SM policy categories. While several prior studies have documented associations between SM state policies and tobacco use (Pachankis et al., 2014; Titus et al., 2021), others point to null findings (Drabble et al., 2022). This inconsistency may be related to aggregating across a range of policy categories, masking the influence of certain policy categories most strongly associated with tobacco use. Indeed, in the current study, policies related to nondiscrimination, relationship/parent recognition, LGBTQ youth, and healthcare were associated with tobacco use after accounting for covariates and minority stress, whereas those related to religious exemptions, criminal justice, and gender identity documents were not associated with such use. These null findings warrant additional investigation given the lack of research investigating specific policy categories in relation to SMYAs’ tobacco use. It is possible that nondiscrimination policies, which comprehensively cover areas related to employment, housing, public accommodations, credit and lending, and state employment account for a greater portion of the variance in SMYAs’ tobacco use relative to religious exemption policies, which allow a smaller number of specific entities (child welfare services, medical professionals) to refuse services to SM individuals on the basis of religion. Similarly, policies related to protecting the rights of gender minority individuals are captured in several policy categories (i.e., LGBTQ youth, healthcare, nondiscrimination), which may be associated with tobacco use over and above those related to gender identity documents specifically.

Several SM policy-tobacco use associations were mediated by minority stress. Healthcare policies were most strongly associated with cigarette and any tobacco use through SM community connectedness. Negative SM healthcare policies are more strongly associated with lower SM population density relative to most other policy categories (MAP, 2024). Thus, SMYAs in these states may experience less SM community support due to having fewer SM peers or community centers (Movement Advancement Project MAP, 2019), which is associated with greater tobacco use (Eisenberg et al., 2020; McDonald, 2018; Watson et al., 2020). Relationship/parent recognition policies were most strongly associated with e-cigarette use; this association was mediated by mental health symptoms. SM parents in states with fewer parent protections report greater intolerance and discrimination from their communities (Goldberg and Smith, 2011) and SM individuals face greater barriers to starting a family via adoption or fostering, leading to greater stress and mental health symptoms, particularly during young adulthood when individuals are most likely to engage in family planning (Morse, 2022). SMYAs may use e-cigarettes to cope with these symptoms, given ample evidence for associations among mental health symptoms and e-cigarette use (Azagba et al., 2024; Riehm et al., 2019; Romm et al., 2023), particularly among SM individuals (Valera et al., 2021). Weaker nondiscrimination policies were most strongly associated with using more tobacco products through greater internalized stigma, consistent with individual-level research suggesting associations among discrimination and internalized stigma related to one’s SM identity (Hatzenbuehler, 2016; Lin et al., 2022; Meyer, 2003). SMYAs may use a greater number of tobacco products to dull internal shame and guilt related to their sexual orientation (Huynh et al., 2022). Relative to single tobacco product use, research suggests that individuals engage in polytobacco use to cope with feelings of stress (Leventhal et al., 2017). However, current findings warrant additional investigation, given the sparsity of the literature regarding the unique influence of specific policy categories in relation to minority stress and tobacco use.

Findings underscore the importance of disaggregating SM policy categories when estimating their impact on public health and examining a wider range of mechanisms through which structural stigma may influence SMYAs’ tobacco use. SM policy categories that were most strongly associated with tobacco use behaviors may be important initial policy targets for reducing tobacco use among SMYAs: policies related to nondiscrimination, relationship/parent recognition, LGBTQ youth, and healthcare. It is also crucial to develop targeted tobacco prevention and intervention efforts for SMYAs residing in negative SM policy contexts. Findings highlight mental health symptoms as an important target for SMYAs in states with limited relationship/parent recognition policies, internalized stigma in states with weaker nondiscrimination policies, and SM community connectedness in states with negative healthcare policies.

Limitations

Findings should be interpreted in light of limitations, including potentially limited generalizability to other US SMYAs, given targeted social media-based recruitment of YAs who use cannabis, likely leading to overrepresentation of SM identity (for whom cannabis use is more common (SAMHSA, 2023) and tobacco use (Schauer et al., 2015); indeed, past-month tobacco use rates in this sample (23.6 % cigarettes, 40.6 % any product) were slightly higher than the national average among SMYAs ages 18–34 (18.0 % cigarettes; 36.8 % any product) (SAMHSA, 2023). We also did not have SM participant representation across 5 states (i.e., AK, ND, SD, CT, and WY) and had limited SM participant representation (<10 participants) in several other states (e. g., HI, ID, MT). Second, survey data were at a single timepoint; future research should examine these associations over time. Third, we were unable to examine every specific policy tracked by MAP given the large number of policies (i.e., >50) and thus, limited power to detect associations. Finally, the internalized stigma measure included questions asking about attraction to individuals of the same/opposite sex only and thus, may be less relevant to participants with attraction to gender-minoritized individuals.

Conclusion

Understanding the impact of SM state policy on SMYAs’ health outcomes is an urgent matter, given rapid increases in harmful anti-SM policies introduced in the US (American Civil Liberties Union ACLU, 2024; Human Rights Campaign, 2023). Findings highlight the importance of disaggregating SM state policy categories in future research examining their relation to SMYAs’ tobacco use behaviors, as well as a range of psychological and social minority stress mediators of these associations in future research. This information is critical to ultimately inform SM-related policy, advocacy, prevention, and intervention efforts aimed at curtailing tobacco use and related health disparities among SMYAs.
