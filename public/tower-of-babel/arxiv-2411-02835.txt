Community detection with the Bethe-Hessian
Ludovic Stephan, Yizhe Zhu

Abstract
The Bethe-Hessian matrix, introduced by Saade, Krzakala, and Zdeborová ( Saade et al., 2014 ) , is a Hermitian matrix designed for applying spectral clustering algorithms to sparse networks. Rather than employing a non-symmetric and high-dimensional non-backtracking operator, a spectral method based on the Bethe-Hessian matrix is conjectured to also reach the Kesten-Stigum detection threshold in the sparse stochastic block model (SBM). We provide the first rigorous analysis of the Bethe-Hessian spectral method in the SBM under both the bounded expected degree and the growing degree regimes. Specifically, we demonstrate that: (i) When the expected degree d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , the number of negative outliers of the Bethe-Hessian matrix can consistently estimate the number of blocks above the Kesten-Stigum threshold, thus confirming a conjecture from Saade et al. (2014) for d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . (ii) For sufficiently large d d , its eigenvectors can be used to achieve weak recovery. (iii) As d → ∞ d\to\infty , we establish the concentration of the locations of its negative outlier eigenvalues, and weak consistency can be achieved via a spectral method based on the Bethe-Hessian matrix. 1 1 1 Accepted for presentation at the Conference on Learning Theory (COLT) 2025.

Introduction

The challenge of recovering community structures in networks has spurred significant advancements in spectral algorithms for very sparse graphs. A popular model for studying community detection on random graphs is the stochastic block model (SBM), first introduced in
Holland et al. (1983)
. The SBM is a generative model for random graphs with a community structure, serving as a useful benchmark for clustering algorithms on graph data. When the random graph generated by an SBM is sparse with bounded expected degrees, a phase transition has been observed around the so-called
Kesten-Stigum threshold
. In particular, above this threshold, a wealth of algorithms are known to achieve weak recovery (better than a random guess)
(
Mossel et al., 2018
;
Abbe and Sandon, 2018
;
Coja-Oghlan et al., 2018
;
Hopkins and Steurer, 2017
;
Ding et al., 2022
)
. Several spectral algorithms have been proposed based on different matrices associated with the SBM, including self-avoiding
(
Massoulié, 2014
)
, non-backtracking
(
Decelle et al., 2011
;
Krzakala et al., 2013
;
Bordenave et al., 2018
)
, graph powering
(
Abbe et al., 2020
)
, or distance
(
Stephan and Massoulié, 2019
)
matrices. For additional references and a more in-depth discussion of the Kesten-Stigum detection threshold, we refer interested readers to the survey of
Abbe (2018)
.

For spectral clustering in random graphs with constant expected degree, a popular choice is the
non-backtracking operator

(
Krzakala et al., 2013
;
Bordenave et al., 2018
)
. In the case of the SBM, it has been shown
(
Bordenave et al., 2018
)
that the spectral method based on the non-backtracking operator can achieve the Kesten-Stigum threshold. In recent years, the non-backtracking operator has been crucial for analyzing the spectrum of sparse random matrices
(
Benaych-Georges et al., 2020
;
Alt et al., 2021b
;
Dumitriu and Zhu, 2024
;
Bordenave, 2020
;
Bordenave and Collins, 2019
)
and for enabling effective low-rank matrix recovery under sparse noise
(
Bordenave, 2020
;
Stephan and Massoulié, 2022
;
Stephan and Zhu, 2024b
)
. Compared to the more classical adjacency matrix or Laplacian matrix, the non-backtracking operator is non-Hermitian, and its spectrum is more informative and stable when the underlying graph is very sparse.

Recent works on the spectra of sparse random matrices
(
Benaych-Georges et al., 2019
;
Alt et al., 2024
;
Hiesmayr and McKenzie, 2025
)
show that, when the expected degree is constant, the top eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the adjacency matrix are not informative about the partition. This phenomenon is known as
eigenvector localization
in random matrix theory for sparse Hermitian random matrices. Removing high-degree vertices can mitigate localization
(
Feige and Ofek, 2005
;
Chin et al., 2015
;
Le et al., 2017
)
, but it causes significant information loss in the original graph, and approaches based on regularized adjacency or Laplacian matrices have yet to reach the fundamental detection threshold
(
Le et al., 2017
)
.

On the other hand, a general phenomenon is that the spectrum of non-Hermitian random matrices is less sensitive to rows or columns with large
\ell_{2}
-norms
(
Benaych-Georges et al., 2020
;
Coste, 2023
;
Bordenave et al., 2022
;
He, 2023
)
compared to the Hermitian ones. This partially explains why the non-backtracking operator performs better on very sparse graphs compared to other Hermitian operators. This
“asymmetry helps”
principle has been leveraged to design novel algorithms with improved performance
(
Chen et al., 2021
;
Bordenave et al., 2023
;
Stephan and Zhu, 2024b
)
in low-rank matrix recovery problems.

The non-backtracking matrix is of size
2m\times 2m
, where
m
is the number of edges in a graph, rather than
n\times n
, where
n
is the number of vertices. When the average degree of a graph is large, finding the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of such a high-dimensional matrix is computationally expensive. Although, as shown in
Krzakala et al. (2013)
;
Stephan and Zhu (2024a)
, we can work with a smaller
2n\times 2n
non-Hermitian block matrix using the Ihara-Bass formula
(
Bass, 1992
)
, this approach is still less efficient than the spectral method on
n\times n
Hermitian matrices. Additionally, linear algebra methods are generally faster and more stable for symmetric matrices than for non-symmetric ones. More importantly, the reduction to a
2n\times 2n
matrix does not apply to weighted graphs
(
Bordenave et al., 2023
;
Stephan and Massoulié, 2022
)
, as the Ihara-Bass formula becomes more complex and requires an additional parameter
(
Benaych-Georges et al., 2020
)
.

Several
n\times n
Hermitian matrices, such as the self-avoiding matrix
(
Massoulié, 2014
)
, the distance matrix
(
Stephan and Massoulié, 2019
)
, and the graph powering matrix
(
Abbe et al., 2020
)
, have been shown to reach the Kesten–Stigum threshold with spectral methods. However, each of these approaches requires setting a hyper-parameter
\ell=c\log n
to transform the adjacency matrix
A
into a new matrix
A^{(\ell)}
, which is essentially a modified version of
A^{\ell}
. The preprocessing step needed to compute
A^{(\ell)}
has a time complexity of
O(n^{1+\kappa})
for some constant
\kappa>0
, rendering these methods impractical for large-scale problems.

This raises a natural question: is there a solution that offers the best of both worlds?

Is there an efficient spectral method with an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix that performs as well as the non-backtracking matrix for community detection?

The Bethe-Hessian matrix

Such an operator exists in the statistical physics literature, known as the
Bethe-Hessian matrix
, proposed by Saade, Krzakala, and Zdeborová in
Saade et al. (2014)
. This operator is a linear combination of the adjacency matrix

A
and the diagonal degree matrix

D
, with one parameter
t\in\mathbb{R}
given by:

H(t):=t^{2}-tA+(D-I).

(1)

H(t)
is also called a deformed graph Laplacian
(
Grindrod et al., 2018
)
.
In the context of SBM, the parameter
t
is chosen as
t=\pm\sqrt{d}
in
Saade et al. (2014)
, where
d
is the average expected degree, and it can be estimated by the empirical mean degree. It was conjectured in
Saade et al. (2014)
that the number of negative outlier eigenvalues of
H(\pm\sqrt{d})
can consistently estimate the number of assortative and disassortative communities respectively above the Kesten-Stigum threshold and the eigenvectors associated with these negative outliers can be used to detect the communities.

Figure 1:
Eigenvalue distributions of the Bethe-Hessian matrix
H(\sqrt{d})
for a 2-block SBM, where
n=4000,P_{11}=P_{22}=10
, and
P_{12}=P_{21}=2
. There are two negative outliers.

The Bethe-Hessian method is perhaps the simplest algorithm conjectured to achieve the Kesten-Sitgum threshold in the SBM. Since the pioneering work in
Saade et al. (2014)
, it has been widely used in spectral clustering
(
Dall’Amico et al., 2019
;
Dall’Amico et al., 2021a
;
Dall’Amico et al., 2021b
)
, change point detection
(
Hwang et al., 2022
)
, and estimating the number of communities in networks
(
Le and Levina, 2022
;
Hwang et al., 2023
;
Shao and Le, 2024
)
. The popular network analysis package NetworkX
(
Hagberg et al., 2008
)
, includes this algorithm.

From a random matrix theory perspective, the Bethe-Hessian matrix introduces a new phenomenon not explained by existing theory. Results in sparse random matrices
(
Alt et al., 2021a
;
Alt et al., 2021b
;
Alt et al., 2023
;
Ducatez and Rivier, 2025
)
suggest that the Hermitian matrix
H(\pm\sqrt{d})
should have many outlier eigenvalues and suffer from eigenvector localization. However, as shown in Figure
1
, there is
separation between informative and uninformative outliers
. The localization effect does appear in
H(\pm\sqrt{d})
: there are many large positive eigenvalues, and their corresponding eigenvectors are localized on high-degree vertices. The striking phenomenon on the negative real line, however, still lacks a theoretical explanation:

Why do the negative outlier eigenvalues and eigenvectors in
H(\pm\sqrt{d})
not suffer from localization and still contain community information?

Our contribution

We take a first step toward explaining this phenomenon and theoretically justifying the Bethe-Hessian spectral method in
Saade et al. (2014)
when the expected degree of the SBM is constant or grows with
n
arbitrarily slowly. Specifically, we establish the following results for the Bethe-Hessian matrix under the SBM:

•

When the expected degree
d\geq 2
, we show that above the Kesten-Stigum threshold, negative outliers of
H(\pm\sqrt{d})
consistently estimate the number of communities in the SBM, which confirms a conjecture in
Saade et al. (2014)
for
d\geq 2
; see Theorem
3
.

•

When
d
is sufficiently large, we can approximate the eigenvalue locations (see Theorem
6
) and eigenvectors (see Theorem
7
) of the Bethe-Hessian above the Kesten-Stigum threshold. This characterization allows us to show that a spectral algorithm achieves weak recovery in the SBM; see Theorem
9
.

•

When
d\to\infty
, the eigenvalue and eigenvector bounds of Theorems
6
and
7
become even sharper, which allows our algorithm to achieve weak consistency (see Corollary
10
). This provides a provable algorithm to achieve consistency without the degree regularization step (removing high-degree vertices).

•

Along the way to proving these results, we also show that in the SBM, all outlier eigenvalues of the non-backtracking matrix are real (see Theorem
5
). This phenomenon has been empirically observed, but no justification exists in the previous literature.

To analyze the performance of the Bethe-Hessian spectral algorithms, we establish new connections between the non-backtracking matrix and the Bethe-Hessian matrix. Roughly speaking, the negative eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Bethe-Hessian matrix approximately perform the function of a non-backtracking operator.

A key component in our argument is to show that the subspace spanned by the informative eigenvectors of
H(\pm\sqrt{d})
can be approximated by the subspace spanned by eigenvectors of the
2n\times 2n
reduced non-backtracking matrix. Therefore, results on the spectra of the non-backtracking matrix
(
Bordenave et al., 2018
;
Stephan and Zhu, 2024a
)
can be used to approximate the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Bethe-Hessian
H(\pm\sqrt{d})
. These approximations are quantified using the Courant minimax principle, perturbation analysis of Hermitian matrices, and a continuity argument based on the Ihara-Bass formula. Additionally, we require a precise asymptotic analysis of the overlap between eigenvector components of the reduced non-backtracking matrix
\tilde{B}
, achieved through a quantitative version of local weak convergence for sparse random graphs studied in
Stephan and Zhu (2024a)
.

Related work

There are only a few rigorous results for the Bethe-Hessian matrix in the literature.
Le and Levina (2022)
showed that if the expected degree is
\omega(\log n)
in an SBM, one can consistently estimate the number of assortative communities (those associated with positive eigenvalues of the expected adjacency matrix) and the locations of the negative eigenvalues of the Bethe-Hessian.
Hwang et al. (2023)
extended the range of consistent estimation down to
d=\omega(1)
; however, their proof technique does not provide any information on the location of the negative eigenvalues of the Bethe-Hessian. Very recently,
Mohanty et al. (2024)
showed that above the Kesten-Stigum threshold, if the SBM has
k
blocks and the probability matrix has
k-1
repeated eigenvalues, then there are at least
k-1
and at most
k
negative eigenvalues of
H(\lambda)
for a properly chosen
\lambda
. We provide a more detailed comparison with existing results in Section
3.3
.

Different choices of the parameter
t
for the Bethe-Hessian were proposed in
Dall’Amico et al. (2019)
;
Dall’Amico et al. (2021a)
;
Dall’Amico et al. (2021b)
, where the authors empirically demonstrated improved performance on spectral clustering in the SBM compared to
H(\pm\sqrt{d})
. This is closely connected to an intriguing eigenvalue insider phenomenon
(
Dall’Amico et al., 2019
;
Coste and Zhu, 2021
)
for the non-backtracking matrix. Beyond community detection, the Bethe-Hessian matrix appears in different forms in various problems, including matrix completion
(
Saade et al., 2015
)
and phase retrieval
(
Maillard et al., 2022
)
. A higher-order generalization of the Bethe-Hessian called the Kikuchi-Hessian
(
Saade, 2016
)
, was introduced to study the computational-to-statistical gap in tensor PCA
(
Wein et al., 2019
)
.

Our work provides a theoretical justification of the Bethe-Hessian method in the statistical physics literature. We expect this simple method can be adapted to develop new algorithms beyond the classical SBM (e.g., random graphs with random weights
(
Stephan and Massoulié, 2022
)
, hypergraphs
(
Stephan and Zhu, 2024a
)
, graphon estimation
(
Abbe et al., 2023
)
) and community detection with additional constraints (e.g., robustness
(
Stephan and Massoulié, 2019
;
Mohanty et al., 2024
)
and privacy
(
Mohamed et al., 2022
)
). Moreover, we expect that this method can be adapted to provable and efficient algorithms for matrix and tensor completion with very few samples
(
Bordenave et al., 2023
;
Stephan and Zhu, 2024b
)
; an example of such a problem where the Bethe Hessian has shown promise can be found in
(
Saade et al., 2015
)
.

Preliminaries

Stochastic Block Model

We work under a general SBM setting as follows.

Definition 1

(Stochastic block model (SBM))
.

Given an
r\times r
symmetric nonnegative matrix
P
. We generate a random graph with
n
vertices in the following way. Let
\sigma:[n]\to[r]
be the label assignment of each vertex. Then
i,j
are connected independently with probability
\frac{1}{n}P_{\sigma(i),\sigma(j)}
.

We have the following model parameters:

•

Denote the block size ratio by

\pi_{k}=\frac{|\{i\in[n],\sigma(i)=k\}|}{n}.

•

We assume constant average degree:

d:=\sum_{j\in[r]}P_{ij}\pi_{j}>1,\quad\forall i\in[r].

When
d<1
, the random graph has no giant component, and detection is impossible.

•

The signal matrix
Q=P\Pi
, where
\Pi=\mathrm{diag}({\pi})
is a diagonal matrix. Since
Q
is similar to
\Pi^{1/2}P\Pi^{1/2}
, its eigenvalues are real and we order them by absolute value:

|\mu_{r}|\leq|\mu_{r-1}|\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{1}=d.

•

Let
\psi_{i},1\leq i\leq r
be the orthonormal eigenvectors of
\Pi^{1/2}P\Pi^{1/2}
. Eigenvectors of
Q
are given by
\phi_{i}:=\Pi^{-1/2}\psi_{i},1\leq i\leq r
. Hence

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\pi}:=\sum_{k\in[r]}\pi_{k}\phi_{i}(k)\phi_{j}(k)=\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}.

(2)

•

The informative eigenvalues are defined by
\mu_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{2}>d.

(3)

Let
r_{0}
be the number of informative eigenvalues, such that
\mu_{r_{0}}^{2}>d\geq\mu_{r_{0}+1}^{2}
; they are split between
r_{+}
positive and
r_{-}
negative eigenvalues, denoted

\displaystyle\mu_{1}^{+}\geq\dots\geq\mu_{r_{+}}^{+}>\sqrt{d}\quad\ \ {}\quad\mu_{1}^{-}\leq\dots\leq\mu_{r_{-}}^{-}<-\sqrt{d}.

(4)

•

Denote

\tau_{i}=\frac{d}{\mu_{i}^{2}}

to be the inverse signal-to-noise ratio associated with
\mu_{i}
, and similarly,
\tau_{i}^{\pm}:=\frac{d}{\left(\mu_{i}^{\pm}\right)^{2}}
. The Kesten-Stigum condition (
3
) thus implies that
\tau_{i}\in(0,1)
for
i\in[r_{0}]
.

Overlap and recovery regimes

Given an estimate
\hat{\sigma}
of the community assignment
\sigma
, the
overlap
between the two vectors is defined as

\operatorname{ov}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})=\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\sigma}(x)=\mathfrak{p}\circ\sigma(x)\},

where the maximum is over all permutations of
[r]
. Following the nomenclature of
Abbe (2018)
, we say that an estimator
\hat{\sigma}
(asymptotically) achieves:

•

weak recovery
if
\operatorname{ov}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})=\max_{i}\pi_{i}+\delta
for some
\delta>0
,

•

almost exact recovery
(or
weak consistency
) if
\operatorname{ov}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})=1-o(1)
.

The baseline performance of
\max_{i}\pi_{i}
corresponds to the dummy estimator that assigns every vertex to the largest community.

Non-backtracking matrix

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. For
V=[n]
, the
(i,j)
-th entry of the
adjacency matrix

A
of a graph
G
is defined as

A_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\{i,j\}\in E,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The degree matrix
D
of a graph
G
is a diagonal matrix where

D_{ii}=\sum_{j\in V}A_{ij}.

Define the oriented edge set
\vec{E}
for
G
as

\displaystyle\vec{E}=\{(i,j):\{i,j\}\in E\}.

(5)

Each edge yields two oriented edges; therefore,
|\vec{E}|=2|E|
. The
non-backtracking operator

B
of
G
is a non-Hermitian operator of size
|\vec{E}|\times|\vec{E}|
. For any
(u,v),(x,y)\in\vec{E}
, the corresponding entry of
B
is defined as

B_{(u,v),(x,y)}=\begin{cases}1&\text{$v=x$, $u\neq y$},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

A useful identity we will use in this paper is the following Ihara-Bass formula
(
Bass, 1992
)
:

Lemma 2

(Ihara-Bass formula)
.

For any graph
G=(V,E)
, and any
z\in\mathbb{C}
, the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\det(B-zI)=(z^{2}-1)^{|E|-n}\det(z^{2}I-zA+D-I).

(6)

Reduced non-backtracking matrix and the Bethe-Hessian

Consider the block matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{B}=\begin{bmatrix}0&D-I\\
-I&A\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}.

(7)

Then from (
6
), we have

\displaystyle\det(B-zI)=(z^{2}-1)^{|E|-n}\det(\tilde{B}-zI).

(8)

The identity (
8
) implies that
B
and
\tilde{B}
share the same spectrum, up to the multiplicity of trivial eigenvalues
\pm 1
. We also call
\tilde{B}
the
reduced non-backtracking matrix
of
G
.

Let
v_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\\
y_{i}\end{bmatrix}
be an eigenvector of
\tilde{B}
with associated eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}:=\lambda_{i}(\tilde{B})
, where
\lambda_{i}
is also the corresponding eigenvalue of
B
and
x_{i},y_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We normalize
y_{i}
such that
\|y_{i}\|_{2}=1
.
By solving

\tilde{B}v_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i},

we obtain the following relation:

\displaystyle(D-I)y_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}x_{i},

(9)

\displaystyle-x_{i}+Ay_{i}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}y_{i}.

(10)

In particular,
H(\lambda_{i})y_{i}=0
, so the
y_{i}
correspond to the zero eigenvectors of the Bethe-Hessian of
G
.

When
G
is a regular graph, one can show that
x_{i},y_{i}
are perfectly aligned
Zhu and Zhu (2023)
. As we will see in Section
A.1
, this is not the case for the SBM, but one can compute the asymptotic values of
\langle x_{i},y_{i}\rangle
.
Spectral clustering with the eigenvectors of
\tilde{B}
was first proposed in
Krzakala et al. (2013)
. This was rigorously justified in
Bordenave et al. (2018)
, with the correspondence made explicit in
Stephan and Zhu (2024a)
, showing that
y_{i},i\in[r_{0}]
are correlated with eigenvectors of
Q
.

Notations

For
n\times n
Hermitian matrices
A
, we order eigenvalues in decreasing order:
\lambda_{1}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(A)
. For any
n\times n
non-Hermitian matrix
B
, we order its eigenvalues in decreasing order for the modulus:
|\lambda_{1}(B)|\geq\cdots\geq|\lambda_{n}(B)|
.

We say that an event
E_{n}
happens
with high probability
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\lparen E_{n}\rparen=1
; unless otherwise specified, in this article all high-probability events happen with probability at least
1-e^{-c\log_{d}(n)}
, where
d
is the average expected degree.

3
Main results

3.1
Bounded expected degree regime

In this section, we make the following assumption:

Assumption 1
.

The parameters
\pi_{i},P_{ij}
(and hence
d,r
and
r_{0}
) are independent of
n
.

Our first result is an almost complete characterization of the relationship between the informative eigenvalues of
Q
and the negative eigenvalues of
H(\pm\sqrt{d})
.
For
t\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
, we define

N_{\varepsilon}(t)
the number of eigenvalues of
H(t)
below
-\varepsilon
, and
N(t)
be the number of negative eigenvalues of
H(t)
.

Theorem 3

(Estimating the number of communities)
.

Assume that the average degree satisfies
d\geq 2
and Assumption
1
holds. For any
\varepsilon>0
small enough, the following holds with high probability for sufficiently large
n
:

N_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{d})=N(\sqrt{d}+\varepsilon)=r_{+}\quad\ \ {}\quad N_{\varepsilon}(-\sqrt{d})=N(-\sqrt{d}-\varepsilon)=r_{-}.

(11)

The above is also valid when taking
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}=\log(n)^{-c}
for any
c>0
.

Theorem
3
rigorously justifies the prediction in
Saade et al. (2014)
for general stochastic block models when
d\geq 2
for estimating the number of communities in the SBM, and extends the previous results from
d\to\infty
in
Le and Levina (2022)
;
Hwang et al. (2023)
to fixed
d
. It remains open to show the consistency of the Bethe-Hessian estimator for a relatively narrow range of
d\in(1,2)
.

In fact, the Bethe-Hessian method is parameter-free since
d
can be estimated by the sample mean degree

\displaystyle\hat{d}:=\frac{1}{n}(d_{1}+\dots+d_{n}),

(12)

where
d_{i}
is the degree of vertex
i\in[n]
, which yields the following corollary:

Corollary 4
.

Let
\varepsilon_{n}=\log(n)^{-c}
for any
c>0
. For sufficiently large
n
, with high probability,

\displaystyle N_{\varepsilon_{n}}\left(\sqrt{\hat{d}}\right)=N\left(\sqrt{\hat{d}}+\varepsilon_{n}\right)=r_{+},\quad N_{\varepsilon_{n}}\left(-\sqrt{\hat{d}}\right)=N\left(-\sqrt{\hat{d}}-\varepsilon_{n}\right)=r_{-}.

(13)

3.1.1
Sketch of the proof for Theorem
3

To lower bound the number of negative eigenvalues, we use the Courant-Fisher theorem by showing that
y^{\top}H(t)y<0
for any unit vector
y
in a specific subspace. The dimension of the subspace provides a lower bound for the number of negative eigenvalues of
H(t)
without finding their exact eigenvectors.
This subspace is given by known eigenvectors of
H(t)
at different values of
t
; we take
y_{i}
to be in the kernel of
H(\lambda_{i})
, where
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i}(B)
is the
i
-th eigenvector of the non-backtracking matrix
B
, and the existence of
y_{i}
is guaranteed by the Ihara-Bass formula
(
Bass, 1992
)
. This choice of
y_{i}
allows us to compute exactly the quadratic form
y_{i}^{\top}H(t)y_{i}
:

y_{i}^{\top}H(t)y_{i}=\left(t-\frac{\langle y_{i},(D-I)y_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}\right)(t-\lambda_{i}),

where
D
is the diagonal degree matrix. It remains to compute both roots of the above polynomial. Classical results on the non-backtracking operator
(
Bordenave et al., 2018
)
show that
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}+o(1)
, and the scalar product
\langle y_{i},(D-1)y_{i}\rangle
can be computed using the local convergence properties of
G
studied in
Stephan and Zhu (2024a)
. We finally find

y_{i}^{\top}H(t)y_{i}=\left(t-\frac{d+1-\tau_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\right)(t-\mu_{i})+o(1)\quad\ \ {}\quad y_{i}^{\top}H(t)y_{j}=o(1)\text{ for }i\not=j.

Whenever
d\geq 2
, the first root is always lower (in magnitude) than
\sqrt{d}
, so the negative eigenspace of
H(t)
gains a dimension exactly when
t=\mu_{i}
.
The upper bound is shown via a continuity argument, similar to the one in
Mohanty et al. (2024)
, which connects the negative eigenvalues of
H(t)
to the real eigenvalue outliers of
B
. The complete proof of Theorem
3
can be found in Appendix
B.3
.

The argument crucially rests on the fact that all outlier eigenvalues of
B
are real with high probability. While generally accepted as folklore, all previous work on the non-backtracking spectrum of SBMs
(
Bordenave et al., 2018
;
Stephan and Massoulié, 2022
;
Stephan and Zhu, 2024a
)
only proved that outlier eigenvalues are close to the real line. Our inner product calculations (see Corollary
13
in the Appendix) allow us to close this gap:

Theorem 5
.

Let
B
be the non-backtracking operator of a stochastic block model under Assumption
1
. Then with high probability, for any
i\in[r_{0}]
,
\lambda_{i}
is a real eigenvalue of
\tilde{B}
and

\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}+O(n^{-c}),

for some constant
c>0
. Moreover, the corresponding eigenvector
\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\\
y_{i}\end{bmatrix}
is a real vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

When
d=\omega(\log n)
, this has been justified in
Le and Levina (2022)
;
Coste and Zhu (2021)
;
Wang and Wood (2023)
based on a deterministic result in
Angel et al. (2015, Theorem 3.7)
.

3.2
Growing degree regime

We now consider the growing degree regime where all parameters
\pi_{i},P_{ij}
are allowed to scale with
n
, such that the number of communities and the average degree satisfy

r,d\lesssim\operatorname{polylog}(n).

(14)

This encompasses both the semi-dense regime
d=\Theta(\log(n))
, at which perfect reconstruction is known to be possible
(
Abbe, 2018
)
, and the intermediary regime in which a (vanishing) fraction of the vertices will be misclassified, also known as weak consistency.

3.2.1
Spectrum of
H(\pm\sqrt{d})

The first theorem establishes the concentration of the negative outlier locations when
d\to\infty
:

Theorem 6

(Eigenvalue locations)
.

Assume that we are in the regime (
14
), and that the inverse signal-to-noise ratio satisfies
\tau_{r_{0}}\leq c
for some constant
c\in(0,1)
independent from
n
. Then, with high probability, the following holds:

(i)

For
1\leq k\leq r_{+}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n-k+1}(H(\sqrt{d}))=(\sqrt{d}-\mu_{k}^{+})\left(\sqrt{d}-\frac{d}{\mu_{k}^{+}}\right)+O(\sqrt{r_{+}d}).

(15)

(ii)

For
1\leq k\leq r_{-}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n-k+1}(H(-\sqrt{d}))=(\sqrt{d}-\mu_{k}^{-})\left(\sqrt{d}-\frac{d}{\mu_{k}^{-}}\right)+O(\sqrt{r_{-}d}).

(16)

(iii)

In particular, assume
d\to\infty
and
d\gg r
, for
k\in[r_{+}]
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n-k+1}\left(\frac{1}{d}H(\sqrt{d})\right)=\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau_{k}^{+}}}\right)\left(1-\sqrt{\tau_{k}^{+}}\right)+o(1).

(17)

And for
k\in[r_{-}]
,

\lambda_{n-k+1}\left(\frac{1}{d}H(-\sqrt{d})\right)=\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau_{k}^{-}}}\right)\left(1-\sqrt{\tau_{k}^{-}}\right)+o(1).

Theorem
6
is proved in Proposition
20
(i). We can also obtain a related result on the eigenvectors of
H(t)
. For
k\in[r_{\pm}]
, define
y_{k}^{\pm}
to be the zero eigenvector associated with

, where
\lambda_{k}^{\pm}
is the
k
-th positive (resp. negative) eigenvalue of

(see Section

for a justification that those eigenvalues are real).

Theorem 7

(Eigenvector approximation)
.

Assume that we are in the regime (
14
), and that
\tau_{r_{0}}\leq c
for some constant

independent from
n
. For

, let

be a unit vector of

Then there exists orthogonal matrices

such that

\lVert V^{+}-Y^{+}O^{+}\rVert=O(\sqrt{r_{+}/d})\quad\ \ {}\quad\lVert V^{-}-Y^{-}O^{-}\rVert=O(\sqrt{r_{-}/d}),

where

(ii).

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem

3.2.2
Weak recovery and consistency for spectral algorithms

Let
\mathbb{M}_{n,r}
be the collection of all
n\times r
matrices where each row has exactly one
1
and
(r-1)
zeros. Given a spectral embedding matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, the
k
-means clustering problem can be defined as

\displaystyle(\hat{\Sigma},\hat{P})=\min_{\Sigma\in\mathbb{M}_{n,r},P\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}}\|\Sigma P-V\|_{F}^{2},

(18)

where
\hat{\Sigma}
represents a partition of
n
data points into
r
clusters.
Efficient algorithms exist
for finding an approximate solution whose value is within a constant fraction of the optimal value
(
Kumar et al., 2004
)
, which finds
(\hat{\Sigma},\hat{P})
such that

\displaystyle\|\hat{\Sigma}\hat{P}-V\|_{F}^{2}\leq(1+\eta)\min_{\Sigma\in\mathbb{M}_{n,r},P\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}}\|\Sigma P-V\|_{F}^{2}.

(19)

Algorithm 1

Spectral clustering with the Bethe-Hessian

0:

Adjacency matrix
A
of a graph
G
, approximation parameter
\eta>0

1:

Let
\hat{d}
be the average degree of
G
. Construct two Bethe-Hessian matrices
H\left(\pm\sqrt{\hat{d}}\right)
.

2:

Count the number of negative eigenvalues below
-\log^{-1}(n)
of
H\left(\pm\sqrt{\hat{d}}\right)
and use it as an estimation of
r_{+},r_{-}
, respectively. Let
r=r_{-}+r_{+}
.

3:

Compute unit eigenvectors associated with negative eigenvalues of
H\left(\pm\sqrt{\hat{d}}\right)
below
\log^{-1}(n)
, denoted by
v_{k}^{\pm}
for
k\in[r_{\pm}]
. Let
V^{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r_{\pm}}
be the matrix whose rows are
v_{k}^{\pm}
for
k\in[r_{\pm}]
and
V=[V^{+},V^{-}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
.

4:

Let
(\hat{\Sigma},\hat{P})
be an
(1+\eta)
-approximate solution to the
k
-means problem (
19
).

5:

For each
i\in[n]
, let
\hat{\sigma}_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}k\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\Sigma}_{i,k}=1\}
.

6:

return

\hat{\sigma}
.

Since the rows of
\Phi_{k}^{\pm}
are clustered w.r.t the true assignment
\sigma
, Corollary
8
suggests that this is also true for the
V^{\pm}
. This indicates that Algorithm
1
can achieve
weak recovery
when the average degree is large enough. We show that this is indeed the case:

Theorem 9

(Weak recovery at large degree)
.

Assume that we are in the regime (
14
), and that

\max_{i\leq r}\tau_{i}\leq c

for some constant
c<1
. Let
\hat{\sigma}
be the output of Algorithm
1
on
G
. Then for any constant
\eta>0
, there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that with high probability,

\mathrm{ov}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\geq 1-C\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\tau_{i}\right)^{2}+O(r/d).

(20)

The proof of Theorem
9
is given in Appendix
D
.
This provides a simple spectral algorithm without degree regularization for weak recovery. When both error terms above converge to zero, we also obtain a weak consistency result:

Corollary 10

(Weak consistency)
.

Assume that the parameters
(\pi_{i}),(P_{ij})
are such that

1\ll d\lesssim\operatorname{polylog}(n),\quad r\ll d\quad\ \ {}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{r}\tau_{i}=o(1).

Then for any constant
\eta>0
, with high probability, the output
\hat{\sigma}
of Algorithm
1
satisfies

\mathrm{ov}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})=1-o(1).

3.3
Discussion

Comparison with
Mohanty et al. (2024)

The most recent result on the spectrum of the Bethe-Hessian matrix comes from
Mohanty et al. (2024)
, which also establishes a relation between the eigenvalues of
Q
and the negative outliers of
H(t)
for
d>1
with a choice of
t>\sqrt{d}
depending on
\mu_{2}
. However,
Mohanty et al. (2024, Theorem 5.1)
actually only addresses a single eigenspace of
H(t)
, and therefore their Proposition 2.1 is only valid for SBMs with symmetric communities of equal sizes and a parameter
t
that depends on the eigenvalue
\mu_{2}
. Our result covers a more general class of SBMs, and we are able to exactly count the number of communities under the assumption that
d\geq 2
. We can also retrieve the results of
Mohanty et al. (2024)
for any
d\geq 1
with a more careful choice of
t
.

Comparison with
Le and Levina (2022)
;
Hwang et al. (2023)

Both
Le and Levina (2022)
and
Hwang et al. (2023)
only considered estimating the number of communities with
H(\sqrt{d})
in the assortative cases where the expected adjacency matrix has nonnegative eigenvalues, while the original conjecture in
Saade et al. (2015)
is for both assortative and disassortative ones by using
H(\pm\sqrt{d})
. We do not make the assortative assumption, and our result holds for a more general SBM setting.
Le and Levina (2022)
demonstrates the consistency of the Bethe-Hessian method for estimating the number of communities when
d=\omega(\log n)
, which can be shown through matrix norm concentration and degree concentration under this regime
(
Le et al., 2017
;
Benaych-Georges et al., 2020
)
.
Hwang et al. (2023)
relates the Bethe-Hessian spectrum to a normalized Laplacian with regularization studied in
Le et al. (2017)
using Sylvester’s Law of Inertia. However, their method is limited to the case
d\to\infty
and does not provide information on the locations of eigenvalues. Our work overcomes these limitations by exploring a novel connection to the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the reduced non-backtracking matrix studied in
Stephan and Zhu (2024a)
down to the bounded expected degree regime.

Spectral algorithms for weak consistency

Most existing methods to achieve weak consistency in the case
d\to\infty
require a regularization step
(
Abbe, 2018
;
Zhang, 2024
)
to remove high-degree vertices.
In the 2-block case, weak consistency without removing high-degree vertices was developed in
(
Le et al., 2017
)
based on regularized Laplacian matrices. The Bethe-Hessian approach is parameter-free, and it does not need additional regularization on high-degree vertices.

Extension to weighted graphs

As discussed in Section
1
, reducing the non-backtracking operator matrix
B
to its
2n\times 2n
form
\tilde{B}
only applies to unweighted graphs, therefore making the non-backtracking method inefficient for clustering weighted graphs. However, for the Bethe–Hessian matrix, a straightforward extension to weighted graphs was presented in
Saade et al. (2014)
. Assume each edge
(i,j)
has a weight
w_{ij}
, then we can define
H(t)
such that

H(t)_{ij}=\delta_{ij}\left(1+\sum_{k\in\partial_{i}}\frac{w_{ik}^{2}}{t^{2}-w_{ik}^{2}}\right)-\frac{tw_{ij}A_{ij}}{t^{2}-w_{ij}^{2}},

where
\partial_{i}
denotes the set of all neighbors of vertex
i
.
When all weights are
1
, this is the same as the unweighted Bethe-Hessian matrix up to a normalization factor
\frac{1}{t^{2}-1}
. Such a generalization might be useful for developing more efficient spectral methods for matrix completion in the very sparse regime
(
Bordenave et al., 2023
)
.

Proof.

(

For example, letting

\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle=n\langle\pi,m_{ij}\rangle+O(n\epsilon_{n}).

(29)

\displaystyle m_{ij}(k)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tilde{d}}Y_{i\ell}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\mu_{j}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\tilde{d}}Y_{j\ell}\right)\right]

\displaystyle m_{ij}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{i}\mu_{j}}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{d}}\left[\tilde{d}[Tm_{ij}](k)+\tilde{d}(\tilde{d}-1)\frac{\mu_{i}\mu_{j}}{d^{2}}\phi_{i}(k)\phi_{j}(k)\right]

Appendix E
Spectral stability of Hermitian matrices

This section contains the proofs of the matrix perturbation results needed in the proofs. We begin with a simple lemma:

Lemma 23

(Approximate eigenvectors imply approximate eigenvalues)
.

Let
M
be a Hermitian matrix and
v
be a normalized vector such that
\|Mv\|\leq\varepsilon
. Then
M
has an eigenvalue in
[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
.

Proof.

Suppose not, then all eigenvalues of
M
in absolute value are greater than
\varepsilon
. Then
\|Mv\|>\varepsilon
for any unit vector
v
, a contradiction.
∎

We show a generalization of this result, dealing with multiple pseudo-eigenvectors at once:

Lemma 24
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a Hermitian matrix, and
(\lambda_{1},v_{1}),\dots,(\lambda_{k},v_{k})
be
k
approximate eigenpairs such that the
v_{i}
are orthonormal and
\lVert Mv_{i}-\lambda_{i}v_{i}\rVert\leq\varepsilon
for all
i
. Then there exist
k
eigenvalues
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{k}
of
M
such that
|\lambda_{i}-\nu_{i}|\leq 2\sqrt{k}\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Let
E
be the subspace spanned by the
v_{i}
, and

S=V\Lambda V^{\top}+P_{E^{\bot}}MP_{E^{\bot}},

where
V=[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})
. Then

\displaystyle\lVert M-S\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert(P_{E}+P_{E^{\bot}})M(P_{E}+P_{E^{\bot}})-S\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert MP_{E}-V\Lambda V^{\top}\rVert+\lVert P_{E}MP_{E^{\bot}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert MP_{E}-V\Lambda V^{\top}\rVert,

since
P_{E}MP_{E^{\bot}}=(P_{E}M-V\Lambda V^{\top})P_{E^{\bot}}
. Using the formula
P_{E}=VV^{\top}
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert MP_{E}-V\Lambda V^{\top}\rVert\leq\lVert MV-V\Lambda\rVert\leq\sqrt{k}\lVert(MV-V\Lambda)^{\top}\rVert_{2,\infty}=\sqrt{k}\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\|Mv_{i}-\lambda_{i}v_{i}\|

(78)

where in the last inequality, we use the fact that for any
n\times k
matrix
A
,

\displaystyle\|A\|\leq\min\{\sqrt{n}\|A\|_{2,\infty},\sqrt{k}\|A^{\top}\|_{2,\infty}\}.

(79)

See, e.g.,
(
Cape et al., 2019
, Proposition 6.5)
.
From the assumption, the right-hand side of (
78
) is at most
\sqrt{k}\varepsilon
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 25

(A local Davis-Kahan theorem)
.

Let
M
be a Hermitian matrix, and
E
be a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
stable w.r.t
M
. Assume that there exists a
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and a unit vector
v
such that
\lVert Mv-\lambda v\rVert\leq\varepsilon
. Then we have

\mathrm{dist}(v,E)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathrm{dist}(\lambda,\mathrm{Sp}(M\big|_{E^{\bot}}))}

Proof.

For simplicity, we assume that
\lambda=0
and we let
\delta=\mathrm{dist}(\lambda,\mathrm{Sp}(M\big|_{E^{\bot}}))
. By the Pythagorean theorem and stability of
E
, we have

\varepsilon\geq\lVert Mv\rVert\geq\lVert P_{E^{\bot}}Mv\rVert=\lVert MP_{E^{\bot}}v\rVert\geq\delta\lVert P_{E^{\bot}}v\rVert,

since every eigenvalue of
M\big|_{E^{\bot}}
has absolute value at least
\delta
. Since
\mathrm{dist}(v,E)=\lVert P_{E^{\bot}}v\rVert
, this concludes the proof.
∎
