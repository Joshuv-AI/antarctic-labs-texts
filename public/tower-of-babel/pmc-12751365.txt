Moderate nitrogen supply alleviates low temperature stress by regulating nitrogen assimilation and hormone signaling in wheat tillering nodes
Authors: Xuefang Sun, Huanyu Du, Xingfei Lv, Qingxia Zhang, Miaodan Fei, Zengxu Li, Xuejie Li, Xin Hao, Qing Sun, Wen Jiang, Chaoyu Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClimate change has brought many low–temperature events, which occurred more frequently in most wheat production regions of China. Low temperature stress inhibits early growth and development in wheat, causing severe damage or yield losses. Nitrogen (N) application enhances wheat growth and yield, yet few studies focus on how nitrogen regulates nitrogen assimilation and hormone signaling in wheat tillering nodes to alleviate low temperature stress. Therefore, in a 14–year long–term field experiment, we investigated physiological responses and transcriptome profiles of winter wheat under low-temperature stress and four N levels (N0–N3: 0, 90, 180, and 270 kg ha-1, respectively).ResultsN application rate significantly enhanced dry weight, tiller number, N accumulation, and yield as compared with the treatment without nitrogen (N0) under low-temperature stress. Moderate nitrogen supply (N2) most effectively increased these parameters by 70.3%, 50.0%, 92.9%, and 20.1%, respectively, compared with N0. The proline (Pro) and amino acids (AAs) content, catalase (CAT) and peroxidase (POD) activities increased with increasing N level, with N2 exhibiting the best performance. N application rate also increased auxin (IAA), trans–zeatin (tZ), and abscisic acid (ABA) contents, and N2 exhibited the highest IAA and ABA contents with increase by 39.2% and 12.2%, respectively, compared with N0. Transcriptome analysis indicated that some key genes such as those encoding nitrate reductase (NR), ferredoxin-nitrite reductase (NiR), cytokinin dehydrogenase and phenylalanine ammonia lyase were involved in the interaction between N supply and low temperature stress.ConclusionThese findings indicate that moderate N at 180 kg ha-1 significantly enhances low temperature stress tolerance in winter wheat by increasing the antioxidant capacity and hormone content of tillers, and promoting nitrogen metabolism to increase the yield.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07692-3.Keywords: Nitrogen metabolism, Low temperature stress tolerance, Tillering physiology, Wheat hormonal response

Introduction

In recent years, extreme climate events have become more frequent and more severe as a result of global climate change [1]. In natural environments, low temperatures are one of the extreme climate events that severely affect crop growth and yield [2]. For instance, crops often face low temperature stress during early growth stages, which restricts agricultural production [3]. Therefore, to reduce the adverse effects of low temperatures on plants and increase crop yields, effective and sustainable practices must be used.

As one of the most important types of abiotic stress, low temperature adversely affects many physiological and biochemical processes in crops. Subsequently, this inhibits the growth, development, and yield of many important crops [4]. Low temperatures impair plant growth by damaging membranes and increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the plants [5]. Plants possess an efficient defense system comprising antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, CAT) and osmoregulatory substances (proline (Pro), amino acids (AAs), and soluble sugars) [6]. Furthermore, phytohormones also play an essential role in plants’ adaptation to adverse environment [7, 8]. As a major stress hormone, exogenous ABA can improve the antioxidant enzyme activity of wheat under low temperature stress and enhance the low temperature resistance of plants [9]. Auxin is one of the main growth hormones, and the effects of low temperature stress on plant growth and development are closely related to intracellular auxin gradients [10]. It has been reported that wheat maintains growth under low temperature stress by altering hormone levels, such as CKs and IAA [11]. Therefore, responses of plants to low temperature stress are complex.

Nitrogen (N) plays a crucial role in the growth and development of plants [12], and it is the main factor limiting wheat yield [13]. High crop yield is largely dependent on N fertilizer application because the natural supply of N in soil is insufficient to meet crop needs [14]. Nitrogen (N) critically affects leaf area, radiation interception, photosynthetic efficiency, and dry matter (DM) accumulation in crop plants [15]. Moreover, N is involved in many physiological processes related to the adaptation of plants to various stresses [16]. Previous studies reported that higher N nutrition contributed to drought tolerance in wheat by maintaining higher photosynthetic activities and antioxidative defense system during vegetative growth periods [17]. Nitrogen fertilization can significantly enhance antioxidant levels and maintain hormone balance in wheat plants under low temperature stress [18, 19]. Therefore, N supply might play a crucial role in low temperature–stress–induced adaptation, growth, and photosynthesis. N fertilizer is often excessively applied by farmers to obtain higher yields [20], but this practice disrupts crop nutrient balance, increases disease susceptibility, and may reduce yields [21]. Crucially, how nitrogen application rate influences wheat cold tolerance under long-term fertilization remains poorly understood.

In China, winter wheat is a vital food crop, and accurate early identification of winter wheat is crucial to estimating yields and ensuring food security [22]. During winter, frost under low temperatures frequently damages wheat. It causes partial or complete freezing of the wheat leaves and even plant death [23]. According to domestic studies, resistance of wheat to freezing-related damage is primarily controlled by the tillering nodes, the main overwintering organs. Under frost stress, tillers can become sources, redistributing fixed carbohydrates to new tillers and roots [24]. A multi–index comprehensive analysis model was successfully developed for assessing the capacity of winter wheat to low temperature tolerate during winter using the physiological index of tillering nodes [25]. In addition, phytohormones such as auxin (IAA), gibberellic acid (GA), and ABA in wheat tillering nodes play a critical role in regulating resistance to freezing stress [26]. Although previous studies have provided insights on the relationship between tillering nodes and low temperature stress, the effects of N supply on the tillering nodes in response to low temperature stress remain largely unknown.

Thus, in wheat production, it is of great significance to understand how different N concentrations affect the tillering nodes exposed to low temperature stress. We hypothesized that optimal nitrogen application enhances tillering nodes cold tolerance, but higher levels confer no further advantage. To investigate this, we used a 14–year field experiment to conduct an extensive series of physiological and molecular studies involving N metabolism. The physiological and stress-related indicators were assessed in winter wheat under low temperature stress and no, low, moderate, and high supply levels of N. The optimal N supply level was determined. The mechanism underlying low temperature stress tolerance under N supply was investigated using transcriptome analysis. This study will improve the understanding of interaction between N supply and low temperature stress and provide a scientific basis for enhancing low temperature tolerance of wheat in changing climate by managing N supply during cultivation.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and design

The experiment was conducted at a 14–year (from 2009 to 2023) long-term fertilization experimental farm in Qingdao Academy of Agricultural Science experimental station, Chengyang district, Qingdao City, Shandong Province, China (36°17’N, 120°21’E). The planting mode used in this study was winter wheat–summer maize rotation. A single–factor, completely randomized experimental design with four repetitions was used with four N level: N0, N1, N2, and N3 with 0, 90, 180, and 270 kg ha-1, respectively. Every plot was 3.6 m × 8 m in size. Urea(with 46% N)was used as the N fertilizer. The N fertilizer was equally divided and applied as a basal fertilizer (50% N) and as a top–dressing at the jointing stage (50% N). Adequate amounts of phosphorus and potassium fertilizers were applied as a basal fertilizer in all experiments. P, as calcium superphosphate (with 16% P2O5), and K, as potassium sulfate (with 50% K2O), were applied at rates of 75 kg ha-1 P2O5 and 150 kg ha− 1 K2O, respectively. The preceding crop was summer maize, grown under the same N treatments as applied in the wheat season. The soil used was collected from the 0–20 cm plow layer of the experimental field before wheat sowing. The soil Alkaline hydrolyzable nitrogen content was 78.24, 105.79, 134.73 and 159.36 mg kg⁻¹ for N0, N1, N2 and N3 treatments, respectively. The other crop management practices were followed local production standards, and no obvious water, pest and disease stress were observed during the wheat growth season.

Plant materials

In this study, Qingyan 126 wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) cultivar, supplied by Qingdao Academy of Agricultural Science was used in all experiments. This cultivar is characterized by high yield and adaptability to a wide range of climate conditions in Shandong Province, China. On October 16, 2023, wheat seeds were sown with a row spacing of 0.25 m at 225 kernels m⁻2. Harvesting was performed on June 8, 2024. The data of monthly rainfall and mean temperature were obtained from Qingdao Agriculture and Rural Affairs Bureau (http://nw.qingdao.gov.cn) (Fig. 1). When the temperature naturally dropped to − 10.3 °C (minimum temperature of the field averaged over 10 consecutive days), the tillering nodes were collected. To reduce differences between the samples and ensure a more representative experiment, 30 different wheat tillering nodes were combined into one sample, wrapped in foil, and stored at − 80 °C.

Assessment of osmoregulatory substances and antioxidant enzyme activity

Pro content was determined using acid ninhydrin colorimetry [27]. Wheat tillering nodes were extracted in 3.0% sulfosalicylic acid via a 15-min incubation in a boiling water bath, followed by filtration. Proline content was assayed by incubating a reaction mixture (2.0 ml extract, 3.0 ml ninhydrin solution, 2.0 ml acetic acid) at 100 °C for 40 min and measuring the absorbance at 520 nm.

The content of free AAs and soluble sugar was determined using ninhydrin colorimetric method and colorimetry, respectively [28]. Wheat tillering nodes were extracted in 5% acetic acid. The reaction mixture containing 1 ml extract, 3.0 ml ninhydrin solution and 0.1 ml of 0.1% ascorbic acid. The mixture was heated at 100 °C for 15 min, rapidly cooled in cold water, mixed with 60% ethanol, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm for free AAs.

Wheat tillering nodes was extracted with 80% ethanol in a conical flask for 1 h, followed by filtration through filter paper. The assay mixture, comprising 0.5 ml of the extract, 4 ml of anthrone reagent (200 mg anthrone dissolved in 100 ml of 72% H₂SO₄), and 1.5 ml of distilled water, was incubated at 100 °C for 15 min. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 620 nm for soluble sugar.

The activity of ROS−scavenging enzymes (CAT, POD, and SOD) and malondialdehyde (MDA) content were measured using respective micro−determination kits (Suzhou Keming Biotechnology Co., China). Four biological replicates were used for each assay.

Measurement of hormone content

The endogenous active forms of IAA, trans-zeatin (tZ), and ABA were quantitatively analyzed as described previously with some modifications [29]. Approximately 120 mg of fresh wheat tillering node (which was stored at − 80 °C) was ground into powder at 50 Hz for 1 min in liquid N using a low temperature grinder. The powder was mixed with 1.5 mL of methanol: water: formic acid (79.9:20:0.1; V/V/V). The extraction was performed using ultrasonication for 30 min, followed by incubation at 4 °C for 16 h. The solution was shaken and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube. The residue was re-extracted with 1 ml methanol under ultrasonication for 30 min and centrifuged. The supernatant was collected, combined with the earlier supernatant, and placed in a low-temperature cold trap for concentration. After concentration, the residue was redissolved in 70 µl of 80% methanol aqueous solution and filtered using a 0.22 µm organic filter membrane. A Triple Quad™ 4500 system (AB SCIEX, USA) was used for LC-MS/MS analysis. Pure IAA, tZ and ABA were used as the standards.

RNA−seq, gene ontology (GO), and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) analyses

Wheat tillering nodes were used to extract and purify total RNA during overwintering stage. NEB Next Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, Houston, TX, USA) for Illumina was used to prepare the sample cDNA library. The NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for sequencing. Raw reads (Q > 20) were filtered and mapped with orientation mode on ensembl_57_triticum_aestivum_iwgsc_toplevel using HISAT2 (v2.0.5). Using the HTSeq (0.9.1) statistics, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change ≥ 1 and p < 0.05 were considered significant. Using topGO analysis, the DEGs associated with N and low temperature tolerance were annotated to various functional pathways. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using ClusterProfiler (3.4.4) to identify the enriched genes and metabolic pathways.

Quantitative real−time PCR validation

For real-time RT-PCR, the synthesis of first-strand cDNA was performed using the Evo M–MLV RT Mix Kit from Accurate Biology. The cDNA reaction mixture was diluted five times, and 2 µl was used as the template in a PCR reaction with a total volume of 20 µl. The ABI 7500 system (Applied Biosystems) was used to perform real–time RT-PCR with the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit from Accurate Biology. Three repetitions were done for each assay. The relative expression level of chosen genes were determined using the comparative Ct method. TaActin served as an internal reference to normalize gene expression. The primers used for real-time RT–PCR are detailed in Appendix A.

Statistical analysis

The effects of N and low temperature stress on wheat tillering nodes were analyzed using one−way ANOVA with the Duncan’s post hoc test (p < 0.05). The analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

The phenotype of wheat seedlings is affected by N supply under low temperature stress

In comparison with no N fertilizer supply (N0), long-term N application markedly affected the tiller number and dry weight of wheat in the overwintering stage (Fig. 2). The tiller number increased with the increase in N supply. However, at N supply rate of 270 kg ha-1, the tiller number did not increase substantially. The tiller number at N2 and N3 were not significantly different (p = 0.340). The dry weight of shoot increased by 70% at N1 and N2 compared with that at N0 (p = 0.000). However, the dry weight of shoot notably decreased by 24% at N3 compared with that at N1 and N2 (p = 0.011).

Improved nitrogen assimilation in high N–supplied plants under low temperature stress

We assessed whether different N levels affect wheat seedling growth under low temperature stress by increasing N utilization or assimilation. With increased N level, the N concentration significantly increased in the stem-sheath and leaf (Table 1). Compared with N0, the N concentration of stem-sheath increased by 36.0%, 48.4%, and 65.3%, and that of leaf increased by 22.4%, 35.3%, and 41.7% at N1, N2, and N3, respectively. Although higher nitrogen level raised tissue concentrations, they did not proportionally increase total accumulation. The N accumulation sharply increased by 95.2%, 97.6%, and 89.5% in the stem-sheath, and by 78.2%, 90.6%, and 68.0% in the leaf, respectively, at N1, N2, and N3 compared with that at N0 (p = 0.000). However, compared with N2, N3 exhibited sharply decreased N accumulation in the leaf by 11.8% (p = 0.006). Total N accumulation in the shoot exhibited a similar trend as that in the leaf. This indicated that the total N accumulation could be correlated with overwintering capacity of winter wheat under low temperature stress.

N supply affects the content of osmoregulatory substances

Proline is a key indicator of plant stress response. Pro content in tillering nodes of winter wheat during the overwintering stage was assessed. N1, N2, and N3 exhibited markedly higher Pro contents than N0 (p = 0.001). The Pro content at N2 and N3 did not differ significantly (p = 0.341) (Fig. 3A). N1, N2, and N3 increased the Pro content by 64.5%, 127.3%, and 145.9%, respectively, compared with N0. No significant difference was observed between different N level in terms of soluble sugar content (p = 0.657), which is also an important indicator of stress in plants (Fig. 3B).

N0, N1, N2 and N3: N application rate of 0 kg ha-1, 90 kg ha-1, 180 kg ha-1, and 270 kg ha-1, respectively

The content of AAs in plants is of great significance for studying the changes of N metabolism, absorption, transport, assimilation, and nutrient status. The free AA content substantially increased as N application rate increased. The AA content increased by approximately 36.9% and 71.3% at N1 and N2 compared with that at N0 (p = 0.007), respectively. The AA content at N2 and N3 was not significantly different (p = 0.073) (Fig. 3C).

N supply improves ROS homeostasis under low temperature stress

Catalase (CAT) activity is a vital indicator of oxidative stress in plants. CAT activity in wheat tillering nodes was notably affected by different N level (p = 0.006). The CAT activity at N1, N2, and N3 increased by 18.5%, 25.6%, and 22.0%, respectively, compared with that at N0 (Fig. 4A). The CAT activity was not significantly different at N1 and N3 (p = 0.350). POD activity is another important indicator of oxidative stress in plants. POD activity of winter wheat tillering nodes dramatically increased with as N level increased (p = 0.000) (Fig. 4B). Compared with N0, the POD activity at N1, N2, and N3 increased by 20.7%, 30.1%, and 21.7%, respectively. The POD activity at N1 and N3 exhibited no significant difference (p = 0.345). SOD activity is regarded as a crucial indicator of plants’ antioxidant capacity under abiotic stress. SOD activity at the four N level was not significantly different (p = 0.878) (Fig. 4C).

The MDA content of plants plays an important role in abiotic stress and acts as an indicator of membrane lipid peroxidation. MDA content of winter wheat tillering nodes during the overwintering stage was significantly different between N0 and N1 and between N0 and N2 (p = 0.012 and 0.046) (Fig. 4D). Compared with N0, the MDA content at N1 and N2 sharply decreased by 23.9% and 14.8%, respectively. However, no significant difference was observed between the MDA contents at N0 and N3 and at N2 and N3 (p = 0.311 and 0.069).

Phytohormone accumulation in tillering nodes is regulated by N supply

Abiotic stress stimulates the synthesis of plant hormones, which extensively interact and crosstalk with each other. To determine whether N supply under low temperature stress affects the biosynthesis and interactions of IAA, tZ, and ABA, their contents in the tillering nodes were determined during overwintering stage (Fig. 5). N2 exhibited the highest accumulation of IAA, with 39.2% increase compared with that at N0 (Fig. 5A). Surprisingly, the IAA content at N3 markedly decreased compared with that at N2 (p = 0.015). tZ content in the tillering nodes increased as N application rates increased (Fig. 5B). It was higher at N2 and N3 than at N0 and N1 (p = 0.002). Compared with N0, the tZ content increased by 28.7% and 36.4%, respectively, at N2 and N3. However, it was not markedly different between N0 and N1 and between N2 and N3 (p = 0.059 and 0.415). ABA content followed a similar trend to that of IAA (Fig. 5C). The ABA content was higher by 12.2% at N2 than at N0 (p = 0.099). However, it was not significantly different at N1, N2, and N3.

DEGs in the tillering nodes associated with N and low temperature

To examine the molecular mechanisms underlying the interaction between N and low temperature stress in the tillering nodes, the DEGs were screened by RNA− seq analysis. Under N1, N2, and N3, 381 (153 up− and 228 downregulated), 296 (75 up− and 221 downregulated), and 773 (408 up− and 365 downregulated) DEGs were identified, respectively (Fig. 6A–C). N application triggered in the differential expression of these genes under low temperature stress. In terms of phenotypic and physiological indicators, N2 treatment was more suitable for wheat growth than other three treatments. N3 treatment produced excessive N stress in the plants. Based on this, further screening was conducted to identify the genes specifically affected by excessive N stress. Compared with N2, N3 exhibited 96 up- and 91 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 6D). In response to excessive N stress, these genes may play an important role in N assimilation.

In addition, compared with N2, the most significantly enriched pathways were ABC transporters, benzoxazinoid biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites under N3 (Fig. 7D). GO pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs under different N application rates were interconnected (Fig. 8). In “biological process,” the most enriched GO terms included photosynthesis, protein − chromophore linkage, and galactose metabolic process. In “cellular component,” the most enriched GO terms were associated with the photosystem and thylakoid. In “molecular function,” the most enriched GO terms were transferase, oxidoreductase, and lyase activity.

Identification of the expression pattern of key regulators

Transcriptome analysis of the DEGs related to phytohormone signaling pathways in the tillering nodes revealed that some DEGs were enriched in the pathways related to phenylalanine metabolism, carotenoid biosynthesis, tryptophan metabolism and zeatin biosynthesis (Table 2). Compared with N0, the number of upregulated DEGs in the phenylalanine metabolism pathway increased with the increasing N level, and most DEGs encoded phenylalanine ammonialyase. Similarly, the number of upregulated DEGs in the carotenoid biosynthesis pathway increased with the increasing N level. Among the DEGs in the tryptophan metabolism pathway, N1 exhibited two upregulated and one downregulated DEGs and N2 exhibited two downregulated DEGs compared with N0.

Discussion

The acclimation of winter wheat under low temperature stress and various N level was explored in this study based on a 14-year long-term field experiment. Unlike previous studies conducted under controlled conditions, this work used field−based stress. Under low temperature stress, the dry weight, tillering ability, and physiological changes were altered. Moderate N application rate could mitigate the damaging effects of low temperature stress but high N supply could not. Moreover, the transcriptional regulatory networks in the tillering nodes underlying the interaction between N and low temperature stress were modified as N supply levels increased.

Moderate N application rate increased the tillering capacity, dry weight, and yield

Frost damage to wheat severely impacts plant growth as the leaves freeze, causing external symptoms such as yellowing and shriveled leaves. Frost damage can lead to drying and death of wheat leaves, further reducing aboveground biomass of wheat [23]. In our study, moderate N supply could increase the dry weight; however, excessive N could not further increase the dry weight under low temperature stress. This may result from excessive nitrogen application disrupting crop nutrient balance [30]. Additionally, under low-temperature conditions, wheat growth slows, preventing the absorption and utilization of surplus nitrogen fertilizer [31].

Wheat tillering is nitrogen − dependent and influences final yield [32]. We observed that the tiller number increased with the increasing N level; however, the highest N level did not produce the greatest number of tillers. Wheat yield is directly related to panicle number, which is determined by tiller number. However, higher number of tillers does not necessarily mean higher yield [33]. This is consistent with our results that moderate N supply increased the grain yield mainly by increasing the number of fertile spikes. While our study was conducted within a long − term N fertilization field trial, further multi−location field experiments are needed to validate whether the 180 kg ha⁻¹ N application rate enhances wheat yield stability under low-temperature stress across diverse soil types and wheat varieties.

Moderate N supply increased antioxidant defense ability under low temperature stress

Pro content is a commonly used indicator to monitor stress in plants. Previous studies have reported that moderate Pro content can help winter wheat in the overwintering period [25]. In our study, Although proline accumulation increased with nitrogen level, excessive N did not further enhance cold resistance.

In plants exposed to stress, ROS are accumulated. This leads to an imbalance, altering the levels of intracellular proteins and nucleic acids and damaging the cellular structure [34]. ROS equilibrium is maintained by plants’ antioxidant system involving CAT, SOD and POD. It maintains the integrity of the cell membrane, corresponding to the plant’s ability to withstand stress [35]. Under low temperature stress, notable increase in the CAT and POD activities was associated with a decrease in MDA content under high N level versus low N level in our study. However, excessive N application (N3) decreased the activity of antioxidant enzymes compared with that under moderate N application rate (N2). Thus, moderate nitrogen supply likely mitigates oxidative damage by enhancing antioxidant defense mechanisms. This may be the main reason why higher dry matter weight and yield were obtained at N2 application rate under low-temperature stress.

Moderate N application rate regulated phytohormone accumulation under low temperature stress

Under ultralow-temperature freezing stress, plants synthesize various hormones to adapt to environmental changes [36]. To date, very few studies have investigated the effects of low temperatures on the endogenous hormones of plants under natural growth conditions. Low temperature stress stimulates the production of IAA, which plays a crucial role in plant growth and development [37]. Plant growth and development are affected by low temperature stress due to gradients in auxin distribution within cells [10]. In our study, IAA content increased with the increasing N level; however, it decreased under N3 compared with that under N2. This supports the conclusion that moderate N supply improves wheat tolerance to low temperature stress.

Several studies have demonstrated that low temperature stress and cytokinin (CK) are closely related, and increased endogenous CK level can improve low temperature resistance of plants [38, 39]. In our results, tZ content in the tillering nodes increased with the increasing N level. However, previous study reported that high N application reduced CK content, which may be due to reduced CK biosynthesis and subsequent maize growth retardation under low temperature stress [40]. This inconsistency in both study results may be attributed to different cultivation environments and crops.

As an essential phytohormone, ABA plays a crucial role in the transduction of stress signals and acclimation to cold conditions. In our study, ABA content in wheat tillering nodes increased in response to increasing N level, as previously reported in various plants [41, 42]. Higher accumulation of ABA in N-treated plants may be due to the increased antioxidant enzyme activity, which reduced ROS generation.

Critical roles of key genes in regulating low temperature stress tolerance in wheat

As plants are exposed to different stressors, the resistance-related genes are activated and exhibit different expression patterns [43]. In plants, N metabolism influences nearly all growth − determining processes; therefore, it is sensitive to various stresses. In this study, N content increased with the increasing N level but the N accumulation did not. This was consistent with a previous study reporting that N is a key factor in tolerance to low temperature stress [40]. N assimilation and AA synthesis are catalyzed by several key enzymes involved in N metabolism (e.g., NR and NiR). In our study, seven NR and NiR genes were upregulated with the increasing N level, indicating that high N supply could promote N assimilation in wheat under cold conditions.

Studying the hormonal regulation mechanism of winter wheat under different N level can contribute to understanding its low temperature stress tolerance. As an essential component of low temperature stress response processes, auxin is of vital importance in plant growth. Most of the auxin synthesis in plants is conducted by Trp − dependent pathways [26]. Low temperature stress in Arabidopsis induced coordinated down- and upregulation of several Aux/IAA and ARF proteins [44]. In our study, up − and downregulated DEGs in response to different N level were enriched in the tryptophan metabolism pathway; they may be involved in the cold tolerance of winter wheat. Carotenoids are synthetic precursors of ABA signaling molecules and play a role in plant development and stress response [45]. We observed that the number of upregulated DEGs (e.g. TraesCS7D02G498100, TraesCS7D02G498300, TraesCS3A02G274300 and TraesCS5A02G238000) involved in carotenoid biosynthesis increased with the increasing N level. Previous studies have indicated that under saline−alkaline stress, the expression level of TraesCS7D02G498300 is upregulated, thereby facilitating ABA synthesis in wheat to mitigate stress [46]. This is consistent with the increased ABA content in our research.

Several essential genes involved in the phenylpropanoid pathway contribute to drought resistance in foxtail millet [47]. In our study, the number of DEGs involved in the phenylalanine metabolism pathway increased with the increasing N level. These findings indicated that the DEGs involved in hormone signaling are critical in low temperature stress tolerance in winter wheat under different N application level.

Conclusions

In summary, using a long-term experiment, this study demonstrates that moderate N (180 kg ha-1) supply mitigates low temperature stress in winter wheat (Fig. 10). Under low temperature stress, moderate N supply can increase Pro and AA contents and CAT and POD activities and establish an improved redox homeostasis to alleviate low − temperature-induced oxidative damage in plants. Furthermore, N supply promoted the up − regulated expression of genes related to nitrogen metabolism and hormone synthesis in wheat. These help to increase N accumulation of plant and the content of IAA, tZ, and ABA to enhance low temperature tolerance during the overwintering stage. Thus, applying 180 kg ha-1 N improves wheat cold tolerance and yield stability. Our research can provide theoretical and technical support to mitigate the damages of low temperature and improve wheat yield. However, whether different varieties and soil types affect the relationship between N application rate and low temperature stress needs to be further analyzed.
