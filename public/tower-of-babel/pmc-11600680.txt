The characteristics of formula milk marketing on health system: a qualitative study from Beijing and Jinan in China
Authors: Yinghang Wang, Hanxiyue Zhang, Suying Chang, Ge Yang, Wen Wang, Xinyi Lv, Zhi Lin, Shuyi Zhang, Angela Y Xiao, Anuradha Narayan, Kun Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreastmilk is widely regarded as the healthiest choice for both infants and mothers due to its numerous advantages over formula, such as higher concentrations of essential nutrients and antibodies, easier digestion, and superior taste. The World Health Organization International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes was adopted over 40 years ago to mitigate the effects of infant formula marketing on a woman’s decision to breastfeed. Yet, the commercial formula milk industry has continued to market their products aggressively and through an increasing variety of social media channels. This study examines the impact of formula milk marketing on specific components of the health system to understand how systems that are built to support and sustain breastfeeding may have been captured and repurposed by industry practices.MethodsAs part of A Multi-country Study of Early Infant Feeding Decisions, this qualitative study was conducted in two representative cities in China, Beijing and Jinan, in 2020. The participants were recruited through convenience sampling and snowballing sampling methods. Data were collected through 40 in-depth interviews and 10 focus group discussions with health professionals, pregnant women, and formula milk marketing executives. A thematic analytical method was used to analyze the main themes and sub-themes that detail the characteristics of formula milk marketing and its impact on the health system in China.ResultsOur results identified the most common practices used by the formula milk industry to reach health workers as well as mothers and their families through health systems. Such practices include (1) provider/facility-based marketing to sponsor academic activities (e.g., seminars, conferences, and research projects) and promote specialized infant formula that targets health care providers and facilities, which influence the health consultations and recommendations provided by health professionals to mothers and families; and (2) receiver-based marketing that targets Chinese women and increases marketing exposure through lectures provided by maternity schools at health facilities during the antenatal period and health promotion activities organized by formula milk companies outside of facilities during the postnatal care period.ConclusionsFormula milk marketing aims to influence the practices and services within the health system in China, both directly through health professionals and facilities, and also indirectly by targeting mothers. These marketing strategies have impacted the perceptions of providers on formula milk, and strategies targeting consumers have aimed to further influence and undermine mothers’ intentions to continue breastfeeding. Stricter regulation of industry marketing practices is needed to create a supportive environment for mothers to breastfeed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20298-y.Keywords: Breastfeeding, Infant formula, Breastfeeding practice, Health claims, Breast milk substitute

Introduction

Breastfeeding is associated with a significantly lower risk of mortality and various comorbidities (e.g., pneumonia, diarrhea, overweight/obesity, and diabetes mellitus) during infancy and early childhood [1–4]. Breastmilk offers several advantages over formula, including the presence of immunoglobulin A, which helps protect infants from infections and diseases [5]. Additionally, breastmilk is more easily digestible, affordable, and helps reduce the risk of bacterial infections in infants [6]. Due to these benefits, breastfeeding is promoted worldwide. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) recommend the initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life and that infants be exclusively breastfed for the first 6 months on-need day and night, with breastfeeding continuing through ages 2 and up [7]. However, the 6-month exclusive breastfeeding rate remains low around the world; only 41% of infants aged 0–6 months are exclusively breastfed [8]. In China, this rate is just 34.1% [9]. It is necessary to note that while breastmilk is generally considered superior to formula, in some cases, such as insufficient breastmilk production or problems with breastfeeding, the use of formula milk is necessary to ensure that the infants obtain adequate nutrients. The behavioral determinants of breastfeeding range from socioeconomic and cultural factors, to structural factors related to the health system [10–12], all of which may contribute to better breastfeeding outcomes [13, 14].

The domestic market for formula milk in China has steadily increased since 2016 and was estimated to reach US $25 billion in 2021. Formula milk marketing has been identified as a major obstacle to promoting breastfeeding practices in China [15]. Aiming to regulate the “inappropriate sales promotions” of formula milk, the WHO published the International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes (the Code) in 1981 [16], which was subsequently adopted in China by the Maternal and Infant Health Care Law in 1995 and the Advertising Law in 2015. However, the former legislation was completely abolished by the National Health Commission’s own ministerial directives in 2017 and has not yet been replaced. To the present day, formula milk marketing has continued to impose a negative influence on breastfeeding practices worldwide. By targeting all key stakeholders, including health professionals and systems as a primary audience [17], the vast array of marketing schemes used by the formula milk industry indicate a violation of the Code and a clear absence of market regulations in China [18, 19].

As an intersection between breastfeeding mothers and health professionals, the health system, especially the thousands of baby-friendly hospitals, is an important avenue for and target of formula milk marketing to influence the informed decisions and behaviors of mothers. Therefore, it is of public health significance to examine the various marketing strategies that target the health system and their subsequent impacts on the attitudes and practice of health professionals, and hence the breastfeeding practices of mothers. The findings of this research will be used to inform national policies that can help cultivate an environment supportive of breastfeeding in China.

Methods

Study settings

As part of A Multi-country Study on Early Infant Feeding Decisions initiated by UNICEF and the WHO, this qualitative study conducted in China aimed to understand how formula milk marketing affects the health system. Two major cities, Beijing (BJ) and Jinan (JN), were selected as the study sites.

Participants

The participants were recruited via convenience sampling (a non-probability sampling technique where subjects are selected because of their convenient accessibility and proximity to the researcher) to ensure maximum engagement with pregnant women, mothers, and health professionals. “Snowballing” referral sampling was also used, in which current research participants recommended other potential participants for recruitment. Health professionals, including pediatricians, obstetricians, and nurses from the department of obstetrics were recruited from local health facilities to participate in the in-depth interviews (IDIs). Pregnant women (with a gestation age≥ 3 months) and postpartum mothers (who delivered in the past 18 months) were recruited to participate in the focus group discussions (FGDs) and IDIs either at the health facilities or by street interviews. For health professionals, doctors, consultants, midwives, nurses, childcare professionals, dietitians, and infant feeding supporters with regular (3+ times per week) contact with pregnant or new (0–18 months) mothers were recruited. Participants were recruited based on their socioeconomic status (SES), with inclusion of participants from high, middle, and low SES. In addition, marketing executives from both domestic and international formula milk companies were invited to participate in the IDIs. Data collection

Interviews were conducted from August 3, 2020 to August 22, 2020. This study collected data using semi-structured interview guides. Three semi-structured interview guides were developed for the IDIs of pregnant women/postpartum mothers, health professionals, and marketing executives, respectively; an interview guide was also developed for the FGDs. A total of 40 women, categorized by their infant-feeding decision, participated in 10 FGDs, which were conducted online, the details are shown in Table A in the appendix. An additional 10 women participated in 90-minute one-on-one interviews. Topics discussed included their infant feeding behaviors, their primary information sources on infant feeding intentions and behaviors, and factors related to the health system, such as their exposure to formula milk marketing in health facilities. Interviews with health professionals and formula milk marketing executives focused on the interactions of health system components with formula milk companies and their marketing schemes. All the interviews were conducted in Chinese, and the qualitative data collection was audio recorded, transcribed, and checked for accuracy.

Data analysis

To make maximum use of the data, an inductive approach was employed to thematically analyze the qualitative data collected [20, 21]. The data was analyzed following a 5-step process which included familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, and defining and naming themes. The researchers identified domains and topics during the coding process. Independent parallel coding was carried out by two groups of researchers to develop two separate sets of coding that constituted the preliminary findings. The two sets of results were combined to form a single robust set after group discussions and thorough deliberation. A minimum of 80% agreement between coders was adopted to ensure the trustworthiness of the results. The clarity of the categories was then reassessed again by another two researchers independently. Triangulation was used to improve the credibility and transferability of the study. Data were collected from participants in different roles for cross-validation, e.g., comparing nonparental opinions with parental opinions. In addition, after each interview, interviewers prepared a brief summary outlining the main findings, noting any significant non-verbal information not captured by the audio recordings and discussion areas that were less effective, and offering suggestions for future sessions. Audio recordings of FGDs and IDIs were encrypted and stored on secure servers. Verbatim transcripts were produced based on these records, with random spot-checks conducted to ensure accuracy against the original audio. All analyses were conducted using Dedoose (version 8.0.44), a qualitative data management and analysis software.

Ethical considerations

The study has been approved by the Capital Institute of Pediatrics, Beijing, China (No. SHERLL2020002). Study participation was voluntary, and all participants had the right to stop participating in the study at any time without disadvantage for them. All participants were informed of the objectives and methods of the study and have provided written informed consent.

Results

Two major themes regarding the characteristics of formula milk marketing on health system in China emerged. First, the formula milk industry implements marketing strategies that target providers and health facilities, including through: (1) sponsoring seminars, conferences, and clinical studies, (2) promoting “so called” specialized formulas without any medical indications, (3) providing inappropriate breastfeeding consultations, and (4) targeting health facilities as sites for carrying out formula milk marketing activities. Second, we found that the formula milk companies also employ receiver-based marketing strategies, which target pregnant women and postpartum mothers by advertising formula milk during antenatal care (ANC) (e.g., at maternity schools) as well as through advertisement and commercial activities during perinatal visits.

Theme one: provider/facility-based formula milk marketing strategies

Health service providers, whose health advice is generally highly valued and trusted by patients, often have direct and close contact with pregnant women and postpartum mothers when providing maternal and child health services. Providers can thus significantly influence the breastfeeding practices of women through medical consultations. As such, influencing the perceptions and attitudes of health providers has been a priority for formula milk marketing. Additionally, health facilities, where a large number of the target population for formula milk marketing (i.e., pregnant women and postpartum mothers) can be easily accessed, have also become a key site for companies to carry out their marketing activities. Thus, to increase exposure to formula milk marketing, companies have sought to establish strong relationships with health providers and facilities and to influence their perceptions and attitudes towards formula milk, which in turn can influence the breastfeeding perceptions and practices of mothers.

Sponsoring seminars, conferences and clinical studies

Formula milk companies seek to influence health providers and facilities by providing sponsorships for physicians to host or participate in academic activities, such as seminars and conferences. They also sponsor clinical studies on pre-term babies, special formulas for milk allergies, human milk composition, and infant feeding for special medical indicators in pediatrics, pregnancy, and breastfeeding, in order to build strong relationships with physicians. As reported by a number of health providers, these activities are generally held on a regular basis (e.g., every 1–2 months). During these activities, gifts and brochures containing formula milk marketing information are freely distributed, which can indirectly influence providers’ perceptions on the use of formula milk.

“I think it seems that in recent years, there are few marketing activities (directly) targeting at the service providers. Sometimes, the formula milk companies will sponsor the seminars hosted by our department, and a short advertisement presentation (to introduce their companies and formula milk) will be conducted during the seminars. After the seminar, colorful brochures and small gifts such as cups with brand names will be given to us……Even if the seminars were sponsored by the formula milk companies, focus should be on the academic activities, and there should be no more marketing or branding other than short advertisements during the seminars.” (HPS #10, BJ, Pediatrician, Private Hospital).

“(During the seminars sponsored by the formula milk companies) We invited renowned doctors to give lectures and training courses. However, we are not allowed to advertise or even mention formula milk products and thus only marketing flyers with the product selling points are freely distributed during the seminars. Our products are not allowed to be mentioned except for our special formula due to medical issues.” (Brand Manager, Formula Company).

The industry also often works closely with service providers to sponsor studies and collaboratively publish academic research. For example, one provider participated in a clinical trial sponsored by a formula milk company to investigate the clinical outcomes of formula-feeding using a special formula for premature infants. Providers’ attitudes towards this type of collaboration with the formula milk industry on clinical studies varied. Those who were opposed argued that industry sponsorship for research projects may potentially involve conflicts of interest, and thus, can compromise the neutrality of the research. In contrast, other researchers expressed support for industry collaborations despite their clear awareness of the potential conflicts of interest.

“I have participated in an observational study (sponsored by the formula milk company) when I was working the pediatric ward to investigate the outcomes of formula-feeding among premature neonates. The study was designed to prospectively examine the weight trend of these premature infants after using a special formula from a formula milk company. They (the coordinators of the sponsoring formula milk company) gave me product flyers and briefly introduced the ingredients and benefits of their formula. I recall that there were also some forms for me to fill out during the observation study.” (HPS #2, BJ, Pediatrician, Public Hospital).

“As long as both parties (i.e., the health facilities and formula milk companies) agree to collaborate on the research projects, there is no violation of laws or the regulation and standards of institutional review board (IRB). Both parties are just working together for the purpose of research, so why not? However, in the early stage, all paper work and procedures should be legalized to avoid any trouble in the future.” (HPS #7, JN, Pediatrician, Public Hospital).

Promoting “so called” specialized formula beyond medical indications

The formula milk industry also markets select products based on their specialized use for certain medical conditions, promoting this as a prominent product feature without scientific evidence due to the lack of double blind prospective trails. In some cases, health professionals will also provide “soft” recommendations to use these specialized formulas for infants with certain medical indications. Yet, evidence and a scientific background for the appropriate use of these specialized formulas is often limited.

“The doctors are not allowed to recommend any formula, except the specialized formula for premature infants and formula milk for diarrhea. These formulas are sold in hospitals because they contribute directly to the overall health of infants and also because the sales of these formulas are over-the-counter, which usually indicates that the ingredients contained are safe and effective even when used without doctors’ prescriptions.” (Brand Manager, Formula Company).

“At least for babies with medical conditions, such as lactose intolerance and diarrhea, there are options of hydrolyzed, galacto-oligosaccharides (GOS), and medical formulas. In these cases, there are ‘soft’ consultations from experts and pediatricians because direct recommendation is banned.” (Strategy Director, Advertising Agency).

Providing inappropriate breastfeeding consultations

During the postpartum period, inappropriate health consultations from service providers can influence postpartum mothers to discontinue breastfeeding. Breastfeeding can be challenging for postpartum mothers; some may face a dilemma between their desire to breastfeed and a low supply of breastmilk. In interviews with postpartum mothers, we found that formula milk was recommended to serve as a supplement to breastmilk. Mothers commonly received these recommendations from health providers at delivery facilities or community health centers during routine postpartum check-ups, or by searching on online maternal consultation platforms. Mothers also indicated that some providers suggested that formula-feeding is a universal and good substitute for breastfeeding and can resolve breastmilk insufficiency. While there are certain clinical indications, such as chronic lactational insufficiency, where formula-feeding can indeed help address the problem of undernourished infants, this situation should not be generalized. Healthcare professionals should only recommend infant formula as a second-best compromise solution if human milk is not available or if there are other clear indications present, like babies with phenylketonuria, such that human milk alone is insufficient to fully feed the infant. In parallel, they should also provide advice on restoring breastfeeding to mothers.

“We had to have physical checkups on a regular basis, once every three months. Pediatricians asked my infant-feeding practices and the volume of breastmilk. If the breastmilk supply was not adequate, the doctors would suggest mothers to supplement with formula to ensure that the nutritional needs for their baby are met. The pediatricians at the Women and Children hospital were quite professional during our visits for regular checkups and I trusted them.” (WC9, BJ, 0–6 months breastfed).

“I used Baidu (a search engine in China) to search for advice from health professionals, for example, “doctor online Q&A” …. Doctors provided some explanations to the causes of inadequate breastmilk supply and recommended mothers to consider formula feeding. The doctors also assured mothers that their baby would grow up as smart and healthy as their breastfed counterparts.” (WC2, JN,0-6 months formula)

“When I took my baby for a regular health checkup at a community hospital, I consulted with my doctor whether my breastmilk volume was inadequate. They advised that if my breastmilk was insufficient, it would be fine to choose to formula feed as the current formulas were quite good, and many babies have also been formula fed.” (WC2, JN, 0–6 months formula).

Formula milk marketing targeting health facilities

Health facilities, especially delivery facilities, are targets of formula milk marketing because they are where pregnant women and postpartum mothers can be most easily accessed. Formula milk companies utilize healthcare facilities as sites to conduct branding activities, such as distributing marketing leaflets with free formula milk samples and collecting personal contact information (i.e., social media accounts) of potential customers.

“When I attended my regular prenatal examination, sales representatives from formula milk companies distributed advertisement leaflets; some offered to send out free samples of diapers if I added them as my WeChat (a personal social media messaging app) friend. I also received phone calls from sales representatives asking for information about my pregnancy, and if I am considering to formula-feed my baby.” (WG1, BJ, Pregnant Woman).

“Currently we are using the formula brand H while we previously used F. We use these brands because it is suitable for our baby, and our baby loves drinking it. I think this is the best. While we were in the hospital, there was a formula marketing booth at the entrance of hospital. If you scanned the QR code, you could get a free sample from H.” (WG5, BJ, 0–5 months breastfed then formula).

Regulated by the Baby-friendly Hospital Initiative policies, no formula milk marketing is allowed in health facilities by regulation. Nevertheless, there is evidence that formula milk is sold at retail stores in health facilities—a clear violation of the standards set by the initiative. Additionally, postpartum mothers shared that formula milk marketing is still common at health facilities, especially in private health facilities, which are not regulated to comply with the initiative. These private hospitals may collaborate with the formula milk industry to advertise formula milk products.

“Formula brands actually target retail stores in the hospital instead of healthcare providers. Whenever I visit a hospital, I like to stop by the retail store, and I often see formulas being sold there.” (HPS #14, JN, Nurse in Obstetrics, Private Hospital).

“If we go to do a 5D or 4D ultrasound in some private medical imaging facilities, they will inform us on their collaboration with formula brands, and they will ask you to register as a member, so that you can buy infant formula at lower price.” (WG1, BJ, Pregnant Woman).

Theme two: direct outreach to mothers by infant formula marketing

One of the goals of adequate prenatal education is to prepare the expectant mother for each stage of pregnancy, childbirth, breastfeeding, and infant care. Proper antenatal education on infant feeding practices can significantly increase their desire to breastfeed and improve their breastfeeding experiences. As a receiver-based marketing strategy, formula milk companies also target expectant/postpartum mothers during ANC, such as in maternity schools and through other health promotion activities.

Formula milk marketing during ANC at health facilities

During the antenatal period, pregnant women are commonly invited to participate in maternity schools, which offer lectures on a regular basis, in order to prepare them for pregnancy, childbirth, and breastfeeding. We found that, in many cases, formula milk marketing information is integrated into the prenatal education lectures (e.g., by comparing formula-feeding versus breastfeeding); moreover, lectures may even include recommendations or advertisements for a specific formula brand. Furthermore, some expectant mothers said that they received free formula milk samples as gifts from maternity schools.

“We also work with social organizations to organize prenatal lectures for mothers in delivery facilities. We invite experts to give lectures on the pros and cons of breastfeeding and formula-feeding, and how to choose high-quality formula for their infants. These educational lectures are held regularly and are a very effective method to reach out to potential consumers.” (Program Director, Strategy Consulting Company).

“When I pre-registered at a hospital with my family, we were invited to participate in lectures at its maternity school. In these lectures, formula milk marketing sales representatives were also present to advertise their products.” (WG2, BJ, 0–6 months breastfed from birth).

“At a hospital event, free formula milk samples were distributed…it was a seminar for mothers. Sales representatives from various formula milk companies were present and they gave us free samples to try at home.” (WG6, JN, Pregnant Woman).

Formula milk marketing in health promotion activities outside facilities

Through their close collaborations with health facilities, formula milk companies are able to host seminars on maternal care and breastfeeding right outside of the facilities. Online live streaming of lectures hosted by health providers is another means of advertising and promotion. These activities are called “industry-sponsored lectures” and held at venues just outside of hospitals. Event invitations are often disseminated to pregnant women by formula milk sales representatives at hospitals.

“The division chiefs of the pediatric department were invited to live stream events throughout the year to provide consultations on breastfeeding and maternal care to mothers. The main formula ingredients of the sponsoring brand are emphasized by these doctors and, occasionally, the brand names are also displayed during the events.” (Strategy Director, Advertising Agency).

“When I was pregnant, I attended a lecture on labor and preparations before childbirth. I heard about this event through an advertisement in the hospital. That lecture was conducted in an office building. F (a formula milk company) was the sponsor.” (WG9, BJ, 6–18 months breastfed then formula).

Discussion

In this study, we found several pieces of evidence of the formula milk industry’s negative impacts on the health system in China. First, formula milk marketing targets service providers to increase exposure to formula milk marketing information and implicitly change providers’ perceptions and attitudes towards formula milk by sponsoring academic activities. Formula milk companies also use facility-based strategies to reach out to pregnant and postpartum women for promotion activities.

Health system factors, such as formula milk recommendations from service providers and formula milk marketing exposure during ANC, are strong predictors of formula milk feeding [22]. As such, they have been the primary targets of formula milk marketing in China. Clear evidence of the negative influence of formula milk promotion on breastfeeding initiation and exclusivity has been found with regards to factors related to the process, outcomes and the structure of medical care [23]. In a quantitative study on formula milk promotion, it was found that more than 40% of postpartum mothers in delivery facilities were suggested to formula-feed and that they received this recommendation by health professionals, such as midwives and pediatricians [24]. This finding indicates the strong influence of formula milk marketing on interactions between mothers and providers, undermining the quality of medical care with regards to the appropriateness of process and evidence of medical management [25]. The commercial influences of the formula milk industry (e.g., sponsorship of academic activities) are reported to significantly affect the decision-making of providers’ prescriptions of infant formula milk [26]. In addition, ANC, which commonly includes parenting lectures covering diverse topics on the upcoming pregnancy, childbirth and breastfeeding, is another health system factor heavily targeted by the industry. Some companies promote formula milk during ANC by sponsoring lectures at maternity schools [24] and distributing free gifts and product samples, as found in our study. These “invisible” incentives have been difficult to assess due to their “behind the scenes” nature [17].

It is also critical to ensure that any infant nutrition research conducted does not interfere with a mother’s intention to breastfeed. Collaborative research between health facilities and formula companies must be conducted to the highest ethical standards so as not to compromise breastfeeding practices. For research on vulnerable groups such as minors, this group should stand to benefit from the knowledge, practices or interventions that results from the research [27]. In addition, for comparative studies, only those infants whose mothers, despite being educated about the superiority of breastmilk, choose not to or are unable to breastfeed should be included in the formula group. Special attention should be given to this criterion during participant recruitment and in interactions between parents and healthcare professionals.

Consistent with findings from previous studies [22, 24], in this study, we found that postpartum mothers were advised to use formula feeding as a supplement or substitute to breastfeeding by service providers, whose recommendations were most trusted by mothers. Thus, practice guidelines on breastfeeding should further emphasize compliance with the Code and monitor implementation. Sustained education and training for health professionals should also be put in place to enforce implementation [18, 28, 29]. It is pivotal to enhance structural factors, such as optimizing breastfeeding settings and adopting independent and close monitoring, to improve the quality of care for breastfeeding mothers. In June 2022, the Chinese government issued directives to regulate inappropriate marketing practices that target health facilities and medical staff [30]. Promotion targeting pregnant and postnatal women is strictly prohibited in health facilities; providing the conditions and venues for marketing activities to take place on facility premises is also banned. We recommend further government action to increase the commitment of companies and health professionals to the current legislation. Implementation, monitoring, and enforcement of the regulations are urgently needed as well.

According to the 2022 Status of Code report by the WHO, UNICEF, and International Baby Food Action Network (IBFAN) [31], while “some provisions of the Code [were] included”, China had low scores for informational/educational materials (0/10), promotion in health care facilities (2/10), as well as engagement with health workers and systems (0/15) and labelling (0/15). Thus, there is still a dire need to protect mothers and their families against unregulated formula milk marketing in the health system, especially the marketing practices that target health professionals and facilities.

In some cases, health professionals need to be in contact with formula companies to develop and improve products on a scientific basis. However, these contacts must be transparent and independent of patient treatment. In addition, in some cases, infant formula may have therapeutic benefits. For example, in cases where a mother with chronic lactation insufficiency produces less milk than required to exclusively feed her infant despite following best practices, formula may be necessary to address the infant’s nutritional deficiencies [32]. The validity of clinical research on formula milk should be ensured, with maximal protection of vulnerable research participants (i.e., infant populations). In our study, we found that while industry-sponsored clinical studies are conducted in collaboration with health facilities, there is a clear lack of appropriate guidance or regulation on conducting and reporting such research, and no regulation of formula milk studies in China. Formula milk advertising in the academic publications of health professionals is another marketing strategy that the industry uses to influence the health system. In the United Kingdom (UK), it is reported that approximately 50% of breastfeeding-related academic publications contain formula milk advertising, and that there is a high-level of non-compliance with the Code and the UK regulations [33].

As the target consumers, mothers and their families are exposed to formula milk marketing from non-health system channels via health system components. As shown in our study, expectant/postpartum mothers are contacted in or around delivery facilities and promotion samples/gifts are freely distributed. This is a clear violation of the Code and national regulations in some countries [34], including China [35]. Formula milk companies also locate potential customers through health system factors and then employ both public and personal social media (e.g., collecting the WeChat contact information of mothers) to market their products, which creates challenges to regulation and supervision because of the online nature of this method of outreach [36].

We also found incidences of inappropriate specialized formula prescription. Specialized infant formulas (SIFs) refer to specially manufactured formulas intended for infants with additional nutritional requirements or specific medical conditions [37]. Various SIFs have been introduced to and heavily promoted in the Chinese market for their “special benefits” for infants, which has created confusion for both health professionals and consumers. However, only a small percentage of infants truly need SIF [37, 38], and there is a non-significant association of developmental growth with SIF feeding [39]. Health professionals should receive training to identify those infants who indeed meet the criteria to use SIF and to thoroughly educate families about the use of SIF for certain medical indications. It is important to note that, despite its disadvantages compared to breastmilk, there is a need for “real” specialized formulas, for example to treat newborns with metabolic diseases such as phenylketonuria. In cases where breastmilk is unavailable, insufficient, or where breastfeeding has failed, formulas with good ingredient composition can serve as suitable substitutes.

The current situation calls for strengthened measures to address or improve the current impact of formula marketing on infant feeding decisions of mothers. For example, relevant government authorities and scientific societies should establish regulations/guidelines on industry practices, such as prohibiting marketing of pre-formula products. Additionally, infant formula packaging should not contain unverified health claims, and product development should be supported by rigorous evidence such as prospective double-blind studies. Moreover, health professionals and their associations should implement and enforce strong conflict-of-interest policies to prevent corporate interests from influencing important health guidances and training on infant and young child feeding. The aim of these initiatives should be to clearly communicate to all stakeholders the benefits and appropriate use of breastmilk versus infant formula, creating a supportive environment for breastfeeding.

This study has several research implications. First, this study identifies a void in the policies and regulations on formula milk marketing in China. The absence of adequate regulations for domestic formula milk marketing alongside evidence of possible violations to the Code suggest the need for a more comprehensive and strict set of rules to regulate industry marketing practices, ranging from commercial to academic activities. In addition, our study has found that important health system components in China, namely service providers and health facilities, have been negatively and implicitly influenced by formula milk marketing. As such, further training and monitoring systems for health professionals and facilities should be implemented to mitigate the influence of formula milk marketing. Furthermore, there is no “one-size-fits-all” measure to strengthen the health system to counteract the negative influence of formula milk; rather, the health system should be strengthened to support health professionals and families to make informed decision and rational choices about their feeding practices.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, this study recruited a variety of stakeholders related to formula milk marketing, including not only health professionals and pregnant/postpartum mothers but also marketing executives, to provide a comprehensive overview on formula milk marketing and its impact, particularly from the industry’s perspective. Second, female participants with different SES were sampled to adjust for the potential confounding effect of SES on their perceptions of and experiences with formula milk marketing. However, due to the small sample size, the sample included was not representative. Moreover, given that this is a qualitative and explorative study, our findings are based on personal perceptions and experiences. In addition, the convenience and snowballing sampling scheme may have been limited by its voluntary participatory nature, as we may have included female participants with higher exposure to formula milk marketing or a strong desire to share their perceptions. Moreover, this approach is likely to result in the recruitment of subjects with similar characteristics, exposure and attitudes. For our study, this may have resulted in the inclusion of subjects who were exposed to a narrower range of marketing techniques than the population as a whole. Furthermore, although we recruited a variety of health professionals, including obstetricians, pediatricians and nurses, and different types of women as participants, including pregnant women and postpartum mothers, the small sample size of nurses and pregnant women may have affected the representativeness of the sample.

Conclusion

Formula milk marketing in China has a negative influence on the health system, primarily on health professionals, facilities, and families. The industry uses various marketing strategies to build relationships with service providers and health facilities and influence their knowledge and attitudes towards formula milk. The findings suggest that a more defined and strict set of rules is needed to regulate formula milk marketing, as well as to monitor implementation and compliance with the Code and domestic regulations in China.
