The ethics-driven data governance framework for health-based AI (EDG-AI4H): an integrated approach for empowering explainable AI in health insurance risk prediction in Iran
Authors: Rezvan Rahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe digital transformation of health data has enabled predictive models in insurance. In Iran, the absence of an indigenous data governance framework integrating ethical, legal, and technical considerations poses serious risks to responsible AI development. This study aimed to design an ethics-driven data governance framework to empower Explainable AI (XAI) in health insurance risk prediction in Iran.MethodsThis qualitative study (2024) used thematic analysis and framework development. Data came from: (1) a narrative review of international articles and Iranian policy documents; (2) semi-structured interviews with five experts in insurance IT, health data science, IT law, epidemiology, and insurance management. Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke's six-step thematic analysis in MAXQDA 2022. The framework was validated through a focus group with the same five experts to ensure continuity and in-depth engagement with the framework design. The study adheres to COREQ guidelines, and a completed COREQ checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1.ResultsThe EDG-AI4H framework comprises four hierarchical and interactive layers: (1) Governance-Ethics (data ownership policies, dynamic consent management, Data Ethics Board, legal compliance); (2) Operational-Integration (metadata-driven catalog, standardized data exchange, data quality assurance, secure storage architecture); (3) Intelligence-Analytics (domain-driven feature engineering, mandatory XAI-by-design, active bias monitoring, model lifecycle governance); and (4) Value-Decision Making (multi-level decision support, preventive and personalized insurance, population health management, policyholder transparency and empowerment).ConclusionThe EDG-AI4H framework addresses a critical gap in Iran's health insurance sector by linking ethics, technology, and business. It offers a roadmap for responsible, transparent, and trustworthy AI in health risk prediction, potentially reducing ethical and legal risks while fostering public trust. The framework's operational feasibility requires empirical validation through future pilot implementation studies.Keywords: Ethics-driven data governance, Health insurance, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI), Health risk prediction, Conceptual framework, Qualitative research

Introduction

Problem statement and its significance

The global health insurance industry is undergoing a fundamental transformation driven by digital technologies and Artificial Intelligence (AI) [1]. The ability to analyze vast amounts of health data to predict future risks holds the promise of revolutionizing this industry's business models—from designing personalized products and preventive cost management to fraud detection [2]. However, this significant opportunity comes alongside unprecedented ethical and governance challenges. Health data is highly sensitive, and its use demands strict adherence to privacy, informed consent, and the prevention of discrimination [3].

In Iran, despite the existence of rich databases within insurance organizations and medical centers, the move towards utilizing AI for risk prediction faces numerous structural obstacles. The absence of a coherent data governance framework that can comprehensively address issues of data ownership, exchange standards, data quality, security, privacy, and especially AI ethics is considered the primary barrier [4]. Without such a framework, the development of AI models could not only lead to violations of policyholders' rights, unfair decision-making, and the reinforcement of existing biases but also face the risk of social non-acceptance and legal hurdles [5]. The necessity of addressing this issue becomes even more apparent when considering the gap in the domestic literature. A review of studies conducted in Iran shows that despite scattered research in health data mining, very few studies have focused on designing integrated, ethics-driven data governance frameworks to guide AI in the health insurance industry. This research aims to fill this important gap by designing an indigenous conceptual framework.

Research innovation

The innovation of this research lies in its deep interdisciplinary integration and the presentation of the first indigenous framework that links five key domains: 1) health insurance and risk management, 2) data science and explainable AI, 3) data governance and information architecture, 4) medical ethics and privacy, and 5) national laws and regulations. This framework is not merely theoretical but is designed considering the realities of Iran's health and insurance ecosystem.

Theoretical foundations and background

Data governance and AI ethics in the health domain

Data governance refers to a set of processes, policies, standards, and roles that determine how data is managed, used, and protected within an organization [6]. In the health domain, this concept is intertwined with additional challenges, including the high sensitivity of health data, the necessity of privacy compliance (such as HIPAA and GDPR principles), and medical ethics requirements (principles of beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, and justice) [7],Simultaneously, the emergence of AI, particularly its subfields like machine learning, has enabled predictive analysis of health risks [8]. However, the “black box” nature of many complex AI models has raised serious concerns about transparency, accountability, algorithmic bias, and discrimination [9]. These challenges have positioned “Explainable AI” (XAI) as an essential paradigm for building trust, ensuring fairness, and facilitating social acceptance of these technologies in sensitive domains like health insurance [10].

Explainable AI (XAI): from concept to technology

Explainable AI refers to a set of methods, techniques, and frameworks in the design and development of AI systems that make understanding, interpreting, and trusting the outputs and decisions of these systems possible for human stakeholders (from developers and regulators to end-users)[11]. In health risk prediction models, XAI answers questions like “Why was this individual classified as high-risk for disease X?” or “Which factors contributed most to this prediction?” Leading techniques in this area include intrinsic methods (like simpler, inherently interpretable models such as decision trees) and post-hoc methods (like LIME and SHAP) that generate explanations at the sample or model level after building more complex models [12], [13]. Successful XAI implementation requires its integration into the AI development lifecycle and data governance.

Ethics-driven data governance: a bridge between technology and values

An ethics-driven data governance framework is a mechanism for translating abstract ethical principles (such as privacy, fairness, transparency, and accountability) into policies, technical controls, and operational processes within an organization [14]. In the context of using XAI for risk prediction in health insurance, such a framework must address key questions:

•Data Ownership and Control: Who owns and controls health data? How is informed consent obtained and managed?

•Data Quality and Bias: How is it ensured that training data is complete, accurate, and unbiased to prevent perpetuating or exacerbating health inequalities?

Experiences from leading countries indicate that success in this path requires a holistic, multi-layered approach:

•European Union: The GDPR regulation establishes the right to an “explanation” for individuals regarding automated decisions, and its proposed AI Act mandates high-risk systems (like health insurance) to comply with stringent requirements for privacy, transparency, and human oversight [15], [16].

European Union: The GDPR regulation establishes the right to an “explanation” for individuals regarding automated decisions, and its proposed AI Act mandates high-risk systems (like health insurance) to comply with stringent requirements for privacy, transparency, and human oversight [15], [16].

•Singapore: The Model AI Governance Framework [17] focuses on accountability, transparency, fairness, and human-centric ethics, offering an operational and testable approach for organizations [18].

Singapore: The Model AI Governance Framework [17] focuses on accountability, transparency, fairness, and human-centric ethics, offering an operational and testable approach for organizations [18].

•United Kingdom: The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) and the Centre for Data Ethics and Innovation (CDEI) have published practical guidance on “explainability” in AI-based decisions, emphasizing the provision of “useful, understandable, and actionable” explanations for individuals [19], [20], [21].

United Kingdom: The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) and the Centre for Data Ethics and Innovation (CDEI) have published practical guidance on “explainability” in AI-based decisions, emphasizing the provision of “useful, understandable, and actionable” explanations for individuals [19], [20], [21].

These experiences underscore the necessity of interdisciplinary collaboration (law, ethics, technology, medicine, and management), the development of technical standards for explainability, and the establishment of specialized regulatory bodies.

Domestic background: identifying the research gap

In Iran, despite the richness of health data in insurance organizations and medical centers, and some initial efforts in health IT, coherent research specifically focused on designing an ethics-driven data governance framework to empower XAI in health insurance is very limited. Existing studies often concentrate on technical aspects of data mining and machine learning in health [22] or general IT challenges in the health system [23]. A major gap in the domestic literature is the lack of an integrated, indigenous framework that can simultaneously and harmoniously address the technical requirements of XAI, ethical and religious principles, national laws (such as the Personal Data Protection Law), and the managerial and economic realities of Iran's health insurance industry. This research aims to take a step towards filling this gap by using qualitative methods and drawing on expert opinions, offering a roadmap for a responsible and trust-based movement towards utilizing AI in health insurance risk prediction.

Research methodology

Research design

This research is a qualitative study with a conceptual framework development approach. The goal is to generate a deep understanding of the problem and provide a novel, indigenous theoretical structure for it.

Participants and sampling

Using purposive sampling with a maximum variation strategy, five prominent Iranian specialists were selected, representing the five key related domains: IT and data governance, AI and data science, IT law and privacy, epidemiology and health information systems, and strategic insurance management. Selection criteria included: 1) at least 10 years of specialized experience, 2) holding a policy-making position, senior management role, or recognized academic standing, and 3) having demonstrated understanding of data challenges in Iran's health or insurance domain. All five invited experts agreed to participate, and no dropouts occurred during the study. The experts' profiles are presented in Table 1.

Profile of participating experts.

The sample size of five experts was justified based on the exploratory and conceptual nature of this framework development study, which prioritizes depth of expertise over statistical generalizability. Given the highly specialized nature of the required expertise—spanning five distinct domains—the number of experts meeting all inclusion criteria in Iran is inherently limited. We assessed thematic saturation systematically after each interview [24]. By the fourth interview, no new codes or themes emerged, and the fifth interview confirmed saturation within the five disciplinary domains represented by the expert panel. The maximum variation strategy ensured that despite the small sample, perspectives from all relevant disciplinary and professional domains were captured. However, it is important to distinguish between thematic saturation within the domains sampled and the adequacy of disciplinary coverage. While the five experts provide breadth across five key domains, this does not necessarily establish saturation across the entire Iranian health-insurance ecosystem. The findings should be interpreted as exploratory and context-specific, providing a foundation for future empirical validation with broader stakeholder groups.

Additionally, relevant Iranian policy documents, including the Personal Data Protection Law and health information exchange guidelines, were reviewed.

Data analysis

The interview data were analyzed using Thematic Analysis based on Braun and Clarke's [25], [26] six-step model with the aid of MAXQDA software version 2022. The analysis process involved six stages: (1) deep familiarization with the data through repeated reading of the texts; (2) generating initial codes from prominent data features; (3) searching for and organizing themes by categorizing extracted codes; (4) reviewing themes at the level of codes and the entire dataset; (5) final definition and naming of themes; and (6) preparing an analytical report of the findings.

Initial coding was performed independently by the primary researcher. To enhance reliability, a second researcher (Dr. Hanieh Zehtab Hashemi, PhD in Medical Informatics, Virtual University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran), who was not involved in data collection, independently coded 20% of the interview transcripts (two randomly selected interviews). Inter-coder agreement was assessed through a qualitative consensus approach rather than a formal quantitative statistic. After independent coding, the two coders met to compare their coding decisions. All discrepancies and disagreements were discussed in joint sessions until full consensus was reached on all coding decisions. This consensus-based approach is widely recognized as an appropriate and rigorous method for establishing inter-coder agreement in qualitative thematic analysis [25], [27]. The final coding structure, comprising 84 initial codes, 16 sub-themes, and 4 main themes, was reviewed and refined through iterative team discussions.

Ethical considerations and research quality assurance

This research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and after obtaining an ethics code from Research Ethics Committees of School of Public Health & Allied Medical Sciences- Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Code: IR.TUMS.SPH.REC.1402.171), and informed consent was obtained orally and in writing from all participants. Confidentiality and the right to withdraw at any stage were guaranteed. To enhance the validity and trustworthiness of the qualitative research, multiple strategies were employed, including member checking by sending extracted theme summaries to each expert for confirmation and revision, data triangulation by combining deep interviews and documentary study, providing thick descriptions of the research process and context, and holding a validation focus group session with the five experts. This 120-min session involved critiquing and refining the designed framework to increase its relevance and practicality. The reporting of this qualitative study adheres to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines. A completed COREQ checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1. The primary researcher (Female, PhD in Medical Informatics) has formal training in qualitative methods and thematic analysis. No prior relationship existed with any participant before the study. While the researcher's professional background in health data governance informed the inquiry, reflexive bracketing was applied throughout data collection and analysis to mitigate potential bias and ensure interpretive rigor.

Layer one: governance, ethics, and compliance (the foundational layer)

This layer establishes the legal, ethical, and policy foundation for the entire framework and is a prerequisite for any operational action. As H3 explicitly stated: ‘Any technical move without resolving issues of ownership, consent, and a legal compliance framework is like building a palace on quicksand.’ Similarly, H4 described dynamic consent as the ‘missing key to trust-building,’ emphasizing that policyholders must have granular control over how their data is used

•Data Ownership, Access, and Sharing Policies: Clear definition of ownership and usage rights for data generated in the tripartite interaction among insurer, service provider, and policyholder. Developing governance models for secure data sharing between these entities while preserving stakeholders' rights.

Layer four: value creation and transparent decision-making

This layer transforms the insights generated in the intelligence layer into tangible actions, innovative services, and value for all stakeholders. All experts considered the policyholder transparency component the pinnacle of the framework. Discussion

In the present research, the EDG-AI4H framework has been presented as a systematic and indigenous response to the identified gaps in both domestic and international literature. This framework encompasses several key conceptual and operational advancements that distinguish it from purely technical approaches.

First, it emphasizes the precedence of ethics and governance over technology. In EDG-AI4H, the governance-ethics layer acts as a prerequisite for the technical layers, aligning with the global paradigm of “Responsible AI” [14]. The establishment of a “Data Ethics Board” and “Dynamic Consent Management” are objective mechanisms for institutionalizing this. This approach complements technical frameworks like SHINFDP, which focuses on fraud detection through the integration of blockchain, machine learning, 5G, and cloud computing [28]. While SHINFDP offers a six-layer architecture focusing on security and efficiency, EDG-AI4H adds the governance-ethics layer as a foundation, ensuring these technologies are employed responsibly with respect to privacy and transparency. The EDG-AI4H approach ensures this “intelligence” and “adaptability” are framed within an appropriate ethical and governance context.

Second, it creates a common language and platform for interdisciplinary dialogue. By simultaneously encompassing legal, medical, technical, and business dimensions, this framework provides a shared conceptual map that enables constructive collaboration between sometimes disparate domains. This is particularly vital in the context of Health Insurance Claim (HIC) processing, which involves multiple stakeholders including Healthcare Service Providers (HSP), policyholders (HINS), and insurance companies (HIP) [28]. The integrated EDG-AI4H approach can reduce the coordination and transparency challenges present in the traditional HIC process. This perspective resonates with the interoperability between professional developers and citizen developers (Business Analysts and End Users). The proposed framework facilitates this collaboration in the complex domain of health insurance by establishing a common language.

Third, it elevates explainability from a technical feature to a governance and ethical imperative. In the sensitive health domain, model accuracy alone is insufficient; explainability and auditability are necessary conditions for social acceptance [29]. The “XAI-by-Design” requirement in the third layer embeds this concept at the core of the development process. This is also addressed in the SHINFDP framework, where the Prediction Layer uses machine learning algorithms to classify legitimate and fraudulent claims [28]. Integrating XAI in EDG-AI4H ensures that automation decisions are not only accurate but also understandable and auditable for all stakeholders, including policyholders and regulators. EDG-AI4H ensures these analytical and decision-making capabilities are transparent and accountable.

Fourth, it focuses on creating bilateral value and shifting the mindset. EDG-AI4H explicitly emphasizes policyholder empowerment and transparency, not just insurer benefit. The “Policyholder Transparency” component can transform the traditional relationship into an active partnership in health management. This aligns with the use of Wearable (WRD) technologies in case studies where policyholders receive incentives like premium reductions for sharing their health data [28]. The proposed framework, by building trust through transparency and privacy-centricity, enables broader adoption of such programs. EDG-AI4H operationalizes these goals in the health insurance domain, centered on value creation for all stakeholders.

The “XAI-by-Design” requirement embedded in Layer Three operationalizes transparency as a governance imperative rather than a technical afterthought. This requirement translates into four concrete governance mechanisms: first, mandatory explanation documentation for every model deployment, which must be reviewed and approved by the Data Ethics Board before production release; second, stakeholder-specific explanation formats that tailor the presentation of model explanations to the needs of different audiences—simple, non-technical language for policyholders, detailed technical reports for data scientists and auditors, and clinically meaningful summaries for physicians; third, the establishment of explainability as a formal model acceptance criterion, meaning that any model failing to meet minimum explainability standards—defined as the ability to produce comprehensive feature impact reports (e.g., SHAP or LIME explanations) within acceptable computational time—is not approved for production deployment; and fourth, regular explanation audits conducted periodically. Importantly, the availability of SHAP or LIME explanations should not be treated as proof that a model is explainable, fair, or trustworthy. In high-stakes health insurance decisions, we recommend a combination of methods—including intrinsic interpretability, post-hoc explanation techniques, and human oversight—rather than reliance on any single explanation technique. Future research should explore how different stakeholders—including policyholders, clinicians, and insurers—perceive, interpret, and act upon these explanations in real-world decision-making contexts.

Compared to established international frameworks, EDG-AI4H offers several distinctive contributions while complementing existing approaches. The OECD AI Principles [30] emphasize transparency, accountability, and human-centered values, which are embedded throughout our framework. The WHO Guidance on AI Ethics [31] provides high-level ethical principles for health AI, including protecting autonomy, promoting human well-being, and ensuring transparency. However, neither offers the operational specificity required for health insurance risk prediction in Iran's context. EDG-AI4H translates these high-level principles into concrete layers—from data ownership policies to XAI-by-design model development—that can be operationalized by insurers.

Similarly, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework [32] provides comprehensive guidance for managing AI risks across sectors, but it does not address the unique challenges of health insurance data governance, such as dynamic consent management, FHIR-based data exchange, or policyholder empowerment through transparent health reporting. The EU AI Act [16] classifies health insurance AI as ‘high-risk’ and mandates transparency and human oversight, but leaves implementation mechanisms to member states. The SHINFDP framework [28] offers a six-layer architecture for smart health insurance with a focus on fraud detection through blockchain, machine learning, and 5G technologies. While SHINFDP emphasizes security and efficiency, EDG-AI4H adds an explicit governance-ethics layer as a foundational prerequisite, ensuring that even technically advanced solutions are deployed responsibly with respect to privacy, transparency, and fairness. This reflects a broader trend in responsible AI where governance frameworks must precede or accompany technical innovation [5], [14].

Implementation challenges and resource requirements

While the EDG-AI4H framework provides a comprehensive governance roadmap, its successful implementation faces several significant challenges that require careful consideration:

Financial and Resource Requirements: Implementing the full framework, including infrastructure development (data lake, warehouse, secure APIs), human capacity building, and ongoing governance (Data Ethics Board operations), requires substantial financial investment. For Iranian insurance organizations, many of which operate with limited IT budgets, prioritizing and sequencing implementation phases is essential. Future research should conduct cost-benefit analyses to estimate economic returns of robust data governance.

Technical Infrastructure Readiness: As noted by Expert H2, Iran's health information systems currently rely on openEHR and ISO 13606 standards, with FHIR adoption still emerging. The framework's operational layer (metadata catalog, standardized data exchange) requires significant technical upgrades to existing systems and industry-wide coordination to develop national interoperability standards.

Organizational and Cultural Barriers: Insurance organizations may face resistance from staff accustomed to traditional claims-based operations. The paradigm shifts from reactive “claims payment” to proactive “health management” requires cultural transformation, change management strategies, and leadership commitment. Additionally, data sharing between insurers, hospitals, and policyholders requires building trust through transparency and demonstrated value.

Regulatory and Policy Gaps: While Iran's Personal Data Protection Law provides a foundation, specific regulations for AI governance, algorithm transparency, and accountability mechanisms are still under development. The framework's implementation will require coordination with regulatory bodies and potential legal reforms.

Human Capacity Development: The interdisciplinary nature of the framework—combining data science, law, ethics, medicine, and insurance—requires professionals with cross-domain competencies. Training programs, workshops, and academic partnerships will be essential to build the necessary workforce.

Implementation Sequencing: We recommend a phased approach: Phase 1—establish the Data Ethics Board and develop data governance policies; Phase 2—implement metadata catalog and data quality frameworks; Phase 3—develop XAI-compliant models with bias monitoring; Phase 4—roll out value-creation components with policyholder engagement.

This research demonstrated that the transition of Iran's health insurance industry towards the era of data and AI requires a solid theoretical and operational foundation in the domain of ethics-driven data governance. The EDG-AI4H conceptual framework, resulting from a synthesis of international knowledge, domestic documents, and the collective wisdom of five prominent and experienced experts, can serve as a national roadmap and practical guide for insurance organizations, regulators, and policymakers in this field.

Research limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings:

Sample Size and Selection: The framework is based on interviews with only five experts. While this sample was appropriate for the exploratory, conceptual nature of this study and was justified by the highly specialized expertise required, the findings may not capture the full diversity of perspectives across Iran's health insurance ecosystem. Additionally, while thematic saturation was achieved within the five disciplinary domains sampled, this does not establish saturation across the broader Iranian health-insurance ecosystem. The findings should be considered exploratory rather than exhaustive.

Validation Limitation: A key methodological limitation is that the validation focus group comprised the same five experts who participated in the initial interviews. This approach may have introduced confirmation bias and limited the independence of the validation process. Ideally, validation should involve independent stakeholders.

Lack of Empirical Validation: The framework remains conceptual and has not been empirically tested in real-world insurance settings. While this is appropriate for an exploratory framework development study, the practical feasibility, effectiveness, and operationalization of the framework's components require testing through field studies, action research, or pilot projects.

Generalizability: The framework was developed specifically for Iran's health insurance context. While the underlying principles may be transferable to other developing countries with similar healthcare systems, the specific implementation mechanisms may require cultural and institutional adaptation.

Implementation Constraints: The framework's implementation requires significant organizational, technical, financial, and human resources that may not be readily available in all insurance organizations, particularly in resource-constrained settings.”
