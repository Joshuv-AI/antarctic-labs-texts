A note on the Littlewood-Offord problem for discrete log-concave distributions
Arnaud Marsiglietti, James Melbourne

Abstract
We present an extension of the famous Littlewood-Offord problem when Bernoulli distributions are replaced with discrete log-concave distributions. A variant of the Littlewood-Offord problem for arithmetic progressions, as well as an entropic version, is also discussed. Along the way, we recover and extend a result of Madiman and Woo (2015) on the entropy power inequality for discrete uniform distributions.



Keywords:
Littlewood-Offord problem, log-concave, arithmetic progression, Rényi entropy, majorization

1
Introduction

Given
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
and independent random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, with a Rademacher distribution, that is, for all
1\leq k\leq n
,
\mathbb{P}(X_{k}=\pm 1)=1/2
,
the question of estimating the quantity

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{P}(a_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}X_{n}=x)

is often referred to as the Littlewood-Offord problem. A classical result of Littlewood and Offord
[
13
]
and Erdös
[
7
]
states that

\displaystyle\sup_{a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{P}(a_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}X_{n}=x)\leq\frac{1}{2^{n}}\binom{n}{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}=O\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg).

(1)

Kleitman extended this result when
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are vectors in a Hilbert space
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
. Many variants of the Erdös-Littlewood-Offord problem have been established, such as improved bounds under certain constraints on the
a_{i}
’s
[
10
]
, an inverse Littlewood-Offord theorem
[
26
]
,
[
22
]
, and a resilience version
[
2
]
, but most are mainly dealing with Bernoulli distribution with parameter
1/2
. Recently, Fox, Kwan and Sauermann
[
8
, Question 6.2]
asked whether the Littlewood-Offord problem can be solved for Bernoulli distribution of arbitrary parameter
p\in(0,1)
. This question has been investigated by Singhal
[
24
]
, where the author provides partial answers. A full solution was given by Melbourne, Madiman and Roberto in
[
16
]
.

Recall that an integer-valued random variable
X
is said to be discrete log-concave if its probability mass function
p
satisfies

p(j)^{2}\geq p(j-1)p(j+1)

for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and the support of
X
is an integer interval. Discrete log-concave distributions form an important class. Examples include discrete uniform, Bernoulli, binomial
and convolutions of Bernoulli distributions with arbitrary parameters, Poisson, geometric, negative binomial, etc. (cf.
[
12
]
and references therein). We refer to
[
25
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
5
]
for further background on log-concavity.

The goal of this article is to extend the solution of the Littlewood-Offord problem to the whole class of discrete log-concave distributions. In particular, we prove the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, be independent discrete log-concave random variables finitely supported. Then,

\sup_{a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{P}(a_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}X_{n}=x)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+c\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{k})}},

(2)

with
c=1
. Moreover, one may take
c=2
when the random variables are, in addition, symmetric about a point.

We note that this recovers the
O(1/\sqrt{n})
bound for independent Bernoulli distribution with parameter
1/2
.

It has been shown in
[
16
]
that the bound (
2
) holds with
c=2
when the
X_{k}
’s have a Bernoulli distribution with arbitrary parameters.

Theorem
1.1
is sharp up to an absolute constant, as the left-hand side of inequality (
2
) can be lower bounded by

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+12\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{k})}},

see Remark
3.2
.

The article also presents an entropic version of the Littlewood-Offord problem. See Section
3
for a precise definition of the Rényi entropy power
N_{\alpha}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,+\infty]
and
n\geq 1
. Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent discrete log-concave random variables finitely supported. Then,

\inf_{a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}}N_{\alpha}(a_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}X_{n})\geq 1+c\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{k}),

(3)

with
c=1
. Moreover, one may take
c=4
when
1<\alpha\leq 2
, and for other values of
\alpha
one may take
c=2
when the random variables are, in addition, symmetric about a point.

It has been shown in
[
16
]
that the bound (
3
) holds with
c=\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-1}
,
\alpha\geq 2
, when the
X_{k}
’s have a Bernoulli distribution. It turns out that Theorem
1.1
is a particular case of Theorem
1.2
.

We also present a version of the Littlewood-Offord problem for arithmetic progressions.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, be independent discrete log-concave random variables finitely supported. Then,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{P}(a_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}X_{n}\in A_{l,m}(x))\leq\frac{l}{\sqrt{1+c\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{k})+c\,\frac{l^{2}-1}{12}}},

with
c=1
. Moreover, one may take
c=2
when the random variables are, in addition, symmetric about a point.

Here,
A_{l,m}(x)
is an arithmetic progression of length
l\geq 1
, that is
A_{l,m}(x)=\{x+mj\}_{j=1}^{l}
for
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In fact,
m
can be taken as a real number (see Section
4
). For example, if the
X_{k}
’s are i.i.d. Bernoulli with parameter
1/2
, then we deduce

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{P}(a_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}X_{n}\in A_{l,m}(x))\leq\frac{l}{\sqrt{1+\frac{n}{2}+\frac{l^{2}-1}{6}}}.

We refer to Section
4
for an estimate when the
X_{k}
’s have a Bernoulli distribution with arbitrary parameter
p\in(0,1)
.

Let us note that the case
l=1
corresponds to the classical Littlewood-Offord problem, hence Theorem
1.1
is a particular case of Theorem
1.3
.

Finally, our method allows us to establish the following entropy power inequality for discrete uniform distributions.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,2]
and
n\geq 1
. Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
be uniformly distributed independent random variables on any set of integers. Then,

N_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}\right)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}N_{\alpha}(U_{k})-(n-1).

The particular case
\alpha=1
and
n=2
of Theorem
1.4
has been established by Madiman and Woo in
[
27
]
.

The article is organized as follows. Section
2
provides the necessary background on the notion of majorization and rearrangement inequalities, which are the main ingredients in the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
are postponed to Section
3
. Section
4
presents a variant of the Littlewood-Offord problem for arithmetic progressions, in particular, Theorem
1.3
is proved. The last section focuses on the Bernoulli and uniform distributions, where Theorem
1.4
is proved.

2
Preliminaries

Throughout the article, we use the notation
x\cdot y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}
, for the dot product of
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
. We also denote

M(X)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{P}(X=x).

(4)

We will need the following result.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
1
]
,
[
4
]
)
.

If the random variable
X
has a discrete log-concave distribution, then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+12{\rm Var}(X)}}\leq M(X)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+{\rm Var}(X)}}.

(5)

Moreover, if the distribution of
X
is symmetric about a point, then the upper bound may be sharpened to

M(X)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2{\rm Var}(X)}}.

Let us note that the lower bound in (
5
) holds for arbitrary random variables. The upper bound in (
5
) was proven by Aravinda
[
1
]
, who refined the bound

M(X)\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+4{\rm Var}(X)}}

obtained by Bobkov and the authors in
[
4
]
(see also
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
).

Recall that a probability distribution
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
written in descending order with positive entries,
p_{i}\geq p_{i+1}
, is majorized by another
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})
also written in descending order with positive entries when

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}

(6)

holds for all
k
. We write
p\prec q
when
p
is majorized by
q
. We also write
X\prec Y
when the probability mass function of
X
is majorized by the probability mass function of
Y
. After extending
q
to
\{1,\dots,n\}
by setting
q_{j}=0
for
m+1\leq j\leq n
, this is equivalent to the existence of a doubly stochastic matrix
A
such that
Aq=p
(see
[
18
]
).

For example, if
q
is a point mass,
(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})=(1,0,\dots,0)
, then for any other
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
we can write

\displaystyle A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}p_{1}&p_{2}&\dots&p_{n}\\
p_{2}&p_{3}&\dots&p_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
p_{n}&p_{1}&\dots&p_{n-1}\end{array}\right),

so that
Aq=p
. More generally, if
q
is not a probability sequence and
q=(M,0,\dots,0)
while
\sum_{i}p_{i}=M
then setting
\lambda_{i}=p_{i}/M
, we can write

\displaystyle A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{2}&\dots&\lambda_{n}\\
\lambda_{2}&\lambda_{3}&\dots&\lambda_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\lambda_{n}&\lambda_{1}&\dots&\lambda_{n-1}\end{array}\right),

so that
Aq=p
.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
Y
is a random variable taking finitely many values, and
f
is a deterministic function, then
Y\prec f(Y)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
Y
is distributed on
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, and denote by
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}
the support of
f(Y)
. If the distribution of
Y
is denoted by
p
, note that the distribution of
f(Y)
, written as
q
, will satisfy
q_{i}=\sum_{j\in f^{-1}(\{a_{i}\})}p_{j}
. Writing
q=(q_{1},0,\dots,0,q_{2},0,\dots,0,\dots,q_{m},0,\dots,0)
where the number of zeros between
q_{i}
and
q_{i+1}
is determined by the cardinality
n_{i}
of
f^{-1}(\{a_{i}\})
, and writing
p
in order such that
f^{-1}(\{a_{i}\})=\{k_{i},k_{i}+1,\dots,k_{i}+n_{i}-1\}
so that
q_{i}=p_{k_{i}}+\cdots+p_{k_{i}+n_{i}-1}
, based on the discussion above we can write a doubly stochastic block matrix,

\displaystyle\Lambda=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}(A_{1})&(0)&(0)&\dots&(0)\\
(0)&(A_{2})&(0)&\dots&(0)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
(0)&(0)&\dots&(0)&(A_{m})\end{array}\right)

where each
A_{i}
is of the form of
(
2
)
for the
p_{j}
such that
f(j)=a_{i}
, so that
\Lambda q=p
, and the lemma holds.
∎

If
f\colon\mathbb{Z}\to[0,+\infty)
is finitely supported, with support
\{x_{0},\dots,x_{m}\}
, denote by
f^{\#}
its squeezed rearrangement, that is,
f^{\#}
is supported on
\{0,\dots,m\}
and
f^{\#}(j)=f(x_{j})
, for
j\in\{0,\dots,m\}
. If
X
is an integer-valued random variable with probability mass function
f
, we denote by
X^{\#}
the random variable with probability mass function
f^{\#}
. The following result was proven in
[
17
]
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
17
]
)
.

If
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are integer-valued independent random variables such that
X_{1}^{\#},\dots,X_{n}^{\#}
are log-concave, then

X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}\prec X_{1}^{\#}+\dots+X_{n}^{\#}.

Finally, recall that the Rényi entropy of order
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
of a discrete random variable
X
with values in a countable set
I
and with probability mass function
p
is defined as

H_{\alpha}(X)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left(\sum_{x\in I}p^{\alpha}(x)\right)=\log(\|p\|_{\alpha}^{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}).

The limit cases are interpreted as

H_{0}(X)=\log(|\mathrm{Supp}(p)|),\quad H_{1}(X)=-\sum_{x\in I}p(x)\log(p(x)),\quad H_{\infty}(X)=-\log(\sup_{x\in I}p(x)).

We note that the
M
-functional defined in (
4
) may be viewed as a member of the family of Rényi entropies via the formula

M(X)=e^{-H_{\infty}(X)}.

(20)

In particular, considering the Rényi entropy power
N_{\alpha}(X)=e^{2H_{\alpha}(X)}
, Theorem
2.1
yields the bound

N_{\alpha}(X)\geq N_{\infty}(X)\geq 1+{\rm Var}(X),

(21)

for arbitrary discrete log-concave random variable
X
, where the first inequality holds by monotonicity of Rényi entropy.

3
The Littlewood-Offord problem for discrete log-concave distributions and an entropic version

The first step in establishing the Littlewood-Offord problem for log-concave distributions is to reduce the problem to signs.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, and
X_{i}
independent, log-concave,
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random variables taking finitely many values, there exist signs
v_{i}\in\pm 1
such that
a\cdot X\prec v\cdot X
.

Proof.

Let us first observe that
a\cdot X
can be majorized by
\tilde{a}\cdot X
, for set of constants
\tilde{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Indeed, it has been observed in
[
16
, Proof of Lemma 5.1]
that one may construct a linear map
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Q}
such that
T(a_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
for all
i
. Thus, by Lemma
2.2
,
a\cdot X\prec T(a\cdot X)
. Further, since the
X_{i}
’s are integer-valued, one has

\displaystyle T(a_{1}X_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}X_{n})=T(a_{1})X_{1}+\cdots+T(a_{n})X_{n}.

(22)

Writing
T(a)=(T(a_{1}),\dots,T(a_{n}))\in(\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
, we thus have
a\cdot X\prec T(a)\cdot X
.

Observe that our result follows from Theorem
2.3
since
T(a)\cdot X\prec(T(a_{1})X_{1})^{\#}+\cdots+(T(a_{n})X_{n})^{\#}
. Indeed,
(T(a_{i})X_{i})^{\#}=v_{i}X_{i}
where
v_{i}=sign(T(a_{i}))
. Since
v_{i}X_{i}
is log-concave one may apply Theorem
2.3
and we have
a\cdot X\prec T(a)\cdot X\prec v\cdot X
where
v_{i}=\pm 1
and the result follows.
∎

Since
\alpha
-Rényi entropy is Schur concave as a consequence of
[
18
, Proposition 3-C.1]
(see also
[
17
]
),
the proof of Theorem
1.2
follows immediately.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem
3.1
combined with Schur concavity of Rényi entropy yields that for
\alpha\in[0,\infty]
,
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
with
X_{i}
independent and log-concave then for all
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
with
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, there exists
v_{i}=\pm 1
such that
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
implies

\displaystyle N_{\alpha}(a\cdot X)\geq N_{\alpha}(v\cdot X).

(23)

Moreover the choice of signs is independent of the
X_{i}
determined only by the coefficients
a_{i}
.
Therefore, using (
21
),

N_{\alpha}(a\cdot X)\geq N_{\alpha}(v\cdot X)\geq 1+{\rm Var}(v\cdot X)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{i}).

It has been shown in
[
4
]
that when
1<\alpha\leq 2
,
N_{\alpha}(X)\geq 1+4{\rm Var}(X)
for arbitrary log-concave
X
, while
N_{\alpha}(X)\geq 1+2{\rm Var}(X)
when
X
is symmetric about a point and log-concave. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Theorem
1.2
is sharp up to an absolute constant. This is a consequence of the bound
N_{\alpha}(X)\leq 1+\frac{4(3\alpha-1)}{\alpha-1}{\rm Var}(X)
proved in
[
4
]
, which holds for all
\alpha>1
. Therefore,

\inf_{a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}}N_{\alpha}(a\cdot X)\leq 1+\frac{4(3\alpha-1)}{\alpha-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{i}).

To obtain a better estimate when
\alpha
tends to 1, one may use the following well-known upper bound for the discrete entropy,
N(X)\leq\frac{2\pi e}{12}+2\pi e{\rm Var}(X)
(see, e.g.,
[
19
]
,
[
3
]
), which yields for all
\alpha\geq 1
,

\inf_{a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}}N_{\alpha}(a\cdot X)\leq\frac{2\pi e}{12}+2\pi e\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{i}).

Specializing Theorem
1.2
to
\alpha=+\infty
, and recalling (
20
), we obtain Theorem
1.1
:

\sup_{a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}}M(a\cdot X)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{i})}},

(24)

holding for arbitrary independent log-concave random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. In particular, we deduce the following.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. Bernoulli distribution of parameter
p
. Then,

\sup_{a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}}M(a\cdot X)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+np(1-p)}}.

As mentioned in the introduction, in this specific case of Bernoulli distribution, a refined argument has been used in
[
16
]
to show that

\sup_{a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}}M(a\cdot X)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2np(1-p)}}.

(25)

We note that one may provide a unification of both Erdos’ result of the Littlewood-Offord problem (
1
) and (
25
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables such that for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
X_{i}\in\{x_{i},x_{i+1}\}
, where
x_{i},x_{i+1}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
x_{i}\leq x_{i+1}
and

\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=x_{i})=1-\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=x_{i+1})=1-\theta_{i},\qquad\theta_{i}\in(0,1).

Then,

\sup_{a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}}M(a\cdot X)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{{\rm Var}(X_{i})}{(x_{i}-x_{i+1})^{2}}}}.

Proof.

Using the same argument as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, one may deduce that for all
a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
,

M(a\cdot X)\leq M(\mathbbm{1}\cdot B),

where
B_{1},\dots,B_{n}
are independent Bernoulli distributions of parameter
\theta_{i}
or
1-\theta_{i}
depending on the sign of
a_{i}
.
The result follows by using the bound

M(\mathbbm{1}\cdot B)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(B_{i})}},

which is a consequence of (
25
), and by noting that

{\rm Var}(B_{i})=\theta_{i}(1-\theta_{i})=\frac{{\rm Var}(X_{i})}{(x_{i+1}-x_{i})^{2}}.

∎

The Littlewood-Offord solution (
1
), as well as Proposition
3.3
immediately follow from Proposition
3.4
. Note that one may even allow Rademacher
\pm 1
distributions with arbitrary parameter
p\in(0,1)
, which yields the same inequality as for Bernoulli distributions. We state this result in the next corollary.

Corollary 3.5
.

If the
X_{i}
’s are independent Rademacher distributions with arbitrary parameter
p\in(0,1)
, then

\sup_{a\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}}M(a\cdot X)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2np(1-p)}}.

4
A Littlewood-Offord type problem for arithmetic progressions

Given independent
\mathbb{Z}
-valued random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, and
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{n}
, we ask for an upper bound on

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{P}(a\cdot X\in A_{l,m}(x))

where
A_{l,m}(x)
is an arithmetic progression of length
l\geq 1
, that is
A_{l,m}(x)=\{x+mj\}_{j=1}^{l}
for
m,x\in\mathbb{R}
. In the case that
l=1
this corresponds to the classical Littlewood-Offord problem for the variables
X_{k}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
Y
be a discrete random variable independent of
U_{l}
, where
U_{l}
is uniform on
\{1,2,\dots,l\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y-mU_{l}=x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathbb{P}(U_{l}=k,Y=x+mk)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{l}\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathbb{P}(Y=x+mk)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{l}\mathbb{P}(Y\in A_{l,m}(x)).

Thus, this Littlewood-Offord problem for arithmetic progressions is equivalent to finding upper bounds on
M(a\cdot X-mU_{l})
. When the
X_{k}
’s are discrete log-concave, one may thus apply Theorem
1.1
to obtain

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\mathbb{P}(a\cdot X\in A_{l,m}(x))=l\,M(a\cdot X-mU_{l})\leq\frac{l}{\sqrt{1+c\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{i})+{\rm Var}(U_{l})\right)}},

which is the desired result since
{\rm Var}(U_{l})=(l^{2}-1)/12
.
∎

5
Specific case of the Bernoulli and uniform distributions

5.1
Bernoulli distribution

This section focuses on strengthening Theorem
1.3
for the Bernoulli distribution. Let
U_{l}
be uniform on
\{1,\dots,l\}
. It has been shown in
[
20
, Proof of Theorem 1.7]
that for all
p\geq 2
the Fourier transform of
U_{l}
satisfies

\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|\mathbb{E}e^{2\pi itU_{l}}\right|^{p}dt\leq\int_{-A}^{A}e^{-\pi(l^{2}-1)pt^{2}/2}dt,

where
A
is determined implicitly through the equation

\int_{-A}^{A}e^{-\pi(l^{2}-1)t^{2}}dt=\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left|\mathbb{E}e^{2\pi itU_{l}}\right|^{2}dt=\frac{1}{l}.

(26)

The first equality in (
26
) gives the implicit definition of
A
(in terms of
l
), while the second equality comes from recalling that
U_{l}
is uniform and using Parseval identity. Therefore,

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left|\mathbb{E}e^{itU_{l}}\right|^{p}dt\leq 2\int_{0}^{A}e^{-\pi(l^{2}-1)pt^{2}/2}dt=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi(l^{2}-1)p}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\pi(l^{2}-1)A^{2}p}}e^{-x^{2}/2}dx=2A\Phi(cp),

(27)

where
\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{x}}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt
and
c=\pi(l^{2}-1)A^{2}
. Note that

A=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(l^{2}-1)}}\cdot\operatorname{erf}^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{l^{2}-1}}{l}\right),

where
\operatorname{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}dt
is the error function. On the other hand, it has been shown in
[
16
, Theorem 2.8]
that if
X
is a Bernoulli random variable with variance
\sigma^{2}
and
q\geq 1
,

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left|\mathbb{E}e^{itX}\right|^{q}dt\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{6\sigma^{2}q}}\int_{0}^{\sqrt{6\sigma^{2}q}}e^{-t^{2}/2}=\Phi(6\sigma^{2}q).

(28)

Let us now consider
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
independent Bernoulli random variables with variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}
and
U_{l}
uniform on
\{1,\dots,l\}
, and denote
v=(\pm 1,\dots,\pm 1)
and
v_{0}=\pm 1
any choice of signs, then

\displaystyle\|f_{\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{k}X_{k}+v_{0}U_{l}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\widehat{f}_{\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{k}X_{k}+v_{0}U_{l}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left|\mathbb{E}e^{it(v\cdot X+v_{0}U_{l})}\right|dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left|\mathbb{E}e^{itU_{l}}\right|^{p}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left|\mathbb{E}e^{itX_{k}}\right|^{q_{k}}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{q_{k}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(2A\Phi(\pi(l^{2}-1)A^{2}p)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Phi\left(6\sigma_{k}^{2}q_{k}\right)^{\frac{1}{q_{k}}},

where we have used the Hausdorff-Young inequality, Hölder’s inequality with
\frac{1}{p}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{q_{k}}=1
, the independence of the variables, and the bounds (
27
), (
28
). Choosing
C=\pi(l^{2}-1)A^{2}+6\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_{k}^{2}
, and setting
q_{k}=\frac{C}{6\sigma_{k}^{2}}
and
p=\frac{C}{\pi(l^{2}-1)A^{2}}
, then if
p\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(a\cdot X\in A_{l})

\displaystyle\leq l\,\|f_{\sum v_{i}X_{i}+v_{0}U_{l}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq l(2A)^{\frac{1}{p}}\Phi(C)^{\frac{1}{p}}\,\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Phi\left(C\right)^{\frac{1}{q_{k}}}

\displaystyle=l(2A)^{\frac{1}{p}}\Phi(C).

Using the bound
\Phi(z)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{z}{3}}}
holding for all
z>0
(see
[
16
, Lemma 2.9]
)
we deduce

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(a\cdot X\in A_{l})\leq(2A)^{\frac{1}{p}}\frac{l}{\sqrt{1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{k})+\frac{l^{2}-1}{12}4\pi A^{2}}}.

(29)

Remark 5.1
.

Let us compare the bound (
29
) with theorem
1.3
. Since
2A\leq 1
, we have

(2A)^{\frac{1}{p}}\frac{l}{\sqrt{1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{k})+\frac{l^{2}-1}{12}4\pi A^{2}}}\leq\frac{l}{\sqrt{1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{k})+\frac{l^{2}-1}{12}4\pi A^{2}}}.

Therefore, for
l=1
, we recover the bound (
25
) proved in
[
16
]
. Moreover, for
l=2
, one can check numerically that
4\pi A^{2}\geq 1
, so that the bound (
29
) is always better for Bernoulli distribution than Theorem
1.3
. Note that for fixed length
l
, the bound (
29
) is stronger as the variance grows.

However, the bound is not always applicable when
l\geq 2
, as
p=1+\frac{6\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(X_{k})}{\pi(l^{2}-1)A^{2}}
needs to be greater than or equal to 2. Hence, one may not choose variances that are too small compared to the length
l
.

5.2
Discrete uniform distribution

This section specializes to the uniform distribution. In particular, we recover and extend a result of Madiman and Woo
[
27
]
on the entropy power inequality for discrete uniform distributions.

Let
U
be a uniform distribution on consecutive integers
\{a,\dots,b\}
, with
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
. Denote
l=b-a+1
. Note that
{\rm Var}(U)=(l^{2}-1)/12
, therefore its Fourier transform satisfies

\|\widehat{f_{U}}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+12{\rm Var}(U)}},

where the first identity follows from Parseval. Therefore, using
[
16
, Lemma 2.7]
, we obtain for
n
independent uniformly distributed random variables
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}

\|\widehat{f}_{\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+12\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(U_{k})}}.

Using the Parseval identity, this leads to

H_{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}\right)=\log(\|p_{\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}}\|_{2}^{-2})=\log(\|\widehat{f}_{\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}}\|_{2}^{-2})\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+12\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(U_{k})\right).

By monotonicity of entropy, we deduce that for all
\alpha\leq 2
,

H_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+12\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(U_{k})\right).

(30)

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
\alpha\leq 2
. Denoting
\Delta_{\alpha}(X)=N_{\alpha}(X)-1
, which reflects the variance better than the entropy power for discrete distributions, we deduce from (
30
) that

\Delta_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}\right)\geq 12\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(U_{k}).

However, we note that for a uniform random variable
U
on an integer interval,
\Delta_{\alpha}(U)=12\ {\rm Var}(U)
, for any
\alpha
. Thus we have

\Delta_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}\right)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta_{\alpha}(U_{k}),

(31)

for
\alpha\leq 2
. Moreover, for
X_{k}
uniform on any set of integers,
X_{k}^{\#}
has a uniform distribution on an integer interval and hence is log-concave, thus
X_{k}^{\#}\sim U_{k}
for
U_{k}
uniform on an integer interval, therefore

\Delta_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\right)\geq\Delta_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}^{\#}\right)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta_{\alpha}\left(X_{k}^{\#}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta_{\alpha}(X_{k}),

where the first inequality comes from Theorem
2.3
together with Schur concavity of Rényi entropy, and the second inequality from (
31
).
∎

Remark 5.2
.

1.

Let us note that (
31
) implies Theorem
1.2
with
c=12
and
\alpha\in[0,2]
when the random variables are uniformly distributed.

2.

Employing the relation

H_{\alpha}(X)\leq H_{\infty}(X)+\log(\alpha^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}})

obtained in
[
21
]
, which is valid for all log-concave distributions and
0<\alpha<\infty
, we deduce by taking
\alpha=2
that

N_{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}\right)\geq\frac{1}{4}N_{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}\right)\geq\frac{1}{4}+3\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(U_{k}),

where the second inequality comes from (
30
). Equivalently,

M\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}U_{k}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+3\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\rm Var}(U_{k})}},

which is an improvement of Theorem
1.1
whenever the random variables are uniformly distributed on at least 3 points.
