Revisiting Randomization in Greedy Model Search
Xin Chen a, Jason M. Klusowski a,1, Yan Shuo Tan b, Chang Yu a

Abstract
Combining randomized estimators in an ensemble, such as via random forests, has become a fundamental technique in modern data science, but can be computationally expensive. Furthermore, the mechanism by which this improves predictive performance is poorly understood. We address these issues in the context of sparse linear regression by proposing and analyzing an ensemble of greedy forward selection estimators that are randomized by feature subsampling—at each iteration, the best feature is selected from within a random subset. We design a novel implementation based on dynamic programming that greatly improves its computational efficiency. Furthermore, we show via careful numerical experiments that our method can outperform popular methods such as lasso and elastic net across a wide range of settings. Next, contrary to prevailing belief that randomized ensembling is analogous to shrinkage, we show via numerical experiments that it can simultaneously reduce training error and degrees of freedom, thereby shifting the entire bias-variance trade-off curve of the base estimator. We prove this fact rigorously in the setting of orthogonal features, in which case, the ensemble estimator rescales the ordinary least squares coefficients with a two-parameter family of logistic weights, thereby enlarging the model search space. These results enhance our understanding of random forests and suggest that implicit regularization in general may have more complicated effects than explicit regularization.

Introduction

Sparse linear regression is a well-studied problem in statistics, having important applications to machine learning, signal processing, and other fields.
Given a response vector
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n})^{\top}
and data matrix
\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1}&\mathbf{x}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{x}_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
, this problem is traditionally framed as best subset selection (BSS)
[
5
,
34

\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}
subject to
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\ell_{0}}\leq k
, where
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\ell_{0}}
measures the number of nonzero entries in
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
Since exactly solving the BSS problem is computationally infeasible, early approaches to sparse linear regression comprised greedy search (GS) algorithms, such as forward selection (FS)
[
21
]
, matching pursuit (MP)
[
61
]
, and orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP)
[
19
]
.
These algorithms iteratively select features
that maximize the immediate reduction in mean squared error
or a proxy thereof.
In recent years, however, greedy methods have been largely supplanted by the lasso
[
50
]
, elastic net
[
62
]
, and other convex relaxation techniques
[
25
,
33
]
.
This shift stems from the fact that, compared to greedy methods, convex relaxation approaches typically offer stronger theoretical guarantees under milder assumptions
[

and often demonstrate superior empirical performance
[

,

Breiman hypothesized in 1996
[

that greedy methods may suffer from instability
[
58
]
—small changes in
\mathbf{y}
or
\mathbf{X}
can cause drastic differences in selected features and resulting models—leading to high variance in cross-validation performance estimates and making hyperparameter tuning especially challenging.
To address this issue, he proposed stabilizing greedy methods through randomized ensembling, which involves averaging the predictions from multiple estimators, each obtained by applying a greedy method to a perturbed version of the dataset.
He investigated perturbation techniques such as bootstrap resampling (“bagging”) and adding noise to the response (“smearing”), and showed empirically, as well as to some extent theoretically, that they led to better stability and prediction performance
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
However, these randomized ensemble methods did not seem to outperform convex relaxation approaches and were further limited by their high computational cost.
They have hence not gained popularity in a sparse linear regression context.
On the other hand, when adapted to the setting of nonlinear estimators, such as via random forests (RFs),
these ensembling techniques have proved to be wildly successful
[
10
,
15
,
26
,
44
]
.
The exact reasons for their success remains somewhat elusive and an active topic of research
[

,

In this paper, we revisit randomized ensembling of greedy methods in sparse linear regression, with the dual objective of (i) developing a state-of-the-art method for solving sparse linear regression and (ii) explaining the general success of randomized ensembling.
We utilize a different type of data perturbation—at each iteration of the greedy method, we select a random subset of
m
features and select the best feature from within this subset.
We call this
feature subsampling
and note that a version of this scheme is used in RFs.
We show that, when used with FS, randomized ensembling via feature subsampling overcomes some of the computational limitations of bagging and smearing, while enjoying marked prediction performance improvements.
This success leads us to call this ensembling method
Randomized Greedy Search (RGS)
.
More precisely, we design a dynamic programming implementation of RGS whose computational complexity can, under some circumstances, scale sublinearly in the number of estimators in the ensemble.
Together with its synergy with modern parallel and distributed computing paradigms, this dynamic programming framework helps bridge the computational efficiency gap between randomized ensembling and convex relaxation methods.
Meanwhile, through extensive experiments on synthetic data, we show that the prediction performance of RGS is comparable with that of convex relaxation methods and even surpasses them under certain settings, such as low signal-to-noise ratio or banded correlated features.

We argue that the reasons for the predictive effectiveness of RGS go beyond Breiman’s earlier hypothesis of stabilization

.
Instead,
RGS, and randomized ensembling more broadly, serves to enlarge the model space being searched over, thereby shifting the entire bias-variance trade-off curve

Indeed, for a fixed number of greedy search iterations

We are able to make this argument even more precise through theoretical analysis.
Following a longstanding precedent in statistics, we focus on the case of orthogonal features, under which FS, MP, and OMP are all equivalent to BSS.

Varying

Meanwhile, our second main result identifies a sufficient concavity condition on the OLS coefficients for when RGS is guaranteed to have fewer degrees of freedom compared to BSS for the same value of

2
Randomized Greedy Search

In this section, we formally introduce our proposed method, RGS,
for solving sparse linear regression.
Note that such a method has generality beyond linear modeling, as one can make use of a dictionary of derived features,
which could consist of, for example, trigonometric, spline, wavelet, radial, or ridge functions.
Such features can either be defined using prior knowledge, or even learned from data.
For conceptual simplicity, however, we use the former framing for the rest of this paper. The inner product between two vectors
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as
\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle=(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{b})/n
, with norm
\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}=(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{a})/n
.
We denote the
j
-th feature vector
in bold as
\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}\coloneqq(x_{1j},x_{2j},\dots,x_{nj})^{\top}
and assume it is normalized so that
\|\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}\|=1
.
Functions, such as the
j
-th coordinate function
x_{j,p}
, will be denoted in regular font.

2.1
Forward Selection

We first describe FS, which is the default base learner used by RGS.
Starting with an empty active set
\mathcal{M}_{0}=\emptyset
and iterating over
l=1,2,\ldots,k
for some prespecified sparsity level
k
, it updates
\mathcal{M}_{l-1}
with the feature indexed by

\displaystyle j_{l}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\argmin_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{l}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{l-1}\cup\{j\}}\mathbf{y}\|

(1)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{l}}\frac{|\langle\mathbf{r}_{l-1},\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}\rangle|}{\|\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\mathcal{M}_{l}}\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}\|},

and sets the updated model predictions to be
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{l,p}\coloneqq\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{l}}\mathbf{y}
.
Here,
\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{l}}
(resp.
\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\mathcal{M}_{l}}
) denotes the projection matrix onto the (resp. orthogonal complement of the) column span of the feature vectors indexed in
\mathcal{M}_{l}
,
\mathbf{r}_{l-1}\coloneqq\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{l-1,p}
denotes the vector of residuals at the
(l-1)
-th step, and
\mathcal{V}_{l}=[p]\backslash\mathcal{M}_{l-1}
.
We use
\hat{f}_{k,p}

to denote the predictive function obtained after
k
steps, henceforth called the FS estimator.

FS is of course not the only greedy search algorithm.
Notable alternatives include OMP, which does not project out the contributions from
\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}
in (
1
) and instead just uses the absolute correlation of
\mathbf{r}_{l-1}
with
\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}
, and MP, which uses the same objective as OMP, but updates the model predictions via
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{l,p}=\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{l-1,p}+\langle\mathbf{r}_{l-1},\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}\rangle\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}
and allows the same feature to be selected multiple times.
When efficiently implemented, all three algorithms have similar computational complexity.
In particular, FS can be implemented by maintaining a QR factorization of the active submatrix
\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{M}_{l}}
, which can be updated with a rank-one modification upon adding a new selected variable.
At step
l
, the computational cost is
O(np)
for computing (
1
),
O(nl)
for updating the QR factorization, and
O(n)
for updating the residuals.
Summing over
k
steps gives a total cost of
O(npk)
. Since
k\leq\min\{n,p\}
, this cost is no more than that of a single OLS fit.

2.2
Randomized Greedy Search

Our proposed RGS estimator comprises an ensemble of randomized FS estimators.
2
2

2

We call our method RGS because the base learner can be easily replaced with other GS methods, and can be further generalized to GS methods for nonlinear models.

Each FS estimator is randomized at each step
l
by modifying (
1
) to instead select the maximizer over
j\in\mathcal{V}_{l}
, where
\mathcal{V}_{l}
, called the candidate set, is now a subset of size
m
drawn uniformly at random from
[p]\backslash\mathcal{M}_{l-1}
.
3
3

3

If
|[p]\backslash\mathcal{M}_{l-1}|<m
, we do not randomize.

Stopping at
k
steps as before, we call the resulting estimator
\tilde{f}_{k,p,b}
.
We repeat this model building process across
B
independent replicates, and aggregate the resulting estimators via simple averaging to get the RGS estimator
\hat{f}_{k,m,p,B}\coloneqq B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\tilde{f}_{k,p,b}
.
Recall that we call this randomization scheme feature subsampling, and note that it differs from bagging and smearing in that the randomization is interleaved with optimization via the modified (
1
), whereas bagging and smearing make use of a one-off dataset randomization prior to running FS.

2.3
Efficient RGS via Dynamic Programming

If computed naively, the computational cost of RGS scales linearly in
B
, the number of replicates.
Since it is standard to take
B
to be in the hundreds (or thousands) in order to minimize the contribution of algorithmic variance to prediction error, this could potentially make RGS prohibitively expensive.
Fortunately, we are able to design an efficient dynamic programming implementation of RGS that significantly reduces the computational overhead.

Our method leverages the observation that the same active set may appear across multiple replicates, which means that
much computation is needlessly repeated.
To avoid this waste, we run all replicates simultaneously, maintaining, at the start of each step
l
, an object
\mathcal{S}_{l-1}
, comprising all unique subsets of size
l-1
selected at the previous step, as well as the number of times each subset occurs.
To update
\mathcal{S}_{l-1}
, we process each entry
(\mathcal{M},B_{\mathcal{M}})
as follows:
We generate
B
candidate sets
\mathcal{V}^{(1)},\mathcal{V}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathcal{V}^{(B)}\subset[p]\backslash\mathcal{M}
uniformly at random.
To solve (
1
) for all candidate sets, we need only compute the correlations of the residual with features in
\cup_{b=1}^{B}\mathcal{V}^{(b)}
, and likewise for the feature projections,
just once in total.
Updating
\mathcal{M}
with these results provides
B
new, possibly repeated active sets
\mathcal{M}^{(1)},\mathcal{M}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathcal{M}^{(B)}
, which we then return as a list (see also Figure
1
).
After processing all entries in
\mathcal{S}_{l-1}
in this manner, we concatenate all the resulting lists and count frequencies to get
\mathcal{S}_{l}
.
After
k
steps, the RGS estimator can then be recovered as
\hat{f}_{k,m,p,B}=B^{-1}\sum_{(\mathcal{M},B_{\mathcal{M}})\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}B_{\mathcal{M}}\tilde{f}_{\mathcal{M}}
, where
\tilde{f}_{\mathcal{M}}
is the OLS estimator given the data matrix
\mathbf{X}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

For
l=1,2,\ldots,k
, let
\phi(l,m,B)
denote the expected cardinality of
\mathcal{S}_{l}
.
Arguing similarly to FS, the computational cost for handling each active set is
O(np)
.
The total cost for our RGS implementation is thus
O\big(np\sum_{l=1}^{k}\phi(l,m,B)\big)
, which can be much smaller than the naive
O(nmkB)
.
We may further reduce the computational cost by repeatedly resampling
\mathcal{S}_{l}
before processing it.
That is, we treat
\mathcal{S}_{l}
as a sample of size
B
from a population whose elements are subsets and repeatedly replace
\mathcal{S}_{l}
with a bootstrap resample of itself.
Similar to the regular bootstrap, this progressively reduces the number of distinct elements in
\mathcal{S}_{l}
, while preserving the property that
\mathcal{S}_{l}
is an unbiased estimate of the original.

Remark
.

It is easy to see that this efficient implementation is unique to RGS, and is unavailable to other ensemble methods, which rely on perturbing data, and tend not to produce repeated calculations across replicates.
It is also for this reason that we do not combine feature subsampling with bootstrapping, as practiced in RFs.
We also note that there are limits to the efficiency gain in edge case hyperparameter choices (i.e., very small
m
or large
k
).

Figure 1
:
Diagram showing how the dynamic programming implementation of RGS processes an active set
\mathcal{M}
. It first uses (
1
) to compute scores for all columns of
\mathbf{X}

not
in
\mathcal{M}
. Simultaneously, it generates
B
candidate subsets
\mathcal{V}^{(1)},\mathcal{V}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathcal{V}^{(B)}
of size
m
from the complement of
\mathcal{M}
.
For within each candidate set, it then computes the index with the maximum score.
\mathcal{M}
is then updated with each of these values, returning a collection
\mathcal{M}^{\prime(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{M}^{\prime(B)}
.

2.4
Prior Research on Feature Subsampling

The idea of combining models that are randomized via feature subsampling was first introduced by Breiman in the context of RFs
[
10
]
.
It has since been applied in various statistical problems, such as linear regression
[
36
]
, M-estimation
[
56
]
, feature importance uncertainty estimation
[
28
]
, clustering
[
27
]
, and graph learning
[
57
]
.
Most closely related to ours are two works that analyze feature subsampling applied to greedy algorithms, which we describe in more detail.

Mentch and Zhou
[
42
]
introduce a version of RGS that incorporates bagging.
However, their primary aim was to use it as a proxy to study RFs, and did not intend their method to be of practical use.
Notably, they did not mention the efficient dynamic programming strategy that we introduced, nor did they provide any theoretical analysis.
We will explain their arguments further in Section
6
when we discuss connections of our work to RFs.
Separately, Zhan, Zhang, and Xia
[
59
]
propose ensemble projection pursuit regression, which applies feature subsampling to projection pursuit, a variant of greedy search that is essentially OMP with ridge-function dictionaries, without using bagging.
While they obtain consistency rates for their method, they do not discuss the differences between the ensemble and non-ensemble versions.
In particular, they do not show how randomized ensembling can shift the bias-variance trade-off curve.

3
Provable Error Reduction

3.1
Analytical Framework

In this section, we provide a theoretical explanation of the role of randomization in RGS.
To do so, we assume a generative model
y_{i}=f(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
, where
f(\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
is a linear function,
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
are IID noise variables with zero mean and variance
\sigma^{2}
and that are independent of the data matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
.
4
4

4

Strictly speaking, we do not need to assume a well-specified linear model for our main results to hold, but we do so here for simplicity.

Our central claim is that randomization can simultaneously increase model fit and reduce model complexity, in contrast to earlier explanations.
In a fixed design setting, the complexity of an estimator
\hat{f}
is often quantified via its
degrees of freedom

[
31
]
:

\operatorname{df}(\hat{f})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(y_{i},\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_{i}))}{\sigma^{2}}.

(2)

Indeed, letting
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\hat{\mathbf{f}}=\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
denote the true and fitted function values respectively, the expected mean squared prediction error
of
\hat{f}
can be decomposed into its expected training error and a constant multiple of its degrees of freedom; that is,

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{f}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}\|^{2}\big]+\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}\|^{2}\big]+\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{n}\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}).

(3)

Therefore, we will analyze how RGS compares to FS on each term on the right hand side of (
3
).

To aid our analysis, we make some simplifying assumptions.
First, we study the performance of the method in the limit as
B\rightarrow\infty
.
By the strong law of large numbers, the estimator
\hat{f}_{k,m,p,B}
converges almost surely to its expected value, with the expectation taken over the randomness of the subset choice.
For the rest of our theoretical discussion, we will use and refer to this large-limit model as the RGS estimator, denoting it as
\hat{f}_{k,m,p}
.
In addition, we assume that the features are orthogonal:

Assumption 3.1

(Orthogonality)
.

The features are uncorrelated with respect to the sample, i.e.,
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}/n=\mathbf{I}
.

While unlikely to describe most real datasets, Assumption
3.1
allows us to derive exact asymptotic expressions for coefficient behavior and precisely characterize how the randomization from feature subsampling propagates through the FS algorithm. This, in turn, enables direct comparisons with the broader regularization landscape, including ridge, lasso
[
50
]
, elastic net
[
62
]
, LARS
[
23
]
, SCAD
[
25
]
, MCP
[
60
]
, and SureShrink
[
20
]
, all of which have been studied in the orthogonal setting.

Remark
.

Note that FS, MP, OMP, and BSS are equivalent to each other under Assumption
3.1
.
In particular, this means that the error reduction that we demonstrate in this section has nothing to do with overcoming the failures of greedy optimization and everything to do with the suboptimal representation obtained via this class of
\ell_{0}
-penalized methods.

3.2
Coefficients and Weights

Let
\hat{f}_{\operatorname{OLS}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\hat{\beta}_{j,p}x_{j,p}
denote the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator for
f
.
Under Assumption
3.1
, it is easy to see that its coefficients can be expressed as inner products
\hat{\beta}_{j,p}=\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}\rangle
.
It turns out that both the FS and RGS estimators can be written in terms of the OLS coefficients.
For ease of notation, we establish an ordering convention for the features
x_{1,p},x_{2,p},\ldots,x_{p,p}
in terms of these coefficients.

Definition 3.1

(Feature Ordering)
.

The inner products
\hat{\beta}_{j,p}\coloneqq\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}\rangle
are indexed and sorted by magnitude in descending order; that is,

|\hat{\beta}_{1,p}|\geq|\hat{\beta}_{2,p}|\geq\ldots\geq|\hat{\beta}_{p,p}|.

(4)

For the sake of simplicity, we assume henceforth that there are no ties in the feature rankings, i.e., the inequalities in (
4
) are strict.
Note also that the definition implies that the feature ordering is random as it depends on the response noise.

Using Definition
3.1
together with
the equivalence of FS and BSS, we can write the
k
-step FS estimator as

\hat{f}_{k,p}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{1}(j\leq k)\hat{\beta}_{j,p}x_{j,p},

(5)

which recounts the familiar fact that, in the orthogonal setting, FS (and BSS) will select the top-
k
features most correlated with the response.
The indicators
\mathbf{1}(j\leq k)
can be thought of as weights that modulate the OLS solution.
Similarly, the
k
-step RGS estimator has the form

\hat{f}_{k,m,p}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_{j}^{k,m,p}\hat{\beta}_{j,p}x_{j,p}.

(6)

The RGS weights
w_{1}^{k,m,p},w_{2}^{k,m,p},\ldots,w_{p}^{k,m,p}
form a non-increasing sequence that takes values between
0
and
1
and which sum to
k
.
Furthermore, they are determined solely by structural parameters (
j,k,m,p
) rather than the actual coefficient values
\hat{\beta}_{j,p}
.
In fact, as
m,p\rightarrow\infty
with a fixed scaling ratio, they are closely approximated by a logistic function with midpoint
k+1/2
and growth rate
-\log(1-m/p)
, viz.,

w_{j}^{k,m,p}\approx\frac{1}{1+\big(1-\frac{m}{p}\big)^{k-j+1/2}}.

(7)

This indicates that, in contrast to FS, RGS distributes its weights across a larger number of features through controlled randomization.

We state these facts more rigorously and discuss them further in Section
4
.
For now, we note that since FS is equivalent to RGS if we set
m=p
, varying
k
and
m
in (
7
) carves out a more flexible family of solutions compared to FS.
We will show that fixing
k
and optimizing over
m
has the potential to yield both a better fit (via training error) as well as better regularization (via degrees of freedom).

3.3
Degrees of Freedom Improvement

We first show that under a reasonable assumption, decreasing the feature subsampling parameter
m
(i.e. decreasing the subsampling ratio) has the effect of decreasing the degrees of freedom of the model.

Theorem 3.1
.

Grant Assumption
3.1
and suppose
k\mapsto\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,p})
forms a concave sequence.
5
5

5

A sequence
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{p}
is concave if its first difference sequence
c_{2}-c_{1},c_{3}-c_{2},\ldots,c_{p}-c_{p-1}
is decreasing.

Let
m<m^{\prime}
. Then, for all
k,p\geq 1
, we have

\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,m,p})\leq\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,m^{\prime},p}).

(8)

In particular,
k\leq\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,m,p})\leq\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,p})
for all
m
.

This result stems from the fact that under Assumption
3.1
, the RGS estimator
\hat{f}_{k,m,p}
can be written as a convex combination of the FS estimators for different values of
k
, while the degrees of freedom operator is linear in its input. The core technical tool is a coupling-based construction of the RGS process, in which estimators at different subsampling levels
m
are defined on a shared probability space using the same underlying randomness. This allows us to directly compare their induced weight vectors
w_{j}^{k,m,p}
and establish that they form a majorization hierarchy indexed by
m
.
6
6

6

Formally, for
m<m^{\prime}
,

\sum_{j=1}^{l}w_{j}^{k,m,p}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{l}w_{j}^{k,m^{\prime},p}\quad\text{for all }l=1,\dots,p,

(9)

with equality when
l=p
.

This linearity also allows us to argue that the concavity condition in Theorem
3.1
is likely to hold in low SNR scenarios, in particular, when
|\hat{\beta}_{k,p}|\gg|\beta_{k,p}|
.
Indeed, in such a scenario, we have

\begin{split}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\left(\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,p})-\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k-1,p})\right)&=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\operatorname{df}\big(\hat{\beta}_{k,p}x_{k,p}\big)\\
&=\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\beta}_{k,p}\big(\hat{\beta}_{k,p}-\beta_{k,p}\big)\big]\\
&\approx\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\beta}_{k,p}^{2}\big],\end{split}

(10)

which is a non-increasing sequence in
k
according to Definition
3.1
.
Empirical support for the condition, even in non-orthogonal settings, can be found in Figures 1 and 4 of
[

Because of the previous section, one can roughly interpret the act of varying

In this section, we further analyze the RGS weights

We first note that the weights are non-increasing.
That is, for all

Lemma 4.1

The weights satisfy the recurrence relation

\displaystyle w_{j}^{k,m,p}=\frac{\binom{p-j}{m-1}}{\binom{p}{m}}+\frac{\binom{p-j}{m}}{\binom{p}{m}}w_{j}^{k-1,m,p-1}+

(12)

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\binom{p-j+1}{m}}{\binom{p}{m}}\right)w_{j-1}^{k-1,m,p-1},

where
w_{j}^{0,m,p}=w_{j}^{k,m,0}=w_{0}^{k,m,p}=0
.

4.1
Logistic Approximation

The recurrence (

k
, and

However, in the scaling limit as
m\to\infty

p\to\infty

m/p\to\gamma

\gamma\in[0,1]

7
7

7

Note that it is standard practice to take

This yields the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1

(Asymptotic Weights for Fixed

The limiting weights
\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma}\coloneqq\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\to\infty,\,p\to\infty,\\
m/p\to\gamma\end{subarray}}w_{j}^{k,m,p}

(a)

exist and equal

\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{k-i}\prod_{l=i}^{k}\big(e^{-\alpha(j-l)}-e^{-\alpha j}\big),

(13)

(b)

\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha\left(h(\alpha,k)-j\right)}}\leq\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma}\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha\left(h(\alpha,k)+1-j\right)}},

(14)

4.2
Comparisons with Elastic Net

It is instructive to compare the behavior of RGS with that of elastic net regression.
Introduced in
[
62
]
, elastic net is a popular method that combines the
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
penalties of lasso and ridge regression, respectively.
Specifically, it solves the convex optimization problem

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\phantom{}^{\text{EN}}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\Big\{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^{2}+\lambda_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\ell_{1}}+\lambda_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\Big\},

(16)

where
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are non-negative tuning hyperparameters.
Under Assumption
3.1
, the elastic net estimator takes a particularly simple form.
As shown in
[
62
]
, the elastic net coefficient for
x_{j,p}
can be expressed as

\hat{\beta}\phantom{}^{\text{EN}}_{j,p}=\frac{(|\hat{\beta}_{j,p}|-n\lambda_{1}/2)_{+}}{1+n\lambda_{2}}\text{sgn}(\hat{\beta}_{j,p}),

(17)

where
\hat{\beta}_{j,p}
is the ordered OLS coefficient from Definition
3.1
.
For comparison, using the representation (
6
) and approximation (
7
), the RGS coefficient for
x_{j,p}
has the form:

\hat{\beta}\phantom{}^{\text{RGS}}_{j,p}\approx\frac{\hat{\beta}_{j,p}}{1+\big(1-\frac{m}{p}\big)^{k-j+1/2}}.

(18)

Elastic net was originally designed to relax lasso’s preference for highly sparse solutions, which was observed to create instability when features are highly correlated.
From (
17
), we see that it has the same effect even in an orthogonal setting.
Since all non-zero coefficients are shrunk by a constant factor of
1/(1+n\lambda_{2})
,
the parameter budget of the model can be spread out across more coefficients.
While this is somewhat similar in spirit to the role of the feature subsampling in RGS, the family of solutions attainable using both strategies is different, thereby yielding different predictive performances on any given dataset.

4.3
Revisiting Best Subset vs Lasso

In the sparse linear regression literature, Hastie, Tibshirani, and Tibshirani
[
32
]
compared BSS and lasso, showing via numerical experiments that in high-SNR settings, BSS (and FS) seem to have higher accuracy than lasso.
In contrast, in low-SNR regimes, they showed that lasso outperforms BSS.
10
10

10

See
[
29
]
for some recent theoretical work supporting this observation.

They explain this by observing that “different procedures bring us from the high-bias to the high-variance ends of the tradeoff along different model paths”, with the solution path of neither algorithm completely dominating the other.
Our weight calculations, together with our earlier theoretical results, adds to their discussion by showing that the BSS solution path is not Pareto optimal.
The RGS weights also offer an alternative solution path to those traversed by lasso and elastic net, thereby revealing how these may potentially have limitations.

5.1.1

We generate i.i.d. training data

[

\beta_{i}=1

We fix

, and vary

We compare RGS against GS (FS) as well as two other randomized ensembling methods for GS: (i) bagging

5.1.3
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluated the performance of each method according to two metrics: (i) the
relative in-sample error
(RISE), defined as
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}^{2}/\sigma^{2}+1
, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
is the sample covariance
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}/n
, and (ii) the
relative test error
(RTE), defined as
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{2}/\sigma^{2}+1
.
The RISE can be shown to be equivalent to the value
\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{\mathbf{f}}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}\|^{2}]/\sigma^{2}
, where
\hat{\mathbf{f}}
is the vector of fitted values, and
y_{i}^{\prime}=f(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}
, with
\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
drawn independently of the training data.
It hence measures the ratio of the in-sample error to the Bayes error.
Similarly, the RTE can be shown to be equivalent to
\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}[(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-y)^{2}]/\sigma^{2}
, where
(\mathbf{x},y)
is a fresh example drawn from the same distribution as the training set.
This is hence the ratio of the test error to the Bayes error.
We include both metrics because while the former is more relevant to fixed design linear regression, which is the framework used in our theoretical analysis, the latter is more relevant to modern machine learning.
The reported values for both metrics were computed by averaging over 10 experimental replicates.

6
Explaining the Role of Randomization in RFs

RFs
[
10
]
consist of ensembles of regression (or classification) trees, each of which recursively partitions the feature space, with each split (on a particular feature and at a particular threshold) selected by greedily optimizing a local performance criterion
[
11
]
.
Each tree is randomized by bootstrapping the training dataset (bagging) and by randomly constraining the set of features that can be split on at each iteration, that is, by feature subsampling.
Because of their resounding empirical success
[
15
,
26
,
44
]
, there has been a significant amount of work on understanding the statistical and mathematical properties of RFs, such as on their consistency, convergence rates, and asymptotic normality, under various data generating scenarios
[
49
,
41
,
6
,
35
,
16
,
2
]
.
On the other hand, the exact role played by randomization in the effectiveness of RFs remains unclear, with there being much disagreement and debate
[
10
,
31
,
55
,
42
,
18
,
37
,
40
,
48
]
.
In this section, we summarize these developments and discuss how our results provide empirical and theoretical evidence for or against various arguments.

Breiman
[
10
]
originally explained the benefits of randomization as increasing diversity among the trees, thereby reducing the variance in their predictions.
Interestingly, in his concluding remarks, he speculated that randomization may “act to reduce bias” but that “the mechanism for this is not obvious”.
These two explanations have had divergent fates, with Hastie, Tibshirani, and Friedman
[
31
]
arguing in their influential textbook that “the improvements in prediction obtained by bagging or random forests are solely a result of variance reduction”.

In 2020, Mentch and Zhou elaborated on this hypothesis.
They focused on the role played by feature subsampling and (i) found in their numerical experiments that decreasing the feature subsampling ratio
\gamma
decreases the degrees of freedom in RFs.
To further explore the generality of this phenomenon, they considered a version of RGS that also incorporates bagging, (ii) finding that the degrees of freedom in RGS also decreases with
\gamma
.
They hence (iii) concluded that feature subsamping “serves as a form of
implicit regularization” and that
\gamma
“serves much the same purpose as the shrinkage penalty in explicitly regularized regression procedures like lasso and ridge regression”.
Their claim inspired and was seemingly supported by a wave of theoretical work analyzing ensembles of (non-adaptive) OLS estimators randomized via feature subsampling, with the broad conclusion that the resulting estimator is mathematically equivalent to ridge regression
[
36
,
47
]
, in an asymptotic sense.

Our results suggest, contrary to this line of work, that the analogy between
\gamma
and
\lambda
, the shrinkage penalty in ridge or lasso regression, is more nuanced in the context of greedy, adaptive methods like RGS (and RFs).
Note that in both ridge and lasso regression, the expected training error (resp. degrees of freedom) is monotonically increasing (resp. monotonically decreasing) in
\lambda

[
63
,
31
]
, thereby giving an exact trade-off between the two quantities.
However, this is not what happens with
\gamma
in RGS.
Our Theorem
3.2
shows that the training error for RGS is not monotonic in
\gamma
and instead is minimized at an intermediate value.
Meanwhile, although Theorem
3.2
in our paper gives a sufficient condition for which the degrees of freedom is monotonically decreasing in
\gamma
, which generally holds in lower SNR regimes and seems to corroborate observation (ii) above, it is easy to construct an example where this monotonicity fails in higher SNR regimes:

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose that the regression vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is exactly
s
-sparse (all nonzero coefficients are equal to 1) and that the noise variables are Gaussian.
Then we have

\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,\,p,\,n\to\infty,\\
m/p\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\frac{\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,m,p})-\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,p})}{\log p}\geq C,

(20)

where
C
is a constant depending only on
\gamma
and
k
.

The breakdown in the analogy between
\gamma
and
\lambda
can also be seen in the fitted coefficients.
While the ridge or lasso coefficients shrink toward zero as
\lambda
increases, the weight formulas (Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
) for RGS show that varying
\gamma
simultaneously shrinks some coefficients while inflating others.
In summary, the role of feature subsampling in RGS is altogether more complex than providing shrinkage.
As mentioned in previous sections, the flexibility of being able to vary
\gamma
has the effect of enlarging the model search space, which can lead to simultaneously better model fit and lower model complexity.

While our theory applies only to RGS, it coheres with the claims of a number of very recent works that randomization in RFs can reduce “bias”
[
18
,
37
,
40
,
48
]
.
While each of these works uses a different definition of bias, all definitions become equivalent to the classical definition if, similar to our analysis of RGS, we consider RFs in the limit of infinite trees, which exists by the law of large numbers.
In this case, the bias of the RF estimator
\hat{f}
is defined as
\mathbb{E}\big[\big(f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\big]\big)^{2}\big]
, where the inner expectation is taken with respect to the training set and the outer expectation is taken with respect to a new data point
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.

In these works, we identify two mechanisms for bias reduction.
The first mechanism is by
enhancing the approximation power of RFs
.
As observed Curth et al.
[
18
]
, the class of decision tree models that can be fitted on a fixed dataset is not convex, so by taking simple averages of these decision trees, we enlarge the class of possible models.
12
12

12

To explain further, any decision tree model fitted on a fixed dataset is constrained to have leaf values that are equal to the average response in the training set over that leaf. In particular, they cannot have empty leaves. It is easy to see that this constraint makes this class of functions non-convex.

Via numerical experiments, they showed that when varying
\gamma
while fixing all other hyperparameters, minimum bias can be achieved at intermediate value of
\gamma
.
Mei et al.
[
40
]
gave a theoretical argument to support this statement when the true regression function is linear.
13
13

13

Their proof, however, relies on an oracle tree-growing process.

Note that this argument is entirely analogous to our explanation for how randomization in RGS improves model fit (c.f., Remark
Remark
.)
A second mechanism is by
overcoming potential failures of greedy optimization
, which is used by the base learners of RFs.
Specifically, Liu and Mazumder
[
37
]
showed numerically how certain “hidden patterns” that would otherwise be missed can be detected upon injecting randomness into the tree-growing process, leading to smaller bias.
While this mechanism has no analogy in our theoretical analysis of RGS (again c.f., the remark in Section 3.A.), it is easy to construct an example showing how it may arise in RGS as well (c.f., Example
A.1
in the supplement).

Taken together, these results broaden our perspective on the role of randomization in the success of RFs.
Whereas Mentch and Zhou
[
42
]
concluded that feature subsampling is only useful in low SNR regimes, these recent results show that it can also be useful even in the absence of any noise
[
18
]
.
Furthermore, since feature subsampling is popularly viewed as a form of implicit regularization, these results urge us to pay more attention to nuance in our understanding of this phenomenon, which has become of great interest to the machine learning community
[
53
,
43
,
3
,
4
]
.
Specifically, our mental models of how regularization works in ridge and lasso regression may not map neatly onto the effects of algorithmic regularization, be it from feature subsampling, dropout, or gradient descent.

7
Discussion and Future Work

The major theoretical limitations of our work are our assumptions that the features are orthogonal.
This gap is somewhat bridged by our empirical results, which show that RGS continues to provide performance gains even in correlated settings, such as under banded and block covariance structures.
Nonetheless, a promising direction for future work is to explain these results theoretically. For example, one could study how small deviations from orthogonality (e.g., through structured perturbations of
\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}/n
) affect the RGS weights and associated bias-variance tradeoffs.
Second, while we make arguments about random forests, we are only able to do so by analogy, having analyzed feature subsampling in RGS.
A direct theoretical analysis of how feature subsampling improves RFs is therefore of significant interest.
Partial results were obtained by Mei et al.
[
40
]
, but they require certain oracle assumptions, which make their analysis deviate from how RFs are used in practice.

In addition, RGS faces certain practical limitations.
While RGS significantly reduces computational complexity via our dynamic programming implementation, its efficiency gains diminish as
k
grows large relative to
p
. Investigating further computational optimizations—such as parallelized implementations or approximate search heuristics—would be beneficial for scaling to ultra-high-dimensional datasets.

By addressing these challenges, RGS has the potential to become a broadly useful tool for sparse modeling, particularly in high-stakes domains that demand both accuracy and interpretability. Promising application areas include high-dimensional causal inference and deep learning pipelines as a feature selection module.

Proofs for Finite RGS Weights

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Let
J
be denote the index of the feature selected at the first step. To derive the recurrence for
w_{j}^{k,m,p}
, we condition on the first selected element
x_{i,p}
, i.e., condition on the event
J=i
.

1.

If
i=j
, which occurs with probability
\mathbb{P}(J=j)
, the element
x_{j,p}
is selected in the first step. As a result,
x_{j,p}
will always be included in
\mathcal{M}_{k}
.

2.

If
i\neq j
, the remaining
k-1
steps are equivalent to running RGS on
p-1
features. The coefficients are reassigned as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{l,p-1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\hat{\beta}_{l,p}\qquad\text{for }l<i

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{l,p-1}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\hat{\beta}_{l+1,p}\quad\text{for }l\geq i,

where
l=1,2,\dots,p-1
.
We then consider two subcases:

(a)

If
i>j
, which occurs with probability
\mathbb{P}(J>j)
, the index
j
remains unchanged in the relative ranking. The weight contribution becomes
w_{j}^{k-1,m,p-1}
.

(b)

If
i<j
, which occurs with probability
\mathbb{P}(J<j)
, the index
j
is shifted to the
(j-1)
-th position in the new ordering. The weight contribution becomes
w_{j-1}^{k-1,m,p-1}
.

At the first step of RGS, we select the feature with the largest absolute inner product
|\hat{\beta}_{j,p}|=|\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}\rangle|
from an
m
-sized subset of
p
features. Because there are no ties in the feature rankings, this is equivalent to selecting the minimum index
j
in this subset. The probability of
x_{j,p}
being selected is thus the probability that
j
is the minimum index in a random
m
-sized subset of
p
elements, which is
\binom{p-j}{m-1}/\binom{p}{m}
. Therefore, by the law of total probability,

\displaystyle w_{j}^{k,m,p}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(J=j)+w_{j}^{k-1,m,p-1}\mathbb{P}(J>j)+w_{j-1}^{k-1,m,p-1}\mathbb{P}(J<j)

(21)

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{p-j}{m-1}}{\binom{p}{m}}+w_{j}^{k-1,m,p-1}\sum^{p}_{i=j+1}\frac{\binom{p-i}{m-1}}{\binom{p}{m}}+w_{j-1}^{k-1,m,p-1}\sum^{j-1}_{i=1}\frac{\binom{p-i}{m-1}}{\binom{p}{m}}.

Now, by the hockey-stick identity from combinatorics, we can simplify the coefficients from (
21
):

\displaystyle\sum^{p}_{i=j+1}\binom{p-i}{m-1}

\displaystyle=\binom{p-j}{m},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\sum^{j-1}_{i=1}\binom{p-i}{m-1}

\displaystyle=\binom{p}{m}-\binom{p-j+1}{m}.

Plugging in these two expressions into (
21
) gives us recurrence (
12
).

To prove monotonicty of the weights with respect to
j
, recall that
w_{j}^{k,m,p}=\mathbb{P}(x_{j,p}\in\mathcal{M}_{k})
.
Here, the randomness is over the choices of the sets,
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{V}}}\coloneqq\{\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{V}_{2},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{k}\}
.
Define the events

\mathcal{E}_{1}\coloneqq\{x_{j,p}\in\mathcal{M}_{k},\;x_{j-1,p}\notin\mathcal{M}_{k}\},

and

\mathcal{E}_{2}\coloneqq\{x_{j-1,p}\in\mathcal{M}_{k},\;x_{j,p}\notin\mathcal{M}_{k}\}.

It suffices to show that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1})\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
.
To show this, we will define a measure-preserving transformation
T\colon\mathcal{E}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}_{2}
with
T(\mathcal{E}_{1})\subset\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
Assuming such a transformation exists, we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{2})\geq\mathbb{P}(T(\mathcal{E}_{1}))=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1}).

To define
T
, consider
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
.
For this random seed, there is some index
1\leq i\leq k
such that
x_{j,p}
is selected at the
i
-th iteration.
This implies that we have
x_{j-1,p}\notin\mathcal{V}_{i}
, otherwise,
x_{j-1,p}
would have been selected in the
i
-th step instead.
Furthermore, we have
x_{j,p}\notin\mathcal{V}_{l}
for
l>i
.
Define
T(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}})\coloneqq\{\mathcal{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{i-1},\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\prime},\mathcal{V}_{i+1}^{\prime},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{k}^{\prime}\}
, where
\mathcal{V}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathcal{V}_{i}\cup\{x_{j-1,p}\}\backslash\{x_{j,p}\}
, and for
l>i
,
\mathcal{V}_{l}^{\prime}=\mathcal{V}_{l}
if
x_{j-1,p}\notin\mathcal{V}_{l}
otherwise
\mathcal{V}_{l}^{\prime}=\mathcal{V}_{i}\cup\{x_{j,p}\}\backslash\{x_{j-1,p}\}
.
Denote
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}\coloneqq T(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}})
for convenience. Let
x_{j_{l},p}
and
x_{j^{\prime}_{l},p}
be the features selected at the
l
-th step with the set of subdictionaries
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
, respectively. Note that
x_{j_{l},p}=x_{j^{\prime}_{l},p}
for
l=1,\ldots,i-1
, since the algorithm only makes use of
\mathcal{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{i-1}
to select these terms. Similarly,
x_{j_{l},p}=x_{j^{\prime}_{l},p}
for
l=i+1,\ldots,k
, because the combinatorics depend solely on the ordinal structure of the coefficients
|\hat{\beta}_{l,p}|
. Thus,
x_{j-1,p}
in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
plays the same role as
x_{j,p}
in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}
.
For the same reason,
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}})=\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime})
.
We also see that
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
, which proves that
T
is well-defined and is measure-preserving.
∎

From the the recurrence in Lemma

\big\{\tilde{w}_{k-d}^{k,\gamma}\big\}^{\infty}_{k=d+1}

\big\{\tilde{w}_{k-d}^{k,\gamma}\big\}^{\infty}_{k=d+1}
exists. Hence,
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}
exists and we must now identify its limit.

We will prove the closed form (
26
) by showing that for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists some
K
such that for all
k>K
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\tilde{w}_{k-d}^{k,\gamma}-\sum^{k}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}\bigg|<\epsilon.

First, fix
\epsilon
and suppose
k
is even. We begin by using the triangle inequality, substituting in the closed form for
\tilde{w}_{k-d}^{k,\gamma}
we obtained in Lemma
A.3
:

\displaystyle\bigg|\tilde{w}_{k-d}^{k,\gamma}-\sum^{k}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum^{k}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}\prod^{k}_{l=k-i+1}\big(1-e^{-l\alpha}\big)-\sum^{k}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{k}_{i=k/2+1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}\left(1-\prod^{k}_{l=k-i+1}\big(1-e^{-l\alpha}\big)\right)+\sum^{k/2}_{i=1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}\left(1-\prod^{k}_{l=k-i+1}\big(1-e^{-l\alpha}\big)\right).

(27)

Suppose that
k>2d
. We bound the first sum of (
27
). By basic inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\sum^{k}_{i=k/2+1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}\left(1-\prod^{k}_{l=k-i+1}\big(1-e^{-l\alpha}\big)\right)\leq\sum^{k}_{i=k/2+1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}\leq\frac{k}{2}\cdot e^{\alpha(k/2)(d-k/4+1/2)}.

When
k\to\infty
, this final expression will go
0
, so there exists some
K_{1}
such that for all
k>K_{1}
, the final expression is less than
\epsilon/2
.

We perform a similar computation to bound the second sum in expression (
27
). Note that

\displaystyle\prod^{k}_{l=k-i+1}\big(1-e^{-l\alpha}\big)\geq\big(1-e^{-(k-i+1)\alpha}\big)^{i}\geq\big(1-e^{-(k/2+1)\alpha}\big)^{k/2}.

Thus,

1-\prod^{k}_{l=k-i+1}\big(1-e^{-l\alpha}\big)\leq 1-(1-e^{-(k/2+1)\alpha})^{k/2}\leq\frac{k}{2}\cdot e^{-(k/2+1)\alpha}.

(28)

Substituting expression (
28
) back into the second sum of expression (
27
), we have that

\displaystyle\sum^{k/2}_{i=1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}\left(1-\prod^{k}_{l=k-i+1}\big(1-e^{-l\alpha}\big)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k}{2}\sum^{k/2}_{i=1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}e^{-(k/2+1)\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k}{2}\cdot\frac{k}{2}\cdot e^{\alpha d(d-d/2+1/2)}e^{-(k/2+1)\alpha}.

Note that as
k\to\infty
, this final expression goes to
0
, so there exists some
K_{2}
such that for all
k>K_{2}
, this expression is less than
\epsilon/2
.

Therefore, for all
k>\max(K_{1},K_{2})
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\tilde{w}_{k-d}^{k,\gamma}-\sum^{k}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k}{2}\cdot e^{\alpha(k/2)(d-k/4+1/2)}+\frac{k^{2}}{4}\cdot e^{\alpha(d(d-d/2+1/2)-(k/2+1))}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon/2+\epsilon/2=\epsilon.

Thus, we have shown that
\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma}
has a limit, and the limit is

\displaystyle\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\to\infty,\,j\to\infty,\\
k-j=d\end{subarray}}\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}.\qed

Now, we show that this form we just proved is the same as (
15
) to finish the proof of the property (a). Using the difference of squares formula,

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{(\alpha/2)[(d+1/2)^{2}-(i-(d+1/2))^{2}]}

\displaystyle=e^{(\alpha/2)(d+1/2)^{2}}\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{-(\alpha/2)(i-(d+1/2))^{2}}.

However, we can cancel some terms in the following way. For
d\geq 0
,

\sum^{2d}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{-(\alpha/2)(i-(d+1/2))^{2}}=\sum^{d}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}\big(e^{-(\alpha/2)(i-(d+1/2))^{2}}-e^{-(\alpha/2)((d+1/2)-i)^{2}}\big)=0.

(29)

Similarly, for
d<0
,

\sum^{2d+1}_{i=0}(-1)^{i+1}e^{-(\alpha/2)(i-(d+1/2))^{2}}=0.

(30)

Therefore, using the cancellation of (
29
) and (
30
),

\displaystyle e^{(\alpha/2)(d+1/2)^{2}}\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{-(\alpha/2)(i-(d+1/2))^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=e^{(\alpha/2)(d+1/2)^{2}}\sum^{\infty}_{i=2d+1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{-(\alpha/2)(i-(d+1/2))^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=e^{(\alpha/2)(d+1/2)^{2}}\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}(-1)^{i+(2d+1)+1}e^{-(\alpha/2)(i+(2d+1)-(d+1/2))^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}(-1)^{i}e^{-\alpha i(d+i/2+1/2)}.

To prove property (b), using the closed form from (
15
), note that

\displaystyle 1-\check{w}_{-(d+1)}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=1-\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}(-1)^{i+1}e^{\alpha i(-(d+1)-i/2+1/2)}

\displaystyle=1+\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}(-1)^{i}e^{-\alpha i(d+i/2+1/2)}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}(-1)^{i}e^{-\alpha i(d+i/2+1/2)}

\displaystyle=\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}.

Therefore,
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}
satisfies symmetry property
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}=1-\check{w}_{-(d+1)}^{\gamma}
.

To prove property (c), by basic asymptotic results, we write the bounds from (
14
) as

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\to\infty,\,j\to\infty,\\
k-j=d\end{subarray}}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha k}}{1-e^{-\alpha k}+e^{-\alpha(k-j)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha d}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\to\infty,\,j\to\infty,\\
k-j=d\end{subarray}}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha k}}{1-e^{-\alpha k}+e^{-\alpha(k-j+1)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha(d+1)}}.

Therefore,
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}
satisfies

\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha d}}\leq\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha(d+1)}}.

(31)

Additionally, using the bounds from (
31
), we have

\displaystyle\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha(d+1)}}\leq\check{w}_{d+1}^{\gamma},

(32)

demonstrating that
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}
is non-decreasing for
d\in\mathbb{Z}
. The inequality
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}\geq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha(d+1/2)}}
for
d\geq 0
follows directly from (
38
). Furthermore, we obtain
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha(d+1/2)}}
for
d<0
by using the relation
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}=1-\check{w}_{-(d+1)}^{\gamma}
.

To prove property (d), note that property (i) in the footnote follows immediately from (
32
).
Next, we prove properties (ii) and (iii) in the footnote. To this end, using the two different series representations (
15
) and (
26
) of
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}
for integer
d
, we extend the definition of
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}
to all real
d
as follows. When
d<-1/2
, let
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{i+1}e^{\alpha i(d-i/2+1/2)}
and when
d\geq-1/2
, let
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}e^{-\alpha i(d+i/2+1/2)}
. For
d>-1/2
, note that
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}
has second derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial d^{2}}\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}=\alpha^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}i^{2}e^{-\alpha i(d+i/2+1/2)}=\alpha^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}z^{2i}i^{2}q^{i^{2}},

(33)

and
z,q\in(0,1)
,
where
q=e^{-\alpha/2}
and
z=e^{-(\alpha/2)(d+1/2)}
.
Now, consider the following identity from
[
17
, Proposition 1.1]
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}z^{2i}q^{i^{2}}=\frac{1}{1+z}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z^{i}\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{1-zq^{j}}{1+zq^{j+1}},\quad|z|<1,\;|q|\leq 1.

(34)

Differentiating both sides of (
34
) with respect to
q
gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}z^{2i}i^{2}q^{i^{2}-1}=\frac{1}{1+z}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}z^{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{1-zq^{j}}{1+zq^{j+1}}.

(35)

By the product rule and quotient rule on (
35
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{1-zq^{j}}{1+zq^{j+1}}=\sum_{l=0}^{i-1}\frac{q^{l-1}z(q^{l+1}z-l(q+1)-q)}{(1+zq^{l+1})^{2}}\prod_{0\leq j\leq i-1,\,j\neq l}\frac{1-zq^{j}}{1+zq^{j+1}}.

(36)

Since
q^{l+1}z-l(q+1)-q\leq 0
for all
l\geq 0
and
(1-zq^{j})/(1+zq^{j+1})\geq 0
for all
j\geq 0
when
z,q\in[0,1)
, it follows that (
36
) is non-positive, implying the same for (
35
). Returning to (
33
), we deduce that
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}
is concave for all
d>-1/2
. An analogous argument shows that
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}
is convex for
d<-1/2
. This proves properties (ii) and (iii) in the footnote when
d\neq-1,0
. For the
d=-1,0
cases, we have from the just established convexity/concavity and continuity that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\check{w}_{-2}^{\gamma}+\frac{2}{3}\lim_{d\uparrow-1/2}\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\geq\check{w}_{-1}^{\gamma},

(37)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}\check{w}_{-1/2}^{\gamma}+\frac{1}{3}\check{w}_{1}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\check{w}_{0}^{\gamma},

where
\lim_{d\uparrow-1/2}\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}=1-\check{w}_{-1/2}^{\gamma}
.
We will show that these equations (
37
) together imply properties (ii) and (iii) in the footnote when
d=-1,0
. Expanding the power series (
34
), ones obtains

\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}=\frac{1}{1+z}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z^{i}\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{1-zq^{j}}{1+zq^{j+1}}\geq\frac{1}{1+z}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}z^{i}\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{1-z}{1+z}=\frac{1}{1+z^{2}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha(d+1/2)}}.

(38)

Thus, we have
\check{w}_{-1/2}^{\gamma}\geq 1/2
and hence
\check{w}_{-1/2}^{\gamma}\geq\lim_{d\uparrow-1/2}\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}
. Thus, from (
37
), we obtain

3\check{w}_{-1}^{\gamma}+\check{w}_{1}^{\gamma}\leq 3\check{w}_{0}^{\gamma}+\check{w}_{-2}^{\gamma}.

(39)

Finally, the identity
\check{w}_{d}^{\gamma}=1-\check{w}_{-(d+1)}^{\gamma}
for
d\in\mathbb{Z}
from part (c) of Theorem
4.2
applied to (
39
) yields properties (ii) and (iii) in the footnote when
d=-1,0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

A.3
.

Proof of Theorem

.

From the decompositions (
6
) and (

), we have the following identity

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k,p}\|^{2}-\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k,m,p}\|^{2}=\sum_{j=k+1}^{p}\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}\big(1-\big(-w_{j}^{k,m,p}\big)^{2}\big)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}\big(1-w_{j}^{k,m,p}\big)^{2}.

(40)

Let

, where

. Then, using (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k,p}\|^{2}-\min_{m}\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k,m,p}\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]\big(1-\big(-w_{j}^{k,m_{p},p}\big)^{2}\big)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]\big(1-w_{j}^{k,m_{p},p}\big)^{2}.

(41)

To simplify further, we use Chebyshev’s sum inequality

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]\big(1-w_{j}^{k,m_{p},p}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]\Bigg)\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-w_{j}^{k,m_{p},p}\big)^{2}\Bigg),

(42)

and the first term in (

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]\big(1-\big(1-w_{j}^{k,m_{p},p}\big)^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\geq\Bigg(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]\Bigg)\Bigg(\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\big(1-\big(1-w_{j}^{k,m_{p},p}\big)^{2}\big)\Bigg).

(43)

We claim that for a fixed
k
and a sequence
\{\gamma_{p}\}
that depends on both
p
and
k
, with
\gamma_{p}\geq C>0
where
C
is some constant depending on
k
, if we set
m_{p}=\lfloor\gamma_{p}p\rfloor
, then there exists a constant
C(k,\epsilon)
such that when
p(1-\gamma_{p})^{3}\geq C(k,\epsilon)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\big(1-\big(-w_{j}^{k,m_{p},p}\big)^{2}\big)\geq(1-\epsilon)\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\big(1-\big(-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\big),

(44)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-w_{j}^{k,m_{p},p}\big)^{2}\leq(1+\epsilon)\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}.

(45)

For simplicity of notation, throughout the proof of this theorem, we use the notation “
A\lesssim B
” to mean that there exists a constant
C
depending on
k
such that
A\leq CB
. To prove the claim, we first show that when
p(1-\gamma_{p})^{2}
is sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\frac{\Big|\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\big(1-\big(-w_{j}^{k,m_{p},p}\big)^{2}\big)-\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\big(1-\big(-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\big)\Big|}{\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\big(1-\big(-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\big)}\lesssim\frac{1}{p(1-\gamma_{p})^{2}}.

(46)

If (
46
) holds, then it is clear that the claim for (
44
) holds.

To prove (
46
), we revisit the proof of Lemma
A.1
. Note that

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{p-j}{m_{p}-1}}{\binom{p}{m_{p}}}=\frac{m_{p}}{p-j-m_{p}+1}\prod^{j}_{i=1}\frac{i+p-m_{p}}{i+p}=\frac{1+p-m_{p}}{p-j-m_{p}+1}\frac{m_{p}}{1+p}\prod^{j}_{i=2}\frac{i+p-m_{p}}{i+p}.

For a fixed
2\leq j\leq 2k
, we have that for any
2\leq i\leq j
,
\big|\frac{i+p-m_{p}}{i+p}-(1-\gamma_{p})\big|\lesssim\frac{1}{p+i}
. Additionally,
|\frac{m_{p}}{1+p}-\gamma_{p}|\lesssim\frac{1}{p+1}
. Thus, we know when
p
is sufficiently large,
\big|\frac{m}{1+p}\prod^{j}_{i=2}\frac{i+p-m}{i+p}-\gamma(1-\gamma)^{j-1}\big|\lesssim\frac{1}{p+1}
. Next, we observe that when
p(1-\gamma_{p})
is sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\frac{1+p-m_{p}}{p-j-m_{p}+1}-1=\frac{j}{p-j-m_{p}+1}\lesssim\frac{1}{p(1-\gamma_{p})}.

(47)

Therefore, when

is sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\binom{p-j}{m_{p}-1}}{\binom{p}{m_{p}}}-\gamma_{p}(1-\gamma_{p})^{j-1}\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{p(1-\gamma_{p})}.

Additionally, using a similar analysis, we know that when

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\binom{p-j}{m_{p}}}{\binom{p}{m_{p}}}-\big(1-\gamma_{p})^{j}\bigg|=\bigg|\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\frac{p-m_{p}-i}{p-i}-\big(1-\gamma_{p})^{j}\bigg|\lesssim\frac{1}{p},

According to the recurrence (

). Therefore, for any
1\leq j\leq 2k
, when

\displaystyle\Bigg|\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\big(1-\big(-w_{j}^{k,m_{p},p}\big)^{2}\big)-\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\big(1-\big(-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\big)\Bigg|\lesssim\frac{1}{p(1-\gamma_{p})}.

(49)

To lower bound

49

\displaystyle 1-\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}=\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big(2-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big),

(50)

so we wish to have simple upper and lower bounds on
\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}
for

to lower bound each

. Recall that

. Since

, we have the following lower bound
\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}
from (

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\geq\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k}}{1-e^{-\alpha k}+e^{-\alpha_{p}(k-j)}}\geq e^{-\alpha_{p}(j-k)}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k}}{1+e^{-\alpha_{p}}},\quad j\geq k+1.

(51)

Now, from (

\displaystyle 1-(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}=\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}(2-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}})\geq\frac{3}{2}e^{-\alpha_{p}(j-k)}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k}}{1+e^{-\alpha_{p}}},\quad j\geq k+1.

Substituting in (

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\big(1-\big(-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\big)\geq\frac{3}{2}e^{-\alpha_{p}}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k}}{1-e^{-\alpha_{p}}}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k}}{1+e^{-\alpha_{p}}}=\frac{3}{2}\frac{e^{-\alpha_{p}}(1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k})^{2}}{1-e^{-2\alpha_{p}}}.

Noting that

To prove (

\displaystyle\frac{\Big|\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-w_{j}^{k,m_{p},p}\big)^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\Big|}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}}\lesssim\frac{1}{p(1-\gamma_{p})^{3}},

Note that from (

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\leq\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k}}{1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k}+e^{-\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}}\leq\frac{1}{1+e^{-\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{e^{-2\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}}{\big(1+e^{-\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}\big)^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{-2\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}=\frac{e^{-2\alpha_{p}}(1-e^{-2\alpha_{p}k})}{4(1-e^{-2\alpha_{p}})}.

Noting that
\gamma_{p}\gtrsim
implies
\alpha_{p}\gtrsim 1
, we obtain (
55
).

Therefore, the claim is proven for (
44
) and (
45
).
To upper bound the sum
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}
from (
45
), we use
the following rearranged version of recurrence (
24
):

\displaystyle 1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}=e^{-\alpha_{p}k}(1-\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}})+e^{-\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}.

(56)

Upon squaring both sides of (
56
), we obtain

\displaystyle\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=e^{-2\alpha_{p}k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}+

\displaystyle\qquad 2e^{-\alpha_{p}(2k-j+1)}\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}(1-\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}})+e^{-2\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}\big(\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}.

(57)

Summing (
A.3
) over
1\leq j\leq k
, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the fact from Theorem
4.1
that
\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\geq\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2\alpha_{p}k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2e^{-\alpha_{p}k}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{-2\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}\big(\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{-2\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}\big(\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2\alpha_{P}k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2e^{-\alpha_{p}k}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{-2\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}\big(\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}}

(58)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{-2\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}\big(\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}.

Solving the quadratic inequality (

) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\leq\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{-2\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}\big(\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}}{(1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k})^{2}}.

(59)

Now, from the upper bound (

), we have

\big(\tilde{w}_{j-1}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\leq(1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k})^{2}
for all

. Substituting this into (

), we obtain the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(1-\tilde{w}_{j}^{k,\gamma_{p}}\big)^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{-2\alpha_{p}(k-j+1)}=\frac{e^{-2\alpha_{p}}(1-e^{-2\alpha_{p}k})}{1-e^{-2\alpha_{p}}}.

(60)

To simplify notation, set

\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}],\qquad B=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}],

(61)

and note that
A\geq B
by applying Definition
3.1
.
Using (
61
) and by the previous inequalities in (
60
) and (
54
), the training error gap (
41
) is at least

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}(1-\epsilon)B\frac{e^{-\alpha_{p}}(1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k})^{2}}{1-e^{-2\alpha_{p}}}-A(1+\epsilon)\frac{e^{-2\alpha_{p}}(1-e^{-2\alpha_{p}k})}{1-e^{-2\alpha_{p}}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\Big(\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k}}{1-e^{-\alpha_{p}}}\Big)\Big[\frac{3}{2}(1-\epsilon)B\frac{e^{-\alpha_{p}}(1-e^{-\alpha_{p}k})}{1+e^{-\alpha_{p}}}-(1+\epsilon)A\frac{e^{-2\alpha_{p}}(1+e^{-\alpha_{p}k})}{1+e^{-\alpha_{p}}}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\frac{3}{2}(1-\epsilon)Be^{-\alpha_{p}}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{p}}}{1+e^{-\alpha_{p}}}-(1+\epsilon)Ae^{-2\alpha_{p}}

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\frac{3}{2}(1-\epsilon)Be^{-\alpha_{p}}\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{0}}}{1+e^{-\alpha_{0}}}-(1+\epsilon)Ae^{-2\alpha_{p}},

(62)

for any
\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha_{p}
. Take
\alpha_{p}^{\star}
such that

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}^{\star}=\log\Big(\frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac{1+e^{-\alpha_{0}^{\star}}}{1-e^{-\alpha_{0}^{\star}}}\cdot\frac{A}{B}\Big),

(63)

where the corresponding

\gamma_{p}^{\star}=1-e^{-\alpha_{p}^{\star}}
, and

. It is straightforward to verify that

\alpha_{p}^{\star}=\log\Big(\frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac{1+e^{-\alpha_{0}^{\star}}}{1-e^{-\alpha_{0}^{\star}}}\cdot\frac{A}{B}\Big)\geq\log\Big(\frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac{1+e^{-\alpha_{0}^{\star}}}{1-e^{-\alpha_{0}^{\star}}}\Big)=\alpha_{0}^{\star}.

(64)

Thus, substituting
\alpha_{p}^{\star}
and
\alpha_{0}^{\star}
into (

gives us the lower bound on expected training error gap (

) as

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{3}{4}\Big)\Big(\frac{3}{4}-\frac{9}{4}\epsilon\Big)\Big(\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{0}^{\star}}}{1+e^{-\alpha_{0}^{\star}}}\Big)^{2}\frac{\big(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]\big)^{2}}{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]}

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{\big(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]\big)^{2}}{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]}.

Therefore, for any fixed

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k,p}\|^{2}-\min_{m}\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k,m,p}\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{\big(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]\big)^{2}}{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}]}.\qed

A.3.1
Proofs for Degrees of Freedom Results

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Note that for
k=1,2,\ldots,p
, we have

\hat{f}_{k,m,p}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_{j}^{k,m,p}\hat{\beta}_{j,p}x_{j,p},\quad\hat{f}_{k,p}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat{\beta}_{j,p}x_{j,p},

(65)

which implies

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{k,m,p}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_{j}^{k,m,p}(\hat{f}_{j,p}-\hat{f}_{j-1,p}).

Since degrees of freedom is a linear operator over estimators, we have

\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,m,p})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_{j}^{k,m,p}\Delta(j),

(66)

where
\Delta(j)\coloneqq\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{j,p})-\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{j-1,p})
, for
j=1,2,\ldots,p
, forms a non-increasing sequence by the concavity assumption.
We claim that whenever
m^{\prime}>m
, the sequence
\big\{w_{j}^{k,m^{\prime},p}\big\}_{j=1}^{p}
majorizes the sequence
\big\{w_{j}^{k,m,p}\big\}_{j=1}^{p}
. This means that for each
l\geq 1
,

\sum_{j=1}^{l}w_{j}^{k,m^{\prime},p}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{l}w_{j}^{k,m,p},

(67)

with equality when
l=p
.

Assuming the majorization in (
67
) holds, the theorem follows directly from Theorem A.3 in
[
38
]
by using the facts that both
w_{j}^{k,m,p}
and
\Delta(j)
are non-increasing in
j
.

We return to proving the claim.
Let
S_{k,m,p}
denote the indices of the features selected by one inner loop of RGS with parameters
k
,
m
, and
p
.
We may write

\begin{split}\sum_{j=1}^{l}w_{j}^{k,m,p}&=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\mathbb{P}(j\in S_{k,m,p})\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{l}\mathbb{1}(j\in S_{k,m,p})\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{k,m,p}(l)\right],\end{split}

(68)

where
Z_{k,m,p}(l)\coloneqq|S_{k,m,p}\cap[l]|
.
Evidently, (
67
) will be established if we can produce a coupling between
S_{k,m,p}
and
S_{k,m^{\prime},p}
such that
Z_{k,m^{\prime}p}(l)\geq Z_{k,m,p}(l)
for
l=1,2,\ldots,p
.

We construct such a coupling by running two instances of RGS concurrently, one with parameter choice
m
and the other with parameter choice
m^{\prime}
.
At iteration
k+1
of the algorithm, we first draw a subset
B_{m}
of size
m
from
[p-k]
uniformly at random.
We then draw a further subset of
m^{\prime}-m
elements from
[p-k]\backslash B_{m}
uniformly at random and take their union with
B_{m}
to form
B_{m^{\prime}}
.
We then construct the candidate subset
\mathcal{V}_{k+1,m,p}
by ordering
[p]\backslash S_{k,m,p}
from smallest to largest and picking the elements at positions in
B_{m}
.
To construct the candidate subset
\mathcal{V}_{k+1,m^{\prime},p}
, we do the same, but with
[p]\backslash S_{k,m^{\prime},p}
and
B_{m^{\prime}}
instead of
[p]\backslash S_{k,m,p}
and
B_{m}
respectively.

We now show the desired property inductively in
k
, with the base case
k=0
trivial.
Assume the inductive hypothesis.
Let
b\coloneqq\min B_{m}
.
Then one can show that the index selected at iteration
k+1
(with parameter
m
) is equal to
l_{*}\coloneqq\min\left\{l\colon b\leq l-Z_{k,m,p}(l)\right\}
.
We therefore have

Z_{k+1,m,p}(l)=\begin{cases}Z_{k,m,p}(l)&\text{if}~l<l_{*}\\
Z_{k,m,p}(l)+1&\text{if}~l\geq l_{*}.\end{cases}

(69)

Similarly, denoting
b^{\prime}\coloneqq\min B_{m^{\prime}}
and letting
l_{*}^{\prime}\coloneqq\min\left\{l\colon b^{\prime}\leq l-Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)\right\}
, we get

Z_{k+1,m^{\prime},p}(l)=\begin{cases}Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)&\text{if}~l<l_{*}^{\prime}\\
Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)+1&\text{if}~l\geq l_{*}^{\prime}.\end{cases}

(70)

To compare
Z_{k+1,m^{\prime},p}
and
Z_{k,m,p}
, we further set
\tilde{l}\coloneqq\min\left\{l\colon b\leq l-Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)\right\}
and construct the function
\tilde{Z}
via

\tilde{Z}(l)=\begin{cases}Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)&\text{if}~l<\tilde{l}\\
Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)+1&\text{if}~l\geq\tilde{l}.\end{cases}

(71)

Since
b^{\prime}\leq b
, we have
\tilde{l}\geq l_{*}^{\prime}
, so that
\tilde{Z}\leq Z_{k+1,m^{\prime},p}
.
Next, notice that

\tilde{Z}(l)-Z_{k+1,m,p}(l)=\begin{cases}Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)-Z_{k,m,p}(l)&\text{if}~l<l_{*}\\
Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)-Z_{k,m,p}(l)-1&\text{if}~l_{*}\leq l<\tilde{l}\\
Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)-Z_{k,m,p}(l)&\text{if}~l\geq\tilde{l}.\end{cases}

(72)

By the induction hypothesis, we have
Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)-Z_{k,m,p}(l)\geq 0
.
Meanwhile, on
l_{*}\leq l<\tilde{l}
, write

Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)-Z_{k,m,p}(l)=(Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)-l)-(Z_{k,m,p}(l)-l).

(73)

Because these functions take integral values, the only way
Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)-Z_{k,m,p}(l)-1
can be negative is if the right hand side of (
73
) is equal to 0.
However, by the way in which
l_{*}
is defined, we would have

l-Z_{k,m^{\prime},p}(l)=l-Z_{k,m,p}(l)\geq b,

(74)

which contradicts the definition of
\tilde{l}
.
This implies that
Z_{k+1,m,p}\leq\tilde{Z}
, which when combined with the earlier comparison, gives
Z_{k+1,m,p}\leq Z_{k+1,m^{\prime},p}
as desired.
∎

Utilizing the majorization in (
67
), we now turn to prove Theorem
4.3
.

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

According to the decomposition (
6
), it suffices to prove

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}\big(w_{j}^{k,m,p}\big)^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{p}\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}\big(w_{j}^{k,m^{\prime},p}\big)^{2}.

(75)

Applying Proposition A.3.a from
[
38
]
, where in their notation we take
p_{j}=\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}
and
g(x)=x^{2}
, noting that
g
is an increasing convex function on the positive real numbers, it follows that (
75
) holds if for any
l\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{l}\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}w_{j}^{k,m,p}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{l}\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}w_{j}^{k,m^{\prime},p}.

(76)

Applying Theorem A.3 from
[
38
]
and using the fact that
\hat{\beta}^{2}_{j,p}
is non-increasing in
j
, we conclude that
(
76
) holds for any
l\geq 1
.
∎

For the remaining proofs in this section, we depart from the notation used in the rest of this paper, and let
\mathbf{x}_{\cdot 1,p},\mathbf{x}_{\cdot 2,p},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{\cdot p,p}
denote a
fixed
ordering of the features.
Correspondingly, their regression coefficients
\hat{\beta}_{1,p},\hat{\beta}_{2,p},\ldots,\hat{\beta}_{p,p}
will no longer be assumed to be in sorted order.

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

Consider any pair of distinct feature indices,
i\neq j
.
Then

\hat{\beta}_{i,p}-\hat{\beta}_{j,p}=(\beta_{i,p}-\beta_{j,p})+\langle\mathbf{x}_{\cdot i,p}-\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\rangle.

(77)

The second term has the distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,2\sigma^{2}/n)
.
In particular, if
i\leq k
and
j>k
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\beta}_{i}-\hat{\beta}_{j}<0\right)\leq(4\pi\sigma^{2}/n)^{-1/2}\exp(-n/4\sigma^{2}).

(78)

Similarly, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\beta}_{i}+\hat{\beta}_{j}<0\right)\leq(4\pi\sigma^{2}/n)^{-1/2}\exp(-n/4\sigma^{2}).

(79)

Putting these together gives

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(|\hat{\beta}_{j,p}|>|\hat{\beta}_{i,p}|\right)&\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|\hat{\beta}_{j,p}|>\hat{\beta}_{i,p}\right)\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\beta}_{j,p}>\hat{\beta}_{i,p}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(-\hat{\beta}_{j,p}<\hat{\beta}_{i,p}\right)\\
&\leq(\pi\sigma^{2}/n)^{-1/2}\exp(-n/4\sigma^{2}).\end{split}

(80)

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the event on which
\operatorname{rank}(|\hat{\beta}_{j,p}|\leq k
, for
j=1,\ldots,k
.
We have

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)&=\mathbb{P}\left(|\hat{\beta}_{i,p}|>|\hat{\beta}_{j,p}|,1\leq i\leq k<j\leq p\right)\\
&\geq 1-k(p-k)(\pi\sigma^{2}/n)^{-1/2}\exp(-n/4\sigma^{2}).\end{split}

(81)

Next, let
\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{k}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbf{x}_{\cdot i,k}\mathbf{x}_{\cdot i,k}^{\top}
.
This is the projection matrix onto the column span of the first
k
features.
Let
\mathbf{H}_{k}
be the smoothing matrix corresponding to
k
steps of FS, noting that it depends on
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\ldots,\epsilon_{n})
.
Observe that on the event
\mathcal{A}
, we have
\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{k}=\mathbf{H}_{k}
.
We can then write

\begin{split}\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,p})&=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{y}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbb{1}(\mathcal{A})\right]+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbb{1}(\mathcal{A}^{c})\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{k}\mathbf{y}\right]-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{k}\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbb{1}(\mathcal{A}^{c})\right]+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbb{1}(\mathcal{A}^{c})\right].\end{split}

(82)

The first term satisfies

\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{k}\mathbf{y}\right]=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{k}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right]=k.

(83)

To bound the third term, notice that since
\mathbf{H}_{k}
is a projection matrix, we can bound

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{y}&=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{f}\\
&\leq\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{2}+\sqrt{kn}\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\|_{2}.\end{split}

(84)

In particular, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{y})^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq C(\sqrt{k}+\sigma)\sigma n,

(85)

where
C
is a universal constant.
Applying Cauchy-Schwarz thus gives us

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbb{1}(\mathcal{A}^{c})\right]&\leq\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{y})^{2}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)^{1/2}\\
&\leq C(\sqrt{k}/\sigma+1)nk(p-k)(\pi\sigma^{2}/n)^{-1/4}\exp(-n/8\sigma^{2}).\end{split}

(86)

By a similar calculation, the second term is bounded by the same value.
Hence,

\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,p})=k+O(e^{-cn}).

(87)

Meanwhile, let
\tau
denote the index of the feature selected on the
(k+1)
-th step of FS.
We then have
\hat{f}_{k+1,p}-\hat{f}_{k,p}=\hat{\beta}_{\tau,p}x_{\cdot\tau,p}
, which gives

\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k+1,p})-\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,p})=\operatorname{df}(\hat{\beta}_{\tau}\psi_{\tau}).

(88)

On the event
\mathcal{A}
, it is easy to see that

\tau=\argmax_{k+1\leq j\leq p}\hat{\beta}_{j,p}^{2}.

(89)

As such, we compute

\begin{split}\operatorname{df}(\hat{\beta}_{\tau}\psi_{\tau})&=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\mathbf{y}-\mathbb{E}y)^{\top}\hat{\beta}_{\tau,p}\mathbf{x}_{\cdot j,p}\right]\\
&=\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{\beta}_{\tau,p}^{2}\right]\\
&\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\argmax_{k+1\leq j\leq p}\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\hat{\beta}_{j,p}^{2}\mathbb{1}(\mathcal{A})\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}\left[\argmax_{k+1\leq j\leq p}\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\hat{\beta}_{j,p}^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=k+1}^{p}\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\hat{\beta}_{j,p}^{2}\mathbb{1}(\mathcal{A}^{c})\right].\end{split}

(90)

Since
n\hat{\beta}_{j,p}^{2}/\sigma^{2}\sim_{\text{IID}}\chi^{2}_{1}
for
k+1\leq j\leq p
, Lemma
A.6
implies that the first term is lower bounded by
\frac{1}{2}\log(p-k)
for
p-k
large enough.
Applying Cauchy-Schwarz to the second term, we can bound it (in absolute value) from above by

(p(p+2))^{1/2}(\pi\sigma^{2}/n)^{-1/4}\exp(-n/8\sigma^{2}).

(91)

Hence,

\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k+1,p})-\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,p})\geq\frac{1}{2}\log(p-k)+O(e^{-cn}).

(92)

Using (
66
), we get

\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,m,p})\geq(w_{k}^{k,m,p}-w_{k+1}^{k,m,p})\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k+1,p}).

(93)

Putting everything together, we therefore get

\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,m,p})-\operatorname{df}(\hat{f}_{k,p})\geq\frac{1}{2}(w_{k}^{k,m,p}-w_{k+1}^{k,m,p})\log(p-k)-k+O(e^{-cn}).

(94)

Taking a limit as
n,m,p
go to infinity finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.6
.

Let
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{N}\sim_{\text{IID}}\chi^{2}_{1}
.
For
N
large enough, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq N}Z_{i}\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}\log N.

(95)

Proof.

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq N}Z_{i}\right]&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}Z_{i}\geq t\right)dt\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}1-\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}Z_{i}<t\right)dt\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}1-\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{1}<t\right)^{N}dt\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}1-(2\Phi(\sqrt{t})-1)^{N}dt,\end{split}

(96)

where
\Phi(z)
is the CDF of a Gaussian distribution.
Using the Mills’ ratio bound, we have
\Phi(z)\leq 1-e^{-z^{2}}
for
z
large enough, which would imply that
(2\Phi(z)-1)^{N}\leq(1-2e^{-z^{2}})^{N}
.
Since the integrand on the right hand side of (
96
) is decreasing, we have, for
N
large enough, that

\begin{split}\min_{0\leq t\leq\log N}1-(2\Phi(\sqrt{t})-1)^{N}&\geq 1-(2\Phi(\sqrt{\log N})-1)^{N}\\
&\geq 1-(1-2/N)^{N}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}.\end{split}

(97)

Plugging this lower bound into (
96
) and integrating from
0
to
N
completes the proof.
∎

Example A.1
.

We wish to provide an example where RGS reduces training error through the mechanism of avoiding local optima that FS may fall into, as opposed to that of enlarging the hypothesis class.
This means that we would like to compare FS to the base estimator of RGS (rather than the ensemble).

In the orthogonal feature setting, for the same value of
k
, the training loss of the base estimator is worse than FS, since we can do no better than selecting the
k
features with the highest correlations with the response.
Hence, we make use of a correlated feature setting.
The idea, in a nutshell, is to create a regression vector that is strongly correlated with the response, but is not part of the optimal representation.
Because of greediness, FS will select this spurious feature, but each base estimator of RGS may have a chance of avoiding its selection if it is omitted from the first candidate set
\mathcal{V}_{1}
.

More rigorously, consider a generative model with features
x_{1,p},x_{2,p},\ldots,x_{p,p}
.
Also assume a feature
x_{0,p}
which is unavailable for selection, but will be used to define the other features.
Suppose that we have
\langle\mathbf{x}_{i,p},\mathbf{x}_{j,p}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
for
0\leq i,j\leq{p-1}
and that
x_{p,p}=3^{-1/2}(x_{1,p}+x_{2,p}+x_{0,p})
, so that
\langle\mathbf{x}_{\cdot i,p},\mathbf{x}_{\cdot p,p}\rangle=3^{-1/2}
for
i=1,2
.
Assume
y=\beta(x_{1,p}+x_{2,p})+\zeta\sum_{i=3}^{p-1}x_{i,p}
.
In other words, we have noiseless observations.
Assume further that
6^{-1/2}\beta>\zeta>0
.

Denote the features indices selected by FS (in order) as
\pi(1),\pi(2),\ldots,\pi(p)
.
Since

\langle\mathbf{x}_{\cdot i,p},\mathbf{y}\rangle=\begin{cases}\beta&i=1,2\\
\zeta&3\leq i\leq p-1\\
2\cdot 3^{-1/2}\beta&i=p,\end{cases}

(98)

we have
\pi(1)=p
.
Next, the first step residual satisfies

\langle\mathbf{x}_{\cdot i,p},\mathbf{r}_{1}\rangle=\begin{cases}\beta/3&i=1,2\\
\zeta&3\leq i\leq p-1.\end{cases}

(99)

Furthermore,

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\perp}\mathbf{x}_{\cdot i,p}\|^{2}&=1-\langle\mathbf{x}_{\cdot i,p},\mathbf{x}_{\cdot p,p}\rangle^{2}\\
&=\begin{cases}2/3&i=1,2\\
1&3\leq i\leq p-1.\end{cases}\end{split}

(100)

Putting these together, the objective for each feature has the value

\frac{|\langle\mathbf{x}_{\cdot i,p},\mathbf{r}_{1}\rangle|}{\|\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\perp}\mathbf{x}_{\cdot i,p}\|}=\begin{cases}6^{-1/2}\beta&i=1,2\\
\zeta&3\leq i\leq p-1.\end{cases}

(101)

As such, we have
\pi(k)=k-1
for
k=2,3,\ldots,p
.
This means that for any
k\geq 3
,

\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k,p}\|^{2}=(p-k)\zeta^{2}.

(102)

We now study the base estimator of RGS, denoted
\check{f}_{k,m,p}
, which depends on a random seed
\theta
.
At iteration
l
, suppose
p\notin\mathcal{M}_{l}
.
Observe that the residual
r_{l}
satisfies

\frac{|\langle\mathbf{x}_{\cdot p,p},\mathbf{r}_{l}\rangle|}{\|\mathbf{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}^{\perp}\mathbf{x}_{\cdot i,p}\|}=\begin{cases}2\cdot 3^{-1/2}\beta&\text{if}~|\{1,2\}\cap\mathcal{M}_{l}|=0\\
2^{-1/2}\beta&\text{if}~|\{1,2\}\cap\mathcal{M}_{l}|=1\\
0&\text{if}~|\{1,2\}\cap\mathcal{M}_{l}|=2.\end{cases}

(103)

Hence, if both
x_{1,p}
and
x_{2,p}
are selected before
x_{p,p}
, then
x_{p,p}
is never selected.
Denote the feature indices selected by RGS (in order) as
\tau(1),\tau(2),\ldots,\tau(p)
.
Then
\tau^{-1}(j)
denotes the step at which feature
x_{j,p}
was selected.
Note that these depend on the random seed
\theta
.
We now decompose the probability space into three events.
Let

\mathcal{E}_{1}=\{\tau^{-1}(1),\tau^{-1}(2)\leq\min(\tau^{-1}(p),k)\},

(104)

\mathcal{E}_{2}=\{\tau^{-1}(1),\tau^{-1}(2)\leq k\}\backslash\mathcal{E}_{1},

(105)

\mathcal{E}_{3}=\left(\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}\right)^{c}.

(106)

We have

\|\mathbf{y}-\check{\mathbf{f}}_{k,m,p}\|^{2}=\begin{cases}(p-k-1)\zeta^{2}&\text{if}~\mathcal{E}_{1}~\text{holds}\\
(p-k)\zeta^{2}&\text{if}~\mathcal{E}_{2}~\text{holds}\\
(p-k-3)\zeta^{2}+C(\theta)&\text{if}~\mathcal{E}_{3}~\text{holds},\end{cases}

(107)

where
|C(\theta)|\leq 2\beta^{2}
.
We next compute bounds for the event probabilities.
For
l=1,\ldots,k
, let
S_{l}
denote the set of
m
indices sampled at iteration
l
.
Denote
\gamma=m/p
as before.
By our previous discussion, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1})&\geq\mathbb{P}(1\in S_{1},0\notin S_{1},2\in S_{2},0\notin S_{2})\\
&=\frac{\binom{p-2}{m-1}\binom{p-3}{m-1}}{\binom{p}{m}\binom{p-1}{m}}\\
&=\gamma^{2}(1-\gamma^{2})+O(1/p).\end{split}

(108)

To compute a bound for
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{3})
, let us redefine the way
S_{l}
is generated as follows:
First draw a subset
\tilde{S}_{l}
of size
m
from
[p]
, i.e. including indices that have already been selected.
If it contains an index that has already been selected, throw it out and draw it again.
Let
W_{l}^{(j)}=\mathbb{1}(j\in\tilde{S}_{l})
for
j=1,2
,
l=1,\ldots,k
.
Then these are Bernoulli random features with probability
\gamma
.
Note also that if
\sum_{l=1}^{k}W_{l}^{(j)}\geq 3
, then
\tau^{-1}(j)\leq k
.
We thus have

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{3})&=1-\mathbb{P}(\tau^{-1}(1),\tau^{-1}(2)\leq k)\\
&=\mathbb{P}(\tau^{-1}(1)>k~\text{or}~\tau^{-1}(2)>k)\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}(\tau^{-1}(1)>k)+\mathbb{P}(\tau^{-1}(2)>k)\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k}W_{l}^{(1)}<3\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k}W_{l}^{(2)}<3\right)\\
&\leq 2e^{-k\gamma}(ek\gamma/2)^{2},\end{split}

(109)

where the last inequality follows from Chernoff’s inequality.

Putting everything together, and using Jensen’s inequality, we compute

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k,p}\|^{2}-\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k,m,p}\|^{2}&\geq\zeta^{2}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{1})-2\beta^{2}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{3})\\
&\geq\zeta^{2}(1-\gamma^{2})\gamma^{2}-4\beta^{2}e^{-k\gamma}(ek\gamma/2)^{2}-O(1/p).\end{split}

(110)

For
k
and
p
large enough, this last quantity is strictly positive.
