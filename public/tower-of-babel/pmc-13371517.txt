Role of glutathione disulfide reductase in colorectal cancer: a tumor suppressor
Authors: Lilin Lin, Zexiu Chen, Cong-yu Zhang, Jingnian Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlutathione-disulfide reductase (GSR) was responsible for maintaining the supply of reduced glutathione, and was found to be abnormally expressed in a variety of cancers. There was an incomplete understanding regarding the roles of GSR in colorectal cancer (CRC) today.MethodsWe used colorectal cancer tissues and cell lines to detect the function of GSR in CRC. We also used bioinformatics to analyze the related pathways of GSR in colorectal cancer.ResultsGSR mRNA was down-regulated in CRC (p < 0.05). The expression of GSR mRNA with T3 and T4 patients was lower than that with T1 and T2 patients, with M1 patients was lower than that with M0 patients, with N1 and N2 patients was lower than that with N0 stage patients, with stage 3 and stage 4 patients was lower than that with stage1 and stage 2 patients (p < 0.05). Overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), progression survival (PFS), post-progression survival (PPS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) time for patients with high expression of GSR was higher than that for patients with low GSR expression (p < 0.05). The expression of p-p38 MAPK and p-ERK increased in GSR knockdown cells, while the expression of p-Nrf2, KEAP1, p-PI3K and p-Akt decreased. Consistent with alterations in the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, cells with GSR knockdown exhibited significantly enhanced proliferative, migratory, and invasive capabilities, along with increased ROS production (all p < 0.001), while showing a reduced apoptosis rate (p < 0.001). Furthermore, both re-expression of GSR and treatment with an ERK inhibitor effectively reversed the phenotypic and molecular changes induced by GSR knockdown.ConclusionThe expression of GSR was closely related to the development of colorectal cancer. GSR was low expressed and played a tumor-suppressive role through inhibiting the production of ROS, and the activation of p38 MAPK/ERK pathways in CRC. GSR has the potential to become a potential biomarker for the diagnosis and treatment of colorectal cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16174-0.

Introduction

Pyridine nucleotide disulfide reductases (PNDR) constitute a large and heterogeneous protein family characterized by a disulfide redox-active site and conserved NAD(P)H- and FAD-binding sites [1]. PNDR comprises approximately 10,000 proteins and is mainly classified into two subfamilies: class 1 (PR001100) containing 6,701 sequences and class 2 (IPR000103) containing 2,809 sequences [2]. These two major classes are further subdivided into additional subfamilies that include proteins sharing specific motifs or domains [2]. Glutathione-disulfide reductase (GSR) encodes a class I pyridine nucleotide-disulfide reductase family member and is located at chr8: 30,535,583–30,585,363 in the human genome; it spans 49,781 bases and consists of 13 exons and 12 introns [3]. The GSR gene encodes a 522-amino-acid protein containing two pyrimidine nucleotidyl-disulphide oxidoreductase regions and a FAD/NAD(P)-binding domain [3].

Reduced glutathione is among the most abundant cellular reducing thiols and is central to controlling 2intracellular ROS, whereas GSR maintains the cellular supply of reduced glutathione. In addition, GSR is a key component of the ascorbic acid–glutathione (ASA-GSH) pathway, which is important for protecting cells against ROS and the potential abnormalities arising from accumulation of reaction products [4]. GSR-dependent redox regulation is also critical for bacterial clearance during host defense against extensive bacterial challenge [5]. In neutrophils lacking GSR, disruption of the hexose monophosphate shunt is accompanied by marked impairment in neutrophil extracellular trap formation [6]. In endothelial cells cultured with maternal plasma from women with preeclampsia, reduced GSR activity and increased adhesion molecule expression alter the pro-oxidant/antioxidant balance of endothelial cells [5].

By maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis, GSR protects normal cells from ROS-induced injury [7–12]. Influenza infection reduces glutathione metabolism and GSR activity in lung epithelial cells, thereby suppressing apoptosis and necrosis in these cells [7]. In vivo, GSR is regulated by iron homeostasis; iron overload affects GSR expression in rats and subsequently promotes oxidative stress [8]. In rats exposed to alcohol, upregulated miR-214 suppresses GSR, leading to oxidative stress in hepatocytes [9]. Vitamin D increases glutathione levels by upregulating GSR and decreasing ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines [10]. In vivo activation of GSR by blood flow elevates the ratio of GSH to oxidized glutathione (GSSG) and keeps endothelial cells in a reduced state [11]. In human fibroblasts, docosahexaenoic acid positively regulates GSR, thereby strengthening the antioxidant response [12]. Against this background, the role of GSR in cancer likely reflects the “double-edged sword” nature of ROS. At moderate levels, ROS support normal signaling (oxidative eustress); when excessive, they cause damage (oxidative distress) [13]. Thus, antioxidant systems can help prevent tumor initiation by removing mutagenic ROS and preserving genome stability, but in established tumors they may also help cancer cells survive under high metabolic and oxidative stress. For examples, low-to-moderate hydrogen peroxide reversibly oxidizes catalytic cysteines in protein tyrosine phosphatases and amplifies growth-factor signaling, showing a normal signaling role for ROS [14]. In mouse colitis-associated colorectal cancer, inflammation-derived ROS/RNS cause oxidative DNA lesions (e.g., 8-oxoG), and limiting this damage reduces tumor formation [15]. In KRAS/BRAF-driven mouse lung cancer, giving N-acetylcysteine or vitamin E lowers intratumoral ROS but paradoxically speeds tumor growth and shortens survival, suggesting stronger antioxidant capacity can protect established tumors [16]. During melanoma metastasis, oxidative stress in the bloodstream and distant tissues suppresses colonization, whereas antioxidants promote distant spread, indicating an anti-metastatic role for ROS at certain stages [17]. Therapeutically, many chemotherapies and radiotherapy increase ROS to kill cancer cells, but tumors that boost their own antioxidant defenses may become treatment-resistant [18].

Although colorectal cancer incidence and mortality have declined over the past two decades due to changes in risk factors and screening practices, it remains a major threat to human health, particularly in developed countries [19, 20]. To advance diagnosis, treatment, and prevention, we systematically examined the roles of GSR at the tissue, cellular, and molecular levels.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

CRC cell lines were obtained from Procell Company (Wuhan, China). All cell lines used in this study were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. Cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 (DLD-1, HCT15), McCoy’s 5 A (HCT-116 and HT-29), or MEM (RKO). At 24 h after seeding, cells were transfected with a GSR shRNA plasmid (Santa Cruz, sc-44843-SH, USA) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by puromycin selection.

Proliferation assay

Briefly, 3500 cells/well were plated in a plate and incubated in serum-free medium for 0, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. Subsequently, 10 µl CCK-8 reagent (Cellcook, USA) was added and incubated for 90 min. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Apoptosis assay

Phosphatidylserine externalization was assessed by flow cytometry using 7-amino-actinomycin (7-AAD) in combination with phycoerythrin (PE)-labeled Annexin V (BD Pharmingen, USA). For apoptosis evaluation, cells were acquired on the instrument after 15 min of pre-staining, and data were analyzed using FlowJo V10 software.

Wound healing assay

A total of 8.5 × 105 cells/well were seeded into 6-well plates. A straight scratch was made with a pipette tip to remove surface cells, followed by PBS washing and addition of serum-free medium. Images were captured at the same field at 0 h and 48 h.

Transwell assay

In migration experiment, 4.5 × 105 cells were placed in the upper chamber (Corning, USA), and medium containing 14% FBS was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 h, chambers were washed with PBS and cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, China). Cells were stained with crystal violet (Beyotime, China) and imaged under a microscope. For invasion assays, Matrigel (Corning, USA) was added to the upper chamber and incubation was extended to 48 h; all other procedures followed those of the migration assay. Data were quantified using Image J.

Subjects and pathology

All tissues and plasma samples were collected from the Affiliated Hospital of Chengde Medical University (China). Structures were identified by HE staining and processed into tissue microarrays. Prior to surgery, no patient received medication or physical therapy. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the research protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Chengde Medical University. Tumor grading, staging, and tissue structure were evaluated according to the Union Internationale Contre le Cancer system and the WHO classification.

Quantitative Reverse Transcriptase-Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT-PCR)

Primers used were as follows: GSR forward 5’-CGCCTGAATGCCATCTAT-3’ and reverse 5’-CATCGCTGGTTATTCCTAAG-3’; β-actin forward 5’-CACTCTTCCAGCCTTCCTTC-3’ and reverse 5’-GTACAGGTCTTTGCGGATGT-3’. Cells and tissue DNA were amplified using NovoStart SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (Novoprotein, China).

Western blot

Briefly, denatured proteins were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck, USA). After blocking with 5% milk for 0.5 h, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) at 37℃ for 2.5 h. PVDF membranes were washed with PBS for 13 min and then incubated with IgG-conjugated horseradish peroxidase (Proteintech, USA). Signals were developed using ECL detection reagent (Beyotime, China), and band intensity was quantified with Image J.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue microarrays were baked at 65℃, deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through alcohol, and subjected to antigen retrieval in target retrieval solution (Beyotime, China). Sections were then incubated for 18 min in methanol containing 2.5% hydrogen peroxide and PBS containing 4.5% bovine serum albumin to block endogenous peroxidase activity and non-specific binding. Tissue microarrays were incubated with anti-GSR antibody, washed with PBS, and incubated with an IgG-labeled horseradish peroxidase antibody (Proteintech, USA) for 1.5 h. Immunoreactivity was visualized using diaminobenzidine. Sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted after counterstaining with Mayer’s hematoxylin.

Intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) detection

According to the instructions of the ROS assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, S0033, Shanghai, China), HCT15 cells were seeded at a density of 8000 cells/well into 9 6-well plates, and incubated with serum-free medium containing 10 µM DCFH-DA at 37℃ for 20 min and then washed with serum-free culture medium for three times. The fluorescence intensity was determined using a microplate reading spectrofluorimeter at the excitation wavelength of 488 nm and the emission wavelength of 525 nm.

Bioinformatics analysis

Expression, methylation, and clinicopathologic data from CRC patients were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and analyzed in R studio 4.1.0. GSR expression, methylation, differential genes, related genes, and associated signaling pathways were examined using the xiantao platform (https://www.xiantaozi.com/) and the UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis.html). The prognostic value of GSR was evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=service&cancer=colon). A PPI network was built for differential genes in String database (string-db.org), and the top ten hub genes were identified using cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape. GO-KEGG and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) were performed to predict signaling pathways. The human protein atlas (THPA) database was used to assess GSR protein expression in colorectal cancer and adjacent tissues.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in more than three independent repeats, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare means, the chi-square test to compare rates, and the Spearman test to evaluate ranked data. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Analysis of the TCGA dataset indicated that GSR mRNA expression was decreased in Colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), Kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), Kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), Prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), and Rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), but increased in Breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), Cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), Liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), Stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), Thyroid carcinoma (THCA), and Uterine Corpus Endometrial Carcinoma (UCEC) (p < 0.05, Fig. 1A). Consistent results from the TCGA and UALCAN databases demonstrated that GSR mRNA was down-regulated in colorectal cancer (p < 0.05, Fig. 1B-C), and qPCR analysis of colorectal cancer tissues and paired adjacent tissues confirmed the same trend (p < 0.05, Fig. 1D). Data from the Xiantao platform showed that GSR mRNA expression in T3 and T4 patients was lower than that in T1 and T2 patients; expression in M1 patients was lower than that in M0 patients; expression in N1 and N2 patients was lower than that in N0 patients; expression in stage 3 and stage 4 patients was lower than that in stage1 and stage 2 patients; expression in male patients was lower than that in female patients; expression in R2 patients was lower than that in R1 patients; expression in patients with lymphatic invasion was lower than that in patients without lymphatic invasion; expression in patients with peritoneal invasion was lower than that in patients without peritoneal invasion; expression in rectal cancer patients was lower than that in colon cancer patients; and expression in adenocarcinoma patients was lower than that in mucinous adenocarcinoma patients (p < 0.05, Fig. 1D; Table 1). Multivariate Cox regression analysis indicated that high T, M, and N stage, high stage, male sex, mucinous adenocarcinoma, high residual tumor stage, peritoneum invasion, lymph node invasion, previous colon polyps, and rectal adenocarcinoma type were independent risk factors for colorectal cancer (Table 2).

The Xiantao platform, Kaplan-Meier plotter database, and UALCAN platform consistently showed that, in CRC, patients with high GSR mRNA expression had higher overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), progression survival (PFS), post-progression survival (PPS), and recurrence-free survival (RFS) time than those with low GSR mRNA expression (p < 0.05, Fig. 2A). Based on TCGA data, we constructed a nomogram for GSR-associated overall survival to estimate 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival probabilities across patients with different individual characteristics (Fig. 2B). A calibration curve was generated to evaluate nomogram performance, and the global assessment of the model demonstrated a concordance index (C-index) of 0.900 (0.868–0.932) (Fig. 2C, p < 0.05).

By dividing patients from TCGA-CRC cohort into high-GSR group and low-GSR group based on GSR expression and performing differential expression analysis using “limma” package in R software (fold change>1, and p<0.05), we identified GSR-associated differential genes in colorectal cancer and visualized them using a volcano plot (p < 0.05, Fig. 3A). Interactions among these differential genes were assessed using a mininum required interaction score of 0.4 in the String database and demonstrated in Cytoscape software (Fig. 3B). We further selected the top 10 hub genes using cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape to build a PPI network (Fig. 3C). Among these ten genes, H2AC14, H3C12, H2AC21, H2AC4, H2AC16, and H2AC12 mRNA levels were increased, whereas H4C6 mRNA levels were decreased in colorectal cancer (Fig. 3D, p < 0.05). GSEA indicated that enriched differential genes were associated with Tca cycle and deficiency of pyruvate dehydrogenase complex pdhc, Metabolism of cofactors, TP53 regulates transcription of death receptors and ligands, Unfolded protein response, Caspase pathway, Metalloprotease dubs, Rmts methylate histone arginines, Recognition and association of DNA glycosylase with site containing an affected purine, and STRT1 negatively regulates rRNA expression and DNA methylation (Fig. 3E, p < 0.05).

Using 367 tumor samples from TCGA-CRC cohort, we further conducted correlation analysis of GSR-related genes using Benjamini-Hochberg method for P-value correction, and displayed the top 10 positively correlated genes in a heat map (Fig. 4A, p < 0.05). GO-KEGG analysis of the positively correlated genes showed enrichment in ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, ncRNA processing, ribosome biogenesis, mitochondrial matrix, mitochondrial inner membrane, mitochondrial protein-containing complex, catalytic activity- acting on RNA, ATP hydrolysis activity, and structural constituent of ribosome (Fig. 4B, p < 0.05). Among the top 10 positively correlated genes, GTF2E2, INTS10, DCTN6, PBK, TTI2, TNFRSF10A, and INT9 mRNA expression in colorectal cancer was higher than that in normal people, whereas PPP2CB mRNA expression in colorectal cancer was lower than that in normal people (Fig. 4C, p < 0.05). We also identified the top 10 negatively correlated genes of GSR and presented them in a heat map (Fig. 4D, p < 0.05). GO-KEGG analysis of the negatively correlated genes indicated enrichment in regulation of trans-synaptic signaling, modulation of chemical synaptic transmission, synapse organization, synaptic membrane, postsynaptic membrane, intrinsic component of synaptic membrane, ion channel activity, gated channel activity, extracellular matrix structural constituent, Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, and Calcium signaling pathway (Fig. 4E, p < 0.05). Among the top 10 negatively correlated genes, SALL4, R3HDML, BLCAP, ANKRD23, MPIG6B, DHRS12, and L3MTL1 mRNA expression in colorectal cancer was higher than that in normal people (Fig. 4F, p < 0.05).

We further performed rescue experiments to clarify the molecular mechanism by which GSR regulates the malignant phenotype of CRC cells. First, we successfully established GSR-rescued HCT15 cell models. qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed efficient Flag-GSR overexpression in mRNA level (Fig. 8A, p < 0.001) and protein levle (Fig. 8B). CCK8 proliferation assays showed that, compared with control HCT15 cells, the proliferative capacity of shGSR cells was significantly enhanced; however, Flag-GSR re-expression and ERK inhibitor treatment reversed the effect of GSR knockdown on cell proliferation (Fig. 8C, p < 0.001). Flow cytometric apoptosis analysis demonstrated that GSR knockdown significantly suppressed apoptosis in HCT15 cells, while Flag-GSR re-expression and ERK inhibitor treatment markedly induced apoptosis (Fig. 8D, p < 0.001). Migration and invasion assays further validated the regulatory role of GSR. Wound healing assays showed that the wound closure rate in the shGSR group was significantly higher than that in the control group, whereas Flag-GSR re-expression and ERK inhibitor treatment markedly reduced the wound closure rate (Fig. 8E, p < 0.001). Consistent with the above results, transwell migration and invasion assays showed that both Flag-GSR re-expression and ERK inhibitor treatment reversed the the migratory and invasive abilities of HCT15 cells enhanced by GSR knockdown (Fig. 8F, p < 0.001). Finally, Western blot analysis showed that the increased phosphorylation level of ERK (p-ERK) in GSR knockdown cells was also reduced after Flag-GSR re-expression or ERK inhibitor treatment (Fig. 8G).

Discussion

GSR has been reported to be closely associated with the development of cervical cancer, esophageal cancer, lung cancer, prostate cancer, and breast cancer [21–25]. Elevated GSR mRNA expression has been observed in multiple tumor types, implying its potential involvement in cancer initiation and progression. In contrast, both GSR mRNA and protein levels were decreased in CRC patients compared with normal people. Moreover, as tumor invasion depth increased and as distant metastasis, regional lymph node invasion, lymphatic invasion, and peritoneal invasion occurred, GSR mRNA expression exhibited a progressive decline, supporting a potential inhibitory effect of GSR on malignant progression in colorectal cancer. In our study, CRC patients with high GSR mRNA expression showed better outcomes than those with low GSR expression, as reflected by OS, DSS, PFS, RFS, and PPS, indicating that GSR may serve as a marker of favorable prognosis in CRC. Accordingly, we constructed a nomogram to estimate overall survival in colorectal cancer, enabling individualized prediction of disease-related 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS. Consistent with the findings of Brzozowa et al., we also observed that serum GSR levels in colorectal cancer patients were lower than those in normal volunteers, suggesting potential diagnostic value of GSR in colorectal cancer [26].

We detected GSR antigen staining with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetra hydrochloride in the nucleus and cytoplasm of glands as well as in peripheral lymphocytes. Brzozowa et al. reported, using transmission electron microscopy, that in tumor cell nuclei, GSR particles were loosely distributed within the nuclear matrix and located near the nuclear membrane; in the cytoplasm, GSR-associated electron-dense particles were mainly present close to the mitochondrial membrane or within the matrix. In addition, GSR was identified in endoplasmic reticulum vesicles [26]. Because mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum are essential for maintaining intracellular redox conditions and limiting oxidative injury, the concordant localization of GSR with these organelles further supports the possibility that GSR suppresses colorectal cancer development by maintaining intracellular redox balance [27, 28].

ROS has a complex and multifaceted function within the tumor microenvironment [29–31]. By modulating tumor redox status, ROS can amplify oxidative stress and thereby influence tumor cell apoptosis. Accumulating evidence indicates that cancer immunotherapy can alter tumor redox homeostasis, intensify oxidative stress, and induce ROS-dependent tumor rejection [29]. On this basis, we examined the association between GSR mRNA expression and immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment. We found that GSR mRNA expression was associated with infiltration of NK cells, T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, and neutrophils. We propose that cancer progression increases ROS and myeloid cell-driven chronic inflammation within the tumor microenvironment, and that interactions between inflammatory and oxidative stress mediators may establish a positive feedback loop that ultimately modulates antitumor immunity [30, 31].

Reduced GSR expression may contribute to colorectal cancer development by impairing intracellular redox homeostasis and promoting ROS accumulation. Tumor cells are exposed to persistent oxidative stress due to metabolic reprogramming and abnormal growth, and they rely on multiple adaptive mechanisms to maintain redox balance. ROS has been widely implicated in tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance, but its biological effects are strongly dependent on cellular context and redox threshold [29, 32]. In our study, GSR knockdown increased intracellular ROS levels and promoted proliferation, migration, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis in HCT15 cells, supporting the view that loss of GSR disrupts redox balance and favors malignant progression. These findings are also consistent with our clinical observations that GSR expression was lower in colorectal cancer tissues than in adjacent tissues and was further decreased in patients with more advanced local invasion, nodal involvement, distant metastasis, and higher pathological stage. We further examined several ROS-related signaling pathways and found that p-p38 MAPK and p-ERK were increased after GSR knockdown, whereas p-PI3K, p-Akt, p-Nrf2, and KEAP1 were decreased, while total levels of Nrf2, PI3K, Akt, ERK, p38 MAPK, and JNK remained largely unchanged. Among these changes, ERK activation is consistent with its established pro-proliferative role in colorectal cancer, and recent evidence has shown that ERK signaling can directly promote CRC cell growth [33]. However, the PI3K/Akt and KEAP1/NRF2 changes observed here do not fully conform to the canonical signaling pattern commonly described in colorectal cancer [34–37]. We therefore interpret these findings with caution. One possible explanation is that oxidative stress induced by GSR loss may trigger context-dependent pathway crosstalk, leading to non-linear signaling outputs rather than a simple uniform response [32]. In this setting, the downstream effects of ROS may vary according to the intensity and duration of oxidative stress, cell-type-specific metabolic state, and compensatory redox adaptation mechanisms [32]. Therefore, our current data support a model in which decreased GSR expression is associated with impaired antioxidant capacity, increased ROS accumulation, ERK activation, and a more aggressive phenotype in colorectal cancer cells, whereas the causal hierarchy among p38/ERK, PI3K/Akt, and KEAP1/NRF2 signaling remains to be further clarified.

GSR-associated genes showed positive associations with TCA cycle and deficiency of pyruvate dehydrogenase complex PDHC, metabolism of cofactors, TP53 regulates transcription of death receptors and ligands, unfolded protein response, and caspase pathway, and negative associations with metalloprotease dubs, RMTs methylate histone arginines, recognition and association of DNA glycosylase with sites containing an affected purine, and SIRT1 negatively regulates rRNA expression and DNA methylation. These pathways are closely related to several key biological processes in colorectal cancer, including metabolic adaptation, oxidative stress response, apoptosis, chromatin regulation, and genome maintenance. In our PPI analysis, several of the top hub genes, including H2AC14, H3C12, H2AC21, H2AC4, H2AC16, H2AC12, and H4C6, belonged to the histone family. Histone dysregulation has been reported to participate in chromatin remodeling, transcriptional reprogramming, and malignant progression in colorectal cancer, indicating that altered GSR expression may be associated with epigenetic changes in CRC [38, 39]. In addition, pathways related to the TCA cycle, PDHC deficiency, and metabolism of cofactors are functionally linked to metabolic reprogramming and redox homeostasis, which are essential for tumor cells to survive under persistent oxidative stress [29, 40] Multiple studies have shown that TP53 and caspase signaling participate in apoptosis regulation in CRC [41, 42]. Similarly, the unfolded protein response is an important adaptive pathway activated under endoplasmic reticulum stress, and its dysregulation may affect tumor cell survival or death depending on the cellular context [43]. We also found enrichment in pathways associated with DNA glycosylase recognition, histone arginine methylation, and regulation of rRNA expression and DNA methylation. These findings suggest that GSR-related transcriptional changes may also be associated with DNA repair and epigenetic regulation in colorectal cancer [38, 44, 45]. Collectively, these findings further support that GSR may participate in colorectal cancer development through redox-dependent and context-dependent regulation of multiple cancer-related pathways.

There were several limitation in our study. Bioinformatics analyses were performed using multiple public datasets from different platforms, thus unavoidable differences in sample size and data source may have introduced bias into the results. The limited numbers of samples and cell lines may also reduce the reliability of our conclusions. In addition, the nomogram has only undergone internal validation and still requires external validation in independent cohorts. Furthermore, the immune infiltration analysis is preliminary and should be interpreted with caution pending further validation. In future studies, we will conduct multicenter researches, expand the sample size, include additional cell lines, and further incorporate animal experiments to validate and extend our findings. As a preliminary investigation of GSR in colorectal cancer, this study provides a foundation for future research.

Conclusion

In summary, we speculated that the expression of GSR was closely related to the development of colorectal cancer. In addition, we speculate that GSR inhibits the invasive phenotype of colorectal cancer by inhibiting ROS and p38 MAPK/ERK signaling pathway. The expression of GSR in different stages of colorectal cancer and its well-predictive ability for patients’ prognosis suggested that GSR has the potential to become a potential biomarker for the diagnosis and treatment of colorectal cancer.
