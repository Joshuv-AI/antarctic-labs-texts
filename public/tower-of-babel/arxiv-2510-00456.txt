Exponential integrability and limiting behavior of the derivative of intersection and self-intersection local time of fractional Brownian motion
Kaustav Das †‡, Gregory Markowsky †, Binghao Wu †, Qian Yu ∗

Abstract
. We give the correct condition for existence of the k k -th derivative of the intersection local time for fractional Brownian motion, which was originally discussed in [Guo, J., Hu, Y., and Xiao, Y., Higher-order derivative of intersection local time for two independent fractional Brownian motions, Journal of Theoretical Probability 32, (2019), pp. 1190-1201]. We also show that the k k -th derivative of the intersection and self-intersection local times of fractional Brownian motion are exponentially integrable for certain parameter values. In addition, we show convergence in distribution when the existence condition is violated for the k k -th derivative of self-intersection local time of fractional Brownian motion under scaling.



Keywords: Brownian motion; local time; self-intersection local time; derivatives of self-intersection local time; fractional Brownian motion; exponential integrability, Malliavin calculus, Central limit theorem.

1.
Introduction and main results

Let
B_{t}
be a Brownian motion for the time being, and consider the following functional introduced in
[
26
,
27
,
25
]
,

\displaystyle A(T,B_{T})=\int_{0}^{T}1_{[0,\infty)}(B_{T}-B_{s})ds.

A formal application of Itô’s formula, using
\frac{d}{dx}1_{[0,\infty)}(x)=\delta(x),\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}1_{[0,\infty)}(x)=\delta^{\prime}(x)
with
\delta
the Dirac delta function, leads to a Tanaka-style formula containing the following expression:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\delta^{\prime}(B_{t}-B_{s})dsdt,

This motivated the influential work
[
30
]
, where existence of this process, known as the derivative of self-intersection local time (DSLT) of Brownian motion, was rigorously proved, and a number of properties of the process provided. The corresponding Tanaka formula was also stated as a formal identity in that paper, although later (
[
21
]
) the following slightly different formula was rigorously proved:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}\delta^{\prime}(B_{t}-B_{s})dsdt+\frac{1}{2}\text{sgn}(x)T=\int_{0}^{T}L_{s}^{B_{s}-x}dB_{s}-\int_{0}^{T}\text{sgn}(B_{T}-B_{s}-x)ds.

Since that time a lengthy sequence of papers devoted to DSLT by many authors have followed, many of which have focused on the same expression for processes other than Brownian motion. We will continue that study in this paper, with our interest being DSLT of fractional Brownian motion (fBm).

In what follows,
B^{H}
will denote a one-dimensional fBm with Hurst parameter
H
. The DSLT of fBm was first introduced by Yan, Yang, and Lu in
[
33
]
; however, as was noted in that paper, there are two natural versions of the DSLT of fBm. The first version is derived from the Tanaka formula, and was justified by Jung and Markowsky
[
16
]
. They showed that when the Hurst parameter
0<H<\tfrac{2}{3}
, the DSLT of fractional Brownian motion

-H\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\delta^{\prime}(B_{s}^{H}-B_{r}^{H})(s-r)^{2H-1}\,dr\,ds

exists in
L^{p}(\Omega)
, where
B^{H}
denotes a one-dimensional fractional Brownian motion. Note that the kernel
(s-r)^{2H-1}
is present due to the form of Ito’s formula for fBm.

The second version is derived from the occupation time formula and was also proven to exist under the same condition on the Hurst parameter by Jung and Markowsky
[
17
]
. Specifically, when
0<H<\tfrac{2}{3}
,

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\delta^{\prime}(B_{s}^{H}-B_{r}^{H})\,dr\,ds

exists in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. In this article, we will work only with this second version, i.e. without the kernel
(s-r)^{2H-1}
.

Inspired by the work above, Yu
[
37
]
showed that for
d
-dimensional fractional Brownian motion
B^{H}
, the
k
-th order DSLT

(-1)^{|k|}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\delta^{(k)}(B^{H}_{s}-B^{H}_{r})\,dr\,ds

exists in
L^{2}(\Omega)
when

H<\min\!\left(\tfrac{2}{2|k|+d},\;\tfrac{1}{|k|+d-\#},\;\tfrac{1}{d}\right),

and exists in
L^{p}(\Omega)
when

H|k|+Hd<1,

where
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
,
|k|=\sum_{j=1}^{d}k_{j}
, and
\#
denotes the number of odd
k_{i}
in
k
. For convenience, we neglect the constant term and denote the following as the DSLT of fractional Brownian motion:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{t}^{(k)}:=\int_{D}\delta^{(k)}(B_{s}^{H}-B^{H}_{r})\,dr\,ds=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\hat{\alpha}^{(k)}_{t,\epsilon}:=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{D}\delta^{(k)}_{\epsilon}(B_{s}^{H}-B_{r}^{H})\,dr\,ds,

where
D=\{(r,s)\,|\,0<r<s<t\}
. Following Yu’s work, a number of subsequent papers have studied these processes more closely; see
[
19
,
4
,
36
,
7
,
38
,
8
,
39
]
.

Another focus of this paper is the derivative of intersection local time (DILT) of fractional Brownian motion, which is formally defined as

(1.3)

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}^{(k)}:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta^{(k)}(B^{H}_{s}-\hat{B}^{H}_{r})\,dr\,ds,

where
B^{H}
and
\hat{B}^{H}
are two independent
d
-dimensional fractional Brownian motions with the same Hurst parameter
H
. Recall that a
d
-dimensional fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
, denoted by
B^{H}
, is a
d
-dimensional centered Gaussian process, continuous a.s., whose
d
components are independent copies of a one-dimensional fractional Brownian motion
B^{H,j}
,
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, with covariance function

\mathbb{E}[B^{H,j}_{t}B^{H,j}_{s}]=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(t^{2H}+s^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\big).

Note that when
H=\tfrac{1}{2}
, fractional Brownian motion reduces to standard Brownian motion. Other than this case, the increments of a fractional Brownian motion are not independent. Naturally, in order to rigorously define
\alpha
and
\hat{\alpha}
, one must begin with an approximate
\delta
function and then show convergence to a well defined process. To be precise, we let

\displaystyle\delta_{\epsilon}(x):=\frac{1}{(2\pi\epsilon)^{\frac{d}{2}}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2\epsilon}}

be our approximate
\delta
function, and it can be shown that
\delta_{\epsilon}
converges weakly to
\delta
as
\epsilon\to 0
. We then utilise the representation of
\delta_{\epsilon}
through the Fourier transform

\displaystyle\delta_{\epsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{i\langle p,x\rangle}e^{-\frac{\epsilon|p|^{2}}{2}}dp,

\displaystyle\delta^{(k)}_{\epsilon}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{i^{|k|}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}p^{k_{j}}_{j}e^{i\langle p,x\rangle}e^{-\frac{\epsilon|p|^{2}}{2}}dp,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the
d
-dimensional Euclidean inner product. For simplicity, we focus on
t=1
, and denote

(1.4)

\displaystyle\alpha^{(k)}:=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\delta^{(k)}(B^{H}_{s}-\hat{B}^{H}_{r})\,dr\,ds,

and define the mollified version

\displaystyle\alpha_{\epsilon}^{(k)}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\delta_{\epsilon}^{(k)}(B^{H}_{s}-\hat{B}^{H}_{r})\,dr\,ds,

(1.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{i^{|k|}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}p^{k_{j}}_{j}e^{ip(B_{s}^{H}-\hat{B}_{r}^{H})}e^{\frac{-\epsilon|p|^{2}}{2}}dpdrds,

where other cases can be obtained by scaling.
The existence of
\alpha^{(k)}
in
L^{p}(\Omega)
was discussed in
[
6
]
; however, unfortunately, an error was noted in their proof, and a counterexample to their result was discussed in
[
5
]
. Our first order of business will therefore be to give the correct range of existence for the process, which we do in our first result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
|k|=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}
. Suppose that
2|k|H+Hd<2
. Then
\alpha_{\epsilon}^{(k)}
defined in
section

1
converges in
L^{n}(\Omega)
as
\epsilon\to 0
for all
n\geq 1
.

Remark 1.2
.

We denote its limit by
\alpha^{(k)}
which is defined in
eq.

1.4
. When
|k|=0
, the existence condition reduces to
Hd<2
, which coincides with the critical existence condition in
[
24
]
.

Having established existence, we turn to the question of exponential integrability. We will say that a random variable
X
is
exponentially integrable
of order
\beta
if there exists a constant
M>0
such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\exp\{M|X|^{\beta}\}]<\infty
. Exponential integrability with
\beta=1
is equivalent to the existence of the moment generating function
M_{X}(t):={\mathbb{E}}[e^{tX}]
for
t
in a neighborhood of
0
, and can also be used to give strong tail estimates on the distribution of
X
. Exponential integrability of various flavors of intersection local time has been an important concept in mathematical physics, particular in relation to models of self attracting or self avoiding Brownian motion and polymer measures. More details can be found in the influential works
[
1
,
18
,
20
]
. In
[
5
]
, exponential integrability was provided for DILT and DSLT of Brownian motion and
\alpha
-stable processes, and we will extend these results to fBm, as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
|k|=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}
. Suppose that

2|k|H+Hd<2\quad\text{and}\quad\beta<\frac{1}{|k|+|k|H+dH}.

Then there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{M(\alpha^{(k)})^{\beta}}\right]<\infty.

We note that there are also studies of the DILT of two fractional Brownian motions with different Hurst parameters (see
[
6
,
9
,
10
,
34
]
), but in this article we will only consider the case where the two fractional Brownian motions have the same Hurst parameter. The property of local nondeterminism of Gaussian processes, first proposed by Berman
[
2
]
, plays an important role in analyzing the moments of DSLT. Local nondeterminism asserts that one cannot accurately anticipate the value of a Gaussian process at a point, no matter how close the available information is to that point. For the case of fractional Brownian motion, we will make use of the version of local nondeterminism established by Hu and Nualart
[
13
]
, which states that for
t,s,r\in[0,T]
, there exists a positive constant
\kappa
depending only on
H
and
T
such that

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\!\left(B_{t}^{H}\,\middle|\,B_{s}^{H},|t-s|>r\right)>\kappa r^{2H}.

We will provide a similar result for
\hat{\alpha}
. For simplicity, we focus on
t=1
, and denote

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}^{(k)}:=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{s}\delta^{(k)}(B^{H}_{s}-B^{H}_{r})\,dr\,ds,

since other cases can be obtained by scaling.

Theorem 1.4
.

When
H|k|+Hd<1
and
\beta<\frac{1}{|k|+|k|H+dH}
, there exists a constant
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{M(\hat{\alpha}^{(k)})^{\beta}}]<\infty,

where
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
and
|k|=k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}
.

It is also of interest to study limiting behavior of the mollified processes in the cases which do not converge. The idea here seems to have originated in
[
35
]
, which in turn was influenced by Varadhan’s celebrated renormalization of self-intersection local time of planar Brownian motion
Varadhan, 1969
. This has led to a large number of similar results, too many to list here; however, even in relation to DSLT of fractional Brownian motion, we can refer the reader to
[
28
,
22
,
29
,
3
,
14
,
15
,
32
,
36
,
39
]

We will prove similar theorems for the DSLT of fBm, as follows.

Theorem 1.5
.

If
d=2
and
\frac{1}{2}<H<1
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{2-\frac{1}{H}}\hat{\alpha}^{(1)}_{t,\epsilon}\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2}),

where
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\frac{(2H-1)t^{2H}}{H8\pi^{2}}B(2,2H-1)B(\frac{1}{H},\frac{4H-2}{2H})^{2}
, and
B(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Beta function.

Theorem 1.6
.

If
d=3
and
\frac{1}{2}<H<\frac{2}{3}
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{\frac{5}{2}-\frac{1}{H}}\hat{\alpha}_{t,\epsilon}^{(1)}\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{2}^{2})

where
\sigma_{2}^{2}=\frac{(2H-1)t^{2H}}{H16\pi^{3}}B(2,2H-1)B(\frac{1}{H},\frac{5H-2}{2H})^{2}
, and
B(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Beta function.

In the ensuing two sections we will prove these theorems, however they will require a significant amount of preliminaries and technical lemmas. We have placed this material into two appendices at the end of this article:
Appendix

Appendix

2.
Proofs of existence and exponential integrability, Theorems
1.1
,
1.3
, and
1.4
.

The heart of the matter is the following proposition, which provides the key estimate for
1.1
and
1.3
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
n
be even. If
2|k|H+Hd<2
, then there exists a constant

C_{H,d,|k|}>0
, depending only on
H,d,
and
|k|
, such that

I_{1}:=\int_{[0,1]^{2n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{nd}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{j}^{\intercal}A\xi_{j}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\prod_{l=1}^{n}|\xi_{lj}|^{k_{j}}\,d\xi\,ds\,dr\;\;\leq\;\;C_{H,d,|k|}^{\,n}(n!)^{\,|k|+|k|H+Hd},

where
A
is the covariance matrix of the random vector

\big(B^{H,1}_{s_{1}}-\hat{B}^{H,1}_{r_{1}},\dots,B^{H,1}_{s_{n}}-\hat{B}^{H,1}_{r_{n}}\big),

and
\xi_{j}=(\xi_{1j},\dots,\xi_{nj})^{\intercal}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Proof.

Clearly,
A
is symmetric and positive definite. Hence there exists a symmetric positive definite matrix
B=(b_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
such that
B^{2}=A^{-1}
.
Note that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\prod_{j=1}^{d}|\xi_{lj}|^{k_{j}}&\leq\prod_{j=1}^{d}|\sum_{m=1}^{d}\xi_{lm}^{2}|^{\frac{k_{j}}{2}}=\left|\sum_{m=1}^{d}\xi_{lm}^{2}\right|^{\frac{|k|}{2}}.\end{split}

As such, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{nd}}\int_{[0,1]^{2n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{j}^{{}^{\intercal}}A\xi_{j}}\prod_{l=1}^{n}\left|\sum_{m=1}^{d}\xi_{lm}^{2}\right|^{\frac{|k|}{2}}d\xi dsdr.

Now we change variables
A^{\frac{1}{2}}\xi_{j}=u_{j}
,
which yields

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{nd}}\int_{[0,1]^{2n}}\det(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}^{\intercal}u_{j}}\prod_{l=1}^{n}\left|\sum_{m=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{lj}u_{jm}\right)^{2}\right|^{\frac{|k|}{2}}dudsdr

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{nd}}\int_{[0,1]^{2n}}\det(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}^{\intercal}u_{j}}\prod_{l=1}^{n}\left|\sum_{m=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{lj}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{jm}^{2}\right|^{\frac{|k|}{2}}dudsdr,

where we apply the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the second inequality.
It is well known that

A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det(A)}C^{\intercal},

where
C=(c_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is the cofactor matrix of
A
. In particular,
c_{ll}=\det(A_{l})
, where
A_{l}
is the submatrix of
A
obtained by deleting its
l
-th row and
l
-th column. Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{lj}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{lj}b_{jl}=A^{-1}_{ll}=\frac{c_{ll}}{\det(A)}.

According to
Lemma

B.15
, for any permutation
\sigma
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\pi
of
\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{l\}
,

\displaystyle\det(A)

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(B^{H,1}_{s_{\sigma(1)}}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(1)}}^{H,1})\times\text{Var}(B^{H,1}_{s_{\sigma(2)}}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(2)}}^{H,1}|B^{H,1}_{s_{\sigma(1)}}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(1)}}^{H,1})\times\cdots

\displaystyle\quad\times\text{Var}(B^{H,1}_{s_{\sigma(n)}}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(n)}}^{H,1}|B^{H,1}_{s_{\sigma(j)}}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1},1\leq j\leq n-1),

\displaystyle c_{ll}

\displaystyle=\det(A_{l})=\prod_{j\neq l}^{n}\text{Var}(B_{s_{\pi(j)}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}_{r_{\pi(j)}}^{H,1}|B^{H,1}_{s_{\pi(i)}}-\hat{B}^{H,1}_{r_{\pi(i)}},i\in\{1,\dots,j-1\}\setminus\{l\}).

Therefore we can choose proper
\sigma
and
\pi
such that

\displaystyle\det(A)/c_{ll}=\text{Var}(B_{s_{l}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}^{H,1}_{r_{l}}|B_{s_{p}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}^{H,1}_{r_{p}},p\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{l\}).

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{nd}}\int_{[0,1]^{2n}}\det(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}d}\prod_{l=1}^{n}\left(\frac{c_{ll}}{\det(A)}\right)^{\frac{|k|}{2}}\times\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}^{\intercal}u_{j}}\left|\sum_{m=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}u^{2}_{jm}\right|^{\frac{n|k|}{2}}dudsdr

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{nd}}\int_{[0,1]^{2n}}\det(A)^{-\frac{1}{2}d}\prod_{l=1}^{n}\left(\text{Var}(B_{s_{l}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}^{H,1}_{r_{l}}|B_{s_{p}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}^{H,1}_{r_{p}},1\leq p\neq l\leq n)\right)^{-\frac{|k|}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{nd}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}^{\intercal}u_{j}}\left|\sum_{m=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}u^{2}_{jm}\right|^{\frac{n|k|}{2}}dudsdr.

Due to the independence between
B^{H}
and
\hat{B}^{H}
and
Lemma

B.3
, we have

\displaystyle\det(A)

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(B^{H,1}_{s_{\sigma(1)}}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(1)}}^{H,1})\times\text{Var}(B^{H,1}_{s_{\sigma(2)}}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(2)}}^{H,1}|B^{H,1}_{s_{\sigma(1)}}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(1)}}^{H,1})\times\cdots

\displaystyle\quad\times\text{Var}(B^{H,1}_{s_{\sigma(n)}}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(n)}}^{H,1}|B^{H,1}_{s_{\sigma(j)}}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1},1\leq j\leq n-1)

(2.2)

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}(\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|B_{s_{\sigma(k)}}^{H,1},1\leq k\leq j-1,\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j)

\displaystyle\quad+\text{Var}(\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|B_{s_{\sigma(k)}}^{H,1},1\leq k\leq j,\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j-1))

(2.3)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{j=1}^{n}\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|B^{H,1}_{s_{\sigma(m)}},1\leq m\leq j-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{Var}(\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|\hat{B}^{H,1}_{r_{\sigma(m)}},1\leq m\leq j-1)^{\frac{1}{2}}

for any permutation
\sigma
. We now give brief explanation for the two inequalities above. For the first inequality
eq.

2.2
we have used
Lemma

B.3
as follows

\displaystyle\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}^{H,1}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}|B_{s_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j-1)\geq

\displaystyle\max(

\displaystyle\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|B_{s_{\sigma(k)}}^{H,1},1\leq k\leq j-1,\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j)

,

\displaystyle\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|B_{s_{\sigma(k)}}^{H,1},1\leq k\leq j,\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j-1)),

which implies

\displaystyle 2\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}^{H,1}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}|B_{s_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j-1)

\displaystyle\geq\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|B_{s_{\sigma(k)}}^{H,1},1\leq k\leq j-1,\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j)

\displaystyle\quad+\text{Var}(\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|B_{s_{\sigma(k)}}^{H,1},1\leq k\leq j,\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j-1)

for
2\leq j\leq n
. Note that

\displaystyle\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(1)}}^{H,1}-\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(1)}}^{H,1})

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(1)}}^{H,1})+\text{Var}(\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(1)}}^{H,1})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(1)}}^{H,1})+\text{Var}(\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(1)}}^{H,1})),

and the first inequality
eq.

2.2
follows. For the second inequality
eq.

2.3
, since the natural filtrations of
B^{H,1}
and
\hat{B}^{H,1}
are independent, the
\sigma

is independent of the one generated by
\hat{B}^{H,1}_{r_{\sigma(1)}},\dots,\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}
for all
1\leq j\leq n
. Hence by
Lemma

B.9
together with
a+b\geq 2\sqrt{ab}
for all
a,b\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|B_{s_{\sigma(k)}}^{H,1},1\leq k\leq j-1,\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j)+\text{Var}(\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|B_{s_{\sigma(k)}}^{H,1},1\leq k\leq j,\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j-1)

\displaystyle\geq 2\sqrt{\text{Var}(B_{s_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|B_{s_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j-1)\text{Var}(\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(j)}}^{H,1}|\hat{B}_{r_{\sigma(i)}}^{H,1},1\leq i\leq j-1)}

for all
1\leq j\leq n
. As such, the second inequality
eq.

2.3
follows.
Denote by
\Phi_{n}
the set of all permutations of
\{1,\dots,n\}
, and
\Delta_{\sigma}^{n}=\{(s_{\sigma(1)},\dots,s_{\sigma(n)})\in[0,1]^{n};0\leq s_{\sigma(1)}\leq\cdots\leq s_{\sigma(n)}\leq 1\}
.

Applying

\displaystyle|\epsilon I+\Sigma|

\displaystyle=(\epsilon+\Sigma_{1,1})(\epsilon+\Sigma_{2,2})-\Sigma_{1,2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq C\left[\epsilon^{2}+\epsilon((a+b)^{2H}+(b+c)^{2H})+a^{2H}(c+b)^{2H}+c^{2H}(a+b)^{2H}\right]

\displaystyle\geq C[\epsilon^{2}+(a+b)^{H}(b+c)^{H}(\epsilon+a^{H}c^{H})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}((a+b)^{2H}+(b+c)^{2H}-a^{2H}-c^{2H})

\displaystyle=Hb\int_{0}^{1}((a+bv)^{2H-1}+(c+bv)^{2H-1})dv

\displaystyle|\epsilon I+\Sigma|

Appendix A
Malliavin calculus preliminaries

In this article, the Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
discussed are separable with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. We denote the norm of an element
h\in\mathcal{H}
by
||\cdot||_{\mathcal{H}}
.

We say a stochastic process
W=\{W(h);h\in\mathcal{H}\}
defined on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is an isonormal Gaussian process if
W
is a centered Gaussian family of random variables with
\mathbb{E}[W(h)W(g)]=\langle h,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
for all
h,g\in\mathcal{H}
.

Let
H_{q}
denote the
q
-th Hermite polynomial, defined as

\displaystyle H_{q}(x)=(-1)^{q}e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\frac{d^{q}}{dx^{q}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}},\quad q\geq 1,

and
H_{0}(x)=1
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two jointly Gaussian random variables with mean zero and variance
1
, then for
n,m\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(X)H_{m}(Y)]=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $m\neq n$ }\\
n!\mathbb{E}[XY]^{n}&\text{ if }m=n.\end{cases}

Definition A.2
.

A topological vector space
A
is said to be a total subset of
B
if

\displaystyle\overline{\text{Span}(A)}=B.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the collection of random variables
\{W(h);h\in\mathcal{H}\}
.

Lemma A.3
.

The random variables
\{e^{W(h)};h\in\mathcal{H}\}
form a total subset of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})
.

Definition A.4
.

Denote by
\mathbb{H}_{n}
the closed subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})
generated by the random variables
\{H_{n}(W(h));h\in\mathcal{H},||h||_{\mathcal{H}}=1\}
with
n\geq 0
, that is

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{n}=\overline{\text{Span}(\{H_{n}(W(h));h\in\mathcal{H},||h||_{\mathcal{H}}=1\})}.

Theorem A.5
.

The space
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})
can be decomposed as an infinite direct sum of subspaces
\mathbb{H}_{n}
:

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{H}_{n}.

Let
C_{p}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
be the set of infinitely differentiable functions
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that all of its partial derivatives have at most polynomial growth. Denote by
S
the class of smooth random variables that has a form

\displaystyle F=f(W(h_{1}),\dots,W(h_{n}))

with
f\in C_{p}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
. We will use notation
\partial_{i}f
to denote
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}
.

Definition A.6
.

The derivative of a smooth random variable
F\in S
is an
\mathcal{H}
valued random variable:

\displaystyle DF=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}f(W(h_{1}),\dots,W(h_{n}))h_{i}.

Proposition A.7
.

The operator
D
is closable from
L^{p}(\Omega)
to
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathcal{H})
for all
p\geq 1
.

We denote the domain of the operator
D
in
L^{p}(\Omega)
by
\mathbb{D}^{1,p}
meaning it is the closure of
S
with respect to the semi-norm defined as

\displaystyle||F||_{1,p}=\left|\mathbb{E}[|F|^{p}]+\mathbb{E}[||DF||_{\mathcal{H}}^{p}]\right|^{\frac{1}{p}}.

The
k
-th iteration of
D
for a smooth random variable
F
is denoted as
D^{k}F
, which is an
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}
valued random variable. As such
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
is the closure of
S
with respective to the semi-norm

\displaystyle||F||_{k,p}=\left|\mathbb{E}[|F|^{p}]+\mathbb{E}[||D^{k}F||_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{p}]\right|^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Denote

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}^{\infty}=\bigcap_{k=1,p=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{D}^{k,p}.

For more details in Malliavin Calculus, please refer to
[
23
]
.

The
k
th DSLT of fractional Brownian motion is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\hat{\alpha}^{(k)}_{t,\epsilon}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{D}\delta^{(k)}_{\epsilon}(B_{s}^{H}-B_{r}^{H})drds,

where
D=\{(r,s)|0<r<s<t\}
,
\{B_{t}^{H}=(B^{H,1}_{t},\dots,B^{H,d}_{t})\}_{t\geq 0}
is a
d
-dimensional fractional Brownian motion
with
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})
and
|k|=\sum_{j=1}^{d}k_{j}
. Consider the space of indicator functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\{1_{[a,b]};a,b\in{\mathbb{R}},a\leq b\}.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the Hilbert space obtained by completing
\mathcal{L}
with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\langle 1_{[a,b]},1_{[c,d]}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{E}[(B^{H,1}_{b}-B_{a}^{H,1})(B^{H,1}_{d}-B_{c}^{H,1})].

For all
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
, we define

\displaystyle B^{H}(f)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}B^{H,j}(f_{j}).

Each
B^{H,j}(\cdot)
is the isonormal Gaussian process with the associated Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
As such
B^{H}(\cdot)
is an isometry from
\mathcal{H}^{d}
to the Gaussian subspace of
L^{2}({\Omega})
generated by the
d
-dimensional fractional Brownian motion. The
q
-th Wiener chaos of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, denoted as
\mathbb{H}_{q}
, is a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega)
generated by the random variables

\displaystyle\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{d}H_{q_{j}}(B^{H,j}(f_{j}));\sum_{j=1}^{d}q_{j}=q,f_{j}\in\mathcal{H},||f_{j}||_{\mathcal{H}}=1\right\},

where
H_{q}
is the
q
th Hermite polynomial. For every
q\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
(\mathcal{H}^{d})^{\otimes q}
the
q
-th tensor product of
\mathcal{H}^{d}
.

For
f^{1},\dots,f^{q}\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
of the form
f^{i}=(f_{1}^{i},\dots,f_{d}^{i})
with
1\leq i\leq q
,
f^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f^{q}
can be defined as a multi-dimensional array:

(A.1)

\displaystyle f^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f^{q}=(f{{}_{i_{1}}}^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{i_{q}}^{q})_{i_{1},\dots,i_{q}=1,\dots,d}.

The tensor product
eq.

A.1
is isomorphic to following form of the tensor product:

\displaystyle f^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes f^{q}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{q}=1}^{d}F_{i_{1}}^{1}\otimes F_{i_{2}}^{2}\otimes\dots\otimes F_{i_{q}}^{q}

where
F_{i}^{j}=(0,\dots,f_{i}^{j},\dots,0)
is a tuple of size
d
, which is equal to
f_{i}^{j}
in the
i
-th position, and zero elsewhere.

In the special case that
f^{1}=f^{2}=\dots=f^{q}
, we then have

(A.2)

\displaystyle f^{\otimes q}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{q}=1}^{d}F_{i_{1}}\otimes F_{i_{2}}\otimes\dots\otimes F_{i_{q}}

where
F_{i}=(0,\dots,f_{i},0,\dots,0)
is a tuple of size
d
, which is equal to
f_{i}
in the
i
-th position, and zero elsewhere.
We will prefer to use this form of the tensor product, as handling sums is more computationally convenient than multi-dimensional arrays.
Denote the symmetrization of
(\mathcal{H}^{d})^{\otimes q}
by
(\mathcal{H}^{d})^{\odot q}
. Let
f\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
be of the form
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{d})
with
||f_{j}||_{\mathcal{H}}=1
. Such
f^{\otimes q}
belongs to
(\mathcal{H}^{d})^{\odot q}
, and we can define a mapping
I_{q}:(\mathcal{H}^{d})^{\odot q}\to\mathbb{H}_{q}
as follows

\displaystyle I_{q}(f^{\otimes q})=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{q}=1}^{d}\sqrt{q_{1}(i_{1},\dots,i_{q})!\cdots q_{d}(i_{1},\dots,i_{q})!}\prod_{j=1}^{d}H_{q_{j}(i_{1},\dots,i_{q})}(B^{H,j}(f_{j})),j=1,\dots,d

where
q_{j}(i_{1},\dots,i_{q})
denotes the number of indices in
(i_{1},\dots,i_{q})
equal to
j
. This mapping is a linear isometry between
(\mathcal{H}^{d})^{\odot q}
and
\mathbb{H}_{q}
. Thus, by
A.5
, any square integrable random variable
F
which is measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the fractional Brownian motion will have a chaos expansion of the type

\displaystyle F=\mathbb{E}[F]+\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}I_{q}(g_{q})

for some
g_{q}\in(\mathcal{H}^{d})^{\odot q}
.

Lemma A.8
.

Let
k=(|k|,0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
d\in\mathbb{N}
and

being odd. Then

defined in

possesses a Wiener chaos expansion,

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}^{(k)}_{t,\epsilon}=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}I_{2q-1}(f^{(k)}_{2q-1,t,\epsilon}),

where

\displaystyle f^{(k)}_{q,t,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\frac{|k|+q}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\sum_{i_{1}=1,\dots,i_{q}=1}^{d}\int_{D}h_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{i_{q}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{(|k|+q_{1}(i_{1},\dots,i_{q})-1)!!\times\cdots\times(q_{d}(i_{1},\dots,i_{q})-1)!!}{((s-r)^{2H}+\epsilon)^{\frac{|k|+q+d}{2}}}drds

with
h_{i}=(0,\dots,1_{[r,s]},\dots,0)\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
which is zero everywhere except at its
i
-th entry.

Remark A.9
.

The proof here adopts similar techniques in Lemma 7 in
[

]
, Appendix
A
in
[
4
]
and Lemma 2.2 in
[

]
. Since we are interested in the limit theorem of

in which
|k|=1
. We assume
k=(|k|,0,\dots,0)
and
|k|\geq 1
is odd.

Proof.

Note that we can rewrite the derivative of self-intersection local time of fractional Brownian motion as

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}^{(k)}_{t,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{i^{|k|}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{D}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}^{k_{j}}e^{ip_{j}(B_{s}^{j,H}-B_{r}^{j,H})}e^{-\epsilon\frac{|p|^{2}}{2}}dpdrds

\displaystyle=\frac{i^{|k|}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{D}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}^{k_{j}}e^{B^{H}(h)}e^{-\epsilon\frac{|p|^{2}}{2}}dpdrds,

where

.
e^{B^{H}(h)}
is obviously in
\mathbb{D}^{\infty}
, and its

\displaystyle D^{q}e^{B(h)}=e^{B(h)}h^{\otimes q},

where

is defined through

(A.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}h^{\otimes q}&=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{q}=1}^{d}(0,..,ip_{i_{1}}1_{[r,s]},\dots 0)\otimes\cdots\otimes(0,\dots,ip_{i_{q}}1_{[r,s]},\dots,0)\\

where

only has non zero at its

-th entry.
Thus, by Stroock’s formula, the chaos expansion for

is

\displaystyle\alpha^{(k)}_{t,\epsilon}=\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t,\epsilon}^{(k)}]+\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}I_{q}(f^{(k)}_{q,t,\epsilon}),

where

\displaystyle f^{(k)}_{q,t,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{i^{|k|}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{D}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}^{k_{j}}\frac{1}{q!}\mathbb{E}[D^{q}e^{B^{H}(h)}]e^{-\epsilon\frac{|p|^{2}}{2}}dpdrds

\displaystyle=\frac{i^{|k|}}{(q!)(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{D}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}^{k_{j}}\mathbb{E}[e^{B^{H}(h)}]h^{\otimes q}e^{-\epsilon\frac{|p|^{2}}{2}}dpdrds

\displaystyle=\frac{i^{|k|}}{(q!)(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{D}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}^{k_{j}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}p^{2}_{j}((s-r)^{2H}+\epsilon)}h^{\otimes q}dpdrds.

Then by

A.3

\displaystyle f^{(k)}_{q,t,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{i^{|k|}}{(q!)(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{D}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}p_{1}^{|k|}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}^{2}((s-r)^{2H}+\epsilon)}h^{\otimes q}dpdrds

\displaystyle=\frac{i^{|k|+q}}{(q!)(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{i_{1}=1,\dotsi_{q}=1}^{d}\int_{D}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}p_{1}^{|k|+q_{1}(i_{1},\dots,i_{q})}\cdots p_{d}^{q_{d}(i_{1},\dots,i_{q})}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}^{2}((s-r)^{2H}+\epsilon)}

\displaystyle\times h_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{i_{q}}dpdrds.

Note that

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}p_{1}^{|k|+q_{1}}\cdots p_{d}^{q_{d}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}^{2}((s-r)^{2H}+\epsilon)}dp=\frac{(|k|+q_{1}-1)!!\times\cdots\times(q_{d}-1)!!}{((s-r)^{2H}+\epsilon)^{\frac{|k|+q+d}{2}}}(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}d}

when

are all even, otherwise it is equal to 0. Consequently,

is 0 when

is even. Thus, when

is odd, we have

\displaystyle f^{(k)}_{q,t,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\frac{|k|+q}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\sum_{i_{1}=1,\dots,i_{q}=1}^{d}\int_{D}h_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes h_{i_{q}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{(|k|+q_{1}(i_{1},\dots,i_{q})-1)!!\times\cdots\times(q_{d}(i_{1},\dots,i_{q})-1)!!}{((s-r)^{2H}+\epsilon)^{\frac{|k|+q+d}{2}}}drds.

Hence when
|k|
is odd, we have

(A.4)

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}^{(k)}_{t,\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\alpha}_{t,\epsilon}^{(k)}]+\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}I_{2q-1}(f^{(k)}_{2q-1,t,\epsilon})

(A.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}I_{2q-1}(f^{(k)}_{2q-1,t,\epsilon}),

as one can easily verify that

∎

Lemma B.6
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random variables. Then

for some

such that
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{2p-q}X_{m}^{q}]

.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{p-q}X_{m}^{q}]
converges to some

as

for all
0\leq q\leq 2p
. Obviously it implies

. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X_{n}-X_{m}|^{2p}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(X_{n}-X_{m})^{2p}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=0}^{2p}{2p\choose q}(-1)^{q}\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{q}X_{m}^{2p-q}].

Letting

converge to

\displaystyle\lim_{n,m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|X_{n}-X_{m}|^{2p}]=r\sum_{q=1}^{2p}(-1)^{q}{2p\choose q}=r(1-1)^{2p}=0,

which implies

.

Lemma B.9
.

Let
X
be an integrable random variable on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{F}
be two
\sigma
-algebras. Assume that
\sigma(\sigma(X)\cup\mathcal{H})
is independent of
\mathcal{G}
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X|\sigma(\mathcal{G\cup\mathcal{H}})]=\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{H}]

almost surely.

Proof.

It suffices to show that

•

\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{H}]
is
\sigma(\mathcal{G\cup\mathcal{H}})
measurable,

•

\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{H}]
is integrable,

•

for all
A\in\sigma(\mathcal{G\cup\mathcal{H}}):

\displaystyle\int_{A}\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{H}]d\mathbb{P}=\int_{A}Xd\mathbb{P}.

The first two are trivial as
\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{H}]
is
\mathcal{H}
measurable and
X
is integrable. Note that

\sigma(\mathcal{G}\cup\mathcal{H})=\sigma(\{E\cap F;E\in\mathcal{G},F\in\mathcal{H}\})
, it therefore suffices to show for all
E\in\mathcal{G},F\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{E\cap F}\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{H}]d\mathbb{P}=\int_{E\cap F}Xd\mathbb{P}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{H}]1_{F}
is
\mathcal{H}
measurable and consequently is
\sigma(\sigma(X)\cup\mathcal{H})
measurable, by independence we have

\displaystyle\int_{E\cap F}\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{H}]d\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[1_{F}1_{E}\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{H}]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[1_{E}]\mathbb{E}[1_{F}\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{H}]]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[1_{E}]\mathbb{E}[1_{F}X].

Since
1_{F}X
is
\sigma(\sigma(X)\cup\mathcal{H})
measurable, then by independence we have

\displaystyle\int_{E\cap F}\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{H}]d\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[1_{E}1_{F}X]

\displaystyle=\int_{E\cap F}Xd\mathbb{P}

as required.
∎

Lemma B.10
.

Let
X
be a continuous random variable defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Then for any Borel set
A
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{A}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{i(X-x)p}e^{-\frac{\epsilon p^{2}}{2}}dpdx\to 1_{A}(X)

in
L^{n}(\Omega)
as
\epsilon\to 0
for
1\leq n<\infty
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\int_{A}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{i(X-x)p}e^{-\frac{\epsilon p^{2}}{2}}dpdx
and
1_{A}(X)
are in
L^{n}(\Omega)
. Since the intervals generate Borel sets, it suffices to show that for any interval
(a,b)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\int_{a}^{b}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{i(X-x)p}e^{-\frac{\epsilon p^{2}}{2}}dpdx-1_{(a,b)}(X)\right|^{n}\right]

converges to
0
as
\epsilon\to 0
. By
Lemma

B.6
and
Lemma

B.8
, it is enough to show

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left(\int_{a}^{b}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{i(X-x)p}e^{-\frac{\epsilon_{1}p^{2}}{2}}dpdx\right)^{n-m}\left(\int_{a}^{b}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{i(X-x)p}e^{-\frac{\epsilon_{2}p^{2}}{2}}dpdx\right)^{m}]\to\mathbb{E}[1_{(a,b)}(X)]

for all even
n
and
1\leq m\leq n
as
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\to 0
. Let
F(x)
be the cumulative function induced by
\mathbb{P}(X<x)
which is continuous in this case, then by Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left(\int_{a}^{b}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{i(X-x)p}e^{-\frac{\epsilon_{1}p^{2}}{2}}dpdx\right)^{n-m}\left(\int_{a}^{b}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{i(X-x)p}e^{-\frac{\epsilon_{2}p^{2}}{2}}dpdx\right)^{m}]

\displaystyle=\int_{(a,b)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\mathbb{E}[e^{i\sum_{j=1}^{n}(X-x_{j})p_{j}}]e^{-\frac{\epsilon_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}p_{j}^{2}+\epsilon\sum_{j=n-m+1}^{n}p_{j}^{2}}{2}}dpdx

\displaystyle=\int_{(a,b)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{i\sum_{j=1}^{n}(y-x_{j})p_{j}}e^{-\frac{\epsilon_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}p_{j}^{2}+\epsilon\sum_{j=n-m+1}^{n}p_{j}^{2}}{2}}dF(y)dpdx

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{(a,b)^{n}}\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon_{1}})^{n-m}}\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon_{2}})^{m}}e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{n-m}\frac{(y-x_{j})^{2}}{2\epsilon_{1}}}e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(y-x_{j})^{2}}{2\epsilon_{2}}}dxdF(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{\epsilon_{1}}^{n-m}(y)f_{\epsilon_{2}}^{m}(y)dF(y),

where

is the probability of a standard normal random variable

staying between
(\frac{a-y}{\sqrt{\epsilon}},\frac{b-y}{\sqrt{\epsilon}})
. Note that, when

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}f_{\epsilon}(y)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{P}(\frac{a-y}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}<Z<\frac{b-y}{\sqrt{\epsilon}})=1,

when

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}f_{\epsilon}(y)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{P}(\frac{a-y}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}<Z<\frac{b-y}{\sqrt{\epsilon}})=0.

Since

is bounded by

, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon_{1}\to 0\epsilon_{2}\to 0}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f_{\epsilon_{1}}^{n-m}(y)f_{\epsilon_{2}}^{m}(y)dF(y)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon_{1}\to 0\epsilon_{2}\to 0}\int_{(a,b)}f_{\epsilon_{1}}^{n-m}(y)f_{\epsilon_{2}}^{m}(y)dF(y)

\displaystyle\quad+\lim_{\epsilon_{1}\to 0\epsilon_{2}\to 0}\int_{(-\infty,a)\cup(b,\infty)}f_{\epsilon_{1}}^{n-m}(y)f_{\epsilon_{2}}^{m}(y)dF(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}dF(y)=\mathbb{E}[1_{(a,b)}(X)]

as required.
∎

Lemma B.13

Let

Proof.

Suppose that
\sigma(Y)
is independent of
\sigma(\sigma(X_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\sigma(X_{n}))
,
Y
is of course independent of any combination of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
as they are
\sigma(\sigma(X_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\sigma(X_{n}))
measurable. Suppose that
Y
is independent of any linear combination of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. Note that

\displaystyle\sigma(\sigma(X_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\sigma(X_{n}))=\sigma(\{\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}E_{j};E_{j}\in\sigma(X_{j}),1\leq j\leq n\}),

and
\{X^{-1}_{j}(B);B\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}})\}=\sigma(X_{j})
. It suffices to show that for all Boreal sets
A
and
\{B_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[1_{\{Y\in A\}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{j}\in B_{j}\}}]=\mathbb{E}[1_{\{Y\in A\}}]\mathbb{E}[\prod_{j=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{j}\in B_{j}\}}].

By Fubini’s theorem and
Lemma

B.10
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[1_{\{Y\in A\}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{j}\in B_{j}\}}]=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n+1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\times A\times B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{n}}\mathbb{E}[e^{i(Y-y)p+\sum_{j=1}^{n}i(X_{j}-x_{j})q_{j}}]dydpdxdq.

Since
Y
is independent of all linear combinations of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{i(Y-y)p+\sum_{j=1}^{n}i(X_{j}-x_{j})q_{j}}]=\mathbb{E}[e^{i(Y-y)p}]\mathbb{E}[e^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}i(X_{j}-x_{j})q_{j}}].

Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[1_{\{Y\in A\}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{j}\in B_{j}\}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n+1}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\times A\times B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{n}}\mathbb{E}[e^{i(Y-y)p}]\mathbb{E}[e^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}i(X_{j}-x_{j})q_{j}}]dpdydxdq

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}\times A}\mathbb{E}[e^{i(Y-y)p}]dpdy\times\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{n}}\mathbb{E}[e^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}i(X_{j}-x_{j})q_{j}}]dxdq

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[1_{\{Y\in A\}}]\mathbb{E}[\prod_{j=1}^{n}1_{\{X_{j}\in B_{j}\}}],

where the last equality is also an application of Fubini’s theorem.
∎

Lemma B.14
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
be a tuple of jointly Gaussian random variables. For each
X_{j}
, there exists a tuple of real numbers
\{a_{i}\}_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{j}|X_{i},1\leq i\neq j\leq n]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]+\sum_{i\neq j}a_{i}X_{i},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]-\sum_{i\neq j}a_{i}X_{i})X_{k}]

\displaystyle=0,\forall k\neq j,

\displaystyle\sigma(X_{j}-\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]-\sum_{i\neq j}a_{i}X_{i})

\displaystyle\perp\!\!\!\perp\sigma(\sigma(X_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\sigma(X_{n-1})).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that all the random variables are centred, and pick
X_{n}
as a representative. Let
\Sigma
be the covariance matrix of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}
and denote

\displaystyle b=\left(\text{Cov}(X_{1},X_{n}),\dots,\text{Cov}(X_{n-1},X_{n})\right)^{\intercal}.

Since
\Sigma
is symmetric positive definite, we can write

\displaystyle a=\Sigma^{-1}b,

and one can verify that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}X_{i})X_{k}]=0,\forall 1\leq k\leq n-1.

Consequently
X_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}X_{i}
is independent of any linear combination of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}
by
Lemma

B.12
. Then by
Lemma

B.13
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma(X_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}X_{i})\perp\!\!\!\perp\sigma(\sigma(X_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\sigma(X_{n-1})).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}X_{i}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}X_{i}]=0,

which implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{n}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_{i}X_{i}.

∎

Lemma B.15
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
be a tuple of jointly centred Gaussian random variables, and
A_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is the associated covariance matrix. We have

(B.1)

\displaystyle\det(A_{n})=\text{Var}(X_{\pi(1)})\text{Var}(X_{\pi(2)}|X_{\pi(1)})\cdots\text{Var}(X_{\pi(n)}|X_{\pi(1)},\dots,X_{\pi(n-1)}),

where
\pi
is any permutation of
\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show

(B.2)

\displaystyle\det(A_{n})=\text{Var}(X_{1})\text{Var}(X_{2}|X_{2})\cdots\text{Var}(X_{n}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1}),

as we can shuffle the conditional variances by relabeling
X_{i}
.
We will proceed by induction, assuming that
n=1
, the covariance matrix is a scalar that is the variance of
X_{1}
. Thus the base case is satisfied. Suppose
eq.

B.2
holds for
n
, the covariance matrix
A_{n+1}
of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n+1}
has a form as follows:

\displaystyle A_{n+1}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{n}&b\\
b^{\intercal}&\text{Var}(X_{n+1})\end{bmatrix},

where

\displaystyle b^{\intercal}=(\text{Cov}(X_{n+1},X_{1}),\dots,\text{Cov}(X_{n+1},X_{n})).

By the formula of determinant of a block matrix, we have

\displaystyle\det{A_{n+1}}=\det(A_{n})\det(\text{Var}(X_{n+1})-b^{\intercal}A^{-1}_{n}b).

By
Lemma

B.14
, there exists
a=(a_{1},..,a_{n})^{\intercal}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X_{n+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i})X_{k}]

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall 1\leq k\leq n,

(B.3)

\displaystyle\sigma(X_{j}-\sum_{i\neq j}a_{i}X_{i})

\displaystyle\perp\!\!\!\perp\sigma(\sigma(X_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\sigma(X_{n}))

This implies

(B.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X_{n+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i})\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}X_{j}]=0,

and

\displaystyle b_{k}

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}(X_{n+1},X_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}X_{k}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}X_{k}]\implies b=A_{n}a.

Since
A_{n}
is symmetric positive definite, we have

(B.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}b^{\intercal}A_{n}^{-1}b&=b^{\intercal}a\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\text{Cov}(X_{n+1},X_{i})\\
&=\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i}].\end{split}

As such, we have

\displaystyle\text{Var}(X_{n+1}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(X_{n+1}-\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n}])^{2}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(X_{n+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i})^{2}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(X_{n+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}^{2}-X_{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}X_{i}]

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(X_{n+1})-b^{\intercal}A_{n}^{-1}b,

where we use
lemma

B.15
in the third equality,
eq.

B.4
in the fourth inequality, and
eq.

B.5
in the last equality. Therefore,

\displaystyle\det{A_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\det(A_{n})\det(\text{Var}(X_{n+1})-b^{\intercal}A^{-1}_{n}b)

\displaystyle=\text{Var}(X_{1})\text{Var}(X_{2}|X_{2})\cdots\text{Var}(X_{n}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1})\text{Var}(X_{n+1}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n})

as required.
∎
