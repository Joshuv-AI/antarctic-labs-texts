Debulking Severe Femoropopliteal Calcification
Authors: Sumit Bhatnagar, Ankit Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Severe arterial calcification poses the greatest challenge to long-lasting femoropopliteal endovascular treatment. The presence of calcium makes the vessel rigid, hinders balloon expansion, and reduces the effectiveness of antiproliferative drugs from a drug-coated balloon (DCB) on the arterial wall. In lesions rated as grade 4 on the Peripheral Arterial Calcium Scoring System (PACSS), restenosis and the need for repeat revascularization are frequent, even with modern DCB therapy.1 The logical solution is to eliminate the calcium before administering the drug, and rotational atherectomy with active aspiration, such as the Jetstream system, has gained popularity for this purpose. However, intuition does not always equate to lasting benefits, and the field still lacks robust evidence to determine when debulking is beneficial, when it merely adds cost and risk, and when it may cause harm to the patient. In fact, the 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend drug-eluting therapy as the preferred strategy for femoropopliteal disease (Class IIa, Level of Evidence: A) but do not specifically endorse routine atherectomy, highlighting this evidence gap.2 In this issue of JACC: Asia, Tokuda et al3 report a multicenter, retrospective, propensity score–matched study comparing Jetstream atherectomy plus DCB with DCB alone in the most heavily calcified lesions (PACSS grade 3-4). From 582 new lesions treated at 8 Japanese centers, 153 well-matched pairs (standardized mean differences all <0.10) were identified and monitored for a median of 439 days. The study population is noteworthy: over one-half were undergoing hemodialysis, and approximately one-third had chronic limb-threatening ischemia, representing a high-risk, real-world group in which medial calcification is prevalent and achieving durability is challenging.

What the Study Contributes

To date, this is one of the largest matched comparisons focused specifically on severe calcification and treated with a contemporary "atherectomy-then-DCB" strategy, and its central message is nuanced rather than promotional. Atherectomy delivered a genuine acute mechanical advantage: postprocedural residual stenosis was lower (16.7% vs 20.3%; P = 0.019), and the proportion of lesions left with >30% residual narrowing was more than halved (5.9% vs 14.4%; P = 0.012). Importantly, this luminal gain came without an excess of severe (≥ type D) dissection (2.6% vs 3.9%; P = 0.52), countering the concern that aggressive debulking of rigid plaque inevitably fractures the wall.4,5 Early patency mirrored the acute result, with a 1-year primary patency of 86.2% vs 75.1% (P = 0.01).6,7 The PANDA (limb Salvage PAteNcy rate after Directional Atherectomy combined with Drug Coated Balloon Angioplasty in Lower extremities) trial reported 1-year patency rates exceeding 88% with TurboHawk directional atherectomy plus DCB,7 consistent with the results of the current study.

If the narrative had concluded at that point, atherectomy might have been hailed as the solution. However, it continues. The predetermined primary endpoint, which was the 2-year primary patency, showed no significant difference (64.4% compared with 57.1%; P = 0.07). The rate of freedom from clinically driven target lesion revascularization, arguably the most crucial endpoint for patients, was nearly the same between the groups (HR: ∼1.0), as were the incidences of acute limb ischemia and major amputation. Essentially, the initial angiographic benefit diminished over 2 years and failed to translate into a tangible clinical advantage. The authors describe this as a "biological ceiling," and this interpretation holds weight: while mechanical intervention can alter the lumen during the procedure, it cannot reverse the medial calcification, chronic inflammation, and adverse remodeling that ultimately determine the outcome of these vessels.8

This trajectory closely aligns with the broader literature. The randomized JET-RANGER (Jetstream atherectomy followed by Paclitaxel coated balloon [RANGER] vs Balloon angioplasty followed by paclitaxel coated balloons) study—the only prospective randomized comparison of Jetstream-plus-DCB against balloon-plus-DCB—was halted early and remains exploratory (47 patients), but it too showed comparable freedom from reintervention between strategies, with atherectomy's main advantage being a reduced need for bailout stenting.8,9 Tokuda et al3 now supply a larger, calcification-enriched, real-world data set that JET-RANGER could not, and they arrive at a strikingly consistent conclusion: acute optimization is real, but durable clinical superiority is not.

Reading the Effect Estimate Honestly

An authoritative reading of this paper requires careful attention to the statistics, and the authors are commendably transparent. In the base propensity-adjusted model, with treatment as the sole covariate, atherectomy was not significantly associated with patency (HR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.41-1.15; P = 0.15). Significance emerged only after sequential adjustment for procedural and acute angiographic variables (HR: 0.56; 95% CI: 0.31-1.00; P = 0.05), an estimate whose CI touched 1.00 and whose P value sat exactly at the threshold. Because residual stenosis and dissection plausibly lie on the causal pathway between debulking and restenosis, adjusting for them may risk overadjustment and yield a mechanistic, hypothesis-generating signal rather than a robust treatment effect. Therefore, it would be advisable for readers to resist the temptation to elevate a borderline, model-dependent HR above the study's own neutral primary endpoint. The honest summary is that atherectomy buys time—better acute results and better 1-year patency—without yet demonstrating that it changes the 2-year clinical destination.

The Embolization Tax

Although the efficacy signal may be modest, the safety signal is notably significant. Procedural complications occurred in 36.6% of atherectomy cases compared with 1.3% with DCB alone, predominantly caused by distal embolization, which affected 32.7% of debulked lesions vs 0.7% (P < 0.001). This is not an anomaly of a single registry; the JET-FORWARD (one year clinical outcomes and predictors of distal embolization after JETSTREAMTM atherectomy for calcified femoropopliteal artery lesions) study reported angiographically significant distal embolization in 35.5% of Jetstream cases and identified poor tibial runoff, severe small artery disease, nodular calcification, and debulking lengths exceeding 10 cm as independent predictors.8 Collectively, these data sets outline a clear risk phenotype—the long, nodular, poor-runoff, calcium-laden limb—where the embolic risk is highest and, unfortunately, often coincides with the lesions that operators most wish to debulk. In this cohort, embolic protection was left to the operator’s discretion and was applied inconsistently, which almost certainly increased the event rate and obscured any conclusions regarding the safety of a protected procedure.

Where the Field Should Go

The most useful contribution of this study may be the questions it sharpens. First, patient and lesion selection must shift from calcium quantity to calcium quality. PACSS is an angiographic surrogate; it does not distinguish nodular or protruding calcium, which may genuinely require debulking, from circumferential medial calcification, which is largely impervious to it.10 The absence of systematic intravascular imaging (IVUS or optical coherence tomography) is the most consequential limitation of the study because the mechanistic argument for atherectomy ultimately rests on morphology, which the study did not capture. Future work should be imaging-defined and prospectively test whether specific calcium phenotypes derive net benefits from plaque modification.

Second, embolic protection deserves to be studied as a variable in its own right, rather than left to habit. Given the predictors from JET-FORWARD and the emergence of protocolized approaches—including multimodal protection and, conversely, deliberately protection-free strategies now being tested—the pertinent question is not whether embolic protection is used but in whom it is mandatory. Standardized, risk-stratified protection embedded in the trial design would allow us to separate the intrinsic risk of debulking from the modifiable risk of an unprotected approach.11,12

Third, the exploratory observation that restenosis after atherectomy tended toward an occlusive rather than diffuse morphology, although not statistically significant and reported without core-laboratory adjudication, is a signal worth pursuing. If a deep wall injury from aggressive debulking converts a future diffuse restenosis into a total occlusion, the long-term calculus for limb salvage could shift unfavorably, even when early patency appears to be better. Mechanistic and imaging-based studies of vascular healing after plaque modification are needed to confirm this.

Finally, and most importantly, the field needs an adequately powered randomized trial with clinically meaningful endpoints—clinically driven target lesion revascularization, limb outcomes, and quality of life—rather than acute angiographic surrogates, enriched for imaging-defined severe calcification, and stratified by embolic risk. Until such data exist, the reasonable posture is that advocated by Tokuda et al3: selective, mechanism-driven atherectomy reserved for lesions that cannot be adequately expanded or that harbor debulking-appropriate nodular calcium—not routine application across all calcified segments. In a hemodialysis-heavy population defined by medial calcification, we should also be candid that no mechanical device will fully overcome the biology and that adjunctive medical strategies targeting calcification and restenosis remain unmet needs.

Tokuda et al3 have done the field a service by resisting an easy narrative. They showed, with unusual clarity, that atherectomy wins the acute battle for the lumen while the calcified artery quietly wins the longer war—and that the victory comes with a 1-in-3 risk of distal embolization. This is not an argument against the tool. This is an argument for using it deliberately, protecting against its predictable hazards, and finally generating randomized, imaging-guided evidence that will tell us which patients it truly helps.
