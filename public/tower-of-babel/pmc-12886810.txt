Design of isolated lithographic SERS structures with enhanced sensitivity
Authors: Xin Jin, Hui Xia, S R J Brueck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) on lithographically fabricated structures has been extensively explored. The consensus is that non-resonant single-molecule sensitivity (SMSERS) is not achievable with isolated structures. Difficult to manufacture closely spaced pairs of structures, akin to the closely approaching (< 1 nm separation) spheres identified in colloidal clusters, are thought to be required. This scale is beyond the capability of current lithographic approaches. FDTD modeling is used to explore improvements in the electric field enhancement for isolated lithographic structures. Electric field enhancement factors (β=Eloc/Ein where Eloc is the maximum local field at the edge of the metal and Ein is the incident laser field) from 40 for conventional structures up to 820 are obtained with simple, scalable, and easily manufacturable isolated structures, with overall SERS enhancements ∝β4 up to 5x1011. Improvements include: replacing a lossy metal adhesion layer (AL) with a lossless SiO2 AL; planarizing the AL with a SiO2 deposition after the lift-off process; using high curvature elliptical structures; thin-film engineering of the substrate to provide constructive interference at the enhancement structure; and metal-insulator-metal structures to introduce a magnetic dipole resonance that allows increased tuning flexibility and broadens the wavelength response. Together, these improvements open a pathway to non-resonant single-molecule SERS on robust, manufacturable, substrates with isolated nanostructures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31076-0.Subject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) was first observed on roughened metal surfaces almost 50 years ago1–3 and has been the topic of many investigations4; a Google Scholar search with the keyword SERS brings up over 632,000 publications. Notwithstanding this rich history and evident scientific interest, SERS has yet to realize its potential for critical societal needs: chemical sensing5; healthcare6; drug monitoring7–9; food inspection10,11; defense and security12; environmental monitoring13; and others.

The practical implementation of SERS has been hindered by challenges related to the fabrication, uniformity, and durability of the metallic nanostructures that are responsible for the strong Raman enhancement. Current SERS substrates can be broadly characterized into two approaches: (1) random assemblies of nanoparticles; and (2) lithographically defined patterns. While each approach offers distinct advantages, significant limitations remain that affect the sensitivity, reproducibility and reliability of SERS measurements.

At least partially stemming from the initial observation of single molecule SERS (SM-SERS) in colloidal assemblies of spherical nanoparticles14, much of the activity has been focused on assemblies of noble metal (typically Au or Ag) nanoscale spherical nanoparticles15. A single sphere has a relatively modest electric field enhancement factor (EF)16, β ~ 6, at resonance (see Supplementary Information, SI); the common solution is to rely on random assemblies of nanoparticles with gap modes that form on close approach, 1 nm, between adjacent nanospheres. This random nanoparticle aggregation leads to a lack of control over the size, distribution, and geometry of the interparticle gaps. As a result, these substrates suffer from poor reproducibility, spatially variable enhancement factors, and difficulties in delivering the target molecule to the hot spot. β is strongly dependent on the size of the gap and only approaches the EF suitable for non-resonant single molecule sensitivities ( 109−1010) at 1 nm gaps. This has several consequences: 1) only a relatively small and undetermined number of molecules in the smaller gaps contribute to the Raman signal; 2) the density of these adsorption sites is nonuniform across the sample; 3) over time, contaminants with higher affinities to the metals can occupy these sites, decreasing the sensitivity and affecting the utility of the substrate; 3) these self-assembled SERS surfaces are fragile and cannot be cleaned for reuse; and 4) in the case of 3D stacked assemblies, the target molecules have to navigate a complex pathway, with frequent interaction and potential adsorption at non-hot-spot metal surfaces, decreasing the probability that the target molecules reach the hot spots. Additional approaches include more complex shapes such as stars17, cubes, and others18. Recently, self-assembled surfaces with uniformities of 6% - 10% across a surface (10 μm diameter focal spots) and between multiple preparations of the surface have been reported19,20.

Numerous self-assembly approaches to creating small gaps in nanoparticle assemblies, including DNA and other molecular linkers, have been experimentally explored21,22. An issue with this approach is that the highest β sites are occupied by the linkers, blocking accessibility of the molecules of interest, and the ideal situation of a uniform large-area rectilinear 2D array is seldom realized, with the likely formation more complex multi-branched 3D configurations, reducing the density of available hot spots and increasing nonuniformity.

With advances in nanopatterning, there are multiple available approaches to lithographically defined SERS surfaces5. The requisite scales for isolated structures with resonances in the visible are ~ 100 nm, well within the range of optical, interferometric, nanosphere, e-beam, and nanoimprint lithography23–26. Lithographic methods offer the advantages of reproducibility and design flexibility. However, nm-scale gaps are beyond the resolution of these techniques and isolated structures are widely thought not to offer sufficient plasmonic enhancement for single molecule sensitivity27–29.

The major aim of this contribution is to establish that appropriately engineered isolated nanoscale features can provide sufficient SERS enhancement and spatial localization to allow single-molecule sensitivity. The experimental demonstration of non-resonant single-molecule sensitivity with isolated structures will be reported elsewhere. We focus solely on the electromagnetic (plasmonic and metamaterial) aspects of the enhancement. The structures we consider are lithographically defined, multi-layered with both metallic and dielectric layers, fabricated using lift-off processes on a substrate (which can also have a multilayered thin-film stack on a bulk substrate) with elliptical (circular) top-down cross sections. Transverse dimensions are ~ 100 nm to keep the resonant wavelengths in the 600–900 nm range appropriate for 633 nm or 785 nm laser excitation. In contrast to spherical particles, where the resonance wavelength is independent of diameter within the dipole approximation, for disks the resonance wavelength is sensitive to multiple factors including disk diameter, disk thickness, ellipticity, the refractive index of the underlying substrate and of the immersion medium offering multiple avenues for fine tuning.

There are limitations to this analysis. All materials are assumed to be continuous media with abrupt terminations, even though the hot-spot dimensions we explore are comparable to interatomic distances. Actual fabricated structures are likely to have deviations from the idealized vertical sidewalls and smooth circumferential shapes of the modeled structures. Chemical enhancements and related quantum tunneling effects are not considered, although they may be significant at these dimensional scales. Raman selection rules depend on the orientation of the molecule relative to the enhanced fields, which is not well controlled.

The incident field is taken as linearly polarized, along the major axis of the elliptical cross section (taken as the x-axis). From the symmetry of the problem, there is no field along the orthogonal (y-axis) planar direction, but there is a strong field, comparable to the field in the x-direction, induced along the vertical (z) axis of the multi-layer stack at the hot spots (see SI). Notwithstanding these caveats, the FDTD results provide important physics and serve as a reliable guide to improvements in SERS enhancement structures. Periodic structures can introduce long-range surface plasma wave (LRSP) effects30. Experiments are carried out with 400 nm period arrays at an illumination wavelength of 633 nm, for which these LSRPs do not play a role.

Several approaches lead to increased enhancement: 1) use of a lossless adhesion layer between the substrate and the ES31; 2) planarization of the interface between the substrate and the ES; 3) use of elliptical ES to take advantage of increased enhancement at high curvature regions32; 4) using thin films on the substrate to enhance the local fields; and 5) use of metal-insulator-metal ES to provide a second resonance that broadens the enhancement vs. wavelength response29. Taken together, these improvements are sufficient to achieve non-resonant single molecule sensitivity in easily fabricated isolated nanostructures. Several of these have been previously discussed individually; this is, to our knowledge, the first time a global optimization has been presented showing overall enhancements up to 5x1011, well above the 109 – 1010 level widely understood to be necessary for single molecule sensitivity33,34.

Results

Lossless adhesion layer and planarization

Lithographic SERS substrates are most often fabricated by lift-off processes where holes are developed in a negative-tone resist film, etched to descum, and subsequently filled in with blanket depositions. The remaining resist along with the blanket depositions away from the resist holes are then removed (lifted off), leaving isolated nanostructures. For the cases of Au and Ag, adhesion to the substrate, often SiO2, is an issue and a thin reactive metal adhesion layer (AL), ~ 5- to 10-nm of Ti, Ni or Cr, is often used to ensure robustness of the nanostructures. An example of a circular ES structure for analysis is shown in Fig. 1. The hot spots are at the top and bottom interfaces of the Au disk with the adhesion and cap layers. Unfortunately, these reactive metals are quite lossy at typical non-resonant Raman wavelengths (500 to 1000 nm) and, as shown below, significantly suppress SERS. We have developed a SiO2 AL, with both the dielectric and the plasmonic metal deposited in the same e-beam evaporation chamber without exposing the SiO2 layer to air, eliminating this issue and resulting in exceptional SERS sensitivity.

Elliptical geometry

Elliptical disks provide an alternative with both a higher field enhancement and a smaller circumferential extent of the hot spot32. This is shown in Fig. 4 which shows the wavelength dependence of a) the cross section and b) the enhancement factor at the center of the hot spot (incident beam polarized along the long axis of the ellipse), just at the edge along the top and bottom interfaces for a 110 nm diameter, 50 nm thick Au disk with a 10 SiO2 AL. Three sets of curves are shown, a 1:1 axis ratio (circular disk), a 2:1 axis ratio; and a 3.3:1 axis ratio. The long axis was adjusted to keep the resonances at approximately the same wavelength. For these curves the resonance was tuned to ~ 633 nm to match the excitation wavelength used in the experiments discussed below. Note the blue shift of the resonance and the higher maximum resulting from the thicker 50 nm thick circular disk as compared with the 35 nm thick disk of Fig. 2. The cross section decreases as the ellipse becomes more asymmetric, since the top-down surface area of the metal decreases. In contrast, the enhancements all increase dramatically, with the bottom interface showing the strongest of about 220. In addition to the magnitude of the enhancement, the circumferential extent of the enhancement decreases as the ellipticity is increased as shown in Fig. 5. With current technology, the minimum resolution for an e-beam written ellipse is ~ 20 nm, so higher aspect ratio ellipses are not shown, but higher ’s and improved circumferential localization are available with improved lithography.

Substrate thin-film effects

Thin film coating effects can be used to further increase the enhancement effects. The incident and reflected fields at the location of the hot spot (the bottom surface of the Au disk) can interfere either constructively or destructively impacting the total field at both the pump and Stokes wavelengths. For a good antireflection (AR) coating, there is no reflected field at the top of the coating (e.g. at the enhancement structure) and only the incident field remains. In contrast with a high reflectivity (HR) coating, the reflected field at the interface is equal to the incident field and the total field at the interface is between zero and twice the incident field. Of course, if the field is zero, there is no SERS signal, but if the field is engineered to be twice the incident field, there is an enhancement of two in the local fields, and of up to 24 = 16 in the SERS signal. A straightforward approach to this enhancement is to apply a blanket Au film, followed by a quarter-wave-thick SiO2 layer to provide the necessary phase shift for constructive interference. Simulation results are shown in Fig. 6 for this case. The simulation includes a Si substrate, a blanket 5 nm thick Ti AL (below the reflecting Au film, so no impact on the EF), a 200 nm thick Au film, and a 110 nm thick SiO2 spacer layer (~/4 at 633 nm). Metal-insulator-metal structures

Multi-layer metal- insulator-metal (MIM) enhancement structures further increase the flexibility of placing the enhancement resonances and the magnitude of the enhancement35. A single disk, as discussed up to this point, has an electric dipole resonance associated with free electrons responding to the incident field. Adding a second disk atop the first, separated by a dielectric spacer, provides a magnetic dipole resonance (current loop) that can be tuned by varying the thickness of the spacer35. Simulation results are presented in Fig. 7 for a 110 nm diameter MIM with a 5 nm SiO2 adhesion layer, an Au bottom disk thickness of 35 nm, an Au top disk thickness of 25 nm and SiO2 spacer thicknesses of 30-, 60- and 90-nm. The evolution of the magnetic dipole resonance in the cross section from 770 nm at a 30 nm thickness, to 710 nm at a 60 nm thickness and to 680 at 90 nm thickness is evident.

Cross section and field enhancement factors for a 110 nm diameter circular MIM with a 35 nm thick Au bottom disk, a 25 nm thick Au top disk and SiO2 spacers of 30-, 60- and 90-nm; all with 5 nm SiO2 AL on an SiO2 substrate.

There are hot spots at the edge of each of the four metal-dielectric interfaces along the polarization direction of the incident field. For the top disc hot spots, the electric fields induced by the electric dipole and magnetic dipole (current loop) align enhancing the magnitude of the hot spot. For the bottom disc, the fields are opposite resulting in dips in the response vs. wavelength, and therefore in significant shifts in the peaks of the wavelength response. The highest enhancement factors are at the bottom of the top disk, rather than at the bottom interface of the bottom disk for this specific geometrical configuration.

Experiment

Experimental results are shown in Fig. 8 along with the corresponding simulations. Three structures were investigated as shown schematically in Fig. 8a: a 110 nm diameter, 35 nm thick, single Au disk with a 10-nm thick SiO2 adhesion layer; a 110-nm diameter MIM with a 10 nm thick AL, 35 nm thick bottom disk, a 90 nm Al2O3 insulator, and a 25-nm thick top Au disk; and a MIM+ with the addition of a planarized adhesion layer and an additional 10-nm thick SiO2 cap. All samples were fabricated in a 400 nm square array on a SiO2 substrate using interferometric lithography and lift-off with e-beam evaporation on fused silica substrates. The final SiO2 deposition for the MIM+ was carried out after the lift-off was performed. Prior to the final deposition, the samples were cleaned with an oxygen plasma; the evaporation chamber was pumped down overnight to assure a good vacuum, and the deposition was performed at a very slow rate to ensure uniformity and film quality.

Optimization

Overall optimization is achieved by combining all the features discussed above: use of a MIM+ with a SiO2 AL; an elliptical MIM+, and thin film effects. The simulation result is shown in Fig. 9. A peak EF of ~ 820 is achieved at the bottom interface of a 78 nm long axis, 20 nm short axis, MIM+ with bottom Au 35 nm thick, 90 nm thick SiO2 insulator and 25 nm top Au disk atop a 110 nm thick (~ quarter wave) blanket oxide over an Au reflector layer. Given the large number of geometric parameters, this is likely a local maximum, and further exploration is warranted.

Discussion

Several approaches to larger EFs for isolated lithographic ESs have been highlighted. The first is to replace the metal adhesion layer commonly used in lift-off processing for depositing Au or Ag nanostructures with a SiO2 adhesion layer. It is important to ensure cleanliness of the SiO2 film by, for example, depositing both the SiO2 and the Au in the same chamber without exposing the SiO2 adhesion layer to contamination in air. As a result of the asymmetry between the top Au disk surface interface with air and the bottom interface with SiO2, the EF is larger at the bottom. More details are provided in the SI.

A Disk+ structure provides additional improvement in the EF. A right-angle corner between the Au and the underlying SiO2 is more effective than a vertical transition. As shown in the SI, the relative EFs saturate with index contrast, and also the gain from the right-angle step is alternately recovered with only a ~5 nm extension of the AL, so other fabrication approaches are available as discussed in the SI.

Elliptical structures provide both much larger EFs and smaller circumferential hot spots. The modeling was limited to > 20 nm short elliptical axes based on available nano-lithographic techniques. With more advanced lithographic techniques, for example EUV, or with fabrication approaches such as ALD along with anisotropic etching, additional improvements are available.

Thin film reflection offers an additional factor of two enhancement in the local fields since the field strength at the nanostructure is |Ein + Eref|, Ein = 1 and −1 <Eref <1 (Eref is the reflected field at the top surface of the substrate film stack). A simple film stack including an Au reflector layer along with a quarter-wave insulator between the nanostructure and the Au reflector was introduced and significantly improves the EF.

MIM and MIM+ provide added flexibility by introducing a second, magnetic dipole, resonance in addition to the electric dipole resonance of the disk that can be tuned independently. This can provide either a larger EF, or a broader wavelength response.

As a reminder, all simulation results presented in this contribution are based on FDTD modeling, assuming idealized continuous media, and do not account for any discrete atomic lattice, quantum or chemical enhancement effects. Actual EFs will depend on the fine details of the deposition that certainly differ from the idealized model structures. Nonetheless, there is a dramatic scaling from an enhancement factor of ~40 for a commonly used circular disk structure with a metal adhesion layer to ~820 for an optimized elliptical MIM+ with a dielectric AL on a reflector film structure. For scaling, this is an improvement in the SERS signal of as much as ~2x105! In a subsequent manuscript, currently in preparation, we demonstrate non-resonant SM-SERS for isolated, circular MIM+ on a fused silica substrate with an adenine/cytosine bianalyte study at an excitation wavelength of 633 nm. The present simulations indicate that substantially better results can be achieved.

Qualitative experimental validation is provided with 110 nm diameter circular structures: a disk; a MIM, and a MIM+, all with SiO2 ALs on a SiO2 substrate. Large-area ES arrays were fabricated by interferometric lithography on a 400 nm period. An MMBN monolayer was adsorbed to the Au; and Raman spectra were collected with 200 W, 633 nm excitation at a ~ 1 m diameter focal spot. The signal/noise at a strong 1606 cm−1 Raman line increased from 30 (disk) to 95 (MIM) to 247 (MIM+). Significant improvements are available with the additional enhancement provided by elliptical structures and thin film effects.

Counter to prevailing expectations, these results (to 820 and EF ~ ~ 5x1011) are more than sufficient for SM-SERS with easily manufactured, scalable, isolated nanostructures. Additionally, lithographic structures are highly uniform, easily and inexpensively reproduced, and robust to both liquid and plasma cleaning procedures. Arrays of these structures provide well-defined densities of hot spots that are easily accessible to molecular species of interest. Taken together, these characteristics presage a dramatic improvement in SM-SERS measurements for many important scientific and societal applications.

Methods

FDTD numerical calculations are carried out with commercial software36. All calculations presented are carried out at a resolution of 0.125 nm. Since we are interested in using these structures embedded in nanochannels, the calculations are all for isolated structures with PML boundary conditions. For metals (Au, Ti) we use the CRC handbook values37 of the refractive index along with standard references38 for the dielectrics (SiO2, Si3N4) and the semiconductor (Si).

Samples (2.5x2.5 cm2) were fabricated by interferometric lithography on a SiO2 substrate at a 400 nm period. Substrates were cleaned in piranha solution (H₂SO₄:H₂O₂, 3:1) for 15 min, rinsed in deionized (DI) water for 10 min, dried under nitrogen, and baked at 200 °C for 10 min. A bottom antireflective coating (ICON-739, ~50 nm) was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 40 s and baked at 175 °C for 1 min, followed by a negative photoresist layer (~850 nm)40 spin-coated under the same conditions and baked at 150 °C for 1 min.

Interferometric lithography19 was performed using the third harmonic of a single-mode YAG laser (355 nm) at 5 mW/cm2, with two 8 s exposures with the sample rotated by 90°. A post-exposure hot-plate bake was carried out at 100 °C for 60 s, and development was performed in RD6:DI water (3:1) for 50 s36. Residual resist was removed by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) O₂ descum (15 s, 20 °C, 30 sccm O₂, 5 mTorr, 50 W RIE, 350 W).

Multilayer deposition was conducted in a custom e-beam evaporator at < 8 × 10⁻⁷ Torr; sequentially depositing 5 nm SiO₂ (0.01 nm/s), 35 nm Au (0.05 nm/s), 30-, 60- and 90-nm Al₂O₃ (0.05 nm/s), and 25 nm Au (0.05 nm/s). Substrates were immersed in negative photoresist remover (Microchem PG41) at 60 °C for 30 min, rinsed, dried under nitrogen, and O₂ plasma-cleaned (2 min, 30 sccm, 50 mTorr, 50 W). For MIM+ structures, a final 5 nm SiO₂ planarization/capping layer was deposited at 0.01 nm/s. Fig. 10a shows the developed photoresist scaffold, and Fig. 10b shows the final MIM+ structures.

(a) 400 nm period negative photoresist scaffold after development. (b) MIM+ structures after liftoff and final SiO2 deposition. (c) Transmission through the array in (b) simulation and experiment.

The 400 nm period arrays were prepared as 2.5×2.5 cm2 samples and later diced for experiments. Transmission spectra revealed plasmonic resonances at ~632 nm and ~666 nm, in good qualitative agreement with simulations Fig. 10c.

Fabrication was performed in a multi-user University facility with limited capabilities and reproducibility. For the best samples, uniformity of the Raman signals was about 10% across cm2 areas (~1 m diameter spot size). Improved results are certainly possible with a more rigorous manufacturing approach.

Raman measurements were carried out at a laser wavelength of 633 nm, a laser power of ~200 W, a FWHM spot size of ~ 1 m (~ 5 structures were illuminated within the laser spot), and an exposure time of 30 s. The signal was collected in epi-mode using a 0.9 NA objective lens, a dichroic beamsplitter to separate the pump and Stokes wavelengths, a long pass filter to suppress reflected pump illumination, a collection lens imaging onto the entrance slit of a f/4.5 spectrometer, and a cooled EMCCD camera. The narrowband Raman signals were riding on a broadband metal fluorescence background, which was primarily responsible for the noise in the spectrum; camera dark current was negligible. This background was subtracted by fitting to a 3rd order polynomial across the full Raman shift range.
