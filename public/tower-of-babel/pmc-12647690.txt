Adaptive composite loss for volumetric whole heart segmentation
Authors: Krittanat Sutassananon, Worapan Kusakunniran, Mehmet Orgun, Thanongchai Siriapisith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate segmentation in medical imaging requires loss functions that capture both regional overlap and boundary alignment. This study evaluates composite losses combining binary cross-entropy (BCE) and a boundary-based term under fixed and adaptive weighting schemes, using U-Net and SwinUNETR on the MM-WHS dataset. For U-Net, a small boundary contribution with adaptive weighting yielded the best results: Standard SoftAdapt (90/10 BCE + BoundaryDoU) achieved the highest Dice score (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.932 \pm 0.01$$\end{document}), surpassing both the baseline (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.923 \pm 0.01$$\end{document}) and fixed ratios. In contrast, SwinUNETR achieved its strongest performance with a fixed 70% BCE + 10% boundary ratio (0.919 ± 0.02). The result showed that combining a boundary-based loss term helps improve the segmentation accuracy. However, the performance gain is dependent on the architecture of the segmentation model; convolution-based U-Net benefited from the adaptive loss weighting scheme, whereas Transformer-based SwinUNETR without strong inductive bias did not benefit from increased influence of the boundary loss term.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a group of disorders of the heart and blood vessels, including those affecting blood vessels supplying organs and damages to cardiac muscles and valves which would result in malfunctioning of the heart. According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), an estimated 17.9 million people died from CVDs in 2019, representing 32% of all global deaths. 85% of those deaths were due to heart attacks and strokes. And out of 17 million premature deaths under the age of 70 due to non-communicable diseases in 2019, 38% were caused by CVDs. Accurate measurement of cardiac substructures is thus crucial for facilitating medical diagnoses of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), as it helps in understanding their anatomical properties and aids in the development of effective treatment plans.

In practice, however, obtaining precise measurements of cardiac substructures demands significant effort from medical practitioners, who must manually analyse and segment each individual slice from a cardiac image sequence. They then aggregate the results from these slices to create a volumetric measurement for the entire image sequence. For instance, generating the ground truth for the Multi-modality Whole Heart Segmentation Challenge (MM-WHS) dataset required trained professionals approximately six to ten hours to annotate a single cardiac volume1. Fully-automatic segmentation methods could also help reduce both the inter-operator and intra-operator variability due to factors such as the difference in the expertise level, and errors due to attention fatigue2.

Whole-heart segmentation with neural networks

U-Net3 was first proposed in 2015, and since then has been considered to be the foundational model for medical image segmentation. U-Net is primarily composed of two parts: the contracting path to capture and extract the underlying context from an input image, and the expanding path to provide accurate localisation of the extracted context. The contracting path and the expanding path in U-Net are equivalent to the encoder and the decoder part in an autoencoder architecture. One of the key contributions to its success lies in the “skip-connection”, or the direct connectivity between layers in the contracting path and their corresponding layers of the same level in the expanding path. This direct feature parsing allows more precise localisation of the extracted context, when compared to the conventional FCNs. An additional benefit from introducing these skip-connections is that the gradients are allowed to flow through the network more easily; alleviating common gradient issues such as exploding and vanishing gradient. Since then, numerous studies have been conducted in addition to the original U-Net architecture to improve its performance; well-known mechanisms in computer vision are studied and incorporated into the baseline U-Net architecture. These include: Attention U-Net4 with the attention mechanism to enhance the ability to focus on the region of interest (ROI) and avoid unnecessary areas, and Residual U-Net5 with the residual modules to overcome the loss of feature identities caused by diminishing gradients especially in very deep networks.

From the success of their two-dimensional counterparts, there have also been studies on three-dimensional models to leverage inter-slice dependencies in medical image volumes. 3D U-Net6 and V-Net7 are two of the most important models, though upgrading from two-dimensional kernels to three-dimensional ones usually requires much increased computational resources.

In recent years, while many classification models applying Vision Transformer8 modules reported improved performance especially with larger datasets, Vision Transformer-based segmentation models such as UNETR9, TransUNet, SwinUNET, and SwinUNETR10 could not perform better than convolution-based models trained with similar methodologies11.

For convolutional-based segmentation models, the core mechanism relies on capturing local receptive fields using convolutional kernels. This results in specific inductive bias: strong spatial locality and translation invariance. To progress from low-level to high-level features, hierarchical feature extraction stacks a group of convolutional kernels on top of one another, to gradually expand the receptive fields. U-Net-based design, in particular, uses the encoder-decoder design usually aligned in symmetric or pyramid architectures.

On the other hand, for Transformer-based models the core mechanism relies on global self-attention with Transformer modules. This introduces minimal inductive bias; the model learns spatial relationships from a huge amount of data. This family of segmentation models operate on flattened image patches, while recent models include hierarchical and hybrid convolution- transformer designs. In Transformer parlance, each image patch is treated akin to tokens in NLP; this inculcates lack of innate spatial structure unless explicitly encoded.

For small and medium datasets, convolution-based models are competitive whereas the Transformer-based models may perform better on large-scale datasets due to the cheer size of data they require. Transformer-based models sacrifices local texture for better global context understanding. Convolution-based models are usually more efficient with fewer parameters, whereas Transformer-based models are typically heavier. Because of its requirement on significantly more data or pretraining, Transformer-based models benefit greatly from transfer learning or hybrid Conv-Transformer models (e.g., TransUNet and SwinUNET).

Transformers are usually slower in inference due to the attention modules. The interpretability for CNNs have been well-studied with techniques such as the saliency map and GradCAM , whereas similar literatures for Transformers are still developing. CNNs may face issues on scalability due to limited global modelling, while Transformers may scale well with compute. CNNs are more robust in low-resource settings, whereas Transformers are much sensitive to hyperparameter settings.

Loss functions for segmentation

Loss functions are used to measure the magnitude of difference between the predicted outputs from the model and their expected results, or in other words the ground truth. With in each iteration during the model training phase, one or multiple loss functions are usually used in combination with an optimiser, to provide signals for the model to adjust its parameter weights accordingly, resulting in smaller discrepancies between the predicted outputs from the model and the expected results.

Cross-entropy (CE) is one of the most common loss functions for segmentation tasks. It is generally used to measure the pixel-wise difference between the probability of the predicted output p and its corresponding ground truth pixel y, for each class c as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Loss_{CE} = -\frac{1}{n}\sum _{i=1}^{n}y_i log (p_i) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where n is the matching number of pixels betweeh the predicted output and its corresponding ground truth.

However, cross-entropy loss function is generally known to be susceptible to the class imbalance problem, where the size of the segmentation target is relatively small compared to the whole input image size12. One solution to alleviate the class imbalance issue is to instead use the Soft Dice loss, which is more robust against the ROI size.2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Loss_{Dice} = 1 - \frac{2\sum _{i=1}^{n}y_i p_i}{\sum _{i=1}^{n}(|y_i| + |p_i|)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}with the numerator in the second term adapted from the numerator of the Dice coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum _{i=1}^{n}y_i \cup p_i$$\end{document}) to allow continuous and smoother gradient, in contrast to a discrete one should the Dice coefficient be used directly.

Both the cross-entropy loss function and the Soft Dice loss function have been used extensively in medical image segmentation, as they can provide a good context for optimising the general area of the segmentation target. What makes one model outperform another, however, usually lies in the context for the boundary area of the segmentation target. This is especially the case where the difference between the segmentation target boundary and the neighbouring background pixel could not be delineated clearly, like in cardiac segmentation with CT scans.

However, focusing solely on optimising the segmentation accuracy around the boundary area does not directly lead to better segmentation accuracy overall. For example, training a segmentation model with Hausdorff Distance-based loss functions on small ROIs can even lead to diverging loss values, and for most of the time resulted in inferior performance compared to optimising the segmentation accuracy over the general area. A better approach would be to combine one loss function to focus on the general area of the ROIs with another to focus on the boundary area, into a composite loss function.

Relying only on Hausdorff Distance Transform (HDT)-based loss for medical image segmentation can lead to suboptimal training, even though it is excellent at enforcing boundary precision. It often fails to adequately capture the overall region structure of the ROI, eventually leading to poor coverage of the full lesion or organ. Combining such boundary-aware or distance-based loss with region-based losses (e.g., Dice, Tversky, or focal-based variants) typically results in more robust, accurate segmentation. In particular, Karimi et al.2 introduced several formulations of HD-based loss functions, including using distance transforms. It showed that HD-focused losses can significantly reduce Hausdorff distance by 18–45% without degrading Dice performance. However, the author did not advocate using HD-only losses independently; it complements regional losses rather than replaces them. Kervadec et al.13 proposed a boundary-based loss designed to complement region-based losses, especially in highly unbalanced segmentation tasks. When combined with generalised Dice loss, it significantly improves both Dice and Hausdorff-based metrics while also stabilising training.

Arguments favouring compound losses can also be found in a large-scale empirical study comparing a range of segmentation losses across diverse 3D tasks by Ma et al.14, which shows that compound losses are more robust across tasks than standalone losses. Jadon15 also describes how direct minimisation of the Hausdorff distance is nominally desirable but typically intractable and unstable for training.

Nevertheless, the weighting of the loss functions should be treated very carefully; one set of weighting configurations that work well on one particular set of medical images may not help achieve the similar result on another set of medical images. Setting the weights of each component of the composite loss function should be made primarily in regard to the characteristic of both the image dataset and the segmentation model in question, which differs from one training setting to another.

Drawing on the analogy of how we would learn materials throughout their course of education, we propose an alternative to the SoftAdapt algorithm, that we gradually increase the difficulty of the learning progress once the fundamentals are well covered. Translating this analogy to the field of semantic segmentation, the idea is to learn to segment the general area of the ROIs well before progressing to refine the accuracy at the boundary area. We refer to this paradigm of adaptive weighting of the composite loss function as Adaptive Composite Loss, which is detailed in this paper.

Objectives

This paper has two main objectives: (1) to show that a boundary loss function, in particular the BoundaryDoU loss proposed by Sun et al.18, can compliment standard loss functions like Binary Cross-entropy for whole heart segmentation resulting in improved performance, and (2) to show that adaptive loss value adjustments are superior than their fixed counterparts.

Methods

Dataset and pre-processing

Multi-modality whole heart segmentation challenge (MM-WHS)

The Multi-modality Whole Heart Segmentation Challenge (MM-WHS)1 provides anonymised cardiac images for both CT and MRI modalities under ethical approval. The dataset was organised as a part of MICCAI 2017 competition to advance researches in automated whole-heart segmentation across different modalities.

The dataset consists of the total 120 subjects acquired from multiple imaging sites. There are 60 cases for each of the imaging modality, with 20 cases for training and 40 cases for evaluation. The cases include a variety of pathologies and anatomical variations to ensure robustness of segmentation algorithms across different patient conditions. Unlike datasets focused only on a specific subset of cardiac substructures, the MM-WHS dataset provides manual annotations of seven cardiac substructures: left ventricle (LV), right ventricle (RV), left atrium (LA), right atrium (RA), myocardium (MYO), ascending aorta (AA), and pulmonary artery (PA).

The dataset challenges segmenting all major cardiac substructures from multi-modality imaging, which is a more comprehensive and clinically relevant task compared to single-structure segmentation. The results can facilitate downstream applications in cardiac modelling, treatment planning, and disease diagnosis across imaging modalities.

In this study, we restrict our experiments to the CT subset, focusing on the performance evaluation of our method in high-resolution CT imaging, which is most widely used in clinical workflows for cardiac morphology assessment. In addition, the cases for evaluation are not publicly accessible and submission to the challenge server is required for evaluation.

Before using the voxels from the dataset in the model training pipeline, we applied window normalisation to lessen the impact of using inputs with different scales. In this technique, two key parameters are: the window width defining the span of Hounsfield unit values of interest, and the window level defining the median value of the Hounsfield unit range. Automated cardiac diagnosis challenge (ACDC)

The Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge (ACDC)19 provides anonymised Cine-MRI scans (SSFP sequences) from patients at the University Hospital of Dijon, France, under ethical approval. The dataset was also organised as a part of MICCAI 2017 competition focusing on automated diagnosis and segmentation of cardiac substructures.

The dataset provides Cine-MRI scans (SSFP sequences) from 150 patients at the hospital, which can be splitted evenly into five groups according to clinical conditions: four pathological (dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, infarction, and abnormal right ventricle) alongside one healthy cohort. Both end-diastolic (ED) and end-systolic (ES) phases were captured for each patient case. The two phases were selected due to the fact that they correspond to the maximum and minimum volumes of the left ventricle, respectively, and that they are clinically the key phases of the cardiac cycle for segmentation and diagnosis. The labelled substructures include the left ventricle (LV), the right ventricle (RV) and the myocardium (MYO).

The dataset challenges for simultaneous segmentation of multiple cardiac substructures in both phases from different viewing angles, and the results could be valuable for further diagnosis through linking the segmented features to classify among five disease categories.

150 MRI volumes from the dataset are separated into: 100 volumes for training, and 50 volumes for evaluation.

Segmentation model

U-Net

U-Net3 was selected as the baseline model due to its proven effectiveness in medical image segmentation. By replacing handcrafted feature extractors with an end-to-end convolutional encoder-decoder, U-Net established a new standard and inspired numerous variants, many of which remain among the top performers in medical image analysis. Benchmarking against the original architecture therefore provides both a strong reference point and an indication of the potential performance achievable by its more advanced adaptations.

Nevertheless, U-Net exhibits structural limitations when compared with transformer-based models such as the Vision Transformer8. Its convolutional design emphasizes local spatial features, which supports accurate boundary delineation but restricts the capacity to model global dependencies, a drawback in cases where broader contextual information is critical. Moreover, the scarcity of annotated medical datasets often constrains its generalizability. In contrast, transformer-based models excel at capturing long-range relationships but demand large-scale training data. These trade-offs suggest that while U-Net remains a strong benchmark, the optimal choice of architecture depends on dataset scale and the relative importance of local versus global feature representations.

SwinUNETR

Compared to the baseline U-Net, SwinUNETR10 directly addresses one of the core limitations of convolutional architectures: their restricted capacity to model long-range dependencies. By embedding Swin Transformer20 blocks within a U-Net-like encoder-decoder architecture, SwinUNETR retains the strengths of convolution in capturing fine-grained local structure while augmenting it with self-attention mechanisms that enhance global contextual understanding. This hybrid design makes SwinUNETR particularly well-suited for segmentation tasks involving diffuse or spatially heterogeneous pathologies, where local feature extraction alone may be insufficient.

Nevertheless, the performance gains of SwinUNETR are not universal. Its reliance on transformer components entails higher computational costs and a stronger dependence on large annotated datasets, which are often limited in medical imaging. In resource-constrained settings or when only small datasets are available, the simpler U-Net may remain the more practical choice, offering competitive results with lower training overhead. The comparative evaluation of these models thus underscores a broader trade-off in medical image segmentation: the balance between efficiency and representational richness, shaped by the availability of data and computational resources.

Loss functions

Boundary DoU loss

Boundary DoU loss, proposed by Sun et al.18, is a loss function formulation that computes the Dice similarity over the boundary area of the predicted segmentation and its corresponding ground truth, in order to minimise the loss and in turn enhances the segmentation performance of the model.

With d being the pixel width of the boundary region, the Boundary DoU loss is formulated as follows:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{DoU} = \frac{G \cup P - G \cap P}{G \cup P - \alpha * G \cap P'} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In addition to its focus on the boundary area, another important aspect of the BoundaryDoU loss function lies in its adaptability to segmentation targets of different sizes. When the target is large, the boundary area only accounts for a small proportion compared to the whole area of the target. Where the general area could be covered efficiently with the Dice loss, the BoundaryDoU loss could instead be geared towards emphasising more on the boundary area. In such case, using a large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} is preferred. However, when the target is small, neither the interior nor the boundary areas are easily distinguishable, so we need to focus simultaneously on the interior and boundary, and(replace this with thus) a small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} is preferred. To adapt the BoundaryDoU loss to correspond to the relative size of the segmentation target, the coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} is computed with11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \gamma = 1 - 2 \times \frac{C}{S}, \gamma \in [0, 1) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where C denotes the boundary length of the target, which are essentially the count of non-interior foreground pixels, and S denotes its size in pixel counts for the ROI. Here \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} decreases as the object becomes smaller (larger boundary-to-area ratio), and increases for larger objects.

The BoundaryDoU loss primarily impacts both the decoder and the skip connections of the U-Net architecture. This is because (1) these components are responsible for pixel-accurate reconstruction, particularly around boundary regions, and (2) emphasising boundaries through the loss function encourages the network to preserve and enhance edge-related features propagated from earlier encoder layers.

However, boundary-based loss functions typically exhibit a gradient signal that rapidly decays away from the object boundaries. As a result, when the model’s predictions are significantly misaligned with the true boundaries–especially during the early stages of training–the gradients may be too weak to guide effective learning. To address this, it is advisable to combine boundary-focused losses with a global loss function such as the binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss. This combination ensures that the network receives meaningful gradients across the entire object area. Without such a balance, overemphasising boundaries can lead to insufficient learning in the interior regions of the object, ultimately degrading the overall segmentation performance due to poor gradient propagation.

To mitigate these issues, a weighted combination of the BCE loss and the BoundaryDoU loss is employed, formulated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_{total} = \alpha * L_{BCE} + \beta * L_{BoundaryDoU} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} are weighting coefficients for controlling the relative importance of each component in the total loss.

Combining the loss functions is just one mitigation for the vanishing gradient. The other two measures are the careful scheduling of the learning rate, and the adaptive adjustment made through the monitoring ot intermediate features (e.g., the loss gradient). Another major mitigation, the residual connections, has already been embedded in the U-Net architecture.

Learning rate scheduling

We used the Learning rate change test21 to discover the change in the loss value in respond to the change in the learning rate, which allows us to pinpoint a proper initial learning rate to be used in the model training phase.

As we traverse the loss landscape in order to reach the optimum, we may opt to reduce the learning rate once the loss value beings to stagnate; when the rate of improvements begin to slow down, which might be caused by having a too-high learning rate. In our experiments, we used the ReduceLROnPlateau22 that reduces the learning rate by a factor of ten when the rate of change of the loss value has been smaller than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1e^{-3}$$\end{document} for at least three consecutive epochs.

Adaptive adjustments of loss function values

For all variants of the SoftAdapt algorithm, we recalibrate the composite loss weightings after each single training epoch. Though Heydari et al.16 suggested averaging the loss weighting over a range of epochs, for example five, from our observation this resulted in inferior performance compared to readily adjusting after each training epoch.

Training U-Net on the MM-WHS dataset

For the U-Net model trained with the baseline BCE loss, with results shown in Table 1 the mean Dice coefficient was 0.923 ± 0.01 across five folds. Incorporating boundary loss with fixed weighting produced mixed results. A small contribution of boundary supervision (90% BCE + 10% boundary) yielded a marginal improvement (0.924 ± 0.01), whereas larger weightings (20-30%) consistently reduced performance, indicating that excessive emphasis on boundary information negatively impacts segmentation accuracy.

Adaptive reweighting strategies generally outperformed fixed ratios. Among the variants, the standard SoftAdapt with 90% BCE + 10% boundary achieved the highest overall performance (0.932 ± 0.01), surpassing both the baseline and all fixed-weight configurations. Normalised and Loss-weighted SoftAdapt produced competitive results (0.929-0.930 ± 0.01), though they did not match the peak performance of standard SoftAdapt. Similarly, the Adaptive weighting scheme performed well (0.927-0.930 ± 0.01), with the strongest configuration observed at c = 0.1.

These findings demonstrate that U-Net benefits from the introduction of boundary supervision when its contribution is modest and adaptively regulated. In particular, standard SoftAdapt provided both improved accuracy and consistent performance across folds.

Training SwinUNETR on the MM-WHS dataset

The SwinUNETR model, with results shown in Table 2 the baseline BCE loss achieved a lower average Dice score of 0.896 ± 0.02 compared to U-Net. Fixed loss weighting revealed that boundary supervision could be beneficial under specific conditions: the 70% BCE + 10% boundary configuration attained the best overall performance (0.919 ± 0.02), surpassing the baseline by a considerable margin. In contrast, higher boundary ratios (20-30%) reduced performance to below the baseline, suggesting that SwinUNETR is particularly sensitive to the proportion of boundary supervision.

Adaptive strategies yielded mixed outcomes. Standard SoftAdapt with 70% BCE + 10% boundary matched the best fixed configuration (0.919 ± 0.02), while other ratios led to weaker or less stable results. Normalised SoftAdapt exhibited high fold-to-fold variability, most notably at the 80/20 ratio, while Loss-weighted SoftAdapt provided moderate and consistent improvements (0.911 ± 0.02 at 70/10). The adaptive variants also achieved competitive scores (0.908 ± 0.02 at c = 0.025), but none of the variants surpassed the best fixed configuration.

Overall, SwinUNETR attained its strongest results when BCE was kept dominant with only a small boundary contribution. Discussion

Comparison across the two architectures highlights both shared patterns and important differences. In both U-Net and SwinUNETR, introducing a modest amount of boundary supervision (10%) consistently improved segmentation performance relative to the baseline, underscoring the value of contour-aware loss terms for medical image segmentation. However, the models diverged in their response to adaptive weighting strategies.

For U-Net, dynamic reweighting was clearly advantageous: Standard SoftAdapt achieved the highest Dice score (0.932 ± 0.01), outperforming both fixed ratios and other adaptive variants. This suggests that U-Net benefits from the flexibility of adjusting the relative importance of BCE and boundary terms during training. In contrast, SwinUNETR reached its peak performance with a fixed 70/10 BCE-boundary ratio, and adaptive methods provided no consistent improvement beyond this setting. Some strategies, such as normalised SoftAdapt, exhibited considerable instability, highlighting a greater sensitivity of transformer-based architectures to unstable weighting schedules. These results indicate that the optimal loss balancing strategy is architecture-dependent. While convolutional architectures like U-Net benefit substantially from adaptive weighting, transformer-based models such as SwinUNETR achieve their strongest performance with carefully chosen fixed ratios, where excessive or unstable reweighting may be detrimental.

One possible hypothesis regarding the model-dependent nature of the adaptive loss weighting maybe due to the choice of segmentation models we experimented with in this study. Convolutional architectures such as U-Net inherently emphasize local feature extraction through the use of convolution kernels, which are particularly effective at capturing abrupt intensity changes along object boundaries. Since the boundary loss explicitly penalizes misalignment at contour regions, it is naturally synergistic with U-Net’s representational bias: even a modest contribution of boundary supervision can guide the network to refine segmentation contours more effectively. This could explain why U-Net not only tolerated but also benefited substantially from adaptive weighting strategies (e.g., SoftAdapt), which dynamically amplify the influence of boundary-focused supervision when most informative.

In contrast, transformer-based models such as SwinUNETR are designed to capture long-range dependencies and global context via self-attention mechanisms. While this global reasoning is powerful, it may not exploit boundary losses as effectively as convolutional models. Boundary information is inherently local, and without strong inductive biases for locality (as in convolutions), the benefit of contour-focused supervision may be diminished or destabilized. This may account for the observation that SwinUNETR performed best with a fixed, small boundary contribution (70/10 ratio), and did not consistently gain from adaptive reweighting schemes, which at times introduced training instability.

Although the proposed Adaptive Composite loss demonstrated promising performance, its formulation remains static. In this experiment, the start_weight was selected heuristically based on prior experiments with fixed coefficients, while end_weight and step_change were similarly defined. More refined strategies–such as drawing directly on gradient information from past training epochs, as in SoftAdapt–may enable more principled scheduling of loss contributions and yield further improvements.

Limitation and future works

This study has several limitations that point to possible directions for future research.

First, all experiments were conducted with a batch size of 64. While this choice reflects the computational resources available, varying the batch size could impact model stability and performance. In particular, very small batch sizes (e.g., 1-4) may lead to higher fluctuations in convergence, and their effects on segmentation accuracy warrant further investigation.

Second, the definition of boundary pixels is influenced by interpolation choices during volume resizing. In this study, bilinear interpolation was applied to images to balance detail and efficiency, while nearest-neighbor interpolation was used for labels to avoid label ambiguity across neighboring pixels. Alternative interpolation schemes may alter boundary representation and, consequently, boundary loss effectiveness. In addition, due to the resizing scale required to enable training within the limits of available computational resources, there may be greater discrepancies between training and testing performance than would be expected with smaller resizing scales.

Finally, this work relied primarily on the Dice coefficient as the evaluation metric. While widely adopted for segmentation tasks, the Dice score captures spatial overlap but does not directly characterize false positives or false negatives. Metrics such as sensitivity and specificity could provide complementary insights, particularly for clinical use cases where the relative costs of false-positive and false-negative predictions differ. For example, as illustrated in Figure 2, the U-Net trained with the best composite loss setting (90% BCE and 10% BoundaryDoU) achieved higher Dice scores than the baseline, but also produced visibly larger regions of false-positive and false-negative predictions. Incorporating a broader set of evaluation metrics would therefore provide a more nuanced assessment of segmentation quality.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effect of incorporating boundary-aware loss functions and adaptive loss-weighting strategies for medical image segmentation using U-Net and SwinUNETR architectures on the MM-WHS dataset. For U-Net, introducing a small boundary contribution and regulating it through adaptive weighting yielded substantial improvements, with Standard SoftAdapt (90% BCE + 10% boundary) achieving the highest Dice coefficient (0.932 ± 0.01). In contrast, SwinUNETR achieved its strongest performance with a fixed 70% BCE + 10% boundary ratio (0.919 ± 0.02), while more complex adaptive strategies provided no consistent advantage and sometimes introduced instability.

These findings suggest that the optimal loss-balancing strategy is architecture-dependent. Convolution-based models such as U-Net benefit from dynamic reweighting due to their inductive bias toward local boundary features, whereas transformer-based models such as SwinUNETR are more sensitive to unstable weighting and perform best with carefully tuned fixed ratios.

Future work should explore adaptive mechanisms that combine the stability of fixed schemes with the flexibility of dynamic weighting, as well as evaluate segmentation performance using a broader range of metrics beyond Dice to capture false positives and false negatives.
