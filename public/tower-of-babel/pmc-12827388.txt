Epidemic dynamics prediction using fractional SIRD and deep learning
Authors: Ramsha Shafqat, Kinda Abuasbeh, Salma Trabelsi, Marwa Balti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper introduces, analyzes, and numerically investigates a fractional-order SIRD epidemic model employing the normalized Caputo-Fabrizio (NCF) derivative. The model incorporates memory effects and explicitly tracks disease-induced mortality through a deceased compartment, offering a more comprehensive framework for epidemic dynamics. We establish existence, uniqueness, positivity, and population conservation for the proposed system, and present a robust numerical scheme. We demonstrate the influence of the memory parameter and kernel normalization through simulations, and highlight their significance for epidemic forecasting and real-world applications. Deep neural network (DNN) was used to approximate SIRD dynamics with fractional order and improve the accuracy of predictions. The model successfully extracted nonlinear time-dependent patterns from simulated epidemic data through its learning process. The proposed framework demonstrated strong predictive ability, achieving mean square error values as low as 0.00027 and root mean square errors below 0.17 across different compartments. By integrating fractional calculus with deep learning techniques, the model establishes a reliable approach for representing complex disease dynamics and provides meaningful insights that can support public health decision-making.

Introduction

Mathematical modeling is a cornerstone for analyzing, predicting, and managing the transmission dynamics of infectious diseases1,2. Among various frameworks, compartmental models such as the susceptible-infectious-recovered (SIR) and its extensions, including the susceptible-infectious-recovered-deceased (SIRD) model, are fundamental tools for capturing the spread of epidemics and informing public health interventions3. However, classical integer-order models often fail to account for the memory and hereditary properties present in real outbreaks such as variable infectious periods, delayed effects of control measures, and non-exponential waiting times4,5.

Fractional-order differential equations have thus gained prominence in epidemiological modeling for their ability to incorporate memory effects, whereby the system’s current state depends on its historical trajectory5–7. The Caputo derivative, with its power-law kernel, is widely used but suffers from singularities, while the Caputo-Fabrizio (CF) derivative, featuring a non-singular exponential kernel, offers both computational advantages and physically realistic fading memory8–10. Nonetheless, the standard CF kernel is not normalized, which can introduce inconsistencies in the interpretation and scaling of memory effects. The NCF derivative addresses this limitation by ensuring the kernel integrates to unity, thereby preserving the operator’s interpretation as a true weighted average11.

Despite theoretical and practical advances, fractional epidemic models with explicit mortality compartments have not been systematically explored with the NCF derivative. The SIRD model extends the classic SIR framework by introducing a deceased class, capturing fatalities due to infection, a key metric for assessing public health impact and evaluating intervention strategies (e.g. COVID-19, SARS, Ebola).

Fractional operators and related numerical schemes have proved decisive for capturing memory and anomalous transport in complex systems, from porous-media diffusion and nonlinear waves to biological propagation12. Recent work combines fractional modeling with machine learning to enhance stability analysis, parameter inference, and forecasting across rabies and hepatitis B13,14. Meanwhile, new SIQR frameworks with the NCF operator and deep-learning surrogates extend these ideas to broader epidemic settings15. Fractional approaches have also been applied to poliomyelitis using neural networks, highlighting data-integrated strategies for control policy design16. Complementary evidence from vaccine safety research covering HLH, Sweet’s syndrome, and field-wide bibliometrics provides empirical context and motivates risk-aware modeling pipelines17–19.

This study presents, analyzes, and numerically investigates a fractional SIRD epidemic model governed by the NCF operator. We provide a rigorous mathematical analysis of the model’s properties (existence, uniqueness, positivity, and boundedness), derive a robust numerical scheme, and compare the NCF-SIRD model with its classical and unnormalized fractional counterparts (Fig. 1).

In addition to classical numerical analysis, we leverage DNNs to approximate the compartmental solutions and perform model validation. Recent research demonstrates that deep learning algorithms, particularly DNNs, can effectively complement fractional-order models by offering enhanced predictive accuracy, robustness to noise, and superior capability in capturing complex, nonlinear relationships within simulation or empirical data20–22. In our approach, DNN techniques are employed to fit and forecast the solution trajectories of the SIRD compartments, with model performance assessed using mean squared error, regression analysis, and error distribution metrics. Theoretical analysis

We establish the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the NCF-SIRD system, providing a foundation for the model’s mathematical validity. The demonstration relies on fixed-point theory and essential properties of the NCF fractional operator.

The simulations confirm that the use of the NCF derivative substantially modifies epidemic dynamics compared to the classical and unnormalized fractional models. In particular, the normalization ensures a physically consistent weighting of historical states, resulting in more interpretable and realistic predictions for diseases with incubation or latent periods. The NCF-SIRD model thus offers a flexible framework for exploring a wide range of memory effects in epidemiology, providing a valuable tool for both theoretical analysis and public health forecasting.

A feedforward DNN was employed to approximate the dynamics of the NCF–SIRD model. The final architecture consisted of three hidden layers with 10, 100, and 10 neurons, utilizing sigmoid activation functions in the hidden layers and a linear activation function in the output layer to perform the regression task. This configuration provided the best performance among several tested architectures, achieving a mean squared error (MSE = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5.6e-08$$\end{document}5.6e-08) and a root mean square error (RMSE = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2.4e-4$$\end{document}2.4e-4) across the training, validation, and testing datasets, and outperforming both shallower and deeper alternatives. The chosen structure is consistent with prior studies, which demonstrate that compact feedforward networks with two to four hidden layers can effectively capture nonlinear interactions in dynamical and epidemiological systems20–22. The wide middle layer (100 neurons) captures high-dimensional nonlinear features, while the narrower outer layers enhance feature compression and generalization. Sigmoid activations were adopted due to their smoothness and proven universal approximation capability24,25, yielding more stable convergence than ReLU when combined with the Levenberg–Marquardt optimization algorithm. The model was trained for 1000 epochs with a data split of 70%, 15%, and 15% for training, validation, and testing, respectively. Hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, and early stopping were tuned to ensure convergence and prevent overfitting. Model trajectories generated using the fractional Adams–Bashforth numerical scheme were used to maintain consistency between the simulated dynamics and DNN predictions.

The integration of DNNs with the fractional Adams-Bashforth scheme significantly enhances the model’s analytical and predictive capabilities. By refining network weights and reducing residual errors, the DNN enables practical training, validation, and testing, allowing precise modeling of dynamic interactions within the system. Regression analyses and error metrics confirm the stability and convergence of this combined approach, highlighting the synergy between data-driven methods and fractional calculus.

Conclusion

This work introduced a fractional SIRD model based on the NCF derivative and provided a rigorous analysis of existence, uniqueness, positivity, and boundedness. Kernel normalization clarifies the physical role of memory and eliminates inconsistencies present in unnormalized operators. We also developed a stable first-order numerical scheme. We demonstrated, through targeted simulations, that the NCF model can reproduce epidemic dynamics more realistically than both classical and unnormalized fractional counterparts. The combination of deep neural networks with the fractional SIRD model yielded an efficient approximation of epidemic trajectories and improved predictive accuracy. The methodology demonstrates that DNNs can effectively work with fractional models, as they combine the clear understanding of mathematical frameworks with the flexibility of data-driven learning methods. The integration of these methods creates a track toward developing real-time epidemic forecasting systems that unite theoretical models with empirical evidence.

The present study adopts homogeneous mixing and omits vital dynamics (births and natural deaths), choices that help isolate memory effects but limit long-horizon realism. The framework admits natural extensions that remain compatible with our analytical results: demographic terms can be added without altering the NCF normalization or regularity requirements; heterogeneous contact structure can be introduced via age groups with a contact matrix or via network models on graphs (recovering homogeneous mixing in the dense, uniform limit); and the memory parameter can vary across subgroups or locations (e.g. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _a$$\end{document}αa by age/region, or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _i$$\end{document}αi on networks) and even in time. Looking ahead, priorities include parameter estimation from real-world data with uncertainty quantification, exploration of generalized fractional operators, incorporation of stochastic effects, and the development of physics-informed neural networks for greater interpretability. These advances will further strengthen the utility of NCF-based fractional models for real-time epidemic forecasting and policy design.
