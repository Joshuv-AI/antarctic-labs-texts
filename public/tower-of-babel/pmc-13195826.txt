Antimicrobial resistance patterns of enteropathogens and probiotic potential of infant gut-derived lactic acid bacteria in Maroua (Far North Cameroon)
Authors: Francky Steve Nodem Sohanang, Fabrice Katoua Ndeguena, Didiane Yemele Mefokou, Marie Jeanne Corina Mengue, Arno Palou Zoua, Simeon Pierre Fodouop Chegaing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastrointestinal infections remain a major public health concern, particularly in regions where antibiotic misuse has accelerated antimicrobial resistance. Identifying safe alternatives including probiotics is therefore critical. This study aimed to evaluate the probiotic potential of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) isolated from the gut microbiota of infants under six months of age in Maroua, Cameroon.MethodologyWe conducted a retrospective study of stool culture records from the Maroua Regional Hospital (from January 2020 to december 2024), followed by a cross-sectional laboratory study. Ten healthy infants provided stool samples, from which LAB were isolated on De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) agar. Isolates were assessed for acid and bile salt tolerance, autoaggregation, and antimicrobial activity were tested using the agar well diffusion method.ResultsAmong 563 records, confirmed gastrointestinal infections accounted for 5.68% [3.78–7.58%]. Bacterial pathogens represented 28.14% (Salmonella enterica, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus spp., Yersinia enterocolitica), parasites 25.01% (Trichomonas intestinalis, amoeba cysts), and fungi 46.87%. Enteropathogens showed multidrug resistance to fluoroquinolones (54.65%), and cephalosporins (42.86%), with complete sensitivity to carbapenems. LAB isolates demonstrated strong probiotic potential: survival at pH 2–5, tolerance to bile salts (0.3–1%), autoaggregation (61–81% at 24 h), and coaggregation with pathogens (62–81%). They inhibited E. coli, Proteus mirabilis, Salmonella spp., and Enterobacter cloacae, particularly in acidic supernatants. Biochemical identification revealed Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, L. pentosus, Lacticaseibacillus casei, and L. paracasei.ConclusionInfant-derived LAB strains exhibit promising probiotic properties and may serve as complementary strategies for preventing gastrointestinal infections. Further studies are needed to confirm their safety and clinical relevance.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal infections are a major public health problem, especially in regions where access to clean water, sanitation, and timely medical care is limited [1, 2]. These infections, which affect the stomach, small intestine, and colon, are caused by a wide range of pathogens, such as viruses, parasites, fungi, and bacteria [2]. It often manifests as symptoms such as abdominal pain, cramping, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and dehydration [1–4]. In many cases, antibiotics are prescribed as the first-line treatment. However, their frequent misuse has led to a troubling rise in antimicrobial resistance (AMR), making certain infections difficult to treat [5, 6]. The spread of resistant strains is not just a clinical challenge; it is a warning signal for the future of infectious disease management.

In this context, probiotics have emerged as a promising alternative strategy to conventional antibiotics for the management of enteric infections [7–9]. These live microorganisms, when administered in adequate amounts, confer health benefits to the host by maintaining intestinal microbial balance, improving gut function, and enhancing resistance to pathogenic colonization [10–14]. In recent years, on the basis of in vitro and in vivo studies, many probiotic products have been developed to improve an organism’s health [8]. LAB constitute a wide range of genera, and they include a considerable number of species with a long history of use by humans and have been used for food production and preservation [15]. Lactic acid bacteria including bifidobacteria are the bacterial groups most extensively studied and is used as a probiotic [16, 17]. LABs with probiotic activity are usually present in the gut of humans and other animals, and their numbers may vary with the animal species, the age of the host, or the location within the gut [11]. Various nutritional and therapeutic effects of probiotics strains has been developed such as maintaining the intestinal microbial balance in humans and animals, improving the nutritional quality of food and feed, stimulating vitamin synthesis and enzyme production to ameliorate lactose intolerance, suppressing cancer, improving digestion, reducing serum cholesterol levels, preventing and treating allergies and modulating the host immune system [13–15]. Many different criteria have been used in the selection of strains to be used as probiotics. These bacteria should be nonpathogenic, resistant to gastric acidity and bile salts, capable of adhering to the intestinal mucosa, and able to inhibit harmful microbes [16–18].

Although digestive microbiota has been investigated in Cameroon, no study has yet examined the occurrence of lactic acid bacteria and their probiotic potential within the digestive microbiota of infants [19]. The infant gut represents a critical ecological niche, where endogenous LAB strains may contribute to early immune development, protection against gastrointestinal pathogens, and modulation of host physiology [20–22]. To date, no study has systematically investigated the probiotic potential of LAB isolated from healthy infants. The aim of this study was to give the prevalence and resistance profile of enteric pathogens in Maroua and therefore explore the probiotic potential of LAB isolated from healthy infants.

Methodology

Study design and data collection

The retrospective dataset covered a period of January 2020 to December 2024 was carried out using stool culture records archived at the Bacteriology Laboratory of the Maroua Regional Hospital (Far North, Cameroon). The study population included all patients who presented to the laboratory for stool culture examinations during this period. Only records with complete information on patient demographics, pathogen identification, and antimicrobial (antibiotics and antifungal susceptibility) testing were included. Records with missing or incomplete data (e.g., absence of demographic details, missing culture results or incomplete antimicrobial resistance profiles) were excluded. Data were collected manually using a structured questionnaire to ensure traceability and consistency, and anonymized codes were assigned to guarantee patient confidentiality. The dataset comprised the identities of the isolated organisms, their resistance profiles, and associated sociodemographic information.

Microbial identification

Bacterial and fungal isolates were identified using standardized biochemical galleries, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Pure colonies from stool cultures were tested with commercial systems: API 20E for Enterobacteriaceae, API Staph for Staphylococcus spp. and API 20AUX for yeasts. Galleries were incubated at 35–37 °C for 24–48 h, and reactions interpreted using the manufacturer’s database. Identification was confirmed by matching biochemical profiles to reference codes, ensuring accuracy and reproducibility.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antibacterial assay

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of bacterial isolates was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar (Biolife Italiana, Italy), following EUCAST guidelines [23]. Standardized bacterial suspensions (0.5 McFarland) were prepared and uniformly inoculated onto agar plates. Commercial antibiotic disks were applied, and plates were incubated at 35–37 °C for 18–24 h. The antibiotics tested included β‑lactams (amoxicillin, ampicillin, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, cefotaxime), carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem), aminoglycosides (gentamicin, amikacin), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin), macrolides (erythromycin), sulfonamides (trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole), and tetracyclines (tetracycline, doxycycline).

Antifungal assay

Antifungal susceptibility testing of yeast isolates was carried out using the disk diffusion method on Sabouraud dextrose agar (Biolife Italiana, Italy). Standardized inocula were prepared and inoculated onto agar plates, and commercial antifungal disks (ketoconazole, clotrimazole, econazole, miconazole, fluconazole, and nystatin) were applied. Plates were incubated at 35 °C for 24–48 h, and zones of inhibition were measured. In line with recommendations from the Ministry of Public Health in the Far North Region, and considering sociocultural practices that may encourage self‑medication, antifungal susceptibility was reported primarily in terms of sensitive isolates, while intermediate and resistant categories were grouped together and considered as resistant. This approach was adopted to minimize misinterpretation and reduce inappropriate antifungal prescriptions.

In both tests, inhibition zone diameters were measured in millimeters and interpreted using EUCAST Clinical Breakpoint Tables corresponding to the year of testing: version 10.0 (2020), version 11.0 (2021), version 12.0 (2022), version 13.1 (2023), and version 14.0 (2024) [23]. Although different versions were applied across the study period, the critical breakpoints for the antibiotics tested in this work did not considerably change between 2020 and 2024. Therefore, all isolates were interpreted consistently, and the classification of susceptible or resistant remained comparable throughout the study.

Eight pathogen isolates preserved during the study period were recovered and used for subsequent laboratory assays, including antimicrobial activity testing with lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and evaluation of their coaggregation capacity.

Isolation and characterization of lactic acid bacteria from stools

Lactic acid bacteria were isolated from stool samples of infants under six months of age following previously described methods [24]. They were in apparent good health and had no recent antibiotic exposure. After homogenization in sterile saline solution, the fecal suspensions were plated on MRS agar (Biolife Italiana, Italy) and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 48 h. Colonies with typical morphology were purified through successive subcultures. The strains were preserved for subsequent physiological and functional analyses, with the aim of identifying endogenous strains potentially suitable for probiotic applications in the local context. For physiological characterization, the thermoresistance capacity (60 °C/30 min) and salt tolerance (2.5%, 5%, 10%) were determined via procedures described in the literature [24].

Probiotic characterization of lactic acid bacteria

Acid stress tolerance tests

The ability of the lactic acid isolates to survive in an acidic environment simulating gastric conditions was evaluated via a quantitative method in a flat-bottom microplate. Each well contained MRS broth adjusted to several pH values (2, 3, 4 and 5) and was inoculated with 10 µL of 24 h broth culture of each isolate. The microplates were incubated at 37°C, and bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 620 nm at 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 12 h and 24 h [15]. An increase in optical density compared with that of the unseeded control was considered an indicator of growth, reflecting the ability of the isolate to resist gastric acidity.

Biliary stress resistance tests

A quantitative method was carried out in a flat-bottomed microplate. The wells contained MRS broth supplemented with Oxbile (Sigma Chemical Co., United States) at the same concentrations (0.3%, 0.5% and 1%), which were inoculated with 10 µL of 24 h culture broth of each isolate [15]. The microplates were incubated at 37 °C, and bacterial growth was monitored by optical density measurement at 600 nm at 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 12 h and 24 h, allowing dynamic analysis of resistance to biliary stress.

Autoaggregation and coaggregation tests

The autoaggregation test was performed to evaluate the ability of LAB isolates to aggregate together, a property associated with their potential for adhesion to mucous membranes according to the method of Del Re et al. [25]. After the isolates were incubated for 18 h at 37 °C in From MRS broth, the bacterial cells were harvested via centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 3 min. The resulting pellet was washed twice and then resuspended in 10 mL of physiological water to obtain a cell density corresponding to the standard of McFarland No. 2. The suspensions were then incubated at 37 °C in flat-bottom tubes without agitation. Autoaggregation was assessed by measuring the optical density at the surface of the preparation using a spectrophotometer set at 620 nm at times t = 0, 2, 4, 12 and 24 h. Particular care was taken not to disturb the tubes (the cells being handled) during the readings to preserve the natural stratification of the cells. The coaggregation test was performed according to the same protocol as that of autoaggregation, with the difference that the LAB suspensions were mixed in equal parts with those of the pathogenic strains. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C without agitation. Coaggregation was assessed by measuring the optical density at the surface of the tubes via a spectrophotometer set at 620 nm at times t = 0, 2, 4, 12 and 24 h.

Evaluation of antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activity of LAB against pathogens was assessed via a previously described method [12]. This protocol is based on the ability of LAB to produce, during their growth, secondary metabolites with antimicrobial properties. Each isolate was cultured in MRS broth at 37 °C for 24 h. Briefly, the supernatants obtained after centrifugation (neutralized and non-neutralized) were preserved for inhibition tests. At the same time, 20 mL of Muller–Hinton agar (Biolife Italiana, Italy) was poured into Petri dishes and then inoculated with 100 µL of a suspension of the chosen pathogenic isolates. Once the agar was dry after inoculation, 6 mm diameter wells were dug using a sterile punch. Each well received 35 µL of culture supernatant from LAB. The dishes were left at room temperature for one hour to allow diffusion and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The appearance of a clear zone around the well was interpreted as an inhibition of the growth of the target strain. The diameter of this area was measured in millimeters, allowing the identification of lactic acid isolates that exhibited significant antimicrobial activity.

Pathogenicity tests: gelatinase, hemolysin production and antibiotic resistance

Gelatinase production

The ability of LAB to produce gelatinase was evaluated as described in the literature [26]. Each isolate was streaked onto MRS agar supplemented with gelatin and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Afterward, the culture was incubated at 4 °C for 5 h, and liquefaction of the medium was observed. In the absence of modification of the medium, the isolate was considered “gelatinase negative”.

Hemolytic activity

Hemolysin is an enzyme involved in the lysis of red blood cells. The isolates were streaked on the surface of blood agar (Biolife Italiana, Italy) previously poured into Petri dish plates and then incubated at 37 °C for 48 h [27]. The appearance of the agar around the colonies suggests the following interpretation: the absence of modification suggested nonhemolytic activity; a greenish area, related to the reduction of hemoglobin to methemoglobin, indicates partial hemolysis of type α; and a clear halo around the colony reveals total hemolysis of type β, which is associated with pathogenic potential.

Antibiotic sensitivity

The sensitivity of the isolated LAB isolates was assessed via the diffusion method in agar discs [23]. Ten antibiotics belonging to different pharmacological families were selected: meropenem, vancomycin, tobramycin, chloramphenicol, gentamicin, ciprofloxacin, amikacin, amoxicillin-acid clavulanic acid, ceftriaxone and levofloxacin. Bacterial inocula were prepared from 24-h-old colonies and then tightly inoculated on MRS agar via the streak technique. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured in millimeters for each antibiotic and compared to the critical diameters of the guide for the assessment of bacterial susceptibility to antimicrobials of human importance and veterinarians of the European Food Safety Authority.

Identification of the isolates

Characterization of the isolates was performed through their analytical profile index (API). The experiments were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The results gathered from the API galleries were computerized via the software Apident 5.2 (BioMerieux, Craponne, France). The identities obtained from the Apident software were confirmed online (https://apiweb.biomerieux.com).

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis of all variables was performed for associations of disease (gastro-intestinal infection) with demographic and temporal profiles via the chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test for significance at the 95% probability level. Logistic regression (univariate and multivariate) was performed for odds ratios at 95%. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was interpreted according to the EUCAST [23], and Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 was used to validate the methodology.

For the experimental assays, each test was performed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was conducted using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for selected datasets. When significant differences were observed, mean comparisons were carried out using Duncan’s multiple range test at a 95% confidence level. The overall functional data, including inhibition percentages under acid and bile salt stress and auto and coaggregation percentages, were subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce data dimensionality, identify correlations between variables, and synthesize the functional profiles of the bacterial strains. The data were entered and structured in Microsoft Excel 2016 and then analyzed via XLStat™ v. 2016.02 software (Addinsoft, France).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The necessary authorizations for the conduct of this study were obtained from the Faculty of Sciences of the University of Ngaoundere (No. 2025-0463/UN/R/D-S/CD-SBM/DFS), of the Regional Delegate of Health Public of the Far North, as well as the Management of the Regional Hospital of Maroua. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Regional Ethics Committee for Research in Human Health of the Far North Region (No. 0186/CE/25/CRERSH-EN/SG approved at 12/08/2025). An Informed, free and written consent was obtained from the participants. Confidentiality was ensured by anonymizing all retrospective records and removing personal identifiers.

For infant stool sampling, written informed consent was prior obtained from parents or legal guardians. All samples were assigned anonymized codes to ensure participant confidentiality. Data were stored securely with restricted access to authorized personnel, and results were reported in aggregate form to protect the privacy of minors.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the population

A total of 563 stool culture records were analyzed from patients clinically suspected of having gastrointestinal infections. A male predominance (52.93%) suggests comparable exposure between sexes. Adults aged 25-55 years were the most affected group (42.63%), followed by children under 5 years (28.60%). July and August presented peak case rates (14.57% and 11.90%), whereas December and January presented the lowest rates (4.61% and 4.44%, respectively). (Table 1).

Overall prevalence of gastrointestinal infections

Among all the stool samples analyzed at the Regional Hospital of Maroua from January 2020 to December 2024, only 5.68% [3.78 – 7.58%] tested positive for gastrointestinal infection (Fig. 1).

Bacteria represented 28.14%, including Salmonella enterica, S. enterica typhi, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus sp., and Yersinia entericolitica. Antifungal resistance profile

The resistance profile of the yeast isolates identified (18) at Hospital Maroua Regional revealed complete sensitivity to ketoconazole and clotrimazole (100%), followed by econazole (94.44%), miconazole and fluconazole (83.33%), which reflects the excellent efficacy of these antifungals. On the other hand, nystatin has the highest resistance rate (33.66%), which could limit its use (Table 4).

Antibiotic resistance profile

The antibiotic susceptibility profiles of nine bacterial isolates revealed variable resistance patterns across drug classes. Penicillin derivatives showed limited efficacy, whereas ampicillin was effective only against Escherichia coli (100%), whereas ticarcillin exhibited complete resistance across the tested strains (100%). Cephalosporins displayed mixed results, with cefotaxime and cefuroxime resulting in 50% sensitivity, and cefepime and ceftriaxone were associated with high resistance rates (100% and 83.33%, respectively). Carbapenems (imipenem and meropenem) demonstrated full efficacy (100%) across all tested isolates (Table 5). With respect to fluoroquinolones, ciprofloxacin was effective in 75% of cases, whereas nalidixic acid and ofloxacin had resistance rates of 100% and 50%, respectively. Aminoglycosides such as amikacin and tobramycin showed good sensitivity (80% and 62.5%), whereas gentamicin had a balanced profile (50% sensitivity). Among other antibacterial agents, chloramphenicol maintained full efficacy (100%), whereas trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and fosfomycin showed moderate resistance, with 42.86% and 75%, respectively. Vancomycin and tetracycline resistance was complete across the tested strains (100%). These findings highlight the high efficacy of carbapenems and chloramphenicol, in contrast with the widespread resistance to penicillins, some cephalosporins, and tetracyclines (Table 5).

Probiotic characteristics of lactic acid bacteria

In this study, ten stool samples were taken from infants aged less than six months, and 5 isolates were retained for assessment of their physiological and probiotic potential and identification. The physiological characteristics of the LAB revealed diverse morphologies, including long and short bacilli. All the isolates grew at both 45 °C and 60 °C, confirming their thermophilic nature and notable heat resistance, traits favorable for industrial and food-related applications. Additionally, all the strains tolerated 2.5% NaCl, indicating moderate osmotic resilience. Only isolates S5, S6, S7, and S10 sustained growth at 5% NaCl, suggesting enhanced resistance to osmotic stress. However, none of the strains survived at 10% NaCl.

Resistance of lactic acid bacteria isolates in acidic environments

The inhibition of the LAB isolates under acidic conditions revealed that resistance was significantly (P<0.05) affected by the pH and incubation duration (Table 6). At pH 2, all the isolates presented high inhibition rates after 2 h, with S6 showing the strongest sensitivity (76.83%), whereas S10 presented the lowest inhibition (58.23%). As the pH increased to 3, the degree of inhibition remained substantial but slightly decreased, indicating persistent acid stress. At pH values of 4 and 5, the inhibition rates decreased significantly for all the isolates, particularly for S5 and S10, which maintained values below 10% at pH 5.

The growth evolution over time varied among the isolates. S2 and S6 maintained relatively high inhibition levels throughout the 24-hour incubation, whereas S5 and S10 showed a progressive decline in inhibition over time, indicative of potential metabolic adaptation or survival. For example, S5 decreased from 65.86% at pH 2 (2 h) to 42.83% at 24 h, whereas S10 decreased from 58.23% to 31.96% under the same conditions. This revealed that S5 and S10 presented greater tolerance rates, particularly at pH values of 4 and 5, with inhibition rates frequently less than 20% after 6 hours.

The ability of the LAB isolates (S2, S5, S6, S7, and S10) to inhibit bile salts varied significantly with the concentration (0.3%, 0.5%, and 1%, respectively) and incubation time (2 h to 24 h). Isolates S5 and S7 presented the most interesting profiles, with sustained viability under bile stress, positioning them as promising probiotic candidates.

Coaggregation between lactic acid bacteria and pathogenic strains

The percentages of coaggregation between LAB and pathogenic isolates (E. coli and S. aureus) over time (2 h, 4 h, 12 h, and 24 h) are shown in Table 9. The results revealed a progressive increase in aggregation levels, with significant differences depending on LAB‒pathogen combinations. For E. coli, the highest degree of coaggregation at 24 h was observed with isolate S7 (73.77%), followed by S2, S5, S10, and S6 (68.14, 67.69, 66.53 and 62.16%, respectively). The initial values at 2 h were low (3.76–13.56%), indicating gradual interaction. Statistical analysis confirmed significant variation with time (P <0.05). The coaggregation rates were generally greater when Staphylococcus aureus was used, reaching 81.36% with isolate S5 at 24 h, followed by isolates S6, S7, S2, and S10. Early interactions were more pronounced than those with E. coli, with values ranging from 4.14% to 26.39% at 2 h. These findings suggest that LAB can physically interact with pathogenic strains, with increasing coaggregation over time. Isolates S7 and S5 stood out for their strong aggregation with both pathogens, indicating potential competitive or synergistic adhesion properties relevant to probiotic strategies aimed at limiting pathogen colonization.

Pathogenicity of lactic acid bacteria

The pathogenicity of the isolated LAB was assessed through gelatinase, hemolysin production and antibiotic resistance. We did not observe gelatinase or hemolysin production in any of the tested LAB isolates. This enzymatic profile is highly favorable for probiotic evaluation, as it indicates a lack of invasive and cytotoxic potential. Specifically, the absence of gelatinase suggests a limited ability to degrade host structural proteins, whereas the lack of hemolysin eliminates the risk of red blood cell lysis. In terms of antibiotic resistance, all the isolates were sensitive to meropenem and amoxicillin–clavulanic acid, with inhibition zones exceeding 30 mm. In contrast, only vancomycin exhibited systematic resistance across all the isolates.

Antimicrobial activity of lactic acid bacteria isolates

The antimicrobial activity of the LAB isolates in non-neutralized and pH-neutralized supernatants was assessed, and the data are presented in Table 10. Under non-neutralized conditions, isolate S6 exhibited the strongest antimicrobial activity, with inhibition zones of 12 mm against Salmonella, 16 mm against E. coli, and 20 mm against Proteus mirabilis. After pH neutralization, the antimicrobial activity decreased significantly. S6 exhibited notable inhibitory effects on E. coli (12 mm) and Proteus mirabilis (15 mm), whereas the other isolates presented reduced or minimal activity (6–10 mm). Inhibition against fungal and gram-negative pathogens remained low and stable (6–7 mm) across all the isolates. Comparative analysis revealed differences (p<0.05) between neutralized and non-neutralized supernatants, confirming the stronger antimicrobial effect of acidic supernatants. These results suggest that the antimicrobial effects are strains dependent, are partially pH dependent, with isolate S6 maintaining the highest bioactivity under both conditions.

Biochemical characteristics of LAB

Bacterial identification revealed that all the isolates belonged to the genera Lacticaseibacillus and Lactiplantibacillus, with identification scores ranging from 98% to 99.8%. Isolates S2 and S7 were both identified as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum. The isolates S5, S6 and S10 were identified as Lacticaseibacillus casei, Lactiplantibacillus pentosus, and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei respectively.

Discussion

The laboratory of the Regional Hospital of Maroua received 563 patients for the diagnosis of gastrointestinal infection between 2020 and 2024. Stool culture revealed a prevalence of gastrointestinal infections of 5.68%. These results are lower than those reported in Kousseri (30%) [28], Douala 82.4% [5] and 27% [2], Mbouda (25.7%) [29], Dschang (74.26%) [1], and Yaoundé (59.5%) [30]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in diagnostic practices, local epidemiological contexts, and environmental exposures, particularly water quality and sanitation infrastructure. The predominance of infections among children under five years and the elderly highlights age‑related vulnerability, consistent with findings from Yaoundé [30, 31], where immunosuppression and seasonal water contamination are major aggravating factors [30, 31]. The microbial spectrum identified in Maroua included fungi, bacteria, and parasites, with Candida spp. predominating (46.85%). This fungal predominance contrasts with bacterial dominance has been reported in Cameroon and may be explained by frequent antibiotic use leading to intestinal dysbiosis, as well as malnutrition [3]. While oral candidiasis can occasionally lead to the presence of Candida spp. in stool samples, the isolates reported here were interpreted as gastrointestinal colonization or infection, given that the samples were obtained specifically for suspected gastrointestinal disease. The predominance observed in the 0–4 age group is consistent with the known vulnerability of young children to intestinal dysbiosis, often linked to frequent antibiotic exposure and malnutrition in the study setting. The detection of Salmonella enterica, E. coli, and Yersinia enterocolitica confirms the persistence of enteric pathogens associated with food and water contamination, while the presence of T. intestinalis and amoebae cysts underscores the role of protozoan parasites in gastrointestinal morbidity. These findings show the need for rational antibiotic use to prevent fungal overgrowth and highlight the importance of improving water quality and hygiene practices to reduce bacterial and parasitic transmission.

In terms of resistance to antimicrobials, Candida strains remained sensitive to classical azoles such as ketoconazole and clotrimazole, while reduced susceptibility was observed with fluconazole and nystatin, underscoring the need for cautious use of antifungal agents to prevent therapeutic failure. Carbapenems maintained full activity (100%) against E. coli and Salmonella spp. in Maroua, confirming their role as last-resort agents that remain preserved locally. In contrast, carbapenem resistance has been reported in Yaoundé among E. coli isolated from childhood diarrhea [4], underscoring the importance of regional surveillance and stewardship to prevent similar emergence. Classical cephalosporins demonstrated good efficacy against E. coli (87.5%) and moderate activity against Salmonella spp. (66.7%), supporting their relevance when microbiological confirmation is available. Penicillins and aminoglycosides showed reduced sensitivity (≈ 50%), reflecting selective pressure from frequent empirical use, as documented in Mbouda [29] and Kousseri [28]. The limited efficacy of fluoroquinolones (50% sensitivity against Salmonella spp.) confirms their declining utility, consistent with reports from Douala [32] and Fako among HIV-positive patients [33]. These findings underscore the imperative for rational prescribing practices, reinforced antimicrobial stewardship interventions, and sustained surveillance systems to safeguard therapeutic efficacy and mitigate the escalation of antimicrobial resistance.

The tolerance of LAB isolates from healthy infants in Maroua to acidic and biliary environments highlights their potential as probiotic candidates. All isolates survived exposure to low pH (2–5) and bile salts (0.3–1%), which indicates resilience to gastrointestinal conditions and supports their ability to colonize the intestinal tract. These findings are consistent with those reported in China, where Lactobacillus spp. and Leuconostoc spp. demonstrated survival rates between 50% and 90% under similar conditions [13]. Furthermore, specific strains such as Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum have been shown to exhibit high tolerance, with survival exceeding 85% at pH 2 and 80% in 0.3% bile salts [14]. The comparable performance of local isolates suggests that infant-derived LAB in Maroua may possess probiotic properties similar to reference recognized strains. This resilience underscores their potential role in promoting gut health and preventing dysbiosis in early childhood, while also contributing to the global effort to identify region-specific probiotic resources.

All LAB had good autoaggregation capacity, especially after 12 to 24 h. Isolates S2, S6, S7, and S10 reached rates above 75% after 24 h, indicating strong cell cohesion. This behavior favors intestinal colonization and biofilm formation, as also observed by other authors working on isolates from children’s stools [16]. Coaggregation tests between LAB and pathogens revealed significant interactions. After 24 h, some microbial associations reached over 80% aggregation, notably between isolate S5 and S. aureus. Isolates S7 and S6 also showed interesting interactions with E. coli and S. aureus. Comparable findings have been documented in previous studies [7, 18], which demonstrated that certain LAB strains are capable of binding to pathogenic bacteria, thereby restricting their multiplication and dissemination.

The antimicrobial activity of the non-neutralized supernatants clearly inhibited E. coli, Proteus, Salmonella, and Enterobacter, especially for isolate S6. Greater inhibition of enterotoxigenic E. coli by L. rhamnosus and Limosilactobacillus reuteri [18] as well as Shigella sp. and Proteus sp [12]. has also been reported. In contrast, tests with neutralized supernatants revealed weaker inhibition, suggesting that antimicrobial effects partly depend on acidity. The increased efficacy observed in acidic supernatants may be attributed to organic acids lowering the pH or to bacteriocin-like inhibitory substances. Further studies are needed to characterize the nature of these antimicrobial compounds. In terms of safety, all our isolates were nonpathogenic. They do not produce gelatinase or hemolysin, eliminating any risk of toxicity or tissue invasion. These findings align with other studies [12, 18], which also reported the absence of hemolysis in isolates from infants.

In this study, antibiotic resistance testing was performed on LAB isolates to verify the absence of acquired resistance genes. LAB intended for probiotic use must remain free of such traits, as they can be horizontally transferred to commensal gut bacteria, posing biosafety risks. This concern justifies our approach and aligns with other reports where transferable resistance genes have been detected [34]. Complementary MIC determination and molecular analyses remain essential to strengthen reliability.

Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (S2, S7), Lactiplantibacillus pentosus (S6), Lacticaseibacillus casei (S5), and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (S10) were identified in the healthy digestive tract of infants from Maroua. These species are well known for their probiotic properties and are frequently cited in the literature [8, 13, 14]. Their presence in healthy infants suggests early colonization by beneficial strains capable of contributing to intestinal microbiota balance. Moreover, these findings reinforce the idea that local isolates may exhibit probiotic potential comparable to internationally recognized strains, opening perspectives for their valorization as region-specific probiotic resources adapted to local populations.

Conclusion

This study investigated the probiotic potential of LAB isolated from healthy infants in Maroua, where gastrointestinal infections had a 5.68% prevalence and high antibiotic resistance among enteropathogens. Five indigenous strains (L. plantarum, L. pentosus, L. casei, and L. paracasei) were identified. All the isolates were nonpathogenic, thermophilic, acid-tolerant and bile-tolerant and demonstrated strong autoaggregation and coaggregation with pathogens. Notably, L. pentosus showed significant antimicrobial activity, especially under acidic conditions. These findings support the development of locally adapted probiotic formulations for pediatric digestive health, although further in vivo and formulation studies are recommended.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, a limitation due to the use of successive EUCAST guideline versions (2020–2024). Annual updates may introduce changes in breakpoints that could affect resistance trends. However, for the antibiotics included in our analysis, the critical thresholds remained stable across versions. Future studies may consider reinterpreting isolates using a single EUCAST version to further strengthen comparability across time. Second, biochemical identification methods (API systems), while standardized, are less definitive than molecular techniques such as 16S rRNA gene sequencing or MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, which could provide more precise species-level identification. Third, the in vitro probiotic assays, while indicative of potential functionality, do not guarantee in vivo efficacy or safety, and clinical trials would be required to validate therapeutic applications. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable preliminary data on gastrointestinal pathogen resistance profiles and the probiotic potential of infant-derived LAB strains in this underexplored region.
