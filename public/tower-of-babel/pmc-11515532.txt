Factors contributing to organelle genomes size variation and the intracellular DNA transfer in Polygonaceae
Authors: Yi Xiong, Xiong Lei, Yanli Xiong, Yingjie Liu, Zhixiao Dong, Junming Zhao, Qingqing Yu, Xiao Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The use of complete organelle genomes, including chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes, is a powerful molecular method for studying biological evolution and gene transfer. However, in the case of Polygonaceae, an important family with numerous edible, medicinal, and ornamental species, the mitochondrial genomes of only three species have been sequenced and analyzed. In this study, we present the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of two important Tibetan medicinal plants, Bistorta viviparum and B. macrophyllum. All the organelle genomes are assembled into a single circular structure and contain a common set of 32 protein-coding genes (PCGs). Some genes such as rps2 and ndhF were found to have high nucleotide polymorphism (Pi) in the chloroplast genomes, while cox1, mttB and rps12 showed pronounced Pi values in the mitochondrial genomes. Furthermore, our analysis revealed that most chloroplast genes and mitochondrial PCGs in Polygonaceae plants are under purifying selection. However, a few genes, including the chloroplast gene psaJ and the mitochondrial genes ccmFc, atp8 and nad4, showed positive selection in certain Polygonaceae plants, as indicated by a Ka/Ks ratio greater than one. Structural variation analysis revealed a wealth of differences between the mitochondrial genomes of five Polygonaceae species, with a particularly notable large-scale inversion observed between Reynoutria japonica and Fallopia aubertii. Furthermore, an analysis of the homologous sequences in the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes revealed that the rps7 has been transferred from the chloroplast to the mitochondrial genome in all five Polygonaceae species. Finally, ecological niche models were constructed for B. viviparum and B. macrophyllum, indicating that mean annual temperature and altitude are the main climatic factors influencing the distribution of both species. Although the current distribution of B. viviparum is significantly wider than that of B. macrophyllum, projections suggest that the optimal growth ranges of both species will expand in the future, with B. macrophyllum potentially exceeding B. viviparum. This study not only contributes to the plastid genome database for Polygonaceae plants, but also provides theoretical insights into the adaptive evolution of these plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10914-x.

Introduction

Endosymbiotic events and subsequent genetic integration led to the formation of chloroplasts and mitochondria [1]. Compared with the highly conserved plant plastid genome and the compact animal mitochondrial genome, the plant mitochondrial genome has many unique features, including significant variation in genome size and structure, yet genes that are extremely conserved; the distribution of genes is highly scattered and contains many non-coding sequences [2]. The coding region of the plant mitochondrial genome undergoes precise repair mediated by gene recombination and base excision repair, while the non-coding region performs non-precise repair mediated by non-homologous end joining and break-induced replication. This means that the mutation rate of plant mitochondrial genes is low and the rate of evolution is very slow [3]. Due to the presence of numerous non-coding sequences, including repetitive fragments, within plant mitochondria, the plant mitochondrial genome is highly susceptible to recombination events, resulting in the formation of linear structures or multiple circular chromosomes [4]. As a result, the number of sequenced plant mitochondrial genomes is much lower, less than a tenth of the number of sequenced chloroplast genomes.

During the process of co-evolution, genes are lost from the organelles, and a significant number from both chloroplasts and mitochondria are transferred to the endosymbiotic nuclear genome [1, 3]. As a result, the number of genes present in both organelles is significantly reduced compared to their ancestral genomes in cyanobacteria and proteobacteria. Although plant mitochondria retain between 3 and 63 protein-coding genes, and chloroplasts encode 80 proteins in land plants and around 200 proteins in red algae lineages to maintain routine biogenesis and specific partitioned cellular functions [5, 6], the process of endogenous transfer of mitochondrial/chloroplast genes to the nucleus is ongoing, and even the entire mitochondrial/chloroplast genome sequences can be transferred to the nucleus [7]. Chen et al. [8] proposed that the mutation rate of DNA sequences in organelles is initially low but increases after their transfer to the nuclear genome. These sequences then evolve and gradually mutate or are lost over time [9]. In addition, phenomena such as the presence of chloroplast genes (predominantly tRNA genes), pseudogenes, and intergenomic transfers to mitochondrial genomes are observed [10], representing important features of plant mitochondrial genome evolution. A comprehensive understanding of gene transfer between chloroplasts and mitochondria is crucial for advancing our knowledge of the evolution of plant mitochondrial genomes.

Polygonaceae contains more than 1100 species in about 50 genera, many of which have significant economic value. Most of these species are well-known Tibetan medicines, with their roots, rhizomes or entire plants used for medicinal purposes [11, 12]. However, to date, only three mitochondrial genomes of Polygonaceae species have been sequenced and are available in the public database. This limited availability of data hinders our ability to study the phylogeographic structure and species differentiation history of the Polygonaceae family. Bistorta vivipara, which is widespread across the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau (QTP), has been used as a Tibetan medicine for thousands of years [13]. This plant has broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, with its tannins exhibiting inhibitory properties against fungi, bacteria, and viruses [14]. In addition to its tannins, B. vivipara contains a variety of secondary metabolites, such as flavonoids. These compounds have anti-free radical, antioxidant, lipid lowering and antihypertensive effects [15–17]. Furthermore, the plant contains geraniol and citronellol, which have been shown to have anti-tumour and anti-cancer properties [18]. In addition to its medicinal properties, B. vivipara is also a perennial high-quality forage grass. It is widely consumed throughout the year by various livestock due to its excellent palatability and high protein content. Similarly, Bistorta macrophylla is a rare and valuable forage plant at high altitudes due to its excellent feeding value and relatively short growing season (less than 120 days) [19]. In addition, Bistorta macrophylla is also used in traditional Tibetan medicine to treat a variety of ailments, including stomach disorders, indigestion and dysentery [20]. Despite their medicinal properties, research on Bistorta species has mainly focused on the extraction of medicinal components [21], while studies on their genetic diversity and adaptive evolution are still lacking. The plant organelle genome, due to its matrilineal inheritance, is a powerful tool for phylogenetic and traceability analysis. In addition, the primary function of mitochondria is to convert the proton concentration gradient into ATP through oxidative phosphorylation, thereby enabling biological activity [22]. Therefore, mitochondrial genome sequencing of representative native plants in high altitude areas such as the QTP can serve as a valuable reference for improving the adaptability of higher plants to the plateau. As B. vivipara and B. macrophylla are both native to high altitude regions, they have developed unique mechanisms for long-term adaptation to the high altitude environment, which are reflected in the basic characteristics of their mitochondria.

In this study, we have sequenced and characterized the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla. These genomes were successfully assembled into a single ring structure, allowing for a comprehensive analysis and comparison with previously published representative plants of the Polygonaceae family. The highly selected regions of organelle genes were identified by analyzing Ka/Ks and Pi. We also performed a structural variation analysis of the mitochondrial genomes of Polygonaceae to explore mitochondrial variation within this plant family. In addition, a comparative analysis between the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes was performed to determine the gene transfer status of five Polygonaceae species. Finally, the ecological niches of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla were modeled and analyzed. Given the limited availability of sequenced mitochondrial genomes in the Polygonaceae family (only three to date), this study provides a comprehensive examination of the biological evolutionary process of two important Tibetan medicinal plants, B. vivipara and B. macrophylla. By integrating chloroplast, mitochondrial genome, and distribution data, we aim to gain a thorough understanding of the biological evolution of these plant species.

Results

General features of the mitochondrial/chloroplast genomes of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla, and gene deletion in Polygonaceae

The mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of both B. vivipara and B. macrophylla have been successfully assembled into circular DNA molecules. The mitogenome of B. vivipara is 564,555 base pairs (bp) in length, while the chloroplast is 159,286 bp. The mitogenome size of B. macrophylla is 548,900 bp and the chloroplast genome is 159,102 bp (Fig S1). The mitogenomes of both species contained a similar number of genes. Of these, 32 PCGs are shared between the two species. B. vivipara has a unique rps3 gene, while B. macrophylla has a unique rps4 gene. However, the number of rRNA and tRNA genes varies, with B. vivipara having 4 and 31 rRNA and tRNA genes and B. macrophylla having 6 and 27 rRNA and tRNA genes. The chloroplast genomes of both species contain a total of 131 genes, the GC content of the chloroplast genomes is 37.95% for B. vivipara and 36.23% for B. macrophylla.

In the context of plant evolution, it is common to observe the loss of PCGs from the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes. Our results showed that 21 PCGs are shared by the five mitogenomes, among which Fallopia aubertii have the most complete set of PCGs, while Reynoutria japonica has lost the largest number of PCGs (Fig. 1). Specifically, the rpl16 is missing in Fagopyrum esculentum, B. vivipara and B. macrophylla, whereas the rps4 gene was only lost in the two Bistorta species. A total of 62 PCGs were found to be shared among 17 chloroplast genomes (Table S1). Overall, the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of the two Bistorta plants will provide valuable insights into understanding the adaptive evolution and species conservation of Polygonaceae species.

Pi values of mitochondrial/chloroplast genomes from Polygonaceae

Pi was calculated for the genes shared by the 17 chloroplast and 5 mitochondrial genomes, respectively (Fig. 2). On average, the Pi values of chloroplast genes were lower than those of mitochondrial genes. In contrast, certain mitochondrial genes, namely ccmB, cox3, nad3, nad4, rps13, showed Pi values close to zero (Fig. 2B). The following genes, rps2, ndhF, ndhK, ndhC, and rpoC2 in the chloroplast genome, and cox1, mttB, nad6, rps12, sdh4, and ccmC in the mitogenome, showed relatively high levels of Pi values, reflecting their high sequence diversity within the Polygonaceae species.

Non-synonymous/synonymous substitution rate analysis

The nonsynonymous/synonymous substitution rate (Ka/Ks) was used to assess the selective pressure on each gene (Fig. 3). Overall, the pairwise Ka/Ks values for most chloroplast genes were less than one (Fig. 3A). However, the pairwise Ka/Ks values for the psaJ gene and some pairwise Ka/Ks values for specific genes such as cemA, clpP, ndhB, psbJ, rpl32 and rpoC2 were greater than one. Among the mitochondrial genes, atp8, ccmFC, and nad4 had Ka/Ks values greater than one (Fig. 3B). The selected genes with high Pi and Ka/Ks values could be treated as potential markers for the systematic genetic study of the Polygonaceae species.

Repeat sequences of organelle genomes

Dispersed repeats, including forward, palindromic, and reverse sequences, were identified in the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of Polygonaceae (Table S2). The proportion of these repeats varied between species, with chloroplast genomes ranging from 17.68% (Ruprechtia albida) to 21.13% (Triplaris americana), and mitogenomes ranging from 7.20% (Fagopyrum esculentum) to 27.72% (B. vivipara). Notably, only two types of repeats (forward and palindromic) were present in the five mitochondrial genomes.

Among the three types of dispersed repeats, palindromic repeats were the most abundant in the chloroplast genomes of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla (Fig. 4C, F). In addition, SSRs were analyzed and showed that mono-nucleotide repeats, especially A and T nucleotides, were the most abundant in both chloroplast genomes. The SSRs were predominantly located in the LSC region, especially in intergenic regions, whereas in the SSC and IR regions, the majority of SSRs were found within exons. Among the trinucleotide SSRs in both chloroplast genomes, the sequence (AAT)3 was the most abundant. It is noteworthy that pentanucleotide SSRs were found exclusively in B. macrophylla.

A total of 116 and 117 SSRs were identified within the mitogenomes of B. macrophylla and B. vivipara, respectively, and were found to be evenly distributed (Fig. 5). In contrast to the chloroplast genome, only two types of dispersed repeats, forward and palindromic, were observed in both mitochondrial genomes. In addition, five pairs of dispersed repeats longer than 5000 bp were identified in both species (Fig. 5). In particular, a forward repeat of 38,550 bp was present in the mitochondrial genome of B. vivipara. Within this repeat, one of the copies contained the genes trnM-CAT, ccmB, nad5, trnM-TTT, and rps7, while the other copy contained the genes ccmB, nad2, trnK-TTT, and rps7 (Fig. 5A). All the repeat sequences detected above and their variation dynamics between species will contribute to the study of their genomic evolution, such as genome size expansion/contraction, intercellular gene transfer, etc.

Structural variations in mitochondrial genomes

Considering the substantial variation in plant mitochondrial genomes, structural variation (SV), including syntenic, inversion, translocation, and duplication events, was examined among the five mitochondrial genomes of Polygonaceae (Fig. 6). Translocations were identified between cox1 in Fagopyrum esculentum and cox1 and atp6 in B. macrophylla (Table S3). Duplications were observed between rps4, rpl5/rps14/cob, and trnD-GUC in Fagopyrum esculentum, and nad1, rpl5/rps14/cob, and trnA-TGC in B. macrophylla. Inversions and duplications were observed between trnS-GGA and trnM-CAU in Fagopyrum esculentum and sdh4/cox3/atp8 and nad2 in B. macrophylla. B. macrophylla and Reynoutria japonica showed two types of SV, namely translocation and syntenic. However, significant SVs were observed between R. japonica and Fallopia aubertii, including a large inverted fragment of 121,457 bp encompassing 23 and 29 genes in R. japonica and F. aubertii, respectively. Additionally, a relatively large inverted fragment was found between F. aubertii and B. vivipara, involving six genes in F. aubertii. These SVs were also confirmed by the mapping results of the Illumina read data, which showed a high level of mapping depth across the SV regions (Table S3). Therefore, the SVs detected in this study will be very useful in distinguishing distant or closely related species.

Collinearity analysis between chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes

Collinearity analysis revealed significant homology between the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of the five Polygonaceae species (Fig. 7, Table S4). Most of the homologous sequences within the mitochondrial genomes were found in intergenic regions or the genic regions of tRNA and rRNA genes. Notably, the rps12 and ccmC genes showed partial homology with the chloroplast genome of B. vivipara, while the nad4 gene showed partial homology to the chloroplast genome of B. macrophylla. In contrast, the rps7 gene showed homology between the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of all five species. Furthermore, several chloroplast PCGs displayed complete homology to corresponding sequences in the mitochondrial genomes. Examples for B. vivipara include psbC, petN, psbM, rps7, and atpE, while for B. macrophylla, examples include rpoC1, psbL, psbF, psbE, petL, petG, atpI, and several others. These results are important for understanding the mechanism of horizontal gene transfer between the plastid genomes and its role in evolution.

Phylogenetic relationships, IR contraction and expansion

The phylogenetic tree of 17 representative species of Polygonaceae was constructed based on their chloroplast genome sequences, the result showed a comprehensive information about the relationships among Polygonaceae species (Fig S2). The IR scope analysis of the 17 Polygonaceae chloroplast genomes revealed substantial variation in the length of the LSC, SSC, and IR regions (Fig S3). Among these species, the IR regions of Rheum officinale and Ruprechtia albida showed significant contraction, resulting in the expansion of the LSC or SSC regions. The contraction and expansion of the IR regions resulted in a relative change in gene position in the IR boundary region, particularly affecting rps19, rpl22, ndhF, rpl2, and trnH. However, R. officinale and Triplaris americana had unique gene compositions in their IRb-LSC and IRa-SSC regions, respectively. Specifically, the IRb-LSC region of R. officinale contained trnL/ycf2 and rps8/rps3, whereas the IRa-SSC region of R. albida contained trnN. It is important to note that although the length of the LSC, SSC and IR regions differed between the two Bistorta species, there was no difference in the relative position of the boundary genes.

Ecological niche analysis of the two Bistorta species

After correlation calculations and screening of climate factors, a total of 13 and 11 climate factors were selected to predict the niche models of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla, respectively (Fig. 8). Ranking of the importance of climate factors revealed that bio1/bio12 and altitude/annual mean UV-B were significant in shaping the distribution of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla (Fig. 8C, D). The current distribution model (1970–2000) shows that B. vivipara is more widespread than B. macrophylla, mainly in Europe and parts of Asia and the Americas, while B. macrophylla is restricted to the Qinghai-Xizang Plateau (Fig. 8A, B). However, B. vivipara will experience significant niche expansion in the mid-latitudes in the future, while its distribution in parts of North America will decline (Fig S4). Similarly, the suitable area of B. macrophylla will also expand more in the future, especially in the mid-latitude region, eventually leading to the suitable area of B. macrophylla exceeding that of B. vivipara (Fig S5). The niche modeling result would benefit to the better management, utilization and protection of those species.

Discussion

Given the critical role of organelle genomes in genome evolution and biotechnology, we have reported on the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of two Bistorta plants and compared them with all other organelle genomes of the Polygonaceae family. While the chloroplast genome is generally conserved, our comparative analysis of 17 Polygonaceae plants revealed a high degree of polymorphism, including the loss of certain genes and particularly high Pi levels. To date, only three mitochondrial genomes have been reported from the Polygonaceae family. Therefore, the mitochondrial genomes of the two Bistorta plants analyzed in this study can complement the limited availability of data for the economically valuable family Polygonaceae. They can also provide a valuable reference for the systematic genetics of the Polygonaceae family and other related species.

Genome size variation in the organelle genomes of the Polygonaceae species

Variations in chloroplast genome size can significantly affect plant growth, development, and adaptation to the environment, as reported in various species such as Datureae [23], Bromatorrhiza [24], Saxifraga [25], Paphiopedilum [26], among others. In our study, the chloroplast genomes of 17 selected Polygonaceae species ranged from 155,838 bp to 171,340 bp in size. We hypothesize that the observed size variability in these chloroplast genomes could be attributed to the expansion and contraction of the boundaries between the IR and single-copy (SC) regions. This is because boundary positions play a crucial role in determining the overall length and structure of the chloroplast genome [27]. As a result, the shift of trnL from IR regions to LSC in Rheum officinale and the inconsistent position of ndhF, rps15, rps19, rpl2, rpl22, and ycf1 (Fig S3) could be the factors contributing to the size variation of both chloroplast genomes and IR regions in Polygonaceae species. Additionally, the presence of repetitive sequences within the genome can have a significant impact on its overall size. For instance, duplications were found to be the major contributor to the size differences observed in the mitochondrial genomes of maize inbred lines [28], the nuclear genome of Pleurothallidinae [29], and the chloroplast genomes of Eriocaulon [30]. Our investigation revealed considerable diversity in the types, abundance, and total length of repeat sequences among the available chloroplast genomes of Polygonaceae species (Table S2). In general, species with larger genome sizes, such as Triplaris Americana, are associated with a greater total length and a higher proportion of repeat sequences. While Koenigia nepalensis has the smallest chloroplast genome length and a moderate repetition rate among the species examined, we found that the minimum number of repeats occurred in its chloroplast genome. These results support the notion that the composition of repetitive elements is a primary driver in generating genome size diversity within species.

The mitochondrial genome is known to be more complex than the chloroplast genome, with significant variations in size, sequence rearrangements, diverse repeat content, and highly variable coding sequences [31]. In our study, we sequenced and successfully assembled the circular mitochondrial genomes of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla. Despite the observed gene loss (e.g., rpl16 and rps4) in the two Bistorta species, these two mitochondrial genomes were found to be the largest among all the published mitochondrial genomes in Polygonaceae. This phenomenon highlights the C-value paradox in plant mitochondrial genomes, suggesting that there is no direct correlation between an increase in mitochondrial genome size and an increase in gene number [32]. In addition, the presence of repeat sequences has been found to have a positive influence on the mitochondrial genome size [28]. This could potentially explain the highest percentage of repeat sequences observed in the genome sizes of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla, which were found to be 27.72% and 20.07%, respectively, compared to the other three mitochondrial genomes (Table S2 and Fig. 5).

Adaptive evolution of PCGs in organelle genomes

The Ka/Ks value of a PCG is a crucial metric for assessing whether the gene is under selective pressure. It provides insight into reconstructing phylogenies and understanding the evolutionary dynamics of PCGs in closely related species [33]. While several chloroplast PCGs (cemA, clpP, ndhB, psbJ, rpl32, and rpoC2) in Polygonaceae species showed evidence of positive selection (Ka/Ks > 1) in certain pairwise comparisons, this phenomenon is not common in most angiosperms. In general, chloroplast PCGs in angiosperms tend to have Ka/Ks values less than one, indicating purifying selection, whereas Ka/Ks values greater than one are typically observed for pseudogenes [34–38]. Specially, the psbJ gene has a Ka/Ks value greater than one in all pairs of comparisons with Polygonaceae species examined. This gene is capable of binding to the chloroplast a protein and is crucial for the assembly of the photosystem II complex [39]. The presence of positive selection in these genes within the Polygonaceae species could be attributed either to the significant differences between species within this family, or to the unique role these genes play in adaptive evolution specific to the Polygonaceae species. It is worth noting that previous studies have consistently reported purifying selection on PCGs in mitochondrial genomes, which is a conservative phenomenon in most angiosperms [40]. However, our analysis has identified three genes, namely atp8, ccmFc, and nad4, that show positive selection in the five analyzed Polygonaceae mitochondrial genomes analyzed. Interestingly, similar results were also observed in the case of Mesona [41]. However, mitochondrial genome analyses of specific plant families to investigate the degree of gene selection remain limited. Further research is therefore needed to provide additional evidence and support for this conclusion.

Putative gene transfer

Organelle genomes and their constituent genes play critical roles in the plant cell, including energy metabolism, photosynthesis, and respiration, all of which are essential for plant growth and development [42]. While organelle genes typically remain stable in sequence and structure to maintain their normal functions, instances of significant gene loss (due to functional redundancy) or gene transfer events from plastids to mitochondria or from mitochondria to the nucleus (IGT) have occurred frequently during evolution. These events are associated with the endosymbiotic origin of organelles from α-proteobacteria [43, 44]. The present study revealed the occurrence of gene loss in all four analyzed Polygonaceae species analyzed when compared with the mitochondrial genomes of F. aubertii. Previous studies have suggested that in order to maintain essential functions of the organism, the lost genes or preudogenes in organelle genomes may have been transferred to the nuclear genome or replaced by nuclear genes [45]. Since only the nuclear genome data of F. esculentum were available, we performed a blast search against the nuclear genome to identify the homologous sequences for the mitochondrial genes that were absent in F. esculentum (Table S5). The analysis revealed the presence of homologous sequences in the partial nuclear genes of F. esculentum, suggesting that these mitochondrial genes may have been transferred to the nuclear genome. Despite the partial homology between the mitochondrial-derived sequences and the nuclear genes, a similar pattern was also observed for chloroplast-derived sequences within the mitochondrial genomes. Previous research has shown that the partial homology between the plastid-encoded rps7 gene and the mitochondrial genome sequence and certain chloroplast genes, such as psaA and psbJ, have migrated into the mitochondrial genome and have undergone pseudogenization [46]. Our study showed that chloroplast-derived sequences in the mitochondrial genomes of five Polygonaceae species were mainly located in intergenic regions and within partial gene regions. However, we also observed IGT of the rps7 gene in all five Polygonaceae species, with no evidence of gene loss or pseudogenization for this particular gene, which could be attributed to the course of endosymbiont integration [47]. Furthermore, we observed the transfer of the chloroplast rpl16 gene to the mitochondrial genomes of F. aubertii and R. japonica (Table S4), whereas this gene was absent in the chloroplast genomes of the other three species. Based on this finding, we hypothesized that the presence of the rpl16 in the mitochondrial genomes of F. aubertii and R. japonica may be attributed to the integration of corresponding chloroplast sequences. Therefore, it is possible that the other lost genes in the mitochondrial genomes of the five Polygonaceae species did not undergo a similar integration process as the rpl16 gene.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have sequenced and annotated the organelle genomes of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla and performed a comparative genome analysis by comparing the organelle genomes of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla with those of other available Polygonaceae species. The results showed that the presence of repetitive sequences within the genomes strongly influences their overall size. Furthermore, our results indicated significant differences between the mitochondrial genomes of the five Polygonaceae species, with a particularly notable large-scale inversion observed between R. japonica and F. aubertii. IGT analysis revealed that chloroplast-derived homologous sequences in the mitochondrial genomes of the five Polygonaceae species were predominantly found in intergenic or partial genic regions. In addition, the ecological model predicted the different niche distribution patterns of the two species, which may be related to the dynamics of genomic variation between the mitochondrial, chloroplast or even nuclear genome. The sequences of the organelle genome and certain key PCGs under positive selection, such as cemA, clpP, ndhB, psbJ, rpl32, and rpoC2 in the chloroplast genome, and atp8, ccmFc, and nad4 in the mitochondrial genome, have the potential to serve as promising markers for diversity analysis and phylogeographic studies in various medicinally valuable plants within the Polygonaceae family.

Materials and methods

Sampling, DNA isolation, library construction and sequencing

Two species of Bistorta, Bistorta vivipara (L.) Gray and Bistorta macrophylla (D. Don) Sojak, were collected from the wild in Hongyuan County, Aba Prefecture, China. The voucher specimens of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla were identified by Dr Xiong Lei of the Sichuan Academy of Grassland Sciences and Professor Xiao Ma of Sichuan Agricultural University. Specimens of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla were deposited in the herbarium of the College of grassland science and technology, Sichuan Agricultural University, with deposition numbers of BVHY2308 and BMHY2307, respectively. Fresh leaves were promptly harvested and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen for quick preservation. Total genomic DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB method and subsequently purified using the Agencourt AMPure XP Kit (Beckman, Brea, CA, USA.). An NGS DNA library was prepared using the NEBNext® library construction kit with an insert size of 350 bp. The library was sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq6000 sequencing platform at Genepianeer in Nanjing, China. To initiate the long-read SMRT sequencing process, the genomic DNA is first randomly fragmented. Larger DNA fragments are then isolated and purified using magnetic bead enrichment. Gel electrophoresis is then used for size selection. Any DNA damage present in the fragmented DNA is repaired and the DNA fragments are purified once again. The fragments are then subjected to end repair and A-tailing, and ligation reactions are performed using adapters sourced from the SQK-LSK109 Ligation kit. Once the SMRTbell library has been prepared, it is quantitatively assessed. A defined concentration and volume of the DNA library is then loaded onto a flow cell. This flow cell is then subsequently transferred to the Oxford Nanopore PromethION sequencer for real-time single-molecule sequencing.

Organelle genome assembly

To ensure the quality of NGS paired-end (PE) sequencing data, the fastp software was used for quality control [48]. Adapters and primer sequences were removed from the reads, and reads with a quality score below Q5 were discarded. Additionally, reads containing more than five N bases were excluded. After filtering, approximately 17.38 Gb PE Illumina clean data was obtained with a Q20 value of 97.25 and a Q30 value of 92.54. The filtlong pipeline within the Nanoq software is used to filter third generation sequencing data [49]. The data is filtered using the parameters: --min_length 1000 and --min_mean_q 7. As a result, 18.93 Gb of high quality and high accuracy reads were retained, with a mean read length of 13,330 bp and an N50 read length of 25,023 bp.

The Minimap2 aligner was used to align the raw third-generation sequencing data to a reference gene sequence of the plant mitochondrial core gene set (https://github.com/xul962464/plant_mt_ref_gene) [50]. Sequences with alignment lengths greater than 50 bp are considered as candidate sequences. From these candidates, sequences with a higher number of aligned genes and better alignment quality are selected as the final seed sequences. In the first step of genome assembly, the original SMRT data are aligned to the final seed sequences using minimap2. Sequences with more than 1 kb of overlap are identified and integrated into the seed sequences. The above iterative process continues until the complete third generation sequencing data for the plant mitochondrial genome is obtained. Subsequently, canu software [51] is used to correct the resulting data set. The corrected sequence is aligned to the Illumina clean data to using Bowtie2 [52], while Unicycler was used to assemble both the aligned Illumina data and the corrected SMRT reads [53]. Finally, the assembly results are visualized and manually adjusted using Bandage [54].

The GetOrganelle toolkit was used to assemble the chloroplast genome from the raw Illumina data [55]. Specifically, the recommended option of ‘21,45,65,85,105’ was set for the best kmer(s) parameter (-k) to optimize SPAdes performance. Furthermore, the ‘-F’ option was configured with the ‘embplant_pt’ database to provide a seed sequence derived from the chloroplast genome. To achieve a more comprehensive and accurate assembly, the Gapfiller software was used to fill any gaps in the reference sequence obtained by GetOrganelle [56].

Organelle genome annotation

The following steps were taken to annotate the mitochondrial genome sequence: (1) Coding protein (CDS) and rRNA genes were aligned with published and reference plant mitochondrial sequences using BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). (2) tRNAs were annotated using tRNAscan-SE [57]. (3) Open reading frames (ORFs) were annotated using the Open Reading Frame Finder tool (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gorf/gorf.html). For chloroplast genome annotation, prodigal (https://www.github.com/hyattpd/Prodigal) was used to annotate the chloroplast CDS. Hmmer [58] was used for rRNA prediction and aragorn (http://www.ansikte.se/ARAGORN/) for tRNA prediction.

Calculation of nucleotide diversity and nonsynonymous and synonymous substitution rates

To calculate the nucleotide diversity (Pi) of each species between the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes, the common genes of each species among were first extracted and joined together to generate a complete sequence. The multi-sequence alignment was then performed using the mafft software [59], with the output being converted to the vcf file format using SNP-sites [60]. The Pi was then calculated using vcftools [61], with a window size of 100 bp and step size of 10 bp. The calculation of the nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates for each shared mitochondrial or chloroplast gene was performed using KaKs_Calculator [62]. Prior to calculation, the CDS sequences of the shared genes were aligned using the clustalW algorithm, with gaps removed from the sequences.

Repetitive sequence identification

The REPuter software [63] was used with default criteria to identify dispersed and palindromic repeats. Tandem Repeats Finder [64] was used to detect tandem repeats, with the following parameter settings: 2 for match, 7 for mismatch, and 7 for indel. Simple sequence repeat (SSR) loci were detected using MISA software [65] with the following thresholds: 10, 6, 5, 5, 5, and 5 repeat units for mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexa-nucleotide SSRs, respectively.

Expansion and contraction of the IR regions among the chloroplast genomes

The typical structure of most plant chloroplast genomes consists of four regions - the large single copy (LSC), the small single copy (SSC), and two inverted regions (IR). Evolutionary events, including contraction and expansion of the IR and SSC regions, are frequently observed. In this study, IRscope [66] was used to analyze and visualize the junctions between these regions, as well as the dynamics of genetic variation near the junctions.

Ecological niche modelling

The study employed the MaxEnt approach [67], which is based on the maximum entropy machine learning algorithm, to model the distribution of B. vivipara and B. macrophylla. The occurrence records used for the analysis were obtained from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) database (http://www.gbif.org), the National Specimen Information Infrastructure (http://www.nsii.org.cn/), and the Chinese Virtual Herbarium (http://www.cvh.org.cn/). To reduce the impact of clustering effects, non-native distribution records were removed, and duplicate points were eliminated. Furthermore, only a single distribution point per 10 km × 10 km grid was retained, resulting in the acquisition of 15,504 and 433 valid samples for B. vivipara and B. macrophylla, respectively. Environmental factors, consisting of 19 bioclimatic variables, altitude, temperature, precipitation, ultraviolet radiation, were obtained from WorldClim (http://www.worldclim.org) for three historical scenarios (Mid-Holocene - about 6000 years ago; Last Glacial Maximum - approximately 22000 years ago; Last inter-glacial − 120000 to 140000 years ago), one present scenario (1971–2000), and four future scenarios (2021–2040; 2041–2060; 2061–2080; 2081–2100). Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated between two pairs of environmental factors. If the coefficients were greater than 0.8 or less than − 0.8, the environmental variables showing ecologically significant relationships were retained. To perform ecological niche modelling, the sample data were randomly divided into training data, which comprised 75% of the sample, and test data, which comprised the remaining 25%. The jackknife testing option was activated to assess the contribution of each variable to the habitat model. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUC) curve value [68]. Map data were obtained from WorldClim, which is free data for ecological modelling and GIS. The map was visualized using ArcGIS 10.5, the URL link to which is https://support.esri.com/zh-cn/products/desktop/arcgis-desktop/arcmap/10-5.

Intercellular gene transfer between mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes

To detect intercellular gene transfer (IGT) events between the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes, blastN was run with a stringent E-value cutoff of 1e-5 and sequence similarity threshold of 70% [69, 70]. To avoid duplicate identifications, each chloroplast genome was represented by a single IR sequence. Overlapping fragments between the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes were merged to generate unique sequences. Circos software was used to generate a visual representation highlighting the homologous segments between the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes [71].

Identification of structural variation among mitochondrial genomes

To investigate structural variation (SV) and collinearity between all five available mitogenomes in Polygonaceae (accessible datasets in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database and the China National GeneBank DataBase), pairwise alignment was performed using minimap2 [50] with the options ‘-ax asm5’ and ‘--eqx’. The SyRI [72] pipeline was then used to identify variants between the mitogenomes. These variants were then annotated using snpEff used to map them back to their respective genomes [73]. Plotsr [74] was then employed to generate visualizations highlighting the types of variants between the genomes: ‘syntenic’, ‘inversion’, ‘translocation’ and ‘duplication’. Finally, to determine whether these SVs were assembly errors or true structural variations, Illumina short reads WGS data were downloaded from the NCBI database and applied to determine the mapping depth across the SV regions in each species.
