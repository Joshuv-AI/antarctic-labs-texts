OsnTNB.11 Encodes a CC-NBS-LRR Type Resistance Protein that Positively Regulates Resistance to Rice Kernel Smut by Modulating the Ethylene Signalling
Authors: Xinyue Shu, Desuo Yin, Aiping Zheng, Aijun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rice kernel smut (RKS), caused by the basidiomycete fungus Tilletia horrida, is a major disease impacting the production of male sterile lines of rice (Oryza sativa) at the globally. Despite its significance, the understanding of mechanisms underlying resistance to RKS remain limited. This study employed map-based cloning to isolate the CC-NBS-LRR (CNL) RKS resistance gene OsnTNB.11 from the resistant rice line Jiangcheng3B. The knockout of OsnTNB.11 in Jiangcheng3B resulted in plants significantly more susceptible to T. horrida than the wild type. Stable OsnTNB.11 overexpression transgenic rice plants developed resistance against T. horrida. We further found that the coiled-coil (CC) domain of OsnTNB.11 (OsnTNB.11-CC) interacted with and stabilised rice methionine synthetases OsMETS1, a key enzyme involved in ethylene (ET) biosynthesis. OsnTNB.11 activated the transcriptional levels of ET-related defence genes and enhance ET contents, whereas the exogenous application of ACC (an ethylene biosynthesis precursor) improved rice resistance to T. horrida. Further, the introduction of OsnTNB.11 into a disease-susceptible rice variety promoted resistance to RKS without impacting grain yield. In summary, we determined a regulatory model of a novel RKS resistance mediated via the ET signalling pathway. These results enhance our understanding of RKS resistance mechanisms and provide a valuable genetic resource for resistant T. horrida rice male sterile lines for breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-025-00874-5.

Background

Plants employ a two-tier innate immune system, comprising pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI), to defend against microbial pathogens (Jones and Dangl 2006). PTI is initiated by cell-surface pattern recognition receptors upon the detection of microbe-associated molecular patterns (Dangl et al. 2013), whereas ETI is initiated by intracellular nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NB-LRR)-containing receptors upon the recognition of pathogen effectors. PTI-induced plant defence is typically mild and transient, whereas ETI-mediated responses are robust and persistent (Dodds and Rathjen 2010). The downstream defense outputs of PTI and ETI overlap extensively (Peng et al. 2018; Yuan et al. 2021; Chang et al. 2022) and PTI can boost ETI defence signalling (Fabro et al. 2011; Irieda et al. 2019).

NB-LRR proteins (NLRs) contain an N-terminal coiled-coil (CC) or a Toll/interleukin-1 receptor, a resistance (TIR) protein domain, an NB adaptor shared by Apaf-1 and resistance proteins, an NB-ARC domain and an LRR domain (Jones and Dangl 2006; Duxbury et al. 2021). The N-terminal CC and TIR domains may serve as platforms for interactions with downstream signalling components or for the activation of immune responses (Collier and Moffett 2009; Bonardi et al. 2012; Slootweg et al. 2013; Steinbrenner et al. 2015). For example, the rice NLR protein Pb1 directly interacts with OsWRKY45—a positive regulator of rice immunity—through its CC domain to trigger blast resistance (Inoue et al. 2013). This interaction may avoid OsWRKY45 degradation by the ubiquitin–proteasome system, thereby enabling defence responses mediated by OsWRKY45 (Qiu and Yu 2009; Inoue et al. 2013). The rice CC-NBS-LRR proteins Pi5-1 and Pi5-2 are reported to be involved in Pyricularia oryzae resistance (Lee et al. 2009). A total of 600 NBS-LRR genes, representing 1.5% of all 40,000 genes in rice, have been found (Azhar et al. 2011), and over 23 NLR genes have been characterised (Liu et al. 2014). However, the mechanism underlying the regulation of Tilletia horrida resistance by NLRs remains unclear, and breeding rice for resistance to T. horrida is limited.

Scholars have reported that ethylene (ET) may play a more prominent role than salicylic acid and jasmonic acid in mediating rice disease resistance (Helliwell et al. 2013). Although the interaction mechanism of rice and T. horrida remains unclear (Wang et al. 2022), extensive research has been performed on the role of ET-dependent defence signalling in the resistance to other rice fungal pathogens (Helliwell et al. 2013; Singh et al. 2004). The overexpression of the ET biosynthesis-related gene 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid synthase ACS2 increases rice resistance to Rhizoctonia solani and P. oryzae (Helliwell et al. 2013). In a previous study, the application of aminoethoxyvinylglycine hydrochloride (AVG, an ET biosynthesis inhibitor) significantly increased the infection of P. oryzae in rice, while the application of ethephon (2-chloroethylphosphonic acid, an ET generator) significantly decreased the infection of P. oryzae in susceptible rice lines (Singh et al. 2004).

Rice kernel smut (RKS), caused by the basidiomycete fungus T. horrida, can result in yield losses ranging from 5 to 20%, and in severe cases, up to 50% (Chen et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2015). It is a major disease that affecting the production of rice male sterile lines in most hybrid rice growing regions, such as China and Pakistan (Khanal et al. 2023; Carris et al. 2006; Brooks et al. 2009). In recent years, progress has been made in understanding the pathogenesis of T. horrida (Wang et al. 2018a, 2022; Shu et al. 2022). However, the mechanisms underlying rice resistance to T. horrida remain largely unknown. In addition, dominant RKS resistance genes and loci have not been determined due to the limited effective resistant rice cultivars with complete resistance or immunity (Bakkeren et al. 2008). Consequently, the identification and mining of novel RKS resistance genes are critical for developing strategies to prevent diseases caused by T. horrida.

In the present study, pools of resistant and susceptible bulk samples (n = 40, each group) were constructed from 508 F2 individuals derived from the cross between Jiangcheng3B (maintainer line of male sterile line Jiangcheng3A) and 9311B (maintainer line of male sterile line 9311A) plants. The samples were then used to detect regions in the rice genome that harbour major QTLs for resistance to T. horrida using bulked segregant analysis (BSA), whole genome re-sequencing (WGS) and single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) index methods. The candidate gene OsnTNB.11 encoding a CC-NBS-LRR (CNL) resistance protein, was cloned from rice line Jiangcheng3B. We also analysed the resistance of OsnTNB.11 to RKS using lines that overexpressing OsnTNB.11 and lines carrying knockout mutations of OsnTNB.11. Further analyses revealed that OsnTNB.11 directly interacts with and stabilises rice methionine synthetases OsMETS1, a key enzyme involved in ET biosynthesis, via the CC domain (OsnTNB.11-CC). Finally, we demonstrate that OsnTNB.11 can activate the expression of ET-related defence genes and enhance ET contents, and the exogenous application of ACC (a precursor of ethylene biosynthesis) improves rice resistance to T. horrida. Overall, our findings indicate that the CC-NBS-LRR type resistance protein OsnTNB.11 positively regulates resistance to RKS through the modulation of ethylene signalling.

Results

Locating OsnTNB.11 by BSA-Sequencing and Transcriptome Analysis

We developed rice populations for efficient genetic mapping by crossing the RKS-resistant accession Jiangcheng3B with the susceptible variety 9311B. F2 individuals from this population were phenotyped for RKS resistance at the heading stage. The results showed that the number of diseased grains in 508 F2 plants fluctuated within the range of 1.2–17.6, and the phenotype of resistance to T. horrida in this population followed a normal distribution (Fig. 1A). We further identified 52 extreme resistant progenies and 61 extreme susceptible progenies from 508 F2 plants (Fig. 1A). Panicle tissues of these extreme resistant and susceptible F2 plant seedlings were separately pooled for BSA-Seq analysis. Genomic DNA samples of two parental varieties and the two extreme resistant and susceptible pools were sequenced using the IlluminaHiSeqTM PE150 sequencer, generating 42.82 Gb of raw data. After filtering, 42.69 GB of clean data remained (Table S1). Jiangcheng3B generated 41,013,606 valid reads, with an average read depth of 15.06-fold. In addition, 9311B generated 41,577,770 valid reads, and the average read depth was 15.45-fold. The average read depths of resistant and susceptible pools were 31.6 and 35.26, respectively (Table S2). The SNPs and insertions and deletions (InDels) in the two parental lines were identified by alignment to the ‘Nipponbare’ reference genome, and 439,111 polymorphic markers were further identified from the homozygous SNPs of Jiangcheng3B and 9311B. Reads of the resistant and susceptible pools were separately aligned to the consensus sequence reads of Jiangcheng3B and 9311B to identify SNPs. The SNP-index of the two pools was calculated using the Jiangcheng3B genotype as a reference. Two peaks in the genomic region of chromosome 6 (Chr6) between 24,918,165–27,398,048 bp and of chromosome 11 (Chr11) between 24,840,540–25,773,482 bp were found by comparing the SNP-index of resistant and susceptible pools with 1,000 displacement tests (95% confidence level) (Fig. 1B, C; Fig. S1). A total of 27 large-effect SNPs and 2 large-effect InDels between 9311B and Jiangcheng3B were identified in qOsT6 and qOsT11 (Tables S3 and S4). These SNPs and InDels were located in the upstream, downstream, and coding regions of 11 candidate genes (Table S5). Based on previously reported transcriptome data (Wang et al. 2020), we determined that 8 of the 11 candidate genes in the two regions were expressed, with fragments per kilobase of transcript sequence per million mapped reads (FPKM) values greater than 1. The expression of the candidate genes in the two rice lines at varying infection time points (0, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h) is shown in Fig. 1D. Among them, Os11g0639600 (denoted as OsnTNB.11) was significantly up-regulated (p < 0.05 and |log2fold change (FC)|> 1) in Jiangcheng3A after infection, whereas no significant differences were observed in 9311A (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, BSA-Seq analysis revealed a non-synonymous SNP with the highest index in OsnTNB.11 between resistant and susceptible rice lines (Table S3). Thus, OsnTNB.11 was prioritised among qOsT11 candidate genes.

Cloning, Expression Patterns and Subcellular Localisation of OsnTNB.11

We further cloned sequences of the OsnTNB.11 gene from Jiangcheng3B using primers designed based on the genome of the rice cultivar Nipponbare, generating a 2,820 bp fragment. OsnTNB.11 encodes a CC-NBS-LRR type resistance protein. Conserved domains analysis using Batch CD-Search showed that OsnTNB.11 contains the CC, NB-ARC and LRR conserved domains. Sequence variation analysis indicated nine SNPs on OsnTNB.11 between resistant and susceptible rice lines (Fig. S2). Among them, two non-synonymous SNPs were located in the NBS domain of OsnTNB.11 (Fig. 2A). However, no other SNPs were found in the conserved domain (Fig. S2). We identified homolog genes of OsnTNB.11 in numerous plants, including cultivated crops such as Triticum dicoccoides, Setaria italica, T. aestivum and Digitaria exilis (Fig. S3). We also found homolog proteins of OsnTNB.11 in weedy plants, including Setaria viridis, Aegilops tauschii, Brachypodium distachyon, Typha angustifolia, Eleusine coracana, Phragmites australis, Alopecurus aequalis and Eragrostis curvula (Fig. S3). Phylogenetic analysis showed that OsnTNB.11 and its homolog proteins were divided into three groups. OsnTNB.11, KAK3120601.1 (Eleusine coracana), and XP_062197065.1 (Phragmites australis), were categorized into the same group (Fig. S3). These results reveal that OsnTNB.11 is conserved among grasses. Furthermore, the expression of OsnTNB.11 was up-regulated at 8, 12 and 72 h after infection with T. horrida. Similarly, in the transcriptome kinetics of Wang et al. (2020), its expression peaked at 12 h (Fig. 1D). Further, we verified the expression of OsnTNB.11 in Jiangcheng3A at 8 h, 12 h and 72 h using qRT-PCR analysis. These results are consistent with the transcriptome data (Fig. 2B), indicating that the resistance signals mediated by OsnTNB.11 are primarily activated during the early stage of infection (8 and 12 h). Knockdown of the OsnTNB.11 Gene Accelerated the Infection of T. horrida

To clarify the impact of OsnTNB.11 on rice resistance to T. horrida, knockout (KO) mutant transgenic lines of OsnTNB.11 in the resistant line Jiangcheng3B background were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing system. We designed sgRNA target sites within the exons of OsnTNB.11 (Fig. 3A), obtaining 10 transgenic plants. The sequencing of targeted regions revealed several independent homozygous mutants with a total of two mutation types (Fig. 3B). We employed T2 transgenic lines of these mutants to perform resistance analysis. The disease severity was scored for five inoculated panicles of two mutant lines, each representing a different mutation in OsnTNB.11. After inoculation with T. horrida, the number of diseased grains on OsnTNB.11-KO plants was statistically higher than that on wild type (WT) (Fig. 3C, D). Overexpression of OsnTNB.11 Enhanced Rice Resistance to T. horrida

To further determine the function of OsnTNB.11, we transformed the susceptible rice variety ‘9311B’ with the dominant OsnTNB.11 from Jiangcheng3B. Three transgenic plants were obtained, and each plant was verified using qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 4A). The T2 overexpressing (OE) transgenic lines were employed for resistance analysis. Compared with WT, the OsnTNB.11-OE plants exhibited significantly enhanced resistance to T. horrida (Fig. 4B, C). OsnTNB.11 Directly Interacts with the OsMETS1 Through its CC Domain

The CC domain of several NLR proteins has been shown to play an essential role in the activation of downstream immune signalling (Slootweg et al. 2013; Steinbrenner et al. 2015). To test whether the CC domain of OsnTNB.11 (OsnTNB.11-CC) can activate immune defence against T. horrida, yeast two-hybrid system (Y2H) assays were performed with the OsnTNB.11-CC domain of Jiangcheng3B as bait against a rice spikelet cDNA library. A total of 109 proteins were identified to potentially interact with OsnTNB.11-CC (Table S6). Among these proteins, methionine synthase OsMETS1 exhibited the highest frequency. Previous research has demonstrated that OsMETS1 is an important immunity factor that can positively regulate resistance to multiple diseases in rice (Zhai et al. 2022). Thus, we further confirmed the interaction between OsnTNB.11-CC and OsMETS1 via co-expressing their cDNA sequences in yeast (Fig. 5A). Bimolecular fluorescent complementation (BiFC) assays were also used to verify the interaction between OsnTNB.11-CC and OsMETS1. A GFP fluorescence signal was detected in both the nucleus and cytoplasm at 48 h after the co-expression of cGFP-OsnTNB.11-CC and nGFP-OsMETS1. GFP fluorescence was not observed in the leaves co-infiltrated with cGFP and nGFP (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, we used a luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay to confirm the interaction between OsnTNB.11-CC and OsMETS1 by the co-transformation of OsnTNB.11-CC-nLUC and OsMETS1-cLUC in N. benthamiana leaves (Fig. 5C). OsnTNB.11 Activates ET Signalling

We investigated the influence of OsnTNB.11-CC on OsMETS1 stability by co-expressing OsMETS1-GFP and OsnTNB.11-CC-Flag in N. benthamiana leaves. Compared with the co-expression of OsMETS1 and GFP, the fluorescence signal in the co-expression of OsMETS1-GFP and OsnTNB.11-Flag considerably increased, indicating that OsnTNB.11-CC could stabilise OsMETS1 in N. benthamiana leaves (Fig. 6A). We also detected the expression of OsMETS1 in kernels of OsnTNB.11 transgenic plants at 12 h after inoculation with T. horrida. The expression of OsMETS1 was observed to be up-regulated in OsnTNB.11-OE plants and down-regulated in OsnTNB.11-KO lines (Fig. 6B). OsMETS1 is reported to be a key enzyme-encoding gene involved in ET biosynthesis (Zhai et al. 2022). Thus, we analysed the expression of two ET biosynthesis-related genes (OsACS2 and OsACO5) and two ET response-related PR genes (PR1a and PR1b) in kernels of OsnTNB.11 transgenic plants at 12 h after inoculation with T. horrida. The expression of these four genes in OsnTNB.11-OE plants was significantly higher than that in the WT plants after T. horrida infection (Fig. 6C). In contrast, OsnTNB.11-KO lines exhibited a markedly reduced induction of all four genes upon T. horrida inoculation (Fig. 6C). Exogenous ACC Treatment Enhanced Rice Resistance to T. horrida

We further examined the impact of OsnTNB.11 on ET contents. ET concentrations were measured in OsnTNB.11-OE, OsnTNB.11-KO and corresponding WT plants. As shown in Fig. 7A, B, OsnTNB.11-OE plants exhibited significantly higher levels of ET compared to WT. In contrast, the ET content in OsnTNB.11-KO lines was significantly lower than in WT plants. In addition, to determine the function of ET in T. horrida resistance, we inoculated T. horrida on 9311B rice kernels pretreated with 100 μM ACC. Pretreated plants with sterile water were used as the control. These results showed that the ACC-treated kernels exhibited fewer diseased grains than the control (Fig. 7C). This proves that ET production could promote rice resistance to T. horrida. Based on the experimental results, we developed a model of RKS resistance regulation by OsnTNB.11. Discussion

QTL mapping is an effective approach for identifying disease resistance genes and provides a foundation for further analysis of the molecular mechanisms of disease resistance in plants (Xu et al. 2015). However, traditional QTL analysis is relatively inefficient and requires substantial time and capital (Salvi and Tuberosa 2005). BSA is a simple and rapid method employed to identify molecular markers linked to target genes or QTLs using two populations of individuals with opposite extreme phenotypes (Michelmore et al. 1991). Scholars have employed BSA to analyse genes or QTLs related to important disease-resistant and yield crop traits (Liang et al. 2016; Takagi et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2016; Pandey et al. 2017; Song et al. 2017). In this study, the QTLs qOsT6 and qOsT11 were located using the BSA-Seq approach. The candidate gene OsnTNB.11, located in qOsT11, was validated using multiple lines of evidence, including natural variation, transcriptome profiling, qPCR and mutation and complementation in rice. Our results revealed the effectiveness of combining high-throughput genotyping, BSA-Seq and transcriptome profiling to rapidly identify functional genes. A total of eight SNPs between resistant and susceptible lines were located upstream of Os06g0627100 within qOsT6 (Table S3), and transcriptome data indicated that this gene was up-regulated at 8 and 12 h in Jiangcheng3B (Wang et al. 2020; Fig. 1D). This suggests that Os06g0627100 may also play an important role in resistance to T. horrida, although further studies are required to clarify this.

The NBS domain of NLRs contains several motifs that regulate the signalling activity of NB-LRR proteins through a direct involvement in binding and/or the hydrolysis of the nucleotide, or by auto-inhibiting or relaying conformational changes in the NBS domain to other parts of the protein (Sueldo et al. 2015). Numerous studies have confirmed that the N-terminal CC within the NB-LRR proteins triggers immune responses, while the LRR mediates the recognition of pathogen effectors (Takken and Goverse 2012). However, our knowledge of the mechanisms by which the NBS domain regulates the activity of NB-LRR proteins is limited (Sueldo et al. 2015). In this study, we determined two non-synonymous SNPs between resistant and susceptible rice lines, located in the NBS domain of OsnTNB.11. This phenotypic difference may be attributed to the potential interference of these NBS domain non-synonymous mutations with the nucleotide binding of the OsnTNB.11 proteins, reducing RKS resistance. However, the role of the OsnTNB.11 NBS domain in regulating RKS resistance requires further experimental validation.

Previous research has revealed that CC-NBS-LRR protein encoded genes typically regulate disease resistance by interacting with transcription factors (TFs) in plants (Jin et al. 2022). The CC-NBS-LRR protein Pm2b can regulate powdery mildew resistance in wheat by interacting with TF TaWRKY76-D, which negatively regulates resistance to wheat powdery mildew (Jin et al. 2022). Wheat CC-NBS-LRR protein MLA10 directly interacts with and suppresses HvWRKY1/HvWRKY2, a negative regulator of wheat immunity, through its CC domain to trigger powdery mildew resistance (Maekawa et al. 2011). In the present study, we found that OsnTNB.11 interacts with methionine synthase OsMETS1 by its CC domain, directly activating the transcription levels of ET-related defence genes and enhancing ET contents. These results enhance our understanding of the role of CC-NBS-LRR protein in plant resistance and highlight the relevance of studies on the OsnTNB.11 regulation of rice resistance to T. horrida.

The development of molecular biology and genomics has advanced molecular breeding in plants. Molecular breeding can enhance breeding efficiency and reduce breeding cycles by directly targeting genes related to target traits (Mumm and Moose 2008). For example, genes resistant to blast Pi-d, Pi-z and Pi-kh have been successfully applied to breed for rice blast resistance (Hittalmani et al. 2000). In addition, Pi40 is reported to be resistant to various strains of P. oryzae (Jeung et al. 2007). In this study, the RKS resistance gene OsnTNB.11 was cloned using BSA and genetic methods. We also determined the RKS resistance function of OsnTNB.11 by transgenic analysis in two parents. This is the first cloned gene associated with resistance to RKS and is therefore a crucial target for developing T. horrida resistance through rice breeding. Thus, OsnTNB.11 or its transgenic plants represent a valuable genetic resource for breeding RKS resistance in rice male sterile lines.

ET can positively or negatively regulate plant disease resistance and has a complex role in plant–pathogen interactions (Broekaert et al. 2006; Geraats et al. 2003). For example, Geraats et al. (2007) observed that ET-insensitive mutants of N. tabacum have high disease resistance to Peronospora parasitica; however, their resistance to Colletotrichum destructivum and Fusarium oxysporum was lower. In fact, the precise role of ET in plant immune responses depends on the pathogen type and environmental conditions (Brown 2002; Kim et al. 2009). Although ET is usually produced by plants to restrict the invasion of biotrophic pathogens (Broekaert et al. 2006), the function of ET in rice resistance to T. horrida remains to be determined. Our results indicate that ET positively regulates rice resistance to T. horrida. This provides a novel strategy for the control of RKS in rice production and warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

This study successfully confirmed the role of the NLR protein OsnTNB.11 in RKS resistance for the first time. No significant differences were observed in the yield and agronomical traits of transgenic OsnTNB.11 plants compared to WT plants. We demonstrated that OsnTNB.11 conferred RKS resistance through the ET signalling pathway. Our results provide a crucial genetic basis for the breeding of rice male sterile lines with resistance to RKS and advance our understanding of the resistance pathways involved in the rice–T. horrida interaction.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Fungal Strains

Two individuals exhibiting marked differences in resistance against T. horrida, the resistant rice maintainer line Jiangcheng3B (male parent), and the susceptible rice maintainer sterile line 9311B (female parent), were provided by the Food Crop Research Institute, Hubei Academy of Agriculture Sciences, Wuhan, China. These materials were employed to create a segregating population. F1 plants were generated after a cross was performed in the spring of 2015 at the experimental farm of the Rice Research Institute in Sichuan Agricultural University, China. A total of 508 F2 seedlings selected through self-fertilisation in the fall of 2017 were used as mapping populations. The tobacco plants were grown under 23–25 °C with 60% relative humidity. The T. horrida strain JY-521 (Wang et al. 2018a) and A. tumefaciens GV3101 (Shu et al. 2022) were stored at the College of Agronomy, Guangxi University, Nanning, China.

Fungal Inoculation Assays

The T. horrida isolate JY-521 was inoculated into rice panicles at the booting stage using injection (Wang et al. 2018b). In summary, the T. horrida strain JY-521 was grown in potato dextrose agar (PDA) liquid medium in an incubator at 28 °C for 7 days on a shaker at 150 rpm. Approximately 1 mL of culture was injected into the immature kernels of field-grown rice plants with a sterile syringe. Inoculations were performed in the late afternoon to facilitate infection. At least five inoculated panicles were used as replicates, and disease severity was scored by estimating the number of diseased grains per panicle at the yellow ripeness stage.

DNA Isolation and Analysis of WGS Data

Immature and healthy kernels of the two parents and the F2 individuals were collected at the booting stage (3–5 d before heading) and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total genomic DNA was prepared using the CTAB method (Murray and Thompson 1980). DNA purity and concentration were identified using a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA) and a Qubit DNA Assay Kit with a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, CA, USA), respectively. Equal amounts of DNA from 40 F2 plants extremely resistant to T. horrida (0–2.7 diseased grains; resistant-pool) were mixed. Similarly, equal amounts of DNA from 40 F2 plants susceptible to T. horrida (15.4–17.8 diseased grains; susceptible-pool) were mixed. DNA from the Jiangcheng3B and 9311B plant kernels, as well as from the two pools, was sonicated to approximately 350 bp, end polished, polyA tail, ligated to sequencing adaptors and subsequently amplified by PCR before sequencing. The sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Library Construction Kit (Illumina USA) and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeqTM PE150 platform. Raw reads were filtered by removing reads with ≥ 10% unidentified nucleotides, reads with a low quality score or those aligned to the barcode adapter. The Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA, Li et al. 2009) was used to align the clean reads of each sample against the Nipponbare reference genome. The SNPs and InDels of all samples were identified using the Unified Genotyper function in GATK, and their annotation was performed by ANNOVAR (McKenna et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2010).

The read depth information of homozygous SNPs/InDels between two parents in the offspring pools was determined to calculate the SNP/InDel index. We used Jiangcheng3B as the reference and counted the number of reads corresponding to its genotype in both pools. Then, we calculated the ratio of the number of reads, which are different from reference parents, to the total number of reads, representing the SNP/InDel index at the genomic base sites. If all measured reads come from the reference parents, the SNP-index is equal to 0 (Takagi et al. 2015). The SNP/InDel positions with index values less than 0.3 were filtered out. The difference between the SNP/InDel index of two pools was calculated as the delta SNP/InDel index, effectively capturing variations at individual loci. Sliding window analyses was performed at 1 Mb intervals, 10 kb steps and a 95% confidence level as the threshold. Regions with consistently elevated delta SNP/InDel indices were considered as candidate regions associated with RKS resistance QTLs (Takagi et al. 2015).

Sequence and Phylogenetic Analysis of OsnTNB.11

The full-length protein sequence of OsnTNB.11 was used as a query to search putative OsnTNB.11 homologs using BLAST on the NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). A total of 20 proteins with significant matches with OsnTNB.11 (query cover > 90%, percent identity > 33% and e-value ≤ 1e–5) were selected as putative homologs of OsnTNB.11. A phylogenetic tree analysis of OsnTNB.11 and its homologous proteins was conducted using MEGA 7.0, and phylogenetic alignment was performed using ClustalX 2 and DNAMAN 8. Predictions of catalytic domain sequences were determined by NCBI Conserved Domain Search analysis (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi).

Plasmid Construction and Rice Genetic Transformation

To construct the 9311B OsnTNB.11 overexpressing plants, the cDNA of OsnTNB.11 was amplified from the rice cultivar Jiangcheng3B by PCR using the designed primers (Table S7). The cDNA product was then inserted into pBWA(V)KS, which harbours a cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter, and the constructed vector was introduced into A. tumefaciens GV3101. Rice transformation was performed following previously described methods (Li et al. 2016). For the cloning of OsnTNB.11-KO, a specific OsnTNB.11 fragment was amplified from Jiangcheng3B cDNA with designed primers. The plasmid was constructed as described in previous studies (Osato et al. 2006; Xu et al. 2012). The OsnTNB.11-KO cis- and trans-fragments with corrected sequences were inserted into the vector pBWA(V)HS. The Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation of Jiangcheng3B was used to obtain the OsnTNB.11-KO transgenic plants. Table S7 lists the primer sequences used in this study.

Quantitative Real-Time Reverse Transcription–Polymerase Chain Reaction

The qRT-PCR was performed on the Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Foster City, CA, United States) to detect the relative expression levels of target genes. The rice Actin gene was used as an internal reference gene to determine the values for relative expression levels. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt algorithm (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Four biological replicates were conducted. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Table S7 lists the primers used for the qRT-PCR analysis.

Subcellular Localization

The OsnTNB.11 sequence was fused to the PHB-GFP vector using special primer sequences (Table S7). These fusion constructs were then transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101, and agroinfiltrated into the N. benthamiana leaves, as described previously. Two days after infiltration, the fluorescence signal was observed using laser confocal fluorescence microscopy.

Yeast Two-Hybrid Assay

The Y2H assay was performed using the Y2H Gold system (Clontech). The OsnTNB.11-CC and OsMETS1 protein CDS were cloned into pGADT7 (AD) and pGBKT7 (BD), respectively. Yeast transformations were completed following the manufacturer's instructions (Clontech) and cultured on SD/-Trp-Leu or SD/-Trp-Leu-His-Ade medium containing X-ɑ-gal at 30 °C in the dark for approximately 3 days. Table S7 reports the primers used.

BiFC Assay

Fusion vectors of OsnTNB.11-CC and the OsMETS1 were constructed using the binary BiFC vectors pSPYNE (nYFP) and pSPYCE (cYFP), respectively (Table S7). BiFC assay analysis was performed in the leaf epidermal cells of N. benthamiana following previously described methods (Waadt and Kudla 2008). GFP fluorescence was visualised using a Nikon A1 laser scanning confocal microscope (Nikon, Minato City, Tokyo, Japan).

LCI Assay

Split-luciferase complementation (LCI) assay was performed as described by Wang et al. (2021). OsnTNB.11-CC and the OsMETS1 were amplified and fused with luciferase (Table S7). The final constructs were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 and used to infiltrate the N. benthamiana leaves. The leaves were stained using the Beetle Luciferin Potassium Salt Kit (E1601; Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, United States) and visualised using the NightOWL II LB 983 in vivo imaging system (Berthold, Bad Wildbad, Germany). The interaction was assessed based on the bioluminescence signal intensity acquired using IndiGO.

ET Observation

The ET concentration of rice kernels was measured using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent (ELISA) assay. The experiments were repeated at least three times.
