Profiles of Anxiety in Children Undergoing Elective Surgery: Predictors and Perioperative Outcomes
Authors: Kristie L Poole, Cheryl H T Chow, Norman Buckley, Desigen Reddy, Ryan J Van Lieshout, Louis A Schmidt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSurgery is an anxiety‐provoking event, but there are individual differences in the magnitude of experienced anxiety. The objective of this study was to identify patterns of children's preoperative anxiety, its predictors, and perioperative outcomes. The sample included 171 children (M age = 9.95 years) undergoing elective surgery. We derived groups of children's anxiety based on multiple indicators (i.e., self‐report, heart rate, and frontal brain EEG activity) during a preoperative visit and on the day of surgery. Parents and children reported on children's temperament, children's behavior was coded in the operating room, and postoperative pain ratings were collected. Two latent profiles of children were identified, including a subset with relatively average anxious responses across indicators (74%) and a subset with relatively heightened anxious responses across indicators (26%). Children in the heightened anxiety subgroup had higher levels of temperamental shyness, displayed greater observed distress behaviors (e.g., resistance to anesthesia, screaming) upon operating room entry, and tended to have higher levels of postoperative pain compared to children in the average anxiety subgroup. These results reveal child‐level predictors and perioperative outcomes associated with the experience of elevated anxiety in the surgical context.Keywords: anxiety, child, pain, preoperative, surgery, temperament

Introduction

Impending surgery is arguably one of the most stressful real‐life experiences one may encounter. As such, the perioperative context provides a unique model and opportunity to study children's emotions (Shin et al. 2026). Over 5 million children undergo surgery annually in North America (Perry et al. 2012), and previous work suggests that many of these children experience significant preoperative anxiety (Perry et al. 2012). Although the experience of pediatric preoperative anxiety is ubiquitous, there are individual differences in the magnitude of anxiety that is experienced by children. For example, some children may experience typical or relatively average anxiety responses, while other children may experience more extreme anxiety that can impair their compliance with medical procedures (Chorney and Kain 2009). Accordingly, it is important to better understand these individual differences because not only can preoperative anxiety have a significant psychological impact that varies by child, but it can also affect clinical outcomes, including delayed anesthetic induction, increased anesthetic requirements, emergence delirium, postoperative pain, and prolonged recovery (Chieng et al. 2014; Chow et al. 2020, 2023; Fortier and Kain 2015; Kain et al. 2004, Kain et al. 2006).

Traditionally, children's preoperative anxiety is gauged using self‐report measures of state anxiety that provide a continuous measure of subjective anxiety (Chieng et al. 2014; Chow, Van Lieshout, Buckley et al. 2016). However, anxiety is a multidimensional construct encompassing not only subjective feelings but also measurable physiological arousal that can be captured across cardiovascular and neural systems (Ko et al. 2024; Steimer 2002). One widely used indicator of physiological arousal is one's heart rate, with an elevated heart rate thought to reflect underlying anxious arousal to a perceived stressor (Gordan et al. 2015; Ko et al. 2024; Wascher 2021). Additional noninvasive indicators such as continuous quantitative electroencephalogram (EEG) have been used as an indirect measure to track one's emotional experience (see Reznick and Allen [2018] for a review). It has been suggested that patterns of frontal brain alpha absolute activity may reflect individual differences in emotion regulatory and dysregulatory processes (see Fox [1994] for a review), with some studies demonstrating that frontal alpha absolute activity was relatively heightened in response to the experience of negative emotions in some children (Dawson et al. 1992; Dawson 1994) and adults (Nitschke et al. 1999; Schmidt and Trainor 2001). As such, the integration of multiple physiological measures with subjective self‐reports may help provide a more comprehensive understanding of individual differences in preoperative anxiety, potentially revealing distinct profiles that are not apparent when examining single indicators in isolation.

While most studies assessing pediatric preoperative anxiety adopt a variable‐oriented approach to examine relations between continuous measures of anxiety (typically self‐report) and various predictors and outcomes, another approach to understanding this phenomenon is to integrate multiple measures, including self‐report and psychophysiological markers of an anxious response, using person‐oriented analyses. A person‐centered approach, such as a latent profile analysis, is a data‐driven analytic approach that identifies unobserved subsets of a population who share a set of common characteristics, often referred to as indicators (Collins and Lanza 2010; Nylund‐Gibson and Choi 2018). This framework may be particularly valuable in the context of preoperative anxiety, where children may exhibit distinct profiles characterized by different combinations of subjectively experienced anxiety and patterns of physiological arousal.

In the present study, we first examined whether we could identify subgroups of children's anxious responses during the perioperative period across multiple indicators of anxiety (i.e., self‐report, heart rate, frontal brain alpha activity) using a latent profile analysis in children undergoing elective surgery. We then examined child‐level predictors of preoperative anxiety subgroups to better understand who may be at greater risk for experiencing heightened anxiety, as well as the behavioral outcomes associated with elevated preoperative anxiety as children entered the operating room (OR) and the postoperative anesthetic care unit, to better understand the clinical implications of elevated anxiety.

When examining child‐level predictors, we focused on children's temperament, defined as a set of early‐emerging biologically based individual differences in emotional reactivity and regulation (Rothbart and Derryberry 1981). Previous work has highlighted that children's temperamental shyness is a factor related to preoperative anxiety (Chow et al. 2019; Quinonez et al. 1997; Wright et al. 2013), which is consistent with work outside of the surgical context noting that children's temperamental shyness is related to increased risk for symptoms of anxiety in school and laboratory settings (e.g., Clauss and Blackford 2012; Coplan and Arbeau 2008; Poole and Schmidt 2021). In addition, we examined perioperative outcomes to better understand the predictive utility of children's preoperative anxiety in two ways. First, we examined how children's profiles of preoperative anxiety related to their behavior as they underwent anesthetic induction in the OR (e.g., resistance to anesthesia, crying, screaming). Second, we examined how children's profiles of preoperative anxiety related to their postoperative pain.

Although the number of subgroups identified is data driven, we hypothesized that there would be at least one group of children who manifested elevated anxious responses across indicators of anxiety (i.e., subjective and physiological markers of anxiety). Based on previous work examining correlates of children's preoperative anxiety (Chieng et al. 2014; Chorney and Kain 2009; Chow et al. 2019; Quinonez et al. 1997; Wright et al. 2013), we predicted that the subgroup of children with elevated anxiety across indicators would be higher in temperamental shyness, would show greater observed distress behaviors as they entered the OR, and would have higher postsurgical pain scores relative to other subgroups.

Method

Sample and Study Overview

The sample included 171 children (M age = 9.95 years, SDage = 1.72 years, age range: 7–13 years, 55.6% boys) and their caregivers. Information on children's ethnicity and family household income was not added to the study until after the first 100 participants were tested, but available data indicated that the majority of the sample was White (78.7%), and the median annual household income was $70,000–$79,999 CAD, which is representative of the catchment area that the hospital serves.

All participants underwent elective outpatient surgical procedures (e.g., tonsillectomy, adenoidectomy, or herniorrhaphy) at the McMaster Children's Hospital in Hamilton, Ontario, Canada. Child assent and parental consent were obtained prior to enrollment in the study. The present report consists of a secondary analysis of data collected and compiled over a span of 5 years (2013–2018). Initially, eligible participants were selected through an authorized screening list from the hospital. The list provided participant information including age, sex, type of surgery, and day of their preoperative visit to the hospital. During the preoperative visit (7–10 days prior to surgery; T1), we collected children's state anxiety (i.e., preoperative anxiety), temperament, and frontal EEG activity. Children's heart rate was also assessed by nurses during the preoperative visit. On the morning of their surgery (T2), children came to the hospital, and, for a second time, we collected measures of their state anxiety, frontal EEG activity, and heart rate. Children were then moved to a waiting area and eventually called into the OR, accompanied by their parents, where we videotaped the child's behavior up until anesthesia was initiated (i.e., induction) (T3). In the post‐anesthetic care unit (PACU), ratings of children's pain were recorded by nurses (T4). All participating families received a $25 gift certificate as a token of appreciation for their participation in the study. Study protocols were approved by the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (HiREB: Study #1220).

Subjective Measures

Child‐Reported State Anxiety

At T1 and T2, children completed the Children's Perioperative Multidimensional Anxiety Scale (CPMAS; Chow, Van Lieshout, Buckley et al. 2016). The CPMAS is a self‐report questionnaire used to assess children's state anxiety in a pediatric surgical setting. The scale contains five items, and a sample item was “Right now, how worried are you?” The questionnaire uses a visual analog scale, and children rate each item on a scale from 0 = not at all to 100 = very. Values were summed across items, with a possible score ranging from 0 to 500. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in our sample across time points (T1: α = 0.88, T2: α = 0.92). Anxiety scores were correlated at T1 and T2 (r = 0.61, p < 0.001) and averaged to create a preoperative anxiety measure.

Temperamental Shyness

At T1, parents completed the five‐item shyness subscale from the Early Adolescent Temperament Behavior Questionnaire (EATQ; Ellis and Rothbart 2001). An example item from the shyness subscale includes: “Child feels shy about meeting new people.” Items are rated on a 5‐point scale ranging from 1 = almost always untrue to 5 = almost always true. Children responded to the five highest‐loaded items from the self‐reported Cheek and Buss Shyness Scale (see Bruch et al. 1989; Cheek 1983; Cheek and Buss 1981). An example item from the shyness subscale includes: “It takes me a long time to overcome my shyness in new situations.” Items are rated on a 5‐point scale ranging from 0 = not at all to 4 = extremely. Both measures of shyness demonstrated good internal consistency in our sample (Parent report: α = 0.89; Child report: α = 0.80).

The parent report and child report measures were correlated (r = 0.29, p = 0.015), and therefore, we created a multi‐informant measure of children's shyness by averaging the z‐scores of both measures. Of note, when we used separate parent report and child report measures of shyness in our main analyses below, statistically comparable results were obtained across informants (see Supplementary Materials).

Psychophysiological Measures

EEG Data Recording and Collection

EEG data were collected with children's eyes open for approximately 40 min while the child was seated at the preoperative visit (T1) and waiting for their surgery after nurse preoperative preparation up until anesthetic induction (T2). EEG data were recorded using a Muse headband (InteraXon Inc., Toronto, ON, Canada). The Muse headband is a wireless quantitative EEG headband equipped with four dry sensor channels (i.e., frontal sites [AF7, AF8] and temporal sites [TP9, TP10]) referenced to a fifth channel located at the medial prefrontal region (Fpz). The Muse headband was paired to an Android tablet (Samsung Galaxy Tab A; Samsung Electronics Co., Ltd., Suwon, Republic of South Korea) via Bluetooth. During the acquisition phase, the Mind Monitor application (an interface software) was used to capture and record data. The raw EEG signals were sampled at 256 Hz. The Muse headband has demonstrated reliability with other existing portable and laboratory based (i.e., “wet”) EEG systems (Ratti et al. 2017).

EEG Data Reduction and Analysis

A real‐time fast Fourier transform (FFT) was employed by the Muse Monitor application to record and compute the power spectral density in the alpha (7.5–13 Hz) frequency band (Hashemi et al. 2016). We examined EEG power in the alpha frequency range because this frequency band has been consistently linked to individual differences in affective style and anxiety (Reznik and Allen 2018). A notch filter was set at 60 Hz to reduce artifacts and noise, and additional noise suppression was applied using the Muse's built‐in driven right leg circuit (with a 2‐µV noise floor) for all channels. Visual inspection was performed to remove artifacts emerging from signal connectivity issues. The onset and offset times were marked, and those data were eliminated from analyses. We examined the first 5 min of artifact‐free EEG alpha activity, given that previous studies have shown that only 60–90 s of EEG data are needed to provide reliable estimates of EEG alpha power in the laboratory (Tomarken et al. 1992; Tower and Allen 2009), and other work has shown that only 5 min of EEG data are needed for reliable estimates of EEG alpha power using the Muse Headband in the preoperative setting (Xu et al. 2023). A composite measure of average overall alpha power was computed for the frontal sites by averaging overall alpha power across the two frontal sites separately for T1 and T2 [i.e., (AF7 + AF8)/2]. Because EEG alpha power is inversely related to cortical activity, lower scores on the overall frontal metrics reflect more brain activity (Davidson and Tomarken 1989; Laufs et al. 2003). Frontal EEG alpha power measures were correlated at T1 and T2 (r = 0.35, p < 0.001) and averaged to create a preoperative index of children's frontal EEG alpha power.

Heart Rate

Children's heart rate was measured and recorded by nurses while the child was seated at the preoperative visit (T1) and waiting for their surgery after nurse preparation up until anesthetic induction (T2). Heart rate was recorded using a medical‐grade blood pressure monitor or using a pulse oximeter. Heart rate readings were then entered into the patient's medical chart by a member of the medical team. Heart rate information was subsequently extracted from the patient's medical chart for data analyses. Heart rate measures were correlated at T1 and T2 (r = 0.35, p < 0.001) and averaged to create a preoperative index of children's heart rate.

Behavioral Measures

Observed Distress Behaviors in the OR

Children's observed distress behaviors in the OR (modified version of Chorney and Kain 2009) were recorded by research assistants through a Go‐Pro camera at T3, beginning as children entered the OR, continuing through anesthetic induction, and ending when parents left the OR before the surgery. All videos were coded for child distress behaviors by two different research assistants, with a 20% overlap to determine interrater reliability. Four categories of behavior were coded in 10‐s intervals for presence or absence. The four specific behaviors coded included crying, screaming, nonverbal distress (e.g., facial expressions and body postures that denoted sadness), and resistance to anesthetic induction. Interrater reliability was assessed using Cohen's kappa and was 0.77 for crying, 1.00 for screaming, 0.69 for nonverbal distress, and 0.78 for resistance to anesthetic induction. We created a composite measure of observed distress behaviors because the four distress behaviors clustered as one component in a factor analysis. An average of the four behavior proportional values was subsequently made into an overall observed child distress measure with a possible value ranging from 0 to 1.

PACU Measure

Postoperative Pain

PACU measures of pain were extracted from medical charts. Pain was recorded by nurses for each child on a 0–10 scale using a measure appropriate for children's age at the nurse's discretion (e.g., numerical rating scale, Faces, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability [FLACC] scale, Wong–Baker Faces scale, Behavioral scale; Merkel et al. 1997; Wong and Baker 1988). Pain ratings were averaged when multiple ratings were recorded by nurses.

Statistical Analysis

A latent profile analysis was conducted to see if distinct profiles could be derived using the following three indicators of stress across the preoperative period: self‐reported anxiety, frontal EEG alpha activity, and heart rate. To avoid solutions based on local maxima, we used 500 random sets of starting values followed by 100 final optimizations. To determine the optimal number of latent profiles, we considered various criteria, including the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), Akaike information criterion (AIC), sample size–adjusted BIC (SSA‐BIC), the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and entropy. Subsequently, we tested differences in children's temperamental shyness, observed distress behavior in the OR, and postoperative pain using BCH to test the equality of means across profiles (Asparouhov and Muthén 2014).

Of the 171 children, 171 had self‐reported anxiety data, 167 had heart rate data, and 162 had postoperative pain data. After recruiting the first 100 children, several new measures and methods were integrated into the study protocol as part of a larger study (i.e., child temperament, continuous EEG, observed behavior in the OR). As a result, we had data on shyness for 71 children, frontal EEG alpha activity for 69 children, and observed distress behavior for 54 children. There was a relatively high proportion of missing data for the observed behavior data due to equipment malfunctions and because, in some cases, the research staff were asked to leave the OR for various logistical and medical reasons before a video could be taken. Males were more likely to have missing OR behavior data than females (p = 0.032), but age was not related to missingness (p = 0.08). Latent profile analysis uses full information maximum likelihood (FIML) to present estimates for children who had data for at least one of the indicators (N = 171). Because BCH uses listwise deletion, missing data on children's shyness, observed distress behavior, and postoperative pain were imputed using the expectation‐maximization algorithm to avoid the biased parameter estimates that can occur with pairwise or listwise deletion (Schafer and Graham 2002). Results without imputation can be found in the Supporting Information. All analyses were performed in Mplus version 8 (Muthén and Muthén 2018), and maximum likelihood estimation with standard errors that are robust to nonnormality was used.

Latent Profile Derivation

A two‐profile solution was selected due to the best model fit (Table S1). The first profile (73.7%, n = 126) had relatively average means across indicators of anxiety (self‐reported anxiety: M = 104.95, SE = 13.36; heart rate: M = 89.04, SE = 1.26; frontal EEG alpha power: M = 0.62, SE = 0.02). The second profile (26.3%, n = 45) had relatively elevated means across indicators of anxiety and may reflect a relatively heightened anxiety response (self‐reported anxiety: M = 296.03, SE = 20.93; heart rate: M = 92.77, SE = 2.00; frontal EEG alpha power: M = 0.51, SE = 0.04). Child‐Level and Perioperative Correlates of the Latent Profiles

Temperamental Shyness

The overall test indicated significant differences in mean levels of children's shyness across profiles, χ 2(1) = 84.995, p < 0.001. Children in Profile 2 had higher levels of shyness (M = 0.987, SE = 0.122) compared to children in Profile 1 (M = −0.394, SE = 0.073, d = 1.22; Figure 2A).

Observed Distress Behavior

The overall test indicated significant differences in mean levels of children's observed distress behavior in the OR across profiles, χ 2(1) = 20.42, p < 0.001. Children in Profile 2 displayed significantly more distress behaviors in the OR (M = 0.208, SE = 0.022) compared to children in Profile 1 (M = 0.093, SE = 0.008, d = 1.06; Figure 2B).

Postoperative Pain

The overall test indicated a marginally significant difference in mean levels of children's postoperative pain across profiles, χ 2(1) = 3.09, p = 0.079. Children in Profile 2 displayed a trend toward more postoperative pain (M = 2.087, SE = 0.300) compared to children in Profile 1 (M = 1.432, SE = 0.162, d = 0.35; Figure 2C).

Discussion

In the present study, we derived distinct person‐centered data‐driven profiles of anxious responses in children undergoing elective surgery across affective and physiological indicators across two preoperative visits. We found evidence for two profiles of children with distinct underlying patterns across these levels of analysis: a larger proportion with relatively average anxious responses and a smaller proportion of children with relatively heightened anxious responses. Children showing the elevated anxious response had higher levels of temperamental shyness and showed greater distress‐related behaviors as they entered the OR (e.g., resistance to anesthesia, screaming), and showed marginally higher postoperative pain compared with children with an average anxious response.

The smaller subgroup of children had heightened anxious responses according to self‐report, as well as two indirect physiological indicators of anxiety, including higher heart rate and higher frontal EEG alpha activity. Previous work has illustrated that both higher heart rate (Gordan et al. 2015; Ko et al. 2024; Wascher 2021) and higher frontal EEG alpha activity (Dawson et al. 1992; Dawson 1994; Nitschke et al. 1999; Schmidt and Trainor 2001) may be noninvasive indirect markers of anxious arousal. Interestingly, these three indicators of anxiety were not correlated with one another across our sample, which may reflect relative independence of the measures in our study. Instead, we found that there was a small subgroup of children who nevertheless experienced coordinated anxious responses on both affective and physiological levels.

We further found that children with greater temperamental shyness were likely to display this pattern of heightened anxiety across measures. Previous work in laboratory or classroom settings has identified shyness as a predictor of anxiety across development (e.g., Clauss and Blackford 2012; Coplan and Arbeau 2008; Poole and Schmidt 2021). Our finding suggests that this pattern of results may also generalize to other real‐world contexts, such as the preoperative setting, which is consistent with some previous work in a surgical context (Chow et al. 2019; Quinonez et al. 1997; Wright et al. 2013). Temperamentally shy children tend to experience fear and anxiety in response to unfamiliarity (Kagan 1997). The surgical setting is typically marked by unfamiliar people (e.g., staff, doctors), unfamiliar physical objects (e.g., medical equipment), and an unfamiliar environment and may be particularly stressful for shy children.

We also examined perioperative outcomes related to preoperative anxiety, including observed behavior as children entered the OR and postoperative pain levels. We found that children who showed the pattern of higher preoperative anxiety were more likely to display higher levels of observed behavioral distress as they entered the OR. This suggests that there may be behavioral implications of preoperative anxiety, including overt distress behaviors and resistance to anesthesia (Chorney and Kain 2009). Additionally, children with higher levels of preoperative anxiety also experienced relatively higher levels of postoperative pain, which is consistent with previous work noting a relation between preoperative anxiety and postoperative pain (see Chieng et al. [2014] and Chow et al. [2019] for reviews). However, it is important to note that this pattern was only marginally significant, and that across the sample, pain levels were relatively low. Therefore, the clinical implication of this finding may be limited.

The findings from this study have both theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, this work corroborates ideas that anxiety has multiple components and manifests along different levels, including affective and physiological. Additionally, pediatric preoperative anxiety is a multidimensional construct observed in childhood and manifesting in highly stressful contexts. This study also aligns with previous work identifying relations between temperament and anxiety in laboratory or classroom settings and extends this pattern of results to real‐world contexts, demonstrating that temperamental vulnerabilities manifest in clinically meaningful ways during pediatric medical procedures.

Practically, the results illustrate heterogeneity in children's preoperative anxiety experience. Although most children showed some degree of anxiety, a subset of approximately 25% experienced higher anxiety responses across levels of analysis, which directly predicted their behavior as they entered the OR and may predict postoperative clinical outcomes, including elevated pain. Given the implications of elevated preoperative anxiety, this study underscores the importance of screening for, and attempting to improve, preoperative anxiety in children. Existing interventions such as audiovisual interventions have been shown useful in reducing preoperative anxiety (Chow, Van Lieshout, Buckley et al. 2016; Perry et al. 2012). Managing preoperative anxiety may also help to reduce some downstream implications, including resistance to anesthetic induction and postoperative pain.

Strengths, Limitations, and Considerations

Strengths of the current study include a large sample of children undergoing elective surgery assessed in a unique, ecologically salient setting (i.e., the surgical context), multiple assessments of anxiety across different methods, including self‐report, heart rate, and frontal brain activity, a multi‐informant assessment of children's temperament (parent and child report), coding of children's observed behavior as they entered and remained in the OR, and assessment of postoperative pain in the PACU.

The study should also be considered in the context of its limitations. Due to modifications to the study protocol part way through data collection and the unique challenges of data collection in the surgical context, there was a relatively large portion of missing data for some measures, particularly EEG and observed behavior. Additionally, there were logistical limitations within the clinical site primarily driven by dynamic surgical scheduling and unavoidable cancellations, complicated participant recruitment, and adherence to the prespecified protocol timeline. It is possible that missingness across study variables could have biased profile identification. It should also be noted that because psychophysiological data were collected in a surgical setting as opposed to a more typical research environment, the data collection procedures could not be completely standardized across children. For example, children were able to engage naturally with their environment during EEG recording in the waiting room (e.g., play with toys), and there were likely differences in the external environment (e.g., noise, lighting, people) across children, which could introduce unavoidable noise into the data. We also acknowledge that frontal EEG alpha power is an indirect, rather than direct, measure of anxiety. Accordingly, our interpretations remain speculative as there are multiple neural mechanisms that likely underlie the observed differences in our study. Further, the pain scales were at the discretion of the nurse, and possible differences may have introduced measurement error.

Further, the sample comprised children undergoing elective surgery, and thus we do not know how the results would generalize to other populations of children such as those with chronic illnesses who may undergo frequent surgeries and/or other types of surgery (like emergency procedures). Our sample also comprised children recruited from one hospital and geographic region, and was relatively homogeneous in terms of sociodemographic factors. Likewise, we focused on middle to late childhood, and thus we do not know how the results would generalize to younger samples of children. It is possible that the proportion of children following the “elevated” anxiety response would differ across study populations and different types of surgery. As such, we need to exercise caution in how representative and generalizable our findings are to varying pediatric perioperative settings.

Conclusion

In the present study, we found that there was a subset of children who showed elevated anxiety responses across multiple affective and physiological measures. Temperamental shyness was related to the likelihood of manifesting this elevated response profile, and these children were more likely to display overt distress behaviors during anesthetic induction and higher levels of postoperative pain compared to children showing relatively average anxious response. These results illustrate predictors and perioperative outcomes associated with the experience of elevated anxiety in the surgical context and suggest that not all children undergoing surgery are alike. To this end, the present findings have theoretical implications for understanding temperament–anxiety relations in different contexts, and have clinical relevance for informing programs directed at managing children's preoperative anxiety.
