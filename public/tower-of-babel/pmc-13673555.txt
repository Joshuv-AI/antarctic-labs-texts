Human immunodeficiency virus infection mimicking very early onset inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Rasha Abi Radi Abou Jaoudeh, Cary Sauer, Teresa Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVery early onset inflammatory bowel disease (VEO‐IBD), defined as diagnosis before 6 years of age, is often associated with immune dysregulation and monogenic etiologies; however, infectious mimics may be overlooked in low‐prevalence regions. We report a 4‐year‐old male with poor weight gain, intermittent diarrhea, elevated fecal calprotectin, and persistently elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate. Endoscopy showed diffuse friable colonic mucosa, and biopsies demonstrated focal active colitis with mild crypt distortion. He was diagnosed with VEO‐IBD and treated with mesalamine, infliximab, adalimumab, and upadacitinib, with incomplete response. At age 7, he was hospitalized with fever, cytopenias, and pulmonary infiltrates; human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) testing was positive, with Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia and cytomegalovirus viremia. After discontinuing immunosuppression and initiating antiretroviral therapy, gastrointestinal symptoms and inflammatory markers improved. HIV‐associated colitis can closely mimic VEO‐IBD, and progressive lymphopenia should prompt evaluation for secondary immunodeficiency.Keywords: chronic diarrhea, fecal calprotectin, immune dysregulation, secondary immunodeficiency, weight loss

INTRODUCTION

Very early onset inflammatory bowel disease (VEO‐IBD), defined as IBD diagnosed before 6 years of age, represents a distinct clinical entity characterized by higher rates of monogenic etiologies, immune dysregulation, and atypical presentations compared with older‐onset IBD. 1

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection can cause gastrointestinal manifestations and HIV‐associated enteropathy/colitis, which can resemble IBD. 2 , 3 Despite this overlap, HIV is not routinely tested for in the work‐up for VEO‐IBD in regions with low pediatric prevalence. 4

We report a child initially diagnosed with VEO‐IBD who was later found to have HIV infection with associated colitis.

CASE REPORT

A 4‐year‐old male born at 35 weeks' gestation with intrauterine growth restriction and a birth weight of 1.93 kg was referred to the gastrointestinal clinic for chronic poor weight gain and intermittent diarrhea. Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated a normal complete blood count, albumin 4.0 g/dL, normal thyroid function, normal celiac serologies, and normal vitamin D level. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated at 38 mm/h (0–15 mm/h). Stool studies were negative for infectious pathogens. Fecal calprotectin was elevated at 312 µg/g (<49 µg/g). Over subsequent months, his stools remained loose, with occasional hematochezia and suboptimal weight gain.

At 5 years of age, upper endoscopy and colonoscopy revealed diffuse friable mucosa from rectum to ascending colon (Figure 1). Histopathology demonstrated focal active colitis with lymphoid aggregates and mild crypt architectural distortion, without granulomas or apoptotic bodies. Terminal ileal biopsies were unremarkable. ESR remained elevated at 42 mm/h (0–15 mm/h), and fecal calprotectin increased to 486 µg/g (<49 µg/g). Based on histologic findings and clinical course, the patient's presentation was attributed to VEO‐IBD. Mesalamine 75 mg/kg/day was initiated, resulting in modest improvement in stool frequency but persistent poor growth.

First colonoscopy: The perianal and digital rectal examinations were normal. A diffuse area of sessile protrusions and mildly friable mucosa with contact bleeding was found in the entire colon, most prominent from rectum to ascending colon. There was otherwise no erythema or signs of inflammation.

Given his young age, immunologic evaluation was pursued. Initial lymphocyte subset analysis revealed a low helper T cell marker count: cluster of differentiation 3(CD3)+cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) count of 640 cells/µL (700–2200 cells/µL) and a CD4/cluster of differention 8 (CD8) ratio of 0.5 (0.8–5)‐indicating the presence of fewer helper T cells compared to cytotoxic T cells, which was attributed to chronic inflammation from VEO‐IBD. Immunoglobulin levels were normal. A primary immunodeficiency gene panel revealed variants of uncertain significance, including a heterozygous c.329A>G (p.Gln110Arg) CARD8 variant, which has been associated with autosomal dominant Crohn's disease. 5 Whole exome sequencing was unrevealing.

Repeat colonoscopy (Figure 2) 3 months after starting mesalamine showed improvement with only scattered areas of nodular mucosa in the sigmoid and descending colon. Biopsies showed mild crypt architectural distortion in the ascending and descending colon. Clinically, diarrhea and poor weight gain persisted, with rising ESR 45 mm/h (0–15 mm/h) and fecal calprotectin 732 µg/g (<49 µg/g). Therapy was thus escalated to infliximab. Initial clinical response was observed; however, secondary loss of response occurred within 6 months due to anti‐drug antibodies.

Second colonoscopy: Scattered areas of mildly nodular mucosa were found in the sigmoid colon and in the descending colon but otherwise normal without inflammation. The terminal ileum appeared normal.

He was then transitioned to adalimumab, and despite dose escalation, fecal calprotectin remained elevated up to 900 µg/g (<49 µg/g). Given incomplete disease control, therapy was transitioned to upadacitinib. At that time, the lymphopenia (decline in CD3+CD4 counts to 420 cells/µL [700–2200 cells/µL]) was attributed to chronic inflammation and ongoing immunomodulatory therapy. The potential benefit of achieving better control of his IBD with upadacitinib was therefore considered to outweigh the risks of further immunosuppression. Stool frequency improved transiently, but poor weight gain persisted. At 7 years of age, he was admitted for 4 weeks of high‐grade fevers and further weight loss. Laboratory evaluation revealed leukopenia, with an absolute lymphocyte count 800 cells/µL (1.5–6.8 cells/µL), a CD3+CD4 count of 218 cells/µL (700–2200 cells/µL), and fecal calprotectin of 540 µg/g (<49 µg/g).

Chest imaging demonstrated bilateral ground‐glass opacities. Given progressive CD4 decline and atypical infectious presentation, HIV testing was performed and returned positive, with markedly elevated viral load. Bronchoalveolar lavage confirmed Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, and serum testing revealed cytomegalovirus viremia. Immunosuppressive medications were discontinued, and antiretroviral therapy was initiated.

Further investigation into the pregnancy course revealed that at 31 weeks of gestation, the patient's mother tested negative for HIV‐1/2 antigen and antibody; therefore, the patient was not tested for HIV at birth. After the patient's diagnosis was established, his mother disclosed that she had been diagnosed with HIV when the patient was 2 years old and was receiving antiretroviral therapy. Given her negative third‐trimester screening result, we suspected that she acquired HIV later in pregnancy or during the peripartum period, with subsequent vertical transmission to the patient. There were no concerning features in the patient's history to suggest postnatal acquisition through another route.

Following initiation of antiretroviral therapy, the patient demonstrated gradual immune recovery, with CD3+CD4 count increasing to 620 cells/µL (700–2200 cells/µL) at 6 months, and his fecal calprotectin decreased to 112 µg/g (<49 µg/g) without additional IBD‐directed therapy. Gastrointestinal symptoms improved significantly, with normalization of stool frequency and adequate weight gain with nutritional support. Given sustained clinical improvement off biologic therapy and normalization of inflammatory markers, his prior colitis was reinterpreted as HIV‐associated enteropathy mimicking VEO‐IBD.

DISCUSSION

This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of chronic colitis in early childhood (Table S1) and underscores the potential for HIV‐associated colitis to mimic IBD. The patient's initial presentation with chronic diarrhea, hematochezia, elevated fecal calprotectin, and focal active colitis with architectural distortion could be attributed to early IBD. However, persistent T‐cell lymphopenia in a child with presumed VEO‐IBD should prompt evaluation for secondary immunodeficiency, including HIV, particularly when accompanied by poor growth, atypical disease course, or inadequate response to immunosuppression.

Gastrointestinal manifestations are common in untreated HIV infection. Chronic diarrhea has been reported in up to 60% of individuals with advanced disease. 6 , 7 Histopathologic findings alone are insufficient to distinguish HIV‐associated colitis from IBD. 8 HIV‐associated colitis may demonstrate focal active inflammation, increased lamina propria lymphocytes, crypt architectural distortion, and lymphoid aggregates, which overlap considerably with IBD. 8

The true prevalence of HIV‐associated colitis in children is not precisely defined, particularly in high‐resource settings where pediatric HIV incidence is low. However, gastrointestinal involvement is more frequently observed in individuals with advanced immunosuppression, particularly when CD4 counts fall below 200 cells/µL. 6 Opportunistic infections such as cytomegalovirus, Cryptosporidium, and P. jirovecii may further complicate the clinical picture and contribute to intestinal or systemic inflammation. 9

In our patient, initiation of antiretroviral therapy was followed by immune recovery as well as resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms with improved growth despite discontinuation of biologic immunosuppression. This clinical response supports HIV‐associated immune dysregulation as the primary driver of his gastrointestinal inflammation.

This case also underscores the risks of immunosuppressive therapy in patients with unrecognized HIV infection. Anti‐tumor necrosis factor agents and Janus kinase inhibitors can impair immune surveillance and increase susceptibility to opportunistic infections. 9 Recognition of underlying HIV infection before escalation of immunosuppression is therefore essential.

Although HIV testing is not uniformly included in the evaluation of all children with suspected VEO‐IBD and may be guided by geographic and individual risk factors, 4 it should be strongly considered in the presence of unexplained lymphopenia, recurrent infections, growth failure, atypical disease behavior, or poor response to conventional therapy. Broader infectious screening before systemic immunosuppression may prevent diagnostic delay and reduce morbidity.

CONCLUSION

HIV‐associated colitis can resemble VEO‐IBD clinically, endoscopically, and histologically. Progressive CD4 decline, poor growth, and/or inadequate response to conventional IBD therapy should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis and evaluation for secondary causes of colitis. Early identification of HIV infection is critical, as antiretroviral therapy can lead to resolution of gastrointestinal inflammation, fundamentally altering management and prognosis.
