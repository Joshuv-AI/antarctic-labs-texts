Abstract
Offline reinforcement learning (RL) aims to find optimal policies in dynamic environments in order to maximize the expected total rewards by leveraging pre-collected data. Learning from heterogeneous data is one of the fundamental challenges in offline RL. Traditional methods focus on learning an optimal policy for all individuals with pre-collected data from a single episode or homogeneous batch episodes, and thus, may result in a suboptimal policy for a heterogeneous population. In this paper, we propose an individualized offline policy optimization framework for heterogeneous time-stationary Markov decision processes (MDPs). The proposed heterogeneous model with individual latent variables enables us to efficiently estimate the individual Q-functions, and our Penalized Pessimistic Personalized Policy Learning (P4L) framework admits suitable local solutions with a fast rate on the average regret under a within-group partial coverage assumption and regularity conditions. In addition, our simulation studies and a real data application demonstrate the superior numerical performance of the proposed method compared with existing methods.



Keywords:
Dynamic treatment regime, Heterogenous data, Markov decision process,
Precision learning

1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful tool for numerous
decision-making problems in variety of domains, including healthcare, robotics,
gaming and pricing strategy. In practice, however, different individuals can exhibit substantial variations in their behaviors and responses
to different actions, together with other individual features, leading to high
population heterogeneity. Ignoring the resulting heterogeneity can lead to suboptimal
policies
(
Chen et al., 2022
;
Hu et al., 2022
)
, especially for some
individuals who are underrepresented, disadvantaged or vulnerable in the
population. For example, this could lead to health disparities for
populations living in rural areas.
The commonly used RL algorithms
(
Munos, 2003
;
Mnih et al., 2015
;
Haarnoja et al., 2018
;
Luckett et al., 2019
;
Chen et al., 2021
, e.g.,)
assume the environment is stationary and homogeneous for
all individuals, which may not hold in general. Therefore, there is a growing
need to develop RL methods which can learn individualized policies to account for
populational heterogeneity.

Recently, several RL methods for heterogeneous data have been developed to
address the suboptimality of learning policy with a homogeneity assumption. Specifically,
PerSim
(
Agarwal et al., 2021
)
learns personalized simulators by assuming a
low rank tensor structure of latent factors for each individual, state, and
action. However, it requires a finite state space to find an optimal policy based on
the learned personalized environment.
Chen et al. (2022)
introduce
heterogeneity only on the reward function, where the Q-functions and groups are
learned simultaneously, and the policies are updated groupwise. However, individual
policies are learned within a group, and information borrowing among groups is
ignored.
Hu et al. (2022)
learn the warm-up groupwise policies from the
last period of stationary episodes based on change-point detection. However,
the methods of
Chen et al. (2022)
and
Hu et al. (2022)
can only make use of
information within the learned population groups, hence sample efficiency is not
fully utilized.
Meta-reinforcement learning (meta-RL) aims to learn a policy that can maximize
the reward of any task from a distribution of heterogeneous tasks

(
Beck et al., 2023
;
Mitchell et al., 2021
;
Zhang and Wang, 2021
)
. However, existing meta-RL methods typically
require the collection of millions of interactions with environments with
different tasks during meta-training online or more pre-collected training data.
In addition, theoretical understanding of offline meta-RL remains limited.
For a single decision point, evaluating the heterogeneous treatment effect (HTE) is
inevitable for learning personalized treatment regimes. Existing works mainly
estimate HTE with panel or non-panel data

(
Athey and Imbens, 2015
;
Shalit et al., 2017
;
Wager and Athey, 2018
;
Künzel et al., 2019
;
Nie and Wager, 2021
;
Shen et al., 2022
, and references
therein)
.
Our goal is to develop a single task RL method with offline data from individuals having
heterogeneous treatment effects and transactions.

The problem we intend to solve
has an element in between the meta-RL and HTE.
More specifically, we aim to solve an offline reinforcement learning (RL) problem under
populational heterogeneity, where we learn subject-specific policies based on offline
datasets collected a priori. One challenge of the populational heterogeneity in offline RL
is that each subject may have different state-action transitions with different
immediate rewards. As a result, existing batch RL methods assuming subject
homogeneity, aiming to learn a common optimal policy for all subjects, may
suffer from biased and inconsistent estimation of subject-specific value
functions and consequently, only sub-optimal policies can be learned.
However, directly applying batch RL methods to detected homogeneous clusters of trajectories

(
Chen et al., 2022
;
Hu et al., 2022
)
diminishes sample
efficiency, as cross-subject information is not incorporated in policy learning.
More importantly, the coverage assumption becomes less feasible for a specific homogeneous
subgroup rather than for the entire population.
In addition, since the offline data follow some fixed behavior policies, and we
cannot interact with the environments of the subjects to collect more data
to evaluate the policies, this may result in a distributional shift between the
behavior policies and the optimal ones
(
Levine et al., 2020
)
. The
distributional shift increases the estimation error in evaluating the value of behavior
policies, and hence causes suboptimality in learning optimal individual policies.

To tackle the above challenges, in this paper we propose a novel
heterogeneous latent variable model with a penalized pessimistic policy learning approach
to learn optimal individual policies simultaneously.
Our method relies on a partial coverage assumption that the
average visitation probability of the batch data from individuals within each
homogeneous subgroup can cover the discounted visitation probability induced by the target policy for each individual in that subgroup.
While directly applying the RL method for an individual episode requires that the
visitations of state-action from an individual episode should cover
the visitation probability induced by the target policy. This is an unrealistic
assumption and could prevent us from
borrowing information from other individuals in the population. By introducing a
model of the shared structure of the Q-function and policy with individual latent variables,
which captures heterogeneous individual information, we could efficiently
utilize the aggregated data from all individual episodes for off-policy evaluation.

In addition, the proposed heterogeneous latent variable model allows us to simultaneously
learn optimal policies for all individuals, instead of learning optimal policy
for each estimated homogeneous cluster, respectively
(
Chen et al., 2022
;
Hu et al., 2022
)
.
To further weaken the coverage assumption for offline RL, we adopt the pessimism
concept and establish a policy optimization algorithm to find the optimal
policy structure with individual latent variables, and aim to
maximize the overall value estimated by the most pessimistic policy evaluator
from an uncertainty set of Q function candidates. With a proper uncertainty
level, we only require a partial coverage assumption for the optimal policies.

By introducing multi-centroid penalties on latent variables, we could encourage
individuals from the same subgroup to have similar latent variables and hence
similar optimal policies. Theoretically, under the stated regularity and tuning
conditions, we show that there exist local penalized estimators
of optimal policies with unknown subgroup information that attain the same regret rate
as oracle estimators when the subgroup information is known. Their regrets are
upper bounded by a rate near the inverse square root of the number of transitions from
heterogeneous data. To resolve the
computational burden introduced by the constraints of the uncertainty set, we propose
to solve a Lagrangian dual problem which also admits local solutions with the same rate of the
upper bound of regret, under an additional assumption on the convexity of
the space of the Q-function.

In practice, our method can be applied to learning optimal individual policies
for the same task, but with heterogeneous time-stationary environments. We
remark that the time-stationarity and Markovianity can be ensured by
concatenating multiple decision points and adding auxiliary variables (e.g.,
time stamps) into the state. Therefore, the proposed method is also applicable
for tasks with rapidly changing environments, for example, critical care. In
addition, the theoretical properties established in our penalized estimation method
guarantee that our method is applicable when the number of
individuals is small but the length of each trajectory is large, which is
common in mobile health applications.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we briefly introduce the discrete-time time-stationary
heterogeneous MDP and the problem formulations. Then we formally introduce the
proposed method for policy optimization with heterogeneous batch data in Section

3
. Theoretical analysis on the regret of policy learned by
the proposed algorithm is given in Section
4
. In Section 5, we
further illustrate the implementation details. Sections 6 and 7 demonstrate the
numerical performance of our method with simulation studies and a real data application.
Section 8 concludes this paper.

Preliminaries

In this section, we provide notations and frameworks for the Markov decision
processes. In addition, we provide several important concepts and
properties associated with MDPs, which will be used as building blocks for the proposed method.

2.1
Notations

We denote the index set
[N]=\left\{1,\dots,N\right\}
. For an index set

{\mathcal{G}}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
, we denote its cardinality by
|{\mathcal{G}}|
. For
sequences
\left\{\varpi_{n}\right\}
and
\left\{\theta_{n}\right\}
, we use

\varpi_{n}\gtrsim\theta_{n}
to denote that
\varpi_{n}\geq c\theta_{n}
for some constant
c>0
, and

\varpi_{n}\lesssim\theta_{n}
to denote that
\varpi_{n}\leq c^{\prime}\theta_{n}
for some constant
c^{\prime}>0
.
For two measures
\nu
and
\mu
, we use
\nu<\!\!<\mu
to denote that
\nu
is absolutely
continuous with respect to
\mu
.
For a collection
{\mathbf{u}}=\{u^{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
, we define
\|{\mathbf{u}}\|_{2,\infty}=\max_{i\in[N]}\|u^{i}\|_{2}
.
In addition, we use
(S^{i},A^{i},R^{i},S_{+}^{i})
or
(S^{i},A^{i},S_{+}^{i})
to denote a
transition tuple (state, action, reward, next state) or (state, action, next state) in the trajectory of subject
i
for any decision time
t
.

2.2
Frameworks

Reinforcement learning is a technique that solves sequential decision making
problems for agents in some unknown environments. The observed data can be
summarized in sequences of state-action-reward tuples over decision time. At
each decision time
t\geq 0
, the agent
i\in[N]
observes the current
state
S_{t}^{i}\in{\mathcal{S}}
from the environment, where the state space
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}}
is a Borel set.
Based on the observed history, a decision
A_{t}^{i}
is selected from the action
space
{\mathcal{A}}
, which is assumed to be a finite discrete set of size
|{\mathcal{A}}|
in this paper. The
environment then provides an immediate reward
R_{t}^{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
to the agent
and moves forward to the next state
S_{t+1}^{i}
.

The policy
\pi^{i}=\left\{\pi_{t}^{i}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
for agent
i\in[N]
is a set
of probability distributions on the action space
{\mathcal{A}}
, which determines the
behavior of the agent.
In this work, we focus on the time-invariant policy, i.e.,
\pi_{0}^{i}=\dots=\pi_{t}^{i}=\dots
, for each

i\in[N]
, respectively.
For each policy
\pi^{i}
, we define the Q-function to measure the expected
discounted cumulative reward for the agent
i
starting from any state-action
pair
(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
, denoted by

Q_{\pi^{i}}^{i}(s,a)={\mathbb{E}}^{\pi^{i}}\left\{\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t}^{i}\mid S_{0}^{i}=s,A_{0}^{i}=a\right\},

(1)

where the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}^{\pi^{i}}
is taken by assuming that the agent
i

selects actions according to the policy
\pi^{i}
, and
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discounted
factor for balancing the short-term and long-term rewards. For example, a myopic
value function (with
\gamma=0
) only cares about immediate reward, which is
typically adopted in contextual bandit problems where the current state is
independent of history.

To evaluate the overall performance of a policy
\pi^{i}
for individual
i
, we
define the value of
\pi^{i}
by

J^{i}(\pi^{i})=(1-\gamma){\mathbb{E}}_{S_{0}^{i}\sim\nu^{i}}Q_{\pi^{i}}^{i}(S_{0}^{i},\pi^{i}(S_{0}^{i})),

(2)

for some given distribution
\nu^{i}
of the initial state
S_{0}^{i}
. Here we define

Q(s,\pi(s))=\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}Q(s,a)\\
\times\pi(a\mid s)
for any policy
\pi
and function
Q
defined over
{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
.
The objective of individualized policy optimization is to find an in-class policy

\pi_{*}^{i}\in\arg\max_{\pi^{i}\in\Pi}J^{i}(\pi^{i}),

(3)

which optimizes the value for each individual

i\in[N]
. In this work, we focus on optimizing the values of policies for a shared distribution

\nu
of the initial states for every individual, i.e.,
\nu_{i}=\nu
for all
i\in[N]
.
Making decisions tailored for each individual is critical in many applications.
For example, in mobile
health, we only consider applying some treatment regimes once the state of an individual
reaches a certain range of risk.

To formalize the framework, we adopt heterogeneous time-stationary Markov decision processes

(
Puterman, 2014
, MDPs,)
to model the data generating processes for
different agents. Specifically, for
each individual
i\in[N]
, the episode

\left\{\left(S_{t}^{i},A_{t}^{i},R_{t}^{i}\right)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
follows an MDP

{\mathcal{M}}^{(i)}=\left\{{\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{A}},{\mathbb{P}}^{i},r^{i},\gamma\right\}
,
which satisfies the standard conditions in Assumption
1
below.

Assumption 1

(Standard Conditions)
.

The following statements hold for MDPs
\left\{{\mathcal{M}}^{(i)}\right\}_{i\in[N]}
.

(a)

There exists a time-invariant transition kernel
{\mathbb{P}}^{i}
such that for any

t\geq 0
,
s\in{\mathcal{S}},a\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
F\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{S}})
,

\mbox{Pr}\left(S_{t+1}^{i}\in F\mid S_{t}^{i}=s,A_{t}^{i}=a,\left\{S_{j}^{i},A_{j}^{i},R_{j}^{i}\right\}_{0\leq j<t}\right)={\mathbb{P}}^{i}(S_{t+1}^{i}\in F\mid S_{t}^{i}=s,A_{t}^{i}=a),

where
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{S}})
is the collection of Borel subsets of
{\mathcal{S}}
and

\left\{S_{j}^{i},A_{j}^{i},R_{j}^{i}\right\}_{0\leq j<t}=\emptyset
if
t=0
.

(b)

There exists a reward function such that

{\mathbb{E}}[R_{t}^{i}\mid S_{t}^{i},A_{t}^{i},\left\{S_{t^{\prime}}^{i},A_{t^{\prime}}^{i},R_{t^{\prime}}^{i}\right\}_{0\leq t^{\prime}<t}]=r^{i}(S_{t}^{i},A_{t}^{i})

for any
t\geq 0
.
Assume that
|R_{t}^{i}|\leq R_{\max}
almost surely for all
i,t
, and normalize rewards so that
R_{\max}=1
. In particular,
\|r^{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.

(c)

The batch data
\left\{(S_{t}^{i},A_{t}^{i},R_{t}^{i},S_{t+1}^{i})\right\}_{0\leq t<T^{i}}
for subject
i
are generated by a stationary behavior policy
\pi_{b}^{i}:{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow\Delta({\mathcal{A}})
, which is a mapping from state space
{\mathcal{S}}
to the probability simplex on action space
{\mathcal{A}}
.
To simplify notations, we assume a balanced dataset with
T_{i}=T
here. In case
of unbalanced data, the proposed method can be extended without much difficulty.

Assumption
1
is standard in the RL literature for
homogeneous MDP, whereas in our heterogeneous setting, the stationary transition kernels

{\mathbb{P}}^{i}
are allowed to be different among individuals. The uniformly bounded assumption on the immediate reward
R_{t}^{i}
is used to
simplify the technical proofs but can be relaxed by imposing some higher-order
moment condition on
R_{t}^{i}
instead.
We optimize stationary policies in a pre-specified policy class
\Pi
.
Existence of an in-class maximizer also requires regularity of that class.
The compactness and continuity assumptions in Section
4.1

provide it for the theoretical analysis. Some commonly used policy classes include linear decision
functions, decision trees and neural networks.

We define the MDP induced probability measures, followed
by an important property in Lemma
1
, which is a building
block for our method. For each MDP
{\mathcal{M}}^{(i)}
, we further introduce the
discounted visitation probability measure over
{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
induced by a policy

\pi^{i}
as follows:

d_{\pi^{i}}^{i}=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}p_{\pi^{i},t}^{i},

(4)

where
p_{\pi^{i},t}^{i}
is the marginal probability measure of
(S_{t}^{i},A_{t}^{i})

induced by the policy
\pi^{i}
with the initial state distribution
\nu
.
Similarly, we define the average visitation probability across
T
decision
points as

\bar{d}^{i}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}p_{\pi_{b}^{i},t}^{i},

(5)

Here
p_{\pi_{b}^{i},t}^{i}
denotes the marginal law in the observed behavior
trajectory, initialized from its own data-collection distribution. In the
stationary analysis below this is an invariant state distribution for

\pi_{b}^{i}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{i}
, and it need not equal the evaluation distribution

\nu
used in (
4
). The corresponding expectation is denoted by
\bar{{\mathbb{E}}}^{i}
.
The grand average visitation probability over all MDPs across
T
decision points
is defined by
\bar{d}=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\bar{d}^{i}
with corresponding
expectation
\bar{{\mathbb{E}}}
.

With the above definitions of MDP induced probability measures, we can quantify the
off-policy evaluation (OPE) for estimating the value of
\pi^{i}
for
individual
i
using Q-function
\widetilde{Q}^{i}
in (
2
), which is
denoted as
\widetilde{J}^{i}(\pi^{i})=(1-\gamma){\mathbb{E}}_{S_{0}^{i}\sim\nu}\widetilde{Q}^{i}(S_{0}^{i},\pi^{i}(S_{0}^{i}))
.
Then we have the following property for the OPE error of
\widetilde{J}^{i}(\pi^{i})
using
\widetilde{Q}^{i}
.

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, for any bounded measurable candidate

\widetilde{Q}^{i}
, we have that

J^{i}(\pi^{i})-\widetilde{J}^{i}(\pi^{i})={\mathbb{E}}_{(S^{i},A^{i})\sim d_{\pi^{i}}^{i}}[R^{i}+\gamma\widetilde{Q}^{i}(S_{+}^{i},\pi^{i}(S_{+}^{i}))-\widetilde{Q}^{i}(S^{i},A^{i})].

(6)

Here the reward and next-state expectations, conditional on
(S^{i},A^{i})
,
are taken under individual
i
’s reward law and transition kernel.

Lemma
1
implies that the OPE error by
\widetilde{Q}^{i}
of policy

\pi^{i}
is the expectation of Bellman error with respect to the discounted visitation
probability
d_{\pi^{i}}^{i}
induced by policy
\pi^{i}
for individual
i
.
This identity controls policy-value error. A single scalar value error does
not, by itself, identify the entire Q-function. In a heterogeneous population,
we use empirical Bellman moments to obtain an upper bound on value error under
the shared model introduced in Section
3
.

3
Methods

In this section, we present our value-based method for learning optimal individualized
policies with a heterogeneous latent variable model. Objective (
3
) implies a straightforward approach to learn the optimal policy

\pi_{*}^{i}
by maximizing the value (
2
) with an estimated Q-function

Q_{\pi^{i}}^{i}
defined in (
1
) for each individual
i\in[N]
using existing RL methods for homogeneous data

(
Munos, 2003
;
Mnih et al., 2015
;
Haarnoja et al., 2018
;
Luckett et al., 2019
;
Chen et al., 2021
, e.g.,)
.
However, utilizing
individual-specific data only in estimating the heterogeneous Q-functions

\left\{Q_{\pi^{i}}^{i}\right\}_{i\in[N]}
and subsequent learning optimal policies

\left\{\pi_{*}^{i}\right\}_{i\in[N]}
could lead to sample-inefficient estimations,
especially when some individuals have a limited number of observations,
e.g., when the episode length
T
is small.

3.1
Heterogeneous Latent Variable Model

To obtain more efficient estimation, it is beneficial to aggregate information
among individuals by imposing shared
structures on policies and Q-functions with latent variables that encode
individual information. Then we can utilize sub-homogeneous information by
encouraging the grouping of individuals with similar latent variables. This ensures
that individuals from the same subgroups have similar optimal policies, by construction.

Our proposed shared structures and learning algorithm is motivated by the
individual OPE errors.
If
d_{\pi^{i}}^{i}<\!\!\!<\bar{d}^{i}
and
d_{\pi^{i}}^{i}/\bar{d}^{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, which is a
symmetric bounded class of functions on
{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
, Lemma
1

implies that

\displaystyle\left|J^{i}(\pi^{i})-\widetilde{J}^{i}(\pi^{i})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\bar{{\mathbb{E}}}^{i}\left\{\frac{d_{\pi^{i}}^{i}(S^{i},A^{i})}{\bar{d^{i}}(S^{i},A^{i})}\left(R^{i}+\gamma\widetilde{Q}^{i}(S_{+}^{i},\pi^{i}(S_{+}^{i}))-\widetilde{Q}^{i}(S^{i},A^{i})\right)\right\}\right|

(7)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f^{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}}\bar{{\mathbb{E}}}^{i}\left\{f^{i}(S^{i},A^{i})\left(R^{i}+\gamma\widetilde{Q}^{i}(S_{+}^{i},\pi^{i}(S_{+}^{i}))-\widetilde{Q}^{i}(S^{i},A^{i})\right)\right\}.

The upper bound in (
7
) circumvents using the policy-induced discounted visitation
probability
d_{\pi^{i}}^{i}
in (
6
), which is typically hard
to estimate. As long as we can find
the space
{\mathcal{F}}
, which contains
d_{\pi^{i}}^{i}/\bar{d}^{i}
, we can always establish an upper
bound of the OPE error by calculating the supremum of expectation in (
7
) with respect to the empirical distribution
\bar{d}^{i}
(
5
).
Then a min-max estimating approach
(
Jiang and Huang, 2020
, e.g.,)
to learn
Q_{\pi^{i}}^{i}
can be formulated as

\min_{\widetilde{Q^{i}}}\sup_{f^{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}}\bar{{\mathbb{E}}}^{i}\left\{f^{i}(S^{i},A^{i})\left(R^{i}+\gamma\widetilde{Q}^{i}(S_{+}^{i},\pi^{i}(S_{+}^{i}))-\widetilde{Q}^{i}(S^{i},A^{i})\right)\right\}.

However, learning
Q_{\pi^{i}}^{i}
separately for each individual suffers from sample
inefficiency and requires individual coverage. This naturally motivates us to
learn Q-functions simultaneously with a combined dataset for the heterogeneous
population. Suppose individuals belong to subgroups
{\mathcal{G}}^{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{G}}^{K}
,
with
{\mathbb{P}}^{i}={\mathbb{P}}^{k}
and
r^{i}=r^{k}
for
i\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
. Let
p_{k}=|{\mathcal{G}}^{k}|/N
and

\bar{d}^{k}=\frac{1}{|{\mathcal{G}}^{k}|}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}\bar{d}^{i}.

We write
\bar{{\mathbb{E}}}^{k}
for expectation with
(S,A)\sim\bar{d}^{k}
, followed
by a transition from
{\mathbb{P}}^{k}
and a reward with conditional mean
r^{k}
.
This allows state–action pairs induced by a target policy to be covered by
behavior policies from other individuals in the same subgroup.

To avoid learning Q-functions individually, we propose shared structures
with latent variables
u^{i}=u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
for
i\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
, where

{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{u}}
is compact. For policies

\pi^{k}=\pi(\bullet;u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}})
, suppose there exists
Q_{\pi}\in{\mathcal{Q}}
such that

Q_{\pi}(\bullet;u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}})=Q_{\pi^{k}}^{k}(\bullet)
. For a candidate

\widetilde{Q}\in{\mathcal{Q}}
, write
\widetilde{Q}^{i}=\widetilde{Q}(\bullet;u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}})
for
i\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
.
Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a bounded symmetric class of functions on

{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{U}}
. Assume
d_{\pi^{k}}^{k}\ll\bar{d}^{k}
and that one
shared function
w_{\pi}\in{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies

w_{\pi}(s,a;u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}})=\frac{d_{\pi^{k}}^{k}}{\bar{d}^{k}}(s,a),\qquad k\in[K],\quad\bar{d}^{k}\text{-almost everywhere}.

For brevity write the sampled Bellman residual as

b_{\widetilde{Q},\pi}^{k}(S,A,R,S_{+})=R+\gamma\widetilde{Q}(S_{+},\pi(S_{+};u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}});u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}})-\widetilde{Q}(S,A;u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}).

Lemma
1
and symmetry of
{\mathcal{F}}
give

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{J^{i}(\pi^{i})-\widetilde{J}^{i}(\pi^{i})\}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k}p_{k}\bar{\mathbb{E}}^{k}[w_{\pi}(S,A;u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}})b_{\widetilde{Q},\pi}^{k}]\right|

(8)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\sum_{k}p_{k}\bar{\mathbb{E}}^{k}[f(S,A;u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}})b_{\widetilde{Q},\pi}^{k}].

The bound controls the OPE error of the population-average value. Given the
latent variables, we can estimate
Q_{\pi}
by solving the min-max problem

\min_{\widetilde{Q}\in{\mathcal{Q}}}\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}\bar{{\mathbb{E}}}^{k}\left[f(S,A;u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}})\{R+\gamma\widetilde{Q}(S_{+},\pi(S_{+};u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}});u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}})-\widetilde{Q}(S,A;u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}})\}\right].

(9)

Specifically, the empirical version of (
9
) is

\min_{\widetilde{Q}\in{\mathcal{Q}}}\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\widehat{\Phi}(\widetilde{Q},f,\pi,{\mathbf{u}}),

(10)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\Phi}(\widetilde{Q},f,\pi,{\mathbf{u}})=\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f(S_{t}^{i},A_{t}^{i};u^{i})\{R_{t}^{i}+\gamma\widetilde{Q}(S_{t+1}^{i},\pi(S_{t+1}^{i};u^{i});u^{i})-\widetilde{Q}(S_{t}^{i},A_{t}^{i};u^{i})\}.

When the groups are unknown, the empirical criterion is optimized jointly
with the latent variables.

To better understand the concept of shared structure, we illustrate the Q-function
under the linear mixed-effect model as follows:

Y_{t}^{i}=(u^{i})^{\top}Z_{t}^{i}+\alpha^{\top}X_{t}^{i}+\epsilon_{t}^{i},\qquad i\in[N],~t=0,\dots,T-1,

where
Y_{t}^{i}
is the response,

(Z_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{i})
are covariates with subject-specific effect coefficient
u_{i}
and fixed
effect coefficient
\alpha
, and
\epsilon_{t}^{i}
is the error term.
If we define

Q(Z_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{i};u^{i})=(u^{i})^{\top}Z_{t}^{i}+\alpha^{\top}X_{t}^{i}={\mathbb{E}}\left\{Y_{t}^{i}\mid Z_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{i}\right\},

then given
u^{i}
,
Q
is determined by the shared parameter
\alpha
.
In our framework, we find
Q
(the fixed effect) by minimizing the loss

\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f(Z_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{i};u^{i})^{\top}\{Y_{t}^{i}-Q(Z_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{i};u^{i})\},

where the error is captured by
f(\bullet;u^{i})
for each individual. After that, we
update individual latent variable
u^{i}
(the random effect), which is included in the policy
optimization step. In our individual policy learning method, the
policy
\pi(\bullet;u^{i})
is estimated simultaneously with
u^{i}
,
i\in[N]
.
See Section
3.2
for
more details.

In the following, we provide an example of linear MDPs with shared structure.

Example 1

(Linear MDP
(
Bradtke and Barto, 1996
;
Melo and Ribeiro, 2007
)
)
.

Suppose all MDPs share the same known bounded measurable feature map

{\bm{\psi}}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with

\operatorname{span}\{{\bm{\psi}}(s,a):(s,a)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
For MDP
{\mathcal{M}}^{(i)}
, the transition probability and reward function are

{\mathbb{P}}^{i}(ds_{+}\mid s,a)={\bm{\psi}}(s,a)^{\top}{\bm{\mu}}^{i}(ds_{+}),\qquad r^{i}(s,a)={\bm{\psi}}(s,a)^{\top}{\bm{\theta}}^{i},

where
{\bm{\mu}}^{i}
is a vector of finite signed measures and the displayed
transition kernel is a probability kernel. We further suppose that

{\bm{\mu}}^{i}={\bm{\mu}}+{\bm{\delta}}^{i}
and
{\bm{\theta}}^{i}={\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\beta}}^{i}
, where
{\bm{\mu}}
and

{\bm{\theta}}
are shared for all MDPs, and impose the normalizations

\sup_{s,a}\|{\bm{\psi}}(s,a)\|_{2}\leq 1,\qquad\|{\bm{\theta}}^{i}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d},\qquad\sup_{\|h\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\left\|\int h(s){\bm{\mu}}^{i}(ds)\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}.

By Lemma
2
in the Supplementary Material,

Q_{\pi^{i}}^{i}(s,a)={\bm{\psi}}(s,a)^{\top}{\mathbf{w}}_{\pi^{i}}^{i},\qquad{\mathbf{w}}_{\pi^{i}}^{i}=(I_{d}-\gamma M_{\pi^{i}}^{i})^{-1}({\bm{\theta}}+{\bm{\beta}}^{i}),

where

M_{\pi^{i}}^{i}=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\bm{\mu}}^{i}(ds_{+})\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\pi^{i}(a\mid s_{+}){\bm{\psi}}(s_{+},a)^{\top}.

For a vector of finite signed measures
\bm{\eta}
, define the
linear operator on finite signed state–action measures by

[\Gamma_{\bm{\eta}}^{\pi}p](ds,a)=\pi(a\mid s)\bm{\eta}(ds)^{\top}\int_{{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}{\bm{\psi}}(s_{-},a_{-})p(ds_{-},da_{-}).

Then, with
(\nu\pi^{i})(ds,a)=\nu(ds)\pi^{i}(a\mid s)
, the discounted
occupancy can be represented as

d_{\pi^{i}}^{i}=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}(\Gamma_{{\bm{\mu}}}^{\pi^{i}}+\Gamma_{{\bm{\delta}}^{i}}^{\pi^{i}})^{t}(\nu\pi^{i}).

If
({\bm{\delta}}^{i},{\bm{\beta}}^{i})
is constant within each subgroup and belongs to
a fixed finite-dimensional parameter family, we can encode it by

u^{i}=(\texttt{para}\{{\bm{\delta}}^{i}\},{\bm{\beta}}^{i})
and set

\pi^{i}=\pi(\bullet;u^{i})
. The Q-functions then admit a shared representation
indexed by
u^{i}
. Under within-group coverage, a density-ratio witness has
slice

w_{\pi}(\bullet;u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}})=\frac{(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}(\Gamma_{{\bm{\mu}}}^{\pi^{k}}+\Gamma_{{\bm{\delta}}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}}^{\pi^{k}})^{t}(\nu\pi^{k})}{\bar{d}^{k}},

where the quotient denotes a Radon–Nikodym derivative. Boundedness and
simultaneous representation of these slices in
{\mathcal{F}}
are assumed, together
with the regularity conditions in Section
4.1
.

3.2
Pessimistic Policy Learning with Latent Variables

Even though we can find the point estimate of
Q_{\pi}
by solving the empirical min-max
problem (
10
) for a given
(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})
, it is still
unclear how to quantify
whether the estimated
Q_{\pi}
could confidently and accurately evaluate the
policy for each individual, partially due to the statistical error and model
misspecification.
For the heterogeneous policy learning goal, to obtain a confident
improvement of the policy, we adopt a pessimistic strategy which
optimizes the policy by using the most pessimistic Q-function estimate
\widetilde{Q}

in some uncertainty set defined by

\Omega(\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\alpha)\triangleq\left\{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}\colon\max_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\widehat{\Phi}(Q,f,\pi,{\mathbf{u}})\leq\alpha\right\},

(11)

where
\alpha=\alpha_{NT}>0
is some constant depending on
N
and
T
.
This ensures
that the lower confidence bound of the values of learned optimal policies is the
largest among all in-class policies.
The construction of the uncertainty set originates from (
10
).

Then we propose to learn the in-class optimal policy
\pi_{*}
together with latent
variables
{\mathbf{u}}_{*}
by solving

\max_{\pi\in\Pi,{\mathbf{u}}}\min_{Q\in\Omega(\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\alpha)}(1-\gamma)\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}_{S_{0}^{i}\sim\nu}Q(S_{0}^{i},\pi(S_{0}^{i};u^{i});u^{i}),

(12)

which maximizes the policy values of the worst case in the uncertainty sets. We
denote the estimated policy as
\widehat{\pi}
and estimated latent variables as

\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}
.
Throughout, outer maximizations are restricted to candidates with nonempty
uncertainty sets. Through this pessimistic learning approach, we can provide
an overall regret gap with coverage required only for the optimal comparison
policy, under the assumptions in Section
4
.

Notice that the policy optimization in (
12
) can be
difficult due to the constraints on
Q
, especially when the user-defined
function classes are complex. To lessen the computational burden, we use
the Lagrangian dual problem of the primal problem, where the Lagrangian is defined as

L(Q,\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\lambda)=(1-\gamma)\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}_{S_{0}^{i}\sim\nu}Q(S_{0}^{i},\pi(S_{0}^{i};u^{i});u^{i})+\lambda(\max_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\widehat{\Phi}(Q,f,\pi,{\mathbf{u}})-\alpha).

(13)

Then we can solve the dual problem by

\max_{\pi\in\Pi,{\mathbf{u}}}\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\min_{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}}L(Q,\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\lambda).

(14)

We denote the learned policy and latent variables by
\widehat{\pi}^{\dagger}
and

\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}^{\dagger}
, respectively. For each fixed feasible pair
(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})
,
weak duality gives

\sup_{\lambda\geq 0}\min_{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}}L(Q,\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\lambda)\leq\min_{Q\in\Omega(\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\alpha)}(1-\gamma)\sum_{i}{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}Q(S_{0},\pi(S_{0};u^{i});u^{i}).

The inequality also holds after maximizing over the common feasible outer
domain. In Section
4
, we establish strong duality in a
strictly feasible neighborhood and show that a local dual solution achieves
the same regret rate as a local primal solution.

In the case when individuals can be classified into several groups, e.g.,

\left\{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
as a partition of indices

\left\{1,\dots,N\right\}
,
to encourage subgrouping we can impose further constraints on the latent variables

{\mathbf{u}}
, e.g.,
\sum_{i\in[N]}\min_{k\in[K]}\|u^{i}-v^{k}\|_{2}
,
\mathrm{rank}({\mathbf{u}})\leq K
or restricting
{\mathbf{u}}={\mathbf{v}}_{1}{\mathbf{v}}_{2}
with penalty on
\|{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\|_{F}^{2}+\|{\mathbf{v}}_{2}\|_{F}^{2}
.
In this paper, we study the multi-centroid penalty

\sum_{i\in[N]}\min_{k\in[K]}\|u^{i}-v^{k}\|_{2}^{2}
, which encourages subgroup
structures by grouping neighboring individuals
u^{i}
,
i\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
to the nearest
centroid
v^{k}

(
Tang et al., 2021
)
. Unlike the fused-type penalty
(
Chen et al., 2022
, see e.g.,)
, which requires pairwise
comparison of complexity
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(N^{2})
, the multi-centroid penalty significantly
reduces the computational complexity to
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}(NK)
, since
K<\!\!<N
in
general. In addition, the multi-centroid penalty reduces estimation bias,
which is typically large with one-centroid shrinkage penalty, e.g., the
L^{2}
-penalty.

By adding the multi-centroid penalty to (
12
), we can solve for
(\widehat{\pi},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}},\widehat{{\mathbf{v}}})
through

\max_{\pi\in\Pi,{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}}\min_{Q\in\Omega(\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\alpha)}(1-\gamma)\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}_{S_{0}^{i}\sim\nu}Q(S_{0}^{i},\pi(S_{0}^{i};u^{i});u^{i})-{\mathscr{P}}_{\mu}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}),

(15)

where

{\mathscr{P}}_{\mu}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=\mu\sum_{i\in[N]}\min_{k\in[K]}\left\{\|u^{i}-v^{k}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(16)

For the guarantees in Section
4
, we optimize
u^{i}\in{\mathcal{U}}

and constrain the centroids to the nonempty compact set

{\mathcal{V}}_{\Delta}=\left\{(v^{1},\ldots,v^{K})\in{\mathcal{U}}^{K}:\min_{k\neq\ell}\|v^{k}-v^{\ell}\|_{2}\geq\Delta\right\},

(17)

where
\Delta>0
is fixed. The separation constraint is vacuous for
K=1
.
Similarly, the penalized dual estimator

(\widehat{\pi}^{{\dagger}},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}^{{\dagger}},\widehat{{\mathbf{v}}}^{{\dagger}})
can be solved by

\max_{\pi\in\Pi,{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}}\max_{\lambda\geq 0}\min_{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}}L(Q,\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\lambda)-{\mathscr{P}}_{\mu}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}).

(18)

The dual problem (
18
) removes the explicit
constraint on
Q
. The computational details are given in
Section
5
.

4
Theory

In this section, we evaluate the theoretical performance of the proposed policy
optimization method. The performance is often measured by the regret, which is
the value loss of the estimated policy compared to the value of the optimal in-class policy.

With our heterogeneous latent variable model, for any
(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})
, the overall
value is defined as

J(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}J^{i}\{\pi(\bullet;u^{i})\},

where each value is evaluated in the actual individual MDP.

Specifically, we study the case when individuals can be partitioned into

K\geq 1
groups
{\mathcal{G}}^{1},\dots,{\mathcal{G}}^{K}
, with corresponding proportions
p_{k}=|{\mathcal{G}}^{k}|/N
,
k\in[K]
. Here
p_{\min}=\min_{k}p_{k}
is bounded away from zero uniformly in
N
.
Within each subpopulation
{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
, the
individuals share the same latent variable, i.e.,
u^{i}=u^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}
for

i\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
. For the optimal policy
\pi_{*}
, the associated
{\mathbf{u}}_{*}
satisfies

u_{*}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}\neq u_{*}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k^{\prime}}}
when
k\neq k^{\prime}
.
Write
g(i)=k
for
i\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
,
{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{v}})^{i}=v^{g(i)}
, and

{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}=\{{\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{v}}):{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\Delta}\}
, with
{\mathcal{V}}_{\Delta}
as in
(
17
). The benchmark
(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})
, where

{\mathbf{u}}_{*}={\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{v}}_{*})
, maximizes
J
over
\Pi\times{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
.
The oracle optimization uses this same domain.

The theoretical challenges of this framework stem from two sources. First, the dimension of

{\mathbf{u}}
grows linearly with
N
, which causes a large variation of

\widehat{\Phi}(Q,f,\pi,{\mathbf{u}})
and therefore requires a larger uncertainty set
\Omega(\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\alpha)
with a
bigger
\alpha
, which is required to capture the true Q-function.
It is crucial that the true Q-function is contained in the proposed uncertainty set,
otherwise the proposed pessimistic learning method would suffer from model misspecification.
However, the increased uncertainty of the true Q-function could further increase the regret
of the estimated optimal policy with latent variables. Therefore, a proper
uncertainty level is required to obtain good individual policies in terms of value. Second, the trajectories

\left\{\left(S_{t}^{i},A_{t}^{i},R_{t}^{i}\right)_{t=0}^{T-1}\right\}_{i\in[N]}
are
time-correlated for each individual and their correlation structures can be
varying among different subpopulations. We establish theoretical properties showing
that our method can learn a pair
(\widehat{\pi},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}})
well, which can be as good as the oracle pair

(\pi_{\circ},{\mathbf{u}}_{\circ})
in terms of the regret asymptotically, through carefully choosing
the uncertainty level
\alpha
.

For the rest of this section, we first list several technical assumptions in
Section
4.1
. In Section
4.2
, we derive the overall regret for the oracle policy, when the
true subpopulation information
\left\{{\mathcal{G}}_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}
is known. In
Section
4.3
, we establish the existence of local penalized
and dual solutions with the oracle regret rate. All proofs of the theorems are provided in the
Supplementary Material.

4.1
Assumptions

We first provide several technical assumptions and discuss their implications.

Assumption 2
.

The individual trajectories are independent. Within each subgroup

{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
, individuals share the transition kernel
{\mathbb{P}}^{k}
and conditional
mean reward
r^{k}
, while their behavior policies may differ.
For each individual
i
, the transition process

Z_{t}^{i}=(S_{t}^{i},A_{t}^{i},R_{t}^{i},S_{t+1}^{i})
is stationary and exponentially

\beta
-mixing, with
\beta_{i}(j)\leq\beta_{0}\exp(-\zeta j)
,

j\geq 1
, for common constants
\beta_{0}\geq 0
and
0<\zeta\leq 1
.

Assumption
2
characterizes the dependency structure within
individual trajectories. The restriction
\zeta\leq 1
is without loss of
generality, since a smaller exponential mixing rate is also valid.
The dependence between observations decays exponentially with their time
separation, as in
Liao et al. (2022)
;
Zhou et al. (2022)
;
Chen et al. (2022)
.
Within-group homogeneity permits pooling under

\bar{d}^{k}=|{\mathcal{G}}^{k}|^{-1}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}\bar{d}^{i}
. Its transition-tuple
expectation is denoted by
\bar{\mathbb{E}}^{k}
.

Definition 1

(Covering Number)
.

Let
\left({\mathcal{C}},\|\bullet\|\right)
be a normed space. For any
{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
, the
set
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}
is a
\epsilon
-covering of
{\mathcal{H}}
if

{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\cup_{i=1}^{n}B(x_{i},\epsilon)
, where
B(x,\epsilon)
is the
\|\bullet\|
-ball with center

x_{i}
and radius
\epsilon
. The
covering number
of
{\mathcal{H}}
is defined as

N(\epsilon,{\mathcal{H}},\|\bullet\|)=\min\left\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\cup_{i=1}^{n}B(x_{i},\epsilon)\text{ for some
}\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}\right\}.

Assumption 3
.

(a)

For any
{\mathcal{H}}\in\{{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{Q}},\Pi\}
,
{\mathcal{H}}
is a nonempty
compact class of measurable functions in the uniform norm, and

N(\epsilon,{\mathcal{H}},\|\bullet\|_{\infty})\leq(A_{0}/\epsilon)^{{\mathfrak{C}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}}
for
0<\epsilon\leq 1
,
with fixed
A_{0}\geq 2
and
{\mathfrak{C}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}\geq 1
.

(b)

The latent domain
{\mathcal{U}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{u}}
is compact and
convex, and
{\mathcal{V}}_{\Delta}
is nonempty for a fixed
\Delta>0
.

(c)

Uniformly over
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
,
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
\pi\in\Pi
, and
s,a
,

\displaystyle|Q(s,a;u)-Q(s,a;u^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq L_{Q}\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2},

\displaystyle|f(s,a;u)-f(s,a;u^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq L_{f}\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2},

\displaystyle\sum_{a}|\pi(a\mid s;u)-\pi(a\mid s;u^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\pi}\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2}.

(d)

We have
\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}}\|Q\|_{\infty}\leq B=(1-\gamma)^{-1}

and
\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\|f\|_{\infty}\leq C_{{\mathcal{F}}}
.
The class
{\mathcal{F}}
is symmetric and contains zero.

Assumption
3
imposes conditions on spaces
{\mathcal{F}}
,
{\mathcal{Q}}
and

\Pi
to bound their complexities. The polynomial covering condition in
part (a) is common in the literature
(
Antos et al., 2008
, e.g.,)
.
Bounded parameter classes, including suitable fixed neural network
architectures, provide examples. The Lipschitz conditions control continuity
in the latent variable, while the uniform bounds permit exponential
concentration inequalities
(
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
)
.

Assumption 4
.

For every
\pi\in\Pi
and
{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\Delta}
, there exists

Q_{\pi,{\mathbf{v}}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}
such that

Q_{\pi,{\mathbf{v}}}(\bullet;v^{k})=Q^{k}_{\pi(\bullet;v^{k})}(\bullet)
for all
k
.
For the optimal policy
\pi_{*}^{k}=\pi_{*}(\bullet;v_{*}^{k})
, let
d_{*}^{k}
be
its discounted visitation measure. We have
d_{*}^{k}\ll\bar{d}^{k}
, and
there is a single
f_{*}\in{\mathcal{F}}
satisfying

f_{*}(\bullet;v_{*}^{k})=dd_{*}^{k}/d\bar{d}^{k}
,

\bar{d}^{k}
-almost everywhere, simultaneously for all
k\in[K]
.

Remark 1
.

Assumption
4
requires that the individual Q-functions
can be modeled by the pre-specified class
{\mathcal{Q}}
over the group-constant
representations considered by the oracle. For the optimal policy, the
symmetric class
{\mathcal{F}}
jointly captures the distribution ratios

dd_{*}^{k}/d\bar{d}^{k}
. In contrast to per-individual coverage, the mixture of
behavior policies within the same subgroup may cover the relevant
state–action pairs collectively. Realizability and comparator coverage are
commonly imposed for value-based methods

(
Jiang and Huang, 2020
;
Liao et al., 2022
)
.

Remark 2
.

For the linear MDP in Example
1
, bounded coefficient
families in the span of
{\bm{\psi}}
can supply suitable Q-functions.
The symmetric bounded class
{\mathcal{F}}
must jointly represent the group ratios,
which, when densities exist, have the form

\frac{1-\gamma}{\bar{d}^{k}(s,a)}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left(\Gamma_{{\bm{\mu}}}^{\pi_{*}^{k}}+\Gamma_{{\bm{\delta}}^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}}^{\pi_{*}^{k}}\right)^{t}[\nu\pi_{*}^{k}](s,a).

Under more complex MDP structures, neural networks can be used for
{\mathcal{Q}}

and a similar architecture for
{\mathcal{F}}
, subject to the boundedness,
regularity, and realizability conditions above.

4.2
Oracle Regret Bounds

In this section, we establish the overall regret bound for the non-penalized
oracle policy with subgroup information. We then establish the same regret
rate for suitable local penalized estimators.

To derive the regret bounds for the oracle policy, we first show that the true
Q-function is a feasible solution under the uncertainty set in (
11
)
with high probability for a proper choice of
\alpha
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
4
hold,
and choose, with a sufficiently large fixed leading constant,

\alpha\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\max\{{\mathfrak{C}}_{{\mathcal{Q}}},{\mathfrak{C}}_{{\mathcal{F}}},{\mathfrak{C}}_{\Pi},d_{u}\}}{NT\zeta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\frac{8}{p_{k}\delta}\log(e|{\mathcal{G}}^{k}|T)},

(19)

where
(NT)^{-2}\leq\delta<1
. With probability at least
1-\delta
,
simultaneously for every
\pi_{\circ}\in\Pi
and

{\mathbf{u}}_{\circ}={\mathbf{u}}({\mathbf{v}}_{\circ})\in{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
,

Q_{\pi_{\circ},{\mathbf{v}}_{\circ}}\in\Omega(\pi_{\circ},{\mathbf{u}}_{\circ},\alpha).

Theorem
1
establishes the foundation of the regret
guarantee for the oracle estimator of the optimal policy with latent variables.
The feasibility of
Q_{\pi_{\circ},{\mathbf{v}}_{\circ}}
in the uncertainty set
\Omega(\pi_{\circ},{\mathbf{u}}_{\circ},\alpha)
by choosing

\alpha
in (
19
) verifies the use of pessimistic policy
learning by solving (
12
) with a high probability. That
is, we pessimistically evaluate the value of given oracle pair
(\pi_{\circ},{\mathbf{u}}_{\circ})
by a lower
bound of its true value during the policy optimization. The pessimism in the
offline reinforcement learning can relax the full coverage
assumption by a partial coverage assumption, which only requires that the
offline data cover the trajectory generated by the optimal policy. However, the
realizability assumption for the existence of
Q_{\pi,{\mathbf{v}}}\in{\mathcal{Q}}
for all admissible
(\pi,{\mathbf{v}})
is still
required
(
Jiang and Huang, 2020
;
Xie et al., 2021
;
Zhan et al., 2022
)
. A recent work
by
Chen et al. (2023)
further relaxes the partial coverage assumption
for policy learning with homogeneous offline data by using the Lebesgue
decomposition theorem and distributional robust optimization, but a
Bellman completeness condition is required in addition. In this paper, we
also impose the partial coverage assumption in Assumption
4
for technical simplicity.

Equipped with Theorem
1
, we establish the following regret
warranty theorem for the oracle estimator.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
–
4
are
satisfied, with
\alpha
and
\delta
given in Theorem
1
, the
regret for an oracle maximizer
(\widehat{\pi}_{\circ},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{\circ})
of
(
12
) over
\Pi\times{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
is bounded by

J(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})-J(\widehat{\pi}_{\circ},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{\circ})\lesssim\alpha,

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Our regret bound in Theorem
2
shows that the overall regret of
finding an optimal policy can converge to zero in a rate near
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}((NT)^{-1/2})
, as long as the length or trajectory
T
or sample size
N
diverges to infinity when the subgroup
information is known. Up to logarithmic factors, our regret bound of order
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}((NT)^{-1/2})
scales reversely with the number of
individuals
N
because our algorithm uses all information from
N

individuals through a shared structure. However, the method of
Chen et al. (2022)
only
establishes the bound for off-policy estimation for a given policy, but not the
regret for a learned optimal policy.

4.3
Feasibility and Regret Bounds for the Dual Problem

When the subgroup information is unknown, the optimizer of (
12
) typically cannot satisfy a regret bound as good as that of the oracle
estimator without constraints on latent variables
{\mathbf{u}}
.
However, by solving (
15
), which includes a
multi-centroid penalty on
{\mathbf{u}}
, the oracle regret rate is asymptotically achievable
under the regularity and tuning conditions below.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
2
hold,

{\mathcal{Q}}
is convex, and the centroids lie in
{\mathcal{V}}_{\Delta}
.
Let
\delta=\delta_{NT}\to 0
and
\alpha\to 0
, and assume

\mu\alpha^{2}\to\infty
. With probability tending to one, there exists
a local maximizer
(\widehat{\pi},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}},\widehat{{\mathbf{v}}})
of
(
15
) such that, after indexing its
centroids by the true subgroups,

\max_{k\in[K]}\max_{i\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}\|\widehat{u}^{i}-\widehat{v}^{k}\|_{2}\lesssim(\mu\alpha)^{-1}=o(\alpha).

(20)

In addition, its regret satisfies

J(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})-J(\widehat{\pi},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}})=\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}_{\mathrm{p}}(\alpha).

Theorem
3
shows that a local solution of the
penalized optimization can achieve the oracle regret rate. Even if the
estimated latent variables are not identical within each subgroup, their
within-group dispersion is small enough to preserve this rate. The bound
concerns dispersion around estimated centroids, rather than identification
of particular latent coordinates. By setting
K=1
, the regret has the same
order, up to logarithmic factors, as the homogeneous rates discussed in

Xie et al. (2021)
;
Zhan et al. (2022)
.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
3
hold,
and the benchmark is individually optimal:

J^{i}\{\pi_{*}(\bullet;u_{*}^{i})\}\geq J^{i}\{\pi(\bullet;u)\}
for every

i
,
\pi\in\Pi
, and
u\in{\mathcal{U}}
. Define

J^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})=|{\mathcal{G}}^{k}|^{-1}\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}J^{i}\{\pi(\bullet;u^{i})\}
.
For the local solution in Theorem
3
,

J^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})-J^{{\mathcal{G}}^{k}}(\widehat{\pi},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}})=\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}_{\mathrm{p}}(p_{k}^{-1}\alpha),\qquad k\in[K].

The same regret order holds for each individual in group
{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
.

Corollary
1
shows that the rate of the regret for
group
{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
is near
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}_{p}((Np_{k}^{2}T)^{-1/2})
, up to the
logarithmic and complexity factors in
\alpha
, when
\alpha/p_{k}\to 0
. For an individual
i
in
subgroup
{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
, we can bound its regret through to the proportion of
group
{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
. However, the rate of this subgroup regret bound is slower than

\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}_{p}((Np_{k}T)^{-1/2})
, which can be obtained by homogeneous RL methods using only data collected from subgroup
{\mathcal{G}}^{k}
, when the subgroup information is

known
. In our scenario, the subgroup information is
unknown

and the subgroups with small proportion could be overwhelmed by larger subgroups,
as our optimization objective is based on average regret.

To alleviate the computational burden of solving (
15
), we propose to
solve for a dual problem (
18
). In the
following Theorem
4
, we characterize the regret of the dual
local optimizer
(\widehat{\pi}^{{\dagger}},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}^{{\dagger}})
with the convexity of
{\mathcal{Q}}
space.

Theorem 4
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3
, with
probability tending to one there exists a local maximizer

(\widehat{\pi}^{\dagger},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}^{\dagger},\widehat{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\dagger},\widehat{\lambda}^{\dagger})
of
(
18
) whose regret satisfies

J(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})-J(\widehat{\pi}^{\dagger},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}^{\dagger})=\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}_{\mathrm{p}}(\alpha).

In Theorem
4
, a suitable local dual solution achieves
the same regret rate as the oracle estimator. Convexity and strict
feasibility of the inner Q-function problem ensure strong duality in the
neighborhood used in the proof. Convexity can be restrictive for some
function classes. Extending this result to nonconvex Q-function classes
with an asymptotically negligible duality gap remains of interest.

5
Computation

In this section, we present our computational algorithm to learn

(\widehat{\pi}^{{\dagger}},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}^{{\dagger}})
by solving the dual problem (
18
) for multicentroid cases. By choosing
{\mathcal{Q}},{\mathcal{F}}
and
\Pi
as some
pre-specified functional classes, e.g., neural network (NN) architectures, we can
apply stochastic gradient descent and ascent to solve the dual problem (
18
), except for
{\mathbf{u}}
and
{\mathbf{v}}
.

For the outer maximization problem of (
18
), maximizing
{\mathbf{u}}
and
{\mathbf{v}}
with the penalty term
{\mathscr{P}}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})

is challenging, since the penalty is non-convex with all individualized latent
parameters
{\mathbf{u}}
and subgroup parameters
{\mathbf{v}}
without subgroup information and
their coupling. To achieve a faster computing speed, we propose to apply the
ADMM method
(
Boyd et al., 2011
)
to update
{\mathbf{u}}
and
{\mathbf{v}}
alternatively.
Specifically, we consider solving an equivalent problem given
\pi,\lambda,Q
,
with auxiliary variables
{\mathbf{w}}
,

\displaystyle\max_{{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{w}}}L(Q,\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\lambda)-{\mathscr{P}}_{\mu}({\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{v}}),

(21)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }{\mathbf{w}}={\mathbf{u}}.

Applying the augmented Lagrangian method to (
21
), we can solve

\max_{{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{w}}}L(Q,\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\lambda)-{\mathscr{P}}_{\mu}({\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{v}})-\bm{\eta}^{\top}({\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{w}})-\frac{\rho}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{w}}\|_{F}^{2},

(22)

with
\rho>0
and Lagrangian multiplier vector
\bm{\eta}
. Consequently,
we perform the ADMM method to update
{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{w}}
and
\bm{\eta}
iteratively:

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbf{u}}\leftarrow\operatorname{argmax}_{{\mathbf{u}}}L(Q,\pi,{\mathbf{u}},\lambda)-\frac{\rho}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{w}}+\rho^{-1}\bm{\eta}\|_{F}^{2},

(23)

\displaystyle({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{w}})\leftarrow\argmin_{{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{w}}}{\mathscr{P}}_{\mu}({\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{v}})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|{\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{w}}+\rho^{-1}\bm{\eta}\|_{F}^{2},

\displaystyle\bm{\eta}\leftarrow\bm{\eta}+\rho({\mathbf{u}}-{\mathbf{w}}),

where the first block
{\mathbf{u}}
can be approximated by gradient ascent.

({\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{w}})
is treated as a single block here in the second step.
With unrestricted centers, this block can be approximated by K-means
clustering on
x^{i}=u^{i}+\eta^{i}/\rho

{\mathbf{v}}
as the cluster means,
then setting
w^{i}=(\rho x^{i}+2\mu v^{c_{i}})/(\rho+2\mu)
, where
c_{i}

For the theoretical parameter space, enforce
{\mathbf{u}}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{N}
and

{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\Delta}
in these updates. The regret guarantees concern suitable
local solutions and do not assert convergence of the numerical updates.

In this section, we use the Multi-parameter Intelligent Monitoring in Intensive
Care (MIMIC-III)
dataset (
https://physionet.org/content/mimiciii/1.4/

the performance of the learned optimal policies. In this dataset,
longitudinal information (e.g., demographics, vitals, labs and standard scores)
was collected from 17,621 patients who satisfied

Nanayakkara et al. (2022)

The dataset is prepared by the same data pre-processing steps used in

Raghu et al. (2017)
. For the cleaned dataset,
the state space consists of a 41-dimensional real-valued vector, which includes
information on patients’ demographics, vitals, standard scores and lab results.
A detailed description is given in Supplementary Material. Specifically, the
most important standard score, which captures the patient’s organ function, is
the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, which is
commonly used in clinical practice to assess SEPSIS severity.
In the preprocessed dataset for each patient, the state records are aligned on
a 4-hour time grid for simplicity. We only consider patients who had at least 3
time stamps, which results in 16,356 patients.
In addition, due to the high dimensionality of the state space, we perform a
dimension reduction procedure via principle component analysis.
Specifically, we select the top 10 principal components that explain
at least 95% of the total variation of the state, resulting in an
11-dimensional encoded state that consists of 10 principal components and the
SOFA score.

At each time point
t
after the enrollment, the patient is treated with
vasopressor and/or intravenous fluid, or no treatment. To ensure each action has considerable
times of repeating in the dataset, we discretize the level of two treatments
into 3 bins, respectively. The combination of the two drugs results in an action
space of size
|{\mathcal{A}}|=9
. We use the negative SOFA score at time
t+1
as the
immediate reward at time
t

R_{t}=-s_{t+1}^{\text{SOFA}}

provides a direct and clinically meaningful measure of the patient’s SEPSIS
condition. Moreover, such a reward function has been used in recent literature
(
Wang et al., 2022

e.g.,)
. Throughout this section, the discount factor of the reward is chosen to be
\gamma=0.8

We apply the proposed method P4L with a number of subgroups selected by the proposed heuristic
method, and a Gaussian RBF basis
with bandwidth selected by the median of pairwise Euclidean distances of states.
For the competing methods, we use the same basis. The same settings are used in Section
6
.

Since the outcomes of optimal personalized policies are not available in the dataset, to evaluate the numerical performance
in terms of value, we learn the personalized transitions for all
patients in the preprocessed data set by PerSim

(
Agarwal et al., 2021

, a state-of-the-art method to estimate personalized
simulators. Then we use the estimated simulator and reward function to evaluate
the values of learned policies by the Monte Carlo method.
In the processed dataset, we randomly subsample 1000 ICU admissions, apply the
P4L method and benchmarks, and evaluate the estimated policies, as well as

8
Discussion

In this paper we introduce a novel RL framework for offline policy
optimization using batch data from heterogeneous time-stationary MDPs.
Our approach incorporates individualized latent variables into a shared heterogeneous
model, facilitating efficient estimation of individual Q-functions. Following
this framework, we propose a pessimistic policy learning method admitting local solutions with a fast
average regret rate under within-group partial coverage and the regularity conditions
in Section
4
.

Our theoretical properties on the regret of the proposed method rely on the
partial coverage assumption that the mixture of behavior policies within each
homogeneous subgroup adequately covers its optimal policy. This coverage assumption can
potentially be relaxed by applying Lebesgue decomposition and distribution
robust optimization
(
Chen et al., 2023
)
, which requires
an additional Bellman completeness assumption. Another potential theoretical
improvement is to relax the convexity assumption on the
{\mathcal{Q}}
space for the dual
problem such that the duality gap is asymptotically negligible.
In addition, the regret bound in our approach is not guaranteed to be tight, as our method focuses on the learning rate rather than the lower bound. Compared to the existing literature on offline RL for homogeneous data under similar data coverage assumptions, our theoretical regret rate for homogeneous data matches that of
Xie et al. (2021)
and

Zhan et al. (2022)
.
Further investigation into the theoretical challenges of establishing regret lower bounds for offline RL with heterogeneous data presents an interesting direction for future research.

The proposed policy optimization method can be applied to dynamic decision-making problems where the state-action transitions are stationary but
vary among individuals, and the agents continue making decisions in
their environments with the updated policies. Examples include mobile health for
patients with chronic diseases and robotics working in
different slowly-changing environments. For a new individual not in the
pre-collected data, one needs to obtain more informative state-action transitions and
classify the individual to a subgroup, then apply the policy of that group.
This can be explored in future work.

In this work, we have focused on resolving populational heterogeneity for time-stationary MDPs.
However, it is important to acknowledge that in practice, the assumptions of
time-stationarity may not hold for episodic MDPs.
To address this issue,
Hu et al. (2022)
try to learn on optimal
policies for the last period of the stationary part, but this cannot guarantee long-term
optimality. Future research could involve exploring the policy learning for time-nonstationary
heterogeneous episodic MDPs.
Moreover, it is also worthwhile to explore individual policy optimization when
confronted with unmeasured confoundings and alternative types of rewards, such
as time to event. These aspects remain largely unexplored in the existing
literature for heterogeneous data and present interesting future research directions.

S.1.1
Continuity bounds

We first derive the continuity estimates used in the proofs. All constants
in this subsection depend only on the bounds in Assumption

3
and on
\gamma
. In particular, they do not depend on

N,T
or on the observed data. Set
B=(1-\gamma)^{-1}
and define

\displaystyle L_{\Phi}

\displaystyle=2BL_{f}+C_{{\mathcal{F}}}\{(1+\gamma)L_{Q}+\gamma BL_{\pi}\},

(27)

\displaystyle L_{V}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)(L_{Q}+BL_{\pi}),\qquad L_{J}=BL_{\pi}.

Action-averaged critics.

Fix
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
and
\pi\in\Pi
. For
u\in{\mathcal{U}}
, write

W_{u}(s)=\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\pi(a\mid s;u)Q(s,a;u).

Since the policy probabilities sum to one,
|W_{u}(s)|\leq B
.
For
d=\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2}
, add and subtract

\sum_{a}\pi(a\mid s;u)Q(s,a;u^{\prime})
to obtain

\displaystyle|W_{u}(s)-W_{u^{\prime}}(s)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a}\pi(a\mid s;u)|Q(s,a;u)-Q(s,a;u^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{a}|\pi(a\mid s;u)-\pi(a\mid s;u^{\prime})|\,|Q(s,a;u^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq(L_{Q}+BL_{\pi})d.

The estimate is uniform in
s,Q,\pi
.

Empirical Bellman moments.

For a transition tuple
z=(s,a,r,s_{+})
with
|r|\leq 1
, define

b_{u}(z)=r+\gamma W_{u}(s_{+})-Q(s,a;u),\qquad h_{u}(z)=f(s,a;u)b_{u}(z),\qquad f\in{\mathcal{F}}.

The reward normalization and the definition of
B
give

|b_{u}(z)|\leq 1+(1+\gamma)B=2B.

The preceding bound for
W_{u}
also gives

|b_{u}(z)-b_{u^{\prime}}(z)|\leq\{(1+\gamma)L_{Q}+\gamma BL_{\pi}\}d.

We next add and subtract
f(s,a;u^{\prime})b_{u}(z)
. It follows that

\displaystyle|h_{u}(z)-h_{u^{\prime}}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq|f(s,a;u)-f(s,a;u^{\prime})|\,|b_{u}(z)|

\displaystyle\quad+|f(s,a;u^{\prime})|\,|b_{u}(z)-b_{u^{\prime}}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\Phi}d.

Apply this pointwise inequality to every observed tuple, sum over
i,t
,
and divide by
NT
. For any
{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{N}
,

|\widehat{\Phi}(Q,f,\pi,{\mathbf{u}})-\widehat{\Phi}(Q,f,\pi,{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{L_{\Phi}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|u^{i}-u^{\prime i}\|_{2}.

(28)

Taking expectations gives the same bound for
\Phi
.
For any two bounded families
a_{f},b_{f}
,

|\sup_{f}a_{f}-\sup_{f}b_{f}|\leq\sup_{f}|a_{f}-b_{f}|
.
Consequently (
28
) also holds after
taking the supremum over
f
separately in the two empirical moments.

Initial-state objectives.

The normalized initial-state objective associated with
Q
is

\frac{1-\gamma}{N}\sum_{i}{\mathbb{E}}_{S_{0}\sim\nu}W_{u^{i}}(S_{0}).

The bound for
W_{u}-W_{u^{\prime}}
implies that changing
{\mathbf{u}}
to
{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}

changes this objective by at most

\frac{L_{V}}{N}\sum_{i}\|u^{i}-u^{\prime i}\|_{2}.

For the unnormalized sum used in (
12
), the bound
is
L_{V}\sum_{i}\|u^{i}-u^{\prime i}\|_{2}
. In particular, changing only coordinate

i
by distance
d
changes that sum by at most
L_{V}d
.

Actual policy values.

These bounds on model objectives do not by themselves compare actual
policy values at different latent coordinates. To obtain the latter
comparison, fix one individual MDP and let
V^{\rho}
denote its true
unnormalized state value under a stationary policy
\rho
. Bounded rewards
give
\|V^{\rho}\|_{\infty}\leq B
. For two policies
\rho,\rho^{\prime}
, subtract
their Bellman equations and insert the intermediate term obtained by
using
\rho
with
V^{\rho^{\prime}}
. For every state
s
,

\displaystyle V^{\rho}(s)-V^{\rho^{\prime}}(s)

\displaystyle=\gamma\sum_{a}\rho(a\mid s)\int\{V^{\rho}(s_{+})-V^{\rho^{\prime}}(s_{+})\}{\mathbb{P}}(ds_{+}\mid s,a)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{a}\{\rho(a\mid s)-\rho^{\prime}(a\mid s)\}\left\{r(s,a)+\gamma\int V^{\rho^{\prime}}(s_{+}){\mathbb{P}}(ds_{+}\mid s,a)\right\}.

The expression in the second pair of braces is bounded by

1+\gamma B=B
. If

\eta=\sup_{s}\sum_{a}|\rho(a\mid s)-\rho^{\prime}(a\mid s)|
, then

\|V^{\rho}-V^{\rho^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\|V^{\rho}-V^{\rho^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}+B\eta.

Rearranging and multiplying by
1-\gamma
gives

|J^{i}(\rho)-J^{i}(\rho^{\prime})|\leq(1-\gamma)\|V^{\rho}-V^{\rho^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}\leq B\eta.

Take
\rho=\pi(\bullet;u)
and
\rho^{\prime}=\pi(\bullet;u^{\prime})
.
Assumption
3
(c) implies
\eta\leq L_{\pi}\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2}
.
Thus, for every individual
i
,

|J^{i}\{\pi(\bullet;u)\}-J^{i}\{\pi(\bullet;u^{\prime})\}|\leq L_{J}\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2}.

(29)

Averaging over individuals proves

|J(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})-J(\pi,{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime})|\leq L_{J}N^{-1}\sum_{i}\|u^{i}-u^{\prime i}\|_{2}
.
This comparison always uses the same actual MDP on both sides for a given
individual. It does not assume realizability at the changed coordinate.

Proof of Lemma
1

Proof.

Fix an individual
i
, a policy
\pi^{i}
, and a bounded measurable
candidate
\widetilde{Q}^{i}
. Put
M=\|\widetilde{Q}^{i}\|_{\infty}
and define

\widetilde{V}^{i}(s)=\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\pi^{i}(a\mid s)\widetilde{Q}^{i}(s,a).

Then
\|\widetilde{V}^{i}\|_{\infty}\leq M
. All expectations in this proof
refer to a trajectory of the actual MDP of individual
i
, initialized
from
\nu
and following
\pi^{i}
.

For a nonnegative integer
m
, consider the finite weighted sum

D_{m}=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{m}\gamma^{t}{\mathbb{E}}^{\pi^{i}}\{R_{t}^{i}+\gamma\widetilde{V}^{i}(S_{t+1}^{i})-\widetilde{Q}^{i}(S_{t}^{i},A_{t}^{i})\}.

Conditioning on
S_{t}^{i}
and using the policy to average over
A_{t}^{i}

gives
{\mathbb{E}}^{\pi^{i}}\widetilde{Q}^{i}(S_{t}^{i},A_{t}^{i})={\mathbb{E}}^{\pi^{i}}\widetilde{V}^{i}(S_{t}^{i})
. Hence the finite sums telescope exactly:

\displaystyle D_{m}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{m}\gamma^{t}{\mathbb{E}}^{\pi^{i}}R_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle\quad+(1-\gamma)\left\{\sum_{t=1}^{m+1}\gamma^{t}{\mathbb{E}}^{\pi^{i}}\widetilde{V}^{i}(S_{t}^{i})-\sum_{t=0}^{m}\gamma^{t}{\mathbb{E}}^{\pi^{i}}\widetilde{V}^{i}(S_{t}^{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{m}\gamma^{t}{\mathbb{E}}^{\pi^{i}}R_{t}^{i}-(1-\gamma){\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}\widetilde{V}^{i}(S_{0}^{i})

\displaystyle\quad+(1-\gamma)\gamma^{m+1}{\mathbb{E}}^{\pi^{i}}\widetilde{V}^{i}(S_{m+1}^{i}).

The absolute value of the last term is at most

(1-\gamma)\gamma^{m+1}M
, which tends to zero. Bounded rewards imply
absolute convergence of the reward series. The definition of the true
Q-function and the initial action distribution give

(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}{\mathbb{E}}^{\pi^{i}}R_{t}^{i}=(1-\gamma){\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}Q_{\pi^{i}}^{i}(S_{0}^{i},\pi^{i}(S_{0}^{i}))=J^{i}(\pi^{i}).

The other initial-state term is
\widetilde{J}^{i}(\pi^{i})
. Thus

\lim_{m\to\infty}D_{m}=J^{i}(\pi^{i})-\widetilde{J}^{i}(\pi^{i})
.

It remains to identify this limit with the stated visitation expectation.
Define the bounded conditional residual

b^{i}(s,a)=r^{i}(s,a)+\gamma\int\widetilde{V}^{i}(s_{+}){\mathbb{P}}^{i}(ds_{+}\mid s,a)-\widetilde{Q}^{i}(s,a).

It satisfies
\|b^{i}\|_{\infty}\leq 1+(1+\gamma)M
.
The conditional reward and transition assumptions imply

D_{m}=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{m}\gamma^{t}\int b^{i}(s,a)\,p_{\pi^{i},t}^{i}(ds,da).

Because the sum of the weights is finite and
b^{i}
is bounded, the
integral can be interchanged with the infinite series. By the definition
of
d_{\pi^{i}}^{i}
, the limit is

\int b^{i}(s,a)\,d_{\pi^{i}}^{i}(ds,da)
.
Expanding
b^{i}
as the conditional expectation of the sampled residual
proves (
6
). This calculation also applies when

\gamma=0
and does not require visitation measures to possess densities.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

Define the normalized initial-state objective and its pessimistic value by

The objective in (

N\widehat{V}(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})

Multiplication by the positive constant

the preceding proof gives

Thus there is no additional factor of

By Assumption

\displaystyle\int\bar{b}_{Q}^{k}\,d_{*}^{k}

\displaystyle=\int f_{*}(s,a;v_{*}^{k})\bar{b}_{Q}^{k}(s,a)\,\bar{d}^{k}(ds,da)

are common within group

. The different behavior policies enter only
through
\bar{d}^{k}
. Multiplying by

and summing now yields

\displaystyle J(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})-V(Q,\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}p_{k}\{J^{k}(\pi_{*}^{k})-V_{Q}^{k}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}p_{k}\Psi_{k}(Q,f_{*},\pi_{*},v_{*}^{k})=\Phi(Q,f_{*},\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*}).

Using one simultaneous witness is what permits the last expression to be
controlled by the single supremum over
{\mathcal{F}}
in the uncertainty set.

If

, the uniform deviation bound and
feasibility give, respectively,

\displaystyle J(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})-V(Q,\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\Phi}(Q,f_{*},\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})+\alpha/4

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\widehat{\Phi}(Q,f,\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})+\alpha/4\leq 5\alpha/4.

V(Q,\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})
over that set therefore gives

\widehat{V}(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})\geq J(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})-5\alpha/4
.
Combining pessimism for the estimated pair, optimality of that pair,
and the comparator bound, in that order, gives

J(\widehat{\pi}_{\circ},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{\circ})\geq\widehat{V}(\widehat{\pi}_{\circ},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{\circ})\geq\widehat{V}(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})\geq J(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})-5\alpha/4.

This proves the claimed regret bound on the same event, with no further
probability loss.

For completeness, the inner minimum is well defined and attained.
For fixed
(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})
, the function

Q\mapsto\sup_{f}\widehat{\Phi}(Q,f,\pi,{\mathbf{u}})
is Lipschitz with constant

C_{{\mathcal{F}}}(1+\gamma)
in the uniform norm, by the residual calculation in
the preceding proof. Its sublevel set
\Omega
is therefore closed in
the compact class
{\mathcal{Q}}
. It is nonempty on the oracle domain by
feasibility. The continuous objective
V(Q,\pi,{\mathbf{u}})
attains its minimum
on this compact set.
For a nonconvex
{\mathcal{Q}}
, the theorem takes an outer maximizer as given.
If only an
\eta
-optimal pair for the normalized objective is used,
replace the middle inequality in the last display by

\widehat{V}(\widehat{\pi}_{\circ},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}_{\circ})\geq\widehat{V}(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})-\eta

The resulting regret bound is
5\alpha/4+\eta
.
Such approximate maximizers exist for every
\eta>0
because the
nonempty oracle domain has a bounded profiled objective.
When
{\mathcal{Q}}
is convex, strict feasibility and the mixture argument in the
next proof also give attainment of the outer maximum on the compact
oracle domain.
∎

The maximizer can be selected measurably. To see the measurability of

. On

E_{NT}

Convexity gives

(39)

\displaystyle J(\pi_{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{*})-J(\widehat{\pi},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}})

Thus the absolute regret is at most

with

solution exists with probability tending to one, and its absolute regret

.
On the exceptional event the selected parameters are the fixed

\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}_{\mathrm{p}}(\alpha)

S.1.7
Proof of Theorem

Proof.

Work on the event and tube
\mathcal{T}_{r}
constructed in the proof of
Theorem
3
. For a fixed triple

\displaystyle q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}(\lambda)

Step 1: The perturbation value and a supporting slope.

when the feasible set is empty.
Continuity and compactness give
m=\min_{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}}g(Q)
.
The effective domain of

[m,\infty)

-N\leq h(t)\leq N
and the infimum is attained.
Strict feasibility in (
38

. In particular,
h
is finite on a neighborhood
of zero and
h(0)=V(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})
.

If
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
, every function feasible at
t_{1}
is feasible at

t_{2}

h(t_{2})\leq h(t_{1})

For
0\leq\theta\leq 1

Q_{\theta}=(1-\theta)Q_{1}+\theta Q_{2}

{\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle g(Q_{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\theta)g(Q_{1})+\theta g(Q_{2})\leq(1-\theta)t_{1}+\theta t_{2},

\displaystyle h((1-\theta)t_{1}+\theta t_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\ell(Q_{\theta},\pi,{\mathbf{u}})=(1-\theta)h(t_{1})+\theta h(t_{2}).

If either
h(t_{j})=+\infty

is automatic for
0<\theta<1

is convex and nonincreasing.

We construct a finite supporting slope explicitly. Put
a=\alpha/4

For

The set in this supremum is nonempty, since

Taking

Thus

is finite and nonpositive. For

At

. We have therefore proved

Step 2: Strong duality and multiplier attainment.

Every
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
is feasible for the perturbation problem at

.
Consequently the supporting inequality gives

\ell(Q,\pi,{\mathbf{u}})\geq h(g(Q))\geq h(0)+sg(Q).

With
\lambda_{0}=-s\geq 0
, this becomes

\ell(Q,\pi,{\mathbf{u}})+\lambda_{0}g(Q)\geq h(0)
for every
Q
.
Taking the minimum over
Q
proves

q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}(\lambda_{0})\geq h(0)
.
For the reverse inequality, let
Q^{*}
minimize the feasible primal
problem. For any
\lambda\geq 0
, feasibility gives
g(Q^{*})\leq 0
,
so

q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}(\lambda)\leq\ell(Q^{*},\pi,{\mathbf{u}})+\lambda g(Q^{*})\leq\ell(Q^{*},\pi,{\mathbf{u}})=h(0).

This is weak duality. At
\lambda_{0}
the two inequalities coincide,
and hence

D(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})=V(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})\quad\text{for every }(\pi,{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\in\mathcal{T}_{r}.

(41)

The construction establishes strong duality relative to the convex
domain
{\mathcal{Q}}
. It requires no interior of
{\mathcal{Q}}
in the ambient function
space.

We also give a quantitative bound for attaining multipliers.
Using the strictly feasible
Q^{0}=Q_{\pi,{\mathbf{v}}}
and

|\ell(Q^{0},\pi,{\mathbf{u}})|\leq N
gives

q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}(\lambda)\leq N-\lambda\alpha/2,\qquad q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}(0)\geq-N.

For
\lambda>4N/\alpha
, the first bound is strictly less than
-N
,
so such a multiplier cannot maximize
q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}
.
For completeness,
q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}
is upper semicontinuous: if

\lambda_{n}\to\lambda
, choose a minimizing function
Q_{\lambda}

at
\lambda
and use

\varlimsup_{n}q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}(\lambda_{n})\leq\lim_{n}\{\ell(Q_{\lambda},\pi,{\mathbf{u}})+\lambda_{n}g(Q_{\lambda})\}=q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}(\lambda).

A maximizing sequence on
[0,4N/\alpha]
has a convergent subsequence,
and upper semicontinuity makes its limit a maximizer.
Thus the dual maximum is attained in
[0,4N/\alpha]
.
This interval may grow with the sample size. Its bound is used only
for attainment and is never multiplied by a concentration error.

The attaining multiplier may also be selected measurably. For the
countable dense subset
\{Q_{j}:j\geq 1\}
used above, continuity in
Q

gives

q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}(\lambda)=\inf_{j\geq 1}\{\ell(Q_{j},\pi,{\mathbf{u}})+\lambda[\widehat{c}(Q_{j},\pi,{\mathbf{u}})-\alpha]\}.

Moreover,
|\widehat{c}(Q,\pi,{\mathbf{u}})|\leq 2BC_{{\mathcal{F}}}
implies

|q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}(\lambda)-q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}(\lambda^{\prime})|\leq(2BC_{{\mathcal{F}}}+\alpha)|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}|.

After the primal triple has been selected measurably, the countable
infimum is measurable in the data and this bound gives continuity in

\lambda
. The measurable maximum theorem on the compact interval

[0,4N/\alpha]
therefore supplies a measurable attaining multiplier.

Step 3: Lifting the local primal maximizer.

Choose the local maximizer
(\widehat{\pi},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}},\widehat{{\mathbf{v}}})
constructed in
Theorem
3
and an attaining multiplier

\widehat{\lambda}\in[0,4N/\alpha]
. That theorem supplies a relative open
neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of the triple contained in
\mathcal{T}_{r}

on which
H(\pi,{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\leq H(\widehat{\pi},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}},\widehat{{\mathbf{v}}})
.
For every
(\pi,{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})\in\mathcal{U}
and every
\lambda\geq 0
,

\displaystyle q_{\pi,{\mathbf{u}}}(\lambda)-{\mathscr{P}}_{\mu}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle\leq D(\pi,{\mathbf{u}})-{\mathscr{P}}_{\mu}({\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle=H(\pi,{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle\leq H(\widehat{\pi},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}},\widehat{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle=q_{\widehat{\pi},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}}(\widehat{\lambda})-{\mathscr{P}}_{\mu}(\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}},\widehat{{\mathbf{v}}}).

The first inequality is the definition of
D
, the first equality
uses (
41
), and the last equality also uses
attainment at
\widehat{\lambda}
. In particular, the inequality holds on

\mathcal{U}\times\{\lambda\geq 0:|\lambda-\widehat{\lambda}|<1\}
,
a relative neighborhood of the quadruple. It is therefore a local
maximizer of (
18
).
Set
(\widehat{\pi}^{\dagger},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}}^{\dagger},\widehat{{\mathbf{v}}}^{\dagger},\widehat{\lambda}^{\dagger})=(\widehat{\pi},\widehat{{\mathbf{u}}},\widehat{{\mathbf{v}}},\widehat{\lambda})
.
Its policy and latent variables equal those of the constructed primal
solution. Their regret is consequently bounded by
C_{1}\alpha
in
absolute value on
E_{NT}
for all sufficiently large sample sizes.
Since
\Pr(E_{NT}^{c})\to 0
, this proves the stated existence probability
and
\mathop{}\mathopen{}\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}_{\mathrm{p}}(\alpha)
regret bound.
∎

S.2
Auxiliary Results and Proofs

Lemma 2
.

Under the linear-MDP conditions of Example
1
, including
bounded measurable features, finite signed component measures, and full feature span,
for every policy
\pi
there is a unique vector
w_{\pi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

Q_{\pi}(s,a)={\bm{\psi}}(s,a)^{\top}w_{\pi}.

Writing

M_{\pi}=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\bm{\mu}}(ds_{+})\sum_{a\in{\mathcal{A}}}\pi(a\mid s_{+}){\bm{\psi}}(s_{+},a)^{\top},

the matrix
I_{d}-\gamma M_{\pi}
is invertible and

w_{\pi}=(I_{d}-\gamma M_{\pi})^{-1}{\bm{\theta}}
.

Proof.

We suppress the individual index. Write

V_{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a}\pi(a\mid s)Q_{\pi}(s,a)
for the true state value.
Since the transition kernel is a probability kernel and rewards are
bounded,
\|V_{\pi}\|_{\infty}\leq B
and
\|Q_{\pi}\|_{\infty}\leq B
.
For each signed component measure
{\bm{\mu}}_{j}
, finite total variation gives

\left|\int V_{\pi}(s_{+}){\bm{\mu}}_{j}(ds_{+})\right|\leq B\,|{\bm{\mu}}_{j}|({\mathcal{S}})<\infty.

The Bellman equation and the linear expressions for rewards and
transitions therefore imply

\displaystyle Q_{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle={\bm{\psi}}(s,a)^{\top}{\bm{\theta}}+\gamma\int V_{\pi}(s_{+}){\bm{\psi}}(s,a)^{\top}{\bm{\mu}}(ds_{+})

\displaystyle={\bm{\psi}}(s,a)^{\top}\left\{{\bm{\theta}}+\gamma\int V_{\pi}(s_{+}){\bm{\mu}}(ds_{+})\right\}.

Define the vector in braces to be
w_{\pi}
. This proves existence of a
linear representation without assuming a matrix inverse in advance.
Averaging the representation over the action distribution gives

V_{\pi}(s_{+})=\left\{\sum_{a}\pi(a\mid s_{+}){\bm{\psi}}(s_{+},a)^{\top}\right\}w_{\pi}.

All terms are integrable against the signed component measures because

{\bm{\psi}}
is bounded. Substitution into the definition of
w_{\pi}
yields

w_{\pi}={\bm{\theta}}+\gamma M_{\pi}w_{\pi}.

(42)

To prove invertibility, define

\|x\|_{\psi}=\sup_{s,a}|{\bm{\psi}}(s,a)^{\top}x|
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Bounded features make this quantity finite. Homogeneity and the triangle
inequality follow from the same properties of the absolute value. If

\|x\|_{\psi}=0
, then
x
is orthogonal to every feature vector.
The full-span assumption gives
x=0
, so this is a norm.
More explicitly, choose
d
linearly independent feature vectors and
form the invertible matrix
F
with these vectors as rows. Then

\|x\|_{2}\leq\|F^{-1}\|_{2}\|Fx\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}\,\|F^{-1}\|_{2}\|x\|_{\psi},\qquad\|x\|_{\psi}\leq\sup_{s,a}\|{\bm{\psi}}(s,a)\|_{2}\|x\|_{2}.

Thus
\|\bullet\|_{\psi}
is equivalent to the Euclidean norm and makes

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
a complete normed space.

For any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, the transition representation gives

\displaystyle|{\bm{\psi}}(s,a)^{\top}M_{\pi}x|

\displaystyle=\left|\int{\mathbb{P}}(ds_{+}\mid s,a)\sum_{a_{+}}\pi(a_{+}\mid s_{+}){\bm{\psi}}(s_{+},a_{+})^{\top}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int{\mathbb{P}}(ds_{+}\mid s,a)\sum_{a_{+}}\pi(a_{+}\mid s_{+})|{\bm{\psi}}(s_{+},a_{+})^{\top}x|

\displaystyle\leq\|x\|_{\psi}.

Both the transition measure and the policy weights are nonnegative and
have total mass one. Taking the supremum over
(s,a)
proves

\|M_{\pi}x\|_{\psi}\leq\|x\|_{\psi}
. In the induced matrix norm this is

\|M_{\pi}\|_{\psi}\leq 1
and hence

\|M_{\pi}^{t}\|_{\psi}\leq 1
for every integer
t\geq 0
.

Let
S_{m}=\sum_{t=0}^{m}\gamma^{t}M_{\pi}^{t}
. If
n>m
, then

\|S_{n}-S_{m}\|_{\psi}\leq\sum_{t=m+1}^{n}\gamma^{t}\leq\frac{\gamma^{m+1}}{1-\gamma}.

Thus
S_{m}
converges in matrix norm to a matrix
S
. Multiplication of the
finite geometric sum, on either side, gives

(I_{d}-\gamma M_{\pi})S_{m}=S_{m}(I_{d}-\gamma M_{\pi})=I_{d}-\gamma^{m+1}M_{\pi}^{m+1}.

The norm of the last remainder is at most
\gamma^{m+1}
.
Passing to the limit proves

(I_{d}-\gamma M_{\pi})S=S(I_{d}-\gamma M_{\pi})=I_{d}
.
Therefore
I_{d}-\gamma M_{\pi}
is invertible, with inverse
S
.
Applying this inverse to (
42
)
proves the displayed formula for
w_{\pi}
.
Finally, if two vectors represent the same Q-function, their difference
has zero
\|\bullet\|_{\psi}
norm and hence is zero. This proves uniqueness.
∎

Assumption 5
.

For each
i\in[N]
, the process
\{X_{t}^{i}\}_{t\geq 0}
takes values in a
standard Borel space
{\mathcal{X}}
, is stationary, and has absolute regularity
coefficients satisfying
\beta_{i}(j)\leq\beta_{0}\exp(-\zeta j)
for all
integers
j\geq 1
, with common constants
\beta_{0}\geq 0
and
\zeta>0
.
The
N
trajectories are independent. Their marginal distributions may
differ. We use the convention

\beta_{i}(j)=\sup_{q\geq 0}\beta\bigl(\sigma(X_{0}^{i},\ldots,X_{q}^{i}),\sigma(X_{q+j}^{i},X_{q+j+1}^{i},\ldots)\bigr).

For a standard Borel random element
Y
and a sigma field
\mathcal{A}
,
the normalization of
\beta
is

\beta(\mathcal{A},\sigma(Y))={\mathbb{E}}\left\|\mathcal{L}(Y\mid\mathcal{A})-\mathcal{L}(Y)\right\|_{\mathrm{TV}},\qquad\|P-Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\sup_{E}|P(E)-Q(E)|.

Lemma 3

(Generalized Berbee’s Coupling Lemma)
.

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
be random elements in standard Borel spaces. On an
extension of the probability space there exist independent random elements

\widetilde{Y}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{Y}_{m}
, with

\widetilde{Y}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Y_{j}
, such that

\Pr(\widetilde{Y}_{j}\neq Y_{j})\leq\beta\bigl(\sigma(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{j-1}),\sigma(Y_{j})\bigr),\qquad j=2,\ldots,m,

and
\widetilde{Y}_{1}=Y_{1}
. Here
\beta(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
denotes
the absolute regularity coefficient between the two sigma fields.
Independent input sequences may be coupled using independent auxiliary
randomness.

Proof.

We first describe the conditional coupling used at each step. Let
P
be
an observed past, let
K_{p}
be a regular conditional law of a random
element
Y
given
P=p
, and let
\nu=\mathcal{L}(Y)
. Such conditional
laws exist because the spaces are standard Borel. With respect to

K_{p}+\nu
, choose jointly measurable densities
k_{p}
and
v_{p}
of
K_{p}

and
\nu
. Define the common subprobability measure and its mass by

\kappa_{p}(dy)=\min\{k_{p}(y),v_{p}(y)\}(K_{p}+\nu)(dy),\qquad c_{p}=\kappa_{p}({\mathcal{Y}}).

Thus
1-c_{p}=\|K_{p}-\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
. Introduce two independent
uniform random variables, independent of all the original variables.
Conditional on
(P,Y)=(p,y)
, retain
y
with probability

h_{p}(y)=\frac{\min\{k_{p}(y),v_{p}(y)\}}{k_{p}(y)}\quad\text{when }k_{p}(y)>0.

The value when
k_{p}(y)=0
is immaterial and can be set to zero. Use the
first uniform variable to make this decision. Upon retention, set

\widetilde{Y}=y
. Upon rejection, use the second uniform variable to
sample from
(\nu-\kappa_{p})/(1-c_{p})
. A measurable sampler exists for a
probability kernel on a standard Borel space. If
c_{p}=1
, rejection has
probability zero, so the residual sampler can be defined arbitrarily.
For every measurable set
E
, the conditional law of the output satisfies

\Pr(\widetilde{Y}\in E\mid P=p)=\kappa_{p}(E)+(1-c_{p})\frac{\nu(E)-\kappa_{p}(E)}{1-c_{p}}=\nu(E),

where the case
c_{p}=1
is read directly as
\kappa_{p}(E)=\nu(E)
.
Also, a mismatch requires rejection, so

\Pr(\widetilde{Y}\neq Y\mid P=p)\leq 1-c_{p}=\|K_{p}-\nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.

To apply this construction successively, put
\widetilde{Y}_{1}=Y_{1}
and
adjoin independent uniform pairs
U_{2},\ldots,U_{m}
that are jointly
independent of the entire original vector
(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m})
. At step

j
, take
P=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{j-1})
and use
U_{j}
in the construction
above. Write

\mathcal{H}_{j-1}=\sigma(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{j-1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{j-1}).

Independence of the raw auxiliary variables from the entire original
vector implies

\mathcal{L}(Y_{j}\mid\mathcal{H}_{j-1})=\mathcal{L}(Y_{j}\mid Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{j-1}).

The construction consequently gives

\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{Y}_{j}\mid\mathcal{H}_{j-1})=\mathcal{L}(Y_{j})
.
Each earlier copy is
\mathcal{H}_{j-1}
-measurable. Hence

\widetilde{Y}_{j}
is independent of all earlier copies. Induction proves
mutual independence and the required marginal laws. Integrating the
conditional mismatch bound gives the stated
\beta
bound for every

j
. Finally, copies for an input sequence are measurable functions of
that sequence and its auxiliary uniforms. Independent sequences with
independent auxiliary uniforms therefore produce independent collections
of copies.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Suppose Assumption
5
holds,
N,T\geq 1
,
and
{\mathcal{F}}
is a class of measurable functions
f:{\mathcal{X}}\to[-B,B]
, where

B>0
. Suppose that, for some
A\geq 2
and
D\geq 1
,

N(\epsilon B,{\mathcal{F}},\|\bullet\|_{\infty})\leq(A/\epsilon)^{D},\qquad 0<\epsilon\leq 1.

For
0<\delta<1
, define

a=a(N,T,\delta):=\min\left\{T,\max\left\{1,\left\lceil\zeta^{-1}\log\frac{8(\beta_{0}\vee 1)NT}{\delta}\right\rceil\right\}\right\}.

There is a universal constant
C
such that, with probability at least

1-\delta
,

\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}\left|\frac{1}{NT}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f(X_{t}^{i})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}f(X_{0}^{i})\right|\leq CB\sqrt{\frac{a}{NT}\left\{D\log(2A)+\log\frac{8}{\delta}\right\}}.

(43)

In particular, if
NT\geq 2
and
(NT)^{-2}\leq\delta<1
, this is at most

C_{A,\beta_{0}}B\sqrt{\frac{(1+\zeta^{-1})D}{NT}\log(eNT)\log\frac{8}{\delta}}.

The first bound applies to every trajectory length
T
, including lengths
shorter than the uncapped logarithmic block length. No lower bound on
T

is needed.

Proof.

Write
n=NT
and
q=\lceil T/a\rceil
. The blocks in each trajectory are
indexed by
j\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}
, with time-index sets and lengths

I_{j}=\{ja,ja+1,\ldots,\min\{(j+1)a,T\}-1\},\qquad\ell_{j}=|I_{j}|=\min\{a,T-ja\}.

In particular, the final block has length
T-(q-1)a\in\{1,\ldots,a\}
,
so no observations are discarded. For
r\in\{0,1\}
, set

\mathcal{J}_{r}=\{j\in\{0,\ldots,q-1\}:j\equiv r\pmod{2}\}
.
For each trajectory
i
, apply Lemma
3
to the ordered
sequence of block vectors
(X_{t}^{i})_{t\in I_{j}}
with
j\in\mathcal{J}_{r}
.
Denote the resulting copies by
(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{i,r})_{t\in I_{j}}
.
For a block
j
with a predecessor of the same parity, all previous
blocks of that parity end by time
(j-1)a-1
, whereas block
j
begins
at time
ja
. Monotonicity of
\beta
under restriction of sigma fields
therefore bounds its coupling error by

\beta_{i}(a+1)\leq\beta_{0}e^{-\zeta(a+1)}\leq\beta_{0}e^{-\zeta a}.

The first block of each parity is copied exactly. Use independent
auxiliary randomness across trajectories. For each fixed parity, all
copied blocks are then mutually independent, including across
trajectories. Independence between the two parities is not required.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the event that every copied block agrees with its
original block. If
a<T
, the definition of
a
gives

a\geq\zeta^{-1}\log\{8(\beta_{0}\vee 1)n/\delta\}
. There are at most

Nq\leq NT=n
blocks in both parities combined, so the union bound gives

\Pr(\mathcal{E}^{c})\leq Nq\beta_{0}e^{-\zeta a}\leq n\beta_{0}\frac{\delta}{8(\beta_{0}\vee 1)n}\leq\delta/8.

If
a=T
, there is only one block per trajectory. These original blocks
are independent, their copies can be taken equal to them, and

\Pr(\mathcal{E}^{c})=0
.

Fix a parity
r
and define

\mathcal{B}_{r}=\{(i,j):i\in[N],\ j\in\mathcal{J}_{r}\},\qquad S_{r}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{B}_{r}}\left\{\sum_{t\in I_{j}}f(\widetilde{X}_{t}^{i,r})-{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{t\in I_{j}}f(X_{t}^{i})\right\}.

An empty parity contributes zero throughout. The block lengths satisfy

\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{B}_{r}}\ell_{j}^{2}\leq a\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{B}_{r}}\ell_{j}\leq an.

For
d=\|f-g\|_{\infty}
, the uncentered block sum of
f-g
lies in

[-\ell_{j}d,\ell_{j}d]
. Hoeffding’s exponential-moment bound for a
centered random variable whose range has length
L
is

{\mathbb{E}}e^{\lambda Z}\leq e^{\lambda^{2}L^{2}/8}
. One direct verification is
to set
\psi(\lambda)=\log{\mathbb{E}}e^{\lambda Z}
. Its second derivative is
the variance of
Z
under the probability measure with density

e^{\lambda Z}/{\mathbb{E}}e^{\lambda Z}
. That measure is supported on the
same interval of length
L
, so its variance is at most
L^{2}/4
. Indeed,
the mean minimizes squared distance over constants, and squared distance
from the midpoint of this interval is at most
L^{2}/4
. Since

\psi(0)=\psi^{\prime}(0)=0
, integration of the second-derivative bound gives

\psi(\lambda)\leq\lambda^{2}L^{2}/8
for either sign of
\lambda
.
Applying this bound to the independent blocks gives, for every real

\lambda
,

{\mathbb{E}}e^{\lambda\{S_{r}(f)-S_{r}(g)\}}\leq\exp\left\{\frac{\lambda^{2}d^{2}}{2n^{2}}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{B}_{r}}\ell_{j}^{2}\right\}\leq\exp\left\{\frac{\lambda^{2}ad^{2}}{2n}\right\}.

Markov’s inequality with
\lambda=nx/(ad^{2})
and then the same argument
for the negative increment prove, for
d>0
,

\Pr\{|S_{r}(f)-S_{r}(g)|>x\}\leq 2\exp\left\{-\frac{nx^{2}}{2ad^{2}}\right\}.

(44)

When
d=0
, the increment is zero. The same exponential-moment and tail
bounds hold for
S_{r}(f)
itself with
d
replaced by
B
.

We now give the finite-net argument, including the bound on each maximum.
Suppose
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{M}
satisfy

{\mathbb{E}}e^{\lambda Z_{k}}\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2}
for all real
\lambda
.
For
\lambda>0
, without any independence requirement, we have

\exp\{\lambda{\mathbb{E}}\max_{k\leq M}|Z_{k}|\}\leq{\mathbb{E}}e^{\lambda\max_{k\leq M}|Z_{k}|}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{M}{\mathbb{E}}(e^{\lambda Z_{k}}+e^{-\lambda Z_{k}})\leq 2Me^{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2}.

Taking logarithms and choosing

\lambda=\sqrt{2\log(2M)}/\sigma
gives

{\mathbb{E}}\max_{k\leq M}|Z_{k}|\leq\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2M)}.

The case
\sigma=0
follows directly because all the variables then
vanish almost surely.

Take finite
B2^{-j}
-nets
\mathcal{F}_{j}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, for
j\geq 0
,
with
|\mathcal{F}_{j}|\leq(A2^{j})^{D}
. Covering numbers here use centers in
the class. For each
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
, select
f_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{j}
with

\|f-f_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq B2^{-j}
. Then

\|f_{j}-f_{j-1}\|_{\infty}\leq 3B2^{-j},\qquad j\geq 1.

At level
j\geq 1
, only pairs in the finite set

\mathcal{P}_{j}=\{(u,v)\in\mathcal{F}_{j}\times\mathcal{F}_{j-1}:\|u-v\|_{\infty}\leq 3B2^{-j}\}

are needed. Let
M_{j}=|\mathcal{P}_{j}|
and set
L=\log(2A)
. The covering
bounds and
D\geq 1
imply

\log(2|\mathcal{F}_{0}|)\leq DL,\qquad\log(2M_{j})\leq 2D(L+j)\quad\text{when }M_{j}\geq 1.

The maximum over an empty set of pairs is taken to be zero. The
exponential-moment bounds above give

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\max_{u\in\mathcal{F}_{0}}|S_{r}(u)|

\displaystyle\leq B\sqrt{\frac{2aDL}{n}},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\max_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{P}_{j}}|S_{r}(u)-S_{r}(v)|

\displaystyle\leq 6B2^{-j}\sqrt{\frac{aD(L+j)}{n}}.

To justify telescoping without a measurability assumption on an
uncountable index set, observe the deterministic continuity bound

|S_{r}(f)-S_{r}(g)|\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{B}_{r}}\ell_{j}\|f-g\|_{\infty}\leq 2\|f-g\|_{\infty}.

Thus
S_{r}(f_{j})\to S_{r}(f)
uniformly in
f
, and

Z_{r}:=\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}}|S_{r}(f)|=\sup_{f\in\bigcup_{j\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{j}}|S_{r}(f)|

is a measurable random variable, as a supremum of a countable family.
For each positive integer
J
, telescoping gives

Z_{r}\leq\max_{u\in\mathcal{F}_{0}}|S_{r}(u)|+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\max_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{P}_{j}}|S_{r}(u)-S_{r}(v)|+2B2^{-J}.

Letting
J\to\infty
and using monotone convergence for the nonnegative
series on the right proves

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}Z_{r}

\displaystyle\leq B\sqrt{\frac{aD}{n}}\left\{\sqrt{2L}+6\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}\sqrt{L+j}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 20B\sqrt{\frac{aDL}{n}}.

For the last inequality, use
L\geq\log 4>1
,

\sqrt{L+j}\leq\sqrt{L}+j
,

\sum_{j\geq 1}2^{-j}=1
, and
\sum_{j\geq 1}j2^{-j}=2
.

It remains to convert this expectation bound into a probability bound.
Replacing a copied block of length
\ell_{j}
changes
S_{r}(f)
by at most

2B\ell_{j}/n
for every
f
. The same bound holds for
Z_{r}
, because

|\sup_{f}|x_{f}|-\sup_{f}|y_{f}||\leq\sup_{f}|x_{f}-y_{f}|
. Thus the squared
bounded-difference constants sum to at most

\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{B}_{r}}(2B\ell_{j}/n)^{2}\leq 4B^{2}a/n.

For completeness, revealing the independent blocks one at a time gives
a Doob martingale for
Z_{r}
. Each centered martingale increment has
conditional range of length at most the corresponding constant

2B\ell_{j}/n
. Conditional application of Hoeffding’s exponential-moment
bound and iteration give

{\mathbb{E}}e^{\lambda(Z_{r}-{\mathbb{E}}Z_{r})}\leq e^{\lambda^{2}B^{2}a/(2n)}.

Markov’s inequality, optimized over
\lambda>0
, consequently yields

\Pr\{Z_{r}-{\mathbb{E}}Z_{r}>t\}\leq\exp\{-nt^{2}/(2B^{2}a)\}.

Set
t=B\sqrt{2a\log(4/\delta)/n}
. With probability at least

1-\delta/4
, the bound for this parity is therefore

Z_{r}\leq 20B\sqrt{\frac{aDL}{n}}+B\sqrt{\frac{2a}{n}\log\frac{4}{\delta}}.

On
\mathcal{E}
, summing the two centered parity processes gives the
original centered empirical process. Stationarity supplies the equality

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbb{E}}f(X_{t}^{i})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathbb{E}}f(X_{0}^{i}).

The two parity bounds and the coupling event hold simultaneously with
probability at least

1-\delta/4-\delta/4-\delta/8=1-5\delta/8\geq 1-\delta
.
On that event, the desired supremum is at most

Z_{0}+Z_{1}\leq 40B\sqrt{\frac{aDL}{n}}+2B\sqrt{\frac{2a}{n}\log\frac{4}{\delta}}\leq 44B\sqrt{\frac{a}{n}\left\{DL+\log\frac{8}{\delta}\right\}}.

This proves (
43
) with, for example,
C=44
.

Finally, suppose
n\geq 2
and
\delta\geq n^{-2}
. Then

\log\frac{8(\beta_{0}\vee 1)n}{\delta}\leq\log\{8(\beta_{0}\vee 1)\}+3\log n.

The ceiling and truncation defining
a
imply, with

c_{\beta_{0}}=4+\log\{8(\beta_{0}\vee 1)\}
,

a\leq 1+\zeta^{-1}\log\frac{8(\beta_{0}\vee 1)n}{\delta}\leq c_{\beta_{0}}(1+\zeta^{-1})\log(en).

Also
\log(8/\delta)\geq\log 8
and
D\geq 1
, so

D\log(2A)+\log(8/\delta)\leq\left\{1+\frac{\log(2A)}{\log 8}\right\}D\log(8/\delta).

Substitution into (
43
) proves the last claim
with

C_{A,\beta_{0}}=44\sqrt{c_{\beta_{0}}\{1+\log(2A)/\log 8\}}
.
∎

S.3
More Details in Numerical Studies

In the empirical study, we use neural networks to approximate the function classes
{\mathcal{Q}}
, ratio class
{\mathcal{F}}
and policy class
\Pi
. The structure of the neural network is shown in Figure
4
. To represent heterogeneity, an intercept-free linear layer
U=[u^{1}|\dots|u^{i}|\dots|u^{N}]
encodes subject
i
’s one-hot encoder
e_{i}=[0,\dots,1,\dots,0]^{\top}
to latent vector
u^{i}
, so that
Ue_{i}=u^{i}
. Then the Feature Encoder (a 1-layer net) takes the concatenated inputs of
u^{i}
and state
s
as the full state, and outputs an encoded feature vector. Here,
{\mathcal{F}}
-net and
{\mathcal{Q}}
-net are two separate neural networks that take the action and encoded feature vector as input and output the visiting probability ratio and Q-function, respectively. The policy network
\Pi
-net takes the encoded feature vector as input and outputs the probability mass function for all actions
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
with a top layer of softmax activation.

Figure 4
:
The structure of the neural network in the empirical study.

All
{\mathcal{F}}
-net,
{\mathcal{Q}}
-net and
\Pi
-net are fully-connected feed forward neural
networks of 2 layers with ReLU activation functions and one residual connection
from input to the layer before output (ResNet). For either architecture, polynomial
uniform entropy can be obtained on a bounded input domain by restricting
all parameters to a compact set. Specifically, if a network has
w

parameters and is uniformly Lipschitz in its parameter vector on that set,
a parameter-space grid gives

N(\epsilon,{\mathcal{H}},\|\bullet\|_{\infty})\leq(C/\epsilon)^{w}
.
Fixed finite architectures with Lipschitz activations have this property
when inputs and weights are bounded. Output bounds and latent Lipschitz
bounds must also be enforced to satisfy Assumption
3
.
These observations do not make a neural-network Q-function class convex,
and the unconstrained numerical implementation is not automatically covered
by the local convex-class guarantees.
