Noncooperative Equilibrium Selection via a Trading-based Auction
Jaehan Im, Filippos Fotiadis, Daniel Delahaye, Ufuk Topcu, David Fridovich-Keil

Abstract
Noncooperative multi-agent systems often face coordination challenges due to conflicting preferences among agents. In particular, agents acting in their own self-interest can settle on different equilibria, leading to suboptimal outcomes or even safety concerns. We propose an algorithm named trading auction for consensus ( TACo ), a decentralized approach that enables noncooperative agents to reach consensus without communicating directly or disclosing private valuations. TACo facilitates coordination through a structured trading-based auction, where agents iteratively select choices of interest and provably reach an agreement within an a priori bounded number of steps. A series of numerical experiments validate that the termination guarantees of TACo hold in practice, and show that TACo achieves a median performance that minimizes the total cost across all agents, while allocating resources significantly more fairly than baseline approaches.

Introduction

Multi-agent systems often require consensus among agents, yet such consensus can be challenging due to conflicts and disagreements. These conflicts stem from differences in the strategic priorities of the agents, making coordination difficult without communication or external mediation. A key example is the equilibrium selection problem

Reaching a consensus in multi-agent systems is particularly difficult when agents are noncooperative, and this difficulty arises from several factors. First, the consensus process must be

We propose an algorithm called the trading auction for consensus (
TACo

Over multiple rounds, agents attempt to persuade others to adopt their preferred choice by offering trades. When disagreements persist, they refine their negotiation strategies, making increasingly precise adjustments to their offers. The process continues until no agent sees a meaningful benefit in further negotiation, ensuring a stable outcome—a property that is provably satisfied within a finite number of steps.

TACo
addresses the core challenges of noncooperative consensus by enabling agents to adjust their preferences based on individual benefit, while ensuring decentralization and privacy preservation. It preserves privacy by not disclosing valuations over choices or trading assets. The algorithm does not require a centralized coordinator and uses broadcast communication—a common method of sharing information in multi-agent systems—which avoids direct communication. This comprehensive design makes
TACo
applicable to a range of noncooperative multi-agent systems, including autonomous vehicles and coordinating drone operations in cities.

The contributions of this work are threefold: (i) We introduce the trading auction for consensus (

enforces an agreement in finite time, and derive an explicit upper bound on the number of steps required for all agents to reach this mutual agreement. (iii) We validate our theoretical findings through numercial experiments and demonstrate that
TACo
outperforms existing negotiation methods regarding social optimality and fairness in a practical equilibrium selection scenario.

III
Coordination Under Conflicting Preferences

A prominent instance of conflicting choices in multi-agent systems is the
equilibrium selection problem
. Before explaining the problem, we first introduce the notation and framework used to model the interactions between agents.

III-A

Equilibrium selection problem

We model the interaction between agents as a noncooperative game with
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
agents, where each agent
i\in[n]\equiv\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
selects an action
x_{i}
from an action set
\mathbb{X}_{i}
. The collective actions of all agents are represented by the joint action profile
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. Each agent
i
wishes to minimize a cost function
c_{i}(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{\neg i})
which depends on its own action
x_{i}
and those of other agents, where
\mathbf{x}_{\neg i}=\mathbf{x}\setminus x_{i}
. The collection of costs of all agents is denoted as
\mathbf{c}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{n}\}
.

Generalized Nash equilibrium is a well-known concept that describes possible outcomes in noncooperative settings. It is a point
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
where no agent has an incentive to unilaterally change their action, while adhering to constraints that are dependent on other agents’ strategies.

Definition 1

(Generalized Nash equilibrium)
.

A set of strategies
\mathbf{x}^{\star}=\{x_{1}^{\star},x_{2}^{\star},\ldots,x_{n}^{\star}\}
is a generalized Nash equilibrium if the following holds for each agent
i\in[n]
,

c_{i}(x_{i}^{\star},\mathbf{x}_{\neg i}^{\star})\leq c_{i}(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{\neg i}^{\star}),~\forall x_{i}\in\mathbb{X}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{\neg i}^{\star}).

(1)

At a generalized Nash equilibrium, each agent’s strategy is optimal given the strategies of all other agents and satisfies the feasibility constraints imposed by the other agents’ actions.

There exist multiple Nash equilibria in many games, meaning that several action profiles
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
may satisfy the Nash equilibrium condition. This multiplicity arises when different combinations of actions lead to optimal outcomes for each agent. The presence of multiple equilibria introduces a coordination challenge known as the
equilibrium selection problem
, where agents must agree on one equilibrium from the set of possible options to ensure efficient and safe interaction.

Example 1

(2-agent quadratic game)
.

Consider two agents,
i
and
j
, aiming to minimize a shared cost while satisfying a constraint. Each agent’s action
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is chosen to solve:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{x_{i}}{\text{minimize}}&c_{i}(x_{i},x_{j})=-(x_{i}+x_{j}-1)^{2},\\
\text{subject to}&x_{i}^{2}+x_{j}^{2}\leq 1.\end{array}

(2)

A Nash equilibrium
(x_{1}^{\star},x_{2}^{\star})
satisfies the
Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT)
conditions. The Lagrangian is:

\mathcal{L}(x_{1},x_{2},\mu)=-(x_{1}+x_{2}-1)^{2}+\mu(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-1),

(3)

where
\mu\geq 0
is the Lagrange multiplier. The first-order conditions are:

\displaystyle\textstyle{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x_{1}}=-2(x_{1}+x_{2}-1)+2\mu x_{1}=0,}

(4a)

\displaystyle\textstyle{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x_{2}}=-2(x_{1}+x_{2}-1)+2\mu x_{2}=0,}

(4b)

\displaystyle\mu(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-1)=0,\quad\mu\geq 0,\quad x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\leq 1.

(4c)

From the first two equations, we obtain:

\textstyle{\mu(x_{1}-x_{2})=0,\quad x_{1}+x_{2}=\frac{2}{2-\mu}.}

(5)

This yields two cases: i)
\mu=0
: The constraint is inactive, The feasible solutions satisfying
x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\leq 1
are all points on the line segment
x_{1}+x_{2}=1
where
0\leq x_{1},x_{2}\leq 1
.
ii)
\mu>0
: The constraint is active, so
x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}=1
. Since
\mu(x_{1}-x_{2})=0
, we must have
x_{1}=x_{2}
. Solving
2x_{1}^{2}=1
gives
x_{1}=x_{2}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
.

The critical points are:

\textstyle{\{(x,1-x)\mid 0\leq x\leq 1\}\cup\left\{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right),\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right\}.}

(6)

However,
\{(x,1-x)\mid 0\leq x\leq 1\}
are local maxima, as an agent can unilaterally decrease their cost by deviating. Thus,
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
and
\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
are Nash equilibria, demonstrating the existence of multiple equilibria in this game.

In decentralized systems, achieving consensus on which equilibrium to select is nontrivial, especially in noncooperative settings where agents have different preferences over equilibria. Without coordination, there is a risk that agents may settle on different equilibria, resulting in inefficiencies or even safety risks in the system. Furthermore, agents may not be able to communicate directly or share their preferences, further complicating the equilibrium selection problem.

IV
Trading auction for consensus

The Trading Auction for Consensus (
TACo
) algorithm is designed to address the challenge of decentralized
conflicting choice
problems among non-cooperative agents. The primary goal of
TACo
is to help agents reach a consensus or agree on a single choice, even when they have conflicting preferences. The algorithm accomplishes this without requiring direct negotiation or communication between agents, and it preserves each agent’s private information during the process.

IV-A

Key elements of
TACo

TACo
consists of several key components: a pay matrix, an offer matrix, a cost matrix, a profit matrix, the private valuations over secondary assets, the amount of trading units, and the decrement factor. These components are central to how agents compute their profit and make decisions during
TACo
. Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
denote the number of agents, and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
the number of choices available in the auction. For any matrix
X
,
X_{ij}
refers to its
(i,j)
entry.

1)

Offer matrix (
\mathcal{O}
):
The offer matrix
\mathcal{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
represents the units that each agent
i
can receive if they select choice
j
. Specifically,
\mathcal{O}_{ij}
denotes the number of units that agent
i
will receive when they choose choice
j
, determined by the offers made by other agents.

2)

Pay matrix (
\mathcal{P}
):
The pay matrix
\mathcal{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
captures the cost each agent incurs when selecting a particular choice.
\mathcal{P}_{ij}
is the number of units that agent
i
has to pay to others when they choose choice
j
.

3)

Cost matrix (
\mathcal{C}
):
The cost matrix
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
reflects the intrinsic cost associated with each agent’s choice.
\mathcal{C}_{ij}
represents the original cost experienced by agent
i
when they select choice
j
.

4)

Private valuation (
\mathbf{b}
):
Each agent
i
assigns a private valuation,
b_{i}
, to the units of the asset being traded in the
TACo
algorithm. This valuation, represented as the
i
-th element of the vector
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
, reflects how much agent
i
values each unit of the secondary asset, such as carbon emission credits. The value
b_{i}
is unique to each agent and remains private, influencing how the agent perceives the offer and pay quantities when calculating their profit.

5)

Amount of trading units (
d
):
The constant
d\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
represents the amount of secondary assets traded in each auction step. This public quantity directly impacts the updates of the offer and pay matrices, influencing each agent’s profit calculation.

6)

Trading unit decrement factor (
\gamma
):
The decrement factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
is a parameter used to reduce the number of trading units when a specified condition is met. When triggered, the amount of trading units
d
is scaled by the decrement factor:

d\leftarrow\gamma d.

(7)

This reduction adjusts the scale of assets exchanged in the auction, allowing for a controlled decrease in trading volume over time or under certain conditions, which can influence the convergence behavior of the auction process.

7)

Profit matrix (
\mathcal{J}
):
The profit matrix
\mathcal{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
represents the profit each agent gains based on their choice. Specifically,
\mathcal{J}_{ij}
denotes the profit experienced by agent
i
when selecting choice
j
. The profit is calculated as follows:

\mathcal{J}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{b})\cdot(\mathcal{O}-\mathcal{P})-\mathcal{C}.

(8)

This equation accounts for each agent’s private valuation
b
, the offers received
\mathcal{O}
, the payments made
\mathcal{P}
, and the intrinsic costs
\mathcal{C}
, resulting in the total profit for each possible choice.

IV-B

Auction mechanism

TACo
operates through sequential steps, where agents take turns making decisions. At each step, an agent selects the choice they value most and attempts to persuade others by offering payments. These decisions are based on the matrices
\mathcal{O}
,
\mathcal{P}
, and
\mathcal{C}
. Before explaining the
TACo
mechanism, we introduce the following definitions.

Definition 2

(Agent-profit matrix tuple
(i,\mathcal{J})
)
.

The agent-profit matrix tuple
(i,\mathcal{J})
represents the association of agent index
i\in\mathbb{N}
with a specific profit matrix
\mathcal{J}
applied in the current step.

A cycle refers to a sequence of auction processes that returns to a previously encountered profit matrix–playing agent pair. Once the process revisits an existing profit matrix-playing agent pair, the sequence repeats, forming a loop. An interesting property of a cycle is that it includes a state where all agents have reached an agreement, particularly in the trivial case
where they continuously select the same choice without termination.

Definition 3

(Cycle,
\sigma
)
.

A cycle,
\sigma
, is a finite sequence of agent-profit matrix tuples,

\sigma=\left((i^{k_{1}},\mathcal{J}^{k_{1}}),(i^{k_{2}},\mathcal{J}^{k_{2}}),\ldots,(i^{k_{\ell}},\mathcal{J}^{k_{\ell}})\right),

in which a tuple
(i^{k_{\ell+1}},\mathcal{J}^{k_{\ell+1}})
revisits any previously encountered tuple, i.e.,

(i^{k_{\ell+1}},\mathcal{J}^{k_{\ell+1}})=(i^{k_{1}},\mathcal{J}^{k_{1}}).

Here,
\ell
is the length of the cycle.

Definition 4

(Step,
k
)
.

A step in the
TACo
process refers to a single update to the profit matrix
\mathcal{J}
, which occurs based on the choice made by one agent during that auction step.

IV-C

Properties of
TACo

There are several properties of the
TACo
procedure (
Algorithm

1
).

IV-C
1
Procedural rationality

TACo
incentivizes agents to act in their self-interest by enabling them to trade and negotiate based on their individual valuations of the available options. As demonstrated in
Example

2
, each agent selects the option that maximizes its profit, naturally driving the auction process toward a consensus.

IV-C
2
Privacy preservation

One of
TACo
’s core strengths is its ability to preserve the privacy of each agent’s valuations. The trading mechanism functions without requiring agents to disclose their private valuations for the available options (
\mathcal{C}
) or trading assets (
\mathbf{b}
).

IV-C
3
Independence from direct communication

TACo
operates under a minimal communication framework, where agents only need to broadcast their choices. This eliminates the need for direct, 1-on-1 communication, making the algorithm straightforward to implement in practical systems.

IV-C
4
Termination guarantee

TACo
is guaranteed to terminate with an agreement within a bounded number of rounds. The proof of termination, as well as an explicit upper bound on the number of rounds needed to terminate, is provided in the following section.

V
Termination Proof of
TACo

The proof of
TACo
’s termination (with an agreement) involves three steps. First, we show that
TACo
always enters a cycle, where the auction steps repeat in a loop as defined in
Definition

3
. Next, we demonstrate that profit differences between choices within a cycle are bounded, with the bound proportional to the trading unit per step
d
. This restricts each agent’s available choices to options with profit differences no greater than the bound. Finally, we prove that
TACo
satisfies the epsilon-termination condition and hence converges.

The intuition behind the convergence of
TACo
lies in that, as soon as a cycle is detected, the trading unit
d
is reduced by a fixed decrement factor
\gamma
. However, as
d
decreases, the profit differences within the cycle shrink and must thus eventually fall below the threshold
\epsilon
for all agents. At this point, the agents reach a consensus by definition, and
TACo
terminates.

V-A

Existence of cycles in
TACo

We first prove that
TACo
always enters a cycle. A cycle occurs when the auction steps repeat in a loop, as defined in
Definition

3
. This result is established by analyzing bounded row sums and discrete profit increments. The following lemma shows that the row sum of the profit matrix fluctuates within a bounded range as shown in
Figure

2
.

Lemma 1

(Row sum of the profit matrix)
.

The row sum
S_{i}^{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{J}_{i,j}^{k}
, where
m
is the number of choices, remains constant every
n
steps, with fluctuations equal to:

S_{i}^{\mathrm{max}}-S_{i}^{\mathrm{min}}=d(n-1)b_{i},

(13)

where
S_{i}^{\mathrm{max}}
and
S_{i}^{\mathrm{min}}
are the maximum and minimum row sums that can occur during the
TACo
process.

The bounded row sums imply that the actual profit values
\mathcal{J}_{ij}
are lower bounded, as established in the next lemma.

Lemma 2

(Lower bound of profit values)
.

The profit value
\mathcal{J}_{ij}
for any agent
i
and choice
j
is lower bounded as

\mathcal{J}_{ij}\geq\min\left(\min(\mathcal{J}_{i,:}^{0}),\textstyle{\frac{S_{i}^{\mathrm{min}}}{m}}-d(n-1)b_{i}\right),

(14)

where
m
is the number of choices and
\min(\mathcal{J}_{i,:}^{0})
is the minimum element for row
i
in the initial profit matrix
\mathcal{J}
.

Since the row sums of the profit matrix
\mathcal{J}
remain constant over every
n
steps (Lemma 1) and since
\mathcal{J}
is lower-bounded (Lemma 2), it directly follows that
\mathcal{J}
is also upper-bounded. We state this consequence in the following corollary.

Corollary 1

(Upper bound of profit values)
.

The profit values in the matrix
\mathcal{J}
are upper bounded.

Exploiting the proved upper and lower bounds on
\mathcal{J}
derived so far, we can now formally prove that
TACo
always enters a cycle. This result is important because, by definition, a state of consensus where all agents have similar preferences – up to a level of tolerance
\epsilon
– is also a cycle. Hence, the convergence of
TACo
will subsequently boil down to showing that the cycle it eventually falls into corresponds to a consensus.

Theorem 1

(The auction always falls into a cycle)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of all possible agent-profit matrix tuples
(i,\mathcal{J})
, where
i\in[n]
and
\mathcal{J}
is a profit matrix satisfying the constraints defined in

Lemma

1
,
Lemma

2
and
Corollary

1
. The
TACo
algorithm must eventually revisit a previously encountered tuple
(i,\mathcal{J})\in\mathcal{S}
, resulting in the formation of a cycle.

V-B

Bounded profit differences between choices in a cycle

Having established that
TACo
always enters a cycle, we now focus on bounding the profit differences among the choices within that cycle. More importantly, we will show that the underlying bound in profit differences is
proportional
to
d
; a property that will allow us to prove
TACo
eventually converges to a state of agreement between agents, particularly since
d
is decremented according to (
12
) once a cycle is detected. To establish this property, we start by introducing the
choice count matrix
(
\Delta^{\sigma}
), which tracks how many times each agent selects each choice during a cycle.

Definition 5

(Choice count matrix,
\Delta^{\sigma}
)
.

The choice count matrix, denoted as
\Delta^{\sigma}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times m}
, represents the number of times each choice is selected by agents during a cycle. Specifically,
\Delta^{\sigma}_{i,j}
denotes the number of times agent
i
selects choice
j
within the cycle
\sigma
.

It follows that the matrix
\Delta^{\sigma}
satisfies the following:

\mathcal{P}^{t+\ell}-\mathcal{P}^{t}=nd\cdot\Delta^{\sigma},

(15)

\mathcal{O}^{t+\ell}-\mathcal{O}^{t}=d\cdot H_{n}\Delta^{\sigma},

(16)

where
\mathcal{P}^{t+\ell}-\mathcal{P}^{t}
and
\mathcal{O}^{t+\ell}-\mathcal{O}^{t}
represent the net changes in the pay matrix and offer matrix after each cycle of length
\ell
, respectively, and
H_{n}=\textbf{1}_{n}\textbf{1}_{n}^{\top}
is an
n\times n
matrix with all elements equal to 1, capturing the aggregation of offers received across all agents. Using the definition of the choice count matrix and its relationship shown in
eq.

15
and
eq.

16
, we establish the structure of
\Delta^{\sigma}
which shows that, within a cycle, all agents select each choice the same number of times.

Lemma 3

(Uniform choice count matrix in a cycle)
.

The choice count matrix
\Delta^{\sigma}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times m}
for a cycle has the form

\Delta^{\sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}&c_{2}&\ldots&c_{m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&\ldots&c_{m}\end{bmatrix},

(17)

where each
c_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
represents the number of times that choice
j
is selected by each agent within the cycle.

This uniform structure naturally leads to the concept of
active choices
: the choices selected by agents during a cycle.

Definition 6

(Active choices in a cycle,
A^{\sigma}
)
.

The active choices in a cycle, denoted
A^{\sigma}
, are the row indices of the choice count matrix
\Delta^{\sigma}
with non-zero values. In other words,
A^{\sigma}
is a set of indices representing the choices selected at least once by some agent within a cycle.

We now analyze the profit differences within a cycle by focusing on active choices only. Leveraging
Lemma

1
,
Lemma

2
, and
Lemma

3
, we prove a key result that bounds these profit differences
proportionally
to the amount of trading units
d
.

Theorem 2

(Bound on profit differences within a cycle)
.

The profit difference among the active choices
A^{\sigma}
in a cycle
\sigma
for agent
i
is bounded as

U_{\sigma}^{i}-L_{\sigma}^{i}\leq(m+1)d(n-1)b_{\max},\quad\forall i\in[n],

(18)

where
m
is the total number of choices,
d
is the trading unit for the current cycle,
b_{\max}=\max_{i\in[n]}(b_{i})
is the highest private valuation among the agents, and
U_{\sigma}^{i}
and
L_{\sigma}^{i}
are the maximum and minimum profit values for the active choices of agent
i
within cycle
\sigma
, respectively.

V-C

Finite termination property of
TACo

With the profit differences bounded proportionally to
d
(
Theorem

2
), we now demonstrate that
TACo
satisfies the epsilon-termination condition, leading to an approximate agreement in a finite number of steps. Recall that each time a cycle is detected – which provably happens according to Theorem
1
– the algorithm reduces the trading unit
d
by a fixed decrement factor
\gamma
. This reduction ensures that the profit differences between choices eventually fall below the threshold
\epsilon
, leading to termination.

Theorem 3

(Termination of
TACo
)
.

The
TACo
algorithm terminates in a finite number of steps.

We also provide a practical bound on the worst-case number of steps required till termination. This bound accounts for the number of cycles needed to meet the epsilon-termination condition as well as the steps within each cycle, hence providing practical insights into the efficiency of
TACo
.

Theorem 4

(Termination bound for
TACo
)
.

The
TACo
algorithm terminates at most within the following number of steps:

\textstyle{\left\lceil\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{(m+1)d_{0}(n-1)b_{\max}}\right)\right\rceil\cdot n\cdot\big((m+1)(n-1)\big)^{nm}},

(19)

with decrement factor
\gamma
and initial trading unit
d_{0}
.

Theorem

4
implies that if
\epsilon
is increased to
\frac{\epsilon}{\gamma}
, then the worst-case termination bound decreases by
n\cdot\big((m+1)(n-1)\big)^{nm}
steps.

VI
Numerical Experiments

VI-A

Waypoint merging game

We model a common noncooperative scenario in air traffic management, where
n
aircraft converging on a waypoint must adjust their speeds to avoid collisions, as shown in
Figure

1
. Each aircraft
i
in this
waypoint merging game
has an estimated time of arrival (ETA)
e_{i}
and seeks to minimize deviations from it while ensuring safe separation. We introduce carbon emission credits as a tradable secondary asset.

Each aircraft (agent)
i
selects an action
x_{i}
, representing its adjustment to the ETA. The adjusted arrival time for agent
i
is then
u_{i}=e_{i}+x_{i}
. The cost structure for each agent is:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{x_{i}}{\mbox{minimize}}&c_{i}(x_{i})=k_{i}x_{i}^{2}\\
\mbox{subject to}&|u_{i}-u_{j}|\geq D,\enskip\forall j\in[n]\setminus i,\end{array}

(20)

where
k_{i}>0
represents the urgency factor, indicating sensitivity to arrival time adjustments, and
D\geq 0
is the required separation distance.

This game
Equation

20
admits multiple generalized Nash equilibria. The absolute value in the constraint introduces two conditions depending on the arrival sequence. Specifically, the sign of the terms inside the absolute value changes based on the order of
u_{i}
and
u_{j}
, leading to two inverted inequalities. This leads to
n!
distinct equilibria. Thus, coordination is essential for safe and efficient merging.

Another key challenge is the lack of direct 1-on-1 communication between aircraft. Instead, aircraft use the
Automatic Dependent Surveillance-Broadcast (ADS-B)
system, which broadcasts publicly available information such as positions, urgency factors (
k_{i}
), and ETAs (
e_{i}
). However,
ADS-B
does not support direct negotiation, making consensus on an equilibrium difficult despite agents being able to compute all equilibria.

In our scenario, each aircraft privately determines its valuation for emission credits and employs the
TACo
algorithm to iteratively negotiate arrival adjustments. By trading emission credits and broadcasting preferences via
ADS-B
, aircraft reach a consensus while acting in their own self-interest, despite the communication constraints imposed by
ADS-B
.

VI-B

Baseline algorithms

We evaluated the effectiveness of the
TACo
algorithm by comparing it with widely used negotiation approaches. While these baseline methods lack key features of
TACo
, they serve as intuitive benchmarks for multi-agent coordination. The baseline algorithms considered in this experiment are:

•

Voting
: Each agent votes for their preferred options, and the final decision is determined by majority rule
[
39
]
. In the event of a tie, the winner is chosen randomly.

•

Random dictator
: A random agent is selected, and all other agents must conform to the preferences of this chosen agent.

•

Utilitarian
: A central coordinator selects the option that minimizes the total costs across all agents, requiring compliance from every agent.

•

Egalitarian
: A central coordinator selects the option that maximizes fairness among agents, and all agents are required to comply with the decision.

The voting mechanism offers a simple, decentralized approach but can lead outcomes which are not unilaterally rational, as agents must accept majority decisions even when misaligned with their preferences. Moreover, the utilitarian and egalitarian methods optimize system-level objectives but impose centralized decisions, making them unsuitable for noncooperative and decentralized settings. Lastly, the random dictator mechanism also relies on centralized selection but ensures fairness over time in repeated interactions by rotating decision-making power among agents.

VI-C

Evaluation method

We evaluated the performance of
TACo
across three key metrics: optimality gap, fairness, and convergence stpng.

•

Optimality gap
: The optimality gap quantifies the deviation of the total cost incurred by the algorithm from the optimal total cost achieved by a utilitarian solution. It is defined as:

\textstyle{\mathrm{OG}(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{c}^{\star})=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}c_{i}}{\sum_{i\in[n]}c_{i}^{\star}}-1,}

(21)

where
c_{i}^{\star}\in\mathbf{c}^{\star}
is the cost of agent
i
from the utilitarian solution, and
c_{i}
is the cost of agent
i
under the evaluated algorithm.

•

Gini index
: The Gini index
[
40
]
measures fairness among agents. A smaller Gini index indicates more equitable outcomes. It is computed as:

\textstyle{\mathrm{GI}(\textbf{c})=\frac{1}{2n\sum_{i\in[n]}c_{i}}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}|c_{i}-c_{j}|.}

(22)

•

Convergence stpng
: The convergence stpng metric measures the algorithm’s efficiency by counting the number of stpng required for termination.

To assess the effectiveness of
TACo
, we conducted a series of Monte Carlo simulations with 1000 trials to ensure statistical significance. Each trial incorporated randomized initial conditions, including diverse weight parameters (
k_{i}
) and varying initial estimated times of arrival. Agents’ private valuations over secondary assets (
b_{i}
) were randomly assigned within a predefined range to reflect diverse private information. The decrement factor
\gamma
was set to 0.9, with the number of aircraft
n
set to 4 and the number of choices
m
set to 24.

The simulations were implemented in the Julia programming language. We used the
ParametricMCPs.jl
package
[
41
]
and the
PATH
solver for mixed complementarity problems
[
42
]
to compute the Nash equilibria of the game
Equation

20
prior to running
TACo
.

VII
Conclusion

We developed the trading auction for consensus (
TACo
) algorithm, a decentralized approach for coordinating decisions among noncooperative agents facing conflicting choices.
TACo
enables agents to reach consensus by trading secondary assets, ensuring procedural rationality without requiring centralized coordination, direct communication, or the disclosure of private information. This flexibility makes
TACo
an effective tool for resolving coordination challenges in multi-agent systems.

Future work includes improving scalability, evaluating performance in more complex environments, and tightening convergence bounds to ensure
TACo
terminates quickly in safety-critical settings. This will clarify the types of problems it can efficiently solve and inform strategies for faster convergence.
