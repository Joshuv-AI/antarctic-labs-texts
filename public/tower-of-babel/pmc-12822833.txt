The effect of chronic care model on self-efficacy and health-related quality of life of patients with ulcerative colitis: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Zahra Sepehrian Shahrezai, Mohammad Hassan Emami, Sedigheh Farzi, Mohammad Javad Tarrahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The chronic care model is a well-known framework aimed at improving the care of chronic diseases including ulcerative colitis (UC). This study aims to investigate the impact of the chronic care model on the self-efficacy and health-related quality of life in individuals with UC.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This randomized controlled trial was conducted in 2022. Participants were 60 patients with UC who were referred to Poursina Hakim clinic, Isfahan, Iran. They were selected through convenient sampling and assigned to control and intervention groups by randomized block design. In the intervention group, the chronic care model was implemented. The control group had only routine visits with a physician to receive a prescription. The self-efficacy score and the health-related quality of life were assessed in both groups before, immediately and 3 months after the intervention. Data were analyzed using descriptive (frequency distribution, mean, and standard deviation) and inferential (Independent sample t-test, Multivariate Analysis of Covariance, analysis of variance using repeated measurements, and Chi square) statistics. A significance level was considered at 0.05.RESULTS:The average age of the participants was 44.98. Intergroup analysis shows that the difference in self-efficacy scores (95.1 ± 15.40, 93.7 ± 15.78) and health-related quality of life (48.7 ± 5.23, 47.7 ± 6.45) between the control and intervention groups was not significant, respectively (P > 0.05). According to intragroup analysis, self-efficacy (114.96 ± 13.04, 116.53 ± 12.92) and quality of life (56.1 ± 3.66, 57.23 ± 3.53) scores in the intervention group increased significantly both immediately and 3 months after the intervention, respectively (P < 0.001).CONCLUSION:The findings showed that the use of the chronic care model has a positive effect on the self-efficacy of UC patients and also improves their health-related quality of life. Therefore, it is recommended that health policymakers and managers of healthcare centers provide the basis for applying this model with an interprofessional team in health centers to provide more effective and quality care to individuals with UC.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is one of the chronic gastrointestinal diseases that include ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD). In UC, the mucosa layer of the large intestine is involved, and the patient experiences symptoms such as severe diarrhea, abdominal pain, rectal bleeding, weight loss, fatigue, fever, and anemia. The disease symptoms intermittently relapse and remission and the patient require lifelong medication treatment. The exact cause of UC is still unknown; however, an uncontrolled immune response to gut microbes triggered by environmental factors in genetically predisposed individuals may be the cause.[1]

The prevalence rate of IBD in the world is 0.1 to 0.4% of the general population.[2] In recent decades, the incidence and prevalence of UC have increased worldwide, particularly in Asian regions and developing countries, including Iran.[3,4] In Iran, in 2012, the annual incidence of IBD (3.11), UC (2.70), and CD (0.41) and the prevalence of these diseases were reported as (40.67), (35.02), and (5.03) per 100,000 individuals, respectively, which is increasing annually.[5] In Isfahan, the number of patients with IBD 2300 person was reported.[6] IBD can cause problems in education, employment, or starting a family, and the occurrence of symptoms such as diarrhea and abdominal pains causes a disturbance in the health-related quality of life (HRQoL).[7]

IBD patients experience lower health-related quality of life compared to the general population, even during periods of disease remission.[7,8] Health-related quality of life includes physical health, mental state, social relationships, level of independence, personal beliefs, and the individual’s relationship with their environment, which provides a more comprehensive assessment of the disease impacts on one’s physical, emotional, psychological, and social dimensions, as well as their perception of health.[9] In addition, patients’ self-confidence, personal relationships, nutritional and health status, and sense of security face challenges[7]; consequently, patients require a specific set of self-management skills, including adhering to complex drug regimens, following cancer prevention guidelines, managing drug adverse effects and extraintestinal symptoms properly, and changing lifestyle if necessary.[10] It is assumed that self-efficacy (SE) is a prerequisite for self-management and a reflection of responsibility for self-care.[11] It is one’s belief in his/her abilities to perform tasks affecting life, which is associated with the patient’s adherence to treatment and positive health behaviors, including a healthy diet and physical activity.[12] Nevertheless, a large number of individuals with UC lack enough knowledge to manage the disease and perform self-care activities, which can cause the recurrence of the disease and further disruption in the quality of life.[13,14]

Interprofessional collaborative models help individuals with chronic diseases accept the disease, adhere to treatment, and resume recovery. They also reduce complications such as hospitalization and surgical interventions and can improve health-related quality of life in patients with IBD.[7] Wagner’s Chronic Care Model (CCM) is a well-known framework for improving the health system and organizing care for chronic diseases. The developers of this model have always emphasized the importance of active, planned, evidence-based, and patient-centered care. CCM integrates several elements designed to foster constructive interactions between healthcare providers and patients. These elements include 1. ‘delivery system (re)design,’ which refers to the coordination between care processes and ensuring the provision of effective, efficient, and inclusive care by using the delegation of tasks in an interprofessional care group including specialist physicians, nurses, nutritionists, psychologists, and others and the follow-up of patients by this team; 2. ‘Self-management support’ refers to empowering and preparing patients for healthcare management; therefore, patients are encouraged to set goals for themselves, identify obstacles and challenges, and take into account their conditions. 3. ‘Decision support’ refers to the promotion of care in accordance with effective evidence-based care guidelines and patient preferences, which requires care providers to have access to the latest guidelines and provide this information to patients using educational methods; 4. ‘Organization of healthcare delivery system,’ which necessitates the attention of an organization’s (hospital/clinic) officials to a system focused on high-quality and valued care; 5. ‘Clinical information systems’ refer to organizing information to facilitate care follow-ups, providing reminders, and sharing information; 6. ‘Community resources and policies’ implies supporting care using community-based resources and public health policies [Figure 1].[15,16]

The conceptual framework (CCM)

CCM emphasizes the importance of supporting self-management through which patients are provided with the essential knowledge, self-confidence, and self-management skills.[17] Magharei et al.’s study (2019)[18] showed that self-management in UC patients improved self-efficacy and quality of life. Randell et al. (2013)[19] investigated the experience of patients with IBD, including UC, from specific dimensions of care adaptable to CCM. The results showed that receiving CCM-based care was associated with a higher quality of life, and the effort to adapt care with this model could benefit patients.

The second Nurses European Crohn’s and Colitis Organization (N-ECCO) Consensus Statements on the nurse’s role in patient care and the advantages of the IBD specialist nurse in the area of health-related quality of life states that the IBD specialist nurse is a worthwhile resource for the patient to obtain further information about the disease and increase self-confidence and the ability to manage the disease, and they directly affect patients’ quality of life. The nurse can improve the person’s adaptation to the disease through communication and make the patient more independent and willing to self-manage through regular contacts and a guided self-management program.[20] High self-efficacy reduces disability and anxiety and improves productivity and adaptation in patients with IBD.[21] Also, the CCM has improved the self-efficacy of osteoarthritis patients.[22] Despite numerous studies on the positive impact of using CCM on quality of life, self-efficacy, and other clinical outcomes in those with chronic diseases, there is a limited number of studies on using CCM in UC patients. This study was conducted to investigate the effect of CCM on self-efficacy and health-related quality of life of patients with UC.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This randomized controlled trial was conducted in 2022. The participants were 60 patients with UC, referring to Poursina Hakim Gastroenterology Clinic located in Salamat Town, Isfahan, Iran.

Study participants and sampling

Patients who met the inclusion criteria were selected through convenient sampling and assigned to control and intervention groups by randomized block design. Inclusion criteria included diagnosis of UC by a specialist physician based on colonoscopy and pathology results, at least 6 months passing since the diagnosis of the disease based on inquiries from the patient and colonoscopy results, obtaining a score of 3 to 10 on the Lichtiger Colitis Activity Index (LCAI) questionnaire, age 18 to 65 years old, being a Persian speaker, willingness to participate in the study, possibility of contacting the patient via phone, the patient’s ability to use the WhatsApp messenger, no participation in a similar study simultaneously, no other chronic diseases, and no confirmed mental or psychological diseases based on the statements of the patient and family. Exclusion criteria included suffering from an acute illness, absence or noncooperation in more than two training sessions, reluctance to continue cooperation, and recurrence of the disease.

Patients who met the study criteria were grouped into eight strata based on gender (female, male) and age (below 30 years old, 30 to 40 years old, 40 to 50 years old, and over 50 years old). Then, within each stratum, random allocation was performed using random block allocation. In this method, blocks were created based on the required variables, and 50% of individuals in each block were included in the intervention group and 50% in the control group. Participants in the intervention group received CCM-based care, and those in the control group received routine care, which included doctor visits and prescriptions. The sample size was determined as 60 (30 participants in each group) based on a similar study,[18] with a standard deviation of 5.92 and 6.02 and a difference size of at least 5 points, taking into account the first type error of 5% and the second type error of 10% based on the following formula [Figure 2].

Data collection and technique

The data collection tool included four questionnaires: Demographics Information, LCAI, Self-efficacy, and Health-related Quality of Life, which were completed by the participants.

Demographic information questionnaire

The questionnaire included gender, age, marital status, education level, job status, residency (private/rented), history of addiction, duration of disease, and history of colorectal surgery. In this study, a CCM-based educational support program was considered as the independent variable, and self-efficacy and health-related quality of life as dependent variables.

Lichtiger Colitis Activity Index (LCAI)

This questionnaire was developed by Lichtiger et al. (1994)[23] to measure the UC patients’ response to treatment. This tool measures objective and subjective symptoms including diarrhea (0-4), nocturnal diarrhea (0-1), bloody discharge (0-3), fecal incontinence (0-1), abdominal pain and heartburn (0-3), general health (0-5), pain when touching the abdomen (0-3), and the need for anti-diarrheal medication (0-1). The total score varies from 0 to 21, where a score higher than 10 indicates no response to treatment, a score less than 2 implies successful treatment, and a score of 3-10 indicates a positive response to treatment, but the disease is not extinguished yet. The validity and reliability of this tool have been confirmed in Iran.[24] We also assessed the reliability of LCAI (the Cronbach Alpha Coefficient was 0.74).

Self-efficacy questionnaire

Self-efficacy was evaluated using Strategies Used by People to Promote Health (SUPPH), developed in 1996 by Lev et al.[25] SUPPH was designed based on self-efficacy theory in four areas: adaptation, stress reduction, decision-making, and enjoying life. The new version of this questionnaire was later presented in three areas: positive attitude (16 questions), stress reduction (10 questions), and decision-making (3 questions). In total, SUPPH consists of 29 questions with a 5-point Likert scale ranging from very little to very much. The maximum and minimum self-efficacy scores are 145 and 29, respectively, with a higher score indicating higher self-efficacy. The validity and reliability have been confirmed in Iran by Moattari et al. (2012).[26] We also assessed the reliability of SUPPH through test–retest, and its intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.85.

Health-related quality of life questionnaire

The short form of the Inflammatory Bowel Disease Questionnaire (IBDQ-9) was used to evaluate patients’ health-related quality of life. This questionnaire was compiled by Casellas et al. (2004)[27] to evaluate the quality of life affected by IBD. It measures four dimensions of disorders related to the gastrointestinal system and systematic, emotional, and social symptoms through nine questions. Each question is answered with a rating scale from 1 to 7 (worst to best status). The total score ranges between 9 and 63; a higher score indicates a more favorable quality of life. The validity and reliability have been confirmed in Iran by Gholamrezaei et al. (2011).[28] We also assessed the reliability of IBDQ-9 through test–retest, and its ICC was 0.75.

Procedures

After obtaining the necessary permissions, the researcher referred to Poursina Hakim Gastroenterology Clinic located in Salamat Town, Isfahan, Iran. Before the intervention, demographics information, LCAI, self-efficacy, and Health-related Quality of Life were completed by the participants. Then, the chronic care model (CCM) and routine care were held for the intervention and the control group, respectively.

Intervention

In this study, the four components of CCM were used: 1) Care delivery system redesign, 2) Supporting self-management, 3) Supporting decision-making, and 4) Clinical information systems. Table 1 provides a summary of the study intervention.

The Intervention

In order to implement the intervention, the researcher created a group called UC patients in the WhatsApp messenger after obtaining permission from UC patients (intervention group). The group members included the UC patients of the intervention group and the researcher. In addition to the interaction with other members of the group, the researcher contacted the patients privately and informed them that they could raise their questions about the disease through WhatsApp messenger throughout the day or night. The researcher was also available for patients’ telephone calls every day except Fridays from 7:00 am to 7:00 pm. Moreover, the researcher had a weekly talk with each member of the intervention group about the tests, digestive and systemic symptoms, doctor’s visits, and compliance with the medications; provided them with guidance, information, and reminders; and monitored how the trained subjects were practiced.

In order to provide training, an educational PDF booklet was sent to patients. Afterward, according to the WhatsApp messaging group members’ opinion, a time was determined for a 60-minute weekly session by researchers. At the beginning of the week, the PowerPoint file and the podcast related to that meeting, which included information and questions about training, were sent to the group. All members participated in the meeting, answered the questions, raised their questions if needed, and received answers from peers and the researcher. Patients could talk with their peers in the group throughout the week and express their experiences regarding the presented materials. A total of five 60-minute sessions were held over 5 weeks with topics such as introduction to UC disease, symptoms and gastrointestinal complications of UC and their management, available UC treatments, emotional symptoms, mental health, social interactions, positive attitude, stress reduction, and decision-making. The follow-up and communication with the patients by the nurse started from the beginning of the training sessions and continued for 2 months. Moreover, during the study, the patients discussed the treatment options of the disease with the researcher, the latest information on UC was provided to them, and their preferences were considered for the continuation of treatment and care. Demographic information, medications used, and results of tests and colonoscopies were also recorded electronically.

In this study, the control group received routine care, including specialist doctors’ visits and a prescription; however, at the end of the study, educational files were provided to them. Self-efficacy and health-related quality of life questionnaires were completed electronically by all subjects before, immediately after, and 3 months after the intervention while observing confidentiality.

Data analysis

The collected data were entered into SPSS statistical software, V. 22 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The variables of this study were quantitative (discrete and continuous) and qualitative (nominal and ordinal). Data were analyzed using descriptive (frequency distribution, mean, and standard deviation) and inferential (Independent sample t-test: to compare mean scores between groups before intervention, Multivariate Analysis of Covariance (MANCOVA): to compare mean scores between groups immediately and 3 months after the intervention, analysis of variance using repeated measurements: to compare the mean scores of the three time points (before, immediately, and 3 months after the intervention) within and between groups, and Chi square to assess association of qualitative variables) statistics. A significance level was considered at 0.05. We used intention to treat strategy for data analysis.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences with the code of ethics IR.MUI.NUREMA.REC.1400.049 and recorded in the Clinical Trial Center of Iran IRCT20170731035424N2. The researcher attended Poursina Hakim Gastroenterology Clinic and, after receiving permission from the authorities, attended the clinic on the days of the physician’s visit and explained the study process and objectives to the patients who were waiting for a specialist’s visit and invited them to cooperate. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients, and they were ensured that their information would remain confidential and that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any time. Numerical codes were used to keep the samples anonymous. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations in the declaration.

Discussion

The CCM has been proposed as a healthcare delivery model to improve disease-related outcomes. In various studies, one or more components of this model have been used to improve the outcomes of patients with chronic diseases, which has led to positive results. In the present study, four components of CCM were used, and the results indicated their positive effects on self-efficacy and its dimensions. In line with this result, a study on osteoarthritis patients in a rheumatology clinic showed the effect of CCM on improving patients’ self-efficacy scores.[22] In addition, using CCM has improved self-efficacy and self-management; reduced HbA1c, hypertension, LDL, and BMI; and increased the knowledge and satisfaction of patients with type 2 diabetes.[16,21,29,30] Application of some components of CCM, including supporting self-management and related measures, has led to positive outcomes, including improving self-efficacy in patients with stroke, and myocardial infarction.[31,32]

According to the study by Finney Rutten et al. (2016)[33] on patients with chronic diseases, those with more disease burden (more severe disease) had significantly lower self-efficacy; in contrast, those who experienced more positive patient-centered communication reported higher levels of self-efficacy. Individuals with high self-efficacy show a perceived ability to manage challenges related to their disease and a sense of control over their lives in dealing with chronic diseases. Nurses play a crucial role in caring for patients with chronic diseases. Through patient education, they can help patients increase self-efficacy, adaptability, and self-management skills. In addition, nurses, as care coordinators, can act as patient advocates in the interprofessional team.[34] Studies support the need to employ IBD specialist nurses and emphasize the nurses’ critical role in education, providing information, follow-ups, counseling, psychosocial support, and helping patients manage nutrition, psychosocial issues, and extraintestinal manifestations of the disease. The results of employing nurses in various studies show their impact on significant improvement of the quality of care and reduction of outpatient visits, referrals to the emergency department, hospitalization, and treatment costs.[35,36]

In addition, the results of the present study showed that care based on the CCM model improved the health-related quality of life in all four dimensions in patients with UC. Consistent with our results, studies show applying CCM components improves patients with UC and children with asthma and has led to the simultaneous improvement of the self-efficacy score and quality of life.[18,37]

In a trial, four components of the chronic care model, including care delivery system redesign, decision support, clinical information systems, and especially self-management support for patients after ischemic stroke, were used. In this study, although patient satisfaction with care increased significantly in the intervention group and individuals’ awareness and information about the disease improved, the self-efficacy score and quality of life did not change significantly after 12 weeks of intervention. The short duration of the intervention, the low severity of the stroke, and patients’ less need for help and relative independence could be the reason.[38]

Rohatinsky et al. (2023)[39] investigated elderly IBD patients’ health-related quality of life and the experience of participation in interprofessional care. The participants acknowledged that participation in care and treatment was of significant importance, increased their satisfaction, and improved their quality of life. However, despite their interest in acquiring information about the disease and its management, they often faced challenges finding up-to-date and reliable information. These patients believed that access to a reliable source to find answers to their questions was essential; in addition, having in-person or online, formal or informal communication with peers increased their quality of life by creating social support. Therefore, involving interprofessional collaboration and patients in care result in improved patient outcomes[40] and promoting quality of care.[41]

Limitations and recommendations

One of the limitations of the present study was the reduction of the intervention duration due to the researcher’s academic period of only 5 weeks. Another limitation was the absence of nutritionists and psychologists, and an effort was made to control this limitation to some extent by coordinating with a specialist physician.

According to the current research findings, the following suggestions are presented for future research. It is suggested that CCM-based interventions should be implemented for longer periods of time in future studies. In addition, recruiting nutritionists and psychologists to follow up on patients’ diets and mental health status in CCM can be advantageous.

Conclusion

The results of this study showed a positive effect of CCM on UC patients’ stress levels, decision-making ability, positive attitude, and self-efficacy. Moreover, employing this model reduced patients’ digestive, systematic, social, and emotional problems and thus improved their health-related quality of life. Therefore, it is recommended that health policymakers and managers of healthcare centers provide the basis for applying this model with an interprofessional team in health centers to provide more effective and quality care to individuals with UC.
