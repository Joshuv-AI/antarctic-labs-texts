Facilitators of and barriers to linkage to HIV care and treatment in Morogoro, Tanzania: a sequentially mixed-methods evaluation
Authors: Boniface G Centa, Godfrey Kacholi, Henry Mollel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLinkage to HIV care and treatment following diagnosis is essential for the prompt initiation of antiretroviral therapy and for effective epidemic control. This evaluation assessed the level of linkage to HIV care and treatment among who tested HIV positive and explored contextual factors in Morogoro Region.MethodsA sequential explanatory mixed-methods design was used. Quantitative data of 392 individuals were collected from HIV testing registers containing data for the period of January 2024 to February 2025. Data were analysed descriptively using stata version 17.0. Qualitative data were collected using audio-recorded in-depth interviews with six purposively selected healthcare providers who provide HIV testing services at various testing points within the facilities. Transcripts underwent inductive coding and theming through interpretative phenomenological analysis managed in ATLAS.ti V.9ResultsOf the newly diagnosed clients, 90% were successfully linked to care and treatment, with 95% of those linked being enrolled on the same day of diagnosis. Key facilitators to linkage were high-quality pretest and posttest counselling, positive provider–client relationships, active phone follow-up, support from expert clients and mentor mothers and weekly departmental meetings. Major barriers included cultural beliefs among Maasai pastoralist communities, religious/spiritual beliefs favouring non-biomedical healing, unavailability of Care and Treatment Clinics services on weekends, stigma and distance to the clinics.ConclusionLinkage to HIV care in Morogoro was high but below universal targets, with same-day enrolment common. This was strengthened by counselling, follow-up mechanisms and peer support. However, linkage remains hindered by sociocultural beliefs, stigma and structural barriers such as service availability and distance. Authorities and implementers should strengthen extensive counselling and follow-up mechanisms, through the engagement of treatment advocates. These efforts should focus on addressing negative traditional beliefs, self-denial and HIV-related stigma.Keywords: HIV, Public Health, Public Health Practice

Introduction

HIV remains a major global public health concern, with a particularly significant burden in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), including Tanzania.1,3 In 2022, approximately 39.9 million people were living with HIV globally, the majority of whom (66%) were in the SSA, a region that accounted for about 60% of HIV-related death.3 In Tanzania, the national HIV prevalence stands at 4.4%, equivalent to approximately 1 548 000 adults and adolescents living with the virus, despite showing a decline from 4.7% in 2016.4 HIV testing services (HTS) are a critical entry point for prevention, care, treatment and support services for people living with HIV (PLHIV).1 2 5 These services aim to identify individuals with HIV and ensure their timely connection to care and treatment, which is crucial for epidemic control and improving health outcomes.2

Linkage to HIV care and treatment following diagnosis is a vital step in the HIV service continuum, ensuring that individuals who test positive are promptly initiated on antiretroviral therapy (ART), provided with ongoing medical support, and offered adherence counselling.6 7 Early linkage is strongly associated with improved clinical outcomes, reduced viral transmission and decreased mortality among PLHIV.6 7 Recognising this, Tanzania has implemented multiple strategies to ensure that all newly diagnosed individuals are promptly enrolled in care.8 9 However, despite the scale-up of HTS and the expansion of care and treatment clinics (CTCs), challenges persist, with many newly diagnosed clients still not effectively linked to treatment services.2 5 10 11

To address these challenges, the Ministry of Health, through the National AIDS, Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs) and Hepatitis Control Programme (NASHCOP), launched the 2022–2026 Strategic Plan aimed at ending AIDS as a public health threat by 2030.1 The plan emphasises differentiated HIV testing approaches and linkage to care for all HIV-positive individuals, regardless of testing modality. Priority strategies include index case testing, community-based testing, male-friendly services and client-initiated testing, with the expectation of achieving timely and successful linkage.2 5 12 Furthermore, the plan sets an ambitious target of 100% linkage of newly diagnosed PLHIV to care by 2025, supported by interventions such as escorted referrals, linkage case management (LCM) using expert clients and the formation of post-test clubs.1

Despite these initiatives, limited evidence exists on their effectiveness, particularly at the facility level in local contexts. Available studies have reported suboptimal linkage rates and highlighted multiple barriers hindering successful connection to care.27 9,12 Moreover, there is a paucity of updated data on how well the current strategies are functioning across different regions. This gap makes it important to assess the performance of linkage strategies in specific contexts, such as the Morogoro Region. Therefore, this evaluation examined the level of linkage to HIV care among newly diagnosed clients in Morogoro and explored the underlying factors influencing linkage performance in this setting. The study was guided by the socio-ecological model (SEM), which provides a comprehensive framework for understanding health behaviours across multiple interacting levels. This framework is particularly relevant for HIV care, where successful linkage to care is influenced not only by individual decision-making but also by broader social and structural contexts. The SEM informed both data collection and analysis by guiding the identification and interpretation of multiple level facilitators and barriers on linkage to care.

Methodology

Evaluation setting

This evaluation was conducted in Morogoro Municipal and Kilosa District in the Morogoro Region, located in the eastern part of Tanzania. The region has an HIV prevalence of 3.3%, reflecting a decline from 4.2% reported in 2016/2017.4 Given this epidemiological profile and the strategic importance of strengthening linkage to care, Morogoro Region provided a relevant and appropriate setting for assessing the implementation of HIV linkage strategies.

Evaluation design

A cross-sectional study design was employed, which is suitable for assessing the prevalence of outcomes at a specific point in time.13 14 This design allowed the evaluation to estimate the proportion of individuals successfully linked to HIV care and treatment services following diagnosis and explore the influencing factors. The use of a cross-sectional approach is consistent with previous studies on similar topics, underscoring its relevance and applicability.5 7 15

Evaluation approach

The evaluation followed a sequential explanatory mixed-methods approach, conducted in two phases: an initial quantitative phase followed by a qualitative phase. This approach facilitated integration of the findings, where qualitative data helped to explain and contextualise the quantitative results.16 17 The quantitative component primarily assessed the proportion and timeliness of linkage to HIV care among newly diagnosed clients. The qualitative component explored healthcare workers’ experiences and perceptions regarding the linkage process from HIV testing points to care and treatment services, thereby enriching the overall understanding of the findings.

Study population and sample

The study population comprised individuals tested HIV positive (between January 2024 and February 2025) for the quantitative component and healthcare workers providing HIV testing and linkage services for the qualitative component. For the quantitative part, the sample size was determined using the standard formula for calculating sample size for known and finite populations, given by n=N/(1+Ne2).18 19 The target population (N), which is the number of individuals on ART in the study area, was 3365, and the margin of error was set at 5%. This yielded an estimated sample of 384; however, we were able to collect data from 401 participants. For the qualitative part, the sample size was guided by the principle of data saturation, which is a recommended practice in qualitative research.20 Six healthcare providers were interviewed through in-depth interviews (IDIs), and data collection ceased once no new insights were emerging, indicating saturation had been reached.20 21

Sampling techniques

For the quantitative component, proportional allocation to size was used across the selected sites. Districts and health facilities were chosen using simple random sampling. Within facilities, systematic sampling was employed to select participants. The sampling frame consisted of the HTS register, which is organised chronologically by client attendance. The process began with the first newly diagnosed client listed in the HTS register for the study period of January 2024 to February 2025, followed by the inclusion of every alternate newly diagnosed client, ensuring a representative sample. Data for all individuals who were newly diagnosed with HIV in the period of January 2024 to February 2025, recorded in the HTS registers from different testing points in the sampled health facilities, were eligible for the study. Participants missing critical information on their HTS register records such as test results and key demographic characteristics were excluded from the study. The use of a small sampling interval (k=2) reduced the risk of systematic bias, while maintaining feasibility in high-volume clinical settings.

For the qualitative component, purposive sampling was applied to identify healthcare workers engaged in HIV testing and linkage activities at the selected health facilities. This approach ensured inclusion of participants from different testing points, both facility-based and community-based, and prioritised those with sufficient experience and knowledge relevant to linkage processes.22 All healthcare workers who worked in the HIV testing units in the sampled health facilities were eligible for the study. However, healthcare workers who had less than 12 months in the testing points were not eligible for the study. There were no participants who refused to participate in the study.

Variables and measurement

The study included variables derived from the HTS registers and related records. These comprised sociodemographic characteristics such as age, sex and facility location as well as key service-related variables including linkage to care (measured as enrolment into HIV care within the specified period) and duration of linkage (measured as the time between HIV diagnosis and enrolment into care). All variables were extracted in their recorded form from routine registers and, where necessary, categorised for analysis.

Data collection methods and instruments

Data collection was conducted sequentially, beginning with the quantitative phase, followed by the qualitative phase. For the quantitative component, data were obtained through systematic review of facility-based and programmatic records for the period of January 2024 to February 2025. Key secondary data sources included HTS registers, HTS referral forms, pre-ART registers, ART registers, LCM registers, CTC2 files and the CTC2 database. A structured data extraction checklist (online supplemental file 1—Data collection tools) was developed to capture relevant information aligned with the evaluation purpose. Before full-scale implementation, the checklist was piloted in one health facility to assess its clarity, completeness and ability to capture essential data. The final version of the tool was digitised using the Kobo Toolbox platform and deployed for data collection via the Kobo Collect Android application installed on a Samsung tablet.

For the qualitative component, data were collected through IDIs with healthcare providers directly involved in HIV testing and linkage services. A Kiswahili translated version of the interview guide (online supplemental file 1—Data collection tools) was used, as Kiswahili is the local language understood by all participants. The interview guide was piloted with two IDIs, after which it was refined by reordering questions and incorporating additional probes. Interviews were physically conducted at the health facilities in private, quiet rooms that provided a conducive environment for open discussion and ensured confidentiality. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. The interviews were facilitated by the principal investigator, a male who had a master's degree in health monitoring and evaluation, supported by a trained research assistant as the notetaker. The research assistant had a bachelor's degree in health systems management. The interviews took between 43 and 67 min to complete. Quantitative and qualitative data were collected in April 2025.

Data management and analysis

Quantitative data were cleaned, organised and analysed using stata version 17.0. Data cleaning included range checks, consistency checks, and verification of data completeness. Observations with missing values were excluded during data cleaning; a total of 9 observations were removed out of 401, resulting in a final analytic sample of 392 observations. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics which were used to summarise the socio-demographic characteristics of participants and to compute proportions related to successful linkage and duration of linkage. These included frequencies, percentages, medians, and IQRs, where appropriate. These analyses allowed for a clear understanding of the distribution of key variables, status and duration of linkage to care, and informed the subsequent qualitative exploration.

Qualitative data were transcribed verbatim within 24 hours of data collection, following best practice recommendations.23 Each transcript was reviewed and cross-checked by both the interviewer and note-taker to ensure accuracy. Swahili transcripts were then translated into English to facilitate systematic analysis, with care taken to preserve meaning and enhance the trustworthiness of the findings.22

An interpretative phenomenological analysis approach was employed, suitable for exploring lived experiences and perceptions of healthcare workers regarding linkage to HIV care.24 Data analysis followed an inductive and iterative approach, whereby codes and themes were derived from the data rather than being predetermined. Data analysis commenced concurrently with data collection and continued iteratively through repeated reading of transcripts, probing during interviews, and reflective memo writing.23 Data saturation was assessed through ongoing review of emerging codes and themes and was considered achieved when no new themes or substantive insights were identified in successive interviews.

Translated transcripts were uploaded into ATLAS.ti V.9 for systematic coding and analysis. Meaningful text segments were highlighted and assigned relevant codes, resulting in 22 distinct codes. Coding was conducted inductively, with codes developed directly from participants’ narratives and refined through constant comparison across transcripts. Concurrent memo writing captured the researcher’s reflections, contextual notes and emerging insights linked to the study codes and themes. Codes with related meanings were grouped into broader themes, representing key facilitators and barriers influencing linkage to care. Coding and theme development were done by the principal investigator. A code report generated from ATLAS.ti, containing themes, memos, codes and illustrative quotations, formed the foundation for reporting the qualitative findings. To enhance analytical rigour, coding and theme development were reviewed and discussed among members of the research team to ensure consistency and credibility of interpretations.

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness of the qualitative findings was ensured by adhering to the principles of credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability.22 23 Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with the data, iterative data collection and analysis and regular cross-checking of transcripts by the interviewer and note-taker. In addition, peer debriefing and team discussions were conducted during coding and theme development to ensure consistency and agreement on interpretations. Although formal member checking was not conducted, findings were grounded in verbatim quotations to reflect participants’ perspectives accurately.

Dependability was ensured by maintaining a clear audit trail of the research process, including documentation of data collection procedures, coding decisions and theme development. The use of ATLAS.ti facilitated systematic data management and transparency in analysis. Confirmability was strengthened through reflexivity, where researchers engaged in ongoing reflective memo writing and discussions to critically examine how their prior experience in HIV services in Tanzanian settings could influence data interpretation. These reflexive practices helped to minimise potential bias and ensure that findings were grounded in the data. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the study setting, participant characteristics and contextual factors, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the findings to similar settings.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study. However, the study addressed a public health issue of high community relevance linkage to HIV care and treatment following diagnosis. Insights were drawn from healthcare providers who interact closely with newly diagnosed clients, ensuring that the perspectives captured reflect real-world challenges and facilitators affecting patients’ access to care. The findings are intended to inform interventions that enhance patient engagement, reduce stigma and improve linkage to HIV services in the community.

Demographic characteristics of healthcare workers engaged in the study

The qualitative part of the study engaged six healthcare providers who participated in the IDIs. These participants had a mean age of 38.2 years and had an average working experience of 12.3 years. The participants worked at different HIV testing points namely as index, laboratory, antenatal care, inpatient and outpatient directory.

Proportion of linkage to CTCs among newly diagnosed HIV clients from January 2024 to February 2025 in Morogoro Region

The majority (90.0%, n=353) of newly diagnosed HIV in this study were successfully linked to CTC services. There was a statistically significant difference in the distribution between male and female, specifically, more female clients were successfully linked compared with male clients (95.5%, n=191 vs 84.3, n=162).

Timeliness of linkage of newly diagnosed clients from testing points to CTCs in selected health facilities in Morogoro Region

Among newly diagnosed HIV clients who were successfully linked to CTCs, the majority (95.0%, n=337) of them were linked on the same day that they received the test results. Three per cent (n=9) were linked on the second day, while 2.0% (n=7) were linked after the second day.

The healthcare workers’ experiences related to linkage of newly diagnosed HIV clients to CTCs

The analysis of six IDIs revealed two broad themes regarding factors facilitating the linkage of newly diagnosed HIV clients from testing points to CTCs. These themes are: facilitators towards linkage to care and treatment clinics among newly diagnosed HIV clients. Second, barriers towards linkage to care and treatment clinics among newly diagnosed HIV clients.

Qualitative findings: facilitators towards linkage to CTCs among newly diagnosed HIV clients

Provision of good pretest and post-test counselling

Healthcare workers emphasised the importance of high-quality counselling as a crucial determinant for successful linkage. They reported that when clients are well-informed about their HIV status and the benefits of enrolling in care and treatment services, they are more likely to accept linkage to CTC. One healthcare provider explained:

First, to successfully link a client, it starts right here at the testing point. As a provider, if you don’t give good counselling, it’s unlikely the client will agree to join CTC services. So, counselling is a key part. When you educate the client well and they understand why they should start treatment and its benefits, it makes it much easier for them to accept the services. (IDI 05).

Customer care and provider–client relationship

Healthcare providers reported that respectful communication, good language and quality services contributed to a positive relationship with clients. This relationship helped build trust and encouraged newly diagnosed clients to enrol in care. One provider stated:

Using good language with clients and having a good relationship are also factors towards linkage of HIV positive clients to CTCs. If a health care worker welcomes the client warmly, provides counselling well, uses polite language so the client understands, and gives them the chance to ask questions, all of this builds trust, which encourages the client to accept care and treatment services” (IDI 04).

Follow-up via phone calls

Some clients initially declined linkage despite counselling. Providers shared that obtaining clients’ phone numbers and conducting follow-up calls in the days after diagnosis helped increase acceptance of CTC services. As one participant explained:

Facility health care providers and community health workers, including expert clients we are also exchange phone numbers with the newly identified HIV positive clients and we keep in touch by communicate in the next days until clients accept CTC services, this method really helpfully in connecting clients to care (IDI 05)

Support from treatment advocates

Healthcare workers noted that expert clients and mentor mothers (‘mama vinara’) play a critical role in encouraging newly diagnosed clients to accept their status and begin treatment. Their testimonies offer real-life hope and reassurance. One provider stated:

Also, our expert clients help a lot in linking new identified positive to CTC. They are willing to share their own experiences, saying, I am HIV positive, I use ARV drugs, and I started in a certain year, but today I am healthy, and if you met me on the street, you wouldn’t know. As you’ve seen yourself, our expert clients are very healthy looking you wouldn’t know they are living with HIV so this helps build trust (IDI 05).

Regular departmental meetings with healthcare workers

Routine weekly meetings among healthcare providers were reported to be instrumental in strengthening linkage practices, especially for inpatient clients. One informant shared:

We also conducting frequent meetings in weekly bases, to review ward-level data of inpatient department and making follow-up on data of positive identified clients, sometimes we find clients have moved to other wards or discharged to home without linking to CTC, these close follow-ups help to reduce number of new diagnosed clients who not linked to CTC (IDI 03).

Barriers towards linkage to CTCs among newly diagnosed HIV clients

Five subthemes emerged from the analysis of the six translated in-depth transcripts (IDI) with healthcare providers. These sub-themes reflect the healthcare workers’ experiences regarding the barriers to linking newly diagnosed HIV clients to CTCs in the Morogoro Region. The sub-themes are described below:

Pastoralist community beliefs

Healthcare providers reported that cultural beliefs among pastoralist communities, particularly the Maasai, pose significant challenges to linkage. These clients often reject their HIV-positive results and refuse to initiate ART. Healthcare workers described persistent misconceptions among these communities, including a belief that HIV does not affect the Maasai. Despite adequate pre-test and post-test counselling, some individuals refused linkage, even threatening healthcare workers in extreme cases. One provider shared the following:

The Maasai are a major challenge because they completely refuse care and treatment services. They don’t believe they can get HIV, but believe they can get TB, and if diagnosed with TB, they accept TB medication easily and take it properly, but not HIV medication. A service provider can even be threatened. I was once threatened with a knife here at the facility by a Maasai man who came with his wife for antenatal services. After we tested them, they were both HIV positive (IDI 04).

Another provider explained through atypical example:

There was a …. Maasai woman we diagnosed with both HIV and syphilis. She refused to start ARVs, saying her husband must come first. When we contacted him, he refused to come. The woman later returned for antenatal follow-up, and we reminded her to start both treatments. She accepted syphilis treatment but still refused ART. Currently, she has disappeared. This is common for pastoralist groups, who are also move place to place and hard to trace (IDI 06).

Religious beliefs

Some clients declined care due to religious beliefs, opting instead to rely on prayer for healing. Healthcare providers noted that even with appropriate counselling, certain individuals expressed strong faith-based convictions that God would cure them, making them unwilling to start ART. A provider shared:

But there are others who completely refuse because they don’t believe it. For example, I had one client who came in very confident, saying I’m fine, I want to test because I’m about to get married. Because he was so confident, when I tried to counsel him, he felt like I was wasting his time. In the end, his test results showed he was HIV positive. Even now, I don’t know if he ever started treatment because after that he told me he went back to his church, prayed, and believed he was healed (IDI 05).

Unavailability of CTC services on weekends

Several providers reported that most CTCs do not operate on weekends, creating a missed opportunity to link clients diagnosed during weekend testing. While HTS especially at the laboratory and outpatient are available daily, clients diagnosed on Saturdays or Sundays are often advised to return on Monday, which many fail to do. As one provider explained:

Yes, among of the factors hindering linkage is CTC services are not offered on weekends particularly Saturday and Sunday. If a client is diagnosed in the laboratory on a weekend, they may be asked to return on Monday, and they don’t come back. That’s a client’s missed opportunity of being linked to CTC (IDI 01).

Distance to care and treatment clinics

Healthcare providers also identified distance as a logistical barrier. Some clients receive HTS far from their residence, making it difficult for them to return for enrolment at that facility. While referral forms are provided for enrolment at nearby CTCs, providers reported that in practice, clients are often retested at the new facility or choose not to follow through with linkage altogether. One participant noted:

Sometimes a client comes here for testing but prefers to start treatment at another facility closer to home. However, some of them end up not enrolling at all. (IDI 05)

Stigma

Stigma emerged as a prominent theme, manifesting at both the personal and community levels. Some clients were reported to struggle with self-acceptance following diagnosis, leading to refusal of services. Others avoided linkage due to fear of being seen accessing services at CTCs, especially when the CTC is located near high-traffic areas like outpatient, the laboratory or the reproductive and child health wings. One healthcare provider shared an experience highlighting the structural challenges related to the nature and spacing of HIV care from other clinical care services:

One major challenge is the small size of our facility. The buildings are very close together, and some clients do not want others to know their HIV status. The CTC room is located along a busy corridor that also houses the RCH room and the laboratory, which makes some clients uncomfortable and reluctant to enroll (IDI 02)

Discussion

This study provides a mixed-methods understanding of linkage to HIV care in Morogoro, showing a relatively high linkage rate (90%) alongside important contextual barriers that prevent universal coverage (table 2). While global and national guidelines emphasise immediate linkage to care following diagnosis,1 10 25 achieving this consistently remains a challenge across many high-burden settings.26,9 A key contribution of this study is the integration of quantitative outcomes with qualitative insights, demonstrating that high linkage performance is not solely a function of service availability but may also be strongly shaped by provider practices, client perceptions and local sociocultural dynamics. Guided by the SEM, these findings highlight that linkage to care is influenced by interacting factors at individual, interpersonal, community and health system levels.26

The observed linkage rate, although below the national target of 100% under the NASHCOP Strategic Plan 2022–2026, is higher than reports from similar settings in Tanzania and SSA. Studies in Mwanza and Mbeya have reported lower linkage rates ranging from 52.3% to 78%,7 8 11 while a study conducted in Cape Town South Africa documented 63.1% linkage.15 This comparatively strong performance suggests that programmatic factors in the study area, such as effective counselling, active follow-up and peer support which were highlighted in the qualitative exploration, may be contributing to improved outcomes. These findings reinforce existing evidence that health system responsiveness and provider engagement are critical determinants of successful linkage8 15 27 28 and highlight the added value of combining quantitative estimates with qualitative explanations to better understand performance differences across settings. From the SEM perspective, these facilitators operate at the interpersonal and health system levels, demonstrating how supportive provider-client interactions and organised service delivery structures can enhance linkage outcomes.

Despite the high overall linkage, the study highlights a critical gap in achieving universal same-day linkage, 5% of clients experiencing delays while 10% not linking at all. While 95% same-day linkage is commendable and compares favourably with findings from Mwanza and Mbeya,7 11 the remaining delays warrant closer interpretation. While certain clinical conditions (like Tuberculosis or severe illness comorbidities) may necessitate delay linkage, the qualitative findings suggest that these delays are not random but are driven by identifiable factors, including service-level constraints such as the unavailability of CTC services on weekends as well as logistical challenges like distance to facilities. In addition, some clients required time to process their diagnosis despite receiving counselling, indicating that immediate readiness for treatment initiation may vary. These findings suggest that achieving 100% same-day linkage requires not only strong service delivery systems but also flexible, client-centred approaches that accommodate individual readiness and structural barriers.6 29 Within the SEM framework, these delays reflect interactions between individual-level readiness, health system constraints and broader structural factors influencing access to care.26

The study also identified key facilitators that help explain the relatively high linkage rates observed. High-quality counselling emerged as a central factor, enabling clients to understand their diagnosis and the benefits of early treatment initiation. This aligns with prior studies emphasising counselling as a cornerstone of the HIV care cascade.8 27 28 Furthermore, positive provider–client relationships and respectful communication were found to build trust and encourage engagement in care, consistent with findings from Mbeya.2 8 The role of peer support, through expert clients and treatment advocates, was useful for providing relatable experiences and emotional reassurance that facilitated acceptance of HIV status and treatment initiation. Similar evidence from Tanzania and other SSA settings highlights the effectiveness of peer-led interventions in improving linkage and retention in care.5 27 30 These findings underscore the importance of strengthening interpersonal and community-based components of HIV service delivery alongside clinical interventions. These facilitators span multiple SEM levels, especially interpersonal (provider–client relationships, peer support) and community levels, stressing the need for multilevel interventions to sustain high linkage rates.

Importantly, this study highlights context-specific barriers that help explain why a proportion of clients were not linked to care and the delays in linkage. Cultural beliefs among Maasai pastoralist communities, including denial of HIV susceptibility, were identified as a significant barrier, often leading to outright refusal of treatment. This finding is consistent with studies in Mbeya, where denial and disbelief in HIV diagnosis have been reported as major obstacles to care.2 Similarly, religious beliefs were found to influence health-seeking behaviour, with some clients opting for prayer or spiritual healing instead of biomedical treatment. These beliefs, while deeply rooted, contribute to delayed or missed linkage despite the availability of services. Together, these findings emphasise that gaps in linkage are not solely due to health system failures but are also shaped by sociocultural and belief systems.30 An important implication emerging from this finding is the potential risk to healthcare workers operating in contexts of strong cultural resistance. Reports of hostility and threats, although not widespread, highlight the need to consider provider safety as part of HIV service delivery in such settings. From the SEM perspective, these barriers operate primarily at the community level, interacting with individual beliefs to influence health-seeking behaviours and decisions regarding HIV care. Addressing this requires culturally sensitive approaches, including sustained engagement with community and traditional leaders to build trust and improve acceptance of HIV services.

A notable finding in this study was the higher linkage to care among female clients compared with males. This pattern is consistent with the existing literature in SSA and Tanzania, where men are often less likely to engage in HIV care services.2 7 8 31 Several factors may explain this disparity, including differences in health-seeking behaviour, with men generally less likely to access health services promptly, as well as gender norms that discourage care-seeking among men.31 In contrast, women may have more frequent contact with health services, particularly through antenatal and reproductive health programmes, which facilitates earlier and more consistent engagement in care.

Study limitations

While the study reported both quantitative and qualitative findings, the quantitative component relied on secondary data extracted from facility registers, which may have been subject to incompleteness or recording errors, potentially introducing bias. The qualitative findings were based on a small sample of healthcare workers drawn from selected facilities within Morogoro Region, which may limit the transferability of the results to other settings. In addition, although the study provides information on individuals who were not linked to care, the qualitative component relied solely on healthcare provider perspectives. Patient perspectives—specifically from those who were not successfully linked to care—were not included, which could have provided deeper insights into individual-level and experiential barriers to linkage. Nevertheless, the study offers valuable insights into linkage to HIV care, and contextual factors influencing the linkage practices.

Conclusion

While linkage performance in Morogoro Region was relatively strong compared with some prior regional reports, it fell short of the universal same-day linkage target that desires 100% same-day linkage. Key facilitators included quality counselling, strong provider–client relationships, follow-up via phone contacts and support from expert clients and mentor mothers, while barriers included cultural and religious beliefs, weekend service gaps, stigma and distance to facilities.

Recommendations

Authorities and implementers should adopt multilevel interventions guided by the SEM to strengthen linkage to HIV care. At the individual level, efforts should focus on enhancing client understanding, readiness and acceptance through comprehensive counselling. At the interpersonal level, strengthening provider–client relationships and expanding the role of treatment advocates and peer supporters is essential to improve follow-up and trust. At the community level, targeted engagement with religious and traditional leaders is needed to address harmful beliefs, reduce stigma, HIV services refusal and promote positive health-seeking behaviours. At the health system level, improving service accessibility, including availability of care on weekends and strengthening follow-up mechanisms, is critical. This may include introducing linkage ‘on-call’ shifts for healthcare providers to facilitate immediate enrolment of clients diagnosed during weekends as well as implementing digital referral and tracking systems to ensure continuity of care and timely follow-up across facilities. Together, these coordinated strategies will enhance timely linkage and sustained engagement in HIV care and treatment.
