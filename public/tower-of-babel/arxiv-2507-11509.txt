On the Complexity of the Optimal Correlated Equilibria in Extensive-Form Games
Vincent Cheval, Florian Horn, Soumyajit Paul, Mahsa Shirmohammadi

Abstract
A major open question in algorithmic game theory is whether normal-form correlated equilibria (NFCE) can be computed efficiently in succinct games such as extensive-form games [ DFF+25 , PR24 , FP23 , HvS08 , VSF08 , PR08 ] . Motivated by this question, we study the associated Threshold problem: deciding whether there exists a correlated equilibrium whose value exceeds a given threshold. We prove that this problem is 𝐏𝐒𝐏𝐀𝐂𝐄 \mathbf{PSPACE} -hard for NFCE in multiplayer extensive-form games with perfect recall, even for fixed thresholds. To contextualize this result, we also establish the complexity of the Threshold problem for Nash equilibria in this setting, showing it is ∃ ℝ \exists\mathbb{R} -complete. These results uncover a surprising complexity reversal: while optimal correlated equilibria are computationally simpler than optimal Nash in normal-form games, the opposite holds in extensive-form games, where computing optimal correlated equilibria is provably harder. Building on this line of inquiry, we also address a related question by [ VSF08 , Discussion and open problems] , who introduced the notions of extensive-form correlated equilibrium (EFCE) and agent-form correlated equilibrium (AFCE). They asked how difficult the Threshold problem is for AFCE; we answer this question by proving that it is 𝐍𝐏 \mathbf{NP} -hard, even in two-player games without chance nodes. Complementing our hardness results, we establish tight complexity classifications for the Threshold problem across several correlated equilibrium concepts—including EFCE, AFCE, normal-form coarse correlated equilibrium (NFCCE), extensive-form coarse correlated equilibria (EFCCE), and agent-form coarse correlated equilibrium (AFCCE). For each of these solution concepts in multiplayer stochastic extensive-form games with perfect recall, we prove 𝐍𝐏 \mathbf{NP} -completeness by providing matching 𝐍𝐏 \mathbf{NP} upper bounds to the previously known hardness results. Together, our results provide the most complete landscape to date for the complexity of optimal equilibrium computation in extensive-form games.

Introduction

Game theory provides a powerful framework for modeling and predicting the behavior of individuals or groups interacting in competitive or cooperative settings. Originally developed to understand strategic decision-making, it has found applications across diverse disciplines, including economics, political science, and the social sciences, where it serves to analyze and design systems involving multiple rational agents. With the advent of artificial intelligence and machine learning, game-theoretic concepts have also gained prominence in computational contexts. For instance, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

and security protocols whose security depends on rational behavior

[

Correlated equilibrium can be viewed as the outcome of a recommendation mechanism: a joint strategy is sampled from a publicly known distribution, and each player privately receives an action recommendation

Extensive-Form Games, Correlations and Equilibria

We study
multi-player stochastic extensive-form games with imperfect information
.
Such a game is played on a rooted, directed tree, whose nodes are controlled
either by
chance
or by one of the participating players.
The game involves
n
players, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
is an input parameter, and each player is identified by a unique integer in
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
The game starts at the root node and proceeds in turns: at a controlled node
v
, the player selects an action
a
from a set
\mathcal{A}
of available actions, leading deterministically to a unique successor
v^{\prime}
, denoted by
v\xrightarrow{a}v^{\prime}
.
At a chance node, the successor
v^{\prime}
is chosen randomly according to a fixed probability distribution, denoted by
v\xrightarrow{x}v^{\prime}
with
x\in\mathbb{Q}
.
The leaf nodes in the game tree are associated with a tuple in
\mathbb{Q}^{n}
, referred to as the
payoff
or
utility vector
, which specifies the payoff received by each player when the game terminates at that leaf.
We define a linear function
\omega:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, named the
objective function
of the game,
which maps the tuple of expected payoff of players to a collective objective value. The objective function is
typically used to capture criteria such as social welfare, which is defined as the expected sum of players’ reward.
For algorithmic purposes, we assume all probabilities and payoffs are rational numbers encoded in binary.

Correlated Equilibria: Normal-form and Extensive-Form.

A
pure strategy
for player
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
is a function
\sigma_{i}
that assigns to each information set
I\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
of the player, one of the actions available at
I
.
A (pure)
strategy profile
is a tuple
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
, where each
\sigma_{i}
is a pure strategy of player
i
.
Let
\Sigma_{i}
denote the set of pure strategies for player
i
, and let
\Sigma=\Sigma_{1}\times\cdots\times\Sigma_{n}
be the set of all pure strategy profiles.
A
correlated plan

\mu
is a distribution over
\Sigma
.

We investigate several types of equilibria in extensive-form games, for instance
normal-form correlated equilibria
(NFCE) and
extensive-form correlated equilibria
(EFCE). Both rely on a
mediator
that provides recommendations through a publicly known correlated plan. At the game’s start, the mediator draws a strategy profile from the correlated plan and gives each player private action recommendations. A correlated plan is an equilibrium if no player has incentive to deviate, assuming all other player adhere to the given recommendations.
The primary distinction between these equilibria is in the timing of the mediator’s recommendations. In NFCE, after drawing a pure strategy profile
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
, the mediator privately communicates each player’s full strategy
\sigma_{i}
before the game begins.
This means that each player is informed of their entire set of recommendations before committing to the equilibrium. In contrast, in EFCE, the mediator reveals the recommended action to each player only when they reach a relevant information set during the game,
allowing players to condition their actions on observed play and the recommendation received. As a result, players update their beliefs and evaluate the expected utility of following the recommendation based on the current information.

While our focus in the main body of the paper is primarily on NFCE and EFCE, we also study other equilibria, such as agent-form correlated equilibrium (AFCE) which is similar to EFCE except that players are only allow to deviate at one single information set and thus must follow recommendation after deviation. The coarse variants of each of these equilibria require player to commit to recommendation
before
receiving them. See
Appendix

B
for the formal definitions of these equilibria concepts.

a variable for each possible pure strategy profile in the support of the correlation plan, write out all the incentive constraints and then solve the resulting system.

For example, the expected payoff of the company is 5 when always accepting the applicant, that is, when playing
\{I_{E}\mapsto A_{E},I_{G}\mapsto A_{G}\}
. Such strategy is dominated by
\{I_{E}\mapsto R_{E},I_{G}\mapsto R_{G}\}
with an expected payoff of
6
for the company, and thus is never played.

Now consider a case where the applicant receives from the mediator the recommendation to play the strategy
\{I_{S}\mapsto E_{S},I_{W}\mapsto E_{W}\}
. Let

be the probabilities of the pure strategy profiles

. Hence for the correlation plan to be a

p\geq p^{\prime}
.
Applying this kind of reasoning to all possible deviations ultimately forces the company to adopt

We now extend our construction to show that the

The key idea is to generalize the coNP-hardness reduction by encoding quantifier alternation into the game’s structure, while preserving the incentive constraints that enforce uniform sampling over certain assignments (rather than all).
Let
\Phi
be a quantified Boolean formula over
n

Q_{1}x_{1}\,Q_{2}x_{2}\,\cdots,\,Q_{n}x_{n}\,\varphi(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})\,,

where
Q_{i}\in\{\forall,\exists\}
and
\varphi
is in DNF.
We construct a polynomial-size extensive-form game
G
from
\Phi
such that

G
has a correlated equilibrium with social welfare at least 3 if and only if
\Phi
is true.

The structure of
G
builds on the coNP case, with following key modifications: only universally quantified variables are assigned a
universal
player and an uncertainty gadget; these player have knowledge gadgets for all variables, existential or universal, appearing earlier in the quantifier prefix. Thus we consider a
\forall_{i}
-player if and only if
Q_{i}=\forall
, and such
\forall_{i}
-player then controls the information sets
x_{i}
-uncertainty
,
\forall_{i}
-knowing-
x_{1}
, …,
\forall_{i}
-knowing-
x_{i-1}
as defined in the coNP case.
This mirrors the logical dependencies in
\Phi
and ensures that a deviation is unprofitable only if the equilibrium enforces uniform sampling consistent with the quantifier structure. The assignment-player and
\varphi
-player retain their previous roles, while the
\forall_{i}
-players now ensure quantifier-respecting uniformity over relevant assignments.

Beyond Chance: From Stochastic to Non-Stochastic Games.

To eliminate the chance nodes from our game, we begin by lifting all probabilistic choices to the root of the game tree; this leaves us with a single chance node to remove. We then generalize the gadget introduced by von Stengel and Forges
[
VSF08
]
, originally used to prove the
\mathbf{NP}
-hardness of
NFCE
in two-player games without chance nodes.
In their construction
[
VSF08
, Figure 9]
, the chance node is replaced with a pre-play zero-sum pursuit-evasion game between the two players, forcing the first player to completely mix over choices
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots

[
VSF08
, Figure 10]
.
In our setting, which involves more than two players, this construction must be adapted. First, we introduce a new player
R
who is responsible for making the choices
c_{i}
. Then, for each of the remaining players (assignment, universal, and formula players), we successively add a zero-sum pursuit-evasion game between that player and player
R
. See
Figure

6
in
Appendix

D
for a schematic illustration of this process, showing the successive applications of the von Stengel and Forges gadget to eliminate chance from the construction in

2

In normal-form games, the
Threshold
problem for correlated equilibria reduces to solving a linear system over the rationals, with a variable for each pure strategy profile and incentive constraints encoded as linear inequalities.
Since the number of variables and constraints is polynomial in the game size, the problem admits a polynomial-time solution.
This approach, however, does not extend to extensive-form games, where the number of pure strategy profiles grows exponentially with the number of information sets.
To address this, previous works
[
FS20
,
FBS20
,
VSF08
,
KMv96
]
have focused on developing more compact representations of correlation plans using the notion of histories, particularly, what are known as relevant histories.

More concretely, von Stengel and Forges
[
VSF08
]
showed that to verify whether a correlation plan
\mu
is an EFCE in 2-player extensive-form games without chance nodes, it suffices to consider the probabilities that
\mu
assigns to certain combinations of player histories—those that are relevant. A history, also known as a sequence in the literature, is commonly understood as a sequence of information sets and actions that describe the path a player takes through the game tree. It can be viewed as a partial pure strategy, encapsulating the player’s decisions within a specific information context.
The relevant histories, in comparison, represent joint behaviors of the players along the game tree and are meant to provide enough information to encode both the correlation plan and the EFCE constraints.

In the definition given by von Stengel and Forges
[
VSF08
]
,
relevant histories are defined as a subset of pairs
(h_{1},h_{2})
, with
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
representing the respective histories of players
1
and
2
, such that the last information sets in these histories, say
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
, lie along the same path from the root to some node in the game tree.

Given a correlation plan
\mu
, the probability
p_{(h_{1},h_{2})}
of reaching a pair of histories
(h_{1},h_{2})
is intuitively the probability of reaching any
node whose path prefixes for the two players are exactly
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
,
respectively.
Given such correlation plans
\mu
, let
\boldsymbol{p}_{\mu}
denote the tuple of values

(p_{(h_{1},h_{2})})
, where
(h_{1},h_{2})
ranges over all relevant histories.
The set of all
\boldsymbol{p}_{\mu}
can be captured by a system
\mathcal{S}
consisting of polynomially many linear equalities
[
VSF08
]
.
Moreover, they also showed that relevant histories can fully characterize the incentive constraints of EFCE using polynomially many inequalities. Solving these linear constraints therefore yields a polynomial time procedure for solving the
Threshold
problem for 2-player game without chance node.

It was shown in
[
FS20
]
that the same definition of relevant histories yields polynomial-time algorithms for computing optimal EFCE in triangle-free games—a class that, in particular, includes games with public chance moves.
Several subsequent works
[

FS20

]

have used a geometric interpretation by viewing correlation plans and solutions to the system

, where
H_{i}
denotes the set of histories of player
i
.

Extending this approach to more general settings faces two major challenges:

•

First, in the presence of chance nodes, the solutions to the system
\mathcal{S}
no longer necessarily correspond to a valid correlation plan, breaking the soundness of the proposed formulation. See
[
VSF08
, Figure 3]
) for an example illustrating this issue. However, in the case of triangle-free games, the definition of relevant histories remained sound as the solutions to the system
\mathcal{S}
still correspond to valid correlation plans
[
FS20
]
.

•

Second, even without chance nodes, natural extensions of the notion of relevant histories to

-player games can result in an exponentially large set of such histories. For instance,
the extension proposed in
[
FBS20
]
defines relevant histories as

, requiring that for all

(h_{i},h_{j})

6
Discussion and Open Questions

Our results provide new insights into the representational complexity (also known as strategy complexity) of optimal correlated equilibria in extensive-form games, particularly when the number of players is part of the input. An important aspect of this complexity is how large the support of an equilibrium must be.

For equilibrium notions such as
EFCE
,
EFCCE
,
NFCCE
,
AFCE
, and
AFCCE
, we show that the support size can be bounded polynomially without loss of expressiveness. More precisely, for any equilibrium of one of these types, there exists an equivalent equilibrium with support consisting of a polynomial number of strategy profiles, which induces the same distribution over the leaf nodes and thus the same payoffs. This result enables NP-membership for the
Threshold
problem under these notions. Related to this, a pressing question in line with the study of
[
FS20
]
, is the following:

•

Which natural, tractable subclasses of games admit a polynomial-time solution to the
Threshold
problem for correlated equilibrium notions?

In stark contrast with the above notions, we showed that this support-size compressibility does not extend to
NFCE
. We construct a family of games where optimal
NFCE
necessarily require exponential-size support to realize their distribution over outcomes (no equivalent equilibrium exists over a small support). This distinction raises a deeper algorithmic question:

•

Could this exponential blowup in representation be a fundamental bottleneck to obtaining a polynomial-time algorithm for the
Any
-
NFCE
problem?

Finally, the complexity landscape for
NFCE
remains open in the setting of two-player games, or games with a constant number of players. Our PSPACE-hardness result for optimal
NFCE
does not yet extend to these restricted cases. A natural first step would be to identify families of two-player games where optimal
NFCE
requires exponential support:

•

Is the
Threshold
problem for
NFCE
in general extensive-form games
\mathbf{PSPACE}
-complete or
\mathbf{EXP}
-complete?

•

Does the
Threshold
problem for
NFCE
remain
\mathbf{PSPACE}
-hard in the two-player (or fixed-player) setting?

Appendix B
Correlated Equilibria in Extensive-form games

In this paper, we study 6 notions of correlated equilibrium in extensive-form games, in addition to Nash equilibrium: namely,
NFCE
,
EFCE
, and
AFCE
, along with their respective coarse variants—
NFCCE
,
EFCCE
, and
AFCCE
. In the remainder of this section, we formally define these equilibria, starting with Nash equilibrium.

For convenience, we fix the following parameters throughout this section:
a correlation plan
\mu\in\Delta(\Sigma)
, a pure strategy profile
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in\Sigma
, and a leaf node
v\in\mathcal{L}
.

B.1
Normal Form (Coarse) Correlated Equilibrium.

In
NFCE
, the mediator privately recommends a complete strategy to each player before the game begins. Let
\alpha
be such a recommended strategy to the
i
-th player, with
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Define
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},v\mid\alpha)
as the
probability that the mediator draws
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
under
\mu
and player
i
reaches
v
, conditional on this player being recommended
\alpha
. Formally,

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},v\mid\alpha)=\mathds{1}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}[v]\,P_{C}(v)\,\frac{\mu({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\,\mathds{1}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}[\alpha]}{\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}\in\Sigma}\mu({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime})\,\mathds{1}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}}[\alpha]}

Observe that
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},v\mid\alpha)
forms a distribution over
\Sigma\times\mathcal{L}
.
Accordingly, the expected payoff of player
i
under
\mu
, conditional on this player being recommended
\alpha
is given by

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(U_{i}\mid\alpha)=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in\Sigma}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{P}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},v\mid\alpha)\,u_{i}(v)

In what follows, we adopt analogous definitions and maintain consistent notation for expected payoffs across different conditional events, omitting repeated definitions for brevity.
To express the expected payoff when player
i
aims to deviate by playing strategy
\beta
,
write
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},v\mid\alpha\leadsto\beta)
as the
probability that the mediator draws
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
under
\mu
and player
i
reaches
v
, conditioned on player
i
receiving the recommendation
\alpha
and deciding to deviate by playing
\beta
. Formally, for all
\beta\in\Sigma_{i}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},v\mid\alpha)=\mathds{1}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}[\beta]}[v]\,P_{C}(v)\,\frac{\mu({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\,\mathds{1}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}[\alpha]}{\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}\in\Sigma}\mu({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime})\,\mathds{1}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{\prime}}[\alpha]}

This equation intuitively indicates that instead of considering the node that can be reached by
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
(i.e.,
\mathds{1}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}[v]
), we should consider the nodes that can be reached by
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}[\beta]
(i.e.,
\mathds{1}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}[\beta]}[v]
).

A correlation plan
\mu
is a
NFCE
when for all
\beta\in\Sigma_{i}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(U_{i}\mid\sigma_{i})\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(U_{i}\mid\sigma_{i}\leadsto\beta)

(NFCE)

In
NFCCE
, the player must decide to deviate before receiving its recommended strategy. This can be expressed by considering only the expected payoff over
\mu
. Define

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}},v\mid\leadsto\beta)=\mu({\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\,P_{C}(v)\,\mathds{1}_{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}[\beta]}[v]

as the
probability that the mediator draws
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
under
\mu
and player
i
reaches
v
, conditional on player
i
deciding to deviate by playing
\beta
.
A correlation plan
\mu
is a
NFCCE
when, for all
\beta\in\Sigma_{i}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(U_{i})\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(U_{i}\mid\leadsto\beta)

(NFCCE)

Assignment Tree

An
assignment
involves choosing boolean values for each variable from
Z
. Formally, an assignment is a function
\theta:Z\mapsto\{\top,\bot\}
.
We write
\theta\vDash\varphi
, to denote
\theta
satisfies
\varphi
, and
\theta\vDash t
to denote
\theta
satisfies the term
t
.
There is an order
\prec
on the quantified variables in the formula
\Phi
, in which an assignment
\theta
assigns values. Hence, it is natural to visualize the set of all assignments as a rooted tree, the
assignment tree
, that follows the quantification ordering.

The
assignment tree

\Gamma
of
\Phi
is a rooted complete binary tree of height
2n
. Each non-leaf node is labelled with a variable from
Z
. Every node at the same level are labelled with the same variable. The root is labelled with
x_{1}
.
All children of a node labelled with
x_{i}
are labelled with
y_{i}
. All children of a node labelled with
y_{i}
for
i<n
are labelled with
x_{i+1}
. For a non-leaf node
v
, we denote by
\mathit{var}(v)\in Z
, the label of node
v
. Note that the leaves are unlabelled. All non-leaf node must have two outgoing edges labelled by
\top
and
\bot
respectively, representing possible truth values. Thus, any node
v
in
\Gamma
(including a leaf) is uniquely determined by the sequence of edge labels on the path from root to
v
, which is also a partial assignment of variables in
Z
upto
\mathit{var}(v)
. Equivalently, any node
v
with

uniquely determines a partial assignment
\theta_{<z}
with values up to
z
. As a consequence, there is a one-to-one correspondence between (complete) assignments and leafs of
\Gamma
. We will use assignments and leaves of
\Gamma
interchangeably.

A set of leaves
L
induces a sub-tree
\Gamma_{L}
of same height containing all paths from root to leaves only from
L
.
For a node
v
, let
\Gamma_{v\rightarrow\top}
denotes the subtree containing all leaf nodes passing through
v
and the edge
\top
from
v
.
\Gamma_{v\rightarrow\bot}
is defined similarly.

Minimal Explicit Proof

An

of a formula
\Phi
is a set of leaves

in
\Gamma
satisfying
\varphi
, such that, for all nodes

in
\Gamma_{P}

is labelled by a universal variable,

must have two children in
\Gamma_{P}
.
We say that an explicit proof is

when for every node
v
, whenever
v
is labelled by an existential variable,
v
must have exactly one child in
\Gamma_{P}
.

A formula

Beyond Chance: from Stochastic to Non-Stochastic Games.

To eliminate the chance nodes from our game, we begin by lifting all probabilistic choices to the root of the game tree, leaving us with a single chance node to remove. We then generalize the gadget introduced by von Stengel and Forges
[
VSF08
]
, originally used to prove the
\mathbf{NP}
-hardness of
NFCE
in 2-player games without chance nodes.
In their construction
[
VSF08
, Figure 9]
, the chance node is replaced with a pre-play zero-sum pursuit-evasion game between the two players, forcing the first player to completely mix over choices
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots

[
VSF08
, Figure 10]
.
In our setting, which involves more than two players, this construction must be adapted. First, we introduce a new player
R
who is responsible for making the choices
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots
. Then, for each of the remaining players (assignment, universal, and formula players), we successively add a zero-sum pursuit-evasion game between that player and player
R
. In order to guarantee that all players have incentive to deviate when the randomization is incorrect, we modify the payoffs so that their expected utility becomes 0:

•

In the uncertainty gadgets, the payoffs for the universal players become +1 if they guess correctly, 0 if they do not guess at all, and -1 if they guess incorrectly.

•

Instead of the formula player getting a payoff of
2
when a universal player plays
\checkmark
in a uncertainty gadget, a payoff of
3
when a universal player plays correctly in a knowledge gadget, and a payoff of
3
in when the formula player chooses a suitable term, the new random player gets a payoff of
2
in all these cases.

See
Figure

6
for a schematic illustration of this process, showing the successive applications of the von Stengel and Forges gadget to eliminate chance from the construction in
Figure

2
. This transformation ensures that the formula
\Phi
is true if and only if there exists an NFCE with a social welfare of
2
.

A

\Sigma_{i}

A

A Nash equilibrium is a mixed strategy profile

\alpha_{i}

\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\forall\beta\in\Delta(\Sigma_{i}),\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(U_{i})\geq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}[\beta]}(U_{i})

(
Nash
)

\boldsymbol{\alpha}[\beta]=(\alpha,\ldots,\alpha_{i-1},\beta,\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})

\prod_{i=1}^{n}\,\prod_{(I,a)\in\mathsf{h}_{i}(v)}\beta_{i}(I)(a)

[EFCE - Best deviation payoff computation]

Let
