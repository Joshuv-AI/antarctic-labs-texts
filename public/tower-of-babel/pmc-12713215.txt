Association between diastolic blood pressure and post-stroke cognitive impairment: a real-world retrospective cohort study
Authors: Defei Chen, Tailin Ran, Yuhui Li, Zheng Yang, Weilin Tan, Qiuyi Lu, Lanxin Tang, Fu Song, Lining Yang, Dingqun Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) is a common complication after stroke, and blood pressure management is vital for stroke survivors. However, the association between diastolic blood pressure (DBP) during post-stroke rehabilitation and the risk of PSCI remains unclear. This study aims to evaluate this association in a real-world stroke cohort.MethodsA total of 508 consecutive stroke patients admitted between May 2022 and March 2024 were included in this study. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify risk factors for PSCI. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to explore the association between DBP and PSCI. Logistic regression models were used to evaluate the risk of PSCI in different DBP groups. Subgroup analysis was employed to assess the effect of DBP across different subgroups.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 5.5 months, 41% of patients were identified as having PSCI. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses indicated that low DBP is an independent risk factor for PSCI (odds ratio [OR] = 0.960 by 1 mmHg, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.933–0.988, p < 0.01). RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear relationship between DBP and PSCI incidence, with an inflection point of around 80 mmHg (P overall < 0.001, P nonlinear = 0.002). Logistic regression models suggested a higher risk of PSCI in the group with DBP < 75 mmHg compared with the group with 75–80 mmHg (OR = 2.606, 95% CI = 1.445–4.699, p < 0.01).ConclusionDBP during post-stroke rehabilitation is associated with the incidence of PSCI in a non-linear manner. Specifically, a low DBP level during this period is an independent risk factor for PSCI. Maintaining a DBP above 80 mmHg during rehabilitation is beneficial for preventing PSCI.

Background

Stroke is a leading cause of mortality worldwide and is frequently associated with prolonged disability [1]. As the global population of stroke survivors increases, there is a growing focus on ameliorating the long-term outcomes following stroke [2]. Post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) represents one of the most common disabilities, with incidence rates ranging from 20 to 80% depending on factors such as country, ethnicity, and diagnostic criteria [3,4]. Research indicates that approximately two-thirds of middle-aged and elderly stroke patients will experience cognitive impairment, with one-third at risk of progressing to dementia [5]. Beyond its impact on social participation and quality of life, PSCI is strongly associated with recurrent stroke and poor functional outcomes [6]. Despite the high prevalence of PSCI, current therapeutic options have limited efficacy. Consequently, the early detection and management of modifiable risk factors are essential strategies for the prevention and treatment of PSCI [4,6].

Blood pressure (BP) management is integral to the prevention and treatment of stroke. In the context of acute stroke, BP is a critical determinant of cerebral blood flow (CBF), and optimizing BP may enhance patient outcomes by improving reperfusion and preserving salvageable tissue [7]. Empirical evidence indicates that reducing BP to 130/80 mmHg can decrease the risk of recurrent stroke by 25–30% [8]. Furthermore, research has demonstrated a strong association between BP management and post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI). During the early stages of acute ischemic stroke, both excessively low and high BP levels are correlated with an increased risk of PSCI at three months post-stroke [9]. Additionally, BP variability is recognized as a significant risk factor for PSCI [10]. These findings suggest that maintaining an optimal BP level may contribute to reducing the incidence of PSCI.

The interplay between diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and cerebral blood flow (CBF) is pivotal in the context of post-stroke brain rehabilitation. A study has demonstrated a significant negative correlation between DBP and CBF, with this relationship being particularly pronounced in hypertensive patients [11,12]. Furthermore, another study posits that DBP may influence collateral cerebral blood flow, which is crucial for patients experiencing acute ischemic stroke, as collateral flow can sustain cerebral perfusion in the presence of arterial occlusions [13]. Additionally, evidence suggests that DBP can affect the burden of small vessel disease in the brain, thereby influencing clinical outcomes post-stroke [14]. Consequently, effective management of DBP during post-stroke rehabilitation may be instrumental in preventing post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI). This study aims to investigate the relationship between DBP levels during post-stroke rehabilitation and the incidence of PSCI, thereby providing evidence to support optimal DBP management following the acute phase of stroke.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the recommendations outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [15]. Figure 1 presents a flowchart of the study design. The cohort was derived from a single rehabilitation center within a tertiary hospital in China, where only patients in clinically stable condition were admitted. All stroke patients come to our center for rehabilitation after the acute phase. Due to our medical insurance policy, their length of stay in our center is ∼3 months. The records of consecutive patients with confirmed stroke, admitted to this center between May 2022 and March 2024, were retrospectively analyzed. General information, along with clinical, laboratory, and imaging data, were extracted from electronic medical records. The criteria for cohort selection were established as follows: Inclusion criteria encompassed patients diagnosed with stroke confirmed by either CT or MRI, those who completed the cognitive assessment at least once during hospitalization, and those with a follow-up duration exceeding three months. Exclusion criteria included patients with incomplete data, a follow-up period of <3 months, a family-reported history of cognitive impairment diagnosed before the stroke, inability to complete the cognitive assessment due to aphasia (NIHSS language score ≥2) or other reasons, concurrent malignant tumors (ongoing-chemo/radiotherapy, recent surgery, or metastatic tumors), and coexisting psychiatric disorders. The flowchart illustrating the process of patient inclusion and exclusion is presented in Figure 1.

Cognitive assessment

The culturally and linguistically adapted Chinese version of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), which has been validated for evaluating cognitive impairment in Chinese populations [16], was employed for cognitive assessment. The MoCA is a 30-point questionnaire designed to evaluate eight cognitive domains, with specific items detailed by Nasreddine et al. [17]. The assessment was administered face-to-face to all stroke patients admitted to this center, both at admission and discharge, by trained investigators adhering to established guidelines and protocols. The total MoCA score, ranging from 0 to 30, is the aggregate of all item scores, with higher scores indicating superior cognitive function. For individuals with 12 years or fewer of education, an additional point was added to their total MoCA score if it was below 30 [16].

Ethics approval and study registration

This study was performed in adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval Number: 2024-155-01). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, as this study was designed to retrospectively collect available data. This study was prospectively registered at Clinicaltrials.gov (Registration Number: NCT06730711).

Data collection

The data pertinent to the objectives of this study encompass several key domains: (1) disease-related information, including stroke type, time of stroke occurrence, affected area, Activities of Daily Living (ADL) score, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, post-stroke epilepsy, dysphagia, urinary incontinence, history of previous strokes, and brain atrophy; (2) demographic characteristics, such as age, gender, and educational attainment; (3) health status and functional indicators, including smoking history, alcohol consumption history, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, liver disease, kidney disease, and anemia; (4) anthropometric and physiological measurements, specifically height, weight, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and DBP.

Outcomes and definition

The primary outcome, PSCI, was defined as being diagnosed with cognitive impairment at least once in a cognitive assessment performed more than three months after stroke. Cognitive impairment was determined using education-specific thresholds in accordance with the Chinese MoCA criteria: a total score of ≤13 for individuals without formal education, ≤19 for those with 1 to 6 years of education, and ≤24 for individuals with 7 or more years of education [16]. BP was measured using an automated cuff, and the average of three measurements taken at different times on the second day after admission was calculated for analysis. The follow-up period was defined as the duration from the stroke event to the last administration of the MoCA scale. Stroke type and affected area were determined through imaging obtained via computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Comorbidities were identified as diseases diagnosed and treated during the current hospital admission or previously, based on the patient’s self-report. A previous stroke was defined as a stroke occurring before the current event, which resulted in clinical symptoms and was confirmed by CT or MRI. The Global Cortical Atrophy (GCA) scale [18], which has been proved reliable for CT [19], was used for visual assessments of cortical atrophy. Brain atrophy was defined as an atrophy score >0.

Statistical analysis

Patients were categorized into three DBP groups. The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of continuous variables. Continuous variables that conformed to a normal distribution were expressed as means ± standard deviation, while those that did not conform were represented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Differences among the three groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, with differences between groups analyzed using Pearson chi-square test. Univariate logistic regression analyses, followed by multivariate analyses incorporating significant factors identified in the univariate analyses, were conducted to identify risk factors for PSCI. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed to explore the form of association between DBP and PSCI. Multivariate logistic regression models were performed to estimate the association between DBP and PSCI risk, model 1 was crude model, model 2 was adjusted for SBP, model 3 was adjusted for age, gender, educational attainment and BMI, model 4 was adjusted for all of the significant (p < 0.05) factors in multivariate logistic regression analysis; model 5 was adjusted for all of the significant (p < 0.05) factors in univariate logistic regression analysis. Subgroup analysis was employed to assess the association between DBP and PSCI risk by stratifying according to age, BMI, stroke type, educational level, gender, atrial fibrillation, previous stroke, brain atrophy, left hemisphere, urinary incontinence, post-stroke epilepsy, hypertension, and combination of age and atrophy. A two-tailed p-value of ≤0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.0).

Results

Patients characteristics

The baseline clinical and demographic characteristics of patients, stratified by DBP levels, are detailed in Table 1. This study included a total of 508 stroke patients, of whom 289 (57%) had ischemic strokes and 357 (70%) were male. The median age of the cohort was 59 years. During a median follow-up period of 5.5 months, 207 (41%) patients were identified as having post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI). The incidence of PSCI was significantly higher in the group with lower DBP (<75 mmHg). At baseline, SBP, body mass index (BMI), activities of daily living (ADL) scores, the prevalence of male patients, history of alcohol consumption, and hyperlipidemia were lower in the lower DBP group. Conversely, pulse pressure, age, and the proportions of urinary incontinence, previous stroke, brain atrophy, diabetes, and atrial fibrillation were higher in the lower DBP group.

DBP level is associated with the risk of PSCI

To identify potential factors associated with PSCI, a univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted. The results indicated that seventeen factors, including DBP, were significantly associated with the incidence of PSCI (p < 0.05) (Table 2). To ascertain whether these factors were independently associated with PSCI, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was subsequently performed, incorporating the seventeen factors identified in the univariate analysis. The findings revealed that DBP (odds ratio [OR] = 0.960 per 1 mmHg, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.933–0.988, p < 0.01), along with educational attainment, left hemisphere involvement, epilepsy, urinary incontinence, atrial fibrillation, and brain atrophy, were independently associated with PSCI (Table 2).

To assess the relative risk of PSCI across different DBP categories, five logistic regression models were developed, incorporating DBP category as a categorical variable and using the mid-level DBP as the reference group. In the unadjusted Model 1, a significantly elevated risk was identified in the lower DBP group (<75 mmHg) with an OR of 3.381 and a 95% CI of 2.152–5.312 (p < 0.001). Upon adjusting for all potential confounding variables, Models 2 through 5 demonstrated that the risk of PSCI remained significantly higher (p < 0.05) in the low DBP group compared to both the mid and upper DBP groups (refer to Table 3).

A nonlinear association exists between DBP and the risk of PSCI

To further investigate the potential linear or nonlinear relationship between DBP levels and the incidence of PSCI, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was conducted, utilizing three knots as the optimal number determined by the Bayesian minimum value. The RCS analysis revealed a nonlinear association between DBP levels and the risk of PSCI in stroke patients (P for overall < 0.001; P for non-linear = 0.002; Figure 2A). Notably, the curve exhibited an inflection point at ∼80 mmHg, with the risk of PSCI negatively correlated with DBP levels below 80 mmHg, and remaining stable when DBP exceeded 80 mmHg. A similar nonlinear pattern, with an inflection point around 80 mmHg, persisted after adjusting for potential confounders (P for overall = 0.008; P for non-linear = 0.019; Figure 2B).

Subgroup analysis

To investigate the risk of PSCI in low DBP (≤80 mmHg) group compared with high DBP (>80 mmHg) group across various demographic and clinical categories, subgroup and interaction analyses were performed based on stroke type, educational level, gender, atrial fibrillation, previous stroke, brain atrophy, left hemisphere, urinary incontinence, post-stroke epilepsy, hypertension, age, BMI, and combination of age and atrophy. The findings indicated that across all subgroups defined by these factors, regardless of statistical significance, lower DBP levels were consistently associated with an elevated risk of PSCI (Figure 3). Notably, although the risk of PSCI in the lower DBP group varied among certain subgroups, no significant interaction effects were observed (Figure 3).

Sensitivity analysis

To ensure the robustness of the findings and to investigate the potential association between other DBP characteristics and the risk of PSCI, a sensitivity analysis was conducted. This analysis utilized mean inpatient DBP, minimum and maximum DBP, and blood pressure variability, defined as the ratio of the standard deviation of DBP to the mean DBP during hospitalization (refer to Table 4). Given that only patients with hypertension had their BP monitored daily during hospitalization, this analysis was restricted to the hypertensive cohort. Patients were stratified into three groups (tertiles 1–3) based on the tertiles of each focal variable. The results indicated that the mean DBP during hospitalization exhibited a similar relationship with PSCI as the mean DBP recorded in the first two days after admission, as illustrated in Table 4. Conversely, while the minimum and maximum DBP during hospitalization were associated with PSCI in the unadjusted model, this association did not persist in the adjusted model. Furthermore, DBP variability was not associated with PSCI in either the unadjusted or adjusted models.

Discussion

This study investigates the link between DBP levels and the occurrence of PSCI in a real-world retrospective stroke cohort. Using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, we found that baseline DBP levels are independently linked to PSCI. Additionally, a nonlinear relationship and a negative correlation between DBP levels and PSCI risk were noted when DBP was below 80 mmHg, as shown in the RCS analysis. Logistic regression models were used to assess the relative risk of PSCI across different DBP groups. The study indicates that keeping DBP above 80 mmHg may help to prevent PSCI in stroke patients.

For stroke patients, the selection of an appropriate BP management strategy is essential for the prevention of recurrent strokes and other cardiovascular events. Research has demonstrated that stringent BP control can substantially decrease the risk of stroke recurrence. However, intensified BP management may also lead to a reduction in CBF and an increased risk of adverse events, such as syncope or dizziness [20,21]. In the current study, a lower DBP (<80 mmHg) was associated with a heightened risk of PSCI, likely due to reduced CBF resulting from decreased DBP. Conversely, a study conducted on a large general population sample found that higher DBP was linked to impaired cognitive status [22]. Furthermore, a randomized controlled trial involving hypertensive patients revealed that, even among those with low baseline DBP in the intensive treatment group, intensive BP control did not adversely affect cognitive outcomes or cerebral perfusion [23]. Notably, in the present study, lower DBP was associated with negative cognitive outcomes in patients with subacute or chronic stroke. These findings suggest that the impact of DBP on cognitive function may vary across different study populations. This might be due to the fact that in the general population and patients with hypertension, elevated DBP is closely associated with increased white matter hyperintensities, a hallmark of cerebral small vessel disease [24]. In one study, every 10 mmHg increase in baseline DBP was linked to a 1.17% rise in white matter hyperintensities [24]. In contrast, certain brain regions in stroke patients experience circulatory disruptions. Increased diastolic blood pressure may improve perfusion through collateral circulation to ischemic brain areas, thereby preserving brain function. However, this protective effect is contingent upon the patient’s degree of vascular sclerosis. Subgroup analysis results corroborate this finding, indicating that in stroke patients of advanced age and those with brain atrophy, which are conditions related to more severe vascular sclerosis [25,26], the protective impact of elevated DBP on cognitive function is diminished.

The present study offers several significant advantages. First, the establishment of personalized BP targets is essential for optimizing stroke prevention and minimizing complications in stroke patients. Prior research indicates that stringent BP control should be prioritized in patients with early-diagnosed hypertension, whereas excessively aggressive interventions in elderly hypertensive patients may exhibit a J-shaped correlation with adverse events [27]. The current study identified an association between lower DBP and an increased incidence of PSCI, suggesting that for patients with low DBP, it may be preferable to utilize antihypertensive medications that exert minimal impact on DBP. Consequently, this study may serve as a significant reference and impetus for the future study design of more rigorous and large-scale prospective studies aimed at investigating personalized BP control targets for stroke patients. Secondly, the MoCA is more sensitive to detecting mild cognitive impairment compared to the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [28,29], making the conclusions of this research even more solid. Third, as a real-world cohort study, it may more accurately reflect actual clinical practice. Fourth, a comprehensive analysis was conducted, and the results demonstrated consistency across these analyses, thereby reinforcing the robustness of the study’s conclusions.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, the cohort was derived from a single center, and the follow-up duration was relatively brief. This may have resulted in inaccurate estimations of values such as the OR and the inflection point when applied to other centers or extended follow-up periods. Second, patients unable to complete the assessment due to aphasia were excluded from the cohort. Given that these individuals frequently exhibit severe cognitive impairment, their exclusion could introduce bias. Nonetheless, this issue is prevalent in most studies utilizing such scales and is therefore deemed acceptable. Third, while our study identified a correlation between DBP during the post-stroke rehabilitation period and the risk of PSCI, the findings are insufficient to establish a foundation for personalized BP management. Future research should focus on conducting more rigorous, large-scale, and comprehensive prospective studies with an expanded set of observational indicators to determine the optimal blood pressure levels for stroke patients during rehabilitation.

In conclusion, strategies for BP management should be tailored to individual patient conditions to effectively balance the risk of stroke recurrence while minimizing the potential for adverse events related to hypotension. This study, conducted in a real-world setting, elucidated the association between baseline DBP and the incidence of PSCI in stroke patients. The findings offer valuable evidence and serve as a reference for strategies aimed at adjusting DBP to prevent PSCI. Additionally, this study lays the groundwork for future rigorous investigations into the impact of DBP on PSCI.

Conclusions

DBP during post-stroke rehabilitation demonstrates a non-linear association with the incidence of PSCI. Specifically, a low DBP level during this phase serves as an independent risk factor for the incidence of PSCI. Furthermore, maintaining a DBP above 80 mmHg during rehabilitation is advantageous for the prevention of PSCI.
