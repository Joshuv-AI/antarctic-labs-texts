Sun Safety Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Among Farmers in Ede‐Oballa, Nigeria
Authors: Ukah Cynthia Chinonso, Majani Edward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundExcessive exposure to ultraviolet (UV) radiation of the sun poses significant health risks to outdoor workers. Farmers are particularly vulnerable, yet their knowledge and practices regarding sun protection are underreported in low‐resource settings.AimTo assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of sun safety among farmers in Ede‐Oballa, Nsukka LGA, Enugu State, Nigeria.Materials and MethodsAn analytical cross‐sectional study was conducted among 155 farmers recruited using a convenience sampling method. Data were collected using a pretested, semi‐structured, self‐administered questionnaire and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize responses. Associations between sociodemographic characteristics and sun safety practices were tested using χ 2 tests, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsMost farmers demonstrated good knowledge of sun protection (89.7%) and held positive attitudes (83.9%) towards sun safety practices. However, actual practice was below average (47.7%), and they showed low willingness to participate in sun safety workshops, possibly due to prior exposure to training/education on sun safety amongst the majority of the farmers (72.9%). Peer influence and perceived inconvenience were key factors affecting behavior. Farmers with tertiary education showed significantly higher sun safety practice levels (p < 0.05), while no significant relationship was found between knowledge and sun safety practices among the respondents, suggesting that knowledge alone may not translate into behavioral change.ConclusionDespite high knowledge and positive attitudes, sun safety practices among farmers remain inadequate, hindered by social and practical barriers. Educational interventions should be context‐specific, peer‐led, and embedded into routine farming activities to enhance adoption of protective behaviors.

Introduction

Sun safety is a critical component of occupational and public health. Prolonged exposure to its ultraviolet (UV) rays without adequate protection has been linked to numerous public health concerns, including sunburn, heat exhaustion, dehydration, premature skin ageing, cataracts, and an increased risk of cancers such as colorectal, breast, prostate, kidney, melanoma, and non‐Hodgkin lymphoma. On the other hand, moderate sun exposure plays a beneficial role in human health by stimulating the synthesis of vitamin D in the skin, which is essential for bone health, and is suggested to have protective effects against certain cancers, including melanomas. Despite these benefits, there is still no clear consensus on what constitutes adequate sun exposure [1]—one that ensures sufficient vitamin D production while minimizing UV‐related harm, as excessive avoidance of sunlight can lead to vitamin D deficiency and complications such as hypovitaminosis D, osteoporosis, and rickets. This presents a public health dilemma, particularly for populations with high occupational sun exposure, such as farmers.

According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (2025), agriculture has long served as the foundation of the Nigerian economy, providing livelihood opportunities to over 70% of the active labor force. The sector is widely projected to play a significant role in reducing poverty in developing countries and contributing to the first Sustainable Development Goal (SDG 1) of ending poverty [2]. In many rural African countries, agriculture contributes not only to food security but also significantly to annual gross domestic product (GDP) and employment rates [2]. The sector is broadly divided into four sub‐fields: crop production, livestock, forestry, and fishing. In Nigeria, crop production is predominantly practised in rural areas [3] and outdoors.

Despite the well‐documented economic benefits of agriculture, there is a noticeable gap in addressing the occupational health risks associated with prolonged sun exposure among rural farmers. In the pursuit of poverty reduction through agriculture, insufficient attention has been paid to the health implications of outdoor work, including sun‐related conditions such as skin and eye diseases caused by ultraviolet (UV) radiation. This oversight highlights a misalignment with Sustainable Development Goal 3, which seeks to ensure healthy lives and promote well‐being for all at all ages.

Although public health campaigns since the 1980s have demonstrated that sun safety education can significantly reduce the risk of skin cancer, such progress has not been mirrored in Nigeria. There remains a scarcity of studies focusing specifically on farmers' knowledge of sun safety, their attitudes, and operational practices. Addressing this research gap is especially timely given projected increases in UV exposure driven by climate change and ozone depletion [4] and the heightened risk of heat stress, dehydration, and UV‐related health harms among farm workers who lack access to preventive knowledge and protective measures.

In addition to malignancies, UV radiation contributes to the development of photodermatoses, chronic inflammatory skin conditions that can severely impact quality of life. Beyond dermatological effects, prolonged UV exposure also contributes to ocular damage, with rising incidences of cataracts, pterygium, and intraocular melanoma now being linked to cumulative sun exposure. The Montreal Protocol has been estimated to prevent approximately 11 million cases of melanoma and 432 million cases of keratinocyte cancer by reducing UV radiation levels [5]. However, the full benefits of such environmental protections may not yet be fully realized in African countries like Nigeria, where sun safety remains a low priority due to high implementation costs and limited awareness among farmers about the risks of prolonged sun exposure.

Farm workers are at an elevated risk of developing skin cancer and other adverse health effects due to occupational sun exposure. Yet, studies show that they often lack adequate knowledge about sun safety and do not consistently adopt protective behaviors such as using sunscreen, wearing protective clothing, or seeking shade. A study by Woo et al. [6] found that only 44% of farm workers used sunscreen regularly, while just 28% wore protective clothing. The study also revealed that most were unaware of key recommendations, such as seeking shade and avoiding peak sun hours [6]. These suggest that many farm workers may not be adequately protecting themselves from the harmful effects of sun exposure.

Farmers in South‐Eastern Nigeria face compounded risks from high UV exposure and poor sun protection practices. For example, a study by Enechukwu et al. reports that despite persistently high UV indices, many farmers still neglect sun safety due to ignorance, limited access to protective gear, and the high cost of sun‐protection tools. Complementing this, a separate investigation in Nsukka (South Eastern Nigeria) measured UV‐B irradiance among outdoor workers, including farmers, and found that the exposure levels at solar noon exceeded international occupational limits for both skin and eye protection (ICNIRP) [7]. These findings highlight an urgent need for targeted education and affordable sun‐safety initiatives in the region.

This study aims to bridge the gap by reviewing the knowledge, attitude, and practices of sun safety among farmers using Amaegbu Ede‐Oballa farmers as a study population.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This is an analytical cross‐sectional study conducted in Amaegbu Ede‐Oballa, Nsukka Local Government Area of Enugu State, Nigeria. This design was appropriate for assessing the knowledge, attitudes, and sun safety practices of farmers at a single point in time. It enabled the collection of quantitative data from a defined population to examine associations between the variables, level of education, and adoption of sun protection behaviors. Data collection was carried out in December 2024.

Study Setting

This study was conducted in Nsukka local government of Enugu state. Enugu State is one of the five states in southeastern Nigeria and shares boundaries with Anambra, Ebonyi, Abia, Benue, and Kogi States. It covers a land area of approximately 7161 km2 and has a projected population of 3,267,837 people, comprising 1,596,042 males and 1,671,795 females.

Nsukka is one of the Local Government Areas in Enugu State. The local government headquarters is sited at the hilly and green areas that Nsukka is known for. In 2006, Nsukka had a population of 309,633, comprising mainly of people who engage in civil service duties, commercial activities, and agrarian peasantry. The prestigious first indigenous University of Nigeria is located at Nsukka and was founded in 1960 by the late Dr. Nnamdi Azikiwe, the first President of Nigeria.

This study was specifically carried out in Amaegbu Ede‐Oballa, which is bounded by Ohodo on the east, Ngwuru Nsukka on the West, Lejja market on the south, and to the north is Ugbele Ede‐Oballa and Abakwuru. Amaegbu is a village in Ede Oballa and comprises well‐known agricultural producers of Ede‐Oballa, in Nsukka Local Government of Enugu state. It has three regions; Ukwuiyi, Orie Agu, and Ulo Amaegbu. Ede‐Oballa is one of the communities under Nsukka East and situated in the middle of five communities: Opi and Ohodo on the East, Eha‐alumona on the North, Nsukka metropolis on the West, and Lejja on the South.

Study Participants

We included all farmers residing in Amaegbu, a village in Ede‐Oballa, Nsukka Local Government Area of Enugu State. These farmers were selected based on their active involvement in outdoor agricultural work, making them a relevant population for assessing sun safety practices. They were considered eligible regardless of socio‐economic status, ethnicity, religion, or gender. The target population was identified using up‐to‐date records obtained from the secretaries of the village's men, women, and youth associations. According to these records, the total village population was 252, out of which 158 individuals were listed as active farmers (Amaegbu Men's Record, 2023; Amaegbu Women's Association Record, 2024; Amaegbu Youth Association Record, 2024). We selected the village of Amaegbu Ede‐Oballa using simple random sampling from among the nine villages in Nsukka LGA of Enugu State.

Sampling Procedure

The sampling procedure involved two stages:

Stage 1: selection of villages: Amaegbu Ede‐Oballa was randomly selected from the 9 villages in Nsukka Local Government Area to ensure an unbiased selection for the study.

Stage 2: selection of respondents: All eligible farmers within Amaegbu Ede‐Oballa were consecutively enrolled in the study. To recruit participants, we approached them consecutively in their houses and only farmers who had worked for at least 1 year and were directly involved in managing farm labor were invited to participate.

Instrument for Data Collection

We used a pretested, semi‐structured, self‐administered questionnaire to collect data. The questionnaire was adopted and modified from previous studies on sun safety among outdoor workers [8, 9] and designed based on relevant literature on occupational sun exposure, UV‐related health risks, and sun protection behaviors. It was transcribed into Igbo to allow non‐literate participants to understand and respond appropriately. Additionally, research assistants were available to explain the questions in the local dialect to ensure clarity and inclusivity. The questionnaire consisted of five sections and a total of 46 items, covering the following areas: sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge of farmers related to sun safety, attitudes of farmers towards sun safety practices, practice of sun safety among farmers, and factors influencing farmers of Ede‐Oballa, Nsukka, towards sun safety practices.

To ensure face validity, we consulted two public health experts and one occupational health nurse with experience in rural health education. The tool was pretested among 25 farmers from a neighboring village not included in the study. Based on their feedback, we made revisions to improve clarity, local relevance, and ease of understanding, while preserving the intended content of the questionnaire.

Variables

Knowledge of sun safety was assessed using a structured questionnaire comprising multiple items on sun protection awareness, health effects of sun exposure, and preventive strategies. Each item had clearly defined correct answers. Correct responses were scored as 1, while incorrect or “I don't know” responses were scored as 0. The total knowledge score for each respondent was computed by summing the correct answers. Knowledge was then categorized as good for respondents who scored more than 50% and poor for those who scored 50% or less.

Attitude toward sun safety was measured using a Likert scale, which assessed beliefs, feelings, and perceptions about sun protection. Each item was rated on a 4‐point scale: strongly disagree (1), disagree (2), agree (3), and strongly agree (4). Negatively worded items were reverse‐scored during analysis. A mean attitude score was calculated for each respondent, and any score above 2.5 was interpreted as a positive attitude, while a score of 2.5 or below indicated a negative attitude.

Practice of sun safety was evaluated using items which measured the frequency of sun protection behaviors. Each item was rated on a 4‐point scale: never (1), sometimes (2), often (3), and always (4). A mean practice score was computed per respondent, and any mean score greater than 2.5 indicated good practice, while a score ≤ 2.5 indicated poor practice.

Influencing Factors were assessed with Likert‐scale items designed to explore barriers and enablers to sun safety behavior. These were also rated on a 4‐point scale of strongly disagree (1), disagree (2), agree (3), and strongly agree (4), and individual mean scores were analyzed to identify the most significant behavioral influences.

Data Management and Analysis

Data were entered, cleaned, and analyzed using Microsoft Excel and IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0. Both descriptive and inferential statistics were applied. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and associations between them were tested using χ 2 test at 5% level of significance. Means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables. A significant relationship exists if the p value is less than 0.05; otherwise, no significance. Results are presented in tables and figures.

Ethical Consideration

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects. Ethical approval was obtained from the Enugu State Ministry of Education to help the researcher gain access to the area of study and also serve as a legal and ethical backing during the course of collecting the data. Participants' confidentiality was ensured, and informed consent was sought before data collection.

Discussion

Our study assessed farmers' knowledge and practices regarding sun protection. Most of the farmers in our study possess good knowledge of sun safety, and they were able to identify measures to prevent sun exposure, including protecting themselves while working on the farm (96.1%), seeking shade whenever possible (96.1%), wearing a wide‐brimmed hat, and wearing protective clothing (95.5%). This is similar to a study carried out by D'Souza et al. [10] on Australian farmers who answered questions on knowledge of sun safety correctly, while 40.8% underestimated its risks. Many farmers in our study correctly identified individuals with albinism as most susceptible, though some underestimated risk in darker skin types. It is well known that fair and skin of color alike are at risk of damage from the sun's UV rays [11]. However, individuals with albinism globally face a significantly higher risk of developing skin cancer from UV exposure compared with the general population, with regional estimates suggesting that Africans with albinism are up to 1000 times more likely to develop skin cancer, like cutaneous malignancies, compared with the overall African population [12, 13].

The farmers had a positive attitude towards sun safety practices (83.9%). They believed that protecting oneself from the sun's harmful rays (3.82 ± 0.48) and sun safety practices are essential for preventing skin damage and related health issues (3.72 ± 0.60). They were also open to adopting new sun safety practices that may improve overall protection from the sun (3.39 ± 0.78) and taking adequate measures to protect themselves from the sun while working on the farm (2.90 ± 1.01). A study by Woods et al. [14] similarly observed that many farmers and outdoor workers acknowledged the importance of sun safety, even when actual practices lagged behind due to practical and contextual barriers. Additionally, the finding demonstrates a proactive attitude of the farmers toward improving personal protection. This is important because adoption of such behaviors requires both awareness and positive disposition as explained by the Health Belief Model, which emphasizes the importance of perceived benefits and willingness to act as critical determinants of health behavior change [15]. However, willingness to adopt sun protective practices may not always translate into consistent practice due to practical and environmental barriers. In this regard, the Social Cognitive Theory complements the Health Belief Model by explaining that behavior change occurs through reciprocal determinism, involving the interaction of personal factors such as knowledge and self‐efficacy, behavioral factors, and environmental influences, including social norms and physical barriers [16].

In our study, despite the farmers' overall positive attitude toward sun protection, they expressed a relatively low willingness to participate in sun safety programs or workshops specifically tailored for farmers (M = 2.05, SD = 0.83). This finding may be partly explained by the fact that a substantial proportion of the farmers had previously participated in sun safety training programs (72.9%), and likely perceive further workshops as redundant. While literature emphasizes the importance of these programs [17, 18], unassessed barriers in our study may also limit their implementation. Nonetheless, it has been observed that educated farmers tend to exhibit higher levels of sun protection behavior [18]. Supporting this, our study found a significant relationship between educational level and sun safety practices. The farmers with tertiary education (60.6%) and primary education (60.0%) in our study demonstrated higher practice levels compared to those with secondary education (33.8%). These differences in practice level may stem from variations in occupation roles, practical farming experience, and exposure to health information. For instance, farmers with tertiary education may have had greater health awareness and resources, while those with secondary education may have had lower practice due to underestimation of risk or competing work priorities.

The overall practice level among the farmers in our study was below average (47.7%), indicating a clear gap between knowledge and behavior. This is consistent with multiple studies that have reported suboptimal sun safety behaviors among farmers, even in the presence of awareness or positive attitudes. A study by Moradhaseli and Rostami [19] found that less than half of surveyed African farmers regularly wore protective clothing, citing forgetfulness, lack of adequate time for the preparation and use of protective equipment, and lack of employer role as key barriers [19]. The inconsistency between knowledge and practice has also been attributed to a lack of access to appropriate protective gear, cultural norms that discourage covering the body in hot weather, and the economic reality that many farmers cannot afford specialized sun‐protective clothing. According to the Occupational Health Psychology model, such environmental and structural constraints often override individual motivation, even when awareness is high [20]. Moreover, shade‐seeking behavior, which is a simple yet effective sun protection strategy, is often not feasible on many farms due to open field design, absence of shelter structures, or intense work schedules. In such cases, even farmers who understand the risk of prolonged UV exposure may be unable to consistently implement avoidance strategies during peak sun intensity hours (11 a.m. to 4 p.m.), as noted in prior occupational health research where shade‐seeking was the least‐used protective behavior at work (8% often/always) and the workers sought shade three times more often in their leisure time [21]. Peters et al. [21] further suggested that outdoor workers may seek out shade at work if it were available.

Our study revealed that farmers' sun safety practices were significantly influenced by social and practical factors. Peer influence was the most strongly endorsed factor among those examined, with respondents reporting that the attitudes and behaviors of farming peers and colleagues played a role in shaping their sun safety behaviors (M = 2.81, SD = 0.83). This could be because informal sharing of information is mostly practised in rural areas where most farming communities are situated, and this can often reinforce or undermine protective practices. Previous studies have also reported deficiencies in sun protection behaviors among individuals working in the agricultural sector [22, 23, 24]. These deficiencies may be further explained by our finding that farmers perceived sun safety practices as inconvenient and time‐consuming (M = 2.56, SD = 1.21). The physical demands of farm work, exposure to heat, and lack of access to affordable protective gear likely contribute to this perception, which in turn limits consistent practice even in the presence of awareness or positive attitudes.

Limitations

While our study demonstrated methodological strengths, including the use of a structured analytical cross‐sectional design, appropriate sample size determination, cultural sensitivity through Igbo translation and local dialect assistance with a 100% response rate, several limitations must be acknowledged. The use of convenience sampling within a single village (Amaegbu) introduces potential selection bias and limits the external validity and generalizability of the findings to other farming communities. Self‐reported practices from the use of questionnaires may be subject to social desirability, potential misclassification and recall bias, particularly given the presence of research assistants during data collection. The study also omitted relevant barriers to sun safety practices, such as economic constraints, availability of protective equipment, or specific cultural beliefs. Nonetheless, the findings provide valuable baseline data on sun safety knowledge, attitudes, and practices among farmers in Ede‐Oballa, offering insights that can inform future community‐based interventions and guide further research across similar rural settings.

Conclusion

This study assessed the knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to sun safety among farmers in Ede‐Oballa. It revealed that although farmers in Ede‐Oballa possess good knowledge and positive attitudes toward sun protection, actual practice remains suboptimal while some misconceptions about susceptibility remain. To address this gap, policymakers and agricultural stakeholders in similar rural settings should integrate sun safety education into existing agricultural extension programs and community activities. Community‐based interventions should also leverage trusted local farmers as peer educators and integrate sun safety messaging into routine structures such as farmer meetings, cooperative society gatherings, market days, or extension worker visits to promote realistic and sustainable engagement.
