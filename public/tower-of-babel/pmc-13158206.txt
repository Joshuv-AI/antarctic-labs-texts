Sleeve Gastrectomy With Proximal Jejunal Bypass: Balancing Simplicity and Efficacy in Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery
Authors: Sang-Yong Son, Yeo-Jin Boo, Jeong Ho Song, Hoon Hur, Sang-Uk Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSleeve gastrectomy with proximal jejunal bypass (SG-PJB) is a simplified sleeve-plus procedure designed to augment the metabolic impact of sleeve gastrectomy while avoiding the long-term complications associated with Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB). Early studies demonstrate that SG-PJB achieves diabetes remission and metabolic improvements comparable to RYGB, with sustained insulin independence in previously insulin-dependent patients. The procedure preserves duodenal continuity and maintains physiologic nutrient absorption, and no cases of hypoalbuminemia or bacterial overgrowth have been reported. Despite its promising balance of metabolic efficacy and nutritional safety, further research is required to clarify optimal indications, determine appropriate bypass limb lengths, and validate long-term safety. As current data suggest, SG-PJB offers a favorable risk–benefit profile and may become a valuable alternative within the sleeve-plus spectrum.Keywords: Bariatric surgery, Diabetes mellitus, Sleeve gastrectomy, Jejunal bypass

INTRODUCTION

Sleeve gastrectomy (SG) has become the most widely performed bariatric procedure worldwide [1]. According to the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity Obesity Fact Sheet, SG accounts for approximately 70–80% of all metabolic and bariatric surgeries performed in Korea, mirroring global trends [2]. Despite its popularity and technical simplicity, SG is known to provide less durable long-term weight loss than Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), with up to 30% of patients experiencing significant weight regain over time [3]. Consistent with this clinical reality, the American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery 2018 report demonstrated a parallel rise in revision procedures as SG utilization increased; even after excluding band removal, revision surgery constituted nearly 10% of all bariatric operations [4]. These findings highlight the limitations of SG as a stand-alone procedure, particularly for sustained weight management.

On the other hand, RYGB offers superior metabolic outcomes and more robust long-term weight loss [5,6]. However, these benefits come at the cost of procedure-specific complications, including marginal ulcers, internal hernias, and dumping syndrome, which may negatively affect long-term quality of life [7]. Furthermore, in East Asian countries such as Korea, Japan, and China—regions with a high prevalence of gastric cancer—the altered anatomy after RYGB poses a critical limitation [8]. The excluded stomach cannot be easily evaluated through routine endoscopy, rendering postoperative surveillance for early detection of gastric neoplasm impractical and diminishing the suitability of RYGB in these populations.

In response to the limitations of both SG and RYGB, sleeve-plus procedures have gained considerable attention. The International Federation for the Surgery of Obesity and Metabolic Disorders recently acknowledged the efficacy and safety of single-anastomosis duodeno-ileal bypass with sleeve gastrectomy (SADI-S), reinforcing the emerging role of SG-based hybrid procedures [9]. The rationale for sleeve-plus techniques can be summarized into 3 major points: 1) they serve as effective revision procedures for patients with recurrent weight regain following SG [10,11,12,13], 2) they preserve endoscopic access to the remnant stomach, an important consideration in gastric cancer–endemic regions [14], and 3) they offer superior glycemic control compared with SG alone and achieve metabolic outcomes comparable to RYGB, while demonstrating a lower incidence of long-term complications such as marginal ulcer or dumping syndrome [15]. Among sleeve-plus variants, procedures involving a duodenal bypass—such as duodenojejunal bypass (DJB) or SADI-S—provide strong metabolic benefits but remain technically demanding. Bypass at the level of the duodenum requires meticulous dissection of the posterior duodenal wall, which can be difficult or unsafe in patients with a history of duodenal ulcer, pancreatitis, or severe inflammation. In such scenarios, secure mobilization may not be feasible, leading to intraoperative conversion to alternative operations and limiting the applicability of duodenum-based sleeve-plus techniques.

Sleeve gastrectomy with proximal jejunal bypass (SG-PJB), also referred to as SG with jejunal bypass, has emerged as an intermediary “sleeve-plus” procedure that offers a technically simpler yet metabolically effective alternative within the spectrum of hybrid bariatric operations. The procedure entails transection of the small bowel immediately distal to the ligament of Treitz, followed by bypass of approximately 200–300 cm of proximal jejunum through a single side-to-side jejunojejunostomy. By avoiding duodenal dissection, SG-PJB reduces operative complexity, shortens operative time, and minimizes the risk of anastomosis-related complications commonly associated with duodenum-based sleeve-plus procedures such as DJB or SADI-S.

This review summarizes the current evidence on the efficacy, safety, and technical considerations of SG-PJB, underscoring its potential as a metabolically advantageous and technically accessible bariatric procedure with an appealing risk–benefit balance.

TERMINOLOGY CLARIFICATION

The surgical technique combining SG with a bypass of the proximal jejunum has been described under various names in the literature, including Sleeve Gastrectomy with Proximal Jejunal Bypass (SG-PJB), Sleeve Gastrectomy with Jejunojejunal Bypass (SG+JJB), Loop Duodenojejunal Bypass, and simply Proximal Jejunal Bypass (PJB). These terms are often used interchangeably, despite referring to procedures that are anatomically and functionally similar. Currently, there is no internationally standardized nomenclature for this class of sleeve-plus procedures.

In this manuscript, we adopt the term ‘SG-PJB’ as the representative terminology. However, for consistency with prior studies and to preserve the original context, we have retained the terminology used by the respective authors when citing their findings.

TECHNICAL EVOLUTION OF SG-PJB

The proximal jejunal bypass (PJB) technique was first introduced by the Chilean surgeon Munir J. Alamo in 2003, during a period when SG had not yet been established as a standalone bariatric procedure [16,17]. In the original configuration, the surgery consists of a vertical resection of the stomach and a jejunal bypass, allowing the earlier arrival of food at the proximal ileum, where the largest concentration of L-cells is present. The proximal jejunum was transected 20–30 cm distally to the ligament of Treitz and the biliopancreatic limb is anastomosed to restore transit 250–300 cm distal to the jejunum transection. Then, defunctionalized jejunum was anastomosed to the gastric remnant without creating a blind loop, reflecting the surgical principles prevalent at that time. The procedure was named “Vertical isolated gastroplasty with gastro-enteral bypass.” However, this initial design was associated with anastomosis-related complications, including 6 documented cases of anastomotic ulcer.

To address these limitations, a modified technique was introduced in 2012 (Fig. 1) [18]. This updated approach formally incorporated SG as the restrictive component and adopted the blind-loop configuration that characterizes contemporary SG-PJB. In the modified version, jejunal division is performed approximately 20–30 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz, and a laterolateral enteroenterostomy (jejunoileal anastomosis) is created 250–300 cm distal to the point of jejunal transection, consistent with the original design principles. The only substantive difference lies in the incorporation of SG itself, with removal of the gastric remnant rather than preservation of the proximal stomach. The technique also emphasizes preservation of at least 300 cm of absorptive small bowel to ensure adequate nutritional capacity while providing effective metabolic diversion.

Several investigators have compared PJB with other established bariatric procedures. Chao et al. [24] conducted a comparative study between gastric clipping alone and gastric clipping combined with jejunal bypass; their findings demonstrated greater body mass index (BMI) reduction at 1 year in the bypass group, indirectly supporting the added weight-loss benefit of jejunal diversion. Lin et al. [21] performed a matched retrospective analysis comparing SG alone, RYGB, and sleeve gastrectomy with jejunojejunal bypass (SG+JJB). They reported that SG+JJB achieved significantly greater %TWL than SG alone at both 1 and 3 years postoperatively. However, when SG+JJB was compared with RYGB, no significant differences were observed at either time point, suggesting comparable medium-term efficacy between the 2 procedures.

In contrast, Qi et al. [25] reported no significant difference in weight-loss outcomes between laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) and laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy with jejunojejunal bypass (LSG+JJB) at 1 year. Furthermore, in their cohort, RYGB produced greater weight loss than the SG+JJB group, highlighting procedural variability and reinforcing the need for standardized technique and high-quality comparative studies. Korean data have also been reported for SG-PJB, although the primary focus of this study was on changes in HbA1c rather than weight-loss outcomes [23]. Nevertheless, BMI comparisons between the SG and SG-PJB groups provide indirect insight into relative weight-loss efficacy. Preoperatively, patients in the SG cohort had a higher BMI (38.02 vs. 33.17), yet by 12 months postoperatively, BMI values were comparable between the 2 groups (29.79 vs. 27.96; P=0.300). Although not designed as a dedicated weight-loss comparison, these findings suggest that SG-PJB may achieve weight reduction at least equivalent to, and potentially greater than, that of SG alone despite lower baseline BMI in the bypass group.

Collectively, the available literature suggests that while PJB reliably enhances weight loss beyond that of SG alone in several studies, its comparative efficacy relative to RYGB remains variable and may depend on technical factors such as limb length, patient selection, and institutional expertise. In addition, no clinical comparative studies have yet evaluated PJB against other sleeve-plus procedures such as DJB, SADI-S, single-anastomosis sleeve ileal bypass, or single-anastomosis sleeve jejunal bypass. However, one experimental study in diabetic rats directly compared SG with DJB and SG+JJB, demonstrating that both bypass configurations achieved superior glycemic control compared with SG alone, with no significant difference between DJB and jejunojejunal bypass across glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity, or glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) secretion outcomes. These findings suggest that bypassing the proximal small intestine—regardless of whether the division occurs at the duodenum or jejunum—may provide comparable metabolic benefits in an experimental setting [26]. As a result, the relative advantages, metabolic performance, and long-term safety profiles of PJB within the broader sleeve-plus spectrum remain largely undefined and warrant further investigation.

METABOLIC EFFICACY AND NUTRITIONAL OUTCOMES OF SG-PJB

The metabolic effects and nutritional safety profile of PJB can be understood through its underlying anatomic configuration and the physiology of nutrient absorption along the small intestine. PJB reroutes ingested nutrients away from approximately 200–300 cm of proximal jejunum while maintaining an intact duodenum and preserving the continuity of gastric emptying into the alimentary limb. Unlike duodenal bypass procedures such as DJB or SADI-S, PJB leaves the duodenum undisturbed; biliary and pancreatic secretions drain through their natural course and re-enter the nutrient stream distally at the jejunojejunostomy, providing a more physiologic mixing point.

From an absorptive standpoint, the exclusion of the proximal jejunum selectively limits early carbohydrate and amino acid absorption—which are normally concentrated in the first 100–150 cm of the small intestine—thereby enhancing distal intestinal stimulation. This shift increases exposure of nutrients to the ileum and distal jejunum, regions rich in L-cells responsible for secretion of GLP-1, PYY, and other incretins that modulate insulin secretion, appetite, and glucose homeostasis [27,28,29,30,31]. The blind-loop configuration further delays transit through the excluded segment, augmenting hindgut-mediated metabolic signaling without substantial impairment of global nutrient absorption.

However, evidence on the metabolic impact of PJB, particularly with respect to diabetes remission, currently remains limited, with only a few studies reporting long-term outcomes. In the earliest and most frequently cited series by Sepúlveda et al. [18], PJB demonstrated metabolic results comparable to those of RYGB. At 1 year postoperatively, complete diabetes remission was achieved in 69.2% of patients undergoing PJB, a rate similar to the 64.7% reported after RYGB. At 3 years, sustained remission rates remained comparable between the 2 procedures—56.1% for PJB and 57.1% for RYGB—indicating durable glycemic improvement. Changes in diabetes medication usage also showed parallel trends between the 2 groups. Notably, 15.8% of patients in the PJB cohort were insulin-dependent preoperatively; however, none of these individuals required re-initiation of insulin therapy by 36 months postoperatively. This finding suggests that PJB may offer a robust insulin-sparing effect, even among patients with more advanced disease at baseline.

More recent data further support the notion that PJB achieves metabolic outcomes comparable to RYGB while outperforming SG alone. Qi et al. [25] conducted a large retrospective cohort study comparing 3 procedures—LSG, LSG+JJB (equivalent to PJB), and laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (LRYGB). In this analysis, all 3 groups demonstrated significant reductions in fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, and homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance during the first postoperative year. However, the magnitude of glycemic improvement was greater in the LSG+JJB and LRYGB groups than in the LSG group, whereas no meaningful differences were observed between LSG+JJB and LRYGB at any time point. These findings suggest that adding a PJB to SG confers a metabolic benefit similar to that of RYGB, without requiring a gastric bypass configuration.

Notably, a recent Korean study by Seo and Ryu [23] involving 298 patients further substantiates these findings in a domestic context. The study demonstrated that, among diabetic patients, SG-PJB resulted in significantly greater reductions in HbA1c compared to SG alone at both 6 and 12 months postoperatively, even after adjusting for BMI differences. In contrast, in prediabetic patients, glycemic outcomes were similar between groups, suggesting that the metabolic benefits of the bypass component are more pronounced in patients with overt diabetes. These results provide valuable real-world evidence from the Korean population and strengthen the case for SG-PJB as an effective metabolic intervention beyond simple weight reduction.

Given that more potent metabolic procedures often carry a higher risk of nutritional deficiency, theoretical concerns have been raised regarding whether the metabolic effects of PJB—reported to be comparable to those of RYGB—might similarly predispose patients to macronutrient malabsorption [32,33]. However, existing evidence suggests that this risk is likely to be modest. Although published data on nutritional outcomes after PJB remain limited, the available reports indicate a generally favorable profile. Notably, no cases of clinically significant hypoalbuminemia have been documented in the literature to date [18]. Moreover, studies comparing postoperative symptoms have shown that PJB tends to result in fewer malabsorptive manifestations such as malodorous flatus and diarrhea than those commonly observed after RYGB [21].

Taken together, these findings suggest that despite its substantial metabolic benefit, PJB does not appear to confer an increased risk of severe macronutrient deficiency, and its overall nutritional burden may be lower than that associated with traditional gastric bypass procedures. Nevertheless, the current evidence base remains sparse, and further prospective studies are warranted to more definitively characterize the long-term nutritional safety of this operation.

CURRENT ISSUES AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES OF SG-PJB

Despite its promising metabolic efficacy and favorable safety profile, SG-PJB remains relatively underutilized worldwide. As a result, several important questions must be addressed before the procedure can be widely accepted or integrated into standardized bariatric practice.

First, the optimal clinical indications for PJB are not yet well defined. Although existing studies suggest that PJB offers superior glycemic control compared with SG and achieves metabolic outcomes comparable to RYGB, the specific patient subgroups who are most likely to benefit—such as those with particular degrees of insulin resistance, β-cell reserve, or obesity severity—remain unclear. Establishing reliable predictors of successful weight loss and diabetes remission will require prospective analyses and could ultimately inform the development of a clinically applicable decision support system to assist surgeons in selecting PJB over competing sleeve-plus procedures [34,35].

Second, although the modified PJB technique introduced in 2012 has yielded consistently favorable outcomes, important technical considerations remain unresolved. The length of the small intestine varies substantially among individuals—by sex, height, and ethnicity—with reported ranges from approximately 300 to over 1,000 cm [36]. Since the metabolic effect of PJB is influenced by the length of the bypassed segment and the functional properties of the blind loop, it is uncertain whether a uniform bypass length (commonly 200–300 cm) is appropriate for all patients. Future work, including imaging-based or intraoperative measurements and proportion-based limb-length strategies, may allow for more tailored bypass configurations that provide consistent metabolic outcomes across diverse patient populations [37,38].

Third, long-term safety concerns—particularly those related to blind loop syndrome, bacterial overgrowth, and hepatic dysfunction from chronic malabsorption—remain a barrier to broader adoption. Although many surgeons express concern regarding the possibility of stagnant-loop bacterial overgrowth progressing to hepatic steatohepatitis, fibrosis, or cirrhosis, current evidence does not support these fears. Alamo, who first described PJB in 2003, published a 20-year follow-up analysis in 2024 and reported no cases of bacterial overgrowth syndrome, blind loop syndrome, or secondary liver disease attributable to the defunctionalized limb [39]. Furthermore, in a cohort of 1,074 patients who underwent SG-PJB between 2004 and 2019, biopsies of the defunctionalized loop stump were performed in 11 patients who required subsequent cholecystectomy, intussusception repair, or conversion to RYGB [40]. Histological analysis revealed no evidence of bacterial overgrowth, mucosal injury, or intestinal pathology, and no potential pathogenic organisms were identified. These findings collectively provide reassuring but still limited early evidence regarding the long-term safety of the blind loop configuration, based on currently available reports.

Moving forward, larger prospective cohorts, multicenter registry data, and physiologic studies are essential to further define the ideal indications, optimal limb-length strategies, and long-term safety profile of SG-PJB. Although early results are promising, there are important limitations—most of the cited studies were retrospectively analyzed and subject to selection bias. A well-designed prospective study, such as a randomized clinical trial, is needed to elucidate the definitive role of SG-PJB in the future. As evidence accumulates, PJB has the potential to evolve into a more widely accepted sleeve-plus procedure offering a balanced combination of technical simplicity, metabolic potency, and nutritional safety.

CONCLUSION

SG-PJB offers a promising balance of technical simplicity, metabolic potency, and nutritional safety. It may serve as an attractive alternative for patients who require greater metabolic benefit than SG can provide but who are poor candidates for duodenal-level bypass or traditional RYGB. Continued research—particularly prospective studies defining optimal indications, tailored limb-length strategies, and long-term outcomes—will be essential to determining the precise role of SG-PJB in the modern bariatric surgery landscape.
