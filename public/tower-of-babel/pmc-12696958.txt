Head kinematic analysis in idiopathic scoliosis: a case–control study
Authors: Xiaopeng Gan, Rongbin Zhang, Chenguang Qiu, Danxian Cai, Fanqiang Li, Haohuang Fang, Caifeng Huang, Yugui Huang, Hongrui Zhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIdiopathic scoliosis (IS) is a three-dimensional spinal deformity associated with impaired sensorimotor integration and diminished postural control; however, the role of head postural stability control remains unclear. This study aims to investigate head postural stability control strategies in IS patients during static and dynamic tasks and their associations with spinal radiographic parameters.MethodsA total of thirty patients with IS and thirty healthy individuals participated in three standing tasks (firm surface, foam surface, and single-leg stance), a 10-m walking test, and stair ascent and descent activities. Head accelerations were recorded using a wearable triaxial accelerometer, and eleven parameters of postural stability were subsequently calculated. Differences between the two groups and correlations with spinal radiographs were analyzed.ResultPatients with IS demonstrated greater head sway during static balance tasks. On firm-surface standing, root mean square (RMS), range of acceleration displacement (RANGE), mean anteroposterior displacement (APD), sway jerkiness (JERK), and sway path length (SPL) were significantly higher than in healthy controls, and the between-group differences were further amplified under eyes-closed conditions (p < 0.05). During foam-surface standing, peak acceleration (PA), RANGE, and APD were markedly elevated (p < 0.05). In single-leg stance, RMS, PA, and RANGE in the anteroposterior direction were consistently higher in patients (p < 0.05). In contrast, dynamic tasks showed a more conservative control strategy: head acceleration fluctuations in the anteroposterior direction were reduced during walking and stair ascent, while no group differences were observed during stair descent. Correlation analyses indicated that APD was positively associated with the C2–C7 sagittal vertical axis (C2-C7SVA) (p < 0.05), whereas anteroposterior acceleration measures showed significant negative correlations with pelvic incidence(PI) and thoracic kyphosis(TK) (p < 0.05).ConclusionPatients with IS employ two distinct strategies for head posture control: exaggerated swaying during static balance and restricted movement during dynamic activities. These patterns may reflect compensatory mechanisms designed to address sensorimotor deficits. Future individualized rehabilitation programs for IS should comprehensively consider the impact of head motor control on overall balance function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06535-z.Keywords: Idiopathic scoliosis, Motion analysis, Wearable sensors, Accelerometer, Postural control

Introduction

Scoliosis is a complex three-dimensional spinal deformity that alters spinal morphology and disrupts intersegmental relationships. Its primary characteristics include lateral curvature in the coronal plane, flattening or abnormal kyphosis in the sagittal plane, and vertebral rotation in the transverse plane [1–3]. According to the Scoliosis Research Society (SRS), scoliosis is diagnosed based on full-spine coronal radiographs that reveal a Cobb angle of ≥ 10° accompanied by vertebral rotation [4]. IS is a multifactorial neuromusculoskeletal disorder of unknown etiology, potentially involving genetic, hormonal, metabolic, environmental, and neuromuscular developmental factors [5–8].

The three-dimensional spinal deformities of IS in the sagittal, coronal, and transverse planes not only alter the position of the center of gravity within the trunk and affect balance control strategies [9, 10], but also disrupt normal head posture [11]. Studies have demonstrated that patients with IS exhibit a significantly greater coronal head tilt compared to healthy controls (2.3° vs. 1.3°, p = 0.001) [12]. Radiographic assessment combined with plantar pressure analysis further indicates that IS patients with spinal imbalance exhibit larger head offset distances compared with those with balanced spines [13].

As the apex of the biokinetic chain, head position plays a crucial role in postural stability. By modulating sensory integration, muscle activity, and balance control, head posture contributes significantly to postural regulation [14]. Maintaining proper head alignment facilitates effective integration of visual, proprioceptive, and vestibular inputs by the central nervous system, thereby promoting optimal alignment of body segments [15, 16]. In contrast, abnormal head posture may disrupt vestibular function, cervical proprioceptive feedback, and visual-spatial referencing, ultimately compromising balance and impairing postural control [12, 14, 17].

Spinal deformity and abnormal head positioning are key contributors to impaired postural control in IS [18]. The displacement of the head in IS patients interferes with the integration of visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive information, thereby influencing motor control of the head and triggering adaptive changes in overall postural strategies [11, 12]. However, current studies on head position in IS patients have predominantly relied on radiographic or static standing image assessments, overlooking the dynamic changes in head orientation during functional activities.

Dynamic postural stability of the head in space is closely related to spinal deformity. Research has shown that following corrective spinal surgery, head sway amplitude during standing decreases significantly, indicating that improved spinal alignment enhances head stability and underscores the influence of spinal deformity on head posture control [19, 20]. Nevertheless, there is limited evidence regarding head postural stability in three-dimensional space during both static and dynamic tasks in IS patients, as well as its correlation with spinal radiologic parameters.

Therefore, this study aims to compare head postural stability control in three-dimensional space between IS patients and healthy subjects, analyze the correlations between head postural control strategies and spinal deformity parameters. It further seeks to investigate alterations in three-dimensional head movement control strategies in individuals with IS, as well as the impact of spinal deformities on head postural balance control. The findings are expected to enhance the understanding of postural balance regulation in IS patients and provide new insights for training and rehabilitation strategies targeting head and overall balance function.

Materials and methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from Zhuhai, China. The inclusion criteria for healthy subjects were as follows: (1) age over 18 years; (2) no history of musculoskeletal disorders or trauma within the past six months; and (3) ability to walk independently and climb stairs without assistance. For participants with IS, the inclusion criteria were: (1) fulfillment of the specific diagnostic criteria for IS [21]; (2) age over 18 years; (3) availability of complete standing anteroposterior and lateral full-spine X-rays, with a main curve Cobb angle greater than 10°; (4) no history of orthotic use or spinal surgery, and no physical therapy or exercise training within three months prior to enrollment; (5) bilateral lower limb length difference less than 2 cm, with the ability to walk independently and climb stairs; (6) right-foot dominance; and (7) absence of flat feet. The exclusion criteria for participants with IS included: (1) severe spinal deformity or major systemic diseases, such as tumors; (2) significant deformities of the lower limbs or feet, or other musculoskeletal malformations; (3) other conditions affecting gait, such as trauma, muscle atrophy, or joint diseases. Participants who met any of the exclusion criteria were excluded from the study.

Data collection

Spinal radiologic parameters

Spinal radiological parameters of patients with IS were assessed using standing anteroposterior and lateral full spinal radiographs obtained through the Neusoft imaging diagnostic system. All measurements were performed independently by two investigators, and the mean of their results was adopted as the final measurement. In cases where a significant discrepancy arose between the two sets of measurements, re-measurement was performed, and the mean of the repeated results was utilized as the final value. Coronal spinal radiological parameters included Cobb angle (Cobb), coronal balance (CB), apical vertebra translation (AVT), and lateral pelvic tilt (LPT) [22–25]. Sagittal spinal radiological parameters encompassed sagittal vertical axis (SVA), PI, lumbar lordosis (LL), TK, thoracolumbar kyphosis (TLK), pelvic tilt (PT), T1 pelvic angle (TPA), sacral slope (SS), lumbar pelvic angle (LPA), global cervical lordosis (CL), lower cervical alignment (LCA), C0–C2 angle(C0C2), C0–C7 angle(C0C7), C0–C7 SVA, C2–C7 SVA, and T1 slope [26–29]. Detailed measurement methods and definitions are provided in Appendix 1.

Testing procedures

Before each test, participants were acquainted with the experimental protocol and completed two practice trials prior to the commencement of data collection.

Modified Romberg Test

Participants were instructed to stand barefoot with their feet together. The examiner guided them to cross their arms over the chest, and throughout the test, they were asked to maintain a self-perceived balanced and natural posture. The modified Romberg test comprised two parts: (1) standing with eyes open and closed on a firm surface, and (2) standing with eyes open and closed on a foam surface. Each standing test, both eyes-open and eyes-closed, was repeated twice, with each trial lasting 30 s. During the eyes-open condition, participants were instructed to focus on a fixed point marked on the wall 1.5 m in front of them and to remain as still as possible. During the eyes-closed condition, participants were instructed to close their eyes immediately after the test commenced.

Single-leg stance

Participants were instructed to stand barefoot on one leg on a firm surface, with their arms crossed over the chest and eyes fixed on a marker placed 1.5 m ahead on the wall. The non-weight-bearing leg was lifted, maintaining the hip in a neutral position and the knee flexed at 90°, without contact with the supporting leg. The single-leg stance test included trials for both the left and right legs, performed alternately. Each side was tested twice, with each trial lasting 15 s.

10-m walking

The walking test was conducted on a straight walkway measuring 20 m in length and 2 m in width. Participants were required to keep their eyes looking straight ahead, avoid turning their heads, and swing their arms naturally during the test. Upon hearing the start command, they walked forward in a natural and comfortable manner for a total distance of 15 m, which included 2.5 m before the starting point and 2.5 m beyond the endpoint. Acceleration data were collected during the 15-m walk, and the time taken by the subjects to traverse the central 10 m was recorded. Each participant performed the test twice.

Stair ascent and descent

The staircase consisted of 12 steps, each measuring 16 cm in height and 32 cm in depth. Participants were instructed to ascend and descend the stairs at a self-selected comfortable speed upon hearing the start command [30]. At the last step, they were required to stand still without turning around until further instructions were given. Both ascent and descent tests were conducted twice, and the duration of each trial was recorded.

Data acquisition

A wearable three-dimensional accelerometer (Xsens DOT, Enschede, NL; weight = 11.2 g, dimensions = 36.3 × 30.4 × 10.8 mm) was used to record head linear accelerations in three anatomical planes (sagittal, coronal, and transverse) during the aforementioned tests. Data were continuously transmitted to a smartphone via Bluetooth. The accelerometer had an internal sampling rate of 800 Hz, an output rate of 60 Hz, and a standard full-scale range of ± 16 g. Data were collected in dynamic mode, and the output signals were preprocessed to remove gravitational acceleration. The sensor was positioned at the occipital pole [31] and secured with an elastic headband and medical double-sided tape. The sensor axes were aligned with anatomical directions: the X-axis corresponded to the anteroposterior (AP) direction, the Y-axis to the mediolateral (ML) direction, and the Z-axis to the vertical (VT) direction.

Data analysis

3D motion acceleration data

Acceleration signals were blinded to the data analysts until all analyses were completed, after which the results were assigned to specific groups. The raw acceleration signals, with gravitational components removed, were processed in MATLAB (R2020b, The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA) through filtering and smoothing techniques. For the 10-m walk and stair ascent/descent tests, signals were filtered with a second-order Butterworth low-pass filter at a cutoff frequency of 10 Hz [32]. For the modified Romberg test and single-leg stance test, a fourth-order Butterworth band-pass filter was applied, with passband frequencies ranging from 0.1 to 5 Hz, to eliminate involuntary human movements [11, 33]. All frequency parameters were normalized by dividing by half the sampling frequency. Zero-phase filtering was applied to the triaxial acceleration signals to eliminate phase distortion. To ensure representativeness and statistical validity, the middle portions of the acceleration signals were randomly extracted: 1200 points (20 s) for the modified Romberg test, 600 points (10 s) for the single-leg stance test, 420 points (7 s) for the 10-m walk, and 300 points (5 s) for the stair ascent/descent tests. Eleven parameters reflecting head postural stability were computed in MATLAB.

Postural stability parameters

Postural stability parameters include RMS, PA, RANGE, APD, mean mediolateral displacement (MLD), JERK, 95% ellipse sway normalized (ESN), 95% elliptical sway area (ESA), SPL, peak-to-peak interval of acceleration (PP), and root mean square jerk (RMSJ) (details in Table 1) [34–38] (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were analyzed using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of the data was initially assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Data that conformed to a normal distribution were analyzed using the independent-sample t-test (for equal variances) or Welch’s t-test (for unequal variances) to compare differences in head postural stability control variables between the two groups. Within-group differences were evaluated using paired t-tests. For data that did not meet the normality assumption, the Mann–Whitney U test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test were employed for non-parametric analysis. Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients were used to examine associations between postural stability control variables and spinal radiographic parameters in the experimental group. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The consistency of spinal radiographic parameter measurements between two researchers was assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficients. Anthropometric data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Continuous data that follow a normal distribution are expressed as mean values with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), while non-normally distributed data are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR: P25, P75). The minimum sample size required for the independent t-test between the IS and healthy groups was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.4 software, with a significance level of 5%, statistical power of 80%, and a large effect size of 0.8 [22].

Significant differences between the groups were observed for RMS-AP, PA-AP, RANGE-AP, JERK, ESN, ESA, and SPL (p < 0.05) (Table 3).

Foam-surface standing

Analysis of head acceleration data during foam-surface standing revealed that, with eyes open, individuals with IS demonstrated superior metrics of head posture stability control, including PA-AP, RANGE-AP, and APD, compared to healthy participants. Statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups (p < 0.05). With eyes were closed, IS patients demonstrated a significantly higher PA-AP value than their healthy counterparts (mean difference, 0.065 [95% CI, 0.004 to 0.126]; p = 0.036), while no significant differences were observed for the remaining parameters (p > 0.05) (Appendix 3).

Single-leg stance

Data analysis indicated that during single-leg stance on the dominant lower limb (right side), individuals with IS exhibited significantly higher RMS-AP values of head movement compared to healthy controls (mean difference, 0.023 [95% CI, 0.002 to 0.044]; p = 0.034), whereas the remaining head posture stability control parameters showed no significant differences (p > 0.05). When standing on the non-dominant lower limb (left side), significant differences were observed between the two groups for the parameters of PA-AP and RANGE-AP (PA-AP: mean difference, 0.107 [95% CI, 0.010 to 0.203]; RANGE-AP: mean difference, 0.164 [95% CI, 0.004 to 0.325]; both p < 0.05). (see Appendix 4). Comparison of head acceleration data during single-leg stance on the concave versus convex lower limb in IS patients revealed no significant differences in head posture stability control between the two sides (p > 0.05) (see Appendix 5).

10-m walking

As shown in Table 4, the 10-m walking task revealed no significant difference in walking speed between patients with IS and the healthy control group (mean difference, 0.036[95%CI,-0.040 to 0.112]; p = 0.351). However, IS patients demonstrated lower head posture stability control metrics compared to healthy participants. Specifically, the head movement parameters, including RMS-AP, RMS-VT, PA-AP, PA-ML, RANGE-AP, RMSJ, PP-AP, PP-ML, and PP-VT, were all significantly lower in IS patients than in healthy controls (p < 0.05).

Stair ascent and descent

Data analysis indicated that the ascent speed during stair climbing was comparable between individuals with IS and the healthy control group (mean difference, − 0.001[95%CI,− 0.026 to 0.023]; p = 0.914).However, the IS group exhibited significantly lower RMS-AP values of head movement compared to the healthy controls (mean difference, − 0.145[95%CI, − 0.266 to − 0.024]; p = 0.020). Furthermore, IS patients had lower PP-AP values than healthy subjects, with the difference reaching statistical significance (mean difference, − 0.423[95%CI, − 0.770 to − 0.076]; p = 0.018). For the remaining parameters related to head posture stability control during stair ascent, no significant differences were observed between the two groups (p > 0.05). During stair descent, individuals with IS exhibited no significant difference in descent speed compared to healthy controls (mean difference, − 0.001[95%CI, − 0.036 to 0.034]; p = 0.939) or in head posture stability control (p > 0.05), as none of the comparisons achieved statistical significance (see Appendix 6).

Correlation between spinal radiological parameters and head postural stability

Following an analysis of the differences in head postural stability control during standing between patients with IS and healthy controls, correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationships between the significantly different postural stability parameters and the full-spine coronal and sagittal imaging parameters of IS patients. The results indicated that, during eyes-open standing on a firm surface, APD of head movement in IS patients was moderately and significantly correlated with C2-C7 SVA (r = 0.403, p = 0.027). During eyes-open standing on a foam surface, head movement parameters, including PA-AP, RANGE-AP, and APD, exhibited significant and moderate negative correlations with PI (r = − 0.391, p = 0.033; r = − 0.384, p = 0.036; r = − 0.558, p = 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 2). During eyes-closed standing, PA-AP showed significant moderate correlations with both PI and TK (r = − 0.363, p = 0.049; r = 0.364, p = 0.048, respectively). The remaining parameters of head postural stability, which exhibited significant differences between patients with IS and healthy controls during standing, did not demonstrate significant correlations with full-spine coronal or sagittal imaging parameters (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The impairment of spatial balance control in the heads of IS patients is closely associated with overall trunk imbalance. Specifically, long-term postural deviations of the head in IS may lead to dual functional deficits: they directly affects the visual system’s ability to maintain horizontal orientation, resulting in an inability to keep both eyes level, while also compromising the integration of vestibular and proprioceptive information [11, 13]. This sensory integrative dysfunction represents a previously overlooked mechanism contributing to balance disorders in IS [39]. The head serves as the primary locus for the integration of visual, proprioceptive, and vestibular information, and its postural stability in space is fundamental to the control of whole-body posture [14]. The present study investigated head postural stability control in three-dimensional space during standing, walking, and stair ascent/descent in individuals with IS. The findings revealed distinct differences in head postural stability control patterns between IS patients and healthy participants.

The results of this study demonstrate that, compared with the healthy control group, patients with IS exhibited more pronounced fluctuations in head acceleration, greater movement amplitude, larger sway area, and less stable and less smooth characteristics of head postural balance control under various standing conditions (including firm-surface standing (Fig. 3), foam-surface standing, and single-leg stance). When visual input was removed (eyes closed while standing on a firm surface), the increase in head acceleration fluctuations was more pronounced in IS patients than in healthy participants (Table 3), suggesting that their balance control is more significantly disrupted in the absence of visual feedback. This phenomenon indicates that following the removal of visual input, individuals with IS begin to depend on somatosensory and vestibular inputs to maintain postural balance control of the head. However, potential integration deficits within these sensory systems further compromise their balance abilities [11, 13, 39]. Consequently, when vision is eliminated, the compensatory role of visual information is lost, thereby more clearly exposing the deficits in head postural stability control among IS patients.

Comparisons of head movement parameters between patients with IS and healthy controls during eyes-open standing on a firm surface and 10-m walking: (A) root mean square (RMS), (B) peak acceleration (PA), and (C) range of acceleration displacement (RANGE). In the violin plots, the upper and lower dashed lines represent the 75th and 25th percentiles, respectively, and the central solid black line indicates the median (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

No significant differences in head stability control were observed between the concave and convex lower limbs during single-leg stance in patients with IS (p > 0.05). Previous studies have suggested that spinal deformities may induce asymmetries in lower limb stability between the concave and convex sides, thereby affecting overall balance during single-leg stance [13, 40]. However, in the present study, despite the pronounced spinal deformities in IS patients resulting in uneven loading on the concave and convex sides, head stability control did not differ significantly between the two lower limbs. This finding may indicate that postural stability control of the head in IS patients is not solely determined by spinal morphological changes, but is likely influenced by multiple factors, including muscle imbalance, proprioceptive function, and vestibular coordination [11, 39, 41].

Overall, this study found that the acceleration characteristics of head movement in patients with IS under different standing conditions were consistent with previous findings on center of pressure (COP) displacement, specifically greater movement fluctuations and larger postural adjustments compared with healthy controls [42]. Prior research has shown that COP displacement in both the AP and ML directions is larger in IS patients than in healthy individuals [42]. In contrast, the present study revealed that IS patients exhibited significantly greater head movement fluctuations and control amplitude in the AP direction compared with controls, while no significant differences were observed in the ML direction. This discrepancy may be related to center of mass shifts induced by scoliosis, adaptive changes or reduced efficiency in neuromuscular control strategies for maintaining AP balance, as well as alterations in sagittal spinal alignment [43]. Sagittal imbalance may induce AP shifts of the body’s center of mass, thereby increasing the difficulty of maintaining AP postural stability [20].

In this study, the correlations between full-spine coronal and sagittal radiographic parameters and head posture stability during bipedal stance in IS patients revealed moderate associations between head posture stability and several sagittal alignment parameters across different standing conditions. During eyes-open standing on a firm surface, APD was moderately and positively correlated with C2–C7SVA, indicating that cervical sagittal imbalance may exacerbate anterior–posterior head sway. PI is a key parameter reflecting pelvic morphology, with higher values indicating greater anterior pelvic tilt. Previous studies have shown that scoliosis patients may compensate for forward trunk inclination by increasing pelvic tilt, a mechanism that enhances lumbar lordosis and helps maintain sagittal trunk balance [44, 45]. In the present study, during eyes-open stance on a foam surface, the parameters PA-AP, RANGE-AP, and APD, as well as PA-AP during eyes-closed stance, were significantly negatively correlated with PI, suggesting that anterior pelvic tilt may serve as a compensatory mechanism to improve head postural stability. Additionally, thoracic curvature may influence cervical alignment and head posture in IS. Thoracic hypokyphosis has been identified as an important compensatory mechanism for maintaining overall balance in individuals with sagittal malalignment [46, 47]. Consistent with this, the present study found a significant moderate positive correlation between PA-AP and TK, indicating that anteroposterior head stability control may be associated with the degree of kyphosis, with greater kyphosis requiring stronger stability control in the AP direction. Current research attributes alterations in postural balance control among individuals with IS to two primary hypotheses: the biomechanical hypothesis and the sensory integration hypothesis. The biomechanical hypothesis posits that changes in the structural relationships between spinal segments, along with variations in the positions of the shoulders, pelvis, and head, collectively contribute to a decline in postural control. Conversely, the sensory integration hypothesis suggests that inaccurate weighting of sensory inputs leads to compromised dynamic regulation of sensorimotor integration, resulting in diminished balance control abilities [18]. In this study, the observed correlations between spinal parameters and head postural stability in IS patients were only moderately strong, suggesting the potential involvement of additional factors, such as alterations in sensorimotor control and sensory integration [39, 48], which warrant further investigation.

During dynamic tasks, including 10-m walking and stair ascent/descent, patients with idiopathic scoliosis (IS) employed a more conservative and stable, yet less efficient, head movement control strategy compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 3). During the 10-m walking task, the RMS-AP and RMS-VT values of the head in IS patients were significantly lower than those in healthy controls (p = 0.001). This finding indicates reduced acceleration amplitudes in both the anteroposterior and vertical directions, resulting in more constrained displacement of the head’s center of mass. This “low-amplitude” pattern is consistent with previous findings suggesting that IS patients adopt conservative gait strategies [49].Such a strategy may reflect a compensatory mechanism in which IS patients actively restrict head movement amplitude to minimize interference with head–trunk coordination, thereby maintaining dynamic balance, visual stability, and preventing gait instability. Further analysis revealed that IS patients exhibited significantly lower values than healthy controls in PA-AP, PA-ML, RANGE-AP, RMSJ, and PP values across all three axes (PP-AP, PP-ML, PP-VT) (p < 0.05). These results indicate that during walking, IS patients demonstrate an overall reduction in movement amplitude, smaller acceleration fluctuations, and lower rates of change, reflecting constrained head control. This behavior likely represents a “restrictive movement pattern” adopted to prevent instability or reduce energy expenditure, limiting excessive head displacement and oscillation, while maintaining visual stability and balance through a low-variability, low-energy “cautious” control strategy [50, 51]. Similarly, during stair ascent, IS patients employed a conservative, low-amplitude head control strategy to minimize anterior–posterior sway and preserve overall trunk stability. However, unlike during stair ascent, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in head posture stability control parameters during stair descent (p > 0.05), suggesting that the head movement amplitude and acceleration characteristics in IS patients during stair descent are comparable to those of healthy individuals. Currently, literature examining head posture control strategies in scoliosis patients during stair ascent and descent is limited. Gou et al. reported that during stair descent, IS patients exhibited greater trunk flexion/extension, lateral bending, rotation, pelvic anterior/posterior tilt, and thoracic lateral bending and rotation compared with stair ascent [30]. These enhanced mobility changes likely represent compensatory strategies to maintain balance [52], which may improve head postural stability during stair descent and reduce differences relative to healthy individuals.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, only participants aged 18 years and older were included, excluding adolescent patients, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, the sample size was relatively small, restricting the ability to fully represent the characteristics of patients with different types of scoliosis. Third, the study focused exclusively on head motion acceleration data and did not incorporate physiological measures such as surface electromyography, limiting the capacity to elucidate the underlying neuromuscular mechanisms of head stability control. Additionally, due to experimental constraints, only static full-spine coronal and sagittal radiographic parameters were collected for IS patients, precluding the analysis of dynamic spinal alignment changes and their association with head postural stability. Furthermore, participants experienced considerable difficulty performing the single-leg stance test with their eyes closed; therefore, this test was not conducted, partially affecting the comprehensiveness of postural control assessment. Future studies should expand the sample size, include a broader age range and scoliosis subtypes, and integrate multimodal kinematic and electromyographic measures to further elucidate head postural stability control in scoliosis patients from both neuromuscular and biomechanical perspectives.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that patients with IS employ two distinct head postural stability control strategies in three-dimensional space. During static postural tasks, including firm-surface standing, foam-surface standing, and single-leg stance, IS patients exhibited increased head sway, greater movement amplitude, and reduced stability, reflecting an overall pattern of impaired balance control. In contrast, during dynamic tasks, such as walking and stair ascent/descent, head movements in IS patients were characterized by reduced amplitude, lower acceleration fluctuations, and smoother variations, indicating a more cautious stability control strategy. This adaptive strategy may compensate for sensorimotor integration deficits induced by spinal deformity. The present study systematically elucidates the regulatory strategies of head postural stability in IS patients. Future individualized rehabilitation strategies should emphasize the importance of head movement control in maintaining overall balance function.
