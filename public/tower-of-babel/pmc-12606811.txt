Anxiety disorder is a common psychopathological comorbidity in patients with SHANK pathogenic variants: description of five new cases
Authors: Carmen Manso-Bazus, Nino Spataro, Laura Plans, Meritxell Tomàs, Mercè Casadesús, Montserrat Pamias, Anna Ruiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPathogenic variants in the SHANK family genes have been linked to autism spectrum disorder, as well as to other neuropsychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders. We aim to characterise the neurodevelopmental, neuropsychiatric and dysmorphic features of five additional patients with SHANK1 and SHANK2 and SHANK3 pathogenic variants to highlight a prevalent neuropsychiatric phenotype common to SHANK family members.MethodsWhole exome sequencing was performed in 115 patients (aged 6–18 years) with mild intellectual disability/borderline intellectual functioning and a psychiatric comorbidity. Neurodevelopmental, clinical, psychopathological and dysmorphological features of patients with pathogenic variants in SHANK genes were collected.ResultsIntragenic pathogenic variants in SHANK1, SHANK2, and SHANK3 were identified in five patients. All patients presented significant language and motor delay and autism spectrum disorder as the most prevalent psychiatric comorbidity. Generalised anxiety disorder was present in four out of five patients, while one patient presented a non-specific anxiety disorder.ConclusionsThis study identifies five new patients with pathogenic variants in the SHANK genes that present generalised anxiety disorder as a common psychopathological comorbidity. Identification of the underlying genetic cause in children and adolescents with mild or borderline intellectual disability may improve their clinical management, allowing a personalized approach, and providing precise genetic counselling to the family.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07510-y.

Background

Psychiatric co-morbidity in patients with intellectual disability (ID) is remarkably prevalent, specifically in those patients presenting mild intellectual disability (MID) or borderline intellectual functioning (BIF) [1]. Up to 50% of ID cases are due to genetic causes, which are highly heterogeneous and often shared with other neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) [2–4]. Several causal genes are shared among various NDDs and converge to a set of biological processes, such as those regulating synaptic biology, chromatin remodelling, gene transcription, and protein degradation [5, 6].

Among them, the alteration of genes involved in synapsis biology (also known as synaptopathies) such as neurogilins, neurexins and SHANKS have been commonly associated with autism, developmental delay and other neuropsychiatric outcomes. These genes play a critical role in the formation, stabilisation, and homeostasis of synapses [7]. SHANK genes encode scaffold proteins located at the postsynaptic density of glutamatergic synapses and interact with a variety of membrane and cytoplasmic proteins. Those genes encode for three proteins (SHANK1, SHANK2, SHANK3) which exhibit many isoforms with specific functional domains [8].

Copy number variants (CNV) involving SHANK gene family members have been repeatedly reported in cases with autism and various degrees of cognitive impairment [9]. Among those, the deletion of the 22q13.3 chromosomal, causing Phelan-McDermid syndrome (PMS), is a the most well-known alteration affecting a SHANK gene. Deletions encompassing SHANK3 are present in 0.05–0.7% of autistic patients, which increases to 2% in autistic people with moderate to profound ID, while SHANK2 haploinsuficiency has been reported in around 0.12–0.17% autism spectrum disorder (ASD) patients [10, 11].

The spread of whole exome sequencing into clinical practice has allowed the identification of patients with pathogenic single nucleotide variants in the SHANK family genes. Pathogenic variants affecting the SHANK3 gene coding region represent approximately 14% of PMS patients [12] and are associated to less severe speech impairment and motor deficits compared to patients carrying CNV deletions [13]. On the other hand, SHANK1 and SHANK2 pathogenic single nucleotide variants have been less commonly identified. SHANK2 pathogenic variants are associated with mild to moderate ID, with ASD and speech delay [11]. Finally, to our knowledge, only 15 patients have been reported in the literature with SHANK1 variants presenting ASD, neuropsychiatric disturbances, cognitive delays and/or learning disabilities, motor delays, speech and language delays, and brain abnormalities [14, 15].

Here, we describe the neurodevelopmental, neuropsychiatric and dysmorphic features of five additional patients with SHANK1, SHANK2 and SHANK3 pathogenic variants to highlight a prevalent neuropsychiatric phenotype common to SHANK family members.

Materials and methodology

Patients

A cohort of 115 patients (aged 6–18 years) with MID or BIF and another psychiatric comorbidity (following DSM-5 criteria) were recruited by the Child Mental Health Centre at the Parc Taulí Hospital Universitari (Sabadell), a third level hospital in Catalunya (Spain). Written informed consent was obtained from patients, parents or enrolled participant’s legal representatives. The study was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee of Institut d’Investigaciò i Innovació Parc Taulí I3PT.

Genetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes from patients and their parents. Exome capture was performed using the KAPA HyperExome probes (Roche) and sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at the National Centre of Genomic Analysis (CNAG-CRG, Barcelona, Spain), producing 2 × 150 nucleotides paired-end reads. Bioinformatics analysis was performed as described in Spataro et al., 2023) [16]. Inheritance pattern was assessed through Sanger sequencing of candidate variants. Finally, variants were classified following the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) guidelines [17] and the recommendations provided by the Sequence Variant Interpretation working group at ClinGen. All variants have been submitted to ClinVar.

Neurodevelopment and clinical information

Patient’s developmental data were collected by reviewing their clinical history and from a retrospective interview to both parents. Information about all major somatic diseases, surgical interventions and current pharmacological treatment was also collected.

Mental health and psychopathology

The level of intellectual capacity was measured using the WISC-V/WAIS test (depending on the patient's age). To assess patient’s functionality the Spanish-validated Honosca scale was administrated [18, 19]. Currently, mental health disorders are diagnosed clinically. A child and adolescent psychiatry specialist conducted comprehensive psychopathological evaluations, with particular attention to the diagnostic criteria outlined in the DSM-5—including neurodevelopmental disorders such as ASD and ADHD, as well as other psychiatric conditions. All evaluations were performed by the same clinician to ensure consistency. A validated Spanish version of the following clinical tests was administered to the patients: Conners Scale for ADHD Assessment [20], Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) [21, 22], Children Depression Inventory (CDI) [23] and State-Trait Anxiety Inventory for Children (STAIC) [24] according to their clinical diagnosis. The psychiatric comorbidity was classified according to DSM-5 criteria. All patients were evaluated by at least one psychiatrist and one psychologist. In addition, we have included the degree of care dependency officially recognized by the public administration, which determines access to various types of benefits.

Dysmorphology

Dysmorphological characteristics were collected by a medical geneticist and codified using the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms at the time of molecular diagnosis.

All individuals presented overlapping clinical features of a neurodevelopment disorder consisting in intellectual disability in the mild/borderline range, with significant language delay, variable motor delay and psychiatric comorbidity.

All patients were born from non-consanguineous parents without major incidences in the prenatal, birth or neonatal period. At the moment, all of them have a recognised disability of at least 50% requiring special education and/or social support. None of them has finished compulsory school. They do not present additional health problems and are being follow-up in our mental outpatients centre.

Autism spectrum traits were reported in all five patients (three of them are diagnosed with ASD and patients four and five are diagnosed with communication social disorder). Additionally, three of them presented an attention-deficit and hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and four patients showed a generalised anxiety disorder (GAD).

Three patients were treated with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and demonstrated a favourable response, showing notable improvement in their clinical condition. It is important to note, however, that the PMS Foundation has issued a medical advisory warning that SSRI use in individuals with PMS is associated with an increased risk of manic symptom emergence and heightened behavioural agitation in a subset of patients [25–27]. Notably, the three patients treated with SSRIs in our cohort were carriers of pathogenic variants in SHANK1 and SHANK2, but not SHANK3.

Hypotonia and seizures were present in only one patient respectively, while an abnormal electroencephalogram (EEG) pattern was detected in two patients, one of them receiving anti-epileptic treatment. Abnormal MRI was observed in two patients; one patient showed hippocampal dysplasia, while demyelination of the bilateral periventricular parietal white matter was observed in the other one.

The affected mother of patient 2 carrying a frameshift deletion in SHANK1 presents mild cognitive difficulties, even though no proper neuropsychological evaluations have been performed. Patient 3 has a brother with mild intellectual disability and a schizophreniform disorder who is not carrier of the SHANK2 pathogenic variant or other pathogenic variants. In the other three families no other neurodevelopmental or neuropsychiatric disorders have been observed to date.

No specific common pattern was identified regarding growth parameters. Patient 2 was above 90th percentile (p90) in all measures (height, weight, and cranial circumference) and patient 3 was below 10th percentile (p10) in height. Dysmorphic features were present in all individuals being high and narrow palate (n = 3) and deep-set eyes (n = 3) the most commons.

Discussion

Here, we report five additional patients carrying pathogenic variants in the SHANK gene family members. Regarding SHANK1 pathogenic variants, a total number of fifteen patients have been described so far in the literature [14, 15], while pathogenic variants in SHANK2 have been reported in 11 patients [11, 28]. SHANK3 pathogenic variants have been more frequently reported being associated with the Phelan-McDermid syndrome [13]. All three SHANK genes are unequivocally involved in ASD and ID. However, a clear and consistent gradient of severity is seen across the family. While pathogenic variants in SHANK1 are generally associated with milder neurodevelopmental phenotypes, most commonly autism spectrum disorder, social communication difficulties, and anxiety, with cognitive impairment usually in the mild or borderline range [14, 15], SHANK2 variants are linked to a broader neurodevelopmental profile that includes moderate intellectual disability, language delay, hypotonia, and increased rates of hyperactivity and behavioral dysregulation [11, 28]. In contrast, SHANK3 variants are responsible for the most severe presentations, characterized by moderate to severe intellectual disability, profound speech delay or absence of speech, global developmental delay, hypotonia, and a high prevalence of epilepsy and sensory abnormalities, as typically seen in Phelan-McDermid syndrome [10, 13].

All patients reported here presented intellectual disability and at least one comorbid psychiatric disorder, being autism spectrum disorder the most prevalent one as previously described [10]. Anxiety disorders were present in all five patients, while it has been reported in literature in only three of six patients with SHANK1 pathogenic variants [15] and in two of eleven patients with SHANK2 pathogenic variants [11]. Although anxiety disorders are firstly approached with psychotherapy in all patients, for three of them, pharmacological treatment was necessary due to severity of symptoms. Interestingly, these three patients —carriers of pathogenic variants in SHANK1 and SHANK2, but not SHANK3— showed a good response to SSRI, leading to a noticeable improvement in their quality of life. However, it is essential to highlight that the use of SSRIs is contraindicated in a proportion of patients with PMS. SSRI use in this population has been linked to an increased risk of manic symptoms and worsening behavioural agitation. In light of these findings, the PMS Foundation has issued a medical advisory warning caregivers and clinicians about the use of SSRIs in individuals with PMS [25–27].

On the contrary, none of the three patients affected by ADHD showed a good tolerance and response to treatment with methylphenidate, a first line pharmacological treatment for patients affected with this condition. These results may indicate that the presence of a pathogenic variant in SHANK gene family could interfere with the treatment, even though further investigation is needed.

EEG abnormal patterns were observed in a patient carrying a SHANK1 pathogenic variant and in a patient carrying the SHANK3 pathogenic variant. Antiepileptic drugs were administered to the latter, preventing seizures appearance. These results suggest that EEG should be performed in all patients carrying pathogenic variants in SHANK genes to prevent seizure onset by pharmacological treatment.

Currently, genetic studies are not part of the evaluation of children with psychiatric disorders in specialized child psychiatry or mental health services. Children and adolescents with MID/BIF constitute 12–15% of the child population and represent 35% of patients attended at mental health services. Identification of the underlying genetic cause in these patients may improve their clinical management, allowing a personalized approach, and providing precise genetic counselling to the family.

In summary, this study identifies five new patients with pathogenic variants in the SHANK genes that present generalised anxiety disorder as a common psychopathological comorbidity. An early identification of the anxiety disorder in these patients is important to consider SSRIs treatment resulting in a significant improvement of symptoms and quality of life.
