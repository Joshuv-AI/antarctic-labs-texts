Learning Half-Spaces from Perturbed Contrastive Examples
Aryan Alavi Razavi Ravari, Farnam Mansouri, Yuxin Chen, Valentio Iverson, Adish Singla, Sandra Zilles

Abstract
We study learning under a two-step contrastive example oracle, as introduced by 14 , where each queried (or sampled) labeled example is paired with an additional contrastive example of opposite label. While Mansouri et al. assume an idealized setting, where the contrastive example is at minimum distance of the originally queried/sampled point, we introduce and analyze a mechanism, parameterized by a non-decreasing noise function f f , under which this ideal contrastive example is perturbed. The amount of perturbation is controlled by f ⁡ ( d ) f(d) , where d d is the distance of the queried/sampled point to the decision boundary. Intuitively, this results in higher-quality contrastive examples for points closer to the decision boundary. We study this model in two settings: (i) when the maximum perturbation magnitude is fixed, and (ii) when it is stochastic. For one-dimensional thresholds and for half-spaces under the uniform distribution on a bounded domain, we characterize active and passive contrastive sample complexity in dependence on the function f f . We show that, under certain conditions on f f , the presence of contrastive examples speeds up learning in terms of asymptotic query complexity and asymptotic expected query complexity.

Introduction

Contrastive information—in the form of two
similar
data points of
opposite
class—is valuable in the context of learning, in various ways. Firstly, pairing a labeled example
(x,\ell(x))
with a highly similar point
x^{\prime}
of opposite label can provide the learner with information on the location of the decision boundary and thus greatly speed up learning, as has been demonstrated in a multitude of application settings such as recommender systems
(
18
)
, reinforcement learning
(
12
)
, NLP
(
21
;
15
)
, learning formal languages
(
19
)
, or program synthesis
(
1
)
. Secondly, contrastive information can explain the predictions of a machine-learned classifier: If such classifier predicts label
\ell(x)
for datum
x
, then a datum
x^{\prime}
similar to
x
, but of opposite label, suggests that the features in which
x
and
x^{\prime}
differ are crucial for the prediction of the label for
x

(
13
)
. Thirdly, in causal inference and decision-making problems, contrastive examples are used in the form of counterfactuals, by deriving highly valuable information from simulating “what-if” situations that contrast with actual observed situations
(
2
)
.

Hence, recently the learning theory community has begun to develop and analyze formal models of learning from contrastive examples. In this context, researchers typically chose a very specific learning setting or hypothesis class
(
3
;
20
;
17
;
8
;
20
)
, or studied the effect of specific model or environment aspects in learning, such as the choice of loss function or the provision of negative examples
(
4
;
10
)
.

Most relevant to our work is a more generic recent model proposed by
14
, in which an active learner queries the label for a data point
x
, and is in addition provided with a contrastive example right on the decision boundary, namely the closest point
x^{\prime}
to
x
that has a label opposite from
\ell(x)
.
1
1

1

Technically, in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, it may happen that there is no closest
x^{\prime}
of opposite label. In that case, the contrastive data point
x^{\prime}
will be the limit of a Cauchy sequence of points whose label differs from
\ell(x)
, even if this results in
\ell(x^{\prime})=\ell(x)
.
Crucial is that the learner knows the rule by which contrastive examples are chosen. Mansouri et al. showed that this setting makes the learner very powerful, with sample complexity drastically shrinking; for example, only a single query is needed in order to learn a linear half-space. This suggests that Mansouri et al.’s model may, in some cases, trivialize learning.

Moreover, in practice it is often unrealistic to assume that an oracle can provide the learner with exactly the closest point
x^{\prime}
to
x
that has a label opposite from
\ell(x)
. Thus the drastic improvements in query complexity, due to contrastive examples, may not be attainable in practice either. We therefore propose to study variants of Mansouri et al.’s model, where the contrastive example presented to the learner is a perturbed version of the “ideal” one from the original model. Specifically, we assume that the amount of possible perturbation of the contrastive example for a point
x
may increase with the distance
r
of
x
from the decision boundary. The relationship between
r
and the permissible amount of perturbation is governed by a function
f
, by which we parameterize our model. Intuitively, the closer
x
is to the decision boundary, the higher the “quality” of the contrastive example for
x
. We study two mechanisms based on this idea, a probabilistic one and a non-probabilistic one.

While
14
considered only active learners, which always select the data point
x
for which both a label and a contrastive example will be provided, we study both active and passive settings.

Given a concept class
\mathcal{C}
, measures of interest in our study are (a) the number of data points required so that the probability of inferring an
\varepsilon
-approximation of the target concept in
\mathcal{C}
is at least
1-\delta
, in dependence on
(\varepsilon,\delta)\in(0,1]^{2}
, and (b) the number of data points required so that the expected value of the error between the conjecture and the target concept in
\mathcal{C}
is at most
\varepsilon
, in dependence on
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

Since linear half-spaces are popular objects of study in computational learning theory, and many real-world machine learning solutions are based on linear classifiers, we focus our analysis exclusively on classes of (i) one-sided threshold functions in
\mathbb{R}
, and (ii) linear half-spaces (over a unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, both with and without homogeneity assumption). Our results complement a rich literature on learning half-spaces
without
contrastive examples; in particular we compare our results to those from studies on learning half-spaces with membership queries
(
7
;
5
;
6
;
11
;
9
)
We demonstrate that our models of perturbed contrastive examples often (but not always) yield remarkable improvements (in terms of the measures mentioned above) compared to learning without contrastive examples. The improvements here depend on the choice of the function
f
that determines the permissible amount of perturbation of the “ideal” contrastive examples. At the same time, our models do not trivialize learning of half-spaces (by contrast with Mansouri et al.’s idealized model, which allows for learning half-spaces with just a single contrastive example). Thus, our proposed approach makes a significant step towards practically relevant models of learning from contrastive examples.

Preliminaries

Let
({\mathcal{X}},d)
be a metric space with bounded diameter
\sup_{x,x^{\prime}}d(x,x^{\prime})

and let
\mathcal{U}[{\mathcal{X}}]
be the uniform distribution over
{\mathcal{X}}
. In this paper, we assume that
d
is the Euclidean distance.
Further, let
\ell\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
be a labeling rule.
For any
C\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
we define the error of
C
w.r.t.
\ell

as

\mathrm{err}(C,\ell):=\Pr_{x\sim\mathcal{U}[{\mathcal{X}}]}[\ell(x)\neq C(x)]
. Here, we implicitly identify a subset of
{\mathcal{X}}
(i.e., a concept) with its binary indicator function.

This paper extends the model for learning with contrastive examples that was proposed by
(
14
)
. In said model, a learner trying to identify a labeling rule
\ell
has access to a two-step oracle. The first step corresponds to an oracle providing a labeled training data point
(x,\ell(x))
(this case is called
passive learning
) or providing the label
\ell(x)
for a data point
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
of the learner’s choice (
active learning
); the resulting data point provided in this step is called the
primary example
. The second step provides the learner with a
contrastive example

(x^{\prime},\ell(x^{\prime}))
to the previously selected primary example
(x,\ell(x))
. This step is governed by a selection mechanism
\CE
that describes how the contrastive examples are chosen; we define various such mechanisms below.

The learner thus collects a sequence of pairs of (labeled) examples
[(x_{i},\ell(x_{i})),(x^{\prime}_{i},\ell(x^{\prime}_{i}))]
for
1\leq i\leq m
, where for all
i
,
(x_{i},\ell(x_{i}))
is a primary example, and
(x^{\prime}_{i},\ell(x^{\prime}_{i}))
is the corresponding contrastive example. Using both primary and contrastive examples, the target of the learner is to output a concept
C\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}
that minimizes
\mathrm{err}(C,\ell)
.

Primary Example Collection.

We analyze two well-studied settings for collecting primary examples (the first step of the oracle). (i) Passive learning: The instances
x_{i}
of primary examples are sampled i.i.d. from
\mathcal{U}[{\mathcal{X}}]
. In this setting the sequence of pairs of examples gathered by the learner is simply an unordered set
S
of pairs of examples. (ii) Active learning: For each
i\in[m]
, the learner chooses an
x_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, for which the primary oracle then provides the label
\ell(x_{i})
. The choice of
x_{i}
may depend on the sequence of previously seen example pairs
[((x_{j},\ell(x_{j})),(x^{\prime}_{j},\ell(x^{\prime}_{j}))]
,
1\leq j<i
, i.e., the learner is adaptive.

Contrastive example collection.

We study three different options for the mechanism by which contrastive examples are selected by the oracle. The first one, proposed in the literature, describes an “idealized” setting with an often unrealistically strong oracle; we then propose two variations using a weaker oracle.

1.

Minimum Distance Model (MDM)
The minimum distance mechanism, denoted by
\CE^{d}_{\mathrm{min}}
, was introduced by
(
14
)
. Given a primary example
(x,\ell(x))\in{\mathcal{X}}\times\{0,1\}
, the oracle returns any contrastive example
(x^{\prime},\ell(x^{\prime}))
for which
x^{\prime}
belongs to

\displaystyle\CE^{d}_{\mathrm{min}}(x):=

\displaystyle\arg\min\{d(x,x^{\prime})\ |\ x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}},

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}x^{\prime}_{k}\mbox{ for a Cauchy sequence }

\displaystyle(x^{\prime}_{k})_{k}\mbox{ with }\ell(x^{\prime}_{k})\neq\ell(x)\mbox{ for all }k\}\,.

2.

Deterministic Approximate Minimum Distance Model (Deterministic AMDM)
We introduce an approximate version
\CE^{d,f}_{\app}
of
\CE^{d}_{\mathrm{min}}
, parameterized by a non-decreasing function
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Here, the oracle returns a perturbed version of the contrastive example selected by the
\CE^{d}_{\mathrm{min}}
mechanism, where the perturbation is dependent on the distance of the primary example
x
to the decision boundary of
\ell
. In addition, we impose the constraint that the label
\ell(x^{\prime})
of the contrastive example be different from
\ell(x)
. Intuitively, the closer
x
is to the decision boundary, the higher the quality of the contrastive example
(x^{\prime},\ell(x^{\prime}))
, in the sense that
x^{\prime}
is not very far from an “ideal” example in
\CE^{d}_{\mathrm{min}}(x)
.

More formally, given a primary example
(x,\ell(x))
, the oracle chooses as its contrastive example any
(x^{\prime},\ell(x^{\prime}))
where
x^{\prime}
is in

\displaystyle\CE^{d,f}_{\app}(x):=\{x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}\mid d(x^{\prime},x^{d}_{\min})\leq f(d(x,x^{d}_{\min}))

\displaystyle\mbox{ for some }x^{d}_{\min}\in\CE^{d}_{\mathrm{min}}(x),

\displaystyle\mbox{ and }\ell(x^{\prime})\neq\ell(x)\}

3.

Probabilistic Approximate Minimum Distance Model (Probabilistic AMDM)
The
\CE^{d,f}_{\app}
mechanism makes no assumption about how the contrastive example is chosen from the set
\CE^{d,f}_{\app}(x)
, enforcing an adversarial analysis in our study below. By contrast, we define a probabilistic version
\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}
of
\CE^{d,f}_{\app}
, again parameterized by a non-decreasing function
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Formally, for any
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
, let
\mathcal{D}_{x}
be a distribution over points in
{\mathcal{X}}
whose labels differ from
\ell(x)
, such that
\mathbb{E}_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}_{x}}[d(x^{\prime},x^{d}_{\min})]\leq f(d(x,x^{d}_{\min}))
for some
x^{d}_{\min}\in\CE^{d}_{\mathrm{min}}
. In the
\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}
mechanism, the oracle independently samples
x^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{D}_{x}
, and provides the contrastive example
(x^{\prime},\ell(x^{\prime}))
for
(x,\ell(x))
.

For all settings, we assume realizability, i.e., the learner outputs hypotheses from a concept class
{\mathcal{C}}
over
{\mathcal{X}}
, such that
\ell\in{\mathcal{C}}
. In the following, we formally define various sample complexity measures that we aim to study.

.
Consider the half-space

C^{*}(\mathbf{z}):=\mathbbm{1}\{\langle\omega,\mathbf{z}\rangle\geq b\}.

Let

\mathbf{x}^{\proj}:=\mathbf{x}-\frac{\langle\omega,\mathbf{x}\rangle-b}{\|\omega\|^{2}}\,\omega

denote the orthogonal projection of
\mathbf{x}
onto the boundary hyperplane of
C^{*}
.
Let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}
be any point such that
C^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\neq C^{*}(\mathbf{x})
. Then

(i)

The angle
\angle(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\proj})
is at most

\min\!\left\{\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}^{\proj}\|}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\proj}\|}\right),\ \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\proj}\|}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|}\right)\right\}.

(ii)

Let
C
be the half-space whose boundary hyperplane is perpendicular to
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}
and passes through
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, and such that
C(\mathbf{x})=C^{*}(\mathbf{x})
.
Then

\mathrm{err}(C,C^{*})\leq\frac{2^{k}\,\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}^{\proj}\|}{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\proj}\|}.

(iii)

Suppose
b=0
. Let
C^{0}
be a homogeneous half-space whose boundary hyperplane is perpendicular to
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}
such that
C^{0}(\mathbf{x})=C^{*}(\mathbf{x})
. Then

\mathrm{err}(C^{0},C^{*})\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}^{\proj}\|}{\pi\,\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\proj}\|}.

Figure
1
illustrates Lemma
3.2
(ii). Statements (i) and (iii) can be illustrated analogously.
Let
\mathbf{x}
be a point at distance
r
from a target half-space.
Lemma
3.2
shows that, given
\mathbf{x}
and its contrastive example from the deterministic AMDM oracle, a learner can find a concept with error at most (i)
\frac{f(r)}{\pi r}
if the target classifier is homogeneous, and (ii)
\frac{2^{k}f(r)}{r}
otherwise. Thus, to obtain a concept with small error, it suffices to find a point that is close to the target concept.
In particular, for homogeneous half-spaces, any point sufficiently close to the center has this property.
In the following corollary, we show that this makes active contrastive learning of homogeneous half-spaces trivial even with respect to the more general probabilistic AMDM.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be a non-decreasing function such that
f(x)\leq x^{1+c}
where
c>0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\act}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{HHS}},\CE^{d,f}_{\app}\right](\varepsilon,\delta)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\act}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{HHS}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}\right](\varepsilon,\delta)=1\,.

The proof of the following theorem shows that for non-homogeneous half-spaces, one can find a point close to the decision boundary by first querying
\mathbf{0}
, and then repeatedly querying the contrastive example of the previous query.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be a non-decreasing function such that
f(x)\leq x^{1+c}
where
c>0
. Then

\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\act}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{HS}},\CE^{d,f}_{\app}\right](\varepsilon,\delta)\leq\tau_{f}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\rightarrow\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)\,,

where
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{c}}
.

Proof.

Denote
m=\tau_{f}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\rightarrow\varepsilon^{\prime}\right).
Suppose at time
t=1
, the learner queries
x_{1}=\mathbf{0}_{k}
and for
t\geq 2
the learner queries
x_{t}=x^{\prime}_{t-1}
. For each time
t
, let
x^{\proj}_{t}
be the projection of
x_{t}
on
\ell
. By definition

\displaystyle\dist(x_{t},\ell)

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{t}-x^{\proj}_{t-1}\|\leq f(\|x_{t-1}-x^{\proj}_{t-1}\|)

\displaystyle=f(\dist(x_{t-1,\ell}))\,.

Therefore, after
m
queries, we have
\dist(x_{m},\ell)\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
. Suppose the learner returns the half-space
C
corresponding to the hyperplane perpendicular to
x^{\prime}_{m}-x_{m}
at
x^{\prime}_{m}
. Due to Lemma
3.2
(ii) we have

\mathrm{err}(C,\ell)\leq\frac{2^{k}f(\varepsilon^{\prime})}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\leq 2^{k}{\varepsilon^{\prime}}^{c}=\varepsilon.

This completes the proof.
∎

Observe that the active contrastive sample complexity of half-spaces can be easily bounded from below by the active contrastive sample complexity of thresholds.

Theorem 3.5
.

For any non-decreasing function
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\act}[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{HS}},\CE^{d,f}_{\app}]\left(\varepsilon,\delta\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\act}[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{thresh}},\CE^{d,f}_{\app}]\left(2^{k}\varepsilon,\delta\right).

3.2
Passive Contrastive Sample Complexity

This subsection focuses on the passive contrastive sample complexity of one-dimensional threshold functions and homogeneous half-spaces with respect to the deterministic AMDM.

Again we begin with threshold functions. The key intuition is that it suffices to identify a point very close to the target threshold (here the learner uses the largest positive example). If this point has distance
r
to the target threshold, then by the deterministic AMDM its contrastive example lies within distance at most
f(r)
. Thus, once
r
is small, a single contrastive example brings the learner even closer, yielding improved sample complexity.

Theorem 3.6
.

For any non-decreasing and invertible function
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
, and for all
\delta\in(0,1]
and
\varepsilon\leq\min\!\left(\frac{f(\tfrac{1}{4})}{2},\tfrac{1}{4}\right)
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\passive}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{thresh}},\CE^{d,f}_{\app}\right](\varepsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle\in O\!\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\min(f^{-1}(\varepsilon),\varepsilon)}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\passive}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{thresh}},\CE^{d,f}_{\app}\right](\varepsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle\in\Omega\!\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\min\left(f^{-1}\!\left(2\varepsilon\right),\varepsilon\right)}\right).

The core idea behind the threshold analysis also applies to linear separators in general: if the learner can find a point in the sample that is close to the target decision boundary (here, it chooses the example in the data set with the smallest distance to its contrastive example), then its contrastive example provides enough geometric information to construct a hypothesis with bounded error via Lemma
3.2
, both for homogeneous and non-homogeneous half-spaces.

However, due to the
2^{k}
factor in the error bound of Lemma
3.2
(ii) for non-homogeneous half-spaces, this approach yields passive learning guarantees (in contrast to active learning) that scale exponentially with the dimension
k
. This is undesirable, since standard passive learning bounds for half-spaces typically depend only linearly on
k
. Fortunately, Lemma
3.2
(iii) allows us to circumvent this issue for homogeneous half-spaces. In fact, our analysis for homogeneous half-spaces is completely dimension-free.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be such that
f(x)\leq x^{2}
and
g(x):=\frac{f(x)}{x}
is non-decreasing and invertible. Then

\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\passive}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{HHS}},\CE^{d,f}_{\app}\right](\varepsilon,\delta)\;\in\;O\!\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{g^{-1}\left(\pi\varepsilon\right)}\right).

4
Probabilistic Approximate Minimum Distance Model

4.1
Active Expected Sample Complexity

Next, we study the active expected contrastive sample complexity
of one-dimensional threshold functions with respect to the probabilistic AMDM. As can be seen from Table
1(b)
, in this case for both representative choices of the noise function
f
, the probabilistic AMDM exhibits a noticeable decrease in sample complexity compared to the deterministic AMDM, even for the class of threshold functions.
Nevertheless, both attain a non-trivial sample complexity improvement over membership queries alone.

Crucially, our analysis requires
g(x):=\frac{f(x)}{x}
to be non-decreasing and convex. Therefore, in the following lemma we first state a basic property of convex functions.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
Z
be a random variable supported on
[a,b]
. Then for any convex function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathbb{E}[f(Z)]\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]-a}{b-a}f(b)+\frac{b-\mathbb{E}[Z]}{b-a}f(a).

The key idea of our analysis is as follows. Suppose that at some time
t
the active learner has a version space of size
r_{t}
. We show that by querying the midpoint of the version space and one of its endpoints, and using their contrastive examples, the learner obtains a new version space of size
r_{t+1}\in[0,r_{t}/2]
, where
\mathbb{E}[r_{t+1}]\leq f(r_{t}/2)
. Lemma
4.1
then allows us to bound the expected size of the final version space.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be such that
f(x)\leq x
and
g(x):=\frac{f(x)}{x}

is non-decreasing and convex. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\expc,\act}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{thresh}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}\right](\varepsilon)\leq

\displaystyle\min\left\{n\geq 0\,\middle|\,2^{-\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}g(2^{-i})\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

A key observation is that in Lemma
4.1
, the upper bound is achieved in the extreme case where the random variable
Z
has only two atoms, at
a
and
b
. This suggests an adversarial strategy for lower bounds: at each time step, choose the distribution of contrastive examples to be supported on two atoms, one at the target concept and the other at an endpoint of the current version space. With this key idea, we obtain a lower bound on the expected sample complexity of active learning for thresholds with respect to
\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be a non-decreasing function. Denote
g(x):=\min\!\left(\frac{f(x)}{x},1\right)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\expc,\act}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{thresh}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}\right](\varepsilon)\geq

\displaystyle\min\left\{n\geq 0\,\middle|\,2^{-(2n+1)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}g(4^{-i})\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

The above theorem also implies a lower bound for the active contrastive sample complexity.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be a non-decreasing function. Denote
g(x):=\min\!\left(\frac{f(x)}{x},1\right)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\act}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{thresh}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}\right](\varepsilon,\delta)\geq

\displaystyle\min\left(\min\left\{n\geq 0\,\middle|\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}g(4^{-i})\leq\delta\right\},\frac{\log_{2}(1/\varepsilon)-1}{2}\right)\,.

4.2
Expected Sample Requirement for Accuracy
\varepsilon

In the previous subsection, we observed a significant drop-off in the active expected contrastive sample complexity from the deterministic to the probabilistic AMDM, even for threshold functions. In this subsection, instead of bounding the expected error, we focus on the expected number of samples a learner requires in order to guarantee error at most
\varepsilon
—a quantity, which we call the
expected sample requirement for accuracy
\varepsilon
. Interestingly, for this quantity in contrast with the previous subsection, we are able to derive bounds for both thresholds and half-spaces, and these bounds are similar to those we obtained in Section
3.1
under the deterministic model, even in the probabilistic setting.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a concept class over
{\mathcal{X}}
, let
\CE
refer to a mechanism for selecting contrastive examples and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be an active learning algorithm. For every
\ell\in{\mathcal{C}}
, let
N_{{\mathcal{A}},\ell,\varepsilon}
be the random variable representing the minimal
m\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\mathrm{err}(C_{\mathcal{A},\ell,m},\ell)\leq\varepsilon
. Here
C_{\mathcal{A},\ell,m}
is the final hypothesis output by
\mathcal{A}
after interacting with the two-step oracle for
m
steps. Then define
\mathcal{N}({\mathcal{C}},\CE,{\mathcal{A}},\varepsilon):=\sup_{\ell\in{\mathcal{C}}}\mathbb{E}[N_{{\mathcal{A}},\ell,\varepsilon}]
to be the
expected sample requirement
of
{\mathcal{A}}
for achieving accuracy
\varepsilon
on
{\mathcal{C}}
with respect to
\CE
. Also, define

\mathcal{N}_{\act}({\mathcal{C}},\CE,\varepsilon)=\inf_{\text{active algorithm }{\mathcal{A}}}\mathcal{N}({\mathcal{C}},\CE,{\mathcal{A}},\varepsilon)\,.

We begin by analyzing threshold functions. Learning proceeds in several sub-phases. In each sub-phase, suppose the current version space is an interval of size
r
. The learner repeatedly queries both endpoints of the interval until their contrastive examples are less than
4f(r)
apart. We then show that each sub-phase takes constant time in expectation. The key idea is to repeat a procedure until a benchmark is met; we refer to this procedure as
verification
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be a non-decreasing
function. Then,
for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,

\mathcal{N}_{\act}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{thresh}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop},\varepsilon\right)\leq 4\tau_{4f}\!\left(1\rightarrow 2\varepsilon\right).

The above theorem also immediately implies an upper bound for the active contrastive sample complexity.

Corollary 4.7
.

For any non-decreasing function
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\act}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{thresh}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}\right](\varepsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle\leq 8\tau_{4f}\!\left(1\rightarrow 2\varepsilon\right)+16\ln\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

We now proceed to analyzing half-spaces. The core idea is similar to the threshold case: learning is again done in several sub-phases, and in each sub-phase the learner performs a verification process. The main difference is that verification is more intricate for half-spaces. Lemma
4.8
explicitly describes this verification procedure.

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{0}_{k},\tfrac{1}{2})
and let
\ell\in{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{HS}}
,
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
. Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be a non-decreasing function such that
f(a)\leq\tfrac{a}{4}
. Denote
r=\dist(x,\ell)
. Then there is a learner that, in expectation, with 4 queries from
\CE^{d,f}_{\mathrm{prob}}
finds a
z^{*}
such that (i)
\dist(z^{*},\ell)\leq r
, and (ii) with probability at least
1/2
we have
\dist(z^{*},\ell)\leq 3f(2r)
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be a non-decreasing function such that
f(x)\leq\tfrac{x^{1+c}}{4}
. Then, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
,

\mathcal{N}_{\act}\left({\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{HS}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop},\varepsilon\right)\leq 8\tau_{\tilde{f}}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\rightarrow\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)+8,

where
\tilde{f}(x)=3f(2x)
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{k-1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{c}}
.

Remark 4.10
.

For half-spaces in contrast with threshold functions, the learner would not be able to verify when the error of its hypothesis is less than
\varepsilon
.

The above theorem also implies an upper bound for the active contrastive sample complexity.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be a non-decreasing function such that
f(x)\leq\tfrac{x^{1+c}}{4}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\act}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{HS}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}\right](\varepsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle\leq 32\tau_{\tilde{f}}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\rightarrow\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)+136\log_{2}\frac{3}{\delta}\,,

where
\tilde{f}(x)=3f(2x)
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{k-1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{c}}
.

An interesting phenomenon can be observed in Table
1(b)
.
For the noise functions
f
introduced, we observe a drop-off in the active
expected
sample complexity when comparing
\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}
to
\CE^{d,f}_{\app}
, even for threshold functions.
Nevertheless, for both
{\mathcal{C}}\in\{{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{thresh}},{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{HS}}\}
, the expected sample requirement for accuracy
\varepsilon
,

\mathcal{N}_{\act}({\mathcal{C}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop},\varepsilon)
, remains of the same order as

\mathcal{M}_{\Pr,\act}\!\left[{\mathcal{C}},\CE^{d,f}_{\app}\right](\varepsilon,1)=\mathcal{M}_{\exp,\act}\!\left[{\mathcal{C}},\CE^{d,f}_{\app}\right](\varepsilon).

Intuitively, this suggests that while with a small number of examples the learner is likely to achieve small error, there remains a non-negligible probability that the error is very large; equivalently, for a fixed sample size, the error distribution is highly skewed.

4.3
Passive Expected Contrastive Sample Complexity

Finally, we analyze the passive expected contrastive sample complexity of one-dimensional threshold functions and homogeneous half-spaces with respect to the probabilistic AMDM.

First, consider learning of threshold functions. We use Lemma
4.12
(stated below) to convert the high-probability guarantees from Theorem
3.6
for passive contrastive learning under
\CE^{d,f}_{\app}
into bounds in expectation. Next, observe that the algorithm in Theorem
3.6
relies on only a single contrastive example. Therefore, it achieves the same expected error under the probabilistic AMDM as under the deterministic one.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
Z
be a random variable such that
Z\in[0,\gamma]
and
\Pr[Z>\varepsilon]\leq\delta
. Then

\mathbb{E}[Z]<\gamma\delta+\varepsilon(1-\delta).

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be non-decreasing and invertible ,
\delta\in(0,1]
, and
\varepsilon<\min\!\left(\frac{f(\tfrac{1}{4})}{4},\tfrac{1}{8}\right)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\exp,\passive}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{thresh}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}\right](\varepsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle\in O\!\left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\min(f^{-1}(\varepsilon/2),\varepsilon)}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\exp,\passive}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{thresh}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}\right](\varepsilon,\delta)

\displaystyle\in\Omega\!\left(\frac{1}{\min(f^{-1}(4\varepsilon),\varepsilon)}\right).

For homogeneous half-spaces, we first show that the error of the same algorithm introduced in Theorem
3.7
for passive contrastive learning with respect to
\CE^{d,f}_{\app}
can be bounded by a quantity that depends only on the point
x_{i^{*}}
closest to the boundary
\ell
and its contrastive example. This allows us to bound the algorithm’s expected error, and in particular implies that the algorithm attains the same expected error under the probabilistic AMDM as under the deterministic one. Finally, we use Lemma
4.12
to convert the high-probability analysis from Theorem
3.7
into bounds in expectation.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{HHS}}=\{\mathbbm{1}\{\langle\omega,\mathbf{x}\rangle\geq 0\}\mid\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\}
for
{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{0}_{k},\frac{1}{2})
be the class of homogeneous half-spaces. Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be such that
f(x)\leq x^{2}
and
g(x):=\frac{f(x)}{x}
is non-decreasing and invertible. Then

\mathcal{M}_{\exp,\passive}\left[{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathrm{HHS}},\CE^{d,f}_{\prop}\right](\varepsilon)\;\in\;O\!\left(\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{g^{-1}\left(\frac{\pi\varepsilon}{2}\right)}\right).

5
Conclusion

We studied learning from contrastive examples under the minimum distance paradigm, moving beyond the idealized setting in which the learner always receives an exact closest contrastive point. We introduced and analyzed two perturbed variants of this model, namely the deterministic approximate minimum distance mechanism and its probabilistic counterpart. While learning under the exact minimum distance model can be trivial for the concept classes we study, these perturbations render the problem non-trivial and lead to a richer sample-complexity landscape.

Despite the added difficulty, both noisy mechanisms still yield substantial improvements over membership query learning. For thresholds, homogeneous half-spaces, and general half-spaces, we established upper and lower bounds showing that contrastive feedback can significantly reduce the number of queries required to learn an accurate classifier.

Beyond these quantitative bounds, our analysis also reveals a qualitative separation between two performance notions in the probabilistic model. Specifically, we observed that the expected number of samples required to
guarantee
error at most
\varepsilon
can differ markedly from the number of samples sufficient to achieve
expected
error at most
\varepsilon
. This indicates that under probabilistic perturbations, the error after a fixed number of queries can be highly skewed—typically small, but occasionally very large.
