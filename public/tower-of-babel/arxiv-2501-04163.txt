Abstract
. All creative tasks require creators to iteratively produce, select, and discard potentially useful ideas. Now, creativity tools include generative AI features (e.g., Photoshop Generative Fill) that increase the number of alternatives creators consider due to rapid experiments with text prompts and random generations. Creators use tedious manual systems for organizing their prior ideas by saving file versions or hiding layers, but they lack the support they want for \rev reusing prior alternatives in personal work or in communication with others. We present HistoryPalette, a system that supports exploration and reuse of prior designs in generative image creation and editing. Using HistoryPalette, \rev creators and their collaborators explore a “palette” of prior design alternatives organized by spatial position, topic category, and creation time. HistoryPalette enables creators to quickly preview and reuse their prior work. In \rev creative professional and client collaborator user studies, participants generated and edited images by exploring and reusing past design alternatives with HistoryPalette.

1.
Introduction

Text-to-image generation tools such as Midjourney
(
MidJourney 2022
)
, Dall-E
(
OpenAI 2021
)
, and Photoshop Generative Fill
(
ado [n. d.]
)

reduce the time, effort and knowledge required to make high-fidelity images.
However, prompt-based generation trades direct control for underspecified text prompts such that creators use frequent trial-and-error to reach their final outcome.
Recent work helps creators improve their prompts by suggesting styles
(
Liu et al. 2022a
;
Wang et al. 2024a
)
, providing refinements
(
Brade et al. 2023
;
Feng et al. 2023
)
, and adjusting model attention to prompt text
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
, but each generation still takes time, costs computing power, and yields uncertain outcomes.
Such costs compound in projects that require many generated elements (
e.g.
, scene composition, photo editing, illustrating slides) as creators try multiple prompts per element and each prompt generates multiple alternatives.
While discarded generations do not suit a creator’s immediate needs, they represent potentially valuable alternatives for future use.
Rather than improving prompts, our work seeks to improve generative workflows by supporting the
exploration
and
reuse
of prior generations.

Managing creative histories is a longstanding challenge across all creative work as creators try, discard, and reuse ideas to reach their final composition
(
Schön 2017
;
Shneiderman 2007
)
.
These histories provide
an ordered record
of the creator’s process and a
collection of alternatives
for communication or reuse
(
Shneiderman 2007
;
Sterman et al. 2022
)
.
For example, a digital artist may use their history to show a collaborator how they created a portrait, or revisit their previous portrait backgrounds to show alternatives
(
Sterman et al. 2022
)
.
Currently creators must manually manage alternatives by using undo/redo logs, saving separate files
(
Sharmin et al. 2009
;
Li et al. 2021
;
O’Leary et al. 2018
)
, or using layers
(
ado [n. d.]
;
fig [n. d.]
)
.
Such manual approaches are error-prone as creators forget to save, struggle to locate, or inadvertently delete alternatives —
thus creators can not access the alternatives they want to find
(
Sharmin et al. 2009
;
Sterman et al. 2022
)
.
Prior research makes histories easier to navigate for personal and collaborative use

(
Klemmer et al. 2002
;
Grossman et al. 2010
;
Angert et al. 2023
;
Dang et al. 2023
;
Rawn et al. 2023
)
by providing timeline annotations
(
Klemmer et al. 2002
;
Grossman et al. 2010
)
and preserving branching
(
Dang et al. 2023
;
Angert et al. 2023
;
Rawn et al. 2023
)
of project histories.
Both current practice and prior research primarily order creative histories chronologically.
While time-based organization supports review of the creative process, finding specific alternatives (
e.g.
, previously created trees or backgrounds) requires manual review of the entire project history that can contain hundreds to thousands of edits.

New prompt-based workflows intensify and also provide opportunities to solve history management challenges.
While the trial-and-error of prompt-based workflows creates more alternatives to manage, prompt-based workflows also provide semantic information absent in traditional editing. Each prompt-based edit provides a semantically meaningful history segment (
e.g.
, a tree vs. a set of strokes) along with two semantically meaningful handles (a target region and a prompt). Such handles may better match how creators and their collaborators think about the work — in terms of what content appears where, rather than when it was created.
Recent advances in generative models also offer opportunities for history management and reuse as modern vision language models can filter out low quality edits by comparing the prompt text to the generated result, and generation-based smoothing can incorporate past alternatives into current work.
We investigate the potential to use such advances for history organization to support the exploration and reuse of the project history in generative workflows.

Towards this goal,
\rev
we contribute
HistoryPalette
, a system for exploring and reusing prior alternatives during image generation and editing (Figure
1
).
HistoryPalette’s Generation View supports an image generation workflow similar to Photoshop Generative Fill
(
ado [n. d.]
)
where creators iteratively select a region of the image to change, then write a prompt to generate a new image that fits seamlessly within that region.
At each editing step, HistoryPalette records a version of the entire image (i.e.
version
) and the latest text-to-image generation edit consisting of a region, image, and prompt (i.e. a partial-project
alternative
).
HistoryPalette then provides four history views: a
position palette
that lets creators access alternatives at the current position, a
concept palette
lets creators access alternatives clustered by prompt themes, a
time palette
that lets creators access alternatives by time, and a linear version timeline that lets creators access the version history of the entire project.

\rev
While the position palette and concept palette are unique to history palette, the time palette and version timeline more closely mimic temporal organization in existing history management.
Creators can hover over any alternative or version to preview it in place, or click to paste it into their project, optionally rasterizing the image to smooth artifacts.
HistoryPalette further supports history filtering via automatic removal of image generation failures using a vision language model, as well as manual removals/favorites for later use or sharing with clients.

We conducted a technical evaluation and two exploratory user studies:
\rev
a creative professional study
with three participants (41 years combined experience) to explore the potential of HistoryPalette in creative workflows of creative professionals, and a
client collaborator study
with eight participants to explore the potential for efficient history access as a collaboration tool.
Our technical evaluation demonstrated that while 18.8% of prompts generated errors, our filter identified them with 93% accuracy (86% precision, 76% recall).
Our user studies demonstrated how semantic organization of generative histories can benefit both creators and their collaborators.
Across both studies, all participants used our semantic organization methods (position or concept palette) more often than they used the traditional time-based organization (time palette).
Professional participants used prior alternatives to inspire new ideas and test alternatives, and expressed enthusiasm about using HistoryPalette in the future for open-ended and collaborative work.
Our client collaborator evaluation revealed that participants used HistoryPalette’s prior generations (11.88 times, SD=6.09 times) more often than they used new generations (4.62 times, SD=1.73 times) to edit the image, reporting that they preferred the efficiency and control of reusing prior generations.
Client collaborator participants also expressed excitement to use HistoryPalette for providing feedback, learning expert prompt strategies, and exploring personal preferences.
Our findings demonstrate the potential for semantic organization to encourage reuse in generative workflows.
We conclude with opportunities to generalize to new domains, implications for prompt-based workflows, and implications for collaborative use.

2.
Background

HistoryPalette supports creators in image generation workflows using their prior history as a source of inspiration and reuse.
Our work relates to prior work in history reuse for authoring and communication, using external reference examples for ideation and reuse, and interfaces for improving text-to-image generation.

2.1.
History Keeping and Reuse for Authoring

Creative work requires creators to ideate, implement, discard, reuse, and recombine ideas until they reach their final composition in a “reflective conversation” with their work
(
Schön 2017
)
. As creators often discard many useful ideas in their process, a longstanding goal for creativity support tools has been to enable “rich history keeping”
(
Shneiderman 2007
)
to support creators in recording what they have tried, comparing alternatives, revisiting and reusing prior versions, and communicating variations to collaborators
(
Shneiderman 2007
)
.
Currently, practitioners manually store rich histories of their projects for later inspiration and reuse via files, copies or layers
(
Sterman et al. 2022
;
Sharmin et al. 2009
)
.
Layers can maintain partial-project alternatives
(
Terry and Mynatt 2002
)
but can be difficult to organize or explore on large projects.
For example, with Photoshop Generative Fill, each generation produces a new layer with three alternatives hidden inside the layer (defaults to a chronological order) such that finding and reusing prior generations remains challenging. In addition, creators often need to flatten their layers to improve subsequent generations thus discarding their alternatives. We aim to support on-demand exploration and reuse of the history of alternatives.

Prior work has explored using history for exploration and reuse in domains where code or parameters generate the image (
e.g.
, for 2D creative coding, generative art, and image manipulation)
(
Subbaraman et al. 2023
;
Angert et al. 2023
;
Rawn et al. 2023
;
Zaman et al. 2015
;
Terry et al. 2004
)
.
For example, Parallel Paths added variations in place during parameter adjustment for image manipulations (
e.g.
, degrees of application of the whirl filter)
(
Terry et al. 2004
)
.
More recently Quickpose, Spellburst, GEM-NI, and Subbaraman et al. explore approaches for branching and remixing alternatives in creative code and generative design
(
Angert et al. 2023
;
Rawn et al. 2023
;
Zaman et al. 2015
;
Subbaraman et al. 2023
)
.
All such prior work focuses on parameter- or code-based authoring and does not yet address creative tasks that operate primarily on pixels rather than parameters (
e.g.
, digital painting, photo manipulation, video editing).
Pixel-based domains lack a clear structure for identifying salient temporal and spatial regions for replacement or reuse (
e.g.
, what set of edits or brush strokes make up a salient scene lighting change).
We explore the potential for history reuse in pixel-based editing by investigating prompt-based workflows that offer new structure (
e.g.
, prompts and regions).
We use the metaphor of a
history palette
inspired by Sterman et al.’s formative work identifying that creators desire to use their history as a “palette of materials” for reuse.

3.
HistoryPalette

We designed a system to support history exploration and reuse for image generation workflows.

3.1.
Design Rationale

We designed HistoryPalette with four main goals:

G1: Palettes of Alternatives for Discovery.
Creators use their past work as a “palette of materials” for future use
(
Sterman et al. 2022
)
. We thus create palettes that support the use of workflow primitives (
i.e.
, a palette
alternative
– or, an image region with a prompt and a generation) for ease of alternative discovery and reuse. We considered three dimensions to support quickly discovering and dipping from the palettes: time, canvas position, and creative concept.
Time provides a default index into the creator’s process. Time groups together related actions
(
Grossman et al. 2010
)
and supports recall for the project creator (
e.g.
, “I know I created flowers after changing the sunset”).
Time most closely mirrors the behavior that layers provide upon generation today
(
ado [n. d.]
)
.
Canvas position supports creators exploring their palettes directly by clicking on the intended object in the image to see local alternatives.
As position lets creators map their intent (
e.g.
, change the flowers) onto the canvas (an x,y coordinate on the flowers), it is useful when creators know what they want to change.
Position palettes limit discovery as they must be hidden until clicked to avoid canvas occlusion, so we aim to create a palette of discoverable alternatives that preserves the relevance of position palettes.
Thus, we create concept groups with text labels to support browsing for alternatives of interest.
Concept groups serve as a palette for creators attempting to problem solve a portion of the design (
e.g.
, “landscaping” palette with grass, bushes, and gardens, or a “buildings” palette with castles and houses).

G2: Support Rapid Reuse of Alternatives.

To let users freely explore and reuse the palette of alternatives, we aimed to support fast in-place previews of alternatives and on-demand flexible reuse. To support rapid reuse, we provide previews on hover to create a low-risk environment for experimenting with alternatives. Such experiments may support decision-making before expensive edit operations.
We then explore two options to let users place elements: paste that simply places the alternative on the canvas, and a generative approach that smooths the image and new alternative to remove editing artifacts.

G3: Provide History Filters and Favorites.
Histories de-risk exploration
(
Shneiderman 2007
)
, so we preserve the entire history. However, the history contains a spectrum of alternatives from equally viable options to complete failures (
e.g.
, a house with a different roof vs. the prompt “house” fails to generate a house).
Generation quality, personal taste, and current image state all dictate the usefulness of alternatives, but “bad” alternatives can clutter alternative palettes.
We aim to filter out alternatives that are not likely to be useful, then let designers further curate their history manually for personal and collaborative use
(
O’Leary et al. 2018
)
.
All filtering and removal are non-destructive such that users can recover filtered alternatives in the interface.

G4: Support Multiple Audiences.
Histories are useful for the creators, but also for collaborators and novices
(
Shneiderman 2007
;
Grossman et al. 2010
;
Grabler et al. 2009
)
.
\rev
While prior work supported novices to learn from the designer’s history to perform the task on their own
(
Grabler et al. 2009
;
Grossman et al. 2010
)
, prior work has not yet explored supporting collaborators to reuse alternatives in a designer’s history.
\rev
If successful, collaborators can use the same abstractions provided to creators to understand the design process and considered alternatives such that they may build common ground with the designer to give useful feedback
(
Shneiderman 2007
)
.
Thus, we aim to support audiences beyond the creator via externally meaningful history organization and surfacing expert prompt use.

3.2.
HistoryPalette Overview

HistoryPalette lets users compose images by iteratively using image generation and views of the design history to explore and reuse past alternatives. HistoryPalette features three main components: the
generation view
that lets users generate images and edit them using image inpainting, three design history palettes (
concept palette
,
position palette
, and
time palette
) that let users browse and reuse their prior history, and the
version timeline
that lets users browse and reuse prior versions of their whole project.
We designed HistoryPalette’s
generation view
to mimic closed-source workflows such as Adobe Photoshop’s Generative Fill
(
ado [n. d.]
)
, and the
version timeline
mimics chronological project history that has been explored elsewhere
(
Zaman et al. 2015
)
— and we specifically follow prior work in visualizing thumbnails of each version to support skimming
(
Klemmer et al. 2002
)
.
Similarly, we implemented the time palette for simple chronological ordering as a baseline — it mimics the behavior of sequentially created layers
(
ado [n. d.]
;
fig [n. d.]
)
.
Our system uniquely explores how history palettes can be used in concert with existing creativity support tools and history-keeping approaches to enable design exploration and reuse.

HistoryPalette uses an image generation and inpainting pipeline to support users generating images, and methods for filtering and sorting design alternatives to support ease of design exploration.

3.3.
Interface

The
composition view
(Figure
1
, left) starts blank or with an uploaded background image and displays the current composition. Authors can add to their composition via the generation view, the palettes, or the version timeline.

HistoryPalette’s
generation view
includes a prompt box along with optional sliders to change generation parameters for strength, guidance, and inference steps. To use the generation view, creators draw a
region
in the composition where they want to place their generation, write a
text prompt
, optionally change parameters, and click submit. The generation view provides four generations
alternatives
that creators can hover over to preview in-place. Creators can click a generation alternative to add it to their composition or click undo to try again.
All discarded and selected alternatives from the generation view are added to HistoryPalette’s palettes of alternatives for later use.
When we add the generated alternatives to HistoryPalette’s palettes, we first run a
filter
over each generated image alternative and prompt pair to filter out alternatives that are not likely to be useful. For example, if the creator drew a circle in the lake and prompted “boat” but the generation failed to add a boat, we flag the alternative as a failed generation. Failed generations are hidden by default, but users can press ‘shift’ to display them in the palettes where they appear with a lower opacity (Figure
1
, right).
Each composition change also adds a
version
of the entire composition to the version timeline.

HistoryPalette’s
palettes
all display generation alternatives and let creators explore, preview, and reuse the prior project history. All palettes let users
hover
over an alternative to preview an alternative in place or
click
it to add to the composition.
Once creators add the alternative they can transform it by dragging the alternative around the image or changing the scale with editing handles.
Creators can then save the alternative in place with
paste
to place the alternative directly or
rasterize
to regenerate the entire composition with the alternative in place. Rasterize decreases alternative background artifacts, but takes more time than paste and occasionally smooths out other composition details.

Creators access the
position palette
by clicking on a region in the composition canvas to see other alternatives generated for that region. For example, a creator may click on a house in an image to see house alternatives generated at that position (Figure
2
), and hover over the alternatives to preview them in place.

Creators access the
concept palette
and
time palette
via tabs (Figure
1
, top). The concept palette groups alternatives by prompt themes (Figure
1
, left) such that creators can explore the palette to add prior alternatives to their scene. For example, a creator may skim the building alternatives in the concept palette (Figure
2
) and decide to add other dwellings from the palette into their scene. The time palette simply includes all alternatives organized from most recent at the top to least recent at the bottom (Figure
2
).

Creators can flag or unflag any alternatives as failed alternatives, and they can
favorite
alternatives for later. For example, a creator may favorite an alternative to show to a client with HistoryPalette. Creators can “show prompts” (Figure
1
, top right) to see what prompts created each alternative.

Finally, HistoryPalette includes a version timeline that displays every prior version of the composition. Similar to generation alternatives, creators can
hover
over the version timeline to preview the prior composition in the composition window, and
click
the version to restore the prior version. When creators restore the prior version, the rest of the history remains. The new version is simply added at the end of the version timeline and the palettes of all the prior alternatives remain available.

3.4.
Technical Methods

Our technical pipeline consists of an image painting pipeline to support generative fill, rasterization to allow reuse of alternatives, an approach for filtering alternatives to hide generation mistakes, and approaches for organizing alternatives by position and category to support alternative browsing and selection.

3.4.1.
Image Inpainting Pipeline

We implemented an image inpainting pipeline to mimic existing generative fill features (
e.g.
, Photoshop Generative Fill
(
ado [n. d.]
)
) in our system.

\rev
Once a user selects a region on the image canvas and provides a prompt (
e.g.
draws a circle around the middle part of the image and prompts “house”), we pass the full canvas, selected region, and prompt to an in-painting module that uses Stable Diffusion
(
Rombach et al. 2021
)
to generate an image from the user’s prompt, and ControlNet
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
to constrain the generation to a specified region.

\rev
To improve the blend of the generated image region into the background canvas, we increase the size of the user’s selected region by 1.3x before providing the bounding box to ControlNet
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. We determined this factor empirically through trial and error.
We set our default parameters to a strength of 1.0 (
\rev
how much the selected area should be denoised before generation), a guidance of 8.0 (how much the model follows the prompt) and a step size of 70 (the number of inference steps the model performs to generate the output image). We determined these values empirically
\rev
and we allow users to optionally adjust these parameters in the Generation View of our interface. We additionally use a negative prompt to discourage unwanted generations (“text, bad quality, poorly drawn, bad anatomy, blurry”).

\rev
We run the pipeline four times to create four generated alternatives for the user’s prompt and selected region (similar to existing text-to-image generation approaches
(
ado [n. d.]
;
MidJourney 2022
)
). We then process all alternatives to be included in the history.

\rev

When users want to reuse prior generated regions, we allow users to optionally resize, transform, and directly paste the alternative into the canvas. However, the background of the prior alternative may not match the current background. To support users in blending their alternatives into the new location, we implemented a refinement pipeline where we used the image-to-image refiner model from the two-stage Stable Diffusion XL pipeline which is a specialized high-resolution model
(
sdx [n. d.]
)
. The model encodes the original pixel space image into a latent space image using VAE Encode
(
Kingma and Welling 2013
)
then applies an SDEdit (an image-to-image model) to the latent space image
(
Meng et al. 2021
)
to obtain the final image.
We use “high resolution, high quality” as the prompt for the refiner model and perform only 7 inference steps so that we avoid the image changing too much from the user input.

3.4.2.
Filtering Alternatives

When users generate an image, the inpainting pipeline occasionally fails such that the generation may be successful (follows the prompt) or entirely unsuccessful (ignores the prompt). For example, if the user selects a grass region and prompts the model to generate “yellow flowers” the model may ignore the prompt and generate a new patch of grass or a bush. To ensure such failures do not clutter the history palette, we classify alternatives as successful or unsuccessful. Specifically, we prompted a multimodal LLM with vision capabilities (GPT-4V
(
OpenAI 2024
)
) to serve as a binary classifier of successful (True) or unsuccessful (False) image generations. We provide the model with our prompt, the generated image with the user’s masked selection only (
e.g.
, the mask with a generated bush and surrounding grass), and the user’s original prompt (
e.g.
, yellow flowers).
We use a few-shot approach by providing successful and unsuccessful examples.
We run our classifier each time the system stores a new generated alternative.
We include our prompt in the supplementary material.

3.4.3.
Organizing and Reusing Alternatives

To power our concept palette, we used a large language model (GPT-4-turbo
(
OpenAI 2024
)
) to cluster a list of tuples consisting of alternative indices and their prompts from the entire history (
e.g.
, [0] yellow flowers, [1] bushes, [2] pink flowers) and output a JSON dictionary with the cluster names and the alternative indices that belong to the respective cluster (
e.g.
, {"flowers": [0, 2], … }).
For each new alternative that the user generates, we re-cluster all alternatives.
For our position palette, we store a
N\times N
matrix representing a
N\times N
pixel image where each entry contains a chronologically ordered list of alternatives for the respective pixel based on the mask of the alternative. For each new alternative, we update this matrix. When a user clicks on the image, we display the palette according to this matrix. If the alternative occupies the entire image, we do not add the image to the matrix and instead display the alternative in the top left of the image to indicate that it is a global rather than a local alternative.
For the time palette, we store a timestamp when each alternative is created to order it chronologically.

3.5.
Implementation

We implemented HistoryPalette’s frontend using React and Fabric.js. We used Python Flask API for our backend. The image inpainting and rasterization models were hosted on Huggingface inference endpoints.
The backend logs six action events: image inpainting, rasterization, copy-pasting, reverting to an old project state, and undo and redo actions.
All actions are saved in a CSV file for each project.
We evaluate and cluster each action using GPT-4 via the OpenAI API.

3.6.
Technical Evaluation

To assess how HistoryPalette may work in practice, we analyzed three existing design projects from creative professionals (from our Creative Professional Evaluation) for our technical evaluation.
We assessed the capability of our system to generate high-quality alternatives in-place and filter out unsuccessful alternatives.

We extracted all image generation prompts with their corresponding sets of four generated alternative images across all projects.
Each project included 11 (
\sigma=2.94
) prompts for a total of 132 prompt and image pairs across all projects.
We then reviewed each pair and annotated a pair as “unsuccessful” if it: (1) did not generate an object, (2) generated the wrong object (
e.g.
, “yellow flowers” led to a bush), (3) generated the object but placed it illogically (
e.g.
, a “horse” prompt generated a horse head in the grass), or (4) the generation created artifacts along with the object.
We labeled no object or wrong object annotations as “unsuccessful” and the remaining as “successful” (18% were unsuccessful).
We ran our filtering approach on the prompt-image pairs and used our annotation as ground truth labels.
Our filtering approach achieved an accuracy of 0.93, a precision of 0.86, and a recall of 0.76. We aimed for lower recall than precision to conservatively hide alternatives and to cope with remaining errors we let users hide/unhide alternatives or toggle to display hidden alternatives.

4.
User Studies of HistoryPalette

We conducted user studies to evaluate how HistoryPalette supports people in using a history of generated artifacts for reuse and communication in creative workflows that use generation. We aimed to assess how participants created their composition with new generations vs. reusing history (
RQ1
), understand how participants used new history organization (concept and position palette) vs. existing history features (time palette and version timeline) (
RQ2
), and assess if organizing the history could facilitate project collaboration with others (
RQ3
). In summary, we aimed to assess:

RQ1

History Reuse.
How do people use
new generations
vs. a
history of generations
to create compositions in HistoryPalette?

RQ2

Organization.
How do people use
linear
(time palette) vs.
non-linear
(concept and position palette) local alternatives? How do people use
local
vs.
global
alternatives?

RQ3

Collaboration.
Does HistoryPalette support the use of project history for communication and collaboration?

To answer these questions, we first conducted
a study with creative professionals
who performed an open-ended image composition project with HistoryPalette. We also conducted
a study with collaborators
who used HistoryPalette to create a composition with an initial composition and already-populated alternatives. We address
RQ1
and
RQ2
by analyzing interactions and participants’ descriptions of their experience in both studies. We address
RQ3
by collecting participant responses about their perceptions of sharing their history or using others’ histories, and by comparing results across the two studies.

4.1.
Methods

Our creative professionals study and client collaborator study followed similar protocols.

Procedure.
We first asked participants demographic and background questions then provided a 15-minute tutorial of HistoryPalette. Participants then performed an editing task with HistoryPalette, as described below. We instructed participants to think-aloud during their editing task while a researcher took notes.
We then conducted semi-structured interviews covering their process, interactions with the system, benefits and drawbacks of HistoryPalette, and desired improvements for HistoryPalette.
Client collaborators also completed a final questionnaire with Likert item ratings asking about their concrete experience with select UI elements and interaction techniques. These Likert items were constructed to reflect the viewpoints of the participants (i.e., "I thought […] was useful.")
We include the full list of questions in the
Appendix A
.
We audio and screen recorded the studies and recorded all participant interactions with the interface. We conducted the studies remotely via Zoom and in person at a lab space depending on participant preference. Studies lasted
\rev
2.5 hours ($40 gift card compensation) for creative professionals and
\rev
1.25 hours ($20 gift card compensation) for client collaborators.

Editing Task (Creative Professionals)
. Creative professionals performed a 60-minute
\rev
open-ended editing activity designed to mimic a creative assignment. We provided professionals the scenario
“imagine you have been hired by a client to create a home surrounded by a landscape”
and an initial background image.

\rev
We selected the task and background image (a blue sky and green grass) to encourage open-ended exploration within the bounds of our system and generation limitations (
e.g.
, works better for landscapes than portraits).

Editing Task (Client Collaborators)
.
Clients instead performed a 30-minute editing task given the scenario
“picture that you are a client who had hired a designer to complete a specific design scenario and had just received a copy of the designer’s project in HistoryPalette”
along with an initial composition with a complete history in HistoryPalette. Each participant completed this task for one of three design scenarios that we assigned with a balanced random assignment.
We then asked client collaborators to explore the project and edit the image to better fit their needs according to the specific design scenario.

\rev
The first author created all starting projects to represent “designer” projects in the client collaborator study. Each full project took 2-3 hours to create due to base generation speed (around 10 seconds per generation, limited by our hardware).

Participants (Creative Professionals).
We recruited three creative professionals (E1-E3) from our professional networks.
E1 had 13 years of experience as a graphic designer with regular clients in retail and e-commerce.
E2 had 20 years of experience as a creative director with regular clients in diverse industries.
E3 had 8 years of experience as a graphic designer with regular clients in retail, fashion, and IT.
All professionals used AI generation tools (e.g., Photoshop Generative Fill, Midjourney, Dall-E, Stable Diffusion).

Participants (Client Collaborators).
We recruited 8 participants (C1-C8) within our professional and university network who were familiar with generative AI tools for creative processes but they did not need prior creative experience.
All participants had tried generative AI at least once before, however no participants self-reported proficiency in using it.

Data Analysis.
We analyzed the user interaction logging data
to identify the frequency of interactions across HistoryPalette features.
We used a German qualitative content analysis method
(
Mayring 2021
)
to analyze the transcribed interviews and observation notes we gained during the study. The analysis method is equivalent to thematic analysis
(
Braun and Clarke 2006
;
Braun and Clarke 2019
)
.
The first and second authors thoroughly reviewed the transcription and observations individually by grouping, splitting, and merging codes to identify themes.
Both authors then discussed their themes and collaboratively reorganized their codes until they agreed on common higher-level themes. We performed this analysis separately for each study, then combined common themes and discussed them using research questions.

4.2.
RQ1: Use of History in Image Generation

All participants across both studies used HistoryPalette to explore, preview, and reuse image generation alternatives from the prior project history (Table
1
). All participants stated in interviews that they appreciated the ability to use the history of the project in their editing task and would use HistoryPalette in the future.

How did creative professionals use history vs. generation?

Creative professionals started with blank projects, so they first generated images to compose their scene (four images were added to the history per prompt), then used the history more often as the project progressed (Figure
4
).
Creative professionals thus added to their composition more often with new generations (mean=24 times; E1=27, E2=24, E3=20) than the history (mean=14 times; E1=7, E2=10, E3=26) (Table
1
).
Creative professionals used the palettes more often towards the end of the session, hinting at the palettes’ usefulness as the project progresses (Figure
4
).
Only one creative professional used the time palette (E2).
All creative professionals interleaved generation and history to perform their editing task (creating a home surrounded by a landscape) in different ways, so we provide three vignettes:

E1 stated that they edited their image by starting from the outside (
e.g.
, background scenery) then gradually
“went with the flow of generations"
to create the main subject, a house (Figure
5
). E1 frequently previewed past alternatives between generations as they went to see which alternative fit best (Figure
4
). E1 noted they previewed and used past alternatives for key parts of the image (
e.g.
, golf course, and house), as those were the generations that
“took [him] longer in the selection process because a few alternatives would have looked good"
.
For example, E1 was once between two ideas as they noticed that the modern house and palm trees they recently generated no longer fit with the casual style of a golf course they added earlier. To decide whether to revise the golf course or the house, E1 used HistoryPalette’s position palette to view alternative golf courses. E1 liked one with a manicured look to match the modern house so they placed the golf course into the image.

How did client collaborators use history vs. generation?

Client collaborators started with a project with a pre-populated history, and thus instead explored the history first and generated images when their desired outcome did not already exist. Clients thus used alternatives from the history (mean=12, SD=6) more often than they used new generations (mean=5, SD=2).
All client collaborators used the position and concept palettes while only 3 of them (C1, C7, C8) used the time palette (Figure
4
).

Client collaborators first explored the history of alternatives using hover to preview (Figure
4
), then added a prior alternative to the image when they disagreed with the designer’s choice or wanted to change the scene to match a higher-level goal (
e.g.
, C8 recombined past designs to get a darker setting).
Participants described that they perceived past designs as starting points (C3, C4, C5) that give options (e.g.,
“I want to see if there are other nice houses?"
) when picking up someone’s idea to improve on it. For example, C6 said
“it looked better in my eyes"
after explaining why they reused a designer’s past house design.
Client collaborators all changed regions of the composition and many made large changes (6 changed the background, 6 changed the main image) (Figure
8
).
C6 uniquely placed a past design (a barn) in the middle of the scene to explore how the scene would look with a house in the middle, then generated a new house over the barn after they were satisfied with the placement.
After bringing past designs back into the image, client collaborators frequently applied transformations to them (i.e. move, resize, and rotate) to make them fit better into the current state of the image.
Client collaborators then most often used generation to add concepts that the designer did not explore (
e.g.
, a “horse”, or “grass” instead of existing landscaping options) rather than for generating new versions of previously explored concepts.

History reuse via preview over addition.

In both studies, participants used prior design alternatives from their history in their image (from 5 to 26 total alternatives used in the final image, Table
1
), but participants experimented with alternatives by hovering to preview them hundreds of times during a project (from 179-781 hover actions per participant).
All participants described in the interview that the most important interaction with the system was to explore past designs by hovering on palette items to preview them in the image.
This is also supported by participants’ interactions with HistoryPalette (Figure
4
).
Participants highlighted the importance of being able to preview past designs
“next to the alternative”
(C7), which made it
“easier to visualize"
(C8) and
“to directly see what it would look like"
(C7)

History reuse via paste over rasterize.
For both studies, participants often applied transformations to alternatives when they brought them back (i.e. move, resize, and rotate) to fit better into the current state of the image. To add alternatives back 10 of 12 participants used paste more often than rasterize.
Participants used paste when the alternative matched the background, and typically used the time-consuming rasterize option more sparingly to smooth out artifacts from alternative/background mismatches.

Creative professionals valued history in generative workflows for control and iterative harmony improvement.

Professionals favorably compared HistoryPalette to their prior workflows with generative AI due to the control provided by the history. E1 mentioned that in a prior project, they wanted to generate “a pair of skis” similar to a pair they had generated before but lost access to.
After long back and forth they had to give up as their existing tool would not provide them with the desired outcome of the generation, but noted that they would be able to bring back the prior version in HistoryPalette.
On a similar note, E2 mentioned that HistoryPalette was more “controllable” than Photoshop.

All creative professionals worked iteratively with the palettes provided by HistoryPalette to achieve harmony in their scenes.
For instance, E1 described when exploring prior house alternatives
“this house looks better in the scenery which I just realized after all my changes”
.
E1 mentioned that the harmony between previously generated objects in the scene changes whenever new objects are added to the scene.
They describe it as “object dependency” which is affected by new generations that are added to the image.
Similarly, E3 noted how
“seeing it [objects] in the surroundings led me to taking a different turtle”
which demonstrates how designers make their next design decisions by trying to achieve harmony between elements in their image.
E2 also noticed how later in their process they suddenly liked a different flower better when previewing it, suggesting the preview makes this evaluation of harmony easier for designers.

Client collaborators valued history for quality, efficiency, and learning.

Client collaborators all used the history more than generation and also all preferred HistoryPalette when asked to compare it to traditional methods like the Generation View in our interface or the Photoshop Generative Fill feature. Clients reported they preferred to use the history compared to generation as it was easier to reuse past designs rather than generate new designs as they are unfamiliar with prompting (C8), worse at prompting than the designer (C5), that they already trust the designer to make good alternatives (C2, C4), and that they find it hard to put their thoughts into words (C2).
Participants noted that seeing the designer’s prompts with the prompt view option helped them articulate their own prompts (e.g., C2 mentioned the prompts provided helpful orientation and C4 mentioned they would not have thought about some prompts).

History reuse supports varied collections.
E3, C2, C5, and C6 used discarded alternatives to create varied collections including a flock of chickens (E3) and a village of houses (C6). E3 prompted for a chicken, received four generated alternatives, and selected one to add to the scene. E3 later decided they wanted to create a flock of chickens and thus revisited the history to add the other discarded chickens with a variety of poses and appearances to the scene. C2 similarly created their village through the reuse of transformed versions of past house designs and discarded alternatives, letting them create variations while keeping consistency.

4.3.
RQ2: Organization of Alternatives

Participants used and preferred position and concept palettes over the time palette.

All participants used position or concept palettes (non-linear, new) more often than they used the time palette (linear, existing) (Table
1
).
10 of 12 hovered on the concept palette most often to preview alternatives (C3 and C6 hovered on the position palette most often), and 9 of 12 clicked on the concept palette most often to bring an alternative back (E3, C3, and C6 clicked on the position palette most often).
4 of 12 hovered on the position palette most often and 3 of 12 clicked on the position palette most often to bring an alternative back.
7 of 12 participants never used the time palette.
In Likert scale ratings client collaborators indicated that the position palette was most useful followed by the concept palette, while the time palette was rated the lowest out of all system features (Figure
9
).

Concept and position palettes reduce the search space.
An advantage of concept and position palettes is that they reduce the search space given a user goal. Creative professionals produced 75-95 alternatives and 62-72 alternatives post-filtering (removed 20% of alternatives). Creative professionals had 6 (SD = 1) concepts with 11 (SD = 5) alternatives, and pixel positions with 10 (SD = 3) alternatives each. Creative professionals thus experienced up to a 11x (concept palette) or 7x (position palette) reduction in the search space (similar reductions for clients). Participants used nearby alternatives in the position and concept palette to flicker between a small set of position or concept alternatives, and skimmed concept palette labels to identify topics of interest (Figure
4
).

History palettes support flow.
E1 and E3 frequently used the position and concept palettes to preview alternatives, and reported that HistoryPalette thus let them stay in the flow as they worked on their design. E1 shared that using HistoryPalette is
“like a river and you flow with the program”
as previewing and using alternatives did not distract their attention from the final image. E3 described:

“I love that I can just go with the flow in creative process here […] you can just click through and see what could be better or not, it’s just a flow.”

Participants previewed but rarely reused global alternatives.

Participants often hovered over prior versions to preview them but rarely clicked prior versions to bring them back.
Client collaborators specifically reported that they used the prior whole project versions to understand the overall process to build the project. A few clients restored full prior versions but still used alternatives from a later version. For example, C5 selected an earlier version of the image with fewer elements and then used recent alternatives to build the scene.

4.4.
RQ3: Use of History for Collaboration

All participants identified potential for using HistoryPalette’s history organization for collaboration.

Experts valued sharing own history for collaboration.
E1, E2, and E3 stated HistoryPalette would support them in communicating with others.
All participants stated it would be useful in creating multiple versions to communicate with clients, but participants varied in whether they thought it would be appropriate to give the tool directly to the client.

E1 noted HistoryPalette would be particularly helpful in supporting
“interactive design exchange with the client”
because it
“allows design finding within 15 seconds.”
E1 also wanted to give the entire interface to a client because it is easy to use for clients and would allow clients to make small adjustments — in fact, the company they worked at attempted to make a custom internal tool for a similar purpose.
However, they mentioned they could see some designers not wanting clients to be able to modify the final design with the tool (
e.g.
, to keep a consistent style like photographers who do not share the raw image).
E3 also expressed that clients may not be interested in seeing the final project, but that it could be useful for designers to walk clients through alternatives they considered in the design (
e.g.
,
“we decided to go for a house but we could have also gone with a bunker instead”
).
E2 noted that they would not want to
“show 100 different options to the client”
but they may want to select alternatives for the client to see (
e.g.
, a client could select between two different houses).

E2 and E3 valued history sharing to collaborate with other designers.
E2 expressed
“if a different designer has to open the files, it’s very useful!”
She elaborated that if a client gave feedback on specific flowers in the final image, another designer could turn on prompt history to see what prompt was used to create those flowers, then change or improve the prompt (
e.g.
., using the position palette).
E3 noted how HistoryPalette would be useful in collaborative settings where
“one lives off of ideas from others"
(
e.g., “let’s take my mountain and your house"
).

Client collaborators valued the ability to understand and communicate about the designer’s process.

Participants perceived HistoryPalette as a way that allows them to go back and not only see the history of the project but also understand why designers’ decisions were made.
C4 described the ability to see the history as getting to talk to the designer.

Participants highlighted how HistoryPalette allows exploring past designs as a means of getting a better understanding of the designers’ process and reasoning.
Participants understood the palette of past designs to show the design space that the designer’s past explorations covered.
Participants noted that they could use HistoryPalette find spaces where designers already explored alternatives that were not good (C6), left room for exploration (C8), and potentially communicate the gap (C4). C4 stated they may ask
“Why did you not change the house that often?”

Participants reported that viewing the prompts further provided them with a way to understand the designer’s reasoning including their goal (C7), content of focus (C7) and intentions (C6).

Participants finally noted that HistoryPalette allows clients to take a step towards the designer role. C4 specifically mentions that HistoryPalette allows them to
“change something and then go back to the designer and tell them how [they] like it"
.

4.5.
Future Use and Improvements

\rev

Creative professionals wanted to use HistoryPalette for long and open-ended projects.
Creative practitioners E1 and E3 suggested HistoryPalette would be particularly helpful for large and long projects. E3 imagines that the system would be particularly helpful when working on big projects with 200 objects or more as
“you can go through them all and have them collected there”
or easily change an object even late in the project (
e.g.
, clicking on a plant to see what else they did there).
E1 described that HistoryPalette solved a common problem in their usual process:

“If I lose a small object and only realize after 1.5 hours that I lost an object that I cannot really bring back […] I need to push CTRL + Z all the time just to bring it back, here I can always just bring it back. […] I have this problem very often, it’s among my top 3 problems.”

All participants stated the tool would be strongest for open-ended settings. E1 described that they know 70% of what they want to create ahead of time, but 30% comes from going with the flow such that HistoryPalette would be strong for the open-ended 30%. E2 reported the tool would be most useful when they were considering a range of possible directions.

\rev

Improving controllability of HistoryPalette
. Participants raised areas to improve HistoryPalette. Participants noticed artifacts from placing an earlier generation on a new background (
e.g.
, mismatched edges), and thus reported that they would like the option to reuse the foreground object of a prior alternative rather the entire mask.
E3 also mentioned that the position palette was helpful but could be difficult to control access to prior small generations (
e.g.
, the mask of a lamp post). While E3 used the concept palette to find the lamp post, they reported that zoom would be useful in the future.

5.
Discussion

We reflect on the creative professional and client studies to summarize our key findings and reflect on our design goals (
G1-G4
), then share limitations and opportunities for future work.

5.1.
Study Reflections

G1: Palettes of Alternatives for Discovery.
Creative professionals and collaborators frequently used unique features of HistoryPalette (position palette, concept palette) to discover and then reuse alternatives.
Participants rarely used or preferred the time palette that most closely mirrors existing support for partial-project alternatives (
e.g.
, layers created by Photoshop Generative Fill
(
ado [n. d.]
)
).
Across both studies, participants used the concept palette most often, followed by the position palette and version timeline (but they rarely clicked the version timeline to revert).
We were initially surprised that the concept palette was used more often than the position palette, as participants expressed the most enthusiasm when talking about the position palette for discovering object alternatives. In practice, the concept palette was always open and easy to browse for alternatives of interest (
G1
).

G2: Support rapid reuse of alternatives.
Across both studies participants shared that reusing alternatives was faster and more predictable than image generation such that they would prefer to reuse their palettes when possible. In both studies, participants successfully added alternatives into their image via rasterize or paste (from 5 to 26 total alternatives used in the final image, Table
1
), but participants experimented with alternatives by hovering over them in the palette hundreds of times during a project (from 179-781 hover actions per participant).
G2
may be best supported by the combination of right-at-hand alternatives (
G1
) with hover functionality that enables rapid experimentation of design alternatives.

G3: Provide history filters and favorites.

Histories were more useful to participants as they got longer, emphasizing the need for history management features.
While participants had the option to toggle our generation alternatives filter on or off, all participants kept the filter on. Our filter culled alternatives but participants never mentioned the filter positively or negatively. Participants did not use the favorites feature during the limited editing time, but creative professionals noted that this feature may be useful for saving their own alternatives in collaboration (
G4
).

G4: Support multiple audiences.
Both sets of participants expressed excitement about using the system for collaboration (
e.g.
, to help another designer see the prompts, or learn about designers’ prompts), indicating the potential for collaborating using non-linear edit histories (
G4
). However, client collaborators and creative professionals had occasionally mismatched expectations about how much of the design should be editable vs. stuck in place (
G4
).

5.2.
Implications and Limitations

Generalizability of HistoryPalette.

History palettes are a new interaction concept inspired by current creative practice
(
Sterman et al. 2022
)

\rev
that aim to provide a new solution to the longstanding challenge of history keeping and management in HCI
(
Shneiderman 2007
)
.

\rev
In contrast to other systems
(
Klemmer et al. 2002
;
Grossman et al. 2010
;
Dang et al. 2023
;
Rawn et al. 2023
)
that support reviewing the project history as an
ordered record
, HistoryPalette uniquely attempts to prioritize retrieval of the design alternatives within a projects history to support the use of alternatives as a
palette of materials

(
Sterman et al. 2022
)
.

\rev
While we designed HistoryPalette to address generative image creation and editing, the concept of history palettes can to many other domains with: (1) semantically meaningful partial-project “alternatives”, and (2) labels to organize alternatives into palettes (e.g. position, concept, time). The concept applies to generative, traditional, and hybrid creative workflows.

We prototyped HistoryPalette with generative image editing as prompt-based generation makes each step a meaningful alternative, and prompts/selections provide labels. HistoryPalette directly extends to emerging prompt-based workflows that share these properties (SVG editing, photo edits, or 3D models). We expect HistoryPalette to be even more useful for traditional or hybrid editing workflows (digital painting, vector editing, photo editing) in which partial-project alternatives consist of low-level steps (strokes, shapes, or filters) —
e.g.
, digital painters iteratively apply brush strokes for portrait backgrounds (a gradient, plants, a porch). HistoryPalette could group low-level actions (brush strokes) into higher-level alternatives (a porch) with labels. Grouping low-level edits is challenging, but recent VLMs/LLMs show potential to create robust methods to group and label low-level edits with recorded user interactions and visual project states.

\rev
The labels we gain from applying history palette to other domains (
e.g.
, grouping brush strokes into a “tree” label) may benefit systems that support novices learning from creative processes of experts (
e.g.
, from videos
(
Grossman et al. 2010
)
, or creative livestreams
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
) by providing semantically meaningful labels to further help novices to browse the history. However, HistoryPalette may need to support a dense
hierarchy of alternatives
when labels have multiple lower level steps to support user retrieval at multiple levels of detail (
e.g.
, “trunk” vs. “tree”).

\rev

Implications for prompt-based workflows.
Uncertainty is inherent to prompt-based workflows where creators specify a text prompt and receive a high-fidelity image in return.
Prior work has explored approaches to support creators wrangling with this uncertainty from helping creators refine their prompts
(
Brade et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Feng et al. 2023
)
to visualizing a space of potential prompt outputs
(
Almeda et al. 2024
)
. However, each new generation costs money and takes time. Our studies demonstrated that access to on-demand alternatives (
G1
,
G2
) promoted reuse rather than generation for clients (clients reserved generation for when their desired concept did not exist), and promoted reuse for professionals (as their projects increased in length). Participants stated that reducing uncertainty by revisiting the palettes improved flow (for professionals) and increased their confidence in achieving a high-quality result with limited prompting experience (for clients).
C6 even used the history to de-risk generation (
e.g.
, previewing a composition with an old object before generating a new one).
Our work suggests that easy-to-access past alternatives provide a tool for creators managing uncertainty and costs inherent to generative AI tools.

\rev

While creators and clients made use of the palettes to experiment with and use alternatives, we do not know what the long-term impact of such a tool might be.
If using prior alternatives is significantly easier, cheaper, or faster than generation, users may slowly converge to a set of alternatives and encounter design fixation. Alternatively, users may try new compositions with ease of palette use (as easy access to history can de-risk exploration
(
Shneiderman 2007
)
). Future work may explore how providing easy to access alternatives will impact the diversity of generations over time. Further, as professionals found HistoryPalette to be more useful as the project progressed, it may be particularly beneficial for professionals to reuse palettes across projects. However, to avoid self-plagiarism, HistoryPalette could indicate or fully remove alternatives creators previously used in a final composition for prior projects (i.e. to “retire” the alternative).

\rev

Finally, a challenge and opportunity for history keeping in prompt-based workflows is that the uncertainty and noise present in generative workflows will replicate within the history keeping tool
(
Benjamin et al. 2021
)
. As the history reflects the creator’s process of moving through uncertainty, the history may feature many irrelevant generations (beyond only errors as addressed in HistoryPalette) as the creator learned to capture their style in prompts. Future work may explore personalized or dynamic filters for history as it accumulates over time (
e.g.
, remove alternatives that do not reflect the creator’s current style). At the same time, the jitter provided by the uncertainty produces more alternatives that could be created manually such that the jitter may support a client in identifying a more optimal alternative for their needs. Keeping the record of uncertainty can also serve to communicate the struggle between the artist and the design tool to ground client feedback on limitations of prompts.

\rev

Implications for sharing project history with others.
While work primarily explored project histories for personal reuse
(
Rawn et al. 2023
;
Dang et al. 2023
;
Angert et al. 2023
)
or sharing a creator’s process
(
Klemmer et al. 2002
;
Grossman et al. 2010
)
, HistoryPalette uniquely explores directly providing collaborators with another person’s project history directly to select alternatives or provide feedback.
To make use of the project history feasible, HistoryPalette provided a low barrier to entry to exploring history by matching client goals (
e.g.
, preview alternative houses by clicking on the house or looking for houses) and low effort previews for clients quickly exploring alternative designs (
e.g.
, compared to the time and effort for new generations, or time-based history alone). Our client study demonstrated that clients without experience with the medium (text-to-image generation) could use the history to create new compositions (Figure
8
).

\rev

Prior work also explores using AI to create artifacts that ground conversation between creators and their collaborators
(
Ko et al. 2022
;
Chung and Adar 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
—
e.g.
, by creating moodboards to establish project direction
(
Chung and Adar 2023
)
, synthesizing references to inform webtoon design
(
Ko et al. 2022
)
, or planning initial room designs
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
. Such systems address early stages of the design process when communicating shared direction
(
Chung and Adar 2023
)
and achieving a breadth of exploration may be the goal
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, but HistoryPalette demonstrates an opportunity to use the project history to provide feedback in later stages of the design process. In the later stage, collaborators may no longer establish direction but rather provide grounded feedback by previewing alternatives, understand designer constraints for the current choice (
e.g.
, the alternatives do not look better), or even use the palettes to directly tweak the final design within a constrained design space.

\rev

As our studies demonstrated the promise of using palettes of alternatives within a single project for personal or collaborative use, an opportunity exists to explore how to manage, share, and reuse palettes across multiple projects or with multiple collaborators. For example, if a creator is working on a project with a colleague, they may combine their palettes. Or, a content creator may sell palettes to people hoping to emulate their style (
e.g.
, like video color grading or photography editing packs).
A creator may keep a stack of palettes for different types of projects. Future work may explore how to suggest new palette additions (
e.g.
, full palettes or single alternatives) based on the user’s current palettes or projects.

Quality artifacts in image reuse.
The primary limitation of our current system is a limited ability to bring objects back to a new location. When the user selects a region in which to generate an alternative, the system also generates the surrounding background. When that alternative is pasted in a new location or resized an obvious boundary can appear between the old background and new background. To mitigate this issue, participants often selected locations for the object with a similar background, accepted the collage-like feel in favor of their preferred composition, or used rasterize to smooth out artifacts. The creative professional who had significant experience with image generation reused the most alternatives without introducing artifacts, while clients were occasionally plagued by image quality. Users may be able to improve their ability to use the interface, but also models are rapidly improving such that this limitation may diminish over time.

Performance on large or long projects.
We conducted two exploratory studies that demonstrated the potential of our new interaction techniques for exploring and reusing editing histories. Both studies were limited to sessions that were 15 minutes to 69 minutes long.
We noticed that professionals (who started with blank palettes) used palettes more often as the study went on. The trend may continue as more objects are added but there is likely some point at which our system’s usefulness may plateau or decrease, necessitating additional history summarization. For example, while the concept view can flexibly handle more examples, it may become challenging to browse by scrolling such that a hierarchical outline may be helpful for navigation. Similarly, our position palette may need to limit its scope over time (
e.g.
, if there have been 100 masks over the middle pixel).
While our work demonstrates that such a position palette may be useful in creative image generation workflows, we will explore how to limit the scope of position palettes (
e.g.
, by object
(
Terry et al. 2004
)
, recency, diversity).

6.
Conclusion

We introduce HistoryPalette, a system for surfacing and reusing design alternatives in image generation workflows. HistoryPalette’s core components are a position palette, a concept palette, a time palette, and a version timeline supported by tools for filtering and clustering alternatives. We conducted two exploratory user studies that demonstrate the potential of our tool for reusing alternatives in personal creative workflows and in communication with others.
