Beyond AI Detection: A Decision-Making Guide for Strategic Assessment Design in Nursing Education
Authors: Katherine Ann McCusker, Betsy B. Kennedy, Abby Parish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The use of artificial intelligence (AI) by pre- and postlicensure nursing students threatens the validity of assessments and raises concerns about competency-based evaluation and public safety.Problem:Nurse educators lack a structured framework for evaluating the degree to which specific assessments are vulnerable to inappropriate AI use and for prioritizing where assessment design efforts are most needed.Approach:Graduate nursing faculty used a backward design process, anchoring criterion development in known low-vulnerability assessments and refining criteria through iterative review and applied testing across multiple assessment types.Outcomes:The Assessment and AI Vulnerability Decision-Making Guide for Nursing produces a total vulnerability score across 2 domains—assessment setting and assessment method—paired with a targeted improvement guide for faculty.Conclusions:This decision-making guide enables nursing educators to strategically direct assessment design efforts toward high-stakes contexts where AI poses the greatest risk to the validity of competency evaluation, while preserving pedagogical flexibility when AI use aligns with professional practice.

In nursing education, assessment serves a vital role, allowing the instructor and learner to understand the critical progress made toward achieving a learning goal or competency.1,2 Assessment in nursing education serves a dual purpose: tracking student learning and safeguarding public safety by verifying that graduates meet the accepted standards for licensure, certification, and safe practice.3 With the evolution of artificial intelligence (AI) and large language models (LLMs), learning behaviors have changed, resulting in an erosion of competencies.4 Simultaneously, AI and LLM tools have become part of many students’ toolkits as they progress through their education and clinical practice. This disruptive technology requires educators to understand and adjust assessments to ensure nursing students meet the educational competencies necessary to enter practice.5 Here, we present a decision-making guide to facilitate the strategic design of assessments in nursing education. The Assessment and AI Vulnerability Decision-Making Guide helps educators identify where AI poses the greatest risk to valid competency evaluation, using a systematic scoring approach to prioritize assessment redesign efforts (Supplemental Digital Content, Assessment and AI Vulnerability Decision-Making Guide https://links.lww.com/NE/C420).

Background

The purpose and stakes associated with assessment range from high-stakes summative assessments that determine a student’s ability to progress in a program to low-stakes formative assessments with feedback that contributes to learners’ growth.2 Common formats for assessments in nursing education include quizzes, papers, simulations, and clinical practice, and each type of assessment may be formative or summative, depending on its role in the curriculum. For example, multiple-choice in-person exams and simulation-based assessments can both be high-stakes evaluations or lower-stakes learning activities, depending on the educator’s objectives.

Authentic assessment involves using realistic contexts to assess thinking, problem-solving, and decision-making.6,7 This assessment strategy allows learning to be transferable to future practice environments.6 The format of an authentic assessment can vary. For example, a written exam composed of scenario-based questions grounded in the clinical context, or a simulation in the clinical context, could both be authentic assessments.

Assessment can also support the learning process. Process-based assessment shifts the emphasis away from the final product and supports the development of reflective practices and critical thinking.4 Process-based assessment is formative and focuses on learning over time, with iterative instructor feedback and self-reflection.4,7 This type of assessment may involve creating a portfolio of specific parts of a final project the student develops and reflects on, ultimately leading to a final presentation or showcase.

Problem

Today, effective assessment is increasingly challenged by learner use of AI. The increasing use of AI has raised concerns about learners’ development of critical thinking skills and academic integrity.7,8 For the purposes of this guide, inappropriate AI use refers to use that results in cognitive offloading—delegating to AI the cognitive work essential to developing competency—or that obscures student performance in ways that prevent the instructor from evaluating student performance with confidence. The development and use of tools to detect AI use is an academic arms race, with detection tools and AI models co-evolving to detect and compete. AI models can typically generate text that is undetectable by AI detection programs.9 Given the difficulties in detecting AI use, Akbar10 focused on assessment design and illustrated that assessment vulnerability to AI use was correlated with assessment structure, noting that assessments that required creative or critical thinking skills fell below a 50% solvability threshold for LLMs. Assessments that were more easily solved by AI were conceptual or definitional, or involved well-established theories or terminology, not requiring subject expertise or contextual knowledge.10

Faculty share concerns about AI use and the potential vulnerability of assessment to AI. Alkouk and Khlaif11 used faculty workshops on AI to determine potential solutions and explore educator perspectives. Educators felt that process-based education and assessment techniques, as well as authentic assessment, were effective methods for minimizing the risk of AI use.11 Additionally, the educators noted that shifting toward authentic, process-based assessment could promote improved learning, given the nature of both assessment and learning models.10,11

While AI-augmented practice is an emerging reality in health care, a foundational knowledge base and sound clinical judgment remain essential for graduate nurses, and there is a pressing need in nursing education to design and implement assessments that give learners the opportunity to demonstrate practice readiness independently. The purpose of this paper is to introduce a decision-making guide to help instructors within clinical disciplines evaluate the vulnerability of assessments to AI while balancing the real-world use case for AI in practice and life.

Approach

The Assessment and AI Vulnerability Decision-Making Guide for Nursing was developed through collaborative expert review by 3 graduate nursing faculty from 2 institutions representing diverse educational contexts. Development followed a backward design approach: rather than defining abstract criteria and mapping them downward, the team began from a known-anchor assessment and worked backward to identify the criterion properties that account for its vulnerability profile. Those criteria were then iteratively refined through expert review and applied testing.

The team began by identifying an assessment already known to carry very low AI vulnerability: a written or multiple-choice exam administered in person, on paper, with a proctor present. Working backward from this anchor case, the authors identified that its setting and the conditions under which it was administered were the primary properties accounting for its low-vulnerability profile—specifically, the clear difference in AI vulnerability between in-person and at-home evaluations, and between proctored and unproctored conditions. These observations established assessment setting as a foundational criterion and provided the starting point for developing the broader criterion structure.

Drawing on their own classroom experience, the authors recognized that in competency-based, learner-centered education, no single assessment type captures the full range of learner skills. With this in mind, an initial set of criteria was drafted and circulated among the team. Feedback was discussed, overlap was identified, and criteria were consolidated where needed. The revised criteria were then recirculated for further refinement. This cycle—draft, circulate, discuss, revise—continued until the team reached consensus.

Throughout the development period, the authors were actively teaching graduate nursing students and observing how students were using AI in their coursework in real time. An important finding from their experience was that when students are not directly observed, AI use cannot be reliably detected. This observation, consistent with the known limitations of current detection tools, reinforced assessment setting as a primary criterion.9 Where and how an assessment is conducted is not an abstract design consideration; it is what determines whether AI use can even be identified.

To evaluate the guide’s ability to predict AI vulnerability and facilitate instructor understanding of scores, it was applied across multiple assessment types: a proctored multiple-choice exam, an asynchronous at-home multiple-choice exam, a case-based at-home written exam, a simulation, and process-based clinical note writing. Outcomes

The Assessment and AI Vulnerability Decision-Making Guide for Nursing can be downloaded and used by nurse educators for evaluating existing assessments or guiding the creation of new assessments (Supplemental Digital Content, Guide https://links.lww.com/NE/C420). The guide went through several iterations before reaching its final form. Supplemental Digital Content, Figure 1 https://links.lww.com/NE/C421 provides a diagram of the revision process.12 Development began with criterion identification and scoring design, progressing through 2 rounds of testing and revision, and culminating in a reframing of the guide’s purpose and name.

The initial subcriteria identified included authenticity, process, modality (synchronous versus asynchronous), personal experience, and collaborative skills. Through iterative expert review, overlap among these subcriteria was identified, and they were consolidated. The consolidated criteria were further refined, with titles revised to better reflect their pedagogical origins, yielding the final criterion structure.

Early drafts of the decision-making guide graded all criteria based on the same scale, one through four. Early testing applied the draft guide to a known low-vulnerability case: a proctored, in-person exam completed without resources. The guide produced a lower-than-expected score, attributable to low marks on the process and personal or lived experience criteria. Thus, the decision-making guide was revised into 2 parts: Part A and Part B. Part A specifically evaluates and rates the assessment setting and is scored from 0 to 6, with 6 being the lowest AI vulnerability. Part B further evaluates the assessment in the context of the assessment method and whether authentic, process, or clinical assessment strategies are being employed. This portion of the decision-making guide is scored on a scale of 0 to 3. Using this new scoring, the known low AI vulnerability assessment scored an 8 out of 15 (Figure).12

Given the importance of holistic assessment of student learning, the AI vulnerability scale is interpreted in the context of both formative and summative, or high-stakes, assessment. While an assessment may have a low likelihood of AI use, that does not mean it is an assessment appropriate for evaluating clinical competency or progression. In turn, the score is not only a reflection of AI vulnerability but also an evaluation of the assessment type in the context of competency-based education and AI.

To support faculty use, a guide was developed to help instructors understand the score and identify potential changes or improvements to address vulnerabilities and align with assessment goals. Based on the individual scores of the 2 parts, the criteria scoring, and the assessment type, recommendations are made to improve the assessment’s AI vulnerability, if necessary. It is important to note that AI is a tool that can facilitate learning and reflect real-world use. The decision-making guide’s goal is to support assessment design in the context of AI for formative and summative assessment.

In construct validation, one opportunity for improvement identified was process-based learning. However, early drafts of the decision-making guide presented the Process criteria as the compilation of a portfolio built on many pieces. This was accurate, but it limited the interpretation and application of this criterion. This was improved by including parts of process-based assessment into the criterion, specifically reflective practices, opportunities for students to share their thought processes, and metacognitive activities. These changes allow the Process criteria to capture process-based assessment more holistically.

With construct validation, it became clear that within the decision-making guide, there was discordance between two score-guideline tables and the interpretation of scores. For example, in evaluating a written, multiple-choice in-person exam, without any resources, the Score Interpretation Table rated it as a moderate AI Vulnerability. This was not accurate, as this is a known low AI vulnerability assessment. The table failed to acknowledge that the total score reflects more than just the setting. This table was the first iteration of the decision-making guide. Given that the Targeted Improvement Guide recognizes the setting and provides commentary on formative versus summative assessment, the decision was made to remove the other score table from the final decision-making guide. Reframing the Aim

The initial decision-making guide was built to facilitate assessment resistance to AI. This initial language reflects a tension between AI and assessment, with the aim of working against each other. However, the aim of developing this decision-making guide is to help instructors assess the knowledge and skills of people in high-stakes environments. Additionally, it is important to recognize that AI is now part of professional settings and activities that nurses are training for. Some assessments should allow and encourage AI use to facilitate appropriate and effective learning. The aim is not to work against AI or LLMs. In turn, the decision-making guide was reframed to assess the AI Vulnerability of assessments, rather than the resistance of assessments to AI.

Discussion

The use of AI is impacting education across many contexts. Successful AI integration into higher education requires a coordinated institutional effort. Jin and colleagues’8 analysis of 40 universities identified specific stakeholder responsibilities: faculty must integrate and communicate AI policies, students must understand ethical use and limitations, and administrators must provide infrastructure and support.

However, institutional frameworks alone cannot address the immediate pedagogical challenges front-line faculty face, and many institutions lack readiness for AI integration.13 As systems and infrastructure evolve, faculty members must continue to educate students effectively while adapting their assessment practices to this new reality. Within nursing, assessment must remain reliable despite disruptive technologies, as it is essential to both competency-based education and to ensuring preparation for practice.

Khlaif et al7 introduced the AAAE framework—Against, Avoid, Adapt, and Explore—reflecting the spectrum of instructors’ philosophical responses to AI in assessment design. This framework provides valuable conceptual categories for understanding faculty positions, though it does not provide actionable guidance for evaluating specific assessments or determining appropriate assessment strategies based on context and/or stakes. The Assessment and AI Vulnerability Decision-Making Guide for Nursing addresses this gap by providing nursing educators with a systematic decision-making framework to evaluate assessment vulnerability to inappropriate AI use.

While this decision-making guide was developed specifically for nursing education, its foundation in authentic assessment principles and competency-based evaluation suggests transferability to other health science disciplines that share similar characteristics: patient safety imperatives, clinical competency requirements, and integration of theory with practice-based learning. Disciplines such as pharmacy, physical therapy, and medicine face parallel challenges in ensuring that assessments validly measure student competence rather than AI-assisted performance.5 However, discipline-specific validation may be necessary before adoption, as each field has unique assessment contexts, regulatory requirements, and professional practice standards that may affect the weight given to the decision-making guide’s criteria or scoring interpretation.

This decision-making guide was developed based on current AI capabilities (as of 2026) and documented patterns of student AI use in educational settings.4,14 As generative AI technology continues to rapidly evolve—potentially developing capabilities such as real-time audio/video generation, enhanced multimodal reasoning, or integration with wearable technology—the decision-making guide’s criteria may require reevaluation. Specifically, the "Setting" criterion’s emphasis on synchronous, observed assessments rests on current limitations in AI’s ability to participate undetected in real-time interactions. Regular review of the decision-making guide’s assumptions against emerging AI capabilities will be necessary to maintain its validity as a decision-making instrument.

Implications for Nursing Education

While the focus of this manuscript remains the description of the decision-making guide, there are clear implications for assessment design. Given the goals of nursing education in developing competency in clinical judgment and decision-making, considering the impact of AI on assessment is imperative. There is a distinct difference in the use of AI as a cognitive extension to support learning, such as creating flashcards or generating practice questions, and cognitive outsourcing or offloading to delegate the cognitive tasks of learning to AI.15 In the event of student cognitive outsourcing and the potentially unreliable output of AI, there are real consequences for the deep learning required in the preparation of nurses.4 Faulty mental models, reduced cognitive engagement and retention, and diminished capacity for problem-solving and creativity are all possible results of unbalanced student AI use.15,16 As this guide indicates, scaffolding assessments that have low AI vulnerability to encourage a foundation of knowledge is key.

With the rapid uptake and accessibility of AI, a focus on faculty productivity and efficiency, and student motivation to improve academic grade performance, we are potentially entering an educational loop where educators use AI to design assessments, students use AI to complete assessments, educators use AI to detect student AI use, and then educators use AI to evaluate student learning on assessments. There are substantial philosophical, ethical, and practical discussions that should be taking place in nursing education, and they should include students.

We continue to understand the effects of AI on learning and create a learning environment that integrates AI in meaningful ways that support competency.17 Therefore, this decision-making guide should also be viewed as an attempt to center the humans engaged in nursing education.

Conclusions

The Assessment and AI Vulnerability Decision-Making Guide for Nursing recognizes that not all assessments carry the same degree of AI vulnerability; rather, the appropriate level of concern depends on assessment stakes (formative vs. summative), pedagogical techniques employed, alignment with real-world professional contexts, and the instructor’s ability to verify student work. By systematically evaluating these factors, nursing educators can strategically direct their efforts toward high-stakes assessments where AI poses the greatest risk to valid competency evaluation, while maintaining pedagogically appropriate flexibility in lower-stakes learning activities and in those where AI use reflects real-world professional practice. Ultimately, this guide is not simply a mechanism for managing technology—it is an instrument for keeping educators and students engaged and at the center of nursing education.
