Plasmons in a network of topological states in twisted bilayer graphene
Brian S. Vermilyea, Michael M. Fogler

Abstract
We study surface plasmons in minimally-twisted bilayer graphene that contains a triangular network of partial dislocations (or AB-BA domain walls) hosting one-dimensional electronic states. We calculate plasmon dispersion by solving classical equations of motion for charge dynamics on the network links with impedance boundary conditions at the network nodes. The plasmon band structure is shown to be quasi-periodic in frequency and damped everywhere except at high-symmetry points of the moiré Brillouin zone. We compare our network-based formalism with the conventional random phase approximation and discuss when each approach is valid. Calculations of plasmon waves launched by local scatterers are presented to simulate terahertz nano-imaging experiments.



I
Introduction

The structural and electronic properties of twisted bilayer graphene sensitively depend on the twist angle
\theta
.
While the emergence of superconducting and insulating phases near the “magic” angle of
\theta\approx 1.1^{\circ}

has been extensively studied
Cao et al. 2018a
;
Cao et al. 2018b
,
this material also exhibits interesting physics at smaller
\theta
.
Such a minimally twisted bilayer graphene (mTBG)
undergoes a structural transformation into a triangular (moiré) superlattice
of energetically-preferred AB and BA stacking domains separated by narrow domain walls
Nam and Koshino 2017
;
Gargiulo and Yazyev 2017
;
Yoo et al. 2019
,
shown schematically in Fig.
1
.
To give an example, a twist angle of
\theta=0.07^{\circ}

creates a superlattice with the lattice constant

L=|\mathbf{l}_{1}|=|\mathbf{l}_{2}|=|\mathbf{l}_{3}|=a_{g}/[2\sin(\theta/2)]\approx 200\,\mathrm{nm}
(Fig.
1
), which greatly exceeds the characteristic width of the domain walls
\ell\sim 6\,\mathrm{nm}
.
In turn, the latter
greatly exceeds the lattice constant
a_{g}=0.246\,\mathrm{nm}
of monolayer graphene.

In the experiment, mTBG is often subject to an out-of-plane electric field
due to external gates or charged impurities.
In the presence of such a field, the AB and BA regions develop a bandgap
\Delta_{\mathrm{TBG}}
,
which can be as high as
250\,\mathrm{meV}

Zhang et al. 2009
,
while AB-BA domain walls remain gapless.
Each domain wall hosts at least two bound states (per valley, per spin) whose energy dispersions cross the bandgap.
These one-dimensional (1D) states are topologically protected and their propagation directions are opposite for the
\mathrm{K}
and
\mathrm{K}^{\prime}
valleys
Martin et al. 2008
;
Zhang et al. 2013
;
Vaezi et al. 2013
;
Ju et al. 2015
;
Yin et al. 2016
;
Hasdeo and Song 2017
.
These states form a triangular network of conducting channels
Yoo et al. 2019
;
San-Jose and Prada 2013
;
Huang et al. 2018
;
Rickhaus et al. 2018
;
Walet and Guinea 2019
;
Xu et al. 2019
;
Fleischmann et al. 2019
;
Tsim et al. 2020
;
Hou et al. 2020
.
The domain walls are the links and their intersections are the nodes (or junctions) of the network, see Fig.
1
.

Figure 1:

The AB and BA stacking domains are separated by domain walls (links), which intersect at AA regions (nodes).
When an interlayer bias is applied, the AB and BA regions are gapped while the domain walls host 1D electron states.
The wiggly lines represent 1D plasmons that propagate along the links and scatter at the nodes.
The red arrows indicate the primitive vectors
\mathbf{l}_{1}
,
\mathbf{l}_{2}
,
\mathbf{l}_{3}
of the moiré superlattice.

Near-field infrared imaging of a doped mTBG
Jiang et al. 2016a
;
Jiang et al. 2017
;
Sunku et al. 2018

revealed that two-dimensional (2D) plasmons in mTBG experience scattering at the domain walls.
This observation was attributed to an enhanced local optical conductivity
due to the topological 1D states.
Direct imaging of these 1D states was not possible
in those experiments since the Fermi energy was outside the bandgap.

On the theory side, the low-energy electronic band structure of the domain-wall network
has been elegantly described by scattering models
Efimkin and MacDonald 2018
;
De Beule et al. 2020
;
Chou et al. 2020
.
A basic assumption is that the electron momentum is conserved when they propagate along the links but it can change when they scatter at the nodes. This momentum change can be crudely estimated as the inverse spatial size of the nodes
\sim\ell^{-1}
,
which is small compared to the separation
4\pi/3a_{g}
of the nearest
\mathrm{K}
and
\mathrm{K}^{\prime}
points in the momentum space.
This implies that scattering at the nodes preserves the valley index.
In particular, electron backscattering at the nodes is negligible in the first approximation.
This property of the mTBG network is rooted in the topological nature of the domain walls
and is not found in previously studied realizations of 1D networks
Parage et al. 1998
;
van de Groep et al. 2012
.
The cited single-particle network models predict band dispersions
\varepsilon(\mathbf{k})
that are repeated in energy with the period
\Delta\varepsilon=2\pi\hbar v/L
,
where
v
is the quasiparticle velocity on the links.
Although realistic electronic structure calculations
Walet and Guinea 2019
;
Tsim et al. 2020
show more complicated band dispersions,
such discrepancies can presumably be accounted for by energy dependence of the model parameters.

A conceptually more important omission of these theoretical models is electron-electron interaction.
It is known that low-energy dynamics of interacting 1D electrons is collective
Giamarchi 2003
.
Therefore, theoretical understanding of the collective modes, such as 1D plasmons, is necessary to describe the response of the system in the microwave and THz domain.
Electron-electron interaction effects in mTBG have been investigated with renormalization group
methods
Wu et al. 2019
;
Lee et al. 2021
;
Chou et al. 2021

but plasmons have not been explicitly addressed.

In this paper, we develop a theory of plasmons in the mTBG network.
We consider two limiting cases distinguished by the relation between
L
and the single-particle phase coherence length
L_{\varphi}

Datta 1995
.
(However, we make no attempt to compute
L_{\varphi}
because it seems to be a complicated problem.)
When
L_{\varphi}\gg L
, the electrons maintain phase coherence upon traversing the links.
In this regime, we employ the standard random phase approximation (RPA) to calculate the dielectric function and the plasmon dispersion of the system.
In the opposite limit,
L_{\varphi}\ll L
, phases of single-particle states are randomized while they propagate along the links, due to inelastic scattering.
The physically relevant degrees of freedom are collective: the total charge density and the total current
governed by the Luttinger liquid theory
Giamarchi 2003
.
We formulate a plasmonic network model (PNM) to describe this incoherent regime.
We assume that the dephasing processes are momentum conserving,
so that they do not cause plasmonic damping on the links.
Therefore, plasmons propagate ballistically along the AB-BA links and scatter at AA nodes of the network.

An important difference between the single-particle and plasmon network models is as follows.
Since Coulomb interaction mixes plasmons of all propagation directions, plasmon backscattering at the nodes can be significant
even when it is negligible for single electrons.
The technical term used to describe it in the context of 1D systems
is the
g_{2}
-coupling
Giamarchi 2003
.
Taking into account such scattering channels is a key distinction of our theory from a prior work
Brey et al. 2020

where the plasmon backscattering was discarded.

The plasmon scattering at a junction can be visualized in terms
of an effective circuit, Fig.
2
,
where interlink admittances
y_{j}
relate currents and voltage differences between the links.
These parameters can be organized into a
6\times 6
admittance matrix
\mathbf{Y}
.
Such an approach is customary in the theory of microwave transmission lines
Montgomery 1987
.
In the actual calculation, we work not with
y_{j}
’s but with the eigenvalues
Y_{m}
of matrix
\mathbf{Y}
,
which are directly related to the plasmon scattering phase shifts in different angular momentum channels.
We show that these phase shifts depend on the strength of electron-electron interactions.
The latter is expressed in terms of the Luttinger liquid parameter
K
,
where
K=1
for non-interacting electrons
and
K<1
for repulsive interactions
Giamarchi 2003
.
In the weakly interacting limit,
K\to 1
, the admittance matrix is determined by the single-particle scattering probabilities
in accordance with the Landauer formula
Kamenev and Kohn 2001
.
The eigenvalues
Y_{m}
are purely real and positive;
hence, the junction behaves as a circuit of resistors.
Coulomb interactions among the links generate imaginary corrections to the phase shifts.
This is equivalent to adding some capacitors in parallel to the resistors.
In principle, Luttinger liquid
Kane and Fisher 1992
;
Guinea et al. 1995
;
Houzet et al. 2024
and pinning
Guinea et al. 1995
effects can produce further real and imaginary contributions to the admittance. However, since the single-particle backscattering in mTBG is weak, these corrections should be small and we ignore them here.

Figure 2:
Effective circuit for a junction of six leads (the thick solid lines). The numbers indicate two different schemes of labeling the leads. For clarity, only the connections originating from lead
1

1-
) are shown, e.g., admittance connecting it to the ground is
y_{0}
, the ones wiring it to the nearest leads are
y_{1}
,
etc
. The remaining leads are wired the same way.

Our two approaches give plasmon spectra that agree in their overall structure but the RPA predicts more fine features compared to the PNM.
In both theories these spectra are not strictly periodic in frequency,
unlike the single-particle ones
Efimkin and MacDonald 2018
;
De Beule et al. 2020
;
Chou et al. 2020
,
because the plasmon velocity on the links is momentum-dependent even without scattering.
Within the RPA, the plasmon modes are punctuated by numerous narrow particle-hole continua.
The plasmons remain sharp outside these continua but experience Landau damping inside of them.
Within the PNM, the damping has a more uniform character in absolute units,
so that in relative terms it has a stronger effect as frequency decreases.
In the low-frequency limit, all the plasmon modes are overdamped according to the PNM
whereas they remain sharp within the RPA.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.
II
, we discuss the RPA approach.
In Sec.
III
, we lay a groundwork for the PNM
by treating the plasmon scattering problem at a junction.
In Sec.
IV
, we
compute the plasmonic spectrum within the PNM and compare the results with those from the RPA.
In Sec.
V
,
we simulate signatures of mTBG plasmons in near-field imaging.
In Sec.
VI
, we conclude the paper with a brief summary and an outlook.
Additional technical details are provided in the Appendix.

II
Random phase approximation

We begin this section with a theoretical model for the single-particle states of the domain-wall network of mTBG.
Next, we explain our choice for the electron-electron interaction model.
Using these ingredients, we compute the dielectric function within the RPA.
Finally, we discuss the predictions of the RPA for the plasmon dispersion of mTBG.

II.1
Single-particle spectrum of mTBG

To obtain the single-particle band structure we employ the network model of Ref.
Efimkin and MacDonald 2018
.
This model has a single parameter: the forward scattering probability
P_{f}
.
As explained in Sec.
I
, the backscattering probability is zero.
The allowed scattering processes at a junction are shown
in the inset of Fig.
3
(b).
In addition to forward scattering, they include deflections
by angles
\pm 2\pi/3
with probability
P_{d}=(1-P_{f})/2
.
Effectively, each of the three intersecting domain wall acts as a partially transparent beam-splitting mirror.
As also mentioned in Sec.
I
,
each domain wall hosts
N_{c}=2
1D channels.
In addition, electrons have the two-fold spin degeneracy
N_{s}=2
,
yielding the total of
N=N_{s}N_{c}=4
modes per domain wall.
For simplicity, we neglect scattering between any of these modes
although the model can be generalized to include some of them
De Beule et al. 2020
.

P_{f}=1
(top),
P_{f}=0.8
(middle), and
P_{f}=0.4

Only the three bands of the

.

).

\overline{D}

.

).

The single-particle scattering matrix of a junction takes the form

\mathbf{S}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&s_{d}&0&s_{f}&0&s_{d}\\
s_{d}&0&s_{d}&0&s_{f}&0\\
0&s_{d}&0&s_{d}&0&s_{f}\\
s_{f}&0&s_{d}&0&s_{d}&0\\
0&s_{f}&0&s_{d}&0&s_{d}\\
s_{d}&0&s_{f}&0&s_{d}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(1)

The rows (colums) of
\mathbf{S}_{0}
correspond to the outgoing (incoming) states of different leads.
Without loss of generality, we can choose the deflection amplitude

s_{d}=i\sqrt{P_{d}}
to be imaginary.
The unitarity of
\mathbf{S}_{0}
then determines

s_{f}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{4P_{f}-P_{d}}+i\sqrt{P_{d}}\right)
.
The eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}_{0}
have the form
e^{2i\delta_{0,m}}
where scattering phase shifts
\delta_{0,m}
are real.
We index these phase shifts by
m=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,3
, which is the angular momentum modulo
6
.
The corresponding eigenvectors
\mathbf{z}_{m}
have components

{z}_{m,a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\,e^{i\pi ma/3},\qquad a=0,1,\ldots,5\,.

(2)

Diagonalizing
\mathbf{S}_{0}
, we find the phase shifts as follows:

\displaystyle\delta_{0,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\arcsin\left(\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{P_{d}}\,\right)\,,

(3a)

\displaystyle\delta_{0,\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}-\delta_{0,0}\,,

(3b)

\displaystyle\delta_{0,\pm 2}

\displaystyle=\pi-\delta_{0,0}\,,

(3c)

\displaystyle\delta_{0,3}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta_{0,0}\,.

(3d)

Note that

P_{d}=\frac{4}{9}\sin^{2}2\delta_{0,0}\,,

(4)

so the admissible values of
P_{d}
are in the range
0\leq P_{d}\leq 4/9
.
According to some studies
Qiao et al. 2014
;
Hou et al. 2020
, deflection is favored over forward scattering, in which case
1/3<P_{d}\leq 4/9
.

Following Ref.
Efimkin and MacDonald 2018
, let us review the computation of the single-particle states.
Let us choose a unit cell of the network such that it is centered at a node and
contains three links of length
L
. One possible way to do so is to start with the usual hexagonal Wigner-Seitz cell
-\pi<\mathbf{G}_{a}\cdot\mathbf{r}<\pi
,
where
\mathbf{G}_{a}
are three shortest reciprocal lattice vectors:

\mathbf{G}_{a}=\frac{4\pi}{3L}\left(\,\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a}-\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a+1}\right),\quad a=1,2,3\>(\mathrm{mod}\ 3),

(5)

and
\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a}=\mathbf{l}_{a}/L
is the unit vector in the direction of the lattice vector
\mathbf{l}_{a}
(Fig.
1
).
This Wigner-Seitz cell contains six links of
half
length, so we add the remaining halves of the links parallel to the lattice vectors
\mathbf{l}_{a}
and remove the half-links anti-parallel to
\mathbf{l}_{a}
.
Each link of such a unit cell has two propagation directions
(+)
and
(-)
,
so in total there are six independent wavefunction amplitudes per unit cell per mode.
Going clockwise from link
\mathbf{l}_{1}
(Fig.
2
), we organize these amplitudes into a six-component column vector

{\bm{\Psi}}=\left(\psi_{1-},\psi_{2+},\psi_{3-},\psi_{1+},\psi_{2-},\psi_{3+}\right)^{T}.

(6)

The even (odd) components represent the states that belong to the
\mathrm{K}^{\prime}
(
\mathrm{K}
) valley.
Consider an eigenstate with a crystal momentum vector
\mathbf{k}
that belongs to the moiré Brillouin zone
-\pi/L<k_{a}<\pi/L
,
where
k_{a}=\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a}
.
At energy
\varepsilon
, each component of
{\bm{\Psi}}
accumulates a phase
\varepsilon L/\hbar v

traversing the length of the link.
Adding the appropriate Bloch phase factors to
{\bm{\Psi}}
,
we obtain the vectors of incoming and outgoing amplitudes at a node:

\bm{\Psi}_{\mathrm{in}}=e^{i\varepsilon L/\hbar v}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\psi_{1-}\\
\psi_{2+}e^{-ik_{2}L}\\
\psi_{3-}\\
\psi_{1+}e^{-ik_{1}L}\\
\psi_{2-}\\
\psi_{3+}e^{-ik_{3}L}\end{array}\right),\quad\bm{\Psi}_{\mathrm{out}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\psi_{1+}\\
\psi_{2-}e^{-ik_{2}L}\\
\psi_{3+}\\
\psi_{1-}e^{-ik_{1}L}\\
\psi_{2+}\\
\psi_{3-}e^{-ik_{3}L}\end{array}\right).

(7)

The amplitudes
\bm{\Psi}_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\bm{\Psi}_{\mathrm{out}}
are related via the scattering matrix
\mathbf{S}_{0}
:

\bm{\Psi}_{\mathrm{out}}=\mathbf{S}_{0}\,\bm{\Psi}_{\mathrm{in}}
.
Therefore, the single-particle states are obtained by solving the eigenproblem

\mathbf{T}_{0}\,{\bm{\Psi}}=e^{-i\varepsilon L/\hbar v}\,{\bm{\Psi}},

(8)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf{I}_{3}\\
\mathbf{I}_{3}&0\end{pmatrix}\bm{\Lambda}_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{0}\,\bm{\Lambda}_{0},

(9)

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{0}=\operatorname{diag}\left(1,e^{-ik_{2}L},1,e^{-ik_{1}L},1,e^{-ik_{3}L}\right),

(10)

and
\mathbf{I}_{3}
is the
3\times 3
identity matrix.
The chosen scattering matrix
\mathbf{S}_{0}
does not mix the valleys. As a result, our
6
-component problem separates into two independent
3
-component ones,

\mathbf{T}^{\nu}_{0}\,{\bm{\Psi}^{\nu}}=e^{-i\varepsilon L/\hbar v}\,{\bm{\Psi}^{\nu}},\qquad{\nu}=+\text{ or }-\,,

(11)

where, e.g.,
{\bm{\Psi}}^{+}=\left(\psi_{2+},\psi_{1+},\psi_{3+}\right)^{T}
and

\mathbf{T}^{+}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}s_{f}&s_{d}&s_{d}\\
s_{d}&s_{f}&s_{d}\\
s_{d}&s_{d}&s_{f}\end{pmatrix}\left(\begin{array}[]{lll}e^{-ik_{2}L}&0&0\\
0&e^{-ik_{1}L}&0\\
0&0&e^{-ik_{3}L}\end{array}\right).

(12)

This matrix is easy to diagonalize numerically.

In Fig.
3
(a,left), we plot the band structure computed for a few values of
P_{f}
.
Only the three bands of the
\mathrm{K}
valley (i.e.,
\nu=+
bands) are shown; those corresponding to the
\mathrm{K}^{\prime}
valley (
\nu=-
) can be obtained by reversing the crystal momentum.
Also, we plot only one period
2\pi\hbar v/L
in energy.
The top plot in Fig.
3
(a) corresponds to
P_{f}=1
, where
\varepsilon_{l}(\mathbf{k})=\hbar v(k_{l}+\pi/L)
,
so that the surface
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{l}(\mathbf{k})
in the energy-momentum space consists of three intersecting planes.
This is why the plot is composed of straight lines.
Along the high-symmetry lines connecting
\Gamma
,
\mathrm{K}
, and
\mathrm{K}^{\prime}
,
two-fold band degeneracies (not counting the valley) occur because
k_{l}=k_{l^{\prime}}
for some
l
and
l^{\prime}
.
For
P_{f}
slightly less than unity,
the off-diagonal terms in
\mathbf{T}^{+}_{0}
lift these degeneracies.
In particular, near the
k_{l}=k_{l^{\prime}}
lines but away from
\Gamma
,
\mathrm{K}
, and
\mathrm{K}^{\prime}
,
the band energies (modulo
2\pi\hbar v/L
) are given by

\varepsilon_{ll^{\prime},\pm}(\mathbf{k})\simeq{\hbar v}\left[\frac{\pi}{L}-\frac{\sqrt{P_{d}}}{2L}+\frac{k_{l}+k_{l^{\prime}}}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{k_{l}-k_{l^{\prime}}}{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{P_{d}}{L^{2}}}\,\right],

(13)

so that
\varepsilon_{ll^{\prime},+}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{ll^{\prime},-}(\mathbf{k})\simeq 2\hbar v\sqrt{P_{d}}/L

along the aforementioned high-symmetry lines.
This approximately constant energy splitting is apparent for the bands with a negative slope in the middle row of Fig.
3
(a) where
P_{d}=0.1
.
As the
\Gamma
point is approached, the two energies
\varepsilon_{ll^{\prime},\pm}(\mathbf{k})
are perturbed by the proximity of the third band.
Precisely at the
\Gamma
point,
there is a triple degeneracy if
P_{d}=0
[the top row in Fig.
3
(a)],
which is split into a singlet of energy
2\hbar v(\pi-\delta_{0,0})/L
and a doublet (a Dirac point) of energy
2\hbar v\delta_{0,0}/L
if
P_{d}>0
,
see the middle and the bottom rows of Fig.
3
(a).
The same pattern is repeated near
\mathrm{K}
and
\mathrm{K}^{\prime}
points.

II.2
2D and 1D interaction kernels

When computing the electrodynamic response of mTBG,
we envision that the electron motion in the direction perpendicular to the domain walls is characterized by a certain fixed density profile.
The electron density per unit area
n_{\mathrm{2D}}(\mathbf{r})
at an arbitrary position
\mathbf{r}
is the convolution of this profile with a 1D electron density
n_{a,\mathbf{R}}(x)
.
The latter has the units of inverse length and is defined at points
\mathbf{r}

that belong to the network of zero-thickness links,

\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{R}+\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a}x
.
Here
0<x<L
is the distance from
\mathbf{r}
to the center
\mathbf{R}=-m_{1}\mathbf{l}_{1}+m_{2}\mathbf{l}_{2}
of a nearby unit cell,

m_{a}
’s are the integer parts of
\mathbf{G}_{a}\cdot\mathbf{r}/(2\pi)
.

For calculation of
n_{\mathrm{2D}}(\mathbf{r})
and other convolutions we may need, we take advantage of the Fourier transform, which we denote by a tilde, e.g.,

\tilde{n}_{\mathrm{2D}}(\mathbf{q})
.
Whenever the argument of the Fourier transform is
a vector, as is
\mathbf{q}
in this example,
the transform is 2D; if its argument is a scalar, then it is a 1D Fourier transform.
All the quantities also depend on time through a common
e^{-i\omega t}
factor,
which we omit.

A charge excitation with a given crystal momentum
\mathbf{q}
and frequency
\omega
creates a density
perturbation that can be expanded in either 1D or 2D Fourier series:

\displaystyle n_{a,\mathbf{R}}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\sum_{p=-\infty}^{\infty}n_{ap}e^{2\pi ipx/L},\quad\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{R}+\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a}x,

(14)

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{2D}}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{\bot}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}e^{i(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{r}}\,\sum_{p}X_{\mathbf{G},ap}\rho_{\bot}\left((\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\cdot\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a\bot}\right)n_{ap}\,,

(15)

\displaystyle X_{\mathbf{G},ap}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\limits_{0}^{L}\frac{dx}{L}\,e^{-2\pi ipx/L}e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a}x}=\delta_{\!p,g_{a}},\quad g_{a}=\frac{\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{l}_{a}}{2\pi}\,.

(16)

Here
L_{\bot}={\sqrt{3}\,L}/{2}
is the separation between nearest parallel domain walls (Fig.
1
),

\mathbf{G}
are reciprocal lattice vectors,
and
\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a\bot}=\hat{\mathbf{z}}\times\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a}

is the unit vector perpendicular to
a\,
th link.
Function
\rho_{\bot}(k)
in Eq. (
15
) is a form-factor,
i.e., the 1D Fourier transform of the domain wall density profile mentioned above.
It is normalized by the condition
\rho_{\bot}(0)=1
and becomes small at
k\gg 1/\ell
.

The density perturbation given by Eq. (
15
) induces an energy shift
e\Phi_{\mathrm{ind}}(\mathbf{r})
for electrons on the links,
which is equal to the convolution of the density
n_{\mathrm{2D}}(\mathbf{r})
with the
electron interaction kernel
U_{\mathrm{2D}}(\mathbf{r})
and the domain-wall density profile.
Here
e=-|e|
is the electron charge.
Utilizing Eq. (
15
),
we can express this periodically varying potential shift as another Fourier series

\displaystyle e\Phi_{\mathrm{ind}}(\mathbf{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L_{\bot}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}e^{i(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{r}}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{2D}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{ap}X_{\mathbf{G},ap}\rho_{\bot}\left((\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\cdot\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a\bot}\right)n_{ap}

(17)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L_{\bot}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}}e^{i(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{r}}\tilde{U}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\sum_{ap}X_{\mathbf{G},ap}n_{ap}.

(18)

The form-factors
\rho_{\bot}
in Eq. (
17
) ensure that the series converges at
|\mathbf{G}|\sim 1/\ell

but make the formulas unnecessarily complicated.
In Eq. (
18
) we drop these factors and
achieve the convergence by replacing
\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{2D}}(\mathbf{q})

with a regularized kernel
\tilde{U}(\mathbf{q})
, which corresponds to
a real-space interaction potential
U(\mathbf{r})
well-behaved at short distances
|\mathbf{r}|\lesssim\ell
.

In this paper, we consider two model forms of such
U(\mathbf{r})
.
One is the bare Coulomb interaction,

U(\mathbf{r})=\frac{e^{2}}{\kappa}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}|+\ell}\,,

(19)

where
\kappa
is the dielectric constant.
The other is a screened interaction

\displaystyle U(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\kappa}\,\frac{1}{|\mathbf{r}|+\ell}-\frac{e^{2}}{\kappa}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbf{r}|^{2}+4d_{g}^{2}}}\,,

(20)

which includes the image term due to a conducting plate
separated by a distance
d_{g}
from the sample.

For working with the 1D Fourier basis we will need the matrix elements

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{U}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q})=&\frac{1}{L}\,\sum_{\mathbf{R}}\int\limits_{0}^{L}dxe^{-iq_{ap}x}\int\limits_{0}^{L}dx^{\prime}e^{iq_{a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}x^{\prime}}\\
&\times e^{-i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{R}}U\left(\mathbf{R}+\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a}x-\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a^{\prime}}x^{\prime}\right)\end{split}

(21)

\displaystyle\mbox{}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L_{\bot}}\,\sum_{\mathbf{G}}\tilde{U}\left(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}\right)X_{\mathbf{G},ap}X_{\mathbf{G},a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}\,,

(22)

where we use a convenient short-hand notation

q_{ap}=q_{a}+\frac{2\pi p}{L}\,.

(23)

For off-diagonal elements,
a\neq a^{\prime}
, the last sum reduces to a single term:

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L_{\bot}}\,\tilde{U}\left(\mathbf{q}+\frac{p}{g}\,\mathbf{G}_{a^{\prime}+1}+\frac{p^{\prime}}{g^{\prime}}\,\mathbf{G}_{a+1}\right)\,,

(24)

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\mathbf{G}_{a^{\prime}+1}\cdot\mathbf{l}_{a}\,,\quad g^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\mathbf{G}_{a+1}\cdot\mathbf{l}_{a^{\prime}}\,.

(25)

If the interaction range
d_{g}
is short enough, such that
d_{g}\ll L
and
L|\mathbf{q}|,p,p^{\prime}\ll L/d_{g}
,
then these matrix elements are relatively small and approximately equal to one another:

\tilde{U}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q})\simeq\frac{1}{L_{\bot}}\,\tilde{U}\left(\mathbf{0}\right)\simeq\frac{8\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\,\frac{e^{2}}{\kappa}\,\frac{d_{g}}{L}\,,\quad a\neq a^{\prime}\,.

(26)

The diagonal elements are given by

\tilde{U}_{ap,ap^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q})\simeq\tilde{U}(0)\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\simeq\frac{2e^{2}}{\kappa}\,\ln\left(\frac{d_{g}}{\ell}\right)\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\,,

(27)

see Eq. (
28
) below.
These formulas can also be derived from the real-space integral in Eq. (
21
),
which is dominated by small regions near the junction of the links,
0<x,x^{\prime}\lesssim d_{g}
,

0<L-x,x^{\prime}\lesssim d_{g}
, and
0<x,L-x^{\prime}\lesssim d_{g}
,
where all the exponential factors in the integrand can be dropped,
see Appendix
B
.

Finally, one more definition is the 1D Fourier transform of
U(x)
:

\displaystyle\tilde{U}(q)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{2e^{2}}{\kappa}\,\ln\left(\frac{d_{g}}{\ell}\right)\quad q\ll d_{g}^{-1},

(28)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{2e^{2}}{\kappa}\,\ln\left(\frac{A}{q\ell}\right)\,,\quad d_{g}^{-1}\ll q\ll\ell^{-1}

(29)

with
A=0.561
.
As will become clear in the following, for
d_{g}
much smaller than the plasmon wavelength
we can use Eq. (
28
) at all
q
, i.e.,

\tilde{U}(q)=\frac{2e^{2}}{\kappa}\,\ln\left(\frac{d_{g}}{\ell}\right)=\mathrm{const}\,.

(30)

This is equivalent to setting
U(x)=\tilde{U}(0)\delta(x)
on the links,
which we refer to as the short-range interaction model.

II.3
RPA and the plasmon spectrum in mTBG

Within the RPA, the electrons respond to the mean-field energy shift

e\Phi(\mathbf{r})=e\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{r})+e\Phi_{\mathrm{ind}}(\mathbf{r})

where
\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{r})
is an external potential and

\Phi_{\mathrm{ind}}(\mathbf{r})
is the induced potential
[Eq. (
18
)].
Similar to the electron density,
e\Phi(\mathbf{r})
can be expanded in a 1D Fourier series:

\displaystyle e\Phi(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\sum_{p}e\Phi_{ap}e^{2\pi ipx/L},\quad\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{R}+\hat{\mathbf{l}}_{a}x\,.

(31)

The induced electron density is related to
e\Phi(\mathbf{r})
via

n_{ap}=\sum_{a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}P^{(0)}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,e\Phi_{a^{\prime}p^{\prime}},

(32)

where
P^{(0)}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
is the free-particle polarization function:

\displaystyle P^{(0)}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle P^{+}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}+P^{-}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}\,,

(33)

\displaystyle\begin{split}P^{\nu}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=&NL_{\bot}\sum_{ll^{\prime}}\int\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\rho_{ll^{\prime},ap}\rho_{ll^{\prime},a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}^{*}\\
\mbox{}&\times\frac{f\left[\varepsilon_{l}(\mathbf{k})\right]-f\left[\varepsilon_{l^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k}+\nu\mathbf{q})\right]}{\hbar(\omega+i\gamma_{d})+\varepsilon_{l}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{l^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k}+\nu\mathbf{q})},\end{split}

(34)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{ll^{\prime},ap}=&\int\limits_{0}^{L}dx\,e^{-iq_{ap}x}\Psi_{l,a}^{*}(\mathbf{k})\Psi_{l^{\prime},a}(\mathbf{k}+\nu\mathbf{q}),\end{split}

(35)

\displaystyle\Psi_{l,a}(\mathbf{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\psi_{l,a}(\mathbf{k})e^{i\varepsilon_{l}(\mathbf{k})x/\hbar v}\,.

(36)

Here
\nu=\pm
is again the valley index,
the
\mathbf{k}
-integration is over the moiré Brillouin zone,

f(\varepsilon)=\Theta(\varepsilon_{F}-\varepsilon)
is the Fermi distribution function,

\varepsilon_{l}(\mathbf{k})
is the energy of the
l\,
th band in valley
\nu=+
,

\psi_{l,a}(\mathbf{k})
is the corresponding wavefunction amplitude on link
a
,
normalized such that
\sum_{a}|\psi_{l,a}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}=1
,
and
\gamma_{d}
is a small damping parameter, added for regularization purposes.

In general, functions
P^{\mu}_{a^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}
, where
\mu=+
,
-
, or
(0)
have to be calculated numerically.
However, in the special case
P_{d}=0
where the domain walls act as decoupled 1D wires,
these functions have a well-known analytical form
Giamarchi 2003

\displaystyle P^{\nu}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{aa^{\prime}}\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\frac{N}{2\pi\hbar v}\frac{\nu vq_{ap}}{\omega-\nu vq_{ap}}\,,

(37)

\displaystyle P^{(0)}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{aa^{\prime}}\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\frac{N}{\pi\hbar v}\frac{v^{2}q^{2}_{ap}}{\omega^{2}-v^{2}q^{2}_{ap}}\,.

(38)

Let us make a few more definitions we will need later on.
The RPA dielectric function is the matrix with elements

\begin{split}\epsilon_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=&\delta_{aa^{\prime}}\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\\
\mbox{}&-\sum_{a^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime\prime}}\tilde{U}_{ap,a^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime\prime}}(\mathbf{q})P^{(0)}_{a^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega).\end{split}

(39)

The matrix inverse of this dielectric function has elements

\begin{split}\epsilon^{-1}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=&\delta_{aa^{\prime}}\delta_{pp^{\prime}}\\
\mbox{}&+\sum_{a^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime\prime}}\tilde{U}_{ap,a^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime\prime}}(\mathbf{q})P_{a^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,,\end{split}

(40)

where

P_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\sum_{a^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime\prime}}P^{(0)}_{ap,a^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\epsilon^{-1}_{a^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

(41)

is the total polarization, i.e.,
the density response function with respect to the external potential:

n_{ap}=\sum_{a^{\prime},p^{\prime}}P_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)e\Phi_{\mathrm{ext},a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,.

(42)

The various polarization matrices may be easily converted to the conventional 2D basis of reciprocal lattice vectors as follows:

P^{\mu}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\frac{1}{L_{\bot}}\,\sum_{aa^{\prime}}\sum_{pp^{\prime}}X_{\mathbf{G},ap}X_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime},a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}P^{\mu}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,,

(43)

with factors
X_{\mathbf{G},ap}
defined by Eq. (
16
).
In turn, the 2D dielectric function and its inverse are given by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}-\tilde{U}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})P^{(0)}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,,

(44)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}+\tilde{U}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})P_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,.

(45)

The plasmon dispersion can be found by locating the poles of the inverse dielectric function.
This is easy to do in the low-frequency, long-wavelength regime where it is sufficient to keep only the
p=p^{\prime}=0
terms
so that
\epsilon^{-1}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}
can be organized into a
3\times 3
matrix.
In the limit of vanishing deflection and short-range interaction,
where the links are decoupled, this matrix is diagonal:

\epsilon^{-1}_{a0,a^{\prime}0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\delta_{aa^{\prime}}\frac{\omega^{2}-v^{2}q^{2}_{a}}{\omega^{2}-v_{p}^{2}q^{2}_{a}}\,,

(46)

where

v_{p}=\frac{v}{K}

(47)

is the 1D plasmon velocity (derived in Sec.
III.1
below) and

K(q)=\left[1+\frac{N}{\pi\hbar v}\,\tilde{U}(q)\right]^{-1/2}

(48)

is the dimensionless function mentioned in Sec.
I
.
Note that for a short-range interaction,
v_{p}
and
K
are constants independent of
q
.
Accordingly, the long-wavelength plasmon dispersion consists of three acoustic branches:

\omega(\mathbf{q})=v_{a}({\mathbf{q}})q

(49)

with velocities
v_{a}=(q_{a}/q)v_{p}
that depend on the direction of vector
\mathbf{q}
.
These velocities are modified if
P_{d}
or the interaction range are nonzero
but as long as the interaction is screened, the three acoustic branches remain.

In the strongly interacting limit
K\ll 1
, the fastest acoustic branch
dominates the spectral weight and has velocity

v_{s}=\sqrt{\frac{D}{2D_{\mathrm{max}}}}v_{p},

(50)

where

D=2\pi e^{2}N\sum_{l}\int\frac{d^{2}\!k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,{v}_{lx}^{2}(\mathbf{k})\delta\left[\varepsilon_{l}(\mathbf{k})-\varepsilon_{F}\right]

(51)

is the Drude weight,
with
\mathbf{v}_{l}(\mathbf{k})=\hbar^{-1}\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}\varepsilon_{l}
the single-particle velocity of band
l
.
The Drude weight has its maximum value

D_{\mathrm{max}}=\sqrt{3}\,\kappa\alpha N\,\frac{v^{2}}{L}

(52)

at full forward scattering
P_{f}=1
and decreases with decreasing
P_{f}
.
Here

\alpha=\frac{e^{2}}{\kappa\hbar v}

(53)

is the dimensionless parameter that characterizes the strength of the Coulomb interaction.
If the interaction is unscreened,
then the plasmon excitations with the largest spectral weight have a
\sqrt{q}\,
dispersion
typical of 2D conductors:

\omega(q)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\kappa}\,Dq}\,,\qquad qL\ll 1.

(54)

The remaining two low-frequency dispersion branches are acoustic.
They have very small spectral weight, see below.

If
P_{f}<1
, the Drude weight varies periodically with
\varepsilon_{F}
,
as seen in Fig.
3
(a,right).
For example, if
v=10^{8}\,\mathrm{cm}/\mathrm{s}
and
L=200\,\mathrm{nm}
,
the oscillations with Fermi energy have a period of
\Delta\varepsilon_{F}=2\pi\hbar v/(3L)=7\,\mathrm{meV}=1.7\,\mathrm{THz}\times h
.
In terms of the electron density per area,
the oscillation period is
\Delta n_{\mathrm{2D}}=2N/A_{0}=2.3\times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2}
.
Here

A_{0}=L_{\bot}L=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,L^{2}

(55)

is the area of a unit cell of the network.
If detecting such rapid oscillations in experiment
proves challenging, then perhaps
\overline{D}
, the Drude weight averaged over one period, is more accessible. Our numerical results for
\overline{D}
as a function of

P_{f}
are shown in Fig.
3
(b),
together with the analytical approximation

\overline{D}\simeq\left(1-\tfrac{3}{2}\sqrt{P_{d}}\,\right)D_{\mathrm{max}}\,,\quad P_{d}\ll 1\,,

(56)

derived in Appendix
A
.

Plasmon dispersion away from the asymptotic low-frequency limit has to be calculated numerically.
In general, this dispersion has both real and imaginary parts.
Its projection onto the space of real frequencies and momenta can be visualized by plotting the
trace of the spectral function
\tr\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=-\im\sum_{ap}\epsilon^{-1}_{ap,ap}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=-\im\sum_{\mathbf{G}}\epsilon^{-1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

over a desired range of
\mathbf{q}
and
\omega
(assuming
\mathbf{q}
stays within the moiré Brillouin zone).

30
)]
is illustrated by Fig.

(a).
The spectrum is quasi-periodic in frequency so we plot a range of
\omega
only slightly larger than one period
2\pi v_{p}/L
. At low frequencies it consists of three linearly dispersing gapless modes, as predicted by Eq. (
49
).
They can be seen more clearly in an enlarged view of this region, Fig.
4
(c, left).
At higher frequencies, the plasmon modes typically lie in one of the numerous particle-hole continua and have a finite lifetime.
However, at high-symmetry points
\Gamma
,
\mathrm{K}
, and
\mathrm{K}^{\prime}
,
there are dissipationless modes. Their presence can be explained by the existence of
two conserved quantities in the system, the total charge and the valley polarization,
see Sec.
IV
.
Also notable are weakly dispersive bands near frequencies

\displaystyle\omega_{\triangle}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi v_{p}}{L}\,,

(57)

\displaystyle\omega_{\Diamond}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi v_{p}}{L}\,.

(58)

Similar bands are also predicted by the PNM, as labeled in Fig.
4
(b)
and discussed further in Sec.
IV
.

The RPA plasmon dispersion calculated for the unscreened Coulomb interaction
[Fig.

4
(a)]
except the dominant low-frequency mode has the

rather than linear in

cf. Figs.

(c, left), in agreement with Eq. (

).
In addition, the four-fold band degeneracy near
\omega=\omega_{\triangle}
found at the
\Gamma
-point is partially lifted in the unscreened case
for the reasons to be explained in Sec.
IV
.

While the RPA is a standard theoretical approach to study electron systems,
it is not completely reliable.
This approximation neglects vertex corrections to the polarization functions
P^{(0)}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
,
which can produce exciton bound states outside the particle-hole continua and excitonic enhancements inside the continua.
For example, the latter can modify the power-law exponents
\pm 1/2
of the 1D van Hove singularities found near the edges of such continua.
In general, the RPA becomes more accurate with increasing space dimension
d
and number of modes
N
.
Applied to mTBG, where
d=2
and
N=4
, it may be a reasonable approximation in the coherent regime.
On the other hand, the PNM [Figs.
4
(b) and
5
(b)], on which we will focus next,
could be a good approximation in the incoherent regime.

III
Plasmon scattering at a junction

A key ingredient of the PNM is the solution of the plasmon scattering problem at a single junction.
The problem is set up by treating the links attached to the node as semi-infinite 1D leads.
We start with briefly summarizing the standard bosonization approach and key equations for collective modes of such 1D systems.
Thereafter we show how to generalize these equations for the case of a six-lead junction and discuss the
solution of the scattering problem in hand.

III.1
Plasmons of a wire

Consider an isolated wire containing a certain number
N
of 1D propagating modes (
N=4
in mTBG).
It is customary
Giamarchi 2003
to describe the long-wavelength fluctuations of the electron density
n_{k}
and the current
j_{k}
of mode
k
in terms of
densities of the right (
+
) and left (
-
) going fermions:

n_{k}=n_{k}^{+}+n_{k}^{-}\,,\qquad j_{k}=ev\left(n_{k}^{+}-n_{k}^{-}\right)\,,

(59)

which, in turn, can be expressed in terms of bosonic phase fields
\phi_{k}^{\pm}
:

n_{k}^{\pm}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\left[\phi_{k}^{\pm}\right]^{\prime}(x)\,.

(60)

We define nonchiral fields
\phi_{k}=\phi_{k}^{+}+\phi_{k}^{-}
and

\Pi_{k}
such that

n_{k}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\phi_{k}^{\prime}(x)\,,\qquad j_{k}=ev\Pi_{k}\,.

(61)

These fields satisfy the canonical commutation relations
[\phi_{j}(x),\Pi_{k}(x^{\prime})]=i\delta_{jk}\delta(x-x^{\prime})
.
The effective Hamiltonian of the wire is
H=H_{0}+H_{\mathrm{int}}+H_{\mathrm{ext}}\,
,
where

H_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{\hbar v}{2\pi}\int dx\left[\pi^{2}\Pi_{k}^{2}(x)+(\phi_{k}^{\prime})^{2}\right]

(62)

is the kinetic energy,

H_{\mathrm{int}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int dx\int dy\,n_{j}(x)U(x-y)n_{k}(y)

(63)

is the interaction energy, and

H_{\mathrm{ext}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int dx\,eV_{\mathrm{ext}}(x)n_{j}(x)

(64)

is the energy of interaction with an external potential
V_{\mathrm{ext}}(x)
.
Let us perform a linear transformation
\varphi_{j}(x)=\sum_{k}O_{jk}\phi_{k}(x)

where
O_{jk}
are constants forming an orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{O}
.
Such a transformation preserves the commutation relations among the fields.
The corresponding canonical momenta are denoted by
\varPi_{k}
.
If we choose all the elements of the first row of
\mathbf{O}
to be equal,
O_{1k}=1/\sqrt{N}
,
then the total density and current in the wire are expressed solely in terms of
\varphi_{1}
and
\varPi_{1}
fields:

n(x)=-\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\pi}\,\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(x)\,,\quad j(x)=\sqrt{N}\,ev\varPi_{1}(x)\,.

(65)

Therefore,
\varphi_{1}
represents the charged excitation (plasmon) whereas the remaining
\varphi_{k}
’s with
k\neq 1
describe neutral collective modes.
The Hamiltonian becomes

\begin{split}H=&\frac{\hbar v}{2\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int dx\left[\pi^{2}\varPi_{k}^{2}(x)+(\varphi_{k}^{\prime})^{2}\right]\\
&+\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}N\int dx\int dy\,\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(x)U(x-y)\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(y)\\
&-\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{N}\int dx\,eV_{\mathrm{ext}}(x)\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(x)\,.\end{split}

(66)

The corresponding equations of motion are

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi_{k}=

\displaystyle\pi v\varPi_{k},

(67)

\displaystyle\pi\partial_{t}\varPi_{k}=

\displaystyle v\varphi_{k}^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle+\delta_{1k}\frac{1}{\pi\hbar}N\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\int dy\,U(x-y)\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle-\delta_{1k}\frac{1}{\hbar}\sqrt{N}\,eV_{\mathrm{ext}}^{\prime}(x)\,.

(68)

For a harmonic time dependence
e^{-i\omega t}
of the fields, the equations for
k=1
entail

\displaystyle-i\omega en(x)+j^{\prime}(x)=0,

(69)

\displaystyle-i\omega j(x)=\frac{Ne^{2}}{\pi\hbar}vF(x)\,,

(70)

\displaystyle F(x)=-V^{\prime}(x)\,,

(71)

\displaystyle eV(x)=\frac{\pi\hbar v}{N}\,n(x)+\int U(x-y)n(y)dy+eV_{\mathrm{ext}}(x)\,.

(72)

The first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
72
) is the local chemical potential and the remaining two terms are the electrostatic energy per electron.
Therefore,
eV
is the electrochemical potential,
V
is the voltage,
and
eF
is the electrochemical gradient.
For simplicity, we will refer to
F
as the electric field.
The true electric field
E
is related to
F
via

E(x)=F(x)+\frac{\pi\hbar v}{eN}\,n^{\prime}(x)=F(x)+\frac{v^{2}}{\omega^{2}}F^{\prime\prime}(x)\,.

(73)

Combining Eqs. (
69
)–(
72
) together, we obtain

\begin{split}F(x)&+\frac{v^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\left[F^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{N}{\pi\hbar v}\int U^{\prime}(x-y)F^{\prime}(y)dy\right]=F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x)\,,\end{split}

(74)

where
F_{\mathrm{ext}}(x)=-V_{\mathrm{ext}}^{\prime}(x)
is the external electric field.
We can solve Eq. (
74
) by taking the Fourier transform of both sides:

\tilde{F}(q)=\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega^{2}-v_{p}^{2}q^{2}}\tilde{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}(q)\,,

(75)

where
v_{p}=v_{p}(q)
is defined by Eq. (
47
).
Note that the inverse dielectric function of the wire is

\epsilon^{-1}_{\mathrm{1D}}(q,\omega)\equiv\tilde{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}(q)/\tilde{E}(q)
.
Using Eqs. (
73
) and (
75
), it may be written as

\epsilon^{-1}_{\mathrm{1D}}(q,\omega)=1-\frac{1-K^{2}(q)}{q^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{v^{2}}\,K^{2}(q)}\,q^{2}=\frac{\omega^{2}-v^{2}q^{2}}{\omega^{2}-v_{p}^{2}(q)q^{2}}\,,

(76)

which has a pole at the 1D plasmon momentum
q_{p}
that satisfies the equation

\omega=v_{p}(q_{p})q_{p}\,.

(77)

III.2
Plasmons of a junction

Consider now a junction of six leads labeled by
a=1,2,\ldots,6
in the clockwise direction,
see Fig.
2
.
(Note the departure from the earlier convention where
a
had only three possible values.)
To model the dynamics of the plasmon modes we generalize Eqs. (
69
)–(
72
) as follows:

\displaystyle-i\omega e\,\mathbf{n}(r)+\mathbf{j}^{\prime}(r)=0,

(78)

\displaystyle-i\omega\,\mathbf{j}(r)=\frac{Ne^{2}}{\pi\hbar}v\,\mathbf{F}(r)\,,

(79)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(r)=\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}(r)-\frac{\pi\hbar v}{eN}\,\mathbf{n}^{\prime}(r)-\frac{1}{e}\frac{d}{dr}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\mathbf{U}(r,s)\mathbf{n}(s),

(80)

where
r
is the distance from the node.
The bold symbols represent six-component column vectors or
6\times 6
matrices.
For example,
\mathbf{U}(r,s)
is the matrix of interaction kernels

U_{ab}(r,s)=U\left(\sqrt{r^{2}+s^{2}-2rs\cos\beta_{ab}}\,\,\right),

(81)

where
\beta_{ab}
is the angle between leads
a
and
b
.
We can eliminate
\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{j}
to obtain the equation for the field

\mathbf{F}(r)+\frac{v^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\left[\mathbf{F}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{N}{\pi\hbar v}\frac{d}{dr}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\mathbf{U}(r,s)\mathbf{F}^{\prime}(s)\right]=\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{ext}}(r)\,,

To establish the boundary conditions at

we postulate that outgoing and incoming chiral currents

\mathbf{j}^{\pm}=(ev\mathbf{n}\pm\mathbf{j}\,)/2

\mathbf{M}

\mathbf{j}^{+}(0)=\mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{j}^{-}(0).

(83)

Each column of
\mathbf{M}

In the spirit of the RPA, we assume that
the matrix elements of
\mathbf{M}
are scattering probabilities:
M_{ab}=|S_{0,ab}|^{2}
,
where
\mathbf{S}_{0}
is the single-particle scattering matrix, Eq. (
1

This yields

\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}R&P_{d}&0&P_{f}&0&P_{d}\\
P_{d}&R&P_{d}&0&P_{f}&0\\
0&P_{d}&R&P_{d}&0&P_{f}\\
P_{f}&0&P_{d}&R&P_{d}&0\\
0&P_{f}&0&P_{d}&R&P_{d}\\
P_{d}&0&P_{f}&0&P_{d}&R\end{pmatrix},

(84)

where, in addition to the previously introduced parameters
P_{f}
and
P_{d}

to regularize the matrix inversion later on.
Provided

\mathbf{M}
indeed sums to unity.

It is instructive to make a connection between our problem and
the physics of microwave circuits and transmission lines
Montgomery 1987

To this end, we define the junction admittance matrix mentioned in Sec.

\mathbf{Y}=\frac{2Ne^{2}}{h}\left(\,\mathbf{I}_{6}+\mathbf{M}\,\right)^{-1}\left(\,\mathbf{I}_{6}-\mathbf{M}\,\right),

Matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}(0)=\mathbf{j}^{+}(0)-\mathbf{j}^{-}(0)=-\mathbf{Y}\>\mathbf{V}_{J},

(86)

\displaystyle e\,\mathbf{V}_{J}=\frac{\pi\hbar v}{N}\,\mathbf{n}(0)\,.

(87)

These equations show that in the presence of a current, the electron densities on any pair of leads are in general different:
n_{a}(0)\neq n_{b}(0)

a\neq b

The corresponding chemical potentials
are the elements of vector

Their differences

Therefore, the admittance matrix

relates the currents and voltages at the junction.
This is why it
can be represented by a certain equivalent circuit (Fig.
2

In turn, the leads behave as transmission lines of admittance

Y_{*}=\frac{2Ne^{2}}{h}K

(88)

each, see below.

Using Eq. (
85

The full set of its eigenvalues

\displaystyle Y_{0}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(89a)

\displaystyle Y_{\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2Ne^{2}}{h}\,\frac{1-R-P_{d}+P_{f}}{1+R+P_{d}-P_{f}}\to\frac{2Ne^{2}}{h}\,\frac{2-3P_{d}}{3P_{d}}\,,

(89b)

\displaystyle Y_{\pm 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2Ne^{2}}{h}\,\frac{1-R+P_{d}-P_{f}}{1+R-P_{d}+P_{f}}\to\frac{2Ne^{2}}{h}\,\frac{3P_{d}}{2-3P_{d}}\,,

(89c)

\displaystyle Y_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{2Ne^{2}}{h}\,\frac{1-R}{R}\to+\infty\,.

(89d)

the boundary condition (

at

[Alternatively, instead of Eq. (

IV
Plasmonic network model

In this section, we first introduce the local PNM, where interactions between distant links are neglected
and the plasmon modes are determined from the scattering matrix at each node.
Using this framework, we compute the plasmon spectrum for a short-range interaction.
We then discuss the non-local PNM, which is valid for arbitrary interaction kernels,
and compare its predictions with those of the local approximation for the case of unscreened Coulomb interactions.
Finally, we employ the non-local PNM to derive an effective medium theory that describes the system
in the low-frequency, long-wavelength regime.

IV.1
Local PNM

In the local PNM, the construction of the eigenproblem for plasmon modes follows the same steps as in
the single-particle case, Sec.
II.1
.
The equation to solve is

\mathbf{T}\,{\mathbf{n}}=e^{-iq_{p}L}\,{\mathbf{n}},

(102)

where

{\mathbf{n}}=\left(n_{1-},n_{2+},n_{3-},n_{1+},n_{2-},n_{3+}\right)^{T}

(103)

is the six-component column vector of plasmon amplitudes on the three links of the unit cell and two propagation directions.
Keeping with Sec.
III
, we also use a numerical index
a
to label the components of
{\mathbf{n}}
and other six-vectors, such that
“
1-
” is
a=1
, “
2+
” is
a=2
, and so on until “
3+
” which is
a=6
,
see also Fig.
2
.
Matrix
\mathbf{T}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf{I}_{3}\\
\mathbf{I}_{3}&0\end{pmatrix}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{S}\,\bm{\Lambda},

(104)

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}=\operatorname{diag}\left(1,e^{-iq_{2}L},1,e^{-iq_{1}L},1,e^{-iq_{3}L}\right),

(105)

[Eq. (

Alternatively, we can visualize this dispersion

\tr\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=-\im\tr\bm{\epsilon}^{-1}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

and

The inverse dielectric function

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-1}_{ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{aa^{\prime}}\delta_{pp^{\prime}}+\tilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{ind},ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}_{\mathrm{ind},ap,a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\,\int\limits_{0}^{L}dxe^{-iq_{ap}x}\int\limits_{0}^{L}dx^{\prime}e^{iq_{a^{\prime}p^{\prime}}x^{\prime}}\phi_{aa^{\prime}}(x,x^{\prime})\,,

\displaystyle\phi_{aa^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\left(1-K^{2}\right)\left(\phi_{aa^{\prime}}^{(1)}+\phi_{aa^{\prime}}^{(2)}+\phi_{aa^{\prime}}^{(3)}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\phi_{aa^{\prime}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\phi_{aa^{\prime}}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}q_{p}\left[B_{a+,a^{\prime}-}e^{iq_{p}(x-x^{\prime})}+B_{a-,a^{\prime}+}e^{-iq_{p}(x-x^{\prime})}\right],

This is based on the assumption that
it is permissible to approximate the dielectric function matrix by a scalar,

\epsilon_{\mathrm{EMT}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\equiv\epsilon_{\mathbf{0}\mathbf{0}}=1-\tilde{U}(\mathbf{q})\frac{1}{L_{\bot}}\,\sum_{a,a^{\prime}}P^{(0)}_{a\,0,a^{\prime}\,0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\,,

(120)

cf. Eqs. (
43
) and (
44
).
The discarded matrix elements
\epsilon_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}
give only small corrections
if the interaction is long-range so that

\tilde{U}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\ll\tilde{U}(\mathbf{q})
.
The general expression for
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EMT}}
is given in Appendix
D
.
Expanding this to the order
O\left(q^{2}\right)
,
we find

\epsilon_{\mathrm{EMT}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\simeq 1-\frac{\tilde{U}(\mathbf{q})}{\pi e^{2}}\,\frac{D_{\mathrm{max}}q^{2}}{\left(\omega+i\gamma\right)\omega}\,,\quad vq\ll\gamma\,.

(121)

This implies that the network behaves as a continuous 2D medium with
conductivity
\sigma_{\mathrm{EMT}}
of the Drude form:

\sigma_{\mathrm{EMT}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\frac{D_{\mathrm{max}}}{\gamma-i\omega}\,.

(122)

It can be shown that the EMT is valid for a wider class of interaction types if instead of
\tilde{U}(\mathbf{q})
we use the effective 2D kernel

\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{EMT}}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{L_{\bot}}{9}\sum_{a,a^{\prime}}\tilde{U}_{a\,0,a^{\prime}\,0}(\mathbf{q})\,.

(123)

For example,
\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{EMT}}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{3}\,L_{\bot}\tilde{U}(0)

for short-range interactions.

Solving
\epsilon_{\mathrm{EMT}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=0
, which is a quadratic equation for
\omega
,
we find two collective mode frequencies

\omega(\mathbf{q})=-\frac{i}{2}\gamma\pm\left[\frac{D_{\mathrm{max}}}{\pi e^{2}}\,\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{EMT}}(\mathbf{q})q^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{2}\right]^{1/2}.

(124)

For short-range interactions,
this formula agrees with our results in Sec.
IV.1

in the strongly interacting limit
K\ll 1
.
At
q\ll\gamma/v_{p}
, it reproduces the first diffusive mode and the relaxational mode, Eqs. (
110
) and
(
113
), respectively.
At larger
q
, it predicts

\omega(\mathbf{q})\simeq\pm\frac{v_{p}}{\sqrt{2}}q-\frac{i}{2}\gamma\,,\qquad\frac{\gamma}{v_{p}}\ll q\ll\frac{\gamma}{v}\,,

(125)

in agreement with Eq. (
115
).
For unscreened interactions with

\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{EMT}}(\mathbf{q})=2\pi e^{2}/\kappa|\mathbf{q}|
,
the fast diffusive mode [Eq. (
110
)]
becomes superdiffusive,

\omega(\mathbf{q})=-i\frac{2D_{\mathrm{max}}}{\kappa\gamma}q\,,

(126)

which is a well-known result.
As
q
increases beyond
\gamma^{2}\kappa/D_{\mathrm{max}}
, this mode evolves into a propagating plasmon mode with the expected
\sqrt{q}
dispersion:

\omega(\mathbf{q})=\sqrt{\frac{2D_{\mathrm{max}}}{\kappa}q}-\frac{i}{2}\gamma\,.

(127)

This is the gapless branch seen in Fig.
8
(a).

To derive Eq. (
111
) for the slower diffusive mode,
the expansion of Eq. (
120
) has to be pushed to the order
O\left(q^{4}\right)
.
The corresponding pole singularity of the inverse dielectric function is given by

\epsilon^{-1}_{\mathrm{EMT}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\simeq\frac{Z(\mathbf{q})}{\omega+i\mathcal{D}_{0}q^{2}}\,.

(128)

The residue of the pole,

Z(\mathbf{q})=\frac{i\pi}{48\gamma^{3}}\,\frac{e^{2}v^{6}q^{4}\cos^{2}3\theta}{D_{\mathrm{max}}\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{EMT}}(\mathbf{q})}\,,

(129)

i.e., the optical strength of this mode, has a six-fold anisotropy
and a small magnitude
\propto q^{4}/\tilde{U}_{\mathrm{EMT}}(\mathbf{q})
.
Here
\theta
is the angle between the vectors
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{l}_{3}
.
As explained earlier, this mode arises due to the absence of single-particle intervalley scattering,
which results in a conserved valley polarization.
Unfortunately, it is hardly possible to resolve this practically neutral, overdamped mode in
either of Figs.
4
(c,right),
5
(c,right), or
8
(a).

V
Near-field imaging simulations

Plasmons can be observed in real-space using scattering-type scanning near-field optical microscopy (s-SNOM)
Chen et al. 2019
that utilizes a movable probe with a sharp polarizable tip positioned near the sample.
The probe acts as a nano-optical antenna that launches or detects propagating surface plasmons.
Alternatively, plasmons can be launched by a fixed scatterer (“impurity”) located on the sample and then detected by the probe.
The near-field signal measured by the s-SNOM may depend on
the response of the sample and the probe-sample coupling in a complicated way
Chen et al. 2019
;
Jiang et al. 2016b
.
Here we use two common simplifying approximations that (i) the probe can be modeled as a point dipole positioned a distance
z_{\mathrm{tip}}
away from the sample and
(ii) the measured signal
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{r})
is proportional to the difference in
z
-component of the electric field at the tip with and without the sample.
In experiment,
z_{\mathrm{tip}}
varies in time as the probe oscillates at a finite tapping frequency
\Omega
, and the signal is demodulated
at some high harmonic of this frequency to suppress the far-field background
Chen et al. 2019
.
We ignore this complication and assume
z_{\mathrm{tip}}
to be fixed.
With these approximations, we show below that
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{r})
is given by the convolution of the inverse dielectric function of the sample with appropriate form factors,
which have a characteristic spread of
\sim z_{\mathrm{tip}}
in real space.

Let
\mathbf{r}_{0}
be the in-plane position of the launcher.
Within the quasi-static approximation,
it produces an external potential
\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{0})
at a point
\mathbf{r}_{1}
on the sample surface.
For tip-launched plasmons, we take

\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{r})=\Phi_{d}(\mathbf{r},z_{\mathrm{tip}})
,
where

\Phi_{d}\propto(r^{2}+z^{2})^{-3/2}

is the in-plane potential of a point dipole oriented along the
z
direction.
The 2D Fourier transform of this model potential is

\tilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{q})=e^{-qz_{\mathrm{tip}}}
.
For simplicity, we use the same form of
\tilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{ext}}
for plasmons launched by an impurity:

\tilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{q})=e^{-qr_{\mathrm{imp}}}
,
where
r_{\mathrm{imp}}
is a characteristic radius of the launcher.
The induced in-plane potential
\Phi
is given by
evaluating the convolution of
\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}
with
the inverse dielectric function
\bm{\epsilon}^{-1}
,
then subtracting off
\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}
.
The Fourier transform of
\Phi
can be expressed as

\tilde{\Phi}({\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}},z=0)=\sum_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\left[\epsilon^{-1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)-\delta_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\right]\tilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime})e^{-i(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{r}_{0}}.

(130)

Within the quasi-static approximation,
\Phi

satisfies the three-dimensional Laplace equation, so at an arbitrary point
z
above the sample,
we have

\tilde{\Phi}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G},z)=\tilde{\Phi}({\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}},0)e^{-|\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}|z}.

(131)

By assumption, the near-field signal
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{r})
is proportional to the
z
-component of the electric field at the tip:

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{r})=&-\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{\mathrm{tip}}}\Phi(\mathbf{r},z_{\mathrm{tip}})\\
\mbox{}=&\sum_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\,\int\frac{d^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\cdot\mathbf{r}}e^{-i(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{r}_{0}}\\
&\times{F}_{d}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G})\,\left[\epsilon^{-1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)-\delta_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\right]\tilde{\Phi}_{\mathrm{ext}}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime}),\end{split}

(132)

where

{F}_{d}(\mathbf{q})=-\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{\mathrm{tip}}}\tilde{\Phi}_{d}(\mathbf{q},z_{\mathrm{tip}})=qe^{-qz_{\mathrm{tip}}}.

(133)

This is the formula we use for our simulations except we add a small imaginary part
\gamma_{d}
to
\omega
, for regularization purposes, see below.

),

\lambda_{p}/2
,

The right panels in Fig.

are a simulation of the near-field signal produced by a local plasmon source in the sample plane.
In a uniform 2D conductor, such a source would produce a cylindrical wave,

\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{r})\sim H_{0}^{(1)}(q_{p}r)
where

H_{0}^{(1)}(x)
is the Hankel function of the first kind.
In our network system, we expect
q_{p}
in this formula to be replaced,
in the first approximation,
by the magnitude
q
of the crystal momentum
\mathbf{q}\parallel\hat{\mathbf{r}}
at which the matrix element
\epsilon^{-1}_{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
in Eq. (
132

At a generic frequency
\omega
, the wavelength of such a wave will be direction-dependent.
Additionally, Eq. (
132
) indicates that
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{r})
may acquire phase factors of the form
e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
. Altogether, if
q
is nonzero and distance
r
is large such that
|qr|\gg 1
, we expect

\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{r})\sim f(\mathbf{r})e^{iqr}/\sqrt{r}
,
where function
f(\mathbf{r})
has the periodicity of a unit cell and some directional anisotropy.
If
q=0
, then the formula in terms of the Hankel function gives infinity.
This is why the regularization parameter
\gamma_{d}
is needed.
For small
\gamma_{d}
,
we expect
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{r})\sim H_{0}^{(1)}(q_{d}r)
with
q_{d}\propto i\sqrt{\gamma_{d}}

so that
|\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{r})|\sim\ln(l_{d}/r)
at
r\ll l_{d}\propto 1/\sqrt{\gamma_{d}}
.

is expected if the frequency is in resonance with a sharp mode at the

corresponding to a predominantly imaginary
q

In Fig.

VI
Discussion

In this work, we have formulated a theoretical model for plasmons
in the network of 1D states formed in a minimally twisted gapped bilayer graphene (mTBG).
We studied two different regimes.
In the phase-coherent regime,
L\ll L_{\varphi}
,
where
L
is the lattice constant of the network and
L_{\varphi}

is the single-particle phase coherence length,
we employed the RPA.
In the phase-incoherent regime,
L_{\varphi}\ll L
, we used the PNM.
The PNM is similar to scattering models used to describe the single-particle band structure of the mTBG network
Efimkin and MacDonald 2018
;
De Beule et al. 2020
;
however, it has two important differences. First, plasmon scattering at each network junction generically dissipates energy,
so the plasmon modes are damped everywhere except at a few high-symmetry points in the mini-Brillouin zone.
Second, the scattering at the junctions is governed by a full
6\times 6
matrix,
i.e., plasmons can scatter in any direction.
In contrast, in a related work,
plasmons were restricted to scatter only in certain directions, same as single particles
Brey et al. 2020
.
The omnidirectional plasmon scattering is possible because the Coulomb interaction
couples all electrons regardless of their valley index.
Indeed, the dielectric function of the system
depends on the sum of
\mathbf{P}^{+}
and
\mathbf{P}^{-}
,
the polarization functions of the two valleys.
Therefore, the equation for the zero modes of the dielectric function
necessarily involves a coupling between
\mathbf{P}^{+}
and
\mathbf{P}^{-}
.

In order to experimentally realize domain-wall plasmons, it is necessary that the Fermi level resides in the band gap,
and that the frequency is low enough to avoid Landau damping associated with optical transitions to the bands in AB and BA regions.
The former condition may be satisfied by using a top gate in addition to a back gate to independently control the displacement field and the Fermi level.
Of course, detecting plasmons in top-gated systems may be challenging.
Methods based on a transmission line could be a viable alternative
to near-field imaging
Gallagher et al. 2019
;
McIver et al. 2020
.
To satisfy the latter condition,
\omega
must be smaller than
\Delta_{\mathrm{TBG}}/2\hbar
, with
\Delta_{\mathrm{TBG}}
being the bandgap.
Band gaps up to
\Delta_{\mathrm{TBG}}=250\,\mathrm{meV}
or

\Delta_{\mathrm{TBG}}/2\hbar=30\,\mathrm{THz}
can be achieved
Zhang et al. 2009
.
In comparison, a characteristic frequency scale of plasmons is
\omega_{*}=2\pi v/(KL)
.
Hence, with
v\sim 10^{8}\,\mathrm{cm}/\mathrm{s}
,

L\sim 200\,\mathrm{nm}
(corresponding to a twist angle of
\sim 0.07^{\circ}
), and
K\sim 0.5
, we have
\omega_{*}\sim 10\,\mathrm{THz}
.
Therefore, 1D plasmons with wavelengths
\lambda_{p}<L
can be realized.

Potential future directions for theoretical investigation include
exploring a crossover between the coherent and the incoherent regimes.
The coherence length
L_{\varphi}
should decrease as temperature and interaction strength increase,
so that a schematic regime diagram may look like as shown in Fig.
11
.
The coherence length can be deduced from measuring Aharonov-Bohm oscillations
in magnetotransport experiments where mTBG is subject
to an external magnetic field
Rickhaus et al. 2018
;
Xu et al. 2019
;
De Beule et al. 2020
.
In a fully coherent case the Aharonov-Bohm oscillations should evolve into
the quantum Hall effect with Hofstadter butterfly characteristics
Chou et al. 2020
.
It would be interesting to study the effect of the magnetic field on the plasmons.
In particular, the localized plasmon eigenfunctions shown in Fig.
7

may exhibit oscillations with a period proportional to the areas they enclose.

Another intriguing possibility is to extend our model to study plasmons in TBG at large twist angles near commensurate ones (e.g.,
\theta=38.21^{\circ}
),
which has also been shown to host a network of topological domain wall states described by a scattering model
Pal et al. 2019
.
Considering effects of elastic strain, which can induce quasi-1D channels in moiré systems
Sinner et al. 2023
, may also be worthwhile.
Narrow-band systems, such as TBG, are predicted to possess undamped plasmons above the particle-hole continuum
Lewandowski and Levitov 2019
.
This may have implications for our model system as well.
Finally, it may be interesting to extend our theory to plasmons in artificial networks
of nanotubes or nanowires
Parage et al. 1998
;
van de Groep et al. 2012
.
