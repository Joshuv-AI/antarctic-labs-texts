Importance Sampling Approximation of Sequence Evolution Models With Site-Dependence
Joseph Mathews, Scott C. Schmidler

Abstract
We consider models for molecular sequence evolution in which the transition rates at each site depend on the local sequence context, giving rise to a time-inhomogeneous Markov process in which sites evolve under a complex dependency structure. We introduce a randomized approximation algorithm for the marginal sequence likelihood under these models using importance sampling, and provide matching order upper and lower bounds on the finite sample approximation error. Given two sequences of length n n with r r observed mutations, we show that for practical regimes of r / n r/n , the complexity of the importance sampler does not grow exponentially n n , but rather in r r , making the algorithm practical for many applied problems. We demonstrate the use of our techniques to obtain problem-specific complexity bounds for a well-known dependent-site model from the phylogenetics literature.

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms

,

,

However, MCMC algorithms suffer from several undesirable properties that limit their practical utility in many applications of DSMs (e.g. phylogenetic tree reconstruction), which often require repeated calculation of marginal likelihoods during iterative sampling (Bayesian MCMC) or optimization (MLE). These undesirable properties include their iterative nature and the necessity of assessing convergence empirically

,
7
]
along with the challenges of doing so in high dimensional spaces
[
2
,
44
]
, the lack of simple parallelization
[

]

,
19
]
. Moreover, estimation of marginal likelihoods directly from MCMC output is notoriously difficult
[
47
,
10
,
5
]
. In situations requiring repeated evaluation within an optimization or sampling loop, we instead desire a highly computationally efficient estimator, ideally with bounded approximation error.

Here we propose an approach to approximating
p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
under an arbitrary DSM using
importance sampling, with instrumental distribution based on an ISM. A practical benefit of this approach is that it allows practitioners to leverage the vast software resources developed by the phylogenetics community over several decades
[
24
]
. Because calculation of the transition probability is straightforward under an ISM, and endpoint-conditioned path sampling under an ISM is feasible (see Section
2.2
), we readily obtain an importance sampling estimator using an ISM as an instrumental distribution.

Our main result is a bound on the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between the corresponding independent and dependent site models, which in turn provides an upper bound on the number of samples needed to approximate
p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
under the DSM with
\epsilon
-relative error. A common shortcoming of importance sampling is that the sample complexity typically grows exponentially in the problem dimension. Here, we show that the required sample size depends on the sequence length
n
only through the number
r

[
13
]
. We also provide a lower bound on the KL-divergence of the ISM and DSM models, providing conditions under which our upper bounds are near-optimal by appealing to results of
Chatterjee and
Diaconis 2018

The main technical difficulty in establishing our results is that the weights (density ratio) between the ISM and DSM models

and
\pi

\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}

Instead we directly bound the
\chi^{2}
-divergence by obtaining a bound on the moment generating function of the total number of such unobserved mutations.
We then take advantage of a connection between
the space of paths from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{y}
and the symmetric group
\SymS
on
\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\{i:x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}
corresponding to the
orderings
in which the
r
observed mutations occur, and establish an inequality relating the

norms of the density ratios (importance weights) on these two spaces.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section
2
establishes notation and the problem setup; Section
3
presents our main results; Section
4

-divergence and a lower bound on the KL-divergence between the ISM and DSM models, and provides an application to the CpG model; Section

3
Main Results

We now state the main results of the paper; supporting results are established in the following sections.

Our first main result concerns the sample complexity of the importance sampler. It is obtained by establishing a bound on the

Assumption 1
.

The time interval

In what follows, we often write

T
instead of
r(n)
and
T(n)
for brevity, except where we wish to emphasize the dependence explicitly.

We can now state our first theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\phi_{\star}=\phi_{\max}/\phi_{\min}
denote the ratio of the largest and smallest context-dependent multipliers. For endpoint-conditioned independent-site process
\mu
and dependent-site process
\pi
defined in Section
2
, there exists a constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\pi\mid\mid\mu)\leq\phi^{2r}_{\star}\exp\left(c(k,\phi_{\star})\cdot(rT+(n-r)T^{2})\right)-1,

for
c(k,\phi_{\star})=\mathcal{O}(\phi_{\star}^{4(k+1)})
and consequently under Assumption
1
, the importance sampling estimator (
12
) with

\displaystyle N^{\star}(\epsilon)=\mathcal{O}(\phi^{2r}_{\star}e^{c^{\prime}(k,\phi_{\star})}\epsilon^{-2})

for
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and constant
c^{\prime}(k,\phi_{\star})=\mathcal{O}(\phi_{\star}^{4(k+1)})
provides a randomized approximation scheme for
p_{(T,\tilde{\mathbf{Q}})}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})
.

The proof of Theorem
1
is given in Section
4.2
. Theorem
1
says that, for
T=\mathcal{O}(r/n)
, the required sample size (and hence runtime) of the importance sampler to approximate the marginal sequence likelihood is at most exponential in the number of
observed
mutations
r
when
r^{2}\leq n
, bounding the complexity of the algorithm independent of the sequence length
n
. Theorem
1
thus establishes that the importance sampler is substantially more efficient than, for example, rejection sampling or matrix exponentiation when
r\ll n
and
T=\mathcal{O}(r/n)
.
Establishing this bound is non-trivial since the mutation count
m(\Pa)
is unbounded, and a key ingredient of the proof is a bound on the moment generating function (MGF) of
m(\Pa)
under
\mu
.
The assumption
r(n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{1}{2}})
is reasonable in situations where
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are close in evolutionary distance (share a recent common ancestor, or correspond to highly conserved regions of the genome). This assumption ensures that the mutational process is far from equilibrium so that
T
can be reliably estimated
[
3
,
28
,
29
]
(for example, under the JC69 model
[
20
]
, the MLE of
T
does not exist if
r(n)>3n/4
). In many applications (e.g. genomic sequences)
n
is very large, but the fraction of sites with observed mutations is low.

The efficiency of the importance sampler depends critically on the assumption that
T=\mathcal{O}(r/n)
, i.e. that
T
not be too far from
r/n
.
Because
T
and mutation rates are not simultaneously identifiable, rate matrices are commonly scaled to one expected substitution per site per unit time, making
r/n
– a well known measure of genetic distance often called the
p-distance
– a natural estimate of
T
. However under DSMs, estimators of
T
such as the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) or posterior mean are not available in closed form and require iterative optimization or MCMC sampling, with the marginal likelihood evaluated at each iteration.
Mathews and Schmidler 2025
show that the likelihood decays exponentially for values of
T
far from
r/n
– and the posterior distribution of
T
concentrates close to
r/n
, under any reasonable prior distribution – such that larger values of
T
can be safely omitted from consideration in such algorithms without compromising their accuracy.

Our next result, established in Section
4.3
, shows that the upper bound in Theorem
1
is tight by establishing a matching
lower bound
. We first define the
CpG model
, a simple DSM
that has been applied to account for low observed CG frequencies across codon boundaries in lentiviral genes
[
17
]
and mammalian genomes
[
16
]
.

Definition 1
.

The
CpG model
is defined by context-dependent rates

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{i}(b;\tilde{x}_{i})=\gamma_{i}(b;x_{i})\lambda^{\mathbbm{1}_{\text{CG}}(x_{i-1},x_{i})+\mathbbm{1}_{\text{CG}}(x_{i},x_{i+1})},

(18)

where
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
is a constant reflecting the relative bias against formation of CG pairs across codon boundaries
[
25
,
1
,
6
]
.

We can now state our second main result:

Theorem 2
.

There exist
T^{\star}=\mathcal{O}(r/n)
and sequences
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
and
\mathbf{y}^{\star}
, such that under the CpG model with rates (
18
):

\displaystyle\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi\mid\mid\mu)=\Theta(r).

The proof of Theorem
2
is given in Section
4.3
, with supporting details in Appendix
B
.

Chatterjee and
Diaconis 2018
provide conditions under which
N^{\star}(\epsilon)
must grow exponentially in
\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi\mid\mid\mu)
. Hence, their result together with Theorem
2
indicates that exponential growth in
r
is also
necessary
whenever
\log(\pi(\Pa)/\mu(\Pa))
concentrates around
\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi\mid\mid\mu)
under
\pi
. However, despite the exponential growth of the lower bound provided by Theorem
2
, we show in Section
4.4
that practical values of
N^{\star}(\epsilon)
may still be obtained even in this worst case.

4
Proofs of Main Results

In this section we provide bounds for the importance sampler which give rise to Theorems
1
and
2
. We begin by providing an overview of the proof strategy in Section
4.1
. The proofs of the upper and lower bounds are given in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
, respectively. Finally, in Section

we show how practical estimates can be obtained for a CpG model.

4.1
Approach

Theorems
1
and Theorem
2
are proved by establishing a mapping
between
\scrP
and
\SymS
, the symmetric group on
\mathcal{S}
the set of observed mutation sites. Specifically, let
\mathbf{s}(\Pa)=(s^{1},\ldots,s^{m(\Pa)})
be the set of sites mutated on the path
\Pa
and
\mathbf{b}(\Pa)=(b^{1},\ldots,b^{m(\Pa)})
the corresponding base changes, and for
\Pa\in\scrP
define

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbf{s}(\Pa),\mathbf{b}(\Pa)):=\prod^{m(\Pa)}_{j=1}\phi_{s^{j}}(b^{j};\tilde{x}^{j-1}_{s^{j}}).

(19)

Recall that at least
r=|\mathcal{S}|=\text{d}_{\text{H}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
jumps must occur among any path satisfying the endpoint constraint
\mathbf{x}(T)=\mathbf{y}
. Hence, for any path
\Pa^{r}
of length exactly
r
, we have

\mathbf{s}(\Pa^{r})\in\SymS
and

\displaystyle\Phi(\mathbf{s}(\Pa^{r}),\mathbf{b}(\Pa^{r}))=\Phi(\mathbf{s}(\Pa^{r}))=\prod^{r}_{j=1}\phi_{s^{j}}(y_{s^{j}};\tilde{x}^{j-1}_{s^{j}}).

(20)

In other words, paths of length
r
correspond to permutations of the order of the observed mutations, and
\Phi
can be viewed as a function defined over
\text{Sym}(\mathcal{S})
instead of
\scrP
. At times we will simply write
\Phi(\mathbf{s}^{r})
for
\mathbf{s}^{r}\in\SymS
. Normalizing (
20
) yields a probability measure on
\SymS
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}(s^{r}):=\frac{\Phi(\mathbf{s}^{r})}{\sum_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}^{r}\in\text{Sym}(\mathcal{S})}\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{s}}^{r})}=\frac{\Phi(\mathbf{s}^{r})}{Z^{r}_{\Phi}},

(21)

where
Z^{r}_{\Phi}\coloneqq\sum_{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}^{r}\in\text{Sym}(\mathcal{S})}\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{s}}^{r})
is the normalizing constant of
\tilde{\Phi}
.
Under the ISM (
\phi\equiv 1
) (
21
) is the uniform measure on
\SymS
, which we denote as
U_{\mathcal{S}}
. We will show that

\displaystyle L^{2}(\pi,\mu)\leq\exp\left(rTc+(n-r)T^{2}c^{\prime}\right)L^{2}(\tilde{\Phi},U_{\Sca})

(22)

for constants
c,c^{\prime}\in(0,\infty)
. Theorem
1
will follow as a special case of this result. The exponential term on the right-hand side of (
22
) can be interpreted as the increase in variability of the importance weights due to the
m(\Pa)-r

extra
jumps. Indeed, establishing (
22
) is non-trivial precisely because the number of extra jumps is unbounded. We address this by establishing a key bound on the MGF of
m(\Pa)
under
\mu
in Appendix
A
.

The connection between
L^{2}(\pi,\mu)
and
L^{2}(\tilde{\Phi},U_{\Sca})
is made by observing that
\scrP
can be partitioned into the following equivalence classes. Define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\scrP
such that
\Pa\sim\Pa^{\prime}
if the two paths agree on the order in which the
final
jumps among sites in
\mathcal{S}
occur.
Let
\scrP_{[i]}
denote the
i
th equivalence class and
\mathbf{s}^{r}_{i}\in\SymS
the permutation associated with
\scrP_{[i]}
. For example, in the case of length
r
paths we obtain

\displaystyle\scrP_{[i]}^{r}:=\scrP^{r}\cap\scrP_{[i]}=\{\Pa:m(\Pa)=r,(s^{1},\ldots,s^{r})=\mathbf{s}^{r}_{i},(b^{1},\ldots,b^{r})=(y_{s^{1}},\ldots,y_{s^{r}})\},

with paths in
\scrP^{r}\cap\scrP_{[i]}
differing only by jump
times
, with jump order given by
\mathbf{s}^{r}_{i}
. Then we can express an integral over the full path space as a summation of integrals over the
r!
equivalence classes:

\displaystyle\int_{\scrP}w^{2}(\Pa)\mu(d\Pa)

\displaystyle=\left[1+\frac{\sum_{m>r}\sum^{r!}_{i=1}\int_{\scrP_{[i]}^{m}}w^{2}(\Pa)\mu(d\Pa)}{\sum^{r!}_{i=1}\int_{\scrP_{[i]}^{r}}w^{2}(\Pa^{r})\mu(d\Pa^{r})}\right]\sum^{r!}_{i=1}\int_{\scrP_{[i]}^{r}}w^{2}(\Pa^{r})\mu(d\Pa^{r})

(23)

We show in Appendix
A.2
that under Assumption
1
there exist constants
0<c<c^{\prime}
such that with probability one under
\mu
:

\displaystyle c\cdot\Phi(\mathbf{s}(\Pa^{r}))\leq w(\Pa^{r})\leq c^{\prime}\cdot\Phi(\mathbf{s}(\Pa^{r}))

that is, the weights for any length
r
paths sharing the same order of the observed mutations lie within a constant factor.
In this sense,
the variance of
w(\Pa^{r})
across all length
r
paths is dominated by the variance of
\Phi(\mathbf{s}(\Pa^{r}))
across the
r!
possible orderings in which the
r
required jumps occur. The bracketed term in (
23
) can be viewed as the “inflation” in this variance due to the presence of extra jumps.
Controlling this inflation is critical and, as mentioned above, we do so by bounding the MGF of
m(\Pa)
under
\mu
.

We then give a specific choice of sequences
(\mathbf{x}^{\star},\mathbf{y}^{\star})
which, evolving under the CpG model, serves to establish a corresponding
lower bound
:

\displaystyle\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi\mid\mid\mu)=\Omega(\text{D}_{\text{KL}}(\tilde{\Phi}\mid\mid U_{\Sca})).

Applying Jensen’s inequality and combining these upper and lower bounds then yields
\log(L^{2}(\pi,\mu))=\Theta(\log(L^{2}(\tilde{\Phi},U_{\mathcal{S}})))
.

5
Conclusion

Calculation of marginal likelihoods under context-dependent evolutionary models is an important problem in phylogenetics and molecular evolution. Given the extensive machinery available for independent site models, the idea of performing inference under DSMs by importance sampling from ISMs is an attractive one. A potential drawback is the sample complexity of importance sampling, which often grows exponentially in the problem dimension.

Theorem
1
shows that as the length
n
of the sequences
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
grows, the number of samples needed for the importance sampler to approximate the marginal sequence likelihood does not grow exponentially in the dimension
n
of the space, but only in the number
r
of observed mutations. Theorem
2
however shows that in the worst case,
\mathcal{O}(e^{r})
samples are indeed
required
. Our analysis in Section
4
shows that this exponential growth can be explained by the variability of the context-dependent rates across the
orderings
of occurrence of these observed mutations when overlapping contexts occur. Section
4.4
shows that the bound can be significantly sharpened and practical estimates on
N^{\star}
may still be obtained for specific models (e.g. the CpG + JC69 model) using the techniques developed in Section
4.2
to prove Theorem
1
. However, the practicality of the importance sampler will critically depend on whether the sites in
\mathcal{S}
have overlapping contexts; if
all
observed mutations have non-overlapping contexts then
\mathcal{O}(\chi^{2}(\tilde{\Phi}\mid\mid U_{\mathcal{S}}))=\mathcal{O}(1)
, and the importance sampler provides a FPRAS for approximating the marginal sequence likelihood under Assumption
1
. Our results also suggest that identifying high probability orderings under
\tilde{\Phi}
may provide a way to to design better proposal distributions
for the importance sampler.

Finally, it is worth repeating that our results rely critically on the assumption that
T=\mathcal{O}(r/n)

and
r^{2}\leq n

(Assumption
1
). The scaling assumption on
T
was justified by the probability concentration tail bound on
T
established in
Mathews and Schmidler 2025
, although that result differs from Assumption
1
by an unsatisfying additional factor of
\log(n)
. It is less clear whether it is possible to relax, or how to do so, the assumption that
r^{2}\leq n
, as relaxing this assumption means that the
\exp(rTc+(n-r)T^{2}c^{\prime})
term that appears in our bounds is no longer of constant order.
