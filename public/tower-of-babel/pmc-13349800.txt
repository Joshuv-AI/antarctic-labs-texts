Serendipity on surveillance: an unusual transbronchial biopsy finding in a lung transplant recipient
Authors: Natasha Banga, Brandon Guenthart, Gerald Berry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flexible bronchoscopy is routinely utilized to monitor allograft function after lung transplantation (LT). While transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) is valuable as it permits direct histological assessment of the allograft, unexpected findings are occasionally encountered. We report a 72-year-old male with advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease who underwent a right single LT. His early posttransplant course was uneventful, and he presented for 6-week surveillance bronchoscopy. Although TBLB of the right lower lobe showed no evidence of rejection, one section revealed a small blood vessel with an organized intravascular thrombus. This prompted hospital admission, and an acute segmental pulmonary embolus was confirmed. Further workup identified acute deep vein thrombosis of the right soleal vein, likely the source of the embolism. The patient was treated with standard anticoagulation and discharged without complications. He was doing well at the 3-month follow-up. This case highlights the high incidence of venous thromboembolism among LT recipients and the unique challenges specific to this population. We emphasize the importance of following through on unexpected findings on TBLB, which may facilitate the timely diagnosis of posttransplant complications.

INTRODUCTION

Flexible bronchoscopy is routinely used to assess and monitor allograft function after lung transplantation (LT). Bronchoscopy provides a wealth of information about the allograft, including visual inspection of the native and transplanted airways and the bronchial or tracheal anastomosis, bronchoalveolar lavage to characterize the inflammatory milieu within the alveolar spaces, and evaluation for pulmonary infections. Transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) can also provide tissue for direct histological assessment. As part of allograft monitoring, most transplant programs perform surveillance TBLB primarily to screen for alloimmune processes, including acute rejection [1,2]. However, TBLB may also detect other causes of allograft dysfunction, such as lymphocytic or constrictive bronchiolitis, infection, drug-induced lung injury, posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorders, and distinct patterns of allograft injury (including eosinophilic pneumonia, diffuse alveolar damage, organizing pneumonia, and recurrent disease)—all of which have practical prognostic and therapeutic significance [3]. Beyond this, TBLB may occasionally reveal unexpected diagnoses, such as silent aspiration, that can help optimize outcomes in LT recipients. Here, we describe a patient who underwent surveillance TBLB in which an unusual finding led to the timely diagnosis of a significant complication.

CASE REPORT

The patient provided informed consent for the publication of this work. A 72-year-old male with advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and chronic respiratory failure underwent a right single LT. Significant comorbidities included a history of left pneumothorax treated with talc pleurodesis, prior stroke (no residual deficits), treated hepatitis C infection (normal liver function), and hypothyroidism. The patient was cytomegalovirus (CMV) seronegative, with a CMV-seropositive donor (high-risk CMV mismatch), and was managed with CMV-specific immunoglobulin infusions in addition to ganciclovir/valganciclovir per the transplant program protocol. His early posttransplant course was uneventful, and he was discharged on day 14. He underwent follow-up at the LT outpatient clinic and had been doing well when he presented for 6-week surveillance bronchoscopy in accordance with protocol. The procedure revealed a normal airway and a well-healed anastomosis. Although TBLB of the right lower lobe indicated no evidence of acute cellular rejection or lymphocytic bronchiolitis, one section showed a small blood vessel with an organized intravascular thrombus (Fig. 1). This prompted hospital admission, and computed tomography angiography (CTA) confirmed an acute pulmonary embolus in a segmental branch of the right lower lobe (Fig. 2). CTA displayed no signs of right ventricular pressure overload, and this was verified by transthoracic echocardiography. An evaluation for the source of pulmonary embolism (PE) revealed acute deep vein thrombosis (DVT) of the right soleal vein (Fig. 3). The patient was started on intravenous heparin and transitioned to a vitamin K antagonist. He returned to the outpatient clinic a week later, where he underwent a walking test in room air and exhibited stable to slowly improving lung function (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

Bronchoscopy with TBLB provides valuable information in patients with diffuse lung diseases [4]. Its diagnostic utility in immunocompromised patients is even more pronounced, and LT recipients represent a unique cohort in whom bronchoscopy is integral to the evaluation of clinical or radiological signs of allograft dysfunction. Although not universally adopted across LT programs, bronchoscopy with TBLB is also useful for active surveillance of clinically silent allograft rejection.

Our patient underwent bronchoscopy for surveillance purposes, which is performed at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months at Stanford. In contrast, patients do not undergo routine CTA screening of the chest in our program; rather, CTA is reserved for clinical indications. Although a minor decline (<10%) in lung function was incidentally noted during surveillance, it did not meet the threshold for clinically indicated bronchoscopy. The organized thrombus identified in a pulmonary vessel was an incidental histologic finding. However, the ensuing workup prompted by the biopsy results led to the diagnosis of PE in a single-LT recipient. The patient was promptly treated with standard anticoagulation and was doing well clinically at the 3-month follow-up after the PE diagnosis.

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is common among LT patients, with an incidence exceeding 40% during the first posttransplant year [5] and a lifetime risk above 60% [6]. Moreover, VTE in this population appears to be associated with worse 3-year survival. The small proportion of VTE patients who develop PE also face a higher risk of pulmonary infarction due to a lack of bronchial artery circulation [7]. Patients with any clinical suspicion of VTE must be promptly evaluated and aggressively managed.

The finding of an organized thrombus on biopsy is not pathognomonic for PE, as histology cannot distinguish an in situ thrombus from an embolus. In fact, TBLB has no role in screening for PE [8], underscoring the serendipitous nature of the finding in the present case. CTA findings consistent with a small PE, along with a new DVT in the right soleal vein, confirmed an embolic etiology. Accordingly, this case highlights the importance of a thorough evaluation of unexpected findings on TBLB. The potential impact of timely diagnosis and treatment of VTE on outcomes among LT recipients cannot be overemphasized.

The gold standard for diagnosing PE remains CTA of the chest, which should be the preferred technique when PE is suspected [8]. CTA has excellent sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of PE [9] and is equivalent or superior to lung scintigraphy [10]. Notably, CTA would have diagnosed PE in this patient regardless of the TBLB findings. However, the CTA examination that revealed the small PE likely would not have been ordered, and the PE would have gone undetected, in the absence of clinical symptoms and with relatively stable lung function. The clot burden of the PE was not high, as differential perfusion favoring the healthier allograft was maintained on a Tc-99m nuclear scan. Nevertheless, perfusion to the right lower zone was markedly reduced at 5.5% (with 28.6% to the right upper zone and 45.7% to the mid-lung zone). Furthermore, the patient was at risk of recurrent PE given the lower extremity DVT, and the biopsy finding prompted timely initiation of appropriate therapy before more extensive embolism or serious complications such as pulmonary infarction developed.

In conclusion, surveillance TBLB can occasionally reveal unexpected findings, which should be assessed promptly with appropriate workup. Timely recognition and management of complications during the early posttransplant period may facilitate improved outcomes in this patient population.
