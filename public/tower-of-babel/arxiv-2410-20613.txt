A quick guide to ordinary state-dependent delay differential equations Thanks: supported by the state of Saxony via a graduate student stipend
Bernhard Aigner and Marcus Waurick

Abstract
. We review H 1 H^{1} -well-posedness for initial value problems of ordinary differential equations with state-dependent right-hand side. We streamline known approaches to infer existence and uniqueness of solutions for small times given a Lipschitz-continuous prehistory. The paramount feature is a reduction of the differential equation to a fixed point problem that admits a unique solution appealing to the contraction mapping principle. The use of exponentially weighted Sobolev spaces in this endeavor proves to be as powerful as for ordinary differential equations without delay. Our result includes a blow-up criterium for global existence of solutions. The discussion of well-posedness is concluded by new results covering continuous dependence on initial prehistories and on the right-hand sides.



1.
Introduction

In this article we recapitulate recent results on delay differential equations, in particular from our articles
[
7
]
and
[
2
]
. One of the main aims of the present article is to streamline the theory and present a largely self-contained account of the selected results. We include some previously unpublished theorems regarding continuous dependence on data. We start with a brief explication of the problem:

We want to investigate so-called ordinary state-dependent
delay differential equations (DDEs)
. As for ordinary differential equations (without delay) we will try to solve initial value problems (IVPs) of the following form

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} v^{\prime}(t)&=G(t,v_{t})&&\text{for }\;\;\;\;\,0<t\leq T\\
v&=\Phi&&\text{for }-h\leq t\leq 0\,\text{,}\end{aligned}

where we use the usual convention

\begin{split}v_{t}\colon\,[t-h,t]&\to H\\
s&\mapsto v(t+s)\,\text{.}\end{split}

Differential equations as in
Equation

1
are called
ordinary
since the lhs is simply the time derivative (and no derivative shows up on the rhs either); a DDE because the dependence contained in the rhs
G
is upon prior instances of the state variable. We investigate the very general case in which the dependence is upon an entire prior history of the state, so-called
state-dependent delay
.

In this article we aim to solve
Equation

1
in exponentially weighted Sobolev spaces. For this purpose we introduce the notation

\left(L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H),\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2,\rho}\right)\coloneq\left(L_{2}(-h,T;H),\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\right)

and accordingly

\displaystyle H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T;H)

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{u\in L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)\colon\,u^{\prime}\in L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)\right\}

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T;H)}

\displaystyle\coloneq\left(\lVert u\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}+\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,\text{,}

where the time derivative is defined in the usual distributional sense. For an introduction to Sobolev spaces we refer to
[
6
, Ch. 5]
. We would like to point out that in this one-dimensial (in time) setting, the following result assures us that
H^{1}_{\rho}
-functions admit a continuous representative:

Theorem 1.1

(Sobolev-embedding)
.

Let
-\infty<a<b<\infty
. Then
H^{1}(a,b;H)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}([a,b];H)
. More precisely, every
f\in H^{1}(a,b;H)
admits an absolutely continuous representative and

\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{C}([a,b];H)}\leq\left((b-a)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+(b-a)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)\lVert f\rVert_{H^{1}(a,b;H)}

This result can be found in any standard reference, e.g.
[
1
, Thm. 4.12]
. For a proof of the estimate we refer to
[
3
, Thm. 4.9]
. In particular, the theorem implies that pointwise evaluation of
H_{\rho}^{1}
-functions is allowed. This will enable us to make use of the fundamental theorem of calculus later on.

Lastly we clarify what we mean by a solution to
Equation

1
. We refer to the following notion:

Definition 1.2
.

A function
v\in H^{1}(-h,T;H)
is called a local solution to
Equation

1
if there exists
0<T_{0}\leq T
such that for all
0<T_{1}<T_{0}
the restriction
v|_{[-h,T_{1}]}\in H^{1}(-h,T_{1};H)
and

\displaystyle v^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=G(t,v_{t})

for

\displaystyle{}\;\;\;\;\,0<t\leq T_{1}

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\Phi

for

\displaystyle-h\leq t\leq 0\,\text{.}

If
T_{0}=T
we call
u
a global solution.

We will state and prove our well-posedness results in section three, prior to that a preliminary section is necessary though. In section four, we will discuss several applications, followed by a brief discussion and contextualization of our findings in the concluding section.

2.
Preliminaries

Before we can present our main results we need to devote some space to discuss important aspects of our approach. In particular we need to investigate the behaviour of the delay, understand the importance of the time derivative with boundary condition and estimate the evaluation functional.

2.1.
The delay

In our aim to better understand DDEs, we particularly need to understand the behaviour of the history map, that is for a given
0\leq t\leq T
the function

\begin{split}\Theta_{t}\colon\,H_{\rho}^{1}(-h,T;H)&\rightarrow H^{1}(-h,0;H)\\
u&\mapsto u_{t}\end{split}\,\text{.}

However, it proves more useful to include the dependence on
u
into the map and investigate

\begin{split}\Theta\colon H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T;H)&\rightarrow L_{2,\rho}\left(0,T;H^{1}(-h,0;H)\right)\\
u&\mapsto(t\mapsto\Theta_{t}u)\end{split}\,\text{.}

Lemma 2.1
.

\Theta
is Lipschitz-continuous with
\lVert\Theta\rVert\leq(2\rho)^{\nicefrac{{-1}}{{2}}}
.

Proof.

We first study
\Theta
as a map from
L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)
to
L_{2,\rho}(0,T;L_{2}(-h,0;H))
. For
u\in L_{2,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
we can estimate:

\displaystyle\lVert\Theta u\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;L_{2}(-h,0;H))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\Theta_{t}u\rVert_{L_{2}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{-h}^{0}\lVert u(s+t)\rVert_{H}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{t-h}^{t}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle=\int_{-h}^{T}\int_{s}^{s+h}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle=\int_{-h}^{T}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\tfrac{1}{2\rho}(\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}-\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho(s+h)})\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\rho}\int_{-h}^{T}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\lVert u\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}

Now we pass from the
L_{2}
-case to the
H^{1}
-case using the fact, that for
u\in H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T;H)
the weak derivative and
\Theta_{t}
commute. We can estimate:

\displaystyle\lVert\Theta u\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}(0,T;L_{2}(-h,0;H))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\Theta_{t}u\rVert_{L_{2}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}+\lVert\Theta_{t}u^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle=\lVert\Theta u\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;L_{2}(-h,0;H))}^{2}+\lVert\Theta u^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;L_{2}(-h,0;H))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\lVert u\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}\qed

We point out the dependence on
\rho
in the estimate. The use of exponentially weighted Sobolev spaces will allow us to increase the weights
\rho
if necessary, resulting in a Lipschitz-constant of
\Theta
as small as we desire. This is purely a technical advantage though, since on finite intervals, the spaces
L_{2,\rho}
and
H^{1}_{\rho}
are isomorphic to
L_{2}
and
H^{1}
respectively.

Remark 2.2

(Continuity with respect to time)
.

We can also investigate the dependence of the delay for some given fixed
u\in H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
with respect to time, i.e. the map
[0,T]\to H^{1}(-h,0;H),\,t\mapsto\Theta_{t}u
. We see from

\lVert u_{t}-u_{s}\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}=\int_{-h}^{0}\lVert u(t+r)-u(s+r)\rVert_{H}^{2}+\lVert u^{\prime}(t+r)-u^{\prime}(s+r)\rVert_{H}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{}r

that
u_{s}\to u_{t}
in
H^{1}(-h,0;H)
for
s\to t
. Hence the map
t\mapsto\Theta_{t}u
is continuous.

2.2.
The time derivative and its inverse

Another advantage of utilizing the spaces
H^{1}_{\rho}
is the fact, that we can define a realisation of the time derivative that proves to be boundedly invertible – at least on a suitable subspace. To that end we define for
-\infty<a<b<+\infty
the operator

\begin{split}I_{\rho}\colon\,L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)&\to L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)\\
u&\mapsto\left(t\mapsto\int_{a}^{t}u(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\right)\,\text{.}\end{split}

Then we can show:

Proposition 2.3
.

I_{\rho}
is a bounded linear operator satisfying
\lVert I_{\rho}\rVert\leq\frac{1}{\rho}
.

Proof.

For
u\in L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)
we can calculate:

\displaystyle\lVert I_{\rho}u\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\bigg\lVert\int_{a}^{t}u(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\bigg\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{a}^{b}\left(\int_{a}^{t}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\right)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{a}^{b}\left(\int_{a}^{t}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{H}\mathrm{e}^{-\nicefrac{{\rho s}}{{2}}}\mathrm{e}^{\rho(\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}-t)}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{a}^{b}\left(\int_{a}^{t}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\right)\left(\int_{a}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{\rho(s-2t)}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\right)\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{t}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\,\mathrm{e}^{-\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{a}^{b}\int_{s}^{b}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho s}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\int_{a}^{b}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho s}\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\lVert u\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)}^{2}\qed

Remark 2.4

(range of
I_{\rho}
and time derivative)
.

The images of
I_{\rho}
are weakly differentiable, since the antiderivative (of an
L_{2}
-function on a bounded interval) is differentiable with the function as derivative, cf.
[
5
, Thm. 6.3.6]
. As such, the images of
I_{\rho}
are
H^{1}_{\rho}
-functions, that can be evaluated pointwise by
Theorem

1.1
. Furthermore, the value at zero is zero. Therefore
I_{\rho}
maps into

H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)\coloneq\{u\in H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)\colon\,u|_{[-h,0]}=0\}

with operator norm
\lVert I_{\rho}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}\to H^{1}}\leq(1+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho^{2}}})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, since for
u\in L_{2,\rho}(a,b;H)
:

\lVert I_{\rho}u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}=\lVert I_{\rho}u\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}}^{2}+\lVert(I_{\rho}u)^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}}^{2}\leq\left(\lVert I_{\rho}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2,\rho}\to L_{2,\rho}}+1\right)\lVert u\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}}^{2}

On the subspace with zero initial condition,
H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(a,b;H\right)
, the time derivative now is just
\partial_{t}=\mathring{\partial_{t}}\coloneq I_{\rho}^{-1}
.

In fact one can show surjectivity as well:

Theorem 2.5
.

H^{1}_{\rho}\left(a,b;H\right)\supseteq\operatorname{dom}\mathring{\partial_{t}}=H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(a,b;H\right)
.

Proof.

That
\operatorname{dom}\mathring{\partial_{t}}\subseteq H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(a,b;H\right)
follows immediately from the definition. We only need to show that
I_{\rho}
is onto to prove the reverse inclusion. For a given
u\in H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(a,b;H\right)
we can simply appeal to the fundamental theorem of calculus, cf.
[
5
, Cor. 6.3.7]
, which is applicable, because
Theorem

1.1
guarantees absolute continuity of
u
. We obtain:

u(t)-u(a)=\int_{a}^{t}u^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s

Since
u(a)=0
, this simply reads:
u=I_{\rho}u^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime}\in L_{2,\rho}\left(a,b;H\right)
because
u\in H^{1}_{\rho}\left(a,b;H\right)
.
∎

This result will allow us to easily transform the problem (
1
) into a fixed point problem by simply applying the inverse time derivative to the differential equation.

2.3.
The evaluation operator

In our examples we will encounter the evaluation operator

\begin{split}\operatorname{ev}\colon\,H^{1}_{\rho}(0,T;H)\times[0,T]&\rightarrow H\\
(u,t)&\mapsto u(t)\,\text{.}\end{split}

We can observe the following immediate result:

Lemma 2.6
.

\operatorname{ev}
is Hölder-continuous with exponent
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
with respect to the second argument, i.e. for given
u\in H^{1}\left(0,T;H\right)
and
s,t\in[0,T]
we can estimate:

\lVert u(s)-u(t)\rVert_{H}\leq\lvert s-t\rvert^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}(0,T;H)}

Proof.

For
u\in H^{1}\left(0,T;H\right)
and
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
we can estimate:

\displaystyle\lVert u(s)-u(t)\rVert_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg\lVert\int_{s}^{t}u^{\prime}(r)\,\mathrm{d}{}r\bigg\rVert_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{s}^{t}\lVert u^{\prime}(r)\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}r\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lvert t-s\rvert\int_{s}^{t}\lVert u^{\prime}(r)\rVert_{H}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{}r

\displaystyle\leq\lvert t-s\rvert\int_{0}^{T}\lVert u^{\prime}(r)\rVert_{H}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{}r

\displaystyle=\lvert t-s\rvert\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2}(0,T;H)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lvert t-s\rvert\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}(0,T;H)}^{2}\qed

For
u\in H^{1}_{\rho}\left(0,T;H\right)
, due to equivalence of norms, a constant appears on the rhs. For later purposes, ideally we want Lipschitz-continuity though. To this end – and for a subsequent projection argument – we introduce the following subspace of
H^{1}(-h,0;H)
for some given
\alpha>0
:

V_{\alpha}\coloneq\{\varphi\in H^{1}(-h,0;H)\colon\,\lVert u^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha\}\,\text{,}

where for
f\in L_{2}(a,b;H)
we set
\lVert f\rVert_{\infty}\coloneq\operatorname{ess}\sup_{t\in(-h,0)}\lVert f(t)\rVert_{H}
.

Remark 2.7

(
V_{\alpha}
is a closed convex subspace of
H^{1}(-h,0;H)
)
.

Convexity is obvious. For closedness consider
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq V_{\alpha}
converging to some
u\in H^{1}(-h,0;H)
. In particular
\{u^{\prime}_{n}\}_{n}
converges to
u^{\prime}
in
L_{2}(-h,0;H)
. Appealing to the Fischer-Riesz theorem, a subsequence
\{u^{\prime}_{n_{k}}\}_{k}
converges pointwise almost everywhere to the limit
u^{\prime}
. Therefore
\operatorname{ess}\operatorname{sup}\lvert u^{\prime}\rvert\leq\alpha
.

Remark 2.8

(
\operatorname{ev}
is Lipschitz in time on
V_{\alpha}
)
.

We can trivially estimate the evaluation mapping as a map

\displaystyle\operatorname{ev}\colon\,V_{\alpha}\times[0,T]

\displaystyle\rightarrow H

\displaystyle(u,t)

\displaystyle\mapsto u(t)

by computing for
s\leq t
:

\lVert u(s)-u(t)\rVert_{H}=\bigg\lVert\int_{s}^{t}u^{\prime}(r)\,\mathrm{d}{}r\bigg\rVert_{H}\leq\int_{s}^{t}\lVert u^{\prime}(r)\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}r\leq\lvert t-s\rvert\alpha\,\text{,}

hence verifying Lipschitz-continuity with respect to time.

3.
Main results

The main theorem of our explication is a generalized version of the Picard–Lindelöf theorem. The conventional Picard–Lindelöf theorem for ODEs in Hilbert spaces (cf.
[
14
, Thm. 4.2.3]
) is not applicable in our scenario, because the evaluation mapping is not Lipschitz-continuous in general. Restricting to the subspaces
V_{\alpha}
, by
Remark

2.8
we can recover Lipschitz-continuity. We adjust our definition of Lipschitz-continuity for the rhs as follows:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
U\subseteq H^{1}(-h,0;H)
be open. We call
G\colon\,[0,T]\times U\to H
almost uniformly Lipschitz-continuous if for all
\alpha>0
there exists some
L_{\alpha}>0
such that

\forall t\in[0,T]\,\forall\varphi,\psi\in V_{\alpha}\colon\,\lVert G(t,\varphi)-G(t,\psi)\rVert_{H}\leq L\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}

The strategy of the proof for our Picard–Lindelöf theorem is to adapt the classical proof by Morgenstern (cf.
[
12
]
) utilizing exponentially weighted spaces to this generalized notion of Lipschitz-continuity. As a first step, we transform the problem (
1
) into a fixed point problem. For that purpose we make the ansatz
u=v+\hat{\Phi}
, where

\hat{\Phi}\colon\,[-h,T]\to H\qquad t\mapsto\begin{cases}\Phi(0)&\text{for}\;\;\;\;\,0<t\leq T\\
\Phi(t)&\text{for}-h\leq t\leq 0\end{cases}

and
v\in H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
. It is clear that
\hat{\Phi}\in H^{1}(-h,T;H)
, since it is absolutely continuous with weak derivatives in
L_{2}
on both sides of
0
. We can then transform

(
1
)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} u^{\prime}(t)&=G(t,u_{t})&&\text{for }\;\;\;\;\,0<t\leq T\\
u&=\Phi&&\text{for }-h\leq t\leq 0\end{aligned}

for
u\in H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T;H)
into the
fixed point problem (FPP)

(2)

v=I_{\rho}G\bigl(\cdot,(v+\hat{\Phi})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)

for
v\in H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
. We prove equivalence of the problems:

Lemma 3.2
.

The IVP (
1
) and the FPP (
2
) are equivalent.

Proof.

We quickly verify both implications:

•

If
u\in H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
is a solution of
Equation

1
, then we can define
v\coloneq u-\hat{\Phi}
, which is in
H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
, since it is weakly differentiable and
v|_{[-h,0]}=0
. Integration of the differential equation then proves the claim.

•

If
v\in H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
satisfies
Equation

2
, let

u\colon\,[-h,T]\to H,\qquad t\mapsto\begin{cases}v(t)+\Phi(0)&\text{for}\;\;\;\;\,0<t\leq T\\
\Phi(t)&\text{for}-h\leq t\leq 0\end{cases}\text{.}

u
satisfies the initial condition and because
v(0)=0
,
v\in\operatorname{dom}\mathring{\partial_{t}}
by
Theorem

2.5
, which after differentiation for almost all
t>0
gives:

u^{\prime}(t)=(v+\Phi(0))^{\prime}(t)=v^{\prime}(t)=G(t,(v+\hat{\Phi})_{t})=G(t,u_{t})\qed

To make use of the subspaces
V_{\alpha}
we introduce the metric projection on
V_{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\pi_{\alpha}\colon\,H^{1}(-h,0;H)

\displaystyle\to V_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{cases}\varphi&if $\varphi\in V_{\alpha}$\\
\operatorname{argmin}\{\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}\colon\,\psi\in V_{\alpha}\}&if $\varphi\notin V_{\alpha}$\end{cases}

Remark 3.3

(properties of
\pi_{\alpha}
)
.

We remind the reader that the best-approximation of a closed and convex subset (which
V_{\alpha}
is by
Remark

2.7
) is unique in Hilbert spaces, cf.
[
18
, Thm. V.3.2]
. The associated projection is in fact a Lipschitz-continuous map with Lipschitz-constant
1
.

The projection allows us to pose a fixed point problem on
V_{\alpha}
. We define:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha}\colon\,H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)

\displaystyle\to H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\mapsto I_{\rho}G\left(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((v+\hat{\Phi})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\right)

Remark 3.4

(
\Gamma_{\alpha}
is well defined)
.

We disperse all concerns in order:

•

First note that
\hat{\Phi}\in H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
, since the derivative of
\hat{\Phi}
exists almost everywhere because of absolute continuity and is in
L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)
, hence the weak derivative is as well.

•

We know that
G
is continuous and we can write the map
t\mapsto G\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((v+\hat{\Phi})_{t}\bigr)\bigr)
as
t\mapsto G\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}\bigl(\Theta_{t}(v+\hat{\Phi})\bigr)\bigr)
, which is continuous as composition of continuous maps, note
Remark

2.2
and
Theorem

1.1
. Therefore the integral is well-defined.

•

\Gamma_{\alpha}u\in H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
, since integrals over continuous functions on finite intervals are differentiable. The zero boundary term is clear by definition.

The modified FPP is then to find a unique
v\in H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
such that

(3)

v=\Gamma_{\alpha}v

We can prove that it is equivalent to the modified IVP

(4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} u^{\prime}(t)&=G\left(t,\pi_{\alpha}\left((u)_{t}\right)\right)&&\quad\text{for }&&&t>0\,\text{,}\\
u(t)&=\Phi(t)&&\quad\text{for }&&&-h\leq t\leq 0\,\text{.}\end{aligned}

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
\Phi\in V_{\alpha}
. Let
U\subseteq H^{1}(-h,0;H)
be an open neighbourhood of
\Phi
. Let
G\colon\,[0,T]\times U\to H
be continuous. Then the following are equivalent:

i)

Equation

4
admits a unique solution.

ii)

Equation

3
admits a unique solution.

In either case, the solutions coincide.
1
1

1

Using a Grönwall argument, one can even show, that it is exponentially bounded.

Proof.

•

Let
u
be a unique local solution of
Equation

4
. We integrate the differential
eq.

4
and obtain:

u(t)-u(0)=\int_{0}^{t}G\bigl(s,\pi_{\alpha}(u_{s})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}{}s

We substitute
v\coloneq u-\hat{\Phi}
and immediately see that
v\in H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
and plugging
v
into the previous equation yields for
t>0
:

v(t)=\int_{0}^{t}G\bigl(s,\pi_{\alpha}((v+\hat{\Phi})_{s})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}{}s

Hence we obtain a fixed point of
Equation

3
. If the fixed point is not unique then because
v
is weakly differentiable (continuously differentiable for
t>0
, weakly for
t<0
and continuous), out of two fixed points
v
and
w
we obtain two functions
u_{v}\coloneq v-\hat{\Phi}
and
u_{w}\coloneq w-\hat{\Phi}
and consequently we are able to identify two different solutions to
Equation

4
.

•

The converse follows by applying the same arguments in reverse direction.

That the solutions agree is apparent from our deduction.
∎

Utilizing the projection, we obtain at least local existence and uniqueness:

Theorem 3.6

(Picard–Lindelöf for DDEs – local version)
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
\Phi\in V_{\alpha}
. Let
U\subseteq H^{1}(-h,0;H)
be an open neighbourhood of
\Phi
. Let
G\colon\,[0,T]\times U\to H
be almost uniformly Lipschitz-continuous. Then the IVP (
1
) admits a unique local solution.

Proof.

We show that the IVP (
4
) admits a global solution first. Appealing to the previous lemma, for this we need to show that the FPP (
3
) has a unique solution for large enough
\rho
. We do this by invoking the contraction mapping principle. We have already shown in
Remark

3.4
that
\Gamma_{\alpha}
is a self-mapping, we only need to verify that it is a contraction. We estimate for
u,v\in H^{1}_{0,\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
:

\displaystyle\big\lVert\Gamma_{\alpha}v-\Gamma_{\alpha}w\big\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)}=\big\lVert I_{\rho}\bigl[G\bigl(.,\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((v-\hat{\Phi})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\bigr)-G\bigl(.,\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((w-\hat{\Phi})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\bigr)\bigr]\big\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rho}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\lVert I_{\rho}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}\rightarrow H_{\rho}^{1}}^{2}\big\lVert G\bigl(.,\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((v-\hat{\Phi})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\bigr)-G\bigl(.,\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((w-\hat{\Phi})_{(\cdot)}\bigr)\bigr)\big\rVert^{2}_{L_{2,\rho}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\right)\int_{0}^{T}\big\lVert G\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((v-\hat{\Phi})_{t}\bigr)\bigr)-G\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}\bigl((w-\hat{\Phi})_{t}\bigr)\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\right)\int_{0}^{T}L_{\alpha}^{2}\big\lVert\pi_{\alpha}((v-\hat{\Phi})_{t})-\pi_{\alpha}((w-\hat{\Phi})_{t})\big\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\right)L_{\alpha}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert(v-\hat{\Phi})_{t}-(w-\hat{\Phi})_{t}\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\right)L_{\alpha}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\lVert v_{t}-w_{t}\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\right)L_{\alpha}^{2}\big\lVert\Theta v-\Theta w\big\rVert^{2}_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H^{1}(-h,0;H))}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\right)L_{\alpha}^{2}\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\lVert v-w\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)}^{2}

For
\rho
large enough this makes
\Gamma_{\alpha}
a contraction. Hence we obtain a fixed point of
Equation

3
and therefore a solution of
Equation

4
by
Lemma

3.5
. Since a solution of
Equation

4
can be differentiated weakly and on
[0,T]
that derivative is continuous by virtue of it satisfying
Equation

4
, we infer that up to some positive time
T_{0}\leq T
the solution satisfies
\lVert u^{\prime}|_{[-h,T_{0}]}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\alpha
. Consequently the metric projection is simply the identity up to
T_{0}
. This proves local existence, uniqueness is assured by uniqueness of the fixed point.
∎

As for the case of ODEs without delay we can state a global version of the theorem:

Theorem 3.7

(Picard–Lindelöf for DDEs – global version)
.

The unique local solution
v
of
Equation

1
from
Theorem

3.6
can be extended to some maximal
T_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\underset{t\uparrow T_{0}}{\operatorname{lim}}\lVert v^{\prime}|_{(-h,t)}\rVert_{\infty}=\infty
or already
T_{0}=T
.

Proof.

We follow the usual strategy: By fixing some initial
\alpha_{1}>\lVert\Phi^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}
we can appeal to
Theorem

3.6
to obtain a solution
x_{1}
up some time
t^{\star}_{1}\leq T
. Then we can increase to
\alpha_{2}\coloneq 2\alpha_{1}
and obtain a solution
x_{2}
up to some time
t^{\star}_{1}\leq t^{\star}_{2}\leq T
. Note that
x_{2}|_{[-h,t^{\star}_{1}]}=x_{1}
. Indeed, since
t^{\star}_{1}
was such that the unique solution of
Equation

1
stays in
V_{\alpha_{1}}
and since
x_{2}
solves
Equation

1
locally as well,
\pi_{\alpha_{1}}\left(x_{2}|_{[-h,t^{\star}_{1}]}\right)=x_{2}|_{[-h,t^{\star}_{1}]}
and by unique solvability
x_{2}|_{[-h,t^{\star}_{1}]}=x_{1}
. We can iterate this process with
\alpha_{n}\coloneq 2^{n-1}\alpha_{1}
and obtain
T_{0}\coloneq\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{n}^{\star}
as monotone limit of positive numbers and a unique solution
x
defined as
x(t)\coloneq x_{n}(t)
for
t\in[-h,t_{n}^{\star}]
, which is well-defined by the preceeding argument.

We move on to the blow-up part. We assume that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lVert x^{\prime}|_{[-h,t_{n}^{\star}]}\rVert_{\infty}\eqcolon M<\infty
. In this case we obtain a maximal solution
x
as above and need to show that
x\in H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
. We estimate:

\lVert x^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}=\int_{-h}^{T}\lVert x^{\prime}(t)\rVert^{2}_{H}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\leq M^{2}\int_{-h}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\leq\tfrac{M^{2}}{2\rho}\mathrm{e}^{2\rho h}

and

\displaystyle\lVert x\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}=\int_{-h}^{T}\lVert x(t)\rVert^{2}_{H}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{-h}^{0}\lVert x(t)\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t+\int_{0}^{T}\lVert x(t)\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{-h}^{0}\lVert x(t)\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t+\int_{0}^{T}\bigg\lVert x(0)+\int_{0}^{t}x^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\bigg\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\lVert\Phi\rVert_{L_{2}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\rho h}+2\lVert x(0)\rVert_{H}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t+2\int_{0}^{T}\bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{t}x^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\bigg\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\lVert\Phi\rVert_{L_{2}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\rho h}+\tfrac{\lVert x(0)\rVert_{H}^{2}}{\rho}\mathrm{e}^{2\rho h}+2\int_{0}^{T}\lVert(I_{\rho}x^{\prime})(t)\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(\lVert\Phi\rVert_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\lVert x(0)\rVert_{H}^{2}}{\rho}\right)\mathrm{e}^{2\rho h}+2\lVert I_{\rho}x^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left(\lVert\Phi\rVert_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\lVert x(0)\rVert_{H}^{2}}{\rho}\right)\mathrm{e}^{2\rho h}+\tfrac{2}{\rho^{2}}\lVert x^{\prime}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;H)}^{2}

All terms in the last line are finite.

Conversely, if
T_{0}<T
and
x\notin H^{1}(-h,T;H)
we can verify
\lim_{n\to\infty}\lVert x^{\prime}|_{[-h,t_{n}^{\star}]}\rVert_{\infty}=\infty
. This is but trivial, because by definition we chose
t_{n}^{\star}
maximal such that
x^{\prime}|_{[-h,t_{n}^{\star}]}\in V_{\alpha_{n}}
and therefore if the limit does not terminate in
T
, the desired limit needs to be
+\infty
.
∎

To finish the discussion of well-posedness we elaborate that the solutions depend continuously on initial data and the right-hand side:

Theorem 3.8

(continuous dependence on initial datum)
.

Let
\alpha>0
. Then there exists
C>0
such that for any given
\Phi,\Psi\in V_{\alpha}
the corresponding solutions
u
and
v
of the IVP
Equation

1
to initial data
\Phi
and
\Psi
respectively exist up to some common time
T_{0}\leq T
. For any
T_{1}<T_{0}
the restrictions satisfy
u|_{[-h,T_{1}]},v|_{[-h,T_{1}]}\in H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T_{1};H)
and the estimate

\lVert u-v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T_{1};H)}\leq C\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}\,\text{.}

Proof.

The first part of the statement is simply an application of
Theorem

3.7
. To obtain the estimate we recall that being a solution to
Equation

1
is equivalent to
u-\hat{\Phi}
and
v-\hat{\Psi}
being solutions to the FPP (
2
). We estimate thusly:

\lVert u-v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T_{1};H\right)}^{2}=\lVert\hat{\Phi}-\hat{\Psi}\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T_{1};H\right)}+\lVert(u-\hat{\Phi})-(v-\hat{\Psi})\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(0,T_{1};H\right)}^{2}

The first term can be estimated as follows:

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\Phi}-\hat{\Psi}\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T_{1};H\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,0;H)}+\lVert\Phi(0)-\Psi(0)\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T_{1};H)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,0;H)}+\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,0;H)}+C\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,0;H)}\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\bigl(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho T_{1}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\bigl(1+\tfrac{C}{2\rho}\bigr)\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,0;H)}

For the second term we estimate

\displaystyle\lVert(u-\hat{\Phi})-(v-\hat{\Psi})\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(0,T_{1};H\right)}^{2}=\big\lVert I_{\rho}\bigl[G\bigl(\cdot,\Theta_{(\cdot)}u\bigr)-G\bigl(\cdot,\Theta_{(\cdot)}v\bigr)\bigr]\big\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(0,T_{1};H\right)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\lVert I_{\rho}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}\to H^{1}_{\rho}}^{2}\big\lVert G\bigl(\cdot,\Theta_{(\cdot)}u\bigr)-G\bigl(\cdot,\Theta_{(\cdot)}v\bigr)\big\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}\left(0,T_{1};H\right)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\bigr)\big\lVert L_{\alpha}\lVert u_{(\cdot)}-v_{(\cdot)}\rVert_{H^{1}\left(-h,0;H\right)}\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}\left(0,T_{1};H\right)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\bigr)L_{\alpha}^{2}\lVert\Theta u-\Theta v\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}\left(0,T_{1};H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,0;H\right)\right)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\tfrac{L_{\alpha}^{2}\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\right)}{2\rho}\lVert u-v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T_{1};H\right)}^{2}

Note that we can make the initial calculations since by truncating the functions to
T_{1}
they remain in
V_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
. Putting these two estimates together, demanding that
\rho
be large enough and rearranging provides us with the inequality

\lVert u-v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T_{1};H\right)}\leq\tilde{C}\lVert\Phi-\Psi\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,0;H\right)}\,\text{.}

Finally, appealing to the equivalence of the weighted and unweighted norms produces the desired result for all
\rho
.
∎

The following theorem on dependence on the rhs is in the spirit of
[
10
, Thm. 3.6 and Rem. 3.7]
.

Theorem 3.9

(continuous dependence on the rhs)
.

Let
\alpha>0
. Then there exists
C>0
such that for any
\Phi\in H^{1}(-h,0;H)
, any open neighbourhood
U
of
\Phi
and almost uniformly Lipschitz-continuous functions
F,G\colon[0,T]\times U\to H
there exist solutions
u
and
v
to the IVP (
1
) to rhs
F
and
G
respectively up to some common time
T_{0}\leq T
. For any
T_{1}<T_{0}
the restrictions satisfy
u|_{[-h,T_{1}]},v|_{[-h,T_{1}]}\in H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T;H\right)
and the estimate

\lVert u-v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T_{1};H)}\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T_{1}]}\lVert F(t,u_{t})-G(t,u_{t})\rVert_{H}\,\text{.}

Proof.

The first part of the statement is clear: We simply apply
Theorem

3.7
to show that the restrictions are in
H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T_{1};H\right)
. To obtain the estimate we recall that being a solution to
Equation

1
is equivalent to
u-\hat{\Phi}
and
v-\hat{\Psi}
being solutions of the fixed point
eq.

2
. We can therefore estimate:

\displaystyle\lVert u-v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T_{1};H\right)}^{2}=\big\lVert I_{\rho}\bigl[F\bigl(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}(u_{(\cdot)})\bigr)-G\bigl(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}(v_{(\cdot)})\bigr)\bigr]\big\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(0,T_{1};H\right)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\bigr)\big\lVert F\bigl(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}(u_{(\cdot)})\bigr)-G\bigl(\cdot,\pi_{\alpha}(v_{(\cdot)})\bigr)\big\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}\left(0,T_{1};H\right)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\bigr)\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\big\lVert F\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}(u_{t})\bigr)-G\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}(v_{t})\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\bigr)\Bigl[\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\big\lVert F\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}(u_{t})\bigr)-G\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}(u_{t})\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\Bigr.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\Bigl.L_{G}^{2}\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\lVert\pi_{\alpha}(u_{t})-\pi_{\alpha}(v_{t})\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\Bigr]

We can estimate the second term in the usual fashion:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\lVert\pi_{\alpha}(u_{t})-\pi_{\alpha}(v_{t})\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\lVert u_{t}-v_{t}\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t

\displaystyle=\lVert\Theta u-\Theta v\rVert_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T_{1};H^{1}(-h,0;H))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\lVert u-v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}

The first term we can estimate using the fact that
t\mapsto u_{t}
is a continuous function by
Remark

2.2
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\big\lVert F\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}(u_{t})\bigr)-G\bigl(t,\pi_{\alpha}(u_{t})\bigr)\big\rVert_{H}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\rho t}\,\mathrm{d}{}t\leq\tfrac{1}{2\rho}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{1}]}\big\lVert F(t,u_{t})-G(t,u_{t})\big\rVert_{H}^{2}

Hence:

\displaystyle\lVert u-v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T_{1};H\right)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{1}]}\lVert F(t,u_{t})-G(t,u_{t})\rVert_{H}^{2}+C_{2}L_{G}^{2}\lVert u-v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}(-h,T;H)}^{2}

We now can make
\rho
large enough to force
C_{2}<1
. Rearranging the inequality yields:

\lVert u-v\rVert_{H^{1}_{\rho}\left(-h,T_{1};H\right)}\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T_{1}]}\lVert F(t,u_{t})-G(t,u_{t})\rVert_{H}\qed

4.
Applications

We outline three applications of our selected results. An additional example warranting its own article is dicussed in
[
2
]
.

4.1.
Constant delay

A trivial case, that is nevertheless worth mentioning is the case of constant delay. Problems of the form

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=G(t,x(t-t_{1}),\dots,x(t-t_{n}))

for

\displaystyle 0<t\leq T\,\text{,}

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=\Phi

for

\displaystyle-h\leq t\leq 0\>\,\text{,}

where
0\leq t_{1}\leq\dots\leq t_{n}\leq h
have a unique solution provided that
G\colon[0,T]\times U^{n}\to H
is locally Lipschitz-continuous with respect to the
H^{n}
-variable and that the prehistory satisfies
\lVert\Phi^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}<\infty
according to
Theorem

3.7
. We simply observe that the rhs can be written as
G(t,r(u_{t}))
, where

r\colon\,H^{1}(-h,0;H)\to H^{n}\qquad\varphi\mapsto\left(\varphi(-t_{1}),\dots,\varphi(-t_{n})\right)^{T}\text{.}

Since the evaluation functional is Lipschitz-continuous on any
V_{\alpha}
according to
Remark

2.8
the composition
G(\cdot,r(u_{(\cdot)}))
is almost uniformly Lipschitz-continuous.

4.2.
An academic example

We consider a delay of the form
\lvert x(t)\rvert
in the simplest possible IVP:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=x(t-\lvert x(t)\rvert)

\displaystyle\text{for}\qquad\quad\,t>0

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=\Phi(t)

\displaystyle\text{for}-h\leq t\leq 0

The importance of this example lies in the fact, that it admits non-unique solutions for continuous initial prehistories, cf.
[
7
, Ex. 4.3]
. This fact proves to be problematic in solution theory of DDEs, for instance in the semigroup approach to DDEs. It highlights, that the problem lies with the initial prehistories, not the equation itself. We verify, that the system satisfies the requirements of our well-posedness result
Theorem

3.6
. If we wish, we can write the equation using the delay functional
\tau\colon\,\varphi\mapsto\lvert\varphi(0)\rvert
.
\tau
is a Lipschitz-continuous functional, since for
\varphi,\psi\in H^{1}(-h,0;H)
we can estimate:

\displaystyle\lvert\tau(\varphi)-\tau(\psi)\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert\lvert\varphi(0)\rvert-\lvert\psi(0)\rvert\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\varphi(0)-\psi(0)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}

where the second inequality is simply the second triangle inequality. Writing the rhs of the differential equation as a function of histories, we obtain
F(\varphi)\coloneq\varphi(-\tau(\varphi))
. We can now verify that this function is almost uniformly Lipschitz-continuous:

For
\varphi,\psi\in V_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
without loss of generality
\tau(\varphi)\geq\tau(\psi)
and for
t>0
we can estimate

\displaystyle\lVert\varphi(-\tau(\varphi))-\psi(-\tau(\psi))\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigg\lVert\varphi(-h)+\int_{-h}^{-\tau(\varphi)}\varphi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s-\psi(-h)-\int_{-h}^{-\tau(\psi)}\psi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\bigg\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\lVert\varphi(-h)-\psi(-h)\rVert+\int_{-h}^{-\tau(\psi)}\lVert\varphi^{\prime}(s)-\psi^{\prime}(s)\rVert\,\mathrm{d}{}s+\int_{-\tau(\psi)}^{-\tau(\varphi)}\lVert\varphi^{\prime}(s)\rVert\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{\infty}+\sqrt{h}\left(\int_{-h}^{0}\lVert\varphi^{\prime}(s)-\psi^{\prime}(s)\rVert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{}s\right)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\alpha\lvert\tau(\varphi)-\tau(\psi)\rvert

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}\bigl(C+\sqrt{h}+\alpha C\bigr)

Appealing to
Theorem

3.6
we obtain at least a unique local solution for given initial prehistories
\Phi
with bounded derivative.

Our approach is in fact applicable for much more general cases of nonconstant delay of the form

x^{\prime}(t)=g\bigl(t,x(t+r_{1}(x_{t})),\dots,x(t+r_{m}(x_{t}))\bigr)\,\text{,}

where the
r_{i}\colon\,H^{1}(-h,0;H)\to[-h,0]
are assumed to be Lipschitz-continuous and
g\colon\,[0,T]\times H^{n}\to H
is supposed to satisfy the following notion of Lipschitz-continuity: Suppose there exists
L\geq 0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
and
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
,
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in H
we have

\lVert g(t,u_{1},\dots,u_{n})-g(t,v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\rVert_{H}\leq L\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lVert u_{j}-v_{j}\rVert_{H}\text{.}

Then the associated IVP for an initial prehistory
\Phi\in H^{1}(-h,0;H)
with bounded derivative is well-posed. A proof of this statement can be found in
[
7
, Thm. 5.2]
, where it is shown for
H=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, but the proof can be generalized effortlessly.

4.3.
Integro-differential equations

Integro-differential equations with state- dependent delay are an important subclass and appear in many applications; for instance in models of wheel shimmy (cf.
[
16
,
15
]
) and car following models (cf.
[
13
]
). They can appear in many forms, however in the spirit of the articles
[
11
]
and
[
9
]
, the type of equation we are interested here is of the form:

(5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}(t)&=F(t,x_{t})+G\Bigl(t,x_{t},\int_{0}^{h}g(t,x_{t}(-s))k(t-s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr)&&\text{for}\;\;\;\;\,0<t\leq T\\
x(t)&=\Phi(t)&&\text{for}-h\leq t\leq 0\,\text{,}\end{aligned}

where

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle\colon\,[0,T]\times H^{1}(-h,0;H)\to H\,\text{,}

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle\colon\,[0,T]\times H^{1}(-h,0;H)\times H\to H\,\text{,}

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\colon\,[0,T]\times H\to H\,\text{,}

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\colon\,[-h,T]\to H\,\text{.}

For the function
G
we will assume the following Lipschitz-continuity property (G): There exists
L\geq 0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
and for all
\varphi,\psi\in H^{1}(-h,0;H)
and all
\alpha,\beta\in H
we can estimate:

\lVert G(t,\varphi,\alpha)-G(t,\psi,\beta)\rVert_{H}\leq L\left(\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}+\lVert\alpha-\beta\rVert_{H}\right)

We can show:

Theorem 4.1
.

For
\Phi\in H^{1}(-h,0;H)
with bounded derivative,
F
almost uniformly Lipschitz-continuous,
G
satsifying property (G),
g
Lipschitz-continuous uniformly with respect to the first variable and a kernel
k\in L^{1}(-h,T;H)
, we have a unique local solution
u\in H^{1}
of
Equation

5
that can be extended to some maximal
T_{0}\leq T
such that
T_{0}=T
or
\lim_{t\uparrow T_{0}}\lVert u^{\prime}|_{(-h,t)}\rVert_{\infty}=+\infty
.

Proof.

Appealing to
Theorem

3.7
, we only need to verify the almost uniform Lipschitz-continuity of the rhs written as a function of
t
and
x_{t}
. It suffices to consider the function

\tilde{G}(t,\varphi)\coloneq G\Bigl(t,\varphi,\int_{0}^{h}g(t,\varphi(-s))k(t-s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr)\,\text{.}

For
\varphi,\psi\in V_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
we can estimate:

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{G}(t,\varphi)-\tilde{G}(t,\psi)\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\quad=\bigg\lVert G\Bigl(t,\varphi,\int_{0}^{h}g(t,\varphi(-s))k(t-s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr)-G\Bigl(t,\psi,\int_{0}^{h}g(t,\psi(-s))k(t-s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\Bigr)\bigg\rVert

\displaystyle\quad\leq L_{G}\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}+L_{G}\bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{h}\bigl(g(t,\varphi(-s))-g(t,\psi(-s))\bigr)k(t-s)\,\mathrm{d}{}s\bigg\rVert_{H}

\displaystyle\quad\leq L_{G}\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}+L_{G}\int_{0}^{h}\big\lVert g(t,\varphi(-s))-g(t,\psi(-s))\big\rVert_{H}\big\lVert k(t-s)\big\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq L_{G}\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}+L_{G}L_{g}\int_{0}^{h}\lVert\varphi(-s)-\psi(-s)\rVert_{H}\lVert k(t-s)\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq L_{G}\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}+L_{G}L_{g}\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{\infty}\int_{0}^{h}\lVert k(t-s)\rVert_{H}\,\mathrm{d}{}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq L_{G}\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}+L_{G}L_{g}C\lVert\varphi-\psi\rVert_{H^{1}(-h,0;H)}\lVert k\rVert_{L^{1}(-h,T;H)}

The last line follows from the Sobolev embedding
theorem

1.1
.
∎

5.
Conclusion

The approach to state-dependent DDEs presented in this article provides an alternative to the existing classical theory for delay differential equations. We used standard tools from the repertoire of the conventional weak approach to differential equations, all our theorems appear very traditional in style.

Established classical well-posedness results appeal to
\mathcal{C}
- and
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-functions, compactness of orbits, and the like. For instance Hale (cf.
[
8
]
) shows that the IVP

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}(t)&=f(t,x_{t})&\qquad\text{for}&&t>0\\
x(t)&=\Phi(t)&\qquad\text{for}&&-h\leq t\leq 0\end{aligned}

where
f\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{C}([-h,0];\mathbb{R}^{n})\supseteq D\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a given continuous function and
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}([-h,0];\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a given initial prehistory, has a solution if
D
is open and
(-h,\Phi)\in D
. Uniqueness is shown if in addition
f
is Lipschitz-continuous with respect to the second argument on every compact subset of
D
and the solution depends continuously on initial datum.

Nominally, Hale’s classical results appear stronger, since the requirement for existence of solutions is mere continuity. We point out the important difference, that Hale’s result is based upon Schauder’s fixed point theorem, which does not guarantee uniqueness, contrary to the contraction mapping principle utilized in this article. The contraction mapping principle is also superior in the sense that it naturally provides continuous dependence on data and allows for easy numerics. Hale’s result additionally comes with the severe restriction of being set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The uniqueness assumption is fairly close to the one stated here, but the setting in our case is more approachable, as we do not require statements about compact subsets. Furthermore, on the space
V_{\alpha}
we investigate, Hale’s Lipschitz-condition implies ours.

Another classical well-posedness approach utilizes the concept of the solution manifold (cf.
[
17
]
), which is a concept that was originally designed to study linearized stability of delay differential equations, but that can also be used to prove existence and uniqueness of solutions. In that context systems of the form

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}(t)&=f(x_{t})&\qquad\text{for}&&t>0\\
x(t)&=\Phi(t)&\qquad\text{for}&&-h\leq t\leq 0\end{aligned}

are studied, where
f\colon\,\mathcal{C}^{1}([-h,0])\supseteq U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some open
U
is supposed to satisfy the additional assumptions

•

Each
Df(\phi)
for
\phi\in U
extends to a linear map
D_{e}f(\phi)
on
\mathcal{C}([-h,0])
which is continuous.

•

For every
\phi\in U
there exist an open neighbourhood
V\subseteq U
and
L\geq 0
such that for all
\psi,\chi\in V
the following estimate holds:

\lvert f(\psi)-f(\chi)\rvert\leq L\lVert\psi-\chi\rVert_{\mathcal{C}^{1}([-h,0])}

In this setting, the first condition implies that the set

\mathcal{X}\coloneq\{\phi\in U\colon\,\phi^{\prime}(0)=f(\phi)\}

is an
n
-dimensional
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-submanifold – provided that it is nonempty in the first place. The second condition implies that the tangent space of
\mathcal{X}
is nice and introduces a continuous semiflow for every prehistory
\phi\in\mathcal{X}
, i.e. the solutions of the problem.

Comparatively in this scenario our findings are a strict improvement in the context of solution theory. Since the assumptions in the solution manifold approach are strictly stronger though, a benefit of this article is that we prove that the solution manifold is non-empty, giving us access to this tool to study stability if we wish to do so. A more in-depth comparison of these two approaches in the case of a DDE from cell biology is contained in
[
2
]
.

A different approach to state-dependent DDEs is the use of semigroups (cf.
[
4
]
and the references therein). The semigroup approach comes with the disadvantage of having to introduce an extended state space, at least if the underlying Banach space is the space of continuous functions. Our approach makes due without. We point out again the importance of the regularity of the prehistories as discussed in the example in
Section

4.2
– mere continuity does not suffice. The presented setting of
H^{1}
-prehistories leads to a simple well-posedness theory though. For comparisons to other approaches we also refer to the concluding discussion in
[
7
]
.

We would like to close by stressing that the key idea behind the presented approach is the use of exponentially weighted Sobolev spaces that grants access to a (boundedly) invertible time derivative. This idea is a foundation of the so-called theory of evolutionary equations that allows the inversion of operators of the form
\partial_{t}M(\partial_{t})+A
, where
M
is a so-called material law and
A
is a (time-independent) skew-adjoint operator, on a suitable
L_{2}
space (Picard’s theorem, cf.
[
14
, Thm. 6.2.1]
). Utilizing this theory, it is possible to extend the ideas presented here to the theory to PDEs with state-dependent delay. This will be the topic of future research.
