Medical emergencies at teaching dental clinics in Saudi Arabia: a systematic review
Authors: Motaz Assas, Bashayr Alshawi, Ameerah Aladwani, Abdullah Hazzazi, Abdullah Alutaibi, Lujain Homeida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Medical emergencies (MEs) are sudden and severe health issues that pose an immediate threat to patients’ lives or well-being if not addressed expeditiously. Annually, between 50 and 70% of dental professionals experience at least one medical emergency, with over a quarter encountering several emergencies each year. Therefore, it is essential to be familiar with the frequency and types of medical emergencies that may occur in dental offices so that dental professionals can be prepared to deal with life-threatening situations. This systematic review aims to report the prevalence and incidence of medical emergencies at teaching dental clinics in Saudi Arabia. Additionally, it aims to highlight assessments of practitioners' knowledge regarding these emergencies and report on the most encountered MEs. Furthermore, the study highlights recommendations from previous research on effective prevention and management strategies for MEs in dental teaching hospitals. The included articles were obtained from three different databases: EBSCO, SCOPUS, and PubMed. Eight studies were included in our analysis based on the eligibility criteria, and their quality assessment was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. The findings show a wide range of emergency reporting among the included articles, ranging from 49.4% to 0.2%. The dental students' knowledge in managing MEs was generally poor to below-average, except for one study that reported above-average performance. Postgraduate students showed better performance. Two studies reported a lack of confidence and practical competence in managing medical emergencies. This review demonstrates that although foundational awareness exists, readiness is often lacking, especially among students and junior practitioners. A standardized, competency-based training framework across Saudi dental institutions is imperative to bridge this gap and ensure patient safety. Further studies are recommended to evaluate the prevalence of medical emergencies and the level of knowledge across dental institutions in Saudi Arabia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44445-025-00096-x.

Introduction

Medical emergencies (MEs) are sudden and severe health issues that can pose an immediate threat to patients’ lives or well-being if not addressed in a timely manner (Malamed 2022). MEs can arise in any healthcare setting, including dental practices. Such incidents can take place before, during, or after treatment. Annually, between 50 and 70% of dental professionals experience at least one medical emergency, with over a quarter encountering several emergencies each year (Šink et al. 2019).

Acute MEs can be triggered and worsened by systemic diseases, such as hypertension, diabetes, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), during dental procedures (Sorenson et al. 2021). Moreover, the incidence of medical crises is thought to be increasing due to the aging of the population, exacerbated by possible geriatric systemic diseases. Also, more medications are being given to patients, along with their potential adverse effects. All these factors contribute to an increase in medical crises (Smereka et al. 2019).

Studies have indicated that vasovagal syncope, seizures, acute angina, and hypoglycemia are the most frequent medical crises in clinical dental setting (Sin et al. 2023). A recent study indicates that the most common emergency encountered was syncope, representing 66% of all reported emergencies, followed by hypoglycemia and foreign bodies, respectively (Osayande, 2020). Another cross-sectional study done in 2013 revealed that less than half of the dentists included in the study (237 out of 548; 43.6%) had encountered a medical emergency during their careers, 34.3% reported cases of vasovagal syncope, 16.1% reported seizures, and 8.4% reported instances of hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia (Marks et al. 2013). According to a survey-based study in France and Belgium, at least one medical emergency occurred over the past 12 months of practice, as reported by more than half of those who responded (53.2%, n = 715). Furthermore, only 39 significant medical crises (severe allergic reaction, inhalation of foreign substances, stroke, angina, and myocardial infarction) and five cardiac arrests were identified among those emergency cases, with vasovagal syncope being the most frequent emergency in dentistry practices (1257 events, 43.90%) (Laurent et al. 2014). This indicates that there are 0.004 heart arrests for every dentist annually. Every year, there are 2.1 MEs of any type per dentist. Therefore, it is essential to be familiar with the frequency and types of MEs that may occur in dental offices so that dental professionals can be better prepared to deal with these possible life-threatening situations (Obata et al. 2021).

Knowledge involves the theoretical understanding and factual recall of Basic Life Support (BLS) principles, emergency protocols, and the recognition of signs and symptoms indicative of medical crises (Oxford Center for Evidence-Based Medicine 2021). Attitude includes the perceptions, beliefs, and readiness to respond to MEs, often reflecting an individual's confidence and sense of responsibility (Price 2015). Competence signifies the practical ability and skill needed for the effective performance of BLS procedures and the management of MEs, which encompasses the appropriate use of emergency equipment and medications (HealthStream 2021). Practice refers to the actual implementation of BLS skills and emergency management protocols in clinical scenarios (StudySmarter, n.d.).

Several studies have been done in Saudi Arabia of this nature, which focus on the topic of MEs occurring in dental schools among dental staff and students (Al-Johani et al. 2022; Al-Sebaei 2024; Gazal et al. 2021), this might be due to the level of training and type of procedures students undergo, compared with their level of expertise, exposes dental students to the risk of malmanagement when encountering MEs. However, no studies have systematically reviewed and critically analyzed the prevalence and incidence of MEs studies at dental schools in Saudi Arabia to highlight this critical and potentially life-threatening event, nor have studies been able to draw constructive conclusions or recommendations to manage these possible occurrences.

This systematic review aims to report the prevalence and incidence of MEs at teaching dental clinics in Saudi Arabia. Additionally, it aims to highlight assessments of practitioners' knowledge regarding these emergencies and report on the most encountered MEs. Furthermore, the study highlights recommendations from previous research on effective prevention and management strategies for MEs in dental teaching hospitals.

Material and method

This systematic review evaluated the incidence, prevalence, and knowledge of MEs among dental practitioners and students in dental teaching hospitals in Saudi Arabia. The protocol of this systematic review was created based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols 2020 (Page et al. 2021).

Search strategy

The articles included were obtained from three different databases: EBSCO, SCOPUS, and PubMed. The research of the articles was done on 2/22/2025. Two independent reviewers (A.A. and A.S) conducted the research, and two moderators were supervising the process (A.H.) to ensure both consistency and accuracy throughout the process. The search was conducted using keywords that include (dent*) AND (emergenc*) AND (prevalence) OR (incidence) OR (know*) AND (Saudi*). The search strategy was guided by PRISMA 2020 (Page et al. 2021).

Selection of the studies

After conducting the electronic search using the selected databases, each title and abstract in the identified studies was examined by the author of the review. This search was subsequently reviewed by a different assessor who thoroughly examined the entire search strategy with a comparable number of articles as previously noted. Criteria of data extraction

The data extraction from the articles included several points of information to ensure a comprehensive analysis of the studies. This involved documenting the author’s name and year of publication. The study design was noted to determine the methodology used—the location or institution where the study was conducted. Additionally, the aim of the study was included. The sample size was extracted to indicate the total number of participants. The gender information was noted, along with the mean age of participants. The prevalence and incidence rates were identified. The method of incidence reporting was included, and the instrument used to assess knowledge was detailed to indicate the tools used to evaluate participants' BLS knowledge. This data provided a comprehensive overview of the studies incorporated in the research.

Newcastle–ottawa quality assessment

Articles included were assessed for their quality using the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment (NOS). The NOS is a tool for evaluating the quality of non-randomized studies, including cohort and case–control designs. It assesses studies across three main domains: the selection of study groups, their comparability, and the determination of exposure or outcome. A star system (up to 9 stars) is used to rate study quality, helping reviewers interpret the reliability of findings in systematic reviews (Wells et al. 2000).

Research significance

MEs in dental settings are becoming more prevalent, especially with the increasing age and/or medical condition of the patients. This review aims to provide an in-depth evaluation of the incidence and prevalence of MEs in dental settings, along with the level of preparedness of dental students and practitioners to identify and manage medical crises.

Results

Following the electronic search, 2419 articles were identified. Of the total, there were 10 duplicates excluded. Following this, 2409 articles were independently reviewed by title. Of these, 2394 records were excluded as being irrelevant. Fifteen articles were then screened by an abstract for eligibility. Ultimately, eight studies were included in our analysis.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

Knowledge, attitude, and competence

All included articles evaluating knowledge, attitude, and competence in MEs occurring in dental teaching hospitals used an administered questionnaire. Most of the studies aimed to evaluate dental students’ and professionals’ knowledge, awareness, and attitudes related to BLS and the management of medical emergencies. According to Hashem Motahir Al Shamiri et al./2017 and Saquib et al./2019, knowledge regarding BLS among dental students and interns ranged from poor to below average. On the other hand, one study (Elnaem et al. 2024) reported that most dental students had an above-average performance. According to Gopinathan, Pillai Arun et al./2023, dental students' overall understanding of cardiac emergencies was poor to moderate, while postgraduate students showed better performance. Regarding confidence in handling and dealing with medical emergencies, two studies (Al-Johani et al. 2022; Abdulrahman et al. 2022) reported a lack of confidence and practical competence in managing MEs as detailed in Table 4. Medical emergencies reported

Two studies report the incidence and the prevalence of MEs in dental teaching hospitals among Saudi Arabian dental students and professionals. The data reveals a wide range of emergency reporting and experience. In the study by Abdulrahman, Baraa Issam, et al./2022, 132 incidences were recorded using a cross-sectional survey, indicating a 49.4% prevalence for medical crises. This suggests that over 50% of the participants experienced an emergency of some kind throughout their practice. In contrast, the prevalence reported by Al-Sebaei in 2024 was significantly lower at 0.2%, according to a retrospective review of event records that identified 300 cases, most of which involved syncopal episodes and hypoglycemia. Most common medical emergencies

The most frequent MEs reported in the two studies. Both Abdulrahman et al. and Al-Sebaei et al. found vasovagal syncope and hypoglycemia to be the most frequent emergencies, with rates of 32.6% and 62.3% for syncope, and 29.2% and 12% for hypoglycemia, respectively. However, the studies differed in the types and frequencies of other reported emergencies. In Abdulrahman et al. (2022), 49% of dentists reported managing emergencies independently, while only 8.2% were able to manage them in clinics without emergency kits. Fewer dentists required assistance from another physician. In contrast, Al-Sebaei (2024) found that most patients 87% recovered within the dental clinics. Discussion

This systematic review comprehensively explores the prevalence and incidence of MEs in dental teaching hospitals across Saudi Arabia, while also evaluating the knowledge and preparedness of dental practitioners, including students, interns, and dentists, in managing such situations. A cross-comparison of the included studies reveals critical themes such as knowledge gaps, insufficient preparedness, and the critical need for improved training. Additionally, the review identifies the most encountered MEs and provides recommendations for an effective strategy for preventing and managing MEs. These themes were intentionally examined as part of the review objectives.

The incidence of MEs appears low, based on a retrospective study that documented an incidence rate of 17.4 per 100,000 patients over 14 years at King Abdulaziz University Dental Hospital. Only 13% of the cases required referral to the emergency department, reflecting a low but non-negligible burden that dental practitioners must be equipped to manage MEs (Al-Sebaei 2024). Conversely, the incidence of MEs was high in a survey-based study (132 cases), demonstrating a prevalence of 49.4% for a medical crisis (Abdulrahman et al. 2022). This difference can be influenced by the method used to record incidence: cross-sectional studies tend to show higher rates of self-reported incidence, while retrospective reporting reflects documented events, which might be affected by under-reporting or inadequate reporting processes. Among the studies included in this review, both Al-Sebaei et al. and Abdulrahman et al. consistently identified vasovagal syncope and hypoglycemia as the most prevalent MEs encountered in Saudi dental teaching settings. In a 14-year retrospective analysis at King Abdulaziz University Dental Hospital, Al-Sebaei et al. documented syncope in 62.3% and hypoglycemia in 12% of 300 emergency cases, often affecting ASA I patients before or during the administration of local anesthesia (Al-Sebaei 2024). Similarly, Abdulrahman et al. reported syncope in 32.6% and hypoglycemia in 29.2% of events in a cross-sectional survey involving six university hospitals (Abdulrahman et al. 2022). These findings emphasize the importance of stress reduction protocols and routine glucose monitoring, especially given Saudi Arabia's high prevalence of diabetes mellitus (International Diabetes Federation 2021). Other important but less frequent emergencies were also reported in both studies. Hyperventilation occurred in 4.6% of cases in the Al-Sebaei (Al-Sebaei 2024). Typically associated with procedural anxiety, hyperventilation can mimic more severe conditions like cardiac events or epinephrine toxicity, making an accurate diagnosis essential. Acute asthmatic attacks were identified in 4% and 2.6% of cases, respectively (Abdulrahman et al. 2022; Al-Sebaei 2024). Asthma-related emergencies are particularly concerning due to the risk of rapid airway compromise, and their management requires immediate availability of bronchodilators and oxygen. While the overall prevalence was low, their potential severity demands readiness among dental teams (AAPD 2024). Heart-related problems, though uncommon, were reported in 3.0% of Abdulrahman's sample (Abdulrahman et al. 2022). These included chest pain and suspected angina, which may be early signs of myocardial infarction. Al-Sebaei et al. documented six cases of chest pain and one suspected cardiovascular accident, all of which were transferred to the emergency department for further evaluation (Al-Sebaei 2024). Given the potentially life-threatening nature of cardiac events, prompt recognition and referral are critical, particularly in older or systemically compromised patients. Foreign body aspiration, while rare (2.2%), presents an acute airway emergency that requires immediate intervention. In most dental institutions, particularly teaching hospitals, such incidents emphasize the importance of rubber dam use, high-speed suction, and upright patient positioning, especially during prosthodontic or surgical procedures (Abdulrahman et al. 2022). Epinephrine reactions occurred in 3.3% of the cases in Al-Sebaei et al.'s study and typically followed intravascular injection of local anesthesia containing epinephrine. Symptoms included tachycardia, hypertension, and restlessness. While often self-limiting, such reactions require close monitoring and clear differentiation from other emergencies like anxiety or hypertensive crisis (Abdulrahman et al. 2022; Al-Sebaei 2024). International comparisons reveal similar trends. Studies from Japan, Australia, and the United Kingdom also identify vasovagal syncope as the leading emergency, followed by hypoglycemia, seizures, and asthma attacks (Obata et al. 2021; Sin et al. 2023; Smereka et al. 2019). A large-scale survey in Germany reported that over half of dentists encountered up to three MEs per year, with vasovagal syncope accounting for the vast majority (1,238 annual cases) and cardiac arrest being extremely rare (two cases across all participants) (Müller et al. 2008). In Saudi Arabia, the younger, healthier patient cohort seen in teaching clinics may explain the lower prevalence of systemic complications but underscores the need to address stress and anxiety-related events. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that although vasovagal syncope and hypoglycemia are the most frequent, dental practitioners must also be prepared to manage a broader spectrum of emergencies, including respiratory, cardiac, and pharmacological events. Regular simulation training, well-stocked emergency kits, and structured protocols remain essential to mitigate these risks and improve clinical outcomes.

When examining the knowledge and awareness among dental personnel, there was a consistent pattern of moderate to low preparedness in Al-Shamiri et al. 2017 and Gopinathan et al. 2023. Another study evaluated senior dental students and their clinical trainers. at Taibah University and found a notable lack of knowledge in managing emergencies; only 30–54% of participants correctly responded to management scenarios involving conditions like benzodiazepine overdose and adrenal crisis (Gazal et al. 2021). These findings highlight a need for more targeted training among advanced students and staff. According to a recent study conducted in India, the level of knowledge and awareness of MEs among dental students is moderate; however, it reveals significant gaps. While a majority of students demonstrated awareness of certain emergencies, such as anaphylaxis (91%) and vital signs, there was limited awareness concerning other critical conditions, including angina, transient ischemia, and lidocaine toxicity, with only 27–37% recognizing these conditions. Moreover, only approximately 15–40% of students possessed knowledge regarding the management of emergencies like cardiac arrest, angina, hyperventilation, and respiratory obstruction (Mohideen et al. 2021).

Similarly, a recent study reported that although 85.3% of students took a thorough medical history and 58.5% were familiar with the national ambulance number, most lacked confidence in handling MEs, and 79% expressed discomfort managing real emergencies, emphasizing a disconnect between theoretical knowledge and clinical readiness (Al-Johani et al. 2022). Two survey-based studies found that awareness of basic life support (BLS) was inadequate; they concluded that postgraduate students exhibited superior knowledge and practice scores compared to undergraduates, underlining the value of accumulated clinical exposure (Al-Shamiri et al. 2017; Gopinathan et al. 2023). According to a study conducted at the University of Ha’il, 78.6% of students were familiar with BLS but varied widely in performing emergency actions despite a sound theoretical foundation. Inconsistencies in actual emergency responses call for periodic simulation-based training from an infrastructure and equipment perspective (Elnaem et al. 2024). Regarding the availability and use of emergency drugs and confidence in managing medical crises, it was found that 72.7% of clinics had emergency kits, and only 28.8% of respondents were confident in using emergency drugs, such as adrenaline (Abdulrahman et al. 2022). The gap between availability and practical competence remains consistent, which highlights the shortcomings of current training methods in translating theoretical knowledge into practical application.

The findings in our systematic review manuscript align with the existing literature; most included studies addressed similar concerns over knowledge and confidence levels. In our review, dental students and staff expressed a general understanding of MEs but showed significant variability in competency, especially in high-risk scenarios (Abdulrahman et al. 2022; Al-Johani et al. 2022). This highlights that BLS certification alone is insufficient without ongoing practical reinforcement (Al-Shamiri et al. 2017). Dental schools should encourage periodic refresher courses, readiness assessments, and team-based emergency scenarios.

In evaluating the validity and quality of the questionnaires used across the included studies, variability was observed in the instruments used for validation procedures. Only two studies demonstrated a strong methodological approach for testing the questionnaire. Gopinathan et al. used a group of experts to assess the pilot questionnaire through content validity and reliability (Gopinathan et al. 2023). Saquib et al. also piloted their tool and conducted face and content validation by expert review, although no reliability statistics were reported (Saquib et al. 2019). In contrast, Al-Shamiri et al. and Gazal et al. mentioned conducting a pilot study using a self-administered questionnaire but did not report construct validity or reliability testing (Al-Shamiri et al. 2017; Gazal et al. 2021).

On the other hand, several studies lacked instrument validation. Abdulrahman et al., Al-Johani et al., and Elnaem et al. used self-administered questionnaires without reported pilot testing or statistical validation (Abdulrahman et al. 2022; Al-Johani et al. 2022; Elnaem et al. 2024). This inconsistency in questionnaire design potentially affects the findings. Studies depending on self-developed tools without validation may lead to measurement bias and affect the accuracy of reported knowledge or preparedness levels. This gap highlights an important area for improving the quality of questionnaires by focusing on the use of validated questionnaires to ensure accurate and reproducible results.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations that must be acknowledged. Firstly, the number of studies focusing specifically on dental teaching clinics in Saudi Arabia was limited, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to all institutions across the country. Secondly, there was considerable heterogeneity in study designs, ranging from retrospective reviews to cross-sectional surveys, and the tools used to assess knowledge and preparedness varied widely. This variation made it difficult to perform direct comparisons or synthesize data quantitatively. Additionally, many included studies were dependent on self-reported questionnaires, which are susceptible to recall and social desirability biases, potentially overestimating the actual level of knowledge or preparedness. Also, no evidence was provided as to the validity, reliability, and responsiveness quality of the questionnaires used. Lastly, the absence of longitudinal studies prevents assessing whether interventions such as training programs sustainably impact preparedness over time.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while MEs in dental clinics may be infrequent, their potentially life-threatening nature warrants exceptional preparedness. This review demonstrates that although foundational awareness exists, readiness is often lacking, especially among students and junior practitioners. A standardized, competency-based training framework across Saudi dental institutions is imperative to bridge this gap and ensure patient safety. Further research is recommended to assess the prevalence of MEs and the level of knowledge across dental institutions in Saudi Arabia.
