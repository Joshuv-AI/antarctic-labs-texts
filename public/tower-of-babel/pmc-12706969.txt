Genome-wide identification, evolutionary and expression analyses of the DUF538 gene family in maize
Authors: Anqiang Jia, Yawen Yang, Ting Wang, Chao Xia, Lujiang Li, Shizhou Yu, Hai Lan, Linggai Cao, Hailan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The DUF538 (Domain of Unknown Function 538) gene family plays critical roles in plant growth, development, and stress responses, yet its phylogenetic distribution, sequence evolution, and expression characteristics remain poorly understood. Here, we identified 196 DUF538 genes across 13 plants, all of which were specific to land plants. Phylogenetic analysis revealed four distinct subgroups within this family, with subgroups A and B likely originating from ancestral whole-genome duplication (WGD) events in angiosperms. The ancestral subgroups (C/D) exhibited distinct sequence features, biophysical properties, and selective pressures compared to subgroups A/B, whereas A/B underwent gene structure simplification, sequence divergence, and relaxation of selective constraints. In maize, co-expression networks analysis linked DUF538 genes to root development (e.g., flavonoid biosynthesis and kinase signaling) and leaf stress responses (e.g., hydrolase activity and defense signaling). Promoter and expression analyses revealed subgroup-specific enrichment of cis-elements, which correlated with differential expression patterns during development and under abiotic stress. Notably, under CaCl₂ stress, paralogs within subgroups were activated sequentially, sustaining a prolonged response while minimizing functional redundancy through temporal differentiation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DUF538 genes have achieved functional diversification through sequence divergence, relaxed selection, and spatiotemporal expression regulation, thereby providing potential targets for enhancing crop stress resilience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07741-x.

Introduction

The evolution of plant gene families is shaped by gene duplication mechanisms, specifically whole-genome duplication (WGD), segmental duplications, and tandem duplications [1–3]. These events lead to increased gene copy numbers and create genetic substrates for potential functional diversification [4–6]. Following duplication, relaxed selection allows the accumulation of mutations, while divergent selective pressures subsequently promote functional divergence [7, 8], often through subfunctionalization (partitioning of ancestral functions) or neofunctionalization (acquisition of new functions) [9, 10]. Such gene duplication and natural selection serve as key drivers of adaptive radiation in plants, enabling evolutionary innovation in response to environmental challenges [6, 11]. Nevertheless, the functional and evolutionary trajectories of many duplicated genes, particularly those within large families of domains of unknown function (DUF), remain poorly understood.

DUFs represent a significant proportion of uncharacterized gene families in plant genomes, accounting for over 24% of the families in the Pfam database [12, 13]. DUF-classified domains (defined by conserved “DUF” sequences with numerical identifiers) are increasingly linked to critical biological processes, including cellular signaling, metabolic regulation, and stress adaptation [14–18]. The DUF538 gene family exemplifies this functional significance and is highly conserved in terrestrial plants, with no homologues detected in bacterial or cyanobacterial lineages [19], suggesting an adaptive importance during land colonization. Notably, accumulating evidence has demonstrated the involvement of DUF538 genes in multiple crucial biological processes, including plant growth, development, and stress responses [].

The DUF538 gene family members SMALLER TRICHOMES WITH VARIABLE BRANCHES (SVB) and its homolog SVB-like (SVBL) regulate plant growth and trichome development by modulating the transcription of the key trichome formation regulator GLABRA1 (GL1) [18, 20]. Notably, the SVB gene enhances osmotic adjustment under salt stress and endoplasmic reticulum stress by increasing phosphatidylinositol levels [20, 21]. In maize (Zea mays), the glossy6 (gl6) mutant, which carries a defective DUF538-encoding gene, exhibits impaired intracellular transport of epicuticular waxes and increased consequent drought sensitivity [22]. DUF538 proteins are hypothesized to function as chlorophyll hydrolases and pectin methylesterases, potentially involved in plant responses to stress stimuli and cell wall-related defense mechanisms [23–25]. Poplar (Populus trichocarpa) DUF538 protein interacts with TRA2 and AbFH2 to coordinately regulate stomatal morphology and density under drought conditions [26]. Switchgrass (Panicum virgatum) DUF538 genes exhibit >50-fold upregulation under heat stress, a response potentially mediated by the activation of ROS-scavenging enzymes such as catalase and peroxidase [27]. Additionally, soybean (Glycine max) DUF538 genes confer resistance to root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) [28], while in Arabidopsis, certain DUF538 members function as small secreted peptides that participate in the regulation of immune responses [29].

Despite these accumulating functional insights, no comprehensive evolutionary or genomic analysis of the DUF538 family has been conducted, particularly in monocots. Moreover, the sequence evolution, expression divergence, and stress-responsive patterns of DUF538 genes across major plant lineages remain poorly characterized. Here, we present an integrated genomic, evolutionary, and functional analysis of the DUF538 family across 13 representative plants. Our study systematically investigates: (1) phylogenetic distribution and expansion patterns associated with WGD events; (2) sequence evolution, including motif variation and intron dynamics under selective constraints; (3) tissue-specific expression profiles and co-expression networks in maize; and (4) subgroup-specific temporal responses to CaCl₂ stress. Taken together, our results reveal how expression partitioning after gene duplication drives the expression specialization of DUF538 genes, thereby advancing our understanding of their contributions to plant stress adaptation.

Materials and methods

Identification and characterization of DUF538 genes in 13 plants

Coding and protein sequences of genes were obtained from the following sources: S. moellendorffii (v1.0), A. thaliana (TAIR Araport11), O. sativa (v7.0), (A) comosus (v3.0), (B) oleracea capitata (v1.0), G. max (v1.1), G. raimondii (v2.1), H. vulgare (r1), and V. vinifera (12X), data were retrieved from Phytozome v12 [30]; P. patens (ASM242v1), Z. mays, and B. napus were obtained from Ensembl Plants [31]; P. heterocycla (v1.0) was downloaded from the NCGR database [32].

To identify DUF538 genes, we performed BLASTP [27] searches against the protein sequences of these 13 plants using the conserved DUF538 domain sequence of A. thaliana (retrieved from TAIR [33] database) as the query (E-value ≤ 1e− 10). Candidate proteins were further verified for the presence of the DUF538 domain (PF04398) using Pfam and SMART databases [34, 35]. Molecular characteristics including theoretical isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weight were calculated with the ExPASy ProtParam tool [36].

Phylogenetic tree construction

Multiple sequence alignment of all identified DUF538 protein sequences was performed using MUSCLE v3.8.31 [37] with default parameters, and the resulting alignment was visualized using Jalview [38]. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE v1.6.5 [39] with the best-fit substitution model selected by ModelFinder [40]. Branch support was assessed using 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates [41]. The resulting tree topology was visualized and annotated by using FigTree v1.4.3 (https://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/).

Conserved motif and gene structure analysis

Conserved motifs in the DUF538 protein sequences were identified using MEME v4.12.0 [42] with the following parameters: nmotifs = 6, minw = 10, maxw = 50. Gene structure information was extracted from genome annotation GFF3 files. Both conserved motifs and gene structures were integrated and visualized using TBtools [43].

Selective pressure analysis

Codon-based sequence alignment of DUF538 genes was performed using PRANK [44] with default parameters. Selection pressures were assessed using CODEML from the PAML package [45] through two approaches: (1) Branch models to detect divergent selection pressures between foreground and background branches; (2) The selection pressure varied significantly across different branches, as calculated using the discrete model.

Expression analysis and co-expression network construction

Transcriptome raw sequencing data from 44 maize tissues (including seeds, leaves, roots, and others) as well as stress-treated samples (cold, salt, and drought stress) were obtained from the NCBI-SRA database (Supplementary Table 1). Expression analysis was performed using the following pipeline: (1) Raw sequencing reads were processed using fastp v0.21.0 [46] to remove adapter sequences and trim low-quality bases; (2) Clean reads were aligned to the maize reference genome (B73 RefGen_v4) using HISAT2 v2.2.1 [47]; (3) Transcript abundance was quantified as transcripts per million (TPM) values using StringTie v2.1.5 [48]. For co-expression network analysis, we employed the WGCNA package [49] in R, and visualized the networks using Cytoscape v3.10.1 [50]. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package [51]. Promoter analysis was performed by extracting the 2-kb sequences upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) of the target genes and submitting them to PlantPAN 4.0 [52] for prediction of cis-regulatory elements.

Plant materials and stress treatments

Seeds of the maize inbred line 08–641 were selected for uniformity and surface-sterilized with 75% ethanol for 1.5 min, followed by rinsing 6 times with distilled water. After soaking in distilled water for 4 h, the seeds were placed on moist filter paper in Petri dishes, covered with another layer of moist filter paper, and wrapped with light-blocking plastic film. The seeds were germinated at 28 °C in an incubator for 3 days. Germinated seeds with consistent radicle and coleoptile emergence were transferred to a hydroponic system containing Hoagland’s nutrient solution. The plants were grown under controlled environmental conditions: 25 °C, a 14-h light/10-h dark photoperiod, and nutrient solution renewed every three days.

At approximately 14 days after sowing, when the seedlings had reached the three-leaf-one-heart stage, uniformly grown individuals were selected for stress treatment. For CaCl₂ stress, seedlings were treated with 100 mM CaCl₂ solution. Root and leaf tissues were harvested at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h after treatment, with untreated plants as controls at each corresponding time point. Samples from three plants were pooled as one biological replicate, and three independent replicates were collected for each time point. All collected samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground into fine powder, and stored at − 80 °C.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from frozen root and leaf tissues using Magzol reagent (Magen, China) after homogenization in liquid nitrogen. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using NovoScript Reverse Transcriptase (Novoprotein Scientific Inc., China). Gene-specific primers for maize DUF538 genes were designed with Primer-BLAST [53] (Supplementary Table 2). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using NovoStart SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA) under the following program: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s and 65 °C for 45 s. Each reaction was performed in triplicate for every biological replicate. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [47], with normalization to internal reference genes.

Results

Distribution of DUF538 gene family members across 13 plants

We annotated 196 DUF538 genes across 13 representative land plants through homology-based searches and conserved domain verification using the Pfam (PF04398) and SMART databases [12, 34, 54]. Notably, no DUF538 homologs were detected in bacteria, archaea, or algae in the NCBI and Ensembl bacteria databases, confirming that this gene family is specific to land plants. The gene family exhibits distinct phylogenetic distribution patterns; basal land plants contain relatively few members (3 in P. patens and 5 in S. moellendorffii), whereas angiosperms demonstrate significant expansion (Table 1). Among eudicots, B. napus (40 members, allopolyploid) contains the highest number of DUF538 genes, followed by G. max (22), B. oleracea (19), and G. raimondii (17) (Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, A. thaliana (13) and V. vinifera (9) show comparatively lower copy numbers. Within monocots, Z. mays (20), O. sativa (14), and H. vulgare (14) exhibit higher gene numbers, while P. heterocycla (12) and A. comosus (8) retain relatively fewer members. This differential distribution pattern suggests that the DUF538 gene family underwent adaptive expansion during land plant evolution.

Phylogenetic profile of the DUF538 gene family

To elucidate the phylogenetic relationships of the DUF538 gene family, a ML phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using the WAG + F + R4 substitution model, which was selected as the best-fit model based on Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) analysis [39]. As shown in Fig. 1a, with moss PP1S47_89V6.1 and PP1S141_31V6.1 genes as outgroups, DUF538 genes were divided into four major subgroups: A, B, C and D, which correspond to 88, 65, 8 and 33 genes, respectively. Notably, subgroups A and B underwent substantial expansion, with gene numbers approximately two-fold higher than those in subgroup D. Subgroup D represents the most ancient lineage and contains homologs from both ferns and angiosperms, indicating that ancestral DUF538 genes were already present in seed plants. Subgroup C subsequently diverged after D, forming a distinct evolutionary branch. Moreover, we observed that both subgroups A and B contain fern, monocot and dicot genes, implying that the ancestral divergence of subgroups A and B may be associated with the ancient angiosperm WGD (ε) event [1]. Further subdivision revealed A1/A2 and B1/B2 clades, with fern genes exclusively retained in A2 and B1. This pattern suggests that A2 and B1 represent older lineages, whereas A1 and B2 may have originated after the ε event.

Motif and gene structure evolution in the DUF538 gene family

To further explore the sequence evolution of DUF538 genes, we analyzed conserved motifs in amino acid (aa) sequences using MEME [42]. Motifs 1 and 2 were identified as the core motifs, present in nearly all DUF538 members (Fig. 1b). In addition, the relatively ancient subgroups D and C contained the largest number of motifs, including motifs 4, 5, and 6, with motifs 5 and 6 uniquely present in these subgroups at the C-terminus and N-terminus, respectively (Fig. 2b). In contrast, subgroups A and B (including A1, A2, B1 and B2) primarily contained motifs 3 and 4, with motif 3 being nearly ubiquitous. Interestingly, motif 4 exhibited a scattered distribution—it was present in almost all A2 and B1 members but only in a subset of A1 and B2 genes. This pattern aligns with the phylogenetic relationships, suggesting that A2 and B1 are likely more ancient, retaining motif 4 from the ancestral subgroups D and C, whereas A1 and B2 may have undergone partial motif 4 loss. These findings reinforce the robustness of the phylogenetic relationships and imply potential functional divergence accompanying DUF538 sequence evolution. Gene structure analysis revealed that most genes in subgroups D and C contained two introns (Fig. 1c), whereas in subgroups A and B, B1 and B2 genes predominantly had one intron, with only a few containing either two or none. Notably, most A1 genes were intronless, while A2 genes mainly retained one intron. These results suggest that more ancient DUF538 genes tend to possess a higher intron number, consistent with previous reports that younger genes often undergo structural simplification [55].

Amino acid sequence characteristics of DUF538 proteins

The DUF538 gene family harbors a conserved DUF538 domain (Fig. 2b), although its detailed sequence features remain insufficiently characterized. MEME analysis revealed that motifs 1, 4, 2, and 3/5 constitute the core elements of the ~ 105 aa DUF538 domain. Three motifs were universally conserved: motif 1 (LxPxGxP, where x denotes variable residues), motif 4 (TGxFxC), and motif 2 (GxGxG) (Fig. 2a). Notably, both motif 5 (specific to subgroups D/C) and motif 3 (specific to subgroups A/B) occupy similar C-terminal positions (Figs. 1b and 2b), yet display distinct sequence signatures: motif 5 contains a VxKxFGxKRxV pattern, while motif 3 displays an FxVGxFxC configuration. These findings indicate that although the DUF538 domain retains strong evolutionary conservation in its core region, substantial sequence diversification has occurred at the C-terminus among different phylogenetic subgroups.

Comparative analysis of biophysical properties revealed marked variations among subgroups. Subgroups A1 and A2 showed markedly reduced molecular weights compared to the ancestral subgroup D, with A2 additionally exhibiting shorter sequence lengths (Fig. 2c). Notably, A1, B1 and B2 subgroups displayed a dramatic shift in pI from approximately 9 in subgroup D to 5–6, indicating substantial changes in protein charge characteristics. These observations suggest potential functional divergence within the DUF538 genes, while also indicating that certain subgroups have retained ancestral sequence features. Collectively, integrated motif and biophysical analyses provide compelling evidence of both evolutionary conservation and functional diversification within the DUF538 gene family.

Divergent selective constraints among DUF538 subgroups

To assess selective pressures across DUF538 subgroups, we reconstructed an ML phylogenetic tree using maize and rice orthologs (Fig. 3a). The tree topology was highly congruent with that of the DUF538 family phylogeny (Fig. 1a), supporting the robustness of these phylogenetic relationships. Multiple sequence alignment revealed pronounced amino acid divergence among subgroups (Fig. 3b), particularly between the ancestral subgroup D and the derived A/B subgroups. Divergent residues were observed at positions 50 (Ser→Ala/Leu/Val), 54 (Gly→Ala/Leu/Val/Ser), 82 (Pro→Ala/Thr), 84 (Leu→Ala), 85 (Pro→Tyr/His/Asn), 112 (Lys→Gly), 139 (Asp→Gly), and 142 (Ser→His), highlighting lineage-specific sequence differentiation between the A/B and D lineages.

Selection pressure analysis showed that the DUF538 family as a whole had ω (dN/dS) = 0.1145, consistent with purifying selection. Branch-model analysis demonstrated significantly stronger purifying selection in subgroup D (ω1 = 0.0043, ω0 = 0.1164, P = 0.012) compared to background branches, while other subgroups showed non-significant differences (Fig. 3c). Notably, the discrete model analysis further showed that subgroup D maintained lower median ω values compared to subgroups A/B, with particularly pronounced differences between A1 and D (P < 0.05, Wilcoxon test) (Fig. 3d). These results collectively indicate that sequence divergence in A/B subgroups is associated with relaxation of purifying selection, potentially facilitating functional diversification after their divergence from the ancestral D lineage.

Co-expression network and functional enrichment of DUF538 genes in maize

Based on transcriptional expression profiles from 44 maize tissues (10 seed, 6 leaf, 10 root, and 18 other tissues), we constructed a weighted gene co-expression network, which was partitioned into 17 distinct modules (Fig. 4a). Notably, seven of these modules (MEorange, MEblue, MEdarkgreen, MElightyellow, MEblack, MElightcyan, and MEred) contained members of the DUF538 gene family and were significantly associated with tissues (P < 0.01), with the number of DUF538 genes per module ranging from 1 to 4. Co-expression networks were visualized for modules containing at least two DUF538 genes (Fig. 4b). Among these, MEred, MElightyellow, MEblack, MEblue, and MEdarkgreen contained 3567, 2802, 1907, 955, and 323 genes co-expressed with DUF538 (weights > 0.1), respectively.

Functional enrichment analysis of genes co-expressed with DUF538 in tissue-specific modules revealed distinct biological functions. The root-associated MEblack module showed significant enrichment for positive regulation of flavonoid biosynthetic process (GO:0009963), transmembrane receptor protein serine/threonine kinase signaling pathway (GO:0007178), and focal adhesion assembly (GO:0048041) (Fig. 4c), while MElightyellow module was enriched for plant-type secondary cell wall biogenesis (GO:0009834), oxalate metabolic process (GO:0033609), and oxalate oxidase activity (GO:0050162). The leaf-associated MEdarkgreen module exhibited specific enrichment for hydrolase activities including retinyl-palmitate esterase activity (GO:0050253), methyl salicylate esterase activity (GO:0080031), and methyl jasmonate esterase activity (GO:0080032). These results suggest that the DUF538 gene may be involved in maize root development, leaf chlorophyll degradation, antioxidant processes, stress tolerance, and defense responses [18–26].

Tissue expression and stress response patterns of DUF538 genes in maize

Analysis of the 2-kb promoter regions of 20 DUF538 genes identified conserved cis-elements associated with both development and stress responses (Fig. 5a). Development-related elements included QELEMENTZMZM13 (starch biosynthesis), and RYREPEAT4 (seed-specific expression). Stress-related elements included ANAERO (anaerobic response), ABRE (abscisic acid signaling), DRE (dehydration/cold response), and GRA (gibberellin response). Distinct distribution patterns of these elements among subgroups suggest functional diversification. Tissue expression profiling revealed heterogeneous patterns across subgroups. Subgroup D contained both anther-specific (Zm00001d044230_T001) and constitutive expressers (Zm00001d038012_T001 and Zm00001d028785_T001). Subgroup B2 showed divergent patterns, with two genes broadly expressed (Zm00001d033574_T001 and Zm00001d013517_T001) and three members remaining silent (Fig. 5b). Subgroups A2 and B1 were expressed across multiple tissues, but showed condition-dependent expression in seeds and leaves. In contrast, the A1 subgroup displayed mixed expression patterns, with only Zm00001d037937_T001 showing ubiquitous expression, indicating potential subfunctionalization via expression divergence.

Stress response analysis demonstrated subgroup-specific differential expression under cold, salt, and drought treatments (|log2(FC)| >1, P-value < 0.05) (Fig. 5c). Cold stress led to downregulation of genes from B1 (Zm00001d042388_T001, Zm00001d006561_T001), B2 (Zm00001d033574_T001, Zm00001d013517_T001) and A1 (Zm00001d037937_T001, Zm00001d041790_T001). Salt-responsive genes included Zm00001d042388_T001 (B1) and Zm00001d041790_T001 (A1). Drought stress upregulated Zm00001d041790_T001 (A1), Zm00001d009902_T001 (A1), and Zm00001d003593_T001 (A2). Collectively, these results reveal both conserved and divergent roles of DUF538 genes. Specific members participate in distinct stress response pathways while maintaining subgroup- and tissue-specific expression patterns, reflecting evolutionary adaptation through functional diversification.

Response pattern of the DUF538 gene under calcium chloride stress in maize

Promoter analysis revealed that all 20 maize DUF538 genes harbor stress-responsive cis-elements (Fig. 5a). To further investigate their stress-responsive characteristics, we conducted a time-course expression analysis under CaCl2 stress (Fig. 6). The results showed marked temporal expression divergence among subgroup members in both root and leaf tissues at 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h. In roots, subgroup A1 genes displayed sequential upregulation patterns: Zm00001d038220_T001 at 6 h, Zm00001d048987_T001 at 12 h, and Zm00001d048985_T001 at 24 h after treatment. The subgroup B1 displayed stage-specific activation, with Zm00001d042388_T001 (6 h) and Zm00001d006561_T001 (12 h) being induced at different time points. Similarly, B2 subgroup members Zm00001d042783_T001 (6 h) and Zm00001d042716_T001 (24 h) exhibited a complementary expression patterns. In leaves, a comparable temporal partitioning was observed. Subgroup A1 demonstrated a relay-like activation pattern, with Zm00001d048985_T001 (6 h), Zm00001d048987_T001 (12 h), and Zm00001d009902_T001/Zm00001d037937_T001 (24 h) sequentially activated. Meanwhile, subgroup B2 achieved temporal expression allocation through Zm00001d009432_T001 (6 h), Zm00001d033574_T001 (12 h), and Zm00001d013517_T001 (24 h). This strict temporal expression partitioning suggests that the DUF538 gene family employs expression specialization among subgroup members to maintain sustained stress protection while effectively avoiding functional redundancy. These findings highlight an evolutionary strategy of subfunctionalization through expression divergence following gene duplication [56, 57].

Discussion

The DUF538 gene family, encoding domains of unknown function, has been implicated in developmental and stress responses [22, 26, 28], yet its evolutionary patterns and functional diversification remain poorly characterized. Through comprehensive phylogenetic and comparative genomic analyses, we establish DUF538 as a land plant-specific innovation, extending previous reports that only demonstrated its absence in bacterial and cyanobacterial lineages [19]. This family has undergone substantial expansion primarily through WGDs, particularly the angiosperm ε event (Fig. 1a) [1]. Expansion was especially pronounced in angiosperms, where high duplicate retention led to large copy numbers in species such as B. napus, G. max, and Z. mays. This pattern suggests that the retention of DUF538 duplicates was selectively favored, potentially due to their regulatory versatility in fluctuating environments.

Phylogenetically, DUF538 genes cluster into four subgroups (A-D). The ancestral subgroups C/D retain higher intron numbers and conserved motifs, while derived A/B subgroups show structural simplification (intron loss) accompanied by both motif loss and innovation (Figs. 1b-c and 2a-b). This structural simplification is further corroborated by selection analyses, which revealed that subgroup D experienced significantly stronger purifying selection compared to the relaxed evolutionary constraints operating on A/B lineages (Fig. 3c-d). This relaxation of selective constraints correlates with lineage-specific amino acid divergence (Fig. 3b) and biophysical property shifts (Fig. 2c). These results suggest that relaxation of selective constraints following gene duplication may have enabled sequence diversification in the DUF538 gene family, potentially facilitating neo- and subfunctionalization events that could contribute to plant adaptation during terrestrial colonization and angiosperm radiation [5, 50, 51]. Although future studies utilizing updated assemblies may refine specific gene copy numbers, the overarching evolutionary patterns—such as the land plant-specific origin, expansion through whole-genome duplications, and subsequent functional diversification—are supported by consistent evidence across multiple species and are expected to remain robust.

In maize, co-expression network analysis provides insights into functional diversification. DUF538 members are embedded in tissue-specific modules (Fig. 4a-c): the root-associated MEblack module is functionally enriched for flavonoid biosynthesis (GO:0009963), kinase-mediated signaling (GO:0007178), and focal adhesion assembly (GO:0048041), implicating DUF538 in root development and stress perception [28, 58, 59]. The leaf-associated MEdarkgreen module is enriched for hydrolase activities targeting signaling molecules such as retinyl-palmitate (GO:0050253), methyl salicylate (GO:0080031), and methyl jasmonate (GO:0080032), suggesting roles in defense signaling and antioxidant responses [18–26]. The MElightyellow module is enriched for secondary cell wall biogenesis (GO:0009834) and oxalate metabolism/oxidase activity (GO:0033609, GO:0050162), implicating DUF538 in structural reinforcement and ROS metabolism. These associations align with prior evidence linking DUF538 to drought (wax) [22, 26], salt (osmotic adjustment) [20], heat (ROS scavenging) [22], and nematode resistance [28, 60]. Collectively, network associations underscore how DUF538 diversification, mediated by tissue-specific co-expression, underpins developmental and stress-related functions.

Cis-regulatory divergence further underpins functional specialization. Promoter analysis revealed differential distribution of developmental and stress-responsive cis-elements among DUF538 subgroups, consistent with functional divergence in growth regulation and stress adaptation (Fig. 5a). Tissue-specific profiling confirmed heterogeneous patterns (Fig. 5b): subgroup D contained both constitutive and anther-specific expressers; subgroup B2 harbored broadly expressed and silent genes; subgroups A2/B1 were predominantly constitutive. Under abiotic stresses, subgroup-specific responses were observed (Fig. 5c): cold stress downregulated six genes in B1, B2, and A1; salt stress induced two genes in A1 and B1; and drought upregulated two A1 and one A2 genes. More than ten genes also showed significant responses to CaCl₂. Temporal partitioning under CaCl₂ stress was particularly striking (Fig. 6): in both roots and leaves, A1 and B2 members were sequentially activated (e.g., A1: Zm00001d038220_T001 6 h → Zm00001d048987_T001 12 h → Zm00001d048985_T001 24 h), creating a relay system ensuring sustained protection. This dynamic activation cascade illustrates a potential regulatory relay among duplicated genes, where temporally shifted expression ensures prolonged system responsiveness to persistent stress.

Taken together, our findings reveal that the DUF538 gene family has diversified functions in plant development and stress responses, and illustrate a classic principle: subfunctionalization among duplicates reduces redundancy while maintaining functional continuity [61, 62]. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, further experiments are needed, including subcellular localization analyses, systematic mapping of protein–protein interactions, and loss- and gain-of-function studies across representative paralogs. Such investigations will clarify the molecular functions of DUF538 proteins and test whether they act as small regulatory domains, enzyme cofactors, or components of stress-responsive pathways. Given their lineage-specific expansion, accelerated sequence evolution, and integration into signaling-related coexpression networks, DUF538 diversification may have contributed to the rewiring of regulatory circuits associated with environmental adaptation. Functional and biochemical validation of individual paralogs will be important to evaluate this possibility and to explore their potential relevance to crop stress resilience.

Conclusions

This study establishes DUF538 evolution as a paradigm of duplication-driven functional innovation in plant adaptation. The gene family originated specifically in land plants, followed by extensive expansion through whole-genome duplications and diversification into four subgroups. Relaxed purifying selection in derived subgroups (A/B) enabled sequence diversification (motif innovation, residue changes, pI shifts), while strong constraints preserved the core function in subgroup D. This sequence-level divergence facilitated regulatory and functional specialization, evidenced by distinct co-expression network integrations (root development, leaf defense signaling, cell wall reinforcement) and precise spatiotemporal expression partitioning under development and stress (tissue-specificity, subgroup-specific stress induction, temporal relay under CaCl₂). Collectively, gene duplication generated the raw material, relaxed selection permitted mutation accumulation, and divergent expression patterns orchestrated subfunctionalization, thereby contributing to the functional diversification of the DUF538 gene family and its role in environmental adaptation. Important next steps include investigating the core DUF538 domain’s biochemical function through structural biology approaches and assessing the functional significance of subgroup-specific motifs via targeted mutagenesis. For instance, key candidate genes for future functional validation via CRISPR-Cas9 may include Zm00001d042388_T001, Zm00001d006561_T001, Zm00001d009902_T001, Zm00001d037937_T001, Zm00001d033574_T001 and Zm00001d013517_T001, and exploring potential applications by characterizing DUF538 in extremophytes. These investigations may contribute to developing novel crop improvement strategies.
