Policy Proposals for Mitigating Intensive Care Unit Strain: Insights from the COVID-19 Pandemic
Authors: Ivor S. Douglas, Anuj Mehta, Jason Mansoori
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Intensive care unit (ICU) strain, characterized by a discrepancy between perceived or actual intensive care resources and demand, significantly impacts patient outcomes and healthcare worker well-being. The coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic exacerbated ICU strain, leading to increased mortality and extended hospital stays, affecting both critically ill patients with and without COVID-19. A systematic review identified 16 leading and lagging indicators of ICU capacity strain, including queuing, premature and after-hours ICU discharge, use of temporary space, length of stay, burnout, staffing and nurse-to-patient ratio, ICU census, acuity and turnover, standardized mortality ratio, readmissions, availability of critical supplies, ventilator use, and surgery cancellation. However, variability in operational definitions and limited evidence regarding the reliability, validity, usability, and feasibility limit the value of single indicators for informed strategic planning and policy guidance. Regional and national policies and programs are essential to enhance real-time monitoring for effective management of critical care resources, and they mitigate the impact of ICU strain, facilitating complex interhospital transfers to reduce strain and ensuring comprehensive strategies for enhancing ICU resilience. Proactive regional cooperation is advocated for policy formulation, knowledge exchange, and resource allocation to anticipate and mitigate ICU strain, ensuring equitable healthcare access during global health crises. The policy implications for future preparedness emphasize the importance of evidence-based triage and adaptable patient management strategies alongside ethical considerations in resource allocation and the role of behavioral economic insights in optimizing resource utilization and collaborative healthcare practices. This multifaceted approach for addressing ICU strain comprehensively and effectively during a pandemic would promote health equity and enhance healthcare system resilience under both routine operations and crisis conditions.

Hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) strain has emerged as a preeminent area of concern within healthcare systems worldwide magnified by the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic. Process, structure, and outcome measures of ICU strain are used to evaluate ICU resource capacity and flexibility, including staffing and bed availability, and medical equipment availability significantly influences patient outcomes, healthcare delivery, and the overall resilience of healthcare systems. There are significant differences, regionally and between individual institutions, in terms of surge capacity and adaptability (1). This variation affects how ICUs can handle crises, impacting the quality of care and patient results. Although the pandemic highlighted the acute challenges faced during unprecedented global health emergencies, it also shed light on inherent vulnerabilities in ICU management that persist even in noncrisis periods. We endeavor to provide a comprehensive understanding of ICU strain’s broad implications and the policy recommendations and planning strategies to be considered in mitigating the impact of ICU strain while enhancing ICU resilience under both routine operations and crisis conditions.

ICU Strain Before and during the COVID-19 Pandemic

ICU strain occurs when the demand for critical care surpasses the availability of resources, which can jeopardize patient care, outcomes, and clinician well-being. This phenomenon involves not only actual discrepancies between patient needs and ICU bed availability but also perceived or impending shortages that may stress the system. It encompasses a spectrum ranging from contingency to crisis standards of care, as classified by the National Academy of Sciences (2). Contingency standards apply in situations where demand exceeds resources, yet strategic interventions may safeguard patient outcomes. Crisis standards, conversely, characterize conditions in which patient care and outcomes are compromised, as well as clinician resilience and well-being.

The ramifications of ICU strain extend across dimensions of individual and population health, including increased patient mortality, extended hospital stays, and the degradation of care quality. In routine circumstances, transient ICU capacity strain can impact care delivery processes before affecting mortality outcomes (3, 4). This suggests that existing ICU capacity and care processes are elastic and may be optimized or streamlined to a significant extent. Protocolized care and explicit clinical pathways are widely implemented and ensure that effective treatments are systematically considered and consistently delivered to achieve timely care (5). However, during periods of increased strain, there may be an overreliance on structured care pathways, leading to their inappropriate application, particularly when the unique aspects of a patient’s condition are not fully aligned with the general criteria established by the protocols. ICU staff experience significant job dissatisfaction and moral distress related to ICU strain, with delivery of ICU care perceived as “nonbeneficial” being a frequently cited patient-level contributor (6, 7). Additionally, the deployment of nonspecialist staff, a common strategy during high-demand periods, requires careful oversight to ensure that they are effectively trained to apply these protocols appropriately.

Before the global COVID-19 pandemic, Gabler and colleagues reported a direct correlation between ICU strain and increased mortality rates among patients, underscoring the immediate effect of resource limitations on patient survival (8). This association was starkly illustrated during the early waves of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020–2022. Among 8,516 patients with COVID-19 who were admitted to 88 Veterans Administration hospitals and treated in the ICU during periods of heightened COVID-19 ICU demand, there was a significant increase in the risk of mortality compared with those treated during low-demand periods (9). Adjusted hazard ratios for all-cause mortality indicated a substantial difference in risk between patients treated during different levels of ICU demand: 0.99 (95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.81–1.22) for demand of more than 25–50%, 1.19 (95% CI = 0.95–1.48) for demand of more than 50–75%, and 1.94 (95% CI = 1.46–2.59) for demand of more than 75–100%, with a significant association observed (P < 0.001) for the latter category. Notably, the association between COVID-19 ICU load and mortality did not remain consistent over time, showing variations between early and late stages of the pandemic.

Multiple other studies have also demonstrated increasing patient mortality associated with greater ICU strain across multiple settings from developed nations with ample resources to settings with chronic resource limitations. Anesi and colleagues, for example, have reported significant interinstitutional variability in practice responses regarding ICU admission decisions on the basis of the degree of hospital strain at a given time for patients presenting with acute respiratory failure in the United States (1). Mean predicted probability of ICU admission for across different levels of hospital strain ranged from 7.4% to 65.7%. Additionally, the difference in the predicted probabilities of ICU admission between the lowest and highest deciles of hospital strain varied substantially, ranging from 6.0% to 64.2%. The wide spectrum of elasticity in the face of ICU strain extends to resource-limited environments, too. In a separate study of South African ICUs, higher ICU occupancy was linked to an increased odds of ICU mortality (10). The association of increased mortality with greater ICU strain across diverse healthcare settings is crucial for developing tailored strategies and policies to address ICU strain across a variety of settings.

In the wake of the H1N1 influenza pandemic, a 2010 report by the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine’s Task Force for Intensive Care Unit Triage during an influenza epidemic or mass disaster provided recommendations for hospital and ICU preparedness. Recommendations included surge capacity and infrastructure considerations, coordination and collaboration with interface units (centralized departments critical for resource management and patient care coordination during emergencies), surge and contingency staffing, essential equipment, pharmaceuticals and supplies, protection of patients and staff, critical care triage, and investment in telemedicine and digital health services. These components are encapsulated in the 6S (staff, stuff, space, systems, support, and sustainability) modification (11) of the original Barbisch and Koenig surge capacity framework (12).

ICU Capacity Strain Indicators

ICU capacity strain indicators or metrics, particularly in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, have been proposed for managing critical care resources and patient care. The pandemic underscored the potential of near–real-time monitoring of these indicators to facilitate rapid responses and adjustments in care delivery and resource allocation (13). Leading and lagging indices span across different aspects of healthcare delivery and offer distinct insights into the operational and clinical dynamics of ICU strain (Table 1; see also the data supplement) (14, 15). Leading indices serve as proactive indicators, offering early warning signs of impending strain that are based on real-time or predictive data. Conversely, lagging indices retrospectively gauge the impact of strain, whereas certain indicators may exhibit characteristics of both depending on the prevailing circumstances (16). Optimally, markers of ICU strain could lead to the development of strategies to alleviate strain in real time, with the ultimate goal of reducing ICU mortality.

In a systematic review of 51 studies, Rewa and colleagues identified 16 indicators of strained ICU capacity, including ICU acuity, ICU readmission, after-hours discharge, and ICU census/occupancy, which aligned with commonly recommended key ICU performance indicators (15). Operational definitions for these indicators varied substantially across studies. Moreover, few studies evaluated the scientific acceptability, usability, or feasibility of the proposed strain indicators, and none specifically described the reliability or validity of these indicators limiting the value of individual measures to inform strategic planning and policy guidance.

Insights and Policy Responses to ICU Strain During the COVID-19 Pandemic

The COVID-19 pandemic precipitated unprecedented and fluctuating strain on ICUs and critical care resources globally. The World Health Organization (WHO) (17), RAND (18), and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (13) have documented the immense pressure faced by ICUs across continents. Reports from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention web-based voluntary surveillance reporting system during the early pandemic waves showed that approximately two-thirds of participating hospitals reported alerts for overcrowding in emergency departments (EDs) and ICUs and ventilator shortages (13). The WHO has continuously highlighted the critical need for adequate ICU capacity to manage severe cases of COVID-19, especially during periods of surges in infections (17). They have underscored the importance of preparedness measures, including preparing hospitals to mobilize surge acute and ICU capacity, ensuring the availability of essential medical supplies and equipment, and bolstering healthcare workforce capacity to handle the influx of critically ill patients. While exposing the persistent vulnerabilities of hospitals and ICU to overwhelming strain, the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the overarching imperative of global partnerships with state and regional governments, international health organizations, and nongovernmental agencies to develop and implement policies that define roles and responsibilities and facilitate cross-border collaboration and resource sharing.

Ethical and Policy Implications of ICU Strain in Pandemic Situations

Ethical rationing during periods of severe ICU strain during a pandemic must address the tension between the principles of maximizing utility, ensuring fairness while upholding individual rights and dignity (19–21). Prioritizing resources to maximize the overall benefit to the population is widely held as morally justifiable, especially when faced with overwhelming demand. Allocating access to scarce resources on the basis of factors such as predicted survival or potential for future contribution to society can save the most lives and achieve the greatest good. However, such utilitarian approaches risk exacerbating existing social, cultural, ethnic, religious, and economic disparities and disproportionately impacting vulnerable populations who may already face barriers to healthcare access (22, 23).

Harm-reduction strategies for ICU bed allocation aim to minimize harm and maximize benefit to the greatest number of people. Prioritizing those with the highest likelihood of survival or the greatest potential to benefit from ICU care can save more lives and reduce overall suffering. However allocating scarce resources in a way that maximizes the chances of survival and minimizes the burden on the healthcare system can inadvertently exacerbate existing inequalities and biases, particularly toward underserved and racially/ethnically diverse populations, leading to inequitable outcomes in ICU bed allocation (24, 25).

Alternative ethical frameworks, such as egalitarianism, emphasize the principle of equal treatment and distribution of resources (21). Egalitarians argue that everyone should have an equal chance of accessing life-saving treatments, regardless of factors such as age, social status, or preexisting health conditions. From this perspective, prioritizing certain groups on the basis of prognostic criteria or potential for societal contribution may perpetuate systemic injustices and discrimination and further marginalize already vulnerable populations.

Policymakers must pursue nuanced approaches to anticipate and address ICU strain during future pandemics and health crises that prioritize harm reduction and are inclusive of diverse perspectives. Policy guidance must be transparent, actionable, and inclusive and must adhere to principles of fairness, equity, and respect for human rights. By necessity, this involves active engagement with community stakeholders (see Table E1 in the data supplement) to discern their specific concerns and requirements, alongside the implementation of safeguards to preempt discrimination and ensure that decisions are founded on objective clinical criteria.

Impact of ICU Strain on Mortality and Hospital Stay

The COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated prevailing ICU strain, presenting unprecedented challenges in managing critical care resources. Anesi and colleagues (2022) emphasized the delicate balance of ICU admissions for patients with sepsis or acute respiratory failure during the pandemic, where evidence-based triage became even more critical amid surging ICU demands (6). Rosenthal and colleagues (2020) noted that patients with COVID-19 in hospitals with high ICU strain had increased in-hospital mortality rates (26). The COVID-19 Hospitalization in England Surveillance System (or, CHESS) study, with a focus on ICU admissions across England, investigated the relationship between mechanical ventilator bed occupancy and mortality risk among patients with COVID-19 in the ICU (27). The study observed over 135,600 patient days, with a mortality rate of 19.4 per 1,000 patient days. High occupancy (>85%) increased mortality odds (odds ratio [OR], 1.23; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.08–1.39) compared with baseline (45–85%), whereas low occupancy (<45%) decreased it (OR, 0.83; 95% CI = 0.75–0.94). Mortality risk increased linearly with occupancy, showing a larger association at individual outcome dates (OR, 2.29; 95% CI = 1.80–2.92) and over the patient’s ICU stay (OR, 2.69; 95% CI = 2.05–3.50). Short-term contract “travel” nurses were a critical component in addressing staffing shortages and dangerously low nurse-to-patient ratios at the peak of the pandemic. Regional and institutional compensation differences, however, amplified existing disparities within the healthcare system in some cases, which led to extreme financial strain for many organizations, adversely affecting their operational capacity.

Policy Recommendations to Optimize ICU Capacity and Staffing

1.Maintain ICU occupancy and turnover within optimal ranges: A first policy imperative involves requirements for healthcare systems to provide high-quality access and care without becoming overwhelmed, especially during sudden demand surges. Healthcare systems, particularly in high-volume urban and referral centers, should adopt dynamic strategies to manage both emergency surges and routine fluctuations in patient volume. This includes establishing flexible occupancy targets, refining admission criteria, and enhancing regional coordination to evenly distribute patient loads. Clear triggers for exceeding occupancy must be defined to handle unexpected surges effectively. This balanced approach ensures that all hospitals, from large referral centers to community hospitals with underutilized beds, maintain high-quality care standards. Continuous training and advanced data analytics support these dynamic strategies by facilitating real-time decision-making.2.Enhance ICU capacity and ensure adequate staffing and healthcare worker well-being and retention: Policies to ensure adequate ICU staffing at local and regional levels would require a national healthcare workforce registry (“medical national guard”) linked to a centralized credentialing system. This would require the development of rapid, tailored training programs and incentives for healthcare workers’ participation in wellness programs and mental health support services that regularly assess burnout levels, leading to targeted interventions that may include work schedule adjustments and improved team communication and support.

Impact of Demographic and Socioeconomic Disparities on ICU Strain and Patient Outcomes

As the pandemic unfolded, disparities in patient outcomes on the basis of hospital strain became more apparent. Asch and colleagues (2021) explored the differences in COVID-19 mortality rates among Black and White Medicare beneficiaries, illustrating how hospital strain could exacerbate existing healthcare disparities (28). This was further amplified by Bagshaw and colleagues (2018), who used path-analysis modeling to uncover the direct and indirect effects of strained ICU capacity on patient outcomes, demonstrating that strained capacity was associated with increased ICU mortality, mediated through greater illness acuity (29).

Together, these findings suggest a strong and causal association of ICU strain with increased mortality risk. Evidence of racial and ethnic disparities in this association highlight the long-standing challenge of equitable critical care service access and delivery that were both preexistent and, to a large extent, persistent beyond the peak of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Policy Recommendations to Address Disparities in Patient Outcomes on the basis of ICU Strain

3.Promote health equity and mitigate implicit biases and disparities: To confront and dismantle systemic disparities, particularly in well-resourced healthcare settings where such inequities stubbornly persist, policies must champion a comprehensive, equity-focused allocation of ICU resources, strategic deployment of investments, medical equipment, and skilled healthcare professionals to the most impacted regions and efforts to address the underlying biases that contribute to disparities. The combination of implementation with targeted audits, rigorous debiasing, and implicit bias training for all healthcare staff, community engagement, and ongoing oversight, is imperative.4.Focus on and invest in reducing disparities in rural areas: Policies should target infrastructure improvements in rural areas, where disparities are often most pronounced because of limited access to high-quality healthcare services. Policies should aim to optimize existing ICU facilities rather than simply expanding them. This includes enhancing the scope of care at rural hospitals to ensure that rural ICUs can provide comprehensive services. Improving transportation for better accessibility to care and investing in telehealth technologies and training, supported by regulatory frameworks that encourage the widespread adoption of telemedicine, would assist in bridging gaps locally that amplify regional ICU strain.

Patient Management Accommodations for ICU Strain

The pandemic has forced hospitals to adapt to rapidly changing conditions, often leading to suboptimal patient management practices. Institutional triage committees, validated prognostic scoring systems (30, 31), and structured utilitarian rationing (19–21), although all of value, were insufficient to guide rationing decisions during periods of maximum COVID-19 ICU strain (22). Early tracheostomy emerged as a strategy to manage ICU capacity by potentially reducing the duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU stays (32). However, the optimal timing and effectiveness of such interventions in overburdened healthcare systems remain areas of debate.

Policy Recommendations for Adaptations in Patient Management and Triage

5.Develop robust, flexible transfer protocols: Policies to standardize an adaptable regional and national framework for the management and transfer of critically ill patients would require the establishment of clear guidelines for patient transfers to optimize patient flow. Real-time data systems for bed availability and the facilitation of communication channels across departments and facilities are imperative in addressing the existing patchwork of systems with limited interoperability.6.Prioritize healthcare worker preparedness training: Policies to prioritize comprehensive training programs that encompass crisis standards of care, emergency management practices, and adaptive patient management strategies should be coupled with simulation-based learning, scenario planning exercises, and continuous professional development opportunities focused on crisis readiness. Creating and reinforcing organizational cultures of resilience and adaptability within healthcare teams in partnership with professional societies could ensure that ICU staff are not only technically proficient but also emotionally prepared to handle healthcare emergencies.

Interface between ED Strain and ICU Strain

Strain in both EDs and ICUs generates extensive disruptions in hospital operations, impeding access and increasing risk of provider burnout. ED strain often results from acute patient surges, leading to overcrowding, increased wait times, and potentially delayed care. In contrast, ICU strain typically involves sustained high demand for critical care resources, including ventilators and specialized care. ICU strain often arises from delays in transferring critically ill patients from the ED or other hospital units and significantly impacting patient outcomes and healthcare worker stress levels (13). The bidirectional impact between ED strain and ICU strain is also significant. Resource scarcity is a common and concomitant challenge in both the ED and ICU under strain, necessitating the allocation of scarce resources and the rationing of medical supplies, beds, and staff time. High ICU strain drives further delayed transfers from the ED, exacerbating overcrowding and prolonging wait times for critical care. Conversely, ED strain can overflow into the ICU, with surges in admissions leading to increased ICU occupancy and resource utilization, as well as the requirement for ICU staff to assist in the care of critically ill patients in the ED. This cyclical dependency was globally amplified as ED and ICU capacities were exceeded during peak pandemic conditions, with adverse consequences for effective patient care and operational efficiency. Incident command systems are optimally designed to streamline this process (23, 33, 34). Care prioritization results in patients experiencing prolonged ED wait times, significantly elevating stress levels among healthcare workers, jeopardizing their physical and emotional well-being and predisposing them to burnout (35). The adverse consequences of burnout on patient safety and care quality were well characterized even before the COVID-19 pandemic (36, 37).

Policy Recommendations to Address the Interface between ED Strain and ICU Strain

7.Emphasize real-time data monitoring for informed decisions: Policies to establish centralized regional information sharing to monitor patient flow, bed availability, and resource allocation in real time, the underpinning of other policy recommendations, would enhance data-driven decisions that optimize patient care and resource use. Implementation involves integrating advanced data analytics and digital health technologies across ED and ICU operations coupled with clinician and staff training to enable a seamless flow of information that supports timely and effective patient transfers and care management.8.Advocate for flexible healthcare systems: Policies that establish structural and procedural adaptability within healthcare facilities and allow them to scale operations up or down on the basis of real-time demand may involve developing modular or expandable facility designs, cross-training staff to perform multiple roles, and establishing protocols that allow for rapid shifts in operational focus. Implementing resilience training would support cultural evolution that embraces change and uncertainty as constants and that encourages and incentivizes innovation.

Interhospital Transfers Impacting ICU Strain

Sarzynski and colleagues analyzed the large PINC AI™ (formerly Premier, Inc.) Healthcare Database to characterize interhospital transfers during periods of peak pandemic ICU strain (38). Despite expectations of increased transfers during surge periods, overall patient transfers decreased, particularly from caseload-strained rural hospitals. Notably, during the delta and omicron waves, transfers increased significantly, especially at small urban hospitals. This paradoxical trend underscores the significant operational challenges in managing hospital flow and ICU strain. In contrast, Sanchez and colleagues analyzed France’s comprehensive SI-VIC national registry of patients in the ICU (39). Transfers were categorized on the basis of regions and evaluated the role of ICU capacity saturation in dictating transfer patterns. Regions with high COVID-19 incidence and ICU saturation over 100% were strongly associated with greater numbers of patient transfers.

These findings highlight the complex regional variations in managing hospital flow in response to ICU strain and underscore the necessity of robust healthcare infrastructure and planning to manage ICU strain and optimize patient outcomes during pandemics. They also highlight the critical importance of adaptability in healthcare logistics to ensure the effective utilization of available resources and the need for continuous assessment of healthcare strategies to respond to evolving challenges. Health policy adjustments are needed to improve transfer capabilities and address disparities, particularly in rural areas.

Policy Recommendations to Address the Impact of Strain on Interhospital Transfers

9.Implement standardized criteria for patient transfers: Policy interventions to streamline and enhance the efficiency of transfers would establish standardized criteria for patient transfers to create a uniform framework that governs how and when patients are transferred between hospitals, addressing both the urgency of the patient’s condition and the capacity of potential receiving facilities. Clear, regularly updated, evidence-based criteria could be incorporated, thereby reducing ambiguity and decision-making time during critical situations.10.Establish regional coordination centers: Policies to incentivize centralized organizational centers to act as hubs for coordinating patient transfers within and across regions—leveraging real-time data on hospital capacities, patient needs, and logistical considerations to optimize patient distribution—would require robust communication networks, comprehensive data integration systems, and dedicated teams who are skilled in logistics and patient care coordination for successful implementation.

Impact on Patients without COVID-19

The redirection of healthcare resources toward patients without COVID-19 has had significant impacts on patients with high-risk non-COVID-19 conditions (e.g., heart failure, asthma, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), including increased mortality and extended hospital stays, compared with prepandemic periods of lower ICU strain (40). This led to postponed surgeries and delayed treatments, necessitating adjustments in care protocols and the implementation of crisis standards. McLarty and colleagues reported the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on patients who did not have COVID-19 but required ICU care across 15 countries (41). With over 1.7 million ICU admissions analyzed, it found that non–COVID-19 ICU mortality increased from 9.3% to 10.4%. This change varied by country income level, with mortality increasing in middle-income countries while decreasing in high-income countries, highlighting the differential impact on ICU outcomes across economic contexts.

Crisis Standards of Care (CSCs) to Address ICU Strain

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the design and adoption of innovative contingency strategies for expanding hospital capacity, such as converting non-ICU spaces for critical care use and utilizing telehealth services to manage less severe cases remotely. These adaptations were crucial in maintaining hospital flow and ensuring that both patients with and without COVID-19 received timely care. However, in anticipation of saturation conditions, existing CSCs were modified to anticipate the potential for overwhelming demand on healthcare resources, particularly ICU care. CSCs represent a significant shift from the conventional individual-centered care approach to a population-centered model, prioritizing the immediate care needs of larger groups over the optimal outcomes for individual patients. CSC guidelines were designed to create an ethical framework that allocates scare resources (e.g., ventilators or ICU beds) when demand exceeds supply (25) and provides health systems the ability to adapt to extreme operating conditions and severe constraints imposed by COVID-19 surges (42). However CSCs can lead to the suspension of many preventive and elective procedures, exacerbating serious conditions among individuals who would have benefited from earlier diagnosis and intervention. Marked declines in U.S. ED visits for critical conditions such as heart attacks, strokes, and diabetic emergencies were reported during the early months of the pandemic and were associated with significant increases in mortality for patients without conditions related to COVID-19 (43). CSCs include triaging patients on the basis of the severity of their condition and the likelihood of benefiting from ICU care. Such measures, although necessary, have raised ethical considerations and have underscored the need for transparent and equitable decision-making processes.

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the limitations of existing CSCs, particularly in triaging patients on the basis of clinical risk scores. Traditional scores on measures such as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment were found inadequate for COVID-19 prognostication, leading to the exploration of disease-specific scores such as the modified 4C (m4C) score to predict COVID-19 mortality. Yek and colleagues critically evaluated the effectiveness of the m4C score across various pandemic stages and surge conditions (31). The m4C score maintained adequate discrimination for mortality prediction early in the pandemic, but its effectiveness decreased in later waves, including delta and omicron variants, underscoring the dynamic nature of the disease and healthcare system strain. This highlights the imperative of frequent reassessment of CSC guidelines and prognostic tools to ensure that they remain effective in real-time resource allocation and patient care.

The activation of CSCs during the pandemic underscores the critical balance between managing immediate public health crises and maintaining the capacity to address ongoing healthcare needs. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency COVID Task Force’s analysis during the period from July 4, 2020, to July 10, 2021—which included the prevalence of the highly transmissible severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) B.1.617.2 (delta) variant—emphasized the importance of controlling case growth and hospitalizations to prevent severe strain on healthcare systems (44). Their findings indicated that, if ICU bed use nationwide reached 75% capacity, an estimated 12,000 additional excess deaths would occur nationally over the next 2 weeks, and with hospitals exceeding 100% ICU bed capacity, 80,000 excess deaths would be expected 2 weeks later.

However, most industrialized, high-income, and middle-income countries operate health systems with very limited excess ICU bed capacity. Therefore, policy solutions that target excess bed capacity as a surge resource are unlikely to have durability in the face of a future pandemic or national health crisis (45). Indeed, individual hospitals, even those that are part of a network, were unsuccessful in addressing the peak pandemic surge and adverse impacts on ICU strain. Regional capacity and patient transfer services that were deployed at the state or local/regional level were somewhat effective during later phases of the pandemic in leveling flow and interinstitutional transfers to mitigate extreme strain. However, many regions experienced adverse selection, and some hospitals were reported to decline transfers routinely, even when there was residual capacity.

Recommendations for Implementing Policy to Mitigate Pandemic ICU Strain

Severe ICU strain amplifies cognitive biases and can distort rational considerations for triage, social norms, and contextual cues. Policies that incorporate behavioral economic interventions that nudge toward more optimal choices and actions can serve as powerful supplements to policy solutions for disaster and pandemic preparedness planning (46, 47). Economic incentives may have outsized effects on healthcare behaviors (48). Regional incentive structures that reward hospitals and healthcare systems for efficient utilization of ICU resources could be effective in alleviating strain by optimizing patient transfer management to maintain ICU occupancy within manageable levels while minimizing delays. These incentives could be tied to specific metrics such as ICU census levels, transfer efficiency rates, and patient outcomes, encouraging healthcare providers to prioritize resource optimization and collaboration.

Additionally, nudges can be used to influence individual behaviors and organizational practices toward better adherence to guidelines and protocols aimed at managing ICU strain (49). Defaults that leverage social influence bias (50) (e.g., targeted messaging strategies that emphasize the importance of preserving ICU beds and resources for the most critically ill patients by preventing the spread of the virus through responsible behavior) and commitment devices, which leverage present bias and loss aversion (e.g., advanced directives), have been proposed (46). Simple, low-cost interventions such as timely reminders and visual cues can help reinforce best practices and streamline decision-making processes. For instance, implementing electronic prompts or checklist reminders for ICU admission criteria and transfer protocols can help standardize workflows and may reduce cognitive burden for healthcare providers, leading to more efficient and timely patient management. Moreover, social norm messaging that highlight successful examples of hospitals and healthcare teams that effectively manage ICU capacity and patient transfers can create positive peer pressure and foster a culture of collaboration and innovation across healthcare networks (51).

Integrating behavioral economic insights into policy frameworks and healthcare delivery systems can complement traditional approaches to mitigating the adverse effects of pandemic ICU strain and enhancing regional capacity and transfer services (46, 52).

Conclusion

COVID-19 amplified ICU strain, negatively impacting patient care outcomes for critically ill patients with and without COVID-19, leading to disparities and compromised patient outcomes, increased ICU and hospital mortality rates, and significant challenges for healthcare providers. Because capacity strain is a feature of ICU care during nonpandemic conditions and is linked to modifications in care delivery processes than mortality rates, this suggests that opportunities exist to refine ICU processes without affecting patient survival. It is important for policymakers and healthcare administrators to recognize the potential for proactive ICU management adjustments that prevent crises and optimize resources without sacrificing care quality. These insights are valuable for both emergency preparedness and routine operational strategies in healthcare settings.

Regrettably, existing governmental and professional society guidance and recommendations regarding ICU surge preparedness, although sound, were disregarded after the 2009 H1N1 influenza pandemic. To mitigate the consequences of ICU strain in future healthcare emergencies and pandemics, policymakers and healthcare leaders will need to integrate lessons learned from the COVID-19 pandemic (Table 2). Innovative strategies and adaptations in patient management practices that emerged from the pandemic—including the activation of CSC and the implementation of regional transfer services to optimize resource allocation and patient flow—would require policy level support to mitigate future overwhelming pandemic-related ICU strain.

Disaster and pandemic preparedness strategies should draw on insights regarding adaptability and ethical considerations in rational triage and resource allocation and leverage principles of behavioral economics (e.g., nudges, commitment devices, and incentive structures) to encourage more optimal decision-making among healthcare providers and patients and to manage ICU strain more efficiently and equitably. This includes developing robust crisis response protocols, including actionable triggers; enhancing regional capacity and transfer services and implementing strategies to address healthcare disparities, particularly in rural areas; improving access to preventive and acute care; addressing social determinants of health; and promoting health equity. Furthermore, prioritizing healthcare worker well-being and retention through adequate protective measures, mental health support, and competitive incentives is crucial to maintain a resilient ICU and hospital healthcare workforce.

Additionally, there is a need to invest in healthcare infrastructure and workforce development, including simulations of high-strain scenarios and patient transfer logistics, to ensure that healthcare systems are resilient and able to effectively manage ICU strain during future global health emergencies. Enhancing ICU capacity and flexible surge adaptation, ensuring adequate staffing, and developing protocols for efficiently managing patient flow during surges are imperative. Prespecifying task shifting to redistribute specific tasks to less specialized health workers, investing in rapid training programs to upskill existing healthcare personnel, leveraging telemedicine to extend the reach of limited staff, and forming partnerships with international health organizations for temporary staffing support are all important considerations. We also need to consider investments in real-time data sharing between hospitals to monitor and predict ICU capacities. Standardized criteria for and centralized monitoring of patient transfers, and continued funding of regional coordination centers to manage the distribution of patients more effectively are further imperatives.

In conclusion, addressing ICU strain requires a comprehensive and multifaceted policy approach that incorporates lessons learned from the COVID-19 pandemic, promotes health equity, and leverages behavioral economic insights to enhance pandemic response efforts. By implementing evidence-based strategies and investing in healthcare infrastructure and workforce development, healthcare systems can better prepare for and mitigate the impact of ICU strain in both routine operating conditions and future global health emergencies.
