Integrative evidence-knowledge marker selection enhances LLM-based cell type annotation in single-cell RNA-seq analysis
Authors: Jiyeong Shin, Soyoung Jeong, Hyun Je Kim, Minsik Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCell type annotation is essential for gaining biological insight from single-cell RNA sequencing data, yet manual labeling remains time-consuming and difficult to reproduce. Various computational approaches have been developed to automate this process, and recent studies suggest that large language models can infer cell types with promising accuracy in single-cell analysis. However, most workflows still rely on cluster-specific markers derived from gene expression alone or manual curation. As a result, marker selection can be sensitive to statistical criteria and dataset-dependent bias, which may lead to the selection of less informative genes or missing important markers, while providing limited biological context.ResultsTo address this limitation, we introduce CELLIA, an LLM-based workflow for automated and robust cell type annotation. CELLIA employs an integrative evidence-knowledge marker selection strategy that combines statistical differential expression criteria with curated tissue-specific marker resources to identify informative marker genes. In benchmarking analyses of 102 cell types, this approach improved agreement with manual annotations. In addition, CELLIA achieved higher agreement in subtype-level analyses of closely related immune populations and was further evaluated in a non-immune stromal subtype setting, covering 25 cell types in total.ConclusionBy integrating evidence-knowledge from gene expression with curated biological prior knowledge, CELLIA provides a more stable marker selection and improves the reliability of LLM-cell type annotation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13040-026-00593-7.

Background

Cell type annotation is a crucial step in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis, linking cellular identity to disease etiology and enabling targeted treatments [1]. In standard single-cell analysis workflows, cell type annotation is typically performed downstream of preprocessing and clustering [2]. Clustering serves as a key step that partitions heterogeneous tissues into transcriptionally distinct subsets, which are then manually annotated using literature-derived canonical marker genes [3, 4]. However, this process is not only time-consuming but also difficult to reproduce, so automated approaches are increasingly adopted [4].

To improve the substantial time and expertise demands inherent in manual cell type annotation of scRNA-seq data, numerous computational approaches have been developed. These automated methods utilize on diverse strategies, including marker-based, reference-based, supervised classification, and deep learning, to achieve accurate and scalable cell type identification.

Tools that utilize marker-based automatic annotation such as Garnett [5], SCINA [6], and ScType [7] assign cell labels based on the characteristic expression of predefined markers [8]. Although these approaches can abstain from uncertain predictions when marker evidence is insufficient, their applicability is often limited by incomplete marker resources across tissues. Reference-based methods, including scmap [9] and SingleR [10], instead annotate cells by matching expression profiles to labeled reference datasets [8]. Supervised learning frameworks such as CellTypist extend this strategy by training machine-learning models on large reference atlases and providing hierarchical label sets for flexible annotation [11]. More recently, deep learning approaches such as scBERT leverage large-scale pretraining to model gene expression patterns in single-cell data [12]. Despite these advances, many approaches remain sensitive to feature selection and data quality, which can limit their ability to capture subtle biological variation [12, 13].

Recent advances have incorporated large language models (LLMs), opening new possibilities for cell type interpretation. Unlike methods that rely solely on expression matrices, LLMs leverage large-scale pretraining and generative pretrained transformers (GPT) to enable reasoning-based inference and contextual knowledge integration [14, 15]. Benchmarking studies show that GPT models can infer cell types with expert-level accuracy when provided with marker gene inputs, highlighting their practical utility for single-cell analysis [16].

Alongside these developments, several frameworks have further extended LLM-based annotation. LICT introduces a reliability evaluation framework combining multi-model integration and iterative feedback to reconcile LLM predictions with expert annotations [17]. Similarly, mLLMCelltype adopts a multi-LLM consensus strategy to reduce model bias and quantify uncertainty [18]. Agent-based systems such as CASSIA further decompose annotation into specialized reasoning steps, including annotation, validation, and interpretability modules [19]. Together, these approaches highlight a shift from single-pass prompting toward more structured and reliability-aware LLM-based annotation frameworks.

Despite recent advances, most current LLM workflows still inherit a key limitation from traditional pipelines by relying on cluster-specific markers derived from differential expression statistics [20]. Such markers can be unstable or weakly discriminative when clusters are transcriptionally similar or when background heterogeneity is high [15]. To mitigate this issue, several resources curate tissue-specific marker genes. Manually curated databases include singleCellBase, a literature-derived compendium supporting standardized cell type annotation across species [21], and CellMarker 2.0, which integrates markers from scRNA-seq datasets and published studies to provide expanded and updated coverage [22]. Complementarily, MarkerGeneBERT applies an NLP-based pipeline to automatically extract and normalize marker associations from biomedical literature [23]. While these resources highlight the importance of tissue-specific marker knowledge for cell type annotation, their largely static nature limits the incorporation of newly emerging biological information. To address this limitation, we integrate expert-curated and automatically extracted marker resources within a dynamically updatable framework.

Here, we propose CELLIA (Curated tissue-specific knowledge and evidence Integration for LLM-based cell type Annotation), an LLM-based cell type annotation workflow that uses an integrative evidence-knowledge for marker selection. In this framework, “evidence” refers to combined statistical differential expression criteria, including adjusted P-value (p-value), average \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log_2$$\end{document} fold-change (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log_2\textrm{FC}$$\end{document}), and expression proportion, whereas “knowledge” denote curated tissue-specific marker database derived from prior biological studies.

CELLIA comprises of two main components: (i) an integrative evidence-knowledge marker selection that prioritize biologically grounded and statistically supported markers for accurate annotation, and (ii) an interactive web interface for reviewing and visualizing annotation results. By integrating evidence-knowledge marker selection, CELLIA moves beyond simple cluster-level heuristics and establishes a more consistent and context-aware basis for LLM-based cell type annotation.

Methods

Overview of CELLIA

The workflow of CELLIA is straightforward. First, CELLIA operates on an scRNA-seq dataset at the cluster level. It identifies differentially expressed gene (DEG) for each cluster (Fig. 1; Step A). To extract tissue-specific markers, CELLIA cross-references each cluster’s DEG list with tissue-specific marker databases and retains the top-k markers (Fig. 1; Step B and C). A structured prompt is then constructed by combining the selected markers with the tissue context and submitted to the LLM for annotation (Fig. 1; Step D). Throughout this entire framework, CELLIA leverages the AnnData. Widely adopted in scRNA-seq analysis, it enables efficient storage of annotated matrices and handling of large-scale datasets [24]. Finally, annotation results can be interactively explored through a web-based interface without requiring computational expertise (Fig. 1; Step E).Fig. 1Overview of CELLIA. Step A. Identification of DEG for each cluster

In scRNA-seq analysis, DEG is defined as those showing statistically significant expression differences between groups [25]. Marker genes represent a more selective subset of DEG that also exhibit cluster specificity and biological interpretability beyond statistical significance [25].

DEG is identified for each cluster using Scanpy’s gene ranking function to characterize cluster-level transcriptional programs [26]. Genes are ranked using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and filtered by p-value and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log_2\textrm{FC}$$\end{document}. To further refined DEG, we incorporate a percentage metric comprising the proportion of cells within the target cluster (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{pct}.1$$\end{document}) and in other clusters (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{pct}.2$$\end{document}). Step B. Filtering DEG using curated marker database

To prioritize biologically meaningful markers, candidate DEGs are filtered using a curated marker database constructed for the CELLIA framework. The database integrates three complementary tissue-specific marker resources: singleCellBase, CellMarker 2.0, and MarkerGeneBERT. These resources combine manually curated expert knowledge with markers automatically extracted from the biomedical literature, providing both reliable reference information and coverage of newly reported markers.

Candidate DEG is cross-referenced with tissue-specific markers complied from three complementary resources. This step retains genes supported by published, tissue-specific annotations, thereby refining each cluster’s DEG list to markers that are biologically plausible and contextually relevant.

Step C. Selection of the top-k markers

From this refined list, the top k markers are selected to represent each cluster. In cases where fewer than k markers are available, the marker set is supplemented with the remaining DEG (i.e., those excluded during database cross-referencing) exhibiting the highest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log_2\textrm{FC}$$\end{document} until the target size is reached. This approach ensures that each cluster is characterized by a balanced combination of validated tissue-specific markers and data-driven candidates. For our framework, we set k=15, as this value yielded stable and accurate annotation performance.

Step D. LLM-based cell type annotation

CELLIA takes the top 15 markers (from Step C) and the tissue context as input, then constructs a structured prompt based on the template (Fig. 1). In this prompt, ‘marker_gene_str’ represents a list of markers, and ‘tissue_type’ and ‘parent_celltype’ are variables that are replaced with the actual name of the tissue (e.g., human PBMC) and the corresponding major cell group (e.g., B cell). Genes belonging to the same cell population are separated by commas.

The LLM was queried via its API using the constructed prompt. The returned response was programmatically parsed to extract two key components: (i) the predicted cell type annotation and (ii) a subset of highly informative markers with their associated biological rationales. This structured parsing enabled simultaneous retrieval of both annotation results and interpretable reasoning traces underlying the LLM’s decision. Specifically, the result included key markers and concise explanations describing their functional roles in supporting the assigned identity. Finally, these results are integrated back into the AnnData as input and exported in JSON and CSV formats to ensure reproducibility and accessibility through the web-based interface (Step E).

Step E. Interactive visualization

After completing the CELLIA workflow, the CELLIA’s results are supplied to the web interface for visualization and review. Results

All analyses were conducted using publicly available datasets. We benchmarked CELLIA nine different tissue datasets across eight tissue types—colorectal cancer (CRC; GSE132257), lung (GSE131907), breast (GSE167036), peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs; Human Immune Health Atlas (HIHA) [27]; and 10X), liver (GSM4008623), kidney (GSM4008620), skin (GSM4008710), and pancreas (GSM4008637)—representing 55 unique cell types. Evaluation metrics for cell type annotation

We compared CELLIA against representative automated scRNA-seq cell type annotation tools, including GPTCelltype, CASSIA, LICT, mLLMCelltype, CellTypist, SingleR, and Seurat. All methods were executed using their recommended configurations. For each dataset, we followed the procedures described in the original publications or, when available, used the normalized matrices provided by the authors.

Predicted cell type annotations by CELLIA and the baseline methods were compared against the manual labels provided in the original studies using a three-level agreement scheme adapted from established benchmarking practice [16]. For each evaluated cluster, the predicted label and the corresponding manual label were first matched to an unambiguous Cell Ontology (CL) term and, when applicable, to a broader cell type category. A prediction was classified as fully match if it corresponded to the same annotation term, the same CL term, or a more specific subtype of the manual label. For example, a prediction of CD4+ T cell or CD8+ T cell was considered fully matched when the manual label was T cell. A prediction was classified as partially match if it belonged to the same broad cell type category but differed in annotation and CL term, including cases in which the predicted label was broader than the manual label. For example, a prediction of T cell was considered partially matched when the manual label was CD4+ T cell. A prediction was classified as mismatch if the predicted and manual labels differed in broad cell type category, annotation term, and CL term.

To enable quantitative comparison, each evaluated cluster i was assigned an agreement score: 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{agreement}\_\mathrm{score}_i = \begin{cases}1, & \text{if fully match}\\0.5, & \text{if partially match} \\0, & \text{if mismatch}\end{cases}$$\end{document}

For a given dataset, the proportion assigned to each agreement category a was calculated at the cluster level as: 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{proportion}\ (a) = \frac{\text{number of cell types in }a}{\text{total number of cell types}}$$\end{document}

where a ∈ {fully match, partially match, mismatch}.

To summarize overall concordance within each dataset, we calculated the average agreement score across all evaluated clusters: 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{average agreement} = \frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\mathrm{agreement}\_\mathrm{score}_i$$\end{document}

where N is the total number of evaluated clusters in the dataset.

Both the agreement proportions and the average agreement score were therefore calculated using clusters as the evaluation unit. When a manually annotated cell type was represented by multiple clusters, each cluster was scored separately and contributed independently to the dataset-level summary. These metrics summarize annotation outcomes at the cluster level and provide a dataset-level average of agreement.

Comparison with existing annotation methods

We benchmarked CELLIA against three traditional computational methods (CellTypist, SingleR, and Seurat) and four recent LLM-based frameworks (GPTCelltype, CASSIA, LIcT, and mLLMCelltype) using the same pre-clustered, normalized single-cell datasets. This design was intended to focus the comparison on the annotation step while controlling for differences in the input clustering results. For cell-level annotation methods, including CellTypist, SingleR, and Seurat, predictions were first generated at the cell level and then aggregated into cluster-level labels by majority voting for comparison within the cluster-level benchmark. LLM-based methods were evaluated on the same clustered input data following their original annotation workflows. Detailed comparison results for each method are provided in Supplementary Tables 1–9.

Traditional computational methods

CELLIA showed overall favorable performance relative to traditional methods in both average agreement scores and the overall distribution of agreement categories across the evaluated datasets (Fig. 3a). While these tools provide rapid and scalable cell identity assignment, their performance can be sensitive to biological context particularly in complex or heterogeneous tissues. Evaluation of Seurat, SingleR, and CellTypist on the PBMC (HIHA) dataset could not be completed. Seurat and SingleR failed during conversion of the input dataset into their respective R-based object formats, before reference mapping or correlation scoring. CellTypist terminated with a process-level error during execution. All three failures were consistent with memory exhaustion given the limitations of the available shared computing resources (1 TB memory; see Supplementary Table 16 for details). A Seurat-based label transfer baseline was otherwise evaluated only for the lung, kidney, PBMC (10X), and pancreas datasets, for which compatible reference atlases were available. LLM-based methods

To evaluate performance across different LLMs, we benchmarked CELLIA using two models: GPT-4.1 (June 2024) and Gemini 2.5 Flash (June 2025).

Across the benchmark datasets, CELLIA showed generally strong agreement with manual labels (Fig. 3b). The relative differences between methods were modest overall and varied across datasets. Most methods showed broadly similar agreement patterns, although some dataset-specific differences in agreement composition were observed in kidney and immune-related populations. Overall, CELLIA remained comparable to the other LLM-based methods. Supplementary Figure 1 is presented to show dataset-specific agreement patterns. In some datasets, CELLIA showed a higher proportion of fully matched annotations or a lower proportion of mismatches (Supplementary Tables 1–9).

To further examine the effect of cluster definition on annotation results, we additionally evaluated CELLIA and the baseline LLM-based methods under alternative Leiden clustering resolutions (0.05, 0.1, and 0.3). Because changing clustering resolution alters cluster number and composition, each newly generated Leiden cluster was assigned a reference label by majority voting over the original manual clusters contained within it. These additional analyses suggested that the influence of clustering resolution varied across datasets, with some settings showing relatively similar agreement across resolutions and others showing more variation, particularly at higher resolution. A similar tendency was observed in subtype-level analyses, where finer-grained annotation produced more variable agreement patterns across resolutions. The corresponding major cell-type and subtype-level results are shown in Supplementary Figures 2–5.

Ablation analysis of marker selection

To systematically evaluate how input marker list affect LLM performance, we benchmarked several key parameters for cell type assignment. Each statistical criterion is detailed in the methods section. Ablation analyses are summarized in Supplementary Tables 10 and 11.

Number of genes

We evaluated the impact of the number of top genes provided to the LLM annotation performance, testing input sizes of 10, 15, 30, 50, 70, and 100 genes under the CELLIA gene filtering method (Fig. 3c). Performance was broadly stable across input sizes, although slight decreases were observed in breast and kidney datasets as the number of top-ranked genes increased.

Subtype-level cell type annotation

Reliable annotation at the subtype level remains challenging, particularly for rare or less well-characterized populations, which often lack well-established markers or reference datasets [29]. We therefore extended our evaluation beyond major cell type annotation to assess LLM performance at the subtype level. For subtype-level benchmarking, we primarily used the PBMC subtype dataset from the Human Immune Health Atlas, in which major immune cell classes were selectively subset and iteratively reprocessed by reclustering. Specific subpopulations were first identified within each major class using marker-based criteria, and each selected subset was subsequently reanalyzed with subtype-specific Leiden resolutions. To additionally examine a non-immune subtype setting, we also included fibroblasts from the NSCLC subset of the human perturbed-state fibroblast atlas reported by Buechler et al. [28].

Subtype-level marker genes were than selected from the resulting subtype clusters using the following criteria: adjusted p-value < 0.1, pct.1 > 0.25, pct.2 < 0.7, and average \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\log_2\textrm{FC}$$\end{document} > 0.3. These subtype-level annotation analyses, including gene filtering ablation experiment and comparisons with existing LLM-based annotation methods, are presented in Supplementary Tables 12 and 13.

Comparison with existing LLM-based methods

We evaluated subtype-level annotation across closely related immune populations and an additional non-immune fibroblast subtype setting, and compared CELLIA with recent LLM-based annotation frameworks. Across T cell subtypes (CD4T, DNT, and Treg), CELLIA consistently achieved high agreement with manual annotations, while alternative approaches frequently produced partial matches or incorrect assignments (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 13). Similarly, for dendritic cell subtypes, CELLIA showed higher agreement and maintained stable performance (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 13). Collectively, these results indicate that CELLIA can support fine-grained subtype annotation not only in closely related immune populations but also in an additional non-immune stromal setting, although performance remained dependent on the biological context and subtype granularity.

Reproducibility of CELLIA annotation

We assessed consistency and reproducibility of LLM-based annotations by repeating CELLIA predictions 10 times across 9 datasets (Supplementary Table 14 and 15). Across tissues, predictions were identical for the vast majority of clusters, with residual variation largely confined to nomenclature (e.g., “NK cell” and “Natural Killer (NK) cell”) rather than substantive changes in cell identity. Notably, these subtle discrepancies mirror the inconsistencies inherent in manual annotations, reflecting the broader absence of standardized naming conventions in single-cell studies, where cell type nomenclature is often individual investigator-defined and taxonomic alignment remains limited [30]. Rare variations were instead observed primarily in biologically similar contexts. For example, in PBMC (10X) dataset, the first and second runs yielded “Monocyte” in place of “Classical Monocyte”, consistent with the subtype-level (Supplementary Table 14). We also evaluated CELLIA using multiple API models, including GPT-5.2 (Dec 2025), GPT-5.4 (Mar 2026), GPT-5.4 mini (Mar 2026), and GPT-5.4 nano (Mar 2026), and observed broadly similar annotation patterns across models (Supplementary Table 15). This suggests that the overall annotation behavior of CELLIA is preserved not only across repeated inference runs under a fixed setup, but also across different API model settings, with the remaining variation largely limited to semantic synonyms or differences in nomenclature rather than substantive changes in cell identity.

Discussion

Cell type annotation is essential for interpreting single-cell analysis, yet manual annotation remains time-consuming and dependent on expert knowledge. To overcome these limitations, automated and LLM-based approaches have expanded rapidly, interpretation of cluster-specific marker gene often still involves manual choices, including which markers to prioritize and how tissue context should be incorporated. CELLIA was designed in part to make this preparatory step more structured by filtering and prioritizing marker genes using a curated tissue-specific marker database together with tissue context before the evidence is presented to the LLM.

This evidence-knowledge integration strategy forms the basis of CELLIA. The database combines manually curated and automatically extracted tissue-specific marker resources, allowing biologically relevant evidence to be organized in a more structured form prior to annotation. In this sense, the database functions not only as a source of marker information, but also as part of a framework for making the preparation of annotation evidence more consistent.

Benchmarking showed that CELLIA achieved strong overall agreement with manual annotations across datasets, although differences from other recent LLM-based methods were generally modest. Gene filtering analyses provide context for this result. Removing either the knowledge-based or evidence-based component reduced agreement across datasets, with the largest reduction observed when both were removed, suggesting that the two components provide complementary information. Taken together, these findings suggest that the contribution of CELLIA lies in part in how marker evidence is filtered and organized before LLM-based interpretation. This does not imply that differential expression statistics alone are insufficient, but it does suggest that their interpretation can benefit from additional biological context.

CELLIA is a knowledge-guided annotation framework that prioritizes database-supported markers during cluster interpretation. This design may be particularly useful in well-characterized tissues, where marker knowledge is relatively well established, but may be more limited in out-of-distribution settings, such as rare tissue states, transitional populations, unusual tumor microenvironments, or non-model species, where marker coverage is sparse. In these contexts, biologically meaningful but less-characterized markers may be deprioritized, potentially biasing annotation toward better-represented canonical labels.

Accordingly, CELLIA is intended for knowledge-guided annotation rather than de novo identification of novel cell types. When available evidence is insufficient for confident assignment to a known cell identity, retaining a cluster as unknown is more appropriate than assigning it to the nearest canonical label. Applications in database-poor contexts therefore require more cautious interpretation.

The current workflow assumes pre-clustered input data and does not include the initial clustering step. Because CELLIA derives the DEG list, marker filtering, top-k marker selection, and final prompt from cluster-level definitions, clustering directly shapes the input evidence used for annotation. An additional sensitivity analysis using alternative clustering algorithms and resolutions showed that some datasets remained highly stable across low resolution to moderate resolution settings, whereas others showed greater sensitivity, particularly under stronger cluster splitting. These findings indicate that CELLIA is not invariant to clustering strategy or resolution, even though it can remain stable under some clustering conditions. Annotation results should therefore be interpreted together with the clustering procedure used to define the input clusters. Future work may focus on closer integration with clustering pipelines and continued expansion of curated marker resources.
