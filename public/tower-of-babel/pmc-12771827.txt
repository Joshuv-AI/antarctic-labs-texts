Low pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) uptake and high prevalence of sexually transmitted infections among men who have sex with men in Zhejiang Province, China: a large cross-sectional survey
Authors: Rui Ge, Chengliang Chai, Guoying Zhu, Lin He, Zhongwen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) demonstrates substantial efficacy in preventing human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) among men who have sex with men (MSM). However, its promotion faces barriers, with implementation raising concerns about declining condom use and a potential increase in the incidence of sexually transmitted infections (STIs). This study investigated the factors associated with PrEP uptake and STIs status among MSM to inform strategies for PrEP implementation in this group.MethodsFrom July to December 2023, a cross-sectional study was conducted among MSM in Zhejiang Province, China. Data on demographic characteristics, PrEP knowledge and uptake, sexual behaviors, and HIV testing were collected using online questionnaires. Chi-square tests were used to compare the differences between the PrEP uptake and non-uptake groups, and logistic regression was applied to analyze the associated factors with PrEP uptake.ResultsA total of 5816 MSM who met the inclusion criteria were recruited, of whom 321 (5.5%, 95% CI: 4.92%–6.08%) reported PrEP uptake. Multivariate logistic regression identified key factors associated with PrEP uptake. Positively associated factors included higher education (junior college: adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 1.54, 95% CI: 1.02–2.33, P = 0.042; bachelor’s degree: aOR: 1.81, 95% CI: 1.22–2.69, P = 0.004; master’s and above: aOR: 2.50, 95% CI: 1.55–4.04, P < 0.001), having two or more sexual partners (aOR: 1.91, 95% CI: 1.17–3.10, P = 0.009), occasional condom use (aOR: 1.83, 95% CI: 1.42–2.35, P < 0.001), frequent (aOR: 2.11, 95% CI: 1.59–2.80, P < 0.001) or occasional (aOR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.02–1.88, P = 0.035) rush popper use, and STI history (aOR: 2.82, 95% CI: 1.71–4.64, P < 0.001). Conversely, freelance occupation was inversely associated with uptake (aOR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.43–0.99, P = 0.046).ConclusionsThe prevalence of PrEP uptake among MSM in Zhejiang Province remains relatively low. Factors including lower educational level, freelance employment, multiple sexual partnerships, occasional condom use, and rush popper use were correlated with uptake. Importantly, individuals with a history of STIs were more likely to use PrEP, underscoring the need to integrate STI screening into routine PrEP services. These findings provide a critical evidence base for promoting PrEP and for developing tailored HIV/STI prevention strategies targeting MSM.

Introduction

Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) is a severe infectious disease that endangers global public health and poses substantial challenges to health security. Globally, it is estimated that 1.3 million people will acquire human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) in 2024, bringing the total number of people living with HIV (PLHIV) to 40.8 million [1]. Since the onset of the HIV epidemic in the 1980 s, men who have sex with men (MSM) have been recognized as one of the most heavily affected populations due to factors such as social stigma, discrimination, barriers to healthcare access, and high-risk sexual behaviors [2]. In mainland China, the MSM population is estimated at 8.3 million [3]. In China, MSM remain the highest-risk group for HIV infection, with an overall HIV prevalence of 2.5–6.5% [4]. Among 1,131,464 newly diagnosed HIV cases reported between 2015 and 2023, homosexual contact accounted for 25.2% of transmissions [5].

HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is one of the most effective interventions for HIV prevention, and the World Health Organization recommends its use for individuals at substantial risk of infection. As an antiretroviral-based prevention strategy, PrEP reduces the risk of HIV acquisition by up to 99% via sexual contact, and by at least 74% among people who inject drugs [6]. PrEP can be administered through daily oral intake, “on-demand” oral use, or long-acting injectable formulations administered every 2 or 6 months, depending on client preference [7]. Many high-income countries have integrated PrEP into their public health responses to HIV, with reimbursement policies that prioritize access for high-risk groups, particularly MSM [8]. Despite global implementation, PrEP uptake among MSM varies significantly across countries and regions [9]. In China, awareness of PrEP among MSM remains insufficient, with uptake and adherence rates extremely low [10]. The pooled prevalence of PrEP uptake among this group is only 4.9% [11].

Several factors impede PrEP uptake. For example, educational level may influence MSM awareness of PrEP [12], whereas previous studies have linked PrEP-related outcomes to chemsex engagement, condom use, and sexual behavior patterns [13–15]. Furthermore, PrEP uptake may alter sexual behaviors; the perception of PrEP-mediated HIV protection can lead to reduced condom use and more frequent sexual activity [16]. This risk compensation may increase STI incidence, given the overlapping transmission routes of HIV and STIs [17].

To further explore the associated factors with PrEP uptake among MSM and to clarify the current burden of STIs in this population, we conducted a large-scale Internet-based survey in Zhejiang Province, China. Zhejiang, one of China’s most economically developed provinces, is located on the southeastern coast of the Yangtze River Delta. In Zhejiang, HIV prevalence is estimated at 0.05%, with approximately 28,520 PLHIV [18]. MSM account for over 40% of newly diagnosed HIV cases annually [19]– [20]. This study will provide evidence to guide PrEP promotion strategies and to inform the development of STI screening protocols for MSM, both before and after PrEP initiation.

Methods

Study design and population

From July to December 2023, we conducted a cross-sectional study involving a large-scale survey among MSM in Zhejiang Province, China. Participants were recruited via convenience sampling by the Sunshine Coast Public Welfare Organization, a non-governmental organization (NGO) that currently has 25 social workers, over 400 registered volunteers, and service points across various cities in Zhejiang Province. The NGO has established a digital HIV prevention service platform using location-based Internet services, through which electronic questionnaires were distributed online in this study. Volunteers recruited participants using online HIV testing platforms, dating applications (e.g., Blued), and instant messaging software (e.g., WeChat). Eligible individuals signed electronic informed consent forms prior to participation; those who agreed to participate were then directed to the platform to complete the electronic questionnaires. All participants took part in the survey voluntarily and without compensation. Additionally, participants could apply for free HIV testing services and preventive counseling through the organization’s official WeChat account “Sunshine Testing,” with options to receive self-test kits by mail or undergo testing at offline service points.

Eligible participants in the study met the following inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 16 years; (2) reported having sex with men within the past year; (3) awareness of PrEP; (4) HIV-negative antibody test result or unknown HIV status; (5) receipt of HIV testing through mobile social media promotion or volunteer-driven recruitment. Exclusion criteria included: (1) unaware of PrEP; (2) inability to complete informed consent due to intellectual or mental impairments.

Questionnaires and measurements

The questionnaire was used to collect information on participants’ demographic characteristics, PrEP knowledge and uptake, sexual behaviors, and HIV testing history. Lin He et al. developed the questionnaire, which has been used in the published papers of the research team [20]– [21]. Demographic variables included: age (< 20, 20–29, or ≥ 30 years); marital status (single, married, or divorced/separated); education level (high school or below, junior college, bachelor’s degree, or master’s degree and above); monthly income (< 350 USD, 350–699 USD, 700–1399 USD, or ≥ 1400 USD); and occupation (company employee, student, freelance, or other). Participants were asked about awareness and uptake of HIV-PrEP (Yes or No). Self-reported sexual behaviors and HIV/STIs-related information within the past 3 months were also collected: sexual role (bottom, versatile, or top); bisexual (Yes or No); number of sexual partners (0, 1, or ≥ 2); condom use (frequent use, occasional use, or never); frequency of rush poppers use during sexual activity (frequent use, occasional use, or never); awareness of partner’s HIV status (yes, unsure, or no); history of STI diagnosis in the past 3 months (such as syphilis, gonorrhea, condyloma acuminatum, or genital herpes); HIV testing pathway (mailed self-testing or offline testing); HIV status (positive or negative); and whether the current test was their first HIV test (Yes or No).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed to summarize the demographic and epidemiological characteristics of participants. Quantitative variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{x}$$\end{document}±s), while qualitative variables were presented as counts and proportions. Categorical data were compared using the Chi-square test. Participants were categorized by PrEP uptake (1 = yes, 0 = no). Variables with P < 0.2 in the univariate regression analysis were included as independent variables in the multivariate logistic regression model. R software (version 4.4.2) was utilized for the statistical analysis. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 9568 MSM were recruited between July and December 2023, of whom 5816 met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled. Among these, 321 (5.5%, 95% CI: 4.92%–6.08%) reported using PrEP. PrEP uptake according to sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics

Factors associated with PrEP uptake among study participants

Variables showing a crude association with PrEP uptake in univariate analysis (inclusion criterion: P < 0.2) included marital status, education level, income, occupation, sexual role, bisexuality, number of sexual partners, condom use, rush popper use, awareness of partner’s HIV status, and history of STIs. These variables were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model, which identified several factors significantly associated with PrEP use as presented in Table 3. Compared to those with a high school education and below, participants with a junior college degree (adjusted Odds Ratio [aOR]: 1.54, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 1.02–2.33, P = 0.042), bachelor’s degree (aOR: 1.81, 95% CI: 1.22–2.69, P = 0.004), or master’s degree and above (aOR: 2.50, 95% CI: 1.55–4.04, P < 0.001) were more likely to use PrEP. Conversely, freelancers were less likely to use PrEP than those in other occupations (aOR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.43–0.99, P = 0.046). Participants with two or more sexual partners were more likely to use PrEP than those with no sexual partners (aOR, 1.91; 95% CI: 1.17–3.10, P = 0.009). Compared to participants who frequently used condoms, those who used them occasionally were also more likely to use PrEP (aOR: 1.83, 95% CI: 1.42–2.35, P < 0.001). In addition, participants who used rush poppers frequently (aOR: 2.11, 95% CI: 1.59–2.80, P < 0.001) or occasionally (aOR: 1.39, 95% CI: 1.02–1.88, P = 0.035) were more likely to use PrEP than those who never used them. STI history was also associated with a higher PrEP uptake (aOR: 2.82, 95% CI: 1.71–4.64, P < 0.001). Discussion

Over the past four decades, China has implemented various AIDS prevention and control strategies. These include formulating relevant laws and regulations, establishing a national AIDS epidemic monitoring and reporting system, implementing a free antiviral treatment policy, and increasing medical insurance reimbursement rates. Although China has made significant progress in AIDS prevention and control, it continues to face challenges such as a high risk of transmission among MSM [5, 22]. Pilot programs for PrEP services among MSM were launched in 2017, and technical guidelines for PrEP implementation were released in 2020. However, the overall effectiveness of these efforts has been suboptimal [23]– [24]. This study presents a large-scale cross-sectional survey of MSM in the eastern coastal region of China, which enhances our understanding of sexual behaviors and HIV/STI status within this population. Notably, the survey was restricted to MSM who were already aware of PrEP. By comparing the disparities between PrEP uptake and non-uptake, this design facilitated a more targeted exploration of the factors underlying the gap between PrEP knowledge and actual use. The proportion of PrEP uptake among MSM in eastern China was lower than that observed in high-income countries such as the United States (17.4%), and in emerging economies such as Brazil (21.4%) [9, 25]– [26].

Our study identified several factors associated with PrEP uptake. Findings revealed that superficial awareness of PrEP among MSM did not necessarily translate into use [10]. This may be attributable to limited understanding of PrEP and a low perceived risk of HIV, which together foster reluctance to initiate or difficulties in correct use [27]. Analysis further revealed that participants with higher educational levels were more inclined to use PrEP. These findings suggest that health education for MSM should focus on essential knowledge about PrEP, including its preventive benefits, safe medication practices, and the fact that PrEP does not prevent STIs or HCV [17]. Compared to participants in other occupations, freelancers exhibited a lower tendency to use PrEP. Specifically, occupational instability, income volatility, and frequent residential relocations among freelancers within this population constitute significant objective barriers to accessing PrEP services. These barriers primarily manifest as interruptions in service continuity, heightened financial burden, and increased challenges in acquiring accurate information and establishing trust [28]– [29]. Future PrEP services for vulnerable MSM subgroups could be improved in two ways. First, PrEP awareness should be expanded through internet-based campaigns, and access to medication should be broadened through online pharmacies to improve service accessibility. Second, PrEP medications should be incorporated into medical insurance schemes to reduce financial barriers.

This study extends prior research by confirming four factors linked to higher PrEP uptake among participants, reinforcing the role of risk awareness and sexual behavior in guiding HIV prevention choices. Participants who reported having two or more sexual partners were more likely to use PrEP, which aligns with PrEP’s purpose as a risk-targeted prevention strategy, given the increased risk of unprotected sex or partners with unknown HIV status [6]. These findings also support the notion that PrEP uptake often follows accurate self-assessment of risk, consistent with earlier studies in diverse populations [25, 30]– [31]. Occasional condom use was associated with greater PrEP uptake, which may be attributed to the psychology of risk compensation—that is, individuals using PrEP may be more willing to forgo condom use [17, 32]. This reflects a form of self-managed risk reduction adopted by MSM. Individuals who face barriers to consistent condom use may adopt PrEP as an alternative preventive measure. However, a “combination prevention” approach—using both condoms and PrEP—not only provides more robust protection against HIV infection but also reduces the risk of other STIs [16]. This dual protective benefit should be strongly emphasized in clinical guidance. Frequent and occasional use of rush poppers was correlated with higher PrEP uptake. Although rush poppers enhance sexual pleasure, they are linked to riskier behaviors through both physiological effects and impaired judgment [33]. The positive association observed here suggests that users recognize elevated HIV risk and seek PrEP as a mitigation strategy, consistent with patterns reported in other high-risk groups [34]. However, PrEP does not address the broader harms associated with popper use; therefore, interventions should combine PrEP provision with targeted harm-reduction counseling.​.

Notably, a history of STIs was strongly associated with PrEP uptake. STI history is a well-established marker of elevated HIV risk, as many STIs cause genital mucosal damage that facilitates viral transmission. Specifically, infections such as chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis can trigger localized inflammation, ulceration, or erosion of the genital, anal, or oral mucosa, which normally serves as the first line of defense against HIV. This mucosal damage compromises the integrity of the protective barriers, creates micro-tears, and increases the density of immune cells at the site of infection—cells that HIV specifically targets for entry [35]– [36]. Beyond serving as a biological marker of HIV risk, STI history also influences an individual’s risk perception and preventive behaviors, which are key drivers of PrEP adoption. An STI diagnosis acts as a “risk awakening,” transforming abstract concerns about HIV into a tangible threat and prompting individuals to reevaluate their sexual health practices and seek proactive protections. This dynamic is especially relevant when STI care is integrated with HIV-prevention services, allowing providers to use STI diagnosis as a teachable moment to discuss PrEP eligibility and benefits [37].

Furthermore, these findings underscore the value of STI history as a pragmatic and objective indicator of PrEP eligibility in both clinical and public health settings [38]. Traditional PrEP eligibility criteria often rely on self-reported sexual behaviors, which are prone to underreporting owing to stigma, memory bias, or discomfort with disclosing personal details. In contrast, STI history is a verifiable clinical outcome documented in electronic health records or laboratory reports, minimizing subjectivity. Targeting individuals with a history of STIs also presents a strategic opportunity to expand the reach and impact of HIV-prevention programs. Globally, STI clinics and sexual health services already serve as entry points for populations at elevated HIV risk, including individuals who are sexually active, have multiple partners, or engage in behaviors that increase vulnerability to both STIs and HIV [39]. Integrating PrEP services into STI care settings allows public health programs to leverage “one-stop shopping” models, thereby improving accessibility and efficiency [39]– [40]. The association between STI history and PrEP uptake is multifaceted: it reflects biological vulnerability to HIV, shapes individual risk perception, and provides a practical tool for clinical eligibility screening. Integrating STI screening with PrEP delivery could enable public health systems to reach high-risk populations more precisely and advance progress toward ending the HIV epidemic.

Several limitations of this study need to be taken into account when interpreting its results. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, reliance on self-reported data may have introduced reporting bias, with social desirability bias being particularly prominent due to the sensitive nature of sexual behaviors. Participants might have underreported socially undesirable behaviors such as having multiple partners or engaging in unprotected intercourse, or conversely, overreported protective practices like consistent condom use, in order to align with social expectations. To mitigate this, we employed anonymous questionnaires, neutral phrasing, and pre-survey briefings that emphasized the importance of truthful responses. However, some residual bias is likely unavoidable; therefore, the self-reported behavioral data should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Third, unmeasured confounders may have influenced the results. Future research should adopt longitudinal designs to clarify the temporal relationships between these factors and PrEP uptake and to evaluate whether tailored interventions for high-risk groups improve PrEP adherence and reduce HIV incidence.

Conclusions

In summary, the prevalence of PrEP uptake among MSM in Zhejiang Province remains relatively low. Several factors were correlated with uptake in this population, including lower educational attainment, freelance employment status, multiple sexual partnerships, occasional condom use, and rush popper consumption. Notably, individuals with a history of STIs demonstrated a higher propensity to use PrEP, underscoring the necessity of integrating STI screening into routine care services for PrEP users. These findings provide both a robust research foundation for efforts to promote PrEP among MSM and actionable insights for developing targeted, context-specific strategies for HIV/STI screening and intervention in this priority population.
