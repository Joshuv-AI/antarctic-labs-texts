Symmetric Preparation of Systems
Nils Dencker Lund University

Abstract
. In this paper we generalize the Weierstrass and Malgrange preparation theorems to the symmetric matrix valued case, proving symmetric preparation of analytic and smooth symmetric systems that vanishes of first order.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 32B05 Secondary 47A56, 26B40

1.
Introduction and statement of results

If
f
is
C^{\infty}
satisfying
\partial_{t}f(0,0)\neq 0
and
f(0,0)=0
then by the Malgrange preparation theorem there exist
C^{\infty}
functions
c(t,x)
and
r(x)
such that
c(0,0)\neq 0
,
r(0)=0
and

(1.1)

f(t,x)=c(t,x)\left(t+r(x)\right)\qquad(t,x)\in\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}

locally near
(0,0)
. In the analytic case, the Weierstrass preparation theorem, one has uniqueness but not in the
C^{\infty}
case, except for real valued
f(x)
when one can use the implicit function theorem.
This also holds for higher order vanishing of
f
and there are corresponding division results.

The Malgrange preparation theorem has been generalized to
N\times N
systems of functions in
[
3
]
. The simplest example is for smooth
N\times N
system
F(t,x)
satisfying
|\partial_{t}F(0,0)|\neq 0
and
F(0,0)=0
. Then there exist smooth
N\times N
systems
C(t,x)
and
M(x)
such that
|C(0,0)|\neq 0
,
M(0)=0
and

(1.2)

F(t,x)=C(t,x)\left(t\operatorname{Id}_{N}+M(x)\right)\qquad(t,x)\in\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}

locally near
(0,0)
, see
[
2
]
. In the analytic case, one has uniqueness but not in the smooth case.

We also proved the following division theorem in
[
2
]
.
If
F(t,x)
is a smooth
N\times N
system, then for any smooth
N\times N
system
M(x)
there exist smooth
N\times N
systems
C(t,x)
and
R(x)
such that

(1.3)

F(t,x)=C(t,x)\left(t\operatorname{Id}+M(x)\right)+R(x)\qquad(t,x)\in\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}

locally near
(0,0)
. As before, one has uniqueness in the analytic case but not in the smooth case. We also have preparation results for higher order vanishing of
F
, adjoint preparation results and corresponding division results, see
[
3
]
.

Now, the question about symmetric preparation results was raised by Gregory Berkolaiko
[
1
]
. The simplest case is for smooth (or analytic) symmetric
N\times N
matrices
F(t,x)
satisfying
\partial_{t}F(0,0)>0
and
F(0,0)=0
. Then, does it exist smooth (or analytic)
N\times N
matrices
U(t,x)
and
M(x)
, such that
|U(0,0)|\neq 0
,
M(x)
is symmetric with
M(0)=0
satisfying

(1.4)

F(t,x)=U(t,x)\left(t\operatorname{Id}_{N}+M(x)\right)U^{*}(t,x)\qquad(t,x)\in\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}

near
(0,0)
, and is this preparation unique? Observe that the conditions on
F(t,x)
are necessary for (
1.4
) to hold since
U(0,0)U^{*}(0,0)=\partial_{t}F(0,0)>0
and
F(0,0)=U(0,0)M(0)U(0,0)^{*}=0
.
Here and in the following we say that the matrix
A
is symmetric if
A^{*}=A
and antisymmetric if
A^{*}=-A
, where
A^{*}=\overline{A}^{t}
.

Remark 1.1
.

Observe that the preparation (
1.4
) is not unique in general. In fact, if
U(t,x)
and M(x) are solutions to (
1.4
), then for any orthogonal
A(x)=(A^{-1})^{*}(x)
we find that
\widetilde{U}(t,x)=U(t,x)A(x)
and
\widetilde{M}(x)=A^{*}(x)M(x)A(x)
solves (
1.4
).

Actually, the preparation is unique in the case when
U
is symmetric and
U>0
, see Theorem
1.2
.
We are going to prove the following, which is the main result of the paper.

Theorem
1.2
.

Assume that
F(t,x)
is a smooth symmetric
N\times N
matrix valued function of
(t,x)\in\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}
such that

(1.5)

\partial_{t}F(0,0)>0\quad\text{and}\quad F(0,0)=0

Then there exists a neighborhood
\omega
of
(0,0)
and smooth
N\times N
matrix valued functions
U(t,x)
and
M(x)
such that
|U|\neq 0
,
M
is symmetric ,
M(0)=0
and

(1.6)

F(t,x)=U(t,x)\left(t\operatorname{Id}_{N}+M(x)\right)U^{*}(t,x)\qquad\text{in $\omega$}

If
F(t,x)
is analytic then we obtain (
1.6
) with analytic
U(t,x)
and
M(x)
. This preparation is unique if
U
is symmetric and
U>0
.

Remark 1.3
.

Observe that we also get uniqueness in other cases, for example if
U<0
by taking
-U
for for the unique preparations with
U>0
.
The proof of Theorem
1.2
also gives the preparation (
1.6
) for some
0\neq|F(0,0)|\ll 1
with
|M(0)|\ll 1
, but the bounds depend on the (semi)norms of
F
.

If
F(0,0)\neq 0
then by subtracting
F(0,0)
we obtain (
1.6
) with the remainder
F(0,0)
giving the following result.

Corollary 1.4
.

Assume that
F(t,x)
is a smooth symmetric
N\times N
matrix valued function of
(t,x)\in\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}
such that

(1.7)

\partial_{t}F(0,0)>0

Then there exists a neighborhood
\omega
of
(0,0)
and smooth
N\times N
matrix valued functions
U(t,x)
and
M(x)
such that
|U(0,0)|\neq 0
,
M(x)
is symmetric
\forall\,x
,
M(0)=0
and

(1.8)

F(t,x)=U(t,x)\left(t\operatorname{Id}_{N}+M(x)\right)U^{*}(t,x)+F(0,0)\qquad\text{in $\omega$}

If
F(t,x)
is analytic then we obtain (
1.8
) with analytic
U(t,x)
and
M(x)
. This preparation is unique if
U>0
.

Note that the uniqueness only holds for symmetric preparations, since Remark
1.1
gives counterexamples for nonsymmetric
U
.
Since (
1.6
) is not linear in
U(t,x)
the proof approaches of Weierstrass or Malgrange preparation results do not work in the symmetric case, instead we shall use inverse function theorems.
Theorem
1.2
will be proved in Section
3
for the analytic case, and in Section
4
for the smooth case. But first we shall consider the formal power series case.

2.
Formal power series solutions

If
F
is analytic we may solve (
1.4
) by taking the Taylor expansion of
F(t,x)
:

(2.1)

F(t,x)=\sum_{{j,\alpha}}F_{j,\alpha}t^{j}x^{\alpha}

where
F_{0,0}=F(0,0)=0
and
F_{1,0}=\partial_{t}F(0,0)>0
,
and take the formal power series expansions
U(t,x)=\sum_{{j,\alpha}}U_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,\alpha\end{subarray}}t^{j}x^{\alpha}
where
|U_{0,0}|\neq 0
and
M(x)=\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}M_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
is symmetric such that
M_{0}=M(0)=0
. Then (
1.4
) gives that

(2.2)

\sum_{{j,\alpha}}F_{j,\alpha}t^{j}x^{\alpha}=\sum_{{j,\alpha}}U_{j,\alpha}t^{j}x^{\alpha}\left(t\operatorname{Id}_{N}+\sum_{\gamma\neq 0}M_{\gamma}x^{\gamma}\right)\sum_{{k,\beta}}U_{k,\beta}^{*}t^{k}x^{\beta}

Simplifying the notations so that
\sum_{{j,\alpha}}A_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,\alpha\end{subarray}}t^{j}x^{\alpha}=\sum_{j,k}A_{j,k}t^{j}x^{k}
, we get

(2.3)

\sum_{j,k}F_{j,k}t^{j}x^{k}=\sum_{j,k}U_{j,k}t^{j}x^{k}\left(t\operatorname{Id}_{N}+\sum_{m>0}M_{m}x^{m}\right)\sum_{{i,\ell}}U_{i,\ell}^{*}t^{i}x^{\ell}

and by matching coefficients we obtain that

(2.4)

F_{j,k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
\ell\leq k\end{subarray}}U_{i,\ell}U_{j-1-i,k-\ell}^{*}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leq j\\
\ell\leq k-m\\
0<m\leq k\end{subarray}}U_{i,\ell}M_{m}U_{j-i,k-m-\ell}^{*}

The first terms of this expansion are
F_{0,0}=0
,
F_{0,1}=U_{0,0}M_{1}U_{0,0}^{*}
and
F_{1,0}=U_{0,0}U_{0,0}^{*}
which gives that
|U_{0,0}|\neq 0
and
M_{1}=U_{0,0}^{-1}F_{0,1}(U_{0,0}^{-1})^{*}
.

When
j=0
we obtain from (
2.4
) that

(2.5)

F_{0,k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\leq k-m\\
0<m\leq k\end{subarray}}U_{0,\ell}M_{m}U_{0,k-m-\ell}^{*}\qquad\forall\,k

so recursion gives

(2.6)

U_{0,0}M_{k}U_{0,0}^{*}=F_{0,k}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\ell\leq k-m\\
0<m<k\end{subarray}}U_{0,\ell}M_{m}U_{0,k-m-\ell}^{*}\qquad\forall\,k

We then obtain symmetric
M_{j}
by

(2.7)

M_{k}=U_{0,0}^{-1}F_{0,k}(U_{0,0}^{-1})^{*}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\ell\leq k-m\\
0<m<k\end{subarray}}U_{0,0}^{-1}U_{0,\ell}M_{m}U_{0,k-m-\ell}^{*}(U_{0,0}^{-1})^{*}\qquad\forall\,k

so
M_{k}
is determined by
F_{0,k}
,
U_{0,\ell}
with
\ell<k
and
M_{m}
with
m<k
.

In general, we obtain from (
2.4
) with
j
replaced by
j+1
that

(2.8)

2\operatorname{Re}U_{j,k}U_{0,0}^{*}=F_{j+1,k}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<i<j\\
\ell\leq k\end{subarray}}U_{i,\ell}U_{j-1-i,k-\ell}^{*}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leq j+1\\
\ell\leq k-m\\
0<m\leq k\end{subarray}}U_{i,\ell}M_{m}U_{j+1-i,k-m-\ell}^{*}

where the right hand side is determined by
F_{j+1,k}
,
M_{m}
with
m\leq k
determined by (
2.6
)
and
U_{i,\ell}
with
i<j
and
\ell\leq k
or

i\leq j+1
and
\ell\leq k-1
, which gives
i+\ell<j+k
and
\ell\leq k
or
i+\ell\leq j+k
and
\ell\leq k-1
.

Since
|U_{0,0}|\neq 0
, (
2.8
) can be solved to give
U_{j,k}
and by Lemma
A.1
we get a unique symmetric solution
U_{j,k}
when
U_{0,0}
is symmetric.
For
k=0
we find from (
2.8
) that

(2.9)

2\operatorname{Re}U_{j,0}U_{0,0}^{*}=F_{j+1,0}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<i<j\end{subarray}}U_{i,0}U_{j-1-i,0}^{*}

which gives
2\operatorname{Re}U_{1,0}U_{0,0}^{*}=F_{2,0}
. Since
|U_{0,0}|\neq 0
we obtain by induction
U_{j,0}
for any
j
from (
2.9
), and if
U_{0,0}
is symmetric then Lemma
A.1
gives a unique symmetric
U_{j,0}
.

Thus, one can determine
U_{j,k}
and
M_{k}
from (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) by induction over
j+k=p\geq 0
. For
p=0
we can take any
U_{0,0}
such that
U_{0,0}U_{0,0}^{*}=F_{1,0}>0
. Then for fixed
p>0
we get the values of
U_{p,0}
from (
2.9
) so we can do a recursion over
(p-r,r)
for
r=0
to
p
. If we have determined
U_{j,k}
for
j+k<p
and
U_{p-\ell,\ell}
for
\ell\leq r
for some
r<p
then by (
2.8
) we find that
U_{p-r-1,r+1}
only depends on
F_{p-r,r+1}
,
U_{i,\ell}
for
i+\ell\leq p
and
\ell\leq r
or
i+\ell<p
and
\ell\leq r+1
, which are previously determined, and on
M_{m}
with
m\leq r+1
which by (
2.7
) are given by
F_{0,m}
and
U_{0,\ell}
with
\ell\leq r<p
which also have been determined. By recursion over
r
and induction over
p
we get the expansion, and if
U_{0,0}
is symmetric we get unique symmetric
U_{j,k}
by (
2.8
) and Lemma
A.1
, which gives the following result.

Theorem
2.1
.

If
F(t,x)
is analytic and symmetric such that
F(0,0)>0
and
F(0,0)=0
then there exists formal power series
U(t,x)
and
M(x)
solving (
1.4
) modulo
O(|t|^{N}+|x|^{N})
,
\forall\,N
.
We get a unique power series solution in the case when
U
is symmetric.

But is it not clear that the formal power expansion converge to an analytic function.

Remark 2.2
.

If
n=1
and
F
is analytic, then by
[
1
]
the nested Artin approximation
(see e.g. Theorem 5.18 in
[
6
]
) gives analytic
\overline{U}(x,y)=\sum_{j,k}\overline{U}_{jk}t^{j}x^{k}
and
\overline{M}(x)=\sum_{k}\overline{M}_{k}x^{k}=\overline{M}^{*}(x)
that solves

F(t,x)=\overline{U}(t,x)(tId+\overline{M}(x))\overline{U}^{*}(t,x)\qquad(t,x)\in\mathbf{R}^{2}

which gives Theorem
1.2
in the two dimensional analytic case. One also obtains that any finite number of terms in the expansions of
\overline{U}(t,x)
and
\overline{M}(x)
coincide with the corresponding terms in formal power series solutions
U(t,x)
and
M(x)
.

When
n>1
this does not work in general, see the counterexamples in
[
6
]
. Instead we will use the inverse function theorem, see Section
3
.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
in the analytic case

It is clear that
N\times N
analytic systems form a Banach space with the usual
L^{{}^{\infty}}
norm, and they can be localized to neighborhoods. We shall prove that if
U(t,x)
and
M(x)
are analytic such that
|U|\neq 0
,
M
is symmetric and
M(0)=0
, then the mapping

(3.1)

(U(t,x),M(x))\overset{\mathcal{F}}{\longrightarrow}\Big(\partial_{t}\big(U(t,x)(t\operatorname{Id}_{N}+M(x))U^{*}(t,x)\big),U_{0}(x)M(x)U_{0}^{*}(x)\Big)\\
=(F_{1}(t,x),F_{0}(x))

with
U_{0}(x)=U({0,x})
, is analytic and surjective in some neighborhood of
(0,0)
for analytic and symmetric
F_{j}
,
j=0,\,1
, when
|F_{1}(0,0)|\neq 0
and
F_{0}(0)=0
.
If we obtain a solution to (
3.1
) in a neighborhood of
(0,0)
then we obtain (
1.6
) with

(3.2)

F(t,x)=F_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}F_{1}(s,x)\,ds\qquad\text{near $(0,0)$}

thus
F_{0}(x)=F(0,x)
and
F_{1}(t,x)=\partial_{t}F(t,x)
.

When
M\equiv 0
we find that
\partial_{t}(tUU^{*})=F_{1}
so that

(3.3)

U(t,x)U^{*}(t,x)=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}F_{1}(s,x)\,ds\to F_{1}(0,x)\text{ when }t\to 0

For any such solution
U(t,x)
, we find that the differential of
\mathcal{F}
at
(U,0)
is given by

(3.4)

(u(t,x),m(x))\overset{d\mathcal{F}}{\longrightarrow}\\
\Big(\partial_{t}\big(2t\operatorname{Re}U(t,x)u^{*}(t,x)+U(t,x)m(x)U^{*}(t,x)\big),U_{0}(x)m(x)U_{0}^{*}(x)\Big)\\
=(A_{1}(t,x),A_{0}(x))

where
u
and
m
are analytic and
m=m^{*}
, which gives analytic symmetric
A_{j}
,
j=0,\,1
. We shall show that this differential is surjective in a neighborhood of
(0,0)
. We find from (
3.4
) that
m=U_{0}^{-1}A_{0}(U_{0}^{-1})^{*}
and

2t\operatorname{Re}U(t,x)u^{*}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}A_{1}(s,x)\,ds+R(x)-U(t,x)m(x)U^{*}(t,x)

for some analytic symmetric
R(x)
.
Since the right hand side has to vanish at
t=0
, we take
R(x)=U_{0}(x)m(x)U_{0}^{*}(x)=A_{0}(x)
and then

2\operatorname{Re}U(t,x)u^{*}(t,x)=\frac{1}{t}\left(\int_{0}^{t}A_{1}(s,x)\,ds+A_{0}(x)-U(t,x)m(x)U^{*}(t,x)\right)=A(t,x)

which is analytic and symmetric. By substituting
m
in
A
we find that

A
depends linearly on
A_{j}
,
j=0,\,1
, and there exists an analytic and antisymmetric
B(t,x)
so that
2Uu^{*}=A+B
, which gives the solution

(3.5)

u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}(A(t,x)-B(t,x))(U^{-1})^{*}(t,x)

Thus (
3.4
) is surjective so by the inverse function theorem we obtain a local right inverse to
\mathcal{F}
and local surjectivity of the nonlinear mapping (
3.1
) in a neighborhood
V
of
(U,0)
.
This neighborhood depend on
U(t,x)
and thus by
F(t,x)
by (
3.3
). Observe that
\mathcal{F}
will also be surjective on some
(F_{1},F_{0})
such that
F_{0}(0)\neq 0
.

For any symmetric
M(x)
such that
M(0)=0
, there exists a neighborhood
\omega
of
(0,0)
such that
(U,M)\in V
at
\omega
, and then (
3.5
) gives a solution to (
3.1
) in
\omega
.
But (
3.4
) is not injective, since different antisymmetric
B
gives different solutions by (
3.5
). Actually, we have for fixed
U
and
M\equiv 0
that the mapping
(A_{0},A_{1},B)\mapsto(u,m)
is bijective near
(0,0)
.

It remains to prove uniqueness in the case
U(t,x)
is symmetric and positive definite. Since
m
already is determined by (
3.4
), we only need to prove that there is a unique symmetric solution
u(t,x)
to equation (
3.5
).
In that case, we obtain from Lemma
A.1
a unique analytic antisymmetric
B(t,x)
that solves

\operatorname{Im}(U^{-1}B)=-\operatorname{Im}(U^{-1}A)\qquad\text{near~$(0,0)$}

for the analytic
A(t,x)
in (
3.5
). Thus the unique analytic symmetric solution is given by

u(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}U^{-1}(t,x)(A(t,x)+B(t,x))

We find that
d\mathcal{F}
is bijective which gives that
\mathcal{F}
is a analytic bijection near
(0,0)
in the analytic symmetric case. ∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
in the smooth case

The smooth
N\times N
systems form a local Fréchet spaces with the
C^{\infty}(\overline{\omega})
norms, which are tame if
\,\overline{\omega}
is a compact manifold with boundary, see Theorem
C.8
.
For tame Fréchet spaces one can use the Nash–Moser theorem, which is the inverse function theorem for tame Fréchet spaces, see Theorem
C.12
. The difference to the analytic case is that is it not enought to check the local surjectivity of the linearized map when
M\equiv 0
, instead one has to check that for any
U
and
M
in a neighborhood.

As before, it suffices to show that if
|U(0,0)|\neq 0
,
M(x)
is symmetric and
M(0)=0
then the following
C^{\infty}
mapping is surjective near
(0,0)

(4.1)

C^{\infty}\ni(U(t,x),M(x))\overset{\mathcal{F}}{\longrightarrow}\\
\left(\partial_{t}\big(U(t,x)(t\operatorname{Id}_{N}+M(x))U^{*}(t,x)\big),U_{0}(x)M(x)U_{0}^{*}(x)\right)\\
=(F_{1}(t,x),F_{0}(x))\in C^{\infty}

where
U_{0}(x)=U({0,x})
. Here
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
are symmetric,
F_{1}(0,0)>0
and
F_{0}(0)=0
.
By (
3.2
) we then obtain
F
from
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
.
It follows from Example
C.4
that the mapping
\mathcal{F}
is tame of degree 1.

For any such
(F_{1},F_{0})
we may take smooth
(U(t,x),M(x))
so that
U(0,0)U^{*}(0,0)=F_{1}(0,0)>0
and
M(0)=0
. Since we only need local solutions, we may assume that
U(t,x)\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\omega}_{n+1})
so that
U(0,0)
is invertible,
M(x)\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\omega}_{n})
is symmetric and
M(0)=0
, where
\overline{\omega}_{k}\subset\mathbf{R}^{k}
is a compact neighborhood of the origin.

Let
V
be a bounded neighborhood of
(U(t,x),M(x))
in
C^{\infty}(\overline{\omega}_{n+1})\times C^{\infty}(\overline{\omega}_{n})
so that the first component is invertible and the second component is symmetric. Since
M(0)=0
we may for any
\varepsilon>0
choose
V
and a compact neighborhood
\overline{\omega}
of the origin so that
(\overline{U}(t,x),\overline{M}(x))\in V
gives
\|\overline{M}(x)\|\leq\varepsilon
when
x\in\overline{\omega}
.
For any
(U(t,x),M(x))\in V
the differential of
\mathcal{F}
at
(U(t,x),M(x))
is equal to

(4.2)

C^{\infty}\ni(u(t,x),m(x))\overset{d\mathcal{F}}{\longrightarrow}\\
\left(\partial_{t}\big(2\operatorname{Re}(U(t\operatorname{Id}_{N}+M(x))u^{*})+UmU^{*}\big),U_{0}mU_{0}^{*}+2\operatorname{Re}(U_{0}Mu_{0}^{*})\right)\\
=(A_{1}(t,x),A_{0}(x))\in C^{\infty}

where
u_{0}(x)=u(0,x)
, and
m
,
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
are symmetric. We shall show that if
\overline{\omega}_{n+1}
and
\overline{\omega}_{n}
are sufficiently small,
then the mapping
d\mathcal{F}
is tame and surjective with a tame right inverse, which will show the surjectivity of
\mathcal{F}
by Theorem
C.12
.
For that, we need that
\|M(x)\|\ll 1
for
|x|\ll 1
.

We find from (
4.2
) that

(4.3)

m=U_{0}^{-1}\Big(A_{0}-2\operatorname{Re}(U_{0}Mu^{*}_{0})\Big)(U_{0}^{-1})^{*}

is linear in
Mu_{0}^{*}
and if

Appendix A
A symmetry lemma

In this section, we are going to prove the following result about symmetric and antisymmetric invariance of matrices.

Lemma A.1
.

If
U(x)>0
is a smooth
N\times N
matrix, then
A(x)\mapsto\operatorname{Re}(UA)(x)
is a bijection of smooth symmetric matrices and
A(x)\mapsto\operatorname{Im}(UA)(x)
is a bijection of smooth antisymmetric matrices. If
U(x)
is analytic, then this holds for analytic
A(x)
.

Proof.

First we consider the case of constant matrices. To simplify, we define the symmetric product
S(U,A)=\frac{1}{2}(UA+AU)
, which is symmetric when
A
is symmetric and antisymmetric when
A
is antisymmetric, thus
A\mapsto S(U,A)
preserves the symmetry.
Since any
N\times N
matrix can be written uniquely as a sum of a symmetric and an antisymmetric matrix, we find that
A\to S(U,A)
is a bijection for any
N\times N
matrix
A
.
We also have that linear ON mappings
O
preserves the symmetry, since
OAO^{-1}=OAO^{*}
.

Thus, by using an ON base of eigenvectors to
U
, we find that
U=\{\mu_{j}\delta_{jk}\}_{jk}
with
\mu_{j}>0,\ \forall\,j
, and
A=\{\alpha_{jk}\}_{jk}
. If
A
is symmetric then
\alpha_{kj}=\overline{\alpha}_{jk}

\forall\,jk
, and if
A
is antisymmetric then
\alpha_{kj}=-\overline{\alpha}_{jk}

\forall\,jk
. In both cases, we find that

S(U,A)=\{(\mu_{j}+\mu_{k})\alpha_{jk}/2\}_{jk}=0

if and only if
\alpha_{jk}=0

\forall\,jk
. So
A\mapsto S(U,A)
is injective, and thus bijective in both cases. Observe that
|\alpha_{jk}|\leq\|U^{-1}\|\|S(U,A)\|
since
\mu_{j}^{-1}\leq\|U^{-1}\|
,
\forall\,j
.

Next, we consider the equation

S(U,A)(x)=S(U(x),A(x))=B(x)\in C^{\infty}

which has a unique solution
A(x)=\{\alpha_{jk}\}_{jk}(x)
such that
|\alpha_{jk}(x)|\leq\|U^{-1}(x)\|\|B(x)\|
. By compactness we find for any
x_{0}
and any sequence
x_{j}\to x_{0}
that there exixts a subsequence having a limit:
A(x_{j})\to A_{0}
. Then by continuity and uniqueness we find that
\lim_{x\to x_{0}}A(x)=A_{0}=A(x_{0})
, which proves continuity of
A(x)
.

To show that
A(x)
is differentiable, we put
\Delta_{h,y}A(x)=\frac{A(x+hy)-A(x)}{h}
for any
x,\,y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
and
h>0
. Then we find

(A.1)

\Delta_{h,y}S(U,A)(x)=S(\Delta_{h,y}U(x),A(x+hy))+S(U(x),\Delta_{h,y}A(x))=\Delta_{h,y}B(x)

which gives that

(A.2)

S(U(x),\Delta_{h,y}A(x))=\Delta_{h,y}B(x)-S(\Delta_{h,y}U(x),A(x+hy))

where the right hand side is continuous in
h
. As before, we that the left hand side has the unique limit
S(U(x_{0}),y\cdot\partial A(x_{0}))
for any
y
, giving
S(U,\partial A)=\partial B-S(\partial U,A)
.

Let
A_{\alpha}(x)=\partial^{\alpha}A(x)
and
A_{k}(x)=\{A_{\alpha}(x)\}_{|\alpha|\leq k}
and similarly for
U(x)
and
B(x)
.
By induction, we can assume that
A(x)\in C^{k}
and
that

(A.3)

B_{\gamma}(x)=\sum_{\alpha+\beta=\gamma}c^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}S(U_{\alpha}(x),A_{\beta}(x))\qquad\text{for $|\gamma|=k$}

Then we find for
|\beta|=k
that

(A.4)

S(U(x),\Delta_{h,y}A_{\beta}(x))=\Delta_{h,y}B_{\beta}(x)-S(\Delta_{h,y}U(x),A_{\beta}(x+hy))\\
-\Delta_{h,y}\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}c_{\alpha\beta}S(U_{\alpha}(x),A_{\beta-\alpha}(x))

where the right hand side is continuous in
h
. As before, we find that that the left hand side has the limit
S(U,y\cdot\partial A_{k})(x)
which gives that
A_{k}(x)\in C^{1}
so
A(x)\in C^{k+1}
. By induction we obtain the result in the
C^{\infty}
case.

If
U(x)
and
B(x)
are analytic, then they have holomorphic extensions in a complex neighborhood of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. We then find that
S(U(z),\partial_{\bar{z}_{j}}A(z))=\partial_{\bar{z}_{j}}B(z)-S(\partial_{\bar{z}_{j}}U(z),A(z))=0
in that neighborhood
\forall\,j
, which gives the analyticity of
A(x)
by uniqueness.
∎

G_{j}(t)=Q^{{\varepsilon}_{j}}(t,B,G)P(t,B)+R^{{\varepsilon}_{j}}(B,G){\quad}\text{if}{\quad}\|B\|<1

where

(B.17)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}^{{\alpha}}\partial_{B}^{{\beta}}Q(t,B,G)\|\leq C_{{\alpha}{\beta}}2^{-j(\nu-{\alpha}-|{\beta}|-3)}\int(\|G\|+\|G^{(\nu)}\|)\,dt

\displaystyle\|\partial_{B}^{{\beta}}R(B,G)\|\leq C_{{\beta}}2^{-j(\nu-|{\beta}|-2)}\int(\|G\|+\|G^{(\nu)}\|)\,dt

With
\nu
chosen as in (
B.11
) we find by summing up the convergent series, that

(B.18)

Q(t,B,G)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}Q^{{\varepsilon}_{j}}(t,B,G)\text{ and }R(t,B,G)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}R^{{\varepsilon}_{j}}(t,B,G)

are smooth functions such that (
B.4
) and (
B.11
) hold.
∎

Now, we can prove the following symmetric version of the Malgrange
division theorem.

Theorem
B.3
.

Let
G(t,x)\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\overline{\omega})
with values in
N\times N
matrices, where
\omega
is a neighborhood of the origin in
\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}
, then for any
M(x)\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\omega})
with values in symmetric
N\times N
matrices we find

(B.19)

G(t,x)=Q(t,x)P(t,M(x))+R(x)\qquad(t,x)\in\omega

where
Q\/
and
R\/
are smooth
N\times N
systems depending linearly on
G
satisfying

(B.20)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}^{{\alpha}}\partial_{x}^{{\beta}}Q(t,x)\|\leq C_{{\alpha}{\beta}}\|G\|_{j}{\quad}j={\alpha}+|{\beta}|+4

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}^{{\beta}}R(x)\|\leq C_{{\beta}}\|G\|_{k}{\quad}k=|{\beta}|+3

Here
\|F\|_{j}=\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq j}\|\partial_{t}^{{\alpha}}\partial_{x}^{{\beta}}F\|
is the

Observe that Theorem

gives that the mapping

surjective tame mapping, having a

tame right inverse of degree 4, see Appendix

, but that mapping is not tame for Malgranges preparation theorem in general.
Observe that the symmetric scalar case when

follows directly from the inverse function theorem.

Proof.

If
G(t,x)\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}^{n})
, then by using Proposition A.2 we obtain (
B.19
) with
Q(t,x)=Q(t,M(x),G(\cdot,x))

∎

In the preparation theorems, we have only done right symmetric division by

Appendix C
Tame Fréchet spaces and the Nash–Moser theorem

In this section, we will recall the Nash–Moser Theorem and the theory of tame Fréchet spaces and tame maps from Sections II and III in
[
4
]
. Recall that a Fréchet space is a convex and complete vector space with topology defined by a countable number of seminorms, thus it is a complete metrizable vector space. But for the Nash–Moser Theorem one needs Fréchet spaces with a more refined structure.

Definition C.1
.

A grading on a Fréchet space is a collection of seminorms
\{\|\cdot\|_{n},\ n\in\mathbf{N}\}
that defines the topology and is increasing:
\|f\|_{j}\leq\|f\|_{k}
when
j\leq k
.

Of course, every Fréchet space has a grading, for example by adding to each seminorm all the lower ones. Examples are
C^{\infty}
functions and vector bundles with
C^{k}
norms. Closed subspaces of graded spaces are graded with the induced norms, and Cartesian products
F\times G
can be graded with the norms
\|(f,g)\|_{n}=\|f\|_{n}+\|g\|_{n}
.

Definition C.2
.

Two gradings
\|\cdot\|_{n}
and
\|\cdot\|^{\prime}_{n}
are tamely equivalent of degree
r
and base
b
if

(C.1)

\|f\|_{n}\leq C_{n}\|f\|^{\prime}_{n+r}\quad\text{and}\quad\|f\|^{\prime}_{n}\leq C^{\prime}_{n}\|f\|_{n+r}

for any
n\geq b
. If this holds for any
n
then they are equivalent of degree
r
.

For example, if
X
is a compact manifold, then the
C^{k}
norms, Hölder norms and Sobolev norms are tamely equivalent.

Definition C.3
.

A linear map
L:\ F\mapsto G
of a graded space into another is tame, if it satisfies a tame estimate of degree
r
and base
b
:

(C.2)

\|Lf\|_{n}\leq C_{n}\|f\|_{n+r}

for any
n\geq b
. If (
C.2
) holds for any
n
then
L
is tame of degree
r
.

Example C.4
.

A
C^{\infty}
linear partial differential operator
L
of order
m
on
C^{\infty}
is a tame of degree
m
since
\|Lf\|_{n}\leq C_{n}\|f\|_{n+m}
for the
C^{k}
norms for any
n
.
In particular, multiplication with
C^{\infty}
functions are tame of degree 0.
Solutions to invertible linear systems of smooth equations give tame maps of degree 0 by Leibniz’ rule.
Similarly, for any
C^{\infty}
system
M(x)
, we find that the mapping

(Q(t,x),R(x))\mapsto G(t,x)=Q(t,x)(t\operatorname{Id}_{n}+M(x))+R(x)

is tame of degree 0.

Example C.5
.

The linear mapping
L:C^{\infty}[0,1]\mapsto C^{\infty}[-1,1]
given by
Lf(x)=f(x^{2})
is tame with
\|Lf\|_{n}\leq C_{n}\|f\|_{n}
. The image of
L
is the closed subspace of even functions
C_{S}^{\infty}[-1,1]
. But the inverse
L^{-1}:\ C_{S}^{\infty}[-1,1]\mapsto C^{\infty}[0,1]
is not tame, since the
2n
:th Taylor coefficient of
Lf=g
is the
n
:th Taylor coefficient of
f=L^{-1}g
so the best estimate is
\|L^{-1}f\|_{n}\leq C_{n}\|f\|_{2n}
.

Compositions of linear tame maps are linear and tame, and
L
is a tame isomorphism if
L
is linear isomorphism such that
L
and
L^{-1}
are tame.

Definition C.6
.

Let
F
and
G
be graded spaces. Then we say that
F
is a tame direct summand of
G
if we can find tame linear maps
L:\ F\mapsto G
and
M:\ G\mapsto F
so that the composition
ML:\ F\mapsto F
is the identity:
F\overset{L}{\mapsto}G\overset{M}{\mapsto}F
.

Definition C.7
.

We say that a graded space is tame if it is a tame direct summand of a space
\Sigma(B)
of exponentially decreasing sequences in some Banach space
B
.

A Cartesian product of two tame spaces is tame.

Theorem
C.8
.

The space
C^{\infty}(X)
is tame if
X
is a compact manifold (with or without boundary), and if
V
is a vector bundle over
X
then the sections of the vector bundle
C^{\infty}(X,V)
is also tame, since it can be written as a tame direct summand of a tame space.

Theorem
C.9
.

If
X
is a compact manifold with boundary, then
C^{\infty}_{0}(X)
and
C^{\infty}_{0}(X,V)
are tame.

Next, we shall look at nonlinear maps.

Definition C.10
.

Let
F
and
G
be graded spaces and
P:\ F\supseteq U\mapsto G
a nonlinear map, then we say that
P
satisfies a tame estimate of degree
r
and base
b
if

(C.3)

\|P(f)\|_{n}\leq C_{n}(1+\|f\|_{n+r})

for all
f\in U
and all
n\geq b
. If (
C.3
) holds for any
n
then
P
is tame of degree
r
.
We say that
P
is a tame map if
P
is defined on an open set, is continuous and satisfies a tame estimate in a neighborhood of any point.

As before, if (
C.3
) holds for any
n
then we say
P
satisfies a tame estimate of degree
r
.

Theorem
C.11
.

A composition of tame maps is tame.

The following is the Nash–Moser theorem that we shall use.

Theorem
C.12
.

Let
F
and
G
be tame spaces and
P:\ F\supseteq U\mapsto G
a
C^{\infty}
tame map of the open set
U
. If the equation for the derivative
DP(f)h=k
has a unique solution
h=VP(f)k
for any
f\in U
and any
k
, and the family of inverses
VP(f):\ U\times G\mapsto F
are tame maps, then
P
is locally invertible and each local inverse is a
C^{\infty}
tame map.

If the derivative
DP(f)h=k
has a solution
h=VP(f)k
for any
f\in U
and any
k
, and the family of right inverses
VP(f):\ U\times G\mapsto F
are tame maps, then
P
is locally surjective and each local right inverse is a
C^{\infty}
tame map.

If the derivative
DP(f)
is injective for any
f\in U
and the family of left inverses
VP(f):\ U\times G\mapsto F
are tame maps, then
P
is locally injective and each local left inverse is a
C^{\infty}
tame map.

Example C.13
.

It follows from Corollary
B.4
that the mapping

C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{R}^{n})\times C^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n})\ni(Q(t,x),M(x))\mapsto G(t,x)=P(t,M(x))Q(t,x)+R(x)

with
M(x)=M^{*}(x)\ \forall\,x
is a
C^{\infty}
tame surjective map of degree 0 with local
C^{\infty}
tame right inverses of degree 4.

The proofs of these results and more examples and counterexamples can be found in sections II and III in
[
4
]
.
