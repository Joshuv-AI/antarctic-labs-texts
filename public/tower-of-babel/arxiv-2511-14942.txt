Analysis of Boundary Behaviour of Quasidisks and Jordan Repellers
Ilia Binder, Adi Glücksam

Abstract
We investigate the fine properties of harmonic measure and boundary rotation. By focusing on quasidisks and, in particular, on connected Jordan Repellers arising from conformal expanding dynamical systems, we explore those using the deep interplay between geometric function theory and dynamical systems as a unified framework. While some of the results presented here are regarded as ’folklore’ among experts, they lack rigorous proofs in the existing literature. We fill this gap by providing a comprehensive, referable treatment using a novel approach that also expands existing results.



1
Introduction

It is by now a classical result that the boundary behavior of conformal maps and the dimension properties of harmonic measure of simply connected domains are intimately related. This connection is profoundly illustrated by Makarov’s celebrated theorem on the dimension of the harmonic measure for simply connected planar domains,
[
7
]
. The proofs of this theorem typically establish that the derivative of a conformal map grows sub-exponentially at almost every point of the boundary. Further insight into this relationship is provided by the celebrated paper,
[
4
]
, which studies negative moments of the derivatives of conformal maps using estimates on the number of disjoint disks with large harmonic measure. Less widely utilized is a similar relationship between the local rotation of a simply connected domain’s boundary and the growth of the argument of its corresponding conformal map’s derivative. These relationships are best formalized using
Multifractal analysis
.

Roughly speaking, the
distortion spectrum
, denoted
d(a,b)
, describes the dimension of the set of points on the circle with a given growth-rate of the derivative and its argument. The entropy function for the distortion spectrum, is the
integral means spectrum
, which conceptually describes the
mean
growth-rate of the derivative of a conformal map raised to a given power
z
as we approach the boundary. We allow the power
z
to be a
complex
number. While in the context of real exponents, this object is classical and has been extensively studied (e.g.,
[
8
]
and
[
11
]
), however, in the context of complex exponents, there are still many important open questions, such as Brennan’s Conjecture. Values of the integral mixed spectrum for real exponents reflect the behavior of the harmonic measure, while purely imaginary exponents correspond to the behavior of the rotation. Most of the classical results can be easily extended to this version of the integral means spectrum; nevertheless, it gives rise to many new and intriguing questions, see, e.g.,
[
1
]
. We informally refer to those spectra as ’conformal’.

On the potential side, the counterpart of the distortion spectrum is the
dimension spectrum
. Similarly to the distortion spectrum, intuitively, the
dimension spectrum
of a measure, denoted
f(\alpha)
, aims to describe the dimension of the set of points where the measure exhibits a local scaling behavior corresponding to dimension
\alpha
. The counterpart of the integral mean spectrum, i.e., the entropy function for the dimension spectrum is the packing spectrum. For a detailed discussion of Multifractal analysis, we refer the reader to
[
5
]
.

In this paper, we study these spectra of harmonic measures, and explore similar notions for the rotation of the local boundary, which necessitates the introduction of a two-parameter version of the dimension spectrum,
f(\alpha,\gamma)
(see Sections
2
for precise definition). Multifractal analysis of harmonic measure was investigated in the one-parameter case in Makarov’s celebrated paper,
[
8
]
. We informally refer to those multifractal spectra as ’geometric’.

The previously mentioned entropy relations manifests as a Legendre-type relation between the integral means spectrum and the distortion spectrum (specifically, a version of the Minkowski distortion spectrum) and between the packing spectrum and the dimension spectrum. These relations will not be explored in this note. We refer to
[
2
,
8
]
for further details.

The relationship between the ’geometric’ and ’conformal’ spectra is substantially more intricate than expected. From a ’naive’ understanding of conformal maps, one expects the following relationship:

(1)

d_{\Omega}(a,b)=(1-a)f_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{1-a},\frac{-b}{1-a}\right).

Nevertheless, somewhat surprisingly, the examples presented in
[
8
]
and
[
3
]
illustrate that this relationship does not hold, even for John domains. More precisely, these examples demonstrate that either spectrum can be strictly dominant for all domains.

On the other hand, in the specific case of quasidisks, domains bounded by quasicircles, Relation (
1
) holds true, as outlined in Theorem
2.4
. While this theorem is widely recognized as a ’folklore result’ among experts in the field, we are not aware of any complete published proof. One of the primary objectives of our paper is to fill this notable gap in the literature, an undertaking that proved to be more complex than expected, as elaborated in Section
4
.

In Section
5
, we introduce an innovative notion that can be thought of as the ’crosscut’ version of the Minkowski dimension spectrum. This new version extends the conventional framework and offers new insights, bridging, in some sense, the ’geometric’ and the ’conformal’ worlds. An essential property of the crosscut spectrum is that it majorates the usual
distortion
spectrum for arbitrary domains and aligns with it for quasidisks. We utilize this version of the spectrum in
[
3
]
to produce counterexamples and to generate an upper bound for the universal distortion spectrum.

Multifractal analysis offers a particularly elegant framework for a subclass of quasidisks: Jordan repellers (see
[
8
]
and Section
2.4
for the precise definition). Jordan repellers can also be described using expanding dynamical systems - shifts over finite alphabets. We introduce parallel definitions of spectra using the framework of words over finite alphabets and demonstrate their equivalence to the original ones. The fact that Jordan repellers are invariant under an expanding dynamics allows us to utilize the power of Thermodynamic Formalism. Using this well-established technique, we show that for repellers, all versions of the spectra - dimension, and distortion as well as Hausdorff, and Minkowski - coincide, and the dimension and distortion spectra are related via Legendre transforms to each other. We provide proofs of these assertions in the case of complex exponents (for the conformal spectra) and the two-parameter geometric spectra, extending the results of
[
8
]
. While all of these results can also be considered ’folklore’, we need a stronger version of these relations in
[
3
]
. These stronger relations come from our extension of the classical Carleson estimate on multiplicativity of harmonic measure, Lemmas
6.2
and
6.6
.

The other objective of this note is to prepare the necessary auxiliary results for
[
3
]
. In
[
3
]
, we show, among other things, that unlike the standard spectra, measured for a specific set, the universal conformal and geometric spectra mentioned above
do
satisfy Relation (
1
). This is yet another ’folklore result’. We establish it by showing that the universal bounds can be obtained by looking solely at
Jordan repellers
.

Section
6
contains one important technical result; The introduction of a
’propagating property’
, which, roughly speaking, is a property whose density does not decrease as the scale shrinks. We prove that the spectra of repellers propagate, enabling controlled approximations of the spectra by using spectra at a specific scale - finer scales yield better approximations. We expect the concept of propagating property to be fruitful in solving other problems as well.

Many of the claims proven in this paper are intuitive and fundamental to the field; however, we were unable to find references or published proofs in standard literature. We have therefore included full proofs both for completeness and to provide a convenient reference for future use.

Background, Definitions, and Results

2.1
Setup and Fundamental Spectra

Let
\Omega
be a bounded simply connected domain, fix
z_{0}\in\Omega
, and let
\phi:\mathbb{D}\to\Omega
be a conformal map sending the origin to
z_{0}
. Let
\arg
denote the branch of the argument of
\phi^{\prime}
satisfying
\arg(\phi^{\prime}(0))\in(-\pi,\pi]
.

We first discuss the ’conformal spectrum’:

Definition 2.1
.

For every
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, the
Minkowski Distortion Mixed Spectrum
of
\Omega
is defined by

d^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{\Omega}(a,b):=\limsup_{\eta\searrow 0}\;\limsup_{r\nearrow 1^{-}}\frac{\log\left({\lambda_{1}\left({L^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{a,b,\eta}(r)}\right)}\right)}{\log\left({\frac{1}{1-r}}\right)}+1,\quad\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\left\{{+,-}\right\}

where
\zeta\in L^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{a,b,\eta}(r)\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
if

(d1)

\left|{\phi^{\prime}(r\zeta)}\right|\begin{cases}>\left({1-r}\right)^{-a+\eta}&,\sigma=+\\
<\left({1-r}\right)^{-a-\eta}&,\sigma=-\end{cases}.

(d2)

\arg\left({\phi^{\prime}(r\zeta)}\right)\begin{cases}>\left({1-r}\right)^{-b+\eta}&,\sigma^{\prime}=+\\
<\left({1-r}\right)^{-b-\eta}&,\sigma^{\prime}=-\end{cases}.

The following version of the spectrum, which is essentially the minimum of the four quantities defined above, and is also extremely useful.

d_{\Omega}(a,b):=\limsup_{\eta\searrow 0}\;\limsup_{r\nearrow 1^{-}}\frac{\log\left({\lambda_{1}\left({L_{a,b,\eta}(r)}\right)}\right)}{\log\left({\frac{1}{1-r}}\right)}+1,

where
\zeta\in L_{a,b,\eta}(r)\subset\partial\mathbb{D}
if

\left({1-r}\right)^{-a+\eta}<\left|{\phi^{\prime}(r\zeta)}\right|<\left({1-r}\right)^{-a-\eta}\quad\quad;\quad\quad\left({1-r}\right)^{-b+\eta}<\arg\left({\phi^{\prime}(r\zeta)}\right)<\left({1-r}\right)^{-b-\eta}.

Given a function
g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is increasing (decreasing) in each variable, we say a point
(a,b)
is a
growth point
if for all
a
and
\eta>0
,
g(a+\eta,b)>g(a-\eta,b)
(
g(a+\eta,b)<g(a-\eta,b)
) and for all
b
and
\eta>0
,
g(a,b-\eta)<g(a,b+\eta)
(
g(a,b+\eta)<g(a,b-\eta)
). By definition,

d_{\Omega}(a,b)\leq\min\left\{{d_{\Omega}^{++}(a,b),d_{\Omega}^{+-}(a,b),d_{\Omega}^{--}(a,b),d_{\Omega}^{-+}(a,b)}\right\},

and if
(a,b)
is a growth point for
d_{\Omega}^{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(a,b)
then
d_{\Omega}(\alpha,\gamma)=d_{\Omega}^{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\alpha,\gamma)
.

Note that the spectrum does not depend on the choice of
z_{0}
or
\phi
. Moreover, by Makarov’s triple logarithm theorem,
d_{\Omega}(0,0):=1
, since almost everywhere
\log\phi^{\prime}
grows like
o\left(\frac{1}{1-r}\right)
. Roughly speaking, the Minkowski Distortion Spectrum measures the size of the set of ’atypical’ points on the circle with fast growth or decay of
\left|{\phi^{\prime}}\right|
and
\arg\left({\phi^{\prime}}\right)
. We note that this definition appears to differ from the one given for the non-rotational case in
[
8
]
. The reason for the more complicated definition lies in the fact that for fixed
a\neq 0
, the function
d_{\Omega}(a,b)
might not achieve its maximum at
b=0
.

Now, let us turn to the ’geometric spectra’. To discuss the
geometric
behavior of the boundary, we first need to define rotation. We define the
rotation
of a domain,
\Omega
, centered at a boundary point
z
at scale
\delta
by

\rot(z,\delta):=\exp\left({\underset{y\in\partial\Omega_{z,\delta}\cap\partial B(z,\delta)}{\inf}\arg_{[z]}(y-z)}\right),

where
\Omega_{z,\delta}
is the connected component of the set
\left\{{y\in\Omega,\;\left|{y-z}\right|>\delta}\right\}
containing
z_{0}
, and the argument,
\arg_{[z]}
, is a branch of the argument satisfying
\arg_{[z]}(z_{0}-z)\in(-\pi,\pi]
.

It will sometimes be useful to discuss the rotation of a disk,
B
. We will abuse the notation of rotation,
\rot
, and define the rotation of a disk
B=B(z,\delta)
by
\rot(B)=\rot(B(z,\delta)):=\rot(z,\delta)
, even when its centre is not necessarily on
\partial\Omega
as long as
\frac{diam(\partial\Omega\cap B)}{diam(B)}\geq\frac{1}{4}
.

Definition 2.2
.

For every
\alpha,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
the
Minkowski Dimension Mixed Spectrum
of
\Omega
is defined by

f_{\Omega}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\alpha,\gamma)=\underset{{\eta}\searrow{0}}{\lim}\;\underset{{\delta}\searrow{0}}{\limsup}\;\frac{\log N^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\delta,\alpha,\gamma,\eta)}{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)},\quad\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\left\{{+,-}\right\}

where
N^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\delta,\alpha,\gamma,\eta)
is the maximal number of pairwise disjoint disks,
\left\{{B(z_{j},\delta)}\right\}
, centered at
\partial\Omega
, for which

(f1)

\omega(B(z_{j},\delta))\begin{cases}>\delta^{\alpha+\eta}&,\sigma=+\\
<\delta^{\alpha-\eta}&,\sigma=-\end{cases}.

(f2)

\rot(z_{j},\delta)\begin{cases}>\delta^{\gamma+\eta}&,\sigma^{\prime}=+\\
<\delta^{\gamma-\eta}&,\sigma^{\prime}=-\end{cases}.

As we have done above for the distortion spectrum, we also define

f_{\Omega}(\alpha,\gamma):=\underset{{\eta}\searrow{0}}{\lim}\;\underset{{\delta}\searrow{0}}{\limsup}\;\frac{\log N(\delta,\alpha,\gamma,\eta)}{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)},

where
N(\delta,\alpha,\gamma,\eta)
is the maximal number of pairwise disjoint disks
\left\{{B(z_{j},\delta)}\right\}
centered at
\partial\Omega
satisfying that

\delta^{\alpha+\eta}<\omega(B(z_{j},\delta))<\delta^{\alpha-\eta}\quad\quad;\quad\quad\delta^{\gamma+\eta}<\rot(z_{j},\delta)<\delta^{\gamma-\eta}.

And, as before,

f_{\Omega}(\alpha,\gamma)\leq\min\left\{{f_{\Omega}^{++}(\alpha,\gamma),f_{\Omega}^{+-}(\alpha,\gamma),f_{\Omega}^{--}(\alpha,\gamma),f_{\Omega}^{-+}(\alpha,\gamma)}\right\},

and if
(\alpha,\gamma)
is a growth point for
f_{\Omega}^{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\alpha,\gamma)
then
f_{\Omega}(\alpha,\gamma)=f_{\Omega}^{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\alpha,\gamma)
.

Remark 2.3
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega
be a conformal map and denote by
\tilde{\phi}(z):=\overline{\phi\left({\overline{z}}\right)}
. Since
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(z)=\overline{\phi^{\prime}\left({\overline{z}}\right)}
,
\tilde{\phi}
is also conformal, and its image,
\tilde{\Omega}
, satisfies

\tilde{\Omega}:=\left\{{\tilde{\phi}(z),z\in\mathbb{D}}\right\}=\left\{{\overline{\phi\left({\overline{z}}\right)},z\in\mathbb{D}}\right\}=\left\{{\overline{\phi\left({z}\right)},z\in\mathbb{D}}\right\}=\overline{\Omega}.

In particular,
\tilde{\tilde{\Omega}}=\Omega
. We conclude that for every
a,b,\alpha,\gamma
we have

d_{\Omega}^{\pm+}(a,b)=d_{\tilde{\Omega}}^{\pm-}(a,-b)\quad,\quad f_{\Omega}^{\pm+}(\alpha,\gamma)=f_{\tilde{\Omega}}^{\pm-}(\alpha,-\gamma).

We will therefore only consider
b\geq 0
or
\gamma\geq 0
, as the general case can be obtained using these cases and the equalities above.

2.2
Relations Among the Fundamental Spectra

The first significant result in this note is a theorem which states that if the harmonic measure is doubling (or equivalently, the domain is a quasidisk), then the Minkowski Dimension Mixed Spectrum is directly related to the Minkowski Distortion Mixed Spectrum (with the correct parameter).

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be a quasidisk. Then

1.

\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad d_{\Omega}(a,b)=\left({1-a}\right)f_{\Omega}\left({\frac{1}{1-a},\frac{-b}{1-a}}\right).

2.

\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\min_{\sigma,\ \sigma^{\prime}\in\left\{{-,+}\right\}}\left\{{d^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{\Omega}(a,b)}\right\}=\left({1-a}\right)\min_{\sigma,\ \sigma^{\prime}\in\left\{{-,+}\right\}}\left\{{f^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{\Omega}\left({\frac{1}{1-a},\frac{-b}{1-a}}\right)}\right\}.

For approximately thirty years, experts in the field believed that the inequality

(2)

d_{\Omega}(a,b)\geq\left({1-a}\right)f_{\Omega}\left({\frac{1}{1-a},\frac{-b}{1-a}}\right)

holds without the assumption that
\Omega
is a quasidisk. While attempting to write down a rigorous proof, the authors encountered a problem - it is possible that every curve in a disk
B
either carries a large portion of the harmonic measure or has a large enough diameter, but not both. Extending the curve would either increase the harmonic measure too much or make the curve too long. Such disks should be counted on a different, smaller scales.

As established in this paper, the issue does not occur for quasidisks. However, the authors constructed a simply-connected John domain for which the Minkowski Distortion Spectrum is smaller than the Minkowski Dimension Spectrum, i.e., inequality (
2
), and consequently Theorem
2.4
, do not hold (see
[
3
]
). Note that the very existence of this counterexample necessitated a new approach for the proof of the famous fractal approximation, which is also covered in
[
3
]
.

In order to prove Theorem
2.4
, we rely on two ’folklore results’ that are of independent interest. To state those results, we require notation, some of which is standard in the field and some is new.

2.3
Corsscut Spectrum

Attempting to relate the ’geometric’ and the ’conformal’ spectra, we define the
Minkowski Crosscut Spectrum
.

Definition 2.11
.

For every
r\in\left({0,1}\right)
and
a,b>0
we denote by
\Gamma\left({a,b,r}\right)
a maximal collection of crosscuts,
C
, with disjoint supports satisfying

1.

\left|{\frac{\omega(\nu_{C})}{1-r}-1}\right|\leq\frac{1-r}{\log\log\log\left({\frac{1}{1-r}}\right)}
.

2.

\diam(\nu_{C})\geq\left({1-r}\right)^{1-a}
.

3.

\rot(C)\geq\left({1-r}\right)^{-b}
.

We define the
Minkowski Crosscut Spectrum
by

d_{\Omega}^{cc}(a,b)=\underset{a^{\prime}\searrow a\atop{b^{\prime}\searrow b}}{\limsup}\;\underset{r\nearrow 1}{\limsup}\;\frac{\log\left({\#\Gamma\left({a^{\prime},b^{\prime},r}\right)}\right)}{\log\left({\frac{1}{1-r}}\right)}.

We relate this new notion of spectrum to the fundamental ones discussed in previous sub-sections:

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
a>0,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

1.

For every simply connected domain,
\Omega
,
d_{\Omega}(a,b)\leq d_{\Omega}^{++}(a,b)\leq d_{\Omega}^{cc}(a,b)
.

2.

For every quasidisk,
\Omega
,
d_{\Omega}(a,b)=d_{\Omega}^{cc}(a,b)
.

Lemma 2.13
.

If
a<0
, then for every simply connected domain,
\Omega
,

d_{\Omega}(a,b)\leq d_{\Omega}^{++}(a,b)\leq\underset{{\eta}\rightarrow{0^{+}}}{\liminf}\;f_{\Omega}^{+\pm}\left({\frac{1}{1-a}+\eta,\frac{-b}{1-a}+\eta}\right).

2.4
Jordan Repellers

The last section of this note deals with the special case of
Jordan Repellers
.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
\Omega
be a simply connected domain, and
\partial
be a sub-arc of its boundary,
\partial\Omega
. We call
\partial
a
Jordan Repeller
if there exists a partition of
\partial
into a finite number of non-intersecting sub-arcs, denoted
\partial_{1},\partial_{2},\cdots,\partial_{N}
(a Markov partition), and a piecewise univalent (though not necessary injective) map
F
, such that for every sub-arc in the partition,
\partial_{j}
, there exists a neighborhood
U_{j}
containing
\partial_{j}
and a univalent map
F_{j}:\,U_{j}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfying:

(1)

F|_{\partial_{j}}=F_{j}
.

(2)

(Geometry invariance)
F_{j}\left({\Omega\cap U_{j}}\right)\subset\Omega
. (This property preserves the geometry of the set).

(3)

(Boundary invariance)
F_{j}\left({\partial\Omega\cap U_{j}}\right)\subset\partial\Omega
.

(4)

(Markov property) The image of each
\partial_{j}
is a finite union arcs
\partial_{k}
, i.e.,
F(\partial_{j})=\underset{k\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}{\bigcup}\partial_{k}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{j}\subseteq\left\{{1,2,\cdots,N}\right\}
.

(5)

(Expanding) There exists
n_{0}
so that
\underset{z\in\partial}{\inf}\;\left|{F^{(n_{0})}(z)}\right|>1
, where
F^{(n_{0})}
denotes the
n_{0}
’th derivative of
F
.

(6)

(Mixing) For any open set
W
satisfying
W\cap\partial\neq\emptyset
, there exists
n
such that
F^{\circ n}\left({W\cap\partial}\right)=\partial
, i.e., the
n
’th iteration of
F
maps
W\cap\partial
to cover
\partial
.

The main result of Section
6
is a refinement and an extension of Carleson’s estimate to explore the multiplicativity properties of rotations and harmonic measure of Jordan Repellers (Lemmas
6.2
and
6.6
).

Finally, we present the notion of ’propagation’:

Definition 2.15
.

A function
\varphi:(0,1)^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

propagates
if there exists a constant
C>1
, so that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

\frac{\log\varphi\left({\delta^{n},C\cdot\eta}\right)}{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta^{n}}}\right)}\geq\frac{\log\varphi(\delta,\eta)}{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}

for every
\delta
small enough (which may depend on
\eta
).

In section
6
, we show that the spectra of Jordan Repellers propagate. This crucial property allows us to lay the foundations to approximate the universal spectra via Repellers.

3
Preliminaries: Conformal Maps, Harmonic Measure, and Rotations

In this section, we provide the necessary background on conformal maps, harmonic measures, and rotations. We include full proofs of several key results here, as published documentation for intuitive or ’folklore claims’ which is absent from the standard literature. This ensures completeness and provides a convenient reference.

The first subsection focuses on quasidisks and contains results concerning the interrelations between conformal maps and harmonic measures in this special setup. The second subsection details the results concerning rotations.

3.0.1
On Two Notions of Doubling Measures on Quasicircles

Definition 3.1
.

Following
[
6
, p. 234-235]
, we say that
\Gamma
is a
quasicircle
if there exists a constant
K
such that if
C_{w_{1},w_{2}}\subset\Gamma
is the curve of smallest diameter connecting
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
, then for every
w\in C_{w_{1},w_{2}}

\frac{\left|{w_{1}-w}\right|+\left|{w_{2}-w}\right|}{\left|{w_{1}-w_{2}}\right|}<K.

A
quasidisk
is a domain
\Omega
, bounded by a quasicircle. We will call
K
the
dilatation
of
\Omega
. Although
K
is not precisely the dilatation of
\Omega
in the classical sense of quasi-conformal maps, we choose this terminology as they are related (see
[
6
, Corollary VII.3.4]
).

Note that by the triangle inequality, for a
K
-quasicircle we have

(3)

\left|{w_{1}-w_{2}}\right|\leq\diam\left({C_{w_{1},w_{2}}}\right):=\underset{z,w\in C_{w_{1},w_{2}}}{\sup}\left|{z-w}\right|\leq\underset{z,w\in C_{w_{1},w_{2}}}{\sup}\left|{z-w_{1}}\right|+\left|{w_{1}-w}\right|<2K\left|{w_{1}-w_{2}}\right|.

This does not imply that the curve has finite length, e.g., the classical Koch Snowflake is a quasicircle, of infinite length.

The following statement is standard; we include a proof for completeness.

Claim 3.2
.

Let
\gamma
be a quasicircle with dilation
K
. For every
L>1
there exists M=M(L, K), depending only on
L
and
K
such that for every
\delta\in\left({\frac{\diam(\gamma)}{L},\diam(\gamma)}\right)
,
\gamma
can be partitioned into
k\leq M
adjacent sub-arcs,
\gamma=I_{1}\cup\dots\cup I_{k}
, with

\diam(I_{j})=\delta,\ j<k,\quad\diam(I_{k})\leq\delta.

Proof.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\mapsto\mathbb{C}
be a parameterization of
\gamma
, and define recursively

t_{0}=0,\quad t_{j}=\min\{1,\min\{t>t_{j-1}\,,\,\diam(\gamma([t_{j-1},t]))=\delta\}\},\quad k:=\min\{j\,:\,t_{j}=1\}.

Note that the collection
\gamma_{j}:=\gamma\left({\left[{t_{j-1},t_{j}}\right]}\right)
satisfies the requirements of the lemma. To conclude the proof it is enough to bound
k
from above, independently of
\delta
.

Since by definition
\diam(\gamma)\leq L\cdot\delta
, one can cover
\gamma
by at most
\sim K^{2}L^{2}
disks of diameter
\frac{\delta}{2K}
.
Assume one of these disks,
B
, contains both
\gamma(t_{i})
and
\gamma(t_{j})
for some
i<j<k
. Then by inclusion

\diam(\gamma([t_{i},t_{j}]))\geq\diam(\gamma([t_{i},t_{i+1}]))=\delta.

However, if
\gamma(t_{j}),\gamma(t_{i})\in B
, then since
\gamma
is a quasicircle, (
3
) implies

\diam(\gamma([t_{i},t_{j}]))<2K|\gamma(t_{i})-\gamma(t_{j})|<2K\diam(B)<2K\frac{\delta}{2K}=\frac{\delta}{2}

which leads to a contradiction. We conclude that every disk contains at most 2 points in
\left\{{\gamma(t_{j})}\right\}
implying that
k\lesssim K^{2}L^{2}
.

∎

Definition 3.3
.

We say a measure,
\nu
, supported on a Jordan arc, is
doubling
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every adjacent sub-arcs,
I,J
, (i.e.,
\overline{I}\cap\overline{J}\neq\emptyset
) if
\diam(I)\leq\diam(J)
then
\mu(I)\leq C\mu(J)
(see
[
6
, p.244]
).

Definition 3.4
.

We say a measure defined on a metric space,
\mu
, is
doubling
if there exists a constant
C>1
so that for every disk,
B
,
\mu(2B)\leq C\mu(B)
.

In quasicircles, the two notions of doubling coincide, as the following lemma shows.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mu
be a measure defined on a quasicircle,
\partial\Omega
. Then
\mu
is doubling in the sense of Definition
3.3
if and only if doubling in the sense of Definition
3.4
.

Proof.

First, assume that
\mu
satisfies Definition
3.3
, and let
B
be a disk centered at
\partial\Omega
, and
\gamma
be the smallest connected arc of
\partial\Omega
containing
2B\cap\partial\Omega
. The distance between endpoints of
\gamma
is bounded by
2\diam(2B)=4\diam(B)
, implying that the diameter of
\gamma
is bounded by
8K_{\Omega}\diam(B)
.

Let
I
be the arc of
B\cap\partial\Omega
that contains the center of
B
. Observe that
\diam(I)\geq\frac{1}{2}\diam(B)
, since it contains the center of
B
. Using Claim
3.2
, we see that each one of the connected components of
\gamma\setminus I
can be covered by at most
M=M(K,K)
adjacent arcs
I_{1},\dots,I_{k}
with
\diam(I_{j})=\diam(B)
,
\diam(I_{k})\leq\diam(B)
. Therefore, by the doubling property of
\mu
,
\mu(I_{j})\leq C^{j}\mu(I)
, implying that

\mu(2B)\leq\mu(\gamma)\leq\mu(I)\left(1+2\sum_{j=1}^{M}C^{j}\right)\leq C_{1}\mu(B).

Now, assume that
\mu
satisfies Definition
3.4
and let
I\subset\partial\Omega
be an arc. Let
z_{1},z_{2}\in I
be two points satisfying
\left|{z_{1}-z_{2}}\right|>\frac{\diam(I)}{2}
, and let
z_{0}
be a point on
C_{z_{1},z_{2}}
which is equidistant from
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
. We will show that

B\left(z_{0},\frac{\diam(I)}{8K_{\Omega}}\right)\cap\partial\Omega\subset I.

Indeed, if
z\in\partial\Omega\setminus I
then since either
z_{1}\in C_{z,z_{0}}
or
z_{2}\in C_{z,z_{0}}
we see that

|z-z_{0}|\geq\frac{\diam(C_{z,z_{0}})}{K_{\Omega}}\geq\frac{\min\{\left|{z_{1}-z_{0}}\right|,\left|{z_{2}-z_{0}}\right|\}}{K_{\Omega}}\geq\frac{\diam(I)}{8K_{\Omega}}.

Let
J
be a curve adjacent to
I
which satisfies
\diam(J)\leq\diam(I)
. In particular,
J\subset B\left(z_{0},2\diam(I)\right)=B\left(z_{0},16K_{\Omega}\cdot\frac{\diam(I)}{8K_{\Omega}}\right)
implying that

\mu(J)\leq\mu\left({B\left(z_{0},16K_{\Omega}\cdot\frac{\diam(I)}{8K_{\Omega}}\right)}\right)\leq C_{1}\mu\left({B\left(z_{0},\frac{\diam(I)}{8K_{\Omega}}\right)}\right)\leq C_{1}\mu(I)

where
C_{1}
depends only on
K_{\Omega}
and
C
in Definition
3.4
.
∎

It is known that if
\Omega
is a quasidisk, then its corresponding harmonic measure is doubling in the sense of Definition
3.3
(see
[
6
, Theorem VII.3.5]
). Thus, the harmonic measure on the quasicircle satisfies Lemma
3.5
.

We will also use the following standard property of quasicircles;

Claim 3.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be a quasidisk and denote by
K_{\Omega}
the dilatation of
\Omega
. For every disk,
B
, for every
L>1
and for every pair,
C_{1},C_{2}
, of connected components of
\partial\Omega\cap L\cdot B
, which intersect
B

\frac{(L-1)\diam(B)}{4K_{\Omega}}\leq\operatorname{dist}(C_{1}\cap B,C_{2}\cap B)\leq\diam(B).

In particular, the number of connected components of
L\cdot B\cap\partial\Omega
that intersect
B
is bounded by a constant depending on
K_{\Omega}
and
L
alone.

Proof.

Fix a disk,
B
, and let
C_{1},C_{2}
be a pair of connected components of
\partial\Omega\cap L\cdot B
which intersect
B
. By definition of quasidisks, for every
x\in C_{1},y\in C_{2}
there exists
C_{x,y}\subset\partial\Omega\cap L\cdot B
satisfying

\diam(C_{x,y})\leq 2K_{\Omega}\left|{x-y}\right|.

Since
C_{1},C_{2}
are disjoint we can write
C_{x,y}\cap L\cdot B=\left({C_{1}\cap C_{x,y}}\right)\uplus\left({C_{2}\cap C_{x,y}}\right)
. Each of those arcs,
C_{j}\cap C_{x,y}
, intersects both
\partial B
and
\partial\left({L\cdot B}\right)
. We conclude that

\underset{x\in C_{1}\cap B\atop y\in C_{2}\cap B}{\inf}\;\left|{x-y}\right|\geq\frac{1}{2K_{\Omega}}\underset{x\in C_{1}\cap B\atop y\in C_{2}\cap B}{\inf}\;\diam(C_{x,y})\geq\frac{\operatorname{dist}\left({\partial B,\partial\left({L\cdot B}\right)}\right)}{2K_{\Omega}}=\frac{(L-1)\diam(B)}{4K_{\Omega}}.

The final assertion, regarding the number of connected components, follows from the lower bound. If the distance between any two components within
B
is at least
(L-1)\frac{\diam(B)}{4K_{\Omega}}
, then the number of such components must be bounded by

\frac{\diam(B)^{2}}{\operatorname{dist}(C_{1}\cap B,C_{2}\cap B)^{2}}\lesssim\frac{K_{\Omega}^{2}}{(L-1)^{2}},

concluding the proof.
∎

3.1
Auxiliary Results for Rotation

In this subsection we reveal intriguing properties of the rotation. The first lemma shows that one can estimate the rotation using integration over curves in
\Omega
:

Lemma 3.7
.

Fix
w\in\partial\Omega
and
\delta>0
, and denote by
\Omega_{w,\delta}
the connected component of
\Omega\setminus B(w,\delta)
containing
z_{0}
. Then, every curve,
C_{y}\subset\Omega_{w,\delta}
, connecting
y\in\partial B(w,\delta)\cap\Omega_{w,\delta}
with
z_{0}
satisfies

\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{y}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]-\log\left({\rot(w,\delta)}\right)}\right|\leq 3\pi.

Proof.

Since the branch of the argument is chosen so that
\argu_{[w]}(z_{0}-w)\in(-\pi,\pi]
we get that

\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{y}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]-\pi\leq\argu_{[w]}(y-w)\leq\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{y}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]+\pi.

Next, for two points
x,y\in\partial B(w,\delta)\cap\Omega_{w,\delta}
, let
\sigma\subset\partial B(w,\delta)
be chosen so that the domain bounded by
C_{x}+\sigma-C_{y}
, which is contained in
\Omega_{w,\delta}
, does not contain
w
. Then, since the mapping
\xi\mapsto\frac{1}{\xi-w}
is holomorphic in a neighborhood of this domain and the curve is closed,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{C_{x}+\sigma-C_{y}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}=\int_{C_{x}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}+\int_{\sigma}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}-\int_{C_{y}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{x}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]-2\pi\leq\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{y}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]\leq\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{x}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]+2\pi,

as the rotation along
\sigma
is bounded by the rotation of a circle, which is
2\pi
. Overall, we conclude that

\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{y}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]-\argu_{[w]}(x-w)}\right|\leq 3\pi.

In particular the argument above holds for
y^{*}\in\partial B(w,\delta)\cap\Omega
which satisfies
\rot(w,\delta)=\exp\left({\argu_{[w]}(y^{*}-w)}\right)
.
∎

The next lemma demonstrates that, unlike harmonic measure, the rotation remains stable; perturbing the center of a disk,
B(w,\delta)
, by less than its diameter does not significantly alter the rotation.

Lemma 3.8
.

For every
\xi,w\in\partial\Omega
if
\left|{\xi-w}\right|<\delta
, then

\left|{\log\left({\rot(\xi,\delta))}\right)-\log\left({\rot(w,\delta))}\right)}\right|\leq 10\pi.

Proof.

Let
\Omega_{1}
denote the connected component of
\Omega\setminus\left({B(\xi,\delta)\cup B(w,\delta)}\right)
containing
z_{0}
, and let
C_{w},C_{\xi}\subset\Omega_{1}
be two curves connecting
z_{0}
with
\partial B(w,\delta)
and
\partial B(\xi,z)
respectively. Let
\sigma\subset\partial B(w,\delta)\cup\partial B(\xi,\delta)
be so that the domain bounded by
\Gamma:=C_{w}+\sigma-C_{w}
does not contain either points,
\xi
or
w
. Since
B(w,\delta)\cap B(\xi,\delta)\neq\emptyset
, every curve either circles both points or circles none of them. Now in the domain bounded by
\Gamma
both functions
z\mapsto\frac{1}{z-\xi}
and
z\mapsto\frac{1}{z-w}
are holomorphic and therefore,

\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\frac{1}{z-\xi}dz=0=\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\frac{1}{z-w}dz,

and in particular

\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\underset{C_{\xi}}{\int}\frac{1}{z-w}dz}\right]-\mathbb{I}m\left[{\underset{C_{w}}{\int}\frac{1}{z-w}dz}\right]}\right|\leq\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\underset{\sigma}{\int}\frac{1}{z-w}dz}\right]}\right|\leq 4\pi,

The last piece of the puzzle we need is to observe that since
C_{w},C_{\xi}\subset\Omega_{1}
then the number of times each of these curves circles
\xi
has to be equal to the number of times it circles
w
for otherwise, the curve separates between the two points, which is impossible by the way
\Omega_{1}
was defined. Note that since the winding index of
C_{\xi}
around
\xi
and the winding index of
C_{\xi}
around
w
is the same,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\underset{C_{\xi}}{\int}\frac{1}{z-w}dz=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\underset{C_{\xi}}{\int}\frac{1}{z-\xi}dz.

Overall, using Lemma
3.7
and the estimates above, we see that

\displaystyle\left|{\log\left({\rot(B(\xi,\delta))}\right)-\log\left({\rot(B(w,\delta))}\right)}\right|\leq 6\pi+\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{\xi}}{\frac{1}{z-\xi}}d{z}}\right]-\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{w}}{\frac{1}{z-w}}d{z}}\right]}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq 6\pi+\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{\xi}}{\frac{1}{z-\xi}}d{z}}\right]-\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{\xi}}{\frac{1}{z-w}}d{z}}\right]}\right|+\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{\xi}}{\frac{1}{z-w}}d{z}}\right]-\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C_{w}}{\frac{1}{z-w}}d{z}}\right]}\right|\leq 10\pi.

∎

Remark 3.9
.

The same proof shows that if two disks,
B_{1},\;B_{2}
are tangential or intersecting and the connected component of
\Omega\setminus(B_{1}\cup B_{2})
containing
z_{0}
is contained in the connected component of
\Omega\setminus B_{j}
containing
z_{0}
for
j\in\left\{{1,2}\right\}
then their rotations are equivalent up to numerical constants.

In particular, for every crosscut,
C
, and every
\xi\in\nu_{C}
,
\rot(C)\sim\rot(\xi,\operatorname{dist}(\xi,C)+\diam(C))
. To see this let
w_{\xi}\in C
satisfy
\left|{w_{\xi}-\xi}\right|=\operatorname{dist}(\xi,C)
, then the disks
B(\xi,\left|{\xi-w_{\xi}}\right|+\diam(C))
and
B(w_{\xi},\diam(C))
are tangential and, since
B(w_{\xi},\diam(C))\subset B(\xi,\operatorname{dist}(\xi,C)+\diam(C))
, the corresponding connected components are nested as required. Following the remark above
\rot(C)\sim\rot(w_{\xi},\diam(C))\sim\rot(\xi,\operatorname{dist}(\xi,C)+\diam(C))
.

Next, it would be interesting to keep the disk centred at
\zeta
and perturb the diameter. However, in general, this cannot be bounded as the domain may rotate a lot in any annulus. Nevertheless, for quasidisks, this can be uniformly bounded, which is the topic of the next lemma.

:
This is an example showing how to disks of the same radius, centered at
x
and
y
, could be close but have very different rotations. The same is true for two concentric disks (centered at
x
) with different radii. The dashed curves are curves connecting
z_{0}
with disks centered at
x
. The red lines are curves connecting
z_{0}
with larger disks, while the blue ones connect it to the smaller disks.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be a quasidisk of dilatation
K_{\Omega}
, fix a point
w\in\partial\Omega
, and let
\delta_{2}<\delta_{1}
. Then

\left|{\log\left({\rot(B_{2})}\right)-\log\left({\rot(B_{1})}\right)}\right|\leq 120K_{\Omega}\frac{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta_{2}}}\right)}{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta_{1}}}\right)}\quad\quad\quad\quad,\text{ for }B_{j}=B\left({w,\delta_{j}}\right).

The same proof can be used to show that John domains also satisfy this property, with some constant
K=K(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
C\subset\Omega\setminus B_{2}
be a curve connecting
z_{0}
and
p\in\partial B_{2}
, and let
C^{\prime}\subset\Omega\setminus B_{1}
is the sub-arc of
C
connecting
z_{0}
to
q\in\partial B_{1}
. Following Lemma
3.7

\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]-\log\left({\rot(B_{2})}\right)}\right|\leq 3\pi;\quad\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C^{\prime}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]-\log\left({\rot(B_{1})}\right)}\right|\leq 3\pi,

implying that

\left|{\log\left({\rot(B_{2})}\right)-\log\left({\rot(B_{1})}\right)}\right|\leq 6\pi+\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C\setminus C^{\prime}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]}\right|.

Define
L=[p,q]
. If
L\subset\Omega
, we define
\Gamma=C\setminus C^{\prime}+L
and note that

\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{C\setminus C^{\prime}}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]}\right|=\left|{\mathbb{I}m\left[{\int_{L}{\frac{1}{\xi-w}}d{\xi}}\right]}\right|\leq\pi,

since the rotation of a straight line is just a change in angle, which is bounded by
\pi
.

If
L\not\subset\Omega
then since the initial point of
L
and the terminal point of
L
both lie in
\Omega
, it must intersect the boundary of
\Omega
an even number of times. Denote the points where it leaves
\Omega
by
\left\{{b_{\nu}}\right\}
and the ones it re-enters
\Omega
by
\left\{{e_{\nu}}\right\}
, i.e., the segment
\left({e_{\nu},b_{\nu+1}}\right)\subset\Omega
while
\left[{b_{\nu},e_{\nu}}\right]\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega
. Since
\Omega
is a quasidisk, for every
\nu

\ell_{\Omega}(b_{\nu},e_{\nu})\leq K_{\Omega}\ell_{\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega}(b_{\nu},e_{\nu})=K_{\Omega}\cdot\left|{e_{\nu}-b_{\nu}}\right|,

where

6.1
Thermodynamic formalism

For the reader’s convenience we repeat the definition of Jordan Repellers.

Definition
.

Let
\Omega
be a simply connected domain, and
\partial
be a sub-arc of its boundary,
\partial\Omega
. We call
\partial
a
Jordan Repeller
if there exists a partition of
\partial
into a finite number of non-intersecting sub-arcs, denoted
\partial_{1},\partial_{2},\cdots,\partial_{N}
(a Markov partition), and a piecewise univalent (though not necessary injective) map
F
, such that for every sub-arc in the partition,
\partial_{j}
, there exists a neighborhood
U_{j}
containing
\partial_{j}
and a univalent map
F_{j}:\,U_{j}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfying:

(1)

F|_{\partial_{j}}=F_{j}
.

(2)

(Geometry invariance)
F_{j}\left({\Omega\cap U_{j}}\right)\subset\Omega
.

(3)

(Boundary invariance)
F_{j}\left({\partial\Omega\cap U_{j}}\right)\subset\partial\Omega
.

(4)

(Markov property) The image of each
\partial_{j}
is a finite union arcs
\partial_{k}
, i.e.,
F(\partial_{j})=\underset{k\in\mathcal{A}_{j}}{\bigcup}\partial_{k}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{j}\subseteq\left\{{1,2,\cdots,N}\right\}

6.2
On Symbolic Representations of Repellers

The function

is locally invertible, i.e., on the domains

is invertible. Following Mauldin - Urbanski in

and note that

. For every finite word,

, over the alphabet

we define the map

and for every (in)finite word we denote by

.

Since

are all contracting, and the set

is compact, for every infinite word,

, the set

is a singleton. We define the map

. For more, see
[
10
]
. The left shift,

, defined by

[X]=\left\{{w\in\partial\Omega,\left({\pi^{-1}(w)}\right)_{k}=x_{k},1\leq k\leq\left|{X}\right|}\right\}=f_{X|_{n-1}}\left({\partial_{x_{n}}}\right).

This makes sense since

and when inverting the composition of functions, the order of composition is reversed.

Remark 6.4
.

For rotations the story is slightly different. The reason is that Jordan Repellers might be defined only near the boundary, but the rotation passes through the entire domain. This is why the very first step, which brings us from the marked point,

Remark 6.7

We would like to present similar definitions for the dimension and the distortion spectra in the context of words. However, while the initial diameter of the repeller has no effect on the harmonic measure or the rotation, as these are scale-invariant, it has a grave impact on the multiplicativity property of the diameter. Looking at the case of a Carleson fractal, that is, when all maps are linear, the magnitude of the derivative of each map is
\frac{\diam([a_{j}])}{\diam(\partial)}
, which implies that the diameter of a word compared to the diameter of the letters composing it is

\frac{\diam([a_{1}a_{2}])}{\diam([a_{1}])\cdot\diam([a_{2}])}=\frac{\diam([a_{1}])\cdot\frac{\diam([a_{2}])}{\diam(\partial)}}{\diam([a_{1}])\cdot\diam([a_{2}])}=\frac{1}{\diam(\partial)}.

For a word composed of
n
letters, the latter should be raised to a power of
n-1
. This demonstrates that some normalization is required for the analysis of the spectra, where the multiplicativity property of the harmonic measure and of the rotation should go hand in hand with the multiplicativity of the diameter.

A similar effect is obtained by the rotation and the harmonic measure of the repeller. We therefore define the spectra of the repeller using these normalised quantities.

We will abuse the notation of harmonic measure, rotation, and diameter from now on, to refer to their renormalised counterparts.

6.5
Propagation:

For the reader’s convenience we repeat the definition of a propagating function here:

Definition
.

A function
:(0,1)^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

propagates
if there exists a constant
C
, so that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

\frac{\log\varphi\left({\delta^{n},C\cdot\eta}\right)}{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta^{n}}}\right)}\geq\frac{\log\varphi(\delta,\eta)}{\log\left({\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)}

for every
\delta

\eta

The first observation is that for Jordan Repellers, the functions
\varphi(\delta,\eta):=N_{word}^{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\left({\delta,\alpha,\gamma,\eta}\right),\;\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\left\{{+,-}\right\}
and
\varphi((1-r),\left|{a^{\prime}-a}\right|)=\#\Gamma(a^{\prime},b,r)
propagate, making this property interesting.

Corollary 6.13
