Concurrent improvement of cardiorespiratory fitness and modulation of inflammatory markers via 12-week high-intensity interval nordic walking in postmenopausal women with overweight or obesity: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Liang Tan, Kinga Humińska-Lisowska, Łukasz Radzimiński, Qing Zhang, Monika Michałowska-Sawczyn, Magdalena Dzitkowska-Zabielska, Zbigniew Ossowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMany postmenopausal women (PMW) with overweight and obesity experience reduced cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) and chronic inflammation, predisposing them to various diseases. However, the effects of High-Intensity Interval Nordic Walking (HIINW) as exercise therapy on these markers remain underexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate physiological responses to a 12-week HIINW program on CRF and inflammatory markers, and their potential correlations, in PMW.MethodsThis quasi-experimental study assigned 45 PMW (age: 69.6 ± 3.93 years; BMI: 27.52 ± 4.34 kg/m²) with overweight/obesity to either an HIINW group (HIINWG; n = 23) or a control group (CG; n = 22). The HIINWG underwent the exercise intervention 3 times a week for 12 weeks, while the CG maintained their habitual lifestyle. Absolute and relative VO2max, along with serum C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and the anti-inflammatory adipokine adiponectin, were measured at baseline and post-intervention. Statistical analyses included repeated-measures ANOVA to assess group × time interactions, followed by post-hoc t-tests, and Pearson correlation analysis to evaluate the relationships between changes in CRF and inflammatory markers.ResultsPost-intervention, HIINWG exhibited greater relative VO2max than CG (36.29 ± 5.63 vs. 32.18 ± 5.17 mL/kg/min, P < 0.01). HIINWG also showed significantly lower CRP (1.17 ± 0.92 vs. 1.65 ± 0.30 mg/L), TNF-α (7.42 ± 0.91 vs. 9.9 ± 1.37 pg/mL), and IL-6 (5.15 ± 1.03 vs. 7.13 ± 3.34 pg/mL), and higher adiponectin (8.55 ± 4.17 vs. 6.18 ± 3.09 µg/mL) compared to CG (all P < 0.05). Significant group×time interactions were observed for all variables (P < 0.05). Changes in absolute VO2max correlated negatively with IL-6 (r=-0.515, P < 0.05), while relative VO2max changes correlated positively with adiponectin (r = 0.427, P < 0.05).ConclusionsA 12-week HIINW intervention significantly enhanced CRF and modulated inflammatory markers (decreased CRP, TNF-α, IL-6; increased adiponectin) in PMW with overweight and obesity. CRF improvements correlated with specific inflammatory markers, providing preliminary evidence of a potential link between cardiovascular adaptations and inflammatory responses. These findings suggest HIINW may serve as a beneficial strategy for concurrent CRF enhancement and inflammation management.Trial registrationThe trial was registered with the Clinical Trials Research Center on 01/17/2025 (NCT06781541, https://clinicaltrials.gov/).

Introduction

As the world’s population surges, nearly 10% of the global population is in the postmenopausal stage, with about 47 million women entering menopause globally each year, and the global population of postmenopausal women (PMW) is projected to grow to 1.2 billion by 2030 [1]. Moreover, some studies have shown that the prevalence of overweight/obesity in PMW is as high as 43% [2]. Studies have shown that the endocrine system of PMW undergoes significant physiological changes as they age, particularly a sharp decline in estrogen levels [3]. Estrogen has anti-inflammatory properties, so a decline in estrogen levels will result in an increased inflammatory response [4]. Obesity and inflammation share a complex, bidirectional relationship. While systemic inflammation can disrupt metabolic homeostasis, the expansion of adipose tissue, often influenced by dietary composition, actively promotes the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, creating a vicious cycle. Consequently, PMW with overweight or obesity often exhibit a distinctive chronic systemic inflammatory profile, driven by the synergistic interaction between estrogen deficiency and adipose tissue dysfunction [5], a bidirectional relationship in which inflammation serves both as a key contributing factor and a consequence [6].

In PMW struggling with overweight and obesity, chronic inflammatory conditions predispose them to a wide range of diseases that require long-term treatment (e.g., breast cancer, multiple sclerosis, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, diabetes mellitus, and others) [7–10], and the process of treating these diseases is not only physically and psychologically distressing for the patients themselves, but also places a significant financial burden on their families and society. In the context of the continuous progress and development of modern medicine, a variety of complementary therapies have emerged in order to alleviate the side effects and adverse symptoms suffered by patients in the course of treatment.

Exercise therapy has gained recognition as a vital non-pharmacological strategy for chronic disease management. Crucially, cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), typically measured as VO2max, is increasingly recognized as a vital indicator in exercise therapy. In aging populations with obesity, lower CRF often accompanies metabolic imbalances and systemic inflammation. Gradually improving CRF through tailored exercise programs can facilitate the restoration of metabolic health and contribute to a more favorable systemic inflammatory profile. Rather than replacing pharmacological interventions, it serves as a powerful complementary approach. Similar to pharmacological treatments, the efficacy of exercise for long-term health management relies heavily on consistent adherence and the appropriate prescription of training variables, specifically volume, intensity, and frequency [11].

Nordic walking(NW) originated in Finland, originally as a summer training method for cross-country skiers. The exercise transforms walking into a full-body workout by utilizing special canes and using leg muscle strength to synergize the activities of the upper body, back, and core muscles [12].NW is recognized as a highly effective and safe health intervention among many physical activity activities [13]. Compared with normal walking exercise, NW needs to recruit more muscles to increase walking speed, which increases calorie output and oxygen consumption and can effectively burn fat and substantially improve CRF [14]. At the same time, NW relies on a stick, which allows the intensity of the exercise to be adjusted at any time, doesn’t require a higher level of physical activity, exerts less pressure on the joints, and allows even the elderly and infirm to participate with ease, greatly reducing the risk of falling during exercise for the elderly [15]. The positive effects of NW in improving muscle strength, blood glucose levels, and gait characteristics in older women have been well-studied in a review of existing studies [13].

Although recent meta-analyses confirm the efficacy of combined exercise modalities in managing inflammation [6], the specific physiological impacts of High-Intensity Interval Nordic Walking (HIINW) remain underexplored. While Nordic Walking effectively engages upper-body musculature and reduces lower-limb joint impact, HIIT is widely recognized for its time-efficient capacity to rapidly enhance CRF. By uniquely integrating these two modalities, HIINW presents a highly promising and comprehensive intervention. However, it remains unclear whether its anti-inflammatory effects are intrinsically linked to improvements in CRF. Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether a 12-week HIINW program can simultaneously improve CRF and modulate inflammatory markers in PMW with overweight or obesity, while seeking to explore the potential correlation between VO2max enhancements and inflammatory profile shifts. Ultimately, considering the physical vulnerability of the PMW with overweight or obesity, we attempt to evaluate the potential of HIINW as a safe, joint-sparing therapeutic strategy for simultaneously enhancing CRF and mitigating chronic low-grade inflammation.

Methods

Study design and participants

This quasi-experimental study, which compared a Nordic Walking group (HIINWG, targeting High-Intensity Interval Nordic Walking intervention) with a control group (CG), was conducted in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the TREND Statement [16]. Before participant allocation, individuals were first stratified based on their residential city and specific community location to mitigate potential geographic biases. Following this stratification, participants were allocated to either the HIINWG or CG. Because the intervention required a significant time commitment, the final group assignment was intentionally determined based on participants’ personal preferences, scheduling availability, and practical constraints to maximize intervention adherence. Notably, while the initial assignment was informed by this randomized sequence, the final grouping was finalized with careful consideration of the participants’ personal preferences and practical constraints to ensure high intervention adherence.

This stratification and preference-aware approach mitigated potential biases arising from urban differences in living conditions, specifically regarding access to fitness facilities and daily activity patterns. By assigning subgroups within the same geographic area while respecting participant willingness, the study achieved a better balance in key baseline variables such as daily physical activity levels and healthcare facility utilization. While participants and intervention coaches were not blinded to group assignments, evaluators responsible for data collection and statisticians analyzing the results were blinded to both participant grouping and geographic stratification details to avoid subjective bias.

Notably, all participants completed their assessments in the Gdańsk region, with identical testing methods and instruments used across all evaluations to ensure the reliability of experimental data.

The study adhered to the ethical principles for medical research involving human participants as specified in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Gdańsk Regional Medical Society (approval decision number: KB-5/22) on 25/01/2022. The training intervention was conducted between 23/02/2022 and 08/06/2022. The trial was retrospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov on 01/17/2025 (identifier: NCT06781541).

In the present study, postmenopausal women were defined according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria as individuals who had experienced 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea, with no other apparent physiological or pathological causes and no clinical interventions to induce this state [17]. Overweight and obesity were categorized based on BMI thresholds: overweight was defined as a BMI > 25 kg/m², while obesity was classified as a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² [18].

The recruitment effort targeted 225 women from clinical institutions and local community groups (including the University of the Third Age) in the Gdańsk and Gdynia regions. Following initial screening using the WHO’s criteria for postmenopausal status and overweight/obesity, 51 women were selected for the study sample (see Fig. 1 for the detailed recruitment flowchart).

Flowchart of participant selection

To be eligible for participation, women were required to meet all of the following criteria: (A) Absence of menstrual periods for the past 12 months; (B) Classification as overweight or obese (per WHO BMI standards); (C) Engagement in moderate-to-high-intensity physical activity fewer than three times per week, with each session lasting no more than 30 min, over the previous six months; (D) No current use of hormone replacement therapy, anti-inflammatory medications, anti-diabetic drugs, anti-hypertensive agents, or statins; (E) No medical contraindications to physical exercise; (F) No history of cardiovascular disease, pulmonary disease, hypertension, diabetes, severe hepatic or renal disease, osteoarthritis, or movement disorders.

Notably, no participants received a targeted dietary intervention during the study period. All women who volunteered and met the above eligibility criteria were invited to participate. Ultimately, 45 participants completed the full trial protocol—including providing a fasting blood sample and undergoing anthropometric measurements. These participants ranged in age from 63 to 79 years, with a mean age of 69.6 years (SD = 3.93).

Exercise program

Consistent with WHO physical activity guidelines, which suggested that health benefits could be achieved through 3 to 5 sessions of moderate-to-high-intensity activity weekly [19], this study implemented a 12-week exercise program. Following recommendations for older adults to enhance functional capacity and reduce fall risk [20], the program was designed to consist of three 60-minute sessions per week. These parameters were established based on standardized intensity metrics for physical activities [21, 22]. This 12-week duration was specifically selected as it was widely established in the literature as a sufficient and necessary timeframe to elicit stable cardiovascular adaptations [23], and to induce meaningful chronic modulations in systemic inflammatory markers within previously sedentary, overweight populations [24].

Each session adhered to guidelines from the International Nordic Walking Federation (INWA) [19] and included three components: a 10-minute warm-up, 40 minutes of NW (integrated into HIIT intervals), and a 10-minute cool-down. During the warm-up phase, participants performed pole-specific mobility exercises, with heart rate gradually elevated to 50–60% of maximum heart rate (HRmax). The primary training segment utilized a HIIT structure: 60 seconds of high-intensity NW (targeting 75–80% HRmax), followed by 60 seconds of active recovery. Participant HRmax was estimated using the formula: HRmax = 220 - age [25]. For this cohort, the 75–80% HRmax target corresponded to approximately 53–63% of the heart rate reserve (HRR) [26]. Although this range was lower than the general ACSM criteria for HIIT in healthy adults, it was classified as a ‘high-intensity’ stimulus based on the specific physiological profile of our participants. In deconditioned older adults, the anaerobic threshold often occurred at lower relative intensities [27, 28]. Furthermore, maintaining higher intensities (> 80% HRR) during Nordic walking would have necessitated running, which significantly increased musculoskeletal risk in individuals with obesity.

This intensity prescription represented a pragmatic, safe, and effective adaptation of interval training for this vulnerable demographic. The total number of intervals performed per session varied between 8 and 13. Rather than enforcing a rigid progression schedule, this variability was driven by an individualized, autoregulated approach designed to maximize safety for this deconditioned cohort. The initial interval dose was determined by the participant’s baseline cardiorespiratory fitness, with less fit individuals starting at 8 intervals. Subsequently, the exact volume was titrated session-by-session based on real-time physiological feedback. Participants were allowed to progress up to 13 intervals only if they maintained the target intensity (75–80% HRmax) with a Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) ≤ 14. Conversely, if RPE reached ≥ 15 or signs of excessive fatigue emerged, the session was capped early to prevent overexertion. Across the entire 12-week intervention, the mean number of completed high-intensity bouts was 10.2 ± 1.6 intervals per session. The cool-down phase incorporated both static and dynamic flexibility exercises, with Nordic poles used as aids to maintain proper form (See Fig. 2 for details of the training program). Exercise intensity was continuously monitored throughout each session using a Polar V-800 fitness monitor (Polar Electro Oy, Finland).

A key methodological strength of this intervention was that all training sessions were strictly supervised. Every session was led and monitored by certified NW instructors. This supervision ensured proper walking technique, immediate real-time adjustments to intensity to maintain the target heart rate, and maximal participant safety. Furthermore, to standardize the biomechanical and metabolic demands of the exercise, all sessions were conducted in a consistent environment. The training took place outdoors in a local park on flat gravel and asphalt surfaces. The designated routes featured no significant inclines, ensuring that the progressive cardiovascular overload was achieved entirely through adjustments in walking velocity and interval duration, rather than topographical changes.

Measurement of parameters

Anthropometric and body composition measurements

Researchers obtained height measurements using a stadiometer. For this assessment, participants were instructed to remove their shoes and hats, then adopt a natural standing posture with their heels, hips, and shoulders pressed firmly against the stadiometer’s vertical bar. A trained assessor gently lowered the device’s sliding horizontal platform onto the top of each participant’s head, read the scale to the nearest millimeter, and documented the value.

Body weight and BFM were evaluated using the Octopole Bioimpedance InBody 720 analyzer (Biospace, Seoul, South Korea).

Cardiorespiratory fitness assessment

CRF (VO2max) was estimated using the validated Ekblom-Bak submaximal cycle ergometer test [29]. Following standard pre-test resting conditions, participants completed two consecutive 4-minute stages at a constant pedaling rate of 60 rpm on a calibrated ergometer. The protocol consisted of an initial low standard work rate (30 watts), followed by an individualized higher work rate (45–90 watts). For our cohort of older women, the higher resistance was strictly individualized. It was adjusted to elicit a target RPE of approximately 14, while simultaneously ensuring that the participant’s heart rate did not exceed the submaximal safety threshold of 85% of their age-predicted HRmax. Across the entire cohort, this individualized approach resulted in observed steady-state heart rates ranging from 110 to 140 bpm during the higher work rate stage.

Steady-state heart rate was determined by averaging values recorded during the final minute of each stage. AbsoluteVO2max (L/min) was then calculated using the established sex-specific equation for women, which incorporates age, changes in heart rate and power output between stages, and the steady-state heart rate of the standard stage. Relative VO2max (mL/kg/min) was obtained by dividing the absolute value by the participant’s body weight.

Blood collection and inflammatory marker analysis

For the blood analysis, we assessed classical pro-inflammatory markers alongside adiponectin, an adipokine recognized as a crucial modulator of systemic inflammation. Guided by principles of clinical relevance and pathological mechanisms, the selected pro-inflammatory factors included C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). Crucially, adiponectin was evaluated not as a traditional inflammatory biomarker, but explicitly as an adipose tissue-derived adipokine with potent anti-inflammatory properties and well-documented metabolic regulatory functions [30].

Venous blood samples were collected in the early morning from all participants, who maintained a fasting state (≥ 12 h of fasting, with only small volumes of water permitted). To minimize confounding effects, participants were instructed to avoid strenuous physical activity for 24 h and refrain from alcohol consumption for 72 h before sample collection. A certified phlebotomist performed venipuncture on the antecubital median vein of the upper limb, applying a tourniquet for no longer than 1 min to collect 5 mL of venous blood into Sarstedt S-Monovette tubes (S-Monovette® Sarstedt AG&Co, Nümbrecht, Germany).

Following collection, each tube was gently inverted five times to ensure thorough mixing, then allowed to stand at room temperature (22 ± 2 °C) for 45 min to facilitate complete blood coagulation. Serum was separated via centrifugation at 1500 × g for 10 min at room temperature. Hemolyzed or lipemic samples were excluded from analysis to ensure data accuracy. Using low-protein adsorption pipette tips, the remaining serum was aliquoted into pre-chilled cryovials (with a minimum volume of 200 µL per vial). All samples were transferred to an ultra-low temperature refrigerator (-80 °C) for long-term storage within 1 h of processing, and repeated freezing and thawing of samples was strictly avoided.

Pro-inflammatory markers (CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6) were analyzed using the MAGPIX Fluorescence Detection System (Luminex Corp., Austin, TX, USA) in conjunction with the Luminex Human Magnetic Assay [6-Plex] (Luminex Corp.). Serum adiponectin concentrations were quantified using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (EMD Millipore Corporation, St Charles, Missouri, USA). The suitability of serum samples for analysis was verified in accordance with the kit’s instruction manual, with a recovery rate of ≥ 90% used as the validation standard.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses for this study were performed using SPSS 23.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Baseline data were summarized using descriptive statistics, with results reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to evaluate the normal distribution of the data.

To assess the intervention effects, a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to each outcome variable (i.e., CRF and inflammatory markers) to evaluate the main effects of time and group, as well as the group × time interaction. When a significant interaction was observed, post-hoc analyses were conducted: paired samples t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-intervention data within each group, while independent samples t-tests were employed to analyze post-intervention differences between the HIINWG and CG. Before conducting independent samples t-tests, the Levene test was used to verify variance homogeneity; if variance heterogeneity was observed (P < 0.05), Welch’s corrected t-test was implemented.

Furthermore, to explore the relationship between aerobic improvements and anti-inflammatory benefits, Pearson’s correlation analysis or Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was utilized to investigate the correlations between the changes in CRF (ΔVO2max) and the changes in inflammatory markers. Pearson’s analysis was selected when the assumption of bivariate normality was satisfied, whereas Spearman’s rank correlation was used for non-normally distributed data. For all analyses, a two-tailed significance threshold was set at P < 0.05, with P < 0.01 denoting a highly significant difference.

Following the intervention, the HIINWG demonstrated substantial improvements in CRF. Specifically, absolute VO2max increased by 0.12 L/min (SD = 0.23), and, relative VO2max increased by a mean of 2.36 mL/kg/min (SD = 3.45), both representing significant within-group improvements (P < 0.01 vs. Pre). In contrast, the CG showed negligible changes, with a slight decrease in relative VO2max of 0.47 mL/kg/min (SD = 3.19). Consequently, between-group comparisons confirmed that post-intervention CRF levels were significantly higher in the HIINWG compared to the CG for both absolute (P < 0.05) and relative VO2max (P < 0.01).

Regarding the systemic inflammatory profile, the HIINWG exhibited a marked reduction in pro-inflammatory markers compared to baseline. CRP levels decreased by a mean of 0.62 mg/L (SD = 0.61), alongside significant reductions in circulating TNF-α (mean decrease of 0.69 pg/mL, SD = 0.89) and IL-6 (mean decrease of 1.43 pg/mL, SD = 0.82) (all P < 0.01 vs. Pre). Concurrently, the anti-inflammatory adipokine, adiponectin, showed a clinically meaningful and statistically significant increase of 1.87 µg/mL (SD = 2.51, P < 0.05 vs. Pre). These favorable modulations were not observed in the CG. Ultimately, the HIINWG presented with significantly lower post-intervention levels of CRP (P < 0.05), TNF-α (P < 0.01), and IL-6 (P < 0.05), and significantly higher adiponectin levels (P < 0.05) compared to the CG.

Correlations between changes in cardiorespiratory fitness and inflammatory markers

Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between the changes in CRF (ΔVO2max) and the changes in inflammatory markers in the HIINWG following the 12-week intervention (Table 3). The results revealed a significant negative correlation between the change in absolute VO2max (ΔVO2max L/min) and the change in the pro-inflammatory marker IL-6 (r = -0.515, p < 0.05). Furthermore, a significant positive correlation was observed between the change in relative VO2max (ΔVO2max ml/kg/min) and the change in the anti-inflammatory marker adiponectin (r = 0.427, p < 0.05). No significant correlations were found between the changes in VO2max and the changes in CRP or TNF-α (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

The primary findings of this study suggest that a 12-week HIINW intervention can effectively improve CRF and favorably modulate the systemic inflammatory profile in PMW with overweight and obesity. Specifically, the HIINW intervention resulted in notable increases in both absolute and relative VO2max. Concurrently, it promoted a positive shift in the inflammatory response, characterized by significant reductions in circulating pro-inflammatory markers (CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6) and a clinically meaningful elevation in the anti-inflammatory adipokine, adiponectin. These results provide encouraging evidence that HIINW may serve as a valuable and targeted strategy for mitigating the compounded cardiovascular and metabolic risks associated with the postmenopausal transition.

The observed enhancement in CRF is consistent with the physiological characteristics of the HIINW modality. In aging populations where obesity can exacerbate functional decline, improving CRF is generally recognized as a relevant target for metabolic health [31]. From a physiological perspective, the use of Nordic poles facilitates the recruitment of the shoulder girdle, back, and core musculature [32]. This broadens the exercise to include more upper-body involvement, engaging a greater total muscle mass compared to conventional walking [33]. When this increased muscular engagement is combined with the cardiovascular demands of high-intensity intervals, it may contribute to a higher total oxygen demand [34, 35]. The interval nature of the training also appears advantageous; it allows participants to achieve higher levels of cardiovascular exertion than might be sustainable during continuous moderate-intensity exercise, while the active recovery periods help manage fatigue [23]. Consequently, the improvements in both absolute and relative VO2max observed in our study likely reflect favorable adaptations in skeletal muscle oxidative capacity and overall cardiovascular efficiency [36].

Building upon these improvements in CRF, our study sought to explore its potential relationship with the systemic inflammatory network. The data revealed a significant inverse correlation between changes in absolute VO2max and IL-6, and a positive correlation between changes in relative VO2max and adiponectin. These associations are particularly intriguing as they suggest a potential association between cardiovascular adaptations and endocrine regulation [37]. IL-6 is a highly sensitive cytokine that, in the context of obesity, is often chronically elevated and contributes to ongoing metabolic stress [38]. Its inverse relationship with absolute VO2max implies that enhancing the body’s overall aerobic metabolic “engine” might help mitigate chronic pro-inflammatory signaling [39]. As skeletal muscle becomes more efficient at utilizing oxygen, the basal state of systemic stress is likely reduced [39]. Similarly, the positive correlation between relative VO2max and adiponectin offers valuable metabolic insights. Unlike absolute VO2max, relative VO2max accounts for total body mass, meaning that improvements in this specific metric reflect not only enhanced cardiovascular capacity but also concurrent reductions in body weight and fat mass [40, 41]. Adiponectin is a crucial insulin-sensitizing and anti-inflammatory hormone secreted by adipose tissue, yet its production is typically blunted in obesity [42]. Because adiponectin levels typically rise as excess body fat is reduced, its association with relative VO2max is physiologically consistent [43]. This suggests that the concurrent benefits of HIINW, specifically the improvement in aerobic metabolic efficiency and the reduction of body fat, may work in tandem to alleviate cellular stress on adipose tissue [44]. Consequently, these weight-adjusted aerobic improvements appear to facilitate the restoration of a more balanced endocrine microenvironment, encouraging a healthier interaction between skeletal muscle and adipose tissue [37].

While changes in CRP and TNF-α did not exhibit direct linear correlations with VO2max alterations in our specific sample, their significant overall reductions following the 12-week intervention provide crucial context regarding the broad systemic benefits of HIINW. The modulation of these markers is likely a multifaceted physiological response rather than the direct result of a single adaptive pathway. Regular participation in HIINW encourages a sustained reduction in the metabolic burden typically exerted by excess adipose tissue, largely driven by the cumulative increase in daily energy expenditure [45, 46]. Additionally, the recurring, manageable physiological stress provided by interval training is well-documented to foster long-term anti-inflammatory adaptations, potentially by downregulating systemic inflammatory tone and improving vascular endothelial function over time [47, 48]. Therefore, the concurrent, significant decline across multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines suggests that HIINW offers a comprehensive, systemic anti-inflammatory effect that acts in parallel with, and complements, the specific aerobic correlations we observed [24, 49].

From a clinical and practical standpoint, the intersection of these findings positions HIINW as an exceptionally promising and accessible paradigm for PMW. This demographic often faces barriers to traditional high-impact exercise due to age-related musculoskeletal vulnerability and the mechanical overload associated with excess body weight [50]. HIINW circumvents these barriers by utilizing poles to redistribute weight, thereby minimizing lower-limb joint impact and reducing the risk of osteoarthritic discomfort [51]. Concurrently, the time-efficient nature of HIIT may help overcome common motivational barriers [52]. The high adherence rate observed in our study underscores that when exercise prescriptions are tailored to accommodate physical constraints while still providing robust cardiovascular stimuli, they can successfully translate into sustainable lifestyle interventions [53].

However, the present findings should be interpreted in light of several methodological limitations. First, due to early administrative and organizational reasons, this trial was retrospectively registered; furthermore, the quasi-experimental design utilized primarily elucidates correlational associations, inherently precluding the establishment of definitive causal relationships. Second, cardiorespiratory fitness was estimated using an indirect, submaximal protocol. While this approach was deliberately chosen to maximize cardiovascular safety and participant compliance among older adults with overweight/obesity, it lacks the absolute precision of direct gas exchange analysis. Third, the study did not objectively monitor or tightly control for potential confounding lifestyle variables, such as variations in daily caloric intake, dietary macronutrient composition, and non-exercise daily physical activity (e.g., daily step counts), all of which can independently influence metabolic inflammation. Fourth, the baseline fitness level of our study cohort was moderate; therefore, caution should be exercised when generalizing the physiological adaptations observed in this study to profoundly deconditioned populations. Notably, the control group exhibited a significant within-group increase in TNF-α and a decrease in adiponectin over the 12-week period. These deleterious changes likely reflect the natural, progressive trajectory of inflammatory dysregulation in postmenopausal women with overweight or obesity. The absence of lifestyle control measures further underscores this progression, highlighting the critical need for active interventions like HIINW to halt or reverse this age- and adiposity-related metabolic decline. This observation also serves as a study limitation, warranting further investigation into the seasonal or long-term behavioral factors influencing baseline inflammation in this un-intervened demographic. Future well-powered, fully randomized controlled trials incorporating stringent dietary tracking, direct gas exchange analysis, and longer follow-up periods are warranted to further elucidate these physiological relationships. Nonetheless, our findings provide encouraging, holistic evidence that HIINW may serve as a highly beneficial, joint-sparing strategy for simultaneously nurturing CRF and managing chronic low-grade inflammation.

Conclusions

A 12-week period of HIINW significantly improved absolute and relative CRF (VO2max) and systematically modulated inflammatory markers by decreasing CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6 levels while simultaneously increasing adiponectin levels in PMW with overweight and obesity. We observed that the CRF improvement was significantly correlated with the regulation of specific inflammatory factors, providing preliminary evidence of a potential association between cardiovascular adaptations and endocrine regulation. Therefore, based on these exploratory findings, HIINW appears to be a promising and safe therapeutic strategy to simultaneously enhance CRF and mitigate chronic low-grade inflammation in PMW with overweight and obesity. Future investigations should aim to decipher the precise biochemical and molecular pathways mediating these beneficial exercise-induced adaptations.
