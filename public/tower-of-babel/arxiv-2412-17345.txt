On the Power and Limitations of Examples for Description Logic Concepts
Balder ten Cate, Raoul Koudijs, Ana Ozaki

Abstract
Labeled examples (i.e., positive and negative examples) are an attractive medium for communicating complex concepts. They are useful for deriving concept expressions (such as in concept learning, interactive concept specification, and concept refinement) as well as for illustrating concept expressions to a user or domain expert. We investigate the power of labeled examples for describing description-logic concepts. Specifically, we systematically study the existence and efficient computability of finite characterisations , i.e., finite sets of labeled examples that uniquely characterize a single concept, for a wide variety of description logics between ℰ ​ ℒ {\cal E\!L} and 𝒜 ​ ℒ ​ 𝒞 ​ 𝒬 ​ ℐ \mathcal{ALCQI} ,both without an ontology and in the presence of a DL-Lite ontology. Finite characterisations are relevant for debugging purposes, and their existence is a necessary condition for exact learnability with membership queries.

Basic Definitions and Framework

In the following, we first define the syntax and semantics of
concept and ontology languages considered in this work.

Concept Languages

Let
{\sf N_{C}}
and
{\sf N_{R}}

be infinite and mutually disjoint sets of
concept
and
role
names, respectively. We work with finite subsets
\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}\subseteq{\sf N_{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}\subseteq{\sf N_{R}}
of these. We denote by
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\geq,-,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot,\neg)[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]
the following concept language:

\displaystyle C,D::=

\displaystyle A\mid\top\mid\bot\mid(C\sqcap D)\mid(C\sqcup D)|

\displaystyle\neg C\mid(\exists S.C)\mid(\forall S.C)\mid(\geq kS.C)

where
k\geq 1
,

A\in\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}
and
S=R
or
S=R^{-}
for some
R\in\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}
. Note that
\exists S.C
is equivalent to
\geq 1S.C
.

For any set of operators
\mathbf{O}\subseteq\{\forall,\exists,\geq,-,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot,\neg\}
, we denote by
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]
the fragment of
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\geq,-,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot,\neg)
that only uses the constructs in
\mathbf{O}
, concept names from
\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}
and role names from
\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}
. We may omit
[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]
from

and simply write
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})
if the role and concepts names are clear from context or irrelevant.

Observe that
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\geq,-,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot,\neg)
is a notational variant of

\mathcal{ALCQI}
, while, e.g.,

\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top)
corresponds
to
\mathcal{EL}
and
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\sqcap,\top,\bot)

to
\mathcal{ALE}

Ontology Language

We consider the very popular

B\sqsubseteq C

respectively, where

Semantics

An
interpretation

\mathcal{I}=(\Delta^{\mathcal{I}},\cdot^{\mathcal{I}})
w.r.t. a signature
(\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}})
is a
structure, in the traditional model-theoretic sense. It consists of a non-empty set
\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
, called the
domain
, and a function
\cdot^{\mathcal{I}}
that assigns a subset
A^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
of the domain to each
A\in\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}
and a binary relation
R^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\times\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
over the domain to each
R\in\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}
.
We extend
(\cdot)^{\mathcal{I}}
to concept and role expressions as follows.

\displaystyle(\top)^{\mathcal{I}}:={}

\displaystyle\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\quad(\bot)^{\mathcal{I}}:={}\emptyset\quad(C\sqcap D)^{\mathcal{I}}:={}C^{\mathcal{I}}\cap D^{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle(\neg C)^{\mathcal{I}}:={}

\displaystyle\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\setminus C^{\mathcal{I}}\qquad\quad\quad(C\sqcup D)^{\mathcal{I}}:={}C^{\mathcal{I}}\cup D^{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle(\exists S.C)^{\mathcal{I}}:={}

\displaystyle\{d\mid\exists e\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\text{ s.t. }(d,e)\in S^{\mathcal{I}}\text{ and }e\in C^{\mathcal{I}}\}

\displaystyle(\geq kS.C)^{\mathcal{I}}:={}

\displaystyle\{d\mid\exists e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\;\text{pairwise-distinct s.t.}

\displaystyle(d,e_{i})\in S^{\mathcal{I}}\text{ and }e_{i}\in C^{\mathcal{I}}\;\text{for all}\;1\leq i\leq k\}

\displaystyle(\forall S.C)^{\mathcal{I}}:={}

\displaystyle\{d\mid\forall e\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\text{ if }(d,e)\in S^{\mathcal{I}}\text{ then }e\in C^{\mathcal{I}}\}

\displaystyle(R^{-})^{\mathcal{I}}:={}

\displaystyle\{(e,d)\mid(d,e)\in R^{\mathcal{I}}\}.

A

Size of Concepts and Examples

For any concept
C\in\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\geq,-,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot)

Let
\mathsf{nr}(C)

Further, let
\mathsf{dp}(C)
be the

(Finite Controllability)

Theorem 2.2
.

Labeled Examples and Finite characterisations

An
example

(\mathcal{I},d)

+

-

(\mathcal{I},d)

C

E=(E^{+},E^{-})
to denote
a finite collection of examples labeled as positive
and negative. A concept
C

fits
a set of positively and negatively labelled examples if it is satisfied on all the positively labelled examples and not satisfies on any of the negatively labelled examples.

1

Equivalently, a concept
C
fits a set of positively and negatively labelled examples if all the positively labelled examples are positive examples for
C
, and all the negatively labelled examples are negative examples for
C

A
finite characterisation
for a concept
C
w.r.t. concept language
\mathcal{L}
is a finite collection of labeled examples
E=(E^{+},E^{-})
such that
C
fits
E
and every concept
D\in\mathcal{L}
that fits

is equivalent to
C
. A concept language
\mathcal{L}

admits finite characterisations
if for every pair of finite sets
\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}\subseteq{\sf N_{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}\subseteq{\sf N_{R}}
, every concept
C\in\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]
has a finite characterisation w.r.t.
\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]
. We say that
\mathcal{L}

admits polynomial-time computable characterisations
if, furthermore, there is an algorithm that, given an input concept
C\in\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]
outputs a finite characterisation
E=(E^{+},E^{-})
of
C
w.r.t.
\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]
in time polynomial in the size of the concept
|C|
and the size of the signature
|\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}|+|\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}|
. This implies that
E^{+}
and
E^{-}
consist of polynomially many, polynomial-size examples. Therefore, if
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})
admits polynomial time computable characterisations it has polynomial
teaching size
in the sense of

We defined examples as
finite

pointed interpretations, because
they are intended as objects that
can be communicated to a user and/or specified by a user. This is justified by Thm.
2.1
, which
shows that any two non-equivalent concepts can be distinguished by a finite pointed interpretation.

\mathcal{I}
be the interpretation with

•

\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}=\{a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime},c,c^{\prime},d,d^{\prime}\}
,

•

\text{Animal}^{\mathcal{I}}=\{a,a^{\prime},c,c^{\prime},d,d^{\prime}\}
,

•

\text{Cat}^{\mathcal{I}}=\{c,c^{\prime}\}
,

\ \text{Dog}^{\mathcal{I}}=\{d,d^{\prime}\}
,
\ \text{Red}^{\mathcal{I}}=\{a,b,c,d\}
,

which satisfies
\mathcal{O}
.
It can be shown that the collection of labeled examples
(E^{+},E^{-})
with
E^{+}=\{(\mathcal{I},c)\}
and
E^{-}=\{(\mathcal{I},a),(\mathcal{I},a^{\prime}),(\mathcal{I},b),(\mathcal{I},b^{\prime}),(\mathcal{I},c^{\prime}),(\mathcal{I},d),(\mathcal{I},d^{\prime})\}
is a finite
characterisation of
C
w.r.t.
\mathcal{L}(\sqcap,\sqcup,\neg)
under
\mathcal{O}
.
On the other hand, the same set of
examples is not a finite characterisation
under the empty ontology, because

\text{Cat}\sqcap\text{Red}\sqcap\neg\text{Dog}
also fits but is not equivalent to
C

in the absence of the ontology.
Thus, in this case, the ontology helps to reduce the number of examples needed
to uniquely characterize
C
. Moreover, the ontology can rule out unnatural examples, since in this case the characterisation would need to include a negative example satisfying
\text{Cat}\sqcap\text{Red}\sqcap\text{Dog}
.

Remark 2.3
.

We have defined an example as a pointed finite interpretation
(\mathcal{I},d)
and we call such an example a
positive example
for
C
if
d\in C^{\mathcal{I}}
and a
negative example
otherwise. In some of the prior literature on learning description logic concepts, examples were instead defined as pointed ABoxes
(\mathcal{A},a)

Funk et al. 2022
;
Konev et al. 2016
;
Ozaki et al. 2020

2
2

2

By a pointed ABox
(\mathcal{A},a)
we mean a finite set
\mathcal{A}
of assertions of the form
A(b)
and
R(b,c)
with
A
a concept name,
R
a role name, and
b,c
individual names (over the given signature), together with an individual name
a
occurring in some assertion in
\mathcal{A}
.
, while e.g.
Fortin et al. 2022
also work with finite interpretations (in their case, linear orders) as examples. Such a pointed ABox is then considered a
positive example
for
C
under an ontology
\mathcal{O}
if

a
is a certain answer for
C
in
\mathcal{A}
under
\mathcal{O}
(i.e.,
a^{\mathcal{I}}\in C^{\mathcal{I}}
for all interpretations with
\mathcal{I}\models\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{I}\models\mathcal{A}
)
, and a
negative example
otherwise.
Let us call the latter type of examples “ABox-examples”.

Consider the concepts

C=\forall R.P
and
C^{\prime}=\forall R.Q
. Although they are clearly not equivalent, it is not possible to distinguish them by means of ABox-examples (under, say, an empty ontology), for the simple reason that
there does not exist a
positive ABox-example for either of these concepts.
This motivates our decision to define examples as pointed interpretations. This is tied to the fact that we study concept languages that include universal quantification and/or negation. Hence, for concept languages contained in
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\geq,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot)
, such as
{\cal E\!L}
, there is essentially no difference between finite characterisations using ABox-examples and finite characterisations using interpretation-examples in the absence of an ontology.

However, there are some further repercussions for having interpretations instead of ABoxes as examples in the case with ontologies, even for such weaker concept languages. Intuitively, in the presence of a DL-Lite
H
-ontology
\mathcal{O}
, any ABox consistent with
\mathcal{O}
can be viewed as a succinct representation of a possibly infinite interpretation (i.e. its
chase
). Conversely, a finite interpretation can always be conceived of as an ABox. The difference
shows up as follows:
it was shown in
Funk et al. 2022

that
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,-)
admits finite
characterisations under DL-Lite
H
ontologies
in the setting where examples are pointed ABoxes. We see in Section
4
below that the same does not hold in our setting.

3
Finite Characterisations without Ontology

Our aim, in this section, is to prove
Thm.
1.1
and
1.2
. We
start by listing relevant known results.

Theorem 3.1
.

The following results are known
(cf.
Cate and Koudijs 2024
).

1.

\mathcal{L}(\exists,-,\sqcap,\top,\bot)
admits
polynomial-time computable characterisations (from
Cate and Dalmau 2022
).

2.

\mathcal{L}(\exists,-,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot)
admits
finite characterisations. However, finite characterisation are necessarily of exponential size (from
Alexe et al. 2011
).

3.

\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\sqcap,\sqcup)
admits
finite characterisations. However, finite characterisation are necessarily of non-elementary size (from
Cate and Koudijs 2024
).

4.

Neither
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\sqcap,\bot)
nor
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\sqcup,\top)
admit finite characterisations (from
Cate and Koudijs 2024
).

To establish Thm.
1.1
,
it remains to show that
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\geq,\sqcap,\top)
admits finite characterisations and that
\mathcal{L}(\geq,\bot),\mathcal{L}(\geq,\sqcup)
and
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,-,\sqcap)
do not admit finite characterisations.

|C|^{|C|}

.

Theorem 3.9
.

For each

, one can construct a frontier

.

\mathcal{L}(\geq,\sqcap,\top)

Remark 3.11
.

The polynomial-time frontier construction from Thm.

is interesting in its own right, particulary because
frontiers are closely related to
upward refinement operators
, a core component of
DL learning systems such as the ELTL variant of
DL Learner
Bühmann et al. 2016

We can show that the function

\mathcal{L}(\geq,\sqcap,\top)
-concept
to its frontier is an
ideal upward refinement operator
as defined in
Lehmann and

.
This follows
from the fact that, for every
\mathcal{L}(\geq,\sqcap,\top)
-concept

\mathcal{L}(\geq,\sqcap,\top)
-concepts

\mathsf{dp}(D)\leq\mathsf{dp}(C)
and
\mathsf{nr}(D)\leq\mathsf{nr}(C)

3.3
Negative Results

The following theorem summarizes our negative
result pertaining to the existence of finite
characterisations.

Theorem 3.12
.

\mathcal{L}(\geq,\sqcup)
,
\mathcal{L}(\geq,\bot)
and
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,-,\sqcap)

do not admit finite characterisations.

In the remainder of this section, we establish
lower bounds on the size and/or the complexity of computing
finite characterisations. The following
lemma will be helpful for this.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\sqcap\in\mathbf{O}
and
(E^{+},E^{-})
be a finite characterisation of a concept
C\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})
w.r.t.
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})
. For all
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})
, if
\not\models C\sqsubseteq C^{\prime}
then
C^{\prime}
is falsified on some positive example in
E^{+}
.

Using this lemma, we show:

Theorem 3.14
.

\mathcal{L}(\geq,\sqcap)
does not admit polynomial size characterisations. In fact, there are
\mathcal{L}(\geq,\sqcap)
-concepts
(C_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of constant depth and number restriction such that every finite characterisation of
C_{i}
has size at least
2^{|C_{i}|}
.

The next lemma establishes a connection between the problem of computing finite characterisations and the problem of checking subsumptions between concepts.

Lemma 3.15
.

If
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})
admits polynomial characterisations,
\sqcap\in\mathbf{O}
and
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})
has a polynomial time model checking problem (in combined complexity), then subsumptions between
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})
concepts can be tested in polynomial time.

This allows us to use hardness results from the literature on subsumption algorithms to establish hardness of computing certain example sets.
Note that
the converse of Lem.
3.15
fails, since by Theorem
3.4

\mathcal{L}(\geq,\sqcap,\top)
has a polynomial time subsumption problem but does not admit
polynomial-time computable characterisations
(Thm.
3.14
).

The following subsumption hardness result follows
from
Donini et al. 1992
.

Proposition 3.16
.

Subsumption checking for
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\sqcap)
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.

Using Lem.
3.15

we can the establish our next result, resolving an open question from
Cate and Koudijs 2024
.

Corollary 3.17
.

\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\sqcap)
does not admit polynomial time computable characterisations, unless
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
.

Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
follow
from the results proved in this section and prior results that we recalled in Thm.
3.1
.

4
Finite Characterisations With Ontologies

In this section we are concerned with finite characterisations of concepts w.r.t. ontologies.

4.1
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top,\bot)
Admits Finite Characterisations Under DL-Lite Ontologies

We show that
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top,\bot)
admits finite characterisations under DL-Lite ontologies.
The proof uses the following key lemma from
Funk et al. 2022
.
Recall the definition of a frontier relative to an ontology
(Def.
3.8
).

Lemma 4.1
.

(
Funk et al. 2022
)

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that takes
as input a
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top)
-concept
C
and a DL-Lite ontology
\mathcal{O}
such
that
C
is satisfiable w.r.t.
\mathcal{O}
, and outputs a frontier
for
C
w.r.t.
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top)
relative to
\mathcal{O}
.

Our second ingredient is a polynomial-time
canonical
model construction
for
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top)
-concepts w.r.t. DL-Lite ontologies.
Intuitively, one can think of a canonical model,
for a concept
C
and ontology
\mathcal{O}
, as a
minimal positive example, i.e., a
positive example for
C
under
\mathcal{O}
that
“makes as few other concepts true as possible”. We show that every
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top)
concept that is satisfiable w.r.t. a given DL-Lite ontology
\mathcal{O}
admits a polynomial-time computable canonical model w.r.t.
\mathcal{O}
that satisfies its CIs.

Theorem 4.2
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that takes a
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top)
-concept
C

and a DL-Lite ontology
\mathcal{O}
,
where
C
is satisfiable w.r.t.
\mathcal{O}
, and that
produces a pointed interpretation

(\mathcal{I}_{C,\mathcal{O}},d_{C})

(which we will call the
canonical model
of
C
under
\mathcal{O}
) with the following
properties.

1.

for all
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top)
-concepts
D
,
d_{C}\in D^{\mathcal{I}_{C,\mathcal{O}}}

iff
\mathcal{O}\models C\sqsubseteq D
. In particular, we have that
d_{C}\in C^{\mathcal{I}_{C,\mathcal{O}}}
.

2.

Moreover,
\mathcal{I}_{C,\mathcal{O}}\models\mathcal{O}
.

The proof is based on previous constructions of canonical model for query answering
Kontchakov et al. 2010
.
Canonical models for query answering usually differ from those for satisfying the ontology. In the case of the
{\cal E\!L}
ontology language (consisting of CIs
C\sqsubseteq D
where
C,D\in\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top)
), it was shown that a polynomial canonical model for
{\cal E\!L}
concepts can be constructed
Baader et al. 2005
;
Lutz and
Wolter 2010
, but it cannot be ensured that this canonical model satisfies the ontology. In fact there are
{\cal E\!L}
ontologies for which there is no
finite
canonical model satisfying the ontology
(
Guimarães et al. 2023
, Example 33)
.

Thm.
4.2

does
produce
models satisfying the DL-Lite ontology, and this is important for
our purpose.
By combining the frontier construction with the
canonical model construction, we can construct
finite characterisations in polynomial time
for concepts that are satisfiable w.r.t. a
given ontology. For inconsistent concepts, we
separately prove the following.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be a DL-Lite ontology. Then the unsatisfiable concept
\bot
admits an exponentially sized finite characterisation w.r.t.
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top,\bot)
under
\mathcal{O}
.

Given the above, we obtain the
main result of this section.

Theorem 4.4
.

\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top,\bot)
admits finite
characterisations under DL-Lite ontologies. Moreover, for concepts
C\in\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top,\bot)
that are satisfiable w.r.t. the given ontology
\mathcal{O}
,
a finite characterisation can be computed
in polynomial time (in
|C|
and
|\mathcal{O}|
).

Remark 4.5
.

The restriction to satisfiable concepts
in the above theorem is essential given that the ontology is treated as part of the input.
Specifically, let
\mathcal{O}
be the ontology

\mathcal{O}:=\{A_{0}\sqsubseteq\neg A_{0}\}\cup\{A_{i}\sqsubseteq\neg B_{i}\mid i=1\ldots n\}
. It can be shown, for the concept
A_{0}
(which is unsatisfiable w.r.t.
\mathcal{O}
), that
every finite characterisation must be of
size at least
2^{n}
(see e.g.
Funk et al. 2021
for a similar style of counterexample). If the ontology is treated as fixed in the complexity analysis, however, a finite characterisation could be computed in polynomial time for
all

\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top,\bot)
-concepts.

4.2
Negative Results

The following two negative results establish that we cannot further
enrich the concept language.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be the DL-Lite ontology
\{A\sqsubseteq\neg A\}
. Then
\mathcal{L}({\geq})
and
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\sqcap)
do not admit finite characterisations under
\mathcal{O}
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be the DL-Lite ontology

\{A\sqsubseteq\exists R,

\exists R^{-}\sqsubseteq A\}
.
Then
\mathcal{L}(\exists,-,\sqcap)
does not admit finite
characterisations under
\mathcal{O}
.

These results, together with Thm.
4.4
, imply Thm.
1.3
, as they show that no extension of
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\top,\bot)
with
\geq
or
\forall
admits finite characterisations under DL-Lite ontologies.
Our positive result in Section
4.1
also cannot be extended to DL-Lite
H
ontologies (that is, DL-Lite extended with role inclusions
Artale et al. 2009
). This contrasts with a recent positive result for
{\cal E\!L}
with DL-Lite
H
ontologies using ABox-examples
Funk et al. 2022
.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be the DL-Lite
H
ontology

\{A\sqsubseteq\exists R,\exists R^{-}\sqsubseteq A,R^{-}\sqsubseteq S\}
. Then

\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap)
does not admit finite
characterisations under
\mathcal{O}
.

5
Summary and Discussion

We systematically studied the
existence and complexity of computing finite characterisations for
concept languages
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})
with
\{\exists,\sqcap\}\subseteq\mathbf{O}\subseteq\{\forall,\exists,\geq,-,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot,\neg\}
, both in the absence of an ontology and in the presence of a DL-Lite ontology.
While we classified most concept
languages, there are a few cases left open,
detailed below.

In passing, we obtained a polynomial-time
algorithm for checking subsumptions between
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\geq,\sqcap,\top)
concepts, and a polynomial time algorithm for computing frontiers of

\mathcal{L}(\exists,\geq,\sqcap,\top)
-concepts
(which gives rise to a polynomial-time ideal upward refinement
operator, cf. Remark
3.11
).

Repercussions for Exact Learnability

As we mentioned in the introduction, the existence of polynomial-time computable characterisations is a necessary precondition for the existence of polynomial-time exact learning algorithms with membership queries.
It was shown in
Cate and Dalmau 2022
that

\mathcal{L}(\exists,-,\sqcap,\top,\bot)
is indeed polynomial-time exactly learnable with membership queries.
Thm.
1.2
therefore implies that
\mathcal{L}(\exists,-,\sqcap,\top,\bot)
is a maximal fragment of
\mathcal{ALCQI}
that is efficiently exactly learnable with membership queries, in the absence of an ontology.

Conversely, every algorithm for effectively computing finite characterisations also gives rise to an effective exact learning algorithm with membership queries, although not an efficient one. Thus,
Thm.
1.1
also tells us something about the limits of (not-necessarily efficient) exact learnability with membership queries, without an ontology.

Finally, in the same way, Thm.
1.3
tells us something about the limits of exact learnability with membership queries where the membership queries can only be asked for examples satisfying a given DL-Lite ontology.
The authors are not aware of prior work on such a notion of learnability.

Open questions

Open questions include
whether
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\geq,-,\sqcap)
or
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\geq,-,\top)
admit finite characterisations (under the empty ontology) and whether concept languages between
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\sqcup)
and
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot)
admit finite characterisations under DL-Lite ontologies.
Moreover, we do not yet have tight bounds on the complexity of computing finite characterisations for many of the concept languages we study that admit finite characterisations. In particular, for concept languages between
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\sqcap)
and
\mathcal{L}(\forall,\exists,\geq\sqcap,\top)
we have an NP lower bound (from Corollary
3.17
) and a non-elementary upper bound (from Thm.
3.3
). In Thm.
3.10
we managed to improve this to a doubly exponential upper bound for
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\geq,\sqcap,\top)
,
while an exponential lower bound for
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\geq,\sqcap)
follows from Thm.
3.14
. It remains
to find tight bounds for these fragments.

Since our setting includes
\forall
and
\neg
, we were forced to work with finite pointed interpretations as examples (cf. Remark
2.3
). If one discards these connectives it becomes natural to adopt pointed ABoxes as examples, as in
Funk et al. 2022
.
Many of our negative results for the existence of finite characterisations under DL-Lite ontologies rely on examples being finite pointed interpretations satisfying the ontology, except Thm.
4.6
because for the ontology in question, there is essentially no difference between a (consistent) ABox and a finite interpretation.
Our study therefore only leaves open the possibility of positive results in the ABox-as-examples setting under DL-Lite ontologies for fragments of

\mathcal{L}(\exists,-,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot)
.

It would be of interest to investigate finite characterisations under
ontology languages other than DL-Lite.
The recent paper
Cate et al. 2024
implies some positive results for
\mathcal{L}(\exists,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot)
relative to ontologies defined by certain universal Horn formulas (e.g. symmetry and transitivity).

One of the motivations of our study was to generate examples to illustrate a concept to a user (e.g, for debugging purposes or for educational purposes). Finding practical solutions for this require more research, e.g., on how to generate “natural” examples (cf.
Glavic et al. 2021
for an overview of
related problems and techniques in data management).

We will use the following abbreviations for writing complex nested concept. Given a concept
C
, we use
[\geq kS.]^{n}C
as a shorthand
notation for

\underbrace{\geq kS.\geq kS.\cdots.\geq kS.}_{\text{$n$ times}}C

and we use
[\forall S.]^{n}C
as a shorthand
notation for

\underbrace{\forall S.\forall S.\cdots.\forall S.}_{\text{$n$ times}}C

Formally,
[\geq kS.]^{0}C:=C
and
[\geq kS.]^{n+1}.C:=~\geq kS.([\geq kS.]^{n}.C)
, where
k,n
are natural numbers and
S
is a role name from
{\sf N_{R}}
or the inverse of a role name in
\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}
inverse. Similarly, we set
[\forall S.]^{0}C:=C
and
[\forall S.]^{n+1}C:=\forall S.[\forall S.]^{n}C
for every role name
S
or the inverse of a role name.
Given a role name
R\in{\sf N_{R}}
, we use

\overline{R}
to toggle between
a role name and its inverse.
That is, if
R\in{\sf N_{R}}
then we set
\overline{R}=R^{-}
and
\overline{R^{-}}=R
.

To simplify our proofs, we assume that all ontologies
are in
named form
, meaning that
we only allow CIs where
one of the sides is a concept name.
Every DL-Lite ontology
\mathcal{O}

can be converted in polynomial time to a conservative extension
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{O}
in named form (over an extended signature)

Krötzsch 2012
. Let
\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O})
denote the finite set of concept and role names occurring in
\mathcal{O}
.

Lemma A.1
.

Every DL-Lite ontology
\mathcal{O}
may be transformed in polynomial time into a DL-Lite ontology
\mathcal{O}^{*}
in named form such that:

•

\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O}^{*})\supseteq\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O})

•

for all CIs
B\sqsubseteq C
over
\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O})
,
\mathcal{O}\models B\sqsubseteq C
iff
\mathcal{O}^{*}\models B\sqsubseteq C

•

For every concept language
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{L}
admits finite (polynomial time computable) characterisations under
\mathcal{O}
iff
\mathcal{L}
admits finite (polynomial time computable) characterisations under
\mathcal{O}^{*}
.

Proof.

We introduce a fresh concept name
A_{\exists S}
for every basic concept
\exists S
over
\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O})
. Let
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
be the result of substituting
A_{\exists S}
for every occurrence of the basic concept
\exists S
in
\mathcal{O}
and observe that
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
is now in named form. Further, let
\mathcal{O}^{*}
be the union of
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
with

\{\exists S\sqsubseteq A_{\exists S},A_{\exists S}\sqsubseteq\exists S\mid S\in\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O})\;\text{or}\;\overline{S}\in\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O})\}

Clearly
\mathcal{O}^{*}
is in named form and can be computed from
\mathcal{O}
in polynomial time. Moreover, since
\mathcal{O}^{*}
is a conservative extension of
\mathcal{O}

Krötzsch 2012
, it is clear that points 1 and 2 hold. Hence, it rests to show point 3.

Let
\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]
be a concept language over the finite signature
(\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}})
.
Let
\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime}
be the union of
\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}
with
\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O})\cap{\sf N_{C}}
and similarly let
\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}
be the union of
\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}
with
\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O})\cap{\sf N_{R}}
. Further, let

\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime\prime}
be the union of
\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}
with
\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O}^{*})\cap{\sf N_{C}}
and similarly let
\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime\prime}
be the union of
\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}
with
\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O}^{*})\cap{\sf N_{R}}
. Observe that as
\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O})\subseteq\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O}^{*})
also
\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime}\subseteq\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime\prime}
and
\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}\subseteq\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime\prime}
.

We show that every pointed interpretation
(\mathcal{I},d)
for the signature
(\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime})
such that
\mathcal{I}\models\mathcal{O}
can be transformed in polynomial time to an interpretation
\mathcal{I}^{*}
for the signature
(\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime\prime},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime\prime})

\mathcal{I}^{*}\models\mathcal{O}^{*}

(\mathcal{I},d)

\mathcal{I}^{*}
by keeping the domain and the interpretations of all the old symbols (i.e. those in

and
\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime\prime}\setminus\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime}

A_{\exists S}^{\mathcal{I}^{+}}:=\{d\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\mid d\in(\exists S)^{\mathcal{I}}\}

\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}

d\in D^{\mathcal{I}}

d\in D^{\mathcal{I}^{*}}

\mathcal{I}^{*}

A_{\exists S}^{\mathcal{I}^{*}}=(\exists S)^{\mathcal{I}^{*}}

\mathcal{I}^{*}\models\mathcal{O}^{*}

The mapping in the other direction just forgets about the interpretation of all the symbols in
\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O}^{*})\setminus\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O})
(note that
\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O}^{*})\setminus\mathsf{sig}(\mathcal{O})=(\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime\prime}\setminus\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime})\cup(\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime\prime}\setminus\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}})

and also that the element

\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]

It follows that every finite characterisation of
C
w.r.t.
\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]

\mathcal{O}
can be transformed in polynomial time to a finite characterisation of

\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]

\mathcal{O}^{*}
and vice versa. Hence, we conclude that
\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]

\mathcal{O}
iff
\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]

Appendix F
Repercussions for Learnability

We will define what a membership query learning algorithm is for learning description logic concepts without a background ontology. For a general definition of membership query learning algorithm, we refer the reader to
Angluin 1988
.

Definition F.1

(Membership Query Learning Algorithms)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a concept language. An
exact learning algorithm with membership queries
for
\mathcal{L}

takes as input finite sets
\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}

and has to learn a hidden target concept
C\in\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]
by making
membership queries
to a membership oracle. In a membership query, the learner presents an example
(\mathcal{I},d)

to the oracle, to which the oracle faithfully has to answer “yes” if
d\in C^{\mathcal{I}}
and “no” otherwise. After asking finitely many such membership queries, the algorithm terminates and
outputs a concept expression
D\in\mathcal{L}[\Sigma_{\mathsf{C}},\Sigma_{\mathsf{R}}]
such that
C\equiv D
.
We say that
\mathcal{L}
is
membership query learnable

if there exists an exact learning algorithm with membership queries for
\mathcal{L}

Moreover, it is
polynomial time membership query learnable
if the algorithm runs in polynomial time.

Recall our discussion on the differences between the ABox-as-examples and interpretations-as-examples setting (Remark
2.3
). This difference carries through in the definition of membership queries, and hence our definition of exact learning differs from the one in e.g.
Funk et al. 2022
.

The following result says that (polynomial time computable) finite characterisations are a necessary condition for (polynomial time) membership query learnability.

Theorem F.2
.

If a concept language
\mathcal{L}
is (polynomial time) membership query learnable
and
\mathcal{L}
has a polynomial time model checking problem in combined complexity, then
\mathcal{L}
admits (polynomial time computable) finite characterisations.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the query learning algorithm using only membership queries to identify concept expressions
C\in\mathcal{L}
in polynomial time. For any concept
C\in\mathcal{L}
, we will generate in polynomial time a finite characterisation of
C
w.r.t.
\mathcal{L}
as follows.

Run the learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on input
C
, and answer membership queries in polynomial time by using the model checking algorithm which runs in polynomial time. We record all the examples visited during the run, and label them as positive or negative depending on the answer of the membership query. The resulting finite set of labelled examples, the
trace
of
\mathcal{A}
on input
C
, must be a finite characterisation of
C
w.r.t.
\mathcal{L}
. For suppose otherwise, then there must be some other non-equivalent concept
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}
with
C\not\equiv C^{\prime}
that is consistent with all labelled examples seen so far. But that means that
\mathcal{A}
will never output
C^{\prime}
as hypothesis, and hence will never be able to learn
C^{\prime}
, contradicting our assumption that
\mathcal{A}
is a membership query learning algorithm. Moreover, if
\mathcal{A}
runs in polynomial then this whole construction of finite characterisations above is also in polynomial time.
∎

Conversely, the existence of finite characterisations guarantees the existence of an inefficient naive membership query learning algorithm. However, the mere existence of polynomial time computable characterisations does
not
imply the existence of a polynomial time membership query learning algorithm.

Theorem F.4
.

If a concept language
\mathcal{L}
admits computable characterisations then
\mathcal{L}
is (not necessarily efficiently) membership query learnable

Proof.

Our naive learning algorithm works as follows. Fix some enumeration of all the concepts in
\mathcal{L}
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}=\{C_{0},C_{1},C_{2},\ldots\}
,
and for each
C_{i}
compute a finite characterisation
(E^{+},E^{-})
w.r.t.
\mathcal{L}

and ask a membership query for every example in
E^{+}\cup E^{-}
. If the answer to the membership queries fits the labeling, output the concept
C_{i}
. Otherwise, continue to the next concept in the enumeration.
∎

Thus, the following corollary is immediate from Theorems
1.1
,
F.2
and
F.4
.

Corollary F.5
.

Let
\{\exists,\sqcap\}\subseteq\mathbf{O}\subseteq\{\forall,\exists,\geq,-,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot,\neg\}
.

1.

If
\mathbf{O}
is subsumed by
\{\exists,-,\sqcap,\sqcup,\top,\bot\}
,
\{\forall,\exists,\sqcup,\sqcap\}
or
\{\forall,\exists,\geq,\sqcap,\top\}
then
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})
is membership query learnable.

2.

Otherwise
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{O})
is not membership query learnable, except possibly if

\{\geq,-,\sqcap\}\subseteq\mathbf{O}\subseteq\{\exists,\geq,-,\sqcap,\top\}
.
