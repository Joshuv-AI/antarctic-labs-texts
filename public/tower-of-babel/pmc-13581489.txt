Second‐Knee C‐Reactive Protein After 1‐Week Staged Bilateral Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Jaemin Lee, Doo Sup Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOne‐week staged bilateral total knee arthroplasty (TKA) operates the second knee before the systemic response to the first has resolved. Whether a higher second‐knee C‐reactive protein (CRP) reflects expected carryover or a distinct second‐surgery response is unknown. Using each patient as their own control, we assessed how far this burden reflects carryover.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 216 patients who underwent 1‐week staged bilateral TKA between January 2020 and April 2026 (primary cohort: 210 staged within 9 days). Primary endpoints were the absolute CRP on Postoperative Days 2 and 5, with the Days 2–5 area under the curve as a complementary summary. Exploratory analyses examined the per‐surgery CRP increment from each operation's own floor, peak reproducibility, and operative‐magnitude adjustment. Paired comparisons used the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test.ResultsThe second knee carried a higher absolute CRP burden than the first: median paired differences +1.44 mg/dL at Day 2, +0.70 at Day 5, and +3.74 mg · day/dL for the Days 2–5 area under the curve, all significant after Holm correction and robust to multiple imputation of missing values. From each operation's own floor, the second‐knee increment was smaller, but the Day‐5 proxy floor exceeded the directly measured pre‐second‐operation value by a median 2.58 mg/dL, underestimating that increment by the same amount. In the 14 patients with a measured floor and a complete increment pair, the increment difference was −0.55 mg/dL and did not reach significance (p = 0.15); the measured floor was itself of similar magnitude to the Day‐2 between‐knee difference. Peak CRP showed moderate within‐patient agreement (intraclass correlation 0.65).ConclusionsIn 1‐week staged bilateral TKA without tranexamic acid or routine corticosteroid, the second knee runs a higher absolute CRP course, consistent with substantial carryover from the first operation; the data cannot exclude an additional per‐surgery response or fix its share. Two periprosthetic joint infections occurred, one during the index admission and one at 48 months, and CRP was not tested against infection outcomes, so these data support no diagnostic threshold. A modestly higher second‐knee CRP may reflect the expected acute‐phase course but must not override clinical suspicion; prospective validation is needed.Keywords: acute‐phase response, C‐reactive protein, paired within‐patient analysis, periprosthetic joint infection, staged bilateral arthroplasty, total knee arthroplasty

Background

Bilateral symptomatic knee osteoarthritis is common in older adults and often requires arthroplasty of both knees, performed simultaneously under one anesthetic or staged as two operations [1]. Simultaneous surgery lowers cost and reoperation but raises the complication and mortality burden, and the optimal inter‐stage interval remains debated [1, 2]. A short, 1‐week interval within a single admission is now an established option, with perioperative safety comparable to simultaneous or delayed staging [3, 4]. At this interval the second knee is operated before the systemic response to the first has resolved, and a second arthroplasty before physiologic recovery carries a greater postoperative burden [5, 6]. C‐reactive protein (CRP) is the acute‐phase marker most widely used to monitor this response after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [7]: after uncomplicated surgery it rises downstream of interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) [8], peaks around the second to third postoperative day, and normalizes over the following weeks [9, 10]. This predictable course makes CRP routine in early periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) surveillance despite limited early specificity [11]. Because the response scales with operative tissue injury [12], the larger cumulative insult of bilateral surgery elicits a correspondingly greater acute‐phase response [13, 14].

Whether the two knees of a staged procedure differ in their inflammatory response, and why, remains poorly defined. Normative data describe broadly similar CRP and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) patterns after the first and second operations [15], and later series characterized the postoperative CRP course at a group level [16, 17]. Such summaries average across patients and cannot resolve whether one patient's second knee differs from that same patient's first. Between‐group comparisons of simultaneous versus unilateral surgery have likewise proved inconclusive [18]. Paired within‐patient comparison of the two staged knees has been applied to patient‐reported outcomes [19] but not to the CRP trajectory at a 1‐week interval. It therefore remains unclear whether any second‐knee difference reflects a true per‐surgery effect or carryover of unresolved first‐surgery inflammation.

We therefore characterized the paired, within‐patient CRP trajectory of the first and second knees in 1‐week staged bilateral TKA, using each patient as their own control. This design was motivated by evidence that the first operation's response, not the interval alone, conditions the second‐stage course [20], and it holds constant the patient‐level factors that between‐group comparisons cannot [21]. The study addressed three questions: (i) whether the second knee carries a higher absolute postoperative CRP burden than the first knee of the same patient, measured at the second and fifth postoperative days and across the Days 2–5 area under the curve; (ii) how far any such elevation is attributable to carryover of the unresolved inflammatory floor left by the first operation rather than to a larger per‐surgery response, examined through each operation's increment from its own preoperative floor; and (iii) whether the elevation persists after accounting for surgical magnitude and for the confounders that a paired design does not hold constant, and how reproducible the peak response is within a patient. The analysis is descriptive: because routine preoperative CRP before the second operation was unavailable, it cannot establish whether the second knee mounts an intrinsically larger second‐stage response and was not designed to test causation.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a single‐center, retrospective, paired within‐patient cohort study at a tertiary academic hospital in the Republic of Korea. Because both knees belonged to the same patient, each patient served as their own control, contrasting the first and second operative stages of staged bilateral knee arthroplasty [21]. The institutional review board approved the protocol (approval no. CR326041, April 28, 2026) and waived informed consent given the retrospective use of de‐identified clinical data, abstracted over the month after approval (April to May 2026). The study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for cohort studies [22], and the completed checklist accompanies this manuscript.

Participants

From institutional records we screened all consecutive patients who underwent staged bilateral TKA between January 2020 and April 2026, identifying 301 candidates [3, 4]. Patients were eligible if they met all of the following criteria: (i) adults undergoing bilateral TKA staged as two separate operations; (ii) primary osteoarthritis as the indication for both knees; (iii) both operations performed within a single admission and no more than 14 days apart; and (iv) at least one routinely scheduled postoperative CRP measurement for each knee. Patients were excluded if any of the following applied: (i) an indication other than primary osteoarthritis, such as inflammatory or posttraumatic arthritis; (ii) operations more than 14 days apart or not performed within a single admission; (iii) incomplete operative records; (iv) no routinely scheduled postoperative CRP measurement; or (v) an active inflammatory or infective process unrelated to the index operations. Of the 301 screened we excluded 85—12 without primary osteoarthritis, 23 whose operations were more than 14 days apart or not performed within a single admission, 18 with incomplete operative records, and 32 without a routinely scheduled postoperative CRP measurement—leaving 216 for analysis (Figure 1). No patient was excluded under criterion (v). To keep a prolonged inter‐stage interval from distorting the within‐patient contrast, the primary paired analysis was restricted to operations separated by no more than 9 days (n = 210), with robustness examined in two predefined sensitivity cohorts: ≤ 7 days (n = 199) and the full ≤ 14‐day cohort (n = 216). No a priori sample‐size calculation was performed; consistent with the descriptive design, each confidence interval (CI) conveys precision rather than a prespecified power target.

Participant flow. Of 301 patients screened for staged bilateral TKA, 85 were excluded, leaving 216 analyzed. The primary paired analysis used the 210 patients staged within 9 days; the ≤ 7‐day (n = 199) and ≤ 14‐day (n = 216) cohorts were predefined sensitivity analyses.

Ascertainment of Complications and Follow‐Up

Postoperative complications were ascertained from the medical record rather than from a standardized surveillance interval, using two sources with different windows. Routine clinical and laboratory contact after the second operation extended to a median of 73 days (IQR 43–266; range 3–358), with 81% of patients having a recorded contact at 30 days or later and 45% at 90 days or later. Reoperation and PJI were ascertained additionally from the operating surgeon's complete case list at this institution, covering every operation that surgeon performed from 2018 to April 23, 2026, which gave a median ascertainment window of 45 months (IQR 22–59; range 1 day to 75 months) after the second operation. Because neither source imposes a fixed surveillance visit, and because the operative source records one surgeon's cases rather than the institution's whole theater log, the absence of an event within these windows cannot exclude delayed presentation, presentation elsewhere, or reoperation by another surgeon at this institution.

Surgical Procedure and Perioperative Protocol

All operations used a standard TKA technique under pneumatic tourniquet control, with operative and tourniquet times recorded for each knee. Perioperative anemia was managed with a single intravenous dose of ferric carboxymaltose 1500 mg. We gave no tranexamic acid and withheld routine perioperative dexamethasone and other systemic corticosteroids. Because this protocol differs from contemporary enhanced‐recovery regimens, we treated the absolute CRP magnitudes as institution‐specific and framed the analysis around relative, within‐patient contrasts rather than transferable absolute thresholds.

Covariates and Potential Confounders

A paired within‐patient design holds constant, by construction, every factor that does not change between the two stages—age, sex, body mass index, comorbidity, genotype, and baseline organ function—so these cannot confound the between‐knee contrast. We therefore characterized them descriptively and directed the confounder analysis at what can differ between the two operations of the same patient. All operations for which an operator is recorded were performed by the same senior arthroplasty surgeon, using one implant philosophy and one perioperative pathway: 422 of the 432 index operations appear in that surgeon's operative case list, covering 211 of the 216 patients at both stages. We record the limits of that evidence rather than overstate it. No source available to us documents the operator independently—the anesthetic records name the anesthetic team and not the surgeon, and the case‐report form has no operator field—so the only operator information in this study comes from a list organized by surgeon, which establishes coverage but cannot by construction exclude a second operator, and the 10 unmatched operations cannot be attributed at all. Surgeon variation is therefore neither demonstrated nor adjusted for, and this is stated as a limitation. Anesthetic technique (spinal or general) was recorded for each operation separately, as were the operated side, the operative and tourniquet times, and the intraoperative blood output. Which knee was operated first was a clinical decision taken before admission, conventionally the more symptomatic side [23]; no radiographic severity grading was available with which to verify that ordering, so the indication for the sequence is reported as recorded rather than reconstructed. Renal function (blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, and the laboratory‐reported estimated glomerular filtration rate) and hepatic function (aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and serum albumin) were taken from the same routine perioperative chemistry panel as the CRP, at two timepoints: the value closest to and on or before the first operation, and the value closest to and on or before the second operation but drawn after the first. Hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and antiplatelet or anticoagulant use were abstracted from the medical record. Each between‐stage covariate was compared between the two operations with the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, and its within‐patient change was correlated with the between‐knee difference in POD2 CRP by the Spearman coefficient; the operated side and any change of anesthetic technique were tested against the same between‐knee difference with the Mann–Whitney U test, changes of technique being examined separately by direction so that opposite transitions could not cancel. These confounder comparisons were made in all 216 patients rather than in the primary cohort, to keep them at full power, and were interpreted against a Holm correction applied within a family of 15 tests: the 12 Spearman correlations between the between‐knee Day‐2 CRP difference and, for each of the six renal and hepatic measures, its baseline value and its between‐stage change; the Mann–Whitney comparison by which side was operated first; the Mann–Whitney comparison of changed against unchanged anesthetic technique; and the Spearman correlation between the between‐stage change in intraoperative blood output and that same CRP difference. The signed‐rank comparison of blood output between the two stages, reported in the Section 3, is a between‐stage description and is not a member of this family.

CRP Measurement

The hospital's accredited clinical chemistry laboratory measured serum CRP during routine perioperative care by a particle‐enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay (Tina‐quant C‐Reactive Protein Gen. 4, CE‐marked reagent) on a Roche cobas c702 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany), reported in mg/dL throughout. The laboratory confirmed the reagent generation and label for this study. The measuring range is 0.6–350 mg/L (0.06–35 mg/dL), the lower limit being the limit of quantitation. The highest value in this cohort was 25.8 mg/dL, so no sample required dilution. Every postoperative value lay well above the lower limit of quantitation, the lowest being 0.37 mg/dL; 66 of the 216 pre‐first‐operation values (30.6%), however, fell below it, as expected of a resting baseline in a patient without infection. The laboratory released these as numeric results and we analyzed them as reported. Because such values enter the analysis only as the floor of the first‐knee increment, we repeated that endpoint with the limit of quantitation substituted for every value below it. A preoperative measurement preceded the first operation (Pre‐Op1), and CRP was drawn routinely on the second and fifth postoperative days (POD2 and POD5) after each operation. A pre‐second‐operation draw was not part of the routine pathway and was available in only a small minority of patients; this absence was the dataset's principal identifiability constraint and shaped the outcome definitions below.

Outcome Definitions

The primary outcome was the absolute postoperative inflammatory burden, captured by the absolute POD2 and POD5 CRP, with the POD2–5 partial area under the CRP–time curve (AUC) as a complementary summary of cumulative burden [24, 25]. To address whether a higher second‐knee burden reflects carryover of unresolved first‐surgery inflammation or a larger per‐surgery response, a secondary analysis examined the per‐surgery CRP increment—the rise above each operation's own preoperative floor: for the first knee, the POD2 value minus Pre‐Op1 (Op1 POD2 − Pre‐Op1); for the second, lacking a routine pre‐second‐operation draw, the second‐operation POD2 minus the first‐operation POD5 (Op2 POD2 − Op1 POD5). This first‐operation POD5 value, drawn 2 days before the second operation at the predominant 7‐day interval and further ahead of it as the interval lengthened, was the closest available proxy for the true pre‐second‐operation floor. Because it lay on the declining limb of the first‐operation response, it likely exceeded the true floor and so underestimated the second‐knee increment, biasing the increment downward; we, therefore, treated it as exploratory and read it only as a check of whether the second‐knee per‐surgery rise exceeded the first. To quantify this bias directly, in the subgroup with a measured pre‐second‐operation value we computed the paired difference between the POD5 proxy and the measured floor; because the increment is defined as the POD2 value minus the floor, this same quantity is, by construction, the amount by which the proxy‐based analysis underestimates the second‐knee increment, and adding it back to the cohort‐level increment difference gives a crude bias‐corrected estimate for triangulation against the directly measured subgroup. Further exploratory analyses were the within‐patient reproducibility of the peak (POD2) response, a surgical‐magnitude‐adjusted model of absolute POD2 CRP, correlations between the between‐knee CRP difference and the corresponding between‐knee differences in surgical magnitude, and the proportion of patients whose second‐knee POD2 CRP exceeded the first‐knee value by at least 5 mg/dL.

Statistical Analysis

We compared paired first‐versus‐second‐knee values with the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, chosen for the non‐normal CRP distribution, and summarized each paired difference as the median of the paired differences (second − first) with a bootstrap 95% CI (10,000 resamples) and the paired Cohen's dz. We computed the partial AUC by the trapezoidal rule from POD2 to POD5; sparse POD0 and POD1 sampling made the full early curve non‐estimable. Confirmatory inference rested on the two absolute‐burden timepoints, the POD2 and POD5 CRP; because the POD2–5 AUC is a deterministic function of these values, we treated it as a derived summary rather than an independent confirmation. Within‐patient reproducibility of the peak response—the POD2 value, the routinely sampled timepoint closest to the known early CRP peak—was quantified on the native CRP scale using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC): ICC(2,1), a two‐way random‐effects model for absolute agreement, and ICC(3,1), a two‐way mixed‐effects model for consistency, complemented by the Bland–Altman bias (95% limits of agreement) and the Spearman rank correlation. We evaluated the contribution of surgical magnitude with a linear mixed‐effects model of knee‐level POD2 CRP, with a random intercept per patient and fixed effects for knee order, operative time, and tourniquet time; we added intraoperative blood output in a supplementary specification and refitted the model on log‐transformed CRP as a sensitivity analysis. We applied the Holm correction across the POD2 CRP, the POD5 CRP, the derived AUC, and the exploratory increment—the AUC and the increment included only for conservatism, neither being an independent confirmation and the increment reaching significance only in its downward‐biased direction—while excluding the reproducibility analysis, the mixed‐model covariate terms, and the between‐knee difference correlations. The significance threshold was two‐sided at α = 0.05, and we emphasized effect sizes with CIs rather than bare p‐values.

We handled missing data by pairwise‐complete analysis in the primary presentation and reported the analyzed sample size for each endpoint. Of the 216 patients, the Pre‐Op1 measurement was available in all; the first‐operation POD2 and POD5 values were each missing in 6, the second‐operation POD2 in 9, and the second‐operation POD5 in 2 (each < 5% per timepoint). To test the missing‐at‐random presumption underlying this approach, we compared patients with and without any missing postoperative CRP value on demographic, baseline, and operative characteristics—including the baseline CRP and the inter‐stage interval—using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables (sex, ASA Class III or higher, and the interstage interval dichotomized at the predominant 7‐day value), and we repeated all four paired endpoints under multiple imputation as a sensitivity analysis: 50 imputed datasets were generated by chained iterative imputation (Bayesian ridge regression with posterior sampling) on log(CRP + 1)‐transformed values, conditioning on all CRP timepoints, age, sex, body mass index, ASA class, interstage interval, operative and tourniquet times, and intraoperative blood output; observed values were retained unchanged, each endpoint was re‐estimated in every imputed dataset, and the paired mean difference was pooled by Rubin's rules. The pre‐second‐operation draw was nonroutine, obtained at the treating team's discretion rather than by protocol and available in only 15 patients, so it reflected the care pathway rather than attrition; being missing by design in the remainder, it was not imputed. Several design features addressed potential bias: consecutive sampling limited selection bias, a single standardized clinical assay limited measurement bias, and reliance on a proxy pre‐second‐operation floor introduced a directional measurement bias that we acknowledge in the outcome definitions and Discussion. We performed all analyses in Python 3 (SciPy, statsmodels, and pingouin).

Four of the 216 patients underwent subsequent knee surgery, accounting for six procedures. One developed an acute PJI of the first knee and underwent irrigation and liner exchange on the fourteenth postoperative day of that knee—the 7th day after the second operation—during the index admission, with a retained suture removed from the same knee at 18 months. Two underwent manipulation or brisement for stiffness at 36 and 57 days. One underwent two‐stage revision of the second knee 48 and 49 months after the index arthroplasty, by implant removal with an antibiotic‐loaded cement spacer followed by reimplantation. The reoperation field of the case‐report form records the indication as loosening and no surgical site infection; we classified the event as a late PJI on the operative record itself—a two‐stage exchange with an antibiotic‐loaded spacer is performed for infection, not for aseptic loosening—together with the same knee's history of open irrigation and debridement for pyogenic arthritis 16 months before the index arthroplasty, the joint having been quiescent and the operative indication recorded as degenerative arthritis at the time of listing. The classification is ours and we report it as such; both readings are given so that a reader may weigh it. Two PJIs therefore occurred among the 216 patients, one during the index admission and one at 48 months. No hematoma, deep venous thrombosis, or pulmonary embolism was recorded.

The patient infected during the index admission reached CRP values far above the cohort at both stages (first‐knee POD5 15.99 mg/dL, second‐knee POD5 20.50, against cohort medians of 5.01 and 5.92) and was still rising at the fifth postoperative day after each operation. Neither feature was unique to that patient: among the 196 patients with all four postoperative values, 8—this patient and 7 others, none of whom developed infection—showed CRP still rising at Day 5 after both operations. Excluding the patient infected during the index admission left all four endpoints unchanged (POD2 + 1.46 mg/dL, POD5 + 0.70, Days 2–5 AUC +3.70, increment −3.48), as did excluding both infected patients (+1.44, +0.70, +3.68, −3.51); all p < 0.001 (Table 2). The late infection presented 48 months after the index arthroplasty. Whether that patient's index‐admission trajectory carried any signal of it cannot be determined from these data; the trajectory reported here is not a predictor of an event 4 years away, and excluding the patient left every estimate unchanged.

Within‐patient peak CRP reproducibility across the two operations was moderate (Table 2, Figure 4). The absolute second‐knee elevation persisted after adjustment for operative time, tourniquet time, and intraoperative blood output, and remained significant when CRP was modeled on the log scale (Table 2); between‐knee differences in these operative parameters correlated at most weakly with the between‐knee difference in Day‐2 CRP (Table 2). Recorded intraoperative blood output proved a degenerate measure under tourniquet. It was zero in 182 of 216 first and 195 of 216 second operations, the median was 0 mL at both stages, and 170 of the 216 paired values were tied. What differed between the stages was how often a nonzero value was charted at all—34 first versus 21 second operations—so the signed‐rank result (p = 0.004) reflects recording frequency rather than a measured difference in shed volume, and we draw no inference about operative bleeding from it. The second operation did, however, begin from a substantially lower red‐cell mass, the hematocrit falling from a median 39.0% before the first operation to 27.6% before the second (median paired difference − 11.6 percentage points; p < 0.001) and hemoglobin from 13.1 to 9.4 g/dL (Table 1).

No confounder that can differ between the two stages accounted for the elevation (Table 1). The first knee was the right in 108 patients and the left in 108. The between‐knee Day‐2 CRP difference was nominally larger when the right knee was operated first (median 1.83 vs. 1.10 mg/dL; p = 0.018), a difference that did not survive Holm correction across the 15 confounder comparisons (adjusted p = 0.27) and for which no mechanism is apparent; the elevation was present with either side first. Anesthetic technique was unchanged between stages in 189 of 216 patients and differed in 27, with no difference in the between‐knee Day‐2 CRP change between these groups (p = 0.07); considered separately, so that opposite transitions could not cancel, neither differed from the unchanged group (spinal to general, n = 17, p = 0.16; general to spinal, n = 10, p = 0.20). Baseline renal and hepatic function was largely preserved—median estimated glomerular filtration rate 85.8 mL/min/1.73 m2 (IQR 71.0–93.2), with 16.2% below 60 and three patients below 30; median aspartate aminotransferase 22.5 U/L, alanine aminotransferase 17.0 U/L, and albumin 3.60 g/dL—and chronic kidney disease was recorded in seven patients (3.2%). Between the two operations the estimated glomerular filtration rate rose slightly (+5.9 mL/min/1.73 m2; p < 0.001) and albumin fell (−0.30 g/dL; p < 0.001), the expected behavior of a negative acute‐phase reactant, while aminotransferases were unchanged. Neither the baseline value nor the between‐stage change in any renal or hepatic measure correlated with the between‐knee difference in Day‐2 CRP (all |ρ| ≤ 0.11, all p ≥ 0.12).

The absolute Day‐2 and area‐under‐the‐curve elevations remained significant in every cohort, and the smaller second‐knee increment was stable across cohort definitions (Table 2). Substituting the assay's limit of quantitation for the 66 pre‐first‐operation values that fell below it left the first‐knee increment and the increment difference unchanged, and a deliberately conservative substitution at 0.30 mg/dL moved the increment difference only from −3.51 to −3.33 mg/dL (Table 2). All four endpoints were also robust to multiple imputation of the missing Day‐2 and Day‐5 values: the median paired differences varied narrowly across the 50 imputed datasets and matched the pairwise‐complete values, every dataset remained significant, and the Rubin‐pooled paired mean differences agreed with their pairwise‐complete counterparts (Table 2). Missingness itself—20 of 216 patients lacked at least one postoperative value—was unrelated to age, sex, body mass index, ASA class, baseline CRP, or any operative characteristic (all p ≥ 0.07) and was associated only with a non‐7‐day interstage interval (6 of 17 vs. 14 of 199 patients; p = 0.002), a scheduling rather than patient‐level pattern; the imputation model conditioned on the interval.

In the 15 patients with a measured pre‐second‐operation value, the first‐operation Day‐5 proxy exceeded that directly measured floor in every patient, by a median 2.58 mg/dL (median paired difference; 95% CI: 1.97–3.06; p < 0.001)—by construction, the same amount by which the proxy‐based analysis underestimates the second‐knee increment; the measured floor itself still exceeded the pre‐first‐operation baseline (median 1.37 vs. 0.13 mg/dL; median paired difference + 1.12; 95% CI: 0.37–2.03; p = 0.001), confirming unresolved carryover on the day of the second operation. Adding the median proxy–floor bias back to the cohort‐level increment difference (−3.51 mg/dL) yields a crude bias‐corrected estimate of approximately −0.9 mg/dL. Concordantly, in the 14 of these patients with a complete increment pair, whose measured floor (median 1.49 mg/dL [IQR 0.66–2.00]) lay well below the same patients' Day‐5 proxy (median 4.09 mg/dL), the increment difference measured from the true floor was small and not statistically significant (median paired difference − 0.55 mg/dL; 95% CI: −1.73 to 0.19; dz −0.32; p = 0.15).

Discussion

This study assessed how far the higher second‐knee inflammatory burden in 1‐week staged bilateral TKA reflects carried‐over residue of the first operation rather than a larger second‐surgery response; using each patient as their own control removes the between‐patient variance group‐level studies cannot. The second knee ran a higher absolute systemic acute‐phase course than the first across postoperative Days 2 and 5 and the Days 2–5 area under the curve, and it did not begin from a resting baseline but from an unresolved inflammatory floor left by the first procedure, well above the near‐baseline value before the first operation. Two convergent estimates point to this carried‐over floor as a substantial contributor: the proxy‐based floor difference exceeded the entire between‐knee Day‐2 difference, and where the floor was directly measured it was of similar magnitude to the Day‐2 difference itself. The data are therefore consistent with carryover as a major contributor but cannot exclude an additional per‐surgery response superimposed on that floor, nor fix its precise share—the true‐floor estimate rests on a small, nonrandomly sampled subgroup in which the increment difference was not significant (p = 0.15). What is clear is that the systemic acute‐phase reaction does not reset within a single week [10, 26, 27]: an elevated second‐knee CRP may reflect the residue of an incompletely resolved first response rather than a fresh inflammatory insult, a context between‐patient averages cannot provide.

Several lines of evidence bear on whether the second knee also mounts an intrinsically larger response. Its proxy floor, the first‐operation Day‐5 value, lay on the declining limb of the first response and overestimated the true floor—by a median 2.58 mg/dL where the floor was directly measured—thereby underestimating the second‐knee increment by the same amount; the proxy‐based increment alone therefore cannot exclude a per‐surgery response comparable to or larger than the first. Measured instead from the lower true floor, the increment difference moved close to zero and lost significance, and the bias‐corrected cohort estimate of approximately −0.9 mg/dL converged on that direct estimate, so the higher absolute burden appears driven substantially by the carried‐over floor. CRP itself limits inference: as a downstream, lagging acute‐phase reactant, its Day‐2 value integrates the residual tail of the first operation and the new response and cannot separate them [10]. The magnitude‐adjusted model and the between‐knee correlations show that operative time, tourniquet time, and intraoperative blood output do not explain the elevation—consistent with evidence that the tourniquet does not itself raise systemic markers in the first 24 postoperative hours [28]—and address surgical size, not carryover [12]. The recorded intraoperative blood output cannot bear an interpretation at all, and we would rather say so than build one on it. It was zero in most operations under tourniquet, its median was 0 mL at both stages, and the between‐stage difference lies in how often a nonzero value was charted rather than in any measured volume; we therefore do not read it as evidence that the second knee bled less. Two considerations make the point moot in either direction. Measured intraoperative output is a poor proxy for true perioperative loss after knee arthroplasty, roughly half of which is hidden from the operative field [29]; and when a unilateral knee arthroplasty is doubled to a bilateral one, total blood loss rises to about 220% of the unilateral value while the CRP response rises to about 127% [30], so shed volume and the acute‐phase response do not scale together. What the perioperative hematology does show is the state the second operation begins from—a hematocrit already fallen by more than 11 points—which parallels the unresolved inflammatory floor described above rather than offering an alternative explanation for it. Nor do the remaining confounders account for the elevation: the only operator record available, one surgeon's own case list, contains no second operating surgeon, though by its construction it could not; the anesthetic technique was unchanged between stages in 88% of patients, and a propensity‐matched comparison found no significant difference in the systemic CRP response to TKA between spinal and general anesthesia, though it reported a tendency toward lower values after spinal anesthesia [31]; the first knee was as often the right as the left; and neither baseline renal and hepatic function nor its between‐stage change correlated with the between‐knee CRP difference (Table 1). The renal result is what physiology predicts: CRP is synthesized by hepatocytes under IL‐6 control and catabolized at a rate independent of its plasma concentration and of the underlying disease, so the circulating level is governed by the rate of synthesis rather than by renal elimination [32]. One‐week staging is itself established as clinically safe [33]; whether the second operation nonetheless imposes a primed, amplified “second‐hit” response on top of the carried‐over floor is best framed against the trauma literature in which the concept originates. There, surgery after major injury superimposes a second inflammatory insult on an already primed host response—the pathophysiologic rationale of damage‐control orthopedics [34]—experimental orthopedic models show that definitive fixation after bilateral femoral fracture amplifies the systemic response of the primed animal [35], and in patients, trauma and subsequent orthopedic surgery re‐induce proinflammatory mitochondrial‐DNA neutrophil extracellular traps, a mechanism that primes the innate response to the next insult [36]. In staged bilateral TKA, the same unresolved‐inflammation reasoning has been invoked to explain greater second‐side pain [6]. An elective 1‐week staged arthroplasty, however, differs in scale and host condition from surgery after major trauma, and the present design cannot resolve the question: CRP measured downstream cannot differentiate pure carryover from an additive, amplified surgical response, so a second‐hit contribution remains an untested hypothesis whose separation requires upstream cytokine kinetics anchored to a measured pre‐second‐operation floor. Peak reproducibility across the two operations was moderate but partly mechanical: because the absolute second‐knee peak incorporates the carried‐over floor, the two peaks are correlated in part by construction, so the intraclass correlation overstates any intrinsic responder set‐point. A patient‐level responder phenotype might contribute, but this partly mechanical correlation prevents these data from establishing one [37]. The wide Bland–Altman limits, which cross zero, confirm that the second knee was not higher in every patient.

These results refine the staged‐arthroplasty literature rather than overturn it. Park et al. reported group‐level normative CRP and ESR values averaged across patients [15]; the paired design recovers the within‐patient structure those means conceal. Nam et al. likewise characterized the group‐level CRP course after uncomplicated TKA, though their sampling did not concentrate on the Days 2–5 window where the between‐knee separation is greatest [16]. Work holding that systemic inflammation scales with surgical magnitude [13, 14] is not contested but complemented by an orthogonal within‐patient axis, because the second knee ran higher even when operative and tourniquet times were comparable. The contrast with Incesoy et al. is instructive: comparing simultaneous bilateral with unilateral groups, they found CRP and ESR numerically but not significantly higher [18], whereas within‐patient pairing removes between‐subject variance and recovers a clear difference, consistent with a greater postoperative burden for the second staged knee [6]. Choi et al. previously described a bimodal perioperative CRP course in a 1‐week staged subgroup, the second‐knee peak rising from an incompletely resolved first‐operation floor [38]; the present study advances that qualitative observation through formal within‐patient pairing and examination of the carried‐over floor and the per‐surgery increment as distinct components, supporting carryover as a substantial contributor in the individual patient rather than describing only a group‐level curve shape.

The clinically relevant implication concerns early infection surveillance. Early‐postoperative CRP cannot discriminate infection from the expected surgical response [11, 39]; our data add that, after 1‐week staged bilateral TKA, the second knee departs from a higher acute‐phase floor and on average reaches a higher peak. As a population reference rather than a per‐patient rule, the second‐knee Day‐2 CRP ran roughly 1.4–2 mg/dL higher than the first and exceeded it by at least 5 mg/dL in only about 15% of patients, with wide between‐patient variability. Because only two PJIs occurred within the available follow‐up, one of them at 48 months, and the diagnostic performance of CRP was not tested, this implication is inferential: a modestly higher second‐knee CRP may be consistent with the expected acute‐phase course rather than infection. These data support no diagnostic threshold and cannot by themselves change surveillance practice; they motivate a prospective diagnostic study against infection outcomes. This expected‐carryover framing must never override genuine clinical suspicion: fever, wound complications, or disproportionate pain, and a second‐knee CRP that is unexpectedly high or fails to resolve still warrant a full infection workup. The patient infected during the index admission bears on the distinction the framing depends on, though less cleanly than a single case invites. That patient's CRP reached 15.99 and 20.50 mg/dL where the cohort medians were 5.01 and 5.92, and was still rising at the fifth postoperative day after each operation, whereas the carried‐over floor described here produces a second‐knee curve that is displaced upward but still falling. Neither feature identified the infection on its own: seven other patients showed the same Day‐5 rise after both operations and none became infected. A single case establishes no rule, and removing the patient changed no conclusion in the analysis; what the observation supports is only that a second‐knee CRP which fails to turn down deserves the same scrutiny as one that is unexpectedly high, not that either pattern is specific. When infection is suspected, IL‐6 has been reported to normalize more rapidly [8] and to discriminate acute PJI better than CRP [40]; it was not measured in this cohort, however, so the present data bear on the performance of neither marker.

Several limitations qualify these conclusions, each noted once. IL‐6, the upstream driver of the CRP response, was not measured—any cytokine‐level priming signal would be invisible to CRP—so the mechanism is inferred from a downstream marker [8]. The routine pre‐second‐operation CRP draw was absent—the central reason carryover and a genuine intrinsic second‐surgery response cannot be fully separated; the 15 patients who did have a directly measured floor, 14 of them with a complete increment pair, formed a small, underpowered subgroup sampled at the treating team's discretion rather than at random, and because such draws are more likely ordered when residual inflammation is clinically apparent, their true floor probably ran higher than the cohort average, if anything overstating the carryover share inferred from this subgroup. Sparse Days 0–1 sampling restricted the area under the curve metric to the Days 2–5 window [24]. Missing Day‐2 and Day‐5 values were few, unrelated to patient characteristics, and left every endpoint unchanged under multiple imputation, but no imputation can recover the pre‐second‐operation floor, unmeasured by design in 93% of patients. Hard clinical endpoints were rare—two PJIs, one during the index admission and one at 48 months, and two manipulations for stiffness among 216 patients—so the analysis addresses serology, not clinical outcomes, and two infections can support no diagnostic inference; because infection ascertainment was not standardized to a fixed surveillance interval, the events recorded here cannot be read as a complete incidence, delayed or out‐of‐institution presentation cannot be excluded, and the effect of carryover on the diagnostic accuracy of CRP for PJI could not be evaluated. The cohort was likewise confined to the 1‐week pathway—92% of patients were staged at exactly 7 days, none between 10 and 13 days, and only six at 14 days—so these data cannot characterize how the carried‐over floor decays with longer inter‐stage intervals or identify the interval at which it becomes negligible; the measured pre‐second‐operation values show only that resolution remains incomplete at 1 week, and defining that decay across 2‐ to 6‐week intervals requires cohorts staged at those intervals [5, 20]. The study is single‐center, retrospective, and confined to one population of predominantly older Korean women with advanced osteoarthritis treated with a uniform technique, which, with age‐related differences in the perioperative inflammatory response, constrains generalizability [41]. The protocol is also atypical: neither tranexamic acid nor routine perioperative steroid was used, and both attenuate the CRP and IL‐6 response, so the absolute values are not transferable to enhanced‐recovery practice and are best read in relative, within‐patient terms [9, 42, 43]. Because each patient served as their own control, the direction of the within‐patient contrast should be more robust to these differences than the absolute magnitudes. Overall, the evidence is of low certainty: the descriptive within‐patient difference is robust, whereas the carryover‐versus‐amplification attribution and any diagnostic implication remain hypothesis‐generating.

Future work pairing serial early IL‐6 measurement with a routine pre‐second‐operation CRP draw could separate a true second‐surgery response from simple carryover and define the determinants of the individual acute‐phase set‐point; extended across graded inter‐stage intervals and standardized infection surveillance, the same design could chart how the carried‐over floor decays with time and whether it degrades the diagnostic accuracy of early CRP for PJI.

Conclusions

In 1‐week staged bilateral TKA, the second knee carried a higher absolute systemic CRP burden than the first, and the paired within‐patient design shows this difference is consistent with a substantial carried‐over contribution from the unresolved first‐surgery inflammatory floor. The observational design cannot exclude an additional intrinsic per‐surgery response or fix its share, and the carryover estimate rests on a small, non‐randomly sampled subgroup. In older patients treated without tranexamic acid or routine corticosteroid, a modestly higher second‐knee CRP may therefore be compatible with the expected acute‐phase course rather than early PJI. This interpretation must not override clinical judgment: fever, wound complications, disproportionate pain, or a CRP that is unexpectedly high or fails to resolve still warrant an infection workup—as in the patient here whose CRP was far above the cohort and still rising by the fifth day after each operation and who proved to have a PJI, a Day‐5 rise that seven uninfected patients also showed. IL‐6 has been reported to discriminate acute infection earlier than CRP, but it was not measured in this cohort, so these data speak to neither marker's diagnostic performance. With two PJIs among 216 patients, one of them at 48 months, and CRP untested against infection outcomes, these data support no diagnostic threshold and require prospective validation before informing surveillance.
