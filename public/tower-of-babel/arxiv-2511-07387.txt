Manifest Symplecticity in Classical Scattering
Joon-Hwi Kim

Abstract
The Liouville theorem states that classical time evolution is an incompressible flow in phase space. We investigate two formulations of classical mechanics in which this property is manifested. First, the traditional Hamilton-Jacobi theory provides an in-out formalism. Second, a recent idea employing an exponential representation of time evolution provides an in-in formalism. Through explicit examples, it is demonstrated that the on-shell action in the former and the exponential generator in the latter are disparate objects. Still, a concrete relation between the two is identified in terms of a matching calculation. A strictly classical derivation and formulation of classical scattering theory is provided.

3.1
Example: Falling Apple

To clarify the points made above to a further extent,
let us investigate an explicit example:
a falling apple.
Despite its simplicity,
this innocuous example
teaches us a valuable lesson that
the on-shell action
F
and the generator
G
are completely different objects,
not only conceptually:
plugging in the explicit solutions
even fails to bring them to the same value.

The falling apple is a Hamiltonian system
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\omega,H)
,
where

\displaystyle\omega\,=\,dp\wedge dx\,,\quad H(x,p)\,=\,\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+mgx\,.

(15)

The constant parameter
g
describes
the uniform gravitational field.
The canonical Poisson bracket reads
{\{\hskip 0.50003pt{x},{p}\hskip 0.50003pt\}}=1
.
The Hamiltonian equations of motion are

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{x}(t)\,=\,p(t)/m\,,\quad\dot{p}(t)\,=\,-mg\,.\end{split}

(16)

Let
U
be the time-evolution symplectomorphism
for this apple,
from time
t=0
to
t=T
,
say.
Our goal is to compute
U
in various data types
and make a comparison between them.

3.1.1
Diffeomorphism

We start with the first candidate’s proposal:
obtain
U
as a diffeomorphism.
Surely, this is the most straightforward and rudimentary approach.
By solving the equations of motion in Eq. (
16
), we find

\displaystyle(x,p)\,\,\,\mapsto\,\,\hskip 0.50003pt(x^{\prime},p^{\prime})\,=\,\bigg(\,{x+\frac{p}{m}\,T-\frac{1}{2}\,g\hskip 0.50003ptT^{2}\,,\,p-mg\hskip 0.50003ptT}\hskip 1.00006pt\bigg)\,.

(17)

Unfortunately,
when stated in this format,
symplecticity
is only
assured
after
an additional
calculation:

\displaystyle dp^{\prime}\wedge dx^{\prime}\,=\,dp\hskip 1.00006pt\wedge\hskip-1.00006pt\bigg(\,{dx+\frac{T}{m}\hskip 1.00006ptdp}\hskip 1.00006pt\bigg)\,=\,dp\wedge dx\,.

(18)

As a differential operator,
U
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}U\,=\,1&+T\,\bigg(\,{\hskip-1.00006pt-\frac{p}{m}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+mg\,\frac{\partial}{\partial p}}\,\bigg)\\
&+\frac{T^{2}}{2}\,\bigg(\,{\frac{p^{2}}{m^{2}}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+m^{2}g^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}-2gp\,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x\hskip 0.50003pt\partial p}-g\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}}\,\bigg)+\mathcal{O}(T^{3})\,,\end{split}

(19)

which can be obtained by
transporting a test function by Eq. (
17
)
and then Taylor expanding.
Unfortunately,
it is hard to tell directly from Eq. (
19
)
if
U
really describes a symplectomorphism
or even a diffeomorphism.
This is because not all differential operators
originate from a diffeomorphism.
That is,
writing down any differential operator
would not guarantee that you have found
a Taylor expansion for shifting coordinates:
a “bitter life lesson,” to say.
Surely,
we will see shortly that the exponential parameterization in Eq. (
13
)
nicely circumvents this issue.

3.1.2
On-Shell Action

Next, we move on to the second candidate’s proposal:
Hamilton-Jacobi formalism.
To this end, we may want to briefly review the fundamentals of the formalism
before diving into explicit computations.

From a mathematical standpoint,
the idea of the Hamilton-Jacobi formalism is to ensure symplecticity
by finding
the difference between two symplectic potentials
for the initial and final phase spaces
as an (locally) exact one-form
dF
,
where
F
is the principal function.
As briefly mentioned in Sec.
2
,
a symplectic potential
is a one-form whose exterior derivative gives the symplectic form.
For our falling apple,
we have
\omega=dp\wedge dx
,
so
the natural choices for the symplectic potential are

p\hskip 1.00006ptdx
and
-x\hskip 1.00006ptdp
.
From these options,
the following four versions of the principal function arise:

\displaystyle\begin{split}dF^{(1)}=p^{\prime}dx^{\prime}-p\hskip 1.00006ptdx&\,,\quad dF^{(2)}=-x^{\prime}dp^{\prime}-p\hskip 1.00006ptdx\,\\
dF^{(3)}=p^{\prime}dx^{\prime}+x\hskip 1.00006ptdp&\,,\quad dF^{(4)}=-x^{\prime}dp^{\prime}+x\hskip 1.00006ptdp\,.\end{split}

(20)

These four realizations
are all related to each other
via Legendre transformations.
It follows that they provide maps
between products of Lagrangian submanifolds:

\displaystyle\begin{split}F^{(1)}\,\,:\,\,(x^{\prime},x)\,\,\mapsto\,\,(p^{\prime},p)&\,,\quad\,F^{(2)}\,\,:\,\,(p^{\prime},x)\,\,\mapsto\,\,(x^{\prime},p)\,,\\
F^{(3)}\,\,:\,\,(x^{\prime},p)\,\,\mapsto\,\,(p^{\prime},x)&\,,\quad\,F^{(4)}\,\,:\,\,(p^{\prime},p)\,\,\mapsto\,\,(x^{\prime},x)\,.\end{split}

(21)

The crucial point is that
acting on an exterior derivative to Eq. (
20
) gives

\displaystyle\text{Eq.\,(\ref{dF})}{\quad\implies\quad}dp^{\prime}\wedge dx^{\prime}\,=\,dp\wedge dx\,,

(22)

so the preservation of the symplectic form is manifest.

From the physical standpoint,
however,
the principal functions
are
nothing but on-shell actions
with proper boundary terms
as is remarked earlier.
For example,
the second principal function in Eq. (
20
) is given by
the on-shell (extremum) value
of a phase space action
with a boundary term
-p(T)\hskip 1.00006ptx(T)
:

\displaystyle F^{(2)}(p^{\prime},x)\,=\,\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}p(T)=p^{\prime}\\
x(0)=x\end{subarray}}{\Ext}\,\bigg[\,{-p(T)\hskip 1.00006ptx(T)+\hskip-0.50003pt\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\,\Big(\,{p(t)\hskip 1.00006pt\dot{x}(t){\,-\,}H(x(t),p(t);t)}\,\Big)}\,\bigg]\,.

(23)

Here,
the computation of the right-hand side
must fix
the initial position
x
and the final momentum
p^{\prime}
,
which is the very boundary condition stipulated by the variational principle.
With this point understood,
it is easily checked that
the on-shell variation of Eq. (
23
)
reads

\delta F^{(2)}(p^{\prime},x)=-x(T)\hskip 1.00006pt\delta p(T)-p(0)\hskip 1.00006pt\delta x(0)
,
deriving the differential relation stipulated in Eq. (
20
).
If a different boundary condition is employed,
the boundary action changes accordingly
so that the Legendre transformations
are precisely reproduced.
In this perspective, the idea of Lagrangian submanifolds
arises naturally
from the boundary conditions for a phase space action.

Now we can switch to explicit evaluations.
The second principal function in Eq. (
21
)
for our falling apple
is explicitly given as

\displaystyle F^{(2)}(p^{\prime},x)\,=\,-p^{\prime}\hskip 0.50003ptx-\frac{{p^{\prime}}^{2}}{2m}\,T-mg\,\bigg(\hskip 1.00006pt{x\hskip 1.00006ptT+\frac{p^{\prime}}{2m}\,T^{2}}\hskip 1.00006pt\bigg)-\frac{mg^{2}}{6}\hskip 1.00006ptT^{3}\,.

(24)

By referring to the differential relation
dF^{(2)}=-x^{\prime}\hskip 1.00006ptdp^{\prime}-p\hskip 1.00006ptdx
in Eq. (
20
),
one then finds

x^{\prime}=-\partial F^{(2)}\hskip-1.00006pt/\partial p^{\prime}
and
p=-\partial F^{(2)}\hskip-1.00006pt/\partial x

as functions of
p^{\prime}
and
x
.
In this sense,

F^{(2)}
provides a map
(p^{\prime},x)\mapsto(x^{\prime},p)

as stated in Eq. (
21
).
Explicitly,

\displaystyle\begin{split}x^{\prime}\,&=\,-\frac{\partial F^{(2)}}{\partial p^{\prime}}\,=\,x+\frac{p^{\prime}}{2m}\hskip 1.00006ptT+\frac{1}{2}\,g\hskip 0.50003ptT^{2}\,,\\
p\,&=\,-\frac{\partial F^{(2)}}{\partial x}\,=\,p^{\prime}+mg\hskip 0.50003ptT\,.\end{split}

(25)

This approach guarantees symplecticity:

0=ddF^{(2)}=d(-x^{\prime}dp^{\prime}-p\hskip 1.00006ptdx)=dp^{\prime}\wedge dx^{\prime}-dp\wedge dx
,
as is shown in Eq. (
22
).
Unfortunately, however,
the map
(x,p)\mapsto(x^{\prime},p^{\prime})

in Eq. (
17
)
can be found
only after
the process of
re-expressing
x^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime}
as functions of
x
and
p
,
which can be involved in general.

Exponential Map

Finally, we consider

As the Hamiltonian

Exponential Generator
\neq

An important remark is
that

and

not only conceptually but also in terms of their explicit values.
Naively, one might conceive a thought that

and

In fact,

However, even the simple example of an innocuous falling apple

Take the exponential generator

Evidently, this is not equal to

While Eq. (

With this failure, we try a different boundary condition.

3.2.2
Conceptual Differences

The explicit mismatch above is already clear,
but there are further differences between
F
and
G
at the conceptual levels as well.

1.

F
is inherently an in-out quantity since it is an action.

G
is inherently in-in, however.

2.

Legendre transformations can never bring
F
to a
G
.
Legendre transformations can never put canonically conjugate variables together.
Physically speaking,

x
and
p
cannot be simultaneously put together on the same side
as a boundary condition for a phase space action.
(Quantum mechanically, this is due to the very uncertainty principle.)

G
is not related to
any of the on-shell actions
via Legendre transformations.

3.

Consider how each formalism deduces the unknown variables.
With
F
, one computes just a
single
derivative as in Eq. (
25
).
With
G
, one adds up
nested
Poisson brackets as in Eq. (
3.1.3
), however.

4.

F
and
G
are also different in terms of the geometric structures they require.

F
requires a symplectic manifold with polarization structures (Lagrangian submanifolds).

G
requires only a Poisson manifold.

Physically speaking,
F
requires an action principle because it is literally an action.

G
, on the other hand, is defined straight out of classical equations of motion
and never has demanded an action principle.
Hence there is no boundary condition (Lagrange submanifolds) to talk about, after all.
One simply needs a Poisson bracket.

Later in Sec.
6.3
, this point will be elucidated by a concrete physical example.

5.

F
is an action, so its perturbative computation admits a path integral approach
(summing over tree Feynman graphs).
However,
it appears that there might be no good path integral representation for the perturbative computation of
G
.

The systematics for the former
has been worked out in Ref.
wlf-ps
:
phase space Feynman rules in the in-out setup.
The combinatorics is that of the usual Feynman graphs.
In contrast,
the diagrammatics for
G
has been worked out in Ref.
eikonal2
.
The combinatorics is quite different, involving even a use of Hopf-algebraic methods.

3.3
Matching

Still, however,
one could hope identifying
a concrete relation between the
G
and
F
.
Regarding this curiosity,
we provide another perspective toward
G
:

G
may be thought of as
an effective Hamiltonian.
Eq. (
13
) states that
the entire time evolution
from the initial time
to the final time
by the Hamiltonian
H(t)
,
which is generally time-dependent,
is directly reproduced by a steady evolution by
G

for a unit time.
In this sense,
G
is an effective Hamiltonian
encapsulating the entire history
within a unit-time flow.

Based on this effective equivalence
at the level of
time evolution,
one could argue that the on-shell actions arising from the two descriptions
should also match:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}x({t_{\texttt{f}}})=x^{\prime}\\
x({t_{\texttt{i}}})=x\end{subarray}}{\Ext}\,\bigg[\,{\int_{{t_{\texttt{i}}}}^{{t_{\texttt{f}}}}dt\,\,\Big(\,{p(t)\hskip 1.00006pt\dot{x}(t){\,-\,}H(x(t),p(t);t)}\,\Big)}\,\bigg]\\

x(0)=x\end{subarray}}{\Ext}\,\bigg[\,{\int_{0}^{1}dt\,\,\Big(\,{p(t)\hskip 1.00006pt\dot{x}(t){\,-\,}G(x(t),p(t))}\,\Big)}\,\bigg]\,.\end{split}

(35)

Here, we have simply assumed the boundary condition fixing positions at both ends
(
F^{(1)}
in Eq. (
20
)),
as other cases will be implied through Legendre transformations.
We have also assumed generic initial and final times,
{t_{\texttt{i}}}
and
{t_{\texttt{f}}}
.

To clarify,
the left-hand side of Eq. (
35
)
computes the on-shell action from the original description given by the Hamiltonian
H(t)
.
The right-hand side of Eq. (
35
),
on the other hand,
computes the on-shell action in the effective description due to
G
.
The statement is that they
match

implementing the same effect on the boundary phase spaces.
As is tested in Sec.

,
this assertion holds true.
For time-independent systems such as the falling apple,

See Ref.
Kim:2025gis
as well.

In fact,

)
could appeal to

Firstly, Eq. (

) is lifted to the fully quantum level

.
Then the left-hand side of Eq. (

) computes a transition amplitude

\langle x^{\prime}|\hskip 1.00006pt\hat{U}\hskip 0.50003pt|x\rangle
,
where

is the time-evolution unitary operator

A quantum-mechanical version of Eq. (

Having established the fundamentals,

For simplicity,

4.1
Dyson Series

Given the understanding that
the vector field

in Eq. (

is taken as a differential operator,
the solution
to the Liouville equation in Eq. (

is immediate by the well-known

Dyson series

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho(T)\,&=\,\mathrm{T}\kern-1.00006pt\exp\hskip-1.00006pt\bigg(\hskip 0.50003pt{\,\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\,X_{H(t)}}\bigg)\,\rho(0)\,.\end{split}

From Eq. (

\displaystyle U\hskip 1.00006pt=\,\mathrm{T}\kern-1.00006pt\exp\hskip-1.00006pt\bigg(\hskip 0.50003pt{\,\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\,X_{H(t)}}\bigg)\,\,\,:\,\,\,\rho(0)\,\,\mapsto\,\,\hskip-0.50003pt\rho(T)\,.

The ordered exponential unpacks into the infinite sum

\displaystyle\begin{split}U\hskip 1.00006pt&=\,1\,+\,\hskip 1.00006pt{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,\int_{t_{1}>t_{2}>\cdots>t_{n}}\hskip-1.00006ptd^{n}t\,\,X_{H(t_{1})}X_{H(t_{2})}\cdots X_{H(t_{n})}}\,,\\
\hskip 1.00006pt&=\,1+\int_{0}^{T}dt_{1}\,\,X_{H(t_{1})}+\int_{0}^{T}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\,\,X_{H(t_{1})}X_{H(t_{2})}+\cdots\,,\end{split}

where each integration variable

runs through

.

In general, Eq. (
38
)
evaluates to a giant sum of differential operators of all higher orders.
For instance,

in Eq. (

an

For

, this is not algebra-valued in general,
meaning that it does not describe an element of

(nor

As a result, the Dyson series formula in Eq. (

)
grossly obscures the fact that

It rather returns a giant differential operator,
which seems so generic that
it is left unclear
why it has to describe the exponentiation of a vector field:
the bitter life lesson.

4.2
Magnus Series

To this end,
consider the

.
When applied to Eq. (

\displaystyle X_{G}\,=\,\log\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{T}\kern-1.00006pt\exp\hskip-1.00006pt\bigg(\hskip 0.50003pt{\,\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\,X_{H(t)}}\bigg)\,.

(39)

Luckily, it has been well-established that

Magnus series

magnus1954exponential

At low orders, the Magnus series reads

\displaystyle X_{G}\,=\,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}dt_{1}\,\,X_{H(t_{1})}+\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{0}^{T}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\,\,[X_{H(t_{1})},X_{H(t_{2})}]+\cdots\,.

Compare this with the second line in Eq. (

The crucial property of Magnus series is that

and thus is manifestly algebra-valued.
That is, Eq. (

Using the very Lie algebra homomorphism in Eq. (

5.1
Classical Scattering Theory

What is a scattering?
A scattering is a physical process in which a well-defined notion of

in- and out-regions
in- and out-regions

exists.
As a typical situation,
one can imagine a space (or spacetime)
where two empty regions sandwich an “interacting zone.”
In the empty regions,
the particle moves freely,
meaning that we can solve its trajectories exactly.
However, in the interacting zone,
the particle is exerted by an extra force:
due to interactions with external fields, for instance.
In a scattering process,
the particle starts from one of the empty regions,
plunges into the interacting zone,
and escapes to the other empty region.
The empty region at the beginning is called the in-region;
the latter is called the out-region.
In the far past,
t\to-\infty
, the particle belongs to the in-region.
In the far future,
t\to+\infty
, the particle belongs to the out-region.

Mathematically, a scattering is modeled by the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H(t)\,=\,H^{\circ}+\epsilon\hskip 1.00006ptH^{\prime}(t)\,.

(46)

Here,
H^{\circ}
governs the free motion of the particle,
taken as time-independent for simplicity.

H^{\prime}(t)
, on the other hand,
implements the extra force in the interacting zone,
taken as time-dependent for full generality.
These are called the free and interaction Hamiltonians, respectively.
Also,
\epsilon
is a (formal) parameter
introduced for describing how strongly does the particle couple
to the external fields in the interacting zone:
a sort of a
coupling constant
coupling constant
.
For simplicity, we will treat
\epsilon
perturbatively.

By plugging in Eq. (
46

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}(t)-X_{H^{\circ}}[\rho(t)]\,=\,\epsilon\hskip 1.00006ptX_{H^{\prime}(t)}[\rho(t)]\,.

5.3

As an application of the S-symplectomorphism and scattering generator,

(63)

6.3
Spin in Magnetic Field

Eventually,
we provide an extreme example
in which the on-shell action is not even defined,
although the exponential generator is perfectly well-defined.
This sharply illuminates a fundamental distinction
between the on-shell action and the exponential generator.

So far,
we have exclusively presumed symplectic manifolds for the mathematical model for phase space.
However,
strictly speaking,
the minimal mathematical setup for a phase space
is a
Poisson manifold
Poisson manifold
:
a smooth manifold equipped with a Jacobi-satisfying Poisson bracket.
A symplectic manifold is a Poisson manifold.
But crucially, a Poisson manifold is not necessarily a symplectic manifold.
This is related to the fact that the Poisson bracket
describes the inverse of the symplectic form,
as is remarked in
Sec.
2
.

A physically relevant example of a Poisson manifold
is the space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
equipped with coordinates

(J_{1},J_{2},J_{3})
and the Poisson brackets

\displaystyle{\{\hskip 0.50003pt{J_{i}},{J_{j}}\hskip 0.50003pt\}}\,=\,\varepsilon^{k}{}_{ij}\hskip 1.00006ptJ_{k}\,,

(95)

which is sometimes called the isotropic Poisson-noncommutative three-space.
This is nothing but a phase space for angular momentum.
Evidently, this phase space is odd-dimensional,
so it cannot be a symplectic manifold on its own.

To reiterate, there is no symplectic form admitted in this space.
In turn, there is no symplectic potential.
Therefore, one cannot formulate an action principle
nor a principal function.
As a result, the Hamilton-Jacobi formalism is not applicable in this phase space.

However,
the exponential generator
takes only the Poisson brackets
and the equations of motion
as strict requirements.
In a Poisson manifold, the vector field
X_{f}
is defined by Eq. (
5
)
by taking the Poisson bracket as granted.
It then follows that the Jacobi identity of the Poisson bracket ensures Eq. (
6
).
Time evolution is now a
Poisson diffeomorphism
Poisson diffeomorphism

U
,
which can be written as the exponentiation

U=\exp(X_{G})

of a
Poisson vector field
Poisson vector field

X_{G}
.

For instance, take the time-dependent Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H(\vec{J};t)\,=\,\gamma\hskip 1.00006pt\vec{B}(t)\cdot\vec{J}\,,

(96)

where we have employed the three-vector notation:

\vec{J}=(J_{1},J_{2},J_{3})
.
This Hamiltonian can describe
a spinning top or
a spinning particle
in an external time-dependent magnetic field
\vec{B}(t)
.
The Hamiltonian equations of motion are given by

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{J}}(t)\,=\,\gamma\hskip 1.00006pt\vec{B}(t){\hskip 1.00006pt\times\hskip 1.00006pt}\vec{J}(t)\,,

(97)

which follows by computing
{\{\hskip 0.50003pt{\vec{J}},{H(\vec{J};t)}\hskip 0.50003pt\}}
.
Physically, this describes spin precession
due to the external magnetic field.
The solution to Eq. (
97
) is, surely,

\displaystyle\vec{J}(T)\,=\,\mathrm{T}\kern-1.00006pt\exp\hskip-1.00006pt\bigg(\hskip 0.50003pt{\,\gamma\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\,\vec{B}(t)\times}\bigg)\,\vec{J}(0)\,,

(98)

from which a rotation matrix—an
\mathrm{SO}(3)
group element—is identified as

\displaystyle R\,=\,\mathrm{T}\kern-1.00006pt\exp\hskip-1.00006pt\bigg(\hskip 0.50003pt{\,\gamma\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\,\vec{B}(t)\times}\bigg)\,\,\,\in\,\,\,\mathrm{SO}(3)\,.

(99)

From the Magnus series,
the total angle of rotation
is found as a pseudovector

\displaystyle\vec{\Theta}\,=\,\gamma\int_{0}^{T}dt_{1}\,\,\vec{B}(t_{1})+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\,\int_{0}^{T}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\,\,\vec{B}(t_{1})\times\vec{B}(t_{2})+\cdots\,,

(100)

so that

R=\exp(\vec{\Theta}\hskip 0.50003pt{\times}\hskip-1.00006pt)
.
Mathematically, this describes
the exponentiation of the Lie algebra
\mathrm{so}(3)

to
the Lie group
\mathrm{SO}(3)
.
The Lie bracket for
\mathrm{so}(3)

is simply the cross product.

Similarly,
the Liouville equation is given by

\displaystyle X_{H(t)}\,=\,\gamma\hskip 1.00006pt(\vec{J}{\hskip 1.00006pt\times\hskip 1.00006pt}\vec{B}(t))\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{J}}{\quad\implies\quad}\dot{\rho}(\vec{J};t)\,=\,\gamma\hskip 1.00006pt(\vec{J}{\hskip 1.00006pt\times\hskip 1.00006pt}\vec{B}(t))\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{J}}\,\,\rho(\vec{J};t)\,.

(101)

By solving the Liouville equation,
the differential operator
U

representing
R

is read off as

\displaystyle U\,=\,\exp\hskip-1.00006pt\bigg(\,{(\vec{J}{\hskip 1.00006pt\times\hskip 1.00006pt}\vec{\Theta})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{J}}\,}\hskip 1.00006pt\bigg){\quad\implies\quad}\rho(\vec{J};T)\,=\,U[\hskip 1.00006pt{\rho(\vec{J};0)}\hskip 1.00006pt]\,.

(102)

Finally,
the exponential generator

that generates the total rotation

\displaystyle G\,=\,\vec{J}\cdot\vec{\Theta}\,=\,\vec{J}\cdot\bigg(\,{\gamma\int_{0}^{T}dt_{1}\,\,\vec{B}(t_{1})+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\,\int_{0}^{T}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\,\,\vec{B}(t_{1})\times\vec{B}(t_{2})+\cdots}\,\bigg)\,,

(103)

such that

\displaystyle U\,=\,\exp(X_{G})\,=\,\exp\hskip-1.00006pt\bigg(\,{\vec{J}{\hskip 1.00006pt\times\hskip 1.00006pt}\frac{\partial G}{\partial\vec{J}}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{J}}\,}\hskip 1.00006pt\bigg)\,.

(104)

It is left as an exercise to reproduce Eq. (
103
)
by plugging in Eq. (
96
) to Eq. (
42
).
Note the identity

{\{\hskip 0.50003pt{\vec{J}{\hskip 1.00006pt\cdot\hskip 1.00006pt}\vec{\alpha}},{\vec{J}{\hskip 1.00006pt\cdot\hskip 1.00006pt}\vec{\beta}}\hskip 0.50003pt\}}=\vec{J}\cdot(\vec{\alpha}{\hskip 1.00006pt\times\hskip 1.00006pt}\vec{\beta})
.

From this physical example,
it is made clear that
the exponential generator

The on-shell action

cannot be defined

Still, one may insist a Lagrangian formulation.
As explicated in Sec.

an action principle is always viable

Hence one considers symplectic realizations of the above Poisson manifold

which means to implement the angular momentum

Note that
the Poisson manifold

Concretely, one can take either

;

The axioms of the star product are

\displaystyle 1\star f\,=\,f\star 1\,

\displaystyle=\,f\,,\quad f\star g\,=\,fg+\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{1})\,,\quad f\star g-g\star f\,=\,i\hbar\,{\{\hskip 0.50003pt{f},{g}\hskip 0.50003pt\}}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{2})\,.

(105)

For each function
f
in phase space,
there is a unique associated differential operator
X_{f}^{\star}
:

\displaystyle X_{f}^{\star}[g]\,=\,{\{\hskip 0.50003pt{f},{g}\hskip 0.50003pt\}}_{\star}\,.

(106)

Here,
{\{\hskip 0.50003pt{f},{g}\hskip 0.50003pt\}}_{\star}
is the
deformed Poisson bracket
deformed Poisson bracket
, defined as

\displaystyle{\{\hskip 0.50003pt{f},{g}\hskip 0.50003pt\}}_{\star}\,:=\,\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,\Big(\,{f\star g-g\star f}\,\Big)\,.

(107)

An important identity reads

\displaystyle[X_{f}^{\star},X_{g}^{\star}]\,=\,X_{{\{\hskip 0.40974pt{f},{g}\hskip 0.40974pt\}}_{\star}}^{\star}\,.

(108)

The exponentiation of
X_{f}^{\star}
returns a differential operator,
which we identify as describing a
noncommutative symplectomorphism
noncommutative symplectomorphism
.
The group of noncommutative symplectomorphisms is denoted as
\mathrm{Symp}_{\star}(\mathcal{P},\omega)
.

By definition, the star product describes the quantum operator product:

f\star g\leftrightarrow\hat{f}\hat{g}
.
Hence the deformed Poisson bracket in Eq. (
107
) describes
the quantum commutator
[\hat{f},\hat{g}]
, divided by
i\hbar
.

In the standard formulation of quantum mechanics,
operators are elements of
\mathrm{GL}(\mathcal{H})
, where
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space.
In the phase space formulation of quantum mechanics,
operators are just functions on the phase space.
In particular, the density matrix
\hat{\rho}(t)

corresponds to a quasiprobability distribution
\rho(t)

on phase space,
governed by the
\hbar
-deformed version of the Liouville equation:

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}(t)\,=\,\frac{1}{i\hbar}\,[\hat{H}(t),\hat{\rho}(t)]\quad\leftrightarrow\quad\dot{\rho}(t)\,=\,X_{H(t)}^{\star}[\rho(t)]\,.

(109)

As a result,
the quantum time evolution is a noncommutative symplectomorphism
mapping the initial
\hbar
-deformed phase space to the final
\hbar
-deformed phase space:

\displaystyle U\,\,\in\,\,\mathrm{Symp}_{\star}(\mathcal{P},\omega)\,.

(110)

Concretely, this is defined from the solution to Eq. (
109
).
The Dyson series approach
yields a mere transcription of the well-known quantum mechanical formula
to deformation quantization,
which describes star-exponentials.
On the other hand, the Magnus series yields Eq. (
45
)
with every Poisson bracket
replaced with the deformed Poisson bracket in Eq. (
107
):

\displaystyle G\,=\,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\,H(t)+\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{0}^{T}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\,\,{\{\hskip 0.50003pt{H(t_{1})},{H(t_{2})}\hskip 0.50003pt\}}_{\star}+\cdots\,.

(111)

This is by virtue of the correspondence in Eq. (
108
).

In the vanishing noncommutativity limit,
\hbar\to 0
,
the differential operator
X_{f}^{\star}
becomes the vector field
X_{f}
as a first-order differential operator,
the quasiprobability distribution becomes the probability distribution,
the noncommutative symplectomorphism in Eq. (
110
)
becomes the time-evolution symplectomorphism in Eq. (
7
),
and
the
\hbar
-deformed exponential generator in Eq. (
111
)
becomes the exponential generator in Eq. (
42
).

Application to the scattering context is straightforward.
The bottom line is that
the S-matrix
is
a noncommutative symplectomorphism on phase space
in the phase space formulation,
which mechanically reduces to a symplectomorphism on phase space
in the commutative limit
\hbar\to 0
.
Therefore, it is established that the S-symplectomorphism is the precise classical limit of the S-matrix in a strict sense.

We shall elaborate more on this formalization in a later work
S-symp
