Incomplete Spoiling Correction for 3D T1 Mapping
Authors: Gabriela Belsley, Damian J. Tyler, Matthew D. Robson, Elizabeth M. Tunnicliffe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeAssess the impact of incomplete spoiling and spatial saturation on T1 mapping using the variable flip angle (VFA) spoiled gradient recalled echo sequence (SPGR). Develop a correction for incomplete spoiling that results in an accurate estimate of T1, essential for quantitative MRI applications.MethodsA correction factor, derived from extended phase graph simulations, was applied to the measured SPGR signal to account for deviations from the theoretical steady‐state signal. An iterative fitting process was used, refining the incomplete spoiling correction factor at each step based on the updated T1 estimate. The incomplete spoiling correction method was validated against inversion recovery spin echo in phantom experiments and applied to in vivo data at 3T. Additionally, the hypothesis that spatial saturation disrupts the steady‐state was also tested and validated in a phantom.ResultsWithout a correction, the median T1 error was +7.4% (IQR [4.4, 9.6]%) in both simulations and phantom experiments. After applying the correction, the median T1 error decreased to −0.3% (IQR [−2.5, 2.1]%). In vivo, incomplete spoiling caused T1 overestimation of 10–50 ms depending on B1+ inhomogeneity. Spatial saturation induced on average a −10% T1 bias.ConclusionA correction for incomplete spoiling is essential to achieve an accurate measure of T1 using the VFA SPGR method, addressing systematic T1 overestimation when using this T1 mapping technique. Furthermore, other mechanisms that may disrupt the steady‐state signal, such as spatial saturation, should be considered.

Introduction

T1 mapping has been applied to multiple pathologies including the assessment of liver fibrosis and stratification of patients with metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [1, 2] and steatohepatitis (MASH) [3], among aetiologies [4, 5, 6, 7]. Whole‐liver T1 mapping is enabled through the variable flip angle (VFA) spoiled gradient recalled echo (SPGR) acquisition [8]. VFA T1 mapping is used in contrast‐enhanced MRI to assess cell viability and liver function [9]. VFA T1 mapping of fat has also been proposed both as a biomarker of tissue oxygenation [10] and as a means of correcting T1‐weighting bias in chemical‐shift‐encoded MRI performed with high flip angles for proton density fat fraction quantification [11].

The VFA SPGR method uses multiple RF pulses to drive the longitudinal magnetization to a steady‐state. However, any deviation of the SPGR signal from the predicted steady‐state signal will result in inaccurate T1 values when fitting the signal to the steady‐state model. To achieve short acquisition times and a strong T1‐weighted contrast, the SPGR sequence is run with a TR that is short compared to T2. For SPGR, the steady‐state model assumes that residual transverse magnetization is fully dephased before the next RF excitation pulse. Otherwise, the signal will present T2 contrast and thus deviate from the steady‐state signal, which presumes only longitudinal magnetization is affected by the RF pulses. In the liver, assuming a normal iron concentration of 1 mg Fe/g (dry weight of tissue), the T2 is approximately 22 ms [12]. For a TR of 4.1 ms only 17% of the transverse magnetization will undergo T2 decay before the next RF excitation pulse, highlighting the need to spoil the transverse magnetization.

RF spoiling applies pulses with incrementing phase at each TR, causing the spins to acquire distinct phase evolutions and cancel each other's contribution through destructive interference. Zur et al. [13] showed that a phase increment of 117° or 123° achieves nearly complete spoiling for a wide range of physiologically relevant T1 s/TR, T2 s/TR, and excitation FAs between 0° and 90°. Figure 1a highlights the importance of carefully choosing the RF phase increment: even for a 117° phase increment at an excitation FA of 15°, the SPGR signal—simulated through the extended‐phase‐graph (EPG) framework—deviates from the steady‐state. An RF phase increment of 50°, default setting on some clinical scanners, results in a signal lower than the steady–state achieved from a perfectly spoiled acquisition (Figure 1b). The lower signal mimics a longer T1 resulting in T1 overestimation.

(a) SPGR signal dependence on RF spoiling phase increment simulated for FAs of 3° (blue), 6° (orange), 9° (yellow), 12° (purple) and 15° (green) through the extended‐phase‐graph (EPG) framework (solid line) and the steady‐state (SS) theoretical equation (dashed line) with parameters: T1 = 800 ms, T2 = 30 ms, TR = 4.1 ms and 1500 RF pulses. (b) SPGR steady‐state signal as a function of the excitation FA represented in dashed lines for different T1 values of 600 ms (red), 800 ms (orange), 1000 ms (light orange), and 1200 ms (yellow). For these parameters, incomplete spoiling decreases the SPGR signal compared to the steady state signal, and the decrease is more severe as the FA value increases above the Ernst Angle.

Spatial pre‐saturation pulses can be used to suppress the signal from tissues outside of the field of view (FoV) [14]. Its main application is to reduce flow, motion, and aliasing artifacts along the PE direction, arising from tissue excited outside of the FoV. Spatial saturation pulses should not interfere with the ability of the magnetization to reach steady–state. One common approach interleaves two saturation pulses within each phase‐encode acquisition block. We hypothesized this implementation perturbs the steady–state, biasing T1 estimates.

The aim of this study was to develop a correction for incomplete spoiling in the VFA SPGR sequence to achieve accurate T1 estimates of the liver. The correction is based on EPG simulations across physiological liver TR/T1 and TR/T2 ranges. We also assessed spatial saturation effects on T1 accuracy.

Methods

Algorithm for Incomplete Spoiling

The six key factors that affect incomplete spoiling of the magnetization are the excitation FAs, the expected T1 and T2 range of the tissue of interest, the sequence TR, and the RF phase increment and a gradient spoiling, which can introduce diffusion effects when dephasing is much greater than the minimum of 2pi per voxel. For a phase increment of 50° we simulated transverse signals using EPG [15, 16] for T1 = 500–1600 ms (100 ms steps), T2 = 20–35 ms (5 ms steps) [17], and FA = 1°–20° (1° steps). A fixed TR of 4.1 ms was used as this allows whole liver coverage within a 15 s breath‐hold with parallel imaging. The k‐space center was reached after 6.9 s (1683 TRs), exceeding the 3*T1 requirement to achieve steady–state for a perfectly spoiled sequence (4.8 s for tissue T1 = 1600 ms).

The incomplete spoiling correction transformed the acquired signal to the steady‐state space. The acquired signal was multiplied by the ratio between the theoretical steady‐state signal (Equation 1a) and the simulated EPG signal at the estimated values of T1 and true FA (Equation 1b). (1a) Ssteady−state=M01−exp−TRT11−cos(α)exp−TRT1sin(α), (1b) SCorrected=Sacquired·Ssteady−stateSEPG, where α is the excitation FA, M0 is a numerical constant including the proton density, signal decay due to T2⋆ relaxation and the B1− receive sensitivity; TR is the repetition time and T1 is the longitudinal relaxation time.

The correction was incorporated into the non‐linear least squares (NLLS) T1 fitting procedure following an iterative approach. An initial estimate of T1 was obtained from the two lowest distinct flip angles (i.e., the two lowest non‐equal flip angles), by computing the ratio ρ = S₁/S2 of the two SPGR signals, after correcting the nominal flip angles using the B1+ map, and solving the steady‐state SPGR equation analytically for E₁ = (sin α₁ − ρ sin α2)/(sin α₁ cos α2 − ρ sin α2 cos α₁), from which T1 = −TR/ln(E₁). The lowest flip angles were selected because spoiling‐related T1 errors are amplified at larger flip angles (Figure 1b). For each voxel, the nominal FAs were first corrected using the B1+ map to obtain the true FAs. The initial T1 estimate and the B1+ corrected FAs were used to interpolate the incomplete spoiling correction factor curves (Figure S1), calculated offline for a grid of T1s, FAs and T2 of 30 ms. The acquired SPGR signal was multiplied by the interpolated correction factor and subsequently fitted to the theoretical steady‐state model to refine the initial T1 estimate using a NLLS fit. The incomplete spoiling correction was iterated at each step with the updated T1 estimate (Figure S2). Iteration stopped when consecutive T₁ estimates differed by less than 1 ms, or after a maximum of five iterations. In practice, convergence was typically reached after 2–3 iterations for both phantom and in vivo dataset.

Because liver T2 was not measured with the VFA SPGR sequence, it was necessary to use a fixed T2 value that would give the best overall results for the B1+ factor, T1 and T2 parameter space observed in the liver. For a liver T2 range of 20–35 ms [17] (at 3T), a T2 of 30 ms gave the smallest error between the true T1 and the fitted T1 including our spoiling correction. Errors remained < 2.3% across the full range of T2s (20–35 ms) [17], T1 s (700–1200 ms) [18] and B1+ factors (0.59–1.14) [19]. The nominal FA set used was [2, 2, 15, 15]°.

Simulations

Monte Carlo simulations compared T1 error with and without incomplete spoiling correction. The error was simulated over a range of T2 s (20–35 ms in steps of 5 ms) and T1 s (500–1600 ms in steps of 100 ms), with 10 000 repetitions per T1 and T2 combination. The EPG and the steady–state simulated signals were corrupted with zero mean additive Gaussian noise, corresponding to an SNR of 25 at a nominal FA of 2°, T1 of 800 ms and TR of 4.1 ms. We used a maximum B1 + = 1.14 [19] since incomplete spoiling error increases with flip angle.

Spatial Saturation

The total duration of a single breath‐hold SPGR acquisition with parallel imaging per FA was 15 s. The acquisition time with parallel imaging is sufficient for liver tissue to reach steady–state. Thus, no difference is expected in the T1 maps from using parallel imaging. However, we hypothesized that the T1 difference observed with parallel imaging on and off originated from the location of the saturation pulses in the SPGR sequence. To test this hypothesis, phantom data was acquired for the SPGR sequence with four different combinations of parallel imaging and saturation, named special saturation on the Siemens' parameter card and applied in the foot‐head direction:

1. Saturation On and Caipirinha On.

2. Saturation On and Caipirinha Off.

3. Saturation Off and Caipirinha On.

4. Saturation Off and Caipirinha Off.

Phantom and In Vivo Scans

Imaging data were acquired on phantoms and 10 healthy volunteers on a 3T Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) with software version syngo MR E11, using a spine array coil and an 18‐channel array coil. Each volunteer underwent three repeated VFA SPGR T1 acquisitions, with B1+ and B0 maps acquired for each repeat and applied to the T1 maps. Volunteers were scanned according to our institution's ethical practices and gave informed consent.

Two different phantoms were supplied by Perspectum Ltd. (Oxford, UK) and imaged: marked as QC0025 and FDA4‐T1. The QC0025 phantom contained 15 vials: 5 agar gel vials doped with NiCl2 with T1 s (at 3T) between 548 and 1429 ms which were used in this work; 5 vials had varying T2* values, and the last 5 vials had varying fat fractions. This phantom was used for the spatial saturation experiments. The FDA4‐T1 phantom contained 14 vials each of which contained 3% agar gel with NiCl2 as the doping agent. This resulted in vials with T1 s (at 3T) ranging from 367 to 1699 ms in steps of approximately 100 ms, an average T2 of 45 ms and no fat.

All sequences used for acquisition were unmodified product sequences that are widely available on any scanner independent of manufacturer, thereby facilitating reproducibility and widespread use of the method in clinical studies.

T1 mapping used a 3D VFA SPGR with Dixon [20] fat‐water separation. Water‐only images were used for calculating the T1 maps. Acquisition parameters: FoV = 450×366×3mm3, matrix = 320×260×48, TR/TEs = 4.1/[1.23/2.46] ms, Bandwidth (BW) = 1040 Hz/pixel, CAIPIRINHA [21] with 3× acceleration along the slice direction and 24 separate GRE reference lines. Nominal in vivo FAs were [2∘, 2∘, 15∘, 15∘], each acquired during a 15 s end‐expiratory breath‐hold. This FA set was selected based on a previously published optimization framework [22], which minimizes T1 variance by accounting for noise propagation from both the SPGR images and the B1+ map across a range of B1+ factors and T1 s values observed in the liver at 3T, yielding an optimal set of [2°, 3°, 15°, 15°]. The low FA was modified from 3° to 2° to additionally enable in vivo SNR estimation, obtained by subtracting two identically acquired images. The signals acquired at all nominal FAs, including repeated acquisitions at the same FA, were used directly in the voxel‐wise liver T1 estimation pipeline depicted in Figure S2, without prior averaging, to avoid propagating potential respiratory misalignments into an averaged image. For the phantom experiment, additional low FAs were acquired to investigate incomplete spoiling effects on FA values, resulting in a total FA set of [2∘, 2∘, 3∘,4∘,15∘, 15∘].

B1+ mapping used a 2D multi‐slice GRE EPI sequence with fat saturation and nominal FAs of 65∘ and 130∘ [22], each a 10 s breath‐hold. B1+ maps included corrections for slice profile effects and B0‐gradient through‐slice variations [23]. B0 mapping acquired through a 2D multi‐slice double echo spoiled GRE, were used for distortion correction of the GRE‐EPI images and modeling of B0‐variations through slice in the B1+ map [20]. Acquisition parameters for the B1+ and B0 mapping are described in the Supporting Information.

The gold standard (GS) T1 map for the phantoms consisted of a slice‐selective inversion recovery spin echo (IR SE) acquisition with 15 inversion times from 25 to 5000 ms (Figure S9).

Fifteen masks were manually drawn over the liver on the high‐resolution SPGR acquisition (Figure S3), acquired at a FA of 15°, for the slices matching the location of each of the 15 slices in the GRE‐EPI acquisition. The mask was down‐sampled to the GRE acquisitions for calculation of the B1+ and B0 maps. Subsequently, the masked B1+ map was interpolated along the three dimensions to the same spatial resolution as the SPGR acquisition, such that the interpolated B1+ factor was used to correct the SPGR nominal FAs at a voxel level. Each T1 map was calculated for the masked liver region, using the processing pipeline depicted in Figure S2, resulting in 30 T1 maps.

Vessels were removed from the liver‐masked T1 map by masking out pixels with T1 > 1150 ms and B1+ < 0.3. Pixel‐wise difference between vessel‐ and liver‐masked T1 maps with and without the incomplete spoiling correction were calculated for the central slice in the liver. For each volunteer, the mean and standard deviation of these differences were computed across all pixels and the three repetitions.

Results

Simulations

Without incomplete spoiling correction, the relative T1 error reached 7.7% at T1 = 1200 ms and T2 = 35 ms (Figure 2a). After applying the correction, the relative T1 error was approximately zero at T2 = 30 ms, as this was the fixed T2 adopted for the correction, and reached −2.1% at T2 = 20 ms and 1.3% at T2 = 35 ms.

(a) Simulated relative error in water T1 (%) due to incomplete spoiling (dashed lines) for a range of true T1 s between 700 and 1200 ms and a range of T2 s between 20 and 35 ms. The water T1 is always overestimated. The relative water T1 error decreases after applying the incomplete spoiling correction (solid lines). These simulations used an RF phase increment of 50°, TR of 4.1 ms and a FA set of [2, 2, 15, 15]°. (b) Water T1 error in percent, relative to the IR SE, without any incomplete spoiling correction applied to the SPGR signals acquired in the FDA4‐T1 phantom at FAs of [2, 2, 15, 15]° (orange). The median water T1 error was 7.4% with an interquartile range (IQR) of [4.4, 9.6]%. Using the SPGR signal acquired at FAs of [2, 2, 3, 4]° (blue) without incomplete spoiling correction the median water T1 error decreased to −0.7% and the IQR to [−3.8, 0.8]%. Applying the incomplete spoiling correction to the signal acquired at FAs of [2, 2, 15, 15]° (green), the median water T1 error was −0.3% with an IQR of [−2.5, 2.1]%.

Phantom

Figure 2b shows that the water T1 error in the FDA4‐T1 phantom without incomplete spoiling correction using FAs of [2, 2, 15, 15]° reached a median value of 7.4% with an IQR of [4.4, 9.6]% across all vials for the four central slices. This bias is also observed in the analysis of the residuals of the NLLS fit in the Supporting Information (Figures S4, S5). With correction, the median water T1 error decreased to −0.3% (IQR of [−2.5, 2.1]%). Using FAs below the Ernst angle of [2, 2, 3, 4]° resulted in unbiased T1 estimates (−0.7% error) without applying the incomplete spoiling correction. The T1 precision was not affected by the low FAs as the phantom is a system with a high SNR of 103, (measured from the difference between two repeated 2° acquisitions). The water SPGR T1 values were compared against the gold standard IR SE T1 values measured in the phantom (Figure S9).

Figure 3 confirms that parallel imaging does not affect the SPGR signal when special saturation is turned off in the QC0025 phantom. On the other hand, with saturation on, differences in SPGR signal exceeding 8% and water T1 differences of on average 10% were observed between parallel imaging on and off. Without saturation, water T1 values agreed regardless of parallel imaging, with smaller fit residuals (Figures S7, S8).

Percent difference in SPGR signals in the QC0025 phantom between parallel Imaging on and off with spatial saturation turned (a) on and (b) off, normalized by parallel imaging off to obtain a relative signal difference. With spatial saturation, parallel imaging resulted in a signal lower by 8% or more relative to parallel imaging off for the central vials which contain T1 s varying between 548, 633, 790, 1044, and 1429 ms. Turning saturation off (b) results in very small SPGR signal differences between parallel imaging on and off as expected.

In Vivo

The water‐liver T1 map in vivo uncorrected for incomplete spoiling for volunteer 10 was on average 51 ms higher than the corrected map, for the posterior part of the liver (lower part of the liver in Figure 4). The anterior portion of the liver had a smaller average difference of 25 ms as this was a location with a B1+ factor less than 1. The posterior part had B1+ factors slightly larger than one, resulting in higher excitation FAs and thus more incomplete spoiling.

Discussion

Correcting for incomplete spoiling in 3D VFA SPGR T1 mapping is essential to achieve accurate measurements. Short TR drives unspoiled transverse magnetisation to the steady‐state, introducing T2 contrast that invalidates fitting of the acquired signal to the steady‐state model (Equation 1a). Simulations showed T1 errors of 7.7% at a T1 of 1200 ms and a T2 of 35 ms when not correcting for this confounding factor. Overestimating water‐liver T1 by almost 100 ms could significantly impair clinical utility by misclassifying healthy liver tissue as diseased or overstating the severity of liver fibrosis. Phantom experiments confirmed the simulation predictions. After applying the correction, the median water‐liver T1 error decreased to −0.3% (IQR [−2.5 2.1] %), indicating a high accuracy of the proposed correction. In vivo data show a clear overestimation of water‐liver T1 in the presence of incomplete spoiling, being worse for the posterior part of the liver where B1+ inhomogeneities are greater than 1. On average, incomplete spoiling introduced a positive water‐liver T1 bias of 28 ms across the 10 volunteers; however, individual water‐liver T1 biases as high as 60 ms were observed in the absence of correction. Depending on its implementation, spatial saturation can bias SPGR water‐liver T1 mapping.

Preibisch and Deichmann [24] developed a correction for incomplete spoiling based on an assumed linear relationship between the true T1 and the T1 predicted by fitting the incomplete spoiling signal to the steady‐state equation. Their correction parameters varied quadratically with B1+ (0.7–1.3) for FAs of 4° and 18°, TR = 7.6 ms, and T1 = 700–1800 ms. In phantom, their correction reduced T1 error from 12% to less than 1%. However, in vivo T1 remained 100 ms above IR EPI reference values. It should be noted that their correction is based on fitting T1 to a linear form of the steady‐state equation obtained using the small‐angle approximation. This linear approximation has been shown to cause T1 inaccuracies [25].

An alternative correction for incomplete spoiling was proposed by Baudrexel et al. [26]. These authors assume the steady‐state signal is valid in the case of incomplete RF spoiling, provided an extra correction is applied to the FA, in addition to the B1+ mapping correction. Using an RF phase increment of 50°, they observed systematic errors in T1 as high as 13% in phantom and 24.2% in vivo for large FAs of 32°. After applying their proposed incomplete spoiling correction, the T1 errors decreased to 0.3% for the phantom, and in vivo to 5.2% for WM, and 9.9% for GM. The approach of Baudrexel et al. [26] aligns with the observation that larger excitation FAs will need larger incomplete spoiling correction factors as a larger amount of longitudinal magnetisation is flipped to the transverse plane. However, their T1‐independent correction does not agree with Figure 1b where the deviation between the steady‐state and the incompletely spoiled signal changes as a function of T1.

Our work does not make any underlying assumptions regarding the correction's dependence on T1 values, nor does it use either the small angle approximation or the linear form of the steady‐state equation. The correction is based on EPG simulations over a range of liver physiological TR/T1 and TR/T2 values. EPG gives an accurate estimate of the echo intensity in the presence of incomplete spoiling [12], enabling signal rescaling to steady‐state.

The 3D SPGR product sequence used in this work uses a combination of gradient spoiling and RF spoiling with a RF phase increment of 50 degrees. The gradient spoiling lies in the weak spoiling regime with a total dephasing of approximately 7.26 rad (slightly above 2 π) per voxel in the readout direction, with additional spoiling along the slice direction. Both the gradient and RF spoiling are taken into account during the EPG simulations. As shown in our simulations (Figure S6) and consistent with the literature, using larger spoiler gradients—above 280 mT*ms/m [27] or 6 π dephasing per voxel [28]—reduces residual transverse magnetization and the dependence of T1 on the RF phase increment. However, as demonstrated in both references [27, 28] and our own simulations, increasing the spoiler gradient area does not eliminate the need for post hoc correction. Importantly, large spoiler gradients are sensitive to diffusion [27, 28] which should be included in the EPG simulations. For our acquisition, diffusion effects are negligible since we are in a weak spoiling regime and the rms diffusion displacement is small compared to the voxel size (x2 ≈ 3 μm per TR, voxel size 1.4 mm) and introduce only 0.016 rad (γAGx2) of additional dephasing. Figure S6 shows a minor difference in the T1 estimate when diffusion effects are included and the gradient spoiler is close to 2 π. In practice, the large spoiler gradient moments required for the strong spoiling regime would increase TR and preclude breath‐hold scanning. Furthermore, the short TR is also constrained by the requirement for two echo times for DIXON‐based fat‐water separation, leaving insufficient time within each TR to accommodate larger spoiler gradient waveforms. Corbin et al. [28], also showed that an RF phase increment of 50 degrees is particularly sensitive to B1+ inhomogeneities and T2, emphasizing the importance of an accurate B1+ map. This sensitivity is accounted for in our work, as the true FAs are used to interpolate the incomplete spoiling correction factor curves.

A careful analysis of the location of the spatial saturation building block within the sequence diagram is essential for determining whether it interferes with the steady‐state SPGR signal. When the saturation pulses are interleaved with the partition acquisition block and the duration of the saturation module is larger than TR, then the longitudinal magnetisation will undergo a larger recovery than the amount flipped by the next RF excitation pulse, violating the steady‐state condition. Our parallel imaging experiments can detect such disruptions when sequence diagrams from vendors are unavailable. In this work we turned off saturation, given the differences in T1 between parallel imaging on and off. No flow artifacts were observed, though monitoring remains advisable. In cases where preparation pulses are desired, the steady‐state equation needs to be modified as shown by Wennen et al. [29]. They also showed an underestimation of T1 when not accounting for the temporal gap introduced by the saturation pulses. However, note that their analysis does not include corrections for B1+ inhomogeneities or incomplete spoiling, which further changes the steady‐state model.

One limitation of our correction method is the use of a fixed T2 value for the liver. Simulations suggest that errors remain < 2% for T2 values within ±10 ms of the chosen value (30 ms). Baudrexel et al. [26] also used a fixed T2 and Preibisch and Deichmann [24] didn't take T2 into account but also simulated T1 errors of less than 5% for T2 s varying between 60 to 110 ms in the brain using an RF phase increment of 50 degrees. Corbin et al. [28] also showed that φ₀ = 50° exhibits higher T2 sensitivity compared to other increments, such that a mismatch of 10 ms in the T2 assumed for the correction can produce residual errors of up to 2.7% at high B1+ efficiencies. Nevertheless, when multiparametric methods are used that simultaneously estimate T1 and T2, the T2 values can be incorporated into EPG simulations for improved accuracy.

The 2‐echo Dixon VFA SPGR is appealing for T1 mapping in the liver as it allows to calculate fat and water‐only whole‐liver images from a single breath‐hold. However, when using FAs near or above the Ernst Angle, different T1‐weightings are introduced in the fat and water signal [30]. This results in an overestimation of the fat‐fraction which consequently results in an underestimation in water‐liver T1, and this error increases as the fat fraction increases [8]. We have shown that for fat fractions up to 10% the T1 error is 3% [8]. To obtain an accurate water‐liver T1 for patients with fat percentages in the liver higher than 10%, the method would require more than two echoes together with a correction for T1‐weighting introduced by FAs near or above the Ernst angle [8].

Given the multiple breath‐hold acquisitions required for VFA SPGR (4 FAs), B1+ map (2 FAs), B0 map (1 FA), slight breath‐hold misalignments between acquisitions were observed in some volunteers. Attempts to register the FAs in the B1+ map or the SPGR were undertaken with different registration methods, including groupwise registration, which is considered state‐of‐the‐art for quantitative imaging [31]. However, registration was abandoned as differences in T1 of 50 ms or higher, between registered and non‐registered T1 maps, were observed for more than half of the pixels in a central liver slice of a volunteer without any discernible breath‐hold misalignment. A hypothesis is that registration shifts anatomy to a location with different B1− factors and both the B1− and the proton density are assumed to remain constant on a pixel‐by‐pixel basis over the various acquisitions used to determine the B1+ factor or estimate T1. In our repeatability study [8] a small within‐subject standard deviation of 21 ± 5 ms was achieved without registration, suggesting that small misalignments between the different acquisitions do not substantially affect the T1 map calculation for the central portion of the liver. The T1 maps near the liver edges were more sensitive to misalignments; however, these extremity regions will always be problematic as they correspond to areas of large B0 inhomogeneities as well as partial volume effects.

Although this work was carried out in the liver at 3T, the proposed framework could, in principle, be applied to other field strengths and anatomical regions using the VFA SPGR‐based T1 mapping. This would require new EPG simulations tailored to the TR/T1 and TR/T2 ranges of the tissue of interest, as well as to the RF spoiling phase increment adopted by the scanner manufacturer. However, the correction algorithm itself and the overall image‐processing pipeline would remain unchanged.

Conclusion

A correction for incomplete spoiling is crucial for accurately determining T1 values using the 3D VFA SPGR sequence. Spatial saturation can also disrupt the steady‐state signal, introducing an additional source of bias in T1 measurements. The incomplete spoiling correction proposed leverages EPG simulations across physiological ranges of liver TR/T1 and TR/T2 values at 3T. This approach is flexible and can be easily adapted to other anatomical regions, field strengths, or RF phase increments.
