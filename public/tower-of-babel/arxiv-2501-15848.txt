The Signals of the Doomsday
Amartya Sengupta, Dejan Stojkovic, De-Chang Dai

Abstract
The measured standard model parameters indicate that we might live in a false Higgs vacuum, though with a very long lifetime. However, small black holes can serve as catalysers and significantly speed up the phase transition. In fact, bubbles of true vacuum might already exist in our universe. We calculate the spectrum of Higgs particles produced by such a bubble, and use event generators to study their decay and subsequent evolution of the decay products to obtain the spectrum of emitted photons and neutrinos as a long-range signature. If the propagation of the bubble walls slows down due to interaction with the surrounding matter and plasma, these signals can reach us before the bubble wall hits us, thus representing the signals of the doomsday.



1
Introduction

It is believed that our universe has so far undergone a number of phase transitions at different energy scales, e.g. GUT, electroweak, QCD… Phase transitions usually introduce drastic changes in the structure of the universe, and if they happen late enough, they would almost certainly be fatal to any existing life forms.
Until recently, late-time phase transitions have not been a topic of intensive study (see however
Stojkovic:2007dw
;
Greenwood:2008qp
;
Isidori:2001bm
).

After the Higgs discovery, it became apparent that a scenario similar to the one outlined in
Greenwood:2008qp
(that the Higgs potential might develop instability with our current universe sitting in the unstable false vacuum) might actually be true
Degrassi:2012ry
. This might not be an immediate problem, since the lifetime of our universe in the false vacuum could be many billions of years. However, as shown in
Coleman:1977py
;
Coleman:1980aw
;
Berezin:1990qs
;
Gregory:2013hja
;
Burda:2015yfa
;
Burda:2016mou
, small primordial black holes can play the role of Higgs vacuum decay catalysers and significantly increase the tunneling probability. Depending on the small black hole mass and the exact parameters in the Higgs potential, the tunneling probability could be high enough to produce bubbles of true vacuum within the present lifetime of the universe.

Once a bubble of true vacuum is created, it will expand at a speed close to the speed of light. A bubble wall sweeps through the universe and destroys (or modifies beyond recognition) everything it encounters. If the bubble wall is approaching with the speed of light, then no signal emitted from the wall can reach the observer before the wall, simply because the wall is already moving with the maximal possible speed. However, interaction with the surrounding plasma and matter could slow down the wall propagation
Dai:2021boq
. For example, if the bubble is created one million light years away from us (somewhere between us and Andromeda) and the wall slows down by only
1
km/s, then the signal can reach us about three years before the bubble wall. It is then of utmost importance to calculate the characteristics of the possible signals that might come from an approaching bubble.

We investigate here the process in which the Higgs tunnelling can leave a signature in the form of produced particles that can potentially reach us before the bubble. In particular, we calculate the spectrum of Higgs particles created due to a vacuum mismatch inside and outside of the bubble. Then, we use
HDecay

Djouadi:1997yw
;
Djouadi:2018xqq
event generator to calculate the immediate Higgs decay products. Since we are mostly interested in obtaining the long range signature (i.e. emitted photons and neutrinos), we then use
Pythia

Bierlich:2022pfr
to hadronize the decay products and obtain the final photon and neutrino energy spectra.

2
The Higgs vacuum decay

In this section, we review the details of the Higgs vacuum decay in the presence of gravity. The high energy effective Higgs potential has been determined by the two-loop calculations in the standard model (without gravity) as

V_{\rm SM}(\phi)=\frac{1}{4}\left(\lambda_{*}+b\ln^{2}\frac{\phi}{\phi_{*}}\right)\phi^{4}.

(1)

The standard model range of parameters is
-0.01\lesssim\lambda_{*}\lesssim 0
,
0.1M_{p}\lesssim\phi_{*}\lesssim M_{p}
, where,
M_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{8\pi G}}=2.435\times 10^{18}
GeV, and
b\sim 10^{-4}

Degrassi:2012ry
;
Burda:2015yfa
;
Burda:2015isa
. This potential indicates that we live in an unstable vacuum, though with a very long lifetime
Andreassen:2017rzq
.

As shown in
Burda:2016mou
(for earlier work see
Greenwood:2008qp
), the inclusion of gravity can significantly modify the dynamics of the electroweak phase transition and drastically shorten the lifetime. We can parametrise the modification by including higher-order operators as

V(\phi)=V_{\rm SM}+\frac{\lambda_{6}}{6}\frac{\phi^{6}}{M_{p}^{2}}+\ldots

(2)

This potential can have a stable true vacuum and supports a first-order phase transition which happens via the nucleation of bubbles of the new vacuum inside the old vacuum.

The nucleation rate in presence of gravity is determined by a bounce solution with Euclidean metric signature (+,+,+,+) with an action

S_{E}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\left[-\frac{1}{16\pi G}\mathcal{R}+\left(\frac{1}{2}g^{ab}\partial_{a}\phi\partial_{b}\phi+V(\phi)\right)\right]\sqrt{-g}d^{4}x.

(3)

The spacetime geometry is taken to be spherically symmetric

ds^{2}=f(r)e^{2\delta(r)}d\tau^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)}+r^{2}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi^{2}),

(4)

with

f(r)=1-\frac{2G\mu(r)}{r},

(5)

where
\mu(r)
is the mass parameter of a black hole remnant.
The phase transition is originally initiated by a black hole of a certain seed mass,
M_{+}
, related to the black hole remnant mass as

M_{+}=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mu(r).

(6)

We distinguish here between the black hole seed mass,
M_{+}
, which triggers the phase transition, and the black hole remnant mass,
\mu(r)
, which is a leftover from the seed black hole after some of its energy is invested into the phase transition.

The Higgs field equations of motion in this curved background are

\displaystyle f\phi^{\prime\prime}+f^{\prime}\phi^{\prime}+\frac{2}{r}f\phi^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime}f\phi^{\prime}-V_{,\phi}=0

(7)

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}=4\pi r^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{2}f{\phi^{\prime}}^{2}+V\Big),\ \ \ \ \delta^{\prime}=4\pi Gr{\phi^{\prime}}^{2},

(8)

where
V_{,\phi}\equiv\partial V/\partial\phi
. The prime and double prime denote spatial derivatives.
The black hole horizon is at
r=r_{h}
, which is the solution to
f(r_{h})=0
. We will solve these equations numerically in order to get the function
\phi(r)
. To do this, we start from the horizon with a particular remnant parameter,
\mu_{-}
, at the horizon. The black hole horizon is given as
r_{h}=2G\mu_{-}
. At the horizon, the field
\phi
satisfies the boundary condition

\displaystyle\mu(r_{h})=\mu_{-}\text{, }\delta(r_{h})=0

(9)

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(r_{h})=\frac{r_{h}V_{,\phi}(\phi(r_{h}))}{1-8\pi Gr_{h}^{2}V(\phi(r_{h}))}.

(10)

At
r\rightarrow\infty
, the field
\phi
satisfies
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\phi(r)\rightarrow 0
.
We use a shooting method which starts from a special
\phi
at
r=r_{h}
and check whether
\phi
becomes
0
for very large values of
r
. If this condition is satisfied, we have a good solution to the equations of motion.

As shown in

When the Higgs field tunnels through the vacuum barrier, a bubble of true vacuum is created and starts expanding. We assume that the geometry around the bubble is not far from a flat spacetime, so that we can neglect gravitational effects. In this case, the pure Higgs action can be written as

S(\phi)=\int d^{4}x\Big(\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\phi)^{2}-V(\phi)\Big),

(11)

where we neglected other fields that couple to the Higgs. The equation of motion is

-\partial_{t}^{2}\phi+\triangledown^{2}\phi-V^{\prime}(\phi)=0.

(12)

To proceed, we perform a Wick rotation,
t\rightarrow i\tau
, and the equation of motion becomes

\partial_{\tau}^{2}\phi+\triangledown^{2}\phi-V^{\prime}(\phi)=0.

(13)

If we consider an
O(4)
symmetric solution, this equation can be written as

\frac{d^{2}\phi_{c}}{d\rho^{2}}+\frac{3}{\rho}\frac{d\phi_{c}}{d\rho}=V^{\prime}(\phi_{c}).

(14)

In this case,
\rho=\sqrt{\tau^{2}+r^{2}}
, while
\phi_{c}(\rho)
depends only on
\rho
. This equation provides a bounce solution with
\partial_{t}\phi(t=0)=0
and
\phi(t=0,\vec{x})=\phi_{c}(\tau=0,\vec{x})
as

\phi(t,\vec{x})=\phi_{c}(\rho=\sqrt{r^{2}-t^{2}}).

(15)

If we plot
r^{2}-t^{2}=\rho^{2}=
constant, we will find that the minimal
r
happens at
t=0
. This is the moment when the bubble bounces back.
Thus, the Higgs bubble is collapsing for
t<0
, bounces at
t=0
, and is expanding for
t>0
. The region
r>t
is still in the false vacuum, while the
r<t
region is in the true vacuum. So in this parametrization, nucleation happens at
t=0
, and then the true vacuum bubble expands. We simplify the problem by applying the thin wall approximation

\phi_{c}(\rho)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}v&\text{, for $\rho>R$}\\
v_{1}&\text{, for $\rho<R$}\end{array}\right.,

(16)

where
v
and
v_{1}
are the expectation valued of the Higgs field in the false and true vacuum respectively.

The creation of particles during the first order phase transition has been studied before in
Yamamoto:1994te
;
MersiniHoughton:1999tt
;
Maziashvili:2003kp
;
Maziashvili:2003sk
;
Maziashvili:2003zy

Vachaspati:1991tq

Swanson:1986hx

In order to study the creation of particles in Minkowski (as opposed to Euclidean) space, we will consider spatially homogeneous false vacuum decay as in
Maziashvili:2003kp

Tanaka:1993ez

4
Particle production due to vacuum mismatch

The mismatch of vacua is usually accompanied by particle production. In our case, the false Higgs vacuum is outside the bubble, while the true vacuum is inside. When the Higgs field tunnels through the potential barrier, this vacuum mismatch will lead to the Higgs particle production. For simplicity, we assume that the Higgs field tunnels to the true vacuum state homogeneously. This should be a good enough approximation since particles are produced locally, i.e. the relevant length scale is smaller than the size of the bubble. The Higgs field,
\phi
can fluctuate around the background
\phi_{c}
as
\phi=\phi_{c}+h
, where
h
is the fluctuation which satisfies equation of motion

\partial_{\tau}^{2}h+\triangledown^{2}h-V^{\prime\prime}(\phi_{c})h=0.

(17)

We again considered only the Higgs part of the Lagrangian and neglected other fields that couple to the Higgs. In this case the equation of motion (
17
) can be simplified to

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}^{2}h+\triangledown^{2}h-M^{2}h=0\text{, for $\tau<\tilde{\tau}$}

(18)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}^{2}h+\triangledown^{2}h-\mu^{2}h=0\text{, for $\tau>\tilde{\tau}$},

(19)

where we assume that it takes
\tilde{\tau}=-R
for the transition to finish.
The solution for
h
can be written as a combination of mode functions
g_{k}
, which satisfy
\triangledown^{2}g_{k}=-k^{2}

\displaystyle g_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}e^{\omega_{-}\tau}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}&\text{, for $\tau<\tilde{\tau}$}\\
A_{k}e^{\omega_{+}\tau}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}+B_{k}e^{-\omega_{+}\tau}e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}&\text{, for $\tau>\tilde{\tau}$}\end{array}\right.

Here,
\omega_{+}=\sqrt{\mu^{2}+k^{2}}
and
\omega_{-}=\sqrt{M^{2}+k^{2}}
, while
M=\sqrt{V^{\prime\prime}(v)}
and
\mu=\sqrt{V^{\prime\prime}(v_{1})}
are the masses of the Higgs field in the false and true vacuum regions respectively. Since
g_{k}
and
\partial_{\tau}g_{k}
must be continuous at
\tau=\tilde{\tau}
, for
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
we get

\displaystyle A_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\omega_{+}}(\omega_{+}+\omega_{-})e^{-(\omega_{+}-\omega_{-})\tau}

(23)

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\omega_{+}}(\omega_{+}-\omega_{-})e^{(\omega_{+}+\omega_{-})\tau}.

(24)

The particle creation spectrum is obtained from the Bogoliubov transform
Tanaka:1993ez

\displaystyle N_{k}=\frac{B_{k}^{2}}{A_{k}^{2}-B_{k}^{2}}=\left[\frac{(\omega_{+}+\omega_{-})^{2}}{(\omega_{+}-\omega_{-})^{2}}e^{4\omega_{+}R}-1,\right]^{-1}

(25)

We can estimate the energy density of Higgs particles inside the bubble (in the new vacuum) at the time of creation as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=

This is enormous total energy which is ultimately transferred into the decay products. Thus, the bubble of the true vacuum should be a very shiny object.

5
Higgs decay into photons and neutrinos

Ultimately, we want the spectrum of photons and neutrinos as a long-range observational signature. For that purpose, we will use the event generators which simulate Higgs decay and subsequent evolution of the decay products.
To simplify the analysis, we set
k=0
in the definitions of
\omega_{+}
and
\omega_{-}
, i.e. we consider the decay of Higgs particles at rest. Using this assumption, we calculate the number of Higgs particles produced due to vacuum mismatch from equation (
25
) for the specific value of the Higgs’s mass in true vacuum of
\mu=7.16\times 10^{-4}M_{p}
, resulting in
N_{k}(k=0)\simeq 1.365\times 10^{-25}
(per Planck volume). Furthermore, we assume that the standard model (SM) remains valid up to the true vacuum range at
\phi\approx 3.6\times 10^{-2}M_{p}
. Our analysis includes only the leading-order contributions to Higgs decay to the fermions, disregarding higher-order corrections. This approximation is justified within the framework of the effective field theory, where the higher-order terms are highly suppressed at the mass scales under consideration.

To obtain the branching ratios and the total decay width of the SM Higgs particle at a true vacuum, we use
HDecay
. We use only the tree level diagrams and switch off the electroweak corrections for the simulations because they become unstable beyond the 1 TeV range. For the rest of the input parameters, we follow the instructions given in
Djouadi:1997yw
without any changes.

The output data consisting of the Higgs mass, branching ratios and the Higgs decay width, generated from

Pythia

1\times 10^{7}

7.16\times 10^{-4}M_{p}=1.75\times 10^{15}
GeV, which provided the most accurate predictions when compared with theoretical decay width calculations for each decay channels. After extrapolation, we then finally get the observational signatures at Earth for the final-state particles.

In the final state, the most abundant particles are photons and neutrinos, while stable particles such as electrons, muons, protons, neutrons, and other heavy baryons are produced in significantly smaller quantities. These final-state particles primarily originate from Higgs decays into quarks, which subsequently hadronize. Since photons and neutrinos vastly outnumber the stable particles, they represent the primary observational signature of Higgs vacuum decay, allowing us to neglect the contribution from stable particles. They anyway represent the desired long-range signature of the bubble formation.

6
Conclusions

The work presented here was motivated by an interplay between the LHC collider data and recent theoretical progress in the field theory in curved spacetime. The standard model precision data coming from the LHC indicate that we currently live in a false vacuum. While the LHC data indicate that the lifetime of the false vacuum is safely large, it has been shown that small primordial black holes can serve as the phase transition catalysers and significantly increase the transition probability. In fact, it might be possible that the bubbles of the true vacuum already exist in our visible universe. One of the signatures of the existence of such bubbles would be copious production of the heavy Higgs particles due to the vacuum mismatch inside and outside
of the bubble. We first calculated the spectrum of Higgs particles produced in this way using the standard field theory techniques. We then used the HDecay event generator to simulate their decay. We finally used Pythia to hadronize the decay products and obtain the photon and neutrino energy spectra as a long-range signature of the bubble production.

The bubble wall’s velocity typically evolves in time as
v_{b}\sim t/\sqrt{t^{2}+R^{2}}
. Thus, for
t\gg R
, this velocity is almost the speed of light. However, it is never exactly equal to the speed of light. In addition, the bubble wall can be viewed as a highly coherent state of a large number of the Higgs quanta. As such it will strongly interact with virtually all the standard model particles. In the early universe, it will interact with the surrounding plasma, while at late times it will interact with other bubbles, stars, planets, interstellar gas etc. These interactions would further slow down the propagation of the wall. It is thus not very unreasonable to expect that these photons and neutrinos should be able to reach us before the bubble wall. No matter if this time delay is
10
seconds or three years, if our gamma ray and neutrino observatories ever detect spectra corresponding to Figures
4
and
5
, they might in fact represent the signals of the doomsday.

We note that the crucial assumption here is that there is no new physics beyond the standard model. New physics could potentially modify the Higgs potential and render our vacuum stable. Another possibility is that small primordial black holes do not exist. Since they are necessary catalysers, their absence in an appropriate mass range
Dai:2019eei
would perhaps invalidate this doomsday scenario.
