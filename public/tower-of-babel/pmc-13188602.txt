Facilitators, barriers, and predictors of social participation among older adults in Qazvin Province, Iran: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sima Rafiei, Saber Souri, Mohammad Amerzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial participation is a crucial component of healthy aging, contributing to older adults’ mental health, life satisfaction, and overall well-being. Identifying the factors that facilitate or hinder participation can inform strategies to promote active engagement in health-promoting activities. This study aimed to examine levels of social participation, associated facilitators and barriers, and predictors of participation among older adults in Qazvin, Iran.MethodsA descriptive–analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among 312 community-dwelling older adults aged 60–85 years (mean = 68.4, SD = 5.9) attending primary health-care centers. Participants were selected using a multistage random sampling method. Data were collected through interviewer-administered questionnaires, including the Participation Scale (P-Scale) and the Facilitators and Barriers Questionnaire (FBQ). Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics and scale scores. Group differences by gender and socioeconomic status (SES) were examined using independent-sample t-tests and one-way ANOVA. Pearson correlations and multiple linear regression were used to identify predictors of social participation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe mean overall P-Scale score was 41.3 (SD = 8.7), indicating moderate social participation. Engagement was highest in social interactions (M = 4.12, SD = 0.88) and family gatherings (M = 4.08, SD = 0.93), and lowest in volunteer work (M = 2.95, SD = 0.79) and civic/community activities (M = 2.84, SD = 0.81). Facilitators were highly endorsed (M = 3.72, SD = 0.64), particularly family support (82.7%), personal motivation (74.0%), and access to local facilities (68.9%). Barriers were moderate (M = 2.86, SD = 0.71), with financial difficulties (59.6%), physical limitations (53.2%), and lack of information (47.1%) being most common. Men participated more in civic/community activities, whereas women engaged more in family gatherings. Higher SES was associated with greater overall participation. Regression analysis showed that facilitators (β = 0.42) and family support (β = 0.28) positively predicted participation, while barriers (β = −0.31) and financial difficulties (β = −0.25) were negative predictors. Age, gender, and SES were also significant predictors.ConclusionSocial participation among older adults is shaped by a combination of personal, social, and socioeconomic factors. Strengthening facilitators—particularly family support—and reducing barriers such as financial and physical constraints may enhance engagement in social activities and promote healthy aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07261-9.

Background

The global population of older adults is increasing rapidly which presents significant social and health challenges for societies. According to the World Health Organization, the number of people aged 60 years and over is projected to grow from approximately 1.1 billion in 2023 to 1.4 billion by 2030, and to reach 2.1 billion by 2050 [1]. In Iran, demographic projections demonstrate a similar trend, with the proportion of older adults expected to rise from around 10% in 2019 to nearly one-third of the total population by 2050 [2, 3]. This demographic shift, accompanied by declining fertility and increasing life expectancy, highlights the urgent need for policies and programs that promote healthy aging and social participation among older adults.

Social participation is a key element of healthy aging which contributes to older adults’ mental health, life satisfaction, and quality of life. In Iran, several studies found that higher social participation correlates with better outcomes; greater social participation was associated with fewer psychological distress symptoms, particularly in social performance and somatic domains. Collectively, these studies indicate that social participation in later life is shaped by the interaction of individual, interpersonal, and contextual factors rather than by isolated determinants. Across different settings, poorer physical health, functional limitations, and financial constraints consistently emerge as key individual-level barriers, while social support from family, friends, and community networks functions as a central facilitator of engagement. At the same time, environmental and structural conditions—including access to age-friendly spaces, transportation, and organised social activities—play a crucial role in enabling or constraining participation. Importantly, these factors do not operate independently: health limitations may be amplified by inadequate social support, while supportive environments can mitigate individual-level vulnerabilities. Taken together, the evidence suggests that promoting social participation among older adults requires integrated approaches that simultaneously address personal capacity, social relationships, and broader structural conditions [4, 5, 6, 7]. While quantitative studies provide important evidence on the prevalence and correlates of social participation among older adults, they offer limited insight into how older people themselves experience facilitators and barriers in everyday life. To address this limitation, qualitative studies have explored the meanings, perceptions, and contextual factors underlying participation, thereby complementing quantitative findings and deepening understanding of the mechanisms through which participation is enabled or constrained. Qualitative studies further illuminate the pathways identified in quantitative research by revealing how individual, social, and environmental factors are experienced and negotiated by older adults in practice. These studies highlight those physical limitations and financial constraints, frequently identified as barriers in quantitative analyses. At the interpersonal level, qualitative evidence underscores the central role of family relationships and social networks, showing how emotional encouragement, companionship, and shared meaning can motivate participation even in the presence of health limitations. At the community level, older adults emphasise the importance of accessible environments, age-friendly public spaces, and culturally appropriate activities, which shape not only opportunities for participation but also a sense of belonging and dignity. Together, these qualitative insights contextualise and enrich quantitative findings by explaining how and why observed associations between health, resources, and participation emerge in lived experience [8, 9].

Existing evidence from Iran and other settings indicates that social participation among older adults is influenced by a constellation of interrelated factors operating at multiple levels. Individual characteristics such as age, education, health status, and motivation shape older adults’ capacity and willingness to engage in social activities, while family-level factors—particularly social and emotional support play a central enabling role. At the community and environmental level, access to facilities, transportation, information, and age-friendly infrastructure further determines opportunities for participation. Conversely, financial hardship, physical limitations, and social isolation function as key barriers that constrain engagement. Together, these findings suggest that social participation is best understood as a multidimensional and context-dependent phenomenon rather than the product of single determinants [8, 10, 11].

Conceptually, this study is informed by the active ageing framework and the social determinants of health perspective, which emphasise participation as a core component of wellbeing in later life. Within this framework, social participation is shaped by interactions between personal resources, social relationships, and structural conditions. Facilitators and barriers can therefore be understood as mechanisms through which broader socioeconomic and environmental contexts translate into differential opportunities for engagement. This perspective highlights the importance of examining not only levels of participation, but also the enabling and constraining factors that underlie observed patterns [12].

Despite growing interest in social participation among older adults in Iran, existing studies have often examined participation levels or specific correlates in isolation, relied on qualitative designs, or focused on single domains of influence. There remains limited quantitative evidence that simultaneously examines facilitators, barriers, and sociodemographic predictors of health-promoting social participation within an integrated analytical model. Moreover, provincial-level evidence from settings such as Qazvin remains scarce. Addressing these gaps is essential for informing targeted interventions and policies aimed at promoting active and equitable ageing. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the level of social participation among older adults in Qazvin Province, Iran, to identify key facilitators and barriers to participation, and to examine the sociodemographic and contextual predictors of participation in health-promoting social activities.

Methods

Study design

This research employed a descriptive–analytical cross-sectional design using quantitative methods.

Study setting and participants

The study population comprised older adults aged 60 years and above attending primary health-care centres in Qazvin Province, Iran which provide routine health and social services to older adults in both urban and semi-urban areas. Eligible participants were required to (1) have sufficient physical and cognitive ability to complete self-report questionnaires, (2) be capable of engaging in social and health-related activities, and (3) provide informed consent.

Qazvin Province was selected as the study setting due to its demographic, socioeconomic, and health system characteristics, which make it a relevant and informative context for examining social participation among older adults. Qazvin reflects a transitional Iranian province, combining urban and semi-urban populations with varying access to social, cultural, and health resources. Similar to many provinces in Iran, Qazvin is experiencing rapid population ageing alongside shifts in family structure, social support networks, and community engagement opportunities. Despite these trends, provincial-level evidence on social participation among older adults remains limited, with most existing studies concentrated in larger metropolitan areas. Studying Qazvin therefore provides valuable insight into facilitators and barriers to social participation in a context that is broadly representative of non-metropolitan Iranian settings and can inform locally relevant and scalable interventions.

Sampling strategy and sample size

The required sample size was determined using Cochran’s formula for finite populations, with parameters set at a 95% confidence level, an estimated proportion of 0.5 (to maximise variability), and a margin of error of 5%. This calculation yielded a minimum required sample of approximately [n = 280]. To account for potential attrition and non-response, an additional 10–15% was added to the estimated sample size, ensuring sufficient statistical power for subgroup analyses.

A multistage sampling method was used to recruit participants. In the first stage, healthcare centres within Qazvin Province were selected randomly based on eligibility criteria of age ≥ [60/65] years, residence in Qazvin Province, ability to communicate verbally, and willingness to participate. Older adults with severe cognitive impairment or acute illness at the time of data collection were excluded. In the second stage, eligible older adults were identified from the registries of older adults attending primary health-care centres. In the final stage, participants were selected using simple random sampling.

To enhance external validity and ensure that the findings reflected the diversity of the older adult population in Qazvin Province, stratification principles were applied during recruitment. Specifically, attention was given to balancing the sample across key sociodemographic variables, including gender, socioeconomic status, and educational level, as these factors are known to influence patterns of social participation and access to health-promoting activities. This strategy was designed to increase representativeness and to allow for meaningful subgroup analyses in subsequent statistical modelling.

Measures

Facilitators and barriers questionnaire (FBQ)

The FBQ was designed to assess factors enabling or hindering participation in health-promoting social activities among older adults. It includes two subscales: (a) facilitators, such as family support, personal motivation, and access to local facilities; and (b) barriers, such as physical health problems, financial difficulties, and lack of information. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale. Previous psychometric work in Iran has demonstrated satisfactory reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.70, and content validity confirmed by expert panels [10].

Participation scale (P-Scale)

The P-Scale was employed to measure the extent and quality of social participation. It evaluates domains such as group activities, social interactions, volunteer work, civic participation, and cultural or recreational involvement. Responses are provided on a Likert scale ranging from “never” to “always.” The P-Scale has been validated internationally and demonstrates strong reliability (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.70) and construct validity [13].

For all scales, higher scores reflect greater intensity or frequency of the measured construct, while lower scores indicate reduced levels. Where applicable, negatively worded items were reverse-coded prior to analysis.

Data collection process

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained interviewers between June and August 2025 at the selected study sites. Interviews were conducted in Persian in a private setting to ensure confidentiality. Trained research assistants explained the study purpose, obtained written informed consent, and supported participants in completing the instruments where necessary.

Study variables

The dependent variable was social participation. Higher scores indicate greater levels of social participation, whereas lower scores indicate more limited participation. The independent variables included: sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, education level, income, employment status); health-related variables (self-rated health, presence of chronic conditions, functional status); social and environmental factors, including perceived facilitators and barriers to social participation.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using STATA statistical software, version 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) were calculated for demographic variables, facilitators, barriers, and participation scores. Group differences in participation by demographic characteristics (gender, education, socioeconomic status) were tested using independent-sample t-tests (for two groups) and one-way ANOVA (for three or more groups). Where normality assumptions were violated, non-parametric alternatives (Mann–Whitney U test, Kruskal–Wallis test) were applied.

Correlational analyses (Pearson or Spearman coefficients depending on distribution) were performed to examine associations between facilitators, barriers, and participation levels. To identify predictors of participation, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted with participation scores as the dependent variable and individual, family, and environmental factors as independent variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Table 1 summarises the sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants. A total of 312 older adults aged 60 to 85 years (M = 68.4, SD = 5.9) participated in the study. Women comprised 54.5% of the sample (n = 170), and men 45.5% (n = 142). The majority were married (72.1%), while 27.9% were widowed, divorced, or single. In terms of education, 38.5% had no formal education, 41.0% had completed primary or secondary schooling, and 20.5% held a high school diploma or higher. Approximately 46.2% of participants reported a low socioeconomic status, 39.4% middle status, and 14.4% high status. Regarding self-rated health, 28.2% reported their health as good, 51.6% as fair, and 20.2% as poor (Table 1).

Facilitators and barriers

For all scales used in this study, higher scores indicate greater intensity of the measured construct. Specifically, higher social participation scores reflect greater engagement in social activities, higher facilitator scores indicate stronger perceived facilitators of participation, and higher barrier scores represent greater perceived obstacles to participation. Lower scores indicate the converse.

Mean scores indicated relatively high endorsement of facilitators of participation (M = 3.72, SD = 0.64, range 1–5). The most frequently endorsed facilitators were family support (82.7%), personal motivation (74.0%), and access to local facilities (68.9%). In contrast, barrier scores were moderate overall (M = 2.86, SD = 0.71, range 1–5). The most common barriers reported were financial difficulties (59.6%), physical limitations (53.2%), and lack of information about available activities (47.1%) (Table 2).

Levels of social participation

The mean overall Participation Scale (P-Scale) score was 41.3 (SD = 8.7, range 20–60), indicating a moderate level of participation. Subscale analysis showed the highest engagement in social interactions (M = 4.12, SD = 0.88) and family gatherings (M = 4.08, SD = 0.93), with lower participation in volunteer work (M = 2.95, SD = 0.79) and civic/community activities (M = 2.84, SD = 0.81).

As shown in Table 2, participants reported moderate levels of social participation overall. Facilitator scores were generally higher than barrier scores, suggesting that enabling factors were present alongside notable constraints to participation.

Group comparison

Group comparisons revealed significant gender differences in participation domains. Men reported higher levels of participation in civic/community activities (M = 3.05, SD = 0.82) compared with women (M = 2.65, SD = 0.79; t(310) = 4.26, p < 0.001). Conversely, women reported higher levels of participation in family gatherings (M = 4.22, SD = 0.86) than men (M = 3.90, SD = 0.97; t(310) = − 3.11, p = 0.002).

One-way ANOVA further indicated significant differences in overall participation by socioeconomic status (SES) (F(2, 309) = 6.72, p = 0.002). Post-hoc Tukey tests showed that participants with higher SES reported significantly greater participation compared with those in the low SES group (Table 3).

Correlational analysis

Bivariate correlational analyses demonstrated that facilitator scores were positively associated with overall participation (r = 0.46, p < 0.001), whereas barrier scores showed a significant negative association (r = − 0.39, p < 0.001). Among individual items, family support displayed the strongest positive correlation with participation (r = 0.49, p < 0.001), followed by personal motivation (r = 0.41, p < 0.001). Conversely, financial difficulties were negatively correlated with participation (r = − 0.33, p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Regression analyses

Univariate analyses were first conducted to identify candidate predictors of social participation. Variables demonstrating statistical significance and theoretical relevance were entered into unadjusted multivariable models (S1 and S2 supplementary file). The final adjusted model retained variables that contributed meaningfully to the explanation of social participation, resulting in a parsimonious and interpretable model.

To improve model interpretability and avoid multicollinearity, composite scores for facilitators and barriers to social participation were used in the regression analysis. In the multivariable regression analysis, social participation score was entered as the dependent variable. Independent variables included age, sex, marital status, education level, self-rated health, and the composite facilitator and barrier scores. Table 5 presents the results of the multivariable linear regression analysis examining factors associated with social participation. After adjustment for demographic and socioeconomic variables, facilitators and barriers demonstrated the largest standardized effects, indicating their central role in shaping participation. Sociodemographic variables showed smaller but statistically significant associations.

In the adjusted model, facilitators demonstrated a large positive effect on social participation (standardized β = 0.42, p < 0.001), while barriers showed a moderate-to-large negative effect (β = −0.31, p < 0.001); socioeconomic status had a moderate positive association (β = 0.18, p < 0.001), whereas age (β = −0.09, p = 0.032) and female gender (β = −0.11, p = 0.032) were associated with smaller but statistically significant reductions in participation. Using composite scores demonstrated that facilitators and barriers exert independent and opposing effects on social participation, with facilitators showing a positive association and barriers showing a negative association with participation levels.

Discussion

This study examined facilitators and barriers influencing older adults’ participation in health-promoting social activities in Qazvin Province, Iran. The findings confirm that social participation in later life is not solely an individual choice but is shaped by the interaction of personal capacities, family relationships, and broader socioeconomic and environmental conditions. This pattern is consistent with the active ageing framework and the social determinants of health perspective, which conceptualise participation as a core component of well-being influenced by layered structural and social factors [12].

The strong role of family support observed in this study can be interpreted through sociological and psychological mechanisms. From a social capital perspective, family networks provide emotional encouragement, practical assistance (such as transportation and financial help), and access to information about social opportunities, thereby lowering the perceived costs of participation [8, 10]. Psychologically, supportive family relationships enhance self-efficacy, reduce fear of dependency, and reinforce a sense of belonging, all of which are critical for maintaining engagement in later life [14]. In the Iranian cultural context, where intergenerational ties remain central, family support may function as a primary gateway to community involvement and social connectedness, explaining its dominant influence on participation.

Personal motivation also reflects important psychological processes related to autonomy, purpose in life, and perceived usefulness—key elements emphasised in theories of successful and active ageing. Older adults who perceive themselves as capable and socially valued are more likely to seek out and maintain social roles, whereas declining confidence or internalised age stereotypes may discourage participation even when opportunities exist [14].

Conversely, the negative influence of financial difficulties highlights the structural constraints shaping social participation. According to resource-based and social inequality perspectives, material resources determine access to transportation, organised activities, communication technologies, and suitable public spaces [14, 15]. In Iran, limited pension coverage and rising living costs may disproportionately restrict older adults’ ability to engage outside the household, reinforcing socioeconomic gradients in participation. Financial strain may also generate psychological stress and uncertainty, further reducing motivation to participate in social activities [15, 16].

The observed gender differences in participation patterns can be understood through life-course and gender-role theories. Men’s greater involvement in civic and community activities and women’s stronger engagement in family-based interactions reflect long-standing cultural expectations regarding public versus domestic roles in Iranian society [8, 14]. These socially constructed roles tend to persist into older age, shaping both opportunities and preferences for social engagement. As a result, women may face structural and normative barriers to participation in formal social domains, even when their desire for engagement remains strong.

Socioeconomic status further conditions participation by shaping educational attainment, health literacy, mobility, and access to social networks. Higher socioeconomic status not only increases material capacity but also enhances “participatory competence,” enabling older adults to navigate institutional settings and organised social environments more effectively [15]. This finding reinforces the view that social participation is unequally distributed and closely linked to broader patterns of social advantage and disadvantage.

The combined positive effects of facilitators and negative effects of barriers observed in this study support previous evidence that social participation emerges from the balance between enabling resources and constraining conditions rather than from isolated factors [8, 10]. Health limitations alone do not inevitably lead to social withdrawal; instead, their impact is mediated by family support, financial security, and access to age-friendly environments [15, 16].

Policy and practice implications

These findings suggest that policies aiming to promote active ageing should adopt a multidimensional approach. Strengthening family-based support systems through caregiver education, family-centred community programs, and intergenerational initiatives could enhance older adults’ confidence and opportunities for engagement [8, 10]. Reducing financial barriers through subsidised transportation, low-cost community activities, and targeted income support for low-SES older adults may also substantially expand participation [15].

Moreover, gender-sensitive interventions are needed to create socially acceptable and accessible public participation spaces for older women, addressing cultural constraints that limit their involvement in formal social activities [8, 10]. Improving access to information through primary health-care centres, local media, and community organisations could further reduce informational barriers and enhance awareness of available opportunities [14].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference regarding relationships between facilitators, barriers, and social participation. Self-reported data may be affected by recall or social desirability bias. Additionally, although participants were recruited from multiple primary health-care centres, the findings may not be generalisable to older adults in rural areas or those not engaged with health services.

Conclusions

This study highlights that older adults in Qazvin Province engage moderately in social participation, with family support, personal motivation, and access to local facilities serving as key facilitators, while financial difficulties, physical limitations, and lack of information act as significant barriers. Gender and socioeconomic status further influence the type and extent of participation, with men engaging more in civic/community activities and women more in family gatherings, and higher SES associated with greater overall engagement. Family support and facilitators emerged as the strongest predictors of participation, underscoring the importance of both social and environmental resources in promoting active aging. These findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions and policies that enhance facilitators, reduce barriers, and address sociodemographic disparities to foster meaningful social engagement and improve the well-being of older adults.
