Bi-directional support of T lymphoma and a lymphoma-associated monocyte population involving Notch signaling in mice
Authors: Sigrid Dubois, Yetzali Claudio-Medina, Jennifer L Marte, James L Gulley, Juergen R Mueller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTumor-induced manipulations of myelomonocytic differentiation have been widely reported. Here, we describe the elicitation of a tumor-supportive cell type in a T lymphoma model in mice. We observed that the development of T lymphoma coincided with the appearance of a lymphoma-associated monocytic cell type (LAM) of host origin accompanied by a diminished dendritic cell (DC) population. RNA-sequencing and cytometric analyses revealed that LAMs shared markers with both DCs and with resident macrophages, most closely resembling monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs). The numbers of T lymphoma cells were negatively affected by reducing LAMs suggesting a helper function of LAMs for tumor growth. LAMs expressed Notch ligands, and treatments of lymphoma-carrying mice with Notch signaling-inhibitors LY3039478 and DAPT selectively decreased expansions of T lymphomas with or without Notch1 mutations, respectively. Mature LAMs did not proliferate in the periphery. Instead, their expansion resulted from altered myelomonocytic differentiation in the bone marrows of lymphoma-carrying mice with increases in common monocyte/DC precursors but with reduced granulocyte, monocyte and DC precursor populations. These data suggest that developing T lymphomas are capable of reprogramming monocyte/DC differentiation to increase the numbers of a tumor-promoting DC type, attract it to tumor locations and may engage in Notch ligand-dependent signaling to support lymphoma survival.Keywords: T cell lymphoma, myeloid supporting cancer cell, myelomonocytic differentiation, Notch1 dependency

Introduction

Tumor disease is accompanied by altered composition landscapes of immune cell populations as well as effects on their functional abilities both in the tumor environment and systemically. Effects on the lymphocytic arm of the immune system involve aberrations of cytotoxic CD8 T and NK cells as well as abnormal helper functions. 1‐4 Many tumors are furthermore able to corrupt myeloid cells by suppressing their function of antigen presentation and by reprogramming their differentiation to elicit myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) with tumor-aiding behavior. 5‐7 MDSCs are found in all cancers, and cancer cells employ a plethora of mechanisms that lead to altered myeloid differentiation, disrupted maturation or even reprogramming of mature myeloid cells causing a substantial plasticity of this cell type. 8 While two major groups of granulocytic/polymorphonuclear and monocytic MDSCs have been defined, 5 , 6 MDSCs are a highly heterogeneous encompassing both immature myeloid cells and myeloid precursor cells. 7 Other cell types such as dendritic cells (DCs) with defective functions and cells with basophilic appearance may also play roles in supporting the growth of T lymphomas and myeloid leukemias, respectively. 9‐12 The importance of MDSCs in tumor progression is underscored by correlations between their accumulation and poor clinical prognoses. 13 Tumor-associated myeloid populations, including monocytes and dendritic cells, have been shown to support malignant T-cell survival and contribute to immunosuppression in peripheral T-cell lymphomas. 14 However, the identity and functional role of specific lymphoma-associated myeloid populations and their interaction with malignant T cells remain incompletely understood.

We have previously described the development of a T lymphoma/leukemia model (referred to as T lymphoma hereinafter) in mice with both TCR transgenes and the deletion of interleukin-15 (IL15)-dependent cells. 15 We had derived several cell lines half of which carried Notch1 mutations. Notch signaling plays a critical role in T-cell development and function. It has been implicated in T-cell lymphomagenesis and tumor–immune interactions within the tumor microenvironment. 16 , 17 We furthermore reported that lymphoma progression can be induced by engaging the T cell receptor on lymphoma cells, 18 and that this progression step coincided with a systemic depletion of NK cells presumably mediated by a tumor-secreted soluble factor. Prior to TCR engagement, the presence of NK cells prevented the development of lymphomas. Here, we describe an additional immunosuppressive effect of T lymphomas that affected the myeloid portion of the immune system. We show an association of lymphoma progression with a suppression of DC differentiation and the appearance of monocyte-derived DCs that promoted T lymphoma survival via Notch signaling.

Materials and methods

All the animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). The experimental protocols were approved under the NIH IACUC protocols MET-074 and CIO-001. All procedures were carried out in compliance with applicable NIH and federal regulations and are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. The mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in NIH animal facilities with ad libitum access to food and water and were maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle. Animals were monitored daily for health status. Humane endpoints were applied when the mice exhibited signs of distress or tumor burden in accordance with IACUC guidelines. Euthanasia was performed using CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary physical method, which is consistent with the recommendations of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals.

Animal sources and numbers: C57BL/6, C57BL/6-Thy1.1, and C57BL/6-CD45.1 mice were obtained from and/or bred within the NIH/NCI animal breeding facility. Female mice aged 8–12 weeks were used for all the experiments. The group sizes were typically n = 5 to 10 mice per experimental group, with a total of approximately 220 mice used across all the experiments reported in this study.

In vivo antibody-mediated NK cell depletions were done by i.p. treatment with anti-NK1.1 (25 µg twice weekly, Bio X Cell). Lymphoma cell lines were routinely passaged into NK cell-depleted mice by i.p. injections of approximately 105 lymphoma cells following anti-NK1.1 treatments. DT (Sigma) treatments were done i.p. using 4 ng/g body weight each. To determine in vivo proliferation rates, 1 mg of BrDU was injected i.p., and BrDU incorporation rates were determined 16 h later using BrDU Flow Kit (BD Biosciences).

Cell culture

All cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 50 µM 2-mercaptoethanol and antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin). Erythrocytes were removed from spleen cell suspensions by lysis in 0.15 M NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, and 0.1 mM EDTA (pH 7.2). Lymphoma-containing spleens were cultured in 4 ng/ml murine GM-CSF (Peprotech). The number of surviving lymphoma cells was determined at day 5 using FACS.

Tumor protocols

Mice (8–12 weeks, female) were randomly distributed into groups of ten animals. The tumors were transferred in 200 µl PBS as follows: 106 MC38 cells i.v., 106 EL4 cells i.v., and 105 SJ cells i.p. Treatments with LY3039478 were done at days 3, 5, 7, 10, 12, 14, 17, 19, and 21 post transplantation by s.c. injections in corn oil. Treatments with DAPT were done at days 29, 30, and 31 post transplantation by s.c. injections in corn oil.

Cytometry and cell population definition

For cytometric analyses, the cells were blocked with anti-CD16/32 (15 min at RT), which was followed by a 30-min incubation on ice with specific antibodies. All the antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S1. For the biotinylated antibodies, an additional 15-min incubation on ice was done with streptavidin-PE-Cy5.5 (eBioscience). Erythrocytes were lysed in PBMC samples after antibody staining using CAL-LYSE (Invitrogen). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software. For depleting mature hematopoietic cells, we used the Cell Depletion Kit, Miltenyi mouse 130-110-470. For depletion, the cells were magnetically labeled with an antibody cocktail against lineage markers (CD5, CD45R (B220), CD11b, Gr-1 (Ly-6G/C), 7-4, and Ter-119). The labeling was performed in a two-step procedure. The cells were first labeled with a cocktail of biotinylated antibodies, followed by labeling with Anti-Biotin MicroBeads. Cell population definitions: Basophil-like cells were defined as CD3−/CD19−/NK1.1−/CD11b+/FcεR1+; non-basophil-like myeloid populations were defined as CD3−/CD19−/NK1.1−/CD11b+/FcεR1− cells, classical basophils were distinguished as CD3−/CD19−/NK1.1−/CD11b−/FcεR1highcells, and DCs were defined as CD3−/CD19−/NK1.1−/CD11b+/CD11chigh.

RNA-seq

RNA preparations were done using the RNeasy plus kit (Qiagen). Each sample was subjected to library preparation using the NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit v2 (NEB #E7405), NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB #E7760) and NEBNext® Multiplex Oligos for Illumina® (96 Unique Dual Index Primer Pairs, NEB, E6440) as described in the manufacturer's manual. High-throughput sequencing was conducted using the Illumina NextSeq 2000 P2 kit. Samples were normalized by Counts Per Million. For presentation, all values were increased by 1, and the data are shown on logarithmic scales.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were done using Prism 7. Data from cell counts and cell percentages were compared using unpaired t-test, and two-tailed p-values are shown (n.s. p > 0.05, *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, and ****p ≤ 0.0001). p-values for mouse survival were calculated by Log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test.

Results

Appearance of distinct lymphoma-associated basophil-like cells and concurrent DC decreases during T lymphoma development

T lymphomas develop in IL-15-deficient mice on a TCR-transgenic background, 15 , 18 and disease progression is associated with aberrations of lymphocyte populations, mainly the systemic disappearance of NK cells that otherwise recognize and lyse the lymphoma cells. We studied whether these T lymphomas are associated with additional changes within the myeloid compartment of the immune system. We followed PBMC populations during T lymphoma development and found substantial increases of a cell type that was scored as basophils in complete blood counts (Figure 1A, top). Cytometric analyses revealed the expression of the basophil-typical surface marker FcεR1 but also of the myeloid marker CD11b, which is not normally found on basophils (Figure 1A, bottom). The appearance of these basophil-like cells was independent of Notch1 mutations, as this population appeared in mice carrying lymphoma lines both with (SJ3 and SJ6) and without mutations (SJ1 and SJ4). In addition, T lymphoma progression caused by TCR engagement was not required, as basophil-like cells were observed in mice with either pre- or post-TCR engagement lymphomas (Figure 1A, SJ3S versus SJ3R). 18 Figure 1B shows that basophil-like cells were of host origin using differences in congenic marker expression between lymphomas and hosts. Together these data show a concurrent appearance of T lymphomas with a basophil-like cell type that was largely absent in control mice.

We had previously observed the cytotoxic activities of DCs using T lymphoma cells as targets, which may contribute to decreasing the initial rate of T lymphoma development. 19 We enumerated DCs in spleens as CD11b+/CD11chigh cells and found their numbers significantly decreased in lymphoma-bearing mice (Figure 1C). Figure 1C shows a CD11b+CD11clow population in tumor-bearing mice that represented basophil-like cells based on FcεR1 expression (Figure 1C red gate and see below). DC number decreases were not limited to mice carrying T lymphomas, since lower numbers were also found in mice bearing the colon cancer line MC38 or the T lymphoblast line EL4 though to lesser extents when compared to the SJ T lymphomas (Figure S1, left). Similarly, the same tumor-carrying mice also showed the appearance of some basophil-like cells, as identified by surface expressions of CD11b and FcεR1 (Figure S1, right). Thus, DC decreases and simultaneous basophil-like cell increases can be observed in several murine tumor models.

Basophil-like cells resemble moDCs

We applied cytometry and RNA-seq analyses to characterize T cell lymphoma-associated basophil-like cells in the spleens of lymphoma-bearing mice. For cytometry, basophil-like cells were identified among the splenocytes of lymphoma-bearing mice as CD3−/CD19−/NK1.1−/CD11b+/FcεR1+ cells and compared with DCs (CD3−/CD19−/NK1.1−/CD11b+/CD11chigh) and monocytes (CD3−/CD19−/NK1.1−/CD11b+/CD11c−) from control spleens as well as with basophils from control PBMCs (CD3−/CD19−/NK1.1−/FcεR1+, Figure S2). While the CD11b+CD11c− gate also contained neutrophils, the gate for basophil-like cells included a low contamination with Ly6C+/Ly6G+ neutrophils (Figure S2). Bulk RNA-seq was done with FACS-sorted splenocytes using the same markers. Figure 2A shows that the only surface marker shared between basophil-like cells and basophils was FcεR1. Basophil-like cells shared some surface markers with DCs, such as CD11c, CD116, FLT3 and CD26, 20 but their nature as conventional DCs could be ruled out by the absence of the lineage-determining transcription factor ZBTB46 (Figure 2B). The surface molecules in common with monocytes included CD115, F4-80, and CD14. Basophil-like cells also shared several expression markers with both conventional DCs (cDCs) (ITGAX, ITGAM, SIRPA) and classical tissue-resident macrophages (Lyz2, MERTK, MAFB) in RNA-seq analyses. Basophil-like cells showed the closest similarity to moDCs based on expressions of FcεR1, 21 CD88, 22 CD64, CD172a, CD11c, MHC class II, and low Ly6C. MoDCs have been described to expand during inflammatory conditions, up-regulate CD11c and MHC class II and down-regulate Ly6C. 23 Thus, T cell lymphomas appear to elicit a cell type most closely related to moDCs, which we will refer to as lymphoma-associated monocytes (LAMs) hereinafter.

LAMs support T lymphoma survival

We hypothesized that T lymphoma cells elicit LAMs for their reciprocal support. We explored ways to negatively affect LAMs in order to study their effects on T lymphoma cells. Transgenic mouse models that delete either basophils or cDCs were of limited use since LAMs lacked both the expression of basophil-specific MCPT8/basoph8 (Figure 2A) and of the cDC-specific ZBTB46 (Figure 2B). DC-targeting backcrosses of CFS1R-specific cre-lox mice with ZBTB46 locus diphtheria toxin (DT) preceded by a lox site-flanked stop cassette caused deletions of cDCs but not of LAMs, nor did cDC deletions affect lymphoma numbers (Figure S3A), indicating that cDCs were dispensable for lymphoma growth in our model.

As an alternative, we used mice that expressed the diphtheria toxin receptor (DTR) under CD11c control. Diphtheria toxin (DT) treatment in this model depletes multiple CD11c-expressing populations, including cDCs, and is therefore not specific to LAMs. However, as the specific deletion of DCs in ZBTB46-DT mice failed to affect lymphoma growth (Figure S3A), the additional deletion of LAMs would mainly address the effect of LAM presence on lymphoma survival. LAM numbers among PBMCs were measured before and 24 h after DT injections into CD11c-DTR mice and into their control littermates. As shown in Figure S3B, DT injections decreased LAM numbers by nearly half within 24 h in CD11c-DTR mice only. We were unable to extend beyond a 24-h period since the majority of the mice succumbed within 48 h, presumably due to the presence of large numbers of dead cells, since murine mortality was not observed without DT injections nor after DT injections into lymphoma-bearing control mice. These results indicate that although CD11c-DTR–mediated depletion is not specific to LAMs, DT treatment in tumor-bearing mice primarily impacted the LAM population.

We then studied whether these DT-induced decreases in LAM numbers would also affect lymphoma numbers. We monitored individual tumor burden by measuring CD30 levels in the plasma of lymphoma-bearing mice. CD30 is expressed and released by T lymphoma cells and exhibits low background levels in tumor-free B6 mice. Plasma CD30 levels strongly correlated with total lymphoma cell numbers in the spleen and lymph nodes (R2 = 0.92; Figure S3C), supporting its use as a surrogate marker in this model. We injected DT at a tumor burden that corresponded to an estimated 50 to 400 million tumor cells per mouse. Figure 3A shows that while lymphoma burdens were similar at the time of DT injections based on CD30 measurements in plasmas, CD11c-DTR mice had significantly fewer lymphoma cells in spleens and lymph nodes 24 h later when compared with wild-type litter mates. Of note, DT injections could not affect lymphoma cells directly since they lacked the DTR transgene. Thus, we conclude that the deletion of CD11c-expressing non-cDCs from lymphoma-bearing mice negatively affected lymphoma numbers and, furthermore, that LAMs are most likely necessary for lymphoma survival.

While lymphoma sizes showed no significant difference prior to treatments, DT injections had reduced the number of lymphoma cells in spleens and lymph nodes 24 h after DT injections in DTR mice only. Notch1 ligation participates in T lymphoma support by LAMs

Notch1 mutations are commonly found in portions of T cell lymphoma/leukemia diseases, including some of our murine T lymphoma lines suggesting the significance of the NOTCH1 pathway in T lymphoma development. 9 , 24 Hence, we explored the impact of NOTCH1 signaling onto T lymphoma growth and survival. Figure 3B shows that the expressions of three different NOTCH ligands were found on the surface of LAMs, including DLL4, which is presumably involved in signaling to T lymphoma cells. 16 The enzyme γ-secretase is crucial for NOTCH activation, 25 and we studied the effects of two of its inhibitors on T lymphoma numbers and proliferation in mice using lymphomas with or without NOCTH1 mutations (Figure 3C). The inhibitor DAPT is generally not used for survival studies because of gastrointestinal side effects; therefore, we treated groups of T lymphoma-bearing mice on three consecutive days with DAPT that were followed by lymphoma counts two days later. DAPT significantly decreased the tumor burdens in mice bearing lymphomas without NOTCH1 mutations but had no effect on mutation-positive tumors (Figure 3C, top). In addition, BrDU incorporation remained unaffected by inhibitor treatments even with mutation-negative tumors (Figure S4), suggesting that the NOTCH1 pathway acted more on the survival than on the proliferation of T lymphoma cells. The stronger effect of DAPT presence on the survival of NOTCH1 mutation-negative lymphomas was also seen in culture experiments (Figure S5), with lower doses having stronger inhibitory effects in cultures of lymphomas without Notch1 mutation.

To confirm the effect of γ-secretase inhibition, we treated lymphoma-bearing mice with an alternative inhibitor, LY3039478 (Figure 3C, bottom). Here, treatments were started three days after lymphoma transfers, and LY3039478 was administered s.c. thrice weekly over three weeks. Surprisingly, this γ-secretase inhibitor, in contrast to DAPT, increased survival exclusively of mice bearing lymphomas with Notch1 mutations. Of note, decreases of lymphoma numbers were more rapid and severe after LAM deletions than after NOTCH1 inhibitions alone suggesting the presence of additional LAM-mediated lymphoma support pathways (compare Figure 3A and C). Overall, these findings indicate that LAMs express NOTCH ligands and that their tumor-supporting effects may partially involve NOTCH signaling within the tumor microenvironment, although their direct role as the source of NOTCH activation in T lymphoma cells remains to be established.

Corruption of myelomonocytic differentiation in bone marrows by T lymphomas

As mentioned previously, cancers use multiple ways to affect the composition of monocyte-derived suppressor cells, including the proliferation of peripheral cells and aberrant differentiation pathways. 8 To analyze the source of the LAMs in our lymphoma model, we initially determined whether LAMs had proliferated by analyzing BrDU incorporation in lymphoma-bearing mice. Figure 4A shows that LAMs had not divided over a 16-h period, suggesting the expansion of LAM precursor cells instead.

The blue rectangular gates in the bottom right panels depict a population largely specific for lymphoma-bearing mice. (D) Averages of progenitor populations from 4 mice per group. Only three populations that represent progenitors of moDCs were expanded (bottom) while all other populations showed decreases. We then studied whether the generation of LAMs was the result of changes in the frequency of myeloid progenitors in the bone marrows. We removed mature hematopoietic cells that included both lymphoma cells and LAMs prior to analyses. We then evaluated these MACS-sorted lineage-negative cells by flow cytometry for c-kit, FcγR, CD34, Ly6C, CD115, and CD135 to enumerate myeloid progenitors (Figure 4B) as previously described 26 as well as for c-kit, FcγR, CX3CR1, CD16, CD11c, CD115, and CD135 to enumerate DC precursors (Figure 4C). Figure 4B shows that increases of progenitor cell numbers in a single pathway leading to the generation of moDCs. 27 Progenitor number increases included FLT3+/CD115low common myeloid progenitors (Figure 4B/D, II), monocyte-DC-progenitors (Figure 4B/D, III) and a population of FLT3+ common monocyte progenitors that are nearly absent in tumor-free mice (Figure 4B/D, blue gate). In contrast, another monocyte precursor population, granulocyte‒monocyte progenitors (Figure 4B/D, IV), 26 showed decreases in numbers suggesting that these cellsare not precursors of LAMs. Stains for common DC progenitors revealed decreased numbers in tumor-bearing mice, suggesting that peripheral cDC number decreases were caused at least in part by reduced bone marrow differentiation (Figure 4C/D). Together, these data suggest the ability of T cell lymphomas to corrupt myeloid bone marrow differentiation (as depicted in Figure 4E) to generate a tumor-supporting myeloid cell type at numbers sufficient for lymphoma growth.

Discussion

Tumor-aiding activities for MDSCs have been shown in many tumor models, 7 but the high plasticity of these cell types makes it difficult to pinpoint MDSCs in individual tumors as well as comparisons of MDSC activities between tumor models. In this study, using a murine lymphoma model, we show that the tumor is able to corrupt myeloid differentiation to elicit a clearly identifiable moDC-like cell type that reciprocally supports lymphoma growth via mechanisms that may include Notch ligation. Different from classical MDSCs, LAMs may not suppress immune responses directly, but anti-tumor responses were affected in the SJ lymphoma model both by direct lymphoma effects on NK cell survival and by decreased DC numbers that resulted from altered myelomonocytic differentiation in the bone marrow.

The multitude of different MDSCs that have been described in individual tumors makes it likely that during its progression the tumor adapts expression patterns that elicit any of the various monocytic cell types at various stages of maturity that best serve its needs. This need is based on the existence of mutation patterns in tumors and the lack thereof; mutations in pathways may completely obviate external signaling while unaffected pathways require outside stimulation. Notch signaling has been shown to regulate interactions between T cells and components of the tumor microenvironment, including dendritic cells and macrophages, and to promote the proliferation and progression of peripheral T-cell lymphomas in a ligand-dependent manner. 16 , 17 In the case of the T cell lymphoma used in this study, lymphoma cells appear to require Notch signaling to survive. In this context, monocytic support cells express several Notch ligands and are therefore well positioned to contribute to Notch ligation within the tumor microenvironment. Somewhat surprisingly, the requirement of Notch ligation appears to be unaffected by the presence of mutations in the lymphomas Notch1 hetero-dimerization domain, mutations that are believed to induce ligation-independent Notch signaling. 28 There appeared to be no difference in the numbers of LAMs between mice that carry lymphomas with or without Notch1 mutations (Figure 1), nor could we find phenotypical differences between mutation-positive and -negative lymphomas or elicited LAMs that included Notch ligand expressions. 18 Such a requirement for Notch ligands in T cell lymphomas/leukemias despite mutations in HD domains has been noted by others. 29 LAMs identified in this study are likely to provide additional tumor-supporting signals, as a deletion of LAMs resulted in a more rapid and pronounced decrease in lymphoma numbers compared to γ-secretase inhibition alone (Figure 3), suggesting that LAM-mediated support extends beyond Notch signaling.

The existence of tumor-dependent specificities of monocytic support cell phenotypes is emphasized by the differences between LAMs in our model to the lymphoma-supporting DCs that have been reported by the Ehrlich group. 30 T cell lymphomas in both models are induced by the expression of pre-rearranged TCR genes while NK cells are absent in our model only. Yet different from moDCs supporting T cell lymphoma growth, which may include Notch ligation, as shown here, T cell lymphomas derived in NK cell presence in their LN3 model were supported by CD11chigh/ZBTB46-expressing cDCs, mainly via the PDGF and IGF pathways. Given that both models result in the development of T cell lymphomas this suggests that differences in monocytic support cell phenotype and function may exist even between individual tumors of the same tumor type.

The choice of moDC-like cells as a tumor-supporting cell type for T cell lymphomas is hardly surprising given their functions under physiological conditions. T cell priming has been described as a function of moDCs, 31 while others attribute the in situ propagation of immune responses rather than the initiation of immune responses to their function. 32 In addition, moDCs rather than cDCs are critical for CD8 activation in the context of anti-tumor responses. 33 It seems likely that T cell lymphoma cells elicit a monocytic cell type that naturally supports T cell responses and re-program them specifically to meet their needs. Such an alteration might be the expression of the chemokine receptor CCR2: LAMs lack the expression of CCR2, which directs moDCs to sites of inflammation; 34 its lack may allow LAMs to migrate to the spleen and lymph nodes, which are sites of lymphoma growth.

Our data show a strong submission of myeloid differentiation in the bone marrows to the needs of the tumor for its survival (Figure 4). It appears that all progenitor populations not involved in LAM generation had been decreased in numbers. Changes in myeloid progenitor differentiation due to the presence of tumors have been well described. 35 , 36 Tumors are able to re-program non-cancerous cells to aid their own progression, 37 mainly through the secretion of cytokines, chemokines and non-protein inflammatory messengers. This process often results in an abundance of immature and defective myeloid cells that can function as MDSCs in the tumor environment. 37 Increased frequencies of distinct immature or suppressive myeloid subsets among peripheral blood cells have been reported for a multitude of different tumor types. 37

A surprising result came from the use of two different γ-secretase inhibitors in attempts to disrupt the lymphoma-aiding activities of LAMs (Figure 3). We had previously established several T cell lymphoma lines 15 half of which carried Notch1 mutations but were otherwise phenotypically undistinguishable from mutation-negative lines. γ-Secretase inhibitors affect Notch cleavage, which is necessary for its activity, and all mutated lines carry base pair replacements in a region that causes ligand-independent activation. In addition, all lymphoma lines independently of mutation status, are also associated with Notch ligand-expressing LAMs in vivo (Figure 1). The tumor-inhibiting efficacy of all γ-secretase inhibitors would be the expected outcome, yet we observed select inhibitions of mutation-positive or -negative tumors if LY3039478 or DAPT were administered, respectively, for which we do not have an explanation at current. It should be noted that this result suggests that γ-secretase inhibitor treatment of cancer patients could be made more efficient by analyzing the status of Notch mutations and choosing specific γ-secretase inhibitors.

A limitation of the present study is that all experiments were conducted in murine models, and the presence of LAMs in human T cell lymphomas remains to be established. While our data identify a monocyte-derived DC-like population that supports lymphoma growth in mice, it will be important to determine whether analogous populations exist in human disease.

Recent single-cell RNA sequencing studies have revealed substantial heterogeneity and continuum relationships between human monocyte and dendritic cell populations. 38 In addition, single-cell analyses of human cutaneous T cell lymphoma have identified diverse myeloid populations, including monocyte/macrophage and dendritic cell subsets, that engage in functional interactions with malignant T cells and contribute to immunosuppressive and tumor-promoting microenvironments. 39 These findings suggest that related cell populations may arise in patients. Future studies will be required to directly validate the presence, phenotype, and functional role of such populations in human lymphoma samples.

MDSCs have emerged as a target for tumor treatments. 6 , 13 The use of chemotherapy or MDSC-specific antibodies was proposed to reduce their numbers, reducing tumor support and immune inhibition as a result. The use of antibodies, however, is hampered by the phenotypical multitudes of MDSCs. Indeed, individual tumors of the same tumor type may depend on different MDSCs for support necessitating their identification in each tumor microenvironment to allow specific targeting. Our data clearly identified one monocytic support cell type, thus enabling us to show that their reduction led to a reduction in tumor size also. It furthermore opens the possibility to test the effect of LAM reduction on tumor size by other means, such as injections of deleting antibodies or CAR-expressing lymphocytes. An obvious advantage of specifically targeting tumor-support cells rather than the tumors themselves is an expected absence of phenotypical drift, 40 which can be a cause of therapeutic failures.

In summary, the data presented in this study describe a clearly identifiable tumor support cell type that is elicited by T cell lymphomas via adjusting myeloid differentiation. This tumor-aiding cell population represents a potential target in order to deny tumor cells the support from non-tumorous cells.
