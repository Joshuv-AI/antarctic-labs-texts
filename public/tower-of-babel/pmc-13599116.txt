Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy globally, and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1, 2]. Postoperative recurrence and metastasis represent key determinants of mortality in affected individuals [3, 4]. In current clinical practice, surgery combined with adjuvant therapy is a common approach to improve prognosis. However, the inherent heterogeneity of breast cancer results in varied responses to identical treatments. Consequently, clinical practice tends to escalate or de-escalate treatments based on patients’ different recurrence risks to maximize improvements in patients’ prognosis and quality of life. Unfortunately, there is no recognized and reliable method for stratification of recurrence risk, which largely hinders the implementation of personalized diagnosis and treatment.

Conventional prognosis evaluation of breast cancer recurrence mainly relies on traditional clinical and histopathological parameters, including age, tumor size, and histological grading [5, 6]. However, histopathological parameters are often obtained by invasive means, which are susceptible to tumor heterogeneity, making each region unique. As such, invasive methods cannot achieve real-time monitoring of macro- and microscale changes in the tumor. Therefore, a noninvasive method that can comprehensively and dynamically assess tumor heterogeneity is needed to provide risk prediction markers for breast cancer patients.

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence have positioned it as an excellent tool for automated quantitative radiology. Through multidimensional mining of grayscale image data, machine learning can characterize and reflect microscopic information, such as tumor molecular features. Machine learning, particularly radiomics analysis and deep learning (DL), has made substantial strides in the early diagnosis, prediction of treatment response, and evaluation of prognosis in breast cancer [7, 8]. However, it is necessary to strictly distinguish breast cancer recurrence prediction from conventional diagnosis or classification tasks. Conventional diagnostic or classification tasks generally focus on classifying the current static status, such as distinguishing benign from malignant lesions and identifying molecular subtypes.

In contrast, recurrence prediction is inherently a survival analysis task for the time-to-event outcome. Such tasks must continuously account for patients without recurrence during follow-up, dynamically model evolving risks over time, and ultimately provide individualized risk assessments of future recurrence events. Currently, DL is playing an increasingly important role in assessing breast cancer prognosis, partly due to its ability to fine-tune a pre-trained feature-extraction model to the target scene rather than training the model from scratch, and, finally, to enable high-throughput prediction with limited data [9]. At present, DL models for breast cancer recurrence prediction are mostly based on clinical and pathological features, and it remains to be determined whether incorporating radiomics and DL features can improve their performance [10].

This study proposed an interpretable deep learning model for breast cancer recurrence prediction and risk stratification. On this basis, we integrated multimodal DL features, radiomics, and clinicopathological features to establish a combined model. This comprehensive model aims to furnish clinical decision support and diagnostic assistance.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort and data collection

This retrospective study, conducted following the TRIPOD-AI (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis – Artificial Intelligence) statement [11] and the Declaration of Helsinki, received approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (2024-KLS-120-01) and the First People’s Hospital of Hangzhou (KY-20230216-0024-01). Since this was a retrospective study, informed consent was waived. A total of 1099 patients with primary breast cancer were initially enrolled, including 815 cases from Center I (The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University) and 284 cases from Center II (Hangzhou First People’s Hospital). All patients underwent preoperative breast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and subsequent surgical treatment. After screening against the exclusion criteria, 624 patients were included in the study. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Fig. 1. Patients were divided into an internal dataset and an external dataset based on the medical institutions. The internal dataset was further randomly divided into a training set and an internal validation set in a 7:3 ratio using a computer-generated random seed.

Clinical data from medical records encompassed patient demographics (age, menopausal status), clinical and pathologic staging, surgical procedures, adjuvant therapies (chemoradiotherapy, endocrine therapy), and recurrence time.

Tumor recurrence was defined as local (ipsilateral breast or chest wall and axilla, infraclavicular, or supraclavicular lymph nodes) or distant metastasis to other organs [12]. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was calculated as the interval from surgery to the first recorded local or distant recurrence, or to the last clinical follow-up without recurrence.

Multicenter data harmonization

To ensure comparability and validity of the external validation, key variables were standardized across the two participating centers (Center I and Center II). First, identical 3T MRI scanners (Siemens Vario) and harmonized acquisition protocols were used, as detailed in the Supplemental Material. Second, all histopathological assessments (including ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-67 status) were performed in accordance with the same international guidelines (ASCO/CAP for HER2 and the St. Gallen criteria for molecular subtyping), ensuring diagnostic consistency. Third, the definition of the primary endpoint (recurrence-free survival, RFS) and the follow-up schedule for detecting recurrence were consistent between institutions, minimizing bias in outcome ascertainment.

Histopathology

Histopathologic parameters, including histologic type, estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and Ki-67, were obtained from the histopathology report and reviewed by an experienced pathologist (10 years of experience). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to evaluate ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-67 levels. ER- or PR-positive (> 1%) was defined as hormone receptor (HR)- positive, and the Ki-67 threshold was set at 14%. Regarding HER-2 status, tumors with an IHC staining score of 0 or 1+ were defined as HER-2 negative, and those with an IHC score of 3+ were defined as HER-2 positive, according to the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO)/College of American Pathologists (CAP) guidelines [13]. For tumors with an IHC staining score of 2+, further fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed to confirm HER-2 status. Unamplified FISH results were HER-2 negative, and amplified FISH results were positive. According to the 2013 St. Gallen Consensus Conference, breast cancer was classified as Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-enriched, and triple-negative breast cancer [14].

Data preprocessing

Prior to model construction, completeness inspection was performed for all variables. This retrospective study exhibited high data integrity. The missing rate of continuous variables (e.g., age, tumor size, and ADC value) was extremely low, below 1% across all cases. The proportion of missing categorical variables (e.g., molecular subtype and TIC pattern) was less than 2%. For the minimal missing data, the following strategies were adopted: For continuous variables (e.g., age and tumor size), median imputation was applied. T he median was preferred over the mean due to its insensitivity to outliers and its better representation of central tendency, making it more robust for potentially skewed distributions in medical data. For categorical variables (e.g., histological type), missing entries were assigned to the category of “Others” or “Unknown” and treated as an independent categorical level, to avoid information loss and imputation bias. All Missing data were imputed using statistics derived only from the training cohort. Specifically, imputation parameters (e.g., mean or median for continuous variables, mode for categorical variables) were fitted on the training set and then applied unchanged to the internal and external validation cohorts. This approach was adopted to avoid information leakage that could result from deriving imputation statistics from the entire dataset before data splitting, which would lead to overly optimistic performance estimates. All preprocessing steps requiring parameter learning from data, including missing value imputation and feature scaling, were performed exclusively on the training cohort, and the learned parameters were subsequently applied to the validation cohorts without modification. No outcome information from the validation cohorts was used during model training or preprocessing parameter estimation.

Multiparametric MRI protocol and radiologic evaluation

Multiparametric breast MRI was obtained using a 3T system (Verio, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) at both centers. The patient was examined in the prone position with both breasts suspended naturally in a dedicated breast coil. The multiparametric MRI sequences included T2-weighted (T2WI) spin-echo series with fat suppression, axial diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI). DCE-MRI included one pre-contrast and five post-contrast axial image acquisitions using gadopentetate dimeglumine (Gd-DTPA; Beijing Beilu Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) injected into the median cubital vein at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg, followed by 15 mL of normal saline at a rate of 2.0 mL/s. Apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) images derived from DWI were processed using scanner software (Supplemental Tables 1–3).

After the multiparametric MRI examination, all data were transferred to a dedicated workstation for analysis. Two radiologists with breast MR experience (10 and 27 years of diagnostic breast experience) independently reviewed and measured lesion morphology, maximum tumor diameter, time-intensity curve (TIC), and ADC mean, adhering to the 2013 Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) MR imaging vocabulary standards proposed by the American College of Radiology. The maximum tumor diameter was measured in the second stage of the DCE-MRI. For the ADC value, three replicate measurements were averaged. The two radiologists were blinded to all histopathologic data. If there was disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion.

Region-of-interest segmentation

The research workflow is illustrated in Fig. 2. Two radiologists (with 10 and 27 years of clinical experience, respectively) manually delineated the regions of interest (ROIs) using 3D Slicer software (Version 5.6.1; https://www.slicer.org/). The multimodal MRI protocols included four sequences: T2-weighted imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging (b = 1000 s/mm²), apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps, and the arterial phase of dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI acquired at the second scan. After a two-week interval, 30 cases were randomly selected from the entire cohort and re-segmented by the radiologist with 10 years of experience. All radiomics features were extracted from the two sets of segmentation contours provided by the junior radiologist to evaluate intra-observer reproducibility. Meanwhile, features derived from the two radiologists’ initial segmentations were used to assess inter-observer reproducibility. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to quantify feature reproducibility, with an ICC cutoff of 0.75 indicating good agreement. The mean intra-observer ICC was 0.82 (range, 0.76–0.92), and the mean inter-observer ICC was 0.78 (range, 0.71–0.88). These results demonstrated that the extracted radiomics features achieved favorable reproducibility.

Feature extraction

Prior to radiomic feature extraction, all magnetic resonance (MR) images were processed using a standardized preprocessing pipeline to ensure comparability and reproducibility of research findings, particularly for data acquired from two independent research centers. The preprocessing workflow mainly consists of three core steps: (1) Resampling: All images were resampled to a unified voxel spacing of 1 × 1 × 1 mm³ to standardize spatial resolution. (2) Intensity discretization: Voxel gray values were discretized with a fixed bin width of 25 to mitigate the impacts of image noise and grayscale heterogeneity. (3) Gray-level normalization: Each imaging sequence was processed independently, and gray values across the entire study cohort were standardized into z-scores with a mean of 0 and a variance of 1.

To reduce heterogeneity across multicenter data, identical 3.0-Tesla MR scanners (Siemens Verio) were used at both centers, and unified imaging acquisition protocols were strictly implemented (detailed parameters are listed in Supplementary Tables 1–3), thereby minimizing systematic device-induced data bias at the source. Common statistical harmonization methods, such as ComBat, were not adopted in this study, and this limitation has been elaborated in the Discussion section.

After image preprocessing, radiomic features were extracted using the open-source PyRadiomics library (version 3.0.1). A total of 6460 features were extracted from four imaging sequences, including T2-weighted imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging, apparent diffusion coefficient maps, and arterial-phase dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI. The extracted features comprised morphological features, first-order statistical features, and texture features derived from the gray-level co-occurrence matrix, gray-level run-length matrix, gray-level size zone matrix, neighboring gray-level difference matrix, and gray-level dependence matrix. In addition, filter-derived features based on Laplacian of Gaussian filtering, wavelet transformation, and local binary patterns were also included.

Pre-trained networks (resnet50, resnet18, and deeplabv3) in pytorch (https://pytorch.org/, version: 1.7.1) were employed for deep feature extraction [15, 16]. The pre-trained weights for three networks were obtained from ImageNet (resnet50, resnet18) and the COCO dataset (deeplabv3) [17, 18]. Using the delineated 3D lesions as the input, 2048 × 4, 512 × 4, and 2048 × 4 DL features were extracted from four sequential lesion images. Subsequently, the extracted features from the four sequences were fused into 2048, 512, and 2048 deep features via Maximum Pooling (MaxPooling), which were then concatenated to yield 4608 DL features (Supplemental Fig. 1).

We adopted a combination of these three pre-trained network architectures to extract comprehensive and complementary deep features. ResNet50, with its 50-layer structure, is adept at capturing high-level features rich in semantic information, thereby characterizing complex patterns and abstract traits within tumor regions. The shallower ResNet18 retains more low- and mid-level spatial and textural details prone to being lost in deep networks, thereby providing refined structural information. Pre-trained on the semantic segmentation dataset COCO, DeepLabV3 leverages atrous convolution to efficiently extract multi-scale contextual features, thereby enabling effective analysis of correlations between tumors and adjacent tissues, as well as intratumoral heterogeneity. Concatenation and fusion of features from the three networks yield multi-scale, multi-architectural fused feature representations that a single network cannot achieve, thereby further enriching the feature dataset for subsequent survival prediction models.

Signature construction

To address the high dimensionality of the extracted features, a Bootstrap-based feature selection strategy was employed. The procedure was as follows: (1) Bootstrap Resampling: 1000 bootstrap samples were generated from the training set. (2) Feature Ranking: For each bootstrap sample, a univariate Cox proportional hazards model was fitted for each feature, and features were ranked by the absolute value of their regression coefficients. (3) Stability Assessment: The frequency with which each feature was selected (ranked within the top k features) across all 1000 iterations was recorded. (4) Threshold Determination: Features were then sorted by their selection frequency. The final number of features to retain was determined by identifying an inflection point in the selection frequency distribution curve, yielding 1024 stable radiomics features and 512 stable deep learning features. This Bootstrap method was chosen over alternatives such as LASSO because it provides a direct measure of feature stability under data perturbations, thereby reducing the selection bias inherent in a single data split and enhancing the robustness of the final feature set for prognostic modeling.

Subsequently, the variational autoencoder-based deep survival (VAE-Based DeepSurv) network was used to train the model (Supplemental Figure 2). This model adopts an encoder-decoder architecture. The encoder maps high-dimensional input features into a low-dimensional latent space and outputs the mean and variance of latent variables. The decoder samples from this latent distribution and reconstructs the input features. The DeepSurv module serves as a survival predictor and is connected to the encoder’s latent representations. The detailed training hyperparameters are set as follows: the Adam optimizer is adopted with an initial learning rate of, a batch size of 32, a latent space dimension of 64, and a maximum of 2000 training epochs. A dropout rate of 0.2 is applied to fully connected layers for further regularization. After iterative screening, the optimal radiomics and deep learning models were identified, and their corresponding risk scores were calculated. Ultimately, a novel combined deep learning-radiomics (DLR) model was established via multivariate Cox regression analysis.

The Shapley Additive explanations (SHAP) algorithm was applied to quantify the importance of individual features within the model. As a mainstream interpretable machine learning method, SHAP can explicitly elucidate the contribution of each feature to the predictive outcomes for individual patients [19].

Combined model development

To assess whether traditional clinical and imaging features can further improve the model’s performance, we incorporated these features into radiomics and DL models. Firstly, traditional clinical and imaging features were collected and preprocessed, including (1) completing missing values with median values and (2) excluding characteristics with a variance of 0. Then, bidirectional stepwise regression and multivariable regression analyses were performed on traditional features, the radiomics model’s risk scores, and the DL models’ risk scores. Of note, during the model development phase, postoperative treatment variables were included as covariates in the multivariable regression analysis. This was performed to statistically adjust for the confounding effect of heterogeneous treatment regimens across patients, thereby enabling a more accurate estimation of the independent prognostic value of the imaging-derived features. Importantly, these treatment variables serve exclusively as statistical adjustments during model fitting and are not required as inputs for model inference in clinical deployment. Once the model is trained, the risk score for a new patient can be computed using only preoperatively available variables, such as imaging features and baseline clinicopathological characteristics. Finally, two breast cancer recurrence prediction models were obtained: (1) the DL-clinical (DLC) model, a risk score based on DL and traditional features; and (2) the DL-radiomics-clinical (DLRC) model, a risk score based on DL, radiomics, and traditional features. The performance of the constructed models was comprehensively evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and Kaplan-Meier curves.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.7.16) and the R language (version 4.3.0). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test with continuity correction, and continuous variables were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Survival differences between risk groups were evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method and the log-rank test. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The model established in this study aims to predict the time-to-event outcome of recurrence-free survival (RFS). The Cox partial likelihood function within the DeepSurv framework can automatically handle censored data, namely, patients without recurrence during follow-up, and censored subjects remain included in the risk set until their censoring time. The individualized 3-year recurrence probability for each patient was calculated from the model outputs. Specifically, the individual risk score was combined with the baseline cumulative hazard function estimated using the Breslow method to derive the survival function, thereby obtaining the 3-year recurrence risk. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to evaluate the model’s discriminative ability at this specific time point. The cumulative-dynamic method (Heagerty et al., 2000) was used to calculate the time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) for predicting 3-year recurrence-free survival. This method defines sensitivity and specificity based on the cumulative incidence of events at the target time point (year 3) to address data censoring. This method has been widely adopted in prognostic radiomics and deep learning research. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox regression models was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and graphical inspection of scaled Schoenfeld residual plots. If violations of the proportional hazards assumption were detected, alternative modeling strategies such as time-varying coefficients or landmark analysis would be considered.

Calibration curves were used to assess each model’s calibration across the three sets. The DCA curve was used to evaluate the model’s clinical value by calculating the net benefit within the threshold probability range. Patients in each set were divided into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score in the training set. Using the log-rank test, the Kaplan-Meier method was used to compare survival probabilities between patients in the high- and low-risk groups. The Sankey plot was used to explore the relationship between DL features, radiomics features, and clinical features of interest. Feature visualization using SHAP graphs highlights the importance of the features that make up DL and radiomics models. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To evaluate whether the DLRC model significantly outperformed the clinical model, a Bootstrap paired test was performed for time-dependent AUC (cumulative/dynamic method, 36-month time point, 2000 bootstrap resamples). The continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) were also calculated to quantify improvements in risk reclassification and overall discrimination. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In addition, for all three cohorts in this study, Harrell’s concordance index (C‑index) was calculated for each model across the entire follow‑up period. For each cohort, the C‑index was estimated using the continuous model risk scores and complete recurrence‑free survival follow‑up time via the `concordance` function in the R `survival` package, with Harrell weighting specified by `timewt = “n"`. Since higher scores correspond to greater recurrence risk and shorter predicted recurrence‑free survival time, the argument `reverse = TRUE` was applied. The 95% confidence intervals were obtained through 2000 patient‑level bootstrap resamples, and identical resampling indices were reused across different models within the same cohort.

Results

Patient characteristics

Patients were divided into internal and external datasets based on institution, with the two datasets comprising 428 and 196 patients, respectively. In the internal dataset, 428 patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 299, mean age, 52.5 ± 12.2 years) and an internal validation set (n = 129, mean age, 53.2 ± 11.5 years). Baseline information was analyzed for both the training and internal validation sets, and there were no statistically significant differences in any clinical baseline characteristics between the two groups (P > 0.05, Tables 1 and 2). At the last follow-up, 104 patients demonstrated disease recurrence (training set, n = 46; internal validation set, n = 20; external validation set, n = 38). As shown in Fig. 3d-e, the SHAP algorithm was employed to interpret the DL model. Deep learning neural networks are often considered black boxes because they approximate complex mathematical functions without providing interpretable insights into their internal mechanisms. Consequently, feature visualization is essential for uncovering the importance of individual features and illustrating their impact on the model’s predictions.

Radiomics and DL models were independent predictors of breast cancer recurrence through bidirectional stepwise and multivariable regression analyses. Therefore, we evaluated the DLR model, which exhibited enhanced predictive performance. The DLR model achieved AUCs of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.74–0.90), 0.83 (95% CI: 0.73–0.93), and 0.67 (95% CI: 0.55–0.80) for predicting 3-year recurrence-free survival (RFS) in the training, internal validation, and external validation sets, respectively (Table 2, Supplemental Figure 3). To further improve performance, we integrated clinical and imaging features to construct the DLC and DLRC models using two risk scores and 10 clinicopathologic features (Table 3; feature details in the supplemental materials).

The DLC model outperformed the DL model alone. The DLRC model achieved the best performance on the training and internal validation sets for predicting 3-year recurrence-free survival. Its predictive ability remained discernible but was attenuated in the external validation set, with time-dependent AUCs of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.91–0.99), 0.89 (95% CI: 0.79–0.99), and 0.79 (95% CI: 0.67–0.91) across the respective datasets (Fig. 4a-c, solid lines). Supplementary 5-year prediction results are also displayed in Fig. 4 but were not the primary focus of this study. The calibration curves demonstrated good visual agreement between the DLRC model’s predictions and the actual recurrence outcomes (Fig. 4d-f). Quantitative calibration metrics are summarized in Supplementary Table 7. The Brier scores for the DLRC model were 0.044 (training), 0.063 (internal validation), and 0.108 (external validation), all substantially lower than those of the clinical model (0.047, 0.055, and 0.202, respectively). The calibration slopes were 1.000 (training), 0.480 (internal validation), and 0.461 (external validation), and the calibration intercepts were − 0.001, − 1.162, and − 1.134, respectively. The slopes below 1.0 in the validation sets indicate some degree of probability compression. However, the Brier scores confirm acceptable overall calibration, particularly in the external cohort, where the DLRC model substantially outperformed the clinical model. Furthermore, the DL, radiomics, and clinical features incorporated in the model showed no significant interdependence (Fig. 4g-i).

Across the entire follow-up period, Harrell’s C-index showed that the DLRC model achieved a C-index of 0.914 (95% CI: 0.872–0.952) in the training cohort, 0.872 (95% CI: 0.789–0.946) in the internal validation cohort, and 0.765 (95% CI: 0.680–0.840) in the external validation cohort (Supplementary Table 4). In the external validation cohort, the corresponding C-indices were 0.608 (95% CI: 0.510–0.701) for the clinical model, 0.650 (95% CI: 0.549–0.744) for the DL model, 0.691 (95% CI: 0.600–0.775) for the DLR model, and 0.729 (95% CI: 0.637–0.815) for the DLC model, confirming that the full multimodal DLRC model provided the highest overall discrimination (Supplementary Table 4).

To further evaluate the clinical utility of the DLRC model, we calculated the sensitivity, specificity, and negative predictive value (NPV) at the optimal Youden index threshold (0.134) in the external validation cohort. The model achieved a sensitivity of 79.2%, specificity of 70.4%, and NPV of 96.0%, indicating a strong ability to rule out low-risk patients.

The Bootstrap paired test for time-dependent AUC demonstrated that the DLRC model significantly outperformed the clinical model in the external validation cohort (ΔAUC = 0.166, Z = 3.51, P = 0.0013, 95% CI: [0.069, 0.261]). Furthermore, the continuous NRI was 0.667 (P = 0.002), and the IDI was 0.199 (P < 0.001) in the external validation cohort, confirming significant improvements in risk reclassification and overall discrimination (Supplementary Table 5).

An ablation-style comparison across models further illustrates the incremental value of each component. In the external cohort, the clinical model achieved an AUC of 0.628, the DLR model (combining radiomics and deep learning features) achieved 0.674, the DLC model (adding clinicopathological features) achieved 0.757, and the final DLRC model achieved 0.788. A consistent pattern was observed in overall prediction error: the Brier scores for the clinical, DLR, DLC, and DLRC models were 0.1134, 0.1123, 0.0898, and 0.0899, respectively. Although the DLC and DLRC models yielded very similar Brier scores, the DLRC model achieved the highest external AUC. The bootstrap paired test further confirmed that the DLRC model significantly outperformed the clinical model (ΔAUC = 0.1601, P = 0.0013, 95% CI: 0.0637–0.2614), demonstrating that the full multimodal fusion provided genuine improvement in discriminative ability beyond clinical factors alone.

Association between predicted risk and recurrence-free survival

Patients were separated into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score of the DLRC model. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed statistically significant differences in RFS between these groups across all cohorts (Fig. 5). These findings suggested that the DLRC model effectively stratified breast cancer patients for recurrence risk.

Clinical utility of the DLRC model

Decision curve analysis (DCA) confirmed that the deep learning nomogram risk stratification model has superior clinical application value. The DLRC model achieved a higher net benefit than the default strategies across a threshold probability range of approximately 5%–45% in the training cohort. Within the clinically critical low-threshold range of 5%–20% (where clinicians prefer to avoid missing high-risk patients), the deep learning nomogram risk stratification model provided a significantly higher net benefit than the treat-all, treat-none, and single clinical model strategies (Fig. 6). This indicates that using this model can lead to more favorable clinical decision-making outcomes compared with formulating management plans based solely on traditional clinical indicators. Although the clinical model offered some improvement over standalone models in the training and internal validation sets, its net benefit substantially decreased in the external validation set, indicating limited generalizability and robustness.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a deep learning (DL) model for breast cancer recurrence prediction that demonstrated strong performance across the training, internal validation, and external validation sets. By integrating DL with radiomics and clinicopathological features, the combined DLRC model exhibited enhanced performance in the internal cohorts. The model maintained a moderate level of discriminatory ability in an independent external cohort, though with a noticeable decrease in accuracy, highlighting the challenge of cross-institutional generalization. The performance of the DLRC model suggests that DL, radiomics, and clinicopathological features may potentially provide information about different aspects of the tumor, thereby improving recurrence prediction and facilitating personalized clinical treatment.

In recent years, the role of DL in breast cancer recurrence prediction has received increasing attention. Yao et al. [20] constructed a new multimodal DL model by using a Residual Network (ResNet) as an encoder, and they found the model possessed better performance in predicting breast cancer recurrence and metastasis. Howard et al. [21] established a combined model based on histopathologic images and clinical factors using a DL network for identifying breast cancer patients with high recurrence scores. However, these studies used pathological images rather than MRI images, which may offer superior predictive value for breast cancer recurrence due to their dynamic and noninvasive tumor assessment capabilities. Yu et al. [22] employed an MRI Radiomics DeepSurv Net (RDeepNet) model based on radiomics features and the DeepSurv network to predict the risk of breast cancer recurrence, and the results showed that the model had good performance in predicting breast cancer recurrence. Comes et al. [23] diverged from radiomics-based approaches by using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to extract DL features for predicting three-year breast cancer recurrence. However, their model employed an SVM classifier rather than a DL-based method, potentially limiting its robustness and stability [24, 25]. Shah et al. [26] employed a dual‑view deep learning model (EfficientViewNet) that separately analyzes craniocaudal and mediolateral oblique mammographic views to enhance breast cancer detection, and the results showed that the model achieved an F1 score and recall of 0.99, demonstrating the power of view‑specific deep learning. Shah et al. [27] Further employed an ensemble deep learning approach integrating EfficientNet, AlexNet, ResNet, and DenseNet for mammogram classification, and the results showed that the ensemble outperformed any single model with an accuracy of 94.6% and an AUC of 98.0%, illustrating the growing trend of leveraging multiple deep architectures for improved diagnostic performance. More recently, Zhang et al. [28] proposed a Vision Transformer (ViT) model on DCE-MRI for HER2 subclassification, in which the derived score was an independent prognostic factor for overall survival; nevertheless, its primary focus is molecular subtyping rather than recurrence, and it uses a classification-then-Cox pipeline instead of the end-to-end VAE-DeepSurv framework adopted here. Our study integrates both DL and radiomics features within a VAE-based DeepSurv architecture, combining the strengths of automated feature learning and survival analysis.

In the present study, regions‑of‑interest (ROIs) were manually segmented by experienced radiologists. Considering the common constraints of limited time and shortage of specialized radiologists in routine clinical practice, we acknowledge that this workflow may hinder immediate clinical translation of the framework proposed in this study. Automatic or semi‑automatic segmentation approaches (e.g., U‑Net, nnU‑Net) have demonstrated promising performance for lesion delineation on breast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which can reduce operator dependence and shorten processing time. Nevertheless, we note that published benchmark studies have reported Dice similarity coefficients generally above 0.80 for automated breast MRI segmentation [29].

However, to what extent segmentation discrepancies at this level alter downstream radiomic features and risk scores largely depends on the feature set and model architecture employed. Deep‑learning features extracted from pre‑trained convolutional networks possess hierarchical and spatially‑invariant representation properties, rendering them more robust to minor segmentation deviations. In contrast, boundary‑sensitive radiomic features (e.g., morphological descriptors and gray‑level co‑occurrence matrix‑based texture features) are more susceptible to segmentation bias. Since no direct head‑to‑head comparison was performed within our study cohort, we cannot precisely quantify its impact on model performance. Therefore, future studies are warranted to systematically evaluate how replacement of manual segmentation with automated segmentation affects the predictive performance and reproducibility of deep‑learning radiomic models.

Likewise, although both centers in this study adopted identical 3.0T MRI scanners (Siemens Verio) and standardized acquisition protocols, we recognize that this controlled research setting cannot reflect real-world clinical scenarios characterized by multi-vendor equipment and heterogeneous scanning protocols. Variations in magnetic field strength, gradient performance, coil design and sequence parameters can induce systematic shifts in the distributions of image signal intensities, textures and deep features, potentially deteriorating model performance. To tackle this challenge, multicenter collaborations covering multiple MRI vendors are warranted to advance the standardization of scanning protocols. Meanwhile, domain adaptation or image harmonization techniques — including ComBat, cycle-consistent generative adversarial networks (CycleGAN), and histogram matching — should be incorporated to improve model generalizability across different scanners. Prospective external validation across multiple medical institutions and diverse scanner platforms is mandatory prior to the clinical deployment of the proposed model.

A critical consideration in high-dimensional radiomics and deep learning studies is the risk of overfitting, particularly when the number of features exceeds the number of events. In our study, the training set contained 46 recurrence events, and the final feature pool entering the VAE-DeepSurv model comprised 1536 features (1024 radiomics + 512 deep learning). However, several layers of protection were implemented to mitigate overfitting. First, the Bootstrap-based feature selection retained only features that demonstrated stable associations with recurrence across 1000 resampling iterations, effectively filtering out noise-driven features. Second, the VAE architecture intrinsically regularizes the model through its unsupervised reconstruction loss and KL divergence penalty, which constrain the latent space and prevent the model from fitting idiosyncratic patterns. Third, dropout (rate 0.2) and early stopping (patience of 100 epochs) further limited the model’s ability to memorize the training data. The combined effect of these measures reduces the effective degrees of freedom substantially below the nominal feature count. The strongest evidence against overfitting is the model’s performance in the independent external validation cohort (AUC = 0.788, Brier = 0.108), which significantly surpassed the clinical model (bootstrap paired test P = 0.0013). If the model were severely overfitted, external performance would likely deteriorate to near-random levels. The sustained discriminative ability across institutions supports the generalizability of the learned representations.

Compared to traditional radiomics methods, deep learning (DL) improves image processing efficiency by automatically learning predictive features and producing class probability vectors [30]. In this study, we developed a DL model based on the VAE-DeepSurv architecture, which demonstrated stable predictive performance. DeepSurv enables automatic feature learning from clinical data while handling censored factors [31–33], and the VAE component further enhances generalization by reducing overfitting through its unsupervised loss and improving adaptability to nonlinear clinical variables. Given the complexity of breast cancer, we built a multi-sequence DeepSurv model that captured multidimensional deep features beyond a single-sequence approach. Furthermore, integrating DL features with radiomics and clinicopathological features allowed comprehensive characterization of tumor heterogeneity, thereby improving recurrence prediction. To date, only Guo et al. [10] have combined radiomics and DL features (DLR) for breast cancer recurrence prediction, but their study included only DCE-MRI and did not incorporate clinicopathological factors.

In current clinical practice, treatments for breast cancer are usually selected based on molecular subtypes. Endocrine therapy is often chosen for patients with HR-positive disease. In contrast, HER2-targeted therapy is recommended for HER2-positive patients, and clinicians will then decide whether to accept other treatments, such as chemotherapy, based on the patient’s clinicopathological features, genetic testing, and other information [34, 35]. However, there is still no appropriate tool to guide the escalation or de-escalation of treatment, so high-risk patients are not escalated, and low-risk patients are overtreated [36, 37]. In such cases, the risk of tumor recurrence must first be assessed to evaluate the cost-benefit ratio adequately. This study developed a predictive model for breast cancer recurrence using multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging(mp-MRI), employing a multimodal analytical framework that integrates deep learning networks, radiomic features, and clinicopathological data. The model can effectively characterize tumor heterogeneity and enable noninvasive evaluation of recurrence risk. In routine clinical practice, the DLRC model could be integrated into the workflow following standard preoperative breast MRI acquisition. After tumor segmentation on MRI images, radiomics and deep learning features are automatically extracted and combined with routinely available clinicopathological variables to generate an individualized 3-year recurrence risk estimate, which may assist clinicians in identifying patients who might benefit from intensified surveillance, multidisciplinary discussion, or individualized adjuvant treatment strategies. Nevertheless, it should be noted that this study only provides a risk stratification tool and cannot serve as a direct basis for formulating therapeutic regimens. Additional steps remain necessary before clinical deployment, including prospective multicenter validation, evaluation of inter-reader variability and segmentation variability, assessment across different scanner vendors and imaging protocols, local calibration or recalibration, decision-curve and clinical impact analyses, workflow integration testing, and evaluation of whether model-guided decisions improve patient outcomes. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting the clinical translational value of this study’s findings.

The Bootstrap paired test for time-dependent AUC further confirmed the incremental predictive value of the DLRC model over the clinical model. In the external validation cohort, the DLRC model significantly outperformed the clinical model (ΔAUC = 0.166, Z = 3.51, P = 0.0013, 95% CI: [0.069, 0.261]), providing direct statistical evidence that the integration of multimodal imaging features yields a genuine improvement in discriminative performance beyond clinical factors alone. Additionally, the continuous NRI (0.667, P = 0.002) and IDI (0.199, P < 0.001) in the external validation cohort confirmed that the DLRC model significantly improved risk reclassification and overall discrimination. These findings, together with the multivariate Cox regression results showing that both deep learning-derived and radiomics-derived risk scores remained independent prognostic factors after adjustment for all clinical characteristics (P < 0.05), and the decision curve analysis demonstrating higher net clinical benefit across clinically relevant threshold ranges, collectively establish that the DLRC model possesses statistically validated incremental predictive value compared with the clinical-only model.

We conducted a systematic comparison of performance decline across all models (Supplementary Table 6). The AUC of the DLRC model decreased from 0.946 in the training cohort to 0.788 in the external validation cohort, corresponding to an absolute reduction of 0.158. By comparison, the AUC decreased by 0.316 for the clinical model (from 0.944 to 0.628) and by 0.140 for the DLR model (from 0.814 to 0.674). Despite thisreduction, the DLRC model a significantly higher AUC than the clinical model in the external cohort (0.788 vs. 0.628; bootstrap paired test, P = 0.0013). It also yielded a lower Brier score (0.108 vs. 0.202), indicating greater overall accuracy of the predicted probabilities.

The performance decline likely reflects both statistical and clinical heterogeneity. Statistically, the training set contained only 46 recurrence events, which may introduce optimism bias in training performance estimates. Clinically, the external cohort differed substantially from the development cohort in disease spectrum (higher lymph node metastasis: 42.9% vs. 14.0%; more endocrine therapy: 59.7% vs. 19.4%) and molecular subtype distribution (Luminal B: 61.7% vs. 55.2%). The calibration slope decreased from 1.000 to 0.461 and the calibration intercept was − 1.134, confirming miscalibration. Besides the aforementioned sources of heterogeneity at the statistical and clinical levels, several other factors may account for the observed deterioration in model performance. First, even with identical scanner models and scanning protocols, inter‑center differences in image quality and signal‑to‑noise ratio persist, which can induce subtle distribution shifts in radiomic and deep‑learning features. Second, unmeasured confounding factors may exist between the two study populations, such as genetic ancestry, lifestyle, and comorbidity profiles. These factors can influence recurrence risk independently of the included predictive variables.

No model recalibration or domain adaptation was applied to the external cohort prior to reporting the primary results; this was an intentional choice to preserve the independence of external validation and assess the raw transportability of the model. We acknowledge that local recalibration would likely improve calibration and clinical utility in new populations. Notably, although no explicit statistical harmonization (e.g., ComBat) was performed, the use of identical 3T MRI scanners (Siemens Verio) and harmonized acquisition protocols at both centers already eliminated the most prominent sources of technical variability. Nevertheless, the observed decline underscores the challenge of cross-institutional generalization, and future work should prioritize prospective, multicenter validation with multi-vendor MRI platforms and diverse populations, incorporating ComBat harmonization and local recalibration to further improve feature stability and generalizability.

SHAP analysis revealed that among the top 20 most influential deep learning features, approximately 55% were derived from DCE-MRI, followed by DWI/ADC (30%) and T2WI (15%). This predominance of DCE-MRI-derived features is biologically plausible, as DCE-MRI captures tumor angiogenesis and vascular permeability—hallmark processes driving recurrence—while DWI/ADC reflects cellular density and membrane integrity, with restricted diffusion indicating aggressive phenotypes. These imaging features may indirectly reflect biological processes such as intratumoral heterogeneity, angiogenesis, and invasive growth patterns. However, without direct pathological or genomic correlation, the biological meaning of individual high-dimensional features remains limited. We therefore present these SHAP results as hypothesis-generating findings, and future studies integrating imaging with histopathology and molecular data are needed to validate their biological interpretation.

Several limitations merit consideration in our research. Firstly, this retrospective study with a predominantly Chinese population may limit generalizability; larger multi-ethnic prospective studies are needed. Secondly, this study only focused on recurrence‑free survival and did not consider other important oncological endpoints such as overall survival and disease‑specific survival. Future studies should incorporate more endpoints and more comprehensive evaluation metrics. Thirdly, imaging features were extracted exclusively from the intratumoral region. Subsequent analyses should include peritumoral regions to achieve a more comprehensive assessment. The Fourth, the number of recurrence events was relatively limited. Although we employed feature dimensionality reduction, a regularized deep learning model, and independent external validation to enhance model stability, this limitation still constrains the exploration of the ultra‑high‑dimensional imaging feature space. The Fifth, no explicit statistical harmonization or model recalibration was applied to the external cohort. While this approach preserves the independence of the validation set, local recalibration may be necessary before clinical deployment. Furthermore, both centers used 3‑Tesla MRI scanners from the same vendor. Therefore, prospective multicenter validation studies involving multiple MRI vendors and more diverse patient populations are essential. The Sixth, the original analysis did not fully report the actual results of the Schoenfeld residuals test; therefore, we could not definitively confirm whether the proportional hazards assumption holds. Future studies should include comparisons with a wider range of model types to further improve the robustness, generalizability, and reproducibility of deep learning combined with radiomics models.

Conclusion

The proposed model integrating breast MRI, radiomic features, and deep-learning features showed promise for predicting breast cancer recurrence and may support individualized risk stratification. However, prospective multicenter validation across MRI systems from multiple vendors and in larger, more diverse patient populations is required to confirm the model’s robustness, generalizability, and clinical utility.
