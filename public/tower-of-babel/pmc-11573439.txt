Local wound care management for pyoderma gangrenosum
Authors: Adeeb Haroon, Jordan Gillespie, Jaclyn Roland‐McGowan, Riyad N H Seervai, Lisa J Gould, Valentina Dini, Alex G Ortega‐Loayza
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare, painful neutrophilic dermatosis characterized by rapidly progressing skin ulcers. Despite the importance of local wound care in managing PG, there is no consensus or evidence‐based guidelines. This systematic review aimed to investigate local wound care strategies for PG. A comprehensive search of Embase, MEDLINE, and the Cochrane Library yielded 1213 references, from which 269 studies were included, covering 351 patients. The most reported treatment methods included sharp debridement (11%), topical corticosteroids (27%) and non‐adherent dressings (12%). However, no clear correlation between these treatments and healing outcomes was found likely due to confounding factors such as varied wound sizes, superinfection and inconsistent reporting. Additionally, directed wound care regimens have not been able to show statistical significance for healing outcomes. Our study describes the current local wound care landscape and underscores a critical gap in the current literature regarding standardized treatment protocols for PG.

Dear Editor,

Pyoderma gangrenosum (PG) is a rare neutrophilic dermatosis of the skin characterized by rapidly progressive and painful skin ulcers. Meticulous attention to wound care is crucial for optimizing ulcer healing; however, no evidence‐based or expert consensus guidelines have been established to aid clinicians.

To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review to investigate local wound care management for PG. A comprehensive literature search was performed of Embase, MEDLINE and the Cochrane Library. Studies were included if they: (1) focused on local wound care for PG, (2) included human participants, (3) were written in English and (4) were published in peer‐reviewed journals (Figure S1). Data extraction was performed using a standardized form, capturing information on study design (reference, study design), participant characteristics (age, sex), disease characteristics (PG subtype, phase of disease, ulcer location, number of ulcers, size of ulcer), intervention details (biopsy, culture, systemic interventions, local interventions) and relevant outcomes (response, time to healing, recurrence, complications).

Our search yielded 1213 references from which 269 studies were included, yielding a 351‐patient cohort. The gender distribution was 203 females (59.4%) and 139 males (40.6%). The location of PG ulcer was reported for 336 patients (95.7%). The most common location was the lower extremity (n = 165) (Table S1). The most reported method for tissue management was sharp debridement by 11% (n = 39). For local inflammation, 27% (n = 95) used topical corticosteroids. For infection, 10% (n = 35) used topical antiseptics. Regarding moisture maintenance, non‐adherent dressings were most frequently used (n = 41, 12%). For epithelial advancement, 26% (n = 90) utilized skin grafting and 20% (n = 69) reported the use of negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) (Table 1).

The primary finding of our study is the absence of a clear correlation between the various treatments for PG and healing outcomes. This can be attributed to several confounding factors including wound size, superinfection and considerably varied reporting methods, all of which can overshadow the effects of the treatments themselves. These factors complicate the assessment of treatment efficacy highlighting the challenge of managing PG in clinical practice. Additionally, directed wound care regimens have not been able to show statistical significance for healing outcomes. Still, they can provide a structured wound care framework that can be tailored to individual patients. 1 , 2 A promising framework is the PG‐TIME approach, which uses the two distinct phases of PG, the inflammatory phase and the healing phase, and the TIME principles (Tissue, Infection and Inflammation, Moisture and Epithelial advancement) to guide treatment. 1

During the inflammatory phase, halting inflammation, preventing infection and optimizing moisture balance are crucial. Locally, this is usually achieved through the use of high‐potency steroids. Intralesional corticosteroids and non‐steroidal agents were also reported to have varying efficacy and tolerability. Infection prevention and optimal moisture balance can be achieved through modern wound dressings. 2 In the healing phase, the focus transitions to tissue management, moisture balance and facilitating epithelial advancement. Effective methods that encourage the formation of granulation tissue and epithelialization are NPWT, soft debridement and skin grafting. 3 During the healing phase, dressings now must preserve moisture. This can be achieved with petrolatum‐impregnated gauze, or hydrogel‐based dressings based on exudate level. Notably, compression therapy can be used during both phases to reduce inflammation and oedema. 4 , 5

CONCLUSION

Our study underscores a critical gap in the current literature regarding standardized treatment protocols for PG. The studies we analysed exhibited considerable heterogeneity in design, patient populations and outcome measures. This variability, coupled with a reliance on observational studies and case reports, limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions. There are indications that frameworks like PG‐TIME may offer clinical benefits, but these need to be validated through well‐designed trials. Addressing these gaps will pave the way for more consistent and effective treatment strategies, ultimately improving outcomes for patients with PG.
