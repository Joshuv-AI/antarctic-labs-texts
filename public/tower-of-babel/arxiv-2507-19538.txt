Rural School Bus Routing and Scheduling
Prabhat Hegde, Vikrant Vaze

Abstract
Long school bus rides adversely affect student performance and well-being. Rural school bus rides are particularly long, incentivizing parents to drive their children to school rather than to opt for the school bus. This in turn exacerbates the traffic congestion around schools, further compounding the problem of long bus rides, creating a vicious cycle. It also results in underutilized school buses and higher bus operating costs per rider. To address these challenges, this paper focuses on the design of rural school bus routes and schedules, a particularly challenging problem due to its unique operational complexities, including mixed loading and irregular road networks. We formalize a rural school bus routing and scheduling model that tackles these complexities while minimizing the total bus ride time of students. We develop an original road network-aware cluster-then-route heuristic that leverages our problem formulation to produce high-quality solutions and demonstrate its superior performance through extensive ablation studies. For real-world case studies, our approach outperforms status quo solutions by reducing the bus ride times of students by 37-39%. Our solutions also make the school bus more attractive, helping address both the underutilization of school buses and the prevalence of private commutes. Our routing and scheduling approach can improve school bus use by 17-19% and reduce car trips that induce congestion near schools by 12-17%. Many rural school districts share the operational characteristics modeled in this study, including long bus rides, high operational expenditures, mixed loading, and a high proportion of car-based school commutes, suggesting the broad applicability of our approach. Ultimately, by reducing student travel times, increasing school bus utilization, and alleviating congestion near schools, our approach enables rural school district planners to address transportation-related barriers to student performance and well-being.

Introduction

Safe and reliable transportation to and from school is fundamental for educational access. The iconic yellow school bus serves as the backbone of this critical service in the United States, ensuring that distance and geography do not become barriers to learning opportunities. Almost half a million yellow school buses transport more than 25 million students (
\sim
55% of the US K-12 population) on a daily basis
(
Burgoyne-Allen and Schiess 2017
)
. The US school bus fleet, which is more than twice the combined size of all other forms of US mass transit (including bus, rail, and airline transportation), plays a central role in both the education and transportation sectors. However, despite this massive scale and importance, the operational decision-making and financing of school buses in the US is done by individual school districts, making it challenging to develop unified approaches for school bus planning.

Almost half of all US school districts, representing approximately 30% of all schools and 20% of all schoolchildren, are classified as rural
(
Showalter et al. 2019
)
. A rural service environment is different from an urban one due to lower population densities, longer bus routes, fewer schools per district, fewer riders per bus stop, more stops per route, more irregular road network structures, more homogeneous fleets, fewer or no one-way streets, fewer or no public transportation options to commute to school, structurally different road congestion patterns, and lower availability of alternative roads
(
Thangiah et al. 2008
)
. Urban school bus routing methods often focus on optimizing routes by minimizing the number of buses used within a dense, well-connected grid but do not adequately address the unique challenges posed by rural service environments. Rural districts require routing and scheduling strategies that account for longer distances, varied road conditions, and the need to balance operational efficiency with the well-being of students who face extended travel times. This paper aims to address these issues and makes the following contributions.

1.

We develop a new mixed-integer linear optimization (MILO) model that, for a given number of school buses, minimizes the total bus ride time of students in a mixed-loading setting. A mixed-loading setting is one in which students from different schools can ride the same bus on the same trip. Our mathematical formulation is informed by analyses of data from an extensive survey of parents in our partner school district and broader evidence showing that many rural school districts share similar characteristics. Our integrated model formulation effectively addresses the three previously formalized subproblems of the school bus routing and scheduling problem: bus stop assignment, bus route generation, and school bus scheduling.

2.

We design an original cluster-then-route heuristic that solves the MILO model in reasonable computational time. First, in the clustering step, we propose a new
Hybrid Road network-Aware Constrained
k
-means Clustering
(
HRACkC
– may be pronounced as “
etch rack
”) approach that assigns buses to service regions that contain candidate bus stops and students. Then, in the routing step, for each service region, we propose an original
Student Assignment, Stop Selection, Sequencing and Scheduling
(
SAS4
) approach to determine the student-to-stop assignment, bus routes, and bus schedules.

3.

In addition to improving bus routes, we address the problems of underutilized buses and localized congestion caused by car commutes in the vicinity of schools by proposing an overall modeling framework that includes two interconnected procedures. The aforementioned cluster-then-route approach solves our MILO model to generate bus routes and schedules for a given set of school bus-using students and reduces the total bus ride time of those students. Shorter bus commutes can incentivize additional students to use the school buses, thus relieving congestion in the vicinity of schools by reducing car commutes, but also requiring re-optimization of school bus routes. Our iterative modeling and computational framework solves the resulting fixed-point problem that captures these interdependencies and generates bus routes, schedules, travel times, and students’ travel mode choices that are in equilibrium with each other.

4.

We perform extensive computational experiments for two school districts in rural New England to evaluate the tractability and performance of our modeling and computational framework. Our cluster-then-route approach solves the MILO model in reasonable runtimes, spanning 20-25 hours. From a practical standpoint, our solutions lead to 37-39% savings in total bus ride time of students compared to the status quo in these two school districts. Moreover, we demonstrate the additional benefits due to the travel mode shift, driven by better routing and scheduling that reduces student travel times. Our approach can transport 17-19% more students while maintaining the same average bus ride time reduction per student. Furthermore, we show a 12-17% congestion reduction in the vicinity of schools by reducing car commutes to school.

1.1
Distinctive features of rural school districts

Rural school districts have different socioeconomic and demographic compositions, which influence their operational planning
(
Greenough and Nelson 2015
)
. However, in terms of school bus travel, research has identified the following common operational challenges and policy concerns related to student well-being.

•

Longer school bus commutes
: Rural school bus commutes are longer than their urban counterparts and often exceed 30 minutes. Students with longer school bus travel times tend to skip breakfast and report lower grades, poorer levels of fitness, fewer social activities, and worse study habits
(
Lutz, Rakowska, and Adams 2024
,
Gottfried, Ozuna, and
Kirksey 2021
)
. Longer commutes disproportionately burden students in lower income households and aggravate absenteeism
(
Gottfried, Ozuna, and
Kirksey 2021
)
. In addition, rural school districts are likely to have more unpaved and hilly roads, are less likely to employ full-time bus superintendents, and have more frequent inclement weather disruptions
(
Howley 2001
,
Zars 1998
,
Spence 2000
)
. Exposure to these characteristics, in addition to longer commutes, compounds the various risks for students riding rural school buses
(
Howley 2001
,
Zars 1998
,
Spence 2000
)
.

•

Higher operating expenses
: Bus operating costs per student are about 40% higher in rural school districts than in urban school districts. The marginal cost of accommodating an additional student in a rural school district is also about 2.5 times higher than in an urban school district. An additional mile traveled by bus in a rural school district costs about 10 times more than in urban school districts
(
Ellegood, Riley, and Berg 2024
)
. Despite these high costs, buses in rural school districts are often underutilized
(
Stanley and Stanley 2021
)
. This empirical evidence shows that the most cost-effective strategy for rural school districts is to plan better routes without increasing the number of school buses
(
Ellegood, Riley, and Berg 2024
)
.

•

Mixed loading
: Rural school buses are more likely to have mixed loading
(
Ellegood, Campbell, and North 2015
)
. Rural school districts in the US have undergone considerable consolidation in the past century, creating larger districts with fewer schools and a higher enrollment per school
(
Bard, Gardener, and Wieland 2006
)
. Mixed loading is more beneficial for geographically larger school districts, when a large percentage of bus stops are shared by students of different schools, and when the school locations are close together
(
Ellegood, Campbell, and North 2015
)
, which makes it widely applicable in rural school districts. Mixing elementary-school children with middle- and high-school children on buses can have adverse effects, including bullying, exposure to age-inappropriate content, and insufficient supervision of younger children in a combined age-group bus journey.

•

Higher proportion of car commutes
: Approximately 50% of school-age children in the US commute to school by car, and likely even higher in rural school districts due to the higher rate of car ownership
(
Lidbe et al. 2020
)
. When schools are close together, have similar start times and a sparse road network surrounding them, car drop-offs cause congestion near schools. This congestion is a major concern because it
(i)
disproportionately increases the commute time of school buses that visit multiple schools, reducing the sleep time of children,
(ii)
creates a safety concern while loading and unloading cars during rush hour, and
(iii)
exposes students to emissions.

Thus, a rural school bus routing and scheduling problem (SBRSP) involves mixed loading with a fixed number of school buses and an emphasis on minimizing total bus ride times of students, improving the utilization of school buses, and reducing congestion near schools. Figure
1
shows that most of the school districts in the US states of New Hampshire and Vermont are rural, with the majority having fewer than five schools. A smaller school count supports a smaller fixed school bus fleet. In many of these districts, the schools are in close proximity – a condition under which mixed loading is particularly effective
(
Ellegood, Campbell, and North 2015
)
. This proximity also contributes to localized congestion near schools. These characteristics make most of these school districts well-positioned to benefit from our rural SBRSP model.

Figure 2:
School bus commuters and private commuters’ agreement on the importance of five factors in school transportation decisions: duration, punctuality, student behavior, safety, and comfort.

The higher proportion of car commutes in rural school districts makes morning transportation particularly challenging. School start times align with parent work schedules, creating concentrated traffic congestion as parents drop children off before continuing to their workplaces. In contrast, afternoon dismissal typically occurs before the parents’ workday ends, reducing the congestion burden. In addition, morning routes operate under stricter time constraints, as schools require students to arrive before instruction begins, while afternoon commutes are more flexible. These challenges make optimizing morning bus routes more critical and impactful for rural school districts. Therefore, we focus specifically on the morning commute problem.

Some studies consider staggering school start times in combination with designing school bus routes and schedules
(
Bertsimas, Delarue, and
Martin 2019
)
, often due to the positive impact on health and educational outcomes of later school start times on high school students
(
Au et al. 2014
)
. However, many school districts face insurmountable logistical challenges that prevent the implementation of later or staggered start times
(
Kirby, Maggi, and D’Angiulli 2011
,
Owens et al. 2014
)
. Facing similar logistical barriers, our partner school districts approached us for assistance with their operational challenges. We have collaborated closely on identifying detailed problem statements, collecting datasets, and providing actionable insights. Thus, our rural school bus routing and scheduling model is motivated by the practical requirements of our partner school districts, the existing literature (described in Section
2
), and our own data collection in one of our partner school districts through parent surveys (Section
1.2
) and traffic counts (Section
1.3
).

1.2
Insights from Parent Survey

The aforementioned characteristics of rural school districts raise concerns that influence parents’ decision about whether or not to opt for the school bus for their child. Many rural school districts face additional challenges such as unpaved and hilly roads that reduce riding comfort, lack of school bus monitors that affects safety, mixing of students of various age groups contributing to bullying, long bus rides and delays in getting to school on time. To understand the impact of these concerns, we partnered with a school district that had administered a survey to families in their school district. This survey was designed to assess which of these factors significantly influence parents’ decisions to opt for the school bus for their children. In a sample of 790 parents and guardians, approximately 41% of the respondents regularly used the school bus, another 23% used it occasionally, while the remaining respondents did not use the school bus. As seen in Figure
2
, parents of school bus commuters (children who regularly take the school bus) and parents of private commuters (children who sometimes or never use the school bus), both overwhelmingly agreed that the duration of the school bus journey greatly affects the decision about whether their child takes the bus. In fact, for both groups, the duration of the school bus ride was the
most
important factor affecting this decision. For the parents of the private commuters, we also notice that the other criteria are not as important. This suggests that shorter bus rides could encourage a mode change toward greater use of school buses.

1.3
Insights from Congestion Data in School Vicinity

To measure the extent of localized congestion around rural schools, we collected vehicle count data from nearby roads, which we report in Figure
3
. For the same day of the week, we counted the number of cars on the streets that led to each of the schools when the school was not in session and then again when the school was in session. In a 25-minute window around the start time of the schools, we see a considerable surge in the number of vehicles in front of each of the three schools on the day the school was open, while this surge is completely absent on the day when the school was closed. Neither of these days was a federal or state holiday and all businesses and offices remained open on both days. The only distinction was that the schools were open on the first day and closed on the second day. These data suggest that fewer car commutes can significantly reduce the number of vehicles near rural schools and the travel time for bus and car commuters, an insight confirmed by our computational results in Section
6
.

2
Relevant Literature

School bus routing and scheduling research has gained traction in the last few decades, with a recent focus on developing models and solution methods that are applicable to real-world instances
(
Park and Kim 2010
,
Ellegood et al. 2020
)
. These studies design bus routes and schedules that transport students from designated stops to schools and back, complying with regulatory requirements and operational constraints that are unique to each school district and its planning priorities. The problem can be decomposed into three subproblems: i) stop assignment (assignment of students to stops), ii) bus route generation (selecting stops and connecting them into routes), and iii) bus scheduling (assigning routes to buses and scheduling their arrival/departure times at stops and schools). The design of effective school bus routes and schedules involves addressing one or more of these subproblems. Our operational context is that of a typical rural school district with mixed loading, where students experience long bus rides and buses are underutilized. These districts have irregular road networks and localized congestion due to car commutes. This paper lies at the intersection of multi-school mixed-loading school bus routing and scheduling, the effects of congestion on school bus transportation, and the impact of bus commute duration on student well-being and academic performance. The next three subsections summarize the relevant literature in these three streams and position our contributions with respect to each stream.

2.1
Multi-School Mixed-Loading School Bus Routing and Scheduling

Although early work on mixed loading
(
Bodin and Berman 1979
,
Chen et al. 1990
)
was inspired by its frequent occurrence in rural areas (Figure
1
,
Ellegood, Campbell, and North 2015
), since then relatively little attention has been paid to school bus routing involving mixed loading (
\sim 15\%
of publications in
Ellegood et al. 2020
). Most of these studies focus on minimizing the number of buses
(
Braca et al. 1997
,
Park, Tae, and Kim 2012
,
Aparicio Ruiz, Muñuzuri Sanz, and
Guadix Martín 2015
)
or a composite cost function of the number of buses and the total distance traveled by the buses
(
Caceres, Batta, and He 2017
,
Lima et al. 2016
,
Miranda et al. 2018
)
, or consider a multi-objective framework with the number of school buses as one of the objectives
(
Kang et al. 2015
,
Mokhtari and Ghezavati 2018
)
. However, rural school districts typically have fewer schools and require a limited bus fleet. Therefore, reducing the bus fleet by a single bus could considerably increase the bus ride time of students while challenging the maximum bus travel time and bus capacity constraints (see Appendix
E
). Some mixed-loading school bus routing models minimize the total bus travel distance
(
Campbell, North, and Ellegood 2015
,
Ellegood, Campbell, and North 2015
,
Yao et al. 2016
)
. Although reducing bus mileage with a fixed fleet helps reduce operating costs in rural school districts
(
Ellegood, Riley, and Berg 2024
)
, it may not align with optimizing bus ride time for students (as confirmed by our results in Section
5.2
), nor does it address the problem of underutilization of buses. Furthermore, although a time loss function, defined as the difference between bus and car travel times, has been considered as an objective before
(
Spada, Bierlaire, and Liebling 2005
)
, it does not adequately capture the actual burden on students, particularly those whose car travel times are already long.

We contribute a practically relevant objective applicable to rural school districts
and
a solution approach to optimize it, addressing all three major sub-problems of the school bus routing and scheduling problem.

2.2
Congestion Considerations in School Bus Transportation

Road traffic flows and congestion have been extensively studied; for comprehensive surveys on macroscopic traffic modeling and congestion dynamics, see textbooks by
May 1990
and
Daganzo 1997
, and a survey paper by
Hoogendoorn and Bovy 2001
. Researchers have also studied congestion near places of interest and special events, in order to mitigate localized traffic build-up
(
Beaudry et al. 2010
,
Kwoczek, Di Martino, and Nejdl 2014
)
. Research on public transit scheduling has shown that modeling the equilibrium between passenger flows and user demand improves overall system performance
(
Pi, Ma, and Qian 2019
,
Wei, Vaze, and Jacquillat 2022
)
. There is a widespread recognition that congestion negatively influences students and increases various costs of school bus transportation
(
La Vigne 2007
,
Lu, Sun, and Zheng 2017
)
. However, the proposed solutions to address this problem have been limited to school choice
(
Wilson, Wilson, and Krizek 2007
)
or school location decisions
(
Lu, Sun, and Zheng 2017
,
Vitale, Millward, and Spinney 2019
)
. Although the school bus routing literature does not directly address traffic flow and congestion, there is research on stochastic link travel times
(
Yan, Hsiao, and Chen 2015
,
Sun, Duan, and Xu 2018
,
Babaei and Rajabi-Bahaabadi 2019
)
and uncertainty in journey times and ridership
(
Levin and Boyles 2016
,
Caceres, Batta, and He 2017
)
. None of these models captures how congestion-induced increases in bus travel times affect the decision to opt for the school bus. This feature is especially important under mixed loading with identical school start times, because school buses travel longer distances on congested links than cars.

We contribute to this literature by addressing localized, time-specific real-world congestion challenges. Additionally, we introduce a new unexplored dimension to the school bus routing and scheduling problem: the impact of congestion-dependent travel times on students’ mode choices and bus ridership patterns.

2.3
Student Travel Time, Sleep and Academic Performance

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends a school start time of 8:30 AM or later for middle and high schoolers to achieve an optimal level of sleep
(
Au et al. 2014
)
, to align with the circadian rhythm of adolescents
(
Crowley, Acebo, and Carskadon 2007
)
. Even a modest postponement of school start time can have a positive influence on sleep, mood, performance, and behavior of students
(
Heissel and Norris 2018
,
Owens, Belon, and Moss 2010
)
. Staggering school start times is a good way to adhere to this recommendation. Staggering also enables a reduction in bus transportation costs by reusing buses to individually serve schools with sufficiently different start times. Recent research on school bus routing and scheduling
(
Bertsimas, Delarue, and
Martin 2019
,
Bertsimas and Delarue 2023
,
Delarue, Lian, and Martin 2023
)
has focused on co-optimizing routes and school start times to aid this effort. Despite awareness around the effect of delaying school start times, only 18% public schools in the United States start at or after 8:30 AM
(
Wheaton, Ferro, and Croft 2015
)
. There are social costs and non-monetary challenges associated with changing school start times
(
Kirby, Maggi, and D’Angiulli 2011
,
Owens et al. 2014
)
that make adhering to the guideline impractical for many school districts. From a routing perspective, in geographically large school districts, picking up distant students on separate trips of the same bus incurs high deadheading operational costs.

Shortening the bus commutes of students is a possible improvement to the status quo in such districts. In addition to better alignment with the adolescent circadian rhythm, the reduction of sleep loss can result in improved academic performance
(
Fredriksen et al. 2004
,
Curcio, Ferrara, and De Gennaro 2006
)
and reduce morning stress. Shorter bus rides can improve well-being by allowing pre-school socialization, a few additional minutes to ensure that students eat breakfast
(
Taras 2005
,
Rampersaud et al. 2005
)
, and flexibility for unexpected emergencies or delays
(
Short and Louca 2015
,
Wahistrom 2002
)
. Our paper proposes a routing and scheduling solution that aims to capitalize on these benefits by minimizing the total bus ride time of students, thus reducing the negative impacts of long commutes.

Model

In this section, we present our mixed-integer linear optimization model for rural school bus routing and scheduling with a fixed number of buses and a mixed-loading setting. Section
3.1
describes the problem setting and the main assumptions. Section
3.2
defines the notation and formulates the mathematical model.

3.1
Setting and Assumptions

Consider a rural school district with multiple schools. We aim to determine the assignment of students to stops, stops to bus routes, and students to bus routes. For each bus, our model decides the sequence of stops and schools visited and the time at which the bus serves each stop and each school. These decisions, combined with the assignment of students to buses and stops, also determine the times when each student is picked up at their assigned stop and dropped off at the school they attend. Our model is based on a network where each school and each candidate stop location is a node. In addition, we include a pair of dummy nodes for the origin and destination of bus routes. Binary decision variables
e_{isk}
determine the assignment of students to stops and buses (and buses to stops as a result). Binary decision variables
x_{ijk}
decide whether a bus travels an arc and the decision variables
w_{ijk}
track the number of students traveling on each bus on each arc. Binary variables
r_{ik}
indicate whether a bus visits a particular node. The variables
v_{mk}
represent the number of students dropped off at a given school by a bus. Continuous decision variables
t_{ik}
denote the time at which a bus serves each node. The variables
p_{sk}
and
d_{sk}
track the pick-up and drop-off times of each student by each bus, while
\tau_{isk}
and
\kappa_{msk}
are the stop-specific versions of these pick-up and drop-off time variables. The objective function of the model is to minimize the total student travel time on the bus – also called total Bus Ride Time of Students (or total BRTS). The model makes the following assumptions.

•

Our formulation solves the morning commute problem, transporting students to school, because of its greater practical importance. As mentioned in Section
1.1
, reducing travel times in the morning is more important from a practical standpoint than reducing those for the afternoon commute from school to home.

•

The size of the school bus fleet is fixed. Appendix
E
evaluates the impact of changing this assumption.

•

Every bus begins its trip at the origin node, visits various stops and schools, and ends at the destination node immediately after dropping off students at the final school it visits.

•

Information about which students take the bus and which schools they attend is known.

•

Mixed loading is allowed; that is, students from different schools can ride the same bus together.

•

Each student must have access to a bus stop within a maximum allowed walking distance.

•

Each bus
k\in\mathcal{K}
has a maximum student carrying capacity of
C_{k}
.

•

The travel time of any bus cannot exceed a maximum value
T
. This prevents excessive delays for any single bus, promotes fairness among routes, and accommodates practical limitations on driver availability before a specified start time in the morning.

•

The pick-up and drop-off times are linear functions (similar to
Braca et al. 1997
and other school bus routing studies) of the number of students picked up at a bus stop and dropped off at a school, respectively.

•

Each student is transported to their school using a single bus and cannot be transferred between buses.

•

Students can share a stop with other students from the same or different schools.

•

A stop can be visited by multiple buses, but each school and stop is visited by each bus at most once.

These assumptions are relevant to our practical context of rural school bus routing and scheduling, but they are different from the existing literature in the following ways.

1.

In the existing mixed-loading school bus routing and scheduling literature, the typical objective function is to minimize the number of buses or the total distance traveled by the buses
(
Ellegood et al. 2020
)
. In contrast, our formulation minimizes the total bus ride time of students for a fixed number of buses.

2.

In existing school bus routing and scheduling models, the assignment of students to stops is often overlooked because students are preassigned to stops. In some cases, stops are assigned exclusively to students attending the same school
(
Spada, Bierlaire, and Liebling 2005
,
Park, Tae, and Kim 2012
)
. However, each additional stop could add a considerable amount of travel time for students already on the bus. We provide flexibility to the model for assigning students to stops and buses, which can enable better solutions.

3.

Previous research identifies assumptions for rural mixed-loading models:
(i)
students of different schools can ride the same bus on the same trip,
(ii)
all students are picked up by a bus before any drop-off from that bus,
(iii)
each bus stop is exclusively served by a single bus,
(iv)
each school can be served by multiple buses, though a bus can only visit a school once,
(v)
a bus is not required to visit all schools
(
Park, Tae, and Kim 2012
,
Lima et al. 2016
)
. Our conversations with rural school bus planners showed that the assumptions
(i)
,
(iv)
, and
(v)
are reasonable from an operational perspective, as they provide flexibility for the operator to assign students to buses and build routes and schedules. However, assumptions
(ii)
and
(iii)
could lead to suboptimal solutions. This is particularly true for stops that are geographically between schools, where students from different schools should be picked up by different buses. In addition, in some cases, they should be picked up after some other students on that bus are dropped off at another school. Ours is the first mixed-loading model that relaxes assumptions
(ii)
and
(iii)
to enable better solutions.

4.

Most formulations model a subset of the aforementioned three major subproblems in school bus routing and scheduling: (i) stop assignment, (ii) bus route generation, and (iii) school bus scheduling. We integrate all three into a single formulation, which can also be extended to capture adjustments to school start times
(
Park and Kim 2010
)
. Our integrated model has binary variables for whether a student is assigned to a stop-bus combination and whether an arc is used by a bus to model subproblems (i) and (ii). The schedule is inferred from the time-based decision variables at the stop, thus handling subproblem (iii). The arrival time at each school can be set as a constraint to also integrate the school start time adjustment subproblem. Note that our conversations with multiple rural school bus routing and scheduling practitioners made it clear that school start time adjustment is impractical due to logistical and stakeholder constraints for their schools (constraints that are also mentioned in literature, such as
Kirby, Maggi, and D’Angiulli 2011
and
Owens et al. 2014
) and therefore this aspect was left out of the computational results presented in this paper.

Solution Approach

In this section, we present our solution approach to the formulation (
1
)-(
35
) presented in Section
3
. We begin in Section
4.1
with a general overview of the solution approaches to school bus routing and scheduling and contextualize the distinctive features of our proposed cluster-then-route methodology. Section
4.2
details the clustering step, which introduces our
Hybrid Road network-Aware Constrained
k
-means Clustering (HRACkC)
approach that assigns buses to service regions containing stops and students. Finally, Section

4.1
Overview

Solution approaches to the school bus routing and scheduling problem (SBRSP) are typically informed by the characteristics of the model, which in turn are shaped by the specific problem statements that could vary district by district. Our problem statement, inspired by our practical context (Sections
1.1
and
1.2
), applies to many rural school districts. It focuses on minimizing the total bus ride time of students, which requires explicit tracking of individual students. Furthermore, pre-assigning students to stops introduces suboptimality (see Section
5.1
), which motivates joint optimization of the student-to-stop assignment within the SBRSP. Even when students are already assigned to stops, obtaining exact solutions to mixed-loading multi-school SBRSP formulations is computationally intractable for real-world instances
(
Caceres, Batta, and He 2019
,
Spada, Bierlaire, and Liebling 2005
)
. Our integrated student-centric model, directly justified by practical problem needs, involves additional complexity compared to previous formulations.

Previous studies with traditional SBRSP formulations often used two-phase solution methods rather than insertion heuristics or savings-based heuristics to address the inherent complexity of the problem
(
Ellegood et al. 2020
)
. Our integrated student-centric model also benefits from a two-phase solution approach. A two-phase method can be implemented as a cluster-first route-second or a route-first cluster-second approach. The literature on the constrained vehicle routing problem suggests that the former approach tends to generate superior solutions
(
Cordeau et al. 2007
)
. In this section, we introduce a new cluster-then-route method based on the formulation (
1
)-(
35
), designed to produce high-quality solutions within practical runtimes. Compared to the existing literature, our approach stands out in four important ways, as listed below.

1.

Integrated Bus Stop Selection, Student Assignment, and Routing
: The routing phase involves three key components: a) stop selection, b) student-to-stop assignment, and c) connecting stops into routes. These three components are typically addressed using one of three general approaches. Location-Allocation-Routing (LAR)
(
Riera-Ledesma and
Salazar-Gonzalez 2012
,
Riera-Ledesma and
Salazar-González 2013
)
first selects bus stops, followed by student assignment, and then routing. Allocation-Location-Routing (ALR)
(
Chapleau, Ferland, and
Rousseau 1985
,
Bowerman, Hall, and Calamai 1995
)
assigns students to potential stops, which informs stop selection, followed by route generation. In Location-Routing-Allocation (LRA)
(
Schittekat et al. 2013
)
, bus stops are selected first, then the route is planned, and finally the students are assigned to the route. Unlike these sequential heuristics, which can exacerbate suboptimality (see Section
5.1
), the second phase (routing phase) of our cluster-then-route method
uses our mixed-integer linear optimization (MILO) model to jointly optimize stop selection, student assignment, and routing.

2.

Road Network-Aware Clustering
: Existing SBRSP approaches overlook road network structure, simplify the underlying road topology and consider only point-to-point Euclidean distances or travel times.
Zeng, Chopra, and Smilowitz 2019
is a notable exception which explicitly leverages network structure, but is tailored specifically for urban networks with regular grid-like connection patterns. In contrast, our work addresses the rural network topology, which is fundamentally different due to irregular, sparse connections that violate grid assumptions. This distinction is critical; rural networks exhibit tree-like substructures and highly variable connectivity where location-based clustering performs poorly. Our network-aware clustering algorithm addresses this gap by prioritizing topological relationships over geometric proximity, enabling effective solutions where Euclidean distance-based methods perform poorly (as shown in Section
5.1
).

3.

Problem Structure-Informed Matheuristic
: The complexity of mixed-loading, multi-school SBRSP has led many researchers to adopt evolutionary or trajectory-based metaheuristics, such as genetic algorithm
(
Oluwadare, Oguntuyi, and
Nwaiwu 2018
,
Thangiah et al. 2008
,
Kang et al. 2015
)
, variable neighborhood search
(
Bögl, Doerner, and Parragh 2015
,
Lima et al. 2016
)
, greedy random search
(
Silva et al. 2015
,
Lima et al. 2016
)
, ant colony optimization
(
Yao et al. 2016
,
Mokhtari and Ghezavati 2018
)
, iterated local search
(
Lima et al. 2016
,
Miranda et al. 2018
)
, tabu search and simulated annealing
(
Spada, Bierlaire, and Liebling 2005
)
. These approaches typically incorporate established heuristic components, such as savings heuristic or sequential insertion heuristic. In contrast, our problem structure-informed matheuristic directly employs our MILO formulation to produce high-quality solutions. Our approach maintains a two-phase structure with a tailored clustering method specifically designed for our problem’s characteristics, followed by the direct application of our formulation to jointly optimize stop assignment, routing, and scheduling. This approach offers an interpretable solution framework that leverages our formulation rather than relying on solution techniques that operate independently of the underlying mathematical structure of the model.

4.

Inter-School En route Student Service
: In mixed-loading SBRSP studies, school nodes are incorporated into routes using three main approaches.
(i)

Insertion
approach: School nodes are added anywhere along the bus routes to minimize the degradation of objective functions
(
Park, Tae, and Kim 2012
,
Aparicio Ruiz, Muñuzuri Sanz, and
Guadix Martín 2015
,
Lima et al. 2016
)
.
(ii)

Sequential
approach: All bus stops are visited first and then the schools are visited in an optimized sequence
(
Campbell, North, and Ellegood 2015
,
Ellegood, Campbell, and North 2015
)
.
(iii)

Closed
approach: Bus routes begin and end at the same school while other schools are visited just before returning to the starting school
(
Silva et al. 2015
,
Yao et al. 2016
)
. In contrast, our method directly optimizes school sequencing decisions within our model formulation, eliminating the need for separate consideration of school nodes while designing solutions. Our approach also enhances both solution quality and practical applicability by allowing buses to pick up students while traveling from one school to another, a capability missing from existing solution techniques.

4.2
Hybrid Road network-Aware Constrained
k
-Means Clustering (
HRACkC
)

Given the sparseness of road networks in rural school districts, standard Euclidean clustering cannot produce high-quality clusters (as demonstrated in Section
5.1
). Therefore, it is desirable to generate student clusters in a road network-aware manner. However, direct generation of road network-aware clusters is computationally challenging (as shown in Section
5.1
). Therefore, we develop a hybrid clustering approach that begins with Euclidean constrained
k
-means clustering, which is computationally tractable and avoids unbalanced clusters using upper limits on cluster sizes. Then, we identify a group of students whose reassignment to another cluster is likely to provide the largest benefit in terms of reducing the total BRTS. Finally, we solve a reduced form of the road network-aware constrained
k
-means clustering problem by focusing on reassigning this promising group of students to other clusters while fixing the cluster membership of the remaining students and respecting the upper limits on cluster sizes.

Section
4.2.1
describes the Euclidean constrained
k
-means clustering formulation that generates the initial assignment of students to buses. Section
4.2.2
presents an approach to reduce the size of the clustering problem by identifying students who could be assigned to a different cluster. Then, Section
4.2.3
describes our reduced road network-aware constrained
k
-means clustering problem, which reshapes the service regions. Finally, Section
4.2.4
, describes our approach to assign bus stops to the student clusters.

(39)

(40)

\displaystyle\bar{x}^{n+1}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}z_{sk}^{n}x_{s}}{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}z_{sk}^{n}}\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle\bar{y}^{n+1}_{k}

(\bar{x}^{n+1}_{k}-\bar{x}^{n}_{k})^{2}+(\bar{y}^{n+1}_{k}-\bar{y}^{n}_{k})^{2}\leq\varepsilon\quad\forall k\in\mathcal{K}

4.2.4
Bus Stop Clustering

As per our problem statement, each student must have access to a bus stop within the maximum allowed walking distance. Students who reside near the edges of the convex region
H_{k}^{\prime}
may not satisfy this requirement; for some of these students, all bus stops within the maximum allowed walking distance may be outside
H_{k}^{\prime}
. To meet this requirement, the bus stop clustering step expands the service region
H_{k}^{\prime}
. Specifically, we shift all edges of the region
H_{k}^{\prime}
outward from its center by a fixed distance equal to the maximum allowed walking distance to a bus stop, creating a new service region
H_{k}^{\prime\prime}
.

Note that the overlapping parts of these expanded service regions may now contain some bus stops, overcoming a key restriction of previous studies that required each stop to be served by a single bus (restriction
(iii)
in point 3 of our model’s unique features in Section
3.1
). This approach is particularly beneficial for stops located between schools (relaxing restriction
(ii)
in point 3 of Section
3.1
, which requires all pick-ups to end before any drop-offs by a bus begin), where students headed to schools in different directions can be picked up by different buses at the same stop – a routing efficiency enabled by our overall approach.

4.3

Our SAS4 approach addresses the inherent computational challenge in our routing problem. As shown in Section

This complexity stems from the need for explicit modeling of student-to-stop assignments rather than using pre-assignments. We overcome this challenge by developing an original three-step routing approach guided by our formulation. First, we reduce the complexity of our formulation by temporarily pre-assigning students to stops such that the number of stops is minimized, using a mathematical formulation presented in Section

\mathcal{A}_{5}

5.4
Drivers of Improvement

In this section, we identify the key drivers of improvement in our solutions compared to the status quo solutions. The first important driver is the substantial reduction in bus ride time for students with the longest status quo commutes. Figure
9
in Appendix
C
demonstrates this by comparing each student’s status quo ride time with the change in ride time that our solution achieves. Students who originally endured the longest commutes, particularly those with initial ride times exceeding 2,000 seconds, experience the largest BRTS reductions in our solutions. This is also reflected in the difference in BRTS savings across the two districts. District II exhibits a wider range of status quo BRTS compared to District I, with several students experiencing particularly long status quo commutes. Therefore, our solutions achieve larger average BRTS savings in District II than in District I. Most importantly, our approach achieves a favorable trade-off: in District I, 73% of students experience BRTS reductions, saving an average of 948 seconds, while only 27% face an average increase of 581 seconds. In District II, 70% benefit from shorter rides with an average saving of 1332 seconds, while the remaining 30% face an average increase of 738 seconds.

Whether a student takes the bus depends on the amount of time the student spends on the bus, as shown in Section
1
and modeled in Section
6
. A performance indicator of this major decision is the detour ratio, which tells us how many times longer a bus journey is compared to a direct drive to school for each student. Figure
10
in Appendix
C
shows the improvement provided by our solutions against the status quo detour ratios of the students. A second driver of our overall improvement is the reduction in detour ratios, primarily benefiting students who currently experience the most circuitous routes. Students with initial detour ratios greater than 10 experience a median improvement of 77% in District I and 63% in District II, while those with an initial detour ratio between 5 and 10 have a median improvement of 55% and 53%, respectively.

The reduction in the number of bus stops and the marginal increase in walking time, as discussed in Section
5.2
, enable a third major driver of improvement: a better pick-up order for students. A student’s pick-up order, defined as their position in the sequence of stops along a route, significantly impacts their total ride time. Each bus stop delays students already on board, creating cumulative effects that particularly burden students who board earlier. Figure
11
in Appendix

The results reveal a clear pattern: The students picked up the earliest, those in the top quartile of the original pick-up order, experience substantially greater ride time savings, with a median ride time improvement of 65%, compared to a 10% increase for the last quartile. Thus, our solution specifically addresses the long ride times experienced by students who endure numerous stops after boarding. Figure

Although adding a single bus could reduce total BRTS, our analysis (in Appendix

6
Impacts of Mode Shift and Congestion Level

So far, our model (Section
3
), our solution approach (Section
4
), and our computational results (Section
5
) have focused primarily on minimizing the total bus ride times of students under the assumption that the students’ decision to use a particular mode (bus or car) is not affected by improvements in bus ride times. However, in practice, shorter bus rides are likely to make bus travel more attractive to students, especially in light of the empirical evidence in Section
1.2
(Figure
2
), which shows that shorter bus rides make the school bus more attractive. As a result, some students may switch from car commutes to school buses, leading to secondary benefits from fewer vehicles on the road, reduced localized congestion in the vicinity of schools, and increased bus utilization. As more students adopt the bus, the roads in the vicinity of the schools become less congested, further reducing bus travel times. Car commutes also become faster. However, congestion in the vicinity of schools disproportionately affects school buses because they visit multiple schools. Thus, reduced congestion near schools provides a greater reduction in the disutility and travel times of school buses than car commutes, and incentivizes mode shift from car commutes to school buses. Quantifying the extent of these potential mode shifts is challenging due to the limited behavioral data. In practice, school districts typically revise bus routes on a seasonal basis, making an iterative approach that accounts for congestion and mode choice both feasible and beneficial. In order to capture these relationships and quantify the aforementioned secondary benefits, in this section, we propose an iterative approach and provide a more comprehensive view of how optimized bus routes may influence the overall travel behavior of students.

6.1
Algorithm

We now describe our iterative algorithm to quantify the additional benefits of school bus routing and scheduling optimization. The algorithm captures three key relationships. First, it captures the effect of changes in travel times on the student mode choice and demand for bus travel (described in Section
6.1.1
). Second, it approximates the effects of changes in car commute patterns on road congestion and travel times (Section
6.1.2
). Finally, it optimizes bus routes and schedules (Section
4
) in response to changes in student demand and road travel times. Section
6.1.3
describes the overall structure of this iterative algorithm. Note that students who regularly bike or walk to school are not considered in this analysis, as these transportation modes do not impact congestion or ridership and are typically used by students living closer to schools.

6.1.1
Mode Choice Model

Consistent with the survey findings in Section
1.2
, we divide the student population into three sets:
\mathcal{S}_{A}
(students who “always” ride the bus),
\mathcal{S}_{S}
(“sometimes” ride the bus), and
\mathcal{S}_{N}
(“never” ride the bus). Our mode choice model, as described by Equation (
63
), assumes that the students in the set
\mathcal{S}_{S}
are the only ones that can consider switching to school buses if the school bus ride becomes more attractive. This survey also suggests that some students in
\mathcal{S}_{N}
could switch to the bus if bus ride times decrease. In that sense, the results here represent a lower bound on the benefits of our approach, and the actual benefits could be even larger. Let
T_{cs}
and
T_{bs}
denote the anticipated travel times by car and bus, for student
s\in\mathcal{S}_{S}
. According to the multinomial logit model, the probability of
s
choosing the bus is:

P_{bs}=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\Bigl(-A\bigl(T_{cs}-T_{bs}\bigr)\Bigr)}\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S}_{S}

(63)

where
A\geq 0
is the utility coefficient of travel time that indicates the sensitivity to travel time differences. The value of
A
is not readily available, but is key to approximating the impact of travel time on mode choice and thus to estimating the additional benefits of our approach. Hence
A
must be calibrated.

Calibration of the utility coefficient of travel time (
A
)
: Let
Y_{b}
be the status quo number of bus riders. We calibrate
A
so that the size of the always-bus group plus the expected number of riders in the
\mathcal{S}_{S}
group matches the status quo number of bus riders
Y_{b}
with the status quo travel times. Mathematically:

\displaystyle\lvert\mathcal{S}_{A}\rvert+\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{S}}P_{bs}=Y_{b}

(64)

Substituting Equation (
63
) into (
64
), and rearranging the terms, we get the following.

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_{S}}\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\Bigl(-A\bigl(T_{cs}-T_{bs}\bigr)\Bigr)}=Y_{b}-\lvert\mathcal{S}_{A}\rvert

(65)

This is a single equation in one variable,
A
, and is solved by binary search to calibrate
A
for each district.

Using the calibrated value of
A
,
we sort the students in the set
\mathcal{S}_{S}
in descending order of
P_{bs}
, assign bus travel to the top (
Y_{b}
-
\lvert\mathcal{S}_{A}\rvert
) students and car travel to the rest. Mathematically, we define the cutoff probability by
P^{C}=P_{bs^{\prime}}
where
s^{\prime}
is the student in the
(Y_{b}-\lvert\mathcal{S}_{A}\rvert)^{\text{th}}
position in this descending order and classify any student
s\in\mathcal{S}_{S}
with
P_{bs}\geq P^{C}
as a bus rider and those below the threshold as car commuters.

6.1.2
Road Congestion and Travel Time Update

Once we determine which students do not ride the bus,
we analyze their shortest car routes from home to school. Drawing on the speed-flow relationships
(
Schrank et al. 2015
)
, we estimate the road congestion and update the corresponding travel times for each segment of the road. Using these updated travel times for the road segments, we then calculate the bus travel times
\Delta_{ij}
for
(i,j)\in\mathcal{A}
. These updated travel times capture the impact of additional cars on the road network. We then update
\Delta_{ij}
in our school bus routing and scheduling model (
1
)-(
33
) with these new values, ensuring that our approach reflects the influence of car commuters on bus (and car) travel times. In the next subsection, we explain our overall algorithm that iterates between the mode choice model, road congestion and travel time updates, and school bus routing and scheduling optimization.

6.1.3
Overall Algorithm

Figure
6
illustrates our iterative approach to school bus routing and scheduling, which combines mode choice, congestion effects, and route and schedule optimization. We start with an initial set of inputs, including status quo travel times,
and generate a baseline routing and scheduling plan. Next, we apply the mode choice model to determine which students opt for the bus. Those who do not ride the bus commute by car and contribute to road congestion. We then update travel times to reflect this increased traffic and re-run the bus route and schedule optimization, adjusting the bus routes and schedules themselves. We repeat this cycle, updating mode choice, congestion levels, travel times, and bus routes and schedules until the number of students choosing the bus remains unchanged in successive iterations.

Ultimately, these results demonstrate that our approach provides a scalable way to improve school bus utilization and ease congestion while maintaining or improving average BRTS savings.

7
Conclusion

Rural school districts face unique challenges in designing bus routes and schedules, including long commutes, underutilized buses, localized congestion, irregular road networks, and mixed loading. This paper introduces a student-centric approach for designing rural school bus routes and schedules within multi-school districts with a fixed fleet.
We formulate an integrated mixed-integer linear optimization (MILO) model that incorporates student-level variables to minimize the total bus ride time of students (BRTS) while addressing all major subproblems of the school bus routing and scheduling problem. We develop an original two-phase cluster-then-route heuristic that leverages the road network structure and our MILO formulation to generate high-quality solutions for real-world instances. Furthermore, we combine our routing and scheduling approach with a mode choice model within an iterative framework to capture students’ responses to the new bus routes and schedules. Improved routes and schedules attract more students to bus transportation, amplifying the systemwide benefits through a positive feedback loop. The results of two real-world rural school districts suggest that our approach reduces the total BRTS by
\sim 37
–
39\%
. Additionally, accounting for student mode choice unlocks the potential to transport
\sim 17
–
19\%
more students.

In future research, our solution approach could be expanded to incorporate time-dependent clustering to explore the staggering of school start times in a rural context to address the challenge of congestion in the vicinity of schools. In addition, future work can collect and use disaggregate mode choice data to train more accurate mode choice models. Finally, even though our solution approach considerably outperforms the status quo, an optimality gap may still exist. This motivates further research into other heuristics or exact techniques to handle the complexity of mixed-loading multi-school rural school bus routing and scheduling.

Appendix D
Tradeoff Quantification Between Bus Ride Time and Walking Time

Figure 12:
Total Bus Ride Time - Maximum Allowed Walking Distance Trade-off

In this appendix, we analyze the effect of increasing the maximum allowed walking distance to a bus stop on the total bus ride time of students (BRTS) and the total student travel times (STT). In our baseline solution presented in Table
3
, we maintain the default maximum allowed walking distances in the two school districts at 0.30 miles, which already produces substantial improvements, reducing bus ride times by 37.1% and 39.74% in Districts I and II, respectively, while decreasing the number of stops by 21.8% and 25%. However, this maximum allowed walking distance parameter represents a key policy lever that districts could adjust to achieve even greater efficiency.

Figure
12
reveals distinct patterns in how the maximum allowed walking distance affects the various travel metrics across the two districts, including average values of bus ride times, walking times, and total travel times of students, as well as the total number of bus stops. In District I, bus ride time consistently decreases with increasing maximum allowed walking distance, dropping from 15 to 12 minutes as the maximum allowed walking distance increases to 0.45 miles. However, the total travel time (which is the sum of the actual walking time and bus ride time of students) follows a non-linear pattern – when maximum allowed walking distance increases from 0.35 to 0.40 miles, the total travel time per student actually increases slightly despite a substantial reduction in the number of bus stops (from 134 to 124). This suggests a tipping point where additional walking time begins to outweigh the benefits of the minimized bus ride time of students enabled by the reduced number of bus stops. In contrast, District II shows consistent decreases in the average values of both the bus ride time and the total travel time across all increments in the maximum allowed walking distance. These results show that the optimal trade-off between the maximum allowed walking distance threshold and the total bus ride time of students varies by district and depends on the specific geographic distribution of students and schools.

Appendix E
Impact of fleet size on the total bus ride time and the total travel time of students

Figure 13:
Impact of fleet size on the average values of bus ride times and total travel times of students

As empirically demonstrated in Section
1
and further invoked in Section
2
, rural school districts tend to operate with a small number of buses. Thus, removing a single school bus could considerably increase BRTS while potentially violating the maximum bus travel time constraint. Adding a school bus could potentially reduce BRTS, but school districts need to evaluate whether the time savings justify the additional operational costs, which are a large percentage cost increase. Therefore, in this appendix, we examine the impact of fleet size on the average values of the Bus Ride Time of Students (BRTS) and the total Student Travel Time (STT) in both districts, with and without the maximum bus travel time constraint (as shown in Figure
13
). All circular markers in Figure
13
correspond to the results obtained with the maximum bus travel time constraint in place, while all square markers
correspond to the results obtained after relaxing the maximum bus travel time constraint.

The results reveal an asymmetric relationship between the size of the fleet and the ride times in both districts. In the first district, reducing the fleet size from nine to eight buses increases the average BRTS by 12% with the constraint and 13% without it. In the second district, operating with six buses instead of seven is infeasible under the maximum bus travel time constraint, although without the constraint, this reduction increases the average BRTS by 16%.

In contrast, adding a bus yields only marginal reductions: increasing from 9 to 10 buses in the first district reduces the average BRTS by just 6% (constrained) and 5% (unconstrained), while in the second district, adding a bus saves only 4% in both scenarios. This suggests that fleet expansion provides diminishing returns while fleet reduction creates disproportionate increases in ride times, providing crucial guidance for districts weighing additional bus costs against modest time savings while recognizing the significant risks of fleet reductions.
