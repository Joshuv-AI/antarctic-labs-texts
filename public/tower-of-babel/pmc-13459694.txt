Chinese Guideline on Risk Management of Osteoporosis in the General Public
Authors: Shiyong Zhang, Jinqiu Yuan, Zhuojing Luo, Jiacan Su, Weibo Xia, Kehu Yang, Fuxin Wei, Osteoporosis Group, Chinese Association of Orthopaedic Surgeons, Youth Osteoporosis Group, Chinese Orthopaedic Association, Chinese Medical Association, Health Communication Branch, Chinese Preventive Medicine Association, Spine and Joint Health Branch, Guangdong Health Care Association, Chinese Health Risk Management Collaboration–Osteoporosis Group (CHRIMAC‐OP)

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOsteoporosis is a common chronic skeletal disorder characterized by decreased bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration, resulting in increased fragility and fracture risk. With rapid population aging in China, the prevalence of osteoporosis is rising, while public awareness and engagement in prevention remain insufficient. Existing national guidelines mainly target healthcare professionals and lack accessible, evidence‐based recommendations for the general public. This guideline aims to provide practical guidance for osteoporosis risk assessment and prevention at the population level.MethodsThe guideline was developed by the Chinese Health Risk Management Collaboration—Osteoporosis Group in accordance with the WHO Handbook for Guideline Development, the Principles for Developing or Revising Clinical Practice Guidelines in China (2022), and AGREE II and RIGHT frameworks. Evidence from both English and Chinese databases was synthesized and graded using the GRADE approach. Fourteen key questions were formulated through a modified Delphi consensus process.ResultsA total of 15 recommendations were developed, covering osteoporosis risk screening (OSTA, IOF One‐Minute Test, FRAX), dietary and nutritional strategies (protein, dairy, calcium, vitamin D, fruits and vegetables), lifestyle interventions (exercise, bodyweight control, sunlight exposure, smoking cessation, alcohol reduction, tea drinking), and fracture prevention in patients with osteoporosis.ConclusionThis guideline provides practical, evidence‐based strategies to improve public awareness and promote risk management of osteoporosis among the general population, potentially reducing fracture incidence and public health burden.Keywords: lifestyle intervention, osteoporosis, prevention, public health, risk assessment

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by loss of bone mass, deterioration of bone microarchitecture, and increased bone fragility [1]. It is primarily associated with an imbalance in bone remodeling, where bone resorption exceeds bone formation. Based on etiology, OP can be classified into two major categories: primary and secondary. Primary OP includes type I (postmenopausal), type II (senile), and idiopathic OP (juvenile) [2]. Type I and II are the most common forms, while idiopathic OP refers to cases in adolescents with unknown causes. Secondary OP results mainly from external factors that disrupt bone metabolism, such as long‐term glucocorticoid use, excessive alcohol intake, or radiation exposure [3, 4].

With population aging and lifestyle changes, the prevalence of OP in China has risen significantly. A nationwide survey conducted in 2018 reported that the prevalence of OP among individuals aged 50 years and older was approximately 19.2% (6.0% in men and 32.1% in women), while among those aged 60 years and older it reached 32.0% (10.7% in men and 51.6% in women) [5]. The high prevalence and wide impact of OP impose substantial economic and healthcare burdens on both patients and the healthcare system. However, public awareness of OP in China remains low, resulting in very limited diagnosis and treatment rates. This situation is often described domestically as “high prevalence but low awareness, diagnosis, and treatment” [6]. Moreover, many OP patients are asymptomatic until the onset of osteoporotic fractures, making early detection and prevention particularly challenging.

Raising public awareness of OP and strengthening the management of risk factors are therefore essential for preventing OP and related fractures. Several clinical guidelines on OP management have been published in China, such as the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Senile Osteoporosis [7] and the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Osteoporosis [8]. However, these guidelines are mainly intended for healthcare professionals in clinical settings and offer limited applicability to the general public. At present, there remains a lack of evidence‐based recommendations focusing on risk assessment and prevention strategies that can guide both primary care providers and the general population.

In line with the Healthy China Initiative (2019–2030), which emphasizes that “every individual is the primary guardian of their own health,” this guideline systematically reviews current evidence on risk assessment and prevention of OP. Using the Delphi method to establish consensus, it provides recommendations for osteoporosis risk management in the general population, aiming to support effective daily practices for OP prevention and control.

Guideline Development Methods

This guideline was developed by the Osteoporosis Group of the Chinese Health Risk Management Collaboration (CHRIMAC‐OP). The development process followed the WHO Handbook for Guideline Development (2014) and the Principles for Developing or Revising Clinical Practice Guidelines in China (2022 edition) issued by the Chinese Medical Association. It also referenced the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II) and the Reporting Items for Practice Guidelines in Healthcare (RIGHT) to prepare both the protocol and the final guideline document. The guideline was registered with the International Practice Guidelines Registry Platform (PREPARE‐2023CN470). Detailed methods are described in the series protocol papers [9]. This guideline is intended for use in hospitals at all levels, community health service centers, centers for disease control and prevention, and among the general public, especially individuals at high risk for osteoporosis.

Question Formulation

Guideline questions were drafted by the Steering Committee based on expertise and literature review. In June 2023, two meetings were held at the Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat‐sen University to generate preliminary questions. An online survey of more than 110 experts in orthopedics, endocrinology, geriatrics, gynecology, preventive medicine, and health management was then conducted to supplement the list. In August 2023, the first guideline consensus meeting was held, where the importance of clinical questions was rated using the Delphi method as recommended by the GRADE Working Group. Fourteen core questions were finalized.

Evidence Retrieval

The Evidence Review Group systematically searched for relevant consensus statements, guidelines, and systematic reviews/meta‐analyses. Databases included PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, China Biomedical Literature Database (CBM), Wanfang Data, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI). Supplementary searches were conducted in guideline repositories (e.g., National Guideline Clearinghouse, Guidelines International Network, WHO database) and Google Scholar. The search period extended from database inception to October 2023, limited to Chinese and English publications. References of included studies were also screened.

Systematic Reviews

Systematic reviews were prioritized as the main evidence source for each question. When no suitable or up‐to‐date (≤ 3 years) reviews were available, or when methodological quality was insufficient, new systematic reviews/meta‐analyses were conducted, following the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, and case–control studies. Literature screening and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved by discussion. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool or the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), depending on study type. Heterogeneity was evaluated clinically, methodologically, and statistically. Where appropriate, a random‐effects model was applied, and publication bias was assessed.

Evidence Appraisal and Body of Evidence

For each key question, the certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach [10]. Evidence quality was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low. Five downgrading factors (study limitations, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias) and three upgrading factors (large effect size, dose–response gradient, and confounding minimizing the effect) were considered. Evidence was summarized in GRADE evidence profiles, forming the basis for recommendations.

Development of Recommendations

Based on the evidence body, the Evidence Review Group drafted preliminary recommendations. The Expert Consensus Group convened in March 2024 for the second Delphi consensus meeting, which involved three rounds of surveys. Voting decisions considered the balance of benefits and harms, certainty of effect estimates, patient values and preferences, and resource use [11].

A recommendation required ≥ 50% agreement, with < 20% of experts favoring alternatives.

To be classified as a “strong recommendation,” ≥ 70% expert agreement was required. Strong recommendations were worded as “recommend,” while weak recommendations were expressed as “suggest.”

Writing, Dissemination, and Updating

After consensus was achieved, the drafting group prepared the full manuscript, which was internally reviewed and revised. The draft was then submitted to the External Review Group for independent evaluation. To enhance readability, five public representatives were also invited to review the document. Guideline Question 1

How should the general population conduct risk assessment and screening for osteoporosis (OP)?

Recommendation 1

We suggest that the general public use the International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF) One‐Minute Osteoporosis Risk Test or the Osteoporosis Self‐Assessment Tool for Asians (OSTA) to evaluate their risk of OP. Individuals identified as being at high risk are advised to undergo bone mineral density (BMD) testing and OP screening in healthcare settings.

Recommendation strength: Weak; certainty of evidence: Low.

Comments

Osteoporosis is often referred to as a “silent disease” [12], as it usually lacks overt clinical symptoms before diagnosis or fracture occurrence. Early risk assessment, combined with lifestyle modification or timely intervention, is crucial to preventing OP and associated fractures. The IOF One‐Minute Test and OSTA are convenient self‐assessment tools that require no specialized equipment or professional training, making them highly suitable for public use. In Chinese populations, both tools have been validated as effective for identifying OP risk, with combined use demonstrating improved predictive value [13].

IOF One‐Minute Osteoporosis Risk Test

The IOF One‐Minute Osteoporosis Risk Test is a simple and effective tool for preliminary risk assessment (Table 2). It should be noted that this test is intended for preliminary risk screening only and is not applicable for the diagnosis of osteoporosis. Studies in Asian populations have shown that the test has acceptable accuracy as a first‐line screening method (AUC: 0.63, 95% CI: 0.56–0.70). The test consists of 19 questions related to medical history and lifestyle factors. Each “yes” response scores 1 point. A total score of ≥ 6 corresponds to a 70% probability of OP, while in postmenopausal women, a score of ≥ 5 indicates significantly increased risk [14]. Individuals scoring 5–6 or above are recommended to undergo professional OP evaluation and initiate risk management strategies.

Osteoporosis Self‐Assessment Tool for Asians

The OSTA is a simple, noninvasive screening instrument based on age and body weight, with good feasibility and high accuracy [15]. It should be noted that the OSTA tool has been specifically validated for use in postmenopausal women. A cross‐sectional study in Chinese postmenopausal women reported that OSTA achieved a sensitivity of 69.64% and a specificity of 75.07% for predicting OP [16]. The OSTA index is calculated using the following formula:

OSTA index = (body weight − age) × 0.2.

Currently, clinical screening and diagnostic methods for OP primarily include the “gold standard” dual‐energy x‐ray absorptiometry (DXA), quantitative ultrasound (QUS), novel ultrasonic bone densitometers, digital x‐ray radiogrammetry (DXR), and opportunistic quantitative computed tomography (opportunistic QCT) [17, 18]. We recommend that individuals at high risk of OP undergo professional evaluation in qualified healthcare facilities, where results should be interpreted by trained physicians, followed by preventive or therapeutic interventions as advised.

Guideline Question 2: How Should Fracture Risk in Osteoporosis Be Assessed?

Recommendation 2

We recommend that individuals with osteoporosis or at high risk use the Fracture Risk Assessment Tool (FRAX) for self‐assessment of fracture risk, followed by timely professional consultation and management.

Recommendation strength: Strong; certainty of evidence: Low.

Comments

Osteoporosis substantially increases the risk of fragility fractures, which represent a major determinant of morbidity and health burden. Therefore, the management of osteoporosis should include not only improving bone quality but also prioritizing fracture prevention. Several tools are currently available for estimating fracture risk in high‐risk populations, including FRAX, Q‐Fracture, the Garvan calculator, DeFRA, and FRA‐HS [19, 20, 21]. Among these, FRAX (official website: https://www.fraxplus.org/) is one of the most widely used and well‐validated fracture risk assessment tools worldwide.

A recent systematic review summarizing 40 studies published up to 2023—including randomized controlled trials, prospective cohorts, retrospective studies, and cross‐sectional analyses across diverse populations—confirmed the predictive value of FRAX for fragility fractures, regardless of whether bone mineral density (BMD) data were incorporated [21]. In women, when BMD data were included, FRAX demonstrated a sensitivity of 67% (95% CI: 30%–93%) and a specificity of 75% (95% CI: 63%–84%). Without BMD data, sensitivity ranged from 57% to 85% (95% CI: 49%–90%), and specificity ranged from 34% to 79% (95% CI: 23%–82%).

Based on this evidence, we recommend that individuals at high risk of osteoporosis use FRAX to estimate their fracture risk and adopt appropriate preventive measures according to the assessment results, while noting that FRAX may underestimate fracture risk in the Chinese population due to limited large‐scale domestic epidemiological data.

Guideline Question 3: Can Increasing Dietary Protein Intake Reduce the Risk of Osteoporosis?

Recommendation 3

We suggest that the general public, particularly older adults (> 65 years), increase dietary protein intake to reduce the risk of osteoporosis and fragility fractures.

Recommendation strength: Weak; certainty of evidence: Low.

Comments

Protein accounts for approximately one‐third of bone mass and is a key structural component of the skeleton [22]. Adequate protein intake is essential for maintaining overall health and bone integrity. Evidence indicates that higher dietary protein intake is associated with improved bone health and reduced fracture risk, especially among older adults [23].

An updated systematic review [24, 25] synthesized 14 studies published up to 2023, including randomized controlled trials and prospective cohort studies, with a total of 239,039 participants worldwide. Compared with the recommended dietary allowance of 0.8 g/kg/day, increased protein intake among older adults was associated with a lower risk of hip fracture (hazard ratio: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.81–0.92) and with improvements in total hip and femoral neck bone mineral density. Notably, animal protein intake demonstrated a stronger positive association with bone density compared with plant protein.

Based on this evidence, we recommend that the general population—and particularly older adults with low body weight—ensure sufficient daily protein intake to reduce the risk of osteoporosis and fragility fractures. The Chinese Dietary Guidelines recommend a daily protein intake of 1.0–1.5 g/kg/day for adults [26]. Within this range, at least half should come from high‐quality animal protein sources, such as fish (two servings per week or 300–500 g), eggs (300–350 g per week), and lean meat or poultry (300–500 g per week). For individuals following vegetarian diets, legumes, soy products, and nuts are recommended to increase protein intake. Patients with chronic diseases requiring protein restriction (e.g., chronic kidney disease) should adjust both the amount and type of protein under medical supervision, with priority given to high‐quality protein sources.

Guideline Question 4: Can Increased Consumption of Fruits and Vegetables Reduce the Risk of Osteoporosis?

Recommendation 4

We suggest the consumption of fruits and vegetables rich in vitamin K and vitamin C to reduce the risk of osteoporosis.

Recommendation strength: Weak; certainty of evidence: Low.

Comments

Fruits and vegetables provide essential vitamins that support metabolism, immune function, and growth. Vitamin C and vitamin K are particularly associated with bone metabolism. Good dietary sources of vitamin C include bell peppers, broccoli, cauliflower, and citrus fruits, while green leafy vegetables (e.g., spinach, kale), cauliflower, and berries such as blueberries and strawberries are rich in vitamin K. Vitamin C contributes to bone health through its antioxidant properties and by supporting amino acid hydroxylation critical for collagen formation. Vitamin K, especially vitamin K2, is essential for bone turnover and mineralization [27, 28].

A recent systematic review of 16 studies (6425 participants) published up to 2022 evaluated the effect of dietary vitamin K2 on osteoporosis prevention, including case–control and randomized controlled trials across diverse populations [29]. Meta‐analysis indicated that vitamin K2 supplementation positively affects total body and lumbar spine bone mineral density and reduces fracture risk (relative risk [RR] = 0.38; 95% CI: 0.20–0.76) compared with control groups.

Regarding vitamin C, a systematic review of 17 studies (19,484 participants) published up to 2020 showed that higher intake of vitamin C–rich foods was associated with reduced risk of osteoporosis and related fractures, as well as improvements in bone mineral density at the lumbar spine and femoral neck (RR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.48–0.92) [30].

According to multiple guideline recommendations, the daily intake of vitamin C for adults is 110 mg/day, increasing to 155–160 mg/day for older adults, individuals with obesity, pregnant women, and smokers. The recommended intake of vitamin K is 80 μg/day, increasing to 85 μg/day for lactating women [31]. Notably, fruit or vegetable juices should not be considered substitutes for whole fruits and vegetables. We recommend that the general public increase consumption of fruits and vegetables rich in vitamin K and vitamin C to support bone health and reduce osteoporosis and fracture risk.

Guideline Question 5: Can Increased Dairy Intake Reduce the Risk of Osteoporosis?

Recommendation 5

We recommend daily consumption of adequate dairy products to reduce the risk of osteoporosis.

Recommendation strength: Strong; certainty of evidence: Moderate.

Comments

Dairy products are one of the five core food groups and provide essential nutrients for bone health, including calcium, phosphorus, and protein. Evidence indicates that higher dairy intake can improve bone mineralization, enhance muscle mass, and reduce fall risk, contributing to osteoporosis prevention and fracture risk reduction [32].

A systematic review including six randomized controlled trials (618 participants) conducted globally up to 2021—covering postmenopausal women from Europe, North America, Asia, and Australia—demonstrated that increased dairy intake was associated with maintenance and improvement of bone mineral density compared with low or no dairy consumption [33]. The standardized mean difference (SMD) for total body bone mineral density improvement was 0.58 (95% CI: 0.37–0.77) compared with placebo or no dairy intake. The effect of dairy intake on bone density was time‐dependent: improvements in hip bone density were observed after 12 months of continuous consumption, and improvements in femoral neck bone density appeared after 18 months.

According to the Chinese Dietary Guidelines, adults should consume 300–500 mL of dairy products per day to maintain health [26]. The U.S. Dietary Guidelines recommend at least three servings (~720 mL) of low‐fat or fat‐free dairy daily for osteoporosis prevention [34]. Therefore, we recommend a total daily dairy intake of at least 300 mL. For individuals who are obese, have metabolic syndrome, or cardiovascular disease, low‐fat or fat‐free dairy products are preferred. For those with lactose intolerance, alternatives such as aged dairy products (e.g., yogurt, hard cheese), plant‐based dairy (e.g., soy milk, almond milk, coconut milk), or lactose‐free milk are recommended.

Guideline Question 6: Can Exercise Reduce the Risk of Osteoporosis?

Recommendation 6

We recommend regular participation in exercise to improve bone mineral density and reduce the risk of osteoporosis.

Recommendation strength: Strong; certainty of evidence: Low.

Comments

Exercise, as a key component of a healthy lifestyle, has demonstrated beneficial effects on musculoskeletal health and overall well‐being. Regular physical activity can prevent or mitigate various common conditions, including cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, and respiratory diseases [35]. Exercise not only enhances muscle strength and reduces fall and fracture risk, but also improves bone mineral density, thereby lowering osteoporosis risk [36].

A recent systematic review of 80 observational studies involving 5581 postmenopausal women up to 2023 indicated that exercise improved bone mineral density across multiple skeletal sites. Compared with no or low‐intensity activity, exercise interventions were associated with an increase in overall bone mineral density (standardized mean difference [SMD] = 0.29; 95% CI: 0.16–0.42) [37]. Another systematic review including 59 studies (1560 participants) published up to 2020 reported that exercise improved bone mineral density in individuals aged ≥ 65 years, with an overall effect of SMD = 0.15 (95% CI: 0.05–0.25) [38].

Various forms of exercise, including Tai Chi [39], resistance training [40], and whole‐body vibration [41, 42], were found to improve bone density and reduce osteoporosis risk.

We recommend that the general public, particularly high‐risk populations such as older adults and postmenopausal women, engage in exercise sessions of at least 60 min, 2–3 times per week. Prior to initiating an exercise program, individuals should assess their health status and select appropriate exercise types and intensity. Low‐risk individuals may perform resistance training, cycling, running, or brisk walking. High‐risk individuals may prefer lower‐intensity or aerobic activities such as brisk walking, Tai Chi, or low‐frequency vibration exercise. Individuals with cardiovascular or other health conditions should consider walking, slow jogging, or other aerobic activities, adjusting intensity accordingly.

Guideline Question 7: Can Smoking Cessation Reduce the Risk of Osteoporosis?

Recommendation 7

We recommend that smokers completely quit smoking to reduce the risk of osteoporosis.

Recommendation strength: Strong; certainty of evidence: Low.

Comments

Tobacco contains multiple harmful substances that adversely affect the respiratory, cardiovascular, and musculoskeletal systems. Certain components, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons [43] and cadmium [44], can directly impact bone cells, inhibiting bone formation and reducing bone mineral density [45, 46]. Additionally, smoking can impair cardiopulmonary function, leading to reduced physical activity and disuse‐related osteoporosis [47, 48].

A systematic review of 27 cohort studies including 1,612,364 participants worldwide (from the Americas, Europe, and Asia) published up to 2022 demonstrated that smoking is associated with an increased risk of osteoporotic fractures [49]. Compared with non‐smokers, male smokers had a relative risk (RR) of 1.37 (95% CI: 1.22–1.53), indicating a 37% higher fracture risk. Further analysis showed that women who smoked more than 15 cigarettes per day had a 30% increased risk of fracture compared with never‐smokers [50].

Therefore, we recommend complete smoking cessation to reduce the risk of osteoporosis and fractures, particularly among older adults. Smoking cessation includes, but is not limited to, traditional tobacco, electronic cigarettes, and other tobacco‐containing products.

Guideline Question 8: Can Reducing Alcohol Consumption Reduce the Risk of Osteoporosis?

Recommendation 8

We recommend reducing alcohol intake or complete abstinence to lower the risk of osteoporosis.

Recommendation strength: Strong; certainty of evidence: Moderate.

Comments

Alcohol is a well‐established risk factor for osteoporosis [51]. Excessive alcohol consumption can impair nutrient absorption and disrupt metabolic processes. Long‐term alcohol intake has been shown to damage bone microarchitecture and inhibit bone cell metabolism and differentiation [52].

A systematic review including 19 studies (287,787 participants) published up to 2022, covering populations from the Americas, Europe, Asia, and Australia, found that alcohol consumption significantly increased osteoporosis risk [53]. Compared with never‐drinkers, consuming three standard alcoholic drinks per day (~24 g of ethanol, equivalent to 60–75 mL of spirits, 1000–1500 mL of beer, or 420–480 mL of red wine) was associated with a 33% increased risk of hip fracture (RR = 1.33; 95% CI: 1.04–1.69). Consumption of four or more standard drinks per day further increased hip fracture risk by 59% (RR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.23–2.05).

Alcohol adversely affects bone health through multiple mechanisms, including increasing the risk of falls and osteonecrosis [54]. Evidence from the Global Burden of Disease study indicates that there is no safe threshold for alcohol consumption and that total health benefits are maximized with abstinence [55].

Guideline Question 9: Can Tea Consumption Reduce the Risk of Osteoporosis?

Recommendation 9

We suggest moderate tea consumption to reduce the risk of osteoporosis.

Recommendation strength: Weak; certainty of evidence: Moderate.

Comments

Tea is one of the most widely consumed beverages worldwide and contains bioactive compounds such as polyphenols and catechins, which have demonstrated preventive and therapeutic effects for various conditions [56, 57]. Certain active compounds in tea have been shown to promote osteogenesis and bone remodeling [58]. Habitual tea consumption is positively associated with bone mineral density, although its association with fracture risk remains less clear [59]. Tea polyphenols and related compounds are being investigated for potential applications in osteoporosis prevention [60].

A recent systematic review of 40 observational studies including 947,865 participants worldwide up to 2024 reported that tea consumption was associated with increased bone mineral density and reduced risk of osteoporosis and fractures [61]. Compared with non‐tea drinkers, tea consumption was associated with a standardized mean difference (SMD) of 0.33 (95% CI: 0.21–0.46) for bone mineral density, a relative risk (RR) of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.67–0.95) for osteoporosis, and an RR of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.84–0.98) for fractures. Further analysis indicated that tea drinkers had a lower risk of hip, femoral, and lumbar fractures, but no significant association was observed for forearm, wrist, phalangeal, or femoral neck fractures. Different types of tea, including black and green tea, were associated with higher overall bone mineral density, particularly among postmenopausal women [62]. Study populations included Asian and European cohorts, with a predominance of Asian participants.

We recommend moderate daily tea consumption, including green or black tea, with a suggested intake of 200–1000 mL per day to support bone health and reduce osteoporosis risk.

Guideline Question 10: Can Coffee Consumption Reduce the Risk of Osteoporosis?

Recommendation 10

We suggest against using coffee consumption as a strategy to reduce the risk of osteoporosis.

Recommendation strength: Weak; certainty of evidence: Very low.

Comments

Coffee is a widely consumed beverage containing multiple bioactive compounds, including caffeine, which have demonstrated antioxidant, anticancer, and cardioprotective effects. Previous studies suggest that caffeine intake does not have a significant adverse effect on bone health [63]. However, the association between coffee consumption and osteoporosis risk remains controversial.

A systematic review of 17 studies including 399,070 participants published up to 2022 analyzed the relationship between coffee intake and osteoporosis or fracture risk [64]. Results indicated that coffee consumption was associated with a reduced overall risk of osteoporosis, but no significant association was observed with site‐specific osteoporosis or fracture risk. Further analysis suggested a non‐linear U‐shaped relationship: daily consumption of 2–4 cups of coffee was associated with a modest reduction in overall osteoporosis risk. Low coffee intake showed no association, whereas excessive consumption may be detrimental to bone health.

Although a specific range of coffee intake might be linked to a slight reduction in overall osteoporosis risk, the effective window is narrow and overconsumption may harm bone health. Therefore, we do not recommend using coffee consumption as a strategy to prevent osteoporosis. For individuals who habitually consume coffee, it is advisable to limit total daily intake.

Guideline Question 11: Can Weight Management Reduce the Risk of Osteoporosis?

Recommendation 11

We suggest maintaining a healthy body weight to reduce the risk of osteoporosis and fractures.

Recommendation strength: Weak; certainty of evidence: Low.

Comments

Body weight is an important indicator of nutritional status. Low body weight (BMI < 18 kg/m2) is often associated with malnutrition and bone loss, whereas high body weight (BMI > 25 kg/m2) may indicate overnutrition or metabolic disturbances. Evidence shows that persistent low body weight is linked to lower bone mineral density and higher risk of osteoporosis and fractures [65], while the relationship between overweight/obesity and osteoporosis or fracture risk is more complex [66]. The International Osteoporosis Foundation recommends an adult BMI of 20–25 kg/m2 to minimize osteoporosis risk.

A systematic review including 17 studies (14,956 participants) up to 2020 reported that obese adults had higher lumbar spine and femoral neck bone mineral density compared with individuals of healthy weight, possibly due to greater nutrient intake and mechanical loading on bones [67]. Regarding fractures, a 2023 systematic review of 23 cohort studies (3,456,631 participants) found that obesity was not associated with overall fracture risk but was linked to increased risk of weight‐bearing site fractures, such as the spine; each 10 cm increase in waist circumference was associated with a 3% higher risk of vertebral fracture [68]. For underweight individuals, a 2023 systematic review of eight studies (10,612 participants) showed a significantly higher risk of osteoporosis [69].

In summary, both underweight and obesity adversely affect bone health. We recommend maintaining a healthy weight (BMI 20–24 kg/m2) through a balanced diet and regular exercise. Underweight individuals should increase intake of protein, vitamins, and dairy products, whereas overweight or obese individuals should adopt lifestyle interventions to manage weight and optimize overall health.

Guideline Question 12: Can Combined Calcium and Vitamin D Supplementation Reduce the Risk of Osteoporosis?

Recommendation 12

We suggest combined calcium and vitamin D supplementation for individuals with insufficient vitamin D intake to reduce the risk of osteoporosis.

Recommendation strength: Weak; certainty of evidence: Low.

Comments

Calcium and vitamin D have long been considered fundamental for the prevention and treatment of osteoporosis. Calcium absorption in the intestine is regulated by vitamin D3. Evidence indicates that calcium supplementation alone does not significantly reduce the risk of osteoporosis or fractures [70, 71]; however, combined supplementation of calcium and vitamin D can significantly improve bone structure [72].

A systematic review including 15 randomized controlled trials (78,206 participants) up to 2021 evaluated the effects of combined calcium and vitamin D supplementation on bone mineral density in postmenopausal women [73]. Pooled analysis showed that supplementation within the recommended daily dose (400 IU of vitamin D) improved hip bone mineral density and reduced fracture risk in postmenopausal women, whereas higher doses beyond the recommended level did not confer additional benefit.

Based on these findings, we recommend combined calcium and vitamin D supplementation for specific populations at risk of deficiency, including individuals with vitamin D levels below normal, those consuming less than 400 IU/day (e.g., due to poor dietary intake or absorption), and individuals with limited sunlight exposure. The Chinese Dietary Guidelines recommend 400 IU/day of vitamin D for adults, and 600–800 IU/day for elderly, pregnant, or lactating women [26]. Supplementation can be achieved through combined micronutrient tablets or vitamin D‐ and calcium‐rich foods (e.g., salmon, sardines, mushrooms, dairy products). For individuals with sufficient calcium and vitamin D intake, combined supplementation is not recommended for osteoporosis prevention.

Guideline Question 13: Can Increased Sunlight Exposure Reduce the Risk of Osteoporosis?

Recommendation 13

We recommend increasing sunlight exposure to reduce the risk of osteoporosis.

Recommendation strength: Strong; certainty of evidence: Very low.

Comments

Sunlight is a primary source of vitamin D for humans. When the skin is exposed to sunlight, precursor molecules in the epidermis are converted into active vitamin D3 under ultraviolet (UV) radiation, providing approximately 70%–80% of the body's vitamin D requirements [74]. Insufficient sunlight exposure is associated with various endocrine disorders and bone loss, particularly during childhood, which increases the risk of rickets [75]. Preclinical studies also demonstrate that increased sunlight exposure, particularly UV radiation, can improve bone metabolism and bone mineral density [76].

A systematic review including three cohort studies (58,754 participants) completed globally up to 2023 analyzed the effect of sunlight exposure on osteoporosis risk. The populations studied were mainly Asian, with some European cohorts. Compared with short or no sunlight exposure, prolonged sunlight exposure was associated with a lower risk of osteoporosis (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.89–0.95). Among individuals with vitamin D insufficiency, increased daily sunlight exposure reduced fracture risk by 48% compared to low sunlight exposure [77].

For adequate vitamin D synthesis, adults generally require 10–20 min of sunlight exposure daily. We recommend that the public ensure sufficient sunlight exposure to reduce osteoporosis risk, particularly for residents in high‐latitude regions with limited winter sunlight. To optimize UV‐mediated vitamin D synthesis, it is advisable to expose the face and limbs without sunscreen. However, to minimize the risk of sunburn or other skin disorders, exposure should avoid peak UV periods or extreme heat, such as midday sunlight.

Guideline Question 14: How Can Osteoporosis Patients Reduce Fracture Risk?

Recommendation 14a

We suggest that individuals with osteoporosis implement lifestyle interventions while initiating anti‐osteoporosis pharmacotherapy as early as possible.

Recommendation strength: Weak; certainty of evidence: Low.

Comments

Fracture is the most serious adverse outcome of osteoporosis and constitutes a primary target for prevention and treatment. Due to bone loss and increased bone fragility, even minor trauma can result in fractures in patients with osteoporosis [78]. Fracture prevention involves both improving bone quality and minimizing exposure to additional risk factors.

Pharmacological treatment is a cornerstone of osteoporosis management. As this guideline is primarily intended for population‐level risk management and public health education, the following section provides an overview of pharmacological options rather than detailed clinical treatment algorithms. Currently, multiple classes of anti‐osteoporosis medications are available, including bisphosphonates (alendronate, ibandronate, risedronate, zoledronate), parathyroid hormone analogs (teriparatide, abaloparatide), receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL) inhibitors (denosumab), selective estrogen receptor modulators (raloxifene, bazedoxifene), and sclerostin inhibitors (romosozumab) [79, 80, 81]. Evidence demonstrates that long‐term, consistent use of these agents improves bone mineral density and reduces fracture risk [82, 83].

A recent systematic review pooling 70 global studies up to 2023 evaluated the efficacy of various anti‐osteoporosis agents (Table 3) [84]. The analysis confirmed that currently recommended first‐line medications have significant fracture prevention benefits [85]. However, these therapies are associated with potential adverse events, including dizziness, headache, nausea, atrial fibrillation, and osteonecrosis of the jaw [84].

Recommendation 14b

We recommend that individuals with osteoporosis implement fall prevention strategies—including optimizing the living environment, enhancing health education, and strengthening care—alongside lifestyle interventions and anti‐osteoporosis therapy.

Recommendation strength: Strong; certainty of evidence: Low.

Comments

Falls are the most immediate precipitating factor for fractures in patients with osteoporosis; therefore, fall prevention is a crucial component of fracture risk reduction [86]. Effective fall‐prevention interventions include patient education, environmental modifications, and structured care programs.

A systematic review of 21 global studies (5293 participants) up to 2023 demonstrated that home‐based fall‐prevention interventions reduced the overall fall rate by approximately 26%, and by 38% among high‐risk individuals [87]. Another systematic review evaluating the impact of health education and care on fall prevention in older adults indicated that structured education and care significantly lowered fall risk compared with standard care [88]. Emerging wearable technologies also show promise in gait monitoring and fall detection, though further validation of sensitivity is required [89].

Discussion

With the increasing aging population and shifts in modern lifestyles, the incidence of osteoporosis (OP) is rising year by year. On one hand, the relatively low awareness of OP among the general public limits early diagnosis and treatment; on the other hand, public‐facing information on OP risk management is often inconsistent and lacks practical applicability, which hinders effective self‐management of risk. This guideline, based on evidence‐based medicine, summarizes the latest evidence on OP screening, risk assessment, and disease management, providing the public with concise, understandable, and actionable recommendations for OP risk management. Moreover, the guideline aligns with the concepts of early disease management and integration of medical care with prevention, which is significant for reducing OP incidence and preventing fractures.

These limitations should be considered when interpreting the strength and generalizability of the recommendations, particularly in the context of application to the Chinese general population. Several limitations of this guideline should be noted:

Most of the evidence included was derived from observational studies, which cannot establish causal relationships;

Certain lifestyle and occupational exposures, such as prolonged or intensive weight‐bearing labor, which may increase the risk of vertebral compression fractures, were not addressed;

Certain lifestyle and occupational exposures, such as prolonged or intensive weight‐bearing labor, which may increase the risk of vertebral compression fractures, were not addressed;

Certain reproductive and postpartum practices that may influence bone health and the risk of postmenopausal osteoporosis could not be addressed because of insufficient epidemiological evidence.

Certain reproductive and postpartum practices that may influence bone health and the risk of postmenopausal osteoporosis could not be addressed because of insufficient epidemiological evidence.

Furthermore, this guideline primarily targets populations at risk of OP and does not provide in‐depth guidance for individuals with complex osteoporosis accompanied by comorbidities or severe secondary osteoporosis‐related fractures. Such patients are advised to seek timely medical care for individualized evaluation and management by qualified healthcare professionals.
