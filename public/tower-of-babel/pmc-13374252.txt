Recovery trajectories of spatiotemporal gait after bipolar hemiarthroplasty versus closed reduction and internal fixation for unstable intertrochanteric fracture: a prospective wearable-sensor study
Authors: Po-An Chen, Shih-Hui Peng, Sheng-Hsun Lee, Tui-Lin Yen, Yi-Hsun Yu, Yu-Chih Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeriatric hip fractures, particularly unstable intertrochanteric fractures, pose a significant global health challenge with high morbidity and mortality. The optimal surgical management remains debated, with both Closed Reduction and Internal Fixation (CRIF) and Bipolar Hemiarthroplasty (BHA) being widely accepted treatment options. However, there is a conspicuous paucity of objective, quantitative data comparing the longitudinal gait recovery trajectories following these two distinct surgical approaches. This study aimed to leverage validated wearable inertial sensors to systematically characterize and compare the spatiotemporal gait recovery patterns of elderly patients undergoing CRIF versus BHA for unstable intertrochanteric fractures.MethodsThis prospective cohort study enrolled 48 patients aged > 70 years with unstable intertrochanteric fractures (AO/OTA 31-A2/A3). Patients were allocated to Group A (CRIF with PFNA, n = 21) or Group B (BHA, n = 27) based on surgeon preference and patient factors. Baseline frailty was assessed using the modified Frailty Index (mFI) and it was comparable between 2 groups. A comprehensive battery of spatiotemporal gait parameters was objectively quantified using validated Gait Up Physilog® 5 wearable inertial sensors at 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively.ResultsAt 3 months postoperatively, the BHA group demonstrated markedly superior gait parameters compared to the CRIF group, including significantly higher cadence (82.9 ± 27.3 vs. 51.7 ± 22.3 steps/min; p = 0.025) and shorter gait cycle time (1.6 ± 0.5 s vs. 2.7 ± 1.0 s; p = 0.028), reflecting a more confident and fluid gait pattern in the BHA cohort. However, this functional advantage attenuated by 6 months, as the CRIF group exhibited a compensatory "catch-up" phenomenon, achieving comparable gait parameters (p > 0.05) once fracture consolidation was established. No postoperative complications were observed in either group during the study period.ConclusionsWhile both BHA and CRIF are effective treatments for unstable intertrochanteric fractures, BHA offers a clinically meaningful advantage in early-to-mid-term gait recovery. The immediate intrinsic stability provided by arthroplasty facilitates earlier return to confident ambulation, which is of paramount importance for frail geriatric patients with limited physiological reserve. For select high-risk patients, BHA may be preferred to mitigate risks of prolonged immobility and optimize functional recovery.Level of EvidenceLevel III, Therapeutic Study.Keywords: Intertrochanteric fracture, Gait analysis, Bipolar hemiarthroplasty, Internal fixation, Wearable sensors, Geriatric trauma

Background

Geriatric hip fractures constitute one of the most pressing orthopedic challenges of the twenty-first century, driven by the exponential growth of the aging population worldwide and the concomitant rise in osteoporosis-related fragility fractures [1, 2]. A comprehensive understanding of the current management paradigms for proximal femoral fractures in the elderly—encompassing early surgical intervention, multidisciplinary perioperative care, and evidence-based thromboprophylaxis—is essential for optimizing patient outcomes [3]. The clinical burden of these injuries extends far beyond the immediate surgical intervention; hip fractures in the elderly are independently associated with a substantial increase in one-year mortality rates, ranging from 20 to 30%, and frequently precipitate a cascade of functional decline that permanently diminishes quality of life [4–6]. Notably, advanced age, male gender, dementia, multiple comorbidities, hypoalbuminemia, and being underweight have been identified as independent predictors of one-year postoperative mortality following fragile intertrochanteric fracture surgery, underscoring the critical role of preoperative health optimization in this vulnerable population [7]. The pathophysiological consequences of prolonged postoperative immobility—including venous thromboembolism, hospital-acquired pneumonia, pressure ulcers, and accelerated sarcopenia—underscore the paramount importance of early mobilization as a cornerstone of contemporary hip fracture management [8]. Furthermore, the involvement of a dedicated orthogeriatrician in the perioperative management of elderly hip fracture patients has been shown to improve haemoglobin levels at discharge and reduce the need for blood transfusions, particularly in patients undergoing hemiarthroplasty [9]. Indeed, emerging evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses has consistently demonstrated that expedited return to ambulation is a critical determinant of favorable functional outcomes and reduced complication rates in this vulnerable population [10].

The assessment of rehabilitation outcomes following hip fracture surgery has traditionally relied upon patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), which, despite their widespread adoption, are inherently limited by subjectivity, recall bias, and ceiling effects that may obscure meaningful differences in functional recovery [11]. In contrast, objective gait analysis offers a quantitative and reproducible method for evaluating the biomechanical quality of ambulation, thereby providing clinicians with actionable insights into specific gait impairments—such as asymmetric stride patterns, reduced walking velocity, or abnormal weight distribution—that may be amenable to targeted rehabilitative interventions [12, 13]. Despite the theoretical appeal of gait analysis as an outcome measure in geriatric hip fracture research, the existing literature remains surprisingly sparse, with most studies limited to cross-sectional designs or single time-point assessments that fail to capture the dynamic nature of functional recovery.

Spatiotemporal gait analysis, which quantifies fundamental parameters including walking speed, cadence, stride length, and gait cycle timing, represents the most clinically accessible approach to objective ambulation assessment. Historically, gold-standard technologies such as three-dimensional motion capture systems and instrumented walkways (e.g., GAITRite®) have been employed to derive these metrics with high precision [14, 15]. However, the substantial capital investment, specialized laboratory infrastructure, and technical expertise required for these modalities render them impractical for routine longitudinal monitoring in typical clinical settings [16]. The advent of wearable inertial measurement units (IMUs) has fundamentally transformed this landscape by enabling accurate, portable, and cost-effective gait assessment outside the confines of the gait laboratory. These miniaturized sensor systems, which integrate accelerometers, gyroscopes, and magnetometers, have been validated against established reference standards and are increasingly recognized as viable tools for tracking recovery trajectories in orthopedic populations [17, 18].

From a surgical perspective, the optimal management of unstable intertrochanteric fractures (AO/OTA classification 31-A2 and 31-A3) in elderly patients remains a subject of considerable controversy. Two fundamentally distinct treatment philosophies have emerged: Closed Reduction and Internal Fixation (CRIF), most commonly performed using cephalomedullary nails such as the Proximal Femoral Nail Antirotation (PFNA), and primary Bipolar Hemiarthroplasty (BHA) [19]. Each approach carries inherent advantages and limitations. CRIF preserves the native hip joint architecture and represents a less invasive option; however, it is predicated upon successful fracture healing and carries a non-negligible risk of mechanical complications—including helical blade cut-out, varus collapse, and nonunion—particularly in patients with severely osteoporotic bone or highly comminuted fracture patterns [20]. Recent biomechanical evidence has demonstrated that newer cephalomedullary nail designs with superior locking mechanisms may offer comparable construct stability to standard long nails for complex trochanteric fractures, potentially reducing surgical invasiveness [21]. Conversely, BHA circumvents the biological uncertainties of fracture healing by replacing the proximal femur entirely, thereby providing immediate mechanical stability and theoretically permitting earlier unrestricted weight-bearing [22]. Notably, when internal fixation fails in intertrochanteric fractures, salvage hip arthroplasty with classification-based femoral stem selection has been shown to reduce the risk of periprosthetic fractures and improve postoperative outcomes [23]. Prior comparative studies have yielded conflicting conclusions regarding functional outcomes, reoperation rates, and mortality between these two modalities, with heterogeneous patient populations, variable follow-up durations, and reliance on subjective outcome measures contributing to this ambiguity [24]. For reverse oblique intertrochanteric fractures (AO/OTA 31A3) specifically, comparative studies of different intramedullary nail systems have reported variable radiographic and clinical outcomes, further highlighting the complexity of implant selection in these challenging fracture patterns [25]. Similarly, short-term comparative analyses of different nail designs for pertrochanteric fractures have shown equivalent outcomes despite differences in implant cost, emphasizing the need for functional outcome measures beyond radiographic assessment [26].

Critically, while numerous investigations have compared clinical scores and complication rates between CRIF and BHA, there exists a conspicuous paucity of data specifically examining how objective spatiotemporal gait parameters evolve over time following each surgical approach. Elucidating the distinct recovery trajectories associated with these two treatment strategies is essential for informing evidence-based surgical decision-making and for developing individualized rehabilitation protocols tailored to the unique biomechanical demands of each procedure. Therefore, the primary aim of this prospective cohort study was to employ validated wearable inertial sensors to systematically characterize and compare the longitudinal spatiotemporal gait recovery trajectories of elderly patients undergoing Bipolar Hemiarthroplasty versus Closed Reduction and Internal Fixation for unstable intertrochanteric fractures.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective cohort study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB). All patients aged > 70 years presenting with unstable intertrochanteric fractures between January 2021 and December 2023 were screened for eligibility. Fractures were classified according to the AO/OTA classification, and inclusion was strictly limited to unstable patterns, specifically types 31-A2.2, 31-A2.3, and all 31-A3 subtypes.

Patients met the exclusion criteria if they presented with: (1) cognitive impairment, defined as a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score < 26 or delirium—a threshold selected to ensure that participants could reliably comprehend and comply with the standardized gait assessment protocol and the postoperative partial weight-bearing instructions; (2) pre-injury immobility (bedridden status) or severe musculoskeletal disorders affecting gait; (3) severe unstable medical conditions; or (4) concomitant fractures influencing rehabilitation.

Based on these criteria, a total of 48 patients were enrolled and underwent comprehensive clinical, radiological, and gait evaluations. Participants were divided into two groups based on the surgical treatment received: Group A (n = 21) underwent Closed Reduction and Internal Fixation (CRIF) with a proximal femoral nail (PFN), and Group B (n = 27) underwent Bipolar Hemiarthroplasty (BHA). For all BHA cases, a calcar-replacement bipolar femoral stem (United GTF, United Orthopedic Corporation, Hsinchu, Taiwan) was used; its calcar-substituting geometry with proximal porous coating provides immediate axial and rotational stability in the setting of deficient medial calcar support typical of unstable intertrochanteric fractures, permitting full weight-bearing without dependence on fracture healing. Clinical assessment and data collection

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were collected upon admission (Table 1). These included age, gender, Body Mass Index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). The modified Frailty Index (mFI), calculated from 11 preoperative variables derived from the Canadian Study of Health and Aging, was used to quantify baseline frailty and enhance the international translatability of our findings [27]. To thoroughly evaluate the patients’ preoperative functional and physiological status, a Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) was performed. This included the Vulnerable Elders Survey-13 (VES-13) for vulnerability, Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) for mood, Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) for delirium, and the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA). Functional status was assessed using the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale and the Short Form-36 (SF-36). Additionally, sarcopenia parameters were evaluated via handgrip strength and muscle mass measurements to account for baseline physical frailty.

Surgical intervention

All surgeries were performed by experienced orthopedic surgeons specializing in joint reconstruction and trauma. The surgical constructs used for unstable intertrochanteric fractures are illustrated in Fig. 2. In Group A (CRIF), patients were positioned supine on a standard radiolucent fracture table. Closed reduction was achieved under fluoroscopic guidance. Fixation was performed using the Proximal Femoral Nail Antirotation (PFNA) system (Synthes, Oberdorf, Switzerland). In cases where severe osteoporosis was identified intraoperatively, polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) cement augmentation was injected through the perforated blade to enhance anchorage and prevent cutout.

Schematic illustration of surgical treatment options for unstable intertrochanteric fractures. A Closed Reduction and Internal Fixation (CRIF) with Proximal Femoral Nail Antirotation (PFNA). The PFNA system consists of an intramedullary nail with a helical blade providing rotational stability within the femoral head. Cerclage wires are applied to secure comminuted trochanteric fragments. B Bipolar Hemiarthroplasty (BHA) with United GTF stem. A cemented calcar-replacement stem is inserted into the femoral canal with a bipolar head articulating within the native acetabulum. Cerclage wires stabilize the greater trochanter fragments

In Group B (BHA), surgeries were performed via a direct lateral approach with the patient in the lateral decubitus position. The United Bipolar Hemiarthroplasty system (United Orthopedic Corporation, Hsinchu, Taiwan) was utilized for all patients. All patients received a cemented United GTF calcar-replacement stem to ensure immediate axial and rotational stability. In both groups, anatomical reduction and stability of the greater trochanter were prioritized. Cerclage wiring was employed strictly when necessary to secure comminuted trochanteric fragments and restore the abductor mechanism.

Postoperative rehabilitation

Rehabilitation protocols were standardized across both groups to minimize bias. Physiotherapy commenced on the first postoperative day with bedside muscle-strengthening exercises and range-of-motion training. Although weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) is the prevailing standard of care following intertrochanteric fracture fixation in many Western centers, the treating surgeons at our institution adopted a more conservative approach for this specific cohort of patients with unstable fracture configurations (AO/OTA 31-A2/A3). Given the inherent instability of these fracture patterns and the high prevalence of severe osteoporosis in our elderly population, a protective partial weight-bearing (PWB) protocol with the assistance of a walker was implemented for the first six weeks postoperatively in both groups to safeguard the integrity of the trochanteric reconstruction and minimize the risk of early mechanical failure [8]. Progression to full weight-bearing was permitted thereafter, contingent upon clinical assessment and radiographic evidence of stability.

Gait analysis equipment

Objective gait parameters were quantified using the Gait Up Physilog® 5 wearable inertial sensors (Gait Up, Lausanne, Switzerland). This device is a standalone, 10-degree-of-freedom Micro-Electro-Mechanical System (MEMS) inertial measurement unit (IMU) comprising a 3D accelerometer, 3D gyroscope, 3D magnetometer, and a barometric pressure sensor. The sensors are compact (19 g, 47.5 × 26.5 × 10 mm) and wireless, minimizing interference with the patient’s natural movement. The accuracy of the Physilog® 5 system has been previously validated against the GAITRite® electronic walkway system, a clinical gold standard for gait analysis [14].

Gait assessment protocol

A longitudinal prospective assessment was designed to capture the recovery trajectories of the patients. Gait evaluations were conducted at four specific postoperative time points: 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively. During each assessment, one Physilog® 5 sensor was firmly secured to the dorsum of each shoe (paired configuration) to ensure consistent orientation. Patients were instructed to walk along a 20-m straight, level hospital corridor at their self-selected comfortable walking speed.

Given the postoperative rehabilitation protocol, patients were permitted to use a standard walker during the early recovery phase (2 weeks and 1 month) assessments to ensure safety while maintaining partial weight-bearing status. For the late recovery phase (3 months and 6 months), patients walked without aids or with a cane, depending on their functional recovery status. Data were processed using the proprietary Gait Up analysis software to extract key spatiotemporal parameters, including gait cycle time (s), gait speed (m/s), cadence (steps/min), stride length (m), and the stance/swing phase distribution (%).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean with range while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was utilized to assess the normality of the data distribution given the relatively small sample size. To evaluate the recovery trajectories, the independent samples t-test (for normally distributed data) or the Mann–Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data) was used for between-group comparisons at each time point. The paired samples t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed for within-group longitudinal comparisons. Categorical baseline variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical computations were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 48 patients with unstable intertrochanteric fractures were enrolled in the study. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The study cohort comprised 21 patients in the CRIF group (Group A) and 27 patients in the BHA group (Group B). The mean age was 79.5 years (range: 77–82) in Group A and 81.5 years (range: 72–91) in Group B, with no significant differences observed between the two groups.

Both groups exhibited similar baseline profiles regarding BMI, with mean values indicating a generally lean body composition (21.2 kg/m2 in Group A vs. 20.8 kg/m2 in Group B). Regarding surgical risk, the majority of patients in both groups were classified as ASA class III (71.4% in Group A and 77.8% in Group B), reflecting a population with multiple systemic comorbidities. The CCI was also comparable between the groups (5.5 vs. 6.0). The modified Frailty Index (mFI) was 0.18 ± 0.07 in the CRIF group and 0.20 ± 0.08 in the BHA group (p = 0.382), confirming comparable baseline frailty between the two cohorts. Furthermore, preoperative comprehensive geriatric assessments, including nutritional status (MNA), cognitive function (MMSE), and depression screening (GDS), showed no significant disparities. Notably, baseline sarcopenia parameters, including handgrip strength (26.0 kg vs. 24.0 kg) and muscle mass (38.5 kg vs. 36.8 kg), were comparable between the two groups, ensuring that any observed differences in the longitudinal gait recovery (Table 2) could be attributed to the surgical intervention rather than baseline physical frailty.

The longitudinal spatiotemporal gait parameters are detailed in Table 2. During the early recovery phase (2 weeks and 1 month postoperatively), both groups exhibited markedly impaired gait patterns characterized by low cadence, prolonged gait cycle time, elevated stance phase (> 75%), and slow gait speed (< 0.22 m/s), consistent with the use of walking aids and partial weight-bearing protocols.

A significant divergence in recovery trajectories was observed at 3 months postoperatively. The BHA group showed a marked improvement in gait fluidity, achieving a significantly higher cadence compared to the CRIF group (82.9 ± 27.3 vs. 51.7 ± 22.3 steps/min; p = 0.025). Furthermore, the gait cycle time was significantly reduced in the BHA group (1.6 ± 0.5 s) compared to the CRIF group (2.7 ± 1.0 s; p = 0.028), indicating a smoother and more confident stepping rhythm (Fig. 3). Although not statistically significant (p = 0.082), the stance phase ratio in the BHA group improved to 68.9%, approaching normalized values, whereas the CRIF group retained a higher ratio of 76.3%. This suggests that by 3 months, patients treated with BHA were able to tolerate weight-bearing more effectively than those treated with CRIF.

By 6 months, the gap between the two groups narrowed as the CRIF group demonstrated a "catch-up" phenomenon in functional recovery. The gait parameters for the CRIF group improved notably (Cadence: 67.3 steps/min; Speed: 0.39 m/s), becoming comparable to those of the BHA group (Cadence: 80.3 steps/min; Speed: 0.37 m/s). No statistically significant differences were found at this time point (p > 0.05), suggesting that while BHA facilitates faster early-to-mid-term recovery, CRIF patients can achieve similar functional outcomes over a longer duration once fracture union is consolidated (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The principal finding of this prospective wearable sensor-based study is that elderly patients with unstable intertrochanteric fractures treated with Bipolar Hemiarthroplasty demonstrated a significantly accelerated spatiotemporal gait recovery trajectory during the early-to-mid-term postoperative period compared to those managed with Closed Reduction and Internal Fixation. Specifically, our longitudinal analysis revealed a marked recovery divergence at the 3-month milestone, wherein the BHA cohort exhibited statistically superior cadence (p = 0.025) and gait cycle efficiency (p = 0.028). Notably, this functional disparity attenuated by 6 months, as the CRIF group manifested a compensatory "catch-up" phenomenon. These objective biomechanical metrics provide quantitative insights into the temporal dynamics of functional recovery that complement conventional patient-reported outcome instruments.

The observed divergence in recovery trajectories at 3 months can be attributed to biomechanical differences between the two surgical approaches. Bipolar Hemiarthroplasty, by virtue of replacing the fractured proximal femur with a prosthetic construct, confers immediate intrinsic stability that is independent of the biological healing process [22]. This biomechanical advantage was quantitatively reflected in our data by the significantly shorter gait cycle time and the normalized stance-to-swing phase ratio observed in the BHA group at 3 months—parameters that collectively indicate enhanced weight-bearing confidence and reduced compensatory limb loading. By contrast, the functional rehabilitation of patients undergoing CRIF remains linked to the biological process of callus formation and fracture consolidation. Although contemporary cephalomedullary nail systems such as the PFNA provide stable mechanical fixation, patients harboring unstable fracture configurations (AO/OTA 31-A2/3) characteristically adopt a protective "guarded gait" strategy—manifested by diminished cadence and prolonged stance phase duration—as a pain-avoidance mechanism to minimize mechanical stress at the healing fracture site [28]. Our quantitative data substantiate this clinical observation, demonstrating that the CRIF cohort maintained an elevated stance phase ratio (76.3%) at 3 months, indicative of persistent reliance on the contralateral limb for dynamic stability pending radiographic consolidation.

A notable finding of this investigation is the attenuation of the functional advantage conferred by BHA at the 6-month follow-up interval. This temporal convergence aligns with the typical biological timeline of intertrochanteric fracture healing, wherein solid osseous union is typically achieved between 3 and 6 months postoperatively [29]. Upon restoration of mechanical integrity at the fracture site, the pain-mediated inhibitory feedback loop that constrains gait parameters in CRIF patients is effectively resolved, thereby permitting progressive increases in walking velocity and cadence. Prior comparative studies of arthroplasty versus internal fixation for unstable intertrochanteric fractures have reported mixed conclusions regarding long-term functional outcomes [19, 24]. Our longitudinal gait analysis offers a framework for reconciling these mixed findings: while the ultimate functional destination (6-month outcome) may be comparable between treatment modalities, the trajectory of recovery (the "journey") differs substantively. This distinction is particularly relevant in the context of orthogeriatric co-management models, which have been shown to significantly reduce the risk of adverse events—including re-operation, complications, and mortality—within one year of hip fracture surgery, especially in patients with multimorbidity [30]. For frail geriatric patients with limited physiological reserve, the clinical significance of an expedited return to efficient ambulation—as demonstrated in the BHA cohort—is substantial, as prolonged immobility and suboptimal gait patterns are independently associated with accelerated sarcopenia, cardiopulmonary deconditioning, and increased fall risk [31]. Indeed, Chen et al. demonstrated that loss of skeletal muscle mass following hip fracture surgery is predictable by baseline sarcopenia status and reflects poor functional recovery at one year, further underscoring the importance of early mobilization strategies to mitigate muscle wasting in this population [32].

A key strength of this investigation is the use of validated wearable inertial sensors to capture granular spatiotemporal parameters across multiple postoperative time points. Traditional patient-reported outcome measures, including the Harris Hip Score and EQ-5D, are susceptible to recall bias, floor and ceiling effects, and limited sensitivity to detect subtle but clinically meaningful differences in functional recovery [11]. In the present study, parameters such as gait cycle time and stance phase ratio served as sensitive biomechanical biomarkers of "gait confidence"—a construct that encompasses both the mechanical capacity and the psychological willingness to bear weight on the operated extremity. For example, even when gait speed demonstrated trends toward improvement in the CRIF group, the persistently prolonged gait cycle time revealed an underlying hesitancy and compensatory strategy that conventional PROMs would likely fail to detect. These findings validate the utility of wearable sensor technology as a complementary outcome assessment tool in orthopedic clinical trials, offering a continuous and objective "digital phenotype" of patient recovery that may inform real-time clinical decision-making and personalized rehabilitation protocols.

To contextualize the clinical relevance of our findings, it is important to consider the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for key spatiotemporal gait parameters. Previous investigations in geriatric and older adult populations have established small-meaningful-to-substantial change estimates of approximately 0.05–0.14 m/s for gait speed [33], with a minimum detectable change in gait velocity during acute hip-fracture rehabilitation of approximately 0.08 m/s [34]; an analogous, formally validated MCID for cadence in this population has not been firmly established. The between-group difference in cadence observed at 3 months in our study (82.9 vs. 51.7 steps/min, Δ = 31.2 steps/min) is large in both absolute and relative terms (~ 60% higher in BHA), strongly suggesting that the superior gait performance in the BHA cohort represents not merely a statistically significant finding but a clinically meaningful functional advantage. Similarly, while the between-group difference in gait speed at 3 months (0.35 vs. 0.32 m/s, Δ = 0.03 m/s) fell below the small-meaningful-change estimate of ~ 0.05 m/s reported by Perera et al., the composite improvement across multiple gait parameters—including cadence, gait cycle time, and stance phase ratio—collectively reflects a substantively enhanced quality of ambulation in the BHA group during this critical recovery window.

The clinical ramifications of our findings warrant consideration in the context of surgical decision-making for elderly patients with unstable intertrochanteric fractures. While both CRIF and BHA represent viable treatment options with comparable long-term functional outcomes, the accelerated gait recovery trajectory associated with BHA may confer particular advantages for specific patient subgroups. Elderly patients with limited physiological reserve, significant baseline frailty, or medical comorbidities that preclude prolonged rehabilitation may particularly benefit from the immediate mechanical stability afforded by arthroplasty. In this regard, the identification of modifiable risk factors for contralateral refracture—including advanced age, osteoporosis, anemia, and visual impairment—further emphasizes the importance of comprehensive perioperative assessment and targeted secondary fracture prevention strategies in this population [35]. Conversely, for patients with adequate bone quality and favorable fracture configurations, CRIF remains an attractive option that preserves native hip joint anatomy while ultimately achieving equivalent functional outcomes, albeit along a more protracted recovery timeline. These nuanced considerations underscore the importance of individualized surgical planning that integrates patient-specific factors, fracture characteristics, and anticipated rehabilitation trajectories.

A methodological consideration that warrants clarification pertains to the inclusion of AO/OTA 31-A3 (reverse oblique and transverse) fracture patterns in our study cohort. While cephalomedullary nailing is the predominant treatment for 31-A3 fractures, primary arthroplasty has been increasingly adopted as an alternative strategy in select elderly patients with severely comminuted fracture patterns, profound osteoporosis, or compromised greater trochanteric integrity that may preclude reliable internal fixation [19, 22]. The decision to include 31-A3 fractures alongside 31-A2 patterns was predicated upon the shared biomechanical characteristic of trochanteric instability, which distinguishes both subtypes from stable fracture configurations and necessitates similar surgical considerations regarding implant stability and weight-bearing progression. Conversely, AO/OTA 31-B fractures (intracapsular femoral neck fractures) were deliberately excluded because they represent a different pathoanatomical entity with distinct healing biology (risk of avascular necrosis), different standard treatment algorithms (BHA or total hip arthroplasty is the established standard of care for displaced femoral neck fractures in the elderly), and disparate rehabilitation trajectories. Including 31-B fractures would have introduced substantial clinical heterogeneity that would confound the interpretation of gait recovery patterns specific to extracapsular trochanteric fractures.

Several methodological limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the sample size of 48 patients, while adequate for detecting the observed between-group differences at 3 months, may have limited statistical power to identify more subtle differences at earlier time points (2 weeks). Nevertheless, the rigorous application of strict inclusion criteria (exclusively unstable AO/OTA 31-A2/A3 fractures) and the longitudinal study design served to minimize inter-subject variability and enhance internal validity. Second, treatment allocation was non-randomized, with surgical approach determined by surgeon preference and intraoperative assessment of bone quality, thereby introducing potential selection bias. Future randomized controlled trials are warranted to definitively establish causal relationships. Third, our follow-up was limited to 6 months; while this duration was sufficient to characterize the primary gait recovery trajectories, extended surveillance is necessary to evaluate longer-term outcomes including implant-related complications such as prosthetic dislocation, aseptic loosening, and periprosthetic fracture in the BHA group, as well as delayed helical blade cutout, varus collapse, and avascular necrosis in the CRIF group—complications that may not manifest within the first six months but carry significant implications for long-term functional outcomes [20, 29]. Fourth, the exclusion of patients with cognitive impairment (MMSE < 26) limits the generalizability of our findings to the broader population of elderly patients with unstable intertrochanteric fractures. A substantial proportion of geriatric hip fracture patients present with pre-existing dementia or perioperative delirium, and these individuals may exhibit fundamentally different gait recovery trajectories due to impaired motor learning, reduced compliance with rehabilitation protocols, and inability to adhere to partial weight-bearing restrictions. Future studies incorporating simplified gait assessment protocols that can accommodate patients with varying degrees of cognitive impairment are warranted to enhance the external validity of these findings [7]. Finally, the single-center design may limit the generalizability of our findings to other clinical settings and patient populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this prospective wearable sensor-based study demonstrates that while both Bipolar Hemiarthroplasty and Closed Reduction with Internal Fixation represent effective surgical treatments for unstable intertrochanteric fractures in elderly patients, BHA confers a distinct biomechanical advantage in the rapidity of spatiotemporal gait recovery, particularly at the clinically critical 3-month postoperative milestone. The subsequent "catch-up" phenomenon observed in the CRIF group by 6 months suggests that the ultimate functional destination may be comparable, but the recovery trajectory differs meaningfully between treatment modalities. For geriatric patients with limited physiological reserve and heightened vulnerability to the sequelae of prolonged immobility, the immediate mechanical stability provided by arthroplasty may facilitate an earlier return to confident, efficient ambulation. Future adequately powered randomized controlled trials incorporating wearable sensor-based gait analysis are warranted to further validate these recovery trajectories and to establish evidence-based algorithms for personalized surgical decision-making in this challenging patient population.
