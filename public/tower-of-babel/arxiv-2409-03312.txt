Quantum Algorithm For Testing Convexity of Function
Nhat A. Nghiem, Tzu-Chieh Wei

Abstract
Functions are a fundamental object in mathematics, with countless applications to different fields, and are usually classified based on certain properties, given their domains and images. An important property of a real-valued function is its convexity, which plays a very crucial role in many areas, such as thermodynamics and geometry. Motivated by recent advances in quantum computation as well as the quest for quantum advantage, we give a quantum algorithm for testing convexity of polynomial functions, which appears frequently in multiple contexts, such as optimization, machine learning, physics, etc. We show that quantum computers can reveal the convexity property superpolynomially faster than classical computers with respect to number of variables. As a corollary, we provide a significant improvement and extension on quantum Newton’s method constructed in earlier work of Rebentrost et al [New J. Phys. 21 073023 (2019)]. We further discuss our algorithm in a broader context, such as potential application in the study of geometric structure of manifold, testing training landscape of variational quantum algorithm and also gradient descent/Newton’s method for optimization.

In this work, we aim to tackle the challenge of testing the convexity of some polynomial function. We begin with a simple case, which is a homogeneous polynomial of even degree (to be defined later) and subsequently, building upon such homogeneous polynomial, we generalize the construction to arbitrary polynomial type. The kind of homogeneous polynomial of even degree was also considered in

II.2
Dissecting Convexity of Function

We are interested in the convexity of
f
in some domain
\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By trivially redefining the function (e.g., with a coordinate shift), we can choose
\mathscr{D}
to be a hypersphere with radius
1
for simplicity. The following criterion is a well-known result in mathematics and can be found in many standard literature.

Criterion:

Over some domain
\mathscr{D}
, if the Hessian matrix of a given function
f
is positive-semidefinite at every point, then the function
f
is convex.

The application of the above criterion is straightforward. We compute the Hessian matrix of
f
at chosen points in the region and check its spectrum. The positive-semidefiniteness of a matrix is equivalent to all eigenvalues being non-negative. Therefore, the sign of the spectrum is necessary and sufficient for revealing convexity. As we mentioned, the Hessian matrix
H
can be written down explicitly, which is convenient for spectral analysis. As the dimension of
H
grows with the number of variables (there are
n
variables), finding the full spectrum of
H
is computationally demanding. Furthermore, the only information that matters is the sign of the smallest eigenvalue, as we only need to see if it is positive or not. Therefore, we propose to find the
minimum
eigenvalue of
H
. If such an eigenvalue is non-negative, then all other eigenvalues are non-negative, which implies that
H
is positive-semidefinite and hence, the corresponding
f
is convex within the domain
\mathscr{D}
. In the following section, we construct a quantum algorithm to first find and then verify the sign of the smallest eigenvalue, thereby dissecting the convexity of the function
f
, given the details of
f
and related assumptions from the previous section Sec.
II.1
.

III.1
Constructing Hessian
H
for a Single Point

In Sec.
II.2
, we first introduce the general idea behind our work: finding the minimum eigenvalue of
H
. Our first task is to produce the block encoding of
H
that can be used for further analysis. In order to achieve this goal, we recall the following important formulation:

\displaystyle\textbf{H}(\textbf{x})=\Tr_{1,2,...,p-1}\big((M_{H}+M_{D})\ ((\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T})^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n})\big),

(13)

where
M_{H}=2\sum_{j\neq k}^{p}\Theta_{jk}

and
M_{D}=\sum_{m=1}^{p}M_{m}
. The oracle access to entries of each
M_{m}
and
H_{jk}
allows us to use Lemma
13
to produce the
\epsilon
-approximated block encoding of
M_{H}/(sp^{2})
and
M_{D}/(sp)
(where
s
is the sparsity of
A
) in time:

\mathcal{O}\Big(p\big(p\log(n)+\log^{2.5}(\frac{1}{\epsilon})\big)\Big)
.
Thus, it is quite simple to obtain the block encoding of
M_{D}/(sp^{2})
from
M_{D}/(sp)
by multiplying with a factor
1/p
(see Lemma
15
). Lemma
14
then allows us to obtain the
\epsilon
-approximated block encoding of
(M_{H}+M_{D})/(2sp^{2})
.

The next recipe that we need includes the following simple relations.

\displaystyle\Tr_{1}(A\otimes B)(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T}\otimes\mathbb{I})=(\textbf{x}^{T}A\textbf{x})B,

(14)

\displaystyle(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T}\otimes\mathbb{I})(A\otimes B)(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T}\otimes\mathbb{I})=\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T}\otimes(\textbf{x}^{T}A\textbf{x})B.

(15)

Using these properties plus the tensor structure of
M_{D}
and
M_{H}
, we can show that:

\displaystyle(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T})^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}\ (M_{H}+M_{D})\ (\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T})^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}

\displaystyle=(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T})^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}).

(16)

The reason comes from the fact that

\displaystyle(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T})^{\otimes p-1}=\textbf{x}^{\otimes p-1}(\textbf{x}^{T})^{\otimes p-1}

(17)

and that

\displaystyle(\textbf{x}^{T})^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}\ (M_{H}+M_{D})\ \textbf{x}^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}=\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}).

(18)

For now, we assume that we have a block encoding of
(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T})
, as subsequently, we will show how to produce such a block encoding and generalize it to deal with multiple points chosen from the domain
\mathscr{D}
, as required by the convexity criterion. Lemma
12
allows us to use
p-1

(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T})
and a trivial block encoding of
\mathbb{I}_{n}
to obtain the block encoding of
(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T})^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}
. Then Lemma
11
(combined with the simple relations derived above) yields the block encoding of
(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T})^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\textbf{H}(\textbf{x})/(2sp^{2})
.

III.2
Constructing “multi-points” Hessian

III.2.1
For homogeneous polynomial of even degree

We remark that the above construction yields the block encoding of the operator
(\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T})^{\otimes(p-1)}\otimes\textbf{H}(\textbf{x})/(2sp^{2})
, which contains the tensor product of
\textbf{x}\textbf{x}^{T}
and Hessian matrix
H
at a given point
x
. As it will become clearer later, in order to take advantage of quantum parallelism for analyzing the Hessian spectrum at multiple points, we need to adjust the above procedure to construct what we call a “multi-point” Hessian.

For further clarity and to avoid confusion with the above construction, we set the following notation for subsequent discussions. Let
\mathscr{N}
be the number of points of consideration;
\textbf{x}_{i}
(
\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
) is the
i
-th point; the corresponding Hessian of
f
evaluated at
\textbf{x}_{i}
is then
\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})
. The first goal is to produce the block encoding of the following operator
\bigoplus_{i}^{\mathscr{N}}(\textbf{x}_{i}\textbf{x}_{i}^{T})^{\otimes{p-1}}\otimes\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})/(2sp^{2})
, which has the matrix representation as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2sp^{2}}\ \begin{pmatrix}(\textbf{x}_{1}\textbf{x}_{1}^{T})^{\otimes{p-1}}\otimes\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{1})&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\cdots&(\textbf{x}_{2}\textbf{x}_{2}^{T})^{\otimes{p-1}}\otimes\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{2})&\cdots&\cdots\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&(\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}^{T})^{\otimes{p-1}}\otimes\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}})\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(19)

Now, we outline the procedure that produces the desired block encoding. First, we have the following lemma:

Lemma 1

Given block encoding of
(M_{H}+M_{D})/(2sp^{2})
(as constructed in Sec.
III.1
), the block encoding of operator
\bigoplus_{i}^{\mathscr{N}}(M_{H}+M_{D})/(2sp^{2})
can be prepared with extra
\mathcal{O}(1)
cost.

Proof:
To show this, we need to add an extra register of dimension
\mathscr{N}
. The resulting tensor product of
\mathbb{I}_{\mathscr{N}}
and unitary block encoding of
(M_{H}+M_{D})/(2sp^{2})
produces the block encoding of
\mathbb{I}_{\mathscr{N}}\otimes(M_{H}+M_{D})/(2sp^{2})
, which is exactly
\bigoplus_{i}^{\mathscr{N}}(M_{H}+M_{D})/(2sp^{2})
by a simple algebraic property.

Next, we introduce the following crucial recipe:

Lemma 2

The block encoding of the operator
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}(\textbf{x}_{i}\textbf{x}_{i}^{T})^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}
can be prepared in time
\mathcal{O}(p(\log(n\mathscr{N}))
.

Proof:
To prove the above lemma, we first consider a
\log(n\mathscr{N})
qubits system which resides a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
of dimension
n\mathscr{N}
. Let
U
be some unitary operator. Once acting on the basis state
\ket{\bf 0}_{\mathscr{H}}
we obtain the state
\ket{\phi}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle U\ket{\bf 0}_{\mathscr{H}}=\ket{\phi}.

(20)

Let
\ket{\phi}=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n\mathscr{N}})/C
where

C=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{n\mathscr{N}}x_{k}^{2}}

is the normalization factor. In this paper, we work in the real regime, i.e.,
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i=1,2,...,n\mathscr{N}
. If we break such a vector into
\mathscr{N}
parts, and denote each part as
\textbf{x}_{j}
(
j=1,2,...,\mathscr{N})
, e.g.,
\textbf{x}_{j}=(x_{(j-1)n+1},x_{(j-1)n+2},...,x_{(j-1)n+n})
. Note that given such notation, the normalization factor
C
is essentially equal to
C=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}|\textbf{x}_{i}|^{2}
where
|.|^{2}
refers to the usual
l_{2}
norm of a vector. It is then straightforward to decompose
\ket{\phi}
as:
\ket{\phi}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\ket{j}\otimes\textbf{x}_{j}
. Before proceeding, we remark on the unitary
U
that generates the desired state
\ket{\phi}
. We are interested in
\mathscr{N}
points
\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}
. If we choose these
\mathscr{N}
points classically, which means that we know their coordinates plus the norms respectively, then we can use the well-known amplitude encoding method
[
23
,
24
]
to load these entries into a quantum state, resulting in a quantum circuit
U
of depth
\mathcal{O}(\log(n\mathscr{N}))
. On the other hand, if we choose
U
to be a random unitary circuit, then a constraint is imposed on the coordinates of
\ket{\phi}
, which implies
C=1
. In this case, the set of points
\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}
must have their norms summing up to 1, which may reduce the number of points considered in a given domain in one go.

Now we append another ancillary system having dimension
\mathscr{N}
initialized in
\ket{\bf 0}_{\mathscr{N}}
, to obtain the state
\ket{\bf 0}_{\mathscr{N}}\otimes\ket{\phi}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\ket{\bf 0}_{\mathscr{N}}\otimes\ket{j}\otimes\textbf{x}_{j}
. Using CNOT gates to copy the second register to the first one, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\ket{\bf 0}_{\mathscr{N}}\otimes\ket{j}\otimes\textbf{x}_{j}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{C}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\ket{j}_{\mathscr{N}}\otimes\ket{j}\otimes\textbf{x}_{j}.

(21)

If we trace out the first register (with subscript
\mathscr{N}
), we obtain the state
(1/C^{2})\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\ket{j}\bra{j}\ \otimes\ \textbf{x}_{j}\textbf{x}_{j}^{T}
(we recall that it should be
\textbf{x}_{j}\textbf{x}_{j}^{\dagger}
, but since we work in the real regime,
\textbf{x}^{T}
and
\textbf{x}^{\dagger}
are identical). Lemma
10
allows us to prepare the (exact) block encoding of
(1/C^{2})\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\ket{j}\bra{j}\ \otimes\ \textbf{x}_{j}\textbf{x}_{j}^{T}
(in complexity
\mathcal{O}(\log(n\mathscr{N}))
, whose matrix representation is as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\ket{j}\bra{j}\ \otimes\ \textbf{x}_{j}\textbf{x}_{j}^{T}=\frac{1}{C^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{x}_{1}\textbf{x}_{1}^{T}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\textbf{x}_{2}\textbf{x}_{2}^{T}&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}^{T}\end{pmatrix}.

(22)

If
C
is greater than
1
, the factor
C^{2}
from the above representation can be removed using the amplification technique (basically uniform singular value amplification from
[
25
]
), with a further complexity
\mathcal{O}(C^{2})
. In reality, if we prefer to choose
\mathscr{N}
points
\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}
uniformly within the hypersphere, then we can expect that
C^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}|\textbf{x}_{i}|^{2}\leq\mathscr{N}
, which means that the complexity of the above step can be
\mathcal{O}(\mathscr{N})
(for
C\geq 1
).

For
C
smaller than
1
, one cannot use the amplification method
[
25
]
. Therefore, the factor
C
cannot be removed by amplification. (Of course, this can be avoided using different or more points.) For now, we continue the construction with
C
being greater or equal to
1
(which means it is removed from the above equation). Subsequently, we will return to the case
C
being smaller than
1
and show that the structure of a homogeneous polynomial allows us to factor out some power of
C
, resulting in a different expression for the Hessian evaluated at given points. Hence, the final complexity is different (from the case
C>1
) by a factor of power of
C
.

Define
\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\equiv\textbf{x}_{i}/|\textbf{x}_{i}|
, e.g., the normalized vector. We have that
\textbf{x}_{i}\textbf{x}_{i}^{T}=|\textbf{x}_{i}|^{2}\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}
. Our goal is to transform the above operator into its square root, e.g., for each

, we aim to transform
|\textbf{x}_{i}|^{2}\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}\longrightarrow|\textbf{x}_{i}|\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}
. In order to achieve such a goal, we recall two results from
[
25
]
and
[
26
]
:

Lemma 3
(Corollary 3.4.14 in
[
26
]
)

Given
\delta,\epsilon\in(0,1/2]
,

and let

. There exists an even/odd polynomial of degree
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\delta}\log(\frac{1}{\epsilon}))
such that

||P-f||_{[\delta,1]}\leq\epsilon,\ ||P||_{[-1,1]}\leq 1.

III.3

Remind that we are interested in the spectrum, or more specifically, the minimum eigenvalue of Hessian

[

]

To see how it applies to our main problem, let

\lambda_{min}<0
(being negative) then
(1-\lambda_{\min})/2>1/2
, and that
(1-\lambda_{\min})/2
is the maximum eigenvalue of
(\mathbb{I}_{n}-\textbf{H}/p^{2})/2
. Therefore, we can track sign of
\lambda_{min}
from estimating minimum eigenvalue of
(\mathbb{I}_{n}-\textbf{H}/p^{2})/2
.

From the previous section we have obtained the block encoding of
\frac{1}{4^{p-1}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\frac{\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})}{2sp^{2}}
. The last recipe that we need to complete our algorithm is the following. First, suppose
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
is a set of Hermitian operators, then the eigenspace of
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{M}A_{i}
is simply the union of the eigenspace of all
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}

[
28
]
. Second, the eigenspace of
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{M}A_{i}
is the tensor product of the eigenspace of each
\{A_{i}\}
, which means that the eigenvalues of
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{M}A_{i}
is the multiplication of eigenvalues of contributing matrices. From the second property, we can claim that for any
i
, the eigenvalues of
(1/4^{p-1})\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})/(2sp^{2})
is the eigenvalues of
(1/4^{p-1})\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})/(2sp^{2})
. The reason is that, each operator
\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}
is a projector and hence, its only eigenvalue is
1
. Therefore, from the first property, we have that

\displaystyle\text{maximum eigenvalue of}\ \bigoplus_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2s4^{p-1}}\big(\mathbb{I}_{n}-\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\frac{\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})}{p^{2}}\big)=\max_{\textbf{x}_{i}}\big\{\frac{\mathbb{I}_{n}-\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})/p^{2}}{4s4^{p-1}}\big\}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}.

One may wonder why the right-hand side is important. We remind that if the function is convex in the given domain
\mathscr{D}
, then its Hessian matrix does not have non-negative eigenvalues at all points in
\mathscr{D}
. Therefore, if
\max_{\textbf{x}_{i}}\{\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})\}
is non-negative, then the Hessian is semi-definite at all
\mathscr{N}
points of consideration. As we pointed out from the beginning of this section, the condition of
\min_{\textbf{x}_{i}}\{\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})\}
being non-negative is equivalent to
\min_{\textbf{x}_{i}}\{(\mathbb{I}_{n}-\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i}))/2\}
being no less than
1/2
.

Fortunately, given the block encoding of
(1/4^{p-1})\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})/2sp^{2}
, it is simple to use lemma
14
(plus a trivial block encoding of identity matrix of dimension
n\mathscr{N})
to obtain the block encoding of their summation, which is exactly:

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\frac{1}{4sp^{2}4^{p-1}}\big(\mathbb{I}_{n}+\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})\big).

Then, we can find its minimum eigenvalue using recent work of
[
27
]
, as we mentioned at the beginning of this subsection, e.g., Lemma
8
.

We finally remark that we are estimating the minimum eigenvalue of the operator

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\frac{1}{4s4^{p-1}}\big(\mathbb{I}_{n}-\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\frac{\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})}{p^{2}}\big),

which contains the extra factor
4s4^{p-1}
. What we actually want is the maximum eigenvalue of the operator

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbb{I}_{n}-\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\frac{\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})}{p^{2}}\big),

which corresponds exactly with

\max_{\textbf{x}_{i}}\big\{\frac{\mathbb{I}_{n}-\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})}{2p^{2}}\big\}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}.

Therefore, we need to use Lemma
8
with an adjusted multiplicative error, e.g., by choosing

\delta\longrightarrow\frac{\delta}{2s4^{p-1}}.

Summary of the quantum algorithm procedure
. For convenience, we provide key points of our framework (which means we leave out technical details) plus corresponding complexity at each step.

\bullet
We begin with a set of points
\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}
(each
\textbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
|\textbf{x}_{i}|\leq 1
) of interest. Define
C^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}|\textbf{x}_{i}|^{2}
as normalization factor.

\bullet
Load the above points into quantum state
\ket{\phi}=\frac{1}{C}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\ket{i}\otimes\textbf{x}_{i}
, then construct the block encoding of the following operator

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{x}_{1}\textbf{x}_{1}^{T}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\textbf{x}_{2}\textbf{x}_{2}^{T}&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}^{T}\end{pmatrix}.

The complexity of the above step is
\mathcal{O}(\log(n\mathscr{N})
.

\bullet
Break into two cases
C\geq 1
and
C<1
. First, consider
C\geq 1
, then use amplification
[
25
]
to remove the factor
C^{2}
. The complexity for amplification step is
\mathcal{O}(C^{2}\log(n\mathscr{N}))
. Then we
construct the following operator

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0.5|\textbf{x}_{1}|\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{1}}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&0.5|\textbf{x}_{2}|\ket{\textbf{x}_{2}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{2}}&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&0.5|\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}|\ket{\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}}\end{pmatrix}.

The complexity of this step is
\mathcal{O}(\frac{C^{2}}{\textbf{x}_{min}}\log(n\mathscr{N}))
where
\textbf{x}_{min}\equiv\min\{|\textbf{x}_{i}|^{2}\}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}
.

\bullet
Using the above operator, we construct the following operator

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}\begin{pmatrix}\big(|\textbf{x}_{1}||\textbf{x}_{1}\rangle\langle\textbf{x}_{1}|\big)^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}&&&\\
&\big(|\textbf{x}_{2}||\textbf{x}_{2}\rangle\langle\textbf{x}_{2}|\big)^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}&&\\
&&\cdots&\\
&&&\big(|\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}||\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}\rangle\langle\textbf{x}_{\mathscr{N}}|\big)^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

The complexity upon this step is
\mathcal{O}(p\frac{C^{2}}{\textbf{x}_{min}}\log(n\mathscr{N}))
.

\bullet
Use oracle access to
A
(defined in section
II.1
) to construct the block encoding of
(M_{H}+M_{D})/2sp^{2}
. The complexity of this step is
\mathcal{O}(p^{2}\log(n))
.

\bullet
Employing mathematical property of homogeneous polynomial (see
III.1
), we construct the block encoding of

\frac{1}{4^{p-1}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}(\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}})^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\frac{\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i})}{2sp^{2}}

that contains our Hessian
H
(for homogeneous polynomial).

\bullet
Generalize the above construction to a polynomial of arbitrary type. The complexity of this step (including everything from the beginning) is

\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{C^{2}}{\textbf{x}_{min}}\log(n\mathscr{N})+p^{2}\log(n)\big).

\bullet
Construct the block encoding of the operator

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}\frac{1}{4s4^{p-1}}\big(\mathbb{I}_{n}-\ket{\textbf{x}_{i}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{i}}^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\frac{\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{i}}{p^{2}})\big).

\bullet
Find minimum eigenvalue of the above operator by using Lemma
8
(with accuracy being scaled
\epsilon/4sp^{2}4^{p-1}
, and infer the convexity from such eigenvalue. More specifically, if the minimum eigenvalue is less than
1/2
, then the function is not convex. Otherwise, it is convex. The complexity of this step is:

\mathcal{O}\Big(4^{p-1}sp^{2}(\log(n\mathscr{N})+\log(4^{p-1}sp^{2}))(p\frac{C^{2}}{\textbf{x}_{min}}(\log(n\mathscr{N})+p^{2}\log(n))\Big).

\bullet
Finally consider the case where the normalization factor
C<1
. Repeat the same procedure, which results in almost the same complexity, i.e.,

\mathcal{O}\Big((4C^{2})^{p-1}sp^{2}(\log(n\mathscr{N})+\log(4^{p-1}sp^{2}))(p\frac{C^{2}}{\textbf{x}_{min}}(\log(n\mathscr{N})+p^{2}\log(n))\Big).

We remark that in the above summary, we did not take into account the error term
\epsilon
that appears in multiple steps, such as amplification, encoding matrices
M_{H},M_{D}
, and taking the square root of the operator. To dissect the convexity, we can ignore the error-dependence factors because we only care about the sign of the minimum eigenvalue instead of a real estimation. Therefore, one can set the error to be some constant. Furthermore, the complexity dependence on the error is polylogarithmic, which is efficient. The generalization to polynomials of arbitrary type is carried out in Section
III.2.2
. A subtle detail about the above running time is that the scaling depends on
|\textbf{x}_{min}|
, which is the minimum norm of the length of chosen points, as well as
C
, which is the normalization factor. Let’s say we choose
\mathscr{N}
points to be distributed uniformly in the domain of interest (hypersphere), then we can assume that
|\textbf{x}_{min}|\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
(being some constant) and hence
1\leq C^{2}\leq\mathscr{N}
. So the factor
C^{2}/|\textbf{x}_{min}|\in\mathcal{O}(\mathscr{N})
, which is linear in
\mathscr{N}
. For
C
being smaller than
1
, which means that we choose
\mathscr{N}
points having very small norm (at least, for most of them). Then we can assume that
|\textbf{x}_{min}|
is smaller than
1/\mathscr{N}
, which means that
1/|\textbf{x}_{min}|
is greater than
\mathscr{N}
, but no greater than
\mathcal{O}(\mathscr{N})
. All in all, for both cases where
C\geq 1
or
C<1
, the factor
C^{2}/|\textbf{x}_{min}|
is
\mathcal{O}(\mathscr{N})
, which is linear in
\mathscr{N}
.

Now we are ready to state our main result formally:

Theorem 1
(Testing Convexity over
\mathscr{N}
Sample Points)

Let an objective function
f
(of arbitrary type), with certain assumptions defined as in Sections
II.1
and
III.2.2
. Over the domain
\mathscr{D}\subseteq(-1,1)^{n}
, let
\mathscr{N}
be the chosen sample points, denoted as
\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}
for convexity testing. Let
C^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}|\textbf{x}_{i}|^{2}(\leq\mathscr{N}))
and
\textbf{x}_{min}=\min_{i}\{|\textbf{x}_{i}|^{2}\}_{i=1}^{\mathscr{N}}
. If
C\geq 1
, then the quantum algorithm outlined above can reveal the positive-semidefiniteness of corresponding Hessian in time

\mathcal{O}\Big(4^{p-1}sp^{2}\ (\log(n\mathscr{N})+\log(4^{p-1}2sp^{2}))\ (p\mathscr{N}(\log(n\mathscr{N}))+p^{2}\log(n)\Big),

where
\alpha
is some bounded constant. In particular, if
C<1
, then the running time is:

\mathcal{O}\Big((4C^{2})^{p-1}sp^{2}\ (\log(n\mathscr{N})+\log(4^{p-1}2sp^{2}))\ (p\mathscr{N}(\log(n\mathscr{N}))+p^{2}\log(n)\Big).

Potential advantage

To see the advantage, we take a look at how classical computers can solve the above problem. The Hessian matrix is computed as

\displaystyle\textbf{H}(\textbf{x})=\Tr_{1,2,...,p-1}\big((M_{H}+M_{D})\ (\rho_{x}^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n})\big),

(37)

which involves matrix operation of size
n^{p}
. Each operator
M_{H}
and
M_{D}
is computed from matrix
A
which takes further time
\mathcal{O}(pn^{p}+p^{2}n^{p})
. The Hessian is then evaluated at
\mathscr{N}
points to find the sign of minimum eigenvalue, which results in the final complexity
\mathcal{O}(\mathscr{N}(pn^{p}+p^{2}n^{p}))
.

The above running time clearly suggests that the quantum algorithm can test convexity superpolynomially more efficiently than its classical counterpart with respect to the number of variables
n
, meanwhile a bit slower in relative to the number of sample points
\mathscr{N}
. Therefore, the quantum method is very efficient when dealing with high-dimensional cases as well as high-degree polynomials.

An interesting question from the above classical procedure is: what if we follow the same routine using a quantum computer for all points? It means that one can run the quantum framework to check the spectrum of Hessian at each point
x
, and repeat the procedure for
\mathscr{N}
points. In such a case, the quantum procedure would be very similar to our prior construction, except that we do not care about other
\mathscr{N}-1
points, more specifically, if we take a look at Eqn.
22
and pay attention only to the top-left corner, e.g., the operator
\textbf{x}_{1}\textbf{x}_{1}^{T}
(the factor
C
is treated in a similar manner as the above construction), which means that we treat this point
\textbf{x}_{1}
as a point of interest. Then, the same procedure can be carried out to first build the squared operator:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\textbf{x}_{1}\textbf{x}_{1}^{T}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\end{pmatrix}\longrightarrow\begin{pmatrix}0.5|\textbf{x}_{1}|\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{1}}&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\\
\cdot&\cdot&\cdot&\cdot\end{pmatrix}.

(38)

Then, from the right-handed side operator, one proceeds to build the block encoding of
\Big(0.5|\textbf{x}_{1}|\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{1}}\Big)^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}
(using the routine below Equation (
23

5

\displaystyle\Big(0.5|\textbf{x}_{1}|\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{1}}\Big)^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}\cdot\ \frac{(M_{H}+M_{D})}{2sp^{2}}\cdot\Big(0.5|\textbf{x}_{1}|\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{1}}\Big)^{\otimes p-1}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}=\frac{1}{4^{p-1}}\ket{\textbf{x}_{1}}\bra{\textbf{x}_{1}}^{p-1}\otimes\frac{\textbf{H}(\textbf{x}_{1})}{2sp^{2}}.

One can see that the above formula is essentially the top-left block corner of Eqn. (
III.2.1
), which is quite obvious. Then, one can use the same Lemma

to reveal the minimum value, which indicates the positivity of Hessian

at given point
\textbf{x}_{1}
. One repeats the process for
\mathscr{N}
different points, which results in asymptotically the same complexity. The difference is that, as we need to store
\mathscr{N}
different eigenvalues (of Hessian at
\mathscr{N}
points), the memory usage is required to be as much as
\mathscr{N}

qubits to handle the same task, which is more effective in terms of memory usage but sharing the same running time.

IV
Remarks and Discussion

In this section, we discuss our algorithm in a larger context, showing the potential application of our result in multiple directions.

IV.0.1
From Hessian to Curvature and Geometric Structure of Manifold

As we mentioned, the Hessian of a function at a given point encodes the local geometric structures of such a function, e.g., convexity. While the sign of eigenvalues of Hessian can reveal the convexity, the magnitude of eigenvalues can actually reveal how curved it is (see Fig.
3
for a simple example in 3D). Figure
3
features two simple surfaces in 3D, which are a plane and a sphere. For the plane, it is easy to compute the Hessian of
z(x,y)=-(a/c)x-(b/c)y+d/c
in this case, which is simply a
2\times 2
matrix of 0 entries, which implies that the surface is not curved at all, i.e., exactly match the plane shape. On the other hand, for a sphere,
z=\sqrt{r^{2}-x^{2}-y^{2}}
so the Hessian matrix is not as straightforward as a plane, but as we can see, the function
z
is convex on the lower half of plane
x-y
, but not on the upper half.

A plane and a sphere. A plane has a canonical representation as

Thus, from this angle, our work suggests a potential aid of a quantum computer in the study of the geometric structure of a manifold, given that a manifold is locally Euclidean and arbitrary smooth function of some variables can be approximated by certain polynomials in some domains.

IV.0.2
Variational Quantum Algorithm

A popular topic of recent progress in quantum computation is the variational quantum algorithm (VQA)
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
15
,
32
]
. One of the reasons that make variational quantum algorithms so appealing is that typically, they require low-depth circuits, which is very suitable in the near-term era. VQA has shown its success in many areas such as combinatorial optimization
[
33
]
, supervised learning
[
15
]
, etc. A common strategy in the context of VQA eventually boils down to the minimization problem:

\text{min}_{\theta}\ f(\theta)=\bra{\textbf{0}}U(\theta)^{\dagger}OU(\theta)\ket{\textbf{0}},

where
U(\theta)
refers to some variational circuit, e.g., a circuit composed of rotational gates with adjustable angles. A common method to optimize the above quantity is gradient descent, where the above observables are usually defined as a cost function, and its gradient is computed classically. Then, the parameters are updated iteratively by tuning corresponding rotational gates. Optimizing a quantum circuit is apparently not easy, as a phenomenon called the barren plateau can occur
[
34
,
35
]
, which prevents the efficient training of quantum circuits. In particular, training general variational quantum circuits is even NP-hard
[
36
]
.

Theoretically, the cost landscape of the above function is also a factor that affects the minimization. If the domain is convex, then any initial randomization falling into that domain can lead to a minimum, which implies that the optimization is efficient. While the exact expression for
f(\theta)
might not necessarily be a polynomial, we remark that over some domains that are sufficiently small, arbitrary functions can be approximated by some polynomials, e.g., Taylor series. Therefore, a visible strategy that we see from our work is that we can consider multiple domains and check the convexity. Given that the task of dissecting convexity can be done superpolynomially fast in the quantum realm, it turns out to be quite useful in this case as we can scan the optimization landscape efficiently with respect to the number of parameters. The challenge then only arises as to how to represent
f(\theta)
with proper polynomials, which allows computable parameters in a form that we assume in our work. At first glance, this challenge seems impossible for a random circuit
U
, but might not be so for a structured
U
that has been employed in several contexts, such as the so-called QAOA ansatz
[
37
]
. We leave this question as a motivation for future exploration of the application of our convexity tester.

IV.0.3
Improving Quantum Newton’s Method

As a striking corollary of our method is the generalization to arbitrary polynomial as well as major improvement on the resource requirement, upon the quantum Newton’s method developed in
[
20
]
. In
[
27
]
, we already made progress on quantum gradient descent, and in fact, the method outlined in this work shares certain similarities with what in
[

]

To begin with, we recall some aspects of quantum Newton’s method, which is basically a modified gradient descent method. We first follow the same notation and assumption from
[
20
]
. In such a problem, we are given an objective function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which is a homogeneous polynomial of even degree as we defined in Section
II.1
. The goal is to find the point
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
at which
f(\textbf{x})
is minimum. A standard method for this kind of optimization problem is gradient descent, which is an iterative method in that one begins with a random solution
\textbf{x}_{0}
and performs the update iteratively as follows:

\displaystyle\textbf{x}_{t+1}=\textbf{x}_{t}-\eta\nabla f(\textbf{x}_{t}).

(40)

As mentioned in Section
II.1
, such particular form of
f
admits an analytical form for the gradient, as
\nabla f(\textbf{x})=D(\textbf{x})\textbf{x}
, which was used in
[
20
]
(and improved in
[
27
]
) to construct the quantum process carrying out the above iteration procedure. As also mentioned in the same work
[
20
]
, Newton’s method modifies directly upon the gradient descent method by taking account of the curvature of
f
, i.e., the update rule is as follows:

\displaystyle\textbf{x}_{t+1}=\textbf{x}_{t}-\eta\ \textbf{H}^{-1}(\textbf{x}_{t})\nabla f(\textbf{x}_{t}).

(41)

Roughly speaking, at a given time step
t
-th, the method in
[
21
]
takes multiple copies of
\textbf{x}_{t}
, use oracle access to simulate
\exp(-iM_{D}t)
and
\exp(-iM_{H}t)

6
and
10

\textbf{x}_{t+1}
. If one wish to obtain a normalized version of temporal solution
\textbf{x}_{t+1}
,
\ket{\textbf{x}}_{t+1}
, then by measuring the ancilla and post-select on ancilla being
\ket{0}
, one achieves the goal, as in
[
21
]
. Here, we lift such requirement and consider the problem in a more general manner, that is obtaining a temporal solution written in a form similar to “density operator”,

From Section
III.1
,
III.2

Therefore, by scanning the landscape, one can see if the given function is convex within such region. If the function is convex, then a minima is guaranteed, and hence, the algorithm is considered successful.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have investigated the potential of quantum computers in the context of functional analysis, specifically testing the convexity of a given objective function. The problem of testing convexity is converted to the problem of determining the non-negativity of a matrix, e.g., all eigenvalues are non-negative. By employing a useful algebraic property of homogeneous polynomials of even degrees, we wrote down an explicit form of the so-called Hessian and built upon it to generalize the Hessian to arbitrary polynomials. We then combine it with a powerful quantum singular value transformation framework, plus a quantum power method to construct a quantum algorithm that allows us to test the positive-semidefiniteness of such Hessian matrix at multiple points from a given domain. The procedure has running time polylogarithmically respective to the dimension
n
and linear with the number of sample points
\mathscr{N}
, which is superpolynomially faster than the best-known classical approach relative to the number of variables, meanwhile keeping the same complexity on the number of sample points. We also point out two examples where we envision a potential application of convexity testing, including studying the geometric structure of manifold, testing training landscape of variational quantum algorithms as well as optimization landscape for gradient descent/Newton’s method for optimization. In particular, as a striking corollary of our result, we provide a major improvement upon the work of
[
20
]
in multiple aspects: running time (with respect to error tolerance), generality (arbitrary polynomial type) and the subtle detail regarding the inversion of the Hessian. As a whole, our work has added one more interesting example to the field of quantum computation, revealing that the area of functional analysis, and many more areas, is a rich avenue deserving of further research from computational aspects. An interesting open direction that we believe is worth looking at is that the model we work on in this case is somewhat explicit, e.g., the function is a polynomial of computable coefficients given via an oracle. In some traditional problems, the input function is typically given as a blackbox, such as Grover’s search problem
[
5
]
, and we wish to reveal hidden properties. Suppose, instead, we are given a blackbox function that computes some analytical function; then, how do we extract the hidden properties, such as the convexity of the function in some domain? We note that there are two prior relevant works such as
[
19
]
, where the author considered the gradient estimation, and
[
38
]
, where the authors considered a sampling problem from a blackbox oracle that computes the value of given function within some domain. While there are seemingly overlaps, we do not see a direct solution to our case, thereby we leave the challenge for future investigation.
