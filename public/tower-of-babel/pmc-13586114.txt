Modulating the periodontal wound microenvironment through oxygen therapy: a narrative review on the biological basis, clinical applications and future perspectives
Authors: Amani M. Basudan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Topical oxygen-releasing formulations have emerged as a potential adjunctive approach to enhance wound healing outcomes in conditions such as gingivitis, periodontitis and peri-implant diseases. Periodontal wound healing is a complex biological process influenced by microbial load, tissue oxygenation, and host inflammatory responses. This narrative review aimed to evaluate the available clinical evidence on the effectiveness of topical oxygen-releasing products in oral soft and hard tissue wound management. Unlike previous broad reviews of topical oxygen therapy, this review specifically synthesizes its role in periodontal wound healing, compares clinical outcomes with conventional therapies, and evaluates emerging opportunities for integration into regenerative periodontal strategies. An organised literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for studies published till 2025. Eligible studies included primarily randomized controlled trials, evaluating clinical outcomes related to periodontal wound healing. A structured search of five electronic databases was undertaken, and eligible studies were narratively synthesized in accordance with the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA). Included studies, were evaluated qualitatively to compare the efficacy of topical oxygen therapy as an adjunct in periodontal wound healing. The findings suggest that topical oxygen-releasing formulations provide adjunctive benefits in specific contexts, particularly in postoperative healing, periodontal therapy, enhancing soft tissue healing, and improving patient-reported outcomes such as pain and discomfort. However, many outcomes were comparable to conventional therapies such as chlorhexidine. Topical oxygen-releasing formulations appear to be a potential adjunct in periodontal wound repair, particularly in hypoxic or inflammatory conditions.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44445-026-00240-1.

Introduction

Periodontal wound healing is a complex and highly regulated biological process involving coordinated interactions between cellular, molecular, and environmental factors to restore tissue integrity (Leśków et al. 2023). Wounds affecting the periodontium—whether involving soft tissues or supporting alveolar bone—may arise from surgical interventions, trauma, or pathological conditions such as periodontitis and peri-implantitis (Leventis et al. 2024). These wounds present unique clinical challenges due to the constant exposure to a diverse microbial biofilm, mechanical stress from mastication, and the dynamic physicochemical environment of the oral cavity (Politis et al. 2016). Conventional adjunctive therapies, including antiseptic agents such as chlorhexidine and protective periodontal dressings like Coe-pak, are widely used to support healing; however, their use may be associated with limitations such as cytotoxic effects, altered microbial ecology, and suboptimal healing outcomes in certain clinical scenarios (Batra et al. 2010).

The wound healing process is classically divided into overlapping phases—hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling—each requiring tightly regulated cellular activity and adequate tissue perfusion (Wilkinson and Hardman 2020). Oxygen plays a central role in all stages of wound healing, serving as a critical substrate for cellular metabolism and host defense mechanisms (Yip 2015). During the inflammatory phase, oxygen supports the oxidative killing of pathogens by neutrophils, while in the proliferative phase it is essential for fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, and angiogenesis (Ukaegbu et al. 2025) (Fig. 1). Fibroblasts contribute to extracellular matrix formation by depositing primarily type III collagen, while endothelial cells facilitate neovascularization to restore tissue perfusion (Wilkinson and Hardman 2020). Moreover, oxygen availability influences key regulatory pathways, including hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which are central to angiogenic signaling (Barros et al. 2019). Although transient hypoxia may initially stimulate angiogenesis, prolonged or severe hypoxia can impair collagen deposition, delay epithelialization, and increase susceptibility to infection, ultimately contributing to chronic wound formation (Usui et al. 2008; Hong et al. 2014; Singh et al. 2023). Clinical and experimental evidence indicates that optimizing tissue oxygenation—through systemic or local approaches such as supplemental oxygen or hyperbaric oxygen therapy—can enhance healing outcomes in selected cases (Guo and Dipietro 2010) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Cellular and molecular mechanisms of topical oxygen therapy

The biological importance of oxygen has stimulated growing interest in therapeutic strategies capable of improving tissue oxygenation (Hong et al. 2014). Hyperbaric oxygen therapy has long been employed in selected maxillofacial conditions, including osteoradionecrosis and refractory osteomyelitis, but its widespread use remains limited by high cost, restricted availability, lengthy treatment protocols, and potential adverse effects (Guo and Dipietro 2010). More recently, topical oxygen therapy has emerged as a practical alternative capable of delivering oxygen directly to injured tissues without systemic exposure (Sindhusha and Rajasekar 2023). Contemporary oxygen-releasing formulations utilize stabilized oxygen compounds incorporated into gels, mouthwashes, toothpastes, and postoperative care products designed to provide sustained local oxygen release while maintaining favorable moisture conditions within the wound environment (Shaheen et al. 2024).

Oxygen gradient in oral tissues vary considerably throughout the oral cavity (Faran Ali and Tanwir 2012). Well-perfused gingival tissues are relatively oxygen rich whereas deep periodontal pockets, extraction sockets and inflamed peri-implant tissues are relatively hypoxic (Zhou et al. 2025). Surgical trauma temporarily disrupts local perfusion, creating oxygen gradients between the wound margin and deeper tissues (Gong et al. 2025). Such gradients regulate cellular migration and angiogenesis but may become detrimental if prolonged by persistent inflammation or impaired vascular supply (Huveneers and Phng 2024). Diabetes, smoking and microvascular disease further reduce tissue oxygenation and are associated with delayed oral wound healing (Ko et al. 2021).

Within dentistry, interest in topical oxygen therapy has expanded considerably during the past decade. Clinical investigations have explored its potential role in improving healing following periodontal therapy, implant surgery, tooth extraction, gingival depigmentation, management of peri-implant diseases, and treatment of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (Emecen-Huja and Leblebicioglu 2024). Proposed mechanisms extend beyond simple oxygen supplementation and include modulation of inflammatory responses, enhancement of angiogenesis, stimulation of fibroblast activity, promotion of collagen synthesis, reduction of anaerobic bacterial burden, and optimization of the wound microenvironment (Abiko and Selimovic 2010). These biological effects suggest that topical oxygen therapy may complement conventional periodontal and surgical treatments by enhancing host-mediated regenerative processes rather than functioning solely as an antimicrobial intervention.

In this context, topical oxygen-releasing formulations have been introduced as a potential strategy to enhance local tissue oxygenation and improve wound healing dynamics in the oral cavity (Alayadi et al. 2024). These formulations, available in various delivery systems including mouthwashes, gels, and toothpastes, are designed to provide sustained oxygen release at the site of application, thereby promoting a more favorable microenvironment for tissue repair (Tandara and Mustoe 2004; Sayadi et al. 2018). Such an approach may be particularly beneficial in hypoxic environments, such as periodontal pockets and post-surgical sites, where reduced oxygen tension may compromise healing processes (Ngeow et al. 2022). In addition to their potential effects on tissue regeneration, these formulations have been proposed to influence microbial activity and oral hygiene parameters, including plaque accumulation and gingival inflammation (Moghanian et al. 2026).

Despite growing interest in topical oxygen therapy, the clinical evidence supporting its use in oral wound management remains fragmented and heterogeneous. While some studies report improvements in healing outcomes and microbial control compared to conventional therapies, others demonstrate comparable or inconsistent effects across different clinical settings (Sindhusha and Rajasekar 2023; Sharma et al. 2024; Alharbi et al. 2025). In par with previous literature (Leventis et al. 2024), which has broadly discussed topical oxygen therapy in implantology, oral surgery, and periodontology, the present review adopts a more focused perspective on periodontal wound healing and tissue repair. In particular, this review critically examines the available clinical evidence for topical oxygen-releasing formulations in periodontal and peri-implant wound-healing contexts, with emphasis on comparative clinical outcomes against established therapies, including chlorhexidine, scaling and root planing, and postoperative periodontal dressings. In addition, the review integrates the biological rationale for local oxygen delivery with clinical findings and identifies the limitations and inconsistencies within the current evidence base. Importantly, it further considers the emerging potential of oxygen-releasing systems as components of regenerative and biomaterial-based strategies, including oxygen-loaded hydrogels, nanobubbles, microcarriers, and scaffolds designed to support periodontal tissue regeneration. Thus, rather than providing a general overview of topical oxygen therapy, this review seeks to bridge the gap between its biological rationale, comparative clinical effectiveness, and its potential future application within regenerative and minimally invasive periodontal therapy.

Materials and methods

Review protocol

A comprehensive and structured literature search was performed across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science, to ensure broad coverage of relevant clinical and biomedical research. The review was conducted following the recommendations for narrative reviews, the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA). Additional databases, including Science Direct and Springer Link, were screened to identify potentially relevant studies published in high-impact journals. Google Scholar was used as a supplementary source to identify grey literature and studies not indexed in primary databases. (Appendix A)

The review methodology (Fig. 2) was designed to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and minimization of selection bias through predefined eligibility criteria, independent screening, and standardized data extraction procedures. Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across electronic databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The search included studies published in English from January 2015 to the date of the review.

The following keywords and Boolean operators were used “Topical oxygen therapy”, “Periodontal wound healing”, “Oxygen therapy”, “Periodontitis”, “Periodontal regeneration”, “Soft tissue healing”, “Dental wound healing”, “Periodontal surgery”, “Chronic wounds”, “Topical oxygen AND periodontitis”, “Oxygen therapy AND periodontal healing”

Search strategies were adapted for each database to account for differences in indexing and search functionality. Reference lists of included studies were also manually screened to identify additional relevant articles. The complete search strategies for all databases are presented in Appendix A to ensure reproducibility.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts retrieved from the electronic search were initially screened for relevance. Full-text articles meeting the inclusion criteria were subsequently reviewed. Studies considered relevant to topical oxygen therapy and periodontal healing were included in this narrative review.

Data extraction was performed using a standardized form. Before formal study selection, the reviewer independently assessed a sample of retrieved records using the predefined eligibility criteria to ensure consistent interpretation and application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies in extracted data were resolved through discussion and consensus. Prior to data extraction, the reviewer also reviewed and standardized the data-extraction fields and procedures using a sample of eligible studies to ensure consistency. Studies were stratified by level of evidence (RCTs), and findings from lower-level evidence were interpreted as hypothesis-generating only. Comparative efficacy with conventional treatments (e.g., chlorhexidine), effects on wound healing and oral disease management, impact on oral hygiene indicators and postoperative applications was done through qualitative analysis. This approach allowed for structured interpretation of the evidence while acknowledging variability across studies.

Quality considerations

Although formal risk-of-bias assessment is not mandatory for a narrative review, the methodological quality of included studies was considered during interpretation of the evidence. Greater emphasis was placed on randomized controlled trials and well-designed clinical studies, while findings from animal experiments and laboratory investigations were used primarily to explain biological mechanisms.

The included studies evaluated topical oxygen-releasing formulations across multiple clinical contexts, including experimental gingivitis, chronic gingivitis, chronic periodontitis, peri-implant health and disease. Interventions included mouthwash, gel, toothpaste, and combined product protocols, while comparators varied across studies and included chlorhexidine, triclosan, Coe-pak dressing, placebo, scaling and root planing (SRP) alone, or pre-/post-intervention comparisons without an external control. This clinical and methodological variability was accompanied by substantial diversity in outcome measures, including plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), bleeding on probing (BOP), probing pocket depth (PPD), clinical attachment level (CAL), microbial load, wound healing indices, epithelialization, pain scores, and descriptive clinical healing outcomes.

Overall, the included evidence suggested that topical oxygen-releasing formulations may provide clinical benefits in selected periodontal wound-healing and oral hygiene settings; however, the strength of this evidence varied considerably by indication and was influenced by study design and methodological quality.

Comparative efficacy of topical oxygen-releasing formulations and chlorhexidine in gingival healing

A subset of the included studies directly compared topical oxygen-releasing formulations with chlorhexidine-based regimens, primarily in the context of plaque control, gingival inflammation, microbial reduction, and tolerability (Rajkhowa et al. 2023; Custódio et al. 2024; Kumar et al. 2024. Rajkhowa et al. (2023) evaluated an active oxygen-containing mouthwash against 0.2% chlorhexidine in patients with chronic gingivitis and reported no statistically significant differences in gingival index or plaque index outcomes between groups. This supports the interpretation that oxygen-releasing formulations may perform comparably to chlorhexidine in short-term gingival health management (Rajkhowa et al. 2023). Similarly, Custódio et al. (2024) found that an oxygen-releasing mouthwash provided biofilm control comparable to 0.12% chlorhexidine in peri-implant maintenance (Custódio et al. 2024).

By contrast, some studies reported outcome-specific advantages favoring oxygen-releasing formulations. Kumar et al. (2024) reported greater plaque index reduction for an oxygen-releasing mouthwash at one month compared with chlorhexidine, although gingival index and bleeding outcomes remained similar between groups (Kumar et al. 2024). Efficacy of topical oxygen therapy in periodontal wound healing

The included studies suggest that topical oxygen-releasing formulations may have adjunctive value in oral wound healing and management of inflammatory oral diseases, particularly relevant in postoperative, periodontal, peri-implant, and difficult-to-heal wound settings.

Periodontal outcomes

In periodontal therapy, Singh et al. (2024) evaluated scaling and root planing (SRP) with and without adjunctive oxygen-releasing gel in chronic periodontitis using a split-mouth design (Singh et al. 2024). The adjunctive therapy group demonstrated significant improvements in clinical parameters including probing pocket depth, clinical attachment level, gingival index, and bleeding on probing, suggesting that topical oxygen therapy may enhance the effects of conventional non-surgical periodontal treatment (Singh et al. 2024). Case-based reports by Alayadi et al. (2024) and Basudan et al. (2024) also described favorable reductions in periodontal inflammation and pocket depth following use of oxygen-releasing formulations (Alayadi et al. 2024; Basudan et al. 2024).

However, the interpretation of the periodontal RCT evidence requires caution. The clinical evidence is derived from relatively few trials, with differences in patient populations, baseline periodontal status, oxygen-releasing formulations, adjunctive periodontal procedures, and outcome assessment. These differences make it difficult to determine whether the observed improvements are attributable specifically to topical oxygen delivery or to interactions with conventional periodontal treatment. Importantly, statistically significant improvements in surrogate clinical parameters such as probing pocket depth, bleeding on probing, or gingival indices should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of long-term periodontal regeneration. Larger, adequately powered multicenter RCTs with prespecified primary outcomes and longer follow-up are therefore required to establish the magnitude and durability of the clinical effect.

Post-surgical wound healing

Scognamiglio et al. (2025) further described favorable postoperative tissue stabilization and closure after periodontal and implant surgery using a combined oxygen-releasing postoperative care protocol (Scognamiglio et al. 2025). While the study design and comparator structure limit inferential interpretation, the findings are consistent with the broader hypothesis that sustained local oxygen availability may support tissue homeostasis and modulate early inflammatory responses.

Effects of oxygen-releasing products on inflammatory healing

Microbiological evidence was more favorable in some studies. Sindhusha and Rajasekar (2023) demonstrated significantly greater reduction of aerosolized microbial load with an oxygen-releasing mouthwash as a pre-procedural rinse compared with chlorhexidine, highlighting a potential role for such formulations in infection control during dental procedures (Sindhusha and Rajasekar 2023). In peri-implant maintenance, Custódio et al. (2024) found that oxygen-releasing mouthwash achieved biofilm control comparable to chlorhexidine, suggesting that these formulations may be effective as part of supportive oral hygiene regimens (CustÓdio et al. 2024).

The effects of oxygen-releasing products on oral hygiene indicators were investigated across studies assessing plaque accumulation, gingival inflammation, microbial load, and postoperative inflammatory signs (Cunha et al. 2019; Mattei et al. 2021; Rajkhowa et al. 2023; Sindhusha and Rajasekar 2023; Custódio et al. 2024; Kumar et al. 2024). Overall, these studies suggest that oxygen-releasing formulations may exert clinically measurable effects on oral hygiene-related parameters, although the magnitude and consistency of these effects differ according to product type, comparator, and clinical context.

Cunha et al. (2019) reported that active oxygen toothpaste had antiplaque efficacy comparable to triclosan-based toothpaste in an experimental gingivitis model, with no statistically significant differences between groups (Cunha et al. 2019). Across oral hygiene outcomes, oxygen-releasing products generally showed comparable or modestly favourable effects relative to conventional antiseptic regimens, although results varied according to formulation and clinical context. These findings are consistent with the comparative evidence summarized in Sect. 3.1 (Rajkhowa et al. 2023). In contrast, Kumar et al. (2024) reported significantly greater plaque index reduction with an oxygen-releasing mouthwash at one month compared with chlorhexidine, although anti-inflammatory measures remained similar (Kumar et al. 2024)

Comparative efficacy of oxygen-releasing gel versus coe-pak dressing in postoperative surgical depigmentation

Evidence regarding the comparative efficacy of oxygen-releasing gel and Coe-pak dressing in postoperative gingival depigmentation was limited but suggestive of a potential clinical advantage for the oxygen-releasing gel. Among the studies included in the present review, Juliana and Tarek (2022) provided the most direct comparative evidence through a split-mouth randomized controlled trial (Juliana and Tarek 2022). In that study, oxygen-releasing gel was associated with significantly faster re-epithelialization and lower postoperative pain scores than Coe-pak dressing (Juliana and Tarek 2022). These findings suggest that such formulations may provide a more favorable wound-healing environment following depigmentation procedures, possibly by maintaining local moisture and oxygen availability without the mechanical bulk of a periodontal dressing (Juliana and Tarek 2022). The split-mouth design strengthened within-patient comparability; however, the modest sample size and single-procedure setting limit external validity. Overall, the qualitative analysis suggests that oxygen-releasing gel may be a promising alternative to Coe-pak in postoperative gingival depigmentation, particularly when patient comfort and accelerated epithelialization are prioritized.

Discussion

Overview of principal findings

The present narrative review evaluated the clinical effectiveness on topical oxygen-releasing formulations in oral wound management. Overall, the findings suggest that these formulations provide adjunctive clinical benefits in selected contexts, particularly in postoperative healing, periodontal therapy, and microbiological control. A key observation across the included studies is the variability in outcomes depending on clinical indication, formulation type, and comparator used. While several studies report favorable trends in wound healing and inflammation reduction, the predominance of small-scale trials and descriptive case-based evidence limits the strength of conclusions that can be drawn. Taken together, the current evidence does not support definitive conclusions regarding the clinical superiority of topical oxygen-releasing formulations over conventional therapies. Rather, the data suggest a potential adjunctive role that requires confirmation through more rigorous investigation.

Mechanisms of topical oxygen therapy

Topical oxygen therapy aims to optimize the wound microenvironment by increasing the local availability of oxygen without exposing patients to systemic oxygen supplementation (Nagarsheth et al. 2024). Unlike hyperbaric oxygen therapy, which relies on elevated atmospheric pressure to increase dissolved oxygen in plasma, topical oxygen-releasing formulations deliver oxygen directly to the oral tissues through locally applied gels, mouthwashes, toothpastes and postoperative care products (Linden et al. 2014). Their therapeutic rationale extends beyond oxygen supplementation alone and encompasses modulation of inflammation, enhancement of tissue metabolism, stimulation of angiogenesis, support of extracellular matrix synthesis and favorable alterations of the oral microbial ecosystem (Choudhury 2018) (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Oxygen release and diffusion from topical formulations into oral tissues

Physicochemical principles of oxygen-releasing formulations

Most contemporary products employ stabilized oxygen complexes, oxygen-rich compounds or peroxide-derived systems capable of gradually releasing molecular oxygen upon contact with saliva and tissue fluids (Rui et al. 2025). Controlled release is critical because rapid liberation of oxygen may be short lived, whereas sustained delivery maintains a favorable oxygen gradient throughout the early stages of healing (Willemen et al. 2021). Modern formulations also incorporate viscous gels or bioadhesive carriers that prolong residence time on oral tissues and facilitate diffusion into superficial wound surfaces and periodontal pockets (Yanwinitchai and Williams Iii 2025) (Fig. 3).

Modification of the wound microenvironment

The principal therapeutic effect of topical oxygen therapy is improvement of the local wound microenvironment. Oral wounds frequently become hypoxic because of vascular disruption, edema and increased metabolic demand (Lohr et al. 2026). Local oxygen supplementation restores tissue oxygen tension sufficiently to support mitochondrial ATP production, normalize cellular metabolism and facilitate progression from inflammation toward tissue regeneration (Vásquez Vélez et al. 2025). This localized action is particularly relevant in extraction sockets, periodontal pockets and peri-implant defects where oxygen diffusion is naturally limited.

Interpretation of findings in relation to biological mechanisms

The findings of the included studies suggest that localized oxygen delivery help modulate micro environmental conditions in oral wound healing (Gurtner et al. 2008; Guo and Dipietro 2010). In recent years, topical oxygen therapy has been proposed as a supportive modality to enhance oral wound healing; however, the clinical evidence remains heterogeneous and limited in methodological rigor (Leventis et al. 2024). For example, improvements in probing depth, clinical attachment levels, and inflammatory indices observed in periodontal studies (Shaheen et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2024) are consistent with enhanced fibroblast activity and collagen deposition, which are known to be oxygen-dependent processes (Ngeow et al. 2022).

Similarly, reported improvements in postoperative healing and epithelialization may reflect enhanced angiogenesis and epithelial cell migration under improved oxygen availability (Juliana and Tarek 2022; Kumar et al. 2024). In addition, increased oxygen tension may influence the oral microbial environment by modulating anaerobic bacterial populations, which could contribute to observed reductions in microbial load in certain settings (Rajkhowa et al. 2023; Sindhusha and Rajasekar 2023). However, these mechanisms remain incompletely characterized in the oral cavity, and current clinical evidence does not allow definitive attribution of observed effects to specific biological pathways (Custódio et al. 2024).

Topical oxygen delivery systems

Contemporary formulations are designed to maintain a moist wound environment, prolong contact with injured tissues, and release oxygen in a sustained manner to support cellular metabolism and tissue repair.

Oxygen-releasing gels

Bioadhesive oxygen-releasing gels are the most widely investigated delivery system in dentistry. Their viscosity prolongs retention on gingival tissues, extraction sockets and peri-implant defects, permitting gradual oxygen diffusion (Alayadi et al. 2024; Shaheen et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2024). They are commonly used following periodontal surgery, implant placement, and gingival depigmentation (Kumar et al. 2024; Scognamiglio et al. 2025). Besides oxygen release, the gel matrix protects the wound surface and enhances patient comfort (Basudan et al. 2024).

Mouthwashes

Oxygen-releasing mouthwashes distribute active oxygen throughout the oral cavity and are primarily used for plaque control, postoperative care and supportive periodontal therapy (Sindhusha and Rajasekar 2023; Shaheen et al. 2024). They provide broad coverage of mucosal surfaces and may reduce anaerobic bacterial burden while improving oral hygiene (Custódio et al. 2024; Kumar et al. 2024). Their short contact time makes them particularly useful as adjuncts rather than standalone therapies (Basudan et al. 2024).

Toothpastes

Oxygen-containing toothpastes combine daily plaque control with continuous low-level oxygen delivery (Basudan et al. 2024; Scognamiglio et al. 2025). They may help maintain gingival health during supportive periodontal care and represent an accessible approach for long-term home maintenance (Shaheen et al. 2024).

Emerging technologies and clinical considerations

Recent research has focused on oxygen-loaded hydrogels, nanobubbles, oxygen microcarriers, electrospun scaffolds and biomaterial-based delivery systems capable of sustained oxygen release (Knowles et al. 2024). These technologies seek to optimize oxygen kinetics, improve penetration into hypoxic tissues and integrate oxygen delivery with regenerative biomaterials and growth factors (Zoneff et al. 2024).

Selection of a delivery system depends on wound location, depth, patient compliance and desired duration of oxygen exposure (Tandara and Mustoe 2004). Gels are generally preferred for localized surgical wounds, whereas mouthwashes are useful for generalized inflammatory conditions and maintenance therapy (Scognamiglio et al. 2025). Future systems are expected to incorporate smart biomaterials capable of responding to local tissue oxygen demand.

Clinical applications

Current evidence indicates that topical oxygen therapy may be considered as an adjunct to conventional dental treatment, particularly following periodontal debridement or surgery, in selected periodontal or peri-implant inflammatory conditions, and for postoperative wound care. Selection should be based on the clinical indication, wound characteristics, formulation, patient compliance, and available evidence, and it should not replace established periodontal or implant treatment protocols.

Gingivitis

Randomized clinical evidence suggests that oxygen-releasing mouthwashes provide gingival and plaque control broadly comparable to chlorhexidine, as discussed in Sect. 3.1. Their potential advantages include patient tolerance and avoidance of staining (Cunha et al. 2019; Rajkhowa et al. 2023). They may therefore represent a useful maintenance option in patients requiring prolonged antiseptic therapy.

Periodontitis

Adjunctive oxygen-releasing gels combined with scaling and root planing have demonstrated improvements in probing depth, clinical attachment level and bleeding on probing (Alayadi et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2024). These benefits are biologically consistent with enhanced collagen synthesis, angiogenesis and modulation of inflammatory responses, although larger multicentre trials remain necessary (Nagarsheth et al. 2024).

Peri-implant diseases

Evidence based studies indicate potential benefit in peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis by improving soft-tissue healing and supporting biofilm control following mechanical decontamination (Ansari et al. 2024). Present evidence supports use as an adjunctive therapy rather than a substitute for established implant maintenance protocols (Shaheen et al. 2024).

Postoperative oral surgery

Following periodontal surgery, gingival depigmentation and implant procedures, oxygen-releasing gels and mouthwashes have been associated with faster epithelialization, reduced postoperative discomfort and improved patient-reported healing (Juliana and Tarek 2022). These effects may be particularly valuable in medically compromised patients with delayed healing potential (Ko et al. 2021). In clinical practice, these applications should be considered selectively according to wound characteristics and treatment objectives, with conventional mechanical and surgical therapy remaining the primary treatment.

Comparative effectiveness with conventional therapies

The comparative evidence suggests that topical oxygen-releasing formulations generally demonstrate clinical outcomes comparable to conventional therapies, particularly chlorhexidine, across several oral hygiene and inflammatory parameters (Rajkhowa et al. 2023; Custódio et al. 2024). However, these findings were inconsistent across the evidence base (Sindhusha and Rajasekar 2023; Kumar et al. 2024).

Importantly, the mechanisms of action differ between these approaches (Rajkhowa et al. 2023; Sindhusha and Rajasekar 2023). Chlorhexidine exerts direct antimicrobial effects, whereas oxygen-releasing formulations may act primarily through modulation of the local tissue microenvironment and support of host-mediated healing processes (Custódio et al. 2024; Kumar et al. 2024). This distinction suggests that such formulations may be more appropriately considered as adjunctive rather than replacement therapies.

With respect to postoperative wound management, the comparison between oxygen-releasing gels and mechanical dressings such as Coe-pak suggests potential advantages in terms of patient comfort and epithelialization rates (Juliana and Tarek 2022). However, mechanical dressings may still provide superior wound stabilization in certain clinical scenarios, indicating that these approaches may be complementary rather than interchangeable (Singh et al. 2024; Grover et al. 2025). Overall, the available evidence does not support replacement of conventional therapies but suggests a potential adjunctive role for topical oxygen-releasing formulations in specific clinical contexts.

From a practical perspective, cost, availability, and patient compliance may influence the choice between topical oxygen-releasing products and chlorhexidine. Chlorhexidine is widely available and generally inexpensive, whereas the cost and availability of topical oxygen products may vary according to formulation and commercial source. Oxygen-releasing formulations may offer potential advantages in patient acceptance and avoidance of staining, which could support adherence during longer-term use. However, the included studies did not provide formal cost-effectiveness analyses; therefore, comparative economic benefits cannot currently be established.

Strengths and limitations of the review

Topical oxygen therapy offers several practical advantages including localized delivery, minimal systemic exposure, and ease of application, favorable patient acceptance and compatibility with existing periodontal and surgical protocols. Publication bias should also be considered. Studies reporting favourable effects of topical oxygen therapy may be more likely to be published than studies showing null or unfavourable outcomes, potentially leading to an overestimation of its clinical benefits. By simultaneously supporting angiogenesis, collagen maturation, epithelial migration, immune regulation and microbial homeostasis, oxygen influences multiple stages of wound healing. Regarding safety, the included clinical studies generally reported favourable tolerability of topical oxygen-releasing formulations, with no major adverse safety signals identified in the available evidence. Patient tolerance and postoperative comfort were also reported as potential advantages in some studies. However, safety reporting was not consistent across all studies, and the small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and diversity of formulations limit the ability to detect uncommon or delayed adverse effects. A key finding of this review is the substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across included studies, which significantly limits interpretability and precludes meaningful quantitative analysis. There is substantial heterogeneity among the investigated oxygen-releasing formulations. Gels, mouthwashes, toothpastes, and combined protocols differ in their composition, oxygen-release characteristics, concentration, retention time, frequency of application, and duration of exposure. Further, considerable variability exists in the outcomes used to assess treatment effectiveness. Studies have evaluated plaque and gingival indices, bleeding on probing, probing pocket depth, clinical attachment level, microbial outcomes, epithelialization, pain, and patient-reported healing. In particular, short-term improvements in inflammatory or hygiene-related indices do not necessarily demonstrate sustained periodontal tissue regeneration.

In addition, follow-up periods are frequently short and are therefore insufficient to determine whether early improvements in wound healing or inflammation are maintained over time. This is particularly important for periodontal regeneration, where clinically meaningful changes in attachment and supporting tissues require longer observation than early epithelial or symptomatic healing. Finally, there is currently no universally standardized protocol for topical oxygen therapy in periodontal care. Important parameters, including formulation characteristics, oxygen-release kinetics, application frequency, treatment duration, wound preparation, concomitant mechanical therapy, and postoperative care, vary considerably between studies. The current evidence therefore supports its investigation as a potential adjunct rather than its routine adoption as a replacement therapy.

Future research directions

Future research should focus on addressing the methodological limitations identified in the current evidence base. There is a clear need for well-designed, multicenter randomized controlled trials comparing topical oxygen-releasing formulations with standard therapies using standardized protocols and clinically relevant outcome measures. In addition, mechanistic studies using advanced imaging and molecular techniques may help clarify how localized oxygen delivery influences cellular and microbial dynamics in different oral environments.

Outcome assessment should also be standardized, with clearly prespecified primary outcomes that distinguish short-term wound-healing responses from clinically meaningful periodontal outcomes. Where appropriate, future studies should incorporate probing pocket depth, clinical attachment level, bleeding on probing, standardized wound-healing measures, patient-reported outcomes, and microbiological or biomarker endpoints. Longer follow-up periods are particularly important to determine whether early improvements in wound healing translate into sustained periodontal stability or regeneration. Comparative trials against established adjunctive therapies should also include appropriate control groups and standardized maintenance protocols. Such methodological harmonization would substantially strengthen the evidence base and clarify which clinical settings, patient populations, and formulations are most likely to benefit from topical oxygen therapy.

Conclusions

Topical oxygen therapy represents a promising adjunctive modality for enhancing oral wound healing by improving tissue oxygenation and modulating multiple biological pathways involved in repair. Unlike conventional antiseptic therapies that primarily target microbial reduction, oxygen-releasing formulations appear to promote host-mediated healing through coordinated effects on cellular metabolism, angiogenesis, collagen synthesis, epithelial regeneration, immune regulation and maintenance of a favorable wound microenvironment. The current body of clinical evidence suggests that topical oxygen-releasing formulations provide potential adjunctive benefits in oral wound management, particularly in postoperative healing and selected periodontal applications.
