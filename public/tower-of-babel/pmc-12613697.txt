QTL detection, validation, candidate genes and KASP markers for pre-harvest sprouting tolerance in white grained common wheat
Authors: Manoj Kumar, Sachin Kumar, Neeraj Kumar, Vivudh Pratap Singh, Hemant Sharma, Sourabh Kumar, Lucas Boatwright, Ron E Knox, Jai Prakash Jaiswal, Harindra Singh Balyan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPre-harvest sprouting (PHS) in common wheat refers to the premature germination of grains on the spike under high humid conditions. This phenomenon increase alpha-amylase activity and degrade starch, resulting in reduced falling number (FN) and compromised milling and baking quality. The genetic control of PHS is complex and polygenic, presenting a significant challenge but also offering opportunities for trait improvement through targeted breeding.ResultsA doubled haploid (DH) population comprising 386 lines, derived from a cross between two white-grained spring wheat genotypes SC8021-V2 (PHS-tolerant) and AC Karma (moderately susceptible to PHS), were evaluated for sprouting score (SS) and FN traits across two locations over two crop seasons in India. The population was genotyped using the Infinium iSelect 90K SNP array. Using composite interval mapping (CIM) and R/qtl, 29 main-effect QTL (MQTL) for SS and FN explaining 2.10–8.63% (SS) and 2.10-10.93% (FN) of the phenotypic variation were identified on 13 chromosomes. CIM exclusively revealed 9 MQTL (SS, 7; FN, 1; SS + FN, 1), R/qtl exclusively revealed 8 MQTL (SS, 6; FN, 2), and 12 MQTL (SS, 6; FN, 5; SS + FN, 1) were revealed by both; 16 MQTL were not previously reported. Nineteen first-order epistatic interactions involving 18 SNPs were also detected. We validated six stable MQTL for SS by confirming their favourable alleles in five highly PHS-tolerant DH lines. A set of 368 candidate genes encoding 42 distinct domains were identified; twenty-two were associated with ABA signaling. Candidate genes have multifaceted role regulating seed dormancy, hormone signaling, seed coat formation, starch metabolism, and stress adaptation contributing to PHS resilience. Three breeder-friendly KASP markers for three QTL for sprouting score (two on 2B, one on 4B) were developed and validated.ConclusionsThis study provides novel insights and discovered 16 previously unreported QTL, epistatic interactions, and key candidate genes, highlighting the complex regulation of PHS-related traits. The availability of three diagnostic KASP markers offer practical tools for marker-assisted selection, facilitating the genetic improvement of PHS in common wheat.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07486-7.Keywords: Wheat, Pre-harvest sprouting, QTL interval mapping, R/qtl, Candidate genes, KASP markers

Background

Pre-harvest sprouting (PHS) is a serious problem that occurs in cereal crops at the grain ripening stage when moisture levels in the environment are high or protracted. PHS in wheat spikes is particularly problematic, leading to low falling numbers (FN), reduced seed viability, degraded grain quality, and lowered production each with immediate economic consequences [1]. Annual economic loss from PHS in wheat was estimated to be close to $1 billion [2, 3]. Moreover, PHS is occurring more frequently across wheat-growing regions due to intensifying climate variability. One of the most cost-effective solutions is cultivating PHS-tolerant wheat varieties characterized by high FN value (>300 s). Generally, FN values of 300 s and above suggest soundness in the state of the seed starch, but values below 300 s frequently indicate that certain seeds have broken dormancy, which negatively impacts the quality of products made from their flour [4]. This necessitates more research and understanding into PHS mechanisms, quantitative trait loci (hereafter referred to as QTL) and key regulatory genes with the goal of developing PHS-tolerant wheat varieties with high FN values.

In wheat, the mechanism of PHS tolerance is complex and influenced by the combination of a variety of factors, which include genetics, environmental conditions, hormonal balance, germination inhibitors present in chaff, the morphology of spikes, seed coat color, seed dormancy and alpha-amylase activation [5–7]. PHS in wheat surges at maturity if the grains are not sufficiently dormant; hence dormancy level in the matured grains has been recognized as a key factor in protection against PHS damage [7–9]. Phenotypic screening of experimental lines for PHS is straightforward and largely based on the visual sprouting of intact spikes under artificial rain simulation chambers. Threshed seeds, on the other hand, are used to characterize PHS based on seed dormancy by evaluating germination index, germination percentage, and germination resistance [10–12]. Although the Hagberg FN test is more complex in phenotyping, it remains widely accepted for detecting alpha-amylase activity from PHS [13, 14]. In wheat, FN quantifies the time in seconds it takes for a weighted plunger to fall through a suspension of wheat flour slurry derived from artificially weathered grains [15, 16]. In addition to PHS, late maturity alpha-amylase (LMA) is a potential cause of low FN and varietal differences with respect to their susceptibility to LMA have been observed in recent studies [17–20].

The genetic regulation of PHS is multi-genic [21–24]. QTL interval mapping and/or association mapping are two powerful approaches to identifying the genetic loci associated with PHS tolerance. The first report on the identification of QTL associated with PHS resistance in wheat was reported using RFLP markers [25]. Since then, QTL for PHS tolerance have been mapped on all 21 wheat chromosomes using a variety of molecular markers [11]. However, major QTL for PHS tolerance were consistently detected on chromosomes of groups 3 and 4. In this context, WheatQTLdb v1.0 and v2.0 (www.wheatqtldb.net) have reported over 250 QTL for PHS tolerance [26, 27]. Furthermore, individual QTL studies were utilized to conduct a meta-analysis of those QTL and consensus genomic regions for PHS tolerance were demarcated [28–30]. In the recent meta-QTL analysis study on PHS tolerance [31], data from 188 QTL from 40 independent studies and 28 characterized genes was utilized to identify 66 meta-QTL dispersed on all 21 wheat chromosomes.

The QTL for PHS tolerance reported on group 3 and 4 chromosomes have been subjected to extensive genetic analysis as they have shown significant impacts on PHS [31, 32]. To date, a few full-length genes for PHS tolerance have also been cloned and characterized across multiple investigations. These include (i) TaPHS1/TaMFT, TaDOG1, TaVp1 and Tamyb10 located on group 3 chromosomes, (ii) Phs-A1, TaMKK3, TaPM19-A1, TaPM19-A2 located on group 4, (iii) TaQsdr1, TaSdr located on group 2, (iv) TaABI4 located on group 1, and (v) TaQsd1 located on group 5 [6, 30, 33, 34]. Recently, the gene TaPHS1 from the short arm of chromosome 3A (3AS) of cultivar Zenkoujikomugi (Zen) and the TaMKK3 (4AL) and TaQsd1 (5BL), both derived from cultivar AUS1408, were pyramided in the triticale (XTriticosecale Wittmack) cultivar Cosinus [35]. The authors revealed that only the TaPHS1 gene on 3AS consistently enhances resistance to PHS in triticale.

Previously, a number of studies have been conducted to understand the genetic control of PHS tolerance but the genetic mechanisms of PHS tolerance is yet to be fully figured out. Opportunity exists for the discovery of new loci utilizing the potential genetic materials, multilocation phenotyping of the trait parameters and QTL analysis methods. Based on our previous knowledge, seed dormancy plays a critical role in conferring PHS tolerance, with both major and minor QTL contributing to the trait [11]. Building on this foundation, the present study evaluated the SC8021-V2/AC Karma doubled haploid population in the Indian environments, specifically within the Indo-Gangetic Plains (IGP) at two distinct locations, where environmental variables can significantly affect wheat performance [36]. This study focuses on how the genetic factors respond to the unique environmental conditions in India, expanding our understanding of PHS tolerance. Additionally, we aimed to discover QTL related to the FN, which is essential for determining wheat grain quality. By utilizing a denser genetic map of the SC8021-V2/AC Karma population, coupled with two models of MQTL (main-effect QTL) detection, the present research enhances the precision of MQTL for both PHS tolerance and FN traits. Epistatic QTL and their first order interactions were also detected. Six selected stable MQTL were validated in highly PHS-tolerant doubled-haploid lines. The SNPs associated with QTL for PHS tolerance were converted into high-throughput breeder-friendly Kompetitive Allele Specific PCR (KASP) markers, which were validated for their practical utility in marker-assisted selection (MAS) strategies.

Methods

Mapping population and experimental trials

A doubled haploid (DH) mapping population consisting of 386 lines was derived from a cross between SC8021-V2 and AC Karma, which was previously described in detail [11]. Briefly, the female parent SC8021-V2 was developed as a PHS-tolerant line, and the male parent AC Karma is moderately susceptible to PHS. A high level of phenotypic variation for PHS traits was observed in the DH population across Canadian environments [11]. In the current research, the 386 DH lines, two parents SC8021-V2 and AC Karma, and six check genotypes AUS1408 [8], CN10955 [37, 38], RL4137 [39], Genesis [40], SPR8198 [41], and HD2967 [42] were seeded in a alpha-lattice design with three replications in field trials during the crop seasons 2017-18 and 2018-19 at two different locations, namely Agricultural Research Farm, Chaudhary Charan Singh University, Meerut (28.9692° N, 77.7405° E.) and Cereal Research Centre, Govind Ballabh Pant University of Agriculture and Technology, Pantnagar (29.0229° N, 79.4879° E.), India. Each experimental plot consisted of four rows, 1.5 m long, with 2 g seeds per row. Rows were spaced 20 cm apart, and plots were separated by an empty row to facilitate access for sampling. The field management, including fertilizer application, irrigation, interculture, pest and disease control, adhered to the standard field practices. The field trials conducted at Meerut and Pantnagar locations during crop seasons 2017–2018 and 2018–2019 were hereafter referred to as M18 and M19 and P18 and P19, respectively.

Phenotypic evaluation for PHS

Two PHS-related parameters, namely, sprouting score (SS) and falling number (FN) for each DH line, parents, and check genotypes, were recorded as earlier described in detail [34]. For each replication and environment, 15 random spikes were hand-harvested at physiological maturity by cutting the peduncle about 10 cm below the base of the first spikelet. Harvested spikes were air-dried at room temperature (25 °C) for five days. For SS, five spikes of each experimental unit were used to access visual sprouting through a spike-wetting test which was performed in an artificial rain simulator (mist chamber). The spikes were sprayed for 30 min every 6 h over seven days. Sprouting data were recorded on a scale of 1 to 9, where a value of 1 represented no visible sprouting and a value of 9 represented normal complete sprouting (modified after [43]).

To quantify the variation in FN, the remaining 10 spikes of each experimental line were artificially weathered in a mist chamber for 48 h to enhance the expression of the alpha-amylase enzyme, as described earlier [34]. The weathered spikes were dried in a hot-air oven, ensuring threshed seeds reached 12–14% moisture content. The moisture content in each dried sample was estimated by the FOSS Infratec™ 1241 Grain Analyzer. Each dried seed sample was ground to flour using Perten Laboratory Mill 3100. About 25 g of flour was properly mixed with water to prepare the slurry in glass tubes, then heated in a 100 °C water bath to gelatinize the starch. A Perten FN1100 apparatus [14] was used to determine the FN by monitoring a plunger that travelled through the flour slurry and counting the number of seconds (s) the plunger took to reach the bottom of the test tube.

Statistical analysis of phenotypic data

Initially, the studentized residual diagnostic (rstudent) [44] was used to identify outliers in the phenotypic data. The SPSS v16.0 software was used for calculating mean, median, range, standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV) for both the traits (SS and FN). Pearson’s correlation coefficient, frequency distribution and scatter plots were generated using the package ‘PerformanceAnalytics’ version 2.0.8 in R (https://github.com/braverock/PerformanceAnalytics). Best linear unbiased estimates (BLUEs) were calculated for each trait across replications and environments, treating genotypes as fixed effects and environments (locations and years) as random effects, using META-R [45]. The BLUE values, which minimize environmental variation and provide reliable means, were subsequently used for QTL analysis with CIM and R/qtl approaches. Heritability was estimated separately for each trait using the R package lme4 [46], treating genotypes as a random effect with the model: H2 = σ2g/(σ2g + σ2e), where H2 is the broad-sense heritability, σ2g is the genotypic variance, and σ2e is the residual variance [46].

Genomic DNA extraction and SNP genotyping

Fresh young leaves (~ 5 cm) from the seedlings of two parents and 386 DH lines were sampled for genomic DNA extraction and purification using BioSprint 96 DNA Plant Kit as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Following quantification using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer, each DNA sample was diluted to 250 ng/µl for SNP genotyping.

The DNA samples of parents and DH population were shipped to the National Research Council-Plant Biotechnology Institute (NRC-PBI), University of Saskatchewan, Saskatoon, Canada, for high-throughput SNP genotyping using the wheat Infinium iSelect 90K SNP array that originally contained 81,587 SNP probes [47]. Fluorescence signals were detected using Illumina’s iScan reader following whole-genome amplification and single base extension (SBE) step for differentiating two alleles per locus among experimental lines.

Additionally, genotypic data of 48 polymorphic markers (Table S1), involving 21 gene-based (related to PHS tolerance) and 27 SSRs (Fig. S1), were also available for all the 386 DH lines of the SC8021-V2/AC Karma population from other studies [11]. This genotypic data was included with the 90K SNP data for linkage map construction and QTL discovery.

SNP filtering and construction of genetic linkage map

The raw intensity data received from the iScan reader were utilized for clustering and calling each SNP using the genotyping module of GenomeStudio v2012 software following Illumina’s recommendations (Illumina Inc.) [48]. The GenTrain2 function implemented in GenomeStudio was used for auto-clustering with default parameters. The numbers and positions of clusters for each SNP were taken into consideration since several SNP clusters were fuzzy. Therefore, all SNPs were visually examined using Genoplot and classified as polymorphic, monomorphic, failed and indistinguishable. Clearly distinct clusters, according to the segregation of SNP alleles in the parental genotypes as well as among DH lines, were accounted to be polymorphic SNPs. The genotypic calls of each polymorphic SNP were exported as genotype calls for linkage analysis.

The genotypic data of polymorphic SNP marker obtained from the 90K array were combined with the data of SSR and gene-based markers. In this manner, a complete genotypic dataset was employed for building a genetic linkage map using the software package MSTMap [49], which was further refined in JoinMap v4.1 software [50], with a LOD ≥ 5 and a recombination frequency of 0.30. The chi-square goodness-of-fit test (p < 0.05) to the expected Mendelian segregation ratio of 1:1 was determined for each marker. Markers showing significantly distorted segregation and a missing rate of 20% were removed from linkage map construction. Recombination fractions were translated into centimorgan (cM) map distances using the Kosambi mapping function [51]. The final map of each linkage group was drawn using MapChart 2.2 software [52].

QTL mapping for SS and FN

Main-effect QTL (MQTL) analysis

QTL analysis was conducted on the SC8021-V2/AC Karma DH population utilizing genotypic data of 9,333 markers and multi-environments phenotypic data generated on the two PHS parameters (SS and FN). QTL with main-effect (additive) i.e., MQTL were detected using two methods. (i) Firstly, composite interval mapping (CIM) using WinQTLCart v2.5 software [53] was employed with forward and backward regression of standard model 6 for QTL discovery amidst background marker selection as cofactors. The window size was set at 10 cM, and the walk speed at 1 cM. The permutation test with 1,000 random repeats at a significance level of p < 0.05 [54] was used to calculate a genome-wide threshold Logarithm of the Odds (LOD) score for each trait in order to identify significant QTL. The percentage of phenotypic variation explained (% PVE) was used to measure the influence of the identified QTL for SS and FN. (ii) Secondly, R/qtl [55–57] in the R software 3.6.2 [58] environment was also used for QTL discovery. We estimated genetic maps and performed interval mapping using the scanone function for detecting significant QTL for each trait parameter and the bayesint function to generate Bayesian credible intervals. Genome-wide LOD scores were estimated using the expectation maximization (EM) algorithm, and a significant QTL was declared at >2.5 LOD score. The multiple QTL model was used to estimate the QTL effects using the Haley-Knott regression model [59].

When multiple QTL occurred in the same genomic region with overlapping flanking intervals, they were merged as a single QTL region. A ‘stable QTL’ referred to a QTL that was mapped to the same marker position for the same trait and demonstrated significance in at least two distinct environments. On the other hand, a ‘pleiotropic QTL’ was a QTL mapped to the same marker position for different trait parameters that were not highly correlated. In cases where genome coordinates of the identified QTL did not overlap with the genome coordinates of QTL reported in WheatQTLdb v2.0 (http://www.wheatqtldb.net/) [27], the QTL was regarded as novel. The naming of identified QTL followed the updated guidelines for gene symbolization/nomenclature in wheat [60]. Briefly, QTL were named as follows: Q + trait/parameter initial +. + institute/laboratory name + – + chromosome at which QTL is located + QTL order on the chromosome. For instance, the QTL QSS.ccsu–2B.2 specifies that this is a second QTL associated with sprouting score located on chromosome 2B of wheat discovered at Chaudhary Charan Singh University (CCSU), Meerut, India.

Epistatic QTL (EQTL) analysis

A two-locus analysis (specifically, additive-by-additive interaction analysis) was conducted to identify and map epistatic QTL for SS and FN using the ICIM-EPI mapping module of QTL IciMapping software [61]. The LOD threshold value used to declare significant interactions between QTL was 4.0, scanning step 1 cM, and stepwise regression probability < 0.0001. All epistatic QTL interactions were visualized with the ‘circlize’ package using the R software [62].

Comparison of QTL with known genes for PHS tolerance

To examine whether QTL identified in the present study are co-localized with the previously known gene for PHS tolerance, the tag sequences of SNPs as well as primer sequences of SSRs and genes associated with QTL for SS and FN, were subjected to a BLAST search against Chinese Spring reference genome sequences IWGSC RefSeq version 2.1 at EnsemblPlants database (http://www.ensembl.org/info/docs/tools/vep/index.html). From this analysis, the physical positions of the sequences were subsequently obtained. In a similar way, the physical positions of previously known genes related to PHS tolerance traits/parameters were also retrieved. Physical coordinates (Mb) of QTL detected in this study and the previously known genes were compared by projecting their actual positions on chromosomes using MapChart v2.3 [52]. In the wheat genome, the linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay has been reported to occur over a distance of roughly 5.0 Mb [34, 63]. Consequently, two loci, such as a QTL and a gene, were regarded as co-localized if they were physically mapped within the 5.0 Mb genomic region on the same chromosome. On the other hand, QTL regions that did not overlap with any previously documented QTL within this genomic window were classified as novel QTL.

Validation of selected MQTL for PHS tolerance

In order to validate six selected and important MQTL (described in the results section) for sprouting score (SS) that determine PHS tolerance in wheat, the presence of the favourable alleles for these MQTL were examined in five DH lines each that were extremely PHS-tolerant and susceptible. The number of favourable alleles for the selected MQTL in the above two sets of DH lines will be helpful in validation of the important MQTL and their possible use in marker assisted selection (MAS) for improvement of PHS tolerance in wheat.

Prediction and annotation of candidate genes underpinning QTL regions

The physical positions of the nearest flanking markers (SNPs/SSRs/genes) of the significant QTL for SS and FN were retrieved. For this, sequences of associated markers were BLAST searched against Chinese Spring reference genome sequences v2.1 (IWGSC RefSeq v2.1) at the EnsemblPlants database (http://www.ensembl.org/info/docs/tools/vep/index.html). The upstream and downstream regions from QTL peak to the flanking markers were used for the prediction of underlying candidate gene (CG) models. The complete information for proteins encoded by the CGs was obtained from the Interpro description using the Biomart tool implemented in the EnsemblPlants database (accessed on 07 April 2024). The functional annotations of the identified CGs model were verified from the published reports to determine their putative roles in controlling the PHS traits. Additionally, the genetic base of these CG models in relation to PHS tolerance (germination/dormancy) was explored and visualized through knowledge graphs generated using the KnetMiner database (http://knetminer.com/Triticum_aestivum/).

Development and validation of KASP markers for PHS tolerance

The SNP tag sequences associated with 10 stable MQTL were converted to Kompetitive Allele Specific PCR (KASP) markers. For each SNP marker, the two locus-specific primers and a common primer were designed using PolyMarker (http://www.polymarker.info/), which is an online automated bioinformatics workflow for designing locus-specific SNP assays by referring to the IWGSC RefSeq v2.0 (Table S2). For KASP assays, two locus-specific primers attaching the standard FAM and HEX tails at the 5’ end and the target SNP at the 3’ end and a common primer were together synthesized by IDT (IDT, India). Laboratory protocols for the preparation of the KASP assay mix and PCR conditions were used as per the recommendation of LGC Genomics (http://www.lgcgroup.com/). Briefly, one KASP genotyping assay was tested in a 96-well plate per reaction setup as 5 µl volume containing 2.0 µl (25 ng/µl) genomic DNA, 2.0 µl KASP master mix, 0.11 µl of KASP primers, and 0.89 µl ddH20. On each SNP reaction plate, at least one water sample was included as a no-template control (NTC). The PCR cycling was performed with the following profile: initial hot-start step at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 10 touchdown cycles (95 °C for 20 s, touchdown at 65 °C to 57 °C, decreasing 1 °C per cycle, 25 s) and 35 cycles of amplification at 94 °C for 20 s and 57 °C for 60 s. Allele calls, based on fluorescence data of amplified products, were detected using Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 qPCR system. These newly designed KASP markers were validated on (i) a random subset of the DH population used in the present study, which was derived from a cross between SC8021-V2 and AC Karma [11], and (ii) a subset of random 96 spring wheat genotypes [34], known for their tolerance and susceptibility to PHS.

Results

Phenotypic variations

The mean values of the parents and check genotypes (hereafter simply referred to as checks) for the two PHS parameters, SS and FN, differed across four environments (years and locations). SC8021-V2, SPR8198, CN10955 and RL4137 were sprouting-tolerant; AUS1408 was moderately tolerant. HD2967 and Genesis (except P18 and P19) were most susceptible, and AC Karma was moderately susceptible (Fig. 1a). The BLUE values of FN among checks ranged from 120 to 560 s in all the four environments. AC Karma (except M18), SC8021-V2, SPR8198, AUS1408, CN10955 and RL4137 had high FN (≥300 s), whereas HD2967 and Genesis exhibited lower (< 300 s) FN in all the environments (Fig. 1b). Higher FN values were observed in 2019 compared to 2018 trials at both the locations. Among checks, ranking from sprouting tolerance to sprouting susceptible was fairly consistent with earlier studies [10, 11].

High-density genetic map

Of the 81,587 SNPs genotyped on SC8021-V2/AC Karma population with 386 DH lines, as many as 71,616 (87.8%) were discarded, leaving 9,971 (12.2%) high-quality polymorphic SNPs for map construction. In addition, genotypic data of 27 SSRs and 21 gene-based markers were also combined with the 9,971 SNPs, which together were used for linkage analysis. Out of these 10,019 markers, 2,618 were filtered by the chi-square test and missing values. As a result, a total of 7,401, involving 7,353 SNPs and 48 SSRs/gene-based markers, were anchored on 23 linkage groups and assigned to 21 wheat chromosomes. Genetic maps of 6D and 7D chromosomes were each split into two separate linkage groups, hence labelled as 6D1, 6D2 and 7D1, 7D2. The marker distribution on individual chromosomes and their genetic length is shown in Fig. 2. The high-density linkage map of SC8021-V2/AC Karma population had a total map distance of 3,526 cM resulting in an average marker spacing of 0.80 cM. The number of markers per chromosome differed from 24 (4D) to 973 (2B), with a mean of 352.43 markers per chromosome. Among homoeologous groups, a maximum of 1,500 markers were mapped on the group 2 chromosomes, followed by 1,265 on group 5 chromosomes and a minimum of 583 markers mapped on group 4 chromosomes. Likewise, more markers were mapped on the B sub-genome (3,939 covered 1,259.2 cM), followed by the A sub-genome (2,735 covered 1,367 cM) and D sub-genome (727 covered 899.9 cM) (Fig. 2). Based on the positions of SSRs and SNPs, the order of these mapped markers over the individual chromosome resembled the genetic linkage maps of wheat published elsewhere [48, 64, 65].

QTL interval mapping of PHS tolerance

The genome-wide significant LOD threshold (p ≤ 0.05) for declaring the presence of QTL obtained by the 1000 permutations for each dataset ranged from 2.28 to 3.67. At these thresholds, a total of 29 MQTL were found to be associated with preharvest sprouting (PHS) tolerance-related parameters SS and FN across four environments (M18, M19, P18 and P19) and genotypic data of 7,401 markers. A summary of the total number of QTL detected for two trait parameters (SS and FN) and two QTL interval mapping methods (CIM and R/qtl) are given in Table 2, and comprehensive data of these QTL are presented in Table 3 and Fig. S3. Among these 29 MQTL, 17 QTL were detected through individual methods (09 with CIM and 08 with R/qtl), whereas 12 QTL were jointly detected with both the methods (CIM and R/qtl), of which one QTL on chromosome 2D was identified for both parameters SS and FN (Table 2).

As many as 13 different wheat chromosomes revealed QTL for PHS tolerance; the minimum number of one QTL each was located on six chromosomes (2A, 4B, 4D, 5D, 6B and 7A) and the maximum number of seven QTL were located on chromosome 5A. More QTL were detected on the A sub-genome (6), followed by the B sub-genome (4), and the D sub-genome (3). QTL exhibiting stability across more than two environments were successfully mapped to chromosomes 2B and 4B for SS, and 2D and 5A for SS and FN. The estimated phenotypic variation explained (PVE) by an individual QTL ranged between 2.10 and 10.93%. The favorable alleles were contributed by both the parents; 18 alleles with positive additive effects originated from SC8021-V2, while 11 negative-effect alleles came from AC Karma, increasing the effects of the QTL for PHS tolerance based on SS and FN (Table 3).

For SS, 19 QTL were identified on 11 chromosomes including 1A, 2A, 2B, 2D, 3A, 3B, 4B, 5A, 5D, 6A and 7A, with individual QTL explaining 2.10–8.63% of the phenotypic variance (Table 3). Of these, six QTL (QSS.ccsu-3A.1, QSS.ccsu-3A.4, QSS.ccsu-5A.3, QSS.ccsu-5A.4, QSS.ccsu-5D, and QSS.ccsu-6A.2) were detected by both the CIM and R/qtl methods, while remaining seven and six QTL were distinctly detected by CIM and R/qtl, respectively. A maximum of four QTL peaks each appeared on chromosomes 3A (89.8, 98.8, 182.0, and 195.6 cM) and 5A (62.8, 68.5, 70.6, and 73.5 cM) followed by three peaks on chromosome 2B (115.6, 128.0, and 130.3 cM).

For FN, eight QTL were discovered on six different chromosomes (1A, 2D, 4D, 5A, 6B, and 7A), and each of these QTL explained a portion of the phenotypic variation, ranging from 2.11% to 10.93%. Notably, a maximum of five QTL (namely, QFN.ccsu-4D, QFN.ccsu-5A.3, QFN.ccsu-5A.4, QFN.ccsu-6B, and QFN.ccsu-7A.2) were jointly identified using both CIM and R/qtl methods. Chromosome 5A was particularly noteworthy, as it exhibited four QTL peaks at genetic positions of 68.5, 163.4, 171.4, and 174.8 cM (Table 3).

Furthermore, there were two QTL regions located on chromosomes 2D (QSS.ccsu-2D and QFN.ccsu-2D at 59.8 cM) and 5A (QSS.ccsu-5A.2 and QFN.ccsu-5A.1 at 68.5 cM) that exhibited pleiotropic effects. Each of these QTL was associated with both the parameters SS and FN, and the favorable alleles for these QTL originated from the PHS tolerant parent SC8021-V2 (Table 3).

Epistatic analysis

In the present study, 19 pairs of significant digenic (two-locus) epistatic QTL (EQTL) were identified, which included 11 pairs for SS and eight pairs for FN at a minimum LOD value of 4.00. A graphical presentation of QTL interactions is displayed in Fig. 3, and detailed information on environments, epistatic QTL-associated marker intervals, phenotypic variation explained and additive effects of SS and FN are presented in Table S4. These epistatic QTL explained 2.61–4.64% (for SS) and 2.44–4.55% (FN) of the phenotypic variation. The epistatic QTL were distributed on 17 of the 21 wheat chromosomes barring chromosomes 4D, 5D, 6A and 6B. None of the epistatic QTL involved MQTL for either trait parameter. Two different QTL detected in M18 and P18 sites for SS located on chromosome 4A at 120.0 cM position with marker interval BobWhite_c19919_516 – BS00009492_51 and at 125.0 cM with marker interval wsnp_Ex_c19773_28772235 – BS00041735_51 showed interactions with a solitary QTL located on chromosome 7A at 45.0 cM position with marker interval wsnp_Ex_c17899_26666328 – wsnp_Ex_c7071_12171222. Favourable SNP alleles for selected MQTL in five PHS-tolerant DH lines

We also examined the composition of favorable SNP alleles for six key, stable MQTL for SS that is responsible for PHS tolerance in five extremely PHS-tolerant DH lines derived from the SC8021-V2/AC Karma cross (Table 4). Candidate genes prediction underpinning MQTL

To further understand the genetic control of PHS tolerance using SS and FN parameters, CGs harboring the identified significant QTL were investigated. A total number of 368 candidate gene models were predicted underlying 29 MQTL associated with PHS tolerance based on SS and FN parameters. Detailed information on CGs and their functional annotations are presented in Fig. 4 and Table S5. It was shown that the transcripts of these 368 putative CGs might potentially encode up to 42 distinct kinds of proteins/domains, of which twenty-two were involved in ABA signaling pathway. Some of the important domains contained in more than 10 CGs include: (i) serine/threonine protein kinase, (ii) leucine-rich repeat domain superfamily, (iii) F-box domain superfamily, (iv) zinc finger, RING-type, (v) Armadillo (vi) tetratricopeptide-like helical domain superfamily, (vii) homeobox-like domain superfamily, (viii) SANT/Myb domain, (ix) glycoside hydrolase family, (x) cytochrome P450, etc. (Fig. 4). To understand their potential involvement in regulating PHS tolerance, the functional annotations of selected CGs were verified from the published literature (Table S5).

Frequencies of candidate genes (with functions related to pre-harvest sprouting tolerance) encoding proteins containing 43 different domains

A maximum of 58 CGs were identified under a QTL QSS.ccsu-6A.2 (PVE 7.14%) on chromosome 6A for SS that mapped in the marker-interval between BS00066623_51 (72.9 cM) – IAAV151 (80.0 cM). Similarly, 41 CGs were associated with QTL QFN.ccsu-5A.4 on chromosome 5A for FN at marker-interval Kukri_c108256_381 (174.8 cM) – RFL_Contig4543_1706 (184.4 cM). Additionally, 49 genes encoded as members of the leucine-rich repeat domain superfamily were detected in the genomic regions of 15 different QTL mapped on 11 chromosomes. These genes act to delay germination, decrease whole root development, and increase susceptibility to ABA. The study also identified 41 CGs containing F-box domain in 15 different QTL regions across eight chromosomes (Table S5).

The markers associated with 29 MQTL for SS and FN were physically mapped alongside 12 cloned genes well-known for their involvement in PHS tolerance across different wheat chromosomes (Fig. S3). Additionally, the gene-based marker Vp-1A_A7-8, mapped at 659.6 Mb on chromosome 3A, was found near the same TaVp1 gene. On chromosome 5A, the gene TaQsd1 was positioned 2.6 Mb away from QSS.ccsu-5A.1 (Fig. 5).

KnetMiner-based gene network analysis

To understand the genetic basis of PHS tolerance in more depth, gene network analysis of all the 368 CG models using KnetMiner database were conducted. Of which, 20 CGs revealed a rich and direct associations with the PHS traits, including SS and FN (Fig. 5 and Fig. S4). Among these, two CGs TraesCS3A02G417300 (QSS.ccsu-3A.2) and TraesCS5A02G216000 (QSS.ccsu-5A.1) exhibited prominent functional associations with PHS tolerance mechanisms. TraesCS3A02G417300, positioned at 659.6 Mb on chromosome 3A, was associated with key biological processes such as abscisic acid (ABA) signaling, allantoin metabolism, aleurone layer degradation, seed coat development, and embryo maturation. Notably, this gene is co-located near the cloned gene TaVp1 (at 659.8 Mb) (Fig. 5). Similarly, TraesCS5A02G216000, located at 432.2 Mb on chromosome 5A, showed strong associations with seed dormancy, germination, seed development, and PHS-related physiological pathways. This gene is positioned within proximity of TaQsd1 (at 432.6 Mb), a cloned gene known for its role in seed dormancy regulation (Fig. 5).

KnetMiner analysis for the remaining 18 CG models associated with SS and FN revealed several biological connections for PHS tolerance and their results are presented in (Fig. S4). For instance, the gene TraesCS1A02G258800 corresponding to QSS.ccsu-1A is connected to seed dormancy and jasmonic acid signaling, mediated through MYC1 and TaMYB10, suggesting a role in hormonal regulation and seed coat color. TraesCS2B02G390600 and TraesCS2B02G427300 (QSS.ccsu-2B.1 and QSS.ccsu-2B.2) showed strong connections with TaABI3 and TaMYB10, which are related to seed dormancy, seed coat pigmentation, and seed maturation. TraesCS3B02G452200 (QSS.ccsu-3B.1) is notable for its involvement in UDP-glucuronate synthesis and pre-harvest sprouting regulation [66]. TraesCS5A02G217900, TraesCS5A02G244500, and TraesCS5A02G245900 (QSS.ccsu-5A.1 and QSS.ccsu-5A.3) displayed functional connections with starch biosynthesis, pericarp color, sugar content, and stem elongation, with evidence from TaGBSS1 and other related genes. Candidate genes underlying QTL on chromosome 6A, like TraesCS6A02G089400 and TraesCS6A02G313800, are linked to hormone signaling (jasmonic acid, abscisic acid), stress responses, and seedling vigor (Fig. S4).

TraesCS4D02G053400 (QFN.ccsu-4D) and CGs on chromosome 5A (TraesCS5A02G51400, TraesCS5A02G468300, and TraesCS5A02G472300) demonstrated roles in abiotic stress tolerance, seed dormancy, salicylic acid regulation, and localization of GAMYB. Furthermore, genes on chromosome 6B such as TraesCS6B02G031300 and TraesCS6B02G031500 (QFN.ccsu-6B) show associations with carbohydrate metabolism, reproductive development, and cell differentiation (Fig. S4).

Discussion

PHS has significantly threatened wheat grain yield and quality, particularly in regions with late-season harvest rainfall [33]. Tolerance to PHS, a multifaceted quantitative trait, is believed to be under the influence of inherent genetic factors [5]. Among different facets, seed dormancy alone plays a pivotal role in determining PHST [67]. The SS, which measures the germination of grains within matured spikes, and the germination index, which measures the germination of threshed grains, stand as common parameters for assessing the genetic control associated with PHS [68, 69]. Additionally, falling number (FN) is an indirect indicator of alpha-amylase activity and, thus, sprout damage not a non-dormancy factor per se [1, 70].

Over the past few decades, researchers have investigated the genetic complexities of PHST in wheat. Employing both QTL interval mapping and genome-wide association mapping methods, numerous studies have illustrated the multifaceted nature of PHST, revealing its control by several QTL and genes distributed across all 21 wheat chromosomes [10, 11, 33, 70–73]. The WheatQTLdb (v1.0 and v2.0; www.wheatqtldb.net/) reports some 599 QTL/MTAs for PHS-related traits [26, 27]. The abundance of loci governing PHST poses a challenge to their deployment in marker-assisted selection (MAS) for improving PHST in wheat. The literature contains many overlapping QTL reports, underscoring the need for meta-analysis. Recognizing this [30], comprehensively searched literature published over 20 years (2001 to 2020) and identified 188 valid QTL from 40 research papers. The authors further conducted a meta-analysis using information from these QTL, resulting in the identification of 66 meta-QTL, which were distributed across all 21 wheat chromosomes. The findings of the meta-analysis not only address the duplication issue prevalent in the literature but also serve as a critical resource for in-depth analysis of PHST in wheat.

In the present investigation, genetic study into PHST through QTL interval mapping has yielded significant findings, shedding light on the molecular basis of this important trait in wheat. The study employed CIM and interval mapping using R/qtl, and examined phenotypic datasets of SS and FN parameters from four distinct environments (M18, M19, P18, and P19) with genotypic data derived from 7,401 markers. Thus, a total of 29 MQTL and 19 pairs of EQTL for the above two parameters of PHST were detected.

Phenotypic variation for SS and FN

The results of the study demonstrate significant variations in mean values of SS and FN, the PHS parameters, among different wheat genotypes across four environments, representing different years and locations. The genotypes SC8021-V2, SPR8198, CN10955, and RL4137 displayed sprouting tolerance, while AUS1408 exhibited moderate tolerance. In contrast, HD2967 and Genesis (except P18 and P19) were identified as the most susceptible to PHS. AC Karma displayed moderate susceptibility to PHS. The observed trends in PHS susceptibility among the check genotypes were largely consistent with previous reports [10, 11], reinforcing the reliability and validity of the results. This consistency in ranking from sprouting tolerance to susceptibility provides valuable insights for wheat breeders and food processors aiming to enhance pre-harvest protection and improve the overall end-use quality of wheat. The identified genotypes with sprouting tolerance and high FN could serve as promising candidates for further breeding programs aimed at developing wheat varieties resilient to PHS under varying environmental conditions.

The ANOVA results underscore the significant contributions of genotype, location, and year, as well as their interactions, to PHST in wheat. The findings provide valuable insights into the multifaceted nature of PHS tolerance and highlight the importance of genetic and environmental factors in breeding strategies aimed at developing wheat varieties resilient to PHS under diverse environmental conditions.

The bell-shaped distributions of SS and FN across different environments suggest a polygenic control of these traits, with multiple genes influencing the observed variability. The presence of transgressive segregants in the mapping population suggests that novel combinations of alleles from the two parental lines contribute to the observed variation in PHS parameters, emphasizing the potential for creating wheat varieties with reduced sprouting susceptibility. Smaller variation in FN versus SS suggests FN may be influenced by fewer major genes and environmental factors.

Moderate to high heritability estimates (0.40–0.77) suggest that the genetic architecture of the SS is relatively stable and heritable, providing a good foundation for selection and breeding efforts aimed at improving PHS tolerance. In contrast, the heritability estimates for FN (0.08–0.33) were relatively low, suggesting that environment variability contributed substantially to the observed phenotypic variation. This emphasizes the intricate genetic regulation of starch breakdown and highlights the critical role of environmental factors in breeding strategies aimed at enhancing FN. Low heritability can reduce the precision of QTL detection because minor-effect loci may be masked by the environmental noise. To overcome this limitation, we have used a large population, multi-environment phenotyping (four environments), precise phenotyping (replications across years and locations), and the use of BLUE values. In addition, QTL detection was performed with two complementary methods (CIM and R/QTL) and supported by permutation testing, which minimized the probability of spurious associations thereby improving the chances to identify real QTL.

Despite the low heritability of FN, a stable and major QTL (QFN.ccsu-2D) with substantial PVE (10.93%) was identified across three of the four environments and two QTL mapping methods. The stability and reproducibility of this locus across multiple environments strongly argue against it being a false positive. Rather, QFN.ccsu-2D likely represents a major-effect locus with reduced sensitivity to environmental fluctuations. Similar findings have been reported in previous such studies. For instance, a major and stable QTL qSl‑2B for spike length was consistently identified across multiple environments and mapping populations of wheat despite the typically low heritability (8.17–10.22%) associated with spike length [74]. It is worth noting that the QTL QFN.ccsu-2D, identified in the present study, has potential utility in future breeding programs aiming to improve FN in wheat.

The observed correlation coefficients between SS and FN provide valuable information about the interplay between SS and the FN across different environments. The moderate but significant correlations suggest a nuanced relationship, and the significantly low negative correlations point towards potential trade-offs or complex interactions between SS and FN in specific conditions. These findings coincide with previous studies, where similar correlations were reported [75, 76].

Genetic linkage map

The construction of a high-density linkage map for the SC8021-V2/AC Karma population represents a crucial step in understanding the genetic architecture of the population. The consistency between the order of SSRs and SNPs in the mapped markers and genetic linkage maps from previously published studies [48, 64] provides confidence in the accuracy and reliability of the newly constructed linkage map. The development of a high-density linkage map for the SC8021-V2/AC Karma population, incorporating a diverse set of markers, offers a comprehensive tool for understanding the genetic basis of traits within this wheat population. The detailed marker distribution across chromosomes, sub-genomes, and homoeologous groups provides a valuable resource for future genetic and breeding projects.

MQTL detected by CIM and R/qtl methods

The same MQTL detected across more than one environment, as well as more than one method of analysis, can be considered consistent or stable MQTL. As many as 11 (~ 38%) of the 29 MQTL for SS and FN were considered stable. Of which, six MQTL associated with both SS and FN were identified by two QTL mapping methods (i.e., CIM and R/qtl) in at least two of the four environments (M18, P18, M19 and P19). Another five QTL related to SS only were detected by any one of the two methods, and such QTL were each identified in more than one environment. The remaining 18 QTL were revealed in only one of the four environments, each detected by a single method. These findings indicate a limited QTL x environment (Q x E) interaction and variations in the effectiveness of the QTL mapping methods. Similarly, 13 of the 29 MQTL detected in this study corresponded with the previously reported QTL, while the remaining 16 were reported for the first time and, thus, considered as novel MQTL.

Over the last five decades, numerous statistical approaches have been applied to QTL analyses. These methods encompass analysis of variance [77], interval mapping (IM) [78], bayesian interval mapping (BIM) [79, 80], composite interval mapping (CIM) [81, 82], multiple interval mapping (MIM) [83], genetic [84], and R/qtl [55–57]. Among these, the CIM method has been extensively used in the discovery of QTL in plants and has evolved into more advanced versions, such as inclusive composite interval mapping (ICIM) [85] and genome-wide composite interval mapping (GCIM) [65], both currently in use. In the present study, only two methods of QTL mapping, namely CIM and R/qtl (Bayesian) were used. These methods differ in their statistical frameworks; CIM is known for its simplicity for Windows users and widespread adoption, particularly in handling genetic background effects. It may be recalled that the CIM also imposes a significant computational burden during permutation test analysis. On the other hand, R/qtl represents implementing the hidden Markov model (HMM) technology to deal with missing genotype data. It is generally considered the better choice due to its wider functionality, flexibility within the R environment, and powerful multiple QTL mapping capabilities (MQM). It also estimates the genetic maps and performs interval mapping using the Bayesint function to generate Bayesian credible intervals. This function incorporated phenotypic covariates into the model, which removed potential confounding effects on the phenotypic characteristics. The results of QTL detection in the present study partially align with these recommendations, as nearly 40% (12/29) of MQTL were commonly detected using CIM and R/qtl methods. In this regard, these MQTL can be considered reliable and would be suitable for deployment in trait improvement programs.

Apart from MQTL, the effect of EQTL in regulating quantitative genetic variation for PHS tolerance in wheat has been determined, and the progressive number of EQTL have revealed their importance [86–90]. The present study adds 19 pairs of significant EQTL, emphasizing that the two-locus interactions (QTL × QTL) play a pivotal role in determining PHS tolerance through SS and FN measures. Although each QTL defines a modest individual contribution, their interaction, particularly within the two-locus genotypes of the parental lines, implies their combined adverse impact on the sprouting. Such interactions are rarely applied in MAS; however, evidence indicates that incorporating epistatic information can enhance genomic prediction models and improve the accuracy of breeding value estimates for complex traits [91]. This highlights the potential relevance of the EQTL detected in our study for future breeding strategies that integrate genomic selection, even though practical examples of applying epistasis in MAS are currently lacking. Nevertheless, it is vital to discover the presence of epistatic interactions for a comprehensive understanding of the genetic architecture related to PHS tolerance. Anticipated future wheat breeding projects are expected to improve quantitative traits by incorporating both additive MQTL and epistatic interactions in their methodologies.

MQTL co-localized with known QTL/genes of PHS tolerance

It is difficult to compare the MQTL that we identified with the QTL reported in other earlier studies because different kinds of molecular markers were used in previous QTL mapping studies. An appropriate way is to use the physical positions of QTL-associated markers to determine the common positions of such QTL [30]. Therefore, the physical positions of all 29 MQTL for SS and FN detected in the present study were aligned with the physical positions of previously mapped QTL for PHS tolerance-related traits. As many as 13 MQTL (QSS.ccsu-2B.1, QSS.ccsu-2D, QSS.ccsu-3A.4, QSS.ccsu-3A.3, QSS.ccsu-3A.2, QSS.ccsu-4B, QSS.ccsu-5A.4, QSS.ccsu-6A.1 for SS, and QFN.ccsu-5A.3, QFN.ccsu-5A.4, QFN.ccsu-6B, QFN.ccsu-7A.2, QFN.ccsu-7A.1 for FN) were found to be consistent with the previous studies [10, 11, 74, 92–105]. Although co-localization with previously reported QTL and known genes supports the validity of our QTL mapping results, further fine mapping and functional validation will be necessary to confirm the causal relationships underlying these genomic regions. Furthermore, co-position does not always imply that two loci are identical, nevertheless, it can undoubtedly assist in the identification of some QTL hotspots that influence the trait expression. Of these 13 MQTL, six MQTL, including four (QSS.ccsu-2B.1, QSS.ccsu-2D, QSS.ccsu-4B, QSS.ccsu-5A.4) for SS, and two (QFN.ccsu-5A.3, QFN.ccsu-5A.4) for FN were detected stably in multiple environments and thus could be considered for use in MAS. These results provide an opportunity to develop diagnostic markers for selecting desirable genotypes with enhanced tolerance to PHS.

Another valuable finding of the present study involves the co-location of three cloned genes with three different MQTL for SS (Table 5), supporting their relevance to PHS tolerance. These genes and related QTL deserve detailed discussion, which may be important for future investigations. (i) TaMFT/TaPHS1: a MOTHER OF FLOWERING TIME (MFT) and TERMINAL FLOWER 1 (TFL1)-like gene of wheat positioned at 7.4 Mb on chromosome 3A is co-located with MQTL QSS.ccsu-3A.4 mapped at SNP marker Kukri_rep_c89183_306 (8.7 Mb), both are merely 1.3 Mb apart (Fig. 5). The MFT (TaMFT)-like gene belongs to phosphatidylethanolamine-binding protein (PEBP) family in higher plants, which plays an important role in regulation of flowering time, seed development, and germination [106]. In previous reports, TaMFT-A1 has been characterized for strong seed dormancy in spring and winter wheat through ABA and GA signaling pathways [6, 89, 107, 108]. Liu et al. [109] cloned a gene from white-seeded wheat, which they designated TaPHS1 instead of TaMFT. However, the structure of the TaMFT and TaPHS1 genes are the same except for some SNPs within the promoter and exon-intron sequences. Additionally, Kumar et al. [11] reported a QTL QPhs.spa-3A in the same region on chromosome 3A using seed dormancy measures, i.e., germination resistance. Martinez et al. [92] also reported a QTL QPHS.wsu-3A.1 in the same genomic region, which emphasizes the importance of the locus for dormancy and, thus, PHS tolerance. (ii) TaVP1: The gene TaVp1 is positioned (659.8 Mb) on the long arm of chromosome 3A (3AL) at a distance of 200 Kb from the QTL QSS.ccsu-3A.2 that we detected with a functional marker Vp-1A_A7-8 mapped at 659.6 Mb. The TaVP1 is an ortholog of the Vp1 (viviparous-1) gene in Zea mays and ABI3 (ABA Insensitive 3) in Arabidopsis thaliana and has a significant effect on seed dormancy in wheat [6, 110–113]. In the past few studies, there has been some disagreement with regard to the role of TaVp1 for seed dormancy or PHS tolerance in wheat [114–116]. Although, six alleles of the TaVp-B1 gene have been identified [117–119], and the KASP assays of some of the alleles, including Vp1B1-83_IND and Vp1B1-193_IND have been developed and validated for the purpose of combining beneficial alleles for improving PHS tolerance through MAS [120]. Further work is required to reveal the effect of QSS.ccsu-3A.2 on the expression of the TaVp1 gene. (iii) TaQsd1: The TaQsd1, an ortholog of Qsd1 in barley, encodes an alanine aminotransferase, was found necessary in the early seed germination [121–123]. This gene is physically co-located (432.6 Mb) with the QTL QSS.ccsu-5A.1 at marker IAAV2080 (430.3); they spanned 2.3 Mb genomic region on chromosome 5A. Till 2018, no functional studies had confirmed the role of Qsd1 for seed dormancy in wheat. Abe et al. [124] used an Agrobacterium-based CRISPR/Cas9 system to generate a transgene-free, triple-recessive mutant of Qsd1 in common wheat. This mutant exhibited significantly prolonged seed dormancy compared to the wild type, leading to a reduced risk of PHS in spikes. This triple-recessive mutation stands as a valuable genetic asset for wheat breeding, introducing an extended seed dormancy period to prevent PHS in other elite cultivars.

Validation of favourable QTL alleles in PHS-tolerant DH lines

Validation of favourable QTL alleles composition in the five superior PHS-tolerant DH lines is among the most notable findings of the present study. These top five DH lines namely 08, 171, 242, 269, and 274 were derived from the SC8021-V2/AC Karma cross and consistently exhibited high levels of tolerance to PHS, with mean SS ranging from 2 to 3. This level of tolerance significantly outperformed the susceptible parent AC Karma (SS 6), highlighting the genetic advantage conferred by the favorable alleles. This further indicates that both parents contributed valuable alleles, and PHS tolerance is governed by the cumulative effect of favorable alleles, regardless of their parental origin. The DH line 171 contained all six favorable alleles, reflecting complete inheritance of the PHS tolerance-associated markers. Lines 08 and 269 each harbored five favorable alleles, while lines 242 and 274 carried four and five favorable alleles, respectively. The accumulation of multiple favorable QTL alleles in these DH lines contributes to enhanced PHS tolerance in wheat, and the validated KASP markers hold potential applications for marker-assisted selection in breeding programs.

On the other hand, five DH lines namely 51, 380, 62, 110 and 289 exhibited extreme susceptibility to PHS with SS values ranging from 7 to 8. Notably, DH lines 62 and 110 carried none of the favorable alleles identified in this study, underscoring a clear negative correlation between the number of favorable alleles and sprouting susceptibility. These observations strongly align with earlier studies that accumulation of favorable alleles enhances tolerance/resistance [16, 125, 126].

Candidate genes related to PHS tolerance

The genes underlying MQTL intervals marked by significant markers, which were related to various aspects of determining PHS tolerance, such as seed germination, ABA/GA biosynthesis, seed color, dormancy, falling number, starch degradation, etc., were considered as potential CGs. The present study predicted 368 putative CGs that encode up to 42 distinct kinds of proteins or domains, providing a comprehensive view of the genetic factors potentially influencing PHS tolerance. Major findings covering important CGs are discussed. For instance, a maximum of 58 CGs within a novel and stable QTL QSS.ccsu-6A.2 region on chromosome 6A for SS (detected by CIM and R/qtl with PVE 7.14%) emphasizes the genetic complexity underlying seed dormancy. The marker interval between BS00066623_51 (72.9 cM) and IAAV151 (80.0 cM) serves as a crucial genomic region for further investigation. Similarly, the prediction of 41 CGs with a stable QTL QFN.ccsu-5A.4 on chromosome 5A for final germination provides additional evidence of the genetic architecture contributing to the overall germination process. The marker-interval Kukri_c108256_381 (174.8 cM) – RFL_Contig4543_1706 (184.4 cM) on chromosome 5A emerges as an important genetic interval for future studies aiming to unravel the molecular mechanisms regulating germination [100]. Moreover, investigating the expression patterns and functional properties of these genes can contribute to a novel understanding of how they collectively influence the final germination process.

The identification of 22 CGs related to the ABA signaling pathway suggests a significant role for this pathway in the context of PHS tolerance. Abscisic acid is a well-known phytohormone involved in various stress responses, including seed dormancy and PHS tolerance [71, 90, 127–130]. Focusing on these specific genes offers a targeted approach for further investigations into the molecular mechanisms linking ABA signaling to PHS tolerance. Additionally, the present study highlighted the presence of 49 genes encoding members of the leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain superfamily in genomic regions associated with 15 different MQTL on 11 chromosomes. These genes are predicted to modulate germination and root development, shedding light on their role in PHS tolerance [131]. The involvement of LRR proteins in seed dormancy has been reported earlier in various species [132–134]. More detailed studies are imperative to validate and fully understand the true functions of these candidate genes. Further investigations could include experiments to study the expression patterns of these genes under different environmental conditions or different phases of seed development.

Networks of genes controlling PHS tolerance

Due to the large number of identified candidate gene models that control PHS tolerance traits, it was worth to investigate the connections of these genes in the biological processes that lead to dormancy/PHS tolerance. For this purpose, gene network analysis was conducted for all the identified gene models and high number of biological connections were observed that are well-known contributors to the regulation of seed dormancy and germination. A proximity (200 Kb) of candidate gene TraesCS3A02G417300 to the TaVp1 gene [114], a major regulator of seed dormancy, supporting its potential role in PHS resistance. TraesCS5A02G216000 demonstrated regulatory linkages with TaQsd1 and TaAIP2, an ABA-insensitive 3-interacting protein known to influence dormancy and sprouting behaviors. Functional relevance of these candidate genes with seed dormancy regulators on chromosomes 3A and 5A, reinforces their potential utility in breeding for enhanced PHS tolerance. Notably, a candidate gene TraesCS2B02G427300 appears to be commonly involved in both SS (germination) and FN (starch degradation), due to its linkage with the transcription factor TaABI3, a known regulator of ABA-mediated seed dormancy, seed coat color, and seed maturation [116]. This gene is underpinned in the QTL regions QSS.ccsu-2B.2 and QFN.ccsu-5A.4, suggesting its functional overlap between the two trait parameters. Therefore, TraesCS2B02G427300 stands out as a common and promising candidate gene affecting SS and FN. Above candidate genes represent promising targets for future validation and functional dissection, if distinct from previously cloned genes, may offer valuable insights for breeding wheat cultivars with improved tolerance to PHS and high end-use quality.

Diagnostic KASP assays for PHS tolerance

The successful conversion of SNPs (Infinium 90K chip) linked to stable MQTL into breeder-friendly KASP markers signifies a vital advancement in the direct practical application for PHS tolerance in wheat. Among the six candidate gene SNPs, three QTL (QSS.ccsu-2B.1 (RAC875_rep_c70705_84), QSS.ccsu-2B.2 (Jagger_c4502_69), and QSS.ccsu-4B (Tdurum_contig47622_234) were efficiently transformed into high-throughput KASP assays, each demonstrating clear and consistent allelic discrimination (Fig. 6). These markers were validated across both the DH population and an independent spring wheat reference set (SWRS), underscoring their robustness and transferability. They can be extended to diverse accessions within an association mapping panel or breeding material.

The favorable SNP alleles G and C from SC8021-V2 for QSS.ccsu-2B.1 and QSS.ccsu-2B.2, respectively, and allele A from AC Karma for QSS.ccsu-4B were significantly associated with reduced sprouting scores (p < 0.05), reinforcing their potential utility in MAS program. This broader validation ensures the reliability and applicability of the developed diagnostic markers across different genetic backgrounds and wheat varieties [24, 94, 102, 130, 135]. More recently, Rajkovic et al. [12] validated KASP assays for previously reported 38 SNP markers associated with PHS resistance using a panel of 200 wheat cultivars adapted to Southeast European environments. The use of KASP assays is encouraged due to their advantages over gel-based PCR markers. KASP assays offer high throughput, cost-effectiveness, and increased efficiency, making them the preferred markers in wheat breeding programs [120, 136]. We encourage immediate integration of validated KASP assays into wheat pre-breeding pipelines aimed at reducing PHS damage, particularly under high-rainfall conditions at crop maturity.

The inability to validate the remaining three markers associated with MQTL QSS.ccsu-5A.3, QSS.ccsu-5A.4, and QSS.ccsu-6A.2 highlights the necessity for further refinement. The lack of clear allelic discrimination for these markers may be attributed to technical limitations in assay design. Additional validation across broader genetic backgrounds and environments may be warranted.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive genetic analysis of PHS tolerance using a common wheat mapping population, with both SS and FN measured. A total of 29 main-effect QTL (16 novel) and 19 epistatic QTL were identified. Several stable loci were consistently validated across environments and methods underscore complex and polygenic control of PHS tolerance. We identified key candidate genes co-localizing with known functional loci (TaMFT, TaVP1 and TaQsd1). The accumulation of favorable alleles in elite DH lines confirms their additive impact on PHS tolerance, and offering promising genetic resources for future breeding. The development and validation of breeder-friendly KASP assays for major and stable main-effect QTL on 2B and 4B chromosomes demonstrated their potential utility for marker-assisted selection. This study also underscore the role of epistatic interactions and the ABA signaling pathway in regulating PHS traits. These findings offer valuable genomic insights and practical tools for enhancing PHS tolerance in wheat breeding.
