Application value of a prognostic risk model based on lactate-related genes in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Jinhua Bao, Jun Gu, Tanao Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Despite therapeutic advances, the outcomes of patients with advanced disease are still poor. This study was conducted to identify pathogenic and prognostic lactate-related genes (LRGs) in NSCLC to improve clinical treatment and prognosis.MethodsConsensus clustering was applied to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) training cohort to delineate subtypes associated with lactate metabolism. On this basis, we established a prognostic signature of LRGs with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression and assessed its predictive value in independent Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets. To clarify the mechanisms related to the model’s ability, we examined immune infiltration, estimated responses to immunotherapy, and performed single-cell analysis. In addition, the transcriptional levels of the prognostic genes were validated via real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) in lung cancer cell lines and compared with alveolar epithelial cells (AECs), thereby strengthening the reliability of the model.ResultsTwo distinct lactate-associated clusters were identified in NSCLC. On this basis, we established an eight-gene prognostic model that stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups with markedly different survival outcomes. The model achieved strong predictive accuracy, with areas under the curve of 0.693, 0.707, and 0.704 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival in the training cohort, and its robustness was further confirmed in external validation datasets. In addition, the lactate risk score showed significant associations with the tumor microenvironment (TME) and revealed clear differences in responsiveness to immunotherapy. Single-cell analysis provided further insight into cell type-specific patterns of lactate metabolism. Finally, RT-qPCR validation of most prognostic genes confirmed the reliability of the model.ConclusionsWe developed an LRG prognostic model for NSCLC, which offers novel insights for both diagnostic assessment and treatment strategies.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Two lactate-associated clusters in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) were identified, and an eight lactate-related gene (LRG) prognostic model was constructed, which demonstrated robust predictive accuracy and was externally validated.

What is known and what is new?

• Lactate serves not only as a crucial energy source but also plays a significant role in regulating metabolism, immune responses, and intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment.

• The lactate risk score exhibited significant prognostic value in NSCLC, with strong correlations to clinical outcomes and survival parameters. Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction revealed the significant differential expression of prognostic genes across various NSCLC cell lines, suggesting their potential functional involvement in NSCLC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Although the LRG prognostic model demonstrates robust predictive performance, the molecular mechanisms underlying the key LRGs in NSCLC remain to be fully elucidated. Future studies will focus on elucidating how these LRGs regulate metabolic pathways.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, while non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constitutes about 85% of all lung cancer cases (1). Due to the absence of effective screening tools and the nonspecific nature of early symptoms, most patients with NSCLC are diagnosed at advanced stages, which results in a poor prognosis (2). Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common subtype of NSCLC, followed by lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) (3). Recent advancements in treatment, particularly with the emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), have provided new opportunities. However, a significant portion of patients with NSCLC, approximately three quarters, do not respond to immunotherapy (4,5). Hence, a deeper exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying NSCLC and the identification of novel therapeutic targets are urgently needed.

Lactate has traditionally been regarded as merely a byproduct of glycolysis and a biomarker of cancer. However, a growing number of studies have demonstrated that lactate is not only a crucial energy source but also plays a significant role in regulating metabolism, immune responses, and intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment (TME) (6). In tumor cells undergoing abnormal glycolysis, excessive glucose uptake and lactate production occur, even when oxygen is sufficient (7). The accumulation of lactic acid and the metabolic environment it creates can drive angiogenesis, promote local invasion, enhance distant metastasis, and influence the anticancer immune response. As a result, lactate produced by cancer cells may serve as a crucial immunosuppressive metabolite within the TME (8). G protein-coupled receptor 132 (Gpr132) serves as a crucial sensor for macrophage lactate in the acidic TME, and its absence was found to impair lung metastasis in a mouse model of breast cancer (9). Programmed cell death protein ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression is elevated in numerous solid tumors, and the expression of PD-L1 is regulated by high lactate levels within the TME (10). In addition, inhibiting lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in combination with interleukin (IL)-21 has been shown to significantly enhance the antitumor responses (10). Moreover, there is a growing body of evidence supporting the predictive and therapeutic value of lactate-related genes (LRGs) in various tumors, including colorectal cancer, osteosarcoma, and ovarian cancer (11-13).

In this study, unsupervised clustering of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) training cohort identified two distinct subtypes linked to lactate metabolism. Based on this classification, a prognostic model incorporating LRGs was constructed via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression, with the model’s diagnostic potential further validated in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Subsequently, the underlying mechanisms related to the signature’s efficacy were examined, which included immune infiltration analysis, predictive analysis of immunotherapy responses, and single-cell analysis. Additionally, real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) validation confirmed that all prognostic genes were differentially expressed between tumor and normal alveolar epithelial cells (AECs). These findings offer new insights for the treatment of NSCLC. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-984/rc).

Methods

Data collection

The gene expression profiles and clinical data for the NSCLC cases analyzed in this study were obtained from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and GEO (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The TCGA dataset of LUAD (n=479) and LUSC (n=470) was used as the training set, and the GSE50081 (n=173) and GSE157011 (n=484) datasets were used for validation. Additionally, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) (GSE117570) included 58 samples from 44 patients with NSCLC. A total of 206 LRGs were also collected from previous studies (8,14-19) (Table S1). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Cluster analysis

The pattern of LRG placement across chromatin was analyzed via the “RCircos” package in R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to assess its ability to differentiate tumor samples from normal tissues. Consensus clustering of LRG expression was then performed via the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package, with Pearson correlation serving as the distance metric and partitioning around medoids (PAM) used for clustering. A total of 1,000 replicates were run to ensure classification stability. Differential expression analysis between the identified clusters was performed via the “DESeq2” R package, with genes selected based on |log2 fold change (FC)| ≥1 and an adjusted P value <0.05. Finally, we combined the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with LRGs to generate a gene set for further analysis.

Generation of lactate scores and performance validation

These genes were subsequently subjected to LASSO regression to reduce dimensionality and mitigate overfitting. The LASSO analysis was conducted via the “glmnet” R package, with the Cox proportional hazards model (family = “cox”) being applied with a maximum iteration limit of 10,000. Ten-fold cross-validation was employed to identify the optimal penalty parameter λ that minimized the partial likelihood deviance. To ensure reproducibility, the random seed was fixed at 163. Following this, multivariate Cox regression was conducted on the genes selected after LASSO to construct the final prognostic model. The risk score for each patient was calculated with a linear combination of the selected gene expression values weighted by their corresponding regression coefficients, according to the following formula: Lactate score=∑(PC{1i}+PC{2i}).

The midstandard values were applied to establish the threshold for dividing samples into high- and low-risk groups. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves were plotted, and log-rank tests were conducted to examine the survival differences between the two groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to assess the predictive accuracy of the risk model for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival. To improve the model’s prognostic precision and predictive ability, a nomogram was developed, with the risk score, age, gender, T stage, N stage, and tumor mutation burden (TMB) being integrated to calculate the probability of overall survival (OS) at 1, 3, and 5 years. Moreover, we validated the prognostic model in the GSE50081 and GSE157011 datasets.

Evaluation of immune infiltration

The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to quantify the composition of 25 tumor-infiltrating immune cells (20). The proportions of immune infiltrating cells between low- and high-risk groups were compared via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with the expression of prognostic LRGs in patients with NSCLC as the basis for the comparison. The potential correlation between the expression levels of these prognostic LRGs and the abundance of immune infiltrating cells was further investigated through the Pearson correlation coefficient.

Immunotherapy response prediction

To investigate differential responses to immunotherapy, the tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) algorithm was applied to compare the low- and high-risk groups (21). An elevated TIDE score indicates increased immune evasion and a lower likelihood of treatment efficacy. Additionally, the IMvigor210 dataset was used to calculate the lactate score and predict response to immunotherapy.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction and establishment of the competing endogenous RNA network

A PPI network was generated for the prognostic LRGs via the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Protein (STRING) database (https://string-db.org/), with an interaction score threshold of ≥0.4 being applied for filtering. The network was visualized with Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1), and the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) plugin was employed to identify functional modules within the PPI network. The module selection criteria were set as follows: node score cutoff =0.2, k-core =2, maximum depth =100, and degree cutoff =2. To identify microRNAs (miRNAs) that potentially interact with the prognostic LRGs, the miRcode database (http://www.mircode.org/) was used. Long noncoding RNA (lncRNA)-miRNA-mRNA regulatory relationships involving key prognostic LRGs were then used to construct a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network, which was visualized via Cytoscape.

scRNA-seq data analysis

scRNA-seq data were processed with the “Seurat” R package. Cells were retained for downstream analysis if they expressed more than 300 genes, contained fewer than 15% mitochondrial transcripts, and exhibited a proportion of erythrocyte genes below 1%. To identify and exclude doublets, the “scDblFinder” R package was applied. Data normalization was performed with the “NormalizeData” function to correct for variations in sequencing depth and minimize batch effects. PCA was then conducted to identify the major sources of variation. Cell clustering was performed via the “FindClusters” function, which was followed by dimensionality reduction through the RunUMAP method. Finally, subpopulation-specific marker genes were identified via the “FindAllMarkers” function and subsequently annotated and visualized with the CellMarker 2.0 (22).

Intercellular communication was analyzed and visualized with the “CellChat” R package based on scRNA-seq data. In addition, pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted with the “Monocle” R package to examine the dynamic changes among subclusters. By examining their distribution along the developmental path, we inferred the evolutionary progression and potential cellular origins of distinct subpopulations.

Cell culture

Human H460 NSCLC cells (Procell Biotech, Wuhan, China) and PC-9 cells (Procell Biotech) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). AECs (Biyuntian Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were maintained in DMEM/F-12 medium containing 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

RT-qPCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from cell lines via TRIzol (cat. No. 15596026; Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All primers used were provided by GenScript Biotech (Piscataway, NJ, USA). The primer sequences are detailed in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed with R software version 4.3.1. For variables with a normal distribution, unpaired t-tests were used, while the Mann-Whitney test was applied for nonnormally distributed variables. Comparisons between three groups were conducted via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test. Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess associations between variable pairs. KM survival analysis was used to compare the OS between the low- and high-risk groups. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Consistency cluster analysis of NSCLC

As shown in Figure 1A, consensus clustering of LRG expression with k=2 divided patients with NSCLC into two subtypes: cluster 1 (C1) and cluster 2 (C2). A significant prognostic difference was observed between the two subtypes (P<0.001; Figure 1B). The heatmap of gene expression patterns and the PCA results indicated well-defined clustering patterns (Figure 1C,1D), suggesting that LRGs may influence the development of NSCLC. A total of 16,005 DEGs were identified between the two subtypes (Figure 1E). Additionally, the intersection of these DEGs with LRGs yielded 56 overlapping genes (Figure 1F).

Prognostic model construction and evaluation

To construct a prognostic model based on LRGs, we first performed LASSO regression analysis of the 56 LRGs that were closely associated with NSCLC prognosis (Figure 2A,2B). Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to eliminate nonsignificant factors. This process identified eight key genes for the prognostic model, with ALDOA, ENO1, PGK2, PSMD2, and SLC16A3 classified as risk genes, and CITED2, IL19, and NOS2 classified as protective genes. Based on the regression coefficients of these prognostic genes, we calculated the individual risk score for each patient using the following formula: (0.028) × ALDOA + (–2.946×10−7) × CITED2 + (8.875×10−6) × ENO1 + (–0.017) × IL19 + (–1.370×10−4) × NOS2 + (0.033) × PGK2 + (1.703×10−4) × PSMD2 + (0.013) × SLC16A3. Patients were stratified based on the median risk score, with higher scores associated with poorer OS (Figure 2C,2D). KM analysis further confirmed a significantly poorer OS in the high-risk group, while ROC curves demonstrated robust predictive accuracy (Figure 2E). The area under the curve (AUC) for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival was 0.693, 0.707, and 0.704, respectively (Figure 2F). A nomogram integrating the risk signature with clinicopathologic variables accurately predicted 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Figure 2G,2H).

Prognostic model validation in the external datasets

To further validate the model’s accuracy, we used the GSE50081 and GSE157011 datasets from GEO. As shown in Figure S1, the risk curves showed that high-risk patients in the validation cohort had a higher mortality risk (Figure S1A,S1B,S1E,S1F). Survival analysis confirmed that patients in the high-risk group had significantly worse OS than did those in the low-risk group (Figure S1C,S1G). ROC curves demonstrated that LRGs effectively differentiated high- and low-risk patients with NSCLC in both cohorts (Figure S1D,S1H). The AUC for GSE50081 at 1, 2, and 3 years was 0.739, 0.701, and 0.672, respectively, while that for GSE157011 was 0.733, 0.702, and 0.669, respectively. These results further confirm the strong power of the prognostic model to predict NSCLC survival.

Immune cell infiltration

The distribution of 25 immune cell types was analyzed and presented in a bar chart (Figure S2A). The correlations between the eight prognostic genes and various immune cell infiltrations are shown in Figure S2B. With the exception of the protective gene CITED2 and the risk gene SLC16A3, whose expression positively correlated with the infiltration of the majority of identified immune cell types, the expression of other prognostic genes was predominantly negatively correlated with immune cell infiltration. However, the correlations of type 1 T helper (Th1) cells and Th2 cells opposed those of other immune cells, as they exhibited a negative correlation with CITED2 and a positive correlation with most of the other risk genes. Additionally, the results indicated that the infiltration of M1 macrophages, monocytes, and natural killer (NK) T cells was significantly higher in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group (Figure S2C). Similarly, CD4+ memory T (Tem) cells exhibited higher infiltration levels in the high-risk group, while CD8+ naive T cells showed higher levels of infiltration in the low-risk group.

Prediction of immunotherapy response

The TIDE score indicated that the low-risk group exhibited higher immune evasion (P<0.001) (Figure 3A), while immune cell activity was significantly lower in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group (P<0.01), suggesting immune dysregulation (Figure 3B). Additionally, using the IMvigor210 dataset, we evaluated the efficacy of our prognostic model in predicting immunotherapy outcomes. We observed a significant improvement in OS in the low-risk group (P<0.001) (Figure 3C). We found that patients responding to PD-1 therapy had significantly lower risk scores (P=0.03) (Figure 3D), with the objective response rate [complete response (CR)/partial response (PR)] being lower in the high-risk group than in the low-risk group (21.5% vs. 24.2%, respectively) (Figure 3E). Furthermore, we assessed the sensitivity of different risk groups to various ICIs and observed that the low-risk group exhibited greater sensitivity to therapies targeting PD-L1, PD-1, and HAVCR2 (Figure 3F-3H). These findings collectively suggest that the prognostic model may serve as a useful tool for identifying biomarkers predictive of sensitivity to immune therapy.

PPI and ceRNA network construction

We constructed a PPI network to characterize the relationships among the eight LRGs (Figure S3A) and further identified submodules using the MCODE plugin in Cytoscape (Figure S3B). Additionally, we constructed a ceRNA network for the hub genes (SLC16A3, ALDOA, and PSMD2) and identified complex interaction patterns between these hub genes and various miRNAs (Figure S3C).

Single-cell analysis of NSCLC

Single-cell immune analysis of NSCLC was conducted with the GSE131907 dataset. Cells were clustered and annotated, and 25 distinct clusters were identified, which included eight cell types, including macrophages, epithelial cells, T cells, B cells, and endothelial cells, among others (Figure 4A,4B). As shown in Figure 4C-4G, the expression levels of ALDOA and ENO1 were similar across all eight cell types but were particularly elevated in macrophages, epithelial cells, and myeloid cells. On the other hand, CITED2 showed generally low expression across cell types, with relatively higher levels in T cells and epithelial cells. PSMD2 and SLC16A3, however, were almost undetectable in all eight cell types.

Tumor cell communication

The PPI network revealed that ALDOA and ENO1 were critical genes in the prognostic model. Single-cell analysis further identified that these two genes were highly expressed in epithelial cells and macrophages, suggesting a cell type-specific expression pattern. Therefore, we next examined the intercellular communication between tumor and normal tissues for these two cell types. First, we observed that the total number of intercellular communications was higher in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues, although the communication strength was weak (Figure S4A). Additionally, we found that specific pathways involving SPP1 were significantly enriched in tumor tissues (Figure S4B). The cell communication network illustrated the frequency and strength of interactions between different cell types (Figure S4C), with epithelial cells and macrophages showing significantly stronger communication with other cell types compared to others. Moreover, using a bubble chart, we observed the ligand-receptor interactions mediating communication between various cell types (Figure S4D), with the probability of CCL3 and CCR signaling between macrophages, T cells, and NK cells being notably increased.

Macrophage differentiation

Pseudotime analysis was performed on macrophages during their differentiation process, with a dendrogram of differentiation trajectories being generated (Figure S5A). The results revealed that the differentiation of macrophages occurred earlier in tumor tissues than in normal tissues. Additionally, with the exception of GPR183, we observed a downregulation of differential genes in macrophages along the pseudotime trajectory, including ACP5, C1QB, FABP4, FTL, GPNMB, MARCO, PDLIM1, CIQA, CTSD, FBP1, GCHFR, and MCEMP1 (Figure S5B). As shown in Figure S5C, Gene Ontology (GO) pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant changes in various biological processes (BPs), such as the transition of mitotic cell cycle phases, receptor-mediated endocytosis, and the genes involved in mitotic cell cycle phase transitions. In terms of cellular components (CCs), we found significant upregulation of pathways related to the regulation of collagen-containing extracellular matrix, secretory granule lumen, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, and collagen trimer. Regarding molecular functions (MFs), pathways associated with amide binding, carboxylic acid binding, organic acid binding, and sulfur compound binding were also upregulated.

Epithelial cell differentiation

The dendrogram of epithelial cell differentiation trajectories revealed that the differentiation timing of epithelial cells in tumor tissues did not significantly differ from that in normal tissues (Figure S6A). With the progression of pseudotime, all differential genes in epithelial cells were downregulated, except for those in the SFTP family (Figure S6B). GO pathway enrichment analysis showed that the upregulated pathways in the BP category were significantly more prominent than those in the CC and MF categories. Notably, genes related to microtubule-based movement, cilium organization, ciliary movement, cilium assembly, axoneme assembly, and microtubule bundle formation were significantly enriched. In the CC category, pathways associated with axoneme, cilium plasma, plasma membrane-bounded cell projection cytoplasm, and cytoplasmic region were significantly upregulated. In contrast, most pathways in the MF category showed minimal upregulation (Figure S6C).

Transcription-level verification of prognostic genes

The reliability of the prognostic model was further verified by examining the transcriptional levels of eight prognostic genes across different cell groups. As shown in Figure 5, the expression of ALDOA, ENO1, PGK2, PSMD2, and SLC16A3 was significantly lower in AECs compared with H460 and PC-9 cells, whereas CITED2 and NOS2 were expressed at higher levels in AECs relative to the tumor cell lines, which was consistent with the model prediction (Figure 5A-5G). However, the validation of IL-19 expression showed a trend opposite to the predicted direction. This discrepancy may reflect the influence of biological sequencing variability, sample size limitations, batch effects, and experimental conditions. Therefore, further experimental validation is necessary to confirm the role of IL-19 and to account for these potential influencing factors.

Discussion

Recent studies have shown that lactate acts not only as a key energy source for tumor cells but also plays an active role in driving tumor progression by enhancing proliferation, metastasis, resistance to therapy, and immune suppression (23). These effects are associated with mechanisms such as acidification of the tumor immune microenvironment and the elevated expression of proteins linked to drug resistance (24). Through integrative analysis of large-scale transcriptomic data from TCGA, we identified two distinct lactate-related clusters in NSCLC and further characterized lactate metabolism by screening DEGs associated with lactate. Based on these findings, we constructed an eight-gene prognostic signature and evaluated its predictive performance in both training and validation sets. Notably, the prognostic lactate score showed significant associations with the TME and the differential responses to immunotherapy between low- and high-risk groups. We also extended our investigation to the single-cell level to further elucidate the cellular heterogeneity related to lactate metabolism. Moreover, RT-qPCR validation strengthened the reliability of our findings. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on LUAD with bulk-level analyses, our research included both patients with LUAD and those with LUSC and further incorporated single-cell level analysis, thereby offering a more comprehensive understanding of lactate-related heterogeneity in NSCLC. Moreover, our model demonstrated better clinical applicability. These findings provide valuable insights into the role of LRGs in the pathogenesis, prognosis, and potential therapeutic strategies related to NSCLC.

This study identified eight LRGs associated with both prognosis and the TME in NSCLC, including five risk genes (ALDOA, ENO1, PGK2, PSMD2, and SLC16A3) and three protective genes (CITED2, IL19, and NOS2). ALDOA is involved in multiple cellular functions and BPs, including muscle maintenance, regulation of cell morphology and motility, and ATP biosynthesis (25). A recent study reported that ALDOA enhances glycolytic activity and promotes histone lactylation, thereby facilitating the malignant potential of non-cancer stem cell populations in lung cancer (26). ENO1 is a multifunctional protein whose diverse intracellular and extracellular activities are determined by its subcellular localization (27). Previous studies have consistently reported elevated ENO1 expression in NSCLC tissues, underscoring its diagnostic relevance and its strong correlation with advanced clinical stage and poor prognosis (28-30). Moreover, ENO1 has been identified as a promising therapeutic target in NSCLC (31). PGK2 is involved in the regulation of DNA replication and repair processes within mammalian nuclei (32). PGK2 mutations have been found to be significantly enriched in the LUAD subtype associated with unfavorable prognosis, suggesting a potential role in predicting clinical outcomes (33). PSMD2, a key regulator of ubiquitinated protein degradation, is involved in modulating cell proliferation and apoptosis (34,35). It has been implicated in the development and progression of multiple cancers, including NSCLC, pancreatic cancer, and renal cell carcinoma (36-38). SLC16A3 primarily facilitates the export of intracellular lactate into the extracellular environment (39). It has also emerged as a potential biomarker for both diagnostic and prognostic applications in pan-cancer (40). CITED2 functions as a molecular regulator in LUAD by acting as a switch that modulates cell cycle progression. It relieves p21CIP1-mediated inhibition and consequently induces tumor cell growth arrest (41). IL-19, a member of the IL-10 cytokine family, plays a diversity of roles in modulating immune responses and the pathogenesis of various diseases (42). It initiates the production of proinflammatory cytokines and may represent a potential therapeutic target for modulating DNA damage responses in cancer (43). NOS2 is a key enzyme involved in nitric oxide (NO) synthesis. High levels of NO have been shown to damage DNA, thereby contributing to carcinogenesis, but can also contribute to inhibiting tumor metastasis under certain conditions (44). In patients with early-onset colorectal cancer, NOS2 and its related genes are significantly downregulated (45).

The results of immune infiltration analysis in our study revealed that compared with the low-risk group, the high-risk group exhibited increased infiltration of M1 macrophages, monocytes, natural killer T (NKT) cells, and CD4+ effector Tem cells. Meanwhile, the abundance of CD8+ naive T cells, CD8+ T cells, and memory B cells was significantly lower compared to that in the low-risk group. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) play a crucial role in promoting tumor invasion, angiogenesis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (46). The cytotoxic activity of type I macrophages may facilitate tumor progression, as the proinflammatory cytokines they release can induce mutagenic changes within tumor cells and the surrounding microenvironment (47). Although these proinflammatory factors are typically mobilized to eliminate malignant cells, they may inadvertently foster genomic instability, ultimately supporting tumor adaptability and survival (48). Notably, TAMs are primarily derived from circulating monocytes that are actively recruited to tumor sites (49). CD4+ T cells exhibit either protumor or antitumor functions depending on the TME. A substantial body of research has established the pivotal role of CD4+ T cells in mediating tumor suppression (50,51). However, a recent study identified a distinct subset of CD4+ T cells that preferentially eliminates MHCII+ antitumor myeloid cells, thereby contributing to immune escape mechanisms (52). Additionally, a growing body of evidence has underscored the critical function of CD8+ T cells in mounting an effective immune response against tumors (53,54). Further TIDE analysis in our study confirmed that tumors from high-risk patients were more prone to immune evasion. Moreover, in the prediction of immunotherapy response, the low-risk group exhibited increased sensitivity to PD-L1, PD-1, and HAVCR2. Overall, our findings suggest the presence of an imbalanced TME in NSCLC, indicating potential opportunities for immunotherapy.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed that the risk genes ALDOA and ENO1 were highly expressed in macrophages, epithelial cells, and myeloid cells. In contrast, the protective gene CITED2 exhibited low expression in eight cell types, but relatively higher levels were observed in T cells and epithelial cells. These findings were consistent with the results of our correlation analysis between prognostic LRGs and immune cell infiltration. Moreover, macrophages and epithelial cells in tumor tissues demonstrated stronger intercellular communication compared to other cell types. Notably, the probability of CCL3-CCR signaling was significantly elevated between macrophages and T cells and between macrophages and NK cells. Chemokines, a subset of cytokines, are secreted to direct the migration of various cell types through chemotactic signaling. These chemokine ligands (CCLs) play keys roles in a range of physiological processes, including wound repair, immune regulation, and the development and progression of cancer (55,56). CCL3, also known as macrophage inflammatory protein-1α (MIP-1α), facilitates the recruitment of inflammatory cytokines to specific sites. It is primarily produced by mononuclear macrophages, lymphocytes, and neutrophils, as well as tumor cells (57). CCL3 has been recognized as a prognostic marker in various malignancies (58), and CCL3-mediated fibroblast chemotaxis has been implicated in the development of colitis-associated colorectal cancer (59). However, it has been suggested that tumor-derived CCL3 not only enhances the infiltration of immune cells but may also promote tumor cell death by inducing factors such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) (60). Our findings highlight the cell type-specific roles in influencing the TME. This single-cell perspective represents a novel extension beyond bulk transcriptome-based prognostic models.

Despite the application of multiple approaches, this study involved certain limitations that warrant consideration. First, the construction and evaluation of our prognostic model rely on data from public databases, which present potential heterogeneity. Additionally, the lack of detailed clinical information restricted the ability of our study to reflect individual patient-level differences. Second, although the model demonstrated robust prognostic performance, the biological mechanisms underlying the relationship of prognostic LRGs with NSCLC progression and response to immunotherapy remain insufficiently understood. Finally, although initial cellular validation was conducted, further mechanistic investigations, including both in vitro and in vivo experiments, are needed to better define the functional roles of these prognostic LRGs in the development of NSCLC.

Conclusions

We constructed a prognostic model for NSCLC based on eight LRGs, which allowed for the effective stratification of patients into distinct risk groups with significantly different survival outcomes. In addition, the lactate risk score was closely associated with immune cell infiltration patterns and predicted responses to immunotherapy. These findings offer new insights into lactate metabolism and may help inform more individualized treatment approaches for patients with NSCLC.
