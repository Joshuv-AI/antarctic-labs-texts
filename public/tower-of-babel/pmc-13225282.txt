The adaptive plasticity of temperate phage λ
Authors: Sylvain Gandon, Tapan Goel, Joshua S Weitz, Sébastien Lion
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhen the temperate phage λ infects its host, the bacterium Escherichia coli, it can either replicate to form new progeny (lytic growth) or integrate its genome into the host chromosome (lysogenization). Crucially, the probability of lysis or lysogeny is tightly regulated and varies with phage and host genotypes and the environment. In particular, the number of viruses coinfecting the same host cell is known to have a strong effect on the outcome of the infection, leading to phenotypic plasticity: the probability of lysogenization is higher when the cellular multiplicity of infection (MOI) increases. However, the selective forces driving the evolution of plasticity in phage λ remain unclear. Here, we analyze the evolution of the plasticity of lysogenization and show that a MOI-varying strategy is adaptive when the abundance of susceptible cells fluctuates periodically. We study how the speed of these fluctuations and various within-host decision rules affect the evolution of viral plasticity. Our results suggest that the complex genetic regulation of lysogeny in temperate phages can be shaped by natural selection allowing viruses to use the MOI as indirect information to persist in environments with fluctuating host densities.

Introduction

Bacteriophages (‘phage’), viruses that exclusively infect bacteria, are among the most abundant and diverse entities in the biosphere (Bergh et al. 1989; Edwards and Rohwer 2005; Suttle 2007; Breitbart 2012; Wigington et al. 2016; Gregory et al. 2019). Phages can adopt different host exploitation strategies. While infections by virulent phages are typically short-lived and end with bacterial lysis and the release of new virus particles, the life cycle of temperate phages includes two alternative paths: lysis or lysogeny. Lysis allows the temperate phage to rapidly produce new virions and, like virulent phages, to transmit horizontally to new hosts. In contrast, lysogeny is characterized by the vertical transmission of the phage genome from mother to daughter cells.

The discovery of lysogeny a century ago (Bail 1925; Bordet 1925; Lwoff 1953) sparked major advances in molecular biology and in deciphering gene regulation. In particular, the study of the temperate phage λ has shed light on the genetic machinery governing the lysis–lysogeny decision, i.e. the probability of lysogenization after the infection of a bacterium (Lederberg 1951; Gottesman and Weisberg 2004; Ptashne 2004). The genetic switch that allows phage λ to shape the host fate (lysis vs. lysogeny) is regulated, in part, by the expression of a consortium of lysis- and lysogeny-associated genes—a more comprehensive treatment is available elsewhere (Ptashne 2004; Golding 2011, 2018). Briefly, the late expression of the gene cI leads to the production and dimerization of the CI repressor protein that suppresses the expression of other phage genes while activating its own transcription. This down-regulation of lysis-associated genes, including the cro gene, allows lysogens to divide and transmit the prophage vertically. In contrast, the gene cro is a (potentially weak) antagonist of lysogeny-associated genes (primarily cII which then activates other genes including cI) (Oppenheim et al. 2005; Svenningsen et al. 2005). The expression of cro and the subsequent up-regulation of lysis-associated phage genes leads to the initiation of a productive infection and the lysis of infected cells.

Systems biology models of the within-cell dynamics of the accumulation of lysogeny- and lysis-associated gene products have explored the role of spontaneous biochemical noise in shaping alternative cell fates (Arkin et al. 1998). Models have also shown how deterministic feedback drives reproducible outcomes, such that cell fate is not driven exclusively by within-host stochasticity (Weitz et al. 2008; Joh and Weitz 2011; Bednarz et al. 2014; Gandon 2016; Golding 2018). Deterministic factors—including phage genotype, host genotype, host physiology, and abiotic environmental factors—can impact the probability of lysogenization upon infection and the induction rate of lysogens. In particular, the probability of lysogenization of phage λ has been shown to increase with the multiplicity of infection (MOI) (Fry 1959; Kourilsky 1973; Zeng et al. 2010; Golding 2016, 2018; Geng et al. 2024). Single-cell studies have revealed the importance of host, virus-associated, and environmental factors in regulating the fate of an infected cell (Shao et al. 2019; Yao et al. 2021; Nguyen et al. 2024). Both population-scale and single-cell studies tend to show that the lysis–lysogeny decision is a tightly regulated phenotypic trait that varies ‘plastically’ with different environmental factors (Fig. 1). We use the term phenotypic plasticity here to refer to the ability of a genotype to produce different phenotypes in response to distinct environmental conditions (Sommer 2020). Here, we study how a specific phenotype of phage λ —the lysis–lysogeny decision—varies with a specific environmental condition—the number of phages coinfecting a single cell, the MOI. The role of natural selection in shaping the plasticity of the phage λ genetic switch remains unclear. In other words, is the observed plasticity of the lysogeny of phage λ adaptive? To answer this question, it is necessary to take into account between-cell interactions at the population level, and how environmental factors affect the dynamics of both the virus and its host.

Theoretical efforts to examine the evolutionary basis for the regulation of the lysis–lysogeny decision switch are rooted in foundational efforts to address an apparent dilemma: why be temperate? In other words, what are the evolutionary advantages of lysogeny when the lytic pathway generates far more virions (and associated viral genome copies) in approximately the same, if not a shorter, period of time than does prophage integration and the establishment of a lysogen (Stewart and Levin 1984)? Despite the apparent disadvantage of lysogeny, early population dynamic models of competing virulent and temperate phages revealed that being temperate was evolutionarily beneficial in fluctuating environments, outcompeting virulent phages during periods of low host abundance (Stewart and Levin 1984). More recent work has revealed the basis for these findings. First, the reproductive output of horizontal vs. vertical strategies measured in terms of completed viral life cycles differs as a function of host availability (Weitz et al. 2019). In the limit of low susceptible cell densities, lysogeny produces more infected cells than does lysis, because the vast majority of extracellular virions will decay/degrade before encountering a host (Li et al. 2020; Correa et al. 2021). Second, the optimal degree of lysogeny can itself be shaped by the ecological context. An intermediate, evolutionarily stable probability of lysogeny emerges in fluctuating environments in which lysis is favoured during resource-replete periods and lysogeny enables persistence during resource-scarce periods, e.g. as demonstrated in models both with (Wahl et al. 2018; Goel et al. 2025) and without (Maslov and Sneppen 2015) explicit ecological feedback. More generally, eco-evolutionary models have found evidence that the degree of lysis vs. lysogeny could be a function of the environment, including the presence of lysis-associated signalling molecules (Doekes et al. 2021a; Shivam et al. 2022) and that viruses whose decisions are context-dependent can outcompete viruses with fixed strategies (Cheong et al. 2022). Altogether, these models imply that viral plasticity is de facto adaptive without establishing how within and between-host selection is coupled.

Here, we model the dynamics of the temperate phage λ and study how selective pressures acting on the lysis–lysogeny decision vary with a particular feature: the MOI. First, we focus on a scenario in which the lysis–lysogen decision evolves freely at each MOI. We show that periodic fluctuations of host density drive the evolution of plastic strategies. This analysis confirms and quantifies the intuitive argument that the MOI provides indirect information about the availability of susceptible bacteria in the environment. Second, we extend this analysis to take into account potential constraints and fitness costs associated with plasticity. We use this general model to predict how temperate viruses should evolve in a broad range of scenarios and identify why phenoptyic plasticity is adapative. We address how within and between-host selection are coupled by exploring the impact of alternative heuristics governing the lysis–lysogeny decision in multiply infected hosts, including a baseline average case and a voting case, consistent with observed cellular outcomes (Golding 2011, 2018). Finally, we discuss the broader implications of this work for the evolution of plasticity in other pathogens.

We examine the evolution of phage phenotypes by modelling environmental fluctuations as a periodic influx of susceptible cells into the population, θ (t) (see Appendix and Fig. 2). This periodic perturbation of the population of Escherichia coli cells is analogous to the periodic influx of nutrients into the gastrointestinal tracts of animals and humans. We utilize square wave periodic perturbations to facilitate analysis, and we focus on oscillations with periods between 1.3 and 25 h. Under a constant influx of susceptible cells (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\theta (t)=\theta _{0}$\end{document}), the epidemiological dynamics converge to a single fixed equilibrium point, where the MOI is geometrically distributed (Lion 2013). But, when the influx of susceptible cells varies periodically, the system is attracted to a stable limit cycle (Fig. 3). Evolution of lysogenization without plasticity

Constant environment

In a constant environment, the temporal covariance is zero, and the selection gradient is constant and equal to Δ v in all MOI classes. Selection tends to reduce the difference between the effective reproductive values associated with lysis or lysogeny and, ultimately, the ES lysogenization strategies satisfy Δ v=0 so that both reproduction routes are equally valuable for the phage (i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\hat{v}_{L}=B\,\hat{v}_{V}$\end{document}). We show in Supplementary material S5 that, if we start from a non-plastic state (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $z_{i}^{k}\equiv z_{i}$\end{document}) that satisfies this condition, viral plasticity does not evolve.

Periodic environment

In contrast, in a periodically fluctuating environment, a positive (resp. negative) covariance at MOI k indicates that the virus should increase (resp. decrease) lysogeny when it is in a k-infected host. This will readily select for MOI-specific strategies, where the optimal viral strategies are different in each MOI class. Hence, the temporal covariance between the frequency of the MOI class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $f_{E^{k}}(t)$\end{document} in the viral population and the relative value of lysogeny Δ v(t) can be viewed as indirect information that may be used by the virus to alter its lysogeny strategy. A positive (negative) covariance at MOI k indicates that the virus should increase (decrease) lysogeny when it is in a k-infected host. When the fluctuations are very fast or very slow, the separation of ecological and evolutionary timescales leads to the evolution of lower plasticity. When the fluctuations are very fast (the period T is small), the system tends to behave as if the influx of susceptible hosts is constant and equal to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\left \langle{\theta (t)}\right \rangle $\end{document}. In contrast, when the fluctuations are very slow (the period T is large), the system switches between two states. First, when θ (t)>0, the system reaches an endemic equilibrium where the virus can transmit vertically but also horizontally via the infections of new susceptible cells so that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\hat{f}_{E^{k}}>0$\end{document}. Second, when θ (t)=0, the system reaches an endemic equilibrium where the virus is only transmitted vertically. In this phase of the life cycle, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\hat{f}_{E^{k}}=0$\end{document} (no new infections are taking place). In this situation, selection without plasticity and without pleiotropy yields the same gradient of selection because selection on lysogeny only takes place when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\hat{f}_{E^{k}}>0$\end{document}. In other words, selection for plasticity can only emerge for intermediate rates of variation of the environment. Interestingly, when plasticity does evolve, it yields extreme bang–bang strategies where the virus switches from a fully lysogenic to a fully lytic strategy (Fig. 3B). This extreme strategy is far from the plasticity observed in phage λ (see Fig. 1). The evolution of this extreme strategy in our model may result from the unrealistic assumption that lysogeny can evolve without any constraints. The within-cell regulation of the lysis–lysogeny decision, however, is expected to impose some pleiotropy among the traits expressed at different MOIs.

Discussion

The ability of the phage λ to modulate its lysis–lysogeny decision as a function of the MOI is an archetypal form of phenotypic plasticity (Ptashne 2004). Here, we presented a theoretical framework to explore the adaptive nature of viral plasticity with a focus on the selection gradient. The derivation of the selection gradient allows us to predict the plasticity of a temperate phage under a range of plausible ecological scenarios (Fig. 4), which are qualitatively consistent with the observed norm of reaction of phage λ (Fig. 1, Supplementary material S5). In addition, our model allows us to dissect the evolutionary forces acting on the evolution of viral plasticity. The increase of lysogeny with MOI allows the virus to optimize its transmission strategy in a environment where the density of the susceptible bacteria fluctuate. Our theoretical analysis highlights the importance of the covariance between the frequency of the different classes of coinfected cells and the difference between the effective reproductive value of lysogeny and lysis. In other words, this covariance measures the ‘quality’ of the information associated with the MOI and the fluctuation of the time-varying selection gradient for lysogeny (Bruce et al. 2021; Lion and Gandon 2022). Since this covariance is maximized for intermediate periods of the fluctuations we expect the plasticity of lysogenization to be more pronounced in environments with intermediate degree of variability (Fig. 4).

The evolutionary outcomes of the model depend on the choice of heuristics governing the cellular level decisions in hosts infected by different strains. While we do not explicitly model the underlying lysis–lysogeny decision circuit, the anlayses in the main text leverage a baseline heuristic correspond to phenotypic averaging of cellular level fate (Golding 2011, 2018). In the analysis, we assume that the overall probability of lysogeny is averaged over all the contributions of the coinfecting virus strains, irrespective of the time at which the different strains infect the host. In contrast, experimental studies suggest that the decision rule may be governed by the ‘unanimity voting rule’ in which each of the coinfecting viruses must initiate lysogeny to avoid lysis (Zeng et al. 2010). This alternative decision heuristic is supported by the observation of an increase in the ‘lyso-lysis’ phenomenon in multiply infected hosts where some phage integrates its DNA into the host followed by cell lysis (Shao et al. 2017). Our theoretical framework can be readily modified to account for this alternative way the collective decision making is reached by the virus sharing the same coinfected host. As shown here, the unanimity voting rule yields a similar selection gradient (e.g. compare Supplementary material S4.1 and Equation (4)) and all the above qualitative predictions regarding the influence of periodic fluctuations of the environment hold (compare Supplementary Fig. S4 and Fig. 4).

The present study comes with some caveats. We simplify our model by assuming that coinfections occur through sequential infection events and that precise simultaneous coinfections are impossible. While phages can block future infection through receptor blocking and/or intracellular antiphage repression, superinfection exclusion takes time to develop. For example, it can take ∼ 4–8 min for superinfection immunity to develop in a E. coli-phage T4 system (Vallée and Cornett 1972). Secondary infections occur every 6 min (on average) in our model with most secondary infections occurring much faster. Thus, we can reasonably assume that coinfections can occur through sequential infections before superinfection exclusion mechanisms affect the MOI. If superinfection exclusion were to be faster or secondary infections slower, the probability of observing higher MOIs would be lower, weakening selection. Because late-arriving strains may contribute less to the lysogeny decision (Cortes et al. 2017), an alternative would be to explicitly model the dominance of earlier-arriving strains—a subject of future work. However, previous studies have shown that infected cells can have reduced adsorption rates (Nguyen et al. 2024) and increased latent periods (Bode 1967). These observations suggest directions to generalize the present findings of plasticity to additional phenotypes beyond the lysogenization probability to other viral life-history traits.

Viruses may use different mechanisms to respond plastically to variations in the environment. For example, other temperate phages produce diffusible signals that provide information on the environmental state (Erez et al. 2017). The phages of the SPbeta group produce a small peptide (arbitrium) that influence the outcome of the initial lysis–lysogeny decision when susceptible cells are infected. In this case, the plasticity of lysogeny is not governed by the MOI of the host, but by fluctuations in arbitrium density (Bruce et al. 2021; Doekes et al. 2021b; Shivam et al. 2022; Bruce et al. 2026). One important difference between the present work and previous models developed for arbitrium is the necessity to account for the contribution of multiple viruses in the lysis–lysogeny decision when MOI ¿ 1. Yet, MOI and arbitrium can both be viewed as distinct ‘social cues’ used by different viruses as indirect information on the availability of uninfected hosts. This homoplasy among different viruses further supports the hypothesis that phenotypic plasticity in cell-fate determination is adaptive. The model also provides quantitative predictions that may be testable experimentally. Lysogenization is well characterized genetically and this trait could evolve to adapt to new environmental conditions (Berngruber et al. 2013, 2015). Besides, viral plasticity has also been shown to be an evolving trait (Leggett et al. 2013) and differs between strains (Kourilsky 1973). It would thus be particularly interesting to explore whether and how phages evolve their reaction norms as an adaptation to a temporally fluctuating environments (Fig. 4) using experimental evolution.

Many other ecological factors may affect the evolution of plasticity in natural populations. For instance, the ability of some phages to infect multiple host species may affect the evolution of MOI-dependent plasticity because the MOI in one cell may not reflect the availability of alternative hosts in the environment. This may explain why the probability of lysogeny in phage P1, known to infect a broad range range of bacteria, does not vary with the MOI (Zhang et al. 2021). Many other biological factors like polylysogeny, spatial structure, and environmental and demographic stochasticity remain to be investigated. The theoretical approach we developed provides a general framework to explore the influence of all these scenarios as well as to consider the evolution of other phenotypic traits. The single-cell monitoring of bacteria exposed to phage λ is revealing the precise regulation of viral life-history traits and the effect of cell size (Zeng et al. 2010), the time since infection (Luque and Silveira 2020; Yao et al. 2021; Geng et al. 2024), and the physiological state of the host cells (Nguyen et al. 2024). These new dimensions of the environment could be added to our model to explore the adaptive nature of these other forms of viral plasticity (Correa et al. 2021). Beyond temperate phages, this theoretical approach provides a new way to investigate the evolution of plasticity in a broad range of pathogens (Kochin et al. 2010; Mideo and Reece 2012; DeLong et al. 2022).
