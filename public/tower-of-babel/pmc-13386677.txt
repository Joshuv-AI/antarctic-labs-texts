Microbiota-derived butyrate inhibits colonic epithelial pyroptosis and mitigates DSS-induced colitis via interacting with aryl hydrocarbon receptor
Authors: Fei Zou, Zengrong Wu, Su Wang, Mengmeng Xu, Pianpian Xia, Yaxian Hu, Tang Fang, Peijun Hu, Chongyang Huang, Feihong Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntestinal barrier defects cause antigen translocation and immune dysregulation. The pyroptosis of colonic epithelial cells (CECs) disrupts the colonic barrier, and its inhibition might be a therapeutic approach for ulcerative colitis (UC), but the mechanisms are not fully understood.MethodsA DSS-induced UC model was established to assess the level of colonic epithelial cell pyroptosis. 16S rDNA sequencing and LC‒MS/MS were applied to screen potential candidate bacterial species and metabolites. The roles of target metabolites were evaluated in vivo using GSDMD-knockout mice. FHC cells treated with LPS + ATP were used as a cellular model of pyroptosis, and the underlying molecular mechanism was explored mainly by siRNA transfection and lentivirus infection.ResultsWe found that DSS-treated mice exhibited increased levels of pyroptosis in the colon. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) significantly suppressed mucosal inflammation and CEC pyroptosis, accompanied by increased levels of butyrate-producing bacteria and butyrate in feces. Butyrate treatment alleviated DSS-induced colitis in mice. Moreover, GSDMD knockout mitigated DSS-induced colitis in mice, whereas a butyrate intervention failed to further ameliorate colitis in GSDMD-knockout mice. Mechanistically, we found that butyrate significantly inhibited LPS + ATP-induced pyroptosis by activating its receptor, aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), in FHC cells, while silencing AhR suppressed this effect. The overexpression of cGAS in FHC increased the level of pyroptosis, whereas the administration of butyrate inhibited the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway. Treatment with a cGAS inhibitor significantly reversed the increase in pyroptosis caused by AhR knockdown in pyroptotic FHC cells.ConclusionsGut microbiota-derived butyrate levels were increased after FMT. Butyrate suppressed the proinflammatory cGAS–STING–NF-κB signaling axis via AhR to inhibit CEC pyroptosis and thereby alleviate UC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08264-1.Keywords: Ulcerative colitis, Butyrate, Aryl hydrocarbon receptor, cGAS–STING signaling pathway, Pyroptosis

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC), a subtype of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), is a chronic, nonspecific inflammation of the colorectum characterized by continuous diffuse lesions extending proximally from the rectum [1]. Lower gastrointestinal hemorrhage and colorectal cancer are serious complications of UC. The pathogenesis of UC remains unclear. Generally, UC occurs in genetically susceptible individuals following exposure to environmental factors. Defects in the mucosal barrier, immune dysregulation, and an imbalance of the gut microbiota have been shown to be closely associated with the onset of UC [1]. Suppressing gut inflammation to achieve mucosal healing under endoscopy is an essential goal of UC treatment.

As the most crucial component of the intestinal mucosal barrier, the intestinal epithelial barrier plays an important role in defending against the invasion of pathogens into the mucosa to maintain the physiological balance of the intestinal lumen and mucosa [2]. Defects in the intestinal epithelial barrier allow antigens from the lumen to invade the intestinal mucosa and activate innate immune cells, which then secrete numerous inflammatory cytokines and activate adaptive immune responses, thereby exacerbating local gut inflammation to initiate UC [3]. Therefore, investigating the mechanisms underlying intestinal epithelial barrier defects is crucial for controlling gut inflammation in patients with UC. Pyroptosis is a programmed, inflammatory cell death pathway. In its classical activation pathway, intracellular inflammasomes (e.g., the NLRP3 inflammasome) activate caspase-1, which then cleaves GSDMD. The resulting N-terminal fragment of GSDMD oligomerizes on the plasma membrane, forms pores and leads to cell rupture, which causes the abundant release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 [4]. Recent studies have shown that inhibiting CEC pyroptosis may be an effective approach for treating UC. Apple polyphenol extract can alleviate DSS-induced colitis by inhibiting CEC pyroptosis [5]. Nigeglanine was also shown to suppress CEC pyroptosis and reduce the expression of proinflammatory factors in the colon [6]. However, the exact mechanisms regulating CEC pyroptosis in UC have not been investigated in depth.

The intestinal epithelial microenvironment comprises the gut microbiota, mucus, and intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), whose interactions are intricately and complexly intertwined [7]. The gut microbiota and its metabolites constitute pivotal components that regulate IEC function. A balanced gut microbiome sustains intestinal homeostasis, whereas dysbiosis, characterized by increased levels of deleterious bacteria and decreased levels of beneficial commensals, triggers epithelial barrier disruption, leading to intestinal pathologies such as IBD [8]. Under physiological conditions, the gut microbiota rarely directly interacts with mucosal epithelial cells and primarily functions through secreted metabolites that traverse the mucus layer to modulate epithelial cell biology [9]. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), key metabolites in the gut lumen produced by the bacterial fermentation of dietary fibers, play essential roles in maintaining intestinal mucosal barrier integrity and regulating inflammation [10]. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) ameliorates intestinal inflammation by enriching beneficial bacteria and modulating gut immunity, representing a significant approach for treating UC, although its mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated [11].

In this study, we found that the gut microbiota metabolite butyrate inhibited colonic epithelial pyroptosis by activating its receptor, aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) [12], and then suppressed the downstream cGAS–STING signaling pathway to decrease the production of proinflammatory cytokines in UC. These findings elucidate the crosstalk between the gut microbiota and IECs in the microenvironment of UC. Treatments that specifically target the butyrate–AhR–cGAS–STING axis may be effective against UC.

Methods

Reagents and antibodies

The following antibodies were purchased: GSDMD-N (ab215203, Abcam; A22523, ABclonal), NLRP3 (15101S, Cell Signaling Technology), caspase-1 (A0964, ABclonal), IL-1β (12703S, Cell Signaling Technology), IL-18 (54943S, Cell Signaling Technology), AhR (A1451, ABclonal), cGAS (55482, Signalway Antibody), STING (19851-1-AP, Proteintech), phospho-STING (AF7416, Affinity), NF-κB p65 (8242S, Cell Signaling Technology), NF-κB phospho-p65 (GB113882, Servicebio), GAPDH (GB15004, Servicebio), β-actin (GB15003, Servicebio), α-tubulin (11224-1-AP, Proteintech), HSP90 (GB11284-1, Servicebio), and histone H3 (9715S, Cell Signaling Technology). An HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (GB23303, Servicebio) was used. Dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) (Cat# 160110, MW 36,000–50,000) was purchased from MP Biomedicals (USA). Sodium butyrate (NaB, Cat# S1539) was obtained from Beyotime (China). Ampicillin (Cat# A433388), metronidazole (Cat# M109874), neomycin (Cat# N109017), and vancomycin (Cat# V105495) were purchased from Aladdin (China). The AhR inhibitor CH-223191 (Cat# HY-12684) was purchased from MedChemExpress (USA).

Cell culture

The FHC cell line was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Cat# CRL-1831, ATCC, USA). FHC cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Cat# 11965092, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cat# S660JY, BasalMedia, China) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. FHC cells were treated with 1000 ng/ml LPS (Cat# L2880, Sigma, Germany) for 4 h, after which 5 mM ATP (Cat# A6419, Sigma, Germany) was added to the cells and incubated for an additional 2 h to induce pyroptosis in vitro. The cells were pretreated with 6 mM NaB for 2 h prior to LPS stimulation to evaluate the effect of NaB on pyroptosis. The GPR41 agonist AR420626 (Cat# T26647), the GPR43 agonist 4-CMTB (Cat# T8642), the GPR109A agonist niacin (Cat# T0879), the HDAC inhibitor TSA (Cat# T6270), the AhR agonist FICZ (Cat# TQ0257), and the cGAS inhibitor G150 (Cat# T11344) were purchased from TargetMol (USA). FHC cells were incubated with 25 µM AR420626, 100 µM 4-CMTB, 1 mM niacin, 50 nM TSA, 400 nM FICZ, or 5 µM G150 for 2 h before pyroptosis was induced with LPS + ATP.

siRNA transfection and lentivirus infection

Small interfering RNAs targeting AhR were purchased from GenePharma (China). The sense sequence (5’-3’) was GGACAAACUUUCAGUUCUUTT. The antisense sequence (5’-3’) was AAGAACUGAAAGUUUGUCCTT. Lipofectamine 3000 (Cat# L3000015, Invitrogen, USA) was used to transfect the siRNAs into targeted cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lentiviruses overexpressing the human AHR gene (NM_001621.5) and human CGAS gene (NM_138441.3) were constructed using the GV492 vector with the sequence Ubi-MCS-3FLAG-CBh-gcGFP-IRES-puromycin. Puromycin selection (2 µg/mL for 48 h) was applied to establish stable transfectants.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction

Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins extracted from cells and separated using a Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Cat# P0027, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions to determine the subcellular localization of AhR. Briefly, cytoplasmic protein extraction reagent was added to the cells, which were subsequently vortexed and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the cytoplasmic protein fraction. Then, nuclear protein extraction reagent was added to the pellet, which was subsequently vortexed and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the nuclear protein fraction.

Western blot analysis

After protein extraction, the samples were separated on SDS‒PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by an incubation with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The proteins were visualized using ECL substrate (Cat# BL520B, Biosharp, China).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues using TRIzol (Cat# G3013, Servicebio, China). Reverse-transcription PCR was performed with a Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Cat# R223, Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR was subsequently conducted using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Cat# Q711, Vazyme, China) and a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad, USA). The sequences of the primers are listed in Table S1.

Cell viability measurement

Cell viability was measured using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Cat# G4103, Servicebio, China) and a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay kit (Cat# G1610, Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the CCK-8 assay, 1000 cells were plated in each well of 96-well plates. After pyroptosis was induced, 10 µL of CCK-8 working buffer was added to each well, followed by a 1 h incubation at 37 °C. Cell viability was determined by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm. For the LDH release assay, the culture medium was collected and transferred to a 96-well plate at a volume of 80 µL/well. Then, 80 µL of working buffer was added to each well, followed by a 30 min incubation in the dark at 37 °C. Cytotoxicity was calculated by measuring the absorbance at 490 nm.

Quantification of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release

FHC cells were incubated in serum-free medium with or without 2% DSS for 24 h. The cell samples were then divided into two equal aliquots. One aliquot was used to extract total cellular DNA using a Cell DNA Extraction Kit (Cat# DP304, TIANGEN, China). The other aliquot was incubated with DNA extraction buffer [13] (containing 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES, and 25 µg/mL digitonin) for 10 min, followed by 3 centrifugation steps at 980 × g for 5 min each. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 17,000 × g for 25 min. Two hundred microliters of the supernatant was removed, and cytosolic DNA was extracted using a Circulating DNA Extraction Kit (Cat# DP339, TIANGEN, China). The amount of cytosolic mtDNA in the supernatant was normalized to the amount of total mtDNA present in the pellet. The sequences of the primers are listed in Table S1.

Animals

Animal experiments were performed with the approval of the Animal Experimentation Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital (20241075). Six- to eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of the Second Xiangya Hospital (Changsha, China). The mice were housed in a mouse cage with a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle and an appropriate temperature and humidity. The mice were randomly allocated to the NC, DSS, and FMT groups (n = 6 mice each) to investigate the efficacy of FMT. Sterile drinking water supplemented with 1.5% DSS was provided for 14 consecutive days. FMT was performed every other day for 6 treatments. The mice were randomly assigned to the NC, DSS, and NaB groups (n = 6 mice each) to verify the antipyroptotic effects of butyrate, and 2% DSS was administered for 10 consecutive days. The mice in the NaB group were additionally administered 200 mM NaB in their drinking water. Colitis was induced in GSDMD-knockout (KO) mice that were kindly provided by Dr. Chongyang Huang from The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine, and the mice were treated with or without 200 mM NaB to confirm the role of pyroptosis in the inhibitory effect of NaB on colitis. The role of AhR in the inhibitory effect of butyrate on pyroptosis was investigated by randomly allocating the mice to the NC, DSS, NaB, and NaB + CH-223191 groups (n = 6 mice each). Colitis was induced by treatment with 2% DSS for 9 days. The mice in the NaB and NaB + CH-223191 groups received concurrent treatment with 200 mM NaB starting on the first day of DSS administration. For mice in the NaB + CH-223191 group, 10 mg/kg CH-223191 was administered via intraperitoneal injection every other day beginning one day prior to the DSS intervention. The body weight and disease activity index (DAI) [14] of the mice were recorded every day after DSS administration to monitor the level of colitis. The mice were sacrificed by CO2 asphyxiation at the end of the experiments.

Fecal microbiota transplantation

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in mice was performed as previously described [15, 16]. First, commensal bacteria were depleted by the administration of a combination of antibiotics (ABX) consisting of ampicillin (1 g/L), metronidazole (1 g/L), neomycin (1 g/L), and vancomycin (1 g/L) in the drinking water for 1 week before FMT. Starting on the third day of DSS administration, FMT was performed every other day for a total of 6 times (Fig. 1D). Briefly, fresh feces was collected from healthy mice by gently rubbing or scratching the abdomen. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was mixed with feces at a concentration of 1 ml/100 mg. The mixture was homogenized, filtered through 100 μm filters and centrifuged at 1000 × g at 4 °C for 3 min. The supernatant was subsequently harvested and promptly administered to recipient mice in the FMT group by gavage (200 µL per mouse). The mice in the NC group and DSS group were gavaged with the same volume of PBS.

16S rDNA sequencing

Stool DNA was extracted using an E.Z.N.A. Stool DNA Kit (Cat# D4015, Omega Bio-tek, USA). After the quality check, the V3–4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with universal primers. The PCR products were subsequently purified using an Agencourt AMPure XP Kit (Cat# A63881, Beckman Coulter, USA). The sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (Cat# E7645, New England Biolabs, USA). The library quality was assessed, and deep sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq/Novaseq (Illumina, USA) platform. Sequence data have been deposited in the NCBI Short Read Archive database (Accession Number: PRJNA1304486). The raw data were subjected to data splitting, data splicing, filtering, chimeras removal, OTU clustering and homogenization, and database comparison (Silva 138), and then utilized in the analyses.

Liquid chromatography‒tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS)

Briefly, 50–90 mg of a stool sample was weighed and homogenized in 1 mL of a 50% acetonitrile (ACN) solution.​ After homogenization, the sample was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant.​ The supernatant was then diluted to a concentration equivalent to 10 mg of the original sample per 1.8 mL of 50% ACN and used for derivatization. Crucially, a known amount of a stable isotope-labeled internal standard mixture was added at this step to correct for variations in derivatization efficiency and the instrument response.​ The mixture was vortexed, centrifuged, and incubated at 40 °C for 30 min. After derivatization, the reaction mixture was diluted with 50% ACN containing 0.1% formic acid and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The final supernatant was injected into the LC‒MS/MS system (Nexera UHPLC LC-30A coupled to a Sciex QTRAP 5500). A calibration curve was constructed for each SCFA using a series of standard solutions. The concentration of each SCFA in the samples was determined by interpolating the measured peak area ratio (target SCFA/its corresponding internal standard)​ of the sample onto the calibration curve. The final concentration represents a normalized value derived from the original stool sample through the sample preparation process (including homogenization, dilution, and derivatization) and was used for subsequent statistical comparisons between groups. The quality control samples were analyzed. A coefficient of variation less than 15% was considered acceptable. Details of the SCFAs used as standards are listed in Table S2.

HE staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Fresh colon tissues were dissected, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. Four-micron-thick sections were sliced for HE staining and IHC. The degree of tissue injury and inflammation was assessed using a histological scoring system by two senior pathologists, with the scoring criteria referenced from our previous studies [17]. For IHC, the sections were dewaxed, subjected to antigen retrieval and blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity, after which they were blocked with 3% goat serum at room temperature for 25 min, followed by an incubation with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C and secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, DAB staining and hematoxylin staining were performed. The intensity of immunostaining was assessed using immunoreactive scores (IRSs), which are presented as the product of the positive intensity and the ratio of positive cells.

Isolation of primary colonic epithelial cells

After the mice were sacrificed, the colon tissues were collected and washed with cold PBS. Then, the colon tissues were sliced into small pieces and incubated with 5 mM EDTA for 30 min on a horizontal rotator with shaking at 200 rpm at 37 °C. DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS was added to neutralize the digestion. A 70 μm cell strainer was used to filter the impurities. The cell suspension was then centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain the cell pellet.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

We applied two scRNA-seq datasets in this research. The scRNA-seq data for colonic mucosa from UC patients (n = 18) and healthy controls (n = 12) were obtained from Christopher et al. [18]. The dataset was downloaded from the Single-Cell Portal (https://singlecell.broadinstitute.org/single_cell) under accession number SCP259. Using R (version 4.2.3), the R package “Seurat” (version 5.1.0) was employed to import the raw data and assemble them into a Seurat object. Rigorous quality control, cell clustering, and annotation were performed by the original authors. scRNA-seq data for colon tissues from healthy mice (n = 3) and mice with DSS-induced acute colitis (n = 3) and chronic colitis (n = 4) were derived from GEO datasets (accession number: GSE264408). The raw expression matrix was read using the R package “Seurat” (version 5.1.0). Doublets were removed using the R package “scDblFinder” (version 1.12.0). Cells were filtered according to the number of genes expressed (no more than 6000) and the percentage of mitochondrial genes (no more than 15%). The data were normalized, scaled and subjected to dimension reduction using principal component analysis (PCA) with 15 PCA components and 10 neighbors. Cell types were annotated with the strategy described in the original article [19]. The GO pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes between normal colonic mucosal epithelium and UC-associated inflamed mucosal epithelium was conducted using the R package “clusterProfiler” (version 4.6.2). DEGs were defined using a threshold of absolute log2 fold-change (log2FC) > 0.1 and an adjusted P value < 0.05 to capture as many potentially altered genes as possible.

Statistics

The data are presented as the means ± SEMs. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t test, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, one-way ANOVA, or the Kruskal‒Wallis test, with test selection guided by a normality test and evaluation of the homogeneity of variance, as implemented in GraphPad Prism (version 8.3.0). Multiple comparisons among group means were performed using Tukey’s test. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05.

Results

FMT ameliorated DSS-induced colitis and alleviated pyroptosis in colonic epithelial cells

We profiled the expression of pyroptosis-associated genes in colonic mucosal epithelial cells from UC patients and healthy controls by performing an scRNA-seq analysis in the Single-Cell Portal (accession number: SCP259). First, all CECs were stratified into 15 subclusters based on the expression of marker genes for distinct cell types (Fig. 1A). Compared with the normal epithelium or noninflamed epithelium from UC patients, the inflamed epithelium displayed a reduction in the proportion of enterocyte progenitors and increases in the numbers of transitional cells and M-cells (Fig. 1B). Notably, the expression levels of pyroptosis-associated genes (GSDMD, NLRP3, CASP1, IL1B, and IL18) were significantly increased in the inflamed epithelium of UC patients compared with the noninflamed epithelium of both UC patients and healthy controls (Fig. 1C). Additionally, the proportion of cells expressing these pyroptosis-associated genes was markedly higher in the inflamed epithelium of UC patients than in that of controls (Fig. 1C). Our data indicated that active UC significantly increased colonic epithelial cell pyroptosis in the colon.

We administered FMT to mice with DSS-induced colitis to explore the role of FMT in altering epithelial cell pyroptosis during UC (Fig. 1D). We found that compared with PBS-treated colitis mice, FMT-treated colitis mice exhibited significantly lower weight loss (P = 0.015) (Figure S1A), decreased colitis severity (P = 0.024) (Figure S1B), and longer colon lengths (P = 0.025) (Figure S1C). Moreover, the colonic tissues of mice in the DSS-treated group exhibited extensive destruction of epithelial and villous structures and prominent infiltration of inflammatory cells. In contrast, the colonic tissues of mice in the FMT group had more intact villous structures and less inflammatory cell infiltration in the mucosal layer (Fig. 1E). Notably, we found that the colons of DSS-treated mice exhibited increased levels of pyroptotic cells, as indicated by the clear upregulation of GSDMD-N, NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β and IL-18 expression. FMT treatment downregulated the expression of these proteins in DSS-treated mice (Fig. 1E–G). Moreover, we examined the expression of GSDMD-N in isolated primary CECs in different groups and observed that FMT treatment significantly reduced its level by approximately 46.3% compared with that in the DSS group, which is consistent with the suppression observed in whole colon tissue (Fig. 1H). Therefore, our results indicated that epithelial cell pyroptosis was significantly increased in the colon of DSS-treated mice, whereas FMT markedly suppressed epithelial cell pyroptosis and alleviated DSS-induced colitis.

FMT increased the levels of butyrate-producing bacteria and SCFAs in DSS-treated mice

We subsequently performed 16S rDNA sequencing on mouse fecal samples to examine the effects of FMT on the gut microbiota and explore the mechanisms by which FMT ameliorated CEC pyroptosis. The alpha diversity, which was calculated using four different methods (Shannon, PD whole tree, observed species, and Chao1 indices), demonstrated that FMT significantly increased the richness and evenness of the gut microbiota (Fig. 2A). An analysis of beta diversity using PLS-DA and PERMANOVA revealed distinct differences in the gut microbiota composition between the DSS group and the FMT group (Fig. 2B). After annotation, we found that at the phylum level, the abundance of Proteobacteria was lower in the FMT group than in the DSS group, indicating an amelioration of gut microbiota dysbiosis [20] (Fig. 2C). At the genus level, the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria, including Odoribacter, Alistipes, and Roseburia, was higher in the FMT group than in the DSS group (Fig. 2D). A subsequent LEfSe suggested that three additional butyrate-producing bacteria, Rikenellaceae, Alloprevotella, and Dorea, were also more abundant in the feces of mice in the FMT group than in those in the DSS group (Fig. 2E). Unfortunately, functional prediction based on Tax4Fun2 [21] did not reveal differences in butanoate metabolism within the gut microbiota between the DSS and FMT groups (Fig. 2F). However, the lack of the necessary sensitivity of functional predictions derived from 16S rDNA sequencing to delineate health-related functional changes in the microbiome [22] weakens the reliability of the results described above.

SCFAs, which are produced by the gut microbial fermentation of dietary fiber, play pivotal regulatory roles in the intestinal mucosal epithelial barrier and mucosal immunity. We then quantified the levels of SCFAs in mouse feces using LC‒MS/MS. As shown in Fig. 2G, FMT increased the fecal levels of acetic acid (~ 1.5-fold compared with DSS), butyric acid (~ 2.5-fold compared with DSS), and isobutyric acid (~ 2.2-fold compared with DSS). In addition, we validated the alterations in intestinal SCFA levels using the fecal metabolomic data published by Arnau et al. from 217 UC patients and 255 non-IBD controls [23]. We found that the fecal levels of eight SCFAs, including butyric acid, were significantly lower in UC patients than in controls (Fig. 2H). Overall, we concluded that FMT increased the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria and the levels of SCFAs, which might contribute to alleviating colonic inflammation after DSS treatment.

Butyrate mitigated colonic inflammation and inhibited epithelial cell pyroptosis in DSS-treated mice

Butyrate, a principal component of SCFAs, functions as a primary energy substrate for IECs, enhances innate immune defenses, and modulates enteric nervous system activity [10, 24]. We administered NaB to DSS-treated mice and tested the level of CEC pyroptosis in the colon to further explore the role of butyrate in colitis (Fig. 3A). We found that mice with DSS-induced colitis exhibited a distinct decrease in body weight starting on Day 4, whereas the NaB group displayed a less pronounced weight reduction (P = 0.020) (Figure S2A). Moreover, compared with DSS-treated mice, NaB-treated mice had a significantly lower DAI (P = 0.008) and longer colon length (P = 0.001) (Figure S2B–C). The histopathological analysis of colonic tissues revealed that the DSS group exhibited widespread crypt structure distortion with extensive inflammatory cell infiltration. In contrast, the NaB group displayed relatively preserved crypt structures and diminished inflammatory cell infiltration, indicating that NaB treatment attenuated tissue damage (Fig. 3B). These results showed that the administration of butyrate could attenuate DSS-induced colitis in mice.

We subsequently investigated the role of NaB in CEC pyroptosis and found that compared with mice with DSS-induced colitis, NaB-treated colitis mice presented significantly lower expression of GSDMD-N in colon tissues (~ 0.5-fold compared with the DSS group) and isolated primary CECs (~ 0.7-fold compared with the DSS group) (Fig. 3C–D). Moreover, the expression of NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 in the colon was reduced by NaB treatment (Fig. 3C). IHC staining also revealed a marked reduction in the number of GSDMD-N-positive cells in the colons of mice with DSS-induced colitis after NaB treatment (Fig. 3B). Therefore, these data suggested that the gut microbiota metabolite butyrate potentially exerted a protective effect on DSS-induced colitis and could inhibit CEC pyroptosis.

Pyroptosis inhibition was the predominant mechanism by which butyrate alleviated colonic inflammation

FHC cells stimulated with LPS + ATP were used as a pyroptotic cell model to validate the effect of butyrate on epithelial cell pyroptosis in vitro. First, we verified the successful induction of cell pyroptosis through Western blot and qPCR analyses, which revealed increased expression of GSDMD-N and NLRP3 (Fig. 4A–B). Importantly, pretreatment with NaB clearly decreased the level of pyroptosis in FHC compared with PBS-treated pyroptotic cells (Fig. 4A–B). In addition, the results of both the CCK-8 and LDH release assays confirmed that FHC cell viability increased after NaB pretreatment (Figure S3A–B). Therefore, these results indicated that butyrate could suppress FHC cell pyroptosis.

Butyrate affects gut homeostasis and alleviates colitis, but the specific underlying mechanisms remain unclear [24, 25]. We used GSDMD-KO mice to explore the effect of pyroptosis on butyrate-treated mice with DSS-induced colitis (Fig. 4C). Immunoblotting first confirmed the successful knockout of GSDMD in the KO mice (Fig. 4D). After DSS treatment, GSDMD-KO mice exhibited significantly less body weight loss (P = 0.047) (Fig. 4E), milder alterations in the stool consistency, and a lower DAI (P = 0.027) than wild-type (WT) mice (Fig. 4F). Additionally, the colon length of the GSDMD-KO mice was significantly longer than that of the WT mice (P = 0.042) (Figure S3C). These results demonstrated that GSDMD knockout protected against DSS-induced colitis in mice. More importantly, considering the therapeutic role of butyrate in DSS-induced colitis in WT mice, we also administered NaB to GSDMD-KO mice with DSS-induced colitis. However, NaB treatment failed to further attenuate colonic inflammation in GSDMD-KO mice, as manifested by similar weight loss (Fig. 4E), disease severity (Fig. 4F), colon length (Figure S3C) and histological alterations (Fig. 4G), indicating that NaB inhibited colonic inflammation predominantly by suppressing the GSDMD-mediated cell pyroptosis after DSS treatment.

AhR mediated the inhibitory effect of butyrate on CEC pyroptosis

We then explored the molecular mechanisms by which butyrate inhibited CEC pyroptosis. In general, butyrate exerts its biological functions through three distinct mechanisms: by acting on G-protein-coupled receptors (GPR41, GPR43 and GPR109A), by serving as a histone deacetylase inhibitor, and by activating AhR [25]. We next investigated the exact mechanism by which butyrate inhibited pyroptosis in FHC cells. In our study, the administration of the GPR41 agonist AR420626, the GPR43 agonist 4-CMTB, the GPR109A agonist niacin, and the HDAC inhibitor trichostatin A (TSA) failed to suppress pyroptosis in FHC cells treated with LPS + ATP. However, FICZ, an AhR agonist, clearly inhibited the expression of GSDMD-N (~ 0.6-fold compared with the Pyro + DMSO group) (Fig. 5A), indicating that butyrate inhibited pyroptosis in FHC cells possibly through the activation of AhR.

AhR is a ligand-activated transcription factor. When binding to its ligands, AhR translocates into the nucleus and regulates the expression downstream target genes, playing important roles in immune activity and stem cell regulation [26]. AhR overexpression could alleviate gut inflammation in animals with DSS-induced colitis [27]. We first conducted a single-cell RNA sequencing analysis using data published by Dawon et al. [19] and found that the number of CECs decreased in the mouse colon after DSS treatment (Fig. 5B). AhR expression was significantly inhibited in CECs from mice with acute or chronic colitis (Fig. 5C). Consistent with the results of the bioinformatics analyses, we found that AhR expression was downregulated by DSS induction and was increased in the colon tissues and primary CECs of mice after FMT (Fig. 5D–G, Figure S4A–B) or butyrate treatment (Fig. 5H–L, Figure S4C–E). A subsequent Western blot analysis of nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extracts showed that the NaB pretreatment upregulated the expression of AhR (~ 1.6-fold compared with the Pyro group), especially the expression of nuclear AhR, in LPS + ATP-treated FHC cells (Fig. 5M). Moreover, the expression of the AhR-related transcriptionally targeted genes AHRR and CYP1A1 increased after the NaB pretreatment, suggesting that the transcription of AhR was activated after NaB treatment (Fig. 5N). These results indicated that butyrate could upregulate AhR expression and activate AhR-mediated transcription during CEC pyroptosis.

Furthermore, we investigated whether activated AhR participated in the inhibition of CEC pyroptosis caused by butyrate by treating mice with DSS-induced colitis with the AhR inhibitor CH-223191 and NaB. We found that the CH-223191 intervention counteracted the protective effects of NaB on DSS-induced colitis, as evidenced by the reduced body weight (Figure S4F), increased disease activity (Figure S4G), and shortened colon length (Figure S4H). CH-223191 also reversed the inhibitory effect of NaB on CEC pyroptosis, as indicated by the increased expression of pyroptosis-related proteins in the colons (GSDMD-N: ~1.9-fold compared with the NaB group) and CECs (GSDMD-N: ~1.6-fold compared with the NaB group) of colitis mice after CH-223191 administration (Fig. 5O–P, Figure S4I–K). These findings were further validated in vitro. We first altered AhR expression in FHC cells by silencing it with an AhR siRNA or overexpressing it with a lentivirus, as validated by the Western blot and qPCR results (Fig. 5Q, Figure S4L). The silencing of AhR reversed the antipyroptotic effect of butyrate (Fig. 5Q) and clearly decreased cell viability (Figure S4M). The overexpression of AhR produced the opposite effects. Therefore, these data indicated that butyrate could bind to and activate cellular AhR to inhibit CEC pyroptosis. The silencing of AhR reversed the butyrate-induced suppression of pyroptosis.

AhR suppressed CEC pyroptosis by regulating the cGAS–STING–NF-κB signaling pathway

The cGAS–STING pathway has been recently identified as a core signal of innate immunity, which is hyperactivated during UC [28]. However, the role of cGAS in UC and the association of the cGAS–STING pathway with pyroptosis are still unclear. Here, we tested whether the cGAS–STING pathway participated in the process through which the butyrate–AhR axis inhibited CEC pyroptosis. First, we used publicly accessible scRNA-seq data [18] to analyze alterations in the cGAS–STING pathway in the context of colitis. The expression levels of cGAS, STING1, TBK1, IRF3, and RELA in the inflamed epithelium of UC patients were clearly higher than those in the noninflamed epithelium (Fig. 6A). Additionally, an enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes between normal colonic epithelium and inflamed epithelium from UC patients revealed significant enrichment in several cGAS-associated GO pathways, including “regulation of the innate immune response” and “positive regulation of type I interferon production” (Fig. 6B). These findings indicated the hyperactivation of the cGAS–STING pathway in the CECs of UC patients.​.

Importantly, the levels of cGAS, STING and p-STING were clearly increased in the colons of the mice in the DSS group compared with those in the negative control group (Fig. 6C, Figure S5A). cGAS was located mainly in CECs and not in immune cells (Fig. 6E). We found that after the DSS intervention, the mtDNA levels were significantly increased in the cytoplasm of FHC cells (Fig. 6F), suggesting that mtDNA release may be a key factor contributing to the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway in CECs in the context of UC. Moreover, by constructing cGAS-overexpressing FHC cells and inducing pyroptosis, we found that cGAS overexpression significantly increased the level of CEC pyroptosis, as indicated by the increased levels of GSDMD-N, NLRP3, caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 (Fig. 6G). Notably, the butyrate intervention significantly downregulated the expression of proteins in the cGAS–STING pathway in both whole colon tissue and isolated primary CECs (Fig. 6C–E, Figure S5A–C). In vitro, butyrate also inhibited the activation of the cGAS–STING pathway in pyroptotic FHC cells, as indicated by the inactivation of NF-κB and decreased type I interferon expression (Fig. 6H–I). Additionally, the silencing of AhR led to the activation of cGAS–STING signaling (Fig. 6J–K). Finally, we explored whether cGAS mediated the inhibitory effects of the butyrate–AhR axis on CEC pyroptosis by conducting a rescue experiment using an AhR siRNA and the cGAS inhibitor G150. G150 reversed the increase in pyroptosis caused by AhR knockdown (Fig. 6L). Thus, the butyrate–AhR axis inhibited pyroptosis in CECs potentially by suppressing cGAS–STING signaling, thereby reducing colonic inflammation and alleviating UC.

Discussions

While mucosal barrier dysfunction is a hallmark of UC [29], the specific triggers and regulatory mechanisms underlying epithelial cell death, particularly pyroptosis, remain incompletely understood. Our study delineates a novel signaling axis that connects a key microbial metabolite to the core pyroptotic machinery within CECs. We demonstrated that the beneficial effects of FMT are associated with the restoration of butyrate-mediated suppression of pyroptosis, a process that is mechanistically governed by AhR and its downstream inhibition of the proinflammatory cGAS–STING pathway (Fig. 7).

A schematic of the mechanism by which the butyrate/AhR/cGAS/STING axis modulates CEC pyroptosis

Pyroptosis is a programmed, inflammatory cell death pathway that is associated with sepsis [30] and autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus [31] and multiple sclerosis [32]. Recent studies have shown that pyroptosis might be a clinical indicator for assessing UC disease activity [33] and has emerged as a novel therapeutic target [34]. SCFAs are critically important metabolites derived from the gut microbiota and serve as functional connections for signaling between the gut microbiota and mucosal epithelial cells. According to previous studies, butyrate is beneficial for inducing and maintaining remission in mouse colitis models and patients with IBD [25]. Preserving colonic barrier function and promoting epithelial restoration are the primary mechanisms [25]. However, whether butyrate modulates pyroptosis in CECs remains unclear. Here, by employing GSDMD-knockout mice, we unequivocally demonstrated that the anticolitic effects of butyrate are primarily mediated by the inhibition of GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis, providing direct evidence for the central role of this cell death pathway in the mechanism of action of butyrate. By establishing an in vitro FHC cell pyroptosis model, we showed for the first time that butyrate could inhibit CEC pyroptosis by activating AhR. Therefore, our findings establish the inhibition of epithelial cell pyroptosis as a novel and central mechanism​ underlying the therapeutic efficacy of butyrate in a colitis model, moving beyond its previously recognized role in barrier maintenance and providing a more precise target for antipyroptotic therapy. It is also worth noting that, NaB and other butyrate derivatives such as sodium phenylbutyrate have also been reported to exert protective effects in colitis models by exerting a reverse regulatory effect on gut microbiota composition [35, 36]. While the core finding of the present study focuses on the unidirectional “microbiota→metabolite→host” axis, it is reasonable to speculate that exogenous supplementation with butyrate and its derivatives could also indirectly reinforce their antipyroptotic effects by ameliorating gut dysbiosis. If such bidirectional interplay does exist, a positive feedback loop may be established between the gut microbiota and its metabolites, which would have implications for optimizing metabolite-based therapeutic strategies. This possibility warrants further investigation in future studies.

The AhR signaling pathway is involved in immune regulation and the pathogenesis of various autoimmune diseases [37]. Genome-wide association studies for IBD have suggested that the AHR gene is a candidate gene associated with IBD susceptibility [38]. AhR expression is reduced in the colonic tissues of mice with DSS-induced colitis [39]. After the induction of colitis by DSS or TNBS, AhR-knockout mice or mice carrying the Ahrd allele (which reduces the ability of AhR to bind to its physiological ligands) exhibited more severe intestinal inflammation than wild-type mice [40, 41]. However, the mechanisms by which AhR suppresses intestinal inflammation remain incompletely defined. Several previous studies have indicated that the mechanisms underlying the inhibitory effect of AhR on UC include regulating T-cell differentiation [42], improving mucosal barrier function [43, 44], and modulating the gut microbiota composition [45]. In this study, we show for the first time that AhR inhibits GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis in CECs, thereby expanding the mechanistic network through which AhR exerts its protective effect on the pathogenesis of UC. Integrating our findings with the results reported by Gao et al., who demonstrated that AhR suppresses intestinal inflammation by inhibiting macrophage pyroptosis [46], and considering the pivotal role of pyroptosis as an innate immune mechanism in initiating subsequent immune processes, we speculate that targeting the antipyroptotic effect of AhR may block the development of immune inflammation at an early stage, thereby preventing secondary immune dysregulation and subsequent damage to the intestinal mucosal barrier. We propose that the butyrate–AhR axis may serve as a critical checkpoint for immune homeostasis in CECs.

cGAS is a receptor for intracellular DNA and is mainly responsible for immune defense. When binding to double-stranded DNA in the cytoplasm, cGAS can induce the production of the second messenger cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP). cGAMP then binds to the endoplasmic reticulum-adapted STING and leads to the phosphorylation of IRF3 and NF-κB to transcriptionally regulate the expression of type I interferon, TNF-α and IL-6 [47]. Aberrant activation of cGAS–STING signaling is closely associated with the pathogenesis of various autoimmune diseases, including IBD [48]. As cGAS is predominantly located at IECs [49], we explored the role of epithelial cGAS in gut inflammation and innovatively found that the expression of epithelial cGAS clearly increased during DSS-induced colitis in mice, which might be associated with mtDNA release. Furthermore, we revealed that cGAS promotes pyroptosis in CECs and identified the cGAS–STING pathway as a critical downstream effector of AhR signaling in CECs. Although previous research by Hong et al. revealed that AhR activated by 2-oxindole in macrophages can inhibit cGAS pathway activity and thereby alleviate colitis [50], our study is the first to establish a connection between these two pathways in CECs within the context of colitis. These findings validate the regulatory role of butyrate—a more abundant metabolite than 2-oxindole in the intestinal lumen—in these pathways and extend the understanding of the effects of the AhR–cGAS axis on the downstream pyroptosis pathway. This AhR–cGAS axis represents a novel mechanism by which butyrate can regulate epithelial innate immunity to prevent excessive inflammatory cell death.

Integrating our findings, the present study delineates a coherent microbiota–metabolite–host signaling axis–namely, the butyrate–AhR–cGAS–STING pathway–that converges on the regulation of CECs pyroptosis in UC. This integrated model provides several conceptual advances. First, it positions the butyrate–AhR interaction as a critical checkpoint that links microbial metabolic cues to epithelial innate immune tone. Second, it identifies epithelial cGAS–STING signaling as a previously underappreciated downstream effector of AhR, thereby bridging two pathways that have largely been studied independently in the context of epithelial dysfunction in IBD. Third, the fact that the therapeutic benefit of butyrate was abrogated in GSDMD‑knockout mice and that AhR inhibition reversed the antipyroptotic effects underscores the pathway’s functional integrity: disruption at any level–ligand availability, receptor activation, or downstream signaling–may compromise the protective outcome. These insights suggest that future therapeutic strategies could target multiple nodes within this axis. For instance, combining butyrate supplementation with AhR agonists or cGAS inhibitors might produce synergistic effects, especially in patients with defective AhR signaling or persistent cGAS–STING activation.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although a bioinformatics analysis was performed on publicly available sequencing data from UC patients, the lack of validation in independent clinical samples may introduce confounding effects from demographic variables such as age, sex, and ethnicity. Second, the absence of a CEC-specific AhR conditional knockout mouse model precludes definitive conclusions regarding the precise role of AhR signaling in regulating gut inflammation. Finally, while previous studies have reported the regulatory role of the cGAS–STING pathway in NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis, this mechanistic link has not been experimentally validated in our current model.

In summary, in our study, excessive GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis in CECs was detected in individuals with UC, and GSDMD-related pyroptosis in the epithelium may be a novel immunological feature of UC. The level of the gut microbiota metabolite butyrate was clearly increased after FMT, which could interact with its receptor AhR in the cytoplasm and then suppress proinflammatory cGAS–STING signaling to inhibit epithelial GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis and alleviate UC. Overall, our research revealed a deep relationship between gut microbiota metabolites and the mucosal immune response in the microenvironment of UC, and targeting GSDMD-related pyroptosis and supplementation with butyrate-producing bacteria or butyrate may serve as potential therapeutic strategies for UC.
