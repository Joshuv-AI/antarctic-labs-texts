Structural Basis for Inhibition of the SARS‐CoV‐2 nsp16 by Substrate‐Based Dual Site Inhibitors
Authors: Gints Kalnins, Laura Rudusa, Anna L Bula, Diana Zelencova‐Gopejenko, Olga Bobileva, Mihails Sisovs, Kaspars Tars, Aigars Jirgensons, Kristaps Jaudzems, Raitis Bobrovs
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoronaviruses, including SARS‐CoV‐2, possess an mRNA 5′ capping apparatus capable of mimicking the natural eukaryotic capping signature. Two SAM‐dependent methylating enzymes play important roles in this process: nsp14 methylates the N7 of the guanosine cap, and nsp16‐nsp10 methylates the 2′‐O‐ of subsequent nucleotides of viral mRNA. The 2′‐O‐methylation performed by nsp16‐nsp10 is crucial for the escape of the viral RNA from innate immunity. Inhibition of this enzymatic activity has been proposed as a way to combat coronaviruses. In this study, we employed X‐ray crystallography to analyze the binding of the SAM analogues to the active site of nsp16‐nsp10. We obtained eleven 3D crystal structures of the nsp16‐nsp10 complexes with SAM‐derived inhibitors, demonstrated different conformations of the methionine substituting part of the molecules, and confirmed that simultaneous dual‐site targeting of both SAM and RNA sites correlates with higher inhibitory potential.Keywords: Medicinal chemistry, Structural biology, SARS-CoV-2, Methyltransferase, nsp16

Introduction

The global pandemic caused by the novel coronavirus, SARS‐CoV‐2, has exposed the vulnerability of modern society to the rapid spread of infectious diseases and the urgent need for effective antiviral therapies. Since its emergence in late 2019, the virus has caused unparalleled disruption to economies, healthcare systems, and daily life across the world. [1] Efforts to combat this devastating disease have focused on vaccines, diagnostics, and therapeutic interventions, including the development of small molecule inhibitors targeting crucial viral proteins. One such target is non‐structural protein 16 (nsp16), a critical enzyme in the virus's replication and immune evasion machinery. [2]

Nsp16 is a methyltransferase that cooperates with nsp10 to cap the 5′ end of viral RNA (see Figure 1A and B), a modification crucial for viral RNA stability and immune evasion. By disguising viral RNA as host RNA, SARS‐CoV‐2 can evade host immune detection, making it a promising target for therapeutic intervention.[ 2 , 3 ] Inhibiting nsp16 activity has the potential to disrupt the virus's lifecycle, hinder its propagation, and bolster the host's immune response against the pathogen.[ 4 , 5 ] This approach not only holds promise for treating SARS‐CoV‐2 infections but also for preventing future outbreaks caused by related coronaviruses.

While several nsp16 inhibitors are not substrate analogues,[ 6 , 7 , 8 ] the vast majority of highly active nsp16 inhibitors are substrate derivatives designed by introducing substituents at the adenine N7 position[ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] or by modifying the methionine part of the substrate (S‐adenosyl methionine; SAM) (see Figure 1C).[ 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ] The modifications in the methionine group aim to mimic the interactions of the substrate, or, in several cases, target both, the SAM methyl group binding site as well as the RNA capping site. The majority of these inhibitors were designed using the available SARS‐CoV and SARS‐CoV‐2 structures in complex with close substrate analogues.[ 15 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 ] This includes nsp16 structures in complex with the substrate (RNA), co‐substrate (SAM), enzymatic reaction product (SAH), and, lately, nsp16 structures in complex with SAM analogues, such as AMP, ADP, ATP, sinefungin, as well as numerous adenosine analogues with a substituent in the N7 position.[ 15 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ] Recently, structural information on covalent nsp16 inhibitors that bind in a cryptic pocket next to the adenine binding site also became available. [8]

Here we report X‐ray crystallographic structural information on the binding mode of adenosine derivatives, designed by replacing the substrate methionine group with an aromatic ring containing bioisosteric groups. The data presented provide information on the methyltransferase dual‐site inhibitor binding mode, and indicate that low nanomolar nsp16‐nsp10 inhibition potency is achieved by the inhibitors that partly bind in the RNA binding grove. The structural information provided holds the potential for structure‐based drug design, to advance the development of novel substrate‐based inhibitor scaffolds.

Results and Discussion

The synthesis of compounds co‐crystallized with nsp16‐nsp10 has been described previously (comp. 1–3, 7–11)[ 7 , 13 , 14 ] or in the methods section (comp. 4–6). The compounds were designed by replacing the methionine of the SAM with an aromatic ring containing functional groups to make the molecule more rigid, and introduce additional aromatic stacking interactions with Y47 of the nsp16 SAM binding site, while maintaining the existing hydrogen bonding interactions with Y47 and G81 backbone. It was envisaged that such compounds would bind in the methionine‐binding subsite. In contrast, compounds bearing additional substituents at the aromatic ring were expected to potentially target the RNA binding site.

The binding mode of the co‐crystallized compounds 1–11 reveals that, depending on the substituent size, the methionine isostere interacts with residues of either the methionine or the RNA binding site (Figure 2). Compounds bearing smaller substituents (comp. 9–11) tend to mimic the SAM binding mode and interact in the methionine binding subsite, whereas the introduction of additional bulky substituents (comp. 2–8) directs the methionine replacing group towards the RNA base binding grove. Such compounds not only block the binding of the SAM, but also clash with the RNA substrate.

The smallest SAM analogue studied within this compound series, 5’‐methylthioadenosine (comp. 1; see Figure 2), does not reach the methionine binding site, nor does it clash with the RNA substrate. Nevertheless, this compound exhibits low micromolar nsp16‐nsp10 inhibition potency (IC50=1.02 μM). In contrast, compounds 2–7 bearing the most extensive methionine replacing groups ([1,1′‐biphenyl]‐2‐carboxylic acid, 4′‐hydroxy‐[1,1′‐biphenyl]‐3‐carboxylic acid, 3′‐hydroxy‐[1,1′‐biphenyl]‐3‐carboxylic acid, 3‐(pyridin‐3‐yl)benzoic acid, 3‐(1H‐imidazol‐1‐yl)benzoic acid and 3‐(3‐hydroxyprop‐1‐yn‐1‐yl)benzoic acid, respectively) bind partly in the RNA binding grove and reach IC50 values of 0.8–9 nM. Nearly identical binding mode is also observed for compound 8, with the single aromatic ring positioned within the RNA binding site (IC50=21 nM). It is worth noting that for compounds 2–8 the electron density of the adenosine part of the molecule is well resolved, while the electron density of the methionine substitutes is much poorer. This suggests that methionine‐replacing groups of these compounds are binding in a flexible manner, showing a minimal preference for specific and well‐defined interactions with the protein molecule. Nevertheless, the aromatic methionine‐replacing groups of compounds 2–8 are positioned in a way that might enable stacking interactions with Y132 (Figure 3), the latter being a part of the RNA binding site. However, due to the flexible nature of the aromatic group positioning in the RNA site, such an interaction seems to be fractional.

Unlike compounds 2–8, which show only one binding mode, the electron density maps of compounds 9 and 10 indicate that the methionine‐replacing group of these compounds binds in two conformations with intermediary states among them being present as well (Figure 2 and Supplementary Figure S1). One conformation partially matches the SAM methionine group orientation, while the other conformer occupies the RNA binding site, similar to compounds 2–8. Although better electron density is observable for SAM methionine pocket conformations, this does not necessarily reflect the real distribution. Since the methionine pocket‐directed conformations have less space for movement and more visible interactions than the RNA pocket‐directed conformations, the electron density would be expected to be better as well. Therefore, it is difficult to conclude which conformation of compounds 9 and 10 are truly predominant. The conformation with the substituent binding in the RNA binding grove could be at least partially stabilized by the π‐π stacking interaction with Y132, similarly as for compounds 2–8 (Figure 3). The SAM‐like binding mode occupied by compounds 9 and 10 differs from each other. Compound 10 binds in a conformation that matches the SAM binding mode closely, and the aromatic methionine bioisostere introduced (benzoic acid) mimics the hydrogen bonding interactions of SAM carboxylic acid with the N43 or G81 (Figure 4). Compound 9, however, fills the methionine binding site only partly and hydrogen bonding with the G81 main chain is not observed for this compound, most likely due to steric clashes introduced by additional chlorine atom. The introduction of an additional chlorine atom in the 2‐position of compound 9 bolsters compound binding in a conformation where the carboxylic acid group is solvent‐exposed. The differences in the binding modes and occupancy ratios for compounds 9 and 10 are also reflected in their inhibitory activities. Compound 10 shows nsp16‐nsp10 inhibition potency comparable to compounds 2–7 (IC50=4 nM), while compound 9 has 6 times lower nsp16‐nsp10 inhibition potency (IC50=25 nM). The high compound 10 potency, despite the preference to bind in a SAM‐like binding mode, and, consequently, lower overall clashing potency with the RNA substrate, might be a result of stabilizing hydrogen bond interaction with G81, which was not observed for compounds 8 and 9.

Interestingly, the binding mode of the structurally similar compounds 8 and 9 is affected by the linker length. Compound 8, bearing a shorter sulphide linker, directs chlorobenzoic acid substituent to the RNA binding site, whereas linker length extension by a CH2 group promotes binding also in the methionine binding site; thus, both binding modes can be identified in the electron density map of compound 9.

Compound 11 mimics the binding mode of the SAM, its electron density is well resolved, and no alternative binding modes outside the methionine binding site are observed (Figure 3). Therefore, compound 11 either does not clash at all or clashes minimally with the RNA substrate. Just like compound 10, the aromatic methionine isostere is involved in hydrogen bonding with the G81 amide group. Moreover, additional hydrogen bonding interaction with the N43 side chain is observed for compound 11. The considerably lower nsp16‐nsp10 inhibition potency for compound 11 (if compared to compounds 2–10; IC50=470 nM), is comparable to other substrate‐based inhibitors that do not bind in the RNA binding site–sinefungin (IC50=860 nM), [13] compound 1 (IC50=1.02 μM), SS148 (IC50=1.2 μM), WZ16 (IC50=3.4 μM), SAH (IC50=2.2 μM; [15] IC50=280 nM determined using our assay) (here we, however, need to keep in mind that slightly different compound activity testing assays have been used in each study). This observation suggests that it is essential for the inhibitor to partly fill the RNA binding pocket to reach low nanomolar inhibition potency. When SAH inhibition potential is compared to compound 1, it is possible to notice roughly 4‐fold improvement in activity, which points out that the additional carboxylic acid moiety interaction with the active site does indeed improve the inhibition potency and binding. However, even partial filling of the RNA pocket results in a 10–20‐fold higher inhibition than using SAH, so this remains a more efficient strategy for increasing the inhibition potency.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have presented eleven high‐resolution structures of SARS‐CoV‐2 methyltransferase nsp16‐nsp10 in a complex with substrate‐based inhibitors. These compounds show high nsp16‐nsp10 inhibition potency, and, encouraged by the fact that structurally similar compounds of comparable activity exhibit low μM activity in antiviral tests, [17] we believe that compounds presented hold potential as antivirals. The structures reveal that depending on the methionine bioisostere size, inhibitors bind either in the SAM binding site or target both–the SAM and RNA binding sites. Our data suggest that such dual‐site inhibitors have considerably higher inhibition potency, reaching the low nanomolar range. This effect is very likely due to nonspecific steric inhibition of RNA binding. This nature of non‐specific interaction, however, results in a relatively high off‐target inhibitory activity on human GNMT, as has been demonstrated in our previous studies,[ 13 , 14 ] and by nearly identical comp. 10 potency against both, viral SARS‐CoV‐2 nsp16 (IC50=4 nM) and human GNMT (IC50=8.6 nM). Thus, more research and optimization of the compounds for more specific interactions are required to ensure higher compound selectivity over human methyltransferases. Nevertheless, the structural information provided is expected to greatly advance the development of novel dual‐site methyltransferase inhibitors.

Experimental Section

Purification and Co‐crystallization of nsp16‐nsp10 Complex

Nsp10 and Nsp16 sequences were cloned into pRSF‐Duet‐1 vector using NcoI and HindIII sites and contained additional N‐terminal His‐6x tags with tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease cleavage sites. Protein expression was performed in E. coli BL21‐DE3 cells, with the seeding stock maintained in LB medium and protein expression performed in 2xTY medium with 30 μg/mL kanamycin, inducing cells with 1 mM IPTG for 16 h at +20 °C. IPTG was added after reaching an OD540 of 0.5.

Cell biomass was pelleted by centrifugation and immediately lysed with ultrasonication in lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 8.5), 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM 2‐mercaptoethanol, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 % (v/v) Triton X‐100, 10 % (v/v) glycerol, and 50 mM imidazole, taking 5–10 mL of lysis buffer per gram of cells. Then, the lysate was centrifuged for 30 min at 11,000×g. Proteins were purified from the lysate with batch IMAC by mixing 50 mL with 3–4 mL Ni2+‐NTA agarose, previously equilibrated in lysis buffer. The mixture was incubated on a rotator for 30 min at +7 °C. The suspension was then poured onto a gravity column and washed with wash buffer containing 50 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM 2‐mercaptoethanol, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 % (v/v) glycerol, and 50 mM imidazole. Afterwards, proteins were eluted with elution buffer containing 50 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM 2‐mercaptoethanol, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 % (v/v) glycerol, and 300 mM imidazole. The concentrations of nsp16 and nsp10 proteins were evaluated with spectrophotometric measurement at OD280, and they were mixed in molar ratios of 1 : 2, respectively. TEV protease was added at a mass ratio of 1 : 20–40. The entire mix was then dialyzed against elution buffer without imidazole overnight, in a volume ratio of 1 : 10 in closed tubes on a rotator at +7 °C. Afterwards, the protein mix was centrifuged for 10 min at 7,000×g to separate precipitates, passed through 3–4 mL of Ni2+‐NTA agarose on a gravity column, and loaded on a 26/600 Superdex 75 column equilibrated in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 5 % (v/v) glycerol, and 1 mM TCEP. The peak fractions containing the nsp16‐nsp10 complex were pooled and concentrated with ultrafiltration to 1.3 OD280, around 1 mg/mL. The mix was either frozen at −70 °C and used later for activity assay tests or immediately used further for crystallization experiments.

For crystallization trials, the compounds of interest were added in a dry form to the nsp16‐nsp10 solution at a 2 mM concentration. The mix was then incubated overnight at +7 °C, concentrated with ultrafiltration to a 5x smaller volume, filtrated, and used for a sitting drop 1+1 μL crystallization trials in MRC plates. The trial screen consisted of 48 conditions of 0.1 M MES (pH 5.6), 3–12 % PEG 3350, and 40–180 mM MgCl2. The crystals were fully formed within 1–4 weeks. The crystals were soaked in a mother liquid containing 30 % glycerol and frozen in liquid nitrogen. The data were collected at BESSYII 14.1 and DIAMOND i03 synchrotron beamlines. The data collection and processing statistics are given in Supporting Information Table S1.

Enzymatic Activity Testing Assay

The enzymatic activity testing and RNA substrate preparation were performed as described previously.[ 7 , 13 ] In brief, nsp16‐nsp10 complex activity was determined with an EPIgeneous Methyltransferase Assay kit by assaying the conversion of SAM to SAH according to the manufacturer's instructions. The enzymatic reaction was performed in white ProxiPlate‐384 Plus microplates using a final reaction volume of 10 μL. The reaction buffer was 40 mM Tris‐HCl at pH 8.0, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 2 mM MgCl2 and 0.01 % Tween20. 3.3 μL of purified recombinant protein nsp16‐nsp10 at 20 μM concentration was added to the assay wells, containing previously dispensed inhibitors. The inhibitor solutions were prepared by dissolving compounds in DMSO to 50 mM concentrations, and then subsequently diluting with reaction buffer to reach final concentrations of 100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 and 0.0001 μM (0.1 % final DMSO concentration). The reaction was started by adding 0.9 μL of a mix of ~5 μM m7GpppA‐RNA and SAM at 10 μM final concentrations to the reaction, which was incubated at 30 °C for 30 min. Testing was repeated in duplicate.

For the detection of released SAH, 0.8 μL of EPIgeneous detection buffer one was added to stop the enzymatic reaction. After 10 min of incubation at room temperature, detection reagents were added: first, 2 μL of a 1/16 dilution of SAH‐d2 conjugate and then 2 μL of Anti‐SAH‐Lumi4‐Tb at a 1/100 dilution. HTRF signals were measured using a CLARIOstar Plus microplate reader using an excitation filter at 337 nm and fluorescence wavelength measurements at 620 and 665 nm, an integration delay of 150 ms, and an integration time of 400 ms. Results were analyzed by calculating a two‐wavelength signal ratio: [intensity (665 nm)/intensity (620 nm)]*104 (HTRF Ratio). The mean HTRF Ratio for each sample was calculated as: Mean HTRF Ratio=Mean Sample HTRF Ratio–Blank HTRF Ratio, where ‘blank’ is the signal with compound (or DMSO in control sample) and Anti‐SAH‐Lumi4‐Tb. The inhibition fraction was calculated using the following formula for each inhibitor dilution: % Inhibition=100‐(max signal comp‐min signal comp)*100/(max signal control‐min signal control), where ‘max signal’ is the signal ratio without protein (negative control) and ‘min signal’ the signal ratio in sample. The IC50 values were calculated using the program Graph Pad Prism 10.0.2.
