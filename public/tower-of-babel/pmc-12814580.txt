Fecal sulfatide as a non-invasive biomarker for predicting coronary heart disease
Authors: Rui Hu, Yihan Li, Kefan Xue, Xiao Hu, Ran Guo, Hongxia Guo, Gang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe study aimed to investigate a non-invasive biomarker for predicting coronary heart disease by analyzing fecal sulfatide levels.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on 593 patients with coronary heart disease, divided into acute myocardial infarction (AMI), unstable angina pectoris (UAP), and stable angina pectoris groups (SAP), and a control group of 200 healthy adults. General information was collected for analysis, and fecal sulfatide levels were compared among groups. Binary logistic regression analysis assessed the correlation between fecal sulfatide levels and coronary heart disease. Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate the risk factors, and predictive value was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsStatistically significant differences were observed in the characteristics of age, complete blood cell count, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol between the group of patients with coronary heart disease and the control group (P < 0.05). Noteworthy, fecal sulfatide levels were notably higher in coronary heart disease groups. Furthermore, fecal sulfatide levels in AMI group significantly exceeded those in SAP and UAP groups (P < 0.05). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified fecal sulfatide as an independent risk factor for coronary heart disease, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.893 (95% CI 0.869, 0.921), indicating strong predictive value.ConclusionsFecal sulfatide may serve as a novel, non-invasive biomarker for early coronary heart disease prediction and risk stratification. However, the retrospective and single-center design of this study limits the generalizability of the results, and future large-scale, multi-center prospective studies are needed to validate these findings.

Introduction

Coronary atherosclerotic heart disease (abbreviated as CHD) is caused by coronary atherosclerosis, which leads to myocardial ischemia and hypoxia. It is highly prevalent among middle-aged and elderly individuals [1–3], with high incidence and mortality rates, and its rising prevalence in younger populations further threatens public health. Dyslipidemia, a key risk factor for CHD, contributes significantly to disease development [4–6]. Sulfatide—a major component of mammalian serum lipoprotein sphingolipids—is widely distributed across the nervous system, kidneys, liver, intestines, and serum [7]. Studies have confirmed a strong correlation between serum sulfatide levels and CHD progression [8, 9]: elevated serum sulfatide may increase fecal excretion [10], while reduced serum sulfatide correlates with lower fecal output. This relationship has shifted research focus to fecal sulfatide as a non-invasive biomarker, which avoids venipuncture risks, enables easy sample collection, and offers better stability than conventional serum biomarkers. It thus holds promise for early CHD screening and large-scale population prevention. This study analyzes the association between fecal sulfatide levels and CHD subtypes, for the first time proposing fecal sulfatide as a novel non-invasive biomarker for CHD prediction and risk stratification. We also evaluate its predictive efficacy to provide a theoretical basis for clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Participants

A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted involving 593 patients who underwent coronary angiography at Hebei General Hospital between September 2023 and May 2024, stratified into three clinical subtypes: stable angina pectoris (SAP group, n = 200), unstable angina pectoris (UAP group, n = 198), acute myocardial infarction (AMI group, n = 195). The control group consisted of 200 healthy individuals who underwent routine physical examinations at the aforementioned hospital during the same period. In total, there were 793 patients, including 544 males and 249 females, with an average age of 62 years. To be included in the study, participants had to meet several criteria: be aged 18 years or older, fulfill the diagnostic criteria outlined in «the 2023 ESC Guidelines for the Management of Acute Coronary Syndromes» [11] or «the 2023 AHA/ACC/ACCP/ASPC/NLA/PCNA Guidelines: Management of Chronic Coronary Artery Disease» [12], and have a confirmed diagnosis of coronary heart disease supported by clinical electrocardiograms, coronary arteriograms, and other imaging tests. Moreover, the participants were required to be mentally conscious, capable of cooperating with treatment, have complete medical records, and provide signed informed consent before enrollment. Exclusion criteria were established to ensure the study's credibility, excluding individuals with blood, immune, or neurological diseases; acute infections; severe dysfunction of important organs, such as the liver or kidneys; pregnant or lactating women; and those with specific cardiac conditions, including rheumatic heart disease, valvular heart disease, severe bradycardia, myocarditis, or pericarditis; individuals who had used medications affecting lipid metabolism (e.g., statins, fibrates, and cholesterol absorption inhibitors) within 3 months prior to enrollment; in addition, individuals who had recently undergone surgical operations were also excluded from the study. Every participant provided signed informed consent, and the study procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hebei General Hospital (approval number: 20220231). The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 as revised in 2024.

This study is a retrospective cross-sectional study. According to the sample size calculation formula for cross-sectional studies: n = [Z2* P * (1-P)]/d2. Where Z is the percentile corresponding to the standard normal distribution, typically 1.96 (corresponding to α = 0.05), P is the expected prevalence, which is 1.68% based on the study «Global burden of ischaemic heart disease from 2022 to 2050: projections of incidence, prevalence, deaths, and disability-adjusted life years» [13]. d is the allowable error, taken as 0.02. By substituting these parameters into the formula, the minimum required sample size for this study is calculated to be 159. To avoid errors from a small sample size, the final sample size included in this study was 593.

Sample collection and assays

General data collection

The clinical data of patients meeting the inclusion criteria were collected by reviewing their medical records, which included age, gender, smoking history (defined as ≥ 1 cigarette/day for at least 1 year), and alcohol consumption history (defined as an average of ≥ 100 ml/day of white wine with alcohol content ≥ 50% in the last year lasting for at least 1 year).

Laboratory examination

After a 10-h fasting period, 10 mL of venous blood was drawn from all participants' elbows. The blood samples were analyzed using a Beckman Coulter automatic biochemical analyzer at the Department of Laboratory of Hebei General Hospital. The laboratory tests comprised a complete blood count (CBC), blood lipids (LDL-C, HDL-C, etc.), and other biochemical parameters.

Determination of fecal sulfatide

Approximately 1 g of fresh feces was collected from each patient within 48 h of admission. The fecal samples were washed three times with Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS), ultrasonically disrupted, and then centrifuged at 11,180 rpm at 4℃ for 10 min to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was immediately aliquoted and stored in a − 80℃ ultra-low temperature refrigerator, with a maximum storage time of 6 months. Before detection, samples were thawed at room temperature to ensure uniform and complete thawing. Freeze–thaw stability was tested by subjecting samples to 3 freeze–thaw cycles (− 80℃ to room temperature), with no significant changes in fecal sulfatide levels. A sulfatide ELISA kit (Jiangsu Meimian Industrialy Co., Ltd., catalog number: MM-60644H2) was used in this study. According to the manufacturer's instructions, the antibody in this kit has high specificity for sulfatide. All samples were tested in duplicate to ensure the reliability of results. To assess the precision of the testing method, we conducted a methodological validation. Samples with high, medium, and low concentrations were selected for both intra-batch and inter-batch repeatability testing. The intra-batch coefficients of variation (CV) were 3.80%, 5.49%, and 3.51%, while the inter-batch CVs were 9.35%, 6.64%, and 6.32%, all of which meet the biomarker testing requirement (CV < 15%).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS 27.0 and a P value < 0.05 indicates statistical significance, with the normality of the data verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test before the primary statistical analyses. Continuous variables following an approximate normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two independent samples t test was employed for group comparisons, Levene's method was used for assessing Homogeneity of Variance among multiple groups, and the LSD-t method was used for pairwise comparisons. Non-normally distributed variables were reported as median (interquartile range). Comparisons between two independent samples were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. For comparisons across multiple independent samples, the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed, with pairwise comparisons also analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Count data were expressed as rates or component ratios, and group comparisons were carried out using the chi-square test. Initially, suspected influencing factors of coronary heart disease were analyzed through univariate logistic regression. Collinearity diagnosis was performed on the variables with statistical significance. Finally, variables without collinearity were included for multivariate Logistic regression analysis. The correlation between fecal sulfatide and coronary heart disease was explored by further analyzing relevant variables after adjusting for confounders, with odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated for each correlation factor. The predictive value of fecal sulfatide for coronary heart disease was assessed using the ROC curve, with calculation of the AUC and 95% CI.

Results

Comparison of clinical baseline data of patients in the control and coronary heart disease groups

Comparison between the control and CHD groups showed no statistically significant differences in gender, smoking history, or alcohol consumption history (P > 0.05). This suggests that these factors may not be primary contributors to the development of CHD in this cohort. Patients in the CHD group showed several key differences compared to the control group. They were older on average. Moreover, their levels of white-cell count (WBC), neutrophils (NE), monocytes (MONO), LDL-C were notably higher. Conversely, the levels of lymphocytes (LY), red-cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), platelet count (PLT), HDL-C were lower in the coronary heart disease group. Comparison of fecal sulfatide levels between the control group and different coronary subgroups

All samples were tested in duplicate. The CV range of the duplicate test results for individual samples was from 1.01% to 10.78%, with an average CV of 5.28%. Among these, 98.99% of the samples had a CV < 10%, indicating good repeatability of the test results. The fecal sulfatide levels were analyzed in groups of patients diagnosed with SAP, UAP, and AMI, revealing concentrations of 2.22 (1.80, 2.65) μmol/L, 2.22 (1.95, 2.66) μmol/L, and 2.53 (2.14, 2.75) μmol, respectively. These levels were notably elevated in comparison with the normal control group, which exhibited levels of 1.63 (1.38, 1.92) μmol/L (P < 0.001). Moreover, the AMI group demonstrated significantly higher sulfatide levels than both the SAP and UAP groups (P < 0.001). The results revealed that in a univariate logistic regression analysis using the backward elimination method, fecal sulfatide, age, WBC, NE, MONO, and LDL-C were identified as risk factors for CHD. Conversely, LY, RBC, HGB, and HDL-C were identified as protective factors. This is represented in Table 3. Collinearity diagnosis was performed on the statistically significant factors selected from the univariate analysis. The results showed that the variance inflation factors (VIF) for WBC, NE, and MONO were greater than 10, indicating the presence of multicollinearity among these variables. Therefore, WBC was removed. A second collinearity diagnosis was conducted, and the results showed that the VIF for all variables was less than 10, indicating the absence of multicollinearity. Assessment of the predictive value of fecal sulfatide for coronary heart disease

The predictive performance of various biomarkers for CHD was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. Results demonstrated that HDL-C showed an optimal cutoff value of 1.165 mmol/L with AUC 0.773 (95% CI 0.738, 0.808), sensitivity 78.5%, specificity 62.2%, and Youden index 0.407. LDL-C exhibited an optimal cutoff value of 3.615 mmol/L with improved AUC 0.839 (95% CI 0.807, 0.871), sensitivity 84.7%, specificity 70.0%, and Youden index 0.547. Fecal sulfatide demonstrated the most prominent predictive performance, achieving AUC 0.893 (95% CI 0.867, 0.921) at the optimal cutoff of 1.825 μmol/L, with sensitivity 90.4%, specificity 70.5%, and Youden index 0.609. Discussion

CHD is characterized by coronary artery atherosclerosis, causing lumen narrowing/occlusion and subsequent myocardial ischemia, hypoxia, or necrosis [14–16]. Its primary pathogenesis involves atherosclerotic lesions and local inflammation, which interact synergistically: lesions provide a substrate for inflammation, while inflammation accelerates lesion progression and plaque instability [17–19].

Sulfatide—a lipid molecule composed of ceramide, galactose, and sulfate (derived from galactosylceramide) [20]—plays a key role in CHD via multiple mechanisms. It modulates inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α) [21–23], with the C16:0 subtype reducing these factors by 20–30%. It also regulates coagulation: under physiological conditions, it inhibits platelet aggregation [24]; pathologically, it promotes thrombus formation via coagulation pathway activation [25, 26]. Sulfatide’s involvement in oxidative stress, endothelial function, and atherosclerosis [27–29] supports its potential as a cardiovascular biomarker. For example, elevated sulfatide correlates with acute myocardial infarction severity [30], carotid intima–media thickness in hypertensive patients [31], and adverse outcomes in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction [32].

Developing non-invasive sulfatide detection is critical for CHD management. Blood testing, while standard, involves painful venipuncture and risks contamination. Fecal sulfatide offers a viable alternative: 5–15% of lipids are excreted in feces, so fecal levels indirectly reflect blood concentrations, providing insights into plaque burden and inflammation. This method is non-invasive, simple, and suitable for longitudinal monitoring, reducing reliance on frequent blood draws in high-risk populations.

Our findings show significantly higher fecal sulfatide in CHD patients vs. controls, with the highest levels in the AMI subgroup—suggesting associations with lesion progression and plaque stability. Multivariate logistic regression identified fecal sulfatide as an independent CHD risk factor. ROC analysis demonstrated its strong predictive capacity (AUC = 0.893), which improved further when combined with LDL-C and HDL-C (AUC = 0.958), indicating synergistic value in integrating gut-derived metabolites with conventional lipids.

The non-invasive nature of fecal sulfatide detection offers key clinical advantages: reduced patient discomfort and infection risk (boosting compliance, especially in elderly/high-risk groups), suitability for longitudinal monitoring, and lower costs—facilitating use in resource-limited settings or large epidemiological studies. Compared to our prior work on fecal sulfatide combined with NLR [33], this study is the first to systematically validate its independent value and innovatively enhance accuracy via integration with traditional lipids.

In summary, our results support fecal sulfatide as a promising non-invasive biomarker for CHD. Its integration with conventional lipids improves predictive precision, advancing the translation of non-invasive tools for early screening and dynamic monitoring.

This study has several key limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings: the retrospective, single-center design limits generalizability, as the cohort may not represent broader populations. The relatively small sample size—particularly in subgroups like severe AMI—further restricts statistical robustness; the study only observes associations between fecal sulfatide and CHD rather than establishing causality. Direct mechanistic experiments (e.g., in vitro assays and animal models) are lacking to verify the biological link between fecal sulfatide and CHD pathogenesis; Controls were recruited from hospital physical examination attendees, who likely have higher health awareness or socioeconomic status than the general population. This may introduce baseline differences that affect comparability with the CHD group; Multivariate logistic regression showed extremely wide confidence intervals for odds ratios, indicating model instability. Potential causes include limited variability in the homogeneous single-center cohort and small subgroup sizes, which reduce the precision of estimates.

These limitations highlight the need for future large-scale, prospective, multi-center studies with diverse samples to validate findings. In addition, mechanistic experiments and improved statistical approaches are required to strengthen causal inference and model robustness.

Conclusions

This study explored fecal sulfatide as a candidate non-invasive biomarker, finding it correlated with coronary artery pathology in CHD patients via quantitative fecal analysis. In this single-center retrospective cohort, fecal sulfatide showed independent predictive value for CHD, with accuracy significantly enhanced when combined with conventional blood biomarkers. These results provide preliminary evidence for its potential in early CHD prediction and risk stratification. While promising, the clinical utility of fecal sulfatide requires confirmation through large-scale, prospective multi-center studies to validate its applicability.
