Early versus delayed enteral nutrition for preterm Neonates: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Abdulwahab Alqahtani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Preterm neonates often face challenges related to enteral nutrition initiation, and the optimal timing remains a subject of ongoing debate.Study Aim:This meta-analysis aimed to synthesize evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing early versus delayed enteral nutrition initiation in preterm neonates.Methods:A systematic literature search identified eight RCTs meeting inclusion criteria, encompassing diverse populations and protocols. The primary outcome assessed was the incidence of feeding intolerance. Meta-analysis was conducted using a fixed-effects model, and heterogeneity was assessed.Results:The meta-analysis demonstrated that early enteral feeding significantly increased the incidence of feeding intolerance among preterm infants (OR: 1.54, 95% CI: 1.18–2.01). Individual studies exhibited varying trends, with some indicating balanced risks and others suggesting increased feeding intolerance in the early initiation group. The findings underscore the importance of considering individual patient characteristics and local protocols when determining the timing of enteral nutrition initiation. Variability in outcomes across different populations highlights the need for tailored approaches in neonatal care.Conclusion:This meta-analysis contributes insights into the association between early enteral feeding initiation and feeding intolerance in preterm neonates. Clinicians should adopt a nuanced approach, emphasizing vigilant monitoring and timely adjustments in enteral nutrition practices. Further research, including large-scale RCTs with standardized protocols, is warranted to refine guidelines and optimize neonatal care practices.

Background

Preterm birth, defined as birth before 37 weeks of gestation, remains a global public health concern with significant implications for neonatal care and long-term health outcomes.[1] The incidence of preterm birth varies worldwide, accounting for approximately 10% of all live births.[12] Preterm infants face numerous challenges related to their physiological immaturity, particularly in organ systems such as the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and immune systems.[23]

One of the critical challenges in caring for preterm infants is addressing their nutritional needs, as these infants often miss out on the essential nutrients and protective factors provided in utero during the last trimester.[4] Adequate nutrition is vital for supporting growth, organ development, and immune function in these vulnerable neonates.[1] However, determining the optimal timing for the initiation of enteral nutrition, the administration of nutrients through the gastrointestinal tract, has been a subject of ongoing research and debate.[5678]

Enteral nutrition is a cornerstone of neonatal care, aiming to bridge the nutritional gap created by preterm birth.[9] The decision of when to initiate enteral nutrition in preterm infants is multifaceted, considering the delicate balance between the benefits of early nutrition and the potential risks of gastrointestinal immaturity.[10]

The gastrointestinal tract of preterm infants undergoes intricate developmental changes, and the ability to tolerate enteral feeds varies widely among these neonates.[78] Early initiation of enteral nutrition has been advocated for its potential benefits, including the promotion of gut development, stimulation of digestive enzymes, and the maintenance of mucosal integrity. Additionally, early enteral feeding is thought to enhance the maturation of the gut-associated lymphoid tissue, contributing to immune function.[1112]

Despite the potential advantages of early enteral nutrition, the risk of feeding intolerance in preterm infants poses a significant concern. Feeding intolerance is a complex clinical phenomenon characterized by the inability to digest and absorb enteral feeds, often manifesting as abdominal distension, vomiting, and an increased gastric residual volume.[1314] The aetiology of feeding intolerance in preterm infants is multifactorial, involving factors such as immature gastrointestinal motility, impaired mucosal integrity, and variations in nutrient composition and delivery.[15]

Feeding intolerance in preterm infants not only compromises nutritional intake but also places these vulnerable neonates at an increased risk of complications such as necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), a devastating gastrointestinal disease with potentially severe consequences.[1216] Striking the right balance between the benefits of early enteral nutrition and the risks of feeding intolerance is a clinical challenge that necessitates evidence-based guidelines.[17]

The optimal timing for initiating enteral nutrition in preterm infants has been a subject of considerable research, leading to varying practices and guidelines across neonatal care units.[17] Some studies have suggested that early enteral feeding is associated with improved growth, reduced infectious complications, and shorter hospital stays. Conversely, other studies have raised concerns about the potential risks of early feeding, particularly in terms of an increased incidence of feeding intolerance and NEC.[1819]

Existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses on this topic exhibit heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and outcomes assessed, contributing to the ongoing controversies. Moreover, as neonatal care practices continue to evolve, incorporating new technologies and interventions, it becomes imperative to revisit and synthesize the available evidence to inform current clinical practice.[17]

Study Aim

Considering the existing uncertainties and controversies, our meta-analysis seeks to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the current evidence on the timing of enteral nutrition initiation in preterm neonates. By systematically reviewing and quantitatively analyzing data from randomized controlled trials, we aim to contribute insights to the ongoing discourse, guiding neonatal care practices and informing evidence-based guidelines.

Methodology

Study design

This study is a systematic review and meta-analysis. The study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to ensure transparency and quality in reporting.[20]

Operational definitions:

The topic of early and delayed feeding were defined as initiation ≤48 hours and >72 hours post-birth, respectively.

Feeding intolerance: ≥2 clinical signs (emesis, abdominal distension, or residual volume >50% of feed) persisting for 24 hours.

Search strategy

A systematic and comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify relevant studies from electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL. The search strategy utilized a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to preterm neonates, enteral nutrition, and randomized controlled trials. The search was restricted to studies published in English up to the search date.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included in the meta-analysis if they met the following criteria: (a) randomized controlled trials; (b) involving preterm neonates; (c) comparing early versus delayed enteral nutrition initiation; and (d) reporting outcomes related to feeding intolerance. Exclusion criteria encompassed non-randomized studies, observational studies, reviews, and studies not meeting the defined population and intervention criteria.

Study selection

Two independent reviewers conducted the initial screening of titles and abstracts to identify potentially relevant studies. Full-text articles of selected studies were then assessed for eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized form. Extracted data included study characteristics (author, publication year, country), participant demographics (gestational age, birth weight), intervention details (feeding initiation protocols), and outcomes (incidence of feeding intolerance). Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data synthesis

We used the Review Manager version 5.4 software for the meta-analysis. Quantitative data synthesis was performed using the Mantel-Haenszel fixed-effects model to calculate pooled odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The primary outcome was the incidence of feeding intolerance. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the impact of studies with a high risk of bias on the overall results.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test. Funnel plots were visually inspected for asymmetry, and Egger’s test was performed to quantify potential bias.

Results

Search results

The initial database search yielded a total of 806 records. After removing duplicates (n = 318), 488 records underwent title and abstract screening. Following this screening, 412 studies were excluded based on predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, the full texts of the remaining 76 studies were assessed for eligibility, with six records unavailable for retrieval. Finally, 70 full-text articles were thoroughly screened, leading to the inclusion of eight randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in the meta-analysis[2122232425262728] [Figure 1].

Characters of the included studies (n=8)

The inclusion criteria across the studies primarily focused on identifying preterm neonates. Abdelmaaboud et al. included neonates with a gestational age below 37 weeks and birth weight below the 10th centile, while Adeel et al. considered newborns born at a gestational age of 35 weeks or less and weighing less than the 10th percentile for gestational age. Armanian et al. focused on preterm very-low-birth-weight neonates, and other studies such as Bozkurt et al., Karagianni et al., Leaf et al., Srinivasan et al., and Tewari et al. also targeted preterm infants with various gestational age criteria.[2122232425262728]

Exclusion criteria were designed to ensure the homogeneity of the studied populations. Common exclusion criteria included major congenital abnormalities, as seen in Abdelmaaboud et al.,[21] Adeel et al.,[22] Bozkurt et al.,[24] Karagianni et al.,[25] Leaf et al.,[26] and Srinivasan et al.[27] Other specific exclusion criteria encompassed conditions such as twin-twin transfusion, intrauterine transfusion, and inotropic drug support, as noted in Abdelmaaboud et al.[21] Armanian et al.[23] excluded neonates with NEC or intestinal perforation before feedings, and Tewari et al. excluded neonates less than 27 weeks.[2328]

The timing of feeding initiation differed among the studies, reflecting variations in clinical approaches. Abdelmaaboud et al. and Adeel et al.[2122] initiated feeding in the early group within the first few days of life (2 days and within 1 day, respectively), while the delayed group started feeding later (By day 6 and after 3 days, respectively).[2122] Armanian et al.[23] implemented a specific feeding regimen, starting with 20 mL/kg/d for 7 days in 2-hour cycles. Bozkurt et al.[24] gradually advanced feeding, and Karagianni et al.[25] initiated feeding within 5 days in the early group and at 6 days or later in the delayed group. Leaf et al.[26] started feeding in the early group within 1–2 days and in the delayed group within 5–6 days, while Srinivasan et al. and Tewari et al.[2728] initiated feeding in the early group at 1–2 days and by day 2, respectively with delayed feeding starting at 5–6 days and by day 5, respectively.[2728]

The sample sizes varied across studies, ranging from 32 participants in Srinivasan et al.[27] to 404 participants in Leaf et al.[26] Adeel et al.[22] had a sample size of 80, Armanian et al.[23] included 82 participants, Bozkurt et al.[24] had 199 participants, Karagianni et al.[25] included 81 participants, Abdelmaaboud et al.[21] studied 125 participants, and Tewari et al.[28] had a sample size of 62.

The distribution of males within the studies’ populations varied. Abdelmaaboud et al.[21] reported 45.20% males in the early group and 49.20% in the delayed group. Adeel et al.[1922] had 15% males in the early group and 30% in the delayed group. Bozkurt et al.[24] had 45.40% males in the early group and 43.40% in the delayed group. Karagianni et al.[25] reported 42.50% males, Leaf et al.[26] had 52% males, Srinivasan et al.[27] reported 50% males, and Tewari et al.[28] had 60% males in the early group and 50% in the delayed group.

Birth weights were crucial parameters in the comparison between early and delayed enteral nutrition. Abdelmaaboud et al.[21] reported birth weights ranging from 620g to 1270g in the early group and 630g to 1945g in the delayed group. Adeel et al.[22] reported birth weights of 1228.09 ± 197.25g in the early group and 1170 ± 239.93g in the delayed group. Bozkurt et al.[24] reported birth weights of 973 ± 158g in the early group and 953 ± 179g in the delayed group. Karagianni et al.[25] reported birth weights of 1080 (680–1440) g in the early group and 1130 (440–1420) g in the delayed group. Leaf et al.[26] reported birth weights of 1042 (303) g in the early group and 1021 (308) g in the delayed group. Srinivasan et al.[27] reported birth weights of 1119 ± 181.26g in the early group and 1116 ± 181.26g in the delayed group. Tewari et al.[28] reported extreme preterm birth weights of 833.5 ± 121.67g in the early group and 833 ± 119.70g in the delayed group, while very preterm birth weights were 1212.38 ± 118.86g in the early group and 1232.61 ± 119.90g in the delayed group.

Bozkurt et al.[24] reported gestational ages of 27.7 ± 1.8 weeks in the early group and 27.6 ± 2 weeks in the delayed group. Karagianni et al.[25] reported gestational ages of 32.0 (27.0–34.0) weeks in the early group and 31.3 (27.0–34.0) weeks in the delayed group. Quantitative data synthesis

The forest plot in Figure 2 illustrates the incidence of feeding intolerance among preterm infants in the early versus delayed enteral feeding initiation groups. The meta-analysis, employing a fixed-effects model, revealed an overall odds ratio of 1.54 (95% CI: 1.18, 2.01). The analysis demonstrated statistical significance (Z = 3.14, P = 0.002), indicating a higher risk of feeding intolerance in the early initiation group. The overall heterogeneity was minimal (I² =0%), suggesting consistency in the direction and magnitude of the effect across studies.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the impact of studies with a high risk of bias on the overall results. Subgroup analyses did not reveal significant variations in the overall effect estimates, suggesting robustness across diverse study populations.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test [Figure 3]. The funnel plot displayed a symmetrical distribution of studies, and Egger’s test did not indicate significant publication bias (P = 0.48). This suggests that the included studies adequately represent the existing literature on the topic.

Discussion

Preterm neonates often face numerous challenges in their early life, and the optimal timing for initiating enteral nutrition remains a topic of considerable interest and debate in neonatal care.[22930] Our meta-analysis aimed to shed light on this critical aspect by synthesizing data from eight randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing early versus delayed enteral nutrition initiation for preterm neonates. The meta-analysis included studies from various countries, each with its unique population and protocols, contributing to a comprehensive evaluation of this clinical question.

Our meta-analysis revealed a statistically significant association between early enteral feeding initiation and an increased incidence of feeding intolerance among preterm infants. The combined odds ratio (OR) across all studies was 1.54 [95% CI: 1.18, 2.01], indicating a higher risk of feeding intolerance in the early initiation group. This finding is of clinical relevance as feeding intolerance in preterm neonates is associated with adverse outcomes such as NEC and prolonged hospital stays.

The meta-analysis findings prompt a critical examination of the current practices regarding enteral nutrition initiation in preterm neonates. Abdelmaaboud et al.,[21] in the Qatari population, reported a balanced risk of feeding intolerance between early and delayed initiation groups. However, studies from Pakistan (Adeel et al.),[22] Iran (Armanian et al.),[23] and the UK (Leaf et al.)[26] indicated trends towards increased feeding intolerance in the early initiation group. These variations highlight the influence of population characteristics and local protocols on the outcomes.

The study by Bozkurt et al.[24] in Turkey presented an intriguing perspective, suggesting a potential trend towards increased feeding intolerance in the early group, although not statistically significant. This finding aligns with the meta-analysis result and underscores the need for a cautious approach to early enteral feeding initiation. Karagianni et al.[25] in Greece and Srinivasan et al.[27] in India reported comparable risks of feeding intolerance between the two groups, emphasizing the global variability in outcomes and practices in neonatal care.

Contrary to Patole,[17] 2005, our RCT-only analysis found a 54% higher intolerance risk with early feeding, possibly due to stricter outcome definitions. Our meta-analysis’s observed association between early enteral feeding initiation and feeding intolerance is consistent with the findings of Leaf et al.,[26] who reported a higher risk of feeding intolerance in the early group. The study by Tewari et al.[28] in India also supported this trend, although the results were not statistically significant. This consistency across studies reinforces the reliability of our meta-analysis in drawing meaningful conclusions.

Our findings align with some earlier observational studies and systematic reviews that suggested caution in early enteral feeding initiation for preterm infants.[15171820] However, it’s essential to note that our meta-analysis, incorporating RCTs, provides a higher level of evidence and strengthens the existing knowledge base.

The observed heterogeneity among the included studies, although minimal (I² =0%), underscores the diversity in study populations, protocols, and outcomes. This heterogeneity supports the need for tailored approaches in neonatal care, considering individual patient characteristics and local practices.

The clinical implications of our findings are significant. While enteral nutrition is crucial for the growth and development of preterm infants, our results suggest a nuanced approach to its initiation. Clinicians should carefully consider the individualized needs of each infant, considering gestational age, birth weight, and other clinical factors.[181920] Regular monitoring for signs of feeding intolerance and prompt adjustments in feeding regimens are crucial components of neonatal care.

Despite the strengths of our meta-analysis, including the use of RCTs, there are inherent limitations. Variability in study protocols, definitions of feeding intolerance, and outcomes assessment may introduce biases.

Future research should focus on refining protocols for enteral nutrition initiation, considering not only the timing but also the volume and composition of feeds. Large-scale, multicenter RCTs with standardized protocols could provide further insights into optimizing enteral nutrition practices for preterm neonates.

Recommendations

For infants <28 weeks or hemodynamically unstable, delay feeding until stability is achieved; advance cautiously with human milk (20 mL/kg/day).

Limitations

Small samples (e.g. Srinivasan et al., n = 32) may limit power. Future RCTs should report feed type (human milk vs. formula) and volume advancement.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our meta-analysis underscores the nuanced relationship between the timing of enteral feeding initiation and feeding intolerance in preterm neonates. While early initiation may be associated with an increased risk of feeding intolerance, individual patient characteristics and local practices play a crucial role. Clinicians should adopt a tailored approach, emphasizing vigilant monitoring and timely adjustments in enteral nutrition practices. This meta-analysis contributes insights to the evolving landscape of neonatal care, paving the way for more informed and evidence-based practices in the care of preterm neonates.
