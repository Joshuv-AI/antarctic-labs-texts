Ceftazidime–Avibactam With or Without Aztreonam Versus Other Active Antimicrobials in the Treatment of Bloodstream Infections by Carbapenem-Resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae
Authors: Rodrigo C. da Fonseca, Beatriz Arns, Adriana Schmidt, Emerson dos S. Hoffmann, Nadiana Inocente, Amanda de F. Balbinot, Erik M. Martins, Jaysa Pizzi, Guilherme G. L. Sório, Renata D. F. Klafke, Luiza M. Perez, Letícia C. Marinho, Cristiane T. da S. Kawski, Alexandre P. Zavascki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCarbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) bloodstream infections (BSIs) are associated with high mortality and limited treatment options. Evidence supporting ceftazidime–avibactam, with or without aztreonam, from studies exclusively with BSIs caused by CRKP remains scarce, particularly from Latin America, where there is a high burden of disease. In this study, we compared ceftazidime–avibactam-based regimens with other active antimicrobials (OAA) regimens for healthcare-associated CRKP BSIs in a Brazilian tertiary-care hospital.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study including consecutive adult patients with healthcare-associated BSIs caused by CRKP between 2014 and 2024. Patients receiving ceftazidime–avibactam with or without aztreonam were compared with those receiving OAAs. The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included acute kidney injury (AKI), the composite of death or AKI, and post-BSI length of hospital stay. Multivariable Cox regression was performed.ResultsA total of 129 patients were included, of whom 73 received ceftazidime–avibactam-based therapy and 56 received OAAs. Carbapenemase types included class A (82.2%), class B (9.3%), and class A+B co-producing isolates (8.5%). Thirty-day mortality was significantly lower in the ceftazidime–avibactam group than in the OAA group (23.3% vs. 41.1%; P = 0.049). After adjustment, ceftazidime–avibactam-based therapy remained independently associated with lower 30-day mortality (hazard ratio, 0.51; 95% confidence interval, 0.27–0.96; P = 0.04). The composite of death or AKI was also significantly lower with ceftazidime–avibactam-based therapy (P = 0.047), whereas median post-BSI hospital stay among survivors was 8 days shorter (P = 0.06).ConclusionsIn this cohort, ceftazidime–avibactam with or without aztreonam was independently associated with lower 30-day mortality than OAAs in the treatment of CRKP BSIs. These findings extend the available evidence to BSIs caused by carbapenemase-producing isolates and support the need for broader access to ceftazidime–avibactam-based regimens in low- and middle-income countries, where therapeutic options remain limited.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40121-026-01440-2.

Introduction

Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) is a major cause of healthcare-associated infection and has been classified by the World Health Organization as a critical priority pathogen and is associated with high mortality and limited therapeutic options [1]. Resistance to carbapenems is majorly driven by class A and also class B carbapenemase production, which KPC and NDM are by far the most common types, respectively [2]. More recently, class A and B coproducing isolates have been emerging worldwide [3], including in Brazil [4].

Before ceftazidime–avibactam, treatment relied mainly on polymyxins, aminoglycosides or tigecycline, all limited by toxicity, suboptimal pharmacokinetics characteristics in bloodstream infection (BSI), or uncertain efficacy [5, 6] ceftazidime–avibactam is active against class A Enterobacterales, including KPC-producing K. pneumoniae, and observational studies and meta-analyses have generally reported better outcomes with ceftazidime–avibactam-based regimens than with other antimicrobial agents (OAA), often polymyxin-containing therapies in CRE infections [7–10]. Avibactam does not inhibit class B metallo-beta-lactamases (MBLs); however, the combination of ceftazidime–avibactam with aztreonam has been used because avibactam inhibits co-produced serine beta-lactamases while aztreonam remains stable against MBL hydrolysis [11]. Although fewer studies with this combination are available, treatment with ceftazidime–avibactam combined with aztreonam for infections caused by MBL-producing Enterobacterales isolates has been associated with lower mortality rates [11–13].

In Brazil and other low- and middle-income countries, treatment of CRKP bloodstream infections (BSIs) are particularly challenging because of high endemicity of this pathogen, restricted access to newer agents, limiting the options to older, less potent, and more toxic antimicrobials [4, 14]. Ceftazidime–avibactam with or without aztreonam as a first-line agent against KPC- or NDM-producing Enterobacterales is largely restricted to private hospitals, and only a few public hospitals have access to this beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor (BL/BLI), which is often limited to patients with isolates displaying resistance to polymyxins or who presented with severe toxicity to polymyxins [15, 16]. Therefore, the potential benefit of BL/BLIs-based therapies is impaired by this selective use in previously failed patients.

Most studies supporting the use of ceftazidime–avibactam with or without aztreonam for carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales come from Europe and North America. Data from lower- and middle-income countries, particularly Latin America, where increasing prevalences of these pathogens have been reported and mortality rates are among the highest in the world remain limited [12, 17–21]. We therefore conducted a retrospective cohort study comparing ceftazidime–avibactam with or without aztreonam versus OAA for the treatment of healthcare-associated BSI caused by CRKP in a private Brazilian tertiary-care hospital, where these drugs have been recommended as a first-line therapy for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a retrospective cohort study performed at Hospital Moinhos de Vento (HMV), a 500-bed tertiary-care hospital in Porto Alegre, Brazil. Patients were included from January 1, 2014, to December 31, 2024. Data were retrieved from electronic medical records. This study was conducted in accordance with the Brazilian and international regulations set forth in the following documents: Declaration of Helsinki; Brazilian National Health Council Resolution CNS 466/2012 and related Brazilian Ministry of Health publications; and 1996 ICH Harmonized Tripartite Guideline for Good Clinical Practice. The study protocol was approved by the research ethics committee (IRB) of Hospital Moinhos de Vento (CAAE 78523124.0.0000.5330).

Participants

Patients were included if they were ≥18 years old and presented a healthcare-associated BSI caused by a CRKP, defined as the recovery of one or more CRKP isolates from blood culture collected after >48 h of hospitalization. Carbapenem resistance was defined by meropenem resistance. Infection onset (D1) was defined as the calendar day of collection of the index blood culture. Exclusion criteria were death within 48 h of BSI onset and lack of active therapy for at least 2 days. In the case of more than one BSI episode, only the first was considered.

Microbiology

Bacterial identification was performed, depending on the study period, using VITEK® 2 and/or MALDI-TOF MS (VITEK® MS; bioMérieux, France). Susceptibility to ceftazidime–avibactam was determined by either gradient strip (Etest®) or disk diffusion. Polymyxin B and tigecycline MICs were determined by broth microdilution. Susceptibility to other agents was assessed by disk diffusion, and results were interpreted according to EUCAST criteria [22]. Susceptibility breakpoints to polymyxin B and tigecycline were MIC ≤2 mg/l and ≤0.5 mg/l, respectively. All class B or class A + B-producing carbapenemase isolates were presumed to be susceptible to aztreonam–avibactam.

Carbapenemase identification was performed by phenotypic (disk diffusion with inhibitors [23], NG-Test® CARBA-5 [24] or improved Blue-Carba test [25], according to the routine diagnostic method used by the microbiology laboratory.

Outcomes and Covariables

The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality, defined as death from any cause within 30 days of infection onset. The secondary outcomes were: (i) AKI, defined according to KDIGO criteria [26]; (ii) a composite of death in 30 days or AKI; (iii) in-hospital mortality, and (iv) post-BSI length of stay among hospital survivors. AKI was assessed within 30 days of infection onset only among patients not receiving hemodialysis at baseline.

Independent covariables evaluated included age, sex at birth, body mass index, Pitt bacteremia score, Charlson Comorbidity Index, baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), intensive care unit admission at BSI onset, mechanical ventilation at BSI onset, time to first in vitro-active (susceptible in susceptibility test) antimicrobial, time to definitive therapy, number of in vitro-active agents, primary source of BSI, defined according to US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and National Healthcare Safety Network [27] (NHSN), and type of carbapenemase (class A, B or A+B). The primary source of BSI was further grouped into a binary high-risk/low-risk variable for analysis. High-risk sources were defined based on the observed mortality according to each site.

Antimicrobial Therapy

Active therapy was defined as the administration of at least one antimicrobial agent with in vitro activity against the respective BSI isolate for ≥2 calendar days. Empiric active therapy was defined as at least one in vitro-active antimicrobial administered during days 1 to 3 (D1–D3) for ≥2 calendar days. Combination therapy was defined as the use of ≥2 concomitant in vitro-active antimicrobials, both initiated within D1–D7 and overlapping for ≥2 calendar days. Definitive therapy was defined as the in vitro-active antimicrobial administered for the longest duration during the treatment period, evaluated up to a maximum of 14 days after BSI onset. Patients were not required to survive until day 14, as treatment duration was assessed only for the period each patient remained under treatment, up to this predefined limit. In cases where more than one eligible regimen was used, the regimen with the longest duration was selected, with ties resolved by the earliest start date.

According to the criteria above, patients were classified to BL/BLI group if the active therapy was composed either as empiric or definitive therapy by either ceftazidime–avibactam or ceftazidime–avibactam plus aztreonam regimens, or to other antimicrobial agents (OAA) group, if the active agents did not include any of the antimicrobials used in BL/BLI group. The use of aztreonam without ceftazidime–avibactam was included in the OAA group.

The decision for antimicrobial therapy was at the discretion of the attending physician. Ceftazidime–avibactam was administered at the dose of 2.5 g infused over 2 h every 8 h or adjusted for renal clearance. Aztreonam was administered at the dose of 2 g every 8 h, or adjusted for renal clearance, and infused concomitantly to ceftazidime–avibactam. Polymyxin B doses were administered according to the international guidelines [28], and the other agents according to doses described in the Brazilian guidelines for the treatment of multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria [14].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v29.0. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, and continuous variables using Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate.

Two univariate analyses were conducted: the first comparing treatment groups (BL/BLI versus OAA), and the second comparing survivors versus non-survivors at day 30. Variables with P ≤0.20 in univariable analyses were entered into a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model for 30-day mortality using forward stepwise selection (from smaller to larger P values). Variables with P ≤0.10 were retained in the final model. All tests were two-tailed and a P ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 251 carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae BSIs were identified during the study period. After exclusion of 79 duplicates, 172 patients were eligible. Of these, 43 were excluded because of the absence of active therapy (n = 19), death within 48 h after BSI onset (n = 6), or BSI identified before 2 days of admission (n = 18). Finally, 129 patients were included in the analysis: 73 (56.6%) were assigned to the BL/BLI group and 56 (43.4%) to the OAA group (Fig. 1). Overall, patients in BL/BLI and OAA groups presented similar demographics and clinical characteristics (Table 1). Patients in the BL/BLI group had higher eGFR (P = 0.05) and different distribution of infection sites (P = 0.03) compared to the OAA group (Table 1). Time to definitive therapy was significantly longer in the BL/BLI group compared to the OAA group (median 3 [IQR, 2–4] vs. 2 [IQR, 0–3] days; P <0.001). Time to first active antimicrobial and the number of active antimicrobials was similar between groups (Table 1).

Until 2018, all patients received OAA. After 2019 (when ceftazidime–avibactam were commercially available in Brazil), the use of BL/BLI-based therapy increased substantially over time. The proportion of patients receiving BL/BLI-based therapy increased from 38.5% to 81.0% from 2019 to 2024 (P = 0.046). In the BL/BLI group, 50 of 73 (68.5%) patients received ceftazidime–avibactam alone, while 23 (31.5%) received it in combination with aztreonam. Overall, 70 of 129 (54.3%) patients did not receive any active empiric therapy. Among those who received empiric treatment, BL/BLI-based regimens were the most frequently used (16/129, 12.4%), followed by polymyxin B-based regimens (31/129, 24.0%) and other antimicrobials (12/129, 9.3%). For definitive therapy, BL/BLI-based regimens were the predominant choice (71/129, 55.0%), whereas polymyxin B-based regimens and OAAs were used in 46 (35.7%) and 12 (9.3%) patients, respectively (Table 2). Of the 73 patients in the BL/BLI group, 14 (19.2%) received polymyxin B as part of empiric therapy, and six (8.2%) as part of definitive therapy, of whom two (2.7%) who initially received a Post hoc Exploratory Analysis

Restricting the cohort to patients with BSI occurring from 2019 onwards, when ceftazidime–avibactam was available, 30-day mortality was 23.3% (17/73) in the BL/BLI group and 45.7% (16/35) in the OAA group (P = 0.018).

The overall 30-day mortality rates according to carbapenemase production of the isolates were 29.2% (31/106) for class A-producing isolates, 41.7% (5/12) for class B-producing isolates, and 36.4% (4/11) for class A+B co-producers (P = 0.63). Among patients with class A-producing isolates, those receiving BL/BLI-based therapy had significantly lower mortality compared to those receiving OAA (21.5% [14/65] vs. 41.5% [17/41]; P = 0.03). Among patients with class B-producing isolates (33.3% [1/3] vs. 44.4% [4/9]; P = 0.74) and class A+B co-producers (40.0% [2/5] vs. 33.3% [2/6]; P = 0.82), mortality was similar between treatment groups.

In an exploratory analysis to assess potential confounding by carbapenemase type, we compared the distribution of key baseline and treatment variables across carbapenemase types groups (Supplementary Table S1). Patients with class B-producing or A+B co-producing isolates were less likely to receive BL/BLI-based therapy (25.0% and 45.5%, respectively) compared to those with class A-producing isolates (61.3%; P = 0.041). Additionally, class B-producing isolates were associated with non-statistically significant higher proportions of neutropenia (25.0% vs. 13.2% for class A) and high-risk infection sites (50.0% vs. 27.4% for class A), while ICU admission at BSI onset was less frequent among class B and A+B isolates (25.0% and 36.4%, respectively) compared to class A carbapenemase (50.9%).

We also explored the use of adjuvant meropenem in antimicrobial regimens. Concomitant meropenem use was more frequent in the OAA group than in the BL/BLI group (53.6% vs. 8.2%; P < 0.001). Overall, meropenem use was not associated with 30-day mortality (30.6% vs. 31.2%; P = 0.95). In the OAA group, among patients with class A-producing isolates, those who received meropenem concomitantly presented a numerically lower mortality proportion compared to those who did not (32.1% [9/28] vs. 61.5% [8/13]; P = 0.08). In the BL/BLI group, the number of patients receiving meropenem was too small (n = 6) to allow meaningful analysis. The same occurred among patients with class B-producing (n = 2) or co-producing isolates (n = 0).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of healthcare-associated bloodstream infections caused by CRKP, we found that treatment with ceftazidime–avibactam with or without aztreonam (BL/BLI-based therapy) was independently associated with lower 30-day mortality compared to OAA-based regimens. Additionally, BL/BLI-based therapy was associated with a numerically shorter post-BSI length of stay and a significantly lower composite endpoint of death or AKI. Although the study included patients from a period preceding the availability of ceftazidime–avibactam in Brazil, mortality did not differ between the pre- and post-2019 periods. Furthermore, restricting the analysis to patients treated from 2019 onwards yielded similar findings, with BL/BLI-based therapy remaining significantly associated with lower mortality.

The mortality difference between treatment groups is consistent with previous observational studies comparing ceftazidime–avibactam-based therapy with OAAs. However, although several observational studies have compared ceftazidime–avibactam, with or without aztreonam, against OAAs for carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative infections, only six retrospective studies have evaluated ceftazidime–avibactam-based regimens exclusively among patients with CRKP infections, most commonly compared to polymyxin-based regimens [13, 29–33]. Of these, only two investigations included exclusively patients with CRKP BSI as in our study [29, 33]. The first of these two included 15 patients treated with ceftazidime–avibactam and 46 with comparator regimens, with significantly higher rates of survival in (86.7% vs. 56.5%) and lower renal toxicity with ceftazidime–avibactam [33]. In the other study evaluating CRKP BSI, despite positive results, ceftazidime–avibactam was used as salvage therapy, not as a preferred regimen [29]. Patients in the ceftazidime–avibactam group (n = 104) had their treatments initiated after a median of 7 days of prior antimicrobial failure and were compared with a matched cohort receiving other second-line regimens [29]. Despite this, ceftazidime–avibactam was associated with significantly lower 30-day mortality (36.5% vs. 55.8%) and was the only independent predictor of survival in multivariable analysis [29].

In a Chinese multicenter study, 82 patients with CRKP infection who were critically ill at various sites (13.4% BSI) were treated with ceftazidime–avibactam and 82 with polymyxin B, with significantly lower 30-day mortality (35.4% vs. 69.5%) observed with ceftazidime–avibactam [31]. Similarly, Fang et al., in a cohort of 115 Chinese patients (58 with bacteremia), found that ceftazidime–avibactam was associated with statistically significant lower 28-day mortality than polymyxin B (8.1% vs. 29.5%) [30]. In a cohort of 106 patients who were critically ill with CRKP infections, including 38 (35.8%) with bloodstream infection, ceftazidime–avibactam with or without aztreonam (n = 41) was compared with polymyxins (n = 65), and a significantly lower 30-day mortality was observed in the ceftazidime–avibactam group (29.2% vs. 56.9%) [13]. In the bacteremia subgroup, mortality was 33% versus 65%, respectively (P = 0.051) [13]. Finally, only one study, including 276 patients with CRKP infections (27.9% with BSI), found no significant difference in 30-day mortality between ceftazidime–avibactam- and polymyxin B-based regimens, both in the overall cohort (17.4% vs. 16.3%) and after propensity score matching of 63 patients per group (19.0% vs. 20.6%) [32]. A significantly lower incidence of AKI was observed with ceftazidime–avibactam both in the overall cohort and after propensity score matching [32]. Notably, mortality rates in both treatment groups were considerably lower in this study than in previous cohorts.

To our knowledge, no previous study has specifically evaluated the effectiveness of BL/BLI-based regimens—particularly ceftazidime–avibactam plus aztreonam—in infections caused by class A+B carbapenemase co-producers. A particularly noteworthy finding of our study is that in this group, mortality was numerically higher among patients receiving BL/BLI-based therapy compared to those treated with OAA (40.0% [2/5] vs. 33.3% [2/6]). Nonetheless, it must be emphasized that the observed mortality difference according to carbapenemase type warrants careful interpretation due to the small number of patients in this subgroup and to other important covariates that were unevenly distributed in these patients, especially compared with patients with class A-producing BSI. Future studies with larger samples of infections caused by class A and B carbapenemase co-producing isolates are needed to confirm whether the benefit of BL/BLI-based therapy is uniform across different carbapenemase types. Given the small sample size in the MBL-producing CRKP subgroup, these results should be interpreted with caution. Importantly, the benefit of ceftazidime–avibactam plus aztreonam has been confirmed in other cohorts [12, 34].

In our cohort, after local availability, ceftazidime–avibactam with or without aztreonam was recommended as preferred therapy for confirmed carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales infections. This may have reduced the salvage-therapy bias seen in a previous CRKP BSI cohort [29]. However, even as a first-line therapy, time to receive the study drug in BL/BLI was longer than in the OAA group, likely because ceftazidime–avibactam was not recommended as empirical therapy. Despite this, mortality differences were observed and this variable did not remain in the multivariate model.

In the present study, post-BSI length of stay among hospital survivors was numerically shorter in the BL/BLI group, suggesting a potential clinical benefit in terms of earlier hospital discharge. The overall AKI rate was high in both groups (~ 50%). This may be explained, at least in part, by the fact that patients in the BL/BLI group were also exposed to polymyxin B and aminoglycosides in 8.2% and 12.3% of cases, respectively. Additionally, the absence of a difference in AKI rates may also be explained by a competing risk phenomenon. AKI is a time-dependent event that requires patient survival to be diagnosed, and the higher mortality observed in the OAA group may have precluded the detection of AKI in patients who died early. In other words, patients in the OAA group may have died before they could develop AKI, thereby attenuating the expected difference in nephrotoxicity. This interpretation is supported by the significantly lower composite endpoint of death or AKI observed in the BL/BLI group (50.0% vs. 73.0%; P = 0.047). Finally, no statistically significant difference was observed in hospital survival, which was very high in both groups, probably reflecting the overall severity of diseases of these patients.

In our study, concomitant meropenem was used more frequently in the OAA group (53.6%) than in the BL/BLI group (8.2%), reflecting national recommendations of use of polymyxin B-based regimens for CRKP infections [14]. Although meropenem use was not associated with lower mortality in the overall cohort, numerically reduced proportions of death were observed in the OAA subgroup with class A-producing isolates treated with adjuvant meropenem (32.1% vs. 61.5%; P = 0.075).

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, given the observational design, residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment cannot be ruled out. Second, the OAA group was heterogeneous, and 61% of patients received only one active antimicrobial, which should be considered when interpreting mortality comparisons. In addition, although most regimens were polymyxin-based, the comparator group included different antimicrobial classes and combinations. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence of superiority over any specific antimicrobial class. However, this comparator reflects current clinical practice in many hospitals, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where ceftazidime–avibactam and other BL/BLI combinations remain unavailable or are not routinely accessible. Fourth, patients who died within 48 h of BSI onset were excluded because antimicrobial therapy was considered unlikely to meaningfully influence outcomes within such a short interval. Although this approach may have introduced a small degree of immortal-time bias and limited the generalizability of our findings to patients surviving beyond 48 h, only six patients were excluded for this reason, making a meaningful impact on the estimated treatment effect unlikely. Another point is that although the multivariable models remained parsimonious in relation to the number of outcome events and yielded consistent results across alternative model specifications, some degree of overfitting cannot be completely excluded. Seventh, because detailed information on source control interventions was not uniformly documented in the medical records, adjustment for this important determinant of BSI outcomes was not possible. Eighth, the small number of MBL-producing and co-producing isolates limited subgroup analyses and conclusions regarding these resistance mechanisms. Additionally, carbapenemase type was determined using different diagnostic methods throughout the study period, according to the routine methods and test availability in the clinical microbiology laboratory. Nevertheless, all methods employed have demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for carbapenemase detection and classification [23–25], making carbapenemase misclassification unlikely. Although carbapenemase genes were not systematically identified, considering the epidemiology of carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales in Brazil, it is likely that the vast majority, if not all, class A and class B carbapenemases, as well as co-producers, corresponded to KPC, NDM, and KPC+NDM, respectively [4]. Finally, routine in vitro susceptibility testing of aztreonam–avibactam was unavailable; therefore, susceptibility to the combination of ceftazidime–avibactam plus aztreonam was presumed; thus; although resistance rates of aztreonam–avibactam in Enterobacterales have been extremely low [35], we could not rule out that it had occurred in some isolates.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort of healthcare-associated BSIs caused by CRKP, treatment with ceftazidime–avibactam with or without aztreonam was independently associated with lower 30-day mortality than OAAs. This association was observed in patients with BSIs caused by class A-, B-, and A+B carbapenemase-producing isolates, extending the available clinical evidence for ceftazidime–avibactam-based regimens across the contemporary epidemiology of CRKP. Given the limited evidence from Latin America and other low- and middle-income countries, the observed reduction in mortality, together with the potential for shorter hospitalization and lower toxicity rates, further supports the need for broader access to ceftazidime–avibactam-based regimens as first-line therapy for severe CRKP infections in these settings.
