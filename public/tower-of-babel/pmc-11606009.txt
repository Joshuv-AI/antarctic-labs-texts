Clinical trial landscape for histone deacetylation inhibitors in breast cancer: a dawn in the darkness?
Authors: Jiajun Li, Yining Yan, Feng Chen
Published: 2024

To the Editor,

Breast cancer (BRCA) is a highly malignant solid tumor with poor prognosis and a high mortality rate among woman worldwide [1]. Conventional therapeutic methods include surgical resection, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormone therapy as well as targeted therapy [2]. However, the poor response of some patients to current standard-of-care strategies prompts the exploration of new treatment approaches. Recent studies have highlighted the roles epigenetic modification play in BRCA, among which histone deacetylation (HDAC) has been reported to be associated with tumor progression and drug resistance [3, 4]. Notably, several HDAC inhibitors have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to treat Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL) and Multiple Myeloma (MM), both of which have received favorable results. Therefore, it is worthwhile to evaluate the trial landscape of HDAC inhibitors and its potential in BRCA treatment.

A comprehensive analysis of clinical trials was conducted using the Trialtrove database (https://clinicalintelligence.citeline.com/) in November 18, 2024. Keywords included “Mechanism of Action: Histone deacetylase inhibitor”, “Therapeutic Area: Oncology” and “MeSH Term: Breast Neoplasms”. After the exclusion of studies without detailed information, ninety-seven trials were identified and analyzed based on drug names, trial phases, objectives, and other key clinical details (Table S1).

Trials evaluating HDAC inhibitors in BRCA have been conducted in 24 regions, with the United States and China leading in the number of studies (Fig. 1B). Most studies are in early phases (phase I and II: 87.6%). Only a small proportion of trials have progressed to phase III (4.1%) and phase IV (8.2%). Notably, half of the phase IV trials registered are currently planning, focusing on long-term safety, efficacy, and expanding the approved indications (Fig. 1A).

Chidamide (Epidaza), an oral selective HDAC inhibitor, is the most investigated HDAC inhibitor in BRCA, with over half of its trials in phase II and 20% reaching phase IV. Vorinostat (Zolinza) is the second most studied HDAC inhibitor in BRCA, which has been approved to treat CTCL by the FDA. Third comes the Entinostat and all the trials across different phases involving this drug have completed or terminated in BRCA. Trials evaluating other HDAC inhibitors are limited and are predominantly in phase I or II trials (Fig. 1C).

Given the complexity of BRCA oncology as well as its four molecular subtypes, combined therapy methods might receive more favourable results. Therefore, a lot of clinical trials combine HDAC inhibitors with other therapeutic methods, including chemotherapy, hormone therapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy (Fig. 1D).

Previous studies have uncovered the association between HDACs and endocrine therapy resistance in ER + BRCA patients [4]. Therefore, a total of 35 clinical trials evaluated the safety and efficacy of HDAC inhibitors in combination with hormone therapies, including selective ER modulators (e.g., Tamoxifen), aromatase inhibitors (e.g., Exemestane, Letrozole, and Anastrozole), and selective ER down-regulators (e.g., Fulvestrant). Among these, 28 trials focused on combining HDAC inhibitors with hormone therapy only, while the remaining 7 trials further incorporated additional treatment strategies (Fig. 1D).

Immunotherapy has revolutionized the landscape of tumor treatment and is getting widespread attention in treating triple negative breast cancer (TNBC). To date, the number of trials testing HDAC inhibitors plus immunotherapy has remained relatively low compared to other combinations like hormone therapy or chemotherapy. And most of the trials are currently planning or ongoing, indicating that this combination method is still at its infancy and requires further exploration (Fig. 1D).

Targeted therapy is an important component of the current standard-of-care strategies of BRCA. Twenty one of the total trials investigated the effectiveness of HDAC inhibitors plus targeted therapy only or with other additional treatments (Fig. 1D). The targeted strategies combined HDAC inhibitors with HER2 inhibitors (e.g. Trastuzumab), CDK4/6 inhibitors (e.g. Dalpiciclib, BEBT-209 and Ibrance), PARP inhibitors (e.g. Olaparib, Fluzoparib), tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g. Lapatinib), VEGFA inhibitor (e.g. Bevacizumab) or mTOR inhibitors (e.g. Everolimus), etc.

Notwithstanding the great strides achieved, mechanisms of these interventions remain largely unknown and warrant further exploration. For example, elucidating how HDAC inhibitors modulate the tumor microenvironment during immunotherapy might uncover novel resistance mechanisms. A recent phase II trial conducted by Baretti et al. combined Entinostat with Nivolumab in metastatic pancreatic cancer, and they used RNA sequencing, CyTOF and multiplex immunohistochemical staining to explore peripheral and tumor immune profiles changes. Such insights are expected to guide future cancer research and treatment [5].

To recapitulate, clinical studies of HDAC inhibitors in BRCA have been widely conducted globally, with early-phase trials dominating the landscape. Chidamide, Vorinostat and Entinostat have been the top three mostly used drug among the studies investigated. Notably, a majority of the clinical trials are conducted combining HDAC inhibitors with other treatments, and the strategy varies according to the molecular subtypes of BRCA. Future research into the mechanisms of HDAC inhibitor treatments will be essential to optimize their integration into BRCA treatment.
