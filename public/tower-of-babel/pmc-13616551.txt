Obstructive Intramural Coronary Amyloidosis
Authors: Luca Fazzini, Emilia D'Elia, Edoardo Sciatti, Giulia Togni, Michele Senni, Eloisa Arbustini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstructive intramural coronary amyloidosis (OICA) can be the unique manifestation of cardiac amyloidosis (CA). The clinical onset may mimic ischemic heart disease as microvascular angina or myocardial infarction.Case SummaryWe report an 83-year-old man with long-standing angina, recurrent ischemic symptoms, and persistently elevated troponin levels despite normal coronary angiographies, negative bone scintigraphy, and nondiagnostic cardiac magnetic resonance. Given the clinical suspicion for CA, endomyocardial biopsy was performed and showed extensive transthyretin amyloid deposition almost exclusively within the intramural coronary arterioles. These findings established the diagnosis of transthyretin wild-type CA with predominant OICA.DiscussionIschemic presentations of CA are increasingly recognized, but isolated OICA remains underdiagnosed. Our case demonstrates that minimal interstitial deposition with marked microvascular involvement may result in negative scintigraphy, confirming the key diagnostic role of endomyocardial biopsy when clinical suspicion remains high.Take-Home MessageOICA can evade detection and should be suspected in patients with angina, troponin elevation, and normal coronary arteries.

History of Presentation and Past Medical History

An 83-year-old man with a history of arterial hypertension, dyslipidemia, and obesity also had a notable history of bilateral carpal tunnel surgery. He had previously undergone multiple coronary angiographies for suspected exertional angina (in 2018, 2021, and most recently in 2024). However, none of these angiographies revealed significant epicardial coronary artery stenosis, despite a stress/rest myocardial scintigraphy in 2018 that was positive for inducible ischemia in the inferior myocardial segments. Over the years, given the long-lasting hypertension and the possible microvascular, rather than vasospastic, cause of his angina, medical therapy was progressively optimized with ranolazine, transdermal nitrates, and the calcium-channel blocker felodipine, albeit with limited symptomatic relief. In January 2025, the patient presented to our emergency department with progressively worsening chest pain (angina) and exertional dyspnea.Take-Home Messages•Bone scintigraphy may yield negative results in ATTR-CA with predominant OICA, likely reflecting the paucity of interstitial deposits and the possible limited access of tracer to intramural coronary vessels.•A subset of patients presenting with myocardial ischemia, elevated troponin levels, and angiographically normal epicardial coronaries may therefore be underdiagnosed for OICA, highlighting the importance of considering cardiac amyloidosis screening and endomyocardial biopsy in selected cases.

Investigations and Management

Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with normal atrioventricular and intraventricular conduction, nonspecific ventricular repolarization abnormalities, and normal QRS voltage amplitudes. An acute coronary syndrome was ruled out. Laboratory results revealed persistently elevated high-sensitivity troponin levels, peaking at 237 ng/L, 74 ng/L at discharge (it was normal 9 months earlier), normal C-reactive protein, and elevated natriuretic peptides (B-type natriuretic peptide: 141 ng/L; N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide [NT-proBNP]: 1,026 ng/L), which were similar to levels reported in the earlier months. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a left ventricle with normal chamber dimensions but concentric hypertrophy (interventricular septum: 15 mm, posterior wall: 13 mm). Ejection fraction was preserved (60%) without significant regional wall motion abnormalities. However, global longitudinal strain was significantly reduced (−4.3%) with an “apical sparing” pattern. Diastolic dysfunction with elevated filling pressures (E/A: 1.1, deceleration time: 262 ms, E/e′ ratio: 14) was present. The left atrium was markedly dilated (left atrial volume index: 48 mL/m2). Additional findings included thickened mitral valve leaflets with moderate regurgitation, fibrosclerotic aortic valve cusps with mild stenosis, and right ventricular free wall hypertrophy. There were no signs of pulmonary hypertension, venous congestion, or pericardial effusion. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) revealed a subtle strip of late gadolinium enhancement with a nonischemic pattern and elevated T1-mapping values only in the basal inferior-septal region, consistent with interstitial myocardial fibrosis. T2 mapping was normal. Myocardial blood flow (MBF) quantification did not reveal significant abnormalities. Given a high clinical suspicion for infiltrative cardiomyopathy, further investigations were performed. Serum and urine immunofixation, Bence Jones proteinuria testing, and free light chain assays (kappa/lambda ratio and levels) were all negative. Additionally, bone tracer (HDP-Tc99m) myocardial scintigraphy (Perugini score 0), periumbilical fat biopsy, and genetic testing (both TTR and ApoA1 genes) were negative. Clinical traits of systemic amyloidosis, such as periorbital purpura, macroglossia, the shoulder pad sign, and Popeye sign, were absent. Despite negative noninvasive findings, a strong clinical suspicion persisted, and an endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) was performed.

Pathological findings

Seven EMB specimens were entirely serially sectioned and examined to tissue exhaustion. The analysis revealed substantial amyloid deposition, primarily affecting the intramural arterioles in all samples, causing partial to complete luminal occlusion. Small and focal interstitial amyloid deposits were identified in 2 samples, 1 for light microscopy, and 1 for electron microscopy. The paucity of interstitial deposits may explain the negative bone scintigraphy. These findings pointed to a predominantly obstructive intramural coronary amyloidosis (OICA). Amyloid deposits specifically immunostained with anti-TTR antibodies, with no reactivity to anti-kappa or anti-lambda light chain, anti-SAA, and anti-ApoA1 antibodies. These histological findings led to the final diagnosis of transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis (ATTR-CA) with a predominantly OICA involvement (Figure 1). Treatment and Follow-Up

The patient was referred to our amyloidosis outpatient clinic. Functional status was impaired (322 m on the 6-minute walk test; NYHA functional class IIb), with reduced quality of life (Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire score: 36). Overall disease staging corresponded to NAC stage II and Columbia stage II (score 2 + 2 + 2). As a result, in February 2025, disease-specific therapy with tafamidis 61 mg daily was initiated, and the non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker was uptitrated. Sodium glucose transporter 2 inhibitor, transdermal nitrate, and ranolazine were already prescribed. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable and reported no anginal symptoms. Exercise tolerance improved significantly, with an increase in the 6-minute walk distance from 322 to 422 m. Left ventricular ejection fraction remained stable at 60%. Global longitudinal strain showed partial improvement (from −4.8% to −8%), although still markedly reduced. High-sensitivity troponin levels decreased (from 237 to 24 ng/L), while NT-proBNP values remained stable (from 1,026 to 926 ng/L), without clinical or functional deterioration. Overall, although a correlation with tafamidis could only be assumed, the absence of anginal symptoms, improved functional capacity, and stable cardiac function suggested a favorable clinical course under tafamidis therapy.

Discussion

Amyloidosis is a systemic disorder comprising a broad spectrum of clinical phenotypes caused by the extracellular deposition of insoluble, misfolded proteins with a beta-pleated sheet conformation, organized into amyloid fibrils. These deposits infiltrate various tissues, leading to progressive organ dysfunction.1 The heart is among the most commonly affected organs. Amyloid deposits can involve the myocardium, endocardium, epicardial fat, valves, and both epicardial and intramural vessels. Typically, amyloid infiltrates the myocardial extracellular matrix, resulting in increased wall thickness and myocardial stiffening.1,2 Consequently, cardiac amyloidosis (CA) often presents with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and arrhythmias, particularly atrial fibrillation and conduction disturbances.

Although involvement of both epicardial coronary arteries and small intramural vessels is well documented in pathological studies,3,4 ischemic heart disease (IHD) as the initial clinical presentation of CA remains relatively uncommon (Table 1). Nevertheless, myocardial ischemia syndromes occur in a significant proportion of patients with CA, ranging from 25% to as high as 38%. IHD-like manifestations in patients with CA are increasingly recognized, with ischemic symptoms often overlapping with those of HFpEF. In a recent review on chest pain in CA, 10% to 20% of patients reported chest pain as the initial symptom. The prevalence of isolated or predominant OICA is hard to estimate. Current knowledge comes from: 1) large cohorts reporting interstitial amyloid and microvascular involvement with ischemic symptoms despite patent epicardial coronary arteries; 2) autopsy or explanted hearts showing OICA alongside interstitial, endocardial, valvular, and coronary artery amyloid; and 3) case reports or small series where intramural coronary deposition is significant but interstitial amyloid is minimal or absent. Our case raises the question of whether bone scintigraphy can detect isolated OICA when interstitial amyloid is absent or minimal. Whereas imaging has limited EMB indications over time in ATTR-CA diagnosis,3 EMB remains crucial when bone scintigraphy is negative (scores 0 or 1), and the clinical suspicion is high, as in our patient. A negative bone scintigraphy in OICA suggests the tracers may not adequately reach small intramural vessels, allowing OICA to go undetected. CMR can significantly aid in diagnosing microvascular angina, but cannot determine the specific cause of small-vessel disease; quantitative perfusion CMR shows severe inducible ischemia, but usually in CA patients with interstitial infiltration.27 In our patient, microvascular angina was suspected based on negative bone scintigraphy and light chain (AL) screening, CMR suggestive of nonischemic interstitial fibrosis, and elevated NT-proBNP and troponin. CMR is currently recommended (class 2A) for diagnosing ischemia with nonobstructive coronary arteries.28 However, MBF was normal in our patient. Microvascular dysfunction may be present with normal MBF, whereas the coronary flow reserve is more sensitive to it. Additionally, the nonischemic pattern of late gadolinium enhancement on CMR might be explained by the cardiac amyloidosis itself, as the minimal interstitial amyloid deposition observed in our patient likely contributed to the subtle late gadolinium enhancement strip. The unique role of OICA versus OICA plus loss of capillary vessels in inducible myocardial ischemia by CMR stress perfusion mapping27 remains to be elucidated.

In our patient, EMB was essential for diagnosing OICA. Although amyloid detection in EMB depends on disease extent and sample number, expert pathologists identify and characterize amyloid even when present in minimal amounts.29, 30, 31 Whereas in systemic amyloidosis, tissue biopsy from noncardiac organs may obviate the need for EMB, when the heart is the sole affected organ and imaging is noninformative, EMB is essential. In our case, all EMB samples showed microvascular involvement, whereas interstitial deposits were minimal and observed in only 2 of 7 samples.

Further research is needed to clarify the prevalence and clinical relevance of OICA as a distinct phenotype of ATTR-CA. Future studies should assess how often OICA is missed in patients presenting with ischemic symptoms, patent epicardial coronary arteries, and negative bone scintigraphy. The diagnostic yield of advanced imaging modalities, including quantitative perfusion CMR and novel molecular tracers, requires systematic evaluation. Emerging evidence suggests that positron emission tomography imaging with novel amyloid-binding tracers may offer enhanced sensitivity in detecting myocardial amyloid deposits, including in cases of predominantly vascular involvement where bone scintigraphy may be nondiagnostic.32 This may be particularly relevant in patients with ATTR-CA presenting with Perugini grade 0 uptake, as in our patient, in whom conventional bone-avid tracers failed to identify amyloid. However, despite these promising features, the available evidence remains limited; ongoing studies, including the PETAL trial (NCT06048601), are expected to clarify the diagnostic role of positron emission tomography imaging in this setting.

In addition, an important future application for EMB may be in elderly patients presenting with microvascular angina, angiographically normal epicardial coronaries, troponin elevation, and particularly when bone scintigraphy is negative. In such cases, multimodal studies integrating histopathology, advanced imaging (including microvascular assessment), and biomarkers are needed to derive noninvasive signatures of OICA and to triage who truly requires EMB.

Ultimately, a better understanding of OICA will improve early diagnosis, risk stratification, and therapeutic management of CA patients with ischemic manifestations.

Conclusions

Isolated or predominant involvement of intramural coronary arteries by amyloid may account for microvascular angina with troponin elevation in patients with patent coronary arteries, where standard diagnostic tools for CA yield negative results. At present, while awaiting imaging modalities capable not only of detecting CA but also of distinguishing interstitial from isolated or predominant microvascular involvement, EMB should be considered when no alternative diagnostic options are available.
