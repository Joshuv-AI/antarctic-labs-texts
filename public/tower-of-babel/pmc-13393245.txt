Automated measurement of the minimum macular hole diameter based on optical coherence tomography images
Authors: Jianguo Xu, Jiahao Wei, Rong Tan, Jianxin Shen, Jin Yao, Fen Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate measurement of minimum macular hole (MH) diameter is essential for diagnosis and treatment. The manual measurement approach by ophthalmologists is time-consuming, poorly reproducible, and exhibits high inter-observer variability. Threshold-based methods are sensitive to image quality, and perform inadequately in low-contrast optical coherence tomography (OCT) images. Deep learning can achieve higher measurement accuracy but shows limited generalization capability.MethodsWe propose an automated measurement method comprising three sub-tasks. Specifically, a tailored MH dataset is first created by cropping publicly available OCT images to minimize interference from non-MH regions. Subsequently, an image processing pipeline, which consists of denoising, binarization, morphological operations, and edge detection, is implemented to extract the contours on both sides of MH. Finally, an automated measurement algorithm is designed to locate the closest points on the bilateral contours of MH and thereby calculate the minimum diameter.ResultsExtensive experiments are conducted to validate the effectiveness of this research. More concretely, on the public dataset, the metric obtained by the proposed method closely aligns with the metric. Background

MH represents localized full-thickness defects in the macular neuroepithelium [1]. Etiologically, they are classified as either idiopathic or secondary. The pathogenesis of idiopathic MHs remains uncertain, while secondary MHs predominantly result from ocular trauma, macular degeneration, or high myopia. In clinical practice, the minimum diameter measurement of the full-thickness defect, as obtained through OCT, constitutes a critical diagnostic parameter [2]. The International Vitreomacular Traction Study Group classification system [3] identifies this measurement as a key determinant for disease staging and treatment planning, thereby highlighting the clinical significance of precise size quantification. Specifically, based on the minimum diameter, macular holes are classified into three size categories: small (<250 µm), medium (250–400 µm), and large (>400 µm). This classification has direct clinical implications. First, preoperative hole size significantly influences surgical decision-making. Large holes often require more extensive internal limiting membrane peeling or adjunctive procedures such as amniotic membrane grafting, whereas small holes may be managed conservatively or with less invasive surgery. Second, the preoperative minimum diameter is strongly associated with postoperative outcomes, with larger holes showing lower closure rates and worse visual recovery. Third, postoperative management, including follow-up frequency, gas tamponade duration, and positioning requirements, also varies according to hole size. Therefore, accurate and reproducible measurement of the minimum diameter is essential for optimal patient care.

In recent years, to promote the diagnosis and treatment of MHs, researchers are increasingly turning to artificial intelligence technologies. Hu et al. [4] developed a deep learning model based on VGG16 that uses preoperative OCT images to predict postoperative closure. Xiao et al. [5] employed machine learning to integrate OCT parameters with clinical variables, enhancing prediction accuracy. Building on this, Xiao et al. [6] introduced a multimodal deep fusion network that combines imaging and clinical data for improved prognostic performance. Sun et al. [7] proposed a convolutional neural network utilizing 2D feature maps to streamline OCT volume classification, thereby reducing the computational burden. Meanwhile, Huang et al. [8] designed a ResNet-34-based model for the fine-grained classification of myopic traction maculopathy. Collectively, these studies provide novel tools for predicting surgical outcomes and classifying macular diseases, paving the way for intelligent ophthalmologic care.

In the field of morphological analysis of MHs, researchers are committed to developing more precise measurement methods to better assess disease severity and treatment outcomes. Chen et al. [9] introduced a 3D segmentation algorithm for OCT images to enhance the accuracy of morphological and volumetric analyses. Pereira et al. [10] further validated an automated deep learning approach for volume segmentation, demonstrating its superiority in predicting surgical outcomes compared to manual methods. Taşlıpınar et al. [11] utilized 3D OCT to evaluate volume changes in lamellar MHs, providing a tool for the early detection of disease progression. Xu et al. [12] developed a multi-scale segmentation algorithm incorporating curvature-based surface techniques for 3D quantification. Hung et al. [13] employed YOLOv7 and Otsu’s method to automate the detection of MHs and the measurement of their minimum diameter. Philippakis et al. [14] demonstrated that en face OCT-based area measurements reduce human bias compared to manual diameter measurements. These studies not only provide innovative approaches for the morphological analysis of MHs but also offer more accurate diagnostic tools for clinical practice.

In terms of measuring the minimum diameter of MH, although existing methods have made significant progress in automation and precision, they still exhibit limitations. Traditional image-processing techniques often suffer from sensitivity to image quality and insufficient adaptability to complex morphological variations. Meanwhile, deep learning-based approaches frequently face challenges such as heavy reliance on large annotated datasets, limited generalization capability across different imaging devices, and poor interpretability of measurement results. Furthermore, currently, the minimum diameter of MH is measured manually by ophthalmologists using caliper tools on OCT images. However, this manual approach suffers from several limitations. First, inter-operator variability arises because different clinicians may place measurement markers at slightly different positions on the same OCT image. Second, intra-operator variability exists even for the same clinician due to the pixel-level difficulty of precise marker placement. Third, selecting OCT images at different scan locations of MH can also lead to measurement inconsistency, as measuring at the periphery rather than at the central foveal slice may underestimate the true hole diameter. These issues may lead to misclassification of hole size and potentially suboptimal treatment decisions.

Therefore, developing more robust and efficient automated measurement methods is of great significance for improving the clinical diagnosis and treatment of MH. Related works

Image denoising

Image denoising is a crucial preprocessing step in image processing, aiming to enhance image quality by removing noise while preserving important details. Various methods have been proposed to address this challenge. Xu et al. [15] combined a Deep Image Prior with sparsity and regularization techniques to effectively eliminate noise and retain details. Zhu et al. [16] enhanced wavelet thresholding by modifying both the threshold values and function to improve the separation of image signals from noise. Zhang et al. [17] proposed a double-weighted correlated total variation method that leverages low-rank and sparse priors to achieve superior denoising performance. He et al. [18] integrated quantum circuits prior to the input of a classical convolutional network to improve feature extraction capabilities. Zhu et al. [19] introduced a multi-branch blind-spot network with replacement refinement to mitigate information loss in self-supervised denoising. Chen et al. [20] utilized a denoising diffusion probability model to eliminate the noise in the image and achieved better results in restoring illumination and detail. Anand et al. [21] developed a channel-wise approach combining discrete wavelet transform and singular value decomposition for adaptive denoising of multispectral images. Gong et al. [22] employed a denoising diffusion probabilistic model, incorporating prior knowledge to reduce uncertainty and outperform traditional PET denoising methods. These methods highlight the diversity and effectiveness of modern image denoising techniques across various domains and noise conditions.

Edge detection

Edge detection is a fundamental task in image processing, crucial for identifying object boundaries and facilitating higher-level computer vision applications. Hassan et al. [23] combined soft computing with image processing to accurately extract bone boundaries from X-rays with low computational cost. Rakesh et al. [24] integrated weighted guided image filtering with the Sobel operator to achieve efficient and accurate real-time edge detection. An et al. [25] employed multi-directional anisotropic Gaussian directional derivatives to enhance edge localization accuracy while reducing edge stretching. Wang et al. [26] proposed a dual-branch attention fusion model with Canny-assisted supervision to bridge traditional and deep learning-based approaches. Li et al. [27] combined wavelet and Gaussian denoising with adaptive thresholding to effectively handle images corrupted by Gaussian noise. Jansirani et al. [28] introduced a butterfly-optimized adaptive resonance neural model, achieving high detection accuracy with improved time efficiency. Xiong et al. [29] developed a machine vision-based approach using an enhanced SqueezeNet model for automated detection of photovoltaic glass edge defects with high accuracy. These studies collectively demonstrate significant advancements in edge detection methodologies, highlighting the field’s diversity in techniques and applications.

It can be observed that image denoising and edge detection are widely distributed across various visual tasks. Although these approaches differ in their specific application targets, the coupling idea of relevant skills and practical events have greatly inspired the construction of the solutions in this paper.

The proposed method

This section describes the implementation process for automatically measuring the minimum diameter of MH based on OCT images. As shown in Fig. 1, the method can be divided into three coherent subtasks. First, a tailored MH dataset is constructed in the data preparation stage through a meticulous cropping process of publicly OCT images, which guaranteed the inclusion of the essential MH areas. Subsequently, image processing is carried out, which includes steps such as image denoising, binarization, morphological operations, and edge detection, ultimately extracting two clear contour lines of the MH. Finally, we design an automated measurement algorithm that identifies the two contour lines of the MH, locates their points of closest proximity, and uses the minimum Euclidean distance between them to determine the MH’s minimum diameter.

The overall flow chart

Data preparation

The study employed MH images sourced from the publicly available OCTID dataset [30], supplemented by an additional clinical dataset of 96 radial OCT images for final testing. Through rigorous screening by two retinal specialists, 60 OCT images from the OCTID database meeting stringent measurement criteria were selected for analysis. Exclusion criteria encompassed images with incomplete lesion presentation, severe artifacts compromising image quality, or ambiguous cases where MH boundaries could not be reliably identified, thereby ensuring only clinically relevant and measurable cases were included. To mitigate interference from non-essential image parts, the OCT images are meticulously cropped in this step to preserve only the critical regions, thus ensuring optimal input for downstream image processing.

As shown in Fig. 2, the OCT images show the MH area as low-reflective, consistent with the vitreous cavity reflection. Its base is the exposed, highly reflective retinal pigment epithelium layer, and its sides are the well-defined, highly reflective retinal tissues. Sometimes, tissue that is completely or partially detached due to traction by the internal limiting membrane of the vitreous can be seen above it. Cystoid macular edema is very common in MH cases and appears in the OCT images as uniformly low-reflective cystic structures with varying sizes and high-reflective intervals between different retinal layers. By performing image cropping, interference from the retinal pigment epithelium layer, cystic structures, excessive image noise, and other tissues in subsequent image processing can be avoided. This step lays the foundation for edge extraction of the MH and the automated measurement of its minimum diameter.

The process of data preparation

Image processing

With the tailored MH dataset completed, the workflow advances to image processing in this section. First, each image is denoised to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. Subsequently, the target region is segmented through binarization. Morphological operations are then utilized to optimize the structural features. Finally, the contour information of the MH is extracted through edge detection.

Image denoising

The original OCT images contain significant noise, which can severely compromise subsequent image processing results. Therefore, a preprocessing step that combines linear and nonlinear filtering is employed to denoise the images.

The result of linear filtering is a linear combination of all pixel values. During the image acquisition process, Gaussian noise is easily introduced, and Gaussian filtering will effectively remove this type of noise. The essence of Gaussian filtering is to perform a convolution operation on the image using a Gaussian kernel generated by a Gaussian function. Here, is the standard deviation of the Gaussian filter. The larger the , the wider the bandwidth of the Gaussian filter, the better the smoothing effect on the image, and the more blurred the image becomes. Thus, it is necessary to choose an appropriate Gaussian kernel size and value to ensure that the image edges do not become overly blurred while denoising.

After linear filtering, noise is not eliminated but remains in a softer form. Using nonlinear filtering at this stage, which filters out noise through logical judgment, can achieve better image processing effects. This section employs median filtering as the nonlinear technique, whose fundamental principle is to replace the pixel value at the center of the filter with the median value of all pixels within its range. As a nonlinear signal processing method based on order statistics theory, it can effectively suppress noise. Median filtering will preserve the edge information of the image well, but an appropriate filter size needs to be chosen to avoid excessive blurring of image details after denoising. Image binarization

In this stage, the denoised image undergoes further binarization processing to generate an image containing only black and white pixels, thereby establishing the foundation for subsequent edge detection. The grayscale threshold is a critical factor affecting binarization results. This paper employs the TRIANGLE method to automatically determine the global threshold. This purely geometry-based approach first calculates the grayscale intensity histogram of the image, assuming the histogram’s highest peak is near the brightest region. Subsequently, it constructs a triangle to find the maximum straight-line distance and determines the threshold based on the grayscale level corresponding to this maximum distance in the histogram, thereby achieving the objective of image binarization. Morphological operations

Since cystoid macular edema is very common in MH cases, corresponding cystic structures can be observed in many OCT images. In the binarized image, the cystic structure appears as a closed black area, which will interfere with the edge extraction of the MH. Through morphological operations, components meaningful for expressing and describing the shape and position of the region can be extracted from the image, so that the subsequent recognition work can grasp the most essential shape characteristics of the object. Therefore, it is of great significance to perform morphological operations on the binarized image to remove the cystic area for subsequent image processing. To complete the task mentioned above, a series of operations (closing, opening, erosion, filling, and dilation) are applied in sequence to the binarized image.

Image erosion and dilation are fundamental morphological operations that require a template matrix during processing, which is referred to as a structural element. Common structural elements include cross-shaped, rectangular, and elliptical ones. In this stage, we select a 3 × 3 rectangular structural element to perform both erosion and dilation operations on the binarized image. The erosion operation is used to remove smaller connected components caused by noise, while the dilation operation, being the inverse of erosion, ensures that the area of the main region does not shrink. Edge detection

The binarized OCT image provides comprehensive information about the entire MH region. However, measuring the minimum diameter of the MH still requires the critical step of edge detection, which identifies areas where pixel intensity values change abruptly. To ensure the integrity of the MH edges and reduce the impact of noise, the Canny algorithm is utilized for this task. The entire detection process comprises image denoising, gradient calculation, non-maximum suppression, and double-threshold screening.

Firstly, image denoising is performed. Noise, which is characterized by significant grayscale changes, can easily be mistaken for false edges. To mitigate this, Gaussian filtering is applied to smooth the image and reduce noise, typically using a 5 × 5 Gaussian filter as shown in formula (4).

Secondly, the image gradient is calculated to detect potential edges using the Sobel operator. This operator computes gradients in both the x and y directions. Based on these gradient components, the gradient magnitude GM and direction are then derived using formulas (5) and (6).

where and represent the horizontal and vertical gradient components of the image, obtained by convolving it with the vertical and horizontal Sobel kernels, respectively. This process of discrete differentiation allows the Sobel operator to effectively extract image edges.

Next, non-maximum suppression is applied to eliminate spurious responses from edge detection. Regions with grayscale changes are typically concentrated. By retaining only the pixels with the maximum gradient magnitude in the local range along the gradient direction and discarding the others, a large number of redundant points can be eliminated. This process converts edges that are multiple pixels wide into single-pixel-wide edges.

Finally, the double-threshold screening step is performed. Although non-maximum suppression has been applied, a considerable number of potential edge points remain. To distinguish between strong and weak edges, the double-threshold technique is employed. Specifically, pixels whose gradient magnitude exceeds the high threshold are identified as strong edges and are retained. In contrast, pixels with a gradient magnitude below the low threshold are discarded entirely. Those pixels with gradient values falling between the two thresholds are categorized as weak edges. Subsequently, a local search is conducted in the neighborhood of weak edges. If a strong edge is found, the weak edge is retained. Otherwise, it is deleted.

This process is repeated for every pixel on the left contour. Results

Results of binarization and edge detection

To demonstrate the superiority of the binarization scheme (namely TRIANGLE) used in this study, we conducted comparative experiments with two other representative binarization schemes: BINARY50 and BINARY+OTSU. The key differences between these schemes primarily lie in their threshold selection mechanisms, edge preservation capabilities, and adaptability to varying image contrast conditions.

BINARY50: This scheme employs a fixed threshold at 50% of the maximum grayscale value for binarization, where all pixels are classified based on this predetermined threshold. As the most fundamental thresholding approach, its primary advantages include extremely high computational efficiency and perfectly reproducible results, establishing an essential performance benchmark for comparative analyses.

BINARY+OTSU: This scheme combines the advantages of global thresholding with Otsu adaptive algorithm, automatically determining the optimal segmentation threshold by minimizing intra-class variance. It dynamically adapts to images with varying contrast levels, significantly improving segmentation accuracy under complex illumination conditions while maintaining high computational efficiency.

Discussions

This section evaluates the performance of the proposed method for measuring the minimum diameter of MH, including a comparative analysis of different binarization schemes, along with quantitative and qualitative experiments on both the public and clinical datasets.

Comparison of different methods for binarization and edge detection

It can be observed from Fig. 11 that the BINARY50 results in numerous scattered noise points and fails to preserve complete boundary information. Although the BINARY+OTSU effectively eliminates most noise points, it still yields discontinuous and fragmented edges. In comparison, the TRIANGLE demonstrates superior performance, generating continuous and intact binarized contours. When it comes to edge detection, the representations produced by BINARY50, as illustrated in Fig. 12, are characterized by oversimplification and a consistent failure to capture subtle edge variations across all cases. Although the BINARY+OTSU demonstrates improved contrast adaptation relative to BINARY50, it still yields discontinuous boundaries containing noticeable spurious edge fragments. In contrast, the TRIANGLE maintains superior performance across all test samples, generating continuous and anatomically precise boundary contours.

Overall, the TRIANGLE demonstrates clear advantages over conventional approaches, as evidenced by both its binarization results and final edge detection performance. It is capable of generating cleaner initial segmentations and more continuous, anatomically accurate boundaries for MHs of varying morphologies, thereby providing a relatively reliable technical foundation for subsequent morphological measurements of MHs.

Performance of the proposed method on the public dataset

Measurement accuracy

From the Dm metric in Table 2, it can be seen that there are significant variations in the minimum diameter of MH among different patients, with a maximum value of 124.02 pixel and a minimum of only 10.05 pixel. This variation arises from differences in hole morphology across patients and further suggests that individual differences should be taken into account in manual measurements. Notably, for each MH patient, the Da value is very close to the value. In most cases, the difference (i.e., the ADE metric) is under 1.00 pixel, and in instances like MH5 and MH11, the values are identical (i.e., ADE = 0.00 pixel), demonstrating a clear potential advantage of the proposed method. From the perspective of the RDE metric, its values are consistently maintained at low levels, which further attests to the stability of the proposed method in this study. Furthermore, Fig. 13 shows that the ADE for each case does not exceed 1.2 pixel, ranging from 0.00 to 1.13 pixel. Figure 14 indicates that RDE values range from 0.00% to 3.29%, with most below 2.50%. These distribution characteristics more intuitively highlight the benefits offered by the automated MH minimum diameter measurement method and its potential application value.

Localization accuracy

Based on the ED1 and ED2 metrics in Table 3, there are certain differences in the localization results for the left and right contour points of the MH. However, the distances from these points to the manually annotated points are generally within 2 pixel. In some test samples, the point localization results of the proposed method even achieve perfect alignment (i.e., a Euclidean distance of 0) with the manual annotations, further indicating the promising performance of the method at the point-localization level. From the perspective of the AED metric, as shown in Table 3 and Fig. 15, the AED values for the MH test samples range from 0.5 to 6.06 pixel, with most samples below 3 pixel. This demonstrates that the proposed method achieves a considerable level of performance in the average localization of bilateral MH contour points. Furthermore, it can be observed that the contour points detected by the proposed method in Fig. 16(b) are spatially very close to the manually annotated points in Fig. 16(a). Although minor discrepancies exist, the overall spatial distribution exhibits remarkable consistency, further confirming the effectiveness of the proposed method.

Actual performance

Regarding the minimum MH diameter measurement, the results obtained using our method and shown in Fig. 17 visually align well with the annotation style in ophthalmic clinical practice, highlighting the potential value of this study. In summary, both the quantitative experiments on minimum MH diameter measurement and contour point localization, together with the corresponding qualitative findings, provide comprehensive support for the effectiveness and competitive strengths of the automated measurement method.

Application effectiveness of the proposed method on the clinical dataset

With the aim of assessing the proposed method’s potential for practical application, we conducted tests on a clinical dataset to evaluate its performance. A significant difference in Table 4 is observed between the maximum and minimum values within each test sample. For instance, the discrepancy between the two metrics for Sample 1 is 27. Therefore, relying solely on manual measurement of the minimum MH diameter from a single OCT image may not be representative. Furthermore, manually measuring all images in a test sample to obtain the minimum value incurs a high time cost, while the proposed method accomplishes this task in approximately 4 seconds. Moreover, these visualizations in Fig. 18 also provide clear evidence that the proposed method effectively accomplishes its core objective of precise minimum MH diameter measurement. Overall, the combined quantitative and qualitative experiments on the clinical dataset corroborate the effectiveness of the proposed automated measurement method and affirm its promising potential to assist in future MH condition assessment.

The above discussion comprehensively evaluates the effectiveness of our proposed method using both public and clinical datasets. At the same time, a substantial number of quantitative and qualitative experiments have demonstrated its potential value for future clinical applications. For example, it is well known that in clinical settings, due to variations in ophthalmologists’ experience, different doctors may place measurement markers at different positions when measuring the minimum diameter of a macular hole on the same OCT image. Even the same doctor may obtain slightly different results upon repeated measurements due to the pixel-level difficulty of manual operation. In contrast, our method is an algorithm-driven automated measurement solution. With fixed internal algorithm parameters, it can produce consistent repeated measurement results, thereby ensuring reliability. Furthermore, differences in manual measurement results among ophthalmologists also arise from variations in the selection of scan locations for the macular hole. For instance, an OCT image captured at the center of the macular hole more accurately reflects the patient’s condition, whereas images taken at the periphery cannot precisely represent disease progression. However, all OCT images of macular holes used in this study were acquired using a radial scanning pattern, with ophthalmologists centering the scan on the center of the macular hole prior to measurement, thereby ensuring the accuracy of our method at the data level.

Limitations and future work

The minimum MH diameter measurement method in this study has demonstrated promising performance on both public datasets and clinical cases, providing an automated approach for the quantitative assessment of this parameter in clinical settings. However, several limitations warrant attention. First, the current scheme relies on a conventional sequential combination of edge detection and distance calculation, making it sensitive to image quality and potentially introducing measurement bias when image edges are indistinct. Second, the automated method currently focuses solely on a one-dimensional perspective and does not yet incorporate considerations at the two- or three-dimensional level. Third, the measurement results of our proposed method are currently presented in pixels rather than in µm, which are used in clinical practice. This limitation arises because the public dataset and clinical dataset we obtained do not include the corresponding OCT device scanning information, preventing pixel-to-µm conversion at this stage. This means that further refinement of our method is needed when considering its clinical applicability and generalizability.

Therefore, future work will explore the integration of image enhancement techniques to improve the method’s robustness, as well as attempt to combine image segmentation with three-dimensional reconstruction technology to expand the dimensionality of parameter quantification. Simultaneously, efforts will also be devoted to unit conversion of the measurement results to pave the way for clinical application.

Conclusions

To address the challenges faced by ophthalmologists in manually quantifying the minimum MH diameter, this study proposes an automated measurement method consisting of three steps. First, a tailored MH dataset is created through cropping to eliminate interference from non-MH regions. Second, an image processing pipeline is designed, including denoising, binarization, morphological operations, and edge detection, to extract the contours on both sides of the MH. Finally, an automated measurement algorithm is developed to locate the closest points between the bilateral contours and perform the diameter measurement. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness and potential value of the proposed method for measuring the minimum MH diameter, providing a reference for facilitating this clinical task in the future. Further work will aim to refine the proposed framework and expand the dimensionality of MH parameter quantification.
