Association Between the Modified Furosemide Response Index and Prognosis in Patients with Chronic Kidney Disease: A Retrospective Cohort Study Based on the MIMIC-IV Database
Authors: Zhibin Chen, Guobao Hong, Hua Xiao, Qiaobin Wu, Kai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe modified furosemide response index (mFRI), derived from a standardized furosemide stress test, is a biomarker of diuretic responsiveness and AKI progression. Its prognostic value in chronic kidney disease (CKD) remains unclear. This study examined the association between mFRI and clinical outcomes in critically ill patients with CKD.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC‑IV database. Critically ill CKD patients receiving furosemide were included. Restricted cubic splines were used to examine the non‑linear relationship between mFRI and mortality. Patients were grouped by mFRI quartiles. Multivariable Cox and logistic regression assessed outcome associations, with subgroup analyses for robustness.ResultsAmong 3,311 patients, a significant L‑shaped non‑linear association was observed between mFRI and 28‑day mortality (P < 0.001). Mortality risk declined sharply as mFRI increased to 0.143, then plateaued. Kaplan‑Meier analysis showed progressively lower mortality from the lowest (Q1) to the highest (Q4) mFRI quartile. Higher mFRI quartiles were associated with lower all‑cause mortality and reduced use of renal replacement therapy.ConclusionmFRI has a non‑linear, L‑shaped association with prognosis in critically ill patients with CKD. Because it is simple, low‑cost, and repeatable, mFRI could be a practical bedside tool for risk stratification in this population.

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major global public health concern with high morbidity and mortality.1 While conventional markers primarily reflect glomerular filtration, the degree of tubular dysfunction and loss of renal functional reserve are increasingly recognized as important outcome determinants.2,3 The furosemide stress test (FST) and its derived quantitative measure, the modified furosemide response index (mFRI), offer dynamic bedside tools for evaluating tubular functional capacity.4

Furosemide acts by specifically inhibiting the Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl− cotransporter in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle.5 In a standardized FST, a weight-based intravenous bolus is administered, and the 2-hour urine output is recorded. The mFRI is calculated as the 2-hour urine volume (mL) divided by the product of the furosemide dose (mg) and body weight (kg), yielding a normalized measure of diuretic responsiveness.4 Because this response requires adequate drug delivery to the tubular lumen and intact epithelial function, mFRI quantitatively captures residual tubular concentrating and excretory capability beyond creatinine or urine output alone.

In acute kidney injury (AKI), a low FST response accurately predicts AKI progression and the need for renal replacement therapy.6,7 However, the prognostic value of mFRI has rarely been examined in patients with preexisting CKD. Critically ill patients with CKD have variable tubulointerstitial fibrosis that may blunt diuretic responsiveness independently of hemodynamics, suggesting that mFRI may carry distinct prognostic information in this population. Given the possibility of a non-linear relationship with mortality, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to examine the association between mFRI and mortality in critically ill adults with CKD.

Method

Data Sources

This study used data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, which contains records of approximately 190,000 patients admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) from 2008 to 2019. The database provides comprehensive, de-identified information, including demographics, vital signs, laboratory and imaging results, diagnoses, medications, in-hospital survival, and post-discharge mortality obtained from the Social Security Administration Death Master File. As all data were rigorously de-identified to protect patient confidentiality, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Author KL was granted access to the MIMIC-IV database (ID: 54890087) after completing the mandatory online training and certification.

Study Population

Eligible patients were adults (aged ≥18 years) with a diagnosis of CKD. CKD was defined according to KDIGO 2012 criteria: either eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (using the MDRD equation) based on the lowest serum creatinine within 7 days prior to admission, or the presence of ICD codes for CKD (585.x, N18.x) recorded prior to or at admission. Diagnoses first coded during the hospital stay were not included. We excluded those who did not require ICU admission (n = 49,484), those who did not receive furosemide within 24 hours of ICU admission (n = 12,247), and those with missing body weight data (n = 69). For patients with multiple ICU or hospital admissions, only the first admission was retained (n = 763 subsequent admissions excluded). After these selections, the final analytical cohort comprised 3,311 patients (Figure 1). Data Collection

Data were extracted using Navicat Premium software with Structured Query Language (SQL). The collected variables included demographics (age, sex, height, weight, race [White, Black, Other], and admission type [emergency or elective]), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Charlson Comorbidity Index, and comorbidities (severe liver disease, malignancy, chronic pulmonary disease, myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, hypertension, and diabetes). Vital signs and laboratory parameters were averaged over the first 24 hours of ICU admission. Vital signs comprised temperature (°C), heart rate (bpm), respiratory rate (bpm), mean blood pressure (mmHg), oxygen saturation (%), and glucose (mg/dL). Laboratory parameters included white blood cell count (K/µL), hemoglobin (g/dL), platelet count (K/µL), bicarbonate (mEq/L), alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, blood urea nitrogen (mg/dL), sodium (mEq/L), calcium (mEq/L), potassium (mEq/L), chloride (mEq/L), anion gap (mmol/L), international normalized ratio, prothrombin time (seconds), partial thromboplastin time (seconds), creatinine (mg/dL), lactate (mmol/L), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), 24-hour fluid input within the first day of ICU admission (24h fluid input, mL) and route of furosemide administration. In addition, the use of invasive mechanical ventilation and renal replacement therapy, as well as the AKI stage at ICU admission, were recorded. AKI staging was determined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria, with baseline serum creatinine defined as the lowest value within 7 days prior to admission. If pre-admission creatinine levels were unavailable, the first measured creatinine upon admission was used as the baseline.4 AKI stage and comorbidities in the multivariate model were all obtained from data recorded at ICU admission. We calculated eGFR using the MDRD equation and then assigned CKD stages per KDIGO 2012 guidelines based on the eGFR at ICU admission: stage G1 (≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2), G2 (60–89), G3 (30–59), G4 (15–29), and G5 (<15).

In this study, the mFRI was calculated as the total urine output over 2 hours following the first intravenous furosemide administration within 24 hours of ICU admission, divided by the furosemide dose and body weight (mL/[mg·kg]/2 h). For patients receiving oral furosemide, the dose was converted by multiplying by a factor of 1/2 to account for reduced bioavailability.8

Grouping Strategy and Outcome Measures

To investigate the association between mFRI and clinical outcomes in patients with CKD, participants were stratified into four groups based on mFRI values: Quartile 1 (mFRI ≤ 0.038), Quartile 2 (0.038 < mFRI ≤ 0.143), Quartile 3 (0.143 < mFRI ≤ 0.368), and Quartile 4 (mFRI > 0.368). The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included 3-month mortality, 1-year mortality, in-ICU mortality, in-hospital mortality, the incidence of a decrease in eGFR to <75% of baseline,9 and the use of renal replacement therapy (RRT).

Statistical Analysis

In this study, categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, and intergroup differences were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were described using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), and non-parametric Mann–Whitney U-tests were employed to evaluate differences between groups.

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three knots were used to explore and visualize potential nonlinear associations between the continuous variable mFRI and 28-day mortality, and to identify thresholds or inflection points. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed to compare the cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality across the four mFRI groups, and differences were assessed using the Log rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all-cause mortality. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Variables with a P-value < 0.1 in univariate Cox regression analyses were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Based on clinical expertise, prior literature, and statistical significance, the final multivariable model included the following covariates: gender, age, race, weight, admission type, SAPS II score, Charlson Comorbidity Index, AKI stage, sepsis, hypertension, mean blood pressure, white blood cell, hemoglobin, lactate, pH, sodium, potassium, partial thromboplastin time, blood urea nitrogen, 24h fluid input, and eGFR (Table S1).

To investigate potential heterogeneity in the association between mFRI and clinical outcomes among patients with CKD and to assess the robustness of our findings, subgroup analyses were conducted according to several key clinical characteristics, including gender (male vs female), age (>65 vs ≤65 years), race (White vs non-White), SAPS II score (>35 vs ≤35) and Route of furosemide administration (Intravenous vs Oral).

Missing data are common in the MIMIC database. To address this, multiple imputation was performed using the “mice” package in R software. To minimize potential bias from excessive imputation, variables with more than 20% missing values were excluded from the final models (Table S2). Furthermore, to ensure model stability, multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF); a VIF < 5 was considered indicative of acceptable collinearity (Table S3). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Result

Baseline Characteristics

Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics across mFRI quartiles. AKI stage distribution differed significantly (P < 0.001). Q1 had the highest proportion of stage 3 AKI, while Q4 was dominated by stage 0 or stage 1. In terms of CKD severity, Q1 patients had more advanced disease (stages 4 and 5), with the lowest eGFR and highest BUN. In contrast, Q4 patients were predominantly stage 3 and had better renal function. Q1 also showed higher rates of heart failure, diabetes, and sepsis. These patterns indicate that lower mFRI is associated with greater comorbidity burden and more severe renal impairment, likely contributing to the higher mortality observed in this group. Additionally, we extracted the baseline characteristics of CKD patients who did not receive any diuretic within 24 hours of ICU admission (Table S4). Discussion

This study is the first to systematically investigate the complex association between the mFRI and mortality risk in patients with CKD. We identified a significant L-shaped nonlinear relationship between mFRI and mortality, with an inflection point at approximately 0.143 based on the restricted cubic spline curves. Below this threshold, mortality risk declined sharply as mFRI increased, suggesting that even modest improvements in diuretic responsiveness among poor responders are associated with substantial survival benefits. Above 0.143, further increases in mFRI were linked to only a gradual reduction in mortality, indicating a plateau effect (Table S5 and Table S6). This pattern was consistent across 28-day to 1-year mortality and remained significant after multivariable adjustment. These findings support mFRI as a potential biomarker for risk stratification in critically ill CKD patients.

Furosemide, a potent loop diuretic, is widely used in patients with CKD to manage fluid retention and hypertension. It exerts its effect by inhibiting the Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl− cotransporter in the renal tubules, thereby promoting diuresis.10 Impaired tubular function in patients with CKD is typically associated with a diminished diuretic response to furosemide.11 The furosemide stress test (FST) has been proposed as a reliable predictor of progression to severe AKI or the need for renal replacement therapy (RRT) in the early stages of AKI.6,12,13 Yet concerns about potential adverse effects, such as hypovolemia, have limited its widespread use in critically ill patients.7,14 To address this limitation, a simplified alternative, the mFRI, has been proposed and validated for predicting progression to severe AKI.4,15 In the present study, we further found that mFRI may also be significantly associated with mortality in patients with CKD.

The mFRI reflects the diuretic efficiency per unit dose of furosemide. The observed L-shaped association may arise from dual pathophysiological mechanisms: an mFRI < 0.143 indicates severe tubular dysfunction and diuretic resistance, which is consistent with previous studies linking diuretic resistance to adverse outcomes in AKI.14,16 In contrast, when mFRI exceeded 0.143, the mortality reduction slope became markedly less steep. While a slight downward visual trend persisted beyond this value, the additional reduction was no longer statistically significant, indicating a plateau effect.16–18 This pattern was consistent across 28-day, 3-month, and 1-year mortality.

The mFRI serves as an effective tool for assessing mortality risk in patients with CKD. This study identifies 0.143 as a threshold to identify high-risk patients (mFRI < 0.143) who may benefit from more aggressive renal support. No evidence of harm was observed at higher mFRI values.19 The association remained robust across subgroups defined by demographic characteristics, route of furosemide administration, and disease severity. Furthermore, we observed a weak association between mFRI and the incidence of a decrease in eGFR to <75% of baseline—a key endpoint of renal function deterioration. This may be because rapid eGFR decline is more closely linked to glomerular structural injury, inflammation, or fibrotic processes,20–23 whereas mFRI primarily reflects tubulointerstitial function and the drainage capacity of the collecting system.11,24–26

This study employed restricted cubic splines to elucidate the nonlinear association and utilized the MIMIC-IV database to ensure the real-world generalizability of the findings. Rigorous exclusion criteria were applied to minimize confounding bias. The mFRI offers several advantages, including simplicity and high clinical feasibility. It can be assessed without reliance on laboratory tests and supports dynamic monitoring, enabling the capture of real-time changes in a patient’s response to diuretic therapy. Compared to serum creatinine or eGFR, mFRI more accurately reflects tubular function and the responsiveness of the collecting system to pharmacological stimulation. Furthermore, the data required for mFRI calculation are routinely documented in clinical records, making it a low-cost and readily accessible metric.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. In addition, the use of the first available admission creatinine as baseline when pre-admission values were missing may have led to misclassification of AKI stage and CKD severity in some patients, which could affect the precision of our risk estimates. Although we adjusted for multiple covariates, residual confounding likely remains. Second, the calculation of mFRI may be influenced by fluid management and concomitant medications. In our cohort, furosemide was prescribed at the clinician’s discretion, so indications and dosing were not standardized. This may reduce the discriminative ability of mFRI. Future studies should consider a prespecified dosing protocol. Third, confounding by indication cannot be excluded: furosemide is prescribed non-randomly in response to clinical states such as fluid overload or oliguria, which are themselves associated with mortality. Fourth, the cohort was restricted to critically ill patients with CKD who received furosemide, which limits generalizability.

Conclusion

The mFRI is a novel predictor of mortality in critically ill patients with CKD. Its L-shaped relationship with mortality offers a reference for individualizing diuretic therapy. Patients with an mFRI below 0.143 should be recognized as a high-risk subgroup warranting closer monitoring. Nevertheless, before clinical implementation, external validation in diverse populations and healthcare settings is needed, together with prospective evaluation of whether mFRI-guided therapeutic adjustments improve clinical outcomes.
