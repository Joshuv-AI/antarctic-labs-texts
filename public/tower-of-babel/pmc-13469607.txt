Early fusion of laser and acoustic features for human orientation detection in non-line-of-sight environments
Authors: Ferdi Doğan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFor search and rescue, security, and autonomous systems, determining the orientation of a human presence outside the line of sight is as crucial as detecting them. Knowing the human’s orientation plays a crucial role in determining the intervention strategy. In this study, data were obtained from scenarios involving human orientation outside the line of sight in a set of experimental settings. The original dataset was created using laser and acoustic chirp signals for the scenarios created to detect human orientation (behind, front, left, right). Data obtained from multiple sensors were correlated at the feature level using early fusion. A dataset with 42 features was created by combining two sensor sources, each with 21 features. Human orientation classification yielded strong performance under the adopted controlled experimental conditions using machine learning methods, artificial intelligence methods, and the proposed LAO-Net (Laser–Acoustic Orientation) model. Furthermore, explainable artificial intelligence analysis revealed the features that influence human orientation detection. The results of the study indicate that the fused laser-acoustic representation achieves strong human orientation detection performance under the experimental conditions evaluated. This study contributes to the limited literature on detecting human orientation in environments outside the line of sight.Keywords: Laser–acoustic data fusion, Human orientation detection, Artificial neural networks, Non-line-of-sight detection, Explainable artificial intelligenceSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Detecting people or objects in a non-line-of-sight (NLOS) environment presents various challenges. Obtaining information about environments outside the sensor’s field of view is crucial today, especially in buildings and post-disaster debris. Line of sight often contains numerous obstacles, such as walls, columns, concrete blocks, and metal surfaces. These obstacles alter the propagation characteristics of the signals for sensors to detect, weaken the signal, and create scattering and reflection problems. Therefore, obtaining information about people or objects in NLOS environments becomes difficult.

Attempts to identify targets behind obstacles and to access details such as their location, posture and orientation are also gaining importance. Determining the orientation a person is facing in an NLOS environment is particularly important for providing accurate intervention to a victim trapped under debris. In indoor security scenarios, knowing human orientation is also crucial for autonomous driving systems.

This study focuses on estimating the orientation of a person behind an obstacle in an NLOS environment (behind, front, left, right) using data fusion of laser and acoustic signals. Features were generated from laser-based time-of-flight measurements and acoustic chirp signals. These features were combined using early fusion and estimation using Machine Learning (ML) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) models. It aims to contribute to the limited body of research in this field.

NLOS detection and application areas

NLOS detection attempts to obtain information about people or objects in an area that cannot be directly observed through indirect reflections. It focuses on obtaining information about the object’s presence and location. Various sensors are used to detect people or objects not in line of sight. These include different devices-sensors such as laser, acoustic, radar-microwave, Wi-Fi, and infrared1. Because each sensor has different characteristics when obtaining information about the environment, it provides a variety of information. This information is also provided by data on propagation properties and noise. In recent years, ToF (Time-of-Flight), LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging), and single-photon detector technologies have led to an increase in interest in interpreting information about scenes behind obstacles. They are used to interpret information obtained from measurements of scenes behind obstacles2. An example NLOS environment is shown in Fig. 1.

An example of an NLOS environment.

NLOS detection plays a role in defense systems, search and rescue operations, indoor security applications, autonomous driving vehicles, and robotic applications2–6. Detecting people or objects is valuable in scenarios where line of sight is limited or lost. This increases the need for reliable, fast, and portable detection systems for human or object information in NLOS environments.

Human detection in NLOS environments: current status and challenges

Obstacles in an NLOS environment block signal paths, making it difficult to obtain information about human presence. Various technologies and carrier sensors have been developed to overcome these challenges. One of these technologies is UWB (Ultra Wide Band) technology. It has a wide frequency range from MHz to GHz. In an NLOS environment, it generates short-duration pulse signals in these frequency ranges. It analyzes the reflection and refraction properties of the signals emitted into the environment. It aims to identify moving or stationary human presence in the environment. UWB receivers can detect small fluctuations such as breathing and movement. This demonstrates their effectiveness in extracting information about human presence7. In an NLOS environment, information about arrival times can be compared by measuring time differences. By achieving high time resolution, the path and reflected paths are distinguished. In recent years, deep learning methods have also been used to detect human presence from UWB signals8.

Radar and LiDAR systems are among the methods used for human detection in NLOS environments. Radar technology produces high-frequency electromagnetic signals. The signals bounce off obstacles in the environment. Values such as the arrival time and intensity of the echo signals are calculated. These values reveal information about the human being. The human body always reflects and absorbs radio waves. Breathing and heartbeat cause changes in radar signals. In this way, information about the human presence is obtained9. The arrival angle and temporal differences of the radar signals provide information about the approximate location of the target. LiDAR, on the other hand, sends laser beams and measures the ToF information of the light reflected from the beams. Changes in the reflected signal provide information about the human, while the flight time and beam reflection data provide approximate values about the location10.

One of the systems used for human detection is GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite System) and RTK-GNSS systems. These systems are satellite-based positioning systems. These systems are used to determine human location information rather than detecting humans. GNSS signals are sent via satellite. The signals reaching the receiver reach the receiver indirectly, which causes the signal to give an incorrect location. RTK-GNSS (Real-Time Kinematic) increases the accuracy of GNSS signals. It compares the phase measurements between the reference station and the mobile sensor. This allows the human location information to be given with very low error11.

Problem definition and motivation: human orientation detection in NLOS environments

There are various studies on human presence. Human detection is a critical topic, particularly for search and rescue, autonomous driving, and localization. Furthermore, knowing a person’s posture after detection is also crucial for complex scenarios. Determining which orientation a person is facing after detection is a particularly important research topic for search and rescue teams. The original problem addressed in this study involves estimating human orientation (behind, in front, to the left, to the right) in an environment without a line of sight. The focus is on classifying the orientation a person is looking using laser and audio signals, independent of the person’s posture (standing, sitting, crouching).

Determining a person’s orientation in an environment without a line of sight is a challenging process. Reflective obstacles (walls, aluminum, etc.), the shape of the environment, multiple reflections, and ambient noise can cause distortions. Orientation information can be directly or indirectly manipulated by these obstacles. Various characteristics of laser and acoustic signals—such as amplitude, time delay, reflection distribution, and frequency content—may not provide sufficient environmental information. Classical thresholding and statistical methods fall short in such situations. It is necessary to extract features from these characteristics and employ machine learning methods.

Considering the studies in the literature, it appears that they focus on human presence. A few rare studies have focused on estimating the direction of moving people or objects25. It appears that most current studies rely on single-sensor configurations, which may limit the amount of available supplementary information for orientation estimation.

In this study, a new dataset was created using early fusion methods using different features of laser and acoustic chirp signals. ML/AI methods were used to determine human orientation from this dataset. This study aims to contribute to this limited area of the literature.

In many scenarios where there is no line of sight, detecting human presence may not be sufficient for effective decision-making mechanisms. Obtaining human orientation information can provide additional benefits for various application scenarios. Based on this need, this study investigates whether combining laser and acoustic signals at the feature level to enrich information in the hidden scene can support human orientation classification in line-of-sight-restricted environments.

Data fusion and artificial intelligence perspective

Human detection in NLOS environments has generally been performed based on a single-sensor approach. The sensitivity of material properties in the environment often negatively affects the performance of a single sensor. Radar and IR-UWB-based systems can be examples of this. These sensors depend on the properties of the obstacle material and the geometric shape of the environment. Wall thickness, humidity, and the presence of metallic surfaces can cause signal reflections to increase or decrease. Furthermore, systems using only thermal-IR cameras are sensitive to factors such as ambient temperature, sunlight, and airflow. These types of sensors lose reliability in environments such as under debris, smoke, or dust. Single acoustic signals are negatively affected by background noise and echo reflections. Laser-only systems, on the other hand, can provide unstable results due to factors such as low SNR, photon statistical information, and narrow field of view. Despite the strengths of these sensors, they are not sufficient when used alone in complex environments10,26.

While studies using data from single sensors are common, studies using sensors with different features together are gaining increasing importance. There are studies that perform decision-level data fusion using different sensors, such as radar-thermal, radar-IR, and gas-acoustic, together. Each of these sensors produces a decision. The generated decisions are combined with specific logical rules or voting schemes. This late fusion process is easy to design and interpret. However, it cannot provide rich information at the feature level. Therefore, it does not provide the flexibility to suppress inconsistent or weak signals. This may cause decision-level fusion of signals to be insufficient to achieve the desired separation when determining human orientation in an NLOS environment27.

Evaluating signal features using machine learning methods using early fusion methods can be a powerful tool. Early-level fusion of features from different sensors prevents the loss of sensor-specific features. This allows for the creation of a common feature pool. The resulting early fusion data makes it possible to access target information using ML/AI methods. It retains complementary information such as distance and time delay patterns in laser signals, frequency information, reflections, and energy distribution in acoustic signals. This information represents detailed information for determining human orientation in an NLOS environment. Studies on signs of life in an NLOS environment have been conducted in the literature using CNN, LSTM, and various machine learning methods. Some of these studies were conducted on single sensors. In a limited number of fusion processes, decision-level fusion was also used. While studies at the early fusion level are limited, they are mostly focused on human presence or location information. In light of this information, the contributions of this study are outlined in the next section28,29.

Studies on human detection

Radar, infrared, and acoustic sensors have physical properties and operational limitations. This has not yet addressed the specific problem of accurate target detection and localization under NLOS conditions36. However, existing studies have been conducted using these technologies under various environmental conditions. These studies compare the technologies used, their accuracies, and limitations, and provide information about the gaps created37,38. Studies offer different perspectives on accurate detection under NLOS conditions. While some studies advocate optimal sensor combinations and fusion strategies, others advocate passive infrared and acoustic sensing27,39. The lack of a standard performance metric and dataset in most of these studies further complicates the process40. Studies show that infrared provides robust information based on light variations. Acoustic sensors, on the other hand, are reported to capture useful audio data for detection and localization in occluded environments27,41.

Human detection in NLOS environments using laser signals has been studied in post-disaster search and rescue operations, military applications, and autonomous systems. There are two types of NLOS-based laser systems: optical methods (ToF, LIDAR, interferometry) and electromagnetic approaches (radar/RF). Optical methods provide high-resolution spatial information. Radar/RF focuses on wall penetration and vital signs detection. Single-Photon Avalanche Diode (SPAD) and commercial LIDAR have a millisecond response time. Information is obtained from the histogram of the scattered light time and photon count42. This process progresses as the laser spot, the interface, and the hidden target, and photons are counted as the hidden target, the interface, and the detector43. Development studies with data fusion in NLOS environments.

Studies have shown that multi-sensor and multi-attribute fusion in NLOS environments increases accuracy and stability. Sensors such as LiDAR, RADAR, optical, and acoustic sensors appear to have a complementary effect. Furthermore, machine learning, artificial intelligence, and deep learning techniques are emphasized for improving accuracy. However, due to the lack of datasets and limitations of real-time applications, these studies are not widely used.

Furthermore, a study has demonstrated that by detecting falls, recognizing daily activities, and identifying posture, it can provide useful insights for existing research74. Another study noted that spatiotemporal feature fusion could contribute to distinguishing a person’s state in fall detection75. In a separate study, sensor-based systems utilizing wearable technologies were employed. Here, an approach was proposed for applications capable of detecting daily body activities and issuing fall alerts76. Studies on human posture recognition have indicated that feature representations can be used to distinguish between different human bodies77. These studies are generally conducted in line-of-sight, vision-based, or wearable-sensor settings rather than in NLOS laser–acoustic environments. These studies support the broader premise that human orientation- and posture-related information can be learned from feature representations. Consequently, this further motivates our efforts to investigate laser-acoustic data fusion features for human orientation in NLOS environments.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup

A two-room laboratory environment, with sound and light insulation, was designed for the system’s installation. The room measures approximately 2.6 × 3 m. The ceiling of the room is covered with furniture material. Acoustic insulation is provided using pyramidal foam. A barrier was constructed to create a 1.6 × 2 m partition inside the room. The interior surfaces of the hidden stage are covered with pyramidal foam. A pleated blackout curtain is used to suppress laser signal reflections and absorb ambient light. The resulting room setup is shown in Fig. 2.

Experimental room setup where data acquisition was carried out.

An acoustic audio signal system was installed to capture audio data. The system utilizes a mobile platform with a 1-m horizontal stroke. Mounted on the platform are eight microphones and an eight-speaker array. Microphones are evenly spaced on the left side of the system, and speakers are evenly spaced on the right. The microphones and speakers are spaced 8.8 cm apart. This allows for horizontal and vertical scanning.

The ECM8000 was used as a sound measurement microphone. This equipment ensures equal detection of sound from all directions and provides excellent performance in the 15–20 kHz range. The sound systems utilize an S-S10 speaker component, which provides high-frequency reproduction. The ADA8200 (8 × 8 preamp) was used as the interface for the microphones. This ensures high-quality recording in this multi-microphone system. The preamp-sound card connection is made via fiber optic. The UMC1820 was used as the USB sound card, which provides high-quality input and output. The MX 8200 (8-channel amplifier) was used to ensure the correct power output for each speaker in the system. In the laser system, the laser beam was transferred to the galvo mirrors via a Polarizing Beam Splitter (PBS). This beam was directed to the reflective relay surface. The returning photons were then accepted back into the system and detected with a time stamp by a Single-Photon Avalanche Diode (SPAD) camera. Photon counting was performed and collected using the Time-Correlated Single-Photon Counting (TCSPC) module. The temporal profile of the signal was generated by histogramming the arrival times of the returned photons. The Time-to-Digital Converter (TDC) combination achieved nanosecond/millisecond precision. Data preprocessing

The audio and laser files were combined and saved under the same name. Laser data is in csv file format, while acoustic chirp signals are in wav file format. Laser data contains 16,220 rows of photon counts. Each row in the laser data indicates the number of photons arriving at 1 ms intervals. The audio files consist of an 8 × 2048 matrix. The raw SPAD-TCSPC laser data were normalized by subtracting the white noise collected before the experiment began. White noise is data collected from a room outside the field of view, indicating that the room was empty. Similarly, the audio data was subtracted from the white noise to form the audio data used in the study.

Laser data preprocessing process:

To capture local temporal variations in the photon count sequence, the entire laser recording was divided into 36 windows rather than being represented as a single data set. Each laser matrix has dimensions of 1 × 16,220. In the study, windows of 24, 36, 48, and 64 samples were generated, but because 36 provided the best results, it was decided to use 36 windows. The portion that was not fully divided was discarded. Each window was labeled to indicate the subject’s stance. However, multiple windows were obtained from the same scan, and these window-level samples may therefore retain some dependence. Accordingly, the reported findings should be interpreted within the adopted window-based evaluation framework rather than as results derived from a set of fully independent observations. The temporal graph of the laser data and the process of dividing this graph into windows are shown in Fig. 6.

The audio data, consisting of an 8 × 2048 matrix, contains acoustic chirp signals from eight microphones. The average of the eight microphones in this audio signal was converted into a 1 × 2048 matrix. Each audio file was labeled according to its orientation. Data fusion

Fusion is a method used to obtain more reliable results by combining data from different devices. The data fusion method employed in this study aimed to obtain more reliable information by combining information from both laser and acoustic chirp signals. Directly combining raw signals and scaling them synchronously for NLOS is quite challenging. Therefore, the signals from each data source were combined after extracting their features. This feature vector was then merged into a single vector. After the laser data was divided into 36-sample windows, 21 features were extracted, while the acoustic chirp signal was extracted from the 2048 × 1 vector. These two 21-dimensional feature vectors were combined into a single 42 × 1 vector. A classification label was then added to this vector, creating a 43 × 1 vector. Models used in the study

In this study, data obtained through early fusion was trained and tested with various ML/AI models. Class information was added to the laser and acoustic chirp signal features obtained through data fusion to create a data set with 43 × 1 vectors. The resulting data set was normalized with a z-score before model training. The models used in the study are presented in groups.

Decision Trees: Classification is a widely used method in prediction processes. Data are divided into groups by branching according to their attributes. Data grouped by branches and leaves are classified. There are three types of trees based on their branches and leaves: fine tree, medium tree, and coarse tree. Fine Tree: It consists of many branches and nodes. It tries to find complex patterns in the data78. Since this model can perceive noise in the data as a pattern, it can cause overfitting in small data sets79. Medium Tree: It has a reasonable number of splits and complexities. It is generally preferred in applications where interpretation is important and some complexity is normal80. Therefore, it cannot capture all the nuances. Therefore, it performs less accurately than fine trees81. Coarse Tree: It has the least splits and branches among decision trees. It is more suitable for small data sets. It oversimplifies the data. Therefore, it may miss patterns. Its accuracy rate is lower than other decision trees82.

Naive Bayes: In cases where conditional independence exists, it performs inference based on class probabilities. The training and testing process is quite fast. Performance degrades when the condition is violated. It can be used in applications where resource requirements are low. However, its performance is relatively lower than other models. There are two types: Gaussian Naive Bayes and Kernel Naive Bayes. Gaussian Naive Bayes assumes that the distribution of features is normal. It is effective for continuous data83. Kernel Naive Bayes, on the other hand, uses kernel functions. It handles nonlinear data distributions better than the Gaussian Naive Bayes method84.

Support vector machines (SVM) It is a powerful supervised learning method for regression and classification. It is capable of processing high-dimensional data. It allows for the separation of nonlinearly separable data. It includes optimization methods such as gradient descent. Linear SVM performs well on linear separation of data85. It cannot capture nonlinear relationships. Quadratic SVM, on the other hand, captures and classifies moderately nonlinear data. The polynomial kernel degree is 2. The probability of overfitting is higher. Cubic SVM has a polynomial kernel degree of 3. It has medium to high complexity. It is more successful in capturing nonlinear patterns. Its computational cost is higher than other SVMs. It is prone to overfitting. Fine Gaussian SVM: The kernel scale parameter is kept low. It has high flexibility. It can capture boundary details in the data well. It can distinguish boundaries with complex and small details. It can overfit when data is scarce. It has high computational cost. Medium Gaussian SVM: The kernel scale parameter is medium. It has medium flexibility. It is shown to be more stable and has a moderate computational cost. It is insufficient for structures containing very fine details. The kernel scale for Coarse Gaussian SVM is kept large. The degree of flexibility is low to moderate. It operates more stably in applications that are not noisy and do not contain excessive detail. The risk of overfitting is low86,87.

K-nearest neighbors-KNN It is one of the most fundamental algorithms used in classification and regression problems. It is a nonparametric method based on edge neighborhoods. It produces quite good results on a dataset with good features. Fine KNN has local decision limitations. The K value is set close to 1. Its ability to capture detail is low and it is sensitive to noise. Medium KNN uses a moderate K value. It is more balanced between detail and balancing. It can overfit at high K values. Coarse KNN uses a high K value. It has better global trends. The noise effect is less. The overall classification trend is more stable. It misses small details. However, its computational cost is lower than others88 Cosine KNN uses distance according to the similarity metric. It looks at the angle difference between two vectors and approaches based on their similarity. It works well on high-dimensional data and is not much affected by feature scale differences. Cubic KNN, on the other hand, establishes a connection using a specific number of neighbors and cubic distance. Calculation and interpretation are difficult. In Weighted KNN, weights are assigned based on the distance between neighbors. As the influence of nearby neighbors increases, the precision of the decision boundary increases. This reduces the detrimental effect of distant neighbors. The calculation is more complex. Distance normalization is important in this model89.

Kernel-based extensions Linear models are transformed into nonlinear models by expanding the feature space. They exhibit strong performance when properly scaled. The SVM kernel transforms nonlinear data into a higher-dimensional space, making it separable. The kernel trick used in this method performs classification without transforming it into a higher-dimensional space. It transforms the data to linearize the decision class. In the logistic regression kernel method, the data is transformed using the kernel function. Nonlinear boundaries are created90,91.

Ensemble methods These are machine learning techniques that combine multiple methods. The aim is to create a more robust, stable, and generalizable prediction model. The primary goal in such methods is to extract strong features by combining weak features. Overfitting and bias-variance tradeoffs are used to balance the challenges of the tradeoff. Boosted Trees sequentially train weak trees, correcting the errors of the previous tree. AdaBoost and Gradient Boosting are the most common. They achieve high accuracy on data with a deep tree structure92. Bagged Trees are also known as Bootstrap Aggregated Trees. They allow trees to be trained with random samples within the tree structure, generally reducing variance in unstable models93. Subspace Discriminant applies the discriminant analysis model to feature subspaces, thus generating multiple models. Each model is trained with a different feature. Its performance is good on high-dimensional datasets94. Subspace KNN trains a KNN model on randomly selected subsets. Each KNN model is trained with its own subspace, and the results are evaluated collectively. It is resistant to noise. It performs better on large datasets95. RUSBoosted Trees are mostly used on imbalanced datasets. It aims to improve learning in classes with small sample sizes. It randomly deletes samples from classes with large sample sizes and applies boosting96.

Artificial neural networks They exhibit quite good performance in solving nonlinear problems. They have a layered structure. They consist of an input layer, a hidden layer, and an output layer. Narrow Neural Network has a small number of neurons in a single hidden layer. It requires fewer computational resources. It is more suitable for applications with limited data and insufficient resources97. Medium Neural Networks are medium-sized neural networks. They are ideal for applications that require a balanced complexity and computational power98. Wide Neural Networks have a large-scale neural network structure. They contain a large number of neurons. They have effective learning and generalization properties. They perform better than other networks99. Bilayered Neural Networks have a structure consisting of two hidden layers. This type of networks has two hidden layers. The two hidden layers provide higher performance by enabling better feature learning. As the number of hidden layers increases, the computational cost also increases100. Trilayered Neural Networks have a three-layer structure. They have three hidden layers. They are quite successful in solving nonlinear problems. Their computational cost and complexity are high101.

Proposed model LAO-Net (laser–acoustic orientation network)

A feedforward artificial neural-based multi-class classification model was designed to detect human orientation in a dataset consisting of 21 laser and 21 audio features. This model consists of three main steps: input data, a hidden layer, and an output layer. The input layer contains 42 features consisting of laser and acoustic chirp signals, while the hidden layer contains an artificial neural network model consisting of 30 neurons, and the output layer contains output information regarding human orientation. The primary reason for choosing a feedforward network in this study is the greater importance of simultaneously processing human information outside the field of view. Furthermore, under the evaluated experimental setup, this model achieved higher performance than the other tested models. The LAO-Net diagram is shown in Fig. 10.

Layered structure of LAO-Net.

Figure 10 shows the input layer of LAO-Net containing 42 features, while the output layer is intended to predict human orientation. The LM (Levenberg–Marquardt) method was used to optimize this neural network. The LM algorithm is better suited for shallow feedforward networks with a relatively small number of trainable parameters. It has been preferred due to its fast convergence. The use of a single hidden layer with 30 neurons enables the model to learn nonlinear relationships in the combined feature space. Therefore, it aims to maintain a compact architecture. Additionally, LM’s computational and memory requirements may be less suitable for larger datasets and deeper architectures. Twenty percent of the data was set aside as an independent test set, while the remaining 80% was used for model development with fivefold cross-validation. Training was conducted over 1,000 epochs. Evaluation metrics

Twenty percent of the dataset was set aside as an independent test set. The remaining 80% was used for model development with fivefold cross-validation. Each fold served once as validation data while the remaining folds were used for training. The validation results are presented as the average across the five folds, whereas the final test performance was derived from the independent 20% test set. Global normalization was not applied to the entire dataset. Z-score normalization was applied separately within each cross-validation fold. The normalization parameters were calculated using only the training set of each fold. They were then applied to the corresponding validation and test sets. This was done to prevent data leakage. Several evaluation metrics were used to assess the performance of the training and testing processes of the models used in the study. Correlation and PCA-based feature analysis

To examine the fused 21 + 21 feature set derived from laser and acoustic data in greater detail, pairwise correlation analysis and PCA (Principal Component Analysis) were performed. The extracted feature set includes statistical, temporal, amplitude-based, and spectral descriptors. Therefore, there may be redundancy among these descriptors. Therefore, a correlation matrix was used to determine the bivariate relationships between the features. PCA-based variance analysis was used to see how the total variance of these features is distributed among the principal components. The analyses were performed to provide clear information regarding the redundancy or complementarity of the features.

The correlation matrix shown in Fig. 15 reveals moderate to high levels of correlation between some of the laser and acoustic properties. This indicates a strong correlation among them. The various features used for each data source in this study were designed to characterize similar aspects of the signal, such as amplitude variation, energy content, and distributional characteristics. At the same time, the correlation structure indicates that not all features are strongly correlated. This suggests that the fused feature set also preserves complementary information from different descriptor groups. Therefore, redundancies are observed among the features. However, the feature set appears to capture multiple distinct characteristics of the laser-acoustic signals. For visual clarity, the strongest relationships in Fig. 15 are interpreted at the group level rather than on a feature-by-feature basis.

Pairwise correlation matrix of the extracted fused features used in the classification task.

The distribution of variance across the principal components indicates how the feature space should be represented. The variance is not concentrated in one or a few components. Discussion

The data obtained in this study was obtained using an experimental environment. Efforts were made to replicate the real-world environment. This study aimed to detect a person’s orientation in a NLOS environment. Laser and acoustic chirp signal data sources were used for detection. Data from both sources were then extracted, and these features were combined into a single dataset using data fusion. This created a fused dataset for detecting human orientation in NLOS environments. It appears that the characteristics of both data sources contribute to the observed model performance.

The acoustic linear chirp signal’s broadband energy impinges on walls and objects outside the field of view, creating a secondary reflection. This secondary reflection provides a distinctive feature. MFCC/STFT-based features capture statistical differences. They provide information regarding human presence (presence/absence) and human orientation. In addition to this data source, the laser has been shown to produce supporting data. Low photon fluctuations resulting from wall reflections of the signal sent from the laser source pose a problem. The efficiency of the laser data was increased by preprocessing the laser data obtained from the experimental environment, i.e., the white noise, obtained from an empty environment, by subtracting it from the real data. Laser and acoustic chirp data are considered complementary features in the experimental setup. In this study, the combined laser-acoustic representation achieved strong performance for human orientation classification. However, since this study does not include a controlled comparison between laser-only, acoustic-only, and fused representations under the same evaluation protocol, the specific incremental contribution of feature fusion cannot be quantified directly and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Literature searches have focused on human presence/absence detection in NLOS environments. These studies generally emphasize success rates exceeding 90%. This study focused on the more specific task of orientation classification and achieved 99.98% accuracy under the experimental conditions evaluated. The results should not be interpreted as direct proof of generalizability to more complex real-world NLOS environments. Specifically, the current findings were obtained under a controlled setup involving a single reflective wall, limited environmental variability, and a small pool of participants. This limits outdoor validity. In addition, because multiple window-level samples were derived from the same original laser scan, the reported results should not be interpreted as if they were obtained from a set of fully independent observations.

An examination of the results reveals that different features are effective in detecting the orientation of people outside the models’ field of view. Some features are more effective in detecting people with their behind turned, while others are more effective when people are facing forward. This finding suggests that the dataset created by combining laser and audio features contains useful information for orientation determination. These results suggest that the early fusion process may have contributed positively to the observed classification performance.

Correlation matrix and PCA-based analyses reveal the relationships among certain variables. Furthermore, they have demonstrated that the fusion feature set preserves complementary information across multiple dimensions.

The very high accuracies (> 99%) obtained by LAO-Net and some ML models raise the possibility of overfitting. The lack of sensor-related data variation and recording the dataset under similar conditions reinforce the possibility of overfitting. However, the results of different models indicate that performance may be low. Although fivefold cross-validation and an independent test split were used, these procedures do not fully eliminate the limitations arising from the controlled setup, the limited number of subjects, and the absence of subject-independent evaluation. This evaluation framework helps provide more stable performance predictions. Therefore, further validation under stricter, subject-independent protocols is still necessary. Furthermore, the results show that the data fusion performs well. The accuracy difference between training and validation is quite low. This small difference may indicate a limited distinction between training and validation performance. However, a more extensive validation is still required to assess its generalizability. Similarly, the closeness of the F1 scores between the training and validation sets may indicate a limited tendency toward overfitting in the current dataset.

It is believed that the high matrix complexity performance may be due to the geometry of the controlled experimental environment created in this study. The fixed positions of the wall, the participant, the laser source, and the acoustic setup may have produced a dependent signal pattern. However, these results should be interpreted within the context of the controlled environment used in this study, and further validation is required in more complex real-world NLOS environments.

Limitations and future work

Despite the success of the study, it also has some limitations. The dataset was obtained from eight subjects and conducted in a controlled laboratory environment. This may limit the ability to generalize to the acoustic reflections of people with different physical characteristics. Furthermore, although the data obtained from the devices installed in the experimental environment is reliable, re-acquiring data with different devices may cause performance degradation due to calibration differences. Using different reflective surfaces in the experimental environment, rather than the wall designated as the reflective surface, could affect performance and stability. To overcome this, future studies will attempt to create datasets that include different reflective surfaces.

One methodological limitation of the study concerns the use of multiple 36-sample windows derived from the same laser scan. To capture local temporal variations, multiple 36-sample segments were obtained from the same 16,220-line laser recording. Although this strategy increased the level of detail in feature extraction, segments derived from the same scan may retain some correlation. Therefore, rather than being interpreted as a set of completely independent observations for the dataset, it should be understood as a window-based representation of the recorded signals. This limitation necessitates future studies to investigate record-level and subject-independent validation strategies to better assess robustness under stricter independence assumptions.

The study did not employ a LOSO (leave-one-subject-out) method. This is considered another limitation of the study. The dataset comprises the complete set of records collected within a fixed experimental setup. The dataset was treated as a whole. Training and test data were randomly selected from this dataset. A subject-independent validation process involving a larger dataset and a broader pool of participants represents an important direction for future studies.

The experimental setup utilized a single reflective wall under controlled acoustic conditions. Real-world off-line-of-sight environments may include heterogeneous wall materials, varying wall thicknesses, cluttered furniture, and ambient noise. A single reflective surface can influence signal behavior and classification performance. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted within the controlled laboratory environment adopted here. Future studies plan to incorporate multiple reflective surfaces.

Another limitation of this study is the absence of a controlled comparison between laser-only, acoustic-only, and fused representations under the same evaluation protocol. Therefore, although the fused dataset demonstrated strong performance under the adopted experimental conditions, the exact performance gain attributable to fusion could not be quantified in the present study. A systematic baseline comparison between single-modality and fused inputs remains an important direction for future research.

The study was conducted with eight subjects. Furthermore, the fact that data collection was conducted in a controlled experimental environment and that a wall was used as a reflective surface are some limitations of the study. In the future, datasets that include different reflective surfaces, a larger number of subjects, and a wider variety of noise conditions may help mitigate these limitations.

One of the limitations of this study is that it was conducted solely on a dataset we created ourselves. The primary reason for this, to the best of our knowledge, is the lack of a publicly available dataset for NLOS laser–acoustic human orientation classification. This study focuses on laser-acoustic sensing; in future studies, a more comprehensive validation study should be conducted once a similar publicly available dataset becomes available.

Conclusion

This study aimed to determine human orientation in an NLOS environment by using data fusion of laser and acoustic chirp signals. Twenty-one features were determined from each data source. A dataset with a total of 42 features was created: 21 features from the laser signal and 21 features from the acoustic chirp signal. This dataset was combined using the early fusion method. The fused dataset yielded strong performance across various machine learning models as well as the proposed feedforward neural network model under the adopted experimental protocol. The results suggest that the combined feature representation is effective across multiple evaluated models.

LAO-Net achieved 99.98% accuracy on the collected dataset under the adopted evaluation protocol. However, this result reflects performance within a controlled laboratory setting involving eight subjects and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Some of the other ML/AI models used in the study achieved success rates above 99%. Bagged Trees, Fine KNN, and Cubic SVM are some of these models. An evaluation of the findings revealed that the fused laser-acoustic representation achieved strong classification performance under the experimental protocol. The fused feature representation was associated with stable and consistently strong model performance under the tested conditions. Nevertheless, because single-modality baselines were not evaluated under the same protocol, the specific benefit contributed by feature fusion could not be quantified directly in the present study and remains to be validated in future work. Furthermore, a global Shapley analysis was conducted, and the features provided discriminative information. Global Shapley analysis identified L_feat_dom_power and A_feat_spec_entropy as the most influential features for orientation prediction. Performance should be interpreted within the framework of the adopted window-based experimental protocol. Feature importance analysis was performed based on the global Shapley values obtained using the MATLAB Classification Learner. The results were summarized using the average absolute Shapley values across all analyzed observations.

The study utilized numerous ML/AI models. Each model, both performing well and performing poorly, is presented comparatively in tables. This may provide a useful reference for researchers conducting similar studies. In addition to its academic contributions, this study may hold potential significance for applications such as search and rescue, security systems, autonomous platforms, and robotic sensing. This study holds potential for post-disaster assessment and related decision-support scenarios. This is an important step towards detecting human orientation in out-of-line-of-sight environments under controlled experimental conditions. However, broader validation under more realistic NLOS conditions, including different wall materials, greater environmental variability, and a larger participant pool, is still required to establish external validity.

Future studies aim to create a new dataset that incorporates different surface materials, a larger number of subjects, and complex environmental conditions. Furthermore, the resulting dataset will be studied with deep learning models, hybrid models, and transformer-based methods. A larger dataset is planned for future studies. In addition, subject-independent validation protocols, such as LOSO evaluation, will be considered in future experiments.
