Using CPAP to treat OSA: Is it time to reverse 30 years of understanding and engineering?
Authors: William H Noah
Published: 2025

For the past 40 years, the most effective and utilized therapy for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) has been continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). The term “continuous” originally referred to maintaining airway pressure above atmospheric levels throughout the respiratory cycle, thereby counteracting the negative intraluminal pressures that, under normal conditions, develop during inspiration (as opposed to expiration that generates positive pressures). These negative pressures during inspirations were postulated by Remmers and colleagues in 1978 as the main cause of upper-airway obstruction and collapse in OSA [1]. Building on this concept, Sullivan et al. introduced CPAP to address upper airway collapse in inspiration, however providing continuous pressure during both inspiration and expiration. Notably, in early studies [2], “continuous” did not necessarily mean “constant”, as these devices did not always maintain identical pressures across the respiratory cycle. Examining the mask pressure tracings from these CPAP studies reveals that inspiratory pressure fell up to 5 cmH2O. Indeed, these early devices had no pressure compensation algorithms, and the inspiratory flow across the resistance in the circuit dropped mask pressure. Nevertheless, upper airway patency was preserved. However, maintaining positive pressure throughout the entire breathing cycle also required patients to exhale against a positive mask pressure, which increased intrathoracic pressure, required greater expiratory effort, and caused discomfort in some individuals.

The introduction of pressure support to treat OSA

In 1990, bilevel PAP (BPAP) was introduced for OSA treatment to reduce the aforementioned discomfort and theoretically improve adherence. In fact, BPAP provides two distinct pressures, a higher inspiratory (IPAP) and a lower expiratory pressure (EPAP). However, it has to be noted that this study tested BPAP on only 13 participants with a mean BMI of 57.2 kg/m2 and with 3 participants requiring supplemental oxygen, plausibly suggesting that obesity-hypoventilation syndrome (OHS), a disorder that may require ventilation, cannot be ruled out for likely most study participants. By contrast, OSA patients without comorbidities associated with tonic hypoventilation during sleep (such as OHS or uncompensated chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) do not require ventilatory support, thus the use of BPAP in this population likely represents unnecessary treatment—particularly given the higher cost of BPAP devices and the minimal gains in treatment adherence. Several meta-analyses demonstrating negligible improvements in treatment adherence with BPAP corroborate this notion, yet BPAP is still commonly utilized for OSA treatment in sleep clinics.

Notably, since BPAP first allowed separation of IPAP and EPAP—with equal inspiratory and expiratory pressure defining CPAP—the term “continuous” consequently evolved to also mean “constant”. Thereafter, manometers with feedback to the motor speed control were added to minimize the inspiratory pressure drop in the mask and keep the pressure more “constant” during CPAP treatment. Later, low inertia turbines with even faster responses to inspiratory pressure loss were implemented. We can find no evidence to support the need for preventing inspiratory pressure drops during CPAP therapy, nor any studies evaluating their impact on comfort or eventual side effects. Rather, a recent study assessing the effect of a simple CPAP machine without pressure compensation during IPAP delivery proved safe in OSA [3]. This adds to the evidence of the early CPAP studies in the 1980’s, where drops in inspiratory pressure did not cause loss of therapeutic efficacy [2].

PAP therapy returned to be only “continuous” and not “constant” (even though pressure compensation feedback control remained in place for most devices) when expiratory pressure relief algorithms (EPRAs) and mask compensation algorithms were introduced to the market. EPRAs, created early in the 2000’s as an attempt to decrease discomfort of exhaling against a positive pressure, reduce EPAP below IPAP by 1–3 cmH2O, practically delivering small amounts of pressure support (i.e., IPAP above EPAP). Similarly, mask compensation algorithms, developed soon after with the purpose of transiently increasing IPAP above the set therapeutic level to offset pressure losses caused by high-resistance masks, such as nasal pillows, may augment this pressure support. However, in line with other works, a recent comprehensive meta-analysis we have conducted (currently under review in a major journal) has shown that no form of pressure support improves treatment adherence. Conversely, it is possible that increasing IPAP above EPAP could cause unwanted effects such as treatment-emergent sleep apnea (TECSA) [4], increased unintentional leak or perhaps aerophagia [5] in uncomplicated OSA patients.

Physiological consequences of reducing EPAP

It must be considered that targeting mainly inspiration to prevent pharyngeal collapse is arguably misguided. This is based on several reasons. First, it is EPAP, not IPAP, that provides higher pharyngeal pressure (Fig. 1), thus reducing EPAP yields both lower airflow and upper airway size [5]. Second, the most vulnerable time for upper airway narrowing is end-expiration, which is associated with the smallest pharyngeal cross-sectional area and lowest lung volume [6]. This, in turn, leads to a subsequent loss of longitudinal traction—a stabilizing effect—on the pharynx [5]. Third, since force equals the product of pressure and surface area, a reduction in pharyngeal cross-sectional area consequent to a lower EPAP, would translate into a lower surface area and thus less force for the same airway pressure [5].

Airway pressure modifications following administration of bilevel positive airway pressure (BPAP) or continuous PAP (CPAP). When BPAP is delivered, the device maintains the expiratory PAP (EPAP) setting until it detects the onset of inspiration via a change in flow. Only then does it trigger the rise to the inspiratory PAP (IPAP) setting. This means that the very first part of inspiration begins while the airway pressure is still on EPAP (in the illustration, 6 cmH2O). Therefore, the patient must generate an intrathoracic pressure below 6 cmH2O (i.e., ⁓2–3 cmH2O) to initiate airflow into the lungs, with pharyngeal pressure that remains between EPAP and alveolar pressure (⁓4–5 cmH2O) (top left). Once inspiratory flow is detected, the device rapidly increases pressure to the set IPAP level (10 cmH2O). However, because the device cycles back to EPAP soon after peak inspiratory flow, pharyngeal pressure never approaches the set IPAP of 10 cmH2O and more likely reflects that of EPAP. By contrast, during expiration, alveolar pressure increases substantially over EPAP to create the pressure gradient that drives flow out of the lungs (top right). In other words, EPAP, not IPAP, determines pharyngeal pressure with BPAP both on inspiration and expiration and this is likely why with raising IPAP values there is minimal dilatory effect on the pharyngeal walls [5]. The introduction of inspiratory pressure relief devices and Kairos PAP (KPAP) for OSA therapy

Based on the above considerations, we elected to reduce IPAP to improve comfort, while maintaining EPAP to preserve upper airway patency. Our reasoning was that, since negative pressures drive spontaneous inspiration, reducing IPAP would more closely resemble natural breathing. However, current BPAP devices cannot be set with IPAP below EPAP, thus we created an inspiratory pressure relief device that adds uncompensated resistance to the PAP circuit (Fig. 2) [7]. The high flow across the resistor during inspiration causes the mask pressure to drop by approximately 1.5-2 cmH2O. During expiration, there is minimal flow across the resistor, thus mask pressure is mostly maintained. Such inspiratory pressure relief device did not compromise therapy or treatment efficacy, and it improved leak and machine usage time vs. CPAP over 4 nights of therapy in a preliminary study [7]. In addition, TECSA severity and mouth leak were decreased with the inspiratory pressure relief device [4].

Subsequently, we introduced KPAP, a device capable of selectively reducing inspiratory pressure by 1–5 cmH2O, while ensuring that IPAP never falls below 5 cmH2O (Fig. 2). KPAP maintains a reduced pressure at the transition from inspiration to expiration, thus providing, other than inspiratory pressure relief, also expiratory pressure relief. However, unlike the EPRAs on current ResMed (San Diego, CA) and Philips (Pittsburgh, PA) CPAP devices—EPR® and Flex®, respectively—KPAP increases pressure back to therapeutic levels before the end of expiration (Fig. 2), where the upper airway is likely most susceptible to collapse and where pressure is theoretically most needed (which explains the Greek word “Kairos”, i.e., “at the right time”). Therefore, supplying therapeutic pressure at this critical time past peak expiratory flow should optimize the airway to prepare for the subsequent inspiratory phase. By contrast, a bench model by Zhu et al. [8] found that both ResMed’s EPR® and Philips’s current Flex® (C-Flex+/A-Flex in Fig. 2) fail to supply therapeutic pressure in late expiration. Detailed analysis of the few in-vivo studies in literature examining the effect of flexible pressure vs. auto-titrating PAP reveals that upper airway patency may be compromised by the use of A-Flex or EPR®, but not C-flex (no longer available on the market). In support of the notion that end-expiration is the most critical time for airway collapse, a recent trial reported comparable efficacy between KPAP and CPAP when applied over the same night, with KPAP being preferred for comfort during in-office administration while awake [9]. Again, our running hypothesis is that KPAP is more comfortable due the fact that patients experience a more natural breathing sensation while wearing it. In addition, lower inspiratory pressures are associated with reduced diaphragm tension and more normal rate of chest wall expansion, which are all factors related to dyspnea—and discomfort [5]. Enhanced comfort is generally associated with better adherence, even though this will need to be evaluated long term with appropriately designed studies.

Because KPAP could not be implemented on standard PAP devices, we developed a proprietary APAP algorithm that allowed integration of the KPAP feature. In a preliminary study comparing our APAP system—with and without KPAP—to a widely used commercial APAP device, no differences in efficacy were observed between treatments [10].

Conclusions

Collectively, these findings suggest that controlled reductions of IPAP below EPAP may offer a novel and safe avenue to enhance comfort and potentially improve upon adherence in patients with OSA. Importantly, while our preliminary results are promising, further studies conducted under real-world conditions and across larger, more heterogeneous populations are required to confirm these effects and establish clinical relevance. For example, no randomized, blinded trial has yet compared long-term home-use adherence of KPAP vs. CPAP. Nonetheless, the concept that lowering inspiratory pressure—rather than expiratory pressure—can improve patient tolerance without compromising efficacy challenges long-standing paradigms in PAP therapy and may represent an important step toward more individualized and comfort-driven treatment approaches.
