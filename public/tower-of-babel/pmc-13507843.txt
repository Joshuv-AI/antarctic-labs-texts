Dual regulation of inflammation and ferroptosis: An injectable hydrogel targeting macrophages for the treatment of temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis and knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Guangxin Sun, Shuze Wang, Zhenning Li, Xuzhuo Chen, Wei Wang, Linxi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis is a common progressive inflammatory degenerative joint disease that causes persistent pain and functional disability. Current treatments are insufficient to halt disease progression; therefore, elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms and developing targeted local drug delivery systems that intervene in critical pathological nodes are of great clinical significance. This study developed a novel injectable hydrogel drug delivery system (DA-HAMA) based on Hyaluronic acid Methacryloyl (HAMA) to achieve local, sustained-release, and targeted delivery of the small-molecule drug Diminazene aceturate (DA) within the osteoarthritic joint cavity. The study first identified Spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase 1 (SAT1) as a key potential target for inflammation and ferroptosis in OA synovial macrophages through bioinformatics analysis. It further demonstrated that its inhibitor DA effectively suppresses macrophage inflammatory responses and ferroptosis processes in vitro. To overcome DA's rapid metabolism and poor targeting, the DA-HAMA hydrogel was developed. This system not only enables stable drug loading and controlled release but also allows mechanical properties to be adjusted by drug loading concentration, demonstrating excellent structural stability and biocompatibility. In complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA)-induced mouse knee OA and rat temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJOA) models, intra-articular injection of DA-HAMA hydrogel significantly reduced synovial inflammation, decreased inflammatory cell infiltration, inhibited cartilage destruction, and improved subchondral bone microarchitecture. Its therapeutic effects were closely associated with downregulating inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression and upregulating glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) expression in synovial macrophages, confirming its dual anti-inflammatory and anti-ferroptotic functions in vivo. In summary, this study systematically elucidates the potential role of DA in alleviating OA by inhibiting SAT1 and regulating macrophage inflammation and ferroptosis pathways, spanning target discovery, molecular mechanisms, delivery system development, and efficacy validation in vivo. More importantly, it successfully develops a DA-HAMA hydrogel tailored for the joint environment, featuring sustained-release properties and mechanical reinforcement effects, providing a novel therapeutic strategy for localized targeted treatment of OA.

Introduction

Temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis is an inflammatory degenerative disease affecting the temporomandibular joint in the oral and maxillofacial region. It shares similar pathological features with osteoarthritis in other joints, primarily manifesting as progressive degeneration of articular cartilage, changes in subchondral bone, and the development of reactive synovitis [1]. As a prevalent joint disorder, osteoarthritis not only causes persistent pain, restricted mobility, and joint dysfunction but also significantly diminishes patients' quality of life and mental well-being [2]. Recent epidemiological studies indicate that the prevalence of osteoarthritis increases with age, while the patient population is showing a trend toward younger onset [[3], [4], [5]]. Although existing treatments—such as intra-articular drug injections, joint lavage, and physical therapy [6,7]—can alleviate symptoms to some extent, their effectiveness in halting disease progression remains limited. For most patients, particularly those in end-stage disease, total joint replacement is considered the final treatment option [8,9]. However, its high cost makes it unaffordable for many families, leaving anti-inflammatory drugs as the primary means of pain relief [10]. Therefore, in-depth exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying TMJOA and its early lesions to identify efficient, safe, and stable therapeutic targets and drugs holds significant importance for the prevention and treatment of this disease [[11], [12], [13]].

The pathological process of osteoarthritis initiation is marked by the degeneration of articular cartilage, involving a disruption of the joint's overall homeostasis that subsequently affects all joint-associated tissues [14]. Although the molecular mechanisms underlying the onset and progression of osteoarthritis remain incompletely understood, synovial macrophages play a pivotal role in this pathophysiological process [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Tissue debris and necrotic cells within inflamed joints stimulate synovial macrophages to produce inflammation and release inflammatory mediators. These mediators further exacerbate macrophage inflammation and recruit additional macrophages. Extensive research indicates that the progression of osteoarthritis is significantly influenced by reactive synovial inflammation [19]. Increased macrophage density and their infiltration within the synovium are considered fundamental pathological hallmarks of synovitis [20]. Furthermore, inflammatory stimuli markedly enhance macrophage phagocytic capacity, leading to intracellular iron accumulation [21]. Concurrently, these stimuli induce respiratory bursts in macrophages, characterized by a rapid increase in mitochondrial oxygen consumption accompanied by NADPH oxidase activation, resulting in substantial H2O2 production. Intracellular iron ions react with H2O2 via the Fenton reaction to generate hydroxyl radicals, triggering lipid peroxidation and resulting in ferroptosis [22]. Concurrently, inflammatory synovial macrophages produce excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), which induce lipid peroxidation and thereby promote ferroptosis in synovial macrophages [21,[23], [24], [25]]. Cellular debris and inflammatory mediators released during ferroptosis further recruit additional macrophages, amplifying inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, ultimately establishing a vicious cycle of persistent intra-articular inflammation. From the perspective of the etiological matrix, osteoarthritis is a complex network system dynamically interwoven by multiple pathogenic factors. This matrix encompasses both systemic triggers—such as mechanical stress, metabolic disorders, aging, and genetic susceptibility—and core pathological pathways, including persistent synovial macrophage activation, inflammatory cascade reactions, and ferroptosis. These factors are interconnected but amplify each other through intricate interactions, forming a self-sustaining vicious cycle that propels the progressive degeneration of joints. Therefore, developing a novel drug delivery system targeting critical nodes within this etiological matrix to precisely regulate core pathological pathways, while leveraging the material's physicochemical properties to lubricate and improve the joint microenvironment, holds promise for reshaping the pathological structure of the entire network. This represents a crucial strategy for systemic treatment of osteoarthritis.

Benefiting from rapid advances in bioinformatics, drug design and optimization have accelerated through database-driven identification of therapeutic targets, characterization of driver genes, analysis of signaling pathways, and network pharmacology-based prediction of target druggability [26,27]. Genes associated with inflammation and ferroptosis, such as Sat1, Acsl4, Il1b, and Il6, exhibit significantly upregulated expression in inflamed joints [28]. Spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase 1 (SAT1), a key enzyme in polyamine metabolism, catalyzes the acetylation of spermidine and spermine to form N1-acetylspermine and N1-acetylspermidine. Subsequently, N1-acetylpolyamine oxidase (PAO) catalyzes the formation of putrescine. PAO expression and activity typically increase under oxidative stress or inflammatory stimuli, and it has been demonstrated to induce lipid peroxidation and trigger ferroptosis under ROS-induced oxidative stress conditions [[29], [30], [31]]. Simultaneously, spermine/spermidine, as reactive substrates of SAT1, exert multidimensional regulatory roles in macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses and the progression of inflammatory diseases [32,33]. As a key component of the endogenous antioxidant defense system, it maintains cellular redox homeostasis by efficiently scavenging free radicals. Abnormal overexpression of SAT1 leads to excessive substrate depletion, resulting in explosive ROS accumulation, significantly elevating oxidative stress levels and inhibiting cell proliferation [34]. Furthermore, spermidine/spermine synergistically inhibits the activation of key inflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-κB, PI3K/AKT, and MAPKs. This effectively suppresses the polarization of macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and promotes their transition to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [35]. Moreover, spermine/spermidine further suppresses inflammation and maintains cellular integrity by enhancing autophagy activity and promoting mitochondrial respiratory function [32]. Therefore, SAT1 represents a potential therapeutic target for osteoarthritis.

Diminazene aceturate (DA), a SAT1 inhibitor, effectively alleviates diseases associated with excessively produced inflammatory mediators [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. However, the DA molecule is rapidly cleared and metabolized in vivo, resulting in low local bioavailability and a short half-life within the joint cavity, severely limiting its therapeutic efficacy. To address these issues, the selection of a drug delivery system is crucial. Sodium hyaluronate hydrogel, as a natural biomaterial with excellent properties, offers good biocompatibility, degradability, and viscoelasticity [40,41]. Moreover, sodium hyaluronate itself has a lubricating effect on joints [42], making it an ideal carrier substrate. Crosslinking DA with sodium hyaluronate is a key strategy to enhance this delivery system's performance. This crosslinking significantly improves multiple material properties: First, it forms a denser, more stable three-dimensional network structure, substantially increasing mechanical strength and structural stability to prevent premature degradation in vivo. Second, this network effectively encapsulates and immobilizes DA molecules, significantly delaying drug burst release and loss to achieve sustained-release effects. Finally, the crosslinked structure provides a protective microenvironment for DA to some extent, enhancing the stability of the entire formulation during storage and use, thereby potentially improving DA's therapeutic efficacy and application value.

In this study, we further validated SAT1 as a potential therapeutic target in OA synovial macrophages and characterized its inhibitor DA through bioinformatics analysis and in vitro experiments. It then investigated the effects of DA on lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ROS production and its associated macrophage inflammation and ferroptosis, while thoroughly analyzing the underlying molecular mechanisms. Additionally, a DA sodium hyaluronate hydrogel was prepared via photopolymerization crosslinking. It's in vivo efficacy was evaluated using a mouse knee osteoarthritis model induced by CFA and a rat temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis model induced by CFA [43]. This study provides evidence supporting SAT1 as a therapeutic target for temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis and develops a highly effective therapeutic hydrogel targeting this molecule, offering a novel strategy for clinical intervention in TMJOA.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The HAMA hydrogel (150 kDa,methacrylation degree ∼60%) and Leucine aminopeptidase (LAP) were procured from Engineering For Life (Suzhou, China). Diminazene aceturate (HY-12404), an inhibitor of SAT1, and Erastin (HY-15763), a ferroptosis agonist, were obtained from MCE (Shanghai, China), dissolved using Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (HY-Y0320, MCE, shanghai, China). LPS from Escherichia coli O55:B5 was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Minimal essential medium alpha (α-MEM) was obtained from HyClone (Logan, UT, USA). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was obtained from Avantor (Gaithersburg, MD, USA). TB Green Premix Ex TaqII and the Prime Script RT Reagent Kit were obtained from Takara Biotechnology (Otsu, Shiga, Japan). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), Hoechst 33342 (C1028), Dihydroethidium (DHE, S0063), ROS assay kit (DCFH-DA, S0033), DAF-FM DA (S0019), DAPI(2-(4-Amidinophenyl)-6-indolecarbamidine dihydrochloride, C1002) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). MitoSOX Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (40778ES50) was obtained from Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). BODIPY 581/591C11(C10445), a Lipid Peroxidation Sensor was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Primary antibodies against β-actin (#4970), JNK (#9252), and phospho-JNK (#9251), ERK (#4695), phospho-ERK (#4370), p38 (#8690), phospho-p38 (#4511), p65 (#8242), phospho-p65 (#3033) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Primary antibody against ACSL4 (22401) was purchased from Proteintech (Rosemont, USA.) Primary antibody against iNOS (15323) and GPX4 (231174) were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK).

Database data mining

This study utilized the GSE248453 dataset (scRNA-seq data from 4 cases of osteoarthritis with severe pain and 4 cases with mild pain). High-quality single-cell data were obtained through rigorous quality control (excluding cells with <400 or >7000 genes and mitochondrial gene proportion >10%). After normalization, cells were clustered via PCA dimensionality reduction and t-SNE visualization, with cell type annotation based on marker genes. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened based on the criteria (P ≤ 0.05 and |log2(fold change)|>1), and DEGs were subjected to in-depth analysis using the KEGG database.

Network pharmacology

To identify potential therapeutic targets associated with temporomandibular joint synovitis, keywords such as “synovitis” and “temporomandibular joint” were searched in the DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org/) and GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) databases to identify disease targets associated with human synovitis and temporomandibular joint disorders. Potential targets for DA were predicted using SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) and PharmMapper (http://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper). Performed intersection analysis between disease-associated targets and drug-predicted targets. Standardized all candidate proteins using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) to unify them under official human protein gene names. Screened for common targets shared between DA and temporomandibular synovitis. The intersection results were visualized using Venn diagrams generated by bioinformatics platforms. The PPI network was constructed using the STRING database, and core proteins were screened using Cytoscape.

Protein-protein interaction network construction and core target screening

To further explore the potential mechanisms of DA intervention in synovitis, the gene names of cross-targets were imported into the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/). “Homo sapiens” was selected as the species, and the interaction confidence score threshold was set to 0.7 to construct a high-confidence protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. The PPI results were downloaded and imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.10.1) for visualization and network topology analysis. Utilize the NetworkAnalyzer plugin to compute network topology parameters for each node (e.g., degree centrality, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality), comprehensively evaluating node importance within the network. Employ Cytoscape's built-in CytoHubba plugin to rank nodes based on the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm, identifying the top 20 key targets to construct a core subnetwork.

Molecular docking

Compound structure files were obtained from the PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and converted from SDF to PDB format using Open Babel 2.3.2 software. Receptor proteins were retrieved from the UniProt database. Receptor proteins were modified using AutoDock Tools software, including hydrogenation and charge balancing, and both receptor proteins and ligand molecules were converted to pdbqt format. Perform molecular docking between the receptor protein and ligand molecules using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2, and analyze the docking results with PLIP.

Transcriptome sequencing

RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with 100 ng/mL LPS or LPS + 20 μM DA for 24 h at 37°C in 5% CO2. Extract and purify total RNA from control, LPS, and 20 μM DA groups using TRIzol reagent. Sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq X platform, with high-quality sequencing data analyzed via R software. DEGs were identified based on the criteria P ≤ 0.05 and |log2(fold change)|>1. DEGs underwent in-depth analysis using the GO database, KEGG database, and GSEA database.

Transmission electron microscopy for mitochondrial ultrastructure

Required cell samples were harvested from culture dishes. First, glutaraldehyde and osmium tetroxide fixation was performed to meticulously preserve ultrastructure. Samples were then dehydrated using a gradient ethanol series and subjected to osmotic embedding. After obtaining 60–90 nm sections via ultramicrotome, samples underwent double staining with uranium acetate and lead citrate to enhance membrane contrast. Key ultrastructural features—including mitochondrial cristae morphology, matrix density, and inner/outer membrane architecture—were ultimately observed under transmission electron microscopy.

Cell culture

RAW 264.7 mouse macrophages were cultured in α-MEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2.

Four-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were euthanized (Cervical Spinal Dislocation), and their femurs and tibias were isolated. Trim both ends of the bones to expose the marrow cavity, then repeatedly flush the cavity with a 1 mL syringe to collect bone marrow cells. Subsequently, place the cells in complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin, and 30 ng/mL M-CSF, and culture them in a humidified environment at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Cytotoxicity assay

RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates for quantitative CellCountingKit-8 detection. After 24 h, add DA at increasing concentrations. Subsequently, add 100 μL of 10% CCK-8 solution to each well and incubate for 2 h. Read at 450 nm wavelength using a Tecan Spark Microplate reader and quantify cell viability based on OD values. Bone marrow-derived macrophages were treated using the same protocol as RAW 264.7 cells.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species, nitric oxide, and superoxide levels

Intracellular ROS, Nitric Oxide (NO), superoxide and mitochondrial superoxide levels were detected using DCFH-DA, DAF-FM DA, DHE and MitoSOX Red probes, respectively. RAW 264.7 macrophages were treated with 100 ng/mL LPS and 10 or 20 μM DA for 24 h, followed by sequential incubation with the respective probes (DCFH-DA 10 μM, 25 min; DAF-FM DA 5 μM, 20 min; DHE 5 μM, 30 min; MitoSOX Red 5 μM, 10 min) at 37°C. Cells were then stained with Hoechst 33342 for 5 min, washed three times with pre-warmed PBS, and fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). Image J software was used for semi-quantitative analysis of DCF (ROS), DAF-FM (NO), DHE (superoxide), and MitoSOX Red (mitochondrial superoxide) fluorescence signals.

Immunofluorescence staining

RAW 264.7 macrophages were incubated with LPS and 10 or 20 μM DA for 24 h. Following fixation, permeabilization, and blocking, cells were incubated with iNOS primary antibody. Subsequently, cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibody for 1 h, with nuclei counterstained using DAPI. Cells were then observed using CLSM and analyzed with Image J software.

Lipid peroxidation detection

BODIPY 581/591C11 is a commonly used fluorescent probe for detecting intracellular lipid peroxidation. Briefly, RAW 264.7 macrophages were cultured for 24 h in the presence of 100 ng/mL LPS and 10 or 20 μM DA. Subsequently, cells were co-incubated with 10 μM BODIPY-C11 at 37°C for 10 min. Following this, cells were washed three times with pre-warmed PBS. Fluorescence images were captured using CLSM, and semi-quantitative analysis of fluorescence was performed using Image J software.

Quantitative PCR analysis

To evaluate the expression of inflammation-related genes, RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in a 6-well plate and cultured for 24 h in the presence of 100 ng/mL LPS and 10 or 20 μM DA. To evaluate ferroptosis-related gene expression, RAW264.7 macrophages were similarly seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and co-cultured with Erastin and 10 or 20 μM DA for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted using the Axygen RNA Mini Prep Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Following reverse transcription using the RNA template, real-time quantitative PCR analysis was performed using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II on an ABI 7500 Sequence Detection System. The 10 μL reaction mixture contained 5 μL TB Green, 3 μL ddH2O, 1 μL cDNA, 0.4 μL of each primer, and 0.2 μL ROX Dye 2. The cycling program consisted of 40 cycles: 95°C for 5 s followed by 60°C for 30 s. Amplification specificity was verified by analyzing the melting curve. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with β-actin as the internal reference gene.

Western Blotting

To collect total cellular proteins, RAW 264.7 macrophages were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in a 6-well plate and cultured for 24 h in the presence of LPS and either 10 or 20 μM DA. For total protein extraction, cells were lysed using a complete cell lysis buffer containing a protease inhibitor mixture. The lysate was then centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected to obtain the protein. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA method. The collected protein was dissolved in loading buffer, separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred to a 0.22 μm PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked in TBST containing 5% BSA for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation with the primary antibody at 4°C. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, and protein bands were visualized using an Odyssey imaging scanning system.

Construction of hydrogels

Weigh a specified amount of solid HAMA (EFL Company, China) and dissolve it in a solution of LAP (EFL Company, China). Heat the mixture in a water bath until HAMA is completely dissolved, yielding a 2% final concentration solution. Subsequently, an appropriate amount of DA was added to the prepared 2% HAMA solution to formulate solutions with final concentrations of 0 μM, 50 μM, and 200 μM. The solutions were stored in aluminum foil to avoid light exposure and cooled to 4°C before use. During use, the UV cross-linking parameters were: wavelength 405 nm, power 30 W, distance 3 cm, exposure time 30 s.

Hydrogel characterization analysis

All characterization was performed on hydrogels after UV cross-linking and gelation. Weigh 10 mg of sample and 500 mg of potassium bromide, mix thoroughly using a sample mixer, press into a tablet mold, place in the sample chamber, and collect infrared spectra (wavenumber range 400-4000). Perform spectral processing as needed, identify peaks, and output the spectrum. To observe the microstructure of the hydrogel, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used. First, the sample was immersed in liquid nitrogen (−196°C) for 5-10 min for deep cryogenic freezing. Subsequently, it was rapidly removed and subjected to an instantaneous force to induce brittle fracture, yielding a fresh, flat original fracture surface. The cross-section was oriented upward and secured to conductive adhesive on the sample stage, ensuring stable loading into the electron microscope chamber. After the chamber reached high vacuum, imaging commenced. Rheological properties of hydrogel samples were studied using a rheometer (Anton Paar MCR 302e, Austria). Place the hydrogel sample (approximately 1 mm thick) between a 25 mm diameter parallel plate fixture. For dynamic frequency sweep testing, set strain to 1%, frequency range to 0.1–100 Hz, and maintain test temperature at 20°C. For amplitude sweep testing, set a fixed frequency of 1 Hz and measure G′ and G″ in the 0.1–100% amplitude mode.

To assess hydrogel swelling performance, samples were immersed in PBS solution for 48 h, then weighed with wet weight recorded as Wi. After thorough freeze-drying in a freeze-dryer, dry weight was recorded as Wd. Swelling rate was calculated using the formula: Swelling rate = (Wi − Wd)/Wd × 100%. To test the degradation rate of the hydrogel, the prepared samples were immersed in PBS at 37°C until reaching a swollen equilibrium state. The initial mass was then recorded as W0. Subsequently, the samples were stored in PBS solution. At specific time intervals, the samples were removed, surface solution was blotted off with absorbent paper, and the sample mass was weighed using an electronic balance, recorded as Wt. The degradation rate was calculated using the formula: Degradation rate = (W0 − Wt)/W0 × 100%. To determine the drug release rate of the loaded hydrogel, 1 mL of hydrogel sample was immersed in 3 mL of PBS buffer and shaken at 37°C to simulate physiological conditions. At predetermined time intervals (1, 5, 10, 15, 25, 30 days), a fixed volume of release solution (100 μL) was collected and immediately replenished with an equal volume of fresh PBS at the same temperature to maintain the total volume of the release system constant. The collected sample solutions were measured for absorbance at 450 nm using a Tecan Spark Microplate reader. Absorbance values were converted to drug concentration based on a pre-established standard curve, and cumulative release amount and cumulative release rate were calculated.

Animal models of knee osteoarthritis and temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of DA-HAMA hydrogel in vivo, a mouse knee osteoarthritis model was established by intra-articular injection of CFA. The pre-crosslinked DA-HAMA hydrogels were loaded into a sterile syringe and injected intra-articularly. Specifically, 20 male 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into four groups of 5 mice each. In the control group, 20 μL of saline was injected into the right hind knee joint of each mouse. In the other groups, 20 μL of CFA (5 mg/mL) was injected into the right hind knee joint of each mouse. Subsequently, mice in the hydrogel treatment groups received 20 μL of DA-HAMA hydrogel injected into the right hind knee joint every 3 days for a total of 4 injections. The L DA-HAMA group received a drug concentration of 50 μM, while the H DA-HAMA group received 200 μM. After 14 days, mice were euthanized, knee joint samples were collected, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h for further analysis.

Concurrently, to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of DA hydrogel on TMJ osteoarthritis, a rat TMJOA model was established via intra-articular injection of CFA [44,45]. Briefly, 32 male SD rats aged 8 weeks were randomly divided into four groups of 8 rats each. In the control group, 50 μL of saline was injected bilaterally into the temporomandibular joints of each rat. In the other groups, 50 μL of CFA (5 mg/mL) was injected bilaterally into the temporomandibular joints of each rat. Subsequently, rats in the hydrogel treatment groups received bilateral injections of 50 μL DA-HAMA hydrogel into the TMJ every 3 days (L DA-HAMA group: 50 μM; H DA-HAMA group: 200 μM). After 7 days, the first group of rats (receiving 2 hydrogel treatments) were euthanized. Temporomandibular joint samples were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h for subsequent analysis. After 14 days, the second group of rats (receiving 4 hydrogel injections) were euthanized. TMJ samples were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h for subsequent analysis.

Micro-computed tomography

Micro-CT scans were performed using a high-resolution micro-CT system. X-ray energy was set at 70 kV and 200 μA, with an exposure time of 300 ms. Three-dimensional skeletal reconstruction was conducted using Mimics software. Multiple bone parameters were analyzed using CTAn software, including bone volume fraction, trabecular number, trabecular separation, and trabecular thickness.

Histology and immunohistochemistry analysis

Following micro-CT scanning, specimens were decalcified in 10% EDTA solution for four weeks before paraffin embedding. Histological sections were prepared using hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and Safranin O-Fast Green(S&F) staining. OARSI (Osteoarthritis Research Society International) scores and synovitis scores were assessed according to previously reported methods [46]. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using primary antibodies (iNOS and GPX4). Stained tissue sections were photographed using a high-resolution microscope, and relative fluorescence intensities of iNOS and GPX4 in the synovium were quantified using Image J software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired Student's t-test after testing for homogeneity of variance. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Bioinformatics suggests SAT1 may serve as a key target for inhibiting synovial macrophage inflammation in OA

Fig. 1A depicts the workflow for identifying SAT1 through database analysis and exploring its inhibitor DA via network pharmacology. Single-cell sequencing data from severely damaged synovial tissue and synovial tissue with relatively normal bone joints (GSE248453) were obtained. After data processing and cell clustering analysis, all cells were classified into six major subpopulations: B cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, mast cells, and T cells (Fig. 1B). Differential gene expression analysis revealed significantly higher SAT1 expression in OA synovial tissue compared to the normal group. Given that SAT1 is a rate-limiting enzyme in polyamine catabolism involved in regulating cellular inflammation and ferroptosis [47], its upregulation in OA suggests potential involvement in disease-related pathological processes. Further single-cell analysis revealed that the proportion of macrophages expressing SAT1 in the OA group (41.37%) was significantly higher than in the normal group (23.09%), indicating that the elevated SAT1 expression likely originates primarily from macrophage subpopulations (Fig. 1D). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that differentially expressed genes were predominantly concentrated in signaling pathways related to inflammatory response, cell death, and ferroptosis (Fig. 1E). Previous studies have established synovitis as a key hallmark and early manifestation of OA progression [48,49]. Consequently, we identified DA, a known SAT1 inhibitor (Fig. 1F). Molecular docking results indicate that DA and SAT1 form a stable complex via hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and salt bridges, with a binding energy of −9.154 kcal/mol (Fig. 1G). This suggests high binding affinity between the two [50], providing a structural basis for DA's targeted inhibition of SAT1. Simultaneously, we integrated DA's known targets (130) with OA-related targets (7047), identifying 76 common targets (Fig. 1H). PPI network analysis revealed that the proteins corresponding to these targets are highly involved in inflammatory responses, metabolic regulation, tissue repair and remodeling (Fig. S1). Thus, it is inferred that DA may improve OA by targeting SAT1 to regulate inflammation and ferroptosis in synovial macrophages.

DA effectively Inhibits Macrophage Inflammation

To establish a safe concentration range for subsequent studies, we first evaluated DA's biological safety toward macrophages using the CCK-8 assay system. Results showed no significant toxicity to RAW 264.7 cells at concentrations of 20 μM and below. To further simulate the real microenvironment within the joint cavity, we replicated the validation in primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs), confirming that DA maintained good biocompatibility within this concentration range (Fig. 1I). Based on this, we determined that 10 μM and 20 μM could serve as safe and effective concentrations for subsequent mechanism studies.

To investigate DA's anti-inflammatory capacity, we established an in vitro inflammatory model by stimulating RAW 264.7 macrophages with 100 ng/mL LPS for 24 h and observed the effects of different DA concentrations. RT-qPCR results showed that the expression of inflammation-related genes (Tnf, Il1b, Nos2) was significantly elevated after LPS stimulation, while DA treatment could suppress their expression (Fig. 2A). Inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), a key effector molecule in the inflammatory response, plays a central role in achieving efficient and sustained synthesis of nitric oxide (NO) under cytokine induction [51]. Immunofluorescence staining and quantitative analysis revealed that DA at various concentrations effectively suppressed iNOS expression at the protein level, with a concentration-dependent inhibitory effect-20 μM DA demonstrated significantly superior efficacy compared to 10 μM DA (Fig. 2B–C). To systematically elucidate the regulatory mechanism of DA on macrophage inflammatory responses, transcriptome sequencing analysis was performed on RAW 264.7 macrophages. Preliminary quality control indicated good reproducibility among replicates in the Control, LPS, and DA-treated groups (Fig. S2A). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering within groups and clear separation between groups (Fig. S2B), reflecting robust experimental reproducibility and appropriate grouping. Differential expression analysis identified 5584 differentially expressed genes between the LPS and DA groups, comprising 2375 upregulated and 3209 downregulated genes (Fig. 2D). Further KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of these differentially expressed genes in biological processes including cellular metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, lipid metabolism, and immune/inflammatory response (Fig. 2E). In-depth analysis of genes downregulated by DA treatment revealed significant enrichment in inflammation-related pathways such as Toll-like receptor signaling, TGF-β signaling, and NF-κB signaling [[52], [53], [54]]. These genes also showed pronounced clustering in processes including insulin resistance and osteoclast differentiation (Fig. S2C). These findings suggest that DA may regulate macrophage differentiation and functional states by influencing metabolic reprogramming, oxidative stress levels, and immune-inflammatory signaling networks.

Genetic set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further captured subtle trends in gene expression under DA treatment. Analysis revealed that gene sets associated with macrophage generation and chemotactic migration were significantly suppressed after DA treatment (Fig. S3A–B), suggesting DA may weaken macrophage proliferation capacity and their recruitment to inflammatory sites. Concurrently, DA treatment broadly downregulated gene sets involved in pattern recognition receptor activation, Toll-like receptor signaling pathways, and immune receptor activation (Fig. S3C–H), indicating DA can alleviate inflammatory processes by inhibiting the immune activation state of macrophages. Notably, activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway was also significantly suppressed under DA influence (Fig. S3I), further corroborating its anti-inflammatory effects. Conversely, DA promoted activation of gene sets related to cell cycle and transcription (Fig. S4), suggesting it may accelerate cell cycle progression and drive cellular renewal, thereby providing potential cellular support for alleviating osteoarthritis pathology.

Based on these transcriptomic features and supporting literature, we hypothesize that the MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways may play central roles in DA's anti-inflammatory effects. To validate this hypothesis, we examined phosphorylation levels of key signaling proteins in these pathways via Western blot analysis. Results showed that LPS stimulation significantly increased the phosphorylation levels of JNK, ERK, p38, and p65, indicating effective activation of MAPK and NF-κB pathways. Conversely, DA treatment significantly suppressed the phosphorylation-activated state of these proteins (Fig. 2F and G). These findings collectively demonstrate that DA effectively suppresses the activation of MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways, thereby regulating the expression of downstream inflammation-related genes and ultimately reshaping the inflammatory microenvironment of macrophages (Fig. 2H).

DA improves oxidative stress and ferroptosis in macrophages

Transcriptome sequencing results suggest that DA regulates lipid metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation in inflammatory macrophages, with ROS generated by oxidative stress persisting throughout the inflammatory process [55]. Therefore, we further evaluated DA's ROS scavenging capacity in inflammatory macrophages. RAW 264.7 macrophages were stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS for 24 h, followed by treatment with 10 μM or 20 μM DA. Detection was performed using DCFH-DA, MitoSOX, and DHE fluorescent probes. The outcomes revealed that DA effectively alleviates LPS-induced oxidative stress by scavenging ROS. Specifically, DCFH-DA detection revealed a concentration-dependent reduction in total intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 3A and D); MitoSOX probe further confirmed DA's specific mitigation of mitochondrial superoxide accumulation (Fig. 3B and D); DHE staining demonstrated DA's significant suppression of superoxide anion generation in inflammatory macrophages (Fig. 3D and E). Concurrently, DAF-FM assays demonstrated that DA effectively suppressed LPS-induced NO release in RAW 264.7 cells (Fig. 3C and D). This effect correlates with DA's inhibition of iNOS (Fig. 2B), thereby preventing its synergistic exacerbation of oxidative stress with ROS. Oxidative stress damage exacerbates cellular inflammatory states [56]. Oxidative stress is a key factor triggering multiple cellular cascades [57,58]. Building upon the confirmation of DA's potent ROS scavenging activity, bioinformatics analysis revealed ferroptosis-related biological phenomena in osteoarthritis synovial tissue (Fig. 1E). We further investigated whether DA modulates macrophage inflammatory responses by regulating ferroptosis. Lipid peroxidation represents a core pathological step in ferroptosis [24,59]. Detection using the BODIPY-C11 probe, along with fluorescence and quantitative analysis, demonstrated that DA significantly mitigates this process (Fig. 3D and F), thereby inhibiting ferroptosis in inflammatory macrophages.

To elucidate the mechanism of DA-mediated ferroptosis regulation, RT-qPCR analysis of key ferroptosis genes revealed that DA not only suppressed LPS-induced Ptgs2 upregulation but also reversed the elevated expression of Acsl4 and Ptgs2 induced by the ferroptosis agonist Erastin (Fig. 3G). At the protein level, Western Blot analysis targeted ACSL4 (promoting factor) and GPX4 (protective factor) [25,[60], [61], [62]], key regulators in ferroptosis. Results demonstrated that DA not only suppressed ACSL4 protein overexpression in inflammatory macrophages but also elevated GPX4 protein levels (Fig. 3H), thereby rescuing ferroptosis through bidirectional regulation. When cells undergo ferroptosis, mitochondrial damage occurs synchronously, and mitochondrial status similarly marks the progression of ferroptosis [[63], [64], [65]]. Transmission electron microscopy ultrastructure analysis confirmed that DA treatment (20 μM) alleviated LPS-induced ferroptosis-related morphological alterations in macrophages, including mitochondrial crimping and membrane thickening (Fig. 3I).

In summary, this study reveals that DA alleviates ferroptosis in inflammatory macrophages through a dual mechanism: first, by scavenging ROS to inhibit oxidative stress initiation; second, by regulating the ACSL4-GPX4 expression balance to block the lipid peroxidation-driven ferroptosis pathway. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into the protective role of DA in inflammation-related diseases (Fig. 3J).

Preparation and Characterization of DA-HAMA hydrogels

Although the aforementioned studies have demonstrated that DA exhibits favorable biological effects in anti-inflammation and anti-ferroptosis, its clinical application still faces several challenges: rapid drug metabolism hinders sustained release; maintaining local drug concentrations proves difficult; and targeted delivery capabilities are lacking. To address these issues, we developed a hydrogel-based drug delivery system designed to achieve precise, adequate, and sustained drug release.

Sodium hyaluronate exhibits multiple mechanisms of action in osteoarthritis treatment: it directly replenishes synovial fluid to enhance lubrication, cushioning, and physical barrier functions [66,67]; simultaneously, it molecularly inhibits inflammatory mediators, reduces pain sensitivity, and promotes endogenous proteoglycan synthesis, thereby alleviating pain and improving joint function [68,69]. To this end, we selected Hyaluronic acid Methacryloyl (HAMA) as the carrier. By incorporating DA solution into a pre-fabricated 2% HAMA-150K hydrogel, we prepared drug-loaded hydrogels with concentrations of 0 μM (HAMA), 50 μM (L DA-HAMA), and 200 μM (H DA-HAMA) (Fig. 4A). To evaluate material properties, freeze-dried hydrogel scaffolds were examined by scanning electron microscopy after 24 h. Results revealed that compared to the blank HAMA group, the drug-loaded hydrogels exhibited more regular pore structures with increased pore wall thickness. This suggests that DA incorporation enhances the hydrogel's network structural strength, facilitating its morphological stability under joint loading conditions (Fig. 4C).

Swelling experiments revealed slight differences in swelling rates among groups after 48-h PBS immersion (HAMA: 999.8 ± 22.96%; L DA-HAMA: 949.1 ± 6.74%; H DA-HAMA: 944.8 ± 9.02%). The swelling rate of the drug-loaded groups was slightly lower than that of the blank group, potentially related to DA enhancing the crosslinking density of the hydrogel (Fig. 4D). In vitro degradation experiments showed similar degradation behaviors across all groups, indicating that DA loading did not significantly alter the fundamental degradation characteristics of HAMA hydrogel (Fig. 4E). Drug release profiles revealed similar release patterns for both high- and low-concentration loaded hydrogels: rapid release from days 5 to 15 followed by a gradual plateau. H DA-HAMA exhibited consistently higher cumulative release rates than L DA-HAMA, likely due to its higher initial drug loading (Fig. 4F). These results indicate that the hydrogel system exhibits excellent water absorption properties, providing cells with a moist microenvironment. Its early structural stability and adaptive degradation behavior facilitate tissue repair. Concurrently, the “fast-then-slow” release kinetics of DA enable high drug concentration delivery during the initial inflammatory phase while maintaining sustained supply in the later stages.

Infrared spectroscopy further revealed material interactions. HAMA exhibited characteristic absorption peaks at 3425 cm−1 (N-H/O-H stretching vibration), 2925 cm−1 (C-H stretching vibration), and 1721 cm−1 (carboxyl C=O stretching vibration). Absorption peaks at 1627 cm−1, 1560 cm−1, and 1309 cm−1 correspond to amide I, II, and III bands, respectively, confirming the presence of amide bonds in HAMA (Fig. 4G). The infrared spectra of the drug-loaded hydrogels exhibited profiles similar to HAMA, but the peak intensities exhibited systematic changes with increasing DA concentration. This indicates the formation of hydrogen bonding interactions between DA and HAMA molecules, which intensified with higher drug concentrations, confirming the stable loading of DA within the hydrogel network.

To further evaluate the mechanical properties of the materials, rheological testing was conducted. Frequency scanning results revealed that all samples exhibited typical elastic solid behavior, with the storage modulus (G′) consistently higher than the loss modulus (G″), indicating a predominantly elastic response (Fig. 4H). As the angular frequency increased from 0.1 rad/s to 100 rad/s, G′ showed a gradual upward trend across all groups, reflecting a certain degree of frequency dependence in the gel network. Notably, H DA-HAMA exhibited the highest G′ values across all frequencies, significantly exceeding those of HAMA and L DA-HAMA. This indicates that the high DA concentration further enhances the network crosslinking density or intermolecular interactions, thereby increasing material rigidity. The G′ of L DA-HAMA was also slightly higher than that of HAMA, suggesting that the mechanical strength of the gel depends on the drug loading concentration. Amplitude-modulated results further reveal the structural response of materials under varying strains (Fig. 4I). Within the low-strain range, G′ remains stable across all hydrogels, exhibiting a distinct linear viscoelastic region, indicating excellent recoverability of the network structure under small deformations. As strain increases, G′ gradually decreases, revealing strain softening that reflects progressive degradation of the gel network under higher shear forces.

These results confirm the successful development of an injectable hydrogel drug delivery system based on HAMA. This system not only enables stable loading and controlled release of DA but also exhibits structural reinforcement, suitable swelling and degradation properties, and concentration-dependent mechanical strengthening effects. It offers a promising novel delivery strategy for targeted treatment of osteoarthritis.

Micro-CT and 3D reconstruction revealed that CFA intra-articular injection successfully induced typical OA bone structural lesions, including subchondral bone resorption, tibial articular cortical bone defects, and surface roughening. DA-HAMA hydrogel treatment significantly ameliorated these pathological changes, effectively preserving articular surface morphology integrity and subchondral bone density (Fig. 5B). Histological analysis (H&E and S&F) revealed marked synovial swelling, inflammatory cell infiltration, synovial hyperplasia, and articular cartilage erosion in the CFA-injected OA model group, accompanied by reduced chondrocyte numbers and matrix loss, consistent with OA pathology (Fig. 5C). Following DA-HAMA hydrogel intervention, synovial inflammatory responses were markedly attenuated, while articular cartilage thickness and matrix content increased, with efficacy exhibiting a DA concentration-dependent pattern. Based on standardized scoring systems, both synovitis scores and OARSI scores were significantly reduced in the treatment group (Fig. S5).

To further explore the molecular mechanisms, we performed immunofluorescence staining on synovial tissue to detect iNOS and GPX4 expression. Results showed that the DA-HAMA hydrogel significantly suppressed excessive iNOS expression in OA synovium while simultaneously upregulating GPX4 protein levels (Fig. 5E–G), indicating dual regulatory effects against inflammation and ferroptosis.

In summary, DA-HAMA hydrogel demonstrates clear therapeutic potential by regulating the iNOS/GPX4 expression balance in synovial tissue, thereby mitigating local inflammatory responses and inhibiting ferroptosis. This protects the integrity of articular surface cartilage and alleviates subchondral bone loss.

Micro-CT scanning and 3D reconstruction results (Fig. 6B and D) revealed that intra-articular CFA injection successfully induced rat TMJ osteoarthritis, characterized by severe condylar bone destruction, surface roughness, cortical bone defects, and significant trabecular bone disruption and remodeling. Notably, 14 days after CFA modeling, some bone destruction indicators showed improvement compared to day 7, with increased condylar bone density and enhanced cortical bone continuity, potentially due to adaptive remodeling of the condyle. Following DA-HAMA hydrogel intervention, the extent of condylar bone destruction significantly improved. Cortical bone integrity was partially restored, and subchondral bone density increased. The H DA-HAMA hydrogel group demonstrated more pronounced effects at 14 days, exhibiting smoother and more continuous condylar surfaces, with treatment outcomes superior to those observed in rats treated for 7 days. Analysis of bone microstructural parameters further confirmed that DA-HAMA hydrogel significantly improved indicators including BV/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Sp, and Tb.Th (Fig. 6C). L DA-HAMA hydrogel only significantly affected BV/TV, whereas H DA-HAMA hydrogel improved all 4 parameters, indicating clear concentration-dependent efficacy.

Histological analysis of condyles and surrounding synovial tissues from TMJ samples revealed that CFA modeling induced marked synovial thickening, increased inflammatory cell infiltration in both quantity and depth, along with articular cartilage erosion and loss of cartilage matrix (Fig. 6E and G), definitively establishing the TMJOA model. However, DA-HAMA hydrogel treatment significantly alleviated synovial inflammatory responses, suppressing both the number and extent of inflammatory cell infiltration. Synovial tissue swelling was reduced, while chondrocyte and cartilage matrix content increased. This treatment also lowered synovitis scores and OARSI scores (Fig. 6F and H). Furthermore, the condylar cartilage structure in the 14-day DA-HAMA hydrogel treatment group showed greater improvement than the 7-day group, with enhanced condylar surface cartilage thickness and more orderly chondrocyte alignment, suggesting a time-dependent manner of DA-HAMA hydrogel therapy efficacy.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying DA-HAMA hydrogel treatment for TMJOA, immunofluorescence staining was employed to assess iNOS and GPX4 expression levels in inflamed synovial tissue, with macrophages labeled by CD68 (Fig. 6E and G). Results showed that CD 68+ macrophages highly colocalized with iNOS expression in the CFA group, indicating that inflammatory macrophages are the primary source of iNOS. Following DA-HAMA hydrogel treatment, iNOS expression in synovial tissue was significantly reduced, with decreased overlap at CD68+ macrophage sites, indicating that DA-HAMA hydrogel significantly suppresses iNOS overexpression in inflammatory macrophages. Similarly, DA-HAMA hydrogel injection treatment significantly upregulated GPX4 expression within inflammatory macrophages. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that DA-HAMA hydrogel downregulated iNOS and upregulated GPX4 in a DA concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 6F and H).

In summary, DA-HAMA hydrogel effectively treats TMJOA by regulating inflammatory responses and ferroptosis processes. It achieves this through suppressing iNOS expression in synovial macrophages of TMJOA and promoting GPX4 upregulation, thereby improving the intra-articular environment.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed a light-curable HAMA hydrogel delivery system loaded with SAT1 inhibitor DA, and systematically investigated its therapeutic efficacy and mechanism of action against OA. Material characterization revealed that DA incorporation did not alter the hydrogel's porous structure, degradation behavior, or rheological properties, indicating excellent structural integrity and stability of the drug delivery system. In vitro experiments confirmed that DA released from the hydrogel effectively suppressed macrophage inflammatory responses and blocked ferroptosis. In CFA-induced mouse knee OA and rat TMJOA models, intra-articular injection of this hydrogel demonstrated significant therapeutic effects: leveraging its sustained drug release capability, it effectively alleviated synovial inflammation, improved subchondral bone destruction, enhanced bone microstructural parameters (e.g., BV/TV, Tb.N), and significantly reduced histopathological scores. Further immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the hydrogel achieves synergistic anti-inflammatory and anti-ferroptotic effects in vivo by regulating the expression levels of iNOS and GPX4 in synovial macrophages. This study confirms that this novel hydrogel delivery system possesses excellent stability and targeted regulatory capabilities, effectively delaying OA progression by improving the joint microenvironment, offering a promising materials-based solution for OA treatment.
