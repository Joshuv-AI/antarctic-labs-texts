Multi-Genome-Wide association studies provide new insights into the genetic architecture of Varroa resistance in honeybees
Authors: Peymaneh Davoodi, Mohammad Razmkabir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Varroa destructor represents one of the most significant global threats to honeybee colonies. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) play a crucial role in identifying genetic markers associated with Varroa resistance in honeybees. Six scenarios of genome-wide association studies (additive, dominance, and epistatic for each case‒control and quantitative approach) were implemented to analyze SNP markers associated with hygienic behavior in worker bees.ResultsIn additive GWAS (case‒control), 10 SNPs were identified, whereas additive GWAS (quantitative) revealed 23 SNPs. For the dominance GWAS, 2 SNPs were found in the case‒control study, and 13 SNPs were found in the quantitative analysis. Moreover, the epistatic GWAS (case‒control) identified 10 paired SNPs, and the epistasis GWAS (quantitative) revealed 13 paired SNPs associated with uncapping behavior. These scenarios, along with some overlapping findings, highlighted unique SNPs. The results demonstrate that the type of data definition and genetic model used significantly influence the identification of SNPs associated with the uncapping trait. Additionally, combining multiple methods enhances SNP detection and provides deeper insights into the genetic architecture of Varroa resistance. The identified SNPs were distributed across the genome and presented distinct features from the perspective of minor allele frequency across different models. Furthermore, enrichment analysis revealed that genes related to these SNPs are predominantly involved in pathways related to the stress response and immunity.ConclusionsTogether, our research provides new insights into the genetics underlying varroa resistance in honeybees. By leveraging genomic data and different GWAS models, this study contributes significantly to the understanding of the genetic architecture of varroa resistance.

Background

The domestication of honeybees (Apis mellifera L.) for honey production and other hive products has been accompanied by persistent threats from parasites, among which the ectoparasitic mite Varroa destructor is one of the most destructive [1, 2]. Since its first report in Apis cerana in 1904 [3] and subsequent infection of A. mellifera in the mid-20th century, V. destructor has spread globally, becoming a major driver of colony losses in apiculture [1, 4]. The mite’s impact on honeybee health has prompted intensive research into resistance mechanisms and breeding strategies. Resistance to V. destructor in honeybees is known to involve behavioral traits such as grooming, brood removal, and Varroa Sensitive Hygiene (VSH), in which worker bees detect, uncap, and remove infested brood, thereby disrupting mite reproduction, and studies suggest a genetic basis for these behaviors [5–7]. Recent studies have indicated that varroa resistance is heritable and polygenic, involving multiple genes with small effects rather than a few genes with major effects [8, 9]. Additionally, honeybees with higher levels of Varroa Sensitive Hygiene (VSH) exhibit superior responses to V. destructor mites by actively locating, uncapping, and removing infested brood, thereby disrupting the mite’s reproductive cycle and suppressing colony mite populations [10].

Hygienic behaviors such as uncapping, auto and allogrooming, and removing affected broods are natural defenses against Varroa mites in honeybees, which form social immunity in the colony and indeed should be considered an innate behavior [11–16]. Additional traits, including Mite Non-Reproduction (MNR), suppressed mite reproduction (SMR), and recapping behavior, have been identified as heritable to varying degrees (heritability estimates 0.18–0.44) and are therefore promising targets for selective breeding [17–19].

According to the concept of pleiotropy, genome-wide association studies on Drosophila melanogaster have identified hundreds of genomic markers associated with behavioral traits [20]. The resistance of Asian bees to mites may be due to their well-developed behavioral defense mechanisms [21]. Moreover a research revealed notable genetic differences between Pol-line and Hilo populations, attributed to closed breeding programs designed to improve hygienic behavior, a trait enabling bees to identify and remove infected or parasitized brood [22].

In general, two sources of variation influencing hygienic behavior in honeybees have been reported: genetic sources, which include genes and polymorphisms that regulate this innate behavior, and environmental sources, which transform the effects of ecological stimuli into alterations in hygienic patterns in honeybees [23].

Intraspecific hygienic patterns can vary with caste, breed, age, gut microbiota, and season, suggesting that hygienic behavior may function as a complex and highly adaptive trait as a form of communication or colony-wide resilience strategy in honeybees [24, 25]. The dynamic interplay of biological and environmental factors turns hygienic behavior into more than just a survival mechanism; hence, it becomes a sophisticated signal influencing colony function, stability, and resistance against exotic invaders [25–27].

Advances in genomic tools, particularly genome wide association studies (GWAS), have facilitated the identification of genetic markers linked to Varroa resistance traits [22, 28–30]. Notably, eight genomic variants associated with drone brood resistance (DBR) to V. destructor have been identified, showing distinct allele frequency patterns across European honeybee populations [31]. Another study has also highlighted quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with Varroa resistance [32]. Furthermore, population-wide modeling has identified eight SNPs across seven genes associated with suppressed mite reproduction (SMR), a trait validated in multiple honeybee populations [31, 33, 34]. In addition, a specific SNP (SNP 9–9224292) on chromosome 9 has been correlated with VSH, which has been validated in both North American and New Zealand honeybee populations [35].

Marker-assisted selection (MAS) has shown promise in accelerating the development of resistant populations, with predictive models achieving up to 76% classification accuracy for DBR in some breeding programs, increasing the frequency of related genes or markers in the population [22, 31, 35]. Therefore, the integration of molecular markers into breeding programs enhances the selection process, with a focus on traits such as hygienic behavior and resistance to Varroa infestation. However, most studies have focused on additive genetic models, potentially neglecting the roles of dominance effects and epistatic interactions in shaping complex resistance traits [36–39]. Despite these advances, the genetic architecture of Varroa resistance remains incompletely understood, particularly regarding the interplay between multiple loci, and gene–gene interactions.

This research aims to understand the genetic architecture of the Varroa resistance trait, explore associated genomic markers through the GWAS approach, integrate additive, dominance, and epistatic GWAS signals from two case‒control and quantitative models of uncapping behavior, and pinpoint functional genes.

Methods

Experiment data

Experimental individuals were sourced predominantly (~ 80%) from a Varroa-tolerant breeding line maintained since 1997 at the Institute for Bee Research in Hohen Neuendorf, Germany, with the remainder obtained from German breeders who select for Varroa tolerance based on breeding values. All bees originated from a controlled cross-breeding program targeting variation in the Varroa‑specific defense trait of uncapping infested brood. 22,000 worker bees were individually labeled with number tags. Colonies were kept in observation hives with glass walls, allowing direct visual access to the comb. The hives were video-recorded continuously in front of an infrared camera over 7 days, so every tagged bee’s behavior could be tracked over time. Subsequently cases and controls were selected over two years, for genotyping categorized as A871 (91 bees) in year one and A1173 (128 bees) in year two. These worker bees originated from 10 distinct labeled queens (families) [40, 41].

SNP genotyping via an Affymetrix 44 K SNP array aligned to the honeybee reference genome assembly Amel_4.5 and a special behavior bioassay using video monitoring phenotyped of uncapping behavior of single worker bees deposited on the Dryad repository (10.5061/dryad.8635cs4h) was used [40, 41] for the current study to perform multigenome-wide association studies (multi-GWASs) to assess defensive behavior against V. destructor.

Data Preparation and quality control

The phenotypic and genotypic data of 219 worker bees from two years were merged and converted into a readable format in PLINK 1.9 [42]. The SNP array with 32,632 SNPs was loaded in PLINK for the quality control task. A total of 1254 variants and 29 sample bees were removed because of missing genotyping data (0.99), 1408 variants were removed because of the Hardy‒Weinberg exact test with a threshold of 10−−10, 12,082 variants were removed because of minor allele frequency with a threshold of 0.05, 2791 variants were removed because of monomorphic issues, finally 15,097 variants and 190 sample bees passed all the QC filters to be included in the further statistical analyses. Among the remaining phenotypes, 116 were cases, and 74 were controls in case-control definition of uncapping phenotype.

Analysis of the Identical-by-State (IBS) matrix

To assess genetic similarity among individual honey bees, we calculated pairwise Identity by State (IBS) metrics using genome-wide single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genotype data. SNPs located on autosomal chromosomes were selected to avoid potential biases associated with sex-linked loci. For each SNP, genotypic comparisons between all possible pairs of individuals were performed using PLINK v1.9, which assigns IBS scores based on allele sharing: IBS = 2 when both alleles are identical, IBS = 1 when one allele is shared, and IBS = 0 when no alleles are shared. These per-SNP IBS scores were aggregated across all non-missing loci for each pair of individuals and normalized by the total number of valid comparisons, yielding a proportion that reflects overall genetic similarity. The resulting output was a symmetric N × N similarity matrix, where N represents the number of individuals in the dataset, and each cell denotes the genome-wide IBS value for a given pair. This matrix served as the basis for downstream analyses of population structure, relatedness, and clustering [42–44].

Genome-Wide additive association study (case‒control and quantitative) (ACC and AQ)

In both case–control and quantitative GWASs (ACC and AQ), the additive model assumes that each additional copy of a given allele has a constant effect on the trait [45]. Thus, genotypes are coded as 0, 1, and 2, representing the number of copies of the causative allele, ensuring that the heterozygote’s effect is exactly midway between the two extreme homozygotes. Additive GWASs are both conceptually straightforward and statistically efficient, providing the underlying true genetic architecture following an additive pattern.

A case‒control association study, with the assumption of a threshold model, was performed in PLINK 1.9 beta [42]. In this study, the hygienic behavior of honeybees was of particular interest. Therefore, individuals with the ability to detect and uncap (3 or 4 and more observations of uncapped) infected cells were defined as the control group or healthy, whereas those with lower hygienic activity (0 or 1 observation of uncapped) were considered the case group or disease [41]. A quantitative model with codes 0, 1, 4, and more (number of uncapped cells by each worker bee) was analyzed alongside qualitative classifications of cases and controls to explore the potential phenotype‒genotype associations. Logistic and linear regressions were used for case‒control and for quantitative analysis of the same trait under the additive assumption, respectively.

Genome-Wide dominant association study (case‒control and quantitative) (DCC and DQ)

Unlike the additive model, which assumes that the trait effect increases linearly with every additional copy of the risk allele, the dominance model tests whether individuals carrying at least one copy of a risk or causative allele differ from individuals with no copies. In a dominant model, both heterozygotes and variant homozygotes are treated as carrier groups. The reference group consists of individuals homozygous for the non-risk (non-causative) allele [42].

Genome-Wide epistatic association study (case‒control and quantitative) (ECC and EQ)

In epistatic GWAS approaches, the fundamental issue is to detect interactions, beyond simple additive effects, between pairs of SNPs [42, 46]. This approach was applied to both case‒control (binary) and quantitative (continuous) views of uncapping infected cells. Logistic and linear regression analyses, excluding covariates, were conducted for epistatic case‒control and quantitative models, respectively, to examine marker epistatic associations.

The PVE here reflects narrow-sense heritability. Bioinformatics analysis

The NCBI genome browser, which uses the genome assemblies Amel_4.5 and Amel_HAv3 of Apis mellifera, with the GenBank accession number GCA_003254395.2, was used in downstream analyses. Since weak linkage disequilibrium (LD) was observed at the genomic level of the studied honeybee population through the high recombination rate in the honeybee genome [49], a distance of 200 kb surrounding significant SNPs was considered to find genes, and the closest genes were selected so that the SNP would be able to capture nearby genes according to the existing LD pattern [50]. As the bees in Spotter et al. (2016) originated from structured breeding lines, where selection can maintain LD over much larger distances. In this context, an LD threshold of r² ≈ 0.2 corresponded to a physical span of roughly ± 200 kb, meaning SNPs within this range could still be moderately correlated with causal variants. The relatively large window also compensated for the uneven marker spacing of the 44 K SNP array (~ 30–40 kb on average) and reduced the risk of missing candidate genes not directly tagged by genotyped SNPs. Thus, despite the species’ high recombination rate, the ± 200 kb window represented a deliberate balance between biological reality, population structure, and the practical resolution limits of the dataset.

The list of discovered genes was then submitted to the STRING database version 12.0 for conducting functional protein association networks and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [51]. The BioCircos R package was used to display the results of SNP-level significant epistatic interactions across the honeybee (Apis mellifera) genome [52].

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Population structure was assessed using the first four principal components (PCs), which explained 8.8%, 6.7%, 6.5%, and 6.0% of the genetic variance, respectively. Together, they accounted for 28% of the variation and revealed subtle clustering patterns (Fig. 1). These PCs were included as covariates in all additive and dominance models to reduce genomic inflation (λ). Additional components were excluded, as they added complexity without improving λ [53].

The best model detection

In this stage of analysis, six models with different numbers of covariates were analyzed to determine the best model on the basis of the Q‒Q plot and inflation index. As a result, the model with covariates of the queen effect, year, and first four PCs (Fig. 3) was determined to be the better model with the lowest possible inflation rate of 1.3 for inclusion in further scenarios of this study (Fig. f3).

Genetic contribution across GWAS models

To assess the genetic architecture underlying the trait of uncapping behavior, the proportion of variance explained (PVE) was calculated using both additive and non-additive models. All significant signals involved 94 SNPs, with a combined PVE of 0.2757, indicating a substantial overall genetic contribution. The Additive Quantitative (AQ) model identified 23 significant SNPs, with the highest total PVE of 0.1469 and an average PVE of 0.0064, showing a strong cumulative effect on continuous traits under additive assumptions. In the Additive Case-Control (ACC) model, 10 SNPs were found, with a total PVE of 0.0123 and an average PVE of 0.0012. In the non-additive models, the Dominant Quantitative (DQ) identified 13 SNPs, with a total PVE of 0.050 and an average of 0.004. The Dominant Case-Control (DCC) model detected only 2 SNPs, with a total PVE of 0.006 and an average PVE of 0.003. The Epistatic Quantitative (EQ) model revealed 13 paired SNPs, contributing a total PVE of 0.0482 and an average of 0.0018. The Epistatic Case-Control (ECC) model also identified 10 paired SNPs, with a total PVE of 0.0123, but the lowest average PVE of 0.0006 (Fig. 9).

Gene network and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

In the post-GWAS stage, the obtained target genes were further analyzed. While researchers have made significant progress in understanding honeybee genetics, many uncharacterized genes have been identified. Studies on the structure and function of honeybee genes are ongoing.

Since more than half of the genes associated with uncapping behavior were identified in this study, these genes were not included in the String database. To establish connections among these genes and construct a cohesive gene‒gene network, 40 auxiliary genes were incorporated, including even weaker gene interactions in the network analysis.

A gene‒gene network comprising 103 nodes and 307 edges was constructed, featuring an average node degree of 5.96, an average local clustering coefficient of 0.6, and a PPI enrichment p value exceeding 1.0e-16 (Fig. 13).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was applied to GWAS signals to identify enrichment for genetic associations with uncapping behavior, the basis of varroa resistance in honeybees (Fig. 14). The significant association of a particular gene set with uncapping behavior was calculated by testing gene sets with more than two genes and P values less than or equal to 0.01.

Five top biological processes (BP) comprising “protein targeting to mitochondrion” (with 7 genes), “protein import into the mitochondrial matrix” (with 5 genes), “protein targeting” (with 8 genes), “cellular response to dopamine” (with 5 genes), and “G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway” (with 10 genes) were enriched for 103 Varroa resistance-associated genes (Fig. 14a). The top five annotated keywords enriched in Varroa resistance-associated genes included “Antibiotic” (3 genes), “Defensin” (2 genes), “Immunoglobulin domain” (4 genes), “Transducer” (8 genes), and “G protein-coupled receptor” (4 genes) (Fig. 14b).

Moreover, the enriched reactome and KEGG pathways were “mitophagy” (5 genes), “signaling by GPCR” (10 genes), “PINK1-PRKN-mediated mitophagy” (4 genes), and “G beta: Gamma signaling through CDC42” (4 genes), and “Tol and lmd signaling” (10 genes) (Fig. 14c and d). These pathways play crucial roles in cellular signaling, survival, and response to stress in honeybees.

Discussion

In the additive GWASs, 10 SNPs and 23 SNPs were identified using case-control and quantitative methods, having the highest influence on uncapping behavior respectively (Fig. 2; Tables 2 and 3). Only five overlapping SNPs were discovered between the two scenarios of additive GWASs. Identifying unique SNPs via each approach highlights the potential importance of using multiple methodologies to capture a more comprehensive genetic architecture.

The significant AMB-01061069 SNP from additive GWAS (case‒control) is located in an intronic region of the LOC725189 gene and upstream of the LOC113219409 gene, which is a putative gene related to neural development and may be involved in the immune response [50]. The AMB-00222976 SNP is located in an intronic region of the LOC410009 gene, which is associated with metabolic regulation. Furthermore, among related genes with significant SNPs, the LOC413577 gene may play a role in olfactory perception, and the LOC724146 gene is linked to behavioral traits in honeybees [54]. The Rx gene is predicted to be related to developmental processes and hormone regulation. While the LOC113218596 gene is potentially involved in stress response mechanisms, the LOC408322 gene may contribute to colony communication, and the LOC412741 gene seems to play a possible role in sensory processing [50].

The AMB-00017354 SNP discovered via additive GWAS is located in an intronic region of the Or19 gene related to odorant receptors in honeybees and other insects and plays a crucial role in olfactory perception. In honeybees (Apis mellifera), odorant receptors contribute to worker bees detecting pheromones; floral scents; behaviors such as foraging, mating, and hive defenses; and environmental signals essential for colony communication [55].

Alongside, the labial (lab) gene in insects is part of the Hox gene family, which plays a crucial role in body segmentation and development. While its primary function is related to head formation, some studies suggest that Hox genes may also influence immune responses and defense mechanisms in insects [56]. The genes A0A087ZZW4 and A0A087ZZC4 are protein-coding genes with unknown functions in honeybees. Also, the significant AMB-00154897 SNP is located in an intronic region of the 5-HT7 gene encoding a serotonin receptor, which is crucial for neurophysiological processes in honeybees and other insects. Serotonin receptors are linked to behavioral traits such as learning, memory, and stress response, and they may also influence immune system regulation by modulating inflammatory pathways [57].

In dominance GWASs of case‒control and quantitative methods, two and 13 SNPs were discovered respectively showing a significant influence on uncapping behavior in dominance underlying genetics (Fig. 5; Table 3). Notably, no overlapping SNPs were detected between the two dominance GWAS scenarios. These results indicate that different GWAS models, such as the case‒control dominance model versus the quantitative dominance model, are sensitive to distinct aspects of genetic variation. Furthermore, the absence of overlapping SNPs suggests that each approach may uncovers a different portion of the genetic architecture of the studied trait. The case‒control study may have accentuated alleles with pronounced differences between the binary states of the case and control groups, whereas the quantitative approach captures finer gradient effects across the phenotype. This divergence is a common scenario in studies of complex traits, where multiple genetic and regulatory mechanisms intertwine [58–60].

The two genes LOC413383 and LOC100576155 were derived from a dominant GWAS (case controls) are predicted to be potentially involved in neural development and the regulation of the immune response, respectively [50]. Several of the genes related to the significant SNPs from the dominant GWAS (quantitative) are uncharacterized with unknown functions in honeybees, including A0A088A9H1, A0A088A5W4, LOC551753, SCR-B5, and A0A088AD25 [50].

Among these candidate genes, the LOC413553 and A0A087ZQF6 may contribute to stress adaptation and stress response mechanisms. The genes A0A088ANB6, LOC408343, and A0A088ALG7 are predicted to be associated with behavioral traits and colony defense in honeybees. The remaining genes, such as LOC410671, A0A088A4T2, A0A088AQT0, and A0A088ALG9, are potentially involved in metabolic regulation, neural development, the immune response, and sensory processing, respectively according NCBI information [50].

In the epistatic GWASs of case-control and quantitative models, 10 and 13 SNP pairs, respectively, were identified as significantly associated with uncapping behavior in worker bees (Fig. 6; Tables 4 and 5) Notably, 8 SNPs overlapped between these two scenarios of epistatic GWASs. In the binary model, significant epistatic interaction terms suggest that the combined effect of the two SNPs modifies the odds of being a case, deviating from the expected multiplicative outcome [42, 46].

In agreement with current study also the previous studies have reported that resistance can be influenced by additive and epistatic interactions between loci, complicating selective breeding efforts in bee breeding [61, 62]. As well as, previously, a QTL analysis utilizing 216 heterozygous microsatellite markers identified three potential target regions on chromosomes 4, 7, and 9, which influence the suppression of Varroa destructor reproduction through epistasis in drone bees [62].

Moreover, another study revealed that epistatic interactions among multiple loci are important for maintaining the genetic diversity of resistance to Varroa mites in haploid honeybee drones. They identified three loci on chromosomes 2, 13, and 15 that collectively account for over 45% of the phenotypic variation in Varroa resistance [61]. Genome-wide epistatic association studies play crucial roles in understanding the complex genetic architecture of the honeybee (Apis mellifera). Traditional GWASs often focus on single-locus effects, but epistatic GWASs capture interactions between multiple loci, offering a more comprehensive view of how genetic variants influence traits [46, 63]. Figure 6 shows that nonsignificant SNPs scattered throughout the genome can have a significant effect on the trait of interest in an epistatic study, both from a binary and quantitative perspective.

For more detail in epistatic candidate genes, we should illustrate that the APIM_APIME gene from epistatic GWAS encodes apisimin, which is a serine-valine-rich peptide in honeybee royal jelly. It plays an important role in honeybee nutrition, but its function is not limited to larval development, as high levels of mRNA are detected in both forager and nurse honeybees [64, 65]. With respect to its connection to Varroa mites, there is no direct evidence linking apismin to Varroa resistance. However, royal jelly proteins, including apisimin, contribute to colony health and immune responses [66], which may indirectly affect honeybee resilience against Varroa infestations.

Likewise, some studies have indicated that the cytochrome P450 gene and odorant receptor gene may play a role in detecting diseased broods before uncapping and removal in honeybees [23, 67].

The epistatic interactions indicate that epistatic genes can be distributed across the entire genome. These genes are believed to exert epistatic effects on others, even across different chromosomes. Unregulated connections may alter gene expression in a nonrandom manner, potentially leading to unexpected relationships between the genotype and the phenotype of interest [68].

Interestingly, all significant epistatic SNP pairs detected in both the case‒control and quantitative models represented interchromosomal epistasis, where genes on different chromosomes interact to influence phenotypic variation. Only one case of intrachromosomal epistasis involving AMB-00036989 and AMB-00508244 SNPs, which are 1,503,805 bp apart on chromosome 12, was identified (Figs. 7 and 8). Previous studies have reported examples of interchromosomal and intrachromosomal epistasis in various species, such as Drosophila, chicken, and mouse [46, 69, 70]. Even in social organisms such as honeybees, intraspecific intergenomic epistasis or intergenomic epistasis between the host and parasite may occur [71]. Such events significantly complicate the occurrence of a trait such that a purely additive approach to predicting the expected trait will be highly biased.

Venn networks created with EVenn illustrated the scenario-specific (quantitative or case-control) and overlapping SNPs and genes among the various scenarios [72]. The scenario-specific and overlapping SNPs and genes among the scenarios enable a deeper understanding of genetic interactions and their contributions to the studied trait. This approach facilitates the identification of potential biomarkers and resistance targets, offering valuable insights into the complex genetic architecture of uncapping behavior as a basis of Varroa resistance. It can be concluded that variations in GWAS signals derived from additive, dominant, and epistatic analyses may be conceptually connected to the Omnigenic model. This model proposes that complex traits are influenced by an extensive network of interacted genes (all genes), not just a few major loci with just additive effect [46, 73–75]. It might even go beyond Omnigenic and propose a new paradigm of the Omnifactor model. This Omnifactor model could suggest that not only are complex traits shaped by a vast network of genes, but also by intricate interactions among environmental factors, genome-by-genome interactions, and social alterations. Such a paradigm would emphasize the dynamic interplay between genetic and non-genetic contributors, offering a more holistic perspective on trait formation which suggest to assess in future research on social spices.

Interestingly, the candidate genes identified in this study showed no overlap with those reported in previous genomic association studies on honeybees, except for the gene LOC724405, which was identified by both epistatic GWAS models [76]. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors, including the polygenic nature of the traits analyzed, variations in population diversity or genetic background, differences in phenotyping methods, trait definitions, and interaction norms [77].

Although each analysis is valuable and has its own achievements, the integration of different GWASs has potential to capture results in a comprehensive genetic view because combining these approaches ensures that all types of genetic influences are considered [38, 39]. The evaluation of multiple genetic effects refines models used to predict phenotypes, leading to more precise estimations, especially where complex traits follow polygenic or Omnigenic inheritance [46, 73, 78]. Moreover, the integration of different GWAS approaches allows the identification of key potential genetic contributors to disease resistance, particularly when resistance traits are not affected by additive genes alone but rather when dominance and epistasis are influential [79].

Furthermore, understanding genetic interactions can help breeders select honeybees with desirable traits more effectively. Therefore, no single analytical method can capture the full picture of the genetic architecture of a complex trait such as varroa resistance or uncapping behavior. By leveraging multiple GWAS approaches, we would be able to identify a broader range of candidate genetic markers.

The SNPs detected by additive models, particularly AQ, contributed significantly to the overall genetic signal, accounting for more than half of the total PVE despite representing less than one-third of the significant SNPs (Fig. 9). This highlights the efficiency of additive frameworks, especially for quantitative traits. In contrast, non-additive models, while collectively contributing a substantial number of SNPs (61 out of 94), exhibited lower total and mean PVEs, suggesting that their individual effects are weaker or more diffuse. Nevertheless, their cumulative contribution remains valuable, especially for capturing complex genetic interactions. These findings support the strategic prioritization of additive effects in the design of low-density selective chips, where maximizing explanatory power with fewer markers is essential. Non-additive signals, while less impactful individually, may still enhance model robustness when integrated appropriately.

Furthermore, by examining the significant SNPs derived from different scenarios from the perspective of MAF values, a specific pattern was observed. The pattern suggests that the SNP association can vary significantly depending on the analytical model and the type of data being used (binomial or quantitative). The additive and dominant models in quantitative analyses may prioritize rare variants due to their subtle contributions to the trait, which might be more detectable in these frameworks. On the other hand, the epistatic model and case‒control approaches appear to discover common SNPs with higher frequencies in the population, likely because such variants exhibit stronger and more consistent associations in these contexts. Understanding these differences is crucial for designing studies and interpreting results in genetic association research now and in future.

In our current data structure, the dominance model seems to be more sensitive to data type than the additive and epistatic association models. Also, a binary view of a particular trait has less detectability than a quantitative view.

In Post-GWAS and GSE analysis, 5 Reactome and 1 KEGG pathways were enriched, which are together involved in varroa resistance in our studied worker bees (Fig. 14). Mitophagy is a pathway that is responsible for the selective degradation of damaged mitochondria, maintaining cellular health and energy efficiency in flies [80]. G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling is a major mechanism for cell communication that regulates diverse processes, such as neurotransmission, immune responses, and hormone activity [80–82]. PINK1-PRKN mitophagy is a stress-activated pathway that involves the proteins PINK1 and Parkin (PRKN) and is crucial for preventing mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly in the context of neuronal health in fly models [83, 84]. G beta: Gamma signaling through CDC42 influences cell migration and cytoskeletal rearrangement and is often linked to developmental processes and immune responses in other species [85]. The only enriched KEGG pathway was the “Toll and Imd signaling pathway,” (Fig. 14d), which is responsible for the immune response in social insects [86, 87]. Therefore, reaching biological pathways with direct and indirect involvement to the nature of behavioral traits underlying Varroa resistance to some extent indicates that the identified genes can be suitable candidates for future studies.

It should be considered that despite the inherent challenges associated with the study of Apis mellifera, including its intricate social structure and the strong influence of uncontrollable environmental variables, particularly on behavioral traits, our investigation provides critical insights into honeybee genetics. The queen’s polyandrous mating behavior and the undefined identity of drone contributors add considerable genetic complexity, which, while a source of variability, enriches the genetic diversity available for analysis and affects the LD pattern. In this regard, due to the low LD typically observed in honeybee populations, gene annotation for the identified markers was limited to those located within a 200 kb window of each marker. As a result, only the nearest genes within this defined genomic range were reported. Moreover, technical limitations, such as the relatively modest sample size and the variable quality of genotyping data, reflect common constraints in entomological research and honeybee trait recording. Nevertheless, by leveraging the most informative part of available data, employing a stringent quality control process, assigning potential covariates, and performing six different robust analytical strategies, we demonstrate the feasibility of extracting meaningful outputs even from limited datasets. Additionally, although the genome-wide epistatic analysis using PLINK did not allow for covariate adjustment and was constrained to SNP effects, this approach offers a foundational view of potential genetic interactions. We acknowledge that larger sample sizes would enhance statistical power for epistasis detection, yet our findings lay important groundwork for future studies and highlight the value of multi-angle genetic investigation in non-model organisms.

Conclusion

We investigated various genomic association models for the first time to identify SNPs and genes associated with complex trait of Varroa resistance through hygienic behavior in honeybees. The models produced differing outputs, with some overlap in results. The definition of the phenotype significantly influenced the outcomes. Additionally, the models identified SNPs with different features based on minor allele frequencies. Among the models, the dominance GWAS was more sensitive to the type of phenotype than the other models, and no overlap was seen in their results. However, the integration of multiple GWASs offered a more comprehensive understanding of the markers associated with Varroa resistance and its genetic underlying. The differences in GWAS signals obtained from additive, dominance, and epistatic analyses can be conceptually linked to the Omnigenic model, which suggests that complex traits are influenced by an extensive network of genes, not just a few major loci. Finally, we acknowledge that high quality genotyping data and larger sample sizes would enhance statistical power for association signal detection; nonetheless, our findings provide crucial foundational insights for future research and underscore the importance of multi-GWASs in non-model organisms.
