Online Spectral Density Estimation
Shahriar Hasnat Kazi

Abstract
This paper develops the first online algorithms for estimating the spectral density function — a fundamental object of interest in time series analysis — that satisfies the three core requirements of streaming inference: fixed memory, fixed computational complexity, and temporal adaptivity. Our method builds on the concept of forgetting factors, allowing the estimator to adapt to gradual or abrupt changes in the data-generating process without prior knowledge of its dynamics. We introduce a novel online forgetting-factor periodogram and show that, under stationarity, it asymptotically recovers the properties of its offline counterpart. Leveraging this, we construct an online Whittle estimator, and further develop an adaptive online spectral estimator that dynamically tunes its forgetting factor using the Whittle likelihood as a loss. Through extensive simulation studies and an application to ocean drifter velocity data, we demonstrate the method’s ability to track time-varying spectral properties in real-time with strong empirical performance.



Keywords:
Spectral density function, online estimation, forgetting factors, time series analysis

1
Introduction

With data being collected at unprecedented volumes and velocities, the analysis of dynamic, continuous data streams presents challenges that traditional
offline
algorithms cannot address. These algorithms rely on storing and preprocessing large datasets, resulting in prohibitive memory and time requirements that make them unsuitable for scenarios requiring real-time, up-to-date results. In contrast,
online algorithms
process data incrementally, as it arrives, operating under strict constraints of fixed memory and computational complexity while avoiding the need to revisit past data
(
5
,
35
,
55
)
. Beyond the challenges of scale and speed, data streams are also inherently dynamic, as the processes generating the data often evolve over time. This evolving nature introduces a fundamental challenge that online algorithms must also address. For example, if the algorithm is estimating a target quantity of interest from the data, that target may itself be changing over time.

Concept drift refers to changes in time of the data-generating process, which can occur gradually or abruptly. If not addressed, such changes can significantly degrade the performance of estimation algorithms that rely on the assumption of strict or weak stationarity (in the probabilistic sense). To handle this,
temporal adaptivity
becomes essential— the ability of an algorithm to adjust to concept drift while maintaining performance. Importantly, temporal adaptivity must not compromise accuracy and precision when the data-generating process remains stable; under such conditions, the algorithm’s performance should approach that of a stationary estimator. Along with fixed memory and computational efficiency, temporal adaptivity is a core requirement for online algorithms, enabling real-time analysis of dynamic systems.

In this paper, our target of interest is the spectral density function (SDF), a fundamental concept in time series analysis (see
42
for a comprehensive theoretical and methodological overview). Defined as the Fourier transform of the autocovariance sequence of a weakly stationary process, the SDF provides an information-rich representation of a process’s second-order structure in the frequency domain, revealing periodic behaviour and the dominant frequencies driving its variability. This frequency-domain representation is often preferred over its time-domain counterpart, particularly when a process’s variability is linked to underlying physical phenomena or periodic components. As a result, the SDF has been widely used in fields such as astronomy
(
57
)
, cognitive science
(
46
)
, earth sciences
(
39
,
40
)
, electrical engineering
(
18
)
, and finance
(
54
)
. Its estimation remains a topic of significant interest
(
62
,
38
,
10
,
56
)
, and its use is expanding to new domains
(
17
,
30
)
.

While the SDF is defined with respect to stationary processes, in reality, data rarely conforms to this assumption; the spectral properties of the data generating process may gradually drift or undergo abrupt changes. This reality motivated the now mature field of time-frequency analysis, both in the formulation of time series models that emit a time-varying spectrum and in computational methods, including the short-time Fourier transform
(
21
,
63
,
28
)
and the widely celebrated discovery of wavelets
(
31
,
33
)
. However, these and subsequent approaches remain offline and are unsuitable for modern applications in which real-time tracking is needed as new data points arrive. We address this shortfall by developing the first online algorithm for the estimation of the SDF that adheres to the requirements of fixed memory, fixed complexity and temporal adaptivity.

To do so, we take the
forgetting factors
approach, popularised in areas such as change-point detection
(
6
,
44
)
, classification
(
2
,
43
)
, clustering
(
11
,
25
)
, density estimation
(
53
)
, pattern mining
(
4
,
15
)
and model validation
(
27
)
. Forgetting factors are a sequence of scalars taking values in
(0,1]
, controlling the rate at which past data is forgotten. Forgetting factors provide a continuous analogue to hard forgetting approaches, where each data point contributes equally, or not at all, to the estimator
(
3
)
. The most immediate alternative to forgetting factors is a sliding window. However, specifying the size of the window is non-trivial. In fact, a commonality between the approaches to ensure temporal adaptivity introduced in the literature is the requirement of specifying control parameters. The selection of suitable values for these parameters is a non-trivial task and frequently requires prior knowledge of the system, making these approaches unsuitable when no such information is available. This selection task is at the core of the
adaptive
streaming estimation literature. Of particular relevance to this paper are adaptive forgetting factors
(
7
,
44
)
, which we incorporate into our algorithms so that the online spectral estimator can learn a local optima for the forgetting factor based on the present behaviour of the data stream it is analysing.

The periodogram remains the most widely used approach to estimating the SDF and sits at the centre of most other estimation schemes that set about to improve upon its bias and variance properties. This includes direct spectral estimators, in which a data taper is used to control bias. In Section 2, after reviewing these in the traditional offline setting, we present a novel online forgetting-factor periodogram. Although the idea of a sequential algorithm for the estimation of the spectral density of a process has found some development in the literature
(
14
)
, our method differs in its approach, since we tackle the problem from a tapering angle. Importantly, we show that under the assumption of stationarity it asymptotically approaches the same distribution as its offline counterpart, while retaining fixed and finite complexity, with each data point only used once, upon arrival. To simplify the exposition, we present the forgetting-factor periodogram for a process with a known and constant mean; without loss of generality, this can be assumed to be zero. We then show how the forgetting factor periodogram can be readily extended to processes with an unknown and potentially time varying mean.

In the offline setting, the Whittle estimator has been studied and applied extensively
(
50
,
41
,
36
)
for parameteric spectral estimation. The asymptotic distribution of the periodogram is used as a finite data approximation to construct a likelihood for the periodogram in terms of parameters of a specified model (e.g. autoregressive process). This can then be optimised via standard techniques. In Section 3, we use the forgetting-factor periodogram and its derived statistical properties, to develop an online Whittle estimator. In Section 4, we then leverage the Whittle likelihood as a loss function for adaptive forgetting to give an adaptive online spectral estimator.

We evaluate the performance of our online estimators using both simulated and real data. In Section 5, we present a series of simulation experiments that highlight the temporal adaptivity of the algorithms. In Section 6, we apply the method to a time series of drifter velocity data from the Global Drifter Programme, showcasing its capability to track the drifter’s position in real-time.

2
Forgetting Factor Periodogram

2.1
Processes with a known constant mean

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a weakly-stationary time series, meaning that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[X_{t}]

\displaystyle=\mu\quad\quad\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle\text{Cov}[X_{t},X_{t+\tau}]

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}[X_{0},X_{\tau}]\quad\forall\tau\in\mathbb{N}\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{Z}.

Let
s_{\tau}=\text{Cov}[X_{t},X_{t+\tau}]
, for
\tau\in\mathbb{Z}
, be the autocovariance sequence. Assuming
\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty}|s_{\tau}|<\infty
, the SDF
S(f)
of a zero-mean weakly stationary process exists, is continuous and bounded, and is defined as

S(f)=\sum_{\tau=-\infty}^{\infty}s_{\tau}e^{-i2\pi f\tau},\quad f\in(-1/2,1/2].

(1)

The SDF describes the contribution oscillations at frequency
f
have to the overall variance of a random process. While it encodes equivalent information to the autocovariance sequence (the Fourier transform is bijective), it often offers a richer environment in which to study the second-order-structure.

Spectral density estimation methods can be broadly classified as parametric or nonparametric. Parametric approaches are primarily based on autoregressive moving average (ARMA) models, but they tend to give misleading inferences when the parametric model is poorly specified
(
19
,
45
)
. Nonparametric models often rely on the periodogram. For a portion of a zero-mean process
X_{1}
,…,
X_{T}
, the periodogram is defined as

\widehat{S}_{T}(f)=\frac{1}{T}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft}\right|^{2},\quad f\in(-1/2,1/2].

This can be efficiently computed with the fast Fourier transform (FFT). Note that should
\mu
be non-zero but known, the time series must be
centered
(have the mean substracted from it) before the periodogram is computed. Failure to do so will result in a significant spike in the periodogram at
f=0
which can obscure important features.

The periodogram is known to be a biased estimator of the SDF
(
48
)
. This occurs due to a blurring caused by having only a finite sample of the infinite sequence. More specifically,

\mathrm{E}[\widehat{S}_{T}(f)]=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mathcal{F}_{T}(f-f^{\prime})S(f^{\prime})df^{\prime},

where

\mathcal{F}_{T}(f)=\frac{\sin^{2}(T\pi f)}{T\sin^{2}(\pi f)},

is Fejer’s Kernel. This effect is sometimes also referred to as a ‘sidelobe leakage’ caused by the poor frequency concentration properties of
\mathcal{F}_{T}(f)
; for details see
49
. Tapering is a technique used to reduce the kernel’s sidelobes. Given a sequence of real valued constants
\boldsymbol{h}_{T}\equiv(h_{t})_{t=1,...,T}
such that

\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{t}^{2}=1,

the tapered spectral density estimator, often referred to as the direct spectral estimator, is defined as

\widehat{S}_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{h}_{T})=\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{t}X_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft}\right|^{2},\quad f\in(-1/2,1/2].

Analogously, we have

\mathrm{E}[\widehat{S}_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{h}_{T})]=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\mathcal{H}_{T}(f-f^{\prime})S(f^{\prime})df^{\prime},

where
\mathcal{H}_{T}(f)=|\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft}|^{2}
, motivating a choice of taper that has a
\mathcal{H}_{T}(f)
kernel with better frequency concentration than
\mathcal{F}_{T}(f)
. An alternative view of a taper is as a tool to control the influence of each datum to the computation of the spectral density estimate. This is analogous to the sequence of scalars used in the forgetting factor paradigm. For a forgetting factor
\lambda
, the taper is defined

h_{t}=C_{T}(\lambda)^{-1/2}\lambda^{T-t},\quad t=1,...,T

where constant
C_{T}(\lambda)
is chosen such that
\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{t}^{2}=1
, giving

h_{t}=\lambda^{T-t}\sqrt{\frac{1-\lambda^{2}}{1-\lambda^{2T}}},\quad t=1,...,T.

From this, we propose the
forgetting factor periodogram
(FFP)

\widehat{S}_{T}(f;\lambda)=\frac{1}{C_{T}(\lambda)}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda^{T-t}X_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft}\right|^{2},

where
f\in(-1/2,1/2]
and

C_{T}(\lambda)=\frac{1-\lambda^{2T}}{1-\lambda^{2}}.

To show this fulfils the requirements of an online algorithm, define

J_{T}(f;\lambda)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda^{T-t}X_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft},

such that
\widehat{S}_{T}(f;\lambda)=|J_{T}(f;\lambda)|^{2}/C_{T}(\lambda)
. For a new data point
X_{T+1}
,

\displaystyle J_{T+1}(f;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T+1}\lambda^{T+1-t}X_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda^{T-t}X_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft}+X_{T+1}e^{-i2\pi f(T+1)}

\displaystyle=\lambda J_{T}(f;\lambda)+X_{T+1}e^{-i2\pi f(T+1)}

(2)

and, similarly,

C_{T+1}(\lambda)=\sum_{t=1}^{T+1}\lambda^{2(T+1-t)}=\lambda^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda^{2(T-t)}+1=\lambda^{2}C_{T}(\lambda)+1.

(3)

Therefore, to update
\widehat{S}_{T}(f;\lambda)
to
\widehat{S}_{T+1}(f;\lambda)
, we only need to store the values of
J_{T}(f;\lambda)
and
C_{T}(\lambda)
; each datum is used only once to perform computations (
2
) and (
3
), satisfying
O(1)
time complexity. To satisfy
O(1)
memory complexity, we only store the values of
\widehat{S}_{T}(f;\lambda)
at a discrete and fixed
M<\infty
number of frequencies, as is common in offline spectral estimation, which typically uses the FFT algorithm. This means, at any one time, storing
M+1
values:
J_{T}(f;\lambda)
for the
M
frequencies of interest and
C_{T}(\lambda)
.

2.2
Processes with unknown mean

If the mean of the process is unknown, and may even be varying with time, then it needs to be estimated and subtracted from the process before the SDF is estimated. Here, we show that this can be implemented within the forgetting factor regime and does not prevent the derivation of a recursive formula for the DFT and periodogram.

Let us expand the notation for the forgetting factor periodogram,

\displaystyle J_{X,T}(f;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda^{T-t}X_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft},

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{X,T}(f;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{|J_{X,T}(f;\lambda)|^{2}}{C_{T}(\lambda)},

where we now include a subscript to indicate the process that is being operated on. Define
\mu_{X,t}=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]
and
\bar{X}_{t}
as an estimate for
\mu_{X,t}
. We have demonstrated how
\widehat{S}_{X,T}(f;\lambda)
can be computed sequentially. The concern is whether the same is true for
\widehat{S}_{Y,T}(f;\lambda)
, where
\{Y_{t}\}
is a sequentially centred process
Y_{t}=X_{t}-\bar{X}_{t}
. Note that

\displaystyle J_{Y,T}(f;\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda^{T-t}[X_{t}-\bar{X}_{t}]e^{-i2\pi ft},

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda^{T-t}X_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda^{T-t}\bar{X}_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft},

\displaystyle=J_{X,T}(f;\lambda)-J_{\bar{X},T}(f;\lambda).

As shown previously,
J_{X,T}(f;\lambda)
can be updated sequentially and so can
J_{\bar{X},T}(f;\lambda)
as long as
\bar{X}_{T}
allows for a recursive formula. This is indeed the case for the sample mean,

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{T+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T+1}X_{t}}{T+1},

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{T+1}\bar{X}_{T}+\frac{X_{T+1}}{T+1}.

Moreover, the sample mean can be handled with a similar forgetting factor to the periodogram, resulting in a weighted mean,

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{T+1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T+1}\lambda^{T+1-t}X_{t}}{D_{T+1}(\lambda)},

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda D_{T}(\lambda)}{D_{T+1}(\lambda)}\bar{X}_{T}+\frac{X_{T+1}}{D_{T+1}(\lambda)},

where
D_{T+1}(\lambda)=\sum_{t=1}^{T+1}\lambda^{T+1-t}=\lambda D_{T}(\lambda)+1
. While this forgetting factor does not need to be the same as the one used in the computation of the forgetting factor periodogram, the role of the two hyper-parameters as indicators of how fast the process is changing would suggest using the same value as a suitable simplification.

3
Online Whittle Estimation

3.1
Whittle Estimation

The Whittle likelihood for a stationary (not necessarily Gaussian) process is motivated by an asymptotic approximation
(
34
,
61
)
to the distribution of the periodogram, or related non-parametric estimator. In the offline setting, for a centered process, consider

J_{T}(f)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}X_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft},\quad f\in(-1/2,1/2],

such that the periodogram
\hat{S}_{T}(f)=|J_{T}(f)|^{2}/T
. Let
\text{Cum}(X_{t+u_{1}},X_{t+u_{2}},...,X_{t+u_{k-1}},X_{t})
denote the
k
th order cumulant function and assume for all
k\geq 2
,
u_{1},...,u_{k-1}
and
t
that
\text{Cum}(X_{t+u_{1}},X_{t+u_{2}},...,X_{t+u_{k-1}},X_{t})=\text{Cum}(X_{u_{1}},X_{u_{2}},...,X_{u_{k-1}},X_{0})
(i.e. strict-stationarity), in addition to the standard mixing condition
(
8
)
,

\sum_{u_{1}=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{u_{2}=-\infty}^{\infty}...\sum_{u_{k-1}=-\infty}^{\infty}|c_{k}(u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{k-1})|<\infty,

(4)

for all
k\geq 2
, where
c_{k}(u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{k-1})\equiv\text{Cum}(X_{u_{1}},X_{u_{2}},...,X_{u_{k-1}},X_{0})
. Then, for
f\notin\{-1/2,0,1/2\}
,

{T^{-1/2}J_{T}(f)\longrightarrow\mathcal{CN}(0,S(f))},

as
T\longrightarrow\infty
, where
\mathcal{CN}(\mu,\Sigma)
denotes a circularly symmetric complex normal distribution. Furthermore, for Fourier frequencies
f_{k}=k/T
, then
\{T^{-1/2}J(f_{k});k=1,...,\lfloor T/2\rfloor\}
are asymptotically pairwise independent. Hence,
\{\widehat{S}_{T}(f_{k})=T^{-1}|J(f_{k})|^{2};k=1...\lfloor T/2\rfloor\}
behave as asymptotically independent exponential random variables with means
S(f_{k})
, and equivalently,
\{2\widehat{S}_{T}(f_{k})/S(f_{k});k=1...\lfloor T/2\rfloor\}
are asymptotically independent
\chi^{2}_{2}
random variables
(
47
)
.Guided by this, an approximate log-likelihood for
\{\widehat{S}_{T}(f_{k});k=1,...,\lfloor T/2\rfloor\}
is

\mathcal{L}_{T}(S)=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k}\left(\log S(f_{k})+\frac{\widehat{S}_{T}(f_{k})}{S(f_{k})}\right).

(5)

This is the Whittle likelihood approximation. Note that for fixed
S
, as
T\longrightarrow\infty
,

\mathrm{E}\left[\mathcal{L}_{T}(S)\right]\longrightarrow\mathrm{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(S)\right]=-\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\left(\log S(f)+\frac{S^{*}(f)}{S(f)}\right)df,

where
S^{*}
is the true spectrum. Therefore, because
\mathrm{E}[\mathcal{L}(S)]
is maximized at
S=S^{*}
,
\mathcal{L}_{T}(S)
should be a reasonable objective function for the identification of the spectrum. This is a key property of the Whittle likelihood from which consistency results can be developed
(
34
)
. From here on,
Whittle estimation
will refer to the inference procedure where, for a parametric family of SDFs
\{S_{\phi}(f);\phi\in\Phi\}
parameterised by
\phi
in parameter space
\Phi
, the
Whittle estimator
is

\widehat{\phi}_{T}=\underset{\phi\in\Phi}{\operatorname{argmax}}\ \mathcal{L}_{T}(S_{\phi}).

In later sections, we consider the ARMA processes, or more specifically the autoregressive process
\{X_{t}\}
of the form

X_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_{i}X_{t-i}+\epsilon_{t}
where
\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
. Here, the target of inference is
\phi=(\phi_{1},...,\phi_{p},\sigma^{2})
. The Whittle likelihood is useful because it involves
S
directly, in contrast to the exact likelihood, which involves
S
indirectly through the autocovariances. Results for autoregressive moving average models on the higher-order efficiency of maximising (
5
) are provided in
58
and
52
. Similar results for long-memory time series were established by
26
,
20
and
29
in parametric models and by
51
in semiparametric models. In a nonparametric set-up, Whittle likelihood has also been used for automated spline smoothing of the periodogram
(
59
)
, sieve maximum likelihood estimation
(
16
)
, penalized maximum likelihood estimation
(
41
)
, polynomial spline fitting
(
37
)
, local-likelihood-based estimation
(
23
)
and Bayesian estimation of the spectral density function
(
12
)
.

3.2
Forgetting factor Whittle estimation

Having defined the Whittle likelihood in the offline setting as an approximate likelihood for the periodogram, we seek to use the same approximation for the FFP in the online setting. However, we need to show that the limiting distribution of
\widehat{S}_{T}(f;\lambda)
as
T\rightarrow\infty
, is appropriate. In Appendix A, we formally set out and prove a theorem which shows that

\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,S(f))

as
T\rightarrow\infty
, under the same assumptions of strict stationarity and meeting the mixing condition (
4
). Note there is a requirement in the asymptotic framework that the forgetting factor has a time dependency and simultaneously must tend to
1
as
T\rightarrow\infty
; this induces the dependency on a sequence
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}\equiv(\lambda_{t})_{t=1,...,T-1}
of time-varying forgetting factors, defined up until time
(T-1)
. This is needed to have an asymptotically growing sample size, but shows that the Whittle likelihood is a valid approximation in the forgetting regime and further motivates the development of an adaptive forgetting factor periodogram, in which the forgetting factor does increase through time when the process remains stationarity. This is presented in Section
4
where the notation and time-varying forgetting factor estimators for the SDF are further explained and developed.

Remaining in the fixed forgetting factor setting for the moment, define the (fixed) Forgetting Factor Whittle Likelihood as

\mathcal{L}_{T}(S;\lambda)=-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k}\left(\log S(f_{k})+\frac{\widehat{S}_{T}(f_{k};\lambda)}{S(f_{k})}\right),

(6)

such that
\mathcal{L}_{T}(S;1)\equiv\mathcal{L}_{T}(S)
. Given a parametric family of SDFs,
\{S_{\phi}|\phi\in\Phi\}
, define the
Forgetting Factor Whittle Estimator
(FFWE),
\widehat{\phi}_{T}^{(\lambda)}
, as

\widehat{\phi}_{T}(\lambda)=\underset{\phi}{\operatorname{argmax}}\ \mathcal{L}_{T}(S_{\phi};\lambda).

(7)

The advantage of using
\widehat{\phi}_{T}(\lambda)
as the estimator for the parameters of a process is that it inherits the temporal adaptivity of the FFP; the re-weighting of the data occurs when computing
\widehat{S}_{T}(f_{k};\lambda)
. As this quantity is the only way for the data to enter the computation of
\mathcal{L}_{T}(S_{\phi};\lambda)
, the re-weighting automatically adjusts the likelihood and thus the parameter estimate
\widehat{\phi}_{T}(\lambda)
.

Solving (
7
) often involves a non-linear maximization problem. Finding a solution in this way for each new observation violates the complexity constraints for the algorithm to be truly online. We therefore opt to instead use gradient descent to approximate a solution for the
(T+1)^{th}
datum based on the solution for the
T^{th}
datum.

Assume we have a sequence
\{X_{t}\}
and parameter estimates
\{\hat{\phi}_{t}(\lambda);t=1,...,T\}
. For a new observation
X_{T+1}
we approximate
\hat{\phi}_{T+1}(\lambda)
with

\widehat{\phi}_{T+1}(\lambda)=\widehat{\phi}_{T}(\lambda)+r_{\phi}\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{T}\left(S_{\widehat{\phi}_{T}(\lambda)};\lambda\right),

where
r_{\phi}
is the learning rate. Typically, the learning rate for a gradient descent algorithm is used to control the speed at which we want our estimated solution to approach the true solution
(
1
)
. However, in the case of data streams, where the data generating process may undergo some conceptual drift,
r_{\phi}
further serves as a control parameter for how quickly the FFWE can adapt to changes in the process.

4
Adaptive Forgetting Factor Spectral Estimation

This forgetting factor scheme, like many others, suffers from a major drawback: there is no clear way to set
\lambda
a priori. This motivates adaptive forgetting factors where we let an online, adaptive method tune the forgetting factor to the data. The method we use is based on stochastic gradient descent and our construction requires that at time
T
we already have a time-dependent sequence of forgetting factors
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}=(\lambda_{t})_{t=1,...,T-1}
. In practice, a burn-in period is used to initialise this.

Given
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}
have been pre-defined, then our (unnormalised) data-taper becomes

h_{t}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&t=T;\\
\prod_{s=t}^{T-1}\lambda_{s}&t=1,...,T-1;\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

From this, the FFP
\hat{S}_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
becomes

\hat{S}_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})=\frac{1}{C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})}\left|J_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right|^{2}

where
J_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{t}X_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft}
and
C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}h^{2}_{t}
. Using these, the definition of the adaptive forgetting factor Whittle likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{T}(S;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
follows naturally from (
6
). Furthermore, the online update rules for
J_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
and
C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
are derived in an analogous manner to their fixed forgetting factor counterparts, giving

\displaystyle J_{T+1}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{T}J_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})+X_{T+1}e^{i2\pi f(T+1)}

(8)

\displaystyle C_{T+1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{T}C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})+1

(9)

To perform updates (
8
) and (
9
) we first need to define the new time
T
forgetting factor
\lambda_{T}
. To do so, we require a cost function
L_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
off which to learn, and minimize it with one-step gradient descent

\lambda_{T}=\lambda_{T-1}-r_{\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}}L_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}),

(10)

where
r_{\lambda}
is a learning rate parameter.

Consider a real-valued quantity of interest
Y(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
that is a function of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}
and a sequence of observations
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1,...,T+1}
, such that

\displaystyle Y(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{T}Y(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})+Z_{T+1}

(11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{T+1}\left[\prod_{j=i}^{T}\lambda_{j}\right]Z_{i}.

Defining the derivative of
Y(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}
as

\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}}Y(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[Y(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}+\epsilon)-Y(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})\right],

where

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}+\epsilon=(\lambda_{1}+\epsilon,...,\lambda_{T}+\epsilon),

it is shown in
7
that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}}Y(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})=\lambda_{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}}Y(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})+Y(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}).

(12)

The update steps (
8
) and (
9
) are in the form of (
11
). This allows us to take advantage of (
12
), substituting
Y(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
for
J_{T+1}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
and
C_{T+1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
, resulting in the update formulas

\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}}J_{T+1}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})=\lambda_{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}}J_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})+J_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}),

and

\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}}C_{T+1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})=\lambda_{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}}C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})+C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}).

Computing
\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}}J_{T+1}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}}C_{T+1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
allows us to compute
\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}}\widehat{S}_{T+1}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
and thus
\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}}L_{T+1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
for any cost function dependent on
\widehat{S}_{T+1}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
. We are now left with the choice of a suitable cost function to minimize, with the Whittle Likelihood making for an obvious objective function. Including
\mathcal{L}(S_{\widehat{\phi}_{T+1}};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
in the cost function allows us a seamless way to incorporate the performance of the current estimators in the tuning regime. Defining

L_{T+1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})=-\mathcal{L}_{T+1}(S_{\widehat{\phi}_{T+1}};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})
, and making the substitution in (
10
) results in the update step

\lambda_{T+1}=\lambda_{T}+r_{\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}}\mathcal{L}(S_{\widehat{\phi}_{T+1}};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}).

We call this procedure
adaptive forgetting factor Whittle estimation
(AFFWE).

5
Experiments

To assess the performance of the presented online algorithms, we start by looking at signals composed of a single major frequency in noise and show how the FFP is able to track changes in this major frequency with a higher degree of fidelity when compared to the periodogram. We then look at a situation where the interest is in estimating the entire SDF as opposed to focusing on a single major frequency. In particular, we study the performance of the FFWE and the AFFWE when applied to AR(2) processes. An advantage of looking at this specific family of SDFs is that we can draw parallels to the first simulation study on a single frequency signal plus noise due to the fact that some AR(
2
) processes exhibit strong cyclical behaviour around a single frequency. We also present a simulation study on AR(3) processes, to investigate the effect of more parameters in the model.

5.1
Single frequency

Let
\{X_{t}\}
be the random process
X_{t}=\sin(g(t)+\zeta)+\epsilon_{t}
. Here,
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
is a standard Gaussian white noise process,
\zeta\sim U[-\pi,\pi]
, and
g(t)
is a modulating function such that
g^{\prime}(t)\in(0,0.5)
for all
t
. Its derivative
g^{\prime}(t)
is the instantaneous frequency of the process at time
t
. For a process with a single dominant frequency
\eta
, we have
g^{\prime}(t)=\eta
and
g(t)=\eta t
(the constant is absorbed into
\zeta
). The corresponding SDF for such a time series is
S(f;\eta)=\sigma^{2}+k\delta(f-\eta)
, where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta function.
We simulate a process with a changing instantaneous frequency by letting

g^{\prime}(t)=(1+0.6\cdot\sin(\gamma t))/4,

(13)

where
\gamma>0
is a parameter controlling the rate at which the process undergoes concept drift. This results in

g(t)=\frac{t}{4}+\frac{3}{20\gamma}(1-\cos(\gamma t)).

Given an estimate
\hat{S}_{T}(f)
for the SDF
S_{T}(f)
, we define the estimator for the instantaneous frequency
g^{\prime}(T)
as

\hat{g}_{T}=\underset{f}{\operatorname{argmax}}\{\hat{S}_{T}(f)\}.

(14)

Moreover, define
\hat{g}_{T}(\lambda)
as the estimator obtained by using the forgetting factor periodogram
\hat{S}_{T}(f;\lambda)
in (
14
). Figures
1(a)
and
1(b)
show the FFP based estimator when applied to this process, where
\gamma=1/1000
and
\gamma=8/1000
, respectively. Recall that
\lambda=1
is equivalent to using a sequentially updated periodogram.

In Figure
1(a)
we see how the base periodogram struggles to adapt to the changing form of the underlying data generating process and indeed obscures some of the features, such as the trough in the second half of the time series. On the other hand, the two estimates obtained using forgetting factors result in a higher fidelity to the ground truth. We also note that, depending on the value of
\gamma
(how quickly the process is undergoing concept drift), the performance of the two forgetting factors differs substantially. In general, we see that a higher value of
\lambda
(less forgetting) results in the estimate lagging behind the true value. However,
\gamma
influences how closely the estimate tracks the true value of
g^{\prime}(T)
. When concept drift is more pronounced (larger
\gamma
), a smaller value for
\lambda
(more forgetting) produces estimates that better follow the true dynamics. This is expected, since a rapidly changing process naturally benefits from an estimator that adapts quickly by giving less weight to outdated data.

Define the Integrated Squared Error of the estimator
\hat{g}_{T}(\lambda)
as

\text{ISE}(\{\hat{g}_{T}(\lambda)\}|g(\cdot))=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\hat{g}_{T}(\lambda)-g^{\prime}(T)\right]^{2}.

In Figure

\gamma

\gamma

\lambda=1

5.2
Autoregressive Processes

We demonstrate the adaptive forgetting factor approach of Section
4
on autoregressive processes. An AR
(p)
process has the form
\Phi(B)X_{t}=\epsilon_{t}
, where
B
is the backward shift operator
BX_{t}\equiv X_{t-1}
and

\Phi(z)\equiv 1-\phi_{1}z-\phi_{2}z^{2}-...-\phi_{p}z^{p}

is the
characteristic polynomial
of the AR(
p
) process, where
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},...,\phi_{p}
are constants such that
\phi_{p}\neq 0
, and
\{\epsilon_{t}\}
is a Gaussian white noise process with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}

(
9
)
. Given the AR
(p)
process satisfies the stationary constraints that the roots of
\Phi(z)
lie outside of the unit circle, its SDF is

S(f)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\left|1-\phi_{1}e^{-i2\pi f}-\phi_{2}e^{-4\pi if}-...-\phi_{p}e^{-2p\pi if}\right|^{2}}.

In particular, for an AR
(2)
process whose characteristic polynomial
\Phi(z)
has complex conjugate roots
z_{0}
and
z_{0}^{\ast}
, parameterised as
z_{0}=(1/r)\exp(-i\pi f^{\prime}t)
, with
0<r<1
to ensure stationarity, the SDF takes the form
(
9
)

S(f)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(A-2r\cos(2\pi(f^{\prime}+f)))(A-2r\cos(2\pi(f^{\prime}-f)))},

where
A=1+r^{2}
. The spectrum will be at its largest when the denominator is at its smallest. For
r
close to 1 this occurs at
f=\pm f^{\prime}
. Furthermore, as
r
approaches 1 from below, this peak at
\pm f^{\prime}
becomes more pronounced. In this setting, the process is said to have
pseudo-cyclical
behaviour, as opposed to the deterministic cycle showing up as a sharp spike in the previous set of simulations.
Instead of smooth concept drift, we now consider change-points. In Figure
3(a)
and Figure
3(b)
we have the estimates obtained via the Forgetting Factor Whittle Estimator (FFWE) for an AR
(2)
process undergoing a change-point at
t=10000
. These were obtained via 1000 Monte Carlo simulations. The black line indicates the ground truth, whereas all the others are Monte Carlo mean estimates for various values of the forgetting factor
\lambda
. Once again, note the periodogram (
\lambda=1
) struggles to react and adapt to the change-point. Furthermore, there is an apparent trade-off between reaction speed and bias. The estimator for
\lambda=0.9
reacts to the change-point significantly faster than the estimator for
\lambda=0.999
. However, this comes at the cost of increased bias.

In Figures
4(a)
and
4(b)
we apply the Adaptive Forgetting Factor Whittle Estimator (AFFWE) to the same data. A key observation is that the learning rate has very little effect on the performance of the estimators. This provides evidence for the Adaptive Whittle Estimator being significantly more robust to hyperparameter misclassification when compared to the Whittle Estimator with fixed forgetting factors.

An interesting behaviour seen in all four figures is a jump in the estimates for
\phi_{2}
despite the true parameter not undergoing a change-point. This dynamic comes as a result of the shape taken by the Whittle likelihood function of an AR(2) process as more data is observed. Appendix B provides more details on this phenomenon.

Figures
6
and
7
show the algorithms being applied to a AR(3) process. As expected, the variance of our estimates is higher due to increased model complexity.

5.3
Beta Prior

From simulation studies, even on stationary processes, we find that the average value for the adaptive forgetting factors hovers around
0.9
. Such a value leads to quite an aggressive discarding of past data, even when the data generating process is stationary, causing the variance of the estimator to be higher than necessary. Taking inspiration from the Bayesian regularization literature
(
13
)
, we modify the cost function to include a Beta
(\alpha,1)
prior for
\lambda_{T+1}
,

L_{T+1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})=-\mathcal{L}_{T+1}(S_{\hat{\phi}_{T+1}};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T})-\log P_{\text{Beta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}|\alpha,1).

(15)

The reasoning behind this modification is to induce a predisposition in the algorithm towards a forgetting factor of
\alpha/(1+\alpha)
. The choice of
\alpha
is not trivial. However, we find a value of 1000 or higher results in similar performances while significantly reducing the noise in sequence
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}
. In Appendix C we provide more examples of how
\alpha
affects the sequence
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}
.

in (

) for the estimation of the process used in Figure

. The process has a change-point at

, where we can see the forgetting factor

\alpha
, the mean
\lambda_{t}
levels off at a different value. The legend shows the values for
\alpha/(1+\alpha)
, the mean of the Beta prior on
\lambda_{t}
.

6
Oceanic Dataset

We analyse real-world data from the Global Drifter Program
(
22
)
. Specifically, we build on the work of
32
in which the authors model jointly the latitudinal and longitudinal velocities obtained from instruments known as drifters, which drift freely according to ocean surface flows
(
24
)
. Those velocities are modelled as the aggregation of two independent complex valued processes, one of which is non-stationary and which we model as a complex-valued AR(1) process. Figure
9
shows the path taken by one of these drifters.
The velocity time series are non-stationary, as they are modulated by oscillations that change in frequency over time. The oscillations are known as inertial oscillations – one of the most ubiquitous and readily observable features of the ocean currents accounting for approximately half of the kinetic energy in the upper ocean
(
24
)
. Inertial oscillations arise owing to the deviation of the rotating Earth from a purely spherical geometry, together with the appearance of the Coriolis force in the rotating reference frame of an Earth-based observer. The modulation of these oscillations occurs because the drifters are changing latitude.

Figure 9
:
Trajectory of drifter 44312 from the Global Drifter Program.

The second-order structure of complex-valued processes is characterised by both the autocovariance
s_{\tau}=\text{Cov}[Z_{t}^{*},Z_{t+\tau}]
and the relation
r_{\tau}=\text{Cov}[Z_{t},Z_{t+\tau}]
sequences. Complex valued processes, unlike real-valued, no longer have a spectrum that needs to satisfy Hermitian symmetry, and if the series represents motion in the plane, the positive and negative frequencies represent anti-clockwise and clockwise rotations respectively. Following the classical modelling framework
(
60
)
for complex-valued processes, we shall assume that the relation sequence takes the value zero for all lags, and the definition for the SDF in (
1
) still applies.

We treat the observed time series as a realisation of the random process
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}=\{X_{t}+iY_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
, where
\{X_{t}\}
is the longitudinal velocities and
\{Y_{t}\}
is the latitudinal velocities. Complex-valued process
\{Z_{t}\}
is conventionally modelled as a modulated AR(1) process

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=re^{i\beta_{t}}Z_{t-1}+\epsilon_{t},

\displaystyle Z_{0}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{CN}\left(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1-r^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2}),

where
\{\beta_{t}\}
is a deterministic sequence, which is a function of the
inertial frequency

\omega_{t}=-k\sin(\xi_{t})
, for some
k\in\mathbb{R}
where
\xi_{t}
is the latitude of the drifter at time
t
. The parameters
r
and
\sigma^{2}
are themselves functions of five parameters,
\{A,\lambda,B,h,\alpha\}
, and if
\omega_{t}
, and hence
\beta_{t}
, were fixed, the modulated AR(1) process above would be stationary with SDF

S(f)=\frac{A^{2}}{(f-\omega)^{2}+\lambda^{2}}+\frac{B}{(\omega^{2}+h^{2})^{\alpha}}.

(16)

In practice,
\omega_{t}
does vary over time as the drifter moves, but it is known. To estimate the unknown parameters,
32
appropriately demodulate the process using the known
\omega_{t}
, thereby transforming it into a stationary AR(1) process. They then apply (offline) Whittle estimation.

In this paper, to illustrate the power of our method on real-data, we take a different approach. We apply the FFWE to the raw time series, using (
16
) to estimate all parameters, including online tracking of
\omega_{T}
as
T
increases. We are then able to compare this tracked online estimate with the ground truth.

Figure
10
compares the FFWE at the end of the run against the offline (batch) Whittle estimate on the demodulated process as presented in
32
. While the batch Whittle estimation on the demodulated process more closely matches the periodogram, the performance of the two methods are comparable. In Figure
11
, we plot our estimates for
\omega_{T}
against the trajectory, as a function of
T
. The FFWE is clearly able to track the ground truth without assuming any prior information aside from the assumption of a modulated complex AR(1) process. Furthermore, even a very modest level of forgetting (
\lambda=0.9999
) gives significant advantages of no forgetting (
\lambda=1
) in the method’s responsiveness to drift.

7
Asymptotic Distribution of the Forgetting Factor Periodogram

To derive the asymptotic distribution of the FFP, we adapt the proof for the asymptotic distribution of the periodogram
(
8
)
.

Assumption 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}=(\lambda_{t})_{t=1,...,T}
be an increasing sequence of forgetting factors with
\lambda_{t}\in(0,1)
for all
t>0
and
1-\lambda_{T}^{2}=o(T^{-\gamma})
as
T\rightarrow\infty
, for some
\gamma>1
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a zero-mean second-order stationary process. For a portion of the process
X_{1},...,X_{T}
, define
J_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{T,t}X_{t}e^{-i2\pi ft}
and
C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{T,t}^{2}
, where

h_{T,t}=\begin{cases}1,&t=T\\
\prod_{s=t}^{T-1}\lambda_{s},&t=1,...,T-1\\
0,&\textnormal{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The FFP is defined as

\hat{S}_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})=\frac{1}{C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})}\left|J_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right|^{2}.

Definition 2
.

For data taper
\boldsymbol{h}_{T}=(h_{T,t})_{t=1,...,T}
, the
k
-th Fourier moment is defined as

H^{(T)}_{k}(f)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}[h_{T,t}]^{k}\exp\{-i2\pi ft\}.

To aid notation, from here on we use
h_{T}(t)\equiv h_{T,t}
, retaining
t
as a discrete index in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
h_{T}(t)
and
H_{k}^{(T)}(f)
be as defined in Definitions
1
and
2
, respectively. For any
u_{1},...,u_{k-1}\in\{0,...,T\}
there exists finite
K>0
s.t.

\left|\sum_{t}h_{T}(t+u_{1})\cdots h_{T}(t+u_{k-1})h_{T}(t)\exp\{-i2\pi ft\}-H^{(T)}_{k}(f)\right|\leq K\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}|u_{a}|.

Proof.

It is true that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{T}(t+u_{1})\ldots h_{T}(t+u_{k-1})h_{T}(t)\exp\{-i2\pi ft\}-H^{(T)}_{k}(f)\right|

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{T}(t+u_{1})\ldots h_{T}(t+u_{k-1})h_{T}(t)\exp\{-i2\pi ft\}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}[h_{T}(t)]^{k}\exp\{-i2\pi ft\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|h_{T}(t+u_{1})\ldots h_{T}(t+u_{k-1})h_{T}(t)\exp\{-i2\pi ft\}-[h_{T}(t)]^{k}\exp\{-i2\pi ft\}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|h_{T}(t+u_{1})\ldots h_{T}(t+u_{k-1})h_{T}(t)-[h_{T}(t)]^{k}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|\prod_{a=1}^{k}h_{T}(t+u_{a})-[h_{T}(t)]^{k}\right|,\,\,\,\,\text{where $u_{k}=0$.}

Assume that

\left|h_{T}(t+u_{1})\ldots h_{T}(t+u_{n-1})h_{T}(t+u_{n})-[h_{T}(t)]^{n}\right|\leq\sum_{a=1}^{n}|h_{T}(t+u_{a})-h_{T}(t)|.

(17)

Then it follows that

\displaystyle\left|h_{T}(t+u_{1})\ldots h_{T}(t+u_{n})h_{T}(t+u_{n+1})-[h_{T}(t)]^{n+1}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left[h_{T}(t+u_{1})\ldots h_{T}(t+u_{n})\right]h_{T}(t+u_{n+1})-[h_{T}(t)]^{n}h_{T}(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max(h_{T}(t+u_{n+1}),[h_{T}(t)]^{n})\left[\left|h_{T}(t+u_{1})\ldots h_{T}(t+u_{n})-[h_{T}(t)]^{n}\right|+|h_{T}(t+u_{n+1})-h_{T}(t)|\right]

(18)

\displaystyle\leq\max(h_{T}(t+u_{n+1}),[h_{T}(t)]^{n})\left[\sum_{a=1}^{n}|h_{T}(t+u_{a})-h_{T}(t)|+|h_{T}(t+u_{n+1})-h_{T}(t)|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max(h_{T}(t+u_{n+1}),[h_{T}(t)]^{n})\left[\sum_{a=1}^{n+1}|h_{T}(t+u_{a})-h_{T}(t)|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a=1}^{n+1}|h_{T}(t+u_{a})-h_{T}(t)|,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{because $h(x)\leq 1$ for all $x$.}

Step (
18
) comes from the fact that given positive values
a,b,c,d
, we have that

\displaystyle|ab-cd|

\displaystyle=|(a-c)b+c(b-d)|

\displaystyle\leq\max(b,c)\left|(a-c)+(b-d)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max(b,c)\left[|a-c|+|b-d|\right].

Given (
17
) is true for
n
implies the same for
n+1
, and that it is trivially true for
n=1
, by induction it is true for all positive integers
n
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{T}(t+u_{1})\ldots h_{T}(t+u_{k-1})h_{T}(t)\exp\{-i2\pi ft\}-H^{(T)}_{k}(f)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|\prod_{a=1}^{k}h_{T}(t+u_{a})-[h_{T}(t)]^{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{a=1}^{k}|h_{T}(t+u_{a})-h_{T}(t)|.

Suppose for convenience
u_{a}\geq 0
, (other cases are handled similarly), then

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_{T}(t+u_{1})\ldots h_{T}(t+u_{k-1})h_{T}(t)\exp\{-i2\pi ft\}-H^{(T)}_{k}(f)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{a=1}^{k}|h_{T}(t+u_{a})-h_{T}(t)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{a=1}^{k}|(h_{T}(t+u_{a})-h_{T}(t+u_{a}-1))+(h_{T}(t+u_{a}-1)-h_{T}(t+u_{a}-2))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\cdots+(h_{T}(t+1)-h_{T}(t))|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|\sum_{b=1}^{u_{a}}h_{T}(t+b)-h_{T}(t+b-1)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{b=1}^{u_{a}}|h_{T}(t+b)-h_{T}(t+b-1)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}\sum_{b=1}^{u_{a}}\left(1+\sum_{t=1}^{T-b}h_{T}(t+b)-h_{T}(t+b-1)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}\sum_{b=1}^{u_{a}}\left(2-h_{T}(b)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}|u_{a}|

(19)

for some finite
K
. The step for (
19
) is given by the fact that
h(x)\in[0,1]
for all
x
, thus

\sum_{b=1}^{u_{a}}\left(2-h_{T}(b)\right)<\sum_{b=1}^{u_{a}}2=2(u_{a}-1).

Furthermore, with

h_{T}(t+b)-h_{T}(t+b-1)=\begin{cases}-1,&t+b=T+1\\
h_{T}(t+b)-h_{T}(t+b-1),&t+b=1,...,T\\
0,&\textnormal{otherwise,}\end{cases}

it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}|h_{T}(t+b)-h_{T}(t+b-1)|

\displaystyle=|-1|+\sum_{t=1}^{T-b}h_{T}(t+b)-h_{T}(t+b-1)

\displaystyle=1+(h_{T}(T)-h_{T}(T-1))+(h_{T}(T-1)-h_{T}(T-2))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\cdots+(h_{T}(b+1)-h_{T}(b))

\displaystyle=1+(h_{T}(T)-h_{T}(b))

\displaystyle=2-h_{T}(b),

thus completing the proof.
∎

Assumption 2
.

Random process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is zero mean and strictly stationary, meaning that
\textnormal{cum}(X_{t+u_{1}},...,X_{t+u_{k}})=\textnormal{cum}(X_{u_{1}},...,X_{k})
for all
k>0
, for all
u_{1},...,u_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
and for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. Furthermore, denoting

c_{k}(u_{1},...,u_{k-1})\equiv\textnormal{cum}(X_{u_{1}},...,X_{u_{k-1}},X_{0}),

the following mixing condition holds,

\sum_{u_{1}=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{u_{2}=-\infty}^{\infty}...\sum_{u_{k-1}=-\infty}^{\infty}|c_{k}(u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{k-1})|<\infty.

Definition 3
.

We define the
k^{th}
order cumulant spectrum,
\mathcal{C}_{k}(f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{k-1})
as

\mathcal{C}_{k}(f_{1},...,f_{k-1})=(2\pi)^{-k+1}\sum_{u_{1},...,u_{k-1}=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{k}(u_{1},...,u_{k-1})\exp\left\{-i2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}u_{j}f_{j}\right\}.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\{X_{t}\}
be a random process such that Assumption (
2
) holds, then

\mathcal{C}_{k}(f_{1},...,f_{k-1})=(2\pi)^{-k+1}\sum_{u_{1}=-T}^{T}\ldots\sum_{u_{k}=-T}^{T}c_{k}(u_{1},...,u_{k-1})\exp\left\{-i2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}u_{j}f_{j}\right\}+o(1).

Proof.

The mixing condition in Assumption
2
implies that there exists an
L>0
such that

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{u_{1}=-T}^{T}\sum_{u_{2}=-T}^{T}...\sum_{u_{k-1}=-T}^{T}|c_{k}(u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{k-1})|=L.

This further implies that

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{k-1}\notin\{-T,...,T\}}|c_{k}(u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{k-1})|=0.

It now follows that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{C}_{k}(f_{1},...,f_{k-1})-(2\pi)^{-k+1}\sum_{u_{1}=-T}^{T}\ldots\sum_{u_{k}=-T}^{T}c_{k}(u_{1},...,u_{k-1})\exp\left\{-i2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}u_{j}f_{j}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-k+1}\left|\sum_{u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{k-1}\notin\{-T,...,T\}}c_{k}(u_{1},...,u_{k-1})\exp\left\{-i2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}u_{j}f_{j}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-k+1}\sum_{u_{1},u_{2},...,u_{k-1}\notin\{-T,...,T\}}|c_{k}(u_{1},...,u_{k-1})|\longrightarrow\,0\quad\textnormal{as}\quad T\longrightarrow\infty.

Thus proving Lemma
2
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
X_{1},...,X_{T}
be a portion of a random process
\{X_{t}\}
that follows Assumption
2
. Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}=(\lambda_{t})_{t=1,...,T}
be a sequence of forgetting factors, with taper
(h_{T,t})_{t=1,...,T}
and
J_{T}(f;\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
as given in Definition
1
, and let the
k
th Fourier moment
H_{k}^{(T)}(f)
be as defined in Definition
2
. For all
k>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\textnormal{cum}\{

\displaystyle J_{T}(f_{1};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}),\ldots,J_{T}(f_{k};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\}=

\displaystyle H_{k}^{(T)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}\right)\sum_{u_{1}=-T}^{T}\ldots\sum_{u_{k}=-T}^{T}\exp\left\{-i2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}f_{j}u_{j}\right\}c(u_{1},...,u_{k-1})+\epsilon_{T},

where
\epsilon_{T}
is
o(T)
.

Proof.

The cumulant in Lemma
3
has the form

\displaystyle\sum_{t_{1}}...\sum_{t_{k}}

\displaystyle h_{T}(t-1)...h_{T}(t_{k})\exp\left\{-i2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}t_{j}\right\}c_{k}(t_{1}-t_{k},...,t_{k-1}-t_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u_{1}}...\sum_{u_{k-1}}\exp\left\{-i\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}f_{j}u_{j}\right\}c_{k}(u_{1},...,u_{k-1})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\sum_{t}h_{T}(t+u_{1})...h_{T}(t+u_{k-1})h_{T}(t)\exp\left\{-i2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}t\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u_{1}}...\sum_{u_{k-1}}\exp\left\{-i2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}f_{j}u_{j}\right\}c_{k}(u_{1},...,u_{k-1})\left[H_{k}^{(T)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{j}\right)+\epsilon_{T}^{\prime}\right],

where
|\epsilon_{T}^{\prime}|\leq K\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}|u_{a}|
from Lemma
1
. Expanding the last bracket leads to the expression in Lemma
3
where

\displaystyle|\epsilon_{T}|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{u_{1}}...\sum_{u_{k-1}}\exp\left\{-i\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}f_{j}u_{j}\right\}c_{k}(u_{1},...,u_{k-1})\epsilon_{T}^{\prime}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u_{1}}...\sum_{u_{k-1}}\left|\exp\left\{-i\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}f_{j}u_{j}\right\}c_{k}(u_{1},...,u_{k-1})\epsilon_{T}^{\prime}\right|

Theorem 1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T}=(\lambda_{t})_{t=1,...,T}
be a sequence of forgetting factors, with taper
(h_{T,t})_{t=1,...,T}
, that follows Assumption
1
, and
X_{1},...,X_{T}
be a portion of a random process
\{X_{t}\}
that follows Assumption
2
and has SDF
S(f)
. Then, for any
k>0
and any set of distinct frequencies
f_{1},...,f_{K}
, where
f_{i}\notin\{-1/2,0,1/2\}
for all
i=1,...,K
, then
\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
,
i=1,...,K
are asymptotically independent

\mathcal{CN}(0,S(f_{i}))
random variables as
T\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we consider frequencies
f_{i}\in(0,1/2)
for all
i=1,...,K
. To show joint Gaussianity, it is sufficient to show that

\textnormal{cum}\left\{\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i_{1}};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}),\ldots,\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i_{k}};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right\}\rightarrow 0,\qquad i_{1},...,i_{k}=1,...,K

as
T\rightarrow\infty
for all
k>2
, and that the first and second order moments are as stated. Proceeding in this way, for
k>2

\textnormal{cum}\left\{\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i_{1}};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}),\ldots,\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i_{k}};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right\}\\
=\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-k/2}\left(H_{k}^{(T)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{i_{j}}\right)\mathcal{C}_{k}(f_{i_{1}},...,f_{i_{k-1}})+o(T)\right)\\
\leq T\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-k/2}\mathcal{C}_{k}(f_{i_{1}},...,f_{i_{k-1}})+o\left(T\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-k/2}\right),

where, directly from Definition
2
, we have
\left|H_{k}^{(T)}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}f_{i_{j}}\right)\right|\leq T
.
Under Assumption
1
we have
1-\lambda_{T}^{2}=o(T^{-\gamma})
. This means that for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
T_{\epsilon}
such that for all
T>T_{\epsilon}
we have
\lambda^{2}_{T}>1-\epsilon T^{-\gamma}
. Fix
\epsilon>0
and the corresponding
T_{\epsilon}
. Then for all
T>T_{\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}

\displaystyle=C_{T_{\epsilon}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})[h_{T}(T_{\epsilon})]^{2}+\sum_{t=T_{\epsilon}+1}^{T}[h_{T}(t)]^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t=T_{\epsilon}+1}^{T}[h_{T}(t)]^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=T_{\epsilon}+1}^{T}\prod_{b=t}^{T-1}\lambda^{2}_{b}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t=T_{\epsilon}+1}^{T}\prod_{b=t}^{T-1}1-\epsilon b^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t=T_{\epsilon}+1}^{T}\left[1-\epsilon\sum_{b=t}^{T-1}b^{-\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t=T_{\epsilon}+1}^{T}\left[1-\epsilon\zeta(\gamma)\right]\qquad\qquad\text{where $\zeta(\cdot)$ is the Riemann zeta function}

\displaystyle=(T-T_{\epsilon})\left[1-\epsilon\zeta(\gamma)\right]

\displaystyle=O(T).

Therefore,
\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1}=O(T^{-1})
. Here, we used the fact that the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(\gamma)
is finite for
\gamma>1
and that

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-a_{i})

\displaystyle=(1-a_{1})\left[\prod_{i=2}^{n}(1-a_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{i=2}^{n}(1-a_{i})\right]-a_{1}\left[\prod_{i=2}^{n}(1-a_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left[\prod_{i=2}^{n}(1-a_{i})\right]-a_{i},

if
\left[\prod_{i=2}^{n}(1-a_{i})\right]<1
. This condition is satisfied in our case because each term in the product corresponds to the square of a forgetting factor term,
\lambda_{b}^{2}
. All forgetting factors
\lambda_{b}
are positive and bounded by
1
, so the same applies to any product of them. By using the previous expression repeatedly, by induction, we see that

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-a_{i})\geq 1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}.

Therefore, for
k>2
,

\textnormal{cum}\left\{\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i_{1}};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}),\ldots,\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i_{k}};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right\}\\
\leq T\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-k/2}\mathcal{C}_{k}(f_{i_{1}},...,f_{i_{k-1}})+o\left(T\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-k/2}\right)\\
\longrightarrow 0\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \textnormal{ as }\ \left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1}=O(T^{-1}).

All joint cumulants of order
k>2
going to 0 implies the random variables
\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
,
i=1,...,K
are jointly Gaussian. We next consider the
k=2
order cumulants. It is true that

\textnormal{cum}\left\{\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}),\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{j};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right\}\\
=\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1}\textnormal{cum}\left\{J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})J_{T}(f_{j};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right\}\\
=\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1}\left(H_{2}^{(T)}\left(f_{i}+f_{j}\right)\mathcal{C}_{2}(f_{i})+o(T)\right).

For
f_{i},f_{j}\in(0,1/2)
, then
f_{1}+f_{2}\neq 0\text{ (mod }1)
. Fixing
\epsilon>0
and the corresponding
T_{\epsilon}
such that for all
T>T_{\epsilon}
we have
\lambda^{2}_{T}>1-\epsilon T^{-\gamma}
, then

\displaystyle\left|\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\exp{(2\pi ift)}\right]-H_{2}^{(T)}(f)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{T}(1-\lambda_{t}^{2})\exp{(2\pi ift)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{T}(1-\lambda_{t}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\epsilon}}(1-\lambda_{t}^{2})+\sum_{b=T_{\epsilon}}^{T}\epsilon b^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq T_{\epsilon}+\sum_{b=T_{\epsilon}}^{T}\epsilon b^{-\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq T_{\epsilon}+\epsilon\zeta(\gamma),

where
\zeta(\cdot)
is the again the Riemann zeta function. Therefore,

|H_{2}^{(T)}(f)|\leq\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\exp{(2\pi ift)}\right|+T_{\epsilon}+\epsilon\zeta(\gamma)=\frac{\sin(T\pi f)}{\sin(\pi f)}+T_{\epsilon}+\epsilon\zeta(\gamma).

As the right-hand side is bounded for all values of
T
if
f\neq 0
, if follows that

\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1}\left(H_{2}^{(T)}\left(f_{1}+f_{2}\right)\mathcal{C}_{2}(f_{1})+o(T)\right)\longrightarrow 0\,\,\text{as}\,\,T\longrightarrow\infty.

Together with asymptotic Gaussianity, this also shows that
\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
,
i=1,...,K
are also asymptotically independent.

We are left with showing that the first and second order moments of
\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
are as stated. The first order moment is immediate from the fact that
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is zero mean. We note that for any
f_{i},f_{j}\in(-1/2,1/2]
such that
f_{i}+f_{j}\equiv 0\text{ (mod }1)
, then
H_{2}^{(T)}(f_{i}+f_{j})=H_{2}^{(T)}(0)=C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
, and therefore asymptotically

\textnormal{cum}\left\{\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}),\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{j};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right\}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{2}(f_{i})=S(f_{i}).

Note that
\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
and
\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(-f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})
are the same random variable, and therefore

\mathrm{Var}\{\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\}\\
=\textnormal{cum}\left\{\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}),\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right\}\\
=\textnormal{cum}\left\{\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1}),\left[C_{T}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right]^{-1/2}J_{T}(-f_{i};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{T-1})\right\}\longrightarrow S(f_{i}),

as
T\longrightarrow\infty
.
∎

\phi_{2}
