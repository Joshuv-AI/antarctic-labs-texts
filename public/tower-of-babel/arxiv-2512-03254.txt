Assumption-Lean Differential Variance Inference for Heterogeneous Treatment Effect Detection
Philippe A. Boileau, Hani Zaki, Gabriele Lileikyte, Niklas Nielsen, Patrick R. Lawler, Mireille E. Schnitzer

Abstract
The conditional average treatment effect (CATE) is frequently estimated to refute the homogeneous treatment effect assumption. Under this assumption, all units making up the population under study experience identical benefit from a given treatment. Uncovering heterogeneous treatment effects through inference about the CATE, however, requires that covariates truly modifying the treatment effect be reliably collected at baseline. CATE-based techniques will necessarily fail to detect violations when effect modifiers are omitted from the data due to, for example, resource constraints. Severe measurement error has a similar impact. To address these limitations, we prove that the homogeneous treatment effect assumption can be gauged through inference about contrasts of the potential outcomes’ variances. We derive causal machine learning estimators of these contrasts and study their asymptotic properties. We establish that these estimators are doubly robust and asymptotically linear under mild conditions, permitting formal hypothesis testing about the homogeneous treatment effect assumption even when effect modifiers are missing or mismeasured. Numerical experiments demonstrate that these estimators’ asymptotic guarantees are approximately achieved in experimental and observational data alike. These inference procedures are then used to detect heterogeneous treatment effects in the re-analysis of randomized controlled trials investigating targeted temperature management in cardiac arrest patients.

Introduction

Causal inference analyses are largely focused on contrasts of means. The canonical analysis of a continuous outcome in a parallel group design takes the average treatment effect (ATE) as the target of inference. The ATE is defined as the difference of the potential outcomes’ means, where potential outcomes are the outcomes that would be observed had observations been assigned to the treatment or control groups
(
32
;
37
)
.

While the difference of the potential outcome distributions’ first moment is widely accepted as a useful summary of the individual treatment effect (ITE) distribution, it only provides a complete characterization of this distribution under the homogeneous ITE assumption. This assumption states that the ITE is constant in the population under study.

The ATE therefore fails to capture all information provided by the ITE distribution unless it is degenerate. Let
Y^{(0)}=f^{(0)}(W)+\epsilon^{(0)}
and
Y^{(1)}=f^{(1)}(W)+\epsilon^{(1)}
, where
Y^{(0)}
and
Y^{(1)}
are continuous potential outcomes for the control and treatment groups, respectively,
W
is a random vector of pre-treatment covariates, and
\epsilon^{(0)}
and
\epsilon^{(1)}
are mean-zero random noise variables that are independent of
f^{(0)}(W)
and
f^{(1)}(W)
, respectively. Then the homogeneous ITE assumption is only met in pathological data-generating processes (DGPs) where
f^{(0)}(W)=f^{(1)}(W)-\gamma
almost surely (a.s). for some fixed
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\epsilon^{(0)}=\epsilon^{(1)}
such that
Y^{(1)}-Y^{(0)}=\gamma
. Here,
\gamma
corresponds to the ATE. Violations of this assumption occur when at least one treatment effect modifier — that is, a pre-treatment covariate that interacts with the treatment — is present such that
f^{(0)}(W)\neq f^{(1)}(W)-\gamma
a.s. for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
or when the treatment is innately heterogeneous, meaning
\epsilon^{(0)}\neq\epsilon^{(1)}
.

Practitioners in numerous fields have readily acknowledged that the ATE provides a generally insufficient comparison of competing treatments’ effects. Decisions informed solely by inference about the ATE can result in unwelcome consequences for subgroups of the population. In medicine, for example, myriad clinical trials that have failed to show that an investigatory therapy meaningfully improves patient outcomes relative to the standard of care on average, yet provided evidence of a differential effect in some patient phenotypes
(
9
;
1
;
54
, see, for instance, the following oncology trials:)
. Patients likely to benefit from these novel therapies cannot access them, however, pending future trials and eventual regulatory approval. Indeed, the subfield of precision medicine seeks to uncover such heterogeneous treatment effects, using them to efficiently tailor patient care and develop treatment guidelines.

Numerous methods for quantifying treatment effect heterogeneity have therefore been developed to supplement inference about the ATE. Chief among them are inference procedures based on the conditional average treatment effect (CATE)
(see
30
;
12
;
49
;
14
;
29
;
52
;
2
;
26

. The CATE, defined as
\mathbb{E}[Y^{(1)}-Y^{(0)}|W]
, can be used to determine strata of observations that experience increased, or decreased, average benefit as a function of their covariates. As a result, these approaches are unable to detect innate heterogeneity due to the treatment — that is, when
f^{(0)}(W)=f^{(1)}(W)-\gamma
a.s. but
\epsilon^{(0)}\neq\epsilon^{(1)}
. Another limitation is that they can only uncover effect heterogeneity if the treatment effect modifiers are included in the set of pre-treatment covariates on which the CATE is conditioned. CATE-based procedures are therefore unable to detect treatment effect heterogeneity when treatment effect modifiers are not measured during data collection. Including a large number of potential effect modifiers in the CATE’s conditioning set may not be a reasonable strategy for uncovering drivers of effect heterogeneity, either.
26
proved that high-dimensional inference about the CATE is unreliable unless unverifiable simplifying assumptions are made about the DGP, like sparsity. Recent work by
5
provided additional empirical evidence that inference procedures for the CATE produced elevated false positive and negative rates when attempting to identify treatment effect modifiers from among many pre-treatment covariates.

Inference procedures for treatment effect modifier variable importance parameters (TEM-VIPs) offer an alternative avenue for detecting treatment effect heterogeneity
(
50
;
7
;
51
;
21
;
6
;
55
)
. Given a set of pre-treatment covariates, these approaches evaluate variables’ individual capacity for treatment effect modification using variable importance parameters. These methods also permit formal hypothesis testing about these parameters in diverse semiparametric models: pre-treatment covariates with statistically significant tests provide evidence of a heterogeneous treatment effect. These TEM-VIP procedures, like the CATE-based approaches, can only uncover effect heterogeneity when the effect modifiers are included in the data. Even when at least one effect modifier is included in a high-dimensional dataset, weak effect modification may be overlooked due to adjustments for multiplicity. Further, like methods relying on the CATE, these approaches were not developed to capture innate treatment effect heterogeneity.

To address the limitation of existing methods, we develop assumption-lean inference procedures for the contrasts of potential outcomes’ variances, which we refer to as
differential variance
estimands. We prove that the homogeneous ITE assumption is violated when the difference of the potential outcomes’ variances is non-zero or, equivalently, when their ratio is not equal to one. We derive one-step and targeted maximum likelihood estimators (TMLE) of these causal contrasts based on their efficient influence functions (EIFs), meaning these estimators can rely on flexible machine learning procedures to estimate nuisance parameters
(
34
;
4
;
46
;
44
;
45
)
. We study these estimators’ asymptotic behavior, showing that they are doubly robust and providing conditions under which they are asymptotically linear. The latter permits formal hypothesis testing about the homogeneous ITE assumption. Importantly, these tests do not require that the effect modifiers driving this heterogeneity be included in the data — so long as standard causal identifiability conditions are otherwise satisfied.

2
Problem Formulation

We consider a parallel group study design wherein observations are randomly assigned,
possibly based on their pre-treatment covariates, to one of two treatment
groups. The treatment assignment mechanism may be known, as in randomized
controlled trials (RCTs), or unknown, as in observational studies. Continuous outcomes are then measured at the end of the study. For simplicity,
we assume that there are no missing data at study completion. We then aim to infer
the variances of the potential outcomes under each treatment condition and contrasts thereof.
These contrasts are then used to evaluate the homogeneous ITE assumption.

We begin by formalizing this causal problem in terms of an idealized full-data
DGP. We then relate the resulting causal model to an observed-data DGP with
which statistical inference can be performed.

2.1
Full-Data DGP

Let the full-data DGP consist of
n
independent and identically distributed
(i.i.d.) copies of

X_{i}=(W_{i},A_{i},Y^{(0)}_{i},Y^{(1)}_{i})\sim P_{X,0}\in\mathcal{M}_{X}
for

i=1,\ldots,n
. We drop the indices where convenient throughout the
remainder of the text.
W
is a random vector of covariates that includes the treatment–outcome confounders, if any.
A
is a random binary variable with support
\mathcal{A}=\{0,1\}

representing treatment assignment; observations are said to be assigned to the
treatment group when
A=1
and to the control group otherwise.
Y^{(0)}

and
Y^{(1)}
are potential outcomes, continuous random variables representing
the outcome under assignment to the treatment and control groups, respectively. We impose
without loss of generality that
Y^{(0)},Y^{(1)}
be bounded between between

0
and
1
. Finally,
P_{X,0}
is the true, but unknown, full-data DGP, an element
of the nonparametric statistical model
\mathcal{M}_{X}
containing all possible
full-data DGPs.

While realizations of
X
are generally unobservable owing to the definition of
potential outcomes, the full-data DGP permits the definition of parameters that
admit causal interpretations. The ATE is a common example. It is a mapping defined as
as
\mu_{F}(P_{X,0};1)-\mu_{F}(P_{X,0};0)
, where

\mu_{F}(P_{X};a)\equiv\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}[Y^{(a)}]
for
a\in\mathcal{A}
. The ATE
therefore represents the difference in mean outcomes that would be observed if all
units in the population were assigned to the treatment group versus the control group.

We aim to perform inference about the variances of the potential outcomes. Let

\mu_{F}^{2}(P_{X,0};a)\equiv\mathbb{E}_{P_{X,0}}[(Y^{(a)})^{2}]
.
Then, for
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the target parameters are mappings defined as

\begin{split}\sigma^{2}_{F}(P_{X,0};a)&\equiv\mathbb{E}_{P_{X,0}}[(Y^{(a)}-\mu_{F}(P_{X,0};a))^{2}]\\
&=\mu^{2}_{F}(P_{X,0};a)-\mu_{F}(P_{X,0};a)^{2}\;.\end{split}

(1)

\sigma^{2}_{F}(P_{X,0};a)
therefore corresponds to the variance of the outcome had all
units in the population been assigned to the treatment group specified by
a
. These parameters are building blocks for creating causal estimands of differential variance.

2.2
Differential Variance Estimands

We propose an absolute and a relative differential variance estimand, though others may be derived where appropriate. The absolute differential variance parameter is given by

\Psi_{F}(P_{X,0})\equiv\sigma_{F}(P_{X,0};1)-\sigma_{F}(P_{X,0};0)\;,

(2)

the difference of the potential outcomes’ standard deviations. As the name suggests,
\Psi_{F}(P_{X,0})
compares the potential outcome variability between groups on the absolute scale and in the same units as the potential outcomes. Positive values indicate that the treatment group’s potential outcomes are more variable than those of the control group. Negative values have an opposite interpretation.

\Psi_{F}(P_{X,0})
also offers a reasonable summary of heterogeneous treatment effects. Per Theorem
1
, it can be used to detect violations of the homogeneous ITE assumption: the treatment effect is necessarily heterogeneous when
\Psi_{F}(P_{X,0})\neq 0
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
X
be defined as in Section
2.1
, and let
Y^{(a)}=f^{(a)}(W)+\epsilon^{(a)}
for
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Here,
\epsilon^{(a)}
is a random noise variable such that
\mathbb{E}_{P_{X,0}}[\epsilon^{(a)}]=0
and
f^{(a)}(W)\perp\epsilon^{(a)}
. If
\Psi_{F}(P_{X,0})\neq 0
, then
Y^{(0)}\neq Y^{(1)}-\gamma
for
\gamma=\mathbb{E}_{P_{X,0}}[Y^{(1)}-Y^{(0)}],\;\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. The treatment effect is heterogeneous.

We caution, however, that
\Psi_{F}(P_{X,0})=0
does not necessarily imply that
Y^{(0)}=Y^{(1)}-\gamma
unless additional assumptions are made. Indeed, we may find that
\mathbb{V}_{P_{X,0}}[f^{(0)}(W)]+\mathbb{V}_{P_{X,0}}[\epsilon^{(0)}]=\mathbb{V}_{P_{X,0}}[f^{(1)}(W)]+\mathbb{V}_{P_{X,0}}[\epsilon^{(1)}]
even when
f^{(0)}\neq f^{(1)}-\gamma
for any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
a.s. and
\epsilon^{(0)}\neq\epsilon^{(1)}
, though we only expect this behavior to occur in pathological DGPs.

When quantifying the relative differences in potential outcome variances across treatment groups is of interest, then we may instead consider the following estimand:

\Lambda_{F}(P_{X,0})\equiv\frac{\sigma^{2}_{F}(P_{X,0};1)}{\sigma^{2}_{F}(P_{X,0};0)}\;.

Values larger than one suggest that the potential outcomes associated with the treatment group have larger variances than those of the control group, whereas values smaller than one indicate the opposite. Similar to the result about
\Psi_{F}
given in Theorem
1
,
\Lambda_{F}(P_{X,0})\neq 1
implies that the homogeneous ITE assumption is violated by Corollary
1.1
.

Corollary 1.1
.

Consider the setting of Theorem
1
. It follows that if
\Lambda_{F}(P_{X,0})\neq 1
, then
Y^{(0)}\neq Y^{(1)}-\gamma
such that the treatment effect is heterogeneous.

2.3
Identification Conditions in the Observed-Data DGP

Now, as previously mentioned, realizations of the full-data DGP are unobservable
owing to the fundamental problem of causal inference

(
24
)
. Instead, we have access to realizations
of
n
i.i.d. random
observations
O=(W,A,Y)\sim P_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
.
W
and
A
are defined
as in
X
, and
Y
is a continuous random variable representing the observed
outcome.
P_{0}
is the true but unknown observed-data DGP and is fully specified
by
P_{X,0}
up to the coarsening of the potential outcomes. Analogously,

\mathcal{M}
is the nonparametric model of possible observed-data DGPs.

As a first step in performing inference about
\Psi_{F}(P_{X,0})
an
\Lambda_{F}(P_{X,0})
, we must
establish the identifiability of these estimands in
\mathcal{M}
. We first
provide identification conditions for the raw moments of potential outcomes. Let

\bar{Q}_{P}^{d}(W,a)\equiv\mathbb{E}_{P}[Y^{d}|W,A=a]
.

Theorem 2
.

Assuming that
A\perp\!\!\!\perp(Y^{(0)},Y^{(1)})|W
(conditional
exchangeability),
Y=AY^{(1)}+(1-A)Y^{(0)}
(consistency), and

0<\mathbb{P}_{P_{X,0}}[A=1|W]<1
a.s. (positivity),
we find that

\mu^{d}_{F}(P_{X,0};a)=\mu^{d}(P_{0};a)

where

\mu^{d}(P;a)\equiv\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{Q}_{P}^{d}(W,a)]
for

d\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Then Corollary
2.1
follows directly from
Theorem
2
.

Corollary 2.1
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
2
, we find
that, for
a\in\mathcal{A}
,

\begin{split}\sigma^{2}_{F}(P_{X,0};a)&=\mu^{2}_{F}(P_{X,0};a)-\mu_{F}(P_{X,0};a)^{2}\\
&=\mu^{2}(P_{0};a)-\mu(P_{0};a)^{2}\\
&\equiv\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)\;.\end{split}

Then absolute and relative differential variance estimands are similarly estimable from the observed-data DGPs:

\Psi_{F}(P_{X,0})\equiv\Psi(P_{0})=\sigma(P_{0};1)-\sigma(P_{0};0)

(3)

and,

\Lambda_{F}(P_{0})\equiv\Lambda(P_{0})=\frac{\sigma^{2}(P_{0};1)}{\sigma^{2}(P_{0};0)}\;.

(4)

The conditional exchangeability, consistency, and positivity assumptions of Theorem
2
are standard in the causal inference literature. In plain language, conditional exchangeability requires that distributional parameters of the potential outcomes, like their (centered) higher moments, are independent of the treatment assignment conditional on covariates. This implies that there must not be any unmeasured confounding. Consistency requires that the observed outcome corresponds to the potential outcome associated with the observed treatment condition. Finally, positivity states that all observations have a non-zero chance of being assigned to the treatment or control groups.

Now, Corollary
2.1
states that
\Psi(P_{0})
and

\Lambda(P_{0})
have causal interpretations when the conditions of Theorem
2
are met. Coupled with Theorem
1
and Corollary
1.1
, these estimands can therefore detect heterogeneous treatment effects from the observed-data even when effect modifiers driving the heterogeneity are unmeasured — so long as the conditional exchangeability condition is satisfied. This is in stark contrast to inference procedures targeting the CATE or TEM-VIPs. Further, inference about
\Psi(P_{0})
and
\Lambda(P_{0})
can refute the heterogeneous ITE assumption in RCTs without needing to condition on
any
pre-treatment covariates, though we suspect that doing so is inefficient. This will be explored in future work.

Inference in a Nonparametric Model

3.1
Preliminaries

Consider some generic parameter
\Theta(P)
for
P\in\mathcal{M}
such that,

\theta(O)
mapping random observations to

\Theta(P)=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\theta(O)]

\hat{P}_{n}
is a plug-in
estimator of

comprised of

(

;
4
)

\begin{split}\sqrt{n}\left(\Theta(\hat{P}_{n})-\Theta(P_{0})\right)&=\sqrt{n}\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[D^{\Theta}(P_{0};O)\right]-\sqrt{n}\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[D^{\Theta}(\hat{P}_{n};O)\right]+\\
&\qquad\sqrt{n}\left(\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}-\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\right)\left(D^{\Theta}(\hat{P}_{n};O)-D^{\Theta}(P_{0};O)\right)-\sqrt{n}R(P_{0},\hat{P}_{n})\;.\end{split}

(5)

Here,
R(P_{0},\hat{P}_{n})
is a second order remainder term in
n
, and
D^{\Theta}_{P}(O)

is the efficient influence function (EIF) of
\Theta
.

The EIF is a functional derivative. Let
\tilde{P}
be a degenerate distribution
with a point mass at
\tilde{o}
, and assume that
\tilde{o}
is in the support
of
P
. Defining the two-component mixture
P_{t}=t\tilde{P}+(1-t)P
for

t\in[0,1]
and appealing to the Riesz representation theorem, the EIF of

\Theta(P)
is defined as

\begin{split}\frac{d\Theta(P_{t})}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}&=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\Theta(P_{t})-\Theta(P)}{t}\\
&=\int\theta(o)\left(d\tilde{P}(o)-dP(o)\right)\\
&=\int D^{\Theta}(P;o)d\tilde{P}(o)\\
&=D^{\Theta}(P;\tilde{o})\;.\end{split}

When
D^{\Theta}(P;O)
has finite variance, we find that
\nicefrac{{d\Theta(P_{t})}}{{dt}}|_{t=0}=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{P}}[D^{\Theta}(P;O)]
and
\Theta
is said to be
pathwise differentiable
. Then

such that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[D^{\Theta}(P;O)]=0
. The EIF therefore generalizes the concept of a directional derivative to functionals:

measures the sensitivity of
\Theta(P)
to perturbations of
P
.

Importantly, pathwise differentiability implies the existence of estimators that can
converge at
o_{P}(n^{-1/2})

(
44
)

\Theta

converges
in probability to

and restrictions are placed on the complexity of

;
4
;

This estimator is obtained by adding the asymptotic

This estimator is the solution to

This estimator is derived by first tilting

3.2
Deriving the Efficient Influence Function of the Potential Outcome Variances

We now apply the general approach outlined in Section

\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)

and

Proposition 1
.

The EIF of
\sigma^{2}(P;a)

is given by

\begin{split}D^{\sigma^{2}(a)}(P;O)&=\frac{I(A=a)\left(Y^{2}-\bar{Q}_{P}^{2}(W,a)+2\mu(P;a)\left(\bar{Q}_{P}(W,a)-Y\right)\right)}{I(A=1)g_{P}(W)+I(A=0)(1-g_{P}(W))}+\\
&\qquad\qquad\bar{Q}_{P}^{2}(W,a)-2\bar{Q}_{P}(W,a)\mu(P;a)+\mu(P;a)^{2}-\sigma^{2}(P;a)\;.\end{split}

It is straightforward to show that the variance of
D^{\sigma^{2}(a)}(P;O)
is
bounded under the assumption of positivity and the boundedness of
Y
. Then

\sigma^{2}(P;a)
is a pathwise differentiable parameter in
\mathcal{M}
for
which asymptotically linear and efficient estimators can be constructed.

3.3
Estimators of the Potential Outcome Variances

We now present nonparametric estimators of
\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
. Let
g_{n}
,

\bar{Q}_{n}^{d}
, and
\mu_{n}(a)
be estimators of
g_{P_{0}}
,
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}^{d}
, and

\mu(P_{0};a)
, respectively, and included in
\hat{P}_{n}
. Then, per
Section
3.1
, the one-step estimator is defined as

\begin{split}\sigma^{2}_{\text{OS}}(\hat{P}_{n};a)&\equiv\sigma^{2}(\hat{P}_{n};a)+\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[D^{\sigma^{2}(a)}(\hat{P}_{n};O)\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\bigg[\frac{I(A=a)\left(Y^{2}-\bar{Q}_{n}^{2}(W,a)+2\mu_{n}(a)\left(\bar{Q}_{n}(W,a)-Y\right)\right)}{I(A=1)g_{n}(W)+I(A=0)(1-g_{n}(W))}+\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\bar{Q}_{n}^{2}(W,a)-2\bar{Q}_{n}(W,a)\mu_{n}(a)\bigg]+\mu_{n}(a)^{2}\;.\end{split}

(6)

Because
D^{\sigma^{2}}(P;a)
is linear in
\sigma^{2}(P;a)
, it follows that

\sigma^{2}_{\text{OS}}(\hat{P}_{n};a)\equiv\sigma^{2}_{\text{EE}}(\hat{P}_{n};a)
,
the estimating equation estimator of
\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
.

The cross-fitted one-step estimator is then given by

\begin{split}\sigma^{2}_{\text{CFOS}}(a)&\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_{k}}{n}\Bigg(\mathbb{E}_{P_{n,k}}\Bigg[\frac{I(A=a)\left(Y^{2}-\bar{Q}_{n,-k}^{2}(W,a)+2\mu_{n,-k}(a)\left(\bar{Q}_{n,-k}(W,a)-Y\right)\right)}{I(A=1)g_{n,-k}(W)+I(A=0)(1-g_{n,-k}(W))}+\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\bar{Q}_{n,-k}^{2}(W,a)-2\bar{Q}_{n,-k}(W,a)\mu_{n,-k}(a)\Bigg]+\mu_{n,-k}(a)^{2}\Bigg)\;,\end{split}

(7)

where
g_{n,-k}
,
\bar{Q}_{n,-k}
,
\bar{Q}_{n,-k}^{2}
and
\mu_{n,-k}
are
nuisance estimators from
\hat{P}_{n,-k}
.

The derivation of a TMLE for
\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
is more involved. Recall from
Section
3.1
that it requires tilting
\hat{P}_{n}
to obtain

P_{n}^{\star}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}[D^{\sigma^{2}(a)}(P_{n}^{\star};a)]\approx 0
. For this estimand,
the asymptotic bias term can be negated by tilting
\bar{Q}_{n}
and

\bar{Q}_{n}^{2}
. Recall that
Y\in(0,1)
a.s., and define the negative
log-likelihood loss function for
\bar{Q}^{d}
as

L(O,a;\bar{Q}^{d})\equiv-\log\left\{\bar{Q}^{d}(W,a)^{Y^{d}}\left(1-\bar{Q}^{d}(W,a)^{(1-Y^{d})}\right)\right\}

and a parametric working submodel for
\bar{Q}^{d}
as

\bar{Q}^{d}(\eta)(O,a)\equiv\text{logit}^{-1}\left(\text{logit}\left(\bar{Q}^{d}(W,a)\right)+\eta H(O,a)\right)\;,

where

H(O,a)\equiv\frac{I(A=a)}{I(A=1)g(W)+I(A=0)(1-g(W))}\;.

The tilted nuisance estimators
\bar{Q}_{n}^{\star}
and
\bar{Q}_{n}^{2\star}
of
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}
and
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}^{2}
, respectively, are obtained by computing
\eta^{d\star}
such that

\eta^{d\star}_{n}\equiv\text{arg min}_{\eta}\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[L\left(O,a;\bar{Q}^{d}_{n}(\eta)(O,a)\right)\right]

for
d=\{1,2\}
. Then

\bar{Q}_{n}^{d\star}\equiv\bar{Q}^{d}_{n}(\eta^{d\star}_{n})
, where we let
\bar{Q}_{n}^{\star}=\bar{Q}_{n}^{1\star}
in a slight abuse of notation. We highlight that,
in addition to
\bar{Q}_{n}^{d}
,
\bar{Q}_{n}^{d\star}
is function of
A
,
Y
, and

g_{n}
through
H_{n}(O,a)
and
\eta_{n}^{d\star}
.
H_{n}
is the empirical
equivalent of
H
that relies on
g_{n}
in place of
g
. Importantly, computing

\eta^{d\star}_{n}
is straightforward: it is the maximum likelihood estimator
of the univariate logistic regression slope coefficient obtained by regressing

Y^{d}
on
H_{n}
using
\text{logit}(\bar{Q}_{n}^{d})
as an offset

(
46
)
. The tilted plug-in estimator of

P_{n}
,
P^{\star}_{n}
, is therefore obtained by replacing
\bar{Q}_{n}
and

\bar{Q}^{2}_{n}
by
\bar{Q}_{n}^{\star}
and
\bar{Q}^{2\star}_{n}
in
\hat{P}_{n}
,
respectively. Owing to the choice of loss function and parametric working
submodels, we find that
\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}[D^{\sigma^{2}(a)}(P_{n}^{\star};a)]=0
.
Finally, the TMLE of
\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
is given by

\begin{split}\sigma^{2}_{\text{TMLE}}(\hat{P}_{n};a)&\equiv\sigma^{2}(P_{n}^{\star};a)\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[\bar{Q}^{2\star}_{n}(O,a)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[\bar{Q}^{\star}_{n}(O,a)\right]^{2}\;.\end{split}

Alternatively, a targeted minimum loss-based estimator using a weighted univariate logistic regression to tilt
\bar{Q}_{n}
and
\bar{Q}_{n}^{2}
could be derived to the same effect
(
39
;
16
)
.

We emphasize that this TMLE is applicable to DGPs in which the outcome is not
bounded between
0
and
1

(
20
)
. It suffices to center and scale the outcome
variable to the unit interval prior to estimating the nuisance parameters. Given
outcomes
\{Y\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the scaled outcome for observation
i
is computed as

\nicefrac{{(Y_{i}-\min_{i}\{Y_{i}\})}}{{(\max_{i}\{Y_{i}\}-\min_{i}\{Y_{i}\})}}
. Once

\sigma^{2}_{\text{TMLE}}(\hat{P}_{n};a)
is evaluated, the appropriately scaled
estimate of
\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
is obtained by multiplying

\sigma^{2}_{\text{TMLE}}(\hat{P}_{n};a)
by
(\max_{i}\{Y_{i}\}-\min_{i}\{Y_{i}\})^{2}
.

The cross-fitted TMLE is then given by

\begin{split}\sigma^{2}_{\text{CFTMLE}}(a)&=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_{k}}{n}\Bigg(\mathbb{E}_{P_{n,k}}\left[\bar{Q}^{2\star}_{n,-k}(O,a)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}\left[\bar{Q}^{\star}_{n,-k}(O,a)\right]^{2}\Bigg)\;,\end{split}

(8)

where
\bar{Q}^{\star}_{n,-k}
and
\bar{Q}^{2\star}_{n,-k}
are the tilted
nuisance estimators of
\bar{Q}_{n,-k}
and
\bar{Q}^{2}_{n,-k}
,
respectively, computed by applying the previously described tilting procedure to

\hat{P}_{n,-k}
.

We emphasize that the above TMLEs possess an important operating characteristic not shared by the one-step estimators: they are plug-in estimators. That is, these TMLEs respect the bounds of the parameter space by construction whereas the one-step estimators do not; the one-step estimators may produce negative estimates when the
\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
is near zero.

3.4
Asymptotic Properties of the Potential Outcome Variance Estimators

We now detail the asymptotic properties of these nonparametric estimators of

\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
, noting that

\lVert f_{n}(O)-f(O)\rVert_{2}^{2}=\int(f_{n}(o)-f(o))^{2}dP(o)
for some
estimator
f_{n}
of
f
under
P\in\mathcal{M}
.

Theorem 3
.

Double Robustness:
If for
a\in\mathcal{A}

1.

\lVert g_{n}(W)-g_{P_{0}}(W)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(1)
or
\lVert\bar{Q}_{n}^{d}(W,a)-\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}^{d}(W,a)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(1)
for
d=1
and
d=2
, and

2.

\mu_{n}(a)-\mu(P_{0};a)=o_{P}(1)
,

then

\sigma^{2}_{\text{OS}}(\hat{P}_{n};a)\overset{P}{\rightarrow}\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
.
The same result holds for
\sigma^{2}_{\text{CFOS}}
,
\sigma^{2}_{\text{TMLE}}
,
and
\sigma^{2}_{\text{CFTMLE}}
.

While the conditions for consistency in Theorem
3

may appear stringent at first glance, we highlight that the first condition
ensures that the second is satisfied when
\mu_{n}(a)
is a doubly robust
estimator of
\mu(P_{0};a)
. That is, for doubly robust estimator
\mu_{n}(a)
,

\lVert\mu_{n}(a)-\mu(P_{0};a)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(1)
when

\lVert g_{n}(W)-g_{P_{0}}(W)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(1)
or

\lVert\bar{Q}_{n}(W,a)-\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}(W,a)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(1)
. This implies
that the previously introduced estimators of
\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
are consistent
in RCTs where the propensity score is known, regardless
of whether the estimators of
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}^{d}(W,a)
are asymptotically biased.
In observational studies, these consistency conditions may be satisfied by
estimating
\mu(P_{0};a)
with a double robust estimator and
g_{P_{0}}
,

\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}
, and
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}^{2}
with flexible machine learning techniques
like the Super Learner ensemble method
(
42
;
35
)
.

We next consider the conditions required of these estimators to be asymptotically linear. We present these results with respect to the non-cross-fit estimators, though they are straightforwardly extended to the cross-fit estimators.

Theorem 4
.

Asymptotic Linearity
: If for
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}

1.

There exists a
P_{0}
-Donsker class
\mathbb{G}_{0}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{P_{0}}[D^{\sigma^{2}(a)}(\hat{P}_{n};O)\in\mathbb{G}_{0}]\rightarrow 1
,

2.

\lVert D^{\sigma^{2}(a)}(\hat{P}_{n};O)-D^{\sigma^{2}(a)}(P_{0};O)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
,

3.

\lVert g_{n}(W)-g_{P_{0}}(W)\rVert_{2}\lVert\bar{Q}_{n}(W,a)-\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}(W,a)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
,

4.

\lVert g_{n}(W)-g_{P_{0}}(W)\rVert_{2}\lVert\bar{Q}^{2}_{n}(W,a)-\bar{Q}^{2}_{P_{0}}(W,a)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
, and

5.

\lVert\mu_{n}(a)-\mu(P_{0};a)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
,

then
\sigma^{2}_{\text{OS}}(\hat{P}_{n};a)-\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)=\mathbb{E}_{P_{n}}[D^{\sigma^{2}(a)}(P_{0};O)]+o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
. The same result holds for
\sigma^{2}_{\text{TMLE}}
.
\sigma^{2}_{\text{CFOS}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\text{CFTMLE}}
are asymptotically linear under conditions 2, 3, 4, and 5.

The first condition of Theorem
4
is an unverifiable assumption that is satisfied when the EIF is Donsker. This is equivalent to placing weak regularity conditions on the nuisance parameters: they must be càdlàg (
continue à droite, limite à gauche
: right continuous with left limits) and have finite suprememum and sectional variation norms
(
19
)
. Note that a class
\mathcal{F}
with bounded supremum norm is
P
-Donsker if
\mathcal{F}
is pre-Gaussian and the empirical process
\mathbb{G}(\mathcal{F})=\{\mathbb{G}(f),f\in\mathcal{F}\}
converges weakly under
L_{\infty}(P)
to the Gaussian process
\mathbb{G}_{P}(\mathcal{F})

(
4
;
44
)
. As previously mentioned, this assumption can be avoided by using cross-fitted estimators. Their reliance on sample-splitting may however reduce their finite-sample efficiency relative their non-cross-fit counterparts when the Donsker condition is in fact satisfied.

The second condition of Theorem
4
is satisfied if, for example, the nuisance parameter estimators of
\hat{P}_{n}
are consistent. As previously discussed, this requirement is generally satisfied by estimating nuisance parameters
g_{P_{0}}
,
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}
, and
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}^{2}
with flexible machine learning methods and
\mu(P_{0};a)
with a doubly robust estimator.

The third and fourth double rate robustness condition of Theorem
4
are met when, for example,
\lVert g_{n}(W)-g_{P_{0}}(W)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
,
\lVert\bar{Q}_{n}(W,a)-\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}(W,a)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
, and
\lVert\bar{Q}^{2}_{n}(W,a)-\bar{Q}^{2}_{P_{0}}(W,a)\rVert_{2}=o_{P}(n^{-1/4})
, though these rates of convergence need not be identical. These shared rate conditions are particularly practical in settings where the propensity score’s functional form is known but those of
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}
and
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}^{2}
are unknown. Then
g_{P_{0}}
can be estimated at the parametric
o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
rate and
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}
and
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}^{2}
can be estimated using flexible machine learning techniques that converge more slowly. Alternatively, when
g_{P_{0}}
,
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}
, and
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}^{2}
are càdlàg and have bounded variation norm, they can be estimated with the Highly Adaptive LASSO to satisfy these rate conditions
(
47
;
3
)
.

Finally, the last condition of Theorem
4
is immediately satisfied when the second condition of the theorem is fulfilled and
\mu_{n}
is a doubly robust estimator. This is because doubly robust estimators of
\mu(P_{0};a)
are efficient and asymptotically linear under the aforementioned double rate robustness requirement, converging at rates of
o_{P}(n^{-1/2})
. In fact, the contribution of the double-robust estimation of
\mu(P_{0};a)
to the second order remainder terms of considered estimators is
o_{P}(n^{-1})
. As such, finite-sample error incurred when estimating
\mu(P_{0};a)
has negligible impact on the finite sample errors of these estimators when using double robust estimators of this nuisance parameter. Inefficient estimators could of course be used instead, though there is little reason to do so given that
g_{P_{0}}
and
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}
must be estimated as part of this inference procedure for
\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
.

Importantly, Theorem
4
implies that
\sqrt{n}(\sigma^{2}_{\text{OS}}(a)~-~\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a))~\overset{D}{\rightarrow}~N(0,\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[D^{\sigma^{2}(a)}(P_{0};O)^{2}])
when the previously described conditions are satisfied. The same is true for
\sigma^{2}_{\text{TMLE}}
,
\sigma^{2}_{\text{CFOS}}
, and
\sigma^{2}_{\text{CFTMLE}}
. As stated in Section
3.1
, this result can be used to construct
\alpha
-level Wald-type confidence intervals, permitting hypothesis testing about
\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
. Additionally, this result extends to RCTs where
g_{P_{0}}
is known and
\mu_{n}
is a doubly robust estimator even if the nuisance estimators of
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}
and
\bar{Q}^{2}_{P_{0}}
are misspecified as, in this scenario, all requirements outlined in Theorem
4
are satisfied.

3.5
Differential Variance Inference

Having derived flexible estimators of
\sigma^{2}(P_{0};a)
and establishing their conditions for semiparametric efficiency in
\mathcal{M}
, we now propose causal machine learning procedures of
\Psi(P_{0})
and
\Lambda(P_{0})
in nonparametric models.

3.5.1
Absolute Differential Variance Estimators

It is straightforward to define the one-step estimator of the absolute differential variance estimand of Equaton (
3
):

\Psi_{\text{OS}}(\hat{P}_{n})=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{\text{OS}}(\hat{P}_{n};1)}-\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{\text{OS}}(\hat{P}_{n};0)}\;.

The variance of this estimator may be constructed from the EIF of
\Psi(P_{0})
, obtained from Proposition
1
and the functional delta method
(
44
)
. The EIF of
\Psi(P_{0})
is given by:

D^{\Psi}(P;O)\equiv\frac{D^{\sigma^{2}(1)}(P;O)}{2\sqrt{\sigma^{2}(P_{0};1)}}-\frac{D^{\sigma^{2}(0)}(P;O)}{2\sqrt{\sigma^{2}(P_{0};0)}}\;.

The TMLE estimator,
\Psi_{\text{TMLE}}
, is similarly defined, as are their cross-fitted counterparts
\Psi_{\text{CFOS}}
and
\Psi_{\text{CFTMLE}}
. These estimators are doubly
robust under the conditions provided in Theorem
3
,
and the cross-fitted estimators are asymptotically linear under the conditions
of Theorem
4
. As such,

\sqrt{n}(\Psi_{\text{OS}}-\Psi(P_{0}))\overset{D}{\rightarrow}N(0,\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[D^{\Psi}(P_{0};O)^{2})])
.
The same is true for
\Psi_{\text{TMLE}}
,
\Psi_{\text{CFOS}}
, and
\Psi_{\text{CFTMLE}}
. These estimators can therefore be used to perform formal hypothesis tests with asymptotic Type-I error rate guarantees about the homogeneous ITE assumption.

3.5.2
Relative Differential Variance Estimators

The one-step estimator of
\Lambda(P_{0})
is then given by

\Lambda_{\text{OS}}(\hat{P}_{n})=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\text{OS}}(\hat{P}_{n};1)}{\sigma^{2}_{\text{OS}}(\hat{P}_{n};0)}\;.

Again relying on Proposition
1
and the functional delta method, we may derive the variance of this estimator based on the EIF of
\Lambda(P_{0})
:

D^{\Lambda}(P;O)\equiv\frac{D^{\sigma^{2}(1)}(P;O)}{\sigma^{2}(P_{0};0)}-\frac{\sigma^{2}(P_{0};1)D^{\sigma^{2}(0)}(P;O)}{\sigma^{2}(P_{0};0)^{2}}\;.

The TMLE, cross-fitted one-step estimator, and cross-fitted TMLE are similarly derived, and denoted by
\Lambda_{\text{TMLE}}
,
\Lambda_{\text{CFOS}}
, and
\Lambda_{\text{CFTMLE}}
, respectively. As with the estimators of
\Psi(P_{0})
, these nonparametric estimators are consistent under the conditions provided in Theorem
3
and are asymptotically linear under the conditions of Theorem
4
.
\Lambda_{\text{TMLE}}
,
\Lambda_{\text{OS}}
,
\Lambda_{\text{CFOS}}
, and
\Lambda_{\text{CFTMLE}}
may in turn be used to perform hypothesis tests about the homogeneous ITE assumption.

4
Simulations

We assess the proposed estimators’ performance through three simulation studies. Simulation Study 1 investigates the double robustness of the relative differential variance estimators established in Theorem
3
. Simulation Study 2 evaluates the asymptotic linearity of the absolute differential variance estimators established in Theorem
4
. Simulation Study 3 gauges the absolute differential variance estimators’ ability to uncover violations of the homogeneous ITE assumption in simulated RCTs. In all numerical experiments,
500
datasets are generated at each of the following sample sizes:
n\in\{125,250,500,1000,2000\}
.

4.1
Simulation Study 1: Double Robustness

We consider the a DGP representative of an observational study:

\begin{split}W_{1}&\sim\text{Bern}(0.3),\\
W_{2}&\sim N(0,1),\\
A\mid W_{1},W_{2}&\sim\text{Bern}(\text{logit}^{-1}((1+W_{1}+W_{2})/4)),\\
Y\mid A,W_{1},W_{2}&\sim N(1+A+W_{1}+W_{2}+AW_{2}+W_{1}W_{2},1+A)\end{split}

Here,
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are the independent confounder variables,
A
is the binary treatment variable, and
Y
is the outcome variable. To introduce treatment effect heterogeneity, we set the variance of
Y|W_{1},W_{2},A
to depend on
A
and specify that
\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}[Y|W_{1},W_{2},A]
includes an interaction between
W_{2}
and
A
. That is,
W_{2}
is a treatment effect modifier.

We take as target of inference the relative differential variance,
\Lambda(P_{0})=2.47
, and evaluate the (absolute) empirical bias of
\Lambda_{\text{OS}}
,
\Lambda_{\text{TMLE}}
,
\Lambda_{\text{CFOS}}
, and
\Lambda_{\text{CFTMLE}}
under four nuisance parameter estimation scenarios. In the first scenario, all nuisance parameter estimators are well-specified. In the second scenario, the propensity score estimator is well-specified and the outcome regression estimators are misspecified. In the third scenario, the propensity score estimators are misspecified and the outcome regression estimators are well-specified. In the final scenario, all nuisance parameters are misspecified.

A main-terms logistic regression conditioning on all confounders is used to estimate the
g_{P_{0}}
in scenarios where the propensity score estimator is well-specified. When the propensity score estimator is misspecified, a logistic regression conditioning on
W_{2}
is used instead. Random Forest estimators are used to estimate
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}
and
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}^{2}
in scenarios where the outcome regressions are well-specified
(
8
)
. When the outcome regressions are misspecified, these nuisance parameters are estimated by the treatment groups’ mean outcomes and mean squared outcomes, respectively.

The results of this simulation study are reported in Figure
1
. As expected, the estimators’ absolute empirical biases are negligible as sample size increases in all but the scenario where every nuisance parameter estimator is misspecified.

These results suggest that
\Lambda_{\text{OS}}
,
\Lambda_{\text{TMLE}}
,
\Lambda_{\text{CFOS}}
, and
\Lambda_{\text{CFTMLE}}
are doubly robust under the considered DGP. Of note,
\Lambda_{\text{OS}}
and
\Lambda_{\text{TMLE}}
have lower empirical bias than
\Lambda_{\text{CFOS}}
and
\Lambda_{\text{CFTMLE}}
across all scenarios. This difference, starkest in the smaller sample sizes, is likely due to the cross-fitted estimators’ reliance on sample splitting.

The absolute empirical bias of the relative differential variance one-step estimator and TMLE, as well as their cross-fitted counterparts, across various sample sizes and nuisance parameter estimator scenarios. Outlying values are truncated and represented by stars to facilitate the interpretation of results. The dashed horizontal lines correspond to the idealized absolute empirical bias.

4.2
Simulation Study 2: Asymptotic Linearity

We consider another DGP resembling that of an observational study:

\begin{split}W_{1}&\sim N(0,1),\\
W_{2}&\sim N(0,1),\\
A\mid W_{1},W_{2}&\sim\text{Bern}(\text{logit}^{-1}((1+W_{1}+W_{2})/4)),\\
Y\mid A,W_{1},W_{2}&\sim N(1-2A+W_{1}^{2}+2I(W_{2}<0),1)\end{split}

Variables
W_{1}
,
W_{2}
,
A
, and
Y
are interpreted as in the previous simulation study. Unlike Simulation Study 1, however, this DGP does not exhibit any heterogeneous treatment effects.

In this simulation study, we take as aim to perform inference about the absolute differential variance,
\Psi(P_{0})=0
, using
\Psi_{\text{OS}}
,
\Psi_{\text{TMLE}}
,
\Psi_{\text{CFOS}}
, and
\Psi_{\text{CFTMLE}}
. In particular, we investigate whether these estimators’ asymptotic linearity properties are approximately achieved in finite samples, as evidenced by their empirical bias, variance, and coverage.

The absolute differential variances nuisance parameters are estimated using Super Learner ensembles. The Super Learner library for
g_{P_{0}}
is made up of a main-terms logistic regression, a multivariate quadratic logistic regression, a multivariate adaptive regression splines estimator
(
18
)
, and Random Forests. The Super Learner library for
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}
is comprised of a main-terms linear model, a multivariate quadratic linear model, a multivariate adaptive regression splines estimator, and Random Forests. Finally, the Super Learner library for
\bar{Q}_{P_{0}}^{2}
consists of a multivariate quadratic linear model and Random Forests.

The results of this experiment are presented in Figure
2
. As expected, absolute empirical bias and empirical variance become negligible as sample size increases. At smaller sample sizes, the empirical bias and variance of the cross-fitted estimators are substantially larger than those of the non-cross-fitted estimators in smaller sample sizes. This behavior is due to the cross-fitted estimators sometimes producing near-zero potential outcome variance estimates. However, the cross-fitted estimators outperform the others in terms of bias starting from
n=500
. The scaled absolute empirical bias, obtained by multiplying the absolute empirical bias by
\sqrt{n}
, is decreasing for all estimators as of
n=1000
, suggesting that these estimators are asymptotically root-
n
consistent. Finally, cross-fitted estimators’ coverage achieves the nominal proportion in samples of size
1000
and
2000
, while their non-cross-fitted counterparts produce empirical coverages slightly below — but converging towards — the desired proportion.

These results imply that
\Psi_{\text{OS}}
,
\Psi_{\text{TMLE}}
,
\Psi_{\text{CFOS}}
, and
\Psi_{\text{CFTMLE}}
are asymptotically linear under the considered DGP, though the cross-fitted estimators’ performance are markedly better than the one-step estimator and TMLE in large samples. Their differential finite-sample behavior might be explained by the use of flexible machine learning algorithms to estimate nonparametric nuisance parameters. Flexible nuisance parameter estimators may be prone to a sub-optimal bias-variance tradeoffs in finite-samples — unless sample-splitting strategies are incorporated — resulting in a slight bias that decreases with growing sample sizes and mildly anti-conservative coverage.

4.3
Simulation Study 3: Detecting Homogeneous ITE Violations Under Measurement Error

We consider the following RCT DGP with an effect modifier that is (possibly) subject to measurement error:

\begin{split}W_{1}&\sim N(0,1)\\
W_{2}&\sim N(0,1)\\
V&\sim\text{Bern}(0.5)\\
M&\sim\text{Bern}(m)\\
U&\sim\text{Bern}(0.2)\\
V^{\text{obs}}&=\begin{cases}U&\text{if }M=1\\
V&\text{if }M=0\end{cases}\\
A|W_{1},W_{2},V=A&\sim\text{Bern}(0.5)\\
Y|W_{1},W_{2},V,A&\sim N(1-2A+W_{1}^{2}+2I(W_{2}<0)+4AV,1)\end{split}

Here,
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are prognostic variables and
V
is a true but possibly mismeasured effect modifier. The measurement error of
V
is introduced through
M
, a Bernoulli random variable with probability
m
representing the likelihood of
V
being replaced by Bernoulli random variable
U
. Thus,
V^{\text{obs}}
is the observed effect modifier subject to measurement error;
V
,
U
, and
M
are unobserved. Binary variable
A
represents the arm assignment, and
Y
is the continuous outcome variable.

In this numerical experiment, we aim to detect treatment effect heterogeneity in four different effect modifier measurement error scenarios:
m\in\{0,1/3,2/3,1\}
. We accomplish this by performing hypothesis tests about the homogeneous ITE assumption using the absolute differential variance estimators,
\Psi_{\text{OS}}
,
\Psi_{\text{TMLE}}
,
\Psi_{\text{CFOS}}
, and
\Psi_{\text{CFTMLE}}
. These estimators’ nuisance parameters are estimated with (misspecified) main-terms linear models, reflecting the standard practice for covariate adjustment in RCTs
(
10
)
, with the exception of the propensity scores. These estimators use the known propensity scores instead. We then benchmark these tests against the treatment effect heterogeneity omnibus test of
14
implemented in the
grf
R package for the causal forest CATE estimator
(
40
)
, comparing their empirical power at a significance cutoff of
0.05
.

The empirical power of the hypothesis tests based on the absolute differential variance estimators and the Causal Forest are presented in Figure
3
. In the absence of measurement error, the Causal Forest approach uniformly dominates the differential variance estimators in samples containing fewer than
500
observations. In samples of at least
500
observations, all but the testing procedure based on
\hat{\Psi}_{\text{CFTMLE}}
correctly reject the homogeneous ITE assumption in virtually all replicates. As the measurement error probability increases, however, the empirical power of the Causal Forest omnibus test steadily decreases — especially in smaller sample sizes — whereas the empirical power of the tests based on the absolute differential variance estimators are minimally affected. Of note, the testing procedures based on
\hat{\Psi}_{\text{TMLE}}
,
\hat{\Psi}_{\text{OS}}
, and
\hat{\Psi}_{\text{CFOS}}
have near identical empirical power in all scenarios and sample sizes, whereas the test based on
\hat{\Psi}_{\text{CFTMLE}}
has noticeably worse performance in all data sets with fewer than
1000
observations. As expected, when the true effect modifier is completely mismeasured, only the proposed differential-variance-based tests can reliably detect heterogeneous treatment effects. Indeed,
\hat{\Psi}_{\text{TMLE}}
,
\hat{\Psi}_{\text{OS}}
, and
\hat{\Psi}_{\text{CFOS}}
have an empirical power above
80\%
even when
n=250
in this setting. The Causal-Forest-based approach’s power, however, is negligible in all sample sizes.

These results demonstrate that differential variance inference procedures can reliably detect heterogeneous treatment effects in settings where CATE-based procedures may be underpowered due to, for example, measurement error. Further, the proposed approach can uncover violations of the homogeneous ITE assumption even when all effect modifiers are omitted from the data — so long as the differential variance identifiability conditions of Corollary
2.1
are satisfied, as in an RCT.

5
Application

Targeted temperature management, an intervention that induces mild, controlled hypothermia, was previously indicated for comatose adults following cardiac arrest. Pre-clinical and observational studies had previously suggested that this intervention could improve these patients’ neurological outcomes
(
23
)
. At the time, however, there was clinical uncertainty regarding the target temperature that optimized patient outcomes. The Target Temperature Management at 33°C versus 36°C after Cardiac Arrest (TTM; NCT01020916) and Hypothermia versus Normothermia after Out-of-hospital Cardiac Arrest (TTM2; NCT02908308) trials investigated whether various target temperatures improved the six-month overall survival of unconscious patients resuscitated from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest
(
33
;
15
)
. Nine hundred fifty (
950
) and
1900
unconscious adult patients admitted to participating hospitals following an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest were randomized to one of two treatment arms in the TTM and TTM2 trials, respectively. In the TTM trial, patients were randomized with equal probability to targeted temperature management at either
33\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
or
36\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
. In the TTM2 trial, patients were randomized with equal probability to either targeted temperature management at
33\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
or targeted normothermia, which aims to maintain patients body temperature below
37.8\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
.

Neither trial reported a statistically significant difference between their respective arms’ outcomes. Unadjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the hazard ratios in each trial. This parameter was the primary estimand in the TTM trial and the secondary estimand in the TTM2 trial. In the TTM trial, the estimated hazard ratio was
1.06
with a
95\%
confidence interval of
(0.89,1.28)
. In TTM2, the estimated hazard ratio was
1.08
with a
95\%
confidence interval of
(0.95,1.23)
. The primary estimand in the TTM2 trial was the relative risk of patients dying by six months; the estimate was
1.04
with a
95\%
confidence interval of
(0.94,1.14)
. As a result, contemporary guidelines recommend maintaining patients’ temperatures between
32\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}
and
37.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolCelsius}

(
23
)
.

While these null results were consistent across subgroups, clinical uncertainty remains. Some clinicians hypothesize that certain clinical traits — such as severity of neurological injury on admission and duration of low blood flow — may modify the effect of target temperature management
(
31
)
, though this has not yet been formally studied. Investigating this hypothesis might be challenging owing to the difficulty of accurately measuring certain variables in critical care settings. For example, the time to initiation of advanced life saving support, a baseline covariate related to the duration of low blood flow, was not recorded directly in the TTM and TTM2 trials, but derived from emergency medical services and hospital notes.

We applied our absolute differential variance procedure as a sensitivity analysis to the TTM and TTM2 trials’ subgroup analyses. Guided by the numerical experiment results presented in Section
4
and using an identical nuisance parameter estimation strategy, we estimated the absolute differential variances of the truncated survival times across the trials’ respective treatment groups using the
\Psi_{\text{OS}}
and
\Psi_{\text{TMLE}}
, adjusting for age, biological sex, time to return of spontaneous circulation, time to advanced life saving support, presence of circulatory shock on admission, initial cardiac rhythm, adrenaline administration, whether the cardiac arrest was witnessed by a bystander, presence of bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and functional outcome before cardiac arrest (defined according to Cerebral Performance Category in the TTM trial and modified Rankin Scale score in the TTM2 trial). Patients’ Charlson Comorbidity Index was additionally adjusted for in the TTM2 trial data analysis
(
11
)
. We note that we treated the six-month survival times as continuous outcomes given the virtual lack of right-censoring. The results are presented in Table

. We highlight that the conditions of Corollary
2.1
and Theorem
4
required for valid causal inference using these estimators are met by virtue of the data being generated in an RCT.

6
Discussion

We proposed causally interpretable contrasts of the potential outcome variances in the full-data DGPs with binary treatment assignment mechanisms continuous outcomes, showed that these contrasts can be used to detect heterogeneous treatment effects even when treatment effect modifiers are omitted from the data, provided conditions under which these causal estimands are estimable in observed-data DGPs, and derived EIF-based, assumption-lean estimators of these contrasts. We then studied these estimators’ asymptotic properties — theoretically and empirically — showing that they are doubly robust under mild conditions about the DGP. We also established that, under unverifiable convergence rate conditions about the nuisance parameter estimators, these differential variance estimators are regular and asymptotically linear in nonparametric models. This result, in turn, permits straightforward hypothesis testing about the homogeneous ITE assumption.

This work gives rise to several exciting avenues of research. First, the proposed methodology may be extended to DGPs with diverse treatment assignment mechanisms and outcome types, permitting the formal detection of heterogeneous treatment effects in myriad settings where CATE-based approaches are inappropriate. Second,
\bar{Q}^{2}_{P_{0}}
is challenging to estimate; it is an uncommon nuisance parameter without, to the best of our knowledge, bespoke estimators described in the literature. Future work might identify best-practices to perform inference about it, potentially improving finite-sample inference about
\Psi(P_{0})
and
\Lambda(P_{0})
. Third, differential variance inference procedures may inform the design of clinical trials aiming to generate evidence of heterogeneous treatment effects. Our proposed approach is particularly amenable to trials where it is challenging to collect potential treatment effect modifiers, such as in settings with resource constraints or instrument error. Alternatively, this methodology might be incorporated into existing heterogeneous treatment effect inference workflows, either as an initial analysis to determine if CATE-based procedures should be applied or as a sensitivity analysis after CATE-based approaches fail to produce evidence of heterogeneity.
