Sleep irregularity is associated with reduced wellbeing in patients with cirrhosis
Authors: Adam P. Buckholz, Himel Mallick, Avesh J. Thuluvath, Olivia Blocker, Brian McSteen, Russell Rosenblatt, Elizabeth Verna, Edwin C. Kan, Ana C. Krieger, Robert S. Brown Jr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsHealth-related quality of life (HRQoL) is frequently impaired in cirrhosis. Among the most critical components of reduced HRQoL in cirrhosis is disturbed sleep. Despite the importance of sleep in cirrhosis and overall health, sleep is rarely clinically assessed and the specific aspects of sleep which drive reduced wellbeing are understudied. Remote monitoring with wearable technology may facilitate sleep assessment and targeted intervention.MethodsWe enrolled 119 participants with cirrhosis across two academic medical centers and collected baseline clinical, cognitive, and laboratory assessment and 6 months of longitudinal wearable tracking with an Oura Ring. HRQoL was assessed with the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ) and subjective sleep quality with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. Association of sleep metrics with HRQoL and subjective sleep was assessed using a multivariable mixed effects model, with mediation analysis further analyzing the relationship.ResultsAcross analysis of 12,516 sleep episodes in 88 participants who completed all assessments and ≥14 valid sleep nights, there was a strong association between measured sleep and reported sleep quality as well as HRQoL, with reduction in sleep efficiency, overall sleep time, and Sleep Regularity Index (SRI). In adjusted mediation analyses, SRI was the strongest mediator (49.9% total mediation effect, ACME = -0.093, p <0.001) of the relationship between liver disease severity (as measured by Child–Turcotte–Pugh score) and CLDQ.ConclusionsIn a large prospective study of remote sleep monitoring in liver disease, we found that sleep regularity had a strong association with patient-reported sleep quality and wellbeing, and was the strongest sleep metric mediating the relationship between progressive liver disease and overall HRQoL. These findings suggest the potential for circadian-targeted interventions in patients with cirrhosis.Impact and implicationsSleep impairment is a major contributor to reduced wellbeing in cirrhosis, but existing sleep assessments often are too cumbersome to be used clinically and fail to capture long-term sleep patterns and circadian disruption. Using 6 months of wearable-derived sleep data, this study identifies sleep irregularity, as captured by the Sleep Regularity Index, as the sleep metric most strongly associated with patient-reported sleep and overall wellbeing impairment. These findings suggest that sleep regularity, a marker of circadian rhythm dysfunction, may be an important digital biomarker for progressive worsening in cirrhosis and highlight a possible target for future interventions. If validated in future studies, wearable monitoring of sleep could provide clinicians and researchers a scalable approach for identifying patients with cirrhosis at increased risk for impaired wellbeing through sleep disturbance and monitor response to treatment.

Introduction

Cirrhosis is a complex and progressive liver disease that is one of the leading causes of morbidity and death worldwide. Although the clinical factors that most strongly correlate to mortality, including variceal hemorrhage, ascites, infection, and hepatic encephalopathy, have been well described, cirrhosis also leads to significant impairment in wellbeing, measured as health-related quality of life (HRQoL). HRQoL is reduced in cirrhosis in both the compensated and decompensated stage.1 Furthermore, the reduced HRQoL is an independent predictor of clinical outcomes such as hospitalization and mortality. Despite this, the factors that most contribute to decline in quality of life, especially in patients with earlier stage disease, are poorly understood. Biochemical indices, such as the model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score, only partially explain the reduction of HRQoL seen in cirrhosis.2 Better understanding of the mediators of reduced HRQoL in cirrhosis can help in identifying those patients at high risk of impairment and potentially for management strategies to ameliorate detriments to QoL and improve outcomes.

The direct relationship between sleep quality and overall wellbeing is well established. Sleep impairment is highly prevalent at all stages of liver disease,3 and is a key predictor of wellbeing.4 For example, both objective5 and subjective sleep impairment6 have been previously associated with neurocognitive impairment in cirrhosis. Reduced sleep quality using questionnaire assessment was associated with later time in bed and reduced total sleep time in a 14-day actigraphy study of patients with cirrhosis.7 Prior research has documented poor sleep efficiency, prolonged wake after sleep onset (WASO), rapid eye movement (REM) sleep loss, and delayed sleep onset in cirrhosis, often linked to hepatic encephalopathy, fatigue, and impaired quality of life.[7], [8], [9] Yet, existing sleep interventions typically target only one domain at a time, and the specific aspects of sleep that most strongly drive impaired wellbeing in cirrhosis remain unclear. This gap contributes to the limited availability of targeted, evidence-based sleep interventions in cirrhosis despite their clear clinical importance.

Traditional approaches to measuring sleep quality pose additional challenges. Polysomnography, often considered the gold standard for sleep assessment,10 research grade actigraphy, and subjective survey-based assessment carry limitations that restrict their clinical utility, including cost, inconvenience, lack of specificity, and inability to capture longitudinal variation. Emerging evidence suggests that modern commercial wearables, which integrate multiple sensors, especially photoplethysmography,11 and the addition of new sensor integration allows wearables to outperform the more expensive, less user-friendly research grade actigraphy.12 Wearables also allow for longer term assessment of day-to-day variability in behavior, providing significantly improved ecological validity compared with single-night measurement or questionnaires.

Given the growing body of evidence pointing to the clinical relevance of circadian misalignment and sleep irregularity in cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases, clinically feasible measurement tools, such as wearable trackers, will likely provide useful and potentially actionable data.13 Actigraphy studies have primarily focused on total sleep time (TST), sleep efficiency (SE), and nocturnal fragmentation to describe these abnormalities. However, sleep irregularity across days, a hallmark of circadian rhythm disruption, has not been formally quantified in this population. The Sleep Regularity Index (SRI) is a validated non-parametric actigraphy metric reflecting the likelihood of being in the same sleep/wake state at any two time points 24 h apart.14 Several studies have noted associations with sleep regularity and HRQoL,15 cardiovascular outcomes,16 and even mortality.17 In one study using actigraphy for 14 days, SRI was lower in patients with cirrhosis compared with controls, highlighting circadian instability as a potential contributor to symptom burden.18 SRI is endorsed as a key circadian biomarker in recent wearable and actigraphy position papers.19,20 In this study, we longitudinally evaluated objective sleep metrics captured using commercial wearable technology alongside patient-reported HRQoL measures of overall health and sleep quality to identify sleep patterns associated with impaired HRQoL. We hypothesized that sleep irregularity is a key driver of impaired wellbeing and may help explain the relationship between progressive liver disease and reduced HRQoL.

Patients and methods

Patient population

Between 1 August 2023 and 1 August 2025, ambulatory patients with cirrhosis were recruited from the Center for Liver Disease and Transplantation, which cares for patients with liver disease at two large academic hospitals, Weill Cornell Medical Center and Columbia University Medical Center. Eligible patients were required to possess a Bluetooth capable smart device and be able to consent and read in English or Spanish. Cirrhosis was diagnosed clinically through laboratory, imaging, and pathology findings. Informed consent was obtained during an ambulatory visit with a hepatologist for cirrhosis. After providing informed consent, participants underwent a series of surveys, including the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ). Baseline clinical, demographic, and laboratory data were collected including cirrhosis history such as history of decompensation, medication use, alcohol use, and current ascites and/or hepatic encephalopathy as graded by the West Haven Criteria. Cognitive status was assessed with the Psychometric Hepatic Encephalopathy Test (PHES), graded according to previously published and validated criteria, with PHES ≤-4 considered impaired cognition.21 This study was approved by the applicable Institutional Review Board at both Weill Cornell and Columbia University Medical Centers. All participants provided written informed consent, and de-identified data was shared using an active, executed data sharing agreement between universities.

Sleep data collection

Participants were given an Oura 3.0 finger-worn fitness tracker (Oura Health Oy, Oulu, Finland) for use during the duration of the study. The tracker and membership were provided free of charge for the duration of the study. They were able to access the data collected for their personal use if desired and asked to wear the ring day and night. No other study incentives were provided. Participants were assisted in downloading the application as well as in choosing an appropriately sized ring and given instruction on appropriate wear, preferably on their non-dominant hand, and on how to charge the ring. Participants were assigned an anonymous username and e-mail address following the convention username: ldps001 and e-mail ldps001@med.cornell.edu to protect privacy when using the Oura application. Patients were not required to have a personal e-mail address or check e-mail. They were instructed to open the application at least weekly to permit passive data transfer. All data were transferred via Bluetooth to their personal smart device, and to a cloud-based server (‘Oura Teams’) where it could be accessed by the study team in .csv format.

Oura Ring and variables of interest

The Oura 3.0 fitness tracker is a consumer grade wearable device that is worn on a finger. Like many other consumer wearables, it is equipped with several sensors. These include photoplethysmography, which utilizes light to assess blood vessel volume across time, a triaxial accelerometer to assess movement and a temperature sensor to assess skin temperature. These signals are then pre-processed using a machine learning model into derived outputs which are available to the research team, including heart rate, respiratory rate, heart rate variability, temperature deviation, sleep staging, and metabolic workload on an epoch level. The metabolic equivalent of task (MET) is reported across a 5-min epoch, where ‘0’ denotes that the participant was not wearing the ring. A total 12 × 24 = 288 epochs are available each day. In our study, a ‘0’ was considered non-wear or non-adherence, such that 12/288 epochs of ‘0’ MET would be considered 5% non-wear, or 95% adherence. A 24-h day with no data available could be related to any of several factors including (1) total non-wear of the device, (2) wearing of an uncharged device, (3) failure to open the Oura application on their smart device to transmit study data, or (4) failure of that device to have internet connection to transmit to the study team. In any case, absence of data was considered non-adherence unless the participant directly withdrew from the study.

Sleep scoring with the Oura Ring is automated, meaning no user input is required, and relies on a proprietary algorithm that incorporates sensor data and correlates to established findings regarding movement, changes in heart rate and body temperature to identify sleep onset, offset and sleep stages including light, deep, and REM sleep. Sleep periods could be punctuated by periods of wakefulness up to 1 h, which are categorized as WASO. If >1 h of wakefulness was recorded consecutively, the sleep period would automatically conclude. If sleep was regained after >1 h, a separate sleep period would be recorded.

Defining sleep metrics of interest

Sleep data were analyzed from all participants who recorded at least 14 full days of wear, defined by <20% of the day as non-wear time and had complete cognitive and survey data at baseline. Sleep periods at least 1 h in length were included in this analysis. As the study was designed to observe for 6 months, the range for possible analyzed days for participants was 14–180. Objective sleep was a priori operationalized by using defined measures recommended by the World Sleep Society (Table S1).20,22

First, a daily sleep midpoint was defined as the midpoint of the longest sleep period of each day. All sleep measured within 6 h before and 6 h after their longest sleep period of the day was considered part of a primary sleep window. The longest sleep period was identified as the longest single recorded sleep period ending after 18:00 on the day of interest and before 18:00 on the following day with less than 30 min of continuous interruption. All other sleep periods were considered outside of window.

Analysis of primary outcome of interest

The primary outcome of this study was measured sleep regularity, defined by the SRI, and its association with HRQoL, defined by the overall CLDQ score. Additional associations between cirrhosis, wellbeing, and measured sleep were also assessed, including for metrics related to circadian rhythm and sleep physiology and homeostasis. With the exception of variability measures (standard deviations of sleep onset, sleep midpoint, and sleep offset), which were summarized to a single value per participant and analyzed using Welch’s two-sample t-tests, all other associations between subjectively impaired sleep quality, defined by a PSQI ≥5, overall quality of life (CLDQ) and objective measures of sleep via wearable tracking was assessed using a univariable mixed effects model (MEM) with a participant-level random intercept to account for repeated daily observations. The association between measured SRI and factors associated with progressive liver disease including Child–Turcotte–Pugh (CTP) score, MELD, presence of ascites, a history of decompensation (defined as an event of overt hepatic encephalopathy, clinically apparent ascites and/or variceal hemorrhage), a history of overt hepatic encephalopathy and cognition at baseline as measured by the PHES, animal naming, and Stroop/EncephalApp tests was further assessed in multivariable MEM adjusting for age and sex. In secondary models focused on PHES and prior Overt Hepatic Encephalopathy (OHE), we additionally fit SRI models further adjusted either for baseline laboratory markers (total bilirubin, albumin, INR, and current ascites) or for MELD 3.0 to evaluate robustness of these associations. Because the continuous SRI analysis suggested numerically small but statistically significant associations, and because prior literature shows that even modest differences in SRI can have clinical relevance, we performed an additional ordinal analysis by categorizing SRI into tertiles (low, medium, high). We then fitted cumulative logit models to evaluate associations with clinical, cognitive, and patient-reported outcomes, allowing further evaluation of whether clinical and patient-reported factors are associated with meaningfully higher levels of sleep regularity.

Mediation analysis

To determine the key sleep factors mediating the relationship between progressive liver disease and HRQoL, a two-stage mediation analysis was performed. First, sleep metrics significantly associated with both CTP and CLDQ were identified and retained as candidate mediators. Second, each candidate mediator was evaluated using subject-level causal mediation models (CTP → sleep metric → CLDQ), implemented with the mediation R package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and adjusted for age and sex. For each model, 2,000 bootstrap simulations were used to estimate the average causal mediation effect (ACME), the average direct effect (ADE), the total effect (TE), and the proportion mediated. The ACME quantifies the indirect effect of CTP on CLDQ operating through the sleep mediator, whereas the ADE reflects the direct effect of CTP on CLDQ not explained by the mediator; the TE is the sum of these components, and the proportion mediated represents the percentage of the total effect attributable to the mediation pathway. Benjamini–Hochberg–adjusted p values were additionally computed, and mediators were considered statistically significant when the ACME adjusted p value was <0.05. We also repeated the mediation analysis using PSQI as the outcome to evaluate whether sleep factors similarly mediated the relationship between CTP and subjective sleep quality. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.6.1.

Results

Study cohort

After excluding those with fewer than 14 fully adherent days and missing survey or clinical data, a total of 88 participants met criteria for analysis, with 20,064 sleep periods captured. After excluding 7,548 episodes <1 h, a total of 12,516 sleep episodes were analyzed for the specified outcomes (Fig. 1). Similarly, compared with those reporting high HRQoL (CLDQ ≥5, n = 39), patients with impaired HRQoL had lower SRI (81.1 vs. 83.3, p <0.01). Discussion

In this prospective cohort of patients with cirrhosis, we used remote monitoring with commercial wearable technology to characterize sleep–wake patterns and evaluate their relationship with liver disease severity and wellbeing, resulting in several key findings. First, the relationship between self-reported sleep quality and wellbeing in cirrhosis is strongly reaffirmed in our study. Second, worsened liver disease defined by CTP is associated with both poorer sleep quality and wellbeing. Third, we identified several sleep-related factors that were strongly associated with both progressive liver disease and reported wellbeing. Finally, mediation analysis demonstrated that sleep regularity was the strongest mediator of the relationship between liver dysfunction and wellbeing, suggesting that circadian organization rather than isolated nightly sleep parameters may be most relevant to patients’ lived experience.

The identification of sleep irregularity as a key mediator of wellbeing in this study has both biological and clinical relevance. First, identification of circadian rhythm dysfunction is not possible using single-night assessment, which remains the most common approach to sleep assessment in clinical care. In cirrhosis, impaired circadian rhythm has been well described, with research linking it to impaired hepatic clearance of melatonin,23 systemic inflammation, ammonia dysregulation, and autonomic dysfunction. There have been mixed previous findings regarding the impact of cirrhosis on sleep architecture. In one study enrolling only patients with obstructive sleep apnea, a common co-morbid condition with cirrhosis, the relative impact of cirrhosis on sleep physiology was modest.24 This study suggests that longitudinal monitoring may be of more benefit for identifying sleep disturbances in cirrhosis. Moreover, the temporal organization of sleep, rather than just its subjective quality, may have increased clinical relevance. A key strength of this study is the large study population, comprehensive baseline clinical assessment, and long-term longitudinal follow up, increasing the power to determine modulators of wellbeing.

There are also potential therapeutic implications of this finding. Given that greater sleep irregularity (lower SRI) was associated with worse quality of life in this cohort of patients with cirrhosis, interventions aimed at improving sleep regularity and stabilizing daily behavioral rhythms, such as consistent sleep–wake scheduling, timed light exposure, structured physical activity, and circadian-aligned behavioral strategies may represent a target for future investigation.25 These approaches directly address sleep regularity rather than just sleep duration or depth. In one small pilot study, the use of melatonin, which is a key treatment option for circadian rhythm misalignment,26,27 significantly improved subjective sleep quality in cirrhosis, but this was not accompanied by objective sleep measurement.28 Additionally, circadian phase misalignment, which has been previously described,13 may be an optimal target given the later and irregular sleep midpoints in our population. Delayed sleep phase, manifested in the late and variable sleep midpoint in our study, can also lead to social jet lag, further impairing wellbeing and performance.29 Together, these data suggest that circadian-based approaches may represent a promising and underutilized therapeutic avenue for sleep disturbance in cirrhosis.

The use of wearable technology in this study is both a key strength and limitation. Unlike traditional polysomnography and actigraphy, commercial wearables can simultaneously capture information regarding sleep physiology (e.g. stages of sleep) and circadian timing patterns over extended periods. Previous research which examines the objective sleep metrics that are associated with sleep disturbance generally has relied on either PSG30 or actigraphy,7 limiting comparative analysis. Continuous, minimally obtrusive measurement with wearables may better reflect habitual sleep behavior; the long duration of this study may also reduce measurement bias with less susceptibility to period effects or Hawthorne bias. Use of wearable technology may introduce selection bias. Although individuals with more advanced disease, alcohol use, and sleep apnea were not excluded, the study cohort had lower reported rates of these conditions than would be expected in a real-world monitoring population. While this may reduce confounding in sleep-related analyses, it limits generalizability. Notably, both obstructive sleep apnea31 and alcohol32 use are frequently underdiagnosed or underreported in cirrhosis populations, further complicating interpretation. In general, the requirement of Bluetooth connectivity and a smartphone device may also have served as a selection bias. The number of patients who declined participation on the basis of technology unavailability was not systematically collected but anecdotally was low. Regardless, study participation and retention and therefore data availability as well as technological capability may vary based on sociodemographic factors and could limit generalizability of the data.

One limitation of this study is the limited research that exists to validate the accuracy of wearable technology in this population for sleep monitoring, as well as the reliance on automatically detected sleep periods. While several studies have validated Oura for sleep–wake determination,33 the accuracy will increase with longer sleep periods. Short sleep periods, such as those <1 h, may be difficult to differentiate from periods of wakeful inactivity, such as watching television or reading in a relatively motionless state. For this reason, the decision was made to systematically exclude sleep periods <1 h. While this likely increases the specificity for sleep, it may reduce sensitivity. It is also possible that short periods of sleep are clinically meaningful in terms of describing sleep irregularity and predicting impaired QoL. Overall, however, it is likely that the use of wearables in this study provides adequate accuracy34,35 with the added benefit of longitudinal granularity. Additionally, there is increasing evidence that wearable trackers like Oura may be more accurate for circadian rhythm analysis than actigraphy.36

In conclusion, there is now ample research describing the biologic underpinnings of sleep disturbance in cirrhosis, as well as its bidirectional relationship with disease outcomes and QoL. However, to date there are limited targeted diagnostic or therapeutic approaches available for patients with cirrhosis and disturbed sleep. The identification in this large longitudinal study of sleep irregularity as a key mediator of subjective sleep quality and overall wellbeing, as identified using scalable wearable technology, could lead to the development of circadian rhythm dysfunction as a critical and targetable biomarker in this condition. A prominent target for future studies utilizing longitudinal remote monitoring should be determining whether clinical deterioration, such as from compensated to decompensated disease, are preceded or paralleled by changes in sleep characteristics, for example circadian rhythm. If so, sleep measurement may also be a reasonable target for predicting clinical worsening and early intervention. Future studies should validate these findings and devise interventions which may help to fill this gap and improve both QoL and clinical outcomes in this population.
