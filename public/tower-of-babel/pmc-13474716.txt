Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria synergistically enhance growth and cadmium accumulation of hyperaccumulator Sedum plumbizincicola
Authors: Guobing Tian, Qian Yang, Senhan Yang, Longhua Wu, Yongmei He, Fangdong Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB) are commonly found in cadmium (Cd)-contaminated soils and have the potential to enhance phytoremediation. However, the synergistic effects and underlying mechanisms of AMF and PSB on plant growth and Cd accumulation in hyperaccumulators remain largely unclear. A pot experiment was conducted using Sedum plumbizincicola grown in Cd-contaminated soil (8.99 mg kg⁻¹) to investigate the effects of single inoculation and co-inoculation with AMF (Funneliformis mosseae) and PSB (Serratia marcescens).ResultInoculation with AMF and PSB, especially co-inoculation, significantly improved nutrient availability and acid phosphatase activity in the rhizosphere soil, root morphology, and leaf mineral nutrient accumulation, thereby promoting plant growth. Compared with the control, co-inoculation (AMF + PSB) increased shoot and root biomass by 79% and 46%, respectively. Co-inoculation also decreased rhizosphere soil pH and markedly enhanced Cd bioavailability, with a 250% increase in CaCl2‑extractable Cd concentration. Meanwhile, co-inoculation upregulated the expression of Cd detoxification-related genes (SpMT2, SepPCS, and SpHIPP45) by 6–9%, leading to 182% and 113% higher Cd accumulation in shoots and roots, respectively. Partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM) indicated that the synergistic association of AMF and PSB contributed to enhanced Cd accumulation, which was linked to improved plant growth and increased rhizosphere soil Cd bioavailability.ConclusionsThese results demonstrate that AMF and PSB synergistically improve growth and Cd phytoextraction in S. plumbizincicola, providing a feasible microbial strategy for enhancing phytoremediation of Cd-contaminated farmland soils.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09186-2.

Introduction

Agricultural soil cadmium (Cd) contamination has become a significant and urgent global environmental issue that hinders safe crop production and threatens public health [1]. Approximately 14–17% of the world’s arable land, equivalent to nearly 242 million hectares, is contaminated with toxic metals, with Cd having the highest exceedance rate among the seven heavy metals, reaching 9.0% (− 1.9%/1.5%) [2]. As a typical toxic heavy metal, Cd is highly mobile and persistent, and excessive input can lead to soil degradation and fertility loss [3]. Cadmium ions are readily absorbed and accumulated by crops, thereby inhibiting plant growth and severely reducing agricultural yield and quality [4]. Therefore, efficient and targeted strategies are urgently required to remediate Cd-contaminated agricultural soils. Notably, remediation of polluted soils should not only reduce the bioavailability of metals in soil but also, more importantly, remove metals from polluted soils [5]. Compared with physical, chemical, and other biological methods, employing hyperaccumulator plants for phytoremediation is environmentally friendly, cost-effective, and can effectively reduce the adverse impacts of heavy metals on ecosystems by transferring them from the soil [6].

Sedum plumbizincicola is a Cd and Zn hyperaccumulator that was discovered at a Pb-Zn mining area in Zhejiang province, eastern China [7]. S. plumbizincicola has become a core remediation material for phytoremediation of Cd-contaminated soils and an ideal model plant for analyzing heavy metal accumulation and adaptation mechanisms under extreme environments due to its strong resistance, accumulation and detoxification ability against cadmium (Cd) [4, 8, 9]. It is suitable for phytoremediation of Cd-contaminated acidic soils owing to its high bioconcentration factor (BCF), translocation factor (TF), and shoot biomass [10]. The shoot Cd concentration of S. plumbizincicola varied from 59.1 to 386 mg kg⁻¹, with total Cd extraction ranging from 61.3 to 1390 g ha⁻¹, averaging 339 g ha⁻¹ [10]. However, the extraction efficiency of hyperaccumulator plants is generally limited by low biomass and slow growth. Accordingly, the optimization of phytoremediation technology hinges on the promotion of the hyperaccumulator plants’ growth, which further enhances their heavy metal accumulation capacity and total metal removal. The synergistic remediation strategy of microbes and hyperaccumulator plants can effectively improve extraction efficiency [6]. One of the most promising and sustainable technologies is rhizosphere inoculation with plant growth-promoting microorganisms (PGPMs) [11]. This technology has advantages such as high cost-effectiveness, environmental friendliness, and low risk. It not only significantly enhances plant resistance against multiple abiotic stressors (e.g., heavy metal toxicity) but also effectively improves plant growth, thereby strengthening remediation effects [12, 13]. Since the improvement of phytoremediation efficiency is closely related to the types of microbes [12, 14], screening and utilizing core functional strains with growth-promoting and heavy metal mobilization potential is crucial for improving phytoremediation efficiency.

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB) are extensively distributed in Cd-contaminated soils. AMF are microorganisms capable of establishing symbiotic relationships with the roots of most terrestrial plants. They can extend the effective absorption area of roots through their hyphal networks, thereby improving root morphology, enhancing nutrient acquisition, and promoting plant growth [15]. AMF can also modulate plant hormone homeostasis to enhance the physiological tolerance of plants and regulate Cd uptake, translocation, and chemical transformation in plants, thereby reducing Cd phytotoxicity [16]. PSB can convert insoluble soil phosphorus fractions (e.g., Po, Ca-P, and Fe-P) into plant-available forms by secreting metabolites (e.g., organic acids) or through synergistic interactions with other organisms [17]. They can also alter soil pH and redox potential, thereby enhancing the solubility of heavy metals in soil [18]. Furthermore, PSB can secrete siderophores that chelate Cd ions to form Cd-siderophore chelates [19], further mobilizing soil-immobilized Cd and facilitating plant Cd uptake and accumulation, thereby enhancing Cd phytoextraction [20]. Collectively, AMF and PSB not only exhibit direct remediation potential for Cd-contaminated soils but also indirectly enhance phytoextraction efficiency by promoting plant growth. Studies have shown that single-strain inoculation can promote the growth of S. plumbizincicola and enhance Cd uptake and accumulation in the plant [21]. Furthermore, co-inoculation with AMF and PSB has been successfully applied in plant remediation under abiotic stress conditions (e.g., drought and salinity), and its synergistic enhancement mechanism has been well validated [22, 23]. Current research largely focuses on the individual effects of AMF or PSB on rhizosphere soil properties [24, 25], yet how co-inoculation synergistically reshapes the rhizosphere microenvironment of S. plumbizincicola remains uncharacterized, and the bidirectional coupling between microbial metabolite crosstalk and Cd bioavailability remains poorly understood. While single microbes can independently mobilize Cd or influence Cd transport [26, 27], whether co-inoculation establishes a functional “mobilization–uptake” relay pathway remains unproven. Single inoculations can modulate the expression of Cd-related genes [28]. However, whether co-inoculation exerts synergistic transcriptional effects in S. plumbizincicola remains unclear. Although AMF alone can upregulate key detoxification genes, such as MT and HIPP family members, to limit Cd uptake and translocation in plants [26], the synergistic regulation of hyperaccumulator-specific detoxification genes (e.g., SpMT2, SepPCS, and SpHIPP45) by co-inoculation with AMF and PSB remains completely unexplored. Therefore, the synergistic mechanisms in the AMF-PSB-S. plumbizincicola tripartite symbiotic system lack direct experimental evidence, and whether co-inoculation exerts a synergistic effect on Cd hyperaccumulation in this species represents a critical and unresolved research gap.

Therefore, elucidating the synergistic mechanisms underlying Cd detoxification and accumulation in the symbiotic system of AMF, PSB, and S. plumbizincicola is crucial for optimizing phytoremediation techniques for Cd-contaminated agricultural soils. This study investigated the synergistic effects of AMF and PSB on growth and Cd accumulation of S. plumbizincicola. The objectives are: (1) to investigate the effects of single and co-inoculation with AMF and PSB on the growth and Cd accumulation of S. plumbizincicola; (2) to explore the effects of co-inoculation (AMF + PSB) on rhizosphere soil Cd bioavailability and Cd absorption, transport, and accumulation capacity of S. plumbizincicola; (3) to analyze the mechanisms by which the plant growth and rhizosphere soil Cd bioavailability of S. plumbizincicola regulate Cd accumulation under co-inoculation using a partial least squares path model (PLS-PM), and to clarify the key pathways regulating Cd accumulation. This study aims to improve the Cd phytoextraction efficiency of S. plumbizincicola and provide a theoretical basis and technical guidance for safe agricultural production in Cd-contaminated farmlands.

Results

Colonization of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria

No AMF colonization or PSB colonies were detected in the CK treatment. In contrast, effective AMF colonization was observed in both the single inoculation with AMF and co-inoculation treatments (Fig. 1). Compared with single inoculation with AMF, co-inoculation (AMF + PSB) significantly increased the AMF colonization rate, spore number in the rhizosphere soil, and easily extractable glomalin-related soil protein (EE-GRSP) content by 11%, 31%, and 5%, respectively (P < 0.05). Total glomalin-related soil protein (T-GRSP) content was significantly increased by 10% (P < 0.01). Compared with single inoculation with PSB, co-inoculation significantly increased the PSB colonization density in the rhizosphere soil by 44% (Fig. S1, P < 0.01). Overall, AMF and PSB exhibited positive synergistic interactions in the rhizosphere, mutually enhancing their colonization performance and promoting soil GRSP accumulation.

Root morphology, plant height, biomass of S. plumbizincicola

All microbial inoculation treatments significantly promoted the growth of S. plumbizincicola, with the co-inoculation (AMF + PSB) showing the most pronounced effect (Table 1). Specifically, the co-inoculation treatment exerted the most significant improvement on root morphology, with root length, root surface area, root volume, and root branch number increased by 69%, 47%, 205%, and 55%, respectively. Compared with CK, single inoculations of AMF, PSB, and co-inoculation treatments increased plant height by 9%, 12%, and 24%, respectively, thereby increasing shoot dry weight by 42%, 44%, and 79%, respectively, and root dry weight by 28%, 31%, and 46%, respectively. AMF and PSB had significant (P < 0.01) effects on root morphology, plant height, and biomass. Moreover, the positive interaction between the two microorganisms was significant for root surface area (P < 0.05) and for root length, root volume, and root branch number (P < 0.01).

Leaf mineral nutrients

Microbial inoculation significantly affected the mineral nutrient composition of S. plumbizincicola leaves (Fig. 2). Compared with the CK treatment, single inoculation with PSB and co-inoculation significantly (P < 0.05) increased N content by 6% and 18%, respectively. Co-inoculation (AMF + PSB) also produced the most pronounced increase in P and K content by 31% and 47%, respectively, relative to the non-inoculated control. AMF and PSB exhibited significant (P < 0.01) individual effects on leaf N, P, and K contents. Furthermore, the positive interaction between AMF and PSB was significant for N (P < 0.05) and for P and K (P < 0.01).

Chemical properties of rhizosphere soil and acid phosphatase activity

Inoculation with AMF and PSB affected the biochemical properties of the rhizosphere soil of S. plumbizincicola (Fig. 3). Compared with CK, single inoculation with AMF and PSB significantly reduced soil pH by 0.08 and 0.27 units, respectively. Notably, co-inoculation (AMF + PSB) significantly increased soil pH by 0.06 units compared with single inoculation with PSB. Compared with CK, the co-inoculation treatment significantly increased AN, AP, AK, and S-ACP by 18%, 88%, 53%, and 46%, respectively, with the largest increase observed in AP content. AMF had no significant effect on pH, while PSB had a significant (P < 0.01) effect on pH, and there was a significant (P < 0.01) interaction between the two microorganisms on soil pH. AMF exerted a significant (P < 0.01) effect on AN content, and both AMF and PSB exerted significant (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01) effects on AP content, AK content, and S-ACP activity.

Bioavailability and speciation of cadmium

Furthermore, microbial inoculation significantly altered the bioavailability and speciation of Cd in the rhizosphere soil (Fig. 4). Compared with CK, single inoculation with AMF, PSB, and co-inoculation (AMF + PSB) increased the CaCl2-extractable Cd concentration by 31%, 219%, and 250%, respectively. Both AMF and PSB exerted significant (P < 0.01) effects on soil CaCl2-extractable Cd concentration. Additionally, inoculation treatments reduced the proportion of residual Cd by 74%, 72%, and 49%, respectively, and decreased the proportion of oxidizable Cd by 6%, 15%, and 25%, respectively. In contrast, inoculation treatments increased the proportion of reducible Cd by 44%, 43%, and 12%, respectively. Both AMF and PSB exhibited significant individual effects on the proportions of residual, oxidizable, and reducible Cd in soil (P < 0.01), and the positive interaction between AMF and PSB was significant for all three Cd speciation (Table S3, P < 0.01). To further clarify the intrinsic relationships among different Cd speciation, Pearson correlation analysis was performed (Fig. 4c). The results showed that CaCl2‑extractable Cd was negatively correlated with all four BCR‑extracted Cd speciation, with the oxidizable speciation exhibiting the strongest negative correlation (r = − 0.80).

Relative expression levels of Cd detoxification-related genes of S. plumbizincicola

Microbial inoculation, especially co-inoculation (AMF + PSB), significantly upregulated the relative expression levels of Cd detoxification-related genes in S. plumbizincicola roots (Fig. 5). Compared with CK, single inoculation with AMF, PSB, and co-inoculation (AMF + PSB) increased the relative expression of SpMT2 by 2%, 6%, and 9%, respectively; SepPCS by 4%, 2%, and 6%, respectively; and SpHIPP45 by 8%, 8%, and 9%, respectively. AMF and PSB exerted significant (P < 0.01) individual effects on the expression of SepPCS and SpHIPP45. However, the positive interaction between AMF and PSB was only significant for the expression of SpHIPP45 (P < 0.05).

Cadmium concentration, accumulation, translocation, and bioconcentration factor

Microbial inoculation increased the Cd concentration, accumulation, and translocation in S. plumbizincicola (Fig. 6). Compared with CK, single inoculation with AMF, PSB, and co-inoculation increased the Cd concentration in the shoot by 38%, 44%, and 63%, and in the roots by 31%, 29%, and 44%, respectively. Meanwhile, Cd accumulation in the shoot increased by 57%, 80%, and 182%, and in the root by 70%, 73%, and 113%, respectively. AMF and PSB had significant (P < 0.01) effects on plant Cd concentration and accumulation, and their positive interaction on shoot and root Cd accumulation was significant (P < 0.01).

Factors affecting Cd accumulation in S. plumbizincicola

To minimize confounding interactions among influencing factors, the PLS-PM was employed to further elucidate the interactions among AMF, PSB, soil chemical properties and enzyme activity, root morphology, leaf nutrient accumulation, plant growth, soil CaCl2-extractable Cd concentration, Cd detoxification-related genes, and plant Cd accumulation (Fig. 7). This model achieved optimal data fitting with a goodness-of-fit index of 0.911.

Blue and red arrows indicate positive and negative directional pathway relationships, respectively. Results indicated that AMF showed a direct positive association with plant Cd accumulation (Path coefficient = 0.432), while PSB showed a weaker direct positive association with plant Cd accumulation (Path coefficient = 0.379, P < 0.01). In addition, both AMF (Path coefficient = 0.168, P < 0.001) and PSB (Path coefficient = 0.920, P < 0.001) showed significant positive associations with soil chemical properties and enzyme activity. Soil chemical properties and enzyme activity showed a significant positive association with root morphology (Path coefficient = 0.259, P < 0.001), which in turn was associated with plant nutrient accumulation (Path coefficient = 0.253), and was thus indirectly linked to plant growth (Path coefficient = 0.045). Concurrently, soil chemical properties and enzyme activity showed a significant positive correlation with soil CaCl2-extractable Cd concentration (Path coefficient = 1.232, P < 0.001). Meanwhile, plant growth (Path coefficient = 0.811, P < 0.01) and soil CaCl2-extractable Cd concentration (Path coefficient = 0.081) were positively associated with Cd detoxification-related genes, ultimately promoting plant Cd accumulation (Path coefficient = 0.107). In summary, AMF and PSB indirectly increased Cd accumulation in S. plumbizincicola by upregulating the expression of Cd detoxification-related genes through two pathways: promoting plant growth and enhancing soil Cd bioavailability. Between these two pathways, plant growth was the primary determinant of Cd accumulation.

Discussion

Single inoculation and co-inoculation with AMF and PSB treatments promoted the growth of S. plumbizincicola, with the co-inoculation (AMF + PSB) being the most effective, which can be attributed to the synergistic interaction between AMF and PSB. Similarly, previous studies have confirmed that AMF and PSB can efficiently colonize plant roots when co-inoculated under both pot and field conditions, thereby promoting the growth of Panax ginseng via improved root morphology and enhanced nutrient uptake [29]. This study further confirmed the universal applicability of the synergistic growth-promoting effect between AMF and PSB. The study found that co-inoculation significantly increased AMF spore numbers, AMF colonization rates, and soil EE-GRSP content and T-GRSP content, and promoted PSB colonization in the rhizosphere soil (Figs. 1 and S2). The efficient colonization of both microorganisms in the rhizosphere of S. plumbizincicola can be explained by two complementary mechanisms: First, AMF can form hypha-microbe complexes by attaching PSB to the hyphal surfaces, while secreting hyphal exudates to create a carbon-rich rhizosphere microenvironment, which provides energy and survival support for PSB proliferation [30, 31]. Moreover, AMF can adsorb and immobilize soil Cd through surface adsorption and secretion of GRSP, thereby alleviating Cd toxicity to PSB [32]. In return, PSB can secrete extracellular enzymes such as acid phosphatase to convert insoluble soil phosphorus (e.g., phytate) into available forms. This process not only meets their own growth requirements but also supplies sufficient phosphorus to AMF and plants, thereby promoting AMF colonization and hyphal expansion [30, 33, 34].

Soil phosphorus cycling is a core ecological process in terrestrial ecosystems, in which microorganisms play an important role. Soil microorganisms and plant roots secrete phosphatases (e.g., S-ACP) to drive soil phosphorus mineralization. As functional phosphate-solubilizing microbes, PSB enhance phosphorus bioavailability and mediate soil phosphorus transformation via the secretion of organic acids and phosphatases [35]. AMF expand the absorption range of host roots through extensive hyphal networks and facilitate efficient phosphorus acquisition by expressing high-affinity phosphate transporters [36]. Meanwhile, their hyphal exudates synergistically interact with PSB [37], further stimulating phosphatase secretion. Studies have shown that S-ACP activity and phytate mineralization efficiency under co-inoculation with AMF and PSB are significantly higher than those under single inoculation, thereby effectively increasing soil AP content [33], which is consistent with the results of this study (Fig. 3c and e).

AMF extend their hyphal networks into the soil, significantly increasing the effective surface area for roots to absorb water and nutrients while enhancing the uptake of mineral nutrients such as N, P, and K. Some AMF species even supply 70% to 100% of the phosphorus required by host plants [15, 38]. The symbiotic system formed between AMF and S. plumbizincicola, combined with the efficient colonization and synergistic effects of PSB in the rhizosphere, not only improved root structure, root length, and branching, but also expanded root surface area for more efficient nutrient uptake (Table 1). Our previous research found that under Cd stress, AMF symbiosis with plant roots enhanced mineral nutrient uptake by improving root morphology [25]. In this study, inoculation with AMF and PSB promoted plant growth by enhancing leaf mineral nutrient (N, P, and K) accumulation (Fig. 2; Table 1). Furthermore, the PLS-PM model revealed that AMF and PSB increased leaf nutrient accumulation by directly enhancing soil nutrient availability and improving root morphology, thereby indirectly promoting the growth of S. plumbizincicola (Fig. 7).

Inoculation with AMF and PSB not only promoted plant growth but also facilitated Cd activation in rhizosphere soil. This study demonstrated that inoculation with AMF and PSB significantly increased CaCl2-extractable Cd concentration and the proportion of reducible Cd, while reducing the proportions of residual and oxidizable Cd (Fig. 4). Consistent with the variation in Cd speciation, correlation analysis further confirmed that CaCl2-extractable Cd was negatively correlated with the four BCR-extracted Cd fractions. Notably, the oxidizable Cd fraction showed the strongest negative correlation with bioavailable Cd (r = − 0.80), suggesting that the depletion of oxidizable Cd was closely coupled with the increase in Cd availability (Fig. 4c). Soil pH is a major factor influencing Cd bioavailability. Lowering soil pH enhances Cd bioavailability, and root exudates also promote Cd solubilization [24, 39]. Compared to the CK, single inoculation with AMF and PSB significantly lowered soil pH by 0.08 and 0.27 units, respectively. Two-way ANOVA results indicated that PSB is the dominant factor driving pH reduction, with a significant (P < 0.01) positive interaction between PSB and AMF (Fig. 3a). This may be attributed to AMF releasing organic acids and H⁺ through hyphae, while PSB produces more acidic compounds [40, 41]. Previous studies further elucidated this mechanism: inoculation with PSB substantially increased amino acid and organic acid concentrations, mobilizing Cd via complexation and acid solubilization, thereby altering soil heavy metal speciation [24]. Previous studies on the Cd hyperaccumulator Solanum nigrum also documented the PSB-mediated Cd mobilization mechanism [42]. This study further revealed the synergistic effect of AMF and PSB, which may mobilize soil Cd by promoting organic acid secretion and reducing soil pH. AMF can secrete GRSP to adsorb and immobilize Cd and reduce Cd leaching [43]. This process ensures that mobilized Cd is efficiently taken up by S. plumbizincicola, rather than being discharged into the surrounding environment. PLS-PM analysis indicated that inoculation with AMF and PSB was directly associated with changes in soil biochemical properties and indirectly associated with the transformation of soil Cd from low-mobility, low-bioavailability forms to high-activity, plant-available forms (Fig. 7).

Compared with CK, co-inoculation (AMF + PSB) increased Cd accumulation in the shoots and roots of S. plumbizincicola by 182% and 113%, respectively, and these increments were significantly higher than those in the single inoculation treatments (Fig. 6). This indicated a synergistic effect between AMF and PSB in promoting Cd accumulation in S. plumbizincicola. Plant Cd accumulation depends primarily on two factors: plant physiological characteristics and soil Cd bioavailability [44]. The PLS-PM model indicated that the indirect association of AMF and PSB with enhanced Cd accumulation in S. plumbizincicola involved upregulated expression of Cd detoxification-related genes and two pathways: one related to plant growth promotion and the other to enhanced soil Cd bioavailability (Fig. 7).

Hyperaccumulators rely not only on membrane transporters but also on efficient intracellular detoxification and sequestration systems to cope with heavy metals. Cadmium is classified as a non-essential metal in plants, and its movement into and out of plant cells is regulated by metal transporters [45]. However, once Cd²⁺ enters the cell, whether it is sequestered in vacuoles or remains mobile for long-distance transport depends largely on the interplay of intracellular chelating and sequestration systems. Genes including SpHIPP45, SpMT2, and SepPCS encode such detoxification proteins and are regulated by Cd stress [46–48]. Zhang et al. cloned a heavy metal-associated isoprenylated plant protein (HIPP) gene SpHIPP45 from S. plumbizincicola, demonstrating that it mediates Cd detoxification by regulating endoplasmic reticulum stress responses [47]. The endoplasmic reticulum is a key organelle for the folding and processing of membrane and secretory proteins, and ER stress induced by Cd exposure can disrupt cellular protein processing [49, 50]. By maintaining ER homeostasis, SpHIPP45 may help preserve root cell metabolic activities and protein processing capacity under Cd stress, which is a prerequisite for sustaining the cellular functions that support continuous Cd uptake. SpMT2 is localized in the cytoplasm and enhances Cd detoxification by chelating Cd, which reduces Cd sequestration into vacuoles and facilitates long-distance Cd transport in plants [48]. This cytoplasmic chelation converts free Cd²⁺ into highly mobile chelated forms, thereby facilitating Cd loading into the xylem and contributing to an increased the overall translocation factor [49]. SepPCS detoxifies Cd by catalyzing the biosynthesis of phytochelatins (PCs); the resulting PC–Cd complexes are then transported into vacuoles for sequestration via tonoplast transporters, and this process is further enhanced in shoots under high Cd stress [49, 51]. Vacuolar sequestration not only detoxifies Cd by isolating it from the cytoplasm but also continuously reduces cytosolic free Cd²⁺ levels, which could alleviate the potential feedback inhibition of root Cd uptake. This process expands the Cd storage capacity of shoots and helps maintain a concentration gradient favorable for sustained Cd influx into roots [51, 52]. This explains why the increase in shoot Cd accumulation (182%) was substantially greater than that in roots (113%). Collectively, SpHIPP45, SpMT2 and the SepPCS pathway operate at three distinct functional sites, namely the ER stress regulatory network, the cytoplasm, and the vacuole, forming a spatial relay network of cell protection, metal mobility maintenance, and storage expansion. Inoculation with AMF and PSB significantly upregulated the relative expression levels of SpHIPP45, SpMT2, and SepPCS in S. plumbizincicola, with the largest increase observed in the co-inoculation treatment (Fig. 5). This indicated that co-inoculation synergistically strengthens this detoxification and sequestration network. By simultaneously maintaining the driving force for uptake, improving Cd mobility, and expanding storage capacity, co-inoculation promoted Cd uptake and root-to-shoot transport, ultimately increasing Cd accumulation in both roots and shoots (Fig. 6). This finding is consistent with previous research showing that inoculation with PSB (Enterobacter sp.) significantly increased Cd concentration in the hyperaccumulator plant Solanum nigrum L [27].

Additionally, the translocation factor (TF) and bioconcentration factor (BCF) are widely used to evaluate the heavy metal phytoextraction capacity of plants [53]. In this study, inoculation with AMF and PSB significantly increased TF, BCF shoot, and BCF root, confirming their promoting effects on Cd translocation, accumulation, and phytoextraction (Table 2). Consistent with our findings, Cheng et al. reported that inoculation with PSB (Leclercia adecarboxylata L1-5) increased the BCF above, BCF under, and TF of Potamogeton crispus L. by 10.1–140%, 10.1–38.4%, and 20.5–89.2%, respectively, relative to the CK [54]. With regard to the potential contribution of AMF to this enhancement, previous studies demonstrated that under high Cd stress, AMF promoted the production of organic acids and chelators. By altering soil Cd speciation, these metabolites reduced Cd toxicity, drove active Cd transport, enhanced Cd accumulation, and facilitated Cd translocation to aboveground plant parts [55].

This study employed the PLS-PM model to indicate that co-inoculation with AMF and PSB was associated with two core pathways related to increased Cd accumulation in S. plumbizincicola: (1) by increasing soil nutrient availability, optimizing root morphology, and improving leaf mineral nutrient accumulation, thereby promoting S. plumbizincicola growth; (2) by lowering soil pH to reshape the rhizosphere microenvironment, which mobilized soil Cd and increased Cd bioavailability. Notably, both pathways were associated with efficient Cd accumulation by upregulating the expression of Cd detoxification-related genes (Fig. 7).

Limitations of this study

This study was conducted under controlled pot conditions. Although the synergistic potential of AMF and PSB in enhancing Cd phytoextraction by Sedum plumbizincicola was demonstrated, several limitations should be noted. First, the experiment was performed under single Cd contamination, without considering the co-occurrence of multiple heavy metals that commonly exist in natural farmland environments. Second, pot systems differ considerably from field conditions in terms of temperature, soil moisture, soil properties, and indigenous microbial communities. Therefore, the long-term stability and adaptability of the co-inoculation strategy under field conditions warrant further verification. Third, this study focused primarily on plant growth promotion, Cd mobilization, and the regulation of Cd detoxification-related gene expression, whereas the mechanisms driving shifts in rhizosphere microbial community structure and function remain insufficiently elucidated. These limitations remain to be explored in future work.

Conclusions

In summary, co-inoculation with AMF and PSB synergistically improved the growth and Cd phytoextraction capacity of Sedum plumbizincicola via two complementary pathways. It increased nutrient availability and optimized root morphology, while elevating leaf mineral accumulation to promote plant growth. Meanwhile, it reduced rhizosphere soil pH, thereby mobilizing insoluble Cd fractions and enhancing Cd bioavailability. These processes upregulated Cd detoxification-related genes and strengthened intracellular chelation, vacuolar sequestration, and long-distance Cd transport, leading to substantially greater Cd accumulation and phytoextraction efficiency. Relative to single inoculation, the combined AMF–PSB treatment represents a more effective method for remediating Cd-contaminated farmland. This study advances mechanistic understanding of synergistic microbial interactions during heavy metal remediation. Future work will focus on field validation across diverse pollution levels and agronomic conditions, alongside the optimization of microbial application techniques to support large-scale deployment of this remediation strategy.

Materials and methods

Preparation of experimental materials

The tested soil was mountain red soil, collected from a polluted farmland (103.3753°E, 26.3425°N) around Zhehai Town, Huize County, Yunnan Province. The soil was naturally air-dried and then passed through a 2 mm sieve. It was sterilized at 121 ℃ for 2 h to eliminate interference caused by native arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB), and other microorganisms. The basic physicochemical properties of the soil are shown in Supplementary File Table S1. The total Cd concentration was 8.99 mg kg− 1, which far exceeded the risk control value (2.0 mg kg− 1 Cd) for contamination of agricultural land in China with a pH range of 5.5–6.5 (GB15618-2018) [56].

The tested AMF strain was Funneliformis mosseae (BGCYN051511C0001BGCAM 0013), provided by the Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Germplasm Resource Bank of the Institute of Plant Nutrition and Resources, Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences. AMF inoculum was propagated using potted maize as the host plant, consisting of a mixture of spores, hyphae, culture medium, and infected root segments (containing approximately 21 spores per gram of inoculum). The PSB strain was Serratia marcescens, which was isolated by our research group from heavy metal-contaminated soils in the Karst region of Yunnan and exhibited excellent Cd tolerance. The phylogenetic tree and Cd tolerance of this PSB strain are shown in Supplementary File Fig. S1. The bacterial suspension was prepared as follows: the activated strain was inoculated into beef extract-peptone liquid medium and incubated at 20 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 24 h until the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) reached 0.8. The bacterial suspension was centrifuged (8,000 r min− 1, 10 min, 4 ℃). The resulting cell pellet was gently washed twice with sterile physiological saline and resuspended in an equal volume of the same saline. The final bacterial concentration was adjusted to 3.0 × 10⁸ CFU mL⁻¹ as determined by the plate count method.

The Sedum plumbizincicola used for this experiment was obtained from the cultivation base of the Institute of Soil Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences (ISSCAS), located at the Lanping Lead-Zinc mine (26°22′N, 99°23′E), Yunnan Province, Southwest China. Prior to the pot experiment, the seedlings were precultured in deionized water for 3 days, followed by cultivation in Hoagland solution for 2 weeks to promote root development. The seedlings were cultured in a growth chamber with a temperature of 25 ℃, relative humidity of 70%-80%, and a light/dark 16 h/8 h regime at an illumination intensity of 1500 lx. Healthy seedlings of uniform size were then selected for the experiment.

Pot experiment and sample collection

The experiment was conducted in June 2024 at the experimental greenhouse located at Yunnan Agricultural University (25°07′43″N, 102°44′56″E). Four treatments were conducted: (1) the control group without F. mosseae and S. marcescens (CK); (2) single inoculation with F. mosseae (AMF); (3) single inoculation with S. marcescens (PSB); (4) co-inoculation with F. mosseae and S. marcescens (AMF + PSB). There were five replicates per treatment, resulting in a total of 20 pots. To eliminate potential effects of AMF and PSB inoculation on soil pH and to provide the same background microflora across treatments, the plants in each pot were inoculated with 50 g of AMF inoculum (the corresponding controls received 50 g of inactivated AMF inoculum) and/or 10 mL of PSB bacterial suspension (the corresponding controls received 10 mL of sterilized PSB bacterial suspension, sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min). Additionally, 50 mL of AMF microbial inoculum filtrate was added to the CK and PSB treatments, and 50 mL of sterile water was added to the AMF and AMF + PSB treatments to balance the total liquid volume across treatments. Inoculation details for the four treatments are presented in Supplementary File Table S2. Before the plants were transplanted, 1 kg of sterilized soil was thoroughly mixed with the corresponding inoculum in each sterile plastic pot. The experiment was conducted in a greenhouse with a temperature of 25 ℃, relative humidity of 30%-40%, and a light/dark 16 h/8 h regime at an illumination intensity of 2000 lx.

Three S. plumbizincicola seedlings were transplanted into each pot. To eliminate environmental differences, the positions of the pots were randomly adjusted daily. Water was replenished regularly every 2 days to keep the soil moisture content at 60–70% of the maximum water holding capacity.

The plants were harvested after 90 days of cultivation. The soil around the roots was carefully removed, and the rhizosphere soil adhering to the roots was collected by gently shaking the plants. Fresh rhizosphere soil samples were stored at 4 ℃for determination of soil properties. S. plumbizincicola was collected and thoroughly rinsed multiple times with tap water, followed by deionized water to remove soil particles. After measuring the plant height, the plants were separated into roots, stems, and leaves. Fresh root samples were divided into three portions. The first portion was stored at − 80 ℃ for RNA extraction; the second portion was preserved in 70% ethanol at 4 ℃ for AMF colonization assessment; the third portion was kept at 4 ℃ for subsequent analysis. Fresh leaf samples were also stored at 4 ℃ for further use.

Note: The preparation of the AMF microbial inoculum filtrate (aimed at removing AMF in the inoculum, while retaining associated symbiotic bacteria): 50 g of AMF inoculum was weighed and added to 50 mL of sterilized water. The suspension was shaken thoroughly, left undisturbed for a short period, and then filtered through a 0.45 μm filter membrane. The filtrate was collected for subsequent use.

Microbial colonization and activity assessment

The rhizosphere soil adhering to the roots was air-dried for 7 days and then passed through a 2 mm sieve for later use. Simultaneously, selected fresh tender roots of 5–10 cm were sampled and washed with deionized water until free of soil residues. Then the clean roots were cut into 2-cm-long segments, and placed into a 10% (w/v) potassium hydroxide solution for dissociation via a water bath at 90 ℃ for 1 h. At least 50 root segments were placed onto glass slides, stained with acid fuchsin, and destained with lactoglycerin solution. AMF colonization in the roots was observed under an optical microscope (BX51, Olympus, Japan) at 200× magnification, and the colonization rate was calculated using the gridline-intersect method [57]. A 20.0 g sample was weighed, and AMF spores were isolated using the wet sieving and decantation-sucrose centrifugation method. The spores were observed under a stereo microscope, and their number was calculated [58]. Total glomalin-related soil protein (T-GRSP) and easily extractable glomalin-related soil protein (EE-GRSP) were measured following the method described by Wright and Upadhyaya [59]. The survival rate of PSB in rhizosphere soil was quantified using the plate count method [60].

Analysis of plant height, biomass, root morphology, and leaf mineral nutrients

S. plumbizincicola height was measured with a tape measure from the root–stem junction to the petiole base, and the plants were separated into shoots and roots. Root samples were scanned using an Epson Perfection V700 Photo scanner (Epson, Japan) and analyzed with WinRHIZO software (Regent Instruments, Canada) to determine total root length, root surface area, root volume, and root branching number. The scanned plant samples were placed in an oven, heated at 105 ℃ for 30 min, and then dried at 80 ℃ to constant weight. Dry weight of the aboveground and root tissues was measured with an electronic analytical balance. After drying, the samples were ground into powder, sieved through a 0.25 mm mesh, and reserved.

The leaf mineral nutrient content, including total nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K), was examined according to Ma et al. [61]. Nitrogen content of S. plumbizincicola leaves was determined using the Kjeldahl method. Phosphorus content was quantified by vanadium-molybdenum yellow colorimetry, and absorbance was measured at 420 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan). Potassium content in leaf tissues was measured using a flame photometer (FP64, Inesa, China).

Determination of rhizosphere soil chemical properties and acid phosphatase activity

Soil pH was measured using a pH meter (PHS-430, China) in soil suspensions prepared at a water-to-soil ratio of 2.5:1 (v/m). Alkaline nitrogen (AN) was determined by the alkaline diffusion method. Available phosphorus (AP) was extracted using the Olsen method, and its content was measured by the Mo-Sb colorimetric method. Available potassium (AK) was extracted using the ammonium acetate method, and its content was measured by flame atomic absorption spectrometry. Soil acid phosphatase (S-ACP) was quantified using a commercial assay kit (Suzhou Grace Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) and an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan) [61]. The detailed methodology is provided in Supplementary File Text S1.

Determination of cadmium in rhizosphere soil and plant tissues

Cd concentrations in S. plumbizincicola and soil were determined following the method described by Li et al. [62]. For each sample, 0.2 g of dried plant powder was accurately weighed, followed by the addition of 10 mL of nitric acid (65% HNO3, v/v) and 2 mL of perchloric acid (70% HClO4, v/v). The mixture was then digested using a microwave digester (MARS6; CEM, NC, USA). After digestion, Cd concentration in the sample solutions was measured by ICP-MS. 0.5 g of dried soil sample was digested with 6 mL HNO3, 2 mL HCl, and 1 mL HF, and then the sample was allowed to dissolve for 1 h at 200 °C. The soil total Cd concentration was determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (NexION 5000, PerkinElmer, MA, USA). Soil available Cd was extracted using CaCl2. Briefly, mixtures of 0.01 M CaCl2 solution and soil at 10:1 (v/w) were shaken at 200 rpm and 25 ℃ for 2 h. Cd concentrations in the extracts were measured using ICP-MS. The translocation factor (TF) was calculated as the ratio of the Cd concentration in the shoot to that in the root. Similarly, the bioconcentration factor (BCF) was determined by calculating the ratio of the Cd concentration in the shoot or root to that in the Cd concentration present in the soil. Cd speciation was determined via the BCR sequential extraction method using 1.0 g of air-dried soil [63], with detailed procedures presented in Supplementary File Text S2.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from root samples of S. plumbizincicola using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Plant RNA Extraction Kit. The quality and concentration of RNA were determined using a microvolume spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Lite, Thermo Scientific, USA). The cDNA synthesized by reverse transcription was quantified by RT-qPCR on a QuantStudio™5 384-well real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA), following the program: pre-denaturation at 95 ℃ for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (95 ℃ for 5 s, 55 ℃ for 30 s, and 72 ℃ for 40 s). A final melting curve analysis was performed to confirm the specificity of each amplified fragment. The primer sequences used were as follows: SpMT2-F, 5′-ATGTCTTGCTGTGGCGGAA-3′ and SpMT2-R, 5′-TCATTTGCAATTGCAGGGGT-3′; SpHIPP45-F, 5′-GCCCTCTCGCAATTAGATGGA-3′ and SpHIPP45-R, 5′-TACCGATCTCCTTACCGCCT-3′; SepPCS-F, 5′-CCTGGTGGATGATGTTCCCATTCTCCTAAA-3′ and SepPCS-R, 5′-CCACAACAACTTGGTTTCGCTTGAGAAGA-3′. The SpWRKY69_3 gene was used as the internal reference. All samples had three biological replicates, each consisting of three technical replicates. The expression levels of the genes were calculated according to the 2^−ΔΔCt method [64].

Modeling and statistical analysis

Partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM)

The “plspm” package in R was employed for PLS-PM analysis to assess the impact of AMF and PSB on S. plumbizincicola. The model incorporated AMF colonization rate and PSB colonization density as exogenous variables; soil chemical properties and enzyme activity included pH, AN, AP, AK, and S-ACP; root morphology parameters included root length, root surface area, root volume, and root branches number; leaf nutrient accumulation variables comprised N, P, and K; plant growth parameters included shoot and root dry weight; soil CaCl2-extractable Cd concentration; Cd detoxification-related genes metrics involved relative expression levels of SpMT2, SepPCS, and SpHIPP45; and plant Cd accumulation was assessed based on total Cd accumulation. Subsequently, permutation tests (999 iterations) were performed to identify the key determinants affecting plant Cd accumulation, and the goodness-of-fit (GOF) index was used to evaluate the model’s overall performance.

Quality control

To avoid heavy metal (HM) contamination throughout the analysis, all experimental operations were performed by personnel wearing standard laboratory clothing and using contaminant-free supplies. All glassware for HM determination was pre-soaked in 3% HNO₃ and then thoroughly rinsed with deionized water. Certified soil reference materials (GBW07407) from the National Center for Standard Materials of China were used for quality control.

Cadmium standard solutions were prepared with CdCl₂ to establish calibration curves at four concentration levels (50, 100, 1000, 10000 µg kg⁻¹), with three replicates for each level. All experimental samples were analyzed in triplicate to ensure the reproducibility of HM measurement data. Blank controls were included in each analytical batch to monitor potential background contamination.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26; IBM, USA). Before statistical analysis, normality of distribution was tested by the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed by Levene’s test. The significance level was uniformly set at P < 0.05 for all tests. When assumptions of normality or homoscedasticity were violated, appropriate data transformations or non-parametric tests were used. AMF colonization data (percentages) were arcsine square‑root transformed prior to analysis, as a standard procedure for proportional data. An independent samples t-test was used to analyze the AMF colonization rate and spore number, soil T-GRSP, EE-GRSP, and PSB colonization. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) combined with Waller–Duncan multiple comparison test was applied for post-hoc analysis to assess significant differences between treatments (P < 0.05). Two-way analysis of variance (Two-way ANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of AMF, PSB, and their interaction on all parameters. Pearson correlation analysis among the five Cd fractions (CaCl2-extractable and four BCR fractions) was performed using R (version 4.2.1), and the results were visualized as a correlation matrix heatmap. All experimental data were visualized using Origin 2021 (OriginLab, USA).
