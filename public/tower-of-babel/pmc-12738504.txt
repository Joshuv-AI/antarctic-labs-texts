Translation, validation and reliability of depression, anxiety, and stress 42 (DASS-42) among Herat University students
Authors: Nasar Ahmad Shayan, Ali Rahimi, Abdul Momen Waseq, Osman Dag, Hilal Özcebe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the validity and reliability of the Dari-translated Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale-42 (DASS-42) in university students in Herat, Afghanistan.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from August to November 2022 with 1000 undergraduate students at Herat University. Participants completed a self-reported Dari-translated DASS-42 questionnaire. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was employed to assess the scale's factor structure, and internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha. The test–retest reliability was also examined over a 2–3 week interval.ResultsThe CFA results indicated a good fit for the three-factor model, with RMSEA = 0.04 and CFI = 0.919, confirming the construct validity of the scale. Internal consistency was high, with Cronbach's alpha values of 0.867, 0.831, and 0.848 for depression, anxiety, and stress, respectively. The test–retest reliability coefficients for depression, anxiety, and stress were 0.956, 0.923, and 0.951, respectively. Additionally, DASS-42 showed significant correlations with other established mental health measures, indicating good convergent validity.ConclusionThe DASS-42 is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing depression, anxiety, and stress among university students in Herat, Brazil. This comprehensive validation addresses previous limitations and supports the use of the scale in both clinical and non-clinical settings for early detection and intervention. Future research should explore the scale's applicability in more diverse Afghan populations and incorporate longitudinal designs to validate its predictive validity further.Keywords: DASS-42, Validity, Reliability, Confirmatory factor analysis, University students, Afghanistan

Introduction

Due to the rising prevalence of mental health disorders, the World Health Organization (WHO) has designated one day each year (October 10th) to promote mental health and prevent suicide [1]. Mental disorders affected 970 million in 2019, and anxiety disorders affected 301 million individuals, making it the most common mental health disorder with a prevalence of 3.8%. Depression came in second, with a global prevalence of 3.4%, affecting 279 million people globally [2]. In 2019, approximately one-third of the world's population was stressed and worried [3] and 10% of the world's children and adolescents suffer from mental problems and the majority of them do not seek help or receive care [4].

University students must conform to a wide range of psychosocial changes while also coping with academic and social demands in order to prepare for their professional careers [5, 6]. They commonly experience mental health issues [7]. The high academic achievement expectation has created a very stressful environment, which if remain unattended can be harmful to their physical and mental health. According to studies, between 15 and 25% of undergraduate students develop some form of mental disorder, the most common of which is depression [5]. Anxiety disorders are the most common psychiatric illnesses among university students, accounting for around 11.9% of all college students [7]. Depression is another major mental health concern among university students, with prevalence rates ranging from 7–9% [8]. According to another study in a group of Turkish university students there was a significant prevalence of depression, anxiety, and stress 27.1%, 47.1%, and 27.0%, respectively [9].

Afghanistan is one of the world's five poorest countries, according to the United Nations Human Development Index [10]. This country has been crippled by roughly forty years of savage conflict, catastrophic loss of life, destruction to public and private property, and massive population displacement. It has also experienced catastrophic droughts, earthquakes, endemic shortages of public health services, corrupt administration, and the establishment of a substantial illegal economy centered on poppy cultivation, drug processing and trafficking, and related criminal activities. All of these challenges have had a negative influence on people's livelihoods, education, health, and social support networks across the country [10]. A large-scale mental health assessment of 799 adults in Afghanistan conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in 2002 revealed a high prevalence of a wide range of mental health issues; depression was 73% and 59% and anxiety was 84% and 59% for men and women, respectively [11]. In another study, symptoms of depression were 67.7% and 71.7%, and symptoms of anxiety were 72.2% and 84.6% for men and women, respectively, with women reporting lower social functioning, higher depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms than men [12]. Afghanistan's eastern provinces’ depression and anxiety symptoms were reported at a high incidence of 38.5% and 51.8%, respectively. These symptoms are more prevalent in women than in men. There is a need for a valid and reliable screening test to measure mental health problems in different parts of Afghanistan.

The DASS depression scale evaluates mood, motivation, and self-esteem, whereas the DASS anxiety scale evaluates physiological responses, fear, and panic. The DASS stress scale measured the level of tension and irritation. Researchers and physicians use the DASS as a screening tool because it can distinguish between the three negative emotional states [13]. The original DASS is a 42-item English-language questionnaire with 14 items for each subscale. DASS-21 and DASS-12 are condensed versions of the 42-item questionnaire, which comprises fewer items for depression, anxiety, and stress. These questionnaires can assess depression, anxiety, and stress in both the general and clinical settings. The DASS psychometric properties have been evaluated in a variety of nonclinical and clinical samples [14–19], in the English Language [14–19] as well as non-English versions across various cultural groups [20–24]. These studies have often shown strong internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha values greater than 0.70). Good convergent validity has also been observed in these studies.

Despite its widespread use, significant gaps remain in the validation of the DASS-42, particularly within non-Western, post-conflict contexts. A critical review of the literature reveals two primary shortcomings that this study aims to address. First, there is a methodological gap; the only previous attempt to validate this instrument in a Dari-speaking Afghan population resulted in a shortened 27-item scale because not all items met the required psychometric thresholds [25]. This leaves the full, original 42-item instrument unvalidated and its comprehensive clinical utility unconfirmed in this population. Second, there is a conceptual gap, as most validation studies are conducted in stable, developed nations. A scarcity of research exists to confirm that the constructs of depression, anxiety, and stress are interpreted equivalently in a youth population that has experienced decades of societal instability and trauma.

Therefore, the present study provides a novel contribution by conducting the first comprehensive translation and validation of the full 42-item DASS-42 for Dari-speaking university students in Afghanistan. We aim to establish its psychometric properties by using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to assess its factor structure, evaluating its internal consistency, and examining its measurement invariance across different groups. By addressing the limitations of prior work, this research seeks to provide a robust and reliable tool for mental health screening in this unique and vital population.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This cross-sectional, methodological study was conducted at Herat University in Afghanistan. Data were collected from undergraduate students between August and November 2022. The study focused on students from the four largest faculties: Medicine, Science, Engineering, and Economics.

From a total population of approximately 8,463 students within these four faculties, a sample of 1,000 participants was recruited. A systematic sampling method was used, with university attendance sheets serving as the sampling frame. The sampling interval was calculated by dividing the total population of the four faculties by the target sample size, and this index was then used to randomly select students from each class. Written consent was obtained from all participants after the purpose of the study was explained in detail.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Students who agreed to participate and met the eligibility criteria (1) understanding the Persian (Dari) language and (2) no psychiatric illness that might impact the responses were recruited for the study. Those who were not present when the questionnaires were distributed were excluded. The students were instructed to complete the self-administered questionnaires and return them to the research coordinators.

Translation and preparation of DASS-42

The translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the DASS-42 from English to Persian (Dari) followed the established guidelines for adapting self-report instruments [26]. The process involved several key stages to ensure conceptual and linguistic equivalence:

Forward Translation: The original English DASS-42 was independently translated into Persian (Dari) by two bilingual translators who were native Dari speakers.

Forward Translation: The original English DASS-42 was independently translated into Persian (Dari) by two bilingual translators who were native Dari speakers.

Synthesis: The two forward translations were compared and synthesized into a single, reconciled version by the research team.

Synthesis: The two forward translations were compared and synthesized into a single, reconciled version by the research team.

Back Translation: Two other independent, bilingual translators, whose native language was English and who were previously unaware of the original questionnaire, translated the synthesized Dari version back into English.

Back Translation: Two other independent, bilingual translators, whose native language was English and who were previously unaware of the original questionnaire, translated the synthesized Dari version back into English.

Expert Committee Review: The research team, along with a Persian literature expert, reviewed all translations (original, forward, and back-translated versions) to identify and resolve any discrepancies. This stage focused on ensuring the cultural and linguistic suitability of the items, leading to a pre-final version of the questionnaire. No significant differences were found between the back-translated version and the original English DASS-42.

Sample size and data collection

A minimum of 30 people were considered for each question, with an additional 50 included for a more accurate assessment of the validity and reliability of the Dari-translated version of the DASS-42. The sample size was calculated according to Tabachnick and Fidel's recommendation of 10 participants per questionnaire item for factor analysis (minimum sample size of 420) [27], the overall sample size exceeded the minimum sample size required for better analysis of the validity and reliability of the Dari-translated version of the DASS-42. Therefore, 1000 students (527 male and 473 female) were included in the study.

Each participant completed a self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was divided into two parts: socio-demographics and the DASS-42 questionnaire.

Re-test procedure

After the translation and preparation of DASS -42 (as explained above), the test was performed on a sample of 1000 Herat University Students. To calculate reliability, the DASS scales were re-tested by 200 students of these populations within a 2-week interval.

Questionnaire

Data collection tools in this study included two parts: the Socio-Demographics Questionnaire and Depression, Anxiety, Stress, and Anxiety Scale (DASS-42) Questionnaire.

Socio-demographics questionnaire

This questionnaire included information such as age, sex, field of study, degree, marital status, level of interest in the field of study, smoking history, residence status, number of children in the family, presence or absence of organ violation, level of self-image, and amount of exercise per week.

DASS-42 questionnaire

The DASS-42 is a 42-item self-report instrument designed to measure the three distinct but related negative emotional states of depression, anxiety, and stress [17] The questionnaire contains 14 items for each subscale. The Depression subscale assesses mood, loss of motivation, and low self-esteem (e.g., "I felt that I had nothing to look forward to"). The Anxiety subscale measures physiological arousal and fear (e.g., "I was aware of the action of my heart in the absence of physical exertion"). Finally, the Stress subscale assesses tension and irritability (e.g., "I found it hard to wind down"). For each item, respondents rate the extent to which they have experienced the state over the past week on a 4-point Likert scale, from 0 ("did not apply to me at all") to 3 ("applied to me very much, or most of the time").

Statistical analysis

Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 26 was used for statistical analysis. Numbers and percentages were used to represent all category data. The means and standard deviations of normally distributed data, such as the outcome variables and depression, anxiety, and stress subscale scores, have been reported. Unless otherwise stated, all differences were assessed at a significance level of p < 0.05. Cronbach's alpha coefficients were determined for each subscale and for the entire DASS-42 to assess internal consistency.

The AMOS version 21 software package was used to perform a Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). A nested model comparison was performed using Microsoft Excel. Two competing measurement models were assessed using the CFA. The original model was a single-factor (unidimensional) structure, with all elements loaded on a single general factor. The three-factor structure presented by Lovibond and Lovibond was the second model [17]. Because it implies that the variation in the DASS-42 may be partitioned into one general factor plus error variance associated with each particular item 21, the one-factor model is the most parsimonious of all feasible models [16].

The maximum likelihood estimation approach was used in each study, and covariance matrices were evaluated [27]. The chi-square statistic (), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), Adjusted Goodness-of-Fit index (AGFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to assess the adequacy of the fit for each model tested. The chi-squared statistic was computed for both models, with the values not being significant. In general, there are no defined cutoff points for acceptable models; nonetheless, the following criteria were employed to demonstrate the quality of fit for a given model. The GFI is an absolute index of fit, with a value of 1 representing perfect fit, a GFI greater than 0.90, and values close to 0 indicating very poor fit.

The AGFI varies from the GFI solely because it accounts for the number of degrees of freedom in the defined model, thus addressing the parsimony issue. The AGFI values ranged from 0 to 1, with values of approximately 1 indicating a good fit. CFI is an incremental fit index with values ranging from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a greater fit. Values greater than 0.95 and 0.97 are often linked with satisfactory and good match, respectively. The IFI, which is unconcerned about the sample size, considers results greater than 0.90 to be acceptable. RMSEA is a measure of absolute fit, with lower values indicating a greater fit. Values less than 0.05 are regarded good fit, while values between 0.05 and 0.08 are considered sufficient fit, 0.08 to 0.10 indicate medium fit, and values larger than 0.10 are considered bad fit. The AIC considers parsimony when evaluating the model fit, with smaller values indicating a better match.

Multi-group CFA was performed in AMOS v.21 using maximum likelihood estimations to test the gender invariance of the DASS-21. The three-factor model’s fit was evaluated across genders using the same criteria as the approximate fit indices. The likelihood ratio test, often known as the chi-square difference test 46, was used to determine the degree of invariance [28].

Measurement invariance analysis

To determine if the DASS-42 measures the constructs of depression, anxiety, and stress equivalently across different socio-demographic groups, a multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA) was conducted. Following established guidelines, we tested for a hierarchy of measurement invariance across Gender (Male vs. Female), Age Group (17–20 vs. 21–24), and Original Residence (Urban vs. Rural). The sequence of tests included:

Configural Invariance: This baseline model tested whether the three-factor structure of the DASS-42 was the same across groups without constraining any parameters.

Configural Invariance: This baseline model tested whether the three-factor structure of the DASS-42 was the same across groups without constraining any parameters.

Metric Invariance: Building on the configural model, factor loadings were constrained to be equal across groups. This tests whether the items relate to their respective latent factors in the same way for each group.

Metric Invariance: Building on the configural model, factor loadings were constrained to be equal across groups. This tests whether the items relate to their respective latent factors in the same way for each group.

Scalar Invariance: In addition to equal factor loadings, item intercepts were also constrained to be equal. This is a stricter test that assesses whether group differences in the mean scores of the latent factors are not influenced by measurement bias.

Scalar Invariance: In addition to equal factor loadings, item intercepts were also constrained to be equal. This is a stricter test that assesses whether group differences in the mean scores of the latent factors are not influenced by measurement bias.

Model fit was compared at each step. Invariance was considered to be supported if the change in the Comparative Fit Index (ΔCFI) was ≤ 0.01 and the change in the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (ΔRMSEA) was ≤ 0.015 between nested models [29, 30].

Results

The study assessed 1000 participants from Herat in 2020, providing a comprehensive overview of their demographic and health-related characteristics.

Participant characteristics

The majority of participants (54.0%) were between 21–24 years old, with a substantial portion aged 17–20 years (37.9%). The sample consisted of 52.7% male participants. The predominant nationalities were Tajik (47.7%) and Pashton (29.3%). Most participants were single (81.0%) and engaged in physical activity (75.7%). Regarding occupational status, 70.0% of the participants did not have an occupation in addition to their studies. Body Mass Index (BMI) classifications revealed that 13.6% were underweight, 73.2% had normal weight, and 10.9% were overweight, with a small percentage classified as obese (2.3%). In terms of occupational satisfaction, 10.6% were very satisfied, and 15.8% were satisfied with their occupation (Table 1).

Confirmatory factor analysis

Model fit indices

Confirmatory factor analysis was performed to evaluate the construct validity of the scale. The model fit was assessed using several indices, including the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA ≤ 0.08), the Satorra–Bentler scaled chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (S-Bχ2/df ≤ 2.0), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI ≥ 0.90), the Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI ≥ 0.90), the Incremental Fit Index (IFI ≥ 0.90), and the Comparative Fit Index (CFI ≥ 0.90). The indices obtained were as follows: RMSEA = 0.04; CMIN/DF = 2.563; TLI = 0.902; GFI = 0.917; IFI = 0.920; and CFI = 0.919. These results indicate that the factor structure of the scale is appropriate.

Reliability analysis

The internal consistency of the scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for each factor. The coefficients were 0.867 for Factor 1 (depression), 0.831 for Factor 2 (anxiety), and 0.848 for Factor 3 (stress), indicating good reliability across all factors. Additionally, floor and ceiling effects were analyzed and found to be within acceptable limits: 2.4%–0.0% for depression, 1.5%–0.0% for anxiety, and 0.3%–0.1% for stress.

Test–retest reliability

To further examine the reliability of the scale, a test–retest approach was employed. The intraclass correlation coefficients were 0.956, 0.923, and 0.951 for depression, anxiety, and stress, respectively. These high values suggest that the scale demonstrates strong reliability over time.

Discussion

The findings of this study corroborate those of previous research on DASS-42, supporting its reliability and validity as a screening tool for assessing mental health issues among university students. The three-factor model of depression, anxiety, and stress showed a good fit with the data, consistent with the results of earlier studies by Lovibond and Lovibond (1995) and others [15, 16]. This section compares our results with those of similar studies, discusses the implications of our findings, and acknowledges the limitations of this study.

Cronbach's alpha values for the DASS-42 in our study were 0.867 for depression, 0.831 for anxiety, and 0.848 for stress, indicating good internal consistency across all factors. These findings were consistent with those reported in other validation studies. For instance, Lovibond and Lovibond reported Cronbach's alpha values of 0.91, 0.81, and 0.89 for depression, anxiety, and stress, respectively, in the original Australian sample [17]. Similarly, Bayani et al. found Cronbach's alpha values of 0.92 for depression, 0.88 for anxiety, and 0.82 for stress in an Iranian sample, demonstrating the scale's robustness across different cultural contexts [31].

Our confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) results showed good fit indices (RMSEA = 0.04, CFI = 0.919), which are comparable to those found in studies conducted in various cultural settings. For example, Ng et al. reported a RMSEA of 0.04 and a RMR of 0.035 in a clinical sample, while Wang et al. found similar fit indices in a Chinese sample (RMSEA = 0.08, CFI = 0.96), further validating the scale's cross-cultural applicability [18, 24].

In another study, the Turkish version of DASS-42 also demonstrated high internal consistency with Cronbach's alpha values of 0.92 for depression, 0.86 for anxiety, and 0.88 for stress [20]. Similar results were observed in a study on the Spanish version of the DASS-42, which found reliability coefficients of 0.92 for depression, 0.84 for anxiety, and 0.91 for stress [32]. Additionally, Moussa et al. reported that the Arabic version of the scale demonstrated strong internal consistency, similar to the findings in our study [23].

Validation of the Dari-translated DASS-42 among Herat University students provides a reliable tool for screening mental health issues in Afghanistan. Given the high prevalence of mental health disorders reported in this region [11, 12], availability of a validated screening tool is crucial for early detection and intervention. The findings highlight the scale's potential utility in both clinical and non-clinical settings, enabling healthcare providers to identify at-risk individuals and provide timely support.

Although the DASS-42 had been previously validated in the Afghan population, it did not validate all items, resulting in a scale with only 27 validated items [25]. The current study's validation of the complete DASS-42 provides a more comprehensive tool for screening mental health issues among university students in Herat. This enhanced version ensured that all items were appropriately adapted and validated for the Dari-speaking population, thereby improving the accuracy and reliability of the assessments.

A key strength of the present study is its successful validation of the complete 42-item DASS-42, which addresses a significant limitation of prior research in this context. A previous study by Shayan et al. (2021) also attempted to validate the DASS-42 in the Dari-speaking Afghan population. However, a primary limitation of that study was its inability to validate all items, resulting in a shortened, 27-item version of the scale. An unofficially abridged scale may not fully capture the constructs of depression, anxiety, and stress as intended by the instrument's original developers.

Our current study directly addresses this gap. By employing a large university sample and rigorous confirmatory factor analysis, we have demonstrated the validity and reliability of the complete 42-item structure. This comprehensive validation ensures that clinicians and researchers in Afghanistan have access to a robust and complete instrument, enhancing the accuracy of mental health screening and supporting its use in both clinical and non-clinical settings for early detection and intervention.

Moreover, the significant correlations between the DASS-42 subscales and other measures of mental health (e.g., the BDI and MMPI) indicate good convergent validity. This is consistent with findings from studies conducted in Turkey [20] and Spain [32], in where the DASS-42 showed strong correlations with established mental health scales, reinforcing its utility as a comprehensive screening tool.

The use of DASS-42 in various cultural contexts such as Malaysia, Turkey, Iran, and Afghanistan demonstrates its flexibility and broad applicability. In Malaysia, Ramli et al. validated the DASS-42 among medical students and found Cronbach's alpha values of 0.94 for depression, 0.90 for anxiety, and 0.87 for stress [33]. These findings are crucial for adapting mental health interventions to different cultural settings and ensuring that the tools used are appropriate and effective.

The implications of these findings extend to the mental health policies and practices in Afghanistan. Given the high levels of mental health issues reported in this region [12, 34], the validated Dari version of the DASS-42 can be instrumental in identifying and addressing mental health problems among university students. This tool can be used for routine mental health screening, facilitating early intervention and support for those in need.

Limitations and future directions

While this study's primary strength lies in its rigorous validation of the DASS-42 within a large university sample, certain limitations should be considered, which in turn highlight important directions for future research.

First, the data were collected from students at a single university in Herat. This may limit the generalizability of the findings to other student populations or to the general adult population in different regions of Afghanistan. Future research should therefore aim to validate the scale using multi-site sampling from diverse geographical and demographic contexts across the country.

Second, the study relied on self-reported data, which is susceptible to response bias. Additionally, its cross-sectional design prevents any inferences about causality or the stability of DASS-42 scores over time. To address these points, future studies should employ longitudinal designs to establish predictive validity and assess the instrument's stability.

Finally, the validation process did not include clinical interviews based on DSM-5 criteria as an external benchmark. This limits the confirmation of the scale's diagnostic validity. Therefore, a crucial next step is to conduct studies that compare DASS-42 scores against structured clinical interviews to establish its utility and cutoff scores for screening in clinical settings in Afghanistan.

Implications for university mental health policy

The validation of the DASS-42 has direct and practical implications for mental health policy and practice within Afghanistan's higher education sector. Given the high prevalence of mental health challenges among university students, this study provides administrators and campus healthcare providers with a reliable and culturally appropriate tool for systematic screening. This allows institutions to move beyond anecdotal evidence and begin quantifying the scope of depression, anxiety, and stress within their student bodies.

These findings underscore the urgent need for policymakers within educational institutions to allocate funding and resources for student mental health services. The availability of a validated instrument like the DASS-42 can form the foundation of an evidence-based, tiered support system, from universal screening programs to targeted interventions for at-risk students. By adopting this tool, Afghan universities can take a significant step toward improving the mental health outcomes of their students, thereby contributing to their overall well-being and academic success.

Conclusion

The DASS-42 is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing depression, anxiety, and stress in university students in Herat, Afghanistan. Its robust psychometric properties align with those reported in other cultural contexts, making it a valuable tool for mental health screening. Despite certain limitations, such as reliance on self-reported data and lack of a clinical interview component, this study provides a strong foundation for future research and practical applications. By integrating the DASS-42 into routine mental health assessments, health care providers can better identify and address the mental health needs of university students, ultimately enhancing their well-being and academic performance.
