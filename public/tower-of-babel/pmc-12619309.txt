Comparing the effectiveness of two community-based multimodal exercise programs (CMEPs) on physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life in older adults: protocol for a multicenter, double-blind, randomized clinical trial
Authors: Kazi Md Azman Hossain, K M Amran Hossain, Suvro Nill Sarker, Md. Feroz Kabir, Md. Zahid Hossain, Sharmila Jahan, Ehsanur Rahman, Suraiya Yesmin Sharna, Jannatul Ferdous Rikti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAging leads to physiological decline, increasing the risk of frailty, sarcopenia, and falls, which impact older adults’ physical activity, performance, and quality of life. Exercise is recommended for mitigating these effects, yet the optimal approach remains unclear. This trial will compare two community-based multimodal exercise programs—sensorimotor and strengthening exercise—to evaluate their effectiveness in enhancing physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life. The findings will guide evidence-based recommendations for promoting functional independence and healthy aging among community-dwelling older adults.MethodsThis trial will be a multicenter, double-blind, parallel, randomized clinical trial in which 140 older adults will be enrolled from July 2025 to May 2026 in Bangladesh. Eligible participants will be allocated into two groups for community-based multimodal exercise programs (CMEPs) at a 1:1 ratio: CMEP-I: sensorimotor exercise group; CMEP-II: strengthening exercise group. The exercise programs will last for 24 sessions/8-week, with a 24-week follow-up. The major outcome measurements will include physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life using a Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE), Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), Modified Falls Efficacy Scale (MFES), and 36-item Short Form Survey (SF-36). All outcomes will be assessed at pretest, posttest after 8 weeks, and follow-up after 32 weeks.DiscussionThis randomized clinical trial will investigate the comparative effectiveness of sensorimotor and strengthening exercise programs as community-based multimodal interventions for older adults. These findings will provide evidence on nonpharmacological strategies for improving physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life, particularly in resource-limited settings. The trial’s robust design strengthens validity, although generalizability may be constrained. Long-term follow-up will provide insights into sustained benefits, potentially informing rehabilitation guidelines and policy recommendations to promote accessible, cost-effective exercise interventions for aging populations.Trial registrationThis trial is registered prospectively in the Clinical Trial Registry India CTRI/2025/03/083260. Registered on 24/03/2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13063-025-09245-3.

Introduction

Background and rationale

Over time, aging naturally impacts all physiological systems, representing an unavoidable process that affects a substantial and growing portion of the global population [1, 2]. The physiological alterations that occur throughout time due to aging include tissue catabolism and organ degradation, hormonal decline, increased stress, disrupted inflammatory processes, mitochondrial malfunction, and a decreased metabolic rate. As a result, there is a gradual decline in nerve fiber density, bone mass, skeletal muscle mass, strength, and physical performance. These cumulative changes lead to conditions such as frailty and sarcopenia, which affect approximately 10% of people aged 65 and older and between 25 and 50% of those over 85 years of age [3]. Research has shown that fall-related injuries account for 38.19% of hospitalizations and 84.6% of deaths among older adults [4]. More than 50% of incidents, or nearly 50% of all falls, result from obstacles encountered during movement [5]. A sedentary lifestyle and aging are associated with declines in cardiorespiratory fitness and muscular function, complicating the performance and maintenance of independent living. Such situations have been demonstrated to decrease overall physical activity and performance, increase fall risk, and impede the ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs), which may lead to poor quality of life [3, 6–10].

In such circumstances, a physical exercise program is a crucial preventative measure to mitigate aging and enhance function in these individuals. Strength, cardiorespiratory fitness, balance, and flexibility should all be acquired as part of different exercises, following the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) guidelines [11]. Several exercise options are available to enhance physical activity, performance, and overall health status. These include stretching, aerobic exercise, resistance-based strengthening, sensorimotor exercise, and endurance workouts, which have increased physical fitness levels, decreased falls, and promote healthy aging in community-dwelling older adults [4, 12]. Among them, a cost-effective adequate exercise prescription should be included in all healthcare recommendations to improve independent physical activity and performance levels, fall prevention, and overall quality of life by encouraging exercise for all older adults, whether healthy or weak [6]. A meta-analysis is recommended to determine the optimal measures or fitness regimens for enhancing older adults’ overall quality of life, especially at the community level [13]. Therefore, this trial is designed for two community-based multimodal exercise programs (CMEPs). Two evidence-based exercise options, sensorimotor exercise, and strengthening exercise, will be compared in terms of physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life for older adults.

Sensorimotor exercise is considered a comprehensive form of balance training. Sensorimotor exercise includes resolving muscular imbalance, facilitating proprioception and somatosensory inputs, and ensuring proper motor development at the central nervous system level [14]. Older adults require continuous improvement in skills to cope with the sensorimotor needs of daily tasks, identify barriers, relay input to the nervous system, interpret it, and select the optimal motor response. Older adults’ fall risk can be decreased by maintaining dynamic stability, static stability, and balance through the synergistic action of the vestibular, visual, and sensorimotor systems. Recent studies indicate that sensorimotor exercise programs significantly impact physical activity, performance levels, fall prevention, and quality of life, promoting a healthy lifestyle [4]. Numerous studies have also shown the substantial advantages of strengthening exercise programs for maintaining muscular mass, strength, and functional ability in older adults. This helps to lessen the impacts of frailty and sarcopenia. Research has shown that progressively increasing exercise increases neuromuscular function and balance, lowers the chance of falls, and improves the quality of life, promoting healthy aging [3, 13]. Despite the well-established benefits of sensorimotor and strengthening exercises, there is a lack of direct comparative studies evaluating their relative effectiveness in community settings for older adults [15]. Further research is necessary to establish robust evidence regarding the efficacy of sensorimotor and strengthening exercise as components of community-based multimodal exercise programs (CMEPs) for older adults. For individuals with varying functional capacities, implementing appropriate exercise regimens may enhance activities of daily living (ADLs) and improve community-dwelling older adults’ overall quality of life.

There is still limited research in Bangladesh comparing different exercise regimens for community-dwelling older adults. Additionally, on a global scale, few studies have directly compared sensorimotor and strengthening exercise programs within community-based settings for older adults. This gap in the literature underscores the need for further investigation of these exercise programs in promoting functional health and well-being among older adults. Therefore, this trial is designed to investigate the comparative effectiveness of two community-based multimodal exercise programs (CMEPs)—sensorimotor and strengthening exercises, when integrated with other exercise programs using a clearly defined methodology and procedure.

Objective

This trial will investigate the short- and long-term comparative effectiveness of two community-based multimodal exercise programs (CMEPs)—sensorimotor exercise and strengthening exercise- on physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life in older adults.

Research question

What is the short- and long-term comparative effectiveness of sensorimotor versus strengthening community-based multimodal exercise programs on physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life in older adults?

Hypotheses

We expect that the community-based sensorimotor exercise program (CMEP-I) will demonstrate superior improvements in outcome measures compared to other programs for community-dwelling older adults.

Methodology

Trial design

The trial will be conducted as an assessor and participant-blinded, multicenter, randomized clinical trial involving two groups: CMEP-I: Sensorimotor exercise group and CMEP-II: Strengthening exercise group with a 1:1 ratio by block randomization procedure between July 2025 and May 2026. There will be 24 sessions total, or 8 weeks of intervention, followed by 24 weeks of follow-up for each group. The intervention period will last from July to October 2025, followed by a 24-week follow-up period, which is expected to conclude by March 2026. This timeline encompasses all trial procedures, including data collection and analysis. All outcome measurements will be collected at three time points: pretest, 8-week posttest, and 24-week follow-up periods.

Trial setting

This trial will be conducted at four community clinics in Jashore, Bangladesh (Bahadurpur, Bajaedurgapur, Baganmath, and Kholadanga). Each participant will be assigned to one of the groups in these community-level settings (Fig. 1) based on eligibility.Fig. 1Trial setting

Trial setting

Sample size calculation

G*power, version 3.1.9.7 (University of Kiel, Kiel, Germany), was used to determine the number of trial participants. Based on the calculations, 110 participants are required with an effect size (ES) of 0.54, an α level of 0.05, and a power value of 0.80 according to the effect [16]. Therefore, each group requires 55 participants. After allowing for a 30% dropout, we increased the number of participants in each group to 70, and the final estimated number of participants for this trial will be 140. Although no single primary outcome is defined, the chosen effect size corresponds to moderate and clinically meaningful improvements in functional performance and health-related outcomes in older adults, as reported in similar studies [16].

Participants and recruitment

One hundred forty male and female eligible participants will be recruited through online-offline advertisements, religious institutes’ miking, and flyers at community clinics in Jashore, Bangladesh. Interested eligible participants will have an informative discussion session about the trial details, plan, objectives, risks, benefits, and a question-answering session. Participants will complete an informed consent form before enrolment (see Supplementary file 1) and can withdraw from the research at any moment without affecting their intervention course. We want to make sure that participants are recruited fairly and impartially.

Eligibility criteria

Responsible health personnel will recruit all participants using an unbiased initial screening procedure based on the trial’s inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria

Individuals will be included if (1) male or female community-dwelling older adults aged 60–80 years, (2) physical independence level ≥ 18 points according to a 12-item Composite Physical Function (CPF) scale [17], (3) able to walk without walking aids, and (4) Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE) score ≥ 18 [15].

Exclusion criteria

Individuals will be excluded if (1) they experienced severe visual disturbances, (2) they have any soft tissue or joint injuries or fractures within the last 3 months [15], (3) they have a severe heart disease diagnosis or uncontrolled systemic arterial hypertension, (4) they have a cancer diagnosis and are receiving palliative care, or (5) they are taking psychotropic, antiarrhythmic, or hypnotic drugs [4, 5].

Randomization, treatment allocation, and blinding

After confirming participant eligibility and obtaining written informed consent, randomization will be conducted using a simple 1:1 allocation method via the online platform (http://randomizer.org) to ensure confidentiality. Participants will be randomly split into two groups, CMEP-I and CMEP-II. The research assistant will be responsible for the randomization process but will not participate in the intervention or data collection. Participants, research assistants, assessors, and data statisticians will remain unaware of group assignments to maintain blinding. Nevertheless, the nature of the intervention eliminates the possibility of blinding the healthcare providers who will administer the interventions. The participants will be informed of the interventions but not of their group allocation. The trial will be overseen by a trial manager and an independent monitoring team to ensure proper coordination and adherence to the trial protocol.

Trial procedure

Eligible participants will be screened in community settings and given a trial overview and a written consent form (see Supplementary file 1). Participants will be contacted for the pretest and intervention following group allocation. Depending on group allocation, participants will receive either sensorimotor or strengthening exercise programs (CMEP-I or CMEP-II) for 8 weeks. After 8 weeks of intervention, blind assessors will administer a posttest and a follow-up evaluation at 32 weeks. During this 24-week follow-up phase, health discussion sessions involving both participants and their guardians will be organized every 2 months. The trial will be coordinated by a dedicated centre responsible for day-to-day management, including participant recruitment, intervention delivery, data collection, and protocol adherence. A Trial Steering Committee will provide overall oversight, meeting regularly to review progress, ensure ethical and scientific standards, and guide trial conduct. Other support groups will assist with intervention logistics, scheduling, and quality control. Weekly interim reviews will be conducted to monitor trial conduct, participant safety, and address any issues. The integrity of this trial’s procedure (enrollment, assessment, and intervention) will be maintained following the SPIRIT 2013 standard protocol (Table 1). Interventions

Eight certified physiotherapists with over ten years of clinical experience will deliver two community-based multimodal exercise programs (CMEPs) to the trial participants. The researcher will monitor the interventions and do assessments to ensure the trial protocol is followed but will not be personally involved in the exercise administration. Both intervention groups will undergo 24 sessions over 8 weeks, with the exercise sessions conducted in small groups of two participants per session. Certified physiotherapists will supervise each session to ensure proper implementation and protocol compliance. The structure of each session will include an initial warm-up, followed by aerobic exercise, stretching, and the assigned intervention: sensorimotor or strengthening exercise, based on group allocation. The session will conclude with a cool-down period. The exercise methods, dosages, and progression plans will be carefully followed to ensure consistency across both intervention groups.

Additional standard care for both groups

Warm-up: Joint mobility exercises to prepare muscles and joints for exercise for 5 min [18, 20].

Aerobic exercise (walking): Light walking to improve cardiovascular fitness. For 30 min, maintain intensity at 60–75% of maximum heart rate [20, 21].

Stretching: Stretch the hamstrings, quadriceps, and triceps surae to improve flexibility and reduce stiffness for a 30-s hold, 3–5 repetitions [5, 19–21].

Cool-down: Breathing exercises to promote relaxation and recovery for 5 min [20, 21].

Outcome measurements

The trial's primary outcomes are physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life, which will be assessed using the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE), Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), Modified Falls Efficacy Scale (MFES), and the 36-item Short Form Survey (SF-36). Additionally, sociodemographic variables such as age, gender, BMI (body mass index), heart rate, blood pressure, the existence of multiple comorbidities (including hypertension and diabetes mellitus) assessed by the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), ongoing medications, prior fall incidents, physical activity or employment status, and other essential variables will be incorporated as generalized determinants and modifiers. The outcomes and variable factors will be evaluated at the pretest, posttest, and follow-up stages. All primary outcomes are listed below with short descriptions.

Physical activity

Physical activity will be measured with the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE), a validated questionnaire that measures physical activity levels in older adults over one week. The PASE score is calculated based on self-reported leisure, home, and work-related participation, with scores ranging from 0 to 400 or higher, where higher values represent higher physical activity levels. This scale shows strong reliability, evidenced by an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) between 0.66 and 0.99, establishing it as a reliable measurement for habitual physical activity in older adults. [22].

Physical performance

Physical performance will be measured using the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), a validated questionnaire that measures physical performance levels in older adults through three key performance-based assessments. The battery consists of (1) a balance test that includes side-by-side, semi-tandem, and tandem stance assessments (scored 0–4); (2) a gait speed test, where participants complete a 4-m walk (scored 0–4); and (3) a sit-to-stand test, which involves five repeated chair rises (scored 0–4). The higher values represent higher physical performance levels, with scores ranging from 0 to 1. This scale shows excellent reliability, with an ICC ranging from 0.81 to 0.91 [23, 24].

Fall prevention

Fall will be measured using the Modified Falls Efficacy Scale (MFES), a 14-item validated questionnaire that measures an individual’s confidence to go about daily tasks without falling. Each task is rated on a 10-point scale (0–10). The MFES includes activities relevant to both indoor and outdoor settings, providing a comprehensive evaluation of fall-related self-efficacy. The higher values represent higher confidence and less fear of falling. This scale shows high reliability, with an ICC of 0.95, supporting its use in fall risk assessments among older adults [22, 25].

Quality of life

Quality of life will be measured using the 36-Item Short Form Survey (SF-36), a validated questionnaire that measures the quality of life across eight domains: physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, role limitations due to emotional problems, energy/fatigue, emotional well-being, social functioning, pain, and general health perception. The higher values represent better health-related quality of life, with scores ranging from 0 to 100. This scale shows strong psychometric properties, with an ICC of 0.90, making it a reliable measure of overall well-being in older adults [26, 27].

Follow-up phase

After the 8-week intervention, the participants will be encouraged to live everyday lifestyles. Each will receive a “Healthy Lifestyle and Advice Guide for Older Adults” in Bengali. Peer support, interactive health education, and individual feedback will be given every two months in discussion sessions to enhance motivation and overcome regular physical activity challenges. After 24 weeks, a final assessment will be performed to determine the long-term effects of the intervention.

Staff training

Training will be conducted online and offline, with continuous access to materials, manuals, checklists, trial protocol, and the principal investigator’s guidance. The training methods will primarily focus on the roles of health professionals, assessors, and research assistants. Health professionals will receive training in social engagement, leadership, communication, and the delivery of interventions. The assessors will receive training in data collection, participant screening, permission processes, and structured interviews. Responsible personnel will monitor the trial procedure and help with adjustments as necessary.

Data management

This trial's data will undergo rigorous verification to ensure accuracy. The verified final dataset will be accessible to the principal investigator, trial manager, and data monitoring personnel. At the end of the trial, each author will have similar access. The principal investigator will safely keep all the data confidential to ensure participant confidentiality. To reduce mistakes, two manual data-entry sessions will be performed. Electronic data will be stored securely on encrypted servers. Each participant will be given a unique ID to save encoded data. Post-trial intervention will be offered only in infrequent instances of undesirable consequences. The findings will be published in aggregate form after statistical analysis using de-identified data.

Monitoring

Four non-trial personnel will monitor all community-level settings, group enrollment, intervention regimens, and side effects. They will also review and carry out a preliminary analysis. The primary researcher will notify the Institutional Review Board if the intervention or procedure changes. Weekly interim reviews will also be carried out by independent research team members to monitor participant safety and identify any possible side effects. A participant satisfaction survey will be completed after each intervention session to gather feedback and evaluate participants’ experiences. In case of any serious concern, the research team will immediately notify the principal investigator and the ethical review board for further action. No formal statistical stopping rules are planned, as the interventions carry minimal risk. The trial will continue until participant recruitment and follow-up are complete, unless significant ethical or safety issues arise.

Safety precautions and adverse effects management

The four monitoring personnel and a qualified physician will closely observe participants for any unexpected events during and after the intervention, promptly alerting the appropriate professionals if necessary. Any unfavorable events will be noted in the SOAP notes and shared with the principal investigator. The participants will be encouraged to contact their physiotherapist immediately if they experience pain, skin irritation, or discomfort. The principal investigator will promptly notify the Institutional Review Board of any procedure modifications and record any notable adverse effects. Protocol amendments, including those prompted by adverse effects, will first be submitted to the ethical review board for approval. Once approved, the principal investigator (PI) will inform all participating centres, and a copy of the revised protocol will be provided to each co-PI for inclusion in the Investigator Site File. All protocol deviations will be documented using a breach report form, and the protocol will be updated in the clinical trial registry to ensure transparency and regulatory compliance.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis will be conducted using SPSS version 20 for Windows. The normality of continuous data will be assessed through visual methods (such as bell curves) and statistical tests (including skewness, kurtosis, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, and Shapiro–Wilk). Descriptive statistics will summarize participant characteristics and outcome measures using mean ± standard deviation, frequency, and percentage. Since all four outcome measures are regarded as equally important for healthy ageing, analyses will examine changes within and between groups for each outcome. Within-group changes will be analyzed using paired t-tests for normally distributed data or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed data. Between-group comparisons of change scores will use independent t-tests for normally distributed outcomes or Mann–Whitney tests otherwise. For repeated measures across time points (baseline, 8 weeks, 24 weeks), repeated measures ANOVA will be employed for normally distributed outcomes, including group, time, and group × time interaction as fixed effects to evaluate differential intervention trajectories; the Friedman test will be used for non-normal data. Post hoc testing will be performed using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney tests with Bonferroni correction. Sensitivity analyses incorporating ANCOVA will adjust for baseline values and potential confounders such as age and gender. Missing data will be handled under the missing at random (MAR) assumption using an intention-to-treat (ITT) approach. The last observation carried forward (LOCF) method will be applied, and multiple imputation will be used if necessary to maintain data integrity and analytical validity. Statistical significance will be set at p < 0.05.

Dissemination

The trial's results will be disseminated to older adults, health professionals, physiotherapists, and researchers at an event. The findings will also be presented at international conferences and submitted to a peer-reviewed publication. The results will be disseminated as an open-access journal paper to optimize accessibility worldwide. These initiatives aim to enhance the lives of older adults who live in the community.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the general public were not involved in the design, reporting, or dissemination strategies of this study.

Discussion

The aging population poses significant challenges, such as a lower life expectancy, a greater chance of falling, and a decline in activities of daily living (ADLs). Evidence-based nonpharmacological treatments are crucial for addressing these issues, as pharmaceutical treatments cannot address them effectively. As a means of slowing the aging process, enhancing physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and improving the health-related quality of life in older adults, evidence-based physical exercise programs are needed [20, 28]. More specifically, it is essential for community-dwelling older adults because of their limited access to early intervention and essential healthcare services. Based on current recommendations and research, two evidence-based community-based multimodal exercise programs were chosen for their ability to reduce various age-related health problems and promote overall quality of life [22, 28–30]. This trial will investigate the comparative effectiveness of two community-based multimodal exercise programs (CMEPs) for older adults in Bangladesh. The goal is to develop an evidence-based exercise protocol for enhancing older individuals’ overall physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life, promoting healthy aging. To ensure an impartial and precise evaluation of the exercise methods, both exercise groups will be subjected to the same kind of exposure, with the same number of sessions and durations. The exercise protocol was chosen to provide flexibility in execution, allowing older adults to administer it in various settings. Moreover, the exercises are structured to facilitate self-management among older adults in the future, potentially enhancing consistency and mobility. After this trial is over, older adults who can perform these exercises at home will be more empowered to take charge of their health, and the healthcare system will be less burdened since there may be a gradual decrease in the requirement for in-person monitoring. The results of this research may have substantial implications for public health and older adults. Positive results could motivate these exercises to be widely adopted in various community and healthcare contexts. Furthermore, this trial will advance the knowledge of how different exercise programs can be chosen to respond to the unique requirements of older adults, especially for community-dwelling individuals who might not have regular access to public healthcare services.

In summary, this trial will contribute significantly to understanding how community-based multimodal exercise programs (sensorimotor and strengthening exercise) effectively improve overall physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life, promoting healthy aging in community-level older adults. This trial may enhance older individuals’ health and well-being and encourage healthy aging more widely by providing an evidence-based exercise protocol that is both practical and accessible. Positive results might promote the implementation of these methods as self-management strategies for older adults and lead to their incorporation into routine care procedures.

This trial addresses a significant research gap by being the first RCT in Bangladesh to investigate the effectiveness of two community-based multimodal exercise programs (CMEPs) on physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life for older individuals. The trial design with blinded participants and assessors increases internal validity by minimizing bias and reinforcing causal inference. Nevertheless, the trial’s generalizability may be compromised by its emphasis on a specific Bangladeshi community. Furthermore, participant retention issues throughout the 24-week follow-up period may have influenced the final sample size and outcome assessments.

Trial status

Recruitment for this trial will start in July 2025, and finalization (including follow-up testing) is expected to be finished by May 2026, in compliance with the eligibility conditions.

Implications of this trial

This trial will involve community-dwelling older adults receiving two types of multimodal exercise interventions. These findings may significantly influence geriatric rehabilitation by highlighting the comparative benefits of sensorimotor and strengthening exercises in improving physical activity, performance, fall prevention, and quality of life. The outcomes from this trial will be valuable to clinicians, researchers, and policymakers by contributing to the evidence base for nonpharmacological strategies in aging care. The results may support the development of accessible, community-based exercise guidelines and encourage further research into sustainable, cost-effective interventions for promoting functional independence and healthy aging in resource-limited settings.
