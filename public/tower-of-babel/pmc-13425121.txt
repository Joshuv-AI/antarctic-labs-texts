Legume and soy consumption and the risk of hypertension: a systematic review and dose–response meta-analysis of prospective studies
Authors: Michael Metoudi, Isabelle Sadler, Shireen Kassam, Dagfinn Aune
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeveral studies have suggested that high intakes of legumes and soy products are associated with a lower risk of hypertension; however, the results have been inconsistent. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to clarify the association between legumes and soy consumption and the risk of hypertension.MethodsPubMed and Embase databases were searched up to 14 June 2025. Random effects models were used to calculate summary relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the association between legume or soy consumption and hypertension risk. Heterogeneity was evaluated using I2. The likelihood of causality was evaluated using World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF) criteria.Results12 prospective studies were included in the meta-analysis. The summary RR for high versus low intake of legumes was 0.84 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.93, I2=65%, pheterogeneity=0.003, n=10 studies, 86 098 cases, 309 853 participants) and for soy was 0.81 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.93, I2=82%, pheterogeneity<0.0001, n=7 studies, 93 934 cases, 278 200 participants). In the linear dose–response analyses, the summary RR per 100 g/day was 0.88 (95% CI 0.80 to 0.97, I2=69%, pheterogeneity=0.001, n=10) for legumes and 0.76 (95% CI 0.60 to 0.96, I2=89%, pheterogeneity<0.0001, n=6) for soy. The test for non-linearity was not significant for legumes (pnon-linearity=0.13), suggesting a linear reduction in risk up to ~170 g/day, while for soy there was indication of non-linearity (pnon-linearity=0.01), and most of the reduction in risk was observed up to an intake around 60–80 g/day. Although there was an indication of publication bias with Egger’s test (p=0.04) for legumes, this was explained by two outlying studies. Using WCRF criteria, the likelihood of causality was considered probable for both legumes and soy in relation to hypertension risk.ConclusionIn this meta-analysis of 12 prospective cohort studies, legume and soy intakes were associated with lower risk of hypertension. These findings support dietary recommendations to increase the intake of legumes in the general population.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective studies identified an inverse association between legume and soy food intake on overall incidence of hypertension and highlights new evidence of a dose-dependent relationship of up to 170 g for legumes and 60–80 g for soy food intake. This is the first meta-analysis to evaluate the association between soy food intake and hypertension risk.

Introduction

Hypertension poses one of the most significant health challenges globally, affecting an estimated 1.4 billion people worldwide.1 Hypertension is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease, the leading cause of premature death globally.2 According to data from the latest Global Burden of Disease Study, hypertension accounts for 8.4% of total disability-adjusted life years globally in 2023,3 representing the number one modifiable risk factor for disease burden. The prevalence of hypertension has significantly increased from 648 million cases in 1990 to over 1.28 billion cases in 2019, representing a 115% increase over three decades.4 These trends are of significant concern given the higher rates of hypertension existing among low- and middle-income countries, thus contributing to further health inequalities.

Legumes, such as beans, lentils, chickpeas and peas, and soy such as soybean, tofu and fermented soy products, represent a particularly promising food group for cardiovascular health due to their unique nutritional profile, including high content of potassium and magnesium, dietary fibre, plant protein and bioactive compounds such as polyphenols and isoflavones. A recent meta-analysis of prospective cohort and case–control studies by Mendes et al showed an inverse association between legume intake and overall incidence of cardiovascular disease but not stroke.5 Similar results were also found in a meta-analysis of prospective studies by Zargarzadeh et al showing a lower risk of all-cause mortality and stroke mortality with higher intake of legumes, but associations with other cardiovascular health outcomes were less clear.6 While not fully elucidated, legumes may offer cardioprotective benefits primarily through improving established risk factors for cardiovascular disease such as lowering lipids, improving endothelial function and reducing inflammation linked to cardiovascular disease.7 Further research is needed to understand the mechanisms through which they might lower blood pressure.

Currently, the evidence for the association of legume consumption in mitigating hypertension risk remains unclear. Several meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have shown mixed results for legumes in lowering the risk of blood pressure.8 9 In a meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies by Viguiliouk et al,10 the authors reported that the highest versus lowest level of legume consumption was associated with a significant 13% lower risk of hypertension. However, they noted very low certainty for hypertension incidence owing to downgrades for risk of bias. Similarly, the data specifically on soy food intake and hypertension risk are limited to date. Most studies, including meta-analyses of RCTs, have evaluated soy extracts such as isoflavones or soy protein isolates on hypertension risk, showing a significant reduction in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure.11 Several additional prospective cohort studies have been published since these meta-analyses were published and showed inverse associations between legumes and soy foods and hypertension risk.12–14

We therefore conducted an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies to evaluate and synthesise the current evidence relating to legume and soy consumption and the association with hypertension risk, with a particular emphasis on clarifying the dose–response relationship.

Methods

Protocol and search strategy

We searched PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and Embase (https://www.embase.com/) databases from inception to 14 June 2025. The full search strategy terms are provided in the online supplemental material. Search terms included additional food items for the purpose of being used as part of a wider project. For this review, the current analysis focused on legumes and soy (see search strategy in supplementary material). This systematic review and meta-analyses followed the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions guidelines15 and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for reporting meta-analyses.16 The review protocol was preregistered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (Registration ID: CRD420251138887) and is available at: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251138887.

Inclusion criteria and study selection

Studies included in the meta-analysis were deemed eligible if they met all the following inclusion criteria: evaluated the association between legume intake and the risk of hypertension, with a prospective cohort, nested case–control study within cohort or case–cohort study design that reported adjusted relative risk (RR) estimates for the association between legume intake and the risk of hypertension or elevated blood pressure, and analysed a general, population-based cohort (without focusing on a specific patient-based population). Retrospective case–control studies, cross-sectional studies, conference abstracts and grey literature were excluded. We defined legumes as any foods within the Fabaceae family. We used the Rayyan systematic review tool for the initial rounds 1 and 2 article screening. Studies were screened independently by two reviewers (MM, IS) in the initial rounds 1 and 2 and checked by a third reviewer (DA) in the event of any outstanding conflict.

Data extraction

The following data were extracted from each study: name of the first author, publication year, country where the study was conducted, study duration, number of participants and cases, age of the study population at baseline, sex, exposure, quantity of legume or soy consumed per category, RR estimates and 95% confidence interval (95% CI), follow-up period and variables adjusted for in the analyses. Two reviewers (MM, IS) extracted the relevant data and these were checked for accuracy by a second reviewer (DA).

Quality assessment and study quality

The quality of each included study was independently assessed by two authors (MM, IS) using a modified version of the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) as previously described17 as adapted from the original version.18 This categorises a scoring domain into low (0–3), medium (>3–6), and high (>6–8) quality, respectively.

Grading of the evidence

The strength of evidence was assessed using grading criteria from the World Cancer Research Fund (WCRF).19 This grading system includes multiple criteria: evidence from different study types, the number of available studies, study heterogeneity, quality of the studies, dose–response relationship, biological plausibility and experimental evidence. The evidence, assessing the likelihood of causality, is graded as follows: convincing, probable, limited-suggestive, limited-no conclusion and substantial effect on risk unlikely.

Statistical analysis

A random effects model by DerSimonian and Laird20 was used to calculate the summary RRs and 95% CIs for the association between legumes and risk of hypertension. The average of the natural logarithm of the RRs was estimated and the RR from each study was weighted using random effects weights. For the linear dose–response analysis, the method of Greenland and Longnecker21 was used to calculate study-specific linear trends (slopes) across the different categories of legume consumption. For studies that reported on legume consumption by ranges of intake, we estimated the midpoint for each category by calculating the average of the lower and higher cut-off points. When extreme categories were open-ended, we used the width of the adjacent interval to estimate a lower or higher cut-off point. When studies reported medians of intake per category, these were used directly. For studies that reported intakes in servings/day or week, we converted the quantities to g/day by using 100 g as a serving size, as is previously reported within the literature and recommended by clinical guidance for a standard measure serving size.22 A potential curvilinear dose–response relationship between legume intake and risk of hypertension was examined by using restricted cubic splines with three knots at percentiles of distribution including 10%, 50% and 90%, which were pooled using multivariable meta-analysis.23

Heterogeneity between studies was assessed with I2 statistics.24 The I2 statistic represents the amount of total variation between studies that could be attributed to heterogeneity. We conducted subgroup and meta-regression analyses for legumes to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity between studies, including sex, duration of follow-up, geographical location, number of cases, study quality, diet assessment method and adjustment for confounding factors. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to investigate the stability of the results by excluding one study at a time from the meta-analysis to test whether the results were determined or driven by one very large or outlying study. We tested for publication bias using Egger’s test,25 with a p value <0.1 indicating potential bias, and by visual inspection of the funnel plots. We estimated E-values for the association between legumes and soy and hypertension to assess potential unadjusted confounding.26 The statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Characteristics of included studies

The literature search resulted in a total of 8322 records which were screened; out of these, ten publications with data from 12 prospective cohort studies12–14 27–33 were included in the current systematic review and meta-analysis (figure 1). A list of excluded studies and reasons for exclusion can be found in online supplemental table S1. Characteristics of the included studies are shown in online supplemental table S2. One publication contained data from three prospective cohort studies.32 Five studies were from the USA, five from Asia (two from China and one each from Iran, South Korea and Japan), and two studies were from Europe (one each from France and the UK). Nine studies included both men and women, two studies included only women, and one study included only men. The number of participants ranged from 1152 to 88 475 and the number of hypertension cases ranged from 144 to 35 375 cases across studies. The mean (median) study quality score was 6.7 (7.0) for the 10 studies included on legumes and 6.9 (7.0) for the seven studies included on soy (online supplemental table S3).

Legumes and hypertension

Ten cohort studies (eight publications) with 86 098 cases and 309 853 participants were included in the analysis of legume consumption and the association with hypertension risk.12–14 27 28 30–32 The summary RR for high versus low legume intake was 0.84 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.93, I2=65%, pheterogeneity=0.003, n=10) (figure 2a). In the linear dose–response analyses, the summary RR was 0.88 (95% CI 0.80 to 0.97, I2=69%, pheterogeneity=0.001, n=10) per 100 g/day of legumes (figure 3a). The summary RR ranged from 0.86 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.95) when excluding the Nurses’ Health Study II (NHSII) cohort by Borgi et al32 to 0.92 (95% CI 0.85 to 0.99) when excluding the study by Guo et al14 (see online supplemental figure S1). There was an indication of publication bias with Egger’s test (p=0.04) (online supplemental figure S2); however, this was attenuated to non-significance when excluding two outlying studies28 34 for Egger’s test (p=0.13) without materially affecting the overall summary estimate (summary RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.85 to 0.98). There was no evidence of non-linearity (pnon-linearity=0.13) with a dose-dependent reduction in risk up to an intake of 173 g/day (figure 3b, Table S4).

Soy and hypertension

Seven cohort studies (five publications) with 93 934 cases and 278 200 participants were included in the high vs. low analysis of soy consumption and hypertension.12 14 29 32 33 and six of these studies (four publications) were included in the dose-response analysis.12 14 29 32 The summary RR for high versus low soy intake was 0.81 (95% CI 0.70 to 0.93, I2=82%, pheterogeneity<0.0001, n=7) (figure 2b) and 0.76 (95% CI 0.60 to 0.96, I2=89%, pheterogeneity<0.0001, n=6) per 100 g/day of soy (figure 3c). The summary RR ranged from 0.71 (95% CI 0.54 to 0.94) when excluding the Health Professionals Follow-up Study (HPFS) by Borgi et al,32 to 0.86 (95% CI 0.76 to 0.98) when excluding the study by Guo et al14 (online supplemental figure S3). There was no indication of publication bias with Egger’s test (p=0.32) or by inspection of the funnel plot (online supplemental figure S4). There was an indication of a non-linear association between soy intake and hypertension (pnon-linearity=0.01) with no further reduction in hypertension risk above an intake of 60–80 g/day (figure 3d, Table S5). There was no indication of publication bias with Egger’s test (p=0.32) (online supplemental figure S4).

Subgroup analyses and E-values

The inverse association between legume intake and hypertension persisted across many, but not all, subgroups for legumes (online supplemental table S6). There was significant heterogeneity between the subgroups stratified by number of cases (p=0.007) and by adjustment for fruits and vegetables (p=0.006), with a stronger association in studies with fewer cases and among the two studies with adjustment for fruit and vegetable intake.13 14 The inverse association between soy intake and hypertension also persisted across several, but not all, subgroups (online supplemental table S6). There was significant heterogeneity between subgroups when stratified by number of cases (p=0.01), study quality (p=0.01), and diet assessment method (p=0.01). However, this was based on five studies in one subgroup and only one study in the other subgroup, across all these study characteristics.

The estimated E-values for an intake of 173 versus 0 g/day intake of legumes was 2.21 (lower CI 1.74) and for 80 versus 0 g/day of soy was 2.17 (lower CI 1.39) based on the non-linear dose–response analyses.

Grading of the evidence

When using the WCRF criteria (online supplemental table S7) for evaluating the likelihood of a causal relationship between legumes and soy and hypertension, we considered the overall evidence to be supportive of a probable causal relationship between both legume and soy intake and a reduced risk of hypertension. A detailed justification for these assessments is found in online supplemental table S8.

Discussion

In this meta-analysis of 12 prospective cohort studies, the highest versus lowest intake of legumes and soy was associated with a 16% and 19% lower risk of incident hypertension, respectively. In the linear dose–response analyses, there was a 12% and 24% lower risk per 100 g/day of legumes and soy, respectively. There was a clear dose–response relationship, with no indication of non-linearity, between increasing intakes of legumes and lower hypertension risk with a 30% reduction in risk at an intake of ~170 g/day, while for soy there was a plateau with a 28–29% reduction in risk at 60–80 g/day, with no further reduction in risk at higher intakes.

One hundred grams of legumes is equivalent to a serving size of approximately one cup or 5–6 tablespoons of cooked beans, peas, chickpeas, lentils, soybeans or a palm-size serving of tofu. This amount is similar to most European food-based dietary guidelines, recommending a plant-based protein source of 60–200 g/day35 or 80 g/day of ‘1 of your 5 a day’ in the UK,36 although the latter is recommended as a vegetable source rather than a protein source.

Our results are consistent with a previous meta-analysis of 7 cohort studies that reported a 9% lower risk of hypertension for high versus low intake of legumes10 but the current study included a larger number of cohorts (n=10) on legumes and is to our knowledge the first meta-analysis to analyse the association between soy foods and hypertension (n=7). A meta-analysis of eight RCTs has shown results partly in line with our findings, where Jayalath et al8 showed a significant reduction in systolic blood pressure and a non-significant reduction in diastolic blood pressure among middle-aged individuals with a mixed metabolic phenotype, with or without hypertension, who consumed an average of 162 g/day portion of legumes. A more recent meta-analysis of 16 RCTs by Reyneke et al9 did not show a significant effect on systolic or diastolic blood pressure overall (median legume intake of 100 g/day), although the effect size was in the direction of a beneficial effect. However, a significant reduction in systolic blood pressure was observed in some subgroups including individuals who were overweight and/or obese, persons without diabetes and with legume intakes of ≤450 g/week and 450 to <1000 g/week. The partial inconsistent results for these meta-analyses of RCTs may be partly due to some potential cardioprotective mechanisms that may require longer exposure periods than these RCTs might capture in order to see any meaningful clinical results. For example, arterial stiffness, which is thought to precede hypertension,37 is a predictor of hypertension incidence.38 The meta-analyses of both Jayalath et al and Reyneke et al included RCTs with a duration of 8–16 weeks whereas it appears from other RCTs of dietary interventions in hypertension that arterial reversal can take at least 12 months to see clinically meaningful results.39 With soy intake, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised double-blind placebo-controlled trials showed that higher soy intakes led to a significant reduction in systolic and diastolic blood pressure for both normotensive and hypertensive adults.40 A second meta-analysis reported that substitution of cow’s milk with soy milk led to reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure,41 and similar results have been observed in meta-analyses of soy protein and soy isoflavones.11

Several potential mechanisms and components within legumes and soy foods may explain or contribute to the observed benefits on hypertension risk, including their content of minerals, fibre and bioactive compounds. Both legumes and soy are high in potassium and magnesium, which have demonstrated blood pressure-lowering effects in meta-analyses of prospective cohort studies.42 Legumes and soy are also rich in dietary fibre, which is linked to lower rates of hypertension and overall cardiovascular disease risk.43 Recent studies have suggested that the fermentation of soluble fibre from legumes and soy produces short-chain fatty acids that influence blood vessel dilation through endothelial nitric oxide (NO) signalling, stimulating NO production and resulting in lower blood pressure.44 With soy in particular, the isoflavone content, such as genistein and daidzein, appears to have blood pressure-lowering effects as demonstrated in several meta-analyses of RCTs.11 45 Finally, there is recent evidence to suggest that intakes of legumes and soy are associated with a lower risk of obesity,34 which itself is a primary risk factor for hypertension.46

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this meta-analysis includes the detailed and systematic search strategy, the detailed dose–response, subgroup and sensitivity analyses, the high quality of the included prospective cohort studies and the assessment of the strength of the evidence using the WCRF criteria. The majority of the studies had long-term follow-up, large sample sizes, with adjustments for multiple important confounding factors, thereby minimising the possibility of potential confounding variables that might have an impact on the observed associations.

There are certain limitations to this meta-analysis that require consideration when interpreting the results. First, there was high heterogeneity for both the high versus low and dose-response analysis of legume and soy intake. This was due to differences in the strength of the associations as well as some studies showing no clear association; however, most of the studies reported risk estimates in the direction of reduced risk. Some of the heterogeneity between studies may be related to differences in legume types, levels of intake, preparation methods and dietary contexts, which our analysis may not have been able to account for fully, although we conducted both dose–response analyses and subgroup analyses stratified by adjustment for multiple confounders and by geographic region. The term ‘legumes’ encompasses a diverse array of foods, including beans, chickpeas, lentils, soybeans, tofu and tempeh, with many regional varieties, and may differ in their impact on hypertension risk. Although we observed some heterogeneity between subgroups with meta-regression analyses, these subgroup analyses are in a sense ecological (at the study level), and some of the heterogeneity between subgroups could be related to fewer studies in some of the subgroups as well as by chance, or other correlated study characteristics. Therefore, they should be interpreted with caution.

Differences between studies in how hypertension was defined could cause some heterogeneity; however, there was no heterogeneity between these subgroups. We cannot exclude the possibility that the observed association between legumes and soy and hypertension could be due to confounding. However, many of the studies adjusted for important confounding factors, and the associations persisted across most subgroup analyses with adjustment for a range of confounding factors. The estimated E-values from the non-linear dose–response analysis for legume and soy intakes and hypertension were 2.21 (lower CI 1.74) at 173 g/day of legumes and 2.17 (lower CI 1.39) at 80 g/day of soy, suggesting an unadjusted confounder would have to be strongly associated with both legume or soy intake and hypertension to fully explain away the observed associations. Our meta-analysis suggested a linear inverse association between legume intake and the incidence of hypertension across the full range of legume intake (0–173 g/day), while the association with soy intake plateaued at 60–80 g/day. Although we cannot exclude the possibility of a differential dose–response relationship between legumes and soy in relation to the incidence of hypertension, we also cannot exclude the possibility that the lower number of studies included in the analysis of soy may have contributed to the observed differences. Given the limited number of studies on specific types of legumes or soy (eg, tofu, natto, etc) we could not conduct further analyses of these. Further large-scale cohort studies are therefore needed to clarify the association between specific legumes and soy products and hypertension risk. Although we found some indication of publication bias, this appeared to be driven by two outlying studies that, when excluded, did not substantially alter the overall conclusions. None of the studies assessed legume intake within the context of Southeast Asian regions, where increasing rates of hypertension exist, and further studies are needed.

Future perspectives

Despite these limitations, the findings of this meta-analysis have major public health implications, given the alarming global increase in hypertension prevalence. Current legume consumption across Europe and the UK remains below dietary recommendations, with average intakes of only 8–15 g/day,47 far below the recommendations of 65 to 100 g/day recommended for overall cardiovascular health.35 Additionally, this public health issue has been reinforced by the recently published 2025 EAT-Lancet Commission report highlighting the latest evidence for healthy, sustainable and just food systems, which maintains legume intake recommendations at 75 g/day in keeping within both planetary boundaries, human health and an equal and just food system.48 The current analysis suggested further benefits in relation to hypertension with higher legume intakes than what is currently recommended. Future research should prioritise several key directions. Additional large-scale prospective cohort and intervention studies are needed to confirm further the associations between intakes of legumes and soy food and their subtypes on hypertension risk. Further studies are also needed across geographic regions and to explore the associations across strata of bodyweight and other risk factors.

Conclusion

Our meta-analysis provides further evidence that high legume and soy consumption is associated with an overall lower risk of hypertension. We observed a linear inverse association up to an intake of ~170 g/day of legumes and 60–80 g/day for soy foods. Although further large-scale cohorts are needed for confirmation, these findings provide further evidence in support of dietary recommendations to the public to prioritise and integrate legumes and soy foods as healthy protein sources in the diet.
