Spline Interpolation on Compact Riemannian Manifolds
Charlie Sire, Mike Pereira, Thomas Romary

Abstract
Spline interpolation is a widely used class of methods for solving interpolation problems by constructing smooth interpolants that minimize a regularized energy functional involving the Laplacian operator. While many existing approaches focus on Euclidean domains or the sphere, relying on the spectral properties of the Laplacian, this work introduces a method for spline interpolation on general manifolds by exploiting its equivalence with kriging. Specifically, the proposed approach uses finite element approximations of random fields defined over the manifold, based on Gaussian Markov Random Fields and a discretization of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on a triangulated mesh. This framework enables the modeling of spatial fields with local anisotropies through domain deformation. The method is first validated on the sphere using both analytical test cases and a pollution-related study, and is compared to the classical spherical harmonics-based method. Additional experiments on the surface of a cylinder further illustrate the generality of the approach.

Introduction

In the context of spatially distributed datasets, interpolation methods are especially relevant, as they aim to construct a function defined over the entire domain that exactly matches the values at the observation points. More precisely, let us consider
n
observations of a phenonemon, denoted by
\left(y_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, measured at points
\mathcal{S}=\left(\mathbf{s}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}
in a spatial domain
\mathcal{X}.
The objective is to find a function
u:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, such that
u(\mathbf{s}_{i})=y_{i},~1\leq i\leq n.

Kriging is a widely used interpolation method for this type of problem. Originating from geostatistics, it is based on the Best Linear Unbiased Predictor (BLUP) framework, providing not only point predictions but also estimates of uncertainty through a covariance structure that encodes spatial dependence. Spline interpolation represents another widely used class of methods for tackling interpolation problems. Among its most general formulations is the thin-plate splines (TPS,
Wahba and Wang 1990
), based on variational principles. This method constructs a smooth interpolant by minimizing a regularized energy functional that incorporates the Laplacian operator
-\Delta
of the function, thereby penalizing excessive roughness. The approach has a compelling physical interpretation, as it models the bending behavior of a thin elastic plate fixed at specific data points. It involves solving a minimization problem under the interpolation constraints. The TPS (referred to simply as splines in the following) will be the main focus of this manuscript, as their associated minimization problem can be easily formulated within various types of spaces
\mathcal{X}
. Moreover, this spline interpolation has been shown to be equivalent to kriging when using a particular covariance kernel and prior trend function that are related to the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Laplacian operator
-\Delta
. This equivalence was first established in the pioneering work of Matheron
Matheron 1981
, and later applied by Olivier Dubrule for the cases
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{2}

Dubrule 1984
;
Dubrule 1983
, as well as in several subsequent studies in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Myers 1988
;
Myers 1992
.

However, many real-world datasets are naturally distributed over non-Euclidean domains, such as surfaces or manifolds, with numerous examples found in environmental sciences
Menafoglio et al. 2021
;
Díaz and del
Castillo 1999
;
Menafoglio
et al. 2022
and geosciences
Menafoglio 2015
. One of the most representative cases of surface-based data observation involves measurements taken on the Earth’s surface, which is inherently spherical. In such cases, relying on Euclidean distance to model correlations between points is no longer appropriate, since standard covariance models may become ill-defined. As a result, extensive research has compared spline interpolation and kriging on the sphere, beginning with the foundational work of Wahba
Wahba 1981
, and subsequently expanded by numerous studies in practical situations
Keller and Borkowski 2019
;
Dunitz 2023
;
Bonabifard et al. 2022
. But these works specifically exploit the geometric properties of the
sphere, by expanding the random fields into bases of deterministic functions known as spherical harmonics, and then do not straightforwardly extend to general, arbitrary surfaces. The theory of Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS) is then needed to obtain the equivalence between splines and kriging interpolation to general Riemannian manifolds, as formally established in
Kim 2001
. The focus of this manuscript is therefore the study of Gaussian random fields defined on general compact Riemannian manifolds, equipped with suitable covariance functions, to develop a spline interpolation framework for data distributed over surfaces.

The framework introduced by Lindgren
Lindgren et al.
and later extended by Pereira
Pereira 2019
forms the foundation of our study, focusing on Gaussian random fields defined on Riemannian manifolds, denoted here by
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{M}
. His approach relies on finite element approximations of random fields over
\mathcal{M}
, introducing Gaussian Markov Random Fields (GMRFs,
Rue and Held 2005
), that are defined on a mesh of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. In our context, this framework enables the discretization of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the triangulated domain, leading to a finite set of identifiable eigenvalues
Lang and Pereira 2023
, as will be detailed in this paper.

A possible application of our work is to support the modeling of functions with local anisotropies, as presented in
Pereira 2019
, through the deformation of the spatial domain. The key idea is that a non-stationary field defined on a domain can be rendered stationary by applying a suitable deformation
Sampson and
Guttorp 1992
. Identifying an appropriate deformation thus allows the problem to be recast in a stationary framework on the transformed domain. Then, although classical spline interpolation inherently assumes stationarity, domain deformation provides a way to construct splines that account for local anisotropies and work with non-stationary covariance functions.

The contribution of this work is to propose a method for solving the spline interpolation problem on compact surfaces beyond the spherical case, extending it to connected orientable Riemannian manifolds. Within this Riemannian framework, splines with local anisotropies can be effectively modeled, and their finite-element approximation results in faster computation times compared to classical spline predictions.

The outline of this paper is as follows. Section
2
introduces the theoretical formulation of the spline interpolation problem on a Riemannian manifold and highlights its connection with kriging. Section
3
presents the main contribution, our finite-element approximation of the spline predictors, while Section
4
provides the theoretical foundations leading to this formulation. Section
5
describes a method for incorporating local anisotropies. Section
6
reports numerical results on two analytical test cases, defined on the sphere and the cylinder, as well as on a global pollution study. Finally, Section
7
concludes the paper with a summary and outlines potential directions for future work.

2
Theory of splines on manifolds

2.1
RKHS on a compact manifold

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a smooth compact manifold of dimension
d
, equipped with a Riemannian metric
g
, and let
-\Delta
denote the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
(\mathcal{M},g)
. The integral of a function
f
over
\mathcal{M}
is denoted by
\int_{\mathcal{M}}fd\mu_{g}
, where
d\mu_{g}
is the canonical measure associated to
(\mathcal{M},g)
and
\partial\mathcal{M}
denotes its boundary. The following assertions are valid for the closed and the Neumann eigenvalue problems. The Spectral Theorem provides that
Craioveanu et al. 2001
:

•

The spectrum of
-\Delta
is an infinite and countable sequence of real values

0\leq\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{k}\leq..

in which each eigenvalue appears as many times as its multiplicity.

•

The eigenvalues
\left(\lambda_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
have finite multiplicity
m_{k}
, and the associated eigenspaces
\left(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{k}}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are orthogonal vector spaces of
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
.

•

There exists
(\phi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
such that
\forall k\in\mathbb{N},\phi_{k}
is an eigenfunction associated to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}.
Then,
\forall u\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M}),u=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}u_{k}\phi_{k}
with

u_{k}=\langle\phi_{k},u\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}u\bar{\phi}_{k}d\mu_{g}.

As
\mathcal{M}
is compact and connected, the eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}=0
corresponds to the constant functions
Urakawa 1993
. Then, we have
m_{0}=1,
and
\phi_{0}=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathcal{M}}d\mu_{g}}.
Note that with Dirichlet boundary conditions, the above properties from the Spectral Theorem still hold, but
\lambda_{0}>0
, which largely simplifies the challenges addressed in this study.

Let us now introduce the following subspaces of
L^{2}(\mathcal{M}).

V_{0}=\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{0}},~~~V_{1}=\underset{k\geq 1}{\bigoplus}~\mathcal{E}_{\lambda_{k}},~~~V=V_{0}\oplus V_{1}=L^{2}(\mathcal{M}),

(1)

with associated inner products

\langle u_{0},v_{0}\rangle_{0}=u_{0}\bar{v}_{0},~~~\langle u_{1},v_{1}\rangle_{1}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}^{2}u_{k}\bar{v}_{k},~~~\langle u,v\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle_{0}+\langle u,v\rangle_{1}

(2)

where
u_{0},v_{0}\in V_{0},u_{1},v_{1}\in V_{1}
and
u=u_{0}+u_{1},v=v_{0}+v_{1}\in V.
It can be shown that
V_{0},V_{1}
, and
V
are RKHS with respective reproducing kernels
Kim 2001

\displaystyle K_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{1})\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{2})

(3)

\displaystyle K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{1})\phi_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{2})

(4)

\displaystyle K(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})

\displaystyle=K_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})+K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2}).

(5)

These eigenfunctions
\left(\phi_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
play a key role in defining the solution to the spline interpolation problem, detailed in the next section.

2.2
Spline interpolation on a compact manifold

Let us consider
n
observations denoted by
\mathbf{y}=\left(y_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, at points
\mathcal{S}=\left(\mathbf{s}_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}
with
\mathbf{s}_{i}\in\mathcal{M},1\leq i\leq n.

The spline interpolation problem on
\mathcal{M}
is to find
u\in V
minimizing
Keller and Borkowski 2019
;
Duchamp and
Stuetzle 2003

E(u)\vcentcolon=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\lvert\Delta u\rvert^{2}d\mu_{g}=\lVert u\rVert_{1}^{2}

(6)

subject to the constraint

u(\mathbf{s}_{i})=y_{i},1\leq i\leq n.

Here,
\lVert u\rVert_{1}^{2}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda_{k}^{2}\lvert u_{k}\rvert^{2}.
Note that
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}
is the norm in
V_{1}
associated with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{1}
, but is only a semi-norm in
V
as
\forall u_{0}\in V_{0},\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{1}=0.
The solution to this spline interpolation problem is given by Theorem
1
.

Theorem 1

(Interpolating splines
Wahba 1990
)
.

If the set of points
\mathcal{S}
is such that the matrix
\mathbf{K}_{1}=\left[K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{j})\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
is positive definite, then the solution
u^{\star}
to the minimization problem is given, for all
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{M}
, by

u^{\star}(\mathbf{s})=a_{0}\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s})+\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{k}_{1}(\mathbf{s}),

(7)

where
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{b}=(b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathbf{k}_{1}(\mathbf{s})=\left(K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}),\dots,K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{n},\mathbf{s})\right)^{\top}.

The coefficients
a_{0}
and
\mathbf{b}
satisfy the system

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{with }\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}}=\left(\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{1}),\dots,\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{n})\right)^{\top}

(8)

\displaystyle a_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}}+\mathbf{K}_{1}\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y},\quad\text{with }\mathbf{K}_{1}=\left[K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{j})\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

(9)

The solution
u^{\star}
given by the theorem can be made explicit, since it is straightforward to verify that Equations (
8
) and (
9
) are equivalent to:

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=\left((\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}}\right)^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{y}

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{1}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-a_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}}\right).

(11)

It leads to

u^{\star}_{0}(\mathbf{s})=a_{0}\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s})+\mathbf{k}_{1}(\mathbf{s})^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{1}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-a_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}}\right)

(12)

with
a_{0}
given in equation (
10
). As shown in Appendix
8
,
\mathbf{K}_{1}
is positive definite if and only if
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{w}_{k}\}_{k>0}=\mathbb{R}^{n},
where
\mathbf{w}_{k}=\left(\phi_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{1}),\dots,\phi_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{n})\right)^{\top}.

This estimator is known as universal kriging
Armstrong 1984
, and it also arises as the posterior mean of a Gaussian process regression model
Williams and
Rasmussen 2006
:

u^{\star}_{0}(\mathbf{s})=\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{0}(\mathbf{s})\mid Y_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{1})=\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,Y_{0}(\mathbf{s}_{n})=\mathbf{y}_{n}\right),

(13)

where
Y_{0}
is a Gaussian process of a priori mean
a_{0}\phi_{0}
and covariance kernel
K_{1}.

2.3
Smoothing splines on a compact manifold

The interpolation constraint may become irrelevant when the observed data are noisy. In such cases, the condition can be relaxed, leading to the smoothing splines problem, which seeks to find
u\in V
minimizing

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-u(\mathbf{s}_{i})\right)^{2}+\tau^{2}\lVert u\rVert_{1}^{2}.

(14)

Theorem 2

(Smoothing splines
Wahba 1990
)
.

The optimal smoothing splines are defined as

u^{\star}_{\tau}(\mathbf{s})=a_{\tau}\phi_{0}(\mathbf{s})+\mathbf{k}_{1}(\mathbf{s})^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}_{1}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-a_{\tau}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}}\right),

(15)

with

a_{\tau}=\left((\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}})^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}_{1}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}}\right)^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\textnormal{obs}})^{\top}\left(\mathbf{K}_{1}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{y}.

(16)

This solution can again be seen as the posterior mean of a Gaussian process regression model:

u^{\star}_{\tau}(\mathbf{s})=\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{\tau}(\mathbf{s})\mid Y_{\tau}(\mathbf{s}_{1})+\tau E_{1}=\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,Y_{\tau}(\mathbf{s}_{n})+\tau E_{n}=\mathbf{y}_{n}\right).

(17)

where
Y_{\tau}
denotes a Gaussian process of a priori mean
a_{\tau}\phi_{0}
and covariance kernel
K_{1},
and
E_{1},\dots,E_{n}
are independent standard Gaussian variables.

Working with a Gaussian process defined on
\mathcal{M}
with covariance kernel
K_{1}
is then the main objective of this work. The central challenge lies in the formulation of the kernel
K_{1}
, which depends on the spectral decomposition of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
-\Delta
.
On the sphere, for example, it is well known that the eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
are the spherical harmonics
Dai et al. 2013
, which enables spline interpolation on this specific manifold, as studied in numerous works
Hitczenko and Stein 2012
;
Traas 1987
;
Keller and Borkowski 2019
;
Dunitz 2023
;
Bonabifard et al. 2022
. However, for a general manifold, the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
are not available in closed form. This limitation motivates the present work, which relies on a discrete approximation of the Laplace-Beltrami operator. In the next section, we present the finite-element approximation of the spline predictors.

3
Finite element approximation of spline predictors

Here, we present a finite element approximation of
u^{\star}_{\tau}
, with
\tau=0
corresponding to interpolating splines and
\tau>0
to smoothing splines, following the approach of
Lindgren et al.
. This section focuses on the main results, while all theoretical justifications are provided in Section
4

The manifold
\mathcal{M}
is discretized using
m
nodes
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{m}\in\mathcal{M}
, forming a triangulation denoted by
\mathrm{T}
. A family of compactly supported basis functions
\left(\psi_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{m}
is then defined over
\mathcal{M}
. Each function
\psi_{j}
is piecewise linear with respect to the triangulation

, taking the value
1
at node
\mathbf{c}_{j}
and
0
at all other nodes, corresponding to the first-order finite element method. Along with these basis functions, we classically have

•

The
m\times m
mass matrix
\mathbf{M}
with
\left[\mathbf{M}\right]_{ij}=\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}
,

•

The
m\times m

\mathbf{F}
with
\left[\mathbf{F}\right]_{ij}=\langle\nabla\psi_{i},\nabla\psi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}

The

\mathbf{A}_{n}
, with
\left[\mathbf{A}_{n}\right]_{i,j}=\psi_{j}(\mathbf{s}_{i}).

It can be shown that
\mathbf{M}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix and
\mathbf{F}
a symmetric positive semi-definite
matrix
Pereira 2019

\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a matrix such that
\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}=\mathbf{M}

Benoit 1924

\mathbf{S}
by

\mathbf{S}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{F}\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-\top}.

(18)

It is important to note that the choice of the basis functions
\left(\psi_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{m}
leads to sparse matrices
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{F}
, which facilitates computations when
m

\mathbf{M}
is replaced by its mass lumping approximation

\left[\mathbf{M}\right]_{jj}=\langle\psi_{j},1\rangle

\mathbf{M}
in the remainder of the text. This choice facilitates straightforward access to the inverse of
\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}

\mathbf{S}

Finally, for any
\alpha>0,
we introduce the matrix

\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}=\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{S}^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top},

where

(see Appendix
10

\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{S}^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}

Using these matrices, Theorem

Theorem 3

(Finite element approximation of spline predictors.)
.

For any

\left(\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\right)\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{x}

6:

return

\mathbf{u}_{\tau,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol{\omega}

All the theoretical justifications for these results are provided in the following section.

4
Theory of finite element splines on manifolds

As shown in Equation (
17
), the spline predictor can be interpreted as the posterior mean of the Gaussian process
Y_{\tau}
, with prior mean
a_{\tau}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
and covariance kernel
K_{1}
. We have
\tau=0
for the interpolation problem, and
\tau>0
for the smoothing problem. However, since
K_{1}
depends on the spectral decomposition of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
-\Delta
, a finite element formulation is proposed here.

First, let us decompose
Y_{\tau}
as

Y_{\tau}=a_{\tau}\phi_{0}+Z,

(21)

with
Z
a centered Gaussian process (GP) defined on
\mathcal{M}
with covariance kernel
K_{1}
defined in equation (
4
),
a_{\tau}
defined in equation (
16
), and
\phi_{0}
the eigenfunction of
-\Delta
associated with
\lambda_{0}=0.

4.1
Discretization of Z

The kernel
K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\phi_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{1})\phi_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{2})
can be written
K_{1}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}f(\lambda_{k})\phi_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{1})\phi_{k}(\mathbf{s}_{2})
for
(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathcal{M}^{2}
, with

f(\lambda)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\lambda=0\\
\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}},&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Thus, the work of
Pereira
et al. 2022
can be adapted to define
Z
through the expansion

Z(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}f^{1/2}(\lambda_{k})W_{k}\phi_{k}

(22)

with
(W_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of independent standard Gaussian variables and
f^{1/2}
such that
(f^{1/2})^{2}=f.
Using finite elements, the random fields defined on
\mathcal{M}
and specifically the field
Z,
are then approximated as

Z^{(m)}(\mathbf{s})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}Z_{j}\psi_{j}(\mathbf{s})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{s})^{\top}\mathbf{Z},

where
Z_{j}=Z^{(m)}(\mathbf{c}_{j})
for
1\leq j\leq m
,
\mathbf{Z}=\left(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}\right)^{\top}
, and
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{s})=\left(\psi_{1}(\mathbf{s}),\dots,\psi_{m}(\mathbf{s})\right)^{\top}
.

The objective here is to replace the expansion of
Z
given in equation (
22
) with an analogous expansion for
Z^{(m)},
based on the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}^{(m)}
and eigenvectors
\phi_{k}^{(m)}
of
-\Delta_{m}
, the Galerkin approximation of
-\Delta
on the triangulation
\mathrm{T}
. The operator
-\Delta_{m}
is defined explicitly in Appendix
9
. Then we want to define accordingly the weights
\mathbf{Z}=\left(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}\right)^{\top}
such that

Z^{(m)}(\mathbf{s})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{s})^{\top}\mathbf{Z}=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}f^{1/2}(\lambda_{k}^{(m)})W_{k}\phi_{k}^{(m)}.

(23)

As shown in
Lang and Pereira 2023
, the eigenvalues of
-\Delta_{m}
are those of the matrix
\mathbf{S}
defined in Equation (
18
). As
\mathbf{S}
is real, symmetric and positive semi-definite, the matrix is diagonalizable and can be written

\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{V}\text{Diag}\left(\lambda_{0}^{(m)},\dots,\lambda_{m-1}^{(m)}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\top}

(24)

where
\mathbf{V}
is an orthogonal matrix whose columns are the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{S}
. The work of
Lang and Pereira 2023
also provides the following important result.

Proposition 4.1
.

The weight vector
\mathbf{Z}=\left(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m}\right)^{\top}
satisfying Equation (
23
) is a centered Gaussian vector with covariance matrix

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-\top}f(\mathbf{S})\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-1},

(25)

where
f(\mathbf{S})
is the matrix function defined from the eigendecomposition of
\mathbf{S}
as

f(\mathbf{S})=\mathbf{V}\text{Diag}\left(f(\lambda_{0}^{(m)}),\dots,f(\lambda_{m-1}^{(m)})\right)\mathbf{V}^{\top}.

Then, the associated random field
Z^{(m)}(\mathbf{s})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{s})^{\top}\mathbf{Z}
is a centered Gaussian process with covariance kernel

K_{1}^{(m)}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}f(\lambda_{k}^{(m)})\phi_{k}^{(m)}(\mathbf{s}_{1})\phi_{k}^{(m)}(\mathbf{s}_{2})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{s}_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{s}_{2}),~~\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}.

(26)

From the covariance kernel of
Z^{(m)}
, the classical kriging formulas can be applied to study the discretization of
Y,
as presented in the next section.

4.2
Interpolation formulas

We investigate

Y_{\tau}^{(m)}=a_{\tau}^{(m)}\phi_{0}^{(m)}+Z^{(m)},

(27)

the finite element discretization of the target Gaussian process
Y
introduced in Equation (
21
), with weights

\mathbf{Y}_{\tau}=\left(Y_{\tau}^{(m)}(\mathbf{c}_{j})\right)_{j=1}^{m}=a_{\tau}^{(m)}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}+\mathbf{Z},

(28)

where

a_{\tau}^{(m)}=\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\left[\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}+\tau\mathbf{I}_{n}\right]^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\left[\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}+\tau\mathbf{I}_{n}\right]^{-1}\mathbf{y}.

(29)

The expression of
a_{\tau}^{(m)}
is based on equation (
10
), using the fact that
\left(\phi_{0}^{(m)}(\mathbf{s}_{1}),\dots,\phi_{0}^{(m)}(\mathbf{s}_{n})\right)^{\top}=\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
and
\left[K_{1}^{(m)}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{j})\right]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}=\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}.

The objective is to investigate the finite element approximation of both interpolating and smoothing splines, which are defined as posterior expectations in Equations
13
and
17
, respectively. More precisely, the approximated solution at the triangulation nodes are

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\star}_{\tau}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{\tau}\mid Y_{\tau}^{(m)}(\mathbf{s}_{1})+\tau E_{1}=y_{1},\dots,Y_{\tau}^{(m)}(\mathbf{s}_{n})+\tau E_{n}=y_{n}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{\tau}\mid\mathbf{A}_{n}\mathbf{Y}_{\tau}+\tau\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{y}\right)

with
\mathbf{E}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})^{\top}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{n}),
and
\tau=0
for the interpolating splines. This leads to Proposition
4.2
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The finite-element approximation of the spline interpolation problem (for
\tau=0
) and of the smoothing splines problem (for
\tau>0
) is given by

\mathbf{u}^{\star}_{\tau}=a_{\tau}^{(m)}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{T}\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathbf{A}_{n})^{T}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}\bigl(\mathbf{y}-a_{\tau}^{(m)}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\bigr).

(31)

The inversibility of
\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathbf{A}_{n})^{T}
and
\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathbf{A}_{n})^{T}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}
are studied for the first and the second scenario in Appendix
11
. Nevertheless, the kriging formula of equation (
31
) is not straightforward to exploit in practice. This issue will be addressed in the following section.

4.3
Intrinsic GMRF

The main challenge here arises from the fact that the size
m
of the triangulation must be very large to ensure accurate approximations. In such cases, computing the eigendecomposition of the matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
becomes impractical, rendering both
f(\mathbf{S})
and the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
intractable. To address this issue, a common approach is to work with the precision matrix
\mathbf{Q}
, that is the inverse of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. In particular, when the function
f
is the inverse of a polynomial
P
that remains positive on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the precision matrix admits a convenient expression:
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)P(\mathbf{S})\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{\top},
which is sparse when using mass lumping. Using this formulation, the approximation
\mathbf{Y}_{\tau}
defines a GMRF
Rue and Held 2005
, which enables computational simplifications.

However, in our situation, as explained in Appendix
11.1
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
has rank
m-1
and is not invertible, rendering
\mathbf{Y}_{\tau}
an intrinsic GMRF
Simpson et al. 2006
. The idea is to introduce a modified GMRF that has an invertible covariance matrix, inspired by the Bayesian kriging framework
Diggle and
Ribeiro Jr 2002
, in which the intercept term
a_{0}^{(m)}
is modeled as a random variable
A
with a Gaussian prior distribution. More precisely, we denote

\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}=A(\alpha)\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}+\mathbf{Z}

(32)

where
A(\alpha)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\alpha)
independent from
\mathbf{Z}.

Proposition 4.3
.

\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}=A(\alpha)\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}+\mathbf{Z}
has

•

Non-singular covariance matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}+\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}

•

Precision matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}=\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{S}^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}

The proof is provided in Appendix
12
. As previously highlighted, the matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
is particularly useful in this context, as it consists of two components that facilitate efficient Cholesky decompositions (see Sections
4.4
and
4.5
):

•

The term
\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{S}^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}
is sparse thanks to the mass-lumping approximation.

•

The term
\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}
is a rank-one update involving the known vector
\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
.

Then, as stated in Theorem
3
, using the posterior expectation

\mathbf{u}_{\tau,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\vcentcolon=\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}\mid\mathbf{A}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}+\tau\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{x}\right)=\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{x},

(33)

for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\tau\geq 0
and
\alpha>0,
provides the tractable expression of Equation (
20
) for our finite element approximation
\mathbf{u}^{\star}_{\tau}:

\mathbf{u}^{\star}_{\tau}=\mathbf{u}_{\tau,\alpha}(\mathbf{y})+\Bigg(\frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}-\mathbf{h}\!\left[\mathbf{u}_{\tau,\alpha}\!\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\right]}{\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{\tau,\alpha}\!\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}+\mathbf{h}\!\left[\mathbf{u}_{\tau,\alpha}\!\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\right]\Bigg)\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{\tau,\alpha}(\mathbf{y}).

The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
13
. The posterior expectation
\mathbf{u}_{\tau,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
is tractable, as explained in Section
4.4
for the first scenario (
\tau=0
) and in Section
4.5
for the second scenario (
\tau>0
). It can then be evaluated for
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
, after which only simple matrix multiplications remain.

4.4
First scenario: computation of
\mathbf{u}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

Here we detail the computation of
\mathbf{u}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
, for the
first scenario
where the observation points are included among the triangulation nodes. The procedure is detailed in Algorithm
1
. This computation is particularly relevant when
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
or
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
in equation (
20
). As introduced in Section
3
, let
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\}

denote the set of indices such that
s_{k}=c_{i_{k}}
for
1\leq k\leq n
.
We define its complement as
\bar{I}=\{1,\dots,m\}\setminus I.

Here,

\mathbf{u}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}\mid\big[\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}\big]_{I}=\mathbf{x}\right),

and the vector
\begin{bmatrix}\big[\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}\big]_{\bar{I}}\\
\big[\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}\big]_{I}\end{bmatrix}

has precision matrix

\begin{bmatrix}\big[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\big]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}&\big[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\big]_{\bar{I},I}\\
\big[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\big]_{I,\bar{I}}&\big[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\big]_{I,I}\end{bmatrix}.

Then, using the classical kriging formula involving the precision matrix, we have

\begin{cases}\big[\mathbf{u}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\big]_{\bar{I}}&=\big[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\big]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}^{-1}\big[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\big]_{\bar{I},I}\mathbf{x}\\
\big[\mathbf{u}_{0,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\big]_{I}&=\mathbf{x}.\end{cases}

The vector
\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}^{-1}\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{\bar{I},I}\mathbf{x}
can be obtained via the Cholesky decomposition of
\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}
, followed by solving a sparse linear system. More precisely, the Cholesky factorization of
\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}=\left[\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{S}^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right]_{\bar{I}}\left[\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right]_{\bar{I}}^{\top}
exploits the sparsity of
\left[\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{S}^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}
and a rank-one update. Additional details about the sparse linear systems are provided in Appendix
14
.

4.5
Second scenario : computation of
\mathbf{u}_{\tau,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

This section focuses on the computation of
\mathbf{u}_{\tau,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}\mid\mathbf{A}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}+\tau\mathbf{E}=\mathbf{x}\right)
with
\tau>0.

Again, this is required when
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
or
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
in equation (
20
). Algorithm
2
summarizes the corresponding steps.

We have
\mathbf{u}_{\tau,\alpha}(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{x},
with
\mathbf{A}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}
invertible as
\mathbf{A}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}
is positive semidefinite:

\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\geq 0.

From Woodbury formula and as detailed in Appendix
15
, it can be shown that

\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{x}=\left(\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{x}.

(34)

Since
\left[\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\right]_{ij}=0
whenever
\psi_{i}(\mathbf{s}_{k})\psi_{j}(\mathbf{s}_{k})=0
for all
1\leq k\leq n
, the matrix
\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}
is sparse. Then, the Cholesky decomposition of

\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}=\tau^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{S}^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}

can be computed by first exploiting the sparsity of
\tau^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{S}^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}
, and then applying a rank-one update. Once the Cholesky factor is available, solving sparse linear systems yields

\left(\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{x}.

4.6
Discussion on
\alpha

Theoretically, all the presented results hold for any
\alpha>0.

In practice, however, the choice of
\alpha
is crucial to avoid numerical instabilities.
If
\alpha
is too close to
0,
computing the inverse of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
may become numerically infeasible.
Conversely, as
\alpha\to\infty,
we obtain
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\approx\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{S}^{2}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top},

which is not invertible. More precise arguments can be provided to rigorously confirm these intuitions.

First Scenario.

Under the mass lumping approximation, the Cholesky decomposition of

\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}=\left[\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\Big(\mathbf{S}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})^{\top}\Big)\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}

can be obtained with the Cholesky decomposition of

\left[\mathbf{S}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})^{\top}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}.

Since
\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\in\ker(\mathbf{S})
(see Appendix
11
), the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}^{2}
and

\tilde{\mathbf{S}}^{2}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{S}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})^{\top}

are identical, except that the zero eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{(m)}=0
is replaced by
\tfrac{1}{\alpha}.

To mitigate numerical issues, it is essential to keep the condition number of
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}^{2}_{\alpha}
as small as possible.
This condition number is defined as

\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2})=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2})}{\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2})}.

where
\lambda_{\max}(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2})
and
\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2})

denote the maximum and minimum eigenvalues of
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2}
, respectively. Hence, the desirable choice is to enforce

\frac{1}{\alpha}\in\big[\lambda_{1}^{(m)},\,\lambda_{m-1}^{(m)}\big],

where
\lambda_{0}^{(m)}\leq\lambda_{1}^{(m)}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{m-1}^{(m)}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}.

The largest eigenvalue of a matrix can, for instance, be computed using the power iteration method
Mises and
Pollaczek-Geiringer 1929
,
which only requires performing matrix–vector products with this matrix. Since
\lambda_{1}^{(m)}
is the largest eigenvalue of
\left(\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{I}_{m}\right)^{-1}-\bigl(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\bigr)\bigl(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\bigr)^{\top},

both
\lambda_{1}^{(m)}
and
\lambda_{m-1}^{(m)}
can be computed using the power-iteration method.

Second scenario.

Analogously, in the second scenario, the Cholesky decomposition of

\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}

can be derived from the Cholesky decomposition of

\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2}+\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-\top}
.
We also have the following bounds on the eigenvalues
Weyl 1912
:

\begin{cases}\lambda_{\max}\!\Big(\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2}+\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-\top}\Big)\leq\lambda_{\max}\!\Big(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-\top}\Big)+\lambda_{\max}(\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2}),\\[10.00002pt]
\lambda_{\min}\!\Big(\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2}+\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-\top}\Big)\geq\lambda_{\min}\!\Big(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-\top}\Big)+\lambda_{\min}(\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2}).\end{cases}

As a consequence, the condition number satisfies

\kappa\Big(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2}+\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-\top}\Big)\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}\!\Big(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-\top}\Big)+\tau^{2}\lambda_{\max}(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2})}{\lambda_{\min}\!\Big(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-\top}\Big)+\tau^{2}\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}_{\alpha}^{2})}.

Choosing
\frac{1}{\alpha}\in[\lambda_{1}^{(m)},\,\lambda_{m-1}^{(m)}]
is thus again a relevant choice in this context.

Introducing
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}
with a carefully chosen
\alpha
provides a tractable formulation of the finite-element solution. Nevertheless, this solution is isotropic, as evidenced by the covariance kernel
K_{1}^{(m)}
(see equation (
26
)). This limitation may lead to unsatisfactory results when the underlying phenomenon exhibits preferred directions, a situation addressed in Section
5
.

5
Anisotropic splines

5.1
Local anisotropy through space deformation

The idea behind constructing solutions with local anisotropies is to introduce a deformation of the manifold
\mathcal{M}
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, through its Riemannian metric
g
, as presented in
Pereira 2019
. More precisely, for any
\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{M}
, we consider a coordinate chart
(U,x)
containing
\mathbf{s}
, where
U
is an open subset of
\mathcal{M}
and
x:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a homeomorphism mapping
U
to an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The metric is then defined as

g_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{s}},\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{s}})=\left(\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{s}}^{x}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{G}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\,\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{s}}^{x},

(35)

with
\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{s}}^{x}
and
\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{s}}^{x}
the representative vectors of
\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{s}}
and
\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{s}}
of the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{s}}\mathcal{M}
at
\mathbf{s}
, with respect to the chart
(U,x).
The matrix
\mathbf{G}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
is the representative of the Riemannian metric at
\mathbf{s}
in these coordinates and is symmetric positive definite. It can be interpreted as a deformation matrix, and admits the following diagonalization:

\mathbf{G}^{x}(\mathbf{s})=\mathbf{R}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\,\mathbf{D}^{x}(\mathbf{s})^{2}\,\left(\mathbf{R}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\right)^{\top},

where
\mathbf{R}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
is an orthogonal matrix and
\mathbf{D}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
is diagonal with positive entries. With this Riemannian metric,
Y^{(m)}_{\tau}
becomes locally isotropic after applying the linear change of variables
\mathbf{h}\mapsto\mathbf{D}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\left(\mathbf{R}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{h}
, where
\mathbf{h}
denotes an infinitesimal displacement vector around
\mathbf{s}
.

For
d\in\{2,3\}
, the matrix
\mathbf{G}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
admits a natural geometric interpretation. The diagonal entries of
\mathbf{D}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
can be interpreted as local scaling factors
\rho_{1}^{x}(\mathbf{s}),\dots,\rho_{d}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
along the principal directions defined by the chart
(U,x)
, while
\mathbf{R}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
represents a rotation matrix parameterized by angles
\delta_{j}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
, denoted by

\mathbf{R}_{\delta^{x}(\mathbf{s})}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta^{x}(\mathbf{s})&-\sin\delta^{x}(\mathbf{s})\\
\sin\delta^{x}(\mathbf{s})&\cos\delta^{x}(\mathbf{s})\end{pmatrix}

when
d=2,
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\delta_{1}^{x}(\mathbf{s}),\,\delta_{2}^{x}(\mathbf{s}),\,\delta_{3}^{x}(\mathbf{s})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta_{3}^{x}(\mathbf{s})&-\sin\delta_{3}^{x}(\mathbf{s})&0\\
\sin\delta_{3}^{x}(\mathbf{s})&\cos\delta_{3}^{x}(\mathbf{s})&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta_{2}^{x}(\mathbf{s})&0&-\sin\delta_{2}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\\
0&1&0\\
\sin\delta_{2}^{x}(\mathbf{s})&0&\cos\delta_{2}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\cos\delta_{1}^{x}(\mathbf{s})&-\sin\delta_{1}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\\
0&\sin\delta_{1}^{x}(\mathbf{s})&\cos\delta_{1}^{x}(\mathbf{s})\end{pmatrix}

when
d=3.

This spatial deformation, represented through the Riemannian metric, affects the computation of the integral over
\mathcal{M}
, and consequently influences the mass and stiffness matrices,
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{F}
and finally the Bayesian precision matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
(see Proposition
4.3
). Further details are provided in the Supplementary Material
Sire et al. 2025
for the specific cases of the cylindrical and spherical surfaces, which are 2-dimensional Riemannian manifolds embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. These cases allow for the introduction of rotations and scaling within the local charts defined by the 2D cylindrical coordinates
(\theta,z)
or spherical coordinates
(\theta,\varphi)
, to obtain interpretable deformations. To estimate the optimal hyperparameters for the matrix
\mathbf{G}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
, the idea here is to use maximum likelihood estimation.

5.2
Maximum likelihood estimation

In practice, a manageable number of parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}
must be specified in order to define the anisotropy fields. For example,
Fuglstad et al. 2015
proposed representing local anisotropies as a vector field, obtained as the gradient of a real-valued sinusoidal function. In simpler settings, the hyperparameters of
\mathbf{G}^{x}(\mathbf{s})
, namely the rotation angles and scaling factors, are assumed to be constant across the manifold
\mathcal{M}.
In that case, all hyperparameters to be estimated can be collected into the vectors

•

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\left(\delta,\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\right)
if
d=2

•

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\left(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3},\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3}\right)
if
d=3
.

These hyperparameters influence
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}
,
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
, and
a_{\tau}^{(m)}
in the following, but this dependency is not explicitly indicated for simplicity.

The objective is to optimize the likelihood of the observations based on our statistical model.
Recall that our finite-element solution is the conditional expectation of the field

Y_{\tau}^{(m)}=\mathbf{A}(s)^{\top}\mathbf{Y}_{\tau}
, with
\mathbf{Y}_{\tau}
defined in
equation (
28
).
Then, the Gaussian field at the observation points

\bigl[Y_{\tau}^{(m)}(\mathbf{s}_{1}),\dots,Y_{\tau}^{(m)}(\mathbf{s}_{n})\bigr]^{\top}=\mathbf{A}_{n}\mathbf{Y}_{\tau}

is Gaussian with mean
a_{\tau}^{(m)}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
and covariance matrix

\mathbf{K}_{\tau}=\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}
,
where
\tau=0
in the
first scenario
and
\tau>0
in the
second scenario
.

The log-likelihood is then

\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=-\frac{1}{2}\left(n\log(2\pi)+\log\lvert\mathbf{K}_{\tau}\rvert+\left(\mathbf{y}-a_{\tau}^{(m)}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{\tau}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-a_{\tau}^{(m)}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\right),

(36)

The computation of the likelihood is split into two terms, the quadratic form
\ell^{(1)}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
and the log-determinant
\ell^{(2)}_{\tau}
:

•

\ell^{(1)}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\left(\mathbf{y}-a_{\tau}^{(m)}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-a_{\tau}^{(m)}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)

•

\ell^{(2)}_{\tau}=\log\lvert\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}\rvert,

with details provided in Appendix
16
. Note that the modified Gaussian vector
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}
does not appear in our statistical model.
It was introduced to enable the computation of the conditional expectation of
Y_{\tau}^{(m)}
,
and will again be useful here for the computation of the likelihood.

An optimization algorithm is then required to maximize the objective function
\boldsymbol{\beta}\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
. In this work, we use the CMA-ES algorithm
Hansen 2006
to estimate the optimal hyperparameters, a robust gradient-free optimization algorithm designed to efficiently explore the search space and escape local minima. In the second scenario, the observation noise can either be fixed a priori or estimated by optimizing
(\tau,\boldsymbol{\beta})\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\beta}).

6
Application

The method is evaluated on two different compact manifolds. It is worth noting that the geometry of the manifold poses no inherent limitation, provided that a triangulation is available. The first test case is the sphere, which is particularly relevant since it allows comparison with classical approaches based on spherical harmonics. The second test case is the surface of a cylinder, which is useful in many contexts, such as studies involving tubes
AIBA et al. 1981
;
Macher et al. 2013
. For both the sphere and the cylinder, we investigate analytical problems. For the sphere, we also consider real-world data through the analysis of carbon dioxide fluxes.

6.1
Splines on the sphere

As previously mentioned, on the sphere, a basis
\left(\phi_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
can be identified with the spherical harmonics
Dai et al. 2013
. Following
Keller and Borkowski 2019
, working with splines on the sphere is equivalent to considering a Gaussian field with covariance kernel

C(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{2k+1}{k^{2}(k+1)^{2}}\,P_{k}\!\left(\cos\big(\xi(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})\big)\right),

(37)

where
P_{k}
is the Legendre polynomial of degree
k
, and
\xi(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})
denotes the spherical angle between
\mathbf{s}_{1}
and
\mathbf{s}_{2}
.

To obtain a more tractable expression for this kernel, one may consider the truncated expansion

C_{K}(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{2k+1}{k^{2}(k+1)^{2}}\,P_{k}\!\left(\cos\big(\xi(\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{s}_{2})\big)\right).

(38)

The truncated kernel
C_{K}
can then be directly incorporated into the classical kriging formula. In this work, we set
K=40
, following the recommendations of
Keller and Borkowski 2019
.

7
Summary and perspectives

This article introduces a novel approach for constructing interpolating or smoothing splines on a general compact, connected and orientable manifold
\mathcal{M}
. The proposed prediction method yields smooth estimates within the domain, since it arises from a minimization problem (under constraints in the case of interpolating splines) involving an energy functional closely related to the Laplace–Beltrami operator
-\Delta
on
\mathcal{M}
. Although the analytical solution has been well established since the pioneering work of Wahba
Wahba 1990
, it is not computationally tractable in practice, as it requires access to the spectrum of
-\Delta
. In this work, we exploit the equivalence between this solution and the Gaussian process regression problem, which can be addressed using finite-element approximations on a triangulated mesh of
\mathcal{M}
, as thoroughly investigated in
Pereira
et al. 2022
. An important advantage of this approach is its ability to construct solutions with local anisotropies by deforming the spatial domain
\mathcal{M}
, thereby producing non-stationary solution. Nevertheless, classical developments encounter difficulties due to the emergence of a singular covariance matrix in our context. To overcome this issue, we propose an augmented GMRF with uncertainty on the trend. This formulation yields a sparse precision matrix that is easy to compute, facilitating calculations, particularly via sparse linear systems. A relation between the kriging formulas based on the augmented GMRF and the initial spline-based solution is then established.

The method is evaluated on analytical toy problems defined on the sphere and the cylinder, as well as on real-world data on Earth. The case of the sphere is particularly interesting, since the spectrum of the Laplace–Beltrami operator is known, with the spherical harmonics serving as eigenvectors. In this setting, interpolating or smoothing splines can be computed without relying on a finite-element approximation. The results show identical solutions between this approach and our proposed method, which strongly supports the validity of the results. Moreover, the introduction of anisotropies enabled by the finite-element approximation proves highly effective in accurately capturing the behavior of the functions, as highlighted by the prediction errors displayed in Figures
2
,
5
and
7
.

Although the study presented here is comprehensive, several avenues for further development remain. First, while the focus has been on the study of a single prediction, incorporating prediction uncertainty would be highly valuable. This uncertainty arises naturally in the Gaussian process framework, but a relation must still be established between the a posteriori variance of
\tilde{\mathbf{Y}}_{\alpha}
and that of
\mathbf{Y}_{\tau}
, in order to work with an invertible precision matrix. Conditional simulations are another option to explore in order to characterize this uncertainty.

Another possible line of study concerns the solution of sparse linear systems.
In our approach, these systems are solved using Cholesky decompositions,
as described in Appendix
14
.
This choice is particularly relevant since the computation of matrix determinants,
required for the log-likelihood estimation (see Appendix
16
),
becomes straightforward once the Cholesky factorization is available.
Nevertheless, alternative techniques could also be considered for solving the linear systems. For example, the conjugate gradient method
Hestenes et al. 1952

may be more appropriate when the size
m
of the triangulation is too large
to allow for Cholesky decompositions.

Finally, an important advantage of the proposed method is its ability to handle anisotropic splines.
The results are particularly promising when anisotropies do not vary significantly at the local scale,
since only a limited number of parameters need to be estimated.
However, in some situations local anisotropies must be taken into account, for instance when anisotropic directions change abruptly between neighboring regions.
This leads to a very large number of parameters in the optimization procedure.
In our pollution case study on Earth, we addressed this by imposing anisotropic directions at
r
pilot points, and then applying an interpolation strategy to define local anisotropies across the entire domain. While this constitutes an original contribution, it could be further improved by optimizing the placement of the pilot points. These points could either be selected manually, with the help of expert knowledge in certain contexts,
or determined automatically through an optimization procedure targeting the maximum log-likelihood. Other strategies may leverage additional variables to identify the optimal anisotropic directions, for example by incorporating wind data in environmental studies.

Computation of
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}

By definition, the function
\phi_{0}^{(m)}
has unit norm in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
, that is,

\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{m}[\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}]_{k}\psi_{k},\ \sum_{k=1}^{m}[\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}]_{k}\psi_{k}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}=1,

which implies that

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=1.

Since
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
is constant, it follows that

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=\frac{\mathbf{1}_{m}}{\lVert\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{m}\rVert_{2}}.

(39)

11
Inversibility of the covariance matrices

This section investigates the inversibility of
\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathbf{A}_{n})^{T}
and
\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathbf{A}_{n})^{T}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}
for the
first scenario
and the
second scenario
, respectively.

11.1
First scenario

We know that
f(\mathbf{S})
is positive semidefinite of rank
m-1
, since for all
1\leq i\leq m-1
, we have
f(\lambda_{i}^{(m)})>0
, and
f(\lambda_{0}^{(m)})=0
. Therefore, the matrix

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-\top}f(\mathbf{S})\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-1}

is also positive semidefinite of rank
m-1
.

Now let us identify a vector in
\text{Ker}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}).
By definition of the basis function
\left(\psi_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{m},
we have
\forall\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{M},\sum_{j=1}^{m}\psi_{j}(\mathbf{s})=1,
and then

\forall\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{M},\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla\psi_{j}(\mathbf{s})=0.

As
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
is constant,
\mathbf{S}\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{F}\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-\top}\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{F}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=0.

Then,
\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\in\text{Ker}(\mathbf{S})\subset\text{Ker}(f(\mathbf{S})).

Finally,

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-\top}f(\mathbf{S})\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{-1}\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=0.

This yields a vector in
\ker(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, and since
\dim(\ker(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}))=1
, it follows that

\text{for any }\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad\mathbf{x}\in\ker(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\;\Leftrightarrow\;\mathbf{x}\propto\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}=\frac{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{m}}{\left\lVert\left(\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{m}\right\rVert_{2}}.

This implies that every component of
\mathbf{x}\neq 0
is non-zero:

\mathbf{x}\in\ker(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\setminus\{0\}\;\Rightarrow\;\forall 1\leq i\leq m,\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\neq 0.

As a reminder,
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\}
denotes the set of indices such that
s_{k}=c_{i_{k}}
for
1\leq k\leq n
.

Let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. We define a vector
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by

\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{y}_{k}&\text{if }i=i_{k}\text{ for some }1\leq k\leq n,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then,

\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{y}=\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}.

Since
\mathbf{y}\neq 0
, there exists at least one index
i_{k}
such that
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{i_{k}}\neq 0
, hence
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\notin\ker(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, and therefore
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}>0
. This shows that the matrix
\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}
is positive definite.

11.2
Second scenario

For the
second scenario
, the covariance matrix
\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n}
is invertible if and only if
-\tau^{2}
is not an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}
.

As
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is positive semidefinite,

\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\},\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\geq 0.

Then,
\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}
is positive semidefinite, and

\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}
and a rank-one update

5:

Solve
\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathbf{y}}=\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{\bar{I},I}\mathbf{y}

6:

\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{y})\leftarrow\left[\mathbf{Q}\right]_{I,I}\mathbf{y}-\left[\mathbf{Q}\right]_{I,\bar{I}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right]_{I}\left[\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right]_{I}^{\top}\mathbf{y}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right]_{I}\left[\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right]_{\bar{I}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathbf{y}}

7:

a_{\tau,\alpha}^{(m)}(\mathbf{y})\leftarrow\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{y})

8:

Solve
\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}}=\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{\bar{I},I}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}

9:

\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})\leftarrow\left[\mathbf{Q}\right]_{I,I}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}-\left[\mathbf{Q}\right]_{I,\bar{I}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right]_{I}\left[\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right]_{I}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right]_{I}\left[\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right]_{\bar{I}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}}

10:

a_{\tau,\alpha}^{(m)}(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})\leftarrow\alpha\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})

11:

\ell^{(1)}_{0}\leftarrow\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{y})-\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{a_{\tau,\alpha}^{(m)}(\mathbf{y})^{2}}{a_{\tau,\alpha}^{(m)}(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})}

12:

Compute Cholesky of
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{Q}+\frac{1}{\alpha}(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})^{\top}
using sparsity of
\mathbf{Q}
and a rank-one update

13:

\ell^{(2)}_{0}\leftarrow\log\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\rvert-\log\lvert\left[\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\right]_{\bar{I},\bar{I}}\rvert-\log\lvert 1-a_{\tau,\alpha}^{(m)}(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})\rvert

14:

return

\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=-\frac{1}{2}\left(n\log(2\pi)+\ell^{(1)}_{0}+\ell^{(2)}_{0}\right)

Note:

\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{y})=\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{y}
and

\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})=\left(\mathbf{A}_{n}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
.

Algorithm 4

Computation of the log-likelihood in the second scenario

1:

\mathbf{M},\mathbf{F},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0},I,\alpha,\mathbf{A}_{n}

2:

\mathbf{S}\leftarrow\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{F}\,\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{-\top}

3:

\mathbf{Q}\leftarrow\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}\,\mathbf{S}^{2}\,\sqrt{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}

4:

Compute Cholesky of
\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}=\tau^{2}\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}
using sparsity of
\tau^{2}\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}
and a rank-one update

5:

Solve
\left(\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\right)\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{y}

6:

Solve
\left(\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\right)\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}}=\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}

7:

a_{\tau,\alpha}^{(m)}(\mathbf{y})\leftarrow\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathbf{y}}

8:

a_{\tau,\alpha}^{(m)}(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})\leftarrow\left(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}}

9:

\ell^{(1)}_{\tau}\leftarrow\tau^{-2}\left(\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\mathbf{y}}\right)-\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{a_{\tau,\alpha}^{(m)}(\mathbf{y})^{2}}{a_{\tau,\alpha}^{(m)}(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})}

10:

Compute Cholesky of
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{Q}+\frac{1}{\alpha}(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})^{\top}
using sparsity of
\mathbf{Q}
and a rank-one update

11:

\ell^{(2)}_{\tau}\leftarrow(n-m)\log(\tau^{2})-\log\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}\rvert+\log\lvert\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{\alpha}+\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n}\rvert+\log\lvert 1-a_{\tau,\alpha}^{(m)}(\mathbf{A}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})\rvert

12:

return

\mathcal{L}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=-\frac{1}{2}\left(n\log(2\pi)+\ell^{(1)}_{\tau}+\ell^{(2)}_{\tau}\right)
