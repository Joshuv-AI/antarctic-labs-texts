Abstract
The environments of deflectors in strong lensing systems affect our ability to test cosmological models and constrain evolutionary properties of galaxies. Here we measure the deflector scale (Einstein mass) and deflector environment (halo mass) of 89 spectroscopically confirmed strong lenses in the ASTRO3D Galaxy Evolution With Lenses ( AGEL ) survey. We classify deflector scale by measuring θ E \mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}} to determine the mass enclosed by the Einstein radius, M ( < θ E ) M(<\theta_{E}) . We quantify deflector environment by using photometric redshifts to determine the galaxy surface density to the fifth-nearest neighbor Σ 5 ​ ( z ) \Sigma_{5}(z) . We find that 47.2% of our deflectors are embedded in cluster environments, whereas only 9.0% have cluster-scale Einstein radii (masses). We measure a weak correlation ( r = 0.38 r=0.38 ) between Einstein mass and Σ 5 ​ ( z ) \Sigma_{5}(z) , suggesting that the assumption of single galaxy-scale deflectors in lens modeling is overly-simplified. We hypothesize that the weak correlation results from galaxy-scale bias in the original AGEL selection and the observational challenge of detecting faint arcs with large Einstein radii. Comparing number densities, N gal \text{N}_{\text{gal}} , between AGEL and control fields, we find that AGEL deflectors are in systematically denser environments. Our study provides a method to identify strong lenses as a function of deflector environment and approximate the impact of large-scale environment in lens modeling. II.1

In the second data release

Measurements of deflector scale and environment require reliable measurements of both the deflector and source redshifts. Accordingly, we consider only those systems with confirmed spectroscopic redshifts of both the source and main deflector. In most cases, we have

AGEL

For example, observer science preferences may select galaxy-scale lenses for simpler mass modeling or cluster-scale lenses for measurements of the interstellar medium. Spectroscopic observations may also be limited by source separation from the deflector or whether the deflector and source both fit in the integral field unit (IFU) field.
AGEL

The
AGEL

The parent catalog also lacks spectroscopic confirmation of deflector and source redshifts. However, because (1) we cannot reliably measure source photometric redshifts and (2) computational limitations, we cannot perform our analysis with the
AGEL
parent catalog. The reliability and degree of bias in human strong lens selections is a subject of ongoing research, and we refer the reader to
69

Number density measurements are known to be correlated with halo mass

3

39

92

(

(

To determine the deflector environment density, we apply a redshift-weighted

\Sigma_{n}(z)=\frac{n}{\pi D_{n,\ \text{Mpc}}^{2}}(1+z_{\text{defl}})

(3)

where
D_{n,\ \text{Mpc}}
is the distance (in Mpc) between the position of the central deflector and the
n
th deflector environment candidate.
\Sigma_{n}(z)
is in units of number per Mpc
2
. Our
\Sigma_{n}(z)
measurements assume that an environment candidate is at
z_{\text{defl}}
, even if it is not fitted precisely at
z_{\text{defl}}
. If the central deflector’s
z_{\text{phot}}
does not fit it to the deflector environment, we use the
z_{\text{spec}}
to include it as the closest deflector environment candidate. For measurement of the deflector environment density, we choose
n=5
to correspond to the fifth innermost member of the deflector environment. We treat the central deflector as one of the five nearest neighbors.

87
. However, our underestimate accounts for lower optical depth of our photometry (
r
-band depth of 23.9 in our paper vs.
27.7
in
87
), our higher redshift distribution (
z\sim 0.2-1
vs.
z\sim 0.15
), and
z_{\text{phot}}
unreliability.

III.2.3
LOS density comparisons with control fields

For each system in our sample, we identify twin galaxies that share characteristics with our central deflectors. Using the twins, we determine whether the
AGEL
field number densities vary significantly from control fields. To identify twins, we obtain
DECaLS
DR10 data for a random sixteen square-degree field. In that catalog, we isolate galaxies with
\text{SNR}_{\text{r}}\geq 5
and apply morphology cuts, as described in Sec.
II.2
. To match deflectors with twin galaxies, we search for galaxies with the following properties:

1.

r_{\text{defl}}-\delta r\leq r_{\text{twin}}\leq r_{\text{defl}}+\delta r
, where
\delta r=0.1
,

2.

(g-r)_{\text{defl}}-\delta(g-r)\leq(g-r)_{\text{twin}}\leq(g-r)_{\text{defl}}+\delta(g-r)
, where
\delta(g-r)=0.1
,

3.

z_{\text{defl}}-\delta z\leq z_{\text{phot, DECaLS}}\leq z_{\text{defl}}+\delta z
, where
\delta z=0.05(1+z_{\text{defl}})
, and

4.

Sersic (SER) morphology, as classified by
Tractor
.

With our matching criteria, we find twins for 64 of our 89 systems. We do not observe any systematic differences between the
AGEL
systems with twins versus those without. The methods we apply to identify control galaxies are similar to
33
, who use
z_{\text{phot}}
and magnitude cutoffs. Because we cannot quantify the reliability of individual
DECaLS

z_{\text{phot}}
measurements, we include
g-r
cutoffs to provide an additional constraint on redshift. Our requirement of Sersic morphology ensures consistency with our central deflectors,
\sim 70\%
of which
Tractor
classifies as Sersic.

Due to computational expenses of fitting
z_{\text{phot}}
, we use the photometric redshifts reported by
DECaLS
(
z_{\text{phot, DECaLS}}
), rather than refitting using EAZY, to extract the field size. We use
z_{\text{phot, DECaLS}}
to create an aperture of radius 250 kpc around each twin galaxy. While we cannot verify the accuracy of our individual
z_{\text{phot, DECaLS}}
measurements, we refer the reader to
99
for further discussion. Applying the same selection criteria as in Sec.
II.2
, we measure
\text{N}_{\text{gal}}
, the total number of galaxies within the aperture, for both the non-lens fields and the
AGEL
fields. Because we lack twin galaxy
z_{\text{spec}}
measurements to anchor our control fields to, we cannot reapply the
\Sigma_{5}(z)
method as we do for the
AGEL
fields. Similarly, because our
z_{\text{phot}}
measurements are biased against higher redshifts, our
\text{N}_{\text{gal}}
measurements are not isolated to deflector environment or line-of-sight objects.

III.3
M(<\theta_{E})
and Deflector Scale Classifications

For a spherical mass distribution, the Einstein radius,
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
, is the radius of the lensed image formed by the source
(
71
)
. Assuming the source lies directly on the LOS and the deflector is spherically symmetric, we express
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
as

\theta_{\rm{E}}^{2}=\frac{4GM}{c^{2}}\frac{D_{\rm{ds}}}{D_{\rm{d}}D_{\rm{s}}},

(4)

where
D_{\rm{d}}
,
D_{\rm{s}}
, and
D_{\rm{ds}}
are the angular diameter distances to the deflector, to the source, and between the deflector and source, respectively, while
M
is the mass enclosed by
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
, called the Einstein mass. For the remainder of this work, we express any reported value of the Einstein mass,
M(<\theta_{E})
, in
\log_{10}M_{\odot}
.

III.3.1
Measuring
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}

We use
M(<\theta_{E})
measurements to determine deflector scale. Progress to streamline batch lens modeling is ongoing (e.g.,
76
), but precise mass modeling of
\sim 10^{2}
deflectors is too computationally expensive for our purposes. Our sample also includes structures with more complex mass distributions than galaxy-scale lenses. Our limitations necessitate simpler methods of measuring Einstein mass.

Figure 6:
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
measurement using a ring model for
AGEL0603
(
\theta_{\text{E}}=17.1^{\prime\prime}
), line model for
AGEL2335
(
\theta_{\text{E}}=3.2^{\prime\prime}
), and blended line model for
AGEL0911
(
\theta_{\text{E}}=3.0^{\prime\prime}
). See Sec.
III.3.1
for the methodology behind each of these measurements.

We use the MCMC algorithm
emcee

(
36
)
to estimate
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
based on the positions of source images in
DECaLS
image data. Our MCMC method follows from previous approaches of estimating
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}

(
68
, e.g.,)
. Compared to manual estimates (e.g., using DS9;
46
), our method minimizes the impact of human error on our
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
estimates. To apply our fit, we visually identify source images and line-of-sight objects in color (
DECaLS

g
,
r
,
z
) cutouts. In order to probe only source light, we mask out light from all line-of-sight objects in the cutout. We apply a uniform prior over a range of possible
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
that we visually estimate. Our fitting method maximizes the source light contribution per pixel along a ring (the ring model) of radius
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
, or in the nine square pixels surrounding the end points of a line (the line models) with half-length
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
. We apply three
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
models based on the source image configuration (as shown in the panels of
fig.

6
):

1.

Ring Model:
If the source images form a ring around the deflector, we fit a ring with the highest source light contribution per pixel. The left panel of
fig.

6
shows a ring model fit for
AGEL0603
. Here,
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
is the fitted radius of the ring. We apply this method to 44 systems.

2.

Line Model:
If the source image configuration is an image-counterimage pair, we fit a line between the image and counterimage. Our MCMC algorithm maximizes the total source light from the nine square-pixel region around each end of the line. The center panel of
fig.

6
shows an example of a line model fit between an image and counter image in
AGEL2335
. Here,
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
corresponds to half the length of the fitted line. We apply this method to 25 systems.

3.

Line Model (blended)
: In
DECaLS
imaging of 16 systems with image-counterimage pairs, the counterimage is blended with deflector light. Here, we estimate the position of the counterimage as the far edge of the deflector light, along the line of axial symmetry with the image. We mask out deflector light and apply a nine square-pixel region centered at the point we estimate the counterimage to be. We measure
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
as half the length of the line fitted between the image and counterimage. The right panel in
fig.

6
shows a blended line model fit for
AGEL0911
.
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
is half the length of the fitted line. This method is an upper limit on the estimated
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
, as the position of the counterimage may be closer to the deflector.

III.3.2
Measuring
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
uncertainties

We calculate uncertainties as half the width of the arcs in
DECaLS
imaging in, as measured in DS9
(
46
)
. We find an average arc width of
1.36
″, approximately the size of the
g
-band PSF in
DECaLS
.

Though uncertainties associated with arc width account for the PSF and pixel scales, there are additional uncertainties based on our assumptions. When the source is directly along the LOS and the deflector has a spherically symmetric mass distribution, the image separation is twice
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
. In reality, few of the lenses in our sample likely fit these requirements.

To include systematic uncertainties, we measure the scatter between our
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
and HST mass models for a subsample of 15 galaxy-scale lenses in
73
and Iwamoto et al. in prep (obtained via private correspondence). The lenses are mass modeled using
Lenstronomy

(
12
)
and
GLEE

(
79
)
.
fig.

7
shows our measurements vs. the HST mass modeled measurements. We measure mean
\frac{\Delta\theta_{\text{E}}}{\theta_{\text{E}}}\ \text{of}\ 0.11,0.15,\ \text{and}\ 0.15
for the ring, line, and blended line models, respectively. We attribute the similar
\frac{\Delta\theta_{\text{E}}}{\theta_{\text{E}}}
between the line and blended line models to a small sample size. We apply the
\frac{\Delta\theta_{\text{E}}}{\theta_{\text{E}}}
to each of our
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
measurements, with the equation

\sigma_{\text{sys}}=\frac{\Delta\theta_{\text{E}}}{\theta_{\text{E}}^{\prime}}\times\theta_{\text{E}},

(5)

where
\theta_{\text{E}}^{\prime}
is the mass modeled measurement and
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
is our measurement. We choose
\Delta\theta_{\text{E}}
based on the model we use to measure
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
.

III.3.4
M(<\theta_{E})
measurements and deflector scale classifications

For each system with measured
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
, we find
M(<\theta_{E})
by solving for
M
, the Einstein mass, in Eq.
4
. We estimate the
M(<\theta_{\text{E}})
for 85 lenses. Our
M(<\theta_{E})
measurements range from
\log\frac{M(<\theta_{\text{E}})}{M_{\odot}}\sim 11.8-14.0
. We measure a median Einstein mass of
\log_{10}(\frac{M(<\theta_{E})}{M_{\odot}})_{\rm avg}=12.30\pm 0.27
. The diverse range of
M(<\theta_{\text{E}})
in our sample provides a simultaneous look at lenses across the Einstein mass spectrum.

For six of the galaxy-scale lenses in
73
, they report
\overline{M}(<\theta_{\text{E}})=12.1\pm 0.3
. For the same six lenses, we estimate a mean
\overline{M}(<\theta_{\text{E}})=12.2\pm 0.3
, consistent within
0.1
dex of the
73
measurements. At higher mass scales, we compare the enclosed mass measured from our
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
measurement of the
z_{\text{src}}=1.432
source from
77
. We find
M(<\theta_{\text{E}})=13.70\pm 0.19
, similar to the measurements reported in
77
.
68
finds that
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
accurately reproduces
M(<\theta_{E})
at larger Einstein mass scales.

To classify lenses according to their deflector scale, we apply deflector mass cuts. Assuming a singular isothermal ellipsoid (SIS) deflector,
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
is given by

\theta_{\text{E}}=4\pi\frac{\sigma^{2}}{c^{2}}\frac{D_{\text{ds}}}{D_{\text{s}}},

(6)

where
\sigma
is the velocity dispersion. If we define
\Sigma_{\text{cr}}=\frac{c^{2}D_{\text{s}}}{4\pi GD_{\text{ds}}D_{\text{d}}}
, then the 2D enclosed mass is

M(<\theta_{\text{E}})=\pi\Sigma_{\text{cr}}(\theta_{\text{E}}D_{\text{d}})^{2}.

(7)

The proportion of mass enclosed by an SIS profile is sensitive to velocity dispersion (i.e. halo mass) and redshifts of the deflector and source. For high mass halos at our typical
z_{\text{defl}}
and
z_{\text{src}}
,
M(<\theta_{\text{E}})\sim 0.1M_{200}
, where
M_{200}
is the halo mass. Therefore, to apply our cuts, we assume that
M(<\theta_{E})
is
10\%
of the total halo mass.

We define (log)
M_{200}<13
as galaxies,
13-14
as groups, and
>14
as clusters. Therefore, using the
M(<\theta_{E})
measurements, we classify deflectors with
M(<\theta_{E})

<12
as galaxy-scale deflectors,
12-13
as group-scale deflectors, and
>13
as cluster-scale deflectors. Our values are consistent with the expected
M(<\theta_{E})
at the mass scales of galaxy, group and cluster halos.

When we cannot make reliable measurements of
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
(for six systems), we make visual classifications of the deflectors using
DECaLS
photometry. If
DECaLS
photometry only shows one deflector enclosed by
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
, then we classify the deflector as galaxy-scale. If
DECaLS
photometry shows multiple deflectors, we classify the deflector as group-scale.

V.1
Einstein mass does not (reliably) trace halo mass

Our results suggest that deflector scale is not closely correlated with deflector environment, especially in the densest (cluster) environments.

. For only systems with measured
\Sigma_{5}(z)

A few possible explanations exist for the lack of a strong observed correlation. First, some deflectors may be satellites in larger halos. In several systems—particularly some of our galaxy-scale deflectors in cluster environments—we visually identify nearby, brighter
galaxies with similar colors to our deflector. Dense cluster environments boost the lensing probability for satellites relative to field galaxies with the same properties, so our result is not unexpected. Nonetheless, spectroscopic or X-ray observations are needed to confirm whether some
AGEL
deflectors are satellites.

In addition,
AGEL
detections may be biased towards systems with high source magnifications,
\mu
.
2
predicts that, for
\mu\gtrsim 10
, the host halo mass distribution is roughly flat for
M_{200}\sim 12-14
. Coupled with
AGEL
’s bias towards higher-
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
fields, we would expect more systems in group and cluster halos, consistent with
fig.

8
. Similarly, because the
44
CNN is trained on galaxy-scale lenses, we expect a bias towards higher-magnification, galaxy- and group-scale lenses.

From Eq.
4
, there is a redshift dependence on
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
—and thus
M(<\theta_{E})
. To investigate the impact of cosmology on our lack of correlation, we isolate our sample to deflectors with
0.5<z_{\text{defl}}<0.6
and
2<z_{\text{src}}<3
. Our method isolates our
\Sigma_{5}(z)
vs.
M(<\theta_{E})
for systems with roughly (within
\sim 20\%
) equivalent
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
dependence on cosmology. For seven systems, we measure a Spearman coefficient of
r=0.5
. Although we find a slightly higher correlation when adjusting for cosmology, cosmology does not explain the observed lack of a correlation for our full sample. Per
table

1
, we do not observe a redshift dependence in our deflector scale classifications, suggesting that
M(<\theta_{E})
is the primary driver of
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
in the
AGEL
sample.

—a large cluster-scale deflector. Our value is 1.3 dex greater than the
M(<\theta_{E})
we measure for
AGEL0424
(
M(<\theta_{\text{E}})=12.97
). As
AGEL0424
is in a cluster-scale deflector environment, our higher-
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
measurements support a stronger correlation between deflector scale and environment. In the absence of follow-up observations, we cannot confirm this selection effect for most of the systems located in dense deflector environments. However, the presence of multiple arcs in HST follow-ups of two clusters hints that many more such arcs may be visible in our cluster environment sample.

of
AGEL

Our result is inconsistent with several past studies comparing lensing fields with control fields
(
85
;
33
;
96
, e.g.,)
. The inconsistency may be due to differences between the
AGEL
sample and samples used in past results. For example, the
85
analysis is based on the Sloan Lens ACS (SLACS;
15
), which probes lenses primarily with
\theta_{\text{E}}\lesssim 2^{\prime\prime}

(
14
)
. Similarly, the
33
and
32
analyses are based on the COSMOS field lenses
(

)
, which also have
\theta_{\text{E}}\lesssim 2^{\prime\prime}
. Compared to other surveys, our
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
distribution is biased toward much larger deflectors (see
fig.

9
).

Relative to twin galaxies chosen with the same individual properties,
AGEL
deflectors are biased towards group and cluster environments. Our result is consistent with our other measurements, which show that the
AGEL
deflector sample is strongly biased towards group-scale Einstein masses and cluster-scale environments. As the
fig.

10
result suggests, many of our deflectors may be satellites in groups and clusters. As a result, we’d expect that
AGEL
fields would be located in denser environments than control fields.

AGEL
lenses may also be located in denser lines-of-sight (i.e. outside the deflector environment) than control fields. However, because of computational constraints, we cannot confirm whether some of the observed discrepancy is due to an excess of objects outside the deflector environment. Both
8
and
32
find insignificant bias in line-of-sight density between lensing and control fields. Because a majority of objects with
EAZY

z_{\text{phot}}
are classified as deflector environment candidates, we do not expect to observe significant overdensity outside the deflector environment. Nonetheless, more work is needed to confirm whether
AGEL
systems are biased towards denser lines-of-sight.

The difference between
AGEL
and control fields is sensitive to the
r
-band magnitude distribution of our
AGEL
deflectors. If we match twins only for
AGEL
deflectors with
r<19
, we find median
\text{N}_{\text{gal}}
of
\overline{\text{N}}_{\text{gal, AGEL}}=50
and
\overline{\text{N}}_{\text{gal, control}}=42
, slightly decreasing the observed difference. Our KS probability also improves to
p=0.05
, although some constraining power is lost due to the smaller (
\sim 20
) sample. The KS
p
value still concludes to
\sim 2\sigma
that the
AGEL
and control distributions are different. We hypothesize that fainter (i.e.,
r>19
) control galaxies may have less reliable
z_{\text{phot, DECaLS}}
measurements, which may lead to imprecise deflector-twin matching. Furthermore, at the brightest
r
-band magnitudes, we are much more likely to choose clusters both for the
AGEL
and control fields. Nonetheless, neither bias accounts for most of the observed difference between
AGEL
and control fields.

Theoretical predictions of gravitational lensing phenomena show that the probability of finding strong lensing events is higher for larger halos (e.g.,
70
), as well as subhalos in larger halos
(
58
)
. As larger halos have larger lensing cross sections than smaller halos, larger halos are associated with higher probability of lensing. Our observation of the high
\text{N}_{\text{gal}}
tail is consistent with the higher proportion of cluster-scale environments in the
AGEL
sample. Observations of cluster substructures also show that cluster-scale halos are more efficient lenses than
\Lambda
CDM predicts by more than an order of magnitude
(
58
)
. Although analysis of the discrepancy is beyond the scope of our paper, it is an interesting result that warrants further investigation. Our observations provide a method to analyze this phenomenon with future, less biased samples.

V.3
Accounting for deflector environment in lens modeling

Our
z_{\text{phot}}
and
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
measurements provide promising and efficient methods to (1) identify systems of interest and (2) minimize systematics due to environment in lens modeling. New surveys from
Euclid
and LSST each are expected to discover
\mathcal{O}(10^{5})
galaxy-scale lenses
(
27
)
. We have shown that our simple
z_{\text{phot}}
and
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
measurement methods are useful to identify lenses with sparse lines of sight or special characteristics (e.g., group-scale lenses or galaxy-scale lenses in clusters). Our method could improve systematics in lens modeling and improve lens selections for a host of science applications.

93
find that external convergence from dense deflector environments can lead to measurements overestimating
H_{0}
by
\sim 10\%
. Similarly, they find that precise measurements of
H_{0}
require accounting for nearby structures along the line-of-sight. Therefore, to make one of the most precise late universe measurements of
H_{0}
yet, the
H0LiCOW
team in
94
uses wide-field spectroscopic data of the deflector environment to capture the line-of-sight mass distribution. Although we lack spectroscopic redshifts of the environment in our sample, our
z_{\text{phot}}
measurements provide a method to roughly account for environment in lens models. Our methods can also be used to identify samples for simultaneous analysis of halo characteristics across the mass spectrum.

In the future, as new spectroscopic surveys such as the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (
DESI
;
23
) provide spectroscopic measurements of the LOS for our sample, we could further improve systematics in lens modeling by using spectroscopic, rather than photometric, redshifts. We suggest a repetition of our analysis once sufficient spatial spectroscopic data is available.

Though we do not provide constraints on individual masses of line-of-sight members, we can use
r
-band magnitudes as a tracer. To that end, we provide
r
-band magnitude maps for all objects with measured
z_{\text{phot}}
along the line-of-sight in each system. In addition to maps, we provide tables of coordinates, fitted
z_{\text{phot}}
, and
r
-mag for all objects fitted in the line-of-sight for each system. All of our data is available as supplement to our paper, and will be made public via the
AGEL

website.

We investigate whether accounting for environment using our lens modeling prescription improves mass models for
AGEL0424
, a group-scale deflector located in a dense cluster halo. Further analysis of this lens, which has been dubbed “Gargantua,” will appear in Iwamoto et al. (in prep). Using
GLEE

(
79
)
without inclusion of the deflector environment in position models, Gargantua does not converge on a lensing solution. We use our
r
-mag and
z_{\text{phot}}
measurements to apply a host halo profile, and we test whether this improves our position model in
GLEE
. We find that accounting for environment leads to convergence and substantial qualitative improvement in image positions. Quantitative results of the position model will be published in Iwamoto et al. (in prep). Our measurements demonstrate that our rough method to account for deflector environment may substantially improve lens models.

VI
Conclusions

In our work, we measure the relation between deflector scale (Einstein radius) and deflector environment (galaxy surface density) for a sample of 89 spectroscopically confirmed strong lenses from the
AGEL
survey. The deflectors are at
z\sim 0.2-1
and the sources at
z\sim 1-3
(see
fig.

1
for
z_{\text{spec}}
distribution). To quantify the halos of the deflectors, we use
EAZY

(
17
)
to measure photometric redshifts (
z_{\text{phot}}
) for all objects along the line-of-sight and use the
z_{\text{phot}}
measurements to measure the surface density at the fifth-nearest neighbor,
\Sigma_{5}(z)
(
fig.

5
). Using
emcee

(
36
)
to fit
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
based on image separations (
fig.

6
), we determine the Einstein mass,
M(<\theta_{E})
, and classify the deflector scale. Our main results are as follows:

1.

Most strong lenses in the
AGEL
sample with galaxy- or group-scale Einstein masses (
M(<\theta_{E})

<13
) are embedded in massive group or cluster halos (
fig.

10
). Including information on (higher density) environments leads to significant qualitative improvements in convergence and source positions for lens modeling.

2.

Einstein mass is not strongly correlated with halo mass for the 89 deflectors in our sample (
fig.

10
). Using
\Sigma_{5}(z)
to quantify deflector environment (halo mass), we find 21 galaxy-, 26 group-, and 42 cluster-scale deflector environments (
table

1
). In contrast, classification of deflector scale (Einstein mass) with
M(<\theta_{E})
finds 22 galaxy-, 59 group-, and 8 cluster-scale deflectors. We consider several factors – including biases in the original
AGEL
selection and lensing by satellites in clusters– that may lead to the observed discrepancy between the two deflector properties.

3.

The deflectors in
AGEL
systems are embedded in denser fields than control fields (
fig.

12
). Our result is inconsistent with past comparisons of lens environments with control fields. We hypothesize that the higher
\mathrm{\theta}_{\rm{E}}
of the
AGEL
distribution biases the
AGEL
fields to denser group and cluster environments. We hypothesize that more massive halos are more efficient at lensing, and that the
AGEL
survey selection favors brighter, more highly magnified source images.

4.

We provide to the community detailed measurements of the 89
AGEL
systems that include
EAZY
’s
z_{\text{phot}}
and
DECaLS
’
r
-mag measurements to, e.g. quantify environment for lens modeling (see
table

2
,
table

3
, and
table

4
). We also provide parameters including Einstein radii and masses, spectroscopic information, and our deflector environment measurements.

Our results are a cautionary tale that simplifying assumptions of deflector scale do not account for the larger-scale properties of deflector host halos. Our results also suggest that more work is needed to address selection biases in current and upcoming lens searches. As we discover
\mathcal{O}(10^{5})
lenses in the coming decade, we could substantially improve cosmological and galaxy evolution models using lensing. However, to realize the full potential of the lensing breakthrough, we must carefully consider how our measurements are impacted by systematics related to lensing environment and selection biases in our lens searches.

Appendix A
Interesting phenomena

In Tables
2
,
3
, and
4
, the column “Characteristics” lists systems we highlight for additional investigation. We henceforth refer to all three tables as the “classifications table.” We include four areas of interest based on deflector scale and environment measurements: (1) galaxy-scale lenses in cluster candidates, (2) galaxy-scale lenses in group candidates, (3) group-scale lenses, and (4) compound lenses.

A.1
Galaxy-scale lenses in cluster candidates

We label galaxy-scale deflectors in cluster environments as “GaGC” in our classifications table. We detect 7 such systems. Such lensing configurations enable probes of inner halo density profiles, which are useful for testing and refining
\Lambda
CDM (e.g.,
67
;
4
;
22
). This sample may also be useful for explaining the discrepancy between lensing efficiency in
\Lambda
CDM-predicted clusters versus observed clusters
(
58
)
.

A.2
Galaxy-scale lenses in galaxy groups

We label galaxy-scale lenses in group environments as “GaGG” in our classification tables. As galaxy groups complete the continuum from galaxies to clusters, they are a useful probe of large-scale structure evolution. Galaxy groups are also more diverse than their cluster counterparts
(
52
)
. “GaGG” systems enable measurements of the galaxy-halo connection (e.g.,
52
;
98
), the concentration parameter
c_{200}
, and density profiles of dark matter halos
(
61
)
. They may also help fix the concentration-mass and mass-luminosity relations across the entire mass spectrum
(
59
)
.

We identify 7 galaxy-scale deflectors in group-scale environments. Our sample provides a large sample of such lenses for future analysis.

A.3
Group-scale lenses

We label group-scale deflector candidates as “GrSL” in our classification tables. We find 59 lenses with Einstein masses at the scale of groups in our sample. We expect to observe many more group-scale lenses in future surveys. Seven of the eight group-scale lenses are in cluster-scale deflector environments.

Though they are not located in groups on their own, group-scale lenses enable larger-scale probes of cluster dark matter halos. Group-scale lenses also possess simpler mass distributions, which could improve constraints on dark matter halo profiles and cosmological parameters
(
51
)
.

A.4
Compound lenses in galaxy or group environments

Compound lenses, also referred to as double source plane lenses (DSPLs), occur when multiple sources at different redshifts are lensed by the same foreground deflector, with the nearer source(s) also contributing to the lensing of more distant source(s)
(
26
)
. We label compound lenses as “CMPD” in our classification tables. We identify 5 compound lenses based on our spectroscopic data, but preliminary modeling of our galaxy-scale lens sample in Iwamoto et al. (in prep) suggests that the
AGEL
sample may contain several other compound lens candidates without spectroscopic confirmation.

Compound lenses enable independent measurements of cosmological parameters such as the Hubble constant,
H_{0}
, and the dark energy equation of state parameter,
w

(
25
)
. Two
AGEL
survey analyses with compound lenses in
72
and
16
show that increasing compound lenses sample sizes substantially improves constraints on cosmological parameters.
72
include analysis of compound lenses
AGEL1507
, a galaxy-scale deflector in a galaxy environment;
16
also use
AGEL0353
, a galaxy-scale deflector in a group environment.

In our classification of compound lenses, we only choose deflectors that (1) are visually classified as galaxy-scale deflectors and (2) are located in galaxy or group environments. Four of the systems have group-scale Einstein mass classifications, but visual inspection of
DECaLS
photometry shows only one central deflector. Our sparse environment requirement minimizes the impact of environment on the lensing profile, which will improve constraints on cosmological parameters.
