Antiphospholipid antibody associated retinal microvascular impairment: a case report and literature review
Authors: Erqian Wang, Jun Li, Lu Sun, Youxin Chen, Jiuliang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOcular involvement in antiphospholipid syndrome typically manifests as retinal artery or vein occlusions. Isolated progressive retinal microvascular impairment as associated with antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL) is rare and poorly characterised.This study is a retrospective case report and literature review. A 35-year-old male presented with bilateral progressive retinal microvascular impairment as the sole clinical manifestation, characterised by concentric capillary dropout beginning in the far periphery and extending towards the juxtafoveal region, accompanied by progressive enlargement of the foveal avascular zone. Laboratory tests confirmed persistently elevated anti-beta-2-glycoprotein-1 IgM. Ultra-widefield fluorescein angiography and optical coherence tomography angiography comprehensively documented the microvascular impairment. Despite sequential treatment with pan-retinal photocoagulation, antiplatelet, anticoagulant and immunosuppressive therapies, microvascular deterioration progressed. Scattered recanalisation of occluded microvasculature was observed following the introduction of sirolimus.This case provides longitudinal in vivo evidence for the involvement of aPL in the pathogenesis of progressive retinal microcirculatory compromise. The advancement of ocular imaging techniques, especially ultra-widefield angiography and optical coherence tomography angiography, is critical for the recognition and characterisation of this microvascular phenotype, which may expand the clinical spectrum of aPL-related microvascular diseases.

Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is a systemic autoimmune disorder characterised by recurrent thrombotic events involving arteries, veins or microvasculature, associated with persistent positive antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL).1 The 2023 classification criteria issued by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) have broadened the clinical scope of APS. Thrombosis is no longer restricted to large vessels. Microvascular manifestations such as diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, livedo reticularis and APS-related nephropathy are now recognised as an independent clinical domain.2

While ocular involvement in APS most commonly presents as retinal artery occlusion or retinal vein occlusion, retinal microvascular impairment has rarely been reported as the initial or isolated sign of the disease.35 Here, we describe a patient with persistently positive aPL and retinal microvascular impairment characterised by progressive capillary dropout initiating in the far periphery and advancing concentrically toward the mid-periphery, with concurrent juxtafoveal microvascular rarefaction threatening central vision. By tracking the longitudinal changes of retinal microvasculature following antiplatelet, anticoagulant and sirolimus treatment, we provided in-vivo evidence for the pathogenic association between aPL and retinal microcirculatory compromise. The progressive retinal microvascular impairment observed in this case demonstrated a close temporal correlation with persistently positive aPL, thereby expanding our understanding of the disease spectrum associated with aPL-related microvascular disorders.

With the advances in ocular imaging and accumulating literature, the spectrum of ocular manifestations of APS is now known to extend well beyond branch or central retinal vessel occlusions. Integrating this index case with a systematic review of the literature, we propose that retinal microvascular assessment represents an underexplored yet potentially informative dimension in APS and should be incorporated into the evaluation of patients with confirmed or suspected APS.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old male first presented to the Peking Union Medical College Hospital in December 2020 with a 1-week history of experiencing floaters and peripheral shadows in the right eye. He denied having ocular pain, redness, photophobia, oral or genital ulcers, rash, recent febrile illness or travel history. His medical history was unremarkable, with no chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, hereditary metabolic disorders or previous thrombosis. The review of systems was negative for symptoms suggestive of APS, such as limb swelling or pain, dyspnoea, chest pain, livedo reticularis or skin ulcers, sudden hemiparesis or slurred speech and oedema or frothy urine. At presentation, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/20 in both eyes. Fundus photography showed a neovascular membrane originating from the temporal retina with minor vitreous haemorrhage in the right eye, as well as peripheral retinal vessels appearing as white lines in both eyes (figure 1a and b). Ultra-widefield fluorescein angiography revealed extensive peripheral retinal microvascular non-perfusion and intact macular microvasculature in both eyes (figure 1c and d).

Ultra-widefield fundus photography ((a) right eye; (b) left eye) and fluorescein angiography ((c) right eye; (d) left eye) at disease onset. At presentation, bilateral, extensive peripheral microvascular disruption was observed, with large areas of capillary non-perfusion (asterisks, (c and d)). Along the perfusion-non-perfusion border, residual vessels were sparse, tortuous and anastomotically rerouted, terminating in bulbous blind endings (short white arrows, (c and d)). The region outlined by the dashed box (c) is shown at higher magnification in figure 3a. Within the non-perfused retina, branch arteries and veins were completely occluded, which appeared as characteristic white lines on retinal photography (long thin arrow, (b)). Widespread ischaemia upregulated vascular endothelial growth factor, promoting neovascularisation. These fine, frond-like neovascular membranes were faintly visible on colour images but sharply delineated on fluorescein angiography (short yellow arrow, (c)). Because the macular microvasculature was intact in both eyes, the disease remained asymptomatic until rupture of a fragile neovascular tuft caused a minor vitreous haemorrhage in the right eye (long thick arrow, (a)), producing floaters and shadows that prompted referral.

Laboratory tests showed normal complete blood count, as well as normal liver and kidney function, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C reactive protein, homocysteine and immunoglobulin (Ig) G. Urinalysis, including microscopic examination of the sediment, revealed no significant haematuria or proteinuria. Serological screening for chronic infections (including hepatitis B virus) and tumour markers (including alpha-fetoprotein and carcinoembryonic antigen) was also negative. ANA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), extractable nuclear antigen antibodies, lupus anticoagulant and anti-phosphatidylserine/prothrombin antibodies were all negative. Anti-beta-2-glycoprotein-1 (aβ2GP1) IgM was positive at 71.3 MPLU/mL (reference <16.0 MPLU/mL) measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (INOVA). Anticardiolipin IgM was mildly elevated at 9.05 MPLU/mL (reference <8.0 MPLU/mL). After 12 weeks, aβ2GP1 IgM remained positive (72.2 MPLU/mL), and primary APS was thus strongly suspected.

Pan-retinal photocoagulation was performed on the non-perfused peripheral retina of both eyes. The neovascular membrane subsequently regressed, and vitreous haemorrhage resolved completely (figure 2a and b). Concurrently, the patient was commenced on a 2-year course of low-dose immunosuppressive therapy, consisting of oral glucocorticoids (initially prednisone acetate 15 mg daily, tapered to a maintenance dose of 5 mg daily) and hydroxychloroquine (0.2 g two times per day). Glucocorticoid therapy was ultimately discontinued in January 2023. Low-dose aspirin was initiated as first-line antithrombotic therapy for APS. However, after 5 months of continuous aspirin therapy, peripheral microvascular dropout progressed insidiously. Warfarin was therefore substituted, with the international normalised ratio (INR) titrated to the therapeutic range of 2.0–3.0. Despite uninterrupted anticoagulation for 3 years, the ischaemic front continued to advance concentrically toward the mid-periphery. Consequently, sirolimus (1 mg once daily) was added to the therapeutic regimen in December 2024 and has been continued to date. Ten months later, although microvascular disruption had progressed in multiple regions, scattered recanalisation of previously occluded retinal microvasculature was documented. This phenomenon had not been observed during prior antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy. Ultra-widefield fluorescein angiography (figure 2c and d) provided a panoramic view of both eyes at the most recent follow-up visit and clearly delineated the worsening microvascular disease in the retina. A magnified view of the peripheral retina of the right eye (outlined by the dashed boxes in figures1c 2c) is shown in figure 3, along with follow-up images for comparison, to better visualise the progressive disruption and scattered recanalisation following antiplatelet, anticoagulant and sirolimus therapy. Additionally, the initially intact macular microvasculature also exhibited progressive disruption, resulting in enlargement of the foveal avascular zone in both eyes (figure 4). Throughout follow-up, aβ2GP1 IgM remained persistently positive, and the patient experienced a mild yet slowly progressive decline in visual acuity, with a final BCVA of 20/40 in the right eye and 20/25 in the left.

Discussion and review of literature

The initial and, to date, sole clinical manifestation of this case was bilateral, progressive retinal microvascular impairment, accompanied by persistently elevated aβ2GP1 antibody titres. Although ocular disorders such as Coats’ disease, macular telangiectasia and radiation retinopathy can manifest as microvascular disease, the fundoscopic findings were incongruent with these diagnoses, and there was no history of radiotherapy. Systemic aetiologies—including diabetic and hypertensive retinopathy, hyperviscosity syndromes, hyperhomocysteinaemia, sickle cell disease, thrombotic microangiopathies, infections, malignancies and autoimmune vasculitis (eg, Behçet’s disease, systemic lupus erythematosus and ANCA-associated vasculitis)—were all considered but ruled out because of the absence of relevant past medical history, supporting clinical features and corroborative laboratory abnormalities. A clear temporal correlation between the dynamic evolution of retinal microvascular impairment and sustained positivity of aPL further supports the key role of aPLs in microcirculatory injury and broadens the understanding of the spectrum of aPL-related microvascular diseases.

The recognition of retinal macrovascular thrombosis as a hallmark of APS is well established, and its diagnostic significance has been consistently reinforced through successive updates to the classification criteria. As early as the late 1980s, several reports documented the association between retinal vessel occlusion and aPL.68 Although the initial case series defining primary APS (PAPS) in 1989 did not include ocular thrombotic events,9 retinal artery and vein occlusions were promptly recognised as important clinical manifestations.1014 The visually dramatic nature of these occlusions, coupled with the ease of direct funduscopic diagnosis, led to a surge in reports establishing retinal macrovascular thrombosis as a key clinical manifestation of APS,1518 sometimes even as its sole presenting sign.19 Consequently, despite not being explicitly listed in the Sapporo or Sydney criteria, expert consensus widely accepted it as a diagnostic criterion,20 21 a status formally codified in the 2023 ACR/EULAR classification.2

In contrast to the earlier recognition of macrovascular involvement, the recognition of microvascular involvement in APS emerged much later. While certain clinical conditions potentially resulting from microvascular thrombosis, such as transverse myelitis22 and chorea,22 23 were known to be associated with APS, the difficulty in obtaining direct histopathological or high-resolution imaging evidence hindered their inclusion in the formal criteria. The 2023 ACR/EULAR criteria marked a paradigm shift by establishing a dedicated clinical domain for microvascular disease, awarding points for specific manifestations like diffuse alveolar haemorrhage and APS nephropathy, particularly when supported by pathological confirmation of microthrombosis.2 However, retinal microvascular manifestations have not yet been explicitly incorporated into this microvascular domain. Therefore, since the patient’s retinal microvascular impairment is currently not included in the microvascular disease domain of the 2023 ACR/EULAR APS classification criteria, and in the laboratory module, only persistently positive aβ2GP1 with moderate titres is met, scoring 1 point and not meeting the threshold of 3 points, the patient does not currently meet the classification criteria for APS.

The decision to add sirolimus (a mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor) was driven by the failure of conventional anticoagulation and the need to target the underlying immunothrombosis. Its selection was based on emerging mechanistic and clinical evidence. Preclinical and molecular studies indicate that mTOR pathway hyperactivation, particularly in a distinct APS patient subset, is linked to severe phenotypes, including microvascular injury.24 25 By inhibiting mTOR, sirolimus may counteract endothelial dysfunction, dampen neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and modulate antibody production.26 Although large trial data are lacking, case reports support its use in refractory APS. The observed scattered retinal microvascular recanalisation in our patient, uniquely following sirolimus initiation, provides preliminary clinical support for this approach, aligning with the view of APS as a thrombo-inflammatory disorder requiring therapies beyond anticoagulation.27

The recognition of retinal microvascular manifestations related to aPL has evolved alongside advancements in ophthalmic imaging techniques. To present this evolutionary process in chronological order, table 1 presents a compilation of illustrative case reports and series of cases. Retinal microvascular alterations associated with aPL were likely first observed as early as 1990 when Tolosa-Vilella et al reported a case of APS manifesting as peripheral retinal ischaemia, which likely represented microvascular occlusion similar to our case.12 However, the report lacked fundus images to document the microvascular involvement. In 1995, Castañón et al conducted fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) on 13 PAPS cases.16 Beyond classic retinal macrovascular occlusions, he observed three cases presenting with peri-macular arteriole occlusion, bilateral choriocapillary obstruction and areas of capillary hypoperfusion, respectively. In 1996, Dunn et al reported a case of PAPS with extensive peripheral capillary non-perfusion on FFA.28 Due to the poor image quality, scarcity of cases and limited understanding of APS-related microvascular alterations, these early microvascular findings were regarded as secondary or incidental to large-vessel occlusion.

As the resolution of FFA improved, clearer evidence emerged supporting primary microvascular involvement as a distinct ocular manifestation in APS.3 In 2001, Smith et al reported a case of aPL-related retinopathy mimicking diabetic retinopathy.29 In 2003, Hartnett et al described a 6-year-old child with PAPS whose sole clinical manifestation was retinal venous beading with progressive capillary non-perfusion despite aspirin therapy.19 In 2009, Tang et al reported a case of APS resembling serpiginous choroidopathy, with FFA and indocyanine green angiography revealing peripheral nasal dropout and choriocapillary non-perfusion.30 In 2012, Turaka et al reported a case of PAPS whose peripheral retinal microvascular alterations were similar to our case.31 Although FFA was useful in assessing retinal microvasculature, this technique requires intravenous dye injection, limiting its use in patients with renal impairment or contrast allergy. Furthermore, conventional FFA systems could not rapidly capture high-resolution, distortion-free, panoramic images of the peripheral retinal microvasculature, often requiring time-consuming montages.

The advent of optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) and ultra-widefield FFA (UWF-FFA) in the late 2010s transformed ophthalmic imaging and greatly enhanced the clinical understanding of microvascular involvement in APS. OCTA enables non-invasive, depth-resolved, high-resolution visualisation of the retinal capillary network. In 2017, Trese et al first used OCTA to identify paracentral acute middle maculopathy (PAMM) associated with APS. They noted perfusion deficits at the level of the superficial capillary plexus of the macula, deep capillary plexus and choriocapillaris.32 Neto et al also used OCTA to detect PAMM in three patients (five eyes) with APS.33 While OCTA provides exquisite detail of capillary abnormalities, UWF-FFA expands the field of microvascular imaging. UWF-FFA can capture 360° peripheral retinal images in 3–4 shots, allowing for rapid, comprehensive assessment of the peripheral microvasculature. In the 2010s, Lyles et al and Cicinelli et al used UWF-FFA to demonstrate capillary non-perfusion and small arteriolar obliteration in the peripheral retina of APS.34 35 These advancing imaging technologies provide powerful tools for characterising the global extent and detailed features of microvascular changes in APS. Our case exemplifies this, using both macular OCTA and UWF-FFA to comprehensively characterise the aPL-associated retinal microvascular manifestations.

An increasingly deep understanding of ocular microvascular involvement in APS raises several critical questions for future research. First, with respect to the classification value of retinal microvascular manifestations, the current APS classification criteria do not include retinal microvasculature within the microvascular domain. Advances in ophthalmic imaging should facilitate the accumulation of robust clinical evidence to define the prevalence of these lesions, their association with aPLs and potentially support their inclusion in future criteria.

Second, concerning optimal and cost-effective screening for ocular involvement, direct ophthalmoscopy, a fundamental non-invasive tool, was initially the primary method for detecting APS-related eye disease.13 Subsequently, improved FFA resolution enhanced detection of retinal abnormalities.16 36 However, a 1999 prospective study by Gelfand et al of asymptomatic or transiently symptomatic APS patients concluded that routine FFA was unnecessary.37 With the emergence of non-invasive OCTA, Neto et al demonstrated that even asymptomatic APS patients harbour structural abnormalities (predominantly PAMM) in 7.7% of cases.33 Our case and previous reports suggest that subclinical macular and peripheral microvascular alterations may exist in APS patients without overt visual symptoms. As a non-invasive modality capable of precise qualitative and quantitative assessment of the macular and mid-peripheral microvasculature, OCTA holds significant promise for systemic disease evaluation.38 39 Future studies should not only further define the detection rate of OCTA abnormalities in APS and their correlation with serological and other clinical features to identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from screening but also investigate whether OCTA-derived metrics correlate with thrombotic risk and disease prognosis, potentially guiding systemic therapy. While FFA is not recommended for routine screening, the specific indications for UWF-FFA in APS patients warrant further investigation.

Third, the pathophysiological nature of APS microvascular manifestations remains debated regarding whether it involves vascular wall inflammation. The classical APS clinical criteria emphasise thrombosis or pregnancy morbidity without significant vessel wall inflammation. However, cases reported by Kurz et al and Turaka et al reported vascular wall staining and leakage observed on FFA, suggesting an inflammatory component.31 40 Furthermore, Wood et al reported a case of PAPS presenting as bilateral frosted branch angiitis, with FFA confirming prominent vascular inflammation.41 In our case, FFA revealed not only microvascular occlusion but also vessel wall staining and leakage, suggesting possible alterations in vascular permeability or a more complex pathophysiology beyond pure thrombosis.

Conclusion

Retinal microvascular impairment may represent a significant yet under-recognised clinical manifestation of microvascular involvement in APS and thus warrants consideration as a diagnostic indicator. The retina serves as a critical in vivo window for assessing microcirculation, as retinal vasculature reflects systemic microvascular status and can be safely evaluated. Advanced retinal imaging technologies hold great promise for enhancing the clinical assessment of APS.
