Genomic origin and consequence of captive red junglefowl in zoological parks
Authors: Yuki Matsuda, Masaki Eda, Shuichi Ito, Tsuyoshi Shimmura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding the genetic status of captive animal populations is essential for their long-term conservation and effective management. In this study, we investigated the genetic origin, admixture patterns, and genomic health of red junglefowls (Gallus gallus; RJFs) maintained in zoological parks (zooRJFs). We conducted whole-genome sequencing of 27 zooRJFs and performed comparative population genomic analyses with wild RJFs and domesticated chicken breeds. Our analyses revealed that zooRJFs originated from Gallus gallus spadiceus. We also found that zooRJFs have undergone severe population bottlenecks, resulting in reduced genetic diversity and extremely long runs of homozygosity. These results highlight the value of genomic approaches for tracing ancestry and assessing genetic health in zoo-managed populations and provide insights into genome-informed breeding strategies for conserving captive animals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12366-3.

Background

The maintenance of wild animals in zoological parks plays an important role not only in public exhibition but also in species conservation and environmental education [1–3]. However, zoo populations often face major challenges [3]. For example, the taxonomic or geographic origins of individuals are sometimes uncertain due to the lack of traceable provenance records [4, 5]. Additionally, prolonged maintenance in small population sizes leads to a reduction in genetic diversity, increasing the risk of inbreeding depression [6, 7]. Recent advances in genomics offer powerful tools to assess genetic relatedness and diversity in captive populations, providing crucial information for population management and conservation planning [8, 9].

Red junglefowls (Gallus gallus; RJF) are thought to be the primary wild ancestor of chickens [10–14], which are the most popular poultry in the world. Although their natural habitat ranges across Southeast Asia, southern China, and India [13], several zoological parks maintain RJFs for exhibition, conservation, and research. These captive RJFs in zoological parks (zooRJFs) isolated from their natural habitats represent unique reservoirs of genetic resources and genomic diversity. In particular, the wild ancestral genetic resources of domesticated species are valuable to understanding the origin and trajectories of animal domestication [15].

However, these zooRJFs face challenges common to many other captive animal populations. First, the history of these populations prior to their introduction into zoological parks is unclear, and the true geographic origins of these birds remain unknown. Moreover, recent studies have reported introgression of domestic chicken alleles into wild RJF populations [16–18], raising the possibility that zooRJFs may not represent genetically pure RJFs, but rather “wild-looking” domestic chickens. In addition, zooRJFs are maintained in relatively small population sizes, which may lead to a reduction in genetic diversity and inbreeding depression [6, 7].

Here, we performed whole-genome sequencing of zooRJFs and conducted population genetic analyses with wild and domesticated populations to investigate the genetic origin, admixture patterns, and genomic health of zooRJFs. Our analyses revealed that zooRJFs are primarily derived from the wild RJF subspecies spadiceus, with evidence of possible domestic admixture. We further characterized the genomic consequences of captive maintenance, identifying a pattern of a few but long runs of homozygosity (ROH), indicative of historical bottlenecks and inbreeding. These findings underscore the utility of genomic approaches in informing the management and conservation of captive populations.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and genome sequencing

A total of 27 RJF samples from 4 zoological parks in Japan were collected opportunistically during routine vet care by vets and staff or from existing collections (Fig. 1). Sample details are provided in Supplementary Table S1. For samples of Tama Zoological Park, iZoo, and Kumamoto City Zoological and Botanical Gardens, blood samples were collected from the wing vein with heparin as an anticoagulant. For samples of Maruyama Zoo, muscle samples were collected from the breast muscles (No euthanasia was performed, as all sampled individuals had died of natural causes). All samples were stored at −20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{}^{\circ\:}$$\end{document}C until DNA extraction. DNA was extracted using the DNeasy blood and tissue kit (QIAGEN) according to the standard protocol of the kit. Library preparation and sequencing were conducted in BGI (https://www.bgi.com/global) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads in a DNBSEQ sequencer. Sequencing was performed until obtaining approximately 10 Gb clean reads with a coverage of ~ 10× for each sample (see Supplementary Table S1).

Read mapping and variant calling

The quality of the raw reads was checked and controlled using fastp 0.23.4 [19]. The read pairs containing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\ge\:30\text{\%}$$\end{document} of bases with base quality \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Q\le\:30$$\end{document} were removed. The mapping of clean reads and variant calling was performed under the same criteria as our previous study [20]. Briefly, we used BWA-MEM algorithm [21] in Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) version 0.7.17 to map the reads against the Gallus gallus reference genome (bGalGal1.mat.broiler.GRCg7b, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_016699485.2/). The duplicated reads were marked using SAMtools version 1.18 [22]. Variant calling was performed using “mpileup” command in BCFtools version 1.18 [23] with “-vm” option [24]. Sites with QUAL >30 and MAPQ >30 were subsampled for the following analysis.

Population structure and phylogenetic analysis

For the population structure analysis, BCFtools and PLINK 1.9 beta [25] were used to subsample the unlinked biallelic SNPs. Sites with minor allele frequencies less than 0.01 or missing call rates more than 0.1 were removed. The linkage disequilibrium of variants was pruned with the parameter “--indep-pairwise 150 50 0.2”. The pruned 3,072,724 variants were used for principal component analysis (PCA) using PLINK. We also ran ADMIXTURE v1.3 [26] at K = 1–10 using the same variant set and calculated the cross-validation error by default parameters.

For the phylogenetic analysis, we used 2,964,076 autosomal variants by excluding SNPs on sex chromosomes and mitochondria from pruned variants using PLINK with “--not-chr NC_052571.1, NC_052572.1, NC_053523.1” arguments. First, we constructed the neighbor-joining tree to infer the phylogeny of individuals using RapidNJ [27]. Subsequently, we used TreeMix v1.13 [28] to estimate the historical relationships and introgression events between populations. We ran TreeMix with 1 to 8 migration edges and 5 iterations for each migration edge. The optimal number of migration edges was selected based on the rate of change in log-likelihood using the OptM R package [29].

Population genetic statistics

To investigate the genetic diversity and signature of population bottlenecks, we used VCFtools v0.1.16 [30] to calculate the following genetic statistics. For each population, the nucleotide diversity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varPi\:$$\end{document}) and Tajima’s D were calculated for non-overlapping 50 kbp windows across the genome. To avoid the effect of a small sample size, G. gallus bankiva (n = 2) was excluded from these analyses.

We also calculated Wright’s inbreeding coefficient F using PLINK by comparing the difference between observed and expected autosomal homozygous loci. To avoid the influence of uneven numbers of missing genotypes caused by sequencing depth, we removed all the missing sites when calculating the inbreeding coefficient.

We detected the Runs of homozygosity (ROH) using PLINK. Along with the previous studies [31–34], we used the following parameters to define ROH: the minimum count of SNPs in a ROH was 50 (“--homozyg-snp 50”); the minimum length of an ROH was 300 kbp (“--homozyg-kb 300”); the minimum SNP density was 1 SNP per 50 kbp (“--homozyg-density 50”); the maximum gap between two consecutive SNPs was 1000 kbp (“--homozyg-gap 1000”); the minimum sliding window size was 50 SNPs (“--homozyg-window-snp 50”); the maximum number of heterozygous calls per window was 3 (“--homozyg-window-het 3”); the maximum number of missing calls per window was 5 (“--homozyg-window-missing 5”); and the minimum rate in which a SNP was included in the total of sliding windows was 0.05 (“--homozyg-window-threshold 0.05”).

Additionally, to investigate the genomic regions that are differentiated in zooRJFs, we used VCFtools to calculate the FST and Tajima’s D for non-overlapping 50 kbp windows on autosomal chromosomes. Considering the population differentiation shown by PCA (Fig. 2a), zooRJFs (Kumamoto, Tama, and Maruyama) were the target populations, and FST were calculated against wild G. g. spadiceus populations. Population demographic history

We performed pairwise sequentially Markovian coalescent (PSMC) [35] analysis to estimate the population demographic changes of each bird population. For each individual, the FASTA file of the consensus genome was generated from the VCF files using BCFtools “consensus” command. We ran the PSMC program version 0.6.5 with following parameters: “-N25 -t15 -r5 -p 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 25*2 + 4 + 6” [36]. Along with the previous studies [37–39], the mutation rate of Gallus gallus, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mu\:=1.91\times\:{10}^{-9}$$\end{document} per year, was used to convert population size to absolute time (as “years ago”). The difference in estimated current population size among groups was tested using one-way ANOVA, followed by pairwise t-tests. The raw p-values were adjusted using Benjamini and Hochberg’s method [40].

Results

Population structure and phylogenetic relationships

Population structure analysis distinguished zooRJFs from other RJFs or chicken breeds. The genetic principal components separated some populations (Fig. 2a and b). PC1 and PC2 separated the zooRJFs from wild RJFs and other chicken breeds (Fig. 2a). RJFs sampled in iZoo were exceptionally placed near the spadiceus, especially spadiceus in Singapore, and local chicken breeds of Thailand. PC2 distinguished wild RJFs into the subspecies. Among Gallus gallus subspecies, bankiva, gallus, and jabouillei were plotted distant from G. g. spadiceus and domesticated chicken breeds. While the zooRJFs were separated in PC1, the following PC2 and PC3 indicated that these populations are genetically close to spadiceus in Singapore (Fig. 2b). The result of the ADMIXTURE analysis was consistent with the PCA results (Fig. 2c). The most likely number of ancestry states, K = 2 (Fig. 2d), distinguished the zooRJFs excluding zooRJFs of iZoo from other RJFs and chicken breeds. This genetic cluster was consistent in each number of ancestry states (K = 3 and K = 4). These results indicate the genetic closeness of three populations of zooRJFs (zooRJFs in Kumamoto City Zoological and Botanical Gardens, Maruyama Zoo, and Tama Zoological Park).

These three populations of zooRJFs formed a monophyletic group in the neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2c). The other population of zooRJFs in iZoo was the same clade of wild RJFs of Singapore, and this clade was a sister clade of the other zooRJFs. In the maximum likelihood tree inferred by TreeMix, the zooRJFs were the paraphyletic group of the clade that includes G. g. spadiceus and commercial or local chicken breeds (Fig. 2f). The TreeMix analysis estimated the migration edges among populations. The most likely number of migrations was 2 (Fig. 2e), both of which estimated the genetic migration between the zooRJFs and domesticated chicken breeds. One migration edge was estimated from RJFs in iZoo to Thai indigenous chickens, and another migration edge was from RJFs in Kumamoto City Zoological and Botanical Gardens to White Leghorns (Fig. 2f).

Since PCA separated three zooRJF populations (RJFs of Kumamoto, Tama, and Maruyama) from other populations (Fig. 2a; Fig. S1a), we calculated FST for non-overlapping 50 kbp windows to detect highly differentiated genomic regions in these zooRJF populations. The FST scan revealed the pronounced genomic differentiation between the zooRJFs and other G. g. spadiceus populations (Fig. S1b). To test whether positive selection contributed these genomic differentiations, we calculated the Tajima’s D for the same windows (Fig. S1c). Genome-wide heterozygosity and patterns of runs of homozygosity

The inbreeding coefficient (F), which measures the difference between observed and expected autosomal homozygous, was different among populations (Fig. 4a). While the levels of F in commercial and local chicken breeds were at the same extent (> 0.5), that in wild RJFs were diverse. F in zooRJFs were relatively low and similar with G. g. spadiceus of Singapore.

To evaluate the extent of ROH accumulation among populations, we plotted the number and the total length of detected ROH segments (Fig. 4b). The distribution of the number of ROH and their total length was roughly separated by population groups. Among all populations, the number of ROH was small and the total ROH length was short in the wild RJFs and local chicken breeds. The commercial breeds had a large number of ROH segments and longer total ROH length than wild RJFs and local breeds. Notably, zooRJFs showed different distributions. The total lengths of ROH were similar with commercial chickens, however, the numbers of ROH segments were smaller than commercial breeds, which were similar with wild RJFs and local breeds. This suggests zooRJFs have fewer but longer ROH segments, and as expected, we observed that zooRJFs had long ROH segments (> 10 Mbp) more than other populations (Fig. 4c). Instead, these populations had a smaller number of short ROH segments (> 0.3 Mbp, < 1 Mbp). Wild RJFs and local breeds had similar ROH segment distributions across length classes. Commercial breeds tended to have more ROH segments in short and medium (> 1 Mbp, < 10 Mbp) length.

Discussion

Genetic origins of ZooRJFs

Our population genomic analysis elucidated the genomic origins of zooRJFs. Genomic principal components and ADMIXTURE analysis showed that the three zooRJF populations of Kumamoto City Zoological and Botanical Gardens, Maruyama Zoo, and Tama Zoological Park grouped together (Fig. 2a, c). The zooRJFs of iZoo were genetically close to RJFs, G. g. spadiceus, in Singapore, and all zooRJFs and wild RJFs in Singapore were clustered into a single clade in the neighbor-joining tree (Fig. 2a). This suggests that Japanese zooRJFs have a shared origin with G. g. spadiceus in the Singapore population. Since RJFs in Singapore were reportedly introduced from Johor, Malaysia [41, 42], it is reasonable to speculate that the zooRJFs in Japan may share ancestral origins with the populations from the Malaysia region.

The TreeMix analysis detected genetic introgression between zooRJFs and domesticated chicken breeds (Fig. 2f). To our knowledge, it was not recorded that the zooRJFs were crossbred with domesticated chickens after they were introduced in the zoological parks. Nevertheless, the domestic admixture was detected in zooRJFs, which might reflect the ancestral introgression events. Previous studies have reported domestic introgression in the RJFs in Singapore, and its extent varies across subpopulations [17, 18]. Taken together, our findings suggest that the Japanese zooRJFs likely originated from the G. g. spadiceus population, which had previously experienced introgression from domestic chickens in their native habitats.

Population bottlenecks shape the genomes of ZooRJFs

Population diversity metrics detected the evidence of population bottlenecks in the zooRJFs. The genome-wide averaged Tajima’s D was larger than 0 in all populations in this study (Fig. 3b). It is noteworthy that the averaged Tajima’s D in the zooRJFs were relatively high among all populations despite the varying levels of nucleotide diversity. The positive values of Tajima’s D reflect the sudden population bottlenecks [43, 44]. Thus, the observed pattern of genome-wide positive Tajima’s D suggests the decline of population size, especially in the zooRJFs.

The result of the PSMC analysis revealed the population dynamics of RJFs and chickens. Supporting the positive values of Tajima’s D, the effective population size has declined until the present day in any group of populations (Fig. 3c). The estimated population sizes were also smaller than wild ancestors (Fig. 3d). Together with the higher values of genome-wide averaged Tajima’s D, this is likely to indicate that zooRJFs experienced a severe population bottleneck when they were established as captive populations.

The FST analysis identified genomic regions contributing to the genetic differentiation of zooRJFs (Fig. S1b). Most of these genomic regions showed moderate Tajima’s D values (within mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:$$\end{document} 3 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:$$\end{document} SD). This suggests that the observed genetic differences were mainly shaped under a neutral evolutionary process within the zooRJF populations. In contrast, a few genomic regions were outliers of the genome-wide Tajima’s D distribution. TDP2 was in the top outlier window with a positive Tajima’s D, suggesting that the gene was under balancing selection. TP53BP1, IQGAP3, and TUBGCP4 were among the top outlier, suggesting the signatures of positive selection. TDP2 and TP53BP1 are both involved in DNA repair pathways [45, 46], while TUBGCP4 is reported to be linked to microcephaly, a condition associated with decreased visual acuity and cognitive impairment [47]. Currently, the functional consequences of these genetic differences and the circumstances under which these genes have been subject to selection remain underexplored. Elucidating the dynamics of evolution under captive conditions may provide insights into the early stages of animal domestication [48].

Genomic health of ZooRJFs characterized by recent isolation

The inbreeding coefficient measured by genome-wide heterozygosity was a relatively low level in the zooRJFs (Fig. 4a). Considering the small current population size of the zoological park, these low inbreeding levels are unexpected. One possible explanation is that insufficient generations have passed since the foundation of these zooRJF populations because inbreeding is known to accumulate over generations [49]. Alternatively, the unexpectedly high heterozygosity may reflect the ancestral introgression from domestic chickens, as discussed above. Population admixture leads to high heterozygosity [50, 51], and thus the inbreeding coefficients observed in zooRJFs may reflect heterozygosity that was already restored through admixture in their ancestral populations. In contrast, the domesticated chicken breeds—both local and commercial breeds—showed relatively high inbreeding coefficients. This might reflect the breeding for many generations in a small and closed population in commercial lines. The linked selection of production-related phenotypes, such as meat and egg yield during domestication, would also lead to the high inbreeding coefficient [52].

The genome-wide accumulation of ROH segments characterized each population group. Most of wild RJFs and local chicken breeds reserve a smaller number of ROH segments (< 400), and thus the total length of ROHs was short (< 300 Mbp). In contrast, commercial chicken breeds possessed a larger number of ROH segments and longer total lengths of ROHs than wild RJFs and local breeds. These ranges of ROHs in wild RJFs and domesticated chickens are consistent with previous reports [33, 34]. Interestingly, the zooRJFs showed different patterns of ROH accumulation from wild RJFs, local, and commercial chicken breeds. While the number of ROH segments was the same extent as the wild RJFs and local breeds, the total length of ROHs was as long as commercial breeds. This unique pattern was likely due to the accumulation of long (>10 Mbp) ROH segments in the zooRJFs. Long ROHs are most likely derived from a recent ancestor, while shorter ones are from a more distant ancestor [53]. Therefore, the extremely long ROH segments in the zooRJFs suggests the recent population bottleneck from the wild ancestral population. It is notable that the zooRJFs accumulated long ROHs in the genome despite their low levels of inbreeding coefficient. This might be due to the small number of generations since the zoological park population was isolated from the wild ancestral population, and thus recombination has not occurred as much [54, 55]. These results are crucial for considering the preservation of small populations because a few but long ROH accumulations could lead to frequent occurrences of deleterious homozygotes [56, 57], resulting in inbreeding depression [58, 59]. Our study highlighted the importance of genomic information in breeding strategies of captive animals with small population sizes.

Conclusion

In this study, we used population genetic analyses to reveal the genomic origin and consequences of captive RJFs in zoological parks. Although the sample size was limited, we leveraged the whole-genome sequencing combined with individual-based genetic approaches and unveiled the genetic characteristics of zooRJFs shaped by the severe population bottleneck. Our results provide insight into the conservation of small populations to help breeding programs maintain the genomic health and genetic diversity.
