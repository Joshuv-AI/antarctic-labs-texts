A Metric Approach to Zero-Free Regions for L L -Functions
Nawapan Wattanawanichkul

Abstract
. For integers m , m ′ ≥ 1 m,m^{\prime}\geq 1 , let π \pi and π ′ \pi^{\prime} be cuspidal automorphic representations of GL ⁡ ( m ) \GL(m) and GL ⁡ ( m ′ ) \GL(m^{\prime}) , respectively. We present a new proof of zero-free regions for L ⁡ ( s , π ) L(s,\pi) and for L ⁡ ( s , π × π ′ ) L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime}) under the assumption that π , π ′ \pi,\pi^{\prime} or L ⁡ ( s , π × π ′ ) L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime}) is self-dual. Our approach builds on ideas of “pretentious” multiplicative functions due to Granville and Soundararajan (as presented by Koukoulopoulos) and the notion of a positive semi-definite family of automorphic L L -functions due to Lichtman and Pascadi.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Notation and motivation

In 1896, Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin, as part of their independent proofs of the prime number theorem, established that the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
is nonzero on
\Real(s)=1
. In 1899, de la Vallée Poussin further showed that

\zeta(s)
remains nonzero in a narrow region to the left of
\Real(s)=1
, where the width at height

t
is proportional to
(\log t)^{-1}
. Such a region is referred to as a
classical zero-free region
of
\zeta(s)
.

To explain their argument, we let
\sigma,t\in\mathbb{R}
and
s=\sigma+it
. For
\sigma>1
, we know that

{\zeta(\sigma+it)}^{-1}=\prod_{p}\left(1-{p^{-(\sigma+it)}}\right).

(1.1)

For
t\neq 0
, suppose that
\zeta(1+it)=0
. By analyticity of
\zeta(s)
, we must have that for some
a\in\mathbb{C}
,
\zeta(\sigma+it)\sim a(\sigma-1)
as
\sigma\to 1^{+}
.
The only way that the product in (
1.1
) can tend to infinity as
\sigma\to 1^{+}
is when
p^{-it}
frequently points towards
-1
. Consequently,
p^{-2it}
must often point towards
1
. Thus, via (
1.1
),
\zeta(s)
should have a pole at
1+2it
, which is impossible as
\zeta(s)
has only one simple pole at
s=1
but
t\neq 0
.

Motivated by the theory of “pretentious” multiplicative functions developed by Granville and Soundararajan (see
[
5
]
), Koukoulopoulos in
[
16
, Theorem 8.3]
formalized the idea of Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin through the following family of metrics. For any positive integer
n
, let
f(n)
and
g(n)
be multiplicative functions taking values in the unit circle. For any
\sigma>1
, he defined

{\mathbb{D}}_{\sigma}(f,g)^{2}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{|f(p^{k})-g(p^{k})|^{2}\log p}{p^{k\sigma}},

(1.2)

where
\sigma
serves as a parameter to be optimized later.

Let
\mu(n)
be the Möbius function. Koukoulopoulos considered the triangle inequality

{\mathbb{D}}_{\sigma}(n^{-i\gamma},n^{i\gamma})\leq{\mathbb{D}}_{\sigma}(n^{-i\gamma},\mu(n))+{\mathbb{D}}_{\sigma}(\mu(n),n^{i\gamma})=2{\mathbb{D}}_{\sigma}(\mu(n),n^{i\gamma}),

which implies, after squaring both sides, that

{\mathbb{D}}_{\sigma}(n^{-i\gamma},n^{i\gamma})^{2}\leq 4{\mathbb{D}}_{\sigma}(\mu(n),n^{i\gamma})^{2}.

(1.3)

The above inequality rigorously demonstrates that if
p^{i\gamma}
often points towards
-1
, then
p^{2i\gamma}
frequently points towards
1
. Each distance in (
1.3
) can be expressed as a linear combination of the logarithmic derivatives of
L
-functions, up to an additive constant
O(1)
. Specifically, (
1.3
) can be rewritten as

0\leq-3\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(\sigma)-4\Real\Big(\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(\sigma+i\gamma)\Big)-\Real\Big(\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{\zeta}(\sigma+2i\gamma)\Big)+O(1),

which recasts the
3-4-1
argument by Mertens.

Let
F
be a number field with adele ring
\mathbb{A}_{F}
,

\mathcal{O}_{F}
the ring of integers of
F
, and
\mathrm{N}=\mathrm{N}_{F/\mathbb{Q}}
the norm defined on nonzero ideals
\mathfrak{a}
of
\mathcal{O}_{F}
by
\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{a}=|\mathcal{O}_{F}/\mathfrak{a}|
.
Let
\mathfrak{F}_{m}
denote the set of cuspidal automorphic representations
\pi
of
\GL_{m}(\mathbb{A}_{F})
whose central character
\omega_{\pi}
is unitary. For convenience, we also define

\mathfrak{F}_{m}^{*}:=\{\pi\in\mathfrak{F}_{m}:\omega_{\pi}\text{ is trivial on the diagonally embedded positive reals.}\}.

(1.4)

In this paper, we restrict ourselves to the case where
\pi\in\mathfrak{F}_{m}^{*}
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{F}_{m^{\prime}}^{*}
and adapt the family of metrics in (
1.2
) to establish zero-free regions for
L(s,\pi)
and
L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime})
.

1.2.
Statements of results

Recall the notation in the introduction and refer to Section
2
for more discussion of
L
-functions.
Let
\pi\in\mathfrak{F}_{m}^{*}
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{F}_{m^{\prime}}^{*}
, and let
\mathfrak{C}(\pi)\geq 3
denote the analytic conductor of
\pi
. We now present the main theorems of this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
m\geq 1
be an integer and
\pi\in\mathfrak{F}^{*}_{m}
. Let
\sigma,t\in\mathbb{R}
and
s=\sigma+it
. Then
L(s,\pi)
has no zero in the region

\displaystyle\sigma\geq 1-\frac{1}{33(2m+3)\log(\mathfrak{C}(\pi)(3+|t|)^{m[F:\mathbb{Q}]})},

except possibly for one simple real zero, in which case
\pi
is self-dual. Moreover, when
\pi
is self-dual, the exponent
m[F:\mathbb{Q}]
of
3+|t|
can be reduced to
[F:\mathbb{Q}]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
m,m^{\prime}\geq 1
be integers, and let
\pi\in\mathfrak{F}^{*}_{m}
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{F}^{*}_{m^{\prime}}
.
Let
\sigma,t\in\mathbb{R}
and
s=\sigma+it
. If
\pi\neq\widetilde{\pi}
,
\pi^{\prime}=\widetilde{\pi}^{\prime}
, and

\displaystyle\sigma\geq 1-\frac{1}{28(m+m^{\prime})\log(\mathfrak{C}(\pi)\mathfrak{C}(\pi^{\prime})(3+|t|)^{m[F:\mathbb{Q}]})},

then
L(\sigma+it,\pi\times\pi^{\prime})\neq 0
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
m,m^{\prime}\geq 1
be integers, and let
\pi\in\mathfrak{F}^{*}_{m}
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{F}^{*}_{m^{\prime}}
.
Let
\sigma,t\in\mathbb{R}
and
s=\sigma+it
. If
L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime})=L(s,\widetilde{\pi}\times\widetilde{\pi}^{\prime})
, then

L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime})
has at most one zero (necessarily real and simple) in the region

\sigma\geq 1-\frac{1}{66(m+m^{\prime})\log(\mathfrak{C}(\pi)\mathfrak{C}(\pi^{\prime})(3+|t|)^{m[F:\mathbb{Q}]})}.

1.3.
Novelty of the present work

We now explain our motivation for adapting the metric in (
1.2
) and highlight the novelty of our work. As in the case of
\zeta(s)
, to prove a zero-free region for
L(s,\pi)
, we assume it has a zero at
s=1+it
with
t\neq 0
. Let
\lambda_{\pi}(\mathfrak{n})
be the Dirichlet coefficient of
L(s,\pi)
. For a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
, we expect
\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}^{-it}
to often point toward
-\lambda_{\pi}(\mathfrak{p})
, suggesting the study of

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{\pi}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\log\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}}{\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}^{ks}},

(1.5)

for
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1
, which arises naturally from (
1.2
).

Assuming progress towards the generalized Ramanujan conjecture (GRC), the sum in (
1.5
) can be approximated by
-(L^{\prime}/L)(s,\pi)+O(1)
, where the implied constant depends on
m
. A similar approximation holds for
L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime})
, but now requires progress towards GRC that is not yet proved in full generality to handle prime powers. To avoid this, we use the observation that the Dirichlet coefficient of
-(L^{\prime}/L)(s,\pi)
at a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
, denoted
a_{\pi}(\mathfrak{p})
, equals
\lambda_{\pi}(\mathfrak{p})
. Thus, instead of (
1.5
), it is more natural to study

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{\pi}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\log\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}}{\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}^{ks}},

(1.6)

where
\mathrm{Re}(s)>1
, which leads naturally to the modified metric in (
3.1
). This shift in perspective not only circumvents the need for progress towards GRC, but also underpins our new approach to proving zero-free regions for both standard and Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions. In addition, our approach improves upon earlier results in several aspects:

(1)

Brumley
[
9
, Theorem A.1]
was the first to establish a classical zero-free region for
L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime})
under the assumption that at least one of
\pi
or
\pi^{\prime}
is self-dual. However, his result does not include an explicit constant and exhibits a weaker dependence on the degrees of
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
compared to Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. Goldfeld and Li
[
4
, Theorem 1.2]
, in contrast, established a zero-free region for
L(s,\pi\times\widetilde{\pi})
of the form

\sigma>1-\frac{c}{(\log(|t|+2))^{5}},

which is narrower than the region in Theorem
1.3
and is proven only in the
t
-aspect.
Extending upon their work, Humphries and Brumley
[
9
, Theorem 1.9]
employed sieve methods to widen the Goldfeld–Li region to a zero-free region of the form

\sigma>1-\frac{c}{\log(|t|+2)}.

More recently, Humphries and Thorner
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
obtained an unconditional refinement of this region with improved uniformity in
\pi
. Along a parallel axiomatic line of inquiry, Leung
[
17
, Theorem 2.1]
established a classical zero-free region for a class of
L
-functions whose Dirichlet logarithms have non-negative coefficients. While this framework encompasses self-dual convolutions such as
L(s,\pi\times\widetilde{\pi})
, it does not apply, for example, to
L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime})
considered in Theorem
1.2
.

(2)

Theorem
1.3
is new, except in the cases where (i) both
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
are self-dual or (ii)
\pi^{\prime}=\widetilde{\pi}
, which have been previously established—for instance, by Brumley
[
9
, Theorem A.1]
and Moreno
[
19
, Theorem 3.3]
in the former case, and by Humphries–Thorner
[
10
, Theorem 2.1]
in the latter.
Furthermore, taking
\pi^{\prime}=\mathbbm{1}
,
Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
together imply Theorem
1.1
.

(3)

The uniformity in
m
and
m^{\prime}
in Theorems
1.1
–
1.3
improves upon or matches all prior results. Degree dependence is crucial in certain problems in analytic number theory. For instance, in
[
24
]
, an effective version of the Sato–Tate conjecture for holomorphic cuspidal newforms relies on uniform prime number theorems for the
L
-functions of their symmetric
m
-th powers, for all
m
up to some integer
M
. The quality of the error term depends on the choice of
M
, which in turn relies on the degree dependence of zero-free regions for these
L
-functions.

(4)

An alternative proof of Theorem
1.1
in
[
12
, Theorem 5.10]
requires a non-negative product of
L
-functions where the multiplicity of a zero
\rho
in a certain region exceeds the number of poles at
s=1
. Such constructions could be difficult without the notion of isobaric sums, which inherently produces a non-negative linear combination of logarithmic derivatives of
L
-functions with a square number of terms.
Our method, in contrast, yields a different range of non-negative linear combinations.
For example, it recovers Mertens’
3-4-1
argument, which clearly has
8
terms. Our approach, which extends the method in
[
16
, Theorem 8.3]
, thus brings the standard and Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions settings in line with the approach of Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin.

(5)

The proof in
[
12
, Theorem 5.10]
requires a minor adjustment due to an oversight in
[
12
, Lemma 5.9]
. Specifically, their argument accounts only for the contribution from the poles of the non-negative product of
L
-function under consideration at
s=1
but overlooks potential contributions from poles at
s=1+i\gamma
for some
\gamma\neq 0
, which can be significant when
\gamma
is small. We address this issue and provide a more complete analysis in Section
4.1.2
.

1.4.
Outline of the metric proof architecture

Our strategy for establishing a zero-free region for the Rankin–Selberg
L
-function
L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime})
is based on a metric framework that systematically replaces the non-negative trigonometric functions utilized in classical proofs.

(1)

First, we introduce our family of distances in (
3.1
), which is a modified version of Koukoulopoulos’s family of “pretentious” distances in (
1.2
). In this variant, the usual Dirichlet coefficients of
L
-functions are replaced by those coming from the logarithmic derivative of the
L
-function. This reformulation helps eliminate any dependence on unproven progress toward GRC.

(2)

Second, we invoke the triangle inequality for a suitable choice of elements within our family. From an inequality of the form
d_{1}\leq d_{2}+d_{3}
, we obtain the equivalent expression
0\leq d_{2}+d_{3}-d_{1}
, which yields a non-negative linear combination of logarithmic derivatives of
L
-functions. This construction serves as a direct analogue of the classical
3-4-1
trigonometric identity. When the level of
\pi
or
\pi^{\prime}
exceeds
1
, i.e.,
\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{q}_{\pi}>1
or
\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{q}_{\pi^{\prime}}>1
, the ramified primes contribute additional terms to this linear combination, but they can be controlled using Lemma
3.2
.

(3)

Let
\rho=\beta+i\gamma
be a nontrivial zero of
L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime})
. By applying Lemma
3.1
to our previously obtained linear combination and isolating the contribution of
\beta
, we arrive at an inequality of the form

0\leq\frac{c_{P}}{\sigma-1}-\frac{c_{Z}}{\sigma-\beta}+E(\pi,\pi^{\prime},\gamma),

(1.7)

for some constants
c_{P},c_{Z}\geq 0
and an error term
E(\pi,\pi^{\prime},\gamma)
that depends on
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
, and
\gamma
. A classical zero-free region then follows, provided that our choice of elements in the triangle inequality ensures that

0\leq c_{P}<c_{Z}.

(1.8)

This final step follows standard techniques, paralleling the treatments in
[
16
,
12
]
.

As an alternative approach to managing the ramified prime contributions, in Section
5
we utilize the framework of positive semi-definite families of Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions introduced by Lichtman and Pascadi
[
2
]
. This machinery yields the same non-negative linear combination of logarithmic derivatives of
L
-functions while entirely eliminating the appearance of ramified prime terms. Consequently, this alternative path provides the clean, classical zero-free regions presented in Theorems
1.1
–
1.3
.

1.5.
Limitations of the metric approach

For a general Rankin–Selberg
L
-function
L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime})
without self-duality assumptions, establishing a classical zero-free region unconditionally remains a formidable open problem. Suppose that
\pi
,
\pi^{\prime}
, and
L(s,\pi\times\pi^{\prime})
are all non-self-dual. If we implement our metric framework as described in Section
1.4
, we encounter a structural barrier showing that a zero-free region in this asymmetric setting cannot be obtained, regardless of the choice of triangle inequality.
In fact, every linear combination arising from an arbitrary triangle inequality can be shown to satisfy
c_{P}\geq c_{Z}
. It follows that the criterion in (
1.8
) is unattainable, and therefore this framework is incapable of producing a zero-free region.
See Section
4.4
for more details.

Nevertheless, the limitations of the metric framework in this asymmetric setting suggests a natural direction for future inquiry. Specifically, one might exploit the framework of Lichtman and Pascadi
[
2
]
, as presented in Section
5
, which generates a broader family of non-negative linear combinations of logarithmic derivatives than our metric approach. Although it is far from immediate, the answer to whether their framework can yield a classical zero-free region in the absence of self-duality is ultimately negative. We will address this question in full detail in a forthcoming work.

Alternatively, several recent works have established non-classical zero-free regions for general Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions within modern frameworks. For instance, Harcos and Thorner
[
6
]
, inspired by Siegel’s ineffective lower bounds for Dirichlet
L
-functions, obtained a new type of zero-free region for all
\mathrm{GL}(1)
-twists of
\mathrm{GL}(n)\times\mathrm{GL}(n^{\prime})
convolutions. More recently, they refined this framework in
[
7
]
to establish a full Tatuzawa-type analogue for these twisted Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions, thereby rendering the underlying constants effectively computable up to a single possible exceptional twist.

Standard
L

If
\pi\in\mathfrak{F}_{m}^{*}

\pi_{v}

\pi_{v}

\pi

\otimes_{v}\pi_{v}

\mathfrak{p}

\pi_{v}

\mathfrak{p}

m

(\alpha_{j,\pi}(\mathfrak{p}))_{j=1}^{m}

L

L(s,\pi_{\mathfrak{p}})=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{j,\pi}(\mathfrak{p})\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}^{-s}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{\pi}(\mathfrak{p}^{j})}{\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}^{js}}.

L

L(s,\pi)

\pi

L(s,\pi)=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}}L(s,\pi_{\mathfrak{p}})=\sum_{\mathfrak{n}}\frac{\lambda_{\pi}(\mathfrak{n})}{\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{n}^{s}},

\mathrm{Re}(s)>1
.
If
v\mid\infty

m

\mu_{1,\pi}(v),\mu_{2,\pi}(v),\ldots,\mu_{m,\pi}(v)\in\mathbb{C}

\Gamma_{v}(s)=\begin{cases}\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s)=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)&\mbox{if $F_{v}=\mathbb{R}$,}\\
\Gamma_{\mathbb{C}}(s)=2(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)&\mbox{if $F_{v}=\mathbb{C}$.}\end{cases}

(2.1)

Luo, Rudnick, and Sarnak
[
18
]

[
20
]

\theta_{m}\in[0,\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{m^{2}+1}]
such that we have the uniform bounds

|\alpha_{j,\pi}(\mathfrak{p})|\leq\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}^{\theta_{m}},\qquad\mathbb{R}(\mu_{j,\pi}(v))\geq-\theta_{m},

(2.2)

and the generalized Ramanujan conjecture asserts that one may take
\theta_{m}=0
.

Let
\mathfrak{q}_{\pi}
denote the conductor of
\pi
, and let
\mathbbm{1}
denote the trivial representation (whose
L
-function is the Dedekind zeta function
\zeta_{F}(s)

\delta_{\pi}=1

\widetilde{\pi}\in\mathfrak{F}_{m}

\Lambda(s,\pi)=(s(1-s))^{\delta_{\pi}}(D_{F}^{m}\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{q}_{\pi})^{s/2}L_{\infty}(s,\pi)L(s,\pi)

is entire of order

.
There exists
W(\pi)\in\mathbb{C}
of modulus

such that
\Lambda(s,\pi)
satisfies

\Lambda(s,\pi)=W(\pi)\Lambda(1-s,\widetilde{\pi})=W(\pi)\overline{\Lambda(1-\overline{s},\pi)}.

Since
\{\alpha_{j,\widetilde{\pi}}(\mathfrak{p})\}=\{\overline{\alpha_{j,\pi}(\mathfrak{p})}\}

\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{q}_{\widetilde{\pi}}=\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{q}_{\pi}

, we have that
L(s,\widetilde{\pi})=\overline{L(\overline{s},\pi)}
. If
v\mid\infty
, we define
\mathfrak{C}_{v}(\pi)=\mathfrak{C}_{v}(0,\pi)

\mathfrak{C}_{v}(it,\pi)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(|\mu_{j,\pi}(v)+it|+3)^{[F_{v}:\mathbb{R}]}.

The analytic conductor is given by
\mathfrak{C}(\pi)=\mathfrak{C}(0,\pi)
, where

\mathfrak{C}(it,\pi)=D_{F}^{m}\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{q}_{\pi}\prod_{v\mid\infty}\mathfrak{C}_{v}(it,\pi).

Lastly, we define
a_{\pi}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})
by the Dirichlet series identity for any
\Real(s)>1

-\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,\pi)=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j,\pi}(\mathfrak{p})^{k}\log\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}}{\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}^{ks}}=\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{\pi}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\log\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}}{\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}^{ks}}.

2.2.
Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions

Let
\pi\in\mathfrak{F}_{m}^{*}
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{F}_{m^{\prime}}^{*}
. Let
\delta_{\pi\times\pi^{\prime}}=1
if
\pi^{\prime}=\widetilde{\pi}
and
\delta_{\pi\times\pi^{\prime}}=0
otherwise. For
\mathfrak{p}\nmid\mathfrak{q}_{\pi}\mathfrak{q}_{\pi^{\prime}}
, define

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\delta_{1}a_{\pi_{1}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}^{ik\gamma_{1}}-\delta_{2}a_{\pi_{2}}(\mathfrak{p}^{k})\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}^{ik\gamma_{2}}|^{2}\log\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}}{\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{p}^{k\sigma}}},

(1)

Nonnegativity

Symmetry:

(3)

Triangle inequality:
The inequality

4.4.
Analysis of the metric limitation

We now extend our discussion from Section
1.5
by demonstrating that when
\pi
,
\pi^{\prime}
, and

are non-self-dual, this metric framework cannot yield a classical zero-free region. Specifically, we explain why the criterion (

For

(4.43)

Assume for simplicity that
\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{q}_{\pi}=\mathrm{N}\mathfrak{q}_{\pi^{\prime}}=1
. Then
\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\pi_{3}
must also have level 1; otherwise, their corresponding Rankin–Selberg convolutions cannot be isomorphic to

Applying the identity (
3.7
) to (

+2\delta_{1}\delta_{3}\Real\Big(\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(\sigma+(\gamma_{3}-\gamma_{1})i,\pi_{1}\times\widetilde{\pi}_{3})\Big)+2\delta_{2}\delta_{3}\Real\Big(\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(\sigma+(\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{3})i,\pi_{3}\times\widetilde{\pi}_{2})\Big)\\

Crucially, if any two of the convolutions

Definition 5.1

If

We note that the proof of this lemma closely follows
[
9

and

\oplus\varphi_{i}
and
\oplus\varphi_{j}^{\prime}

and
\varphi^{\prime}_{j}

of
F_{v}
. By definition, we have

L(s,\pi_{v})=\prod_{i}L(s,\varphi_{i}),\quad L(s,\pi_{v}^{\prime})=\prod_{j}L(s,\varphi_{j}^{\prime}),\quad L(s,\pi_{v}\times\pi_{v}^{\prime})=\prod_{i,j}L(s,\varphi_{i}\times\varphi^{\prime}_{j}).

This factorization also applies to the associated conductors. Let
d_{i},d^{\prime}_{j}
denote the dimensions of
\varphi_{i}
and
\varphi^{\prime}_{j}
, respectively, so that
n=\sum d_{i}
and

and

\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
of
W_{F_{v}}
, of respective dimensions
d
and
d^{\prime}
, we have

\mathfrak{C}_{v}(it,\varphi\otimes\varphi^{\prime})\leq\mathfrak{C}_{v}(\varphi)^{d^{\prime}}\mathfrak{C}_{v}(\varphi^{\prime})^{d}(|t|+3)^{dd^{\prime}[F_{v}:\mathbb{R}]}.

(A.1)

When
F_{v}=\mathbb{C}
, the Weil group is given by
W_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, implying that all irreducible representations are one-dimensional. Any such character can be expressed as
\chi_{k,v}(z)=(z/|z|)^{k}|z|^{2\nu}
, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
. Defining
\mu=\nu+|k|/2
, the corresponding
L
-factor is
\Gamma_{\mathbb{C}}(s+\mu)
(see
[
15
, (4.6)]
). According to the recipe in
[
11
, (21) and (31)]
, we obtain

\mathfrak{C}_{v}(it,\varphi)=(|\mu+it|+3)^{2}.

Now if
\varphi=\chi_{k,\nu}
and
\varphi=\chi_{k^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}
, then
\varphi\otimes\varphi^{\prime}=\chi_{k+k^{\prime},\nu+\nu^{\prime}}
. This leads to the bound

\mathfrak{C}_{v}(it,\varphi\otimes\varphi^{\prime})=\Big(\Big|\frac{|k+k^{\prime}|}{2}+\nu+\nu^{\prime}+it\Big|+3\Big)^{2}\leq\Big(\Big(\frac{|k|}{2}+|\nu|\Big)+\Big(\frac{|k^{\prime}|}{2}+|\nu^{\prime}|\Big)+|t|+3\Big)^{2}.

(A.2)

Next, we establish that
\frac{|k|}{2}+|\nu|\leq 3|\mu|+1
. This part of the proof in
[
9
, Appendix A.2]
is slightly incomplete, as the inequality preceding
[
9
, (A.13)]
fails when
k=1
. This issue was identified and corrected in
[
22
, P. 9]
. We present a complete proof of this part as follows.

(1)

If
k=0
, then
\frac{|k|}{2}+|\nu|\leq 3|\mu|+1
holds trivially.

(2)

If
|k|=1
, we have
\frac{|k|}{2}+|\nu|=|\nu|+\frac{1}{2}\leq|\nu+\frac{1}{2}|+1\leq 3|\nu+\frac{1}{2}|+1=3|\mu|+1
.

(3)

If
|k|\geq 2
, we recall
[
14
, Corollary 2.5]
, namely,
|\Real(\nu)|\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Since
\frac{|k|}{2}>1
, we have
|\Real(\nu)|\leq|\Real(\nu)+\frac{|k|}{2}|
, and also
2|\Real(\nu)+\frac{|k|}{2}|\geq\frac{|k|}{2}
. It follows that

|\nu|+\frac{|k|}{2}\leq\Big|\nu+\frac{|k|}{2}\Big|+\frac{|k|}{2}\leq\Big|\nu+\frac{|k|}{2}\Big|+2\Big|\Real(\nu)+\frac{|k|}{2}\Big|\leq 3\Big|\nu+\frac{|k|}{2}\Big|\leq 3|\mu|+1.

Thus we have shown that
\frac{|k|}{2}+|\nu|\leq 3|\mu|+1
. Substituting this into (
A.2
), we obtain

\mathfrak{C}_{v}(it,\varphi\otimes\varphi^{\prime})\leq(3|\mu|+3|\mu^{\prime}|+|t|+5)^{2}\leq(|\mu|+3)^{2}(|\mu^{\prime}|+3)^{2}(|t|+3)^{2},

which implies (
A.1
) in the case where
F_{v}=\mathbb{C}
.

When
F_{v}=\mathbb{R}
, each irreducible representation
\varphi
of
W_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{C}^{\times}\cup j\mathbb{C}^{\times}
has dimension 1 or 2.

•

If
\varphi
is one-dimensional, its restriction to
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
takes the form
\chi_{0,\nu}
for
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
(see
[
15
]
, (3.2)). Defining
k=1-\varphi(j)\in\{0,2\}
, we set
\mu=\nu+k/2
, yielding

L(s,\varphi)=\Gamma_{\mathbb{R}}(s+\mu),\qquad\mathfrak{C}_{v}(it,\varphi)=|\mu+it|+3.

•

If
\varphi
is two-dimensional, then it is the induction of
\chi_{k,\nu}
from
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
to
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, where
k\geq 1
and
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
. Setting
\mu=\nu+k/2
, we obtain

L(s,\varphi)=\Gamma_{\mathbb{C}}(s+\mu),\qquad\mathfrak{C}_{v}(it,\varphi)=(|\mu+it|+3)^{2}.

In both cases, let
(k,\nu)
and
(k^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})
be the parameters associated with
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
, respectively. We now analyze the tensor product parameters.

(1)

If both
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
are one-dimensional, then
\varphi\otimes\varphi^{\prime}
remains one-dimensional, with parameter
(1-\varphi(j)\varphi^{\prime}(j),\nu+\nu^{\prime})
. In this case, (
A.1
) is equivalent to

\Big|\frac{1-\varphi(j)\varphi^{\prime}(j)}{2}+\nu+\nu^{\prime}+it\Big|+3\leq\Big(\frac{k}{2}+|\nu|\Big)+\Big(\frac{k^{\prime}}{2}+|\nu^{\prime}|\Big)+|t|+3.

(A.3)

Since
1-\varphi(j)\varphi^{\prime}(j)\leq(1-\varphi(j))+(1-\varphi^{\prime}(j))=k+k^{\prime}
, we
can apply the same analysis as in the complex setting to obtain (
A.3
).

(2)

If
\varphi
is one-dimensional, and
\varphi^{\prime}
is irreducible and two-dimensional, then
\varphi\otimes\varphi^{\prime}
is irreducible and two-dimensional, induced from
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
by the character
\chi_{0,\nu}\chi_{k^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}=\chi_{k^{\prime},\nu+\nu^{\prime}}
. Thus
\varphi\otimes\varphi^{\prime}
has parameters
(k^{\prime},\nu+\nu^{\prime})
. Then (
A.1
) is equivalent to

(|\mu+\mu^{\prime}+it|+3)^{2}\leq(|\mu|+3)^{2}(|\mu^{\prime}|+3)^{2}(|t|+3)^{2},

which holds by the triangle inequality.

(3)

Let
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
be both irreducible and two-dimensional, and let
k\geq k^{\prime}
. Then
\varphi\otimes\varphi^{\prime}
is the direct sum of two two-dimensional representations, induced from
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
from the characters
\chi_{k,\nu}\chi_{k^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}=\chi_{k+k^{\prime},\nu+\nu^{\prime}}
and
\chi_{-k,-\nu}\chi_{k^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}=\chi_{k^{\prime}-k,\nu^{\prime}-\nu}
. (The latter representation is reducible when
k=k^{\prime}
.) This shows that

\displaystyle L(s,\varphi\otimes\varphi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{\mathbb{C}}(s+\mu+\mu^{\prime})\Gamma_{\mathbb{C}}(s+\mu-\mu^{\prime}),

and

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{v}(it,\varphi\otimes\varphi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(|\mu+\mu^{\prime}|+3)^{2}(|\mu-\mu^{\prime}|+3)^{2}.

Thus (
A.1
) is equivalent to

(|\mu+\mu^{\prime}+it|+3)^{2}(|\mu-\mu^{\prime}+it|+3)^{2}\leq(|\mu|+3)^{4}(|\mu^{\prime}|+3)^{4}(|t|+3)^{4},

which holds by the triangle inequality.

This completes the proof of Lemma
A.1
.
∎
