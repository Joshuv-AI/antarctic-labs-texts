Multiconfigurational Excitonic Couplings in Homo- and Heterodimer Stacks of Azobenzene-Derived Dyes
Authors: Razan
E Daoud, Roberto Cacciari, Luca De Vico
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMolecular excitons play a major role within dye aggregates and hold significant potential for (opto)electronic and photovoltaic applications. Numerous studies have documented alterations in the spectral properties of dye homoaggregates, but only limited work has been reported for heteroaggregates. In this article, dimeric dye stacks were constructed from azobenzene-like dyes with identical or distinct structures, and their excitonic features were computationally investigated. Our results show that strong exciton coupling is not limited to identical chromophores, as often assumed, based on a recently made available Frenkel Exciton Hamiltonian and multiconfigurational plus second-order perturbation theory energetics methodology. Heteroaggregate stacks were found to exhibit different absorption features from the corresponding interacting monomers, indicating considerable coupling interactions between units. We analyzed how such coupling may vary according to various aspects, such as the relative positions of the interacting monomers or the differences in their energetics. Such qualitative and semiquantitative analyses allow the evaluation of the excitonic behavior of these dye aggregates to encourage further efforts toward a deeper understanding of the excitonic properties of tailored dye heteroaggregate systems.

Introduction

The phenomenon of exciton coupling has garnered significant attention in the scientific discourse due to its fundamental importance in comprehending coherent energy transfer in natural light-harvesting systems,1−3 as well as its relevance in diverse functional aspects of organic dyes and semiconductors.4−6 These functional aspects are crucial for (opto-)electronic and photovoltaic applications.7−9 Furthermore, molecular exciton theory, initially pioneered by Davydov10,11 and Kasha12,13 six decades ago, serves as the fundamental framework for deducing structural and optical information regarding supramolecular arrangements in dye aggregates, such as H- and J-aggregates. Consequently, over the past few decades, a multitude of dye aggregates have been successfully designed, and substantial efforts have been dedicated to elucidating the optical characteristics and gaining a deeper understanding of the functional properties exhibited by dye aggregates based on exciton coupling.14−19

Nonetheless, the primary focus of these research efforts lies predominantly on molecular aggregates derived exclusively from identical dye molecules (i.e., homoaggregates). In contrast, investigations about heteroaggregates have rarely been reported as, for instance, new quantum information devices20,21 or systems designed for solar energy conversion.22−24 This in itself is surprising since the self-assembly of distinct chromophores into heteroaggregates can yield systems with distinctive properties that are unattainable with homoaggregates.25,26 The limited number of reported heteroaggregates might be due to challenges associated with their preparation in terms of predetermined size and orientation27 or because of the alleged common notion that exciton coupling between different chromophores is typically weak, and so, the alterations in the optical properties of the heterosystem are negligible.28 Conversely, compelling optical evidence has been reported for chromophores in closely π-stacked dye aggregates, as well as in mixed films comprising different dyes, which exhibited spectral deviations from the simple summation of absorption spectra of the individual interacting monomers owing to relatively strong electronic coupling.29−32

Azobenzene systems stand out as exemplary molecular entities within supramolecular dye assemblies, as they possess exceptional optical and electronic characteristics that make them highly suitable for diverse technical applications.33,34 Furthermore, their distinct self-assembly behavior, facilitated by similar molecular sizes and π–π stacking interactions, endows them with remarkable versatility as building blocks for constructing functional supramolecular assemblies.35,36

Azobenzene molecular aggregates have different properties than the single chromophore, e.g., a decreased photoisomerization quantum yield.37,38

A number of experimental and theoretical studies have been conducted to design and investigate different homoaggregates of azobenzene models. For instance, synthetic double-stranded DNA and RNA were used to scaffold parallel and antiparallel stacks of azobenzene derivatives, leading to observed narrowing and blue-shifting of the dimer absorption band (H-band) due to strong exciton coupling between the dye molecules.39,40 Hypsochromic effects have also been reported in the context of micellization of surfactants functionalized with azobenzene,41 as well as in self-assembled monolayers containing azobenzene compounds.42 From the theoretical and computational point of view, local and charge transfer contributions to the lowest electronic excitations in azobenzene dimers of different spatial dispositions were quantitatively assessed through quantum chemical calculations followed by transition density matrix analysis.43 The same approach was previously used to characterize the excited states and compute the exciton splitting of free and adsorbed 4-nitroazobenzene dimers.44

On the other hand, heteroclustering of azobenzene dyes has been exclusively reported in experimental studies wherein the spectroscopic behavior was systematically evaluated as a function of the difference in the absorption maximum wavelength of the constituting dyes.36,40 The observed spectral changes were partly explained using the molecular exciton theory of heterodimerization, which is essentially applied to predict the spectroscopic behavior of H- and J-aggregates of identical monomers.12,13 The observed discrepancies could be attributed to limitations imposed by the point dipole approximation used in the conventional molecular exciton theory because these asymmetric dye systems are firmly stacked into heterodimers and cannot be approximated as mere “points.” Therefore, a significant gap remains between theory and experimental findings, which requires the implementation of an improved model to bridge it.

Within the context of Frenkel’s exciton theory,45,46 aggregate optical properties are mainly dependent on the intermolecular Coulomb coupling between neighboring chromophores (e.g., Coulomb interaction of electronic transition densities), thus accounting for molecular packing. In practice, the point dipole approximation is commonly used to approximate the Coulomb coupling between molecules.47 However, such approximation breaks down when the distance between monomers becomes comparable to their molecular size, and it becomes necessary to describe the strong Coulomb interactions more accurately.48 In such cases, the Frenkel Exciton Hamiltonian (FEH), also known as the Frenkel–Davydov Hamiltonian, is built using coupling terms that are more accurate than those obtained through a point dipole approximation.

Several approaches have been proposed to evaluate such terms with varying degrees of accuracy, such as the atomic transition charge model,49 the line-dipole approximation for conjugated polymers,50 the density cube method,51 up to supermolecular calculations considering the whole aggregate.2,52 Recently, we presented a novel and efficient method to compute the Coulomb couplings through the calculations of collective electronic excited states of the aggregate system using a multiconfigurational wave function description for the individual monomers and their corresponding local transition densities.53 This method is implemented in the OpenMolcas program,54 and its performance was evaluated by example applications on homo- and heteroaggregates, in which multiconfigurational character and double excitations play a vital role. The investigated heterodimer, in that case, comprised two parallel distinct trans-azobenzene monomers, which represents the starting point for our current study on the exciton coupling in azobenzene-based homo- and heteroaggregates. The dimeric computed spectrum was red-shifted with respect to the monomeric peaks, in line with the experiment. However, the quality of the computed wave functions and, so, the energetics of the interacting monomers needed to be improved by optimizing the description of the active space and the number of states in the multistate calculations.

Keeping these considerations in mind, in the present work, the FEH code is exploited to investigate the photochemical and photophysical properties for a set of homo- and heterodimers of trans-azobenzene-derived models, exploiting an accurate description of the monomers’ energetics computed using a multiconfigurational plus second-order perturbation theory method. New insights on the excitonic behavior of these systems were obtained while avoiding any approximations within the Frenkel exciton formalism, which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously applied to heterodimeric aggregates, other than for application examples.54

In particular, we studied, computationally, all possible homo- and heterodimers of the following molecular models: trans-azobenzene (A), trans-4′-dimethylaminoazobenzene (M), trans-4′-methylthioazobenzene (H), trans-4-nitro azobenzene (R), and trans-4-amino-4′-nitroazobenzene (N). The derivatives contain either an electron-donating group (−SMe and −NMe2, model H and model M, respectively); an electron-withdrawing group (−NO2, model R); or both an electron-donating and an electron-withdrawing group (−NH2 and –NO2, model N) in a so-called push–pull substitution (Scheme 1).

We constructed dimers with different intermonomeric distances as π-stacked and slip-stacked arrangements. We also qualitatively assessed how different contributions (static dipole terms and excitonic coupling) vary and affect the dimers’ photophysical properties. However, in this illustrative application, we did not consider other factors, such as vibrational, environmental (e.g., solvent), and geometrical (e.g., how the geometry of one monomer is influenced by the other) contributions to the dimers’ properties, which will be taken into account in future work.

Geometry Optimization

For all azobenzene monomeric models, geometry optimization was performed at the density functional theory (DFT) level employing the B3LYP functional56−59 and def2-TZVP basis set.43,60 While trans-azobenzene (i.e., A model) exhibits a perfectly planar geometry, as well as H and M models to a great extent, N and R models show a slightly bent conformation. The Cartesian coordinates of the optimized geometries are available in Section S1.3.1 in the Supporting Information.

Monomeric Energy Evaluation

Vertical excitation energies and oscillator strength values were computed at the ground-state minimum geometries by performing single-point energy calculations at the eight roots, state-average restricted active space self-consistent field (SA-RASSCF) level of theory,61 using the ANO-RCC basis set with a double-ζ quality and polarization functions,62 with different active space dimensions for the various azobenzene-derived systems based on the specific substituents of each model. This variation is in terms of both the number of included active orbitals and/or electrons and their distribution in RAS1, RAS2, and RAS3. More details about the active space for each system are given in Section S1.3.3 in the Supporting Information. The computed SA-RASSCF wave functions were used to perform a subsequent energy re-evaluation at the multistate restricted active space second-order perturbation theory (MS-RASPT2)63,64 with an ionization potential–electron affinity (IPEA) shift65 of 0.25, an imaginary level shift66 of 0.1, and a Cholesky decomposition of 10–4.67,68 To further reduce the computational cost, the MS-RASPT2 calculations for each system were split into eight separate jobs for each state running on different CPUs, and then, a final multistate calculation was performed.69 Further details about the computational time can be found in Table S6 in the Supporting Information.

Excitonic Coupling Calculation

We studied all possible homo- and heterodimer combinations of the molecules shown in Scheme 1. The dimers were constructed as cofacial π-stacked arrangements, with intermolecular distance d. Other arrangements include slip-stacked heterodimers with different distances z along the Z-axis (see Scheme 2). For all dimers, we always included d = 3.5 Å, which represents the interdye distance reported in experimental studies.36 More details about the different geometrical specifications are reported in Section S1.4.1 in the Supporting Information.

After computing the monomer excitation energies as detailed in Section 2.3 and the dimer Coulomb coupling terms according to the theory in Section 2.1, the FEH was built and diagonalized to obtain the eigenvectors that describe the character of the excitonic states in terms of the monomeric state components, and the eigenvalues that represent the excitonic excitation energies. The eigenvectors were then used to obtain the oscillator strength values f for each corresponding excitonic excitation. We differentiate between monomer and dimer oscillator strength values using a superscript, while we use a subscript to indicate the electronic transition. For example, refers to the oscillator strength on a generic monomer a that is excited from the ground to the second excited state; indicates the oscillator strength for a generic heterodimer ab that is excited from its ground state to its 9th excitonic excited state.

All DFT geometry optimizations (Section 2.2), single-point MS-RASPT2/SA-RASSCF calculations (Section 2.3), and excitonic coupling calculations (this section) were performed using OpenMolcas.54

Results and Discussion

Monomer Energetics

The multiconfigurational character of azobenzene-like systems has been demonstrated by previous studies.70,71 Accordingly, we found that in all azobenzene-derived systems, the first excited state is a n → π* excitation (where one electron is excited from one nitrogen lone pair to the conjugated π* system) that is, formally, forbidden and, thus, a dark state.72 The nature of the first bright state (S2 for all models) is often complex and not easily attributed to a single excitation due to its highly multiconfigurational nature.73 Nevertheless, the lowest π → π* excitation is consistently prominent in the characterization of the bright state, as evidenced by the oscillator strength and summarized in Table 1 for the different models. Further details regarding the active space specifications for each model can be found in Section S1.3.3 of the Supporting Information. Our computed data compare well with available and comparable computational and experimental data, which was produced with multiconfigurational methods or obtained through in vacuo spectroscopy,70,73−75 as shown in Table S17 in the Supporting Information. In particular, the description of the parent model A is improved (both as active space and number of states included in the state averaging), in terms of closeness to the experimental values, with respect to what we previously presented,54 which was just an application of the coupling code.

Three basic effects are at play to modulate the spectroscopic properties of the parent model A and induce a bathochromic effect: (i) an electron-donating substituent destabilizes the conjugated π occupied orbitals; (ii) an electron-withdrawing substituent stabilizes the conjugated π* virtual orbitals; and (iii) the substituents can induce a degree of rotation around the central bonds, thus partially disrupting the whole conjugated π system. From the computed data, it is possible to appreciate how M (substituted with the most electron-donating group) shows the largest red shift, followed by the other electron-donating substituted derivative H. The two remaining derivatives show very similar excitation energy values, although R has only an electron-withdrawing group, while N is the push–pull model. Possibly, the fact that these two models adopt a slightly bent conformation further affects their spectroscopic properties. Nevertheless, such tuning is in line with previous theoretical work.73

Finally, it is interesting to note that all four azobenzene derivatives show oscillator strength values higher than the parent model. In particular, the push–pull model N has the highest value, which obviously translates into the highest computed absorbance peak.

Homodimer Excitonic Coupling

The computed absorption spectra for π-stacked homodimeric models at d = 3.5 Å are depicted in Figure 2. The shift in absorption peak from monomer to dimer can be explained with the molecular excitonic model,11,13 which relates the exciton behavior to the stacking mode of the constituting monomers. More specifically, in a dimeric system, the excitonic coupling between monomer a and monomer b (that is, the coupling relative to the excitation of monomer a and de-excitation of monomer b and vice versa) induces a splitting of the exciton into two levels: an in-phase and an out-of-phase linear combination of the monomers’ excited states. In other words, for a two-level system (TLS) of a homodimer, we have excitonic states , where the in-phase excitonic state (|Ψ+⟩) represents an allowed transition, while the out-of-phase excitonic state (|Ψ–⟩) represents a forbidden transition. Based on the orientation of the two monomers, one has different aggregation modes: H- and J-aggregates. In H-aggregates, the allowed transition is to the higher excitonic state (i.e., |Ψ+⟩ is at higher energy than |Ψ–⟩), therefore, a hypsochromic shift in the dimer absorption band is observed. Usually, π-stacking configurations result in H-aggregates. In contrast, J-aggregates are characterized by a bathochromic shift because the permitted transition is to the lower excitonic state (i.e., |Ψ+⟩ is at lower energy than |Ψ–⟩). Usually, staggered configurations result in J-aggregates.

The dimer peak spectral shift with respect to the corresponding monomeric absorbance (Figure 2) clearly shows a blue shift for all five models, which confirms the formation of H-aggregates, as expected given the π-stacked disposition of the monomers. The extent of our computed dimer–monomer shift in excitation energies (ΔΔE) is different for the various models, as evidenced in Table 2, and this is mainly because of the differences in the excitonic coupling strengths (Jab) of all considered homodimers, where a stronger coupling implies a larger blue shift.

In literature, it has been reported that AA homodimers showed a blue shift of 0.1–0.6 eV.41−43 A hypsochromic shift with different method-dependent extents was also previously reported for RR dimers both in vacuo and attached on a surface.44 Moreover, spectroscopic analyses revealed that the intercalation of MM dimeric aggregate into DNA and RNA duplexes induced distinct blue shifts of 0.06 and 0.14 eV, respectively.40

We will focus now on the particular AA model with d = 3.5 Å, for which the brightest excitonic transition is to the |5⟩ excitonic state with an oscillator strength = 0.66 and a blue shift of ≈0.08 eV compared to the monomeric bright transition (i.e., S0 → S2 transition). However, the peak depicted in Figure 2 looks broader compared to other homodimeric peaks, and this is due to the overlap with the second brightest excitonic transition to the |9⟩ excitonic state ( = 0.58) whose peak is located at 4.69 eV.

TDDFT-computed AA dimer with a 3.5 Å intermonomeric distance predicted a bright |9⟩ excitonic state with an 0.14 eV blue shift.43 Experimental studies have documented instances of blue shift between ca. 0.1 eV occurring during the self-assembly of surfactants functionalized with azobenzene41 and approximately 0.6 eV, as observed in azobenzene-containing self-assembled monolayers.42

To understand the nature of AA excitonic behavior, we analyzed the respective Frenkel’s eigenvectors (Table S20 in the Supporting Information) which reveal that the |5⟩ excitonic state is due to the linear combination of S0 → S2 (i.e., the brightest) electronic excitation of each A monomer with a smaller contribution from its S0 → S4 second brightest transition. On the other hand, the |9⟩ excitonic state is formed by a linear combination of the latter and a less significant weight given to the monomeric S2 states.

We further analyzed the nature of the in-phase and out-of-phase combinations of the monomer excited states by enlarging the intermonomeric distance d. As shown in Figure 3, the AA models exhibit two symmetrically split excitonic bands centered around the respective monomeric transition of the largest contributions. However, with increasing d values, the splitting becomes smaller due to weaker excitonic interactions, and correspondingly, the transition to |5⟩ becomes more permitted.

Regarding the AM heterodimer, the experimental spectrum shows two peaks, of which the one close to the absorption of A shows hyperchromicity (stronger absorbance) with respect to the monomer, while the one close to M shows hypochromicity (weaker absorption), and both peaks are slightly red-shifted with respect to the corresponding monomer absorbance maxima. Our computed spectrum respects the change in absorbance and the red shift of the peak close to the absorbance of M. However, the other peak is predicted to be slightly blue-shifted (Figure 5a).

Having seen how the distance between monomers may influence the excitonic excitation energy, we studied the effect of changing the intermonomeric distance d also for the AM heterodimer, as reported in the top part of Figure 6. The AM intermonomeric distance clearly has minimal effects on the excitonic excitation energies. However, the predicted absorbance changes with the excitation to |4⟩ become increasingly permitted compared with that to state |5⟩.

Regarding the HM heterodimer, the experimental results report two bands, both red-shifted with respect to the monomer absorptions, and of which the one close to the absorption of H is much stronger than the one close to the absorption of M. Our computed spectrum (Figure 5g) also shows two peaks, with absorbance in agreement with the experimental results. Although the computed dimeric peak located at a longer wavelength is predicted correctly with a bathochromic shift, the main peak at ca. 354 nm is slightly blue-shifted with respect to the corresponding monomer.

Once more, we tested the dependence of the HM excitation energies on the d distance (bottom panel of Figure 6). As expected, the excitonic coupling diminishes with enlarging distance, which in turn induces changes in absorbance and excitation energies. Nonetheless, we were unable to reproduce the experimental red-shifted band. As evidenced in Figure S1 in the Supporting Information, slip-stacked models of HM through small displacements along the Z axis also did not imply any modulations in the spectroscopic behavior.

Another way to rationalize the discrepancy in our computed shift is the environmental effects that are present in the experimental model and absent in our gas-phase dimers. In particular, the azobenzene derivatives were experimentally converted to phosphoramidite monomers and then heteroclustered and embedded in a DNA-like structure.36 In terms of the monomeric components of HM dimer, M is more polar than H since the former has a dimethylamino group that is more electron-donating than the methyl thio group in the latter, and this leads to an increase in the electron density around the central N atoms. Moreover, the presence of the amino group with its lone pair of electrons contributes more significantly to the overall polarity of M compared to the methyl thio group of H. This discussion is also supported by the dipole moment values given in Table 2. Overall, one might expect that the polar DNA scaffold with its phosphate backbone and charged functional groups can induce polarizable effects more on one monomer than on the other. Other potential effects that have been neglected in our FEH model are the couplings between monomeric local excited states and charge-transfer (CT) states, which can exert a significant influence on the absorption spectra of molecular assemblies and crystalline structures and open up the potential of producing red-shifted H-aggregates.76,77

Generally speaking, the reported heterodimeric aggregates can have a large variety of excitonic coupling, from weak28 to strong.78 However, their inherent heterogeneity complicates matters when trying to rationalize the overall effects.79 One useful index is the absolute difference between the most absorbing excitation energies of the constituting monomers53,55

In light of the above, heterodimeric dyes can exhibit strong excitonic coupling with considerable spectral shifts with respect to the underlying monomer absorption peaks. Conclusions

In this paper, we investigated the excitonic properties of homo- and, more interestingly, heterodimeric aggregates composed of azobenzene-derived models as π-stacked and slip-stacked models (Scheme 2). This was obtained within the context of the FEH model and using a multiconfigurational wave function description of the individual monomers, thanks to a code recently implemented into the OpenMolcas programs package.53,54 To the best of our knowledge, such a combination of methods and level of theory has not been previously systematically applied to heterodimeric aggregates.

The investigated azobenzene derivatives are highly conjugated systems substituted with electron-donating and/or -withdrawing groups (Scheme 1), and thus, we used the multiconfigurational MS-RASPT2/SA-RASSCF method, which is not so often used (see Table S17)70,73 but necessary for a proper description of this class of molecules.

Our results showed how heterodimeric dyes, constructed with monomers of particular chemical and physical characteristics, can exhibit strong excitonic coupling with considerable spectral shifts (Figure 5) and excitonic properties that are different from what can be achieved with homodimers (Figure 2). Moreover, we found that the main factor that influences the excitonic coupling in a heterodimeric system is the excitation energy difference between the coupled monomers (as reported in Table 4), and these differences are directly related to the monomer energetics that implicitly takes into account the chemical structure and electronic characteristics of the system. In practice, a smaller value induces a more excitonic and delocalized aggregate, with the possibility to tune such delocalization depending on the nature of the interacting monomers, as shown in Table 5 and relative discussion. Regarding homodimers, several factors come into play due to both static dipole contributions (vdW-like) and excitonic interactions in regulating the energetics of the aggregates. For example, in-phase and out-of-phase excitonic states can be symmetric or asymmetric concerning the monomer excitation energy, as shown in Figure 4 and Table 3 and discussed in Section 3.2.

Our work provides new insights toward the understanding of the optical properties of dye homo- and, most importantly, heteroaggregates. However, it is important to mention that exchange terms and charge-transfer transitions are still missing from the treatment, and they may modify the results, especially for shorter d distances, where neglecting such terms is no longer justified. An effort is currently underway to include these terms, and once available, we will revisit the data using the new code. Furthermore, it is worth mentioning that the current work represents mostly an illustrative application of the involved methodologies through the here-presented purely model cases. The effects related to molecular vibrations, environmental factors, or geometrical changes due to the vicinity of the monomers were not included in our models. They will be considered in future studies using, for instance, the QM/MM method. Finally, since the chemical nature and position of the substituents significantly tune the chemistry of the monomeric models, it will be interesting in the future to expand the library of azobenzene-derived models and extend the evaluation of the respective monomer/aggregate photophysical and photochemical properties that can be leveraged into the eventual application as functional materials in different fields like organic electronics and photovoltaics.
