The natural history of traumatic posterior glenohumeral dislocations in a high-risk population
Authors: Hannah P Gibbs, Aidan M McQuade, Scott M Feeley, Conor F McCarthy, Valentina Ramirez, Benjamin W Hoyt, Jonathan F Dickens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose of this study was to define the clinical trajectory and treatment outcomes after traumatic posterior shoulder dislocation in patients treated both operatively and non-operatively.MethodsWe queried for all patients ≤50 years old who had a posterior shoulder dislocation or subluxation event, confirmed on imaging between January 2017 and December 2024. Patients were excluded for <6 months of follow-up from the date of the dislocation or subluxation event. Functional failure was defined as persistent subjective instability/pain with a positive Kim/Jerk exam, permanent activity restrictions, or failure to return to work. Additionally, recurrent dislocation, revision surgery, or transition from therapy to surgery was considered structural failure at any time point.ResultsThirty-three shoulders met inclusion criteria. Mechanisms of injury included falls (n = 15, 45%), seizures (n = 7, 21%), combat/military training (n = 7, 21%), sports (n = 2, 6%), and motorcycle accident (n = 2, 6%). The average age at the time of injury was 28.5 years. There were 31/33 (94%) active duty service members and 2/33 (6%) immediate family of service members. Non-operative treatment was undergone in 18/33 patients (55%), and 11/18 patients (61%) failed treatment most commonly due to recurrent dislocations (n = 8, 73%). Operative treatment was undergone in 15/33 patients (45%), and the most common procedure was an arthroscopic posterior labral repair (n = 11, 73%). The average posterior glenoid bone loss in the non-operative group was 11.9% and 12.2% in the operative group. The average gamma angle for the reverse Hill-Sachs lesions in the non-operative group was 97.5° and 83.13° in the operative group. The overall failure rate of operatively managed shoulders was 8/15 shoulders (53%), due to revision surgery in 4/8 patients (50%), redislocation events in 2/8 patients (25%), and subjective instability in 2/8 patients (25%). Seven of 15 (47%) of the operative cohort were placed on permanent profile restrictions or failed to return to duty due to the injured shoulder.ConclusionTraumatic posterior dislocations in young, active individuals demonstrated high failure rates with both operative (53%) and non-operative (61%) management, though direct comparison between treatment groups is limited by treatment selection bias. Nearly all patients with failure after operative intervention failed to return fully to their occupation.Keywords: Posterior shoulder dislocation, Glenohumeral instability, Traumatic shoulder instability, Recurrent instability, Military population, Return to duty

The overall incidence of posterior shoulder instability in the young and athletic military population is rising, currently estimated at 0.12 to 1.84 per 1,000 person-years.4,16 However, true posterior glenohumeral dislocations remain rare and represent a distinct clinical entity.2,4,15,22,23 An acute posteriorly directed force may cause chondrolabral shearing and a posterior glenohumeral dislocation.11 Despite growing recognition, posterior glenohumeral instability remains less common than anterior instability, with an estimated incidence of 2-5% among all glenohumeral instability cases.11,15,24 Management typically begins with non-operative treatment and physical therapy (PT).2,20,25 PT programs emphasizing dynamic stabilization have shown success rates of 94% in low-demand patients with atraumatic posterior subluxation cases,6 although outcomes are poorer in patients with traumatic subluxations, with a 36% success rate.6 For those who fail non-operative management and for a more active population, arthroscopic posterior labral repair is the most common primary surgical intervention, constituting 24% of all labral repairs in the military population.2,12 Open techniques may be required in complex situations such as glenoid hypoplasia, significant glenoid bone loss (GBL), or revision settings.2

Despite increased awareness and evolving treatment algorithms, the natural history of posterior shoulder dislocations, especially in the relatively higher-demand military population, remains poorly characterized. The purpose of this study was to define the clinical trajectory and treatment outcomes after traumatic posterior shoulder dislocation in patients treated both operatively and non-operatively. We hypothesized a high failure rate in both operative and non-operative cohorts given the occupational demands of active duty service members.

Materials and methods

After approval by the institutional review board, the Military Health System Data Repository (MDR) was queried for all patients who had undergone a closed shoulder reduction (Current Procedural Terminology code 23650) and had a diagnosis code of posterior shoulder instability (International Classification of Diseases-10 code S43.024) between January 2017 and December 2024 across all military treatment facilities. Patients were included if they experienced at least 1 traumatic posterior glenohumeral dislocation confirmed by imaging, specifically by axillary shoulder radiographs, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging (Fig. 1), and were ≤50 years of age. Patients were excluded if they had <6 months of follow-up or found to have atraumatic posterior shoulder dislocation on chart review (ie, dislocation due to high 5, throwing a softball, or putting on a shirt).

A review of the electronic medical record was performed to identify demographic information and clinical course surrounding shoulder instability. Collected variables included patient sex, military branch and rank, mechanism of injury, number of dislocation events, time from dislocation event to PT and/or surgery, treatment type (operative versus non-operative), treatment success versus failure, separation from military service, and follow-up duration. Indications for operative versus non-operative treatment were decided upon by the patient and treating provider. This shared decision-making process was based on the patient's individual clinical presentation, symptom severity, corresponding imaging, and occupational demands; therefore, treatment was not standardized for all patients.

Functional failure was defined as persistent subjective instability/pain with a positive Kim/Jerk exam, permanent activity restrictions due to the shoulder injury, or failure to return to unrestricted military or work duties >6 months after the dislocation event. Additionally, recurrent dislocation, revision surgery, or transition from PT to surgery was considered structural failure at any time point.

We compared baseline characteristics between patients with successful outcomes and those who failed using independent samples t-tests performed in SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 30.0 [IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA]. Continuous variables, such as age at the time of event, number of shoulder dislocations, and follow-up duration, were summarized as mean ± standard deviation for each outcome group. Normality and equal variances were assumed. A 2-tailed P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

Figure 2 demonstrates the study flow diagram. The mean age was 28.5 years ±8.5 years, with 28 men (85%) and 5 women (15%). There were 31/33 (94%) active duty service members and 2/33 (6%) immediate family of service members (ages 17 and 38). The active-duty cohort included service members from the Army (n = 15, 48%), Navy (n = 10, 32%), Air Force (n = 5, 16%), and Marine Corps (n = 1, 4%).

Treatment outcomes

Of the 33 shoulders, 18 (55%) underwent non-operative management with PT and 15 (45%) underwent surgery. The average posterior GBL in the non-operative group was 11.9% and 12.2% in the operative group. The average gamma angle for the reverse Hill-Sachs lesions in the non-operative group was 97.5° and 83.13° in the operative group. Of the 18 shoulders managed non-operatively, structural failure occurred in 9/18 shoulders (50%), to include recurrent dislocation (n = 8) and transition from non-operative treatment to surgery (n = 1). The patients who redislocated had the following mechanism of injury: falls (n = 6) and seizure (n = 2). Functional failure without structural recurrence occurred in 2/18 shoulders (11%) due to persistent instability symptoms with Kim/Jerk testing (Table II). None of the patients in this cohort were placed on a permanent profile or referred to the medical evaluation board.

For the operative cohort, the most common surgical procedure was arthroscopic posterior labral repair with capsulorrhaphy (11/15, 73%). Other procedures performed included arthroscopic labral débridement with biceps tenodesis and subacromial decompression (n = 3, 20%) and open posterior glenoid reconstruction using an iliac crest autograft bone block (n = 1, 6%). Structural failure occurred in 6/15 shoulders (40%), including revision surgery (n = 4) and recurrent dislocation without revision (n = 2). Functional failure without structural recurrence occurred in 2/15 shoulders (13%) due to persistent instability on Kim/Jerk testing (Table II). Persistent pain after posterior stabilization is a recognized challenge. Clavert et al10 reported persistent post-operative pain in 18% of their cohort of patients who underwent posterior bone block reconstruction. The patients who redislocated had the following mechanism of injury: falls (n = 2). There were no seizure patients in the operative cohort that redislocated. The earliest revision surgery in the operative cohort occurred at 1.3 years following the index procedure, with a range of 1.3-9.0 years. The average time to failure in the non-operative group was 4.5 months. Revision procedures included revision posterior labral repair with capsular shift (n = 3, 75%) and a posterior glenoid reconstruction with iliac crest autograft (n = 1, 25%). Seven of 15 (47%) of the operative cohort were placed on permanent activity restrictions or failed to return to duty (Table III). Of the 8 surgical failures, 3 were posterior labral repairs (37.5%), 2 were posterior labral repairs with capsular plication (25%), 1 was a posterior labral repair with capsulorrhaphy (12.5%), 1 was a posterior labral repair with a superior labrum anterior-to-posterior (SLAP) repair and capsulorrhaphy (12.5%) and 1 was a posterior labral repair with a capsular shift (12.5%).

Discussion

There are little existing data on the natural history and treatment outcomes of posterior glenohumeral dislocation in high-risk populations. While posterior shoulder subluxations are increasingly recognized among military service members,2,20 true posterior dislocations remain uncommon and represent a challenging entity to treat. In this cohort of predominantly young, male, active duty service members with osterior dislocations, 45% of patients underwent operative treatment and 55% of patients were managed non-operatively. These results come with the caveat that this was a retrospective study that has an inherent treatment selection bias. Patients who were indicated for surgery likely represented a cohort with worse symptom severity, higher functional demands, and more concerning imaging characteristics. As a result, these cohorts are not necessarily directly comparable. As such, the goal of our study is to be descriptive rather than comparative. Selection bias remains a recognized challenge even in prospective posterior instability comparisons. Cruz-Ferreira et al11 acknowledge baseline treatment group differences and surgeon selection bias as important limitations in their prospective non-randomized comparison of operative and non-operative posterior instability treatment. Garret et al13 describe posterior shoulder instability as 3 distinct entities: recurrent involuntary dislocation and/or subluxation; voluntary instability, which may progress to involuntary; and painful posterior shoulder instability. The authors also highlight the heterogeneity within posterior instability cohorts, with varying clinical presentations that ultimately lead to differing treatment strategies.

The sex distribution in this study reflects that of the military population overall, as men comprise 80% of all active-duty personnel. In this high-demand military cohort with frank traumatic dislocations, substantial rates of recurrent instability and functional limitation were observed across operative and non-operative pathways. Given the non-randomized, retrospective nature of this study, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive of a particularly high-risk population rather than comparative between treatment strategies. Previous literature on posterior instability defines failure in regard to structural endpoints alone; however, we further stratified our outcomes into structural and functional failure. In the operative cohort, 75% of failures were due to structural causes and 25% were due to functional reasons. In the non-operative cohort, 82% of failures were structural, whereas 18% were functional failures.

These overall failure rates are comparable to previously reported cases in low demand, traumatic posterior shoulder subluxators, where non-operative management has demonstrated success in 36% of patients.6 Our non-operative cohort demonstrated an elevated gamma angle for the reverse Hill-Sachs lesions, and the operative cohort demonstrated a higher degree of posterior GBL. Both factors could account for the high failure rates in both groups. Increased gamma angles and posterior GBL contribute to treatment failure by promoting re-engagement of the humeral head defect with the posterior glenoid, particularly in the setting of bipolar bone loss, which has been associated with the highest risk of recurrent instability.19

The most common mechanisms of posterior dislocation in our cohort were traumatic falls (45%) and seizures (21%), which are similar to prior epidemiologic studies on posterior dislocation.21,22 Of those who experienced redislocation, the most common mechanisms were traumatic falls (8/10, 80%) and seizures (2/10, 20%). Previously identified risk factors for recurrent posterior shoulder dislocation include age <40 years at the time of the first dislocation event21 and seizure-related dislocations,13,21 both of which were present in our cohort. This emphasizes the importance of early risk stratification and a multidisciplinary approach with neurology for optimal seizure control to minimize recurrence of posterior shoulder dislocation.

As recognition of posterior shoulder instability continues to grow, the rate of surgical intervention increases as well, with prior literature reporting 96% of cases being managed with an arthroscopic posterior labral repair.17 This trend aligns with our findings, as 73% of the operative cohort underwent an arthroscopic posterior labral repair. In our cohort, patients who underwent surgery were generally younger, which likely reflects the demographics of our population rather than a methodological preference for arthroscopic stabilization based on younger age.17 Of note, only one patient in our cohort underwent an open bone block procedure, despite prior studies documenting high rates of posterior GBL in posterior glenohumeral instability13,14,18 In our surgical cohort, we observed a 53% failure rate, defined as recurrent shoulder dislocations, revision surgery, or persistent instability/pain on positive physical exam. This failure rate is high, relative to that reported by Chan et al, who showed a 17% failure rate for primary arthroscopic posterior labral repairs in a military posterior subluxator population; however, Chan et al performed these surgeries due to posterior instability, whereas our cohort were frank posterior shoulder dislocations.8

Posterior shoulder instability repairs in the military have been previously associated with higher recurrence rates compared to anterior stabilization, likely due to posterior shoulder loading military demands such as pushups and overhead carrying.4,19 The current study highlights that frank posterior dislocation may have worse recurrence rates when compared to recurrence rates reported with posterior instability in general.4 The majority of the existing posterior instability literature also focuses on posterior subluxation, microinstability, and atraumatic injury patterns rather than frank dislocations. These vary substantially in mechanism and pathology compared to the frank dislocations seen in our high-risk population.6,7 In a multicenter French Arthroscopy Society study of 101 patients undergoing capsulolabral reconstruction for posterior instability, failure rates remained 22-35% despite generally favorable functional outcomes, underscoring the inherent treatment challenges even outside the setting of frank dislocation.1 It is important to note, however, that our operative cohort was very heterogeneous, including a wide spectrum of interventions to include soft tissue stabilization, débridements, and bone block reconstruction, thereby limiting procedure-specific conclusions.

The analysis of baseline characteristics between patients with successful and failed treatment outcomes for posterior dislocation revealed no statistically significant differences in age, number of dislocations, or follow-up duration. However, the study was not adequately powered to detect small to moderate differences between these factors. Prior studies identified recurrent instability as risk factors for increased GBL, and thus greater arthroscopic failure rates.14,18 The male predominance in both outcome groups (M:F, 28:5) reflects the demographics of an active-duty military population and is consistent with prior epidemiologic data on posterior shoulder instability in military cohorts.4,20 These findings suggest baseline demographic and clinical factors alone may not fully predict treatment success in patients with posterior shoulder dislocations.

The failure to return to full duty/work rate of 7/33 patients (21%) in this study is greater than previously reported literature of return to duty/play after “non-dislocation” posterior instability.3,5,7,13,24 This study is unique in its focus on a young, active population with traumatic posterior shoulder dislocations, which appear to have less favorable outcomes than prior literature on posterior dislocations.7,24 The increased military separation rate could be due to the inability to modify combat activities that load the posterior shoulder, such as overhead lifting, pushups, shooting weapons, or participating in high-impact activities.7, 8, 9 It is possible that this medical separation rate could be confounded by secondary gain and other comorbidities.

Limitations

This retrospective study is susceptible to incomplete documentation, potential recall bias, and variation in outcome reporting across providers and treatment facilities. The sample size of 33 shoulders is small, limiting the generalizability of subgroup comparisons. This also limits statistical power; therefore, the absence of obtaining results with statistical significance should not be interpreted as equivalence between groups. Treatment protocols varied and lacked standardization, with heterogeneity in both surgical and therapeutic interventions across the military, which precludes any procedure-specific conclusions. In this military-heavy population there may have been underreporting of symptoms to avoid duty limitations. Data from this military population may not be entirely generalizable to civilians.

Conclusion

Traumatic posterior dislocations in young, active individuals demonstrated high failure rates with both operative (53%) and non-operative (61%) management, though direct comparison between treatment groups is limited by treatment selection bias. Nearly all patients with failure after operative intervention failed to return fully to their occupation.
