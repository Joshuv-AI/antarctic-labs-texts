On the Subspace Orbit Problem and the Simultaneous Skolem Problem Conference: Mystery Event; One Day; In Mystery Town CCS: Theory of computation Logic and verification
Piotr Bacik, Anton Varonka

Abstract
. The Orbit Problem asks whether the orbit of a point under a matrix reaches a given target set. When the target is a single point, the problem was shown to be decidable in polynomial time by Kannan and Lipton. This decidability result was later extended by Chonev et al. to targets of dimension 3 (in arbitrary ambient dimension), but decidability remains open for subspaces of dimension 4. At the other extreme, the special case of the Orbit Problem in which the target set is a hyperplane of codimension 1 is equivalent to the Skolem Problem for linear recurrence sequences, whose decidability has been open for many decades. In this paper, we show that the Orbit Problem is decidable if the target subspace has dimension logarithmic in the dimension of the orbit. Over rationals, we moreover obtain a complexity bound 𝐍𝐏 𝐑𝐏 {\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathbf{NP}$}}{\hbox{$\mathbf{NP}$}}{\mathbf{NP}}{\mathbf{NP}}}^{{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathbf{RP}$}}{\hbox{$\mathbf{RP}$}}{\mathbf{RP}}{\mathbf{RP}}}} in this case. On the other hand, we show that the version of the Orbit Problem where the dimension of the target subspace is linear in the dimension of the orbit is as hard as the Skolem Problem.

Introduction

A
linear dynamical system
(LDS) consists of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{K}^{d\times d}

\bm{x}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}

\bm{x}
under repeated application of

(
18

in the context of the reachability problem for linear sequential machines, asks whether a given LDS
(A,\bm{x})\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{Q}^{d}

A decade later, this problem was shown to be decidable in polynomial time by Kannan and Lipton

20

19

A natural generalisation to the problem where the target point

(

Subspace Orbit Problem
.

Given an LDS
(A,\bm{x})\in\mathbb{K}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{K}^{d}
and a subspace
S\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{d}
, decide whether
\mathcal{B}(A,\bm{x})\cap S\neq\varnothing

Around 30 years later, a significant step forward was achieved by Chonev, Ouaknine and Worrell

when the dimension of the target subspace is at most 3, regardless of the dimension
d
of the ambient space.
To date, this is the state of the art for the Subspace Orbit Problem, with decidability for higher-dimensional target subspaces remaining a major open problem.

Other domains for targets have been studied in
(
13

3

24
)

An important application of the orbit problems comes from program analysis:
note that LDS can be viewed as linear while loops

\bm{v}\leftarrow\bm{x};\kern 5.0pt\textbf{while}\ \textsc{cond}(v)\ \textbf{do}\kern 5.0pt\bm{v}\leftarrow A\cdot\bm{v},

where reachability of a target

We refer the reader to

Taking one step back, a major obstruction on the way to deciding orbit problems is the Skolem Problem.
A
linear recurrence relation
of order
d

\mathbb{K}
is an equation

(1.1)

u_{n}=c_{1}u_{n-1}+\dots+c_{d}u_{n-d},

where

for
1\leq i\leq d

A linear recurrence sequence (LRS)
u=\langle u_{n}\rangle_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of elements in
\mathbb{K}
satisfying (
1.1
).
In the case that
\mathbb{K}
has characteristic 0, the set
\mathcal{Z}(u)=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:u_{n}=0\}
of zero terms of an LRS
is described by the theorem of Skolem, Mahler, and Lech
(
36
;
29

27
)
:

Theorem 1.1 (Skolem-Mahler-Lech).

The zero set
\mathcal{Z}(u)
is a union of finitely many arithmetic progressions and a finite set.

The proof is unfortunately ineffective — though we can find the arithmetic progressions, there is no known algorithm to compute the exceptional finite set. Computing this finite set is equivalent to solving the
Skolem Problem
.

Skolem Problem
.

Given an LRS
u
, decide whether

\mathcal{Z}(u)
is non-empty.

We denote the Skolem Problem on
\mathbb{K}
-LRS that satisfy a recurrence relation of order

as
\textnormal{{Skolem}}_{\mathbb{K}}(d)
; when
\mathbb{K}=\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
we simply write
\textnormal{{Skolem}}(d)
. It is straightforward to see that deciding the Skolem Problem is equivalent to deciding the Subspace Orbit Problem with a hyperplane target, that is, a linear subspace of
\mathbb{K}^{d}
of dimension
d-1
. However, despite being posed close to a century ago, decidability of the Skolem Problem has stubbornly remained open; decidability is known for
\textnormal{{Skolem}}(4)

(
37
;
39
;
6
)
, and furthermore,
\textnormal{{Skolem}}_{\mathbb{Q}}(4)
lies in
\mathbf{coRP}

(
5
)
, but
\textnormal{{Skolem}}_{\mathbb{Q}}(5)
remains open
(
9
)
. In the orbit problems terminology, the decidability of reaching a 4-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{Q}^{5}
is open.

When considering
non-degenerate
sequences, the statement of
Theorem

1.1
can be further refined. Define the

of

satisfying

1.1

\displaystyle g(x)=x^{d}-c_{1}x^{d-1}-\dots-c_{d}\,.

We say

The Skolem-Mahler-Lech Theorem shows that a non-degenerate LRS is either

Crucially, for every LRS
\langle u_{n}\rangle_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
there exists an integer

\langle u_{Ln+r}\rangle_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

The Reduced Subspace Orbit Problem

The Subspace Orbit Problem with target space
S\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{5}
of dimension 4 is equivalent to
\textnormal{{Skolem}}_{\mathbb{Q}}(5)
, the decidability of which appears to be beyond the reach of current methods. A natural question is to ask whether the problem with target dimension 4 becomes tractable when the dimension of the LDS is sufficiently large. However, for this we need a sufficiently robust notion of the dimension of an LDS. For example, if
(A,\bm{x})\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{K}^{d}
, then one might naïvely take the dimension of
(A,\bm{x})
to be the ambient dimension
d
. We argue against it, as any LDS may trivially be viewed as being inside a space of arbitrarily large dimension by adding new components to
A,\bm{x}
that are zero everywhere.
Instead, we introduce the notion of
reduced
LDS, and of the
inherent
dimension of an LDS.

Call a LDS
(A,\bm{x})

non-degenerate
if no ratio of distinct eigenvalues of the matrix
A
is a root of unity. Define the
Krylov dimension
of an orbit to be the smallest dimension of any subspace that contains it. We say an LDS
(A,\bm{x})\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d\times d}\times\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d}
is
reduced
if it is non-degenerate and the Krylov dimension of any tail of its orbit is
d
; that is,
\spn\{A^{n}\bm{x}:n\geq m\}=d
for all
m\geq 0
.

By removing finitely many initial points, taking sub-orbits and restricting to smaller subspaces (see
Section

2
), the orbit of any LDS may be rewritten as a union of finitely many points, and finitely many orbits of reduced LDS with equal Krylov dimensions. We define the
inherent dimension
of an LDS to be the Krylov dimension of its associated reduced LDS.
1
1

1

Reduced LDS have the property that their Krylov dimension remains the same upon removing finitely many points or taking sub-orbits, so the notion of inherent dimension is well defined.
The inherent dimension may be more intuitively characterised by the following property: if an LDS has inherent dimension
d
then its orbit cannot be contained within a finite union of dimension
d-1
subspaces.

It turns out that the notion of inherent dimension is the right one for investigating the problem at higher dimensions — although deciding the Subspace Orbit Problem for LDS of inherent dimension
d
and target subspaces of dimension
t
is
\textnormal{{Skolem}}(5)
-hard when
(d,t)=(5,4)
,
the problem becomes tractable already when
(d,t)=(6,4)
. This motivates the following question, which this paper investigates:
for each fixed target dimension, what is the smallest inherent dimension of LDS for which we can decide the Subspace Orbit Problem?

The Subspace Orbit Problem on arbitrary LDS can be solved by solving the Subspace Orbit Problem on each associated reduced LDS. Therefore, in the rest of the paper we assume that
any LDS
(A,\bm{x})
is reduced
, unless stated otherwise.
We denote the Subspace Orbit Problem restricted to reduced LDS
(A,\bm{x})\in\mathbb{K}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{K}^{d}
and subspaces
S\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{d}
of dimension
t

\textnormal{{Orbit}}_{\mathbb{K}}(d,t)
.

We investigate the central question via the Simultaneous Skolem Problem, also discussed in
(
4
)
.

Simultaneous Skolem Problem
.

Given a finite set of LRS
u^{(1)},\dots,u^{(k)}
, decide whether

\mathcal{Z}(u^{(1)},\dots,u^{(k)})=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:u^{(i)}_{n}=0\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq k\}

is non-empty.

Moreover, we are interested in computing the set
\mathcal{Z}(u^{(1)},\dots,u^{(k)})
. While the Skolem Problem corresponds to the Subspace Orbit Problem where targets are hyperplanes, the Simultaneous Skolem Problem can express any subspace target.

We further denote the Simultaneous Skolem Problem on
k
linearly independent
2
2

2

Say
u^{(1)},\dots,u^{(k)}
are linearly dependent if there exist
b_{1},\dots b_{k}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
such that
b_{1}u^{(1)}_{n}+\dots+b_{k}u^{(k)}_{n}=0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
non-degenerate
\mathbb{K}
-LRS satisfying the same recurrence relation of order
d
by
\textnormal{{SimSkolem}}_{\mathbb{K}}(d,k)
.

Just like how the Subspace Orbit Problem for general LDS reduces to considering reduced LDS, the Simultaneous Skolem Problem for general LRS reduces to linearly independent and non-degenerate LRS by passing to non-degenerate subsequences and removing linearly dependent LRS.
We shall see that while known algorithms only yield
\mathcal{Z}(u)
for

of order at most 4, considering
k\geq 2
LRS simultaneously renders decidable problems for LRS of higher orders.

Preliminaries

Reducing LDS

The
Krylov subspace

V\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d}
associated to an LDS
(A,\bm{x})\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d\times d}\times\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d}
is the
\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
-vector space spanned by the orbit of
\bm{x}
under the powers of
A\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d\times d}
:

V\coloneq\spn(\bm{x},A\bm{x},A^{2}\bm{x},\dots).

Let
V_{k}\coloneq\spn(\bm{x},A\bm{x},A^{2}\bm{x},\dots,A^{k-1}\bm{x})
be a vector subspace spanned by the first
k
vectors of the orbit.
By definition, let
V_{0}\coloneqq\{\bm{0}\}
.
Clearly,

(2.1)

V_{0}\subseteq V_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq V_{k}\subseteq\dots\subseteq V.

Let
\mu\leq d
be the minimum power such that
A^{\mu}\bm{x}\in V_{\mu}
.
Then the increasing chain (
2.1
) stabilises at
k=\mu
, that is,
V_{k}=V_{k+1}=\dots=V
.
Moreover,
\mu
is the dimension of the Krylov subspace
V
which we also call the
Krylov dimension
of
V
.
The use of the eponym is a tribute to the seminal paper
(
26
)
by Krylov;
for similar usage see e.g.
(
38
, Part VI)
.
We write
\dim(A,\bm{x})\coloneq\mu
. If
\mu=d
we say
(A,\bm{x})
is
full-dimensional
.
We say
(A,\bm{x})
has
stable dimension
if
\dim(A,\bm{x})=\dim(A,A^{n}\bm{x})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Observe that
(A,\bm{x})
certainly has stable dimension if
A
is invertible. The following lemma shows how we can reduce to considering invertible
A
, and that when taking sub-orbits, each sub-orbit has equal Krylov dimension. See also
(
25
, Section 6.1)
for similar discussion.

Lemma 2.1.

Let
(A,\bm{x})\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d\times d}\times\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d}
be an LDS. Then for each
L\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a
\mu\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(A^{L},A^{d+r}\bm{x})
has stable dimension
\mu
, for all
0\leq r\leq L-1
. Furthermore, for each
0\leq r\leq L-1
we have
\mathcal{B}(A^{L},A^{d+r}\bm{x})=\mathcal{B}(M,A^{d+r}\bm{x})
for invertible matrix
M
.

Proof.

First, note that if
A
is nilpotent then
A^{n}=0
for all
n\geq d
, and therefore the conclusion of the lemma follows trivially, with
\mu=0
and
M=I_{d}
, the
d\times d
identity matrix. Henceforth assume
A
is not nilpotent, and so has some non-zero eigenvalue. Let
J=PAP^{-1}
be the Jordan normal form with block-diagonal such that
J=\mathrm{diag}(J_{1},J_{2})
with
J_{1}\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}}
invertible and
J_{2}\subseteq\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d_{2}\times d_{2}}
nilpotent, with
d_{1}>0
.
Let
\bm{s}=P\bm{x}=(\tilde{\bm{s}}_{1},\tilde{\bm{s}}_{2})^{\top}
, for
\tilde{\bm{s}}_{1}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d_{1}}
,
\tilde{\bm{s}}_{2}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}^{d_{2}}
. Then, since
J_{2}^{n}=0
for all
n\geq d
, we have
J^{n}\bm{s}=(J_{1}^{n}\tilde{\bm{s}}_{1},\bm{0})^{\top}
for all
n\geq d
.

Define
\tilde{J}=\mathrm{diag}(J_{1},I_{d_{2}})
.
Since
J^{n}\bm{s}=(J_{1}^{n}\tilde{\bm{s}}_{1},\bm{0})^{\top}
for all
n\geq d
, we have
J^{n}J^{d}\bm{s}=\tilde{J}^{n}J^{d}\bm{s}
for all
n\geq 0
.
That is,
\mathcal{B}(\tilde{J},J^{d}\bm{s})=\mathcal{B}(J,J^{d}\bm{s})
.

Fix an integer
L
. Since
J_{1}
is invertible,
\dim(J_{1}^{L},J_{1}^{d}\tilde{\bm{s}}_{1})
is stable; define
\mu\coloneq\dim(J_{1}^{L},J_{1}^{d}\tilde{\bm{s}}_{1})
.
For each
0\leq r\leq L-1
, we have

\tilde{J}^{r}\mathcal{B}(\tilde{J}^{L},J^{d}\bm{s})=\mathcal{B}(\tilde{J}^{L},J^{d+r}\bm{s}).

The Krylov subspaces of
(\tilde{J}^{L},J^{d+r}\bm{s})
,
0\leq r\leq L-1
, are related by multiplication by an invertible matrix. It follows that

\dim({\tilde{J}}^{L},J^{d}\bm{s})=\dim({\tilde{J}}^{L},J^{d+1}\bm{s})=\dots=\dim({\tilde{J}}^{L},J^{d+L-1}\bm{s})=\mu.

Recall that
P
is invertible.
By letting
M=P^{-1}\tilde{J}^{L}P
, it follows that that
(M,A^{d+r}\bm{x})
has stable dimension
\mu
for each
0\leq r\leq L-1
. Note also that
M
is invertible, and that
\mathcal{B}(A^{L},A^{d+r}\bm{x})=\mathcal{B}(M,A^{d+r}\bm{x})
, proving what was desired.
∎

3.1.

Definition 3.2 (

The MSTV

Originally introduced in
(
28

and named after the authors of the seminal papers
(

In contrast to the definition in
(
28

the LRS we consider are over

since there are several embeddings of an arbitrary number field into

\mathbb{Q}
-LRS only the modulus is used.

The idea we employ to decide

for
k

is to take a linear combination

\displaystyle w_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\beta_{j}u_{n}^{(j)}

Then all simultaneous zeros of

are zeros of a non-degenerate

Unfortunately, this method has a complication. Sometimes, the coefficients in front of the dominant roots in the exponential-polynomial representation of the LRS will be particularly pathological, and there is no way to choose a linear combination that leaves at most 3 dominant roots without eliminating all of them (at which point there are "new" dominant roots of second-largest absolute value, which complicates application of the MSTV class). Let us illustrate this vexing situation with an example.

Example 3.3.

Choose
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d}\in\smash{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}

|\lambda_{1}|=\dots=|\lambda_{4}|>|\lambda_{5}|\geq\dots\geq|\lambda_{d}|

\displaystyle u_{n}^{(i)}=\alpha_{1}^{(i)}\lambda_{1}^{n}+\alpha_{2}^{(i)}\lambda_{2}^{n}+\alpha_{3}^{(i)}\lambda_{3}^{n}+\alpha_{4}^{(i)}\lambda_{4}^{n}+\sum_{j=5}^{d}\alpha_{j}^{(i)}\lambda_{j}^{n}

5.
Complexity

In this section, we will prove complexity upper bounds on the reduced Subspace Orbit Problem in the MSTV-reducible cases we have identified.

We emphasise that unlike in previous sections, we consider
\textnormal{{Orbit}}_{\mathbb{K}}
over the field
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}

Our input

such that no ratio of distinct eigenvalues of

is a root of unity,
an initial vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}

and a linearly independent set of rational vectors

Let

measure the bit-size of an input

Recall from

that an input to the
\textnormal{{SimSkolem}}_{\mathbb{Q}}(d,k)
is
k

k
rational vectors of initial values.
We denote the sum of their bitsizes by

The notation

will denote any quantity bounded above by

, and

To decide

Step 1.

Show that
\textnormal{{Orbit}}_{\mathbb{Q}}
on

reduces to

on

such that

Step 2.

If there is a linear combination of the LRS

that lies in the MSTV class, then such a linear combination exists,
whose coefficients (of the exponential polynomial form (
2.3
)) have bounded degree and height.

Step 3.

Apply bounds on the zeros of non-degenerate LRS in the MSTV class to prove upper bounds on the simultaneous zeros of

.

We note that Steps 1–3 as listed justify the correctness of
Corollary

5.11
,
which provides an effective upper bound on the solutions

to
\textnormal{{Orbit}}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.
The actual decision procedure amounts to the “guess and check” described in the proof of

To this end, our complexity results require neither finding the sequences

(Step 1), nor explicitly computing an MSTV sequence (Step 2).

For Step 1, the reduction from
\textnormal{{Orbit}}_{\mathbb{Q}}
to
\textnormal{{SimSkolem}}_{\mathbb{Q}}

which is not too large. We use a version of Siegel’s lemma, by Bombieri and Vaaler

Theorem 5.1.

Let

be a matrix with

, whose rows are linearly independent. Suppose

for all

and

. Then there are

linearly independent integral solutions
\bm{x}^{(i)}=(x^{(i)}_{1},\dots,x^{(i)}_{n})^{\top}
to

such that

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n-m}\max_{j}|x^{(i)}_{j}|\leq G^{-1}\sqrt{|\det(AA^{T})|}\,,

where

is the greatest common divisor of all the
m\times m
minors of

.

Lemma 5.2.

Let
S
be a subspace of
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
with
\dim S=d-k
.
There is a basis
\bm{w}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{w}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
of

.

Proof.

S
is specified by a set of linearly independent rational vectors

, where

. The lowest common multiple

of the denominators of all the entries has modulus at most

. Let

be an integral

whose rows are

By

, there are

Then

is exactly a basis of

.
It remains to show

to get the claimed result. By Hadamard’s inequality,

\displaystyle\leq(\max_{i,j}|(AA^{T})_{ij}|)^{t}t^{t/2}\leq(d\max_{i,j}|A_{ij}|^{2})^{t}t^{t/2}

Then we have

Proof.

Since

corresponds to an LRS in the MSTV class, it corresponds to an LRS with at most

Suppose

This lemma allows us to construct a linear combination in the MSTV class of small height. First, we need an elementary height bound on the determinant of a matrix.

Lemma 5.7.

For a matrix

\displaystyle h(\det A)\leq\frac{m}{2}\log m+m\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq m}h(a_{ij})\,.

The proof is elementary, and follows from Hadamard’s inequality and the definition of the height; it may be found in Appendix

Lemma 5.8.

Suppose there exists

If there exists a linear combination in the MSTV class, then certainly there exists

By
