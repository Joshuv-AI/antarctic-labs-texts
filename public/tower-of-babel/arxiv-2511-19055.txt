Large Language Model-Assisted Planning of Electric Vehicle Charging Infrastructure with Real-World Case Study Journal: Sustainable Energy Technologies and Assessments
Xinda Zheng, Canchen Jiang, Hao Wang

Abstract
The growing demand for electric vehicle (EV) charging infrastructure presents significant planning challenges, requiring efficient strategies for investment and operation to deliver cost-effective charging services. However, the potential benefits of EV charging assignment, particularly in response to varying spatial-temporal patterns of charging demand, remain under-explored in infrastructure planning. This paper proposes an integrated approach that jointly optimizes investment decisions and charging assignments while accounting for spatial-temporal demand dynamics and their interdependencies. To support efficient model development, we leverage a large language model (LLM) to assist in generating and refining the mathematical formulation from structured natural-language descriptions, significantly reducing the modeling burden. The resulting optimization model enables optimal joint decision-making for investment and operation. Additionally, we propose a distributed optimization algorithm based on the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) to address computational complexity in high-dimensional scenarios, which can be executed on standard computing platforms. We validate our approach through a case study using 1.5 million real-world travel records from Chengdu, China, demonstrating a 30% reduction in total cost compared to a baseline without EV assignment.

2.4
Summary

The literature establishes strong foundations in siting, behavioral modeling, and partial joint formulations, yet three gaps persist that materially affect practice. First, investment and operational assignments are seldom co-optimized at fine spatial-temporal resolution; as a result, utilization and cost outcomes remain sensitive to unmodeled spatial-temporal demand patterns. Second, constructing high-fidelity models is still expert-intensive, and existing uses of LLMs have not been systematized within EV planning workflows to reduce formulation burden while preserving reliability. Third, city-scale joint models with explicit spatial-temporal coupling remain computationally prohibitive for centralized solvers, and decomposition methods are rarely tailored to investment-assignment structure.

Accordingly, we develop an LLM-assisted joint optimization framework that explicitly couples long-horizon investment with short-horizon EV assignment over space and time. Building on this framework, we design an ADMM-based distributed algorithm that exploits the problem structure to achieve scalable solutions on standard hardware. Finally, we validate the approach using 1.5  million real-world trips from Chengdu, demonstrating a 30% reduction in total cost and improved facility utilization relative to a baseline without assignment.

LLM-Assisted problem modeling and optimization formulation

In this section, we introduce the overall problem setting and optimization framework for EV charging infrastructure planning. The urban area comprises multiple locations that differ in traffic flow intensity, investment cost, and spatial distance, leading to heterogeneous and time-varying charging demand. These factors jointly influence both infrastructure investment and EV assignment decisions. Building on this scenario, we investigate how to allocate charging capacity across locations and assign EVs efficiently, balancing long-term investment and continuous operational objectives. Furthermore, to enhance the formulation process, we propose a human-LLM collaborative framework in which the LLM constructs optimization formulations from textual problem descriptions, while human expertise iteratively guides and refines the process to ensure consistency, interpretability, and validity. The following subsections first analyze the planning problem in detail, then describe the LLM-assisted modeling procedure, and finally present the resulting mathematical formulation.

3.1
Planning problem analysis

The EV charging infrastructure planning problem comprises two tightly interconnected components, charging-capacity investment and operational EV assignment, whose mutual dependence induces a spatial-temporal coupling that shapes planning outcomes. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, the urban system contains multiple locations that differ in traffic-flow intensity, land-related investment cost, and pairwise distance. These spatial and temporal variations produce charging demand that varies across locations and over time. Each location’s investment cost is location-specific cost (e.g., land-use price), while operational assignment costs primarily arise from distance-dependent transfers of EVs between charging locations. Consequently, investment and assignment must be treated jointly rather than in isolation.

The decision process involves two roles. The investor selects capacity levels at each location subject to cost-effectiveness and budget constraints; the operator assigns EVs to charging locations in response to real-time demand and available capacity, aiming to minimize the associated operational cost. The two decisions are mutually coupled, as captured in Fig.
1
by the light orange arrows: once the charging capacity at a location is decided, it influences the upper bound in the capacity-satisfaction constraint, limiting the number of EVs that can be feasibly charged in that location (as encoded in constraint (
4
)). Conversely, the cumulative assignment patterns across time slots indicate whether the installed capacities are adequate or insufficient. High assignment costs due to long travel distances or frequent reallocation pressures may indicate the need for additional investment at certain sites. This closed-loop interaction between infrastructure availability, assignment behavior, and charging demand underlies the overall planning efficiency and cost.

The planning problem is further complicated by the spatial-temporal coupling of charging demand. Distinct demand patterns arise across different location types (e.g., residential, office, and recreational) and time periods (e.g., morning, noon, and evening), as well as between weekdays and weekends. For example, residential areas typically experience evening peaks, office areas peak around weekday, and recreational areas reach their highest demand during weekends. Consequently, planning based on a single representative period may bias investment toward certain location types while overlooking significant demand peaks elsewhere, resulting in infrastructure underutilization during off-peak hours and potential shortages during local peak periods.

These dynamics call for planning strategies that move beyond static snapshots and instead consider demand patterns holistically. Locations with short but intense demand surges may not warrant large-scale investment, as infrastructure would remain idle for much of the time. In contrast, areas with consistently high demand across multiple time slots, though requiring higher initial investment, can achieve better long-term utilization and cost-effectiveness. Attempts to avoid such investment by reallocating EVs to lower-cost areas may incur higher cumulative assignment costs, offsetting any savings. Furthermore, incorporating spatial-temporal coupling enables planners to stagger demand across both time and space, reducing peak pressure on the grid and improving overall system efficiency.

From the perspective of temporal dimension, the spatial-temporal coupling inherently links long-term investment planning with short-term operational decisions. Investment choices are infrequent and span several years or decades, reflecting strategic commitments that are costly to revise once implemented. By contrast, operational EV assignment decisions occur in short and reduplicative cycles, whose patterns are estimated from historical data, are expected to recur. It is this recurrence that binds the horizons: once the infrastructure is fixed, essentially the same assignment cycles unfold repeatedly under the same structural constraints, and the cumulative performance of these cycles determines the realized adequacy and value of the investment. This nested temporal structure highlights how long-term infrastructure design and recurrent operational behavior jointly shape system performance over time.

Ultimately, the planning system strives to achieve an optimal balance between initial investment expenses in infrastructure and recurrent operational costs associated with EV assignments. Explicitly incorporating the spatial-temporal coupling and mutual interdependencies between these decision-making horizons ensures more effective infrastructure deployment, optimized resource allocation, and promotes sustainable EV infrastructure development. Modeling such a complex, spatial-temporally coupled system manually can be laborious and error-prone, prompting the need for a more efficient modeling paradigm, as explored in the following subsection.

3.2
LLM-assisted modeling procedure overview

LLMs are transformer-based neural networks trained on large-scale text corpora to learn statistical patterns in language, capturing both syntactic structures and semantic relationships. This linguistic competence enables them to translate structured natural-language specifications into formal mathematical representations. Given semantically organized descriptions of decision variables, objective functions, and constraints, an LLM can automatically produce symbolic formulations in algebraic or modeling-language-friendly formats (e.g., linear inequalities, summations, index-based expressions). In this way, LLMs facilitate the efficient construction of optimization models directly from domain-level problem descriptions, substantially reducing the reliance on manual formulation in algebraic modeling environments.

Contrasting traditional and LLM-assisted approaches to the modeling process.

Building on this capability, we propose an LLM-assisted modeling workflow that redefines traditional model formulation by embedding natural-language interaction into the process (see Fig.
2
). In conventional workflows, planners manually analyze problems, design model structures, and construct mathematical formulations step by step, often relying on expert tuning for refinement. This process is typically labor-intensive, fragmented, and rigid, which limits its efficiency and exploratory potential.

In contrast, the proposed workflow integrates human expertise with LLM-based reasoning in an iterative and collaborative loop. The planner begins by describing the planning scenario in structured natural language, outlining decision variables, objectives, and qualitative constraints. The LLM interprets this input and generates an initial mathematical formulation as a draft model. The human expert then reviews and refines the output, identifying missing constraints, unrealistic assumptions, or ambiguous notation. Through successive exchanges, where human judgment guides machine generation, the formulation iteratively converges toward both mathematical soundness and domain relevance.

This human-LLM co-modeling paradigm fundamentally transforms the modeling landscape. Compared with traditional methods that proceed manually, laboriously, partially, and rigidly, the LLM-assisted approach operates semi-automatically, efficiently, holistically, and broadly. The transformation manifests in faster formulation, enhanced iterative flexibility, greater structural completeness, and richer exploratory potential. Collectively, these advantages enable planners to generate high-quality optimization models with significantly reduced manual effort, marking a shift from problem-solving through explicit derivation to collaborative knowledge synthesis between humans and intelligent systems.

While the LLM facilitates model development and helps structure complex optimization problems, its role is assistive rather than autonomous. The generated outputs are not guaranteed to be correct or complete and must be reviewed and refined by human experts to ensure mathematical validity and contextual relevance. In this collaborative framework, the LLM acts as a modeling assistant, supporting rapid prototyping and iteration, while deferring critical modeling decisions to domain experts.

The reliability of LLM-assisted modeling is grounded in a human-in-the-loop validation process embedded throughout the interaction. Rather than deferring correctness checks to a post hoc stage, model components are incrementally verified and refined during generation. This stepwise validation enables the detection of structural inconsistencies, ensures alignment with domain constraints, and maintains semantic coherence. As such, correctness in this workflow emerges not from the LLM’s autonomous capacity, but from a collaborative modeling dynamic in which expert judgment continuously shapes and safeguards the formulation. In summary, validation in our LLM-assisted modeling workflow relies on three key mechanisms that ensure the correctness and contextual relevance of the generated models while maintaining expert oversight throughout the process:

1.

Prompt specification
: Clear and well-specified prompts are used to accurately convey modeling intent, guiding the LLM to generate relevant and interpretable mathematical components.

2.

Iterative human-in-the-loop refinement
: Human experts interact with the LLM during model development, validating and modifying intermediate outputs to ensure semantic correctness and alignment with modeling objectives.

3.

Final expert validation
: The completed model is reviewed by experts to verify mathematical soundness, completeness, and consistency with problem-specific requirements.

\displaystyle 0\leq\beta\cdot(\alpha^{t}_{i}\cdot F^{t}_{i}-\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{I}}z^{t}_{ij}+\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{I}}z^{t-\tau_{ji}}_{ji})\leq c_{i},\forall{i,t},

(4)

\displaystyle z^{t}_{ij}\geq 0,\forall{i,j},

(5)

\displaystyle z^{t}_{ij}=0,\forall i=j,

(6)

\displaystyle z^{t}_{ij}=0,\forall i,j,\text{if }L_{ij}\geq R.

(7)

Constraint (
3
) specifies that the number of EVs assigned from location
i
to
j
must be no more than the original traffic flow at location
i
requiring charging, with
\alpha^{t}_{k}
denoting the estimated coefficient between charging demand and traffic flow at location
k
during time
t
. Constraint (
4
) ensures that the charging capacity at each location is sufficient (i.e., greater than or equal to the charging demand) in order to meet the total charging demand across all time slots, where the charging demand is calculated by incorporating EV assignments. The coupling between long-term investment and short-term assignment is explicitly captured in such a constraint. For example, the charging capacity variable
c_{i}
appears in the capacity constraint (
4
), ensuring that EV assignment flows at each time slot cannot exceed the installed infrastructure capacity. At the same time, the assignment variables
z^{t}_{ij}
aggregate across time slots and locations to determine the effective utilization of charging infrastructure, thereby influencing the optimal allocation of
c_{i}
. Thus, the constraints jointly encode how investment limits operational feasibility, while operational flows retroactively reveal the adequacy and efficiency of investment decisions.

The coefficient
\beta
represents the relationship between the number of EVs requiring charging and the charging capacity demand, describing the connection between investment in charging infrastructure and charging demand. To accurately capture the arrival dynamics of EVs assigned across locations, we introduce a delay-aware constraint. Specifically, vehicles assigned from location
j
to
i
require
\tau_{ji}
time slots to arrive. Thus, the available charging demand at i in time slot t accounts for inflows originating at
t-\tau_{ji}
. Given that variables
z^{t}_{ij}
and
z^{t}_{ji}
respectively represent the flows in opposite directions, the sign of these variables is not required to be considered. Hence, Constraint (
5
) mandates that these assignments must be non-negative, ensuring that the flow values are appropriately restricted to reflect actual capacities and prevent computational anomalies associated with negative allocations. Constraint (
6
) prevents the model from assigning vehicles to their original location to avoid causing non-convexity issues, as the distance between a location and itself is 0. Lastly, Constraint (
7
) stipulates that the distance of assignments must be within an acceptable range, denoted as
R
.

Joint investment and assignment model.
Considering the interdependence between investment and assignment, the investment and operation are integrated by incorporating a one-time investment cost and the expected recurring assignment costs over the entire investment horizon. Specifically, the operation costs are multiplied by the expected recurrences
E_{t}
for each corresponding time slot
t
, where
E_{t}
is a set of numbers. The term “expected recurrence” implies that we anticipate a similar scenario in a time slot
t
occurring
E_{t}
times throughout the investment horizon. The goal is to minimize the global investment costs for each location
i
, as well as the expected operational costs for all locations
i
and all time slots
t
over
E_{t}
expected recurrences. Therefore, the joint investment and assignment optimization problem is formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{c_{i},z^{t}_{ij}}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}}c_{i}\times(\pi_{0}+\pi_{i})

(8)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{I}}E_{t}\times z^{t}_{ij}\times L_{ij},

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\eqref{total_budget}-\eqref{range}.

In summary, the final formulation is a linear programming (LP) model, with both objective and constraints linear. The total is minimized subject to all constraints across both the investment and operational horizons. The decision variables are
c_{i}
and
z^{t}_{ij}
, corresponding to the optimal charging capacities and EV assignment decisions, respectively. The overarching goal is to achieve a globally optimized solution by minimizing the overall investment and assignment costs. This bidirectional linkage is explicitly embedded in the joint formulation, where investment decisions set the feasible capacity region and assignment decisions determine the realized utilization and costs. As such, the constraints serve as the mechanism through which long-term investment limits short-term operational feasibility, while short-term operational flows retroactively inform the adequacy and efficiency of long-term investment decisions.

4
Distributed solution method via the ADMM

As previously noted, the temporal-spatial variation in charging demand must be thoroughly considered to guarantee precise and dependable outcomes. Nevertheless, as more data are incorporated into the optimization process, the computational burden grows substantially. Moreover, when considering larger geographic regions, data privacy emerges as a potential concern for governmental bodies. A distributed algorithm can mitigate the extent of information sharing required, including sensitive data pertaining to investment budgets, capacities of power systems, and traffic flows. Consequently, we design a distributed algorithm leveraging the ADMM algorithm.

Given the complex dimensionality of the decision variables for EV assignments, particularly as more locations or time slots are incorporated, central optimization becomes less practical. Postulating a scenario involving
n
locations results in
n^{2}
location pairs. If
m
time slots are taken into consideration, there will be
n^{2}\cdot m
EV assignment decisions to be made. Therefore, this poses a substantial challenge for optimal decision computation. To overcome this computational challenge, we can decompose the computation of EV assignment decisions. In the proposed distributed algorithm, each location independently optimizes decisions regarding its own charging capacity and the assignment of EVs to other locations, treating them as parallel sub-problems. Subsequently, the algorithm only adjusts decisions related to charging capacities in a master problem and then relays the outcomes back to each sub-problem to enable result adjustments.

Specifically, based on ADMM, we introduce auxiliary variables
\tilde{c_{1}},\cdots,\tilde{c_{n}}
for
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
, and the Lagrangian multipliers (dual variables)
\lambda_{i}
for
c_{i}-\tilde{c_{i}}=0
, and we define
z^{t}_{i}=[z^{t}_{i,1},z^{t}_{i,2},...,z^{t}_{i,j}]
, where
j\in\mathcal{I}
indicating the assignment decisions made by location
i
to location
j
. Then, we derive the augmented Lagrangian function as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle f_{i}(z^{t}_{i},c_{i})+\lambda_{i}(\tilde{c_{i}}-c_{i})+\frac{\rho}{2}(\tilde{c_{i}}-c_{i})^{2},

(9)

where
\rho>0
is the penalty parameter, and the objective function for each location
i
is

\displaystyle f_{i}(z^{t}_{i},c_{i})=c_{i}\cdot(\pi_{0}+\pi_{i})+E\cdot\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}z^{t}_{i,j}\cdot L_{ij}.

(10)

Since the formulated optimization problem is a LP, it is convex, and the ADMM algorithm applies with its theoretical guarantees. Based on the augmented Lagrangian function, we divide the optimization problem into sub-problems of locations and a master problem of center. In sub-problems, each location optimizes its primal variables
c_{i}
and independent variables
z^{t}_{i}
to minimize their investment and assignment costs. Then, locations submit their variables
c_{i}
and
z^{t}_{i}
to the center while the center updates the auxiliary variables
\tilde{c_{i}}
and dual variable
\lambda_{i}
. The algorithm iterates to update the auxiliary variables and dual variables until it converges.

In each location’s sub-problem, the cost is minimized in parallel by taking the auxiliary variable
\tilde{c_{i}}
and dual variable
\lambda_{i}
as given. The sub-problem is formulated as

\displaystyle\min_{c_{i},z^{t}_{i}}

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}f_{i}(z^{t}_{i},c_{i})-\lambda_{i}\cdot c_{i}+\frac{\rho}{2}(\tilde{c_{i}}-c_{i})^{2},

(11)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\eqref{total_budget}-\eqref{range}.

Note that the primal investment-assignment problem is an LP (linear objective, affine constraints) and hence convex. For fixed multipliers, the augmented Lagrangian in Eq. (
11
) is convex in the primal variables (linear terms + convex quadratic penalty + indicator of an affine set). With the chosen splitting, each ADMM update solves a convex subproblem.

Recalling the constraints outlined in the centralized optimization problem, the distinction emerges: each location imposes constraints solely on its own decisions. Notably, Constraint (
4
) specifies that the decision regarding charging capacity must fulfill the resultant charging demand, which is based on the original traffic flow deducted by the EV assignments to other locations and augmented by assignments received from other locations. It is imperative to note that a location cannot determine the assignments it receives from other locales. Such EV assignments are provided as parameters by the overarching master problem, denoted as
z^{\prime}_{i}
. The
z^{\prime}_{i}
is made by transformation according to the EV assignment decisions
z_{i}
made from the previous iteration, and it does not require any calculation.

Besides updating the auxiliary variables
\tilde{c_{i}}
and the dual variables
\lambda_{i}
, the center manages the capacity satisfaction constraint for all locations. For the center’s master problem, it obtains primal variables
c_{i}
and also the variables
z^{t}_{i}
from all location
i\in\mathcal{I}
, and then calculates the auxiliary variables
\tilde{c_{i}}
through the master problem as

\displaystyle\min_{\tilde{c_{i}}}

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}}[\lambda_{i}(\tilde{c_{i}}-c_{i})+\frac{\rho}{2}(\tilde{c_{i}}-c_{i})^{2}],

(12)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}~\eqref{capacity_satisfaction}.

The master problem needs to satisfy Constraint (
4
), which ensures the satisfaction of charging capacity for every location. Overall, the decision variables and auxiliary variables are exchanged between each location and a computational center, as illustrated in Fig.

.

5
Case study using real-world data

To demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed model and evaluate the potential benefits it can provide for EV charging infrastructure planning with EV assignment, we conduct an in-depth case study on a joint investment and operation system using real-world data from Chengdu, China.

5.1
Data processing and optimization model parameters

The data employed in this study are sourced from the “Didi Chuxing GAIA Initiative"
[
68
]
, an anonymized dataset provided by Didi Chuxing Technology Co., Ltd-a mobile transportation platform. This dataset contains 1.5 million realistic travel records from Chengdu, China, gathered between November 21 and 27, 2016. November in Chengdu represents a typical working month without major holidays
[
69
]
or extreme weather conditions
[
70
]
, which makes it relatively stable for baseline analysis. The available data capture marked variability within a weekly operational cycle, revealing distinct weekday–weekend contrasts in both temporal intensity and spatial distribution of charging demand, such as concentrated peaks around business districts on weekdays and broader dispersion toward residential and recreational areas during weekends. We partition Chengdu into
201
distinct sub-districts for optimization purposes, each representing a unique location. The dataset incorporates travel time and destination data, expressed in longitude and latitude coordinates, which are subsequently used to calculate the initial traffic flow
F^{t}_{i}
for each location
i
and time slot
t
. We adopt one week as the operational horizon and a ten-year investment horizon, implying 520 expected recurrences; accordingly, the recurrence factor
E_{t}
is set to 520.

5.1.1
Charging demand

Traffic flow data in OD-matrix form are processed to derive time-varying traffic volumes across different districts, with origins and destinations identified from their geographic coordinates. The data are then organized into 15-minute time slots over seven days of a week, resulting in 672 distinct time slots. Adopting a 15-minute time scale offers greater precision in operations and captures more detailed fluctuations in charging demand over time. The data are then aggregated into 201 distinct locations. A zero value is imputed for cases where no traffic flow is observed for a specific time and location. This process yields a dataset comprising 135,072 (
672\times 201
) rows, enabling the determination of traffic flow distribution across each location and time slot. This distribution serves as the foundation for simulating Chengdu’s overall traffic flow and is utilized as the initial vehicular flow
F^{t}_{i}
for the optimization.

The coefficients
\alpha^{t}_{i}
in Constraints (
3
) and (
4
) represent the relationship between the traffic flow at time slot
t
in location
i
, and the number of traffic flows requiring charging. To represent the proportion of traffic flow requiring charging (denoted as
\alpha
in (
4
)), we implement it using a Beta distribution which is defined on the interval
[0,1]
and well-suited for proportion-type variables. By adjusting the shape parameters, the Beta distribution can flexibly represent various charging demand patterns and incorporate prior knowledge or spatial-temporal variability. For the case study, we set the Beta distribution parameters to
\text{Beta}(10,90)
, which assumes an average charging demand of 10% with moderate variability across different locations and time periods, ensuring smooth and stable simulation outcomes. The coefficient
\beta
in (
4
) represents the relationship between the number of traffic flows requiring charging and the demand for charging capacity. In this context, we refer to
[
71
]
, the foremost EV brand in today’s market, and adopt a fast charging power of 250 kW as the value of
\beta
. This choice is based on the expectation that fast charging will become pervasive in the future, specifically over a ten-year time horizon.

5.1.2
Distances

The distances between two locations are calculated by the average distance from all the records. We believe that the dispatch distance between two locations cannot be represented by the straight distance between two locations because the trajectory of a trip is rarely straight between two points. Moreover, a sub-district cannot be simply assumed as a point. However, real-world traffic data can provide information on which points in the region are where traffic congregates or which points are more likely to be the start and end points. We obtain the actual assignment distance by summarizing all the records of each pair of locations, taking the average value and generating the distances for optimization. The acceptable range
R
of assignment is set to 3 km, since we considered drivers are generally willing to tolerate only short detours to access charging facilities in urban contexts
[
38
]
. According to
[
72
]
, the assignment cost is estimated at
0.2
Chinese Yuan per unit distance. To more accurately represent the traffic road network topology within the context of model constraints, we define the assignment cost per EV
L_{ij}
in the optimization as

L_{ij}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{$\infty$},~\text{if}~\text{distance(}i,j\text{)}\geq R,\\
&0,~\text{if}~i=j,\\
&\text{distance(}i,j\text{)}\cdot 0.2,~\text{otherwise},\end{aligned}\right.

where distance(
i
,
j
) is the average travel distance between location
i
and location
j
.

5.1.3
Investment cost and budget

The investment cost comprises a base cost and a location-dependent cost per unit of charging capacity. The base cost per unit capacity
\pi_{0}
is estimated at
500
Chinese Yuan for rapid charging, as calculated by the price of the charging pile from
[
71
]
. The location-dependent cost per unit capacity
\pi_{i}
is approximated based on the distance between the location and the center of Chengdu, Chunxi Street, which has the highest property prices in the city
[
73
]
. Consequently, we assume that Chunxi Street, as Chengdu’s city center, possesses the highest location-dependent cost per unit capacity. Utilizing Chunxi Street as a reference point, we decrease the location-dependent costs for other locations following an exponential distribution to generate the respective costs.

According to the Chengdu EV charging and battery swapping infrastructure specialized planning by Chengdu Municipal Bureau of Economy and Information Technology
[
74
]
and the estimated investment cost for a charging station
[
75
]
, this case study estimates a maximum budget
B
of 20 billion Chinese Yuan over a ten-year investment horizon. Note that this is a synthetic study, and the budget is adjustable.

5.2
Optimization results analysis

This section illustrates the convergence of the proposed ADMM algorithm and the performance compared to the centralized method. Also, the insights on optimal planning resulted from the proposed model considering EV assignment compared to a basic flow-capture model. The optimization solving process is performed on a computer equipped with an Apple M1 Pro CPU, 16 GB random access memory (RAM), and the macOS system.

5.2.1
Performance of proposed ADMM algorithm

We validate the proposed ADMM algorithm in the case study. As described in Algorithm
1
, we iteratively set every location to solve for their own optimal decision as a single sub-problem and use the master problem to converge to a globally optimal solution. Fig.
4
presents the primal and dual errors over iterations, and the errors are in a logarithmic scale. The result shows that the algorithm is able to converge efficiently to an optimal solution in 14 iterations for our problem. The main parameter settings used in our experiments as follows: the penalty parameter
\rho
was set to 0.1, the convergence threshold
Q_{threshold}
was set to
10^{-4}
. These values follow common practice in
[
20
]
and were found to provide stable and efficient convergence in our case study. For completeness, we note that the decision variables were assigned practical bounds to ensure feasibility and numerical stability during optimization. Specifically, the upper bound of charging capacities
C_{i}
was set to
10^{7}
and the upper bound of assignment flows
Z_{ij}
was set to
10^{4}
, while the lower bounds were set to zero. These bounds are sufficiently large so as not to restrict the feasible solution space, but they prevent the algorithm from exploring unrealistic or numerically unstable regions.

:
Primal and dual errors over iterations for the ADMM approach.

For comparison, we also centrally solve the optimization problem as a baseline. We employ the
[
76
]
optimization solver to solve the optimization problem centrally at once to identify the optimal global solution for the infrastructure investment decisions for every location and their EV assignments for every time slot. The centralized approach demands 50 minutes of computational time. However, this computational process is associated with an excessively high memory consumption of 76.31 GB of RAM. Owing to the memory swapping management mechanism inherent to the macOS, the procedure is able to reach completion despite the substantial memory demands. However, this level of computational burden likely represents the upper threshold of what a standard computer can manage, particularly given the data span only one week.

The distributed optimization employing the ADMM method starkly contrasts with the centralized approach. The distributed method markedly improves efficiency, with each sub-problem and the corresponding master problem being solved in under one second. We also employ the Gurobi optimization solver in each sub-problem and master problem for calculating the optimal decision and auxiliary variables. Each sub-problem can be solved independently in parallel in an iteration. Presuming the operation within a truly distributed computing environment and discounting the latency attributable to data communication, it is feasible to complete each iteration in two seconds. Consequently, the optimization problem can be resolved in an estimated 30 seconds over the course of 14 iterations. This represents nearly a 100-fold reduction in computation time compared to the approximately 50 minutes required by the centralized solver. Moreover, the memory footprint for each sub-problem is markedly reduced, necessitating less than 500 MB of RAM. This modest memory consumption and computational burden facilitate the practicality of the distributed method for deployment on most commercial computers.

Comparing outcomes, the distributed approach incurs a total cost of 15,744 million Chinese Yuan, which is marginally higher than the 15,674 million Chinese Yuan achieved via the centralized method, representing an acceptable error margin of approximately 0.45% in total cost.

Conclusion and future work

We present an LLM-assisted framework to address the joint investment and assignment problem for EV infrastructure planning, effectively capturing the interdependence and synergy between long-term investment decisions and short-term assignment operations. To overcome computational challenges, we design a distributed ADMM algorithm that enables efficient solution of large-scale, spatial-temporal optimization problems. Our approach is validated using 1.5 million real-world travel records from Chengdu, China. The results demonstrate a 30% reduction in total system cost compared to a baseline without EV assignment, highlighting the economic benefits of explicitly capturing operational interactions. In addition, the distributed ADMM solver achieves approximately over a 100-fold reduction from minutes to seconds in computational time relative to a centralized solver, making city-scale applications tractable on standard hardware. Together, these findings underline the potential of combining LLM-assisted formulation with distributed optimization to support more effective and scalable EV infrastructure planning.

The proposed model, along with the solution method, holds significant potential for future development. The distributed algorithm facilitates efficient problem-solving through a distributed computing system, where ADMM demonstrates strong adaptability, enabling the model to incorporate a broader range of research factors related to EV charging infrastructure planning. There are several practical challenges warrant careful consideration. For instance, while our empirical analysis uses November data as a baseline month. We acknowledge, however, that demand patterns may vary across months (e.g., due to holidays or weather conditions). Extending the dataset to incorporate multiple months would allow richer capture of seasonal temporal-spatial variation.
The distributed ADMM framework is expressly designed to scale to extended horizons, enabling future work to capture seasonal temporal-spatial variation when broader datasets are available. Moreover, the charging time for EVs with existing technology is significant, impacting the overall system and user experience

. The potential for pile-ups or queues of EVs at charging stations, as well as additional delays arising from traffic congestion or traffic lights, may further increase waiting time and should be accounted for in future research. Additionally, as time considerations become more intricate, examining the electricity price at varying times and locations is crucial, as they profoundly affect operational costs. Furthermore, although current capabilities remain limited, the rapid development of LLMs suggests the potential for their integration into the design of optimization algorithms in the future, which may lead to further improved efficiency in solving complex problems. In future work, we aim to incorporate a more comprehensive power system that includes power grid, renewable energy sources, and energy storage system. Additionally, we will consider bi-directional charging (e.g. vehicle-to-grid or vehicle-to-vehicle) and charging queue management while integrating stochastic elements into our problem modeling.
