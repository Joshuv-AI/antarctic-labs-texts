Expanding Access to Early Diabetes Detection: A Pharmacy‐Based Screening Pilot in Rural New South Wales
Authors: Osuagwu Levi Uchechukwu, Ross Wilson, Kingsley Agho, David Simmons, Krista Reed, Eme Eseme John, Siddarth Sethi, Peter Simmons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsType 2 diabetes mellitus remains a major public health challenge in Australia. This cross‐sectional pilot feasibility study evaluated the feasibility of pharmacy‐led diabetes screening in rural New South Wales and examined diabetes knowledge, AUSDRISK, and follow‐up behavior after referral.MethodsThis was a 5‐month (January–May 2023) cross‐sectional pilot feasibility study conducted across community pharmacies within Western New South Wales LHD. Pharmacies were enrolled, into the study following expression of interest. Point‐of‐care HbA1c and/or random blood glucose (RBG) testing was conducted for eligible participants. All individuals with abnormal results were referred to general practitioners (GP) and followed up by a clinician. Descriptive analysis was performed using R (version 4.3.1). All tests were two‐sided with α = 0.05.ResultsOf the 17 enrolled pharmacies, 9 completed the study with a total of 116 adults recruited from the participating pharmacies. Ninety‐five participants completed the Diabetes Knowledge Questionnaire and AUSDRISK assessment. Seventy‐six percent (76.6% [95% CI: 67.1%, 84.0%]) were classified as high risk using AUSDRISK. Fifty‐seven percent (57.7% [95% CI: 47.7%, 67.2%]) had elevated HbA1c and/or RBG levels. Following GP referral, only 15% attended follow‐up testing. Among attendees, 44% were diagnosed with diabetes.ConclusionPharmacy‐based diabetes screening is operationally feasible in rural Australia and identifies substantial undiagnosed diabetes. However, effective linkage to primary care remains a significant challenge.Keywords: diabetes knowledge test (DKT), opportunistic screening, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)

Introduction

Early detection of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is essential in rural communities with limited access to specialist care. Community pharmacies provide accessible venues for opportunistic screening. Although diabetes knowledge may influence self‐management, knowledge alone does not ensure healthcare engagement or behavioral change, particularly where structural barriers such as cost and appointment availability exist [1, 2, 3]. T2DM presents a significant challenge in Australia, affecting approximately 1.2 million (4.6%) individuals, with around 125 new diagnoses daily [4]. The prevalence of T2DM has seen an alarming 80% increase between 2000 and 2012 [5], emphasizing the growing burden on public health resources. In rural communities with limited access to specialist care, the need for early screening and identification of diabetes is even more pronounced. Opportunistic testing by allied health services, including pharmacists [6, 7], for screening purposes has been identified as a way of bridging the gap between healthcare services and underserved populations [8, 9]. Pharmacists, often accessible and trusted members of rural communities, have the potential to significantly enhance the reach and effectiveness of public health efforts aimed at early detection and intervention for diabetes control, and to improve health outcomes in underserved areas.

Evidence of the effectiveness of opportunistic screening in improving early diagnosis of diabetes is mounting, and this study employed pharmacists for diabetes screening in rural communities. Studies have shown a “fair” yield from an opportunistic targeted diabetes screening, and that a 12‐month pharmacy care program for T2DM reduced glycemia and blood pressure, and proved to be a valuable component of primary and community‐based multidisciplinary diabetes care [10, 11]. Another opportunistic screening of 5977 visitors to community pharmacies in Piedmont, Italy, revealed that 53% were at risk of developing diabetes and identified 51 diabetes cases [12]. An Australian study further reported that sequential screening was cheaper and had a significantly higher rate of diagnosis of diabetes compared to completing the tick test assessment [13].

The Central West of New South Wales, Australia, spans hundreds of kilometers. It has several small regional cities (Bathurst, Orange, Dubbo), but is largely a rural setting that struggles in many parts to recruit an adequate health workforce. Many have to travel long distances for care. Bathurst is relatively well served within this region, but specialist endocrinology services are available only once weekly, leading to reliance on general practitioners (GPs) for diabetes screening [14, 15, 16]. In 2023–2024, 36.3% of people living in outer regional, remote, or very remote areas reported waiting longer than they felt was acceptable for a GP appointment compared to those living in major cities [17], and the public diabetes clinics do not usually prioritize uncomplicated T2DM. Previous studies have largely focused on diabetes knowledge screening or cost‐effectiveness rather than knowledge–behavior relationships and referral completion. The current study extends prior Australian pharmacy screening by integrating a validated diabetes knowledge assessment (Diabetes Knowledge Questionnaire [DKQ]) and real‐world GP linkage data within a rural local health district (LHD) context.

The impact of awareness and knowledge on diabetes prevention cannot be overstated. Equipping individuals with an understanding of their health condition empowers them to make informed lifestyle choices, thereby reducing the risk of progression to full‐fledged diabetes and associated complications, particularly cardiovascular diseases [18]. However, the proportion of people who reported that cost was a reason for delaying or not seeing a GP when needed increased to 8.8% in 2023–2024, from 7.0% in 2022–2023 [17]. This presents a substantial barrier to early detection [19, 20].

While knowledge is an important component of diabetes prevention and self‐management, it should not be interpreted as a direct driver of healthcare engagement. Behavioral science and health services research consistently show that knowledge operates within broader structural and behavioral contexts that shape health actions. Individuals may understand their risk and still be unable or unwilling to seek care because of barriers such as cost, appointment availability, travel distance, competing life priorities, or low perceived urgency of symptoms detection [19, 20]. In this sense, knowledge can be considered a necessary but insufficient condition for behavior change, requiring supportive health system structures and behavioral facilitation to translate awareness into action [1, 21]. Studies on chronic disease prevention indicate that educational interventions alone rarely produce sustained behavioral change unless they are integrated with structural supports such as accessible referral pathways, navigation assistance, and reduced financial or logistical barriers to care [1, 21, 22]. Recognizing this interaction between knowledge, behavior, and structural context is particularly important in rural health settings, where access constraints may limit the translation of risk awareness into timely medical follow‐up.

This study aimed to (1) pilot the efficacy and feasibility of pharmacy‐led diabetes screening, (2) assess diabetes knowledge and risk among participants, and (3) examine follow‐up behavior after referral to GPs.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was secured from the Human Research Ethics Committee (H15352). Additional approvals were obtained from the Pharmacy Guild Australia, which assisted with issuing the call for interest to pharmacy stores in the region. Participants provided verbal and written informed consent, ensuring their rights to anonymity and confidentiality before testing and data collection. Additional consent was obtained for the care referral of cases with abnormal findings from blood testing and follow‐up with their blood test results.

Study Design and Setting

This was a 5‐month cross‐sectional pilot feasibility study conducted across community pharmacies within Western New South Wales LHD. Western New South Wales is the largest LHD in NSW, covering an area of 246,676 km2. The region is characterized by a predominantly white population majority born in Australia (83.2%), with an estimated population of 279,000 people and a median age of 38 years. Administratively, this district is made up of 22 local government areas (LGAs) [23]. With a population of 43,567 as of 2021 [24], Bathurst residents grapple with a diabetes prevalence rate of 6.1%, surpassing the national average by 33% or a 40% higher likelihood of succumbing to diabetes compared to the rest of the state of New South Wales [25]. The Bathurst region also hosts a notable Indigenous population, comprising 7.2% of the community [26]. Indigenous adults exhibit suboptimal clinical control and poorer outcomes compared to their non‐Indigenous counterparts [27]. The reporting of this study followed the CONSORT guidelines as much as possible, although this study is not a randomized trial [28].

Pharmacy Store Recruitment

An invitation to participate in this study was sent to pharmacy stores in various regional communities in the LHD through the Pharmacy Guild. Twenty pharmacies responded and indicated their interest in participating in the study. Three of the responding pharmacies were located outside the areas of interest and were excluded, and the remaining 17 were enrolled in the study.

A 2‐day training workshop was conducted online by the researchers before the recruitment of pharmacy representatives from each store. To ensure consistency and reduce recruitment variability, a standardized recruitment script was provided to all pharmacists, who were trained on its implementation during the 2‐day workshop. The training covered information about the project, project design, method of recruitment, data collection, obtaining consent, and blood testing using the devices. A nominated pharmacy representative from participating stores was trained, and the individual was assigned to train other members of their team. Fortnightly meetings were held with the partners during the set‐up month, and subsequently, monthly meetings once the data collection had commenced. The research team provided training on data collection/management and obtaining consent, while ROCHE Diabetes Care provided training on the use of the COBAS 101 (Roche Diagnostics portable point‐of‐care analyzer for HbA1c/RBG testing) and random blood glucose (RBG) machines. Each store received the test machines and the lancet devices, test strips, and quality control pack at no cost. They were allowed to promote the program through their media team and social media handles using the provided flier (see Supporting Information S2: Appendix 2). Each store generated a list of local GPs for referral of participants with no known GP. At the end of the program, stores were allowed to keep the HbA1c machines at a highly discounted rate if they wished, with a 6‐month free supply of the lancet devices. The researchers visited stores from time to time to monitor the screening. Three stores agreed to keep the machines. Another opportunity for retraining staff was offered during the study period.

Staff at each pharmacy store were tasked with approaching visiting customers with scripted information about the project and asking for consent to take part in the study. Participants without a history of diabetes completed the Australian Type 2 Diabetes Risk Assessment Tool (AUSDRISK) evaluation, along with a DKQ, and had their waist circumference measured for AUSDRISK score computation.

Following the training, stores were randomly allocated to one of two groups by a researcher using computer‐generated random numbers. Figure 1 illustrates the study design. Group 1 stores offered HbA1c to visiting participants, while Group 2 provided RBG during the initial phase of data collection. Demographic information was collected from the surveys. Participants completed the AUSDRISK and DKQ assessments, and the pharmacists were not blinded to the scores, as this was factored into the decision for testing referrals. Those at moderate risk (with an overall AUSDRISK score between 6 and 11) or high risk (with an overall score of 12 or more) [29, 30] underwent HbA1c or RBG testing. Individuals with elevated results were referred to their GP for confirmatory testing. The HbA1c/RBG testing phase was blinded from the AUSDRISK/DKQ phase.

In Month 3, processes mirrored those of Month 2, except that the blood test technique offered in each store was swapped to give staff equal opportunity to participate in the use of both test techniques. However, in the final month of blood testing, all participating stores conducted HbA1c and RBG testing for all interested participants, regardless of prior diabetes risk assessment, with no AUSDRISK screening utilized. This option was introduced to ensure that participants who may have been excluded due to AUSDRISK scores were also captured in the survey [31, 32]. This meant that all individuals were screened during the last month of the study, rather than only moderate‐ to high‐risk individuals. Participants who were identified with an elevated risk for diabetes were referred to their GPs for confirmatory tests and follow‐up.

The minimum sample was calculated using Cochran's formula for a single proportion at 95% confidence, an expected local diabetes prevalence of 6.1%, and an absolute precision of 5 percentage points, which gave n 0 = 88. Finite population correction was negligible for both Bathurst (N = 43,567) and the wider LHD (N = 279,000), so we targeted about 90 participants and inflated by 20% to account for nonresponse, giving a recruitment target of 108. We enrolled 116 participants, of whom 95 provided complete knowledge and risk data. Because recruitment occurred within pharmacies, we considered the impact of clustering. With an average cluster size of 13 and plausible ICC values of 0.01–0.02 for questionnaire outcomes, the design effect is approximately 1.12–1.24, corresponding to an effective sample size of 94–104. This precision is appropriate for a feasibility study and aligns with the study's aims to estimate proportions with 5 percentage point precision and to inform parameters for a larger trial.

The study randomly recruited adults who attended participating pharmacies across Western NSW LHD in rural NSW for routine purchases and met predefined eligibility criteria. The sample size estimation was based on a previous study from a similar population in rural NSW [33]. Eligible participants were aged 18 years and older, lived in Western NSWLHD, presented to participating pharmacy stores during the study period, and completed the survey as part of diabetes awareness during the pharmacy‐led diabetes screening project. Otherwise, they were excluded if they were younger than 18 years, opted out, or were unable to provide consent. Pharmacy staff were also excluded as they could have prior knowledge of the answers to the questions.

Outcomes

Primary outcomes included the feasibility of screening, the prevalence of high‐risk classification, the prevalence of T2DM, and GP follow‐up attendance. Secondary outcomes included DKQ scores and exploratory subgroup comparisons.

Data Collection Tools

Assessment of the Level of Diabetes Knowledge of Participants

Diabetes knowledge was assessed using the validated DKQ [34], which has been widely used in previous studies [33, 35, 36, 37]. The Supporting Information: S1 presents the survey which consists of 31 diabetes‐related items categorized into key areas: general knowledge of diabetes (5 items), complications (6 items), diet (foods that have high fat and sugar contents) (8 items), lifestyle (4 items), symptoms (4 items) and risk factors of diabetes (4 items). Correct responses were allocated 1 point, with a cumulative maximum score of 31. The questions were designed to gather an understanding of the participant's level of diabetes knowledge and, subsequently, use that information to build an understanding of the gap in health literacy existing in the community.

Assessment of the Level of Diabetes Risk of Participants

The AUSDRISK tool was employed to assess each participant's risk of diabetes. This tool is a simple, non‐invasive method designed to evaluate the risk level for T2DM, comprising 10 items with a maximum score of 35 [38]. This AUSDRISK tool was developed and validated for the Australian population using a sample of 6060 Australians aged 25 years and older who were followed for 5 years and did not have diabetes at baseline (area under the operating characteristic curve = 0.78 [95% CI, 0.76–0.81]) [38]. Participants falling into the “low risk” category scored 5 or lower, those in the “intermediate risk” category scored between 6 and 11, and individuals scoring 12 or higher were classified as “high risk” [38]. All surveys were web‐based, created in Qualtrics, with the e‐link made available to each participating store and a QR code to be posted in strategic locations within the stores.

Blood Test for Diabetes (HbA1c and RBG)

Participants identified as having intermediate or high risk on the AUSDRISK were offered free point‐of‐care HbA1c testing using the DCA Vantage Analyzer (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics Ltd., Camberley, UK) and RBG testing using the Accu‐Chek Performa blood glucose meter (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). Sixty individuals with HbA1c levels ≥ 5.7% (39 mmol/mol) and/or RBG levels ≥ 5.5 mmol/L were referred to their GPs for further diagnostic testing, aligning with NHMRC recommendations for case detection. Uptake of Referrals to GP and Diabetes Diagnosis

After the blood tests, participants who were referred and provided their contact information were followed up via phone calls and emails from the research team (medical professional) to confirm whether they secured an appointment with the GP, attended the GP appointment, and the result of their Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) results if the GP had requested it.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used. Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied for subgroup comparisons. Missing data were handled using complete‐case analysis. Subgroup analyses were exploratory and not adjusted for multiple testing. We analyzed the demographic information, the DKQ scores, attendance at GP appointments, and diabetes diagnosis. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were used to present the results. For analysis of the DKQ, the scores were not normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk tests W = 0.97, p = 0.02, mean = 26.62, median = 27.0, skewness = −0.19, kurtosis = −0.71), so the median (interquartile range [IQR]) for each section was calculated (general knowledge of diabetes, knowledge of complications, diet, lifestyle, symptoms, and risk factors of diabetes) based on the total possible score in that section. Median differences in the DKQ for dichotomous categories of variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon (W) test, while the Kruskal–Wallis (H) test was used for comparing variables with more than two categories. Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.3.2 [39]. All tests were two‐sided, and an a priori significance level of α = 0.05 was established.

However, 4 dropped off due to a shortage of staff, a change in priority, and financial difficulties following the change in pharmacy prescribing announced by the Commonwealth government at the start of the project. By the end of the project, 9 of the 17 (52.9%) pharmacy stores completed the project, recruiting 116 participants, aged (57 ± 18), and mostly females (56.9%). Only 95 participants completed both knowledge and risk assessments due to incomplete questionnaires and staff shortages at some pharmacies. Upon follow‐up, it was observed that the majority (85%) of the referred participants did not undergo the OGTT for various reasons. These included difficulties in securing a GP appointment (2%), failure to return to check the OGTT result (2%), and outright refusal to undergo the test (8%). While more referrals were generated through RBG testing (51.7%) compared to HbA1c (36%), the low overall follow‐up rate (15%) precluded a statistically significant comparison of follow‐up behavior between these two groups.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that pharmacy‐led diabetes screening is both feasible and acceptable in a rural Australian setting. Community pharmacies successfully administered the AUSDRISK tool alongside point‐of‐care HbA1c and RBG testing, identifying a substantial burden of previously unrecognized diabetes risk. More than three‐quarters of screened individuals were classified as high risk, and over half had elevated glycaemic markers suggestive of diabetes or prediabetes. These findings align with prior rural screening studies reporting similarly high prevalence [12]. In regions where specialist and primary care access is constrained, pharmacies can serve as accessible entry points for early risk detection.

The importance of timely detection is well established, as tight glycaemic control reduces the risk of diabetes‐related complications [41, 42, 43]. Pharmacy‐based screening addressed the detection component of this pathway; however, without coordinated referral mechanisms, identification alone does not translate into improved clinical outcomes. Similar patterns have been reported in systematic reviews of pharmacy‐based screening programs, which demonstrate high detection rates but inconsistent referral completion [30, 44].

Operational feasibility was supported by strong participant uptake and the ability of pharmacies to integrate screening into routine workflows, albeit with noted resource constraints. Subgroup analyses indicated higher knowledge scores among participants taking blood pressure medication, engaging in regular physical activity, and those previously informed of elevated blood glucose levels. These findings suggest that prior healthcare engagement may enhance diabetes awareness. However, no consistent differences were observed across most demographic subgroups, and moderate knowledge levels did not translate into improved follow‐up behavior.

While screening and risk identification were successfully achieved, effective linkage to care was not. Fewer than 15% of referred participants attended GP follow‐up, limiting the clinical impact of screening. This gap between detection and diagnostic confirmation represents the central implementation challenge identified in this study. Despite a median diabetes knowledge of 4 (maximum of 5), awareness alone did not result in health‐seeking behavior, consistent with evidence that knowledge is insufficient to drive action [45]. Low referral uptake likely reflects both structural and behavioral barriers. Structural constraints included limited GP appointment availability, travel burden, and out‐of‐pocket costs, consistent with national primary care utilization data [17, 46, 47]. Behavioral factors such as perceived low urgency or competing priorities may have further reduced attendance. The observed knowledge–behavior gap reinforces the need for integrated referral systems and active patient navigation to improve care linkage.

The AUSDRISK tool proved valuable in stratifying risk using readily obtainable demographic and lifestyle factors and supporting personalized preventive counseling [38, 48]. However, its utility depends on embedding risk assessment within structured care pathways.

Strengths and Limitations

This pilot study provides preliminary evidence of the feasibility and acceptability of pharmacy‐led diabetes screening in rural Australia, where workforce shortages and limited specialist care are ongoing challenges. Its community‐based participatory design, leveraging pharmacist–community relationships, enhanced engagement and reflected real‐world conditions, supporting the scalability of pharmacy‐led screening within primary care. The use of validated tools (DKQ, AUSDRISK) enabled national and international comparison, while combining HbA1c and RBG testing improved diagnostic accuracy. The study adds novel insights from a rural cohort often underrepresented in national diabetes surveillance.

The study limitations include a small sample size (n = 95), which limits statistical power and generalizability, and the cross‐sectional design, which prevents causal inference. Recruitment through pharmacies may have introduced selection bias, as participants were likely more health‐conscious than the broader rural population. The study also relied on self‐reported lifestyle data, subject to recall and social desirability bias. Operationally, variable pharmacy participation created recruitment inconsistencies, and low GP follow‐up (15%) after abnormal results reflected systemic barriers to rural primary care access [46, 47, 49].

The generalizability of these findings should be interpreted in the context of the Western New South Wales LHD in which the study was conducted. Healthcare access in this region is characterized by workforce shortages, extended GP appointment waiting times, and travel burden. While these constraints are common across many rural Australian regions, healthcare organizations, funding models, and pharmacists' scope of practice vary internationally. Therefore, although the feasibility of pharmacy‐based screening and the high prevalence of undetected risk are likely broadly relevant, referral completion rates and system integration challenges may differ in settings with stronger primary care integration or expanded pharmacist prescribing roles. For pharmacy‐based screening models to translate into improved clinical outcomes, structural integration with primary care is essential. Our findings suggest that the most critical system‐level changes include electronic referral pathways enabling direct booking or secure data transfer between pharmacists and GPs; funded or subsidized follow‐up pathology to minimize cost barriers; collaborative practice arrangements allowing pharmacists to initiate confirmatory testing; and structured reminder or patient navigation systems to support care linkage. Screening programs that operate without embedded referral infrastructure risk identifying high‐risk individuals without ensuring timely diagnostic confirmation or management. Future implementation research should evaluate integrated digital referral models and shared‐care frameworks to strengthen linkage to care in rural populations.

Despite these limitations, the study demonstrates the feasibility of pharmacy‐based diabetes screening and informs the design of larger trials incorporating system‐level strategies to improve GP follow‐up and care continuity in rural Australia.

Conclusion and Recommendation

This study's results indicate that specific health behaviors and medical conditions have a discernible impact on diabetes knowledge levels. Notably, participants who engaged in regular physical activity and those who were aware of their high blood glucose levels demonstrated higher levels of diabetes complications and knowledge, respectively. The absence of significant differences in knowledge levels across age groups and genders emphasizes the necessity for targeted interventions to address specific risk factors. Furthermore, the study underscores the importance of integrating lifestyle factors into diabetes education programs, aiming to enhance overall knowledge and awareness. This prompts the need for additional research to delve into these associations more comprehensively and to formulate targeted interventions for specific at‐risk groups. The findings underscore the imperative for targeted interventions to enhance diabetes awareness, with a focus on specific health behaviors and medical conditions. The study emphasizes the pivotal roles that lifestyle factors, including regular physical activity and awareness of high blood glucose levels, play in augmenting diabetes knowledge among participants. There is a call for increased efforts in diabetes education in rural areas to empower individuals to make necessary lifestyle changes, thereby promoting health and well‐being even among individuals without diabetes. This approach has the added advantage of potentially alleviating the burden on the already stretched health systems in rural communities, as diabetes cases and complications may be mitigated through proactive education. This pilot study demonstrates the feasibility of pharmacy‐based diabetes screening in rural Australia. However, low GP follow‐up rates highlight persistent structural and behavioral barriers to care. Further research is warranted to develop tailored diabetes education interventions for specific at‐risk groups and strengthen follow‐up and the linkage of primary care to other service providers.

Recent advances in data‐driven approaches, such as technology‐enabled risk stratification using glucose profiling, offer opportunities to strengthen early detection of diabetes when paired with accessible platforms like pharmacy‐led programs [50]. Scaling this model effectively requires structural integration, specifically: (1) Electronic referral pathways for direct GP booking; (2) Funded follow‐up pathology to remove cost barriers; and (3) Expanded pharmacist scope to allow initiation of confirmatory testing under protocol. In summary, this pilot study demonstrates the operational feasibility of pharmacy‐based screening in rural Australia; however, the low GP follow‐up rates underscore critical systemic barriers to care linkage that must be addressed in future shared‐care frameworks.
