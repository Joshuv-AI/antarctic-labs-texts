Dynamic Latent Class Structural Equation Modeling: A Hands-On Tutorial for Modeling Intensive Longitudinal Data
Roberto Faleh ∗ ⁣ † {}^{*\,\dagger}, Sofia Morelli ∗, Vivato Andriamiarana, Zachary J. Roman, Christoph Flückiger, Holger Brandt

Abstract
In this tutorial, we provide a hands-on guideline on how to implement complex Dynamic Latent Class Structural Equation Models (DLCSEM) in the Bayesian software JAGS. We provide building blocks starting with simple Confirmatory Factor and Time Series analysis, and then extend these blocks to Multilevel Models and Dynamic Structural Equation Models (DSEM). Leading through the tutorial is an example from clinical psychology using data on a generalized anxiety treatment that includes scales on anxiety symptoms and the Working Alliance Inventory that measures alliance between therapists and patients. Within each block, we provide an overview, specific hypotheses we want to test, the resulting model and its implementation as well as an interpretation of the results. The aim of this tutorial is to provide a step-by-step guide for applied researchers that enables them use this flexible DLCSEM framework for their own analyses.

Translational Abstract

In psychological treatment, people’s symptoms and emotional experiences often shift over time—and not everyone changes in the same way. Clinicians and researchers need tools to understand these patterns, especially when working with data collected repeatedly over days or weeks. This tutorial introduces a method called Dynamic Latent Class Structural Equation Modeling (DLCSEM) that helps identify different types of change in therapy outcomes. For example, some people may show steady improvement, while others follow more complex or delayed paths. Using real-world data from people undergoing treatment for anxiety, we show how this method can reveal important differences in how people respond to therapy. The goal is to give mental health professionals and applied researchers a tool to better understand individual progress, which can support more personalized treatment planning. The tutorial is designed to be practical and clear, with step-by-step guidance using free, open-source software—making it accessible for anyone working with rich, time-based psychological data.

Introduction

Intensive longitudinal data (ILD) have become an extremely important data source for researchers in clinical and health psychology (e.g.,
[
34
,
50
]
). ILD capture information from multiple subjects observed over numerous time intervals, ranging from thirty to several hundred distinct time points
[
6
]
. Often, ILD include information from several sources, such as self-reports, physiological, behavioral, environmental and geographic data (e.g.,
[
27
]
).

The growing availability of mobile phones, smartwatches, and other personal wearable devices has made ILD collection more accessible and cost-effective
[
47
]
.

The collection of ILD in intervention studies (Ecological Momentary Assessment; EMA) has several advantages compared to traditional intervention studies with few time points
[
31
]
. Self-reports are not as strongly affected by biases caused by retrospective recalls of events because data is obtained in real-time and natural settings, thus increasing their generalizability and ecological validity
[
52
]
.

The richness of ILD enables researchers to capture both intra- and inter-individual processes and dynamic changes, enhancing the ability to predict future events and improve individual forecasts. This is particularly important for identifying potential relapse triggers or the conditions necessary for optimal treatment efficacy (e.g.,
[
15
]
).

Recently, new Bayesian approaches of Dynamic Structural Equation Modeling (DSEM) and its extension to Dynamic Latent Class Structural Equation Modeling (DLCSEM) were developed that are targeted at analyzing ILD (e.g.,
[
3
,
4
,
35
]
).

DSEM can be viewed as a combination of time-series models and multilevel SEM. The time-series model on the within-level can be set up for both observed and latent variables. It can include all types of auto-regressive (AR) and moving-average (MA) specifications.
Each within-level coefficient can be specified as a random effect to account for person- and time-specific effects. Person-specific random effects provide information about individual differences. This information can produce more precise forecasts of individual trajectories and, for example, improve designing tailored interventions. Time-specific random effects describe dynamical (e.g., accelerated) changes over time
[
23
]
.
The between model can include further information such as (observed or latent) baseline covariates.

DLCSEM builds upon the foundation established by DSEM but introduces a key extension: a latent class model that captures categorical shifts over time, referred to as ’states.’ Unlike traditional Latent Class Analysis (LCA), where state membership remains static (as in growth curve mixture models), DLCSEM allows state membership to change dynamically over time. This flexibility allows individuals to follow different state-specific processes at each time point, offering a more detailed representation of temporal dynamics and deeper insights into the underlying dynamical process.

The ability of DLCSEM to identify critical states, detect stable a priori differences, account for unobserved heterogeneity, and capture intra-individual changes extends the capabilities of traditional dynamic modeling. It enables real-time dynamic classification of individual states, providing a powerful tool for personalized evaluations and prompt interventions. Moreover, it offers aggregated results comparable to those obtained from DSEM, supporting the evaluation of treatments while also classifying individuals into distinct states based on their dynamic responses.

In
Kelava et al. 2022
, the framework was used to investigate student dropout from university. Their findings revealed that the latent state variable acted as a proxy for students’ intention to drop out. This variable was shown to predict actual dropout, which typically occurred, on average, six measurement occasions after the estimated intention to drop out. Additionally, they identified relevant time-varying covariates, such as negative affect, which significantly predicted this shift beyond baseline covariates like cognitive skills.

Roman et al. 2024
used DLCSEM to identify inattentive response patterns in long questionnaires. In this application, one latent state represented the response pattern for attentive participants using a hypothesized factor structure; and the second state modeled inattentive response behavior using person-specific parameters that are independent of the underlying constructs that were supposed to be measured.

Flückiger et al. 2022
applied the DLCSEM framework on the dimensional structure of Working alliance inventory WAI
[
32
]
. They showed that patients’ response patterns changed over the course of a psychotherapeutic intervention. While patients used a three-dimensional scale initially that represented the subfacets of the working alliance, the majority of the patients started to follow a single-dimensional scale (global factor) during later sessions.

Scope and Outline

In this tutorial, we will provide a step-by-step, didactic guide on implementing Bayesian latent variable models for ILD. We start from basic concepts to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the foundational tools and methods, and gradually build up to more complex frameworks. We will introduce simple Bayesian implementations of Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), Time Series Models, and Multilevel Models (illustrations 1 through 3).
Then, we will combine these approaches into a Dynamic Structural Equation Model (DSEM) in illustration 4. Finally, we will extend this approach to Dynamic Latent Class Structural Equation Modeling (DLCSEM) and provide to illustrations: a model that captures sudden gains in therapy progression (illustration 5) and a model that captures dimensional shifts in the underlying factor model (illustration 6).

This tutorial is designed to provide a complete toolbox, equipping the readers with all the necessary concepts and techniques for understanding and applying the final, complex models. For readers who are already familiar with the foundational material, sections can be skipped as needed, allowing them to focus on advanced topics.

All materials used in this paper, along with additional examples, are available in the GitHub repository:
https://github.com/PsychometricsMZ/dsem_tutorial
.

1
Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM)

Before we explore ILD-specific time series analysis, we will provide a brief overview of latent variables and the techniques used to estimate them in a static setting(a more detailed introduction, see e.g.,
[
7
,
38
]
). These methods provide the foundation for more complex models and may assist in selecting meaningful hyperparameters in dynamic settings, such as the initial number of factors.

Latent variables are unobserved constructs that cannot be measured directly, like intelligence or democracy
[
54
]
. Factor Analysis (FA) is used to infer these underlying constructs from observed indicators by separating systematic variance from random error. This is achieved by minimizing the difference between the observed covariance matrix and the one implied by the model. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) uncovers latent structures without imposing constraints, whereas Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) tests specific factor structures derived from theoretical expectations. Bayesian CFA
[
37
]
further extends this framework by allowing for the incorporation of prior information and providing more flexibility in parameter estimation.

CFA may be seen as a special case of Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), where no structural relationships among latent variables are modeled
[
43
]
. SEM extends CFA by combining factor models with linear regressions to capture hypothesized relationships between latent constructs
[
8
]
.

In the following, we demonstrate Bayesian CFA in a cross-sectional setting. As a general strategy, applying CFA to selected time points can provide an initial understanding of the measurement structure over time to inform the setup of a full dynamic model.

2
Time series models

Time series models have become indispensable tools in various scientific disciplines, including psychology, for analyzing temporal data. Originally developed for single-case studies and econometric data
[
10
,
30
]
, time series analysis has been increasingly applied to understand dynamic processes within individuals
[
28
]
.

In psychology, such single-case experimental designs leverage longer time series from individuals to explore and describe underlying psychological processes.

Autoregressive
\operatorname{AR}

[
10
]
or moving average (
\operatorname{MA}
) models and their combination
\operatorname{ARMA}
[
11
]
, or more advanced variants like autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity (
\operatorname{ARCH}
) models
[
18
]
and their generalized form
\operatorname{GARCH}

[
9
]
, are standard statistical tools for modeling such data.
5
5

5

Alternative approaches, including continuous time models
[
57
]
and seasonal extensions of ARMA (e.g., SARIMA; LIT), are not discussed here as they have not yet been applied to DLCSEM.

AR models are often seen as more appropriate for behavior that is caused by regular, stable, internal mechanisms, such as addictive behaviors or progression of depressive symptoms
[
14
]
. MA models are more dynamic in the sense that external, occasional events affect the change process, such as for occasional smokers or drinkers (e.g.,
[
56
]
).
The attention in the following will be focused on the
\operatorname{AR}
models of order 1, because these models offer good flexibility, are parsimonious and can describe in a simple way complex psychological processes that are suitable for this didactical work.

2.1
Introduction of
\operatorname{AR}
processes

A key feature of AR models is their ability to preserve a “long memory” by explicitly modeling dependencies across time points.

A general
\operatorname{AR}(p)
process linearly depends on the outcomes from the previous
p
time steps:

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=\alpha+\beta_{1}y_{t-1}+\ldots+\beta_{p}y_{t-p}+\varepsilon_{t}

(2)

\displaystyle=\alpha+\sum_{k=1}^{p}\beta_{k}y_{t-k}+\varepsilon_{t},

where
p
is called the order of the process. The difference between time points
t
and
t-t_{k}
is termed lag. Higher-order processes (larger
p
) include more lags, allowing the model to account for broader, direct influences of past outcomes on future values.

The error term
\varepsilon_{t}
introduces random “shocks” that propagate through time via the autoregressive structure. They are modeled as a white noise sequence, defined to have zero mean (
E(\varepsilon_{t})=0
), constant variance (
E(\varepsilon_{t}^{2})=\sigma^{2}
), and no autocorrelation (
E(\varepsilon_{t},\varepsilon_{\nu})=0
, for
t\neq\nu
). While it is not required for
\varepsilon_{t}
to follow a normal distribution, we assume it to be normally distributed in our examples:

\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\right).

(3)

A first-order autoregressive process,
\operatorname{AR}(1)
, is often sufficient for modeling simple psychological phenomena
[
14
]
. It is defined as

y_{t}=\alpha+\beta y_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t},

(4)

including only one lag. The parameter
\alpha
acts as intercept. The parameter
\beta
is the autoregressive coefficient determining the persistence of the memory over time.

Even with a single lag, each step continues to propagate the influence of past errors, creating an infinite memory. If
\mid\beta\mid>1
, then any shocks described by the white noise process
\varepsilon_{t}
will accumulate over time. However, such non-stationary behavior is rare in natural systems, as feedback mechanisms often prevent unbounded growth. For instance, in predator-prey dynamics, increases in prey lead to more predators, stabilizing the system. In biological systems, various mechanisms are employed to provide negative feedback to disturbances, enabling the system to restore equilibrium, a process commonly referred to as homeostasis
[
16
]
.

For this reason, we typically assume stationarity of the time series, with
\mid\beta\mid\ <1
such that the effects of past events fade over time. This can be interpreted as a gradually diminishing memory.

Multilevel modeling (MLM)

In the previous section, we examined the development of a single patient over time. We now extend this time series modeling approach to data collected at multiple time points from multiple patients, building what is known as a longitudinal model
[
49
]
. This approach allows us to capture diverse trajectories across patients, making it possible to study inter-individual differences in a structured way. A suitable framework for this purpose is multilevel (hierarchical) modeling
[
25
]
, which we will introduce in the following.

As in the first part of the previous section, we will start by considering a case where the outcome is directly observable. The extension to include latent variables, enabling a comprehensive dynamical structural equation modeling approach, will be introduced in the following section. Here, we focus on constructing a classical multilevel model for longitudinal data. In this framework, the outcome
y_{it}

depends on the patients
i\in(1,..,N)
and time points
t\in(1,..,N_{t})
. This outcome can be decomposed into a person-specific between-level model
y_{2i}
which is constant over time, a time-specific between-level model
y_{3t}
which is the same for all patients, and a within-level model
y_{1t}
which may change over time and vary between different patients:

y_{it}=y_{1it}+y_{2i}+y_{3t}.

(7)

Let us make this more concrete by considering the development of an anxiety item over time and which factors may influence each component.

•

y_{2i}
: personal characteristics such as baseline anxiety, gender, or genetic predispositions. These are the person-specific characteristics in the developmental trajectories that remain constant over time or do not depend on it.

•

y_{3t}
: the general effectivity of the intervention over time for anyone participating, which may depend on factors like the experience of the psychotherapist (if the same across patients).
This component represents the time-specific effect, capturing the average time trend that is independent of the patients and does not convey any information about inter-individual differences in the growth process.

•

y_{1it}
: the personal development process which accounts for changes that patients go through during the intervention process such as responsiveness to the treatment or understanding of the intervention components. These are the person-specific and time-specific aspects.

Each part of the model may include random effects, which are captured through residual variances and their correlations. Time-specific deviations are included in
\varepsilon_{3t}
at the higher-level model
y_{3zi}

\varepsilon_{2i}
at the between-level model
y_{2i}

\varepsilon_{3t}

\varepsilon_{3t1}

\varepsilon_{2i1}

In this section, we extend the time series modeling approach for multiple patients within the multilevel framework by incorporating latent variable modeling, specifically the CFA models introduced in
the first section. This integration creates a dynamical structural equation modeling (DSEM) framework
[

In addition to the directly observable outcome

\eta_{3t}

5
DLCSEM

The major feature of the DLCSEM that distinguish it from DSEM, is its ability to model categorical shifts over time, represented by time-dependent latent dynamic states that are modeled in a Hidden Markov Model
[
29
]
, short HMM. Its original implementation was provided in
Asparouhov et al. 2017
, and it was later extended to include more flexible nonlinear relationships between variables and time-dependent covariates in the HMM
[
35
]
.

5.1
Mathematical model formulation

Like DSEM, DLCSEM is based on the multilevel decomposition of the outcome defined in
Equation

7
. To incorporate the latent state, we introduce the variable
S_{it}
and model the within-level outcome as
\left[Y_{1it}\mid S_{it}=s\right]
. When considering two states,
S
may take the values
S=1
for state 1 and
S=2
for state 2.

Intuitively, extending the previous example of anxiety progression during treatment, we now assume the existence of two distinct latent classes: patients who have not (yet) responded to the treatment and patients who have responded. Individuals may transition from the non-responders state to the responders state over time. This transition impacts the subsequent development of outcomes, as it is associated with potentially different sets of parameters or more generally with potentially different structural constraints within the model.

A general form of the within-level DLCSEM equations can be expressed as follows
10
10

10

The latent variable model can be extended further by including an additional term
h_{1ll^{\prime}}\left(\eta_{1i(t-l)},\eta_{1i(t-l^{\prime})}\right)

[
44

useful in conditions where the specific functional form is unknown or not appropriate to define in advance
[

]
.
:

\displaystyle{\left[Y_{1it}\mid S_{it}=s\right]}

\displaystyle=v_{1s}+\sum_{l=0}^{L}\Lambda_{1ilts}\,\eta_{1i(t-l)}+\sum_{l=0}^{L}R_{ilts}\,Y_{1i(t-l)}+\sum_{l=0}^{L}K_{1ilts}\,X_{1i(t-l)}+\epsilon_{1it}

(14)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1s}++\sum_{l=1}^{L}B_{1ilts}\eta_{1i(t-l)}\sum_{l=0}^{L}Q_{ilts}\,Y_{1i(t-l)}+\sum_{l=0}^{L}\Gamma_{1ilts}\,X_{1i(t-l)}+\zeta_{1it},

In its most general form, all parameters on the within level can be state specific, leading to completely different models for each state. In practical applications, it is meaningful to constrain these parameters using theoretic considerations. We will provide two didactic examples of how these constraints can be used.

To determine the state
s
of an individual
i
at time
t
, we need to define a transition model.
Therefore, we assume that all individuals are subject to dynamic transitions governed by a HMM
11
11

11

In the literature, HMMs may also be referred to as ”Regime Switching Models”.
.

HMMs are a widely utilized class of models that introduce time-variation in parameters by allowing them to assume different values depending on the underlying state or regime. This time-variation is driven by a discrete-valued latent stochastic process characterized by the Markov property, which implies that the current state depends solely on the immediate previous state.

The transition dynamics of the Markov process are encapsulated in a transition matrix,
\mathbf{P}
, which specifies the probabilities of moving from one state to another in successive time periods. Specifically, the transition matrix defines:

P(S_{t}=s\mid S_{t-1}=s^{\prime})\quad\text{for all states }s,s^{\prime}

thereby determining the likelihood of each possible state transition. The complete transition matrix for two states
S=1,2
is given by

\begin{bmatrix}P(S_{it}=1\mid S_{i(t-1)}=1)&P(S_{it}=2\mid S_{i(t-1)}=1)\\
P(S_{it}=1\mid S_{i(t-1)}=2)&P(S_{it}=2\mid S_{i(t-1)}=2)\end{bmatrix}=:\begin{bmatrix}\text{P}_{11it}&\text{P}_{12it}\\
\text{P}_{21it}&\text{P}_{22it}\end{bmatrix}

(15)

For these probabilities, we need to provide two models, one for
\text{P}_{11}
and one for
\text{P}_{21}
, while the remaining probabilities are simply
\text{P}_{12}=1-\text{P}_{11}
and
\text{P}_{22}=1-\text{P}_{21}
. Depending on the available knowledge, the models can be very simple or more complex.

If we assume that patients who responded to the treatment are unlikely to become unresponsive again, we can set

\text{P}_{21}=.05
or by using a prior such as
\text{P}_{21}\sim Unif(0,.1)
, both suggesting a small probability to return to the non-respondent state. More complex models use logistic models for time- and person-specific probabilities (or multinomial for extensions with more states).

7
Discussion

In this tutorial, we demonstrated how to construct sophisticated latent variable models by starting with foundational techniques such as Factor Analysis and Time Series Analysis, progressively building the necessary framework to arrive at DSEM and DLCSEM. DLCSEM provides extensive capabilities for capturing and analyzing complex data dynamics, with additional examples and applications available in the associated GitHub repository.

As ILD become increasingly standard in research and applied fields, for example, via ambulatory assessments
[
55
]
, the corresponding methodological advancements need to evolve accordingly. Our goal in this tutorial was to illustrate the potential applications of DLCSEM, as a method that surpasses traditional approaches in flexibility and analytical power, while offering a comprehensive yet accessible introduction to the topic.

7.1
Proposed strategy for modeling dimensional shifts

The proposed model in illustration 6 can be adapted to other contexts. Psychometric properties of many tests were assessed in cross-sectional settings, and there are situations where the underlying factor pattern can change. Besides theoretical considerations, a practical strategy is to start with cross sectional models at different time points and evaluate, for example, correlation patterns or model fit for competing models. If it is reasonable that a change occurs over time, one might use a good fitting model from the initial time point for state 1, and similarly a good fitting model for the last time point.

If the model provides reasonable state switches and proper parameter estimates, this may point to dimensional shifts.
If the model does not converge or only very few patients use one of the different states, the proposed model might not work for the data set at hand. A sufficient number of patients in each state is necessary to ensure that parameter estimates are reliable (i.e., higher effective sample size, smaller Rhat, reasonably sized SD of the posterior).

7.2
Limitations and future directions

We would like to highlight a few limitations both with regard to our demonstration and with regard to the proposed framework.

Prior selection is an important part of Bayesian model building. In our tutorial, we used rather weakly informative priors. We did not compare alternative priors, particularly shrinkage priors that might improve estimation if models become more complex (e.g., for testing measurement invariance
[
12
]
).
The impact of priors will decrease with larger sample sizes, but in our example with
N=57
patients, prior choice can affect results. Simulation studies using this framework suggest (a) minimal sample sizes should be above
N=50
, and (b) shrinkage priors should be included carefully
[
2
,
1
]
.

An additional assumption implied by the model formulation(s) regards equal time intervals. The dynamic parameters assume equal magnitudes of time between each observation. This should be treated with care, as differences in time between measurement periods can introduce bias in the dynamic estimates. In the literature, two possible solutions are typically cited: First, the use of continuous time models directly accounts for unequal intervals
[
57
]
. Second, using a simple procedure that inserts missing data for time points that then balances the intervals has been suggested in combination with DSEM and/or DLCSEM
[
4
]
.

With increasing sample sizes and/or number of time points, the computational burden particularly of DLCSEM increases. Depending on the complexity of the model (i.e. number of latent factors), estimation time can easily increase to several days without possibilities to parallelize estimation. New estimation beyond Bayesian approaches might need to be developed to overcome this issue.

Finally, we did not discuss model fit in this tutorial. Current recommendations are limited to model comparison via information criteria (e.g.,
[
28
]
). This implies active specification of several competing models. With the complexity of DLCSEM, this strategy seems rather limited in its use to identify misfit (e.g., measurement invariance), and may only apply to a comparison of a few theory-derived models. Future research is needed to fill this gap.
