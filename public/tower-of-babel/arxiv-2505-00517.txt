An explicit description of the Kähler-Einstein metrics of Guenancia–Hamenstädt
Jean-François Lafont, Barry Minemyer

Abstract
. Fine and Premoselli (FP) constructed the first examples of manifolds that do not admit a locally symmetric metric but do admit a negatively curved Einstein metric. The manifolds here are hyperbolic branched covers like those used by Gromov and Thurston, and the construction of their model Einstein metric is a variation of the hyperbolic metric written in polar coordinates. Very recently, Guenancia and Hamenstädt (GH) proved the existence of the first examples of manifolds that are not locally symmetric but admit a negatively curved Kähler-Einstein metric. The GH metrics are realized on complex hyperbolic branched covers constructed by Stover and Toledo. In this article we generalize the construction of FP to the complex hyperbolic setting and show that this yields a negatively curved Einstein metric that asymptotically approaches the metric of GH.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 53C25, 53C35, 51M15; Secondary 53C55, 53B20, 57R18

1.
Introduction

Prior to 2020, the only known examples of closed manifolds which admit an Einstein metric with negative sectional curvature were compact quotients of real, complex, quaternionic, and Cayley hyperbolic spaces.
Then, in
[
8
]
, Fine and Premoselli constructed the first examples of compact
4
-manifolds which admit a negatively curved Einstein metric, but which are not homotopy equivalent to a quotient of a locally symmetric space (see also the corresponding conference proceedings by Premoselli
[
19
]
).

A brief outline of the construction in
[
8
]
is as follows.
Fine and Premoselli construct a sequence of compact hyperbolic
4
-manifolds
M_{k}
, each containing an embedded, totally geodesic, (possibly disconnected) codimension two submanifold
N_{k}
which is nullhomologous in
M_{k}
.
This sequence is arranged so that, as
k\to\infty
, the normal injectivity radius
\eta_{k}
of
N_{k}
in
M_{k}
also approaches infinity.
Fix an integer
d\geq 2
and let
X_{k}
denote the
d
-fold cyclic branched cover of
M_{k}
about
N_{k}
.
These manifolds
X_{k}
are specific examples of the hyperbolic branched cover manifolds considered by Gromov and Thurston in
[
9
]
, and so the more interesting portion of their construction is the negatively curved Einstein metric.
Fine and Premoselli give a very explicit construction of a negatively curved orbifold model Einstein metric defined on the
\eta_{k}
-neighborhood of
N_{k}
.
This metric
\lambda_{k}
is everywhere Riemannian except it has a cone angle of
2\pi/d
about
N_{k}
, and so
\lambda_{k}
pulls back to a smooth metric within this same neighborhood of the ramification locus of
X_{k}
.
On the
((1/2)\eta_{k},\eta_{k})
-annulus of the ramification locus, Fine and Premoselli use a smooth cut-off function to interpolate between
\lambda_{k}
and the hyperbolic metric
\mathfrak{h}_{4}
on each page of the cover.
For
k
large, this interpolated metric
g_{k}
is negatively curved and approximately Einstein.
Fine and Premoselli then construct an inverse function theorem to show that, for
k
sufficiently large, this metric can be perturbed to a genuine Einstein metric
\mathfrak{e}_{k}
with negative sectional curvature.

In
[
11
]
, Hamemstädt and Jäckel extend the construction in
[
8
]
to all dimensions.
Their approach is the same as above, and they use the same model orbifold Einstein metric as in
[
8
]
.
But they use subgroup separability to obtain stronger volume bounds on the submanifolds
N_{k}
, and they develop an extension of the inverse function theorem mentioned above.

In
[
20
]
, Stover and Toledo construct complex hyperbolic branched cover manifolds analogous to the hyperbolic branched covers in
[
9
]
.
Stover and Toledo proved that these manifolds are not homotopy equivalent to a manifold admitting a locally symmetric metric, and by
[
24
]
it is known that these manifolds admit a negatively curved Kähler metric.
Thus, since the first Chern class of this manifold is negative, it admits a unique (up to scaling) Kähler-Einstein metric with negative Einstein constant by the celebrated work of Aubin
[
2
]
and Yau
[
23
]
.
Very recently, Guenancia and Hamenstädt
[
10
]
proved that this Kähler-Einstein metric has negative sectional curvature, thus verifying the first known examples of negatively curved Kähler-Einstein metrics on manifolds that are not locally symmetric.

The argument in
[
10
]
is similar in nature to that in
[
8
]
, but one major difference is that Guenancia and Hamenstädt do not give a direct construction for the model Kähler-Einstein metric.
They use
[
6
]
and
[
22
]
to show that a unique model Kähler-Einstein metric exists
[
10
, Theorem 2.2]
, they prove that the coefficient of the horizontal distribution of this metric must satisfy a specific second-order differential equation
[
10
, Theorem 2.9]
, and then use this fact to give a quantitative description of the sectional curvatures.
They also show that, as the distance from the ramification locus increases, this model Kähler-Einstein metric converges exponentially to the pullback of the complex hyperbolic metric (
[
10
, Theorem 2.4]
).
The fact that this model Kähler-Einstein metric is negatively curved is due to Bland
[
5
]
.

Prior to the announcement of
[
10
]
the authors were working on this same problem, but taking a slightly different approach.
Our goal was to take the entire process from
[
8
]
and
[
11
]
and try to generalize it to the complex hyperbolic setting.
This has the advantage of giving a very explicit description of the model Einstein metric, but has the downside of making it difficult, if not impossible, to determine whether or not the resulting metric is Kähler.

Upon inspection of
[
10
]
, the authors have realized that the natural extension of the model negatively curved Einstein metric from
[
8
]
to the complex hyperbolic setting coincides with the model Kähler-Einstein metric whose existence is guaranteed by
[
10
, Thm. 2.2]
.
We state this formally as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

The negatively curved model Einstein metric from Fine–Premoselli
[
8
]
, generalized to complex hyperbolic branched cover manifolds, produces the model Kähler-Einstein metric whose existence is guaranteed by Guenancia–Hamenstädt
[
10
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Combining our work in Sections
2
and
3
below with the inverse function theorems of
[
8
]
and
[
11
]
proves the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist Kähler manifolds of every complex dimension which admit a negatively curved Einstein metric but do not admit a locally symmetric metric.

.

One potential application of the results of this paper is the following.

is divisible by

.

We also prove that

In Section

The Metric

The purpose of this section is to develop curvature formulas (Theorem

2.1.
The complex hyperbolic metric written in polar coordinates

All of the results in this subsection were discovered by Belegradek in
[
3
]
and rescaled to have constant holomorphic curvature of
-4
in
[
17
]
.
Let
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}
denote complex hyperbolic space of dimension
n
normalized to have constant holomorphic sectional curvature
-4
, and let
q\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}
.
With respect to an appropriately chosen frame about
q
, the standard complex hyperbolic metric
\mathfrak{c}_{n}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{n}

\displaystyle=\cosh^{2}(r)\mathfrak{c}_{n-1}+\frac{1}{4}\sinh^{2}(2r)d\theta^{2}+dr^{2}

(2.1)

\displaystyle=\cosh^{2}(r)\mathfrak{c}_{n-1}+\cosh^{2}(r)\sinh^{2}(r)d\theta^{2}+dr^{2}

We now describe the implicit frame for this representation of
\mathfrak{c}_{n}
.

Let
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
denote a totally geodesic copy of complex hyperbolic
(n-1)
-space in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}
, and let
\phi:\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
denote the orthogonal projection map.
Let
p=\phi(q)
, and let
r
denote the distance from a given point to
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
.
The space
\phi^{-1}(p)
is a totally geodesic copy of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{1}
which contains
q
.
There is a
2

\sigma\subset T_{q}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}
such that
\text{exp}_{q}(\sigma)=\phi^{-1}(p)
.
Using polar coordinates
(\partial/\partial\theta,\partial/\partial r)
on
\sigma
, we have

\mathfrak{c}_{n}\bigr|_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{4}\sinh^{2}(2r)d\theta^{2}+dr^{2}.

Now, let
\mathcal{H}=\sigma^{\perp}\subset T_{q}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}
denote the
horizontal distribution
of
\mathfrak{c}_{n}
at
q
.
The map

\varphi:=d\phi_{q}\bigr|_{\mathcal{H}}:\mathcal{H}\to T_{p}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}

is an isomorphism.
If we let
(\check{X}_{i})_{i=1}^{2n-2}
be an orthonormal basis for
T_{p}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
, then
(\varphi^{-1}(\check{X}_{i}))_{i=1}^{2n-2}
is an orthogonal basis for
\mathcal{H}
.
Let
X_{i}=\varphi^{-1}(\check{X}_{i})
for each
i
.
The basis
(X_{i})
is not orthonormal: we have
\mathfrak{c}_{n}(X_{i},X_{i})=\cosh^{2}(r)
for each
i
.
Putting this all together gives the desired frame for (
2.1
) and, moreover, gives a natural diffeomorphism
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2n-2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\times(0,\infty)
.

A very important feature of this decomposition of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}
is that the horizontal distribution is not integrable with respect to
\mathfrak{c}_{n}
.
Consider two orthonormal vectors
\check{X}_{i},\check{X}_{j}\in T_{p}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
, and extend them to a frame near

(in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
) in such a way that
[\check{X}_{i},\check{X}_{j}]_{p}=0
.
As above, let
X_{i}=\varphi^{-1}(\check{X}_{i})
and
X_{j}=\varphi^{-1}(\check{X}_{j})
.
It was proved in
[
3
]
for constant holomorphic curvature
-1
and rescaled in
[

]

that
[X_{i},X_{j}]_{q}=0
if and only if
(\check{X}_{i},\check{X}_{j})

spans a complex line.

In our curvature calculations below it will be convenient to use a
holomorphic frame

(X_{i})_{i=1}^{2n-2}
for
\mathcal{H}
, which we define as follows.
Let
\check{X}_{1}\in T_{p}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
be any unit vector and define
\check{X}_{2}=J\check{X}_{1}
, where

denotes the complex structure on
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}
.
We will refer to such a pair

.
Then, assuming that

\check{X}_{2k+1}
to be any unit vector orthogonal to

\check{X}_{2k+2}=J\check{X}_{2k+1}
.
It is an easy exercise to check that

is in the orthogonal complement of

.
We then extend this basis to a frame near
p
in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
in such a way that
[\check{X}_{i},\check{X}_{j}]_{p}=0
for all
i,j
, and define
X_{i}=\varphi^{-1}(\check{X}_{i})
for each

.

The non-integrability of

can be described in terms of a holomorphic frame as follows.
For a holomorphic frame

about

(2.2)

[X_{i},X_{i+1}]_{q}=2\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\qquad\text{for }1\leq i\leq 2n-2\text{ and }i\text{ odd}

Coordinate Change and Curvature Formulas

Following

\displaystyle Y_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{u}X_{i}\qquad 1\leq i\leq 2n-2

\displaystyle Y_{2n-1}

.

With respect to the orthonormal basis defined in equations (

(2.9)

\displaystyle R^{\lambda}_{i,i+1,i,i+1}=-4\left(\frac{1+W^{2}}{u^{2}}\right)\thinspace\text{ for }i\text{ odd.}

(2.10)

\displaystyle R^{\lambda}_{i,j,i,j}=-\frac{1+W^{2}}{u^{2}}.

(2.11)

\displaystyle R^{\lambda}_{i,2n-1,i,2n-1}=R^{\lambda}_{i,2n,i,2n}=-\frac{WW^{\prime}}{u}.

(2.12)

\displaystyle R^{\lambda}_{2n-1,2n,2n-1,2n}=-3\frac{WW^{\prime}}{u}-\left(WW^{\prime\prime}+\left(W^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right).

(2.13)

2.3.
Reduction to
n=3

Curvature formulas for
\mathfrak{c}_{n}
in equation (

) with respect to a holomorphic frame are worked out in
[
3
]
and
[
17
]
.
In making the substitution
u=\cosh(r)
to obtain (
2.3
), the only change in our implicit frame is that
\partial/\partial u
is now a (positive) scaled copy of
\partial/\partial r
.
So, in particular, equations (
2.6
) through (

) still provide a holomorphic frame for (
2.3
).

A
mixed term
of a Riemann curvature tensor, with respect to a given fixed orthonormal frame, is any term that does not correspond to the sectional curvature of a coordinate plane with respect to the given frame.
Equivalently, this is any term that is not of the form
R_{i,j,i,j}
for some
i
and
j
.
With respect to a holomorphic frame, a mixed term
R^{\mathfrak{c}_{n}}_{a,b,c,d}
of the curvature tensor
R^{\mathfrak{c}_{n}}
of
\mathfrak{c}_{n}
is nonzero only when the respective vectors
\{Y_{a},Y_{b},Y_{c},Y_{d}\}
contain exactly two holomorphic pairs.
In other words, if
\{Y_{a},Y_{b},Y_{c},Y_{d}\}
is not of the form
\{Y_{i},Y_{i+1},Y_{j},Y_{j+1}\}
for some
i,j
odd, then the mixed term
R^{\mathfrak{c}_{n}}_{a,b,c,d}=0
.
This property is then inherited by the warped-product metric
\lambda
from (
2.5
).

There are two distinct ways in which the collection
\{Y_{i},Y_{i+1},Y_{j},Y_{j+1}\}
can contain two holomorphic pairs:

(1)

Both
1\leq i,j\leq 2n-2
. In this case, both pairs are contained in the horizontal distribution
\mathcal{H}
.

(2)

i=2n-1
or
j=2n-1
. In this case, one pair comes from
\mathcal{H}
and the other pair has the same span as
\{\partial/\partial\theta,\partial/\partial u\}
.

All of these cases are covered when the complex dimension of
\mathcal{H}
is at least two or, equivalently, when
n\geq 3
.
Therefore, to simplify calculations below, we will restrict to
n=3
which, again, covers all possible nonzero values for
R^{\lambda}
.
Note that, when
n=3
, the horizontal distribution
\mathcal{H}
is a scaled copy of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

A frame and Lie Brackets for

Using

\mathbb{R}^{2n}=\mathbb{R}^{2n-2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,\infty)

When

and thus defines a smooth metric on

.
But when
\alpha\neq 0

\mathbb{R}^{2n-2}
.
The following Lemma, which is mostly the same as from
[

]
with the substitution

, quantifies how this cone angle depends on

In what follows we let
u_{\alpha}
denote the largest root of

.
Note that when
u_{\alpha}>0
, the metric
\lambda_{\alpha}
is well-defined for all

.
We also define
f(u)=(1-u^{2})u^{2n}
and observe that roots of

are equivalent to solutions of the equation

.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

v=\sqrt{\frac{n}{n+1}}\qquad\alpha_{\text{max}}=\frac{1}{n+1}v^{2n}\qquad c_{\alpha}=\frac{u_{\alpha}}{2}V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(u_{\alpha})=u_{\alpha}^{2}-\frac{n\alpha}{u_{\alpha}^{2n}}.

(1)

The root
u_{\alpha}
is positive if and only if
\alpha\in(-\infty,\alpha_{\text{max}}]
. The map
\gamma:(-\infty,\alpha_{\text{max}}]\to[v,\infty)
defined by
\gamma(\alpha)=u_{\alpha}
is a decreasing homeomorphism.

(2)

When
\alpha\in(-\infty,\alpha_{\text{max}})
, the metric
\lambda_{\alpha}
has a cone angle about
\mathbb{R}^{2n-2}
at
u=u_{\alpha}
with cone angle
2\pi c_{\alpha}
.

(3)

The map
\beta:(-\infty,\alpha_{\text{max}}]\to[0,\infty)
defined by
\beta(\alpha)=c_{\alpha}
is a decreasing homeomorphism. In particular, as
\alpha
varies from
0
to
\alpha_{\text{max}}
, the cone angle

takes every value from
2\pi
to
0
exactly once.

Lemma
3.5
is the last thing that we needed to prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Recall that we need to prove that our model Einstein metric
\lambda_{\alpha}
is isometric to the Kähler-Einstein metric
\omega_{\alpha}
whose existence is guaranteed by
[
10
, Theorem 2.2]
.
In
[
10
, Theorem 2.9]
the authors argue that

, the warping function for the horizontal fiber of

, must satisfy a specific differential equation.
The initial conditions for this differential equation are
f^{\prime}_{\alpha}(0)=0
and
f_{\alpha}(0)\in\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{n+1}},1\right)=(v,1)
.
With respect to equation (
2.4
), the coefficient of the horizontal distribution is

.
Also, recall in (

) that we made the substitution
V(u)=u^{2}-1=\sinh^{2}(r)=(\cosh^{\prime}(r))^{2}
.

In the differential equation (
3.4
) we set

and

.
Note that, in terms of (

V^{\prime}=\frac{dV}{du}=\frac{dV}{dr}\cdot\frac{dr}{du}=2f^{\prime}(r)f^{\prime\prime}(r)\left(\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(r)}\right)=2f^{\prime\prime}(r).

Substituting into (

) gives

2f^{\prime\prime}+\frac{2n(f^{\prime})^{2}}{f}=(2n+2)f-\frac{2n}{f}

which can be rearranged as

\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f}+n\frac{(f^{\prime})^{2}}{f^{2}}+n\frac{1}{f^{2}}=n+1.

This is the differential equation derived in Theorem 2.9 of
[

]
.
To see that the solution
V_{\alpha}
of this equation is the same solution as
\omega_{\alpha}
, just compare the initial conditions listed above for

with Lemma

.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

Again, this proof is very similar to
[

]
, but the presence of the

in the

) causes enough differences that we verify all details below.

For (1), just note that
u_{\alpha}
is the largest value of

which satisfies

.
Statement (1) then follows from consideration of the graph of
f(u)-\alpha
.

To prove statement (2), fix
\alpha\in(-\infty,\alpha_{\text{max}})
.
To calculate the cone angle of
\lambda_{\alpha}
we need to consider values of

near
u_{\alpha}
.
One has, for

V_{\alpha}(u)=V_{\alpha}(u_{\alpha})+V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(u_{\alpha})(u-u_{\alpha})+O\left((u-u_{\alpha})^{2}\right)\approx\frac{2c_{\alpha}}{u_{\alpha}}(u-u_{\alpha}).

We then, in the metric
\lambda_{\alpha}
, make the substitution

s=\sqrt{\frac{2u_{\alpha}}{c_{\alpha}}(u-u_{\alpha})}

Direct calculations verify the following:

\displaystyle du^{2}=\frac{2c_{\alpha}}{u_{\alpha}}(u-u_{\alpha})ds^{2}\approx V_{\alpha}ds^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{c_{\alpha}^{2}s^{2}}{u_{\alpha}^{2}}=\frac{2c_{\alpha}}{u_{\alpha}}(u-u_{\alpha})\approx V_{\alpha}

\displaystyle u^{2}=\left(\frac{s^{2}c_{\alpha}}{2u_{\alpha}}+u_{\alpha}\right)^{2}.

Substituting for
\lambda_{\alpha}
in (
2.5
), we have

(3.5)

\lambda_{\alpha}\approx\left(\frac{s^{2}c_{\alpha}}{2u_{\alpha}}+u_{\alpha}\right)^{2}\mathfrak{c}_{n-1}+\left(\frac{s^{2}c_{\alpha}}{2u_{\alpha}}+u_{\alpha}\right)^{2}\frac{c_{\alpha}^{2}s^{2}}{u_{\alpha}^{2}}d\theta^{2}+ds^{2}.

Notice that, as

, we have

.
Therefore, as

, the leading (linear) coefficient of

in (

) approaches

.
Thus,
\lambda_{\alpha}
has cone angle of
2\pi c_{\alpha}
about
\mathbb{R}^{2n-2}
.

To verify (3), recall that
u_{\alpha}
is the largest solution to the equation

or, equivalently,

.
This gives

c_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha}^{2}-\frac{n\alpha}{u_{\alpha}^{2n}}=u_{\alpha}^{2}-\frac{n(1-u_{\alpha}^{2})u_{\alpha}^{2n}}{u_{\alpha}^{2n}}=(1+n)u_{\alpha}^{2}-n.

This equation defines an increasing homeomorphism from
[v,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
.
Composing with
\gamma
from (1) yields the desired decreasing homeomorphism
\beta
.
For the last claim, just note that when

and therefore

.
Thus, when
\alpha=0
, the cone angle is
2\pi
(which is clear since
\lambda_{0}=\mathfrak{c}_{n}
).
The claim then follows from the fact that
\beta
is decreasing.
∎

Notice the immediate corollary of Lemma
3.5
.

Corollary 3.6
.

For any integer

, there exists a unique

It turns out that

Recall that
W^{2}=V_{\alpha}=u^{2}-1+\alpha u^{-2n}

From this, we observe that

\displaystyle 2WW^{\prime}=V_{\alpha}^{\prime}=2u-2n\alpha u^{-2n-1}

\displaystyle 2\left(\left(W^{\prime}\right)^{2}+WW^{\prime\prime}\right)=V_{\alpha}^{\prime\prime}=2+2n(2n+1)\alpha u^{-2n-2}.

The following Corollary restates the curvature formulas from Theorem

with the above calculations.

Corollary 3.7
.

With respect to the orthonormal basis defined in equations (
2.6
) through (
2.8
) we have, up to the symmetries of the curvature tensor, the following formulas for the nonzero components of the Riemann curvature tensor
R^{\alpha}
of

(3.6)

\displaystyle R^{\alpha}_{i,i+1,i,i+1}=-4-\frac{4\alpha}{u^{2n+2}}\qquad\text{ for }i\text{ odd.}

(3.7)

\displaystyle R^{\alpha}_{i,j,i,j}=-1-\frac{\alpha}{u^{2n+2}}.

(3.8)

\displaystyle R^{\alpha}_{i,2n-1,i,2n-1}=R^{\alpha}_{i,2n,i,2n}=-1+\frac{n\alpha}{u^{2n+2}}.

(3.9)

\displaystyle R^{\alpha}_{2n-1,2n,2n-1,2n}=-4-\frac{2n(n-1)\alpha}{u^{2n+2}}.

(3.10)

\displaystyle R^{\alpha}_{i,i+1,j,j+1}=2R^{\alpha}_{i,j,i+1,j+1}=-2R^{\alpha}_{i,j+1,i+1,j}=-2-\frac{2\alpha}{u^{2n+2}}\quad\text{ for }i,j\text{ odd}.

(3.11)

\displaystyle R^{\alpha}_{i,i+1,2n-1,2n}=2R^{\alpha}_{i,2n-1,i+1,2n}=-2R^{\alpha}_{i,2n,i+1,2n-1}=-2+\frac{2n\alpha}{u^{2n+2}}.

In the formulas above we have
1\leq i,j\leq 2n-2
and, if
i
and

appear in the same formula, we assume that
(Y_{i},Y_{j})
does not form a holomorphic pair.
We also assume
i
is odd in (
3.11
).

With these formulas we can now prove the following.

Proposition 3.8
.

For all
\alpha\in(0,\alpha_{\text{max}})
, the metric
\lambda_{\alpha}
has all sectional curvatures bounded above by a negative constant.
Moreover, the sectional curvature

of
\lambda_{\alpha}
satisfies

-4-\frac{2n(n-1)\alpha}{u^{2n+2}}\leq K\leq-1+\frac{n\alpha}{u^{2n+2}}

Proof.

First note that, of the curvatures of the coordinate planes in equations (

) through (
3.9
), the only one that is greater than

is (
3.8
).
But, using the notation for

and

-1+\frac{n\alpha}{u^{2n+2}}<-1+\frac{n\alpha_{\text{max}}}{v^{2n+2}}=-1+\frac{n}{n+1}\cdot\frac{1}{v^{2}}=0.

Therefore,
K<0
for all coordinate
2
-planes with respect to the orthonormal frame defined in (
2.6
) through (
2.8
), but we also have to account for the nonzero mixed terms.

In what follows we use the notation from Subsection 2.1.
Let
E=\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
q\in E\times(0,\infty)
, and
p=\phi(q)
where
\phi
is the orthogonal projection onto the

Let

.
Our goal is to choose a convenient frame to calculate

.
By construction,

is at least

Let

be a unit vector orthogonal to

which satisfies that

\check{X}_{1}\in T_{p}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}

.
Choose

is an orthonormal basis for
\sigma
.
Let
\check{X}_{2}=J\check{X}_{1}

\check{X}_{3}

Extend
(\check{X}_{1},\check{X}_{2},\check{X}_{3})

about

Finally, let

Y_{1}=\frac{1}{u}X_{1},\quad Y_{2}=\frac{1}{u}X_{2},\quad Y_{3}=\frac{1}{u}X_{3},\quad Y_{5}=\frac{1}{uW}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta},\quad Y_{6}=W\frac{\partial}{\partial u}.

Then there exist constants

with

We then compute

\displaystyle K(\sigma)=\lambda_{\alpha}(R^{\alpha}(A,B)A,B)

\displaystyle=a_{1}^{2}b_{2}^{2}R^{\alpha}_{1,2,1,2}+a_{1}^{2}b_{3}^{2}R^{\alpha}_{1,3,1,3}+(a_{1}b_{5}-a_{5}b_{1})^{2}R^{\alpha}_{1,5,1,5}+a_{1}^{2}b_{6}^{2}R^{\alpha}_{1,6,1,6}+a_{5}^{2}b_{2}^{2}R^{\alpha}_{2,5,2,5}

Remark 3.9
.

The extreme curvature values of
\lambda_{\alpha}
are functions of both the dimension
n
and ramification degree
d
.
The maximum and minimum curvatures both occur at the ramification locus, when
u=u_{\alpha}
.
As
d\to\infty
, we have
u_{\alpha}\to v
and
\alpha\to\alpha_{\text{max}}
.
We saw in the above argument that, in this situation,
(n\alpha)/u^{2n+2}
will then approach
1
as
d\to\infty
.
The lower curvature bound thus approaches
-4-2(n-1)=-2(n+1)
while the upper curvature bound approaches
0
.
Therefore, for any
\varepsilon>0
, one can choose the ramification degree
d
sufficiently large so that the lower curvature bound of
\lambda_{\alpha}
lies in
(-2(n+1),-2(n+1)+\varepsilon)
while the upper curvature bound lies in
(-\varepsilon,0)
.
Note that these are the same curvature bounds stated in the Introduction of
[
10
]
.

It is also easy to deduce
[
10
, Theorem 2.4 (4)]
from our previous work.
Note that
|\lambda_{\alpha}-\mathfrak{c}_{n}|\leq 2\alpha/u^{2n}
.
Recalling that
u=\cosh(r)
and
r
denotes the distance from the branching locus, we see that the model Einstein metric
\lambda_{\alpha}
approaches
\mathfrak{c}_{n}
exponentially in
r
.

4.
Complex hyperbolic branched covers and convergence to the Kähler-Einstein metric

In this section we first show how to construct a sequence of complex hyperbolic branched cover manifolds
(X_{k})
with a specific geometric property (namely, that the normal injectivity radius of the branching locus goes to infinity while the diameter of the branching locus remains fixed).
We will then discuss how to use our model Einstein metric
\lambda_{\alpha}
to construct a smooth, approximately Einstein metric
g_{k}
on
X_{k}
for each
k
.
This metric will be negatively curved for
k
sufficiently large.
Finally we will show how to use an inverse function theorem to perturb
g_{k}
to obtain a negatively curved Einstein metric
\mathfrak{e}_{k}
on
X_{k}
.
Since our approximate Einstein metric
g_{k}
is isometric to
\omega_{d,R}
from
[
10
]
, equation (30) from
[
10
]
shows that
\mathfrak{e}_{k}
asymptotically approaches the unique Kähler-Einstein metric on
X_{k}
with negative Ricci constant.

The authors want to emphasize that the work in this section was completed
after
the announcement of
[
10
]
.
This section really just combines known results from the literature, including
[
10
]
, with a small amount of work to check that they apply to our metric
\lambda_{\alpha}
.
But we include full details here for completeness.

4.1.
The sequence of complex hyperbolic branched cover manifolds

Fix integers
d,n\geq 2
.
The goal of this subsection is to prove the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

There exists a sequence of compact Kähler manifolds
(M_{k})
of complex dimension
n
with connected, totally geodesic submanifolds
(N_{k})
which satisfy the following.

(1)

The universal cover of
M_{k}
is isometric to
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

(2)

The submanifold
N_{k}\subset M_{k}
is embedded, has real codimension
2
, and its preimage in the universal cover of
M_{k}
is isometric to multiple disjoint copies of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
.

(3)

N_{k}
is isometric to
N_{\ell}
for all indices
k,\ell
.

(4)

The fundamental class
[N_{k}]\in H_{2n-2}(M_{k},\mathbb{Z})
is
d
-divisible.

(5)

The normal injectivity radius
\eta_{k}
of
N_{k}
in
M_{k}
satisfies
\eta_{k}\geq k
.

Remark 4.2
.

By condition
(4)
, the cyclic
d
-fold branched cover
X_{k}
of
M_{k}
about
N_{k}
is a smooth manifold.
By
(5)
the normal injectivity radius of the branching locus of
X_{k}
will approach infinity as
k\to\infty
.
The branched cover manifold
X_{k}
is Kähler by
[
24
]
and, for
k
sufficiently large, this is the manifold which admits a negatively curved Kähler-Einstein metric.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

The construction of the pair
(M_{k},N_{k})
is recursive.
We first show how to construct
(M_{0},N_{0})
and then, given
(M_{k},N_{k})
, we show how to construct
(M_{k+1},N_{k+1})
which satisfies
(1)-(5)
above.

Using the notation of
[
20
]
, let
\Gamma<\text{PU}(n,1)
be a cocompact congruence arithmetic lattice of simple type.
It is known that, after possibly passing to a finite congruence cover, we may assume that the quotient
M=\Gamma\setminus\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}
contains an embedded submanifold
N
satisfying
(2)
above.
In the event that this submanifold is disconnected, we let
N
denote one of its components.

By a result of Stover and Toledo
[
20
, Proposition 5.1]
, there exists a finite cover
(M^{\prime},N^{\prime})
of
(M,N)
such that
[N^{\prime}]\in H_{2n-2}(M^{\prime},\mathbb{Z})
is

-divisible.
We set
M_{0}=M^{\prime}
and, if
N^{\prime}
is connected, we let
N_{0}=N^{\prime}
.
If
N^{\prime}
contains multiple components, then an application of the Universal Coefficient Theorem
[
10

shows that each individual component of

must also have fundamental class that is

-divisible.
Choose one such component and call it

.

We now assume that the pair
(M_{k},N_{k})

.
This argument is analogous to that of
[

To start, if the normal injectivity radius

.
So we may assume that

Let
\Gamma_{k}<\text{PU}(n,1)

.
The preimage of

Fix one of these components and call it

Let

Since

Connecting these endpoints with a geodesic contained in

Note that, by construction,

By a result of Bergeron

By
[

.
So, after finitely many steps, we obtain a finite index subgroup

\Lambda_{k}<\Gamma_{k+1}
which satisfies the following.
Let

Then

.
This verifies conditions
(1)-(3)
and
(5)
in the Theorem.

For property
(4)
, first notice that
M_{k+1}
is a finite cover of
M_{k}
since
\Gamma_{k+1}<\Gamma_{k}
has finite index.
Since the fundamental class of
N_{k}
is
d
-divisible, by
[
10
, Lemma 3.7]
we have that the lift of
N_{k}
in
M_{k+1}
has
d
-divisible fundamental class.
This lift may have multiple connected components, one of which is
N_{k+1}
.
But
[
10
, Lemma 3.8]
shows that, if the fundamental class of the preimage of
N_{k}
is
d
-divisible, then the fundamental class of each component is
d
-divisible.
Thus,
N_{k+1}
satisfies condition
(4)
of the Theorem.
∎

4.2.
The approximate Einstein metric

In this subsection we consider the manifold pairs
(M_{k},N_{k})
constructed in Theorem
4.1
.
Fix an integer
d\geq 2
as in the Theorem, and let
X_{k}
denote the
d
-fold cyclic branched cover of
M_{k}
about
N_{k}
.

By Corollary
3.6
there exists a unique
\alpha=\alpha_{d}\in(0,\alpha_{\text{max}})
such that the cone angle of
\lambda_{\alpha}
about some copy of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n}
is
2\pi/d
.
Define a smooth function
\chi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
which satisfies
\chi(u)=1
if
u\leq 1/2
and
\chi(u)=0
for
u\geq 1
.
We then write

V_{k}(u)=u^{2}-1+\frac{\alpha}{u^{2n}}\chi\left(\frac{u}{\eta_{k}}\right)

and let
g^{\prime}_{k}
denote the metric
\lambda
associated with this choice of
V_{k}
.
Since
V_{k}=u^{2}-1
at all points of distance at least
\eta_{k}
from
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
, the metric
g^{\prime}_{k}
descends to a well-defined orbifold metric on
M_{k}
.
By abuse of notation we again denote this metric
g^{\prime}_{k}
, and note that
g^{\prime}_{k}
will be everywhere Riemannian except at
N_{k}
.
At each point of
N_{k}
the metric
g^{\prime}_{k}
will have a cone angle of
2\pi/d
.

Let
\rho_{k}:X_{k}\to M_{k}
denote the
d
-fold cyclic branched covering map, and equip
X_{k}
with the metric
g_{k}:=\rho^{*}_{k}(g^{\prime}_{k})
.
By an abuse of notation we also use
N_{k}
to denote the branching locus of
X_{k}
.
By construction the metric
g_{k}
is smooth about
N_{k}
, and
g_{k}
is certainly negatively curved and Einstein within the
(1/2)\eta_{k}
-tube of
N_{k}
and outside of the
\eta_{k}
-tube of
N_{k}
.
Since
\eta_{k}\to\infty
, all derivatives of
\chi(u/\eta_{k})
approach
0
as
k\to\infty
.
Therefore, for
k
sufficiently large,
g_{k}
will be negatively curved everywhere and will be approximately Einstein on the
((1/2)\eta_{k},\eta_{k})
-annulus about
N_{k}
.
This approximation becomes finer as
k\to\infty
.
We summarize this information in the following Proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

There exists an integer
K
such that, for all
k\geq K
, there exists a smooth Riemannian metric
g_{k}
on
X_{k}
with the following properties.

(1)

There is a constant
c>0
such that
\text{sec}(g_{k})<-c
, where
sec
denotes the sectional curvature.

(2)

\text{Ric}(g_{k})+(2n+2)g_{k}
is nonzero only in the annular neighborhood
(1/2)\eta_{k}<r<\eta_{k}
.

(3)

For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
A
depending on
m
but not on
k
such that

||\text{Ric}(g_{k})+(2n+2)g_{k}||_{C^{m}}\leq\frac{A}{\left(\cosh\left(\frac{1}{2}\eta_{k}\right)\right)^{2n+2}}

(4)

We have

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{X_{k}}\bigr|\text{Ric}(g_{k})+(2n+2)g_{k}|^{2}d\text{vol}_{g_{k}}=0

Proof.

Statement
(2)
is clear by the definition of
\chi
.
Statement
(1)
is also clear since the curvature of
g_{k}^{\prime}
is bounded above by a negative constant, and
g_{k}
is a
C^{2}
-approximation of
g_{k}^{\prime}
.
Statement
(3)
is identical to
[
8
, Proposition 3.1 (1)]
but with replacing
n-3
by
2n
.

For
(4)
, first note that the integrand is supported on the
((1/2)\eta_{k},\eta_{k})
-annulus about
N_{k}
.
The metric
g_{k}
only differs from
\mathfrak{c}_{n}
within the normal bundle of
N_{k}
, and the maximum length of any unit vector in this annulus is

uW\approx u^{2}\leq\cosh^{2}(\eta_{k})
.
Thus, an upper bound for the
g_{k}
-volume of this region is given by

d\cdot\text{vol}(N_{k})\cdot\cosh^{2}(\eta_{k})\cdot\eta_{k}^{2}.

From Theorem
4.1
(3) we know that
\text{vol}(N_{k})
is constant.
Then, using the approximation
\cosh(x)\approx(1/2)e^{x}
for
x
large, statement
(4)
then follows from statement
(3)
assuming
n>1
.
∎

4.3.
Perturbing
g_{k}
to obtain a negatively curved Einstein metric

In this subsection we will closely follow the notation and results from
[
11
, Sections 2, 4]
and
[
8
, Section 4]
, both of which originate (at least) from the work of Anderson in
[
1
]
.
The proof of Theorem
1.2
below is identical to the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 4.3]
.
Technically, one would need to replace
n-1
with
2n+2
in equations (2.1), (2.3), and the subsequent Lemmas of
[
11
]
, but the proofs of these Lemmas are independent of this positive constant.
Alternately, one could simply scale our approximate Einstein metric
g_{k}^{\prime}
appropriately so that its Einstein constant is
-(n-1)
.
Then, the results of
[
11
]
apply directly.
The goal of this subsection is to provide the terminology and results necessary from
[
8
]
and
[
11
]
to justify Theorem
1.2
, with the hope that this subsection can also serve as a gentle introduction for non-experts to the above references.

Let
\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right)
denote the collection of smooth, symmetric, bilinear forms on
TX_{k}
of any signature.
The collection of Riemannian metrics on
X_{k}
forms an open cone within
\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right)
.
One would like to define an operator
E:\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right)\to\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right)
by

E(g)=\text{Ric}(g)+(2n+2)g

but this operator is not elliptic and thus the inverse function theorem will not generally be applicable.
The remedy for this issue is called
Bianchi guage fixing
.
Given
g\in\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right)
, define
\Phi_{g}:\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right)\to\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right)
by

\Phi_{g}(h)=\text{Ric}(h)+(2n+2)h+\text{div}^{*}_{h}(\beta_{g}(h))

where
\text{div}^{*}_{h}
is the
L^{2}
-adjoint of the divergence with respect to
g
and
\beta_{g}:\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right)\to T^{*}X
is the
Bianchi operator
.
We omit the definitions since we will not use them here, but we direct the interested reader to see
[
8
]
,
[
11
]
, and the references therein.
An important property of
\Phi_{g}
is that, if
|\beta_{g}|
is bounded and
\text{Ric}(g)<\lambda g
for some
\lambda<0
, then
\Phi_{g}(h)=0
if and only if
h
satisfies both
\text{Ric}(h)=-(2n+2)h
and
\beta_{g}(h)=0
.
See
[
1
, Lemma 2.1]
or
[
11
, Lemma 2.1]
.
So, at least for metrics with negative Ricci curvature,
\Phi_{g}
is sufficient to be able to detect Einstein metrics near
g
.
But it is generally not necessary as it will miss Einstein metrics
\mathfrak{e}
which satisfy
\beta_{g}(\mathfrak{e})\neq 0
as discussed in
[
1
]
.

The reason why it is advantageous to add
\beta_{g}
to
E
to obtain
\Phi_{g}
is that the operator
\Phi_{g}
is now elliptic.
Its linearization (at
g
) can be explicitly calculated as

\mathcal{L}_{g}(h):=(D\Phi_{g})_{g}(h)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{L}h+(2n+2)h

where
\Delta_{L}=\nabla^{*}\nabla h+\text{Ric}(h)
is the
Lichnerowicz Laplacian
.
A good reference for the notation is
[
18
, Section 9.3]
.
Koiso
[
14
]
proved that, if
g
is Einstein with negative sectional curvature, then
\mathcal{L}_{g}
has a uniform
L^{2}
-spectral gap.
Our metric
g_{k}
is only approximately Einstein, but an analog to this result was proved in
[
8
, Lemma 4.4]
(see also the corresponding
[
11
, Lemma 4.1]
) to apply to this situation.

All of the results above apply in all dimensions.
The reason why the negatively curved Einstein metrics from
[
8
]
are only guaranteed to exist in dimension
4
is twofold.
The first reason is because the construction of the manifold pairs
(M_{k},N_{k})
in
[
8
]
do not give sufficient
L^{2}
-control of
\Phi_{g_{k}}(g_{k})
.
Hamenstädt and Jäckel in
[
11
]
observed that, by using subgroup separability to bound the diameter of the branching locus, one could obtain such
L^{2}
-control of
\Phi_{g_{k}}(g_{k})
.
This is encoded in Proposition
4.3
(4) above.
The second reason is due to the bound on
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-1}
.
In
[
8
]
Fine and Premoselli are able to find an upper Lipschitz bound for
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-1}
that depends on
k

[
8
, Lemma 4.13]
, whereas Hamenstädt and Jäckel are able to construct a
C^{0}
-estimate for
\mathcal{L}_{g}^{-1}

[
10
, Lemma 2.2]
and use this to find such a bound that is
independent of
k

[
10
, Proposition 4.2]
.
This
[
10
, Proposition 4.2]
replaces the estimate needed by Fine and Premoselli in
[
8
, Theorem 4.15]
and is the key to extending their results to all dimensions.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Consider

\Phi_{g_{k}}:C^{2}(\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right))\to C^{0}(\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right))

where
C^{\ell}(\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right))
denotes
\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right)
with the
C^{\ell}
-topology.
By
[
10
, Proposition 4.2]
there exists
\varepsilon>0
, independent of
k
, such that
\Phi_{g_{k}}
surjects onto the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
\Phi_{g_{k}}(g_{k})
, denoted
B(\Phi_{g_{k}}(g_{k}),\varepsilon)
.
By Proposition
4.3
(4) we have that
||g_{k}||_{L^{2}}\to 0
, and consequently
||\Phi_{g_{k}}(g_{k})||_{C^{0}}\to 0
.
Thus there exists
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
k\geq K
,
0\in B(\Phi_{g_{k}}(g_{k}),\varepsilon)
.
Therefore, there exists
\mathfrak{e}_{k}\in C^{2}(\text{Met}\left(X_{k}\right))
such that
\Phi_{g_{k}}(\mathfrak{e}_{k})=0
.
It is known that
\Phi_{g_{k}}
is Lipschitz with constant independent of
k
(see
[
8
, Lemma 4.1]
and
[
11
, Proposition 4.2]
).
Hence, by possibly choosing a larger
K
if necessary, we have that
\mathfrak{e}_{k}
is positive-definite and negatively curved.
The metric
\mathfrak{e}_{k}
is thus the desired negatively curved Einstein metric.
Moreover, by letting
\varepsilon\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, one can ensure that

\lim_{k\to\infty}||g_{k}-\mathfrak{e}_{k}||_{C^{2}}=0

∎

Remark 4.4
.

From the equation above, we see that our model Einstein metric
C^{2}
-approximates the negatively curved Einstein metric
\mathfrak{e}_{k}
, and this approximation gets closer as
k\to\infty
.
By equation
(30)
in
[
10
]
we see that
g_{k}
also
C^{2}
-approximates the negatively curved Kähler-Einstein metric
\omega_{k}
.
It is unclear to the authors if, eventually, one must have
\mathfrak{e}_{k}=\omega_{k}
for all
k
, or if this sequence of manifolds supports a sequence of negatively curved Einstein metrics that
C^{2}
-converge to
\omega_{k}
.
