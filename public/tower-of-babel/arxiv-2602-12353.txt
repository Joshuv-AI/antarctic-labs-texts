Bayesian Analysis for Remote Biosignature Identification on exoEarths (BARBIE) IV: Analyzing CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} Detections in the Near-IR to Determine the Long-Wavelength Cut-off for the Habitable Worlds Observatory Coronagraph
Celeste Hagee, Natasha Latouf, Avi M. Mandell, Michael D. Himes, Michael Dane Moore, Geronimo L. Villanueva

Abstract
We present our analysis of how the detectability of carbon dioxide ( CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} ) on an Earth-like planet varies with respect to signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), wavelength, and molecular abundance. Using the Bayesian Analysis for Remote Biosignature Identification on exoEarths (BARBIE) methodology, we can inform the optimal long-wavelength cut-off for the future Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO) coronagraph. We test 25 evenly-spaced 20% bandpasses between 0.8-2.0 μ \mu m, and simulate data spanning a range of SNRs and molecular abundance to analyze the relationship between wavelength and detectability for different planetary archetypes. We examine abundance levels from varying Earth epochs and a Venus-like archetype to investigate how detectability would change throughout the evolution of a rocky planet. Here, we present our results on the planetary conditions and technological requirements to strongly detect CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} . In addition, we analyze the degeneracy of CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} with carbon monoxide ( CO ), methane ( CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} ), and water ( H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} ). We determine that any abundance of CO does not achieve strong detections and that CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} and H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} play a pivotal role in the ability to detect CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} . We conclude that the optimal long-wavelength cut-off for the Habitable Worlds Observatory coronagraph should be 1.68 μ \mu m.



I
Introduction

Since the first discovery of an exoplanet around a main-sequence star in 1995
(
21
)
, more than 6,000 exoplanets have been discovered
(
1
)
. Astronomers are now looking towards characterizing these exoplanets, especially small, terrestrial planets, to tell us more about their formation, evolution, and composition. However, in order to constrain the properties of potentially Earth-like exoplanets around Sun-like stars, future telescopes will need to use high-contrast direct imaging, which requires a highly effective starlight suppression system such as a high-contrast coronagraph or starshade
(
23
;
25
;
10
)
. Based on the recommendations of the
22
, NASA began developing a future space telescope that could accomplish this goal; this concept is now called the Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO). HWO will be designed to have the technological imaging capability to detect and characterize Earth-like planets and identify signs of planetary habitability and possibly biology. Naturally, one of the key instruments currently in active development for HWO is the coronagraph instrument, nominally planned to have the capability to observe in
\sim
20% bandpasses across the UV, visible, and NIR wavelength regimes
(
25
;
15
)
.

A yet-undetermined parameter for the coronagraph instrument is the long-wavelength cut-off. One of the driving factors for constraining the long-wavelength cut-off in the NIR is the telescope temperature. At higher telescope temperatures, a higher background flux will contribute to a higher overall photon noise, impacting the exposure time necessary to reach a required SNR. Two strategies can help avoid the impact of a higher thermal background on spectral characterization: targeting spectral features at shorter wavelengths, where absorption features are weaker but thermal background is minimal, or cooling the telescope to enable low-background noise measurements of deeper spectral features at longer wavelengths.

It is clear that for wavelengths past
\sim

\mu
m, higher telescope temperatures produce more noise. However, cooling a massive space telescope below room temperature (

(

However, we move forward with this study based on the current baseline of having a telescope operating at room temperature. Thus, our goal is to determine whether exoplanet characterization is possible with a room temperature telescope (i.e., below 1.7
\mu
m). This will enable us to find the optimal long-wavelength cut-off that provides the opportunities to maximize our spectral characterization while minimizing additional costs.

13

24

IV
Conclusions

In this paper, we explored the conditions necessary to achieve a strong
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
detection based on molecular abundance, SNR, and wavelength using the BARBIE methodology and KEN grid set. Using L-KEN, we found that at modern levels of
CO
, there is an insignificant impact on the detectability of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and no strong
CO
detections are achievable at the highest
CO
abundance. Using B-KEN, we found that with modern levels of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
, only the three highest
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
abundances in our test - Venus-like, Archean-like, and Proterozoic-like - result in strong
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
detections. We found strong links between SNR, abundance, and wavelength for the case of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. Generally, stronger detections are more likely at higher SNRs and longer wavelengths. We also found that with increasing abundance, there are more strong detections at lower SNRs and shorter wavelengths. This study also described the detectability relationships of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
. We confirm that there is a strong correlation between these molecules and their impact on detectability of each other at NIR wavelengths. In particular, greater abundances of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
increased the difficulty to detect
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
.

Each of our results allow us to better constrain the optimal long-wavelength cut-off for the HWO coronagraph and understand the inter-connectedness between
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
