Extended LSTM: Adaptive Feature Gating for Toxic Comment Classification
Noor Islam S. Mohammad

Abstract
Toxic comment detection remains a challenging task, where transformer-based models (e.g., BERT) incur high computational costs and degrade on minority toxicity classes, while classical ensembles lack semantic adaptability. We propose xLSTM , a parameter-efficient and theoretically grounded framework that unifies cosine-similarity gating, adaptive feature prioritization, and principled class rebalancing. A learnable reference vector 𝐯 ∈ ℝ d \mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} modulates contextual embeddings via cosine similarity, amplifying toxic cues and attenuating benign signals to yield stronger gradients under severe class imbalance. xLSTM integrates multi-source embeddings (GloVe, FastText, BERT-CLS) through a projection layer, a character-level BiLSTM for morphological cues, embedding-space SMOTE for minority augmentation, and adaptive focal loss with dynamic class weighting. On the Jigsaw Toxic Comment benchmark, xLSTM attains 96.0% accuracy and 0.88 macro-F1, outperforming BERT by 33% on threat and 28% on identity_hate categories, with 15 × \times fewer parameters and < < 50 ms inference latency. Cosine gating contributes a +4.8% F1 gain in ablations. The results establish a new efficiency–adaptability frontier, demonstrating that lightweight, theoretically informed architectures can surpass large pretrained models on imbalanced, domain-specific NLP tasks. https://github.com/csislam/xLSTM-Project .

Introduction

The exponential growth of user-generated content across social media platforms has created unprecedented challenges for content moderation. Current estimates suggest over 4.5 billion social media users generate approximately 2.5 quintillion bytes of data daily, with platforms like Facebook processing nearly 900 million comments per day
[
1
]
. Within this vast corpus, toxic comments—encompassing hate speech, personal attacks, harassment, threats, and discriminatory remarks—actively harm vulnerable populations and disrupt civil discourse
[
2
,
3
]
. Traditional human-centered moderation faces insurmountable scalability constraints. Major platforms process approximately 350,000 abuse reports daily while maintaining a moderation capacity of only 15,000 reviews, creating a 23-fold coverage gap
[
4
]
. Beyond capacity limitations, human annotation exhibits substantial inter-rater disagreement (
\kappa=0.67
–0.72) with systematic biases favoring censorship of marginalized voices
[
5
]
. Furthermore, exposure to toxic content causes documented psychological harm to moderators, manifesting as PTSD, depression, and occupational burnout
[
6
]
.

Recent advances in deep learning have substantially improved toxicity detection performance. Transformer-based architectures (BERT, RoBERTa, XLM-RoBERTa) achieve competitive F1-scores (0.81–0.84 macro) through massive pretraining on diverse corpora
[
3
]
. However, these approaches present critical deployment limitations: (1)
computational overhead
: BERT-base requires 110 million parameters and 500ms CPU inference, prohibiting resource-constrained deployment
[
7
]
; (2)
minority-class collapse
: recall on rare categories (threat F1: 0.51, identity hate: 0.58) severely lags overall accuracy
[
8
]
; (3)
interpretability deficits
: attention patterns lack mechanistic insight into which semantic dimensions drive predictions
[
9
]
. Classical approaches (Logistic Regression: 0.79 F1, Linear SVM: 0.80 F1) remain interpretable and efficient but struggle to capture implicit toxicity requiring deep semantic understanding
[
9
]
. Ensemble methods (XGBoost: 0.83 F1) provide marginal improvements but rely on handcrafted features and exhibit poor recall under class imbalance
[
11
]
.

We posit that the fundamental challenge in imbalanced toxicity detection is not raw model capacity but
intelligent feature prioritization
. Toxic linguistic markers are often obscured within a predominantly benign context. Standard attention mechanisms reweight temporal positions but provide no explicit mechanism to suppress irrelevant semantic dimensions. We hypothesize that explicit feature-dimension gating—operating in embedding space rather than temporal space—can concentrate optimization signals on toxic-indicative semantic subspaces, yielding superior minority-class performance without transformer-scale complexity.

This paper presents
xLSTM
(Extended LSTM with Gated Attention), a parameter-efficient architecture combining: (1) learned cosine-similarity gating that filters embeddings based on a learnable reference vector; (2) multi-source semantic embedding fusion (GloVe, FastText, BERT CLS); (3) character-level BiLSTM for morphological pattern detection; (4) embedding-level SMOTE for class rebalancing; (5) weighted focal loss with adaptive class weighting. Evaluated on the Jigsaw Toxic Comment Classification Challenge (159,571 multilabel Wikipedia comments, six toxicity categories), xLSTM achieves 96.0% accuracy and 0.88 macro F1-score, outperforming BERT (0.81 F1) and XGBoost (0.83 F1) while requiring 15
\times
fewer parameters and maintaining <50ms CPU inference. Ablation studies demonstrate that cosine gating contributes 4.8% F1 improvement on minority classes, validating explicit feature prioritization mechanisms over implicit attention.

Related Work

2.1
Classical and Ensemble Approaches

Early toxicity detection work leveraged classical machine learning pipelines combining manual feature engineering with shallow classifiers
[
12
]
. Kumar et al.
[
13
]
demonstrated that TF-IDF vectorization combined with Support Vector Machines achieved reasonable binary toxicity classification performance. These approaches provided strong interpretability advantages, enabling practitioners to inspect which linguistic patterns drove predictions. However, classical methods fundamentally struggled to capture contextual nuance, particularly for implicit toxicity, sarcasm, and code-switching phenomena requiring understanding beyond local n-gram patterns.

Ensemble learning methods such as Random Forests and Gradient Boosting Machines emerged as natural successors
[
14
]
. These approaches aggregated predictions from multiple learners to improve generalization. Comparative studies demonstrated that XGBoost achieved competitive performance (F1
\sim
0.85) on balanced development sets, though performance degraded substantially under class imbalance without explicit resampling
[
15
]
. A critical limitation of ensemble methods is their dependence on manually crafted features; while ensembles can weight features effectively, they cannot discover novel feature combinations automatically.

2.2
Deep Learning and Sequential Architectures

Recurrent Neural Networks, particularly Long Short-Term Memory networks and Bidirectional LSTMs, became dominant architectures for toxicity detection, leveraging their natural capacity to model sequential dependencies
[
16
,
21
]
. Rahman et al.
[
17
]
demonstrated that BiLSTM networks with pretrained GloVe embeddings substantially outperformed classical and ensemble baselines, achieving 0.90+ F1-scores on benchmark datasets and establishing LSTMs as a new standard.

Attention mechanisms, introduced by Belal et al.
[
18
]
, became ubiquitous in toxicity detection. These mechanisms compute context-aware relevance weights across input positions via scaled dot-product attention. Attention-based BiLSTM architectures achieved empirically superior performance compared to unattended variants
[
19
,
21
]
. However, standard attention operates across the temporal dimension exclusively, reweighting
which tokens
are important without explicit mechanisms to suppress irrelevant semantic dimensions.

Character-level modeling emerged as a complementary approach, capturing morphological patterns such as misspellings, creative spellings, and Unicode variants that word-level tokenizers inherently miss
[
19
,
20
]
. Character-level LSTMs demonstrated particular improvements on informal, noisy text from social media
[
21
]
. However, character-level approaches alone prove insufficient for capturing semantic toxicity, necessitating hybrid word+character architectures.

Transformer-Based Models and Transfer Learning

BERT fundamentally transformed NLP performance through self-supervised pretraining on massive text corpora
[

. BERT’s bidirectional context modeling and deep feature learning (12 layers, 110M parameters) yielded state-of-the-art results across benchmarks. Fine-tuning BERT on toxicity datasets achieved F1-scores of 0.81–0.84, substantially outperforming prior LSTM-based approaches. Multilingual variants (mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa) extended these capabilities to 100+ languages
[
23
]
.

Despite these achievements, BERT-based approaches exhibit critical limitations for production deployment. First, computational costs are prohibitive: BERT-base inference requires
\sim
500ms on CPU, violating latency constraints in many production systems. Second, BERT exhibits severe overfitting and recall degradation on imbalanced, domain-specific datasets
[
23
,
24
]
. Fine-tuning shallow layers over massive pretrained representations can memorize limited toxic examples rather than learning robust patterns. Third, transformer attention mechanisms, while visualizable, do not mechanistically explain why particular semantic dimensions contribute to toxicity.

Recent work has explored parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA, adapter modules) to reduce transformer overhead
[
25
,
3
]
, but these methods maintain millions of parameters and substantial GPU requirements. Notably, few studies directly compare lightweight engineered architectures against transformers with identical computational budgets on the same imbalanced toxicity task, leaving the fundamental trade-off frontier between model complexity and performance uncharacterized.

2.4
Class Imbalance in Neural Networks

Extreme class imbalance is endemic to toxicity datasets, where non-toxic comments comprise 80–95% of corpora
[
26
]
. Standard approaches include weighted loss (assigning higher penalties to minority classes)
[
25
]
, SMOTE (generating synthetic minority examples via interpolation)
[
27
]
, undersampling (reducing majority-class samples), and focal loss (down-weighting easy negatives via
(1-p_{t})^{\gamma}
term).

While effective in traditional ML contexts, these techniques provide limited benefit for neural networks due to learned representation collapse: neural networks can inadvertently compress toxic signals into small subspaces through feature reuse
[
26
,
27
]
. Recent work proposes decoupled training using separate forward/backward passes for balanced minibatches
[
28
]
, but requires careful hyperparameter tuning. To our knowledge, no prior work systematically combines embedding-level SMOTE with learned gating mechanisms to explicitly suppress non-toxic context while amplifying sparse toxic signals.

2.5
Feature Fusion and Multi-Source Embeddings

Hybrid embedding strategies, concatenating multiple representation sources, have demonstrated complementary strengths across NLP tasks

3
Cosine-Similarity Gating

Standard attention mechanisms compute position-weighted aggregations:

\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{T}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V

(1)

This mechanism reweights
temporal positions
(
t=1,\ldots,T
) but operates uniformly across all feature dimensions (
d
). Under extreme class imbalance, this symmetric treatment is suboptimal: we possess abundant non-toxic examples enabling rich benign context representations, yet toxic examples are sparse and their representations may be diluted through unnecessary semantic dimensions.

We propose cosine-similarity gating, which operates in the feature dimension rather than the temporal dimension. The key insight is to learn a sparse, interpretable reference vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
capturing a prototypical toxic feature direction. For each token embedding
\mathbf{e}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we compute cosine similarity:

\text{sim}_{t}=\frac{\mathbf{e}_{t}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{e}_{t}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}}\in[-1,1]

(2)

This similarity score indicates whether the token embedding aligns with the learned toxic direction. In differentiable form, we employ soft gating via sigmoid:

g_{t}=\sigma(\beta\cdot\text{sim}_{t})=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\beta\cdot\text{sim}_{t})}

(3)

where
\beta
is a learnable temperature parameter controlling gate sharpness. The gated embedding is:

\mathbf{m}_{t}=g_{t}\odot\mathbf{e}_{t}

(4)

When
g_{t}\approx 0
, dimensions contributing to a non-toxic context are suppressed; when
g_{t}\approx 1
, toxic-indicative dimensions are preserved.

3.1
Theoretical Analysis and Gradient Signal Concentration

Consider a batch of
N
samples partitioned into majority (non-toxic) and minority (toxic) subsets such that
N=N_{\text{maj}}+N_{\text{min}}
, where
N_{\text{min}}\ll N_{\text{maj}}
. Under binary cross-entropy loss, the objective is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{BCE}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}[y_{i}\log(\hat{y}_{i})+(1-y_{i})\log(1-\hat{y}_{i})].

(5)

The gradient of the loss with respect to the model parameters
\theta
can be expressed as:

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{i}(\theta),

(6)

where
\ell_{i}
denotes the per-sample loss contribution. For minority-class instances, the expectation of the gradient term satisfies:

\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\text{minority}}[\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{i}]=\frac{N_{\text{min}}}{N}\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{\text{minority}}.

(7)

As
N_{\text{min}}/N\rightarrow 0
under severe imbalance, the aggregated gradient is dominated by the majority-class distribution, leading to
gradient dilution
for minority samples. Conventional attention mechanisms, which uniformly weigh feature dimensions, fail to mitigate this effect since they lack class-aware anisotropy in gradient flow.

To address this, the proposed
feature-dimension gating
introduces a selective amplification mechanism that suppresses majority-class-aligned directions in the embedding space. Let
\mathbf{u}_{\text{benign}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the principal direction of benign embeddings. The cosine gating vector
\mathbf{v}
is optimized such that
\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{u}_{\text{benign}}
, producing:

\text{sim}_{t}^{(\text{benign})}\approx 0\Rightarrow g_{t}^{(\text{benign})}\approx 0.5\quad\text{(neutral gating)},

(8)

while toxic embeddings exhibit high directional similarity:

\text{sim}_{t}^{(\text{toxic})}\gg 0\Rightarrow g_{t}^{(\text{toxic})}\approx 1\quad\text{(gradient amplification)}.

(9)

This mechanism induces a form of
orthogonal subspace gating
, where minority-relevant gradients are concentrated along discriminative manifolds, enhancing separability and stabilizing optimization under class imbalance. The model

integrates three complementary components: (a)
Time Mixing
, which transforms temporally normalized input sequences into aligned latent embeddings via shared linear projections; (b)
Joint Mixing
, wherein a bidirectional stacked sLSTM captures long-range dependencies and inter-view interactions through context-gated recurrent pathways; and (c)
View Mixing
, which hierarchically fuses multi-view embeddings into a unified spatio-temporal latent representation for forecasting. The hierarchical fusion pipeline establishes coherent gradient propagation across time and view dimensions, enabling interpretable alignment and generalization in heterogeneous sequential domains.

3.1.1
Gradient Concentration Under Extreme Imbalance

Theorem 1
(Gradient Concentration Under Extreme Imbalance)

Consider a classification model with embedding-based representation
\mathbf{e}_{t}
. Under position-based attention, the effective learning rate for minority-class features is scaled by the factor
\frac{N_{\text{min}}}{N}
. Under feature-dimension gating with gate
g_{t}=\sigma(\beta\cdot\text{sim}_{t})
, if
\mathbf{v}
is initialized to align with toxic-class centroids, the effective learning rate is scaled by
\alpha>\frac{N_{\text{min}}}{N}
, where
\alpha
depends on gate sharpness
\beta
and feature overlap between classes. For
\beta\to\infty
(hard gating),
\alpha\to 1
.

Proof Sketch:
Under soft gating, minority-class samples receive gate activations
g_{t}^{(\text{minority})}\approx\sigma(\beta\cdot c)
where
c
is the similarity between
\mathbf{e}_{t}
and
\mathbf{v}
. For toxic-aligned samples,
c>0
and
g_{t}\to 1
as
\beta\to\infty
. Backpropagation through the gate function preserves gradients:
\frac{\partial g_{t}}{\partial\text{sim}_{t}}=\beta\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(\beta\cdot\text{sim}_{t})>0
. Thus, minority-class gradients scale with
g_{t}\approx 1
rather than being diluted by
\frac{N_{\text{min}}}{N}
. The scaling factor
\alpha
depends on feature separability: if classes are well-separated in embedding space,
\alpha\approx 1
.

3.2
Initialization Strategy

The reference vector
\mathbf{v}
is initialized via toxic-example clustering. We sample 1,000 randomly selected toxic comments, compute their embeddings (averaged across positions), and compute the k-means centroid:

\mathbf{v}^{(0)}=\text{centroid}\left(\{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{e}_{t}^{(i)}:i\in\text{Toxic},|i|=1000\}\right)

(10)

This initialization biases the gate to preserve toxic features from training onset, providing
\sim
3% F1 improvement compared to random initialization.

4
Architecture and Components

4.1
Multi-Source Embedding Fusion

We concatenate three complementary embedding sources:

\mathbf{e}_{t}^{\text{raw}}=[\mathbf{e}^{\text{GloVe}}_{t}\|\mathbf{e}^{\text{FastText}}_{t}\|\mathbf{e}^{\text{BERT}}_{t}]\in\mathbb{R}^{1368}

(11)

where
\mathbf{e}^{\text{GloVe}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{300}
captures static co-occurrence semantics
[
31
]
,
\mathbf{e}^{\text{FastText}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{300}
encodes subword composition
[
32
]
, and
\mathbf{e}^{\text{BERT}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{768}
provides contextual representations
[
33
]
.

The concatenated embedding is projected via a learned linear transformation:

\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_{t}=\mathbf{W}_{\text{proj}}\mathbf{e}^{\text{raw}}_{t}+\mathbf{b}_{\text{proj}},\quad\mathbf{W}_{\text{proj}}\in\mathbb{R}^{512\times 1368}

(12)

This projection achieves three objectives: (1) dimensionality reduction (1368
\to
512), reducing computational overhead while retaining 92% variance; (2) unsupervised alignment of embedding sources; (3) regularization through controlled dimension reduction.

4.1.1
Theoretic Justification

Each embedding source provides distinct signals. GloVe captures lexical semantic associations, FastText captures morphological patterns, and BERT captures contextual dependencies. The mutual information
I(X_{i};X_{j})
between sources is non-trivial but non-maximal, indicating complementary rather than redundant information. Pearson correlation coefficients between sources yield:
\rho_{12}=0.42
(GloVe-FastText),
\rho_{13}=0.38
(GloVe-BERT),
\rho_{23}=0.35
(FastText-BERT). All correlations < 0.5 indicate relatively independent information. Empirically, concatenation outperforms averaging (F1: 0.881 vs. 0.875) and learned fusion via attention (F1: 0.881 vs. 0.868).

4.2
Stacked Bidirectional LSTM with Attention and Residual Connections

The proposed encoder employs a hierarchical architecture that integrates gated embeddings, recurrent modeling, multi-head attention, and residual normalization to capture long-range dependencies and semantic nuance within toxic discourse.

4.2.1
Component 1: Gated Embedding Layer

Cosine gating (Eq.
4
) filters redundant information from the fused embeddings, yielding task-adaptive token representations
\mathbf{m}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for each timestep
t\in[1,T]
. This operation aligns high-variance minority-class subspaces while preserving local contextual coherence.

4.2.2
Component 2: Stacked Bidirectional LSTM

Sequential dependencies are modeled using two layers of bidirectional LSTM units (BiLSTM), each with 256 hidden dimensions per direction:

\mathbf{h}^{(1)}_{t}=\overrightarrow{\mathrm{LSTM}}^{(1)}(\mathbf{m}_{t})\|\overleftarrow{\mathrm{LSTM}}^{(1)}(\mathbf{m}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{512}

(13)

The first layer encodes shallow contextual transitions, while the second refines higher-order temporal patterns:

\mathbf{h}^{(2)}_{t}=\mathrm{BiLSTM}^{(2)}(\mathbf{h}^{(1)}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{512}

(14)

Dropout regularization (
p_{\text{drop}}=0.3
) and recurrent dropout (
p_{\text{rec}}=0.2
) are applied to mitigate overfitting and enhance generalization across imbalanced toxicity categories.

4.2.3
Component 3: Multi-Head Self-Attention

To enrich inter-token dependencies beyond the recurrent horizon, we incorporate an 8-head self-attention mechanism:

\mathrm{MultiHead}(\mathbf{H}^{(2)})=\mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{head}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{head}_{8})\mathbf{W}^{O}

(15)

Each attention head computes context-weighted representations via scaled dot-product attention:

\mathrm{head}_{i}=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_{i}\mathbf{K}_{i}^{\top}}{\sqrt{64}}\right)\mathbf{V}_{i}

(16)

This formulation enables the model to attend selectively to toxicity-bearing segments (e.g., insults or hate tokens) while retaining global syntactic alignment.

4.2.4
Component 4: Residual Connections and Layer Normalization

Residual pathways facilitate gradient propagation through deep recurrent-attentive stacks, while layer normalization ensures stable feature distributions:

\mathbf{h}_{\text{att}}=\mathrm{LayerNorm}\!\big(\mathbf{h}^{(2)}+\mathrm{MultiHead}(\mathbf{h}^{(2)})\big)

(17)

These operations collectively mitigate vanishing-gradient phenomena and accelerate convergence
[
3
,
34
]
.

4.2.5
Component 5: Global Max Pooling

Given that toxicity is often concentrated in short lexical spans, global max pooling is adopted:

\mathbf{h}_{\text{pool}}=\max_{t\in[1,T]}\mathbf{h}_{\text{att},t}\in\mathbb{R}^{512}

(18)

This operation extracts the most salient activation across timesteps, emphasizing strong toxicity cues. Empirically, max pooling outperforms mean pooling (macro-F1: 0.881 vs. 0.875).

4.3
Character-Level BiLSTM

A parallel character-level encoder complements word-level modeling by capturing morphological and subword toxicity variants (e.g., obfuscated slurs, Unicode manipulations):

\mathbf{c}_{i}=\mathrm{Embed}_{\text{char}}(c_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{200},\quad\mathbf{h}^{\text{char}}_{i}=\mathrm{BiLSTM}_{\text{char}}(\mathbf{c}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{256}

(19)

Pooling across characters yields compact representations:

\mathbf{h}_{\text{char,pool}}=\max_{i}\mathbf{h}^{\text{char}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{256}

(20)

Character-level fusion contributes an average
+2.4\%
improvement in macro-F1, validating its importance for robustness to non-standard spellings and adversarial text perturbations.

4.4
Final Representation and Classification

The final hybrid embedding concatenates both linguistic granularities:

\mathbf{h}_{\text{final}}=[\mathbf{h}_{\text{pool}}\|\mathbf{h}_{\text{char,pool}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{768}

(21)

The concatenated vector passes through a dense transformation:

\mathbf{z}=\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_{1}\mathbf{h}_{\text{final}}+\mathbf{b}_{1})\in\mathbb{R}^{256}

(22)

Finally, a sigmoid layer outputs category-wise probabilities:

\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{2}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_{2})\in[0,1]^{6}

(23)

Each output dimension corresponds to a toxicity attribute (
toxic, severe_toxic, obscene, threat, insult, identity_hate
). Binary thresholds at 0.5 yield the final multilabel decision vector.

5
Loss Functions and Class Rebalancing

5.1
Weighted Focal Loss

Severe class imbalance is addressed via a weighted focal loss
[
23
]
, which dynamically scales the cross-entropy gradient to emphasize hard and minority samples:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\!\left[\alpha_{k}(1-p_{i,k})^{\gamma}\log(p_{i,k})\mathbf{1}[y_{i,k}=1]+(1-\alpha_{k})p_{i,k}^{\gamma}\log(1-p_{i,k})\mathbf{1}[y_{i,k}=0]\right]

(24)

where
\gamma=2.0
controls the focusing strength,
p_{i,k}
is the predicted probability, and
\alpha_{k}
is the normalized inverse-frequency class weight:

\alpha_{k}=\frac{1-\rho_{k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}(1-\rho_{j})},\quad\rho_{k}=\frac{N_{k}}{N_{\max}}

(25)

This formulation ensures that underrepresented toxicity categories (e.g.,
threat
,
\rho=0.002
) receive proportionally higher gradient contributions (
\alpha_{\text{threat}}\approx 0.994
), mitigating dominance by frequent classes.

5.1.1
Theoretical Justification

Under standard cross-entropy loss, confident predictions contribute negligibly to the loss, regardless of correctness. Focal loss modulates this by applying
(1-p_{t})^{\gamma}
, making the loss more sensitive to misclassifications on hard examples (low confidence). For imbalanced data, the model quickly learns to classify majority-class examples with high confidence, making these contributions negligible. Focal loss forces the model to continue learning from hard minority-class examples
[
35
]
. This formulation provides two sources of imbalance compensation: (1) the focusing term
(1-p_{t})^{\gamma}
automatically down-weights confident predictions (which tend to be majority-class non-toxic examples), (2) class weights
\alpha_{k}
directly scale loss contributions by category rarity.

5.2
Embedding-Level SMOTE

Beyond loss weighting, we apply SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) at the embedding level to increase the effective sample size for rare toxicity categories
[
36
]
:

\mathbf{e}^{\text{synth}}=\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\text{toxic}}+\lambda(\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\text{toxic}}-\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\text{toxic}})

(26)

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\text{toxic}}
is an existing toxic example embedding,
\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\text{toxic}}
is its
k
-nearest neighbor (within toxic class,
k=5
), and
\lambda\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)
. SMOTE on embeddings rather than raw features offers three advantages: (1) operates in the learned representation space where toxic patterns are concentrated, (2) preserves linguistic coherence by interpolating between real examples, and (3) avoids feature collisions that plague pixel-space SMOTE for images.

We generate synthetic examples to maintain a minority/majority ratio of 1:2 in training batches, effectively tripling the minority-class sample size across epochs. This synthetic data is treated identically to real data during backpropagation, ensuring gradients flow through minority-class examples with full strength.

5.2.1
Statistical Justification

SMOTE assumes feature space is continuous and that interpolating between same-class examples produces valid examples. This assumption holds for embedding spaces trained via unsupervised pretraining (GloVe, FastText) or self-supervised objectives (BERT masked language modeling), which encourage locally-smooth manifolds
[
36
,
37
]
. For toxicity detection, interpolating between two threat examples should produce a valid (though synthetic) threat example, not a non-toxic comment. This approach has been validated in imbalanced learning contexts and aligns with recent work on synthetic data generation in high-dimensional spaces
[
38
]
.

5.3

The proposed

algorithm (Algorithm

) integrates three complementary components—
synthetic oversampling
,
cosine-gated embedding alignment
, and
focal loss optimization
—to enhance minority-class generalization in imbalanced toxic-comment datasets
[
54
]
. At the start, model parameters
\theta
are initialized, including the gating vector
\mathbf{v}
(Eq.
10
), which regulates directional selectivity in the embedding space. For each epoch, the training data
\mathcal{D}
are divided into mini-batches
\mathcal{B}
of size
B

\mathcal{B}_{\text{toxic}}
are extracted to generate synthetic counterparts
\mathcal{B}_{\text{synth}}
using the SMOTE procedure, which constructs new examples by interpolating within the minority manifold. The augmented batch
\mathcal{B}_{\text{balanced}}
thus mitigates label skewness and prevents majority-class dominance during optimization.

Each token sequence in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{balanced}}

\mathbf{e}_{t}

11
). Subsequently, the

(Algorithm

\mathbf{u}_{\min}

\mathbf{m}_{t}

\hat{\mathbf{y}}
.

The

(Eq.
24

\eta
. Early stopping is applied when the validation F1-score fails to improve for seven consecutive epochs, ensuring convergence without overfitting. Consequently, this training scheme jointly addresses class imbalance, embedding misalignment, and loss-based bias, resulting in an adaptive and geometrically balanced representation space where minority classes achieve improved margin separation and robustness.

6
Optimization and Training

6.1
Adam Optimizer with Gradient Clipping

We optimize the model parameters by minimizing the focal loss objective using the Adam optimizer
[
39
]
:

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta\cdot\frac{\mathbf{m}_{t}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{v}_{t}}+\epsilon},

(27)

where
\eta
is the learning rate,
\mathbf{m}_{t}
and
\mathbf{v}_{t}
denote the first- and second-moment estimates of the gradients, and
\epsilon
ensures numerical stability. The hyperparameters are set as follows: learning rate
\eta=10^{-4}
, exponential decay rates
\beta_{1}=0.9
,
\beta_{2}=0.999
, and
\epsilon=10^{-8}
.

To mitigate gradient explosion in deep recurrent stacks, we employ
gradient clipping
with a maximum norm threshold of 1.0:

\text{if }\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}\|_{2}>1.0,\quad\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}\leftarrow\frac{\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}}{\|\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}\|_{2}}.

(28)

This regularization stabilizes backpropagation through long sequences and prevents the exploding gradient phenomenon commonly observed in LSTM and GRU-based architectures
[
40
]
.

6.2
Training Schedule and Early Stopping

Training is conducted for a maximum of 60 epochs with a batch size of 64, balancing computational efficiency and gradient stability. Early stopping is applied to monitor the validation macro F1-score, employing a patience window of 7 epochs. If the validation F1 does not improve for seven consecutive epochs, training halts, and the best-performing model parameters (maximizing validation F1) are restored. The validation macro F1-score is computed after each epoch using a stratified 10% validation set. We select the F1-score, rather than loss, as the early-stopping criterion because it directly reflects the target evaluation metric for imbalanced multilabel classification tasks. This protocol ensures convergence stability while avoiding overfitting and promoting generalization to unseen data.

6.3
Learning Rate Scheduling

Although the main experiments adopt a fixed learning rate for reproducibility, we further evaluate a
cosine annealing schedule with warm restarts

[
41
]
as an optional optimization enhancement. The schedule periodically decays and resets the learning rate according to:

\eta_{t}=\eta_{\min}+\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})\left(1+\cos\left(\pi\cdot\frac{t\bmod T_{i}}{T_{i}}\right)\right),

(29)

where
\eta_{\min}=10^{-6}
,
\eta_{\max}=10^{-4}
, and
T_{i}
denotes the restart period (set to 15 epochs). Empirically, cosine annealing yields a marginal improvement in macro F1 (0.881 vs. 0.880 without scheduling) but increases hyperparameter complexity. Hence, the fixed learning rate configuration is used in the main study to ensure experimental transparency and reproducibility.

7.1
Dataset and Preprocessing

We evaluate the proposed extended LSTM framework on the Jigsaw Toxic Comment Classification Challenge dataset, comprising
N=159{,}571
Wikipedia discussion comments. The dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and test splits (80%/10%/10%) while preserving class distributions via stratified sampling. Each comment is annotated across six toxicity dimensions, yielding a highly imbalanced multilabel setting. The label imbalance ratios are as follows:

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{threat}}

\displaystyle=0.002,\quad\rho_{\text{severe\_toxic}}=0.003,\quad\rho_{\text{identity\_hate}}=0.009,

(30)

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{toxic}}

\displaystyle=0.046,\quad\rho_{\text{obscene}}=0.053,\quad\rho_{\text{insult}}=0.050.

Comment length distributions are right-skewed: median 45 tokens, 95th percentile 320 tokens, and a maximum of 3,000 tokens. This short-text characteristic necessitates robust contextual encoding to extract toxicity signals from a limited linguistic context, in contrast to long-document scenarios where contextual redundancy aids robustness. After preprocessing and stratified splitting, the dataset consists of 127,656 training samples, 15,957 validation samples, and 31,914 test samples. The multilabel nature of the data allows multiple toxicity categories to co-occur within a single comment, further motivating the need for an architecture capable of modeling inter-label dependencies and shared semantic subspaces.

7.1.1
Preprocessing and Text Normalization

Raw comments undergo systematic preprocessing: (1) lowercasing all text to normalize capitalization; (2) lemmatization via spaCy’s morphological analyzer to reduce vocabulary sparsity; (3) removal of URLs, email addresses, and numeric sequences (replaced with generic token
<NUM>
); (4) stopword removal using language-specific NLTK lists; (5) subword tokenization via SentencePiece byte-pair encoding. Word sequences are padded to
T_{\max}=300
tokens using post-padding (appending zeros), which preserves early-token information critical for toxicity cues often appearing at comment onset. Character-level sequences are constructed separately, mapping each character to its vocabulary index, then padding to
T_{\text{char}}=800
maximum characters (equivalent to
\sim
160 tokens). Out-of-vocabulary tokens, both at the word and character levels, are represented via special tokens (
<UNK>
), enabling graceful handling of rare phenomena without vocabulary expansion.

7.2
Evaluation Metrics

The multilabel toxicity detection task is evaluated using multiple complementary metrics that capture both overall and class-specific performance.

7.2.1
Macro F1-Score

The
Macro F1-score
represents the unweighted mean of per-class F1-scores, assigning equal importance to each toxicity type regardless of frequency:

\text{Macro F1}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}F1_{k}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{2P_{k}R_{k}}{P_{k}+R_{k}},

(31)

where
P_{k}
and
R_{k}
denote the precision and recall for category
k
, respectively.
This metric is particularly suitable for imbalanced datasets, as it equally weights minority and majority categories, thus providing a class-balanced measure of overall system fairness and robustness.

7.2.2
Micro F1-Score

The
Micro F1-score
aggregates true positives (TP), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) across all classes:

\text{Micro F1}=\frac{2\sum_{k}\text{TP}_{k}}{2\sum_{k}\text{TP}_{k}+\sum_{k}\text{FP}_{k}+\sum_{k}\text{FN}_{k}}.

(32)

Unlike the Macro F1, it is weighted by class frequency and thus more reflective of dominant classes.
While Micro F1 provides a global performance summary, it can obscure minority-class weaknesses, making it complementary to the Macro F1 analysis.

7.2.3
Accuracy and Per-Category Metrics

Accuracy
measures the proportion of samples where all predicted labels exactly match the ground truth. This strict criterion is valuable in multilabel toxicity classification, where partial correctness may still indicate harmful bias. Additionally, per-category F1-scores and precision/recall values are reported to diagnose heterogeneity across toxicity types (e.g., threats, obscenity, hate speech). This enables a fine-grained understanding of which categories are most challenging and which model components mitigate class-specific errors.

7.2.4
Hamming Loss and Statistical Significance

The
Hamming Loss
quantifies the fraction of incorrectly predicted labels across all instances and classes:

\text{Hamming Loss}=\frac{1}{N\times K}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_{i,k}\neq y_{i,k}],

(33)

where
\mathbf{1}[\cdot]
is the indicator function.
Lower values correspond to higher multilabel precision and reduced partial misclassification.

To assess the reliability of reported metrics, we compute 95% confidence intervals using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) and perform paired
t
-tests between xLSTM and baseline models.
Differences with
p<0.05
are considered statistically significant, confirming that observed performance gains are not due to random variation.

8.0.1
Mutual Information Analysis

We formalize embedding fusion using information-theoretic principles
[
45
]
. Let
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
, and
X_{3}
denote embeddings from GloVe, FastText, and BERT, respectively. Optimal fusion seeks complementary sources (low mutual information) that jointly maximize task-relevant information.

Empirical correlation analysis yields:

\displaystyle\rho_{12}

\displaystyle=0.42\ \text{(GloVe\textendash FastText)},

(37)

\displaystyle\rho_{13}

\displaystyle=0.38\ \text{(GloVe\textendash BERT)},

\displaystyle\rho_{23}

\displaystyle=0.35\ \text{(FastText\textendash BERT)},

indicating moderate independence across embeddings. Instead of averaging, concatenation preserves the unique representational capacity of each source. The total correlation (multi-information)
[
46
]
across sources is:

\text{TC}(X_{1};X_{2};X_{3})\approx 0.15\ \text{nats},\quad\text{where }I(X_{1};X_{2})=0.18\ \text{nats}.

(38)

Thus, adding a third source contributes additional non-redundant information, capturing
\approx 0.85
nats of unique information per source on average. This empirically supports the multi-source fusion hypothesis and aligns with prior findings on distributed representation synergy
[
47
]
.

8.0.2
Complementarity of Embedding Sources

GloVe embeddings capture static co-occurrence-based semantic relationships, encoding which words frequently appear in similar contexts across large corpora. FastText embeddings incorporate subword information via character n-grams, enabling representation of morphological variations and out-of-vocabulary words through compositional semantics
[
45
]
. BERT contextual embeddings model position and discourse-dependent meaning through bidirectional transformer context, capturing syntactic dependencies and pragmatic interpretation
[
31
]
.

These three representation types operate at different levels of linguistic abstraction: (1) global semantic co-occurrence (GloVe), (2) morphological composition (FastText), (3) contextual dependency (BERT). This hierarchical complementarity explains why concatenation outperforms fusion mechanisms that might collapse representations to dominant sources. Empirically, concatenation achieves F1 = 0.881 compared to averaging (F1 = 0.875) and learned attention-based fusion (F1 = 0.868).

8.1
Class Imbalance and Margin Maximization Geometry

8.1.1
Geometric Interpretation of Cosine Gating

Under extreme class imbalance, consider the geometry of the classification problem in embedding space. Minority-class (toxic) points are sparse and isolated, while majority-class (non-toxic) points form dense clusters. Standard margin-maximization objectives
[
29
]
aim to maximize the margin between classes:

\text{margin}=\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}

(39)

However, under imbalance, the optimal decision boundary is skewed toward the minority-class cluster (to maximize overall accuracy), resulting in small margins for minority samples. Cosine gating addresses this by explicitly filtering away majority-class-aligned dimensions, effectively rotating the coordinate system to align minority-class clusters with the axes
[
22
]
. This geometric transformation increases minority-class margins in the gated embedding space. Formally, let
\mathbf{u}_{\min}
and
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{maj}}
denote principal components of minority and majority clusters (computed via PCA
[
18
]
). Cosine gating learns
\mathbf{v}\approx\mathbf{u}_{\min}
, so the gated embedding space is approximately rotated such that
\mathbf{u}_{\min}
aligns with a coordinate axis. In this rotated space, class separability improves dramatically
[
28
]
.

8.1.2
Feature Space Rotation and Class Separability

Under cosine gating, embeddings aligned with the learned reference vector
\mathbf{v}
receive high gate activation (
g_{t}\approx 1
), while embeddings orthogonal to
\mathbf{v}
receive suppressed activation (
g_{t}\approx 0.5
). This selective amplification concentrates the model’s representational capacity on toxic-indicative dimensions. Consider the decision boundary in the gated space. For a linear classifier, the decision boundary is defined by
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{m}_{t}=0
where
\mathbf{m}_{t}=g_{t}\odot\mathbf{e}_{t}
are gated embeddings. The gating mechanism
g_{t}
acts as a learned dimensionality-dependent weighting that automatically suppresses features associated with the majority class. This is more effective than fixed feature weighting because it adapts to the learned toxic patterns
[
12
]
. Empirically, this geometric insight explains why cosine gating provides 4.8% F1 improvement over standard attention. We increase minority-class separability margin without imposing transformer-scale computation overhead by rotating the feature space to align with the toxic-class structure.

Societal Impacts and Ethical Considerations

xLSTM’s lightweight architecture enables equitable access to AI moderation while also introducing ethical challenges requiring proactive governance.
Positive Impacts:
The model’s 15× parameter efficiency and sub-50ms CPU latency enable deployment on low-resource servers, democratizing access for underfunded platforms such as educational or activist forums. Automated pre-filtering reduces human moderator exposure to toxic content, alleviating burnout and trauma. Moreover, cosine gating visualizations provide interpretable insights into model reasoning, enhancing transparency and trust

Potential Harms:
Despite its benefits, xLSTM risks amplifying existing dataset biases
[
3

or suppressing marginalized voices through misclassification of reclaimed or emotional expressions. Further, adversarial perturbations (e.g., “t0xic”) remain a vector for evasion, underscoring the need for robust and fair model auditing before deployment.

10.1
Limitations of Current Work

Despite encouraging results, several limitations constrain generalizability. First, xLSTM was evaluated exclusively on English Wikipedia comments; generalization to other languages, domains (Twitter, Reddit, YouTube), and moderation contexts remains unexplored. Toxicity manifests differently across languages (morphological and syntactic richness varies)

11
Conclusion

This paper presents xLSTM, a parameter-efficient neural architecture for scalable, interpretable toxicity detection on imbalanced multilabel classification tasks. Through integration of cosine-similarity gating, multi-source embeddings, character-level fusion, and principled class rebalancing, xLSTM achieves state-of-the-art performance (96% accuracy, 0.88 macro F1) while maintaining 15
\times
parameter efficiency and <50ms CPU latency compared to transformer baselines. Ablation studies confirm that engineered feature prioritization mechanisms provide superior minority-class performance compared to implicit attention mechanisms. Our work establishes a novel trade-off frontier between model complexity and task-specific adaptation, with significant implications for future research in imbalanced classification, efficient NLP, and content moderation systems. The consistent 7-8% F1 improvement across comment lengths demonstrates xLSTM’s robustness to context availability, while error analysis reveals interpretable failure modes and directions for future research. Production deployment analysis shows that xLSTM requires only 5.8 CPU cores for 10M daily comments, compared to 570 for BERT—enabling practical toxicity detection at unprecedented scale and cost efficiency. Future work should prioritize cross-domain evaluation, multilingual extension, contextual modeling, and comprehensive fairness audits to ensure xLSTM’s responsible deployment in real-world moderation systems serving diverse communities and languages.
