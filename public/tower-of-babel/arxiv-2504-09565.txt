Zero-energy Edge States of Tight-Binding Models for Generalized Honeycomb-Structured Materials
Borui Miao, Yi Zhu

Abstract
Generalized honeycomb-structured materials have received increasing attention due to their novel topological properties. In this article, we investigate zero-energy edge states in tight-binding models for such materials with two different interface configurations: type-I and type-II, which are analogous to zigzag and armchair interfaces in the honeycomb structure. We obtain the necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of such edge states and rigorously prove the existence of paired zero-energy edge states. More specifically, type-II interfaces support two zero-energy states exclusively between topologically distinct materials. For type-I interfaces, zero-energy edge states exist between both topologically distinct and identical materials when hopping coefficients satisfy specific constraints. We further prove that the two energy curves for edge states exhibit a strict crossing. Numerical simulations are presented to demonstrate that wave packets associated with these edge states remain localized near the interface and can be chosen to propagate in either direction, reflecting the pairing of the two edge modes.

Introduction and Summary of Results

1.1
Introduction

Owing to their novel physical properties and potential applications, topological materials have attracted considerable attention from researchers in condensed matter physics and quantum mechanics. One of their key features is topologically protected edge states localized near the interface between two topologically distinct materials
Shockley (1939)
;
Kane and Mele (2005)
;
Hasan and Kane (2010)
;
Rechtsman et al. (2013)
. Specifically, the interface between two gapped materials with different topological indices gives rise to localized edge states in the band gap. Topological concepts can also be generalized to describe edge states in various physical systems, such as magnetic systems
Ablowitz and Cole (2020)
;
Shapiro and Weinstein (2022)
, non-linear systems
Ablowitz and Cole (2017)
;
Ablowitz and Cole (2019)
and non-Hermitian systems
Ammari et al. (2024b)
;
Ammari et al. (2024a)
.

A typical system that supports edge states is the 2D honeycomb structure. Its novel properties are related to the conical intersection in the dispersion surfaces, known as the Dirac point
Wallace (1947)
;
Neto et al. (2009)
;
Fefferman and Weinstein (2012)
;
Ammari et al. (2020a)
. A rigorous characterization of these properties was first provided in the pioneering work of Fefferman and Weinstein
Fefferman and Weinstein (2012)
. Following the framework, the existence of a Dirac point can be extended to general elliptic operators with honeycomb structures
Lee-Thorp et al. (2018)
. Ammari and his collaborators also proved similar results for materials in the subwavelength regime by applying layer potential methods to Helmholtz systems
Ammari et al. (2018)
;
Ammari et al. (2020a)
;
Ammari et al. (2020b)
. In honeycomb structures, a band gap can be created by breaking the inversion symmetry
Neto et al. (2009)
;
Schaibley et al. (2016)
;
Xiao et al. (2007)
.
Recently, generalized honeycomb-structured materials
Wu and Hu (2015)
;
Yves et al. (2017)
have been shown to support the existence of a double Dirac point at the center of the Brillouin zone
\Gamma

Miao and Zhu (2024)
;
Cao and Zhu (2023)
. A band gap can also be opened by contracting or dilating the inclusions. The band inversion at
\Gamma
is commonly regarded as a signature of a topological transition in the local model obtained from perturbations near
\Gamma
; see
Wu and Hu (2015)
. However, the precise definition of a bulk topological invariant is more subtle and remains under discussion
Palmer and Giannini (2021)
;
Xu et al. (2023)
. In this work, we therefore focus on a direct spectral analysis of the corresponding tight-binding Hamiltonians.

To generate edge states in honeycomb structures, domain wall modulation or sharp termination at the interface between two topologically distinct materials can be employed
Lee-Thorp et al. (2016)
;
Xia et al. (2023)
. The rigorous characterization of the existence of edge states for domain wall modulation was first provided by
Fefferman et al. (2016)
. It has also been verified that the edge or interface configuration determines the existence of edge states
Drouot and Weinstein (2020)
;
Fefferman et al. (2022)
;
Fefferman et al. (2023)
. However, analysis of continuous systems remains challenging, especially for sharply-terminated materials that are frequently used in photonics. Therefore, physicists adopt tight-binding approximations that reduce the continuum models to discrete models to obtain insights into the edge states
Fefferman et al. (2017)
;
Fefferman and Weinstein (2020)
;
Ammari et al. (2022)
. In
Fefferman et al. (2023)
, the authors proved mathematically that flat-band edge states arise for a zigzag-type edge but disappear for an armchair-type edge in tight-binding models.
The existence of edge states in generalized honeycomb materials remains largely unexplored, with only a few existing studies addressing this issue
Cao and Zhu (2025)
.

In this article, we investigate the existence of zero-energy edge states in tight-binding models for generalized honeycomb-structured materials with different interface configurations. Specifically, we study the
zigzag
and
armchair
interfaces, which will be called
type-I
and
type-II
interfaces, respectively. With the Floquet-Bloch transform, we seek to determine the point spectrum of an infinite discrete Hamiltonian operator with quasi-momentum
k
. We obtain results on:

1.

The existence of a pair of zero-energy edge states at
k=0
. For the type-I interface, zero-energy edge states may occur between either topologically distinct or identical materials when the parameters satisfy specific conditions. For the type-II interface, they occur only between topologically distinct materials.

2.

The local behavior near
k=0
given the existence of zero-energy edge states. The point spectrum of the corresponding Hamiltonian operators strictly crosses at
k=0
. This result is valid for both types of interfaces.

These results provide insights into the dynamical behavior of wave packets; see
Fefferman and Weinstein (2013)
;
Watson and Weinstein (2018)
;
Lee-Thorp et al. (2018)
. Additionally, we numerically simulate the wave packet dynamics at the interfaces.

Problem Formulation

In this section, we introduce the tight-binding model for generalized honeycomb structures with nearest neighborhood approximation. The tight-binding model arises from reducing eigenvalue problems on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to problems on lattice sites.

\Lambda\triangleq\mathbb{Z}\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}

2.2
The Full Hamiltonian Operator

Given the configuration of the discrete system, we define the wave function on each site
\Psi\in l^{2}(\mathbb{GH})\simeq l^{2}(\Lambda;\mathbb{C}^{6})
.

\Psi\triangleq\{(\psi_{{}_{\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{j}+\lambda}})_{j=1}^{6}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}=\left\{(\psi_{{}_{j,\lambda}})_{j=1}^{6}\right\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}.

Here the Hilbert space
l^{2}(\Lambda;\mathbb{C}^{6})
is equipped with an inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
given by

(\Phi,\Psi)\triangleq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{6}\overline{\phi}_{{}_{j,\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{j,\lambda}},\quad\Phi,\Psi\in l^{2}(\Lambda;\mathbb{C}^{6}).

From (
2
), the
full Hamiltonian operator

\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{full}}
acting on
\Psi\in l^{2}(\Lambda;\mathbb{C}^{6})
, with nearest neighborhood approximation, is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{full}}\Psi)_{{}_{1,\lambda}}&\triangleq-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{4,\lambda}}-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{5,\lambda}}-a_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{6,\lambda-\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}}},\\
(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{full}}\Psi)_{{}_{2,\lambda}}&\triangleq-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{4,\lambda}}-d_{{}_{\lambda+\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}-\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}}}\psi_{{}_{5,\lambda+\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}-\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}}}-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{6,\lambda}},\\
(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{full}}\Psi)_{{}_{3,\lambda}}&\triangleq-c_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{4,\lambda+\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}}}-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{5,\lambda}}-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{6,\lambda}},\\
(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{full}}\Psi)_{{}_{4,\lambda}}&\triangleq-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{1,\lambda}}-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{2,\lambda}}-c_{{}_{\lambda-\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}}}\psi_{{}_{3,\lambda-\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}}},\\
(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{full}}\Psi)_{{}_{5,\lambda}}&\triangleq-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{1,\lambda}}-d_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{2,\lambda-\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}+\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}}}-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{3,\lambda}},\\
(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{full}}\Psi)_{{}_{6,\lambda}}&\triangleq-a_{{}_{\lambda+\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}}}\psi_{{}_{1,\lambda+\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}}}-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{2,\lambda}}-b_{{}_{\lambda}}\psi_{{}_{3,\lambda}}.\end{aligned}

(4)

The
hopping coefficients
between nearest neighborhoods
\{a_{{}_{\lambda}},b_{{}_{\lambda}},c_{{}_{\lambda}},d_{{}_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
are strictly positive real numbers with uniform lower and upper bounds.

\max_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\{a_{{}_{\lambda}},b_{{}_{\lambda}},c_{{}_{\lambda}},d_{{}_{\lambda}}\}\leq C<+\infty,\quad\min_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\{a_{{}_{\lambda}},b_{{}_{\lambda}},c_{{}_{\lambda}},d_{{}_{\lambda}}\}\geq c>0.

(5)

Here
\{b_{{}_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
denotes the

\{a_{{}_{\lambda}},c_{{}_{\lambda}},d_{{}_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
denote the

. It is clear that the full Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{full}}
is self-adjoint and bounded from the Hilbert space
l^{2}(\Lambda;\mathbb{C}^{6})
to itself. Therefore, its spectrum satisfies
\sigma(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{full}})\subset\mathbb{R}
.

2.3
Interface Configuration and Floquet-Bloch Decomposition

In this article, we investigate two types of interface configurations, namely the type-I interface and the type-II interface.

We now give a rigorous definition for both types of interfaces. First, we define two infinite subsets of the generalized honeycomb structure
\mathbb{GH}_{\pm}
, which satisfy
\mathbb{GH}_{+}\cup\mathbb{GH}_{-}=\mathbb{GH}

\mathbb{GH}_{+}\cap\mathbb{GH}_{-}=\emptyset

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{int}\big(\mathbb{GH}_{\pm}\big)&\triangleq\big\{\omega:\mathcal{N}_{\omega}\subseteq\mathbb{GH}_{\pm},\omega\in\mathbb{GH}_{\pm}\big\},\\
\partial\big(\mathbb{GH}_{\pm}\big)&\triangleq\big\{\omega:\mathcal{N}_{\omega}\nsubseteq\mathbb{GH}_{\pm},\omega\in\mathbb{GH}_{\pm}\big\}.\end{split}

(6)

It follows easily that

As shown in

From the definition, it is clear that for both types of interfaces, the operators depend on the parameters

3
Edge States for Type I Interface

In this section, we first investigate the point spectrum at
E_{\mathrm{I}}(k)=0
for the type-I interface near
k=0
. More specifically, we look for nonzero solutions to the following equations that decay as
n\to\pm\infty
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\{\begin{aligned} &-b_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{4,n}}-b_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{5,n}}-c_{{}_{n-1,\mathrm{I}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k}u_{{}_{6,n-1}}=0,\\
&-b_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{4,n}}-d_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k}u_{{}_{5,n}}-b_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{6,n}}=0,\\
&-c_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{4,n+1}}-b_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{5,n}}-b_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{6,n}}=0,\end{aligned}\right.\\

&-c_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k}u_{{}_{1,n+1}}-b_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{2,n}}-b_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{3,n}}=0.\end{aligned}\right.\end{split}

(14)

Before investigating the existence of zero eigenvalues, we first notice the intrinsic symmetry of the Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k)
. Let
\mathbf{u}=\{\mathbf{u}(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in l^{2}_{k}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6})
, where
\mathbf{u}(n)=(u_{{}_{j,n}})_{j=1}^{6}\in\mathbb{C}^{6}
denotes its components. The bounded operators
\widehat{T}(k),\widehat{V}:l^{2}_{k}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6})\to l^{2}_{k}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6})
can be defined componentwise as follows

\begin{split}[\widehat{T}(k)\mathbf{u}](n)&\triangleq\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}nk}\begin{pmatrix}0_{3\times 3}&\mathrm{Id}_{3\times 3}\\
\mathrm{Id}_{3\times 3}&0_{3\times 3}\end{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}(n)},\\
(\widehat{V}\mathbf{u})(n)&\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}_{3\times 3}&0_{3\times 3}\\
0_{3\times 3}&-\mathrm{Id}_{3\times 3}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}(n).\end{split}

(15)

Geometrically,
\widehat{V}
represents the sublattice symmetry of the Hamiltonian operators, while
\widehat{T}(k)
is the antiunitary reflection operator with respect to the blue axis, as shown in the left panel of
Figure

2
.

By direct calculation one has

Proposition 1
.

For any
k\in[-\pi,\pi)
and
\mathbf{u}\in l_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6})
satisfying
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k)\mathbf{u}=E_{\mathrm{I}}(k)\mathbf{u}
for some
E_{\mathrm{I}}(k)\in\mathbb{R}
, one has

\displaystyle\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k)\widehat{T}(k)\mathbf{u}=\widehat{T}(k)\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k)\mathbf{u}=E_{\mathrm{I}}(k)\widehat{T}(k)\mathbf{u},

(16)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}(k)^{2}=\operatorname{Id},\quad\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k)\widehat{V}\mathbf{u}=-E_{\mathrm{I}}(k)\widehat{V}\mathbf{u}.

When
E_{\mathrm{I}}(k)=0
, the two sets of equations in (
14
) are decoupled. Therefore, to construct a zero-energy state, it is sufficient to first seek a nonzero solution that takes the form

\mathbf{u}(n)=(0,0,0,u_{{}_{4,n}},u_{{}_{5,n}},u_{{}_{6,n}})^{T}.

(17)

Then
\widehat{T}(k)\mathbf{u}
is another nonzero solution, and
\mathbf{u}
and
\widehat{T}(k)\mathbf{u}
are linearly independent. It follows that:

Proposition 2
.

For any
k\in[-\pi,\pi)
, the eigenspace associated with the zero eigenvalue
\{\mathbf{u}\in l^{2}_{k}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6}):\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k)\mathbf{u}=0\}
is even dimensional.

when

Theorem 3
.

And the matrix
P(b,\varepsilon,k)
has two distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(b,\varepsilon,k)
,
\lambda_{2}(b,\varepsilon,k)
when
k\neq 0
or
\varepsilon\neq 0
. Moreover, when
k\neq 0
or
\varepsilon\neq 0
, we have

0<|\lambda_{1}|<1,\quad|\lambda_{2}|>1.

(24)

Proof.

We begin by carrying out explicit calculations:

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{2}^{-1}A_{1}=\frac{1}{b^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}b^{2}-b(b+\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k}&-(b+\varepsilon)^{2}\\
b^{2}&b(b+\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k}\end{pmatrix}\\
A_{4}^{-1}A_{3}=\frac{1}{b(b+\varepsilon)}\begin{pmatrix}(b+\varepsilon)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k}-b^{2}&-b^{2}\\
b^{2}&b^{2}\end{pmatrix},\\
A_{6}^{-1}A_{5}=\frac{1}{b(b+\varepsilon)}\begin{pmatrix}b^{2}-b(b+\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k}&-b^{2}\\
(b+\varepsilon)^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k}&b(b+\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

From this, one can easily calculate the determinant of
P(b,\varepsilon,k)

\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}=\det[P(b,\varepsilon,k)]=\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}k},

As for its trace, a direct but lengthy calculation yields

P(b,\varepsilon,k)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha(b,\varepsilon,k)&\beta(b,\varepsilon,k)\\
-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k}\beta(b,\varepsilon,k)&\gamma(b,\varepsilon,k)\end{pmatrix},

(25)

where the elements are given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha(b,\varepsilon,k)&=-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k}\frac{(b+\varepsilon)^{2}}{b^{2}}+2\frac{b+\varepsilon}{b}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k}+\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}k}-4\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k}\frac{b}{b+\varepsilon}+4\frac{b^{2}}{(b+\varepsilon)^{2}},\\
\beta(b,\varepsilon,k)&=-\frac{(b+\varepsilon)^{3}}{b^{3}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k}\frac{(b+\varepsilon)^{2}}{b^{2}}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k}\frac{b+\varepsilon}{b}-3+2\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k}\frac{b}{b+\varepsilon},\\
\gamma(b,\varepsilon,k)&=\frac{(b+\varepsilon)^{4}}{b^{4}}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k}\frac{(b+\varepsilon)^{2}}{b^{2}}+2\frac{b+\varepsilon}{b}-\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k}.\end{split}

(26)

Therefore, we have

\operatorname{trace}[P(b,\varepsilon,k)]=\Big(\frac{2b}{b+\varepsilon}+\frac{(b+\varepsilon)^{2}}{b^{2}}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k}\Big)^{2}-2\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k}.

A minimizer
k^{\ast}
of the real part of
\operatorname{trace}(P(b,\varepsilon,k))
with respect to
k
satisfies

\sin k^{\ast}\bigg(2+\frac{4b}{b+\varepsilon}+\frac{2(b+\varepsilon)^{2}}{b^{2}}\bigg)-2\sin 2k^{\ast}=0.

The above relation holds if and only if
k^{\ast}=0
or
k^{\ast}=\pi
. It is easy to check that the minimizer is
k^{\ast}=0
. Now we have

\Re\{\operatorname{trace}[P(b,\varepsilon,k)]\}\geq\Big(\frac{2b}{b+\varepsilon}+\frac{(b+\varepsilon)^{2}}{b^{2}}-1\Big)^{2}-2.

Minimizing over
\varepsilon
, we conclude that the minimizer can be achieved if and only if
\varepsilon=0
. Therefore, we have

|\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}|\geq\Re(\operatorname{trace}(P))>2.

However, if
\lambda_{1}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
, then
\lambda_{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(2k+\theta)}
and one has

|\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}|=|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}+\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(2k+\theta)}|\leq 2.

This contradiction finishes the proof. ∎

Denote the eigenvector
v_{i}(b_{+},\delta_{+},k)
corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
.
To ensure that the eigenvector
\mathbf{u}
decays at
\pm\infty
, we impose the following two conditions

\displaystyle A_{1}^{-1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},k)\widetilde{A}_{1}(k)\widetilde{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=g_{{}_{1}}v_{1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},k),

(27)

\displaystyle A_{6}^{-1}(b_{-},\delta_{-},k)\widetilde{A}_{6}\widetilde{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=g_{{}_{2}}v_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-},k).

(28)

Here
g_{{}_{1}},g_{{}_{2}}
are two complex nonzero constants, while
\widetilde{v}_{0}=(u_{{}_{4,0}},u_{{}_{6,-1}})^{T}
. Note that the first condition (
27
) corresponds to exponential decay as

. By (

) and

\displaystyle\begin{split}(u_{{}_{4,2m}},u_{{}_{6,2m-1}})^{T}&=(P(b_{+},\delta_{+},k))^{m}A_{1}^{-1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},k)\widetilde{A}_{1}(k)\widetilde{v}_{0}\\
&=g_{{}_{1}}[\lambda_{1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},k)]^{m}v_{1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},k).\end{split}

For

\displaystyle\begin{split}(u_{{}_{4,2m}},u_{{}_{6,2m-1}})^{T}&=(P(b_{-},\delta_{-},k))^{m}A_{6}^{-1}(b_{-},\delta_{-},k)\widetilde{A}_{6}\widetilde{v}_{0}\\

The above procedures give

for given constants

provided that
\delta_{+},\delta_{-}\neq 0
or

.

To simplify the conditions (

v_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-},k)=g_{{}_{3}}A_{6}^{-1}\widetilde{A}_{6}\widetilde{v}_{0}=g_{{}_{4}}A_{6}^{-1}\widetilde{A}_{6}\widetilde{A}_{1}^{-1}(k)A_{1}v_{1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},k),

where we let

Therefore, we are led to the constraints by properly choosing a complex constant

(29)

Combining the above results and Proposition

\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

Here

Theorem 5
.

When
k=0
and
\varepsilon\neq 0
, the matrix
P(b,\varepsilon,0)
has two eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(b,\varepsilon,0),\lambda_{2}(b,\varepsilon,0)
such that
0<\lambda_{1}<1
and
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}^{-1}>1
. Their corresponding eigenvectors are given by

v_{1}=(1,f_{1}(b,\varepsilon,0))^{T},\quad v_{2}=(1,f_{1}^{-1}(b,\varepsilon,0))^{T}.

(31)

Here

\displaystyle f_{1}(b,\varepsilon,0)=\frac{-\alpha+\gamma-\sqrt{(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}-4\beta^{2}}}{2\beta}\left\{\begin{split}&<-1,\quad\varepsilon<0,\\
&\in(-1,0),\quad\varepsilon>0.\end{split}\right.

(32)

The parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are given in (

) for fixed
(b,\varepsilon,0)
.

Proof.

Notice that the matrix

is given explicitly by

-\beta(b,\varepsilon,0)&\gamma(b,\varepsilon,0)\end{pmatrix}.

(33)

One concludes that the eigenvector can be represented by
v_{1}=(1,f_{1})^{T},v_{2}=(1,f_{2})^{T}
, where

are nonzero since

for

by

. From the definition

, one has

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{split}\alpha+\beta f_{j}&=\lambda_{j}\\
-\beta+\gamma f_{j}&=\lambda_{j}f_{j}\end{split}\right.,\quad j=1,2.

By simple calculation, one can check that

satisfy the following quadratic equation

\beta f_{j}^{2}+(\alpha-\gamma)f_{j}+\beta=0,\quad j=1,2.

Here the equation has two distinct real roots since the determinant

by

. It is also obvious that

.
As a result,

are given by

\frac{-\alpha+\gamma\pm\sqrt{(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}-4\beta^{2}}}{2\beta}.

The corresponding eigenvalues

are given by

To identify the eigenvalue

satisfying

and associated coefficient

,

gives

Therefore

From

, when

\alpha-\gamma+\sqrt{(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}-4\beta^{2}}>2\beta,

while for
\varepsilon>0

2\beta<\alpha-\gamma+\sqrt{(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}-4\beta^{2}}<0.

As a result, one has

\displaystyle f_{1}(b,\varepsilon,0)=\frac{-\alpha+\gamma-\sqrt{(\alpha-\gamma)^{2}-4\beta^{2}}}{2\beta}\left\{\begin{split}&<-1,\quad\varepsilon<0,\\
&\in(-1,0),\quad\varepsilon>0.\end{split}\right.

∎

This fact means one can always choose

such that

\frac{(b_{+}+\delta_{+})(b_{-}+\delta_{-})}{c^{2}}f_{1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},0)=f_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-},0)=f_{1}^{-1}(b_{-},\delta_{-},0),

(34)

since

are always negative for

. It is obvious that

is unique, if we suppose the hopping coefficients are positive. Combining the above arguments, we have

.

For
k=0
and
\delta_{+},\delta_{-}\neq 0
, the necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a two-fold eigenvalue for the Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)
, as defined in (

(b_{+}+\delta_{+})(b_{-}+\delta_{-})f_{1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},0)f_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-},0)=c^{2}.

(35)

Here

is given in (

) and (

).

Remark 1
.

From the above existence result, one does not need to combine two topologically distinct materials to guarantee the existence of zero eigenvalues. Further, zero-energy edge states exist only when the hopping coefficients satisfy specific conditions, which means that they are not robust against perturbations. Nevertheless, they can be used to build propagating states. For example, choose
b_{+}=b_{-}=60
and
\delta_{+}=\delta_{-}=30
so that the two bulk materials are identical. The condition in
Theorem

6
is satisfied when

c=\frac{15\times(23-\sqrt{385})}{2}.

Thus the type-I interface can support zero-energy edge states even in the absence of a topological transition.

From Proposition
2
and previous discussions, we know that for the type-I interface, the eigenvectors corresponding to the zero eigenvalue at
k=0
can be properly chosen such that

\begin{split}\mathbf{u}^{A}&=\{(0,0,0,u_{{}_{4,n}},u_{{}_{5,n}},u_{{}_{6,n}})^{T}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\\
\mathbf{u}^{B}&=\widehat{T}(0)\mathbf{u}^{A}=\{(u_{{}_{4,n}},u_{{}_{5,n}},u_{{}_{6,n}},0,0,0)^{T}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}.\end{split}

(36)

In the following discussions, we suppose
\mathbf{u}^{A},\mathbf{u}^{B}
have real components and are normalized so that
(\mathbf{u}^{A},\mathbf{u}^{A})=(\mathbf{u}^{B},\mathbf{u}^{B})=1
.
Although
\widehat{T}(0)
relates the two zero-energy modes, these
modes do not form a Kramers pair, since
\widehat{T}(0)^{2}\neq-\operatorname{Id}
. Consequently, this symmetry alone does not imply a Kramers-type prohibition of backscattering.

3.3
Local Behavior of
E_{\mathrm{I}}(k)
near
k=0

In this section, we study the local behavior of
E_{\mathrm{I}}(k)
given the existence of zero eigenvalues at
k=0
.

Remark 2
.

The two-fold zero eigenvalue of
\widehat{H}_{\rm I}(0)
is isolated from the remaining spectrum. By Weyl’s sequence argument, it can be verified that

\bigcup_{k\in[-\pi,\pi)}\sigma_{\rm ess}\bigl(\widehat{H}_{\rm I}(k)\bigr)=\sigma_{\rm ess}\bigl(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(b_{+},\delta_{+})\bigr)\cup\sigma_{\rm ess}\bigl(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(b_{-},\delta_{-})\bigr).

Therefore,
Proposition
12
implies

\sigma_{\rm ess}\bigl(\widehat{H}_{\rm I}(0)\bigr)\cap(-g_{\rm ess},g_{\rm ess})=\emptyset,\quad g_{\rm ess}:=\min\{|\delta_{+}|,|\delta_{-}|\}>0.

Moreover, zero cannot be an accumulation point of nonzero eigenvalues.
Otherwise, there would exist distinct eigenvalues

E_{j}\neq 0
,
E_{j}\to 0
, and normalized eigenvectors
\mathbf{u}_{j}
satisfying

\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\mathbf{u}_{j}=E_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}.

Since
\widehat{H}_{\rm I}(0)
is self-adjoint, the vectors
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
are orthonormal and hence
\mathbf{u}_{j}\rightharpoonup 0
. Furthermore,

\bigl\|\widehat{H}_{\rm I}(0)\mathbf{u}_{j}\bigr\|=|E_{j}|\longrightarrow 0.

Thus
\{\mathbf{u}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is a Weyl sequence for zero, which would imply
0\in\sigma_{\rm ess}(\widehat{H}_{\rm I}(0))
, contradicting the essential spectral gap above. Consequently,

\operatorname{dist}\left(0,\,\sigma\bigl(\widehat{H}_{\rm I}(0)\bigr)\setminus\{0\}\right)>0.

From this we can choose a contour
\Gamma
in
\mathbb{C}
around
0
that contains no other point of
\sigma(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0))
. Since
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k)
is analytic with respect to
k
, define

P(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\Gamma}(z-\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k))^{-1}\mathrm{d}z.

For small
|k|
,
P(k)
is analytic and has rank two. This justifies the perturbation arguments in the proof of
Theorem

7
.

We will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose the parameters
(b_{\pm},\delta_{\pm},c)
are properly chosen such that for the Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k)
, there exists a two-fold zero eigenvalue at
k=0
. Then there exist two distinct point spectra
E_{\mathrm{I},-}(k),E_{\mathrm{I},+}(k)
near zero that admit the following asymptotic expansion.

E_{\mathrm{I},\pm}(k)=\pm E^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}|k|+\mathcal{O}(k^{2}).

(37)

Here
E^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}
is a positive real constant determined from the parameters
(b_{\pm},\delta_{\pm},c)
.

Proof.

For the type-I interface, one has by direct Taylor expansion

\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k)=\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)+k\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}+\mathcal{O}(k^{2}).

Here the first order part
\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}:l^{2}_{k}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6})\to l^{2}_{k}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6})
is a bounded operator given by

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{split}(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}\mathbf{u})_{{}_{1,n}}&=\mathrm{i}c_{{}_{n-1,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{6,n-1}},\\
(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}\mathbf{u})_{{}_{2,n}}&=-\mathrm{i}d_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{5,n}},\\
(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}\mathbf{u})_{{}_{3,n}}&=0,\end{split}\right.\quad\left\{\begin{split}(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}\mathbf{u})_{{}_{4,n}}&=0,\\
(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}\mathbf{u})_{{}_{5,n}}&=\mathrm{i}d_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{2,n}},\\
(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}\mathbf{u})_{{}_{6,n}}&=-\mathrm{i}c_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{1,n+1}}.\end{split}\right.

(38)

Therefore, we suppose that the eigenvalue problem near
k=0

\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k)\mathbf{u}_{k}=E_{\mathrm{I},\pm}(k)\mathbf{u}_{k},

(39)

admits the following asymptotic expansions

\begin{split}\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(k)&=\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)+k\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}+k^{2}\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(r)},\\
E_{\mathrm{I}}(k)&=kE^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I},\pm}+\mathcal{O}(k^{2}),\\
\mathbf{u}_{k}&=a_{1}\mathbf{u}^{A}+a_{2}\mathbf{u}^{B}+k\mathbf{u}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}+\mathcal{O}(k^{2}).\end{split}

(40)

Here
a_{1},a_{2}
are complex constants. By matching the first order terms in (
39
), we have

\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}(a_{1}\mathbf{u}^{A}+a_{2}\mathbf{u}^{B})+\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\mathbf{u}^{(1)}=E_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}(a_{1}\mathbf{u}^{A}+a_{2}\mathbf{u}^{B}),

Taking the inner product with
\mathbf{u}^{A},\mathbf{u}^{B}
on both sides, one has

\left\{\begin{split}(\mathbf{u}^{A},\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}(a_{1}\mathbf{u}^{A}+a_{2}\mathbf{u}^{B}))+(\mathbf{u}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)})&=(\mathbf{u}^{A},E_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}(a_{1}\mathbf{u}^{A}+a_{2}\mathbf{u}^{B})),\\
(\mathbf{u}^{B},\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{I}}(a_{1}\mathbf{u}^{A}+a_{2}\mathbf{u}^{B}))+(\mathbf{u}^{B},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)})&=(\mathbf{u}^{B},E_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}(a_{1}\mathbf{u}^{A}+a_{2}\mathbf{u}^{B})).\end{split}\right.

(41)

Since the Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)
is self-adjoint, we are led to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\mathbf{u}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)})&=(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\mathbf{u}^{A},\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)})=0,\\
(\mathbf{u}^{B},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)})&=(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}(0)\mathbf{u}^{B},\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)})=0.\end{aligned}

Therefore, we conclude that the values
E_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}
are given by the eigenvalues of the matrix

\mathbf{M}_{0,\mathrm{I}}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}(\mathbf{u}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}\mathbf{u}^{A})&(\mathbf{u}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}\mathbf{u}^{B})\\
(\mathbf{u}^{B},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}\mathbf{u}^{A})&(\mathbf{u}^{B},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}\mathbf{u}^{B})\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

From the definition (
36
), we have

\begin{split}(\mathbf{u}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}\mathbf{u}^{A})&=(\mathbf{u}^{B},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}\mathbf{u}^{B})=0,\\
(\mathbf{u}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}\mathbf{u}^{B})&=\mathrm{i}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(d_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}|u_{{}_{5,n}}|^{2}-c_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{6,n}}u_{{}_{4,n+1}}),\\
(\mathbf{u}^{B},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}\mathbf{u}^{A})&=\mathrm{i}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-d_{{}_{n,\mathrm{I}}}|u_{{}_{5,n}}|^{2}+c_{{}_{n-1,\mathrm{I}}}u_{{}_{6,n-1}}u_{{}_{4,n}})=-(\mathbf{u}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}\mathbf{u}^{B}).\end{split}

(43)

Thus, one only needs to calculate
(\mathbf{u}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{I}}^{(1)}\mathbf{u}^{B})
. We recall (
27
) and (
28
), which yield the following equality

\widetilde{v}_{0}=g_{{}_{6}}\widetilde{A}_{1}^{-1}(0)A_{1}v_{1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},0)=g_{{}_{7}}\widetilde{A}_{6}^{-1}A_{6}v_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-},0),

(44)

Here
g_{{}_{6}},g_{{}_{7}}
are two real constants and
\widetilde{v}_{0}=(u_{{}_{4,0}},u_{{}_{6,-1}})^{T}
. By direct calculation, one has

\widetilde{v}_{0}=g_{{}_{6}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\frac{b_{+}+\delta_{+}}{c}\end{pmatrix}v_{1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},0).

From previous results, we have, for

(u_{{}_{4,2m}},u_{{}_{6,2m-1}})^{T}=(P(b_{+},\delta_{+},0))^{m}A_{1}^{-1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},0)\widetilde{A}_{1}(0)\widetilde{v}_{0}=g_{{}_{6}}\lambda_{1}^{m}v_{1}.

(45)

For

(u_{{}_{4,2m}},u_{{}_{6,2m-1}})^{T}=(P(b_{-},\delta_{-},0))^{m}A_{6}^{-1}(b_{-},\delta_{-},0)\widetilde{A}_{6}\widetilde{v}_{0}=g_{{}_{7}}\lambda_{2}^{m}v_{2}.

(46)

Thus we have, from

u_{{}_{6,2m-1}}u_{{}_{4,2m}}<0,\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}.

Now we continue to calculate

for
m\geq 0

, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}-A_{2}^{-1}(b_{+},\delta_{+},0)\widetilde{A}_{1}(0)\widetilde{v}_{0}&=(u_{{}_{6,0}},u_{{}_{5,0}})^{T},\\

\displaystyle\begin{split}u_{{}_{6,0}}&=g_{{}_{6}}\big[(t_{+}-1)+t_{+}^{2}f_{1}\big],\\

Here

Similar procedures hold for

In this section, we investigate the existence of zero-energy edge states for the type-II interface and the behavior of the associated eigenvalue branches near

\{b_{{}_{n,\mathrm{II}}},c_{{}_{n,\mathrm{II}}},d_{{}_{n,\mathrm{II}}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\{\begin{aligned} &-b_{{}_{n,\mathrm{II}}}w_{{}_{4,n}}-b_{{}_{n,\mathrm{II}}}w_{{}_{5,n}}-c_{{}_{n,\mathrm{II}}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k}w_{{}_{6,n+1}}=0,\\

(47)

We will discuss two situations in this section: connecting topologically distinct materials and connecting topologically identical materials. In the former situation, one has
\delta_{+}\delta_{-}<0
, while in the latter situation, one has
\delta_{+}\delta_{-}>0
.

First and foremost, we also notice the intrinsic symmetry of the Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}
. Let
\mathbf{w}=\{\mathbf{w}(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, where
\mathbf{w}(n)=(w_{{}_{j,n}})_{j=1}^{6}\in\mathbb{C}^{6}
denotes its components. Define the antiunitary reflection operator

[\widehat{R}(k)\mathbf{w}]\triangleq\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}nk}\begin{pmatrix}P_{3\times 3}&0_{3\times 3}\\
0_{3\times 3}&P_{3\times 3}\end{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{w}(n)},\quad P_{3\times 3}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&1&0\\
1&0&0\end{pmatrix},

(48)

which is an analogue of
\widehat{T}(k)
.
Similarly,
\widehat{R}(k)
represents the reflection symmetry across the blue axis in type-II interface, as shown in the right panel of
Figure

2
. By direct calculation, one also has

Proposition 8
.

For any
k\in[-\pi,\pi)
and
\mathbf{w}\in l_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6})
satisfying
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}(k)\mathbf{w}=E_{\mathrm{II}}(k)\mathbf{w}
for some
E_{\mathrm{II}}(k)\in\mathbb{R}
, one has

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}(k)\widehat{R}(k)\mathbf{w}&=\widehat{R}(k)\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}(k)\mathbf{w}=E_{\mathrm{II}}(k)\widehat{R}(k)\mathbf{w},\\
\widehat{R}(k)^{2}&=\operatorname{Id},\;\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}(k)\widehat{V}\mathbf{w}=-E_{\mathrm{II}}(k)\widehat{V}\mathbf{w}.\end{split}

(49)

One can also deduce from the above proposition that for
E_{\mathrm{II}}=0
, one can seek two solutions
\mathbf{w}^{A}=\{w^{A}(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\mathbf{w}^{B}=\{w^{B}(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
that take the form

w^{A}(n)=(0,0,0,w_{{}_{4,n}},w_{{}_{5,n}},w_{{}_{6,n}})^{T},\quad w^{B}(n)=(w_{{}_{1,n}},w_{{}_{2,n}},w_{{}_{3,n}},0,0,0)^{T},

(50)

respectively. It is worth noting that the eigenspace associated with the zero eigenvalue
\{\mathbf{w}\in l^{2}_{k}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6}):\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}(k)\mathbf{w}=0\}
need not be even dimensional, which is different from the type-I interface.

In the following section we seek solutions
\mathbf{w}^{A},\mathbf{w}^{B}
that satisfy

\widehat{R}(k)\mathbf{w}^{A}=\mathbf{w}^{A},\quad\widehat{R}(k)\mathbf{w}^{B}=\mathbf{w}^{B}.

(51)

The reason is that, if
\widehat{R}(k)\mathbf{w}^{A}\neq\mathbf{w}^{A}
, then one defines

\mathbf{w}_{{}_{1}}^{A}\triangleq\mathbf{w}^{A}+\widehat{R}(k)\mathbf{w}^{A},\quad\mathbf{w}_{{}_{2}}^{A}\triangleq\mathbf{w}^{A}-\widehat{R}(k)\mathbf{w}^{A}.

For
\mathbf{w}_{{}_{1}}^{A}
, the desired symmetry relation is immediate. For
\mathbf{w}_{{}_{2}}^{A}
, one has
\widehat{R}(k)[\mathrm{i}\mathbf{w}_{{}_{2}}^{A}]=\mathrm{i}\mathbf{w}_{{}_{2}}^{A}
. Thus, without loss of generality, one supposes the relations (
51
) hold. From these relations, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}w_{{}_{6,n}}=\overline{w_{{}_{4,n}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}nk},\quad w_{{}_{5,n}}=\overline{w_{{}_{5,n}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}nk};\\
w_{{}_{3,n}}=\overline{w_{{}_{1,n}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}nk},\quad w_{{}_{2,n}}=\overline{w_{{}_{2,n}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}nk}.\end{split}

Therefore, one can recast the solution
\{(w_{{}_{j,n}})_{j=1}^{6}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
into another set of solutions
\{(v_{{}_{j,n}})_{j=1}^{6}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}

\displaystyle\begin{split}w_{{}_{4,n}}=v_{{}_{4,n}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{nk}{2}},\quad w_{{}_{5,n}}=v_{{}_{5,n}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{nk}{2}},\quad w_{{}_{6,n}}=\overline{v_{{}_{4,n}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{nk}{2}},\\
w_{{}_{1,n}}=v_{{}_{1,n}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{nk}{2}},\quad w_{{}_{2,n}}=v_{{}_{2,n}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{nk}{2}},\quad w_{{}_{3,n}}=\overline{v_{{}_{1,n}}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{nk}{2}},\end{split}

(52)

where
v_{{}_{1,n}},v_{{}_{4,n}}\in\mathbb{C}
and

4.2
Existence of Zero Eigenvalue at

In this section, we analyze the existence of zero eigenvalues by taking advantage of

9
at

.
To investigate the decay as
n\to\pm\infty
, one can directly calculate the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
Q(b,\varepsilon,0)
.

Lemma 3
.

For
b>0,\varepsilon\neq 0
satisfying
b+\varepsilon>0
, the matrix

Q(b,\varepsilon,0)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{b+\varepsilon}{b}&\frac{b+\varepsilon}{b}\\
-\frac{b+\varepsilon}{b}&0&\frac{b+\varepsilon}{b}\\
\frac{b^{2}}{(b+\varepsilon)^{2}}&\frac{b^{2}}{(b+\varepsilon)^{2}}&0\end{pmatrix},

has three distinct eigenvalues
\{\mu_{j}(b,\varepsilon)\}_{j=1}^{3}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{split}\mu_{1}&=-\frac{b+\varepsilon}{2b}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{(b+\varepsilon)^{2}}{b^{2}}+\frac{8b}{(b+\varepsilon)}}<-2,\\
\mu_{2}&=-\frac{b+\varepsilon}{2b}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{(b+\varepsilon)^{2}}{b^{2}}+\frac{8b}{(b+\varepsilon)}}\left\{\begin{aligned} &>1,\quad\varepsilon<0,\\
&\in(0,1),\quad\varepsilon>0,\end{aligned}\right.\\
\quad\mu_{3}&=\frac{b+\varepsilon}{b}.\end{split}\right.

(66)

The corresponding eigenvectors
\{v_{j}(b,\varepsilon)\}_{j=1}^{3}
are

v_{1}=(t_{1},t_{1},1)^{T},\quad v_{2}=(t_{2},t_{2},1)^{T},v_{3}=(\mathrm{i},-\mathrm{i},0)^{T},

(67)

where
t_{1}(b,\varepsilon),t_{2}(b,\varepsilon)
are real numbers given by

t_{1}=\frac{b+\varepsilon}{\mu_{1}b+b+\varepsilon}=-\frac{b+\varepsilon}{\mu_{2}b},\quad t_{2}=\frac{b+\varepsilon}{\mu_{2}b+b+\varepsilon}=-\frac{b+\varepsilon}{\mu_{1}b}.

(68)

Remark 4
.

Although the matrix
Q(b,\varepsilon,0)
is real, we let
v_{3}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
to make sure that the original eigenvector
\mathbf{w}\in l_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6})
satisfies
\widehat{R}(0)w=w
.

We will prove the following theorem characterizing the existence of zero eigenvalues of
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}(k)
at
k=0
.

Theorem 10
.

Suppose the hopping coefficients of the Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}(k)
are given by (
13
). Then the following statements hold.

(i)

When
\delta_{+}\delta_{-}<0
, a two-fold eigenvalue at
E_{\mathrm{II}}(0)=0
at
k=0
always exists.

(ii)

When
\delta_{+}\delta_{-}>0
, zero does not belong to the spectrum of
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}(0)
.

Proof.

We first begin with (i). By (
64
) and (
65
), if
\chi_{{}_{n}},\xi_{{}_{n}}
decay as
n\to\pm\infty
, it should hold that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\{\begin{aligned} \chi_{{}_{0}}&=h_{1}v_{1}+h_{2}v_{3},\;\chi_{{}_{-2}}=h_{3}v_{3},\\
\xi_{{}_{1}}&=h_{4}v_{2},\;\xi_{{}_{-1}}=h_{5}v_{1}+h_{6}v_{2},\end{aligned}\right.\quad\delta_{+}>0,\delta_{-}<0,\\
\left\{\begin{aligned} \chi_{{}_{0}}&=h_{1}v_{1}+h_{2}v_{2},\;\chi_{{}_{-2}}=h_{3}v_{2},\\
\xi_{{}_{1}}&=h_{4}v_{3},\;\xi_{{}_{-1}}=h_{5}v_{1}+h_{6}v_{3},\end{aligned}\right.\quad\delta_{+}<0,\delta_{-}>0.\end{split}

(69)

where
\{h_{j}\}_{j=1}^{6}
are real constants chosen so that the original eigenvector
\mathbf{w}\in l_{0}^{2}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6})
satisfies
\widehat{R}(0)\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}
. Further, it should hold that

Q_{{}_{A,-2}}Q_{{}_{A,-1}}(0)\chi_{{}_{0}}=\chi_{{}_{-2}},\quad\xi_{{}_{1}}=Q_{{}_{B,0}}Q_{{}_{B,-1}}(0)\xi_{-1}.

(70)

By direct calculation, one can write each element of the real matrices.

\displaystyle\begin{split}Q_{{}_{A,-2}}Q_{{}_{A,-1}}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{ct_{-}}{b_{-}}+\frac{t_{-}b_{+}}{ct_{+}}&\frac{t_{-}b_{+}}{ct_{+}}&-\frac{ct_{-}}{b_{-}}\\
\frac{t_{-}b_{+}}{ct_{+}}&\frac{ct_{-}}{b_{-}}+\frac{t_{-}b_{+}}{ct_{+}}&-\frac{ct_{-}}{b_{-}}\\
-\frac{b_{+}}{c}&-\frac{b_{+}}{c}&\frac{2b_{+}}{c}\end{pmatrix},\\
Q_{{}_{B,0}}Q_{{}_{B,-1}}(0)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{ct_{+}}{b_{+}}+\frac{t_{+}b_{-}}{ct_{-}}&\frac{t_{+}b_{-}}{ct_{-}}&-\frac{ct_{+}}{b_{+}}\\
\frac{t_{+}b_{-}}{ct_{-}}&\frac{ct_{+}}{b_{+}}+\frac{t_{+}b_{-}}{ct_{-}}&-\frac{ct_{+}}{b_{+}}\\
-\frac{b_{-}}{c}&-\frac{b_{-}}{c}&\frac{2b_{-}}{c}\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(71)

where
t_{\pm}\triangleq(b_{\pm}+\delta_{\pm})/b_{\pm}
. For
\delta_{+}>0,\delta_{-}<0
, by (
70
) and the fact that the matrix
Q_{{}_{A,-2}}Q_{{}_{A,-1}}(0)
is real, one has
h_{1}=0
. Therefore, supposing
h_{2}=1
, one has

\chi_{{}_{-2}}=h_{3}v_{3}(b_{-},\delta_{-})=Q_{{}_{A,-2}}Q_{{}_{A,-1}}(0)v_{3}(b_{+},\delta_{+}).

It can be easily seen that
h_{3}=ct_{-}/b_{-}>0
.
Next we proceed to deal with the equality
\xi_{{}_{1}}=Q_{{}_{B,0}}Q_{{}_{B,-1}}(0)\xi_{-1}
. Setting
h_{4}=1
, a direct calculation gives

[Q_{{}_{B,0}}Q_{{}_{B,-1}}(0)]^{-1}v_{2}(b_{+},\delta_{+})=(f_{1},f_{1},f_{2})^{T}=\xi_{-1}=h_{5}v_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})+h_{6}v_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-}).

Here
f_{2}>f_{1}>0
. Moreover, since we have
t_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})\neq t_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-})
, elementary linear algebra shows that there exist unique real parameters
h_{5},h_{6}
such that

h_{5}t_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})+h_{6}t_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-})=f_{1},\quad h_{5}+h_{6}=f_{2}.

Thus, there exist unique nonzero vectors
\{\chi_{{}_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{\xi_{{}_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, up to a constant. By
Theorem

9
, the Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}(0)
admits a two-fold eigenvalue at zero when
\delta_{+}>0,\delta_{-}<0
.

The other sign configuration is analogous, and hence we have proved the existence of nonzero vectors
\{\chi_{{}_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{\xi_{{}_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
when
\delta_{+}\delta_{-}<0
.

As for (ii), we argue by contradiction. Still by (
64
) and (
65
), it should hold that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\{\begin{aligned} \chi_{{}_{0}}&=h_{1}v_{1}+h_{2}v_{3},\;\chi_{{}_{-2}}=h_{3}v_{2},\\
\xi_{{}_{1}}&=h_{4}v_{2},\;\xi_{{}_{-1}}=h_{5}v_{1}+h_{6}v_{3},\\
\end{aligned}\right.\quad\delta_{+}>0,\delta_{-}>0,\\
\left\{\begin{aligned} \chi_{{}_{0}}&=h_{1}v_{1}+h_{2}v_{2},\;\chi_{{}_{-2}}=h_{3}v_{3},\\
\xi_{{}_{1}}&=h_{4}v_{3},\;\xi_{{}_{-1}}=h_{5}v_{1}+h_{6}v_{2},\end{aligned}\right.\quad\delta_{+}<0,\delta_{-}<0.\end{split}

(72)

for real constants
\{h_{j}\}_{j=1}^{6}
. It can be directly seen that (
70
) does not hold since the left-hand sides of the equations are complex, while the right-hand sides are real. This contradiction proves the case when
\delta_{+}<0
and
\delta_{-}<0
.

When
\delta_{+}>0
and
\delta_{-}>0
, one first observes that
h_{2}=h_{6}=0
. Supposing
h_{1}=h_{5}=1
, we have directly

\chi_{{}_{0}}=v_{1}(b_{+},\delta_{+}),\;\chi_{{}_{-2}}=h_{3}v_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-}),\;\xi_{{}_{1}}=h_{4}v_{2}(b_{+},\delta_{+}),\;\xi_{{}_{-1}}=v_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-}).

where

is given by (

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{split}\frac{t_{-}c}{b_{-}}t_{1}(b_{+},\delta_{+})+\frac{2t_{-}b_{+}}{t_{+}c}t_{1}(b_{+},\delta_{+})-\frac{t_{-}c}{b_{-}}=h_{3}t_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-}),\\

The left-hand sides of the above equations are negative, while the right-hand sides are positive. This contradiction proves the desired result.

∎

, the eigenvectors of

(73)

4.3

In this section, we study the local behavior of

When

To prove this theorem, we will first characterize the eigenfunctions
\mathbf{w}^{A},\mathbf{w}^{B}
.

Lemma 4
.

When
\delta_{+}\delta_{-}<0
, the eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}
,
\mathbf{w}^{A},\mathbf{w}^{B}
, that correspond to the zero eigenvalue can be properly chosen such that

\mathbf{w}^{A}=\{(0,0,0,\mathrm{i}x_{{}_{n}},0,-\mathrm{i}x_{{}_{n}})\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},\quad\mathbf{w}^{B}=\{(y_{{}_{n}},z_{{}_{n}},y_{{}_{n}},0,0,0)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}},

such that

Proof.

After multiplying by suitable complex constants, one can check that the eigenvectors take the form (
75
). It remains to prove that the coordinates
x_{n},y_{n}
satisfy the positivity constraints. For simplicity, we suppose
\delta_{+}>0,\delta_{-}<0
.
Therefore, from (
69
) and (
70
), one has

\displaystyle\begin{split}&Q_{{}_{A,-1}}(0)v_{3}(b_{+},\delta_{+})=\chi_{{}_{-1}},\;Q_{{}_{A,-2}}(0)\chi_{{}_{-1}}=h_{3}v_{3}(b_{-},\delta_{-}),\\
&[Q_{{}_{B,0}}(0)]^{-1}v_{2}(b_{+},\delta_{+})=\xi_{{}_{0}},\;[Q_{{}_{B,-1}}(0)]^{-1}\xi_{{}_{0}}=h_{5}v_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})+h_{6}v_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-}).\end{split}

For
\{\chi_{{}_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, one has by direct calculation

\chi_{{}_{-1}}=(c/b_{-},-c/b_{-},0)^{T},\quad\chi_{{}_{-2}}=(ct_{-}/b_{-},-ct_{-}/b_{-},0)^{T}.

By definition, one has

\displaystyle\chi_{{}_{n}}=[Q(b_{+},\delta_{+},0)]^{-n}\chi_{{}_{0}}=\mu_{3}^{-n}(b_{+},\delta_{+})\chi_{{}_{0}},

\displaystyle\chi_{{}_{-n}}=[Q(b_{-},\delta_{-},0)]^{n-2}\chi_{{}_{-2}}=\mu_{3}^{n-2}(b_{-},\delta_{-})\chi_{{}_{-2}}.

Therefore,
\{x_{{}_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are positive for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

As for
\{\xi_{{}_{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, still by calculation,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\xi_{{}_{0}}=(\frac{c^{2}}{2b_{+}b_{-}},\frac{c^{2}}{2b_{+}b_{-}},\frac{t_{2}(b_{+},\delta_{+})}{t_{+}}+\frac{c^{2}}{2b_{+}b_{-}})^{T},\\
&\xi_{{}_{-1}}=(f_{1},f_{1},f_{2})^{T}=h_{5}v_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})+h_{6}v_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-}),\end{split}

where

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{ct_{-}}{2b_{-}}\left(\frac{t_{2}(b_{+},\delta_{+})}{t_{+}}+\frac{c^{2}}{2b_{+}b_{-}}\right),

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=f_{1}+\frac{c}{2b_{-}}.

In particular,
f_{2}>f_{1}>0
. Therefore, one has

\displaystyle\begin{split}&w_{{}_{1,0}}=w_{{}_{3,0}}=-\frac{t_{2}(b_{+},\delta_{+})}{t_{+}}<0,\quad w_{{}_{2,0}}=\frac{t_{2}(b_{+},\delta_{+})}{t_{+}}+\frac{c^{2}}{2b_{+}b_{-}},\\
&w_{{}_{1,-1}}=w_{{}_{3,-1}}=-\frac{c}{2b_{-}}<0,\quad w_{{}_{2,-1}}=f_{2}.\end{split}

To investigate the quantity
w_{{}_{1,n}}
for
n\leq-2
, one first observes that

\xi_{{}_{n}}=h_{5}\mu_{1}^{n+1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})v_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})+h_{6}\mu_{2}^{n+1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})v_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-}),\quad n\leq-2.

Thus, one needs to give a precise description of the parameters
h_{5},h_{6}
, which satisfy the following relations

h_{5}+h_{6}=f_{2},\quad h_{5}t_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})+h_{6}t_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-})=f_{1}.

Directly, one has

\displaystyle h_{5}=\frac{f_{1}[t_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-})-1]+\frac{c}{2b_{-}}t_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-})}{t_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-})-t_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})},

\displaystyle h_{6}=\frac{f_{1}[1-t_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})]-\frac{c}{2b_{-}}t_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})}{t_{2}(b_{-},\delta_{-})-t_{1}(b_{-},\delta_{-})}.

Therefore,

w_{{}_{1,n}}=h_{5}\mu_{1}^{n+1}(t_{1}-1)+h_{6}\mu_{2}^{n+1}(t_{2}-1)=\frac{h_{5}\mu_{1}^{n+2}}{\mu_{2}}+\frac{h_{6}\mu_{2}^{n+2}}{\mu_{1}},\quad n\leq-2.

Here we have used

t_{1}-1=\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}},\quad t_{2}-1=\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}.

Combining the formulae of
h_{5}
and
h_{6}
, one has

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} w_{{}_{1,n}}&=\frac{f_{1}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}\left[\frac{(t_{2}-1)\mu_{1}^{n+2}}{\mu_{2}}-\frac{(t_{1}-1)\mu_{2}^{n+2}}{\mu_{1}}\right]\\
&\quad+\frac{c}{2b_{-}(t_{2}-t_{1})}\left[\frac{t_{2}\mu_{1}^{n+2}}{\mu_{2}}-\frac{t_{1}\mu_{2}^{n+2}}{\mu_{1}}\right].\end{aligned}

When
n
is even, tedious calculations show that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} w_{{}_{1,n}}&<\frac{c}{2b_{-}(t_{2}-t_{1})}\left[\frac{t_{2}\mu_{1}^{n+2}}{\mu_{2}}-\frac{t_{1}\mu_{2}^{n+2}}{\mu_{1}}\right]\\
&=\frac{c}{2b_{-}(t_{2}-t_{1})}\left[-\frac{t_{-}\mu_{1}^{n+1}}{\mu_{2}}+\frac{t_{-}\mu_{2}^{n+1}}{\mu_{1}}\right]\leq 0.\end{aligned}

The equality comes from
Lemma

3
.

When
n
is odd, one similarly has, for
n\leq-3
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} w_{{}_{1,n}}&<\frac{f_{1}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}\left[\frac{(t_{2}-1)\mu_{1}^{n+2}}{\mu_{2}}-\frac{(t_{1}-1)\mu_{2}^{n+2}}{\mu_{1}}\right]\\
&=\frac{f_{1}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}\left[\mu_{1}^{n+1}-\mu_{2}^{n+1}\right]<0.\end{aligned}

Therefore, we have proved that
w_{{}_{1,n}}<0
for all

n\in\mathbb{Z}
, given that

\xi_{{}_{1}}=v_{2}(b_{+},\delta_{+})
.
∎

Now we can give a full proof of
Theorem

11
. We suppose
\mathbf{w}^{A}
and
\mathbf{w}^{B}
are normalized.

Proof of
Theorem

11
.

A Taylor expansion gives

\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}(k)=\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}(0)+k\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}+\mathcal{O}(k^{2}).

The first-order part
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}:l_{k}^{2}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6})\to l^{2}_{k}(\mathbb{Z};\mathbb{C}^{6})
is given by

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{split}&(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{II}}\mathbf{w})_{{}_{1,n}}=\mathrm{i}c_{{}_{n,\mathrm{II}}}w_{{}_{6,n+1}},\\
&(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{II}}\mathbf{w})_{{}_{2,n}}=-\mathrm{i}d_{{}_{n-2,\mathrm{II}}}w_{{}_{5,n-2}},\\
&(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{II}}\mathbf{w})_{{}_{3,n}}=0,\end{split}\right.\quad\left\{\begin{split}&(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{II}}\mathbf{w})_{{}_{4,n}}=0,\\
&(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{II}}\mathbf{w})_{{}_{5,n}}=\mathrm{i}d_{{}_{n,\mathrm{II}}}w_{{}_{2,n+2}},\\
&(\widehat{H}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{II}}\mathbf{w})_{{}_{6,n}}=-\mathrm{i}c_{{}_{n-1,\mathrm{II}}}w_{{}_{1,n-1}}.\end{split}\right.

(76)

Following the aforementioned procedure, we calculate the quantities
(\mathbf{w}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{B})
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\mathbf{w}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{A})&=(\mathbf{w}^{B},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{B})=0,\\
(\mathbf{w}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{B})&=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{i}c_{{}_{n-1,\mathrm{II}}}y_{{}_{n-1}}x_{{}_{n}},\\
(\mathbf{w}^{B},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{A})&=-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{i}c_{{}_{n,\mathrm{II}}}y_{{}_{n}}x_{{}_{n+1}}=-(\mathbf{w}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{B}).\end{split}

From
Lemma

4
, the eigenvectors can be properly chosen such that
x_{{}_{n}}>0,y_{{}_{n}}<0
. Therefore, it is obvious that

(\mathbf{w}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{B})\neq 0.

And the matrix

\mathbf{M}_{0,\mathrm{II}}\triangleq\begin{pmatrix}(\mathbf{w}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{A})&(\mathbf{w}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{B})\\
(\mathbf{w}^{B},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{A})&(\mathbf{w}^{B},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{B})\end{pmatrix},

(77)

is nontrivial, with two distinct real eigenvalues

\pm\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{w}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{B})
, which means that the crossing at
k=0
is also non-tangential. And we also have

E_{\mathrm{II},\pm}(k)=\pm|(\mathbf{w}^{A},\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}\mathbf{w}^{B})|\,|k|+\mathcal{O}(k^{2})\triangleq\pm E_{\mathrm{II}}^{(1)}|k|+\mathcal{O}(k^{2}).

∎

Remark 5
.

The above results only concern the local behavior near
k=0
. In the next section, we numerically show that the energy curves
E_{\mathrm{I}}(k),E_{\mathrm{II}}(k)
do not vanish for
k\neq 0
. Therefore, there exist only two counter-propagating zero-energy edge states concentrated near
E_{\mathrm{I}}(0)=E_{\mathrm{II}}(0)=0
.

6
Bulk Properties of the Operator

In this section, we suppose that the hopping coefficients
\{a_{{}_{\lambda}},b_{{}_{\lambda}},c_{{}_{\lambda}},d_{{}_{\lambda}}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
are given by

\displaystyle b_{{}_{\lambda}}=b,\quad a_{{}_{\lambda}}=c_{{}_{\lambda}}=d_{{}_{\lambda}}=b+\varepsilon.

The assumptions (
5
) ensure that
b>0
and
\varepsilon>-b
. We denote the operator by
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}=\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(b,\varepsilon)
.

Remark 6
.

From a physical point of view, if the intercell interaction is larger than the intracell interaction, then the corresponding hopping coefficient
b+\varepsilon
is larger than
b
, and vice versa. To realize it practically, one can dilate or contract the inclusions in super-honeycomb structures; see, for example,
Miao and Zhu (2024)
.

Since the operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}
is periodic in
\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}
and
\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}
, one can decompose the Hilbert space
l^{2}(\Lambda;\mathbb{C}^{6})
into a direct integral over the space
\mathbb{C}^{6}_{\mathbf{k}}
, with quasi-momentum
\mathbf{k}\in\{s\mathring{\mathbf{k}}_{\alpha}+t\mathring{\mathbf{k}}_{\beta}:s,t\in[0,1)\}\triangleq Y^{\ast}
, where
\mathring{\mathbf{k}}_{\alpha},\mathring{\mathbf{k}}_{\beta}
are dual basis vectors defined by

\mathring{\mathbf{k}}_{\alpha}\cdot\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}=\mathring{\mathbf{k}}_{\beta}\cdot\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}=2\pi,\quad\mathring{\mathbf{k}}_{\alpha}\cdot\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}=\mathring{\mathbf{k}}_{\beta}\cdot\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}=0.

The Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}
can also be decomposed into a direct integral of operators
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\mathbf{k})
. For
\nu\in\mathbb{C}^{6}_{\mathbf{k}}
, the Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\mathbf{k})\nu)_{{}_{1}}&\triangleq-b\nu_{{}_{4}}-b\nu_{{}_{5}}-(b+\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}}\nu_{{}_{6}},\\
(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\mathbf{k})\nu)_{{}_{2}}&\triangleq-b\nu_{{}_{4}}-(b+\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}-\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta})}\nu_{{}_{5}}-b\nu_{{}_{6}},\\
(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\mathbf{k})\nu)_{{}_{3}}&\triangleq-(b+\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}}\nu_{{}_{4}}-b\nu_{{}_{5}}-b\nu_{{}_{6}},\\
(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\mathbf{k})\nu)_{{}_{4}}&\triangleq-b\nu_{{}_{1}}-b\nu_{{}_{2}}-(b+\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta}}\nu_{{}_{3}},\\
(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\mathbf{k})\nu)_{{}_{5}}&\triangleq-b\nu_{{}_{1}}-(b+\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}-\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\beta})}\nu_{{}_{2}}-b\nu_{{}_{3}},\\
(\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\mathbf{k})\nu)_{{}_{6}}&\triangleq-(b+\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathring{\mathbf{v}}_{\alpha}}\nu_{{}_{1}}-b\nu_{{}_{2}}-b\nu_{{}_{3}}.\end{split}

(79)

For
\mathbf{k}\in Y^{\ast}
, one can verify the following fact about the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\mathbf{k})
.

Proposition 12
.

For
\mathbf{k}\in Y^{\ast}
, the Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(\mathbf{k})
has six real eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{k})\}_{j=1}^{6}
in ascending order. Moreover, the eigenvalues satisfy

\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{k})\leq-|\varepsilon|,\quad\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{k})\geq|\varepsilon|,\quad j=1,2,3,\;l=4,5,6.

Specifically, when
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}
, one has

\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{0})=-(3b+\varepsilon),\;\lambda_{2}(0)=\lambda_{3}(\mathbf{0})=-|\varepsilon|\;,\lambda_{4}(\mathbf{0})=\lambda_{5}(\mathbf{0})=|\varepsilon|\;,\lambda_{6}(\mathbf{0})=3b+\varepsilon.

For
\varepsilon=0
, it can be proved easily that there exists a double Dirac point at
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}
.

Theorem 13
.

For
\varepsilon=0
, there exists a double Dirac point at
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}
. More specifically, there exists a positive constant
\lambda^{\sharp}
such that the following relations hold

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{k})=-\lambda^{\sharp}|\mathbf{k}|+\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{k}|^{2}),\quad j=2,3,\\
&\lambda_{l}(\mathbf{k})=\lambda^{\sharp}|\mathbf{k}|+\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{k}|^{2}),\quad l=4,5.\end{split}

(80)

We plot the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{k})\}_{j=1}^{6}

and

\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}
when

. For
\varepsilon\neq 0
, a band gap opens at
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}
.

Proposition 14
.

For
\mathbf{k}=0
, the Hamiltonian operator
\widehat{H}_{\mathrm{bulk}}(0)
has six eigenvectors
\{\nu_{{}_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{6}
that correspond to the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{0})\}_{j=1}^{6}
. Moreover, when

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nu_{{}_{2}}=(1,0,-1,1,0,-1)^{T},&\quad\nu_{{}_{3}}=(1,-2,1,-1,2,-1)^{T},\\
