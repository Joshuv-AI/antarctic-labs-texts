Cerebral fat embolism syndrome in sickle cell disease: a case highlighting the predominance of susceptibility-weighted imaging findings and literature review
Authors: Giovanni Failla, Francesco Tiralongo, Placido Di Grigoli, Daniele Falsaperla, Corrado Inì, Daniele Grippaldi, Renato Farina, Giuseppe Messina, Emanuele David, Pietro Valerio Foti, Stefano Palmucci, Antonio Basile
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral fat embolism syndrome (CFES) is a rare but severe complication, most commonly associated with long bone fractures. Its occurrence in patients with sickle cell disease (SCD) is exceptional and may be triggered by vaso-occlusive crises. Its clinical manifestations are variable and nonspecific, frequently overlapping with stroke, sepsis, and other SCD-related complications, making diagnosis particularly challenging. We report the case of a 56-year-old woman with SCD admitted for vaso-occlusive crisis who developed rapid neurological deterioration with encephalopathy and tetraplegia. Brain MRI revealed both small punctate DWI hyperintensities and innumerable petechial hemorrhages on SWI, highly suggestive of CFES. Additional imaging demonstrated vertebral osteonecrosis, bone marrow infarction, and extensive iliac and femoral venous thrombosis, pointing to widespread vaso-occlusion as the embolic source. The patient was treated with exchange transfusion, anticoagulation, targeted antibiotic therapy, and rehabilitation, resulting in gradual clinical stabilization. This case highlights the diagnostic value of susceptibility imaging, as SWI-detected microhemorrhages predominated over the classic “starfield” pattern on DWI and may serve as a more consistent marker across disease stages. To our knowledge, this report represents one of the few cases of CFES in patients with SCD in which SWI findings clearly predominated over DWI abnormalities and were documented longitudinally. A normal brain CT does not exclude CFES, and in patients with SCD presenting with acute neurological deterioration, early MRI, particularly including SWI, is essential. Raising awareness of this imaging profile is crucial for the timely recognition and effective management of CFES in SCD, with potential implications for patient outcomes.Keywords: Cerebral fat embolism syndrome, Sickle cell disease, Microbleeds, MRI, Vaso-occlusive crisis

Introduction

Fat embolism syndrome (FES) is a rare but severe complication that typically occurs when fat emboli enter the systemic circulation after long-bone fractures [1]. FES is classically characterized by respiratory compromise, petechial rash, and neurological symptoms, with the latter usually occurring 12-72 hours after the initial insult [2], even if the presentation can be highly variable, and in some cases, these hallmark features are absent, making the diagnosis particularly difficult [3]. The term cerebral fat embolism syndrome (CFES) refers to the neurological manifestations that result from embolization in the cerebral vessels. CFES has been reported not only in traumatic settings but also, at lower incidence, in nontraumatic conditions, including corticosteroid therapy, pancreatitis, and sickle cell disease (SCD) [4]. SCD is an autosomal recessive genetic disorder characterized by the presence of sickle hemoglobin, which causes red blood cells to assume a sickle-like shape, giving the condition its name [5,6]. It includes several genotypes, most commonly HbSS, HbSC, and HbS/β-thalassemia. HbSS, resulting from inheriting 1 sickle cell allele from each parent, is the most common and typically the most severe form, in contrast, HbSC and HbS/β-thalassemia usually manifest as milder variants [6]. When deoxygenated, sickle hemoglobin becomes insoluble and polymerizes into rigid fibers, causing the red blood cells to adopt a sickle-shaped form. These rigid cells obstruct the microvasculature, restricting blood flow and oxygen delivery, resulting in ischemia and cell damage that can progress to tissue necrosis [7]. Patients with SCD often present with painful vaso-occlusive episodes, persistent anemia due to ongoing hemolysis, acute aplastic events, heightened vulnerability to infections, and recurrent episodes of splenic pooling [8]. The underlying mechanism of CFES is thought to involve ischemic injury of the bone marrow, resulting in the release of lipid globules and hematopoietic elements into the venous system, which subsequently embolize to various organs, including the brain [9]. An intracardiac or intrapulmonary shunt may facilitate this passage of emboli from the right to the left circulation (Fig. 1); however, cerebral fat embolism may also occur in the absence of any demonstrable shunt [10].

Schematic illustration of the pathophysiology of cerebral fat embolism in sickle cell disease. Bone marrow necrosis releases fat globules into venous circulation; an intracardiac or intrapulmonary shunt may allow these emboli to bypass the pulmonary capillary bed and reach the cerebral circulation, resulting in cerebral embolism.

Clinically, CFES presents with a constellation of neurological deterioration, hypoxemia, seizures, and altered mental status, often accompanied by systemic features such as fever, anemia, thrombocytopenia, and multiorgan dysfunction [11].

Neuroimaging plays a crucial role in the recognition of CFES. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particularly diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), typically demonstrates multiple punctate hyperintensities distributed throughout the white matter and deep gray nuclei, forming the so-called “starfield pattern” that has been described as characteristic of cerebral fat embolism [12]. Susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) may reveal numerous cerebral microbleeding, which have been correlated with disease severity and neurological outcome [13].

CFES in SCD remains underrecognized and is associated with poor prognosis, marked by high morbidity and mortality [14].

Case presentation

A 56-year-old female had previously presented to multiple hospitals for recurrent chest pain and arthralgia, where initial diagnostic suspicions included acute coronary syndrome and myopericarditis. Despite these evaluations, her symptoms persisted, and following further clinical reassessment and hematologic consultation, based on the patient's medical history and previous diagnosis of SCD, a sickle-cell crisis was suspected. She was then admitted to our Emergency Department, where laboratory testing confirmed HbS positivity (44.5%) and hemoglobin of 6.5 g/dL. The patient underwent therapeutic erythroexchange and transfusion of 1 unit of packed red blood cells, resulting in a reduction of HbS levels to 10.4%. Given the elevation of inflammatory markers, empirical antibiotic therapy was also started, and she was admitted to our department of emergency medicine with a working diagnosis of sickle-cell crisis in sickle-cell anemia.

On the day of admission, due to dyspnea, elevated D-dimer (59.210 ng/mL) and dorsal pain, a contrast-enhanced chest CT was performed, which excluded pulmonary embolism. As an incidental finding, the examination revealed severe changes involving several cervical and thoracic vertebrae (Fig. 2). Given these findings, a spinal MRI with contrast was performed for further diagnostic investigation, confirming vertebral osteonecrosis, bone marrow activation, and intravertebral spongy degeneration (Fig. 3).

On the second hospital day, the patient developed progressive cognitive decline, so a neurological consultation was promptly requested. Neurological examination documented severe flaccid tetraplegia with diffuse areflexia and impaired consciousness; the patient appeared drowsy, but arousable with vigorous tactile and pain stimulation; her speech was poor. Her Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 7/15 (E2, V2, M3). Based on this clinical deterioration, a brain computed tomography (CT) was performed which showed no acute hemorrhage or early signs of ischemia. Given the persistent symptoms, on the same day, a brain MRI was performed which demonstrated several small, punctate foci of altered signal intensity on DWI with restriction on ADC maps, distributed in a characteristic “starfield pattern” and countless point foci of hypointensity on T2* sequences, as from diffuse microhemorrhages; highly suggestive of cerebral fat embolism in the context of sickle-cell crisis (Fig. 4). Electroencephalography (EEG), performed the same day, confirmed findings consistent with encephalopathy. Given the acute neurological deterioration, several differential diagnoses were initially considered, including ischemic stroke, infectious meningoencephalitis, pulmonary thromboembolism, and diffuse axonal injury. However, the absence of territorial diffusion restriction, lack of meningeal enhancement, negative CT pulmonary angiography, and no history of trauma made these alternatives unlikely. In conjunction with the characteristic MRI findings and the clinical context of sickle cell disease, these features strongly supported the diagnosis of cerebral fat embolism syndrome. To further investigate the apparent discrepancy between the negative chest CT for pulmonary embolism and the positive brain MRI indicating fat embolism, a contrast echocardiography with microbubbles was conducted to assess for the presence of a patent foramen ovale (PFO). The examination ruled out any intracardiac shunts.

(A) Axial Diffusion-weighted image (DWI) shows some punctate hyperintensities with a “starfield” appearance, predominantly involving the white matter and deep gray nuclei (white arrows). (B) Corresponding axial ADC map confirms restricted diffusion in these areas (white arrows) and (C, D) Axial T2*-weighted images reveal numerous punctiform hypointensities in the subcortical white matter and cerebellum, consistent with cerebral microhemorrhages.

On the third hospital day, a CT of the lumbar spine and pelvis was obtained to further evaluate vertebral lesions. This examination demonstrated diffuse vascular compromise involving the lumbar vertebrae, sacrum, and pelvic bones, with extensive thrombosis of the right external iliac vein extending to the common iliac and femoral veins noted as an incidental finding (Fig. 5). Anticoagulation with fondaparinux (7.5 mg s.c.) was subsequently initiated. A rehabilitation protocol was initially involving passive mobilization of all 4 limbs, followed by active-assisted exercises and progressive reconditioning to a sitting position over the following weeks. A follow-up brain MRI, performed 10 days after the first, showed the disappearance of the previously observed DWI-restricted lesions, along with extensive confluent hyperintense areas on long-TR sequences involving the corona radiata, centrum semiovale, and splenium of the corpus callosum, consistent with the evolutionary stage of diffuse cerebral fat microembolism. Additionally, there was a marked increase in the number of millimetric foci of signal drop on SWI sequences with ubiquitous distribution, consistent with diffuse petechial hemorrhages (Fig. 6).

During hospitalization, the patient developed febrile episodes, which were managed with targeted antibiotic therapy as recommended by the Infectious Disease consultation. She also experienced pancytopenia, managed with transfusion support and G-CSF. A peripheral blood smear excluded immature precursors, and the cytopenia was attributed to an ongoing infection.

At the time of discharge, the patient had completed targeted antibiotic therapy for febrile episodes that had occurred during hospitalization, with clinical and laboratory improvement, including normalization of inflammatory markers, clearance of blood cultures, and hematologic stabilization. HbS levels at the last evaluation were 26.9% and hemoglobin had increased to 9 g/dL. Neurologically, she showed a slight improvement with GCS rising to 10/15 (E3, V3, M4) compared to admission. Given the diagnosis of SCD with severe complications, referral to a specialized Thalassemia Center was arranged for long-term management and follow-up care in a rehabilitation facility. Hemodynamic parameters remained stable throughout hospitalization without need for inotropic support.

Discussion

CFES represents a rare but devastating complication of SCD, often underrecognized in clinical practice and associated with significant morbidity and mortality [1]. Diagnostic and therapeutic management is challenging, given the nonspecific clinical presentation, heterogeneous imaging findings, and overlap with other acute complications of SCD such as acute chest syndrome, ischemic stroke, and sepsis [14]. The diagnosis of fat embolism syndrome remains primarily clinical, as no universally accepted diagnostic standard exists. Among the most widely used clinical tools are Gurd and Wilson's criteria, based on a combination of major features (including respiratory insufficiency, neurological impairment, and petechial rash) and minor findings, and the Schonfeld score, a point-based system in which a score greater than 5 strongly supports the diagnosis. However, these criteria have not been formally validated and may be difficult to apply in incomplete or atypical presentations, particularly in cases of isolated cerebral fat embolism [22,24].

The pathophysiology of CFES has not yet been fully elucidated; however, 2 theories have been proposed to explain how fat emboli can enter the systemic circulation. A mechanical theory suggests that fat microemboli enter the venous sinusoids and subsequently deposit in the pulmonary microcirculation. In some cases, they can reach the systemic circulation by crossing the pulmonary capillary bed or through right-to-left cardiac shunt [15]. A chemical theory, however, more closely related to fat embolism resulting from trauma or fracture, proposes that trauma-induced release of plasma mediators triggers lipolysis, producing free fatty acids that promote inflammation, vascular obstruction, and tissue damage. Platelet aggregation and endothelial injury contribute to clot formation, while these combined mechanisms help explain the delayed onset of symptoms in CFES [16]. The clinical deterioration of our patient in the context of a vaso-occlusive bone crisis, combined with the radiological documentation of diffuse bone marrow infarction, likely supports the mechanical theory as the primary pathophysiological mechanism in this setting. Importantly, no intracardiac shunt was detected, consistent with prior reports showing that cerebral fat emboli can traverse the pulmonary capillary network even in the absence of demonstrable anatomical defects [10]. This reinforces the need to consider CFES even when conventional predisposing features are absent. Clinically, the initial presentation with chest pain, dyspnea, and elevated D-dimer mimicked pulmonary embolism, which was excluded on CT angiography. The subsequent neurological decline, manifesting as encephalopathy and tetraplegia, raised the differential diagnosis of ischemic stroke or infectious meningoencephalitis, highlighting the diagnostic uncertainty that often surrounds CFES.

MRI imaging is the most sensitive technique for detecting brain alterations associated with fat embolism syndrome [17]. In patients with SCD, several reports have described CFES, with most emphasizing the DWI “starfield” pattern as the principal finding [2,18]. Parizel et al. [12] and Butteriss et al. [18] emphasized multifocal punctate DWI hyperintensities in white and deep gray matter as the pathognomonic signature of CFES. More recent studies, however, demonstrate that SWI is at least as informative, and in many cases more sensitive. In a major systematic review involving 140 patients, Giyab et al. [19] defined the characteristic "walnut kernel" microhemorrhage pattern of CFE as uniformly sized, punctate hypointensities diffusely distributed throughout the subcortical white matter, internal capsule, and corpus callosum, largely sparing the corona radiata and deep centrum semiovale. This distribution, seen on T2* sequence or SWI, was present in 98% of cases, compared with 68% for the starfield pattern. Shadmani et al. [20] also in a recent series of studies and a systematic review of 33 cases of nontrauma-associated CFES in sickle cell-related hemoglobinopathies reported a prevalence of punctate microhemorrhages (84.7%) and a "starfield" pattern in DWI (65.4%) in the acute phase, even when the initial cranial CT was normal. It was also highlighted that brain MRI findings follow a dynamic pattern with scattered foci of restricted diffusion (a starfield pattern), which presents for a short period during the acute phase. Punctate foci of microhemorrhage appear slightly later during the acute phase, peak during the subacute phase, and persist for up to 6 months. Recognition of this distinct pattern is highly relevant, as cerebral microbleeds in different distributions may reflect a variety of other conditions, including diffuse axonal injury (DAI), sepsis, or even cardiovascular disease. Unlike the walnut kernel pattern, microbleeds associated with DAI typically occur in clusters of heterogeneous size and morphology, ranging from linear and curvilinear to ovoid, and are localized to characteristic regions [21]. Microbleed distribution can help distinguish CFE from other conditions. In hypertension, microbleeds are usually confined to deep brain regions, while in cerebral amyloid angiopathy they follow a lobar, more diffuse, and heterogeneous pattern [22]. Acute hemorrhagic leukoencephalopathies are generally associated with widespread T2 white matter lesions and randomly distributed microbleeds, [23] whereas patients receiving extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) or those with sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, or COVID-19 may exhibit diffuse microhemorrhages of variable size and morphology that can mimic, but not replicate, the distribution of CFE [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Awareness of these differential patterns and of relevant clinical history, such as ECMO therapy or a history of trauma in the DAI, is essential for accurate radiological interpretation [28].

In our patient, innumerable petechial hemorrhages on SWI predominated over the classic starfield appearance on DWI. This imaging pattern corresponds to the so-called type 2C pattern described by Kuo et al. [29], characterized by diffuse, punctate microhemorrhages involving the subcortical white matter and deep gray nuclei. Notably, this characteristic hemorrhagic pattern may be observed throughout the entire disease course, from the acute to the chronic phase, and may therefore represent a more reliable and persistent imaging marker than diffusion abnormalities, which are often transient. The predominance of SWI microbleeds in our case thus not only reinforces the diagnosis of CFES but also highlights the essential role of susceptibility imaging in the neuroradiological workup. This reinforces the concept that relying solely on DWI may miss or underestimate the extent of brain involvement. Furthermore, the combination of a spinal MRI documenting a bone marrow infarction and a pelvic CT scan demonstrating extensive venous thrombosis provides multimodal evidence of a systemic vaso-occlusion as the source of embolism, a level of integration rarely achieved in previous studies.

Management of CFES in SCD remains largely supportive, as no standardized therapeutic protocol exists. Exchange transfusion is generally considered the mainstay of treatment in severe vaso-occlusive crises, aiming to reduce HbS levels, improve oxygen delivery, and limit further sickling episodes [30,31]. Early initiation of exchange transfusion has been shown to interrupt the sickling cycle, prevent progression of bone marrow necrosis and fat embolism, and thereby improve overall prognosis [32]. Supportive measures, including oxygen supplementation, mechanical ventilation when required, and meticulous fluid and electrolyte management, are equally essential [14]. Although corticosteroids have been investigated in trauma-related fat embolism, their role in SCD remains uncertain and is not routinely recommended [25]. In our case, additional anticoagulation was required to address extensive iliac and femoral venous thrombosis, underscoring the need for individualized treatment strategies in patients with multifactorial complications [33]. Early rehabilitation and infection control were also key components of the clinical course, contributing to gradual stabilization and improved functional outcomes.

Conclusion

CFES is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication, often marked by dramatic neurological and systemic manifestations. While early recognition and supportive treatment can improve outcomes, its diagnosis remains challenging. In this context, the predominance of SWI microhemorrhages over the classic starfield pattern underlines the pivotal role of susceptibility imaging, which may provide a more reliable marker across different stages of the disease. Raising awareness of these features among clinicians and radiologists is essential for timely diagnosis, accurate differential consideration, and optimized patient care.
