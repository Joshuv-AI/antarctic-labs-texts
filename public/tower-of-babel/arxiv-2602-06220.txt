Minimum ℱ \mathscr{F} -covers: the supersolvable and metabelian cases
Mihai-Silviu Lazorec

Abstract
Given a set ℱ \mathscr{F} of finite groups, it is said that a group G G is an ℱ \mathscr{F} -cover if every group in ℱ \mathscr{F} is isomorphic to a subgroup of G G . Moreover, G G is a minimum ℱ \mathscr{F} -cover if there is no ℱ \mathscr{F} -cover whose order is less than | G | |G| . In [Cameron P. J., et al., Minimal cover groups, J. Algebra 660 (2024)], the authors pose the following question: For which classes 𝒳 \mathscr{X} of groups, closed under taking subgroups and direct products, is it true that, if ℱ \mathscr{F} is a set of 𝒳 \mathscr{X} -groups, then there is a minimum ℱ \mathscr{F} -cover which is an 𝒳 \mathscr{X} -group? In this paper, we give a negative answer in two cases: 𝒳 ∈ { ` ​ ` ​ s ​ u ​ p ​ e ​ r ​ s ​ o ​ l ​ v ​ a ​ b ​ l ​ e ​ " , ` ​ ` ​ m ​ e ​ t ​ a ​ b ​ e ​ l ​ i ​ a ​ n ​ " } . \mathscr{X}\in\{``supersolvable",``metabelian"\}.

1
Introduction

All groups considered in this paper are finite.
\mathscr{F}
always denotes a finite set of groups. The order of an element
x
of a group, the cyclic group of order
n
(
n\geq 2
), the dihedral group of order
2n
(
n\geq 3
) and the Schur multiplier of a group
G
are denoted by
o(x)
,
C_{n}
,
D_{2n}
and
M(G)
, respectively.

We say that a group
G
is an
\mathscr{F}
-cover if every group in
\mathscr{F}
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
G
. Inspired by Cayley’s theorem stating that any group of order
n
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
S_{n}
, the
\mathscr{F}
-cover concept is introduced and studied by Cameron, Craven, Dorbidi, Harper and Sambale in
[
1
]
. Some of the problems that are approached there and the corresponding results mainly refer to two types of
\mathscr{F}
-covers: minimal and minimum. Assuming that
G
is an
\mathscr{F}
-cover, it is said that this cover is:

–

minimal if no proper subgroup of
G
is an
\mathscr{F}
-cover;

–

minimum if there is no
\mathscr{F}
-cover whose order is less than
|G|
.

It is clear that any minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover is also minimal.

One of the main results in
[
1
]
is stated as follows:

Theorem 1.

Let
\mathscr{F}=\{C_{q},C_{r}\}
, where
q,r
are prime numbers such that
q<r
. Then
\mathscr{F}
has finitely many minimal covers if and only if
q=2
and
r
is a Fermat prime. More exactly, these covers are:
C_{2r},D_{2r}
and
C_{2}^{2a}\rtimes C_{r}
, where
r=2^{a}+1
.

Hence, if
\mathscr{F}
is formed of two cyclic groups of prime order, then Theorem 1 provides an answer to the following question:
For which sets
\mathscr{F}
are there only finitely many minimal
\mathscr{F}
-covers up to isomorphim?
(see Question A in
[
1
]
).

A further research direction that is outlined in
[
1
]
is Question 8.2:
For which classes
\mathscr{X}
of groups, closed under taking subgroups and direct products, is it true that, if
\mathscr{F}
is a set of
\mathscr{X}
-groups, then there is a minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover which is an
\mathscr{X}
-group?
Regarding this direction, the authors prove the following result (see Theorem 7.3 in
[
1
]
) that provide an affirmative answer to Question 8.2 when
\mathscr{X}=``nilpotent"
.

Theorem 3.

Let
\mathscr{F}
be formed of nilpotent groups. Then there is a minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover which is nilpotent.

A way to construct a minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover in the nilpotent case is shared in the proof of Theorem 7.3 of
[
1
]
. Example 7.8 of the same paper shows that if
\mathscr{F}=\{D_{10},A_{4}\}
, then
A_{5}
is the unique minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover. This shows that the answer to Question 8.2 is negative if
\mathscr{X}=``solvable"
. If
\mathscr{X}=``abelian"
, Question 8.2 can be reduced to the case of abelian
p
-groups. At the moment of writing this paper, it is unknown if starting with
\mathscr{F}
formed of abelian
p
-groups, then there is a minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover which is an abelian
p
-group. Still, if this is true, a way to construct this cover is outlined in the paragraph that precedes Proposition 7.4 of
[
1
]
.

Even though by taking
\mathscr{X}=``cyclic"
, we get a class of groups that is closed only under taking subgroups, we think that it is worth mentioning that if
\mathscr{F}
contains only cyclic subgroups, we can always find a minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover which is cyclic. Theorem 7.1 of
[
1
]
outlines this cover. This result is stated below.

Theorem 3.

Let
\mathscr{F}=\{C_{n_{1}},C_{n_{2}},\ldots,C_{n_{k}}\}
and
N=lcm(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{k})
. Then
C_{N}
is a minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover.

In this paper, we provide a negative answer to Question 8.2 in two more specific cases:

\mathscr{X}\in\{``supersolvable",``metabelian"\}.

We outline infinitely many sets
\mathscr{F}
formed of
\mathscr{X}
-groups such that they have a unique minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover which is not an
\mathscr{X}
-group. More exactly, we prove the following two results:

Theorem 4.

Let
p\geq 5
be a prime number and let
\mathscr{F}_{p}=\{C_{3}\rtimes S_{3},C_{4p}\}
be a set of supersolvable groups. Then the unique minimum
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-cover is the non-supersolvable group
C_{p}\times(C_{3}^{2}\rtimes C_{4})
.

Theorem 5.

Let
p
be an odd prime such that
p\neq 5
and let
\mathscr{F}_{p}=\{D_{10},C_{p}\times A_{4}\}
be a set of metabelian groups. Then the unique minimum
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-cover is the non-metabelian group
C_{p}\times A_{5}
.

Regarding the statement of Theorem 4, we note that
C_{3}\rtimes S_{3}
is SmallGroup(18,4) and
C_{3}^{2}\rtimes C_{4}
is SmallGroup(36, 9) in GAP’s
[
4
]
small groups library. The proofs of the two results above are given in the following section.

We end the introduction by pointing out that if “minimum” is replaced with “minimal” in Question 8.2, its corresponding answer is always affirmative by Proposition 2.6 of
[
1
]
.

2
Proofs of main results

As preliminary results, we recall Proposition 2.1 (
a
) of
[
1
]
and Theorem 2.1 (
a
) of
[
3
]
. The first one is a consequence of Lagrange’s theorem and it gives us a clue on the order of a minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover. The second one provides information on the structure of a group having a cyclic normal subgroup with specific properties.

Lemma 2.1.

Let
G
be an
\mathscr{F}
-cover. Then lcm
\{|F|\mid F\in\mathscr{F}\}
divides
|G|
.

Lemma 2.2.

Let
G
be a group,
H
be a cyclic normal subgroup of
G
and
K
be a simple non-abelian group. If
\frac{G}{H}\cong K
and gcd
(|H|,|M(K)|)=1
, then
G\cong H\times K
.

Proof of Theorem 4.
Let
G
be a minimum
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-cover. By Lemma 2.1, we have that
36p\mid|G|
, so we may assume that
|G|=36p
.

If
p\in\{5,7,11,13,17\}
, then one can use GAP to check that
G\cong C_{p}\times(C_{3}^{2}\rtimes C_{4})
is the unique minimum
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-cover.

In what follows, we assume that
p\geq 19
. By using Sylow’s theorems, it is easy to check that
G
has a unique Sylow
p
-subgroup
H\cong C_{p}
. Hence,
H
is normal in
G
and since its order is coprime with the order of the quotient
K=\frac{G}{H}
, it follows that

G\cong H\rtimes K

by the Schur-Zassenhaus theorem. Let
\pi:G\longrightarrow K
be the surjective projective homomorphism. Since
G
is an
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-cover, we know that
G
has subgroups isomorphic to
F_{1}=C_{3}\rtimes S_{3}
(SmallGroup(18, 4)) and
F_{2}=C_{4p}
, respectively. Consequently,
G
has a subgroup
L_{1}\cong S_{3}
and a subgroup
L_{2}\cong C_{4}
. Let
\pi_{/L_{1}}:L_{1}\longrightarrow K
be the restriction of
\pi
to
L_{1}
. We easily obtain that its kernel is

ker(\pi_{/L_{1}})=H\cap L_{1}=\{1\}.

Hence,
\pi_{/L_{1}}
is actually a group isomorphism from
L_{1}
to
\pi_{/L_{1}}(L_{1})
, so
K
has a subgroup isomorphic to
L_{1}\cong S_{3}
. By following a similar reasoning, one deduces that
K
also has a subgroup isomorphic to
L_{2}\cong C_{4}
. By inspecting the list of groups of order 36, the only possible choice is
K\cong C_{3}^{2}\rtimes C_{4}
(SmallGroup(36, 9)). Hence,

G\cong H\rtimes K\cong C_{p}\rtimes(C_{3}^{2}\rtimes C_{4}).

Let
f:K\longrightarrow Aut(H)
be the group homomorphism that induces the above semidirect product. We are going to show that
f
is trivial. It is known that
ker(f)=C_{K}(H)
(see, for instance, Exercise 1, Section 5.5 of
[
2
]
). Without loss of generality, we may assume that
F_{2}=L_{2}\times H
. Then
L_{2}\subseteq C_{K}(H)
. Let
N\cong C_{3}^{2}
be the Sylow 3-subgroup of
K
. Since
C_{K}(H)
and
N
are normal subgroups of
K
, we have
M=C_{K}(H)\cap N\triangleleft K
. The group
K
does not have normal subgroups of order 3, so we deduce that
M=\{1\}
or
M=N
. In the first case,
C_{K}(H)N
would be a subgroup of order
9|C_{K}(H)|
of
K
. By taking into account that
L_{2}\subseteq C_{K}(H)
, it follows that
C_{K}(H)=L_{2}\cong C_{4}
. It follows that
K\cong C_{3}^{2}\times C_{4}
, a contradiction. Hence,
M=N
and this implies that
N\subseteq C_{K}(H)
. Since
N\cap L_{2}=\{1\}
and
NL_{2}\subseteq C_{K}(H)
, we get that
C_{K}(H)
is a subgroup of
K
with
|C_{K}(H)|\geq 36
. It follows that
C_{K}(H)=K
, so
f
is trivial. Then,

G\cong H\times K\cong C_{p}\times(C_{3}^{2}\rtimes C_{4}),

is the unique minimum
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-cover.

For any prime
p\geq 5
,
G
is a
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-cover which is a solvable group, but it is not supersolvable since its subgroup
K
is a non-supersolvable group. The proof is complete.

With the notations established in the statement of Theorem 4, we mention that if
p=2
, then the unique minimum
\mathscr{F}_{2}
-cover is a non-supersolvable Frobenius group of order 72:
F_{9}=C_{3}^{2}\rtimes C_{8}
(SmallGroup(72, 39)). If
p=3
, there are 4 minimum
\mathscr{F}_{3}
-covers, all of them being supersolvable groups:

–

C_{3}^{2}\rtimes(C_{2}\times C_{4})
(SmallGroup(72, 21));

–

C_{3}\rtimes D_{24}
(SmallGroup(72, 23));

–

C_{4}\times(C_{3}\rtimes S_{3})
(SmallGroup(72, 32));

–

C_{12}\rtimes S_{3}
(SmallGroup(72, 33)).

If
\mathscr{F}
is formed of supersolvable groups, it may happen that there are at least 2 minimum
\mathscr{F}
-covers, all of them being non-supersolvable. For instance, if
\mathscr{F}=\{C_{4}\rtimes C_{4},C_{3}\rtimes S_{3}\}
, there are 3 minimum
\mathscr{F}
-covers and all are non-supersolvable: SmallGroup(144, 116), SmallGroup(144, 120), SmallGroup(144, 133). These 3 groups are some semidirect products of
C_{3}^{2}
and
C_{4}\rtimes C_{4}
. The same 3 groups are also the unique minimum
\mathscr{F}
-covers of
\mathscr{F}=\{C_{4}\rtimes C_{4},C_{2}\times(C_{3}\rtimes S_{3})\}
. This shows that two different sets of supersolvable groups may have the same minimum covers, all of them being non-supersolvable.

A second example of infinitely many sets
\mathscr{F}
formed of supersolvable groups such that they have a unique
\mathscr{F}
-cover which is not a supersolvable group can be given. More exactly, if
p\geq 5
is a prime and
\mathscr{F}_{p}=\{C_{3}\rtimes S_{3},C_{8p}\}
is a set of supersolvable groups, its unique minimum
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-cover is the non-supersolvable group
C_{p}\times F_{9}
. An argument would follow similar ideas that are part of the proof of Theorem 4.

Proof of Theorem 5.
By Lemma 2.1, the order of a minimum
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-cover is
60p
. Let
G
be such a cover.

If
p\leq 59
, then one can check that the theorem holds via GAP. If
p\geq 61
, then
G
has a cyclic normal subgroup
H\cong C_{p}
. Let
K=\frac{G}{H}
. By following a part of the reasoning that was done in the proof of Theorem 4, we deduce that
K
has subgroups isomorphic to
D_{10}
and
A_{4}
, respectively. Up to isomorphism,
A_{5}
is the unique group of order 60 satisfying such properties, so
K\cong A_{5}
. Then
K
is a simple non-abelian group and, since gcd
(|H|,|M(K)|)=
gcd
(p,2)=1
, we obtain that

G\cong H\times K\cong C_{p}\times A_{5},

by Lemma 2.2.

Obviously,
G
is a non-solvable group, so it is also non-metabelian. The proof is complete.

By following the notations established in the statement of Theorem 5, we also study the cases
p\in\{2,5\}.
If
p=2
, then there are two minimum
\mathscr{F}_{2}
-covers:
C_{2}\times A_{5}
and
D_{5}\times A_{4}
. If
p=5
, there are also two minimum
\mathscr{F}_{5}
-covers:
C_{5}\rtimes S_{4}
(SmallGroup(120, 38)) and
D_{5}\times A_{4}
. For both values of
p
, the first corresponding minimum
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-cover is a non-metabelian group, while the second is metabelian.

As in the supersolvable case, we mention that for specific choices of
\mathscr{F}
formed of metabelian groups, we can get at least 2 minimum
\mathscr{F}
-covers, all of them being non-metabelian. As an example, if one chooses
\mathscr{F}=\{D_{8},C_{3}\times A_{4}\}
, then the only minimum
\mathscr{F}
-covers are
C_{3}\times S_{4}
and
C_{3}\rtimes S_{4}
(SmallGroup(72, 43)). Also, a non-metabelian group can be the unique minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover for different choices of
\mathscr{F}
formed of metabelian groups. For instance,
S_{3}^{2}\rtimes C_{2}
(SmallGroup(72, 40)) is the unique minimum
\mathscr{F}
-cover if
\mathscr{F}\in\{\{D_{8},C_{3}^{2}\rtimes C_{4}\},\{D_{8},S_{3}^{2}\}\}
.

Finally, we point out infinitely many sets
\mathscr{F}
formed of metabelian groups such that they have only two minimum
\mathscr{F}
-covers, both of them being non-metabelian. More precisely, if
p\geq 5
is a prime and
\mathscr{F}_{p}=\{D_{8},C_{p}\times A_{4}\}
, its only minimum
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-covers are
C_{p}\times S_{4}
and
C_{p}\rtimes S_{4}
. If
p\leq 23
, this can be checked via GAP. If
p\geq 29
, as in the proofs of Theorems 4 and 5, we get that a minimum
\mathscr{F}_{p}
-cover
G
is isomorphic to
H\rtimes K
, where
H\cong C_{p}
and
K
is a group of order 24 having subgroups isomorphic to
D_{8}
and
A_{4}
, respectively. It follows that
K\cong S_{4}
, so
G\cong C_{p}\rtimes S_{4}
. If
f:S_{4}\longrightarrow Aut(C_{p})
is the group homomorphism that induces the previous semidirect product, we deduce that

\frac{S_{4}}{ker(f)}\cong im(f)\leq Aut(C_{p})\cong C_{p-1}.

Hence,
\frac{S_{4}}{ker(f)}
is an abelian group and this leads us to
S_{4}^{\prime}=A_{4}\leq ker(f)
. Therefore,
f
induces a unique nontrivial semidirect product or a direct product. Consequently,
G\cong C_{p}\rtimes S_{4}
or
G\cong C_{p}\times S_{4}
.
