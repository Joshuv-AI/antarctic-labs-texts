T cell KAT6A deficiency relieves inflammatory bowel disease in mice
Authors: Shi-Jia Huang, Hui-Lin Ye, Shuo Xu, Ting Liu, Wu-Chang Zhang, Yong-Li Wang, Sheng-Zhong Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe incidence of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) has been increasing, and while the interaction between T cells and intestinal microorganisms is crucial in its pathogenesis, the related epigenetic mechanisms remain unclear. This study found that the expression of lysine acetyltransferase 6A (KAT6A) was increased in T cells of patients with acute colitis. Knocking out KAT6A in CD4⁺ T cells alleviated dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis in mice, as manifested in body weight, disease activity index, colon length, inflammation, and the expression of proinflammatory factors. Mechanistically, KAT6A deficiency upregulated the senescence of CD4⁺ T cells and affected the expression of related genes. Moreover, the regulation of colitis by CD4⁺ T cell KAT6A was dependent on the gut microbiota. Antibiotic treatment could reverse the protective effect in T cell KAT6A knockout (TK6AKO) mice, and fecal transplantation experiments confirmed that it was related to the change of the microbiota. 16S rRNA sequencing showed that the composition of the gut microbiota was changed, and specific bacteria such as Akkermansia muciniphila were enriched in TK6AKO mice. This study reveals that KAT6A affects colitis through the interaction between regulating T cell senescence and the gut microbiota, providing a new strategy for treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01509-4.Keywords: Inflammatory bowel disease, T cell, Lysine acetyltransferase 6A, Senescence, Microbiota

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), mainly encompassing ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease, has witnessed a continuous increase in its incidence in recent years, imposing a heavy burden on society and patients [1]. During the pathogenesis of IBD, the interaction between T cells and intestinal microorganisms is considered to play a crucial role [2]. However, relatively few studies have been conducted on the role of epigenetic mechanisms in T cells regulating IBD. This knowledge gap severely hampers a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of IBD and also restricts the development of effective therapeutic strategies.

T cells play a multifaceted role in colitis. Normally, as the "guardians" of the intestinal immune system, they precisely identify various antigens in the intestine with their high antigen specificity and secrete immunomodulatory cytokines like IL-2, IL-4, and IL-10 to shape and balance the immune microenvironment, protecting the intestinal mucosa [3–5]. However, when T cell function is abnormal, such as immunomodulatory imbalance, they may overproduce pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α [6, 7]. These cytokines act as "summons", attracting and activating immune cells that release more inflammatory mediators, triggering a cascade reaction [8, 9]. This leads to inflammation, damage to the intestinal mucosa, and promotes the development of colitis.

Monocytic leukemia zinc finger protein KAT6A belongs to the MYST family and is a histone acetyltransferase, participating in many critical cellular processes [10]. Regarding T cell function, previous studies found that it can regulate T cell function and boost strong effector responses during infection [11]. Our previous study has also confirmed that in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, KAT6A can promote the proliferation and differentiation of proinflammatory CD4⁺ T cell subsets to regulate T cell activity [12]. In the regulation of cellular senescence, a large number of studies have shown that KAT6A can regulate cellular senescence through the INK4A-ARF signaling pathway [13]. Moreover, senescent CD4⁺ T cells will exhibit phenomena such as shortened telomeres, loss of proliferative capacity, and resistance to apoptosis [14, 15]. Although these senescent T cells have metabolic activity and secrete IL-6 and TNF-α, the secretory phenotype is complex and has both pro-tumoral and anti-tumoral effects in different contexts [16, 17]. Whether KAT6A can regulate T cell senescence and further play a regulatory role in the process of IBD has not been reported yet.

In this study, we specifically knocked out KAT6A in CD4⁺ T cells in a mouse model to observe its impact on colitis and deeply analyzed the underlying mechanisms from multiple perspectives such as cellular senescence, gene expression, and changes in the intestinal microbial community. The aim was to provide a new perspective for revealing the pathogenesis of IBD and a theoretical basis for developing effective therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Mice

KAT6Afl/fl mice on a C57BL/6 background were generated by Cyagen Laboratory Animal Corporation (Suzhou, China) and crossed with Cd4Cre mice to establish KAT6A-specific knockout transgenic mice (KAT6Afl/fl Cd4Cre+, TK6AKO) and littermate control mice (KAT6Afl/fl Cd4Cre−, LC). The mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with free access to food and water. All experimental procedures used age- and sex-matched mice (6–8 weeks old).

Dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis model.

To induce colitis, TK6AKO and LC mice were given 3% DSS (36–50 kDa; MP Biomedical, California, USA) in their drinking water for 6 days, followed by 1 day of regular water. The disease activity index (DAI) was monitored daily on the basis of weight loss (0–4), stool consistency (0, 2, 4), and the presence of bloody stool (0, 2, 4). On day 8, the mice were sacrificed, and their spleen weights and colon lengths were recorded, as we previously reported in our earlier study [18].

CD4+ T cell purification and culture

CD4+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of 8-week-old TK6AKO and LC mice via magnetic sorting (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 50 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (b-ME), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 2 mM glutamine. For in vitro stimulation, 1 × 105 CD4+ T cells were seeded into 24-well plates and activated with plate-bound anti-CD3 (Clone 17-A2, 1 µg/mL) and soluble anti-CD28 antibodies (Clone 37.51, 2 µg/mL).

Histological analysis

Tissue samples from the murine colon were collected, fixed in freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h at 4 °C, then embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 µm thickness were cut for histological analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed using reagents purchased from Sigma, following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Total mRNA was extracted via TRIzol (Invitrogen, California, USA) and reverse transcribed into cDNA via the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan). Quantitative PCR was performed as previously described [19], with the primer sequences provided in Table S1.

Flow cytometry

The isolation of immune cells from the mouse small intestinal lamina propria is a multi-step and precise process. It begins with the physical dissection and cleaning of the mouse small intestine tissue, followed by the removal of fat and lymph nodes. The tissue is then subjected to a pre-digestion with a solution containing EDTA and DTT (sigma) (PBS + 3%FBS + 1 mM EDTA + 1 mM DTT) to gently dissociate the epithelial cells. Subsequently, the tissue is transferred to a cocktail of digestive enzymes, including collagenase IV (sigma), DNase I (Roche), and Dispase II (Roche) (1640 + 0.2 mg/ml collagenaseIV + 0.1 mg/ml DNaseI + 1 mg/ml DispaseII) for thorough digestion to release the deeper lamina propria cells. Finally, the target immune cell population is purified and enriched through centrifugation, filtration, and red blood cell lysis. Single-cell suspensions were prepared, and the cells were stained with Live/Dead Fixable Viability Stain 510, followed by staining with anti-CD45, anti-CD3, and anti-CD4 antibodies (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). To assess T cell senescence, CD4+ T cells from TK6AKO and LC mice were stimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies for 48 h, stained with anti-CD27 and anti-CD28 antibodies (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and analyzed by flow cytometry. A list of the antibodies used was presented in Table S2.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining

SA-β-gal staining was performed via a commercial kit (C0602, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Initially, the cells were washed with PBS and then fixed with 1 mL of β-galactosidase staining fixative for 15 min at room temperature. Following washing with PBS three times, the cells were incubated with the SA-β-gal working solution at 37 °C overnight. The senescent cells were stained green.

Fecal microbiota analysis

Fresh fecal samples of TK6AKO and LC mice treated with vehicle or DSS were collected and stored at -80 °C. Genomic DNA was extracted using OMEGA Soil DNA Kits (Omega Bio-Tek). The 16S rRNA gene was amplified for PCR-based library construction and sequenced on an Illumina platform by Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The sequencing data were analyzed via QIIME2 following standard protocols to understand the gut microbiota.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

The original single-cell RNA sequencing data were previously published [20]. The data were processed via 10 × Genomics' Cell Ranger software (V6.0) and filtered with Seurat (V4.3.1). Cluster and subcluster were performed via Seurat, with the data normalized via the LogNormalize method. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, and the top 10 principal components were used for downstream analysis. Clustering was conducted via the Louvain algorithm, with UMAP and t-SNE applied for dimensionality reduction. Cell types were identified via SingleR, and gene expression differences between healthy and inflamed cohorts were assessed via the Wilcoxon algorithm.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the means ± SEMs. Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism. Two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons, and Student's t test was used for two-group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Increased expression of KAT6A in T cells from colonic biopsies of acute colitis patients

To elucidate the role of immune cells in the progression of acute colitis, we reanalyzed publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing data from human colonic biopsies [20]. Using the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) clustering method, we distinguished immune cell populations in healthy (n = 6) and inflamed (n = 3) groups (Fig. 1A). Notably, T cells were the main immune cell subset (Fig. 1B). While T cells' regulatory role in acute colitis is known, their epigenetic impact is unclear. KAT6A, a lysine acetyltransferase, regulates key signaling pathways. Our analysis showed KAT6A was most highly expressed in T cells (Fig. 1C). Further analyses found KAT6A expression in T cells was significantly higher in the inflamed group than the healthy group (Fig. 1D–G). These results highlight KAT6A's important role in T cells during acute colitis progression.

KAT6A deficiency in CD4+ T cells mitigates DSS-induced colitis in mice

To clarify the impact of KAT6A in T cells on intestinal diseases, we generated mice with KAT6A deficiency in T cells (TK6AKO) and their littermate control mice (LC). Subsequently, we induced IBD by using drinking water containing dextran sulfate sodium (DSS), with normal drinking water as the control. Compared to LC mice, TK6AKO mice had less weight change (Fig. 2A), lower DAI score (Fig. 2B), longer colon and less swelling (Fig. 2C, D). Histological analysis showed that in TK6AKO mice treated with DSS, epithelial damage and inflammation in the colon were less than in LC mice (Fig. 2E, F). With normal water, LC and TK6AKO mice had similar weight, colon length, spleen weight and histological changes (Fig. 2A–F). In line with the reduced colitis in TK6AKO mice, the gene expression of proinflammatory cytokines like Il1β, Il6, Il12, Il17 in TK6AKO mice's colon was lower than in LC mice's after DSS treatment (Fig. 2G). No difference was seen between LC and TK6AKO mice without DSS. These results showed that KAT6A deficiency in CD4+ T cells protected against DSS-induced colitis.

KAT6A deficiency promotes T cell senescence

Subsequently, we studied how KAT6A regulates CD4⁺ T cell function. Flow cytometry analysis showed that after DSS exposure, the percentage of CD4⁺ T cells in TK6AKO mice's colonic lamina propria (cLP) was significantly lower than in LC mice (Fig. 3A, B). Previously, we reported that KAT6A can regulate CD4⁺ T cell differentiation through the glucose metabolism pathway, influencing autoimmune diseases [12]. In KAT6A-deficient mice, DNA replication and cell cycle related to senescence were downregulated. KAT6A modulates p53 to regulate p21 expression and cell senescence [21] and can also inhibit senescence through the CDK4 inhibitor and INK4A-ARF pathway [13]. qRT-PCR assays showed that senescence-related genes were upregulated in KAT6A—deficient CD4⁺ T cells activated by anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies (Fig. 3C), while genes like E2F2, MELK, NSD2, and CDC6 were downregulated (Fig. 3D). These genes are involved in senescence and are potential targets of KAT6A [13, 22, 23]. When activated by anti-CD3/CD28, the percentage of SA-β-gal- positive cells and the proportion of CD4⁺CD27⁻CD28⁻ (senescent) T cells in KAT6A-deficient CD4⁺ T cells were higher than in controls (Fig. 3E, F). These results indicated that the knockout of KAT6A in CD4+ T cells promoted cell senescence.

T cell KAT6A deficiency alleviates colitis through the gut microbiota

In the current model, microbial metabolic pathways control the host's immune balance by adjusting CD4+ T cell numbers [24]. To study whether the regulation of T cells by KAT6A during intestinal inflammation is microbiota-dependent, we used broad-spectrum antibiotics (ABX) to deplete the microbiota of TK6AKO and LC mice. Notably, ABX treatment eliminated the initial disparities between the two groups, as manifested by the similarity in body weight, colon length, spleen weight, and DAI scores of the two groups after exposure to DSS (Fig. 4A–D). Moreover, the percentage of CD4+ T cells in both groups was similar after ABX treatment (Fig. 4E, F), and no difference was found between ABX- and water- treated mice (Fig. 4A - F).

Then we transferred feces from LC or TK6AKO mice to ABX- treated C57BL/6 J mice by daily gavage for a week. After DSS exposure, TK6AKO mice's feces protected better against DSS-induced colitis than LC mice's, shown by less weight loss (Fig. 4G), longer colon (Fig. 4H), lighter spleen (Fig. 4I), and better DAI scores (Fig. 4J). Flow cytometry showed fewer CD4+ T cells in the TK6AKO Mb + DSS group's cLP (Fig. 4K, L). In contrast, no significant difference was found between LC Mb and TK6AKO Mb groups with regular water (Fig. 4G–L). These results indicate that KAT6A-regulated T cells protect against colitis, relying on the intestinal microbiota.

T cell KAT6A deficiency improves the composition of gut microbiota

We then investigated changes in the intestinal microbiota of T cell KAT6A deficiency mice. Using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, we analyzed the microbiota composition in LC, TK6AKO, LC + DSS, and TK6AKO + DSS groups. Alpha diversity in the TK6AKO group was higher than others (Fig. 5A), and there were no significant differences in species evenness among the four groups. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) of β diversity separated the samples into four distinct clusters (Fig. 5B). KAT6A deficiency in T cells changed the gut microbiota composition at the family level, characterized by an increased abundance of Lachnospiraceae and Lactobacillaceae, and a decreased abundance of Erysipelotrichaceae and Bacteroidaceae (Fig. 5C). Similar trends were observed after DSS treatment (Fig. 5C). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis showed Akkermansia muciniphila was more abundant in TK6AKO mice than in LC mice (Fig. 5D). Escherichia coli, Bacteroides acidifaciens, and Parabacteroides goldsteinii were elevated in DSS-treated LC mice, while Lactobacillus johnsonii was enriched in DSS-treated TK6AKO mice (Fig. 5E). Random forest analysis also identified Akkermansia muciniphila as the most distinctive species between the two groups, and Lactobacillus johnsonii and Escherichia coli as key species with significant abundance differences in DSS-treated LC and TK6AKO mice (Fig. 5F–G). These findings highlight the distinct dynamics of microbial species in TK6AKO and LC mice and their responses to DSS-induced colitis. Finally, a working model was presented to illustrate KAT6A's role in T cell senescence and intestinal inflammation related to the gut microbiota (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The prevalence of IBD has been increasing steadily. The interaction of T cells and the intestinal microbiota is vital for IBD pathogenesis, yet the epigenetic mechanisms remain unclear. In the present study, we have shown that deficiency of KAT6A in CD4+ T cells alleviated the symptoms of DSS-induced colitis. Mechanistically, KAT6A influenced CD4+ T cell senescence, which in turn had an impact on the composition of the gut microbiota and ultimately affected the manifestation of colitis symptoms. These findings provide new perspectives and possible therapeutic targets, facilitating a deeper understanding of IBD pathogenesis and the development of effective treatments.

KAT6A played a crucial role in regulating T cell function. It has been reported that KAT6A is essential for shaping the functional diversity of the cytotoxic T cell pool [11]. Meanwhile, KAT6A inhibits cellular senescence through the INK4A-ARF pathway and regulates cell proliferation of hematopoietic and neural stem cells by suppressing p16INK4a transcription [13, 25]. Our data further confirm that KAT6A can regulate T cell senescence. In vitro, the senescence-related genes identified as potential targets of KAT6A were significantly upregulated in KAT6A-deficient CD4⁺ T cells activated by anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies. Meanwhile, the percentage of SA-β-gal- positive cells in KAT6A-deficient CD4⁺ T cells were higher than in controls. These findings highlight the critical role of KAT6A in maintaining T cell function and suggest its potential as a therapeutic target for modulating immune aging and related disorders.

This study demonstrates that CD4+ T cell senescence plays a crucial regulatory role in colitis. Several lines of evidence suggest that cellular senescence plays a pivotal role in shaping the fate and function of immune cells, influencing their responses under various physiological and pathological conditions [26]. Upon activation, T cells secrete cytokines that enhance immune responses. However, in aged T cells, re-exposure to antigens leads to diminished cytokine production, resulting in impaired immune functions. This reduced cytokine secretion limits their capacity to activate and regulate other immune cells, weakening their contribution to the overall immune response. In cancer patients, senescent-like CD4+ cytotoxic T cells downregulate CD27 and CD28 while upregulating CD57 and NK cell receptors, such as NKG2C, NKG2D, and KIR, enabling antigen-independent cytotoxic activities [27, 28]. Interestingly, in the DSS-induced colitis mouse model, KAT6A-deficient CD4+ T cells presented marked downregulation of CD27 and CD28, accompanied by a decrease in inflammatory cytokines. This study highlights the critical role of KAT6A in regulating T cell senescence and its interaction with the gut microbiota, offering new insights into the epigenetic modulation of immune responses during colitis.

This study has provided evidence to substantiate the roles of KAT6A in the progression of colitis, especially through their interaction with the gut microbiota. In the DSS-induced colitis model, the alleviated colitis phenotype in TK6AKO mice was abolished after antibiotic treatment and restored after fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT). Further analysis of the gut microbiota before and after DSS treatment revealed that Akkermansia muciniphila was enriched in TK6AKO mice compared with that in LC controls. Studies have shown that supplementation with Akkermansia muciniphila or its protein Amuc_1100 improved colitis by reducing infiltrating macrophages and CD8+ T cells in the colon [29]. Meanwhile, Akkermansia muciniphila ameliorated colitis by upregulating RORγt Treg cell-mediated immune responses [30]. Animal experiments have shown that IL-10 levels in the colon tissue of SPF mice inoculated with Akkermansia muciniphila strains (AH39 and BAA835) were significantly elevated compared to controls or other Lactobacillus strains. Similarly, Akkermansia muciniphila AH39 can also induce increased IL-10 levels in the colon of germ-free mice [31]. Additionally, DSS treatment caused distinct microbial shifts between TK6AKO and LC mice, with elevated Escherichia coli in LC mice, whereas Lactobacillus johnsonii was enriched in TK6AKO mice. Escherichia coli has been reported to exacerbate colitis by modulating the Th17/Treg cell balance [32, 33]. Lactobacillus johnsonii, a known probiotic, has been reported to alleviate colitis [34]. It has also been shown to enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) by promoting the expression of the adenosine A2A receptor (A2AR) on T cells, thereby amplifying immune responses through A2AR signaling [35].

This study emphasizes the functions of KAT6A in repressing T cell senescence and in its interaction with the gut microbiota in the setting of colitis. These findings suggest that targeting KAT6A could be a therapeutic strategy for treating colitis by regulating T cell senescence and leveraging the gut microbiota to reduce inflammation and restore the intestinal balance.

Conclusion

This study highlights the role of KAT6A in regulating T cell senescence and its interaction with gut microbiota in colitis. KAT6A was found to modulate intraepithelial T cells, improving DSS-induced colitis and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines. The beneficial effects in KAT6A-deficient mice were linked to the enrichment of Akkermansia muciniphila. The study also observed that Escherichia coli, which worsens colitis, was higher in control mice, while Lactobacillus johnsonii, known to alleviate colitis, was enriched in KAT6A-deficient mice. These findings suggest that targeting KAT6A could be a therapeutic strategy for colitis by harnessing the gut microbiota to reduce inflammation and restore intestinal balance.
