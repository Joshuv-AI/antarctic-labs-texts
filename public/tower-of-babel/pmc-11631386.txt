Caesarean section and operative vaginal delivery in Estonia and Finland from 1992 to 2016: registry-based study
Authors: Kaire Sildver, Piret Veerus, Mika Gissler, Katrin Lang, Heti Pisarev
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractProportion of normal deliveries is decreasing worldwide. This study analysed operative vaginal deliveries (OVD) and Caesarean sections (CS) with some background factors in Estonia and Finland from 1992 to 2016. Data on all deliveries from 1992 to 2016 were obtained from the Finnish Medical Birth Registry (1 481 160 births) and the Estonian Medical Birth Registry (356 063 births). Time trends were analysed by joinpoint regression, and factors associated with OVD and CS by logistic regression. Odds ratios with 95% CIs were calculated, adjusted for year, maternal age, foetal birthweight, and use of epidural/spinal anaesthesia. One out of four deliveries were operative in Estonia and in Finland by 2016. By 2016, the Estonian CS rate had tripled to 20.9% and the OVD rate had increased by nine times to 5.6%. In Finland, the CS rate increased slightly to 16.4% while the OVD rate nearly doubled to 9.4%. In Estonia, the incidence of OVD was 24% lower (aOR 0.76, 95% Cl 0.74–0.78) and the incidence of CS 9% higher (aOR 1.09, 95% Cl 1.07–1.10) than in Finland. Use of epidural/spinal anaesthesia and foetal birthweight increased the risk of OVD in both countries, maternal age increased the risk of CS in both countries. Even if the CS and OVD rates are different, operative delivery rates may be similar in different countries. Combined analysis of operative deliveries together with background factors gives a better understanding of the trends in birthcare than monitoring CS rates alone.

Introduction

Operative obstetric care may be classified as follows: abdominal operative delivery via caesarean section (CS), and operative vaginal delivery (OVD), which includes vacuum extraction (VE, also known as ventouse delivery) and forceps delivery [1]. Worldwide, CS rates have increased in recent decades, and OVD has become an important intervention in modern obstetric care [2] as a safe alternative to CS under suitable circumstances [3]. European Association of Perinatal Medicine and European Midwives Association in 2024 drew attention to CS rates as a significant public health problem and stated that CS rates at a country level should be in the range of 15%–20% [4]. CS rates in Europe (median rate 27.0% in 2015 and 26.0% in 2019) varied in 2015 from 16.1% in Iceland to 56.9% in Cyprus and in 2019 from 16.4% in Norway to 53.1% in Cyprus. The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) has taken the position that the epidemic of caesarean sections worldwide must end [5].

OVD rates in Europe (median rate of 7.2% in 2015 and 6.1% in 2019) also varied over time (in 2015 from 0.5% in Romania to 15.1% in Spain and Ireland, and in 2019, 1.4% in Croatia to 14.4% in Spain) [6, 7].

With all surgical and other interventions, there is always an increased risk for maternal and perinatal complications [8–10]. Forceps have been used for OVD for centuries and used to be a common aid in obstetrics [11]. But in recent decades, forceps have been pushed aside by vacuum extraction, which is easier to master and has a lower risk for perineal trauma, but a higher risk of birth trauma for the newborn [9]. In the Nordic countries, in the year 2020, forceps births were rare. In Norway, the forceps birth rate was 1.3%; in Iceland, 0.3%; and in Sweden, Finland, and Denmark, the rate is even lower [12]. However, in England in 2022, using forceps as a delivery method remains more common, with rates in recent years ranging from 3.3% for low forceps to 3.7% for other forceps [13].

The main maternal factors driving OVD are slow to progress in the second stage of labour, maternal chronic diseases, and foetal factors, mainly pathological cardiotocography [2]. In addition, the desire for epidural anaesthesia as pain relief is increasing [14]. Although epidural anaesthesia works well as a pain relief aid, side effects include a delay in the second stage of labour and a substantially increased risk of CS or OVD [14].

Operative delivery can be associated with significant short-term and long-term maternal and perinatal complications [5, 9, 10]. OVD can be associated with a higher incidence of episiotomy, significant vulvo-vaginal tear, obstetric anal sphincter injuries, postpartum haemorrhage, urinary or bowel incontinence and perinatal complications, such as cephalhaematoma, subgaleal and intracranial haemorrhage, facial or scalp lacerations, retinal haemorrhage, jaundice, or hyperbilirubinaemia [9]. CS can cause anaesthetic and urological complications, bleeding, infections, thromboembolism. Pregnancies after CS face an increased risk of preterm birth, uterine rupture, abnormal placentation [5]. CS increases the risk of respiratory problems in newborns [15].

The aim of this study was to analyse the time trends of OVD and CS in Estonia and Finland from 1992 to 2016, also by some possible background factors.

Methods

All deliveries in Estonia and Finland in 1992–2016 were included in this study. The data obtained from the Estonian Medical Birth Registry and Finnish Medical Birth Registry included the following variables: mother's age; gestational age; number of previous deliveries; mode of delivery: vaginal delivery, forceps delivery, vacuum extraction, planned CS, other CS; anaesthesia in the delivery: epidural/spinal anaesthesia; birthweight; number of foetuses.

Statistical analyses

Data on births were obtained from two registries: the Estonian Medical Birth Registry (n = 356 063) and the Finnish Medical Birth Registry (n = 1 481 160).

We analysed total CS rates and OVD (vacuum and forceps) rates according to the following descriptive characteristics and obstetrical factors: maternal age; birthweight; Caesarean section; forceps and vacuum extraction delivery; epidural/spinal anaesthesia. Epidural and spinal anaesthesia were analysed together as data on epidural and spinal anaesthesia are not collected separately in Estonia. Data were divided into five-year periods (1992–1996; 1997–2001; 2002–2006; 2007–2011; 2012–2016).

Joinpoint regression was used to estimate the change in OVD trends over time and results are presented in annual percentage change (APC). To identify factors related to OVD and CS, logistic regression models were constructed for Estonia and Finland separately. Data on epidural/spinal anaesthesia has not been collected in Estonia and Finland using the same standard on CS, and this part was left out of the CS model. OVD models were adjusted for vacuum/forceps; year of birth; mother's age (per 10 years’ increase); birthweight (per 500 g increase), and epidural/spinal anaesthesia use. CS models were adjusted for CS; year of birth; mother's age (per 10 years’ increase) and birthweight (per 500 g increase). An interaction term was added to this model to compare Estonia and Finland.

Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical software used was Stata/IC 14 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and the Joinpoint Regression Program 5.0.2.

Results

Both in Estonia and Finland, the total proportion of operative deliveries increased during the period and became similar by 2016, but the proportions of CS and OVD differed across time.

Figure 1 shows that in 1992, 6.5% in Estonia and 15.1% of all births in Finland ended with CS. Until 2003, the share of CS was higher in Finland, but thereafter, the share of CS in Estonia exceeded Finland. In 1992, 0.6% of all births in Estonia and 5.1% of all births in Finland ended with OVD (vacuum or forceps). The total number of CS and OVD in Estonia increased rapidly until 2007. Later, the CS remained stable between 20% and 21%, and the growth of OVD slowed down, but by 2016, the share had increased to 5.6%. In Finland, during the entire period, the proportion of CS has been quite stable, remaining between 15% and 17%, but the share of OVD increased during the entire period, reaching 9.4% of all births by 2016.

Table 1 shows that OVD rates in Estonia and Finland increased significantly over time. In Finland, the proportion of CS remained quite stable. In Estonia, the proportion of CS increased rapidly until 2007, when it exceeded that of Finland, and since 2011, it has been quite stable. The total proportion of all operative deliveries increased in Finland and Estonia throughout the entire period and now represents approximately 25% of all births in both countries. The average maternal age increased in Estonia and Finland during the entire period, in Estonia more rapidly than in Finland, and the difference between the countries diminished to 0.5 years. The average birth weight had a downward trend in Finland and an upward trend in Estonia. In Finland and Estonia, the total number of births with epidural/spinal anaesthesia increased rapidly. In Estonia, epidural/spinal anaesthesia was used in 0.5% of births at the beginning of study period and increased to 30.1% by the end of study period. The increase in Finland was even bigger, from 20% to 59.1% during the entire period.

The increase in OVD in Estonia can be divided into four periods (Fig. 2). In the first period between 1992 and 1999, the share of OVD increased by 25.05% per year. In the next period, from 1999–2008, the increase slowed down but continued to grow at 7.37% per year. Then the growth stopped until 2014, and then it turned upward again. The changes in the first two time periods are statistically significant. Figure 2 shows that a significant change in the proportion of OVD has taken place in Finland over the past 25 years as well. The share of OVD has increased throughout the period. The changes can be divided into three periods. The share of OVD increased by 1.92% every year in the period 1992–2002. Further, in 2002–2006, the increase was faster, and the share of OVD increased by 7.71% per year. After that, the increase slowed down, and the annual increase remained at 1.25%, but still, for all time periods, the change is statistically significant.

Table 2 shows that maternal age was associated with a decrease in the incidence of OVD in Estonia (aOR 0.66, 95% Cl 0.64–0.69). In Finland, however, increase in maternal age was associated with increase in the incidence of OVD. Maternal age increased the risk of CS both in Estonia (aOR 1.53, 95% Cl 1.50–1.56) and Finland (aOR 1.63, 95% Cl 1.61–1.64). An increase in foetal birthweight was associated with an increase in the incidence of OVD both in Estonia and Finland (Estonia aOR 1.11, 95% Cl 1.10–1.13 and Finland aOR 1.10, 95% Cl 1.10–1.11), but decreased the risk of CS (Estonia aOR 0.81, 95% Cl 0.80–0.82 and Finland aOR 0.77, 95% Cl 0.77–0.78). The predictor with the highest OR appeared to be the use of epidural/spinal anaesthesia as a pain relief method. Epidural/spinal anaesthesia increased the incidence of OVD in Estonia by 32% (aOR 1.32, 95% Cl 1.26–1.38) and in Finland more than three times (aOR 3.46, 95% Cl 3.41–3.51). A cross-country comparison showed that in Estonia, the incidence of OVD was 24% lower (aOR 0.76, 95% Cl 0.74–0.78) and the incidence of CS 9% higher (aOR 1.09, 95% Cl 1.07–1.10) than in Finland.

Discussion

This registry-based study, which covered 25 years, compared time trends of OVD and CS in Estonia and Finland. Estonia and Finland had very different starting points in 1992. However, in 2016, Estonia and Finland were similar when combining CS and OVD as an operative delivery: a quarter of all births in Estonia (26.5%) and Finland (25.7%) ended with an operative delivery. However, when constituent CS and OVD rates are compared, Estonia had a higher proportion of CS (20.9%) compared to Finland (16.4%), while Finland had a higher proportion of OVD (9.4%) compared to Estonia (5.6%). After controlling for some confounding factors, the odds of OVD in Estonia remained 24% lower (aOR 0.76, 95% Cl 0.74–0.78) than in Finland.

Normal vaginal delivery is more cost-effective than elective caesarean section [16]. Elective repeat caesarean delivery is significantly more expensive than a vaginal birth after caesarean delivery [17]. Operative deliveries also have several short-term and long-term risks [5, 9, 10]. Delivery hospitals need to find a balance between operative and normal deliveries, between CS and OVD. Euro-Peristat analysis based on 2010 data confirms that countries with higher OVD rates do not have lower CS rates [18]. At the same time, OVD as an alternative to CS can only affect the proportion of CS performed in the second stage of labour since the instruments can only be used for a fully dilated cervix [9]. Obstetric training in forceps is decreasing, and the current practices are insufficient to ensure competence. This raises a policy question: should medical schools abandon teaching the forceps technique and instead focus on vacuum delivery or develop a high technological simulation allowing physicians to gain experience? [11].

Various factors can influence the proportion of operative deliveries. During the 25-year period of the study, there have been many parallel developments, such as increased maternal age and changes in birthweight distribution. The mean age of women in the European Union (EU) giving birth to their first child gradually increased and reached 29.4 years in 2019. The most significant change was observed in Estonia, where the mean age increased from 27.2 years in 2015 to 28.2 years in 2019 [19]. The lowest small birth weight trends are in Estonia, Finland, and other Northern European countries. In contrast, high birth weight is more common in Northern European countries, including Estonia and Finland [7]. A high birth weight of 4000 grams or more is a risk factor for OVD [2] and CS [20], but the data from Nordic countries does not support this completely [21]. Previous studies show that the number of operative deliveries is significantly influenced by the use of epidural anaesthesia. Thus, it is crucial to consider the relative advantages, disadvantages, and risks for each mode of delivery [2, 14]. There was a substantial difference in pain relief between the two study countries as well: epidural/spinal anaesthesia was used in 2016 in 33% of Estonian births and 59% of births in Finland. Women at birth should be counselled about the risks associated with epidural/spinal analgesia.

Clinical practice guidelines have been described as ‘…statements that include recommendations intended to optimise patient care, that are informed by a systematic review of evidence and an assessment of the benefits and harms of alternative care options’ [22]. Guidelines play an essential role in having an impact on operative deliveries. Guidelines provide recommendations and standards for healthcare professionals regarding the management of childbirth. They should influence clinical decision-making and practices related to the use of interventions such as CS or OVD. Guidelines may provide criteria for when these interventions are appropriate and help ensure their appropriate and safe use, promote evidence-based practices, and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes [9]. In Estonia and Finland, several guidelines regarding maternity care exist. In Estonia, there are national guidelines for the induction of childbirth and pain relief during childbirth using epidural anaesthesia, in addition to guidelines for monitoring normal childbirth. There are less relevant national guidelines in Finland [23, 24].

This study leverages 25 years of population-based registry data built on a similar framework. Data are collected in the registry using the birth card. The birth card used in Estonia was developed in 1992, following the example of Finland [25], which allows direct data comparisons.

The limitation is that some data cannot be directly compared between the two countries. For example, data on epidural and spinal anaesthesia are not collected separately for statistical purposes in Estonia, and therefore epidural anaesthesia could not be analysed in this study. In addition to the factors considered, several others can influence the number of OVD and CS, such as maternal concurrent diseases and BMI, foetal distress, as well as other reasons, such as the rise in the proportion of induced labour [23, 24, 26–28] and application of new and updated clinical practice guidelines [22]. There may be a need for European guidelines to harmonise the operative delivery trends. Non-reassuring and false-positive foetal cardiotocography during labour may increase operative delivery rates [29], but the data did not allow us to analyse it.

This article offers an opportunity to contrast the operative delivery rates over an extended period in two countries with different starting points. Very often, only CS rates between and within countries are compared. Our study shows that monitoring the proportion of CS and OVD is important when analysing perinatal health care. Women should receive accurate information about all operative deliveries' advantages and potential risks. Delivery services balance between improving maternal and foetal outcomes and increasing health care costs. Our data show that operative delivery trends can be similar between countries even if CS rates differ. It may be an inspirational example for other countries to compare trends in all operative deliveries to improve maternal and foetal outcomes. A more granular analysis of trends in operative deliveries is necessary, and it is helpful when shaping public health policy and clinical practice. The factors influencing operative deliveries deserve further research. Good-quality registry data is necessary for evidence-based health policy decisions.
