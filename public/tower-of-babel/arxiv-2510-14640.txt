Intent Clustering with Shared Pseudo-Labels
I-Fan Lin, Faegheh Hasibi, Suzan Verberne

Abstract
In this paper, we propose an intuitive, training-free and label-free method for intent clustering that makes minimal assumptions using lightweight and open-source LLMs. Many current approaches rely on commercial LLMs, which are costly, and offer limited transparency. Additionally, their methods often explicitly depend on knowing the number of clusters in advance, which is often not the case in realistic settings. To address these challenges, instead of asking the LLM to match similar text directly, we first ask it to generate pseudo-labels for each text, and then perform multi-label classification in this pseudo-label set for each text. This approach is based on the hypothesis that texts belonging to the same cluster will share more labels, and will therefore be closer when encoded into embeddings. These pseudo-labels are more human-readable than direct similarity matches. Our evaluation on four benchmark sets shows that our approach achieves results comparable to and better than recent baselines, while remaining simple and computationally efficient. Our findings indicate that our method can be applied in low-resource scenarios and is stable across multiple models and datasets. Our source code is available here: https://anonymous.4open.science/r/pseudo_label-7AE1/README.md



1
Introduction

Intent clustering groups unlabeled short texts into clusters of similar intents. It plays an important role in the Information Access systems by facilitating query intent identification
[
4
,
3
,
10
]
and enabling the development of intent-aware information-seeking systems
[
12
,
20
]
and customer-service chatbots
[
16
]
.

Many studies have addressed this task, mostly aiming at fine-tuning an embedder to learn representations for clustering
[
15
,
29
]
. However, these contrastive learning approaches generally require labeled training data, and their loss functions tend to be complex, making hyperparameter optimization difficult. LLM-guided clustering approaches have been proposed to address these challenges. These approaches can be implemented under training-free and label-free scenarios
[
26
,
17
]
. Despite advances with these approaches, proprietary models are often required
[
28
,
6
]
, and human-understandable insights are often hard to obtain
[
7
,
17
]
.

To deal with these limitations, we propose a method that constructs a pseudo-label set to improve text representations. The idea is based on the assumption that text representations (embeddings) become closer when the texts share similar words. The pseudo-label set serves as a set of candidate labels for an LLM to assign to each text. We then encode the text together with its pseudo-labels to form more effective representations for clustering. Our method works with small, open-source LLMs and produces human-readable pseudo-labels that simplify analysis and post-processing.

We empirically show that our method remains competitive with light-weight LLMs, without relying on proprietary models. It improves performance over directly using an embedder and even outperforms state-of-the-art approaches that require contrastive learning in three of the four datasets. In summary, our main contribution is an unsupervised, intuitive, and human-readable method that allows domain experts to perform analysis more easily.

2
Related Work

Short text clustering is often used to benefit downstream tasks. For example, the grouped intent cluster can be applied to intent detection, which is an important task to construct a conversational agent
[
16
]
. In information retrieval, it plays an important roles in grouping and indexing related document
[
1
]
. Additionally, clustering can be used for intrinsic analysis, such as topic discovery
[
19
]
and emotion mining
[
23
]
.

Several approaches have explored using LLMs to improve representations for clustering. We categorize them into two types: LLM-as-a-matcher and LLM-as-a-labeler: In the
LLM-as-a-matcher
setting, the LLM is used only to select similar candidates. Zhang et al.
[
28
]
use LLMs to select quality triplets for fine-tuning an embedder. Feng et al.
[
7
]
use LLMs to re-cluster edgepoints. Lin et al.
[
17
]
first encode all texts using an existing embedder and then use an LLM to select similar candidate texts for each instance; the embeddings of these candidates are average-pooled to produce the final representation for clustering.

In contrast, the
LLM-as-a-labeler
approach directly uses LLMs to generate (pseudo-)labels for texts. De Raedt et al.
[
6
]
first select prototype texts and use an LLM to generate their labels. They then use the LLM again to label/generate the remaining texts with these prototypes. The enriched texts are subsequently used to improve representations. Viswanathan et al.
[
27
]
use LLMs to generate key phrases for each text, which are then separately encoded into embeddings and concatenated for clustering. This approach is more human readable because an engineer, developer, or expert can check the generated labels. We focus on this family of approaches. Unlike prior work, our label set does not require a known number of clusters
K
, as in De Raedt et al.
[
6
]
. In addition, unlike Viswanathan et al.
[
27
]
, who consider only key phrases for each individual text – which may not be directly relevant to the cluster information – we generate labels by considering both the text and its neighbors, and we encourage important labels to be selected through multi-label classification.

Methodology and Experiments

3.1
Problem formulation

We follow Lin et al.
[
17
]
in formulating the problem of identifying intents from emerging data, with each cluster corresponding to a newly identified intent. Accordingly, there is a single collection of text data, denoted as
D=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where each data point
x_{i}\in D
corresponds to an utterance, and
N
is the total number of data points. The task is to partition
D
into
K
cluster sets, denoted as
\{S_{l}\}^{K}_{l=1}
. We assume
K
is known, but only for evaluation purposes. Our approach does not rely on a known
K
. This is unlike previous studies that assume the value of
K
in their method pipeline
[
6
,
28
,
7
]
.

3.2
Methodology

The method pipeline is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Method assumption.

Consider any
x_{i},x_{j}\in D
with
i\neq j
and
x_{i},x_{j}\in S_{k}
for some cluster
S_{k}
. Let
F(\cdot)
denote a pre-trained embedder,
l
be a pseudo-label that captures at least part of the semantic meaning of
S_{k}
, and
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
a distance function. We hypothesize that the two concatenated texts
l\oplus x_{i},l\oplus x_{j}
tend to be more similar to each other than the original texts. That is,
\delta(F(l\oplus x_{i}),F(l\oplus x_{j}))\leq\delta(F(x_{i}),F(x_{j}))
. Accordingly, we aim to assign similar pseudo-labels to texts belonging to the same cluster.

Construction of the label set with LLM

For each text
x_{i}\in D
, we derive its initial pseudo-label by prompting an LLM. To ensure labels reflect cluster-level semantics, we provide the LLM with the top
m
closest texts from the collection based on cosine similarity, where the embeddings are obtained from an existing pre-trained embedder. To reduce noise, we prompt the LLM to generate labels based only on the neighbors that match well with the text (see Table
1(a)
). We denote
l_{i}
as the initial pseudo-label of
x_{i}
. We set
m=10
in our experiments.

Iterative multi-label classification

To make texts belonging to the same cluster more similar, we iteratively perform multi-label classification for each text until its pseudo-label(s) stop changing. At each iteration, we provide the LLM with the top
m
closest texts,
each with a different pseudo-label
, for multi-label classification (see Table
1(b)
). The top
m
embeddings are derived based on the concatenation of the pseudo-label(s) from the previous iteration and the text, i.e.,
F(L_{i}^{t-1}\oplus x_{i})
, where
L_{i}^{t-1}
represents all pseudo-label(s) of
x_{i}

from iteration
t-1
used to derive the embeddings at iteration
t
. Note that
L_{i}^{0}=l_{i}
, and we ensure a nested structure:
L_{i}^{0}\subset L_{i}^{1},...\subset L_{i}^{j}
, meaning that labels from previous iterations are merged into the subsequent ones. Also, because each text will have one or more labels after iterations, we always use the initial pseudo-label in the prompt for classification.

For example, for the text “Does it cost to exchange currencies with this card?”
[
5
]
, the initial pseudo-label is ‘ask about currency exchange fees’. The updated text representation is encoded from the concatenation of the text and its pseudo-label, will be used for finding top
m
candidates. After the first iteration, we query the LLM with the these candidates and their initial pseudo-labels. Suppose the new pseudo-labels ‘ask currency exchange cost’ and ‘exchange currency cost’ are selected. The text ended up with all three labels together.

4.2
Qualitative analysis of Pseudo-Labels

To illustrate readability of our output, we compare the pseudo-labels with the ground truth intents. For example, the pseudo-label ‘ask calorie count of food item’ appears only in the ‘calories’ cluster, and ‘track package location’ appears only in the ‘order status’ cluster. However, we also observe cases where pseudo-labels appear in multiple clusters. For instances, the pseudo-label ‘cancel transaction mistake’ appears in both the ‘cancel transfer’ and ‘request refund’ clusters.

5
Conclusion

We propose a novel way to improve text representations by including LLM-generated pseudo-labels to texts, which also provides human-readable insights. Our method does not rely on the impractical assumption of knowing the number of clusters
K
, requires no labels, and works with smaller LLMs. The results show that our approach achieves performance comparable to or better than SOTA methods. This indicates that our method is a practical alternative for low-resource scenarios. One opportunity for future is search is that our approach only focuses on short text clustering. For longer texts, the method may exceed the token limit and require more time for LLM inference. In the future, we plan to explore how it can be adapted for longer texts.
