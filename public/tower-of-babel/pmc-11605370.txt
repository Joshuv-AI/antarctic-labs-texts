Exploring high entropy alloys: A review on thermodynamic design and computational modeling strategies for advanced materials applications
Authors: Peter Ifeolu Odetola, Bukola Joseph Babalola, Ayodeji Ebenezer Afolabi, Ufoma Silas Anamu, Emmanuel Olorundaisi, Mutombo Christian Umba, Thabang Phahlane, Olusoji Oluremi Ayodele, Peter Apata Olubambi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the quest for materials that can withstand the rigors of modern engineering applications, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) have emerged as a frontier in material science owing to their unprecedented combination of properties. This review focuses on intricate thermodynamic and computational modeling to guide the design and optimization of HEAs. By dissecting the foundational "four core effects" intrinsic to HEAs—high entropy, sluggish diffusion, severe lattice distortion, and cocktail effect—we illuminate the path towards predictable and tailored material properties. Central to the present discourse is the application of valence electron concentration (VEC) and cutting-edge strategies, including the CALculation of PHAse Diagrams (CALPHAD) method, first-principles approach, and machine-learning algorithms, which collectively empower the prediction and understanding of HEA behavior. Through a novel case study of a septenary equiatomic Ni-Al-Co-Cr-Cu-Mn-Ti HEA, this analysis demonstrates the utility of these computational tools in unveiling the alloy's phase stability and microstructural evolution, reinforcing the synergy between theoretical predictions and experimental validation. Furthermore, the review explores the burgeoning applications of HEAs across diverse sectors, such as aerospace, automotive, energy, and biomedical engineering, highlighting their transformative potential. Despite these advancements, challenges such as empirical design limitations, processing complexities, and the need for comprehensive databases are acknowledged, setting the stage for future exploration. This review not only charts a course for the rational design of HEAs, but also envisages their role in advancing material science towards novel applications, urging a concerted effort to overcome existing hurdles and explore uncharted territories in HEA research.Keywords: High-entropy alloys, Theoretical calculations, Computational modeling, Thermodynamic design, CALPHAD, Multi-component, Composition, Microstructural evolution, Mechanical properties

Introduction

The field of HEAs is at the frontier of the discovery of new material systems. However, principles for the design of these alloys remain underdeveloped. Traditional alloy design strategies are insufficient to navigate the compositional complexity of HEAs. There is a pressing need for new design principles to systematically explore and exploit the vast compositional space of HEAs to unlock materials with novel property profiles for various engineering applications across industries.

The quest for lightweight materials with superior high-temperature performance and exceptional mechanical properties has increasingly steered research toward HEAs. These novel alloys, characterized by their multi-principal element system and high configurational entropy, exhibit a unique blend of attributes including unparalleled strength, fracture toughness, wear resistance, thermal stability, and resistance to oxidation and corrosion [[1], [2], [3]]. Such properties render HEAs highly desirable for a myriad of engineering applications spanning the aerospace, automotive, and energy sectors.

Despite their promising potential, the synthesis and optimization of HEAs presents notable challenges [4,5]. Predicting the phase stability and mechanical properties of HEAs with high accuracy remains a significant challenge. The extensive compositional space of HEAs introduces complexities in understanding the interplay between multiple principal elements, necessitating advanced modeling tools and strategies to guide the alloy design process. Addressing these challenges, this review delves into the thermodynamic core effects underpinning HEAs and explores a range of computational approaches, from thermodynamic theoretical calculations and the CALculation of PHAse Diagram (CALPHAD) [6] method to first principles [7] and machine learning algorithms [8].

Current computational models, although invaluable, still struggle to capture the full complexity of HEAs, particularly in predicting the formation of secondary phases and their impact on properties. The reliance on empirical data limits the predictive capability of models such as CALPHAD for unexplored alloy compositions, necessitating the development of more advanced or hybrid modeling techniques that combine the strengths of first-principles calculations and machine learning. These computational strategies not only facilitate the understanding of phase formation and stability in HEAs but also pave the way for the prediction of their properties and behavior.

A critical gap in the current understanding of HEAs lies in the process-structure-property relationships. Although experimental studies have revealed promising properties of HEAs [3,9], a comprehensive theoretical framework that links the processing conditions to the resulting microstructure and properties is lacking. This gap hinders the ability to tailor HEAs for specific applications such as high-temperature or corrosion-resistant environments.

Moreover, this review underscores the utilization of computational modeling to expedite the development of HEAs with tailored properties for specific applications. By harnessing the power of advanced computational tools, researchers can navigate the intricate interplay of elements in HEAs and optimize their composition for the desired functionalities. This tailored approach to material design promises to revolutionize industries by delivering materials that meet the requirements of high performance and durability under extreme conditions. This manuscript provides a comprehensive overview of the advances in thermodynamic design and computational modeling strategies for HEAs, highlighting their critical role in unlocking the full potential of these advanced materials for future technological applications.

The milestones of developmental impacts in high entropy alloys

High-entropy alloys (HEAs), also known as multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs) or multi-component alloys, are alloys containing at least five principal elements, each restricted to concentrations between 5 and 35 at.% [1,2]. Originally, these alloys were believed to favor single-phase or dual-phase solid solutions owing to their high configurational entropies. However, recent research has shown that HEA properties can be achieved even with compositions that deviate from this strict rule. The focus has shifted towards understanding how the specific combination of elements, their interactions, and phase formation criteria influence the final properties of HEAs [10,11]. HEAs have marked significant milestones in materials science by shifting design strategies from focusing on a single principal element to a multi-element approach, beyond targeting stable phases and intentionally designing HEAs with metastable solid solutions [12]. Contrary to the initial premise of HEAs, the likelihood of forming solid-solution alloys decreases as the number of alloying elements increases [13]. This development has been driven by experimental, theoretical, and computational advancements that allow high-throughput experiments and artificial intelligence, which facilitate the efficient discovery of new alloys with tailored microstructures [14,15].

Since the onset of exploration in 2004, significant research efforts have contributed to a better understanding of the foundational principles and mechanisms of HEAs that lead to exponential growth [[16], [17], [18]]. The periodic table comes first in alloy design consideration, and understanding the elemental properties will go a long way to make a good choice for material development. The property profiles of elements range from atomic radius, electronegativity, density, ionization energy, atomic mass, melting, and boiling points. Careful selection and combination of elements from the periodic table are the key to unlocking the full potential of HEAs. Refractory metals are a group of metallic elements characterized by high heat resistance, exceptionally high melting points, and good wear resistance. Therefore, they are appropriate for high-temperature applications in furnaces, boilers, heaters, gas-turbine blades, rocket parts, and turbochargers [20]. Elements in this category are Zr (2128K), Nb (2750K), Mo (2896K), Re (3459K), Ta (3290K), Ir (2719K), Os (3306K), Ru (2607K), Rh (2237K), Hf (2506K), and W (3695K). The 3D transition metals characterized by partially filled d orbitals are a suite of ten elements: Sc (1814K), Ti (1941K), V (2183K), Cr (2180K), Mn (1519K), Fe (1811K), Co (1768K), Ni (1728K), Cu (1358K), and Zn (693K). Their unique properties include a moderately high melting point, high density, good tensile strength, excellent thermal conductivity, and the ability to form complex ions, making them suitable for structural applications. Light-weight metals have relatively low density and high strength-to-weight ratios, which make them suitable for weight-sensitive aerospace and automobile transportation applications. Generally, Mg (923K), Al (933K), and Ti (1941K) are lightweight metals of significant importance in HEAs. As a standard, an HEA is considered lightweight if its density is less than 7g/cm3 [21,22]. Precious functional metals such as Pt (2042K), Au (1337K), and Pd (1828K) are particularly suitable for catalytic applications. Interstitial compounds, also known as transition compounds, are formed when small atoms of B, Si, C, N, or O are trapped within the crystal lattices of metals, forming borides, silicides, carbides, nitrides, and oxide variants of HEAs. They are promising alloy materials for thin-film and bulk coatings to enhance protection from wear damage and diffusion barriers.

The enormous compositional space of HEAs results from their ability to leverage the synergistic combination of elements, which also guides their choice of compositions. Notably, these elements have varying atomic sizes, electronegativities, melting points, densities, thermal conductivities, atomic weights, and chemical behaviors. The atomic size [23,24] of the elements influences the lattice structure and phase formation in HEAs. Elements with significantly different atomic sizes can disrupt the regularity of crystal lattices, create solid-solution phases, and improve mechanical properties. Elements with different electronegativities are selected to create a cocktail of metallic bonds.

Crystal structures in high entropy alloys

The crystal structures commonly observed in HEAs are FCC, BCC, and HCP [25,26]. These structures are fundamental to understanding the properties and behavior of HEAs. In the Strukturbericht notation, a system designed for classifying crystal structures, the disordered phases of FCC, BCC, and HCP are designated as A1, A2, and A3, respectively. The corresponding ordered phases are denoted as L12, B2, and D019. This classification system helps researchers and metallurgists to quickly identify and communicate about specific crystal structures in HEAs.

The distinction between disordered and ordered phases is crucial in HEAs. Disordered phases (A1, A2, A3) are characterized by a random distribution of different elements on the lattice sites, which is a key feature of HEAs contributing to their unique properties. Ordered phases (L12, B2, D019), on the other hand, exhibit a more regular arrangement of atoms, where specific lattice sites are preferentially occupied by certain elements. The presence and proportion of these different phases can significantly influence the mechanical, thermal, and chemical properties of HEAs, making the study of crystal structures essential for tailoring these alloys for specific applications in fields such as aerospace, energy, and advanced manufacturing.

FCC structure, designated as A1 in its disordered form, is characterized by atoms located at the corners of a cube and at the center of each face. This arrangement results in a close-packed structure with high ductility and formability. The ordered L12 structure, often observed in FCC-based HEAs, has a specific arrangement of atoms where one element occupies the corner positions while another element occupies the face-center positions. This ordering can lead to increased strength and improved high-temperature stability in HEAs. BCC structure, denoted as A2 in its disordered state, features atoms at the corners of a cube with an additional atom at the center.

BCC structures generally exhibit higher strength but lower ductility compared to FCC structures. The ordered B2 structure, common in BCC-based HEAs, is characterized by two interpenetrating simple cubic lattices, each occupied by different elements. This ordering can significantly enhance the mechanical properties and thermal stability of HEAs.

HCP structure, labeled A3 in its disordered form, consists of alternating layers of atoms arranged in a hexagonal pattern. HCP structures often provide a balance between strength and ductility. The ordered D019 structure, observed in some HCP-based HEAs, features a specific arrangement of atoms within the hexagonal layers, which can lead to unique mechanical and physical properties.

The formation and stability of these crystal structures in HEAs depend on various factors, including the composition of the alloy, processing conditions, and thermodynamic parameters. The complex interplay between multiple elements in HEAs can lead to the coexistence of different phases or the formation of metastable structures. This complexity offers opportunities for tailoring the microstructure and properties of HEAs through careful composition design and processing techniques.

Understanding the relationship between crystal structures and properties is crucial for the development of HEAs with enhanced performance. HEAs exhibit a diverse range of crystal structures beyond the basic FCC, BCC, and HCP arrangements. While these three structures are the most common in HEAs [27], research has shown that more complex structures can be achieved through various processing methods and compositional adjustments. Complex intermetallic phases, quasicrystalline structures, and nanocrystalline phases have also been observed in certain HEA systems. Liu et al. [28] discusses the strategy of precipitation strengthening in HEAs, where nanoscale precipitates are formed within the FCC matrix to enhance the alloy's strength. This approach takes advantage of the multiple principal elements and slow diffusion effect in HEAs to create fine and stable nanoprecipitates, significantly improving the alloy's mechanical properties. Jiang et al. [29] reports the formation of a nanoscale hierarchical dual-phase structure in a nanocrystalline NiFeCoCrCu HEA film through ion irradiation. The original single-phase FCC structure partially transforms into alternating nanometer layers of a BCC structure, following the Nishiyama-Wasserman orientation relationship. This demonstrates the potential for creating complex, multi-phase structures in HEAs through controlled processing techniques.

Beyond traditional alloys: the rise of high-entropy alloys

The uniqueness of HEAs is evident in every facet of material science: properties, structure, processing, performance, and applications. HEAs, which are composed of multiple elements in equiatomic or near-equiatomic ratios, offer a promising alternative to conventional alloys because of their outstanding combinatorial properties [30,31]. Fig. 2.3 compares HEAs and traditional alloys, also known as conventional alloys. The traditional alloy typically consists of a dominant base element (B consisting of 47 atoms) with minor additions of other elements (C, D, E). In contrast, the HEA showcases a multi-component composition with elements A, B, C, D, E, and F present in approximately equal proportions.

Contrasting the microstructures of HEAs and Traditional alloys.

While conventional alloys often rely on a dominant base element with minor additions, HEAs introduce a new paradigm with their multi-component principal elements. This complexity leads to novel phenomena and properties that challenge traditional theories. Unlike conventional alloys, which are governed by well-established principles of phase diagrams and metallurgical phenomena, HEAs exhibit less predictable behaviors. A phase diagram is a geometric representation of a system at thermal equilibrium. This serves as the foundation for investigating solidification, phase transformation, crystal growth, and solid-phase transformation. The intricate interactions between multiple principal elements create unique microstructures and properties that are not easily understood or controlled using conventional methods.

High-entropy alloys have emerged as a promising class of materials with unique properties and potential applications. Characterized by their multi-component composition and high configurational entropy, HEAs offer a departure from conventional alloys. The high configurational entropy arising from the multiple principal elements can stabilize crystal structures that would otherwise be unstable in conventional alloys. This entropy-driven stabilization allows for the formation of solid solutions with FCC, BCC, or even FCC + BCC dual-phase structures that are often not observed in conventional alloys. Dual-phase HEAs, typically composed of FCC and BCC phases, leverage the complementary properties of each phase to achieve a balance of strength and ductility. The FCC phase contributes to ductility and toughness, while the BCC phase provides enhanced strength and hardness. This combination often results in HEAs with superior mechanical properties compared to their single-phase counterparts, making them suitable for applications demanding both strength and toughness. Multi-phase HEAs, with three or more phases present, can exhibit even more complex and tailored properties. While HEAs are often associated with single-phase solid solutions, intermetallic compounds can also form in certain HEA compositions. However, the nature and stability of these intermetallic compounds in HEAs can differ significantly from those in conventional alloys. The high configurational entropy of HEAs can stabilize intermetallic compounds that would be unstable in conventional alloys, leading to unique and complex microstructures. The specific combination of principal elements in an HEA can also influence the stability of different crystal structures. Certain combinations of elements may favor the formation of FCC structures, while others may promote BCC or FCC + BCC structures. Additionally, the relative concentrations of the principal elements can affect the stability of intermetallic compounds and other secondary phases. Processing conditions, such as cooling rate and annealing, can also play a role in determining the structure of HEAs. By controlling these factors, researchers can manipulate the formation and stability of different phases within the alloy. To better understand and classify HEAs, various researchers have proposed different definitions and criteria as shown in Fig. 2.4.

Classifying high-entropy alloys by mixing entropy.

Miracle et al. [22] defined HEAs to be alloys with ΔSConf≥1.5R. Yeh [32] established a specific condition for classifying the different degrees of HEAs by the number of component elements in the alloy system. Low Entropy Alloys (LEAs) have a mixing entropy of ΔSmix<1R, Medium Entropy Alloys (MEAs) have a mixing entropy within the range of 1R<ΔSmix<1.5R, and high-entropy alloys (HEAs) must have a mixing entropy of ΔSmix≥1.5R. MEAs typically have fewer constituent elements than HEAs and often exhibit simpler microstructures. This can lead to improved processability and reduced manufacturing costs compared to HEAs with more complex microstructures.

Classification of high entropy alloys

Single-phase HEAs are characterized by their distinct crystal structures: FCC, BCC, and HCP [33]. These structures determine the alloy's properties and behavior. In contrast, multi-phase HEAs exhibit a more complex microstructure, featuring two or more phases coexisting within the alloy. This category includes dual-phase alloys and those with three or more phases, each contributing to the overall properties of the material. The compositional classification of HEAs is based on the types of metals they incorporate, ranging from 3D transition metals to refractory metals, light metals, and even rare earth elements. This diversity in composition allows for a wide range of potential applications and properties.

Functional and structural HEAs represent two major categories based on their intended applications and properties. Functional HEAs are designed for specific purposes, such as magnetic applications, superconductivity, or shape memory effects. Structural HEAs, on the other hand, are developed for their mechanical properties, including balanced strength and ductility, high-temperature performance, and cryogenic applications. Interstitial HEAs incorporate small atoms like carbon, nitrogen, or boron into the lattice structure to enhance specific characteristics. The concept of Complex Concentrated Alloys (CCAs) broadens the definition of HEAs to include non-equiatomic compositions and medium-high entropy alloys, offering more flexibility in alloy design. Eutectic HEAs are particularly notable for their unique microstructural patterns, such as lamella and rod-like structures, which can significantly influence the alloy's properties and performance.

The introduction of Eutectic High Entropy Alloys (EHEAs) by Yu et al. [34] marked a significant advancement in the field of high-temperature alloys. By combining the principles of eutectic alloys with high entropy alloys, they developed a novel system that demonstrated superior mechanical properties compared to conventional single-phased HEAs. The as-cast AlCoCrFeNi2.1 alloy, with its FCC/B2 lamellar microstructure, exhibited an impressive fracture strength of 944 MPa and a strain of 25.6 %. Further improvements in mechanical properties were achieved through various processing techniques, such as rolling and annealing/aging. For instance, Wani et al. [35] reported that cold rolling followed by annealing resulted in an ultimate strength of 1.2 GPa with 12 % total elongation, while Liu et al. [36], demonstrated that hot rolling could increase the ultimate strength to 1.3 GPa with 18.5 % total elongation [34]. While Yu et al.'s AlCoCrFeNi2.1 alloy demonstrated impressive mechanical properties, its commercial viability was hindered by high manufacturing costs due to the cost of Co within, which is approximately six times higher than the other elements [[37], [38], [39]]. Recent research has focused on developing cobalt-free compositions. Jin et al. [40] created Al0.8CrFeNi2.2 using vacuum-arc melting, maintaining the desirable lamellar eutectic microstructure and good balance between strength and ductility. This development represents a promising step towards more cost-effective EHEAs with exceptional mechanical properties, potentially expanding their industrial applications. The AlCoCrFeNi2.1 EHEA exhibits exceptional mechanical properties, surpassing conventional high-temperature alloys [41]. Its unique microstructure, consisting of alternating soft FCC and hard BCC phases, contributes to superior ductility at room temperature and enhanced strength at elevated temperatures. The alloy's complex deformation mechanisms involve dislocation movement through the alternating FCC and BCC phases. The softer FCC phase allows dislocation movement, while the harder BCC phase acts as a barrier, causing dislocation pile-up [42]. This interaction leads to significant strain hardening, with dislocations accumulating within the FCC phase and at FCC-BCC interfaces. Nanoscale BCC structures within the FCC phase further enhance this hardening effect. As a result, the AlCoCrFeNi2.1 EHEA demonstrates an impressive combination of strength and ductility, with potential for ultrahigh strength after large plastic deformation. These properties make it a promising candidate for high-temperature applications requiring both high strength and formability. The tribological performance of Eutectic High-Entropy Alloys (EHEAs) is influenced by multiple factors, including thermal-softening resistance, phase transformation, oxidation behaviors, and tribolayer formation. Among these, tribolayer formation and temperature-dependent hardness are particularly crucial. Yu et al. [34] demonstrated that CoCrFeNiNbx EHEAs exhibit exceptional thermal-softening resistance compared to conventional alloys, making them promising for high-temperature tribological applications where traditional alloys may fail.

Eutectic High-Entropy Alloys (EHEAs) offer unique advantages for high-temperature applications due to their distinctive solidification structure. Key features include low energy phase boundaries, controllable near-equilibrium microstructures, stable defect structures, and high rupture strength. Their regular eutectic organization contributes to excellent creep resistance and castability, making them suitable for complex high-temperature components [34]. Unlike conventional HEAs, EHEAs lack a solidification temperature range, minimizing segregation and shrinkage cavities. This results in improved structural integrity and performance consistency. EHEAs with dual FCC and BCC phases are expected to outperform some conventional HEAs under extreme temperature conditions. These properties make EHEAs promising for aerospace, power generation, and advanced manufacturing applications requiring materials that maintain integrity at high temperatures.

However, EHEA development faces challenges due to compositional complexity, which complicates phase diagram construction and traditional design approaches. To address these issues, researchers employ computational modeling, high-throughput experimentation, and machine learning techniques to predict phase formation, estimate properties, and identify promising compositions. Ongoing efforts focus on refining these methods and developing more accurate predictive tools for EHEA design and optimization. One approach to designing nanostructured EHEAs involves utilizing parameters such as valence electron concentration (VEC) and mixing enthalpy (△Hmix) in a pseudo-binary method. This technique aims to create alloys with ordered body-centered cubic (B2) and face-centered cubic (FCC) phases [44]. Another strategy combines simple mixing enthalpy calculations with computed phase diagrams based on a high-entropy alloy database. This method employs the Scheil model to analyze solidification paths, providing insights into the alloy formation process [45].

Researchers have also developed empirical mathematical rules to expedite the design of AlCoCrFeNi EHEAs, offering a more streamlined approach to alloy development [46]. In recent years, there has been a shift towards more advanced and data-driven approaches in EHEA design. Machine learning (ML) based modeling has emerged as a promising technique to accelerate the search for EHEAs and overcome the limitations of traditional empirical methods [47]. This approach leverages large datasets and complex algorithms to predict alloy properties and compositions more efficiently. Additionally, researchers have proposed a strategy based on an infinite solid solution and pseudo-ternary model to design triple-phase eutectic high-entropy alloys (TEHEAs) with more than seven elements [48].This innovative approach expands the possibilities for creating complex, multi-element alloys with enhanced properties, potentially opening new avenues for materials science and engineering applications.

Recent research has highlighted the development of innovative EHEAs with exceptional properties. Wang et al. [49] introduced an AlCr1.3TiNi2 EHEA with a low density and remarkable mechanical performance at elevated temperatures. This alloy outperformed most refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), EHEAs, and traditional Ni- and Ti-based alloys in terms of room- and high-temperature hardness and specific yield strength. In another study, Jin et al. [50] developed a Fe20Co20Ni41Al19 EHEA with a nano-lamellar eutectic microstructure. This alloy exhibited outstanding tensile strength and elongation, surpassing many as-cast high-entropy alloys. Alloying elements have been used recently to tweak the properties of EHEAs. Study by Yang et al. [51] found that adding molybdenum (Mo) to a Co30Cr10Fe10Al18Ni32-xMox alloy resulted in solid solution strengthening. The alloy containing 2 at.% Mo exhibited the highest fracture tensile strength (1250 MPa) and maintained acceptable elongation (14 %). Additionally, the development of Cr-free EHEAs, such as the (CoFe2NiV0.5Mo0.2)100-xNbx system, has shown promising results in combining mechanical and corrosion properties [52].

Revisiting four core effects in high entropy alloys

High entropy alloys have emerged as a promising class of materials with unique properties and potential applications. In 2006, Yeh et al. [6] proposed the core fore effects (high entropy, sluggish diffusion, severe lattice distortion, and cocktail effect) as a fundamental concept to guide the understanding and mechanism of HEAs, which distinguish them from conventional alloys. These four core effects [53] are interconnected to give an understanding of the fundamental mechanisms upon which HEAs are built.

As illustrated in Fig. 2.7, the four core effects are interrelated and contribute to the unique properties of HEAs. Therefore, they are crucial for advancing the field of HEAs, pushing materials science boundaries, and unlocking new possibilities in advanced materials research.

Four core effects of high entropy alloys.

High entropy effects

High entropy in multicomponent alloys promotes simple solid solution phases at higher temperatures due to increased configurational entropy, reducing Gibbs free energy. As temperature rises, the entropic term (-TΔS) dominates, favoring disorder over ordered intermetallic compounds or phase separation. The most stable phase at specific conditions is determined by minimizing Gibbs free energy (Equation (2.1)). This principle allows researchers to predict phase stability, transformations, and equilibrium states in multicomponent alloys, enabling the design of high-entropy alloys with tailored properties. Understanding these thermodynamic principles is crucial for optimizing processing conditions and predicting alloy behavior under various environments.

The solidification of High Entropy Alloys is complex, with melting points of principal elements significantly influencing phase formation and microstructure. While high mixing entropy promotes disordered solid solutions at elevated temperatures [54], ordered intermetallic compounds may form if formation enthalpy overcomes entropic effects. Increased temperature improves mutual solubility among principal elements, favoring thermodynamically stable solid solution phases and reducing the number of phases present [55,56].The interplay between mixing entropy, formation enthalpy, and mutual solubility determines the phase composition and microstructural evolution of HEAs during solidification and cooling. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for tailoring HEA properties and developing new alloy compositions.

Sluggish diffusion

The unique atomic structure and composition of HEAs contribute to their slower diffusion rates and phase transformation kinetics compared to conventional alloys [57]. In HEAs, the local atomic environment is highly diverse, with each lattice site surrounded by a distinct arrangement of different elements. This heterogeneity leads to variations in bonding strengths and local energy levels across the material. When an atom moves into a vacancy, it encounters a significantly different neighborhood than its original position, unlike in conventional alloys where the local atomic configuration remains largely unchanged during vacancy jumps [58]. The complex energy landscape can trap atoms in low-energy sites, reducing their mobility and ability to diffuse further. Conversely, high-energy sites may cause atoms to return to their original positions more frequently. These factors collectively hinder the diffusion process in HEAs, resulting in slower kinetics compared to conventional alloys with low solute concentrations. The reduced diffusion rates and slower phase transformation kinetics in HEAs have important implications for their microstructural stability, mechanical properties, and potential applications in extreme environments where resistance to diffusion-related phenomena is crucial [59].

The study by Tsai et al. [60] utilizing a seven-bond model to investigate the effects of local energy fluctuations on diffusion has provided valuable insights into the behavior of atoms in high-entropy alloys (HEAs). Their findings reveal that Ni atoms in single-phase FCC structures of CoCrFeMnNi alloys experience an average potential energy difference of 60.3 meV between lattice sites. This significant energy difference highlights the complex atomic interactions within HEAs and their impact on diffusion processes. The comparison between pure metals, traditional alloys, and HEAs further emphasizes the unique characteristics of HEAs. While pure metals exhibit no measurable difference (MD) in potential energy between lattice sites, HEAs demonstrate the highest MD values. Specifically, the energy difference observed in CoCrFeMnNi alloys is 50 % greater than that found in Fe-Cr-Ni alloys, as illustrated in Fig. 2.8. This substantial increase in energy fluctuations within HEAs contributes to their distinct properties, including enhanced diffusion rates and improved mechanical performance. These findings underscore the importance of understanding local energy landscapes in multi-component alloy systems and their role in determining macroscopic material properties.

Investigations involving diffusion couples have revealed that the activation energy for diffusion in CoCrFeMnNi alloys is higher than in pure elements and other FCC ternary alloys. This increased activation energy leads to longer occupancy times at low-energy sites due to a greater energy difference compared to high-energy sites.

Within HEAs, diffusion rates vary among elements, with those having high melting temperatures generally exhibiting lower activity and reduced likelihood of occupying voids or moving into vacancies. These diffusion characteristics significantly impact phase transformations, which often require the simultaneous diffusion of multiple elements to achieve the desired composition in newly forming phases. The slow kinetics observed in HEAs have several important implications for their properties and applications [61]. This sluggish diffusion enables the formation of nano-sized precipitates even in the solid state, contributing to the alloy's strength and stability [62,63]. HEA coatings have shown improved performance as diffusion barriers, enhancing high-temperature strength and structural stability. Additionally, the slow diffusion kinetics in HEAs contribute to their high creep resistance, making them suitable for applications requiring long-term stability under elevated temperatures and stresses. The complex interplay between multiple elements in HEAs not only affects their diffusion behavior [64,65] but also influences grain boundary migration and overall phase transformation processes, ultimately determining the alloy's microstructure and properties.

Severe lattice distortion

High entropy alloys are a unique class of materials characterized by their multi-component composition and complex microstructure. The distinctive feature of HEAs is the presence of severe lattice distortion, which arises from the co-existence of various principal elements. This distortion creates a state of "ordered chaos" at the atomic level, where larger atoms push neighboring atoms outward while smaller atoms create voids or regions of lower atomic density. This distortion is primarily due to variations in atomic size, bond energies, and crystal structures among the constituent elements. As illustrated in Fig. 2.9(a–c), the effects of lattice distortion can be observed in the comparison of lattice structures between pure metals, conventional alloys, and high entropy alloys.

(a–c). Lattice distortion in (a) pure metal, (b) conventional alloy, and (c) high entropy alloy crystal structures.

The crystal structures show the progression from simple to complex atomic arrangements in metallic materials. Pure metals, as shown in Fig. 2.9 (a), exhibit a highly ordered and uniform crystal structure. This regularity stems from the identical nature of all atoms present, resulting in consistent interatomic forces and spacing. While this structure provides certain advantages, such as high electrical and thermal conductivity, it also limits the material's ability to resist deformation under stress, as there are fewer obstacles to dislocation movement. Conventional alloys, illustrated in Fig. 2.9 (b), represent a step towards increased complexity and enhanced mechanical properties. By introducing different elements into the crystal lattice, these alloys create a more diverse atomic environment. This diversity leads to variations in atomic size, electronegativity, and bonding characteristics, which can impede dislocation movement and strengthen the material. The deliberate selection of alloying elements allows for the fine-tuning of properties such as strength, ductility, and corrosion resistance, making conventional alloys suitable for a wide range of engineering applications. HEAs, depicted in Fig. 2.9 (c), take this concept even further by incorporating multiple principal elements in near-equiatomic proportions, resulting in a highly complex and disordered crystal structure that offers unique properties and potential for advanced materials development in various fields requiring materials with enhanced mechanical, thermal, and chemical properties.

Lattice distortion in HEAs is a complex phenomenon that arises from the interplay of multiple elements within the alloy structure. Unlike binary alloys, which can experience severe lattice distortion due to large atomic size differences, HEAs often exhibit a more moderate level of distortion due to the averaging effect of multiple constituent elements. This unique characteristic is attributed to the random distribution of atoms with varying sizes throughout the crystal structure. The extent of lattice distortion in HEAs is influenced by several factors, including atomic size differences, bonding characteristics, and electronic structure of the constituent elements. Larger disparities in atomic sizes generally contribute to increased lattice distortion, while the nature of interatomic bonding and electronic properties of the elements can further modulate this effect. To mitigate the potential negative impacts of lattice distortion, HEAs tend to form disordered, high-symmetry phases such as FCC or BCC structures. These phases are better equipped to accommodate the random distribution of differently sized atoms, thereby minimizing overall lattice distortion. However, studies have shown that severe lattice distortion can still significantly affect the characteristics of HEAs, particularly in BCC structures [66]. The presence of multiple phases and grain boundaries in HEAs can also help absorb and distribute the stresses caused by lattice distortion. This structural adaptation mechanism contributes to the unique mechanical properties of HEAs, including their strength, ductility, and toughness. Furthermore, lattice distortion plays a crucial role in determining the stability of different phases within the alloy and can influence its corrosion behavior, making it a critical factor in the design and optimization of HEAs for specific applications.

Cocktail effects

The cocktail effect in HEAs represents a fascinating phenomenon that goes beyond the simple additive properties of individual elements. This synergistic interaction among diverse atomic species results in emergent properties that can be significantly different from, and often superior to, those of traditional alloys. The complex atomic arrangements and interactions in HEAs lead to unique electronic, magnetic, and phononic structures, which in turn contribute to their exceptional characteristics. The complex interactions among multiple principal elements involve mechanisms such as solid solution strengthening, entropy-driven stabilization, and synergistic interactions, leading to improved mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, and high-temperature performance. The cocktail effect for metallic alloys was first described by Ranganathan and its mechanical and physical features have since been proven [67].

The multi-principal elements in Fig. 2.10 showcase diverse properties that synergistically contribute to HEAs. Aluminum and copper represent extremes, with aluminum offering lightness and potential ductility, while copper provides density, conductivity, and strength. Other elements like chromium, iron, and nickel offer intermediate properties. The cocktail effect in HEAs leverages this diversity, allowing precise compositional adjustments to tailor overall characteristics. By carefully selecting element proportions, researchers can fine-tune properties such as strength, ductility, corrosion resistance, and thermal stability. This flexibility enables the design of novel alloys with unique property combinations unattainable in traditional systems, opening new possibilities for material design across various industries.

Recent advancement on the “four core effects” of high entropy alloys

The "four core effects" in high-entropy alloys have been subject to scrutiny and re-evaluation in recent years. While these effects initially provided a framework for understanding HEAs, researchers have recognized the need for a more nuanced and comprehensive approach to fully describe the underlying mechanisms applicable across different HEA classes [57]. This re-assessment has led to focused investigations in specific areas, including phase selection, the strength-ductility tradeoff, corrosion resistance, and anomalous slow diffusion kinetics [68,69]. Despite these challenges to the original framework, it is important to note that certain fundamental strengthening mechanisms, such as solid solution strengthening, precipitation hardening, and sluggish diffusion, continue to play crucial roles in determining the properties of HEAs. To gain a deeper understanding of HEAs, researchers are now emphasizing the complex interplay between elemental interactions and processing conditions. Elemental interactions, characterized by factors such as atomic size differences, valence electron concentration, and formation enthalpies, significantly influence the alloy's microstructure and properties. Simultaneously, processing conditions, including temperature, pressure, and heat treatment, can dramatically affect the formation of strengthening precipitates and the extent of solid solution strengthening. This multifaceted approach to studying HEAs acknowledges the intricate relationships between composition, processing, and final properties, paving the way for more accurate predictions and tailored design of these advanced materials for specific applications.

Traditionally, configurational entropy has been the cornerstone for designing HEAs. This concept, calculated based on the assumption of random element mixing, has been instrumental in understanding their behavior. However, recent research in HEA literature is pushing the boundaries of this concept into intricate atomic arrangements involving the spatial distribution of elements and the influence of their local atomic preferences. The presence of short-range order (SRO), where specific elements exhibit a higher probability of neighboring each other, is now factored into the calculations. Recent findings prove that the maximized entropy theory alone cannot describe phase selection in HEAs [70] as other factors like mixing enthalpy and atomic size differences play a significant role. Insufficient evidence exists to establish the extent of lattice strain in all HEAs, which varies depending on the specific element combination and processing conditions.

The phenomenon of “sluggish diffusion” due to high entropy and complex microstructures is currently being reconsidered, as it is not valid across all compositions and conditions in HEAs. Also, rapid precipitation observed in some HEAs contradicts the initial notion of sluggish diffusion kinetics being a universal characteristic. Further studies into diffusion mechanisms at elevated temperatures show that the phenomenon of sluggish diffusion of HEAs is less pronounced at higher temperatures. Anti-sluggish diffusion has been observed in Ti diffusion of HCP HfTiZr and AlScHfTiZr reported by Sen et al. [71] to exceed the rates predicted by models on pure metals (see Fig. 2.11).

The diffusion properties of HEAs are intricately linked to their potential energy landscape, which is highly sensitive to temperature and compositional variations. This relationship, as highlighted by Xu et al. [72], underscores the complex nature of atomic movement within these multi-component systems. The potential energy landscape in HEAs is characterized by numerous local minima and energy barriers, which can significantly influence the diffusion pathways and rates of atomic species. As temperature increases, the thermal energy available to atoms allows them to overcome these energy barriers more easily, potentially altering the diffusion behavior in ways that may not be directly extrapolated from low-temperature observations.

The work of Zhang et al. [73] on HfTiZrNbTa and HfTiZrNbV BCC refractory HEAs provides further insights into the unique diffusion characteristics of these materials at elevated temperatures. Their findings reveal that the diffusion rates in these HEAs can exceed the geometric mean of the diffusivities observed in their constituent pure elements. This enhanced diffusion is attributed to several factors inherent to the HEA structure: the chemical complexity arising from the presence of multiple principal elements, the atomic size mismatch between these elements, and the phonon broadening effect. The latter refers to the broadening of phonon spectra due to the presence of atoms with different masses and interatomic forces, which can influence lattice vibrations and, consequently, atomic mobility. These factors collectively contribute to a diffusion environment that is distinctly different from that of conventional alloys, highlighting the need for specialized models and experimental approaches to fully understand and predict diffusion behavior in HEAs, particularly at high temperatures.

Recent research on HEAs has shifted from attributing corrosion resistance solely to high entropy and cocktail effects to examining specific compositional and microstructural factors [[74], [75], [76]]. Studies now focus on element interactions, passive film formation, and grain boundary roles in corrosion processes. This deeper understanding aids in designing HEAs with enhanced corrosion resistance for specific applications. Simultaneously, investigations into HEAs' mechanical properties have led to the proposal of a new mechanism: high slip-plane density. This concept suggests that HEAs' complex crystal structures provide numerous slip planes for dislocation movement, enhancing plasticity and work hardening. Combined with solid solution strengthening and nano-precipitation, this mechanism contributes to HEAs' exceptional strength and toughness. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for tailoring HEAs to specific structural applications requiring a balance of strength, toughness, and corrosion resistance.

Advanced characterization techniques like high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atom probe tomography (APT) have revolutionized HEAs research by providing unprecedented insights into their nanoscale microstructure and chemical composition [77,78]. The combination of TEM and APT overcomes individual limitations, offering a comprehensive understanding of material structure and composition. TEM provides detailed information about crystal structure, defects, and phase distributions, while APT excels in three-dimensional chemical mapping with near-atomic resolution, surpassing energy-filtered TEM (EFTEM) in chemical analysis precision [79]. This complementary approach has led to significant discoveries, such as identifying nano-scale core-shell precipitates contributing to material strength [80]. By combining these techniques, researchers can better understand complex microstructures and compositional variations in HEAs, essential for developing new alloys with enhanced properties and performance.

Machine learning is revolutionizing high-entropy alloy exploration and design by enabling rapid screening of compositional spaces and property prediction. Zhang et al. [81] demonstrated how these techniques can tailor HEA yield strength. Machine learning algorithms, trained on extensive datasets, efficiently identify promising HEA candidates, reducing time and resources compared to experimental approaches. HEA composition and processing conditions determine strengthening mechanisms and performance. Machine learning models capture complex relationships between composition, processing, microstructure, and properties, enabling accurate predictions and insights into underlying physical phenomena. This approach accelerates HEA discovery and deepens understanding of structure-property relationships, allowing researchers to design HEAs with tailored properties for specific applications, such as aerospace components or energy systems.

HEAs offer vast potential across applications due to their compositional flexibility. For structural uses, understanding atomic size differences, dislocation behavior, and precipitation hardening is key to achieving exceptional strength. Lightweight applications focus on low-density elements and innovative processing [82,83]. In functional materials, tailoring electronic structure through valence electron concentration enables specific electrical or magnetic properties, opening avenues for catalytic and sensing applications [84,85]. HEAs' ability to combine multiple elements in near-equiatomic proportions creates unique property combinations, making them promising for extreme environments like aerospace, nuclear reactors, and high-temperature industrial processes.

Enthalpy of mixing

Enthalpy represents the heat absorbed or released during the mixing of elements to form an alloy. The mixing enthalpy greatly impacts the stability of phases in HEAs. Solid solutions form more easily when the mixing enthalpy has a low absolute value. This, in turn, enhances the stability of HEAs. Alternatively, a high mixing enthalpy can lead to phase separation or the formation of intermetallic compounds. This can occur when the enthalpy of mixing is sufficiently negative to overcome the entropy stabilization. Depending on the specific alloy system, both entropy and enthalpy can contribute to phase stability. Despite the possibility of phase separation, HEAs can still exhibit unique and desirable properties, such as high strength, ductility, and corrosion resistance. When designing a HEA, it is beneficial to choose elements that have similar atomic sizes and compatible crystal structures to minimize the enthalpy of mixing.

Significant efforts have been made to develop empirical parameters for predicting the formation of single-phase, solid-solution HEAs. Zhang et al. [106] and Guo et al. [107] both studied the impact of mixing enthalpy, mixing entropy, and atomic size differences on phase formation in HEAs and came to similar conclusions. Guo et al. [108,109] proposed using parameters ΔHmix,ΔSmix,andδ to identify solid solution formation. The suggested conditions for the formation of the solid solution HEA are −22≤ΔHmix≤7kJ/mol, 11≤ΔSmix≤19.5J/Kmol, and 0≤δ≤6.4%. BMG will form if the values of ΔHmix,ΔSmix,andδ satisfy the following conditions: −49≤ΔHmix≤25.5kJ/mol, 7≤ΔSmix≤16J/Kmol, and δ≤9%. Zhang et al. [106] demonstrated that alloys can form simple solid solutions under the following conditions: −20≤ΔHmix≤5kJ/mol, 12≤ΔSmix≤17.5J/Kmol, and δ≤6.4%. Zhang et al. Strengthening mechanisms in high entropy alloys

HEAs exhibit exceptional mechanical properties due to various strengthening mechanisms. Understanding the mechanisms that contribute to the strength and toughness of HEAs is crucial for optimizing their design and performance.

Precipitation strengthening

Precipitation strengthening involves the formation of secondary phases within the HEA matrix, which act as obstacles to dislocation movement. Precipitation strengthening is a highly effective method for enhancing the mechanical properties of HEAs by introducing secondary phases that impede dislocation motion. This technique has been successfully applied to various HEA systems, resulting in improved strength-ductility combination. The formation of precipitates can be achieved through careful control of thermo-mechanical processes, such as solution annealing, cold-rolling, and aging treatments. Different types of precipitates have been observed in HEAs, including L12, B2, and NbC phases, each contributing to strengthening through various mechanisms like Orowan strengthening, coherency strengthening, and ordering strengthening [[119], [120], [121]]. The effectiveness of precipitation strengthening in HEAs depends on several factors such as precipitate morphology, size, distribution, and volume fraction. For instance, dual-morphology B2 precipitates in a Co36Cr15Fe18Ni18Al8Ti4Mo1 MPEA resulted in significant improvements in yield strength and ultimate tensile strength, reaching up to 1120 MPa and 1480 MPa, respectively [120]. Additionally, the interplay between multiple strengthening mechanisms, including Hall-Petch strengthening and precipitation strengthening, can lead to complex relationships between microstructure and mechanical properties [122]. The careful control of precipitation kinetics and thermomechanical processing can optimize the precipitate structure for maximum strengthening effect. Coherent precipitates, which maintain a continuous crystal structure with the matrix, can be particularly effective in impeding dislocation motion. Incoherent precipitates, while not maintaining lattice continuity with the matrix, can also contribute significantly to strengthening by creating local stress fields and physical barriers to dislocation movement.

Grain boundary strengthening

Grain boundary strengthening, also known as Hall-Petch strengthening, relies on the presence of multiple fine-grained microstructures within the alloy. The grain boundaries serve as barriers to dislocation motion, and as the grain size decreases, the number of these barriers increases, leading to enhanced strength and hardness. The Hall-Petch relationship quantifies this effect, showing that the yield strength of a material is inversely proportional to the square root of its grain size [123]. As grain sizes approach the nanometer range, a breakdown of the Hall-Petch relation can occur, leading to softening mechanisms in some materials [124,125]. This breakdown is particularly complex in nanocrystalline alloys, where grain boundary solute segregation can stabilize nanosized grains and influence interfacial plasticity. To overcome these limitations, innovative approaches such as introducing nanotwins or replacing grain boundaries with graphene in nanocomposites have shown promise in enhancing strength beyond traditional Hall-Petch limitations [126,127]. In HEAs, the complex composition and processing routes can lead to unique grain structures, further enhancing the effectiveness of this strengthening mechanism.

Phase transformation strengthening

Phase transformation strengthening in HEAs is a dynamic process that occurs when the material undergoes a structural change. One common example is martensitic transformation, where the crystal structure changes from a high-temperature phase (typically austenite) to a low-temperature phase (martensite). This transformation introduces additional obstacles to dislocation motion in the form of phase boundaries or newly precipitated strengthening phases. The martensitic transformation can be induced by various factors, including temperature changes, applied stress, or a combination of both. The resulting microstructure often contains a high density of dislocations and twin boundaries, which contribute to the overall strength of the alloy.

In some HEAs, the phase transformation can be reversible, leading to shape memory effects or superelasticity. In the CrMnFeCoNi alloy system, researchers have demonstrated the ability to design HEAs with high-temperature shape memory effects by tuning the composition to increase the thermodynamic equilibrium temperature between FCC and HCP phases [128]. This compositional adjustment leads to thermally- and stress-induced martensitic transformations, resulting in shape recovery temperatures as high as 698 K in non-equiatomic CrMnFeCoNi alloys.

Solid-solution strengthening

This is a fundamental mechanism in HEAs and is particularly effective due to the multi-element composition of these alloys. This strengthening arises from the presence of multiple elements with different atomic sizes occupying lattice sites in the crystal structure [129,130]. The size mismatch between atoms creates local distortions in the crystal lattice, resulting in a complex stress field throughout the material. These distortions impede dislocation movement, contributing to increased strength and hardness. In HEAs, the effectiveness of solid-solution strengthening is often more pronounced than in conventional alloys due to the higher number of alloying elements and their more uniform distribution throughout the lattice. The severe lattice distortion in HEAs can lead to a "cocktail effect," where the properties of the alloy exceed those predicted by simple rule-of-mixtures calculations.

The interplay between these strengthening mechanisms in HEAs can lead to synergistic effects, resulting in mechanical properties that surpass those of traditional alloys. For instance, the combination of solid-solution strengthening and precipitation hardening can create a matrix that is inherently strong and further reinforced by finely dispersed precipitates. Similarly, the interaction between grain boundary strengthening and phase transformation can result in materials with both high strength and good ductility. Understanding and controlling these mechanisms allows materials scientists to design HEAs with tailored properties for specific applications, ranging from aerospace components to nuclear reactor materials.

Strengthening phases in high entropy alloys

HEAs characterized by multiple principal elements exhibit a range of microstructures, including solid solutions and intermetallic phases [131]. The assertion that the overall performance of HEAs is not necessarily compromised by the presence of intermetallic compounds (IMCs) aligns with current advancements in alloys. Researchers have explored various aspects of high entropy alloys, including the evolution of intermetallic compounds within these materials [132]. Studies have shown that the addition of intermetallic compounds can serve as a coherent strengthening phase in high-entropy alloys, thereby enhancing their mechanical properties [[132], [133], [134]].

While the initial premise of HEAs suggested that increased configurational entropy favors the formation of disordered solid solution phases, recent findings indicate that the likelihood of forming solid solution alloys decreases as the number of alloying elements increases [135]. This is because of the increasing probability of element pairs that prefer to form intermetallic compounds. Furthermore, the mechanical properties of HEAs, such as fracture toughness and resistance to shear band formation, are not solely dependent on the avoidance of intermetallic phases but are also a result of intrinsic material behavior, such as work-hardening and twinning [136].

The B2 phase in HEAs is a specifically ordered intermetallic phase with a BCC structure. This phase can be significant in HEAs as it may influence the mechanical properties and phase stability of the alloys. The complexity of phase formation in HEAs is influenced by factors such as entropy, lattice distortion, and the mixing properties of constituent elements [137,138]. The presence of short-range order (SRO) in BCC multi-principal element alloys, as discussed by Zhao (2021), suggests that the B2 phase could be a manifestation of such ordering tendencies. Additionally, the role of computational methods, such as density functional theory (DFT) calculations and Monte Carlo simulations, in predicting the phase stability and properties of HEAs is noteworthy, as these methods could potentially be applied to specifically study the B2 phase [139,140].

In addition, sigma phases (D8b) with tetragonal crystal structures, laves phases with cubic (C14), hexagonal (C15), or hexagonal (C36) structures, and μ phases (D85) with rhombohedral structures are indeed common intermetallic (IM) phases observed in HEAs. These phases play a crucial role in determining the overall properties and performance of HEAs. The formation of these IM phases is often influenced by the composition, processing conditions, and thermal history of the alloy.

Sigma phases, characterized by their complex tetragonal structure, are known for their high hardness and brittleness. They typically form in systems with a significant size difference between constituent elements and can significantly affect the mechanical properties of HEAs. The presence of sigma phases often leads to increased strength but reduced ductility and toughness. In some cases, the formation of sigma phases can be beneficial for wear resistance applications, but their brittle nature can be detrimental in structural applications requiring high impact resistance.

Laves phases, on the other hand, exist in three different crystal structures (C14, C15, and C36) and are often found in HEAs containing elements with large atomic size differences. The C14 structure is hexagonal, while C15 is cubic, and C36 is also hexagonal but with a larger unit cell. The formation of Laves phases is often driven by the atomic size factor and electron concentration. In HEAs, laves phases can provide precipitation strengthening, improving the alloy's strength and creep resistance. However, controlling the volume fraction and distribution of these phases is crucial to maintain a balance between strength and ductility.

The μ phase, with its rhombohedral structure, is less common but can still form in certain HEA compositions, particularly those rich in refractory elements. This phase is often observed in systems containing elements like Mo, W, or Cr. The μ phase can contribute to the overall strength of the alloy but, like other IM phases, can lead to embrittlement if present in large quantities. Its formation is typically favored in alloys with specific electron-to-atom ratios and can be influenced by heat treatment processes.

Understanding the formation, stability, and effects of these IM phases is crucial for designing HEAs with optimal properties for specific applications. The presence and distribution of these phases can be controlled through careful alloy design and processing techniques. For instance, adjusting the composition to alter the valence electron concentration (VEC) or the atomic size difference can influence the formation of specific IM phases. Heat treatment processes, such as solution treatment and aging, can also be employed to modify the phase structure and optimize the mechanical properties of HEAs.

Moreover, the interaction between these IM phases and the matrix in HEAs can lead to complex microstructures and unique property combinations. For example, the presence of finely dispersed IM phases in a ductile matrix can result in an excellent combination of strength and toughness. However, excessive formation of these phases, particularly at grain boundaries, can lead to intergranular fracture and reduced overall performance. Advanced characterization techniques, such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM), atom probe tomography (APT), and synchrotron-based X-ray diffraction, are often employed to study the formation, distribution, and crystal structure of these IM phases in HEAs. These techniques provide valuable insights into the phase evolution and stability under different conditions, aiding in the development of HEAs with tailored properties for specific applications.

Thermo-Calc, a powerful software tool for predicting phase diagrams in multi-component systems, has proven to be invaluable in the study of HEAs [141]. This computational approach allows researchers to predict the formation and stability of various phases within complex alloy compositions. By utilizing thermodynamic databases and advanced algorithms, Thermo-Calc can simulate the behavior of multiple elements interacting simultaneously, providing insights into phase equilibria, solidification pathways, and microstructural evolution. The predictive capabilities of Thermo-Calc extend beyond simple binary or ternary systems, making it particularly suited for the analysis of HEAs, which typically contain five or more principal elements in near-equiatomic proportions [142,143]. This software has been successfully employed to forecast the presence of solid solution phases, intermetallic compounds, and other secondary phases that may form in HEAs under different conditions. Such predictions help guide experimental design, reduce the need for extensive trial-and-error approaches, and accelerate the development of novel HEAs with tailored properties for specific applications.

Computational design of high entropy alloys

HEAs are compositionally complex, and consist of multiple principal elements. Given their complexity and vast compositional space, computational modeling is an invaluable tool for predicting and understanding their behavior. As computational techniques continue to advance, coupled with the constant growth of computational resources, the future presents even more promising possibilities for the full potential of HEAs in shaping materials for future technological advancement and sustainability.

Computational models include calculation techniques that are commonly used to accelerate the creation of novel HEA compositions. These methods include empirical models (e.g., Miedema's model), semi-empirical models (e.g., CALPHAD), first-principles calculations (e.g., ab Initio Molecular Dynamics Simulation, Monte Carlo Simulation, and Density Function Theory), and machine learning [144].

Empirical model

Empirical models are mathematical or statistical models that rely solely on observed data to describe and predict phenomena. Unlike theoretical models, which are based on underlying principles, empirical models are data-driven and focus on identifying patterns and relationships within the data. This makes them valuable tools for understanding and predicting the behavior of complex systems. One of the main advantages of empirical models is their simplicity and ease of use. They can be developed based on readily available data and do not require a deep understanding of the underlying mechanisms of the system. This makes them accessible to a wide range of researchers and practitioners.

Bosi et al. [145] evaluated the effectiveness of empirical models and CALPHAD-based method to predict the phases formed in CoCrFeNi, CoCrFeNiMo0.2, CoCrFeNiMo0.5, AlCrFeMnNi and AlCoCrFeNi HEAs under various cooling conditions, including thermal spraying. The empirical model effectively predicted a solid solution for CoCrFeNi due to the similarity in the atomic size among the principal elements. Conversely, the presence of Sigma (σ) and Laves (μ) intermetallic phases prompted by the minimal difference in the atomic size for CoCrFeNiMo0.2 and CoCrFeNiMo0.5 affected the prediction accuracy of the empirical model.

Similarly, Tian et al. [146] combined empirical relations and first principle calculations to predict the phases present in CrMoW, CrMoWMn, CrMoWCo and CrMoWNi HEAs. The adopted tools enhanced the understanding and design of these complex alloys by predicting single-phase BCC crystal structures. It was observed that a further increase in the composition of Co or Ni will result in a dual-phase.

However, empirical models also have limitations. Their accuracy and applicability may be limited by the quality and quantity of the data used to develop them. Additionally, empirical models may not be able to explain the underlying mechanisms of the system, making it difficult to generalize their findings to new situations. Despite these limitations, empirical models can provide valuable insights into complex systems. Regression models can be used to predict a dependent variable based on independent variables, while correlation models can measure the strength of relationships between variables. Black-box models, such as machine learning algorithms, can be used to predict the output of a complex system without understanding the underlying mechanisms.

Semi-empirical models: alloy design through CALPHAD approaches (Thermo-Calc)

A semi-empirical model combines data-driven approaches with theoretical principles or hypotheses. This requires theoretical understanding or prior knowledge of the system being modeled. It uses observable data and theory-derived parameters or equations to increase the forecast accuracy and dependability.

Experimental methods for investigating the phase formation in HEAs often involve laborious and time-consuming processes. These alloys are primarily designed to form simple microstructures with solid solutions being the dominant phase [109]. Approaches such as the Calculation of Phase Diagrams (CALPHAD) method use the known thermodynamic properties of simpler binary and ternary alloy systems to make predictions about more complex HEAs. These computational models can guide the design of new HEAs, helping select the best compositions for specific applications.

Designing HEAs with a large compositional space requires significant effort. The CALPHAD (CALculation of PHase Diagrams) method, originally introduced by Kaufman and Bernstein in 1970, is a powerful computer-aided design technique that systematically explores phase stability and phase diagrams in multicomponent systems [[147], [148], [149]]. This is achieved by integrating experimental thermodynamic data with theoretical models [22,150], using semi-empirical computational approaches. CALPHAD is capable of extrapolating phase diagrams for higher-order alloy systems based on existing data from unary, binary, or ternary systems to provide clarity and interpretation to the experimentally inaccessible phase diagrams of multicomponent or higher-order systems [135,151,152]. By employing the Gibbs energy of formation, heat capacity, and enthalpy of mixing, it calculates the composition and temperature ranges of different phases within an alloy system, thus determining the phase equilibria [22,152]. The CALPHAD method, fully integrated to do phase diagram calculations, is commercially available in various databases [153,154] (panHEA, TCNi7, TCHEA1, TCHEA3, TCHEA4, SSOL5 etc.) and public domain thermodynamic software packages, including FactSage, Thermo-Calc, Pandat™, MatCalc, CatCalc, and MTDATA [155]. Cintron-Nuñez et al. [156] utilized CALPHAD calculations along with experiments to validate theoretical predictions and optimize the phase stability and microstructure of HEAs.

Recent studies have described the creation of a database and the use of CALPHAD modeling for HEAs. Zhang et al. [157] played a pivotal role in creating an extensive database specifically tailored to AlCoCrCuFeNi HEA systems. This database was sufficiently large to enable reliable phase-diagram calculations for HEAs. This was employed to explain the experimental observations, particularly concerning the primary solidified phases. Additionally, it was instrumental in predicting the transition between FCC and BCC phases in AlxCoCrCuFeNi HEAs. Al can be incorporated into the FCC CoCrFeNi solid solution at high temperatures to create AlxCoCrFeNi up to x=0.4. As the temperature decreased, the single-phase FCC region shrank toward x=0. It first loses stability to a B2 phase composed of chemically ordered A1 and transition metal atoms on a BCC lattice (see, for example, Fig. 4.1), and then to a mixture of a B2 phase and a Cr-rich sigma phase [158].

In recent studies conducted by Kube et al. [159] and Yang et al. [114], experiments and CALPHAD calculations were employed to revisit the valence electron concentration rule. Notably, their findings revealed that VEC thresholds were not consistent across different systems and processing conditions. Temperature has been identified as a pivotal factor in determining the criteria for phase selection. Chen et al. [95] contributed by establishing the TCHEA1 thermodynamic database, specifically designed for HEAs within a 15-element framework. TCHEA1 [160] encompassed more than 100 binary systems and over 200 ternary systems, providing comprehensive coverage of stable solution phases and intermetallics within each of the alloy systems. Moreover, Gurao and Biswas [161] demonstrated the substantial advantages of the CALPHAD method in reducing the number of experimental trials required to identify single-phase HEAs, as shown in Fig. 4.2.

Their study examined 1287 equiatomic quinary alloy systems using both the parametric and CALPHAD methods. The parametric approach reduced the number of trials to 124, and further application of the CALPHAD method resulted in only ten compositions. This means that researchers only need to prepare two FCC alloys and seven BCC alloys, such as AlFeMnNbTi and AlCrMnTiV. Characterization using techniques such as X-ray diffraction and scanning electron microscopy allowed for the efficient selection of optimized compositions.

The study by Senkov et al. [135] represents a significant advancement in the application of CALPHAD for high-throughput combinatorial studies of alloy systems. By utilizing nine different CALPHAD databases to calculate equilibrium phases for 130,000 unique compositions of equiatomic alloys containing 3–6 elements out of 26 elements, the researchers were able to challenge the conventional understanding of solid solution formation in multi-component alloys. Their findings revealed that the proportion of alloys with solid solution microstructures decreased as the number of components increased, contradicting the general notion that higher configurational entropy promotes solid solution formation. To explain this discrepancy, the authors calculated the entropy and enthalpy of mixing for solid solutions and the entropy and enthalpy of formation for intermetallic phases, enabling them to predict phase formation quantitatively and compare their results with experimental literature. The study's significance extends beyond its immediate findings, demonstrating the power of large computational datasets in challenging fundamental assumptions in alloy design. By providing extensive statistical data, such approaches can reveal trends that may not be apparent from experimental testing alone. Furthermore, the research highlights the potential of CALPHAD methods for rapidly screening composition phases, particularly for identifying compositions that maintain stable solid solution phases at low temperatures. This capability is especially valuable in the context of high-entropy alloys, which often exhibit metastable solid phases due to sluggish diffusion. The ability to screen millions of compositions across multiple alloy systems efficiently, as demonstrated in the study of AlCrMnNbTiV, AlCrMoNbTiV, AlCrFeTiV, and AlCrMnMoTi systems, showcases the power of high-throughput CALPHAD methods in narrowing down vast design spaces to a manageable number of promising candidates for experimental exploration.

Thermo-Calc is a powerful CALPHAD-based software for alloy design, particularly for high-entropy alloys. Its user-friendly interface and flexible database program enable exploration of extensive compositional spaces. Unlike other CALPHAD-based tools, Thermo-Calc permits users to define external conditions for equilibrium states. Additionally, it enables users to specify explicit conditions, such as entropies, enthalpies, mole/mass fraction, volume fraction, and activities on the composition of each observable phase in a multicomponent system. Thermo-Calc allows customization of equilibrium conditions and accommodates various parameters for each observable phase in multicomponent systems. The software's design procedure offers a systematic approach to understanding complex alloy systems, starting with sub-binary alloy thermodynamics and extrapolating to higher-order systems. Thermo-Calc computes phase diagrams, assesses phase stability and transformations, and performs comprehensive thermodynamic evaluations. Thermo-Calc also predicts the formation of diverse phases including solid solutions, intermetallic compounds, and alloys with complex microstructures. These capabilities make it invaluable for investigating critical material phenomena such as solidification, melting, and crystal growth. The detailed and reliable information provided by Thermo-Calc's phase diagrams forms the basis for modeling and predicting material system phase stability [163,164].

Thermo-Calc offers comprehensive tools for materials analysis and prediction, including phase diagram prediction, solidification simulation, and equilibrium state calculation. It is crucial for alloy design and process metallurgy optimization. Integration with kinetic modeling tools like DICTRA and TC-PRISMA enables simulation of diffusion-controlled transformations and precipitation kinetics. This holistic approach benefits industries such as aerospace, automotive, energy, and electronics. Thermo-Calc continually evolves, incorporating new models and data to remain at the forefront of materials science.

Zeng et al. advanced materials science by studying lightweight quinary high-entropy alloy fabrication using Al, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Mn, Ni, and Ti. They focused on 35 quinary alloy families containing aluminum, using the TCHEA3 database and Thermo-Calc software to calculate equilibrium phases. This approach emphasizes the role of computational methods in predicting multi-component alloy systems' phase behavior. They used two parameters to evaluate the credibility of TCHEA3 [13,165], namely the proportions of the assessed end-binary (FAB) and end ternary (FAT) systems, demonstrating a systematic approach to assessing thermodynamic calculations in HEA design. Senkov et al. [166] defined credibility criteria for CALPHAD calculations based on the fraction of fully thermodynamically assessed binary systems (FAB) and the fraction of fully assessed ternary systems (FAT) included in the database. A credibility criterion is presented for the evaluation of commercial thermodynamic databases to accurately predict the thermodynamic features of multicomponent systems. Using these theoretical credibility criteria, Wertz et al. [154] recently demonstrated the importance of the number of assessed constituent binary systems and their compositional distance on the accuracy of the ternary predictions. Their study compared equilibrium calculations for different levels of credibility (FAT=0,FAB=0,13,23) with baseline predictions that were assumed to be accurate because they were obtained from fully assessed ternaries (FAB=FAT=1). Their work highlights the importance of fully assessed binary and ternary systems. However, expanding this approach to higher-order systems, particularly quaternary phase diagrams without ternary descriptions, remains a critical research area. This is relevant because specialized HEA databases like TCHEA3, despite covering 26 elements and containing fully assessed binary descriptions for 295 elements, still rely heavily on binary descriptions for CALPHAD calculations. This reliance underscores the ongoing challenges in accurately modeling complex, multi-component alloy systems and highlights the need for continued research to enhance advanced materials design and development.

The TCHEA4 [167], thermodynamic database, developed using the CALPHAD approach, is a comprehensive tool for studying HEAs and Multi-Principal Element Alloys (MPEAs). It incorporates experimental data, first-principle calculations, and CALPHAD modeling to provide accurate thermodynamic descriptions for BCC, FCC, and HCP solutions. The database covers 26 elements (Al, B, C, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Hf, Ir, Mn, Mo, N, Nb, Ni, Re, Rh, Ru, Si, Sn, Ta, Ti, V, W, Y, Zn, and Zr), enabling calculations for 307 binary and 493 ternary systems, with 192 ternary systems fully assessed. TCHEA4's extensive coverage and accuracy make it invaluable for researchers and engineers in the field of HEAs and MPEAs. It enables the calculation of phase and property diagrams for assessed and higher-order systems, facilitating the prediction of material properties and behavior in complex alloy systems. This capability accelerates the design and development of new materials with tailored properties and allows for a thorough exploration of the vast compositional space characteristic of HEAs and MPEAs. Consequently, TCHEA4 plays a crucial role in advancing our understanding of these complex alloy systems and driving innovation in materials science and engineering.

High entropy alloys design using the first principles approach

The emergence of first-principles computational techniques [168] has ushered in new possibilities for exploring the intricate atomic interactions that dictate the phase stability and formation in HEAs. In contrast to the CALPHAD approach, which relies on empirical parameters and experimental data, first-principles computational approaches utilize quantum mechanics to investigate the electronic structures and energetics of materials, offering a more fundamental and accurate way to study HEAs. Energy minimization, which is the core of first principles, identifies different atomic arrangements to predict the formation energy of diverse phases, enabling the identification of stable phases at specific compositions and temperatures [169].

First-principles calculations are limited to studying the structures and phase equilibria of HEAs because of their small scale, limited number of atoms, and significant computing costs [170]. First-principles calculations [171] can be combined with thermodynamic CALPHAD models to enhance the predictive capabilities of phase stabilities and phase diagrams. Therefore, combining thermodynamics and first-principles approaches has shown promising results in the study of HEAs [[172], [173], [174]].

Ab initio molecular dynamics (MD)

Choi et al. [175] investigated the migration vacancy energies of species in the CoCrFeMnNi alloy. Similarly, Sharma et al. [176] used MD simulations to calculate the crystallization temperatures and stable phases in the AlxCrCoFeNi alloy. They employed the second nearest-neighbor modified embedded atom method (2NN−MEAM) and Lennard-Jones (LJ) potentials, respectively.

A common challenge in utilizing MD simulations is the lack of accessible interatomic potential. If the interatomic potentials are not specifically tailored for the target materials or desired temperature, the obtained results may not be useful or accurate. The development of interatomic potentials is a time-consuming and computationally expensive task.

Monte Carlo simulations (MC)

Researchers have used Monte Carlo simulations and a variety of computerized mathematical algorithms to assess the probability of various objectives [177]. Anzorena et al. [178] studied the temperature-dependent evolution probabilities of various coordination matrices in MoTaVWZr while Feng et al. [179] used Monte Carlo simulations to calculate the pair correlation functions in AlCoCrFeNi. A hybrid Monte Carlo and MD (hybrid MC/MD) approach was utilized to simulate the structure and determine the partial radial distribution functions in Al11.33CoCrFeNi MPE alloy [180]. The abundance of data required in Monte Carlo simulations often leads to increased complexity in these simulations.

Forward and inverse design with machine learning for high-entropy alloys

Machine learning has recently been used to explore the structure-property relationships in HEAs. By integrating ML into the HEA design process, researchers can significantly enhance the efficiency and accuracy of the discovery of novel materials. The uniqueness of data-driven ML over experimental, theoretical, and computational approaches lies in the ability of forward design (forecasting properties dependent on composition and microstructure) and inverse design (proposing compositions for desired properties).

In forward design, ML algorithms are employed to identify the optimal material chemical composition, processing conditions, phase diagrams, and microstructural attributes as inputs and outputs for a specific set of target properties such as strength, ductility, resilience, fatigue resistance, and corrosion resistance. In contrast, inverse design, also termed the generative model, is used in alloy design to reverse-engineer materials with desired properties. Rather than predicting properties from a given material composition, it uses the desired properties as inputs and outputs the anticipated chemical composition and processing conditions necessary to achieve those properties. The precision of both the forward and inverse design models relies heavily on the quality and quantity of data used for training. Extensive and reliable data on HEA composition, processing methods, and properties are crucial for effective ML implementation. Although ML models can provide predictions, comprehending the reasoning behind these predictions can be challenging. Efforts to develop interpretable ML models are ongoing to bridge this gap.

Li et al. [52] created an intelligent modeling approach to achieve property-oriented composition and process design for complex reduced activation ferritic-martensitic (RAFM) steels. Initially, a gradient-boosting regression algorithm was employed to develop a forward model capable of predicting the tensile properties of the RAFM steel based on its composition. Subsequently, an artificial neural network algorithm was applied to establish a reverse model to identify the potential combinations of composition and heat treatment that would achieve the specified tensile properties. The integration of forward-reverse design models facilitated the development of an intelligent design framework. Bansal et al. [200] presented an ML framework for predicting phases from given compositions, representing a forward approach similar to that used by Li et al. [201] and Gao et al. [202] to predict the mechanical properties and phase structures, respectively, from known alloy compositions. Conversely, Li and Birbilis [203] introduced a non-dominant sorting optimization-based generative adversarial network (NSGAN) framework for the inverse design of MPEAs by optimizing specific alloy properties. This represents an inverse design approach in which the desired properties guide the discovery of new compositions. Singh and Singh [204] also touched on the use of AI for inverse design, suggesting that AI can predict new alloys with the desired properties from a vast compositional space. While both forward and inverse designs are crucial for HEAs, the literature emphasizes forward design using machine learning to predict properties from known compositions [202,203]. However, inverse design is recognized as an emerging and powerful approach for discovering new alloys [203,204].

Machine learning algorithms for high entropy alloys

Support vector machine (SVMs) algorithms are used to identify patterns in high-dimensional data, which are crucial for analyzing the vast amount of information associated with HEA compositions and their properties. SVMs can address both classification tasks (e.g., categorizing HEAs as high-strength or ductile) [205] and regression tasks (e.g., predicting the formation of specific phases based on composition) [206]. Unlike SVMs, which function as individual units, Random Forests (RFs) leverage the collective synergy of multiple decision trees to enhance predictive performance. This "ensemble" approach offers robustness to mitigate the risk of overfitting, a common challenge in ML, where a model performs well on training data but poorly on unseen data [207]. Although RFs are designed to reduce overfitting, they are not immune to it, especially when dealing with noisy datasets. However, the ensemble nature of RFs typically results in a more robust model by averaging the errors of individual trees, assuming that the trees are not correlated [208]. In addition, the use of ensemble methods such as RFs is supported by the principle of multiple explanations, which suggests that a combination of models often performs better than individual models [208]. RFs excel at handling complex nonlinear relationships between variables within the HEA data. They can be used to predict various HEA properties, such as the tensile strength, yield strength, and hardness, based solely on the composition of the material. In addition, RFs can identify the optimal processing parameters to achieve the desired properties in HEAs. Gradient Boosting Machines (GBMs) build upon the strengths of RFs by sequentially combining multiple models, allowing them to progressively refine predictions for complex problems. Additionally, gradient boosting has been highlighted for its exceptional precision in various predictive tasks, such as phase prediction, bulk modulus estimation, and mechanical property estimation of HEAs [206]. The Gradient Boosting Classifier (GBC) was identified as the most accurate model for classifying elemental compositions as HEA or non-HEAs with a test accuracy of 78 % [209]. Similarly, Gradient Boosting has shown over 90 % accuracy in phase prediction, aligning well with experimental data [210]. Moreover, the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model was optimized to predict microhardness in HEAs, demonstrating the potential for engineering applications [211].

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) use a unique approach that mimics the structure and function of the human brain. These networks consist of interconnected nodes (artificial neurons) that process information in multiple layers. This allows them to excel at the learning complex, nonlinear relationships unseen by simpler models. ANNs are powerful tools for discovering novel HEA compositions with targeted functionalities based on desired properties. They can also predict the "cocktail effect," where elements in an HEA interact synergistically to create unique properties. The novel ANN architecture described in Dixit et al. [212] predicts eight (BCC, FCC, FCC1+FCC2, BCC1+BCC2, B2, Laves, C14_Laves, and σ) coexisting phases in HEAs based on composition and processing routes to compute their physical and thermodynamic parameters. The model demonstrated a prediction accuracy of 87.083 %. The δ and χ have the most significant impact on phase prediction. This approach is beneficial for designing new HEAs and selecting the appropriate processing routes. Feugmo et al. [213] introduced a neural evolution structure (NES) that combines ANNs with evolutionary algorithms to generate HEA structures efficiently, offering substantial computational speed and the ability to generate large structures. Dewangan et al. [214] emphasizes the importance of machine learning-assisted ANN modeling in predicting phase formation and mechanical properties, which can circumvent complex experimental procedures. Al-Shibaany et al. [215] extends the application of Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) to HEA phase prediction, achieving a high accuracy of 90 % using TensorFlow and Keras. Jain et al. [216] reported the successful design of eutectic HEAs using ANN modeling to predict microhardness values, demonstrating less than 5 % error compared to experimental measurements (see Fig. 4.3).

Machine Learning Procedure Loop used for hardness prediction of light weight alloys.

Dewangan et al. [217] showcase the use of ANNs to model the creep behavior of tungsten-containing HEAs, with excellent agreement between model predictions and experimental data, highlighting the potential of ANNs in forecasting material behavior under specific conditions. ANNs have been reported to achieve higher accuracy in phase prediction tasks than SVMs [218].

The concept of integrating AI, specifically ML and ANN, in modeling phase formation is introduced as a possible novel interdisciplinary method. Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) incorporating thermodynamics based directly into the ANN architecture were proposed for material constitutive modeling [219], and they were named Thermodynamics Artificial Neural Networks (TANNs) [219,220]. The integration ensures that TANN predictions remain within thermodynamic bounds for both novel and non-trainable data while also decreasing the data requirement for training. This demonstrates the robustness of TANNs to noise in the training data to provide high confidence as well as accurate predictions for phase formation modeling [220]. While the development of TANNs is a major step forward for physically driven predictions, a considerable number of proposed applications in AI-ML in materials science have arisen, including phase formation modeling [221]. The application of ML and ANN in computational materials science for ACC has expedited computational insights, such as empirical interatomic potential development and property predictions for molecular discoveries [221]. A deficiency in the current version of ANNs is the absence of a strict framework based on the laws of physics that TANNs try to overcome [219,220]. In summary, the creation of TANNs is a significant development in the direction of blending ML and ANN with thermodynamics in modeling phase formation. They provided a systematic way to incorporate data combined with a physics-informed basis set to constrain thermodynamic consistency while minimizing data dependence. This work illustrates the increasing use of AI-ML across materials science, especially for predicting material phases, but also underscores the continued difficulty related to the theoretical consistency of computational models.

Genetic Algorithms (GAs) are inspired by the principles of natural selection. They iteratively refine a population of candidate solutions by mimicking processes, such as selection, crossover, and mutation. This approach is well-suited for optimizing complex multidimensional problems. GAs are employed in the design and optimization of HEAs to navigate the complex compositional space and identify alloys with desirable properties, while considering constraints such as element availability and cost. Li and Birbilis [203] introduce a framework that combines GAs with Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), non-dominant sorting optimization-based GANs (NSGAN), to address the multi-objective optimization challenges in HEA design. This approach demonstrates the generation of MPEAs with targeted properties and includes an online tool for disseminating the methodology.

LASSO Regression is a specific regression technique that can handle high-dimensional data while reducing overfitting. This makes it particularly useful for tasks where researchers want to understand the specific relationships between features (e.g., element composition) and a single HEA property. This method can potentially be used to select influential factors from a large number of variables affecting hydrogen embrittlement in HEAs, as identified by Li et al. [222]. This could include variables related to hydrogen migration behavior, alloy elements, microstructure, and mechanical properties.

Zhang et al. [223] proposed a connection between the phases and nine thermodynamic parameters using Machine Learning and the extended Miedema approach. These parameters, used to predict the different phases for the HEAs include the formation enthalpy of intermetallic compound (HIM), mixing enthalpy of amorphous phase (HAM), formation enthalpy of solid solution (HSS), mixing enthalpy of liquid phase (HL), elastic energy of alloy (HE), the entropy of mixing (Smix), and atomic size difference (δ), Ω parameter and weight melting temperature of the alloy (Tm). Also, Kernel Principal Component Analysis (KPCA) was proposed as a method that combines feature selection and feature variable transformation while the thermodynamic parameters of HEAs were estimated using the geometric model and Miedema theory. The results indicate that differences in the atomic size and elastic energy play a significant role in the formation of different phases. The SVM model, using 4 features and KPCA, achieved an accuracy of 0.9743 on the testing set. The Miedema concept provides the specific thermodynamic parameters for the design of HEAs. Machine Learning approaches, specifically the combination of SVM and KPCA models, are effective for predicting phases in HEAs.

Huang et al. [218] used three machine learning techniques, namely KNN, SVM, and ANN, to predict the phase in HEAs. The Machine Learning model successfully discovered phase selection rules using a comprehensive dataset of 401 distinct high entropy alloys. These HEAs include 174 solid solutions, 54 intermetallic compounds, and 173 solid solutions with intermetallic phases. The KNN, SVM, and ANN models have accuracies of 68.6%, 64.3%, and 74.3% respectively. Subsequently, ANN and SVM were employed to classify two out of the three phases. The testing accuracy values for classification using ANN can be observed as follows: 86.7% for the pair of solid solution and Intermetallic phases, 94.3% for the pair of solid solution + intermetallic and Intermetallic phases, and 78.9% for the pair of solid solution and solid solution + Intermetallic phases. The trained ANN model outperformed the other two machine learning models and was useful for predicting the phases of new HEAs. Islam et al. [131] utilized machine learning to forecast phase in high entropy alloys. The study established the correlation between five parameters that determine the phase selection in different High Entropy Alloys. Additionally, a neural networking model was trained to classify the outcomes based entirely on the input variables. The model achieved a high accuracy of 99% when trained on the entire dataset. The weight matrix in the neural network model shows that the most important factor is the correlation between VEC and the entropy of mixing. According to the trained NN parameters, VEC is the most effective predictor of phase formation in HEAs.

Thermodynamics design strategy for high entropy alloys

This design approach uses parametric equations of the thermodynamic parameters to perform calculations that can guide the design of HEAs. Parametric approaches, leveraging thermodynamic physiochemical properties of multi-principal elements (e.g., mixing entropy, enthalpy, Gibb's free energy, Pauline electronegativity difference, atomic size mismatch, valence electron concentration, geometric parameter, interacting parameter, and melting point difference), can predict HEAs' ability to form solid solutions and intermetallic phases. These thermodynamics predictors [224] can effectively analyze phase stability, and predict the phase transformations under the given compositions of simple solid solutions, to provide valuable insights into the behavior and characteristics of HEAs [92,225,226].

Several studies [[227], [228], [229]] have used parametric approaches to guide the design and development of novel HEAs. The innovations and contributions of these researchers have considerably advanced the field of HEAs, promoting the development of new materials with improved properties and broadening the range of potential applications for these alloys. Thermodynamics-based methods are commonly used to investigate the phase equilibria of materials without the need for expensive and time-consuming experiments [142,239]. However, the complex phase diagrams of multicomponent systems cannot be obtained experimentally. Therefore, empirical thermophysical parameter calculations are insufficient for the design of HEAs. It is computationally difficult to extend these calculations to higher-order systems, such as HEAs with numerous principal elements. For this reason, computation-based CALPHAD [150,240,241] approaches are required to analyze multicomponent system material properties, which combine experimental data with thermodynamic modeling to generate phase diagrams and assess phase stability as a function of composition and temperature.

Applications of high entropy alloys in industry and technology

Materials are the basic building blocks of functional parts of any technology. In engineering, materials such as metals, polymers, ceramics, and composites [[247], [248], [249]] are the basic building blocks of components of any equipment or system. As demands have increasingly grown for lightweight, high-strength, and high-temperature applications, especially in the energy, aerospace, and automotive sectors, computational modeling has paved the way for maximizing the immense compositional possibilities to generate novel alloys. Profound multifaceted material properties that can be realized through design and development make HEAs relevant for advanced applications.

Transportation applications

One of the major challenges in material design is the trade-off between strength and ductility. HEAs often exhibit a combination of high strength and ductility, making them promising candidates for applications that require both properties. The utilization of HEAs to create lightweight constructions with excellent strength-to-weight ratios makes them ideal for automobile bodyworks, frames, and engine components [250]. The major players in the transport industry are the aerospace and automotive manufacturing industries. In the aerospace industry, HEAs can be used in aircraft components to reduce weight, improve fuel efficiency, and enhance structural integrity [249]. Furthermore, the corrosion resistance of HEAs is particularly beneficial for aerospace applications where materials are often exposed to harsh environmental conditions [251,252]. In the automotive industry, HEAs can be used in engine components, suspension systems, and body panels to improve the durability, fuel efficiency, and safety [253].

HEAs can serve the needs of these industries because of their flexibility in designing tailored materials with exceptional property profiles that are required for better performance, structural integrity, fuel economy, eco-friendly integration, cost reduction, and good service life. The remarkable resistance to high temperatures makes HEAs ideal for components such as exhaust manifolds and engine parts. The high-temperature strength allows these components to operate more efficiently, potentially even at higher temperatures, leading to improved engine performance. HEAs are known for their excellent temperature resistance, which is important in exhaust manifolds and engine parts [254,255]. Because of their unique microstructures, HEAs can maintain stability and mechanical strength at high temperatures [254,256]. HEAs have high thermal stability, although their oxidation resistance at high temperatures can be troublesome, especially for tungsten-containing HEAs [257]. Tungsten oxides can prevent the formation of protective oxide layers, thereby preventing high-temperature oxidation. Thus, WMoTaAlCr, which forms a protective rutile-type oxide layer, was developed to resist oxidation. HEAs that can withstand wear and tear are ideal for brake discs, gears, and bearings. This translates to lengthier lifespans for these components, decreased maintenance needs, and improved overall vehicle reliability. The wear properties of HEAs have been demonstrated to be enhanced by the use of advanced processing methods such as additive manufacturing and spark plasma sintering [258,259].

HEAs can withstand corrosion well in salty roads and marine applications. A blend of transition metals, including Co, Cr, Fe, and Ni, in HEA alloys makes them corrosion-resistant in oxidizing and non-oxidizing acids and salt solutions [260]. Corrosion-prone applications require resistance to avoid material loss and economic damage [252,261].

Clean energy applications

High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have motivated researchers to search for novel materials to aid the transition to clean energy sources owing to their multifaceted composition unlocking uncharted possibilities. The development and exploration of HEAs in the energy sector offers opportunities to enhance energy conversion and storage technologies, leading to more efficient and sustainable energy production. Certain HEAs have shown promising hydrogen storage capabilities, making them potential candidates for hydrogen fuel cells and hydrogen storage systems for clean energy applications [262,263].

HEAs offer promise for hydrogen storage, which is a crucial element in clean energy transitions. However, further exploration of theoretical guidance, composition design, processing methods, and the interplay between the microstructure and hydrogen storage properties is necessary [262]. Some HEAs exhibit excellent hydrogen absorption capacities, making them suitable for hydrogen storage tanks in fuel cell vehicles. Unique attributes, such as compositional tenability and beneficial lattice distortion, contribute to their high hydrogen storage performance [264,265]. The TiZrFeMnCrV HEAs demonstrated rapid absorption of hydrogen and remarkable cycle stability up to 1.93 wt % of hydrogen at 30 °C [264]. In addition, the hydrogen storage capacity of HEAs is improved by the incorporation of elements, such as Ni, with some alloys having a reversible hydrogen storage capacity of up to 2.2 wt % at room temperature [265]. HEAs with good electrical conductivity and corrosion resistance can be used as electrodes in hydrogen production processes such as electrolysis [266]. This can improve the efficiency and durability of these processes for generating clean hydrogen fuels.

HEAs can be employed as interconnects and metallic components that electrically link cells in series and separate the SOFC anode and cathode [267]. Next-generation SOFCs with higher temperatures and efficiencies may benefit from their high-temperature robustness, oxidation resistance, and electrical conductivity. The thermal stability, low Area Specific Resistance (ASR), and suppression of Cr2O3 layer growth of HEAs such as FeCoCrNi and its variants with Mn addition are promising attributes for SOFC performance at intermediate temperatures of 600–800 °C [267]. The rate of electrochemical reactions in devices such as hydrogen fuel cells and water-splitting systems for pure hydrogen production can be accelerated by the design of specific HEA compositions that function as electrocatalysts [268,269].

Solar water splitting systems may employ HEA photoelectrodes with good light absorption and corrosion resistance. These systems produce clean hydrogen fuel from solar energy. Light absorption properties and corrosion resistance are essential for the efficient conversion of solar energy to hydrogen and maintenance of the long-term stability of HEAs as photoelectrodes [222].

HEAs with high thermoelectric efficiency [[270], [271], [272], [273], [274]] can be employed in thermoelectric generators (TEGs) to convert residual heat from industrial operations or power facilities into electrical energy [275]. The scalability of thermoelectric material production is the primary focus, with reference to the development of TEGs for high-temperature applications up to 600 °C and their integration into automotive and cogeneration power facilities [276].

Additionally, the material quality and device design are crucial for real-world efficiency [277]. The challenges of coupling material development with systems engineering for TEG product development are also discussed, indicating the complexity of optimizing system-level performance [278].

HEAs have been identified as potential materials for use in concentrated solar power (CSP) systems owing to their capacity to function as both structural and functional materials at high temperatures [279]. The surface oxides of these alloys have excellent optical-to-thermal conversion efficiencies, which are important for the CSP solar absorber performance. CSP systems and solar water heaters use selected solar absorber coatings to optimize the thermal losses and solar radiation absorption [280,281]. High electrical conductivity, corrosion resistance, and structural stability make HEAs suitable for energy storage devices, such as rechargeable batteries and supercapacitors [282,283]. The "cocktail" effect and high entropy impact of HEAs make them promising next-generation electrocatalytic materials [284].

High temperature applications

HEAs exhibit excellent high-temperature stability and mechanical strength, making them suitable for components in gas turbines, rocket engines, and other high-temperature applications. HEAs exhibit excellent radiation resistance and high-temperature stability, making them suitable for nuclear reactor components such as fuel cladding, structural materials, and coolant pipes [[285], [286], [287]]. Al0.1CoCrFeNi HEA has shown a significantly higher diffusion activation energy for oxidation compared to conventional steels and Ni-based superalloys, indicating a lower oxidation rate at elevated temperatures [288]. HEAs with exceptional thermal stability and mechanical strength can be used in power plants, gas turbines, and other high-temperature applications that require materials with improved operational efficiencies and reliabilities [[289], [290], [291]].

Biomedical engineering applications

In addition to conventional engineering applications, HEAs are the materials of choice for medical applications in the areas of biocompatible implants, surgical instruments, bioresorbable materials, tissue scaffolds, biomedical sensors, and surgical instruments. The environment within a living system like the human body demands materials that are biocompatible, corrosion-resistant, strong, and durable. HEAs can be designed with these targeted properties for support systems and compatibility without affecting the body's mechanisms and metabolism [292].

Generally, HEAs for medical applications are used to address disability issues in the body system by providing necessary support systems for body sustenance with challenges of broken bones, displaced joints, impaired tissues, and disjointed teeth [293,294]. Exploring the use of HEAs in biomedical [295] engineering holds promise for developing advanced materials and devices that offer improved biocompatibility, longevity, and enhanced performance in various medical applications.

HEAs can be used for producing biocompatible implants [296,297] such as joint replacements, dental implants, and orthopedic implants due to their excellent corrosion resistance and mechanical properties. Studies have shown that specific HEAs, such as the TiNbTaZrMoV alloy, exhibit superior wear and corrosion resistance, as well as good biocompatibility, making them suitable for orthopedic bioimplant applications [298]. Additionally, the incorporation of elements like Al and Cr has been found to enhance the corrosion resistance of HEAs, which is a critical factor for implant longevity [299,300]. HEAs have the potential to be used as scaffolds for tissue engineering, providing a supportive structure [301] for cell growth and tissue regeneration.

Some HEAs have shown promise as bioresorbable [302] implant materials, meaning they can gradually dissolve in the body over time, alleviating the need for subsequent surgeries for implant removal. HEAs can be utilized for manufacturing surgical instruments [303] that require high strength, durability, and resistance to wear or corrosion [297,304].

The application of HEAs in the biomedical field is not without challenges. While certain Ti-based HEAs have shown promising mechanical properties under impact loading, which is crucial for implant materials, the addition of elements like Zr and Mo can reduce ductility and energy absorption capacity [305]. Moreover, the medical-grade radiation response of some Ti-based HEAs indicates a higher shadowing effect than conventional alloys, suggesting that further research is needed to fully understand their biocompatibility [306].

Structural applications

HEAs can be employed in infrastructure projects, such as bridges and buildings, where high strength, durability, and resistance to environmental factors are required [307,308]. The use of HEAs in the structural industry presents opportunities for developing advanced materials that offer improved performance, efficiency, and durability in a variety of structural applications.

Future directions for thermodynamic and computational modeling of high entropy alloys

Current challenges and limitations

The design of HEAs [309] currently relies mostly on empirical rules, and there is a lack of strong theoretical frameworks for selecting and optimizing alloy compositions. The production of HEAs often requires complex processing methods, including high-temperature alloying, casting of alloys with multiple components, and severe plastic deformation. These techniques can pose challenges when attempting to scale up for industrial production. Obtaining desired microstructures and phase stability in HEAs is difficult because of the presence of multiple phases, chemical segregation, and the high probability of forming undesired intermetallic phases. HEAs possess diverse properties, making it difficult to optimize specific characteristics like strength, ductility, and corrosion resistance. This challenge arises from the presence of multiple alloying elements and their interactions. Comprehensive and reliable property databases are necessary for guiding alloy development and accurately predicting properties for various applications. Certain alloying elements used in HEAs can be costly or limited in availability, potentially impeding their extensive use in industry. Standardized testing methods, characterization techniques, and alloy naming conventions are necessary to improve reproducibility and enable comparisons across studies. Addressing these challenges will maximize the potential of HEAs and their practical applications in different industries.

Emerging trends and opportunities

Exploring HEAs for applications in extreme environments, such as aerospace, nuclear energy, and high-temperature applications, are potential future directions in the field. Additionally, researchers are investigating the use of HEAs in functional materials, such as catalysis, magnetocaloric effects, and thermoelectric applications. The untapped potential exists in creating multi-component HEAs with customized characteristics, examining how processing methods affect alloy performance, and researching HEA-based composites or coatings for applications involving advanced materials. For industrial scale-up, HEA production procedures and long-term stability need further research.

Future applications for HEAs are anticipated in advanced nuclear reactors, where their exceptional radiation resistance and outstanding high-temperature properties can improve reactor safety and performance. Additionally, they have potential applications [250] in the energy sector, such as thermoelectric materials for waste heat recovery and advanced battery systems with improved performance and durability. They are appropriate for use in offshore and maritime environments due to their corrosion resistance [310,311]. Medical devices, sensors, and electronic components may use HEAs due to their biocompatibility, high electrical conductivity, and magnetic characteristics.

Conclusions

The present comprehensive review has elucidated the critical role of thermodynamics and computational modeling in the innovative design and development of HEAs. Through a meticulous examination of the thermodynamic "four core effects" alongside advanced computational strategies, including CALPHAD, first-principles approaches, and machine learning algorithms, we have demonstrated the potential of HEAs to meet the evolving demands of various industrial and technological applications. Our investigation into the equiatomic septenary Ni-Al-Co-Cr-Cu-Mn-Ti HEA, supported by both thermodynamic theoretical calculations and Thermo-Calc simulations using the TCHEA4 database, has revealed the alloy's eutectic structure characterized by a balance of strength and ductility, suitable for high-temperature and structural applications. The findings underscore the significance of selecting principal elements based on their periodic properties to tailor the HEA compositions for specific performance criteria, such as lightweight, high-strength, and high-temperature capabilities. The parametric approach, grounded in the physiochemical properties of multi-principal elements, has proven effective in predicting the formation of solid solutions and intermetallic phases, thereby providing valuable insights into the phase stability and transformations of HEAs. The Thermo-Calc evaluation of the Ni-Al-Co-Cr-Cu-Mn-Ti HEA, considering credibility criteria and the encrypted TCHEA4 database, has further confirmed the presence of complex phases consisting of both disordered-ordered solid solutions and intermetallic compounds. This evaluation not only corroborates the theoretical predictions but also highlights the intricate phase behavior and transformation dynamics during solidification, offering a path toward optimizing HEA compositions for enhanced material properties.

While the computational tools employed in this study have proven invaluable, it is important to acknowledge their limitations. The CALPHAD approach, although powerful, relies heavily on the quality and completeness of thermodynamic databases. For novel HEA compositions, especially those involving less common elements, the available data may be limited, potentially affecting the accuracy of predictions. Additionally, first-principles calculations, while providing fundamental insights, are computationally intensive and may struggle to capture the full complexity of multi-component systems at larger scales. Despite these limitations, the computational tools offer significant advantages. They enable rapid screening of vast compositional spaces, significantly reducing the time and resources required for experimental trials. The ability to predict phase stability and transformations across a wide range of temperatures and compositions provides invaluable guidance for alloy design. Furthermore, the integration of machine learning algorithms with traditional computational methods shows promise in overcoming some of the current limitations, potentially leading to more accurate and efficient predictions of HEA properties.

In conclusion, this review advocates for a synergistic approach that integrates thermodynamic insights with computational modeling techniques to accelerate the discovery and optimization of HEAs. The promising outcomes of our study, particularly in the context of the Ni-Al-Co-Cr-Cu-Mn-Ti HEA, pave the way for future research endeavors aimed at exploiting the full potential of HEAs in addressing the challenges of modern engineering applications. By embracing these advanced methodologies, the materials science community can continue to push the boundaries of alloy design, uncovering new horizons in the quest for materials that combine exceptional strength, ductility, and resilience at elevated temperatures.
