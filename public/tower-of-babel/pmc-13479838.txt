Co-detection of human papillomavirus and human herpesvirus in women from Yunnan, China: associations with cervical lesion severity
Authors: Xiangcong Wei, Xinlong Huang, Yafei Huang, Xinyan Yang, Ting Su, Qiuting Duan, Jinxiu Wan, Zhuxian Ping, Yi Sun, Ya Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman papillomavirus (HPV) is necessary but not sufficient for cervical carcinogenesis. Human herpesviruses (HHVs) may act as cofactors, yet evidence is inconsistent. We assessed HPV genotypes, HHVs, and their co-detection patterns across cervical disease grades in Yunnan, China.MethodsCervical exfoliated cell samples were collected from women attending gynecology clinics and classified as NILM, CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, or cervical cancer. HPV genotyping and detection of HSV-1/2, VZV, EBV, CMV, HHV-6, HHV-7, and HHV-8 were performed by PCR based assays.ResultsThe cohort included 1096 women. Median age increased with disease severity, from 39.00 years in NILM to 51.00 years in cervical cancer (p < 0.001). HPV prevalence was higher in lesion and cancer groups than in NILM (any HPV 48.56% vs. 64.71% to 70.00%, p = 0.033; any high-risk HPV 43.69% vs. 58.82% to 65.12%, p = 0.013). The most frequent genotypes were HPV-52, HPV-16, HPV-58, and HPV-45. HPV-16 positivity increased with severity (p = 0.023), while HPV-18 did not (p = 0.084). Overall HHV DNA detection was 19.89%. CMV was the most frequently detected HHV at 9.67%, followed by HHV-7 at 5.11%, VZV at 3.74%, and EBV at 1.92%. HSV-1/2, HHV-6, and HHV-8 were rarely detected, each in approximately 0.5% of samples. Trend tests revealed no monotonic gradient in any HHV detection rate across lesion grades. VZV detection rates differed significantly across disease categories by Fisher’s exact test (p = 0.022). In adjusted models, overall HHV DNA detection was not significantly associated with either CIN2 + or CIN3+. In contrast, high-risk HPV and HPV-16 were consistently associated with increased odds of CIN2 + and CIN3+. Exploratory analyses identified genotype-specific HPV-HHV co-detection patterns, including HPV-26 with HSV-1/2, HPV-83 with EBV, and HPV-45 with CMV.ConclusionsIn this hospital-based cohort from Yunnan, age and high-risk HPV, particularly HPV-16, were associated with cervical disease severity. HHV DNA was detectable in a subset of participants but showed no significant association with CIN2 + or CIN3 + after adjustment. These findings highlight the dominant association of HPV, particularly HPV-16, with cervical lesion severity and provide regional data for future longitudinal studies of HPV-HHV DNA co-detection.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13751-3.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) remains a major cause of cancer morbidity and mortality in women worldwide. Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a common sexually transmitted virus, and the lifetime probability of acquisition is close to 80% [1]. However, the majority of HPV infections are transient and asymptomatic, posing no health risk. Persistent high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) infection is an established cause of premalignant genital lesions, such as low-grade (CIN1) to high-grade cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN2/3), and can ultimately lead to CC [2]. Although HPV infection is essential for the development of CC, it is not sufficient. Genetic and epigenetic alterations, as well as immunosuppression, co-infection with other mucosally transmitted pathogens and cervicovaginal microbiota dysbiosis, also contribute to disease development and progression [3–5].

Human herpesviruses (HHVs) are lifelong DNA viruses that can establish latency and reactivate under immune perturbation. HHVs are a group of DNA viruses that include herpes simplex virus type 1 and 2 (HSV-1 and HSV-2), varicella zoster virus (VZV), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), cytomegalovirus (CMV), human herpesvirus 6, 7, and 8 (HHV-6, HHV-7, and HHV-8). Several HHVs have been investigated as potential cofactors or co-detected viruses in HPV-associated cervical disease. For example, HSV-1 has accounted for an increasing proportion of genital herpes infections in recent years [6], and HSV-1/2, CMV, and EBV have been reported together with HPV in genital or cervical samples [7, 8]. However, the epidemiological and clinical implications of these detections remain inconsistent across studies.

Among gamma-herpesviruses, EBV and HHV-8 have attracted attention because of their oncogenic potential and reported detection in cervical lesions. Previous studies have detected EBV or HHV-8 DNA in HPV-positive cervical cancer or cervical lesion samples, while other studies reported absent or very low detection of HSV-1/2 or HHV-8 in cervical specimens [9, 10]. EBV gene expression has also been proposed to increase from 27% in CIN1 to 35% in CIN2/3 [11]. Szostek et al. [12] suggested that HPV and EBV co-detection may increase the likelihood of HPV16 genome integration, which may contribute to cervical cancer development. These observations suggest possible HPV-HHV interactions, but they also highlight substantial heterogeneity in sample type, detection method, and disease endpoint.

Among beta-herpesviruses, CMV, HHV-6, and HHV-7 have also been detected in cervical or endocervical samples. CMV DNA has been reported in HPV-positive cervical samples, although its association with cervical lesion severity remains controversial [13, 14]. A prior study in Córdoba, Argentina, detected HHV-6B DNA in 27% and HHV-7 DNA in 84% of endocervical specimens [15]. HHV-8 DNA was detected in 2% of cervical samples from sex workers and 1% from women in the general population in Spain [16], whereas experimental studies suggest that HHV-8 can establish latent infection in HPV-positive cervical cancer cell lines and can be reactivated to produce infectious particles [17, 18]. In 276 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) cervical biopsy samples, HR-HPV DNA was detected in 40.6% and was significantly associated with increasing lesion severity, whereas HHV-8 and HSV-2 were not detected, and HSV-1 was found only in three HPV-negative, non-dysplastic samples [19]. Taken together, these findings indicate biological plausibility but do not establish a consistent epidemiological relationship between HHVs and cervical disease. This inconsistency may reflect geographic variation, different HPV genotype distributions, variations in tissue types, variations in specimen collection methods and heterogeneous endpoints. It also highlights the need for region specific data using unified assays and comparable outcome definitions.

Data regarding multi-HHV detection across lesion grades remain limited in southwest China. In this study, we characterized HPV genotypes and the prevalence of eight HHVs across cervical lesion grades in a clinical cohort from Yunnan, China. These results may enhance epidemiologic understanding of HPV-HHV co-detection in cervical disease. They also provide region-specific evidence that may inform risk stratification and future mechanistic studies on viral activity and immune modulation.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province, Kunming, Yunnan. Women attending the gynecology clinic between October 2024 and August 2025 were screened for eligibility. During the study period, 1,984 women with cervical specimens and available clinical records were assessed. Inclusion criteria were: (1) availability of cervical exfoliated cell specimens; (2) completed HPV genotyping; (3) completed HHV testing; and (4) a cervical disease classification that could be assigned as NILM (Negative for Intraepithelial Lesion and Malignancy), CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, or cervical cancer.

Exclusion criteria were defined operationally as follows. Incomplete HPV testing referred to missing HPV genotyping results or invalid HPV testing results that could not be used for genotype classification. Inadequate cervical specimens were defined as specimens with insufficient cellular material, failed internal control amplification, or insufficient residual material for HHV testing. Indeterminate diagnosis referred to records that could not be assigned unambiguously to NILM, CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, or CC because of unclear, missing, or discordant cytological or histological information. Missing key clinical information referred to records with missing age or missing information required to link laboratory results with cervical disease classification. After excluding 888 women because of incomplete HPV testing (n = 24), inadequate cervical specimens (n = 318), indeterminate diagnosis (n = 529), or missing key clinical information (n = 17), 1,096 women were included in the final analytic cohort. The participant selection flow is shown in Supplementary Figure S4. Cervical lesion categories were defined as NILM, CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, and CC. Histology was used when biopsy was available. Cytology was used for NILM when histology was not indicated. Most NILM cases did not undergo histological confirmation because biopsy is not routinely performed for women with negative cytology in routine clinical practice. Therefore, NILM classification was based primarily on cytological assessment. For cervical cancer cases, disease status was assigned according to the clinical and pathological diagnosis available at the time of cervical sample collection. Because this was a retrospective study based on existing clinical records and residual specimens, information on whether all cervical cancer cases were newly diagnosed, previously treated, or sampled before treatment could not be uniformly reconstructed. Therefore, treatment timing was not included as an analytic variable. For regression analyses, two binary outcomes were defined. CIN2 + included CIN2, CIN3, and CC. CIN3 + included CIN3 and CC. Because the number of participants in the CIN2 group was small, CIN2-specific regression models were not performed. Instead, clinically relevant combined endpoints, CIN2 + and CIN3+, were used to improve estimate stability.

Ethical approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province with approval number [KHLL2025-KY191]. This was a retrospective cross-sectional study using existing clinical records and residual specimens. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee because the study posed minimal risk to participants and all data were de-identified prior to analysis.

Specimen collection and laboratory testing

Cervical exfoliated cell specimens were collected using a cervical brush. For cervical cancer cases, the available records allowed classification of disease status at the time of HPV sampling, but did not consistently document the exact timing of sampling in relation to diagnosis or treatment. Samples were stored at 4 °C in sterile sample tubes containing cell preservation solution (HEALTH Gene Technologies Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China). Nucleic acids were extracted using a nucleic acid extraction kit (HEALTH Gene Technologies Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China) according to the protocol.

HPV genotyping

HPV genotyping was carried out with a commercial kit supplied by HEALTH Gene Technologies (Ningbo, China) that resolves twenty-five distinct genotypes. After DNA extraction, each specimen was examined for adequacy by co-amplifying a fragment of the human β-globin gene and a plasmid sequence (pcDNA 3.1+). Primers directed against the early viral open reading frames E6, E7 and E1 were then used in a single-tube reaction. Cycling was done in a Veriti thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using the following program: 55 min at 42 °C, pre-denaturation for 8 min at 94 °C, 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 70 °C for 1 min, followed by a final 1-minute extension. Amplicons were separated by capillary electrophoresis on a 3500 Dx Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Fragment sizes were automatically matched to a built-in library that detects 25 HPV genotypes in a single run. According to the manufacturer’s assay design and clinical reporting system, the panel included 18 genotypes in the assay-defined high-risk category and seven low-risk genotypes. The assay-defined high-risk category comprised HPV-16, -18, -26, -31, -33, -35, -39, -45, -51, -52, -53, -56, -58, -59, -66, -68, -73, and − 82. The low-risk category comprised HPV-6, -11, -42, -43, -44, -81, and − 83. Thus, the total number of detected HPV genotypes was 25. Because HPV-26, HPV-53, and HPV-66 are variably classified across different systems, these genotypes are described in this manuscript as additional risk-associated genotypes rather than universally established high-risk HPV types. Internal controls for both the plasmid and β-globin targets were included in every assay to rule out false-negative results caused by insufficient material or inhibition. According to the manufacturer, the analytical sensitivity is ≤ 500 copies/reaction per genotype, the assay shows no cross-reactivity among the 25 targeted HPV types or with a panel of common potential interfering pathogens, and repeat testing yielded consistent results across 10 replicates.

Human herpesvirus detection

Seven HHV targets were tested using conventional PCR, including HSV-1/2, VZV, EBV, CMV, HHV-6, HHV-7, and HHV-8. The HSV assay was designed as a combined HSV-1/2 target and did not distinguish HSV-1 from HSV-2. Therefore, HSV-related results were reported as HSV-1/2 DNA detection and were not interpreted in a type-specific manner. Each PCR run included virus-specific positive and negative controls. For each HHV target, the positive control consisted of a residual clinical specimen that had previously tested positive for the corresponding virus in routine clinical testing using a CFDA-registered/NMPA-approved real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR kit. The positive-control status of these specimens was determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the corresponding approved clinical qPCR assays. Thus, separate positive-control specimens were used for HSV-1/2, VZV, EBV, CMV, HHV-6, HHV-7, and HHV-8. DEPC-treated water was included as the negative control in each PCR run. A run was considered valid only when the virus-specific positive control produced the expected amplification/product and the negative control showed no detectable product. Each reaction was prepared in a total volume of 25 µl. This contained 12.5 µl of GoTaq Master Mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), 1 µl of each primer at 10 pmol/ml, 2 µl of DNA template, and 8.5 µl of water. Specific primers and annealing temperatures were used for each virus (Supplementary Table 1). PCR was carried out using a Veriti thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA). After amplification, 10 µl of each product was loaded onto a 1.5% agarose gel. The gel was stained with SYBR Safe DNA Gel Stain and visualized under UV illumination (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

PCR-based detection identifies viral DNA and does not distinguish between latent infection, intermittent viral shedding, and active viral replication. Therefore, HHV positivity in this study should be interpreted as HHV DNA detection rather than evidence of active infection, viral transcription, viral replication, or biological involvement in cervical carcinogenesis. No quantitative viral-load assay, viral RNA detection, antigen detection, or serological marker of viral reactivation was performed. Viral families were grouped as follows. Alpha HHV included HSV-1/2 and VZV. Beta HHV included CMV, HHV-6, and HHV-7. Gamma HHV included EBV and HHV-8.

Statistical analysis

Age was analyzed as a continuous variable. HPV category was defined with a hierarchical rule. HPV-16 was prioritized, then HPV-18, then HR-HPV, then low-risk or other HPV, then HPV-negative. Positivity for the assay-defined high-risk HPV category was operationally defined as the presence of at least one genotype included in the manufacturer-reported high-risk category. This category included HPV genotypes with established, probable, or possible carcinogenic evidence in previous epidemiological and IARC-based classifications. HPV-26, HPV-53, and HPV-66 were therefore retained in this analytic category because they have been classified as possibly carcinogenic or probable high-risk types in some classification systems.

Any HHV was defined as detection of DNA from at least one of the eight HHVs. HHV count was defined as the number of HHVs with detectable DNA per participant. HHV count was categorized as 0, 1, and greater than or equal to 2. For co-detection pattern plots, HPV plus at least one HHV was used to define HPV-HHV co-detection.

Continuous variables were summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR). Group comparisons across lesion grades were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test. Categorical variables were summarized as n and percentage. Group comparisons were assessed using Fisher’s exact test due to sparse cells in some categories. The Cochran-Armitage trend test was used to evaluate monotonic changes across ordered lesion grades from NILM to CC. Trend P values were reported as Ptrend. For HPV-HPV co-detection heatmaps, pairwise associations were evaluated using the Phi coefficient derived from 2 × 2 contingency tables. Odds ratios (ORs) were calculated and were reported as log2 transformed ORs. P values were obtained using Fisher’s exact test. Multiple-testing correction was applied only to analyses involving multiple related comparisons. Specifically, Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate correction was applied to the HPV-HPV pairwise co-detection analysis shown in Figure S1, the HPV-HHV pairwise co-detection analysis shown in Fig. 3B, and the exploratory regression comparisons shown in Fig. 4 and Figure S2. For regression analyses, BH correction was performed separately within each outcome, namely CIN2 + and CIN3+. Descriptive comparisons across cervical disease categories, including Kruskal-Wallis tests, Fisher’s exact tests, and Cochran-Armitage trend tests, were not corrected for multiple testing and were interpreted as exploratory when sparse cells were present. Unless explicitly stated in the corresponding figure legend, P values are reported as nominal two-sided P values. For association analyses, two binary outcomes were defined. CIN2 + included CIN2, CIN3, and cervical cancer, and CIN3 + included CIN3 and cervical cancer. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were fitted to estimate ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Covariates were selected a priori based on biological and epidemiological relevance rather than data-driven screening. Age was included because it is associated with both cervical lesion risk and viral detection patterns. HPV category was included because HPV infection is the primary established determinant of cervical lesion severity and could confound the association between HHV DNA detection and cervical outcomes. To reduce overlap among HPV-related variables, a hierarchical HPV category was used rather than entering multiple correlated HPV variables into the same model. Separate HPV variables were considered for sensitivity analyses, but were not included because the small number of lesion and HHV-positive events would have increased model complexity and reduced estimate stability. Therefore, the hierarchical HPV category was retained as the main adjustment variable. Because the number of participants in individual lesion categories was limited, especially in the CIN2 group, regression analyses were performed using the combined CIN2 + and CIN3 + outcomes rather than separate disease-grade-specific models. Estimates from sparse strata were interpreted cautiously, particularly when individual HHVs or genotype-specific co-detection patterns were evaluated. The main regression analyses evaluating major HPV and HHV exposure categories in relation to CIN2 + and CIN3 + were treated as primary analyses. Age-adjusted joint HPV-HHV exposure models were treated as secondary analyses. Pairwise genotype-specific HPV-HHV DNA co-detection analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

All tests were two sided. A P value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless stated otherwise. Analyses were performed in R 4.5.2.

Results

Baseline characteristics and key viral detections across cervical disease categories

A total of 1,096 women were included (Table 1). The distribution of participants across disease categories was markedly imbalanced, with most women classified as NILM and relatively few participants in the CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, and CC groups. Therefore, grade-specific percentages and subgroup comparisons, particularly those involving CIN2 and individual HHVs, should be interpreted cautiously. The majority of cervical cancer cases were squamous cell carcinoma (n = 39), with a small number of adenocarcinomas (n = 4). Median age differed significantly across cervical disease categories (p < 0.001), increasing from 39.00 years (IQR 31.00–48.00) in the NILM group to 51.00 years (IQR 46.00–58.00) in the CC group. Among NILM women, HPV detection showed an age-related decline, decreasing from 64.41% in women aged < 25 years to 38.46% in those aged ≥ 55 years, while HHV DNA detection showed a similar decline from 33.90% to 11.97% across the same age range (Figure S3). In lesion and cancer groups, age-stratified HPV and HHV detection patterns were more variable because several age-grade strata contained small numbers of participants. Therefore, these age-specific patterns in lesion subgroups should be interpreted descriptively rather than as stable subgroup trends. Overall, 50.00% of participants were HPV-positive and 45.26% were positive for any HR-HPV, with higher proportions observed in lesion/cancer categories compared with NILM (any HPV: 48.56% vs. 64.71-70.00%, p = 0.033; any HR-HPV: 43.69% vs. 58.82–65.12%, p = 0.013). In contrast, low-risk HPV positivity remained similar across groups (12.77% overall, p = 0.985). Regarding herpesviruses, HHV DNA was detectable in 19.89% of participants overall and was not significantly different across disease categories (p = 0.363). co-detection with ≥ 2 herpesviruses was uncommon (2.01% overall, p = 0.228). Among individual HHVs, CMV DNA (9.67%) and HHV-7 DNA (5.11%) were the most frequently detected, whereas the remaining HHVs were infrequent or rare. VZV detection rates differed significantly across lesion grades (p = 0.022). However, because several lesion subgroups contained very small cell counts, this difference should be considered exploratory and interpreted cautiously rather than as a stable grade-specific pattern.

HPV genotype landscape across cervical lesion grades

Detections with two or more HPV genotypes occurred in 18.98% of samples. The rate rose with lesion grade (18.57% in NILM, 26.32% in CIN3, 27.91% in CC), but the difference across groups was not significant (p = 0.457) (Fig. 1A). HPV-16 positivity significantly increased with disease severity (6.36% in NILM, 10.53% in CIN3, and 18.60% in CC, p = 0.023), whereas HPV-18 showed no statistically significant difference across categories (p = 0.084) (Fig. 1B). When ranking HPV genotypes by overall prevalence in the entire cohort, the top genotypes were led by HPV-52 (12.14%), followed by HPV-16 (7.03%), HPV-58 (6.75%), and HPV-45 (6.39%), grade-specific positivity rates for top 10 genotypes are summarized in Fig. 1C. In the HPV-HPV co-occurrence heatmaps, correlations were generally weak in NILM, with no genotype pairs remaining significant after BH correction. For descriptive completeness, the smallest nominal p values were observed for HPV42-HPV59 (Phi = 0.12, log2OR = 2.14, FDR = 0.74) and HPV52-HPV53 (Phi = − 0.11, log2OR = − 1.45, FDR = 0.74), but these associations were not significant after multiple-testing correction. In CIN3+ (HPV-positive n = 41), effect sizes appeared larger but were unstable due to sparse data. The most prominent descriptive pairs were HPV66-HPV33 (Phi = 0.47, log2OR = 4.68, FDR = 1.00) and HPV52-HPV81 (Phi = 0.40, log2OR = 4.21, FDR = 1.00), but none survived multiple-testing correction (Figure S1).

HHVs positivity landscape across cervical lesion grades and trend analysis

Figure 2 displays HHV DNA detection rates across lesion grades, including NILM (n = 1007), CIN1 (n = 17), CIN2 (n = 10), CIN3 (n = 19), and CC (n = 43). Overall, CMV was the most frequently detected HHV at 106/1096 (9.67%), followed by HHV-7 at 56/1096 (5.11%), VZV at 41/1096 (3.74%), and EBV at 21/1096 (1.92%). HSV-1/2, HHV-6, and HHV-8 were rare, each detected in fewer than 1% of samples. Across disease grades, CMV remained the predominant HHV, whereas the other HHVs showed low and variable detection rates. VZV differed across disease categories by Fisher’s exact test (p = 0.022), but this result was based on small cell counts in lesion subgroups and should be interpreted as exploratory. Trend tests revealed no monotonic gradient in any HHV DNA detection rate across lesion grades. Because HHV testing was qualitative and DNA-based, these results reflect viral DNA detection only and should not be interpreted as evidence of active HHV infection or replication.

HPV-HHVs co-detection patterns across cervical lesion grades

Among 1,096 women, 196 had detectable HPV together with at least one HHV, yielding an overall co-detection rate of 17.88%. The co-detection proportion varied across grades, including 178/1007 (17.68%) in NILM, 4/17 (23.53%) in CIN1, 3/10 (30.00%) in CIN2, 3/19 (15.79%) in CIN3, and 8/43 (18.60%) in CC. This pattern did not demonstrate a monotonic trend with lesion severity. HHV detection was significantly more common among HPV-positive than HPV-negative women (35.77% vs. 4.02%, p < 0.001), though this association may reflect shared risk factors or detection biases rather than causal relationships (Fig. 3A). Among HPV-positive women, detection of a single HHV was common (182/548, 33.21%). Multi-HHV co-detection (defined as concurrent detection of at least two HHV types) was uncommon (14/548, 2.55%) (Fig. 3A). No HPV-positive participant had three or more HHVs detectable concurrently. Among women with HPV-HHV co-detection (n = 196), CMV (87/196, 44.39%) was the most frequently detected HHV, followed by HHV-7 (56/196, 28.57%) and VZV (41/196, 20.92%) (Fig. 3A).

Pairwise co-occurrence analysis revealed exploratory genotype-specific DNA co-detection patterns (Fig. 3B). Because several strong effect sizes were derived from sparse 2 × 2 contingency tables, we added absolute counts for the key pairs in Supplementary Table S2. The strongest associations were observed for HPV-26 with HSV-1/2 (Phi = 0.83, log2OR = 12.41, FDR < 0.001), HPV-83 with EBV (Phi = 0.76, log2OR = 9.42, FDR < 0.001), and HPV-45 with CMV (Phi = 0.75, log2OR = 8.66, FDR < 0.001). However, the corresponding 2 × 2 counts showed that some of these estimates were based on small absolute numbers: HPV-26/HSV-1/2 had 5 double-positive cases, HPV-83/EBV had 16 double-positive cases, and HPV-45/CMV had 66 double-positive cases. Weaker but statistically significant associations were also observed for HPV-51 with CMV (Phi = 0.12, log2OR = 1.93, FDR = 0.019), HPV-52 with CMV (Phi = 0.11, log2OR = 1.33, FDR = 0.019), HPV-16 with HHV-7 (Phi = 0.11, log2OR = 1.85, FDR = 0.028), and HPV-11 with VZV (Phi = 0.16, log2OR = 3.65, FDR = 0.028), with absolute counts provided in Supplementary Table S2. These co-detection patterns indicate the concurrent presence of HPV and HHV DNA in the same cervical specimen, but they do not establish active viral co-infection, temporal sequence, or biological interaction between HPV and HHVs.

Associations of HPV and HHVs exposures with CIN2 + and CIN3 + lesions

We used logistic regression to evaluate associations of HPV and HHV detection with CIN2+ (CIN2, CIN3, CC, n = 72) and CIN3+ (CIN3, CC, n = 62) lesions. Because the lesion groups were much smaller than the NILM group and several HHVs were infrequently detected, the regression models had limited statistical power for low-prevalence exposures. Therefore, both null findings and large effect estimates from sparse strata should be interpreted cautiously. Analyzing HHV as a main effect with adjustment for age and HPV category revealed no significantly independent associations. Any HHV showed no association with CIN2 + with an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.50–1.73, p = 0.820) or with CIN3 + with an aOR of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.45–1.72, p = 0.698) (Fig. 4A). HHV count categories and alpha, beta, and gamma HHV groups also showed no significant associations (Fig. 4A). In single-virus models adjusted for age and HPV category, none of the individual HHVs showed significant associations, and confidence intervals were wide for low-prevalence viruses (Figure S2).

As a secondary analysis, age-adjusted joint exposure models were used to evaluate combined HPV and HHV DNA detection patterns. The reference group was HPV-negative and HHV-negative. HPV-related exposures showed the strongest associations. For CIN2+, HR-HPV positivity was associated with higher odds in both HHV-negative and HHV-positive strata (aOR = 2.88, 95% CI: 1.64–5.07, p < 0.001 and aOR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.14–4.63, p = 0.020, respectively) (Fig. 4B). For CIN3+, the aOR was 3.20 (95% CI: 1.74–5.86, p < 0.001) for HR-HPV positive and HHV negative, and 2.22 (95% CI: 1.02–4.83, p = 0.045) for dual positivity (Fig. 4B). A similar pattern was observed for HPV-16 (aOR = 3.28, 95% CI: 1.17–9.18, p = 0.024 for CIN2+; aOR = 2.95, 95% CI: 0.95–9.14, p = 0.061 for CIN3+) (Fig. 4B). Women with at least two HPV types who were HHV- negative had aOR of 2.75 (95% CI: 1.34–5.65, p = 0.006) for CIN2 + and 3.39 (95% CI: 1.62–7.09, p = 0.001) for CIN3+ (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

Cervical cancer remains a major public health burden, and persistent infection with HR-HPV is the necessary cause of most cases. However, HPV infection alone does not fully explain why only a subset of women have high-grade lesions or cancer. In this study, we evaluated HPV genotypes and the prevalence of eight HHVs in cervical exfoliated cell samples from women attending gynecology clinics in Yunnan, China. We found a strong association between HR-HPV-related markers and lesion severity. In contrast, HHV DNA detection showed no significant independent association with CIN2 + or CIN3 + after adjustment for age and HPV category.

HPV-52, HPV-16, and HPV-58 were the most common genotypes in this population. Detection of one or more HR-HPV types was associated with increasing lesion severity. HPV-16 positivity increased with disease grade. HPV-52 was the most prevalent genotype in our cohort, consistent with prior reports from this region [20, 21]. These results are consistent with HR-HPV being the factor most strongly associated with cervical lesion severity. One possible interpretation is that HHV DNA detection may represent viral co-detection or reflect underlying host immune conditions rather than an independent risk factor in this cross-sectional setting. However, host immune status was not directly measured in this study, and this explanation should be regarded as a hypothesis requiring further validation. HHV detection rates were higher in HPV-positive than in HPV-negative women (35.77% vs. 4.02%, p < 0.001). This pattern may reflect shared behavioral, clinical, or immune-related factors, but HPV-related immune microenvironment changes were not directly assessed in this study. Therefore, this mechanism remains speculative and should be evaluated in future studies incorporating local immune profiling. Exploratory co-detection analysis identified statistically significant genotype-specific pairs, including HPV-26 with HSV-1/2, HPV-83 with EBV, and HPV-45 with CMV, though these require validation. Among women with HPV-HHV co-detection, CMV was the most frequently detected HHV (44.39%), followed by HHV-7 (28.57%) and VZV (20.92%). This pattern is comparable to that reported in population studies. One meta-analysis reported CMV prevalence of 36.5% in HPV-positive cervical samples [22]. HHV-7 prevalence varies widely across studies. Our rate was higher than 5.66% reported by Broccolo et al. [23], but was lower than the 80.70% reported in an Argentinian study [15]. These discrepancies likely reflect geographic variation, different sample type, and differences in assay platforms and detection thresholds.

HSV-2 has been proposed to facilitate HPV oncogenesis through epithelial damage, inflammation, or immune modulation [24–26]. Other studies reported no significant contribution to high-grade lesions or to CC [27, 28]. However, our assay detected HSV-1/2 as a combined target and could not distinguish HSV-1 from HSV-2. Therefore, our data cannot be used to evaluate type-specific associations for HSV-1 or HSV-2, nor can they specifically assess the proposed role of HSV-2 in cervical carcinogenesis. In this cohort, combined HSV-1/2 DNA detection was rare, with an overall detection rate of 0.55%, and HSV-related findings should be interpreted only as descriptive HSV-1/2 DNA co-detection. This finding aligns with recent FFPE biopsy studies reporting minimal or no detection of HHV-8 and HSV-2 and emphasizing the central role of HR-HPV [7, 19, 29]. Low-copy-number latent infection may also contribute to low detection, as PCR sensitivity can be limited in FFPE samples or low viral load settings.

CMV showed the highest overall positivity among the eight HHVs (9.67%) but was not associated with lesion grade. This is consistent with meta-analytic evidence suggesting no overall association between CMV and cervical cancer or precancerous lesions [30]. Our findings are also comparable to those of Daxnerova et al., who reported CMV detection rates of 2.3% in normal cervix, 6.5% in LSIL, and 8.0% in HSIL [31]. However, conflicting evidence exists. A meta-analysis reported CMV prevalence of 36.5% in HPV-positive cervical samples and suggested an increasing trend from normal cervix/cervicitis and LSIL to HSIL and carcinoma [22]. Another study suggested that CMV detection may be associated with HPV-16 integration, which is considered a key step in the transition from HSIL to CC [12]. Therefore, the absence of association in our cohort does not exclude biological effects. CMV may act through immune modulation and chronic inflammation and may modulate apoptosis and DNA repair pathways. Future work should focus on high-viral-load subgroups and functional evidence, including transcriptional or protein markers and pathway activation profiles, rather than DNA positivity alone.

EBV positivity was low in our cohort (1.92%) and was not associated with lesion grade. This differs from studies reporting frequent EBV detection in cervical cancer tissue and suggesting a role in carcinogenesis [32]. The literature on EBV in CC is markedly heterogeneous. A meta-analysis reported EBV prevalence of 43.63% in CC samples, 19.00% in healthy individuals, 27.34% in CIN1 samples, and 34.67% in CIN2/3 samples [11]. Similarly, studies from North Africa and Syria documented EBV-HPV co-detection rates of 67.2% and 34%, respectively, the Syrian study demonstrated exclusive co-detection patterns and correlation between LMP1/E6 co-expression and poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma [33, 34]. The discrepancy may be partially explained by the “hit-and-run” oncogenic mechanism, in which EBV initiates malignant transformation but is not persistently required during tumor development. This hypothesis is supported by observations of higher EBV viral loads in control groups compared to CIN or squamous cell carcinoma patients [35]. However, other studies found that co-detection does not substantially affect clinical characteristics or prognosis, further complicating the interpretation [36]. Methodological and sample type differences likely explain much of this variability. Many studies have used PCR to detect EBV DNA. For tumor samples, EBER in situ hybridization is widely considered the preferred method because it localizes EBV within tumor cells and reduces confounding from infiltrating lymphocytes [37]. To clarify the role of EBV, future studies should include EBER in situ hybridization. Some previous studies cited in this field used cervical biopsy tissues, FFPE tumor specimens, non-cervical cancer tissues, or experimental cell models rather than cervical exfoliated cells. These differences in specimen type may influence viral DNA detection rates and the cellular origin of viral signals. Therefore, evidence from non-cervical tissues or cell models should be considered indirect and should not be directly extrapolated to the present cervical exfoliated cell cohort.

VZV positivity was 3.74% overall. Although crude differences across groups reached statistical significance, VZV was not independently associated with lesion grade after adjustment for age and HPV status. VZV has received limited attention in cervical samples. Our data suggest that VZV nucleic acids can be detected in this setting. The clinical relevance remains uncertain. This may reflect systemic latent infection and episodic reactivation, or very low level of viral material. HHV-6, HHV-7, and HHV-8 showed low prevalence in our cohort, with overall detection rates of 0.46%, 5.11%, and 0.46%, respectively. None were associated with lesion grade. These findings contrast with the heterogeneous literature in this field. For HHV-6, some studies have reported a potential association with cervical disease severity. Yadav et al. used in situ hybridization and identified HHV-6 DNA in 40% of CC specimens compared with 12.5% of normal tissues [38]. An Italian group found higher HHV-6 prevalence in HSIL (40%) compared with controls (14%) [23]. HHV-6 shares a complex relationship with HHV-7. T-cell activation can induce reactivation of latent HHV-7 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells, which may occur in response to HPV infection. The reactivated HHV-7 may in turn induce reactivation of HHV-6. This cascade leads to viral shedding detectable in genital secretions. Therefore, DNA detection may reflect systemic immune activation rather than localized viral replication relevant to oncogenesis. The inability of PCR to differentiate latent from lytic infections further obscures any causal interpretation [39, 40]. The data for HHV-8 are similarly inconsistent. An Iranian cohort study documented HHV-8 DNA in 22.9% of cervical cancers and 17.7% of precancerous lesions [29]. Conversely, Bakir et al. detected no HHV-8 DNA in 276 FFPE cervical tissues [19]. Such stark differences likely reflect geographic variation in epidemiology, assay performance, or the use of exfoliated cells versus tissue biopsies. Our low detection rate aligns with the latter report. These findings suggest that HHV-8, if present, is rare or confined to specific tissue niches not captured in cytological samples. Another limitation relates to analysis strategies in previous studies. Many datasets are limited in available covariates. Age is a major confounder for both viral detection and lesion risk. HPV genotype category is also a strong confounder for any association with HHVs. Models that do not adjust for HPV risk groups may overestimate HHV associations.

A major strength of this study is that multiple HHVs were tested within the same cohort using a uniform laboratory workflow. This ensures internal comparability across viruses. However, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective cross-sectional study. The analyses reflect associations but cannot establish temporality or causality. In particular, HHV detection may occur before or after HPV positivity or lesion development, which cannot be determined with the current design. We emphasize that genotype-specific HPV-HHV associations are exploratory, based on small numbers, and should be considered hypothesis-generating. Second, HHV testing was based on conventional qualitative PCR and measured only the presence or absence of viral DNA. This approach cannot distinguish latent infection, intermittent shedding, active viral replication, or transcriptionally active infection. In addition, HHV DNA detected in cervical exfoliated cell samples may originate from epithelial cells, infiltrating immune cells, or cell-free viral DNA, and therefore does not prove that HHVs were biologically active in cervical tissue. We did not measure HHV viral load, viral RNA expression, viral antigen expression, serological reactivation markers, or tissue localization. Therefore, HHV DNA detection should not be interpreted as evidence of active infection, replication, or causal involvement in cervical carcinogenesis. We also lacked viral load and transcriptional markers, which limits biological interpretation and may lead to underestimation of true associations. For HHVs, detection was based on conventional PCR for qualitative viral DNA positivity, and we did not perform an independent analytical validation using certified reference materials, therefore, some degree of outcome misclassification is possible. Third, although the overall cohort was relatively large, the distribution across cervical disease grades was highly imbalanced, with a large NILM group and relatively small numbers of CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, and CC cases. This imbalance reduced statistical power for subgroup comparisons and disease-grade-specific analyses, especially for CIN2-specific estimates. In addition, the low prevalence of several HHVs further increased data sparsity, leading to unstable estimates in some models, including wide confidence intervals and non-estimable odds ratios. Therefore, both null findings for low-prevalence viruses and apparently strong associations from sparse strata should be interpreted cautiously. Accordingly, statistically significant findings from sparse subgroup comparisons, such as individual HHV differences across lesion grades, should be viewed as exploratory signals rather than confirmatory evidence. Larger, better-balanced studies are needed to validate these findings. The use of a hierarchical HPV category may have obscured the independent contributions of multiple HPV infection and non-16/18 high-risk genotypes. Future larger studies should evaluate these HPV variables separately. Fourth, confounding control was constrained by data availability. Although we adjusted for major measured factors, including age and HPV-related variables, several important immune-related, behavioral, and clinical variables were not systematically available. These included immunocompromising conditions, HIV status, immunosuppressive therapy, major comorbidities, smoking status, parity, sexual behavior, contraceptive use, HPV vaccination status, previous cervical disease, and cervicovaginal microbiome characteristics. These factors may influence HPV acquisition and persistence, HHV latency, reactivation, or shedding, and the risk of cervical lesion development. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal. In addition, for cervical cancer cases, the timing of cervical sample collection in relation to diagnosis and treatment could not be uniformly reconstructed from retrospective records. We classified cervical cancer cases according to the disease status documented at the time of HPV sampling, but could not reliably distinguish newly diagnosed, previously treated, or pre-treatment cases for all participants. Prior treatment may alter cervical cellular composition, viral DNA detectability, or local immune conditions, which could affect HPV and HHV detection patterns. Therefore, this uncertainty should be considered when interpreting viral detection results in the cervical cancer group. Another limitation is potential diagnostic heterogeneity in disease classification. CIN1, CIN2, CIN3, and CC were mainly defined by histology, whereas most NILM cases were classified by cytology alone because histological confirmation was not routinely indicated for women with negative cytology. This difference may have introduced classification bias, because cytology is less definitive than histology and some occult or early lesions may have been misclassified as NILM. Such misclassification could attenuate differences between NILM and lesion groups and may affect the estimated associations between viral detection and cervical disease severity. Finally, as this cohort was derived from a tertiary hospital-based clinical setting, findings may be influenced by referral patterns and may not fully generalize to community-based screening populations.

Our findings indicate that HR-HPV remains centrally associated with cervical lesion severity. Most HHVs did not show a significant grade-dependent association in this cohort. However, the elevated HHV detection rate among HPV-positive women suggests that HHVs may represent co-detected viral DNA signals or markers of host or local immune conditions rather than independent causal factors in this cross-sectional setting. In summary, HPV-related markers, particularly HPV-16, remained the dominant factors associated with cervical lesion severity in this hospital-based cohort. In contrast, HHV DNA detection was not independently associated with CIN2 + or CIN3 + after adjustment. However, given the sparse lesion subgroups and qualitative PCR-based HHV testing, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The possibility that HHV DNA detection reflects specific immune-status contexts, viral-load thresholds, or HPV-related features such as HPV-16 integration was not directly tested in the present study. Larger, better-balanced longitudinal studies using quantitative viral-load assays, viral RNA or antigen detection, HPV integration analysis, and local immune profiling are needed to clarify whether HPV-HHV DNA co-detection has clinical or biological relevance in cervical disease.
