Particle migration in constricted flows: Effects of size and shape
Raquel Dapena-García, Vicente Pérez-Muñuzuri

Abstract
Cardiovascular diseases are a leading cause of death globally. Among them, some are linked to stenosis, which is an abnormal narrowing of blood vessels, as well as other factors. Smart drug delivery systems based on micro- and nanoparticles are a promising method to offer non/minimal-invasive therapeutic mechanisms. Here we investigate the propensity of particles with different shapes and sizes to drift laterally (marginate) towards an occlusion area in a two-dimensional (2D) parallel plate laminar flow using the Lattice-Boltzmann method (LBM). To verify the outcomes on both sides of the stenosis, a probability of adhesion to the borders was calculated. Analysis was done on the impact of wall-shear stress on both sides of the stenosis. Our results show that rectangular particles migrate in larger amounts and earlier than circular ones.



1
Introduction

Particle migration in flows refers to the cross-streamline motion and redistribution of suspended particles or droplets within a flowing fluid. This can occur in a variety of contexts, such as in rivers, pipelines, and even in the human body. Particle migration can be affected by several factors, including the size and shape of the particles
[
30
]
, the properties of the fluid, and the presence of external forces such as gravity or magnetism. Understanding particle migration is important in a wide range of fields, including chemical engineering, environmental science, and medicine
[
3
]
.

There has been a great deal of research on particle migration in flows in recent years, and significant progress has been made in understanding the underlying mechanisms and developing predictive models
[
31
]
. One area of active research is in developing methods to control or manipulate particle migration in flows, such as using external fields or by altering the properties of the fluid or particles
[
28
,
1
]
. Another focus is on understanding the impact of particle migration on the transport of heat, mass, and momentum in a system, as well as on the overall stability and performance of the flow
[
35
,
34
]
. Additionally, much work has been carried out on developing experimental techniques to measure and characterize particle migration in flows. Notable methods from previous decades include the use of optical scanning devices coupled with electronic counting circuits
[
43
]
, and setting large reservoirs to pump red cell-plasma suspensions through capillaries of different sizes
[
4
]
. More recently, advanced techniques such as laser-based imaging or tracking systems
[
11
]
have emerged as powerful tools to study this phenomenon. Interaction of particles with solid and elastic obstacles in the flow may lead to changes in lateral migration.

Nanoparticles, particularly spheres, are typically proposed as carriers for vascular-targeted drug delivery due to their relative ease of fabrication and their capacity to navigate the circulatory system with minimal risk of vessel occlusion
[
2
]
. However, injected targeted therapeutics have often led to suboptimal and adverse systemic effects due to several factors, including early lateral migration and an overall inability to reach the designated target
[
10
]
. Some investigations have shown that efficacy of carriers for drug delivery depends on their ability to marginate (localize and adhere) to the vessel wall
[
18
]
. In this regard, several studies suggest that, for particles circulating in a flow, the strategy of using larger particles to marginate could be more effective than using small ones
[
13
]
. In fact, particles with submicrometer diameters have a lower capacity to bind the endothelium possibly because they are entrapped within the main flow
[
24
]
. Furthermore, recent research shows the benefits of using non-spherical particles that can favor margination and interaction with the endothelium
[
44
]
. Particle size, shape, core-shell structure and stiffness play important roles in influencing particle behavior in blood flow in terms of cell-particle and vessel-particle interactions, transport and lateral margination. Computational models
[
14
]
have suggested that spherical particles tend to follow streamlines, while non-spherical particles (discs, rods…) can suffer significant lateral drift, even in the absence of external forces, which favors migration
[
20
,
6
,
15
,
16
]
.

Several types of shear stress-sensitive drug vehicles are under research, in which drug delivery would be triggered by shear stress changes
[
26
,
25
,
38
]
. Recently, there has been an increased interest in shear-activated particle clusters for the treatment of stenosis
[
9
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no drug-carriers guided to the vascular lesion (by the flow characteristics and the change in wall shear stress) which enable a localized internalization in the diseased cells/tissues while providing a sustained release of the therapeutic cargo.

In recent years, Lattice-Boltzmann methods (LBMs)
[
33
]
have evolved enough to compete with classical tools in solving complex fluid flows problems, and be able to simulate particulate flows. Rooted in the mesoscale, the LBM models the fluid as a collection of particle distribution functions, and simulates their evolution over time. In this work, the LBM combined with a particle tracking scheme was used to study the lateral migration of particles of different shapes and sizes in a 2D stenotic domain.

3
Results

3.1
Flow trajectories of particles

The LBM was used to simulate the migration trajectories of particles as a function of their size and shape. Simulation results in Fig.
3
unveiled two distinct shape dependent particle behaviors in a constricted channel: (i) both circular and rectangular particles migrate towards both lateral boundaries; however, the rectangles do so before the disks. Additionally, (ii) rectangular particles migrate to both sides of the stenosis and in greater quantity than disks. This finding aligns with previous studies focusing on the behaviour of particles with different aspect ratios ranging from spherical to discoidal
[
19
,
49
]
. Particles migrate toward the lateral walls as they approach the stenosis, then pass through the opening, where they are entrained by the main jet and exit. Some particles remain grouped in small clusters that move more slowly than the particles that passed earlier. These aggregates reduce the fluid’s velocity and modify the surrounding flow. Note that both types of particles create a wake, in which others are caught and therefore experience less drag, resulting in slower motion and
eventual clustering. This effect is weaker for rectangular particles, which exhibit cumulative angular rotation that enhances dispersion, unlike circular particles.

Particles roll along the walls of the geometry. Their probability of adhering to the boundaries will be considered later. One may find a small overlapping area of touching particles, especially for rectangles. This is expected from the numerical method because of the smooth transition of the object boundaries. The size of the overlapping area was reduced to its minimum size adjusting the relaxation time
\tau
.

For disks,

confined, large rectangles would also be expected to separate from one another more
slowly along the streamwise direction, likely due to increased correlated motion

trajectory, although we note that

3.2
Probability of adhesion

Particle migration occurs naturally due to flow conditions, being faster and in greater quantity for non circular shapes. However, particles were not intended to adhere to the boundaries so they may roll down the walls and thus modify the number of particles migrating to either side of the stenosis.

There are numerous models for calculating the general adhesion strength
[
7
,
22
,
47
]
. In this paper we used a simplified version of the bioadhesion model by Decuzzi and Ferrari
[
12
]
, in which the adhesion strength directly correlates with the higher chance of successful bonds of particles to the boundaries,

4
Conclusions

Particle–particle and particle–wall interactions in a constricted channel were investigated using the Lattice-Boltzmann method, with particular emphasis on the effects of particle size, shape, and adhesion probability to the boundaries.

Rectangular particles exhibited greater lateral migration toward the channel walls on both sides of the constricted region compared to circular particles. They also showed higher sensitivity to variations in fluid flow, responding more rapidly in terms of momentum. Nevertheless, particle size remained the dominant parameter governing transport behavior, with smaller particles reacting more quickly in all cases. Notably, the use of medium-sized rectangular particles, rather than large circular ones, resulted in enhanced particle entrapment on both sides of the stenosis. This finding suggests a strategy to improve the efficacy of drug-delivery carriers without relying on very large particles or high particle concentrations that could increase the risk of vessel occlusion.

Particle size was also found to influence the mean wall residence time. The residence time increased with particle radius upstream of the stenosis, but decreased with increasing disk size downstream. Due to the presence of recirculating flow in the wake of the stenosis, particle migration increased with occlusion severity, particularly for rectangular particles downstream of the constricted region.

Stronger adhesion promoted earlier particle migration toward the channel walls on both sides of the constricted zone. In contrast, upstream regions characterized by high wall shear stress exhibited a lower probability of particle adhesion. Local flow characteristics and particle position were found to modify the wall shear stress, thereby influencing the particle–wall bonding probability. Future studies should address the potential for particle aggregation and pile formation at the boundaries, which may lead to clustering effects capable of significantly altering the flow velocity within the constricted region. Moreover, incorporating a probabilistic description of particle detachment under varying shear conditions would enable a more realistic assessment of the balance between particle retention and release. Such an approach is essential for optimizing drug-delivery efficiency by ensuring sufficient residence time for therapeutic action while simultaneously reducing the risk of excessive accumulation and particle-induced clogging in stenotic vessels.

It should be noted that extending the present analysis to three dimensions could alter the quantitative outcomes. In such geometries, secondary flows, radial shear gradients, and out-of-plane particle motions can modify migration dynamics compared to the 2D case presented here. Blockage of blood flow in the constricted region due to particle clogging is unlikely in 3D vessels, reducing the likelihood of complete obstruction. In addition, particle orientation and contact area with the vessel wall are more complex in 3D, potentially influencing adhesion probabilities. While the qualitative trends reported here—such as the dependence of margination and adhesion on particle size and aspect ratio—are expected to remain valid, the precise migration rates and adhesion efficiencies may differ. A full three-dimensional study would therefore be an important direction for future work.

Overall, the results presented in this work provide physics-level insight relevant to carrier design considerations targeting stenotic regions.
