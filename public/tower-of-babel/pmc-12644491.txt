Clinical impact of an emergency fast-track pathway on early intervention and outcomes in acute coronary syndrome: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Wei Xiong, Wei Gao, Qibing Zhou, Zhiyong Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute coronary syndrome (ACS) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and clinical outcomes are highly dependent on rapid diagnosis and treatment. Although guidelines emphasize time-sensitive targets, delays remain common in real-world emergency care. Evidence on the effectiveness of structured fast-track pathways, particularly in Asian populations and across the full ACS spectrum, is limited. To evaluate the impact of an emergency fast-track pathway on process-of-care metrics and short-term clinical outcomes in ACS patients. We conducted a prospective cohort study including 870 consecutive patients with ACS (STEMI, NSTEMI, and unstable angina) presenting to the emergency department from January 2022 to December 2023. Patients managed via the fast-track pathway (n = 438) were compared with those receiving conventional care (n = 432). The primary endpoints were door-to-electrocardiogram (ECG) time, door-to-balloon (D2B) time, and time to first antiplatelet therapy. Secondary endpoints included in-hospital major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and 30-day outcomes. Confounding was minimized using propensity score–based inverse probability of treatment weighting. The fast-track pathway significantly reduced door-to-ECG time (median 8 vs. 18 min, p < 0.001), D2B time in STEMI (68 vs. 102 min, p < 0.001), and time to first antiplatelet therapy (14 vs. 28 min, p < 0.001). Patients in the fast-track group had a lower incidence of in-hospital MACE (6.6% vs. 11.6%, p = 0.011), fewer recurrent myocardial infarctions (2.5% vs. 5.1%, p = 0.046), and shorter hospital stay (7 vs. 9 days, p < 0.001). At 30 days, the composite endpoint of death, recurrent myocardial infarction, or unplanned revascularization was reduced (8.7% vs. 13.9%; HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.42–0.90; p = 0.011). Major bleeding rates were similar between groups. Emergency fast-track pathways significantly improve treatment timeliness and clinical outcomes in ACS without increasing bleeding risk. Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS), encompassing ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and unstable angina (UA), remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide1,2. Despite significant advances in pharmacological therapy and interventional cardiology, the prognosis of ACS is highly dependent on timely recognition, accurate diagnosis, and prompt initiation of reperfusion therapy3. Global epidemiological data demonstrate that cardiovascular diseases account for approximately one-third of all deaths, with ACS representing the most time-sensitive presentation4,5. In China, where cardiovascular mortality has surpassed cancer as the leading cause of death, the incidence of ACS continues to rise in parallel with aging demographics, lifestyle changes, and increasing prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia6.

Time is myocardium—a concept deeply embedded in modern cardiology. For STEMI patients, guidelines from the American Heart Association (AHA), European Society of Cardiology (ESC), and Chinese Society of Cardiology emphasize the critical importance of achieving a door-to-balloon (D2B) time of ≤ 90 min and a first medical contact-to-device time of ≤ 120 minutes4,5. Similarly, for NSTEMI and high-risk UA, early invasive strategies within 24 h are recommended to reduce recurrent ischemic events1,7. However, despite the clear guideline mandates, real-world studies reveal substantial delays in diagnosis and treatment, particularly in emergency settings with high patient volume and limited resources. These delays translate into missed opportunities for myocardial salvage, higher complication rates, prolonged hospitalizations, and increased mortality.

In response to these challenges, emergency fast-track pathways—structured protocols designed to streamline the triage, diagnostic, and therapeutic processes for suspected ACS—have been widely advocated8,9. These pathways typically integrate rapid electrocardiogram (ECG) acquisition within 10 min of arrival, early biomarker testing, expedited cardiology consultation, and priority access to catheterization laboratories10. Chest pain centers and regional STEMI networks, established in both developed and developing healthcare systems, have further optimized inter-hospital coordination and prehospital activation11. Evidence from observational studies suggests that such models can reduce treatment delays, improve reperfusion success, and lower short-term mortality12,13. Nevertheless, several important limitations persist in the existing literature. First, most reports originate from Western populations, with relatively limited data from Asia, where patient characteristics, healthcare infrastructure, and disease burden differ substantially11. Second, the majority of published studies are retrospective or registry-based, inherently limited by confounding factors, selection bias, and incomplete data capture13. Third, many existing evaluations have focused exclusively on STEMI patients, while evidence on the broader ACS spectrum, including NSTEMI and UA, remains insufficient14. Finally, although quality improvement initiatives and accreditation systems such as the Chest Pain Center program in China have gained momentum, rigorous prospective cohort studies evaluating the real-world effectiveness of emergency fast-track pathways across diverse ACS subtypes are scarce12,15. This knowledge gap constrains the ability of clinicians and policymakers to generalize the benefits of these protocols and to optimize ACS care in different healthcare contexts.

Given the time-sensitive nature of ACS management and the limitations of current evidence, there is an urgent need for high-quality prospective studies that systematically evaluate the clinical effectiveness of emergency fast-track pathways. By prospectively enrolling ACS patients presenting to the emergency department, and comparing those managed through a structured fast-track pathway with those receiving conventional care, it becomes possible to generate robust evidence regarding both short-term and intermediate-term clinical outcomes16. Such outcomes include not only process metrics (e.g., door-to-ECG time, D2B time, time to antiplatelet therapy initiation) but also patient-centered endpoints such as major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), length of hospital stay, healthcare resource utilization, and in-hospital mortality17,18.

The novelty of the present study lies in its prospective cohort design, which overcomes the limitations of retrospective analyses by enabling standardized data collection, consistent application of inclusion criteria, and prospective monitoring of outcomes. Furthermore, this study encompasses the full ACS spectrum rather than focusing solely on STEMI, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment of the fast-track pathway’s effectiveness. To our knowledge, few prospective cohort studies from China have systematically assessed the real-world impact of an emergency fast-track pathway on both process and clinical outcomes in ACS patients. We hypothesize that the implementation of an emergency fast-track pathway for ACS patients significantly reduces diagnostic and therapeutic delays, improves clinical outcomes, and optimizes resource utilization in the emergency setting. Specifically, we anticipate that patients managed through the fast-track pathway will achieve shorter door-to-ECG and D2B times, higher adherence to guideline-recommended therapy, and lower incidence of in-hospital MACE compared with those receiving conventional emergency care. By addressing these critical gaps, our findings aim to provide strong prospective evidence to support the integration and expansion of fast-track protocols in ACS management, inform healthcare policy, and ultimately improve patient survival and quality of life.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a prospective, observational cohort study to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of an Emergency Fast-Track Pathway for patients with ACS presenting to the emergency department (ED). Consecutive, eligible patients were enrolled in real time and followed from ED arrival through hospital discharge (and to 30 days for post-discharge outcomes).

The Fast-Track Pathway was implemented as part of a quality-improvement program led by emergency medicine and cardiology. During the study period, ED clinicians could activate the fast-track at first medical contact in the ED based on pre-specified criteria; patients who did not enter the pathway received conventional ED care. Because activation was contingent on clinical judgment and operational readiness rather than randomization, the study used advanced confounding control.

Study design and analytic framework

This study was designed as a prospective cohort study rather than a randomized controlled trial, given the ethical and practical challenges of randomizing patients with suspected ACS in the emergency setting. Primary analyses followed an “as-assigned at arrival” principle: patients were analyzed according to whether the fast-track pathway was activated during the initial ED assessment. This approach reflects a policy-relevant effect, simulating real-world practice in which pathway activation is determined by frontline clinicians rather than experimental allocation. A secondary “as-treated” analysis was also conducted to account for pathway cross-over, providing an estimate of the effect of the actual treatment received. By combining policy-relevant (as-assigned) and treatment-received (as-treated) perspectives, this analytic framework balances external validity with causal inference, while minimizing the ethical concerns of withholding potentially beneficial interventions in a time-sensitive clinical context.

Ethics

The protocol was approved by The Second People’s Hospital of Hunan Province (Hunan Brain Hospital) (Approval No. K2022032) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Depending on the clinical situation, written informed consent was obtained at presentation; for patients unable to consent (e.g., transient instability), deferred consent procedures were used per IRB policy. Patient data were de-identified prior to analysis and stored on a secure server with access logging.

Participants

Eligible patients were consecutively screened in the emergency department between January 2022 and December 2023. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Presentation to the ED with suspected ACS, defined as ischemic chest pain or equivalent symptoms within 24 h, and/or ischemic ECG changes, and/or elevated cardiac troponin; (3) Final adjudicated diagnosis of ACS during the index hospitalization, including STEMI, NSTEMI, or unstable angina (UA). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Chest pain clearly due to non-cardiac or traumatic causes, or type 2 myocardial infarction secondary to supply–demand mismatch (e.g., sepsis, severe anemia) without suspected coronary plaque rupture; (2) Confirmed alternative diagnoses such as myocarditis, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy, or pericarditis after complete diagnostic work-up; (3) Inter-hospital transfer patients who arrived after initiation of definitive reperfusion therapy at the referring facility (e.g., post-primary PCI); (4) Patients with documented do-not-resuscitate (DNR) orders precluding standard ACS therapy at presentation; (5) Previous enrollment in this study.

High-risk groups such as out-of-hospital cardiac arrest with return of spontaneous circulation, cardiogenic shock on arrival, and very late presenters (> 24 h from symptom onset) with ongoing ischemia were not excluded; instead, they were prespecified for subgroup analyses.

Exposure groups

Fast-track pathway group (intervention group)

Activation of the emergency fast-track pathway required meeting at least one of the following criteria: (1) STEMI diagnosed on the first ECG; (2) Dynamic ST–T changes accompanied by ischemic symptoms; (3) High-risk clinical features, such as hemodynamic or electrical instability, or a GRACE score ≥ 140 with suspected ACS.

Once activated, the pathway included: (1) Rapid triage and ECG: nurse-initiated 12-lead ECG with a target of ≤ 10 min from ED arrival, with automatic “STEMI alert” if criteria were met; (2) Standing orders: immediate venous access; aspirin loading and a P2Y12 inhibitor unless contraindicated; nitrates or analgesia as indicated; anticoagulation per protocol; (3) Biomarker algorithm: immediate high-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) with repeat testing at 0/1 hour if the initial result was non-diagnostic; results automatically integrated into the clinician dashboard; (4) Team activation: one-click cardiology and catheterization laboratory activation for STEMI (direct-to-cath if feasible); expedited cardiology consultation for NSTEMI/UA; (5) Bypass of non-essential steps: bedside portable imaging and point-of-care testing, with administrative procedures (admission paperwork, payment) deferred until after stabilization; (6) Standardized order sets: predefined antithrombotic and supportive medication templates to reduce practice variability.

Conventional care group

Patients managed under conventional ED processes received: (1) Standard triage procedures without nurse-initiated ECG trigger; (2) Physician-directed orders for ECG, blood tests, and medications; (3) Routine cardiology consultation workflows without automatic activation; (4) No access to standardized standing orders or pathway-specific time targets.

Diagnostic definitions and clinical management

ACS subtypes were adjudicated by two cardiologists using the Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction and all available data (symptoms, serial ECGs, hs-cTn kinetics, imaging, and angiography). STEMI was defined by ST-segment elevation in qualifying leads or new LBBB with ischemic features. NSTEMI required hs-cTn rise/fall with at least one value above the 99th percentile and evidence of ischemia; UA required ischemic symptoms/ECG changes without biomarker elevation.

Reperfusion/management followed current guidelines: primary PCI for STEMI (fibrinolysis only if PCI delay unacceptable), and early invasive strategy for high-risk NSTEMI/UA. Antithrombotic regimens (aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor; parenteral anticoagulation) and secondary prevention (statins, ACEI/ARB, β-blockers) were provided unless contraindicated. Bleeding was classified using BARC criteria.

Outcomes

Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint focused on time-sensitive process metrics, including: (1) Door-to-ECG time (minutes); (2) Door-to-balloon (D2B) time for STEMI patients (minutes); (3) First medical contact-to-device time for STEMI patients (minutes), when prehospital data were available; (4) Time to first antiplatelet administration (minutes). These process metrics were chosen as proximal outcomes most responsive to the fast-track pathway and directly related to myocardial salvage.

Secondary endpoints

Secondary endpoints encompassed three domains: (A) process achievements, (B) in-hospital clinical outcomes, and (C) 30-day post-discharge outcomes. Process endpoints included the proportion of patients achieving guideline-recommended targets, namely electrocardiogram (ECG) within 10 min of arrival, door-to-balloon (D2B) time ≤ 90 min, first medical contact-to-device time ≤ 120 min in STEMI, and early invasive strategy within 24 h in high-risk NSTEMI. In-hospital clinical outcomes comprised major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE, defined as a composite of all-cause death, recurrent myocardial infarction, urgent revascularization, or stroke), development or worsening of cardiogenic shock, sustained ventricular arrhythmias, acute heart failure requiring intravenous therapy, major bleeding classified as Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) type 3–5, and total length of hospital stay. Finally, 30-day outcomes were evaluated by structured follow-up and record review, including all-cause mortality, recurrent myocardial infarction, unplanned revascularization, stroke, BARC type 3–5 bleeding, and cardiac-related rehospitalization. All clinical outcomes were adjudicated by an independent committee blinded to exposure status.

Data collection and quality assurance

Timestamps (ED registration, first ECG, medication times, cath lab milestones) were captured automatically from the ED information system and cath lab logs; manual times (e.g., first antiplatelet given at bedside) were recorded on standardized case-report forms (CRFs). Data elements included demographics; risk factors; vitals; symptom onset-to-door time; Killip class; GRACE score components; baseline labs including hs-cTn; ECG interpretations; imaging; angiographic findings; medications; and complications.

All research staff underwent protocol training. Data underwent double entry with logic checks, range checks, and periodic source data verification on a 10% random sample. Implausible or negative intervals (e.g., D2B < 0) triggered review. For extreme outliers in time metrics, we winsorized at the 1st/99th percentiles in sensitivity analyses.

Sample size and power

The sample size was calculated on the basis of the primary process outcome. We assumed that the implementation of the fast-track pathway would lead to a 15-minute reduction in the median door-to-balloon (D2B) time among STEMI patients, with a standard deviation of 45 min. With a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and a power of 80%, the required sample size was calculated as 142 patients per group (284 in total). Allowing for an anticipated 10% rate of attrition or missing data, the final target enrollment for STEMI patients was 316 participants.

For comparison, powering the study on a clinical endpoint (e.g., a reduction in in-hospital major adverse cardiovascular events [MACE] from 8% to 5%) would have required more than 2,300 patients to achieve 80% power, which was not feasible for a single-center study. Therefore, MACE was prespecified as a secondary endpoint, with the primary emphasis placed on process-of-care metrics.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using two-sided tests with a significance level of 0.05, and results are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate, and were compared between groups using Student’s t test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test.

Because activation of the fast-track pathway was not randomized, potential confounding was addressed through a propensity score–based approach. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression including clinically relevant covariates (age, sex, cardiovascular risk factors, prior cardiac history, presenting characteristics, baseline laboratory values, and arrival mode). Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) with stabilized weights was applied, and covariate balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences, with values < 0.10 considered acceptable. When residual imbalance was present, weighted regression models were further adjusted for those covariates.

For the primary outcomes of time-to-treatment metrics (e.g., door-to-ECG time, door-to-balloon time), weighted linear regression was used, with log-transformation or quantile regression as sensitivity analyses to account for skewed distributions. For binary process outcomes (e.g., achievement of guideline targets), weighted logistic regression was applied. Secondary clinical outcomes, including in-hospital MACE, were analyzed using weighted logistic regression, while time-to-event outcomes at 30 days were assessed with weighted Cox proportional hazards models, with proportional hazards assumptions tested using Schoenfeld residuals.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted according to ACS subtype (STEMI vs. NSTEMI/UA), age (≥ 75 vs. <75 years), sex, arrival mode (ambulance vs. walk-in), and baseline risk (GRACE score ≥ 140 vs. <140). Effect modification was tested by introducing interaction terms, and these analyses were considered exploratory. Sensitivity analyses included an as-treated analysis, restriction to first medical contact in our ED, and alternative definitions of time metrics.

Missing data were handled under a missing-at-random assumption using multiple imputation with chained equations, with 20 imputations; results were pooled using Rubin’s rules. Analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and Stata (version 17, StataCorp).

Results

Study population and sample size

Between January 2022 and December 2023, a total of 972 patients with suspected ACS were screened in the emergency department. After exclusion of 102 patients (non-cardiac chest pain, type 2 MI, alternative diagnoses, or prior enrollment), 870 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Of these, 316 (36.3%) were STEMI, 332 (38.2%) NSTEMI, and 222 (25.5%) unstable angina (UA). The emergency fast-track pathway was activated in 438 patients (50.3%), while 432 patients (49.7%) received conventional care. Among the 316 STEMI patients, 162 were managed via the fast-track pathway and 154 received conventional care. The pre-specified STEMI enrollment target (316 patients) was successfully achieved, ensuring adequate power for the primary analysis of door-to-balloon time.

The mean age of the overall cohort was 64.7 ± 12.1 years, and 612 patients (70.3%) were male. Hypertension (56.8%), diabetes mellitus (28.5%), and current smoking (31.6%) were the most common risk factors. Process-of-care metrics

Implementation of the emergency fast-track pathway was associated with a marked improvement in key diagnostic and therapeutic process indicators (Table 2). The median door-to-ECG time was reduced from 18 min (IQR 12–27) in the conventional group to 8 min (IQR 6–12) in the fast-track group (p < 0.001, Fig. 2A). Among patients with STEMI, the median door-to-balloon (D2B) time decreased significantly from 112 min (IQR 81–127) to 68 min (IQR 55–88) (p < 0.001, Fig. 2B). In line with these reductions, the proportion of STEMI patients achieving guideline-recommended D2B ≤ 90 min increased from 57.9% under conventional care to 81.6% in the fast-track group (p < 0.001). Time to first antiplatelet therapy was also significantly shortened (28 vs. 14 min, p < 0.001). For patients with NSTEMI, the proportion undergoing an early invasive strategy within 24 h was higher in the fast-track group compared with conventional care (71.2% vs. 58.4%, p = 0.008).

In-hospital clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes during hospitalization are presented in Table 3. Patients managed through the fast-track pathway experienced a lower incidence of MACE compared with those receiving conventional care (6.6% vs. 11.6%, p = 0.011). This reduction was primarily driven by a lower rate of recurrent myocardial infarction (2.5% vs. 5.1%, p = 0.046) and urgent revascularization (1.8% vs. 4.6%, p = 0.019). Although in-hospital mortality was numerically lower in the fast-track group (2.1% vs. 3.9%), the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.103). Rates of acute heart failure (7.5% vs. 10.4%, p = 0.137), cardiogenic shock (3.0% vs. 4.9%, p = 0.150) and sustained ventricular arrhythmias (3.7% vs. 4.9%, p = 0.378) were also lower but without statistical significance. Stroke occurred infrequently and did not differ significantly between groups (1.4% vs. 1.9%, p = 0.572). Major bleeding (BARC type 3–5) was comparable (3.2% vs. 2.8%, p = 0.719). Importantly, length of hospital stay was significantly reduced in patients treated via the fast-track pathway, with a median of 7 days (IQR 6–9) compared with 9 days (IQR 7–11) in conventional care (p < 0.001). Thirty-day clinical outcomes

At 30-day follow-up, patients in the fast-track group continued to demonstrate improved clinical outcomes compared with those receiving conventional care (Table 4). The composite endpoint of all-cause death, recurrent myocardial infarction, or unplanned revascularization occurred in 8.7% of patients in the fast-track group versus 13.9% in the conventional group, corresponding to a hazard ratio of 0.61 (95% CI 0.42–0.90, p = 0.011). Although not statistically significant, there was a trend toward lower all-cause mortality in the fast-track group (3.0% vs. 4.9%, HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.31–1.10, p = 0.098). The incidence of recurrent myocardial infarction was significantly lower (2.7% vs. 5.6%, HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.25–0.94, p = 0.029), while unplanned revascularization also showed a numerical reduction (2.5% vs. 4.4%, HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.27–1.10, p = 0.154). Stroke occurred infrequently and did not differ significantly between groups (1.4% vs. 1.9%, HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.25–2.09, p = 0.553). Major bleeding (BARC type 3–5) was rare and similar between groups (2.3% vs. 2.5%, HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.39–2.20, p = 0.851). Cardiac-related rehospitalization occurred in 4.8% of fast-track patients compared with 7.4% in conventional care (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.36–1.14, p = 0.120). Figure 4 shows the Kaplan–Meier curves for 30-day event-free survival, demonstrating a significantly higher survival free from major events in the fast-track group.

Discussion

The present prospective cohort study demonstrates that implementation of an emergency fast-track pathway for patients presenting with ACS substantially improves process-of-care metrics, reduces in-hospital MACE, and enhances 30-day event-free survival compared with conventional emergency care. By prospectively enrolling 870 consecutive patients across the full spectrum of ACS and systematically evaluating both time-sensitive process endpoints and clinical outcomes, our study adds robust evidence to support the integration of structured fast-track protocols into emergency practice. To our knowledge, this is one of the few prospective investigations from China evaluating a comprehensive ACS population, thereby providing novel insights into the applicability and effectiveness of such models in real-world, resource-constrained settings.

This study proposes that structured emergency fast-track pathways—incorporating rapid triage, nurse-initiated electrocardiography, standardized standing orders, early biomarker testing, and expedited cardiology activation—are an effective systems-based approach to bridge the gap between clinical guidelines and real-world practice. The fast-track pathway constitutes a multifaceted intervention combining several system-level components; hence, it is not possible to isolate the independent contribution of each element (e.g., early ECG acquisition, biomarker testing, or expedited consultation) to the overall improvement in outcomes. Despite clear recommendations from the AHA, ESC, and Chinese Society of Cardiology to achieve door-to-balloon (D2B) times within 90 min for STEMI and to initiate early invasive strategies for high-risk NSTEMI/UA, substantial treatment delays remain prevalent in many emergency departments4,5. Our findings indicate that activation of the fast-track pathway halved the median door-to-ECG time (from 18 to 8 min) and reduced the median D2B time in STEMI patients by more than 30 min, with over 80% achieving the guideline target of ≤ 90 min. These improvements directly translated into better clinical outcomes, including lower in-hospital MACE and superior 30-day event-free survival. Thus, the proposed model is not merely a process refinement but represents a paradigm shift toward systematized emergency cardiology care, with measurable patient-centered benefits.

Our study contributes several key advancements. First, it provides prospective evidence that fast-track protocols are effective across the entire spectrum of ACS, not only STEMI. Previous research has largely focused on STEMI, whereas our study demonstrates that patients with NSTEMI also benefited from earlier invasive management, with a significantly higher proportion undergoing angiography within 24 h when managed under the pathway16. This broadens the generalizability of fast-track interventions to the heterogeneous ACS population. Second, by employing a real-world, “as-assigned at arrival” analytic framework complemented by propensity weighting, we generated evidence that is both clinically relevant and methodologically rigorous19. The findings reflect the true impact of frontline clinical decision-making rather than experimental allocation, thereby enhancing external validity. Third, our study fills an important geographic and demographic gap. While Western data dominate the literature, the burden of cardiovascular disease is increasingly shifting toward Asia20–22, where healthcare systems differ in infrastructure, patient demographics, and disease prevalence. Our results, drawn from a large Chinese cohort, provide context-specific evidence that may inform policy decisions and quality-improvement programs in similar healthcare environments. Finally, the observed reduction in hospital stay (median 7 vs. 9 days) highlights potential system-level efficiencies, suggesting that fast-track pathways may alleviate hospital crowding and optimize resource utilization—an outcome of particular relevance in high-volume emergency centers.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability. Although our cohort was large and diverse, multicenter validation is needed to confirm applicability across varying healthcare settings. Second, while propensity score weighting minimized measured confounding, the observational and non-randomized design cannot completely eliminate residual bias. Minor variations in unmeasured factors—such as provider experience, ambulance arrival, or baseline GRACE score—may have influenced pathway activation. Moreover, although the distribution of STEMI, NSTEMI, and unstable angina was generally balanced between groups, subtle differences in subtype composition could still have contributed to observed outcome variations and should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. Third, although the sample size was adequately powered for process outcomes, it was not powered to detect modest differences in hard clinical endpoints such as mortality. Indeed, while trends toward lower in-hospital and 30-day mortality were observed, these did not reach statistical significance. Larger multicenter studies or pooled analyses will be necessary to definitively establish survival benefits. Additionally, the fast-track pathway requires organizational commitment, training, and infrastructure support. Implementation in under-resourced settings may be challenging, and outcomes could vary based on local capabilities. Finally, our follow-up was limited to 30 days; longer-term outcomes, including 6-month or 1-year mortality, recurrent ischemia, and quality-of-life measures, remain unknown.

Randomized controlled trials are traditionally considered the gold standard for evaluating interventions. However, in the context of ACS, where delays can have fatal consequences, randomization to delayed care is ethically and practically problematic. Pragmatic cluster-randomized designs or stepped-wedge implementation trials could provide valuable complementary evidence, allowing for gradual introduction of fast-track pathways across centers while preserving ethical standards. In addition, registry-based prospective studies with larger populations could provide broader insights, especially if linked to national databases or quality improvement initiatives. Advanced analytic methods, such as instrumental variable analysis, may further mitigate residual confounding in observational studies. Finally, simulation modeling could be employed to predict system-wide effects of pathway implementation, including resource utilization and cost-effectiveness, thereby informing policy decisions.

The clinical and policy implications of our findings are substantial. First, the reduction in diagnostic and therapeutic delays demonstrates that systematic process redesign can achieve guideline targets in real-world practice. This has direct implications for accreditation programs such as China’s Chest Pain Center initiative, which could incorporate fast-track protocols as core criteria. Second, the significant reduction in in-hospital MACE and improved 30-day event-free survival highlight the potential of fast-track pathways to reduce recurrent ischemia, rehospitalizations, and downstream healthcare costs. Importantly, the pathway achieved these benefits without increasing bleeding complications, underscoring its safety. Third, beyond ACS, the fast-track model may serve as a template for other time-sensitive conditions such as acute stroke or sepsis, where structured protocols and rapid activation of multidisciplinary teams are similarly critical. The principles of early recognition, standardized orders, and expedited specialty input are broadly transferable across emergency medicine.

Future research should focus on several priorities. Multicenter prospective studies across different regions in China and internationally are needed to validate generalizability. Incorporating longer follow-up will clarify the durability of benefits and effects on long-term survival and quality of life. Cost-effectiveness analyses are essential to guide policymakers regarding resource allocation, particularly in low- and middle-income settings. Further work should also evaluate the role of digital health technologies, such as artificial intelligence–assisted ECG interpretation, real-time data dashboards, and telemedicine-enabled cardiology consultations, in enhancing pathway efficiency. Additionally, implementation science frameworks can identify barriers and facilitators to adoption, helping scale fast-track protocols nationwide. Finally, future studies should examine subgroup effects more closely, including sex-specific responses, elderly populations, and high-risk groups such as patients with cardiogenic shock or cardiac arrest at presentation, to tailor interventions more precisely.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this prospective cohort study demonstrates that emergency fast-track pathways significantly improve time-sensitive process metrics and short-term clinical outcomes for patients with ACS, without compromising safety. By providing robust real-world evidence, our findings strongly support the integration of structured fast-track protocols into routine emergency care. Broader adoption and further evaluation of such models have the potential to transform ACS management, reduce preventable morbidity and mortality, and serve as a framework for other time-critical emergencies.
