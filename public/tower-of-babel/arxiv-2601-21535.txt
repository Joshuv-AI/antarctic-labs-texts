Abstract
. We introduce a simple representation for isotropic spherical random fields and we discuss how it allows to discuss different notions of sparsity under isotropy. We also show how a suitable construction of sparse fields can mimic well the angular power spectrum and the polyspectra of some popular non-Gaussian fields, at the same time allowing for computationally efficient simulation algorithms. Using related ideas we also show how it is possible to obtain sparse approximations of spherical random fields which preserve isotropy, thus addressing an issue which has been raised in the Cosmological literature. Keywords and Phrases: Sparsity, Isotropy, Gaussianity, Spherical Random Fields MSc Classification: 60G60, 33C55, 43A90



1.
Background and objectives

Over the last two decades, isotropic spherical random fields have attracted
a lot of interest and have been considered for an enormous number of applications in very diverse
areas, including Cosmology, Climate Science, Geophysics, Quantum Mechanics,
Machine Learning and Neural Networks, among others. The overwhelming
majority of this literature has focused on Gaussian circumstances; this is
also due to the mathematical difficulty to introduce principled non-Gaussian
models which do not violate isotropy.

In particular, the main tool for the investigation of finite variance, isotropic spherical
random fields is the spectral representation theorem, which allows expressing
T(x),

x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
as

(1.1)

T(x)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}(x)\text{ ,}

where the equality holds both in the
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and in the
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega\times\mathbb{S}^{2})
sense; the spherical harmonics
Y_{\ell m}(.)
are an orthonormal
system of eigenfunctions of the spherical Laplacian operator, and
the coefficients
\left\{a_{\ell m}\right\}
are zero mean and
uncorrelated, with variance
\mathbb{E}[|a_{\ell m}|^{2}]=C_{\ell}
, the latter
defining the angular power spectrum of the random field. The
randomness of the whole field is hence entirely encoded in the vector of harmonic
coefficients
(\mathbf{a}_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}=(a_{\ell m})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N},m=-\ell,\dots,+\ell}
, (see
[
Yad83
]
,
[
Leo99
]
,
[
MP11
]
) .

A question that can appear natural is whether it is possible to achieve an alternative representation that, while preserving isotropy, may turn out to be "more sparse", i.e. to require a smaller number of random coefficients under some circumstances. The question arises in particular if one keeps in mind that many of the most popular modern statistical or machine learning procedures (such as the Lasso, see
[
BvdG11
]
) are based on various forms of penalization/sparsity-enforcing
algorithms. Naive applications trying to enforce sparsity on the coefficients
a_{\ell,m}
in spectral representation
eq.

1.1
have been considered in the literature, but they necessarily
lead to anisotropic features, as discussed in
[
CM15
]
,
[
FMM+14
]
,
[
LGSWW20
]
and others.

Indeed, it can be shown that a spherical random field is isotropic if and
only if the triangular array of coefficients
\left\{a_{\ell m}\right\}

satisfies the following invariance conditions; we must have that

\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\overset{Law}{=}D_{\ell}(g)\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\text{ ,}

where we write
\mathbf{a}_{\ell}=(a_{\ell,-\ell},\,\dots,\,a_{\ell\ell})^{T}
for the
(2\ell+1)\times 1
column vector of random spherical
harmonic coefficients. Here we have introduced the set of “Wigner”
(2\ell+1)\times(2\ell+1)
matrix-valued functions
D_{\ell}(g),

g\in SO(3)
, which provide a family of irreducible representations for the special group of rotations
SO(3)
(see
[
VMK88
]
, Chapter 4 and
[
MP11
]
, Chapter 3.3). In particular, this implies that the spectral
representation cannot be sparse for isotropic fields, i.e., it cannot be
imposed that some of these coefficients are zero-valued, because this would
clearly violate the invariance condition: the matrix functions
D_{\ell}(.)

yield an irreducible representations of the group
SO(3)
and hence no
non-trivial subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2\ell+1}
can be invariant to their
action; hence if some components of the vector of harmonic coefficients
\mathbf{a}_{\ell}
are null, they must necessarily be non-zero for some
other rotation
g\in SO(3)
.

In order to preserve isotropy and enforce sparsity the only existing approach that we are
aware of is the one proposed in
[
LGSWW20
]
, where sparsity is enforced by introducing a regularization term on the sample angular power spectrum at different multipoles. As discussed in more detail in
section

3.1
, the approach is rigorous and computationally feasible; however, from a certain point of view it leads more to smoothness in a Sobolev sense than to sparsity in the sense we advocate in this paper, that is, a drastically lower number of random coefficients. Moreover, the approach preserves isotropy and Gaussianity, which is definitely a useful feature for many applications; however, it cannot enforce sparsity for given values of the angular power spectrum sequence, which can be a limitation for some applications.

The purpose of this paper is to introduce a novel notion of sparsity for
isotropic random fields, alongside with a general construction method that
generates isotropic (although generally non-Gaussian) spherical random fields. We
shall exploit this construction in different directions:

•

We shall show how our construction is flexible with respect to
higher-order moments/cumulants of the spherical harmonic coefficients (the
so-called polyspectra). In particular, this construction allows us to generate isotropic, non-Gaussian random fields
with explicit prescribed power spectra and polyspectra, generating set of
random coefficients whose cardinality is (much)
smaller than constructions with similar angular resolutions, based instead on the
spectral representation
eq.

1.1
.

•

We will show, in particular, that these models
can easily mimic the most important features for Cosmological applications, i.e., those of “local" non-Gaussianity, while also covering a plethora of cases which are not covered by local models. We refer to the classical survey
[
BKMR04
]
for a full overview on other forms of nonGaussianity which can be induced on Cosmic Microwave Background radiation by the so-called inflationary models, the dominant paradigm for the dynamics of the primordial Universe.

•

We shall show how, given the realization of a spherical random field, it is possible to approximate the latter to an arbitrary degree of accuracy in terms of this new representation. More precisely, we will introduce a sparse reconstruction algorithm which is invariant to the choice of coordinates and is computationally feasible, as illustrated in our Monte Carlo simulations.

The main idea behind our strategy is to consider a general decomposition of isotropic random fields built as weighted superpositions of deterministic waves centered in random directions
on the sphere, i.e.,

(1.2)

T(x)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\eta_{\ell k}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\left\langle\xi_{k},x\right\rangle)\text{ , where }\xi_{k}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\,,

where
\left\{\eta_{\ell k}\right\}
is a finite-variance random sequence, whose properties will be discussed below.

Remark 1.1
.

It is immediate to see that the random fields defined in (
1.2
) are strongly isotropic; indeed for any
g\in SO(3)
we have that

\displaystyle T^{g}(x):=

\displaystyle T(gx)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\eta_{\ell k}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\left\langle\xi_{k},gx\right\rangle)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\eta_{\ell k}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\left\langle g^{-1}\xi_{k},x\right\rangle)\overset{Law}{=}T(x)\,.

Remark 1.2
.

Neglecting the weighting factors
[\eta_{\cdot,\cdot}]
and considering fields supported on a single frequency/multipole
\ell
, we obtain as special cases the Spherical Poisson Waves recently introduced by
[
BDMT24
]
, see also
[
CD25
]
.

We shall show (see
Theorem

3.1
) that all isotropic random fields admit the representation (
1.2
); moreover, with suitable assumptions on the array of random variables
\{\eta_{\ell k}\}
we will be able to construct fields which mimic the spectral properties of widely popular non-Gaussian cosmological models.

Heuristically, we will consider an isotropic random field
sparse
if the expansion in (
1.2
) involves a "small" number of components. More
precisely, we say that

Definition 1.3
.

(Weak Sparsity)

An isotropic spherical random field is weakly
sparse if for any
L\geq 0
its projection on the spherical harmonics in the first
L
-multipoles
\{Y_{\ell m}\}_{\ell=1,...,L}
is measurable
with respect to a
\sigma
-algebra generated by
N
real random variables, with
N=O(L^{2\gamma})
and
\gamma\in[0,1)
.

Clearly, any field such as (
1.2
) is weakly sparse when
N=\sum_{\ell}K_{\ell}=O(L^{2\gamma})
, where the
\sigma
-algebra is the one generated by the collection
\{\eta_{\ell k},\xi_{k}\}
. Moreover, it is immediate to see that a Gaussian random field with strictly positive
angular power spectrum cannot be sparse, as its generated
\sigma
-algebra
includes
O(L^{2}
) non-zero, independent Gaussian spherical
harmonic coefficients.

We refer to the notion above as
weak sparsity
, since this definition still allows for fields where (weak) sparsity is achieved by entirely dropping some multipole components (in some sense, this form of sparsity can be considered closer in spirit to the approach by
[
LGSWW20
]
, i.e., implementing a penalization at the level of the frequency components). In particular, isotropic Gaussian random fields can be weakly sparse as soon as their angular power spectrum
(C_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
is non-zero only for a subset whose cardinality grows sublinearly with
\ell\geq 0
,
i.e.
, such that for any
L\geq 0
we have
\#\{C_{\ell}\neq 0\,|\,0\leq\ell\leq L\}\leq L^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\in[0,1)
. In order to rule out this possibility, we introduce here the following stronger notion of sparsity.

Definition 1.4
.

(Strong Sparsity)

An isotropic spherical random field is
strongly sparse if for any
L\geq 0
its projection on the first
L
-multipoles spherical harmonics is
measurable with respect to a
\sigma
-algebra generated by
N
real random
variables, with
N=O((\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}(L)}2\ell+1)^{\gamma}
),
\gamma\in[0,1)
and where
\mathcal{S}(L)
is the support of the angular power
spectrum in
[0,L]
:

\mathcal{S}(L)\coloneqq\{0\leq\ell\leq L\,\colon\,C_{\ell}\neq 0\}\,.

For reader’s convenience, notice that for any field with strictly positive angular power spectrum we have
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{S}}^{L}(2\ell+1)=(L+1)^{2}
.

Clearly strong sparsity implies weak sparsity, whereas the opposite does not hold. In particular, it is possible to obtain weakly sparse Gaussian fields by simply assuming that the angular power spectrum
\{C_{\ell}\}
is exactly equal to zero for some multipoles
\ell
; however these fields would not be strongly sparse, indeed no Gaussian random field can belong to the strongly sparse class under isotropy (see also
Corollary

3.2
).

1.1.
Sparse fields with prescribed bispectrum

In order to better understand our sparsity proposal, let us recall some properties
that the isotropy assumption imposes on the joint moments (and cumulants) of
the random spherical harmonic coefficients. As regards the first two
moments, it is very well known that the harmonic coefficients must have
expected value zero and be uncorrelated for
(\ell_{1},m_{1})\neq(\ell_{2},m_{2}):

\mathbb{E}[a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\overline{a}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}]=C_{\ell_{1}}\delta_{\ell_{1}}^{\ell_{2}}\delta_{m_{1}}^{m_{2}}\,,

with
\delta_{a}^{b}
denoting the Kronecker delta. For the third order
moment (the so-called bispectrum), we have

\mathbb{E}[a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}]=b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\mathcal{G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}\text{ ,}

where
b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
is the so-called
reduced bispectrum
and
\mathcal{G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}
represents the
Gaunt integral:

\mathcal{G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Y_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}(z)Y_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}(z)Y_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}(z)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}\,.

The value of this integral is explicitly known as a function of the index
parameters
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}
and
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}
(see
[
VMK88
]
Section 5.9 or
[
MP11
]
Sections 3.5.2 and 3.5.3). This
shows that both second and third moments can be decomposed in a part which
solely depends only on the multiple levels
\ell_{i}
, and another component, depending on both the indexes
\ell_{i},m_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
,
whose role is only to enforce isotropy and which does not carry any physical information on the law of a given field (see again
[
MP11
]
, Chapters 6.5 and 9.2 for more discussion and details). Similar representations hold for joint moments and
cumulants of arbitrary order, which can always be written as a part
depending only on the multipoles
\ell_{1},...,\ell_{p}
which encodes the
statistical property of the field and a deterministic component which
involves multiple integrals of spherical harmonics and enforces isotropy.

We shall now show how the representation (
1.2
) can be exploited to generate spherical random fields to respect isotropy and hence to produce polyspectra with the required invariance properties. We shall consider the case where the coefficients
\{\eta_{\ell k}\}
are fully independent from the sequence
\{\xi_{k}\}
; under these circumstances, starting from the case
K_{\ell}\equiv 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}]=(4\pi)^{-1}\delta_{m_{1}}^{m_{2}}\,\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell_{1}}\eta_{\ell_{2}}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}]=(4\pi)^{-1}\,\mathcal{G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell_{1}}\eta_{\ell_{2}}\eta_{\ell_{3}}]\,.

This shows that moment constraints for the isotropic field are met once suitable constraints are imposed on the random array of weighting factors
\{\eta_{\ell k}\}
. We refer the reader to
Section

2
where we show how our sparse proposals match the features of popular isotropic non-Gaussian models with spectrum and bispectrum constraints; we will focus in particular on the "local" non-Gaussian model based on the nonlinearity parameter
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
, see again
[
BKMR04
]
,
[
Col20
]
and the references therein for a much more detailed discussion. It is also easy to check (see again
Section

2
) that this can be obtained by means of
O(L)
random coefficients, rather than
O(L^{2})
, so sparsity is indeed achieved.

Remark 1.5

(Power spectrum)
.

When the coefficients
\{\eta_{\ell k}\}
are fully independent from the sequence
\{\xi_{k}\}
, it is immediate to investigate the relationship between the variance of the random weights
\{\eta_{\ell k}\}
and the angular power spectrum of the corresponding field. Indeed, writing as usual
T_{\ell}=\sum_{m}a_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}
for the projection of
T
on the space spanned by spherical harmonics of order
\ell
, we have

\displaystyle C_{\ell}=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(\sum_{m}a_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}(x)\biggr)^{2}\biggr]=\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\mathbb{E}[T_{\ell}^{2}(x)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\eta_{\ell k}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\left\langle x,\xi_{k}\right\rangle)\biggr)^{2}\biggr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\sum_{k,h=1}^{K_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell k}\eta_{\ell h}]\sum_{m,\tilde{m}}Y_{\ell m}(x)Y_{\ell\tilde{m}}(x)\mathbb{E}[Y_{\ell m}(\xi_{k})Y_{\ell\tilde{m}}(\xi_{h})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\sum_{k,h=1}^{K_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell k}\eta_{\ell h}]\sum_{m,\tilde{m}}Y_{\ell m}(x)Y_{\ell\tilde{m}}(x)\,\frac{\delta_{k}^{h}\,\delta_{m}^{\tilde{m}}}{4\pi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\ell+1)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell k}^{2}]\,\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}|Y_{\ell m}(x)|^{2}=(4\pi)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell k}^{2}]

In particular, in case
K_{\ell}=1
we have exactly
\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell}^{2}]=4\pi\,C_{\ell}
.

1.2.
Sparse reconstruction algorithm

Theorem

3.1
shows that any monochromatic isotropic random field (of frequency
\ell
) can almost surely be expressed as the superposition of
2\ell+1
random waves, centered upon random points. It is therefore natural that, in order to obtain any sparse approximation, we need to focus only a smaller number of
most representatives
random waves and evaluate their superposition. By these means we plan to obtain a sparse approximation of the original random field, independent from the choice of coordinates, with any given degree of accuracy (Gaussianity, however, will be lost). The iterative algorithmic procedure we propose is explained below; it is immediate to verify that the procedure does not depend upon the choice of coordinates, as required to preserve isotropy.

Input:

Monochromatic field
T_{\ell}
;
Sparsity parameter
K\ll 2\ell+1

Initialize residual field
T_{\ell}(\cdot,0)=T_{\ell}

for

k=1,\dots,\,K

do

Find best direction
\xi_{k}\in\argmax_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}|T_{\ell}(x,k-1)|^{2}

Update residual field
T_{\ell}(\cdot,k)\coloneqq T_{\ell}(\cdot,k-1)-T_{\ell}(\xi_{k},k-1)P_{\ell}(\langle\xi_{k},\cdot\rangle)

end for

Output:

Sparse superposition field
\sum_{k=1}^{K}T_{\ell}(\xi_{k},k-1)P_{\ell}(\langle\xi_{k},\cdot\rangle)

We postpone to
Section

3.3
a detailed comment on this algorithm, in particular the discussion on the resulting spherical harmonic coefficients. Notice here that the algorithm we have proposed applies also to deterministic random fields.

Some possible paths for the generalization of the previous iterative algorithm for general, non-monochromatic random fields
T=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}T_{\ell}
is discussed in
Section

3.4
.

The paper is organized as follows. In

, while some background material is collected in a short Appendix.

2.
Sparse isotropic random fields with prescribed bispectrum

In this section we show how our sparse model allows to generate easily some non-Gaussian fields with prescribed bispectrum. More precisely, we show how it is
possible to generate a sparse random field with the same third order moments
as the ones considered in the very popular single field model of inflation in Cosmology; this model has been addressed and empirically investigated in literally hundreds of papers over the last two decades, see again
[
BKMR04
]
,
[
Col20
]
and the references therein for more discussion.

We are interested in nonlinearly perturbed random fields of the form

(2.1)

T(x)=T_{G}(x)+f_{\mathrm{NL}}(T_{G}^{2}(x)-\mathbb{E}[T_{G}(x)]^{2})\,,

where
T_{G}
is a mean-zero Gaussian isotropic field with the prescribed
spectrum, whereas
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
is a (small) nonlinearity parameter. This class of perturbed fields
arises in standard models of inflationary Cosmology (see
[
BKMR04
]
,
[
MP11
]
,
[
Col20
]
).
The non-Gaussianity parameter
f_{\mathrm{NL}}

is usually estimated from observed maps via the Komatsu–Spergel–Wandelt (KSW) estimator
(see for example

[
SFS23
]
,
[
Dur25
]
).

In this section we are going to show that our sparse model can easily match
the spectrum and bispectrum generated by the class of fields (
2.1
). To this aim, let
K=1
in our sparse model (
1.2
) and consider
\xi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
uniformly distributed on the sphere. In order to generate the
weights
\{\eta_{\ell}\}
in (
1.2
), consider a
sequence of independent, identically distributed real-valued standard
Gaussian random variables
\{z_{\ell}\}
, independent from
\xi
, and set

\eta_{\ell}\coloneqq\sqrt{4\pi\,C_{\ell}}\,z_{\ell}+3\,f_{\mathrm{NL}}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\sqrt{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}}\,\colon z_{\ell_{1}}\,z_{\ell_{2}}\colon

where
\colon z_{\ell_{1}}\,z_{\ell_{2}}\colon
denotes the Wick product, see
Appendix

A
.
Then, one can
easily see that the harmonic coefficients in (
1.1
) associated to the sparse field (
1.2
) (with
N=1
) equal

\displaystyle a_{\ell m}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}T(x)\overline{Y}_{\ell m}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\geq 0}\eta_{\ell^{\prime}}\frac{2\ell^{\prime}+1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}P_{\ell^{\prime}}(\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle)\overline{Y}_{\ell m}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\geq 0}\sum_{m^{\prime}}\eta_{\ell^{\prime}}\overline{Y}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\xi)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(x)\overline{Y}_{\ell m}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}=\eta_{\ell}\,\overline{Y}_{\ell m}(\xi)\,.

and hence that the spectrum of this field corresponds to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[a_{\ell m}\overline{a}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}]=\mathbb{E}[\overline{Y}_{\ell m}(\xi)Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\xi)]\,\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell}\,\eta_{\ell^{\prime}}]=(4\pi)^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell}\,\eta_{\ell^{\prime}}]\,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\overline{Y}_{\ell m}Y_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}

\displaystyle=(4\pi)^{-1}\,\delta_{\ell}^{\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{m}^{m^{\prime}}\,\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(\sqrt{4\pi\,C_{\ell}}\,z_{\ell}+3\,f_{\mathrm{NL}}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\sqrt{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}}\,\colon z_{\ell_{1}}\,z_{\ell_{2}}\colon\biggr)^{2}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\delta_{\ell}^{\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{m}^{m^{\prime}}\,\biggl(C_{\ell}+9\frac{f_{\mathrm{NL}}^{2}}{4\pi}\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}(1+\delta_{\ell_{1}}^{\ell_{2}})\,C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}\biggr)=\delta_{\ell}^{\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{m}^{m^{\prime}}\,\biggl(C_{\ell}+O(f_{\mathrm{NL}}^{2})\biggr)\,.

Up to lower order terms, this expression is consistent with the spectrum of the perturbed field (
2.1
) whose spectrum indeed equates
C_{\ell}+O(f_{\mathrm{NL}}^{2})

(see for instance
[
MP11
]
, Chapter 6.7 and
[
Dur25
]
, Proposition 2 e 3, together with the references in both these works).

Likewise, we may compute the bispectrum as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[a_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}a_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}a_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}]=\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell_{1}}\eta_{\ell_{2}}\eta_{\ell_{3}}]\,(4\pi)^{-1}\,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\overline{Y}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\overline{Y}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}\overline{Y}_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell_{1}}\eta_{\ell_{2}}\eta_{\ell_{3}}]\,\sqrt{\frac{(2\ell_{1}+1)(2\ell_{2}+1)(2\ell_{3}+1)}{(4\pi)^{3}}}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}&\ell_{3}\\
m_{1}&m_{2}&m_{3}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell_{1}}\eta_{\ell_{2}}\eta_{\ell_{3}}]\,(4\pi)^{-1}\,\mathcal{G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}

where we have expressed the Gaunt integral
\mathcal{G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}
in terms of the Wigner 3j-symbols
[
VMK88
, Equation 5 in Section 5.9]
.
Therefore the reduced bispectrum equals

\displaystyle b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}=

\displaystyle(4\pi)^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell_{1}}\eta_{\ell_{2}}\eta_{\ell_{3}}]=3\,f_{\mathrm{NL}}\,\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(\sqrt{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}}z_{\ell_{1}}z_{\ell_{2}}+\sqrt{C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell_{3}}}z_{\ell_{2}}z_{\ell_{3}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sqrt{C_{\ell_{3}}C_{\ell_{1}}}z_{\ell_{3}}z_{\ell_{1}}\biggr)\sum_{\tilde{\ell}_{1},\tilde{\ell}_{2}}\sqrt{C_{\tilde{\ell}_{1}}C_{\tilde{\ell}_{2}}}\colon z_{\tilde{\ell}_{1}}z_{\tilde{\ell}_{2}}\colon\biggr]+\frac{27\,f_{\mathrm{NL}}^{3}}{4\pi}\,\mathbb{E}\biggl[\biggl(\sum_{\tilde{\ell}_{1},\tilde{\ell}_{2}}\sqrt{C_{\tilde{\ell}_{1}}C_{\tilde{\ell}_{2}}}\colon z_{\tilde{\ell}_{1}}z_{\tilde{\ell}_{2}}\colon\biggr)^{3}\biggr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 6\,f_{\mathrm{NL}}\biggl(C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}+C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell_{3}}+C_{\ell_{3}}C_{\ell_{1}}\biggr)+\frac{54\,f_{\mathrm{NL}}^{3}}{\pi}\biggl(\sum_{\ell}C_{\ell}\biggr)^{3}\,,

which matches the
f_{\mathrm{NL}}
-first order bispectrum for the Sachs-Wolfe model with Bardeen’s potential associated to (
2.1
)
[
MP11
, Section 6.7.1, Equation 6.55]
, commonly considered for studying the Cosmic Microwave Background radiation.

Remark 2.1
.

Our model can be easily generalized in order to take into account different non-Gaussianities, beyond the bispectrum prescription. Indeed we may in general consider a strongly sparse field (
1.2
), with
K_{\ell}=1
generated by

\xi\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
uniformly distributed on the sphere, by considering the random weights

\eta_{\ell}:=\sqrt{4\pi C_{\ell}}z_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}c^{\ell}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\,\colon z_{\ell_{1}}z_{\ell_{2}}\colon+\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}c^{\ell}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}\,\colon z_{\ell_{1}}z_{\ell_{2}}z_{\ell_{3}}\colon+...

where
\colon\dots\colon
denotes the Wick product,
\{z_{\ell}\}
is
an independent sequence of i.i.d. real-valued standard
Gaussian random variables, and
c^{\ell}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\dots}
are parameters that should be chosen in order to fit the prescribed non-Gaussian statistics. For the general model, the reduced bispectrum would be

\displaystyle b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}=

\displaystyle(4\pi)^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\ell_{1}}\eta_{\ell_{2}}\eta_{\ell_{3}}]=2\sqrt{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}}\,c^{\ell_{3}}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}+2\sqrt{C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell_{3}}}\,c^{\ell_{1}}_{\ell_{2},\ell_{3}}

\displaystyle+2\sqrt{C_{\ell_{3}}C_{\ell_{1}}}\,c^{\ell_{2}}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{3}}+\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{\tilde{\ell}_{1},\tilde{\ell}_{2},\tilde{\ell}_{3}}c^{\tilde{\ell}_{1}}_{\tilde{\ell}_{2},\tilde{\ell}_{3}}c^{\tilde{\ell}_{2}}_{\tilde{\ell}_{1},\tilde{\ell}_{3}}c^{\tilde{\ell}_{3}}_{\tilde{\ell}_{1},\tilde{\ell}_{2}}+\dots

where the lower-order terms depends on the Wick products of higher order.
For this bispectrum to be maximized by equilateral configurations where
\ell_{1}\simeq\ell_{2}\simeq\ell_{3},
it is enough to assume that
C_{\ell_{3}}
decays fast and
c^{\ell}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}
resembles a delta
function. For instance, assuming that

c^{\ell}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{C_{\ell}}}{(1+|\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}|)^{\gamma}}\text{ , }\gamma\gg 0\text{ ,}

leads to

\displaystyle b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell_{3}}}}{(1+|\ell_{2}-\ell_{3}|)^{\gamma}}+\frac{2\sqrt{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell_{3}}}}{(1+|\ell_{1}-\ell_{3}|)^{\gamma}}+\frac{2\sqrt{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell_{3}}}}{(1+|\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}|)^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{\tilde{\ell}_{1},\tilde{\ell}_{2},\tilde{\ell}_{3}}\frac{\sqrt{C_{\tilde{\ell}_{3}}}}{(1+|\tilde{\ell}_{1}-\tilde{\ell}_{2}|)^{\gamma}}\frac{\sqrt{C_{\tilde{\ell}_{1}}}}{(1+|\tilde{\ell}_{2}-\tilde{\ell}_{3}|)^{\gamma}}\frac{\sqrt{C_{\tilde{\ell}_{2}}}}{(1+|\tilde{\ell}_{1}-\tilde{\ell}_{3}|)^{\gamma}}\,.

We refer to
[
VBDC25
]
for much more discussion on alternative forms of nonGaussianity arising from the inflationary scenarios (for instance, those generated by so-called
multiple fields inflation
) and the corresponding bispectrum shapes.

3.
A general decomposition for isotropic random fields

As mentioned above, one of our aims is to show that each isotropic random field admits a general decomposition of the form (
1.2
).
More precisely, we shall show that

Theorem 3.1
.

For any isotropic spherical random field
T=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}a_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}
there exist a sequence
\{K_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
with
K_{\ell}\leq 2\ell+1
and a sequence of real random weights
\{\eta_{\ell k}\}
such that almost surely it holds

T(x)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\eta_{\ell k}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\left\langle x,\xi_{k}\right\rangle)\text{ , where }\xi_{k}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\text{ , }k=1,2,...

This result provides a representation of any isotropic random field from which it is easier to investigate its (strong or weak) sparsity properties.
Moreover, it suggests how to achieve sparse approximations for isotropic spherical random fields: in a first step it is possible to approximate with a finite multipole expansion and the desired degree of accuracy any field, and in the second to use an expansion such as (
1.2
) for random directions that grow sublinearly (i.e.,
K_{\ell}=o(\ell)
).

In this section we prove
Theorem

3.1
; we also argue that no Gaussian isotropic random field can be strongly sparse. Before we do so, however, we believe it can be useful to recall a few previous attempts to achieve sparse representations for isotropic random fields and to compare them with our current proposal.

3.1.
A comparison with the existing literature

Quite a few papers over the last 15 years have attempted different forms of sparse reconstruction techniques for isotropic random fields on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The reasons for this interest is easily understood, again referring to Cosmological applications: Cosmic Microwave Background maps are in fact observed with several forms of super-imposed noise, which can have either instrumental origin or can be generated by astrophysical contaminants (foregrounds). Overall, we are in the framework of image reconstruction techniques, where of course sparsity enforcing tools have long proved to be efficient and practical; in this case, however, we want to ensure also that the reconstruction will not produce spurious statistical features (like anisotropies) in the random map that we wish to analyze. Probably the single most popular sparsity enforcing techniques in the mathematical statistics literature is the
Lasso
, which imposes a
\ell_{1}
convex regularization term into a standard quadratic loss procedure; in the case of spherical random fields, this suggests to search for solutions of the following convex minimization problem:

(3.1)

\left[T^{+}:=\sum_{\ell,m}a^{+}_{\ell,m}Y_{\ell,m}\right]:=\argmin_{T^{b}:=\sum_{\ell,m}b_{\ell,m}Y_{\ell,m}}\left[\|T(\cdot)-T^{b}(\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}+\lambda\sum_{\ell,m}|b_{\ell,m}|\right],

where
\lambda
is a penalization parameter. It is well-known from the standard literature (see e.g.
[
BvdG11
]
) that these optimization procedures provide a convex approximation for the solution with a
\ell_{0}
loss; the latter would lead to sparsity in a strict sense (in some sense analogous to our terminology in this paper), but by itself is
NP-
hard from the computational point of view and hence numerically unfeasible. The convex regularization procedures leads to solutions which are arbitrarily close with very high probability, see some classical textbooks such as
[
BvdG11
]
; in the context of CMB data analysis, regularization procedures based on the Lasso are advocated for instance in
[
SFR13
]
. It was however argued in
[
FMM+14
]
and
[
CM15
]
that these forms of Lasso procedures will lead to maps which do not satisfy Gaussianity and isotropy assumptions; heuristically, it is indeed immediate to notice that the minimization procedure introduced in
eq.

3.1
is not invariant with respect to the choice of coordinates (the
||\cdot||_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}
, "fidelity" term is rotationally invariant, but the
|\cdot|_{\ell_{1}}
regularization term clearly isn’t - see
[
LGSWW20
]
for a simple and illuminating counterexample).

To overcome these issues sparsity enforcing techniques that can preserve isotropy and Gaussianity have been recently proposed in
[
LGSWW20
]
, whose authors suggest the regularized estimates

(3.2)

\left[T^{+}:=\sum_{\ell,m}a^{+}_{\ell,m}Y_{\ell,m}\right]:=\argmin_{T^{b}:=\sum_{\ell,m}b_{\ell,m}Y_{\ell,m}}\left[\|T(\cdot)-T^{b}(\cdot)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}+\lambda\sum_{\ell}\beta_{\ell}\sqrt{\sum_{m}|b_{\ell,m}|^{2}}\right],

for constants
\beta_{\ell},\lambda
to be determined. This procedure is shown to preserve Gaussianity and isotropy and to perform well on simulations; in this sense, it is theoretically well-grounded and empirically valid. However, by imposing a penalization on the reconstructed angular power spectrum
(2\ell+1)\widehat{C}_{\ell}:=\sum_{m}|b_{\ell,m}|^{2}
it is actually performing a form of Sobolev regularization, so ensuring smoothness more than sparsity in the sense defined in this paper.

Summing up, the procedure we advocate in this work has different goals and takes a new perspective. It does not preserve Gaussianity, nor it could because we argued that sparsity and Gaussianity are incompatible; on the other hand, it does achieve a form of sparsity/data compression, in the sense that it approximates the input field with a much lower number of coefficients, at the same time preserving isotropy (and invariance to the choice of coordinates).

3.2.
The proof of the general decomposition

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Firstly, take
T=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}
and take i.i.d. uniform random variables on the sphere
(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
.
Now fix
\ell\geq 0
, any non-negative number
K_{\ell}
and for each
k\in\{1,\dots,K_{\ell}\}
consider the column vector

\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k}):=(Y_{\ell,-\ell}(\xi_{k}),...,Y_{\ell,\ell}(\xi_{k}))^{T}\,,

and the corresponding random matrix generated by these column vectors

\mathbb{Y}_{\ell,K_{\ell}}:=\left\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),...\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{K_{\ell}})\right\}\,.

Step 1.

We claim that
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbb{Y}_{\ell,K_{\ell}})=K_{\ell}\wedge(2\ell+1)
with probability one. This follows from the left action
\mathcal{L}
of
SO(3)
on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
which induces a representation of
SO(3)
on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
. More precisely, if
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
denotes the function space generated by the spherical harmonics
\{Y_{\ell,m}\}_{m=-\ell,\dots,\ell}
, then the couple
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{H}_{\ell})
defines an irreducible representation of
SO(3)

[
MP11
, Proposition 3.27]
, which means that no non-trivial subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
is preserved under the action
\mathcal{L}
. This implies the existence of
2\ell+1
points
\zeta_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that
\{\textbf{Y}_{\ell}(\zeta_{1}),\dots,\textbf{Y}_{\ell}(\zeta_{2\ell+1})\}
is a non-degenerate matrix (see also
[
Mü66
, Lemma 6]
). Therefore if we set
d=K\wedge(2\ell+1)
, there exists at least a
d\times d
minor
[\textbf{Y}_{\ell}(\zeta_{1}),\dots,\textbf{Y}_{\ell}(\zeta_{d})]_{d\times d}
non-degenerate and hence such that
\det([\textbf{Y}_{\ell}(\zeta_{1}),\dots,\textbf{Y}_{\ell}(\zeta_{d})]_{d\times d})\neq 0
(up to relabeling the points we may assume it involves the first
d
points, and hereafter we fix one such minor and for any matrix
[\cdot]_{d\times d}
denotes its minor in correspondence with our first choice).
Next, consider the function
F\colon(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
defined as

F(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d})\coloneqq\det\biggl(\biggr[\textbf{Y}_{\ell}\biggl(\frac{x_{1}}{\|x_{1}\|}\biggr),\dots,\textbf{Y}_{\ell}\biggl(\frac{x_{d}}{\|x_{d}\|}\biggr)\biggr]_{d\times d}\biggr)\,.

On the open domain
\Omega=(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{d}\setminus B_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(0)
this is a real-analytic function, more precisely both its real and imaginary components
\textit{Re}(F),\,\textit{Im}(F)
are real-analytic.

Now, suppose by absurd that

\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{rank}([\mathbb{Y}_{\ell,K_{\ell}}]_{d\times d})<d]=\mathbb{P}[\det([\textbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\textbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{d})]_{d\times d})]=0]=\mathbb{P}[F(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d})]>0\,,

and hence that
F
vanishes on a subset of the sphere with positive spherical-volume. Then, since
F
is constant along radial trajectories, this further implies that
F
vanishes on a subset of
\Omega
with positive Lebesgue measure. Since
\textit{Re}(F),\,\textit{Im}(F)
are real-analytic and their zeros sets have positive measure we conclude that both
\textit{Re}(F)
and
\textit{Im}(F)
are null
[
Mit20
, Proposition 1]
. This yields to
F\equiv 0
on
\Omega
which clearly contradicts the fact that
F(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d})=\det([\textbf{Y}_{\ell}(\zeta_{1}),\dots,\textbf{Y}_{\ell}(\zeta_{d})]_{d\times d})\neq 0
.

Therefore we may conclude that
\mathbb{P}[\mathrm{rank}([\mathbb{Y}_{\ell,K_{\ell}}]_{d\times d})<d]=0

and hence that with probability one the random matrix
\mathbb{Y}_{\ell,K_{\ell}}
has a
d\times d
minor of full-rank and hence that
\mathrm{rank}(\mathbb{Y}_{\ell,K_{\ell}})=K_{\ell}\wedge(2\ell+1)
.

Step 2.
As a direct consequence of the first step, if we take
K_{\ell}=2\ell+1
, almost surely there exist coefficients
\eta_{\ell 1},...,\eta_{\ell K_{\ell}}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\coloneqq(a_{\ell m})_{m=-\ell,\dots,\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\eta_{\ell k}\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})=\mathbb{Y}_{\ell,K}\mathbf{\eta}_{\ell}\,,

where
\mathbf{\eta}_{\ell}=(\eta_{\ell 1},...,\eta_{\ell K_{\ell}})^{T}
. Therefore, for any fixed
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we have almost surely

(3.3)

\displaystyle T_{\ell}(x)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}(x)=\mathbf{a}_{\ell}^{T}\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(x)=\mathbf{\eta}_{\ell}^{T}\mathbb{Y}_{\ell,K_{\ell}}^{T}\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\eta_{\ell k}\,\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}Y_{\ell m}(\xi_{k})Y_{\ell m}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\eta_{\ell k}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\langle x,\xi_{k}\rangle)\,.

Moreover, since the field
T
(and hence
T_{\ell}
) is real, from the independence of the uniform directions
(\xi_{k})_{k=1,\dots,K_{\ell}}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
and from the above expression we conclude that the random weights
(\eta_{\ell k})_{\ell,k}
are real as well. Finally, since
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and for any fixed
\ell\geq 0
the decomposition (
3.3
) holds almost surely, we may conclude that our decomposition holds almost surely for the whole field
T=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}T_{\ell}
.
∎

The previous proof further provides an explicit construction for the coefficients
(\eta_{\ell k})_{k=1,\dots,K}
as a function of harmonic coefficients. Indeed, if the
\ell^{th}
frequency component of a strongly sparse random field equals
T_{\ell}=\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}
, then the coefficients of the strongly sparse representation can be easily computed by applying the Moore-Penrose inverse:

\mathbf{\eta}_{\ell\cdot}=(\mathbb{Y}_{\ell,K}^{\star}\mathbb{Y}_{\ell,K})^{-1}\mathbb{Y}_{\ell,K}^{\star}\mathbf{a}_{\ell}\,.

This further suggests that the class of strongly sparse random fields does not contain Gaussian fields. This is a standard result that we recall here with our notation.

Corollary 3.2
.

No monochromatic isotropic Gaussian random field can be sparse.
As a consequence, no Gaussian isotropic random field can be strongly sparse.

Proof.

It suffices to show that no monochromatic Gaussian random field
G_{\ell}=\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell m}^{G}Y_{\ell m}
,
i.e.
, such that there are
K<2\ell+1
i.i.d. uniform random variables on the sphere
(\xi_{k})_{k=1,\dots,K}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
such that

G_{\ell}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\eta_{\ell k}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\langle x,\xi_{k}\rangle)\,.

Now, assuming that such decomposition holds and considering the real-spherical harmonics
\{Y_{\ell m}^{R}\}_{m=-\ell,\dots,\ell}
it is immediate to see that the (real-valued) harmonic coefficients associated to
G_{\ell}
are given by

\displaystyle a_{\ell m}^{R}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}G_{\ell}(x)\,Y_{\ell m}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\eta_{\ell k}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\int P_{\ell}(\langle x,\xi_{k}\rangle)Y_{\ell m}^{R}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\eta_{\ell k}\,Y_{\ell m}^{R}(\xi_{k})\,.

Therefore, the
(2\ell+1)
-Gaussian vector
\textbf{a}^{R}_{\ell}
can be written as a combination of
K<2\ell+1
random variables
\{\textbf{Y}_{\ell}^{R}(\xi_{k})\}_{k=1,\dots,K}
, which is clearly a contradiction.

∎

Remark 3.3

(Empirical power spectrum)
.

Even though the random weights
\eta_{\ell k}
are not independent of the directions
\xi_{k}
in general, we can still study the relationship between the realization of these random variables and the empirical angular power spectrum
\widehat{C}_{\ell}
associated to our field’s realization. More precisely we may mimic the computations performed for
Remark

1.5
and deduce that, almost surely for any fixed realization, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{\ell}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}|\widehat{a}|^{2}_{\ell m}=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\biggl|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}T_{\ell}(x)Y_{\ell m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}(x)\biggr|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\biggl|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\eta_{\ell k}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\left\langle x,\xi_{k}\right\rangle)Y_{\ell m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}(x)\biggr|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\eta_{\ell k}\sum_{m^{\prime}=-\ell}^{\ell}Y_{\ell m^{\prime}}(\xi_{k})\overline{Y}_{\ell m^{\prime}}(x)Y_{\ell m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}(x)\biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\biggl|\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\eta_{\ell k}Y_{\ell m}(\xi_{k})\biggr|^{2}=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{h,k=1}^{K_{\ell}}\eta_{\ell k}\eta_{\ell h}\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})^{T}\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{\ell}(\xi_{h})\,.

Therefore
\widehat{C}_{\ell}
can explicitly be calculated as the quadratic norm of the vector of weights
\{\eta_{\ell k}\}_{k}
weighted with the spherical harmonics scalar product
\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})^{T}\overline{\mathbf{Y}}_{\ell}(\xi_{h})
. In particular, for
K_{\ell}=1
we have
4\pi\widehat{C}_{\ell}=\eta_{\ell}^{2}
.

3.3.
Sparse reconstruction algorithm for monochromatic fields

We conclude this section by analyzing
Algorithm

1
by showing how it can be equivalently defined at the harmonic coefficients’ level. Recall that at each iteration step we choose the best direction by solving

(3.4)

\xi_{k}\in\argmax_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}|T_{\ell}(x,k-1)|^{2}=\argmax_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}|\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(x)\rangle|^{2}\,,

where
\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k)
denote the harmonic coefficients of the residual field
T_{\ell}(\cdot,k)
which is iteratively defined via

T_{\ell}(\cdot,k)\coloneqq T_{\ell}(\cdot,k-1)-T_{\ell}(\xi_{k},k-1)P_{\ell}(\langle\xi_{k},\cdot\rangle)\,.

Therefore, the update for the harmonic coefficients can be directly defined as a Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization since

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k)=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}T_{\ell}(x,k)\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}T_{\ell}(x,k-1)\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}(x)-T_{\ell}(\xi_{k},k-1)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}P_{\ell}(\langle\xi_{k},x\rangle)\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1)-T_{\ell}(\xi_{k},k-1)\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})=\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1)-\frac{\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\rangle}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\|^{2}}\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\,.

Therefore, our algorithm reads as follows

Input:

Monochromatic field’s harmonics
\mathbf{a}_{\ell}
;
Sparsity parameter
K\ll 2\ell+1

Initialize residual harmonic coefficients
\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(0)=\mathbf{a}_{\ell}

for

k=1,\dots,\,K

do

Find best direction
\xi_{k}\in\argmax_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}|\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(x)\rangle|^{2}

Update residual harmonics
\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k)\coloneqq\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1)-\frac{\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\rangle}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\|^{2}}\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})

end for

Output:

Sparse superposition field
S^{K}(\cdot)\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\rangle P_{\ell}(\langle\xi_{k},\cdot\rangle)

Algorithm 2

Sparse reconstruction algorithm: harmonic coefficients

Notice that by summing over
k=1,\,\dots,\,K
in (
3.5
) we immediately see that our sparse approximation field equals

(3.6)

\displaystyle S^{K}(\cdot)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\rangle P_{\ell}(\langle\xi_{k},\cdot\rangle)=\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\rangle}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\|^{2}}\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell m}(\xi_{k})\biggr)Y_{\ell m}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\biggl\langle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\rangle}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\|^{2}}\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k}),\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\cdot)\biggr\rangle=\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(0)-\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(K),\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\cdot)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell},\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\cdot)\rangle-\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(K),\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\cdot)\rangle=T_{\ell}(\cdot)-T_{\ell}(\cdot,K)\,.

The harmonic coefficients formulation obtained in
Algorithm

2
further helps us in understanding the performance of the algorithm; the latter can be seen as a Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization for
\mathbf{a}_{\ell}
, as we detail in the next result.

Theorem 3.4
.

Given
(\xi_{k})_{k=1,\dots,2\ell+1}
computed according to
Algorithm

2
with
K=2\ell+1
, the vectors
\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{2\ell+1})\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2\ell+1}
are orthogonal and hence linearly independent. Thus,
\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(2\ell+1)=\underline{0}
and
T_{\ell}(\cdot,2\ell+1)\equiv 0
. Equivalently, the reconstruction algorithm automatically stops after
K=2\ell+1
steps and fully recovers the original field

(3.7)

T_{\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{2\ell+1}T_{\ell}(\xi_{k};k-1)P_{\ell}(\langle\xi_{k},\cdot\rangle)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\rangle P_{\ell}(\langle\xi_{k},\cdot\rangle)\,.

Proof.

It is enough to show that the vectors
\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{2\ell+1})\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2\ell+1}
are orthogonal.
In view of that, let us first notice that

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k)=\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1)-\frac{\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\rangle}{\|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\|^{2}}\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})=\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{Y_{\ell}}(\xi_{k})^{\perp}}(\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1))\,.

We will show by induction that
\xi_{k}
is chosen such that
\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\in\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y_{\ell}}(\xi_{k-1})\}^{\perp}
and that

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k)=\mathrm{proj}_{\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y_{\ell}}(\xi_{k})\}^{\perp}}(\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(0))=\mathrm{proj}_{\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y_{\ell}}(\xi_{k})\}^{\perp}}(\mathbf{a}_{\ell})\,,

The base case
k=1
trivially follows from our previous discussion. Now assume our claim holds for
k-1
; by definition of
\xi_{k}
we immediately see that

\xi_{k}\in\argmax_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}|\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(x)\rangle|^{2}=\argmax_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\left|\biggl\langle\mathrm{proj}_{\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y_{\ell}}(\xi_{k-1})\}^{\perp}}(\mathbf{a}_{\ell}),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(x)\biggr\rangle\right|^{2}\,.

This shows that
\xi_{k}
is chosen such that
\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\in\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y_{\ell}}(\xi_{k-1})\}^{\perp}
in order to maximize its projection onto such orthogonal space. From this we may conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k)=\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{Y_{\ell}}(\xi_{k})^{\perp}}\circ\mathrm{proj}_{\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y_{\ell}}(\xi_{k-1})\}^{\perp}}(\mathbf{a}_{\ell})=\mathrm{proj}_{\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y_{\ell}}(\xi_{k})\}^{\perp}}(\mathbf{a}_{\ell})\,,

where the last step follows from the fact that
\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\in\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y_{\ell}}(\xi_{k-1})\}^{\perp}
and hence the two orthogonal projection operators commute.

This shows, by induction, the orthogonality of
\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{2\ell+1})\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2\ell+1}
, and hence linear independence. Moreover, from this it immediately follows that the algorithm stops in
K=2\ell+1
steps. Hence
\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(2\ell+1)=\underline{0}
and
T_{\ell}(\cdot,2\ell+1)\equiv 0
since
\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{2\ell+1})\}^{\perp}=\emptyset
. Finally, (
3.7
) follows from (
3.6
) with
K=2\ell+1
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

In practice, the
\xi_{k}
are chosen as the (ranked) most informative directions of the field, similarly to what happens when considering principal components in PCA for covariance matrices.

Remark 3.6
.

In the Gaussian case, any pair of multipoles
T_{\ell},T_{\ell^{\prime}}
is independent for
\ell\neq\ell^{\prime}
. As a consequence, the random variables
\xi_{\ell,k},\xi_{\ell^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
will also be independent, and their sample realization could be exploited as a test for isotropy and Gaussianity. The possibility that some multipole components
T_{\ell},T_{\ell^{\prime}}
may show some unexpected alignment has drawn a lot of attention and a heated debate in the literature on CMB data, see in particular
[
CHSS06
]
,
[
OPQ20
]
; in these papers, possible alignments were probed by means of so-called Maxwell’s vectors (see
[
Den05
]
,
[
Den05
]
), so the construction we advocate here could be used to implement an alternative testing procedure.

Lastly, let us notice that the orthogonality of the vectors
\{\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{2\ell+1})\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2\ell+1}
established in the proof of
Theorem

3.4
combined with
Remark

3.3
immediately implies the following result.

Corollary 3.7
.

Fix
K=2\ell+1
and consider the output sparse field obtained by applying
Algorithm

1
(or
Algorithm

2
) to one single realization of a monochromatic field
T_{\ell}
, that is the field with
\eta_{\ell k}=T_{\ell}(\xi_{k};k-1)

T_{\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{2\ell+1}T_{\ell}(\xi_{k};k-1)P_{\ell}(\langle\xi_{k},\cdot\rangle)=\sum_{k=1}^{2\ell+1}\langle\mathbf{a}_{\ell}(k-1),\,\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})\rangle P_{\ell}(\langle\xi_{k},\cdot\rangle)\,.

Then the empirical power spectrum equals

\widehat{C}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{k=1}^{2\ell+1}\eta_{\ell k}^{2}\,|\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(\xi_{k})|^{2}=(4\pi)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{2\ell+1}\eta_{\ell k}^{2}\,.

3.4.
Sparse reconstruction algorithm for polychromatic fields

In this last section we suggest a possible generalization of our algorithm for polychromatic isotropic random fields. In what follows we fix a spherical random field
T
with power spectrum
(C_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
and sample empirical power spectrum

\widehat{C}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\widehat{a}^{2}_{\ell m}=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\langle T,Y_{\ell m}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}^{2}\,,

which we consider here being a random variable depending on the realization of the original random field
T(x)=T(\omega;x)
.

A priori we further assume the original field
T
to admit a decomposition as in
Theorem

3.1
with positive weights. A discussion on this last assumption is postponed to the end of the current section.

Owing to the computations portrayed in
Remark

3.3
and
Corollary

3.7
, we know that any sparse approximation of the original field with
K=1
must satisfy

\eta_{\ell}^{2}=4\pi\widehat{C}_{\ell}
. Therefore, in this single-wave first approximation a good weight candidate is given by the empirical spectrum,
i.e.
, we can consider the random weights
\widehat{\eta}_{\ell 1}\coloneqq\sqrt{4\pi\widehat{C}_{\ell}}
, which guaranties our variance constraint. Therefore, we can consider as a first approximation the random field

G(x,\xi)\coloneqq\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\widehat{\eta}_{\ell 1}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\langle x,\xi\rangle)\,,

where the parameter
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
is chosen in order to maximize the projection of the original field onto
G(x,\xi)
. More precisely, we seek

\xi_{1}\in\argmax_{\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{|\langle G(\cdot,\xi),\,T(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}|}{\|T\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}^{2}}=\argmax_{\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\frac{|\langle G(\cdot,\xi),\,T(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}|}{\sum_{\ell\geq 0}(2\ell+1)\widehat{C}_{\ell}}\,,

and hence a direction which maximizes the explanatory power of our approximation. Since

\displaystyle\langle G(\cdot,\xi),\,T(\cdot)\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\int\widehat{\eta}_{\ell 1}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\langle x,\xi\rangle)\widehat{a}_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\widehat{\eta}_{\ell 1}\widehat{a}_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}(\xi)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sqrt{4\pi\widehat{C}_{\ell}}\,T_{\ell}(\xi)\,,

we can equivalently seek for
\xi_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
maximizing the projection index

R(\xi)\coloneqq\sqrt{4\pi}\frac{|\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sqrt{\widehat{C}_{\ell}}\,T_{\ell}(\xi)|}{\sum_{\ell\geq 0}(2\ell+1)\widehat{C}_{\ell}}\,.

To summarize, so far we have defined the sparse field with
K=1

T(\cdot\,;1)\coloneqq\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\widehat{\eta}_{\ell 1}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\langle\cdot,\xi_{1}\rangle)\,,

and
R(\xi_{1})=\frac{|\langle T(\cdot\,;1),\,T\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}|}{\|T\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}}
measures its expressiveness. More precisely, if for a fixed small tolerance
\epsilon>0
we have
R(\xi_{1})>1-\epsilon
then
T(\cdot\,;1)
faithfully represents the original field.

On the contrary, if
R(\xi_{1})\leq 1-\epsilon
we can consider the residual field

T^{R}(\cdot)=T(\cdot)-R(\xi_{1})T(\cdot\,;1)
and iterate the previous steps for this residual random field. This would eventually generate a new sequence of weights
\widehat{\eta}_{\ell 2}
and a direction
\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
together with a projection index
R(\xi_{2})
. The latter will measure how far the residual field would be from this new single wave approximation; hence, it will allow to control whether the procedure should be halted or whether it should proceed with a new iteration.

We summarize the above discussion in the following algorithm; here, we take a fixed parameter
K
as we did in
Algorithm

1
and
Algorithm

2
.

Input:

Realization of a random field T with empirical power spectrum
\widehat{C}_{\ell}
;
Tolerance parameter
\epsilon>0
; Maximal sparsity parameter
K

Start counter
k=0

Initialize sparse approximation component
S(\cdot\,;0)=0
and output field
F(\cdot)=0

Initialize projection index
R=0

Initialize residual field
T(\cdot\,;0)=T(\cdot)
and its empirical power spectrum
\widehat{C}_{\ell}(0)=\widehat{C}_{\ell}

while

R\leq 1-\epsilon
and
k<K

do

Update output field
F(\cdot)\leftarrow F+R\,S(\cdot;k)

Update residual field
T(\cdot\,;k+1)=T(\cdot\,;k)-R\,S(\cdot\,;k)=T(\cdot)-F(\cdot)

k\leftarrow k+1

Compute residual empirical power spectrum
\widehat{C}_{\ell}(k)

Define
\hat{\eta}_{\ell k}=\sqrt{4\pi\widehat{C}_{\ell}(k)}

Find
\xi_{k}=\argmax\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sqrt{\widehat{C}_{\ell}(k)}T_{\ell}(\cdot\,;k)

Built new sparse component
S(\cdot\,;k)=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\widehat{\eta}_{\ell k}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}P_{\ell}(\langle\cdot,\xi_{k}\rangle)

Update projection index
R=\sqrt{4\pi}\frac{\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sqrt{\widehat{C}_{\ell}(k)}T_{\ell}(\cdot\,;k)}{\sum_{\ell\geq 0}(2\ell+1)\widehat{C}_{\ell}(k)}

end while

Last update of output field
F(\cdot)\leftarrow F(\cdot)+S(\cdot\,;k)

Output:

Return sparse field
F(\cdot)

Algorithm 3

Sparse reconstruction algorithm for a polychromatic field

Lastly, let us briefly comment on the assumption that our original field
T
admits a decomposition as in
Theorem

3.1
with positive weights. This is a simplicity assumption, motivated by our algorithm design since at each iteration we define
\hat{\eta}_{\ell k}=\sqrt{4\pi\widehat{C}_{\ell}(k)}
in order to enforce the validity of
|\widehat{\eta}_{\ell k}|^{2}=4\pi C_{\ell}(k)
(see also the discussion in
Remark

3.3
and
Corollary

3.7
). However, this last condition is clearly met also for the choice
\hat{\eta}_{\ell k}=-\sqrt{4\pi\widehat{C}_{\ell}(k)}
. We hence believe that a suitable modification of the algorithm should be able to relax our current assumption; we plan to address the mathematical analysis and generalization of this polychromatic sparse algorithm in future works.

Appendix A
On the Wick Product

In this appendix, we recall the definition and main properties of Wick’s product for Gaussian random variables. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and let

Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d})
be a centered Gaussian vector with covariance matrix

\Sigma
. Wick products (or normal ordered products) provide a
renormalized notion of multiplication for real-valued Gaussian random variables,
which plays a fundamental role in Gaussian analysis and Wiener chaos
theory.

Main properties.

Wick products satisfy the following fundamental properties:

(i)

Centering:

\mathbb{E}[\colon Z_{i_{1}}\cdots Z_{i_{k}}\colon]=0

for all
k\geq 1
.

(ii)

Orthogonality:
Wick monomials of different total
degrees are orthogonal in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.

(iii)

Product formula:
for Gaussian variables
X,Y
,

XY=\,\colon XY\colon\,+\mathbb{E}[XY],

and more generally any product of Gaussian variables can be expanded as
a finite sum of Wick products corresponding to all possible contractions.

(iv)

Stability under limits:
Wick products extend by
continuity to limits of polynomial functionals in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.

(v)

Connection to Hermite polynomials:
If
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d})
is a vector of independent standard Gaussian random
variables, then for any multi-index
\underline{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
the Wick monomial
\colon Z^{\underline{\alpha}}\colon\;=\;\colon Z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots Z_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}\colon

coincides with the product of univariate Hermite
polynomials, namely

\colon Z^{\underline{\alpha}}\colon=\prod_{j=1}^{d}H_{\alpha_{j}}(Z_{j}),

where
H_{k}
denotes the
k^{th}
Hermite polynomial.

Wick products therefore provide a canonical orthogonalization of
polynomial Gaussian functionals and are naturally identified with
elements of Wiener chaos, a viewpoint that is central in limit theorems
and diagram formulae.

For instance, if

Z
is a centered Gaussian random variable with unit-variance, then for any integers
p,q\geq 0
, the product of Wick
powers admits the expansion

:Z^{p}:\,:Z^{q}:=\sum_{r=0}^{p\wedge q}r!\binom{p}{r}\binom{q}{r}:Z^{p+q-2r}:,

which follows from the multiplication formula for Hermite polynomials (see
[
NP12
]
).
In particular, Wick products are not multiplicative when sharing common
Gaussian factors, and all possible contractions explicitly contribute.

More generally, for jointly Gaussian random variables

Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
, the product of Wick monomials decomposes as a finite sum
of Wick products obtained by pairing common variables according to their
covariance structure, a representation that underlies the classical
diagram formula.

Example A.1
.

For instance, if
Z_{1},Z_{2},Z_{3}
are jointly standard Gaussian, one obtains

:Z_{1}Z_{2}:\,:Z_{2}Z_{3}:\,=Z_{1}\,:Z_{2}^{2}:\,Z_{3}=H_{1}(Z_{1})\,H_{2}(Z_{2})\,H_{1}(Z_{3})=Z_{1}(Z_{2}^{2}-1)Z_{3}=Z_{1}Z_{2}^{2}Z_{3}+Z_{1}Z_{3}\,,

which illustrates explicitly how common variables generate lower-order
Wick products (hence Hermite polynomials) through Gaussian contractions.

Similarly, for
Z_{1},Z_{2}
jointly standard Gaussian one gets

\colon Z_{1}Z_{2}\colon\,\colon Z_{1}Z_{2}^{2}\colon=\colon Z_{1}^{2}Z_{2}^{3}\colon=H_{2}(Z_{1})\,H_{3}(Z_{2})=(Z_{1}^{2}-1)(Z_{2}^{3}-3Z_{2})\,.
