Exposure to heavy metals and metalloids in PM2.5 and thyroid hormones in pregnant women: a longitudinal study in China
Authors: Dandan Xu, Hao Ding, Zhe Mo, Simeng Gu, Zhengquan Ji, Ping Cheng, Jie Xiang, Yuan Chen, Peiwei Xu, Zhijian Chen, Xiaofeng Wang, Xiaoming Lou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous epidemiological studies have established associations between fine particulate matter (PM2.5) exposure and maternal thyroid hormone suppression. However, as PM2.5 is a complex chemical mixture, the specific contribution of metallic constituents to PM2.5-induced thyroid disruption remains poorly characterized. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between maternal thyroid function and exposure to metallic and metalloid components of PM2.5 during pregnancy. In a longitudinal study of 847 pregnant women recruited from three maternal and child healthcare hospitals, we collected fasting blood samples at 10, 17, and 32 weeks of gestation for thyroid hormone assessment. Concurrently, ambient PM2.5 monitoring stations near participating hospitals quantified 11 metallic/metalloid elements. Linear mixed-effects regression models assessed individual element associations with thyroid parameters, while weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression evaluated combined effects of metal mixtures. Significant inverse associations were observed between lead (Pb) exposure and serum thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) levels. Multiple elements – including aluminum (Al), arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), lead (Pb), antimony (Sb), selenium (Se) and thallium (Tl) – demonstrated negative correlations with free thyroxine (FT4) and free triiodothyronine (FT3) concentrations. WQS analysis revealed that each inter-decile rise in mixed metal exposure was associated with a 0.52% decrease in TSH (95% CI: -3.84%, 2.91%), a 1.52% reduction in FT₄ (95% CI: -1.95%, -1.09%), and a 1.18% decline in FT₃ (95% CI: -1.60%, -0.77%). Tl, Al, and As emerged as primary contributors to these effects, collectively accounting for 86%- 97% of the weighted mixture impact. These findings demonstrate that metallic components of PM2.5, particularly Tl, Al and As are significantly associated with suppressed maternal thyroid function during pregnancy. This highlights the importance of metal-specific toxicity in PM2.5-related endocrine disruption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48647-4.

Introduction

Air pollution has been extensively documented as a critical public health concern, with particular emphasis on the adverse effects of fine particulate matter (PM2.5) exposure1–5. Substantial epidemiological evidence demonstrates that PM2.5 exposure elevates risks of health emergencies, hospitalizations, and mortality6–10. As a complex mixture comprising organic compounds, carbonaceous species, and toxic metallic constituents11,12, PM2.5-associated metals such as arsenic (As), lead (Pb), and cadmium (Cd) have been implicated in multi-system toxicity, including neurological impairment and carcinogenicity13–15.

The thyroid system, responsible for regulating metabolic homeostasis and neurodevelopment through thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) secretion, exhibits particular vulnerability to environmental insults16–18. Maternal hypothyroidism, linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes and fetal neurodevelopmental deficits19,20, has shown epidemiological associations with PM2.5 exposure21–23. While accumulating evidence has demonstrated associations between PM2.5 exposure and thyroid dysfunction during pregnancy24–28, most studies have treated PM2.5 mass concentration as the exposure metric, considering this complex mixture as a single entity. However, PM2.5 is a heterogeneous mixture containing various chemical constituents (including heavy metals) that may exhibit differential toxicity and exert distinct effects on endocrine function29–31. Recent reviews have emphasized that investigating the specific components within air pollution mixtures, particularly heavy metals, represents a critical research gap32. Epidemiological evidence directly linking PM2.5-bound metal exposures to alterations in maternal thyroid hormones remains extremely limited.

Although several studies have explored associations between PM2.5-bound metals and thyroid function in pregnant populations33, key questions remain unresolved. It is still unclear which specific metal constituents exert the greatest impact on maternal thyroid function, and whether synergistic or antagonistic interactions exist among these metal components. Furthermore, existing studies are predominantly cross-sectional or cohort-based without fine-grained temporal characterization33,34, lacking a comprehensive assessment of the causal relationship between the timing of metal exposure across different gestational windows and the dynamic changes in thyroid hormone levels. Some studies have focused on exposure during early pregnancy26,33, while others have covered the entire gestational period28, but systematic investigations into the susceptibility of specific exposure windows are still lacking.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a comprehensive investigation into the associations between PM2.5-bound metal constituents and maternal thyroid hormone levels across pregnancy. This longitudinal study was carried out in three maternal and child health hospitals in Zhejiang Province, China, integrating atmospheric PM2.5 monitoring, metal composition analysis, and serial assessments of thyroid function during the first, second, and third trimesters. By establishing exposure-response relationships between PM2.5 metal constituents and maternal thyroid function, we aimed to identify the specific metal components associated with thyroid hormone alterations and provide novel insights into the thyroid-disrupting potential of particulate matter constituents.

Methods

Study population and design

This longitudinal observational study was conducted from November 2019 to July 2021 at three maternal and child healthcare hospitals in Taizhou, Lishui, and Quzhou, Zhejiang Province, located in eastern China. The three study sites - Taizhou (eastern coastal), Lishui (southwestern mountainous), and Quzhou (western inland) - were selected to capture spatial variability in PM2.5 composition and sources across different geographic and industrial contexts in Zhejiang Province. All three locations have maternal and child healthcare hospitals capable of conducting prenatal follow-up and biological sample collection, ensuring the feasibility of longitudinal data collection. Zhejiang Province exhibits considerable heterogeneity in industrialization and air pollution levels across cities. Previous studies have documented significant differences in PM2.5-bound metal constituents among the three selected areas. Specifically, concentrations of Pb, Cd and As in PM2.5 were significantly higher in Taizhou and Quzhou than the provincial average, whereas Lishui exhibited relatively better overall air quality35. This inter-city gradient in metal composition enhances our ability to detect exposure-response relationships between specific metal constituents and maternal thyroid hormone levels. During the study period, 847 women in early pregnancy (gestational age ≤ 10 weeks) meeting the following criteria were enrolled: (1) Aged 18–45 years; (2) Resided in the study area for ≥ 1 year with no relocation plans for the next three years; (3) No family history of thyroid diseases; (4) Absence of hypertension, thyroid disorders, or other chronic diseases (medically confirmed); (5) No iodine-containing contrast agent exposure within the preceding year; (6) Proficient communication skills and provision of written informed consent. All participants committed to regular prenatal examinations and biological sample collection.

Baseline data, including demographics (age, education level, occupation) and lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol use), were collected using a standardized questionnaire administered through face-to-face interviews by trained research staff at the time of enrollment. Fasting venous blood and first-morning urine samples were systematically collected at three time points: first trimester (10 weeks), second trimester (17 weeks), and third trimester (32 weeks). Blood and urine samples were transported under cold chain conditions to a central laboratory, where thyroid hormone concentrations were measured using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay. Urinary iodine concentration (UIC) was determined via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry.

After excluding participants with incomplete baseline data (n = 46), missing data on thyroid hormones (n = 108), thyroid dysfunction requiring pharmacological intervention [Guideline on Diagnosis and Management for Thyroid Diseases during Pregnancy and Postpartum (2nd Edition), TSH > 4.52 mIU/L, and FT4 < 13.15 pmol/L, n = 45], 648 pregnant women were included in the statistical analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (No. 2019-006-01) in July 2019. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measurement of metal elements in PM2.5

During the study period, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention near the maternal and child healthcare hospital conducted ambient airborne PM2.5 sampling, and the distance between them was less than 3 km. The median distance between participants’ residential addresses and the monitoring station was 2.64 km (IQR: 1.85–3.60), 64.1% of participants lived within 3.0 km of the monitoring station, and only 10.7% of participants lived outside 4.0 km. A map of the study area is shown in Fig. 1. Continuous sampling was conducted with a medium-volume air sampler (LECKEL MSV6, German) at a flow rate of 38.3 L/min from the 10th to the 16th of each month, and a filter membrane was collected every day. Airborne PM2.5 was trapped on the filters. After sampling, the filter membranes were sent to a laboratory through a -20 °C cold chain. All filters were weighed by an automatic filter-weighing system (WZZ-02, Hangzhou, China) before and after sampling. The mass concentrations of PM2.5 were calculated based on the weight differences and sampling volumes. After ultrasonic extraction pretreatment, the concentrations of metal and metalloid elements in PM2.5 were determined using inductively coupled plasma‒mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, ICAP Qc ICP-MS). The filter was positioned in the bottom of a 15-mL centrifuge tube filled with 10 mL of 5% nitric acid. The tube was then heated to 70 °C and subjected to ultrasonic extraction for a duration of 3 h. Following cooling, centrifugation was performed at 4500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant, collected from the top layer, was further filtered through a 0.45-µm membrane prior to ICP-MS analysis. We detected 11 metal and metalloid elements, including antimony (Sb), aluminum (Al), arsenic (As), beryllium (Be), cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr), mercury (Hg), lead (Pb), manganese (Mn), selenium (Se), thallium (Tl). The limits of detection (LODs) and detection rates are shown in Table S1. The detection rates for Be, Cr, and Hg were too low to be included in the data analyses (< 60%). The detection rates for the other eight metal and metalloid elements were all above 90%. Metal concentrations below the LOD were assigned a value of LOD/2.

Measurement of biological samples

At three gestational time points (10, 17, and 32 weeks of pregnancy), physicians at maternal and child healthcare hospitals collected fasting blood and random urine samples from pregnant women. For each blood sample, 2 mL of serum was isolated and aliquoted into two tubes (1 mL per tube). Similarly, each 10 mL urine sample was divided into two aliquots (5 mL per tube). All biological specimens were transported to the same central laboratory under a -20 °C cold chain. Serum concentrations of thyroid hormones were measured using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA; Cobas® e601; Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Biological specimen analyses and quality control were performed following the manufacturer’s protocols. Quality control samples were randomly tested in parallel with study samples to validate the precision of the analytical methods. Thyroid hormone indicators included serum thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb), and thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb). We used the laboratory recommended cutoffs for TPOAb and TgAb positivity (TPOAb positive if > 60 IU/ml, TgAb positive if > 60 IU/ml).

The two urine samples were allocated for the determination of urinary iodine concentration (UIC) and urinary creatinine concentration (UCC), respectively. UIC was quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; ICAPR02041, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) following pretreatment with acidic (1% HNO3 + 5% CH3OH) diluents‌. Urinary creatinine was analyzed via the creatinine oxidase method on an automated biochemical analyzer (Beckman AU480, Beckman Instruments, Brea, CA). Quality control samples were randomly tested in parallel with study samples. Creatinine-normalized UIC values (µg/g creatinine) were calculated to mitigate urinary dilution variability‌.

Statistical analyses

Correlations among different metal components were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation, performed with R software (version 4.2.1; https://www.r-project.org/). We adopted linear mixed-effects regression models with study region and participants as random effects to estimate the associations between single metal elements and thyroid hormone indicators. Based on evidence that metals can disrupt thyroid function through rapid mechanisms (within weeks to months), such as inhibition of thyroid peroxidase and sodium-iodide symporter31,36, and following the methodology of previous large cohort studies that employed similar lagged exposure windows31,37, we used the three-month average concentration of PM2.5-bound metal elements prior to each thyroid hormone measurement as the exposure metric. Metal concentrations were categorized into deciles to align with the assumptions of weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression, which requires all exposure variables to be on a common scale (e.g., quantiles) to facilitate interpretable mixture weights. This approach also enhances comparability across different metal elements with varying concentration ranges and units. The outcome indicators (TSH, FT4, and FT3) were transformed by natural logarithm to improve the approximation of the normal distribution. All models were adjusted for maternal age (years), body mass index (BMI) before pregnancy (kg/m2), education (junior high school and below, high school, college, and above), gestational week (weeks), smoking status (yes, no), passive smoking status (yes, no), drinking status (yes, no), creatinine-normalized UIC (µg/g creatinine), TPOAb (negative, positive), TgAb (negative, positive), and temperature on the day of the thyroid hormone measurement (℃). To account for multiple comparisons across the three thyroid outcomes and multiple metal elements, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure to the p-values obtained from the single-metal linear mixed models. Adjusted p-values were calculated and are presented alongside the raw p-values in Table S3. Associations with FDR-adjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The WQS model was used to analyze the effect of exposure to mixed metal elements on thyroid hormones in pregnant women. The WQS model assigned weights to individual metals based on their relative contributions to the overall mixture effect. This approach addresses multicollinearity among pollutants and quantifies the cumulative impact of metal mixtures on thyroid function. Like in the single metal element analysis model, the thyroid hormone concentrations were converted to natural logarithms, and the metal element concentrations were categorized into deciles. Cumulative exposure to each PM2.5‑bound metal was estimated as the average concentration across the entire pregnancy before the third‑trimester thyroid hormone measurement. WQS regression was applied to investigate the associations between cumulative metal mixture exposure and maternal thyroid hormone levels in the third trimester, adjusting for the same covariates as in the main analyses.

We performed stratified analyses by gestational period (first, second, and third trimesters), age (< 30, ≥ 30), BMI (< 24.0, ≥ 24.0), TPOAb (negative, positive), TgAb (negative, positive), and iodine nutrition status (adequate, insufficient, with UIC < 150 µg/g creatinine as insufficient)38 to analyze the association between metal exposure and thyroid hormones in different population subgroups to determine the sensitive population. We excluded TPOAb- and TgAb-positive pregnant women from the sensitivity analysis to test the robustness of our results. To address potential concerns regarding the stability of weight estimates derived from 100 iterations, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using 1,000 bootstrap iterations for the primary mixture models.

The data analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.1; https://www.r-project.org/) and the gWQS package. The map of the study area was created using ArcGIS software (version 10.2, 64-bit; https://www.esri.com/arcgis). All the statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

The mean age of the participants was approximately 28 years, and the mean BMI was 21.8 kg/m2. We investigated the participants’ smoking and drinking habits before pregnancy. There were few smokers (only 2.0%), but pregnant women who smoked passively accounted for 28.9% of the sample. Pregnant women who drank accounted for 6.79% of the sample. Results of the descriptive data analysis

Table 2 presents the descriptive statistics of the thyroid hormones in the pregnant women, while Table 3 shows the descriptive statistics of the metal concentrations in air PM2.5 during the study period. TSH concentrations were significantly greater in the third trimester (P < 0.001). FT4 and FT3 were significantly greater in the first trimester and gradually decreased in the second and third trimesters (P < 0.001). In the first trimester, 7.25% of the pregnant women tested positive for TPOAb, while the positivity rates were 5.56% and 4.01% in the second and third trimesters, respectively, with statistically significant differences between the trimesters (P < 0.05). In the first trimester, 12.7% of the women tested positive for TgAb, while the positivity rates were 9.57% and 5.40% in the second and third trimesters, respectively, with statistically significant differences between the trimesters (P < 0.05). The median UIC concentrations were 106.0 µg/L in the first trimester, 107.0 µg/L in the second trimester, and 97.10 µg/L in the third trimester.

According to Table 3, among the components of PM2.5, the concentrations of Al, Mn and Pb were the highest. The concentrations of As, Mn, Pb and Sb increased progressively from the first to third trimester (P < 0.001). The exposure concentrations of Cd, Se, and Tl showed statistically significant variations across different trimesters (P < 0.05), with the highest levels observed during the second trimester‌. Figure S1 shows the results of the correlation analysis. The correlations between Tl and Se and Cd were high, with values of 0.89 and 0.86, respectively. The correlation between As and Pb was also high, at 0.85. The correlations between Mn and Se and Tl were relatively low, with values of 0.13 and 0.38, respectively.

Associations of single metals in PM2.5 exposure with thyroid hormones

Figure 2 and table S3 illustrate the associations between individual metals and thyroid hormones in pregnant women. We found that exposure to Pb was associated with a decrease in TSH, but there were no statistically significant associations between other metals and TSH. Exposure to individual metals was associated with decreases in FT4 and FT3 (P < 0.05). For each decile increase in Pb concentration, TSH decreased by 2.27% (95% CI: -4.14%, -0.37%). For each decile increase in the Al concentration, FT4 decreased by 0.42% (95% CI: -0.68%, -0.17%), and FT3 decreased by 0.65% (95% CI: -0.88%, -0.42%). For each decile increase in Tl concentration, FT4 decreased by 1.12% (95% CI: -1.45%, -0.78%), and FT3 decreased by 0.99% (95% CI: -1.30%, -0.68%). Among the eight metals, only the association between Mn and FT4 was not statistically significant.

Association of metals in PM2.5 co-exposure with thyroid hormones

Figure 3 illustrates the associations between mixed metal exposure and thyroid hormones in pregnant women. To evaluate the combined effects of PM2.5-bound metal mixtures on maternal thyroid hormones, we performed WQS regression with a random split of the study population into a training set (40%, n = 259) and a validation set (60%, n = 389). Weight estimations were calculated across 100 bootstrap samples in the training set. The validation set was then used to assess the significance of the estimated mixture effect. For TSH, the mixture index showed no significant association in the validation set: a decile increase corresponded to a 0.52% decrease (95% CI: − 3.84%, 2.91%). Weight estimates from the training set identified Pb (32.8%) and Sb (23.7%) as the main contributors. For every decile increase in mixed metal exposure, FT4 decreased by 1.52% (95% CI: -1.95%, -1.09%), with Tl contributing 55.9% and As contributing 22.6%. For every decile increase in mixed metal exposure, FT3 decreased by 1.18% (95% CI: -1.60%, -0.77%), with Tl contributing the most (60.0%) and Al contributing 37.0%. The consistency between training and validation findings supports the robustness of the observed associations.

Results of stratified analyses

The results of the stratified analyses are presented in Table 4. They showed that in the first, second, and third trimesters, mixed metal exposure was associated with a decrease in FT4 and FT3. For every decile increase in mixed metal exposure in the first trimester, decreases of 0.99% (95% CI: -1.83%, -0.13%) in FT4 and 0.88% (95% CI: -1.60%, -0.16%) in FT3 were observed. In the third trimester, the effect of mixed metal exposure on thyroid hormones was more significant. For every decile increase in mixed metal exposure, decreases of 1.31% (95% CI: -2.08%, -0.54%) in FT4 and 1.57% (95% CI: -2.35%, -0.78%) in FT3 were observed. Cumulative exposure to PM2.5‑bound metals was also associated with decreased FT4 levels. Each decile increase in the cumulative WQS index was associated with a 0.96% (95% CI:-1.71%, -0.20%) reduction in FT4, although the effect size was slightly attenuated relative to single‑trimester estimates. No significant associations were observed for TSH or FT3 in cumulative exposure models. A stratified analysis by maternal age showed that the effect of mixed metal exposure on thyroid hormones during pregnancy was more significant in pregnant women over 30 years of age. For every decile increase in mixed metal exposure, decreases of 1.57% (95% CI: -2.24%, -0.89%) in FT4 and 1.41% (95% CI: -1.99%, -0.83%) in FT3 were observed. A stratified analysis by maternal BMI showed that the effect of mixed metal exposure on FT4 during pregnancy was more pronounced in overweight pregnant women. For every decile increase in mixed metal exposure, a decrease of 1.70% (95% CI: -2.86%, -0.52%) in FT4 was observed. The stratified analyses by TPOAb and TgAb status showed that the effect of mixed metal exposure on pregnancy FT4 was more pronounced in pregnant women who were TPOAb-positive and TgAb-positive. For every decile increase in mixed metal exposure, decreases of 2.63 (-4.64, -0.59) and 3.30 (-5.13, -1.44) in FT4 were observed. The stratified analysis by iodine nutrition status showed that the association between PM2.5-bound metal mixtures and maternal thyroid hormones varied by iodine status, with a slightly stronger association observed in pregnant women with insufficient iodine nutrition (P < 0.05), suggesting potential effect modification.

Results of sensitivity analyses

Figure S2 shows the results of the sensitivity analysis. We conducted a sensitivity analysis by excluding TPOAb- and TgAb-positive pregnant women. The results show that, for each decile increase in mixed metal exposure, there was a decrease of 0.77% (95% CI: -1.26%, -0.28%) in FT4, with Cd, Tl, and Se making the greatest contributions, and a decrease of 0.77% (95% CI: -1.19%, -0.35%) in FT3, with Tl and As making the greatest contributions. This is consistent with our main analysis results, indicating that our results are relatively robust. In the sensitivity analysis examining the stability of bootstrap weight estimates (Fig. S3), we observed high consistency between models using 100 and 1,000 bootstrap iterations. The weight estimates for individual metal elements were strongly correlated across the two approaches, and the significance of the overall mixture effect remained unchanged. These findings indicate that our results are robust to the number of bootstrap samples.

Discussion

Key findings

This study investigated the effects of airborne PM2.5-bound metal exposure on thyroid hormones in pregnant women, revealing significant associations between specific metals and hormone disruption. Exposure to Pb was linked to reduced TSH, while Al, As, Cd, Pb, Sb, Se, and Tl correlated with decreased FT4 and FT3. Using WQS modeling, mixed metal exposure demonstrated a synergistic suppression of thyroid hormones, with Tl, Al, and As identified as primary contributors, collectively accounting for 86%- 97% of the weighted mixture impact. These effects were consistently observed across all trimesters, underscoring the persistence of metal-induced thyroid disruption during critical fetal developmental windows. Subgroup analyses highlighted heightened vulnerability among pregnant women over 30 years old, those with pre-pregnancy overweight (showing greater declines in FT4 and FT3), and individuals positive for thyroid autoantibodies (TPOAb or TgAb), who exhibited exacerbated FT4 reduction. These findings emphasize the role of PM2.5-bound metals in thyroid dysfunction, particularly in susceptible populations, and advocate for targeted interventions to mitigate prenatal exposure risks.

Comparison with previously published studies

A key contribution of our study is the assessment of metal mixture effects using WQS regression. Our finding that mixed metal exposure exerted a synergistic inhibitory effect on thyroid hormones, with Tl, Al, and As identified as primary contributors, aligns with the growing recognition in the literature that co-exposure scenarios are important to consider. Several studies have begun to examine metal mixtures in relation to maternal thyroid function39,40. For example, a study evaluating exposure to Cd, Sb, Pb, and their mixtures found associations with thyroid homeostasis during pregnancy that were more pronounced in mixture models than in single-metal analyses40. Another investigation into metals bound to PM2.5 and maternal thyroid function in early pregnancy suggested that mixed exposure to multiple metals in PM2.5 may have interactive effects on thyroid hormones33. Our identification of Tl and Al as major contributors is particularly noteworthy. Tl has been less studied in the context of thyroid disruption, and our findings suggest it may warrant greater attention. Al, while often considered relatively benign, has been implicated in endocrine disruption, and our results are consistent with a small number of studies suggesting potential thyroid effects41.

Our observation that Pb exposure was associated with decreased TSH levels differs from several previous studies. A number of investigations have reported positive associations between Pb and TSH in pregnant populations37,42. For instance, a study among Chinese pregnant women found that urinary Pb exposure was associated with elevated TSH levels, particularly during the first and third trimesters37. The discrepancy between our finding of Pb-associated TSH reduction and the more commonly reported TSH elevation may relate to differences in exposure assessment matrices (PM2.5-bound metals in our study vs. blood or urinary biomarkers in others), exposure levels, the timing of thyroid hormone measurements, or the presence of co-exposures that modify Pb’s effects.

The identification of As as a metal associated with reduced FT4 and FT3 levels, and as a major contributor in the mixture model, aligns with studies suggesting that arsenic exposure during pregnancy may affect maternal and neonatal thyroid hormone parameters. A prospective cohort study reported that maternal serum arsenic exposure was inversely associated with total thyroxine (TT4) levels43. The Ma’anshan Birth Cohort Study in China explored prenatal exposure to fourteen metals, including arsenic, and found associations with fetal and neonatal thyroid function, with maternal vitamin D levels potentially playing a modulatory role44. Our findings for As are consistent with these reports and further highlight its importance as a thyroid disruptor in the context of PM2.5 exposure.

The association between Cd exposure and reduced FT4 and FT3 levels observed in this study is consistent with a substantial body of epidemiological evidence. Several studies have reported that Cd exposure is associated with decreased FT4 and increased TSH in pregnant women40,42. A study of Greek pregnant women found associations between exposure to cadmium, antimony, lead, and their mixtures with altered thyroid homeostasis during gestation40. In a Chinese study, increases in whole blood cadmium levels during the second trimester were associated with decreases in FT4 levels42. Our results for Cd are therefore in strong agreement with the existing literature.

Our analysis provided preliminary evidence that iodine nutrition status may modify the association between PM2.5-bound metal mixtures and maternal thyroid hormones, with a slightly stronger association in iodine-insufficient pregnant women. This is biologically plausible, iodine is essential for thyroid hormone synthesis, and iodine deficiency may impair thyroid function, increasing its vulnerability to metal-induced disruption. This observation aligns with previous studies45,46, which report that iodine deficiency enhances metal-induced thyroid toxicity, potentially through synergistic interference with thyroid hormone synthesis and metabolism.

Our study further evaluated the cumulative effects of prenatal exposure to PM2.5‑bound metals across the entire pregnancy. Consistent with trimester‑specific findings, cumulative exposure to metal mixtures was associated with a small reduction in FT4 levels. However, the effect size of cumulative exposure was slightly weaker than that observed in single‑trimester analyses. This attenuation may be partly attributed to the dilution of transient but critical exposure peaks when averaging across the whole pregnancy. In addition, the maternal thyroid axis may exhibit mild adaptive responses to long‑term, low‑level metal exposure, which could also contribute to the relatively modest cumulative effect. Despite the smaller magnitude, the persistent association between cumulative metal exposure and reduced FT4 supports that chronic exposure to PM2.5‑bound metals throughout pregnancy may exert subtle but sustained disruption on maternal thyroid function.

In this study, we observed that exposure to PM2.5-bound metal elements was associated with decreased levels of both free thyroid hormones (FT4 and FT3) and TSH. This pattern appears inconsistent with the classical negative feedback regulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis, where decreased thyroid hormone levels typically stimulate TSH secretion. Several potential mechanisms may explain this observation. First, this concurrent decrease may point to central hypothyroidism, characterized by low FT4 accompanied by normal or low TSH. Certain metals may directly or indirectly affect hypothalamic TRH neurons or pituitary thyrotrophs, leading to reduced TSH secretion and consequently decreased thyroid hormone levels47. Second, environmental pollutants may disrupt normal feedback sensitivity at the hypothalamic or pituitary level, resulting in “central resistance to thyroid hormone”48,49. Third, chronic metal exposure may induce a state resembling non-thyroidal illness syndrome (NTIS), where systemic inflammation suppresses TSH production despite low thyroid hormone levels50. Similar findings have been reported in previous research. The PROTECT cohort study (2025) also observed no significant association between PM2.5 exposure and TSH, attributing this pattern to subclinical disruption of the HPT axis51. Future studies with repeated hormone measurements and functional assays are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Potential underlying mechanisms

The thyroid-disrupting effects of PM2.5-bound Tl, Al, and As—identified as primary contributors in our WQS model—arise from distinct yet interconnected toxicological pathways. Tl, a coal combustion byproduct, exhibits potent neuroendocrine toxicity through ionic mimicry: its physicochemical similarity to potassium enables competitive inhibition of Na+-K+-ATPase, disrupting cellular ion homeostasis52. This interference likely extends to thyroid follicular cells, where Tl may impair thyroglobulin iodination and hormone secretion via hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis dysregulation. Al, ubiquitous in industrial emissions, induces oxidative stress-mediated damage across multiple organ systems. Emerging evidence suggests Al promotes amyloidogenic protein aggregation in thyroid tissue through ROS overproduction53, potentially accelerating autoimmune thyroiditis—a mechanism amplified in TPOAb/TgAb-positive pregnancies. As, a metalloid from fossil fuel combustion and mining activities, disrupts thyroid function via dual pathways: direct inhibition of deiodinase enzymes critical for T4-to-T3 conversion, and epigenetic modulation of thyroid autoimmunity genes54. An experiment corroborate these findings, with As-exposed guinea pigs showing dose-dependent declines in circulating thyroxine linked to follicular cell apoptosis55. Collectively, these metals exploit endocrine vulnerabilities through molecular mimicry, redox imbalance, and immunomodulation, establishing PM2.5 as a multi-mechanistic thyroid disruptor in pregnancy.

Health risks of decreased thyroid hormone levels in pregnant women

While the observed percentage changes are modest at the individual level, they may have meaningful implications at the population level, particularly for pregnant women with pre-existing thyroid conditions or iodine insufficiency. Small shifts in thyroid hormone distributions could increase the proportion of women falling below clinically relevant thresholds. Decreased thyroid function during pregnancy is significantly associated with adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes20,56–58. Comprehensive cohort studies demonstrate that this condition elevates risks of preterm birth, miscarriage, and preeclampsia59, while correlating with a 35% increased incidence of neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, 6.3-fold higher risk of low birth weight60, and long-term neurodevelopmental impairments (15–20% reduction in cognitive scores)19. Our findings demonstrate that exposure to metal constituents in PM2.5 significantly suppresses thyroid hormone levels. It is recommended that pregnant women take protective measures such as wearing masks, using air purifiers, and regularly checking thyroid function indicators to reduce maternal health risks and the risk of adverse birth outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several advantages. First, PM2.5 compositional profiling quantifying 11 metal species enables precise exposure characterization, while a longitudinal design tracking thyroid hormone dynamics across trimesters (gestational weeks 10, 17, and 32) captures pregnancy-stage-specific thyroid hormone changes. Crucially, the application of WQS regression advances beyond conventional single-pollutant models by quantifying synergistic effects of metal mixtures and revealing the effects of mixed exposure on thyroid hormones.

However, there are also some study limitations. First, the exposure data were obtained from a fixed location near the maternal and child healthcare hospital, and the actual exposure concentration of each pregnant woman were not measured, which may have resulted in misclassification of the exposure concentrations. However, the three maternal and child healthcare hospitals we selected were district-level hospitals, and pregnant women who visited these hospitals for pregnancy tests lived nearby. Thus, the data from the monitoring stations can be used to effectively estimate the actual exposure level of pregnant women. Any resulting exposure misclassification is likely non-differential, which would tend to bias effect estimates toward the null. Monitoring instruments that can be carried by individuals should be used in future studies to obtain precise air pollution exposure data. Second, although medication data gaps persist, rigorous exclusion of subjects requiring thyroid pharmacotherapy (based on early pregnancy thyroid hormone test results) minimizes confounding. Third, our analysis focused exclusively on metallic constituents within ambient PM2.5, potentially overlooking contributions from other exposure pathways such as dietary intake, drinking water, or soil contact, which may independently or synergistically influence maternal thyroid homeostasis. Additionally, while we identified associations between metal exposure and thyroid hormone suppression, the clinical implications of these findings remain incompletely defined due to the lack of linkage to adverse pregnancy outcomes (e.g., preterm birth, fetal neurodevelopmental deficits). Future studies integrating multi-media exposure assessments and longitudinal tracking of pregnancy endpoints are warranted to clarify the mechanistic bridge between metal-induced thyroid disruption and perinatal health consequences. Fourth, we only focused on the association between PM2.5-bound metals and thyroid hormone levels during pregnancy, and did not consider other important constituents of PM2.5, such as organic carbon, elemental carbon, sulfates, nitrates, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Previous studies have shown that these constituents may also have toxic effects on the thyroid gland34,61. Due to the limitations of research funds and detection techniques, we were unable to measure all constituents of PM2.5 in this study. In future studies, we will try to measure multiple constituents of PM2.5 simultaneously and use mixture analysis methods to assess the combined effects of different PM2.5 constituents on thyroid hormone levels during pregnancy, which will help to better understand the mechanism of PM2.5-induced thyroid disruption.

Conclusions

Our study advances PM2.5 toxicology research by demonstrating that specific metallic constituents (not bulk particulate mass) drive thyroid endocrine disruption in pregnancy. While prior epidemiological work established associations between total PM2.5 exposure and maternal hypothyroxinemia, we quantify mixture effects of 8 PM2.5-bound metals through WQS regression. The model identified Tl, Al, As as dominant contributors to trimester-specific thyroid suppression, with effects persisting across all gestational phases. This metallic interference with thyroid homeostasis may affect pregnancy outcomes and the health of their offspring. These findings necessitate revised air quality standards incorporating metal-specific thresholds and targeted obstetric interventions.
