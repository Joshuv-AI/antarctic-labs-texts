Regularity and Convergence Properties of Finite Free Convolutions
Katsunori Fujie

Abstract
Finite free convolutions, ⊞ d \boxplus_{d} and ⊠ d \boxtimes_{d} , are binary operations on polynomials of degree d d that are central to finite free probability, a developing field at the intersection of free probability and the geometry of polynomials. Motivated by established regularities in free probability, this paper investigates analogous regularities for finite free convolutions. Key findings include triangle inequalities for these convolutions and necessary and sufficient conditions regarding atoms of probability measures. Applications of these results include proving the weak convergence of ⊞ d \boxplus_{d} and ⊠ d \boxtimes_{d} to their infinite counterparts ⊞ \boxplus and ⊠ \boxtimes as d → ∞ d\to\infty , without compactness assumptions. Furthermore, this weak convergence is strengthened to convergence in Kolmogorov distance.

Introduction

1.1
Background and motivation for the research

Finite free probability is a branch that has been rapidly developing since the celebrated foundational work by Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava
[
19
]
(see also
[
18
]
).
Their work was originally motivated by longstanding problems in operator algebra and graph theory; they provided an affirmative answer to the Kadison–Singer problem and constructed non-trivial Ramanujan graphs
[
20
,
21
]
.
They primarily utilized techniques known from geometry of polynomials, such as real-rootedness and interlacing properties (see Definition
2.17
and the explanation below). Their main contribution was to highlight the connections among free probability, random matrix theory, and polynomial convolutions defined almost 100 years ago by Szegő and Walsh, which are termed finite free additive and multiplicative convolutions, respectively, in this paper.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
denote the set of monic polynomials of degree
d
.
We also use
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
to denote the subset of
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
having only real roots.
For
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
, we express
p
as

p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}x^{d-k}(-1)^{k}\binom{d}{k}\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(p).

We adopt this seemingly unfamiliar notation as we believe it is optimal for stating the definitions and various results.
Given polynomials
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
,
the finite free additive and multiplicative convolutions
are respectively defined as follows:

\displaystyle p\boxplus_{d}q(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{d}x^{d-k}(-1)^{k}\binom{d}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\tilde{e}_{i}^{(d)}(p)\tilde{e}_{k-i}^{(d)}(q);

(1.1)

\displaystyle p\boxtimes_{d}q(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{d}x^{d-k}(-1)^{k}\binom{d}{k}\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(p)\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(q).

(1.2)

The main interest of this paper lies in these two operations. The first, (
1.1
), was previously defined and studied by Walsh
[
33
]
, while the second, (
1.2
), was essentially defined by Szegő
[
27
]
and is known as the Schur–Szegő composition (see also
[
22
, Chapter 4]
). For the reader’s convenience, their basic properties are summarized in Section
2.4
. The foundational work of Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava establishes the following connection
[
19
, Theorems 1.2 and 1.5]
: let
A
and
B
be
d\times d
normal matrices and
U
be a
d\times d
Haar-distributed unitary matrix (i.e., a random unitary matrix uniformly distributed on the unitary group of degree
d
). If
p(x)=\det(xI-A)
and
q(x)=\det(xI-B)
, then

p\boxplus_{d}q(x)=E_{U}[\det(xI-A-UBU^{*})]\qquad\text{and}\qquad p\boxtimes_{d}q(x)=E_{U}[\det(xI-AUBU^{*}A)],

which indicates their connection to free probability.

Free probability, pioneered by Voiculescu in the 1980s, provides a powerful framework for understanding non-commutative random variables, particularly in the context of operator algebras and random matrix theory
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
(see also the standard textbooks
[
24
,
26
,
28
]
and references therein). At its heart lies the concept of “freeness,” an analogue of classical independence for non-commutative variables, and many analogues to concepts in classical probability have been established.
For instance, given probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
,
the free additive convolution

\mu\boxplus\nu
is defined as the distribution of
X+Y
, where
X
and
Y
are freely independent non-commutative random variables distributed as
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively. Additionally, if the support of
\nu
is contained in
[0,\infty)
(equivalently,
Y\geq 0
),
the free multiplicative convolution

\mu\boxtimes\nu
is defined as the distribution of
\sqrt{Y}X\sqrt{Y}
.
It then becomes natural to define and study, for example, the law of large numbers, central limit theorems, free infinite divisibility, and the Lévy–Khintchine representation, among others.

This theory has achieved remarkable success in describing the asymptotic behavior of large
N\times N
random matrices as
N
approaches infinity.
For instance, the limiting eigenvalue distribution of the sum of two independent random matrices, whose distributions are invariant under unitary action, is described by the free additive convolution of their individual limiting distributions.
This phenomenon is well known as the asymptotic freeness of random matrices.
The relation between the operations
\boxplus_{d}
and
\boxtimes_{d}
and random matrices, discovered by Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava, suggests a fundamental connection to the free convolutions
\boxplus
and
\boxtimes
as
d\to\infty
.

Indeed, many free probabilistic analogues hold in finite free probability, including the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem
[
18
]
, finite free cumulants
[
5
]
, and finite
S
-transforms
[
2
]
, among others (see Section
2.4
).
The most basic of these are the approximations of
\boxplus_{d}
and
\boxtimes_{d}
to
\boxplus
and
\boxtimes
as
d\to\infty
.
To explain this briefly: given
p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\lambda_{i})\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, the empirical root distribution is defined as
\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{\lambda_{i}}
.
For sequences
p_{d},q_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu
and
\mu\llbracket q_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\nu
, it holds that
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\boxplus_{d}q_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu\boxplus\nu
; a similar convergence holds for
\boxtimes_{d}
to
\boxtimes
under the additional condition
q_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.
To date, however, proofs have essentially relied on moment convergence methods, which necessitate conditions on the supports of the measures.
One result of this paper (Theorem
1.3
) is to demonstrate that this condition on the supports can be removed.

Generally, studying the properties of free convolutions is difficult.
Historically, definitions were initially given only for measures with compact support.
More general definitions were provided in the 1990s
[
9
,
23
]
.
However, various regularity properties are now known due to the efforts of many researchers in this area. These include: the monotone property for order
[
9
, Propositions 4.15 and 4.16]
and distances
[
9
, Proposition 4.13 and 4.14]
; properties of atoms and absolute continuity with respect to Lebesgue measure for
\boxplus

[
10
, Theorem 7.4]
,
[
7
, Theorem 4.1]
, and for
\boxtimes

[
1
, Corollary 6.6]
,
[
14
, Theorem 3.1]
,
[
4
, Theorem 1.9]
; among others.
These properties are summarized in Section
2.2
.

In this paper, motivated by known regularities in free probability, we aim to contribute to the research on the regularity of finite free convolutions.
Usually, investigating the properties of free convolutions requires analytical approaches involving tools such as the
R
-transform,
S
-transform, and their associated free subordination functions. However, in finite free probability, with the exception of the finite
S
-transform for
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
defined in
[
2
]
, many challenges remain in developing analytical approaches (see
[
11
,
15
,
18
,
19
]
).
Therefore, this paper employs a more elementary approach, similar to that taken in
[
9
]
, to establish regularity properties of finite free convolutions.
Our main tools are the partial order defined on
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
and approximations using cut-up
\mu|^{a}
and cut-down
\mu|_{a}
measures.

1.2
Main results

To save space, this section primarily presents results for the finite free additive convolution
\boxplus_{d}
; similar results hold for the finite free multiplicative convolution
\boxtimes_{d}
(see Propositions
3.1
,
3.4
, and
3.5
; Theorems
4.2
,
4.4
, and
4.5
).

The first result concerns atoms in finite free convolutions.
Let
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
(resp.
q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
) be a monic polynomial of degree
d
having a root
\alpha
with multiplicity
m^{p}_{\alpha}
(resp. having a root
\beta
with multiplicity
m^{q}_{\beta}
).
If
m^{p}_{\alpha}+m^{q}_{\beta}-d>0
, then
p\boxplus_{d}q
has a trivial root
\alpha+\beta
with multiplicity
m^{p}_{\alpha}+m^{q}_{\beta}-d
.
We call such a triplet
(\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta)
an atom triplet of
(p,q,p\boxplus_{d}q)
.
The other roots of
p\boxplus_{d}q
are said to be non-trivial.
Research on the finite free multiplicative convolution (Schur–Szegő composition) was previously conducted by Kostov and Shapiro in 2006
[
16
]
.
Inspired by their work, we obtained the following results.
In the statements that follow, for
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, we use the abbreviation
\mathcal{F}_{p}
to denote the cumulative distribution function of the empirical root distribution
\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket
; that is,
\mathcal{F}_{p}=\mathcal{F}_{\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
be monic polynomials of degree
d
having only real roots.

1.

Every non-trivial root of
p\boxplus_{d}q
is simple.

2.

For an atom triplet
(\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta)
of
(p,q,p\boxplus_{d}q)
, we have

\mu\llbracket p\boxplus_{d}q\rrbracket(\{\alpha+\beta\})=\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket(\{\alpha\})+\mu\llbracket q\rrbracket(\{\beta\})-1

and also

\mathcal{F}_{p\boxplus_{d}q}(\alpha+\beta)=\mathcal{F}_{p}(\alpha)+\mathcal{F}_{q}(\beta)-1.

Our second main result establishes a monotone property for distances under finite free convolutions.
We consider two distances defined on probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
: the Kolmogorov distance
d_{K}(\mu,\nu)
and the Lévy distance
d_{L}(\mu,\nu)
, which are defined in the usual manner (see Definition
2.1
).
For polynomials
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, we adopt the abbreviations
d_{K}(p,q):=d_{K}(\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket,\mu\llbracket q\rrbracket)
and
d_{L}(p,q):=d_{L}(\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket,\mu\llbracket q\rrbracket)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
p,q,r\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
be monic polynomials of degree
d
having only real roots.

1.

d_{K}(p\boxplus_{d}r,q\boxplus_{d}r)\leq d_{K}(p,q)
.

2.

d_{L}(p\boxplus_{d}r,q\boxplus_{d}r)\leq d_{L}(p,q)
.

The preceding relation concerning the monotonicity of distances (Theorem
1.2
) is instrumental in extending convergence arguments to the setting of free convolutions.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
p_{d},q_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
such that their empirical root distributions
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket
and
\mu\llbracket q_{d}\rrbracket
weakly converge to
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
as
d\to\infty
, respectively.
Then
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\boxplus_{d}q_{d}\rrbracket
weakly converges to
\mu\boxplus\nu
as
d\to\infty
.
If
\lim_{d\to\infty}d_{K}(\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket,\mu)=0
and
\lim_{d\to\infty}d_{K}(\mu\llbracket q_{d}\rrbracket,\nu)=0
, then
\lim_{d\to\infty}d_{K}(\mu\llbracket p_{d}\boxplus_{d}q_{d}\rrbracket,\mu\boxplus\nu)=0
.

This theorem demonstrates that the finite free additive convolution
\boxplus_{d}
effectively approximates the free additive convolution
\boxplus
as
d\to\infty
.

2
Preliminary

2.1
Probability measures

We use the symbol
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
to denote the family of all Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{C}
.
When we want to specify that the support of the measure is contained in a subset
K\subset\mathbb{C}
, we use the notation
\mathcal{M}(K)
.
For instance,
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the set of Borel probability measures on the real line.
For a sequence of probability measures
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, the weak convergence
\mu_{n}\xrightarrow{w}\mu
is defined by

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\mu_{n}(dx)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\mu(dx),

for all bounded continuous functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Given
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, the cumulative distribution function (CDF)
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
is defined by

\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x):=\mu((-\infty,x]),

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
It is well known that the weak convergence of
\mu_{n}
to
\mu
is equivalent to the point-wise convergence of their CDFs
\mathcal{F}_{\mu_{n}}
to
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
at all continuity points of
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
.

Definition 2.1

(Distances on measures)
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
.

1.

The Kolmogorov distance of
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as

d_{K}(\mu,\nu):=\inf\{\varepsilon>0\mid\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x)-\varepsilon\leq\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x)+\varepsilon\quad(\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R})\}.

2.

The Lévy distance of
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as

d_{L}(\mu,\nu):=\inf\{\varepsilon>0\mid\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon\leq\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\quad(\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R})\}.

Clearly,
0\leq d_{L}(\mu,\nu)\leq d_{K}(\mu,\nu)\leq 1
by definition.
It is well known that the topology induced by the Lévy distance is the same as the weak topology on probability measures, whereas the Kolmogorov distance induces a stronger topology.
For
\mu,\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
), the convergence
\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{K}(\mu_{n},\mu)=0
is equivalent to
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\mu_{n}}(x)=\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence, if
\mu_{n}\xrightarrow{w}\mu
as
n\to\infty
, and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\mu_{n}}(x)=\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mu(\{x\})>0
, then
\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{K}(\mu_{n},\mu)=0
.

Definition 2.2

(Transformations on measures)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
and
c\in\mathbb{C}
.

1.

The shift of
\mu
by
c
, denoted
\Shi_{c}\mu
, is the measure satisfying

(\Shi_{c}\mu)(B)=\mu(\{x-c\mid x\in B\})\qquad\text{for every Borel set }B\subset\mathbb{C}.

2.

For
c\neq 0
, the dilation of
\mu
by
c
, denoted
\Dil_{c}\mu
, is the measure satisfying

(\Dil_{c}\mu)(B)=\mu(\{x/c\mid x\in B\})\qquad\text{for every Borel set }B\subset\mathbb{C}.

If
c=0
, we adopt the convention
\Dil_{0}\mu=\delta_{0}
.

3.

The reflected measure
\widehat{\mu}=\Dil_{-1}\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
is given by

\widehat{\mu}(B)=\mu(\{-x\mid x\in B\})\qquad\text{for every Borel set }B\subset\mathbb{C}.

4.

For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})
, the reversed measure
\mu^{\langle-1\rangle}
is given by

\mu^{\langle-1\rangle}(B)=\mu(\{1/x\mid x\in B\})\qquad\text{for every Borel set }B\subset\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}.

It is clear that
\mathcal{F}_{\Shi_{c}\mu}(x)=\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x-c)
for
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence,
d_{K}(\Shi_{c}\mu,\Shi_{c}\nu)=d_{K}(\mu,\nu)
and
d_{L}(\Shi_{c}\mu,\Shi_{c}\nu)=d_{L}(\mu,\nu)
for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
.
In other words, the shift operation preserves these distances.
Similarly, for
c\neq 0
,
\mathcal{F}_{\Dil_{c}\mu}(x)=\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x/c)
, and hence

d_{K}(\Dil_{c}\mu,\Dil_{c}\nu)=d_{K}(\mu,\nu).

Furthermore,

\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x)+\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{\mu}}(-x)=1+\mu(\{x\}),

(2.1)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2.3

(A partial order on measures)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
.
If
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then we write
\nu\leq\mu
.

It follows directly from the definition that
\mu\leq\Shi_{c}\mu
for every
c>0
.

Definition 2.4

(Cut-up and cut-down measures)
.

Given a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
with CDF
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
, we define the cut-up measure at
a\in\mathbb{R}
as the measure
\mu|^{a}\in\mathcal{M}((-\infty,a])
with CDF

\mathcal{F}_{\mu|^{a}}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\quad\text{if }x\geq a,\\
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x)&\quad\text{if }x<a.\end{cases}

Similarly, we define the cut-down measure at
a\in\mathbb{R}
as the measure
\mu|_{a}\in\mathcal{M}([a,\infty))
with CDF

\mathcal{F}_{\mu|_{a}}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\quad\text{if }x<a,\\
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x)&\quad\text{if }x\geq a.\end{cases}

For
a>0
, we use the notation

(\mu)_{a}:=(\mu|^{a})|_{-a}\in\mathcal{M}([-a,a]).

The following basic properties of cut-down and cut-up measures follow directly from their definitions:

•

\mu|_{a}\xrightarrow{w}\mu
as
a\to-\infty
, and
\mu|^{a}\xrightarrow{w}\mu
as
a\to\infty
.

•

Since
\mathcal{F}_{\mu|_{a}}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\mu|^{a}}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\mu|^{a}\leq\mu\leq\mu|_{a}
.

2.2
Free probability

We briefly review the regular properties of free additive and multiplicative convolutions, which are our main research topics.
For more basic knowledge, the readers are referred to the standard textbooks in free probability
[
26
,
24
,
28
]
.

Given measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, the free additive convolution
\mu\boxplus\nu
is defined as the distribution of
X+Y
, where
X
and
Y
are freely independent non-commutative random variables distributed as
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively.
Additionally, if
\nu\geq 0
(equivalently
Y\geq 0
), the free multiplicative convolution
\mu\boxtimes\nu
is defined as the distribution of
\sqrt{Y}X\sqrt{Y}
.
Bercovici and Voiculescu proved the following monotonicity for the free convolutions
[
9
, Propositions 4.15 and 4.16]
:

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
, and
\nu
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
such that
\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}
. Then
\mu_{1}\boxplus\nu\leq\mu_{2}\boxplus\nu
.
If
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
then
\mu_{1}\boxtimes\nu\leq\mu_{2}\boxtimes\nu
.

Using these inequalities, we can easily obtain the following basic fact.

Corollary 2.6
.

For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, if
\mu\in\mathcal{M}((-\infty,a])
and
\nu\in\mathcal{M}((-\infty,b])
then
\mu\boxplus\nu\in\mathcal{M}((-\infty,a+b])
.
Similarly, for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, if
\mu\in\mathcal{M}([0,a])
and
\nu\in\mathcal{M}([0,b])
then
\mu\boxtimes\nu\in\mathcal{M}([0,ab])
.

Proof.

By assumption,
\mu\leq\delta_{a}
and
\nu\leq\delta_{b}
. Hence, by Proposition
2.5
, we have

\mu\boxplus\nu\leq\mu\boxplus\delta_{b}\leq\delta_{a}\boxplus\delta_{b}=\delta_{a+b}.

A similar argument applies to the free multiplicative convolution
\boxtimes
.
∎

Bercovici and Voiculescu also showed the monotonicity for the Kolmogorov and Lévy distances with respect to free additive convolution:

Proposition 2.7

(
[
9
, Proposition 4.13]
)
.

If
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\nu_{1}
, and
\nu_{2}
are probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
, then

d_{L}(\mu_{1}\boxplus\nu_{1},\mu_{2}\boxplus\nu_{2})\leq d_{L}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})+d_{L}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}),

(2.2)

and

d_{K}(\mu_{1}\boxplus\nu_{1},\mu_{2}\boxplus\nu_{2})\leq d_{K}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})+d_{K}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}).

Notice that inequality (
2.2
), by the triangle inequality for the Lévy distance, is equivalent to

d_{L}(\mu\boxplus\rho,\nu\boxplus\rho)\leq d_{L}(\mu,\nu)

for
\mu,\nu,\rho\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
; the same holds for the Kolmogorov distance.

The analogue also holds for free multiplicative convolution, but only with respect to the Kolmogorov distance.

Proposition 2.8

(
[
9
, Proposition 4.14]
)
.

If
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\nu_{1}
, and
\nu_{2}
are probability measures on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, then

d_{K}(\mu_{1}\boxtimes\nu_{1},\mu_{2}\boxtimes\nu_{2})\leq d_{K}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})+d_{K}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}).

Note that a similar inequality with respect to the Lévy distance does not hold; for instance, consider
\mu_{1}=\delta_{0}
,
\mu_{2}=\delta_{1/2}
, and
\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}=\delta_{2}
.
Actually, in
[
9
]
, the inequality

d_{K}(\mu\boxtimes\rho,\nu\boxtimes\rho)\leq d_{K}(\mu,\nu)

(2.3)

was proved for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
and
\rho\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
. We use this in Section
4
.

Bercovici and Voiculescu also pointed out that the following atom regularity holds.

Proposition 2.9

(
[
10
, Theorem 7.4]
)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
and let
\gamma
be a real number.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

\gamma
is an atom of
\mu\boxplus\nu
;

(ii)

there exist atoms
\alpha
of
\mu
and
\beta
of
\nu
such that
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
and
\mu(\{\alpha\})+\nu(\{\beta\})>1
.
In this case, we have
(\mu\boxplus\nu)(\{\gamma\})=\mu(\{\alpha\})+\nu(\{\beta\})-1
.

Furthermore, we can prove the following.

Proposition 2.10
.

Given
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, if
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
are atoms of
\mu,\nu
, respectively, such that
\mu(\{\alpha\})+\nu(\{\beta\})>1
, then

\mathcal{F}_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(\alpha+\beta)=\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(\alpha)+\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\beta)-1.

Proof.

Define the cut-down measures
\mu|_{\alpha}
and
\nu|_{\beta}
. We have
\mu\leq\mu|_{\alpha}
and
\nu\leq\nu|_{\beta}
.
Then, by Proposition
2.5
,
\mu\boxplus\nu\leq\mu|_{\alpha}\boxplus\nu|_{\beta}
, which implies

\mathcal{F}_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(\alpha+\beta)\geq\mathcal{F}_{\mu|_{\alpha}\boxplus\nu|_{\beta}}(\alpha+\beta).

Note that
\mu|_{\alpha}\boxplus\nu|_{\beta}\in\mathcal{M}([\alpha+\beta,\infty))
by Corollary
2.6
.
The mass at the point
\alpha+\beta
is given by Proposition
2.9
as

\mu|_{\alpha}\boxplus\nu|_{\beta}(\{\alpha+\beta\})=\mu|_{\alpha}(\{\alpha\})+\nu|_{\beta}(\{\beta\})-1
.
Since
\mu|_{\alpha}(\{\alpha\})=\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(\alpha)
and
\nu|_{\beta}(\{\beta\})=\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\beta)
, and
\alpha+\beta
is the leftmost point in the support of
\mu|_{\alpha}\boxplus\nu|_{\beta}
, we have
\mathcal{F}_{\mu|_{\alpha}\boxplus\nu|_{\beta}}(\alpha+\beta)=\mu|_{\alpha}\boxplus\nu|_{\beta}(\{\alpha+\beta\})
.
Thus,

\mathcal{F}_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(\alpha+\beta)\geq\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(\alpha)+\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\beta)-1.

(2.4)

The same argument applies to the reflected measures
\widehat{\mu}
and
\widehat{\nu}
, noting that
\widehat{\mu\boxplus\nu}=\widehat{\mu}\boxplus\widehat{\nu}
, with atoms
(-\alpha,-\beta,-\alpha-\beta)
. This yields

\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{\mu\boxplus\nu}}(-\alpha-\beta)\geq\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{\mu}}(-\alpha)+\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{\nu}}(-\beta)-1.

(2.5)

Combining inequalities (
2.4
), (
2.5
) and Equation (
2.1
), we obtain

1+\mu\boxplus\nu(\{\alpha+\beta\})\geq\mu(\{\alpha\})+\nu(\{\beta\}).

Since equality holds by Proposition
2.9
, it implies that equality must hold in both (
2.4
) and (
2.5
).
∎

The corresponding result for
\boxtimes
was first established for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
by Belinschi and finally extended for
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
by Arizmendi et al.

Proposition 2.11

(
[
6
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
1
, Corollary 6.6]
)
.

Suppose that
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
and
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.

(i)

(\mu\boxtimes\nu)(\{0\})=\max\{\mu(\{0\}),\nu(\{0\})\}
.

(ii)

Let
\gamma\neq 0
. We have
(\mu\boxtimes\nu)(\{\gamma\})>0
if and only if there exist
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\gamma=\alpha\beta
and
\mu(\{\alpha\})+\nu(\{\beta\})>1
.
In this case, we have
(\mu\boxtimes\nu)(\{\gamma\})=\mu(\{\alpha\})+\nu(\{\beta\})-1
.

In particular, if
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, we can obtain the corresponding result to Proposition
2.10
, whose proof is almost identical.

Proposition 2.12
.

Given
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
being atoms of
\mu,\nu
, respectively, such that
\mu(\{\alpha\})+\nu(\{\beta\})>1
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{\mu\boxtimes\nu}(\alpha\beta)=\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(\alpha)+\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\beta)-1.

Proof.

In the same manner as the proof of Proposition
2.10
, using cut-down measures
\mu|_{\alpha}
and
\nu|_{\beta}
, we obtain

\mathcal{F}_{\mu\boxtimes\nu}(\alpha\beta)\geq\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(\alpha)+\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\beta)-1,

because
\mu\boxtimes\nu\leq\mu|_{\alpha}\boxtimes\nu|_{\beta}
implies
\mathcal{F}_{\mu\boxtimes\nu}(\alpha\beta)\geq\mathcal{F}_{\mu|_{\alpha}\boxtimes\nu|_{\beta}}(\alpha\beta)=\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(\alpha)+\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\beta)-1
.

Similarly, using cut-up measures,
\mu|^{\alpha}\leq\mu
and
\nu|^{\beta}\leq\nu
. Thus
\mu|^{\alpha}\boxtimes\nu|^{\beta}\leq\mu\boxtimes\nu
. This implies
\widehat{\mu\boxtimes\nu}\leq\widehat{\mu|^{\alpha}\boxtimes\nu|^{\beta}}
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{\mu\boxtimes\nu}}(-\alpha\beta)\geq\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{\mu|^{\alpha}\boxtimes\nu|^{\beta}}}(-\alpha\beta)
.
Using Equation (
2.1
), Corollary
2.6
, and Proposition
2.11
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{\mu|^{\alpha}\boxtimes\nu|^{\beta}}}(-\alpha\beta)

\displaystyle=1+\mu|^{\alpha}\boxtimes\nu|^{\beta}(\{\alpha\beta\})-\mathcal{F}_{\mu|^{\alpha}\boxtimes\nu|^{\beta}}(\alpha\beta)

\displaystyle=\mu|^{\alpha}(\{\alpha\})+\nu|^{\beta}(\{\beta\})-1,

since
\mathcal{F}_{\mu|^{\alpha}\boxtimes\nu|^{\beta}}(\alpha\beta)=1
as the support of
\mu|^{\alpha}\boxtimes\nu|^{\beta}
is in
(-\infty,\alpha\beta]
(for
\alpha,\beta>0
).
We also have
\mu|^{\alpha}(\{\alpha\})=\mu([\alpha,\infty))
and
\nu|^{\beta}(\{\beta\})=\nu([\beta,\infty))
.
So,
\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{\mu\boxtimes\nu}}(-\alpha\beta)\geq\mu([\alpha,\infty))+\nu([\beta,\infty))-1
.
The rest of the argument, showing that equality holds, is similar to the discussion in the proof of Proposition
2.10
.
∎

2.3
Polynomials

Let the symbol
\mathbb{C}_{d}[x]
denote the set of polynomials over
\mathbb{C}
of degree at most
d
, i.e.,
\mathbb{C}_{d}[x]=\{\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}x^{k}\mid a_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\}
.
We use
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\subset\mathbb{C}_{d}[x]
to denote the set of monic polynomials of degree
d
.
For a subset
K
of the complex plane,
\mathcal{P}_{d}(K)
denotes the set of
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
such that the roots of
p
are contained in
K
.
For instance,
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
is the set of monic polynomials of degree
d
having only real roots.
Given
p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\lambda_{i})\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
, the symbol
\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(p)
denotes the normalized
k
-th elementary symmetric polynomial on the roots of
p
, namely

\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(p)=\binom{d}{k}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq d}\lambda_{i_{1}}\dots\lambda_{i_{k}},

with the convention that
\tilde{e}_{0}^{(d)}(p)=1
.
Hence, we can express any
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
as

p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\lambda_{i})=\sum_{k=0}^{d}(-1)^{k}\binom{d}{k}\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(p)x^{d-k}.

(2.6)

The empirical root distribution of
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is defined as

\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket:=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{\lambda_{i}}.

We always identify a polynomial
p
, its coefficients
\{\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(p)\}_{k=0}^{d}
, and its roots
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
, or equivalently, its empirical root distribution
\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket
.
When the coefficient
\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(p)
is represented, its superscript
(d)
and the polynomial
(p)
will often be omitted if
d
and
p
are clear from the context.
Note that in most situations,
\tilde{e}_{0}^{(d)}(p)=1
, since we usually restrict our attention to monic polynomials.

The map
\mu\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
provides a bijection from
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
onto the set

\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}):=\left\{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{\lambda_{i}}\mid\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}.

Moreover, we have that
\mu\llbracket\mathcal{P}_{d}(K)\rrbracket=\mathcal{M}_{d}(K)
for every
K\subset\mathbb{C}
, where the latter is defined as
\mathcal{M}_{d}(K)=\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\cap\mathcal{M}(K)
.
Notice that for every
d
, the subset
\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is invariant under the transformations in Definition
2.2
; thus, we can use the bijection
\mu\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
to define analogous transformations on the set of polynomials.

Definition 2.13

(Transformations on polynomials)
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
.

1.

For
c\in\mathbb{C}
, we define the
shifted polynomial
as
[\Shi_{c}(p)](x):=p(x-c)
.

2.

For
c\neq 0
, we define the
dilated polynomial
as
[\Dil_{c}(p)](x):=c^{d}p(x/c)
.
If
c=0
, we use the convention
[\Dil_{0}(p)](x)=x^{d}
.

3.

The
reflected polynomial

\widehat{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is given by
\widehat{p}(x):=[\Dil_{-1}(p)](x)=(-1)^{d}p(-x)
.

4.

If
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})
(i.e.,
p(0)\neq 0
, so
\tilde{e}_{d}(p)\neq 0
), the
reversed polynomial

p^{\langle-1\rangle}(x)
is defined as
x^{d}p(1/x)/\tilde{e}_{d}(p)
.

A partial order on
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
is also induced from
\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
(see Definition
2.3
):

p\leq q\quad\overset{\text{def}}{\Longleftrightarrow}\quad\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket\leq\mu\llbracket q\rrbracket

for
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.

We can also define the corresponding cut-up and cut-down operations on polynomials using the bijection
\mu\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
between
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.
That is, given
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
with roots
\lambda_{i}(p)
for
i=1,\dots,d
, and
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
the
cut-up polynomial

p|^{a}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\leq a})
has roots
\lambda_{i}(p|^{a})
defined by

\lambda_{i}(p|^{a})=\min(\lambda_{i}(p),a),

and the
cut-down polynomial

p|_{a}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq a})
has roots
\lambda_{i}(p|_{a})
defined by

\lambda_{i}(p|_{a})=\max(\lambda_{i}(p),a).

Using the previous definitions:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(p|^{a})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\lambda_{i}(p)&\quad\text{if }\lambda_{i}(p)<a,\\
a&\quad\text{if }\lambda_{i}(p)\geq a;\end{cases}

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(p|_{a})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}a&\quad\text{if }\lambda_{i}(p)\leq a,\\
\lambda_{i}(p)&\quad\text{if }\lambda_{i}(p)>a.\end{cases}

If
(p_{d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of polynomials such that
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu
, then
\mu\llbracket p_{d}|_{a}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu|_{a}
and
\mu\llbracket p_{d}|^{a}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu|^{a}
.
Similarly,
(p)_{a}:=(p|^{a})|_{-a}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}([-a,a])
.

Let the symbol
D
denote the derivation operator
d/dx
for polynomials.
For
j\leq d
, the derivative map
\partial_{j|d}:\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathcal{P}_{j}(\mathbb{C})
is given by

\partial_{j|d}p:=\frac{D^{(d-j)}p}{(d)_{j}},

where
(d)_{j}=d(d-1)\dots(d-j+1)=d!/(d-j)!
is the normalization constant.

2.4
Finite free probability

This section briefly reviews the definitions and essential properties of finite free probability, highlighting its crucial connections and analogies to the established results in free probability, which will serve as a foundation for the subsequent discussion.

Definition 2.14

(Finite Free Convolutions)
.

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{C}_{d}[x]
be polynomials of degree at most
d
.

1.

The finite free additive convolution
p\boxplus_{d}q
is defined by

\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(p\boxplus_{d}q)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\tilde{e}_{i}^{(d)}(p)\tilde{e}_{k-i}^{(d)}(q),

for
0\leq k\leq d
.

2.

The finite free multiplicative convolution
p\boxtimes_{d}q
is defined by

\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(p\boxtimes_{d}q)=\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(p)\tilde{e}_{k}^{(d)}(q),

for
0\leq k\leq d
.

By definition, it immediately follows that these operations are bilinear, commutative, and associative.
Furthermore, the shift, dilation, and derivatives can be represented using finite free convolutions.

Example 2.15
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
.

1.

If
q(x)=(x-c)^{d}
for
c\in\mathbb{C}
, then
p\boxplus_{d}q(x)=p(x-c)=[\Shi_{c}(p)](x)
and also
p\boxtimes_{d}q=\Dil_{c}(p)
.

2.

If
q^{(k)}(x)=x^{d-k}(x-1)^{k}
for
k=0,\dots,d
, then
p\boxtimes_{d}q^{(k)}(x)=x^{d-k}\diff_{k|d}p(x)
.

3.

For
q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
, we have
\widehat{p\boxplus_{d}q}=\widehat{p}\boxplus_{d}\widehat{q}
and
\widehat{p\boxtimes_{d}q}=\widehat{p}\boxtimes_{d}q=p\boxtimes_{d}\widehat{q}
.

4.

Assume
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})
.
Then,
{(p\boxtimes_{d}q)}^{\langle-1\rangle}=p^{\langle-1\rangle}\boxtimes_{d}q^{\langle-1\rangle}
for
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\})
.

Moreover, the finite free convolutions preserve the real-rootedness of polynomials under many situations.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
d
be a fixed degree.

1.

If
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, then
p\boxplus_{d}q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.

2.

If
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, then
p\boxtimes_{d}q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.

3.

If
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, then
p\boxtimes_{d}q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.

Definition 2.17

(Interlacing)
.

Let
q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.
We say that
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
interlaces
q
, denoted by
p\lessdot q
, if their roots
\lambda_{i}(p)
and
\lambda_{i}(q)
, when ordered non-decreasingly, satisfy

\lambda_{1}(p)\leq\lambda_{1}(q)\leq\lambda_{2}(p)\leq\lambda_{2}(q)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{d}(p)\leq\lambda_{d}(q).

Similarly, we say that
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d-1}(\mathbb{R})
interlaces
q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, denoted by
p\lessdot q
, if

\lambda_{1}(q)\leq\lambda_{1}(p)\leq\lambda_{2}(q)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{d-1}(p)\leq\lambda_{d}(q).

For
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, it is clear that if
p\lessdot q
, then
p\leq q
(in the sense of Definition
2.3
).
Besides, it is well known that
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
having
p\lessdot q
is related to the real-rootedness of linear combinations
ap+bq
.

Proposition 2.18
.

If
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
p\lessdot q
, then

\displaystyle r\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow p\boxplus_{d}r\lessdot q\boxplus_{d}r,

\displaystyle r\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow p\boxtimes_{d}r\lessdot q\boxtimes_{d}r.

For
p\leq q
, there exists a family of polynomials
(p_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
with
p_{0}=p
,
p_{1}=q
, such that
p_{s}\lessdot p_{t}
for
s\leq t
, effectively interpolating
p
and
q
through interlacing polynomials.
Hence, we obtain the following result, which is a finite free analogue of Proposition
2.5
.

Proposition 2.19

(
[
2
, Theorem 4.4]
)
.

If
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
p\leq q
, then

\displaystyle r\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow p\boxplus_{d}r\leq q\boxplus_{d}r;

\displaystyle r\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow p\boxtimes_{d}r\leq q\boxtimes_{d}r.

Identifying a polynomial
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
with its root distribution
\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket
, we write

\mathcal{F}_{p}(x):=\mathcal{F}_{\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket}(x)\qquad\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Similarly, for
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, we write
d_{K}(p,q)=d_{K}(\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket,\mu\llbracket q\rrbracket)
and
d_{L}(p,q)=d_{L}(\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket,\mu\llbracket q\rrbracket)
.

Using the above monotonicity of finite free convolutions, we can provide an alternative proof of the bounds on the roots of convoluted polynomials in
[
19
, Theorems 1.3 and 1.6]
in the same as the proof of Corollary
2.6
.
In the following, for
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, let
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(p)
denote the maximum roots of
p
.

Corollary 2.20

(
[
19
, Theorems 1.3 and 1.6]
)
.

Given
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(p\boxplus_{d}q)\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(p)+\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(q)
.
If
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, then
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(p\boxtimes_{d}q)\leq\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(p)\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(q)
.

Arizmendi and Perales construed the theory of finite free cumulants
[
5
]
inspired by the work of finite
R
-transform by Marcus
[
18
]
, and they obtained the convergence of finite free additive convolution
\boxplus_{d}
to free additive convolution
\boxplus
under the assumption that the limiting measures have compact support.

Proposition 2.21

(
[
5
, Corollary 5.5]
)
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be real probability measures with compact support.
For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
p_{d}
and
q_{d}
be monic polynomials of degree
d
.
If, as
d\to\infty
,
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu
and
\mu\llbracket q_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\nu
, then we have the weak convergence
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\boxplus_{d}q_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu\boxplus\nu
.

In the next work
[
3
]
, Arizmendi, Perales, and Garza-Vargas obtained the multiplicative formula for finite free cumulants and then proved the convergence of
\boxtimes_{d}
to
\boxtimes
under a similar condition.

Proposition 2.22

(
[
3
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability measures supported on a compact subset of the real line.
Let
(p_{d})_{d=1}^{\infty}
and
(q_{d})_{d=1}^{\infty}
be sequences of real-rooted polynomials, and assume that the
q_{d}
have only non-negative roots.
If the empirical root distributions of these sequences of polynomials converge weakly to
\mu
and
\nu
respectively, then the empirical root distributions of the sequence
(p_{d}\boxtimes_{d}q_{d})_{d=1}^{\infty}
converge weakly to
\mu\boxtimes\nu
.

Recently, Arizmendi et al.
[
2
]
constructed the finite
S
-transform for
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.
As a result, for the case where both
p_{d},q_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
, the condition on compact supports can be dropped for the multiplicative convolution.

Proposition 2.23

(
[
2
, Proposition 10.1]
)
.

Let
p_{d},q_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
and assume their root distributions
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket
and
\mu\llbracket q_{d}\rrbracket
converge weakly to
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
as
d\to\infty
, respectively.
Then, the empirical root distributions
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\boxtimes_{d}q_{d}\rrbracket
converge weakly to
\mu\boxtimes\nu
as
d\to\infty
.

More recently, Jalowy, Kabluchko, and Marynych
[
15
]
dealt with these kinds of problems in
[
15
, Theorems 3.5 and 3.7]
by using exponential profiles; however, they also required similar constraints on the support.

3
Atoms for finite free convolutions

This section investigates the nature of atoms in finite free convolutions, providing detailed proofs for the conditions governing their existence and multiplicities.
We begin in Section
3.1
with the multiplicative convolution
\boxtimes_{d}
, building upon the foundational work of Kostov and Shapiro on the Schur–Szegő composition
[
16
]
.
While reviewing their key insights, we also present alternative derivations for several results using the framework of finite free probability (with Proposition
3.5
being an exception where their original arguments are noted).
Subsequently, Section
3.2
establishes analogous theorems for the additive convolution
\boxplus_{d}
, largely by adapting the methodologies employed for the multiplicative case.

3.1
Finite free multiplicative convolution

Interestingly, Kostov and Shapiro already studied the atoms of the Schur-Szegő composition in 2006
[
16
]
.
In this subsection, we review their work from the viewpoint of finite free probability.
Given polynomials of degree
d
,

p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{d}{k}a_{k}x^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad q(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{d}{k}b_{k}x^{k},

the Schur-Szegő composition is given by

p*q(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\binom{d}{k}a_{k}b_{k}x^{k}.

They were interested in the roots of the Schur-Szegő composition
p*q
and obtained helpful results for our goal concerning atom information for finite free convolutions.
The connection to finite free multiplicative convolution is clear as follows:
\widehat{p\boxtimes_{d}q}=p*q
under appropriate identification of coefficients.
Thus, we can interpret their results in terms of finite free multiplicative convolutions.
For the reader’s convenience, we restate Kostov and Shapiro’s relevant results in our terminology.

Proposition 3.1

(Theorem 1.4 in
[
16
]
)
.

Given two polynomials
p
and
q
in
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\alpha\neq 0
and
\beta\neq 0
are roots respectively of
p
and
q
of multiplicity
m_{\alpha}^{p}
and
m_{\beta}^{q}
with
m=m_{\alpha}^{p}+m_{\beta}^{q}-d\geq 0
, one has that
\alpha\beta
is a root of
p\boxtimes_{d}q
of multiplicity
m
.
(If
m=0
,
\alpha\beta
is not a root of
p\boxtimes_{d}q
.)

In the proposition’s setting, if
m>0
, we call
\alpha\beta
a
trivial root
of
p\boxtimes_{d}q
, and the triplet
(\alpha,\beta,\alpha\beta)
is termed an
atom triplet
of
(p,q,p\boxtimes_{d}q)
. This corresponds to what Kostov and Shapiro called “A-roots”
[
16
]

For this alternative proof, we review an expression for

Let
D=d/dx

r_{d}^{(1)}(x)=x(x-1)^{d-1}

r_{d}^{(k)}=(r_{d}^{(1)})^{\boxtimes_{d}k}=(x(x-1)^{d-1})^{\boxtimes_{d}k}

For a fixed degree

\{r^{(k)}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}

Clearly,

Lemma 3.2
.

The set of polynomials
\{r^{(k)}\}_{k=0}^{d}
forms a basis for the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space
\mathbb{C}_{d}[x]
.

Proof.

Consider the operator
\mathcal{D}_{x}=\frac{xD}{d}
. The action of
\boxtimes_{d}
with

on

is given by
r^{(1)}\boxtimes_{d}p(x)=\frac{xDp(x)}{d}=\mathcal{D}_{x}p(x)
.
Thus,
r^{(k)}(x)=(\mathcal{D}_{x})^{k}(x-1)^{d}
for
k\geq 0
.
The polynomial
r^{(k)}(x)
has a root at
x=1
of multiplicity
d-k
for
0\leq k\leq d

Consider a linear combination
\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}r^{(k)}(x)=0

Evaluating at
x=1

a_{d}r^{(d)}(1)=0

a_{d}=0
.
Now consider
\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}a_{k}r^{(k)}(x)=0

Similarly, differentiating and evaluating at

.
This inductive step shows all

\{r^{(k)}\}_{k=0}^{d}

\mathbb{C}_{d}[x]

∎

By Lemma

, any polynomial

can be uniquely expressed as

.
This allows us to associate

.
For
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
with associated

and

\displaystyle p\boxtimes_{d}q(x)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}a_{i}r^{(i)}(x)\right)\boxtimes_{d}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{d}b_{j}r^{(j)}(x)\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\sum_{j=0}^{d}a_{i}b_{j}(r^{(i)}\boxtimes_{d}r^{(j)})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\sum_{j=0}^{d}a_{i}b_{j}r^{(i+j)}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{2d}\left(\sum_{i+j=m}a_{i}b_{j}\right)r^{(m)}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{2d}\left(\sum_{i+j=m}a_{i}b_{j}\right)(\mathcal{D}_{x})^{m}(x-1)^{d}=[(PQ)(\mathcal{D}_{x})](x-1)^{d},

where
(PQ)(X)=P(X)Q(X)
.

.

The product

To express

where

\{r^{(k)}\}_{k=0}^{d}

r^{(d+l)}=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c^{(k)}r^{(k+l-1)}=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}(k+1)c^{(k)}r^{(k)}+l\sum_{k=l}^{d}c^{(k)}r^{(k)}

.

If

4
Convergent sequence of polynomials

This section investigates the convergence of sequences of finite free convolutions to their free counterparts, representing a core contribution of this paper.
We begin in Section
4.1
by establishing that the monotonicity of distances, known for free convolutions (cf. Proposition
2.7
), also holds for the finite versions
\boxplus_{d}
and
\boxtimes_{d}
.
Building upon this crucial property, Section
4.2
extends prior convergence results (like Proposition
2.21
) by removing compactness assumptions on the underlying measures. This subsection further provides alternative proofs for existing theorems (e.g., Proposition
2.22
), strengthens weak convergence to convergence in Kolmogorov distance, and demonstrates a slight generalization of Proposition
2.23
.

4.1
Monotone property for distances

We examine the relationship between Kolmogorov and Lévy distances for empirical measures of polynomials in
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.
First, recall that for
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, its CDF,
\mathcal{F}_{p}=\mathcal{F}_{\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket}
, is a step function with jumps of size
k/d
for integers
k
. Specifically,

\mathcal{F}_{p}:\mathbb{R}\to\left\{0,\frac{1}{d},\frac{2}{d},\dots,\frac{d}{d}\right\}.

Consequently, for any
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, the Kolmogorov distance
d_{K}(p,q)=d_{K}(\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket,\mu\llbracket q\rrbracket)
must be a multiple of
1/d
:

d_{K}(p,q)\in\left\{0,\frac{1}{d},\frac{2}{d},\dots,\frac{d}{d}\right\}.

Furthermore, if
p\lessdot q
(i.e.,
p
interlaces
q
), then their CDFs are “close,” leading to
d_{K}(p,q)\leq 1/d
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. If for some integer
0\leq l\leq d
,

\mathcal{F}_{q}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{p}(x)+\frac{l}{d}\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R},

(4.1)

then there exists a sequence of polynomials
q=q^{(0)},q^{(1)},\dots,q^{(l)}
such that
q^{(k-1)}\lessdot q^{(k)}
for
k=1,\dots,l
, and
p\leq q^{(l)}
.
As a consequence, for any
r\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
,

\mathcal{F}_{q\boxplus_{d}r}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{p\boxplus_{d}r}(x)+\frac{l}{d}\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R},

and for any
r\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
,

\mathcal{F}_{q\boxtimes_{d}r}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{p\boxtimes_{d}r}(x)+\frac{l}{d}\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{1}(p)\leq\lambda_{2}(p)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{d}(p)
be the roots of
p
and
(\lambda_{i}(q))_{i=1}^{d}
be those of
q
.
By the assumption (
4.1
), we know that

\lambda_{i}(p)\leq\lambda_{l+i}(q)

(4.2)

for
i=1,\dots,d-l
.
Choose some
a>\max(\lambda_{d}(p),\lambda_{d}(q))
.
For
k=1,\dots,l
, we inductively define

q^{(k)}(x):=q^{(k-1)}(x)\cdot\frac{x-a}{x-\lambda_{k}(q)},

that is,
q^{(k+1)}
obtained by replacing the minimum root
\lambda_{k}(q)
of
q^{(k)}
with
a
.
This construction ensures
q^{(k-1)}\lessdot q^{(k)}
.
Besides,

p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\lambda_{i}(p))\leq(x-a)^{l}\prod_{i=1}^{d-l}(x-\lambda_{l+i}(q))=q^{(l)}(x)

by Equation (
4.2
).

The rest follows from Propositions
2.18
and
2.19
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
p,q,r\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.
If
d_{K}(p,q)=l/d
then

\mathcal{F}_{p}(x)-\frac{l}{d}\leq\mathcal{F}_{q}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{p}(x)+\frac{l}{d}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence, by Lemma
4.1

\mathcal{F}_{p\boxplus_{d}r}(x)-\frac{l}{d}\leq\mathcal{F}_{q\boxplus_{d}r}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{p\boxplus_{d}r}(x)+\frac{l}{d}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, which implies
d_{K}(p\boxplus_{d}r,q\boxplus_{d}r)\leq l/d=d_{K}(p,q)
.

When
r\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
the inequality
d_{K}(p\boxtimes_{d}r,q\boxtimes_{d}r)\leq l/d=d_{K}(p,q)
is proven by the same way.

For Lévy distance, it is enough to note that if

\mathcal{F}_{p}(x-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon\leq\mathcal{F}_{q}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{p}(x+\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}

for some
\varepsilon\in[\frac{l}{d},\frac{l+1}{d})
then

\mathcal{F}_{\Shi_{\varepsilon}p}(x)-\frac{l}{d}\leq\mathcal{F}_{q}(x)\leq\mathcal{F}_{\Shi_{-\varepsilon}p}(x)+\frac{l}{d}\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

∎

In the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we obtained the corresponding result for multiplicative convolution.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
r\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
. Then,

d_{K}(p_{1}\boxtimes_{d}r,p_{2}\boxtimes_{d}r)\leq d_{K}(p_{1},p_{2})
.

4.2
Convergence of finite free convolutions

This subsection considers sequences of polynomials
p_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
whose empirical root distributions
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket
converge to a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})

.
We first confirm that any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(K)
(for
K=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
K
be
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, or
[a,b]
for

with

For any

Proof.

For
k=1,\dots,d-1
, define the quantile
\lambda_{k}=\inf\{x\in K\mid\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(x)\geq k/d\}
.
Define
p_{d}(x)=(x-\lambda_{d-1})\prod_{k=1}^{d-1}(x-\lambda_{k})
and then we get
d_{K}(\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket,\mu)\leq 1/d
and
p_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(K)
by the construction.
∎

Therefore, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, we can construct a sequence of polynomials
(p_{d})_{d=1}^{\infty}
such that
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket\to\mu
in Kolmogorov distance (and thus weakly).
In particular, if
d_{K}(\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket,\mu)\to 0
then
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket(\{x\})\to\mu(\{x\})
for every

by Equation (
2.1
).

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

\varepsilon>0

and
\mathcal{F}_{\nu}

are continuity points for both
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\nu}

.
This ensures

and
d_{K}(\nu,(\nu)_{a})<\varepsilon

d_{K}(\mu\boxplus\nu,(\mu)_{a}\boxplus(\nu)_{a})<2\varepsilon

Since
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu
and
\mu\llbracket q_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\nu
, and
\pm a
are continuity points of
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\nu}
,
\mathcal{F}_{p_{d}}(\pm a)\to\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(\pm a)
and
\mathcal{F}_{q_{d}}(\pm a)\to\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\pm a)
as

.
This implies
d_{K}({p_{d}},({p_{d}})_{a})<\varepsilon
and
d_{K}({q_{d}},({q_{d}})_{a})<\varepsilon
, and hence

d_{K}(p_{d}\boxplus_{d}q_{d},(p_{d})_{a}\boxplus_{d}(q_{d})_{a})<2\varepsilon

by Theorem

for
d\in\mathbb{N}
large enough.

The measures
(\mu)_{a}
and
(\nu)_{a}
have the supports contained in
[-a,a]
.
Also
\mu\llbracket(p_{d})_{a}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}(\mu)_{a}
and
\mu\llbracket(q_{d})_{a}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}(\nu)_{a}
.

d_{L}(\mu\llbracket(p_{d})_{a}\boxplus_{d}(q_{d})_{a}\rrbracket,(\mu)_{a}\boxplus(\nu)_{a})<\varepsilon

by Proposition
2.21

large enough.

Therefore, combining the inequalities above, we have

d_{L}(\mu\llbracket p_{d}\boxplus_{d}q_{d}\rrbracket,\mu\boxplus\nu)\leq 5\varepsilon

large enough.

For convergence in Kolmogorov distance, assume
d_{K}(\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket,\mu)\to 0
and
d_{K}(\mu\llbracket q_{d}\rrbracket,\nu)\to 0
as
d\to\infty
.
It is sufficient to prove the convergence

\mathcal{F}_{p_{d}\boxplus_{d}q_{d}}(\gamma)\to\mathcal{F}_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(\gamma)

for every non-continuous point
\gamma
of
\mu\boxplus\nu
, which means
(\mu\boxplus\nu)(\{\gamma\})>0

By Proposition
2.9

(\mu\boxplus\nu)(\{\gamma\})=\mu(\{\alpha\})+\nu(\{\beta\})-1
.
Since, the empirical root distributions of
p_{d}
and
q_{d}
converge
\mu
and
\nu
in Kolmogorov distance, respectively, we have

\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket(\{\alpha\})\to\mu(\{\alpha\})\quad\text{and}\quad\mu\llbracket q_{d}\rrbracket(\{\beta\})\to\nu(\{\beta\}).

Hence, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,(\mu\boxplus\nu)(\{\gamma\})/2)
, there exists
d_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p_{d}
(resp.
q_{d}
) has a root at
\alpha
(resp.
\beta
) with multiplicity more than
d(\mu(\{\alpha\})-\varepsilon)
(resp.
d(\nu(\{\beta\})-\varepsilon)
) for all
d>d_{0}
.
Thus, Proposition
3.6
implies that
p_{d}\boxplus_{d}q_{d}
has a root
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
with multiplicity more than
d(\mu(\{\alpha\})+\nu(\{\beta\})-1-2\varepsilon)=d((\mu\boxplus\nu)(\{\gamma\})-2\varepsilon)>0
.
That is,
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is the atom triplet of
(p_{d},q_{d},p_{d}\boxplus_{d}q_{d})
for
d\in\mathbb{N}
large enough.
Therefore,

\mathcal{F}_{p_{d}\boxplus_{d}q_{d}}(\gamma)=\mathcal{F}_{p_{d}}(\alpha)+\mathcal{F}_{q_{d}}(\beta)-1\to\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(\alpha)+\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\beta)-1=\mathcal{F}_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(\gamma)

as
d\to\infty
by Propositions
2.10
and
3.7
.
∎

A similar argument yields the multiplicative version, providing an alternative proof for Proposition
2.22
and extending it to Kolmogorov distance.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
(p_{d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(q_{d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
be sequences of polynomials with
p_{d},q_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
. Assume
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu
and
\mu\llbracket q_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\nu
for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
as
d\to\infty
.
Then,
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\boxtimes_{d}q_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu\boxtimes\nu
.
Moreover, if
d_{K}(\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket,\mu)\to 0
and
d_{K}(\mu\llbracket q_{d}\rrbracket,\nu)\to 0
, then
d_{K}(\mu\llbracket p_{d}\boxtimes_{d}q_{d}\rrbracket,\mu\boxtimes\nu)\to 0
.

The same method also extends to the case where one measure is not restricted to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, provided the other has compact support in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Given
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
and
\nu\in\mathcal{M}([0,a])
for some
a>0
.
If
(p_{d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
with
p_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies
\mu\llbracket p_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu
, and
(q_{d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
with
q_{d}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}([0,a])
satisfies
\mu\llbracket q_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\nu
, then

\mu\llbracket p_{d}\boxtimes_{d}q_{d}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu\boxtimes\nu
.

The Kolmogorov distance result might also hold but would require ensuring that Proposition
2.8
or Theorem
4.2
applies appropriately when
p_{1},p_{2}
are not restricted to
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Final remarks

This paper has advanced the understanding of finite free convolutions by extending the convergence of
\boxplus_{d}
and
\boxtimes_{d}
to their respective infinite counterparts,
\boxplus
and
\boxtimes
, notably without requiring prior compactness assumptions on the underlying measures.
A significant outcome is the demonstration that improved initial polynomial approximations, specifically in the Kolmogorov distance, translate directly to enhanced convergence for the resulting convolutions.
Despite these advances, the most general scenario for the finite free multiplicative convolution—involving measures
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
and
\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
—remains open, with questions persisting about both weak convergence and the possibility of strengthening this to Kolmogorov distance.

More broadly, this research has sought to illuminate the fruitful interplay between finite free probability (often approached via the geometry of polynomials) and the established theory of (infinite) free probability.
By leveraging perspectives from one domain to address problems in the other, we have shown that solutions can often be developed through analogous methodologies.
This synergy is not unidirectional; insights from polynomial theory also provide valuable feedback to free probability.
A case in point is the role of Propositions
2.10
and
2.12
in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. These propositions, which are critical for understanding atomic structure, build upon Kostov and Shapiro’s work on the Schur–Szegö composition
[
16
]
and translate findings from polynomial theory into the language of free probability—a connection that, despite its directness, appears to be a novel observation in this context.
It is hoped that this paper will stimulate further collaborative advancements at the interface of these two fields.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
3.8

The argument relies on the continuity of polynomial roots with respect to coefficients. Specifically:

1.

If
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
has only simple roots, then
p(x)+\varepsilon
remains in
\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
(i.e., retains
d
real roots, which will also be simple) for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.

If
p\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
\alpha
is a root of
p
with multiplicity
2
, then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that

p+\varepsilon\notin\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
or

p-\varepsilon\notin\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
.

Lemma A.1
.

If
p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\alpha_{i})
where
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
are distinct roots. Let
p_{j}(x):=p(x)/(x-\alpha_{j})
for
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. Then, the set of
d+1
polynomials
\{p(x),p_{1}(x),\dots,p_{d}(x)\}
forms a basis for the vector space
\mathbb{C}_{d}[x]
.

Proof.

Since
\dim(\mathbb{C}_{d}[x])=d+1
, it suffices to prove linear independence. Assume

c_{0}p(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}p_{j}(x)=0
for some
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
.
Substituting
x=\alpha_{k}
for any
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
:
Since
p(\alpha_{k})=0
and
p_{j}(\alpha_{k})=0
for
j\neq k
, the equation becomes
c_{k}p_{k}(\alpha_{k})=0
.
As
\alpha_{k}
are distinct,
p_{k}(\alpha_{k})=\prod_{i\neq k}(\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{i})\neq 0
. Thus,
c_{k}=0
for all
k=1,\dots,d
.
The equation then reduces to
c_{0}p(x)=0
. Since
p(x)
is not identically zero,
c_{0}=0
.
Hence, all coefficients are zero, proving linear independence.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.8
.

The proof is by contradiction.
Assume
p\boxplus_{d}q
has a nontrivial root at
0
by using the shift.
After derivation, we may also assume the multiplicity is
2
.
We will do the case-by-case arguments.

First, suppose that every root of
p
is simple.
Then, for small enough
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, one has
p+\varepsilon\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
.
Now, take
\varepsilon_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
p\boxplus_{d}q+\varepsilon_{0}\notin\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, but
(p+\varepsilon_{0})\boxplus_{d}q=p\boxplus_{d}q+\varepsilon_{0}
by Proposition
2.16
.
This is a contradiction.

Second, let us assume
p
has a root
\alpha
with multiplicity
m^{p}_{\alpha}>1
.
Then, define
p_{\alpha}(x)=p(x)/(x-\alpha)
and consider
p^{\delta}(x)=p(x)-\delta p_{\alpha}(x)
for
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Note that
p^{\delta}
has a root
\alpha
with multiplicity
m^{p}_{\alpha}-1
and instead a new root
\alpha+\delta
.
Then,

p^{\delta}\boxplus_{d}q=p\boxplus_{d}q-\delta p_{\alpha}\boxplus_{d}q\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R}).

Note that
p_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{P}_{d-1}(\mathbb{R})
and
U_{\alpha}(x):=p_{\alpha}\boxplus_{d}q=p_{\alpha}\boxplus_{d-1}\diff_{(d-1)|d}q\in\mathcal{P}_{d-1}(\mathbb{R})
. If
U_{\alpha}(0)\neq 0
, we can take a small
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
and then

p^{\delta}\boxplus_{d}q\notin\mathcal{P}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, so a contradiction.
Hence, we should consider the case
U_{\alpha}(0)=0
.
There are two cases,
U^{\prime}_{\alpha}(0)=0
or
U^{\prime}_{\alpha}(0)\neq 0
.

If
U_{\alpha}(0)=0
and
U^{\prime}_{\alpha}(0)=0
then we may take
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
as
\alpha+\delta
is a root of
p^{\delta}
with multiplicity
1
.
Besides,
p^{\delta}
has a root at
0
with multiplicity more than
1
and the multiplicity at
\alpha
decreases.

The case
U_{\alpha}(0)=0
and
U^{\prime}_{\alpha}(0)\neq 0
is a problem.
We will see that if there is another root
\beta\neq\alpha
of
p
such that
U_{\beta}(0)=0
and
U^{\prime}_{\beta}(0)\neq 0
then we may decrease the multiplicity of
p
at
\alpha
by the similar argument.
Let
p_{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=\frac{p(x)}{(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)}
.
Then

p^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}(x)=p(x)-\delta p_{\alpha}(x)-\varepsilon p_{\beta}(x)+\delta\varepsilon p_{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)

for
\delta,\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
.
Note that
p_{(\alpha,\beta)}=\frac{p_{\alpha}-p_{\beta}}{\alpha-\beta}
and hence
p_{(\alpha,\beta)}\boxplus_{d}q(0)=0
.
If
\varepsilon
is taken as sufficiently small then
((p_{\alpha}-\varepsilon p_{(\alpha,\beta)})\boxplus_{d}q)^{\prime}(0)=U^{\prime}_{\alpha}(0)-\varepsilon(p_{(\alpha,\beta)}\boxplus_{d}q)^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
.

Also, setting
\delta=-\varepsilon\frac{U^{\prime}_{\beta}(0)}{U^{\prime}_{\alpha}(0)-\varepsilon(p_{(\alpha,\beta)}\boxplus_{d}q)^{\prime}(0)}
implies

(p^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}\boxplus_{d}q)^{\prime}(0)=0
.
Hence,
p^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}
has a root at
0
with multiplicity more than
1
and the multiplicity of every root of
p^{(\delta,\varepsilon)}
decreases.

Finally, the worst case is as follows:
p
is not simple, and for every root
\alpha
with multiplicity
m^{p}_{\alpha}>1
we have
U_{\alpha}(0)=0
and
U^{\prime}_{\alpha}(0)\neq 0
.
By the discussion above, such a root is only one, just call
\alpha
with multiplicity
m^{p}_{\alpha}>1
and the other roots
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d-m^{p}_{\alpha}}
.
Also, for any other root
\alpha_{i}
of
p
, we have
U_{\alpha_{i}}(0)=U^{\prime}_{\alpha_{i}}(0)=0
.
It is same in
q
as well, so define the root
\beta
with multiplicity
m^{q}_{\beta}>1
and the other roots
\{\beta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d-m^{q}_{\beta}}
.
By lemma, a linear combination of
p
and
\{p_{\alpha_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{d-m^{p}_{\alpha}}
expresses
(x-\alpha)^{d}
and hence
(x-\alpha)^{d}\boxplus_{d}q(x)=q(x-\alpha)
is a linear combination of
p\boxplus_{d}q
and
\{p_{\alpha_{i}}\boxplus_{d}q=U_{\alpha_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{d-m^{p}_{\alpha}}
.
Thus,
q(x-\alpha)
has a root at
0
with multiplicity more than
1
.
It means
\beta=-\alpha
.
Since
0
is a non-trivial root of
p\boxplus_{d}q
,
m^{p}_{\alpha}+m^{q}_{\beta}-d<0
by Proposition
3.6
.
By lemma again, a linear combination of
p
and
\{p_{\alpha_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{d-m^{p}_{\alpha}}
expresses
r(x)=(x-1-\alpha)^{m^{q}_{\beta}}(x-\alpha)^{d-m^{q}_{\beta}}
and hence
r\boxplus_{d}q
has a root at
0
with multiplicity more than
1
. This is a contradiction by Proposition
3.6
.
∎
