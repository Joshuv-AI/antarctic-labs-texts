Effect of vanadium layer thickness on the phase transition of fabricated vanadium dioxide thin films
Authors: Mohamed Ibrahim, Zhang Xiao, Jiajun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxidizing a thin Vanadium layer is one way to develop VO2; however, the different thicknesses of deposited Vanadium thin layers respond differently to the same annealing parameters during the transformation of vanadium into vanadium dioxide and influence the final VO2 layer. In this work, we investigated how pure Vanadium layers with thicknesses of 25 nm, 40 nm, 55 nm, 70 nm, and 90 nm will respond to the same annealing parameters by characterizing the final oxidized layer. E-beam was used to deposit the Vanadium layers, then annealed under N2 and O2 to form VO2. X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Raman spectroscopy were used to analyze phase composition and phase fractions. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) was used to investigate the phase change temperature along with the sample hysteresis curves during heating and cooling. Focused Ion Beam (FIB) equipped with Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) was used to investigate the thickness and O2 diffusivity.Keywords: Vanadium oxide, MIT, E-beam, Annealing, Thin film, HysteresisSubject terms: Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Vanadium (V) has the electronic configuration [Ar] 4s²3d³ and is well-known for its ability to form various oxides. Developing a high-quality VO2 layer is challenging. The reason for this is the multiple stoichiometries of vanadium oxides, which contain multivalent vanadium cations (e.g., V2+, V3+, V4+, and V5+).1,2. The principal oxides of vanadium are vanadium monoxide (VO, violet color), vanadium sesquioxide (V2O3, green color), vanadium dioxide (VO2, blue color), and vanadium pentoxide (V2O5, yellow color).3,4 Most vanadium oxides exhibit a metal−insulator transition (MIT). One of the well-studied oxides of Vanadium is Vanadium dioxide.5–9 The vanadium dioxide phase change property from semiconductor to metal was first discovered in 195910. This phase transition is reversible and accompanied by changes in crystallographic, magnetic, optical, and electrical properties.1,2,11. Below the phase change temperature, which is around 68 °C, the VO2 layer shows a semiconductor phase behavior where it is transparent to infrared radiation and characterized by a monoclinic lattice structure, while above the phase change temperature, the VO2 transforms to the tetragonal (rutile) phase, showing a metallic phase where it becomes highly reflective to infrared radiation12–15.

Several reports discuss changes in the resistivity and infrared transmittance of VO2 films, specifically focusing on their electrical and optical switching properties; however, these reports are inconsistent. This indicates that the deposition, the substrate, and annealing parameters highly influence the thin film properties during deposition16.

Different studies have shown that the VO2 layer’s phase-change temperature, morphology, optical, and electrical properties are strongly influenced by several parameters, including deposition method, film thickness, annealing conditions, oxidation timing, and substrate material, on top of that, the final oxidized vanadium layer behavior can’t be explained by a single parameter, instead, annealing, substrate, deposition method, thickness, and starting material are all interconnected, and the final oxidized layer behavior depends on how these factors influence each other. Despite extensive studies on VO₂ thin films, the specific role of vanadium thickness under identical deposition, substrate, oxidation, and annealing conditions remains unclear, as most work varies multiple parameters simultaneously, making it difficult to isolate its direct effect.

For instance, several studies showed that annealing at moderate temperatures around 350 ˚C to 450 ˚C can improve film crystallization and switching behavior.17,18 However, in other cases, annealing under similar annealing conditions can result in mixed phases or degraded properties when factors such as substrate, oxygen environment, or film structure are not controlled19,20. which emphasizes that temperature alone does not determine the final phase. Depositing on the same substrate can also lead to different outcomes if other factors are not controlled. For example, a study showed PLD-grown films on silicon, combined with controlled annealing, can show low transition temperatures (~ 52 °C) and very small hysteresis (~ 3 °C)17 However, another study showed that VO₂ films deposited on silicon can also exhibit different crystallinity, hysteresis, or phase composition when deposition or annealing conditions are changed20.

Also, the same deposition method does not guarantee the same results, if other factors are not taken into consideration, for example, a study showed that reactive sputtering followed by annealing can lead to films with large hysteresis and low transition temperature (~ 32 °C)21. However, other sputtered films show mixed phases or different properties when deposition parameters or annealing conditions change20.

While these overall trends provide useful insight, a clearer understanding can be achieved by examining each study individually, treating the full combination of processing parameters as a single system.

Regarding the deposition method, for example, studies have shown that the morphological evolution of sputtered thin VO2 films is dominated by the strain imparted during sputtering. In contrast, for films deposited by PLD and electron beam evaporation, the substrate plays a more important role22. Other work showed that, by Pulsed-Laser Deposition followed by Rapid Thermal Annealing in oxygen on four different substrates, good transition behavior can be obtained at annealing temperatures as low as 350 °C, with the highest infrared transmission shifts observed at 450 °C, and transition temperatures as low as 52 °C on both silicon and transparent quartz, 16 °C lower than the usual bulk transition temperature of 68 °C.17.

Another study used V2O5 to prepare vanadium oxide films via the sol-gel method. After annealing at 390 °C for 4 h, a pure VO2 (M) phase was obtained. The maximum transmittance change at 2500 nm was 39%, the phase transition temperature was 64.3 °C, and the hysteresis loop width was 12.1°C18. Both the hysteresis loop width and phase transition temperature are correlated with the particle size of the VO2 film23. Other researchers used ALD to deposit VO2 films with thicknesses from 8.6 to 57 nm on c-Al2O3. As the film thickness increased, the transition temperature decreased slightly. The transition steepness increased with increasing temperature.24. One study showed that Vanadium dioxide films with large phase-transition hysteresis loops were fabricated on glass substrates by reactive direct current (DC) magnetron sputtering in an Ar/O2 atmosphere, followed by an in-situ annealing process in pure oxygen. 21. Others used reactive deposition in oxygen-containing plasma, followed by vacuum deposition of vanadium, then oxidation in air. It has been shown that the latter process allows the formation of films with a reduced phase transition temperature of 50 ◦C.25. Other research deposited VO2 on TiO2 (001) and (110) substrates. An increase in the phase transition temperature from 68 °C to 96 °C for films deposited on TiO2 (110)26. Other researchers use atmospheric pressure thermal oxidation of Vanadium to synthesize VO2 films on glass and ITO-coated glass substrates. The results showed high optical reflectance switching in the long-wavelength infrared (8–14 μm) on glass substrates and in the mid-wavelength infrared (3–5 μm) on ITO-coated glass substrates, respectively.27.

Another work, using the oxidation method on magnetron-sputtered vanadium metal thin films, fabricated a series of vanadium dioxide thin films by varying the annealing duration and substrate (quartz glass or AZO-covered glass) under fixed annealing parameters of temperature (400 ℃). The results showed that an appropriate annealing duration is a key factor for obtaining pure VO2 films, and AZO-coated glass is more suitable for producing VO2 films with high visible transmittance, good crystallinity, and larger near-infrared switching efficiencies than quartz glass. 20. Another study showed that the VO2 film deposited by radiofrequency reactive sputtering is quite stable when annealed in air below 200 °C. In contrast, the pure VO2 film oxidizes to V2O5 upon annealing in air at 300 °C.28. Other researchers used Hydrothermal techniques to prepare monoclinic vanadium oxide (VO2). The as-grown samples were annealed in an Argon atmosphere at 350 °C, 450 °C, and 550 °C for 6 h each. Two other phases, V6O13 and V2O5, were observed after annealing of the as-grown samples. The percentages of each depend on the annealing temperature. 19. Another study prepared (VO2) films by direct-current magnetron sputter deposition on m, a, and r plane sapphire substrates. VO2 films deposited on differently oriented sapphire substrates have different microstructures.29. Another study deposited Vanadium oxide films on glass and silicon substrates by using magnetron sputtering of the vanadium target at various substrate temperatures (180–500 °C), followed by low-temperature annealing. In films deposited at low substrate temperatures (200–220 °C) and subsequently annealed at low temperature, flat crystallites of vanadium dioxide form, providing a strong thermochromic effect. In films deposited at 250–300 °C, followed by low-temperature annealing, a microcrystalline mixture of different vanadium oxides forms. For films deposited at 450–500 °C, low-temperature annealing does not significantly change their properties.30. A study analyzed the electrical and optical properties of VO2 thin films deposited on quartz substrates by the sol-gel spin-coating method. The results revealed that the VO2 thin films deposited at the lowest spin coating speed exhibit high electrical conductivity, high transparency, and a high optical band gap.31. A study showed that VO2 thin films of varying thickness were deposited onto ITO substrates by direct current magnetron sputtering. The results showed that grain size increases with film thickness.32. Other researchers studied the effects of V film thickness deposition temperature on the optimal oxidation time for achieving VO2 films. It was found that the optimal oxidation time for synthesizing VO2 films increases monotonically with increasing V film thickness and increases with the deposition temperature.33. In another study, Nanocrystalline vanadium dioxide thin films were grown on glass substrates by reactive direct-current magnetron sputtering and in situ thermal treatments at low preparation temperatures (≤ 350 °C). The testing results showed that the crystal structure, morphology, grain size, and semiconductor-metal transition temperature (TSMT) significantly changed as the film thickness decreased.34.

It can be noticed that the final formed oxidized layer is highly affected by several factors. However, based on the reviewed studies, it can be inferred that the deposition method mainly affects the strain and initial film structure, while the annealing conditions strongly influence phase formation and possible degradation. Also, shifting the transition temperature seems to be affected mostly by the substrate material, while film thickness is closely related to grain size, stress, and oxidation behavior. In addition, the starting material affects the oxidation pathway and phase stability. However, these are only general trends and cannot be taken as guaranteed, since the final behavior depends on the combined effect of multiple factors, as mentioned earlier.

Based on these reports, it is concluded that there is no consistent or specific method for developing VO2 thin layers that undergo phase change, and any change in the deposition tool or annealing parameters affects the behavior of the resulting VO2 layer.

This work aims to study how the thickness of vanadium films affects their behavior when all samples are treated under the same annealing conditions during oxidation. By keeping the deposition method, the substrate, and the annealing conditions identical, the effect of the initial pure vanadium thickness layer can be clearly observed in the final oxidized vanadium oxide layer. The response of the films is evaluated by analyzing the phase fractions, measuring the phase transition temperatures during both heating and cooling, examining the IR transparency at different temperatures, and studying the hysteresis behavior. This approach helps to clearly understand how thickness influences the structural and functional properties of the films.

A study inspired our work for this research on developing VO2 35. The method introduced here is to deposit pure vanadium using E-beam, then anneal the sample under N2 and O2 to oxidize the V to VO2.

Vanadium dioxide

Five samples with thicknesses of 25 nm, 40 nm, 55 nm, 70 nm, and 90 nm of pure vanadium were prepared. Subsequently, the five samples were oxidized in an inert gas furnace under Nitrogen, with Oxygen added to oxidize the Vanadium. The formed layer was characterized, followed by a phase-change test, and, finally, O2 diffusivity and the layer thickness after oxidation were measured.

Results and discussion

Preliminary inspection for oxidized layer color

It was observed that a color change occurred in pure Vanadium after annealing and oxidation; however, this change depends on the initial thickness of the pure vanadium layer and the annealing parameters. Figure 1 shows the color of the pure vanadium sample (which is the same for all Vanadium thicknesses) and the color of each oxidized sample after annealing. The color difference in the oxidized layers is clearly visible. As mentioned earlier, each Vanadium oxidation state has its own color1–4,11,36. This color change indicates that each sample has a different Vanadium oxidation state, even though they were all annealed at the same time in the same furnace under the same conditions. X-ray diffraction

XRD was used to identify the phases present in the Vanadium oxidized films. Grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) measurements were performed using a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation operated at 45 kV and 200 mA. The incident angle was fixed at 0.42° to enhance surface sensitivity and suppress substrate contributions. This is the reason behind the absence of the silicon wafer diffraction signal. Data were collected over a 2θ range of 10°–60° with a step size of 0.01° and a scan speed of 4°/min. A parallel beam (PB) optical configuration with a HyPix-3000 detector was used to ensure accurate diffraction measurements for thin-film analysis.

As shown in Fig. 2a, the 25 nm, 40 nm, and 55 nm samples all exhibited a peak at 2θ = 41.2˚, corresponding to pure Vanadium.37,38 which is more of a hump than a sharp peak, indicating that the surfaces of these three samples are more likely to be amorphous than crystalline. The color changes in these three samples indicate that oxidation has already occurred. The 70 nm and 90 nm samples show several peaks at 2θ = 20.3˚, 37˚, 42.1˚, and 55.4˚, indicating different vanadium oxide mixtures.

To quantitatively evaluate the phase composition of the films, Rietveld refinement was performed on background-corrected GIXRD patterns. The Rietveld refinement of the thinner three samples reveals that the films consist primarily of oxygen-deficient vanadium oxide structures rather than stoichiometric VO2, consistent with under-oxidized or partially oxidized Vanadium.

For the 25 nm sample, the Rietveld refinement shows high values of (Rwp ≈ 48.9%, Rp ≈ 39.5%, χ² ≈ 3.78), which means the model does not fit the data well. Although the fitting indicates the presence of more than one vanadium oxide phase, the obtained structural parameters are not reliable, indicating that the refinement is unstable. This is likely due to the broad hump observed in the GIXRD pattern, which suggests low crystallinity and structural disorder in the thin film. Therefore, the phase composition of this sample is discussed qualitatively rather than quantitatively, indicating incomplete oxidation and the presence of disordered or oxygen-deficient vanadium oxide.

Rietveld refinement of both the 40 nm and 55 nm samples, as shown in Fig. 2b, indicates that the films are composed entirely of a single vanadium oxide phase, VO0.16. This phase crystallizes in a tetragonal structure with refined lattice parameters of a = b = 3.05(3) Å and c = 3.21(12) Å for the 40 nm sample, and a = b = 3.02(7) Å and c = 3.15(15) Å for the 55 nm sample, corresponding to unit cell volumes of 29.83 Å³ and 28.67 Å³, respectively (PDF-4-Axiom 04-020-6075). The refined weight fraction for both samples is 100%, indicating homogeneous single-phase films. The refinement yielded reliability factors of Rwp = 26.50% and Rp = 19.38% for the 40 nm sample, and Rwp = 24.90% and Rp = 18.30% for the 55 nm sample, with goodness-of-fit values of S = 1.073 (χ² = 1.15) and S = 1.079 (χ² = 1.16), respectively, indicating acceptable overall fits despite the relatively high R-factors. These elevated Rwp and Rp values can be attributed to the nature of grazing-incidence XRD (GIXRD) measurements on thin films, where preferred orientation, peak broadening, background contributions, and low signal-to-noise ratio can reduce refinement accuracy. The refined strain values of 1.12% for the 40 nm sample and 1.09% for the 55 nm sample further indicate the presence of moderate lattice distortion, likely associated with thin-film stress, surface effects, and microstructural imperfections.

It is observed that the 25 nm, 40 nm, and 55 nm samples show a similar broad diffraction hump, which indicates the same vanadium oxide phase. However, the thinner 25 nm film has weaker and less clear diffraction peaks, which leads to poorer fit and less reliable refinement compared to the 40 nm and 55 nm samples.

Rietveld refinement of the 70 nm sample indicates that the film is predominantly composed of paramontroseite (VO₂), with a refined weight fraction of 85(13) wt%. This phase crystallizes in the orthorhombic structure (PDF-4-Axiom 04-005-4318), with refined lattice parameters of a = 5.00(2) Å, b = 8.93(2) Å, and c = 2.879(7) Å, corresponding to a unit cell volume of 128.70 Å³. In addition to VO₂, two minor oxygen-deficient vanadium oxide phases were detected: a cubic VO0.03 phase (PDF-4-Axiom 04-012-8107) with a = b = c = 3.072(12) Å and a unit cell volume of 28.99 Å³, contributing 6.0(16) wt%, and a cubic V0.9O phase (PDF-4-Axiom 04-010-5832) with a = b = c = 4.246(18) Å and a unit cell volume of 76.54 Å³, contributing 9(13) wt%. The presence of these substoichiometric cubic phases indicates incomplete oxidation and residual oxygen-deficient vanadium regions within the film. The refinement yielded reliability factors of Rwp = 26.06%, Rp = 19.47%, with a goodness of fit (S) of 1.27 and χ² = 1.62, indicating an acceptable fit. Microstrain values for all phases are below 0.5%, suggesting moderate lattice distortion due to multiphase coexistence and film stress.

For the 90 nm sample, Rietveld refinement indicates a multiphase composition comprising both fully oxidized and substoichiometric vanadium oxides. The predominant phase is paramontroseite (VO₂, PDF-4-Axiom 04-005-4318), contributing 59(3) wt%, with refined lattice parameters of a = 4.96(3) Å, b = 9.65(3) Å, and c = 2.823(10) Å, corresponding to a unit cell volume of 135.19 Å³. A second minor phase, a cubic VO0.03 phase (PDF-4-Axiom 04-012-8107), accounts for 28.0(19) wt%, with a = b = c = 3.058(10) Å and a unit cell volume of 28.60 Å³. In addition, a monoclinic V₂O₃ related phase contributes 13(3) wt%, with lattice parameters a = 14.05(5) Å, b = 5.04(2) Å, c = 8.610(17) Å, and β = 91.6(2) °, corresponding to a unit cell volume of 609.39 Å³. The coexistence of VO0.03 and V₂O₃ phases indicates incomplete oxidation, while the presence of VO₂ demonstrates partial formation of the fully oxidized phase. The refinement yielded reliability factors of Rwp = 25.93%, Rp = 18.93%, with a goodness of fit (S) of 1.12 and χ² = 1.26, indicating an acceptable overall fit. Microstrain values range from 0.16% to 1.01%, reflecting moderate lattice distortion likely associated with multiphase coexistence and thin-film stress effects.

The addition of V2O5 in the refinement was tested; however, it did not significantly improve the agreement factors and caused mismatches with the experimental pattern.

Raman spectroscopy

was used to investigate the Vanadium oxide phases for different sample thicknesses under the same annealing parameters. Figure 3 shows that the thinnest films exhibited only one peak at 146 cm-1 and showed only a broad Raman feature, indicating limited material scattering and low crystallinity. As the film thickness increased, the Raman peaks became sharper and more intense. This shows improvement in film crystallinity and an increase in material content. The peaks at 146 cm-1, 199 cm-1, 283 cm-1, 404 cm-1, 701 cm-1, and 992 cm-1 indicate different Vanadium oxides39. These results indicate that, under identical annealing conditions, thicker vanadium films oxidize more fully and crystallize better than thinner ones, which also aligns with the XRD results.

Raman spectroscopy pattern for oxidized Vanadium layer after annealing for thicknesses of a) 25 nm, b) 40 nm, c) 55 nm, d) 70 nm, and e) 90 nm.

Although Raman spectroscopy suggests the presence of V₂O₅, its inclusion in the Rietveld refinement did not improve the fit and resulted in noticeable mismatches with the diffraction pattern. This discrepancy indicates that V₂O₅ is likely present in a poorly crystalline or minor form, below the reliable detection limit of XRD.

Phase change investigation

As mentioned, VO2 exhibits a unique phase transition, transitioning from an insulating phase to a metallic phase below and above a phase transition temperature of approximately 68 °C. Below 68 °C, VO2 is in the insulating phase, which is transparent to IR. Above 68 °C, VO2 undergoes a metal-to-insulator transition, becoming more reflective of IR radiation. By comparing the amount of transmitted IR through the VO2 layer below and above the phase-change temperature, it can be determined whether a phase change has occurred. For this purpose, we used an FTIR equipped with a hot stage and measured the amount of transmitted IR through the samples at 30 °C and 90 °C between 2.5 μm and 20 μm.

Figure 4 shows the amount of transmitted IR through each sample for each thickness. It was observed that the amount of transmitted IR through the 25 nm, 40 nm, and 55 nm samples remained constant at 30 °C and 90 °C, indicating that no phase change occurred in any of the samples. This result aligns with the XRD and color change, indicating that only the surface layer was oxidized, while the underlying layers remained unoxidized, with no indication of VO2.

Transmitted IR through VO2 samples for thicknesses of a) 25 nm, b) 40 nm, c) 55 nm, d) 70 nm, and e) 90 nm. below phase change temperature (Solid line) and above phase change temperature (Dotted line)

A reduction in the amount of transmitted IR through the 70 nm and 90 nm samples was observed, indicating a phase change in these samples, which aligns with the XRD data, as those two samples showed a VO2 phase fraction. It can be observed that both samples showed poor deviation in the transmitted IR, and this behavior was expected as both samples showed a mixed phases of different vanadium oxides, however it is noticed that the 70 nm sample exhibited more IR transmittance change when compared to the 90 nm sample, and this behavior also aligns with the Rietveld analysis for these two samples, as for the 70 nm sample with a higher relative VO₂ fraction, is expected to show enhanced thermochromic infrared deviation when compared to the 90 nm, but still showing less deviation than pure VO2 as it still a mixture of VO2 and other vanadium oxides.

Since both the 70 nm and 90 nm samples showed deviations in infrared transmittance measured by FTIR, their temperature-dependent phase transition behavior was investigated. FTIR measurements were performed during heating and cooling cycles between 30 °C and 90 °C in 10 °C increments. Figure 5 shows the infrared transmittance at 2.5 μm during heating and cooling for both samples. An apparent thermal hysteresis is observed between heating and cooling. During heating, for both samples, the phase transition began at approximately 35 °C; it progressed gradually between 35 °C and 50 °C, then abruptly decreased from 50 °C to 70 °C, and finally transitioned smoothly from 70 °C to 80 °C; however, during cooling, the 70 nm sample shows essentially unchanged transmittance down to 60 °C, followed by a smooth transition between 60 °C and 55 °C, then a rapid increase in transmittance between 55 °C and 30 °C. Unlike the 70 nm sample, the 90 nm sample shows essentially unchanged transmittance down to 70 °C, followed by a smooth transition between 70 °C and 55 °C, then a rapid increase in transmittance between 55 °C and 35 °C.

Phase change behavior for heating and cooling for the 70 nm, and 90 nm samples.

The asymmetric shape of the hysteresis loop reflects differences in phase nucleation and growth during heating and cooling, which are influenced by microstructure, strain, and stoichiometry. Both samples showed wide hysteresis, as expected, given that XRD revealed mixed vanadium oxides and relatively highly strained films.

Methods

Vanadium dioxide was prepared by depositing pure vanadium using Electron beam deposition (E-beam) on one-inch-diameter silicon wafers. The pure vanadium deposition was performed under a 3 × 10^-6 torr chamber vacuum at a deposition rate of 0.7 A°/s. Five samples with thicknesses of 25 nm, 40 nm, 55 nm, 70 nm, and 90 nm of pure vanadium were prepared. Subsequently, the five samples were oxidized in an inert gas furnace under 30 ml/min of Nitrogen, mixed with 6 ml/min of Oxygen to oxidize the Vanadium.

The oxidation took place at 300 °C for 3 h. First, when the furnace was at room temperature, the samples were placed inside. Then, the furnace temperature was ramped from room temperature to 300 °C in 1 h under a flow rate of 30 ml/min of Nitrogen. Once the temperature reached 300 °C, 6 ml/min of Oxygen gas was introduced to the chamber. After three hours, the oxygen flow was turned off, and the cool-down process began under a nitrogen flow of 30 ml/min. The samples were left in the furnace until they cooled to room temperature under Nitrogen before being removed. These parameters were adapted based on another work.35.

Denton Vacuum Infinity was used to deposit pure vanadium. While the annealing took place inside an inert gas furnace. The Rigaku Smart Lab X-ray diffraction system was used to characterize the samples. An IR-100 tracer Shimadzu FTIR was used to investigate the phase-change properties of the samples below and above the phase-change temperature. An electric thin-film heater connected to a power supply was attached to the sample holder, allowing the samples to be heated. The IR transmittance of each sample was measured at room temperature (below the phase-change temperature) and at 90 °C (above the phase-change temperature). The transparency measurements were also done during heating and cooling cycles between 30 °C and 90 °C in 10 °C increments.

Conclusion

In this work, it was found that the initial thickness of the pure vanadium layer plays a crucial role in the final formed oxidized Vanadium layer. Five vanadium samples with different thicknesses were deposited and annealed simultaneously under identical conditions; however, they resulted in different vanadium oxide compositions. For the 25 nm, 40 nm, and 55 nm samples, the films primarily consisted of oxygen-deficient vanadium oxide phases rather than stoichiometric VO₂, indicating under-oxidized or partially oxidized material. In contrast, the 70 nm and 90 nm samples exhibited a mixture of vanadium oxides with varying phase fractions. Notably, the presence of the Paramontroseite VO₂ phase in these thicker films explains their ability to undergo a phase transition, unlike the thinner samples. Although both the 70 nm and 90 nm samples showed some deviation in optical transparency, the change was not considered significant due to the absence of pure VO₂. However, the 70 nm sample exhibited a greater deviation compared to the 90 nm sample, consistent with its higher VO₂ phase fraction. It should also be noted that the 25 nm sample showed poor fitting accuracy, and that the V₂O₅ phase fitting was not consistently representative across all samples.
