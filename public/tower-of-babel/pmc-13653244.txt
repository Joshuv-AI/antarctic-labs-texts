ABSTRACT
AbstractMigraine is one of the most common chronic health problems in children and adolescents, resulting in high levels of functional disability. Symptoms frequently persist into and impact the course of adulthood for affected individuals. Current practice recommendations include a combined therapy approach of preventive medication and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT); however, no trials have systematically assessed whether a combined therapy approach is superior to a cognitive behavioral monotherapy intervention. The REACH (Responding With Evidence and Access for Childhood Headaches) trial will compare the impact of a CBT monotherapy to a combined treatment for prevention of migraine in youth, while addressing barriers to CBT access by utilizing a teletherapy approach. The study will include youth with migraine between the ages of 10 and 17 years from 15 sites across the United States, who will be randomly assigned to a CBT + pill (amitriptyline) group or CBT‐alone group. Both groups will complete six sessions of telehealth CBT, as well as three booster sessions, over the course of 6 months. Participants will report on headache and migraine frequency and related disability via a daily headache diary before (28‐day baseline), during, and after completion of the intervention (the last 28 days on the protocol). The CBT + pill group will also undergo a dose titration of clinically prescribed amitriptyline throughout the active treatment phase. Primary treatment outcomes are a ≥50% reduction in headache days and reduction in migraine‐related disability (i.e., Pediatric Migraine Disability Assessment score ≤20). Findings of the REACH study may confirm current practice guidelines or provide evidence of comparable effectiveness of combined therapy and CBT monotherapy for prevention of migraine. This trial is designed to reinforce current guidelines for preventive headache management strategies in youth with migraine. The study will also advance knowledge by determining whether CBT alone, without the potential side effects of medication, is sufficient.

INTRODUCTION

Migraine is a neurological disorder that globally affects approximately 10% of children and adolescents, 1 suggesting that up to 7 million youth in the United States alone may be impacted by the disease. Characteristic symptoms of migraine include pulsing head pain, sensitivity to light and sound, and nausea and vomiting. 2 , 3 , 4 Migraine is the second leading cause of disability worldwide, and research to date indicates that migraine in childhood and adolescence disrupts quality of life and aspects of academic and social–emotional functioning in a manner comparable to that of youth impacted by pediatric cancer, heart disease, and rheumatic disease. 5 , 6 Population‐based studies demonstrate that over 130,000 school days are missed every 2 weeks due to migraine, and 3 million bedridden migraine days occur per month among children with migraine. 7 Children with migraine endorse decreased positive social interactions 8 , 9 and increased rates of anxious and depressive symptomatology. 10 Unsurprisingly, the costs associated with managing such an impactful condition are substantial; health care costs are 70% higher for a family with a child with migraine relative to those without. 11 Using data collected in 1989, migraine prevalence was strongly negatively associated with household income, with 60% higher frequency in households with income <$10,000 (equivalent to approximately $26,000 in 2026), as compared to higher income groups. 12 Thus, societally, those who most frequently experience migraine are less likely to be able to access the resources they need to manage migraine (perhaps in part due to the caregiver's inability to work due to child‐migraine management demands) and may be more adversely impacted by the functional disability associated with migraine. Such diminished functioning has implications across the lifespan, as migraine tends to persist into adulthood, 6 increasing the long‐term health care burden and functional impairment associated with this condition.

Early intervention to address migraine symptoms before they become chronic and refractory is crucial. Prior work suggests that preventive migraine treatment for youth can lead to significant reductions in headache days and associated disability for up to 5 years after initiation of interdisciplinary care. 13 Such interventions include pill‐based treatment (e.g., amitriptyline) and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), an evidence‐based nonpharmacologic treatment. 14 , 15 Current practice guidelines from the American Headache Society and the American Academy of Neurology for migraine prevention in children and adolescents support the integration of CBT and amitriptyline as the most effective treatment approach, because it is superior to medication alone. 16 However, these recommendations are challenging to implement in real‐world practice due to numerous barriers to the pharmacologic and nonpharmacologic management of migraine. Nearly 40% of states have between 0 and 2 certified headache subspecialists, 17 and this is even more pronounced in the Western and Plains states. 18 Children living in these areas may experience longer time to diagnosis and treatment due to lack of access of specialized neurology care and discomfort among primary care providers in treating and managing migraine, 19 thereby increasing the likelihood of these youth developing chronification of the disease, or even intractable symptoms. Furthermore, psychologists specializing in behavioral medicine (let alone those specializing in the application of behavioral medicine to headache disorders) also are not often available outside of large metropolitan areas, and insurance payors do not consistently provide coverage for CBT, limiting the availability of behavioral therapies tailored to the unique needs of children and adolescents with migraine. 20 Moreover, the combined preventive interventions of CBT plus medication and CBT alone have not been compared in a pragmatic trial using contemporary best practices. This research is of the utmost importance to pediatric patients, their families, medical providers, and insurance companies, all of whom need clarity as to which treatments afford the most rapid and durable clinical outcomes with the fewest side effects and impact on disruption to daily life and development. Of note, a pragmatic clinical trial is optimal to address known barriers to care of youth with migraine, given the focus on real‐world effectiveness and inclusive and flexible eligibility criteria reflective of the diverse populations of youth with migraine.

The REACH (Responding With Evidence and Access for Childhood Headaches) study (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05889624) will address these needs and current gaps in the literature. This multisite, telehealth, comparative effectiveness study will test combined therapy (CBT + amitriptyline, herein referred to as CBT + pill) as compared to nonpharmacologic monotherapy (herein referred to as CBT alone) in youth with migraine.

The specific aims of this project are to:

Aim 1: Compare the impact of CBT + pill prevention therapy versus CBT alone on headache days, as measured through a participant‐reported daily headache diary, both immediately upon completion of CBT and at the 6‐month time point relative to baseline.

Aim 2: Compare the impact of CBT + pill versus CBT alone on migraine‐related disability, comparing 6 months relative to baseline.

Aim 3: Compare the safety of and participant satisfaction with CBT + pill versus CBT alone.

Aim 4 (exploratory): Compare the impact of CBT + pill versus CBT alone on functional impact and quality of life.

Guided by feedback from International Headache Society trial guidelines, 1 the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke Common Data Elements, 21 and feedback from medical provider and patient and caregiver stakeholders, we will use the clinically meaningful end points of headache days and migraine‐related disability (measured by the Pediatric Migraine Disability Assessment [PedMIDAS]). 21 , 22 Headache days at baseline will be compared to posttreatment in three ways: (1) ≥50% reduction in headache days, (2) absolute reduction in headache days, and (3) reduction in headache days reported by participants to be severe. Secondary and exploratory outcomes considered include functional disability 23 and quality of life. 20 This multisite study will be coordinated by the Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center and is funded by the Patient‐Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI). PCORI as a funding agency emphasizes and requires engagement of patients, caregivers, and the broader health care community impacted by the funded research (herein referred to as stakeholders) throughout the research process to ensure the resultant product addresses a need identified not only by the research community, but also by those with lived experience and by providers and advocates working to support them. By including stakeholders of several perspectives, such as youth with migraine and medical providers, primary outcomes (i.e., headache days, migraine‐related disability) are decided upon based on clinical and lived experience. Moreover, cutoff scores and meaningful reductions in various scales utilized (i.e., for the PedMIDAS, described below) are guided not only by existent literature, but by what clinicians in practice see translating to changes in quality of life and enhanced functioning, enhancing the real‐world applicability of findings. Additionally, soliciting the perspective of these stakeholder perspectives during CBT intervention development and implementation can strengthen acceptability of and adherence to the treatment protocol. Stakeholders will continue to be advisors and key partners in the project during active recruitment and study execution. A steering committee will incorporate feedback from the stakeholder groups as study‐related decisions are made.

BACKGROUND

Why CBT?

Fundamentally, CBT focuses on (1) improving an individual's understanding of connections among the domains of thoughts, emotions, bodily sensations, and behaviors; and (2) development of skills to improve functioning in these domains. Key components of CBT are well‐suited to address migraine, especially when the disease is conceptualized using the biopsychosocial model of health. 24 This view of migraine recognizes that a complex interplay of biological (e.g., genetics, hormone fluctuations, altered pain processing), social (e.g., social support, socioeconomic factors, cultural beliefs), and psychological (e.g., personality traits, coping strategies, cognitive) factors are involved in the etiology and maintenance of migraine. 24 In CBT treatment, individuals learn to engage in adaptive self‐management of migraine symptoms while simultaneously participating in meaningful activities they may have avoided or stopped doing altogether due to their symptoms or fear of provoking symptoms. Broadly, the goal of CBT for migraine is to reduce symptoms and their impact, promote long‐term well‐being, and support optimal daily functioning. Foundational aspects of this treatment include migraine psychoeducation, instruction in mind–body skills designed to strengthen the parasympathetic nervous system, healthy lifestyle habits critical for migraine management (e.g., getting adequate sleep and hydration), activity pacing, and strategies to manage maladaptive cognitions. 25 Although prior Cochrane reviews conclude that behavioral treatments (including CBT) are effective for headache prevention, 26 no research to date has compared its effectiveness to a combined therapy for headache management.

Why 8 weeks of active treatment?

Duration of CBT in real‐world clinical practice can vary due to many factors including patient characteristics (e.g., chronicity of symptoms, presence of comorbidities), provider preferences, and mode of treatment delivery. The REACH study will employ six sessions across an 8‐week timeline for active treatment. This approach was determined based on findings from a prior trial of CBT + pill (amitriptyline) compared to headache education + pill, 27 which utilizing ancillary trajectory analysis revealed that the majority of improvement seen in a CBT + pill approach to pediatric migraine was observed at the 8‐week time point.

Why telehealth?

Telehealth delivery of CBT will be utilized to address the known barriers to accessing CBT generally (e.g., cost and time demands, geographic isolation 28 ) and specifically as it pertains to migraine care (e.g., dearth of migraine‐specific trained professionals 20 ). Clinical outcomes for CBT for migraine delivered via telehealth indicate decreased headache days and related disability, 29 while simultaneously addressing a known barrier to accessing care (i.e., proximity to psychologists). To ensure the delivery of telehealth CBT for migraine was feasible for providers, psychologist stakeholders were involved in the design process for the REACH study. Nine multidisciplinary headache psychologists from four children's hospitals in the United States completed Likert‐type scale items and answered open‐ended questions regarding the feasibility, acceptability, and comparability of telehealth as compared to traditional face‐to‐face CBT. All rated the feasibility of delivering telehealth CBT for migraine as highly feasible (i.e., at a level of 4 or 5, Mean (M) = 4.43), indicated it to be comparable to traditional CBT delivery (i.e., at a level of 4 or 5, M = 4.5), and of excellent effectiveness in reducing headache frequency, disability, and bolstering self‐management strategies (i.e., at a level of 4 or 5, M = 4.67). Given clinician and preliminary evidence of feasibility of telehealth delivery, the present study presents an opportunity to bolster the limited evidence to date, address any uncertainties families and clinicians may feel about telehealth treatment efficacy, and identify a more accessible treatment‐delivery option. Stakeholder perspectives will be used to anticipate and address potential challenges to telehealth intervention delivery.

Why amitriptyline?

Our group previously conducted a randomized placebo‐controlled trial comparing the two most prescribed preventive medications: amitriptyline and topiramate (CHAMP [Childhood and Adolescent Migraine Prevention] trial). 30 Although approximately 60%–70% of participants in the pill‐based treatment groups showed a clinically significant response (i.e., at least 50% reduction in headache days and related disability), this was similar across both treatment groups and placebo, and this study was stopped due to futility. As prescription of a placebo is not practical in real‐world clinical care, prioritizing a preventive medication that has the fewest side effects is of the utmost priority 31 , 32 and guided our selection of amitriptyline in the current trial. Although neither amitriptyline nor topiramate is without side effects, some clinicians endorse a preference for amitriptyline, especially in pediatric practice, 33 , 34 reporting a less intrusive side effect profile and the increased adherence potential for a once in the evening dosing regimen. 35 , 36 Although amitriptyline does carry a Food and Drug Administration black box warning for increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behavior in children and adolescents when used at higher doses for the treatment of depression, in our previous work, 30 when prescribed at the lower doses used in management of pediatric migraine, no increased risk of suicidality was observed. This, coupled with the observed endorsement of suicidality in a pediatric participant treated with topiramate during the CHAMP trial, guided our selection of amitriptyline. 30

Why comparative effectiveness?

Among youth with migraine, no direct comparisons have been made between evidence‐based nonpharmacologic treatment alone to a combined pharmacologic–nonpharmacologic therapy. Regardless of the results of this trial, the findings will inform clinical care. Results that support superior outcomes for combined treatment would formally set the standard of care for pediatric migraine. However, if comparison of both treatment groups shows equivalent effects, or superiority of behavioral monotherapy is found, the standard of care may warrant reconsideration. Although data from adult headache studies suggest that combined treatment is more effective than either form of monotherapy (CBT or medication), this effect may not hold in youth and adolescents, wherein a larger effect size for CBT is noted. 26 In such cases as treatment equivalency, or superiority of behavioral monotherapy, CBT could be utilized as a first‐line treatment to reduce youth exposure to potentially unnecessary medications and related side effects, and preventive mediations could then be reserved for prescription in the cases of nonresponders.

Use of comparative effectiveness trials is important, because it focuses on outcomes and aspects of treatment that matter to patients and their families. Youth with migraine and their families value treatments that both decrease headache and related disability and have minimal side effects 14 , 20 , 32 ; they seek sustained benefits and skills that promote coping over time. Feedback from initial interactions with patient and family stakeholders supports this; one responder (14 years old, female) observed, “[CBT] gave me more options of what to do with my headache while I was dealing with them or to prevent them.” Aligned with the values and goals of the PCORI, the REACH study aims to address gaps as identified by the existent research, but also by the patients and families the research aims to speak to and on behalf of.

Comparative effectiveness studies are especially useful for enhancing shared decision‐making among patients and families and their providers. For example, if CBT alone was found to be equivalent in efficacy to CBT + pill, then CBT alone could be an appealing treatment option for youth with migraine who are not interested in or unable to take pharmacologic preventive medications. Additionally, this may present a treatment recommendation for primary care clinicians who may feel uncomfortable navigating prescription treatments, but who are unable to refer to a neurologist due to limitations in care access.

METHODS

Overall study design

This study type is interventional, with a parallel‐group design, two treatment arms, and a masked primary investigator (PI). It is a comparative effectiveness study that will test telehealth‐delivered CBT + pill (amitriptyline) versus CBT alone. A total of 400 children and adolescents between 10 and 17 years old (inclusive) with migraine will be recruited from 15 sites in the United States. Study sites are geographically dispersed nationwide to allow for diverse participant access and participation (see Figure 1). Inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarized in Table 1. Participants will be randomized to CBT + pill or CBT alone (n = 200 each). Key end points will be headache days (i.e., headache days, 50% or greater reduction pre‐ to posttreatment) and migraine‐related disability. Participants will be recruited over approximately 40 months. Each participant will be followed for approximately 28 weeks in the various phases of the study, with visits, timelines, and interventions as indicated in the flowchart (Figure 3) and intervention summary table (Table 3).

Recruitment process, consent, and screening for eligibility

All study procedures and protocol changes have been approved by the PCORI and Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB) (study title: Responding With Evidence and Access for Childhood Headaches, IRB no. 2023–0146). Participants will be recruited from pediatric headache medicine specialty groups, pediatric neurology practices, and other neurology practices treating children and adolescents. To reach the enrollment goal (N = 400) over the study period (40 months), sites are expected to identify potential participants at a rate that supports randomization of eight new participants per 12 months. Methods and processes for identifying patients will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. To support recruitment and retention, progress with enrollment and study completion will be evaluated weekly. Participant's families will receive a detailed schedule of visits and reminders (emails, texts, phone calls if needed) from study staff to support retention.

Informed consent will be obtained prior to conducting any study procedures via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) electronic consent, either in person or remotely (via phone or video/audio conferencing), using a mouse or tablet to provide a signature to indicate agreement. The IRB has also allowed for use of paper consents in line with local institution IRB policy. Assent is obtained following the study site's state laws regarding age of assent, with most sites utilizing an IRB‐approved combined parental consent–youth assent form when indicated. A Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) form will be incorporated into the informed consent form or provided separately, per each site's institutional policy. The HIPAA form describes participant and data confidentiality associated with the study.

The site investigator or designated medical provider will identify potential participants considering the inclusion/exclusion criteria. As youth with a history of depression and suicidal ideation are at increased risk if prescribed amitriptyline, 37 clinical judgment will be used to determine if the patient may be an appropriate study participant. If the medical provider determines that it is appropriate to discuss the study, they will educate the patient and family about the potential risk of amitriptyline and review the black box warning of increased risk of suicidal ideation and behavior at initial treatment or secondary to dose changes.

If appropriate for the study, and consented by study coordinators, the participant will enter the initial 4‐week baseline screening period (Visit 1). Following the baseline screening period (Visit 2), an independent statistician not involved in recruitment will use the R statistical analysis software package to generate a randomization table. Block randomization will be used to assign participants into the two treatment groups (CBT + pill, CBT alone), and by age (10–13 years [younger] or 14–17 years [older]), sex, and headache frequency at baseline (4–14 [low], 15–27 [moderate], and 28 days to continuous [high]). The following stratification results in 12 cells, with participants randomized equally across cells. Only the independent statistician and clinical research supervisor will have access to this stratification plan before enrollment, through the randomization module offered in REDCap. The notification of the treatment assignment will then be delivered electronically to the relevant study personnel (site coordinator, project manager, and teletherapist delivering CBT).

Assessment points

All assessment points and measures administered are summarized in Figure 3.

At Visit 1, after obtaining informed consent, participants will provide demographic information, and information on headache and medication history based on their clinical visit. Such information includes their lifestyle habits (diet, sleep, activity), number of missed school days, height and weight, diagnosis, and current headache treatments (e.g., acute and preventive headache medications and treatments). Study coordinators will instruct participants and their caregivers on daily headache diary completion, and schedule Visit 2 for at least 4 weeks following this first visit.

At Visit 2, eligibility will be determined based on whether the participant had four or more headache days based on prospective review of the headache diary of the prior 28 days. If so, and all other eligibility criteria are met, baseline questionnaires will be administered (see Measures section below), changes in acute and preventive headache medication will be assessed, and headache diaries for the next study interval will be dispensed. Participants will be randomized as described above. If the participant is randomized to CBT + pill, the study coordinator will contact the site PI or designated medical professional to activate the prescription of amitriptyline and advise the participant to start taking the amitriptyline once the prescription is filled. Following randomization, all participants will enter the 8‐week active treatment phase involving six sessions of telehealth CBT. Those assigned to CBT+ pill will also undergo dose titration of amitriptyline. This will be followed by a maintenance phase (16 weeks from Visit 2) when participants will attend three booster telehealth CBT sessions and maintain their dose of medication (if assigned to the CBT + pill group).

Visit 3 will occur approximately 16 weeks after Visit 2, following completion of the active treatment phase. At this time, headache diaries will be reviewed, and changes in medication use assessed. Headache diaries for the final study interval will be dispersed.

Visit 4 will occur approximately 24 weeks after Visit 2. Headache diaries will be reviewed, and changes in medication assessed. All measures administered at baseline (except for the Physical Development Scale) will be readministered to assess change from baseline.

Covariates

Age, 38 , 39 sex, 40 , 41 and baseline headache frequency 41 , 42 data will be included in all primary, secondary, and exploratory aims analyses as covariates. The two following measures will be administered based on feedback from the funder; given potential relationships with migraine‐related outcomes, they will be explored as potential covariates in future analyses and included when appropriate: Patient‐Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System measure (Visit 2, 4): Patient‐Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System measures will be used to assess self‐reported emotional distress (anxiety and depression) and pain interference. These measures, comprising 24 items, are rated on a five‐point scale (0–4; never to almost always). Scores range from 0 to 32, with higher scores indicating greater impact associated with migraine. These scales have been previously utilized and validated in other pediatric disease populations including those with inflammatory bowel disease and chronic pain. 43 , 44 , 45 Physical Development Scale (Visit 2): An eight‐item measure 46 that assesses characteristics of pubertal development will be used to allow for consideration of the impact of puberty on headache related outcomes.

INTERVENTIONIST TRAINING

Sessions will be conducted either by licensed clinical psychologists with experience in treating migraine in children and adolescents, or by trainees under their supervision. In the case of trainees, a licensed clinical psychologist will provide training through use of study‐developed training manuals, designed for each cognitive behavioral intervention delivered, combined with readings related to the delivered module. Prior to contact with participants, all trainees will complete fidelity check sessions for each module in which they complete a mock session observed by their supervisor. Weekly supervision of the trainee teletherapists will occur. As described in more detail below, fidelity assessment of teletherapist sessions will also be used for retraining in cases of nonadherence to the study‐developed manual or tele‐therapist drift.

TREATMENT FIDELITY AND ADHERENCE ASSESSMENT

Treatment integrity to CBT treatment manual

To reliably assess the efficacy of CBT, it is essential that sessions and key components therein are delivered in keeping with the study‐designed treatment manual. The manualized protocol is designed to ensure similar length and number of sessions between teletherapists, as well as skills delivered (see Figure 2). Sessions will be audio recorded, and a trained independent evaluator will review 20% of sessions for each participant to ensure treatment fidelity using a checklist and standardized process guide. 36 Therapist drift from manualized treatment will be mitigated by scheduling regular training reviews (every 12 weeks), and if more significant issues with treatment fidelity present, immediate retraining will occur under supervision of a licensed clinical psychologist. In prior trials, treatment integrity was 95% or greater, 36 which we aim to replicate in the current trial.

Medication adherence

Amitriptyline at (maximum) doses of 1.0 mg/kg daily is not typically associated with significant negative effects, so serious problems with nonadherence are not anticipated. All participants in the CBT + pill group will report whether they took their medication daily when completed prescribed headache diaries (selecting yes/no), an approach that has worked well in previous trials. 30 , 49 Based on these prior trials, adherence is expected to be 80%–90% or greater, with a minimal threshold for recommendation of this therapy as part of best practice being 70% or greater.

Cognitive behavioral treatment adherence

To assess the extent to which participants receive an adequate dose of CBT, the number of treatment sessions completed will be assessed, with the expectation that 88% (eight of nine) or more of sessions will be completed. 50 We anticipate a minimum attendance threshold of six of nine sessions (i.e., 67%). Home practice completion will similarly be assessed as a means of measuring skill engagement between sessions during the active treatment stage, given the expected need for between‐session skill use to experience desired treatment results. We anticipate, based on prior trails, completion at 90% or greater. 50 We view a minimum threshold for completion of home practice as 70%. 51

Stakeholder engagement and integration of feedback

Our research team created a steering committee comprising study researchers, and our stakeholder groups include youth with migraine, caregivers of youth with migraine, psychologists (with expertise in pediatric and/or adult migraine), medical providers (neurologists, neurology advanced practice nurse practitioners), advocates (including professional organization leaders), and payors (and other financial professionals). Different (and sometimes multiple) stakeholder groups have weighed in on numerous aspects of study development including study database creation, intervention development, participant‐facing intervention materials development, recruitment, and (in the future) dissemination.

For example, stakeholder groups consisting of youths, psychologists, and medical providers have made substantial contributions to study intervention development. Through focus groups, individual interviews, and surveys, these stakeholders have provided feedback and guidance on matters such as (1) what are the most important aspects of pediatric headache interventions, (2) what are reasonable expectations for home practice, (3) what language is viewed as person‐centered and respectful, and (4) how best to provide migraine education to youth.

In addition, engagement with payors stakeholder groups has been a critical component of the study design. Understanding how payors evaluate and provide coverage for behavioral medicine interventions for pain and migraine is essential to ensure the study's real‐world applicability and translation. These discussions also address the potential for delivering CBT for pediatric migraine management via telehealth. If proven effective for migraine treatment, it will be necessary for payors to support telehealth coverage to extend access to patients in regions where pain psychologists are not available, thereby ensuring equitable access to the highest standard of care.

We continue to be guided by the Engagement Rubric for Applicants published by the PCORI. 52 , 53 This rubric emphasizes six key principles for stakeholder engagement including colearning (stakeholders learn about research and researchers learn about patient‐centeredness and lived experience), partnership (including reasonable time and contribution requests of stakeholders accompanied by fair compensation), and transparency (including open and honest communication with stakeholders).

ADVERSE EVENT MONITORING

Teletherapists will inquire about changes in health or other concerns during CBT sessions, and study staff will inquire about adverse events during their contact with participants. This will occur about weekly during the 8‐week medication titration and CBT active treatment phase. Thereafter, this will occur monthly until the end of the trial. Throughout the study, for those participants who are prescribed amitriptyline, the medical provider will follow their standard clinical practice to address any safety concerns that may arise related to the medication therapy. If substantial side effects are noted, and the participant chooses to discontinue medication use, they may remain in the study and continue CBT and other study appointments. Adverse events will be recorded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events and reported through REDCap by the teletherapist. Their entry will notify the site coordinator that an adverse event has been entered, and the site coordinator will be responsible for informing the site PI of these adverse events, and if needed, the teletherapist will communicate directly with the site staff about any concerns and take appropriate measures to safeguard the well‐being of the participant.

DATA AND SAFETY MONITORING

As the current study involves randomization but no increase over minimal risk, an Independent Monitoring Committee (IMC) will be utilized to provide oversight and monitoring to ensure the safety of participants, and to assess study progress and data quality and management. The IMC will be composed of a child neurologist, a psychologist with experience in CBT, and a statistician, with no financial, scientific, or other conflict of interest in the study. They will convene twice annually to assess study progress and safety, reviewing a safety report that addresses (1) whether adverse effect rates are consistent with prestudy assumptions, (2) reason for dropouts from the study, (3) whether all participants met entry criteria, (4) whether continuation of the study is justified on the basis that additional data are needed to accomplish the stated aims of the study, and (5) conditions whereby the study might be terminated prematurely.

DATA MANAGEMENT AND QUALITY CONTROL

Data will be managed by the main study site using the REDCap data management system. This system provides a secure, web‐based application designed specifically around HIPAA‐Security guidelines with built‐in features to address confidentiality and compliance requirements, and data will be entered into this system across all participating sites. A detailed data management plan will be developed under the guidance of the study statistician, collaboratively with the Data Management and Analytic Center to guide the preparation of regular safety, performance, and data monitoring progress reports for the IMC's biannual review.

ANALYTIC PLAN

All hypothesis tests will be two‐sided and considered significant at α = 0.05, with familywise type 1 error controlled via Holm's sequentially rejective procedure across the co‐primary family. All aims analyses will be conducted including sex, age, and baseline data as covariates. Nested and nonindependent data arising from participants assessed within C = 15 assessment sites will be handled via a random‐intercept two‐level analysis model. Familywise type 1 error will be managed through the use of Holm's 54 sequentially‐rejective mechanism, applying a family‐wise correction for all end points across all four aims above listed. Missing data will be handled via two‐level fully conditional specification multiple imputation (i.e., H A : imputation; see Keller and Enders 55 ) for all analyses.

Binary logistic analyses will be conducted to test primary aims examining proportional differences between CBT + pill and CBT‐alone groups on binary indicators of achieving greater than 50% reduction in headache days and a PedMIDAS ≤20.

A two‐group (CBT + pill versus CBT alone) comparative analysis will test for mean rate differences in counts of headache days and severe headache days, after testing the significance of the dispersion parameter to determine whether a negative binomial or Poisson count data analytic model is needed. 56

Finally, multivariate two‐group (CBT + pill versus CBT alone) comparative analyses will be used to test for significant mean differences in correlated PedMIDAS, FDI, and PedsQL scores. 57

Power calculation and sample size

Statistical power estimates for all aims were obtained from Monte Carlo simulation power analyses performed in Mplus (version 8.6; Muthén and Muthén 58 ) based on aggregated results from 5000 generated data sets, and were conducted assuming a sample size of 400 (200 per intervention (IV) arm) will be available for analysis (based on proper handling of missing data); that sex, age, and baseline scores will be included as covariates; and that site‐level differences in response variables will be addressed analytically (described above). Parameter estimate assumptions were based on results from a trial examining CBT + amitriptyline 49 and results from several trials in adults by Holroyd and colleagues. 59 , 60 Of note, CBT alone has not been assessed in a recent clinical trial held to the standards of behavioral trials (in adult or pediatric populations). Therefore, we had to estimate these effects and chose to do so based on the response demonstrated by the placebo group in the CHAMP trial. 30 We also assumed minimal (less than 20%) missing at random missing data. Monte Carlo power analysis results showed power ≥0.80 for any two‐group mean rate difference of 0.14 or greater (odds ratios [OR] = 1.77–1.79; based on Powers et al. 36 , 49 ). However, the effects of including age, sex, and baseline headache frequency were not factored into power analysis calculations. As such, these estimates should be treated as conservative.

Simulation power analysis for primary aims examining proportional differences in binary indicators of a 50% or greater reduction in headache days and a PedMIDAS score ≤ 20 showed power 0.80 or greater for a (Δp = 0.14) 14% or greater difference between the IV arms in achieving those primary aims, assuming estimates obtained from Powers et al. 36 are accurate (but see also Powers et al. 49 for an alternative estimate). If the difference in response between the two groups is less than 14%, we may not reliably be able to detect said differences. This power estimate holds for analyses at both the 8‐week and 6‐month assessments assuming Holm's 54 type‐1 error control. We asked about the end points of 50% or greater reduction in headache days and decrease of disability, because they are primary end points and found to be of particular importance to patients and families, 61 as well as this being the recommendation in migraine‐related pharmaceutical trials. 62 Of note, neurologists who care for youth with migraine were surveyed and asked about the difference between two treatments that would change, or reinforce, their current practice. Our colleagues suggested that a 15% difference along with sound safety data would be a clear threshold for them to adopt (or continue) a therapy into first‐line preventive care practice.

Simulation power analyses assessing the minimal mean rate headache and severe headache frequency counts 59 , 60 between the two IV arms were conducted assuming a negative binomial model with dispersion (δ) parameter estimate (δ = 0.15; based on Reidy et al. 63 and δ = 0.766) for headache and severe headache, respectively. Results showed power >0.80 for any unstandardized (b) estimate quantifying the effect of randomization on headache frequency count of b ≤ −0.150823 indicating a (1 − exponent (exp.)−0.1323 = 0.14) 14% decrease in the mean rate of headache counts for CBT + amitriptyline over CBT alone, and for b ≤ −0.2882 indicating a (1 − exp.−0.2882 = 0.25) 25% decrease in the mean rate of severe headache counts, both favoring CBT + pill over CBT alone. Again, as stated above, these estimates are conservative. Finally, multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) simulation results showed power >0.80 for any two‐group PedMIDAS, FDI, or PedsQL mean difference of d > 0.31 favoring CBT + pill over CBT alone.

Handling of missing data

Study staff will employ a variety of methods to minimize the percentage of missing data in this study. Nevertheless, we anticipate a small percentage of missing data. Regarding daily headache diaries, requiring a yes/no answer as to whether a headache occurred on a given day, if no value is marked, study staff will review the diary with the participant to try to determine whether a headache occurred that day and if said headache had migraine features. If no determination can be made, those days will be assumed to be migraine free.

Guided by our team's experience in prior trials utilizing longitudinal models (e.g., the Childhood and Adolescent Migraine Prevention multisite clinical trial), 30 we do not expect any clinically meaningful differences in primary outcome results if missing data were to occur. However, if nonnegligible missing data (e.g., missing not at random (MNAR)) were to be suspected (e.g., if missing headache day data were due to headache frequency or severity), we will immediately engage in a series of sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of any treatment effects to MNAR suspicions. 64 Otherwise, we will handle missing data in all two‐level random intercept analyses via fully conditional specification (HA:) two‐level multiple imputation. 55 The study, as designed, meets two of the four conditions for using multiple imputation; therefore, we would analyze primary outcome data addressing missing data using multiple imputation based on currently accepted best‐practice analysis procedures.

CONCLUSION

The REACH study is uniquely positioned to aid in the determination of optimal pediatric migraine care, while addressing well‐established barriers most families face in accessing this care. Implications of the potential findings of this study are profound, either confirming that a combined treatment approach is best or that monotherapy of behavioral intervention may be an appropriate first‐line treatment for pediatric migraine. Furthermore, by involving stakeholders in the study design and intervention development process, this intervention was designed with its intended audience in mind, bridging the gap between research and clinical care, and ensuring the findings are meaningful to those they aim to treat and support.
