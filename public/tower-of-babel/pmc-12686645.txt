Quality characteristics of white tea prepared from albino tea cultivars: A comprehensive study based on metabolomics and quantitative analyses
Authors: Zhaoqi Li, Jing Chen, Shiyu Zhang, Zhuolin Cheng, Tengxia He, Hongying Huang, Xinzhuan Yao, Long Jiang, Litang Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Albino tea cultivars are primarily used for green tea production, with limited application in other tea types. This study used two representative albino cultivars, Huangjinya (HJY) and Baiye 1 (BY1), to prepare white tea and compare them with the normal cultivar, Fudingdabai (FDDB). BY1 exhibited superior quality owing to its high amino acid content (17.13 ± 0.32 mg/g), low catechin (50.58 ± 1.41 mg/g) and caffeine (30.09 ± 0.41 mg/g) levels, and a pleasant aroma. Pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of differential metabolites in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. HJY contained abundant flavonoids, suggesting enhanced bioactivity and potential health benefits. Among the twelve key aromatic volatiles, geraniol and benzeneacetaldehyde were unique to BY1, while theaspirane was specific to HJY. This work demonstrates the influence of albino cultivars on white tea quality, providing the first evidence of their suitability for processing high-quality white tea. It advances current knowledge, offering a theoretical basis for their broader utilisation.

Introduction

Tea germplasm originated in southwest China approximately 60 to 70 million years ago (Pan et al., 2022). During growth and development, tea plants (Camellia sinensis) are highly susceptible to climatic and ecological factors, leading to extensive genetic diversity. During this process, unique mutations result in albino cultivars that exhibit pale yellow or off-white shoots due to chlorophyll deficiency and abnormal chloroplast development under specific environmental conditions, such as low temperatures or intense sunlight. These cultivars are broadly classified into two types: light-sensitive and temperature-sensitive, based on the underlying mechanisms of albinism (Cheng et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). Albino cultivars have attracted growing attention not only for their rarity and distinctive phenotypes but also for their superior quality. Compared with normal green tea cultivars, albino ones typically contain enhanced levels of free amino acids, primarily attributed to weak catabolism rather than activation of L-theanine biosynthesis (Cheng et al., 2019). This trait imparts tea prepared from albino cultivars with improved sensory qualities, notably a pronounced umami flavour, while simultaneously counteracting astringency and bitterness in tea infusion (Feng et al., 2014).

White tea, a traditional Chinese tea, has gained increasing popularity owing to its delicate flavour and notable health benefits. Recent studies have highlighted its diverse bioactivities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, anti-diabetic, anti-obesity, and neuroprotective effects (Sanlier et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2023). Unlike other types of tea, white tea undergoes minimal processing, involving only two primary steps: withering and drying (Dai et al., 2017). A slight fermentation occurs during the prolonged withering stage, playing a crucial role in the development of white tea quality. Enzymes such as polyphenol oxidase and peroxidase become more active, facilitating chemical reactions including polyphenol oxidation and glycoside hydrolysis. These transformations result in partial browning of the leaf surface and contribute to the characteristic appearance of white tea (Zhou et al., 2023). Concurrently, levels of free amino acids and soluble sugars increase markedly, alongside polyphenol conversion and the formation of numerous volatile aroma compounds (Zhou et al., 2023). Moreover, white tea contains more abundant flavonoids with various physiological functions, compared to the other types of tea (Dai et al., 2017). These compounds closely shape the mellow, umami, and sweet taste of white tea and greatly contribute to its health-promoting properties.

The quality of processed tea is largely influenced by the cultivars used. It has been demonstrated that different tea cultivars can develop distinctive flavour profiles even under identical processing conditions (Feng et al., 2024). Currently, albino cultivars are predominantly employed for high-grade green tea production, characterised by a strong umami taste and fresh aroma (Yang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020). However, their application in other types of tea remains limited, suggesting an underutilisation of albino tea resources. White tea production is an excellent and favourable option, given the high amino acid content of albino cultivars, making them a material with great potential. To date, the quality characteristics of white tea prepared from albino cultivars have not been fully elucidated. Therefore, a systematic investigation in this area is of significant importance.

This study used two representative albino cultivars, the light-sensitive cultivar Huangjinya (HJY) and the temperature-sensitive cultivar Baiye 1 (BY1), to prepare white tea and compare them with the normal green cultivar, Fudingdabai (FDDB). The effects of cultivars on the quality characteristics of white tea were comprehensively analysed through sensory evaluation, quantification of main quality compounds, and profiling of volatile and non-volatile metabolites. Our findings provide deeper insights into the quality characteristics, essential metabolites, and unique aromatic profile of white tea produced from representative albino cultivars, establishing a theoretical basis for the further exploration and utilisation of albino tea resources in the future.

Materials and methods

White tea sample preparation

Fresh tea leaves of Huangjinya (HJY), Baiye 1 (BY1), and Fudingdabai (FDDB) were harvested in Weng'an County, Qiannan Buyi and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, Guizhou Province, China. The above-mentioned tea cultivars were cultivated in the same tea plantation and followed the same white tea processing techniques, mainly including withering and drying. All the tea materials used in this study have been authenticated by a panel of senior tea industry experts to ensure the reliability of the experiment. All these white tea samples were ground into homogeneous powder for subsequent experimental analyses.

Chemicals and reagents

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade chemical standard reagents, including L-Theanine (Thea), Caffeine, (−)-Gallocatechin (GC), (−)-Epigallocatechin (EGC), (+)-Catechin (C), (−)-Epicatechin (EC), (−)-Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), (−)-Gallocatechin gallate (GCG), (−)-Epicatechin gallate (ECG) and (−)-Catechin gallate (CG), were obtained from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Amino acids mixture standard solution (type H) containing L-Aspartic acid (Asp), L-Threonine (Thr), l-Serine (Ser), L-Glutamic acid (Glu), Glycine (Gly), L-Alanine (Ala), L-Cysteine (Cys), L-Valine (Val), L-Isoleucine (Ile), L-Leucine (Leu), L-Methionine (Met), L-Tyrosine (Tyr), L-Phenylalanine (Phe), L-Histidine (His), l-Lysine (Lys), L-Arginine (Arg), L-Proline (Pro), were obtained from Wako (Shanghai, China). Ethyl decanoate, serving as the internal standard (IS) for HS-SPME-GC/MS detection, was obtained from TCI (Shanghai, China).

Sensory evaluation and quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA)

Sensory evaluation of white tea was conducted by ten well-trained panellists according to the Methodology for Sensory Evaluation of Tea (GB/T 23776–2018). The sensory panel research was approved by the Guizhou University Research Ethics Committee. Participation was entirely voluntary, with no coercion, and the rights, privacy, and health of all participants were well protected. Briefly, 3.0 g of dry tea was added to a specialised evaluation cup, brewed with 150 mL of boiling water for 5 min, followed by filtration. Quantitative Descriptive Analysis (QDA) was then employed to characterise the sensory properties of white tea. Five taste attributes were evaluated: sweet, umami, mellow, astringent, and bitter. Four aroma attributes were assessed: floral, fruity, sweet, and fresh. A 5-point intensity scale was used to score both taste and aroma, where 1 indicated “very weak”, 2 “weak”, 3 “moderate”, 4 “strong”, and 5 “extremely strong”. Each white tea sample was evaluated three times by each panellist, and the mean values were calculated for further analysis.

Main quality compound quantification

The contents of L-Theanine, caffeine and catechins (GC, EGC, C, EC, EGCG, GCG, ECG and CG) were determined via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) following the National Standards (GB/T 8313–2018) and (GB/T 23193–2017). An automatic amino acid analyzer (S-433D, Sykam, Germany) coupled with a LCA K07/Li cation separation column (4.6 × 150 mm, 3 μm, Sykam, Germany) using ion-exchange chromatography (IEC) was employed to measure the free amino acid contents in the white tea samples. The injection volume was 50 μL, the separation column was set at 37 °C, and the reactor was set at 130 °C. Channel 1 was set to a detection wavelength of 440 nm for L-Proline detection, while Channel 2 was set to a detection wavelength of 570 nm for monitoring the other free amino acids. The infusion pump maintained an operating pressure range of 3–4.2 MPa; the elution pump flow rate was 0.45 mL/min, and the ninhydrin derivative pump flow rate was 0.25 mL/min. Mobile phase: (A) citric acid‑lithium citrate buffer (pH of 2.90), (B) citric acid‑lithium citrate buffer (pH of 4.20), (C) citric acid‑lithium citrate buffer (pH of 8.00), (D) 0.5 mol/L lithium hydroxide regeneration solution.

Non-volatile metabolite analysis based on UPLC-MS/MS

Nexera UHPLC LC-30 A (Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) coupled with a TripleTOF 6600+ mass spectrometer (SCIEX, Foster City, CA, USA) was employed for the analysis of non-volatile metabolites. The following procedures were described in accordance with the minimum reporting standards for untargeted metabolomics, as recently proposed by (Alseekh et al., 2021).

Sample preparation and extraction

White tea samples were ground into powder using a grinder. Subsequently, 50 mg of the powdered sample was weighed and 1200 μL of pre-cooled 70 % methanolic aqueous (−20 °C) was added. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s every 30 min, for a total of 6 cycles. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, and transferred into an injection vial for UPLC-MS/MS analysis.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis

Metabolite separation was performed using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) maintained at 40 °C. The flow rate was set to 0.40 mL/min, and the injection volume was 4 μL. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 % formic acid in water (A) and 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile (B). The gradient programme was as follows: 95 % A and 5 % B at the start, linearly changing to 35 % A and 65 % B within 5 min, then to 1 % A and 99 % B within 1 min and held for 1.5 min, before returning to 95 % A and 5 % B within 0.1 min and held for 2.4 min.

Data acquisition was performed in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode using Analyst TF 1.7.1 software (Sciex, Concord, ON, Canada). Source parameters were: ion source gas 1 (GAS1), 50 psi; ion source gas 2 (GAS2), 60 psi; curtain gas (CUR), 35 psi; temperature (TEM), 550 °C; declustering potential (DP), ±80 V in positive and negative ion modes; and ion spray voltage floating (ISVF), +5500 V and − 4500 V in positive and negative ion modes.

TOF MS scan parameters were: mass range, 50–1250 Da; accumulation time, 200 ms; and dynamic background subtraction enabled. Product ion scan parameters were: mass range, 50–1250 Da; accumulation time, 40 ms; collision energy, ±30 V in positive and negative ion modes; collision energy spread, 15 V; resolution, UNIT; charge state, 1; intensity threshold, 100 cps; isotope exclusion within 4 Da; mass tolerance, 50 mDa; and a maximum of 12 candidate ions monitored per cycle.

Metabolic data processing

The raw mass spectrometry data were converted to the mzXML format using ProteoWizard software. Peak extraction, alignment, and retention time correction were performed with the XCMS programme. Peak areas were corrected using the support vector regression (SVR) method. Peaks with missing rates greater than 50 % within each sample group were removed. After correction and filtering, data were annotated using a combination of the laboratory's in-house database, integrated public databases, and metDNA. Data from the positive and negative ionisation modes were then merged to generate the final metabolite dataset for analysis.

Volatile metabolite analysis based on HS-SPME-GC/MS

Aroma compounds in white tea samples were extracted and analysed using headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC/MS), following the previously established method (Chen et al., 2025). Briefly, 0.8 g of dry tea powder was placed into a 20 mL headspace extraction vial containing 1 μL of Ethyl decanoate (100 mg/L, internal standard). After adding 8 mL of boiling water, the vial was immediately sealed and placed in a water bath at 80 °C for 50 min, with the extraction fibre exposed to the sample headspace. The SPME fibre was then inserted into the inlet of the gas chromatography-mass spectrometer (Agilent 7000D GC/TQ, Agilent 7693 A Autosampler) and desorbed at 260 °C for 5 min, followed by GC–MS analysis.

The Agilent MassHunter Qualitative Analysis 10.0 platform was used for data processing. Identification of volatile compounds was performed by comparing their mass spectra with the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) database. The concentrations of aroma compounds were calculated based on the peak area and concentration of the internal standard (IS) Ethyl decanoate, following the calculation formula (1):(1)Ci=AiAIS×CIS

Note: Ci is the concentration of the aroma compound (μg/L), Ai is the peak area of the aroma compound, AIS is the peak area of Ethyl decanoate, and CIS is the concentration of Ethyl decanoate (μg/L).

The Odour activity values (OAVs) of volatiles were calculated as the ratio of the concentration of each aroma compound to its respective odour threshold (OT), following the calculation formula (2):(2)OAV=CiOTi

Note: Ci is the concentration of the aroma compound (μg/L), and OTi is the odour threshold of the aroma compound (μg/L). Threshold values were obtained from relevant references. Compounds with OAV > 1 are considered major contributors to the aroma characteristics.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data were tested for normality and homoscedasticity using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Brown-Forsythe test, respectively, in GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2, GraphPad Software, USA). Inter-group differences were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's post-hoc test via SPSS Statistics (version 27.0, IBM, USA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using the Metware Cloud platform (https://cloud.metware.cn). The reliability of the OPLS-DA model was validated by a permutation test with 200 iterations. Differential metabolites among white tea samples were identified based on the variable importance in projection (VIP > 1) and p-values (p < 0.05). For data visualisation, bar charts were generated in GraphPad Prism, and the remaining charts were created on the Metware Cloud platform.

Results and discussion

Effects of cultivars on the sensory characteristics of white tea

Three white tea samples, FDDB, HJY and BY1, were prepared following the same white tea processing, mainly including withering and drying. To investigate the effects of cultivars on the sensory characteristics of white tea, the aroma and taste of the three samples were evaluated by well-trained panellists using quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA). Sensory attributes were scored on a five-point scale, where 1 indicated very weak intensity and 5 indicated extremely strong intensity. The results from the present study demonstrate that the sensory quality of white tea samples varied across cultivars. As shown in Fig. 1A, in terms of taste, two albino cultivars, BY1 and HJY, exhibited higher umami and mellow intensities than the normal green cultivar, FDDB. Sweetness scores showed minimal variation among the three samples, although BY1 demonstrated a slightly higher score. Regarding astringency and bitterness, HJY exhibited the highest intensity, whereas BY1 showed the lowest. In terms of aroma (Fig. 1B), more pronounced floral and fruity notes characterised both BY1 and FDDB, while a relatively stronger fresh aroma distinguished HJY. Notably, BY1 exhibited the highest intensity of sweet fragrance among the three samples. These results suggested that white tea prepared from BY1 possessed a superior sensory quality in both taste and aroma compared to the other two cultivars.Fig. 1Sensory evaluation and quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA) of white tea samples.(A) Radar plot of taste evaluation. Effects of cultivars on the main quality compounds of white tea

Free amino acids, caffeine, and catechins are crucial compounds that strongly contribute to tea flavour (Jiang et al., 2024; Jiao et al., 2024). To explore the effects of cultivars on the main quality compounds of white tea, several representative compounds were quantified and analysed (Fig. 2A and Table S1). Caffeine, the predominant alkaloid accounting for over 90 % of total alkaloids in tea, is primarily responsible for a bitter taste (Ye et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 2C, caffeine content was least in BY1 (30.09 ± 0.41 mg/g), followed by FDDB (32.22 ± 0.41 mg/g) and HJY (35.28 ± 0.45 mg/g). Tea polyphenols, particularly catechins, are recognised as principal compounds that impart astringency and bitterness to tea infusions (Tian et al., 2024). Regarding catechins, the white tea samples also followed the trend of caffeine. The total catechin content was least abundant in BY1 (50.58 ± 1.41 mg/g), which was significantly lower than that in FDDB (54.74 ± 1.97 mg/g) and HJY (113.73 ± 1.85 mg/g) (p < 0.05), as shown in Fig. 2D. GC, EGC, EC, EGCG, and ECG were the major catechins identified in this study, accounting for over 90 % of the total catechin content. Among them, EGCG was the most abundant catechin, with a notably higher concentration in HJY (52.54 ± 0.52 mg/g), followed by BY1 (21.33 ± 0.43 mg/g) and FDDB (19.60 ± 0.54 mg/g). Its de-galloylated form, EGC, also exhibited greater accumulation in the two albino tea cultivars. After the de-galloylation of EGCG and ECG, the bitterness and astringency of tea infusion were reduced, while the sweet aftertaste was enhanced. EGC and EC are associated with a sweet aftertaste perception (Zhang et al., 2016). Among the three samples, HJY exhibited the highest concentrations of both EGC and EC, which contributed to a sweet aftertaste amid the bitterness and astringency, enhancing a mellow sensation of the tea infusion.

Amino acids are primary contributors to a fresh and refreshing taste of tea, which can help to weaken the bitterness and astringency of tea infusion (Chen et al., 2025). Previous studies have reported that albino tea cultivars generally contain higher levels of amino acids than typical green cultivars (Yang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020), which was confirmed in the present study. The total amino acid contents were considerably more abundant in both albino tea cultivars compared to FDDB (Fig. 2E), with the highest level observed in HJY (24.01 ± 0.67 mg/g), followed by BY1 (17.13 ± 0.32 mg/g) and FDDB (11.13 ± 0.47 mg/g). Specifically, the amino acids detected can be classified based on flavour properties into four categories: umami (Thea, Asp and Glu), sweetness (Thr, Ser, Gly, Ala and Pro), bitterness (Val, Ile, Leu, Tyr, Phe, Lys, His and Arg) and others (Mao et al., 2018; Zou et al., 2022). Umami amino acids accounted for the largest proportion among all the detected amino acids, with total contents significantly higher in both albino cultivars, especially in HJY relative to FDDB (p < 0.05). Notably, L-theanine is a specialised metabolite in tea plants, serving as the principal umami enhancer that effectively balances bitterness and astringency and improves the smoothness of tea infusion (Chen, Han, & Tong, 2024). Consistent with previous studies (Cheng et al., 2019; Feng et al., 2014), L-theanine was the most abundant amino acid among the detected compounds and was enriched in both albino tea cultivars compared to the normal green tea cultivar (Fig. 2F), with the highest level found in HJY (10.83 ± 0.28 mg/g), followed by BY1 (9.08 ± 0.26 mg/g) and FDDB (4.25 ± 0.34 mg/g).

In conclusion, white tea prepared from BY1 exhibited a more harmonious taste profile, distinguished by a high abundance of amino acids and the lowest levels of catechins and caffeine. The interaction among the high contents of amino acids, catechins, and caffeine in HJY contributed to a balanced tea infusion, which may help refine the mouthfeel and enhance its mellow taste. In contrast, FDDB displayed a relatively moderate taste, attributed to its lowest amino acid content. The sensory evaluation results further confirmed these findings, demonstrating that the chemical composition strongly influences the flavour quality of white tea.

The differences observed among the three white tea samples reflect the crucial role of cultivar in determining tea quality. The final quality of processed tea is largely influenced by the cultivars used. In this study, BY1 contained high levels of amino acids and L-theanine, and this distinctive chemical profile is intrinsically associated with the unique albinism mechanism of the albino cultivar, which leads to chlorophyll deficiency and consequently to a substantial accumulation of amino acids in the shoots. This genetically determined feature is largely independent of post-harvest processing and provides the fundamental biochemical basis for the superior taste of BY1. As all samples were processed under identical and standardised white tea processing conditions, the observed differences in chemical composition and flavour are therefore primarily attributed to cultivar-specific metabolic characteristics rather than differences in processing compatibility.

After peak extraction, alignment and filtration, a total of 927 metabolites were retained for analysis in this study (Table S2). An orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) based on these identified metabolites was conducted, where PC1 and PC2 accounted for 40.4 % and 27.5 % of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 3B). The reliability of this model was subsequently verified by cross-validation with 200 permutation tests (R2Y = 0.998, Q2 = 0.953), as shown in Fig. 3C. According to the principle of p-value (< 0.05) and VIP value (> 1), a total of 307 differential metabolites were screened, comprising 83 amino acids and derivatives, 14 organic acids, 45 lipids, 51 flavonoids, 33 alkaloids, 19 terpenoids, 16 phenolic acids, 23 nucleotides and derivatives, 5 lignans and coumarins, 5 tannins and 13 others (Fig. 3D). Among them, amino acids and flavonoids were the two most abundant classes, suggesting that they are the main quality-related differential metabolites between albino and green tea cultivars, which is consistent with previous findings (Chen, He, et al., 2024).

To obtain deeper insights into the key non-volatile compounds among the three white tea samples, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was further conducted. KEGG functional annotation revealed that the selected differential metabolites were mainly enriched in 20 metabolic pathways (Fig. 3E), including flavonoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, isoflavonoid biosynthesis, glucosinolate biosynthesis, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and others. The results demonstrated that the flavonoid, phenylpropanoid, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis pathways were significantly enriched, as indicated by rich factors and p-values (rich factor > 0.9, p < 0.05). Accordingly, great emphasis was placed on the differential metabolites involved in these three key metabolic pathways in the subsequent analysis.

Key non-volatile compounds involved in the crucial metabolic pathways

White tea is renowned for its outstanding health benefits, primarily attributed to the presence of diverse natural phytochemicals within it (Zhou et al., 2023). The pivotal roles of secondary metabolites in the medicinal properties of many plants have been highlighted in numerous studies (Demirtas et al., 2013; Elmastas et al., 2004; Erenler et al., 2015; Topçu et al., 1999). Flavonoids, a class of polyphenolic compounds exhibiting a wide range of physiological functions and pharmacological activities, including antioxidation, anti-inflammatory effects, anti-tumour properties, and cardiovascular protection (He et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2023), have been proven to be more abundant in white tea than in other types of tea (Dai et al., 2017). A large proportion of flavonoids is synthesised via various branches of the phenylpropanoid biosynthetic pathway, one of the most characterised secondary metabolic routes in plants (Xiong et al., 2013).

The key non-volatile compounds involved in the three crucial metabolic pathways mainly comprised 2 amino acids, 4 phenolic acids and 31 flavonoids. As illustrated in Fig. 4, L-phenylalanine and L-tyrosine were found at higher levels in BY1. Phenolic acids are not only important antioxidant components in tea, but also serve as primary substances for the synthesis of flavonoid compounds (Tan et al., 2016). Cinnamic acid and chlorogenic acid were enriched in HJY, while p-coumaric acid and caffeic acid were more abundant in BY1.Fig. 4Key non-volatile compounds involved in the crucial metabolic pathways.Fig. 4

Overall, compared to the normal green tea cultivar, FDDB, most flavonoids showed enrichment in white tea produced from the albino tea cultivars, particularly in HJY (Fig. 4), which may be due to the lack of chlorophyll in albino leaves, making them more vulnerable to light stress thereby resulting in increased flavonoid accumulation in tea leaves (Zhao et al., 2023). Eriodictyol chalcone and naringenin chalcone were highly accumulated in BY1. Naringenin, a 2S-flavanone that serves as a crucial precursor for the synthesis of various flavonoids via branched metabolic pathways under the action of different enzymes (Zou et al., 2016), also exhibited a higher level in BY1. As for other flavanones, naringin and neohesperidin were both enriched in BY1, whereas sakuranetin was enriched in HJY. Apigenin and chrysin, the two flavones involved in the flavonoid biosynthesis metabolic pathway, were found in higher amounts in FDDB and BY1, respectively. Among the four isoflavones detected, higher accumulation of formononetin 7-O-glucoside, glycitein 7-O-glucoside and genistein 7-O-glucoside was observed in HJY, and a higher level of 2′-hydroxygenistein was presented in FDDB. In terms of flavanonols, dihydrokaempferol and dihydroquercetin were both abundantly accumulated in HJY. Myricetin, quercetin, and kaempferol are major flavonols in tea occurring primarily as O-glycosides (Fang et al., 2019). In this study, kaempferol and quercetin were enriched in BY1, while myricetin was abundant in HJY, and their glycoside derivatives (kaempferol 7-O-rhamnoside, kaempferol 7-O-glucoside, kaempferol 3-O-arabinoside, and quercetin 3-O-α-arabinoside) exhibited higher levels in HJY. The natural anthocyanidin, leucodelphinidin, was detected at a higher level in HJY. Additionally, abundant flavanols, including 8 monomeric catechins and (−)-epiafzelechin, were also detected in HJY.

In summary, these findings suggest that flavonoids may serve as key factors distinguishing white teas prepared from different cultivars. Among them, HJY tend to accumulate higher levels of flavonoids. Consequently, it was speculated that white teas prepared from albino cultivars, particularly HJY, may exhibit more potent biological activity and greater health benefits than those from the normal green cultivar, FDDB.

Effects of cultivars on the volatile metabolite profiles of white tea

White tea is produced through the simplest processing method under the mildest conditions, involving essentially two steps: withering and drying. Thus, the natural state of fresh leaves is largely preserved, yielding a unique and delicate aroma. To investigate the effects of cultivars on the volatile metabolite profiles of white tea, volatiles in three samples were analysed using headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) coupled with GC–MS.

As listed in Table S3, a total of 21 distinct aroma compounds were detected among the three white tea samples after peak data processing. These compounds were mainly categorized into five classes: alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, esters, and heterocyclic compounds (Fig. 5A). Consistent with previous findings (Huang et al., 2024), alcohols, along with aldehydes and ketones, served as the predominant volatile compounds in the white tea samples, collectively accounting for over 80 % of the aroma compounds identified in this study. Specifically, FDDB and BY1 exhibited a more diverse aroma profile, with 17 aroma compounds identified in each cultivar. In contrast, HJY displayed a relatively simpler aroma composition, containing only 11 detectable aroma compounds (Fig. 5B). Geraniol with a rose-like and citrus-like fragrance, alongside benzeneacetaldehyde with a sweet and honey-like aromatic quality (Huang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024), were uniquely identified in BY1.

Key aroma-active compounds contributing to aroma characteristics

The sensory impact of volatile compounds on tea aroma is determined not only by their absolute concentrations, but also by their respective odour thresholds (OTs). The Odour Activity Value (OAV) provided a comprehensive assessment of each aroma compound by considering both its concentration and its odour threshold. Aroma compounds with OAV > 1 are generally regarded as aroma-active compounds and major contributors to the aroma characteristics (Liang et al., 2025).

OAV values for the 16 differential aroma compounds mentioned above were calculated, and the results were listed in Table S4. A total of 12 compounds were further screened and identified as the key aroma-active compounds in this study, namely linalool, benzaldehyde, α-ionone, β-ionone, (E)-geranylacetone, 2-phenylethanol, geraniol, benzeneacetaldehyde, methyl salicylate, (E)-2-hexenal, dihydroactindiolide, and theaspirane. These key aroma-active compounds significantly contributed to the aroma characteristics of white tea (OAV > 1, VIP > 1, and p < 0.05), and their concentrations were visualised separately in bar charts (Fig. 6B).

Previous studies have indicated that white tea is typically characterised by fresh, floral, fruity, and sweet aromas (Feng et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2024), which was also confirmed by the present study. Most representative aroma compounds, associated with prominent fruity and floral scents (α-ionone, β-ionone, benzaldehyde and linalool), as well as those contributing to fresh aromas (methyl salicylate and (E)-2-hexenal) (Chen, He, et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024), were detected in all three white tea samples. These aroma compounds played a crucial role in the formation of the fresh, floral, and fruity aroma characteristics of white tea, with a similar distribution trend, with markedly higher concentrations and OAVs observed in BY1, followed by FDDB and HJY.

(E)-geranylacetone (floral, fresh, green, fruity), 2-phenylethanol (floral, honey-like) and dihydroactindiolide (fresh, herbal) were the three shared aroma compounds detected in both BY1 and FDDB, and they were also found at higher levels in BY1, further enriching its pleasant aroma. Notably, several key aroma-active compounds were unique to the albino cultivars. Geraniol, featuring a pleasant rose-like and citrus-like note (Lu et al., 2024), was particularly present in BY1. Benzeneacetaldehyde, associated with a sweet and honey-like aroma attribute (Huang et al., 2024), was also uniquely found in BY1, serving as the main compound responsible for its sweet fragrance enhancement. The specific presence of theaspirane in HJY, with a cooling, minty, herbal, and woody scent (Chen, He, et al., 2024), imparted a distinctive fresh aroma.

Overall, white tea prepared from BY1 exhibited higher concentrations and OAVs of aroma-active compounds, along with greater aroma diversity compared to FDDB and HJY. These findings suggested that BY1 possessed a more desirable aroma profile, characterised by a richer, more intense fragrance, which aligned with the sensory evaluation results.

Conclusion

Albino tea cultivars are primarily used for green tea production, with limited application in other tea types. To date, the potential of albino cultivars for white tea production remains unexplored. The characteristics of albino white tea have not been reported, and there is also a lack of standardised and comprehensive comparative research in this area. The present study systematically investigated the quality characteristics and metabolite profiles of white tea prepared from two representative albino cultivars (HJY and BY1) and a normal green cultivar (FDDB). All samples were processed under identical processing conditions to ensure a fair comparison among different cultivars. The results showed that BY1 exhibited superior taste and aroma profiles, characterised by high amino acid content, low levels of catechins and caffeine, and a pleasant aroma with sweet, floral, fruity, and fresh notes. Pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of differential metabolites in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway. HJY contained abundant flavonoids, suggesting enhanced bioactivity and potential health benefits. Moreover, twelve aroma-active volatiles were identified as major contributors to the aroma attributes of the white tea samples. Floral notes were associated with linalool, α-ionone, β-ionone, (E)-geranylacetone, 2-phenylethanol, and geraniol; fruity and sweet aromas were related to benzaldehyde and benzeneacetaldehyde, respectively; fresh scents were linked to methyl salicylate, (E)-2-hexenal, dihydroactindiolide, and theaspirane. Notably, geraniol and benzeneacetaldehyde were unique to BY1, while theaspirane was specific to HJY.

Overall, this work comprehensively revealed the strong influence of albino cultivars on white tea quality, providing the first evidence that albino cultivars are not only suitable for green tea production but also serve as ideal materials for processing high-quality white tea. It advances current knowledge on albino tea cultivars beyond prior studies, offering a theoretical basis for their broader and novel utilisation. Given the potential compatibility between cultivars and processing conditions, future research will focus on optimising cultivar-specific processing approaches to develop innovative tea products with improved quality and distinctive characteristics.
