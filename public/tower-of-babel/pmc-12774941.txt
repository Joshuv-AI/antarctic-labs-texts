Osteosarcopenia, bone-muscle interactions, and frailty risk: A prospective cohort study of community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Mizhgan Fatima, Ben Kirk, Sara Vogrin, Jason Talevski, Sharon Brennan-Olsen, Gustavo Duque
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteosarcopenia, defined as the co-existence of osteopenia/osteoporosis and sarcopenia, may influence frailty risk in older adults. However, the longitudinal association between osteosarcopenia or its components and frailty remain unclear. This study aimed to address this.MethodsData from a prospective cohort study of community-dwelling adults in Australia. Frailty was defined by the presence of ≥ 3 components based on Fried criteria: exhaustion, slow gait speed, low grip strength, unintentional weight loss, and low physical activity. Osteosarcopenia was defined by osteopenia/osteoporosis (WHO criteria) and sarcopenia (European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People [EWGSOP2] and Sarcopenia Definition and Outcome consortium [SDOC]). Multivariable logistic regression models evaluated the associations between osteosarcopenia, its components and frailty.ResultsOf 300 enrolled, 151 (mean age: 65.1years, 59.6%women) completed follow-up (median: 4.8 years). Among 143 non-frails at baseline, 13 (9.1%) transitioned to frailty. Osteosarcopenia (versus non-osteosarcopenia) was associated with frailty irrespective of the definition used: EWGSOP2: OR = 9.53, 95%CI 2.53–35.92; SDOC: OR = 9.19, 95%CI 2.19–38.56). Grip strength reduction by 1 kg (OR = 0.92, 95%CI 0.84–1.00) and gait speed reduction by 0.1 m/sec (OR = 0.67, 95%CI 0.53–0.85) were associated with 8% and 33% lower odds of frailty, respectively, whereas bone mineral density or lean mass was not. An interaction between bone-muscle function and frailty risk was observed (p < 0.011), whereby higher values of grip strength were associated with lower odds of frailty when bone density was lower.ConclusionIn this prospective cohort study, osteosarcopenia increased the risk of frailty. Our interaction analysis suggests therapies targeting bone density and grip strength may mitigate frailty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03242-x.Keywords: Aging, Osteosarcopenia, Sarcopenia, Osteoporosis, Frailty

Introduction

Frailty is an age-related syndrome characterized by diminished physiological reserve and resilience, increasing susceptibility to stressors, and the risk of hospitalization, disability, and mortality [1]. It results from senescence-related cellular and subcellular changes that disrupt key physiological systems, including the musculoskeletal system, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, and metabolic function. These disruptions give rise to the Fried frailty phenotype, defined when three or more of the following five criteria are present: unintentional weight loss, exhaustion, slow gait speed, reduced grip strength, and low physical activity [2]. As the global population ages, frailty is becoming increasingly prevalent, with significant implications for individual health and healthcare systems [3].

Osteosarcopenia is a recently defined clinical entity that describes the co-occurrence of osteopenia or osteoporosis (low bone mineral density) and sarcopenia (low muscle mass and strength) [4]. Each of these conditions individually contributes to an increased risk of falls, fractures, and physical functional decline in older adults; however, their combined impact is synergistic and accelerates musculoskeletal deterioration in older people [5]. The pathophysiological basis of osteosarcopenia stems from impaired bone-muscle crosstalk and shared mechanisms such as chronic inflammation, hormonal dysregulation, and environmental factors involving both bone and muscle [6]. Through its compounded impact on bone and muscle, osteosarcopenia may contribute to the development of frailty.

Several cross-sectional studies have reported an association between osteosarcopenia and frailty, suggesting a potential biological and clinical link [7–14]. However, the inherent limitations of cross-sectional designs preclude conclusions about causality or temporal sequence. It remains uncertain whether osteosarcopenia precedes frailty or if both arise concurrently in response to common age-related mechanisms. To date, longitudinal data exploring osteosarcopenia as a potential predictor of frailty are limited.

To address this gap, the present study aimed to: determine whether osteosarcopenia at baseline predicts the onset of frailty over time; [b] investigate whether the individual components of osteosarcopenia—specifically, low bone mass and reduced muscle strength—independently predict frailty; and [c] explore whether bone-muscle interaction contributes to frailty development. We hypothesize that osteosarcopenia, due to its combined impact on bone and muscle, is associated with future frailty risk in older adults.

Materials and methods

Study design

The Brimbank Ageing Well Study (BRAW) was a prospective cohort study that investigated the association between baseline osteosarcopenia and future frailty risk among community-dwelling older adults aged ≥ 50 years in the Western suburbs of Melbourne, Australia. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling and assessed at two time points: baseline (2018–2020) and follow-up (2023–2024). Inclusion criteria were aged ≥ 50 years, living in the Western suburbs of Melbourne, and the ability to attend the study site for assessments. Exclusion criteria included inability to provide informed consent; severe cognitive impairment (dementia or dementia with Lewy body); medical conditions affecting balance or physical performance (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, progressive neurological disorders, multiple sclerosis, uncontrolled psychiatric disorders); severe arthritis, chronic lung disease requiring oxygen or renal disease requiring dialysis; history of stroke or recent major surgeries (e.g., knee replacement, spinal, or cardiac surgery) within the past 6 months; life expectancy < 12 months; or body weight > 150 kg (a contraindication for DEXA scanning). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee at Melbourne Health (HREC/45024/MH-2020).

Study assessments

Participants attended an assessment session at Sunshine Hospital that lasted approximately 2 to 2.5 h. They were provided with a self-administered questionnaire capturing sociodemographic information (age, sex, country of birth, living arrangements, income, education, quality of life scores), lifestyle factors (smoking and alcohol consumption history), and physical activity levels using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [15]. Interpreters were available for non-English speaking participants.

Subsequently, study personnel collected clinical data, including comorbidities (using the Charlson Comorbidity Index), medication use, Mini Nutritional Assessment, and fracture history [16, 17]. Physical assessments included anthropometric measurements (height, weight, body mass index), handgrip strength (using a handheld dynamometer), sit-to-stand test, gait speed, Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB). A whole-body Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DEXA) scan was performed to assess Bone Mineral Density (BMD), body composition, and Appendicular Lean Mass adjusted for height (ALM/height2). Finally, participants underwent blood tests to assess serum vitamin D and C-Reactive Protein (CRP) levels. Blood tests were conducted only at baseline; all other assessments were conducted at both visits.

Osteosarcopenia

Osteosarcopenia was defined by the concomitant presence of osteopenia or osteoporosis and sarcopenia [5]. Osteopenia and osteoporosis were defined using the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria based on BMD measured via DEXA. Osteoporosis was defined as a BMD T-score of ≤ −2.5SD, while osteopenia was classified as a BMD T-score between − 1.0 and − 2.5SD [18]. The lowest recorded BMD at three sites, lumbar spine, femoral neck, and distal 1/3rd of the radius, was used to define osteopenia or osteoporosis. Sarcopenia was defined using two criteria: the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People, revised (EWGSOP2, 2019), and the Sarcopenia Definition and Outcomes Consortium (SDOC, 2020). The EWGSOP2 criteria were employed, as the majority of our study population was Australian-born Caucasian, and these criteria have recently been endorsed for use in Australia via the Delphi convention [19]. SDOC criteria are recommended in Caucasians. According to EWGSOP2, probable sarcopenia was identified by either a low handgrip strength (< 27 kg in men and < 16 kg in women) or a five-times sit-to-stand time of >15 s. Confirmed sarcopenia required the presence of probable sarcopenia along with DEXA evidence of low ALM/height2 (< 7.0 kg/m² in men and < 5.5 kg/m² in women). Gait speed of < 0.8 m/sec or SPPB < 8 with confirmed sarcopenia was considered severe sarcopenia [20]. As per SDOC, sarcopenia was defined based on a low handgrip strength (< 35.5 kg in men and < 20 kg in women) and slow gait speed (< 0.8 m/s). In our analysis we used SDOC definition where handgrip strength was adjusted for BMI [21].

Frailty

The validated criterion for frailty, the Fried criteria, was used, which classified individuals as frail if they met at least three out of five criteria, prefrail if they met two or one, and non-frail if they met none. The five criteria included:

1) Unintentional weight loss of >4.5 kg in the past year or a BMI < 18 kg/m², modified as in previous studies [22–24].

2) Self-reported exhaustion (assessed using two questions from the CES-D depression scale, if the participants provided a positive answer to any of the following two questions, i) I felt that everything I did was an effort, for ≥ 3 days in a week, ii) I could not get going, for ≥ 3 days in a week)

3) Low physical activity, defined based on the IPAQ score, was categorized as low physical activity, using the gender-specific lowest quintile for the study population.

4) Slowness (gait speed < 1.0 m/s).

5) Weakness (grip strength in the lowest 20% by gender and BMI) [25].

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on previous evidence and studies suggesting a potential influence on the exposure (osteosarcopenia) and outcome (frailty) variables [26]. These included demographic factors (age, sex, living status), socioeconomic factors (education level, income status), and lifestyle behaviors (alcohol consumption, smoking status). Annual income was classified as binary, categorizing those earning less than AUD 40,000 and those earning more, based on an Australian couple’s full age pension of approximately AUD 39,640 per year [27]. Clinical variables such as comorbidities, current medications, serum levels of vitamin D, C-reactive protein (CRP), and nutritional status were also considered.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was estimated based on the hypothesis that osteosarcopenia increases the risk of developing frailty over the study period (4 years). Based on our assumption of a threefold increased relative risk of frailty associated with osteosarcopenia and the 13.6% incidence of frailty over three years [28], the sample size was calculated to achieve 80% power with a 5% significance level. An estimate of 46 participants with osteosarcopenia at baseline was made. Taking the global prevalence of osteosarcopenia of approximately 20% (range between 5% and 46% in meta-analysis [29] and further accounting for a 25% loss to follow-up (higher loss of follow-up noted in previous cohorts [30]) and a 5% mortality rate, the final required sample size was estimated to be 300.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics of study participants. Categorical variables (frequencies and percentages) were compared using chi-square tests, and continuous variables (means ± SD or medians ± IQR for skewed data) using one-way ANOVA. Participants were classified into two groups (non-frail and frail), and frailty transition was reported as a percentage. At baseline, participants were categorized into four mutually exclusive groups: [1] no condition (neither osteopenia/osteoporosis nor sarcopenia) [2], sarcopenia only [3], osteopenia or osteoporosis only, and [4] osteosarcopenia.

Logistic regression was used to estimate the odds of developing frailty at follow-up, comparing each condition group to the no-condition group. Due to the limited number of frailty cases for multivariable analyses, participants were stratified into two groups: those with osteosarcopenia and those without. To minimize overfitting, each confounder was adjusted individually in separate models, adhering to the recommended guideline of at least 10 events per variable in logistic regression analyses [31]. Confounders were selected based on significant associations with frailty in univariate analyses and included demographic (age, sex), lifestyle (smoking, alcohol use, physical activity), clinical (polypharmacy, vitamin D, CRP), and socioeconomic (education, income) factors.

Muscle parameters (grip strength, gait speed, five-times sit-to-stand test, SPPB, ALM, ALM/height²) and bone measures (BMD and T-scores at various sites) were analyzed as continuous variables using logistic regression to assess associations with frailty at follow-up. Models were adjusted for sex only, physical activity only, and income only, based on significance in prior analyses.

An exploratory approach was used by employing interaction analysis models to assess whether muscle measures significantly interacted with bone measures in predicting frailty. If significant interactions were identified, indicating effect modification by value level, no further analysis was conducted. Otherwise, mutually adjusted models were used to assess the independent effects of bone and muscle measures. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, except for interactions where p < 0.06 was used, aligning with exploratory research practices that recognize the value of marginal trends [32]. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Participants characteristics

Out of 300 participants at baseline, 151 completed follow-ups. Specifically, two participants died, 59 were unreachable, 67 refused to participate without providing a reason, nine reported feeling unwell and could not attend, and 12 had moved out of the study area (West Melbourne) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Participants who did not attend the follow-up assessment were, on average, older, less likely to have completed post-secondary or tertiary education and had significantly lower annual household incomes compared to those who attended follow-up. Significant differences were also observed between the groups in hand grip strength, gait speed, and pre-frailty status (Supplementary Table 1).

Of those who completed follow-ups, 143 were non-frail and eight were frail at baseline assessment. Over the study follow-up, out of the non-frail, 13 (9.1%) became frail; of the frail, 4 (50%) improved to non-frail (Supplementary Fig. 2). The follow-up period ranged from 3.5 to 6.3 years (median 4.8 years, mean 4.7 years).

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of 151 participants categorized as robust, prefrail, or frail. Eight frail participants at baseline were excluded from follow-up analysis, leaving 143 non-frail individuals for the final analysis. The mean age of participants at first visit was 65.1 years (range 50.1 to 84 years), with 59.6% female. No age or sex differences were found between frailty groups. Most participants lived with others (78%), were Australian-born (66.9%), English-speaking (96%), and had completed secondary school education or higher (> 80.8%). Socioeconomic differences were noted across frailty groups when classified by an annual income > 40 K AUD (p = 0.002), private health insurance (p < 0.001), and health care card ownership (p = 0.002). Differences were also noted between groups in lifestyle factors; alcohol use (p = 0.026), physical activity (IPAQ, p < 0.001), and inflammatory markers (hs-CRP levels, p = 0.004). No differences were found for smoking, comorbidities, fractures, medications, polypharmacy, or vitamin D. While muscle function measures (grip strength, gait speed) differed between groups, muscle and bone mass (ALM, ALM/height², BMD, T-scores) did not. Supplementary Fig. 3 shows the overlap of osteopenia/osteoporosis and sarcopenia at baseline. Each condition (osteosarcopenia, sarcopenia and osteopenia/osteoporosis) was analyzed as a distinct group to assess its association with frailty at follow-up.

Association between osteosarcopenia and frailty

In our first analysis, comparing the association of future frailty risk among participants with osteopenia/osteoporosis alone, sarcopenia alone and osteosarcopenia (each categorized as mutually exclusive groups), to those without any of these conditions at baseline, we noted that osteosarcopenia was consistently associated with an increased risk of developing frailty, regardless of the definition used, EWGSOP2 criteria (OR = 8.13, 95% CI: 1.61–41.10, p = 0.011) and SDOC criteria (OR = 8.00, 95% CI: 1.42–45.06, p = 0.018) (Table 2). Sarcopenia alone at baseline showed a trend toward higher frailty odds (OR = 1.86, 95% CI: 0.17–20.51, p = 0.613), while interestingly osteopenia/osteoporosis alone showed a trend toward lower frailty odds (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.15–3.21, p = 0.63).

Given the limited number of outcomes (n = 13), we conducted additional analyses comparing two groups, osteosarcopenia vs. non-osteosarcopenia, sarcopenia vs. non-sarcopenia and osteopenia/osteoporosis vs. non-osteopenia/osteoporosis in relation to future frailty risk. The odds of frailty for osteosarcopenia (9.5) were higher than for sarcopenia (6.6), with both associations reaching statistical significance (Supplementary Table 2).

In a multivariable analysis comparing two groups, osteosarcopenia vs. non-osteosarcopenia, in relation to future frailty, models were individually adjusted for age, sex, polypharmacy, smoking, alcohol use, education, income, physical activity, CRP levels, presence of ≥ 2 comorbidities and pre-frailty (Table 3). Osteosarcopenia (EWGSOP2) remained a consistent predictor of frailty across all models. Among the covariates, comorbidities and prefrailty were associated with future frailty risk, and an income of ≥ AUD 40,000/year was protective (Table 3). Similar trends were observed using SDOC criteria (handgrip adjusted for BMI) (Table 3).

A 1 kg increase in handgrip strength was associated with an 8% reduction in frailty odds (OR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.84–1.00, p = 0.038). When adjusted for BMI, a 1-unit increase in handgrip strength corresponded to an 85% lower risk of frailty (OR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.03–0.90, p = 0.037). This association remained significant after adjusting for income but was attenuated after further adjustment for physical activity. For gait speed, each 0.1 m/s increase was linked to a 33% reduction in frailty odds (OR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.53–0.85, p = 0.001), with the association persisting across all models. Similarly, a 1-point improvement in SPPB score was associated with 42% lower odds of frailty (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.42–0.81, p = 0.001), consistent across adjusted models (Table 4).

Bone-Muscle interaction and frailty

Bone–muscle interaction analyses were conducted to assess whether the effects of muscle function (handgrip strength adjusted for sex, gait speed, sit-to-stand time) on frailty vary by bone density (Table 5). Given the exploratory approach, a higher p-value (< 0.06) was accepted to recognize marginal trends in exploratory analyses [32]. Handgrip strength (adjusted for sex) showed significant interactions with lumbar spine BMD (p = 0.061), lumbar spine T-scores (p = 0.051), and the lowest site T-score (p = 0.011), the latter being clinically relevant for defining osteopenia and osteoporosis (WHO criteria). These findings suggest that the protective effect of handgrip strength on frailty is more pronounced at lower T-scores (using T-score at the lowest site and lumbar spine) and BMD (at lumbar spine) (Fig. 1A and B). In contrast, gait speed showed no significant interaction with bone measures, indicating its association with frailty is independent of bone density .

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to assess whether bone and muscle measures independently predicted frailty. In models adjusted for both, gait speed remained significantly associated with frailty, independent of bone strength. Similarly, the sit-to-stand test retained a significant association after adjusting for bone parameters. In contrast, DEXA-derived muscle mass measures (ALM and ALM/height²), adjusted for sex and bone measurements (BMD and T-scores), were not significantly associated with frailty.

Discussion

Our longitudinal cohort study of community-dwelling older adults extends previous evidence linking osteoporosis and sarcopenia to frailty by demonstrating that osteosarcopenia is a strong determinant of frailty risk. Among individual components, handgrip strength and gait speed—representing muscle function and physical performance—were independent predictors of frailty, whereas BMD and ALM, representing structural measures, were not. Exploratory analyses further showed that higher handgrip strength attenuated frailty risk even more among individuals with low BMD, and vice versa, underscoring the importance of bone–muscle interplay. Although sex-specific analyses may have provided additional insights, these were not feasible due to the low number of frailty outcome cases. Together, these findings establish osteosarcopenia as a clinically relevant risk phenotype and highlight the potential for early detection and targeted interventions to mitigate frailty progression.

The temporal relationship between osteosarcopenia and frailty remains insufficiently understood due to limited longitudinal data and inconsistent results. In the ILAS (I-Lan Longitudinal Study on Aging) cohort from Taiwan, osteosarcopenia (WHO + AWGS 2019) was not associated with frailty (Fried criteria) over eight years [30]. By contrast, the Japanese ROAD (Research on Osteoporosis/osteoarthritis against Disability) study reported a strong association over four years (OR = 5.80, 95% CI: 1.38–24.4) using same criteria, while the Canadian CLSA (Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging) found no association (using SDOC and the Rockwood model) over three years [33, 34]. However, in the CLSA cohort, osteosarcopenia increased the risk of falls and functional decline [33]. Our findings are consistent with the ROAD cohort in Japan but differ from the CLSA and ILAS cohorts. These discrepancies are likely attributable to methodological heterogeneity, including differences in frailty definitions (Fried phenotype vs. Rockwood index), sarcopenia classifications (EWGSOP2, AWGS, or SDOC), cohort characteristics (lifestyle factors and socioeconomic status), geographic settings, and follow-up duration [35–37]. In addition, osteosarcopenia and frailty are complex, dynamic conditions and interact bidirectionally with multiple feedback loops, challenging the unidirectional assumptions of longitudinal cohort analyses. Nevertheless, our results add weight to the evidence that osteosarcopenia predicts adverse trajectories of ageing, including frailty and functional decline [33, 34].

A noteworthy limitation in analyzing the association between osteosarcopenia and frailty is the definitional overlap, as handgrip strength and gait speed are components of both sarcopenia and the frailty phenotype. Nevertheless, these are conceptually distinct conditions, and prior work, such as the Toledo Study of Healthy Aging, has shown that the degree of overlap is modest [38]. To address this issue, we examined the individual associations of handgrip strength and gait speed with frailty, analyzing them as continuous rather than categorical variables, thereby providing a more meaningful and precise assessment.

Our finding that muscle measures of physical performance were more predictive of frailty than structural measures of bone and muscle has a biologically plausible basis. Muscle strength and function are dynamic, sensitive to early physiological and functional changes, easily measurable and directly influence reserve capacity, influencing frailty. In contrast, DEXA-derived bone and muscle mass are relatively static, evolve gradually, and are not easily measurable; therefore, they may only be evident in contributing to frailty risk over longer follow-up periods. Our results are consistent with the GOS (Geelong Osteoporosis Study), where muscle parameters predicted frailty more effectively than bone measures, and contrast with the OPRA (Osteoporosis Risk Assessment) cohort, which identified low BMD as a frailty predictor over 10 years [39, 40]. The discrepant result in our analysis may be due to a shorter follow-up duration, since bone loss and its consequences often accumulate more slowly than muscle decline, limited sample size or possible use of bone-targeted therapies that could also improve muscle strength and attenuate frailty risk [41]. Overall, our findings align with mechanistic evidence that muscle activity promotes bone adaptation through mechanical loading and biochemical crosstalk, supporting the view that muscle function drives earlier changes in the frailty trajectory, while bone deficits contribute later as additive effects. It is also possible that this pattern reflects the relative ease of detecting changes in muscle performance compared with the slower and less apparent progression of bone loss.

In our exploratory analysis of clarifying the interaction between bone-muscle in predicting frailty, we observed a significant synergistic trend. Higher hand grip strength mitigated frailty risk in participants with low BMD, while higher BMD reduced risk in those with reduced hand grip strength. These findings suggest that bone and muscle act synergistically in frailty development and that improving either may reduce frailty risk. Figure 1a and b illustrate that individuals with both low BMD and low hand grip strength had the highest frailty risk, while improvements in either measure were associated with meaningful risk reduction. Although our analysis did not account for intervening events such as fractures (due to low BMD) or falls (due to low muscle strength), which independently increase frailty risk, these findings nonetheless underscore the importance of integrated musculoskeletal interventions in reducing frailty risk. Prior work from our group reporting a synergistic effect of hip BMD and gait speed on fracture risk further supports the clinical significance of bone–muscle interplay in ageing outcomes [42].

The biological bases of our observed associations between osteosarcopenia and frailty are explained by the shared and interacting biological pathways, which include endocrine (e.g., IGF-1, growth hormone) and paracrine (myokines, osteokines) signaling as well as the influence of environmental factors (nutrition, inflammation, lifestyle, and socioeconomic factors) [1, 6, 43, 44].

From a clinical therapeutic perspective, our findings highlight that early identification and proactive management of osteosarcopenia and its components may help in reducing frailty and frailty-related adverse outcomes in older people. From a clinical diagnostic perspective, osteosarcopenia may represent a measurable pre-frail state within the frailty continuum. Unlike pre-frailty, defined solely by symptom-based criteria, osteosarcopenia offers objective and reproducible measures of bone-muscle deficits, making it a promising marker for early identification and a therapeutic target for preventive interventions to prevent frailty-related adverse events [45, 46].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of our study include its longitudinal cohort design, the use of validated sarcopenia criteria (EWGSOP2 and SDOC), and standardized DEXA-derived measures of bone and muscle mass. Frailty was defined using the widely validated Fried phenotype, enabling comparison with other musculoskeletal ageing cohorts. Comprehensive data collection across demographic, lifestyle, and clinical domains allowed for robust adjustment for potential confounders. Additionally, we analyzed both the independent contributions and the interaction between bone and muscle measures, providing novel insights into their combined role in frailty risk.

However, our study has several limitations. First, although the sample size was adequately powered based on the expected transition to frailty, attrition was higher than anticipated (50% vs. 25%). Most losses were due to withdrawal of consent, relocation, or loss of contact, and comparison of baseline characteristics suggested non-attenders had lower annual income and lower educational status. Despite our significant findings, this reduced the number of transitions observed and limited the ability to adjust for potential confounders. Second, while the follow-up period was sufficient to capture a single transition to frailty, the complex and dynamic nature of frailty would be better characterized through longer follow-up with repeated assessments. Third, the study population comprised community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 50 years, which may limit generalizability to older people in nursing homes. Fourth, although participants were drawn from diverse backgrounds, with 70% Australian-born and 30% overseas-born, these demographics should be considered when interpreting and applying the findings. Fifth, as with many longitudinal studies in older adults, unmeasured selection bias is possible due to the likely participation of a relatively health-conscious and motivated subgroup, which may affect generalizability. Sixth, although some nutritional measures were collected, a comprehensive dietary assessment was not available, and dietary influences could not be fully accounted for. Finally, definitional overlap exists between sarcopenia and the Fried frailty phenotype through shared measures (grip strength and gait speed); both frailty and sarcopenia are conceptually distinct, with modest overlap reported in previous studies [38], this overlap may still contribute to the observed associations. Nonetheless, these performance-based measures are integral to frailty assessment and remain particularly relevant when exploring musculoskeletal determinants.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study demonstrates that osteosarcopenia is associated with an increased risk of frailty in older adults. Among its components, clinical measures of muscle function, handgrip strength and gait speed were stronger predictors of frailty than DEXA-derived structural measures of bone and muscle, highlighting the importance of functional assessment in clinical practice. Moreover, the observed synergistic interaction between bone density and muscle strength suggests that interventions targeting either domain may mitigate frailty risk. Future studies should validate these findings in larger and more diverse cohorts, with more extended follow-up periods, repeated assessments, and the inclusion of broader frailty definitions.
