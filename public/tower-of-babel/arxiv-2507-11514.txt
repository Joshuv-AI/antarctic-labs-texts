Abstract
. Let K K be a field of characteristic zero over which every diagonal form in sufficiently many variables admits a nontrivial solution. For example, K K may be a totally imaginary number field or a finite extension of a p p -adic field. Suppose f 1 , … , f s f_{1},\ldots,f_{s} are forms of degree d d over K . K. Bik, Draisma and Snowden recently proved that there exists a constant B = B ⁡ ( d , s , K ) B=B(d,s,K) such that the rational solutions to the system of equations f 1 = … = f s = 0 f_{1}=\ldots=f_{s}=0 are Zariski dense, as long as the Birch rank of f 1 , … , f s f_{1},\ldots,f_{s} is greater than B . B. We establish an effective bound for this constant. Combined with results of Skinner, we obtain as a corollary a sufficient condition for the integer zeros of a system of forms over a number field to satisfy the Hardy-Littlewood asymptotic with a positive leading term. This corollary generalizes a seminal result proved by Schmidt for the rational numbers.



1.
Introduction

We are interested in studying solutions to systems of forms (i.e. homogeneous polynomials) over a field
K.
An especially simple class of forms is the diagonal ones.

Definition 1.1
.

The quantity
\phi_{d}=\phi_{d}(K)
is defined to be the minimal integer such that the following holds: Whenever
n>\phi_{d}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in K
there is some
0\neq x\in K^{n}
satisfying
a_{1}x_{1}^{d}+\ldots+a_{n}x_{n}^{d}=0.
If there is no such threshold, then
\phi_{d}=\infty.

For example,
\phi_{1}=1
over any field and
\phi_{2}(\mathbf{Q})=\infty.
From now on we assume that
\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{d}
are finite. Consider a collection of forms (i.e. homogeneous polynomials)
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
of common degree
d.

1.1.
Existence of solutions

In a landmark 1945 paper
[
5
]
, Brauer proved:

Theorem 1.2

(Brauer)
.

There exists a constant
V_{d,s}=V(d,s,\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{d})
such that if
n>V_{d,s}
then there is some
0\neq x\in K^{n}
with
f_{1}(x)=\ldots=f_{s}(x)=0.

The proof of theorem
1.2
proceeded via an elegant diagonalization argument, but resulted in an extremely large value for
V_{d,s}.
It was long thought impossible to obtain a reasonable bound by similar methods. However, in 1983 Leep and Schmidt
[
8
]
succeeded in that task, via carefully setting up an efficient inductive scheme. Their bound was further improved by Wooley
[
12
]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Wooley)
.

Theorem
1.2
holds with

V_{d,s}=2s^{2^{d-1}}\phi_{d}^{2^{d-2}}\prod_{i=2}^{d-1}(\phi_{i}+1)^{2^{i-2}}.

1.2.
Density of solutions

Suppose now that
K
is infinite. Bik, Draisma and Snowden recently
[
3
]
proved a certain parallel of theorem
1.2
in this setting. To state it requires the following definition.

Definition 1.4
.

The
Birch rank
of
f
is

\textnormal{Brk}(f)=\min(n,\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}(x:\nabla f(x)=0)).

The minimum only plays a role when
f
is linear. More generally, the collective Birch rank of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
is

\textnormal{Brk}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})=\min\{\textnormal{Brk}(a_{1}f_{1}+\ldots+a_{s}f_{s}):0\neq a\in\overline{K}^{s}\}.

Bik, Draisma and Snowden’s result is as follows.

Their result is phrased in terms of the strength of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s},
but is equivalent to theorem

.

Theorem 1.5
.

(Bik, Draisma and Snowden)
There exists a constant
B_{d,s}=B(d,s,\varphi_{2},\ldots,\varphi_{d})
such that if
\textnormal{Brk}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})>B_{d,s}
then the
K
-points of
Z(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
are Zariski dense.

As in theorem
1.2
, the constant

that can be extracted from their argument is extremely large. Our main result is an effective version of theorem
1.5
.

Assume henceforth that the characteristic of
K
is either zero or greater than
d.

Theorem 1.6
.

Theorem
1.5
holds for
B_{d,s}=s^{2^{d-1}}C_{d},
where

C_{d}=2^{(2\varphi)^{d-2}}\prod_{k=2}^{d}(\phi_{k}+11)^{2^{d-2}(\varphi^{d-k-1}+2^{k-d})+4}

and
\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\approx 1.62
is the golden ratio.

1.3.
Corollaries

Bounds for
\phi_{d}
were obtained by Skinner
[
11
]
when
K
is a finite extension of
\mathbf{Q}_{p}.
Plugging these bounds into theorem
1.6
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 1.7
.

If
K
is a finite extension of
\mathbf{Q}_{p}
and

\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})>s^{2^{d-1}}(4d)^{(2\varphi)^{d-1}+2^{d}+8d}\cdot 2^{(2\varphi)^{d-2}}

then the
K
-points of
Z(\underline{f})
are Zariski dense.

This is of particular interest for the Hardy-Littlewood circle method.

Definition 1.8
.

Let
K
be a number field. Following Schmidt
[
9
]
, we say that:

(1)

\underline{f}
is a
Hardy-Littlewood system
(HLS for short) when the distribution of integer zeros of
\underline{f}
satisfies the asymptotic formula predicted by the Hardy-Littlewood circle method and

(2)

\underline{f}
is a
proper Hardy-Littlewood system
(PHLS for short) when it is a HLS with a positive coefficient for the leading term.

Example 1.9
.

Let
K=\mathbf{Q}.
A HLS satisfies

\#\{x\in[-P,P]^{n}\cap\mathbf{Z}^{n}:f_{i}(x)=0\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq s\}=\mathfrak{J}\mathfrak{S}P^{n-ds}+o(P^{n-ds}),

where
\mathfrak{J},\mathfrak{S}
are the
singular integral
and
singular series
respectively. A PHLS is one for which also
\mathfrak{J}\mathfrak{S}>0.

Skinner
[
10
]
established a sufficient criterion for
\underline{f}
to be a PHLS. Together with corollary
1.7
, this yields:

Corollary 1.10
.

Suppose either that
d
is odd or else that
K
is totally imaginary. Then
\underline{f}
is a PHLS as soon as

\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})>s^{2^{d-1}}(4d)^{(2\varphi)^{d-1}+2^{d}+8d}\cdot 2^{(2\varphi)^{d-2}}.

We are not aware of the existence of such a bound in the literature. For
K=\mathbf{Q},
Schmidt used other methods to prove a result
[
9
, supplement to theorem II]
which implies that any system of forms of odd degree
d
satisfying
\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})\geq C_{d}\cdot s^{2^{d-1}+1}
is a PHLS. In fact, together with the recent results of Baily and the author
[
2
]
, corollary
1.10
can also be used to give a new proof of Schmidt’s result, albeit with a weaker constant
C^{\prime}_{d}.

Remark 1.11
.

Skinner’s result was generalized by Lee to the setting of function fields
[
7
]
. Theorem
1.6
implies the analog of corollary
1.10
in that context as well.

2.2.
Three propositions

We begin with a more general version of theorem
1.6
. Consider a system of forms
\underline{f}=(f_{i,j})_{i\in[d],j\in[s_{i}]},
where each
f_{i,j}
has degree

The definition of Birch rank generalizes as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

The Birch rank of
\underline{f}
is

\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})=\min_{i\in[d]}\textnormal{Brk}(f_{i,1},\ldots,f_{i,s_{i}}).

Theorem

is a special case of the following result.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
s=\max_{i\in[d]}s_{i}^{2^{i-d}}.
If

\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})>s^{2^{d-1}}2^{(2\varphi)^{d-2}}\prod_{k=2}^{d}(\phi_{k}+11)^{2^{d-2}(\varphi^{d-k-1}+2^{k-d})+4}

K

are Zariski dense.

The proof of theorem

Definition 2.4
.

Define

to be the minimal integer such that for any collection of forms
\underline{f}=(f_{i,j})_{i\in[d],j\in[s_{i}]}

\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})>B,

are Zariski dense. We sometimes write

There is a special case of this which will be particularly important for us. A
non-degenerate system of diagonal forms

\underline{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d+1})
is a collection where:

•

Each
f_{i}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is a diagonal form with nonzero coefficients,

•

The degree of
f_{i}
equals

for
1\leq i\leq d
and
\deg(f_{d+1})>d.

Definition 2.5
.

Define
\beta_{d}
to be the minimal integer such that the following holds. Whenever

and
\underline{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{d+1})
is a non-degenerate system of diagonal forms then there exists
x\in K^{n}

f_{1}(x)=\ldots=f_{d}(x)=0
and
f_{d+1}(x)\neq 0.

Our first proposition is a bound for
\beta_{d},
which may be of independent interest.

Proposition 2.6

(Bound for non-degenerate diagonal systems)
.

There exists a sequence of even integers
n_{d}
such that for

we have

\beta_{d}+1\leq n_{d}\leq 2^{1+\varphi^{d-1}}\prod_{k=2}^{d}(\phi_{k}+11)^{\varphi^{d-k}}.

To state our next propositions, recall that the Taylor expansion of a form
f
of degree
d
is given by

f(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{m})=\sum_{|e|=d}f^{e}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}),

where each
f^{e}
is multi-homogeneous of multi-degree

Write
\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{d}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)=\binom{m+d-1}{d}
for the number of terms in the above sum. For a collection of forms
\underline{f}=(f_{i,j})_{i\in[d],j\in[s_{i}]},
write
T_{m}(\underline{f})=(f_{i,j}^{e})_{i\in[d],j\in[s_{i}],|e|=i}
for the forms on

given by the Taylor expansion. These are also the coefficients of the polynomials obtained by restricting
\underline{f}
to
\textnormal{span}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}).
We abbreviate
Z_{m}(\underline{f}):=Z(T_{m}(\underline{f})).

Assume
s_{d}>0
and write
\underline{f}=\underline{g}\sqcup\{f\},
where
f=f_{d,s_{d}}.
Recall the following useful definition from
[
3
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

Subspaces
U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}
are

f(u_{1}+\ldots+u_{m})=f(u_{1})+\ldots+f(u_{m})

for any tuple of vectors
u_{i}\in U_{i}.
Equivalently,
f^{e}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})=0
for any exponent

with multiple nonzero entries.

We consider the algebraic set

D_{m}=((w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m-3})\in Z_{m}(\underline{g}):\textnormal{span}(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}),v_{1},\ldots,v_{m-3}\textnormal{ are }f\textnormal{-orthogonal}).

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\delta=\delta_{m}(s_{d},\ldots,s_{1})
be the minimal number such that whenever
\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})>\delta
the
K
-points of
D_{m}
are Zariski dense.

Our second main proposition bounds
B(s_{d},\ldots,s_{1})
in terms of
\delta_{m}(s_{d},\ldots,s_{1}).

Proposition 2.9

(Diagonalization implies density)
.

For
d\geq 2
define

m=m_{d}=(\phi_{d}+9)\left\lceil\frac{\beta_{d-2}+1}{2}\right\rceil.

Then

B(s_{d},\ldots,s_{1})\leq\max(\delta_{m}(s_{d},\ldots,s_{1}),2\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{i}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)+d^{\prime}-1).

Conversely,
\delta_{m}(s_{d},\ldots,s_{1})
can be bounded in terms of
B(s_{d}^{*},\ldots,s_{1}^{*}),
where
(s_{d}^{*},\ldots,s_{1}^{*})
is a lexicographically smaller sequence. This is the content of our third main proposition.

Proposition 2.10

(Efficient diagonalization)
.

For any
m
we have

\delta_{m}(s_{d},\ldots,s_{1})\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}m^{i}+B(s^{*}_{d},\ldots,s^{*}_{1}),

where
s^{*}_{d}=s_{d}-1
and
s^{*}_{i}=s_{i}+ms_{i+1}+\ldots+s_{d}m^{d-i}
for
1\leq i\leq d-1.

Taken together, these three propositions allow us to prove theorem
2.3
by induction on the sequence
(s_{d},\ldots,s_{1}).
This analysis is carried out in the next section.

2.3.
Proof idea

The proof of theorem
2.3
requires synthesizing the techniques used in the proofs of theorems
1.3
and
1.5
. There are several technical challenges that arise, but we do not dwell on these here. Instead we emphasize a subtle but significant change in perspective compared to the proof of theorem
1.5
– this is the use of Birch rank rather than strength as our fundamental quantity.

Although Birch rank and strength are bounded in terms of each other (see
[
4
]
), there are many simple examples (such as
x^{2}+y^{2}
) where they differ by a factor of
2.
If the right hand side of proposition
2.10
were so much as doubled, then our inductive argument would no longer result in polynomial bounds for theorem
2.3
. Thus, a key reason that the proof techniques of theorem
1.3
may be adapted to the setting of Birch rank is the
additive behavior
it exhibits – in the sense that proposition
2.10
holds.

Another important property of Birch rank (which also holds for strength) is that it is preserved under Taylor expansion. This is the content of lemma
4.2
and it is the reason we need to assume that the characteristic is either zero or greater than
d.
Since our argument proceeds by restricting forms to subspaces with desirable properties, this property plays a major role in our proofs.

\displaystyle B(s_{d},\ldots,s_{1})

\displaystyle B_{d}(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{2^{d-2}},0)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i=2}^{d}s^{2^{d-i}}(ms)^{i}+B_{d-1}(r_{d-1},\ldots,r_{2},r_{1})

\displaystyle\leq 2s^{2^{d-1}}\cdot(m+1)^{d}+B_{d-1}(r_{d-1},\ldots,r_{2},r_{1})

For
1\leq i\leq d-1
note that

\displaystyle r_{i}

\displaystyle=s^{2^{d-i}}+(sm)s^{2^{d-i-1}}+\ldots+(sm)^{d-i}s

\displaystyle\leq s^{2^{d-i}}(m+1)^{d-i}\leq[s^{2}(m+1)]^{2^{d-1-i}}.

By inductive hypothesis and lemma
3.1
,

\displaystyle B_{d}(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{2^{d-1}})

\displaystyle\leq s^{2^{d-1}}\cdot[2(m_{d}+1)^{d}+2]

\displaystyle+(s^{2}(m_{d}+1))^{2^{d-2}}(d-1)(m_{2}+1)^{2}(m_{3}+1)^{3}\prod_{i=4}^{d-1}(m_{i}+1)^{2^{i-2}}

\displaystyle\leq ds^{2^{d-1}}(m_{2}+1)^{2}(m_{3}+1)^{3}\prod_{i=4}^{d}(m_{i}+1)^{2^{i-2}},

which completes the induction step.
∎

We are now ready to prove our main result.

Proof of theorem
2.3
.

By lemma
3.1
, it’s enough to prove the bound for
B(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{2^{d-1}}).
Thanks to lemma
3.3
, all that remains is to bound
(m_{2}+1)^{2}(m_{3}+1)^{3}\prod_{i=4}^{d}(m_{i}+1)^{2^{i-2}}.
This will be accomplished using proposition
2.6
.

By definition,
m_{2}+1\leq\phi_{2}+11.
By proposition
2.6
we have
m_{3}+1\leq 2(\phi_{3}+11)
and in general

\displaystyle\prod_{i=4}^{d}(m_{i}+1)^{2^{i-2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\sum_{i=4}^{d}2^{i-2}\varphi^{i-3}}\prod_{i=4}^{d}(\phi_{i}+11)^{2^{i-2}}\prod_{k=2}^{i-2}(\phi_{k}+11)^{2^{i-2}\varphi^{i-2-k}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{(2\varphi)^{d-2}}\prod_{k=2}^{d}(\phi_{k}+11)^{2^{k-2}+\sum_{i=k+2}^{d}2^{i-2}\varphi^{i-2-k}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{(2\varphi)^{d-2}}\prod_{k=2}^{d}(\phi_{k}+11)^{2^{d-2}(2^{k-d}+\varphi^{d-k-1})}.

∎

4.1.
Taylor expansion and Birch rank

We begin by recording several elementary identities for the Taylor expansion.

Lemma 4.1
.

The terms of the Taylor expansion satisfy the following formulas:

(1)

f^{(1,\ldots,1)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=f^{(1,\ldots,1)}(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(d)})
for any permutation
\sigma\in S_{d},

(2)

f^{(1,d-1)}(h,x)=\nabla f(x)\cdot h,

(3)

e_{1}!\ldots e_{m}!f^{e}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=f^{(1,\ldots,1)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},\ldots,x_{m}),
where each
x_{i}
appears
e_{i}
times and

(4)

for any scalars
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m},

\frac{\partial f^{e}}{\partial x_{k}}(\lambda_{1}x,\ldots,\lambda_{m}x)=\binom{d-1}{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}-1,\ldots,e_{m}}\cdot\lambda_{1}^{e_{1}}\ldots\lambda_{k}^{e_{k}-1}\ldots\lambda_{m}^{e_{m}}\nabla f(x).

Proof.

For the first property, note that for any permutation
\sigma
we have

f(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{d})=f(x_{\sigma(1)}+\ldots+x_{\sigma(d)}).

Comparing the multi-homogeneous terms of multi-degree
(1,\ldots,1)
on both sides proves the claim.

For the second formula, first note that
f^{(0,d)}(h,x)=f(x)
follows from setting
h=0.
Therefore,
f(x+th)=f(x)+tf^{(1,d-1)}(h,x)+O(t^{2}),
proving the claim.

To prove the third formula, compute the coefficient of
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{e_{i}}t_{i,j}
in the expression
f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{e_{i}}t_{i,j}x_{i}\right)
in two different ways. On the one hand, using the
d
-term Taylor expansion, this equals
f^{(1,\ldots,1)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},\ldots,x_{m}).
On the other hand, using the
m
-term Taylor expansion, this equals
e_{1}!\ldots e_{m}!f^{e}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}).

For the fourth identity, both sides are multi-homogeneous of the same multi-degree, so we may assume without loss of generality that
\lambda_{1}=\ldots=\lambda_{m}=1
and that
k=1.
Using formulas (1-3) and the multi-linearity of
f^{(1,\ldots,1)},
we get

\displaystyle e_{1}!\ldots e_{m}!f^{e}(x+th,x,\ldots,x)

\displaystyle=f^{(1,\ldots,1)}(x+th,\ldots,x+th,x,\ldots,x)

\displaystyle=e_{1}!\ldots e_{m}!f^{e}(x,\ldots,x)+te_{1}\cdot f^{(1,\ldots,1)}(h,x,\ldots,x)+O(t^{2})

\displaystyle=e_{1}!\ldots e_{m}!f^{e}(x,\ldots,x)+te_{1}(d-1)!f^{(1,d-1)}(h,x)+O(t^{2})

\displaystyle=e_{1}!\ldots e_{m}!f^{e}(x,\ldots,x)+te_{1}(d-1)!\nabla f(x)\cdot h+O(t^{2}),

which proves the claim.
∎

This last formula has a very useful consequence.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
m\geq 1,

\textnormal{Brk}(T_{m}(\underline{f}))\geq\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f}).

Proof.

The statement is about each degree separately, so we may assume without loss of generality that
\underline{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
is composed entirely of forms of degree
d.
For
d=1
the claim is immediate so assume also that
d>1.
Let
g=\sum_{j\in[s],|e|=d}a_{j,e}f^{e}_{j}
be a nontrivial linear combination and
S(g)=(\underline{x}:\nabla g(\underline{x})=0).
For
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m})\neq 0,
consider the diagonal embedding
V\cong V_{\lambda}\subset V^{m}
given by the image of
x\mapsto(\lambda_{1}x,\ldots,\lambda_{m}x).

The intersection
S(g)\cap V_{\lambda}
is nonempty because it contains
0,
and we have

(4)

\textnormal{codim}_{V_{\lambda}}(S(g)\cap V_{\lambda})\leq\textnormal{codim}_{V^{m}}S(g)=\textnormal{Brk}(g).

By assumption, there is some
e_{0}
for which
a_{j,e_{0}}
are not all zero. Let
k\in[m]
be such that
(e_{0})_{k}>0.
By lemma
4.1
,

\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{k}}(\lambda_{1}x,\ldots,\lambda_{m}x)=\sum_{j\in[s]}\nabla f_{j}(x)\sum_{|e|=d,e_{k}>0}a_{j,e}\binom{d-1}{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}-1,\ldots,e_{m}}\cdot\lambda_{1}^{e_{1}}\ldots\lambda_{k}^{e_{k}-1}\ldots\lambda_{m}^{e_{m}}.

Choose
\lambda
such that the coefficient of some
\nabla f_{j}(x)
above is nonzero and let

f=\sum_{j\in[s]}f_{j}\sum_{|e|=d,e_{k}>0}a_{j,e}\binom{d-1}{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}-1,\ldots,e_{m}}\cdot\lambda_{1}^{e_{1}}\ldots\lambda_{k}^{e_{k}-1}\ldots\lambda_{m}^{e_{m}}

be the corresponding nontrivial linear combination of the
f_{j}.
We have

S(f)\supset(x\in V:(\lambda_{1}x,\ldots,\lambda_{m}x)\in S(g)),

so that

\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})\leq\textnormal{Brk}(f)=\textnormal{codim}_{V}S(f)\leq\textnormal{codim}_{V_{\lambda}}(S(g)\cap V_{\lambda}).

Combining this with inequality (
4
) gives
\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})\leq\textnormal{Brk}(g)
as desired.
∎

4.2.
Birch rank, restriction to subspaces and singularities

We have already mentioned that our arguments will often rely on restricting forms to subspaces. An issue that arises is that the Birch rank of the restricted form may be smaller than that of the original form.

Consider, for example, the quadratic form
f=x^{2}-y^{2}
and the hyperplane
U=\{(x,x):x\in K\}.
We have
\textnormal{Brk}(f)=2
but
\textnormal{Brk}(f\restriction_{U})=0,
so the Birch rank decreases by
2.
Fortunately, this is the largest possible drop in Birch rank upon restriction to a hyperplane – as we will soon see. This is a consequence of the following result of Ananyan and Hochster
[
1
, Theorem 2.4]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
M
be a
h\times n
matrix such that for each
i\in[h]
the
i
-th row consists of forms of degree
d_{i}
in
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and the
d_{i}
are mutually distinct integers. If for each
i\in[h]
we have

\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}(x:M_{i,j}(x)=0\ \forall j\in[n])\geq c

then

\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}(x:\textnormal{rk}(M(x))<h)\geq c-h+1.

We can now easily bound the decrease in Birch rank of the restriction of a form to a subspace.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\underline{f}
be a collection of forms of degree two or higher and
U\subset K^{n}
a subspace of codimension
c.

Then
\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f}\restriction_{U})\geq\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})-2c.

Proof.

It is enough to prove this in the case of a single form
f
and
c=1.
In this case,
U=(x\in K^{n}:a_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+a_{n}x_{n}=0)
for some
0\neq a\in K^{n}.
We have

\displaystyle\textnormal{Brk}(f\restriction_{U})

\displaystyle=\textnormal{codim}_{U}(x\in U:\nabla f(x)\in\textnormal{span}(a))

\displaystyle\geq\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}(x\in\mathbf{A}^{n}:(\nabla f(x),a)\textnormal{ are linearly dependent})-1\geq\textnormal{Brk}(f)-2,

where the final inequality follows from lemma
4.3
.
∎

Lemma
4.3
also implies a close connection between
\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})
and the codimension of

S(\underline{f})=(x\in\mathbf{A}^{n}:(\nabla f_{i,j}(x))_{i\in[d],j\in[s_{i}]}\textnormal{ are linearly dependent}).

Lemma 4.5
.

We have

\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}S(\underline{f})\geq\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})-\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}-d^{\prime}+2,

where
d^{\prime}=|\{i:s_{i}\neq 0\}|.

Proof.

Let
s=\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}.
Write
S=\cup_{a\in\mathbf{P}^{s-1}}S_{a},
where

S_{a}=\left(x:\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{s_{i}}a_{i,j}\nabla f_{i,j}(x)=0\right).

It’s enough to show that for any fixed
a\in\mathbf{P}^{s-1}
we have
\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}S_{a}\geq\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})-d^{\prime}+1.
Fixing
a,
let
I=\{i\in[d]:(a_{i,j})_{j\in[s_{i}]}\neq(0,\ldots,0)\}.
For each
i\in I,
let
f_{i}=\sum_{j\in[s_{i}]}a_{i}f_{i,j}.
We now have the inclusion

S_{a}\subset(x\in\mathbf{A}^{n}:(\nabla f_{i}(x))_{i\in I}\textnormal{ are linearly dependent}).

Applying lemma
4.3
yields

\displaystyle\textnormal{codim}_{A^{n}}S_{a}

\displaystyle\geq\textnormal{codim}_{\mathbf{A}^{n}}(x\in\mathbf{A}^{n}:(\nabla f_{i}(x))_{i\in I}\textnormal{ are linearly dependent})

\displaystyle\geq\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})-|I|+1\geq\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})-d^{\prime}+1.

∎

There is an important criterion relating singularities to irreducibility.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{s}
be a sequence of forms of positive (possibly different) degrees such that
Z=Z(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
is a complete intersection of codimension
s.
If

\textnormal{codim}_{Z}(x\in Z:(\nabla f_{1}(x),\ldots,\nabla f_{s}(x)\textnormal{ are linearly dependent})>1,

then the ideal
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
is prime. In particular,
Z(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
is irreducible.

Proof.

This is a well known fact that follows from
[
6
, Theorem 18.15b]
. Note that the hypothesis of that theorem is satisfied by
[
6
, Proposition 18.13]
and that
Z(f_{1},\ldots,f_{s})
is connected since it’s a cone. Therefore, if its coordinate ring is a product of domains then it must in fact be a domain.
∎

As a consequence of the results of this section, we have:

Lemma 4.7
.

If
\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})>2\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{i}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)+d^{\prime}-1
and
\underline{g}\subset T_{m}(\underline{f})
consists of
t
equations then
Z(\underline{g})
is an irreducible complete intersection of codimension
t.

Proof.

It follows from lemmas
4.2
and
4.5
that

(5)

\textnormal{codim}_{V^{m}}S(\underline{g})\geq\textnormal{Brk}(\underline{f})-\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{i}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)-d^{\prime}+2>\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}\left(\kern-3.00003pt\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{i}\right)\kern-3.00003pt\right)+1.

The right hand side is larger than
t,
so in particular the zero locus
Z=Z(\underline{g})
is a complete intersection of codimension
t.
Inequality (
5
) also implies that we have
\textnormal{codim}_{Z}Z\cap S(\underline{g})>1,
so
Z
is irreducible by lemma
4.6
.
∎
