Hamiltonian Systems as an Example of Invariant Measure
Lopes, Daniel F

Abstract
Hamiltonian systems are a classical example in the ergodic theory of flows with an invariant measure. In this matter, we present a brief introduction to measure theory and prove the Poincaré recurrence theorem to present the conditions for a system to be conservative. In the following, we discuss the Hamiltonian differential equations, vector fields, and their respective flows as an example of this invariance.

2
Poincaré Recurrence Theorem

We now present an important theorem on ergotic theory, with a structure of presentation based on
[
1
]
, Chapter 2.

2.1
First Ideas

By
dynamical systems
we understand the transformations
f:M\to M
under a metric or topological space
M
. Since it is hard to analyze
all
points under some transformation, the main objective of ergodic theory is to study the behavior of non-empty measure sets under some invariant measure.

In this sense, the Poincaré recurrence theorem states that, for every
finite invariant
measure, almost every point
x\in M
is recurrent, which means its trajectory under a transformation comes closer to
x
when the time goes to infinity.

We will present and prove the metric version of the theorem, which is about measurable sets. The topological version, about open sets, is quite similar.

2.2
Metric version

Let
\mu
be a measure defined in a Borel
\sigma
-algebra of a space
M
.

We say that a measure
\mu
is invariant over a transformation
f
if:

\mu(E)=\mu(f^{-1}(E))
for all measurable sets
E\subset M

for flows:

\mu(E)=\mu(f^{-t}(E))
for all measurable sets
E\subset M
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f:M\in M
be a measurable transformation and
\mu
a finite invariant measure. Let
E\subset M
be any measurable set with
\mu(E)>0
. So,
\mu
-almost every point
x\in E
has some iterated
f^{n}(x)
,
n>1
that is in
E
.

Before proving the theorem itself, we will prove a strong consequence of it:

Corollary 2.1
.

Given Theorem 2.1, for
\mu
-almost every point
x\in E
there exists an infinite number of values of
n\geq 1
such that
f^{n}(x)
is in
E
.

Proof.

Let, for each
k\geq 1
,
E_{k}
be the set of points
x\in E
that return exactly
k
times to
E
. This means that, for a
x\in E_{k}
, there exists exactly
k
values of iterates
n\geq 1
such that
f^{n}(x)\in E
.

The set of points that return to
E
a finite number of times is:

\displaystyle\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}E_{k}

Thus, it is enough to prove that
\mu(E_{k})=0
for all
k\geq 1
. We will prove it by contradiction.

Suppose that
\mu(E_{k})>0
for some
k\geq 1
. The, by the previous theorem, we got that almost every point
x\in E_{k}
has an iterate
f^{n}(x)
in
E_{k}
. Let’s fixate
x
and let
y=f^{n}(x)
. Since
y\in E_{k}
,
y
has exactly
k
future iterates in
E
. But, since
y
is an iterate of
x
,
x
has
k+1
future iterates in
E
. That contradicts the fact that
x\in E_{k}
. Thus,
\mu(E_{k})=0
for all
k\geq 1.

∎

Now, to the main proof:

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Let
E^{0}
be the set of points
x\in E
that never return to
E
. The objective is to prove that
\mu(E^{0})=0
. First, let state that the pre-images
f^{-n}(E^{0})
are two by two disjoints. Suppose by contradiction that there exist
m>n\geq 1
such that
f^{-m}(E_{0})
intersects
f^{-n}(E_{0})
. Let
x\in f^{-m}(E_{0})\cap f^{-n}(E_{0})
and
y=f^{n}(x)
. So,
y
is in
E^{0}
and
f^{m}(x)=f^{m-n}(f^{n}(x))=f^{m-n}(y)
. Since
f^{m}(x)\in E^{0}
and
E^{0}\subset E
,
y
returns to
E
at least once (
m-n>0
). That contradicts the definition of
E^{0}
. So the pre-images are two by two disjoints, in fact.

That implies:

\mu(\displaystyle\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}f^{-n}(E_{0}))=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu(f^{-n}(E^{0}))=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu(E^{0})

In the last equality we used the hypothesis that
\mu
is invariant, so
\mu(f^{-n}(E^{0}))=\mu(E^{0})
for all
n\geq 1
. Since the measure is finite, the left side is finite. But the right side is an infinite sum of equal terms. The only way it can be finite is by
\mu(E^{0})=0
.
∎

3.1

We now present a proposition characterizing an invariant measure over a transformation or a flow:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let

\mu

f
preserves
\mu

\displaystyle\int\varphi\ d\mu=\displaystyle\int\varphi\circ f\ d\mu

Proof.
(\Rightarrow)
.

Assume that

preserves
\mu
. If
\mu
is the characteristic function of a set

, then:

\displaystyle\chi_{f^{-1}(A)}

\displaystyle=\chi_{A}\circ f

\displaystyle\chi_{f^{-1}(A)}

\displaystyle=\varphi\circ f

(1)

Since:

\mu(f^{-1}(A))=\displaystyle\int\chi_{f^{-1}(A)}\ d\mu

By
(1)
:

\mu(f^{-1}(A))=\displaystyle\int\varphi\circ f\ d\mu

Using the fact that
\mu(f^{-1}(A))=\mu(A)
:

\displaystyle\mu(A)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int\varphi\circ f\ d\mu

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int\chi_{A}\ d\mu

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int\varphi\circ f\ d\mu

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int\varphi\ d\mu

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int\varphi\circ f\ d\mu

Then, it is proved when
\varphi
is a characteristic function. By the linearity of the integral, if
\varphi
is a simple function (which means, a linear combination of characteristic functions
\chi_{A_{k}}
of disjoint sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
), then the equality holds. Finally, if
\varphi
is an arbitrary function, by the integral definition:

\displaystyle\int\varphi\ d\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}\displaystyle\int\varphi_{n}\ d\mu

where
\varphi_{n}
are simple crescent functions and
\varphi_{n}\to\varphi
. Still,
\varphi_{n}\circ f
are simple crescent functions and
\varphi_{n}\circ f\to\varphi\circ f
. Then:

\displaystyle\int\varphi\circ f\ d\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}\displaystyle\int\varphi_{n}\circ f\ d\mu

Since
\int\varphi_{n}\ d\mu=\int\varphi_{n}\circ f\ d\mu
, taking the limit on both sides:

\displaystyle\int\varphi\ d\mu=\displaystyle\int\varphi\circ f\ d\mu

∎

Proof.
(\Leftarrow)
.

Given a set
A
in a Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
M
, let
\varphi=\chi_{A}
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int\varphi\ d\mu

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int\varphi\circ f\ d\mu

\displaystyle\mu(A)

\displaystyle=\mu(f^{-1}(A))

∎

3.2
Characterization of Conservative Systems

Let
U
be an open set on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
and let
f:U\to U
a
C^{1}
diffeomorphism. We represent by

the Lebesgue measure volume in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
:

m(B)=\displaystyle\int_{B}dx_{1}\dots dx_{d}
and
\displaystyle\int_{B}\phi\ dvol=\displaystyle\int_{B}\phi(x_{1},\dots x_{d})\ dx_{1}\dots dx_{d}

for any measurable set
B
and any integrable function
\varphi
.

The change of variables formula stats that, for any measurable set

:

m(f(B))=\displaystyle\int_{B}|\det Df|\ dm

Then we can deduce the following:

Lemma 3.1
.

A
C^{1}
diffeomorphism

preserves the volume if and only if
|\det Df|=1
.

Proof.
(\Rightarrow)
.

Let
E
be a measurable set and
B=f^{-1}(E)
. If
|\det Df|=1
, then:

m(E)=\displaystyle\int_{B}1\ dm=m(B)=m(f^{-1}(E))

∎

Proof.
(\Leftarrow)
.

Suppose
|\det Df|>1
at some point
x
. Since the Jacobian is continuous, there is a neighborhood
U
of
x
and some
\sigma>1
such that:

|\det Df(y)|\geq\sigma
for all
y\in U

The, by making
B=U
:

m(f(U))\geq\displaystyle\int_{B}\sigma\ dm\geq\sigma m(U)>m(U)

Denoting
E=f(U)
, then
m(E)>m(f^{-1}(E)
and the volume is not invariant by f. It is similar for
|\det Df|<1
at some point.
∎

We now analyze the case of flows
\phi^{t}:U\to U
,

. Suppose a

\det D\phi^{t}(x)=1
for all

and for all

Suppose the flow
\phi^{t}
as the trajectories of a

\det Df^{t}(x)=\exp{\left(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{div}\textbf{F}(\phi^{s}(x))\ ds\right)}

For volume invariance,

Lemma 3.2
.

A flow

4.1
Hamiltonian Differential Equations

If we have a particle moving through a configuration space
S=\{\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
, we can describe the system by determining the position
\mathbf{q}
and the momentum
\mathbf{p}
of the particle over time
t\in\mathbb{R}
. In this sense, there exists the potential energy
V:S\to\mathbb{R}
, that depends on
\mathbf{q}
, the kinetic energy
T:S\to\mathbb{R}
, that depends on
\mathbf{p}
, and the Hamiltonian:

H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=V(\mathbf{q})+T(\mathbf{p})

That depends on both
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{p}
, and represents the total energy of the phase space (the space of all possible combinations of
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{p}
)
S\times\mathbb{R}
. It is natural, then, to imagine that some of this measures would help to study the behavior of
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{p}
over time, namely, the Hamiltonian
H
.

First, by the equation of motion:

m\ddot{q}=-\nabla V_{q}

(2)

and by the definition of momentum
\mathbf{p}=m\mathbf{v}
, we have:

\displaystyle\dot{q_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{j}}{m}

\displaystyle\dot{p_{j}}

\displaystyle=m\ddot{q}_{j}

(3)

By
(2)
:

\dot{p_{j}}=-\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial q_{j}}

(4)

for
1\leq j\leq n

Equations
(2)
and
(3)
can be written in the Hamiltonian form. First, deriving
H
in terms of
q_{j}
:

\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{j}}=\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial q_{j}}+\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial q_{j}}

Since
\partial T/\partial q_{j}=0
, by
(3)
:

\dot{p_{j}}=-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{j}}

Deriving in terms of
p_{j}
, remembering that
T(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{p}/2m
:

\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}=\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial p_{j}}+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\left(\dfrac{1}{2m}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{2}\right)

Since
\partial V/\partial p_{j}=0
:

\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}=\dfrac{p_{j}}{m}\Rightarrow\dot{q}_{j}=\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}

Then, we have the system:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{q}_{j}&=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}\\[5.69054pt]
\dot{p}_{j}&=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{j}}\end{aligned}\right.,\;1\leq j\leq n

If we consider the phase space as
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, the time-dependent Hamiltonian is
H:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
H(q_{1},\dots,q_{n},p_{1},\dots p_{n},t)
, with
t\in\mathbb{R}
being the time. Then, we have the system of differential equations:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{q}_{j}&=\frac{\partial H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},t)}{\partial p_{j}}\\[5.69054pt]
\dot{p}_{j}&=-\frac{\partial H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},t)}{\partial q_{j}}\end{aligned}\right.,\;1\leq j\leq n

The Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
for this system is:

X_{H}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=\left(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{1}},\dots,\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{n}},-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{1}},\dots,-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{n}}\right)

If
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=(z_{1},\dots,z_{2n})
, then:

X_{H}(\mathbf{z})=(\dot{z_{1}},\dots,\dot{z_{2n}})

In time-dependent case, the flow of the Hamiltonian vector field for a point
\mathbf{z}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n},p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
in the phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
at the time
t
is:

\phi^{t}(\mathbf{z})=(q_{1}(t),\dots,q_{n}(t),p_{1}(t),\dots,p_{n}(t))

where
(\mathbf{q}(t),\mathbf{p}(t))
satisfies the Hamiltonian systems of equations at time
t
, which means, the flow takes the initial point
\phi^{0}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})
to
\mathbf{z}(t)
following the vector field
X_{H}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
H
be a time-independent Hamiltonian for a Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
. Then,
H
is constant along solutions of
X_{H}
.

Proof.

\dot{H}(\mathbf{q}(t),\mathbf{p}(t))=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{j}}q_{j}+\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}p_{j}\right)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}+\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}\left(-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{j}}\right)=0

∎

Proposition 4.2
.

A Hamiltonian vector field has zero divergence, so its flow preserves the volume.

Proof.

\operatorname{div}(X_{H})(\mathbf{z})=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial q_{j}}\left(\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}\right)(\mathbf{z})+\dfrac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\left(-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{j}}\right)(\mathbf{z})=0

∎

4.2
Examples and conclusion

In this section we will analyze two important examples of Hamiltonian systems, depicting their Hamiltonian function, the solutions
(p,q)
and
(\theta,p)
of the equations and the phase portraits (the geometric representation of the solutions). Finally, we present our conclusions about the topics discussed.

Example 4.1
.

The equation of the linear harmonic oscillator is:

m\ddot{q}+kq=0

with
k=m\omega^{2}
, we have:

\ddot{q}+\omega^{2}q=0

(5)

The kinetic energy is:

T(p)=\dfrac{p^{2}}{2m}

and the potential energy is:

\displaystyle V(q)

\displaystyle=-\int m\ddot{q}\,dq

\displaystyle V(q)

\displaystyle=-\int-\omega^{2}q\,dq

Making the constant
C=0
, such that
V(0)=0
, it comes:

V(q)=\dfrac{m\omega^{2}q^{2}}{2}

Thus, the Hamiltonian function is:

H(q,p)=\dfrac{p^{2}}{2m}+\dfrac{m\omega^{2}q^{2}}{2}

Choosing
m=1
for simplicity:

H(q,p)=\dfrac{p^{2}+\omega^{2}p^{2}}{2}

The solutions of
(5)
can be found by the characteristic equations and are the following:

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=A\cos(\omega(t-\delta))

\displaystyle p(t)

\displaystyle=-A\omega\sin(\omega(t-\delta))

Since
H
is time independent, we can fixate
H=E
:

\displaystyle\dfrac{p^{2}}{2m}+\dfrac{m\omega^{2}q^{2}}{2}=E

\displaystyle\dfrac{p^{2}}{2mE}+\dfrac{q^{2}}{\left(\dfrac{2E}{m\omega^{2}}\right)}=1

That means that the phase portrait of
(q,p)
represents concentric ellipses depending on
E
. Normalizing (
m=1
,
\omega=1
), we have concentric circles depending on
E
:

Example 4.2
.

The equation of a pendulum of mass m and length L is:

mL\ddot{\theta}=-mg\sin\theta

(6)

The potential energy is:

\displaystyle V(\theta)=

\displaystyle-\displaystyle\int mL^{2}\ddot{\theta}\ d\theta

\displaystyle V(\theta)=

\displaystyle-\displaystyle\int-mgL\sin\theta\ d\theta

\displaystyle V(\theta)=

\displaystyle-mgL\cos\theta+C

Choosing
C=mgL
, such that
V(0)=0
:

V(\theta)=mgL(1-cos\theta)

Dividing by
mL^{2}
for simplification:

V(\theta)=\dfrac{g}{L}(1-\cos\theta)

So, we got the normalized Hamiltonian:

H(\theta,p)=\dfrac{p^{2}}{2}+\dfrac{g}{L}(1-\cos\theta)

In the case of small oscillations (
\sin\theta\approx\theta
), the pendulum behaves like a linear oscillator and its phase portrait is the same (concentric ellipses depending on
E
). The solutions are also similar:

\displaystyle\theta(t)

\displaystyle=\theta_{max}\cos(\omega t-\phi)

\displaystyle p(t)

\displaystyle=-\theta_{max}\omega\sin(\omega t-\phi)

For greater amplitudes, we have:

p(t)=\pm\sqrt{2(E-(1-\cos\theta(t)))}

and a different phase portrait:

:

•

If
E<2
, we have closed trajectories (represents the oscillations)

•

If
E>2
, we have open curves (represents complete rotations)

In summary, Hamiltonian systems are an example of conservative systems, since their flow
\phi^{t}
preserves the volume (in terms of Lebesgue measure) in phase space, or equivalently, the divergence of its vector field
X_{H}
equals zero. This property can be visualized in the examples we discussed. Since they are linear, their phase space is
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(the phase portrait). If we take any nonempty set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have
\mu(U)=\mu(\phi^{t}(U))
, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
