Self-care practices among women with gestational diabetes in low-resource settings in Ghana: a phenomenological study
Authors: Felix Kwasi Nyande, Priscilla Kumi, Cynthia Tetteh, Florence Bosomtwe, Elizabeth Ewoenam Hamenu, Patience Abla Ashiadey, Sylvia Catherine Atsu, Lydia Kudjo, Kennedy Diema Konlan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) poses significant challenges to pregnant women and requires self-care practices for optimal outcomes. This study explored the self-care practices of pregnant women diagnosed with GDM in the Ho Municipality. A qualitative study using a Husserlian phenomenological design was employed. Purposive sampling was used to recruit 20 pregnant women with GDM. Data were collected through in-depth interviews, audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic analysis. Three (3) major themes and eleven (11) subthemes were identified. The themes that emerged included (a) knowledge of GDM, (b) concerns related to the management of GDM, (c) self-care practices for GDM, and (d) barriers and facilitators towards the management. Participants demonstrated awareness of hormonal and genetic risk for GDM; however, there was limited understanding of its development and management, particularly regarding the use of oral medications during pregnancy. Many participants reported emotional distress following the diagnosis. Key challenges included difficulty adhering to dietary and medication regimens and inadequate knowledge. Time constraints were identified as major barriers, while strong social support systems facilitated effective blood glucose management. The findings reveal gaps in knowledge and challenges in self-care among women with GDM in the Ho Municipality. There is a need for targeted education and support interventions to improve self-care practices and health outcomes.Keywords: Gestational diabetes, Self-care, High blood sugar, Pregnancy, ComplicationsSubject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) is one of the most prevalent medical complications that poses a significant risk to both maternal and neonatal health1,2. Globally, the prevalence of GDM affects 17% of pregnancies, making it a major public health concern3,4. GDM is characterised by glucose intolerance of varying severity with onset or first recognition during pregnancy. The condition arises when a hormone made by the placenta prevents the body from using insulin effectively or inhibits its secretion, causing glucose to build up in the blood5. GDM is characterized by carbohydrate intolerance of varying degrees, with its onset or first recognition occurring specifically during pregnancy6–8. This pathophysiological process is similar to type 2 diabetes, where there is a progressive rise in tissue resistance to insulin5,8. In pregnancies, the heightened demand for insulin is met inefficiently, leading to an imbalance between insulin resistance and insulin supply9,10. This shift commonly occurs in the second trimester of pregnancy, with insulin resistance steadily increasing before birth11. In normal pregnancies, the blood sugar returns to normal rapidly after birth5. The condition represents a more pronounced and resolved imbalances beteween insuline demand and supply5. The prevalence of GDM varies widely across regions, with an estimated prevalence of 14% in sub-Saharan Africa12. In Ghana, the prevalence has risen markedly form 0.5% in 2004 to 9.3% in 20159,13. The global obesity epidemic, characterized by an increased body mass index (BMI) across populations, birthing at later stages of life, and advanced maternal age, multiple fetuses pregnancies, such as twins or triplets, were influential in the increasing prevalence of the disease13–15.

GDM is associated with numerous adverse outcomes. The risk of GDM complications commonly significantly impacts both mothers’ and babies’ health16. Maternal risks include a heightened likelihood of preeclampsia, increased rates of cesarean section births, an elevated risk of developing type 2 diabetes, and a greater vulnerability to cardiovascular diseases10,17. For infants, GDM includes excessive birth weight (macrosomia), an increased risk of neonatal hypoglycemia, and a higher incidence of respiratory distress syndrome18. However, effective self-care practices such as dietary modification, regular physical activity, blood glucose monitoring, and medication adherence can significantly improve glycemic control and reduce this risk18,19. Despite the importance of self-care, several barriers hinder effective management. This includes limited knowledge of GDM, time constraints related to work and family responsibilities, and inadequate access to health resources16,20. Poor understanding of ion may reduce adherence to the recommended practices and increase the risk of adverse outcomes8,17,19,21,22. Consequently, GDM management necessitates vigilant self-care practices to mitigate associated complications19. It is important to promote the adoption of self-care, educate high-risk women on GDM, encourage efficient time management, promote practical measures, and encourage sustained practices, especially in resource-limited settings. Therefore, health care practitioners must encourage and promote the adoption of self-care practices among pregnant women to control GDM. This will warrant adopting specific, culturally tailored, acceptable, cost-effective, and feasible guidelines that empower women in low-resource settings.

Despite the availability of guidelines and recommendations, there remains a critical gap in the understanding of pregnant women diagnosed with GDM and their adoption of self-care practices. Also, in some parts of the developing countries, critical gaps exist in the nature and quality of literature that promotes the adoption of self-care practices. Few studies use an in-depth qualitative approach to elicit the inherent challenges influencing women’s decision-making and subsequent self-care practices. Also, women’s experiences in utilizing self-care practices, particularly in resource-limited settings where access to services may be limited, are also relatively underreported. Elucidating these experiences will highlight the challenges, motivations, and perceptions that influence self-care practices among women with GDM. A crucial component for developing targeted strategies that can enhance the quality and effectiveness of GDM management. Therefore, this study aims to explore the self-care practices of women diagnosed with GDM in the hospital municipality of Ghana, using the phenomenological approach.

Methods

Design

This study employed a qualitative approach, specifically a Husserlian phenomenological design. The Husserlian Phenomenology design was appropriate for exploring the lived experiences (understanding consciousness) and perceptions (nature of reality) of pregnant women regarding self-care practices in the management of GDM23,24. This Hesserlian phenomenological design allowed for a deeper exploration of the essence of self-care experiences and how pregnant women perceived disease and the care processes23.

Study setting

The study was conducted in two selected public health facilities in the Ho Municipality; specifically, the Ho Teaching Hospital and Ho Municipal Hospital.

The Ho Teaching Hospital (HTH) is a 320-bed-capacity tertiary-level facility and was the fifth public Teaching Hospital in Ghana, recommissioned in April, 2019. It serves as a referral center for the Volta region and the surrounding areas and provides 24-hour maternity services. The hospital has a staff strength of approximately 650.

The Ho Municipal Hospital (HMH) was established in 1927 and redesignated as a district hospital in 2000. It was the regional hospital and was re-designated as a district hospital in 2000. The Ho Municipal Hospital is a 186-bed capacity facility. It has a staff strength of 362, which includes 7 Doctors, 3 Physician Assistants, 33 Midwives, 125 Nurses, and 144 paramedical staff. The hospital provides 24-h maternity services to pregnant women.

Population and sampling

The study population comprised pregnant women who had been clinically diagnosed with GDM for at least a month. This criterion ensured that participants had sufficient experience with the care practices. A total of twenty20 pregnant women were recruited using purposive sampling. The sample size was determined by data saturation, defined as a point at which no new themes or insights emerged from the data25. The pregnant women were chosen to ensure variability in the sample to include those with varied demographic characteristics. Pregnant women diagnosed with GDM living in the Ho Municipality and receiving service in the HTH or the HMH who met the inclusion criteria were purposefully selected. The nurse in charge of the ANC units of the HTH and HMH was a gatekeeper to the recruitment of participants. The gatekeepers only facilitated the identification of the participants and conducted the initial phone call to participants to inform them about the study. Pregnant women were identified through ANC records and contacted via phone call to be informed about the interviews during their next visit. Before contacting potential study participants, the socio-demographic characteristics based on the information in the ANC records were assessed to ensure their eligibility. All eligible participants who were contacted were eventually recruited into the study.

Data collection and analysis

Data collection was done using a semi-structured interview guide that was self-developed based on literature and previous experiences of the researchers in qualitative research. The interview guide covered four sections. Section A explored the sociodemographic characteristics of the pregnant women; Section B explored pregnant women’s knowledge of GDM; Section C examined the perception of pregnant women towards GDM, and Section D assessed the self-care practices adopted by the pregnant women. The antenatal clinic (unit) heads – nurse managers (all were midwives with over 15 years of work experience in maternity care) served as the gatekeepers. The nurse managers facilitated the identification of patients who were diagnosed with GDM and were receiving antenatal care in the unit. The nurse managers of the maternity units in the various hospitals helped to identify study participants and served at least 24 h’ notice through a phone call. The pregnant women were given information about the study, the purpose, confidentiality, privacy of information, protection of participants’ rights, and voluntariness before informed consent was sought. Informed consent (verbal and written) was obtained from the participant after explaining the research objectives, procedures, and ethical considerations.

Interviews were conducted individually with consenting participants, providing a private and comfortable setting for open discussions. Individual interviews were conducted in a private and comfortable setting within the healthcare facilities. Each interview session was audio-recorded, and detailed field notes were also taken during and after each interview to provide additional context. During the interview sessions, participants were allowed to freely express themselves without interference. The individual interview sessions lasted between 30 and 35 min. The patient interviews were conducted in English (10 participants), Ewe (4 participants), Guan (2 participants), and Akan (4 participants). The audio recordings that were conducted in the local languages were given to native speakers to be translated into English for transcription. In the HMH, nine pregnant women were selected, and in the HTH, eleven pregnant women were selected.

The data were analysed using the thematic analysis approach26. During the analysis, researchers consciously set aside their own assumptions, biases, and prior knowledge to study participants’ experiences more objectively27,28. Initially, all interview audio recordings underwent verbatim transcription (and for those in the local languages, it was translated). The use of thematic analysis in Husserlian phenomenological design is appropriate, as we ensured the actual lived experiences of self-care were elicited and not merely patterns. The transcripts were reviewed multiple times to ensure familiarity with the data. Subsequently, a meticulous, systematic coding procedure was implemented, involving multiple researchers thoroughly reviewing and revisiting the transcripts to identify recurring concepts, ideas, and significant statements. The transcripts were coded to identify and discuss the variations in coding to check for intercoder reliability. Code processes were undertaken, during which meaningful units of text were identified and coded. Multiple researchers were involved in the coding process to enhance analytical rigor. The researchers constantly compared codes by referring to the original transcripts26. During the revision of codes, an iterative process was adopted through backward and forward assessment of data, resulting in adjustments and refinement of codes, subthemes, and themes. Similar phrases were grouped into categories, and similar categories were formed into themes. These initial codes were organized into overarching subthemes and then themes. Finally, quotations from participants were selected and presented as examples of their responses. Following the Husserlian phenomenology, the researchers ensured that the generated themes were coherent, insightful, and relevant to the self-care needs and practices of women by continuously reviewing29,30.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the trustworthiness of the study, credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability were addressed31. Credibility was established by prolonged engagement with participants, which involves spending sufficient time in the field to develop rapport before data collection. Member checking was conducted, where three participants were allowed to review and validate the findings after the initial coding. Additionally, peer debriefing was carried out by involving other experienced researchers who critically reviewed the research process, data collection, and analysis32. Moreover, to enhance transferability, a comprehensive description of the study context, including the healthcare facilities and participant characteristics, was provided. Furthermore, confirmability was ensured by maintaining reflexivity throughout the research process. Also, an audit trail of all activities was kept, while reflexive journaling was used to ensure that field experiences were appropriately reported. Researchers critically reflected on their own biases, assumptions, and perspectives that influence data collection and analysis. Additionally, transparency in reporting and clear documentation of the research process further enhanced confirmability33. Also, dependability was achieved by maintaining a rigorous audit trail throughout the research process.

Ethical consideration

The data collection procedure adhered to ethical principles, beginning with receiving ethical approval from the University of Health and Allied Science Research Ethics Committee. This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the institutional ethics review committee (UHAS REC A.5 [67] 23–24) and the Declaration of Helsinki for the conduct of human subjects research34. Participants provided both written and verbal consent before they were recruited for the study. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time without consequence. Confidentiality was ensured through the use of pseudonyms. No financial compensation was provided, although participants’ time and contributions were acknowledged. No respondent received a compensation package of any kind; however, their time and effort were duly acknowledged.

Knowledge of GDM among pregnant women.

Knowledge of GDM among pregnant women.

This theme explored the participants’ knowledge of GDM. The interviews revealed the knowledge of participants about the antecedents, the clinical features, the management, complications, and prevention practices. The sub-themes that emerged included (a) Perceived antecedents of GDM, (b) identifiable presentations of GDM, (c) diagnosis and management of GDM, and (d) Possible complications and prevention of GDM.

Perceived antecedents of GDM.

Perceived antecedents of GDM.

The participants expressed understanding of the concept of GDM, emphasizing that it occurs during pregnancy. This was shown as an elevated blood sugar level occurring during pregnancy. The participants likened GDM to diabetes mellitus (especially type 2) and pointed out that GDM occurs during pregnancy. The results showed that shared understanding (of all the participants) was that GDM is a condition where individuals develop high levels of blood sugar, specifically during pregnancy.

“When you have diabetes during pregnancy, i.e., which is called GDM is like diabetes, but this one occurs during pregnancy.”-p2.

“The disease is just like having diabetes. But this one, you have diabetes due to the pregnancy, or it occurs during the pregnancy”- p20.

Diverse and multiple factors were identified to be associated with the occurrence of GDM. Some participants (70%) were aware of hormonal and genetic predispositions to the occurrence of GDM. The participants expressed knowledge that having a close family relation with diabetes could predispose someone to GDM. There was a familial relationship in the occurrence and presentation of GDM among pregnant women.

" I know family history might play a role in the occurrence of GDM … like it’s something that runs in families or is passed down from one generation to another. … someone in the family had it during pregnancy, so it has been passed to me.“-p19.

“I am sure that family sickness is connected to this whole thing, wondering if it’s because our parents or grandparents had diabetes.“-p6.

Multiple sources were identified as participants’ awareness of GDM. These sources included the mass media, family and friends, and health care professionals. Some participants (40.0%) showed that health care providers are a critical source of information for pregnant women regarding GDM. Health care workers were critical for participants’ identification of GDM as a primary predisposing factor for related complications. However, some (80%) participants could not clearly explain how these predispositions led to the development of GDM.

“The nurses were critical of my understanding of GDM, especially about genetic predisposition. Someone also mentioned that hormones can put you at risk, too.”-p16.

However, there were misconceptions regarding the role of hormones in the development of GDM. Also, others (40%) indicated a lack of knowledge on how the hormonal levels influence the development of GDM. One participant expressed doubt about the hormonal causes of GDM.

“I’m aware hormones are linked to GDM, but honestly, I’m not sure how it all fits together. It’s one of those things that I think I should understand better.“-p7.

“I find it difficult to believe all the things they say could lead to GDM, especially that ‘hormone thing’” -p7.

Some (50%) participants indicated that personal lifestyle and engaging in at-risk behaviors are likely to increase an individual’s likelihood of developing GDM. Many participants (60%) also speculated that personal lifestyle choices, such as diet and lack of exercise, could have been the predisposing factors for developing GDM.

“I’m thinking maybe the way I’ve been eating, like lots of ‘Indomie’ at night, and less physical activity…. It’s just a guess…. ”-p5.

“Sometimes I wonder if not eating enough healthy stuff and sitting around a lot could lead to this GDM …; I think for me, it is the way I eat”-p16.

Some (40%) of the participants thought that external pressures, like high levels of stress (physical and emotional) during pregnancy, could potentially lead to GDM, although they were unclear about the direct mechanisms involved.

“I’ve been wondering if all the stress I’m going through led to this condition. I’m not sure how, but it seems like it could be linked.“-p14.

“I’m thinking that maybe the pressure and worries I have right now could actually lead to GDM, I don’t know, maybe I’m wrong.“-p9.

Other (40%) participants posited that environmental factors, perhaps relating to where they live or work, might have contributed to the risk of GDM, indicating a perception that onset is not solely based on individual behavior.

“I believe that there might be things in my environment, like pollution or something, that could add to the risk of GDM. It feels like it’s not just about the way I live my life. Maybe it hasn’t been found yet.“-p1.

“I’ve been thinking, maybe it’s not all on me. Perhaps where I live, or the stuff I’m exposed to at work might have a role”-p14.

b. Identifiable presentations.

Identifiable presentations.

The participants based the descriptions of the manifestations of diabetes on personal experiences. Many (60%) participants attributed excessive thirst and frequent urination to pregnancy, only to discover it was a symptom of GDM. The pregnant women expressed difficulty in distinguishing between the manifestations of normal pregnancy and those associated with GDM.

“I remember when I used to feel thirsty and urinate every time. I kept thinking, ‘I’m pregnant, so I have to be urinating a lot,’ and I will feel thirsty too. But I realized that it was not just pregnancy but something else.”-p8.

“I felt so tired all the time. But it was the tests that we had during ANC that confirmed GDM. It doesn’t make sense to me, when you are pregnant, you feel tired, how am I supposed to tell the difference between a normal pregnancy and when you have GDM?”-p3.

Other participants (40%) showed a link between excessive foetal growth and the development of GDM. They were informed that excessive growth of the foetus could result in GDM, but did not understand the pathophysiological processes that led to that.

“I’ve got it, and I’ve heard it can make the baby big, but I don’t really know if it’s true. But I’ve been hearing them say that when you give birth, and the baby is big, then it’s because you had diabetes”-p15.

Regarding the source of information on the manifestations of GDM, participants mentioned that healthcare providers are the primary source of information. Some participants (50%) highlighted that the ANC visits are the major places where vital information about pregnancy, including pregnancy-related diseases, such as GDM, is obtained.

“For me, I learn all these kinds of things when I come for the antenatal. …during the teachings that I heard about GDM.“-p3.

“I trust the nurses to give me the right information.“-p8.

c.Diagnosis and management.

Diagnosis and management.

Most participants (70%) showed that a GDM diagnosis ought to be done during early pregnancy screening. They mentioned that diagnosis occurs during pregnancy, emphasizing a recognition of the importance of timely GDM screening. Some participants (40%) with GDM showed that diagnosis is usually done only during pregnancy, and any other sugar elevation other than during pregnancy is considered diabetes mellitus

“It was somewhere during my first visit to the hospital, the ANC. It’s like, sometimes during the pregnancy, they check for it.“-p4.

“I knew about GDM testing during pregnancy, it’s part of the diagnostic investigation they run for pregnant women. It is done to help identify anything that could be harmful to both the mother and the unborn baby”-p12.

However, a few participants (20%) believed that GDM is usually discovered later in pregnancy, if symptoms and risks become more apparent as the pregnancy progresses.

“It shows up later because, you know, as the baby grows, things get complicated. It is during the last months, when you are about to give birth, …. I didn’t realize it could happen earlier.”-p9.

“You only find out about GDM in the sixth month, going down. But now, I see it’s not like that. It was a surprise for me to know it can happen earlier, too.“-p1.

Concerning the management, most participants stated that diet plays a role in managing GDM. They understood that GDM patients will have to watch what they eat, suggesting the importance of a balanced diet.

“Since I got the diagnosis, it’s been all about the type of food I eat and the way I eat it. I have to be careful about everything I put in my mouth, making sure it’s balanced for the baby and me, and not increasing my blood sugar.“-p2.

“Now I know that with GDM, you can’t just eat whatever. It’s about managing your diet but still getting enough nutrition. “-p16.

Other participants indicated that engaging in regular exercise is beneficial for the management of GDM. They expressed that incorporating physical activity into their routine contributes positively to controlling blood sugar levels and overall well-being during pregnancy.

“I’ve heard getting regular exercise can really help with the sugar levels, so I’ve started taking walks every day.“-p12.

“Staying active is good for controlling GDM, so I’ve been trying to do some walking exercises when I can—it makes me feel better too.“-p17.

Some participants (45%) were conscious of the fact that GDM requires regular check-ups, supposing that constant monitoring would ensure the health of the mother and baby. They mentioned follow-up visits and monitoring as important after a GDM diagnosis. The participants (45%) showed during ANC visits that any changes to the mother or the baby can be detected early and immediate interventions instituted.

“I understand that after you have been diagnosed with GDM, you have to go for more reviews. “-p10.

“With conditions like this, the check-ups and monitoring become part of your routine”-p7.

A few participants (15%) had heard of oral medications being used to control blood sugar levels for GDM. They were familiar with the concept of pill-based treatments for diabetes but were not sure if these were applicable in the context of pregnancy.

“I’ve heard about medicines for diabetes and so for GDM, but I don’t know if they’re okay during pregnancy”-p13.

“Yeah, I know some people take medicines for diabetes. In this case, with gestational diabetes, which is during pregnancy, won’t put my baby and me at risk?“-p3.

d.Possible complications and prevention.

Possible complications and prevention.

Most participants (65%) indicated that, per their understanding, GDM could result in larger babies. They were aware that the newborns might face certain health issues, such as hyperglycemia.

“I knew GDM might make my baby bigger. “-p16.

“I was aware that babies born to mothers with GDM might have some health issues. “-p12.

One of the participants mentioned GDM could lead to breathing difficulties for the baby, like respiratory distress syndrome. The participant indicated that this can result in fetal asphyxia and subsequently detrimental consequences on the baby during birth

" I heard from others that if I don’t manage it well, it will make it hard for my baby to breathe when born. My friend shared her experience, saying that GDM can make my baby have trouble breathing at the beginning. It scared me, because why would I go through nine months of suffering and end up losing my baby? But now I’m more careful about what I eat and making sure I keep things under control.“-p2.

Another participant speculated that adhering diligently to advice from the antenatal clinic to manage GDM helps to prevent any complications and ensure the baby’s healthy development.

“I learned that GDM could make the baby get too big, so I made sure to follow all the advice they gave us at the antenatal [clinic]. “-p16.

Some of the participants (20%) expressed the desire to prevent these complications that their babies could suffer.

I don’t want that for me, so I try my best to follow the advice to prevent the disease “-p16.

“I’ve heard that the sooner you know about a problem in pregnancy, the better”-p12.

2.Concerns related to the management of GDM.

Concerns related to the management of GDM.

The second theme presented the concerns and perceptions of participants towards GDM, highlighting perceived impacts on pregnancy, concerns, and thoughts about the management and the adequacy of treatment. The perception of participants concerning GDM varied and ranged from negative to positive perceptions. The participants perceived that GDM would hurt the pregnancy and the resultant outcome, especially if the woman did not control her overall blood sugar levels. The participants also highlighted concerns related to the nature of presentation of GDM and the level of adequacy of the treatments. The sub-themes that emerged included (a) perceptive impact on pregnancy, (b) concerns and stress of managing GDM, and (c) treatment adequacy.

Perceived impact on pregnancy.

Perceived impact on pregnancy.

Some participants (75%) showed a relatively negative perception about the ability to manage GDM, expressing a lack of confidence in mitigating the health risks, feeling overwhelmed by the negative prospect of potential complications. Participants expressed inadequacy and insufficiency in grading the ability to control and manage GDM.

“It’s hard to believe I can protect the baby when there are so many things that could go wrong.“-p19.

“There’s a lot to think about, and it just feels like too much. All the things they advise me to do, hmm….“-p3.

Some participants (55%) were worried about the long-term consequences of having GDM. Participants envisaged that this may become a permanent situation after the birth of the baby. Most participants believed that GDM would inevitably become Type 2 diabetes later, fearing a permanent diagnosis.

“It feels like having GDM now …. I will definitely get Type 2 diabetes.“-p1.

“I’m always worried that this GDM isn’t just for now. It’s hard not to think of this condition as a permanent thing.“-p12.

Other participants (25%) were concerned about the long-term consequences of the diseases, especially on the method of birth. The general perception was that having GDM makes the baby big and, consequently, can influence the ability to have a spontaneous vaginal birth. Some participants reported that GDM means they will have to have a cesarean section, associating the condition directly with surgical intervention.

“I can’t stop thinking that I’m going to end up with a CS. I always thought that I would have a normal vaginal birth. …. I’m afraid of having surgery.“-p15.

“I’m worried because having other conditions during pregnancy means I won’t be able to have my baby the natural way.“-p8.

Other participants (20%) described a pervasive sense of stress, resulting not only from managing the daily monitoring and treatment of GDM but also from the uncertainty of its impact on overall health. The rigorous nature of monitoring and taking treatment or medication with medical follow-up is critical for the concerns of participants.

“I am always stressed, not just because I have to keep checking my blood sugars and watch what I eat, but also because I don’t know what it’s going to bring “-p18.

“I’m struggling with so much stress these days. It’s a lot of work managing the disease”-p2.

b. Interests in managing GDM.

Interests in managing GDM.

There were divergent opinions regarding the level of participation in managing the blood sugar levels. Many expressed how managing GDM has significantly disrupted daily routines, with constant attention to diet, exercise, and monitoring making them feel seriously concerned. The continued experience of complications and symptoms was also an important worry among participants

“Managing GDM has turned my daily life upside down. I must be so careful with my diet, make sure I do exercise, and check my blood sugar.“-p7.

" I’m always concerned about my food, when and how to exercise, and keeping records of blood glucose levels. I don’t enjoy being pregnant; it’s like I’m just living from one medical problem to the next.“-p14.

In other participants, living with GDM made them express anger and frustration. Some participants expressed irritation with the extensive list of dietary restrictions, lamenting the loss of enjoying favorite foods and the added stress of meal planning and preparation.

“They said ‘Hausa koko’ is not good for me, I should not eat plenty, if I eat fufu, it should be small or dry fish with a lot of soup. And I should drink oats, wheat, and no sugar bread, but brown bread. I’m not happy about how many foods I can’t eat now. I miss just having my favorite food.“-p1.

“It is frustrating having so many rules about what I can and can’t eat. Planning every meal and cooking special stuff all the time is stressful, and I really miss just enjoying food like I used to.“-p12.

Most participants (60%) found the regular blood glucose monitoring to be a taxing routine, with some viewing the finger-pricking process as invasive and anxiety-inducing.

“Pricking my finger several times is painful and stressful.”-p7.

“Most of the time, the whole routine of testing my blood sugar is just too much. It’s annoying to have to interrupt my day all the time for finger pricks. It’s not just the physical part that worries me; it really gets to you mentally, too.“-p19.

Other participants (35%) expressed concern about the financial impact of managing GDM, highlighting the added expenses of specialized diet ingredients, monitoring kits, and additional healthcare services that strained household budgets.

“I’m worried about how much it’s costing to manage this GDM. Between the special foods I have to buy, the glucose testing kits, and all the extra review visits, it puts pressure on the family budget.“-p13.

" It’s not just the usual groceries anymore; it’s all these pricey diet-specific items, plus the cost of glucose monitors and the healthcare stuff that’s needed.“-p18.

A few participants (15%) indicated distrust towards the health information about GDM, doubting whether the lifestyle changes recommended would make a significant difference in the health outcomes.

“I’m not sure if I trust all this advice about eating different foods and exercising. Do these changes really help my baby and me? I’m doing my best to follow them, but I still worry that it might not be enough.“-p6.

“I keep hearing about how I need to eat better, but honestly, I wonder if it’s really going to make things better. It’s hard to believe all these rules will make a difference”-p11.

c.Treatment adequacy.

Treatment adequacy.

Many participants expressed contentment with their treatment plans, feeling that the guidance and support from their healthcare providers were personalized and effective. This afforded them a sense of control over pregnancy and possible outcomes.

“I’m actually really happy with the plan. The nurses have been great about making sure everything’s tailored to me. I feel like I’ve got a good handle on things”-p12.

“I felt really supported by the nurses, and the personalized advice …. It’s made a big difference, and I’m feeling pretty good about where we’re at with everything.“-p7.

Some individuals (40%), however, voiced dissatisfaction with the one-size-fits-all approach they perceived in treatment. Participants lamented a lack of consideration for personal circumstances and preferences.

“I’m not happy with how the nurses are helping me. It’s like they’ve got a one-plan for all and are not really looking at what I need.“-p4.

“The way they want me to manage the condition isn’t working for me. It feels like they didn’t plan just for me. It’s like they have planned something that has worked for them, and so, they think it will work for all.“-p6.

Other participants (35%) felt that while healthcare professionals were accessible, their brief interactions did not allow for thorough discussions or the provision of detailed advice, leaving them to seek out additional information independently.

“The doctors and nurses are there, yes, but those ‘sharp talks’ we have are not enough. I end up having a lot of questions and needing to look up more information”-p2.

“It’s nice that I can reach the midwives easily, but our talks are always in a rush. They don’t really get into the details of what I should be doing, what is specific to me”- p10.

Participants identified the cost and inconvenience of obtaining medications and monitoring supplies as significant barriers, causing them stress and making it difficult to consistently maintain tight glucose control.

“Getting all the medicines and test stuff is not only a pain, but it’s also draining”-p1.

“The stress of dealing with the high costs and how to get my medication and strips is really getting to me”-p7.

3.Self-care practices for GDM.

Self-care practices for GDM.

This theme showed the self-care practices adopted by pregnant women with GDM and showed insights into the daily management, the motivators and strategies used in adhering to the self-care practices, and the involvement of healthcare professionals in these practices. The subthemes that were identified included (a) the multifaceted daily management needs, (b) opportunities and strategies, and (c) health professional involvement.

The multifaceted daily management needs.

The multifaceted daily management needs.

The daily needs of patients were identified to be multifaceted, requiring patients to juggle through daily activities of care. One critical component of this care need was the requirement of physical activity. This multifaceted need requires physical activity, keeping to a rigorous care schedule, continuous daily monitoring of blood sugar levels, monitoring of diet, ensuring proper eating habits, and ensuring medication adherence. One critical component of the self-care practices adopted by participants (60%) was daily walking and physical activity.

“I know exercise is supposed to be good when pregnant, and I think it helps with avoiding diabetes too”-p1.

“I know I need to keep active, especially now. It’s a straightforward way to stay on top of my health, and it does feel good.“-p11.

Some pregnant women (15%) showed that they monitored their blood glucose levels, as prescribed, using home glucose meters and meticulously logging the details for healthcare providers’ review.

“I have to use the glucose meter always and write down every number to show to the midwives.“-p10.

“I’m always using my glucose meter to keep track of my sugar levels. It’s become a part of my routine …. I show the recordings to the healthcare team.“-p7.

However, the challenge of monitoring blood sugar had to do with fear of needles and associated anxiety from always testing. A few participants (10%) experienced emotional distress related to frequent monitoring, citing anxiety around testing, fear of needles, and stress from the constant reminder of the GDM diagnosis.

“The constant need to check my blood sugar is too much. I feel anxious every time, and the pricking never gets easier. It’s a constant reminder that I have GDM.“-p3.

“Having to test my blood so much is tough. I don’t like needles; nobody likes them, and doing it all the time makes me anxious. It’s also like I can’t escape thinking about the condition.“-p8.

Some participants (50%) faced challenges in adhering to dietary recommendations due to cravings and the social pressures of eating out, which often conflicted with the strict dietary regime required for GDM.

“Sticking to the diet for GDM is hard “-p16.

“I find it really difficult to follow all the diet rules with GDM, especially when I’ve got these strong urges to eat certain foods”-p12.

Another important component of self-care is to adhere to medical appointments and reviews. Most participants remained committed to attending their scheduled hospital appointments, recognizing the critical importance of regular reviews.

“I visit the antenatal clinic for check-ups. It might not be the most convenient thing”-p19.

“I haven’t missed any appointments at the hospital because I understand they’re key in managing GDM.“-p14.

Other individuals (40%) struggled with the routine of medication adherence, sometimes forgetting doses or finding it difficult to integrate this into their daily lives, especially in the case of multiple or complex medication schedules.

“There are days I just forget to take my medications, and when you have a lot of different medicines, it gets even harder to keep everything straight”-p8.

“I won’t lie, keeping up with all the medications, I can give you all the problems that come with it. Sometimes I forget to take the medications.“-p13.

b.Opportunities and strategies.

Opportunities and strategies.

This subtheme indicated the opportunities and strategies used by pregnant women with diabetes to cope with the disease. Many participants found the primary source of motivation in the desire to have a healthy pregnancy and baby. Personal reasons were identified as drivers for adherence to their GDM management plans, despite the challenges. The strategies commonly used by pregnant women were to ensure prolonged medication adherence, appropriate meal planning, use of family support systems, and leverage the support of spouses.

“My biggest motivator is wanting a healthy pregnancy and a healthy baby. That’s why I stick to everything I am told to do.“-p1.

“The main thing that keeps me is the thought of my baby being healthy.“-p5.

A few individuals (25%) focused on establishing habits for long-term adherence, considering the management of GDM as a stepping stone towards adopting a healthier lifestyle beyond pregnancy.

“I’m taking this GDM management as an opportunity to build habits that last way beyond pregnancy.“-p12.

“I see dealing with GDM not just as a short-term thing but as a first step, even after the pregnancy.“-p15.

Other participants (20%) appreciated the practical ways in which family members contributed, like assisting in household chores and providing psychological support and encouragement.

“I’m grateful for how my family is doing extra chores so I can take a break or get some exercise. It’s really helped lower my stress and made me feel better”-p14.

“It’s been a huge relief having my family pitch in. Their help gives me the time I need to focus on resting or staying active.“-p3.

Some individuals (20%) noted that spouses’ active involvement in attending appointments and helping with meal planning and preparation provided a sense of partnership that made the process more manageable.

“My husband is really involved, coming with me to appointments and getting hands-on with planning and making meals.“-p7.

“Having my husband’s support in this whole GDM journey has been amazing. He’s there for every doctor’s visit and helps with the food stuff. It’s teamwork.“-p11.

Many participants (70%) highlighted the importance of having a strong support system, including family, friends, and healthcare providers, which positively influenced their ability to adhere to self-care practices.

“Having a good support network has been key for me. My family, friends, encourage me and help me stick with the routines of care.“-p1.

“I can’t overstate how much my support system has helped me with staying on track. Close ones and my colleague healthcare providers, offer help.“-p7.

Having a structured educational system was critical for promoting self-care adoption practices. Participants who had access to well-structured educational programs expressed greater confidence and adherence. Clear information on GDM, its management, and the rationale behind specific practices empowered participants.

“Being a part of a structured education program for GDM has made an impact. I feel more confident because I understand what’s happening and why I need to do certain things”-p19.

“Having access to good educational resources has really helped me feel in control”-p12.

Some participants (35%) who successfully integrated self-care practices into daily routines found it easier to adhere. Establishing habits, such as regular exercise and meal planning, enhanced the feasibility of recommended practices.

“Once I got into the rhythm of my daily routine, fitting in the management of GDM got easier. Making habits out of things like exercise and planning my meals has improved”-p14.

“After I managed to turn the self-care steps for GDM into regular habits, everything fell into place. Having a set time for walks and preparing meals ahead of time means I’m not constantly trying to figure out when to do these things—it’s just automatic now”-p10.

c.Healthcare professional involvement.

Healthcare professional involvement.

The involvement of health care providers and the detailed health information provided were identified as important in promoting clients’ adoption of a healthy lifestyle and managing blood sugar. Most participants appreciated the range of educational materials provided, such as brochures and picture illustrations, which helped to demystify GDM and offered practical advice for daily management.

“The pamphlets and pictures improved my understanding. The health information they offer really helps with managing it day-to-day.“-p14.

“The information we got, with all the brochures and visual aids, has been a big help. It explained what it is and how to handle it”-p19.

As health information is critical, there were challenges in the ability to comprehending health information. Some pregnant women expressed frustration with the clarity of self-care guidelines. The pregnant women indicated that the health information is too complex or too generalized, leading to confusion and uncertainty.

“Sometimes the guidelines of the condition are just too much. They’re either really complicated or too broad, and it just leaves me confused”-p6.

“I’ve been frustrated with the instructions for self-care. They’re not clear enough; it feels like they’re packed with complex medical information, either. I just end up with more questions than answers.“-p2.

Some participants (50%) underscored the value of continuous involvement from healthcare providers who actively monitored progress and made timely suggestions to improve individual care plans throughout the pregnancy.

“I really value the ongoing support from the health care providers. They keep a close eye on how I’m doing and are quick to offer advice to adjust the care”-p7.

“Having healthcare providers who are really involved and responsive makes a huge difference. They track my progress closely and adjust my care as needed.“-p12.

d.Challenges in adherence to self-care practices.

Challenges in adherence to self-care practices.

Multiple challenges were identified that influence the adoption and sustained use of self-care practices. Many participants identified time limitations as a significant challenge in adhering to self-care practices. Balancing medical appointments, dietary considerations, and lifestyle changes within busy schedules proved difficult.

“I have challenges trying to manage all the antenatal visits, plan out my special diet, and make time for exercise”-p19.

“With work and family, squeezing in time for my health, like daily glucose monitoring and meal prepping, is a major challenge”-p2.

Several participants (60%) expressed challenges related to emotional and psychological aspects, including stress, anxiety, and the emotional toll of managing GDM. Emotional well-being emerged as a key factor influencing adherence.

“I feel stressed and anxious a lot. It’s like my emotional state is playing a big role in how well I manage this condition.“-p4.

“Sometimes, the hardest part of having this isn’t the diet or the glucose checks—it’s the emotional weight itself.“-p20.

Some participants faced challenges due to a lack of comprehensive knowledge about GDM, its management, and the importance of certain self-care practices. This lack of awareness hindered the ability to make informed choices.

“I feel like I still don’t know enough about GDM. That lack of understanding makes it tough to make the right decisions of care.“-p6.

“I’m stumbling a bit with myself… not fully understanding why I’m doing all these self-care practices. I just wish I knew more so I could be sure I’m doing the best.“-p13.

Other participants (40%) reported difficulties in navigating social situations, family expectations, and cultural norms that sometimes conflicted with recommended self-care practices.

“I’ve run into some tough spots where what my family expects at meals or gatherings doesn’t match up with the ones I’m supposed to adhere to. It’s hard to explain why I can’t eat certain foods or need to step away to check my blood sugar without causing disappointment.“-p20.

Discussion

This study assessed the utilization of self-care practices among women with GDM in a low-resource setting in Ghana. Self-care practices for GDM are critical for the effective control of pregnancy and promote pregnancy outcomes for both mother and baby35,36. Many participants could not clearly explain the factors predisposing to the risk of GDM. Identification of GDM risk factors is critical for primary adoption of prevention measures and early detection and control37. Consequently, comprehensive education and awareness campaigns must be instituted to improve pregnant women’s knowledge of the complex interplay between genetics, familial predisposition, and the risk of developing GDM. In other developing countries, pregnant women link GDM and hormonal and genetic predispositions, but do not know how these factors contribute to the risk19,38. Therefore, in enhancing knowledge in the management of GDM, healthcare providers must empower pregnant women to make informed decisions and adopt preventive measures. This level of knowledge is critical for the adoption of self-care practices for the control and prevention of GDM15,36. This is particularly important because in low-resource settings, critical variations have been identified in women’s knowledge relative to GDM39. In Uganda, Southern Nigeria, and Cameroon, most of the pregnant women were not aware of the use of oral medications for controlling blood sugar levels during pregnancy19,40,41.

Participants’ knowledge of the prevention of diabetes, specifically GDM, revealed a positive recognition of the importance of early screening. In other sub-Saharan African countries, pregnant women were aware of the significance of early detection10,42,43. The positive perception of early screening highlights the importance of education and awareness initiatives in promoting preventive healthcare practices during pregnancy. However, this education must continuously focus on women’s understanding by providing critical, detailed information related to the trend and behavior of the disease. By fostering a better understanding of the benefits of early detection and intervention, healthcare providers can empower pregnant women to prioritize prenatal screening for GDM and take proactive steps towards optimizing maternal and fetal well-being. In Ghana already, blood sugar screening is a primary investigation for all pregnant women, but the overall detailed knowledge on diverses perspective of the disease remains low44,45.

Pregnant women expressed a poor perception of their capabilities in adopting self-care practices. This conveys feelings of inadequacy and lack of confidence in effectively mitigating the health risks. Inadequacy and lack of confidence in effectively mitigating the health risks are usually related to having a relatively poor perception of the disease and relative risk17,46. Social perception and overall lay knowledge about a disease are critical contributors to the adoption of management and preventive practices. It is imperative to address the psychosocial aspects alongside the medical management of pregnant women with GDM. This is because managing GDM requires constant attention to diet, exercise, and monitoring, leading to significant disruptions in daily routines47,48. The shared challenges inherent in GDM management, coupled with the emotional and practical implications of navigating pregnancy, could lead to disruption to daily routines. The pregnant women expressed satisfaction with treatment, citing personalized guidance and support from healthcare providers as effective. Health care providers play a critical role in supporting pregnant women to adopt sustained self-care practices for effective sugar control49. Consequently, health care interventions need to be tailored to address specific self-care barriers. However, some pregnant women indicated dissatisfaction with the perceived one-size-fits-all approach for service provision. In other areas, pregnant women had a perceived dissatisfaction with the same care being provided, and neglecting individuality50. The perception of a one-size-fits-all approach reflects a disconnect between the treatment plans provided and the unique needs and preferences of individual patients. Healthcare providers should prioritize patient-centered care and actively involve pregnant women’s specific needs18,19,38. This may involve conducting thorough assessments of patients’ medical history, lifestyle factors, cultural beliefs, and individual preferences to tailor treatment.

Fostering open communication and providing opportunities for patients to express their concerns, preferences, and goals can enhance treatment satisfaction and promote positive outcomes in GDM management. Self-care practices, being a critical component, require GDM patients to meticulously control their diet. However, some participants encountered difficulties in adhering to the prescribed dietary regime due to cravings and social pressures. Previous studies identified dietary requirements as a critical challenge for adoption among pregnant women, especially in culturally inclined lower-resource settings51–54. Managing cravings while adhering to a strict dietary regimen requires significant discipline and self-control. Consequently, addressing these challenges requires a multifaceted approach that encompasses education, support, and coping strategies. By offering practical tips for managing cravings, navigating social situations, and making healthier food choices when dining out, healthcare providers can empower women to make informed decisions about their dietary intake. This study highlights the intrinsic personal factors that drive adherence to GDM management plans and the adoption of self-care practices despite the challenges. Pregnant women are usually motivated to adopt self-care practices for diabetes control because of the intrinsic motivation to have a healthy birth and baby48,55,56. The desire for a healthy pregnancy and baby reflects the deeply held values, emphasizing the profound maternal instinct to prioritize maternal health. Healthcare providers must recognize and leverage the intrinsic motivation of pregnant women. The intrinsic motivation entails tailoring support services to reinforce pregnant women’s roles in ensuring the health of the baby through personalized counseling, provision of educational materials, and encouraging peer support groups57. Many participants identified time limitations as a significant challenge in adhering to self-care practices. In addition, tailored interventions that recognize time constraints and offer practical solutions to facilitate adherence to GDM self-care must be adopted36,58. This can be done by leveraging the critical role of family, friends, and social support, which were identified as major facilitators to GDM management among pregnant women. A supportive environment can provide encouragement, motivation, and assistance with implementing self-care practices such as dietary modifications, physical activity, and glucose monitoring.

Implications

This study highlighted diverse and multiple factors associated with the adoption of self-care practices for GDM among pregnant women. Given the multiplicity of these factors, it is incumbent upon health care providers to tackle the challenge of GDM; tailored, cost-effective, culturally sensitive interventions must be developed and tested for implementation in low-resource settings44,59,60. Also, longitudinal studies should focus on specific life modification practices adopted by women for control by highlighting those that have proven useful in resource-limited settings61,62. As some progress has been made in improve matenal health indicators, the specific knowledge variables relative to GDM are still limited. Consequently, acknowledging the contributing factors, such as the physical and social environment, is important for promoting safe self-care practices. Also, healthcare workers must accept the influence of GDM on pregnancies and maternal health outcomes. The routine screening of pregnant women for GDM in Ghana ought to be strengthened for early detection and prompt management. Also, tailored health education that addresses pregnant women’s specific concerns can improve the adoption of self-care practices.

Strength and limitations

This study highlighted the lived experiences and the adoption of self-care practices among pregnant women in resource-limited settings. The study findings add to the body of knowledge on maternal health and provide a basis for researchers to delve into this terrain to develop targeted interventions. Using the Husserlian phenomenological research design allowed for reflexivity and independent exploration of the lived experiences of pregnant women. However, there were some limitations in this study. Firstly, the study, even though it used the principles of data saturation, did not consider the perspective of health workers, especially nurses and midwives. This perspective could have added a layer for comparisons of knowledge and adoption of self-care practices47,48. For example, there could have been health systems challenges, like the influence of nurses and midwives’ counselling, antenatal attendance, and the nature of protocols critical for diabetes self-care adoption. Also, the findings of this study were not organised around a specific research framework or model, limiting its role in testing the application of previous theories in the management GDM63. Using a conceptual framework could have allowed for analysing the problem using a multi-dimensional approach to gain a deeper understanding of the associated factors. Another important limitation of this study was the fact that the participants were all from a single geographical location in Ghana. This can have an influence on the access to resources, locally available foods, and cultural unanimity in how health information is communicated, therefore limiting the variation in perspective. This was overcome by ensuring multiple variability in the sampling procedure. Further, the participants were asked to self-report, and this could be influenced by recall and social desirability bias. The researchers continuously encouraged the participants to give correct and accurate responses to promote in-depth understanding.

Conclusion

This study provided a comprehensive understanding of self-care practices among pregnant women with GDM, highlighting the critical role of knowledge, perception, and support systems. Family and healthcare provider support emerged as key facilitators, while time constraints and emotional stress were significant barriers to effective self-care. Consequently, efforts to enhance patient education, promote multidisciplinary collaboration, and implement supportive strategies are crucial in optimizing outcomes and ensuring the well-being of both mother and baby. Nursing education programs should prioritize comprehensive instruction on GDM self-care. Future research should focus on investigating innovative approaches to GDM care, evaluating the effectiveness of interventions aimed at improving patient education and self-management skills, and exploring the impact of social determinants of health on GDM outcomes.
