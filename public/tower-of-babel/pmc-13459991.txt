Effect Of Intraarticular Triamcinolone Hexacetonide versus Betamethasone on Intraocular Pressure: A Prospective Randomized Study
Authors: Hilal Yağar, Kadir Eren Biçer, Gamze Yıldırım Biçer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCorticosteroids may cause glaucoma by increasing intraocular pressure (IOP) due to their systemic side effects. Few studies have investigated the effect of intraarticular corticosteroids (IACS) on IOP. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of IACS preparations (betamethasone and triamcinolone hexacetonide) on IOP.MethodsIn this single-center randomized study, patients receiving IACS were randomized into two groups: Group 1 received betamethasone (Diprospan®, betamethasone dipropionate 6.43 mg + betamethasone sodium phosphate 2.63 mg, equivalent to 7.0 mg betamethasone) and Group 2 received triamcinolone hexacetonide (Artropan®,20 mg triamcinolone hexacetonide). IOP was measured before injection and at 1 week after injection. The change in IOP (ΔIOP) was calculated using the following formula: IOP (after injection) – IOP (before injection).ResultsA total of 80 patients and 160 eyes were included in the study, with 40 patients in each group. No significant change was found in ΔIOP in Group 1 (p > 0.05). A statistically significant increase in ΔIOP was observed in both eyes and in the left eye in Group 2; (both eyes: p = 0.002; left eye: p = 0.012; right eye:p = 0.060). ΔIOP was significantly higher in Group 2 than in Group 1 (p = 0.043).ConclusionTriamcinolone hexacetonide may increase IOP after IACS more than betamethasone; caution is warranted in patients with risk factors for glaucoma.Trial RegistrationThe trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov retrospectively (No: NCT07242846, Date: 17.11.2025).

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis is a disease characterized by degeneration and mechanical wear in the joint following damage to the knee joint cartilage.1 Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and physical therapy modalities are recommended as first-line treatment for knee osteoarthritis. Also, intra-articular corticosteroid (IACS) injections are recommended for patients whose pain persists despite first-line treatment.2 Among their systemic effects, corticosteroids (CSs) can raise intraocular pressure (IOP), predisposing patients to glaucoma.3 Ongoing anticoagulant therapy, presence of intra-articular (IA) infection, and lack of blood sugar regulation are important contraindications for IACS injection.4 However, high-quality studies on whether IACS is contraindicated for patients with high IOP or whether follow-ups should be performed due to the effect of IACS on IOP are lacking. Moreover, information regarding the changes in IOP following various CS preparations injected into the shoulder and knee is conflicting.5,6 Although intermediate- to long-acting corticosteroid preparations are commonly used for intra-articular injections in knee osteoarthritis, betamethasone and triamcinolone are among the commonly preferred agents because of their anti-inflammatory effects and clinical availability.7 These agents differ in formulation, solubility, and expected duration of local effect; however, comparative evidence regarding the relative efficacy and duration of different intra-articular corticosteroids remains limited.7

The aim of this study was to demonstrate the effects of IA betamethasone and triamcinolone hexacetonide injections on IOP.

Materials and Methods

This prospective randomized study was conducted at the Orthopedics and Traumatology Clinic of Niğde Ömer Halisdemir University Training and Research Hospital between September and November 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Nigde Omer Halisdemir University, Faculty of Medicine, Institutional Review Board (No:2025/55). The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed while conducting the study. Before starting the study, all patients were provided with detailed information, and their informed consent forms were obtained based on the principle of voluntariness. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov retrospectively (No: NCT07242846, Date: 17.11.2025). This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Patients aged 18–75 years who had been clinically and radiologically diagnosed with symptomatic early-stage osteoarthritis (Kellgren lawrence grade 1–2), who had not responded to first-line treatment, and who were scheduled to receive IACS injection were included in the study.

Inclusion Criteria

Symptomatic early-stage knee osteoarthritis (Kellgren lawrence grade 1–2)

Patients aged 18–75 years

No prior eye surgery

No history of elevated IOP or glaucoma diagnosis

Patients not receiving any medical treatment altering IOP

Exclusion Criteria

IOP >21 mm Hg

Patients with a previous or current diagnosis of glaucoma

Patients aged <18 and >75 years

History of or ongoing ocular disease

Use of medications (oral CSs, diazoxide, etc.) altering IOP

The following commonly used intermediate- to long-acting CS preparations were employed in the study: 20 mg triamcinolone hexacetonide (Artropan®) and betamethasone dipropionate: 6.43 mg (equivalent to 5.0 mg betamethasone) and betamethasone sodium phosphate: 2.63 mg (equivalent to 2.0 mg betamethasone) (Diprospan®). No additional medication was administered during the IACS procedure. IOP was measured using a noncontact tonometer combined with a pachymeter, Canon TX-20P (Canon Medical Systems Europe B.V., Zoetermeer, the Netherlands). IOP was measured immediately before the IA injection and 1 week after the injection. IOP measurements for all patients were taken between 10:00 a.m. and 12:00 p.m. to avoid circadian rhythm effects.

Randomization was performed before patient enrollment by an independent physician(KEB) using a drawing-lots method in a 1:1 ratio. The allocation sequence was kept by the independent physician. The ophthalmologist who performed the IOP measurements (GYB) was blinded to the treatment allocation. All intra-articular knee injections were performed by the same orthopedic surgeon(HY) using the inferolateral landmark-guided approach, without ultrasound guidance.

Patients’ demographic data and IOP values for both eyes measured immediately before IA injection and at 1 week after IA injection were recorded.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation along with descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. Change(s) in IOP (ΔIOP) were calculated using the following formula: ΔIOP = IOP (after injection) – IOP (before injection). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine whether all continuous data were normally distributed. The test results showed that the continuous variables were not normally distributed. Therefore, the Mann–Whitney U-test (U) was used to evaluate ΔIOP in both drug groups. The change in IOP before and after injection in both drug groups was examined using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (T) in conjunction with the Hodges–Lehmann median difference estimate (HL) and effect size (r). Also, the chi-square test and Mann–Whitney U-test were used to assess the similarity of demographic data (age and sex) between groups. The ΔIOP, depending on age, was evaluated using the Spearman correlation analysis. The sample size to be included in this study was determined to be at least 39 for each group using the G*Power program, based on type I error (alpha 0.05), confidence (1–alpha 0.95), type II error (beta 0.20), test power (1–beta 0.80), and effect size (d, 0.64). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data were entered into an Excel database (Microsoft Office 2010, WA, USA), and statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics Version 22, SPSS Inc., IL, USA).

Results

The study included 80 patients and 160 eyes. The mean age of the patients was 62.65 ± 9.39 years. Of the patients, 73.8% were women (n = 59) and 26.2% were men (n = 21). Patients who received IA betamethasone dipropionate: 6.43 mg (equivalent to 5.0 mg betamethasone) and betamethasone sodium phosphate: 2.63 mg (equivalent to 2.0 mg betamethasone) (Diprospan®) injections were classified as Group 1, and those who received IA triamcinolone hexacetonide(Artropan®) injections were categorized as Group 2. The study included 40 patients from each group.

No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of age (p = 0.12) and sex (p = 0.611). Discussion

IACS injections effectively relieve pain and improve function while treating knee osteoarthritis.8 However, despite their IA administration, CSs can exert systemic effects.9,10 The present study also examined the effects of CSs on IOP, a systemic side effect often overlooked after IA administration. It was novel in demonstrating that the effect of CSs on IOP might vary between different preparations.

Taliaferro et al reported that IA triamcinolone injection into the knee resulted in an average IOP increase of 2.7 mm Hg after the first week. They also noted that 29% of patients experienced an increase greater than 7 mm Hg.5 Similarly, the present study found an average increase of 1.41 mm Hg in IOP with triamcinolone. Engin et al found no significant difference in IOP changes on day 1 and in week 1 after subacromial betamethasone injection.6 Similarly, the present study did not find a significant increase in IOP after IA betamethasone administration. However, a comparison of both studies revealed that the injection site was different, and the differences in the locally applied sites could alter the systemic effects of CSs.

The systemic effects of glucocorticoids following IA administration depend on their molecular solubility and pharmacokinetic properties.11 Triamcinolone hexacetonide has a longer systemic effect following IA injection due to its low solubility.12,13 Stout et al discovered that less soluble CSs remained at the injection site for a longer time, resulting in longer-lasting local and systemic effects.10 A meta-analysis reported that triamcinolone hexacetonide was superior to betamethasone in terms of efficacy in the first 4 weeks.14 The longer retention of triamcinolone hexacetonide within the joint may explain why triamcinolone hexacetonide increases IOP, whereas betamethasone does not, as demonstrated in the present study.

The increase in IOP associated with steroid use was first reported in 1950.15 Previous studies have shown that the increase in IOP is related to the type, dose, and route of CS administration.16 The clinical signs of glaucoma develop with topical, periocular, or oral steroid treatment, but glaucoma can also develop after CS treatment via any route.17 The present study is important because it is one of the few studies examining the effect of IACS on IOP. The increase in IOP may vary depending on the structure of the steroid, the method and duration of administration, as well as the personal characteristics.17,18 Personal risk factors for steroid-induced IOP elevation include older age, myopia greater than 5 diopters, diabetes, collagen tissue diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, family history glaucoma, or a diagnosis of glaucoma.19–21 Thus, inquiring about the presence of glaucoma and evaluating risk factors in patients for whom triamcinolone injection is preferred are essential. The results of this study indicate that selecting betamethasone instead of triamcinolone may be beneficial in patient groups at risk of glaucoma. On the contrary, the degree of IOP increase is correlated with dose and duration.22,23 Steroid-induced glaucoma is generally slow-progressing, and an acute onset is rare. In acute cases, an increase in IOP may be observed within hours following steroid administration. It usually occurs after intensive topical or systemic use of steroids or after intravitreal injections.18,24 Therefore, patients who have received triamcinolone should also be monitored for IOP.

The asymmetrical statistical finding between the left and right eyes in Group 2 should be interpreted cautiously. Because intra-articular corticosteroids can produce systemic effects after local administration, a pharmacological effect on IOP would be expected to occur as a systemic response rather than as a true eye-specific response.25,26 Therefore, the unilateral statistical significance observed in the left eye is biologically unlikely to represent a true unilateral corticosteroid effect. Instead, this finding may reflect the limited sample size, random variation, measurement variability, or heterogeneous individual responses.

Despite being a prospective randomized study, this study had certain limitations. First, IOP was measured only at 1 week after injection; therefore, the peak IOP increase after IACS injection may not have been captured, and the observed mean increase may underestimate the maximum post-injection IOP elevation. The study was conducted with a limited number of patients and lacked a control group. Although randomization was performed by an independent physician, the drawing-lots method is a simple randomization technique and may be associated with potential limitations in allocation concealment; therefore, a risk of selection bias cannot be completely excluded. In the triamcinolone hexacetonide group, the unilateral statistical significance observed in the left eye, but not in the right eye, should not be interpreted as a true physiological difference between the eyes, as a systemic pharmacological response would be expected to affect both eyes. This finding may reflect limited sample size, measurement variability, or random variation. The wide standard deviation of ΔIOP in this group also suggests heterogeneous individual responses; therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. In addition, injections were performed using a landmark-guided inferolateral approach without ultrasound guidance, which may have introduced variability in intra-articular drug delivery and systemic absorption, although all injections were performed by the same experienced orthopedic surgeon. Finally, the physiological variability of cortisol levels at the time of measurement, which might have affected IOP values, was not considered.

Intra-articular triamcinolone hexacetonide may be associated with a short-term increase in IOP, whereas betamethasone did not show a significant short-term IOP elevation in this study. These findings suggest that betamethasone may be considered a more cautious option in patients with knee osteoarthritis who have glaucoma or glaucoma susceptibility. However, because IOP was assessed only at 1 week after injection, high-quality randomized studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are needed.
