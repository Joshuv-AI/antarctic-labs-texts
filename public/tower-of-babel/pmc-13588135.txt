Cross-Species Translation and Target-Dependent Pharmacology of GalNAc-siRNAs Using a Multi-Parameter K-PD Framework for Biophase Kinetics, Apparent Potency, and Pharmacodynamic Turnover
Authors: Xiaofei Wu, Xuanji Peng, Ranran Jia, Jiahua Ma, Hongyun Wang, Xiaohong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall interfering RNAs (siRNAs) conjugated with N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) are liver-targeted therapeutics with sustained activity. However, their cross-species pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) translation remains poorly defined, particularly regarding target dependence and PD endpoint selection. This study aimed to develop a kinetic-pharmacodynamic (K-PD) modeling framework to characterize their long-term pharmacology, cross-species translational relationships, and target mRNA effects by quantifying biophase half-life, in vivo potency (IDK50), and PD turnover.MethodsPublicly available PD time-course data for 30 GalNAc-siRNAs across four species (mice, rats, monkeys, and humans) were compiled through a targeted search of the literature and other public sources. A unified K-PD modeling framework was applied to estimate key parameters and evaluate cross-species translational behavior and target-related variability.ResultsHuman biophase half-life exceeded that in preclinical species, whereas human IDK50 was lower. PD half-life in humans approximated that in nonhuman primates. Despite consistent cross-species trends overall, K-PD parameters varied across compounds and targets. PD turnover half-life showed greater within-target consistency than IDK50, suggesting differential sensitivity of these endpoints to target biology.ConclusionThe K-PD modeling framework characterized the cross-species pharmacology of GalNAc-siRNAs, confirmed consistent translational patterns, and identified target-specific effects on PD kinetics. These findings facilitate early human pharmacological prediction, enabling a shift from empirical scaling toward model-informed translational decision-making.

Introduction

N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc)-conjugated small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) are a class of therapeutics that selectively silence pathogenic gene expression via RNA interference.1,2 The GalNAc ligand mediates efficient and hepatocyte-specific delivery through high-affinity binding to the asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR), which is highly expressed on the hepatocyte surface, thereby promoting receptor-mediated endocytosis and subsequent hepatic uptake.3,4 Following intracellular internalization, siRNAs are incorporated into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), leading to sequence-specific cleavage of the target messenger RNA (mRNA) and subsequent suppression of protein expression.5–7 Owing to their hepatocyte-specific delivery, sustained pharmacodynamic durability and infrequent dosing regimens,8,9 GalNAc-siRNA therapeutics have demonstrated considerable clinical value across diverse therapeutic areas.10–13 As of May 2026, seven GalNAc-siRNA therapies have received regulatory approval,14–20 with numerous additional candidates in clinical development.

Unlike small-molecule drugs or antibody-based therapeutics, GalNAc-siRNA exhibits a pronounced dissociation between plasma pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD).21,22 Following ASGPR-mediated hepatocellular uptake, plasma exposure is transient (typically hours), whereas pharmacological effects may persist for weeks to months.23,24 This disconnect limits the applicability of conventional plasma exposure–based PK/PD modeling for predicting long-term pharmacological outcomes. In addition, direct measurement of intrahepatic siRNA concentrations is generally not feasible in clinical settings, further constraining the application of mechanism-based models during early clinical development.24 Consequently, PD time-series data serves as a critical information source for characterizing the long-term pharmacological behavior.

The kinetic-pharmacodynamic (K-PD) model, using only dosing history and PD data, provides an alternative approach for GalNAc-siRNAs.25 Drug response can be described by three fundamental components: the time course of the drug at the target site, the concentration-response relationship reflecting drug potency and efficacy, and the turnover of the target or drug-target complex.25 Within the K-PD framework, these are reflected in three key parameters: biophase half-life, apparent in vivo potency (defined as the drug input rate required to produce 50% of the maximum inhibitory effect, IDK50), and PD half-life. Currently K-PD modeling has been successfully applied to GalNAc-siRNA development.26

Despite increasing application of K-PD modeling in siRNA drug development, two key challenges persist in the cross-species translation of K-PD parameters for GalNAc-siRNAs. First, the translational relationships of these parameters remain incompletely characterized. Although a previous K-PD analysis has explored cross-species scaling of biophase kinetics and potency for GalNAc-siRNAs, that analysis was based on a relatively limited set of 11 compounds across three species (mice, monkeys, and humans), and the translational behavior of PD half-life was not systematically characterized.27 Whether these parameters exhibit consistent and generalizable species-dependent relationships requires further evaluation with larger datasets across broader target classes. Second, although the previous analysis included GalNAc-siRNAs directed against multiple targets, target dependence was not systematically evaluated. GalNAc-siRNAs target diverse mRNAs, and their corresponding PD endpoints represent different levels of biological regulation, ranging from direct target proteins to downstream biomarkers. Whether K-PD parameters exhibit within-target consistency or differ according to target and PD endpoint characteristics therefore remains unresolved.

In this study, we compiled PD time-course data from four species (mice, rats, monkeys and humans) for GalNAc-siRNAs that had entered clinical development. Using K-PD modeling, we estimated compound-specific biophase half-life, potency, and PD half-life, and assessed cross-species translational relationships and target-associated variability. These estimates serve as a quantitative framework for predicting human pharmacological effects in early clinical development of GalNAc-siRNAs.

Methods

Data Collection

Literature Search and Study Selection

A targeted literature and public-source compilation, rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, was conducted to identify PD time-course data suitable for K-PD modeling. Preclinical and clinical PD time-course data for GalNAc-siRNAs that have entered clinical development were collected from publicly available sources, including published literature, regulatory review documents from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA), clinical trial registries, corporate announcements, and official websites of originator companies. Both approved and investigational GalNAc-siRNAs were included. The search cutoff date was March 2026. Search terms included “GalNAc”, “siRNA”, “RNAi”, “clinical trial”, “Phase I/II/III”, “pharmacodynamics”, “biomarker”.

Studies were included if the GalNAc‑siRNA had reached clinical development, longitudinal PD data were available for at least one species, and dosing and sampling records were adequate for K‑PD modeling. Studies were excluded if PD data were limited to a single time point, or if dosing or sampling information was insufficient. The included PD data were extracted as mean values from published summary figures, representing aggregated population averages rather than individual-subject observations. Accordingly, model fitting was performed deterministically on these aggregated mean profiles.

Data Extraction and Preprocessing

The following data were extracted: drug name, target mRNA, target protein, PD biomarker, species, dosage regimen, and sampling time points. Digitization was performed using WebPlotDigitizer (version 4.8; Automeris, Pacifica, CA). To minimize digitization error, all PD curves were independently digitized by two investigators. The independently extracted values were compared between investigators and, when available, cross-checked against numerical values explicitly reported in the source publications. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus. All PD data were baseline-corrected and normalized to ensure comparability across studies and species.

Data Overview

A total of 30 GalNAc-siRNAs were included in this study, of which 7 have received regulatory approval. The PD time-course data were available from mice, rats, monkeys, and humans. Human data were available for all 30 compounds, while mouse data were available for 16, rat data for 3, and monkey data for 14, respectively. K-PD Model

The K-PD model consisted of two components: (i) a one-compartment pharmacokinetic model representing a hypothetical biophase, which described the time course of drug amount at the effect site; and (ii) an indirect response (IDR) model describing the production and elimination of the PD response. Model parameters were estimated for each drug, including ke, IDK50 (normalized by body weight and dosing interval, expressed in mg/kg/day), γ and kout. Biophase half-life and PD half-life were subsequently derived from the estimated model parameters. Model fitting was performed using maximum likelihood estimation. The negative log-likelihood was minimized using the optim function with the Nelder–Mead algorithm in base R. The ordinary differential equation system was solved using the ode function in the deSolve package (version 1.36). Parameter uncertainty was quantified using asymptotic 90% confidence intervals (CIs), with standard errors derived from the inverse Hessian matrix evaluated at the final parameter estimates using the hessian function in the numDeriv package (version 2016.8–1.1). These CIs reflect uncertainty in the estimated model parameters and do not represent interindividual variability. Goodness-of-fit was evaluated by visual predictive assessment, coefficient of determination (R2), and root mean squared error (RMSE).

Species-Based Analysis of K-PD Parameters

The estimated K-PD parameters (biophase half-life, IDK50, and PD half-life) for each GalNAc-siRNA across species were used to evaluate cross-species translational relationships. Descriptive statistics were summarized for each parameter within each species to characterize distributional patterns. For compounds with matched human and animal data, human-to-animal ratios were calculated to quantify interspecies scaling differences. Before correlation analysis, the distributions and dynamic ranges of the K-PD parameters were examined. Because IDK50 values spanned approximately 2–3 orders of magnitude, a negative base-10 logarithmic transformation was applied to reduce the potential influence of high-value observations. Biophase half-life and PD half-life were analyzed on their original scales. Correlations between human and animal parameters were subsequently assessed to determine whether animal-derived parameters reflected the relative trends observed in humans.

Target-Based Analysis of K-PD Parameters

To evaluate potential target dependence of K-PD parameters, subgroup analyses were performed for targets with at least two GalNAc-siRNA compounds in the dataset. For each target subgroup, the distributions of human biophase half-life, IDK50, and PD half-life were compared with the overall distribution from all 30 compounds to assess potential target‑specific consistency. For compounds with matched human and animal data within each target subgroup, human-to-animal ratios were calculated to assess the consistency of cross-species translational relationships.

Owing to the small number of compounds per target subgroup, the influence of PD endpoint biology on K-PD parameters was further assessed using a classification-based approach. The 30 compounds were classified into three categories based on the site of target protein synthesis, site of action, and PD endpoint characteristics (Table 1). (i) Intrahepatic-acting targets, for which the protein was synthesized and exerted its function within hepatocytes; PD endpoints were downstream metabolites or indirect biomarkers (eg, urinary 5‑aminolevulinic acid [ALA] or urinary oxalate). (ii) Hepatic-secreted targets, for which proteins were synthesized in hepatocytes and secreted into circulation; PD endpoints were circulating protein levels that directly reflected hepatic target protein levels. (iii) Targets that fit neither Category I nor II, exemplified by viral antigen hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), where PD responses were influenced by multiple concurrent factors, including viral transcription, antigen production.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median (range). Simple linear regression and Pearson correlation analysis were used to assess the relationships between animal and human parameters. For comparisons across species or target categories, the Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric test was applied for overall between-group comparisons; when a significant overall difference was detected, Dunn’s test was used for pairwise comparisons with multiple-comparison correction. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data processing, model fitting, statistical analyses, and figure generation were performed using R (version 4.4.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Discussion

In recent years, GalNAc-siRNAs have become a rapidly advancing platform in RNA interference therapeutics. Through ASGPR-mediated hepatic delivery and RISC-mediated mRNA degradation, GalNAc-siRNA enables sustained suppression of disease-causing gene expression and has demonstrated clinical efficacy across multiple indications.2,5 However, the pronounced temporal dissociation between plasma pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD) limits the ability of conventional PK-based approaches to fully characterize long-term pharmacological behavior and constrains quantitative translation from preclinical species to humans.23 To address this, the present study systematically compiled PD time-course data from 30 GalNAc-siRNAs that have entered clinical development, applied a K-PD modeling framework to estimate biophase half-life, IDK50, and PD half-life, and evaluated these parameters from the cross-species translation and target-associated heterogeneity. Collectively, this work provided a quantitative framework for understanding the long-term pharmacological behavior and translational properties of GalNAc-siRNA therapeutics.

Three principal findings emerged from this study. First, human biophase half-life was longer than in preclinical animals, with the closest values observed in monkeys. Human IDK50 was lower than in animals, and the strongest concordance was observed between humans and monkeys, indicating prolonged apparent pharmacological driving at the effect site and higher potency in humans. Second, human PD half-life was markedly longer than in rodents but comparable to that in monkeys, suggesting conservation of target or biomarker turnover between non-human primates and humans. In addition, K-PD parameters showed greater within-target clustering than across the full dataset, particularly for PD half-life, indicating that target-related factors contribute to inter-compound variability in GalNAc-siRNA pharmacology. Overall, these findings systematically quantified the cross-species K-PD translational relationships of GalNAc-siRNA therapeutics.

Biophase half-life reflects the apparent persistence of drug-driven activity at the effect site rather than chemical siRNA stability. It integrates multiple intracellular processes, including endosomal/lysosomal retention, cytoplasmic release, RISC loading, and downstream mRNA cleavage.28 Previous studies have reported that more than 99% of GalNAc-siRNA molecules are retained within endosomal/lysosomal compartments following ASGPR-mediated uptake, with only a small fraction escaping into the cytoplasm to associate with RISC.29 Such an endosomal retention enables sustained siRNA delivery to RISC over a period of weeks to months, thereby prolonging the biophase half-life. In the present study, the median human biophase half-life was 11.1 weeks, longer than in mice, rats, and monkeys. These results were consistent with previous cross-species analyses of GalNAc-siRNA, which reported similar scaling trends between humans and preclinical species,27 and suggested that intracellular retention and slow-release mechanisms in human hepatocytes may contribute to the clinical feasibility of infrequent dosing regimens, such as quarterly or biannual administration.

IDK50 serves as an apparent in vivo potency parameter, reflecting the drug-driven input rate required to achieve 50% inhibition. In this study, IDK50 values were lower in humans than in all preclinical species, indicating higher potency in humans. This may reflect optimization of clinical candidates for human target sequences, which could enhance RISC loading efficiency and mRNA cleavage activity in human hepatocytes. In addition, species differences in mRNA sequence context and local secondary structure may also alter accessibility to RNA-induced silencing machinery, a key determinant of siRNA efficacy.30 The strongest human–monkey concordance suggested that non-human primates provided a relevant translational model for relative potency ranking. However, human-to-animal IDK50 ratios varied widely across compounds (human/monkey range: 0.005–1.07), indicating that the translational scaling was compound dependent and influenced by sequence conservation and chemical design features.

PD half-life reflects target or PD biomarker turnover kinetics. Human PD half-life was longer than in mice and rats but comparable to that in monkeys, with a median human/monkey PD half-life ratio approaching 1. This finding suggested conserved turnover kinetics across primates, supporting the translational relevance of non-human primates for predicting PD recovery dynamics. Compared with biophase half-life, PD half-life more directly reflects host physiological processes and therefore exhibits stronger target dependence. This was further supported by the within-target subgroup analysis, in which PD half-life values for different compounds sharing the same target consistently clustered together, suggesting that intrinsic target biology was a determinant of PD recovery kinetics in GalNAc-siRNA therapeutics.

A key finding of this study is the presence of target-dependent clustering of K-PD parameters, particularly for pharmacodynamic half-life. Compounds targeting the same mRNA exhibited reduced variability compared with the overall dataset, suggesting that target biology imposes constraints on pharmacodynamic behavior beyond compound-specific properties. When stratified by target action category, differences in IDK50 and PD half-life were observed across categories. Category I had a higher IDK50 than both Categories II and III, suggesting lower apparent potency when PD endpoints were based on downstream metabolic readouts rather than direct target protein levels. In contrast, PD endpoints in Categories II and III were more directly linked to changes in target protein expression following mRNA silencing, resulting in lower IDK50 estimates. PD half-life was shortest in Category II and longer in Categories I and III. This likely reflected differences in the processes governing PD recovery: plasma protein turnover in Category II primarily depends on synthesis and clearance, whereas downstream metabolic pathways and distribution processes may additionally contribute in Category I. In Category III, recovery dynamics may be further influenced by virus-related biological processes, resulting in greater system-level complexity. Collectively, these findings indicated that K-PD parameters were influenced not only by drug-specific characteristics but also by biological hierarchy and mechanistic nature of PD endpoints. This target-category stratification represents a novel contribution that extends beyond previous compound-level scaling analyses, providing a biological context for interpreting inter-compound variability in GalNAc-siRNA pharmacology.

Beyond the biological differences among target categories, disease-related characteristics may also influence the turnover dynamics of PD biomarkers. Disease state and severity may alter target expression, biomarker production, protein synthesis, or clearance processes, thereby affecting baseline biomarker levels and recovery kinetics. Consequently, differences in estimated PD half-life across studies may reflect not only intrinsic target biology but also differences in the underlying study populations. Such disease-related effects were not systematically captured in the present summary-level dataset and may introduce additional uncertainty when translating K-PD parameters across populations. Therefore, application of the K-PD framework to specific patient populations should consider disease-related changes in PD biomarker turnover and, where available, incorporate patient-derived PD data.

Chemical design may represent an additional source of variability in biophase kinetics and apparent potency. An exploratory within-target comparison of three TTR-targeting compounds (Table 1) developed using different generations of Alnylam siRNA chemistry—revusiran (STC), vutrisiran (ESC), and nucresiran (IKARIA)—showed longer biophase half-lives and lower IDK50 values for vutrisiran and nucresiran than for revusiran, whereas differences in PD half-life were comparatively modest (Table S2). This exploratory pattern suggests a potential influence of chemical design on biophase kinetics and apparent potency,31 whereas PD half-life may be more strongly governed by target and biomarker turnover. Nevertheless, given the limited number of directly comparable compounds and the close association between modification patterns, compounds, and targets across the overall dataset, the independent contribution of specific chemical modifications could not be systematically quantified and these observations should be considered exploratory.

From a drug development perspective, the proposed K-PD translational framework provided a practical tool for characterizing the long-term pharmacological behavior of GalNAc-siRNAs. By integrating biophase half-life, IDK50, and PD half-life derived from PD time-course data, this framework enabled quantitative translation of preclinical observations to human pharmacological effects and supports early-stage decision-making. Compared with single-parameter analyses, the combined use of K-PD parameters with within-target and target-category stratification allowed a more comprehensive characterization of biophase persistence, potency, and PD response recovery.

This study has several limitations. The analyses were based on summary-level published data, and differences in study design, sampling schedules, and PD endpoints may have introduced variability in parameter estimation despite standardized processing. In addition, PD profiles digitized from published figures are inherently subject to extraction uncertainty, which may not be fully eliminated despite independent duplicate digitization and cross-checking procedures. Data availability was uneven across compounds and species, particularly for rats and certain target subgroups, which limited statistical power. The small sample size for rats (n = 3) limits robust statistical inference for this species; therefore, findings involving the rat subgroup should be interpreted cautiously. In particular, the target-category comparisons (Category I, n=5; Category III, n=6) were based on relatively small subgroup sizes, which may reduce the statistical power to detect differences between categories. The observed between-category differences should therefore be interpreted as exploratory findings that warrant confirmation in larger datasets. Although chemical modifications were explored and actual dosing histories were incorporated during model fitting, their independent effects, together with those of disease-related characteristics, could not be systematically quantified using the available summary-level data. Future studies incorporating larger compound datasets and individual-level data, along with mechanism-based PK/PD modeling and integration of tissue exposure and RISC-related data, may further improve the robustness of translational predictions. Nevertheless, this study provided a biologically interpretable quantitative framework to support early clinical translation, PD study design, and dosing optimization for GalNAc-siRNA therapeutics.

Conclusion

This study systematically characterized the cross-species translational behavior and target-associated variability of GalNAc-siRNA pharmacology using a multi-parameter K-PD framework. Across 30 compounds and four species, consistent species-dependent patterns were observed, with prolonged biophase persistence and enhanced apparent in vivo potency in humans compared with preclinical species, while pharmacodynamic turnover showed closer alignment between humans and non-human primates. Beyond species effects, target biology contributed to pharmacodynamic heterogeneity. In particular, PD half-life exhibited greater within-target consistency compared with apparent potency, indicating that PD recovery kinetics are more closely associated with the biological characteristics of the pharmacodynamic endpoint. Collectively, the multi-parameter translational framework provided a quantitative basis for predicting human pharmacological effect, designing PD sampling schedules, and optimizing dosing interval in future GalNAc-siRNA development, facilitating a shift from empirical extrapolation to model‑informed quantitative prediction.
