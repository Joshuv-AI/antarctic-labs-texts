The genome of Cynanchum thesioides (Freyn) K. Schum provides insights into its systematics and candidate genes potentially associated with drought tolerance as a xerophyte
Authors: Xiaoyan Zhang, Xiaoyao Chang, Dong Zhang, Yangyang Li, Zhenfan Wang, Zhongren Yang, Fenglan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCynanchum thesioides (Freyn) K. Schum is an important xerophytic plant, renowned for its exceptional drought resistance. However, the genetic basis of its adaptation to extreme arid environments remains largely unknown, and the candidate genes potentially involved have yet to be identified. Additionally, as a unique resource plant, this species requires clarification of its precise phylogenetic position. Analysis of the C. thesioides genome will provide critical resources for elucidating its key drought resistance mechanisms and taxonomic status.ResultsWe generated a chromosome-level genome assembly of C. thesioides with a total size of 242 Mb. Phylogenetic analysis placed it within the Apocynaceae family, where it is closely related to A. pictum and C. roseus. Compared to other species in the order Gentianales, C. thesioides had significantly expanded gene families. These families were functionally enriched in pathways related to the biosynthesis of stilbenoids, phenylpropanoids, and flavonoids—processes that were commonly associated with drought responses. Additionally, integrative genomic and transcriptomic analyses revealed expression changes in genes involved in lignin biosynthesis. Notably, key gene families such as POD and Laccase showed marked clustering on chromosomes 3 and 9.ConclusionIn conclusion, this work provides important information on the evolutionary development of C. thesioides (Apocynaceae). It elucidates the candidate genes underlying its exceptional drought tolerance and emphasizes the importance of integrated multi-omics approaches for studying plant adaptation to environmental stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09131-3.Keywords: Cynanchum thesioides (Freyn) K. Introduction

Drought is driven by climate change and rising agricultural water demand. It increasingly constrains crop production. Annual yield losses from drought exceed those from all pathogens combined [1]. Drought causes dehydration, wilting, and ROS accumulation. These effects disrupt photosynthesis and respiration [2–4]. In response, plants accumulate osmotic regulators and activate antioxidant systems. They also synthesise secondary metabolites, such as lignin and flavonoids [5, 6]. The phenylpropanoid pathway plays a central role in this response. It is initiated by the deamination of phenylalanine via phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamate-4-hydroxylase (C4H), and 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL) to form p-coumaroyl-CoA. The pathway then branches to produce anthocyanins, lignin, and flavonoids [7, 8]. Under drought conditions, plants enhance their stress tolerance. They modulate the expression and activity of key enzymes in the phenylpropanoid pathway. This promotes the accumulation of protective metabolites, such as lignin and flavonoids [9–11]. This regulation occurs at multiple transcriptional levels. MYB transcription factors play central roles [12, 13]. Specific downstream genes involved in lignin and flavonoid biosynthesis have been functionally linked to improved drought tolerance in various plant species [14–17]. However, much of the existing knowledge comes from model organisms or major crops studied under controlled conditions. Wild plant species have naturally evolved robust drought resilience. Yet the genetic and regulatory dynamics of their phenylpropanoid pathway remain largely unexplored. This represents an untapped reservoir of genetic resources.

C. thesioides is a valuable plant species recognised for its ecological and economic significance. This upright shrub thrives in semi-arid regions. It is therefore well-suited to dry climates. The species is prevalent from East to Central Asia. It also exhibits remarkable resilience to drought, cold temperatures and nutrient-poor soils [18]. The plant is known for its medicinal and edible qualities. Its fruits contain beneficial compounds, such as flavonoids and polysaccharides. Additionally, it serves as a potential source of high-quality forage. It is also effective for windbreaks and sand stabilisation. Its contributions to soil and water conservation and ecological restoration are substantial [19, 20]. Notably, C. thesioides demonstrates exceptional drought resistance. It has a high seed germination rate that requires little moisture. Even under drought conditions, the plant can rejuvenate the following year. It also maintains a strong survival rate. Although the above-ground parts may suffer from wilting or dieback during dry spells, the root system remains healthy. This highlights the plant's overall drought resilience.

Over its long evolutionary journey, C. thesioides has developed various morphological and physiological adaptations. These include elongated leaves, pointed tips, epidermal hairs, a protective cuticle, unique stomatal structures, abundant latex, and a robust taproot. Its structure combines traits of both upright semi-shrubs and herbaceous plants. This enhances its drought resistance [21]. Previous research has investigated the plant's drought-resistant physiological traits and transcriptomic responses (using non-reference analysis) [22, 23]. Our study builds on this work. For the first time, we have successfully assembled and integrated the complete genome of C. thesioides. This breakthrough addresses the challenges of transcriptomic studies that lack a reference genome. It enables a thorough investigation of drought-resistance-related gene families. These gene families can now be studied at the chromosomal level. Moreover, we uncover the underlying processes that contribute to the plant's strong drought resistance. We do this from the perspectives of genetic mechanisms and adaptive evolution. A detailed examination of these mechanisms will illuminate key genetic resources. This provides a foundational understanding for improving drought resistance in other crops. It also helps develop new drought-tolerant varieties.

Methods

Plant material and drought treatment

The C. thesioides used in this experiment has been deposited in the Herbarium of the Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences (collection number: 141073). The plant material was formally identified by Fenglan Zhang. No specific permissions were required for the conduct of this experiment or its associated activities. Seeds of C. thesioides were germinated. They were then transferred to a growth substrate. The seedlings were cultivated in the tissue culture facility of the Germplasm Resources and Germplasm Innovation of Wild Endemic Vegetables at Inner Mongolia Agricultural University. Conditions were controlled: 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness, with a temperature maintained at 25 ± 3 °C. To ensure genetic consistency, leaves were collected from two-week-old seedlings selected for genome sequencing. These leaves came from a single plant. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent DNA and RNA extraction. Additional fresh leaf samples were subjected to specific techniques for flow cytometry analysis.

The seeds were surface sterilised in a 2% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution for 15 min. They were then rinsed three times with distilled water. After that, they were placed on wet filter paper. They were pre-germinated at 25 °C in the dark for 3 days. The germinated seeds were sown 2–3 cm deep in plastic pots. Each pot measured 26 cm in height and 18 cm in diameter, with a weight of 0.9 kg. Each pot contained 2.4 kg of a sand-soil-fermented sheep dung mixture (7:5:2, v/v/v) for the drought treatment [23]. Eight seeds were sown per pot. The pots were kept in a greenhouse at Inner Mongolia Agricultural University. The university is located in Hohhot, Inner Mongolia, Northwest China (112°09′E, 41°20′N). The mean temperatures were 26/19 °C (day/night).

At the ten-leaf stage, morphologically uniform seedlings were selected. They were randomly divided into four treatment groups (n = 20 per group). Drought stress was imposed based on soil moisture content. This was expressed as a percentage of the soil's maximum water holding capacity (24.4%). The four levels were: 65–70% (control), 45–50% (mild drought), 25–30% (moderate drought), and 5–10% (severe drought). To achieve these levels, each pot was first saturated with water. It was then left to dry naturally until the target moisture content was reached. Soil moisture was determined by weighing, drying and re-drying soil samples. Each treatment was carried out in triplicate. Every day at 5 p.m., soil moisture was measured in the same way. Any lost water was replenished to maintain the required level. To exclude rain or other external moisture, the experiment was conducted under a shade house. The shade house was covered with a clear plastic rain shield. After 5 days of treatment, leaf samples were collected. They were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for 5 min, and then stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction.

Cytogenetic study of C. thesioides: karyotype and ploidy analysis

Seeds of C. thesioides were selected and surface-sterilised in a 2% NaClO solution for 15 min. They were then washed three times with distilled water. Any remaining moisture was removed using paper towels. The seeds were placed uniformly in Petri dishes lined with two layers of filter paper. They were allowed to germinate in darkness at 25 °C. When the radicles grew to about 5 cm, root tip segments of 1–2 cm were cut for karyotyping and ploidy assessment. A dual method combining traditional squashing and fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) was used. Root tips were pre-treated in a 0.002 mol·L−1 8-hydroxyquinoline solution at 25 °C in the dark for 3 h. They were then rinsed five times with ultrapure water (2 min each rinse). The samples were fixed on ice using Carnoy’s fixative (3 parts anhydrous ethanol to 1 part glacial acetic acid) at 4 °C for 2 h. This was followed by another five washes with ultrapure water (2 min each). The root tips were hydrolysed in a 45% glacial acetic acid and 1 mol·L−1 HCl mixture (1:1, v/v) in a 60 °C water bath for 5 min. They were rinsed five more times with ultrapure water (2 min each). The samples were then treated hypotonically in ultrapure water for 2 h. After staining with modified Carbol fuchsin for 10–15 min, the samples were squashed and examined under a LEICA DM1000 optical microscope. Metaphase cells were located using a 20 × objective. Detailed images were captured with a 100 × oil immersion objective for chromosome enumeration. Further karyotype analysis was conducted with a fluorescence microscope fitted with a high-resolution CCD camera. After DAPI staining, FISH was performed using fluorescence probes targeting conserved rDNA repetitive sequences. High-resolution chromosomal images were captured under a fluorescence microscope. This allowed precise identification of chromosome ploidy and structural variations in C. thesioides.

Genomic DNA sequencing and Hi-C sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from two-week-old seedling leaves of C. thesioides. The cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method was used. DNA concentration and purity were evaluated with a Qubit fluorometer (ThermoFisher, Q33226) and 0.3% agarose gel electrophoresis. A paired-end library with an insert size of 300–400 bp was constructed. The MGIEasy Universal DNA Library Prep Kit V1.0 (CAT#1,000,005,250, MGI) was used, following the manufacturer's protocol. The library was sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform. Raw reads were processed with fastp (version 0.23.2) based on six criteria: 1) Filtering problematic reads. These include those with excessively low quality, short length, or an excessive number of Ns; 2) Evaluating the average quality value of reads through a sliding window. Low-quality bases at both the 5' and 3' ends of each read were trimmed; 3) Automatically detecting and removing adapter sequences from each read; 4) Correcting bases in overlapping regions of paired-end reads based on quality values; 5) Trimming short polyG or polyX bases at the 3' end. These are caused by specific sequencing instrument characteristics; 6) Removing PCR duplicates through deduplication. After filtering, a total of 46.61 Gb of clean short-read data was retained. This data was used for subsequent genome assembly and analysis.

For PacBio sequencing, a SMRTbell library was prepared. The target fragment size was 20 kb. The library was prepared using the SMRTbell Template Prep Kit 1.0 (PacBio). This was done according to the manufacturer's instructions. The library was then sequenced on the PacBio Revio platform. Raw reads were processed with the standard CCS (circular consensus sequencing) pipeline provided by PacBio (ccs v6.0). This generated high-fidelity (HiFi) reads.

For Hi-C analysis, leaves from C. thesioides were digested with the restriction enzyme MboI. This was used to construct the Hi-C library. Following validation of insert size, concentration, and effective concentration, the high-quality Hi-C library was sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform. This generated 176.84 Gb of raw data. Raw reads were processed using fastp (version 0.23.2) with the following parameters: sliding window trimming (mean quality ≥ Q20); automatic adapter detection; filtering out reads with more than 40% of bases below Q15 or more than 5 Ns; discarding reads shorter than 50 bp; and retaining only paired reads. After filtering, 166.47 Gb of clean data were obtained. These data were used for subsequent Hi-C analysis.

Genome assembly and assessment

Before genome assembly, a 17-mer size (k = 17) was selected for genome characteristic estimation, as it yields sufficient k-mer species (41⁷) to cover the entire genome. All k-mer frequency distributions and subsequent estimations were performed using GCE (version 1.0.0) [24]. Because the raw sequencing depth far exceeded the upper limit of GCE (255), half of the clean reads were randomly subsampled prior to analysis. GCE was used both to generate the k-mer frequency distribution and to estimate genome size, heterozygosity, and repeat content. Genome size (G) was calculated as G = total number of k-mers / peak depth, where the total k-mer count refers to the sum of all k-mer occurrences (including unique and repeated k-mers), and the peak depth represents the expected depth at the main Poisson peak obtained from GCE’s model fitting. Heterozygosity was estimated from the proportion of heterozygous k-mer species using the formula described in the GCE manual. Repeat content was defined as the proportion of k-mers occurring at a depth greater than 1.8-fold the main peak depth.

For genome assembly, hifiasm (version 1.0) was applied to PacBio long reads. The resulting primary assembly was then filtered by alignment against the NT database. This step identified and removed sequences originating from organelles and potential contaminants. Random sequencing errors in the assembled genome were then corrected using two polishing steps. First, Quiver (version 3.2.7) was used with PacBio long reads. Second, another round of base-level correction was performed with Pilon (version 1.24) using DNBSEQ-T7 clean reads.

For chromosome-level scaffolding, the cleaned Hi-C reads were mapped to the polished assembly using BWA. Only uniquely mapped read pairs were retained for subsequent analysis. Based on Hi-C interaction signals, only one set of the genome was retained for further scaffolding. Next, Juicer (version 1.6) [25] was used to construct a genome-wide interaction map. This map was then used by 3D DNA to cluster, order and orient the assembled contigs. This produced chromosome-level scaffolds. Finally, manual visual correction of the 3D DNA (version 180,922) results was performed with JuiceBox (version 1.11.08) [26]. This allowed adjustment of contig order and orientation errors. The result was a high-quality final chromosome-level genome. To evaluate the quality of the assembled genome, its completeness and accuracy were assessed. This was done by read mapping and BUSCO (version 5.5.0) analysis [27].

Genome annotation

To effectively identify protein-coding genes within the genome of C. thesioides, a variety of complementary approaches were employed. These included ab initio predictions, homology-based methods, transcriptome evidence, and alignment data from the BUSCO (version 5.5.0) conserved protein database. The comprehensive gene set was generated through a combined analysis using MAKER and HiFAP software. Following this, the predicted gene sequences were compared against several functional databases. These included KEGG, Nr, TrEMBL, KOG, and GO. BLAST (version 2.2.31) was used for systematic functional annotation. Additionally, a thorough annotation of non-coding RNAs was conducted: 1) For tRNAs, tRNAscan-SE (version 1.3.1) was used to identify them based on their structural features; 2) For rRNAs, sequences from closely related species were selected as references due to their high conservation. BLASTN alignment was then performed to locate rRNAs in the genome; 3) For miRNAs and snRNAs, covariance models from the Rfam database were used. INFERNAL software was applied to scan and annotate these RNA types. In conclusion, four primary categories of non-coding RNAs were successfully annotated: transfer RNAs, ribosomal RNAs, microRNAs, and small nuclear RNAs.

Identification of repeat sequences

To develop a repeat library for C. thesioides, a structure-based de novo methodology was employed. RepeatModeler (version open-1.0.11) was used, which incorporates LTR-FINDER (version 1.0.7) along with additional tools. The repeats identified from the de novo library were categorised through the RepeatClassifier module in RepeatModeler. These categorised repeats were then merged with the Repbase database to form a comprehensive, curated repeat library. The assembled library was then aligned with the genome sequences using RepeatMasker (version open-4.0.9). Both established and new repetitive elements were detected and annotated by this alignment.

Comparative and evolutionary analysis of C. thesioides

A comparative genomic study was conducted involving the genomes of ten species, which included nine additional dicot plants from five different families: Apocynaceae (Apocynum pictum, Catharanthus roseus), Rubiaceae (Coffea arabica, Coffea eugenioides, Oldenlandia corymbosa), Euphorbiaceae (Euphorbia lathyrism, Euphorbia peplus), Asteraceae (Lactuca virosa), and Celastraceae (Tripterygium wilfordii). To determine the similarity relationships among the protein sequences of these species, all-vs-all BLASTP alignments were performed, applying an E-value cutoff of 1e−5. Following this, OrthoMCL (version 2.0.9) [28] was utilized to group the results, with an inflation parameter set to 1.5.

To create the phylogenetic tree, protein sequences were extracted from single-copy gene families. The sequences were aligned using MAFFT (version 7.471) [29] and then refined with Gblocks (version 0.91b) [30]. The refined sequences were used to construct a phylogenetic tree with 1,000 bootstrap replicates using RAxML (version 8.2.12) [31].

Divergence times were estimated using MCMCTree (version 4.9) with fossil data from the TimeTree database (http://www.timetree.org/) [32]. Gene family expansion or contraction was calculated using CAFÉ (version 4.2) [33] under a maximum likelihood model (q-value ≤ 0.05). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the expanded gene families using a hypergeometric test, with all protein-coding genes as the background. To correct for multiple testing, the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method was applied. An adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

CODEML (version 4.9) from PAML [32] was used to compute dN/dS ratios and identify genes under positive selection. Gene family annotations were obtained from the PANTHER database [34]. Enrichment analysis was conducted with TopGO (version 2.22.0) using an exact Fisher test (p-value < 0.01). The synonymous substitution rate (Ks) was calculated using WGDI (version 0.5.6) [35] and statistically filtered according to collinearity blocks to minimise errors from tandem repeats. Finally, a frequency distribution plot of Ks values was generated. Whole-genome duplication events were inferred by analysing the number of peaks in the plot.

RNA isolation and library preparation

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA). The extraction followed the manufacturer's protocol. RNA purity and concentration were assessed using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Libraries were then constructed using the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Premixed Version). The construction followed the manufacturer's instructions. Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

RNA sequencing and differentially expressed genes analysis

The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. This generated 150 bp paired-end reads. The raw reads in fastq format were initially processed using fastp (version 0.20.1). Low-quality reads were removed to obtain clean reads. Approximately 166.46 Gb of clean reads per sample were retained for subsequent analyses. The clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 (version 2.1.0). The FPKM of each gene was calculated. Read counts for each gene were obtained using HTSeq-count (version 0.11.2). PCA was conducted using R (version 3.2.0) to evaluate the biological duplication of samples. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.22.2). A q-value < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange|> 1 were set as the threshold for identifying significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Quantification was performed with a two-step reaction process: reverse transcription (RT) and PCR. Each RT reaction consisted of 0.5 μg RNA, 2 μl of 5 × TransScript All-in-one SuperMix for qPCR and 0.5 μl of gDNA Remover, in a total volume of 10 μl. Reactions were performed in a GeneAmp® PCR System 9700 (Applied Biosystems, USA) for 15 min at 42℃,5 s at 85℃.The 10 μl RT reaction mix was then diluted × 10 in nuclease-free water and held at -20℃.

Real-time PCR was performed using LightCycler® 480 Ⅱ Real-time PCR Instrument (Roche, Swiss) with 10 μl PCR reaction mixture that included 1 μl of cDNA, 5 μl of 2 × ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix, 0.2 μl of forward primer, 0.2 μl of reverse primer and 3.6 μl of nuclease-free water. Reactions were incubated in a 384-well optical plate (Roche, Swiss) at 94℃ for 30 s, followed by 45 cycles of 94℃ for 5 s, 60℃ for 30 s. Each sample was run in triplicate for analysis. At the end of the PCR cycles, melting curve analysis was performed to validate the specific generation of the expected PCR product. The primer sequences were designed in the laboratory and synthesized by TsingKe Biotech based on the mRNA sequences obtained from the NCBI database (Additional file 1: Table S1). The expression levels of mRNAs were normalized to ACT7 and were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Three technical replicates were performed for each sample.

Results

Genome sequencing, assembly, and annotation

The genomic DNA for sequencing C. thesioides was obtained from a specimen grown in the greenhouse at Inner Mongolia Agricultural University. DNA extraction and sequencing were performed using three different techniques: BGI, 10X Genomics, and PacBio HiFi. A library with fragment sizes ranging from 300 to 400 bp was constructed for C. thesioides and then sequenced. After removing low-quality reads and duplicates, 46.61 Gb of high-quality data were obtained.

The analysis revealed a low heterozygosity rate of approximately 0.87% and a high proportion of repetitive sequences, accounting for 52.59%. Based on these characteristics, the genome size was estimated to be around 242 Mb, as assessed by 17 k-mer frequency analysis (Additional file 1: Table S2 and Additional file 2: Fig. S1). Flow cytometry provided an estimate of the haploid genome size as 240 Mb (Additional file 2: Fig. S2), consistent with the 17 k-mer analysis. Fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) results confirmed that C. thesioides was diploid (2n = 22) (Additional file 2: Fig. S3). Additionally, FISH with an Arabidopsis-type telomere probe (AGGGTTT × n) showed faint telomeric signals at the ends of chromosomes, indicating a telomere structure similar to that of Arabidopsis (Additional file 2: Fig. S3).

A total of 176.84 Gb of sequencing data were obtained. The initial assembly size was 506.32 Mb. After Hi-C-assisted correction, the final genome was assembled into 11 pseudochromosomes and 128 scaffolds. Detailed statistics were provided in Tables 1 and 2. Species chromosome and whole-genome interaction maps were then constructed, as shown in Fig. S4 (Additional file 2). The observed interaction patterns confirmed the effectiveness of the Hi-C-assisted assembly for this species. The distributions of gene density, GC content, and repeat density across the 11 pseudochromosomes were shown in Fig. 1B. The Hi-C heatmap clearly outlined the 11 pseudochromosomes. It demonstrated strong internal interactions, which indicated high-quality anchoring. Each pseudochromosome contained seven contigs on average. Few discrepancies were noted between the Hi-C interaction map and the original contigs. For example, chromosomes Chr1, Chr4, and Chr10 each comprised only two contigs, highlighting the success of Nanopore long reads in overcoming complex genome assembly issues (Additional file 2: Fig. S5). Chromosome lengths ranged from 13,333,410 bp (Chr10) to 20,715,738 bp (Chr9). Chr5 measured 19.67 Mb and contained the largest number of contigs, totalling 25 (Additional file 1: Table S3; Fig. 1B).

BUSCO analysis revealed that the C. thesioides genome was complete. The genome contained 95.97% complete BUSCO genes. Among these, 94.98% were complete and single copies, while 0.99% were complete and duplicated. The proportion of missing BUSCOs was only 3.1% (Additional file 1: Table S4).

A combination of de novo methods, RepeatMasker, and protein masking techniques was used to identify and annotate repetitive sequences. The results (Additional file 1: Table S5) showed that the genome of C. thesioides contained 82.85 Mb of repetitive elements. This represented 39.96% of the entire genome. The predominant type of repeats found was retrotransposons (class I elements), which accounted for 20.64% of the genome assembly. Within this category, long terminal repeat retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) were the most abundant. Non-LTR retrotransposons made up 2.46% of the total repeats (Additional file 1: Table S6). Furthermore, class II elements, which include DNA transposons and helitrons, constituted 3.62% of the genome (Additional file 1: Table S6).

Through a combination of de novo modelling, homology searches, and transcript predictions, a total of 19,685 protein-coding genes were discovered. Of these, 94.91% (18,683 genes) received functional annotations (Additional file 1: Table S7). Non-coding RNA (ncRNA) gene analysis revealed 2,693 ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), 577 small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), 86 microRNAs, and 515 transfer RNAs (tRNAs). Notably, small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) was absent from the C. thesioides genome (Additional file 1: Table S8).

A comprehensive cluster analysis of gene families identified 24,414 distinct gene families across ten species. C. thesioides contributed 19,685 genes to this analysis. These genes were organised into 13,700 families (Additional file 1: Table S9). Among these families, 140 were exclusive to C. thesioides (Fig. 2). The gene distribution for C. thesioides included 4,234 multi-copy orthologs, 4,998 single-copy orthologs, 654 unique paralogs, and 1,209 genes that did not belong to any cluster (Fig. 3A). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the 654 unique gene families of C. thesioides were mainly associated with several pathways. These included “Flavonoid biosynthesis”, “Stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid and gingerol biosynthesis”, “Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism”, “MAPK signalling pathway – plant”, “Diterpenoid biosynthesis”, “Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis”, “Plant–pathogen interaction”, and “Plant hormone signal transduction” (Fig. 3B). This suggests that the significant expansion of genes related to stress response, secondary metabolism, and growth is likely a key factor in the enhanced stress resistance and medicinal properties of C. thesioides.

Using Orthofinder, 1,430 common single-copy genes were identified from ten species. These were used to construct a maximum likelihood (ML) tree. C. thesioides formed a monophyletic group with five other species from the order Gentianales (Fig. 3C). Based on divergence time calibration with known fossil records, the Apocynaceae lineage was inferred to have diverged from the ancestors of three Rubiaceae species approximately 58 to 75 million years ago (Mya). C. thesioides formed a tight cluster with A. pictum and C. roseus, both well-established members of the Apocynaceae family. The divergence times of C. thesioides from A. pictum and C. roseus were estimated at approximately 20.2 to 39.4 Mya and 33.2 to 54.3 Mya, respectively (Fig. 3D).

Examination of gene duplication patterns in the C. thesioides genome indicated that whole-genome or segmental duplications were the main source of paralogous genes. These were followed by dispersed duplications, tandem duplications, and proximal duplications (Fig. 3F). MCScanX was used to assess collinearity in the C. thesioides genome. A total of 218 homologous segments, containing 4,506 genes, were identified (Additional file 1: Table S10). Analysis of the 4dTv (four-fold degenerate transversion rate) of these genes revealed a notable peak in their distribution. This peak supports the hypothesis of a whole-genome duplication (WGD) event in C. thesioides (Fig. 3G). From the 4dTv value distribution, collinear segments with 4dTv values ranging from 0.2 to 2.3 were selected for enhanced visualisation using a Circos plot. The Circos plot depicts homologous relationships within the genome (Additional file 2: Fig. S6). The plot also revealed overlapping areas among certain collinear segments. This provides further evidence for the WGD event (Additional file 2: Fig. S7).

To determine when the whole-genome duplication (WGD) event occurred in C. thesioides, the distributions of Ks (the rate of synonymous substitutions) were analysed for homologous gene pairs from C. thesioides and two closely related species, Tripterygium wilfordii and A. pictum. The results showed that the Ks peak for C. thesioides and A. pictum occurred earlier than that for C. thesioides and T. wilfordii. The latter pair exhibited a pronounced peak at higher Ks values (Fig. 3H). This pattern indicated that a historical WGD event in C. thesioides took place before its divergence from other species within the order Gentianales. Furthermore, the Ks distribution suggested that the split between C. thesioides and T. wilfordii occurred before the separation from A. pictum. These observations were consistent with the outcomes of the 4dTv analysis (Fig. 3G).

The estimated evolutionary rate for C. thesioides was approximately 0.00808795 substitutions per site per million years. This rate suggested that the whole-genome duplication (WGD) event likely occurred around 123.78 Mya (Fig. 3D and Additional file 2: Fig. S8). The Ks and 4dTv analyses further supported the occurrence of a significant WGD event in C. thesioides during the mid-Cretaceous era.

Gene family dynamics and evolution

A comparison was made with the gene families of five other plants within the order Gentianales. C. thesioides showed an increase of 580 gene families and a decrease of 1,509 families. Of these changes, 519 expansions and 1,249 contractions were statistically significant (Fig. 3E). Enrichment analyses using GO and KEGG were performed on the significantly expanded genes of C. thesioides. These analyses revealed notable enrichment in several biological pathways. These included the biosynthesis of stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid and gingerol, as well as pathways related to ribosomes, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis (Additional file 2: Fig. S9 and Fig. S10). Positive selection in protein-coding sequences was identified using the CodeML module from PAML. A total of 155 genes under positive selection were detected, with a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 (Additional file 1: Table S11). Additionally, 35 KEGG pathways were found to be enriched, each containing one to three positively selected genes. Notable pathways included aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (ko00970, Cth_5G0017310.1/Cth_4G0001390.1/Cth_10G0011090.1), selenocompound metabolism (ko00450, Cth_5G0017310.1/Cth_5G0018400.1), and glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchor biosynthesis (ko00563, Cth_9G0023250.1) (Additional file 1: Table S12).

Drought-related gene families and expression

A notable increase in C. thesioides was observed in gene families associated with O-methyltransferase, UDP-glycosyltransferase (UGT), oxidoreductase functions, and defence mechanisms (Additional file 1: Table S13). This expansion was linked to the accumulation of various antioxidant secondary metabolites. These included methylated flavonoids and glycosylated terpenoids (Additional file 2: Fig. S11). Together, these traits indicated that the remarkable drought resistance observed in C. thesioides was likely due to the enhanced combined effects of glycosylation, methylation, and antioxidant properties.

Lignin biosynthesis began with the phenylpropanoid pathway, three CtPAL, two CtC4H, and six Ct4CL genes were identified, most of these genes were significantly upregulated under moderate (MS) and severe (SS) drought stress. The CtPAL genes acted as rate-limiting core enzymes and show sustained high expression. Ct4CL1 and Ct4CL2 provided activated substrates for lignin biosynthesis (Fig. 4). Downstream of the phenylpropanoid pathway, the synthesis diverged at p-coumaroyl-CoA. This step gave rise to the three lignin monomers: H, G, and S. Two key reductases catalysed the final conversion stages. These were cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR) and cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD). Our analysis identified CtCCR and CtCAD2. Both genes displayed markedly elevated expression under stress conditions. In contrast, CtCAD1 and CtCAD3 were downregulated under mild stress. However, they recovered at MS and SS. This pattern was consistent with the increased demand for lignification under adverse conditions (Figs. 4 and 5). The CtHCT family marked a metabolic branch point. Functional differentiation emerged among its members. Six genes increased expression under MS. This response diminished at SS. Conversely, CtHCT3 and CtHCT4 reached peak expression at SS. This pattern suggested finely tuned regulation of G/S-type lignin production. Members of the CtCSE and CtC3H families exhibited stress-intensity-specific profiles. Within the methylation module, most CtCCoAOMT genes showed high expression at SS. CtF5H expression first fell. Levels later recovered with rising stress intensity. CtCOMT expression, however, culminated at MS (Fig. 4).

Relative expression of genes by RT-qPCR under drought stress

The movement of lignin relied on ABC transporters. It also depended on the glycosylation-deglycosylation pathway. This pathway was facilitated by UGT and BGLU enzymes. Researchers identified 20 ABC transporter genes. Six of them, including CtABCT8, CtABCT9, and CtABCT10, showed the most notable regulation under MS conditions (Fig. 4). Furthermore, among the 12 CtBGLU genes, two CtUGT (CtUGT1 and CtUGT2) and three CtBGLU (CtBGLU5, CtBGLU8, and CtBGLU12) showed the highest expression levels under MS conditions. In addition, six other genes, including CtBGLU1 and CtBGLU2, were significantly upregulated under the same conditions.

The formation of lignin polymers within the cell wall necessitated the presence of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Researchers identified two CtNOX genes, CtNOX1 showed peak expression levels during MS. This was followed by a swift reduction during SS. In contrast, CtNOX2 had its highest expression during CK. Its expression diminished as stress levels increased (Fig. 4). There were 9 genes encoding CtSOD enzymes, 28 encoding CtPOD enzymes, and 16 encoding CtLac enzymes. A majority of these genes exhibited their highest expression levels during MS or SS. These included CtSOD2, CtSOD5, CtPOD1, CtPOD2, CtLac2, and CtLac4 (Figs. 4 and 5). This suggested that both ROS production and the polymerisation process were significantly activated in response to drought conditions. Nine representative genes were studied to verify the RNA-seq findings. Overall, the qRT-PCR and RNA-seq data showed consistent expression trends (Fig. 5).

An examination of the gene localisation map in C. thesioides was performed at the chromosome level. The results showed significant clustering of functional gene families across its 11 chromosomes. Noteworthy families, such as CtPOD and CtLac, were particularly concentrated on chromosomes 3 and 9. Chromosome 9 had the highest overall gene density. This chromosome also featured a terminal grouping of CtPOD, CtABCT, and Ct4CL genes. Meanwhile, chromosome 3 was identified as a key chromosome for lignin biosynthesis and antioxidant defence. It contained essential pathway genes, including CtPOD, CtLac, and CtCCoAOMT (Fig. 6). Several stress-response genes (CtSOD and CtABCT), secondary metabolism genes were also clustered on these chromosomes. This arrangement illustrated a genomic structure that facilitates an adaptive strategy. It promoted the synchronised regulation of stress resilience and secondary metabolic processes. In contrast, gene-poor regions on chromosomes 1 and 4 exhibited traits of gene deserts. These regions played a role in the functional diversification of the genome (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Systematics and evolutionary history of C. thesioides

The genome assembly of C. thesioides provides valuable information. It reveals the genetic basis of its adaptive characteristics. A high-quality reference genome supports in-depth research on the genetic basis of these important traits. In this study, a comprehensive chromosome-scale genome assembly for C. thesioides was produced. PacBio long-read sequences, BGI short-read sequences, and Hi-C data were combined for this purpose. These resources improve the genomic data available for the Apocynaceae family [18]. They also serve as a key resource for studying the genetic mechanisms behind unique adaptive traits. These traits include drought resistance and its distinct subshrub-herbaceous growth form [22, 23, 36].

A notable observation is the relatively small size of the nuclear genome in C. thesioides. Our analysis reveals a genome size of approximately 242 Mb. This value closely aligns with the flow cytometry estimate of about 210 Mb for this species and its variety, which is around 220 Mb. This positions the genome within the small-to-medium range for the Apocynaceae family. Importantly, the genome size of the related species C. wilfordii (178 Mb) [37] shows a 1.36-fold variation compared to C. thesioides. This indicates considerable genomic divergence among closely related taxa, likely due to the activity of transposable elements [38]. At the family level, the genome size of C. thesioides is similar to that of Calotropis procera (203 Mb, Asclepiadoideae) [39] and A. pictum (225 Mb, Apocynoideae) [40]. Despite their evolutionary distance, this resemblance suggests a pattern of independent genome reduction across various lineages within Apocynaceae. Nevertheless, the family is characterised by a wide range of genome sizes [41]. This is illustrated by Hoya carnosa (465.7 Mb, Asclepiadoideae) [42], whose genome is nearly 1.92 times larger than that of C. thesioides. This remarkable variation within the family underscores the dynamic processes of genome evolution in Apocynaceae [43]. The relatively small genome of C. thesioides may have been linked to its extensive ecological versatility. This could have enabled more effective gene regulation and enhanced its resilience to stress [44].

The reclassification of C. thesioides into the Apocynaceae family is strongly supported by molecular phylogenetic data. According to modern taxonomic systems such as APG, these data clearly place the species within a well-established clade of Apocynaceae [18, 45]. This classification is further supported by genomic comparisons. Divergence time estimates suggested that C. thesioides, A. pictum, and C. roseus shared a common ancestor around 28.6 to 40.7 Mya. This highlights their close evolutionary ties within the family. Importantly, research by Yao et al. [46] provided additional genomic perspectives. These perspectives aligned closely with this taxonomic shift. Their findings revealed a significant whole-genome duplication event in the lineage leading to C. thesioides. This event occurred during the mid-Cretaceous. This timeframe aligns with the proposed emergence of the Apocynaceae family. This timing implies that the duplication was not merely a random genomic occurrence. Instead, it may have played a crucial role in the early evolution of the family. The duplication likely provided a rich source of genetic variation. This variation may have driven both morphological and ecological diversification in the lineage of C. thesioides during the family’s early development. Therefore, this ancient genomic evidence supports the classification of C. thesioides within Apocynaceae. It also sheds light on the unique evolutionary journey of this lineage.

Expansion of phenylpropanoid-related gene families

C. thesioides is a drought-tolerant erect semi-shrub found in dry environments. It withstands low rainfall (250–400 mm) and long periods of soil dryness. In summer, while nearby species wither, C. thesioides remains robust, showing remarkable drought resilience.

Research links flavonoid glycosylation and lignan production to oxidative stress tolerance [47, 48]. In C. thesioides, gene families related to antioxidant functions and drought resistance, such as UGTs, O-methyltransferases, and oxidoreductases, have expanded. This suggests enhanced antioxidant defence through selective gene family expansion.

Lignin supports growth, structure, water transport, and stress resistance [49, 50]. Its biosynthesis begins with phenylalanine, which is converted to PAL, then to p-coumaroyl CoA via C4H and 4CL. The pathway branches: one route (CCR, CAD) produces H-unit lignin; the other (hydroxylation, methoxylation) yields C, G, and S units [8]. After glycosylation, monolignols are transported to the apoplast and polymerised by peroxidases and polyphenol oxidases into crosslinked lignin [5]. Higher expression of F3H, PAL, and 4CL correlates with drought resilience [51–53]. In C. thesioides, three CtPAL genes were consistently upregulated under drought, confirming their role as rate-limiting enzymes [54]. In alfalfa, 14 PAL genes were downregulated during 6–24 h of drought [55]. Expression patterns of 4CL, CCR, and CAD varied among cultivars. In C. thesioides, Ct4CL3 was activated only under moderate stress, while Ct4CL1 and Ct4CL2 remained high under both moderate and severe stress, suggesting a shift of metabolic flux towards lignin production via Ct4CL1/2.

HCTs redirect carbon from flavonoid synthesis to chlorogenic acid, caffeoylshikimic acid, and lignin, boosting stress resilience [56]. In tea, two CsHCTs responded to cold, salt, drought and MeJA [56]. In C. thesioides, the greatest gene expansion was found in stilbenoid, diarylheptanoid and gingerol pathways (44 genes), followed by phenylpropanoid pathway (33 genes). Within the latter, CtHCT (19 genes), CtCOMT (4) and CtCCoAOMT (7) showed notable amplifications. Under drought, three CtHCT genes (CtHCT3,4,6) showed varied expression: CtHCT6 only under mild stress, while CtHCT3/4 peaked under severe stress.

The CCoAOMT family is essential for oxidative methylation of phenolics and for growth, development and stress responses [57]. It is the main methyltransferase for G-type lignin monomers [15]. Under drought, five CtCCoAOMT genes were identified; CtCCoAOMT1 was highest under moderate stress, while CtCCoAOMT2-4 were highest under severe stress. In tobacco, CCoAOMT, F5H and COMT were downregulated in varieties with different lignin levels [58]. However, other studies report increased CCoAOMT expression under drought in tobacco roots/leaves and jute stems [59, 60]. These discrepancies may arise from differences in genetic background, stress conditions (intensity/duration), and tissue sampled.

Candidate genomic basis of drought tolerance

Lignin deposition within the cell wall is facilitated by oxidative polymerisation. This process relies on reactive oxygen species (ROS, primarily H₂O₂) and the enzymatic functions of peroxidase and laccase [61, 62]. Recent research highlights the importance of NADPH oxidases (RBOHs). These enzymes are vital sources of ROS for the lignification process [63]. NADPH oxidases are carefully regulated at both transcriptional and post-translational levels. This regulation ensures a precise and timely supply of ROS. Such a supply is essential for reinforcing the cell wall during development and in response to stress [64, 65].

In this research, the expression levels of CtNOX1/2 varied under drought conditions. This indicates their significant role in modulating ROS production. They also promote lignification in C. thesioides. Further investigations showed that several genes related to antioxidants and lignin polymerisation were markedly upregulated under moderate to severe stress. These genes include SOD, POD, and LAC. Specific examples are CtSOD2/5, CtPOD1/2, and CtLac2/4. This suggests that during drought, the systems for generating and managing ROS are activated in a coordinated manner. They mitigate oxidative damage caused by excessive ROS. At the same time, they supply the oxidative substrates needed for lignin synthesis. Collectively, these results illustrate a well-orchestrated redox regulatory network in C. thesioides in response to environmental challenges. Additionally, chromosomal gene localisation analysis indicates that functional genes within the C. thesioides genome are distributed in a non-random, modular fashion. Such genomic organisation is closely linked to the spatial arrangement of essential metabolic pathways. It also reflects the unique adaptive strategies that this species has developed throughout its evolutionary history.

Limitations and future directions

A major limitation of this study is the lack of functional validation for the candidate genes and gene families implicated in drought tolerance. Although we identified significant expansions in gene families such as CtUGT, CtCOMT, CtPOD, CtLac, and CtCCoAOMT, and observed their differential expression under drought stress, these findings remain correlative. Without direct experimental evidence—such as overexpression, knockout, or complementation assays—the causal roles of these genes in lignin biosynthesis, ROS homeostasis, and drought adaptation cannot be firmly established. Future work will focus on functional characterization of these candidates to validate their roles in drought tolerance.

Conclusions

In summary, a high-quality, chromosome-level genome of C. thesioides was assembled. This is the first genomic reference for this important xerophytic species. By integrating multi-omics data, insights were gained into its remarkable drought tolerance. The findings include the expansion of gene families involved in phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis, as well as the coordinated regulation of these genes during stress responses. However, caution is needed. Expansions of these metabolic pathways could also be linked to other ecological factors, such as herbivory, UV radiation, or pathogen resistance. Therefore, the specific role of these pathways in drought adaptation still requires further functional validation. Over its long evolutionary history, events such as whole-genome duplication have likely shaped the genetic architecture underlying its adaptation to extreme aridity. The complete genome of C. thesioides will serve as a key resource for comparative genomic studies focusing on plant drought adaptation and the evolution of stress-related traits. This resource will also be valuable for identifying candidate genes and for informing molecular breeding strategies aimed at improving drought resistance in crops.
