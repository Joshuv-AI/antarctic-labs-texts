A chemoresistance-associated proliferation gene signature identified by single-cell and machine learning analysis predicts prognosis in high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma
Authors: Yanmin Zhang, Sihuang Wu, Huizhu Lin, Ling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Single-cell analysis reveals chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes enriched in tumor cells, SPP1+/TREM2+ macrophages and CAFs.•Consensus clustering defines two molecular subtypes with distinct immune landscapes and cell-cycle programs.•Machine-learning benchmarking of 117 algorithms yields a nine-gene prognostic signature with superior performance.•qPCR and Western blot confirm upregulation of BIRC5, CENPH, CKAP2, PAK1IP1 and PBK in cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer cells.

Introduction

Globally, ovarian cancer ranks as the seventh most common malignancy among women and remains the deadliest gynecological cancer, accounting for approximately 3.7% of new cases and 4.7% of cancer-related deaths in 2020 [[1], [2], [3]]. High-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC), which comprises 70–80% of cases, is responsible for the majority of mortality [4]. Despite initial responses to platinum-based chemotherapy, approximately 80% of patients experience recurrence accompanied by the development of chemoresistance, resulting in a five-year overall survival of only 45–50% [5].

The poor outcomes of HGSOC are driven by extensive molecular heterogeneity, near-ubiquitous TP53 mutations, frequent loss of RB1/NF1/PTEN, and marked polyclonality [[5], [6], [7]]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has revealed diverse malignant, stromal, and immune subpopulations that underlie this complexity and contribute to treatment resistance [3,4]. Nevertheless, the systematic integration of tumor proliferation kinetics into molecular prognostic frameworks for HGSOC remains largely unexplored.

Tumor doubling time (TDT), defined as the interval required for a neoplasm to double its volume, is a clinically relevant measure of growth rate [8]. In HGSOC, lesions exhibit rapid kinetics, with average doubling times of 2.3 months for ovarian masses and approximately 2 months for omental deposits [9]. Despite this clinical significance, the molecular drivers linking TDT to tumor progression, microenvironment remodeling, and chemoresistance are poorly defined. Genes associated with TDT therefore represent an underutilized resource for refining molecular classification and identifying therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Existing transcriptomic subtypes of HGSOC (differentiated, immunoreactive, mesenchymal, and proliferative) have limited clinical utility, as subtype assignment is often driven by varying cell-type admixtures rather than intrinsic epithelial programs [5,10,11]. There is consequently a need for classification systems that incorporate additional biological dimensions, such as proliferation kinetics and chemoresistance phenotypes. Similar machine-learning frameworks have recently been applied to mine TDT-related genes in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma[12]. However, studies that systematically integrate TDT-related genes with chemoresistance phenotypes in ovarian cancer remain lacking. In particular, no prior work has combined single-cell profiles of refractory and resistant tumors with a proliferation-focused gene signature and experimental protein-level validation.

In this study, we hypothesized that genes associated with chemoresistance-driven proliferation play critical roles in HGSOC progression and tumor microenvironment modulation. By integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data, we systematically characterized chemoresistance-associated tumor doubling time-related genes (TDTRGs), established a robust prognostic signature, and experimentally confirmed hub-gene overexpression in cisplatin-resistant cell lines. Our findings provide a TDTRG-based framework for molecular classification and prognostic stratification, while nominating potential therapeutic targets for precision oncology.

Materials and methods

Collection and preparation of transcriptomic datasets

Transcriptomic profiles and corresponding clinical data for ovarian cancer (OV) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) portal. The TCGA-OV cohort comprised 379 tumor samples. In addition, 88 normal fallopian tube or ovarian tissue samples from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project were included as controls. A scRNA-seq dataset, GSE154600, was retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository; this dataset contains samples from ovarian cancer patients classified as refractory, resistant, or sensitive to chemotherapy. For external validation of the prognostic model, an independent bulk ovarian cancer cohort (GSE26193) was also downloaded from GEO. Where appropriate, normalized expression matrices were converted to transcripts per million (TPM) and subsequently log₂-transformed [13]. A list of chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes was assembled based on previously published literature [8].

Differential expression analysis and identification of chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes

To assess differential expression within the GSE154600 scRNA-seq dataset, we applied the FindMarkers function from the Seurat package (version 5.2.1) using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Two independent comparisons were performed: refractory versus sensitive, and resistant versus sensitive. Genes with an absolute log₂ fold change > 1 and an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. For bulk transcriptomic data, the DESeq2 package (v1.36.0) was used to identify genes differentially expressed between TCGA-OV tumor samples and GTEx normal tissues, applying the same significance thresholds. We then intersected three gene sets: (i) genes upregulated in refractory or resistant samples from the scRNA-seq analysis, (ii) genes upregulated in TCGA-OV tumors relative to normal tissues from the bulk analysis, and (iii) a curated list of tumor doubling time-related genes. This intersection yielded a core set of 105 chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes.

The curated list of tumor doubling time-related genes was used as a prior-knowledge filter to enrich for biologically relevant candidates associated with tumor proliferation, rather than as direct experimental evidence that these genes regulate tumor doubling time in the present ovarian cancer cohort.

Functional enrichment analysis of the 105 chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the 105 chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.6.0). Multiple testing correction was conducted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and an adjusted P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Dot plots and enrichment network maps were generated for visualization using the enrichplot and ggplot2 packages [14,15].

Consensus clustering for molecular subtyping based on chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes

To determine whether the 105 chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes could define clinically relevant molecular subgroups in HGSOC, unsupervised consensus clustering was performed on the TCGA-OV cohort with the ConsensusClusterPlus package (version 1.66.0). The procedure used 1000 resampling iterations, with 80% of samples and 80% of features randomly selected in each iteration, and k-means as the clustering algorithm. Optimal cluster number was selected by jointly evaluating the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves, the delta-area plot, and the consensus matrix heatmaps [16]. Survival differences among the resulting subtypes were compared by Kaplan–Meier analysis using the log-rank test, and principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to visualize the separation of the identified clusters.

Following identification of the two subtypes (C1 and C2), the immune microenvironment was further characterized using three complementary algorithms. The ESTIMATE algorithm was used to calculate StromalScore, ImmuneScore and ESTIMATEScore. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed with the GSVA package using immune cell signature gene sets. The CIBERSORT algorithm with the LM22 signature matrix was employed to quantify the relative proportions of 22 immune cell types. Differences between subtypes were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Machine learning-based development of the prognostic signature

To derive a clinically applicable prognostic signature from the 105 chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes, we implemented a multi-algorithm machine-learning framework. Ten survival-oriented algorithms-random survival forest (RSF), elastic net (Enet), Lasso, ridge regression, stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, partial least squares Cox (plsRcox), supervised principal components (SuperPC), gradient boosting machine (GBM) and survival support vector machine (survival-SVM) [16]-were systematically combined into 117 distinct algorithm–feature-selection pipelines using the Mime1 package. Models were trained on the TCGA-OV cohort (n = 379) with overall survival as the endpoint and independently evaluated in the GSE26193 cohort (n = 60). Concordance index (C-index) was used to rank predictive performance; the pipeline with the highest mean C-index across both datasets was selected as optimal. Genes with non-zero coefficients in this top-performing model were retained as the final signature. A continuous risk score was calculated as the weighted linear sum of these genes according to their model coefficients, and patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups by the median risk score.

Survival analysis of individual hub genes using the Kaplan‑Meier Plotter

To evaluate the prognostic significance of the nine hub genes identified by the machine-learning approach, we used the Kaplan–Meier Plotter ovarian cancer module. Patients were stratified according to the optimal expression cutoff for each gene, and overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and post-progression survival (PPS) were analyzed. For each gene, hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals and log-rank P-values were calculated.

Single‑cell expression characterization of the nine hub genes

The GSE154600 scRNA-seq dataset was processed using the Seurat pipeline (version 5.2.1) [5]. Cells with fewer than 200 or >5000 detected genes, or with a mitochondrial read proportion exceeding 10%, were excluded. After normalization and integration, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied for dimensionality reduction. Cell identities were assigned based on well-established marker genes. To visualize the expression patterns of the nine hub genes, UMAP feature plots and violin plots were generated, with cells stratified by clinical response (refractory, resistant, or sensitive). Group-wise statistical comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Additionally, virtual knockout simulations for six experimentally validated hub genes were performed using scTenifoldKnk [17] in malignant epithelial cells, with significantly perturbed genes (P < 0.05) subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses.

Cell lines and culture conditions

The A2780 cell line was purchased from CELL RESEARCH (China, #ZQ0486), and the TYKnu cell line was obtained from Cellosaurus (China, #CVCL_1776). Cisplatin-resistant A2780-DDP and TYKnu-DDP cell lines were purchased from Shanghai Jinyuan Biotechnology (China). A2780 and TYKnu cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Procell, #PM150110) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; LONSERA, #S711– 001S) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; Procell, #PB180120) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. A2780-DDP cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 15% FBS, 1000 ng/mL cisplatin (MCE, #15,663-27– 1), and 1% P/S under the same conditions. TYKnu-DDP cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 800 ng/mL cisplatin, and 1% P/S at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Prior to all experiments, the identity of each cell line was authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely excluded using a PCR-based detection kit (Vazyme, #D101– 01), and all cell lines tested negative.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cell lines using the FastPure Total RNA Kit (Vazyme, #RC101– 01), and cDNA was synthesized using the PureScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, #PR211– 02). Primers were synthesized by Tsingke (Beijing, China). Specific transcripts were amplified with ChamQ SYBR Master Mix (Vazyme, #Q711– 02) on a Roche LightCycler 480II (Germany, #05,015,243,001). Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated as fold changes relative to the control group.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Solarbio, #R0020) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Solarbio, #P1260) for 30 min on ice. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto 0.22 µm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, #ISEQ00010) at 300 mA for 80 min. Membranes were blocked with protein-free rapid blocking buffer (Epizyme, #PS108P) for 15 min, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies and subsequent incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. After washing with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (TBST; Servicebio, #G0001 and #G5058), signals were detected using Omni-ECL™ (Epizyme, #SQ201) on a chemiluminescence imaging system (Cytiva, Amersham ImageQuant 800). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

The primary antibodies used and their dilutions were as follows: anti-SURVIVIN (1:2000, Proteintech, #10,508-1-AP), anti-CENPH (1:8000, Proteintech, #12,841-1-AP), anti-CKAP2 (1:8000, Proteintech, #25,486-1-AP), anti-PAK1IP1 (1:2000, Thermo Fisher, #PA5– 100,854), anti-SDF2L1 (1:4000, Proteintech, #20,888-1-AP), anti-PBK/SPK (1:10,000, Proteintech, #16,110-1-AP), and anti-GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech, #60,004-1-Ig).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression was used for both univariate and multivariate analyses. Model discrimination was evaluated using Harrell’s C-index. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Results

Single-cell landscape of chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes in ovarian cancer

To delineate the cellular ecosystem underlying treatment resistance in ovarian cancer, we analyzed single-cell RNA-sequencing data from high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma samples in the GSE154600 dataset. After rigorous quality control, dimensionality reduction, and unsupervised clustering, 15 transcriptionally distinct cell populations were identified based on the expression of canonical marker genes (Fig. 1C). These populations included cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), CD8_ActMem, CD8_Eff, CD8_Exh, endothelial cells, gamma-delta T cells, inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), inflammatory macrophages, M2-like macrophages, naive B cells, PAX8+ tumor cells, pericytes, plasmablasts, SPP1+/TREM2+ lipid-associated macrophages (LAMs), and tumor cells. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used to visualize the spatial distribution of these cell types (Fig. 1A). Circular stacked bar plots revealed marked differences in cell-type composition across clinical response groups (Fig. 1B). The sensitive group was characterized by a higher proportion of CD8_ActMem cells. In contrast, the resistant group exhibited enriched fractions of PAX8+ tumor cells, gamma-delta T cells, SPP1+/TREM2+ LAMs, and tumor cells. To identify genes associated with treatment resistance and tumor proliferation kinetics, we performed differential expression analysis for three independent comparisons: refractory versus sensitive, resistant versus sensitive (both from scRNA-seq data), and TCGA-OV versus GTEx (bulk data). The results were visualized using multi-group circular volcano plots (Fig. 1D, E) and a gradient volcano plot (Fig. 1F), with significance thresholds set at |log₂ fold change| > 1 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. By intersecting the upregulated genes from these three comparisons with a curated list of tumor doubling time-related genes, we identified 105 core chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes (Fig. 1G).

Functional enrichment analysis of these 105 chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes revealed their prominent involvement in cell division and genomic stability. GO biological process analysis showed significant enrichment in terms including chromosome segregation, organelle fission, nuclear division, nuclear chromosome segregation, mitotic nuclear division, sister chromatid segregation, mitotic sister chromatid segregation, regulation of chromosome organization, regulation of chromosome segregation, and kinetochore organization (Fig. 1H). KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated significant enrichment in five pathways: cell cycle, oocyte meiosis, progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation, cellular senescence, and the p53 signaling pathway (Fig. 1I).

Using the expression profiles of the 105 chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes, we performed consensus clustering with the ConsensusClusterPlus algorithm to classify patients in the TCGA-OV cohort. Comprehensive evaluation of the consensus matrix, cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves, delta area metrics, and sample tracking plots identified K = 2 as the most stable clustering solution, defining two molecular subtypes designated C1 and C2 (Fig. 2E-H). The robustness of this classification was further supported by principal component analysis (PCA), which demonstrated clear separation between the two subtypes (Fig. 2I).

To characterize the immune microenvironment of the two subtypes, we employed three complementary algorithms: ESTIMATE, ssGSEA, and CIBERSORT. ESTIMATE analysis revealed that the C1 subtype exhibited significantly higher StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore compared with C2 (P < 0.001; Fig. 2J). Concordant results from ssGSEA and CIBERSORT indicated that infiltration scores for most immune cell types were elevated in C1 relative to C2 (Fig. 2K, L).

Differential gene expression analysis between the C2 and C1 subtypes is presented as a volcano plot (Fig. 2M). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed that genes upregulated in C2 were significantly enriched in pathways related to DNA replication, M phase, cell cycle checkpoints, DNA repair, DNA replication pre-initiation, and DNA methylation (Fig. 2N). In contrast, genes downregulated in C2 (i.e., enriched in C1) were associated with the complement cascade, initial triggering of complement, creation of C4 and C2 activators, Fcγ receptor activation, interferon-gamma signaling, and antigen processing and presentation (Fig. 2O).

Machine learning-driven development and validation of a prognostic signature

To construct a robust prognostic model for ovarian cancer, we incorporated the 105 chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes into a comprehensive machine-learning framework comprising ten algorithms and 117 distinct algorithm–feature selection combinations. The model was trained on the TCGA-OV cohort and independently validated in the GSE26193 cohort. Among all combinations evaluated, the random survival forest (RSF) model achieved the highest mean concordance index (C-index) of 0.685 in the validation set and was therefore selected as the optimal model (Fig. 3A). Using the median risk score derived from the RSF model, patients in each cohort were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients in the high-risk group had significantly worse overall survival (OS) than those in the low-risk group in both the TCGA-OV cohort (log-rank P < 0.001) and the GSE26193 validation cohort (P = 0.043) (Fig. 3C, D). A meta-analysis of univariate Cox regression results across the two cohorts showed a significant hazard ratio (HR) in TCGA-OV (P < 0.001) and in GSE26193 (P = 0.043). The fixed-effect model yielded a pooled HR with P < 0.001, whereas the random-effect model gave P = 0.103 (Fig. 3E).

We further benchmarked our risk signature against previously published gene signatures in the same cohorts. To the best of our knowledge, our model ranked first in both the TCGA-OV and GSE26193 cohorts when compared with multiple existing prognostic signatures, indicating superior predictive performance (Fig. 3F, G).

Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to assess the predictive accuracy of the RSF model. The area under the curve (AUC) values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival were 0.832, 0.922, and 0.921, respectively, in the TCGA-OV training cohort, and 0.447, 0.556, and 0.654, respectively, in the GSE26193 validation cohort (Fig. 3H-J). The RSF model identified nine genes with non-zero coefficients that collectively constituted the prognostic signature: BIRC5, CENPH, CKAP2, PAK1IP1, PBK, SDF2L1, TEAD4, TPM3, and UBE2T.

Prognostic evaluation of the nine hub genes in ovarian cancer

To evaluate the clinical relevance of the nine genes selected by the machine-learning approach, we analyzed their associations with survival outcomes using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter ovarian cancer database. Overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and post-progression survival (PPS) were assessed for each gene by dichotomizing patients according to the optimal expression cutoff (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6). For overall survival (Fig. 4), high expression of CENPH, CKAP2, PBK, TEAD4, TPM3, and UBE2T was significantly associated with worse OS, whereas high expression of SDF2L1 correlated with better OS. No significant associations with OS were observed for BIRC5 or PAK1IP1. For progression-free survival (Fig. 5), high expression of CENPH, CKAP2, TEAD4, and TPM3 was associated with poorer PFS. Interestingly, high expression of BIRC5 and SDF2L1 correlated with better PFS. No significant differences in PFS were detected for PAK1IP1, PBK, or UBE2T. For post-progression survival (Fig. 6), high expression of BIRC5, PAK1IP1, and SDF2L1 was significantly associated with better PPS, whereas high expression of UBE2T correlated with worse PPS. Gene set enrichment analysis of the perturbed genes, summarised as colour-coded bar plots combining Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), Molecular Function (MF), and KEGG pathways (Fig. 9M-R), revealed a core convergent signature across all six knockouts. Consistently enriched KEGG pathways included Oxidative phosphorylation, Ribosome, Spliceosome, and Chemical carcinogenesis- reactive oxygen species; corresponding GO terms comprised ATP metabolic process, respiratory electron transport chain, oxidative phosphorylation, ribosome biogenesis, cytoplasmic translation, and molecular functions such as NADH dehydrogenase activity, electron transfer activity, and structural constituent of ribosome. This shared pattern implicates mitochondrial energy metabolism, protein synthesis, and RNA splicing as central functional modules perturbed by each of the hub genes.

Beyond this common core, distinct pathway-specific features emerged. CENPH knockout uniquely enriched Glutathione metabolism and Ferroptosis, suggesting a role in redox balance and iron-dependent cell death. TEAD4 knockout specifically affected Cell adhesion molecules, Phagosome, and Antigen processing and presentation, along with BP terms related to B cell mediated immunity and MHC class II complex assembly, indicating an immune-modulatory function for TEAD4. The remaining genes (CKAP2, PBK, TPM3, UBE2T) largely recapitulated the core mitochondrial-ribosomal signature without major pathway-specific deviations. Collectively, these results indicate that the six hub genes converge on fundamental bioenergetic and biosynthetic processes, while CENPH and TEAD4 also engage distinct redox and immune-associated networks, respectively.

Discussion

In this study, we performed an integrative multi‑omics analysis to systematically characterize chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes in HGSOC, focusing on the underexplored concept of chemoresistance-associated TDTRGs. By integrating single‑cell RNA‑seq data with bulk transcriptomic profiles, we identified 105 core genes predominantly enriched in cell cycle, chromosome segregation, and p53 signaling pathways. Consensus clustering revealed two molecular subtypes (C1 and C2) with distinct immune and functional profiles. A robust nine-gene prognostic signature (BIRC5, CENPH, CKAP2, PAK1IP1, PBK, SDF2L1, TEAD4, TPM3, UBE2T) was constructed using a comprehensive machine-learning framework of 117 algorithm combinations and validated across independent cohorts. Single-cell analysis showed predominant enrichment of these hub genes in malignant epithelial cells, SPP1+/TREM2+ macrophages, and CAFs. Furthermore, scTenifoldKnk virtual knockout simulations revealed a convergent core of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, ribosome biogenesis, and spliceosome pathways, with CENPH uniquely affecting glutathione metabolism and ferroptosis, and TEAD4 modulating immune-related processes. These findings were experimentally supported in cisplatin-resistant cell lines. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically link tumor growth kinetics with single-cell ecosystem biology in HGSOC, connecting proliferative states to macrophage-, CAF-, and ECM-associated interactions and nominating potential therapeutic targets.

The clinical importance of tumor proliferation kinetics in ovarian cancer has been well recognized, with previous studies demonstrating the exceptionally rapid growth of HGSOC [9]. Despite this established clinical relevance, the molecular mechanisms linking tumor proliferation to chemoresistance in HGSOC remain incompletely understood. The present study addresses this gap by defining a transcriptomic signature of chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes and mapping their cell-type-specific distribution, thereby connecting macroscopic tumor growth kinetics with microscopic cellular heterogeneity.

Previous attempts to classify HGSOC into discrete transcriptomic subtypes have been controversial. The TCGA study initially identified four molecular subtypes-differentiated, immunoreactive, mesenchymal, and proliferative-based on bulk tumor data [11]. However, subsequent single-cell studies have questionedthe robustness of these classifications, demonstrating that subtype assignments are largely driven by varying proportions of immune and stromal cells rather than intrinsic properties of malignant epithelial cells [5]. Geistlinger et al. used single-cell sequencing of over 40,000 tumor cells to demonstrate that epithelial cells within the same tumor exhibit transitional expression patterns spanning the differentiated-to-proliferative spectrum [5]. They further showed that immunoreactive subtype calls were largely attributable to myeloid cell infiltration, whereas mesenchymal calls corresponded to stromal cell abundance. These findings led to the proposal that discrete transcriptomic subtypes in HGSOC should be replaced by a continuous model of tumor evolution. Our results are consistent with this paradigm. The two molecular subtypes (C1 and C2) defined by chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes displayed distinct immune infiltration profiles. The immune-enriched C1 subtype exhibited significantly higher StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore, along with greater infiltration of most immune cell types, compared with the proliferative C2 subtype. In contrast, the C2 subtype, which was associated with poorer prognosis, was enriched for cell cycle progression, DNA replication, and DNA repair pathways, whereas the C1 subtype showed enrichment for immune-related processes, including the complement cascade, antigen processing and presentation, and interferon-gamma signaling. This dichotomy supports the notion that HGSOC evolves from a more differentiated, immune-active state toward a proliferative, genomically unstable phenotype[5,18]. Such dysregulation of proliferation-associated genes is not unique to ovarian cancer. For example, in hepatocellular carcinoma, promoter hypomethylation and ZNF384 binding activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling through NOL9, which may similarly contribute to the proliferative phenotype of the C2 subtype in HGSOC [19].

The nine hub genes in our prognostic signature have well-established roles in cell cycle regulation, chromosome stability, and tumor progression. BIRC5 (survivin) is a key inhibitor of apoptosis that promotes tumor cell survival and has been linked to chemoresistance and poor prognosis in ovarian cancer [20,21]. CENPH, CKAP2, PBK, and UBE2T are all involved in mitotic progression and chromosome segregation. CENPH is a core component of the kinetochore, and its upregulation has been linked to poor prognosis in LUAD [22]. PBK, a serine/threonine kinase that phosphorylates p53, has been associated with aggressive disease and reduced survival in ovarian cancer [23,24]. Notably, SDF2L1 exhibited a protective effect in our survival analyses, with higher expression correlating with improved overall survival, progression-free survival, and post-progression survival. This finding aligns with recent reports identifying SDF2L1 as a potential tumor suppressor in ovarian cancer [[25], [26], [27]]. Of note, although SDF2L1 mRNA was upregulated in TYKnu-DDP cells, its protein levels did not differ significantly between sensitive and resistant cell lines. This discrepancy may reflect post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms or cell line-specific effects that warrant further investigation. Collectively, the nine hub genes displayed divergent prognostic associations across different survival endpoints, underscoring their potential value as both risk stratifiers and candidate therapeutic targets. TEAD4, a transcription factor with established oncogenic roles in multiple cancers, was also overexpressed in serous ovarian cancer and has been shown to promote proliferation, migration, and invasion through transcriptional regulation of PLAGL2 [28]. TPM3 contributes to cytoskeletal organization and has been implicated in epithelial-mesenchymal transition and metastasis [29]. The coordinated expression of these genes in high-risk patients suggests that the aggressive phenotype associated with chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes arises from interconnected biological processes centered on proliferation, chromosomal instability, and cytoskeletal remodeling. Importantly, chemoresistance is often multifactorial and can also arise through mechanisms beyond proliferation-related pathways. In addition to these processes, chemoresistance can also develop through enhanced drug efflux and apoptosis inhibition, as recently demonstrated for PUF60 in gastric cancer [30].

Single‑cell analysis revealed that all nine signature genes were highly expressed in malignant epithelial cells, SPP1+/TREM2+ macrophages, and CAFs. SPP1+/TREM2+ macrophages represent a lipid‑associated macrophage subset previously linked to immunosuppression poor prognosis in ovarian cancer [31]. Their enrichment for prognostic genes suggests that these macrophages may contribute to tumor progression not only through their own proliferative activity but also via paracrine signaling to malignant cells. The distinct expression patterns of SDF2L1 and BIRC5 across cell types further highlight the context-dependent functions of these genes and underscore the value of single-cell approaches in biomarker research.

Although our experimental validation primarily confirmed the association of the hub genes with cisplatin resistance, substantial evidence supports a close link between enhanced cell proliferation and chemoresistance in ovarian cancer. Dysregulation of cell-cycle control, particularly CCNE1 amplification, contributes to primary platinum resistance by allowing tumor cells to progress through the cell cycle despite DNA damage [32,33]. These observations provide a theoretical rationale for prioritizing chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes in HGSOC.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes were identified from a single scRNA-seq dataset (GSE154600) combined with a curated gene list; additional relevant genes may not have been captured. Second, although the prognostic signature was validated in an independent cohort (GSE26193), prospective validation in larger, multi-center cohorts is required before clinical application. Third, our analysis was limited to transcriptomic data and did not incorporate proteomic or metabolomic layers. Fourth, the single-cell data were derived from only six patients, and larger single-cell cohorts would enhance the generalizability of our findings. Finally, although we validated the upregulation of hub genes in cisplatin-resistant cell lines by qPCR and Western blot, we did not perform gene knockdown or overexpression experiments to establish their causal roles in chemoresistance or tumor proliferation. Furthermore, the functional significance of these genes was not confirmed using in vivo animal models. These limitations point to important future research directions. Nevertheless, this study provides a novel framework linking tumor growth kinetics with single-cell ecosystem biology, offering new insights into chemoresistance and nominating potential therapeutic targets in HGSOC.

In conclusion, we identified a robust nine-gene chemoresistance-associated proliferation genes signature with strong prognostic value in HGSOC, offering a potential tool for risk stratification and personalized management. By integrating single-cell transcriptomics with machine learning and linking tumor proliferation biology to the tumor microenvironment, this study provides new molecular insights and identifies promising therapeutic targets. Future prospective validation and functional studies of chemoresistance-associated proliferation gene-related pathways will be essential for clinical translation.
