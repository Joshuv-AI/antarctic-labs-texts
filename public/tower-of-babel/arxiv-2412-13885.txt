Q-ball perturbations with more details: linear analysis vs lattice
Aleksandr Azatov

Abstract




SISSA 21/2024/FISI

Abstract

We analyze in detail the interactions between non-topological soliton (Q-ball) and its perturbations. We extend the previous literature by carefully identifying the domain of applicability of linear analysis as well discussion of the FLS Q-balls. Applications to the early universe physics are briefly commented.

\displaystyle E^{Q}_{\text{Q-ball}}<Qm_{\Phi}.

Given the charge conservation and the energy considerations, such configurations are stable.
These objects have garnered significant interest within particle physics phenomenology community due to their potential applications in dark matter model building and their possible connection to the baryon asymmetry of the universe

Unlike topological solitons, the production of Q-balls does not occur via the Kibble mechanism.
Instead, it can take place during first- or second-order phase transitions, where regions with net non-zero charge are compressed by bubble walls (or domain walls)
[

Later, the Q-ball distribution can evolve further by accreting free particles from surrounding plasma

in a process dubbed solitosynthesis.
A precise calculation of this process requires detailed knowledge of Q-ball interaction with the surrounding plasma particles, particularly with the quanta of the
\phi

The analysis of these interactions will be the primary focus of this paper.

Recently the Ref.
[

,
24

have analyzed the interactions of the Q-ball with its perturbations, focusing on the processes when the Q-ball energy can be extracted.
In our paper we extend these results in the following directions:

•

We study in detail the applicability of the linear treatment of the perturbations by making comparison with lattice simulation, and highlight the possible applications for the process of solitosynthesis.

•

We provide an intuitive understanding of the energy extraction process by matching energy of the perturbations to the change of the Q-ball self-energy.

•

We provide the analysis of the energy extraction process for the Q-ball model with two fields (the simplest UV complete lagrangian) both in linear and non-linear regimes.

2
One-field Q-balls

Let us start by reviewing the Q-ball solutions and their perturbations following the recent literature
[
23
,
24
]
.
Both of the mentioned references have considered the model with one complex scalar field with the action given by:

\displaystyle S[\Phi]=\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\;\left[|\partial_{\mu}\Phi|^{2}-V\right],\qquad V=\mu^{2}\,|\Phi|^{2}-\lambda\,|\Phi|^{4}+g\,|\Phi|^{6},

(2)

where we have used mostly negative metric
\eta_{\mu\nu}=\rm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)
.
This action can be written in terms of dimensionless quantities if we perform the transformations
x\to x/\mu,\,\Phi\to\mu\,\Phi/\sqrt{\lambda},\,g\to\lambda^{2}\,g/\mu^{2}
,

\displaystyle S[\Phi]=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\;\left[|\partial_{\mu}\Phi|^{2}-V\right],\quad\qquad V=|\Phi|^{2}-|\Phi|^{4}+g\,|\Phi|^{6}.

(3)

We will use the action in this form for the rest of this section.
The Q-ball is a time-dependent classical solution of the equations of motion of the following form:

\displaystyle\Phi_{Q}(\mathbf{x},t)=\frac{\phi_{Q}(r)}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-i\omega_{Q}t},

(4)

where the function
\phi_{Q}
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\phi_{Q}^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{2}{r}\phi_{Q}^{\prime}(r)+\dfrac{1}{2}\,\omega_{Q}^{2}\phi_{Q}(r)-\frac{\partial V}{\partial\Phi^{\ast}}\Big|_{\Phi=\phi_{Q}/\sqrt{2}}=0,

(5)

and
~{}^{\prime}~
stands for derivative in radial direction.
We have assumed for simplicity that the solution has zero angular momentum (non-rotating Q-ball).
The function
\phi_{Q}
satisfies the boundary conditions
\phi_{Q}^{\prime}(r=0)=0
(requirement of regularity at the origin), and
\phi_{Q}(r\to\infty)=0
(requirement of finite energy).
For the quantum stability, the Q-ball must satisfy:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,r^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\big(\phi_{Q}^{\prime\,2}+\omega_{Q}^{2}\phi_{Q}^{2}\big)+V(\phi_{Q})\right)<\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,r^{2}\,\omega_{Q}\phi_{Q}^{2}.

(6)

The equation of motion for the
\eta_{\pm}(r)
functions then becomes:

\displaystyle{\bf\nabla^{2}}\eta_{\pm}+\left[\omega_{\pm}^{2}-U\right]\eta_{\pm}-W\eta^{\ast}_{\mp}=0.

(10)

Far from the Q-ball, where
\phi_{Q}\to 0

\eta_{+},\eta_{-}

Interestingly as was shown in the original references

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{\eta}\equiv 2\operatorname{Im}\left(\eta_{+}^{\ast}\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\eta_{+}-\eta_{-}^{\ast}\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\eta_{-}\right),

(11)

where in our notations the bold letters,
{\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{J}}}
, denote the spatial vectors. Then obviously the divergence
\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{\eta}=0
, and the corresponding flux through any closed surface must be equal to zero.
We proceed by analysis of the S-wave scattering, which is sufficient to grasp the most important qualitative features of the Q-ball interaction with its perturbations and the discussion for non-spherical waves is presented in the Appendix.
B
.
For the S-waves the equation of motion becomes

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime\prime}_{\pm}+\frac{2\eta^{\prime}_{\pm}}{r}+\left[\omega_{\pm}^{2}-U\right]\eta_{\pm}-W\eta^{\ast}_{\mp}=0.

(12)

Far from the Q-ball the potential becomes constant, and consequently we obtain the solutions of free spherical waves

\displaystyle\eta_{\pm}(r\to\infty)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left\lvert k_{\pm}\right\rvert}\,r}\left[A_{\pm}^{\rm in}e^{-ik_{\pm}r}+A_{\pm}^{\rm out}e^{ik_{\pm}r}\right],

(13)

\displaystyle k_{\pm}=\pm\mathrm{sign}(\omega)\sqrt{(\omega_{\pm}^{2}-1)}=\mathrm{sign}(\omega_{\pm})\sqrt{(\omega_{\pm}^{2}-1)}.

The
\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{\eta}
current conservation imposes the following constraints on the amplitudes:

\displaystyle\boxed{\left\lvert A^{\rm out}_{+}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert A^{\rm out}_{-}\right\rvert^{2}=|A^{\rm in}_{+}|^{2}+|A^{\rm in}_{-}|^{2}.}

(14)

Let us comment on the various symmetry properties of these perturbations.
The system in Eq. (
10
) is linear, which leads to the following relation between the expansion coefficients:

\displaystyle\vec{A}^{\rm out}=S\vec{A}^{\,\rm in},\qquad\vec{A}^{\,\rm out,in}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A_{+}^{\,\rm out,in}\\
\left(A_{-}^{\,\rm out,in}\right)^{\ast}\end{array}\right]

The matrix
S
must be unitary due to the Eq. (
14
) and symmetric, since the Eq. (
10
) is real (see for details and proof Appendix
A
).
Additionally, we note that exchanging
\omega\to-\omega
simply flips the modes
\eta_{+}
and
\eta_{-}
.
This together with the symmetries of the matrix
S
leads to the following relation

\displaystyle\left|\frac{A_{\pm}^{\rm out}(\omega)}{A_{-}^{\rm in}(\omega)}\right|_{A_{+}^{\rm in}=0}=\left|\frac{A_{\mp}^{\rm out}(\omega)}{A_{+}^{\rm in}(\omega)}\right|_{A_{-}^{\rm in}=0},

(18)

and we refer for the derivation and more detailed discussion to the Appendix.
A
.
Similarly to the current
\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{\eta}
, we can also build the
U(1)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{Q}=(-i)\big[\Phi_{1}^{\ast}\,\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\,\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{1}\,\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\,\Phi^{\ast}_{1}\big]

(19)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{E}=(-1)\big[\dot{\Phi}_{1}^{\ast}\,\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\,\Phi_{1}-\dot{\Phi}_{1}\,\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\,\Phi_{1}^{\ast}\big].

(20)

In the asymptotic region, the average fluxes of charge and energy are

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{Q}

\displaystyle=(4\pi)^{-1}\lim_{r\to\infty}\oint\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}\cdot\langle\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{Q}\rangle_{T\Omega}=2s_{\omega}\sum_{\pm}s\,\left(\left\lvert A_{s}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert A_{s}^{\rm in}\right\rvert^{2}\right),

(21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{E}

\displaystyle=(4\pi)^{-1}\lim_{r\to\infty}\oint\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}\cdot\langle\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{E}\rangle_{T\Omega}=2s_{\omega}\sum_{\pm}s\,\omega_{s}\,\left(\left\lvert A_{s}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert A_{s}^{\rm in}\right\rvert^{2}\right),

(22)

where
\langle\dots\rangle_{T\Omega}
denotes the average over sufficient long time and over all directions in space.

After this preliminary discussion we can proceed to the discussion of the charge and energy exchange between Q-ball and its perturbations.
Similarly to the Ref
[
24
]

\displaystyle 1+Z_{Q}\equiv\frac{{\cal F}_{Q}^{\rm out}}{{\cal F}_{Q}^{\rm in}}=\left\lvert\frac{\left\lvert A_{+}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert A_{-}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert A_{+}^{\rm in}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert A_{-}^{\rm in}\right\rvert^{2}}\right\rvert,\qquad 1+Z_{E}\equiv\frac{{\cal F}_{E}^{\rm out}}{{\cal F}_{E}^{\rm in}}=\left\lvert\frac{\omega_{+}\left\lvert A_{+}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}-\omega_{-}\left\lvert A_{-}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}}{\omega_{+}\left\lvert A_{+}^{\rm in}\right\rvert^{2}-\omega_{-}\left\lvert A_{-}^{\rm in}\right\rvert^{2}}\right\rvert.

(23)

Note that
[
23
]
used another notion of amplification factors based on the charge/energy contained within a sphere far away from the source, instead of the fluxes through the surface of such a sphere.
In our paper, we only use the definition in Eq. (
23
), hence for convenience the superscript
\mathcal{F}
will be dropped from now on without causing confusion.

A_{\rm in}^{+}=0
or

).
Then using Eq. (

\pm

, implying in principle there is energy and charge exchange between the Q-ball and the perturbations.

The energy exchange with the Q ball at the linear level can only occur due to the following processes (we are using the total
U(1)
charge conservation )

\displaystyle Q+\phi\to Q+\phi

\displaystyle Q+\phi^{\dagger}\to Q+\phi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle Q+\phi\to(Q+2)+\phi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle Q+\phi^{\dagger}\to(Q-2)+\phi,

(24)

where
(Q\pm 2)
stand for the Q-balls with the corresponding charges and
\phi,\phi^{\dagger}
are the quanta of the field
\Phi
.
The first two processes are just elastic scatterings, so they cannot extract or absorb neither charge nor energy.
To analyze the other two let us assume that Q-ball has positive charge
Q>0
, then in the process
Q+\phi\to(Q+2)+\phi^{\dagger}
its charge is increased, so the energy will be increased as well, thus the energy is absorbed from the perturbation.
Similarly the process
Q+\phi^{\dagger}\to(Q-2)+\phi
leads to the decrease of the charge of the Q-ball and extraction of energy.
From this we can immediately see that energy extraction happens only if the incoming particle has the charge opposite to the Q-ball charge.
As a sanity check we can calculate the energy amplification from this considerations and compare it with Eq. (
23
)

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}_{E}(A_{+}^{\rm in}=0,A_{-}^{\rm in}=1)=\frac{|A_{+}^{\rm out}|^{2}}{|\omega_{-}|}\big[E(Q)-E(Q-2)\big],

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}_{E}(A_{+}^{\rm in}=1,A_{-}^{\rm in}=0)=\frac{|A_{-}^{\rm out}|^{2}}{\omega_{+}}\big[E(Q)-E(Q+2)\big],

(25)

where the factor
\left\lvert A_{+}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}
takes into account the probability of the transition
Q+\phi^{\dagger}\to(Q-2)+\phi
.
Comparing with the expression in Eq. (
23
) we can see that quantities indeed match approximately,

\displaystyle Z_{E}(A_{+}^{\rm in}=0,A_{-}^{\rm in}=1)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\omega_{+}\left\lvert A_{+}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}-\omega_{-}\left\lvert A_{-}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert\omega_{-}\right\rvert}-1=\dfrac{2\omega_{Q}}{\left\lvert\omega_{-}\right\rvert}\left\lvert A_{+}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2(dE_{Q}/dQ_{Q})\left\lvert A_{+}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert\omega_{-}\right\rvert}\approx\dfrac{\left\lvert A_{+}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert\omega_{-}\right\rvert}\left[E(Q)-E(Q-2)\right],

(26)

where we have used the relations (see for example
[
5
]
,
[
28
]

\displaystyle\frac{dE(Q)}{dQ}=\omega_{Q},~~E(Q+2)-E(Q)=2\omega_{Q}+2\frac{d^{2}E_{Q}}{dQ^{2}}+...

(27)

It is clear that two expressions for the energy extraction are equal up to the second and higher derivative terms

\displaystyle Z_{E}(A_{+}^{\rm in}=0,A_{-}^{\rm in}=1)\simeq\tilde{Z}_{E}(A_{+}^{\rm in}=0,A_{-}^{\rm in}=1)\left(1-\frac{2\frac{d^{2}E}{dQ^{2}}}{E(Q)-E(Q-2)}+\dots\right).

(28)

For large values of the Q-ball charge the second term scales as
Q^{-1}
, and is strongly suppressed.
Interestingly the sign of it is always positive, since the Q-ball classical stability dictates
[

that

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}E_{Q}}{dQ^{2}}=\frac{d\omega_{Q}}{dQ}<0.

(29)

On the wave-equation side, the energy extraction can be seen from the following.
Let us assume that

and

2.1
Q-ball evolution in the early universe

The discussion of the linear perturbations and the selection rules for the Q-ball and its perturbation interactions lead to a very interesting conclusion.
Suppose Q-ball is formed and in the early universe and we are looking at its evolution due to the interactions with the surrounding plasma.
If the plasma particles are energetic enough, both of the reactions

\displaystyle Q+\phi\to(Q+2)+\phi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle Q+\phi^{\dagger}\to(Q-2)+\phi

(32)

are open, even though the rates are different (see Fig.
4
).

Figure 4
:

We can see that at classical level once the energy of

becomes less than
\omega_{+}<1+2\omega_{Q}
corresponding
\eta_{-}

becomes bounded, and classically we can have only the following processes:

\displaystyle E<1+2\omega_{Q}

\displaystyle Q+\phi^{\dagger}\to(Q-2)+\phi\hbox{~~open}

\displaystyle Q+\phi\to(Q+2)+\phi^{\dagger}\hbox{ ~~forbidden}.

(33)

Therefore, in this range of energies Q-balls can only lose their charge due to the interactions with plasma.
Note that a very similar conclusion can be obtained just by considering the energy conservation conditions. Let us suppose that the
\phi
particle has energy
E_{*}
then

\displaystyle Q+\phi\to(Q+2)+\phi^{\dagger}\hbox{ is forbidden if}

\displaystyle m_{\Phi}>E(Q)+E_{*}-E(Q+2).

(34)

Then the minimal energy when the inelastic reaction can occur is equal to:

\displaystyle E_{*}=E(Q+2)-E(Q)+m_{\Phi}=1+2\omega_{Q}+2\frac{d^{2}E}{dQ^{2}}+...

(35)

So we can see that up to higher derivative terms this expression matches with the expression in Eq. (
33
).

We remind the reader that these results have been obtained in the linear approximation and ignoring the quantum effects.
The validity of the linear approximation will be discussed in the next session, however for sufficiently small perturbations we expect these results and in particular the conservation of the current Eq. (
11
) to be exact.
On the other hand, the quantum effects are obviously absent in this treatment of perturbations.
For example, if we add an interaction

\displaystyle\delta{\cal L}=\frac{1}{\Lambda_{*}^{2}}F_{\mu\nu}^{2}|\phi|^{2}

(36)

to our lagrangian, the reaction

\displaystyle Q+\phi\to(Q+1)+\gamma\gamma

(37)

will be open, however it will be suppressed compared to the “classical transition” by the coupling size and phase space factors.

2.2
Non-linear regime

So far our analysis was focused solely on the linear treatment of the perturbations.
We proceed here with the analysis of the non-linearities by solving the system of differential equations on the lattice.

We do it by discretizing the spatial coordinate using 4th order finite difference, and evolve over time with Runge-Kutta 4th order method.
We have used the absorbing boundary condition at the boundary corresponding to the spatial infinity.
Implementing the other boundary conditions (e.g Dirichlet) is also possible, as long the boundary is significantly far away so that the reflected waves do not spoil the measurements of fluxes.
The initial conditions of the system including the Q-ball background and a monochromatic Gaussian wavepacket that scattering off the Q-ball read

\displaystyle\Phi(0,r)=\Phi_{Q}(0,r)+\delta\left(\dfrac{r_{0}}{r}\right)e^{-\frac{(r-r_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{r}^{2}}}e^{-is_{\omega_{0}}\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}-1}r},

(38)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi(0,r)=-i\bigl[\omega_{Q}\Phi_{Q}(0,r)+\omega_{0}(\Phi(0,r)-\Phi_{Q}(0,r))\bigr].

(39)

The factor
\left(\dfrac{r_{0}}{r}\right)
is negligible when the packet width
\sigma_{r}
is relatively smaller than the distance from the wavepacket to the Q-ball
r_{0}
.
This is not the case when
\omega_{0}
is close to the threshold of having two propagating modes, i.e when one of the wavenumber
k_{+}
or
k_{-}
approaches
0
, and consequently the wavelength becomes significantly large.
In such a case, in order to capture enough oscillations to have good resolution to the mode frequencies, we need to have sufficiently large
\sigma_{r}
and hence the asymptotic damping behavior becomes relevant.

Figure 5

Here, we artificially put large wave packet to visualize the propagation of the incoming and outgoing waves.

In what follows, we pick the following value of the coupling
g=1/3
, and study only the spherically symmetric perturbations.
During the lattice computations we will calculate the quantities
Z_{E},Z_{Q}
(see Eq.
23
) and compare them to the predictions of the linear analysis.
The energy current
J_{E}^{r}
and the corresponding flux integrated over the time are given by:

\displaystyle J_{E}^{r}(r,t)=-2\,\mathrm{Re}(\partial_{r}\Phi^{\ast}\partial_{t}\Phi)=-2\left(\partial_{r}\Phi_{R}\partial_{t}\Phi_{R}+\partial_{r}\Phi_{I}\partial_{t}\Phi_{I}\right),

(40)

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{flux}}(r)=\int_{0}^{t}dt\oint d\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{E}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)=A(r)\int_{0}^{t}dt\ J_{E}^{r}(r,t),

(41)

where we employed the spherical symmetry to factor out the area of the sphere
A(r)
.
Similarly for the flux of
U(1)

\displaystyle J_{Q}^{r}(r,t)=-2\,\mathrm{Im}(\Phi^{\ast}\partial_{t}\Phi)=-2\left(\Phi_{R}\partial_{t}\Phi_{I}-\Phi_{I}\partial_{t}\Phi_{R}\right),

(42)

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{flux}}(r)=\int_{0}^{t}dt\oint d\bm{\mathrm{\Sigma}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{Q}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)=A(r)\int_{0}^{t}dt\ J_{Q}^{r}(r,t).

(43)

Thus the energy and charge amplifications are given by

\displaystyle Z_{E}=-\dfrac{E^{\mathrm{flux}}(t=\infty,R)}{E^{\mathrm{flux}}(t=t_{*},R)},~~~~Z_{Q}=-\dfrac{Q^{\mathrm{flux}}(t=\infty,R)}{Q^{\mathrm{flux}}(t=t_{*},R)}.

(44)

We take
t_{*}\simeq r_{0}-R+\mathcal{O}(10)\sigma_{r}
in order to make sure that we measure the flux after the initial wavepacket has passed through the radius
R
.
In our analysis we will focus primarily on the validity of the linear perturbation discussion and we report it as a function of the perturbation size.
Since the amplitude of the perturbation will grow as
1/r
as the wavepacket approaches the Q-ball, it is better to use a rescaled version of it

\displaystyle\delta_{r}\equiv\frac{\delta\times r_{0}}{R_{Q}\times\phi_{Q}(0)},

(45)

where
R_{Q}
is the radius when the Q-ball field becomes equal to the half of its value in the center.

\omega_{0}=3
due to the threshold effects.

These effects, as expected, grow with the larger size of the perturbations. Additionally there are some sub-leading technical effects feeding the discrepancies between linear and lattice analysis, which we list below:

•

The wave-packet width is not sufficiently large and hence we lose the resolution of the incoming wave momenta:
This effect becomes particularly important if
\omega
is close to the threshold of having two propagating modes
\pm
, since one of the
k_{\pm}
becomes very small and we need to make sure having broad enough wavepacket
\sigma_{r}
in order to capture enough oscillations in space.
For example, in Fig.
6
, with
\omega_{0}=4
we only need to put
\sigma_{r}=10
and still obtain good agreement of linear/lattice.
Meanwhile, with
\omega_{0}=3
, we are closer to the limit
\omega=1+\omega_{Q}
(recall that in our set-up,
\omega_{0}=\omega_{+}=\omega_{Q}+\omega
).
In order to obtain somewhat comparable precision with
\omega_{0}\geq 4
, we need to tune
\sigma_{r}=30
, and consequently increase the lattice size to correctly capture the whole incoming-outgoing wavepackets.

•

Precision of initial Q-ball profile: the profile of the initial Q-ball must be exact, otherwise it will radiate energy and charge and this becomes a background for the “measurement” of the charge and energy extractions during the interaction with the perturbations.

•

Discretisation of time and space leading to the generic systematic error of lattice results.

2.3
Physics implications

We find that the linear analysis results remain valid up to the perturbations of order
\delta_{r}\lesssim 10^{-2}
.
This leads to the natural question of whether such a regime is realized in the early universe.
In order to effectively describe particle interactions with Q-balls, we must set the charge to be equal to one.
Consequently, the constraint on the amplitude becomes a constraint over the wave packet size (coherence length)
L_{\omega}
, indeed

\displaystyle Q=1\sim\frac{\mu^{3}}{\lambda}\delta_{\rm min}^{2}\phi_{Q}(0)^{2}L_{\omega}^{3}\omega\Rightarrow L_{\omega}\gtrsim\left(\frac{\mu^{3}}{\lambda}\delta_{\rm min}^{2}\phi^{2}_{Q}(0)\omega\right)^{-1/3},

(46)

where
\phi_{Q}(0)
and
\omega
are rescaled dimensionless (see Eq.
3
) . Assuming the coherence length is of the order of the the inverse plasma temperature (we expect the wave packet length to be roughly of order of mean free path) we arrive at the condition

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{1/2}T^{3/2}}{\mu^{3/2}}\times\left[\phi_{Q}(0)\omega^{1/2}\right]^{-1}<10^{-2}.

(47)

So once the temperature drops by one order of magnitude below the typical energy scale of the Q-ball field (
\mu
) we expect the linear approximation to lead to the reliable results.
For such temperatures the particle corresponding to the Q-ball perturbation
is non-relativistic (unless we have have a very small coupling
\lambda
), then from Fig.
4
we expect that most of the particle Q-ball interactions will be either elastic (reflection) or leading to the Q-ball charge reduction.

\chi

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=|\partial_{\mu}\Phi|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\,(\partial_{\mu}\chi)^{2}-V(|\Phi|,\chi).

(48)

The potential breaks spontaneously the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
\chi\to-\chi

\displaystyle V(\left\lvert\Phi\right\rvert,\chi)

\displaystyle=g_{\chi\Phi}\chi^{2}\left\lvert\Phi\right\rvert^{2}+g_{\chi}\left(\chi^{2}-v_{\chi}^{2}\right)^{2}+m_{\Phi}^{2}\left\lvert\Phi\right\rvert^{2}+g_{\Phi}\left\lvert\Phi\right\rvert^{4}.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\square\chi+\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial\chi}=0\\[10.0pt]

\left[\square+g_{\chi\Phi}\chi^{2}+m_{\Phi}^{2}+2g_{\Phi}\left\lvert\Phi\right\rvert^{2}\right]\Phi=0\end{cases}.

(50)

This system admits a spherically symmetric Q-ball solution

\displaystyle\Phi_{Q}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)=\dfrac{\phi_{Q}(r)}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-i\omega_{Q}t},\qquad\chi_{Q}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)=\chi_{Q}(r),

(51)

with
\phi_{Q}(r)\in\mathds{R}
,
where at infinity
\chi_{Q}\rightarrow v_{\chi},\phi_{Q}\rightarrow 0
at

(see Fig.

3.1
Linear perturbation

Once the solution for the Q-balls are obtained, we can proceed to the analysis of the perturbations.
This analysis follows the discussion in the Section.
2
, and the only difference is the perturbation of the real field, which we need to take into account.
Denoting the perturbations as
\Phi=\Phi_{Q}+\Phi_{1}
and
\chi=\chi_{Q}+\chi_{1}
,
the following equations of motion are obtained:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left[\square+\dfrac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial\chi^{2}}\biggr|_{(\chi,\Phi)\rightarrow(\chi_{Q},\Phi_{Q})}\right]\chi_{1}+\left[\dfrac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial\chi\partial\Phi}\biggr|_{(\chi,\Phi)\rightarrow(\chi_{Q},\Phi_{Q})}\Phi_{1}+h.c\right]=0\\[15.0pt]
\left[\square+\dfrac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial\Phi^{\ast}\partial\Phi}\biggr|_{(\chi,\Phi)\rightarrow(\chi_{Q},\Phi_{Q})}\right]\Phi_{1}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial(\Phi^{\ast})^{2}}\biggr|_{(\chi,\Phi)\rightarrow(\chi_{Q},\Phi_{Q})}\Phi^{\ast}_{1}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial\Phi^{\ast}\partial\chi}\biggr|_{(\chi,\Phi)\rightarrow(\chi_{Q},\Phi_{Q})}\chi_{1}=0.\end{cases}

(52)

Using the explicit form for the potential and the Q-ball solution Eq. (

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left[\square+g_{\chi\Phi}\phi_{Q}^{2}(r)+4g_{\chi}\left(3\chi_{Q}^{2}(r)-v_{\chi}^{2}\right)\right]\chi_{1}+\sqrt{2}g_{\chi\Phi}\chi_{Q}(r)\phi_{Q}(r)\left(\Phi_{1}e^{+i\omega_{Q}t}+\Phi^{\ast}_{1}e^{-i\omega_{Q}t}\right)=0\\[5.0pt]
\left[\square+g_{\chi\Phi}\chi_{Q}^{2}(r)+m_{\Phi}^{2}+2g_{\Phi}\phi_{Q}^{2}(r)\right]\Phi_{1}+g_{\Phi}\phi_{Q}^{2}(r)e^{-2i\omega_{Q}t}\Phi_{1}^{\ast}+\sqrt{2}g_{\chi\Phi}\chi_{Q}(r)\phi_{Q}(r)e^{-i\omega_{Q}t}\chi_{1}=0\end{cases}.

(53)

This system admits time dependent solutions of the following form:

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)=\eta_{\chi}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})e^{-i\omega t}+\eta^{\ast}_{\chi}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})e^{+i\omega t},\qquad\Phi_{1}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},t)=\eta_{+}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})e^{-i\omega_{+}t}+\eta_{-}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})e^{-i\omega_{-}t},

(54)

with
\omega_{\pm}\equiv\omega_{Q}\pm\omega
.
We can see that comparing to the one complex field Q-ball discussion in the Section.
2
, here the modes with three different energies are mixed.
By substituting this ansatz to (
53
), we obtain the equations for different modes

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\left[\nabla^{2}+\omega^{2}-g_{\chi\Phi}\phi_{Q}^{2}-4g_{\chi}\left(3\chi_{Q}^{2}-v_{\chi}^{2}\right)\right]\eta_{\chi}-\sqrt{2}g_{\chi\Phi}\chi_{Q}\phi_{Q}\left(\eta_{+}+\eta^{\ast}_{-}\right)=0\\
\left[\nabla^{2}+\omega_{+}^{2}-g_{\chi\Phi}\chi_{Q}^{2}-m_{\Phi}^{2}-2g_{\Phi}\phi_{Q}^{2}\right]\eta_{+}-g_{\Phi}\phi_{Q}^{2}\eta^{\ast}_{-}-\sqrt{2}g_{\chi\Phi}\chi_{Q}\phi_{Q}\eta_{\chi}=0\\
\left[\nabla^{2}+\omega_{-}^{2}-g_{\chi\Phi}\chi_{Q}^{2}-m_{\Phi}^{2}-2g_{\Phi}\phi_{Q}^{2}\right]\eta_{-}-g_{\Phi}\phi_{Q}^{2}\eta^{\ast}_{+}-\sqrt{2}g_{\chi\Phi}\chi_{Q}\phi_{Q}\eta^{\ast}_{\chi}=0.\end{array}\right.

Similar to the one field Q-ball case at the linear perturbation analysis there is a conserved current, corresponding to the total particle number conservation and is given by:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{\eta}=2\,\text{Im}\left(\eta_{\chi}\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\eta_{\chi}^{\ast}+\eta_{+}\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\eta_{+}^{\ast}-\eta_{-}\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\eta_{-}^{\ast}\right),\qquad\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{\eta}=0.

Far from the Q-ball the mixing between various modes is switched off and the functions
\eta

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\eta_{\chi}(r\rightarrow\infty)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\left\lvert k_{\chi}\right\rvert}\,r}\left(A_{\chi}^{\rm out}e^{ik_{\chi}r}+A_{\chi}^{\rm in}e^{-ik_{\chi}r}\right)\\[10.0pt]

\displaystyle k_{\chi}\equiv s_{\omega}\sqrt{\omega^{2}-8g_{\chi}v_{\chi}^{2}},\qquad k_{\pm}\equiv\pm s_{\omega}\sqrt{\omega_{\pm}^{2}-g_{\chi\Phi}v_{\chi}^{2}-m_{\Phi}^{2}}.

\displaystyle\left\lvert A_{\chi}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert A_{+}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert A_{-}^{\rm out}\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert A_{\chi}^{\rm in}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert A_{+}^{\rm in}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert A_{-}^{\rm in}\right\rvert^{2}.

Similarly to the one field case we can define

(A_{-}^{\rm out,in})^{*}\\

4
Summary

In this paper, we expand upon the investigation of Q-ball perturbations, drawing on previous works
[
23
,
24
]
, with a particular emphasis on non-rotating Q-balls in 3+1 dimensions.
Our findings enhance the understanding of Q-ball formation mechanisms in early universe cosmology, particularly within models involving solitosynthesis.

Compared to the original works, the novelty of our results lies in three distinct yet interconnected directions.
First, we provide an intuitive explanation of the energy extraction process, linking it to differences in the binding energies of Q-balls with varying charges—analogous to energy release in nuclear reactions.
Second, we analyze in detail the physics of the linear perturbations.
We have identified the symmetry properties of various transition rates and we have carefully determined the validity range of linear perturbation analysis for Q-balls.
This enables us to estimate when, in the early universe, linear analysis leads to the reliable predictions for the Q-ball interactions with plasma particles.
Interestingly we find that the scattering of the non-relativistic particles over the Q-ball most likely lead to its charge and energy reduction at the classical level.
Third, for the first time we have analyzed the perturbations of the FLS Q-ball solution.
We have demonstrated that energy extraction occurs in this case via transformations among modes with three distinct energies.

Appendix A
Symmetry properties of scattering at linear order

The system of linear equations of motion that describe the scattering processes of quanta off a Q-balls is of the following form (see Eq. (
10
),(
3.1
))

\displaystyle\left[\nabla^{2}+O(r)\right]\vec{\eta}(r)=0,

(74)

where we have introduced the following notation:

\vec{\eta}\equiv(\eta_{+},\eta_{-}^{*},\eta_{\chi})
and
O
is some matrix which at large distances becomes diagonal

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}O(r)\to K^{2},~~K\equiv\mathrm{diag}(k_{1},\,k_{2},\dots)

(75)

where
k_{i}
are the momenta of the incoming and outgoing modes.
Since we are using
\eta_{-}^{*}
(and not
\eta_{-}
) as a component of the
\vec{\eta}
we can always choose all
k_{i}
to be of the same sign, then at large distances the solutions are as follows:

\displaystyle\vec{\eta}(r\to\infty)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{{K}}r}e^{iKr}\vec{A}^{\rm\;out}+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{{K}}r}e^{-iKr}\vec{A}^{\rm\;in},

\displaystyle\vec{A}^{\rm in,out}\equiv(A_{+},A_{-}^{*},A_{\chi})^{\rm in,out}.

(76)

Since the Eq. (
74
) is linear in perturbations, the incoming and outgoing amplitudes
\vec{A}
will be related by some matrix
S
(which is some analogue of the scattering S-matrix).

\displaystyle\vec{A}^{\rm\;out}=S\;\vec{A}^{\rm\;in}.

(77)

This matrix in general will depend on the Q-ball potential and its charge and the frequency of the incoming mode, additionally it must satisfy the following properties:

•

The S-matrix is unitary: this follows from the conservation of the
\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{\eta}
current

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{\eta}=i\left[\vec{\eta}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\vec{\eta}^{\,\dagger}-\vec{\eta}^{\,\dagger}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\vec{\eta}\right]=2\mathrm{Im}\left(\vec{\eta}^{\,\dagger}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\vec{\eta}\right).

(78)

By substituting the asymptotic solutions, we obtain

|\vec{A}^{\rm\,out}|^{2}=|\vec{A}^{\rm\,in}|^{2}

(79)

This implies that

S^{\,\dagger}\,S-\mathds{1}=0\implies S^{-1}=S^{\,\dagger}

(80)

•

The S-matrix is symmetric:
This follows from the reality of function
O(r)
. Then we immediately see that if

\vec{\eta}(r)
is a solution of the equations
(
74
) then
\vec{\eta}^{*}(r)
will be a solution as well.

\displaystyle\vec{\eta}^{\,\ast}(r\to\infty)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{K}r}e^{-iKr}\vec{A}^{\rm\,out\ast}+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{{K}}r}e^{-iKr}\vec{A}^{\rm\,in\ast}

\displaystyle\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\vec{A}^{\rm\,in\ast}=S\,\vec{A}^{\rm\,out\ast}

(81)

where in the second line we used the fact that
S
matrix connects
amplitudes with negative and positive exponents. Then using the
eqn. (
77
), we obtain

S\,S^{\ast}=\mathds{1}\implies S^{T}=S

(82)

The unitarity and symmetricity of the S-matrix constrain it to have only
n(n+1)/2
independent real parameters for the case where
\vec{\eta}
is a
n
dimensional vector.

•

Transformation of S-matrix with
\mathds{Z}_{2}
: for two-field model, under
\omega\rightarrow-\omega
we have
(\eta_{\pm},\eta_{\chi})\rightarrow(\eta_{\mp}^{*},{\eta}_{\chi}^{*})
up to a complex scaling factor and a complex conjugation.
Such an observation comes trivially from the equations of motion Eq. (
3.1
).
This imposes the following constraints that are independent of the unitarity and symmetricity:

\displaystyle\left\lvert S^{++}(-\omega)\right\rvert=\left\lvert S^{--}(\omega)\right\rvert,\qquad\left\lvert S^{+\chi}(-\omega)\right\rvert=\left\lvert S^{-\chi}(\omega)\right\rvert,

(83)

\displaystyle\left\lvert S^{+-}(-\omega)\right\rvert=\left\lvert S^{+-}(\omega)\right\rvert,\qquad\left\lvert S^{\chi\chi}(-\omega)\right\rvert=\left\lvert S^{\chi\chi}(\omega)\right\rvert.

(84)

It is straightforward to obtain the coresponding relation for the one-field case by restricting the indices of S-matrix to be either
+
or
-
.

•

S-matrix in limit of having bounded modes:
Let
\omega_{j}^{\rm bound}
is the frequency where the corresponding wavenumber
k_{j}=0
, i.e the mode
j
becomes bounded.
Assuming the solution of the bounded mode close to the Q-ball is still sufficiently small for the validity of linear perturbation; this solution drops exponentially with respect to
r
and becomes negligible when we stay sufficiently far. Then, the particle number conservation (
79
) implies there is no conversion between the propagating modes and the bounded modes.
Consequently we should obtain the following limits:

\displaystyle\left\lvert S^{fi}\left(\omega\rightarrow\omega^{\rm bound}_{i}\right)\right\rvert=\left\lvert S^{fi}\left(\omega\rightarrow\omega^{\rm bound}_{i}\right)\right\rvert=\delta_{fi}.

(85)

Especially in the one-field model with
\vec{\eta}=(\eta_{+},\eta_{-}^{\ast})
,
\omega_{-}^{\rm bound}=1+\omega_{Q}
and
\omega_{+}^{\rm bound}=-1-\omega_{Q}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert S\left(\omega\rightarrow\omega_{-}^{\rm bound}\right)\right\rvert=\left\lvert S\left(\omega\rightarrow\omega_{+}^{\rm bound}\right)\right\rvert=\mathds{1}_{2\times 2},

(86)

which have been verified numerically.

•

S-matrix for high frequency scattering:
In the linearized equations, if
\left\lvert\omega\right\rvert
is sufficiently large, the matrix
O(r)
is approximately diagonal for all
r
, meaning that all modes decoupled and we restore a
U(1)^{n}
global symmetry with each
U(1)
corresponds to a phase rotation of each mode.
We then have
n
approximately conserved current for each mode, implying
\left\lvert A_{i}^{\rm out}\right\rvert=\left\lvert A_{i}^{\rm in}\right\rvert
for
i=1,\dots,n
, and hence the S-matrix reduces to a diagonal matrix of phases, or equivalently

\displaystyle\lim_{\omega\rightarrow\pm\infty}S(\omega)=\mathds{1}.

(87)

This fact has been observed numerically for both one-field and two-field models.

Using the S-matrix language, one can express the amplification factors of a spherical incoming wave in terms of the S-matrix elements

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{Q}=\left\lvert\dfrac{\left\lvert\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}S^{+i}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}S^{-i}\right\rvert^{2}}{\left\lvert\alpha_{+}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert\alpha_{-}\right\rvert^{2}}\right\rvert,

(88)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{E}=\left\lvert\dfrac{\omega_{+}\left\lvert\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}S^{+i}\right\rvert^{2}-\omega_{-}\left\lvert\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}S^{-i}\right\rvert^{2}+\omega\left\lvert\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}S^{\chi i}\right\rvert^{2}}{\omega_{+}\left\lvert\alpha_{+}\right\rvert^{2}-\omega_{-}\left\lvert\alpha_{-}\right\rvert^{2}+\omega\left\lvert\alpha_{\chi}\right\rvert^{2}}\right\rvert,

(89)

where
\alpha_{i}\equiv\dfrac{A_{i}^{\rm in}}{\sum_{j}A_{j}^{\rm in}}
is the “amplitude fraction” of the mode
i
, assuming
\sum_{j}A_{j}^{\rm in}\neq 0
.

In case of having exactly one monochromatic incoming wave
i
, we have
\alpha_{j}=\delta_{ji}
, and the expression above reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{Q}=\left\lvert\left\lvert S^{+i}\right\rvert^{2}-\left\lvert S^{-i}\right\rvert^{2}\right\rvert,\qquad i\neq\chi,

(90)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{E}=\left\lvert\dfrac{\omega_{+}}{\omega_{i}}\left\lvert S^{+i}\right\rvert^{2}-\dfrac{\omega_{-}}{\omega_{i}}\left\lvert S^{-i}\right\rvert^{2}+\dfrac{\omega}{\omega_{i}}\left\lvert S^{\chi i}\right\rvert^{2}\right\rvert,\qquad\forall i\in(\pm,\chi).

(91)

Interestingly for the one complex field case the
S
is a
2\times 2
matrix with only
3
independent components. It can be fixed completely by solving the Eq. (
10
) only for one set of the initial conditions at the origin
(r\to 0)
.
Indeed in this case we will need to solve the system of equations

\displaystyle\vec{A}^{\rm\,out}=S_{2\times 2}\vec{A}^{\rm\,in}

(92)

to find all of the entries of
S
. Naively it looks like there are four independent equations since
\vec{A}^{\rm in,out}
are complex vectors.
However one equation is redundant due to the unitarity of S-matrix, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\lvert\vec{A}^{\rm\,out}\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lvert\vec{A}^{\rm\,in}\right\rvert^{2}.

(93)

In the case of FLS model S-matrix is
3\times 3
and has 6 independent real components.
However solving Eq. (
3.1
) with one initial condition leads only
(2n-1)=5
equations which are not sufficient to fix the S-matrix.

Appendix B
Scattering beyond S-wave

In the main text we focused only on the S wave (
\ell=0
) scatterings.
In this appendix we discuss briefly the case of non-spherical perturbations.
We will focus only on the non-spinning Q-ball solutions (see Ref
[
35
]
for a recent study in a general case of the spinning Q-balls) and we find that results for
\ell\neq 0
are qualitatively similar to the
\ell=0
case.
Indeed performing the decomposition in terms of the angular momentum

\displaystyle\eta_{\pm}(r,\theta,\varphi)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\eta^{m\ell}_{\pm}(r)e^{im\varphi}P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos\theta)

(94)

where
P_{\ell}^{m}
are associated Legendre polynomials, we then obtain the following equation

\displaystyle\left[\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{2}{r}\frac{d}{dr}-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}\right]\eta^{m\ell}_{\pm}+\left[\omega_{\pm}^{2}-U\right]\eta^{m\ell}_{\pm}-W(\eta^{-m\ell}_{\mp})^{\ast}=0.

(95)

We can see that there will be a similar current
\bm{\mathrm{J}}_{\eta}^{\ell m}
conservation for all the possible values of
\ell,m
. Asymptotic solutions to the
\eta^{m\ell}
have exactly the same form as in Eq. (
13
)

\displaystyle\eta_{\pm}^{\ell m}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|k_{\pm}|}\,r}\left[A_{\pm,(\ell,m)}^{\rm in}e^{-ik_{\pm}r}+A_{\pm,(\ell,m)}^{\rm out}e^{ik_{\pm}r}\right].

(96)

and the relation between the amplitudes becomes

\displaystyle\boxed{|A^{\rm out}_{+,(\ell,m)}|^{2}+|A^{\rm out}_{-(\ell,-m)}|^{2}=|A^{\rm in}_{+,(\ell,m)}|^{2}+|A^{\rm in}_{-(\ell,-m)}|^{2}.}

(97)

We can see that Eq. (
97
) imposes the number conservation for the particles with given angular momentum.
Recently the Ref.
[
35
]
have looked at the perturbations for the rotating Q-ball.
In this case, states with different values of
\ell
are mixed and the value
m
of the particle of the
\eta_{\pm}
fields are related by
m_{\pm}=m_{Q}\pm m
.
However even in this case the total number of particles is conserved and the particle and antiparticle energies are fixed to be
\omega_{\pm}
.
At last note that we can define the
S
matrix in this case as well

\displaystyle\vec{A}^{\,\rm out}_{\ell,m}=S_{\ell,m}\vec{A}^{\,\rm in}_{\ell,m},

(98)

and it will be symmetric and unitary as for the
\ell=0
wave case.

B.1
Total cross section

We can proceed to the analysis of the perturbations which can be described in terms of the plane waves. For example let us assume that incoming state is
\eta_{+}
,
then the
solution of the equations of motion for the perturbations takes the form

\displaystyle\eta_{+}(r,\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{+}}}\Big[{f_{+}}(\theta)\,\frac{e^{ik_{+}r}}{r}+e^{ik_{+}z}\Big],

\displaystyle\eta_{-}(r,\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|k_{-}|}}\,{f_{-}}(\theta)\,\frac{e^{ik_{-}r}}{r}.

(99)

We expand the plane-wave and the scattering amplitudes in partial waves

\displaystyle e^{ik_{+}z}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\,i^{\ell}\,j_{\ell}(k_{+}r)\,P_{\ell}(\cos{\theta}),

\displaystyle f_{\pm}(\theta)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\,f_{\pm,\,\ell}\,P_{\ell}(\cos{\theta}).

(100)

Substituting this into (
99
), and recall the asymptotic behavior of the spherical Bessel function
j_{\ell}(z)\sim\left(2iz\right)^{-1}\left(i^{-\ell}e^{iz}-i^{\ell}e^{-iz}\right)

\displaystyle\eta_{+}(r,\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k_{+}}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\,\Bigg[\Big(f_{+,\,\ell}+\frac{1}{2ik_{+}}\Big)\,\frac{e^{ik_{+}r}}{r}+\frac{(-1)^{\ell+1}}{2ik_{+}}\,\frac{e^{-ik_{+}r}}{r}\Bigg]\,P_{\ell}(\cos{\theta}),

\displaystyle\eta_{-}(r,\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|k_{-}|}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\,f_{-,\,\ell}\,\frac{e^{ik_{-}r}}{r}\,P_{\ell}(\cos{\theta})

(101)

Following the usual quantum mechanics procedure we arrive for the expression for cross sections
\sigma_{+f}
for producing
f=\pm
states

\displaystyle\sigma_{++}=4\pi\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\left|f_{+,\ell}\right|^{2},\qquad\sigma_{+-}=4\pi\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\left|f_{-,\ell}\right|^{2}.

(102)

The coefficients
f_{\ell}
can be expressed in terms of the
A_{\pm,\ell}^{\rm in,out}
introduced in the previous section as follows:

\displaystyle f_{-,\ell}=\frac{(-1)^{\ell+1}}{2ik_{+}}\left.\frac{A_{-\ell}^{\rm out}}{A_{+,\ell}^{\rm in}}\right|_{A_{-,\ell}^{\rm in}=0},\qquad f_{+,\ell}=\frac{i}{2k_{+}}\left[1+(-1)^{\ell}\frac{A_{+\ell}^{\rm out}}{A_{+,\ell}^{\rm in}}\right]_{A_{-,\ell}^{\rm in}=0}.

(103)

One can similarly derive the cross sections with other incoming mode.
In terms of S-matrices, the cross sections
\sigma_{if}
for various initial states
i
and final states
f
are

\displaystyle\sigma_{++}(E)=\frac{\pi}{(E^{2}-1)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\left|1+(-1)^{\ell}S_{\ell}^{++}(\omega=E-\omega_{Q})\right|^{2},

(104a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{+-}(E)=\frac{\pi}{(E^{2}-1)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\left|S_{\ell}^{-+}(\omega=E-\omega_{Q})\right|^{2},

(104b)

\displaystyle\sigma_{--}(E)=\frac{\pi}{(E^{2}-1)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\left|1+(-1)^{\ell}S_{\ell}^{--}(\omega=E+\omega_{Q})\right|^{2},

(104c)

\displaystyle\sigma_{-+}(E)=\frac{\pi}{(E^{2}-1)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)\left|S_{\ell}^{+-}(\omega=E+\omega_{Q})\right|^{2},

(104d)

with energy
E^{2}=1+|\bm{\mathrm{k_{\pm}}}|^{2}
since we are using dimensionless action, see Eq.(
3
).
Note that even though S- matrix is defined in terms of
(A_{-,l}^{\rm in,out})^{*}
the equations above are still valid due to the absolute value squared.

From the symmetricity of the S-matrix:
S_{\ell}^{-+}(\omega)=S_{\ell}^{+-}(\omega)
, we can deduce the relation between the cross sections:

\displaystyle\sigma_{+-}(E+\omega_{Q})=\left[\frac{(E-\omega_{Q})^{2}-1}{(E+\omega_{Q})^{2}-1}\right]\sigma_{-+}(E-\omega_{Q}).

(105)

Note that similar equation for the
\sigma(\pm\pm)
is not valid, as these expressions are sensitive to the phases of the
S_{\ell}
, which is not fixed using symmetricity and unitarity of the S-matrix.
We report our findings for the total cross section on the Fig.
