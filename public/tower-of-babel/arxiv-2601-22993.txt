Value-at-Risk Constrained Policy Optimization
Rohan Tangri, Jan-Peter Calliess

Abstract
We introduce the Value-at-Risk Constrained Policy Optimization algorithm (VaR-CPO), a sample efficient and conservative method designed to optimize Value-at-Risk (VaR) constraints directly. Empirically, we demonstrate that VaR-CPO is capable of safe exploration, achieving zero constraint violations during training in feasible environments, a critical property that baseline methods fail to uphold. To overcome the inherent non-differentiability of the VaR constraint, we employ the one-sided Chebyshev inequality to obtain a tractable surrogate based on the first two moments of the cost return. Additionally, by extending the trust-region framework of the Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) method, we provide rigorous worst-case bounds for both policy improvement and constraint violation during the training process.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated remarkable utility in optimizing complex decision-making simulations such as autonomous driving
(
10
)
, robotics
(
17
;
18
;
16
)
and finance
(
9
;
5
)
. The standard RL objective typically seeks to maximize the expected return. While effective in simulations, this formulation is often insufficient for real-life scenarios, where minimizing the probability of a costly catastrophic failure is a prerequisite for deployment. In these safety-critical settings, the cost of shaping a risk-aware reward function is too high, necessitating the use of chance-constrained optimization to directly limit the likelihood of high-cost events.

In many applications, a natural formulation for safety is the Value-at-Risk (VaR) constraint, which enforces that the probability of the cumulative cost exceeding a threshold remains below a safety level
(
19
;
20
)
. While Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) is frequently favored for its theoretical and computational advantages
(
14
;
15
)
, VaR often provides a more intuitive and direct representation of risk in scenarios characterized by absolute failure states. However, enforcing VaR constraints in a Markov Decision Process (MDP) presents significant optimization challenges. First, these constraints are not time-consistent
(
6
)
, meaning the optimal policy at each timestep depends on the costs accumulates so far. More critically, indicator-based methods (treating the cost signal as a Bernoulli variable that activates when the bound is violated) suffer from a sparse cost signal
(
3
)
that is difficult to differentiate and requires failure experience to learn safe behavior. To address these limitations, we introduce a novel approach that leverages the one-sided Chebyshev inequality to construct a differentiable surrogate for the VaR constraint based on the first and second moments of the cost distribution. While conservative, this constraint form allows the method to learn safe behavior without needing to explicitly test the boundary between safe and unsafe modes. We then integrate this formulation into the Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) framework
(
1
)
by augmenting the state signal with accumulated episode costs. The resultant Value-at-Risk Constrained Policy Optimization (VaR-CPO) algorithm benefits from dense gradients and rigorous theoretical guarantees on the worst-case constraint violation during training. Our primary contributions are as follows:

•

We formulate a tractable surrogate for Value-at-Risk constraints in RL using the one-sided Chebyshev inequality with a state augmentation scheme to ensure Markovian dynamics.

•

We provide a comprehensive worst-case analysis of the constraint violation, extending the guarantees of the original trust-region based CPO algorithm to the VaR setting.

•

We empirically find that the VaR-CPO algorithm can achieve safe exploration in feasible environments with zero constraint violations by learning a conservative bound through the first two moments of the cost return instead of its explicit exceedance.

Related Work

There exists a rather large literature on safe reinforcement learning (SafeRL); however, the area has mainly focused on the CMDP problem structure with constraints on the expected cost return rather than any tail risk
(
11
)
. The simplest way to solve such problems is to use primal-dual methods to solve a Lagrangian form of the constrained optimization problem
(
21
)
. Although the resulting policy will obey the constraints set at convergence, there is no bound on the violation during training. To resolve this, the CPO algorithm
(
1
)
uses trust region theory from the Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) paper
(
16
)
to provide a worst-case constraint violation even during training, which was followed by Lyapunov-based policy optimization, providing a similar guarantee on the upper bound of the cost
(
7
)
.

In contrast, there are only a few examples that examine the upper tail risk of the cost return, and to the best of our knowledge, the methods that do exist mostly focus on CVaR constraints
(
24
;
23
;
12
)
. These frequently use theory from distributional RL, learning the entire cost return distribution to then make a quantile-based estimation of the constraint
(
24
;
12
)
. However, this tends to add extra complexity, increasing training times, and the CVaR constraint can be non-intuitive in scenarios where there is a binary failure state. The exception explicitly addressing the VaR constraint proposes policy gradient and actor critic methods to solve both CVaR and VaR constrained costs with a Lagrangian form
(
6
)
. However, their methods fail to provide any guarantee on constraint violation during training. Other applications use a Bernoulli surrogate for the cost function, which equals 1 when the constraint is violated and 0 otherwise, such that the problem is mapped back to an expectation constraint
(
11
)
. However, this introduces a sparse cost signal, which is hard to learn from and sample inefficient
(
3
)
.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Constrained Markov Decision Process

We model the agent’s interaction with the environment as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP)
(
2
)
, which extends the standard MDP tuple to include a separate cost function. A CMDP is defined by the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,C,\rho_{0},\gamma)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the state space and
\mathcal{A}
is the action space. Defining
\Delta(\mathcal{X})
as the set of all probability distributions over a set
\mathcal{X}
, the state transition kernel
P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{S})
maps each state-action pair to a probability distribution over next states. The reward function
R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathbb{R})
provides a scalar feedback signal, and the cost function
C:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathbb{R})
represents the safety penalty associated with a state-action pair. The initial state is sampled from
\rho_{0}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
, and
\gamma\in[0,1]
serves as the discount factor. A policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A})
maps states to a probability distribution over actions. In an ideal case, the agent samples infinite length trajectories
\tau=(s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},a_{1},\dots)
, where
s_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
,
a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t})
, and
s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})
. The standard reinforcement learning objective is to maximize the expected discounted return, denoted as
J(\pi)
:

J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_{t}\right]

(1)

where
r_{t}\sim R(s_{t},a_{t})
is the reward at time
t
.

In a safety-critical setting, we are also concerned with the return of a cost signal,
C(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{c}^{t}c_{t}
, where
\gamma_{c}\in~[0,1]
is the cost discount factor. The goal in a CMDP is to find a policy
\pi^{*}
that maximizes the expected reward return while ensuring the safety objective,
J_{C}(\pi)
, satisfies a specific limit
d
:

\pi^{*}=\arg\max_{\pi}J(\pi)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad J_{C}(\pi)\leq d.

(2)

3.2
Constrained Policy Optimization

Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) is an iterative algorithm for solving CMDPs that bounds worst-case constraint violation and performance degregdation at each update step
(
1
)
. To ensure stable learning, CPO employs a trust-region approach
(
16
)
, constraining the step size of the policy update via the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. First, let
\rho_{\pi}(s)
and
\rho^{c}_{\pi}(s)
define the reward and cost discounted state visitation frequencies respectively for policy
\pi
:

\displaystyle\rho_{\pi}(s)=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}P(s_{t}=s\mid\pi)

(3)

\displaystyle\rho_{\pi}^{c}(s)=(1-\gamma_{c})\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{c}^{t}P(s_{t}=s\mid\pi).

(4)

Given an existing policy
\pi_{k}
at iteration
k
, the goal is to update to a new policy
\pi
satisfying Equation
2
. However, it is impossible to calculate
J(\pi)
or
J_{C}(\pi)
given trajectories only sampled from
\pi_{k}
. Instead, valid first order approximations,
L(\pi)
and
L_{C}(\pi)
, for
J(\pi)
and
J_{C}(\pi)
respectively can be defined:

\displaystyle L(\pi)=J(\pi_{k})+\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim\rho_{\pi_{k}}\\
a\sim\pi\end{subarray}}\left[A_{\pi_{k}}(s,a)\right]

(5)

\displaystyle L_{C}(\pi)=J_{C}(\pi_{k})+\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim\rho^{c}_{\pi_{k}}\\
a\sim\pi\end{subarray}}\left[A^{C}_{\pi_{k}}(s,a)\right].

(6)

Here
A_{\pi_{k}}
and
A_{\pi_{k}}^{C}
are the advantage functions
(
17
)
following policy
\pi_{k}
for the reward and cost, respectively.

Following this, at each iteration
k
, given a policy
\pi_{k}
, CPO seeks a new policy
\pi_{k+1}
that solves the following local approximation problem:

\displaystyle\pi_{k+1}=\arg\max_{\pi}

\displaystyle\text{ }L(\pi)

(7)

s.t.

\displaystyle L_{C}(\pi)\leq d

(8)

\displaystyle\bar{D}_{KL}(\pi,\pi_{k})\leq\delta

(9)

where
\bar{D}_{KL}(\pi,\pi_{k})
is the average KL divergence over states between the policies
\pi_{k}
and
\pi
which defines the trust region by setting
\delta>0
:

\bar{D}_{KL}(\pi,\pi_{k})=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho_{\pi_{k}}}[D_{KL}(\pi,\pi_{k})[s]].

(10)

Given
\alpha_{\pi}=\max_{s}|\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[A_{\pi_{k}}(s,a)]|
and

\alpha_{\pi}^{C}=~\max_{s}|\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[A^{C}_{\pi_{k}}(s,a)]|
represent the maximum expected advantages for the reward and cost signals respectively, the worst case performance degradation and constraint violations are defined as follows:

\displaystyle J(\pi_{k+1})\geq J(\pi_{k})-\frac{\sqrt{2\delta}\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\alpha_{\pi_{k+1}}

(11)

\displaystyle J_{C}(\pi_{k+1})-d\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\delta}\gamma_{c}}{(1-\gamma_{c})^{2}}\alpha^{C}_{\pi_{k+1}}.

(12)

4
Method

In this section, we present VaR-CPO, a robust algorithm for enforcing Value-at-Risk constraints. We first formulate the VaR objective and demonstrate how the one-sided Chebyshev inequality can be used to construct a tractable, differentiable surrogate. We then introduce a state-augmentation scheme to estimate the required second-order moments of the cost distribution, before presenting a worst-case constraint violation bound on the resultant update step. Further proofs and derivations can be found in Appendix
A
.

4.1
Chebyshev Surrogate for Value-at-Risk

We formally define the Value-at-Risk (VaR) constraint with threshold
\rho
and confidence level
1-\epsilon
. The objective is to ensure that the probability of the discounted cumulative cost,
C(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{c}^{t}c_{t}
, exceeding the threshold
\rho
is bounded by
\epsilon
:

P(C(\tau)\geq\rho)\leq\epsilon.

(13)

Directly optimizing this probabilistic constraint is challenging due to the lack of an analytical gradient for the indicator function inherent in the probability definition. Furthermore, VaR constraints are not time-consistent; the optimal action depends on the accumulated cost incurred so far, violating the Markov property of the standard state space.

Figure 1
:
Conservative Chebyshev Surrogate
: The feasible VaR regions for cost threshold
\rho=100
and violation probability
\epsilon=0.05
. The Chebyshev surrogate is valid for any distribution with finite first and second moments, which requires it to be overly conservative compared to a scenario where the underlying cost distribution is known to be Gaussian for example.

To address the optimization difficulty, we employ the one-sided Chebyshev inequality to construct a differentiable, conservative surrogate constraint. For a random variable
C(\tau)
with mean
\mu(\pi)
and variance
\sigma^{2}(\pi)
:

\displaystyle\mu(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}[C(\tau)]

(14)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}(\pi)=\text{Var}_{\tau\sim\pi}[C(\tau)]

(15)

the inequality states that for any
\lambda=\rho-\mu(\pi)>0
:

P(C(\tau)-\mu(\pi)\geq\lambda)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}(\pi)}{\sigma^{2}(\pi)+\lambda^{2}}.

(16)

We can now satisfy the original VaR constraint by enforcing the stronger condition:

\frac{\sigma^{2}(\pi)}{\sigma^{2}(\pi)+[\rho-\mu(\pi)]^{2}}\leq\epsilon.

(17)

Rearranging terms, this yields a quadratic constraint on the policy’s moments:

J_{C}(\pi)=\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}-1\right)\sigma^{2}(\pi)-[\rho-\mu(\pi)]^{2}\leq 0.

(18)

4.2
Augmented Cost Formulation

Optimizing the surrogate in Equation
18
requires computing the variance
\sigma^{2}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}[C(\tau)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[C(\tau)]^{2}
. While the first moment
\mathbb{E}[C(\tau)]
is standard, the second moment
\mathbb{E}[C(\tau)^{2}]
cannot be computed as a sum of local costs in the original state space.

To resolve this and the time-inconsistency issue, we augment the state space with a variable
y_{t}
tracking the discounted accumulated cost up to time
t
and the discount at time
t
:

\displaystyle y_{t+1}=y_{t}+\gamma_{c}^{t}c_{t},\quad y_{0}=0

(19)

\displaystyle x_{t}=(s_{t},y_{t},\gamma_{c}^{t}).

(20)

Using this augmentation, the square of the return can be decomposed into a sum of local rewards:

C(\tau)^{2}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{c}^{t}(\gamma_{c}^{t}c_{t}^{2}+2y_{t}c_{t}).

(21)

Substituting the moment decompositions into the Chebyshev surrogate (Eq.
18
), we can rewrite the constraint in the form of a standard cumulative return inequality, albeit with a dynamic upper bound. We define the augmented local cost
\tilde{c}
given
\beta=\frac{1}{\epsilon}-1
:

\tilde{c}(x_{t},a_{t})=\beta\gamma_{c}^{t}c_{t}^{2}+2\left(\beta y_{t}+\rho\right)c_{t}.

(22)

Let
J_{\tilde{C}}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}[\sum\gamma_{c}^{t}\tilde{c}_{t}]
be the expected return of this augmented cost. The Chebyshev constraint then takes the form:

J_{\tilde{C}}(\pi)\leq d(\pi),\quad\text{where }d(\pi)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\mu(\pi)^{2}+\rho^{2}.

(23)

4.3
Update Step

Given an initial policy
\pi_{k}
, we want to make an update to
\pi_{k+1}
following the update rule:

\pi_{k+1}=\arg\max_{\pi}J(\pi)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad J_{\tilde{C}}(\pi)\leq d(\pi).

(24)

We can do this by creating a policy trust region and obtaining valid first order surrogates for the return and constraint in Equation
23
around the current policy
\pi_{k}
where
Z=\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\rho^{c}_{\pi_{k}}\\
a\sim\pi\end{subarray}}[A_{\pi_{k}}^{C}(x,a)]
:

\displaystyle L(\pi)=J(\pi_{k})+\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\rho_{\pi_{k}}\\
a\sim\pi\end{subarray}}\left[A_{\pi_{k}}(x,a)\right]

(25)

\displaystyle L_{\tilde{C}}(\pi)=J_{\tilde{C}}(\pi_{k})+\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\rho^{c}_{\pi_{k}}\\
a\sim\pi\end{subarray}}\left[A^{\tilde{C}}_{\pi_{k}}(x,a)\right]

(26)

\displaystyle\hat{d}(\pi)=d(\pi_{k})+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\frac{2\mu(\pi_{k})}{(1-\gamma_{c})}Z+\frac{1}{(1-\gamma_{c})^{2}}Z^{2}\right)

(27)

such that the final update step is given by:

\displaystyle\pi_{k+1}=\arg\max_{\pi}

\displaystyle\text{ }L(\pi)

(28)

s.t.

\displaystyle L_{\tilde{C}}(\pi)\leq\hat{d}(\pi)

(29)

\displaystyle\bar{D}_{KL}(\pi,\pi_{k})\leq\delta.

(30)

4.4
Worst-case Violation Bounds

A primary contribution of this work is establishing that this approximation is safe. Extending the theoretical analysis of CPO, we derive a bound on the worst-case constraint violation introduced by the surrogate approximation.

Theorem 4.1
.

(Worst-Case Chebyshev Violation)
For a policy update
\pi_{k+1}
derived under the trust region constraint
\bar{D}_{KL}(\pi_{k},\pi_{k+1})\leq\delta
, the true Chebyshev constraint violation is bounded by:

J_{{C}}(\pi_{k+1})\leq K\left(\alpha_{\pi_{k+1}}^{\tilde{C}}+\frac{2\alpha_{\pi_{k+1}}^{C}}{\epsilon}M\right)

(31)

where
\alpha^{\tilde{C}}_{\pi}=\max_{s}|\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[A^{\tilde{C}}_{\pi_{k}}(s,a)]|
and

\alpha_{\pi}^{C}=\max_{s}|\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[A^{C}_{\pi_{k}}(s,a)]|
represent the maximum expected augmented and standard cost advantages respectively,
K=\frac{\sqrt{2\delta}\gamma_{c}}{(1-\gamma_{c})^{2}}
, and
M=\mu(\pi_{k})+\frac{\alpha_{\pi_{k+1}}^{C}}{1-\gamma_{c}}
. See Appendix
A.5
for proof.

Since the VaR-CPO reward return objective is identical to CPO, it also inherits the worst case performance degradation bound in Equation
11
.

4.5
Cost Return Constraint

A critical limitation of the one-sided Chebyshev surrogate is its validity condition. The bound in Equation
16
holds strictly when the expected cost lies below the threshold,
\mu(\pi)<\rho
. In the regime where
\mu(\pi)\geq\rho
, the update rule is counterproductive, and standard optimization may result in unstable updates that fail to reduce risk.

To address scenarios where the policy is initialized in, or enters, this infeasible region, we employ a recovery mechanism. When
\mu(\pi_{k})\geq\rho
, we temporarily relax the VaR objective and instead constrain the expected cost directly to restore the policy to the valid region where the Chebyshev approximation holds. We can redefine the linear approximation for
\mu(\pi)
:

\displaystyle L_{\mu}(\pi)=\mu(\pi_{k})+\frac{1}{1-\gamma_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim\rho^{c}_{\pi_{k}}\\
a\sim\pi\end{subarray}}\left[A^{C}_{\pi_{k}}(s,a)\right].

(32)

such that the update rule is modified to the standard CPO form as follows:

\displaystyle\pi_{k+1}=\arg\max_{\pi}\quad

\displaystyle L(\pi)

(33)

s.t.

\displaystyle L_{\mu}(\pi)\leq\rho

(34)

\displaystyle\bar{D}_{KL}(\pi,\pi_{k})\leq\delta

(35)

This fallback ensures that the policy prioritizes returning to the feasible set defined by the moments of the cost distribution before resuming optimization of the VaR surrogate.

For a policy parameterized by

, this optimization is made computationally tractable using Taylor expansions. The objective and cost constraints are approximated to first order, while the KL divergence constraint is approximated to second order. Defining

as the gradient of the objective,

as the gradient of the cost,

\displaystyle g=\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_{k}}

\displaystyle c=L_{\tilde{C}}(\theta_{k})-\hat{d}(\theta_{k})=J_{\tilde{C}}(\theta_{k})-d(\theta_{k})

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}=\arg\max_{\theta}\text{ }

\displaystyle g^{T}(\theta-\theta_{k})

\displaystyle c+b^{T}(\theta-\theta_{k})\leq 0

5.1
Experimental Setup

Although the OpenAI Safety Gym package provides an excellent set of benchmark environments for CMDPs
(
13
)
, we wanted to leverage the benefits of running Just-in-Time (JIT) compiled JAX code end-to-end on the GPU for accelerated experiments
(
4
)
. To this end, we translated environments from Google’s brax software
(
8
)
and the Farama Foundation’s gymnasium package
(
22
)
to fit the CMDP framework. We have published these environments to PyPi for easy import into other Python projects.

We evaluate our method on two environments inherited from well-known existing benchmarks:

•

EcoAnt
: A modified version of the brax Ant environment
(
8
)
. In this scenario, the agent must maximize forward velocity while managing a limited battery budget and navigating additive action noise to simulate stochastic actuator dynamics. High torque usage depletes the battery; if the battery runs dry, the episode terminates.

•

IcyLake
: A modified version of the gymnasium FrozenLake environment
(
22
)
. In this scenario, the agent must traverse a frozen lake grid to reach the goal state. There are two types of tiles: deep snow tiles take some effort to move through, while icy tiles are easier to glide over but introduce a small probability of falling over.

We compare
VaR-CPO
against three baselines:

•

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO):
A popular unconstrained RL baseline without an explicit safety objective
(
18
)
.

•

Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO):
The standard method for CMDPs which constrains the expected cost rather than the tail risk. This can be used to satisfy a VaR constraint when the cost signal is a sparse Bernoulli random variable with value 1 when the constraint is violated
(
1
)
.

•

CVaR Proximal Policy Optimization (CPPO):
A Lagrange augmented PPO method that enforces a CVaR constraint, which strictly bounds the original VaR constraint as a conservative surrogate
(
23
)
.

For fair comparison, each agent is given the same experience budget in which to learn. Furthermore, networks are kept consistent using a disjoint actor-critic architecture with tanh activations. The exception is where a cost critic needs to be included, in which case a structurally identical reward critic is used. Finally, to maintain the integrity of the benchmark, hyperparameters that would alter the VaR objective itself are excluded from the manual search, ensuring that all algorithms optimize for the same target.

The IcyLake environment is specifically designed to evaluate an agent’s ability to manage tail risk in scenarios where minimizing expected cost leads to unsafe behavior. This environment consists of a
4\times 4
grid based on the classic FrozenLake layout. The agent receives a reward of
1
upon reaching the target state and
0
otherwise.

The primary distinction in this environment lies in the cost structure of the traversable tiles, demonstrated in Figure
3
. Deep snow tiles represent a conservative path, incurring a constant base cost of
2
. Conversely, ice tiles appear more efficient on average but carry significant tail risk; they have a low base cost of
0.5
but a
10\%
probability of a ”slip” event, which adds a stochastic cost of
10
. Consequently, the expected cost of an ice tile (
1.5
) is lower than that of a deep snow tile (
2.0
), yet the ice tiles present a much higher risk of catastrophic cost accumulation.

All four algorithms: PPO, CPO, CPPO and VaR-CPO, successfully learn to reach the target state. However, as shown in Figure
2
, their navigation strategies differ fundamentally based on their treatment of cost. Both the unconstrained PPO baseline and the expected-cost-constrained CPO baseline converge on the absolute shortest path containing ice tiles. Because these algorithms optimize for the expected return, they perceive the ice tiles as the ”cheaper” and more efficient route. These agents maintain high ice tile visitation rates throughout training, obtaining lower average costs but remaining blind to extreme events with frequent, high-magnitude cost spikes in the tail.

In contrast, CPPO and VaR-CPO are configured to constrain the
95\text{th}
percentile of the cost return distribution to remain below a threshold of
15
. To satisfy this chance constraint, the agent must avoid the ice tiles, where a sequence of slips could easily exceed the safety limit. While CPPO satisfies the constraint on average, it still suffers from violations. Our results demonstrate that VaR-CPO is the only algorithm that successfully identifies and entirely adopts the safer deep snow path, trading off average-case efficiency for rigorous safety guarantees.

5.3
EcoAnt Performance Analysis

The EcoAnt environment serves as a benchmark for high-dimensional continuous control. Based on the Brax Ant environment, this task requires the agent to coordinate complex joint actuations to maximize forward velocity. We introduce a critical safety constraint in the form of a limited battery budget. The agent consumes energy at time
t
proportional to the torque applied at each step in the action vector:
E_{t}=\frac{1}{2}||\textbf{a}_{\textbf{t}}||_{2}^{2}
.

If the battery depletes entirely, the episode terminates immediately, mimicking a catastrophic failure state. This feature gives unconstrained RL algorithms such as PPO a fair shot by indirectly penalizing failure through the prevention of of future reward accumulation. All risk-aware simulations in Figure
4
set an objective VaR constraint on the likelihood of battery depletion to 10%.

There are two versions of the environment with different cost signals to accommodate a comparison between the VaR-CPO and CPPO algorithms with the CPO agent:

•

Sparse Bernoulli Cost:
In this variant, the cost signal is binary. The agent receives
c_{t}=0
for all safe steps and
c_{t}=1
only upon battery depletion (failure). This setup directly mirrors the definition of a VaR constraint for the CPO agent.

•

Dense Energy Cost:
In the second variant, the cost is defined as the scalar energy consumed at each time step,
c_{t}=E_{t}
. This is suitable for the CPPO and VaR-CPO algorithms.

Of particular note is the VaR-CPO performance with the larger battery of 500, allowing the agents to start in a safe region. Here, it uniquely achieves a zero threshold exceedance, highlighting its ability to satisfy a VaR constraint without experiencing any failures. This is possible since it learns to balance a conservative mean-variance tradeoff in the cost return signal rather than the explicit VaR bound, and understanding this tradeoff requires zero knowledge of the original VaR constraint itself. In contrast, CPO and CPPO measure the VaR and CVaR constraints respectively, forcing them to learn by testing the constraint boundaries directly and experiencing some failure.

Testing with a 50-unit battery and 10% depletion constraint assesses recovery when agents are initialized in an unsafe zone. The VaR-CPO algorithm is the only candidate able to constrain battery usage while achieving competitive performance, while both CPO and CPPO struggle to learn reward generating behaviors within the safe region.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced Value-at-Risk Constrained Policy Optimization (VaR-CPO), a novel framework for safety-critical reinforcement learning that addresses the challenges of chance-constrained optimization. By leveraging the one-sided Chebyshev inequality, we transformed the often intractable Value-at-Risk constraint into a tractable surrogate based on the first and second moments of the cost distribution. Our approach provides a mathematically rigorous bridge between the theoretical guarantees of trust-region methods and the practical necessity of tail-risk management, ensuring that safety objectives are prioritized without sacrificing the stability of the learning process.

Our primary contributions include a state-augmentation scheme that allows for the Markovian decomposition of second-order moments introduced by the one-sided Chebyshev VaR bound surrogate. Furthermore, we extended the theoretical foundations of Constrained Policy Optimization to provide explicit worst-case violation bounds for the update step, offering a safety guarantee even during the iterative training phase. Finally, empirical results in high-performance JAX-based environments demonstrate that VaR-CPO provides a sample-efficient method capable of safe exploration without any failure experience in feasible settings. While the Chebyshev surrogate is inherently conservative, it offers a principled path toward deploying RL agents in domains where minimizing the probability of catastrophic failure is a prerequisite.
