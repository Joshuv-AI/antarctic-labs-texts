Colostrum from MERS-CoV seropositive camels for MERS prophylaxis and SARS-CoV-2 infection, a placebo controlled randomized trial
Authors: Hagit Masika, Meir Cherniak, Yelena Britan-Rosich, Yonatan Oster, Daniel Grupel, Ahmad Nama, Batel Nissan, David Planer, Jacob Strahilevitz, Eitan Israeli, Sujata Kumari, Orna Mor, Saveliy Kirillov, Joel Alter, Moshe Dessau, Jamal Fahoum, Einav Cohen-Kfir, Matan Golan, Reuven Wiener, Dan David, Reaan Amer, Alexander Rouvinski, Hila Elinav
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
COVID-19 pandemic is currently relatively controlled, mainly due to effective vaccines. Preparedness for future outbreaks should include means for reducing transmission of SARS-CoV-2 and other coronaviruses like MERS-CoV. Approximately 72% of camels in Israel are seropositive for MERS-CoV and may exhibit cross-reactivity with serologically related SARS-CoV-2, suggesting therapeutic possibilities. Aims:To investigate the potential of camel-derived anti-MERS-CoV antibodies from camels colostrum for mucosal use in humans, as MERS-CoV prophylaxis and to control COVID-19 progression and infectivity. Methods:Using ELISA assay, we screened serum and colostrum of MERS-CoV seropositive camels for MERS-CoV antibody titers and neutralization potency and for cross-reactivity with SARS-CoV-2 spike. Next, we performed an open label placebo controlled randomized trial, comparing the effect of mucosal application of colostrum to placebo. Infectivity and viral load levels were evaluated daily up to 96 h. Results:Anti-MERS-CoV spike reactive antibodies with partial SARS-CoV-2 cross-reactivity were detected in 22 serum and 12 colostrum samples. While SARS-CoV-2 cross-neutralization was detected, its potency was significantly weaker than that of MERS-CoV. Neutralization of spike pseudotyped reporter viruses representing MERS-CoV reached ~ 1:500, while neutralization against SARS-CoV-2 wild type and variants was only at (NT50) ≤ 1:120. Forty-three COVID-19 patients were recruited to the randomized controlled trial. The primary endpoints did not differ between groups, with comparable decline in viral load (p = 0.311) and infectivity (p = 0.9641) after 24-h. Conclusions:In-vitro, camel colostrum-derived antibodies neutralize MERS-CoV, but a thin colostrum preparation did not reduce infectivity or viral load in SARS-CoV-2 infected individuals. The role of camel colostrum-derived antibody-concentrate and more viscous preparations merit further evaluation as potential prophylaxis and treatment against MERS-CoV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26000-5.

Introduction

COVID-19 a global pandemic that emerged in 2019 with catastrophic medical, social1 and economic implications2 is finally relatively controlled, due to tremendous scientific and medical efforts3. As the pandemic evolved, multiple viral variants resulted in waves of morbidity and mortality while developed monoclonal antibodies, were found to be non-effective for treatment of new variants4. Preparedness to future outbreaks should include in addition to physical barriers as facial masks and social distancing, further anti-viral medications, and measures that can reduce transmission.

SARS-CoV-2, the causative pathogen of COVID-19, a Betacoronavirus family member, bears a ~ 80% amino acid identity to SARS-CoV and is also serologically related to MERS-CoV5,6. A hallmark feature of the coronaviruses, is the highly immunogenic spike glycoprotein, eliciting antibodies against conserved and variable epitopes7–9. Cross-reactive antibodies, naturally developed as consequence of exposure to serologically related coronaviruses, may be explored for their cross-protective properties 10–12.

Since 2012, camels’ sera in Israel are routinely surveyed for the presence of MERS-CoV antibodies13. Although no case of human MERS-CoV infection has been reported in Israel to date, 71.8% of the camels were found to be seropositive14. Importantly, camel species possess a versatile immunoglobulin repertoire comprising conventional IgGs in combination with heavy chain-only immunoglobulins, HCIgs15,16, that have unique properties, including better tissue penetration and resilience to high temperature17. In mammals, colostrum, the milk from the first days post-delivery, is known to be highly immunoglobulin enriched. Such enrichment protects the offspring during the early days of life, before the independent functional humoral immune system forms18. Presence of potent neutralizing antibodies in colostrum, may be harnessed towards the development of a rapid, safe and effective preventive and therapeutic MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 interventions.

The aims of this study were to investigate both in-vitro and in-vivo the therapeutic potential of antibodies derived from blood and colostrum of MERS-seropositive camels. We characterized the MERS-CoV immunoglobulins repertoire and neutralization potency and explored the cross-reactivity towards SARS-CoV-2. We further studied the effect of mucosal application of camels’ colostrum-derived preparations in healthy volunteers and in SARS-CoV-2 infected patients.

Methods

Screening of blood and colostrum of MERS-CoV-seropositive camels

Samples of blood and colostrum (at days 1-to-3, Figure S1), were screened as specified by ELISA using recombinant spikes or Receptor Binding Domain of spike (RBD) and nucleocapsid proteins of SARS-CoV-2 (Wuhan wild type) and MERS-CoV (Figure S2).

In-vitro SARS-CoV-2 neutralization assay

Neutralization assay was performed using single round infectious particles19,20 produced by pseudotyping Vesicular Stomatitis Virus (VSV), lacking its VSV-G envelope and expressing GFP-reporter, with either SARS-CoV-2 spikes representing variants (Wuhan wild type, Delta, Omicron BA.1 and BA.5), or with MERS-CoV spike19,20. Due to toxicity of colostrum to VeroE6 cells, we isolated the soluble fraction of total IgGs and HCIgs from the camel’s colostrum fluid (Whey). VeroE6 cells were infected with constant pseudovirus titer, and either left untreated, or preincubated with whey at two-fold dilutions. Fluorescence was determined by an automated fluorescence microscope (Cytation5, Agilent, BioTek) to quantify infected cells, see supplementary for details.

In-vivo kinetics of MERS-CoV immunoglobulins in mucus membranes.

Healthy volunteers used colostrum nasal spray–2-puffs at each nostril and the nasopharynx, every 4 h for 24 h, with 6 doses in total. Samples were obtained from right nostril before the last dose (4 h from the previous dose) and from the left nostril immediately after the last dose. Additional sampling was performed 1, 2 and 24 h after the last dose. Samples were immediately frozen at − 80 °C until tested for antibodies by ELISA.

In-vivo efficacy of mucosal spray derived from camels MERS-CoV-seropositive colostrum assayed for reduction of SARS-CoV-2 viral load and infectivity.

This was an open label randomized placebo controlled prospective study, approved by the Hadassah-Hebrew University Medical Center’s Institutional Ethics Board (HMO-0238-20). The study was registered in the Israeli clinical trial website “myTrials” on 31/03/2021 registration number MOH_2021-03-31_009860, and was registered in ClinicalTrial.gov on 04/03/2025, registration number NCT06857318. Research was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and GCP (good clinical practice) guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects, at recruitment. The study population included adult individuals, with mild to moderate symptomatic COVID-19, diagnosed by SARS-CoV-2 rapid antigen kit or PCR, with symptoms duration of less than seven days. See protocol in supplementary for more details.

Study participants underwent medical assessment including questionnaire regarding COVID-19 symptoms and medical history, measurement of fever, O2 saturation and pulse rate. Clinical score was calculated for each patient according to symptoms. (fever > 38 °C, sore throat, running nose, cough, dyspnea, fatigue, myalgia, appetite loss, diarrhea, nausea, headache, anosmia/ageusia). Anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgGs were tested by SARS-CoV-2 IgG II Quant assay (Abbott, Chicago IL). Patients were randomized in 1:1 ratio to either study group (resuspended camel colostrum) or placebo group (sterile water for injection). Swab was obtained from the right nostril and frozen immediately on dry ice. These swabs were later tested for infectivity in cell culture and for viral load (VL) by PCR (see supplementary). Patients used nasal spray (2-puffs at each nostril, and the nasopharynx), every 4 h for 24 h with 6 doses in total. Additional swabs were obtained at 24 h before the last dose from the right nostril (for infectivity and PCR tests), and from the left nostril immediately after last dose. Additionally, swabs were obtained at 48, 72 and 96 h post treatment initiation.

The primary endpoint was defined as the reduction of VL (expressed as R) and infectivity (log infectious particles) after 24 h of treatment, while secondary end points were defined as the percentage of undetectable VL and absence of infectious virus 4 days after treatment initiation.

Statistical methods

Baseline characteristics were compared by Chi-square test for categorical variables, and t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables “Time 0” was defined as treatment (or placebo) initiation. Results

MERS antibodies in serum and colostrum samples of dairy camels

We have serologically surveyed 22 female Dromedary camels farmed in the Negev desert. Anti-S IgGs were significantly more abundant than anti-NP IgGs (Fig. 1Ai). While we found anti-S MERS HCIgs, along with classical IgGs, only minor levels of anti-NP MERS HCIgs were identified (Fig. 1Aii). When colostrum collected from 12 camels within the first 24h post-delivery, was tested for MERS-CoV anti-S antibodies, we found significant amounts of IgG and HCIgs antibodies in 11 out of 12 colostrum samples (Fig. 1B). We next tested the potential application of pasteurized camel colostrum, for mucosal humoral treatment in human subjects and evaluated whether and to which duration anti-MERS-CoV camel Igs are retained at mucosal surfaces. SARS-CoV-2 cross-reactivity in serum and colostrum of MERS-seropositive camels

Given the serological relatedness of MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV-221, we evaluated the degree of cross-reactivity of sera and colostrum samples using ELISA.

Anti MERS-CoV IgGs exhibited weak, yet measurable cross-reactivity with SARS-CoV-2 antigens (Fig. 3A-serum, 3B-colostrum). Of note, we observed a cross reactivity, specifically towards the RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 spike, known to be involved in receptor mediated entry22,23. Neutralizing potential of colostrum derived antibodies

In order to assess the functional aspects of the observed low level of cross-reactivity of MERS-CoV antibodies toward SARS-COV-2 we evaluated the neutralization potential of colostrum-derived Igs using pseudotype neutralization assay on VeroE6 cells. To achieve this aim, we selected two samples of colostrum positive for anti-MERS-CoV that exhibited measurable cross-reactivity toward SARS-CoV-2 RBD. The complexity of colostrum fluid precludes the possibility of direct application in cell-culture based virus neutralizion assay. Therefore, we isolated the soluble fraction of total IgGs and HCIgs from the camel’s colostrum fluid by adapting a classical acidification-induced casein precipitation for whey preparation, the procedure is outlined in Figure S426,27. Subsequent non-reducing SDS-PAGE analysis followed by coomassie blue staining confirmed strong enrichment of two populations of immunoglobulins with apparent molecular weights of ~ 150kDa and ~ 80kDa, corresponding to classical IgGs and HCIgs (Fig. 4A). Accordingly, reducing SDS-PAGE revealed band species corresponding to heavy chains at ~ 50kDa and light chains at ~ 25kDa (Fig. 4A, compare − DTT versus+ DTT, as indicated). Viral load (VL) and infectivity of SARS-CoV-2 patients treated with colostrum spray

Forty-three SARS-CoV-2 patients were enrolled between May and August 2023, for a randomized placebo-controlled study for treatment by the pasteurized colostrum nasal spray. Two Paxlovid (nirmatrelvir/ritonavir) treated patients were excluded (one in the colostrum group and one in the placebo group), and two other patients were found to be false positive (one from each group), thus excluded from analysis. Three participants were hospitalized before the recruitment to the study and one patient (colostrum group) was treated with dexamethasone due to hypoxia.

Discussion

Our study explores serum and colostrum of MERS-seropositive camels and characterizes their antigenic repertoire and breadth of neutralization in cell-culture assays. We revealed SARS-CoV-2 partially cross-reactive IgGs and HCIgs in serum and colostrum samples, showing low level cross-neutralization toward SARS-CoV-2 variants. We confirmed the potential application of colostrum derived antibodies in nasal spray preparations of colostrum, residing at nasal membranes for 2–4 h post-application in healthy humans. While our study suggests potential use for MER-CoV prophylaxis or treatment, our findings, did not support any clinical efficacy, expressed as infectivity or VL reduction of SARS-CoV-2, in human patients treated with nasal spray. These results are in line with low level of cross-neutralization between MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 targeting antibodies.

Our results in healthy volunteers demonstrated significant amounts of anti-S MERS-CoV IgGs and HCIgs residing in the upper airways for as long as 2 to 4 h after application of colostrum-derived spray. This suggests a potential window for short term prophylaxis against infection, which might be expanded by repetitive application or by implementation of mucosal adhesive delivery strategies. The usage of intranasal spray for prevention of coronaviral infections including COVID19 was previously evaluated by others28–31. Kangro et al.26 used vaccinated cow-colostrum whey with artificial viscosity enhancers for nasal spray that persisted for at least 4-h in nasal mucosa in line with our results. Recently, this group indicated that this preparation can effectively block SARS-CoV-2 transmission in ferrets infection model32. Monoclonal antibodies, administered nasally, were shown by Lin et al., to persist and maintained in-vitro neutralization efficacy against SARS-CoV-2 when extracted from the nasal mucosa 24-h after administration33. Recently, a randomized controlled trial indicated 80% efficacy of post-exposure prophylaxis of anti-SARS-COV-2 monoclonal antibody nasal spray (SA58), administered up to 72 h after exposure34.

The obvious pitfall of using monoclonals antibodies in therapeutics and prophylaxis, stems from emerging escape variants. This eventually precluded almost all intravenous monoclonal therapies towards SARS-CoV-2 variants that gained multiple escape mutations. To address this obstacle Xian et al. recently have shown promising preclinical data established, using adaptive multi-epitope targeting by recombinant scaffolding of different nanobodies into human IgM scaffold35.

The camel colostrum whey from MERS-seropositive camels used in our study, is naturally polyclonal. To assess whether this natural anti-MERS response is represented by functionally broad epitopic coverage we compared potency of in-vitro neutralization against MERS-CoV and the magnitude of cross-neutralization against multiple SARS-CoV-2 variants tested, and revealed only weak cross-neutralization potential. The respective NT50 was about 2.5 to fourfold stronger towards MERS-CoV, than towards SARS-COV-2. Such weak, yet relatively equal coverage of SARS-CoV-2 variants stems from their phylogenetic proximity to each other, compared to the distance from MERS-CoV.

In line with weak in vitro cross-neutralization, our in-vivo study precluded the identification of SARS-CoV-2 protective features of mucosal colostrum spray. The amount of antibodies in the colostrum combined with the lower affinity to SARS-CoV-2 in comparison to MERS-CoV, might be not sufficient to neutralize SARS-CoV-2 in-vivo. Future developments may need higher titers of enriched cross-neutralizing Igs and prolonged mucosal residence using viscosity enhancers26 to achieve therapeutic or prophylactic performance. Such future, improved colostrum nasal spray could be also evaluated as a pre- or post-exposure prophylaxis, as suggested by Liu at el.33, rather than therapeutics. Given its neutralization potency toward MERS-CoV pseudoparticles the colostrum nasal spray should be assessed for MERS-CoV prophylaxis using animal models. Future studies are needed to decipher the specific epitope repertoire of the cross-neutralizing antibodies and to identify potential strategies to enhance heterologous protection by hybrid vaccination boosts of MERS-CoV seropositive camels with SARS-CoV-2 antigens10,36,37.

There are several limitations to our study. First, we did not evaluate the presence and the contribution of anti-MERS-CoV and anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgA species, due to the lack of secondary anti-Camelid-IgA antibodies. Second, the relatively small sample size of the clinical cohort, might not be compensated for the heterogeneity of the natural COVID-19 VL kinetics and of immunogenicity among the study participants. Lastly, we cannot exclude that the amount of colostrum might have varied between doses and between the individuals.

Conclusions

Polyclonal whey, derived from colostrum of MERS-CoV-seropositive camels potently neutralizes in-vitro MERS-CoV pseudoparticles, while exhibiting only weak neutralization to a spectrum of SARS-CoV-2 variants. Accordingly, a small in-vivo clinical trial of colostrum nasal spray, did not reveal a reduction of infectivity and VL in COVID-19 patients. While further developments should be explored to attain in-vivo efficacy against SARS-CoV-2, colostrum derived antibodies concentrate and more viscous preparations of the formulations used in our study may be relevant for MERS-CoV preparedness in the pre- and post-exposure settings.
