Eradication of Mycoplasma contamination in HeLa cells using neomycin resistance gene introduction and aminoglycoside G418 (Geneticin) treatment
Authors: Yun Ji Ga, Jung-Yong Yeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycoplasma contamination remains a persistent problem in continuous cell culture, compromising cellular physiology, altering gene expression, and potentially leading to erroneous experimental conclusions. Mycoplasmas can profoundly affect cultured cells, underscoring the need for efficient eradication strategies. Here, we developed a simple and effective method to eliminate Mycoplasma from HeLa cell lines. A neomycin resistance gene was introduced into Mycoplasma-contaminated cells, conferring resistance to aminoglycoside-induced cytotoxicity. Subsequently, a high concentration of the neomycin analogue G418 (Geneticin), combined with single-cell cloning, was applied to achieve complete removal of the Mycoplasma contamination. Mycoplasma presence and clearance were confirmed by PCR targeting the 16 S rRNA gene and immunofluorescence using a Mycoplasma-specific monoclonal antibody. This genetic–antibiotic combination proved technically simple and highly effective for long-term Mycoplasma eradication in continuous cell lines. To investigate the functional impact of Mycoplasma contamination, we compared protein expression following transient transfection with GFP and FAM-labeled reporters in Mycoplasma-eradicated versus Mycoplasma-contaminated cells. Fluorescence analysis revealed a marked increase in transfection efficiency and reporter expression in Mycoplasma-eradicated cells. Our findings provide an effective strategy for eliminating Mycoplasma contamination in cell line cultures, ensuring the reliability of cell-based research and the accuracy of experimental data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55513-w.

Introduction

Mycoplasmas belong to the phylum Mycoplasmatota (formerly named Tenericutes) and the class Mollicutes1–5. They are evolutionary descendants of Gram-positive bacteria with decreased chromosomes and are the smallest known self-replicating organisms in terms of diameter (approximately 240 to 700 nm) and genome size (approximately 500 to 1500 kb)6–8. Mycoplasmas with minimal genomes are characterized not only by the absence of a cell wall but also by significantly reduced metabolic capacities7,9,10. Based on their limited energy and structural biosynthetic capacities, Mycoplasmas are dependent on exogenous resources, such as parasitizing their hosts, for survival8,11,12. Therefore, most of them are non-pathogenic commensals, and others are classified as parasites or saprophytes11,13.

Since the first isolation of Mycoplasma in a cell culture system in 1956, more than 200 species of Mycoplasmas are known to colonize invertebrates, plant, and vertebrate (including fish) cells14–17. Unlike bacteria and fungi, Mycoplasmas grow relatively very slowly even under optimal conditions, so that they do not alter the turbidity or pH of the culture medium and do not cause acute cell damage, enabling them to grow chronically undetected for long periods of time18,19. In addition, Mycoplasmas are very small and have flexible cell membranes that allow them to easily pass through commonly used antimicrobial filters, so they can cause widespread contamination of media and other cell lines10,18,20,21. To date, large-scale studies estimate that approximately 15–35% of cell lines are contaminated with Mycoplasma, with the incidence estimated to be much higher in certain populations due to the lack of accurate diagnosis in many laboratories18,22,23.

The leading method of eradicating Mycoplasma from cultured cells is the administration of antibiotics, and a variety of antibiotics have been designed and are commercially available as anti-Mycoplasma drugs24. These drugs include quinolones (e.g. Ciprobay, Baytril and Zagam), which prevent DNA replication; macrolides, which inhibit protein synthesis by binding to the peptidyl-purifying enzyme component of the 50 S subunit of the ribosome; tetracyclines, which inhibit protein synthesis by binding to the 30 S subunit of the ribosome; or a combination of these (e.g. BM-Cyclin and Plasmocin)18,25,26. Although several commercial antibiotics, including Plasmocin, have shown considerable efficacy in eliminating Mycoplasma contamination in cell cultures, their overall success has been limited. Insufficient concentrations of antibiotics result in only temporary growth inhibition and the Mycoplasmas can re-emerge, while high antibiotic concentrations that are lethal to Mycoplasma cause cytotoxicity and reduce the viability of the cells27–29.

HeLa, the first human cell line derived from cervical cancer in 1951, was the first cell line to be recognized as having Mycoplasma contamination14,30. Continuous cell lines, exemplified by the HeLa cell line, are indispensable tools in medical research and development owing to their rapid proliferation and capacity to efficiently produce diverse proteins. The HeLa cell line alone has been directly used or referenced in over 97,000 publications, accounting for approximately 0.3% of all articles indexed in PubMed31,32. HeLa cells have contributed to significant advances in fields such as cancer biology, virology, immunology, and vaccine development. However, the impact of Mycoplasma contamination on HeLa cells, which has the potential to alter numerous parameters determined in the experimental environment, is not yet well understood33.

In this study, we identified naturally occurring Mycoplasma contamination in the HeLa cell line. A neomycin resistance gene was introduced into the contaminated cells to confer resistance against aminoglycoside-induced cytotoxicity. This enabled the application of high concentrations of the neomycin analogue G418 (Geneticin) to completely eradicate the contamination. The resulting Mycoplasma-free cells were then compared with contaminated HeLa cells to evaluate the impact of Mycoplasma on the expression efficiency of transiently transfected proteins. Our findings provide a practical and effective strategy for eliminating persistent Mycoplasma contamination in cell line cultures.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HeLa CCL-2™ (RRID: CVCL_0030) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA). HeLa cells were propagated and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) and a 100 µg/ml penicillin/streptomycin cocktail (Invitrogen Life Technologies) in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely monitored in cells maintained in vitro using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), DNA sequencing, and immunocytochemistry with monoclonal antibodies34,35. These assays confirmed that a subset of cultures maintained for over 10 passages was contaminated with Mycoplasma for more than one month. In this study, Mycoplasma-contaminated and Mycoplasma-eradicated cells were cultured in separate incubators in order to avoid cross-contamination.

Introduction of a neomycin resistance gene and cytotoxic antibiotic treatment

Transfection of hCas9 plasmid carrying neomycin resistance genes (https://www.addgene.org/plasmid41815/) into HeLa cells was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagents (Invitrogen Life Technologies) following the manufacturer’s instructions36. Two days after transfection, cells were exposed to various concentrations of the neomycin analogue G418 (Invitrogen, Life Technologies). The culture medium containing fresh G418 was replaced every 3 days for a total of 12 days (Stage 1). After Stage 1, each cell was sub-cultured as a single cell in 96-well plates and treated with different concentrations of G418 for the next 12 days (Stage 2 to Stage 5). A brief summary of the G418 treatment protocol is shown graphically in Fig. 1, and the G418 treatment concentrations for each group at each stage are shown in Table 1. After Stage 1, each cell was sub-cultured as a single cell in 96-well plates and treated with different concentrations of G418 for the next 12 days (Stage 2 to Stage 5). Groups A–L represent distinct treatment regimens consisting of various G418 concentrations (300–600 µg/ml) and exposure durations across the sequential stages, as summarized in Table 1. The “Mycoplasma eradication percentage” indicates the proportion of Mycoplasma-negative colonies confirmed by PCR among the total number of viable single-cell colonies. The numbers in parentheses denote the exact counts of Mycoplasma-negative colonies over total viable colonies (e.g., 6/6 indicates that all colonies tested negative for Mycoplasma).

Comparison of fluorescent protein expression in Mycoplasma-contaminated and Mycoplasma-eradicated cells

To assess the potential impact of Mycoplasma contamination, we compared the protein expression efficiency of transiently transfected genes between Mycoplasma-eradicated and Mycoplasma-contaminated cells. Transiently transfection of 25 nM fluorescein amidite (FAM)-labeled siRNA and pEGFP-C1 plasmid (Takara Bio USA, Inc., CA, USA) into HeLa cells was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX 2000 reagents and Lipofectamine 3000 reagents (Invitrogen Life Technologies) following the manufacturer’s instructions, respectively. We transiently transfected Mycoplasma-contaminated (Mycoplasma⁺) and Mycoplasma-eradicated (Mycoplasma⁻) HeLa cells with FAM-labelled siRNA and compared their fluorescence intensity. The green FAM fluorescence signal was noticeably weaker in Mycoplasma⁺ cells than in Mycoplasma⁻ cells. In a similar approach, we transiently transfected both cell types with a plasmid encoding EGFP and compared the resulting green fluorescence intensity.

Mycoplasma detection and species identification from contaminated HeLa cells

Cell culture supernatants were centrifuged for 5 min at 1,500 rpm to separate cellular debris. The supernatant without cell debris was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to precipitate Mycoplasma. The resulting supernatants were discarded, and the pellets were resuspended in sterile water and incubated for 10 min at 98 °C. After boiling, the solution was finally centrifuged for 5 min at 12,000 rpm and the supernatant was used as a PCR template37. Mycoplasma contamination was detected using the AccuPower HotStart Pfu PCR PreMix (Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

To detect Mycoplasma contamination in HeLa cells, we used universal primers previously reported to be sufficiently sensitive to detect the most common Mycoplasma species contaminating cell cultures, while remaining specific enough to avoid amplification of any other bacterial 16S rRNA genes38–41. These primers target a 450 bp region of the Mycoplasma spp. encoding 16S rRNA gene. The sequences of the primers were as follows: 450F, 5’-GGCGAATGGGTGAGTAACACG-3’, 450R, 5’-CGGATAACGCTTGCGACCTATG-3’38–40.

To obtain sequencing information for Mycoplasma species identification, PCR was additionally performed using a Mycoplasma-specific 450F forward primer and a bacterial 16S rRNA universal reverse primer. The sequence of the universal primer for the conserved bacterial 16S rRNA region was as follows: 800R, 5’- GGACTACCAGGGTATCTAATCC-3’ to amplify a longer 16 S region for species identification41. PCR conditions were identical to those described above, except for the reverse primer. Purified PCR products were submitted to Cosmogenetech Inc. (Seoul, South Korea) for sequencing. Species identification was performed using BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) searches based on the sequencing results.

Immunofluorescence

Mycoplasma-contaminated HeLa cells and Mycoplasma-eradicated HeLa cells were seeded on a chamber slide one day prior to FAM-labeled siRNA or pEGFP-C1 plasmid transfection. Following transfection, the cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Next, 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) in PBS with 0.05% Tween® 20 (PBST, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to permeabilize the fixed cells for 10 min, and then blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBST for 30 min at 25 °C. The cells were then incubated with F9 mouse-derived Mycoplasma monoclonal antibody overnight at 4 °C. The F9 monoclonal antibody has previously been reported to react with several Mycoplasma-contaminated cell lines using immunofluorescence staining34,35. After washing, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594 conjugated Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody (Invitrogen Life Technologies) for 1 h at 25 °C. Hoechst 33,342 (Invitrogen Life Technologies) was used to stain the nuclei for 10 min at 25 °C. Axio Vision version 4.0 (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) was used to examine the data after the cells were seen using an epifluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss).

Flow cytometry

Two days after transfection with the pEGFP-C1 plasmid, Mycoplasma-contaminated HeLa cells and Mycoplasma-eradicated HeLa cells were trypsinized and harvested. we counted the number of EGFP-positive cells using flow cytometry. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using the LSR II flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) at Core-facility for Cell to In-vivo imaging.

Statistical analyses

Three independent assays were conducted in triplicate, and statistical analyses were performed using a standard statistical software package (Prism; GraphPad Software, San Jose, CA, USA). To assess the significance of differences, either the student’s t-test, two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-test, or one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s multiple comparison test was employed.

Results

PCR-based detection of Mycoplasma contamination and identification of Mycoplasma species

Using the supernatant of HeLa cells as a template, the PCR product showed an obvious Mycoplasma-positive band of 450 bp amplicon (Fig. 2a). In order to identify the genus of the contaminated Mycoplasma, reverse primers specific for the 16s rRNA gene, which is conserved in a wide range of bacteria, were combined with forward primers used in Mycoplasma diagnostics to produce a PCR product of approximately 800 bp (Fig. 2b). The amplified 16 S rRNA sequence showing 93% homology to Mycoplasma arginini has been deposited in GenBank under the accession number PX559944.1 (Uncultured Mycoplasma sp. clone HL-23 16 S ribosomal RNA gene, partial sequence). In addition, M. arginini specific PCR primers42 for M. arginini also produced a distinct band of approximately 326 bp (Fig. 2c).

Neomycin resistance gene transfection and cytotoxicity evaluation

Mycoplasma-contaminated HeLa cells were transfected with the neomycin resistance gene to confer tolerance to high-dose G418 (Geneticin). In Stage 1, cells were treated two days after transfection with 100, 300, or 600 µg/mL G418 for 12 days (Fig. 1). PCR analysis of culture supernatants on days 8 and 12 (Fig. 3a and b) confirmed Mycoplasma contamination under all conditions, indicating that Stage 1 alone did not achieve eradication. As shown in Fig. 3c, although cell viability was low at 600 µg/mL, neomycin resistance gene transfection markedly enhanced the selective survival of cells at higher G418 concentrations. Transfected cells that survived treatment with 300–600 µg/mL G418 in Stage 1 were subsequently used for single-cell cloning in Stage 2.

Elimination of Mycoplasma contamination via single-cell cloning

Single cells were cultured in 96-well plates and exposed to different concentrations of antibiotics for 12 days across Stages 2 to 5, resulting in 12 experimental groups (Table 1). After four medium changes up to Stage 5, a total of 155 viable cell colonies were obtained and assessed for Mycoplasma contamination by PCR. Mycoplasma-negative results were detected in the supernatants of 99 of the 155 colonies. Among the 12 groups, seven groups (E, F, G, I, J, K, and L) that had received 600 µg/mL G418 in Stage 1 achieved > 85% Mycoplasma eradication, regardless of the antibiotic concentration used in subsequent stages. Notably, group E yielded 18 of 19 Mycoplasma-negative colonies despite being cultured in G418-free medium from Stage 2 onward. In contrast, groups A, B, and H, which received 300 µg/mL G418 in Stage 1, showed < 10% eradication. Group C, which continued 300 µg/mL G418 treatment until Stage 3, achieved 66.6% eradication, whereas group D, which continued 300 µg/mL G418 treatment until Stage 5, achieved complete (100%) eradication. These results are summarized for all 12 groups in Table 1.

In assessing the impact of Mycoplasma contamination, we cultured cells that had been confirmed to be Mycoplasma-negative by PCR diagnostics in antibiotic-free medium for at least 2 weeks and then determined whether Mycoplasma eradication was sustained by immunofluorescence staining using monoclonal antibodies (Fig. 4a and b). As a result, cells contaminated with Mycoplasma prior to antibiotic treatment showed extensive red stained spots throughout the cell, whereas cells eradicated of Mycoplasma showed no red staining at all.

Complete removal of Mycoplasma contamination enhances the efficiency of transient transfection in HeLa cells

The results showed that the green EGFP signal was much stronger in Mycoplasma− HeLa cells, consistent with the FAM fluorescence expression that was labelled by the siRNA. In addition, we used flow cytometry to precisely quantify EGFP fluorescence expression observed by fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 4c). As a result of evaluating transfection efficiency by measuring the proportion of EGFP positive cells, Mycoplasma− HeLa cells showed an approximate 19% increase in transfection efficiency compared to Mycoplasma+ HeLa cells.

Discussion

Cell culture is an indispensable tool in diverse biomedical research fields; however, contamination by Mycoplasma remains a persistent problem that can compromise or invalidate experimental outcomes43,44. A substantial proportion of cell lines worldwide are chronically infected with Mycoplasmas. In the absence of effective treatments, discarding contaminated cultures is generally considered the best option, as they pose a risk of cross-contamination to other cell lines45,46.

In this study, Mycoplasma contamination of HeLa cells was confirmed by PCR, the most sensitive and specific detection method, and further identified as Mycoplasma arginini by sequencing of PCR products targeting the 16 S rRNA gene (Fig. 2). Common sources of Mycoplasma in cell culture include M. orale, M. fermentans, and M. hominis from the human oropharynx; M. arginini and Acholeplasma laidlawii from fetal or newborn bovine serum; and M. hyorinis from trypsin preparations derived from swine10. Today, serum and media obtained from reputable manufacturers are rarely a source of Mycoplasma contamination, but in the 1960s and 1970s, serum products such as FBS and NBS were recognized as common sources of Mycoplasma47,48. Therefore, contamination of HeLa cell lines in the laboratory is generally presumed to have originated from FBS, emphasizing the importance of testing for Mycoplasma in cell lines with a long history of culture, such as HeLa cell lines49.

Commercial antibiotic-based Mycoplasma removal kits are available but are recommended only for irreplaceable cell lines, due to high cytotoxicity and the potential for Mycoplasma re-emergence18,25,50. In our approach, a neomycin resistance gene was introduced into contaminated HeLa cells to overcome the cytotoxicity of the aminoglycoside antibiotic G418 (Geneticin), which inhibits protein synthesis in a broad range of organisms51. Cells were then exposed to high concentrations of G418, and single-cell cloning was performed to prevent re-infection. PCR analysis confirmed Mycoplasma-negative status in 99 of 155 single-cell colonies (Fig. 4). Groups treated with 600 µg/mL G418 in Stage 1 exhibited markedly higher eradication rates (> 85%) than those treated with 300 µg/mL, highlighting the importance of an initial high-dose treatment prior to cloning. The Mycoplasma-free status was stably maintained in antibiotic-free culture.

In this study, positive selection of transfected cells was achieved using G418, the standard antibiotic for neomycin resistance genes (neo/nptII), which has well-established efficiency and reproducibility in mammalian cell culture protocols52. Although the primary function of G418 is the selection of transfected clones, several reports indicate that aminoglycosides, including G418, may also suppress Mycoplasma growth under certain conditions, likely due to its broad-spectrum inhibition of protein synthesis in prokaryotic as well as eukaryotic cells24,53–55. Previous studies have suggested that plasmids bearing strong promoters or selection markers may exert a minor transient influence on overall gene expression or cellular physiology56,57. Therefore, we plan to conduct further studies to comprehensively evaluate whether transient transfection with the resistance gene influences endogenous gene expression or cellular functions in our system, and additionally to assess the possibility of using alternative antibiotics such as puromycin.

Mycoplasma contamination can cause slow or abnormal cell growth, changes resembling nutritional deficiencies, and irregular monolayer morphology. However, these alterations are often subtle and can be reversed by frequent medium changes, making them difficult to detect by light microscopy58,59. Despite the absence of overt cytopathic effects, Mycoplasmas can significantly alter the biochemical and genetic properties of host cells, leading to misleading results44,60–64. Additionally, even if contaminated cells are completely cured, important sequelae, such as loss of cell properties that may occur during treatment, are not yet understood. In our study, both siRNA- and plasmid-mediated transfection efficiency, as measured by fluorescent protein expression, were markedly enhanced after Mycoplasma eradication (Fig. 6).

Although there are other effective methodologies for the eradication of Mycoplasma, including commercial antibiotic treatments validated in previous studies18,25,26, we achieved complete elimination of M. arginini contamination from HeLa cells through a combination of neomycin resistance gene introduction, high-dose G418 (Geneticin) treatment, and single-cell cloning. This approach provides an effective, technically accessible, and cost-efficient strategy for eliminating Mycoplasma from continuous cell cultures, improving transfection efficiency, and supporting the reliability and integrity of cell-based research.
