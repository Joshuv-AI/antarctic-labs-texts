Neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery versus upfront surgery for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yanmo Liu, Cuiyun Wu, Qifeng Huang, Yizhao Zhou, Kezhong Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhether neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAT) is beneficial for resectable and locally advanced intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is still controversial. This study aimed to compare the efficacy of NAT followed by surgery and upfront surgery for ICC treatment.MethodEmbase, Pubmed, Web of Science and Cochrane library databases were searched to obtain comparative studies. The non-randomized interventions (ROBINS-I) was used to assess the methodological quality of included studies. The primary outcomes included 1-year, 3-year and 5-year overall survival (OS). The secondary outcomes were R0 resection, 1-year and 3-year recurrence-free survival (RFS).ResultsNine retrospective studies were included in the meta-analysis. A total of 8211 patients were included in the study, of whom 1836 patients received NAT followed by surgery and 6375 patients received upfront surgery. Compared with upfront surgery, NAT group had a significantly better 1-year OS (relative risk (RR) 1.09; 95%CI 1.06, 1.12. p = 0.449) and 3-year OS (RR 1.15; 95%CI 1.06, 1.24. p = 0.386). NAT group had similar results compared with upfront surgery group in 5-year OS (RR 1.16; 95%CI 0.94, 1.44. p < 0.05), 1-year RFS (RR 1.05; 95%CI 0.90, 1.23. p = 0.481), 3-year RFS (RR 1.03; 95%CI 0.82, 1.30. p = 0.969) and R0 resection rate (RR 0.94; 95%CI 0.82, 1.08. p < 0.05).ConclusionCompared with upfront surgery, NAT followed by surgery had better results for resectable and locally advanced ICC. Considering the patients in NAT followed by surgery group had more advanced ICC cases, NAT followed by surgery might be a rationale choice for ICC treatment. Further studies about effective downstage treatments for locally advantage ICC are needed in future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15203-8.Keywords: Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery, Upfront surgery

Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is the second most common liver tumor and the third most common liver malignancy worldwide, accounting for 10% to 20% of all cholangiocarcinoma [1]. Many patients present with advanced disease and have poor outcomes due to late diagnosis. Surgical resection is widely accepted as curative therapy for ICC [2]. However, even patients received curative-intent resection, prognosis can vary markedly due to high recurrence rate [3]. To improve the prognosis of ICC, a multimodal treatment including neoadjuvant, adjuvant chemotherapy and radiation therapy was considered [4, 5]. Recently, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAT) has emerged as a promising method to potentially downstage initially unresectable patients [6]. The role of NAT in ICC beyond unresectable patients remains poorly defined. Some studies mentioned NAT may benefit patients with resectable/locally advanced ICC to reduce micrometastases [7, 8], which is beneficial to overcome the high incidence of very early recurrence following upfront surgery. However, some scholars questioned whether the introduction of NAT prolonged the waiting time for surgical resection (about 6.8 months), which may delay and even worsen the disease [9, 10].

As limited by small, single-center cohorts, whether patients with resectable/locally advanced ICC can get benefits from NAT followed by surgery is still controversial. A systematic review and meta-analysis including currently available studies is valuable research to answer this question. A recent meta-analysis published in 2023 shows there are no benefits for NAT followed by surgery to prolong 1-year overall survival (OS) and 3-year OS compared with upfront surgery [11]. NAT followed by surgery group has lower R0 resection rate compared with upfront surgery. However, the meta-analysis involved only 5 studies with one study lack of critical prognosis results, which makes the results not so reliable.

As the development of multimodal treatment strategies for ICC, a lot of studies about NAT followed by surgery versus upfront surgery for ICC emerged in recent years. This study aims to reevaluate the efficacy of NAT followed by surgery comparing with upfront surgery for ICC treatment.

Materials and methods

Literature search strategy

An electronic literature search was undertaken using Embase, Pubmed, Web of Science and Cochrane library databases up to May 2025. Additional articles were identified by hand searching. Search terms included “Neoadjuvant therapy”, “Neoadjuvant chemotherapy”, “Biliary tract cancer”, “Cholangiocarcinoma” and “Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma”. Two authors Liu and Tang performed the electronic search independently in May 2025. Abstracts of the literatures were reviewed to determine their suitability for inclusion in the pooled analysis. Any discordances regarding study inclusion between these 2 authors were settled in discussion with a third independent author. The PROSPERO registration number of this study was CRD420251060064.

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

Publications were included in this review if they meet the following criteria:

Comparing NAT followed by surgery with upfront surgery for ICC.

Comparing NAT followed by surgery with upfront surgery for ICC.

Involving at least one outcome of interest such as OS, progression-free survival (PFS) and R0 resection rate.

Involving at least one outcome of interest such as OS, progression-free survival (PFS) and R0 resection rate.

In case of duplication, only the most detailed and complete study included.

In case of duplication, only the most detailed and complete study included.

Published in English.

Published in English.

Publications were excluded if they met any of the following criteria:

Without information of ICC treatment.

Without information of ICC treatment.

Involving other treatments, such as liver transplantation for ICC.

Involving other treatments, such as liver transplantation for ICC.

No original data included in the study.

No original data included in the study.

Data extraction

A standardized data collection form was used to extracted the following information: first author’s name, publication year, country, type of study, treatment, sex, age range, number of cases, tumor characteristics and outcomes. When a study presented models that adjusted for different numbers of control variables, data were extracted from the model that adjusted for the largest number of variables. In the situation in which authors from the same institution had published a primary paper and then an updated analysis with a larger patient cohort was included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Two independent reviewers extracted data from the selected articles by using a predefined data extraction form, including 1-year OS, 3-year OS, 5-year OS, 1-year RFS, 3-year RFS and R0 resection rate. To estimate RR and its variance, this was extracted from the study directly or required additional calculation depending on the method of data being presented: the number of patients with outcomes of interest in NAT followed by surgery group and upfront surgery group.

Meta-analysis of data was conducted using a random effects model. Publication bias was explored graphically with funnel plots to detect asymmetry and any outliers. Inter-study heterogeneity was assessed using the x2 statistic and the I2 value to measure the degree of variation not attributable to chance alone. This was graded as low (I2 < 25%), moderate (I2 25% to 75%), or high (I2 > 75%). The significance level was set at P < 0.05. This meta-analysis is exempt from ethical approval and informed consent form as the analysis involves only already published and anonymized data.

Results

Search results

Figure 1 shows the literature search flowchart. During the literature search we found 2659 studies. 623 articles were removed because of duplication. After reviewing the titles and abstracts we found 2036 articles to be not eligible as they were review articles, editorials, meta-analysis or not focusing on the review topic, and others not meeting the inclusion criteria. We identified 26 articles as potentially eligible for this review. However, 4 of these articles were Conference summary. 7 studies did not meet inclusion criteria. 2 studies were without results of interested and 4 of them were not for ICC. We finally selected 9 retrospective studies [12–20], including two with propensity score matching [16, 19] (Fig. 1).

The PRISMA flowchart of literature research

Characteristics of the studies

In this meta-analysis, we included 9 studies [12–20] to evaluate the efficacy of NAT followed by surgery compared with upfront surgery for treating ICC. In Tables 1 and 2, we report the main characteristics of these studies. A total of 8211 patients were included in the study, of whom 1836 patients received NAT followed by surgery and 6375 patients received upfront surgery. Of 9 studies included, 4 from USA [15, 17, 19, 20], 2 from France [13, 14], 2 from Italy [16, 18] and 1 from multiple regions [12] respectively. Most of the studies included patients in NAT followed surgery group with poorer diagnosis of ICC compared those in upfront surgery group. Three studies [12–14] included locally advanced ICC patients in NAT group. One study included oncologic high-risk resectable ICC patients in NAT group [20].

1-year OS

Eight studies [12–15, 17–20] reported 1-year OS of the two procedures. Compared with upfront surgery group, NAT followed surgery group obtained a significantly better 1-year OS (RR 1.09; 95%CI 1.06, 1.12) with no significant heterogeneity (p = 0.449, I2 = 0) (Fig. 2).

Meta-analysis forest plot of 1-year OS between NAT followed by surgery and upfront surgery for ICC treatment

3-year OS

Eight studies [12–15, 17–20] reported 3-year OS of the two procedures. Compared with upfront surgery group, NAT followed surgery group obtained a significantly better 3-year OS (RR 1.15; 95%CI 1.06, 1.24) with no significant heterogeneity (p = 0.386, I2 = 5.7%) (Fig. 3).

Meta-analysis forest plot of 3-year OS between NAT followed by surgery and upfront surgery for ICC treatment

5-year OS

Eight studies [12–19] reported 5-year OS of the two procedures. Compared with upfront surgery group, NAT followed surgery group obtained a better 5-year OS (RR 1.16; 95%CI 0.94, 1.44) with a moderate heterogeneity (p = 0.003, I2 = 68.0%), though the result had no significant difference (Fig. 4).

Meta-analysis forest plot of 5-year OS between NAT followed by surgery and upfront surgery for ICC treatment

1-year RFS

Five studies [12–15, 20] reported 1-year RFS of the two procedures. Compared with upfront surgery group, NAT followed surgery group obtained a better 1-year RFS (RR 1.05; 95%CI 0.90, 1.23) with no significant heterogeneity (p = 0.481, I2 = 0), though the result had no significant difference (Fig. 5).

Meta-analysis forest plot of 1-year RFS between NAT followed by surgery and upfront surgery for ICC treatment

3-year RFS

Five studies [12–15, 20] reported 3-year RFS of the two procedures. There was no significant difference between the two group (RR 1.03; 95%CI 0.82, 1.30). The result had no significant heterogeneity (p = 0.969, I2 = 0) (Fig. 6).

Meta-analysis forest plot of 3-year RFS between NAT followed by surgery and upfront surgery for ICC treatment

R0 resection rate

Six studies [12–14, 16, 17, 19] reported R0 resection rate of the two procedures. There was no significant difference between the two group (RR 0.94; 95%CI 0.82, 1.08). The result had a high heterogeneity (p = 0.000, I2 = 86.8%) (Fig. 7).

Meta-analysis forest plot of R0 resection rate between NAT followed by surgery and upfront surgery for ICC treatment

Sensitivity analysis

We used leave-one-out sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of each study on pooled results and heterogeneity. The outcomes of 1-year OS, 1-year RFS and 3-year RFS were stable. There was no effect on statistical significance after removing any individual study. For 3-year OS, the heterogeneity reduced when the study by Sutton et al. [15] was excluded (p = 0.468, I2 = 0), the pooled result remained consistent with those obtained before (RR 1.14; 95%CI 1.05, 1.22). For 5-year OS, the heterogeneity reduced slightly when the study by Sutton et al. [15] was excluded (p = 0.014, I2 = 62.5%), the pooled result remained consistent with those obtained before (RR 1.09; 95%CI 0.90, 1.34). For R0 resection, no significant change was found in results and heterogeneity after removing any one of studies included.

Risk of bias assessment

The results of bias assessment for retrospective studies involved in the study are presented in Figs. 8. ROBINS-I was performed for the nine studies [12–20] included to assess risk of bias. Eight studies [12–15, 17–20] showed moderate overall risk of bias, and one study [16] showed serious overall risk of bias.

Assessment according to the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for evaluating risk of bias in non-randomized trials (ROBINS-I)

The result of publication bias for 1-year OS was performed and shown in Fig. 9. A symmetrical distribution was obtained in the funnel plot analysis of 1-year OS indicating no publication bias existed. The p values of Begg’s test and Egger’s test were 0.458 and 0.036 respectively.

Discussion

NAT before surgical resection has been incorporated into multimodal management pathways of many gastrointestinal tumors, such as esophageal, gastric, rectal, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma, even when resectable, with intent to down-stage and systemic disease control [21, 22]. However, the importance of NAT for resectable ICC is still controversial. It was always considered a standard therapy to convert initially unresectable ICC to resectable [23]. National guidelines also advocate upfront surgery with adjuvant therapy a standard therapy for patients with resectable ICC [24]. So, a better understanding of the potential benefits of NAT for resectable/locally advanced ICC would be useful. The intent of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of NAT followed by surgery compared with upfront surgery for treating ICC.

In this meta-analysis, we included 9 studies comprising 8211 patients to perform the analysis. Our main findings in this study were as follows: (1) NAT followed by surgery was related to better 1-year OS and 3-year OS compared with upfront surgery for treating ICC. The results had significant difference; (2) NAT group had similar results compared with upfront surgery group in 5-year OS (RR 1.16; 95%CI 0.94, 1.44), 1-year RFS (RR 1.05; 95%CI 0.90, 1.23), 3-year RFS (RR 1.03; 95%CI 0.82, 1.30) and R0 resection rate (RR 0.94; 95%CI 0.82, 1.08). In this meta-analysis, eligibility criteria for resection were tumors amenable to complete extirpation regardless of margin width and leaving an adequate volume of liver tissue (minimum of two contiguous Couinaud liver segments representing at least 30% of the total liver volume) with adequate perfusion, venous, and biliary drainage [13, 25]. Locally advanced ICC was defined as patients presenting with a tumor in contact with the future remaining hepatic or portal veins, or those with contralateral metastases [14]. Patients with extrahepatic metastases or distant lymphadenopathy were considered initially unresectable ICC.

Neoadjuvant therapy can downstage tumors and improve the probability of complete resection of tumors with negative margins. This theory has been demonstrated in many kinds of tumors [26, 27]. However, in this study, NAT followed by surgery groups just obtained comparable result in R0 resection rate compared with upfront surgery for treating ICC. This may be due to the selection bias that patients in NAT group were more likely to have more advanced ICC. In the nine studies included in this meta-analysis, three studies included locally advanced ICC patients in NAT group [12–14]. One study included oncologic high-risk resectable ICC patients in NAT group [20]. Compared with three studies including locally advanced ICC, R0 resection rate of the other three studies [16, 17, 19] was significantly better. In a recent retrospective study [17] of 399 resectable ICC patients receiving NAT, R0 resection rate was significantly higher in NAT group compared that in upfront surgery group (RR 1.19; 95%CI 1.11, 1.27). For resectable ICC patients, NAT would not reduce R0 resection rate compared with upfront surgery. At the same time, NAT could reduce micrometastases and select patients who would get benefits from curative-intent resection.

In this study, we found a significant better OS benefit of NAT followed by surgery group compared with upfront surgery, whether on short-term basis or on long-term basis. This result was quite different with another similar research published in 2023. This may also be due to the selection bias that patients in NAT group. In NAT group, all studies included in the article included patients who received curative-intent resection for ICC. While the characteristics of patients in these studies have several differences. In the five studies [12–16] included in the meta-analysis published in 2023, Bueter et al. included some patients with extrahepatic metastases [12]. Le Roy et al. just included patients with locally advanced ICC [13]. The other three studies [14–16] included patients in NAT group including T1-4 and N0-1, including resectable and locally advanced ICC. While in four extra studies [17–20] included in this article, Wehrle et al. included patients with resectable ICC including T1-3 and N0-1 [19]. The other 3 studies [17, 18, 20] included patients in NAT group including T1-4 and N0-1, including resectable and locally advanced ICC. Patients included in the five studies [12–16] included in meta-analysis published in 2023 were in a later stage of ICC compared with those in the four extra studies [17–20] in general. The selection of multiagent chemotherapy for NAT is another potential reason to support NAT group obtaining better survival benefits (Table 1S). Different types of downstaging treatment, such as types of chemotherapy and Ytt-90, will be applied in NAT group before 2010 [14]. After the ABC-02 trial published in 2010 [28], gemcitabine combined with cisplatin was established as standard of care for locally advanced and unresectable biliary tract cancers and applied in neoadjuvant therapy for ICC gradually. In the retrospective study including patients from 2014 to 2020, gemcitabine plus cisplatin for NAT group account for 65.2% in neoadjuvant chemotherapy [20]. While in the retrospective study including patients from 1997 to 2017, types of chemotherapy including gemcitabine plus cisplatin were applied in NAT group [14]. However, in the study reported by Wehrle et al. [19], patients in two groups were matched using the propensity score. There was no survival benefit obtain in NAT group. The possible reason is patients including in NAT and upfront surgery group were in earlier stage compared with other studies. Unlike OS benefit obtained in NAT group, little improvements in RFS were obtained Compared with upfront surgery group. The possible reason is patients in NAT group had more advanced ICC cases, which effects the result of RFS.

This study has some limitations as follows: (1) There is no high quality randomized controlled trial meeting the inclusion criteria. To obtain a reliable conclusion, we included 9 retrospective studies. More high quality and well-designed clinical trials are needed in future. (2) Patients who received NAT and finally received surgery were including in NAT group in this meta-analysis. While patients who failed to perform operation after NAT were not taken into account, which may overestimate the benefits of NAT for ICC. (3) Some studies included in the research involved small number of patients, which make the pooled result not so stable. Large scale and multicenter studies are needed in future. (4) The selection bias that patients in NAT group were more likely to have more advanced ICC may affect the result of NAT followed by surgery for treating ICC. (5) All studies included were retrospective study up to 2020. In a near future, targeted therapy, immunotherapy and effective locoregional treatment like transarterial radioembolization (TARE) were considered effective downstage treatments for locally advantage ICC [1, 29]. Further studies about effective downstage treatments for locally advantage ICC are needed in future.

Conclusion

Compared with upfront surgery, NAT followed by surgery had better results for resectable and locally advanced ICC. Considering the patients in NAT followed by surgery group had more advanced ICC cases, NAT followed by surgery might be a rationale choice for ICC treatment. Further studies about effective downstage treatments for locally advantage ICC are needed in future.
