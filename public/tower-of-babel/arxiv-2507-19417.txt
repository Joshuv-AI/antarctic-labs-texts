Cycle-factors of regular graphs via entropy
Micha Christoph, Nemanja Draganić, António Girão, Eoin Hurley, Lukas Michel, Alp Müyesser

Abstract
It is a classical result that a random permutation of n n elements has, on average, about log ⁡ n \log n cycles. We generalise this fact to all directed d d -regular graphs on n n vertices by showing that, on average, a random cycle-factor of such a graph has 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( n ​ log ⁡ d ) / d ) \mathcal{O}((n\log d)/d) cycles. This is tight up to the constant factor and improves the best previous bound of the form 𝒪 ⁡ ( n / log ⁡ d ) \mathcal{O}({n/\!\sqrt{\log d}}) due to Vishnoi. Our results also yield randomised polynomial-time algorithms for finding such a cycle-factor and for finding a tour of length ( 1 + 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( log ⁡ d ) / d ) ) ⋅ n (1+\mathcal{O}((\log d)/d))\cdot n if the graph is connected. This makes progress on a conjecture of Magnant and Martin and on a problem studied by Vishnoi and by Feige, Ravi, and Singh. Our proof uses the language of entropy to exploit the fact that the upper and lower bounds on the number of perfect matchings in regular bipartite graphs are extremely close.

Preliminaries

Let
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\}
, and let
S_{n}
denote the family of permutations of
[n]

\sigma\in S_{n}
and
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[n]

\sigma(\mathcal{I})=\{\sigma(i):i\in\mathcal{I}\}
. A cycle of
\sigma\in S_{n}
is a minimal non-empty subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[n]
such that
\sigma(\mathcal{I})=\mathcal{I}
. We denote the number of cycles of
\sigma
by
\lvert\sigma\rvert
.

For our proofs, we will need Stirling’s approximation which states that as
n\to\infty
, we have
n!=(1+o(1))\cdot\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^{n}
. In fact, it will be more useful for us to use the weaker bounds
(n/e)^{n}\leq n!\leq en(n/e)^{n}
which hold for all
n
.

We now recall some basic definitions and properties of Shannon entropy which will be essential for our proofs. For a more thorough exposition, we refer the reader to
[
AZ18
, Chapter 37]
. This also contains a nice exposition of Radhakrishnan’s proof
[
Rad97
]
of the Brégman-Minc inequality (see
Theorem

3.1
), which serves as the starting point of our approach. In the following, all logarithms are base
2
, and we use the convention
p\log p=0
for
p=0
.

Suppose that
X
is a random variable that takes values in a finite set
\mathcal{X}
. Then, the
Shannon entropy
of
X
is defined as

\mathcal{H}(X)\coloneqq-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{P}(X=x)\log(\mathbb{P}(X=x)).

One basic property of entropy is that
\mathcal{H}(X)\leq\log\lvert\mathcal{X}\rvert
with equality if and only if
X
is distributed uniformly at random in
\mathcal{X}
. For
p\in[0,1]
, write
\mathcal{H}(p)\coloneqq-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)
for the entropy of a Bernoulli random variable with probability
p
.

The definition of entropy easily extends to conditional probability. If
E
is some event, then the entropy of
X
conditioned on
E
occurring is

\mathcal{H}(X\mid E)\coloneqq-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{P}(X=x\mid E)\log(\mathbb{P}(X=x\mid E)).

If
Y
is another random variable that takes values in a finite set
\mathcal{Y}
, then the
conditional entropy
of
X
given
Y
is

\mathcal{H}(X\mid Y)\coloneqq\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{P}(Y=y)\cdot\mathcal{H}(X\mid Y=y).

Note that
\mathcal{H}(X\mid Y)=0
if and only if
Y
completely determines
X
.

The only fact about conditional entropy that we will need is the
chain rule
which says that if
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are random variables, then

\mathcal{H}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=\mathcal{H}(X_{1})+\mathcal{H}(X_{2}\mid X_{1})+\dots+\mathcal{H}(X_{n}\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{n-1})

where the tuple
X_{1},\dots,X_{i}
is viewed as a single random variable whose distribution is the joint distribution of
X_{1},\dots,X_{i}
.

3
Expected number of cycles in a random cycle-factor

Suppose that
G
is a directed
d
-regular graph on
n
vertices. We wish to show that the expected number of cycles of a uniformly random cycle-factor of
G
is
\mathcal{O}((n\log d)/d)
. To achieve this, we will reveal the edges of the random cycle-factor one by one in a random order and bound the probability that each new revealed edge closes a cycle. We do this by going through the vertices in a random order and revealing their out-neighbours in the cycle-factor. If the revealed out-neighbour of a vertex were always uniformly distributed among all remaining available out-neighbours, then we could show the desired bound. Indeed, since the edges are revealed in a random order, it is possible to show that the number
t
of remaining out-neighbours of a vertex is uniformly distributed in
[d]
, and so the probability that each new revealed edge closes a cycle would be at most

\frac{1}{d}\sum_{t=1}^{d}\frac{1}{t}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right).

To handle the general case where out-neighbours are not uniformly distributed, we connect our process to Radhakrishnan’s entropy proof
[
Rad97
]
of the Brègman-Minc inequality
[
Brè73
]
. Towards that end, construct a
d
-regular bipartite graph
H
with bipartition
V(H)=U\cup V
where
U
and
V
are both copies of
V(G)
, and for each directed edge
(u,v)
of
G
we add an edge between
u\in U
and
v\in V
to
H
. It is easy to see that cycle-factors of
G
correspond to perfect matchings of
H

to show that a typical cycle-factor in a

Every

We pursue a very similar strategy to analyse random cycle-factors of
G
. Conveniently, revealing an edge of a random perfect matching of the auxiliary bipartite graph

Theorem 3.2

Every

This lower bound allows us to quantify how much entropy can be lost while revealing the edges of the random cycle-factor of

compared to what the entropy would have been had the edges been uniformly distributed throughout the process. The key fact that ties everything together is that any loss in entropy increases the probability of closing a cycle at most by an additive linear term. Together, this implies

We start by proving the following simple lemma that formalises the relationship between a loss in entropy and an increase in the probability of a random variable attaining a specific value. In our case, this will correspond to the probability of closing a cycle.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
be a random variable taking values in a set
\mathcal{X}
of size
s
, and let
\ell\coloneqq\log s-\mathcal{H}(X)
. Then, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
it holds that

\mathbb{P}(X=x)\leq\frac{2}{s}+\ell.

Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
and let
p\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(X=x)
. Consider the indicator random variable
\mathbbm{1}_{X=x}
of the event that
X=x
. By the chain rule for entropy,

\mathcal{H}(X)=\mathcal{H}(\mathbbm{1}_{X=x})+\mathcal{H}(X\mid\mathbbm{1}_{X=x})=\mathcal{H}(p)+p\cdot\mathcal{H}(X\mid X=x)+(1-p)\cdot\mathcal{H}(X\mid X\neq x).

Clearly,
\mathcal{H}(X\mid X=x)=0
. Also, if
X\neq x
, then
X
takes at most
s-1
distinct values, and so
\mathcal{H}(X\mid X\neq x)\leq\log(s-1)
. This implies that

\mathcal{H}(X)\leq\mathcal{H}(p)+(1-p)\log(s-1).

Let
h(q)\coloneqq\mathcal{H}(q)+(1-q)\log(s-1)
. Note that
h(q)=\mathcal{H}(Y)
for the random variable
Y
with
\mathbb{P}(Y=x)=q
and
\mathbb{P}(Y=y)=(1-q)/(s-1)
for all
y\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{x\}
, and so
h(q)\leq\log s
. Moreover,

h^{\prime}(q)=-\log q+\log(1-q)-\log(s-1)=\log\left(\frac{1-q}{q(s-1)}\right).

Since this is decreasing in
q
, we get for
q\geq 2/s
that

h^{\prime}(q)\leq h^{\prime}\left(\frac{2}{s}\right)=\log\left(\frac{s-2}{2(s-1)}\right)\leq\log\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=-1.

So, for
q\geq 2/s
, we have
h(q)\leq h(2/s)-(q-2/s)\leq\log s-(q-2/s)
. Since we know that
h(p)\geq\mathcal{H}(X)=\log s-\ell
, it follows that
p\leq 2/s+\ell
.
∎

We can now prove our main result using the strategy outlined earlier in this section. The first part of the proof closely follows Radhakrishnan’s proof of the Brègman-Minc inequality, with the twist being that we keep track of the total loss in entropy. This is used in the second part to prove an upper bound on the expected number of cycles, by passing through
Lemma

3.3
.

We identify the vertices of
G
with
[n]
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of all permutations
\sigma
of
[n]
such that
(i,\sigma(i))\in E(G)
for all
i\in[n]
. Note that
\mathcal{C}
corresponds to the collection of all cycle-factors of
G
.
Theorem

3.2
in combination with Stirling’s approximation implies that
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\geq n!\cdot d^{n}/n^{n}\geq(d/e)^{n}
.

Pick
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
uniformly at random, so
\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=\mathcal{H}(\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(n))=\log\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
. For every permutation
\tau\in S_{n}
, the chain rule for entropy implies that

\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}(\sigma(\tau(j))\mid\sigma(\tau([j-1]))).

For
i\in[n]
, let
\tau_{<i}\coloneqq\tau([j-1])
where
j=\tau^{-1}(i)
. If we reorder the above sum, we get

\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}(\sigma(i)\mid\sigma(\tau_{<i})).

Expanding the definition of conditional entropy, we get

\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\pi}\mathbb{P}(\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\pi)\cdot\mathcal{H}(\sigma(i)\mid\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\pi),

where
\pi
ranges over all possible values of
\sigma(\tau_{<i})
. Since
\sigma
is chosen uniformly at random, observe that
\mathbb{P}(\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\pi)=\lvert\{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}:\sigma^{\prime}(\tau_{<i})=\pi\}\rvert/\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert
. Therefore,

\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}(\sigma(i)\mid\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\sigma^{\prime}(\tau_{<i})).

For all
i\in[n]
,
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
, and
\tau\in S_{n}
, let
s(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)\coloneqq\lvert N^{+}(i)\setminus\sigma^{\prime}(\tau_{<i})\rvert
be the number of out-neighbours of
i
in
G
that are not contained in
\sigma^{\prime}(\tau_{<i})
. If
\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\sigma^{\prime}(\tau_{<i})
, then
\sigma(i)
must be one of these out-neighbours, and so
\mathcal{H}(\sigma(i)\mid\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\sigma^{\prime}(\tau_{<i}))\leq\log s(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)
. Define
\ell(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)\coloneqq\log s(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)-\mathcal{H}(\sigma(i)\mid\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\sigma^{\prime}(\tau_{<i}))
. Then,

\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}(\log s(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)-\ell(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)).

Since this holds for all
\tau\in S_{n}
, we may pick
\tau\in S_{n}
uniformly at random. This yields

\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}(\log s(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)-\ell(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau))\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau}(\log s(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau))-\mathcal{L}

where

\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau}(\ell(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau))

denotes the loss in entropy compared to a uniform distribution.

For a fixed
i
and
\sigma^{\prime}
, note that
s(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)
is the number of out-neighbours
j\in N^{+}(i)
of
i
in
G
such that
(\sigma^{\prime})^{-1}(j)
does not appear before
i
in
\tau
. Since

is a uniformly random permutation, this number is distributed uniformly at random in
[d]
, and so it follows that

\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{t=1}^{d}\log t-\mathcal{L}=\frac{n}{d}\log(d!)-\mathcal{L}.

Using Stirling’s approximation and recalling that
\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert\geq(d/e)^{n}
, we get

n\log\left(\frac{d}{e}\right)\leq\log\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert=\mathcal{H}(\sigma)=\frac{n}{d}\log(d!)-\mathcal{L}\leq n\log\left(\frac{d}{e}\right)+\frac{n}{d}\log(ed)-\mathcal{L},

and therefore
\mathcal{L}\leq(n\log(ed))/d
.

We now bound the number of cycles
\lvert\sigma\rvert
of
\sigma
using a very similar calculation. For every permutation
\tau\in S_{n}
, we say that
\sigma(i)
closes a cycle if
\tau_{<i}
contains all other vertices that are on the same cycle as

\sigma(i)
to

creates one additional cycle. Note that
\lvert\sigma\rvert
is equal to the number of vertices

for which
\sigma(i)
closes a cycle, and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(\lvert\sigma\rvert)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\sigma(i)\text{ closes a cycle})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\pi}\mathbb{P}(\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\pi)\cdot\mathbb{P}(\sigma(i)\text{ closes a cycle}\mid\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{P}(\sigma(i)\text{ closes a cycle}\mid\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\sigma^{\prime}(\tau_{<i})).

If
\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\sigma^{\prime}(\tau_{<i})
, then either
\sigma(i)
cannot close a cycle, or
\sigma(i)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sigma(i)\text{ closes a cycle}\mid\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\sigma^{\prime}(\tau_{<i}))

\displaystyle\leq\max_{j\in N^{+}(i)}\mathbb{P}(\sigma(i)=j\mid\sigma(\tau_{<i})=\sigma^{\prime}(\tau_{<i}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{s(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)}+\ell(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau).

This implies that

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(\lvert\sigma\rvert)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\left(\frac{2}{s(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)}+\ell(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)\right).

Since this holds for all
\tau\in S_{n}
, we may again pick
\tau\in S_{n}
uniformly at random. As noted before,

is then distributed uniformly at random in
[d]
, and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(\lvert\sigma\rvert)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\left(\frac{2}{s(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)}+\ell(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left(\frac{2}{s(i,\sigma^{\prime},\tau)}\right)+\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{C}\rvert}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{t=1}^{d}\frac{2}{t}+\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\frac{n}{d}(\log d+1)+\mathcal{L}\leq 4\cdot\frac{n}{d}(\log d+1).\qed
