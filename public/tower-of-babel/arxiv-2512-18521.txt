The Euclidean distance degree of curves: from rational to line multiview varieties
Bella Finkel and Jose Israel Rodriguez

Abstract
The Euclidean distance (ED) degree is an invariant that measures the algebraic complexity of optimizing the distance function of a point to a model. It has been studied in algebraic statistics, machine learning, and computer vision. In this article, we prove a formula for the ED degree of curves parameterized by rational functions with mild genericity assumptions. We apply our results to resolve conjectures on one-dimensional line multiview varieties from computer vision proposed by Duff and Rydell.



Introduction

One of the most impactful applications of algebraic geometry is in computer vision.
This has led to the development of what is now known as
algebraic vision
.
A featured problem in this area is to determine the number of critical points of the triangulation and, more generally, the multiview triangulation problem in multiple view geometry
[
13
]
.
This amounts to minimizing the Euclidean distance function to an algebraic set known as the (affine) multiview variety.
Recent work by Duff and Rydell introduced an extensive catalogue of multiview varieties motivated by new vision applications. In turn, this led to questions and conjectures on the Euclidean distance degrees for these models.
In this article, we prove two of these conjectures using a new result on the ED degree of curves in products of projective spaces.

Preliminaries

1.1
Multiprojective varieties and multidegrees

A subvariety of a product of projective spaces is known as a
multiprojective variety
.
Let
Z\subset(\mathbb{P}^{h})^{n}
be such a variety.
Its defining ideal lies in the multigraded ring

\mathbb{K}[\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{n}]\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ where }\hskip 10.00002pt\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=(x_{i,0},\dots,x_{i,h})

and
\mathbb{K}
is a field.
If
Z
is irreducible and of dimension
\dim{Z}
,
then the multidegree of
Z
is the polynomial

\operatorname{Deg}(Z):=\sum_{\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}=\dim{Z}}c_{\alpha}\frac{T_{1}^{h}\cdots T_{n}^{h}}{T^{\alpha}}

in the quotient ring
\mathbb{Z}[T_{1},\dots,T_{n}]/\langle T_{1}^{h+1},\dots,T_{n}^{h+1}\rangle
.
We use the standard multi-index notation here,
so that if
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
then
T^{\alpha}:=T_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots T_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
.
The coefficient
c_{\alpha}
is the number of intersection points of
Z
with a general linear space of the form

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}:=\cap_{i=1}^{n}V(\ell_{i,1},\dots,\ell_{i,\alpha_{i}})

where
\ell_{i,j}
is a general linear form in the unknowns
\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
.

If
Z
is reducible, then the multidegree of
Z
is defined to be the sum of the multidegrees of the irreducible components.
If
Z_{1}
,
Z_{2}
are each equidimensional
and intersect transversally,
then

\operatorname{Deg}(Z_{1}\cap Z_{2})=\operatorname{Deg}(Z_{1})\cdot\operatorname{Deg}(Z_{2}).

Example 1.1
.

Consider the bi-homogeneous polynomials

f_{d}=x_{0}y_{0}^{d}+x_{1}y_{1}^{d}+x_{2}y_{2}^{d}+x_{3}y_{3}^{d},\hskip 10.00002ptd=0,1,2,3.

Each polynomial
f_{d}
defines a hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{3}\times\mathbb{P}^{3}
.
The multidegree of
V(f_{d})
is
T_{1}+dT_{2}.

The multidegree of
V(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})
is

(T_{1}+1T_{2})(T_{1}+2T_{2})(T_{1}+3T_{2})=T_{1}^{3}+6T_{1}^{2}T_{2}+11T_{1}T_{2}^{2}+6T_{2}^{3}

and the multidegree of

V(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})\cap V(f_{0})

is

6T_{1}^{3}T_{2}+11T_{1}^{2}T_{2}^{2}+6T_{1}T_{2}^{3}.

1.2
Multiprojective versus affine multiview varieties

In multiview geometry, the natural setting is multiprojective space. However, optimization problems such as Euclidean distance minimization are typically formulated in an affine chart. We now describe both perspectives to get a multiprojective and an affine multiview variety.

An arrangement
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})
of full rank
3\times 4
matrices, called
cameras
, gives a
multiprojective multiview variety of
\mathbb{P}^{3}

as the Zariski closure of the image of the rational map

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbf{C}}:\mathbb{P}^{3}

\displaystyle\dashrightarrow(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{n}

(1.1)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\mapsto(C_{1}z,\dots,C_{n}z).

As in
Section

1.1
, we take the coordinates of
(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{n}
to be
\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,\overline{\mathbf{x}}_{n}

The multidegree of the image of (
1.1
) is

\sum_{\max\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}=2,\,\,\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}=3}1\cdot{T_{1}^{2-\alpha_{1}}\cdot T_{2}^{2-\alpha_{2}}\cdot\cdots\cdot T_{n}^{2-\alpha_{n}}},\hskip 10.00002ptn>1.

(1.2)

For a textbook introduction to multidegrees see
[
23
, Chapter 8]
. Ideals of multiview varieties are also well studied in algebraic vision, and for more details we highlight
[
1
,
3
,
9
]
.

The
affine multiview variety of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
is obtained by restricting each factor of
(\mathbb{P}^{2})^{n}
to an affine chart.
Our convention is to take the charts given by
x_{i,0}=1
for
i=1,\dots,n
. Under this choice, the affine multiview variety has the following parameterization:

z\mapsto\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle{\frac{C_{1}^{(1)}\cdot z}{C_{1}^{(0)}\cdot z}}&\dots&\displaystyle{\frac{C_{n}^{(1)}\cdot z}{C_{n}^{(0)}\cdot z}}\\
\\
\displaystyle{\frac{C_{1}^{(2)}\cdot z}{C_{1}^{(0)}\cdot z}}&\dots&\displaystyle{\frac{C_{n}^{(2)}\cdot z}{C_{n}^{(0)}\cdot z}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{K}^{2\times n}.

In general, if
Z
is a multiprojective variety
then
we denote its restriction to the conventional affine chart
by
Z_{{\operatorname{Aff}}}\subset(\mathbb{K}^{h})^{n}
.

1.3
Euclidean distance degree

The
Euclidean distance degree
(ED degree) of an algebraic variety measures the algebraic complexity of the problem of minimizing Euclidean distance to that variety.
This
invariant plays a central role in applications such as computer vision, statistics, and optimization. We recall its definition in this subsection.

If
X
is an affine variety in
{{\mathbb{C}}}^{h}
, then we define

X_{\mathbb{R}}:=X\cap\mathbb{R}^{h}

to be the real points of
X
.
We call

u\in\mathbb{R}^{h}
a data point.
The optimization problem

\min_{x\in X_{\mathbb{R}}}\|x-u\|^{2}

has first-order optimality conditions.
Namely, for a smooth point
x\in X_{\mathbb{R}}
, we require

(x-u)^{T}v=0
for all
v
in the tangent space
T_{x}X_{\mathbb{R}}
of
X_{\mathbb{R}}
at
x
.
This condition gives rise to a polynomial system
[
6
, Equation 2.1]
that has
x
as the unknown and
u
as the given generic data.
The number of complex (real or nonreal) solutions to this polynomial system is called the Euclidean distance degree of
X_{\mathbb{R}}

[
6
]
.
The ED degree is a measure of complexity for minimizing the Euclidean distance from
X_{\mathbb{R}}
to
u
.

Example 1.2
.

Let
X
denote the variety of the ideal generated by

g_{1}=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-1,\hskip 10.00002ptg_{2}=x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4}+ax^{2}y^{2}+by^{2}z^{2}+cz^{2}x^{2}-s\left(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}\right)^{2}

where
(a,b,c,s)=(-0.8,-0.74,-0.5,0.32)
.
Then, for a generic data point
u
, the ideal of the critical points is generated by

g_{1},g_{2}
and the determinant of

\begin{bmatrix}\nabla d_{u}&\nabla g_{1}&\nabla g_{2}\end{bmatrix}

where
d_{u}
is the squared Euclidean distance function from the data
u
and
\nabla g
denotes the
3\times 1
matrix of partial derivatives of
g
.
In
Figure

2
,
the silver curve is the restriction of the variety of the determinant to the sphere and the real ED critical points are the intersection points of the silver and red curves.

Note, when
X
is singular an additional step of saturation needs to be performed to obtain the ideal of critical points.

\diamond

Figure 2
:

The red curve is
X_{\mathbb{R}}
and the real ED critical points for the distance function to the orange points is the set of intersection points with the silver curve.

Beyond its algebraic definition, the Euclidean distance degree also has a topological interpretation. For more details, we refer to the survey article
[
22
]
.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
20
]
)
.

Suppose
X
is an irreducible closed subvariety of
\mathbb{C}^{h}
. Then for general
\boldsymbol{\beta}:=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{h})\in\mathbb{C}^{h+1}
we have

\text{EDDeg}(X)=(-1)^{\dim X}\chi(\operatorname{Eu}_{X}|_{X\cap U_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})

where

\operatorname{Eu}_{X}
is the local Euler obstruction function on
X
and

U_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\mathbb{C}^{h}\setminus\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{h})\in\mathbb{C}^{h}:\sum_{i=1}^{h}(z_{i}-\beta_{i})^{2}+\beta_{0}=0\}.

For many of our purposes,
X
is smooth and it suffices to consider the
Euler-Poincare characteristic
\chi
.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
20
]
)
.

Suppose
X
is a smooth closed subvariety of
\mathbb{C}^{h}
.
Then for general
\boldsymbol{\beta}:=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{h})\in\mathbb{C}^{h+1}
we have

\text{EDDeg}(X)=(-1)^{\dim X}\chi(X\cap U_{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

where
U_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\mathbb{C}^{h}\setminus\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{h})\in\mathbb{C}^{h}:\sum_{i=1}^{h}(z_{i}-\beta_{i})^{2}+\beta_{0}=0\}
.

In
Section

2
we will apply these results to compute the ED degree of one dimensional multiview varieties.

1.4
The projective ED degree

The
projective ED degree
provides a natural way to study ED degrees of affine cones by avoiding the singularity at the cone point and enabling the use of intersection-theoretic tools.
In this subsection, we recall this invariant and prior work for curves.

Let
Z\subset\mathbb{P}^{h}
be an irreducible closed subvariety of codimension
c
and let
I_{Z}
be its defining ideal.
If the homogeneous polynomials
g_{1},\dots,g_{s}\in\mathbb{C}[z_{0},z_{1},\dots,z_{h}]
generate
I_{Z}
, then the
projective Euclidean distance degree correspondence
is a biprojective variety in
\mathbb{P}^{h}\times\mathbb{P}^{h}
defined by the saturated ideal

\biggl(I_{Z}+\biggl\langle\hbox{$(c+2)\times(c+2)$-minors of $\begin{pmatrix}u\\
z\\
J(g)\end{pmatrix}$}\biggr\rangle\biggr):\bigl(I_{Z_{\rm sing}}\cdot\langle z_{0}^{2}+\cdots+z_{h}^{2}\rangle\,\bigr)^{\infty}

(1.3)

where

J(g)
is the
s\times(h+1)
matrix of partial derivatives of
g
and
the singular
locus
Z_{\operatorname{sing}}
of
Z
is given by the ideal

I_{Z_{\operatorname{sing}}}\,\,=\,\,I_{Z}+\bigl\langle\hbox{$(c\times c)$-minors of $J(g)$}\bigr\rangle.

The
ED bidegree
is defined to be multidegree of the ED degree correspondence.
The coefficient of
T_{2}^{h}
is the
(projective) ED degree of
X
, and is denoted by
\PEDdeg(X)
.

The other coefficients of the multidegree are known as the sectional ED degree, and the degree of
X
is the coefficient of

T_{1}^{c}T_{2}^{h-c}
.

The affine cone over
Z
is

\{(z_{0},\dots,z_{h})\in\mathbb{C}^{h+1}:[z_{0}:\dots:z_{h}]\in Z\}.

The ED degree of this affine cone agrees with
\PEDdeg(Z)

[
6
]
. The advantage of working projectively is to avoid the singularity at the cone point. It also allows the use of several topological formulas.

Theorem 1.5

(Theorem 1.3
[
21
]
)
.

Let
Z
be any irreducible closed subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{h}
. Then for a general
{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}=(\beta_{0},\ldots,\beta_{h})\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{{h+1}}\setminus\{0\}
we have

\PEDdeg(Z)=(-1)^{\dim X}\chi\big({\rm Eu}_{Z}|_{\mathcal{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}\big),

where

\mathcal{U}_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}:=\mathbb{P}^{{h}}\setminus(Q\cup H_{{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}})

with
Q
denoting the
quadric
\{[z_{0}:\dots:z_{h}]\in\mathbb{P}^{{h}}:\sum_{i=0}^{h}z_{i}^{2}=0\}
and

H_{{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}:=\{[z_{0}:\dots:z_{h}]\in\mathbb{P}^{{h}}:\beta_{0}x_{0}+\ldots+\beta_{{h}}x_{h}=0\}.

When
Z\subset\mathbb{P}^{h}
is smooth, the formula simplifies.

Theorem 1.6
.

[
2
, Theorem 8.1]

Let
Z
be a smooth subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{h}
, and assume that
Z\nsubseteq Q
, where

Q=\{[z_{0}:\ldots:z_{h}]\in\mathbb{P}^{h}\mid z_{0}^{2}+\ldots+z_{h}^{2}=0\}
is the
quadric in
\mathbb{P}^{h}
.
Then

\PEDdeg(Z)=(-1)^{\dim Z}\chi\big(Z\setminus(Q\cup H)\big),

where
H
is a general hyperplane.

As noted in
[
2
, Section 9]
, an immediate application of the previous theorems gives a formula for the ED degree of a smooth curve
Z
in
\mathbb{P}^{h}
of degree
d
:

\PEDdeg(Z)=d+\#(Q\cap Z)-\chi(Z).

(1.4)

The next examples illustrate some of the discrepancies that occur between an affine variety’s ED degree (which is our setting) and the ED degree of related projective varieties.

Example 1.7

(Standard grading giving discrepancy)
.

Consider the curve
Z
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}
parameterized by

\mathbb{P}^{1}\to Z\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{2}

[s:t]\mapsto\left(\begin{bmatrix}s^{3}+2t^{3}\\
s^{2}t+3t^{3}\\
st^{2}+5t^{3}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}s^{3}+7t^{3}\\
s^{2}t+11t^{3}\\
st^{2}+13t^{3}\end{bmatrix}\right).

The affine variety
Z_{\operatorname{Aff}}\subset\mathbb{K}^{4}
has ED degree 10. On the other hand, we can regard the ideal of
Z
as a homogeneous ideal in the usual
\mathbb{Z}
-grading. This defines a projective surface in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
that has ED degree 14.

\diamond

(Projective closure discrepancy)
.

The affine
n
-view variety discussed in
Section

1.2

is known

[

]

to have ED degree

\frac{9}{2}n^{3}-\frac{21}{2}n^{2}+8n-4.

However, by
[

1.5
More multiview varieties and triangulation

In this subsection, we define what are known as
anchored point multiview varieties
.
To stay organized and minimize notation, we focus on the camera arrangement and the image it induces.

Definition 1.9
.

Let
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})
be a

, where each
C_{i}
is a full-rank
(h+1)\times(N+1)
matrix.
Define the rational map

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbf{C}}:\mathbb{P}^{N}\dashrightarrow(\mathbb{P}^{h})^{n},\hskip 10.00002ptX\mapsto(C_{1}X,\dots,C_{n}X).

The
(point) multiview variety of

with respect to

is

where the overline denotes the Zariski closure.
If

is a subvariety,
we define

\mathbf{C}\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}Y:=\overline{\Phi_{\mathbf{C}}(Y)}

and call this the

with respect to
\mathbf{C}
.
We denote its restriction to the standard affine chart by

.

When

and

, the map

models the procedure of taking

pictures with

possibly distinct cameras. A point

is mapped to the tuple of images

provided that

is not in the kernel of any

. Points

that lie in the kernel of some camera are referred to as being at a

, where

.

1.6
A Schubert variety in
\operatorname{Gr}(1,\mathbb{P}^{3})

In this subsection, we define a subvariety of the Grassmannian that will induce the multiprojective varieties appearing in our results in
Section

3
.
Let
\operatorname{Gr}(1,\mathbb{P}^{3})
denote the Grassmannian of lines in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Given three skew lines
{\ell^{(1)}},{\ell^{(2)}},{\ell^{(3)}}
we define

{\texttt{L}^{\texttt{3}}}({\ell^{(1)}},{\ell^{(2)}},{\ell^{(3)}}):=\{{\ell}\in\operatorname{Gr}(1,\mathbb{P}^{3}):{\ell}\cap{\ell^{(i)}}\neq\emptyset,\,i=1,2,3\}.

(1.7)

This is an example of a Schubert variety
[
15
]
. We often abbreviate
{\texttt{L}^{\texttt{3}}}({\ell^{(1)}},{\ell^{(2)}},{\ell^{(3)}})

by
{\texttt{L}^{\texttt{3}}}
.
To understand the geometry of
{\texttt{L}^{\texttt{3}}}
, we recall two classical results about lines and quadrics in
\mathbb{P}^{3}.

Theorem 1.11
.

There is a unique smooth quadric
S
that contains any three given pairwise disjoint lines in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
.

A consequence of
Theorem

1.11
is that any line
{\ell}\in\operatorname{Gr}(1,\mathbb{P}^{3})
satisfying the incidence condition (
1.7
) must intersect the quadric at three points and is therefore contained in it.
Thus,

{\texttt{L}^{\texttt{3}}}

lies within the family of lines contained in the quadric.
The next theorem characterizes the lines contained in a quadric.

Theorem 1.12
.

A smooth quadric
S
in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
contains two rulings.
Moreover,
any two lines from different
rulings
intersect in exactly one point, and
any two lines from the same
ruling
are disjoint.

Theorem

1.12

implies that the three skew lines in our incidence condition are in the same ruling. All together, these theorems imply that
{\texttt{L}^{\texttt{3}}}
is the other ruling.

The Plücker embedding

\iota:\operatorname{Gr}(1,\mathbb{P}^{3})\to\mathbb{P}^{5},

provides us a way to embed
{\texttt{L}^{\texttt{3}}}\subset\operatorname{Gr}(1,\mathbb{P}^{3})
into
\mathbb{P}^{5}
.
It turns out
\iota({\texttt{L}^{\texttt{3}}})
is a conic curve in
\mathbb{P}^{5}
, and the next example illustrates how to parameterize it.

(Parameterizing
{\texttt{L}^{\texttt{3}}}
)
.

In this example, we describe the parametrization of

In this section,

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume we are given a generic camera arrangement
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})
of full rank
(h+1)\times(N+1)
matrices
C_{i}
with

N\geq 3
and

h\geq 2
.
Let

be as in (
2.1
) so that

is degree
e
and
Y
is smooth or has only nodal singularities.
Then the ED degree of an affine patch
(\mathbf{C}\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}f(\mathbb{P}^{1}))_{\operatorname{Aff}}
of
\mathbf{C}\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}f(\mathbb{P}^{1})
is

\operatorname{affEDdeg}(\mathbf{C}\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}f(\mathbb{P}^{1}))=3en-2.

(2.2)

Proof.

Recall from
Section

1.5

that a camera

is a full rank
(h+1)\times(N+1)
matrix. We
write

for the

th row of

.
The subvariety

has the parameterization

\Phi_{\mathbf{C}}\circ f([s:t])=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}C_{1}^{(0)}\cdot f\\
C_{1}^{(1)}\cdot f\\
\vdots\\
C_{1}^{(h)}\cdot f\end{pmatrix}:\dots:\begin{pmatrix}C_{n}^{(0)}\cdot f\\
C_{n}^{(1)}\cdot f\\
\vdots\\
C_{n}^{(h)}\cdot f\end{pmatrix}\end{bmatrix}\in(\mathbb{P}^{h})^{n}.

(2.3)

Let
X_{\operatorname{Aff}}:=(\mathbf{C}\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}Y)_{\operatorname{Aff}}
denote the restriction of
\operatorname{im}\Phi_{\mathbf{C}}
to an affine patch with coordinates

\begin{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{C_{1}^{(1)}\cdot f}{C_{1}^{(0)}\cdot f}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{C_{1}^{(h)}\cdot f}{C_{1}^{(0)}\cdot f}\end{pmatrix},\dots,\begin{pmatrix}\frac{C_{n}^{(1)}\cdot f}{C_{n}^{(0)}\cdot f}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{C_{n}^{(h)}\cdot f}{C_{n}^{(0)}\cdot f}\end{pmatrix}\end{pmatrix}\in(\mathbb{K}^{h})^{n}.

(2.4)

In

, each factor of

gives a hyperplane at infinity. We denote the union of these hyperplanes as

. If the

th factor in
\mathbb{P}^{h}
has coordinates

then

Using the parameterization of
X
in (
2.3
),
we have

X\cap H_{\infty}=\Phi_{\mathbf{C}}\circ\phi(S_{\infty})\text{\hskip 10.00002ptwhere\hskip 10.00002pt}S_{\infty}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}V(C_{i}^{(0)}\cdot f)\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}.

If each
C_{i}^{(0)}\cdot f
has distinct roots and

(2.5)

for all
i,j\in[n],\ i\neq j
,
then

has
ne
points.
This holds by genericity of the camera arrangement: the degree

polynomial

has a multiple root if and only if its
X
-discriminant vanishes, and

share a root if and only if their resultant is nonvanishing
[
11
, Section 1.1]
.

Therefore, if

is a generically one-to-one map then

H_{\infty}\cap X
has precisely
ne
points.

For

\beta=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1,1},\dots,\beta_{n,h})\in\mathbb{R}^{nh+1}
, define
the hypersurface

Q_{\beta}:=\left\{(z_{1,1},\dots,z_{n,h})\in\mathbb{C}^{nh}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{h}(z_{i,j}-\beta_{i,j})^{2}+\beta_{0}=0\right\}

(2.6)

and the Zariski open set

U_{\beta}:=\mathbb{C}^{hn}\setminus Q_{\beta}.

(2.7)

Let
S_{Q}
be the set of points in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus S_{\infty}

that are the zeros of

\displaystyle d_{\beta}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{h}\left(\frac{C_{i}^{(j)}\cdot f}{C_{i}^{(0)}\cdot f}-\beta_{i,j}\right)^{2}+\beta_{0}.

By construction, we have
(\Phi_{\mathbf{C}}\circ\phi(S_{Q}))_{\operatorname{Aff}}=X_{\operatorname{Aff}}\cap Q_{\beta}
. We may write
d_{\beta}
as a quotient of polynomials

d_{\beta}=\frac{g_{\beta}}{D}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ with }\hskip 10.00002ptD:={\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(C_{i}^{(0)}\cdot f\right)^{2}}

where

g_{\beta}=\beta_{0}D+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{D}{\left(C_{i}^{(0)}\cdot f\right)^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{h}\left({(C_{i}^{(1)}\cdot f)}-\beta_{i,j}{(C_{i}^{(0)}\cdot f})\right)^{2}

is a homogeneous degree
2ne
polynomial in
s,t
. Because
\beta_{0}
is generic,
if

has no common roots with

then

has
2ne
distinct roots.
On the other hand, if

had a common root with

, then there would exist a unique

such that

vanishes on that root.
This implies the polynomial

vanishes at the root. By the genericity of

The degree of

equals

It can be obtained by counting the degree of, for instance, the first term as we clear denominators. Multiplying through by

gives a leading monomial of degree

, to which multiplication with subsequent

.

If

is smooth,

, Theorem 3.8]

implies that

\operatorname{affEDdeg}(X)=-(\chi(X)-\chi(X\cap H_{\infty})-\chi(X\cap Q_{\beta})).

(2.8)

Hence

\operatorname{affEDdeg}(X)=-(\chi(\Phi_{\mathbf{C}}\circ\phi(\mathbb{P}^{1}))-\chi(\Phi_{\mathbf{C}}\circ\phi(S_{\infty}))-\chi(\Phi_{\mathbf{C}}\circ\phi(S_{Q})))
,
and therefore

\operatorname{affEDdeg}(X)=(-1)(2-en-2en)=3en-2.

(2.9)

If

is singular then
we use

1.3
, which implies
for general

that

\operatorname{affEDdeg}(X)
equals

-\left(\chi(X_{{\operatorname{reg}}})-\chi(X_{{\operatorname{reg}}}\cap H_{\infty})-\chi((X_{\operatorname{reg}})_{\operatorname{Aff}}\cap Q_{\beta})+\sum_{x\in\text{Sing}(X_{\operatorname{Aff}})\setminus Q_{\beta}}m_{x}\right)

Similar arguments as in the smooth case show

and

as a consequence of the genericity of

.
Also by the genericity of

we have

Substituting in the known quantities, we find

Since each point in the singular locus of

Plugging into (

), we get the desired formula.

By
Section

2
, the result follows.
∎

3.2
Exterior algebra review and wedge cameras

Next, we show how to realize

To that end, we review the exterior algebra
(see
[

for a textbook reference)
to state

3.2

Recall the
exterior algebra
of a vector space
V
over a field
\mathbb{K}
is the graded algebra

\bigwedge V:=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\dim V}\bigwedge^{k}V,

where
\bigwedge^{k}V
is the space of alternating
k
-linear forms on
V
.
Elements of
\bigwedge^{k}V
are called
k
-vectors
, and the wedge product satisfies

u\wedge v=-v\wedge u
and
v\wedge v=0
.
A
simple wedge
is one of the form

v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}
,
v_{i}\in V.

Every element of
\bigwedge^{k}V
is a linear combination of simple wedges.
The dimension of
\bigwedge^{k}V
is
\binom{\dim V}{k}
, and a basis is given by

e_{i_{1}}\wedge e_{i_{2}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_{k}},\hskip 10.00002pti_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}

where
\{e_{i}\}
is a basis for
V

Any linear map
f:V\to W
induces the map

\bigwedge^{k}f:\,\bigwedge^{k}V\longrightarrow\bigwedge^{k}W,\hskip 20.00003pt\bigwedge^{k}f(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k})=f(v_{1})\wedge\cdots\wedge f(v_{k}).

(3.4)

In our vision setting, each camera matrix
C
is a linear map

f_{C}:\mathbb{K}^{N+1}\to\mathbb{K}^{h+1}.

After fixing ordered bases of
\mathbb{K}^{N+1}
and
\mathbb{K}^{h+1}
, we obtain induced wedge bases on

\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{K}^{N+1}
and

\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{K}^{h+1}
.
The
wedge camera matrix
{\wedge^{k}C}
represents the linear map

\bigwedge^{k}f_{C}:\;\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{K}^{N+1}\to\bigwedge^{k}\mathbb{K}^{h+1},\text{ where }v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}\mapsto f_{C}(v_{1})\wedge\cdots\wedge f_{C}(v_{k}).

The entries of
{\wedge^{k}C}
consist of all
k\times k
minors of
C
, arranged according to the chosen multi-index ordering.

Definition 3.4
.

If
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})
is an arrangement of
n
cameras, then we call

\left({\wedge^{k+1}C_{1}},\dots,{\wedge^{k+1}C_{n}}\right)

(k+1)
-wedge camera arrangement of
\mathbf{C}
.

Remark 3.9

(Rational scrolls as curves in
\operatorname{Gr}(1,\mathbb{P}^{N})
)
.

The ideas used in the proof above can be put into a broader framework.
In this remark, we provide an additional example to motivate the study of ED degrees of subvarieties of the Grassmannian.

A
Bezier curve
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
of degree

E
is defined by a set of control points
P_{0},\dots,P_{E}\in\mathbb{K}^{N}
.
The curve is given parametrically by

\mathbf{B}(s,t)=\sum_{i=0}^{E}B_{i,E}(s,t)\cdot{P}_{i},\hskip 10.00002pts=1,\,t\in[0,1]

where
B_{i,E}(s,t)
are the homogenized Bernstein polynomials

B_{i,E}(s,t)=\binom{E}{i}(s-t)^{E-i}t^{i}.

This parameterization has the advantage that the curve segment
\{\mathbf{B}(1,t):t\in[0:1]\}
on the standard affine chart in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is in the convex hull of the control points, starts at
P_{0}
, and ends at
P_{E}
.
It follows from
Theorem

2.3

that if the control points are in generic position, then

\operatorname{affEDdeg}(\mathbf{C}\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}\mathbf{B}(s,t))=En-2

for a generic camera configuration
\mathbf{C}
.

Suppose now we have a pair of Bezier curves
\mathbf{B}_{i}(1,t)
,
i=1,2
, in
\mathbb{K}^{3}
with degree
E_{i}
. We obtain a one-dimensional family of lines by considering the line spanned by

\mathbf{B}_{1}(1,t)
and
\mathbf{B}_{2}(1,t)
for
t\in[0,1]
. This is visualized in
Figure

5
.
Again we assume the control points are in generic position.
The Zariski closure of the family of lines is a subvariety of
\operatorname{Gr}(1,\mathbb{P}^{3})
,
and we denote it as
\Lambda_{\mathbf{B}_{1},\mathbf{B}_{2}}
.
The Plücker embedding of this variety is given by the two by two minors of the
2\times 4
matrix

\begin{bmatrix}s^{E_{1}}&\mathbf{B}_{1}(s,t)\\
s^{E_{2}}&\mathbf{B}_{2}(s,t)\end{bmatrix}.

This gives a degree
E_{1}+E_{2}
polynomial map from
\mathbb{P}^{1}
to
\mathbb{P}^{5}
, and
the results of
Theorem

2.3
imply

\operatorname{affEDdeg}(\mathbf{C}\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\square$}}}\Lambda_{\mathbf{B}_{1},\mathbf{B}_{2}})\,=\,3(E_{1}+E_{2})n-2

where
\mathbf{C}
is an arrangement of
n
cameras of size
3\times 4
.

4
Conclusion and future directions

In this article, we considered one-dimensional multiview varieties. We used topological arguments to give the first theoretical results for ED degrees of multiview varieties anchored at a Schubert variety. One of the key features of our proof is that each genericity condition is the complement of a union of hypersurfaces whose defining equations only rely on a pair of cameras or a single camera. This resulted in a surprising
Section

2
to determine the ED degrees of multiview varieties where cameras have a prescribed structure. This is relevant for families of “dual cameras” found in resectioning
[
4
]
as well as calibrated
cameras
[
14
,
19
]
used in practice.
We leave for future work the task of of generalizing these ideas to higher dimensional multiview varieties. This becomes more difficult because the camera centers may intersect the world variety. We also leave for future work the problem of determining the ED degree of line multiview varieties using a different embedding into the Grassmannian
[
16
,
17
,
5
]
.
