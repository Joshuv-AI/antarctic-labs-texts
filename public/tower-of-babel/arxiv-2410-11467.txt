On L ∞ L^{\infty} stability for wave propagation and for linear inverse problems
Rima Alaifari, Giovanni S. Alberti, Tandri Gauksson

Abstract
Stability is a key property of both forward models and inverse problems, and depends on the norms considered in the relevant function spaces. For instance, stability estimates for hyperbolic partial differential equations are often based on energy conservation principles, and are therefore expressed in terms of L 2 L^{2} norms. The focus of this paper is on stability with respect to the L ∞ L^{\infty} norm, which is more relevant to detect localized phenomena. The linear wave equation is not stable in L ∞ L^{\infty} , and we design an alternative solution method based on the regularization of Fourier multipliers, which is stable in L ∞ L^{\infty} . Furthermore, we show how these ideas can be extended to inverse problems, and design a regularization method for the inversion of compact operators that is stable in L ∞ L^{\infty} . We also discuss the connection with the stability of deep neural networks modeled by hyperbolic PDEs.



1
Introduction

One of the key mathematical questions regarding the study of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and of partial differential equations (PDEs) is the continuous dependence of the solutions on the initial data. This property guarantees that the solution to a certain ODE or PDE is stable with respect to small perturbations of the initial data. The absence of this stability is at the core of chaos theory, which studies those dynamical systems that are highly sensitive to the initial conditions: a very small change can lead to a very different outcome. However, many physical models do satisfy this stability property.
As simple examples, we can consider the linear heat or wave equations, which are second-order PDE: for these problems, such continuous dependence holds, and because of the linearity, it can be expressed by the boundedness of the linear operator

initial conditions
\;\longmapsto\;
solution.

As a consequence, we have Lipschitz stability of the solutions with respect to the initial conditions.

Let us focus on hyperbolic equations. One of the key tools employed to derive stability estimates is the energy conservation principle
[
7
,
10
]
. As a result, these estimates are written in terms of
L^{2}
-based norms.
In order to treat general
L^{p}
norms, different methods are needed. For example, in the case of the linear wave equation with constant coefficients, the stability estimates correspond to the boundedness of Fourier multipliers between suitable
L^{p}
spaces
[
21
,
24
]
. In addition to the theoretical motivation, considering different norms may be relevant in the applications, as we will discuss below. In particular, the
L^{\infty}
norm quantifies features (like localized spikes) that may be qualitatively different from those measured by the
L^{2}
norm (see e.g. Figs.
1
and
2
below).

In this work, we consider the linear wave equation with constant coefficient as a prototypical example. Stability in
L^{\infty}
does not hold: if we consider the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
norm, a small perturbation of the initial condition
u_{0}
can lead to arbitrarily large deviations at time
t=1
. In order to overcome this issue, we introduce a regularized solution of the PDE. This is constructed by means of a double filter (in space and in frequency) of the corresponding Fourier multiplier. Intuitively, and in simple terms, this gives rise to a family of regularized solution operators
B_{\alpha,\beta}
that are bounded in
L^{\infty}
and that approximate, as
\alpha,\beta\to 0
, the true solution
u
, namely,
B_{\alpha,\beta}u_{0}\to u
in
L^{\infty}
.

Further, we extend these ideas to inverse problems, in which a similar issue arises:
classical regularization techniques give rise to reconstruction algorithms that are bounded in the relevant Hilbert space norms, such as
L^{2}
, but may be unbounded with respect to other norms, such as
L^{\infty}
. Regularization methods in Banach spaces have been studied extensively, but mostly with a focus on the case
p=1
, promoting sparsity (see
[
23
]
and the references therein). In this paper, we take inspiration from the approach developed for the wave equation and design a regularization method for inverse problems that is stable in
L^{\infty}
. This is achieved by considering a nonstandard spectral filtering of the singular value decomposition.

The results of this paper are relevant for the study of the stability of deep neural networks (DNNs). The instability of DNNs is linked to the existence of
adversarial examples
, first observed in the context of image recognition with deep neural networks
[
4
,
25
,
13
,
20
,
19
,
5
]
.
These are images that have been perturbed intentionally and imperceptibly so that the network makes an incorrect prediction.
More recently, and naturally, adversarial examples have also found their way to the domain of deep learning for inverse problems
[
3
,
11
,
14
]
.
While these perturbations are usually quantified in terms of the
L^{2}
norm, several works have studied perturbations measured in different ways
[
27
,
1
,
2
]
, which may be qualitatively more relevant for human perception.

When both the layers and the spatial variable are seen as continuous, the action of a residual convolutional neural network (CNN) can be seen as the input-to-solution map associated to a nonlinear time-dependent PDE
[
22
]
. In particular, hyperbolic CNNs were introduced by using hyperbolic PDEs with the aim of obtaining an energy preserving propagation and, consequently, a stable network with respect to the
L^{2}
norm. The derivations in this paper show, for a simpler linear problem, that direct extensions to the
L^{\infty}
norm, which is more adapted to localized perturbations, are not possible. Furthermore, the proposed regularized solution offers a possible way to stabilize the network. The extension to more complicated nonlinear PDEs is needed to fully capture the nonlinearity of DNNs, but cannot be treated with the current methods; it is an interesting avenue for future research.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we expand upon the connections to the stability of deep neural networks. In Section
3
we introduce the notion of adversarial example for linear operators, focusing on the role played by the different norms involved. In Section
4
we consider, as motivating example, the linear wave equation, and propose a method for the
L^{\infty}
regularization of Fourier multipliers. In Section
5
, we adapt these ideas for the regularization of inverse problems. Finally, some proofs are collected in
A
.

2
Connections to the stability of deep neural networks

We begin by briefly discussing the link between feed-forward deep neural networks (DNN) and hyperbolic equations, and why the results of this paper can provide interesting insights for the study of the stability of DNNs.

Let us start by introducing DNNs; the reader is referred to
[
12
,
17
]
for additional details on the basics of DNNs. A DNN is a function
f_{\theta}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{N}}
that can be written as a composition of
N
layers:

f_{\theta}(u_{0})=u_{N},

where

u_{j}=f_{\theta_{j}}(u_{j-1}),\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

(1)

Here,
f_{\theta_{j}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n_{j-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}
describes the action of the
j
-th layer, and is a map parametrized by
\theta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}}
. We collect all the weights
\theta_{j}
into a single vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, which is typically learned during training. The most common choice for
f_{\theta_{j}}
is

f_{\theta_{j}}(s)=\sigma(W_{j}s+b_{j}),

where
W_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}\times n_{j-1}}
,
b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}
is a bias term, and
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a fixed nonlinearity, such as the rectified linear unit
\sigma(y)=\max(y,0)
, acting pointwise. In the case when no additional structure is imposed on the weights, the free parameters are
\theta_{j}=(W_{j},b_{j})
, and the network is called fully connected, because every node of the output is linked to every node in the input through the linear map
W_{j}
. It is also possible to restrict the choice of the maps
W_{j}
: for example, considering convolutions allows for a reduction in the number of free parameters, and yields translation invariant (often called equivariant) layers.
An alternative to (
1
) was proposed in
[
16
]
in order to learn a perturbation of the identity only, namely

u_{j}=u_{j-1}+f_{\theta_{j}}(u_{j-1}),\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

(2)

Here, for simplicity we consider only the case when all the dimensions
n_{j}
coincide, and
f_{\theta},f_{\theta_{j}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This choice gives rise to the so-called residual neural networks (ResNets).

It is possible to view the discrete variable
j
parametrizing the layers of a ResNet as a discretization of the continuous time of a dynamical system
[
8
,
15
,
6
]
. More precisely, (
2
) can be seen as the forward Euler step (with step size
1
) of the Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}u(t)=f_{\theta_{t}}(u(t)),\qquad t\in(0,T],\\
u(0)=u_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(3)

The output of the network is given by the solution at time
t=T
, namely

f_{\theta}(u_{0})=u(T)
. Furthermore, we can view the vectors
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as discretizations of functions
u
of two or three variables, modeling 2D and 3D signals, respectively. In this continuous settings, convolutions with filters with small support become differential operators
[
22
]
. As a consequence, the maps
f_{\theta_{t}}
can be taken as certain, possibly nonlinear, differential operators, acting on the function
u
, and (
3
) becomes an initial value problem for a PDE, called parabolic CNN in
[
22
]
. The simplest example in this setting is associated to
f_{\theta_{t}}u=\Delta u
, giving rise to the (linear) heat equation. Considering time/spatial-dependent, possibly nonlinear, differential operators with learnable parameters gives rise to more complicated evolutions.

Heat-type equations tend to dissipate energy. For energy preservation, it is natural to consider hyperbolic CNNs, which take the form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial^{2}_{t}u(t)=f_{\theta_{t}}(u(t)),\qquad t\in(0,T],\\
u(0)=u_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(4)

As above, the trivial choice
f_{\theta_{t}}u=\Delta u
corresponds to the (linear) wave equation with constant coefficients, and other choices give rise to more complicated, possibly nonlinear, hyperbolic PDEs.

The theoretical results of
[
22
]
show that both PDEs (
3
) and (
4
) have Lipschitz stability properties, in the sense that

\|u^{(1)}(T)-u^{(2)}(T)\|_{2}\leq C\|u^{(1)}_{0}-u^{(2)}_{0}\|_{2}

for some
C>0
.
These estimates are useful in the context of stability of DNNs and, especially, for studying robustness against adversarial attacks, as discussed in Section
1
. Indeed, they guarantee that the output of a neural network depends continuously (in fact, in a Lipschitz way) on the input, and they quantify this dependence by the constant
C
. However, as we argue in Section
1
, the
L^{2}
norm may not capture all the relevant deformations of a signal. For instance, the
L^{\infty}
norm is more suited to capture localized perturbations. The results of this paper, and especially those presented in Section
4
address this issue in the very simple setting of the linear wave equation: stability in
L^{\infty}
does not hold in general, and a filtering approach is introduced to overcome this issue. Our approach does not directly apply to more complicated nonlinear settings, which are interesting for applications to neural networks, but can serve as an initial investigation on this issue.

3
Adversarial sequences for linear operators

The central theme of this work is that a bounded linear operator between Banach spaces may not be bounded with respect to other norms that are of interest.
Vectors in the domain of the operator that reveal this behaviour will be termed
adversarial
.
In this section, we define this term formally in an abstract setting.

A
Banach couple
is a pair of Banach spaces such that each can be continuously embedded in a common Hausdorff topological vector space.
The archetypal Banach couples are pairs of
L^{p}
-spaces, which are all continuously embedded in the space of (equivalence classes of) Lebesgue-measurable functions with the topology of local convergence in measure
[
18
]
.
With an abuse of notation, we interpret all set operations on Banach couples as operations in the ambient topological vector space under the continuous embeddings.

Definition 3.1

(Adversarial perturbations and sequences)
.

Let
(X,X^{\prime})
and
(Y,Y^{\prime})
be two Banach couples, and let
A\colon X\to Y
be a bounded linear operator.
Let
r\in X\cap X^{\prime}
and let
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
X\cap X^{\prime}
.

(i)

If
Ar\notin Y^{\prime}
, i.e.,
\left\lVert Ar\right\rVert_{Y^{\prime}}=\infty
, then we say that
r
is an
adversarial perturbation
for
A
(relative to
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
).

(ii)

If
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in both
X
and
X^{\prime}
, and
(Ar_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is unbounded in
Y^{\prime}
,
then we say that
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an
adversarial sequence
for
A
(relative to
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
).

Example 3.2
.

Take
X=X^{\prime}=Y=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
Y^{\prime}=L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, and let
A
be
the Fourier transform
A=\mathcal{F}\colon X\to Y
.
Any square integrable function with an unbounded Fourier transform is an adversarial perturbation for
\mathcal{F}
.
Define the functions

\rho_{n}=n\mathbbm{1}_{[0,n^{-2}]}

and
r_{n}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\rho_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then
\left\lVert r_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\left\lVert\rho_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=1
and
\left\lVert\rho_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an adversarial sequence.

Although adversarial perturbations exhibit a more dramatic effect, it can be convenient to construct adversarial sequences instead.
The following consequence of the closed graph theorem shows that existence of the latter implies existence of the former.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(X,X^{\prime})
and
(Y,Y^{\prime})
be two Banach couples
and let
A\colon X\to Y
be a bounded linear operator.
If there exists an adversarial sequence for
A
(relative to
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
),
then there exists an adversarial perturbation for

A
(relative to
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}
).

Proof.

See
A
.
∎

Example 3.4
.

Theorem
3.3

does not hold if we lift the requirement in Definition
3.1

that adversarial sequences are bounded in the domain of the operator.
Take

X=L^{1}(\mathbb{R}),\quad X^{\prime}=L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),\quad Y=Y^{\prime}=\mathbb{R},

and let
A
be the integration operator

A\colon X\to Y,\ f\mapsto\int f.

Then
A
is bounded, as
\left\lVert Af\right\rVert_{Y}=\left\lvert\int f\right\rvert\leq\int\left\lvert f\right\rvert=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{X}
,
and
it is not bounded with respect to
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{\prime}}
on the intersection
X\cap X^{\prime}

since the functions
\mathbbm{1}_{[0,n]}\in X\cap X^{\prime}
satisfy

\lVert\mathbbm{1}_{[0,n]}\rVert_{X^{\prime}}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert A\mathbbm{1}_{[0,n]}\rVert_{Y^{\prime}}=\left\lvert\int\mathbbm{1}_{[0,n]}\right\rvert=n

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
However, no adversarial perturbation exists for
A
since
\left\lvert Ar\right\rvert<\infty
for all
r\in X\cap X^{\prime}
.
This example does not violate Theorem
3.3
because the sequence
(\mathbbm{1}_{[0,n]})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not bounded in
X
, and thus is not an adversarial sequence.

4
L^{\infty}
stability of the wave equation via Fourier multipliers

In this section, we consider a Fourier multiplier operator

B\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

Bf=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mu\cdot\mathcal{F}{f}\right)

(5)

with some
\mu\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Here,
\mathcal{F}\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the Fourier transform with the normalization
\mathcal{F}f(\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\xi}\,dx
for
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Such an operator
B
is bounded, with
\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
,
but may be unbounded when viewed under a different norm.
We wish to approximate
B
with an operator that is bounded in a stronger sense,
for example as an operator
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in addition to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

4.1
Instability in wave propagation

As a motivating example, consider the wave equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}u(t)-\Delta u(t)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle u(0)

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(0)

\displaystyle=0,

in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
and the operator
B
which propagates the initial condition
f
up to time
t=1
, namely,
Bf=u(1)
. Here,
u
is a function of two variables,
t
and
x
, and with an abuse of notation we write
u(t)
for the function
u(t,\cdot)
.
Viewing the wave equation under the spatial Fourier transform,

\widehat{u}(t)=\cos\left(2\pi\left\lvert\cdot\right\rvert t\right)\widehat{f},\quad t\geq 0,

we verify that
B
is a Fourier multiplier of the form (
5
) with
\mu(\xi)=\cos\left(2\pi|\xi|\right)
, hence a bounded linear operator
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
However,
it is simple to find bounded initial conditions that can become arbitrarily large in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
at time
t=1
.
For example,
consider smooth radially symmetric initial conditions,

f(x)=g(\left\lvert x\right\rvert),\quad g\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}.

When evaluated at
x=0
, the solution
u
satisfies

u(t,0)=g(t)+t\cdot g^{\prime}(t)

(see e.g. Kirchhoff’s formula
[
26
, §12]
).
Thus, by increasing the slope of
g
at
\left\lvert x\right\rvert=1
, we can increase the
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
norm of
Bf=u(1)
.
We construct an adversarial sequence (cf. Definition
3.1
) relative to
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

by setting,
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

f_{n}(x)=g_{n}(\left\lvert x\right\rvert)
with
g_{n}
compactly supported and such that
\left\lvert g_{n}(r)\right\rvert\leq 1
,
r\geq 0
, as well as
\left\lvert g_{n}^{\prime}(1)\right\rvert\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty
.
Then, we get

\|Bf_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\geq\left\lvert Bf_{n}(0)\right\rvert=\left\lvert g_{n}(1)+g_{n}^{\prime}(1)\right\rvert\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\infty.

See Fig.
1
for an example. Figure
2
shows such a sequence as perturbations to a signal of larger amplitude.

4.2
Filtering in space and frequency

In order to mitigate effects such as the adversarial sequences in Section
4.1
,
we can employ filters in both frequency and space to approximate
B
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(\kappa_{\alpha})_{\alpha>0}

and

(h_{\beta})_{\beta>0}

be two families of functions such that

\kappa_{\alpha},h_{\beta}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

for all

\alpha,\beta>0
.
Then the operator

B_{\alpha,\beta}\colon L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad f\mapsto\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mu\cdot\kappa_{\alpha}\cdot\mathcal{F}\left({h_{\beta}}f\right)\right)

is well defined and bounded for every
2\leq p\leq\infty
. In particular,

\displaystyle\|B_{\alpha,\beta}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert{h_{\beta}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

\displaystyle\|B_{\alpha,\beta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert{h_{\beta}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Proof.

Straightforward computation,
using
Hölder’s inequality and
the Plancherel theorem,
gives

\displaystyle\left\lVert B_{\alpha,\beta}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mu\kappa_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}\left({h_{\beta}}f\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\mathcal{F}\left({h_{\beta}}f\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert{h_{\beta}}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert{h_{\beta}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

and further,

\displaystyle\left\lVert B_{\alpha,\beta}f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mu\kappa_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}\left({h_{\beta}}f\right)\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}\left({h_{\beta}}f\right)\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\mathcal{F}\left({h_{\beta}}f\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert{h_{\beta}}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert{h_{\beta}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Therefore
B_{\alpha,\beta}
is bounded
both as an
operator
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and

L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
By the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem
it is therefore bounded as an operator
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
p\in[2,\infty]
.
∎

One can, for example, take as filter the families

\kappa_{\alpha}={h_{\alpha}}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{B}\left({\alpha^{-1}}\right)},

(where
\mathcal{B}\left({\delta}\right)
is the ball around the origin with radius
\delta>0
).
The bounds above become

\left\lVert B_{\alpha,\alpha}f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

and

\left\lVert B_{\alpha,\alpha}f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq V_{d}\alpha^{-d}\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

where
V_{d}
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We shall see that this choice is not optimal.

For certain families of filters,
the operator
B_{\alpha,\beta}
can be written as a composition of
B
with a preconditioning operator

.
This is highly beneficial for implementation if the multiplier
\mu

Let us record some boundedness properties of this operator.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(k_{\alpha})_{\alpha>0}

and

(h_{\beta})_{\beta>0}

be two families of functions such that

k_{\alpha}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

for all

\alpha>0
, and
{h_{\beta}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
\beta>0
.
The linear operator

T_{\alpha,\beta}\colon L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad f\mapsto k_{\alpha}*(h_{\beta}\cdot f),

(6)

is well defined and bounded for
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
where

q=\begin{cases}2p/(p+2)\in[1,2],&\text{if}\ p>2\\
p,&\text{if}\ 1\leq p\leq 2.\end{cases}

Proof.

By Young’s convolution inequality,
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert k_{\alpha}*(h_{\beta}f)\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert k_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert h_{\beta}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert k_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert h_{\beta}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

for
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which
shows that
T_{\alpha,\beta}
is bounded
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Now, for
2\leq p\leq\infty,

q=2p/(p+2)

we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert k_{\alpha}*(h_{\beta}f)\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert k_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert h_{\beta}f\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert k_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert h_{\beta}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

by applying Young’s convolution inequality again, together with Hölder’s inequality.
This shows that
T_{\alpha,\beta}
is bounded
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as well.
∎

An interpretation of Lemma
4.2
is that
for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the operator
T_{\alpha,\beta}\colon L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is bounded and maps into the domain of the Fourier transform.
In fact, a corollary of Lemma
4.2
is that
T_{\alpha,\beta}\colon L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is bounded for all
2\leq p\leq\infty
.

Remark 4.3
.

If
\kappa_{\alpha}=\mathcal{F}k_{\alpha}
with
k_{\alpha}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
\alpha>0
,
then
\kappa_{\alpha}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and we get

B_{\alpha,\beta}=BT_{\alpha,\beta}

by the convolution theorem:

\displaystyle B_{\alpha,\beta}f

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mu\cdot\kappa_{\alpha}\cdot\mathcal{F}\left({h_{\beta}}\cdot f\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mu\cdot\mathcal{F}(k_{\alpha})\cdot\mathcal{F}\left({h_{\beta}}\cdot f\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mu\cdot\mathcal{F}\left(k_{\alpha}*({h_{\beta}}\cdot f)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=BT_{\alpha,\beta}f.

Note that Lemma
4.2
, Hölder’s inequality, and the Hausdorff-Young inequality ensure that the Fourier transforms of
T_{\alpha,\beta}f
and
h_{\beta}\cdot f
exist for

for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
.
However, in this case,
\kappa_{\alpha}
is continuous by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma,
so filters such as

are excluded.

Theorem 4.4

(Pointwise approximation in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
.

Let
k,\widehat{h}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be two functions satisfying
\int k=\int\widehat{h}=1
.
Define the families
(k_{\alpha})_{\alpha>0}
and
(h_{\beta})_{\beta>0}

through dilation,

\displaystyle k_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-n}k(x/\alpha)

(7)

\displaystyle\widehat{h}_{\beta}(x)

\displaystyle=\beta^{-n}\widehat{h}(x/\beta),\qquad h_{\beta}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\widehat{h}_{\beta}).

(8)

Then, for every
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\left\lVert T_{\alpha,\beta}f-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\xrightarrow[\alpha,\beta\to 0]{}0

and

\left\lVert B_{\alpha,\beta}f-Bf\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\xrightarrow[\alpha,\beta\to 0]{}0.

Proof.

Note that
h_{\beta}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\widehat{h}_{\beta}]\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
We have established that
B_{\alpha,\beta}=BT_{\alpha,\beta}

and that
\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
.
Therefore

\left\lVert B_{\alpha,\beta}f-Bf\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert T_{\alpha,\beta}f-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Using the triangle inequality,
Young’s convolution inequality,
and the fact that
\left\lVert k_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\left\lVert k\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
,
we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert T_{\alpha,\beta}f-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert k_{\alpha}*(h_{\beta}f)-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert k_{\alpha}*(h_{\beta}f)-k_{\alpha}*f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\left\lVert k_{\alpha}*f-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert k_{\alpha}*(h_{\beta}f-f)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\left\lVert k_{\alpha}*f-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert k_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert h_{\beta}f-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\left\lVert k_{\alpha}*f-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert k\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\mathcal{F}(h_{\beta}f-f)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\left\lVert k_{\alpha}*f-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert k\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lVert\widehat{h}_{\beta}*\widehat{f}-\widehat{f}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\left\lVert k_{\alpha}*f-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Since
(k_{\alpha})_{\alpha>0}
and
(\widehat{h}_{\beta})_{\beta>0}
form an approximation to the identity,
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert k_{\alpha}*f-f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}0\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widehat{h}_{\beta}*\widehat{f}-\widehat{f}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\beta\to 0]{}0,

which concludes the proof.
∎

.

The choice of filters (

Theorem 4.6

(Pointwise approximation in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
.

Define the families
(k_{\alpha})_{\alpha>0}
and
(h_{\beta})_{\beta>0}
as in Theorem
4.4
.
Then, for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\widehat{f}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\left\lVert B_{\alpha,\beta}f-Bf\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\xrightarrow[\alpha,\beta\to 0]{}0.

Proof.

First note that
\kappa=\widehat{k}
is a continuous function by the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma, and that

\kappa_{\alpha}(\xi)=\kappa(\alpha\xi)\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}\kappa(0)=\int k(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1,

and hence

\kappa_{\alpha}(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}\widehat{f}(\xi)

for almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

Moreover, we have

\left\lvert\kappa_{\alpha}(\xi)\widehat{f}(\xi)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lvert\widehat{f}(\xi)\right\rvert=\left\lVert\kappa\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\lvert\widehat{f}(\xi)\right\rvert

for all
\xi
,
and since
\widehat{f}
is integrable,
we have

\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\widehat{f}-\widehat{f}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}0

by the dominated convergence theorem.

Assuming
\lVert\mu\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
for simplicity,
we get:

\displaystyle\lVert B_{\alpha,\beta}f-Bf\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[\mu\kappa_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}(h_{\beta}f)-\mu\mathcal{F}f\right]\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mu\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}(h_{\beta}f)-\widehat{f}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle=\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\left(\widehat{h}_{\beta}*\widehat{f}\right)-\widehat{f}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\left(\widehat{h}_{\beta}*\widehat{f}\right)-\kappa_{\alpha}\widehat{f}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\widehat{f}-\widehat{f}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lVert\widehat{h}_{\beta}*\widehat{f}-\widehat{f}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\widehat{f}-\widehat{f}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\kappa\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lVert\widehat{h}_{\beta}*\widehat{f}-\widehat{f}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\lVert\kappa_{\alpha}\widehat{f}-\widehat{f}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\xrightarrow[\alpha,\beta\to 0]{}0.

Here we have used the fact that
(\widehat{h}_{\beta})_{\beta>0}
is an approximation to the identity.
This concludes the proof.
∎

5
Uniform approximation with spectral filtering and applications to inverse problems

In this section,
A\colon X\to Y
will denote a compact linear operator between two separable Hilbert spaces with an infinite dimensional range.
The space
Y
is otherwise arbitrary, but we will identify
X
with
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
for some measure space
\mathcal{M}
.
We are interested in finding a regularization of the pseudoinverse
{A}^{\dagger}\colon Y\to X
that is also well behaved as an operator
Y\to X^{\prime}
with the auxiliary space being
X^{\prime}=L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.

As a compact operator,
A
has a singular value decomposition

Ax=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{n}\langle x,v_{n}\rangle_{X}u_{n},\quad x\in X,

where
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are orthonormal systems in
Y
and
X
, respectively, and
(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a non-increasing sequence of positive real numbers with
\sigma_{n}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
.
Likewise, the pseudoinverse
{A}^{\dagger}
can be expressed by

{A}^{\dagger}y=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}}{\sigma_{n}}v_{n},

for

y\in\mathcal{D}({{A}^{\dagger}})=\left\{y\in Y\colon\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{n}^{-2}\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}<\infty\right\}\subsetneq Y.

We make use of this fact heavily in what follows.

5.1
Instability in Tikhonov regularization

Consider the Tikhonov regularizer
[
9
]

T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}\colon Y\to X,\quad\alpha>0,

which can be expressed in terms of the SVD of
A
as

T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}y=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha}\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}v_{n},\quad y\in Y.

This is a bounded operator as a mapping
Y\to X
, with
X=L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
,
but it is not, in general, bounded as a map
Y\to X^{\prime}
for
X^{\prime}=L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.

In the simplest case, the left singular vectors
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
form an adversarial sequence for
T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}
(cf. Definition
3.1
).
While they are bounded in
Y
(with norms equal to 1), we have

\displaystyle\lVert T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}u_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\alpha}\sigma_{n}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\lVert T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}u_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\sigma_{n}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}.

Therefore, for every
\alpha>0
, the sequence
(\lVert T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}u_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})})_{n}
is bounded if and only if the sequence
(\sigma_{n}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})})_{n}
is bounded.
In particular, if
(\sigma_{n}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})})_{n}\notin\ell^{\infty}
, then
T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}
is not bounded from
Y
into
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.
Further, if one of the right singular vectors
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is itself an unbounded function, then
T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}
is not bounded from
Y
into
X^{\prime}
.

This is only a necessary condition: there are situations where the sequence
(\sigma_{n}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})})
is bounded, but the Tikhonov regularizer is not bounded as an operator into
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.
To see this, we consider the following example.

Example 5.1

(Periodic convolution)
.

Let
X=Y=L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, where
\mathbb{T}
is the unit circle (identified with
[0,1]
), and
consider the periodic convolution operator
A\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T}),

defined for
x\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
by

\left(Ax\right)(t)=\left(k*x\right)(t)=\int_{0}^{1}k(t-s)x(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad t\in\mathbb{T},

with some kernel
k\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
.
This operator is diagonal in the Fourier basis

(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}=(\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}n\cdot})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}

and its singular values are the absolute values of the non-zero Fourier coefficients of
k
.
Assuming, for ease of notation, that all Fourier coefficients of
k
are non-zero, we thus obtain an SVD of
A
with

(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
as right singular vectors, and a phase shifted version
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
of the same basis as left singular vectors.

Suppose that the kernel is badly singular, so that
k\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})\setminus L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
,
and that the (rearranged) singular values satisfy

\sigma_{n}=\left\lvert\widehat{k}[n]\right\rvert\geq\frac{1}{\left(\left\lvert n\right\rvert+1\right)^{\rho}},\quad\text{for all}\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}

with some
0<\rho<1/2
.
Define perturbations
(r_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
by

r_{N}=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}a_{n}u_{n}\in Y,\qquad a_{n}=\frac{1}{\left(\left\lvert n\right\rvert+1\right)^{1-\rho}}.

Since
1-\rho>1/2
, we have
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, and hence the perturbations are bounded by

\lVert r_{N}\rVert_{Y}^{2}\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}^{2}<\infty
.

Adversarial artifacts now appear at
0
:

\displaystyle\left(T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}r_{N}\right)(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha}a_{n}v_{n}(0)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}+\alpha}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}a_{n}\sigma_{n}v_{n}(0)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}+\alpha}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\frac{1}{\left(\left\lvert n\right\rvert+1\right)^{1-\rho}}\frac{1}{\left(\left\lvert n\right\rvert+1\right)^{\rho}}\cdot 1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}+\alpha}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\frac{1}{\left\lvert n\right\rvert+1}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\infty,

where
\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}\geq\sigma_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The perturbations
(r_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
form an adversarial sequence, and
r=\lim_{N\to\infty}r_{N}\in Y
is an adversarial perturbation, namely,
T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}(Y)\not\subseteq L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.
Hence,

T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}
is not bounded
Y\to L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.

5.2
Spectral filtering

In order to ensure a bounded reconstruction into
X^{\prime}=L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
,
we modify the Tikhonov regularizer by placing non-uniform weights in the SVD.
That way, we can regularize more strongly in those directions that correspond to problematic right singular vectors.

Let
c=(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of real numbers
that is bounded below by some positive number
c_{0}
,

0<c_{0}\leq c_{n}\quad\text{for all}\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

Denote by
T^{c}_{\alpha}
the
spectral filtering
operator

T^{c}_{\alpha}y=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}}\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}v_{n},\quad y\in Y.

(9)

The threshold
c_{0}
ensures that this operator is dominated by a Tikhonov regularizer,

\lVert T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{X}^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}\right)^{2}}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{0}\right)^{2}}=\lVert T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha c_{0}}y\rVert_{X}^{2}

for all
y\in Y
,
and is thus bounded with

\lVert T^{c}_{\alpha}\rVert_{Y\to X}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha c_{0}}}

(10)

by Lemma
A.1
.
The fact that
\lVert T^{c}_{\alpha}\rVert_{Y\to X}\leq\lVert T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha c_{0}}\rVert_{Y\to X}
will later be useful for obtaining a convergence rate for
T^{c}_{\alpha}y\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}{A}^{\dagger}y
.

We remark that an alternative formulation of this operator is

T^{c}_{\alpha}y=\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{D}({W})}\left\lVert Ax-y\right\rVert_{Y}^{2}+\alpha\left\lVert Wx\right\rVert_{X}^{2},

where
W\colon\mathcal{D}({W})\to X
is the linear operator

Wx=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{c_{n}}\langle x,v_{n}\rangle_{X}v_{n},

defined on a subspace
\mathcal{D}({W})\subseteq X
.
With
c_{n}=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we recover the familiar formulation of Tikhonov regularization.

Next, we show that spectral filtering is indeed a regularization method in
X=L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
,
and that, under mild conditions, it converges in
X^{\prime}=L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
as well. Note that in order for it to be a regularization as an operator from
Y
to
X^{\prime}
, we also need to establish that
T_{\alpha}^{c}:Y\to X^{\prime}
is bounded. This is the subject of Section
5.2.3
.

5.2.1
Convergence

We now prove two approximation results: pointwise in
X
for all
y\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\dagger})
and pointwise in
X^{\prime}
under more restrictive assumptions.
These theorems do not provide a convergence rate, as that will require more restrictions on
y
.

Theorem 5.2

(Pointwise approximation in
X
)
.

We have

\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{X}\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}0

for all
y\in\mathcal{D}({{A}^{\dagger}})
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
We will show that the error is smaller than
\varepsilon
for all
\alpha
smaller than some
\alpha_{0}>0
.

Since
y\in\mathcal{D}({{A}^{\dagger}})
and by Picard’s criterion,
we can select an integer
N
large enough so that

\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}<\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}

and the error can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}}-\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}}\right)\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}v_{n}\right\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\alpha^{2}c_{n}^{2}\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}\right)^{2}}}+\sqrt{\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{2}c_{n}^{2}\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}\right)^{2}}}

\displaystyle<\alpha\sqrt{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{c_{n}^{2}\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{6}}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=:\alpha C_{N}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

The result now follows by choosing
\alpha_{0}
small enough so that
\alpha_{0}C_{N}<\varepsilon/2
.
∎

Note that
C_{N}
, and hence
\alpha_{0},
depends on
y
,
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}
and the SVD of
A
. As previously stated, we are interested in finding solutions to
Ax=y
that lie in
X^{\prime}=L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.
Therefore, our regularization method should approximate
{A}^{\dagger}y
uniformly whenever
\left\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
.
Write

U=\left\{y\in Y\colon\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty\right\}.

(11)

This is a subspace of
Y
, and

y\in U
is
a somewhat stronger condition than
\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
, indeed

\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}=\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}}{\sigma_{n}}v_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}.

Similarly, if
y\in U
, then
T^{c}_{\alpha}y\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.
We have the following convergence result, whose proof is similar to that of Theorem
5.2
.

Theorem 5.3

(Pointwise approximation in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
)
.

We have

\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}0

for all
y\in U
.

Proof.

As before, we need to show that for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists some
\alpha_{0}>0
, such that for all
\alpha<\alpha_{0},

\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}<\varepsilon.

Since
y\in U
,
we can select an integer
N
large enough so that

\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

The error can now be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}}-\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}}\right)\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}v_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\alpha c_{n}\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}}{\sigma_{n}\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}\right)}v_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}+\left\lVert\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha c_{n}\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}}{\sigma_{n}\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}\right)}v_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle<\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{c_{n}\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}^{3}}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=:\alpha C_{N}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Choosing
\alpha_{0}
so that
\alpha_{0}C_{N}<\varepsilon/2
yields the result.
∎

5.2.2
Convergence rates on source sets

Theorems
5.2
and
5.3
provide no rate of convergence in terms of
\alpha
.
In order to establish a convergence rate,
we require some degree of “smoothness” of
y
, something commonly known as a “source condition” in inverse problems
[
9
]
.
Recall that the operator
A
is assumed to be compact, so that
\sigma_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
\eta\geq 0
.
We say that
y\in Y
satisfies the
\eta
-strong Picard criterion

if

P_{\eta}(y):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2+\eta}}<\infty.

(12)

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose that
A
is a compact operator,
and let
\eta\geq 0
.
The set of elements of
Y
that satisfy the
\eta
-strong Picard criterion,

V_{\eta}=\left\{y\in Y\colon P_{\eta}(y)<\infty\right\}

forms a subspace of
Y
,
and for any
\mu\leq\eta
, we have

V_{\eta}\subseteq V_{\mu}\subseteq V_{0}=\mathcal{D}({{A}^{\dagger}}).

Proof.

For
y,z\in Y
, and
\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
, we have

P_{\eta}(\beta y+\gamma z)\leq 2\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert^{2}P_{\eta}(y)+2\left\lvert\gamma\right\rvert^{2}P_{\eta}(z)<\infty,

where we have used the triangle inequality and the basic fact that
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
for any
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.
This proves that
V_{\eta}
is a subspace of
Y
.

Now let
0\leq\mu\leq\eta
.
Since
A
is compact, we can find an
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sigma_{n}\leq 1
for all
n\geq N
.
Then
\sigma_{n}^{-1}\geq 1
, so
\sigma_{n}^{-\mu}\leq\sigma_{n}^{-\eta}
for
n\geq N
,
and therefore

\displaystyle P_{\mu}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sigma_{n}^{-\mu}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\sigma_{n}^{-\eta}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sigma_{n}^{-\mu}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+P_{\eta}(y).

In other words,
P_{\eta}(y)<\infty
implies
P_{\mu}(y)<\infty
,
and therefore
V_{\eta}\subseteq V_{\mu}
.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

For
\eta>0
,
inclusion in the space
V_{\eta}
can also be characterized in terms of

source sets
in
X
.
One can show that

y\in V_{\eta}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad{A}^{\dagger}y\in X_{\eta/4}=\mathcal{R}({\left(A^{*}A\right)^{\eta/4}})

(see Proposition 3.13 of
[
9
]
).
However, the explicit dependence of
V_{\eta}
on the singular values of
A
through (
12
) is more useful to our analysis, so we state our results in terms of these subspaces of
Y
.

To establish rates of convergence for
T^{c}_{\alpha}
on the source sets,
we follow a similar approach as in Chapter 4 of
[
9
]
.
However, it needs to be adapted to the current case where the spectral filter does not depend exclusively on the singular values
\sigma_{n}
, but rather on their index
n
.
By imposing a condition on the growth of the coefficients
c_{n}
relative to the decay of
\sigma_{n}
, standard proofs can be emulated.
This comes at the cost of having to assume more smoothness of the data.
Before we state the next convergence results, we need the following calculus fact.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\eta,\alpha>0
and define the function
h\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
by

h(\sigma)=\frac{\sigma^{\eta}}{\left(\sigma^{2}+\alpha\right)^{2}},\qquad\sigma>0.

If
\eta<4
, then

h(\sigma)\leq C_{\eta}\alpha^{\eta/2-2}

for some
C_{\eta}>0
,
and if
\eta\geq 4
, then
h
is strictly increasing.

Proof.

See
A
.
∎

Theorem 5.8

(Pointwise approximation in
X
with convergence rate)
.

Suppose that

A
is compact,
and that the weights
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy

c_{n}\leq C\sigma_{n}^{-\beta}\quad\text{for all}\ n\in\mathbb{N},

(13)

for some
C,\beta>0
.
Let
y\in V_{\eta+2\beta}
for some
\eta>0
.
Then there exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
, depending only on
C
,
\beta
,
\eta
,
\sigma_{1}
and
y
,
such that

\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\leq C^{\prime}\alpha^{\min\{1,\eta/4\}}

for all
\alpha>0
.

Proof.

Taking squared norms, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha c_{n})^{2}\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2+\eta}}(\alpha c_{n})^{2}\frac{\sigma_{n}^{\eta}}{\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}\right)^{2}}.

Now, we employ Lemma
5.7
.
If
\eta<4
, then

\displaystyle\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\eta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2+\eta}}(\alpha c_{n})^{2}(\alpha c_{n})^{\eta/2-2}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{\eta/2}C_{\eta}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2+\eta}}(C\sigma_{n}^{-\beta})^{\eta/2}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{\eta/2}C_{\eta}^{\prime}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2+\eta+\beta\eta/2}}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\eta/2}C_{\eta}^{\prime}P_{\eta+\beta\eta/2}(y),

and since
\eta\leq 4
, we have
y\in V_{\eta+2\beta}\subseteq V_{\eta+\beta\eta/2}
.
If
\eta\geq 4
, then, by monotonicity of
h(\sigma)
,

\displaystyle\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2+\eta}}(\alpha c_{n})^{2}\frac{\sigma_{1}^{\eta}}{\left(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{2}C^{2}\sigma_{1}^{\eta-4}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2+\eta+2\beta}}=\alpha^{2}C^{2}\sigma_{1}^{\eta-4}P_{\eta+2\beta}(y).

Taking square roots on both sides of the above inequalities gives the result.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

Tikhonov regularization satisfies the conditions of Theorem
5.8
with
\beta=0
,
and the above rate is exactly the optimal rate for Tikhonov regularization (see
[
9
, Example 4.15]
).

Next, we consider uniform approximation on the sets
V_{\eta}
,
\eta>0
.
Again, we need a condition on the SVD of
A

that relates
the growth of
\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}
to
the decay of
\sigma_{n}
.

Lemma 5.10
.

If the operator
A
satisfies

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{n}^{\eta}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}<\infty,

(14)

then
V_{\eta}\subseteq U
(defined in Eq. (
11
)).
Consequently,
{A}^{\dagger}y\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})

for all
y\in V_{\eta}
.

Proof.

Take
y\in V_{\eta}
. We use the triangle inequality and Hölder’s inequality for infinite series:

\displaystyle\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}}\left\lVert v_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}^{1+\eta/2}}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}\left\lVert\left(\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\left\lVert v_{n}\right\rVert_{\infty}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})},

which is finite by
(
14
) and using that
y\in V_{\eta}
.
∎

Remark 5.11
.

Condition (
14
)
significantly limits the choice for the parameter
\eta
.
For example, if
\left\lVert v_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}=1/\sigma_{n}
, then
\eta
is always at least 2 if the condition is to be satisfied.
Consider a mildly ill-posed problem satisfying

\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}=1/\sigma_{n}=n^{s}
for some
s>0
.
Then

\sigma_{n}^{\eta}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}=n^{-s\eta+2s}=n^{s(2-\eta)}

so (
14
) is satisfied if and only if
\eta>2+1/s
.
For severely ill-posed problems with
\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}=1/\sigma_{n}=\mathrm{e}^{sn}
,
any
\eta>2
suffices.

Theorem 5.12

(Pointwise approximation in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
with convergence rate)
.

Suppose that

A
is compact and that its SVD satisfies

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{n}^{\eta}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}<\infty.

(15)

Moreover, suppose that the weights
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy

c_{n}\leq C\sigma_{n}^{-\beta}\quad\text{for all}\ n\in\mathbb{N},

for some
C,\beta>0
.
Let
y\in V_{2\eta+2\beta}
.
Then there exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0

such that

\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\leq C^{\prime}\alpha^{\min\{1,\eta/4\}}

for all
\alpha>0
.

Proof.

We use the triangle inequality and rearrange exponents:

\displaystyle\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha c_{n}\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}\left(\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}\right)}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}^{1+\eta}}(\alpha c_{n})\frac{\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}}\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}.

As in the proof of Theorem
5.8
,
we now use Lemma
5.7
,
by remarking that since the square root is an increasing function,
\sigma^{\eta/2}(\sigma^{2}+\alpha)^{-1}
has the same maxima as
h(\sigma)
.
For
\eta<4
we get

\displaystyle\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}^{1+\eta}}(\alpha c_{n})(\alpha c_{n})^{\eta/4-1}\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{\eta/4}C^{\prime}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}^{1+\eta}}(C\sigma_{n}^{-\beta})^{\eta/4}\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{\eta/4}C^{\prime\prime}\left\lVert\left(\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}^{1+\eta+\beta\eta/4}}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}\left\lVert\left(\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}.

The two
\ell^{2}
norms are finite, because of (
15
) and using that
y\in V_{2\eta+2\beta}\subseteq V_{2\eta+\beta\eta/2}
for
\eta\leq 4
.
For
\eta\geq 4
:

\displaystyle\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}^{1+\eta}}(\alpha c_{n})\frac{\sigma_{1}^{\eta/2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}}\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha C^{\prime}\sigma_{1}^{\eta/2-2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}^{1+\eta}}(C\sigma_{n}^{-\beta})\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha C^{\prime\prime}\sigma_{1}^{\eta/2-2}\left\lVert\left(\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}^{1+\eta+\beta}}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}\left\lVert\left(\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})},

which, similarly, is finite because of (
15
) and
y\in V_{2\eta+2\beta}.

∎

5.2.3
Boundedness of the spectral filter

We now have a recipe for a spectral filtering operator that guarantees convergence both in
X=L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
and in
X^{\prime}=L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
,
provided that certain conditions are satisfied. We have also noted at the beginning of this section, that this operator is bounded from
Y
into
X
. However, for it to be indeed a regularization as an operator
T_{\alpha}^{c}:Y\to X^{\prime}
, we need to establish that it is (under certain conditions) a bounded operator with respect to these spaces.
We assume that
A
is compact, and that
v_{n}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

As for the convergence rate results, we will again suppose that
(\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\|v_{n}\|_{\infty})\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
for some
\eta>0
, cf. (
15
).
Under this condition, we can always find a sequence of weights

(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

such that
T_{\alpha}^{c}:Y\to X^{\prime}
is bounded.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
A
be a compact operator with SVD such that
v_{n}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let (
15
) hold for some
\eta>0
. If
\eta\leq 2,
any lower bounded sequence of weights
c_{n}\geq c_{0}>0
results in
T_{\alpha}^{c}:Y\to X^{\prime}
being bounded. If
\eta>2,
any choice
c_{n}\geq C\sigma_{n}^{1-\eta/2},
with arbitrary constant
C>0
implies boundedness of
T_{\alpha}^{c}:Y\to X^{\prime}
. In both cases,

\left\|T_{\alpha}^{c}\right\|_{Y\to L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\alpha},\qquad\alpha>0,

for some
C^{\prime}>0
independent of
\alpha
.

Proof.

If
\eta\leq 2
, for any
y\in Y,

\displaystyle\left\|T_{\alpha}^{c}y\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}}\left|\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right|\left\|v_{n}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma_{1}^{1-\eta/2}}{\alpha c_{0}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right|\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\left\|v_{n}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sigma_{1}^{1-\eta/2}}{\alpha c_{0}}\left\|\left(\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\left\|v_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}\cdot\left\|y\right\|_{Y}.

If
\eta>2,
choosing
c_{n}\geq C\sigma_{n}^{1-\eta/2}
yields,

\frac{\sigma_{n}^{1-\eta/2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha C},

and we can similarly deduce that for all
y\in Y,

\displaystyle\left\|T_{\alpha}^{c}y\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\alpha c_{n}}\left|\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right|\left\|v_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha C}\left\|\left(\sigma_{n}^{\eta/2}\left\|v_{n}\right\|_{\infty}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}\cdot\left\|y\right\|_{Y}.

∎

By Lemma
5.10
and Theorem
5.3
,

T^{c}_{\alpha}y\xrightarrow[\alpha\to 0]{}{A}^{\dagger}y
in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
for all
y\in V_{\eta}
,
and Theorem
5.12
provides a convergence rate for
y\in V_{2\eta+2\beta}
, as long as
\beta>0
is such that

c_{n}\leq C\sigma_{n}^{-\beta}

for some
C>0.
Thus, for
\eta\leq 2,
the choice
c_{n}=c_{0}>0
allows for any
\beta>0
and for
\eta>2,
setting
c_{n}=C\sigma_{n}^{1-\eta/2}
one can choose
\beta=-1+\eta/2.

Therefore if
y\in V_{2\eta+2\beta}
, with
\beta
appropriately chosen depending on
\eta,
and
r\in Y
is the noise in the measurements with
\|r\|_{Y}\leq\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert T^{c}_{\alpha(\delta)}(y+r)-{A}^{\dagger}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert T^{c}_{\alpha(\delta)}y-{A}^{\dagger}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}+\lVert T^{c}_{\alpha(\delta)}r\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle\leq C\alpha(\delta)^{\min\{1,\eta/4\}}+\frac{C^{\prime}}{\alpha(\delta)}\delta

\displaystyle=C^{\prime\prime}\delta^{\min\{1/2,\eta/8\}}

with the choice
\alpha(\delta)=\sqrt{\delta}
.

5.3
A numerical example

We revisit Example
5.1
to visualize the differences in approximation with

T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}
and
T^{c}_{\alpha}
.
The operator
A\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is a convolution with a singular kernel
k\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})\setminus L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
,

Ax(t)=\left(k*x\right)(t)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}k(s)x(t-s)\,\mathrm{d}s

for all
t\in\mathbb{T}
.
We choose a real valued kernel with

\widehat{k}[n]=(n+1)^{-1/3}

(16)

for
n\geq 0
.
As shown in Example
5.1
(with
\rho=1/3
), this leads to a Tikhonov regularizer that admits adversarial perturbations.

The SVD of
A
satisfies (
15
)
for any
\eta>3
.
We choose weights for the modified regularizer
according to Proposition
5.13
,
using
\eta=4
,

c_{n}=\sigma_{n}^{1-\eta/2}=\sigma_{n}^{-1}=(\left\lvert n\right\rvert+1)^{1/3}

for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
By Proposition
5.13
,
the resulting operator,
T^{c}_{\alpha}

is bounded

L^{2}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})

and by Theorem
5.3
it is guaranteed to converge in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
to
{A}^{\dagger}
pointwise on the subspace
U\subseteq Y
from (
11
).
By Theorem
5.12
,
we have

\lVert{A}^{\dagger}y-T^{c}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\leq C\alpha^{\min\{1,\eta/4\}}=C\alpha

for

y\in V_{2\eta+2(\eta/2-1)}=V_{10}

and some constant
C>0
.

We note that
for any

s>1/2+\rho=5/6
,
the Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{T})
is a subspace of
U
.
Namely,
there exist constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert}{\sigma_{n}}\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\left\lvert\widehat{y}[n]\right\rvert/\left\lvert\widehat{k}[n]\right\rvert\right)\cdot 1

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert n^{\rho}\ \widehat{y}[n]\right\rvert

\displaystyle=C\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert n\right\rvert^{-1/2-\epsilon}\left\lvert n\right\rvert^{1/2+\epsilon+\rho}\left\lvert\widehat{y}[n]\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert\left(\left\lvert n\right\rvert^{-1/2-\epsilon}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\left\lVert\left(\left\lvert n\right\rvert^{1/2+\rho+\epsilon}\left\lvert\widehat{y}[n]\right\rvert\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{H^{1/2+\rho+\epsilon}(\mathbb{T})}

for every
\epsilon>0
and every
y\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
.
Likewise, we can characterize the source sets
(V_{\eta})_{\eta>0}
as follows:

\displaystyle P_{\eta}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left\lvert\langle y,u_{n}\rangle_{Y}\right\rvert^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2+\eta}}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\lvert n\right\rvert^{\rho(2+\eta)}\left\lvert\widehat{y}[n]\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=C\left\lVert\left(\left\lvert n\right\rvert^{\rho+\eta\rho/2}\left\lvert\widehat{y}[n]\right\rvert\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{H^{\rho+\eta\rho/2}(\mathbb{T})},

so

H^{\rho+\eta\rho/2}(\mathbb{T})=H^{(2+\eta)/6}(\mathbb{T})\subseteq V_{\eta}
for all
\eta>0
.

We collect here some proofs and auxiliary results.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

We assume that
A(X\cap X^{\prime})\subseteq Y\cap Y^{\prime}

(i.e. no adversarial perturbation exists)
and show that no adversarial sequence can exist.

The intersections
U=X\cap X^{\prime}
and
V=Y\cap Y^{\prime}
are Banach spaces when equipped with the norms

\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{U}=\max\{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{\prime}}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{V}=\max\{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{Y},\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{Y^{\prime}}\}.

We show that the operator
A
is bounded with respect to these norms by using the Closed Graph Theorem.
Let
((x_{n},y_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in the graph of
{\left.\kern-1.2ptA\right|_{U}}
,

\Gamma=\left\{(x,y)\in U\times V\colon y=Ax\right\},

that converges to a point
(u,v)\in U\times V
in the product norm

\left\lVert(x,y)\right\rVert_{U\times V}=\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{U}+\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{V},\quad\forall x\in X\ \forall y\in Y.

Since

\left\lVert x_{n}-u\right\rVert_{X}\leq\left\lVert x_{n}-u\right\rVert_{U}\leq\left\lVert(x_{n},y_{n})-(u,v)\right\rVert_{U\times V}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

we have

\left\lVert y_{n}-Au\right\rVert_{Y}=\left\lVert Ax_{n}-Au\right\rVert_{Y}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{X\to Y}\left\lVert x_{n}-u\right\rVert_{X}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Since

\left\lVert y_{n}-v\right\rVert_{Y}\leq\left\lVert y_{n}-v\right\rVert_{V}\leq\left\lVert(x_{n},y_{n})-(u,v)\right\rVert_{U\times V}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

we can see that
the sequence
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to both
Au
and
v
in
Y
.
This means that
v=Au
, and hence
(u,v)\in\Gamma
.
We have shown that
\Gamma
is a closed subspace of
U\times V
,
and thus that
A
is bounded with respect to
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{U}
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{V}
.

The result now follows.
Since

\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{Y^{\prime}}\leq\left\lVert Ax\right\rVert_{V}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{U\to V}\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{U}

for all
x\in X
,
any sequence in
U=X\cap X^{\prime}
that is bounded in both
X
and
X^{\prime}
has a bounded image in
Y^{\prime}
and hence is not an adversarial sequence.
∎

]

C

Lemma A.1
.

For all
y\in Y
, we have

\lVert T^{\mathrm{Tikh}}_{\alpha}y\rVert_{X}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\lVert y\rVert_{Y}.

Proof.

The proof of Theorem 4.2 in
[
9
]
establishes that

\left\|AA^{*}(AA^{*}+\alpha I)^{-1}\right\|_{Y\to Y}\leq 1

and

\left\|(AA^{*}+\alpha I)^{-1}\right\|_{Y\to Y}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}.

Noting that
T_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{Tikh}}=(A^{*}A+\alpha I)^{-1}A^{*}
and employing the fact

\left(A^{*}A+\alpha I\right)^{-1}A^{*}=A^{*}\left(AA^{*}+\alpha I\right)^{-1},

we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|T_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{Tikh}}y\right\|^{2}_{X}

\displaystyle=\langle A^{*}\left(AA^{*}+\alpha I\right)^{-1}y,A^{*}\left(AA^{*}+\alpha I\right)^{-1}y\rangle_{X}

\displaystyle=\langle AA^{*}\left(AA^{*}+\alpha I\right)^{-1}y,\left(AA^{*}+\alpha I\right)^{-1}y\rangle_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|AA^{*}(AA^{*}+\alpha I)^{-1}\right\|_{Y\to Y}\left\|(AA^{*}+\alpha I)^{-1}\right\|_{Y\to Y}\left\|y\right\|_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\|y\right\|_{Y}^{2}.

∎

Proof of Lemma
5.7
.

We derive:

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\eta-4)\,\sigma^{\eta+1}+\alpha\,\eta\,\sigma^{\eta-1}}{(\sigma^{2}+\alpha)^{3}}.

This is strictly positive for
\eta\geq 4
. For
\eta<4
,

h^{\prime}(\sigma)=0
if and only if

\sigma^{2}=\frac{\eta\alpha}{4-\eta},

which is a maximum (by straightforward calculation,
h^{\prime\prime}(\sqrt{\frac{\eta\alpha}{4-\eta}})<0
).
We evaluate:

\displaystyle h\left(\sqrt{\frac{\eta\alpha}{4-\eta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\eta\alpha}{4-\eta}\right)^{\eta/2}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{\eta\alpha}{4-\eta}+\alpha\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\eta/2}\left(\frac{\eta}{4-\eta}\right)^{\eta/2}\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}\left(\frac{\eta}{4-\eta}+1\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=C_{\eta}\alpha^{\eta/2-2},

where
