Self-propelled nanozyme motors for targeted therapy of radiation cystitis by disrupting the inflammation-cfDNA vicious cycle
Authors: Yongbiao Huang, Yang Zhong, Yuanmei Yan, Lei Cao, Wenhao Li, Renjie Liang, Wencheng Wu, Chenglong Sun, Bo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiation cystitis is a common complication of pelvic radiotherapy that significantly compromises clinical outcomes. A principal pathogenic factor is the accumulation of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) released from damaged cells, which promotes inflammatory cytokine production and disturbs tissue homeostasis. The study aims to engineer a self-propelled nanozyme motor that clears cfDNA and restores redox balance as a dual-mechanistic therapy for radiation cystitis. The system operated using endogenous urea, a naturally abundant metabolite present in bladder urine, enabling sustained and coordinated autonomous propulsion following intravesical instillation. This mobility facilitated extensive mucosal coverage and deeper penetration, allowing the motors to capture cfDNA deposited on the irradiated mucosal surface. The nanozyme core eliminated radiation-induced reactive oxygen species, resulting in a reduction of oxidative stress. These combined effects suppressed activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway and lowered the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Introduction

Radiation cystitis (RC) represents a common and debilitating complication observed in 23% to 80% of patients receiving pelvic radiotherapy for malignancies [1]. The condition is characterized by urinary frequency, urgency, and dysuria, which significantly impair quality of life and frequently lead to dose reduction or early discontinuation of radiotherapy, compromising therapeutic efficacy [2]. The pathogenesis of RC involves multiple interconnected processes. Ionizing radiation induces injury to bladder epithelial and microvascular endothelial cells, initiating a cascade that includes persistent oxidative stress, elevated inflammation, and tissue fibrosis [3]. Existing clinical management strategies, including behavioral modification, pharmacological treatment, and hyperbaric oxygen therapy, mainly provide symptomatic control and do not prevent or reverse the underlying disease progression [4–6]. This limitation highlights the need for strategies that directly address the primary drivers of RC.

Accumulating evidence identifies two interrelated determinants in the pathogenesis of RC: disrupted redox homeostasis and abnormal inflammatory signaling [5, 7]. Ionizing radiation induces a significant increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS), which not only produce oxidative injury to cellular components but also function as signaling mediators that sustain inflammatory responses [8, 9]. At the same time, radiation-induced cell death results in the release of considerable amounts of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) into the bladder lumen and mucosal environment [10]. These double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) fragments, particularly after uptake by macrophages, are detected by the cytosolic DNA sensor cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) [11]. Further activation of the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling pathway promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and intensifies tissue damage [12]. A strategy capable of concurrently eliminating ROS and removing pathogenic cfDNA may interrupt this cycle and re-establish tissue homeostasis.

In recent years, self-propelled nanomotors have been developed as advanced platforms for targeted drug delivery and in vivo imaging due to their capacity to convert chemical or physical energy into autonomous motion [13]. This active propulsion offers advantages compared with conventional passive delivery systems, particularly in complex physiological environments [14]. During intravesical administration, the continuous turnover of urine and the mucus barrier covering the bladder wall restrict the penetration and retention of conventional therapeutics [15]. Nanomotors, through self-driven mobility, can achieve wider mucosal distribution and improved tissue penetration, thus improving therapeutic performance.

The clinical application of many nanomotor systems remains constrained by dependence on exogenous or cytotoxic fuels [16]. Systems based on the catalytic decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), for instance, often require fuel concentrations capable of inducing oxidative stress and cellular injury [16, 17]. These limitations have stimulated interest in nanomotors powered by biocompatible endogenous substrates naturally present in the body, including water, glucose, and urea. Among these systems, urease (Ur)-powered nanomotors have been considered particularly suitable for bladder-related applications because of the high and continuous availability of urea in urine [18–20].

To overcome the limitations associated with current RC therapies, a self-propelled nanozyme motor termed Ur-CFNs was developed for the combined removal of cfDNA and ROS. This platform is composed of hollow Ce/Fe/Ni-based microcapsules (CFNs) possessing intrinsic multi-enzyme mimetic activities, with the surface modified by Ur (Scheme 1 A). The Ur component enables the use of endogenous urea as fuel, producing sustained autonomous propulsion after intravesical instillation. This mobility promotes active movement across the bladder mucosa, allowing effective interaction with and removal of cfDNA deposited on the irradiated surface via electrostatic sequestration [19–21]. At the same time, the nanozyme core functions as a ROS scavenger, catalytically decomposing reactive species and reducing oxidative stress. Through the combined processes of motion-enhanced clearance and catalysis-mediated redox regulation, the Ur-CFNs system is designed to inhibit the cGAS-STING inflammatory pathway, limit tissue injury, and support functional recovery (Scheme 1B). In this study, a comprehensive assessment of Ur-CFNs is presented, demonstrating anti-inflammatory activity in both in vitro and in vivo models of RC. These results indicate the potential of this self-propelled nanozyme platform as a therapeutic strategy for inflammatory bladder disorders.

Results

RC induces pathological alterations and elevates cfDNA levels in urine and serum

Cystoscopic examination of healthy controls and patients with RC revealed distinct pathological changes in the latter group, including mucosal congestion, edema, and disrupted vascular architecture (Fig. 1A). Quantification of cfDNA in serum and urine demonstrated significantly higher concentrations in RC patients compared with healthy controls (Fig. 1B).

A mouse model of RC was developed via pelvic irradiation (IR). Histological evaluation using H&E staining showed significant tissue damage in the IR group, including disruption and thinning of the urothelium, whereas control mice showed preserved structural integrity (Fig. 1C). Consistent with clinical observations, urinary and serum cfDNA levels were significantly elevated in irradiated mice (Fig. 1D). Analysis of bladder tissue revealed increased inflammatory and molecular responses. Immunofluorescence staining showed increased expression of TNF-α (Figs. 1E) and IL-1β (1F) in the urothelium of irradiated mice. Radiation exposure also induced oxidative stress, as indicated by elevated ROS levels (Fig. 1G). TUNEL assay confirmed extensive apoptosis in irradiated bladder tissue (Fig. 1H), likely contributing to the elevated cfDNA.

These findings indicate that RC is associated with significant cfDNA release, which amplifies local inflammatory responses, creating a self-sustaining “inflammation–cfDNA” loop. Targeting this positive feedback mechanism may provide an effective strategy for preventing and treating RC.

Fabrication and physicochemical characterization of Ur-CFNs

To enable the concurrent scavenging of ROS and cfDNA for RC therapy, Ce-doped FeNi layered double hydroxide capsules (CFNs) were synthesized using a hydrothermal approach. The resulting CFNs displayed a significant positive surface charge and strong antioxidant activity. These CFNs were then functionalized with Ur to obtain self-propelled Ur-CFNs. The preparation procedure is outlined in Scheme 11 A, with full experimental details provided in the surporting imformation (SI).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images (Fig. 2A and B) revealed a well-defined hollow structure with an approximate diameter of 260 nm, indicated by the clear contrast between the thin layered shell and the empty core. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis confirmed a high specific surface area, supporting the hollow mesoporous architecture of the CFNs (Fig. S1). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping demonstrated a uniform distribution of Ni, Fe, and Ce throughout the CFNs framework. Structural analysis of the CFNs microcapsules was performed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Fig. 2C), which showed characteristic diffraction peaks at 11.8°, 23.1°, 34.6°, and 60.3°, corresponding to the (003), (006), (012), and (110) planes of a hydrotalcite-like phase. XPS analysis further revealed that Ce ions within the CFNs exist as a mixture of Ce (III) and Ce (IV) (Fig. 2D–F and Fig. S2). This mixed-valence state contributes to the intrinsic antioxidant activity of the CFNs. To validate the successful functionalization of CFNs with Ur, comprehensive physicochemical characterizations were performed. Surface charge measurements (Fig. 2G) showed that the zeta potential decreased from highly positive for CFNs to near-neutral after PEGylation and shifted to negative upon Ur conjugation, confirming effective surface modification. Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy (Fig. 2H) revealed a distinct near-UV signal for Ur-CFNs, absent in unmodified CFNs, indicating the presence of chiral Ur molecules on the surface. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 2I) further supported covalent conjugation, with Ur-CFNs displaying characteristic amide bands (C = O stretching at ~1650 cm− 1 and N–H bending at ~1550 cm− 1), consistent with the formation of amide linkages between Ur and CFNs. Enzymatic activity assays (Fig. 2J) confirmed that conjugated Ur retained high catalytic function, essential for the self-propelled motion of Ur-CFNs.

Ur-CFNs show potent cfDNA and ROS scavenging properties

To investigate the surface and interfacial interactions underlying the recognition of cfDNA and ROS by CFNs, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed. A positively charged model of Ce-doped FeNi layered double hydroxides was constructed (Fig. 3A), with nucleotides and H₂O₂ selected as representative units for cfDNA and ROS, respectively. The MD simulations were employed to model the adsorption of cfDNA and H2O2 onto the CFNs surface. The adsorption strength was quantified by interaction energies (EINT), with the optimal CFNs configuration yielding EINT values of −23.3 kcal/mol for cfDNA and −268.3 kcal/mol for H2O2, indicating strong binding affinity for both targets (Fig. 3B and C). The self-propulsion of Ur-CFNs, driven by Ur-catalyzed decomposition of urea into CO2 and NH3, facilitates effective contact with cfDNA and ROS, enabling rapid scavenging. Propulsion performance was assessed in PBS with varying urea concentrations (0, 50, and 100 mM) and in raw urine. Ur-CFNs displayed enhanced Brownian motion in urea-containing media, with motion intensity increasing in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3D). This was supported by linear increases in MSD over time and higher effective diffusion coefficients (Deff) at elevated urea concentrations (Fig. 3E). The robust self-propulsion was also observed in simulated urine. Ur-CFNs were employed as scavengers, achieving near-complete removal of calf thymus DNA (ctDNA, serving as a model for cfDNA) within 4 h. The clearance efficiency of Ur-CFNs was significantly higher in urea-containing solutions compared with urea-free conditions (Fig. 3F). With increasing adsorption time, the surface potential of Ur-CFNs progressively decreased (Fig. 3G), indicating that electrostatic interactions likely dominated the cfDNA removal process. Using the intrinsic antioxidant properties associated with the multivalence of cerium ions, the ROS-scavenging capacity of Ur-CFNs was systematically evaluated using 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) assays. Distinct color transitions were observed, with DPPH changing from violet to light yellow and ABTS from blue to colorless, confirming the radical-scavenging activity of Ur-CFNs (Fig. 3H and I). H2O2 scavenging assays showed a concentration-dependent increase in clearance efficiency, accompanied by visible oxygen evolution (Fig. 3J and K). Qualitative electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy verified that Ur-CFNs effectively neutralized both hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and superoxide anions (·O2⁻) (Fig. 3L and M). Quantitative measurements further demonstrated that the reduction of these free radicals by Ur-CFNs was concentration-dependent (Fig. 3N and O). The enhanced cfDNA and ROS scavenging performance of Ur-CFNs supports their potential as a nanoplatform for catalytic therapy in cystitis.

CFNs show radioprotective effects in vitro

Before investigating their radioprotective activity in vitro, the cytotoxicity of CFNs was evaluated in human urothelial SV-HUC-1 cells. As shown in Fig. 4A, CFNs showed negligible cytotoxicity at concentrations ranging from 10 to 500 µg/mL, indicating favorable biocompatibility. Colony formation assays and survival analysis showed that pretreatment with CFNs significantly increased cell survival compared with the irradiation (IR) group (Fig. 4B and C). Ionizing radiation induces excessive production of ROS, resulting in cellular damage. DCFH-DA fluorescence staining (Fig. 4D) and flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 4E and F) showed a significant increase in ROS levels following radiation exposure, whereas CFNs treatment significantly reduced ROS accumulation. Because radiotherapy can disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), MMP was evaluated using TMRE staining (Fig. 4G). The results (Fig. 4H and I) showed that CFNs significantly preserved MMP, indicating their role in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis. Given that γ-H2AX levels increase following radiation-induced injury [22], its expression was examined using immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analysis (Fig. 4J–L). CFNs treatment significantly reduced γ-H2AX protein levels compared with the IR group. Functional recovery of the cells was further assessed. Transwell migration assay (Fig. 4M) and scratch would healing assay (Fig. 4N) showed that radiation significantly impaired cell migration capacity, whereas CFNs treatment significantly restored this function. Consistent with the reduction in cellular damage, the level of cfDNA released into the culture medium was significantly lower in the Ur-CFNs group than in the IR group (Fig. 4O).

CFNs inhibit cGAS-STING pathway activation and regulate macrophage polarization

TEM confirmed that CFNs were efficiently internalized by SV-HUC-1 cells and localized intracellularly (Fig. 5A). Macrophages detect dsDNA and activate the cGAS–STING signaling pathway, which initiates downstream inflammatory responses. To investigate this mechanism, a co-culture system was established in which PMA-treated THP-1 macrophages were seeded in the upper chambers, while IR or IR + CFNs-treated SV-HUC-1 cells were placed in the lower chambers (Fig. 5B). After 48 h of co-culture, THP-1 macrophages were collected for further analysis. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that co-culture with IR-treated SV-HUC-1 cells significantly increased the expression of P-TBK1, P-STING, and P-IRF3 in THP-1 cells, whereas this elevation was significantly reduced following CFNs treatment (Fig. 5C). Western blot analysis further confirmed that CFNs significantly reduced the elevated expression of key proteins associated with activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway (Fig. 5D). Activation of the cGAS-STING pathway promotes macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, which initiates downstream inflammatory responses [23]. THP-1 macrophages co-cultured with IR- or H2O2-treated SV-HUC-1 cells displayed a typical M1-polarized morphology, characterized by a flattened and irregular shape with pseudopodia formation. CFNs treatment efficiently reversed these morphological alterations (Fig. 5E). Flow cytometric analysis showed that SV-HUC-1 cells exposed to IR or H2O2 significantly promoted macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype, accompanied by a reduction in the proportion of M2 macrophages. In comparison, CFNs treatment reversed this pattern and supported the maintenance of the M2 phenotype in macrophages (Fig. 5F). Consistent with these observations, ELISA results showed that CFNs significantly decreased the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) by macrophages, while increasing the level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 5G). These findings indicate that CFNs suppress cfDNA-induced activation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway in macrophages, therefore limiting the inflammatory cascade.

Ur modification enhances intravesical CFN retention

In intravesical instillation therapy, unmodified nanoparticles often show limited residence time in the bladder due to frequent voiding and periodic urine clearance. To overcome this limitation, CFNs were functionalized with Ur (Ur-CFNs) to increase their coverage and permeability on the bladder mucosa. Comparative biodistribution studies in mouse bladders showed that, based on ex vivo fluorescence imaging using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) and quantitative analysis, the Ur-CFNs group displayed significantly higher fluorescence intensity than the CFNs group at both 1 h and 6 h post-instillation (Fig. 6B and Fig. S3). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and quantitative analysis revealed marked differences in micro-distribution, with Ur-CFNs displaying significantly higher retention than CFNs (Fig. S4). These findings demonstrate that Ur modification significantly enhances the intravesical adhesion and retention of CFNs.

Ur-CFNs effectively mitigate radiation-induced cystitis in vivo

The therapeutic potential of Ur-CFNs was assessed via intravesical instillation in a murine RC model (Fig. 6A). No significant differences in body weight were observed between irradiated and treatment groups (Fig. S5). H&E staining revealed significant urothelial damage in the irradiated (IR) group, whereas Ur-CFNs preserved bladder tissue integrity, maintaining morphology comparable to normal bladders (Fig. 6C). TUNEL (Figs. 6D) and DHE (6E and Fig. S6) staining showed that Ur-CFNs significantly reduced irradiation-induced apoptosis and ROS accumulation in bladder epithelial cells. Ur-CFNs also alleviated inflammation and DNA damage, indicated by decreased myeloperoxidase (MPO) (Fig. 6F) and γ-H2AX expression (Fig. 6G and Fig. S7). Ur-CFNs also reduced M1 macrophage marker CD86 while enhancing M2 marker CD206 expression, indicating a shift from M1 to M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 6H). Immunofluorescence of P-STING (Fig. 6I and Fig. S8) and Western blot analyses (Fig. 6J–M) revealed significant downregulation of P-TBK1, P-IRF3, and P-STING in the IR + Ur-CFNs group, indicating inhibition of cGAS-STING pathway activation. Consistent with previous observations, Ur-CFNs significantly attenuated radiation-induced urinary cfDNA elevation (Fig. 6O) and decreased key pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) in bladder tissues, outperforming unmodified CFNs (Fig. 6P–R). Ur-CFNs show increased antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and tissue-protective effects in vivo, effectively ameliorating RC.

Transcriptomic sequencing analysis reveals the therapeutic mechanism of Ur-CFNs

To investigate the molecular basis of Ur-CFNs’ efficacy, RNA sequencing was performed on bladder tissues from IR and IR + Ur-CFNs groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated that Ur-CFNs treatment significantly altered the gene expression profile of irradiated tissues, confirming clear sample segregation and supporting the robustness of downstream analyses (Fig. 7A). Differential expression analysis identified 1,176 upregulated and 1,169 downregulated genes in the IR + Ur-CFNs group relative to the IR group (Fig. 7B and C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were mainly associated with immune and inflammatory functions, including “immune response”, “inflammatory response”, “chemokine activity”, and “cytokine activity” (Fig. 7D). KEGG pathway analysis indicated significant enrichment in inflammatory signaling pathways, such as the “TNF signaling pathway”, “NF-kappa B signaling pathway”, and the “Cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway” (Fig. 7E), consistent with previous observations implicating modulation of the cGAS-STING pathway.

RNA sequencing reveals the therapeutic mechanism of Ur-CFNs. (A) PCA analysis, n = 3. (B, C) Heatmap and volcano plot of DEGs between IR and IR + Ur-CFNs groups. (D, E) GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of DEGs. (F–M) GSEA for GO terms and KEGG pathways

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was further applied to confirm the directionality of these pathway changes. The analysis revealed that gene sets associated with immune response (Fig. 7F), cytokine activity (Fig. 7G), activation of innate immune response (Fig. 7H) and chemokine activity (Fig. 7I) showed significantly negative Normalized Enrichment Scores (NES). The critical pro-inflammatory pathways, including TNF (Fig. 7J), Cytosolic DNA-sensing (Fig. 7K), Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Fig. 7L) and NF-kappa B (Fig. 7M), were significantly suppressed in the Ur-CFNs treatment group. These findings indicate that Ur-CFNs mitigate RC primarily through inhibition of the cGAS-STING pathway and its downstream inflammatory signaling networks.

Ur-CFNs show potent preventive effects against RC

Mice received prophylactic Ur-CFNs administration for three days before irradiation to evaluate preventive efficacy (Fig. 8A). H&E staining showed that Ur-CFNs effectively preserved bladder tissue architecture against radiation-induced damage (Fig. 8B). Prophylactic treatment also significantly reduced ROS accumulation (Fig. 8C and Fig S9), γ-H2AX (Fig. 8D), and MPO levels (Fig. 8E) and inhibited p-STING expression (Fig. 8F and Fig S10). Urinary cfDNA levels were significantly lower in the Ur-CFNs group compared to the CFNs group (Fig. 8G). ELISA analyses further demonstrated that pretreatment with Ur-CFNs suppressed the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (Fig. 8H–J). These findings demonstrate that Ur-CFNs possess potent prophylactic activity against RC.

Ur-CFNs loaded with dexamethasone (DXMS) show enhanced therapeutic efficacy

DXMS is widely used clinically to manage radiation-induced injuries, but direct intravesical instillation is limited by the bladder’s urine storage and voiding functions, resulting in short contact time and low local bioavailability. To overcome this, DXMS was loaded onto Ur-CFNs to prolong bladder retention and increase local drug concentration, and the therapeutic effects of different formulations were compared (Fig. 8K). H&E staining revealed that, although Ur-CFNs alone mitigated radiation-induced bladder epithelial damage, the Ur-CFNs/DXMS formulation provided superior protection (Fig. 8L). Immunofluorescence analyses showed that apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses were further reduced in the Ur-CFNs/DXMS group relative to Ur-CFNs alone (Fig. 8M–O and Fig. S11-S13). Furthermore, Ur-CFNs/DXMS more effectively promoted M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 8P). These findings indicate that DXMS loading onto Ur-CFNs improves therapeutic efficacy, offering an improved strategy for treating RC.

A comprehensive assessment of the potential toxicity of Ur-CFNs was performed. H&E staining conducted on day 30 post-treatment showed no detectable tissue damage or histopathological alterations in the major organs of healthy mice (Fig. S14). Additionally, no obvious changes in hematological and biochemical parameters were observed (Fig. S15). These findings indicate that Ur-CFNs exert strong therapeutic effects against RC while maintaining a favorable biosafety profile.

Discussion and Conclusion

RC remains a significant clinical challenge due to the lack of mechanism-driven therapeutic strategies. In this study, a self-propelled nanozyme motor (Ur-CFNs) was engineered to exploit the urea-rich microenvironment of the bladder as a bioavailable fuel source. This design enabled active mucosal penetration and prolonged intravesical retention. This system concurrently targets two major pathogenic mediators, cfDNA and ROS. By interrupting the inflammation–cfDNA feedback loop at its origin, this dual-functional platform demonstrates significant therapeutic and prophylactic efficacy in preclinical models of RC.

The pathophysiology of RC is characterized by a self-sustaining inflammatory loop, wherein radiation-induced cellular damage leads to the release of cfDNA [24, 25]. The cfDNA then activates the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, promoting further inflammation and tissue injury [26]. The present findings demonstrate that cfDNA levels are markedly increased in both patients and murine models following pelvic irradiation, with a strong association to tissue damage and elevated inflammatory cytokine expression. This is consistent with accumulating evidence identifying cfDNA as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) in diverse inflammatory and autoimmune disorders [27, 28]. The reciprocal amplification between tissue injury and cfDNA-mediated inflammatory signaling indicates that targeted clearance of cfDNA may serve as a rational therapeutic strategy.

Conventional nanomedicines intended for intravesical delivery encounter a major biological barrier, as continuous urine production and voiding result in rapid dilution and clearance of therapeutic agents, limiting their interaction with the affected mucosa [29, 30]. Our Ur-driven design addresses this constraint by utilizing endogenous urea as a fuel source. This enables Ur-CFNs to show autonomous propulsion, leading to enhanced surface distribution across the bladder and improved penetration into the urothelium. The significantly extended retention observed in vivo, with Ur-CFNs displaying higher fluorescence intensity at 6 h post-instillation relative to non-motile CFNs, highlights the advantage of active propulsion in intravesical applications. This design strategy may apply to a broader range of bladder disorders, including interstitial cystitis, bladder cancer, and recurrent urinary tract infections, where sustained interaction between therapeutic agents and the mucosal surface is essential for effective treatment [31]. Following their therapeutic action, Ur-CFNs in the bladder are eliminated via urinary excretion.

The dual functionality of Ur-CFNs underpins their therapeutic mechanism. The positively charged surface enables electrostatic sequestration of negatively charged cfDNA, while the cerium-doped multivalent nanozyme core mediates sustained ROS scavenging via reversible redox cycling between Ce3+ and Ce4+ states. This combined functionality is particularly effective in the context of radiation-induced injury, where oxidative stress and DAMP release act in parallel to sustain inflammation. Concurrent targeting of these factors allows Ur-CFNs to disrupt the inflammatory cascade at multiple levels, as supported by the significant attenuation of cGAS-STING pathway activation and downstream cytokine expression [32–34]. Transcriptomic analysis further demonstrated that Ur-CFNs treatment resulted in broad downregulation of gene sets associated with innate immune responses, cytokine activity, and cytosolic DNA-sensing pathways, providing mechanistic support for the observed biochemical and histopathological outcomes.

Macrophage polarization was identified as a central effector in Ur-CFNs-mediated immunomodulation. The cGAS-STING pathway is recognized as a key regulator of macrophage phenotype, with persistent STING activation driving pro-inflammatory M1 polarization [35]. Co-culture experiments showed that cfDNA released from irradiated urothelial cells strongly induced M1 polarization in macrophages, an effect that was effectively reversed following Ur-CFNs treatment. This transition from an M1 to an M2 phenotype was accompanied by decreased production of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, along with increased secretion of IL-10, reflecting a cytokine profile associated with tissue repair and resolution of inflammation. In vivo, the predominance of M2 macrophages in Ur-CFNs-treated bladder tissues was associated with reduced tissue injury, lower levels of apoptosis, and preservation of urothelial structure, indicating that macrophage reprogramming constitutes a key therapeutic mechanism.

The versatility of Ur-CFNs as a drug delivery platform was further demonstrated through their capacity to enhance the efficacy of DXMS, a clinically established corticosteroid with well-defined anti-inflammatory properties but limited intravesical bioavailability [36]. By extending bladder retention and promoting deeper tissue penetration, Ur-CFNs markedly improved the therapeutic performance of DXMS, resulting in near-complete preservation of bladder architecture at radiation doses that induced severe damage in untreated controls. This synergistic interaction underscores the potential of integrating propulsion-enabled delivery systems with conventional pharmacological agents to achieve improved therapeutic outcomes, while enabling dose reduction and limiting systemic exposure.

An unanticipated yet clinically relevant finding was the prophylactic efficacy of Ur-CFNs. Pre-instillation over three consecutive days before irradiation significantly reduced the tissue injury, inflammatory responses, and cfDNA release. These results suggest that Ur-CFNs may precondition the bladder mucosa to better tolerate radiation exposure, potentially by enhancing intrinsic antioxidant defenses or by promoting a regulatory phenotype in tissue-resident macrophages [33, 37]. Translationally, prophylactic administration could offer significant benefit for patients scheduled to undergo pelvic radiotherapy, providing a strategy aimed at preventing, rather than solely treating, RC.

In conclusion, this study shows that Ur-CFNs, a Ur-functionalized, self-propelled nanozyme platform, effectively mitigates radiation-induced cystitis through dual mechanisms of cfDNA sequestration and ROS scavenging. Ur-CFNs attenuate cGAS-STING pathway activation, reprogram macrophages from a pro-inflammatory M1 to a reparative M2 phenotype, and reduce oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inflammatory cytokine production in bladder tissues. Loading Ur-CFNs with DXMS further enhances therapeutic efficacy by prolonging bladder retention and increasing local drug bioavailability. The prophylactic administration of Ur-CFNs before irradiation provides significant protection against bladder injury, highlighting their potential as a preventive strategy. Collectively, these findings establish Ur-CFNs as a safe and potent nanotherapeutic approach for both the prevention and treatment of RC, with promising translational implications for patients undergoing pelvic radiotherapy.
