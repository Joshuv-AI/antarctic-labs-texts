Impact of perioperative glucocorticoids on postoperative recurrence and complications in chronic subdural hematoma: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yangbin Xia, Xuchen Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic subdural hematoma (CSDH) is a common neurosurgical condition in older adults and is characterized by a high postoperative recurrence rate. The impact of systemic glucocorticoids administered perioperatively as an adjunct to surgical evacuation for CSDH remains uncertain.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials from inception to 30 September 2025 for randomized controlled trials comparing glucocorticoids with placebo as we included randomized controlled trials comparing perioperative systemic glucocorticoids versus placebo as an adjunct to surgery for CSDH. Eligible studies enrolled adult patients (≥ 18 years) with radiologically confirmed CSDH. Relative risks (RRs) were calculated to assess clinical outcomes.ResultsSix randomized controlled trials involving a total of 1,374 patients were included. Compared with placebo, adjuvant perioperative glucocorticoid therapy significantly reduced the risk of postoperative recurrence of CSDH (RR = 0.39; 95% CI: 0.24–0.63; I2 = 0%; P < 0.01). Notably, glucocorticoid therapy was associated with a significantly higher incidence of psychiatric symptoms (RR = 2.94; 95% CI: 1.65–5.23; I2 = 4%; P < 0.01). However, glucocorticoid use did not increase the risk of postoperative infection (RR = 1.93; 95% CI: 0.75–4.96; I2 = 59%; P = 0.17), and there was no significant difference in mortality between the two groups (RR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.00–2.54; I2 = 0%; P = 0.05).ConclusionsPerioperative glucocorticoid therapy appears to reduce postoperative recurrence of chronic subdural hematoma. No clear increase in infection was observed, while the evidence for mortality remains imprecise and cannot exclude potential harm. However, glucocorticoids were associated with a higher risk of psychiatric symptoms compared with placebo. Clinicians should balance the benefit of reduced recurrence against the increased likelihood of psychiatric adverse events when considering glucocorticoids as adjuvant therapy in patients with CSDH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00701-026-06869-5.Keywords: Chronic subdural hematoma, Glucocorticoids, Dexamethasone, Recurrence

Introduction

Chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH) is a chronic space-occupying lesion caused by the accumulation of blood between the arachnoid and dura mater. It typically develops more than three weeks after head trauma, with an incidence ranging from 1/100,000 to 13.1/100,000 in the general population. With population aging and the increasing use of anticoagulant and antiplatelet agents, the incidence of CSDH continues to rise, reaching 127/100,000 among individuals older than 80 years [8, 10].

For patients with symptomatic CSDH, burr-hole craniostomy with subdural drainage is generally considered the first-line treatment. Surgical evacuation can markedly improve neurological function; however, postoperative recurrence still occurs in approximately 10%–25% of patients [2, 15, 28]. The increasing incidence and substantial recurrence rate of CSDH highlight the need for a better understanding of its pathophysiology and for optimization of treatment strategies.

In recent years, the management of CSDH has expanded beyond surgery alone. Several pharmacological agents, including atorvastatin calcium [7], glucocorticoids [5], and tranexamic acid [14, 16], have been introduced into clinical practice as adjunctive therapies. Glucocorticoids exert their effects mainly by suppressing the recruitment and activation of immune and inflammatory cells, inhibiting phagocytosis and the release of inflammatory mediators, and thereby non-specifically attenuating the inflammatory response and limiting tissue damage. In neurosurgical patients, including those with CSDH, perioperative glucocorticoids such as dexamethasone may be selectively administered as replacement therapy or as adjuvant symptomatic treatment, depending on the clinical scenario.

As early as the 1960 s, investigators abroad applied high-dose dexamethasone in the clinical management of CSDH [3]. Subsequent reports suggested that dexamethasone might promote hematoma resolution and reduce postoperative recurrence in patients with CSDH [20, 25]. However, these studies used heterogeneous treatment courses and dexamethasone doses, and their findings have not been robustly confirmed by high-quality evidence-based research.

Although some clinical trials and systematic reviews have suggested that glucocorticoids may be effective in the treatment of CSDH, meta-analyses based exclusively on randomized controlled trials (RCTs) remain limited. Consequently, the efficacy and safety of adjuvant perioperative glucocorticoid therapy for preventing recurrence and improving outcomes perioperative around surgery for CSDH have not been fully established. Therefore, we conducted a meta-analysis of RCTs to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of perioperative systemic glucocorticoids administered as an adjunct to surgery in patients with CSDH.

Methods

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria:(1) Adult patients (≥ 18 years) with chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH) confirmed by imaging or intraoperative findings;(2) The intervention group received systemic glucocorticoids (intravenous or oral) as perioperative adjuvant therapy in addition to surgery, while the control group received placebo;(3) The study reported clearly defined clinical outcome measures;(4) Randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if:(1) They were not RCTs;(2) Glucocorticoids were not used systemically as adjuvant therapy, or glucocorticoids were used only as conservative treatment without surgery; Trials comparing glucocorticoid monotherapy with surgery (i.e., not adjunct to surgery) were excluded. (3) The full text was unavailable. The current meta-analysis study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) online database, under the registration number CRD420251153216.

Definition of perioperative

Perioperative systemic glucocorticoid therapy was defined a priori as systemic (oral or intravenous) glucocorticoids initiated within 72 h before surgical evacuation up to 72 h after surgery, administered as an adjunct to surgery (i.e., both groups underwent surgery; glucocorticoids vs placebo/no glucocorticoids), regardless of subsequent taper duration.

Outcomes

Primary outcome: Recurrence requiring repeat surgery (reoperation) at the longest reported follow-up. Secondary outcomes: overall complications, postoperative infection, psychiatric symptoms, and mortality. When outcome definitions varied across trials, we prioritized clinically comparable definitions and extracted data at the longest follow-up reported.

For outcome harmonization, recurrence was restricted to reoperation-confirmed recurrence whenever possible. Overall complications were defined as the number of patients experiencing ≥ 1 complication as reported by trial investigators (trial-defined). Postoperative infection was defined as any infection event reported during follow-up; when a trial reported only surgical-site infection, that endpoint was used for pooling. Psychiatric symptoms were extracted as trial-defined events (e.g., delirium, psychosis, agitation or other psychiatric symptoms). In addition, steroid-related adverse events of special interest (e.g. diabetic control deterioration, gastrointestinal bleeding) were extracted when available and summarized descriptively. Detailed trial-specific definitions and components are provided in Appendix 3.

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from inception to 30 September 2025. The following keywords and their combinations were used: “chronic subdural hematoma”, “Subdural Hematoma, Chronic”, “dexamethasone”, “glucocorticoid”, and “corticosteroid”. The full electronic search strategies for PubMed, Embase, and CENTRAL (including the complete search strings, date last searched, and any limits) are provided in Supplementary Appendix 1. No language restrictions were applied, and duplicates were removed using Zotero followed by manual checking.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias for each included study was assessed using RevMan software (version 5.4). Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials. We judged bias across key domains (random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other bias). Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Domain-level judgments with supporting justifications for each trial are provided in Appendix 2.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using RevMan software (version 5.4). For dichotomous outcomes, relative risks (RRs) and risk differences (RDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated as measures of effect. To enhance clinical interpretability, the number needed to harm (NNH) was calculated for significant safety outcomes. Heterogeneity among studies was qualitatively assessed using Cochran’s Q test and quantitatively using the I2 statistic. An I2 ≥ 50% was considered to indicate substantial heterogeneity. Given the anticipated clinical heterogeneity across the included trials regarding corticosteroid regimens (e.g., dosage, tapering duration) and patient characteristics, a random-effects model (Mantel–Haenszel method) was uniformly applied for all meta-analyses, regardless of statistical heterogeneity measures (I2). We considered this approach more rigorous as it accounts for both within-study and between-study variance, providing a more conservative estimate of the pooled treatment effect. Sensitivity analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and to assess the robustness of the results by excluding individual studies. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We prespecified a random-effects model for all outcomes because substantial clinical heterogeneity was expected across trials (steroid agent, dose, taper duration, timing, and follow-up), and statistical heterogeneity metrics may be underpowered with few studies. Fixed-effect analyses were explored as sensitivity analyses. We extracted intention-to-treat data when available; otherwise, we used the data as reported. When a trial reported zero events in one arm, RevMan’s default continuity correction (0.5) was applied; trials with zero events in both arms were not estimable for RR and were excluded from that pooled estimate but described qualitatively. For time-dependent outcomes, we used the longest follow-up reported in each trial; sensitivity analyses by follow-up window were considered but limited by data availability.

Results

A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials up to 30 September 2025 initially identified 300 records related to the topic of interest. One additional eligible study was identified through manual searching of journal websites. After removal of duplicates and screening of titles and abstracts, 82 articles were retrieved for full-text review. Of these, 20 observational studies, 9 study protocols, 7 reviews, 3 studies with incompatible outcome measures, and 37 studies with incompatible interventions were excluded. Ultimately, 6 RCTs comparing glucocorticoids with placebo as adjuvant therapy in patients with CSDH were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1) [4, 12, 13, 17, 19, 26].

Meta-analysis results

Risk of recurrence requiring reoperation

Among the six included studies, heterogeneity was negligible, with I2 = 0% (< 50%) and a Q test P value of 0.59 (> 0.1), indicating no statistically significant heterogeneity across trials. However, consistent with our statistical methodology to account for potential clinical heterogeneity regarding corticosteroid regimens, a random-effects model was applied. The pooled RR for postoperative recurrence requiring reoperation was 0.39 (95% CI, 0.24–0.63), with a statistically significant overall effect (Z = 3.88, P = 0.0001). Across studies reporting reoperation for recurrence, events occurred in 3.3% in the glucocorticoid group and 9.3% in the placebo group, corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 6.0% (RD = − 5.99%), or an NNT of approximately 17 to prevent one reoperation. These findings suggest that perioperative glucocorticoid therapy is effective in reducing the risk of postoperative recurrence of CSDH. The detailed results are presented in the corresponding forest plot (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of the risk of recurrence requiring reoperation

Complications

Across the included trials, substantial heterogeneity was observed for overall complications (I2 = 62%; Q test P = 0.02). Therefore, a random-effects model was applied. The pooled analysis showed that perioperative glucocorticoids were associated with a higher risk of overall complications (RR = 1.72; 95% CI, 1.24–2.40). Across studies reporting overall complications, events occurred in 31.6% in the glucocorticoid group and 16.5% in the placebo group, yielding an absolute risk increase of 15.1% (RD = + 15.14%), corresponding to an NNH of approximately 7 for one additional complication.

To explore the source of heterogeneity, we performed sensitivity analyses. A leave-one-out analysis indicated that Hutchinson et al. (2020) [12] contributed most to between-study heterogeneity. After excluding this trial, heterogeneity was no longer evident (I2 = 0%; P = 0.95), and the direction of effect remained consistent (RR = 1.46; 95% CI, 1.19–1.80), suggesting that the increased complication risk associated with glucocorticoids was robust. Differences in complication definitions, ascertainment, and reporting across trials may partly explain the observed heterogeneity. The detailed results are shown in the corresponding forest plot (Fig. 5).

Forest plot of complications

Psychiatric symptoms

Four studies reported data on psychiatric symptoms. Heterogeneity across these trials was minimal (I2 = 4%; Q test P = 0.37), indicating no statistically significant heterogeneity. In accordance with our prespecified approach, a random-effects model (Mantel–Haenszel method) was applied for the meta-analysis. The pooled RR for psychiatric symptoms was 2.94 (95% CI, 1.65–5.23), with a statistically significant overall effect (Z = 3.66, P = 0.0003). Across the four studies reporting psychiatric symptoms, events occurred in 9.4% in the glucocorticoid group and 3.0% in the placebo group, corresponding to an absolute risk increase of 6.5% (RD = + 6.46%), or an NNH of approximately 16 for one additional psychiatric adverse event. These findings suggest that perioperative systemic glucocorticoid therapy as an adjuvant treatment is associated with an increased risk of psychiatric symptoms. The detailed results are shown in the corresponding forest plot (Fig. 6).

Forest plot of psychiatric symptoms

Infections

For postoperative infection, six studies were included. Moderate heterogeneity was observed (Tau2 = 0.69; Chi2 = 12.10, df = 5, P = 0.03; I2 = 59%), therefore a random-effects model was applied. The pooled analysis did not show a statistically significant difference between groups (RR = 1.93, 95% CI 0.75–4.96; Z = 1.36, P = 0.17). Across studies reporting postoperative infection, events occurred in 7.1% in the glucocorticoid group and 3.8% in the placebo group, an absolute risk increase of 3.3% (RD = + 3.25%), corresponding to an NNH of approximately 31; however, this estimate is uncertain given the imprecision of the pooled effect. In absolute terms, infections occurred in 48 of 677 patients (7.1%) in the glucocorticoid group and 26 of 677 (3.8%) in the control group. Although the point estimate suggests a higher infection risk with glucocorticoids, the confidence interval is wide and includes no effect, indicating substantial uncertainty. Differences in infection definitions, ascertainment, follow-up duration, and steroid regimens may contribute to the observed heterogeneity. The detailed results are shown in the corresponding forest plot (Fig. 7).

Forest plot of infection

Mortality

Five studies reported data on mortality. Heterogeneity across these trials was negligible (I2 = 0%; Q test P = 0.94), indicating no statistically significant heterogeneity. However, consistent with our overall statistical strategy to account for potential clinical heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied. The pooled RR for mortality was 1.59 (95% CI, 1.00–2.54), with an overall effect of Z = 1.94 and a P value of 0.05. Across studies reporting mortality, deaths occurred in 7.1% in the glucocorticoid group and 4.5% in the placebo group, an absolute risk increase of 2.7% (RD = + 2.66%), corresponding to an NNH of approximately 38; nevertheless, the pooled estimate was imprecise (95% CI touches 1.00) and should be interpreted cautiously, as clinically important harm cannot be excluded. Although this result lies on the threshold of statistical significance, the confidence interval touching 1.00 suggests that while perioperative glucocorticoid therapy is not definitively associated with an increased risk of death, a potential trend toward higher mortality cannot be ruled out. The detailed results are shown in the corresponding forest plot (Fig. 8).

Discussion

In this meta-analysis of six RCTs involving 1,374 patients with CSDH, we found that perioperative glucocorticoid therapy significantly reduced the risk of postoperative recurrence compared with placebo (RR = 0.39; 95% CI: 0.24–0.63; I2 = 0%; P < 0.01). Postoperative recurrence rates in CSDH have been reported to be as high as 10%–25%, and recurrence usually necessitates reoperation, which not only increases healthcare costs but also exposes patients to an additional risk of neurological deterioration. Our findings suggest that adjunctive glucocorticoid therapy may meaningfully mitigate this clinical burden.

However, the addition of glucocorticoids was associated with an increased risk of psychiatric symptoms (RR = 2.94; 95% CI: 1.65–5.23; I2 = 4%; P < 0.01), including anxiety, depression, cognitive impairment, and manic or delirium-like manifestations. Although these symptoms are often reversible and tend to resolve after dose reduction or discontinuation of therapy, they can transiently impair quality of life, particularly in elderly patients or those with pre-existing psychiatric disorders or cognitive decline. In such patients, careful baseline assessment of mental status (e.g. using the MMSE and anxiety scales), close monitoring during treatment, and early recognition of subtle behavioral changes are warranted to avoid missing emerging psychiatric complications.

By contrast, we did not detect a statistically significant increase in postoperative infection (RR = 1.93; 95% CI: 0.75–4.96; I2 = 59%; P = 0.17). For mortality, however, the pooled estimate was imprecise and lay at the conventional threshold of statistical significance (RR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.00–2.54; I2 = 0%; P = 0.05). Therefore, while a clear increase in death was not demonstrated, clinically important harm cannot be excluded, and this finding should be interpreted cautiously. In clinical practice, glucocorticoids are used cautiously in neurosurgical patients because of concerns regarding immunosuppression, infection, and systemic decompensation; our results suggest that short-term perioperative regimens, as used in the included trials, do not show a definitive signal for increased infection, but the uncertainty around mortality warrants careful patient selection and monitoring.

Our mortality estimate is broadly consistent in direction and magnitude with the meta-analysis by Zhao et al. [29], who reported a higher mortality risk in the glucocorticoid group (RR = 1.63; 95% CI: 1.02–2.61; I2 = 0%; P = 0.04). The slight difference in statistical significance across meta-analyses is likely attributable to differences in included trials, follow-up horizons, and the instability of effect estimates when events are relatively infrequent. We applied a prespecified random-effects model to maintain a consistent analytic approach across outcomes and to account for expected clinical heterogeneity in steroid regimens and perioperative management. When statistical heterogeneity is minimal, fixed-effect and random-effects models typically yield very similar point estimates; thus, emphasis should be placed on the confidence interval and clinical plausibility rather than dichotomizing results by whether P falls just above or below 0.05. Moreover, the very wide confidence interval in the Tariq and Bhatti trial [26] (RR = 1.00; 95% CI: 0.06–15.51) reflects sparse events and contributes to imprecision in the pooled mortality estimate.

Moreover, most patients with CSDH are elderly and frequently have substantial comorbidities. Mortality in this population is often driven by underlying cardiovascular disease, infections, and multiorgan failure rather than CSDH itself. In the present meta-analysis, the perioperative glucocorticoid regimens were generally short in duration and of low-to-moderate dose, which may not have been sufficient to induce a marked increase in infection-related mortality. At the same time, the anti-inflammatory effects of glucocorticoids could potentially alleviate perihematomal edema and intracranial mass effect, partially offsetting any potential harm. Taken together, these factors may partly explain why the pooled estimate was imprecise and did not provide a definitive conclusion regarding mortality. Nevertheless, the P value of 0.05 for mortality lies at the conventional threshold of statistical significance and should be interpreted with caution. For CSDH patients with poor baseline status, such as those with severe cardiopulmonary disease or significant immunosuppression, the potential risk of death should not be overlooked when considering steroid therapy, and close monitoring of hemodynamics and infectious signs is essential. In patients with relatively preserved general condition, mortality may not be the primary concern, but decisions should still integrate recurrence risk, psychiatric adverse events, and other clinically relevant outcomes.

From a pathophysiological perspective, the mechanisms underlying the development, progression, and resolution of CSDH are not yet fully understood. Traditional hypotheses have emphasized bridging vein rupture, increased osmotic pressure, repeated bleeding from the neomembrane, and local hyperfibrinolysis, yet no single mechanism fully accounts for the clinical course of CSDH [6, 11]. Recent evidence suggests that trauma and other insults can lead to the accumulation of blood and/or cerebrospinal fluid in the subdural space, triggering the local release of inflammatory cytokines and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [6]. These mediators promote the proliferation of fragile neocapillaries within the hematoma membrane, endothelial injury, disruption of tight junctions, and increased vascular permeability, thereby facilitating persistent leakage of plasma components and progressive hematoma enlargement.

At the same time, patients with CSDH may exhibit insufficient levels or functional impairment of key anti-inflammatory and reparative cell populations, such as regulatory T cells (Treg) and endothelial progenitor cells (EPC), leading to a vicious cycle of “immature neovascularization–endothelial damage–vascular leakage” in the hematoma wall [9, 11, 23, 24]. Other studies have demonstrated that dysregulated immune responses, impaired vascular repair and maturation, and dysfunction of the dural lymphatic system all play important roles in the initiation and progression of CSDH [21, 22, 27]. Glucocorticoids, as potent anti-inflammatory and antiallergic agents, may interrupt this pathological cascade through multiple mechanisms. They can inhibit the recruitment of inflammatory cells such as neutrophils and macrophages, reduce the release of inflammatory mediators (e.g. IL-6 and TNF-α), and attenuate the inflammatory response surrounding the hematoma. In addition, glucocorticoids can downregulate VEGF expression, suppress neovascularization, stabilize the endothelial barrier, and reduce exudation, ultimately lowering the risk of hematoma persistence and recurrence [1, 7]. On the other hand, glucocorticoids can modulate central neurotransmitter systems, activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, and act directly on emotion-related brain regions such as the prefrontal cortex and amygdala, which may explain the increased risk of psychiatric symptoms observed with perioperative steroid use. Although not included in our meta-analysis due to its design comparing conservative treatment versus surgery, the recent large-scale DECSA trial by Miah et al. (2023) provides compelling supportive evidence regarding safety [18]. They reported that dexamethasone monotherapy was associated with significantly higher rates of complications (59% vs. 32%) and adverse events compared to surgery, including delirium and hyperglycemia. These findings align with our meta-analysis results, reinforcing the conclusion that dexamethasone carries a substantial burden of side effects.

Clinically, for patients at high risk of recurrence—such as the elderly, those with bilateral hematomas, or those receiving long-term anticoagulant therapy—short-course, standardized tapering regimens of perioperative glucocorticoids may provide meaningful benefit in reducing recurrence, provided that adverse events are actively monitored and managed, particularly psychiatric symptoms. Conversely, in patients with a history of psychiatric disorders or significant cognitive impairment, glucocorticoid use as an adjuvant therapy should be approached with greater caution. In such cases, a careful risk–benefit assessment is mandatory, and alternative adjunctive strategies may be preferable if the risk of psychiatric decompensation is deemed high.

This study has several limitations. First, there were methodological and reporting differences among the included trials. Inclusion criteria, follow-up durations, and definitions of primary endpoints were not fully consistent, which may have introduced clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Second, despite systematic searches of multiple databases, only six RCTs ultimately met the inclusion criteria. Third, the assessment of publication bias was constrained by the limited number of included studies (n = 6). Formal statistical tests for small-study effects, such as funnel plot asymmetry or Egger’s regression, are generally not recommended when fewer than 10 studies are included due to insufficient statistical power. We attempted to mitigate this risk by performing a comprehensive search of major databases and checking clinical trial registries (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov) to identify unpublished or ongoing trials. Nevertheless, the possibility of publication bias—where smaller trials with negative or non-significant results are less likely to be published—cannot be completely excluded, and our pooled estimates should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Furthermore, a few large trials contributed substantial weight to the pooled estimates, which may have influenced the stability of the results. Therefore, additional high-quality, multicenter, large-sample RCTs are needed to provide more robust evidence regarding the effects of perioperative glucocorticoid use on postoperative recurrence and complications in CSDH.

In summary, perioperative glucocorticoid therapy appears to have promising clinical potential as an adjuvant treatment for patients with CSDH. It significantly reduces the risk of postoperative recurrence without a clear increase in infection, however, the evidence for mortality remains imprecise and cannot exclude clinically important harm. However, clinicians should remain vigilant regarding steroid-related adverse events, particularly psychiatric complications, and should carefully individualize treatment decisions based on each patient’s risk profile and comorbidities.
