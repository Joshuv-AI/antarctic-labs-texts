Exploring phenotypic and genotypic diversity among methicillin-resistant, vancomycin-resistant, and sensitive Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Walid Bakeer, Marwa Gaafar, Ahmed O. El-Gendy, M. A. El Badry, Noaf Abdullah N. Alblwi, Heba M. R. M. Selim, Nada K. Alharbi, Mahmoud M. Bendary
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a global concern owing to the increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains. Vancomycin has been the primary treatment for MRSA; however, Vancomycin-resistant strains are being increasingly reported worldwide. Therefore, comparative studies are essential to support antimicrobial stewardship and improving clinical management. Ultimately, the findings from this study are expected to inform treatment strategies and guide public health interventions effectively.Material and methods:This study investigated the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance, and virulence characteristics of Vancomycin-sensitive S. aureus (VSSA) and Vancomycin-resistant S. aureus (VRSA) within MRSA strains. By employing a combination of phenotypic methods, such as antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and genotypic techniques, including molecular typing and identification of virulence genes, we obtained comprehensive insights into VRSA and VSSA profiles.Results:Of 250 clinical samples, 62 (24.8%) were S. aureus and 27 (43.5%) were identified as MRSA. All MRSA isolates exhibited MDR patterns. Most MRSA strains were VSSA (20/27, 74.1%), while 7 (25.9%) were VRSA. The VRSA isolates showed more antimicrobial resistance than VSSA isolates; however, the VRSA isolates had less virulence than VSSA isolates. Linezolid was the most effective treatment, with a 3.7% resistance rate. A higher percentage of biofilm-producing MRSA (96.3%) was confirmed by both phenotypic and genotypic methods. All isolates, except one VRSA, showed multi-virulence patterns (harbored more than 3 virulence genes). High diversity and low clonality (D-value = 0.99) were found in both VSSA and VRSA. Based on our correlation findings, the emergence of vancomycin resistance could modify the association between antimicrobial resistance and virulence, potentially affecting the pathogenic profile of these strains. The study also revealed complex interactions among host factors (including age and gender), sample origin, antimicrobial resistance, biofilm production, and virulence genes.Conclusion:This study highlights the alarming spread of MRSA and VRSA, which show significant resistance and virulence.

1. Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a dangerous Gram-positive bacterial pathogen that are responsible for a significant variety of human diseases ranging from superficial skin infection to deep seated necrotizing complication, endocarditis, arthritis, and pneumonia. It can cause life-threatening diseases if it invades the bloodstream or internal tissues.[1] The wide spreading of resistant phenotypes was announced worldwide owing to the inappropriate antibiotics use, prolonged hospitalization, and intravenous drug abuse. Methicillin resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is one of the resistant phenotypes, which is important because of not only, being resistant to methicillin, but also, resistant to other antimicrobial drugs specially β-lactam antibiotic.[2] Several hospitals and community acquired infections, which is commonly linked with severe cases, were attributed to MRSA strains. Increasingly, there is a significant rise of the MRSA strains showing multidrug resistance patterns. Notably, glycopeptide is the drug of choice for treating life-threatening MRSA infections, as most of these strains are Vancomycin-sensitive S. aureus (VSSA). Unfortunately, glycopeptide resistance has been reported, and Vancomycin-resistant S. aureus (VRSA) related infections has been seen in several health care centers.[3,4] The key reason for methicillin and vancomycin resistance is due to the expression of the mecA and vanAB genes respectively. The mecA gene expressed PBP2a with reduced binding affinity to antimicrobial drugs.[5] The van gene is responsible for changing the vancomycin target through the synthesis of a new target (D-alanine-D-lactate), which has a reduced binding affinity with glycopeptides.[6]

Several pathogenic strain strains can evade the host immune response owing to their virulence fitness.[7–9] Both VSSA and VRSA produced virulence proteins facilitate the adaptation and survival of these pathogens in different organs inside the human body.[10] Moreover, different virulence factors, which are carried by different VSSA and VRSA strains, can enable them to cause various pathogenic properties, resulting in different diseases. The severity of these diseases depends in the wide variety of virulence factors production on the site of infection ranging from minor skin infections to life-threatening diseases such as endocarditis, skin and soft tissue infection, meningitis and pneumonia, thus the recently medical researchers have focused on the relationship between bacterial infection, virulence factors and host defenses.[11]

S. aureus is a versatile pathogen responsible for a wide range of infections, including skin and soft tissue infections, pneumonia, sepsis, and endocarditis. Its pathogenicity is largely attributed to various virulence factors, including several key virulence genes. These virulence genes play critical roles in its ability to establish infections and evade the host immune response through various mechanisms of action.[12,13] For instance, alpha-toxin, encoded by the hla gene, forms pores in the membranes of host cells, leading to cell lysis and inflammation, which facilitates tissue invasion. Panton-Valentine leukocidin targets and destroys leukocytes, particularly neutrophils, thereby impairing the immune response and allowing the bacteria to proliferate unchecked. Enterotoxins (SEs) are superantigens that provoke excessive immune responses, leading to symptoms such as vomiting and diarrhea, which can further aid in bacterial spread during foodborne outbreaks. Adhesins like clumping factor and fibronectin-binding proteins promote bacterial adhesion to host tissues and medical devices, enabling colonization and biofilm formation that protects the bacteria from both the immune system and antibiotic treatment. The polysaccharide capsule, produced by some strains, provides an additional layer of defense against phagocytosis, enhancing survival in the bloodstream. Immune evasion is further supported by protein A, which binds to the Fc region of immunoglobulins, preventing opsonization and inhibiting phagocytosis. Lastly, the staphylococcal complement inhibitor (SCIN) interferes with complement activation, further diminishing the host’s ability to clear the infection. Collectively, these virulence factors and their mechanisms contribute to the pathogenicity of S. aureus and its ability to cause a diverse array of infections.[14]

The emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) S. aureus poses a significant public health challenge worldwide, necessitating urgent attention and action to combat this crisis. As these strains develop resistance to multiple classes of antibiotics, common infections that were once easily treatable are becoming increasingly difficult to manage, leading to longer hospital stays, higher medical costs, and increased mortality rates. For instance, infections caused by MDR strains of MRSA and VRSA exemplify the urgent need for new therapeutic options. The spread of these resistant strains is enhanced by factors such as inappropriate antibiotic prescribing, inadequate infection control measures, and the agricultural use of antibiotics, which further complicates treatment strategies. Moreover, the progress in developing new antibiotics has slowed considerably, with few novel agents emerging to replace those rendered ineffective due to resistance.[15,16] Therefore, there is an imperative need for the discovery and development of potent and safe antimicrobial agents, alongside the implementation of robust antimicrobial stewardship programs, to mitigate the impact of MDR pathogens.[17,18] This includes investment in research and development, the promotion of alternative therapeutic approaches such as bacteriophage therapy and immunotherapy, and the enhancement of diagnostic capabilities to ensure timely and accurate treatment.[15] Addressing the public health implications of MDR bacterial pathogens is critical to safeguarding global health and maintaining the effectiveness of our healthcare systems.

Of note, the antimicrobial resistance and virulence fitness vary according to genetic background of VSSA and VRSA strains. Several VSSA and VRSA phenotypes showed different antimicrobial resistance patterns. Therefore, the infection with VSSA and VRSA strains are problematic owing to the wide spreading of different clones carrying several antimicrobial resistance genes. The degree of microbial pathogenesis depends on the existence of virulence genes.[19] The absence of virulence arrays is always associated with nonpathogenic strains.[20,21] Of note, the severity of infections mainly depends on the strain type. Despite extensive research on S. aureus, significant gaps in understanding remain, particularly regarding the phenotypic and genotypic diversity among MRSA, VRSA, and VSSA strains. While previous studies have examined various aspects of these strains individually, they often fail to provide a comprehensive analysis encompassing the correlation between resistance mechanisms and virulence factors in a comparative context. Questions persist about the specific phenotypic traits that contribute to the pathogenicity of VRSA compared to VSSA, as well as the genetic variations that underlie these differences. Moreover, the role of biofilm formation in the persistence and treatment challenges associated with these isolates remains inadequately explored. Addressing some of these unanswered questions is crucial for developing effective strategies to combat the rising threat of VRSA strains. Therefore, this study aims to partially address these gaps by systematically investigating the phenotypic and genotypic characteristics of MRSA, VRSA, and VSSA isolates. In this study, we seek to identify patterns of resistance and virulence that could inform the development of effective prevention and control strategies. This research will contribute valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying the fitness and pathogenicity of VSSA and VRSA. Additionally, we aim to provide evidence-based recommendations for clinicians and public health officials to optimize antibiotics.

2. Material and methods

2.1. Collection and transport of clinical specimens

A total of 250 clinical samples were collected from individual patients aged between 20 and 60 years, comprising 140 males and 110 females. Each sample was collected from a different patient admitted to various hospitals in Egypt over a four-month period between January and April 2022. These samples were obtained from various clinical sites, including wound infections, bloodstream infections, and urinary tract infections. The clinical samples consisted of blood, wound exudates, urine, and pus. The pus originated from a skin abscess, an intra-abdominal infection (such as an abscess or peritonitis), or a bone infection (osteomyelitis). This approach enables a comprehensive analysis of the prevalence of MRSA and its associated antimicrobial resistance patterns. All specimens were collected aseptically following standard methodology according to instructions and guidelines of previous published report.[22,23] The isolation and identification of S. aureus isolates start with the collection of clinical specimens, followed by culturing on enrichment media such as brain heart infusion broth, and then transferring to nutrient agar and selective media like Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA) produced by Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis. After incubation, colony morphology is examined, and a Gram stain is performed to assess the organism’s characteristics. Biochemical tests, including the catalase test using hydrogen peroxide from Sigma-Aldrich, and the coagulase test utilizing rabbit plasma from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, are crucial for differentiating S. aureus from other staphylococci.[22,24] All S. aureus isolates were confirmed by APIs and genetic detection of nuc gene.[25]

2.2. Characterization of MRSA, VSSA, and VRSA

To identify the MRSA isolates, cefoxitin and oxacillin discs were used on Mueller-Hinton Agar, and susceptibility was interpreted according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute.[26] Further additional confirmation tests that were used to verify MRSA included the utilization of HardyCHROM™ MRSA chromogenic agar and Biomerieux VITEK®2, automated ID/AST System.[27] Categorization of tested isolates according to antimcirobial susceptibility was depended on guidelines roles of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute.[26] Further molecular confirmation was done by genetic detection of mecA gene as previously described.[28]

2.3. Identification of VRSA

More additional accurate confirmation test was done on isolates of MRSA that showed complete and intermediate resistance to the Vancomycin antimicrobial disc to verify VRSA and was performed for determination of the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) by Broth Microdilution Method. The absorbance was determined by using (ELISA) reader at wavelength equal to 630 nm. The value of MIC was interpreted by referring to CLSI, 2022,[26] and the tested isolates were reported as Resistant (R) or Susceptible (S). Moreover, the genetic detections of vanA and vanB gene were used to confirm the VRSA isolates.[29]

2.4. Antimicrobial susceptibility of multiple drug-resistant MRSA

The antimicrobial susceptibility test of all identified isolates was performed in vitro on 20 different commonly used antimicrobial agents with different categories, therapeutic actions and mechanisms of action, by using Disc Diffusion Method according to the criteria and guidelines of CLSI, 2022.[26] The antimicrobial discs (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK) as shown in Table 1, included Cefoxitin (FOX), Benzylpenicillin (P), Oxacillin (OX), Amoxiclav (AMC), Gentamicin (CN), Erythromycin (E), Clindamycin (DA), Nitrofurantoin (NIT), Linezolid (LZ), Vancomycin (VA), Teicoplanin (TEC), Tetracycline (TE), Doxycycline (DO), Ciprofloxacin (CIP), Levofloxacin (LEV), Chloramphenicol (C), Fusidic Acid (F), Rifampin (RIF), Cotrimoxazole(SXT), Pristinomycin (Quinupristin/Dalfopristin) (PR). The isolates that were resistant to one antimicrobial or more in three categories were interpreted as Multi Drug Resistant (MDR).[30] Multiple Antibiotic Resistance (MAR) index was calculated to assess the extent of resistance for both antimicrobial and isolates. For each antimicrobial, the MAR index was obtained by dividing the number of resistant isolates by the total number of isolates tested, reflecting the frequency of resistance within the population. For individual isolates, the MAR index was calculated by dividing the number of antimicrobials to which the isolate was resistant by the total number of antimicrobials tested, indicating the breadth of resistance for that specific isolate. A higher MAR index for either antimicrobials or isolates indicated substantial antimicrobials exposure, often associated with high-risk environments, and provided critical insights for infection control and antimicrobial management strategies.[31]

2.5. Phenotypic detection of some virulence factors of identified VSSA and VRSA isolates

All isolates of MRSA and VRSA were screened for biofilm and hemolysin production by using Qualitative and Quantitative Methods.

2.5.1. Qualitative detection of biofilm formation by Congo red agar (CRA) method

All the tested isolates were inoculated in streaks in Congo red agar (CRA) plates and incubated at 35°C aerobically for 24 to 48 hours. The isolates which appeared as black colonies were biofilm producers, whereas the non-biofilm producers appeared as red or pale colonies.[32,33]

2.5.2. Quantitative determination of biofilm production by micro-titer plate (MTP) method

This method was performed according to standard instructions submitted protocol.[34,35] The isolates were classified into the following categories, No biofilm production (OD ≤ ODc), Weak biofilm producer (ODc < OD ≤ 2 × ODc), Moderate biofilm producer (2 × ODc < OD ≤ 4 × ODc) and Strong biofilm producer (4 × ODc < OD).

2.6. Qualitative and quantitative detection of hemolysin production

Blood plate assay was used for determination of both Qualitative and Quantitative Hemolytic activity and performed as following: The microorganisms from the stock culture were inoculated onto TSA and to incubate overnight to 24 hours. aerobically at 35°C. Then a few pure colonies were suspended in sterile saline to prepare bacterial suspensions and the turbidity of them were adjusted using turbidity meter to adjust turbidity comparable to that of the 2 McFarland standard. Sheep blood TSA was prepared by adding 7% v/v of fresh sheep blood and 3% w/v of glucose. Then, 10 µL of inoculation was spotted on sheep blood TSA plates and incubated at 35°C for 24 hours.[36,37] The diameter of clear zones around the inoculate indicated hemolysin production, then hemolytic activity was determined in millimeters by measurement the diameter of inoculate divided by the diameter of clear zone around inoculate. Precipitation zone was measured and expressed as (Pz) value from 1 to 4 as follows: Pz 1 (negative) for no clearance, Pz 1+ (0.9–1) for mild, Pz 2+ for moderate (0.89–0.80), Pz 3+ (0.79–0.70) for strong and Pz 4+ (≥0.69) for very strong hemolysin activity. This experiment was done three times to detect the average measurements.[38,39]

2.8. Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was done by using Microsoft excel (version 2003). Qualitative data were expressed as percentage and frequency. Numerical data were interpreted as mean, standard error, standard deviation or median, range as appropriate and Anova: Two-Factor Without Replication and SPSS software (version 20; SPSS, Chicago) was used for categorical and numerical data analysis. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r-value) was calculated to assess the strength and direction of linear relationships between variables. This analysis was performed using R software, where the r-value was applied to compute pairwise correlations for selected variables, with Pearson specified as the method. The resulting r-values range from −1 to 1, where values closer to 1 or −1 indicate a strong positive or negative correlation, respectively, and values near 0 indicate weak or no linear correlation.

3. Results

3.1. Phenotypic characteristics of the S. aureus isolates

The widespread occurrence of S. aureus as a notable human pathogen has led to extensive research focused on its phenotypic and genotypic diversity, especially among MRSA, VRSA, and susceptible strains. This study aims to investigate the variations in antimicrobial resistance patterns, virulence factors, and genetic profiles of these isolates to better understand their public health implications and the mechanisms driving resistance. Our hypothesis suggests that MRSA, VSSA, and VRSA isolates display greater phenotypic diversity and unique genotypic characteristics. To test this, we collected clinical samples and employed a combination of culture techniques, biochemical assays, and molecular methods to characterize S. aureus isolates and detect their phenotypic traits and genotypic features, ultimately providing insights into their evolutionary adaptations and resistance mechanisms.

In this study, S. aureus was identified in 62 of 250 clinical samples using standard microbiological techniques and the API assay, showing a prevalence rate of 24.8%. The phenotypic identification was relied on several distinct tests. S. aureus typically formed smooth, round colonies that appeared golden-yellow or white on nutrient agar and turned Mannitol Salt Agar yellow due to mannitol fermentation. Under the microscope, it was observed as Gram-positive cocci arranged in grape like clusters. It was catalase-positive, producing bubbles in response to hydrogen peroxide, and coagulase-positive, meaning it clotted plasma, an important feature that distinguished it from other staphylococcal species. Additionally, S. aureus frequently exhibited beta-hemolysis on blood agar and was DNase-positive, creating a clear halo on DNase agar. The phenotypic identification methods were consistent with the genotypic detection of nuc genes, as all the identified S. aureus isolates harbored the nuc gene.

3.2. Prevalence of MRSA, VSSA and VRSA strains among S. aureus isolates:

Notably, a relatively high prevalence of MRSA strains was observed among the S. aureus isolates, accounting for 43.5% (27 out of 62). Among these MRSA strains, 20 isolates were identified as VSSA (74.1%), while 7 isolates were confirmed as VRSA strains, reflecting a relatively lower prevalence (25.9%). Interestingly, all MRSA strains showed phenotypic resistance to methicillin and cefoxitin and they were confirmed by genetic detection of mecA gene; meanwhile, all VRSA strains were resistance to vancomycin and harbored vanA or vanB gene. As expected, all VRSA strains showed absolute methicillin and vancomycin resistance patterns. A higher percentage of MRSA isolates was found in females (51.85%, 14/27) compared to males (48.15%, 13/27). Similarly, VRSA isolates were more common in females (57.14%, 4/7) than in males (42.86%, 3/7). Overall, MRSA and VRSA were more prevalent in female patients than in male patients. All VRSA isolates were obtained from patients over 30 years old, whereas VSSA isolates were found in clinical samples from patients aged 20 to 50 years. Most VSSA isolates were found in pus specimens (48.15%), followed by blood (29.63%) and wound specimens (14.81%). The lowest prevalence was in urine specimens (7.41%). For VRSA isolates, the highest prevalence was also in pus specimens (57.14%), followed by urine specimens (28.57%), with the lowest prevalence in blood specimens (14.29%).

3.3. Antimicrobial resistance patterns of all tested MRSA isolates

The antimicrobial susceptibility testing of all 27 MRSA isolates was done in vitro using both disc diffusion method and VITEK AST against different commonly used antimicrobial agents within different categories, therapeutic actions and mechanisms of action. all the 27 tested isolates were resistant to cefoxitin, as well as methicillin which assured that all these isolates were MRSA. Moreover, all these isolates showed absolute resistance to penicillin G. Only 7 isolates from all 27 MRSA isolates showed resistance to vancomycin, which indicated that these isolates were VRSA. Unfortunately, all the tested MRSA isolates (VSSA and VRSA) showed MDR patterns. Of note, the highest antimicrobial resistance percentage was announced to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (92.6%) followed by fusidic acid (77.8%); however, the most effective drug was linezolid followed by rifampin with an antimicrobial resistance percentage 3.8% and 7.5% respectively as shown in Table 3.

3.4. Antimicrobial resistance patterns of VSSA versus VRSA isolates

Regarding to the antimicrobial resistance patterns of both VSSA and VRSA isolates, VRSA isolates showed higher resistance percentages than VSSA isolates to all antimicrobial drugs with the exception of trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and fusidic acid as shown in Figure 1. Of note, all VSSA isolates were sensitive to vancomycin, teicoplanin and linezolid. Interestingly, Linezolid exhibited the highest sensitivity pattern for all VSSA and VRSA strains. In addition to cefoxitin, oxacillin, penicillin G, all VRSA strains showed absolute resistance to vancomycin and teicoplanin; however, all VSSA strains had absolute resistance to cefoxitin, oxacillin, penicillin G. Interestingly, all VSSA isolates demonstrated absolute sensitivity, with no resistant isolates detected, to vancomycin, teicoplanin, and linezolid. In contrast, absolute resistance was observed in all VRSA strains, as no sensitive isolates were detected against vancomycin and teicoplanin.

3.5. MAR Index for antimicrobial drugs and bacterial isolates

The MAR index provided insights into antimicrobial resistance patterns among the isolates in this study. Cefoxitin, penicillin, and oxacillin showed MAR indices of 1.0, indicating that 100% of isolates were resistant to these antimicrobials, suggesting extensive resistance linked to high-risk, high-exposure environments. Amoxicillin-clavulanate and gentamicin exhibited MAR indices of 0.96 and 0.7, respectively, while lower MAR indices were noted for linezolid and rifampicin, at 0.04 and 0.07, respectively. Intermediate MAR indices (0.2–0.4) were observed for tetracycline, doxycycline, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, reflecting varied resistance patterns. For VSSA isolates, MAR indices ranged from 5 to 11, with moderate resistance typically between 6 and 7. Two VSSA isolates showed higher MAR indices of 11 and 9, indicating broader resistance. VRSA isolates generally had higher MAR indices, ranging from 8 to 14. These findings highlighted that VRSA isolates developed resistance to a broader array of antimicrobials than VSSA isolates, emphasizing the increased resistance burden associated with vancomycin resistance and underscoring the need for vigilant monitoring and targeted therapeutic strategies to manage VRSA infections.

3.6. The existence of virulence genes among all tested isolates

Regarding to the virulence fitness, the existence percentages of each virulence factors using both phenotypic and genotypic methods were shown in Figure 3. Of note, all the tested isolates showed hemolytic activities on blood agar and harbored seb gene. Surprisingly, none of the tested isolates harbored sec, sea, and etb genes. Interestingly, about 96.3% of the tested isolates showed strong biofilm producing activities by MTP and they had at least one of the icaA/D genes; however, only 63% and 33.3% of the tested isolates were strong and weak biofilm producing by CRA respectively as shown in Table 4. Interestingly, all strong and weak biofilm producing isolates by CRA were strong biofilm producing by MTP and harbored one of the icaA/D genes. This observation indicates high specificity and sensitivity of the MTP, and low sensitivity and high specificity of CRA. With the exception of one VRSA isolates, almost all the detected MRSA isolates were biofilm producing isolates.

3.7. Virulence arrays of VSSA versus VRSA isolates

As it was known all VSSA and VRSA strains showed hemolytic activities on blood agar. All VSSA and VRSA isolates harbored seb gene and none of these isolates had sea, sec, and etb genes. Interestingly, the virulence fitness was further characterized in VSSA strains than VRSA strains as shown in Table 5 and Figure 2. The percentages of the detected virulence genes, including sed, see, and hlg, were nearly equal between VSSA and VRSA; however, the virulence genes such as lukED, hlb, icaA, icaD, and hla genes existed with higher prevalence among VSSA isolates than VRSA isolates. The tst was the only virulence gene, which existed with higher percentage among VRSA isolates than VSSA isolates as shown in Figure 2. Interestingly, With the exception of one VRSA isolates, all our isolates showed multi-virulence patterns (harbored more than 3 virulence genes).

3.8.1. Correlation between biofilm production and antimicrobial resistance and virulence genes

The correlation matrix illustrated the association between various antimicrobial resistances and biofilm production (Fig. 3A). Notably, some antimicrobials, such as erythromycin (E, 0.33), showed a positive correlation, suggesting that increased resistance was associated with higher biofilm production. Conversely, there were moderate to strong negative correlations between biofilm formation and certain antimicrobials, such as gentamicin (CN, −0.55) and ciprofloxacin (CIP, −0.62), indicating that higher resistance to these antimicrobials was linked with lower biofilm production. In Figure 3B, the correlation matrix displayed the relationships among virulence genes and biofilm formation. Genes like icaD and sed exhibited a positive correlation with biofilm (0.41 and 0.69, respectively). In contrast, genes such as tst and hla had weak or slightly negative correlations with biofilm, indicating a complex interplay between virulence factors and biofilm production.

3.8.3. Correlation between patient demographics (age and gender), sample type, biofilm production, antimicrobial resistance, and virulence genes

Age showed varied correlations with different factors, indicating potential age-dependent susceptibility patterns (Fig. 5). For instance, a moderate positive correlation was observed between the 40 to 45 age group and resistance to antimicrobials like rifampicin (RIF, 0.47) and doxycycline (DO, 0.33). Certain virulence genes, such as hlg, exhibited weak correlations across age groups, suggesting that age had limited impact on virulence gene expression. Notably, gender also presented distinct correlations with antimicrobial resistance and virulence factors. Male samples showed a mild positive correlation with resistance to ciprofloxacin (CIP, 0.32) and biofilm formation (0.22), while female samples demonstrated weak associations with some resistance genes, such as icaA (0.15), indicating a potentially higher biofilm production tendency in males. Sample type (pus, blood, urine, wound) was also associated with specific resistance patterns. For instance, wound samples showed a positive correlation with resistance to antimicrobials like tetracycline (TE, 0.33) and gentamicin (CN, 0.32), while blood samples displayed weak associations with virulence genes like sed and tst, which could imply that certain bloodstream infections had unique virulence profiles. in another context, biofilm formation showed moderate correlations with specific antimicrobials, such as rifampicin (RIF, 0.33) and tetracycline (TE, 0.25), indicating that biofilm-producing strains exhibited increased resistance to these antimicrobials. Additionally, biofilm formation was positively correlated with certain virulence genes, such as icaA (0.47) and sed (0.41), supporting the role of biofilm-related genes in enhancing biofilm production.

3.8.4. Correlation between vancomycin resistance and other antimicrobial resistance, virulence genes, biofilm formation, and patient demographics (age and gender)

Resistance to vancomycin exhibited positive correlations with certain antimicrobials, such as doxycycline (DO, ~0.33) and ciprofloxacin (CIP, ~0.35), suggesting that vancomycin-resistant isolates also showed resistance to these antimicrobials, likely due to multidrug resistance mechanisms within the bacterial population (Fig. 5). Mild positive correlations were also observed between vancomycin resistance and virulence genes like icaA and sed, indicating that vancomycin-resistant strains could harbor virulence factors associated with infection severity. Notably, icaA was linked to biofilm formation, which can enhance bacterial survival under antimicrobials pressure. Additionally, a weak positive correlation between vancomycin resistance and biofilm formation suggested that vancomycin-resistant strains may have increased biofilm-forming capacity, complicating treatment efforts in biofilm-associated infections. On the other hand, resistance to vancomycin exhibited negative correlations with certain antimicrobials, such as erythromycin (E, −0.29) and gentamicin (CN, −0.17), suggesting that isolates resistant to vancomycin were still susceptible to these drugs. These negative correlations implied that distinct resistance mechanisms were involved, as vancomycin resistance did not confer cross-resistance to these antimicrobials. Additionally, weak negative correlations were observed between vancomycin resistance and specific sample types or demographic factors, indicating that vancomycin-resistant isolates were less common in certain age groups or infection sources, possibly due to varying antimicrobial exposures or infection origins.

3.9. Coloniality and heterogeneity of the tested isolates

Based on the antimicrobial resistances and virulence profiles, all the tested VSSA and VRSA strains (27) were distributed into 24 clusters (Fig. 6). Twenty-one isolates were segregated into separate branches and 6 isolates were collected in 3 clusters (single pairs in each cluster). Notably, no relatedness or clonality was observed among VRSA isolates, as all these isolates were distributed in different unique clusters as shown in Figure 6. However, some clonality was recorded among the VSSA strains, as 3 pairs of isolates were collected in 3 different clusters including the following pairs (VSSA11 and VSSA4; VSSA14 and VSSA17; VSSA9 and VSSA16). Therefore, a high degree of diversity and very low clonality with D-value equal to 0.99 were announced for all tested isolates.

4. Discussion

The S. aureus especially methicillin resistance (MRSA) is of significant importance and global concern because it is a flexible pathogen responsible for causing both community and healthcare associated infections in animals and humans.[2] In an effort to mitigate the impact of MRSA related infections new antimicrobials were developed, but treatments given were not successful especially in complicated and serious infections like pneumonia, toxic shock syndrome (TSST), meningitis, wound infections, sepsis, endocarditis, food poisoning, and osteomyelitis.[45,46] Moreover, the inappropriate use of antimicrobials has unfortunately contributed to the emergence of vancomycin resistance in VRSA strains.[47] Therefore, our study focused on assessing the prevalence, biofilm formation, antimicrobial resistance, and virulence profiles of MRSA isolates, comparing these characteristics between VSSA and VRSA strains.

Our study reported that, (24.8%) of patients admitted to several Hospitals in Egypt, during period between January and April 2022, were colonized with S. aureus. This prevalence is slightly similar to several studies that were done in Iran[48] and in South America.[49] Our results stating that 43.5% of the isolated S. aureus strains were resistant to Methicillin (MRSA). Comparing this result with findings from other publications can uncover variations in MRSA prevalence influenced by geographical location, healthcare settings, and study methodologies. For instance, some studies reported higher prevalence rates,[50–52] while others documented lower MRSA prevalence.[53–55] These differences may stem from varying antimicrobial usage patterns, noncompliance with infection control principles, or patient demographics, and the type of specimen collected. Understanding these variations is essential for precise infection control and antimicrobial treatment, enabling the tracking of resistance trends and development of effective interventions against resistant bacteria.

Our results indicate that a majority (74.1%) of the MRSA strains examined were classified as VSSA, while a smaller proportion (25.9%) were confirmed as VRSA. This finding suggests that while Methicillin resistance is prevalent among the infected patients, Vancomycin resistance, which is crucial for treatment options, is less common in the studied population.[56,57] The variation in the overall prevalence of VSSA and VRSA in different countries explained by the negligence on infection control guidelines and safety next, the difference in the location, time period of the study, the duration of the study, sample size, sample handling, processing and the nature of the sample taken.[57] In addition, the widespread and haphazard use of Vancomycin to treat infections caused by MRSA.[58]

Regarding the antimicrobial susceptibility results, this study revealed that all 27 MRSA isolates showed (100%) multi-drug resistant (MDR), this is due to the misuse or overuse of multiple broad-spectrum antibiotics indiscriminately particularly β-lactams represented by penicillin and cephalosporins. This may have contributed to an increase in MRSA-related infections and facilitated the development of further resistance, which aligns with a previous study conducted in Iraq.[59] This study found that all 27 MRSA isolates were completely resistant (100%) to both β-lactam antibiotics as (Cefoxitin, Penicillin G, Oxacillin) and showed high resistance pattern to β-lactams with β-lactamase inhibitors as Amoxiclav (Amoxicillin/Clavulanic Acid), this was in same that pointed with previous published reports,[60,61] which found that MRSA were fully resistant to most β-lactam antibiotics. MRSA is usually resistant to β-lactam antibiotics, including methicillin and related penicillins. However, some MRSA strains can be sensitive to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (Augmentin), a combination of amoxicillin and the β-lactamase inhibitor clavulanic acid. This sensitivity can occur when MRSA strains produce low levels of β-lactamase enzymes, which the clavulanic acid can inhibit, allowing amoxicillin to be effective. Additionally, certain genetic factors or mutations in MRSA can alter the structure of penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), making some strains susceptible to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid.[62–64] However, the majority of the MRSA isolates were susceptible to linezolid, as a low resistance percentage (3.7%) was announced. This result is congruent with other studies.[65] The low resistance pattern has been noted because the medication’s availability is limited and thus not frequently prescribed because this drug is available in a limited way and is not prescribed frequently.[66,67]

The biofilm-forming ability of S. aureus has increasingly been accepted as an important virulence trait.[68] In this study, about 96.3% of the tested MRSA isolates showed strong biofilm producing activities by microtiter plates and they had icaA/D gene; however, 63% of the tested isolates were strong biofilm producing by congo red method. Interestingly, all biofilm producing isolates by congo red method were biofilm producing by microtiter plate and harbored icaA/D gene indicating. This observation indicating high specificity and sensitivity of the microtiter plate, and high specificity and low sensitivity of congo red agar method this was demonstrated in other studies.[69–71]

The differences in the degree of pathogenicity may be attributed to virulence factors found in some isolates and absent in others.[72,73] With the exception of one VRSA isolate, all our isolates showed multi-virulence patterns (harbored more than 3 virulence genes). The existence of multi-virulence factors may lead to serious and potentially lethal infections and increase the morbidity and mortality rate. Interestingly, the VRSA isolates showed antimicrobial resistance patterns higher than VSSA isolates; however, the VSSA isolates had virulence fitness more than VRSA isolates. This confirmed the previous hypothesis “This confirms further that negative correlation between the existence of virulence genes and antimicrobial resistance.” The accepted explanation of this finding is that the virulence genes are acquired on mobile elements at the expense of antimicrobial resistance genes and vice versa.[74,75]

Regarding to correlation analysis, we found positive and negative correlation between biofilm production and some antimicrobial drugs and virulence genes. These observations highlight the potential influence of antimicrobial resistance and specific virulence genes on biofilm formation, which could impact the persistence and pathogenicity of VRSA and VSSA strains. Moreover, the detected correlation between antimicrobial resistance and virulence genes among VRSA and VSSA isolates suggest that the development of vancomycin resistance may impact the association between antimicrobial resistance and virulence in VRSA and VSSA, potentially affecting its pathogenic profile. Additionally, we found complex interactions between host factors (age and gender), sample origin, antimicrobial resistance, biofilm production, and virulence genes, suggesting that specific patient demographics and infection sources could influence the resistance and virulence profiles of VRSA and VSSA strains. The positive and negative correlations of vancomycin with other antimicrobials, virulence genes, and biofilm production suggested a complex interplay of resistance and virulence factors. Positive correlations with other resistance factors and virulence genes indicated a trend toward multidrug-resistant, highly virulent strains in specific populations.[76] Conversely, negative correlations with certain antimicrobials implied potential treatment options, as isolates resistant to vancomycin were still susceptible to other drugs, which could guide therapeutic decisions. Understanding these correlations was crucial for effective treatment and infection control strategies in clinical settings.

Based on the antimicrobial resistances and virulence profiles, the tested VSSA and VRSA showed a high degree of diversity and very low clonality. These pathogens had unique antimicrobial and virulent profiles. The difference in the antimicrobial profiles may attribute to the different in local infection control programs implemented, antimicrobials prescribing policies, also differences in source of samples, conditions of tests used, type of techniques, the time of the study, and difference in sampling technique, site of infection, the number of cases studied, and host factors.[77] However, the difference in the genetic background may lead to the variation in the virulence profiles. Several MRSA genotypes have been reported, which affect the severity, complications, and mortality associated with infections. Additionally, new MRSA clones have emerged that may have undergone distinct evolutionary processes.[78]

5. Conclusion

The increase in VRSA prevalence, alongside the high mortality rates they cause, makes them significantly concern in clinical settings. In comparison, VSSA are more common but generally less lethal. Our findings indicate that MDR isolates are the most common phenotypes and are likely linked to biofilm production, as a significant majority of our isolates demonstrated this trait. Treating these resistant phenotypes is complex; however, linezolid remains the most effective drug for both VSSA and VRSA infections. The development and implementation of effective prevention and control measures are significantly complicated by the high heterogeneity and low clonality found in both VSSA and VRSA strains. Thus, it is imperative to conduct further molecular epidemiological studies to track the evolution of virulence and resistance characteristics in these pathogens, as this knowledge is essential for informing strategies to combat their impact on public health.

6. Limitation

One significant limitation of this study is the low number of VRSA isolates available for analysis. This small sample size is due to several factors, including the relatively low prevalence of VRSA compared to VSSA and MRSA strains, resulting in fewer identified cases. Additionally, the lack of genetic detection of resistance genes limits the ability to draw robust conclusions about the prevalence, characteristics, and patterns of resistance among VRSA strains. As a result, this limitation may impact the statistical power of the analysis. Therefore, future studies should focus on increasing the number of VRSA isolates collected to enhance the comprehensiveness of findings and provide a stronger foundation for understanding the clinical implications of these resistant strains.
