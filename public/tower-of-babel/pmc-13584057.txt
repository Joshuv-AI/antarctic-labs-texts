Synthesis of RARα–PROTAC and its delivery via Polygonatum sibiricum exosome-like nanoparticles for the treatment of colorectal cancer
Authors: Gengzhen Zhu, Yanping Zhao, Mingzhu Chen, Shi Lu, Lilan Xu, Guanyu Chen, Lupeng Zeng, Jinghua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Retinoic acid receptor α (RARα) is a promising yet challenging target in treating colorectal cancer (CRC). Traditional RARα-binding modulators often fail to fully inhibit downstream oncogenic pathways and cannot eliminate the pre-existing RARα protein, frequently resulting in drug resistance and treatment failure. To overcome these limitations, we designed a series of proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) based on the ligand CA77.1, which are bifunctional molecules that recruit the ubiquitin–proteasome system for RARα degradation.​Among these candidates, compound Z1 proved the most potent degrader, demonstrating a DC50 of 6.02 ± 1.05 μM and effective suppression of CRC cell proliferation and migration. Considering the generally poor solubility and membrane permeability of PROTACs, we encapsulated Z1 within Polygonatum sibiricum exosome-like nanoparticles (PsELNs). Compared with free Z1, the Z1/PsELNs delivery system enhanced RARα degradation in vitro by 2.2-fold and improved in vivo antitumor efficacy by 1.8-fold, while also promoting tumor targeting and overall bioavailability. These findings provide a feasible strategy for degrading RARα in CRC and highlight the potential of plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles as efficient carriers for PROTAC delivery, indicating a new direction for targeted cancer therapy.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with an estimated 1.9 million new cases and 904,000 deaths globally in 2022, posing a persistent challenge to public health [1,2]. Patients with the advanced disease have a dismal five-year survival rate less then 15% [3], highlighting the inadequacy of current treatment options. Although therapies targeting epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) are utilized, their efficacy is often constrained by​ the expression level and activation status of these targets. Additionally, resistance to these treatments frequently develops, limiting their long-term efficacy. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop innovative therapeutic strategies for CRC [4,5]. Notably, the retinoic acid receptor α (RARα) is highly active in CRC tumors, promoting cancer cell proliferation [6] and enhancing invasion and metastasis [7]. Given its crucial role at various stages of the disease, RARα has emerged as a promising therapeutic target in the development of more effective CRC treatments.

Existing RARα modulators are significantly limited by insufficient target specificity, which can lead to drug resistance and tissue toxicity, particularly affecting the liver, skin, and bones. Common modulators, such as BMS453 [8], CD2665, ER50891 [9], and Ch55 [10] (Fig. 1A), are built on a retinoic acid scaffold and feature structural components such as conjugated polyenes and carboxyl groups. These characteristics contribute to their chemical instability [11], excessive lipophilicity, and poor oral bioavailability [12]. Proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) technology offers a promising solution to the aforementioned challenges. Unlike traditional modulators that require constant binding to a target protein's active site for functional inhibition, PROTACs operate through an event-driven mechanism, covalently linking an E3 ubiquitin ligase ligand, such as thalidomide, with a target protein ligand, such as an RARα ligand, using a specific linker to create a bifunctional molecule. This molecule can simultaneously recruit the target protein and the E3 ligase, forming a ternary complex. The resulting complex directs the E3 ligase to ubiquitinate the target protein, leading to its degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome system [13]. Notably, the catalytic degradation mechanism of PROTACs enables dissociation after each successful degradation event, allowing repeated target engagement [14,15]. This feature has the potential to overcome drug resistance due to target mutations. Furthermore, by continuously eliminating the target protein, it addresses the limitations of traditional inhibitors, such as incomplete suppression of downstream RARα signaling and susceptibility to compensatory activation. Therefore, applying PROTAC technology to address the limitations of existing RARα ligands is both promising and essential.

Although PROTAC technology holds significant therapeutic promise, challenges such as high molecular weight, poor water solubility, and low membrane permeability limit its bioavailability and clinical potential [16]. Nanocarriers are often employed to overcome these delivery issues. However, traditional synthetic nanocarriers, such as liposomes [17,18] and metal-organic frameworks [19], as well as other systems like carbon nanotubes [20], require complex preparation processes and are associated with potential immunogenicity and toxicity from carrier materials, restricting their clinical use [21]. In contrast, plant-derived extracellular vesicles (PELNs) are natural nanocarriers that offer a superior alternative for PROTAC delivery. Extracted and isolated from plants, PELNs provide excellent biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, effectively reducing the safety risks associated with synthetic carriers [22]. Their natural phospholipid bilayer structure enables the efficient encapsulation of hydrophobic PROTAC molecules, enhancing solubility and stability. Additionally, the size range of PELNs (30–150 nm) facilitates accumulation in tumor tissues through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, thereby improving targeted delivery efficiency [23].

Given their excellent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and high delivery potential, we selected Polygonatum sibiricum exosome-like nanoparticles (PsELNs) as the delivery vehicle in this study. PsELNs share the common benefits of PELNs and exhibit additional unique features. First, the active components in Polygonatum sibiricum, such as polysaccharides and saponins, are known for their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and potential antitumor effects, which may synergize with loaded PROTAC molecules to achieve combined therapeutic effects [24,25]. Second, existing literature indicates that PsELNs naturally target colonic tissues, offering a significant advantage for targeted CRC therapy [26]. Thus, we developed a strategy using PsELNs to deliver RARα–PROTAC, thereby enhancing its targeted accumulation and intracellular internalization, while also addressing the limited solubility and membrane permeability of traditional PROTAC molecules.

We used the atypical RARα antagonist AR7 [27,28] as a lead compound and selected the optimized RARα antagonist CA77.1 [29], known for its enhanced activity and drugability, as the target protein ligand. The ligand was covalently linked to the E3 ubiquitin ligase thalidomide, yielding a PROTAC (Z1) that efficiently degrades RARα (DC50 = 6.02 ± 1.05 μM). To enhance delivery efficiency, we encapsulated Z1 in PsELNs, which possess natural pharmacological activity and excellent biocompatibility, thereby forming a novel Z1/PsELNs drug delivery system (Schematic 1). This system significantly improved cellular drug uptake, boosting Z1's in vitro degradation efficiency by 2.2-fold. Leveraging the high accumulation of PsELNs in tumor tissues through the EPR effect, the antitumor efficacy in vivo increased by 1.8-fold, markedly enhancing Z1's bioavailability and overall therapeutic performance. This study introduces an efficient and safe novel therapeutic strategy for CRC and demonstrates the potential of delivering PROTACs via PELNs, paving the way for integrating protein degradation therapy with naturally active carriers.Schematic 1Construction and application of the Z1/PsELNs administration system for CRC treatment.

Results

Design and synthesis of RARα–PROTAC

The RARα antagonist AR7 (Fig. 1B) initially demonstrated antagonistic activity against RARα in preliminary in vitro screenings. However, its drug-like properties were notably suboptimal, in particular its poor pharmacokinetics, which contributed to limited in vivo efficacy and restricted clinical potential. CA77.1 (Fig. 1B), developed via terminal methyl modification, effectively addressed these pharmacokinetic shortcomings, enhancing oral bioavailability and in vivo stability while enabling sustained and potent antagonistic activity. Thus, this modification substantially improved the overall efficacy of CA77.1 as an RARα antagonist.

To clarify this optimization, we performed molecular docking analysis using AutoDock (PDB:5K13 [30]). The results showed that CA77.1, obtained through structural optimization of AR7 with improved activity, effectively binds to RARα with a calculated binding energy of −7.379 kcal/mol. Binding mode analysis revealed that the interaction between CA77.1 and RARα is primarily mediated by hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic forces. Specifically, CA77.1 forms a key hydrogen bond with Arg276 (bond length: 3.29 Å), while an extensive hydrophobic network is constituted by surrounding residues including Trp225, Phe228, Leu231, Ser232, Cys235, Leu269, Ile273, and Val395 (Fig. 1C). This affinity primarily stems from these synergistic interactions, thereby leading to enhanced activity.​ Furthermore, a critical structural advantage was observed: the terminal methyl group of CA77.1 protrudes into the solvent-exposed region outside the ligand-binding pocket (Fig. 1D). This unique spatial feature provides an ideal attachment point for the linker in PROTAC design, as modifications at this site are unlikely to perturb the core binding interactions. This site was selected based on structural analysis of the RARα–CA77.1 complex (PDB:5K13), which indicates that the terminal methyl group is oriented toward the solvent-exposed region and does not contribute to the key hydrogen-bonding (Arg276) or hydrophobic networks within the buried ligand-binding pocket, thereby preserving target affinity while permitting linker conjugation. Consequently, this design strategy effectively preserves the warhead's intrinsic targeting capability while enabling conjugation.

The antitumor activity of CA77.1 and its parent compound AR7 is strictly dependent on hypoxic activation, which significantly limits their use in solid tumor therapy [27]. Although hypoxic regions are common in tumors, the oxygen partial pressure within the tumor microenvironment varies greatly. This hypoxia-dependent mechanism limits the drug's effectiveness to the deep, hypoxic “core” of the tumor, preventing activation in the more oxygen-rich margins and invasive fronts [31], and posing a potential risk for tumor recurrence and metastasis.

To overcome this limitation, we applied PROTAC technology by covalently linking CA77.1 with an E3 ligase ligand (thalidomide) [32], creating a bifunctional degradation molecule that remains active under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. This approach leverages CA77.1's strong and sustained binding affinity to RARα, bypassing its hypoxia-activated property and thus avoiding the original activity limitation. By making site-specific modifications at the solvent-exposed end of CA77.1, the design maintains its targeting affinity while fully utilizing the catalytic degradation mechanism of PROTACs. Using this approach, we successfully converted CA77.1 from a hypoxia-dependent prodrug into a targeted degrader that operates effectively across the heterogeneous tumor microenvironment. This advancement overcomes the oxygen-dependent limitation, significantly enhancing the drug's therapeutic applicability and potential efficacy.

Building upon the design concept discussed above, we systematically examined how different linker types influence the activity of PROTAC molecules. Initially, the RARα antagonist CA77.1 was synthesized efficiently using the Suzuki–Miyaura cross–coupling method [27]. Following this, two distinct synthetic routes for PROTACs were developed.

The first approach aimed to create pure carbon-chain PROTACs by varying the alkyl chain length to achieve different linker flexibilities. In this synthetic process, the E3 ubiquitin ligase ligand, thalidomide, was initially synthesized via a dehydration condensation reaction. Simultaneously, the RARα antagonist CA77.1 was produced using Suzuki–Miyaura cross-coupling. The amide group of CA77.1 was then converted into the amino intermediate B via acid hydrolysis. Following this, the amide end of CA77.1 was extended through a DCFH-mediated amide bond condensation reaction [33], introducing alkyl fragments of different carbon chain lengths to produce derivative C, which features a brominated halogen terminus. Finally, a nucleophilic substitution reaction between the halogen group of derivative C and the hydroxyl group of thalidomide resulted in the successful generation of a series of RARα–PROTACs with pure carbon chain structures (Z1–Z4) (Schematic 2).

The second approach altered the spatial conformation of the linker by incorporating a piperazine ring as a rigid segment [34]. During synthesis, the halogen end of intermediate C1 underwent nucleophilic substitution with N-Boc-piperazine under reflux conditions in acetonitrile, resulting in the formation of intermediate D. The Boc group's steric hindrance prevented unwanted substitutions on the piperazine ring. Trifluoroacetic acid hydrolysis was used to remove the Boc protective group, yielding the free amine intermediate E. The pre-synthesized thalidomide derivative A, which contained a halogen end, was then linked to intermediate E through a nucleophilic attack by the amine group, establishing a covalent bond between the piperazine ring and thalidomide. Analysis of protein degradation and cytotoxic activity

RARα–PROTAC forms a ternary complex by simultaneously linking the target protein RARα to the E3 ubiquitin ligase, prompting the ligase to polyubiquitinate RARα and leading to its degradation via the ubiquitin–proteasome system (Fig. 2A). To evaluate the protein degradation potential of the synthesized PROTAC molecules, we conducted western blotting to verify their effects on RARα degradation. As shown in Fig. 2B–H, all synthesized PROTAC molecules (Z1–Z6) exhibited varying levels of RARα degradation. Notably, compound Z1 demonstrated the highest degradation efficiency, with a DC50 value of 6.02 ± 1.05 μM and a maximum degradation rate (Dmax) of 85.4%. Its activity was approximately 43.6 times greater than that of the lead compound CA77.1, significantly outperforming other molecules (Z2–Z6) modified with longer carbon chains or piperazine rings. This enhanced efficiency likely resulted from the effects of the carbon chain extension or changes in molecular rigidity from the binding of the PROTAC molecule to RARα, which may hinder the optimal spatial arrangement between RARα and the E3 ligase, ultimately reducing degradation efficiency. This finding highlights the significant advantage of the PROTAC strategy in achieving efficient target protein clearance. Additionally, methylthiazolyldiphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) cytotoxicity assays revealed that the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of Z1 against CRC cells was 7.58 ± 0.21 μM. Furthermore, Z1 showed significantly greater cell-killing activity than CA77.1 and other PROTAC molecules (Fig. S1). Having established Z1's potent RARα degradation and cytotoxicity profiles, we next evaluated its subtype selectivity by assessing the expression of other RAR family members (RARβ and RARγ) via Western blotting (Fig. S2). Z1 treatment did not alter the protein levels of RARβ or RARγ, confirming its high specificity for RARα. These results indicate that Z1 both effectively degraded RARα and inhibited tumor cell proliferation, underscoring a strong correlation between its degradation activity and cytotoxicity. Mechanistic study of PROTAC–Z1

After confirming Z1's efficient RARα degradation capability and antiproliferative activity, as well as its high selectivity for RARα isoforms (see preceding sections), we first validated its mechanistic specificity.​ RT-qPCR (Fig. S3) and Western blot analyses (Fig. S4) demonstrated that Z1 had no significant effect on the transcription of key upstream genes (CRABP2, PI3K p85α, JNK, P38 MAPK, TP53, CDK7, and mTOR) or the expression of their corresponding proteins. This suggests that Z1 neither interferes with the upstream regulatory network of RARα nor exerts its effects via ubiquitination of downstream signaling nodes. Subsequently, cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays revealed that co-treatment with 10 μg/mL CHX and 50 μM Z1 significantly accelerated RARα protein decay compared with CHX treatment alone (Fig. 3A and B). This result confirms that Z1 specifically targets and degrades pre-existing RARα protein rather than inhibiting its translation. To further exclude off-target effects and elucidate the degradation mechanism, we pre-treated cells with ATRA [35] (competitive occupancy of the RARα ligand-binding domain), MLN4924 [36] (E1 ubiquitin-activating enzyme inhibitor), MG132 [37] (proteasome inhibitor), or Thalidomide (competitive occupancy of the E3 ubiquitin ligase substrate-binding pocket). As shown in Fig. 3C and D, all these interventions significantly blocked Z1-mediated RARα degradation, indicating that Z1 induces RARα degradation through direct binding and via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway regulated by Thalidomide-bound E3 ubiquitin ligase and E1 ubiquitin-activating enzyme—a process fully consistent with the classical PROTAC mode of action.Fig. 3Target validation of Z1, molecular docking, and its impact on downstream pathways. Building on these findings and given Z1's established isoform selectivity, we performed molecular docking using AutoDock Vina and generated 3D and 2D interaction diagrams using PyMOL 2.0 and LigPlus to elucidate the structural basis of its specificity at the atomic level. Molecular docking results demonstrated that Z1 stably binds to RARα (PDB: 5K13) and the E3 ubiquitin ligase CRBN (PDB: 4CI3) [38]. Structural analysis further revealed the key residues within the binding pockets and the interaction patterns (Fig. 3E), wherein Z1 forms stable hydrogen bonds with Asn353 and His380 of CRBN. Comprehensive 2D interaction mapping further indicated that the binding of Z1 to the RARα–CRBN complex is synergistically mediated by hydrogen bonds and extensive hydrophobic forces (Fig. 3F). Specifically, the hydrogen bonds with Asn353 and His380 constitute the main energy source for CRBN binding, exhibiting bond lengths of 3.12 Å and 3.08 Å, respectively. Furthermore, an extensive hydrophobic pocket surrounds the Z1 binding interface, with key residues participating in hydrophobic interactions including Ser381, Phe404, Trp388, Trp402, Trp382, Pro354, His355, Ser405, Pro407, Ser229, Phe228, Phe302, Leu266, Ile270, Gly391, Val395, Ser232, Met406, Trp225, Asp226, and Glu379 (Fig. 3F).

Notably, while molecular docking identifies residues constituting the binding pocket and forming direct contacts (Fig. 3E and F), MM/PBSA-based per-residue free energy decomposition quantifies the actual thermodynamic contribution of each residue during dynamic simulations.​ To further verify the dynamic stability of the docking conformation and quantify binding efficacy, we performed a 100-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. The simulation revealed that 1–2 stable hydrogen bonds formed between RARα and Z1 (Fig. 3G). Binding free energy calculations during the last 10 ns (90–100 ns) of the trajectory indicated a value of −139.394 kJ/mol (Fig. 3H). Per-residue energy decomposition analysis identified Pro407, Val395, and Ser225​ as the primary contributors to the binding free energy (Fig. 3I). These residues, although not always the primary hydrogen-bond donors in the static docking model, exhibit dominant van der Waals and nonpolar solvation energy contributions during the dynamic binding process, thereby serving as the thermodynamic core for RARα recognition. The 100-ns MD simulation further demonstrated that CRBN forms hydrogen bond interactions with Z1, maintaining 1–5 hydrogen bonds throughout the trajectory (Fig. 3J). Binding free energy calculations during the last 10 ns (90–100 ns) yielded a value of −111.138 kJ/mol for the CRBN–Z1 interaction (Fig. 3K). Per-residue energy decomposition revealed that the binding free energy of the CRBN–Z1 complex is predominantly contributed by Trp382, Trp388, and Tyr357​ (Fig. 3L). Collectively, these results provide robust molecular-level theoretical support for Z1's pronounced degradation activity and target specificity observed in our earlier experiments.

To further elucidate how RARα degradation influences the malignant phenotype of CRC, we focused on two proteins that are involved in integral pathways for cell proliferation and invasion: the AKT signaling pathway plays a central role in regulating cell survival and proliferation, and is typically monitored through phosphorylation of AKT at Ser473 (p-AKT Ser473) [39]; matrix metalloproteinase MMP2 is an enzyme that critically promotes tumor invasion and metastasis by degrading extracellular matrix components [40]. Subsequently, the effects of Z1 treatment on the expression levels of p-AKT (Ser473) and MMP2 were assessed by western blotting.

To clarify the regulatory role of RARα degradation in tumor progression, we examined its effects on key downstream signaling components. As shown in Fig. 3M, Z1-induced degradation of RARα resulted in a marked alteration in the expression of two critical downstream proteins, p-AKT (Ser473) and MMP2. Notably, this regulatory link has not been systematically characterized in earlier studies of RARα–SNIPPER models [41]. To further investigate this observation, we performed quantitative chemiluminescence analysis, revealing that although total AKT levels remained unchanged across increasing Z1 concentrations, the phosphorylation of AKT at Ser473 (p-AKT S473) was progressively and significantly suppressed in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3N–P). Collectively, these data indicate that Z1 likely suppresses cellular proliferation by selectively inhibiting AKT activation via RARα degradation. This provides a new mechanistic perspective on the antiproliferative action of Z1.

In addition to identifying proteins associated with cell proliferation, the results also indicated that Z1 regulates MMP2, which is closely linked to cellular invasion. Specifically, treatment with Z1 significantly reduced MMP2 protein expression in cells (Fig. 3N–Q). The present study further clarified that Z1 downregulates MMP2 expression by degrading RARα, a mechanism that suggests degradation disrupts RARα-mediated transcriptional regulation of MMP2, thereby inhibiting the invasiveness of cancer cells. Notably, this regulatory pathway, which appears to play a critical role in tumor invasion, has not been thoroughly examined in previous mechanistic studies on RARα-targeted interventions.

By integrating these findings, we identified Z1 as a novel regulator of AKT S473 phosphorylation and MMP2 expression, a mechanism that complements the established conclusion that “RARα knockout regulates the p21/cyclin D1 pathway [6]”. Together, these discoveries reveal an interconnected molecular network, illustrating how RARα exerts synergistic antitumor effects through multiple downstream pathways. This integrated perspective deepens our mechanistic understanding of RARα-targeted interventions and provides a critical experimental basis for the systematic evaluation of Z1's anti-tumor potential in future studies.

Given that cell cycle detection is a short-term assay requiring relatively high drug concentrations to clearly observe cell cycle arrest, the maximum concentration of Z1 was set at 100 μM. This assay requires long-term culture (typically 7–14 days), and high drug concentrations can lead to excessive cell death, complicating the accurate assessment of colony-forming ability. Thus, the maximum concentration was established at 50 μM. As illustrated in Fig. 4C and D, 50 μM Z1 completely inhibited colony formation, whereas the same concentration of CA77.1 permitted the formation of over 300 colonies. Furthermore, in the Annexin V-FITC/PI double-staining experiments, conducted at a maximum concentration of 100 μM (consistent with the cell cycle assay), 12.5–100 μM Z1 induced late apoptosis in 34.6–55.2% of cells, a rate significantly higher than that observed in the CA77.1-treated group (24.6%) (Fig. 4E and F).

After confirming the antiproliferative activity of Z1, we investigated its effect on cell migration using a scratch assay. The migration rate was assessed by analyzing the scratch area in ImageJ, with a maximum concentration of 100 μM. As shown in Fig. 4G and H, 100 μM CA77.1 did not significantly inhibit cell migration. In contrast, Z1 concentrations from 12.5 to 100 μM demonstrated concentration-dependent cell migration inhibition, with the strongest inhibitory effect (90.4%) observed at 100 μM.

The Transwell assay, conducted with a maximum concentration of 50 μM to prevent excessive cell death from prolonged exposure, confirmed that the reduction in cell invasiveness induced by Z1 is concentration-dependent. Untreated cells (blank control group) easily traversed the polycarbonate membrane, whereas treatment with 50 μM Z1 reduced the cell invasion rate to 10.8%, marking an 81.2% decrease compared with the CA77.1-treated group (Fig. 4I and J).

The cell cycle, colony formation, apoptosis, migration, and invasion assay findings demonstrate that Z1 significantly inhibits CRC cell proliferation, promotes apoptosis, and suppresses migration and invasion. Additionally, we adjusted the maximum drug concentration according to the specific characteristics of various in vitro functional assays, such as short-term detection versus long-term culture, to enhance the accuracy and reliability of the results. These findings indicate that Z1 has significant potential to prevent tumor metastasis.

PsELNs isolation and characterization and Z1/PsELNs construction and performance assessment

Although compound Z1 shows remarkable antitumor activity in vitro, its limitations as a PROTAC molecule, specifically its poor water solubility and low membrane permeability, pose significant challenges for effective delivery and efficacy in vivo. To address this, we employed a stepwise extraction method using “juicing–filtration–low-speed centrifugation–ultracentrifugation” to isolate PsELNs, encapsulating the PROTAC molecule Z1 within the phospholipid bilayer and lumen of PsELNs (Fig. 5A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations (Fig. 5B) revealed that PsELNs have a characteristic “disk-like” structure, aligning with the structural criteria for exosome-like vesicles outlined in the MISEV2023 guidelines [42]. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis (Fig. 5C) indicated an average PsELNs particle size of 116 nm, which effectively exploits the EPR effect for optimal accumulation in tumor tissues. The zeta potential was −5.8 mV (Fig. 5D), a feature that can significantly reduce phagocytic clearance by monocytes and macrophages, thereby extending the in vivo circulation time of the vesicles [43]. An oxygen radical absorbance capacity experiment further validated the antioxidant activity of PsELNs. Further proteomic analysis identified 1045 characteristic proteins in PsELNs (Fig. S6A), with ATP-binding proteins being the most abundant. These proteins, as established in previous studies, enhance vesicle stability by hydrolyzing ATP to generate energy, thereby effectively reducing the risk of vesicle rupture caused by membrane protein denaturation in complex extracellular environments such as blood and body fluids [44]. This mechanism ensures that PsELNs are delivered intact to tumor tissues, providing a structural foundation that enhances subsequent cellular uptake efficiency [45,46]. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotation (Fig. S6B) revealed that PsELNs are also enriched with metal ion-binding proteins and proteins related to the ribosomal pathway. Metal ion-binding proteins maintain charge balance and structural stability of the vesicle membrane by chelating metal ions, whereas ribosomal pathway proteins participate in the synthesis and modification of endogenous vesicle proteins, further optimizing biocompatibility. Additionally, Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis showed that many proteins in PsELNs are involved in substance transport and membrane stability regulation pathways, forming the core molecular basis for the delivery function of natural vesicles. In summary, the protein composition of PsELNs confers excellent structural stability and biocompatibility, making them a highly promising vehicle for tumor-targeted delivery.

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis identified triglycerides as the primary lipid component in PsELNs (Fig. S6C). This key characteristic endows PsELNs with outstanding carrier properties. First, CRC cells naturally favor triglyceride uptake, enabling the substantial accumulation of PsELNs at tumor sites [47]. Second, the high triglyceride content forms a hydrophobic core that effectively stabilizes the encapsulation of hydrophobic drugs, preventing their precipitation or degradation in aqueous environments [48]. Third, triglycerides enhance the vesicle membrane's fluidity and flexibility, reducing the risk of PROTAC leakage during in vivo transport and ensuring the drug's intact delivery to the colorectal target site [49]. Additionally, PsELNs are rich in functional lipids, such as phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylcholine, and ceramide (Fig. S6C & Table S1). These lipids enhance delivery efficiency through a synergistic effect that includes maintaining vesicle structural stability [50], precisely regulating membrane fluidity [51], and promoting vesicle uptake by target cells [52]. Moreover, metabolomic analysis confirmed that PsELNs contain bioactive components like polysaccharides and flavonoids (Fig. S6D). These components preserve the antioxidant properties of Polygonatum sibiricum and protect the structural integrity of the Polygonatum sibiricum vesicles [[53], [54], [55]].

After confirming the physicochemical properties of PsELNs as a drug carrier, we next evaluated their biosafety, a crucial factor for in vivo application. We assessed the cytotoxicity of PsELNs using an MTT assay. Treatment with 0–200 μg/mL of PsELNs maintained the viability of the human CRC cell line HCT116, the human cervical cancer cell line HeLa, the human immortalized keratinocyte cell line HaCaT, and the mouse macrophage cell line Raw 264.7 at levels above 85% (Fig. S7), indicating favorable biocompatibility. This feature is essential for minimizing damage to normal tissues and reducing systemic toxicity risk during in vivo administration, laying a vital foundation for their application. By systematically optimizing the PsELNs-to-Z1 ratio and ultrasonic parameters, we identified optimal drug-loading conditions: a Z1 concentration of 200 μM and a 6-min ultrasound treatment. Under these conditions, the drug-loading efficiency reached 43.1% (Fig. S8), significantly exceeding previously reported values for goji berry vesicles (13.5%) [56] and ginger vesicles (37.2%) [57]. Characterization of the drug-loaded vesicles (Z1/PsELNs) revealed several key findings. First, TEM observations (Fig. 5E) showed that the vesicles maintained an intact phospholipid bilayer structure after drug loading, with no significant structural damage. Second, DLS analysis (Fig. 5F) indicated that the particle size increased to 143 nm, with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 23.4%, which is suitable for in vivo administration. Third, the zeta potential decreased to −11.2 mV (Fig. 5G), suggesting enhanced colloidal stability of the vesicles. Finally, ultraviolet spectroscopy confirmed effective drug release upon vesicle demulsification (Fig. S9), demonstrating that the ultrasound-assisted method facilitates efficient Z1 encapsulation.

To evaluate the functionality of the Z1/PsELNs delivery system in complex in vivo environments, we systematically examined its stability under various simulated physiological and stress conditions. In acidic conditions (pH 5.5) that mimic the tumor microenvironment, dynamic monitoring of drug structural integrity showed that the 24-h retention rate of Z1 in Z1/PsELNs was 77.38 ± 1.71%, compared with only 40.65 ± 2.61% for free Z1. This finding indicates that the PsELNs carrier effectively reduces Z1 degradation in acidic environments (p < 0.005, Fig. 5H) and maintains the drug's structural stability. Under thermal stress conditions (44 °C) simulating in vivo temperature fluctuations, continuous monitoring confirmed that Z1/PsELNs degraded significantly less than free Z1 (p < 0.005, Fig. 5I). This further supports the delivery system's ability to stably retain the drug's active components under temperature stress. Furthermore, under simulated normal physiological conditions (pH 7.4), we assessed the release behavior of Z1/PsELNs over 2–24 h (Fig. S10). The release rate at pH 7.4 (9.51 ± 3.04%) was significantly lower than that at pH 5.5 (27.98 ± 2.92%) after 24 h, indicating that PsELNs encapsulation confers excellent stability and effectively delays Z1 release in physiological environments.​ When stored at −20 °C for 60 days, a significant difference in the Z1 retention rate compared with that in the blank control group was observed only after 48 days (Fig. 5J), demonstrating the PsELNs carrier's excellent protective efficacy in preventing drug leakage. Additionally, after incubating Z1/PsELNs in 10%–50% fetal bovine serum for 72 h, the particle size remained stable at approximately 140 nm, with a PDI < 30% (Fig. S11). These results confirm that Z1/PsELNs exhibit remarkable stability in physiologically relevant environments, providing a reliable foundation for enhancing Z1's in vivo efficacy.

Subsequently, we conducted histopathological examinations to evaluate the tumor-killing effectiveness and biosafety of this intervention strategy. Discussion

This study addresses two major challenges in the clinical treatment of CRC. First, current targeted therapies, including those targeting EGFR and VEGF, are often complicated by drug resistance and show limited effectiveness against new key targets, such as RARα. Second, traditional RARα modulators are constrained by drugability issues, such as poor chemical stability and low oral bioavailability, which impede the complete elimination of the target protein. To overcome these challenges, we devised a synergistic strategy comprising two main components: PROTAC technology to induce the fundamental degradation of RARα, and PsELNs use as an innovative delivery carrier. This approach addresses delivery challenges associated with PROTAC molecules, such as poor water solubility and low membrane permeability.

This study's findings strongly confirm the effectiveness of the proposed strategy. We successfully designed and screened the highly active RARα–PROTAC molecule Z1, which demonstrated a degradation activity (DC50) 43.6 times greater than that of the lead compound CA77.1. This finding highlights the significant potential of PROTAC technology to surpass the traditional “occupancy inhibition” model and efficiently clear the RARα protein. Additionally, our Z1/PsELNs nanodelivery system significantly improved the cellular uptake efficiency and target degradation capability of Z1, demonstrating superior antitumor efficacy and favorable biosafety in in vivo models compared with free Z1. This integrated degradation and delivery approach provides a practical and viable solution to the clinical application challenges associated with PROTAC.

This study underscores the advantages of molecular design. The RARα–SNIPPER, as reported by Yukihiro et al., in 2013, showed relatively low activity, likely due to the lipophilicity–hydrophilicity imbalance caused by the carboxyl group in its ligand, Ch55 [41]. In contrast, Z1 uses CA77.1, a ligand modified with a terminal methyl group. This carboxyl-free structure significantly enhances the molecule's drug-like properties, resulting in degradation activity nearly twice that of RARα–SNIPPER. This finding confirms the benefits of carboxyl-free ligands in PROTAC design and offers new insights for optimizing traditional retinoid drug structures.

Mechanistically, this study revealed that acute protein degradation mediated by RARα–PROTAC primarily inhibits the AKT–MMP2 signaling axis. Previous studies using genetic approaches, such as CRISPR/Cas9 [58], to knock out RARα have more often revealed effects on the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [59]. This discrepancy may stem from differences in the temporal dynamics of intervention methods. PROTAC-mediated degradation is acute and reversible, enhancing its ability to capture the inhibition of rapid signaling events, such as AKT phosphorylation and MMP2 secretion. In contrast, the permanent loss of RARα due to gene knockout forces cells into long-term adaptive remodeling, potentially activating more fundamental and chronic signaling pathways, such as the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, through compensatory mechanisms [60]. Thus, the observed inhibition of the AKT–MMP2 pathway in this study may more accurately reflect RARα′s direct downstream role in CRC progression, providing a more clinically relevant immediate target for intervention in tumor invasion and metastasis.

The clinical application of the potent degradation capabilities of PROTAC hinges on the development of efficient and safe delivery systems. In this study, we selected PsELNs as carriers, capitalizing on their advantages over synthetic systems and our research group's extensive experience with PELNs. Previously, we demonstrated effective drug delivery using vesicles derived from aloe and cucumber [61,62]. This study shows that PsELNs' drug-loading efficiency for Z1 (43.1%) is comparable to that of camel milk-derived exosomes loaded with the PROTAC ARV-825, highlighting the significant potential of natural vesicles for delivering complex drug molecules [63]. Although drug-loading efficiencies vary among vesicles from different sources (13.5% for isoquercetin in goji berry vesicles [56] and up to 55.6% for terbinafine in cucumber vesicles [57]), these differences may stem from the specific properties of the drugs and the vesicles' membrane compositions. Nevertheless, the successful high-efficiency loading of Z1 into PsELNs in this study offers a novel and promising option for PROTAC delivery.

This study has several limitations that warrant further investigation. Specifically, future studies should be conducted in more physiologically relevant or larger-scale models to clarify Z1's subtype selectivity for RARα, elucidate the specific cellular uptake mechanism of PsELNs, and evaluate the long-term in vivo toxicological profile. To address these gaps and advance the current work, we will focus in the future on three primary objectives. First, optimizing the linker structure of Z1 to enhance its degradation efficiency; second, implementing surface functionalization modifications on PsELNs to improve their tumor-targeting capability; and third, integrating our group's advances in aptamer-functionalized DNA nanostructures to develop more intelligent and precise next-generation delivery systems.

In summary, this study demonstrates the feasibility of targeting RARα degradation for CRC treatment. By developing the innovative Z1/PsELNs delivery system, the therapeutic potential of PROTAC technology has been effectively translated into a viable antitumor strategy. This work not only presents a novel, efficient, and safe approach to targeted CRC therapy but also advances the integration of protein-degradation therapeutics with natural carrier technologies. Consequently, it establishes a promising translational pathway for the clinical development of PROTAC-based treatments.

Conclusion

In this study, six PROTAC molecules targeting RARα were designed and synthesized, with the optimal candidate, Z1, identified through systematic screening. Z1 exhibited exceptional RARα degradation efficiency and antitumor activity, establishing a foundational material for further research. To overcome common delivery challenges of PROTAC drugs, we developed an innovative Z1/PsELNs nanodelivery system. Utilizing the carrier properties of PsELNs, this system significantly improved Z1's water solubility and cellular uptake efficiency. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirm that Z1/PsELNs not only enhance the apoptosis-inducing and proliferation-inhibiting effects on tumor cells but also demonstrate good biological safety in animal models, effectively mitigating the toxic side effects associated with traditional antitumor drugs. This work provides an innovative strategy for the efficient, low-toxicity delivery of PROTACs and a new technological pathway for the precise treatment of colorectal cancer.

Building upon these findings, we propose a translational research direction leveraging the intestinal stability and colon-specific accumulation properties of PsELNs to develop an oral PROTAC formulation. This approach is specifically designed to address two key challenges associated with oral administration: gastrointestinal degradation and targeted accumulation at the lesion site. By simultaneously overcoming these barriers, the formulation offers a novel strategy for the precise oral treatment of colorectal cancer and holds significant potential to facilitate the translation of PROTAC technology from fundamental research to clinical application.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chemistry

All intermediates were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich and Macklin, both at analytical grade (≥98%). Purification of intermediates and final products was achieved through column chromatography using silica gel (100-200 mesh, Qingdao Marine Chemical Factory) with gradient elution of petroleum ether/ethyl acetate (v/v = 10:1 to 1:2) or ethyl acetate/methanol (v/v = 20:1 to 1:1). Reaction progress was monitored via thin-layer chromatography (TLC) on silica gel GF254 plates (Qingdao Marine Chemical Co., Ltd.), with fluorescent spots visualized under UV light at 254 nm and 365 nm. All reagents used were of commercial chemical purity grade. Specific reagents, such as THF and DCM, were dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate before use, while others were used directly without additional treatment. In vitro experiments

MTT

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested and seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates. Once the cells adhered, the supernatant was removed, and complete medium with Z1 was added in varying concentrations. After 24 h of drug treatment, the supernatant was discarded, and 150 μL of a 1 mg/mL thiazolyl blue solution (Sigma-Aldrich) was introduced to each well. Following a 4-h incubation, the supernatant was aspirated, and 150 μL of DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, AR) was added. The mixture was then shaken for 15 min, and absorbance was measured at 490 nm.

Western blot

HCT116 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. Experiments began after cell adhesion. Each cell group was treated with Z1 at specified concentrations, with DMSO as the blank control. After 24 h of treatment, cells were trypsinized and collected. The supernatant was removed by centrifugation, and the cell pellet was lysed on ice for 1 h using RIPA lysis buffer. Protein concentration was determined with the BCA assay and adjusted to 3 mg/mL. Samples were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min and stored at −20 °C for later use. Electrophoresis buffer and gels were prepared in advance. Protein samples were mixed with loading buffer and subjected to electrophoresis at 80 V for the stacking gel and 100 V for the separating gel until complete separation was achieved. After electrophoresis, the gel was excised and transferred using an ice-bath “sandwich” method at 200 mA for 2 h. The membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk at room temperature for 4 h, washed three times with 1×TBST for 5 min each, and then washed with 1×TBS for 10 min. The β-actin antibody (1:40000, CST) was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was then washed three times with 1×TBST (5 min each) followed by a 10-min wash with 1×TBS. Subsequently, RARα (1:1000, CST), AKT, AKT S473, MMP2 (1:1000, UpingBio), and additionally CRABP2, CDK7, p38 MAPK, p53, PI3K p85α, JNK, and mTOR (1:1000, Proteintech) antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, the membrane was washed three times with 1×TBST (5 min each) and then with 1×TBS for 10 min. Finally, the PVDF membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:10000, CST) at room temperature for 4 h. Chemiluminescent development was performed using ECL substrate, and gray values were analyzed with Image J and plotted using Origin 8.0.

Genomic DNA contamination was removed according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit with 1 μg of total RNA as template under standard conditions (37 °C for 15 min, 85 °C for 5 s). RT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix in a 20 μL reaction system containing 10 μL of 2× SYBR qPCR Mix, 0.4 μL of forward primer (10 μM), 0.4 μL of reverse primer (10 μM), 2 μL of cDNA template, and 7.2 μL of RNase-free water. Molecular docking

The RARα protein (PDB: 5K13) and the E3 ubiquitin ligase CRBN (PDB: 4CI3) were retrieved from the PDB database, and the cdx format file of Z1 was imported into the docking program. Molecular docking was performed using Autodock Vina. The receptor proteins and small molecule were then loaded using PyMOL 2.0 and Ligplus software. Ultimately, the active sites of the small molecules and the proteins were visualized.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2023.4. The protein was described using the Amber99sb-ildn​ force field, and the system was solvated in a cubic water box using the TIP3P​ water model. Both electrostatic and van der Waals interactions were truncated at 1.0 nm, with long-range electrostatics handled by the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME)​ method. The integration time step was set to 2 fs. Simulations were conducted at 310.15 K (37 °C)​ and 1 bar. The constructed system was initially energy-minimized using the steepest descent algorithm​. This was followed by two equilibration phases: 100 ps under the NVT ensemble​ and 100 ps under the NPT ensemble. Temperature coupling was achieved using the V-rescale​ thermostat, while pressure control was maintained using the Parrinello–Rahman​ barostat. Production MD was subsequently run for 100 ns. Post-simulation analyses, including hydrogen bonding and binding free energy calculations, were performed using the built-in GROMACS modules and the gmx_MMPBSA​ script.

Wound healing assay

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested and seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well in 6-well plates. Once the cells adhered and reached 90% confluence, scratches were made using a 200 μL pipette tip. The wells were then washed three times with PBS to remove any floating cells. Five positions were marked, and the supernatant was aspirated. Complete medium containing Z1 at concentrations of 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 μM was added, with DMSO as the control. Images of the wound areas were taken using an optical microscope at 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, and 48 h. Wound width analysis and closure rate calculations were performed using Image J and Origin 8.0. The scratch closure rate was calculated as follows: (wound migration width at 24 h or 48 h/initial wound width at 0 h) × 100%.

Transwell assay

HCT116 cells underwent serum starvation in RPMI 1640 medium without serum for 12 h. Afterward, the cells were digested with trypsin, and the digestion was halted. The cells were then centrifuged to remove the culture medium, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium, adjusting the cell density to 5 × 105 cells/mL. A 100 μL aliquot of the cell suspension was added to each well of the upper chamber of transwell inserts, with three duplicate wells set up for both control and experimental groups. Z1 was diluted in complete medium to achieve drug concentrations of 0.62, 1.86, 5.56, 16.7, 50, and 150 μM. Then, 600 μL of the drug solution was added to the lower chamber of 24-well plates, and the transwell inserts were carefully placed into the plates for incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the transwell inserts were removed, the culture medium was discarded, and the inserts were washed twice with PBS. They were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and allowed to air-dry. The bottom of the transwell inserts was stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min, and non-migrated cells on the upper surface were gently wiped away with a cotton swab. Finally, the inserts were washed three times with PBS. Five random fields of view were examined using an inverted microscope for cell counting, and the average value was calculated. The migration rate relative to the control group was determined using the formula: Migration rate (%) = (number of migrated cells in the experimental group/number of migrated cells in the control group) × 100%.

Colony formation assay

HCT116 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well and cultured in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. After cell adhesion, 2 mL of drug solutions containing Z1 at concentration gradients of 0.13, 0.41, 1.23, 3.7, 11.1, and 33.3 μM, along with the control drug CA77.1, were added to each well. The medium was replaced every two days. Cultivation continued for 10 to 15 days until each cell cluster (colony) contained more than 50 cells. The medium was discarded, and the wells were rinsed once with PBS. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, after which the fixative was aspirated. Following another PBS rinse, 0.005% crystal violet was added for staining at room temperature for 30 min. The stain was subsequently removed, and the wells were rinsed three times with PBS, air-dried, and photographed. The number of colonies was counted to calculate the colony formation efficiency using the formula: Colony formation efficiency = (number of colonies/total number of seeded cells) × 100%.

Cell cycle assay

HCT116 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight to ensure complete adherence. After adhesion, Z1 was added at concentrations of 100, 33.3, 11.1, 3.7, 1.23, 0.41, and 0.13 μM, along with 100 μM CA77.1 and DMSO as a control, with three replicates for each concentration. The cells were treated for 48 h. After treatment, the supernatant was aspirated, and the cells were washed twice with pre-cooled PBS. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were digested with trypsin, collected, and the supernatant was removed. The cell pellet was resuspended in pre-cooled PBS, centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded, leaving 250 μL of suspension. Then, 450 μL of pre-cooled 70% ethanol was added, mixed thoroughly, and the cells were fixed at 4 °C overnight. Following fixation, cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 6 min to collect the pellet. The pellet was washed with pre-cooled PBS and centrifuged again to remove the supernatant. Cells were resuspended in 0.3 mL PBS and transferred to 1.5 mL EP tubes. RNAse A was added to reach a final concentration of 50 μg/mL, and the mixture was incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min. Subsequently, 5 μL of PI was added, and the cells were stained in the dark at 4 °C for 15 min. The cells were then transferred to flow cytometry tubes for detection using a flow cytometer. Cell cycle profiles were generated with FlowJo software, and percentage bar charts were plotted using Origin 8.0.

Cell apoptosis assay

Begin by preparing a 1× working solution of Dilute Binding Buffer (10×) for later use. Use EDTA-free trypsin to digest the cells, then transfer them to centrifuge tubes and centrifuge at 1000 rpm for 5 min to collect the cells. Wash the cell pellet twice by gently resuspending it in pre-cooled PBS, followed by centrifugation. Resuspend the cells in 1× Binding Buffer to reach a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Transfer 100 μL of the cell suspension to a new tube, add 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 5-10 μL of PI, mix gently, and incubate at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. After staining, add 400 μL of 1× Binding Buffer to each tube, mix thoroughly, and analyze by flow cytometry within 1 h.

Isolation and characterization of PsELNs

The roots of Polygonatum sibiricum were washed, de-rooted, peeled, juiced, and filtered to remove residues. The filtrate was centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the precipitate was discarded. This was followed by further centrifugation steps at 3000 g for 20 min, 10000 g for 30 min, and 100000 g for 30 min, discarding the precipitate each time except for the final step, where the supernatant was discarded. The resulting pellet was resuspended in PBS, and protein concentration was measured using the BCA assay before storage at −80 °C for future use. For morphological observation, 20 μL of vesicles (3 mg/mL) were placed on a carbon support film for 20 min, and excess liquid was removed. Then, 20 μL of 4% paraformaldehyde was added for fixation for 20 min, followed by aspiration of any remaining liquid. Next, 20 μL of 1% uranyl acetate was applied for staining for 3 min, and after removing excess liquid, the sample was air-dried. Vesicle morphology was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). For size and zeta potential analysis, 100 μL of vesicles (1 mg/mL) were mixed with 900 μL of PBS, and particle size was measured via dynamic light scattering (DLS) over 100 scanning cycles. Zeta potential was determined, and the data were processed and analyzed using Origin 8.0.

Omics study

A pre-cooled chloroform-methanol mixture (chloroform:methanol = 2:1) was added to the PsELNs samples. The samples were vortexed for 30 s and incubated on ice for 40 min. Ultrapure water was then added, followed by another 30 s of vortexing and a 10-min ice incubation. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 5 min at room temperature. The lower layer was transferred to a new centrifuge tube and re-extracted with the pre-cooled chloroform-methanol mixture. After 30 s of vortexing and centrifugation at 12,000 g for 5 min, the lower layer was collected. The extract was concentrated using a vacuum concentrator, dissolved in isopropanol, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and subjected to LC-MS analysis.

Antioxidant activity assay

The oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC) method was employed to assess the antioxidant activity of PsELNs, utilizing sodium fluorescein (FL) as the signal source and 2,2-azobis(2-amidinopropane) dihydrochloride (AAPH) as the radical initiator. A solution of 1 mM AAPH and 200 nM FL was thoroughly mixed and incubated in a metal bath at 37 °C for 30 min to generate an adequate concentration of radicals for reaction with FL, thereby establishing a fluorescence quenching system. Subsequently, varying concentrations of PsELNs were introduced into the reaction mixture, which was maintained at 37 °C for an additional 15 min. The fluorescence recovery of FL was measured at an excitation wavelength of 480 nm to evaluate the antioxidant activity of PsELNs.

Preparation of Z1/PsELNs

The linear equation for Z1 was established through UV quantification. Subsequently, PsELNs equivalent to 200 μg of protein were incubated with Z1 stock solutions (50, 100, 150, 200, 250 μM) at 37 °C for 60 min. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, centrifuged again under the same conditions, and resuspended in PBS once more. Demulsification was performed using 11% Triton X-100. Absorbance was measured and applied to the UV-derived linear equation to calculate the drug loading mass, which was used to determine the drug loading efficiency and identify the optimal drug concentration. Ultrasonic treatment was then applied at this optimal concentration for 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 min to determine the best sonication time, thereby finalizing the optimal drug loading conditions.

Serum stability assay

PsELNs and Z1/PsELNs were filtered through 0.22 μm membranes. PsELNs (1 mg/mL) were diluted to 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 μg/mL with PBS containing 10% or 50% FBS. Particle size distribution and PDI were measured for 1 mL samples until no impurity peaks were detected. Subsequently, PsELNs were combined with 10% and 50% FBS to achieve a final concentration of 200 μg/mL and incubated for 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h. Their particle sizes and PDI were then assessed using a particle size analyzer. Data analysis was conducted with Origin 8.0.

Anti-degradation stability assay

Free Z1 and Z1/PsELNs were incubated at pH 5.5 and 7.4. At 4, 8, 12, and 24 h, 200 μL of the supernatant was collected for UV quantification to determine the remaining drug amount. The changes in release rate were analyzed using Origin 8.0. Under the optimal pH condition identified earlier, free Z1 and Z1/PsELNs were further incubated at 44 °C. At the same time intervals (4, 8, 12, 24 h), 200 μL of the supernatant was sampled for UV quantification to measure the remaining drug, with results analyzed and compared using Origin 8.0.

Leakage Rate Assay: To examine the impact of long-term storage on leakage rate, Z1/PsELNs were prepared and divided into 200 μL aliquots, then stored at 4 °C. At various intervals over 60 days, samples were retrieved. Free Z1 was isolated using Sephadex gel micro-column filtration, and the eluate was adjusted to 1 mL with 11% Triton X-100 before being diluted to a suitable concentration for UV absorption analysis. The leakage rate (%) was determined using the formula: [A(Z1, n = 0) - A(Z1, n = i)]/A(Z1, n = 0) × 100%, where n indicates storage days, and i corresponds to 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 15, 30, 40, 50, 60.

Laser confocal microscopy

HCT116 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded at 2.5 × 105 cells per confocal dish in a total volume of 600 μL. After allowing the cells to adhere overnight, the medium was removed, and free Z1 and Z1/PsELNs (both at 5 μM) were added. Three groups were established: a blank control, a 6-h treatment, and a 12-h treatment, each with three replicates. At the end of the treatment period, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were rinsed twice with PBS. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, after which the fixative was aspirated. A 10 μg/mL DiO membrane staining reagent was added for 50 min in the dark, followed by two PBS rinses and then 10 μg/mL PI staining for 30 min in the dark. After two more PBS rinses, the cells were kept moist with PBS and imaged within 2 h using a confocal microscope. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using Image J.

Flow cytometry

HCT116 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well, with a total volume of 2 mL. Free Z1 and Z1/PsELNs were used at a concentration of 5 μM. We established wells for blank control and treatment groups at 6, 12, 18, and 24 h, with three replicates for each group. After the designated treatment time, cells were digested with trypsin, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in PBS and centrifuged twice, discarding the PBS each time. Then, 500 μL of paraformaldehyde was added to each tube, mixed thoroughly, and incubated for fixation for 15 min. Following fixation, samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and cells were resuspended in 1 mL PBS. They were stored at 4 °C in the dark and analyzed within 24 h using a flow cytometer (channel: BV-421). Data were analyzed and plotted using FlowJo 7.6.

In vivo experiments

In vivo imaging

DiR served as a fluorescent probe to assess the in vivo distribution and enrichment of PsELNs. Following quantification, DiR and DiR/PsELNs were administered via tail vein injection into HCT116 tumor-bearing mice at a concentration of 10 mM in a 200 μL volume. Fluorescent distribution images of the nude mice in each treatment group were captured at 12, 24, and 48 h using a small animal imaging system. At 48 h post-administration, the mice were euthanized after imaging. Heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and tumor tissues were collected to examine fluorescent distribution. Data were analyzed through spectral separation using Living Image 4.4. The Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University approved the experiment.

Hemolysis assay

Nude mice had their vibrissae clipped and eyes enucleated for orbital blood collection. Fresh blood was collected into centrifuge tubes, allowed to stand for 45 min, then centrifuged at 4 °C, 1000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded to isolate erythrocyte pellets, which were then resuspended and centrifuged three times with PBS until the supernatant was colorless. These pellets were stored at 4 °C for later use. Z1 and Z1/PsELNs, both at a concentration of 10 μM, were incubated with erythrocytes at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 1000g for 5 min. The supernatant was collected, and its absorbance at 540 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Ultrapure water served as the positive control, while PBS was the negative control. The hemolysis rate was calculated using the formula: Hemolysis rate (%) = [(OD540 (treatment group) - OD540 (PBS group))/(OD540 (ultrapure water group) - OD540 (PBS group))] × 100%.

Tissue sectioning

Twenty-four hours post-final administration, the mice were euthanized, and tissues from the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and tumors were collected. The tissues were fixed in a tissue fixative, followed by paraffin embedding. Serial sections were prepared for staining. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were subjected to hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, while tumor tissues were subjected to H&E staining as well as immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for Ki67 and RARα. All sections were subsequently examined under a microscope to evaluate the histopathological changes.

Antitumor efficacy assay

HCT116 cells (5 × 106) were subcutaneously injected into the right inguinal region of 6-8-week-old Balb/c nude mice. After 1 week, when the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice with CRC models were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5). These groups received treatments with PBS, PsELNs, Z1, or Z1/PsELNs. The drugs were administered every 2 days over a 16-day period. Tumor growth was photographed, and changes in tumor volume and body weight were recorded. At the end of the treatment, the mice were euthanized, and heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and tumor tissues were collected. The tissues were washed twice with PBS, fixed in tissue fixative, and embedded in paraffin for immunohistochemical analysis.
