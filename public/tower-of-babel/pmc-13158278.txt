Intestinal Ultrasound Adds Diagnostic Value and Safety to a Dietitian‐Led Clinic for Disorders of Gut–Brain Interaction
Authors: R. M. Mathias, S. L. Plush, L. E. Edwards, D. Bogatic, L. Bibb, T. M. Goodsall, A. S. Day, R. V. Bryant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDisorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI) affect approximately 40% of the global population. Multidisciplinary models incorporating dietetic management are increasingly used, yet objective assessment tools remain limited. This study evaluated the clinical utility of intestinal ultrasound (IUS) within a dietitian‐led DGBI clinic.MethodsConsecutive patients underwent IUS at baseline and after dietetic intervention. Irritable bowel syndrome severity scores (IBS‐SSS) and sonographic parameters were recorded at both visits. Fecal loading was defined by posterior acoustic shadowing and/or colonic diameter > 50 mm. Patient satisfaction was assessed using a 100‐point Visual Analog Scale (VAS), with scores ≥ 75 indicating satisfaction.ResultsNinety‐seven patients underwent IUS: 85 met Rome IV criteria for irritable bowel syndrome, 7 for functional constipation, and 5 for functional diarrhea. IUS confirmed the clinical diagnosis in 67% (65/97) and led to management changes in 28% (27/97). Fecal loading was identified in 22% (6/27) of patients reporting diarrhea. Mean satisfaction with IUS was high (97; 95% CI: 87.5–100). Sequential IUS was performed in 38 patients; management of IUS‐defined fecal loading significantly reduced colonic diameter (p < 0.001) and posterior acoustic shadowing (p < 0.001), with corresponding improvement in IBS‐SSS scores (p < 0.0001). New inflammatory bowel disease was detected in 3 (8%) patients.ConclusionIUS enhances diagnostic confidence and informs management in a dietitian‐led DGBI clinic. It represents a safe, well‐tolerated, and cost‐effective adjunct to clinical assessment, supporting its integration into multidisciplinary models of care. Further studies should clarify its role in functional gastrointestinal evaluation.

Introduction

Disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI) represent a spectrum of conditions, including irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and functional constipation (FC) [1]. These conditions account for a significant burden globally, both in patient quality of life and economic impact [2, 3]. Recent evidence shows that up to 40% of the global population identifies as having at least one DGBI, with rates of IBS and FC in Australia reported at 4%–5% and 6%–30%, respectively [4, 5]. It is well documented that patients with DGBI undergo more invasive or harmful investigations and generally spend more time interacting with specialist medical care than healthy controls [6, 7]. Furthermore, the appropriate management of patients with DGBI involves a multidisciplinary approach, a resource lacking in both community practice and even tertiary Australian centers [8, 9].

Dietary strategies such as fiber manipulation and supervised low FODMAP diets supported by a dietitian with expertise in gastroenterology are recommended in DGBI management, amongst other strategies [10, 11]. Dietitian‐led gastroenterology clinics have demonstrated benefits in improving patient experience and reducing pressure on outpatient gastroenterology services [12]. Innovative models of care are useful to alleviate pressure on conventional health resources; however, appropriate assessment of patients with DGBI remains a challenge, especially given that limited and directed noninvasive investigations are preferred.

Intestinal ultrasound (IUS) is a safe, reproducible, and noninvasive imaging modality that has been extensively validated in the assessment and monitoring of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [13]. Unlike IBD, in which inflammation contributes to bowel damage, DGBI may be difficult to diagnose and monitor due to a lack of identifiable objective biochemical or radiological changes beyond symptoms [14, 15]. IUS may be useful in DGBI, in that it provides a point of care evaluation of luminal contents, in particular fecal loading [16]. Evaluation of luminal contents can direct management in the commonly encountered setting of overflow diarrhea due to underlying fecal loading [17]. Moreover, IUS is accurate in identifying intestinal inflammation and complications thereof, reducing the likelihood of missing an underlying diagnosis of IBD. IUS also promotes clinician–patient rapport in communicating a diagnosis of DGBI, and empowers patients to understand their symptoms and disease course [18]. IUS has previously been used as a first‐line tool to evaluate low‐risk patients with abdominal symptoms in a gastroenterology service, resulting in reduced invasive investigations, increased dietitian encounters, and reduced total time in care [19].

The combination of an initial dietitian assessment paired with a point‐of‐care intestinal ultrasound (POCUS) poses to be a resource‐efficient and patient‐focused multidisciplinary approach for identifying and managing low‐risk patients with DGBI. The aim of this study is to evaluate the role of IUS in managing low‐risk patients with a diagnosed DGBI alongside expert gastroenterology dietitian advice.

Methods

Study Design

This was a prospective observational study at a single tertiary gastroenterology service in South Australia. Electronic referrals were screened by a senior gastroenterology dietitian with supervision from a gastroenterologist (Figure 1). Inclusion criteria for the dietitian‐led DGBI clinic included those aged < 40 with abdominal symptoms (pain, diarrhea, constipation), fecal calprotectin < 50 μg/L, and CRP < 8 mmol/L. Exclusion criteria included rectal bleeding, unexplained weight loss, personal or family history of inflammatory bowel disease or colorectal cancer, and abnormal pathology outside the values for inclusion. Also eligible for the dietitian‐led DGBI clinic were patients outside of the inclusion criteria if reviewed by a gastroenterologist in general outpatient clinics.

Clinic model from primary care referral through to discharge following dietetic consult and intestinal ultrasound assessment.

During an initial dietetic consultation, patients underwent a POCUS by a gastroenterologist with expertise in IUS. Results of the POCUS were included in the clinical decision‐making, informing dietary management with the dietitian. Dietary strategies are categorized and described in Table S1. Patient care could be escalated to conventional ambulatory gastroenterology care pathways due to clinical concern at any point during the study period.

At the end of the study, patients underwent a second IUS along with a dietetic consultation to assess changes in sonographic parameters and symptom scores. Patients completed an irritable bowel syndrome symptom severity score (IBS‐SSS) at the initial visit and subsequent visits. A change in IBS‐SSS of greater than or equal to 50 points was considered a clinically significant response [20]. A bespoke satisfaction survey (a 100‐point Visual Analog Scale) on patient experience with POCUS was completed on the final study visit, with a cut‐off of 75 points being consistent with satisfaction with IUS.

IUS

Patients underwent a POCUS assessment by a Gastrointestinal Network of Intestinal Ultrasound (GENIUS)‐accredited gastroenterologist (> 1000 IUS scans). GENIUS is a national IUS accreditation organization within Australasia. The absence of bowel wall thickening and of color doppler signal was noted to exclude active inflammation. If bowel wall thickness was found to be > 3 mm or patients had a positive Doppler signal within the bowel wall, the patient was removed from the study and escalated to the gastroenterology service for further management. Luminal contents were evaluated using established metrics, including colonic diameter (measured in mm), stool contents assessment (empty, solid, liquid, mixed), and posterior acoustic shadowing (present/absent) [16]. The presence of gas was a binary variable, as there are no currently accepted metrics for quantifying or stratifying volumes of colonic gas in the literature [21]. The Leech score is a validated fecal loading score used in abdominal x‐ray to describe the amount of stool within the colon and the presence or absence of distension. The score was adopted in the study to suit ultrasound reporting and to include the rectum as its own segment (Figure S1) [22]. It has been used in other validation studies of IUS and luminal contents assessment [23]. Images were obtained on a Canon Aplio i700 with linear high frequency (2–18 MHz) and curvilinear low frequency (3–5 MHz) probes. IUS was performed at the index visit and at discharge from the dietitian‐led DGBI clinic. At any point during the clinical service, the gastroenterologist or dietitian could initiate an additional POCUS.

IUS in DGBI Work‐Up

Rome IV criteria were applied to patients managed within the dietitian‐led DGBI clinic [24]. IUS evaluation of luminal contents was then described as concordant or discordant with the Rome IV criteria diagnosis. An example of a concordant study was when the dietitian's assessment was one of Rome IV FC, and the IUS revealed fecal loading, augmenting diagnostic confidence and the appropriate management strategy. Alternatively, in a patient meeting the criteria for Rome IV diarrhea‐predominant IBS, an IUS finding of fecal loading would be discordant with the clinical diagnosis and alter the management strategy.

Statistical Analysis

All colonic segments were analyzed separately and defined as a “rectum,” “left,” or “right” colon. Analysis of paired comparisons and proportions was performed using Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests, paired Student's t‐test, McNemar's test, and Fisher's exact test. Statistical software used included GraphPad Prism version 10.2.2 for MacOS, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA.

Ethics

Ethics approval was provided by the Central Adelaide Local Health Network Human Ethics and Research Committee (2021_HRE00185).

Results

Of 126 patients seen in the dietitian‐led DGBI clinic (Table 1), baseline IUS was performed in 76% (97/126) of patients. Amongst the total cohort, 48% (60/126) of patients completed IBS‐SSS surveys at initial and follow‐up visits. Mean IBS‐SSS scores were significantly reduced at follow‐up visits with a mean reduction of 87 points (95% CI: 54–120; p < 0.0001) for 60 paired cases (Figure 2). At the follow‐up visit, 61% (59/97) of patients completed the IUS service satisfaction survey with a median satisfaction score of 97 (IQR: 87–100). There were 29 patients (24%) who did not have a baseline IUS. This was due in part to scheduling in this real‐world clinic model, in which patients may have been scheduled by the hospital for a diet clinic when the gastroenterologist was not available. These patients then did not attend for a follow‐up appointment for the IUS to be performed to assess concordance with the dietetic assessment.

Additional Value to Clinic

IUS Change to Management

In 70% (65/97) of patients, the IUS evaluation of luminal contents was concordant with the initial diagnostic Rome IV label applied by the gastroenterology dietitian (Figure 3, Table 1). However, in 32/97 (32%) patients, the IUS evaluation of luminal contents was discordant with the initial Rome IV label, prompting a change of management strategy in these patients. An example of a discordant assessment would be when the dietetic assessment assigned a diagnosis of diarrhea, but the IUS identified fecal loading, changing the diagnosis to constipation with overflow diarrhea and resulting in a change in management. An alternate scenario is one in which the dietitian may diagnose constipation based on symptoms; however, a normal stool pattern with no significant fecal loading was detected on IUS. This then resulted in a change in management strategy based on IUS findings. Dietetic management strategies based on POCUS are shown in Table S1.

Management strategies following IUS assessment during the initial dietetic consultation. Management was deemed concordant or discordant depending on whether the assessment by the dietitian was confirmed by the sonographic findings. IUS Identification of Organic Pathology

Overall, 5% (5/97) of patients required escalation of care beyond the dietitian‐led DGBI clinic (Figure 3). Three were newly diagnosed cases of ileal Crohn's disease on IUS, and two developed new abnormal results on blood testing during the study. These cases would have otherwise been triaged as a low‐risk category for review based on clinical information included in the outpatient referral.

Of the 97 patients who underwent IUS, 38 patients presented for a second visit in which a sequential IUS pre‐ and post‐management was performed. Left and right colon IUS assessments were available for all 38 patients, while rectal assessment was available for 19 of 38 (50%) due to limitations with transabdominal views [25]. Significant changes were seen in colonic diameter and the presence of posterior acoustic shadowing (PAS) at the follow‐up visit in patients with fecal loading (Table 2).

Symptom Scores and Patient Satisfaction

Of the 38 patients with two IUS assessments, 86% (33/38) had paired IBS‐SSS scores. Overall, there was a significant improvement in IBS‐SSS score amongst the 38 patients (mean difference: −91 points, 95% CI: −53, −130; p < 0.0001; Figure 5). There was a significant improvement in IBS‐SSS in the group that underwent catharsis (18/38) due to fecal loading (mean difference: 97 points, 95% CI: 38, 156; p = 0.005). In the 12 of 38 who had another form of dietetic management, there was a significant improvement in IBS‐SSS scores of 83 points (95% CI: 33, 132; p = 0.005).

Discussion

The addition of IUS to a dietitian‐led DGBI clinic alters clinical management, provides a safety net for alternative diagnoses, and is met with high levels of patient satisfaction. Previous studies employing IUS in a low‐risk outpatient clinic model have demonstrated that the use of IUS results in fewer contact episodes and a shorter median time in the clinic service [19]. These findings provide evidence to substantiate guidelines recommending the expansion of IUS into the domain of DGBI assessment [16, 26].

Incorporation of IUS into a dietitian‐led DGBI clinic can not only aid in clinical diagnosis and subsequent management but also act as a safety net to identify missed pathology. The Rome IV criteria traditionally employ a constellation of symptoms to diagnose a DGBI [1]. Using noninvasive testing such as blood and stool tests, patients are deemed suitably low‐risk and, as such, are eligible for a DGBI label given the absence of organic pathology [27, 28]. The pitfall of this strategy, as demonstrated in this study, is that the diagnosis of patient pathology can be missed. In isolated terminal ileal Crohn's disease, fecal calprotectin is not always elevated [29]. IUS provides a safe, noninvasive, and reliable test to provide confidence and safety in the clinical diagnosis of DGBI, concurrently allowing for evaluation of luminal contents. Moreover, IUS offers an alternative to CT, which is associated with ionizing radiation and commonly deployed in this cohort [6].

IUS can accurately define luminal contents and can be an adjunct to the traditional symptom‐only evaluation of DGBI diagnosis. Studies have demonstrated IUS to be accurate in the assessment of luminal contents when compared with CT [23, 30]. In this study, catharsis for patients with IUS‐defined fecal loading resulted in a significant improvement in IBS‐SSS symptom scores. Paradoxical or “overflow” diarrhea, in which liquid stool leaks past impacted feces, can lead patients to report symptoms of classic diarrhea [31]. The Rome IV criteria for functional diarrhea and IBS with diarrhea predominance can still misclassify a patient presenting with loose stools where fecal loading and constipation are the underlying cause [1, 32]. Therapy for diarrhea, such as a low FODMAP diet or pharmacologic agents, in this situation may worsen underlying constipation or fecal loading and thereby symptoms. One study demonstrated that whilst there is minimal correlation between IUS findings and symptoms, the identification of fecal loading on IUS helped in differentiating fecal loading with “overflow” from true diarrhea [17]. Accurately identifying a cause for symptoms and developing a tailored treatment strategy can lead to improvements in outcomes for patients.

IUS resulted in high degrees of satisfaction amongst patients in the dietitian‐led DGBI clinic. Studies have demonstrated the benefit of utilizing IUS to help patients better understand their condition and improve clinician–patient rapport [18]. Importantly, the doctor–patient relationship in DGBI management is key to fostering mutual understanding, helping patients to make sense of symptoms and improve their ability to manage their illness [33, 34]. Allowing a patient to visualize the contents of their bowel and understand how this relates to their symptomology and treatment plan can be empowering [18]. Following dietetic advice, sonographic parameters were responsive to therapy, particularly in those patients who underwent catharsis for fecal loading. There was a trend toward a larger colonic diameter in the left colon following catharsis; however, this was not significant. One possibility is that the movement of stool from the right to the left colon increases stool volume and thus diameter; however, without formal motility studies, this is not a clinically significant observation but may serve as a basis for paired motility studies with IUS in this area. These findings are useful not only to guide therapy but also to allow patients to see tangible changes in their bowel following shared decision‐making on their treatment strategy.

This study has several strengths. IUS parameters for constipation have been validated and are accurate in the assessment of luminal contents [23]. The parameters chosen for this study were agreed upon by experts in the field and guided by previous literature exploring IUS in the assessment of DGBI [16, 26]. Limitations of this study include the small number of patients who presented for follow‐up IUS, limiting the number of patients available to assess responsiveness to sonographic parameters. While the reasons for this cannot be definitively determined, this may reflect the fluctuating nature of these conditions, where patients often seek care during symptomatic periods and disengage following symptom improvement [24, 35].

Another limitation is related to the classification of IBS according to the Rome IV criteria. Subtyping is based on stool form thresholds rather than overall symptom pattern, which may lead to misclassification of patients with a predominantly constipated phenotype [36]. Patients with intermittent loose stools may be classified as IBS‐M, or as IBS‐U if strict frequency thresholds are not met, despite underlying constipation leading to a discordance in the dietitian assessment and the IUS findings [32, 37]. This limitation of symptom‐based criteria alone supports the use of IUS as a complementary tool in designing a therapeutic strategy based on both patient‐reported symptoms and objective findings.

This study demonstrates that point‐of‐care IUS can help direct the management of patients with DGBI in an innovative dietitian‐led model of care. IUS lends safety to up‐front allied health assessment and management, identifying patients with organic pathology not evident on initial investigations. Patient satisfaction with the use of IUS in an outpatient DGBI clinic lends credence to its ongoing application in this setting. Further studies evaluating the economic and clinical benefits of IUS in DGBI clinics are warranted, as well as clinical trials comparing clinics with and without IUS integration.
