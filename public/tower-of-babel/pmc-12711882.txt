Quantitative modeling of the non-monotonic sensitivity of defect-engineered graphene sensors
Authors: Sunmog Yeo, Junhyeok Seo, Young Jun Yoon, Kibeom Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDefect engineering has been explored as a means to improve the performance of graphene-based gas sensors. However, sensor sensitivity often exhibits a non-monotonic relationship with defect density, presenting challenges for device optimization. In this study, we introduce a quantitative physical model aimed at describing and explaining this behavior. Graphene samples were irradiated with deuteron ions to generate a controlled range of defect densities, which were characterized using Raman spectroscopy. Sensitivity to hydrogen showed a Λ-shaped dependence on defect density, with a peak response observed at an intermediate concentration. This behavior is attributed to the competing effects of signal enhancement and structural degradation induced by defects. The proposed model expresses sensitivity as a function of two experimentally derived parameters: an activation coefficient (Cn) and a degradation coefficient (γn). These parameters provide physical insight into the underlying sensing mechanism.Keywords: Graphene, Gas sensor, Defect engineering, Ion beam irradiation, Quantitative model, Non-monotonic behaviorSubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

The development of low-power, room-temperature gas sensors is pivotal for advancing portable environmental monitoring and point-of-care diagnostics. Traditional metal-oxide semiconductor sensors, while offering high sensitivity, rely on elevated operating temperatures, resulting in substantial power consumption and integration challenges.1,2 These limitations have accelerated the search for alternative sensing materials, positioning graphene as a highly promising candidate due to its unique physical properties.3–8 Graphene’s atomically thin, two-dimensional lattice endows it with an exceptionally high surface-to-volume ratio, making its electronic characteristics particularly charge carrier density extremely sensitive to ambient conditions.9 This sensitivity underpins its chemiresistive sensing behavior, where adsorbed gas molecules modulate electrical resistance by acting as electron donors or acceptors.10,11 However, the chemically inert basal plane of pristine graphene, dominated by sp2 hybridized bonding, weakly interacts with most gas species via van der Waals forces. This weak interaction limits adsorption energy, resulting in modest sensitivity and sluggish response kinetics.12 To address these intrinsic limitations, defect engineering has emerged as an effective strategy for enhancing surface reactivity. Early studies relied on chemical treatments and plasma exposure to introduce structural imperfections, which improved sensing performance but lacked reproducibility and precision.13–17 In contrast, ion beam irradiation a well-established technique in the semiconductor industry offers fine control over defect generation by adjusting ion species, energy, and dosage, enabling systematic modulation of graphene’s properties.18–22 Experimental evidence consistently demonstrates that defect-induced activation significantly enhances gas sensitivity.23–25 Yet, the relationship between defect density and sensing performance is non-monotonic: sensitivity improves with increasing defect density up to an optimal point, beyond which it deteriorates.26–28 This suggests a trade-off between the creation of reactive sites and the degradation of charge transport pathways. This phenomenon has been repeatedly observed, yet the absence of a quantitative physical model has hindered rational sensor optimization. Consequently, researchers have largely relied on trial-and-error approaches. In this study, we present a physics-based model to explain the observed non-monotonic trend. The model attributes this behavior to the two competing physical effects of activation and degradation, which are described by an activation coefficient and a degradation coefficient, respectively.

Experimental details

The devices were fabricated on commercial single-layer graphene, grown by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) on a copper foil catalyst and subsequently transferred onto heavily doped silicon wafers with a 300 nm thick thermally grown SiO2 dielectric layer. The quality and monolayer nature of the transferred graphene were confirmed by Raman spectroscopy. A photolithography step was performed to define the source and drain electrode patterns. Metal contacts consisting of Ti/Au (5 nm/50 nm) were deposited by e-beam evaporation. The prepared devices were irradiated with deuteron (D+) ions at the Korea Multipurpose Accelerator Complex (KOMAC), utilizing an electrostatic accelerator. The ion energy was maintained at a constant 120 keV to ensure that the ions fully penetrate the graphene layer and that the primary interaction mechanism is collisional energy transfer rather than implantation. The ion dose was systematically varied from 5 × 1013 to 1 × 1015 ions/cm2 in a high-vacuum target chamber (pressure < 5 × 10–6 Torr). To ensure uniform irradiation across the entire device area, the ion beam was electrostatically raster scanned over a 0.5 × 1 cm2 area. The total accumulated dose was precisely measured in-situ using a Faraday cup connected to a current integrator.

Structural changes in the irradiated graphene were characterized using a Renishaw InVia Reflex confocal Raman spectrometer. A 532 nm solid-state laser was used for excitation, focused onto the sample through a 100 × objective lens with a numerical aperture of 0.9, resulting in a spot size of approximately 1 μm. To prevent any laser-induced heating or annealing of the defects, the incident power on the sample was kept below 1 mW. For each sample, spectra were acquired from at least ten different points to ensure and verify the homogeneity of the irradiation.

The gas sensing performance was evaluated in a custom-built, sealed stainless-steel chamber with a volume of approximately 50 cm3. The flow of gases into the chamber was precisely regulated using a set of calibrated mass flow controllers. The devices were mounted on a probe station inside the chamber, and electrical connections were made to external measurement instrumentation. All measurements were conducted at room temperature. The devices were first stabilized in a flow of dry air until a stable baseline resistance (R₀) was achieved. Subsequently, the target gas, 1000 ppm of hydrogen (H2) balanced in dry air, was introduced into the chamber at the same flow rate of 10 sccm. The device resistance was continuously monitored by applying a constant DC bias voltage and measuring the current using the source meters(current: Keysight B2901A, voltage: Keysight 34970A). The sensor response (or sensitivity, S) is defined as the relative change in resistance: S [%] = (Rgas − R₀) /R₀ × 100, where Rgas is the resistance after a fixed exposure time of 600 s.

Structural characterization of ion-induced defects

Raman spectroscopy provides a powerful non-destructive probe of the sp2 carbon lattice, offering detailed information on defect structures.29–31 The representative spectra of our samples are shown in Fig. 1. The pristine graphene exhibits a sharp G peak at ~ 1595 cm−1 and a symmetric 2D peak at ~ 2700 cm−1, with an intensity ratio I(2D)/I(G) > 2 and a negligible D peak ~ 1352 cm−1, all of which are characteristic of high-quality monolayer graphene.32 Upon deuteron irradiation, the D peak emerges and its intensity grows in proportion to the ion dose. In addition to the prominent D peak, a shoulder peak on the high-frequency side of the G peak, known as the D' peak (~ 1602 cm⁻1), is observed to emerge at low to moderate ion doses (5 × 1015 and 1 × 1014 ions/cm2). The appearance of the D' peak is another characteristic signature of defect introduction in the graphene lattice, as part of the initial stage of disorder. At higher doses, the D' peak becomes indistinguishable as both the G and D' peaks broaden and merge, which is a typical evolution as the disorder progresses.29,32 Specifically, the intensity ratio of the D and G peaks, I(D)/I(G), allows for a quantitative analysis of the point-like defect density (nD) and the average inter-defect distance (LD). nD is calculated from the relationship between the I(D)/I(G) ratio and the excitation laser wavelength (λL).33 The prefactor in this relationship can vary with the type and stage of disorder and the excitation energy, while a comprehensive analysis of this topic has also been reported.34 In this study, using λL = 532 nm, the following relation is applied33: nD(cm-2) = (1.8 × 1022/λL4)∙I(D)/I(G). Subsequently, is determined from the calculated using a physical model that considers an effective circular area for each defect: LD(nm) = √{1014/(πnD)}. These relations are known to provide a reliable estimate in the low-defect-density regime, which is typically characterized by an inter-defect distance LD > 10 nm. The calculated values for each dose, shown in Table 1, all fall within this valid range.

Quantitative model for sensor sensitivity

To quantitatively account for the observed Λ-shaped sensitivity behavior, we developed a physical model grounded in the fundamental mechanisms of chemiresistive sensing. The sensor response is approximated by the relative change in charge carrier concentration, expressed as S ∝ − ∆n/n0, under the assumption that charge transfer dominates over mobility variations in this system.10 Here, n0 is the initial carrier concentration and Δn is the change induced by gas exposure. Our model posits that the sensitivity is governed by the product of two competing physical effects. (i) Activation-Dominated Regime: The potential signal strength is proportional to the number of available active sites, represented by nD; (ii) Degradation-Dominated Regime: The efficiency of signal transduction decreases as the conductive lattice becomes increasingly disrupted. Assuming defects are introduced randomly and independently, their spatial distribution follows Poisson statistics. The probability that a critical signal pathway remains free of disruptive defects i.e., the zero-event probability decays exponentially with increasing defect density. By combining these effects, the sensitivity can be modeled as: S(nD)≈Cn·nDexp(− γnnD), where Cn is the activation coefficient (or per-defect efficiency) and γn is the degradation coefficient (or lattice fragility). This expression is not merely an empirical fit. Instead, it is derived from the physical principles that govern signal generation and transduction within the sensor and is also grounded in statistical theories describing charge transport in defect-rich materials. Given the limited dataset, a two-step fitting procedure is employed. First, γn is estimated from the experimentally observed peak position nD,peak, using the relation γn≈1/nD,peak. With γn fixed, Cn is determined by fitting the resulting single-parameter model to the full dataset. Using this approach, the extracted fitting parameters were determined to Cn≈32.7 × 10–11%⋅cm2 and γn≈2.94 × 10–11 cm2. The fitted curve, represented by the dashed line in Fig. 4, exhibits good agreement with the experimental data. Despite the limited dataset, this simplified fitting method yields an intuitive and physically grounded approximation. Notably, the extracted parameters possess clear physical significance. Cn characterizes the intrinsic sensing efficiency of an individual defect site, while γn captures the structural fragility of the graphene lattice. With access to more extensive datasets, the parameter γn and Cn can be determined with greater statistical reliability using standard non-linear regression analysis. Accordingly, the relationship nD,opt = 1/γn provides a useful approximation for estimating the defect density that yields an optimal response.

Conclusion

In this study, we propose a quantitative model to explain the non-monotonic sensitivity observed in defect-engineered graphene. By considering the balance between defect activation and lattice degradation, the model captures the characteristic Λ-shaped sensor response with good agreement to experimental trends. One of the main insights from this work is the identification of two physically interpretable parameters, per-defect efficiency (Cn) and the lattice fragility (γn), which help assess the factors influencing sensor performance. These parameters offer a useful way to distinguish between performance limitations caused by low defect activity and those arising from a compromised charge transport pathway. The relationship nD,opt = 1/γn derived from the model, also offers a method for estimating the optimal defect density. Building on the results of this study, the proposed quantitative model can be used to investigate other aspects of sensor performance. For instance, it offers a basis for optimizing device parameters to improve metrics such as the limit of detection and selectivity. Furthermore, future experimental work could explore the model’s applicability across a broader range of gas species and under varying environmental conditions, such as humidity.
