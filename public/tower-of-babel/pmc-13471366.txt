Primary care physicians’ perspectives on managing mild-to-moderate COVID-19 in populations at risk for progression to severe disease: a survey-based study
Authors: Carmel Kasher, Tal Distelman-Menachem, Avital Bareket-Samish, Shirley Shapiro Ben David
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntiviral medications are recommended for outpatients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 at risk for complications. We evaluated the knowledge of primary care physicians (PCPs) regarding mild-to-moderate COVID-19 and its treatment in outpatients at risk for progression to severe disease.MethodsIn this survey-based study, PCPs answered 15 closed-ended questions regarding COVID-19 and its treatment. The predefined sample size was 150.ResultsOverall, 150 PCPs completed the questionnaire between 9/2024 and 10/2024; 3.3%, 14.0%, 26.0%, and 56.7% of responders had 0-5, 6-10, 11-20, and ≥ 21 years of medical practice experience, respectively. Overall, 98.0% of participants knew that COVID-19 is not a seasonal disease, 66.7% that COVID-19 poses significant risks for certain people, 32.7% knew the number of COVID-19 deaths in Israel in 2023 was > 500, and only 17.3% read the report published by the Ministry of Health (MoH) showing an increase in COVID-19 cases and hospitalizations in the preceding 2 months. Additionally, only 48.0% were familiar with the MoH recommendations reported that, for treating mild-to-moderate COVID-19 in ambulatory patients at risk of progression to severe disease, they would prescribe antiviral medications. When given a specific case (80-year-old, mild symptoms), only 60.7% would prescribe antiviral treatment. Moreover, 48.7% would recommend an antigen test to patients with COVID-19-like symptoms. Most participants (90.7%) were aware of the differences between the various antiviral medications; 79.3% knew about the efficacy of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir and only 76.7% had some experience with nirmatrelvir-ritonavir. Interestingly, 40.0% responded that drug-drug interactions with nirmatrelvir-ritonavir deter them from using it, and 32.0% were not familiar with tools for managing drug-drug interactions. Lastly, nearly half (47.3%) were not familiar with the recommendations of international health authorities regarding antiviral treatments for COVID-19.ConclusionsOur study demonstrated gaps in knowledge and awareness regarding COVID-19 burden, treatment, antiviral medications, guidelines for managing mild-to-severe disease, and the handling of relevant drug–drug interactions. These findings highlight the need for targeted educational interventions and improved clinical decision-support tools to facilitate timely and appropriate diagnosis and antiviral use, prevent underdiagnosis, enhance adherence to treatment guidelines, and reduce complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03358-7.Keywords: Antiviral treatment, Comorbidities, Coronavirus, COVID-19, Drug-drug interactions, Family physician, Survey

Background

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) outbreak, caused by the novel coronavirus SARS-CoV-2, began in China in 12/2019, and by 3/2020 had spread globally and was considered a pandemic [1]. The cumulative number of COVID-19 cases worldwide reported to the World Health Organization (WHO) reached approximately 779 million by 9/2025 [2]. In Israel, the first cases were identified in 2/2020 [3], and the cumulative number of cases approached 5 million by 9/2025 [2].

Managing the pandemic required coordinated public health measures targeting both outpatients with mild-to-moderate disease and hospitalized patients with severe illness [4, 5]. From the early stages of the pandemic, most patients with COVID-19 were managed in the community, placing primary care physicians (PCPs) at the forefront of early diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment decisions. An Israeli study conducted during the first half of 2020 demonstrated that centralized triage followed by primary care management of outpatients was safe and effective [6], further highlighting the central role of PCPs in outpatient COVID-19 care. In particular, timely outpatient management became increasingly important with the introduction of antiviral therapies, as their clinical benefit depends heavily on early initiation after symptom onset. Therefore, PCP decision-making directly influences whether eligible patients receive treatment within the narrow therapeutic window required to prevent disease progression.

With the first approval of COVID-19 vaccines in December 2020, vaccination became a key strategy for controlling the pandemic, alongside the development of antiviral therapies for patients with mild-to-moderate disease who are at risk for progression to severe illness [7–9]. The two recommended antiviral treatments in 2024 were remdesivir (intravenously administered, approved in 2020), and nirmatrelvir-ritonavir (oral, approved in 2023). These drugs must be initiated as early as possible after diagnosis, within 7 (remdesivir) or 5 (nirmatrelvir-ritonavir) days of symptom onset [8, 9].

According to the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines from 2023, patients at high risk for disease progression, such as older adults, individuals with comorbidities, or immunocompromised patients, may benefit from antiviral therapy [10]. During the study period in 2024, antiviral therapy for mild-to-moderate COVID-19 in Israel was prescribed according to Ministry of Health guidance implemented by the health maintenance organizations (HMOs) [11]. Outpatient services are provided by four non-profit HMOs, and all residents have universal healthcare coverage. Antiviral therapy is included in this coverage and is provided free of charge. Most mild-to-moderate COVID-19 cases were managed primarily by PCPs. For example, in the largest HMO, patients with confirmed COVID-19 were eligible for nirmatrelvir–ritonavir if their oxygen saturation was ≥ 93%, treatment was initiated within 5 days of symptom onset, and they were classified as at risk for progression to severe disease based on age and comorbidities (Supplementary Table 1) [12].

Gaps in adherence to COVID-19 treatment guidelines in the outpatient setting may result in underuse of antiviral therapies, potentially leading to disease progression, hospitalization, and increased mortality [8–10]. Despite clear guideline recommendations, limited evidence exists regarding how well PCPs understand and apply evolving outpatient COVID-19 treatment recommendations in routine clinical practice. In particular, data are scarce on physicians’ knowledge, awareness, and decision-making regarding antiviral therapy for ambulatory patients at risk for disease progression.

This study aimed to evaluate Israeli primary care physicians’ knowledge, awareness, and self-reported practices regarding the management of mild-to-moderate COVID-19, with a specific focus on the use of antiviral treatment in ambulatory patients at risk for progression to severe disease.

Methods

Study design

The study reports on data from an online survey in which PCPs answered questions regarding COVID-19, populations at risk for progression to severe disease, and antiviral medications.

Physicians who took part in this survey had previously registered on the Medic website and agreed to receive messages, including surveys. The data were handled in compliance with applicable data protection regulations. Since all the data were anonymized, no informed consent was required. The survey was non-interventional and did not provide clinical data; hence, ethics approval was not required. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study design, data collection, and statistical analyses were conducted independently of the funding source.

Participant selection and recruitment

All the physicians surveyed were PCPs. A link to an online survey was sent by email to 5,052 PCPs in Israel, who previously agreed to participate in survey studies. The study was designed to focus on PCPs whose COVID-19 patients were likely to meet the criteria for antiviral treatment for mild-to-moderate COVID-19. Therefore, only physicians who responded to the selection question about the age range of their patients, noting that the majority of their patients were at least 50 years, were given the opportunity to complete the questionnaire. Participants who completed the questionnaire received modest remuneration (a voucher for coffee and a pastry). The predefined sample size was 150, primarily determined by feasibility and expected response rate rather than a formal power calculation. When 150 physicians completed the questionnaire, the link became inactive for subsequent responders.

Survey questions

The online survey was in Hebrew and included 15 closed-ended COVID-19-related questions where only 1 response could be submitted, focusing on general COVID-19-related knowledge (4 questions), populations at risk for progression to severe disease (4 questions), and antiviral treatments (7 questions) (Table 1).

In addition, basic demographic data were collected, including years of medical practice experience and the geographic area in which they practice medicine. For the latter, participants were allowed to submit multiple responses.

Data analysis

The sample size was 150 participants, resulting in a margin of error of 8.0%. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the responders and the survey results. The chi-square test was used to compare the proportion of participants responding in alignment with current evidence/guidelines across subgroups by years of medical practice experience and geographic area of the practice (North, Central, South).

SPSS versions 30 and 31 were used for the analyses. All tests were 2-sided. p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 208 PCPs responded to the survey between 9/2024 and 10/2024, of whom 58 (27.9%) were excluded from completing the survey after responding that the majority of their patients were younger than 50 years. The majority of participants (56.7%) had ≥ 21 years of medical practice experience, whereas only a small minority (3.3%) had 0—5 years of medical practice experience (Table 2). In response to the question regarding the location of their practice, 2 responders had duplicate answers and were removed from the analysis. Of the 148 responders with unique responses, the majority (71.6%) practiced medicine in Central Israel (Table 2).

Responder’s knowledge about general COVID-19-related topics

Figure 1 presents participants’ responses to questions about general COVID-19-related knowledge. Supplementary Table 2 presents the responses overall and by respondents’ subgroups based on years of medical practice experience and the geographic area of their practice. Almost all participants (98.0%) knew that COVID-19 is not a seasonal disease, and a majority (66.7%) responded that COVID-19 poses significant risks for certain people. Only about a third of the participants (32.7%) knew the number of COVID-19 deaths in Israel in 2023 (> 500). The majority of participants (74.0%) were aware of an increase in COVID-19 cases and hospitalizations in the preceding two months; 56.7% learned this from colleagues, while only 17.3% were informed by a report published by the MoH.

Subgroup analyses by years of experience and geographic region

Overall, responses were largely consistent across subgroups, with only minor variations observed. Recognition that COVID-19 may be dangerous for certain populations was similar across years of experience and geographic regions, with no statistically significant differences (p = 0.78 and p = 0.89, respectively). Knowledge of COVID-19–related mortality in Israel in 2023 was heterogeneous across all groups, with no significant differences by years of experience or geographic region (p = 0.50 and p = 0.75, respectively). Most participants correctly identified COVID-19 as a year-round disease with fluctuations in morbidity, with no significant differences by years of experience (p = 0.74). A statistically significant difference was observed by geographic region (p = 0.037), with correct responses slightly lower in the South (90.9%) compared with the North (100%) and the Center (99.1%). However, overall recognition of COVID-19’s non-seasonal nature remained high across all regions (Supplementary Table 2).

Responder’s knowledge on populations at risk for progression to severe disease

Figure 2 presents the responses of the participants to questions referring to the management of mild-to-moderate COVID-19 in patients at risk for progression to severe disease. Supplementary Table 3 presents the responses overall as well as by years of medical practice experience and the geographic area of their practice. Less than half (48.0%) of participants indicated that they were familiar with the Israeli Ministry of Health recommendations regarding the treatment of mild-to-moderate COVID-19 in ambulatory patients at high risk for progression to severe disease, and most participants (87.3%) responded, in accordance with the guidelines, that they would prescribe antiviral medications for them. However, when given a specific scenario (an 80-year old with confirmed COVID-19 experiencing mild symptoms), the proportion of participants responding that they would prescribe antiviral treatment to this patient was down to 60.7%. Less than half (48.7%) responded that they would recommend an antigen test to patients with symptoms consistent with COVID-19, in order to confirm diagnosis so that appropriate treatment could be offered.

Subgroup analyses by years of experience and geographic region

Responses were generally consistent across years of medical experience and geographic regions, with no statistically significant differences. Overall willingness to prescribe antiviral treatment and knowledge of management recommendations were similar across subgroups. Some variability was observed in prescribing decisions for an 80-year-old patient and in attitudes toward testing; however, these differences did not show a consistent pattern across groups (Supplementary Table 3).

The vast majority of responders (90.7%) knew about the differences between the antiviral medications (68.0% responded that they knew that not all treatments were equally effective, and an additional 22.7% responded that they were aware of differences between the antiviral medications with respect to both effectiveness and administration modes). Specifically about nirmatrelvir-ritonavir, 79.3% responded that they were aware of its efficacy; however, only 20.7% responded that they were aware nirmatrelvir-ritonavir demonstrated effectiveness in reducing hospitalizations and mortality in both randomized clinical trials and real-world studies. In a question referring to potential drug-drug interactions with nirmatrelvir-ritonavir, 40.0% responded that this would deter them from using the drug. The 60.0% who responded that they were not deterred by it comprised of 32.0% who responded that there were tools to manage such interactions through the HMO and an additional 28.0% who responded they knew which specific drugs interact with nirmatrelvir-ritonavir and could manage this issue. When asked about tools for managing drug-drug interactions, about a third (32.0%) noted that they would consult a specialist, whereas 68.0% were familiar with such tools (56.0% with their HMO-specific tool, and 12.0% with the University of Liverpool COVID-19 drug interaction tool [13]). Lastly, when asked about familiarity with international health authorities’ recommendations on antiviral treatments for COVID-19, nearly half (47.3%) were unfamiliar with them. The 52.7% who were familiar with these recommendations included 28.0% who reported reading the WHO recommendations and an additional 24.7% who noted they had heard about them from colleagues. No statistically significant differences in responses between subgroups by years of medical practice experience or geographic area of medical practice were observed (Supplementary Table 3).

Overall, 76.7% of the responders had some experience treating COVID-19 patients with nirmatrelvir-ritonavir; 66.6% had more substantial experience (33.3% reported that many of their patients received this treatment, and an additional 33.3% reported recommending it to their patients).

Subgroup analyses by years of experience and geographic region

Overall, patterns across subgroups were generally similar, with no statistically significant differences observed for most comparisons. By years of experience, responses were broadly consistent across groups for perceptions of drug-drug interactions related to nirmatrelvir-ritonavir, familiarity with interaction-management tools, knowledge of antiviral effectiveness, and awareness of international recommendations. Similarly, no significant differences were identified across geographic regions (North, Center, and South) for most survey items (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

This survey-based study involving 150 PCPs in Israel demonstrated that in multiple aspects related to COVID-19, PCPs showed both a gap in knowledge and awareness and to a lesser extent a gap between knowledge and practice. These aspects included the COVID-19 burden in Israel, the need for testing, antiviral medications, and guidelines-recommended antiviral treatment of ambulatory patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 at risk for progression to severe disease. The knowledge/awareness of the PCPs did not differ by years of medical practice experience. Interestingly, while most responders appeared to be aware that such patients should receive antiviral medications, the proportion indicating they would provide this treatment declined dramatically when asked about a specific case of an elderly patient with mild symptoms (by 30% from 87.3 to 60.7%).

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to address PCPs’ knowledge of COVID-19, particularly antiviral medications. Several studies worldwide have examined the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of healthcare personnel (physicians, nurses, paramedics) regarding COVID-19, primarily before the approval of nirmatrelvir-ritonavir. Regardless, these studies consistently demonstrated the need for continued educational efforts surrounding COVID-19 awareness and treatment [14–17]. These studies align with recent reports suggesting substantial underuse of antiviral medications in outpatients with COVID-19 [18–20]. A retrospective cohort study aimed to elucidate the reasons for the observed undertreatment. The study included 110 patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 and high risk for complications due to underlying conditions (organ transplantation or hematologic malignancies), treated at the US Veterans Health Administration, who did not receive antiviral medication. The study demonstrated that 88 (80%) of these patients were not offered antiviral medications. The reasons listed for not offering the medications to these 88 patients included concerns about drug interactions (6%), symptoms present for > 5 days (23%), and patients being asymptomatic or with only mild symptoms (71%) [19]. Furthermore, a recent study that surveyed 456 infectious disease specialists in North America reported that responses regarding perceived barriers to prescribing antiviral medications to COVID-19 patients included difficulty assessing drug interactions (58%), a short treatment window (54%), and concerns about adverse effects (46%) [21]. Notably, in both studies, the reasons for not prescribing antiviral medications align with the current study’s findings.

Within the Israeli healthcare system, outpatient COVID-19 management is delivered through four HMOs under a universal coverage model, with PCPs serving as the first point of contact for most patients. Guidelines for antiviral use (e.g., oseltamivir and nirmatrelvir–ritonavir) are issued periodically by the Israeli Ministry of Health and disseminated to HMOs, typically via structured communications to PCPs. While this system enables rapid distribution of national recommendations, variability in implementation across HMOs and differences in clinical workflows may still influence real-world adherence to antiviral guidance. In this context, our findings of variable knowledge and decision-making regarding antiviral therapy likely reflect both individual-level factors and system-level heterogeneity in guideline uptake. Notably, the Israeli healthcare system provides broad and equitable access to antiviral medications, suggesting that the observed gaps are more likely related to knowledge translation and clinical decision-making than to access barriers. To the best of our knowledge, published data on antiviral use in Israel are limited to hospitalized patients [22], with no studies specifically addressing community settings.

There was a discrepancy between physicians’ general willingness to prescribe antiviral therapy (87.3%) and their responses to a specific clinical scenario of an 80-year-old patient with mild COVID-19 symptoms (60.7%). This reduction may reflect increased clinical uncertainty when decisions are framed in concrete patient-level contexts, where factors such as perceived mild symptom severity, concerns about drug-drug interactions, or the perception of the disease’s self-limiting nature may become more prominent [19]. In addition, patient-level barriers such as anticipated adherence issues, tolerance concerns, or expectations regarding the necessity of treatment may contribute to more conservative prescribing in individual cases [23]. Gaps between theoretical knowledge and case-based clinical decision-making have been described in prior studies of physician prescribing behavior, particularly in situations involving newer therapies or evolving evidence, including COVID-19 [24, 25]. These findings highlight the importance of targeted educational interventions that address not only guideline awareness but also the real-world clinical application in specific patient scenarios [26].

The COVID-19 pandemic underscores the importance of PCPs within the healthcare system as the first point of contact for patients, facilitators of patient care, resources for managing public health crises, and direct care providers for patients with COVID-19 [27]. Our findings point to knowledge gaps among PCPs regarding the extent of the disease, guidelines for its management, the need for COVID-19 testing in symptomatic patients, and antiviral treatments. This could lead to underdiagnosis of COVID-19, which in turn could result in misdiagnosis as a bacterial respiratory infection, and consequently contribute to unnecessary antibiotic prescribing. Additionally, it could increase the risk of morbidity and mortality by not offering antiviral medications within the appropriate time window. These gaps could be addressed by education, increased awareness of the guidelines, and the implementation of computerized alarms triggered by suspicion or diagnosis.

Subgroup analyses by years of medical experience and geographic region demonstrated overall consistent patterns in knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported practices regarding COVID-19 across most domains. Overall, these findings suggest a broadly uniform level of knowledge and management approaches among PCPs, with limited subgroup variation. A statistically significant regional difference in participants’ knowledge of COVID-19’s non-seasonal nature was observed; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Although slightly lower proportions of correct responses were noted in the South compared with the North and the Center, overall knowledge remained high across all regions. The absolute differences were modest and do not suggest a meaningful disparity in clinical understanding. Given the exploratory nature of the subgroup analyses and the multiple comparisons performed, this isolated statistically significant finding is likely of limited clinical relevance and may reflect random variation rather than a true regional knowledge gap.

This study has some limitations. First, the survey was exploratory, and the questionnaire was not pilot-tested/validated. Second, the recruitment for the survey relied on an email-based survey distributed to PCPs registered on a specific website, which may have introduced selection bias. This approach may have favored physicians who are more engaged with digital platforms, more computer-savvy, and more likely to access information via email (e.g., guidelines and updates), thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader physician population. Third, the sample size of 150 participants represents approximately 3% of the PCPs practicing in Israel, which is relatively modest and thus may not be representative of PCPs across Israel, particularly given the uneven geographic distribution, with 71.6% of respondents from Central Israel. Fourth, this study was primarily descriptive, and although additional analyses, such as identifying predictors of responses, could have provided further insight, the relatively modest sample size and exploratory design limited the feasibility of multivariable modeling and could have resulted in unstable estimates and reduced interpretability. Finally, the study relied on self-reported data, which are inherently subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Participants may have overestimated their knowledge or adherence to clinical guidelines, or provided responses they perceived as more appropriate rather than reflecting actual clinical practice. Therefore, reported knowledge and decision-making may not fully correspond to real-world behavior. Future studies with larger and more geographically diverse samples, more information regarding the responding PCPs, and randomization methods of responders to enable responder diversity are warranted to confirm our findings.

Conclusions

This national survey of PCPs identified variability in knowledge and awareness regarding the burden of COVID-19 and the management of mild-to-severe COVID-19 cases, including antiviral treatments, clinical guidelines, and potential drug–drug interactions. Given the central role of PCPs in early diagnosis and treatment initiation, these findings suggest a need to strengthen dissemination and uptake of updated guidance in primary care settings. Targeted educational interventions and improved clinical decision-support tools may help support timely and appropriate diagnosis and antiviral use. As the analysis is primarily descriptive, further studies are warranted to better understand factors associated with knowledge variation and their potential impact on clinical practice and patient outcomes.
