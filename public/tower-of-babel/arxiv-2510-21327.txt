On the Complexity of Distributed Edge Coloring and Orientation Problems
Sebastian Brandt CISPA Helmholtz Center for Information Security Saarbrücken, Germany brandt@cispa.de and Fabian Kuhn University of Freiburg Freiburg, Germany kuhn@cs.uni-freiburg.de and Zahra Parsaeian University of Freiburg Freiburg, Germany zahra.parsaeian@cs.uni-freiburg.de

Abstract
Understanding the role of randomness when solving locally checkable labeling (LCL) problems in the LOCAL model has been one of the top priorities in the research on distributed graph algorithms in recent years. For LCL problems in bounded-degree graphs, it is known that randomness cannot help more than polynomially, except in one case: if the deterministic complexity of an LCL problem is in Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log n) and its randomized complexity is in o ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) o(\log n) , then the randomized complexity is guaranteed to be poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) {\rm poly}(\log\log n) . However, the fundamental question of which problems with a deterministic complexity of Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log n) can be solved exponentially faster using randomization still remains wide open. We make a step towards answering this question by studying a simple, but natural class of LCL problems: so-called degree splitting problems. These problems come in two varieties: coloring problems where the edges of a graph have to be colored with 2 2 colors and orientation problems where each edge needs to be oriented. For Δ \Delta -regular graphs (where Δ = O ⁡ ( 1 ) \Delta=O(1) ), we obtain the following results. • We gave an exact characterization of the randomized complexity of all problems where the edges need to be colored with two colors, say red and blue, and which have a deterministic complexity of O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) . • For edge orientation problems, we give a partial characterization of the problems that have a randomized complexity of Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log n) and the problems that have a randomized complexity of poly ​ log ⁡ log ​ n {\rm poly}\log\log n . While our results are cleanest to state for the Δ \Delta -regular case, all our algorithms naturally generalize to nodes of any degree d < Δ d<\Delta in general graphs of maximum degree Δ \Delta .

Understanding the role and power of randomness in the context of distributed LCL problems has been a major objective of the research in the area (see, e.g.,

Which

Binary Labeling Problems.

In an attempt towards exactly characterizing the distributed complexities of all problems in a large class of LCL problems, the authors of
[
BBE+20
]
considered the class of binary labeling problems in the edge labeling formalism
[
Bra19
]
. In this formalism, labels have to be assigned to the edges of a two-colored bipartite graph. For each side of the bipartite graph, the valid configurations are given by a set of allowed multisets of edge labels. The standard way to use this framework in general graphs is to consider the bipartite graph between nodes and edges. Each of the original nodes of a graph
G=(V,E)
is colored white and in the middle of each edge
e\in E
, we place an additional black node. Each edge
e=\left\{u,v\right\}\in E
is then split into two half-edges
(u,e)
and
(v,e)
. The labels are placed on the half-edges and the validity of a labeling is determined by the allowed white node configurations and the allowed black edge configurations. The binary labeling problems are all problems that can be expressed with only two different labels (we call them red (
\mathsf{R}
) and blue (
\mathsf{B}
) in the following) in the edge labeling formalism. In the graph setting with labels on half-edges, binary labeling problems correspond to edge orientation and edge
2
-coloring problems. If the allowed edge configurations are
\left\{\mathsf{R},\mathsf{R}\right\}
and
\left\{\mathsf{B},\mathsf{B}\right\}
, we get problems where the edges of a graph need to be colored red and blue. If the only allowed edge configuration is
\left\{\mathsf{R},\mathsf{B}\right\}
, we get problems where all edges need to be oriented and if the allowed edge configurations are
\left\{\mathsf{R},\mathsf{R}\right\}
and
\left\{\mathsf{R},\mathsf{B}\right\}
, we get problems where we need to compute a partial orientation of the edges. All other combinations are symmetric or they lead to trivial cases.

In
[
BBE+20
]
, the deterministic complexity of all binary labeling problems in the general edge labeling formalism in biregular bounded degree trees
4

4

More concretely, for two constants
d
and
\delta
, it is assumed that the bipartite graph is properly colored with colors white and black and that all white nodes have degree
d
and all black nodes have degree
\delta

\Delta
-regular general graphs, we have
d=\Delta
and
\delta=2
.

[
BBE+20

However, as pointed out above, classifying the randomized complexity of those problems is still largely open and our results can be seen as a step towards closing this gap. We in particular give an exact characterization of the randomized complexity of solving arbitrary edge
2
-coloring problems in regular bounded-degree trees.

Degree Splitting Problems.

A special and important case of binary labelings are balanced oriented and unoriented degree splittings. Unoriented degree splittings are colorings of the edges with two colors such that every node
v
is incident to approximately
\deg(v)/2
edges of each color. An oriented degree splitting is an orientation of the edges so that for every node, indegree and outdegree are approximately equal. Degree splitting problems are not only natural to study, but also serve as key tools in other distributed tasks. Such problems lie at the heart of distributed divide-and-conquer algorithms and variants of splitting have in particular enabled efficient edge-coloring algorithms. In
[
GS17
,
GHK+20
]
, splitting was used to obtain the first deterministic polylog-time edge coloring algorithm that uses only by a
(1+o(1))
-factor more colors than the commonly considered
2\Delta-1
colors (thereby solving a long-standing open problem from the 90s). In
[
GKMU18
]
, splitting is even used to obtain edge colorings with only
(1+o(1))\Delta
colors. Also, recent work on hypergraph sinkless orientation
[
BMN+25
]
(which is a weak form of degree splitting) improved edge coloring and already inspired further follow-ups
[
JM25
,
JMS25
]
. Since our results provide stronger splitting guarantees, we expect them to similarly facilitate future advances, especially for randomized algorithms where previous progress has been limited. The distributed computation of degree splittings was in particular studied in
[
GS17
,
GHK+20
]
. In
[
GHK+20
]
it was shown that one can compute both versions of degree splitting with constant discrepancy in time
\widetilde{O}(\Delta)\cdot\log n
deterministically and in time
\widetilde{O}(\Delta)\cdot\log\log n
randomized. The discrepancy is the maximum absolute difference between the two kinds of edges at any node. For oriented degree splitting, it was even shown that for odd-degree nodes, a discrepancy of
1
, and for even-degree nodes, a discrepancy of
2
can be achieved. We improve the algorithm of
[
GHK+20
]
and we show that also for unoriented degree splitting, the same discrepancy as for oriented degree splitting can be achieved.

Communication Model.

In the

expressed as a function of global network parameters such as

or
the maximum degree
\Delta
. We
assume that those global parameters are known to the nodes of

.

Constructive LLL:
The Lovász Local Lemma (LLL) considers the following setting. A set
V_{H}
of (bad) events that are defined as functions over some underlying product probability space. Let
H=(V_{H},E_{H})
be the
conflict graph
of those events, i.e.,

contains an edge between two events iff they are dependent (i.e., if they depend on intersecting sets of random variables in the underlying product space). The LLL states that if the maximum degree of

is
d

ep(d+1)\leq 1

for a constant

for deterministic algorithms in tree-like dependency graphs
[

]

for randomized algorithms in tree-like dependency graphs
[

]
,
\widetilde{O}\big(\log^{4}n\big)
for deterministic algorithms in general graphs
[

,
GHK18
,
GGH+23
]
, and
O\big(\frac{d}{\log d}\big)+\widetilde{O}(\log^{4}\log n)
for randomized algorithms in general graphs
[

,
GHK18
,
GGH+23
,
Dav23
]
.

if the following holds.

a)

For every node

. Just requiring this subproblem is also defined as problem
\Pi_{\Delta}(y)

b)

For every degree

, there exists a value

such that the number of red edges of all nodes of degree

c)

The value of

\tau_{d}:=\frac{d}{2\Delta}

\alpha_{d}:=\left\lfloor\widetilde{x}_{d}\right\rfloor
, and

\beta_{d}:=\widetilde{x}_{d}-\alpha
.

d)

If
\beta_{d}\leq\tau_{d}

\beta_{d}\in(\tau_{d},1-\tau_{d})

Because this will turn out to be more convenient, as a first step, we extend the definition of
\Pi(y)
to all

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
y\in\mathbb{Z}
, the problems
\Pi(y)
and
\Pi(\Delta-1-y)=\Pi(-y)
are equivalent.

Proof.

Assume that
y\in\left\{0,\dots,\Delta-2\right\}

\Pi_{\Delta}(y)
, the number of red edges of nodes of degree
\Delta
is required to be in
\left\{y,y+1\right\}
. In problem
\Pi(\Delta-1-y)
, the number of red edges of nodes of degree
\Delta
is required to be in
\left\{\Delta-y-1,\Delta-y\right\}
and the number of blue edges is thus required to be in
\left\{y,y+1\right\}
.

Let us also consider nodes of degree
d<\Delta
. We use the variables
\widetilde{x}_{d}
,
x_{d}
,
\tau_{d}
,
\alpha_{d}
, and
\beta_{d}
for the problem
\Pi(y)
and
\widetilde{x}_{d}^{\prime}
,
x_{d}^{\prime}
,
\tau_{d}^{\prime}
,
\alpha_{d}^{\prime}
, and
\beta_{d}^{\prime}
for the problem
\Pi(\Delta-1-y)
. Note that
\widetilde{x}_{d}:=\frac{d}{\Delta}\cdot\left(y+\frac{1}{2}\right)
and

\widetilde{x}_{d}^{\prime}=\frac{d}{\Delta}\cdot\left(\Delta-1-y+\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{d}{\Delta}\cdot\left(\Delta-y-\frac{1}{2}\right)=d-\widetilde{x}_{d}.

It is sufficient to show that if
x_{d}
is unique, then
x_{d}^{\prime}
is unique and we have
x_{d}^{\prime}=d-x_{d}
. In this case the number of red edges in
\Pi(y)
is in
\left\{x_{d}-1,x_{d},x_{d}+1\right\}
and the number of red edges in
\Pi(\Delta-1-y)
is in
\left\{d-x_{d}-1,d-x_{d},d-x_{d}+1\right\}
and thus the number of blue edges is in
\left\{x_{d}-1,x_{d},x_{d}+1\right\}
. This implies that the two problems are equivalent. If
x_{d}
can take two different values
x_{d,1}
and
x_{d,2}
, then we show that also
x_{d}^{\prime}
can take two different values and we have
x_{d,1}^{\prime}=d-x_{d,1}
and
x_{d,2}^{\prime}=d-x_{d,2}
. This then again implies that the two problems are equivalent because the allowed settings for the blue edges in
\Pi(\Delta-1-y)
is the same as the allowed setting for the red edges in
\Pi(y)
. We need to make a case distinction based on whether
\widetilde{x}_{d}
is an integer or not.

The value
\widetilde{x}_{d}
is an integer.

In this case, we have
\alpha_{d}=\widetilde{x}_{d}
and thus
\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=d-\alpha_{d}
. We further have
\beta_{d}=\beta_{d}^{\prime}=0
. We therefore have
x_{d}=\widetilde{x}_{d}=\alpha_{d}
and
x_{d}^{\prime}=\widetilde{x}_{d}^{\prime}=\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=d-\alpha_{d}
as required.

The value
\widetilde{x}_{d}
is not an integer.

In this case, we have
\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=\lfloor\widetilde{x}^{\prime}_{d}\rfloor
and thus
\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=\lfloor d-\widetilde{x}_{d}\rfloor=d-\alpha_{d}-1
. We further have
\beta_{d}=\widetilde{x}_{d}-\alpha_{d}
and
\beta_{d}^{\prime}=d-\widetilde{x}_{d}-\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=\alpha_{d}+1-\widetilde{x}_{d}=1-\beta_{d}
. We also clearly have
\tau_{d}^{\prime}=\tau_{d}
as this value only depends on
d
and
\Delta
. We make a further case distinction based on the value of
\beta_{d}

•

If
\beta_{d}\leq\tau_{d}
, we have
x_{d}=\alpha_{d}
. For problem
\Pi(\Delta-1-y)
, we have
\beta_{d}^{\prime}=1-\beta_{d}
and thus
\beta_{d}^{\prime}\geq 1-\tau_{d}
. We therefore have
x_{d}^{\prime}=\alpha_{d}^{\prime}+1=d-\alpha_{d}=d-x_{d}
.

•

If
\beta_{d}\geq 1-\tau_{d}
, we have
x_{d}=\alpha_{d}+1
. For problem
\Pi(\Delta-1-y)
, we have
\beta_{d}^{\prime}=1-\beta_{d}
and thus
\beta_{d}^{\prime}\leq\tau_{d}
. We therefore have
x_{d}^{\prime}=\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=d-\alpha_{d}-1=d-x_{d}
.

•

Finally, if

\beta_{d}^{\prime}=1-\beta_{d}

x_{d}^{\prime}=\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=d-1-\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=d-x_{d}
.

\Pi(y)
.

\widetilde{x}_{d}=\alpha_{d}+\beta_{d}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{x}_{d}^{\prime}=\alpha_{d}^{\prime}+\beta_{d}^{\prime}=2\alpha_{d}^{\prime}+2\beta_{d}^{\prime}-\tau_{d}.

(11)

We make a case distinction based on the value of
\beta_{d}
.

–

2\beta_{d}<\tau_{d}
: In this case, we know that
\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=2\alpha-1
and
\beta_{d}^{\prime}=1+2\beta_{d}-\tau_{d}\geq 1-\tau_{d}
. Note that this implies that
x_{d}^{\prime}=\alpha_{d}^{\prime}+1=2\alpha_{d}
. Since
\beta_{d}<\tau
, we further know that
x_{d}=\alpha_{d}
. We therefore have
d_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}(v)\in\left\{2\alpha_{d}-1,2\alpha_{d},2\alpha_{d}+1\right\}
and we need
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\in\left\{\alpha_{d}-1,\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d}+1\right\}
. By applying
Equation

8
, we get that
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\geq\left\lfloor\frac{2\alpha_{d}-1}{2}\right\rfloor=\alpha_{d}-1
and
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{2\alpha_{d}+1}{2}\right\rfloor+1=\alpha_{d}+1
.

–

0\leq 2\beta_{d}-\tau<1
. In this case, we know that
\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=2\alpha_{d}
and
\beta_{d}^{\prime}=2\beta_{d}-\tau_{d}
. We make a further case distinction

*

\beta_{d}\leq\tau_{d}
: In this case, we have
\beta_{d}^{\prime}=2\beta_{d}-\tau_{d}\leq\tau_{d}
. We therefore know that
x_{d}^{\prime}=\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=2\alpha_{d}
and we have
x_{d}=\alpha_{d}
. We therefore have
d_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}(v)\in\left\{2\alpha_{d}-1,2\alpha_{d},2\alpha_{d}+1\right\}
and we need
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\in\left\{\alpha_{d}-1,\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d}+1\right\}
. By
Equation

8
, we have
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\geq\lfloor(2\alpha_{d}-1)/2\rfloor=\alpha_{d}-1
. We similarly have
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\leq\lfloor(2\alpha_{d}+1)/2\rfloor+1=\alpha_{d}+1
.

*

\tau_{d}<\beta_{d}<1-\tau_{d}
: Note that since
\beta_{d}
is between
\tau_{d}
and
1-\tau_{d}
, in this case, we only need to show that either
x_{d}=\alpha_{d}
and thus
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\in\left\{\alpha_{d}-1,\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d}+1\right\}
or
x_{d}=\alpha_{d}+1
and thus
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\in\left\{\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d}+1,\alpha_{d}+2\right\}
. Independently of what the value of
\beta_{d}^{\prime}
is, we know that
x_{d}^{\prime}=2\alpha_{d}
or
x_{d}^{\prime}=2\alpha_{d}+1
. In the first case, we get
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\geq\lfloor(2\alpha_{d}-1)/2\rfloor=\alpha_{d}-1
and
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\leq\lfloor(2\alpha_{d}+1)/2\rfloor+1=\alpha_{d}+1
. In the second case, we get
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\geq\lfloor(2\alpha_{d})/2\rfloor=\alpha_{d}
and
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\leq\lfloor(2\alpha_{d}+2)/2\rfloor+1=\alpha_{d}+2
. We therefore get a valid solution for
\Pi(y)
in either case.

*

\beta_{d}\geq 1-\tau_{d}
: In this case, we have
\beta_{d}^{\prime}=2\beta_{d}-\tau_{d}\geq 2-3\tau_{d}>1-\tau_{d}
(because
\tau_{d}<1/2
). We therefore know that
x_{d}^{\prime}=\alpha_{d}^{\prime}+1=2\alpha_{d}+1
and thus
d_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}(v)\in\left\{2\alpha_{d},2\alpha_{d}+1,2\alpha_{d}+2\right\}
. As above, in this case, we get
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\in\left\{\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d}+1,\alpha_{d}+2\right\}
, which is what we need because
\beta_{d}\geq 1-\tau_{d}
.

–

2\beta_{d}-\tau_{d}\geq 1
: In this last case, we have
\beta_{d}^{\prime}=2\beta_{d}-\tau_{d}-1
and
\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=2\alpha_{d}+1
. We cannot bound
\beta_{d}^{\prime}
in this case and we therefore only know that
x_{d}^{\prime}\in\left\{2\alpha_{d}+1,2\alpha_{d}+2\right\}
and thus
d_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}(v)\in\left\{2\alpha_{d},\dots,2\alpha_{d}+3\right\}
. This implies that
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\geq\lfloor(2\alpha_{d})/2\rfloor=\alpha_{d}
and
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\leq\lfloor(2\alpha_{d}+3)/2\rfloor+1=\alpha_{d}+2
.
Since
2\beta_{d}\geq 1+\tau_{d}
, we have
\beta\geq(1+\tau_{d})/2>\tau_{d}
and it is thus fine if
x_{d}=\alpha_{d}+1
and thus
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\in\left\{\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d}+1,\alpha_{d}+2\right\}
.

•

y>\frac{\Delta-2}{2}
: Note that
2y\geq\Delta-1
. Assume that
2y=\Delta-1+k
for some
k\in\left\{0,\dots,\Delta-3\right\}
. By definition, we have
\Pi(2y)=\Pi(k)
. As step (1), we switch the colors, we move from
\Pi(k)
to the equivalent
\Pi(\Delta-1-k)
(cf.
Lemma

4.1
). That is, before defining
G^{\prime}
and applying degree splitting part to
G^{\prime}
, the set of red edges satisfies the conditions of
\Pi(\Delta-1-k)
. Note that
\Delta-1-k
has the same parity as
\Delta-1+k
and since
\Delta-1+k=2y
, we know that
\Delta-1-k
is even. We can therefore write the value as
\Delta-1-k=2z
for some
0\leq z\leq(\Delta-2)/2
. Steps (2) and (3) are now the same as steps (1) and (2) in the first case. With the same analysis as before, we can therefore conclude that the red-blue labeling after step (3) is a valid solution to
\Pi(z)
. Since we again switch the colors in step (4), by
Lemma

4.1
, at the end, the red-blue labeling satisfies the conditions of
\Pi(\Delta-1-z)
. We have

\Delta-1-z=\Delta-1-\frac{\Delta-1-k}{2}=\frac{\Delta-1+k}{2}=y.

This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We next show that a solution to

Lemma 4.3
.

We again first describe the transformation from

to

and we afterwards prove that the resulting problem satisfies all the requirements of problem

. W.l.o.g., we assume that

. We distinguish two cases.

•

y\leq\frac{\Delta-3}{2}
:

a)

Let

and let

be the subgraph of

b)

We apply

3.3
(with

) to

. The edges that are colored red in the resulting red-blue labeling of

remain red and the remaining edges of

together with the edges in

become blue in the final labeling.

•

y>\frac{\Delta-3}{2}
:

a)

We first switch the colors in the given solution to
\Pi(2y+1)

b)

Let
E^{\prime}
be the set of red edges in the current solution to
\Pi(2y+1)
and let
G^{\prime}
be the subgraph of

We apply

3.3
(with
Equation

7
) to
G^{\prime}
. The edges that are colored red in the resulting red-blue labeling of

remain red and the remaining edges of
E^{\prime}
together with the edges in

become blue.

d)

We again switch the colors for the final labeling of the edges.

For the analysis, we also consider the two cases.

•

y\leq\frac{\Delta-3}{2}
: For a node
v
, let
d_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}(v)
be its degree in
G^{\prime}
(or equivalently,
d_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}(v)
is the number of red edges in the initial solution to

to denote the number of red edges after executing the algorithm. We thus need to show that the values of

satisfy the requirements of problem
\Pi(y)
. Note that for every
v\in V
, by
Equation

7
, the number of red edges after executing the algorithm is

d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\in\left\{\left\lceil\frac{d_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}(v)}{2}\right\rceil-1,\left\lceil\frac{d_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}(v)}{2}\right\rceil\right\}.

(12)

We first concentrate on nodes

\Delta
in

. Since the initial red-blue labeling is a solution to

, we know that for each such node
v
,

. Note that

.

–

2\beta_{d}+\tau_{d}<1

. This implies that we need

–

1\leq 2\beta_{d}+\tau_{d}<2

*

\beta_{d}\leq\tau_{d}

d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\leq\lceil(2\alpha_{d}+2)/2\rceil=\alpha_{d}+1

*

\tau_{d}<\beta_{d}<1-\tau_{d}
: Note that since
\beta_{d}
is between
\tau_{d}
and

, in this case, we only need to show that either
x_{d}=\alpha_{d}
and thus
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\in\left\{\alpha_{d}-1,\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d}+1\right\}
or
x_{d}=\alpha_{d}+1
and thus
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\in\left\{\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d}+1,\alpha_{d}+2\right\}

in either case.

*

\beta_{d}\geq 1-\tau_{d}
: In this case, we have
\beta_{d}^{\prime}\geq 2\beta_{d}-\tau_{d}-1>1-\tau_{d}
(because
\beta_{d}<1
). We therefore know that
x_{d}^{\prime}=\alpha_{d}^{\prime}+1=2\alpha_{d}+2
and thus
d_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}(v)\in\left\{2\alpha_{d}+1,2\alpha_{d}+2,2\alpha_{d}+3\right\}
. As above, in this case, we get
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\in\left\{\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d}+1,\alpha_{d}+2\right\}
, which is what we need because
\beta_{d}\geq 1-\tau_{d}
.

–

2\beta_{d}+\tau_{d}\geq 2
: In this last case, we have
\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=2\alpha_{d}+2
and
\beta_{d}^{\prime}=2\beta_{d}+\tau_{d}-2<\tau_{d}
(because
\beta_{d}<1
). We further have
\beta_{d}\geq(2-\tau_{d})/2=1-\tau_{d}/2>1-\tau_{d}
. We therefore know that
d_{\mathsf{R}}^{\prime}(v)\in\left\{\alpha_{d}^{\prime}-1,\alpha_{d}^{\prime},\alpha_{d}^{\prime}+1\right\}=\left\{2\alpha_{d}+1,2\alpha_{d}+2,2\alpha_{d}+3\right\}
and we need
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\in\left\{\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d}+1,\alpha_{d}+2\right\}
. By using
Equation

12
, we get
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\geq\lceil(2\alpha_{d}+1)\rceil-1=\alpha_{d}
and
d_{\mathsf{R}}(v)\leq\lceil(2\alpha_{d}+3)\rceil=\alpha_{d}+1
as needed.

•

y\geq\frac{\Delta+1}{2}
: Note that since
y\geq(\Delta+1)/2
, we have
2y-1\geq\Delta
. Assume that
2y-1=\Delta-1+k
for some
k\in\left\{1,\dots,\Delta-4\right\}
. By definition, we have
\Pi(2y-1)=\Pi(k)
. As step (1), we switch the colors, we move from
\Pi(k)
to the equivalent
\Pi(\Delta-1-k)
(cf.
Lemma

4.1
). That is, before defining
G^{\prime}
and applying degree splitting part to
G^{\prime}
, the set of red edges satisfies the conditions of
\Pi(\Delta-1-k)
. Note that
\Delta-1-k
has the same parity as
\Delta-1+k
and since
\Delta-1+k=2y-1
, we know that
\Delta-1-k
is odd. We can therefore write the value as
\Delta-1-k=2z+1
for some
0\leq z\leq(\Delta-3)/2
. Steps (2) and (3) are now the same as steps (1) and (2) in the first case. With the same analysis as before, we can therefore conclude that the red-blue labeling after step (3) is a valid solution to
\Pi(z)
. Since we again switch the colors in step (4), by
Lemma

4.1
, at the end, the red-blue labeling satisfies the conditions of
\Pi(\Delta-1-z)
. We have

\Delta-1-z=\Delta-1-\frac{\Delta-2-k}{2}=\frac{\Delta+k}{2}=y.

This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

1.4
, i.e., that for every

, problem

can be computed in time

.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.
.

We first note that for some values of
y
,
\Pi(y)
is straightforward to obtain. First,
\Pi(0)
can be solved in
0
rounds by just coloring all edges blue. In this scenario,
\Pi_{\Delta}(0)
is satisfied and for all vertices with degree

,
\widetilde{x}_{d}=\frac{d}{2\Delta}<\frac{1}{2}
leads to
x_{d}=0
, thereby satisfying
\Pi(0)
. Further, by using
Lemma

3.3
(i.e., the second part claim of
Theorem

1.1
), we can compute a red-blue coloring of the edges that satisfies
Equation

6
(or one that satisfies
Equation

7
). This approach directly satisfies
\Pi_{\Delta}(y)
for
y=\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta}{2}\right\rfloor
. For vertices of degree

, the value of
\widetilde{x}_{d}
is determined as follows, based on whether
\Delta
is odd or even:

•

For even
\Delta
:
\widetilde{x}_{d}=\frac{d}{\Delta}\left(\frac{\Delta}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{2\Delta}
. If
d
is odd, then

(
\frac{d}{2\Delta}<\frac{1}{2}
), and the values
\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor
and
\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor+1
fall within the set
\left\{\frac{d-3}{2},\frac{d-1}{2},\frac{d+1}{2}\right\}
. For even
d
,

, with
\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor
and
\left\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\right\rfloor+1
belonging to the set
\left\{\frac{d}{2}-1,\frac{d}{2},\frac{d}{2}+1\right\}
.

•

For odd
\Delta
:
\widetilde{x}_{d}=\frac{d}{\Delta}\cdot\frac{\Delta}{2}=\frac{d}{2}
. If
d
is odd, then
x_{d}=\frac{d-1}{2}
and if
d
is even
d
,
x_{d}=\frac{d}{2}
, which are the two cases that were already covered in the previous case.

Hence, for
y=\big\lfloor\frac{\Delta}{2}\big\rfloor
, problem
\Pi(y)
follows from
Equation

6
. By
Lemma

4.1
, we further know that problems
\Pi\left(\big\lfloor\frac{\Delta}{2}\big\rfloor\right)
and
\Pi\left(\big\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\big\rceil-1\right)
are equivalent and therefore also
\Pi\left(\big\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\big\rceil-1\right)
can be solved in time
\mathsf{T_{BO}}(\Delta)
.

In the case, where
y
does not fit one of the straightforward scenarios, we proceed as follows. For some integer
k
, let
y_{1},\dots,y_{k}
be
k
consecutive integers such that for all
i\in\left\{1,\dots,k-1\right\}
,
y_{i+1}=y_{i}+1
. Assume that we want to solve one of the problems
\Pi(y_{1}),\dots,\Pi(y_{k})
. If for some
i
,
y_{i}\mod(\Delta-1)\in\left\{0,\lceil\Delta/2\rceil-1,\lfloor\Delta/2\rfloor\right\}
, we know that the task of solving one of the

can be completed in time at most
\mathsf{T_{BO}}(\Delta)
. Assume therefore that

\forall i\,:\,y_{i}\mod(\Delta-1)\not\in\left\{0,\left\lceil\frac{\Delta}{2}\right\rceil-1,\left\lfloor\frac{\Delta}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}.

(16)

We show that in this case, one can construct a sequence
y_{1}^{\prime},\dots,y_{2k}^{\prime}
of

consecutive integers (again

) such that given a solution to
\Pi(y_{j}^{\prime})
for some

, it is possible to solve one of the problems
\Pi(y_{i})
for

in time
\mathsf{T_{BO}}(\Delta)
. Note that if

holds, then for all
i\in\left\{1,\dots,k\right\}
, it either holds that
y_{i}\mod(\Delta-1)\leq\frac{\Delta-3}{2}
or
y_{i}\mod(\Delta-1)\geq\frac{\Delta+1}{2}
. We consider the two cases separately.

•

If
y_{i}\mod(\Delta-1)\leq\frac{\Delta-3}{2}
for all

, then

4.2
and
4.3
imply that for
i\in\left\{1,\dots,k\right\}
, we can compute
\Pi(y_{i})
in time
\mathsf{T_{BO}}(\Delta)
when given a solution to
\Pi(2y_{i})
or
\Pi(2y_{i}+1)
. For
y_{1}^{\prime}=2y_{1}
and
y_{2k}^{\prime}=2y_{k}+1
, given a solution to one of the problems
y_{1}^{\prime},\dots,y_{2k}^{\prime}
, we can therefore compute one of the problems
\Pi(y_{i})
for
i\in\left\{1,\dots,k\right\}
in time
\mathsf{T_{BO}}(\Delta)
.

•

If
y_{i}\mod(\Delta-1)\geq\frac{\Delta+1}{2}
for all
i
, then
Lemmas

4.2
and
4.3
imply that for
i\in\left\{1,\dots,k\right\}
, we can compute
\Pi(y_{i})
in time
\mathsf{T_{BO}}(\Delta)
when given a solution to
\Pi(2y_{i}-1)
or
\Pi(2y_{i})
. For
y_{1}^{\prime}=2y_{1}-1
and
y_{2k}^{\prime}=2y_{k}

for

.

The theorem now follows by repeating this process until the obtained sequence contains one of the problems in

. As the sequence of problems that suffice to solve

Proof of

.
.

Recall that for a graph

\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in[0,1/2]
such that
\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}\geq 1/2
, we need to show that there is a
\mathsf{T_{LLL}}(\Delta^{2})
-round algorithm to compute an edge orientation in which every
v\in V
has outdegree at most
\rho_{1}\cdot\deg(v)+O(\sqrt{\Delta\log\Delta})
or indegree at most
\rho_{2}\cdot\deg(v)+O(\sqrt{\Delta\log\Delta})
. To prove this, we show that the construction of such an edge orientation can be phrased directly as an LLL problem with polynomial criterion. To see this, we define parameters

\eta:=\frac{1/2-\rho_{2}}{1-\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\nu:=\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}-\frac{1}{2}.

We define the following (independent)
0
/
1
-random variables: A random variable
X_{v}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
with
\Pr(X_{v}=1)=\eta
for every node
v\in V
, as well as random variables
Y_{e},Z_{e}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
with
\Pr(Y_{e}=1)=\nu
and
\Pr(Z_{e}=1)=1/2
for every edge
e\in E
. We use those random variables to define a random edge orientation of
G
. For every edge
e=\left\{u,v\right\}\in E
with
\mathrm{ID}(u)<\mathrm{ID}(v)
, we define the orientation of
e
as follows.

•

If
X_{u}=X_{v}
,
e
is oriented from
u
to
v
if
Z_{e}=1
and it is oriented from
v
to
u
otherwise.

•

If
X_{u}=0
and
X_{v}=1
,
e
is oriented from
u
to
v
if
Y_{e}=0
and it is oriented from
v
to
u
otherwise.

•

If
X_{u}=1
and
X_{v}=0
,
e
is oriented from
u
to
v
if
Y_{e}=1
and it is oriented from
v
to
u
otherwise.

In the following, let
\gamma>0
be a constant that we will fix to be sufficiently large. For every node
v\in V
, let
d_{0}(v)
be the number of neighbors
u
of
v
for which
X_{u}=0
and let
d_{1}(v)
be the number of neighbors
u
of
v
for which
X_{u}=1
. Note that for every node
v
, we have
{\rm\mathbb{E}}[d_{0}(v)]=(1-\eta)\dot{d}(v)
and
{\rm\mathbb{E}}[d_{1}(v)]=\eta\cdot d(v)
. Note that
d_{0}(v)
and
d_{1}(v)
are both binomially distributed. By
Lemma

5.1
(see below), for a sufficiently large constant
c>0
, we therefore have

\Pr\left(\left|d_{0}(v)-(1-\eta)\cdot d(v)\right|\leq c\sqrt{\Delta\log\Delta}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\Delta^{\gamma}}

(17)

and

\Pr\left(\left|d_{1}(v)-\eta\cdot d(v)\right|\leq c\sqrt{\Delta\log\Delta}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\Delta^{\gamma}}.

(18)

In the following, we fix the assignment of variables
X_{v}
for all
v\in V
and only consider the conditional probability space over the random choice of the edge random variables. Let
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
be the set nodes
v\in V
that choose
X_{v}=0
and
X_{v}=1
, respectively. We first consider the case of a node in
v\in V_{0}
. Let
u
be a neighbor of node
v
and w.l.o.g., assume that
\mathrm{ID}(u)<\mathrm{ID}(v)
. Let
e=\left\{u,v\right\}
be an edge between
u
and
v
. Edge
e
is an incoming edge for
v
if either
X_{u}=0
and
Z_{e}=1
or
X_{u}=1
and
Y_{e}=1
. Let
D_{in}^{(0)}(v)
be the indegree of a node
v\in V_{0}
. We have

{\rm\mathbb{E}}\big[D_{in}^{(0)}(v)\big]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot d_{0}(v)+\nu\cdot d_{1}(v).

(19)

We now consider the case of a node in
v\in V_{1}
. Let
u
be a neighbor of node
v
and w.l.o.g., assume that
\mathrm{ID}(u)<\mathrm{ID}(v)
. Let
e=\left\{u,v\right\}
be an edge between
u
and
v
. Edge
e
is an outgoing edge for
v
if which either
X_{u}=1
and
Z_{e}=0
or
X_{u}=0
and and
Y_{e}=1
. Let
D_{out}^{(1)}(v)
be the indegree of a node
v\in V_{1}
. We have

{\rm\mathbb{E}}\big[D_{out}^{(1)}(v)\big]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot d_{1}(v)+\nu\cdot d_{0}(v).

(20)

In the conditional probability space, where the
X_{v}
-variables are already fixed, the two random variables
D_{in}^{(0)}(v)
and
D_{out}^{(1)}(v)
are sums of two (independent) Binomial random variables. We can therefore use
Lemma

5.1
to show that with probability at least
1-\Delta^{-\gamma}
, for a sufficiently large constant
c>0
, both variables deviate at most
c\sqrt{\Delta\log\Delta}
(additively) for their expectations as given in
Equations

19
and
20
. Together with
Equations

17
and
18
, this implies that also in the original non-conditional probability space,
D_{in}^{(0)}(v)
and
D_{out}^{(1)}(v)
deviate at most
c\sqrt{\Delta\log\Delta}
from their expectation with probability at least
1-\Delta^{-\gamma}
. The expectations of
D_{in}^{(0)}(v)
and
D_{out}^{(1)}(v)
can be computed as follows.

\displaystyle{\rm\mathbb{E}}\big[D_{in}^{(0)}(v)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot{\rm\mathbb{E}}\big[d_{0}(v)\big]+\nu\cdot{\rm\mathbb{E}}\big[d_{1}(v)\big]\ =\ \frac{1-\eta}{2}\cdot d(v)+\eta\cdot\nu\cdot d(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1/2-\rho_{1}}{2(1-\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})}+\frac{(1/2-\rho_{2})\cdot(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}-1/2)}{1-\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}}\right)\cdot d(v)\ =\ \rho_{2}\cdot d(v),

\displaystyle{\rm\mathbb{E}}\big[D_{out}^{(1)}(v)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot{\rm\mathbb{E}}\big[d_{1}(v)\big]+\nu\cdot{\rm\mathbb{E}}\big[d_{0}(v)\big]\ =\ \frac{\eta}{2}\cdot d(v)+(1-\eta)\cdot\nu\cdot d(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1/2-\rho_{2}}{2(1-\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})}+\frac{(1/2-\rho_{1})\cdot(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}-1/2)}{1-\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}}\right)\cdot d(v)\ =\ \rho_{1}\cdot d(v).

With probability
1-\Delta^{-\gamma^{\prime}}
for a
\gamma^{\prime}
that we can choose, there therefore exists a constant
c^{\prime}>0
such that for every node
v\in V
that picks
X_{v}=0
, the indegree is
\rho_{2}\cdot d(v)\pm c^{\prime}\cdot\sqrt{\Delta\log\Delta}
and for every node
v\in V
that picks
X_{v}=1
, the outdegree is
\rho_{1}\cdot d(v)\pm c^{\prime}\cdot\sqrt{\Delta\log\Delta}
.

We now have a setting to which we can directly apply a constructive LLL algorithm (with polynomial LLL criterion) to obtain an orientation in which every node either has indegree
\rho_{2}\cdot d(v)\pm c^{\prime}\cdot\sqrt{\Delta\log\Delta}
or it has outdegree
\rho_{1}\cdot d(v)\pm c^{\prime}\cdot\sqrt{\Delta\log\Delta}
. We create a bad event for every node such that the bad event for node
v
occurs if
v
does not satisfy the required criterion. The event for node
v
only depends on the random variables
Y_{e}
and
Z_{e}
of the edges of node
v
and on the random variables
X_{v}
, and well as
X_{u}
for the neighbors of
v
. The events of two nodes
u
and
v
are therefore dependent if the two nodes are within distance
2
in the graph and otherwise they are independent. The dependency degree of the graph of bad events is therefore
O(\Delta^{2})
. The desired orientation can therefore be computed in time
\mathsf{T_{LLL}}(\Delta^{2})
as claimed.
∎

The following simple technical lemma is used in the above proof.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
n
and
k
be positive integers such that
k\leq n
and let
p\in[0,1]
be a probability (which can depend on
n
or
k
). Consider a random variable
X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(k,p)
. For every constant
c>0
, there exists a constant
c^{\prime}>0
such that

\Pr\left(|X-pk|\leq c^{\prime}\cdot\sqrt{n\log n}\right)\leq\frac{1}{n^{c}}.

Proof.

By a standard Chernoff-Hoeffding bound
[
Hoe63
]
, we know that if
X
is a sum of
n
Bernoulli random variables, then for every
t>0
, we have

\Pr\big(|X-{\rm\mathbb{E}}[X]|\geq t\big)\leq 2\cdot e^{-2t^{2}/n}.

The same bound also holds if
X
is the sum of
k<n
independent Bernoulli random variables, because we can always add
n-k
additional dummy variables that are
0
with probability
1
. The claim of the lemma therefore follows directly by choosing
t=c\sqrt{n\log n}
for a sufficiently large constant
c>0
.
∎

We next prove
Theorem

1.6
and show that the orientations that are achieved by
Theorem

1.5
are in some sense essentially optimal.

Proof of
Theorem

1.6
.
.

Suppose there is an algorithm that orients the edges of
\Delta
-regular trees in
o(\log_{\Delta}n)
rounds such that each vertex either has outdegree at most
\rho_{1}\cdot\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta}
or has indegree at most
\rho_{2}\cdot\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta}
, where
\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}=\frac{1}{2}
. This implies that for any
\Delta
-regular graph
G
of girth
\Omega(\log_{\Delta}n)
, this problem can be also solved in
o(\log_{\Delta}n)
rounds because nodes can locally not distinguish between the two cases. We will show that for
\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}=\frac{1}{2}
and sufficiently large
c
, there are
\Delta
-regular graphs with girth
\Omega(\log_{\Delta}n)
for which no such orientation exists.

For this proof, we need to recap some basic spectral graph theory. The adjacency matrix of an undirected
n
-node graph
G=(V,E)
, denoted by
A(G)
, is an
n\times n
squared matrix, where for each pair of node
u,v\in V
,
A_{u,v}=A_{v,u}=1
if
\left\{u,v\right\}\in E
and
A_{u,v}=A_{v,u}=0
otherwise. The degree matrix of
G
, denoted by
D(G)
, is a diagonal
n\times n
matrix, where for each vertex
v\in V
,
D_{v,v}=d(v)
. In addition, the Laplacian matrix of
G
, denoted by
L(G)
, is defined by
L(G):=D(G)-A(G)
. In the following for simplicity we use
A
,
D
, and
L
whenever we refer to
A(G)
,
D(G)
, and
L(G)
and
G
is clear from the context. Let
\mu_{1}\geq\dots\geq\mu_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
A
and let
0=\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
L
. We note that all those eigenvalues are in
\mathbb{R}
and if
G
is
\Delta
-regular, then for each
i
, it holds that
\lambda_{i}=\Delta-\mu_{i}
. Friedman
[
Fri04
]
showed that for the smallest eigenvalue
\mu_{n}
of the adjacency matrix of a random
\Delta
-regular graph, w.h.p. it holds that
|\mu_{n}|\leq 2\sqrt{\Delta-1}+\varepsilon
, for any
\varepsilon>0
and thus
\mu_{n}\geq-2\sqrt{\Delta-1}-\varepsilon\geq-3\sqrt{\Delta}
for a large enough
\Delta
. We therefore know that the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}\leq\Delta+3\sqrt{\Delta}
.

The largest eigenvalue of a Laplacian matrix can be computed as the following:

\lambda_{n}:=\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}L\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}}=\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}=1}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}L\boldsymbol{x}.

The quadratic form
\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}L\boldsymbol{x}
can be conveniently written as

\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}L\boldsymbol{x}=\sum_{\left\{u,v\right\}\in E(G)}(x_{u}-x_{v})^{2},

where
x_{v}
is the coordinate of
\boldsymbol{x}
corresponding to node
v\in V
. For a vector
\boldsymbol{x}
with
\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}=1
, we therefore have
\sum_{\left\{u,v\right\}\in E(G)}(x_{u}-x_{v})^{2}\leq\lambda_{n}
. Let
C
be a subset of the vertices,
C\subset V
of the graph
G
, where
|C|=k<n
. Furthermore, let
E(C)
be the set of edges that are incident to a vertex in
C
and a vertex in
V\setminus C
. In addition, for each vertex
v\in C
, let
x_{v}=w
and for each vertex
v\in V\setminus C
let
x_{v}=-y
where
w
and
y
are both positive numbers. Following the above equation we have the following:

\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}L\boldsymbol{x}=\sum_{\left\{u,v\right\}\in E(G)}(x_{u}-x_{v})^{2}=|E(C)|\cdot(w+y)^{2}.

Therefore, in a random
\Delta
-regular graph,
|E(C)|(w+y)^{2}\leq\lambda_{n}\leq\Delta+3\sqrt{\Delta}
. As we will see, based on this, one can show that a random
\Delta
-regular graph does not have an orientation where every node either has outdegree at most
\rho_{1}\cdot\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta}
or indegree at most
\rho_{2}\cdot\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta}
, where
\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}=\frac{1}{2}
. However, we need to show this for a
\Delta
-regular graph with girth
\Omega(\log_{\Delta}n)
, which is not true for a uniformly random
\Delta
-regular graph. With a large probability, such a graph however has at most
O(\sqrt{n})
short cycles and as described in
[
BBE+20
,
Alo10
]
, it is possible to change
O(\sqrt{n})
edges to turn a uniformly random
\Delta
-regular graph into a
\Delta
-regular graph of girth
\Omega(\log_{\Delta}n)
. In the following, we assume that
G
is such a
\Delta
-regular graph. Clearly, when exchanging at most
O(\sqrt{n})
edges, the size of every cut in a graph can shrink or grow by at most
O(\sqrt{n})
. We can therefore assume that
G
is a high-girth
\Delta
-regular graph for which for some constant
\gamma>0
,

(|E(C)|-\gamma\sqrt{n})(w+y)^{2}\leq\lambda_{n}\leq\Delta+3\sqrt{\Delta}.

We have to choose the values
w
and
y
such that
k\cdot w^{2}+(n-k)\cdot y^{2}=1
. We choose
w
and
y
as follows.

w=\sqrt{\frac{n-k}{nk}}\quad\text{and}\quad y=\sqrt{\frac{k}{n(n-k)}}.

Note that those values satisfy
k\cdot w^{2}+(n-k)\cdot y^{2}=1
. One can show that they also maximize the value of
(w+y)^{2}
subject to this constraint.
For this choice of
w
and
y
, we now get

(w+y)^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{k}{n-k}+\frac{n-k}{k}\right)+2\sqrt{\frac{k(n-k)}{n^{2}k(n-k)}}=\frac{n}{k(n-k)}.

We therefore get

(|E(C)|-\gamma\sqrt{n})(w+y)^{2}=(|E(C)|-\gamma\sqrt{n})\cdot\frac{n}{k(n-k)}\leq\Delta+3\sqrt{\Delta}.

This leads to the following upper bound on the cut size
|E(C)|
:

|E(C)|\leq\frac{k(n-k)}{n}\cdot(\Delta+3\sqrt{\Delta})+\gamma\sqrt{n}.

(21)

In the following, we will assume that for some given
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
such that
\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}=1/2
, it is possible to compute an orientation in which every node either has outdegree at most
\rho_{1}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta}
or indegree at most
\rho_{2}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta}
. We will assume that
|C|=k
and that
C
is the set of nodes for which the outdegree is at most
\rho_{1}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta}
and that
V\setminus C
is the set of nodes for which the indegree is at most
\rho_{2}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta}
. We will see that this leads to a contradiction, independently of the size
k
of
C
.

First, observe that we need
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\geq c/\sqrt{\Delta}
. Assume that this is not the case and that, e.g.,
\rho_{1}<c/\sqrt{\Delta}
. Then, the requirement becomes that all nodes either have outdegree
<0
, which is clearly impossible, or indegree
<\Delta/2
, which is not possible for all nodes. We next argue that both
k
and
n-k
cannot be too small. For a given orientation, let
d_{out}(v)
be the outdegree of some node
v
. In a
\Delta
-regular graph in which
k
nodes have outdegree at most
\rho_{1}\Delta
, we have

\frac{n\Delta}{2}=\sum_{v\in V}d_{out}(v)\leq k\cdot\rho_{1}\Delta+(n-k)\cdot\Delta.

This implies that

n-k\geq n\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{2(1-\rho_{1})}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\rho_{1}\leq 1/2-c/\sqrt{\Delta})}}{{\geq}}n\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{1+\frac{2c}{\sqrt{\Delta}}}\right)\geq n\cdot\frac{c}{\sqrt{\Delta}}.

(22)

In the last inequality, we use that
2c\leq\sqrt{\Delta}
. Note that otherwise, the requirement that nodes must have outdegree at most
\rho_{1}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta}
or indegree at most
\rho_{2}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta}
becomes trivially impossible. We analogously also get the same lower bound for
k
.

In the following let
G_{C}
be the subgraph of
G
induced by the nodes in
C
and let
G_{V\setminus C}
be the subgraph of
G
induced by the nodes in
V\setminus C
. Because the outdegree of every nodes in
C
is at most
\rho_{1}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta}
, the average degree in
G_{C}
is at most
2(\rho_{1}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta})
. Similarly, the average degree in
G_{V\setminus C}
is at most
2(\rho_{2}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta})
. Because every node in
C
has degree
\Delta
, on average, the nodes in
C
have at least
\Delta-2(\rho_{1}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta})
edges in the cut
E(C)
and analogously, the nodes in
V\setminus C
on average have at least
\Delta-2(\rho_{2}-c\sqrt{\Delta})
edges in the cut
E(C)
. We therefore have the following two lower bounds on the size of
E(C)
:

\displaystyle|E(C)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle k\cdot\big(\Delta-2(\rho_{1}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta})\big),

\displaystyle|E(C)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(n-k)\cdot\big(\Delta-2(\rho_{2}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta})\big).

If we combine the two inequalities with
Equation

21
, we get

\displaystyle\Delta-2(\rho_{1}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta})

\displaystyle\leq\ \frac{|E(C)|}{k}\ \leq

\displaystyle\frac{n-k}{n}\cdot(\Delta+3\sqrt{\Delta})+\gamma\cdot\frac{\sqrt{n}}{k},

\displaystyle\Delta-2(\rho_{2}\Delta-c\sqrt{\Delta})

\displaystyle\leq\ \frac{|E(C)|}{n-k}\ \leq

\displaystyle\frac{k}{n}\cdot(\Delta+3\sqrt{\Delta})+\gamma\cdot\frac{\sqrt{n}}{n-k}.

By using the lower bound on
k
and
n-k
from
Equation

22
, we get that
\gamma\sqrt{n}/k\leq\gamma\sqrt{n\Delta}/(cn)\leq\gamma/c
and similarly that
\gamma\sqrt{n}/(n-k)\leq\gamma/c
. As we can choose the constant
c
sufficiently large, we assume that
c\geq 2\gamma
such that
\gamma/c\leq 1/2\leq\sqrt{\Delta}/2
. By combining the two inequalities above, we then get

2\Delta-2(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})\Delta+2c\sqrt{\Delta}\leq\Delta+4\sqrt{\Delta}.

Solving for
\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}
, we get

\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}+(c-2)\cdot\sqrt{\Delta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\text{if }c>2)}}{{>}}\frac{1}{2}.

Hence, if we choose
c>2
, we get a contradiction to the assumption that
\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}=1/2
. This concludes the proof.
∎

6
Implementation with Small Messages

The primary focus of this paper is on exploring the randomized
complexity of LCL problems in the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model, where messages can be
of arbitrary size. We thus do not specifically discuss algorithms for
the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model, where messages have to consist of
O(\log n)

bits. We however believe that none of our algorithms inherently rely on the unbounded bandwidth offered by the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model. In fact, we expect that all our algorithms can be adapted to work in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model with minimal modifications. We next briefly sketch the main ideas of how to achieve this.

The degree splitting algorithm, for example, is built on a reduction to multiple instances of the sinkless orientation problem. To adapt this algorithm to the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model, we thus need to ensure that both the reduction process and the sinkless orientation problem can be handled within the message size constraints of the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model. We are aware of the existence of an
O(\log n)
-round deterministic and an
O(\log\log n)
-round randomized sinkless orientation algorithm for the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model
[
Fis
]
. Moreover, using the general result of
[
MU21
]
, a
(\log\log n)^{O(1)}
-round randomized
\mathsf{CONGEST}
sinkless orientation algorithm is straightforward. Each step of the degree splitting algorithm operates on a virtual graph where several instances of the sinkless orientation are executed in parallel on edge-disjoint subgraphs. Since the nodes of the virtual graph are a subset of the nodes of the communication graph, and the edges consist of edge-disjoint paths, this simulation can be efficiently executed without increasing the message size. Given sinkless orientation algorithms for the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model that are as efficient as the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model counterparts, we therefore expect that the algorithms underlying the results of
Theorems

1.1
and
1.4
can be transformed to work in the
\mathsf{CONGEST}
model at no additional asymptotic cost.

For the edge orientation algorithms that depend on black-box
applications of LLL algorithms, an efficient
\mathsf{CONGEST}
implementation can likely be achieved by leveraging recent generic
\mathsf{CONGEST}
algorithms for LLL problems, such as those in
[
MU21
,
HMP24
]
.

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we explored the randomized complexity of a subclass of
distributed binary labeling problems in the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model. We provided
in some cases tight and in some cases almost tight characterizations
of the randomized complexity of several natural problems, including
red-blue edge colorings and various edge orientation tasks. In
particular, we showed that certain edge coloring and orientation
problems with a deterministic complexities of
\Omega(\log n)
can be
solved exponentially faster using randomization, achieving time

O(\log\log n)
in bounded-degree trees and time

\widetilde{O}(\Delta\cdot\log^{4}\log n)
in general graphs.

We hope that our paper provides a foundation for several interesting
directions for future research. One immediate avenue is to explore the
extension of our techniques to the full set of binary labeling
problems that have been studied in
[
BBE+20
]
(which
includes coloring and orientation problems in
hypergraphs). Characterizing the randomized complexities of binary
labeling problems is a natural next step since they form the only
general class of problems for which a full characterization of the
deterministic complexity is known. Additionally, degree splittings
might be useful for highlevel problems beyond standard edge coloring
for which they have so far mostly been used. We hope that these
directions will lead to deeper insights into the power of
randomization for distributed graph algorithms.
