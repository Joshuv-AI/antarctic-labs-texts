The Periodic Table and the Group SO ⁡ ( 4 , 4 ) \SO(4,4)
V. V. Varlamov

Abstract
The periodic system of chemical elements is represented within the framework of the weight diagram of the Lie algebra of the fourth rank of the rotation group of an eight-dimensional pseudo-Euclidean space. The hydrogen realization of the Cartan subalgebra and Weyl generators of the group algebra is studied. The root structure of the subalgebras of the group algebra of a conformal group in the framework of a twofold covering is analyzed. Based on the analysis, the Cartan-Weyl basis of the group algebra is determined. The root and weight diagrams are constructed. A mass formula associated with each node of the weight diagram is introduced. Spin is interpreted as the fourth generator of the Cartan subalgebra, whose two eigenvalues correspond to two three-dimensional projections of the weight diagram containing elements of the periodic system from hydrogen to moscovium (the first projection) and from helium to oganesson (the second projection). One of the main advantages of the proposed group-theoretic construction of the periodic system is the natural inclusion of antimatter in the general scheme.



Keywords
: periodic law, conformal group, group algebra, Cartan subalgebra, Weyl generators, root structure, weight diagram, mass formula, spin, antimatter

Historically, the graphical representation of the periodic law has been very important. The graphical aspect is extremely important, since the first form in which the periodic law is represented is its well-known tabular representation. The book of Mazurs

Mendeleev himself, as is known, preferred a three-dimensional spiral structure (similar to the design of Chancourtois). The original tables of Mendeleev and Meyer, in addition to a clear visual image, indicate the distribution of chemical elements according to periods, groups and subgroups, and also contain connections between homologous elements, which allowed Mendeleecv to predict new elements at that time (gallium and germanium). However, from the very first years after the appearance of the Mendeleev table (1869), attempts began to be made to go beyond the two-dimensional tabular representation. So, in 1882, Bailey’s pyramidal model appeared, further improved by Thomsen (1895) and Bohr (1922). Moreover, screw and spiral models appeared earlier than the Mendeleev and Meyer tables (the ”Telluric screw” by Chancourtois (1862) and the Hinrichs spiral (1867)). A spiral is a two-dimensional figure, while a screw has three dimensions. Both designs were used in order to give a clear idea of the periodic table of elements. The advantage of both spiral and helical (spiral) systems is that they form a single whole. The projection of the screw onto the plane is a spiral. The screw system provides an additional means of expressing analogies in properties and, importantly, differences. Since this advantage was initially underestimated, the screw was used less frequently than the spiral. It was only after the discovery of isotopes that some researchers came to the conclusion that the shape of the screw makes it possible to solve the problem of including isotopes in the periodic table. For various graphical representations of the periodic table, see

Cartan elements
. Cartan generators satisfy permutation relations

\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{j}\right]=0,\quad\forall i,j=1,\ldots,m.

(1)

This means that all
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}
are simultaneously diagonalizable. Denoting their eigenvalues by
h_{i}
, we get

{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}\left|h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{m}\right\rangle=h_{i}\left|h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{m}\right\rangle,\quad\forall i=1\rightarrow m.

The eigenvalues
h_{i}
are called
weights
.
h_{i}
can be considered as components of the
m
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{h}
, which is called
weight vector
. The weights of the Cartan subalgebra are used as quantum numbers to denote a given multiplet.

Next, from the remaining generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf X}}_{i}
of the algebra
\mathfrak{g}
(
i=1\rightarrow n-m
), which are not elements of the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{K}
, we form linear combinations, which, in turn, form the set of
increasing
and
decreasing
operators (
ladder operators

{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}
–
Weyl generators
or
Weyl elements
). Along with Cartan generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}
, they make up the
Cartan-Weyl basis

\left\{{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}\right\}
of the algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

Thus, the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
can be decomposed into a direct sum consisting of the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{K}
(
m
generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}
) and
n-m
one-dimensional subalgebras
\mathfrak{W}_{\alpha}
formed by Weyl generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}
:

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{K}\bigoplus^{n-m}_{\alpha=1}\mathfrak{W}_{\alpha}=\mathfrak{K}\oplus\mathfrak{W}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{W}_{2}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathfrak{W}_{n-m}.

Generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}
and
{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}
satisfy permutation relations

\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}\right]=\alpha_{i}{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha},\quad\forall i=1\rightarrow m,\;\alpha=1\rightarrow n-m.

(2)

The relations (
2
) can be written as follows:

\begin{bmatrix}\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}\right]\\
\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}\right]\\
\vdots\\
\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{m},{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}\right]\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{m}\end{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}.

The various
\alpha_{i}
are called the
roots
of generator
{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}
. The set of roots
\alpha_{i}
can be considered as a collection of components of the vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, called the
root vector
, which belongs to the
m
-dimensional
root space
. The root vector for each Weyl generator
{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}
can be depicted in a diagram called a
Weyl diagram
, the dimension of which is equal to the rank
m
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. It should be noted that by virtue of (
1
) all Cartan generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}
have roots
\alpha_{i}=0
and, therefore, are located in the center of the Weyl diagram.

In general, the standard form of commutation relations for generators of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{lclcl}\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{j}\right]&=&0,&&\forall i,j=1,\ldots,m;\\
\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}\right]&=&\alpha_{i}{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha},&&\forall i=1\rightarrow m,\;\alpha=1\rightarrow n-m;\\
\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha},{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{-\alpha}\right]&=&\alpha^{i}{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i};&&\\
\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha},{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\beta}\right]&=&N^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\gamma},&&\beta\neq-\alpha.\end{array}

(3)

The quantities
N^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}
can also be expressed in terms of root vectors, so we know the algebra
\mathfrak{g}
if its roots are known. These roots have the property

\sum_{\alpha}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}=\delta_{ij},

where
\alpha
can accept only
n-m
values:

\alpha=\pm 1,\,\pm 2,\,\ldots,\,\pm\frac{1}{2}(n-m).

(4)

Next,
Casimir invariants

{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu}
commute with all generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf X}}_{i}
of the algebra
\mathfrak{g}
:

\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu},{\boldsymbol{\sf X}}_{i}\right]=0,\quad\forall\mu=1\rightarrow m,\;i=1\rightarrow n.

The number of Casimir invariants (operators) for a given Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is determined by the rank of this algebra.

Theorem 1
.

(Racah theorem) For every
n
-dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of rank
m
, there are a total of
m
Casimir operators
{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu}
(
\mu=1\rightarrow m
) that commute with generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf X}}_{i}
(
i=1\rightarrow n
) of the algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.

As a consequence, all
{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu}
also commute with Cartan generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}
:

\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu},{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}\right]=0,\quad\forall\mu,i=1\rightarrow m.

Thus, it is possible to find a complete set of states that are simultaneously the eigenstates of all
{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu}
and
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}
. Let’s define a ket-vector of the following form:

\left|c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{m};h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{m}\right\rangle=\left|c_{\mu};h_{i}\right\rangle,

where
c_{\mu}
,
h_{i}
are the eigenvalues of the operators
{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu}
,
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}
. Then

{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu}\left|c_{\mu};h_{i}\right\rangle=c_{\mu}\left|c_{\mu};h_{i}\right\rangle,\quad{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}\left|c_{\mu};h_{i}\right\rangle=h_{i}\left|c_{\mu};h_{i}\right\rangle

for all
\mu,i=1\rightarrow m
. We conclude that the Casimir invariants
{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu}
and the generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}
of the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{K}
allow us to label each state of the multiplet, while the ladder operators
{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}
allow us to move between states inside the multiplet, as shown in the Weyl diagram. Thus, when the ladder operator
{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}
acts on the ket-vector
\left|c_{\mu};h_{i}\right\rangle
, it shifts the eigenvalue of the operators
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}
by the value
\alpha_{i}
in accordance with

{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}\left|c_{\mu};h_{i}\right\rangle\approx\left|c_{\mu};h_{i}+\alpha_{i}\right\rangle.

The conformal group
\SO(4,2)

A special pseudo-orthogonal group in six dimensions,
\SO(4,2)
, corresponds to the rotation group of the six-dimensional pseudo-Euclidean space
\bb R^{4,2}
, or, equivalently, the set of
6\times 6
orthogonal matrices leaving a quadratic form

Q(\boldsymbol{r})=x^{2}_{1}+x^{2}_{2}+x^{2}_{3}+x^{2}_{4}-x^{2}_{5}-x^{2}_{6}=\boldsymbol{r}^{T}\boldsymbol{r}

invariant, where
\boldsymbol{r}=\left[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5},x_{6}\right]^{T}
.

The structure of the corresponding Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
is determined by the commutation properties of its generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\alpha\beta}
.
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\alpha\beta}
form the basis of the algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
. The number of independent generators is easy to find: out of 36 possible combinations of indices
\alpha
and
\beta
, six combinations disappear due to
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\alpha\alpha}=0
, this reduces the number of generators to 30. Moreover, by virtue of
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\alpha\beta}=-{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\beta\alpha}
, only 15 independent generators remain, the number of which can also be obtained using the formula
n(n-1)/2
. Thus,

{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}\Leftrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}0&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{12}&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{13}&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{14}&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{15}&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{16}\\
&0&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{23}&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{24}&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{25}&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{26}\\
&&0&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{34}&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{35}&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{36}\\
&&&0&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{45}&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{46}\\
&&&&0&{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{56}\\
&&&&&0\end{bmatrix}.

(5)

The system of fifteen generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\alpha\beta}
of the algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
satisfies the following permutation relations:

\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\alpha\beta},{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\gamma\delta}\right]=i\left(g_{\alpha\delta}{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\beta\gamma}+g_{\beta\gamma}{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\alpha\delta}-g_{\alpha\gamma}{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\beta\delta}-g_{\beta\delta}{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{\alpha\gamma}\right),

(6)

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta=1,\ldots,6
,
\alpha\neq\beta,\;\gamma\neq\delta
, at this point
g_{11}=g_{22}=g_{33}=g_{44}=1
,
g_{55}=g_{66}=-1
.

Hydrogen implementation of the algebra

(7)

{\sf L}_{2}

{\sf A}_{1}

Next, the full spectrum-generating algebra

\mathfrak{so}(2,1)

\Delta_{i}

\left[{\sf L}_{i},\Delta_{i}\right]=0.

Therefore, the generators
\Delta_{i}

\Delta_{1}={\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{46},\quad\Delta_{2}={\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{45},\quad\Delta_{3}={\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{56}.

Combining the generators
{\sf A}_{i}

{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{15}

{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{25}

\left[\Delta_{2},{\sf A}_{i}\right]=i{\sf B}_{i}.

The three generators

{\boldsymbol{\sf B}}

Similarly, the elements

3.2
Cartan subalgebra

Let’s find the maximum subset of commuting generators of the algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
. As is known, two generators commute if they do not have common indexes. It is easy to see that among the generators of the algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
, this condition is satisfied by three generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{12}
,
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{34}
and
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{56}
(i.e.
{\sf L}_{3}
,
{\sf A}_{3}
and
\Delta_{3}

\left[{\sf L}_{3},{\sf A}_{3}\right]=\left[{\sf L}_{3},\Delta_{3}\right]=\left[{\sf A}_{3},\Delta_{3}\right]=0.

The triplet
\left\{{\sf L}_{3},{\sf A}_{3},\Delta_{3}\right\}
forms the basis of
maximal abelian subalgebra

\mathfrak{K}\subset\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
(
Cartan subalgebra
).
{\sf L}_{3}
,
{\sf A}_{3}
and
\Delta_{3}
are called
Cartan generators
. The dimension of the subalgebra
\mathfrak{K}
defines the
rank
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
. As a result, all root and weight diagrams for
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
will be three-dimensional.

Casimir invariants
. Due to Racah theorem and the fact that the algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
has rank 3, it can be expected that the group
\SO(4,2)
admits three independent
Casimir invariants

{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu}
(
\mu=1\rightarrow 3
), which commute with all generators of the algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
. The most important Casimir operator of the group
\SO(4,2)
is a quadratic combination of invariants of various subgroups:

{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{2}={\boldsymbol{\sf L}}^{2}+{\bf\sf A}^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\sf B}}^{2}-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{2}+\Delta^{2}_{3}-\Delta^{2}_{1}-\Delta^{2}_{2}.

Here
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}^{2}+{\bf\sf A}^{2}
and
\Delta^{2}_{3}-\Delta^{2}_{1}-\Delta^{2}_{2}
are known as Casimir operators of the groups
\SO(4)
and
\SO(2,1)
, respectively. The other two Casimir operators of the group
\SO(4,2)
are polynomials of the third and fourth degree with respect to the generators of the algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
:

{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{3}=\frac{1}{48}\varepsilon_{abcdef}{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}^{ab}{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}^{cd}{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}^{ef},\quad{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{4}={\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{ab}{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}^{bc}{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{cd}{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}^{da}.

\left.\begin{array}[]{cc}{\sf X}_{+}={\sf X}_{1}+i{\sf X}_{2},&{\sf X}_{-}={\sf X}_{1}-i{\sf X}_{2},\\[2.84544pt]
{\sf Y}_{+}={\sf Y}_{1}+i{\sf Y}_{2},&{\sf Y}_{-}={\sf Y}_{1}-i{\sf Y}_{2}.\end{array}\right\}

(22)

Four Weyl generators (
22
), along with two Cartan generators
{\sf X}_{3}
and
{\sf Y}_{3}
, make up the
Cartan-Weyl basis
of the algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\bb C)
:

\left\{{\sf X}_{3},{\sf Y}_{3},{\sf X}_{+},{\sf X}_{-},{\sf Y}_{+},{\sf Y}_{-}\right\}.

Weyl generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}=\left\{{\sf X}_{\pm},{\sf Y}_{\pm}\right\}
and generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}=\left\{{\sf X}_{3},{\sf Y}_{3}\right\}
of the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{K}\subset\mathfrak{sl}(2,\bb C)
satisfy permutation relations (2) and (3), where
\forall i,j=1,2;\;\alpha=1\rightarrow 4
:

\left[{\sf X}_{3},{\sf X}_{+}\right]=-{\sf X}_{+},\quad\left[{\sf X}_{3},{\sf X}_{-}\right]={\sf X}_{-},\quad\left[{\sf X}_{+},{\sf X}_{-}\right]=-2{\sf X}_{3},

(23)

\left[{\sf Y}_{3},{\sf Y}_{+}\right]=-{\sf Y}_{+},\quad\left[{\sf Y}_{3},{\sf Y}_{-}\right]={\sf Y}_{-},\quad\left[{\sf Y}_{+},{\sf Y}_{-}\right]=-2{\sf Y}_{3}.

(24)

In this case, the commutator
\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha},{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{-\alpha}\right]=\left[{\sf X}_{+},{\sf X}_{-}\right]
gives the roots of
\pm 2
. Thus, in accordance with formula (4), we have four different roots:
\alpha=\pm 1,\,\pm 2
.

Since
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\bb C)
is a Lie algebra of rank 2, it follows from the Racah theorem (see paragraph 1) that there are two independent
Casimir invariants

{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu}
that commute with all generators of the algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\bb C)
, including two Cartan elements
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}
:

\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{\mu},{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{i}\right]=0,\quad\forall\mu=1\rightarrow 2;\;i=1\rightarrow 2.

(25)

The Casimir invariants for the algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\bb C)
have the form

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sf X}}^{2}={\sf X}^{2}_{1}+{\sf X}^{2}_{2}+{\sf X}^{2}_{3}=\frac{1}{4}\left({\bf\sf A}^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\sf B}}^{2}+2i{\bf\sf A}{\boldsymbol{\sf B}}\right),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sf C}}_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sf Y}}^{2}={\sf Y}^{2}_{1}+{\sf Y}^{2}_{2}+{\sf Y}^{2}_{3}=\frac{1}{4}\left({\bf\sf A}^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\sf B}}^{2}-2i{\bf\sf A}{\boldsymbol{\sf B}}\right).

Within the framework of the complex shell of the algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\bb C)
, these invariants, better known as
Laplace-Beltrami operators
, lead to Fuchs class differential equations for hyperspheric functions (see
[
61
]
). The Laplace-Beltrami operators contain the Casimir operators
{\bf\sf A}^{2}-{\boldsymbol{\sf B}}^{2}
and
{\bf\sf A}{\boldsymbol{\sf B}}
of the Lorentz group as parts of a complex-valued function.

By virtue of (
21
) and (
25
), a complete set of states is defined in the eigenspace
{\sf H}_{E}
of the energy operator
H
, which are simultaneously eigenstates of the operators
{\boldsymbol{\sf X}}^{2}
,
{\boldsymbol{\sf Y}}^{2}
,
{\sf X}_{3}
and
{\sf Y}_{3}
. Let’s make a ket-vector
\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle
. It should be noted that
l
and
\dot{l}
are not quantum numbers, but only
set
them, real quantum numbers, i.e. the eigenvalues of the Casimir operators
{\boldsymbol{\sf X}}^{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{\sf Y}}^{2}
are
l(l+1)
and
\dot{l}(\dot{l}+1)
according to the following relations:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sf X}}^{2}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle l(l+1)\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sf Y}}^{2}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{l}(\dot{l}+1)\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle,

where
l,\dot{l}\in\left\{0,\frac{1}{2},1,\frac{3}{2},\ldots\right\}
. Each subspace
{\sf H}_{E}
has dimension
(2l+1)(2\dot{l}+1)
, which implies that

\displaystyle{\sf X}_{3}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle,

(26)

\displaystyle{\sf Y}_{3}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{m}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

(27)

with
m\in\left\{-l,-l+1,\ldots,l-1,l\right\}
and
\dot{m}\in\left\{-\dot{l},-\dot{l}+1,\ldots,\dot{l}-1,\dot{l}\right\}
. The eigenvalues
m
and
\dot{m}
are the
weights
of the Cartan generators
{\sf X}_{3}
and
{\sf Y}_{3}
.

The construction of the
Weyl diagram
of the group
\SL(2,\bb C)
is based on the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{K}=\left\{{\sf X}_{3},{\sf Y}_{3}\right\}
, where the generators
{\sf X}_{3}
and
{\sf Y}_{3}
form the basis of a two-dimensional orthogonal coordinate system. In these diagrams, the weights
m
and
\dot{m}
are used as coordinates to construct each state of the
\SL(2,\bb C)
-multiplet in the plane
({\sf X}_{3},{\sf Y}_{3})
, i.e. they form components of a two-dimensional
weight vector

\boldsymbol{h}=(m,\dot{m})
, which exits the origin of the coordinate system to the state
\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle
. The Weyl generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}=\left\{{\sf X}_{\pm},{\sf Y}_{\pm}\right\}
allow us to move between the states of the
\SL(2,\bb C)
-multiplet by shifting the eigenvalues
m
and
\dot{m}
of any ket-vector
\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle
by the value that is given by the roots
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
of this Weyl generator relative to the Cartan generators
{\sf X}_{3}
and
{\sf Y}_{3}

{\boldsymbol{\sf E}}_{\alpha}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle\;\longrightarrow\;\left|l,\dot{l};m+\alpha_{1},\dot{m}+\alpha_{2}\right\rangle.

As an example, consider the action of the generator
{\sf X}_{+}
on the state
\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle{\sf X}_{3}{\sf X}_{+}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\left[{\sf X}_{3},{\sf X}_{+}\right]+{\sf X}_{+}{\sf X}_{3}\right)\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\sf X}_{+}+m{\sf X}_{+}\right)\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(m+1\right){\sf X}_{+}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle.

Thus,
{\sf X}_{+}
increases the eigenvalue
m
by
+1
, which is equal to the root of the generator
{\sf X}_{+}
relative to the Cartan generator
{\sf X}_{3}
according to (
23

27

\displaystyle{\sf Y}_{3}{\sf X}_{+}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\left[{\sf Y}_{3},{\sf X}_{+}\right]+{\sf X}_{+}{\sf Y}_{3}\right)\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(0+\dot{m}{\sf X}_{+}\right)\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{m}{\sf X}_{+}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle,

where the generator
{\sf X}_{+}
leaves the eigenvalue
\dot{m}
unchanged. Therefore,

{\sf X}_{+}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle\;\longrightarrow\;\left|l,\dot{l};m+1,\dot{m}\right\rangle.

The actions of the other three Weyl generators are defined similarly:

{\sf X}_{-}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle\;\longrightarrow\;\left|l,\dot{l};m-1,\dot{m}\right\rangle,

{\sf Y}_{+}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle\;\longrightarrow\;\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}+1\right\rangle,

{\sf Y}_{-}\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}\right\rangle\;\longrightarrow\;\left|l,\dot{l};m,\dot{m}-1\right\rangle.

For each generator of the subalgebra
\mathfrak{K}\subset\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
there are six generators that do not share indexes with it. For example, for
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{56}
(
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{3}
), these generators are
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{12}
,
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{23}
,
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{31}
,
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{14}
,
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{24}
and
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{34}
. Due to the permutation relations for the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
(see formula (6)), all these generators commute with
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{56}
and, therefore, can be positioned in the horizontal plane of the root diagram of the algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,2)
. They correspond to the components
{\sf L}_{i}
and
{\sf A}_{i}
of the angular momentum vector
{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}
and the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector
{\bf\sf A}
.

Introducing linear combinations

{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{1}=1/2\left({\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{23}+{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{14}\right),\quad{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{2}=1/2\left({\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{31}+{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{24}\right),\quad{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{3}=1/2\left({\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{12}+{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{34}\right),

(29)

{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{1}=1/2\left({\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{23}-{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{14}\right),\quad{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{2}=1/2\left({\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{31}-{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{24}\right),\quad{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{3}=1/2\left({\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{12}-{\boldsymbol{\sf L}}_{34}\right)

(30)

(see generators (8) and (9) of the Yao basis (
8
)–(
13
)), we obtain

\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf K}},{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}\right]=0,

and also

\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{j}\right]=i\varepsilon_{ijk}{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{k},\quad\left[{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{j}\right]=i\varepsilon_{ijk}{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{k}.

It follows that the generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}
form the bases of two independent algebras
\mathfrak{so}(3)
. Thus, the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4)
of the group
\SO(4)
is isomorphic to the direct sum

\mathfrak{so}(4)\simeq\mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2).

(31)

Accordingly, this means that the group
\SO(4)
is isomorphic to the direct product
\SO(3)\otimes\SO(3)
.

It is easy to see that the generators
{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{3}
and
{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{3}
form the Cartan subalgebra of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4)
,
\mathfrak{K}_{\mathfrak{so}(4)}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{3},{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{3}\right\}
. From the remaining generators in (
29
)–(
30
) we will make up four Weyl generators

\begin{array}[]{cc}{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{+}={\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{1}+i{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{2},&{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{-}={\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{1}-i{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{2},\\[2.84544pt]
{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{+}={\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{1}+i{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{2},&{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{-}={\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{1}-i{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{2}.\end{array}

Then the Cartan-Weyl basis of the subalgebra
\mathfrak{so}(4)
will take the form

\left\{{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{3},{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{3},{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{+},{\boldsymbol{\sf K}}_{-},{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{+},{\boldsymbol{\sf J}}_{-}\right\}.

(32)

The basis generators (
32
) satisfy the relations

A general note on the Haenzel and Finke systems

In both systems, the first three quantum numbers
n
,
l
and
m
have a clear geometric representation, originally borrowed by Bohr in his planetary model of the atom from celestial mechanics (radial and azimuthal motions). In contrast, the fourth quantum number
s
(spin) on a plane (Polygonaläche) or in space (Finke system) has an obviously artificial representation in the form of two points on transversals. All this suggests that the concept of spin does not fit into a three-dimensional representation. Therefore, all attempts to give a mechanical interpretation of spin within the framework of three-dimensional space were unsuccessful (for example, the original Uhlenbeck-Goudsmith idea of a spinning top).
Spin is an object of the four-dimensional world
, or, perhaps more correctly, –
the effect of the fourth dimension
12
12

12

In 1971, V.A. Fock, in his work
[
70
]
, posed the main question for the doctrine of the principle of periodicity and the theory of the periodic system: ”Do the properties of atoms and their constituent parts fit into the framework of purely spatial representations, or do we need to somehow expand the concepts of space and spatial symmetry in order to accommodate the inherent properties of atoms and their components?”
[
70
, P. 108]
. Obviously, by spatial representations and symmetries, Fock means the usual three-dimensional Euclidean space in which the Bohr model is implemented. At the end of the article
[
70
]
Fock himself answers the question he posed: ”Purely spatial degrees of freedom of an electron are not enough to describe the properties of the electron shell of an atom and it is necessary to go beyond purely spatial concepts in order to express the laws that underlie the periodic table. The electron’s new degree of freedom, its spin, makes it possible to describe properties of physical systems that are alien to classical concepts. This internal degree of freedom of the electron is essential for the formulation of the properties of multielectronic systems, and thus for the theoretical justification of the periodic table”
[
70
, P. 116]
.
. Thus, we come to the need to define a four-dimensional representation for an adequate description of spin, which includes all four degrees of freedom (
n,l,m,s
) in a single way.

Conclusion

This article provides an interpretation of the periodic table of chemical elements within the framework of the weight diagram of the group algebra
\mathfrak{so}(4,4)
of the rotation group
\SO(4,4)
of the eight-dimensional pseudo-Euclidean space
\bb R^{4,4}
. The central place in this generalization is played by the concept of spin. The origin of this concept is inextricably linked to the periodic table. As is known, the concept of spin was introduced by Pauli in 1925, explaining the doublet structure of the spectrum of alkali metals (the anomalous Zeeman effect): ”The doublet structure of the alkali spectra, as well as the violation of the Larmor theorem are,
according to this point of view, a result of a classically not describable two-valuedness of the quantum-theoretical properties of the valence electron”
[
71
]
. Later, Van der Waerden noted that this “
classically non-describable two-valuedness
” of electron we call spin
[
72
, P. 215]
.

It is well-known that, starting from the work of Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit
[
73
]
,
all the attempts to describe the electron spin classically had failed. In memorials
Yu. B. Rumer
[
74
]
wrote: “At his time Pauli said to Kronig that the spin theory is
nonsense because a point cannot rotate around itself”
[
74
, P. 56]
. Viewing electron as
a point is required in the special relativity; therefore, both electrons in quantum
electrodynamics and quarks in quantum chromodynamics are treated as pointwise
fermions with spin 1/2. As V.A. Fok argued
[
70
]
,
spin is not a mechanical concept
. V.A. Fok noted: ”The word ”spin” literally means ”spinning”; the name came about because the operators related to this degree of freedom can be formally interpreted as operators of the intrinsic angular momentum of the electron. This does not mean, however, that an electron can be likened to a spinning top or a rotating ball, etc.; mechanical comparisons are decidedly unsuitable here… spin is not a mechanical concept”
[
70
, p. 111]
.

The first theory providing a
correct mathematical formulation of the “classically non-describable two-valuedness”
of the electron spin was proposed by Pauli in 1927
[
75
]
. Avoiding any visual
mechanical models, Pauli introduced doubled Hilbert space
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{2}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{\infty}
(vector space of
wave functions), whose vectors are two-component spinors. That was the first advent
of two-component spinors in physics and the first case of doubling. The next doubling that yielded the
{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{4}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\sf H}}_{\infty}
space of bispinors was accomplished by Dirac in 1928
[
76
]
. In 1933, Gustav Mie introduced 8-component spinors
[
77
]
:

\text{{2-spinors} (Pauli)}\longrightarrow\text{{4-spinors} (Dirac)}\longrightarrow\text{{8-spinors} (Mie)}.

Doubling is a universal characteristic of matter. The electron’s spin is a manifestation of the duality of matter, not its intrinsic property. Recall that experimentally observed electrons exist with only one direction of spin, and not with two at the same time, duality exists in matter (substance), which gives its accidents (electrons and other states) by one spin value or another, depending on the experimental situation (manifestation).
