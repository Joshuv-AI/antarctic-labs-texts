A Benchmark and Knowledge-Grounded Framework for Advanced Multimodal Personalization Study
Xia Hu, Honglei Zhuang, Brian Potetz, Alireza Fathi, Babak Samari, Howard Zhou

Abstract
The powerful reasoning of modern Vision Language Models open a new frontier for advanced personalization study. However, progress in this area is critically hampered by the lack of suitable benchmarks. To address this gap, we introduce Life-Bench, a comprehensive, synthetically generated multimodal benchmark built on simulated user digital footprints. Life-Bench features over 16,000 16,000 questions evaluating a wide spectrum of capabilities, from persona understanding to complex reasoning over historical data. These capabilities expand far beyond prior benchmarks, reflecting the critical demands essential for real-world applications. Furthermore, we propose LifeGraph, an end-to-end framework that organizes personal context into a knowledge graph to facilitate structured retrieval and reasoning. Our experiments on Life-Bench reveal that existing methods falter significantly on complex personalized tasks, exposing a large performance headroom, especially in relational, temporal and aggregative reasoning. While LifeGraph closes this gap by leveraging structured knowledge and demonstrates a promising direction, these advanced personalization tasks remains a critical open challenge, motivating new research in this area.

The absence of a comprehensive benchmark for these more complex scenarios limits the development of advanced multimodal personalization methods.

To address this critical gap, we introduce Life-Bench: a comprehensive multimodal benchmark designed to evaluate advanced personalization, with a focus on complex retrieval and reasoning tasks.
Life-Bench is structured around “virtual accounts” (Vaccounts).
Each Vaccount simulates a user’s digital footprint, containing a social network of personal concepts and a multimodal life log composed of timestamped images and descriptions.

Life-Bench consists of fully synthetic data to ensure privacy, while the generation process is seeded with textual scenarios derived from public, real-world image captions for realism.

The complex reasoning tasks introduced in Life-Bench highlight the need for retrieval methods that go beyond simple semantic matching.
To explore this technical direction, we propose LifeGraph, an end-to-end framework that organizes personal context into a knowledge graph to enable structured reasoning for multimodal personalization.
The framework comprises two core components:
a VLM-assisted pipeline that builds a structured representation of personal context with indexed links to the source multimodal data;
and a tailored retrieval algorithm adapted from Think-on-Graph

;
37

We validate the effectiveness and efficiency of this graph-based methodology by theoretical analysis and empirically demonstrate its strong performance.

We evaluate LifeGraph and existing retrieval-based methods

;
12
;
25
on Life-Bench.
Our key findings:
(1) Current methods exhibit a critical weakness in complex reasoning, struggling significantly with tasks that require temporal and aggregated understanding.
(2) LifeGraph consistently excels at complex reasoning tasks, while RAG-based approaches shows a clear advantage on tasks favoring direct information matching.
(3) Increasing the retrieval context size does not guarantee better performance, highlighting the challenge of context precision in personalization.

3.1
Benchmark Overview

To mirror real-world personalization scenario, we introduce the term
Vaccount
(short for virtual account).
Each Vaccount simulates a user’s digital footprint and consists of two components:
(1) 3 to 5
concepts
including the user and their relatives, forming a small social network within the vaccount. Each concept is defined by a representative image and a brief persona.
(2)
Historical images
where each image is annotated with a date and a short, social-media style description. Images from the same event share the same date and description.
This Vaccount design aligns our benchmark with realistic setting, and enable complex reasoning tasks that requires retrieving and reasoning over social relationship and historical data.
Building on this, tasks in Life-Bench evaluate the ability to leverage this personalized context in different granularity, with each question being grounded in a specific Vaccount.

Life-Bench comprises 16,315 question-answer pairs constructed from 10 distinct Vaccounts. These Vaccounts are built upon 33 concepts and 2,479 historical images.
The questions are organized in 10 tasks over 2 primary categories: Relational Concept Identification, Historical Retrieval and Understanding.
The questions span over two input formats (text-only, text+image) and three output formats (multiple-choice, binary, open-generation).
Each question is assigned a difficulty rating (easy, medium, hard) to enable detailed performance analysis.
Table

2
shows key statistics of the Life-Bench.

3.1.1
Categories

To ensure our benchmark is comprehensive and reflects real-world use cases, we design Life-Bench to assess a broad spectrum of reasoning capabilities moving beyond prior work
2
;
31
;
12
;
18
.
These include not only foundational tasks like concept identification and preference study, but also advanced reasoning over relationships, detailed events and scenes, temporal sequences, and aggregated historical patterns (
Table

To systematically evaluate this wide range of abilities, we structure our benchmark tasks into two overarching categories.
The first,
Relational Concept Identification
, assesses the crucial skills of reasoning over personal concepts and relationships.
The second,
Historical Retrieval and Understanding
, targets the more complex, dynamic reasoning required to make sense of a user’s evolving multimodal history.
The distribution of tasks across these categories is shown in
Fig.

2
, and concrete examples of all tasks in
Fig.

.

Relational Concept Identification.

This category evaluates a model’s foundational ability to recognize and reason about personal concepts and relationships.
The tasks require the model to ground named concepts within a Vaccount and understand their attributes or actions.
A key challenge introduced in our benchmark is relational reasoning. Questions are designed with varying difficulty over relations, from easy direct queries about a concept to medium level multi-hop (
e.g
.
, “aunt’s dog”) relational concept queries that requires resolving a chain of social relationships.
As examples shown in
Fig.

1
, 3 tasks in this category assess a model’s capability to identify concepts, resolve multi-hop relation chain, analyze their presence and attributes, and perform concept-centric visual reasoning.

Historical Retrieval and Understanding.

This category assesses a model’s capability to query and reason over a user’s entire Vaccount history, a critical area for advanced personalization that remains underexplored in multimodal research.
We structure the 7 tasks in this category into two distinct subcategories, based on the required granularity of personal context.
Four tasks under
Event-centric
subcategory evaluates the ability to reason over a specific event from the user’s context (
e.g
.
, images from a single date) and answer detailed questions about that isolation context.
Three tasks under
Complex Logical
subcategory demand a broader, more holistic understanding. These tasks require aggregating information across multiple dates and events, to infer personalization patterns, such as personas and preferences, activity frequency, or complex relational-temporal sequences.
As examples in
Fig.

1
, 7 tasks are classified as medium or hard level difficulty to reflect their inherent complexity and multi-hop retrieval requirements.

3.2
Benchmark Construction

All data in Life-Bench, including images, personas, and historical contexts, is synthetically generated.
This approach is chosen for two primary reasons.
Primarily, it fundamentally addresses the privacy concerns inherent in using real data.
Second, as a training-free evaluation benchmark, the synthetic pipeline allows us to craft complex, consistent and controllable scenarios specifically designed for model evaluation.
Our data generation pipeline is powered by advanced generative models, including Gemini 2.5 Pro
11
and Gemini 2.5 Flash Image
17
. To ensure data integrity, we applied a comprehensive quality control process at each stage.
Below we describe this data generation pipeline.

Seeds Preparation.

The first step of our data generation pipeline is to prepare three types of text-based seeds: Vaccount, concept, and event seeds.
First, we generate 50
Vaccount seeds
using Gemini 2.5 Pro
11
. Each Vaccount seed is an outline for a virtual account, specifying a primary concept’s name, age, career, a social network of 3 to 5 relatives, and a brief life story.
Next, to source realistic scenarios, we leverage captions of images derived from a CC-licensed subset of the YFCC100M personal photo dataset
40
. We use Gemini 2.5 Pro to generate descriptive captions for these images. The captions are then partitioned into two classes. Captions describing a single, clear subject (person or animal) are classified to
concept seeds
. The remaining captions are grouped by date for each account, then Gemini 2.5 Pro is prompted to synthesize them into narrative event descriptions, forming the
event seeds
.
Only the text captions are used as seeds in our pipeline; no source images from YFCC100M are incorporated into the final dataset.

Vaccount Data Generation.

The data for each Vaccount is generated through a multi-step process.
First, we establish the Vaccount concepts by selecting a Vaccount seed and using Gemini 2.5 Pro to assign corresponding concept seeds.
The model then generates detailed personas and visual descriptions for each individual.
Then we perform a refinement step to revise the descriptions to ensure logical consistency (
e.g
.
, shared facial features among family members).
Using these refined descriptions, we generate a portrait image for each concept with Gemini 2.5 Flash Image
17
.

Next, we construct historical data for each Vaccount.
Gemini 2.5 Pro selects a series of events from event seeds and rewrites them into coherent narratives that align with the established Vaccount personas. Meanwhile, a synthetic date stamp is assigned to each event.
For each event, the model selects and refines a set of descriptive image captions from the event seed to complete the story and explicitly name the involved concepts.
Finally, to generate historical images and maintain visual consistency of concepts, the image generation
17
is conditioned on both the refined captions and the portrait image of relevant concepts.
This multi-step process ensures generated contents are logically coherent, and centered on unique Vaccount personas.

Question-Answer Generation.

After generating Vaccount data, we then proceed to create the question-answer (QA) pairs.
For each task, we provide Gemini 2.5 Pro with the relevant Vaccount context (
e.g
.
, social relationships, historical images) and a set of predefined question templates to generate question-answer pairs.
A key advantage of our synthetic pipeline is that the generation process itself provides ground-truth context.
For example, the textual captions used to generate an image serve as a factual basis for creating related visual questions.
We provide this source information as ground truth metadata directly to the model during QA generation to ensure the initial accuracy of the answers.
This step produces QA pairs of tasks, each grounded in the factual context of its Vaccount.

Quality Control and Verification.

To guarantee a high-quality benchmark, we perform quality control and verfication from three perspectives:

•

Image fidelity and consistency:
We generate 3 candidate images for each image. Then we use Gemini 2.5 Pro ensemble vote for the best candidate based on realism and consistency. Low quality images without selected candidates are filtered out. This is followed by a manual review by authors and regenerated unqualified images.

•

QA Correctness:
We use a two-step process to verify QA pairs. First, each generated QA pair undergoes several rounds of verification by Gemini 2.5 Pro to filter out questions that are factually incorrect, ambiguous or unanswerable, using the relative ground-truth context as references. A final manual verification is then performed.

•

Data Diversity:
To ensure data diversity and mitigate generative model bias, our entire process is seeded with textual scenarios derived from the large-scale YFCC100M dataset
40
. This approach grounds our benchmark in realistic situations while fully preserving privacy through a multi-step rewriting process.

LifeGraph for Personalization

4.1
Retrieval-Enhanced VLM Personalization

Retrieval-enhanced personalization provides a powerful, training-free framework for adapting VLMs.
The core principle is to externalize a user’s personal knowledge and dynamically retrieve only the most relevant information at inference time.
This approach is crucial as a user’s rich, multimodal life history is often too vast to fit within a model’s finite context window.
It also offers the scalability and efficiency required for practical applications, especially in resource-constrained scenarios (
e.g
.
, on-device
42
).
This paradigm effectively grounds model reasoning in private, evolving data without overhead of continuous fine-tuning.

We provide a formal, approach-agnostic definition for retrieval-enhanced VLM personalization.
Let
\mathcal{F}
denote a Vision-Language Model, which in a general non-personalized setting maps a query
x
to a response
y=\mathcal{F}(x)
.
For personalization, we introduce a user-specific personal knowledge space
P
and a personalized retriever
\mathcal{R}
.
Given a query
x
, the retriever
\mathcal{R}
extracts a set of relevant context from
P
, denoted by
C_{x}=\mathcal{R}(x,P)
.
Then the personalized response is conditioned on both the original query
x
and the retrieved context
C_{x}
.
Thus, retrieval-based personalization system can be represented as:

y=\mathcal{F}(x,C_{x})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002ptC_{x}=\mathcal{R}(x,P)

4.2
Personal Knowledge Graph Solution

To handle the complex, structured nature of personal data, the personal knowledge graph (PKG)
6
;
4
;
43
;
36
offers a powerful framework.
To develop this solution, we first provide a formal definition of the personal knowledge graph, then introduce LifeGraph in
Section

4.3
and theoretical discussion of its effectiveness in
Section

4.4
.

Formally, we define a personal knowledge graph as
G=({N},{T},{E})
where
{N}
is a set of node entities,
{E}
is a set of edge relations,
{T}\subseteq{N}\times{E}\times{N}
is the set of triples. Each triple
(u,r,v)\in{T}
represents a personal fact.
Under this definition, the retrieval system
\mathcal{R}
becomes a graph retrieval.
Given a query
x
, a graph retriever
\mathcal{R}_{G}
searches
G
to extract relevant context
C_{x}
, which is typically a set of factual triples, written as
C_{x}\subseteq{T}
.

4.3
LifeGraph

We introduce LifeGraph, an end-to-end personal knowledge graph solution for advanced personalization.
LifeGraph consists of two main components: a semi-automated graph construction approach, and a graph retrieval enhanced personalized reasoning algorithm.
We outline the core design principles below, with comprehensive implementation details provided in the appendix.

LifeGraph construction.

Inspired by recent works that use generative models to assist with knowledge graph construction
16
;
44
, we leverage VLMs to build the LifeGraph.
To ensure a high-quality and expressive graph, we incorporate several key designs:
(1)
Hybrid Schema.
We use a semi-structured schema. Node entity types are predefined to ensure consistency, while relations are open-ended, allowing the model to capture the diverse, nuanced connections present in personal data.
(2)
Two-step conversational construction.
We structure the graph construction in two main steps. The first step extracts concepts and relationships to form a stable personal social network scaffold. The second step then process dynamic historical events and intergrates them into the graph. Each step employs a two-turn conversational VLM prompt to first identify candidate entities then validate and formalize the factual triples.
(3)
Graph to multimodal source indexing.
To preserve the full fidelity of personal data, node entities and edge relations are indexed to the source data. This allows retrieving to fetch original rich multimodal context on demand, complementing the structured facts from a LifeGraph.
(4)
N-ary relation representation.
To model complex
n
-ary facts
27
;
41
that involve more than two entities, we format relations as hyper-edges with auxiliary attributes. This allows us to capture richer,
n
-ary facts without losing factual integrity.

LifeGraph Retrieval

To query the LifeGraph, we employ a graph retrieval algorithm based on the Think-on-Graph
37
;
28
framework, which integrates LLM-driven reasoning with an iterative beam search for graph traversal, to expand paths by retrieving relevant entities and relations at each step.

We adapt the original Think-on-Graph for the personalized, multimodal context of LifeGraph with two key modifications.
First, given the smaller and denser nature of personal LifeGraph, we consolidate the original two-step entity and relation retrieval into a single, unified
\textsc{search}-\textsc{prune}
step.
Second, our graph retriever uses the graph’s source indexes to fetch the source multimodal context when needed by the query, enabling a deeper, more context-aware reasoning process.

4.4
Structural Properties and Retrieval Efficiency

Figure 3
:
Degree frequency statistics of LifeGraph demonstrating it approximately aligns with power-law
5
;
30
of scale-free graph.

Personal knowledge graphs, akin to many real-world networks, often exhibit the properties of a scale-free random graph
30
;
8
.
This structure is characterized by a power-law degree distribution
5
;
30
, where most nodes have low degrees while a few “hub” nodes serve as highly connected entities. We empirically verify this property in our constructed LifeGraph, as shown in
Fig.

3
, confirming its structural similarity to scale-free graph.

This structure has profound implication for its storage and retrieval efficiency.
To store
m
facts in a graph, the number of nodes is generally bounded within
(\sqrt{m},2m]
.
In the specific case of a scale free graph, the number of nodes is approximately
m+c
for some constant
c

7
.
More critically, such graphs are expected to have a small diameter that scales logarithmically with the number of nodes, approximately
\log(m)

7
.
This “small-world” property ensures that the path length between any two nodes is short.
Consequently, a graph retriever
\mathcal{R}_{G}
can theoretically find a path within
\log(m)/2
hops.
While retrieval cost is linear with traversal depth, this logarithmic scaling of the graph diameter ensures this depth remains small.
This makes the PKG an expressive and computationally efficient substrate for complex, multi-hop personalization queries.

Experimental Setup

We evaluate LifeGraph against state-of-the-art retrieval-enhanced personalization methods across the 10 tasks in our Life-Bench benchmark.

Evaluation Metric.

Our primary evaluation metric is accuracy.
For multiple-choice and binary questions, we measure exact-match accuracy.
For open-ended tasks, we use a LLM-as-a-Judge setup

Baselines.

We compare against a suite of leading retrieval-enhanced personalization methods:

Conclusion

In this work, we advance the study of complex multimodal personalization.
We introduce Life-Bench, a comprehensive benchmark that moves beyond simple concept identification by evaluating complex reasoning over personal history, social relationships, and temporal patterns.
To address these advanced tasks, we propose LifeGraph, a novel knowledge graph-based framework that demonstrate strong performance through structured context retrieval.
Our contributions offer the community a robust evaluation testbed and a new technical direction for developing advanced personalization models.
We hope this work motivate future research into more sophisticated personalization methods, particularly graph-based approaches and backbone models for robust, efficient personalized reasoning.

•

: This task probes for fine-grained details about a concept’s involvement in a historical event. It requires retrieving the event context and identifying the reletive concepts to answer questions about their appearance, actions, and interactions.

•

: This task requires reasoning about an concept’s involvement in a historical event, where the target individual is identified through a relational description. LifeGraph Details

This section provide details of the LifeGraph design.

8.1
LifeGraph Construction

LifeGraph is implemented as a directed graph. This structure is essential for accurately representing personal facts, where relations are often asymmetric.

Hybrid Schema.

We employ a semi-structured schema for the graph, where node entity types are predefined and the edge relations are open-ended. This design choice is motivated by two key objectives:
(1) Consistency and Graph Quality: Predefined entity types enforces a consistent graph structure. This constraint guides the VLMs during the automated graph construction process to ensure a high-quality and logically sound knowledge graph.
(2) Flexibility and Expressiveness: Open-ended edge relations provide the flexibility to capture the diverse and nuanced information in different personal histories and scenarios. This allows the graph to represent a wide range of specific contexts and scenarios without being limited by a rigid, predefined set of relations.

We define the following six entity types:

•

PersonAnimal
: Represents core personal concepts.

•

Event
: Represents a specific occurrence in the user’s history, typically associated with a date and one or more images.

•

Date
: Represents a specific date or timestamp.

•

Location
: Represents a geographical place associated with an Event or PersonAnimal .

•

Activity
: Represents an action or occasion occurring within an Event.

•

Object
: Any other descriptive facts related to a concept or event will be covered by Object.

In this schema, the PersonAnimal and Event entities serve as the primary ‘hub’ nodes. Most other entities are connected to these hubs.
This design creates a richly interconnected knowledge structure centered on key individuals and historical events. The explicit linking of Event and Date nodes further enables reasoning over the user’s life as a chronological trajectory.

Figure 8
:
Graph structure of a constructred LifeGraph.

Two-Step Conversational Construction.

The graph construction process is structured into two sequential steps to ensure the integrity and quality of the resulting knowledge graph.
The first step extracts the primary PersonAnimal entities and their static, foundational relationships. This establishes a stable social network structure that serves as the core of the Personal Knowledge Graph.
The second step processes the dynamic historical events and integrates them into the pre-built scaffold.
The initial social-network LifeGraph serves as contextual input, alongside the multimodal historical data, which is processed in batches.
To optimize this, we employ a specialized bin-packing algorithm that clusters relevant images together.
Specifically, we group historical images chronologically and adopt a greedy bin-packing strategy to consolidate sequential events into batches.
This algorithm restricts each batch size to the VLM’s effective reasoning capacity rather than filling the entire context window, which is a good balance between graph construction efficiency and maintaining the stability and quality of the graph.
This step enables the model to extract historical facts in a personalized manner, grounding the new information in the user’s established concepts and relationships.
This decoupled approach is crucial for quality control.
By first establishing an accurate representation of the social network, we ensure the core structure of LifeGraph is consistent before integrating the more complex and varied information from historical data.

Inspired by multi-turn conversational knowledge graph construction methods
32
, we design a two-turn conversational LifeGraph construction process.
The first turn identifies candidate entities from the source multimodal data.
We instruct the VLM to extract candidate entities, strictly limiting them to the predefined entity types.
The second turn formalizes these candidates into structured factual triples.
Based on the conversation history, the model generates triples to comprehensively describe the source data.
Crucially, entities in this step are constrained to the candidate set established in the first turn.
This strictly anchors graph relations to verified entities and enables expressing complex
n
-ary relations as edge attributes.

Graph to Multimodal Source Indexing.

To preserve the full fidelity of the source data, entities and relations within LifeGraph maintain an index to the original multimodal context. During construction, the VLM is prompted to cite the source image IDs that provide factual support for each extracted triple. These references are stored as attributes within the corresponding nodes and edges. This indexing allows the retrieval algorithm to fetch the original rich multimodal context on demand, complementing the graph’s structured facts.

N-ary Relation Representation.

To model complex
n
-ary facts
27
;
41
, we identify a primary binary relation and encode all additional information as (key, value) attributes on the corresponding edge, forming what we term a ‘hyper-edge’.
This property graph approach allows us to represent multi-faceted facts within a simple triple structure, preserving factual integrity without creating intermediate nodes. For instance, a
n
-ary fact “In Christmas Party event, David and Rylen are decrocating the Christmas tree using red ornaments” can be express as “Christmas Party event - hasActivity
<
attendee: [David, Rylen], object: red ornaments
>
- Decorate Christmas Tree”.

The complete construction process results in a richly interconnected personal knowledge graph.
