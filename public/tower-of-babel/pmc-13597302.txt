CSTF2-mediated 3′UTR shortening drives oncogenic activation of GPC3 in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Soyoung Jeon, Eunbi Shin, Jin Woong Ha, Min Jeong Na, Sang Yean Kim, Suk Woo Nam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alternative polyadenylation (APA) generates mRNA isoforms with distinct 3′ untranslated regions (3′UTRs), thereby influencing transcript stability and translation. In cancer, 3′UTR shortening can activate oncogenes by escaping microRNA (miRNA)-mediated repression, but its role in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains poorly defined. Here, we profiled mRNA length alterations in multistage human HCC transcriptome datasets and investigated their functional consequences. Approximately 77% of mRNAs with altered length exhibited 3′UTR shortening. Glypican-3 (GPC3) was the most prominently upregulated shortened transcript, and high GPC3 expression was associated with poor prognosis in HCC. GPC3 knockdown reduced proliferation and induced apoptosis, whereas GPC3 overexpression promoted cell growth. Among APA regulators, Cleavage Stimulation Factor 2 (CSTF2) was upregulated in HCC, correlated positively with GPC3 expression, and predicted adverse clinical outcomes. Modulation of CSTF2 expression altered GPC3 3′UTR length, with CSTF2 overexpression promoting GPC3 3′UTR shortening, increasing GPC3 protein expression, enhancing proliferation, and suppressing apoptosis. Further analysis revealed that GPC3 3′UTR shortening removed binding sites for miR-96-5p and miR-140-5p, relieving miRNA-mediated translational repression. These findings identify CSTF2-driven APA as a mechanism of oncogenic GPC3 activation in HCC and suggest the CSTF2-GPC3 axis as a potential therapeutic target.

Introduction

Alternative polyadenylation (APA) is an important post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism that affects gene expression by modifying mRNA 3′ untranslated regions (3′UTRs) [1]. The 3′UTR contains regulatory elements, including miRNA and RNA-binding protein binding sites, which play critical roles in mRNA stability, translation, and localization. By selectively using different polyadenylation sites, APA provides an additional layer of gene expression regulation that can be dynamically altered in response to cellular conditions [2, 3]. Recently, emerging evidence suggests that APA plays an important role in oncogenesis, particularly by activating proto-oncogenes [4]. APA-mediated 3′UTR shortening is common in many cancers, including kidney, gastric, breast, and lung cancers, and this removes tumor-suppressive miRNA-binding sites, allowing oncogenic transcripts to avoid miRNA-mediated repression and increase mRNA stability and protein expression [5]. In kidney renal clear cell carcinoma, MORC2 has an oncogenic potential via the APA-regulated factor NUDT21 [6]. Additionally, the 3′UTR of KHDRBS1 is shortened in gastric cancer and KHDRBS1 mRNA with a shortened 3′UTR can avoid the inhibitory effect of miR-142-3p, miR-200b-3p and miR-204-5p [7]. Thus, it was suggested that 3′UTR shortening interferes with miRNA-mediated gene silencing to activate oncogenes in tumor cells, increasing proliferation and survival.

The core APA machinery is composed of multiple protein complexes, including Cleavage and Polyadenylation Specificity Factor (CPSF), Cleavage Stimulation Factor (CstF), and Cleavage Factors Im (CFIm) and IIm (CFIIm), which collectively recognize poly(A) signals and catalyze mRNA 3′-end processing [8]. By generating distinct 3′UTR isoforms from a single gene, APA contributes to dynamic gene expression changes during development and disease. Dysregulation of APA has been increasingly linked to cancer, as it can result in shortened 3′UTRs that facilitate oncogene overexpression or elongated 3′UTRs that disrupt tumor suppressor function [9]. For example, the downregulation of CFIm25 in glioblastoma enhances tumorigenic properties and increases tumor growth [10]. Similarly, the reduction of CPSF6 leads to a shortened 3′UTR of cell-cycle-related genes and accelerates cell proliferation [11].

Glypian-3 (GPC3) is a member of the glypican family of GPI-anchored heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) [12]. While GPC3 expression is almost undetectable in normal liver tissues, it is highly expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and is closely associated with tumor growth and progression [13, 14]. Previous reports showed that GPC3 interacts with multiple signaling pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin, YAP, and Hedgehog, thereby influencing cell proliferation, survival, and migration in HCC [12]. For example, GPC3 activates the Wnt signaling pathway, promoting tumor cell proliferation [15]. GPC3 also interacts with FAT1, a component of the Hippo pathway, to regulate YAP activity, thereby influencing cell growth and survival [16]. Furthermore, serum GPC3 detection has demonstrated higher specificity than α-fetoprotein (AFP), highlighting its potential for early HCC diagnosis [17]. In cancer cells, the regulation of GPC3 has been partially attributed to promoter hypomethylation—in contrast to hypermethylation observed in normal tissues—and the involvement of specific long non-coding RNAs [18]. However, upstream regulators and the precise mechanisms underlying GPC3 overexpression remain largely unexplored. Therefore, a deeper understanding of GPC3 regulatory mechanisms during HCC development is critical for its potential application as a biomarker or therapeutic target.

APA leading to excessive 3′UTR shortening of specific mRNAs is known to drive gene overexpression during tumorigenesis. To investigate whether this mechanism contributes to the oncogenic upregulation of GPC3, we applied the DaPars algorithm (Dynamic Analysis of Alternative Polyadenylation from RNA-seq) to analyze candidate 3′UTR length changes [19]. Surprisingly, the majority of genes exhibited significant 3′UTR shortening, among which GPC3 showed the most pronounced increase in expression. Further investigation revealed that CSTF2 promotes GPC3 3′UTR shortening, thereby facilitating malignant transformation and proliferation of hepatocytes. Additionally, prediction of miRNA binding sites identified miR-96-5p and miR-140-5p as potential post-transcriptional regulators of GPC3 via interactions with its 3′UTR, implicating their role in modulating HCC cell growth.

This study aims to elucidate whether APA-mediated 3′UTR shortening of GPC3 contributes to its oncogenic potential by enabling escape from miRNA-mediated repression. Understanding the role of GPC3 in HCC development and progression may offer novel insights into its regulatory mechanisms and suggest new avenues for therapeutic intervention in HCC.

Materials and methods

Publicly available genomic data analyses

To investigate the expression of genes involved in 3′UTR shortening of mRNA and APA regulator gene in HCC, data were obtained from Catholic university_ multistage Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (Catholic_mLIHC, GSE114564), The Cancer Genome Atlas liver hepatocellular carcinoma project (TCGA_LIHC), the International Cancer Genome Consortium Liver Cancer–RIKEN, JP (ICGC_LIRI) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (Accession Numbers: GSE77314). Clinicopathological characteristics of the Catholic_mLIHC and TCGA_LIHC cohorts used in this study, including gender, age, American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Tumor–Node–Metastasis (TNM) stage, and—where available—etiology and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels, are summarized in Tables S4 and S5. For TCGA_LIHC mRNA expression analysis, we used the TCGA-LIHC.star_fpkm.tsv dataset, and FPKM values were transformed as log2(FPKM + 1) prior to analysis.

For Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, patients were classified into high- and low-expression groups according to gene expression levels. For GPC3 survival analysis in TCGA_LIHC, the best expression cut-off provided by the Human Protein Atlas was used. This cut-off was defined as the pTPM value that yielded the maximal survival difference between the two groups based on the lowest log-rank P value. For other datasets were stratified based on the average expression value of each gene within each cohort. Survival differences were assessed using the log-rank test.

To evaluate miRNAs potentially involved in GPC3 3′UTR shortening, miRNA expression datasets from the Catholic_mLIHC (GSE114564) and TCGA_LIHC. For TCGA_LIHC miRNA expression analysis, we used the TCGA.LIHC.sampleMap/miRNA_HiSeq_gene dataset, and RPM values provided by the TCGA pipeline were processed as log2(RPM + 1).

DaPars analysis

DaPars was used for the identification of the most significant APA events between tumor and normal samples. Data were obtained from TCGA_LIHC and Catholic_mLIHC. The analysis workflow involved processing raw RNA-seq data into formats suitable for DaPars, alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19), and computation of the Percentage of Distal PolyA site Usage Index (PDUI), enabling quantitative assessment of APA dynamics between tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues. The PDUI score for a given gene represents the percentage of distal PAS usage in the sample. A higher PDUI score represents greater usage of the distal PAS site. To compare 3′UTR changes for a given gene between tumor and normal samples, the PDUI scores for the gene averaged over tumor samples (PDUIT) and normal (PDUIN) samples. A change in the mean PDUI score between tumor and normal samples for each gene (ΔPDUI = PDUIT − PDUIN) was computed as a measure of 3′UTR differences.

Tissue samples

A total of 10 matched pairs of HCC tissues with their corresponding noncancerous liver tissues were obtained from the Biobank of InJe University Paik Hospital (InjeBiobank), a member of Korea Biobank Network. Written informed consent was obtained from each subject according to the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study was approved by the Institutional Review of Board (IRB) of the Songeui Campus, College of Medicine, the Catholic University of Korea (IRB approval number: MC22TESI0069, MC25TESI0098). The clinicopathological characteristics of the 10 patients, including age, sex, etiology, background liver disease, tumor parameters (size and multiplicity), AJCC TNM stage, grade, and AFP levels, are summarized in Table S6.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and calculation of distal polyadenylation site usage (dPAS usage)

Total RNA was isolated from cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. For synthesis of cDNAs specific to mRNAs, Tetro cDNA synthesis kit (Bioline, London, UK) was used. qRT-PCR was performed with a GoTaq® qPCR Master Mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). To normalize differences in the amount of total cDNA added to each reaction; glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) for mRNA were used as the endogenous control. The following was calculated using primer sets designed in the common part of the isoforms of a transcript (called “common primer set”) as well as in the distal part of long isoform of the same transcripts (called “distal primer set”): ΔCt (common and distal) was normalized to the levels of GAPDH, where ΔCt (common or distal) = ΔCt (common or distal)–ΔCt GAPDH. ΔΔCt = ΔCt distal-ΔCt common. To show fold changes normalized to the control siRNA-transfected samples, the following equation was used: normalized ΔΔΔCt =ΔΔCt target siRNA-ΔΔCt control siRNA. Then the increase or decrease in dPAS usage was calculated as 2 normalized ΔΔΔCt. All primers are listed in Table S8.

3′ rapid amplification of cDNA ends (3′ RACE) analysis

3′-RACE analysis was performed to assess the 3′-end of the indicated mRNA sequence. Total RNA was isolated from cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA was reverse transcribed using the primer that contains oligo(dT)-containing adapter primer provided in the Genracer™ kit (Invitrogen). 5′-GCTGTCAACGATACGCTACGTAACGGCATGACAGTG(T)24-3′, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primers specific for both the proximal and distal poly(A) tails of the GPC3 3′UTR were designed and mixed with the reverse primer. The primer mixture, cDNA and Taq polymerase were then combined to perform the PCR reactions under standard conditions. PCR was performed with nTaq Tenuto DNA polymerase (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). The PCR products were resolved on a 2.0% agarose gel and visualized using a UVP Gelsolo Imager (analytikjena, Jena, Germany). All primers are listed in Table S8.

Statistical analysis

Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier product limit method, and significant differences between survival curves were determined using the log-rank test. All experiments were performed at least three times, and all samples were analyzed in triplicate. No samples or animals were excluded from the analysis. Blinding was not performed during the in vitro experiments, human tissue sample analyses, or the allograft assay. Variance was not formally tested for all datasets. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical differences between groups were evaluated by unpaired two-tailed Student t test using GraphPad™ 8.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA). P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Integrative APA analysis identifies GPC3 3′UTR shortening as a clinically relevant event in HCC

To investigate the prevalence of APA events in HCC, we applied the DaPars algorithm to analyze APA profiles of the multistage human liver cancer (Catholic_mLIHC) dataset (Fig. S1A). A widespread shift toward proximal polyadenylation site usage was observed in tumor samples, indicating a global trend of 3′UTR shortening (Fig. 1A). We subsequently performed differential PDUI analysis across the transcriptome and identified 1147 genes with significant alterations in APA usage. Among these, 53 genes exhibited more than a 2-fold change in APA pattern, as determined by comparison of PDUI values between normal and tumor tissues (Fig. 1B). To prioritize candidates potentially affected by APA-associated post-transcriptional regulation, we integrated differential APA usage with corresponding mRNA expression changes. This analysis identified genes in which altered 3′UTR isoform usage was accompanied by altered transcript abundance. Notably, among the identified candidates, GPC3 emerged as one of the top-ranked APA-regulated genes, characterized by consistent 3′UTR shortening and high expression levels across multiple independent HCC cohorts, including TCGA_LIHC, ICGC_LIRI, and GSE77314 datasets (Table S1). In accordance with our observations in Catholic_mLIHC, all datasets consistently demonstrated elevated expression of GPC3 in HCC tissues compared to corresponding normal. Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) analysis further confirmed the alteration in polyadenylation site usage of GPC3 mRNA, revealing predominant usage of proximal polyadenylation sites in HCC (Fig. 1C). We then further analyzed the association between APA events and GPC3 expression levels in HCC. Our analysis revealed that the presence of GPC3 mRNA isoforms with shortened 3′UTRs was associated with increased transcript abundance. Consistently, decreased PDUI values correlated with elevated GPC3 expression across the spectrum from normal liver tissue to precancerous lesions and progressive stages of HCC. (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, patients exhibiting high GPC3 expression demonstrated significantly reduced overall survival, suggesting that 3′UTR shortening of GPC3 mRNA contributes to its overexpression and is strongly associated with poor clinical prognosis (Fig. 1E). In addition, paired sample analyses from the TCGA_LIHC and ICGC_LIRI cohorts revealed consistently higher GPC3 expression in HCC compared to non-tumorous tissues (Fig. S1B). We next used the UCSC Genome Browser to define the GPC3 transcript structure and APA sites underlying the short and long 3′UTR isoforms. APA-mediated overexpression of GPC3 promotes HCC progression

Given the APA-mediated shortening of the 3′UTR of GPC3 observed in HCC, we next investigated the biological impact of its upregulated expression at both mRNA and protein levels. Western blot analysis of paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues from HCC patients consistently revealed elevated GPC3 protein levels in tumor samples (Fig. 2A). To further characterize the APA regulation of GPC3, we assessed the usage dPAS as a surrogate marker for APA dynamics. Tumor tissues with high GPC3 expression exhibited significantly reduced dPAS usage, indicative of a preferential shift toward proximal polyadenylation site selection and consequent production of shorter 3′UTR isoforms (Fig. 2B). We further assessed GPC3 protein expression across a panel of HCC and normal liver-derived cell lines. Immunoblot analysis using membrane protein fractionations demonstrated that GPC3 expression was significantly higher in Hep3B and Huh7 cells compared to non-tumorigenic MIHA and the relatively low GPC3-expressing HCC cell lines, PLC/PRF/5 and SNU-761 (Fig. S1C). To elucidate the functional role of GPC3 in tumorigenesis, we performed loss-of-function studies using siRNA-mediated knockdown of GPC3 in Huh7 and Hep3B cells. Knockdown efficiency was validated by immunoblotting, followed by proliferation assays (MTT and BrdU), which revealed a significant reduction in cell viability and proliferative capacity upon GPC3 silencing (Figs. 2C and S2A). Moreover, flow cytometry analysis of Annexin V-stained cells demonstrated increased apoptotic cell death following GPC3 depletion, accompanied by enhanced cleavage of caspase-3 and PARP (Figs. 2D and S2B). Conversely, ectopic overexpression of GPC3 in MIHA and SNU-761 cells resulted in enhanced cellular proliferation and a decrease in apoptotic cell population (Figs. 2E, F and S2C, D).

To further examine the in vivo relevance of GPC3, we performed an allograft assay using Hepa1-6 cells transfected with si-NC or si-Gpc3. Tumors from si-Gpc3-transfected cells showed a decreasing trend in tumor burden compared with the si-NC group, although the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. S2E).

CSTF2-driven APA regulates GPC3 expression and promotes HCC

Given the pivotal role of APA in modulating GPC3 expression and its apparent influence on HCC pathogenesis, we sought to identify upstream regulatory factors that drive APA-mediated 3′UTR shortening of GPC3. To this end, we profiled the expression patterns of 46 established APA regulators across multiple independent HCC cohorts, including Catholic_mLIHC, TCGA_LIHC, ICGC_LIRI, and GSE77314. Among the candidates, PABPC1, CPSF6, CSTF2, and CDC73 displayed consistently elevated expression in tumor tissues relative to matched normal counterparts (Table S2). We then performed siRNA-mediated functional screening of these candidates in HCC cell lines. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed, and among the tested regulators, only CSTF2 depletion markedly reduced GPC3 protein expression (Figs. 3A and S3A). To further delineate this result, we performed correlation analyses between CSTF2 and GPC3 expression levels, as well as CSTF2 and GPC3 percentage of distal PDUI. CSTF2 expression positively correlated with GPC3 expression and an inverse association with PDUI values, supporting a potential link between CSTF2-mediated APA and 3′UTR shortening of GPC3 (Fig. 3B, C). Notably, Kaplan-Meier survival analyses across multiple cohorts revealed that elevated CSTF2 expression is significantly associated with reduced overall survival in HCC patients (Figs. 3D and S3B). Next, to experimentally validate CSTF2 overexpression in clinical specimens, we performed western blotting and qRT-PCR on paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues. To determine whether CSTF2 physically associates with GPC3 mRNA, we performed RIP assay using an anti-CSTF2 antibody. GPC3 mRNA was significantly enriched in the CSTF2 RIP fraction compared with the IgG control, indicating an association between CSTF2 and GPC3 mRNA (Fig. 4C). Luciferase reporter assays further showed that CSTF2 knockdown reduced wild-type GPC3 3′UTR reporter activity, whereas mutation of either the proximal PAS-like motif or downstream U-rich element abolished this response (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that proximal cis-elements within the GPC3 3′UTR are required for CSTF2-dependent regulation. We next examined whether GPC3 3′UTR shortening affects mRNA stability, we performed actinomycin D chase assays in parental HCC cells (Fig. 4E). CSTF2-mediated 3′UTR shortening of GPC3 evades miR-96-5p and miR-140-5p-mediated repression in HCC

The 3′UTRs of mRNAs are enriched with regulatory elements, including miRNA binding sites that modulate transcript stability and translation. Consequently, APA-driven shortening of 3′UTRs can significantly alter post-transcriptional regulation. Based on this, we hypothesized that APA-mediated 3′UTR shortening of GPC3 enables the transcript to escape miRNA-mediated repression, thereby contributing to its oncogenic upregulation in HCC.

To investigate this, we utilized three independent miRNA target prediction platforms—TargetScan, miRDB, and miRabel—to identify miRNAs with putative binding sites located exclusively within the distal (long) region of the GPC3 3′UTR (Fig. S5A). This integrative analysis identified 40 candidate miRNAs potentially targeting the distal portion of GPC3’s 3′UTR (Table S3). To refine this list to physiologically relevant candidates, we evaluated their expression levels in tumor and adjacent non-tumor liver tissues, and selected six miRNAs (miR-96-5p, miR-140-5p, miR-204-5p, miR-450b-5p, miR-1271-5p, and miR-6842-3p) that are actually expressed in normal conditions and exhibited expression levels above 1.0 RPM in both tissue types. To determine whether these miRNAs directly repress GPC3 via its 3′UTR, we co-transfected HCC cells with either long or short GPC3 3′UTR reporter constructs, along with synthetic mimics of the six candidate miRNAs. Immunoblot analysis revealed that only miR-96-5p and miR-140-5p significantly reduced GPC3 protein expression in cells expressing the long 3′UTR isoform, whereas no effect was observed in cells expressing the short isoform (Fig. 6A). We therefore examined whether these miRNAs differentially regulate GPC3 according to 3′UTR isoform status. HCC cells were then transfected with miR-96-5p or miR-140-5p mimics to assess their effects on GPC3 expression and cellular phenotypes. Together, these findings define a mechanistic axis in which CSTF2 promotes APA-mediated 3′UTR shortening of GPC3, enabling escape from miRNA-mediated repression by miR-96-5p and miR-140-5p in HCC (Fig. 7E). Discussion

GPC3 is a well-characterized oncofetal protein that is highly upregulated in HCC and has been widely investigated as a biomarker and therapeutic target [20]. However, the mechanisms underlying its overexpression in HCC remain incompletely understood. Although several epigenetic and transcriptional modulators have been proposed, a direct post-transcriptional mechanism has not been clearly established. A prominent hallmark of cancer transcriptomes is widespread 3′UTR shortening, which frequently enhances oncogene expression by removing tumor-suppressive regulatory elements, particularly miRNA-binding sites [21]. While this phenomenon has been reported in various cancers, its contribution to HCC has remained poorly defined [19]. Through integrative analyses of multiple independent HCC cohorts, we reveal that most APA events during HCC progression involve 3′UTR shortening and identify GPC3 as one of the most significantly upregulated genes affected by this process. We show that shortened GPC3 transcripts are associated with increased mRNA abundance, high protein expression, and poor clinical outcomes, underscoring the functional and prognostic relevance of this APA-mediated regulation (Figs. 1 and 2).

While APA dysregulation has been implicated in various cancers, most prior studies have focused on factors such as CPSF6 and NUDT21 in tumor types like lung adenocarcinoma and pancreatic cancer [22, 23]. The role of CSTF2 in HCC, however, has not been explored in depth. CSTF2 expression is elevated in HCC and correlates with both shortened GPC3 3′UTRs and poor patient survival (Figs. 3 and S4A). Gain- and loss-of-function studies further confirm that CSTF2 modulates GPC3 3′UTR isoform patterns, with CSTF2 depletion increasing the long isoform and CSTF2 overexpression promoting the shortened isoform (Fig. 4A, B). Additional mechanistic analyses suggest that CSTF2-dependent GPC3 3′UTR shortening contributes to increased GPC3 mRNA stability (Fig. 4C–E). CSTF2 modulation also altered cell proliferation and apoptosis in both HCC and non-malignant hepatic cells (Fig. 5).Importantly, CSTF2-dependent oncogenic effects are abolished upon GPC3 knockdown, while GPC3 re-expression rescues the growth defects caused by CSTF2 depletion, suggesting a direct regulatory link between CSTF2 and GPC3 in driving HCC pathogenesis (Fig. 7A–D). Thus, we identify CSTF2 as a key APA regulator that drives GPC3 3′UTR shortening in HCC.

miRNAs are known to bind to the 3′UTRs of their target transcripts and suppress translation or induce degradation [24]. Such an APA-dependent escape from miRNA regulation has been increasingly recognized as a hallmark of transcriptomic dysregulation in cancer and further reinforces the central role of APA in post-transcriptional control of oncogenes [25]. Notably, these regulatory sites are often located in the distal 3′UTR region and are frequently lost through APA [26]. At the post-transcriptional level, we show that 3′UTR shortening enables GPC3 to evade repression by miR-96-5p and miR-140-5p, two miRNAs whose binding sites are located within the distal portion of the GPC3 3′UTR. Functional assays confirm that these miRNAs suppress GPC3 expression and cell proliferation only when the full-length 3′UTR is present (Fig. 6). Thus, APA-driven loss of miRNA binding capacity emerges as a key mechanism by which HCC cells achieve sustained GPC3 expression.

Interestingly, both miR-96-5p and miR-140-5p have previously been reported as tumor suppressive miRNAs across multiple malignancies, including HCC [27, 28]. miR-96-5p is known to negatively regulate cell proliferation and invasion through targeting multiple oncogenic transcripts, while miR-140-5p has been implicated in suppressing epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and tumor progression [29, 30]. In our study, we show that miR-96-5p and miR-140-5p effectively suppress GPC3 expression and inhibit HCC cell proliferation only when the full-length GPC3 3′UTR is intact (Fig. 6F, G). These findings not only highlight the functional importance of these miRNAs in controlling GPC3 expression but also underscore how APA serves as a mechanism of miRNA evasion in HCC.

While our findings reveal a compelling mechanism of GPC3 upregulation in HCC, several important questions remain. A limitation of this study is that the direct in vivo contribution of CSTF2-mediated GPC3 3′UTR shortening was not fully demonstrated using an isoform-specific animal model. Future studies using stable CSTF2 gain- or loss-of-function models with isoform-specific quantification of GPC3 polyadenylation site usage will be needed to define the precise contribution of CSTF2-driven GPC3 APA remodeling to HCC progression in vivo. In addition, although CSTF2 appears to be a dominant APA regulator of GPC3, additional RNA-binding proteins, transcription factors, or epigenetic modulators likely contribute to APA activity in a context-dependent or tumor stage-specific manner. Moreover, while GPC3 has already been extensively studied as a diagnostic biomarker and immunotherapy target in HCC [31]. Our data raise the novel possibility that APA-regulated GPC3 isoforms may offer isoform-specific targeting strategies. For example, antisense oligonucleotides designed to modulate APA events, miRNA mimics to restore tumor-suppressive regulation, or small-molecule inhibitors that target the APA machinery could selectively affect oncogenic GPC3 isoforms while sparing normal tissue expression.

Several GPC3-targeted immunotherapeutic approaches, including monoclonal antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates, and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies, are currently under clinical evaluation [32, 33]. However, most of these strategies focus on targeting GPC3 at the protein level on the cell surface. Our findings introduce an additional mechanistic rationale for targeting GPC3 indirectly at the RNA-processing level. Targeting CSTF2 may suppress tumor GPC3 expression and offer an alternative approach for tumors resistant to GPC3-targeted therapies or with heterogeneous GPC3 expression.

In conclusion, our study defines a novel oncogenic mechanism in which CSTF2 promotes 3′UTR shortening of GPC3, leading to miRNA evasion and enhanced tumorigenicity in HCC. These findings expand our understanding of post-transcriptional gene regulation in HCC and suggest that targeting APA machinery and miRNA-mediated pathways may represent promising and innovative strategies for therapeutic intervention in HCC. Further exploration of the CSTF2–GPC3–miRNA axis holds considerable potential to inform the next generation of biomarker development and targeted therapies for patients with HCC.
