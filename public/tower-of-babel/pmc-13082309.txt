Addressing equity and validity in faculty career advancement
Authors: Center for Excellence in Faculty Advancement, Juan M. Madera, Christiane Spitzmueller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A framework to move the promotion and tenure process forward, emphasizing more equitable approaches and outcomes.

Promotion and tenure (P&T) processes are core elements of the scientific enterprise. Recent evidence demonstrates that extraneous factors influence who gets promoted (e.g., letter writer gender, candidate race, and tenure-clock extensions), threatening the integrity of the P&T process and the fair advancement of the most meritorious contributors to science (1, 2). This highlights the need for a system overhaul toward more valid and equitable processes and outcomes. Using practices and policies developed in a convening of nationally recognized faculty career experts, along with findings from a large consortium dataset comprising nearly 2000 P&T candidates and 10,000 external review letters (ERLs), we outline pathways to advance P&T equity and validity through a Structure, Empowerment, and Transparency (SET) framework for three critical P&T stakeholder groups: (i) P&T decision-makers, (ii) candidates, and (iii) ERL writers.

DECISION-MAKERS

Faculty affairs administrators guide policy development and adherence, while P&T committees decide who will ultimately earn tenure. However, autonomy and variability regarding norms within a loosely coupled university system, anonymous committee voting without decision rationale, and a lack of structure for interpreting diverse applicants contribute to inconsistent decisions. Committees need to be trained on specific required actions that facilitate uniform evaluation processes, provide a rationale for their decisions that is transparent to equity advisors, and understand how P&T criteria are applied appropriately or, at the very least, consistently.

In addition, the composition of the P&T committee should be publicly available and diversity-balanced in terms of both discipline and lived experiences. Ensuring committee diversity promotes a consideration of potential candidate disadvantages such as service taxation (e.g., higher levels of “invisible” service), bias in teaching evaluations, and the undervaluation of interdisciplinary scholarship and innovation and entrepreneurship activities and publications. Furthermore, in our consortium dataset, faculty who used tenure clock extensions received more negative committee votes, disproportionately affecting women, who are more likely to take extensions. Institutions should incorporate policies to protect candidates from being penalized for using university-approved extensions and leaves.

Finally, underrepresented minority (URM; i.e., Latinx, Black) faculty, especially Black women, face harsher P&T committee evaluations than non-URM candidates, with productivity metrics in particular judged more harshly (2). This is pronounced for unanimous votes, as, at the college-level, URM received 7% more negative votes and were 44% less likely to receive a unanimous vote. These results suggest that the premium institutions place on unanimous committee voting as a “gold standard” should be reduced, given the impact of such votes on the process and the tendency for URM faculty to receive fewer unanimous votes. Furthermore, research has found that these racial disparities were mitigated when URM faculty were evaluated simultaneously with other promotion candidates, termed joint evaluation (3). This highlights the role altering decision environments can play in addressing racial disparities and suggests that, where possible, P&T candidates should be evaluated jointly, either with a candidate going up that year or compared to a candidate’s portfolio materials from a previous year.

CANDIDATES

At many institutions, a lack of P&T portfolio standardization and access to mentorship and networks can disadvantage P&T candidates, particularly those from minority backgrounds and those with nontraditional portfolios (e.g., interdisciplinary researchers). To level the playing field, departments and colleges may revise portfolio structures and communicate concise instructions and formal templates with defined performance indicators. Furthermore, universities can provide candidates with access to information about P&T processes, the opportunity to review committee reports before voting occurs, and formal mechanisms to rebut misrepresented information. In addition, institutions should create a formal mentorship constellation that connects all candidates with well-trained mentors and individual development plans (IDPs) when they begin working at the institution.

ERL WRITERS

Committees rely heavily on ERLs in their appraisal of a candidate. Unfortunately, the ERL writer selection processes and rationale are rarely tracked and vary across institutions and candidates (3). Similar to the composition of P&T committees, ERL writers are disproportionately senior scholars and more likely to be male and white, which relates to less comprehensive evaluations of diverse candidates. The characteristics of ERL writers relate to differences in ERL language (4) and P&T voting outcomes (5). In fact, evidence from our consortium dataset shows that letters are oftentimes more reflective of the letter writer (e.g., gender, institution, etc.) than the candidate (4) and that candidates who have more letters written by women are more likely to receive a promotion as ERLs written by women use a more positive tone and have less doubt language (5). As such, institutions should have consistent rules regarding the number of individuals that come from lists that candidates generate, and support candidates in developing such lists, as well as how many letters come from writers with certain characteristics (e.g., h-index, institution type/rank, and gender). Furthermore, our preliminary evidence suggests that using structured solicitations mitigates disparities found between those who do and do not take tenure extensions; also, a greater ERL focus on research helps to diminish shifting standards for URM women (2). We recommend using structured solicitations and templates that solicit specific and relevant criteria and outline the content that should be omitted.

A CALL TO POLICY REFORM

Three keys to reforming P&T processes that will set faculty up for more equitable success are woven into our recommendations:

1) Structure processes to reduce inequities in what information candidates provide, what prompts ERL writers respond to, and how committees evaluate candidates (e.g., joint evaluation).

2) Empower candidates by providing them with routine, formal mentoring, formal mechanisms for vetoing ERL writers and committee members, and the option of submitting responses to committee decisions.

3) Transparency should pervade the P&T process, including equal access to the “hidden curriculum” and specific case-by-case details such as committee composition and rationale.

Given emerging evidence on bias and mechanisms for building equity in P&T decisions, now is the time for continued discourse, further experimental and field research to elucidate barriers and interventions to support equity and validity, and evidence-based reform.
