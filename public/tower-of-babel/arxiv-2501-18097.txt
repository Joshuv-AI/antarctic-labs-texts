On the universal approximation of real functions with varying domain
W. Jung, C.A. Morales, L.T.T. Tran

Abstract
. We establish sufficient conditions for the density of shallow neural networks [ 5 ] on the family of continuous real functions defined on a compact metric space, taking into account variations in the function domains. For this we use the Gromov-Hausdorff distance defined in [ 4 ] .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 41A65; Secondary 54C2

1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to find sufficient conditions for the shallow neural network generated by a
family of real-valued continuous functions on compact metric spaces to satisfy a “universal approximation property”. We will not assume that the domains of the functions coincide.
To make this precise, we use the Gromov-Hausdorff distance on the space of compact metric spaces
[
3
]
and its related distance for functions
[
4
]
.
Our main result (Theorem
2
) provides the desired sufficient conditions. A concrete corollary (Corollary
3
) asserts that the shallow neural network generated by functions defined on finite metric spaces is dense in the space of all continuous functions on compact metric spaces.
This is new and stronger than the well-known fact that the finite metric spaces are dense in the space of compact metric spaces endowed with the Gromov-Hausdorff metric
[
3
]
(see for instance
[
8
]
or the proof of Example
9
).

A potential application of our result is in the representation of shapes by point clouds.
This is because finite spaces can be used to approximate objects with the
Gromov-Hausdorff distance measuring the dissimilarity of these objects
(see for instance Section 1 in
[
4
]
).
Then, real-valued functions over finite spaces can therefore represent some shape data to be measured.
Let us state our results in a precise way.

Let
X
be a metric space.
Denote

\mathcal{C}(X)
the set of continuous maps
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
. This is a
Banach algebra under the usual operations and the supremum norm

\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)|.

Define the
C^{0}
-distance

d_{C^{0}}(f,g)=\|f-g\|_{\infty}
, for all
f,g\in\mathcal{C}(X).

An
activation function
is a
continuous function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
It is of
sigmoidal type
if

\lim_{t\to-\infty}\sigma(t)=0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\sigma(t)=1.

Following the standard terminology of neural networks with a single hidden layer we introduce the definition below.

Definition 1
.

The shallow neural network
N_{\sigma}(F)
generated by an activation function
\sigma
and
F\subset\mathcal{C}(X)

is
\emptyset
(if
F=\emptyset
) or the collection of functions
g:X\to\mathbb{R}
of the form

g(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\sigma(f_{j}(x)+\theta_{j})\quad\quad(\forall x\in X)

where
N
,
a_{1},\cdots,a_{N},\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{N}
and

f_{1},\cdots,f_{N}
run over
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{R}
, and
F
respectively.

Cybenko
[
5
]
proved that, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
the shallow neural network generated by an activation function of sigmoidal type and the dual of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
on the
n
-dimensional cube
[0,1]^{n}
is dense in
C([0,1]^{n})
w.r.t the
d_{C^{0}}
-distance.
Our goal is to generalize this result by allowing the domain of functions to vary in the Gromov-Hausdorff space.

To make this precise, let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of all compact metric spaces.
Define

\mathcal{C}=\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{C}(X).

Let
D:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{M}
be the
domain map
defined by

D(f)=X\quad\iff\quad X\mbox{ is the domain of }f.

Given
X,Y\in\mathcal{M}
and
\epsilon>0
we say that

i:X\to Y
is an
\epsilon
-isometry
if

•

|d(i(x),i(x^{\prime})-d(x,x^{\prime})|\leq\epsilon
(
\forall x,x^{\prime}\in X
);

•

For every
y\in Y
there is
x\in X
such that

d(i(x),y)\leq\epsilon
.

The
Gromov-Hausdorff distance
in
\mathcal{M}
is defined by

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\inf\{\epsilon>0:\exists\mbox{ $\epsilon$-isometry from $X$ to $Y$ and vice versa}\}.

The
C^{0}
-Gromov-Hausdorff distance
in
\mathcal{C}
is defined by

\displaystyle d_{GH^{0}}(f,g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\{\epsilon>0:\exists\mbox{ $\epsilon$-isometries $i:D(f)\to D(g)$}

\displaystyle\mbox{ and $j:D(g)\to D(f)$ s.t. }\|g\circ i-f\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\|g-f\circ j\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon\}.

(The latter is not the original definition
[
4
]
but an equivalent one based on the approach in
[
1
]
).
Both distances generate topologies induced by the open balls.
For brevity, we say that a
given collection of subsets of
\mathcal{C}
is
dense in
\mathcal{C}

if it is dense in
\mathcal{C}
with respect to
d_{GH^{0}}
.

For every
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{C}
we define

\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{C}
by

\mathcal{F}(X)=\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{C}(X)\quad\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{M}.

Definition 2
.

The shallow neural network generated by an activation function
\sigma
and
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{C}

is defined by

\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{F})=\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{M}}N_{\sigma}(\mathcal{F}(X)).

The main problem is the following one.

Problem 1
.

Find sufficient conditions for the shallow neural network generated by an activation function
\sigma
and a collection
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{C}
to be dense in
\mathcal{C}
.

To attack this problem we first we note that both
D(\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{F}))=D(\mathcal{F})

and
\mathcal{F}\subset D^{-1}(D(\mathcal{F}))
.
Then, for
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{F})
to be dense in
\mathcal{C}
it is necessary that

(1)

D^{-1}(D(\mathcal{F}))\text{ is dense in }\mathcal{C}

Clearly, this condition is insufficient (see the next section).
Second, let
B(X)
be a set of regular signed Borel measures of
X\in\mathcal{M}
.
Given a measurable map
\varphi:X\to Y
where
Y\in\mathcal{M}
, we define the pushforward
\varphi:B(X)\to B(Y)
by
\varphi_{*}(\mu)=\mu\circ\varphi^{-1}
for
\mu\in B(X)
.
The following definition can be found in p. 112 of
[
6
]
.

Definition 3
.

Given
X\in\mathcal{M}
, we say that
F\subseteq\mathcal{C}(X)
separates
B(X)
if

\mu\in B(X)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad f_{*}(\mu)=0\>\>(\forall f\in F)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mu=0.

Next, we introduce some basic terminology about fibrewise sets (see
[
7
]
for details). A
fibrewise space
over a topological space
M
consists of a topological space
C
together with a continuous function
D:C\to M
, called the projection. For each
x\in M
the fibre over
x
is the subset
M_{x}=D^{-1}(x)
of
C
;
fibres may be empty since
D
is not required to be surjective.
When the fibres have a similar structure, the resulting space is termed a fibre bundle, emphasizing the compatibility of the fibres and the continuity of their transitions over
M
.
We will use the following definition.

Definition 4
.

A fibrewise set
C
over
M
is locally sliceable if for every
x\in M
,
f\in M_{x}
and every neighborhood
U
of
f
in
C
there is a neighborhood
W
of
x
in
M
such that
M_{x^{\prime}}\cap U\neq\emptyset
for every
x^{\prime}\in W
.

This definition is weaker but closely related to the one with the same name in Definition 1.16 of
[
7
]
.
The fibrewise sets we are going to deal with are those with total space within
\mathcal{C}
, base space within
\mathcal{M}
and projection giving by the restriction of
D
.
About them we have the following definition.

Definition 5
.

We say that
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{C}
is locally sliceable
if the fibrewise subset of
\mathcal{C}
with total space
D^{-1}(D(\mathcal{F}))
and base
D(\mathcal{F})
is locally sliceable.

We shall present examples related to the above definitions in the next section.
Now, we can state our main result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
a locally sliceable subset of
\mathcal{C}
satisfying (
1
) and
\sigma
be an activation function of sigmoidal type. If

\{X\in D(\mathcal{F}):\mathcal{F}(X)\mbox{ separates }B(X)\mbox{ and }\mathbb{R}\mathcal{F}(X)\subset\mathcal{F}(X)\}

is dense in
D(\mathcal{F})
,
then the shallow neural network generated by
\sigma
and

\mathcal{F}
is dense in
\mathcal{C}
.

An anonymous referee asked if the locally sliceable condition can be removed from this theorem.
However, our methods depend heavily on this condition so we do not know if it can be removed or not.

A short application of this result is as follows.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{fin}
be the family of all real-valued functions defined on finite metric spaces.
In Example
9
we will show that
\mathcal{F}_{fin}
satisfies the conditions this theorem. Then, the following corollary holds.

Corollary 3
.

The shallow neural network generated by an activation function of sigmoidal type
and
\mathcal{F}_{fin}
is dense in
\mathcal{C}
.

The remainder of the paper is divided as follows.
In Section
2
, we give some examples, properties of
\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}(\mathcal{F})
and provide families separating signed Borel measures.
In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
2

by extending Cybenko methods
[
5
]
to the varying domain case.

The authors would like to thank the Vietnam Institute for Advanced Study in Mathematics (VIASM) for their hospitality and financial support.

.

.

I_{n}=[0,1]\times\overset{(n)}{\cdots}\times[0,1]
.
The set of linear operators from
\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{R}^{n}

.

.

If

and

be the dual of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

.

Con\subset\mathcal{C}

Example 3
.

Since

, we have
\sigma\circ Con=Con
.

The following example shows that the condition (
1
) is not sufficient to guarantee the density of
\sigma(\mathcal{F})
on
\mathcal{C}
.

Example 4
.

The collection of all zero maps
\mathcal{F}=\{0:X\to\mathbb{R}\}_{X\in\mathcal{M}}
satisfies (

), but
N_{\sigma}(\mathcal{F})

is not necessarily dense in
\mathcal{C}
for it consists of constant maps
(by Example
3
).

Given
F\subset\mathcal{C}(X)
for some
X\in\mathcal{M}
, we observe that
N_{\sigma}(F)
is a subspace but not necessarily a subalgebra of
C(X)
. Therefore, the Stone-Weierstrass theorem may not apply to determine when
N_{\sigma}(F)
is dense in
C(X)
. For instance, we can consider the conditions under which
Con(X)\subseteq N_{\sigma}(F)
. Below, we provide a very satisfactory answer.

Remark 1
.

If
F\cap Con(X)\neq\emptyset
, then
Con(X)\subseteq N_{\sigma}(F).

Next, we consider the problem when

\sigma(F)
separates points.

Remark 2
.

If
\sigma
is injective and
F
separate points of
X
, then
N_{\sigma}(F)
separates points as well.

Proof.

Pick
x\neq y\in X
and take
f\in F
such that
f(x)\neq f(y)
. Take
N=1,a_{1}=1
and
\theta=0
, thus
g=\sigma\circ f\in\sigma(F)
and
g(x)\neq g(y)
.
∎

Next we consider the problem when the
N_{\sigma}(F)
is an algebra of
\mathcal{C}(X)
.

Example 5
.

Suppose there is a real number
A\neq 0
such that

(2)

\sigma(t)\cdot\sigma(s)=A\sigma(t\cdot s)\>\>{\text{f}or\>all}\>\>t,s\in\mathbb{R}.

If
F
is an algebra of
\mathcal{C}(X)
, then so is
N_{\sigma}(F)
.

Proof.

Given
N,M\in\mathbb{N}
, for every
a_{i},b_{j},\theta_{i},\check{\theta}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f_{i},\check{f_{i}}\in F
,

\displaystyle\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\sigma(f_{i}(x)+\theta_{i})\Bigg]\cdot\Bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{M}b_{j}\sigma(\check{f}_{j}(x)+\check{\theta}_{j})\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{i}b_{j}[\sigma(f_{i}(x)+\theta_{i})\cdot\sigma(\check{f}_{j}(x)+\check{\theta}_{j})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{i}b_{j}A\sigma[(f_{i}(x)+\theta_{i})\cdot(\check{f}_{j}(x)+\check{\theta}_{j})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{i}b_{j}A\sigma[(f_{i}\cdot\check{f}_{j})(x)+\check{\theta}_{j}f_{i}(x)+\theta_{i}\check{f}_{j}(x)+\theta_{i}\check{\theta}_{j}].

Since
F
is an algebra, we have

(f_{i}\cdot\check{f}_{j})+\check{\theta}_{j}f_{i}+\theta_{i}\check{f}_{j}\in F
(for all
i=1,\cdots,N
and
j=1,\cdots M
)
hence
N_{\sigma}(F)
is an algebra.
∎

\sigma
satisfying

) are:

(1)

\sigma(t)=a|t|
,

(2)

\sigma(t)=a\cdot t^{p}

(3)

Example 6
.

We say that
F\subseteq C(X)
is a Stone-Weierstrass family if it is an algebra containing a non-zero constant function and separating points
(hence they are dense in
C(X)
).
If
\sigma
is injective and satisfies (

) and

N_{\sigma}(F)
is also a Stone-Weierstrass family.

Let us give some examples of families separating the signed Borel measures.

Example 7
.

Let
X=I_{n}
and
F=\{L|_{X}:L\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}\}
be the dual of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
restricted to
X
. Then,
F
separates
B(X)
.

Proof.

(See
[
5
]
).
Let
\mu\in B(X)
such that
f_{*}(\mu)=0
for all
f\in F
. Then, for every bounded measurable
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\int_{I_{n}}h(\langle y,x\rangle)d\mu=0,\>\text{for all}\>x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In particular, for
h(u)=sin(u)
and
h(u)=cos(u)
one has

\int_{I_{n}}e^{\sqrt{-1}\langle m,x\rangle}d\mu=\int_{I_{n}}cos(\langle m,x\rangle)d\mu+\sqrt{-1}\int_{I_{n}}sin(\langle m,x\rangle)d\mu=0

for all
m\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with integer entries. Then, the Fourier transform of
\mu
is
0
and so
\mu=0
as well. Therefore,
F
separates
B(X)
.
∎

Example 8
.

Let
X
be a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
.
If
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
and

f(x)=x
for every

, then
F=\{f\}

.

Proof.

Just note that
f_{*}(\mu)=\mu
for all

, then the conclusion follows.

Now, we give an example of a family satisfying the hypotheses of our main theorem.

Example 9
.

The family
\mathcal{F}_{fin}=\{f\in\mathcal{C}:D(f)\mbox{ is finite}\}
satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
2
.

Proof.

Since
D(\mathcal{F}_{fin})
is the set of finite metric spaces
which dense in
\mathcal{M}

(see Exercice 7.4.9 in
[
3
]
),
D(\mathcal{F}_{fin})
is dense in
\mathcal{M}
.
Therefore, (
1
) holds.

Now take
\hat{f}\in D^{-1}(D(\mathcal{F}_{fin}))
and
\Delta>0
.
Choose
0<\rho<\Delta
such that

d(a,b)<\rho\quad(a,b\in D(\hat{f}))\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|\hat{f}(a)-\hat{f}(b)|<\Delta.

Suppose
\hat{X}\in D(\mathcal{F}_{fin})
satisfies

d_{GH}(\hat{X},D(\hat{f}))<\frac{\rho}{4}.

Then, there is a
\frac{\rho}{4}
-isometry
i:\hat{X}\to D(\hat{f})
.
By Lemma 1.6 in
[
9
]
there is a
\frac{3\rho}{4}
-isometry
j:D(\hat{f})\to\hat{X}
such that

d_{C^{0}}(i\circ j,id_{D(\hat{f})})<\frac{\rho}{4},

where
id_{Z}
denotes the identity of
Z\in\mathcal{M}
.
Define

\check{f}=\hat{f}\circ i.

Then,

\|\check{f}-\hat{f}\circ i\|_{\infty}=0<\Delta.

Moreover,

|\check{f}\circ j(x)-\hat{f}(x)\|_{\infty}=|\hat{f}\circ i\circ j(x)-\hat{f}(x)|=|\hat{f}(a)-\hat{f}(b)|<\Delta

for
a=i\circ j(x)
and
b=x
satisfies
d(a,b)<\rho
.
Then,

\|\check{f}\circ j-\hat{f}\|_{\infty}<\Delta.

Since both
i
and
j
are
\delta
-isometries we get

d_{GH^{0}}(\check{f},\hat{f})<\Delta.

Therefore,
D(\mathcal{F}_{fin})
(and so
\mathcal{F}_{fin}
) are locally sliceable.

Finally, we observe that
if
X\in D(\mathcal{F}_{fin})
, then
X
is finite so

\mathcal{F}_{fin}(X)=\mathcal{C}(X)
thus

\mathcal{F}_{fin}(X)
separates
B(X)
and satisfies

\mathbb{R}\mathcal{F}_{fin}(X)\subset\mathcal{F}_{fin}(X)
for all
X\in D(\mathcal{F}_{fin})
.
This proves the result.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
2

First we prove the following result.

Proposition 4
.

Let
X
be a compact metric space, and
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be an activation function of sigmoidal type. If
F\subseteq\mathcal{C}(X)
separates
B(X)

and
\mathbb{R}F\subset F
, then
N_{\sigma}(F)
is dense in
\mathcal{C}(X)
with respect to the distance
d_{C^{0}}
(or
C^{0}
-dense for short).

Observe the disparity between this statement and the findings in
[
2
]
, where the assumption entails
\sigma
being non-polynomial and
F
separate points to establish the density of
N_{\sigma}(F)
’s
span
in
C(X)
.

The proof this proposition is based on the methods by Cybenko
[
5
]
. We just need some preliminaries. The first one is the definition of discriminatory
\sigma
, motivated by
[
5
]
.

Hereafter
X
is a compact metric space,
\sigma
is an activation function, and
F\subseteq\mathcal{C}(X)
.

Definition 6
.

We say
\sigma
is discriminatory with respect to
F
if

\int_{X}\sigma(f(x)+\theta)d\mu=0\>\>\text{for all}\>\>f\in F,\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mu=0.

The statement of lemmas
5
and
6
are similiarly in
[
5
]
(the proof also) by replacing
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
X
.

Lemma 5
.

If
\sigma
is discriminatory with respect to
F
, then
N_{\sigma}(F)
is dense in
\mathcal{C}(X)
.

Proof.

We know that
N_{\sigma}(F)
is a subspace of
\mathcal{C}(X)
. If
\sigma(F)
is not dense (i.e
\overline{\sigma(F)}\neq\mathcal{C}(X)
), there is bounded linear functional
L\neq 0
of
\mathcal{C}(X)
such that
L|_{\sigma(F)}=0
.
By Riesz representation theorem, there is
\mu\in B(X)
such that

L(h)=\int_{X}h(x)d\mu(x),\quad\quad\forall h\in\mathcal{C}(X).

Then, for every
h\in N_{\sigma}(F)
one has

\int_{X}h(x)d\mu(x)=0.

So,

\int_{X}\sigma(f(x)+\theta)d\mu(x),\quad\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{F}(X),\theta\in\mathbb{R}

thus
\mu=0
since
\sigma
is discriminatory with respect to
F
. Therefore,
L=0
which is absurd. This contradiction completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 6
.

If
\sigma
is of sigmoidal type,
F
separates
B(X)
and
\mathbb{R}F\subset F
, then
\sigma
is discriminatory with respect to
F
.

Proof.

For every
x\in X,f\in F,
and
\theta,\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\sigma(\lambda(f(x)+\theta)+\varphi)\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\to 1&\text{for}&f(x)+\theta>0&\text{as}\>\lambda\to+\infty,\\
\to 0&\text{for}&f(x)+\theta<0&\text{as}\>\lambda\to+\infty,\\
=\sigma(\varphi)&\text{for}&f(x)+\theta=0&\text{for all}\>\>\lambda.\end{array}\right.

Then,
\sigma_{\lambda}(x)\overset{\mbox{def.}}{=}\sigma(\lambda(f(x)+\theta)+\varphi)
converges to

\gamma(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1&\text{for}&f(x)+\theta>0,\\
0&\text{for}&f(x)+\theta<0,\\
\sigma(\varphi)&\text{for}&f(x)+\theta=0\end{array}\right.

as
\lambda\to+\infty.

Now, suppose that
\mu\in B(X)
satisfies

\int_{X}\sigma(f(x)+\theta)d\mu(x)=0,\quad\quad\forall f\in F,\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
\mathbb{R}F\subset F

(and so
\lambda f
belongs to
F
,
\forall f\in F
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
), one has

\int_{X}\sigma_{\lambda}(x)d\mu(x)=0,\quad\quad\forall f\in F,\,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Then,

0=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\int_{X}\sigma_{\lambda}(x)d\mu(x)=\int_{X}\gamma(x)d\mu(x)=\sigma(\varphi)\cdot\mu(\Pi_{f,\theta})+\mu(H_{f,\theta})

where
\Pi_{f,\theta}=\{x:f(x)+\theta=0\}
and
H_{f,\theta}=\{x:f(x)+\theta>0\}
, for all
f\in F
and
\theta,\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
.
But
\sigma
is sigmoidal so by fixing
(f,\theta)\in F\times\mathbb{R}
and letting
\varphi\to\infty
above we obtain

\mu(\Pi_{f,\theta})+\mu(H_{f,\theta})=0,\quad\quad\forall f\in F,\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

Now, fix
f\in F
and define the linear functional
G:L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
by

G(h)=\int_{X}h(f(x))d\mu(x).

By taking
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and evaluating
G
at the indicator function
\chi_{[-\theta,\infty)}
one has

\displaystyle G(\chi_{[-\theta,\infty)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{X}\chi_{[-\theta,\infty)}(f(x))d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Pi_{f,\theta}}d\mu+\int_{H_{f,\theta}}d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(\Pi_{f,\theta})+\mu(H_{f,\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad\quad\forall\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

Replacing
-\theta
by
\theta
in the above result
we obtain

G(\chi_{[\theta,\infty)})=0,\quad\quad\forall\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

It follows that

\int_{X}\chi_{[\theta,\infty)}(x)d(f_{*}(\mu))(x)=\int_{X}\chi_{[\theta,\infty)}(f(x))d\mu(x)=G(\chi_{[\theta,\infty)})=0,\quad\quad\forall\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

By writing
h
as finite linear combination of indicator functions we obtain that

\int h(x)d(f_{*}(\mu))(x)=0,\quad\quad\forall h\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

As in
[
5
]
, this proves that Fourier transform of
f_{*}\mu
equals
0
for all
f\in F
, and so,
f_{*}\mu=0
for all
f\in F
.
Since
F
separates
B(X)
, we get
\mu=0
and so
\sigma
is discriminatory with respect to
F
. This completes the proof.
∎
