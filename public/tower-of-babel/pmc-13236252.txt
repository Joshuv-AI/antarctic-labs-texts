Single-staged kyphoscoliosis correction using Smith-Petersen’s osteotomy (SPO) in an adolescent with severe thoracolumbar kyphoscoliosis and Marfanoid habitus: a case report
Authors: Karya T. Biakto, Leonard C. Singjie, Malvin Tandry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Marfanoid individuals exhibit certain phenotypic features similar to those of Marfan syndrome; however, they do not fulfill the diagnostic criteria required for it. Spinal deformities are characteristic of Marfanoid syndrome and can worsen with age. This case report presents a unique case of an adolescent presenting with Marfanoid habitus and severe thoracolumbar kyphoscoliosis.Case presentation:A 16-year-old boy with Marfanoid features presented with a progressive spinal deformity and concomitant back pain and esthetic complaint for 6 years. Physical examination revealed pectus carinatum, as well as anthropometric features consistent with Marfan syndrome, including arachnodactyly, retinitis pigmentosa, and flat feet. However, no cardiovascular or neurological concerns were noted. Imaging findings demonstrated a kyphotic angle of 92.3° and scoliosis measuring 41.2°. The patient was diagnosed with thoracolumbar kyphoscoliosis in Marfanoid habitus.Clinical discussion:Surgical management included posterior spinal fusion with two rod constructs and bone grafting at the 13 vertebrae osteotomy levels, such as in a Smith-Petersen osteotomy (SPO). Significant postoperative correction was achieved with kyphosis and scoliosis measuring 11.9° and 10.2°, respectively. Rapid functional recovery with SF-36 scores improved over 6 months. This case demonstrates the utility of SPO and Cantilever techniques in achieving structural and functional improvement in complex spinal deformities associated with Marfanoid features.Conclusion:Thoracolumbar kyphoscoliosis in Marfanoid individuals can be effectively managed with single-stage SPO and Cantilever correction. Early surgical intervention and postoperative orthosis play a crucial role in achieving favorable outcomes and enhancing quality of life.

Introduction

Marfanoid habitus exhibits similar physical traits yet is different from Marfan syndrome (MFS)[1,2]. Spinal deformities (kyphoscoliosis, spondylolisthesis, and atlantoaxial subluxation) are frequently observed in these individuals[3,4]. A study by Wozniak-Mielczarek et al found that 168 out of 306 patients with such physical features were diagnosed with Marfanoid habitus[5]. Spinal deformities (kyphoscoliosis, spondylolisthesis, and atlantoaxial subluxation) are frequently observed in these individuals and progress rapidly if left untreated[6,7]. In syndromic cases like Kyphoscoliotic Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, Marfanoid habitus and congenital kyphoscoliosis co-exist with ligamentous laxity and hypotonia, creating unique clinical challenges[1,7] and preserving function and quality of life. In resource-limited settings, delayed diagnosis often results in paraplegia and cosmetic sequelae. In resource-limited areas, late diagnosis and complications such as paraplegia and severe cosmetic issues are common[7,8]. Delays in treating severe spinal deformities may lead to significant functional impairments and respiratory issues. Early surgical intervention prevents the progression of the deformity, reduces the risk of neurological complications, and improves overall patient outcomes[7,9,11]. Advances in surgical techniques, such as 3D correction, pedicle screw instrumentation, and osteotomies like the Smith-Petersen osteotomy (SPO), have significantly enhanced recovery and postoperative quality of life. SPO is a valuable treatment option for cases requiring correction of rigid spinal deformities. Timely treatment is therefore essential, as it minimizes both the physical and psychological burdens on patients while ensuring better clinical outcomes[8–10]. This report aimed to demonstrate the successful surgical treatment of severe thoracolumbar kyphoscoliosis in an adolescent with Marfanoid habitus using a single-stage surgical correction using SPO. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[12].

Case presentation

A 16-year-old male presented with a 6-year history of progressive low back pain, scaled 4–5/10 on the visual analog scale. The pain felt continuous, worsened with movement, and improved upon resting. Over time, the patient developed a visible thoracolumbar kyphoscoliosis, dyspnea on exertion, and psychological distress due to cosmetic concerns. These clinical features are unacceptable and troubling for the patient at his age, as they impacted his self-esteem and social interactions. Lately, the patient reported experiencing respiratory distress during physical activity, which had limited his ability to engage in exercise.

It is known that there were no skeletal abnormalities nor aortic pathologies present in first-degree or distant relatives. On the physical examination, the patient presented with a height of 177 cm and 190 cm. He exhibited Marfanoid habitus with pectus carinatum, arachnodactyly, and pes planus (Fig. 1). The neurological assessment was regular: motor strength was 5/5 in all muscle groups, sensation was intact, and physiological reflexes were normal, with no pathological findings. Ophthalmology revealed bilateral visual acuity of 2/20, fundoscopic changes of narrowed arterioles, irregular retinal pigmentation with bone spicule–like formations, and constricted visual fields consistent with retinitis pigmentosa. Standing radiographs demonstrated a 92.3° thoracic kyphosis apex at T12–L1, a 41.2° primary thoracic scoliosis, an 18° proximal thoracic curve, and a 14° lumbar curve (Fig. 2). Right-bending reduced the proximal thoracic angle to 8° and the main thoracic angle to 16°. Pelvic obliquity was 22°, the sagittal vertical axis exceeded 60 mm, and the pelvic incidence–lumbar lordosis mismatch was 15°. MRI showed no spinal canal stenosis or dural ectasia, and echocardiography plus laboratory tests were unremarkable. Due to its lack of an ectopic lens and cardiac abnormality, the patient was diagnosed with thoracolumbar kyphoscoliosis in the setting of Marfanoid body habitus. Given severe deformity, intractable pain, functional limitations, and Risser stage V, surgical correction was indicated.

A single-stage procedure involved SPOs from T4 to L4, followed by Cantilever deformity correction with dual titanium rods and polyaxial screws, and no bone grafts were used. The 8-hour operation also required a 1000 mL packed red blood cell (PRBC) transfusion. Postoperatively, he spent 4 days in the intensive care unit (ICU) in a head-up position with oxygen by nasal cannula, opioid and non-steroidal anti inflamator drug (NSAID) analgesia, third-generation cephalosporins, tranexamic acid, and mecobalamin. He then recovered in the general ward with wound care, physical therapy, and IV medications (Fig. 3). Figure 3.Intraoperative of the one-staged correction for the kyphoscoliotic deformity.

Nine days following the surgery, he ambulated with a Thoraco-Lumbo-Sacral-Orthosis (TLSO) for 23 hours per day. First erect radiographs showed a reduction in kyphosis to 11.9° and scoliosis to 10.2°, achieving corrections of 80° and 30°, respectively. At 1 month, he resumed daily activities independently (SF-36 score 81.6). At 6 months, alignment remained stable (proximal thoracic 13°, main thoracic 12.7°, lumbar 6.2°, and thoracic kyphosis 34.6°), implants were secure, and he was pain free with full ambulation (SF-36 score 84.2). The patient reported satisfaction with both functional and cosmetic outcomes (Fig. 4). At 6 months, his SF-36 score was 84.2. Radiographs showed maintained alignment (proximal thoracic 13°, main thoracic 12.7°, lumbar 6.2°, and thoracic kyphosis 34.6°), normal bone density, and unremarkable discs, foramina, and soft tissues. Posterior instrumentation from T3 to L5 remained secure. The patient was pain free and ambulating without restriction.

Discussion

Spinal deformities in children are more flexible than in adults, but achieving satisfactory results remains challenging, as cosmetic concerns are more significant in pediatric populations because they often have multiplanar deformities, such as kyphoscoliosis, which can involve chest deformities and require multidisciplinary care.[9–11,13]

Scoliosis is a complex three-dimensional spinal deformity characterized by lateral deviation, anteroposterior deviation, and axial vertebral rotation of the apical vertebra. Scoliosis in MFS progresses rapidly and responds poorly to non-surgical treatment. Thoracic spine involvement is common, and vertebral deformities (narrow pedicles, vertebral and sacral scalloping, wide transverse processes, thin laminae, and low bone mineral density) pose challenges for surgery[2,4]. Appendicular abnormalities such as leg length discrepancies and lower extremity deformities add to the complexity of managing scoliosis in MFS[1,2].

As scoliosis curves increase in size, the risk of progression grows. While ample thoracic curves over 100° can lead to cardiopulmonary complications, smaller curves are more likely to progress. Scheuermann kyphosis (SK) is a thoracic or thoracolumbar kyphosis caused by anterior vertebral wedging. It is the most frequent cause of hyperkyphosis in adolescents. Treatment focuses on correcting deformities, relieving pain, and improving cosmetically, with intervention thresholds varying by patient and surgeon[8,14].

The most common reason for surgery is the progression of the curve. In children, respecting the spinal growth is key, and surgery aims to stop deformity progression while allowing further growth and improving pulmonary function[7]. Managing severe, untreated CK and CKS presents significant surgical challenges, especially after years of anatomical adaptation. Complete removal of one or more vertebral bodies is often necessary to address the altered anatomy. Flexible spinal deformities can usually be treated with soft tissue release and facet joint excision; rigid deformities may require osteotomies to achieve correction[7].

SPO involves removing posterior ligaments and facet joints to release the spine, allowing for correction through compression. It involves removal of the posterior ligaments including the supraspinous, interspinous, and ligamentum flavum; as well as the facet joints to achieve posterior release[8]. In current practice, the SPO is performed for a kyphosis that corrects to a greater degree. This technique is safer and easier compared to others, with less risk of blood loss and neurological injury. It is typically used for kyphosis that corrects to >40°–50° or scoliosis >70°–75° that does not bend to <40°. Multiple SPOs can be performed to correct severe kyphosis, often achieving 5°–10° correction per level[11,15] The SPO is considered less aggressive than three-column osteotomies. It can be applied to both sagittal and coronal plane deformities and is regarded as the least complex posterior osteotomy, making it suitable for surgeons who are less experienced with more extensive procedures such as pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO), bone-disc-bone osteotomy (BDBO), or vertebral column resection (VCR)[8,11,14,15].

In contrast, other advanced osteotomies are reserved for deformities requiring greater angular correction. The PSO is a transpedicular V-shaped wedge osteotomy that shortens the posterior elements without lengthening the anterior column[8,9]. It achieves a much larger focal correction, typically between 30° and 40° at a single lumbar segment, and unlike SPO, does not depend on anterior disc mobility[8,11]. The BDBO involves resection of the disc and adjacent endplates and is particularly indicated when the deformity apex lies at the disc space or when correction beyond PSO levels is required. BDBO generally provides correction in the range of 35°–60°[8,9,11]. The VCR is the most aggressive technique, requiring complete removal of the vertebral body, adjacent discs, and posterior elements. It offers the highest degree of correction and is reserved for rigid multi-planar deformities or fixed coronal deformities that cannot be adequately addressed by other methods[8,9].

SPO carries several intraoperative and postoperative risks. Epidural bleeding may occur during resection of posterior ligaments or facets, particularly the superior facet of the inferior vertebra; this is usually controlled with bipolar cautery or gelatin packing, although excessive packing risks cord compression[8,9]. Neural impingement can result from reduced foraminal height during closure, necessitating wide facetectomy and vigilant neuromonitoring. Significant correction may destabilize the anterior column, with disc space opening >10 mm requiring grafting to ensure fusion. In scoliotic cases, posterior column shortening and anterior column lengthening may precipitate coronal imbalance toward the concavity, mitigated by trapezoidal wedge resections or limiting SPOs to neutral, stable segments[8,9,16].

Posterior-only approaches are becoming increasingly popular for addressing severe spinal deformities[8,13]. Recent systematic reviews by Huq et al (2019) revealed the superiority of the posterior-only approach in treating SK, showing that greater correction was noted postoperatively in the posterior-only approach group. Correction at final follow-up was greater in the posterior-only group when compared to other approaches (anterior-posterior group and bracing group). However, they are also associated with a higher rate of hardware complications, including hardware failure[15].

Studies have shown that one-stage posterior correction surgery using segmental pedicle screw instrumentation can effectively treat scoliosis. This approach achieves significant correction of scoliosis and kyphosis, with minimal long-term loss of correction. However, complications such as blood loss remain a concern[3].

A solid multidisciplinary approach is essential for safe and efficient recovery in adolescent spinal correction. The single-stage posterior approach, particularly with the paraspinal intermuscular approach (PIA), minimizes surgical trauma by preserving paraspinal muscles and ligaments through natural tissue planes[14,17]. This technique reduces soft tissue injury, lowers intraoperative blood loss (mean 256cc in one series), shortens operative time, and markedly decreases transfusion requirements and postoperative drainage, thereby accelerating recovery[8,14].

Postoperative care emphasizes early mobilization and functional rehabilitation. Patients typically ambulate with brace support by day 3, with brace removal possible at 3 weeks due to reliable facet joint fusion achieved via PIA. This rapid progression is reflected in significant improvements in patient-reported outcomes, including the function/activity domain of the SRS-22 questionnaire[16,17]. The TLSO braces are commonly used to limit motion, promote healing, and prevent kyphotic progression following thoracolumbar fractures[18].

Overall, comprehensive preoperative assessment, precise surgical execution with neuromonitoring, and minimally invasive technique establish optimal conditions for rapid rehabilitation, yielding superior functional recovery and quality of life.

Conclusion

This case report describes an adolescent patient with severe thoracolumbar kyphoscoliosis who underwent a single-stage surgical intervention combining SPO and the cantilever technique, resulting in significant deformity correction. A postoperative orthosis was applied to enhance spinal stability during the recovery period. At 6 months’ follow-up, radiographic and clinical assessments confirmed maintained alignment and satisfactory functional outcomes. These findings suggest that a one-stage approach combining posterior osteotomy, cantilever correction, and postoperative orthosis may be an effective strategy for managing complex and severe kyphoscoliosis in adolescents.

Patient perspective

The patient described a long and emotionally challenging journey of years of progressive spinal deformity, discomfort, and self-esteem caused by the deformity. The patient recalled how as the curvature of the spine progresses, it does not only caused persistent back pain but eventual shortness of breath during activity and also led to psychological distress.

Despite initial fears about undergoing major spinal surgery, the patient expressed his gratitude for the clarity provided by the medical team and the thorough explanations that reassured him.

Following the single-stage correction, the patient reported a profound sense of relief. He was pleased with the rapid recovery and the ability to return to daily activities with confidence. He emphasized that understanding his condition and receiving compassionate care made a significant difference in his overall experience.
