Vectorial Kato inequality for p p -harmonic maps with optimal constant
Andreas Gastel, Katarzyna Mazowiecka, Michał Miśkiewicz

Abstract
. We derive the sharp vectorial Kato inequality for p p -harmonic mappings. Surprisingly, the optimal constant differs from the one obtained for scalar valued p p -harmonic functions by Chang, Chen, and Wei [ 2 ] . As an application we demonstrate how this inequality can be used in the study of regularity of p p -harmonic maps. Furthermore, in the case of p p -harmonic maps from B 3 B^{3} to 𝕊 3 {\mathbb{S}}^{3} , we enhance the known range of p p values for which regularity is achieved. Specifically, we establish that for p ∈ [ 2 , 2.642 ] p\in[2,2.642] , minimizing p p -harmonic maps must be regular.

Introduction

For differentiable vector fields, or sections of a vector bundle with metric
connection
\nabla
, the trivial
pointwise inequality
|\nabla V|\geq|\nabla|V||
is known as
Kato’s inequality. It has been known for a long time that the constant

1
in this inequality can be improved for solutions of (systems of)
partial differential equations. This dates back at least as early as 1975
when Schoen, Simon, and Yau
[
12
]
proved
|\nabla A|^{2}\geq\frac{n+2}{n}\,|\nabla|A||^{2}

for the second fundamental form
A
of any minimal hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. This may not have been stated explicitly in
[
12
]
, see
[
1
, Lemme 3]
for this interpretation.

For harmonic maps
u\colon\mathcal{M}^{n}\to\mathcal{N}^{d}
, the vector field to be considered is

\nabla u
, giving estimates between
|\nabla^{2}u|
and
|\nabla|\nabla u||
.
The most striking application of Kato inequalities here is Okayasu’s paper
[
11
]
. He proved

|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}\leq\frac{n-1}{n}\,|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}
in the sense of
distributions, with optimal constant, and used
this to improve the results by Schoen and Uhlenbeck
[
13
]
about partial
regularity for harmonic maps to spheres, resulting in optimal regularity
for maps to
{\mathbb{S}}^{4}
and
{\mathbb{S}}^{5}
. Note, however, that Schoen and Uhlenbeck
already had some improved (but non-optimal) Kato-type inequality with constant

\frac{2nd}{2nd+1}
instead of
\frac{n-1}{n}
.

Improved Kato type inequalities are most significant in regularity proofs where
the values of constants are crucial. This is the case in all of the papers
mentioned so far, and more generally in many regularity proofs that perform
“dimension reduction of the singular set” by ruling out conical solutions
— representing the blow-up of a solution near a singular point — by playing
out a stability inequality against some Bochner–Weitzenböck formula.
As this technique has widespread applications, the search for optimal
constants in Kato’s inequality is relevant for solutions of many types
of equations and systems.

For harmonic maps to targets of any dimension, Okayasu’s constant
\frac{n-1}{n}

is optimal. The situation is different for
p
-harmonic maps, which are defined as critical points of

(1.1)

E_{p}(u)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla u|^{p}\qquad\text{among $u\in W^{1,p}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N})$.}

It is remarkable that the optimal constant for
p
-harmonic maps is not the same in the scalar and the vectorial case. This may be one of the reasons why Kato-type inequalities for
p
-harmonic
maps have not been proven but recently. Let us write the inequalities in
the form
|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}\geq\kappa|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}
here.
In 2016, Chang, Chen, and Wei
[
2
]
proved a Kato inequality for
p
-harmonic

functions
(
\mathbb{R}
-valued) with the optimal constant

\kappa=\min\{1+\frac{(p-1)^{2}}{n-1},2\}
. In 2019, the first-named author
[
6
]
studied a micropolar elasticity model which couples
p
-harmonic
maps with some other equation. In the course of a proof that the theory
is sort of well-behaved also for exponents
p
slightly larger than
2
, he provided a first Kato-type inequality for targets other than
\mathbb{R}
, with some non-optimal
\kappa>1
for suitable
combinations of
n
and
p
. This prompted the
second- and third-named authors
[
8
]
in 2023 to study the
n=2
case more
thoroughly, with the optimal result
\kappa=\min\{1+(p-1)^{2},2\}
, which happens to
coincide with the constant for scalar-valued case mentioned above.

These results, combined with the harmonic case
p=2
, might lead one to
guess that
\kappa=\min\{1+\frac{(p-1)^{2}}{n-1},2\}
could be the
best constant in the general case
n\geq 2
,
p>1
. But
[
8
]
also gave numerical evidence that in some cases the constant should be worse than that. This was our motivation to tackle the problem of the optimal Kato constant for

p
-harmonic maps in general. Our result is that the constant is given by

\kappa(p,n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1+\frac{(p-1)^{2}}{n-1}&\quad\text{for }&1\leq p\leq 1+\frac{n-1}{\sqrt{2n}-1}\\
2-\frac{(p-\sqrt{2n})^{2}}{(\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{2})^{2}}&\quad\text{for }&1+\frac{n-1}{\sqrt{2n}-1}\leq p\leq\sqrt{2n}\\
2&\quad\text{for }&p\geq\sqrt{2n}.\end{array}\right.

The constant above is sharp in a pointwise sense: it is achieved by some map that solves the
p
-harmonic equation at the given point.
This is the main result of the present paper and it is proved in
Section

2
.

In
Section

3
we show how our Kato type inequality can be used to study the regularity of
p
-harmonic maps. As already mentioned in this introduction, the reasoning is classical and follows by combining a stability inequality with a Bochner formula, as in
[
13
]
. For suitably chosen
p
,
n
and
d
, this leads to full or partial regularity of minimizing
p
-harmonic maps
B^{n}\to{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
. In particular, if
n
and
d
are chosen so that harmonic maps from
{B}^{n}
into
{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
are known to be regular (see
[
8
]
for a summary of all such cases), then we provide an interval of
p\in[2,p_{0}(n,d)]
for which
p
-harmonic maps are also regular. To give a concrete example, we study the case of
p
-harmonic maps
B^{4}\to S^{4}
in detail, obtaining the interval
p\in[2,p_{0}]
with
p_{0}=\frac{5+\sqrt{13}}{3}\approx 2.86
.

Section

4
provides further improvement in the special case of
p
-harmonic maps
B^{3}\to{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
, which was the main focus in
[
8
]
. There regularity was established for all
2\leq p\leq 2.366
and
p\geq 2.962
. Here, the main innovation lies in a new choice of test functions for both the stability and Bochner inequalities: instead of
|\nabla u|
and
|\nabla u|^{p-2}
(respectively), which is the natural choice, one can take
|\nabla u|^{1+\gamma}
and
|\nabla u|^{p-2+2\gamma}
with an additional parameter
\gamma
. By formal calculation, one can check that choosing
\gamma
strictly below zero leads to a better constant. However, an optimal choice
\gamma\approx-0.6
is not admissible, as the exponent
p-2+2\gamma
becomes negative in this case. For this reason, we perform a non-trivial regularization procedure, and finally obtain regularity of minimizing
p
-harmonic maps
B^{3}\to{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
for all
2\leq p\leq 2.642
as a result.

2.
Kato inequality

We may assume that
\mathcal{N}
is isometrically embedded into some Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{L}
. We recall the Euler–Lagrange equations for
p
-harmonic maps
u\in W^{1,p}({\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{N})

(2.1)

-{\rm div}_{\mathcal{M}}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)=|\nabla u|^{p-2}A(\nabla u,\nabla u),

where
A
is the second fundamental form of the isometric embedding
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{L}
.

Lemma 2.1

(an elementary fact from Linear Algebra)
.

For any linear map
A\in L(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
there is an
orthonormal basis
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for which

(Ab_{1},\ldots,Ab_{n})
are pairwise orthogonal in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Let
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
be the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We need a
B\in SO(n)
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle ABe_{i},ABe_{j}}}\right\rangle=\lambda_{i}\delta_{ij}
for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. This is equivalent to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle B^{T}A^{T}ABe_{i},e_{j}}}\right\rangle=\lambda_{i}\delta_{ij}
, which means all we have to do is diagonalize
A^{T}A\in L(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
. But, since
A^{T}A
is symmetric, such
B\in SO(n)
exists. Now
b_{i}\coloneqq Be_{i}
proves the assertion.
∎

Having this, we prove

Theorem 2.2

(Kato’s inequality for
p
-harmonic maps)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{N}
be smooth Riemannian manifolds of dimensions
n\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
. Let
u\colon\mathcal{M}\to{\mathcal{N}}
be a
p
-harmonic map,
p>1
. Then, at any point
q\in\mathcal{M}

where
\nabla u(q)\neq 0
, we have

(2.2)

\kappa(p,n)\,|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}\leq|\nabla^{2}u|^{2},

where

(2.3)

\kappa(p,n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1+\frac{(p-1)^{2}}{n-1}&\quad\text{for }&1\leq p\leq 1+\frac{n-1}{\sqrt{2n}-1}\\
2-\frac{(p-\sqrt{2n})^{2}}{(\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{2})^{2}}&\quad\text{for }&1+\frac{n-1}{\sqrt{2n}-1}\leq p\leq\sqrt{2n}\\
2&\quad\text{for }&p\geq\sqrt{2n}\end{array}\right.

is the sharp constant in (
2.2
), in the sense that there is map that solves the
p
-harmonic equation at
q
and achieves equality at that point.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that for
n=2
in the formula (
2.3
) we have the equality

1+\frac{n-1}{\sqrt{2n}-1}=2=\sqrt{2n}

and hence the formula corresponds to the one in
[
8
]
, i.e.,
\kappa(p,2)=\min\left\{1+(p-1)^{2},2\right\}
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.2
.

From now, we fix a point
q\in{\mathcal{M}}
with
\nabla u(q)\neq 0
and use normal coordinates around
q\in{\mathcal{M}}
and
u(q)\in\mathcal{N}
. Then, the
p
-harmonic equation at this particular point takes the Euclidean form.
That is, for
a\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

(2.4)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{ii}^{a}+\frac{p-2}{|\nabla u|^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{b=1}^{d}u_{i}^{a}u_{j}^{b}u_{ij}^{b}=0.

By
Lemma

2.1
, we may choose orthonormal bases of
T_{q}\mathcal{M}
and
T_{u(q)}\mathcal{N}
in which
\nabla u
has only diagonal components. In other words, a linear change of coordinates ensures that

(2.5)

u_{i}^{a}=\alpha_{i}\delta_{i}^{a}\quad\text{for all }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\ a\in\{1,\ldots,d\}.

From now on, we will additionally assume
|\nabla u(q)|^{2}=1
, which translates to the condition
\sum_{a=1}^{n}\alpha_{a}^{2}=1
. Otherwise, we would just work with coefficients
\tilde{u}_{i}^{a}=u_{i}^{a}/|\nabla u|
instead of
u_{i}^{a}
. Moreover, for notational convenience we shall adopt the convention that
u_{ij}^{a}=0
whenever
a>d
.
Thus, the
p
-harmonic system (
2.4
) takes the form

(2.6)

\Delta u^{a}+(p-2)\alpha_{a}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\alpha_{b}u_{ab}^{b}=0\quad\text{for each }a\in\{1,\ldots,d\}.

Using (
2.5
), we introduce the quantity

(2.7)

I_{i}:=|\partial_{i}|\nabla u||^{2}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{b=1}^{n}u_{j}^{b}u_{ij}^{b}\right)^{2}=\left(\sum_{b=1}^{n}\alpha_{b}u_{ib}^{b}\right)^{2}\quad\text{for }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

and aim to show the estimate

(2.8)

\begin{split}\kappa(p,n)I_{i}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{jj}^{i})^{2}+2\sum_{b\neq i}(u_{ib}^{b})^{2}.\end{split}

Once we establish (
2.8
), the Theorem follows, since then

(2.9)

\begin{split}|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{a=1}^{d}(u_{ij}^{a})^{2}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{jj}^{i})^{2}+2\sum_{b\neq i}(u_{ib}^{b})^{2}\right)\\
&\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa(p,n)I_{i}=\kappa(p,n)|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}.\end{split}

Note the first inequality becomes an equality if and only if
u_{ij}^{a}=0
for all triples
i,j,a
of pairwise distinct indices.
Thus, it suffices to show (
2.8
). For simplicity of notation we will focus on the case when
i=1
(the remaining cases are proved in the same way).

First, we rewrite the equation (
2.6
) with
a=1
in the following way:

(2.10)

\sum_{j=2}^{n}u_{jj}^{1}=-u_{11}^{1}-(p-2)\alpha_{1}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\alpha_{b}u_{1b}^{b}.

Combining this with the elementary inequality between the quadratic and arithmetic means, we obtain

(2.11)

\sum_{j=2}^{n}(u_{jj}^{1})^{2}\geq\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\sum_{j=2}^{n}u_{jj}^{1}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{n-1}\left(u_{11}^{1}+(p-2)\alpha_{1}\sum_{b=1}^{d}\alpha_{b}u_{1b}^{b}\right)^{2}.

Note that equality in (
2.11
) occurs when all terms
u_{jj}^{1}
coincide for
j\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
.

Applying (
2.11
), we see that (
2.8
) reduces to showing

(2.12)

\kappa(p,n)\left(\sum_{b=1}^{n}\alpha_{b}u_{1b}^{b}\right)^{2}\leq(u_{11}^{1})^{2}+\frac{1}{n-1}\left(u_{11}^{1}+(p-2)\alpha_{1}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\alpha_{b}u_{1b}^{b}\right)^{2}+2\sum_{b=2}^{n}(u_{1b}^{b})^{2}.

As a consequence, our problem now simplifies to the elementary inequality above, which we shall show is valid for any
\alpha_{i}
(
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
) satisfying
\alpha_{1}^{2}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}^{2}=1
, and any
u_{1b}^{b}
(
b\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
).

The next step can be seen as a further reduction to the case
d=2
. Let us rewrite the above, extracting all Hessian terms other than
u_{11}^{1}
:

(2.13)

\begin{split}\kappa(p,n)&\left(\alpha_{1}u_{11}^{1}+\sum_{b=2}^{d}\alpha_{b}u_{1b}^{b}\right)^{2}\\
&\leq(u_{11}^{1})^{2}+\frac{1}{n-1}\left(u_{11}^{1}+(p-2)\alpha_{1}\left(\alpha_{1}u_{11}^{1}+\sum_{b=2}^{d}\alpha_{b}u_{1b}^{b}\right)\right)^{2}+2\sum_{b=2}^{d}(u_{1b}^{b})^{2}.\end{split}

Let us denote
\beta=|(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})|
and choose
y
satisfying
\beta y=\sum_{b=2}^{d}\alpha_{b}u_{1b}^{b}
(if
\beta=0
, choose
y=0
). By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
(\beta y)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\sum_{b=2}^{d}(u_{1b}^{b})^{2}
, which we can rewrite as
y^{2}\leq\sum_{b=2}^{d}(u_{1b}^{b})^{2}
. Thus, after denoting
x:=u_{11}^{1}
and
\alpha:=\alpha_{1}
, our inequality reduces to

(2.14)

\kappa(p,n)\left(\alpha x+\beta y\right)^{2}\leq x^{2}+\frac{1}{n-1}\left(x+(p-2)\alpha\left(\alpha x+\beta y\right)\right)^{2}+2y^{2}.

which holds, as we shall see, for all
x,y,\alpha,\beta
satisfying
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=1
.
The appearance of
\alpha x+\beta y
suggests a change of variables (a rotation), which we now perform:

(2.15)

\begin{cases}z=\alpha x+\beta y,\\
w=\beta x-\alpha y,\end{cases}\quad\text{ or }\quad\begin{cases}x=\alpha z+\beta w,\\
y=\beta z-\alpha w.\end{cases}

Inequality (
2.14
) now becomes

(2.16)

\begin{split}0&\leq z^{2}\left(-\kappa(p,n)+2+\alpha^{2}\left(\frac{(p-1)^{2}}{n-1}-1\right)\right)+w^{2}\left(2-\frac{n-2}{n-1}\beta^{2}\right)-zw\frac{2\alpha\beta(n-p)}{n-1}.\end{split}

Now (
2.16
) is a quadratic form in
z
and
w
, with a positive coefficient of
w^{2}
. Hence the inequality is univerally true if only its discriminant
\Delta_{zw}
is nonpositive, that is

(2.17)

\begin{split}0\geq\Delta_{zw}&=\left(\frac{2\alpha\beta(n-p)}{n-1}\right)^{2}-4\left(-\kappa(p,n)+2+\alpha^{2}\left(\frac{(p-1)^{2}}{n-1}-1\right)\right)\left(2-\frac{n-2}{n-1}\beta^{2}\right)\\
&=-\frac{4}{n-1}\left(-\kappa(p,n)((n-2)\alpha^{2}+n)+\alpha^{4}(p-2)^{2}+\alpha^{2}(p^{2}-4)+2n\right).\end{split}

Note that we have used the equality
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=1
to eliminate
\beta
. Now (
2.17
) is equivalent to

(2.18)

\kappa(p,n)\leq\frac{\alpha^{4}(p-2)^{2}+\alpha^{2}(p^{2}-4)+2n}{(n-2)\alpha^{2}+n}

for all
\alpha\in[-1,1]
. Substituting
a:=\alpha^{2}\in[0,1]
, we obtain

(2.19)

\kappa(p,n)=\min_{a\in[0,1]}\frac{a^{2}(p-2)^{2}+a(p^{2}-4)+2n}{(n-2)a+n}\eqqcolon\min_{a\in[0,1]}f(a).

Minimization of
f(a)
is straightforward, but its result depends — quite unsurprisingly — on
n
and
p
. Let us compute:

(2.20)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}(n-2)(p-2)^{2}+2an(p-2)^{2}+n(p^{2}-2n)}{(a(n-2)+n)^{2}}.

Note that the numerator of
f^{\prime}(a)
is a quadratic polynomial with nonnegative discriminant
\Delta_{a}=8n(p-2)^{2}(n-p)^{2}
. Thus,
f^{\prime}(a)
is nonpositive exactly on an interval
a\in[a_{-},a_{+}]
satisfying
a_{+}+a_{-}=-2n(p-2)^{2}
and
a_{+}\cdot a_{-}=n(p^{2}-2n)
. Naturally, the behavior of
f
on the interval
[0,1]
is different in the following three cases:

Case 1:

f^{\prime}(0)\geq 0
. This happens when
p\geq\sqrt{2n}
. In that case, we see that
a_{-},a_{+}
are both nonpositive, so
f
is increasing on
[0,1]
. In conclusion:

\kappa(p,n)=f(0)=2\qquad\text{for }p\geq\sqrt{2n}.

Case 2:

f^{\prime}(1)\leq 0
. If we restrict to
p\geq 1
, this happens when
p\leq 1+\frac{n-1}{\sqrt{2n}-1}
. In that case, we see
a_{-}\leq 0\leq 1\leq a_{+}
, so
f
is decreasing on
[0,1]
, and in consequence:

\kappa(p,n)=f(1)=1+\frac{(p-1)^{2}}{n-1}\qquad\text{for }1\leq p\leq 1+\frac{n-1}{\sqrt{2n}-1}.

Case 3:

f^{\prime}(0)<0<f^{\prime}(1)
. By elimination, this is true in the regime
1+\frac{n-1}{\sqrt{2n}-1}<p<\sqrt{2n}
. In that case, we have
a_{+}\in(0,1)
and this is where the minimum is achieved. We have already computed the discriminant – since in this regime
p\in[2,n]
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Delta_{a}}

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{2n}(p-2)(n-p),

\displaystyle a_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{-n(p-2)+\sqrt{2n}(n-p)}{(n-2)(p-2)},

\displaystyle\kappa(p,n)=f(a_{+})

\displaystyle=2-\frac{(p-\sqrt{2n})^{2}}{(\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{2})^{2}}\qquad\text{in the middle regime.}

This analysis completes the proof of the Kato inequality.

Finally, we complete the proof of
Theorem

2.2
by showing that the constant
\kappa(p,n)
defined in (
2.3
) is sharp. For simplicity, we shall do so for a map
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. One can then obtain an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued map by the inclusion
\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a map between manifolds via normal coordinates (as in the beginning of the proof). To find the values of the derivatives
u_{i}^{a}
and
u_{ij}^{a}
(at
0
), we work backwards so that each step is optimal:

•

Take
a\in[0,1]
for which the minimum
\min_{a\in[0,1]}f(a)
in (
2.19
) is achieved, and set
\alpha:=\sqrt{a}
,
\beta:=\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}
. In the case
d=2
, we then simply put

u_{1}^{1}:=\alpha_{1}=\alpha,\qquad u_{2}^{2}:=\alpha_{2}=\beta,

and all other first order derivatives equal zero.

•

By this choice, we have
\Delta_{zw}=0
in (
2.17
). This means that we can pick a nonzero pair
(z,w)
for which equality in (
2.16
) is achieved. Reversing the change of variables in (
2.15
), we obtain a nonzero pair
(x,y)
achieving equality in (
2.14
).

•

Choose
u_{11}^{1}=x
,
u_{12}^{2}=y
. For
d=2
, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is trivially an equality. In consequence, (
2.13
) and (
2.12
) are also equalities.

•

In order to satisfy the
p
-harmonic equation (
2.10
), we choose

u_{22}^{1},\ldots,u_{nn}^{1}:=-\frac{1}{n-1}\left(u_{11}^{1}+(p-2)\alpha_{1}\sum_{b=1}^{n}\alpha_{b}u_{1b}^{b}\right).

That way, the quadratic–arithmetic mean inequality between these terms (
2.11
) is an equality, which means that (
2.8
) is, too:

\kappa(p,n)|\partial_{1}|\nabla u||^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{jj}^{i})^{2}+2\sum_{b\neq i}(u_{ib}^{b})^{2}.

•

We let all other Hessian terms be zero, which makes the
p
-harmonic system (
2.5
), (
2.6
) satisfied on all coordinates. Also, the equality above is then actually the equality between
\kappa(p,n)|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}
and
|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}
, as all other terms on both sides vanish.

∎

Remark 2.4
.

The
p
-harmonic map
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
u(x)\coloneqq|x|^{\frac{p-n}{p-1}}

As an application of the vectorial Kato inequality, we can obtain regularity results for

Proof of Remark

. It follows from the formula (

Remark 4.2

Lemma 4.3

(Improved Stability and Bochner inequalities)

(a)

\begin{split}3(1+\gamma)^{2}&\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}|\nabla u|^{p-2+2\gamma}|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}+(p-2)(1+\gamma)^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}|\nabla u|^{p-2+2\gamma}\left|\left\langle\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|},\nabla|\nabla u|\right\rangle\right|^{2}\\

\begin{split}((p-2)&+2\gamma(p-1))\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}|\nabla u|^{p-2+2\gamma}|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}+\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}|\nabla u|^{p-2+2\gamma}|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}\\

Lemma A.1

(Stability inequality)
.

Assume that
u\colon{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}\to{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
is a stable-stationary
p
-harmonic tangent map.
Then

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|\nabla u|^{p-4}\left(d|\nabla u|^{2}|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}+(p-2)|\langle\nabla u,\nabla|\nabla u|\rangle|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(d-p)\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|\nabla u|^{p+2}-\frac{d(n-p)^{2}}{4}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|\nabla u|^{p}.

The proof can be found in
[
9
, Lem. 2, 3]
, but the result there is simplified, and thus slightly weaker. For this reason, certain technical details of the calculations will be omitted here.

Proof of
Lemma

A.1
.

We start with the derivation from
[
9
, Lem. 2]
, with one final step omitted. It will be useful to consider the map
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
(not restricted to the sphere), as we will exploit the stable stationary condition, i.e., the fact that the second variation is nonnegative. Following
[
13
, Proof of Thm. 2.7]
let us choose the perturbation family as in
[
14
]

u_{t}(x)\coloneqq\frac{u(x)+t\varphi(x)\alpha}{|u(x)+t\varphi(x)\alpha|},

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is a fixed unit vector and
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
(later we will take an average over all
\alpha
and build
\varphi
based on
|\nabla u|
).

The stability condition tells us that

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{1}{p}\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\bigg|_{t=0}E_{p}(u_{t})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{p-2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}(1-|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u,\alpha}}\right\rangle|^{2})

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\varphi^{2}(p|\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u,\alpha}}\right\rangle|^{2}+(|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u,\alpha}}\right\rangle|^{2}-1)|\nabla u|^{2})

\displaystyle+(p-2)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{p-4}|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u,\alpha}}\right\rangle,\nabla\varphi}}\right\rangle|^{2}.

Summing the above over all
\alpha

, we obtain

0\leq d\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{p-2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}-(d-p)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{p}\varphi^{2}+(p-2)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{p-4}|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi}}\right\rangle|^{2}.

Crucially,
we choose not to bound the last term via Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
. Instead, we proceed as in
[
9
, Lem. 3]
and choose
\varphi
in the form

\varphi(rz)=f(r)g(z),\quad\text{with }f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\text{ and }g\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}).

Rewriting the previous inequality in this special case is straightforward; one thing worth noticing is that since
u
is
0

\displaystyle|\nabla\varphi(rz)|^{2}=f^{\prime}(r)^{2}g(z)^{2}+r^{-2}f(r)^{2}|\nabla g(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle\text{but}\quad|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\nabla u(rz),\nabla\varphi(rz)}}\right\rangle|^{2}=r^{-4}f(r)^{2}|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\nabla u(z),\nabla g(z)}}\right\rangle|^{2},

\begin{split}0&\leq\left(d\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|\nabla u(z)|^{p-2}g(z)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime}(r)^{2}r^{n-p+1}\,\mathrm{d}r\right)\\

We can now optimize over all

\inf_{f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime}(r)^{2}r^{n-p+1}\,\mathrm{d}r}{\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)^{2}r^{n-p-1}\,\mathrm{d}r}=\frac{(n-p)^{2}}{4}

(which can be derived by ODE considerations as in

(A.1)

\begin{split}&d\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|\nabla u(z)|^{p-2}|\nabla g(z)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z+(p-2)\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}|\nabla u(z)|^{p-4}|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\nabla u(z),\nabla g(z)}}\right\rangle|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}z\\

Finally, by approximating

∎

Proof of
Lemma

4.4

(b)
(
\delta
–Bochner inequality).

In order to prove (
4.4
), we proceed similarly as
[
13
, pp. 93-94]
,
[
9
, Lemma 1]
, or
[
10
, Lemma 2]
, see also
[
3
, p. 24]
.
That is, we employ the Bochner–Weitzenböck formula, or rather its corollary, the inequality (
A.3
) for maps
u\colon{\mathbb{S}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
. We test this with

g=|\nabla u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2\gamma}_{\delta},\quad\text{ where }|\nabla u|_{\delta}=(\delta^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}

and integrate by parts. We do not need to exchange the order of derivatives, so the computation follows like in the flat case:
we obtain

(A.18)

\begin{split}&-\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}\langle\nabla|\nabla u|^{2},\nabla(|\nabla u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{{2\gamma}}_{\delta})\rangle\\
&\geq\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}|\nabla u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{{2\gamma}}_{\delta}+|\nabla u|^{p}|\nabla u|^{2\gamma}_{\delta}-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{p+2}|\nabla u|^{2\gamma}_{\delta}-\langle\Delta u,{\rm div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2\gamma}_{\delta}\nabla u)\rangle.\end{split}

First, we note that the term on the left-hand side of (
A.18
) is

(A.19)

\begin{split}-\frac{1}{2}&\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}\langle\nabla|\nabla u|^{2},\nabla(|\nabla u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{{2\gamma}}_{\delta})\rangle\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle 2|\nabla u||\nabla|\nabla u||\,,\,(p-2)|\nabla u|^{p-3}\nabla|\nabla u||\nabla u|^{2\gamma}_{\delta}+|\nabla u|^{p-2}{2\gamma}|\nabla u|^{{2\gamma}-2}_{\delta}|\nabla u|\nabla|\nabla u|}}\right\rangle\\
&=-\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}\left((p-2)|\nabla u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2\gamma}_{\delta}+{2\gamma}|\nabla u|^{p}|\nabla u|^{{2\gamma}-2}_{\delta}\right).\end{split}

Then, let us transform the last term of (
A.18
) by exploiting the equation of
p
-harmonic mappings

0=\Delta_{p}u:={\rm div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u).

It will also be useful to write this equation in a different form, at least at points where
\nabla u\neq 0
:

0=|\nabla u|^{2-p}\Delta_{p}u=\Delta u+(p-2)\Delta_{\infty}u,\quad\text{where }\Delta_{\infty}u\coloneqq\big\langle\nabla|\nabla u|,\tfrac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}\big\rangle

is the normalized (i.e., homogeneous)
\infty
-Laplace operator. This way, we have

\begin{split}X&\coloneqq-\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}\langle\Delta u,{\rm div}(|\nabla u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2\gamma}_{\delta}\nabla u)\rangle\\

Since

, the pointwise inequality

|X|\leq 2|\gamma|(p-2)\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}|\nabla u|_{\delta}^{{2\gamma}-2}|\nabla u|^{p}|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}.

A.19
), (
A.20

\begin{split}&-\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}\left((p-2)|\nabla u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2\gamma}_{\delta}+{2\gamma}|\nabla u|^{p}|\nabla u|^{{2\gamma}-2}_{\delta}\right)\\

Proof of
Lemma

4.5
(mixed Kato–Cauchy–Schwarz inequality).

We will derive an optimal constant in the inequality (at points where
\nabla u\neq 0
)

3|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}+A\left|\left\langle\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|},\nabla|\nabla u|\right\rangle\right|^{2}\leq C\left(|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}+B|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}\right),

where
A=p-2
,
B=(p-2)+2\gamma(p-1)
,
\gamma<0
.

We follow the proof of
[
8
, Lemma 5.2 (c)]
. Similarly to equation (5.5) on page 3934 therein, it suffices to show that

\begin{split}3&(\theta_{1}x+\theta_{2}y)^{2}+A\theta_{1}^{2}(\theta_{1}x+\theta_{2}y)^{2}\\
&\leq C\Big(x^{2}+2y^{2}+(x+(p-2)\theta_{1}(\theta_{1}x+\theta_{2}y))^{2}+B(\theta_{1}x+\theta_{2}y)^{2}\Big),\end{split}

for all
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
satisfying
\theta_{1}^{2}+\theta_{2}^{2}=1
. Taking the same steps, we introduce the rotated coordinates
z=\theta_{1}x+\theta_{2}y
,
w=\theta_{2}x-\theta_{1}y
, notice
x^{2}+y^{2}=z^{2}+w^{2}
,
x=\theta_{1}z+\theta_{2}w
, and thus rearrange the above to the form

3z^{2}+A\theta_{1}^{2}z^{2}\leq C\left(2(z^{2}+w^{2})+Bz^{2}+(p-2)^{2}\theta_{1}^{2}z^{2}+2(p-2)\theta_{1}zx\right)

or, substituting
C=\frac{3}{2+B}
:

A(2+B)\theta_{1}^{2}z^{2}\leq 3\left(p(p-2)\theta_{1}^{2}z^{2}+2w^{2}+2(p-2)\theta_{1}\theta_{2}zw\right).

Now take into account that
A=p-2
and
2+B=p-E
, where
E=-2\gamma(p-1)\geq 0
. Then the inequality becomes

0\leq(2p+E)\theta_{1}^{2}z^{2}+\frac{6}{p-2}w^{2}+6\theta_{1}\theta_{2}zw,

and the discriminant is

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=36\,\theta_{1}^{2}\theta_{2}^{2}-4\cdot(2p+E)\theta_{1}^{2}\cdot\frac{6}{p-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{12\,\theta_{1}^{2}}{p-2}\left(3(p-2)\theta_{2}^{2}-2(2p+E)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{12\,\theta_{1}^{2}}{p-2}\left(3p-4p\right)

\displaystyle\leq 0.

This shows that indeed we have

3|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}+A\left|\left\langle\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|},\nabla|\nabla u|\right\rangle\right|^{2}\leq C\left(|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}+B|\nabla|\nabla u||^{2}\right),

with
C=\frac{3}{2+B}
in our case.
∎
