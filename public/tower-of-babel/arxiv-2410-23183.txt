The Hadamard multiary quasigroup product
Raúl M. Falcón, Lorenzo Mella, Petr Vojtěchovský

Abstract
. The Hadamard quasigroup product has recently been introduced as a natural generalization of the classical Hadamard product of matrices. It is defined as the superposition operator of three binary operations, one of them being a quasigroup operation. This paper delves into the fundamentals of this superposition operator by considering its more general version over multiary groupoids. Particularly, we show how this operator preserves algebraic identities, multiary groupoid structures, inverse elements, isotopes, conjugates and orthogonality. Then, we generalize the mentioned Hadamard quasigroup product to multiary quasigroups. Based on this product, we prove that the number of m m -ary quasigroups defined on a given set X X coincides with the number of m m -ary operations that are orthogonal to a given m m -set of orthogonal m m -ary operations over X X .

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega_{m}(X)
denote the set of
m
-ary operations on a set
X
, with
m\geq 2
. An
m
-ary
groupoid
over
X
is any pair
(X,f)
, with
f\in\Omega_{m}(X)
. This pair is an
m
-ary monoid
if it has an
identity element

e\in X
. That is,

f(\underbrace{e,\ldots,e}_{i},a,\underbrace{e,\ldots,e}_{m-i-1})=a

for all
a\in X
and every non-negative integer
i<m
. Furthermore, the
m
-ary groupoid
(X,f)
is an
m
-ary quasigroup
if the following statement holds for each positive integer
i\leq m
: For every
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i+1},...,a_{m})\in X^{m-1}
and every
b\in X
, there exists a unique
a_{i}\in X
such that
f(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1},a_{i},a_{i+1},\dots,a_{m})=b
. If this is the case, then the Cayley table of
f
is a
Latin hypercube
of dimension
m
. That is, an
m
-dimensional array with entries in
X
such that, when
m-1
coordinates are fixed, then every element in
X
appears exactly once. This is a
Latin square
if
m=2
, and a
Latin cube
if
m=3
. A
Latin transversal
in
(X,f)
is any set of
|X|
cells in its Cayley table, no two of which coincide in any coordinate or contain the same symbol. From here on, we denote by
\mathcal{Q}_{m}(X)\subset\Omega_{m}(X)
the subset of
m
-ary operations on
X
describing an
m
-ary quasigroup.

An
n
-ary operation is called a
superposition operator
if it results after applying a finite number of times some of the following procedures: permute, identify or add variables in an operation, or replace variables by operations. (See
[
8
, Part II, Chapter 1]
for a more comprehensive development of this concept.) This paper focuses on the superposition operator that is defined as follows. Let
f\in\Omega_{m}(X)
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in\Omega_{n}(X)
. Then, we define the superposition operator
\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\in\Omega_{n}(X)
such that

(1)

\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\left(\overline{a}\right):=f\left(g_{1}\left(\overline{a}\right),\ldots,g_{m}\left(\overline{a}\right)\right)

for all
\overline{a}:=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in X^{n}
. Moreover, we define the operator

(2)

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\odot:&\Omega_{m}(X)&\to&\Omega_{m}(\Omega_{n}(X))\\
&f&\mapsto&\begin{array}[]{cccc}\odot_{f}:&\left(\Omega_{n}(X)\right)^{m}&\to&\Omega_{n}(X)\\
&(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})&\mapsto&\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}).\end{array}\end{array}

It is said that
f

preserves
a subset
S\subseteq\Omega_{n}(X)
if
f(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\in S
, for all
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in S
. This notion plays a relevant role in the theory of clones in universal algebra
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
8
]
. Furthermore, the superposition operator (
1
) allows the construction of
m
-ary operations endowing
\Omega_{n}(X)
with a semigroup structure. (That is, it gives rise to an associative groupoid.) Thus, for instance, if
m=n
and
g_{1}=\ldots=g_{m}=g
in (
1
), then
\Omega_{n}(X)
is endowed with semigroup structure with the superposition operator
f+g:=\odot_{f}(g,\ldots,g)
(see
[
2
]
). Moreover, if
m=n=2
and we have three binary operations
\star
,
*
and
\circ
on
X
, then the superposition operator (
1
) is defined so that

(3)

\odot_{\star}(*,\circ)(a,b):=\left(a*b\right)\star\left(a\circ b\right)

for all
a,b\in X
. The following two binary operations arise from this operator.

•

The pair
\left(\Omega_{2}(X),\rhd\right)
is a monoid
[
10
,
11
]
, where

a*\rhd\star b:=(a*b)\star b

for all
*,\star\in\Omega_{2}(X)
and
a,b\in X
. (Note that
*\rhd\star=\odot_{\star}(*,\circ)
, where
a\circ b=b
, for all
a,b\in X
.) Its identity element is the left projection operation
a*b=a
. Based on this operation, every group
(X,\cdot)
can be embedded into
\Omega_{2}(X)
so that the image of every
a\in X
in
\Omega_{2}(X)
is a right distributive operation on
X
(see
[
10
]
).

•

The pair
\left(\Omega_{2}(X),\lhd\right)
is a non-commutative monoid
[
9
]
, where

a*\lhd\star b:=(a*b)\star(b*a)

for all
*,\star\in\Omega_{2}(X)
and
a,b\in X
. (Note that
*\lhd\star=\odot_{\star}(*,\circ)
, where
a\circ b=b*a
, for all
a,b\in X
.) Its identity element is again the left projection operation.

Furthermore, for each binary operation
\star\in\Omega_{2}(X)
, let
\mathrm{Mult}_{\star}(X)
be the subset of binary operations
*\in\Omega_{2}(X)
that are distributive over
\star
. That is,

a*(b\star c)=(a*b)\star(a*c)\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt(a\star b)*c=(a*c)\star(b*c)

for all
a,b,c\in X
. Fuchs
[
7
, Chapter 18]
realized that, if
(X,\star)
is an abelian group, then the superposition operator (
3
) makes the pair
\left(\mathrm{Mult}_{\star}(X),\odot_{\star}\right)
to be also an abelian group. Its identity element
*_{e}\in\mathrm{Mult}_{\star}(X)
is defined so that
a*_{e}b:=e
for all
a,b\in X
, where
e
is the unit element of the group
(X,\star)
. In addition, the inverse of a binary operation
*\in\mathrm{Mult}_{\star}(X)
is the binary operation
*^{-1}\in\mathrm{Mult}_{\star}(X)
that is defined so that
a*^{-1}b:=(a*b)^{-1}
for all
a,b\in X
. Fuchs also proved that the group
\left(\mathrm{Mult}_{\star}(X),\odot_{\star}\right)
is isomorphic to the group of homomorphisms from
X\otimes X
to
X
, and also to the group of homomorphisms from
X
to the endomorphism group of
X
. Clay
[
4
]
proved a similar result for the set
{\mathrm{Mult}_{\star}}_{L}(X)
of binary operations
*\in\Omega_{2}(X)
that are left distributive with respect to
\star
. More precisely, he proved that, if
(X,\star)
is an abelian group, then the pair
\left({\mathrm{Mult}_{\star}}_{L}(X),\,\odot_{\star}\right)
is an abelian group isomorphic to the set of functions from
X
to the endomorphism group of
X
.

Much more recently, the superposition operator (
3
) has been used in
[
6
]
to introduce the so-called
Hadamard quasigroup product
as a natural generalization of the classical Hadamard product of matrices. Note in this regard that, if
X
is a finite set of
n
elements, then the Cayley table of every binary operation
*\in\Omega_{2}(X)
constitutes an array
A_{*}=(A_{*}[x,y])
in the set
\mathcal{A}_{n}(X)
of
n\times n
arrays with entries in
X
, where
A_{*}[a,b]:=a*b
for all
a,b\in X
. The superposition operator (
3
) constitutes a Hadamard quasigroup product whenever
\star\in\mathcal{Q}_{2}(X)
. That is, whenever
A_{\star}
is a Latin square. If this is the case, then the Hadamard quasigroup product
\odot_{\star}
of two matrices
A_{*},\,A_{\circ}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(X)
is defined so that

(4)

\left(A_{*}\odot_{\star}A_{\circ}\right)[a,b]:=A_{\star}\left[A_{*}[a,b],\,A_{\circ}[a,b]\right]

for all
a,b\in X
. This can be seen as the Hadamard product of
A_{*}
and
A_{\circ}
with respect to the operation
\star
, rather than with respect to the usual multiplication of numbers. If
*=\circ=\star
, then the successive iteration of this product is endowed with a cyclic behaviour that allows the definition of new isomorphism invariants of quasigroups. The set of binary operations
\star\in\mathcal{Q}_{2}(X)
preserving the set
\mathcal{Q}_{2}(X)
requires the existence
of successive localized Latin transversals in
(X,\star)
(see
[
6
]
).

In this paper, we generalize the Hadamard quasigroup product to multiary quasigroups and study its fundamentals. The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
deals with some preliminary concepts on multiary groupoids that are used throughout the paper. Then, we study in Section
3
the operator
\odot
described in (
2
). Particularly, we show how this operator preserves algebraic identities, multiary groupoid structures, inverse elements, isotopes, conjugates and orthogonality. Finally, the mentioned Hadamard quasigroup product is generalized in Section
4
to multiary quasigroups. Based on this product, we prove that the number of
m
-ary quasigroups defined on a given set
X
coincides with the number of
m
-ary operations that are orthogonal to a given
m
-set of orthogonal
m
-ary operations over
X
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we show some preliminary concepts on multiary groupoids that are used throughout the paper. For more details about this topic, we refer the reader to
[
1
]
.

Let
(X,f)
be an
m
-ary groupoid. The
image
of
f
is defined as the set

\mathrm{Img}(f):=\left\{f(\overline{a})\colon\,\overline{a}\in X^{m}\right\}.

Further, for each non-negative integer
i<m
, we denote by

I_{i}(X,f):=\left\{e\in X\colon\,f(\underbrace{e,\ldots,e}_{i},a,\underbrace{e,\ldots,e}_{m-i-1})=a,\text{ for all }a\in X\right\}

the set of all
i
-identity elements
of
(X,f)
. (If
m=2
, then
I_{0}(X)
(respectively,
I_{1}(X)
) is the set of all
left
(respectively,
right
)
identity elements
of
(X,f)
.) In addition, we denote by

I(X,f):=\bigcap_{i=0}^{m-1}I_{i}(X,f)

the set of all
identity elements
of
(X,f)
. The pair
(X,f)
is an
m
-ary monoid if and only if
I(X,f)\neq\emptyset
.

Let
(X,f)
be an
m
-ary monoid. An element
a\in X
is said to be
invertible
in
(X,f)
if there exist an element
a^{-1}\in X
and an identity element
e\in I(X,f)
such that

(5)

f(\underbrace{a,\ldots,a}_{i},a^{-1},\underbrace{a,\ldots,a}_{m-i-1})=e

for every non-negative integer
i<m
. The element
a^{-1}
is said to be an
inverse
of
a
in
(X,f)
. We denote by
\mathrm{Inv}(a,f)
the set of inverses of
a
in
(X,f)
. Then, the pair
(X,f)
is said to
have unique inverses
if
|\mathrm{Inv}(a,f)|=1
, for all
a\in X
. If this is the case, then
|I(X,f)|=1
.

Example 2.1
.

Let
X=\{1,2,3\}
. We consider the ternary groupoid
(X,f)
described by the following arrays in
\mathcal{A}_{3}(X)

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}A_{1}\equiv\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 1&2&3\\
\hline\cr 2&1&1\\
\hline\cr 3&1&1\\
\hline\cr\end{array}&&A_{2}\equiv\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 2&1&2\\
\hline\cr 1&2&3\\
\hline\cr 2&3&2\\
\hline\cr\end{array}&&A_{3}\equiv\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 3&3&1\\
\hline\cr 3&3&3\\
\hline\cr 1&3&1\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\end{array}

so that
f(i,j,k):=A_{k}[i,j]
, for all
i,j,k\in X
. In particular,
I_{0}(X,f)=I_{1}(X,f)=I_{2}(X,f)=\{1,2\}
. Thus,
I(X,f)=\mathrm{Inv}(1,f)=\mathrm{Inv}(2,f)=\{1,2\}
. Moreover,
\mathrm{Inv}(3,f)=\{1\}
. Hence,
(X,f)
does not have unique inverses.

Now, let
(X,g)
be the ternary groupoid described by the following arrays in
\mathcal{A}_{3}(X)

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}B_{1}\equiv\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 2&2&2\\
\hline\cr 2&1&1\\
\hline\cr 3&1&1\\
\hline\cr\end{array}&&B_{2}\equiv\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 1&1&2\\
\hline\cr 1&2&3\\
\hline\cr 2&3&2\\
\hline\cr\end{array}&&B_{3}\equiv\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr 3&3&1\\
\hline\cr 3&3&3\\
\hline\cr 1&3&2\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\end{array}

so that
g(i,j,k):=B_{k}[i,j]
, for all
i,j,k\in X
. In particular,
I_{0}(X,g)=I_{1}(X,g)=I_{2}(X,g)=\{2\}
. Thus,
I(X,g)=\{2\}
. Moreover,
\mathrm{Inv}(i,g)=\{i\}
, for
i\in\{1,3\}
. Hence,
(X,g)
has unique inverses.
\lhd

From now on, we denote the symmetric group on a set
X
by
\mathcal{S}_{X}
. (By
\mathcal{S}_{n}
if
X=[n]
for some positive integer
n
.) Then,
(X,f)
is said to be
isotopic
to an
m
-groupoid
(X,g)
if there exist
m+1
permutations
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{m+1}\in\mathcal{S}_{X}
such that

g\left(\pi_{1}(a_{1}),\ldots,\pi_{m}(a_{m})\right)=\pi_{m+1}\left(f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\right)

for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in X
. The tuple
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{m+1})
is called an
isotopism
from
(X,f)
to
(X,g)
. This is an
isomorphism
if
\pi_{1}=\ldots=\pi_{m+1}
, in which case the
m
-groupoids
(X,f)
and
(X,g)
are said to be
isomorphic
. Furthermore,
(X,f)
is said to be
conjugate
to
(X,g)
if there exists a permutation
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m+1}
such that

g(a_{\pi(1)},\ldots,a_{\pi(m)})=a_{\pi(m+1)}\Leftrightarrow f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})=a_{m+1}

for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m+1}\in X
. If this is the case, then
(X,g)
is said to be the
\pi
-conjugate
of
(X,f)
and it is denoted
g=f^{\pi}
. This
\pi
-conjugate always exists when
\pi(m+1)=m+1
. Moreover, if
(X,f)
is an
m
-ary quasigroup, then all its conjugates exist.

Finally, a set of
orthogonal

m
-ary operations is any subset
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\}\subseteq\Omega_{m}(X)
such that the map

(6)

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&X^{m}&\to&X^{m}\\
&\overline{a}&\mapsto&\left(g_{1}\left(\overline{a}\right),\ldots,g_{m}\left(\overline{a}\right)\right)\end{array}

is a bijection.

3.
The operator
\odot

From here on, we assume that
f\in\Omega_{m}(X)
. Moreover, for each
c\in X
, we define the constant map

(7)

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\gamma_{c}:&X^{n}&\to&X\\
&\overline{a}&\mapsto&\gamma_{c}(\overline{a}):=c\end{array}

Lemma 3.1
.

The map
c\mapsto\gamma_{c}
is a homomorphism that embeds
(X,f)
into
\left(\Omega_{n}(X),\,\odot_{f}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\in X
and
\overline{a}\in X^{n}
. We have from (
1
) that

\gamma_{f\left(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\right)}(\overline{a})=f\left(\gamma_{c_{1}}\left(\overline{a}\right),\ldots,\gamma_{c_{m}}\left(\overline{a}\right)\right)=\odot_{f}(\gamma_{c_{1}},\ldots,\gamma_{c_{m}})(\overline{a}).

Hence,
\gamma_{f\left(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\right)}=\odot_{f}(\gamma_{c_{1}},\ldots,\gamma_{c_{m}})
.
∎

The
m
-ary groupoid
\left(\Omega_{n}(X),\,\odot_{f}\right)
inherits indeed the algebraic structure of
(X,f)
. More precisely, the embedding described in Lemma
3.1
enables us to prove that the operator
\odot
preserves algebraic identities. In this regard, let
\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})
and
\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})
be two algebraic terms based on
m
-products of universally quantified variables. In both of them, if
m>2
, then we identify the
m
variables associated to each
m
-product within a pair of curly brackets and separated by commas. Thus, for instance, we could consider for
(m,t)=(3,9)
the terms

\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{9})=\left\{\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\},\{x_{4},x_{5},x_{6}\},\{x_{7},x_{8},x_{9}\}\right\}

and

\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{9})=\left\{\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\},\{x_{4},\{x_{5},x_{6},x_{7}\},x_{8}\},x_{9}\right\}.

For
m=2
, these curly brackets are not necessary and the product of variables is simply written as a juxtaposition. Thus, for instance, we can consider the term
x_{1}x_{2}
for
t=2
, or the term
(x_{1}x_{2})x_{3}
for
t=3
.

Further, from here on, we write the algebraic identity
\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})=\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})
simply as
\varphi=\psi
. The
m
-ary groupoid
(X,f)
(respectively,
\left(\Omega_{n}(X),\,\odot_{f}\right)
) is said to satisfy the algebraic identity
\varphi=\psi
if all the products in the equation refer to the operation
f
(respectively,
\odot_{f}
), and
\varphi(a_{1},\dots,a_{t})=\psi(a_{1},\dots,a_{t})
for all
a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\in X
(respectively,

\varphi(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})=\psi(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
for all
g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\in\Omega_{n}(X)
). Consider as an example commutativity for
m=2
, and suppose that
f
is a binary operation
\star
. Then, the
m
-ary groupoids
(X,\star)
and
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{\star})
are commutative if and only if they satisfy the algebraic identity
x_{1}x_{2}=x_{2}x_{1}
. Equivalently,
a\star b=b\star a
for all
a,b\in X
, and
\odot_{\star}(g_{1},g_{2})=\odot_{\star}(g_{2},g_{1})
for every pair of
n
-ary operations
g_{1},g_{2}\in\Omega_{n}(X)
. This last condition means that

g_{1}(\overline{a})\star g_{2}(\overline{a})=g_{2}(\overline{a})\star g_{1}(\overline{a})

for all
\overline{a}\in X^{n}
. The following result shows how the operator
\odot
preserves algebraic identities.

Lemma 3.2
.

The
m
-ary groupoid
(X,f)
satisfies the algebraic identity
\varphi=\psi
if and only if the
m
-ary groupoid
\left(\Omega_{n}(X),\,\odot_{f}\right)
satisfies it.

Proof.

Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in\Omega_{n}(X)
and
\overline{a}\in X^{n}
. If the
m
-ary groupoid
(X,f)
satisfies the algebraic identity
\varphi=\psi
, then we have that

\varphi\left(g_{1}\left(\overline{a}\right),\ldots,g_{t}\left(\overline{a}\right)\right)=\psi\left(g_{1}\left(\overline{a}\right),\ldots,g_{t}\left(\overline{a}\right)\right)

with respect to the product
f
. From (
1
), we have that
\varphi\left(g_{1},\ldots,g_{t}\right)=\psi\left(g_{1},\ldots,g_{t}\right)
with respect to the product
\odot_{f}
. Hence, the
m
-ary groupoid
\left(\Omega_{n}(X),\,\odot_{f}\right)
also satisfies the algebraic identity
\varphi=\psi
.

Conversely, let
a_{1},\dots,a_{t}\in X
. If the
m
-ary groupoid
\left(\Omega_{n}(X),\,\odot_{f}\right)
satisfies the algebraic identity
\varphi=\psi
, then we have that

\varphi\left(\gamma_{a_{1}},\ldots,\gamma_{a_{t}}\right)=\psi\left(\gamma_{a_{1}},\ldots,\gamma_{a_{t}}\right)

with respect to the product
\odot_{f}
. Thus, from (
1
) and (
7
), we have that
\varphi(a_{1},\dots,a_{t})=\psi(a_{1},\dots,a_{t})
. Hence, the
m
-ary groupoid
(X,f)
also satisfies the algebraic identity
\varphi=\psi
.
∎

The operator
\odot
also preserves
m
-ary monoid structures. In this regard, the following lemma describes all the
i
-identity elements in the
m
-ary groupoid
\left(\Omega_{n}(X),\,\odot_{f}\right)
, for every non-negative integer
i<m
.

Lemma 3.3
.

The following statements hold.

(1)

Let
c\in X
. Then,
c\in I_{i}(X,f)
if and only if
\gamma_{c}\in I_{i}(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
.

(2)

For each non-negative integer
i<m
,

I_{i}(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})=\{g\in\Omega_{n}(X):\mathrm{Img}(g)\subseteq I_{i}(X,f)\}.

Proof.

We prove separately each statement.

(1)

If
c\in I_{i}(X,f)
, then

(8)

f(\underbrace{c,\ldots,c}_{i},a,\underbrace{c,\ldots,c}_{m-i-1})=a

for all
a\in X
. As a consequence,

\odot_{f}(\underbrace{\gamma_{c},\ldots,\gamma_{c}}_{i},g,\underbrace{\gamma_{c},\ldots,\gamma_{c}}_{m-i-1})(\overline{a})=g(\overline{a})

for all
g\in\Omega_{n}(X)
and
\overline{a}\in X^{n}
. Hence,
\gamma_{c}\in I_{i}(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
.

Conversely, if
\gamma_{c}\in I_{i}(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
, then

\odot_{f}(\underbrace{\gamma_{c},\ldots,\gamma_{c}}_{i},g,\underbrace{\gamma_{c},\ldots,\gamma_{c}}_{m-i-1})=g

for all
g\in\Omega_{n}(X)
. In particular, if
g=\gamma_{a}
, for some
a\in X
, then (
8
) holds readily. Hence,
c\in I_{i}(X,f)
.

(2)

Let
g\in I_{i}(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
. It means that
g\in\Omega_{n}(X)
satisfies that

\odot_{f}(\underbrace{g,\ldots,g}_{i},h,\underbrace{g,\ldots,g}_{m-i-1})=h

for all
h\in\Omega_{n}(X)
. Particularly, if
h=\gamma_{c}
for some
c\in X
, then we have that

(9)

f(\underbrace{g(\overline{a}),\ldots,g(\overline{a})}_{i},c,\underbrace{g(\overline{a}),\ldots,g(\overline{a})}_{m-i-1})=c

for all
\overline{a}\in X^{n}
. Thus,

The operator
\odot
also preserves inverse elements.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(X,f)
be an
m
-ary monoid. The following statements hold.

(1)

Let
g\in\Omega_{n}(X)
be an invertible element in
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
. Then,

\mathrm{Inv}\left(g,\,\odot_{f}\right)=\left\{h\in\Omega_{n}(X)\colon\,h(\overline{a})\in\mathrm{Inv}(g(\overline{a}),\,f)\text{ for all }\overline{a}\in X^{n}\right\}.

(2)

Let
c\in X
. If
c^{-1}\in\mathrm{Inv}(c,f)
, then
\gamma_{c^{-1}}\in\mathrm{Inv}\left(\gamma_{c},\,\odot_{f}\right)
.

(3)

Let
c\in X
. If
(\gamma_{c})^{-1}\in\mathrm{Inv}\left(\gamma_{c},\,\odot_{f}\right)
, then
\mathrm{Img}((\gamma_{c})^{-1})\subseteq\mathrm{Inv}(c,f)
.

(4)

If
(X,f)
has unique inverses, then
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
also has unique inverses. More precisely, the inverse
g^{-1}
of
g\in\Omega_{n}(X)
is given by
g^{-1}(\overline{a})=\left(g(\overline{a})\right)^{-1}
for all
\overline{a}\in X^{n}
.

(5)

If
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
has unique inverses, then
(X,f)
also has unique inverses.

Proof.

Proposition
3.5
implies that an
n
-ary operation
h\in\Omega_{n}(X)
is an inverse of an invertible
g\in\Omega_{n}(X)
in
\left(\Omega_{n}(X),\,\odot_{f}\right)
if and only if, for each non-negative integer
i<m
and each
\overline{a}\in X
, there exists an identity element

such that

\odot_{f}(\underbrace{g,\ldots,g}_{i},h,\underbrace{g,\ldots,g}_{m-i-1})(\overline{a})=f(\underbrace{g(\overline{a}),\ldots,g(\overline{a})}_{i},h(\overline{a}),\underbrace{g(\overline{a}),\ldots,g(\overline{a})}_{m-i-1})=e_{i,\overline{a}}.

That is, if and only if
h(\overline{a})\in\mathrm{Inv}(g(\overline{a}),\,f)
for all
\overline{a}\in X^{n}
. Hence, the first statement holds.

Statements (2)–(4) follow readily from the first one. (Note in the third statement that, if
I(X,f)=\{e\}
, then Proposition
3.5
implies that
I(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})=\{\gamma_{e}\}

(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
has unique inverses, then we have from Proposition

that

where

. In addition, we have from the first statement that, for each

\mathrm{Img}((\gamma_{c})^{-1})\subseteq\mathrm{Inv}(c,f)
. As a consequence, the first statement also implies that
\gamma_{u}\in\mathrm{Inv}\left(\gamma_{c},\,\odot_{f}\right)
for all

has unique inverses, it must be

be the unique element in
\mathrm{Img}((\gamma_{c})^{-1})

from the third statement. It is necessarily unique from the second statement. Hence, the fifth statement holds.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
(X,f)
is isotopic to an
m
-ary groupoid

(X,g)
, then
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
is isotopic to
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{g})
.

Proof.

Let
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{m+1})
be an isotopism from
(X,f)
to
(X,g)
. For each positive integer
i\leq m+1
, we define the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\rho_{i}:&\Omega_{n}(X)&\to&\Omega_{n}(X)\\
&h&\mapsto&\pi_{i}\circ h\end{array}

where
\circ
denotes the composition of maps. (That is,
\rho_{i}(h)(\overline{a}):=\pi_{i}(h(\overline{a}))
, for all
\overline{a}\in X^{n}
.) This map is a bijection because
\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{X}
. Finally, let
h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}\in\Omega_{n}(X)
and
\overline{a}\in X^{n}
. Since
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{m+1})
is an isotopism from
(X,f)
to
(X,g)
, we have that

\displaystyle\odot_{g}\left(\rho_{1}(h_{1}),\ldots,\rho_{m}(h_{m})\right)(\overline{a})

\displaystyle=g(\pi_{1}(h_{1}(\overline{a})),\ldots,\pi_{m}(h_{m}(\overline{a})))=

\displaystyle=\pi_{m+1}(f(h_{1}(\overline{a}),\ldots,h_{m}(\overline{a})))=

\displaystyle=\pi_{m+1}(\odot_{f}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m})(\overline{a}))=

\displaystyle=\rho_{m+1}(\odot_{f}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}))(\overline{a}).

Thus,
\odot_{g}\left(\rho_{1}(h_{1}),\ldots,\rho_{m}(h_{m})\right)=\rho_{m+1}(\odot_{f}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}))
and hence,
(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m+1})
is an isotopism from
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
to
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{g})
.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

(X,f)
is conjugate to an
m
-ary groupoid

(X,g)
if and only if
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
is conjugate to
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{g})
. Moreover, if
g=f^{\pi}
for some
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m+1}
, then
\odot_{g}=(\odot_{f})^{\pi}
, and reciprocally.

Proof.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m+1}
be such that
g=f^{\pi}
. For each
h_{1},\ldots,h_{m+1}\in\Omega_{n}(X)
and
\overline{a}\in X^{n}
, we have that

\displaystyle\odot_{f}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m})(\overline{a})=h_{m+1}(\overline{a})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow f\left(h_{1}(\overline{a}),\ldots,h_{m}(\overline{a})\right)=h_{m+1}(\overline{a})\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow f^{\pi}\left(h_{\pi(1)}(\overline{a}),\ldots,h_{\pi(m)}(\overline{a})\right)=h_{\pi(m+1)}(\overline{a})\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\odot_{f^{\pi}}\left(h_{\pi(1)},\ldots,h_{\pi(m)}\right)(\overline{a})=h_{\pi(m+1)}(\overline{a}).

Hence,
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{f})
and
(\Omega_{n}(X),\odot_{g})
are conjugate and
\odot_{g}=(\odot_{f})^{\pi}
.

Conversely, let
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{m+1}
be such that
\odot_{g}=(\odot_{f})^{\pi}
. For each
c_{1},\ldots,c_{m+1}\in X
, we have that

\displaystyle f(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})=c_{m+1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\odot_{f}\left(\gamma_{c_{1}},\ldots,\gamma_{c_{m}}\right)=\gamma_{c_{m+1}}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\odot_{f})^{\pi}\left(\gamma_{c_{\pi(1)}},\ldots,\gamma_{c_{\pi(m)}}\right)=\gamma_{c_{\pi(m+1)}}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow g\left(c_{\pi(1)},\ldots,c_{\pi(m)}\right)=c_{\pi(m+1)}.

Hence,
(X,f)
and
(X,g)
are conjugate and
g=f^{\pi}
.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{n+1}
be such that
\pi(n+1)=n+1
. Then, for each

\odot_{f}(g_{1}^{\pi},\ldots,g_{m}^{\pi})=\left(\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\right)^{\pi}.

For each
\overline{a}:=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in X^{n}

\overline{a}^{\pi}:=(a_{\pi(1)},\ldots,a_{\pi(n)})
. Since

\displaystyle\odot_{f}(g_{1}^{\pi},\ldots,g_{m}^{\pi})(\overline{a}^{\pi})

\displaystyle=f(g_{1}^{\pi}(\overline{a}^{\pi}),\ldots,g_{m}^{\pi}(\overline{a}^{\pi}))=

\displaystyle=f(g_{1}(\overline{a}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{a}))=

\displaystyle=\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})(\overline{a})=

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\}\subseteq\Omega_{m}(X)
. This is a set of orthogonal
m
-ary operations if and only if the map

(10)

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\Omega_{m}(X)&\to&\Omega_{m}(X)\\
&f&\mapsto&\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\end{array}

is a bijection.

Proof.

Let
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\}\subseteq\Omega_{m}(X)
be a set of orthogonal
m
-ary operations. First, we prove that the map (
10
) is onto. For each
h\in\Omega_{m}(X)
, let
f_{h}\in\Omega_{m}(X)
be defined so that
f_{h}(g_{1}(\overline{a}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{a}))=h(\overline{a})
, for all
\overline{a}\in X^{m}
. It is well-defined because
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\}
is a set of orthogonal
m
-ary operations and thus, the map (
6
) is a bijection. As a consequence,
\odot_{f_{h}}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})=h
and hence, the map (
10
) is onto.

In order to prove that the map (
10
) is injective, let
f_{1},f_{2}\in\Omega_{m}(X)
be such that
\odot_{f_{1}}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})=\odot_{f_{2}}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
. Then,

(11)

f_{1}(g_{1}(\overline{a}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{a}))=f_{2}(g_{1}(\overline{a}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{a}))

for all
\overline{a}\in X^{m}
. If
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\}
is a set of orthogonal
m
-ary operations, then (
11
) implies that
f_{1}(\overline{a})=f_{2}(\overline{a})
for all
\overline{a}\in X^{m}
. So,
f_{1}=f_{2}
and hence, the map (
10
) is injective.

For the converse, suppose that the map (
10
) is a bijection, but the map (
6
) is not a bijection. If this last map is not onto, then there is an element
\overline{a}\in X^{m}
such that
(g_{1}(\overline{b}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{b}))\neq\overline{a}
, for all
\overline{b}\in X^{m}
. If
|X|=1
, then
|\Omega_{m}(X)|=1
and
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{m}\}
is a set of orthogonal
m
-ary operations mapping the unique
m
-tuple in
X^{m}
to itself. So, we may assume that
|X|>1
. Let
x,y\in X
be such that
x\neq y
. Then, let
f_{1},f_{2}\in\Omega_{m}(X)
be defined so that

•

f_{1}\left(g_{1}(\overline{b}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{b})\right)=f_{2}\left(g_{1}(\overline{b}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{b})\right)=x
, for all
\overline{b}\in X^{m}
;

•

f_{1}(\overline{a})=x
;

•

f_{2}(\overline{a})=y
; and

•

the rest of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are arbitrarily defined.

Then,
\odot_{f_{1}}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})=\odot_{f_{2}}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
, but
f_{1}\neq f_{2}
, so the map (
10
) is not injective, which is a contradiction.

Finally, suppose that the map (
6
) is not one-to-one. Let
\overline{a}\neq\overline{b}\in X^{m}
be such that
(g_{1}(\overline{a}),\dots,g_{m}(\overline{a}))=(g_{1}(\overline{b}),\dots,g_{m}(\overline{b}))

, we have that

such that

shows that the map (

Proposition 4.1
.

f\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}(X)
if and only if
\odot_{f}\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}\left(\Omega_{n}(X)\right)
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}(X)
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in\Omega_{n}(X)
. Then, let
h:=\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\in\Omega_{n}
and
\overline{x}\in X^{n}
. From (
1
), we have that

(12)

f\left(g_{1}\left(\overline{x}\right),\ldots,g_{m}\left(\overline{x}\right)\right)=h\left(\overline{x}\right).

Since
f\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}(X)
, for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, there exists a unique
a_{\overline{x},i}\in X
such that

f\left(g_{1}\left(\overline{x}\right),\ldots,g_{i-1}\left(\overline{x}\right),\,a_{\overline{x},i},\,g_{i+1}\left(\overline{x}\right),\,\ldots,g_{m}\left(\overline{x}\right)\right)=h\left(\overline{x}\right).

Thus, given the set
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{i-1},\,g_{i+1},\ldots,g_{m},\,h\}
, there is a unique
g_{i}\in\Omega_{m}(X)
satisfying (
12
), namely
g_{i}(\overline{x}):=a_{\overline{x},i}
. Hence,
\odot_{f}\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}\left(\Omega_{n}(X)\right)
.

Conversely, suppose that
\odot_{f}\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}\left(\Omega_{n}(X)\right)
. Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in X
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
be such that there exists
a^{\prime}_{i}\in X
satisfying that

f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m})=f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a^{\prime}_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m}).

From Lemma
3.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\odot_{f}(\gamma_{a_{1}},\ldots,\gamma_{a_{i-1}},\,\gamma_{a_{i}},\,\gamma_{a_{i+1}},\,\ldots,\gamma_{a_{m}})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m})}=

\displaystyle=\gamma_{f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a^{\prime}_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m})}=

\displaystyle=\odot_{f}(\gamma_{a_{1}},\ldots,\gamma_{a_{i-1}},\,\gamma_{a^{\prime}_{i}},\,\gamma_{a_{i+1}},\,\ldots,\gamma_{a_{m}}).

Since
\odot_{f}\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}\left(\Omega_{n}(X)\right)
, it must be
\gamma_{a_{i}}=\gamma_{a^{\prime}_{i}}
and thus,
a_{i}=a^{\prime}_{i}
. Hence,

.

Lemma 4.2

The quasigroup structure of

makes the operator

(13)

\mathrm{Ort}(S):=\left\{g\in\Omega_{m}(X)\colon\left(S\setminus\{g_{i}\}\right)\cup\{g\}\text{ is a set of orthogonal operations }\forall i\leq m\right\}.

The next result particularizes Lemma
3.10
in case of dealing with

-ary quasigroups on the set

instead of the whole set
\Omega_{m}(X)
. It shows in particular that the number of

-ary quasigroups on

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
S=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\}\subseteq\Omega_{m}(X)
be a set of orthogonal
m
-ary operations. Then, the map

(14)

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\mathcal{Q}_{m}(X)&\rightarrow&\mathrm{Ort}(S)\\
&f&\rightarrow&\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\end{array}

is a bijection.

Proof.

The map (
14
) is well-defined. To see it, assume that
f\in\Omega_{m}(X)
and let us prove that
\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\in\mathrm{Ort}(S)
. To this end, we shall see that
\{\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}),

g_{2},\ldots,g_{m}\}
is a set of orthogonal
m
-ary products. (The remaining cases in the definition (
13
) follow similarly.) So, we have to prove that the map

(15)

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&X^{m}&\to&X^{m}\\
&\overline{a}&\mapsto&\left(\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})(\overline{a}),g_{2}(\overline{a}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{a})\right)\end{array}

is a bijection. First, we prove that it is injective. To this end, let
\overline{a},\overline{b}\in X^{m}
be such that

\left(\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})(\overline{a}),g_{2}(\overline{a}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{a})\right)=\left(\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})(\overline{b}),g_{2}(\overline{b}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{b})\right).

Then,
g_{i}(\overline{a})=g_{i}(\overline{b})
for all
i\in\{2,\ldots,m\}
, and

f\left(g_{1}(\overline{a}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{a})\right)=f\left(g_{1}(\overline{b}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{b})\right).

Since
f\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}(X)
, it must be
g_{1}(\overline{a})=g_{1}(\overline{b})
. But then,
\overline{a}=\overline{b}
because
S
is a set of orthogonal
m
-ary operations. Hence, the map (
15
) is injective.

Second, we prove that the map (
15
) is onto. To this end, let
\overline{a}:=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in X^{m}
. Since
f\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}(X)
, there exists an element
x\in X
such that
f(x,a_{2},\ldots,a_{m})=a_{1}
. Moreover, since
S
is a set of orthogonal
m
-ary operations, there exists an element
\overline{b}\in X^{m}
such that
g_{1}(\overline{b})=x
and
g_{i}(\overline{b})=a_{i}
for all
i\in\{2,\ldots,m\}
. Hence,
\left(\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})(\overline{b}),g_{2}(\overline{b}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{b})\right)=\overline{a}
. Thus, the map (
15
) is onto and hence, the map (
14
) is well-defined.

Further, the injectivity of the map (
14
) follows from Lemma
3.10
. It therefore remains to prove that this map is also onto. To this end, let
h\in\mathrm{Ort}(S)
. Then, let
f\in\Omega_{m}(X)
be defined so that

\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})(\overline{a})=h(\overline{a})

for all
\overline{a}\in X^{m}
. (We have already seen in the proof of Lemma
3.10
that this
m
-ary operation is well-defined.) It remains to show that
f\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}(X)
. That is, we have to prove that, for every
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i-1},a_{i+1},...,a_{m})\in X^{m-1}
and every
b\in X
, there exists a unique
a_{i}\in X
such that
f(a_{1},\dots,a_{i-1},a_{i},a_{i+1},\dots,a_{m})=b
. Without loss of generality, we prove this condition for
i=1
. In this regard, let
(a_{2},\ldots,a_{m})\in X^{m-1}
and
b\in X
. Since
\{h,g_{2},\ldots,g_{m}\}
is a set of orthogonal
m
-ary operations, there exists a unique element
\overline{c}\in X^{m}
such that

\left(h(\overline{c}),g_{2}(\overline{c}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{c}))\right)=\left(b,a_{2},\ldots,a_{m}\right).

Then, the element
g_{1}(\overline{c})\in X
satisfies that

f(g_{1}(\overline{c}),a_{2},\ldots,a_{m})=\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})(\overline{c})=h(\overline{c})=b.

In order to prove that
f\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}(X)
, we have to prove that
g_{1}(\overline{c})
is the unique element in
X
satisfying the previous condition. To this end, let

\overline{a}:=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m})\in X^{m}

and

\overline{a}^{\prime}:=(a^{\prime}_{1},a_{2}\ldots,a_{m})\in X^{m},

be such that
f(\overline{a})=f(\overline{a}^{\prime})=b
. Since
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\}
is a set of orthogonal
m
-ary operations, there exist two elements
\overline{b},\overline{c}\in X^{m}
such that
(g_{1}(\overline{b}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{b}))=\overline{a}
and
(g_{1}(\overline{c}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{c}))=\overline{a}^{\prime}
. Then,

h(\overline{b})=\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})(\overline{b})=f(\overline{a})=f(\overline{a}^{\prime})=\odot_{f}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})(\overline{c})=h(\overline{c}).

As a consequence,

(h(\overline{b}),g_{2}(\overline{b}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{b}))=(h(\overline{c}),g_{2}(\overline{c}),\ldots,g_{m}(\overline{c})).

Since
h\in\mathrm{Ort}(S)
, we have that
\overline{b}=\overline{c}
and hence,
\overline{a}=\overline{a}^{\prime}
. Therefore,
f\in\mathcal{Q}_{m}(X)
.
∎

As is the custom in the theory of Latin squares, let us call two binary operations
g_{1},g_{2}\in\Omega_{2}(X)

orthogonal
if
\{g_{1},g_{2}\}
is an orthogonal set. The following corollary follows straightforwardly from Theorem
4.3
. In spite of its relevance, it seems not to have been noticed in the literature.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in\Omega_{2}(X)
be orthogonal binary operations on
X
. Then there is a one-to-one correspondence between all quasigroup operations on
X
and all binary operations on
X
that are orthogonal to both
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
.

Note that we do not claim that for any two orthogonal Latin squares
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
the number of Latin squares that are orthogonal to both
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
is independent of these two Latin squares. Indeed, Proposition 4.3 yields all Latin squares orthogonal to both
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
, but it also yields other non-Latin operations orthogonal to both
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
.
