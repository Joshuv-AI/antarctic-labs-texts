Backward Oversmoothing: why is it hard to train deep Graph Neural Networks?
Nicolas Keriven

Abstract
Oversmoothing has long been identified as a major limitation of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs): input node features are smoothed at each layer and converge to a non-informative representation, if the weights of the GNN are sufficiently bounded . This assumption is crucial: if, on the contrary, the weights are sufficiently large, then oversmoothing may not happen. Theoretically, GNN could thus learn to not oversmooth. However it does not really happen in practice, which prompts us to examine oversmoothing from an optimization point of view. In this paper, we analyze backward oversmoothing , that is, the notion that backpropagated errors used to compute gradients are also subject to oversmoothing from output to input. With non-linear activation functions, we outline the key role of the interaction between forward and backward smoothing. Moreover, we show that, due to backward oversmoothing, GNNs provably exhibit many spurious stationary points : as soon as the last layer is trained, the whole GNN is at a stationary point. As a result, we can exhibit regions where gradients are near-zero while the loss stays high. The proof relies on the fact that, unlike forward oversmoothing, backward errors are subjected to a linear oversmoothing even in the presence of non-linear activation function, such that the average of the output error plays a key role. Additionally, we show that this phenomenon is specific to deep GNNs, and exhibit counter-example Multi-Layer Perceptron. This paper is a step toward a more complete comprehension of the optimization landscape specific to GNNs.

While theoretical and empirical studies of GNNs form an extremely large literature, with e.g. significant progress in the understanding of GNN expressivity or generalization

Oversmoothing is rather easy to theoretically prove

Contribution.

In this paper, we thus look at oversmoothing from the

Our results rely on a precise characterization of the smoothed limit of the backward signal – which is generally unfeasible in forward oversmoothing due to the non-linearity

and it is slightly out of scope here. Besides, our results only apply to vanilla GNNs without the strategies that are typically used to address oversmoothing, which are left for future work. Instead, we explore important aspects of oversmoothing that were relatively ignored, and hope that it may give inspirations for further developments in the future.

Related work.

Theoretical optimization for deep learning is a vast field that is still riddled with open questions. Many existing results characterize the existence or absence of “bad” critical points that are local minima or saddle points

,
39
,

. Other works study the convergence to these stationary points

,

. In the specific case of GNNs, the authors in

proves global convergence of

). Overall, optimization for GNNs remains a relatively under-explored topic from a theoretical point of view
[
22
]
.

As mentioned above, oversmoothing is a long-standing problem in GNNs
[
17
,
24
]
, see
[
27
]
for a survey. Typical strategies to mitigate it include variants of skip connections
[
16
,
26
]
or normalization
[
41
]
. On the theoretical side, various measures of diversity of node representations have been proven to converge exponentially fast to zero
[
24
,
27
]
, see Thm.
1
. On the other hand, depth is also provably useful for GNNs
[
15
,
18
]
. Modern approaches to oversmoothing also relate it to

[

,
43
]
, where deep GNNs may lose information about distant neighborhoods if they are not wide enough. However we keep our focus on depth and oversmoothing here.

To our knowledge, there are not many works at the intersection of optimization and oversmoothing. Recently, in
[
43
]
, the authors relate oversmoothing to vanishing gradients, however: a) they assume Gaussian weights, and b) to our understanding, by assuming contracting Jacobians they place themselves in a case where
the forward signal itself converges to zero
with increasing layer index. This of course results in oversmoothing (a zero signal has zero Dirichlet energy), but is a somewhat strong hypothesis. We will rather look at a more general framework where the forward signal can oversmooth without necessarily converging to

itself. Closer to us, in
[
25

the authors introduce the idea of backward oversmoothing (calling it

) and show that the backpropagated error is oversmoothed from output to input. However, their work has two main limitations: a) it is only valid in the linear case (
\rho=Id
), and b) they do not explain why precisely having a smoothed backpropagated error is detrimental for optimization. We consider a general

instead, which allows us to uncover non-trivial

between forward and backward oversmoothing (Thm.
2
). We then use these results to formally prove the existence of spurious stationary points caused by oversmoothing, which we argue is the main reason why deep GNNs are hard to train.

Preliminaries

Notations.

The norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{2}
is the euclidean norm for vectors and spectral norm (max singular value) for matrices. The norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. The sign
\lesssim
is an upper bound up to a multiplicative constant that does not involve the graph size
n
or the number of layers
L
. For
A
and
B
of the same size,
A\odot B
is the Hadamard (element-wise) product.

Graphs.

In this paper, graphs of size
n
are represented by propagation matrices
P\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times n}
with non-negative coefficients. Of particular interest to us will be
symmetric bi-stochastic
matrices, that is,
P1=1
and
P
is symmetric. It is not so usual (e.g. the random walk matrix
D^{-1}A
is a typical stochastic propagation matrix, but it is not symmetric), but will be convenient. In decentralized optimization, such matrices are called
gossip
matrices
[
2
]
. A possible choice is
P=Id-(D-A)/d_{\max}
, where
A
is the adjacency matrix of the graph,
D
its diagonal matrix of degrees, and
d_{\max}
any constant larger than its maximum degree. Our main assumption on

will be the following.

Assumption 1

(Propagation matrix)
.

Assume that
P\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times n}
is symmetric and (bi-)stochastic:
P1_{n}=1_{n}
. Moreover, assume that
1
is a simple eigenvalue, and that all other eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
are such that
|\lambda_{i}|\leqslant\lambda<1
.

In other words, the eigenvector
u_{1}=\frac{1_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}
associated to the eigenvalue
1
is simple, and all other directions are shrinked by at least
\lambda<1
, which will naturally results in oversmoothing toward the constant vector
u_{1}
. For connected graph, this is generally satisfied: for instance, for the matrix
P
outlined above, the largest connected component of Cora
[
37
]
has
1-\lambda\approx 8.81\cdot 10^{-5}
, for that of Citeseer
[
37
]

1-\lambda\approx 6.58\cdot 10^{-5}
, and so on. For the Contextual Stochastic Block Model (CSBM)
[
6
]
synthetic data that we use in Fig.
1
and
2
, we have the more reasonable
1-\lambda\approx 0.16
.

GNN: forward.

Recall that the input node features are
X^{(0)}=X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{0}}
. We decompose the GNN (
1
) into the following notations:

\displaystyle F^{(k)}=PX^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{k}},

(message-passing)

\displaystyle H^{(k)}=F^{(k)}W^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{k+1}}

(weights multiplication)

\displaystyle X^{(k+1)}=\rho(H^{(k)}),

(activation function)

\displaystyle out=H^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{out}},

(output after
L
layers)

where
\rho
is a non-linear activation function applied element-wise. We call
F^{(k)}
the

, going “from” input (

) to output (
k=L
). We assume that the input node features are bounded
\left\|X^{(0)}_{i,:}\right\|_{2}\leqslant D_{\mathcal{X}}
. The following classical assumption encompasses most activation functions.

Assumption 2
.

The function
\rho
is
1
-Lipschitz, and
\left\lvert\rho(x)\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert x\right\rvert
.

Spectral norm of the weights.

A crucial ingredients in oversmoothing is the “contracting” vs. “expanding” nature of the weights
W^{(k)}
. This is generally quantified by
s_{k}=\|W^{(k)}\|_{2}
the leading singular value of
W^{(k)}
, or more simply the maximal
s=\max_{k}s_{k}
. Note that the
s_{k}
evolve over the optimization process, however here most of our results are expressed at a particular point in time and involve the “current”

Loss function.

We consider a loss function
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{out}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
that we aim to minimize. We classically assume that it can be decomposed into a sum over the graph nodes:

\mathcal{L}(H)=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(h_{i})

(2)

for some functions
\ell_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{out}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where the

are the rows of

. Note that transductive semi-supervised learning can be emulated by having some
2
2

2

Generally this may also involve replacing the normalization

\ell_{i}=0

Assumption 3

There are constants

\left\|\tfrac{\partial\ell_{i}(h)}{\partial h}\right\|_{2}\leqslant D_{\mathcal{L}}\left\|h\right\|_{2}+D^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}}

Our two main running examples will be regression with the Mean Square Error (MSE) loss, or classification with the Cross-Entropy loss. We show that they verify Assumption

Example 1

(Regression)
.

In the regression case, every node

has a label
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{out}}
in a bounded domain
\left\|y_{i}\right\|\leqslant 1
(w.l.o.g.), and

\ell_{i}(h)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|h-y_{i}\right\|^{2}

(3)

Assumption
3
is satisfied with

.

Example 2

(Classification)
.

In the classification case, every node
i
has a discrete label
y_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,C\}
, the output dimension is
d_{out}=C
, and

\ell_{i}(h)=-\log\left(e^{h_{y_{i}}}/\sum\nolimits_{c}e^{h_{c}}\right)

(4)

Assumption

GNN: backpropagation.

First-order optimization methods rely on gradients
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(k)}}
, which in deep learning are computed by backpropagation. For GNNs, the backpropagation equations are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(k)}}={F^{(k)}}^{\top}B^{(k)},

\displaystyle\text{where }B^{(k)}:=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial H^{(k)}}=\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)})\odot\left(P^{\top}B^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right)\text{ and }B^{(L)}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial H^{(L)}}

(5)

Note that
P^{\top}=P
in our case. We have written these equations here to emphasize the object
B^{(k)}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial H^{(k)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{k+1}}
that we call the
backward signal
3
3

3

It has several names in the literature, but here we emphasize its similarity to the forward signal.
. It satisfies a recursion equation, going “from” output (
k=L
) to input (
k=0
). Often, the initialization
B^{(L)}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial H^{(L)}}
at the last layer represents the predictive “error” of the GNN: for instance, for the MSE loss, it is directly
B^{(L)}=\frac{1}{n}(H^{(L)}-Y)
where
Y
contains the labels
y_{i}
as its rows.
The full gradient
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(k)}}={F^{(k)}}^{\top}B^{(k)}
is a product between the forward and the backward signal in the node dimension. Note that we do not perform mini-batching of nodes here
4
4

4

Indeed, batching is notoriously hard to define for node-tasks, as nodes are not independent. A GNN would typically require the
L

, is generally the whole graph.
and only consider full gradients.

Oversmoothing: forward and backward

As is usual in the literature
[
27
]
, we measure oversmoothing by the sum of the pairwise differences between nodes. For
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,

\mathcal{E}(X):=\left(\tfrac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum\nolimits_{i,j=1}^{n}\|X_{i,:}-X_{j,:}\|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(6)

Note that oversmoothing is sometimes measured by the
Dirichlet energy
, where pairwise distances are considered only along the edges of the graph. However for connected graphs the two definitions are within a (graph-dependent) multiplicative constant of each other, and we find this choice more convenient to work with in our framework.

3.1
Forward oversmoothing

We recall the classical “forward” oversmoothing result
[
24
]
with our notations here.

Theorem 1

(Forward oversmoothing
[
24
]
)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
we have

\mathcal{E}(X^{(k)})\leqslant(\lambda s)^{k}\mathcal{E}(X^{(0)})

(7)

In particular, if
s\leqslant\lambda^{-\alpha}
for
0\leqslant\alpha<1
, then

\mathcal{E}(X^{(k)})\leqslant\lambda^{(1-\alpha)k}\mathcal{E}(X^{(0)})
.

The proof of this theorem is quite straightforward and is included in App.
A.1
for completeness. It states that
\mathcal{E}(X^{(k)})
decreases exponentially fast with
k
, which is the classical rate of oversmoothing
[
27
]
(Fig.
2
, left). Note that with our hypotheses its initial value is bounded
\mathcal{E}(X^{(0)})\leqslant D_{\mathcal{X}}
.

Expansion.

We see that the main condition for oversmoothing is
s\lambda<1
. This authorizes
some
expansion
s>1
, but
s
is still bounded. In the rest of the paper, we will put an emphasis on “how much” expansion is authorized, as we believe that this quantifies how much the weights should deviate from their initial value to escape the oversmoothing regime.
More in details, we will consider rates of the form
s\leqslant\lambda^{-\alpha}
for some
expansion rate

\alpha\leqslant 1
. As
\alpha
decreases, this decreases the range of authorized
s
above
1
, up until
\alpha=0
and
s\leqslant 1
. As remarked in the introduction, if at some point during training
s>1/\lambda
, then the GNN does not necessarily oversmooth
[
9
]
. Hence the GNN could learn to
not
oversmooth
[
36
]
, but this does not really happen in practice (Fig.
1
) hence the need to examine the optimization process in more details.

Linear vs. non-linear oversmoothing.

Remark that while
X^{(k)}
converges to a constant vector across nodes, due to the presence of the non-linear activation function
\rho
it is generally difficult to explicitly characterize
what
this limit constant vector is (although some fixed point theorems may apply in certain circumstances
[
43
]
). This is one of the reasons why many theoretical analyses of oversmoothing focus on linear GNNs
\rho=Id
, which greatly simplifies the scenario
[
15
,
25
]
. In this case, the forward signal become closer and closer to to
X^{(k)}\approx\frac{1_{n\times n}}{n}X^{(0)}W_{1}\ldots W_{k}
, which involves the
average of the input features
across nodes
\frac{1_{n}^{\top}X^{(0)}}{n}
. As we will see later, this intuition plays a crucial role in backward oversmoothing.

3.2
Backward oversmoothing

Due to the multiplication by
P^{\top}=P
from the output layer
k=L
to the input
k=1
, the backward signal will naturally also be oversmoothed. This has been showed in the linear case
[
36
]
. In the general case, we need the following.

Assumption 4
.

Assume that
\rho^{\prime}
is

-Lipschitz.

Note that this excludes vanilla ReLU (which is not fully differentiable anyway) but includes any smoothed variants like softplus

]
. The following result is proved in App.

.

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumptions
1
to
4
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(B^{(k)})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{D_{\mathcal{X}}D_{\mathcal{L}}s^{L+1}+D^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}}}{n}\left(\frac{D_{\rho}s^{L+1}}{1-\lambda^{2}s}\lambda^{k+1}+(\lambda s)^{L-k}\right)

.

The bound on
\mathcal{E}(B^{(k)})
involves two terms: one that decreases exponentially in
k
, the other one in
L-k
. Hence backward oversmoothing happens
at the middle layers
(Fig.
2
, right), when
both

and

are high. This is due to the term
\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)})
in the backpropagation equations (

) which modulates the rows of

When the forward signal is oversmoothed
(high

. It leads to backward oversmoothing after sufficiently many iterations (high

). On the contrary, at low

, the modulation

can be high. Hence, compared to the linear case

]
, it is the

between forward and backward smoothing that leads to backward oversmoothing, at the middle layers.

The previous theorem describe a quite non-trivial relationship between
\lambda
and

, making it difficult to assess the effects of the expansion rate

Corollary 1
.

Take

for a regression problem with the MSE loss, or

for a classification problem with the cross-entropy loss. Under Assumptions
1
to
4
, if
s\leqslant\lambda^{-\alpha}
where
0\leqslant\alpha<1-\sqrt{1-1/q}
, then for all

,

\mathcal{E}\left(B^{(\beta L)}\right)\lesssim\tfrac{1}{n}\left(\lambda^{(\beta-q\alpha)L}+\lambda^{(1-q\alpha-(1-\alpha)\beta)L}\right)\xrightarrow[L\to\infty]{}0

(8)

In other words, taking the layer index

proportional to the depth
L
, with sufficiently low

we obtain an explicit exponential rate. The level

is more permissive in the classification case, since

. Note that, if
s\leqslant 1
, we obtain that for all

,
\mathcal{E}\left(B^{(\beta L)}\right)\lesssim\frac{1}{n}(\lambda^{\beta L}+\lambda^{(1-\beta)L})
.

Backward oversmoothing is not (necessarily) vanishing gradients.

While one might feel that backward oversmoothing is detrimental for GNN training, it is not clear exactly why this is true. A first idea would be to relate backward oversmoothing to
vanishing gradients
: if the pairwise differences
\mathcal{E}(B^{(k)})
vanish, then maybe the
norm

\left\|B^{(k)}\right\|
vanish altogether, as was shown in
[
43
]
in very specific cases. However, this is not true in general, and we prove the following in App.
A.5
.

Proposition 1
.

For all
L
and
n
, there is a GNN that satisfy assumptions
1
to
4
with
s\leqslant 1
such that for all
k
:

\left\|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W^{(k)}\right\|_{F}=1

(9)

That is, we can have both backward oversmoothing but with arbitrarily high gradients (the constant
1
is arbitrary here). Hence the answer is not vanishing gradients, at least not automatically. In the next section, we relate backward oversmoothing with stationary points.

4
Backward oversmoothing and spurious stationary points

While backward oversmoothing is not directly responsible for vanishing gradients, in practice we observe the following when training deep GNNs (Fig.
1
, left): the norms of the gradients will quickly decrease to a very small value in the few first iterations, then get stuck and remain constant. This points us to the following hypothesis, which we analyze in this section: after a few iterations, deep GNNs reach a
stationary point
. Note that this does not seem to necessarily happen for MLPs (Fig.
1
, center). We first introduce the following definition for near-stationary points
[
29
,
12
]
.

Definition 1

(
\delta
-stationary point)
.

The weight
W^{(k)}
is said to be at a
\delta
-stationary point
if

\left\|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W^{(k)}\right\|_{F}\leqslant\delta

(10)

If this is true for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant L
, then the GNN is said to be at a
global
\delta
-stationary point
.

Note that, in terms of vocabulary, we somewhat distinguish the notion of stationary points with that of
vanishing gradient
, even for small
\delta
. Indeed, “vanishing gradients” often refers to the fact that gradient amplitudes may have vastly
different
orders of magnitude across layers, and is generally understood to be a strictly detrimental phenomenon. On the contrary, stationary points can be detrimental -- e.g., spurious local minima
5
5

5

Note here that the term “spurious” is used loosely, as it is generally admitted that in deep learning local minima may generalize better than an elusive global minimum.
, saddle points – or simply the sign of successful training: after all, characterizing the rates of convergence to near-stationary points is the main goal of theoretical non-convex optimization
[
29
,
12
]
.

We now turn to the main result of this paper, which will exists in two main flavors, depending on our assumptions. For convenience we define the function

\xi_{q}(\alpha)=\tfrac{1-(2q+1)\alpha+q\alpha^{2}}{2(1-\alpha)}

(11)

and note that
\xi_{q}(\alpha)>0
for
\alpha\leqslant 1+\frac{1}{2q}-\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{4q^{2}}}
, which is roughly equal to
0.38
for
q=1
and
0.21
for
q=2
. The following theorem is proved in App.
A.7
.

Theorem 3

(A stationary output layer is a global stationary point)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
to
4
hold. Assume that
the parameter
W^{(L)}
is at a
\bar{\delta}
-stationary point
.

1.

Taking
q=2
for regression or
q=1
for classification, suppose that
s\leqslant\lambda^{-\alpha}
, with
\xi_{q}(\alpha)>0
.
Assume that the output forward signal is lower-bounded
:
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|F^{(L)}\right\|_{F}\geqslant D_{F}
. For
\delta>0
, if

L\gtrsim\frac{\log(1/(D_{F}\delta))}{\xi_{q}(\alpha)\log(1/\lambda)}\quad\text{ and }\quad\bar{\delta}\lesssim\lambda^{\alpha L}D_{F}\delta

(12)

then
the GNN is at a global
\delta
-stationary point
.

2.

Alternatively, for
\delta>0
:

(a)

in the regression case, assume that the node values
y_{i}
are drawn i.i.d. from a
centered bounded
distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, i.e. such that
\mathbb{E}y=0
and
\left\|y\right\|\leqslant 1
almost surely. Suppose that
s\leqslant\lambda^{-\alpha}
such that
\xi_{2}(\alpha)>0
, and

L\gtrsim\tfrac{\log(1/\delta)}{\xi_{2}(\alpha)\log(1/\lambda)}

(b)

in the classification case, assume that the node labels
y_{i}
are drawn iid
uniformly
from
\{1,\ldots,C\}
. Suppose that
s\leqslant\lambda^{-\alpha}
, with
\alpha<\frac{1}{3}
, and

L\gtrsim\tfrac{\log(1/\delta))}{(1-3\alpha)\log(1/\lambda)}

for
\nu>0
, if

n\gtrsim\delta^{-2}\lambda^{-2\alpha L}\log(1/\nu)\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\delta}\lesssim\lambda^{2\alpha L}\delta^{2}

(13)

then, with probability at least
1-\nu
,
the GNN is at a global
\delta
-stationary point
.

This theorem show that, for sufficiently deep GNNs, under some mild assumptions,
every
\bar{\delta}
-stationary point
for the output layer

L
is also a
global

\delta
-stationary point
, for some small
\delta
related to
\bar{\delta}
and
L
. In other words, as soon as the last layer of the GNN is “trained”, then gradients vanish
at every layers
and the GNN cannot train anymore. As one might guess, the last (linear) layer is exceedingly easy to train, this generally happens in the first few iterations (Fig.
1
). Note that, while backward oversmoothing is the underlying mechanism for this result as we will explain next,
\delta
-stationarity happens
at every layers
, not just the middle or last ones.

Theorem
3
exists in two main flavors. Either we assume that the output layer
F^{(L)}
is bounded away from zero – otherwise the output
H^{(L)}=F^{(L)}W^{(L)}
is always zero and
W^{(L)}
does not play any role. Or, without this assumption, we might suppose instead that the problem is “balanced”, that is, centered distribution for regression or balanced classes for classification, and obtain the result in a probabilistic version. This gives rise to different rates, as well as different conditions on
\bar{\delta}
.

Spurious stationary point.

To complete the previous result, we might now ask: is it possible to have a global
\delta
-stationary point, for an arbitrary high loss? As one might expect, the answer is yes. We give an example below, proved in App.
A.7
.

Corollary 2

(Spurious stationary points)
.

There is an activation function
\rho
that satisfy Assumptions
2
and
4
, and a joint distribution
\mathcal{P}
on
(x,y)
where the marginal distribution on
y
is centered bounded with a variance
\sigma_{y}^{2}
, such that the following holds.
Considering the regression case, assuming that the
(X^{(0)}_{i,:},y_{i})
are drawn iid from
\mathcal{P}
, and that
s\leqslant\lambda^{-\alpha}
with
\xi_{2}(\alpha)>0
then for all
\delta>0
, if

L\gtrsim\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\xi_{2}(\alpha)\log(1/\lambda)},~n\gtrsim\delta^{-2}\lambda^{-2\alpha L}\log(1/\nu)
, then with probability at least
1-\nu
, there exists a
global
\delta
-stationary point
such that

\mathcal{L}(H^{(L)})\geqslant\sigma_{y}^{2}-\delta

(14)

In other words, there is a family of GNNs and a regression problem that satisfy all the Assumptions of the paper, such that any sufficiently deep GNN can have
\delta
-stationary points
with a loss close to the variance of the labels
.
Moreover, remark that, since the conditions
s<\lambda^{-\alpha}
and
\xi_{2}(\alpha)>0
define open sets, there is in fact a
non-empty open set
of parameters
\{W^{(1)},\ldots,W^{(L)}\}
that have near-zero gradients and high loss. Informally, it will then be difficult for a first-order optimization to escape from such a bad critical region. Note however that we do not show that such an open set contains exact local minima or saddle points, just that it is a bad
flat
region.

GNNs are not MLPs.

It is known, both in theory and in practice, that the landscape of classical MultiLayer Perceptrons is riddled with local minima and saddle points
[
33
,
42
,
7
,
39
]
. As a final remark on our results, we show that the near-stationary points that we characterize in this paper are indeed specific to GNNs.

Proposition 2

(MLPs are not GNNs)
.

Consider a regression problem, in the MLP case
P=Id
. There is a centered bounded distribution of labels
y_{i}
, such that the following holds. For all depth
L>0
, there is an MLP that satisfy Assumptions
2
to
4
, such that
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(L)}}=0
,
s\leqslant 1
, but there exists
k
such that

\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(k)}}\right\|=1

(15)

with probability
1
.

This proposition contradicts Thm.
3
for MLPs. Hence, our results are indeed not another characterization of existing bad critical points in deep learning, but are instead specific to deep GNNs.

Sketch of proof.

We finish this paper by a sketch of proof of Theorem
3
, as we consider that it provides interesting intuitions. The full proof can be found in App.
A.6
.

In forward oversmoothing, the signal
X^{(k)}
converges to a constant node representation when
k
increases. As mentioned in the previous section, in the linear case
\rho=Id
it is easy to show
[
15
]
that it converges to the
average
of the input signal
X^{(k)}\approx 1_{n}\left(\frac{1_{n}^{\top}X^{(0)}}{n}\right)W^{(0)}\ldots W^{(k-1)}
. On the contrary, in the non-linear case, the limit does not have a simple characterization in general. The situation is different for
backward
oversmoothing
B^{(k)}=\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)})\odot\left(P^{\top}B^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right)
.
When the forward signal is oversmoothed
(near the output layer), the term
\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)})
is approximately constant, and
the smoothing equation is approximately linear

B^{(k)}\approx cP^{\top}B^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}
for some scalar
c
, even with general
\rho
. This major difference between forward and backward oversmoothing is a key point of our analysis: to some extent, we
can
compute the limit representation of the backward oversmoothed signal, and it relates to the
average
of the output error: at the middle layers,
B^{(k)}\approx c1_{n}\left(\frac{1_{n}^{\top}B^{(L)}}{n}\right)(W_{L}\ldots W_{k+1})^{\top}
. We thus obtain the following theorem, which is the core of the proof of Thm
3
.

Theorem 4
.

Take
q=2
for a regression problem, or
q=1
for a classification problem. Under Assumptions
1
to
4
, if
s\leqslant\lambda^{-\alpha}
with
\xi_{q}(\alpha)>0
, the GNN is at a global
\delta
-stationary point, with

\delta\lesssim\lambda^{\xi_{q}(\alpha)L}+\lambda^{-\alpha L}\left\|1_{n}^{\top}B^{(L)}\right\|_{2}

(16)

The key component here is the norm of
1_{n}^{\top}B^{(L)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\ell_{i}(h_{i})}{\partial h_{i}}
, which is indeed the
average
of the output error of the GNN. One notable aspect of this result is that it applies
to every layer

k
, even though oversmoothing is the underlying phenomenon. This is due to several mechanisms acting on the gradients
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W^{(k)}={F^{(k)}}^{\top}B^{(k)}
:

1.

In the last layers:
the forward signal is oversmoothed and approximately constant
F^{(k)}\approx 1_{n}v^{\top}
for some
v
, and thus the dot product
{F^{(k)}}^{\top}B^{(k)}\approx v1_{n}^{\top}B^{(k)}
directly relates to the average of
B^{(k)}
. Since
\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)})
is also approximately constant, the recursive equation (
5
) to compute
B^{(k)}
is simplified, and the average of
B^{(k)}
relates to that of
B^{(L)}

2.

In the middle layers:
backward oversmoothing kicks in, and as described above it is
similar to the linear regime
:
B^{(k)}\approx c1_{n}\left(\frac{1_{n}^{\top}B^{(L)}}{n}\right)(W_{L}\ldots W_{k+1})^{\top}
accumulates around the average of
B^{(L)}

3.

In the first layers:
the norm of the backward signal does not grow much from that of the middle layers, so we can conclude.

To conclude with the proof of Theorem
3
, we then bound the average output error
\left\|(B^{(L)})^{\top}1_{n}\right\|_{2}
when the last layer is at a stationary point: since the output of the GNN is almost constant due to forward oversmoothing, when the last layer is trained the average error automatically vanish.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we explored the links between oversmoothing and optimization. Our major finding is that deep GNNs possesses many
spurious near-stationary points
, that are a) easy-to-reach: as soon as the last layer is trained; and b) fully specific to GNNs: we exhibit a counter-example MLP. The underlying mechanism is
backward oversmoothing
, that is, the backward errors are also prone to oversmoothing. Two key observations on backward oversmoothing are that: a) for non-linear GNNs, it
interacts
in non-trivial ways with forward oversmoothing, and b) when the forward signal is also oversmoothed, the recursive iterations on the backward signal are almost linear, meaning that we can fully
characterize the constant oversmoothed limit
, despite the non-linearity
\rho
.

Outlooks.

An important limitation of our results is that they only apply to vanilla GNNs, without the common fixes to oversmoothing: skip connections, normalization, and so on. A direct extension would be to examine to which extent these fixes also act on the optimization side, and what are the remaining limitations. Nevertheless, our results may help design better optimization algorithms for GNNs, that
directly
compensate for spurious flat regions without necessarily changing the underlying architecture by adding skip connections, normalization, and so on.

More generally, given the relative scarcity of theoretical works on optimization for GNNs
[
23
]
, these results are only a first step towards a better comprehension of this fundamental field. As mentioned above, a major characteristic of our results and intuitions is that
they are specific to GNNs
and do not apply
at all
to regular MLPs, which is not so common. An important path for future work would be to identify more fundamental differences between existing results for MLPs
[
38
,
39
,
42
,
33
,
7
]
and their GNN counterpart, beyond simple adaptations.

\displaystyle\lesssim s\left\|v\right\|\varepsilon_{n}+\varepsilon_{n}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim s\left(\delta+(D_{\mathcal{L}}D_{\mathcal{X}}s^{2L+1}+D^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}}s^{L})\lambda^{L+1}D_{\mathcal{X}}\right)+\varepsilon_{n}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}

By solving the order-
2
inequality, this leads to

\varepsilon_{n}\lesssim\sqrt{\delta+(D_{\mathcal{L}}D_{\mathcal{X}}s^{2L+1}+D^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}}s^{L})\lambda^{L+1}D_{\mathcal{X}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}

Combined with Thm
4
, the GNN is at a global
\delta
-stationary point, with

\delta\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{1-5\alpha+2\alpha^{2}}{2(1-\alpha)}L}+\lambda^{(1/2-2\alpha)L}+\lambda^{-\alpha L}\left(\sqrt{\delta}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}\right)

where the multiplicative constant does not depend on
L
or
n
. A few computations show that
\xi_{2}(\alpha)=\frac{1-5\alpha+2\alpha^{2}}{2(1-\alpha)}\leqslant 1/2-2\alpha
, We thus get

\delta\lesssim\lambda^{\xi_{2}(\alpha)L}+\lambda^{-\alpha L}\sqrt{\delta}

(28)

Second case: classification

Let
SM(h)=\left[\frac{e^{h_{j}}}{\sum_{c}e^{h_{c}}}\right]_{j}
be the softmax function. In the classification case, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}SM(H^{(L)}_{i,:})-e_{y_{i}}\right\|\leqslant\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}SM(H^{(L)}_{i,:})-\frac{1_{C}}{C}\right\|+\left\|\frac{1_{C}}{C}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}e_{y_{i}}\right\|

(29)

Since the
y_{i}
are uniformly distributed among all the classes,
\mathbb{E}e_{y}=1_{C}/C
and by Hoeffdings inequality

\left\|\frac{1_{C}}{C}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}e_{y_{i}}\right\|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}

Since the softmax function is
1
-Lipschitz and
1_{C}/C=SM(0_{C})
, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}SM(H^{(L)}_{i,:})-\frac{1_{C}}{C}\right\|+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left\|H^{(L)}_{i,:}\right\|+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|H^{(L)}\right\|_{F}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\left\|JH^{(L)}\right\|_{F}+\left\|J^{\perp}H^{(L)}\right\|_{F}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n}1_{n}^{\top}H^{(L)}\right\|_{2}+\mathcal{E}(H^{(L)})+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant s\left\|v\right\|_{2}+\mathcal{E}(H^{(L)})+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant s\left\|v\right\|_{2}+s^{L+1}\lambda^{L}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim s\left(\delta+(D_{\mathcal{L}}D_{\mathcal{X}}s^{2L+1}+D^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}}s^{L})\lambda^{L+1}D_{\mathcal{X}}\right)+\varepsilon_{n}\left(s^{L+1}\lambda^{L}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\nu)}{n}}\right)

The additional term
s^{L}\lambda^{L}
is negligible. We thus obtain the same result as in the regression case when combined with Thm
4
:

\delta\leqslant\lambda^{\frac{1-3\alpha+\alpha^{2}}{2(1-\alpha)}L}+\lambda^{(1-3\alpha)L/2}+\lambda^{-\alpha L}\sqrt{\delta}

(30)

Here for
q=1
however, the condition
\alpha\leqslant 1/3
is stronger, and
\lambda^{(1-3\alpha)L/2}
becomes the dominant term.
∎

y

A.9
Proof of Thm
4

Proof of Thm
4
.

Define

\varepsilon^{(k)}_{n}=\left\|1_{n}^{\top}B^{(k)}\right\|_{2}

(33)

and
\varepsilon_{n}=\varepsilon_{n}^{(L)}
. We divide the proof in several steps.

Last layers.

In the last layers, the bound is due to the forward oversmoothing: since the forward signal is almost constant, the gradient
F^{\top}B
is almost the average of the backward signal, which is directly related to
\varepsilon_{n}
. We have

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{(k)}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left\|1_{n}^{\top}B^{(k)}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|1_{n}^{\top}\left(\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)})\odot\left(PB^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right)\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|1_{n}^{\top}\left((J\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)}))\odot\left(PB^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right)\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|1_{n}^{\top}\left((J^{\perp}\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)}))\odot\left(PB^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right)\right)\right\|_{2}

By Lemma
1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|1_{n}^{\top}\left((J\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)}))\odot\left(PB^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right)\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)})\right\|_{\infty}\left\|W^{(k+1)}(B^{(k+1)})^{\top}P1_{n}\right\|_{2}

and since
P1=1
and
\rho^{\prime}(\cdot)\leqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\left\|1_{n}^{\top}\left((J\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)}))\odot\left(PB^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right)\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant s_{k+1}\varepsilon_{n}^{(k+1)}

Similarly, by Lemma
1
and by the same computations as in the proof of Thm
2
:

\displaystyle\left\|1_{n}^{\top}\left((J^{\perp}\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)}))\odot\left(PB^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right)\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{n}\left\|PB^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|J^{\perp}\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)})\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|PB^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right\|_{\infty}n\mathcal{E}(\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)}))

\displaystyle\leqslant D_{\rho}\mathcal{E}(X^{(0)})A_{L}\lambda^{k+1}

Hence, by the same computation as in the proof of Cor
1

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{(k)}_{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant D_{\rho}\mathcal{E}(X^{(0)})A_{L}\sum_{\ell=k}^{L-1}\lambda^{2\ell+1-k}s^{(k+1:\ell+1)}+s^{(k+1:L+1)}\varepsilon_{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{D_{\rho}\mathcal{E}(X^{(0)})A_{L}\lambda^{k+1}}{1-\lambda^{2}s}+s^{L-k}\varepsilon_{n}

From this we get, using
\left\|F^{\top}1_{n}/n\right\|_{F}\leqslant\left\|F\right\|_{2,\infty}
and
\left\|B\right\|_{F}/\sqrt{n}\leqslant\left\|B\right\|_{2,\infty}
, Lemma
3
and
2
,

\displaystyle\left\|{F^{(k)}}^{\top}B^{(k)}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|(JF^{(k)})^{\top}B^{(k)}\right\|_{F}+\left\|(J^{\perp}F^{(k)})^{\top}B^{(k)}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{{F^{(k)}}^{\top}1_{n}}{n}\cdot 1_{n}^{\top}B^{(k)}\right\|_{F}+\left\|B^{(k)}\right\|_{F}\sqrt{n}\mathcal{E}(F^{(k)})

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|F^{(k)}\right\|_{2,\infty}\varepsilon^{(k)}_{n}+n\left\|B^{(k)}\right\|_{2,\infty}\lambda^{k+1}s^{(k)}\mathcal{E}(X^{(0)})

\displaystyle\leqslant s^{(k)}D_{\mathcal{X}}\varepsilon^{(k)}_{n}+\frac{A_{L}}{s_{k}}\mathcal{E}(X^{(0)})\lambda^{k+1}

\displaystyle\leqslant D_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{D_{\rho}\mathcal{E}(X^{(0)})A_{L}\lambda^{k+1}s^{k}}{1-\lambda^{2}s}+s^{L}\varepsilon_{n}\right)+\frac{A_{L}}{s_{k}}\mathcal{E}(X^{(0)})\lambda^{k+1}

Since
\lambda s<1
, we obtain that: for all
\ell\geqslant k
,

\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial H^{(\ell)}}\right\|_{F}\leqslant D_{\mathcal{X}}(D_{\mathcal{X}}D_{\mathcal{L}}s^{2L+1}+D^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}}s^{L})\left(\frac{D_{\rho}D_{\mathcal{X}}}{1-\lambda^{2}s}(\lambda s)^{k+1}+\lambda^{k+1}\right)+D_{\mathcal{X}}s^{L}\varepsilon_{n}

(34)

First layers.

In the middle layers, due to forward but also backward oversmoothing, the norm of
B
itself is small. In the first layers, we simply relate the norm of the backward signal to that of the middle layers.

Considering
\left\|AB\right\|_{F}\leqslant\left\|A\right\|_{2}\left\|B\right\|_{F}
, and
\left\|\rho^{\prime}(A)\odot B\right\|_{F}\leqslant\left\|B\right\|_{F}
, and
\left\|P\right\|_{2}
, for
\ell<k
:

\displaystyle\left\|B^{(\ell)}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|\rho^{\prime}(H^{(\ell)})\odot\left(P^{\top}B^{(\ell+1)}(W^{(\ell+1)})^{\top}\right)\right\|_{F}\leqslant\left\|P\right\|_{2}\left\|B^{(\ell+1)}\right\|_{F}\left\|W^{(\ell+1)}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant s_{\ell+1}\left\|B^{(\ell+1)}\right\|_{F}\leqslant s^{k-\ell}\left\|B^{(k)}\right\|_{F}

Now,

\displaystyle\left\|B^{(k)}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|JB^{(k)}\right\|_{F}+\left\|J^{\perp}B^{(k)}\right\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\varepsilon_{n}^{(k)}+\sqrt{n}\mathcal{E}(B^{(k)})

and from this,

\displaystyle\left\|{F^{(\ell)}}^{\top}B^{(\ell)}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|{F^{(\ell)}}\right\|_{F}\left\|B^{(\ell)}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{n}s^{\ell}D_{\mathcal{X}}s^{k-\ell}\left\|B^{(k)}\right\|_{F}\leqslant D_{\mathcal{X}}s^{k}(\varepsilon_{n}^{(k)}+n\mathcal{E}(B^{(k)}))

\displaystyle\lesssim D_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\frac{D_{\rho}\mathcal{E}(X^{(0)})A_{L}\lambda^{k+1}s^{k}}{1-\lambda^{2}s}+s^{L}\varepsilon_{n}+\lambda^{L-k}A_{L}\right)

such that for all
\ell<k
,

\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial H^{(\ell)}}\right\|_{F}\leqslant D_{\mathcal{X}}A_{L}\left(\frac{D_{\rho}D_{\mathcal{X}}}{1-\lambda^{2}s}\lambda^{k+1}s^{k}+\lambda^{L-k}\right)+D_{\mathcal{X}}s^{L}\varepsilon_{n}

(35)

Combining (
34
) and (
35
), we obtain that for all
\ell
:

\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial H^{(\ell)}}\right\|_{F}\lesssim D_{\mathcal{X}}(D_{\mathcal{X}}D_{\mathcal{L}}s^{2L+1}+D^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}}s^{L})\left(\frac{D_{\rho}D_{\mathcal{X}}}{1-\lambda^{2}s}(\lambda s)^{k+1}+\lambda^{k+1}+s\lambda^{L-k}\right)+D_{\mathcal{X}}s^{L}\varepsilon_{n}

(36)

Now, taking
s\leqslant\lambda^{-\alpha}
and
k=\beta L
,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial H^{(\ell)}}\right\|_{F}\lesssim\lambda^{((1-\alpha)\beta-q\alpha)L}+\lambda^{(1-\beta-q\alpha)L}+\lambda^{-\alpha L}\varepsilon_{n}

The terms that do not depend on
\varepsilon_{n}
decrease with
L
when
\frac{q\alpha}{1-\alpha}<\beta<1-q\alpha
. This is possible when
0<1-(2q+1)\alpha+q\alpha^{2}
, which translates to having

\alpha<1+\frac{1}{2q}-\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{4q^{2}}}

(37)

which is equal to
\approx 0.38
for
q=1
and
\approx 0.21
for
q=2
. Choosing then
\beta=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{q\alpha}{1-\alpha}+1-q\alpha\right)
, we get the final rate

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial H^{(\ell)}}\right\|_{F}\lesssim\lambda^{\xi_{q}(\alpha)L}+\lambda^{-\alpha L}\varepsilon_{n}

∎

Appendix B
Technical Lemma

Lemma 1
.

For two matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
we have

\left\|(A\odot B)^{\top}1_{n}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\sqrt{n}\left\|B\right\|_{\infty}\left\|A\right\|_{F}

(38)

Moreover, denoting
J=1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}/n
,

\left\|((JA)\odot B)^{\top}1_{n}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\left\|A\right\|_{\infty}\left\|B^{\top}1_{n}\right\|_{2}

(39)

Proof.

Simply

\displaystyle\left\|(A\odot B)^{\top}1_{n}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}b_{ij}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{ij}^{2}\right)\leqslant n\left\|B\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\left\|A\right\|_{F}^{2}

and

\displaystyle\left\|((JA)\odot B)^{\top}1_{n}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k}a_{kj}\right)b_{ij}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k}a_{kj}\right)^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{ij}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|A\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\left\|B^{\top}1_{n}\right\|_{2}^{2}

∎

Lemma 2
.

We have

\left\|PX\right\|_{2,\infty}\leqslant\left\|X\right\|_{2,\infty}

(40)

and

\left\|XW\right\|_{2,\infty}\leqslant\left\|W\right\|_{2}\left\|X\right\|_{2,\infty}

(41)

Proof.

It is immediate that

\displaystyle\left\|PX\right\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{i}\left\|\sum_{j}P_{ij}X_{j,:}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|X\right\|_{2,\infty}\max_{i}\sum_{j}P_{ij}=\left\|X\right\|_{2,\infty}

Since the
P_{ij}
are non-negative and sum to
1
.

The second equation is immediate by

\left\|XW\right\|_{2,\infty}=\max_{i}\left\|WX_{i,:}^{\top}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\left\|W\right\|_{2}\left\|X\right\|_{2,\infty}

∎

Lemma 3
.

For all
k
we have:

\left\|X^{(k+1)}\right\|_{2,\infty}\leqslant s^{(k)}D_{\mathcal{X}}

(42)

and

\left\|B^{(k)}\right\|_{2,\infty}\leqslant\frac{1}{n}(S_{L}D_{\mathcal{X}}D_{\mathcal{L}}+D^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}})s^{(k+1:L+1)}

(43)

Proof.

By recursion, since
\left\lvert\rho(x)\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert x\right\rvert
and by Lemma
2
,

\displaystyle\left\|X^{(k+1)}\right\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\rho(PX^{(k)}W^{(k)})\right\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|PX^{(k)}W^{(k)}\right\|_{2,\infty}\leqslant s_{k}\left\|X^{(k)}\right\|_{2,\infty}\leqslant s^{(k)}D_{\mathcal{X}}

and similarly
\left\|H^{(k)}\right\|_{2,\infty}\leqslant s^{(k+1)}D_{\mathcal{X}}
.

In the same fashion, since
\rho^{\prime}\leqslant 1
and using Lemma
2
,

\displaystyle\left\|B^{(k)}\right\|_{2,\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\rho^{\prime}(H^{(k)})\odot\left(PB^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right)\right\|_{2,\infty}

(44)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|PB^{(k+1)}(W^{(k+1)})^{\top}\right\|_{2,\infty}

(46)

and
\left\|B^{(L)}\right\|_{2,\infty}\leqslant\frac{1}{n}(D_{\mathcal{L}}\left\|H^{(L)}\right\|_{2,\infty}+D^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}})\leqslant\frac{1}{n}(s^{(L+1)}D_{\mathcal{X}}D_{\mathcal{L}}+D^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}})
.
∎
