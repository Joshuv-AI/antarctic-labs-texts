Comments on “Low-Dose Morphine Intrathecal Analgesia in Elderly Patients with Hip Fracture Undergoing Single Spinal Anesthesia: A Randomized Controlled Trial” [Response to Letter]
Authors: Denis Buršík
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We thank Wenjuan Qiao and Jie Meng for their careful reading of our study and for raising intriguing methodological and interpretative points.1,2 After considering their comments, we respond to each point below.

We acknowledge that allocation by calendar week represents a cluster-like randomization structure. However, allocation weeks were randomly assigned using permuted-block randomization in a 1:1 ratio. The unequal group sizes resulted from variation in weekly enrollment, not from unequal allocation of weeks. This imbalance was anticipated a priori, and Welch’s t-test was prespecified for comparisons involving unequal group sizes or variances. To account for potential within-week clustering, we included allocation week as an additional random intercept in the linear mixed-effects model. The model showed that variability attributable to allocation week was minimal, suggesting a limited clustering effect related to the allocation procedure.2

We agree that our definition of respiratory depression was broad. This variability in definitions across studies also complicates comparison of its incidence.3 In addition to SpO2 <90% and tidal volume <4 mL/kg, we monitored respiratory rate <10/min, PvO2 <4.5 kPa, oxygen therapy, and naloxone administration, which may have led to overestimation, as acknowledged in the Discussion. The conclusion that ITM “did not increase” adverse effects was not supported by the study design. The study was not powered for non-inferiority or equivalence testing. Therefore, the findings indicate only that no statistically significant difference was detected.

The sample-size re-estimation was indeed not prespecified in the study protocol. However, it was performed in a blinded manner after 42 patients had been enrolled and was based solely on the observed variability (maximum SD 1.78), a minimal clinically important difference of 1.27 NRS points, and the current 2:3 allocation ratio, without examining treatment effects or between-group differences.4 The recalculated sample size of 68 was increased by 20% to 82 to account for potential dropout. As no interim efficacy testing was performed, no alpha-spending adjustment for repeated testing was required. Nevertheless, because the re-estimation was not prespecified, it cannot be regarded as a formally planned adaptive design with prospectively established type I error control.5

The CONSORT diagram accounts for all 82 randomized patients and explicitly documents the withdrawal of one patient from the control group due to failed spinal anaesthesia, as reported in the manuscript. Therefore, Table 1 correctly includes the 81 patients who were analyzed.

We would like to clarify that the adjusted p-values of 1.000 are not a consequence of an excessively conservative application of the Holm procedure or of rounding artefacts. The Holm procedure adjusts the p-values sequentially, while ensuring that the adjusted values are bounded above by 1.6 Consequently, when the unadjusted p-values are sufficiently large relative to the number of comparisons, the corresponding Holm-adjusted p-values can legitimately equal 1.000. This is a mathematical consequence of the adjustment procedure and does not imply that the underlying unadjusted p-values were exactly 1.000.

We agree that our original conclusion was too broad. ITM prolonged the time to first analgesia and reduced non-opioid analgesic use, whereas opioid consumption did not differ significantly between groups. However, opioid use was very low in both groups, limiting assessment of a potential opioid-sparing effect.

Accordingly, our conclusion should be interpreted as showing improved postoperative pain control, prolonged time to first analgesic administration, and reduced non-opioid analgesic requirements. No statistically detectable increase in adverse effects was observed, but the study was not designed to establish safety equivalence.
