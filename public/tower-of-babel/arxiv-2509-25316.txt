Topological non-Abelian Gauge Structures in Cayley-Schreier Lattices
Zoltán Guba, Robert-Jan Slager, Lavi K. Upreti, Tomáš Bzdušek

Abstract
Recently, novel crystalline constructions known as Cayley-Schreier lattices have been suggested as a platform for realizing arbitrary gauge fields in synthetic crystals with real hopping amplitudes. We show that Cayley-Schreier lattices can naturally give rise to implementable lattice systems that incorporate non-Abelian gauge structures transforming into a space-group symmetry. We show that the symmetry sectors can, moreover, be interpreted as blocks of spin models that can effectively realize a wealth of different topological invariants in a single setup. We underpin these general results with concrete models and show how they can be implemented in current experimental platforms. Our work sets the stage for a systematic investigation of topological insulators and metals with non-Abelian gauge structures.

Results

Cayley-Schreier lattices

We now review the concept of a Cayley-Schreier lattice (CSL).
The key insight here is that the conventional sites are replaced with “pillars” of internal degrees of freedom that transform under a general finite symmetry group
G
.
While our results can be directly generalized, we focus in particular on the case where this symmetry group entails the quaternion group
Q_{8}=\{\pm 1,\pm\textrm{i},\pm\textrm{j},\pm\textrm{k}\}
, where
i
,
j
, and
\textrm{k}=\textrm{i}\cdot\textrm{j}
are pairwise anticommuting imaginary units.
In the CSL construction, illustrated in Fig.
1
a
, each pillar (blue) consists of several levels, or ‘orbitals’ (black dots), that implement the elements of the group
G
.
Taking
Z_{2}=\{\pm 1\}
as an elementary example, each pillar has two orbitals, whereas when
G=Q_{8}
there are eight.
A hopping Hamiltonian is then formed by connecting the orbitals of different pillars, which constitute the bonds of the lattice that has a given space group (
\mathsf{SG}
).

Most importantly, these connections are chosen so that they respect the group multiplication in the following sense:
we assign a group element
a_{ij}\in G
to the bond from
j
to
i
and ensure that orbital
g_{j}
at site
j
is connected to orbital
a_{ij}\cdot g_{j}
at site
i
(the dot indicates the binary composition in
G
).
For the
Z_{2}
example, this yields two types of bonds: ‘horizontal’ for
a_{ij}=+1
and ‘swapped’ for
a_{ij}=-1
, both featured as dashed lines in Fig.
1
a
.
Several allowed connections
a_{ij}
between the pillars for the case
G=Q_{8}
are illustrated in Fig.
1
b
.
This construction ensures each orbital at site
i
is connected to a unique orbital at site
j
, with all the bonds representing the same hopping amplitude
t_{ij}
.
This simple resulting internal structure enables universal feasibility in meta-material implementations.

We point out that CSLs as considered in this work
extend the
earlier constructions of the same name
Lux and Prodan 2024
;
Marciani 2025
in two important aspects.
First, we do not require the entirety of the orbitals (i.e., all physical sites times the pillar content) to transform as elements of a single group, but merely that the construction respects a specified
\mathsf{SG}
in a sense that will be specified shortly.
Second—and crucially for the realization of topological models—we allow the hopping amplitudes
t_{ij}
to depend on the bond
ij
in a manner that preserves
\mathsf{SG}
.

Synthetic gauge structure

CSLs naturally give rise to synthetic gauge structures with the chosen group
G
, henceforth called the
gauge group
.
Specifically, we can permute the orbitals within a pillar as
g_{i}\mapsto\chi_{i}\cdot g_{i}=\tilde{g}_{i}
where
\chi_{i}\in G
is the gauge transformation at site
i
.
In the elementary case of gauge group
Z_{2}
, the non-trivial gauge transformation
\chi_{i}=-1
exchanges the two orbitals at site
i
, which results in a change of the connection on bonds adjacent to
i
(see lower two rows in Fig.
1
c
).
For a general choice of
G
and
\chi
, the connection transforms as

\tilde{a}_{ij}=\chi_{i}\cdot a_{ij}\cdot\chi_{j}^{-1}.

(1)

This gauge freedom allows us to reorder orbitals within a pillar and to apparently reshuffle the couplings between orbitals of adjacent pillars while still describing the same physical system.
Nevertheless, certain characteristics remain invariant in this process.
Specifically, the ‘integral’ of connection on a closed loop
\gamma
(assumed to be based at site
i
and passing through sites
j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}
), defined as

W_{\gamma}=a_{i,j_{n}}\cdot a_{j_{n},j_{n-1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot a_{j_{2},j_{1}}\cdot a_{j_{1},i},

(2)

transforms under a general choice of gauge transformation to
\tilde{W}_{\gamma}=\chi_{i}\cdot W_{\gamma}\cdot\chi_{i}^{-1}
.
It follows that the integral, called
Wilson loop
or
flux
on
\gamma
, is well-defined as a conjugacy class in the gauge group
G
.
(Note that we here slightly deviate from the standard lattice gauge theory nomenclature
Goldman et al. 2014
where these terms are instead reserved for the
trace
of
W_{\gamma}
.)
For Abelian groups like
Z_{2}
, each element of
G
constitutes its own conjugacy class.
For
Q_{8}
, one finds five conjugacy classes, namely
\{1\}
,
\{\pm\textrm{i}\}
,
\{\pm\textrm{j}\}
,
\{\pm\textrm{k}\}
and
\{-1\}
, which were previously related to non-trivial braiding of vortex defects in biaxial nematic liquids
Poenaru and Toulouse 1977
;
Volovik and Mineev 1977
;
Mermin 1979
and of band nodes in metals
Wu et al. 2019
;
Bouhon et al. 2020b
;
Bouhon et al. 2020a
;
Tiwari and Bzdušek 2020
;
Bouhon et al. 2021
;
Slager et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2021
.
This noncommutativity also plays a pivotal role when introducing concrete models with quaternion fluxes in the sections below.

Symmetry representations

The inherent presence of synthetic gauge structures implies that the space group symmetry of CSLs can be projectively represented.
With this, we mean that simply translating the pillar locations with symmetry
s\in\mathsf{SG}
from position vectors
{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}
to
s({\boldsymbol{r}}_{i})
may not preserve the arrangement of the connections; however, the connections could be restored if composing the geometric transformation with a gauge transformation
\chi
.
To illustrate the concept of projectively represented symmetry, consider the one-dimensional chain with
G=Z_{2}
and alternating connections
a_{j+1,j}=\pm 1
shown in Fig.
1
c
(top row).
Translation by one pillar (middle row) results in an exchange of the horizontal vs. swapped bonds; however, applying a gauge transformation
\chi_{i}=-1
at every second site (red rectangles) results in the restoration of the original connection (bottom row).
Projectively represented
\mathsf{SG}\textrm{s}
in lattices with synthetic
Z_{2}
gauge have been explored recently in one
Jiang et al. 2024
, two
Zhao et al. 2020
;
Xue et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Lai et al. 2024
;
Pu et al. 2023
, and three
Zhu et al. 2024
;
Teo et al. 2025
spatial dimensions, resulting in a non-trivial extension of band theory.

We find that going beyond
G=Z_{2}
introduces the additional option of
\textrm{Aut}(G)
-represented symmetry
.
Here,
\varphi\in\textrm{Aut}(G)
is an automorphism of
G
: an isomorphism of the group such that
\varphi(g_{1}\cdot g_{2})=\varphi(g_{1})\cdot\varphi(g_{2})
.
A non-trivial example for
Q_{8}
is the cyclic permutation of the imaginary units,

\varphi_{\textrm{cyclic}}:+\textrm{i}\mapsto+\textrm{j}\mapsto+\textrm{k}\mapsto+\textrm{i}.

(3)

[The complete characterization of
\textrm{Aut}(Q_{8})
is included in the Methods section.]
The key observation is that a global permutation of orbitals within all pillars with automorphism
\varphi
ensures that the transformed CSL maintains a synthetic gauge structure with connection valued in
G
.
Therefore, one can attempt to compose the real-space translations
s\in\mathsf{SG}
of the pillars with such a global permutation within the pillars to restore the original connection.
The possibility of
\textrm{Aut}(G)
-represented
\mathsf{SG}
constitutes a new avenue in band theory, and it naturally arises in the two models introduced in the next pages.

Peter-Weyl decomposition

Owing to the interpretation of the orbitals within each pillar as elements of the gauge group, the dynamics of a CSL decouples into several blocks.
To see this, let
\psi_{i}(g)
be the amplitude of a wave function at site
i
in orbital
g
.
Gauge transformation by
\chi_{i}
at site
i
acts on the amplitudes by so-called regular representation
Serre 1977

\rho(\chi_{i})
, corresponding to permutation matrices of dimension
\absolutevalue{G}
(the number of elements in
G
).
Standard result known as the Peter-Weyl theorem
Sun et al. 2024
;
Marciani 2025
ensures that the regular representation is reducible, with every irreducible representation (‘irrep’)
K
of the group appearing a number of times equal to its dimension
d_{K}
.
Specifically, the decomposition

U^{\dagger}\rho(\chi_{i})U=\bigoplus_{K}[D^{K}(\chi_{i})]^{\oplus d_{K}}

(4)

is achieved with the unitary
U
in Eq. (
10
) of the Methods section, with
D^{K}(\chi)
the matrix representation of
\chi
in irrep
K
.
We show in the Methods section that the connection
a_{ij}
, by virtue of its compatibility with the group structure, couples the same irrep
D^{K}
at various pillars.
It therefore follows that the unitary
U
results in block decomposition of the Hamiltonian of a CSL as well as of the Wilson operators in Eq. (
2
).

We investigate the implications of Eq. (
4
) for gauge group
Q_{8}
.
The quaternion group has five irreps: the trivial one-dimensional irrep (
A_{0}
; maps all elements to
+1
), three non-trivial one-dimensional irreps (
A_{x,y,z}
; each maps half of the elements to
+1
and the other half to
-1=e^{i\pi}
), and one two-dimensional irrep (
E
).
Higher-dimensional irreps are unique only up to unitary equivalence; here we specify the matrices
D^{E}(g)
as

\begin{split}D^{E}(\pm 1)=\pm\mathbb{1},\,\;&\;\;\;\;D^{E}(\pm\textrm{i})=\mp i\sigma_{x},\\
D^{E}(\pm\textrm{j})=\mp i\sigma_{y},&\,\;\;\;\;\;D^{E}(\pm\textrm{k})=\mp i\sigma_{z}\,,\end{split}

(5)

where
\sigma_{x,y,z}
are the Pauli matrices, and
\mathbb{1}
is the identity matrix.
The Hamiltonian therefore decouples into six sectors as
U^{\dagger}HU=\bigoplus_{K}[H^{K}]^{\oplus d_{K}}
with physical interpretation as follows.
Each of the sectors
H^{A_{0,x,y,z}}
supports a single (i.e., spinless) degree of freedom per pillar.
Due to the reduction of the Wilson operators, sectors
H^{A_{x,y,z}}
perceive an effective
U(1)
flux quantized to
\phi\in\{0,\pi\}
on every closed loop (thus replicating lattice structures with synthetic
Z_{2}
gauge
Zhao et al. 2020
;
Xue et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Lai et al. 2024
;
Pu et al. 2023
), while fluxes are absent in sector
H^{A_{0}}
.
Finally, the two-dimensional irrep
E
results in two identical sectors,
H^{E,1}=H^{E,2}\equiv H^{E}
, each supporting a two-level (i.e., spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
) degree of freedom per pillar.
In these sectors, the connection
a_{ij}
takes the values listed in Eq. (
5
), reminiscent of Rashba-type antisymmetric spin-orbit coupling for spinful electrons moving in a locally inversion-breaking environment
Kane and Mele 2005
;
Winkler 2003
;
Bauer and Sigrist 2012
.
In this work, we adopt an alternative interpretation as spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
particle moving in
SU(2)
gauge field whose flux on any closed loop is quantized to the right-hand sides of Eq. (
5
).

We thus find that a CSL with gauge group
Q_{8}
naturally encompasses models of spinless particles moving on a lattice with quantized flux
Z_{2}<U(1)
(where ‘
<
’ denotes a subgroup) as well as spinful particles with quantized flux
Q_{8}<SU(2)
.
By default, the energetics of these sectors overlap.
We present in the Supplementary Information File a possible approach to splitting the energetics of the sectors by decorating each site with an on-site ‘pillar Hamiltonian’.
As an experimentally practical alternative, we also discuss within the context of envisioned experimental simulations how a desired sector could be extracted even in the absence of the energy splitting through means of signal interference.
In the remainder of our work we therefore focus solely on the sector
H^{E}
as it accommodates a faithful representation of the quaternion group, realizing a synthetic gauge structure with non-Abelian gauge group.

(6)

Honeycomb model with quarternion fluxes

CSLs extend naturally to higher spatial dimensions.
To demonstrate this, we construct a model on the honeycomb lattice with a non-Abelian synthetic gauge field as shown in Fig.

The notation and the labeling follow that of Fig.

In the

Kane and Mele 2005

We probe the model’s band topology by evaluating the Kane-Mele invariant.
Setting

Owing to the presence of non-Abelian gauge structure, the model exhibits

Blueprint for electric-circuit realization

Experimental realization of CSLs with arbitrary gauge fields requires systems that can faithfully capture their inner structure.
Electric-circuit networks—assemblies of resistors, inductors, and capacitors—provide an ideal platform, even enabling lattice geometries impossible in natural materials.
Lenggenhager et al. 2022

The circuit Laplacian maps directly to tight-binding Hamiltonians

A particular advantage lies in controlling the boundary condition: direct edge wiring implements periodic boundaries whereas grounding of the edge sites creates open boundaries, with the specific choice potentially controllable within a single platform through a switch.

Alternatively, variable impedance elements based on operational amplifiers

Discussion

We show that Cayley-Schreier lattices allow one to construct crystalline structures with an underlying space group while naturally incorporating non-Abelian fluxes.
In addition, these models can be decomposed into sectors that correspond to spin-
N
particles coupled to quantized
U(2N+1)
fluxes, with
N
set by the dimension of the sector.
Each of these spin models can, in principle, realize any kind of band topology within a single setup.
We demonstrate this by formulating representative models in one and two spatial dimensions.
Due to the simple internal structure of CSLs, the construction of these models in fact only requires elementary hopping terms, making them suitable for meta-material realizations as we also detail.

The above results set the stage for multifold follow-up directions, both theoretically and experimentally.
In this regard, an immediate pursuit is to realize the presented models, as well as generalizations thereof, in electric-circuit setups or other meta-material platforms along the specific details outlined.
From a theoretical angle, our work invites a broad range of generalizations.
In particular, our results are directly generalizable to more involved gauge structures to encode higher spin models.
Moreover, the presented models call for a systematic characterization of non-Abelian gauge structures within this setup even if narrowing attention to the quaternion group:
to enumerate the symmetry-compatible synthetic gauge structures for all wallpaper groups and space groups on the one hand, and to classify topological bands in every such gauge arrangement on the other.
That CSLs with non-Abelian fluxes may harbor novel kinds of band topology is hinted at by the necessity to involve projectively and
\textrm{Aut}(G)
-represented symmetries.

Finally, our work also opens up new avenues with regard to deeper structural questions.
Indeed, the CSL construction invites refined mathematical methods to capture non-Abelian synthetic gauge structures and CSLs, because conventional methods such as group cohomology evaluations
Chen et al. 2013
;
Alexandradinata et al. 2016
are specific to Abelian gauge groups.
Additionally, such analyses do not include symmetries generated by automorphisms of the gauge group, suggesting that a deeper framework is at play.
Not in the least place, taking the non-Abelian CSL identified so far and considering quantum and correlated phases as well as disorder in this new geometry, in which case all sectors interplay, sets yet another intriguing direction to consider.

Methods

Automorphisms of the quaternion group

We here provide further elucidation of
\textrm{Aut}(G)
-represented symmetries.
Since two automorphisms
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
of a group
G
can be composed,
\textrm{Aut}(G)
is itself a group.
All automorphisms preserve the conjugacy class
\{+1\}
of the identity element; in addition, they can only relate conjugacy classes of the same order, implying that
\varphi(-1)=-1
is also invariant.
It follows that there are
24
automorphisms of
Q_{8}
, corresponding to the six permutations of conjugacy classes
\{\pm\textrm{i}\}
,
\{\pm\textrm{j}\}
,
\{\pm\textrm{k}\}
times the four signs of
\varphi(+\textrm{i})
and
\varphi(+\textrm{j})
[with the third sign fixed by
\varphi(+\textrm{i})\cdot\varphi(+\textrm{j})=\varphi(+\textrm{k})
].
A careful analysis reveals that
\textrm{Aut}(Q_{8})=S_{4}
(the permutation group of four elements).

The group
\textrm{Aut}(G)
can be understood as a product of two components: inner automorphism
\textrm{Inn}(G)
and outer automorphisms
\textrm{Out}(G)
.
Specifically, elements
\varphi^{(h)}\in\textrm{Inn}(G)
correspond to conjugation of elements in
G
with a fixed element
h\in G
, i.e.,

\varphi^{(h)}:g\mapsto h\cdot g\cdot h^{-1}.

(8)

For the quaternion group, such conjugations can at best result in a sign flip of the imaginary units; therefore,
\textrm{Inn}(Q_{8})=Z_{2}\times Z_{2}
is the Klein four-group, with the two factors interpreted as the signs of
\varphi(+\textrm{i})
and
\varphi(+\textrm{j})
.
However, the interpretation of inner automorphisms per Eq. (
8
) implies that, within the CSL framework, they are equivalent to performing a global gauge transformation with
\chi_{i}=h
.
Therefore,
\textrm{Inn}(G)
does not extend the theoretical framework for representing space group symmetries beyond projective representations.

The second component of
\textrm{Aut}(G)
is obtained by observing that
\textrm{Inn}(G)
constitutes a normal subgroup; therefore, it is possible to define the quotient
\textrm{Aut}(G)/\textrm{Inn}(G)=\textrm{Out}(G)
.
For quaternion group,
\textrm{Out}(G)=S_{3}
corresponds to the permutation of the conjugacy classes of the imaginary units.
The adjective ‘outer’ suggests that each of these further automorphisms can be understood as a conjugation with some element
outside
of
G
.
Specifically for
Q_{8}
, assuming its embedding inside
SU(2)
according to Eq. (
5
), every
\varphi\in\textrm{Out}(G)
can be interpreted as a conjugation with certain unitaries in the complement
SU(2)\backslash Q_{8}
.
The involvement of transformations outside the gauge group goes beyond the usual formalism of projectively represented symmetries.
We refer to symmetries that require a composition with an outer automorphism as being
\textrm{Aut}(G)
-represented.
We leave the systematic characterization of
\textrm{Aut}(G)
-represented space groups to future studies.

Block-diagonalization of CSL Hamiltonians

The Peter-Weyl decomposition of CSL Hamiltonians is achieved via a unitary rotation in the orbital basis.
The original basis, where each basis vector corresponds to a group element
g\in G
, is transformed to a basis labeled by the irreducible representations of the gauge group.
Following Ref.
42
, the transformation is defined as

\left[\tilde{\psi}^{\dagger}\right]_{\mu}=\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{\mu}=\sum_{g}U_{g,\mu}c^{\dagger}_{g}=\sum_{g}U_{g,\mu}\left[\psi^{\dagger}\right]_{g},

(9)

where the
\left[\tilde{\psi}\right]_{\mu}=\tilde{c}_{\mu}
are components of the annihilation operator vector in the irrep basis and
U_{g,\mu}

are the components of the unitary matrix of the basis transformation.
We introduced
\mu
as a composite index representing the different
(K,\lambda,\nu)
tuples, where
K
denotes an irreducible representation of
G
,
\lambda
enumerates the occurrence of
K
in the decomposition of the regular representation, and
\nu\in{1,\dots,d_{K}}
labels the basis states within the irrep of dimension
d_{K}
.
In this way, Eq. (
9
) is compactly written as
\tilde{\psi}^{\dagger}=\psi^{\dagger}U
.
Generally, the matrix
U
is given by
Sun et al. 2024

U_{g,(K,\lambda,\nu)}=\sqrt{\frac{d_{K}}{\absolutevalue{G}}}\left[D_{\nu\lambda}^{K}(g)\right]^{*},

(10)

where
D^{K}_{\nu\lambda}(g)
are the matrix elements of the irrep, and the star denotes complex conjugation.

CSL Hamiltonians are kept invariant under such unitary transformations, since each quadratic term is invariant,

\psi^{\dagger}_{i}\rho(a_{ij})\psi_{j}=\psi_{i}^{\dagger}UU^{\dagger}\rho(a_{ij})UU^{\dagger}\psi_{j}=\tilde{\psi}^{\dagger}_{i}\tilde{\rho}(a_{ij})\tilde{\psi}_{j},

(11)

where
\rho(a_{ij})
is the regular representation of the connection that encodes hopping amplitudes from site
j
to site
i
.
Importantly, rotation to the irrep basis

results in a block-diagonal form given by the decomposition in Eq. (
4
).
Since the same block diagonalization occurs for every bond, the entire CSL Hamiltonian separates into distinct sectors as described in the paragraph below Eq. (
5
) in the Results section.

For the specific choice
G=Q_{8}
