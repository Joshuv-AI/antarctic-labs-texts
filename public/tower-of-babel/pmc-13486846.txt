Management of systemic lupus erythematosus and antiphospholipid syndrome during CAR-T therapy: insights from two clinical cases
Authors: C. Normand, S. Almaz, R. Stadelmann, M. Goodyer, B. Navarro, A.I. Martin Quesada, A. Mulvey, P. Szturz, B. Gentner, L. Trueb, C. Arber, E. Ghisoni, M. Le Moine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) are autoimmune disorders marked by the presence of pathogenic autoantibodies predominantly produced by B cells. Both conditions are chronic, incurable, and require lifelong treatment. Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has emerged as a promising treatment for refractory hematological malignancies, with anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy demonstrating high efficacy in relapsed or refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). The profound depletion of B cells induced by anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy suggests that this treatment may influence the clinical course of patients with DLBCL and concomitant SLE or APS.Materials and methodsWe report two clinical cases of patients diagnosed with both DLBCL and concomitant SLE and APS who underwent anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy at our institution. We describe the lymphoproliferative disease course as well as the serological evolution and longitudinal cytokine profiles measured before and after CAR-T therapy.ResultsIn both cases, CAR-T therapy resulted in complete and long-lasting metabolic remission of the lymphoproliferative disorder. Concurrently, autoantibody titers stabilized, permitting stepwise de-escalation of the immunomodulatory treatment.ConclusionThese cases demonstrate that CAR-T therapy for DLBCL is feasible and appears safe in patients with concomitant autoimmune disease but requires close monitoring and interdisciplinary management. These findings support further preclinical and clinical investigation of CAR-T therapy as a potential therapeutic approach in patients with DLBCL and concomitant SLE and APS.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) are autoimmune diseases characterized by pathogenic autoantibodies, including anti-double-stranded (ds)DNA and antiphospholipid antibodies [lupus anticoagulant (LA), anticardiolipin (aCL), and anti-β2-glycoprotein I (anti-β2GPI)], which drive chronic inflammation, organ damage, and thrombosis.1,2 These autoantibodies are predominantly produced by B cells and plasma cells, although other immune compartments also contribute to disease pathogenesis. Despite advances in immunosuppressive and anticoagulant therapies, many patients remain refractory to treatment, and durable drug-free remission is rarely achieved.3,4

Anti-CD19 chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy, initially developed for refractory B-cell malignancies such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL),5,6 induces profound B-cell depletion and may promote an immune reset in B-cell–mediated autoimmune diseases.7, 8, 9, 10 Preclinical studies in lupus-prone mice and early clinical trials have demonstrated promising safety and efficacy of CD19 CAR-T cells in SLE, supporting their potential as a novel therapeutic strategy for severe refractory autoimmune disease.11,12

Elevated cytokine levels in SLE and APS, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-17A, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), are associated with inflammation, immune dysregulation, and increased thrombotic risk.13, 14, 15 CAR-T therapy also induces release of cytokines, notably IL-6, IL-1, interferon-γ, and TNF-α, contributing to cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) and guiding the use of tocilizumab (anti-IL-6 receptor),16 corticosteroids, and anakinra (recombinant IL-1 receptor antagonist).13 In patients with concomitant SLE and APS, careful monitoring of cytokine dynamics before, during, and after CAR-T therapy may provide valuable clinical information.14,15

In this study, we report two clinical cases of patients diagnosed with DLBCL and concomitant SLE and APS who received anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy.

Materials and methods

Biological samples were collected during routine clinical practice, with no modifications to standard care protocols. All patients treated with CAR-T therapy at Centre Hospitalier Universitaire Vaudois (CHUV) provided written informed consent covering both the clinical treatment and the anonymized use of related biological samples and data for research purposes. In addition, both patients provided specific written informed consent for the publication of this case report. Cytokine measurements were carried out in real time at specific time points as a part of standard CHUV protocols, as previously described,17 using a multiplex bead-based immunoassay (ProcartaPlex™; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Ecublens, Switzerland) and acquired on a Luminex FlexMAP 3D® instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Ecublens, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Antiphospholipid antibodies were assessed according to institutional standard practice, measuring aCL, anti-β2GPI, and LA, as detailed in Supplementary Material (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101609). Grading of CRS and ICANS was carried out according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy and American Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation consensus grading systems, respectively.18

Case 1

A 67-year-old man received an initial diagnosis of APS in 2016 based on serological triple positivity [LA, aCL, and anti-β2GPI immunoglobulin (Ig) G antibodies] (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101609). In 2021, he started prophylactic treatment with hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) (at a daily alternate dosage of 200 and 400 mg) and low-dose aspirin (100 mg/day). Despite persistent serological positivity, he experienced no thrombotic events under treatment.

In 2024, the patient was diagnosed with stage IE DLBCL, CD5−, nongerminal center subtype according to the Hans algorithm, without BCL2, MYC, or BCL6 rearrangements. The Revised International Prognostic Index score was 3, corresponding to a high-intermediate risk category. He received first-line systemic immunochemotherapy with six cycles of R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone), but post-treatment assessment revealed refractory disease with the persistence of a metabolically active lesion in the right scapula (Figure 1A). CD19-directed CAR-T therapy with lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel) was proposed to the patient for early progressive disease. After diagnostic surgical resection of the right scapula lesion, bridging radiotherapy treatment (30 Gy in 10 fractions) was carried out. Of note, APS remained serologically active before CAR-T therapy, with persistent triple positivity despite treatment (Figure 1B).

Upon admission to our cell-therapy unit, HCQ treatment for his chronic APS was discontinued and prophylactic anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated, in addition to low-dose aspirin. LMWH was continued during the entire stay because of the high thrombotic risk associated with APS.

Regarding B-cell phenotyping before CAR-T therapy, circulating B cells were detectable and predominantly displayed a double-negative phenotype (IgD− CD27−).

The patient underwent lymphodepletion [day (D) −5] to D−3 with fludarabine at 30 mg/m2/day and cyclophosphamide at 300 mg/m2/day, followed by the infusion of 100 × 106 CAR-T cells (liso-cel). On D+1 after CAR-T cell infusion, the patient developed grade 1 CRS manifesting as a persistent fever only. Due to persistent symptoms despite symptomatic treatment after 72 hours, four doses of tocilizumab (8 mg/kg per dose) were administered according to our institutional guidelines. He also experienced grade 1 ICANS from D+8, manifesting as dysgraphia and fine motor skills disorder (Immune Effector Cell Encephalopathy score 9 of 10), which persisted until D+13. Treatment with anakinra (100 mg b.i.d. from D+5, then q.i.d. from D+8 to D+13) was introduced to prevent ICANS aggravation. The patient’s clinical condition improved rapidly, and he was discharged on D+19.

On D+30, radiological assessments showed complete remission of the lymphoma, which persisted at the latest follow-up (D+180) (Figure 1A).

After CAR-T cell treatment, HCQ was not reintroduced. Prophylactic anticoagulation was continued during the post-CAR-T hospitalization period and subsequently discontinued at 6 months given the progressive decline in antiphospholipid antibody titers, with complete normalization of aCL IgG and a barely detectable anti-β2GPI IgG titer (Figure 1B), and the absence of new thrombotic events. Antiplatelet therapy alone was maintained with close clinical and serological follow-up.

In addition, cytokine concentrations were longitudinally monitored. The patient presented with elevated IL-18 levels before the initiation of CAR-T therapy, while the other cytokines (IL-4, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17A, IL-23, and TNF-α) were within normal limits under HCQ treatment. Angiogenic factors implicated in APS, such as placental growth factor 1 (PIGF-1) and VEGF-A, were within the upper normal range. After CAR-T cell infusion, a transient peak in IL-6 was observed (maximum 137 pg/ml), concomitant with the CRS, which was effectively controlled by the administration of tocilizumab. IL-6 subsequently decreased during follow-up. Additionally, IL-18 levels were above the normal limit before CAR-T cell infusion and showed a continuous increase until D+13 (218 pg/ml), before declining again thereafter, without reaching normal values at the last cytokine measurement. A peak in C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL-2) concentration was observed immediately after CAR-T cell infusion, which declined in the subsequent days (Figure 1C and D and Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101609).

Phenotyping of circulating lymphocytes showed a persistent B-cell aplasia for several months posttreatment as expected in this context, with gradual recovery beginning at D+184 with a B-cell count of 88 cells/μl.

Case 2

A 57-year-old woman was diagnosed with SLE associated with APS in 1988, manifesting with hematological, dermatological, and rheumatological symptoms. Serological evaluation revealed positive aCL and anti-β2GPI antibody titers. No specific treatments were initially introduced.

The medical history was marked by subsequent thromboembolic events (ileocecal vasculitis, deep vein thrombosis, and recurrent pulmonary emboli) and autoimmune manifestations (secondary Sjögren syndrome and class II lupus glomerulonephritis). At disease onset, the global context corresponded to a high initial disease activity (estimated Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index >15-20 points). Since 2012, she has been treated with HCQ 200 mg once daily. Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) was added to the treatment 1 year later. The patient was also fully anticoagulated with warfarin. Complete serological remission was achieved in 2024.

In June 2023, the patient was diagnosed with stage IV DLBCL. MMF was discontinued, and the patient received first-line R-CHOP immunochemotherapy. Although she obtained complete metabolic response, the disease relapsed 4 months later with supra- and infradiaphragmatic disease. Anti-CD19 CAR-T cell treatment (liso-cel) was proposed. She received initial bridging therapy with rituximab, ifosfamide, cyclophosphamide, and epirubicin, but evaluation after two cycles revealed progressive disease, with left pleural lesions, progression of pulmonary and hepatic lesions, and splenomegaly. A second bridging regimen with gemcitabine, cisplatin, and dexamethasone was initiated, but the disease was entirely resistant (Figure 2A). Before leukapheresis and CAR-T cell infusion, circulating B cells were already undetectable, likely reflecting prior intensive immune-chemotherapy. HCQ was discontinued 1 month before CAR-T cell infusion. From D−5 to D−3, she received fludarabine 30 mg/m2/day and cyclophosphamide 300 mg/m2/day, followed by an infusion of 100 × 106 CAR-T cells (liso-cel). At D0 of infusion, she developed grade 1 CRS, which resolved rapidly with antipyretics within 72 hours, without the need of tocilizumab administration. No neurological events were noted. Her clinical status improved rapidly, and she was discharged on D+12.

At D+30 radiological assessments showed complete remission of the lymphoma, which was sustained at the subsequent follow-up [D+180 (Figure 2A) and at 1 year].

Anticoagulation was interrupted 1 month after CAR-T therapy due to grade 4 thrombocytopenia and resumed 3 months later with a vitamin K antagonist after platelet recovery. HCQ was not reintroduced. The patient remains in complete serological remission of APS antibodies up to D+455 (Figure 2B). No SLE flare or thrombotic events occurred after CAR-T therapy.

Baseline cytokine monitoring before CAR-T therapy showed an elevated IL-6 concentration, with a peak of 60.8 pg/ml at D−5. Although the patient developed CRS grade 1 after CAR-T cell infusion, IL-6 concentrations steadily declined from D−5 to D+26 (19.8 pg/ml). Other cytokines (IL-4, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, L-17A, IL-23, and TNF-α) and angiogenic factors (PIGF-1 and VEGF-A) remained within normal limits throughout the treatment and follow-up. Additionally, IL-18 levels were above the normal range at the first measurement (D−8) and had nearly tripled by the final follow-up (D+26). Moreover, we observed an initial peak in CCL-2 concentration before CAR-T cell infusion, which subsequently decreased in the days following infusion (Figure 2C and D and Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101609).

Consistent with case 1, phenotyping of circulating lymphocytes after CAR-T therapy showed persistent complete depletion of B lymphocytes 6 months post treatment.

Discussion

We describe the cases of two patients with DLBCL and concomitant autoimmune disorders who were treated with anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy. In both cases, CAR-T cell infusion induced profound B-cell depletion followed by a progressive decline or normalization of APS-associated autoantibodies during longitudinal follow-up. Notably, in case 1, a discrete rise in anti-β2GPI IgG levels was detected at the last assessment (D+180) concomitant with early B-cell reconstitution (Figure 1B and Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.iotech.2026.101609), warranting continued serological follow-up. In contrast, the second patient achieved complete and persistent serological remission at 1 year.

Multiple cases of autoimmune disease treated with CAR-T cells have recently been reported, mainly in patients with refractory SLE. A recent systematic review and pooled analysis identified 47 patients across 10 studies treated with CAR-T therapy in the setting of autoimmune disease,19 and a broader review published by Nordmann-Gomes et al.20 describes outcomes in over 145 patients with SLE treated with CAR-T therapy. The seminal phase I-II CASTLE basket trial demonstrated that 9 of 10 patients with SLE achieved remission according to Definition Of Remission In SLE criteria, remaining free of steroids or other immunosuppressive drugs throughout the follow-up period.10 A propensity score-matched analysis by Wang et al.21 further reported that CD19-directed CAR-T therapy administered for B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma was associated with improvement in concomitant rheumatic autoimmune diseases, providing a clinical context particularly relevant to our cases. Reports of CAR-T therapy specifically in patients with APS remain considerably rarer than those in patients with SLE, underscoring the novelty of the APS-related findings in our series. A similar case of concomitant APS and lymphoma treated with CAR-T cells has been previously reported.22 In contrast to previous reports, our study provides longitudinal monitoring of APS-associated autoantibodies and cytokine profiles after CAR-T cell infusion, offering additional insight into the biological evolution of autoimmune activity during and after hematological treatment.

B cells are major drivers of SLE and APS pathogenesis by producing autoantibodies, presenting antigens, and secreting proinflammatory cytokines, leading to dysregulated germinal center reactions.23 During flares, (i) naïve and memory B cells replenish the pool of autoreactive high-affinity antibodies to nuclear and other self-antigens (e.g. anti-dsDNA, anti-Smith, and antiphospholipid antibodies); (ii) they secrete proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α that fuel systemic inflammation; (iii) regulatory B cells are downregulated, tilting the balance toward inflammation; and (iv) long-lived CD27+ CD19− CD20− plasma cells (LLPCs) are increased.24 The central role of B cells is supported by the success, albeit limited, of current B-cell depleting therapies. Anti-CD20 therapies target naïve and memory B cells but spare long-lived CD20− plasma cells, resulting in the incomplete elimination of autoreactive antibodies and frequent clinical relapses.25,26 An important distinction must be made between B-cell subsets in terms of their susceptibility to targeted therapies. Plasmablasts are typically CD19+ CD20− and may be targeted by CD19-directed CAR-T cells but are spared by anti-CD20 therapies. Short-lived plasma cells generally retain CD19 expression and may therefore also be accessible to CD19 CAR-T cells. In contrast, LLPCs frequently downregulate both CD19 and CD20 expression, rendering them largely resistant to both anti-CD20 antibodies and CD19-directed CAR-T therapies. Consequently, persistence of residual autoantibody production despite profound circulating B-cell depletion may reflect the survival of autoreactive LLPCs. The persistence of low-level antiphospholipid antibody positivity in the first patient could be explained by the persistence of autoreactive LLPCs. Because LLPCs lack the canonical surface markers targeted by current immunotherapy, alternative therapeutic strategies are under investigation. B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) is expressed on LLPCs and represents a promising target; recent reports using BCMA-targeted therapies have demonstrated encouraging activity in refractory SLE.27,28 Several trials with bispecific CAR-T cells as well as CAR-T regulatory and natural killer cells are currently in development (NCT05858684, NCT05474885, and NCT06350110).

Regarding the therapeutic potential of anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy in autoimmune diseases, the prevailing hypothesis suggests that the transient yet profound depletion of the autoreactive B-cell subset induced by anti-CD19 CAR-T cells could allow the hematological system to re-educate B cells to a less autoreactive state.29 Indeed, a recent study in five SLE patients showed that reappearing B cells were naïve and showed non–class-switched B-cell receptors.30 Durable clinical responses in autoimmune diseases after CD19-directed CAR-T therapy have been reported despite the gradual recovery of circulating B cells and in the absence of persistent B-cell aplasia, suggesting that the therapeutic effect may extend beyond prolonged B-cell depletion. This supports the hypothesis that CAR-T therapy may induce a broader immunological reset, with qualitative differences in the reconstituting B-cell repertoire compared with the pretreatment autoreactive repertoire.31 Of note, although the importance of the depth of B-cell depletion in secondary lymphoid tissues for the efficacy of CD19 CAR-T therapy in patients with autoimmune diseases is increasingly being recognized,32 in our patients, B-cell monitoring was limited to the peripheral blood compartment.

The hypercoagulable state and endothelial dysfunction associated with SLE and APS could theoretically contribute to vascular complications during CAR-T therapy, although this has not been specifically demonstrated in CAR-T clinical trials to date. Nevertheless, some retrospective studies have reported a substantial increase in thrombotic complications after CAR-T therapy, with venous thromboembolism occurring in ∼2%-11% of patients, particularly among those developing ICANS or severe inflammatory toxicities,33, 34, 35 whereas lymphodepleting chemotherapy treatment may additionally induce thrombocytopenia and coagulation abnormalities. Together, these factors create a challenging clinical situation in which thrombotic and hemorrhagic risks must be carefully balanced. Current evidence therefore supports careful monitoring of coagulation parameters during CAR-T therapy, especially in patients with pre-existing hypercoagulable conditions such as SLE or APS, even though formal recommendations and prophylactic anticoagulation strategies remain insufficiently defined.36

In autoimmune diseases treated with CAR-T therapy, a personalized approach is needed concerning immunosuppressive medications. Before apheresis and CAR-T cell administration, immunosuppressive medications are often reduced or discontinued with the aim of preserving autologous T-cell fitness and improving CAR-T cell expansion and persistence. However, evidence supporting specific washout strategies remains limited, and available data are not entirely consistent. Although some protocols restrict corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, or other immunosuppressive agents before leukapheresis and CAR-T cell infusion, others permit selected therapies with variable washout periods, reflecting substantial heterogeneity across clinical trials and commercial platforms. The recent European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation recommendations suggest reducing corticosteroids to <10 mg/day before leukapheresis but do not provide specific washout guidance for HCQ.37 According to our institutional guidelines, both patients discontinued HCQ before leukapheresis and CAR-T therapy and did not resume it afterward. Although not directly cytotoxic to LLPCs, HCQ has been shown to inhibit Toll-like receptor (TLR) 7 and TLR9 signaling, thereby reducing the production of type I interferons and proinflammatory cytokines that are implicated in the APS course and its clinical manifestations.

Finally, it is important to note that in both SLE and APS, cytokine levels are known to typically rise during disease flares and decline with remission. Nevertheless, serial cytokine monitoring is not a part of routine clinical practice because of assay variability, cost constraints, and the absence of standardized reference thresholds. In our cases, the patients underwent cytokine surveillance throughout CAR-T therapy, enabling correlation of cytokine dynamics with potential SLE- and APS-related clinical events. IL-6 kinetics reflected CAR-T cell activation and concomitant CRS development rather than an autoimmune flare. IL-18 was persistently elevated throughout cytokine monitoring in both patients. IL-18 has been shown to be induced by pyroptosis of APS-related cells, which may aggravate the autoimmune disease course.38 CCL-2 concentrations increased around the time of CAR-T cell infusion, followed by a clear decline thereafter, and a subsequent low-grade increase at the last follow-up in both patients. CCL-2 levels have been shown to correlate with SLE disease activity and flares, and declining CCL-2 concentrations have been associated with therapeutic response.39 Other cytokines commonly associated with SLE or APS flares showed no significant elevations in either case during CAR-T therapy. Although elevations in these proinflammatory mediators have been associated with increased thrombotic risk,40,41 no thrombotic complications were observed in our two patients. Notably, both individuals remained on strict prophylactic anticoagulation throughout these periods of cytokine fluctuation. Furthermore, cytokine monitoring during longer-term follow-up may provide a surrogate marker of clinical and biological remission of autoimmune disease.

Additional clinical studies are warranted to characterize cytokine profiles during CAR-T therapy in patients with autoimmune diseases, with the goal of improving the interpretation of these biomarkers and elucidating their therapeutic implications.42

Conclusion

These two cases demonstrate that CAR-T therapy for DLBCL is both feasible and safe in patients with concomitant autoimmune disease requiring specific treatment, provided that close monitoring and interdisciplinary management are ensured. Although CD19-targeted CAR-T therapy is widely established as a standard of care treatment for patients with refractory DLBCL, the impact on autoimmune disease is still under active investigation. The capacity for inducing profound and long-lasting B-cell depletion, associated with progressive normalization or stabilization of autoantibody titers and successful de-escalation of immunomodulatory treatment, represents a new era for patients with concomitant SLE and APS. Ongoing clinical trials are evaluating the potential of CAR-T cells for achieving sustained remissions and potentially curative approaches for a variety of autoimmune diseases.
