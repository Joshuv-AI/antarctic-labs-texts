Early evidence of the effects of xylazine-adulterated fentanyl in Ohio
Authors: Daniel Rosenblum, George Karandinos, Jay Unick, Dennis Cauchon, Daniel Ciccarone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Xylazine is becoming a prevalent fentanyl adulterant in the US. It has been associated with severe wounds and withdrawal symptoms. However, its impact on fatal overdose rates is poorly understood.Methods:Poisson and ordinary least squares regression analyses are used to estimate the relationship between xylazine prevalence and unintentional overdose death and death rates at the county-month level in Ohio from April through December 2023. Xylazine prevalence is calculated from the Ohio Bureau of Criminal Investigation’s (BCI) Crime Lab Data, and mortality data is from the Ohio Department of Health.Results:Xylazine prevalence is positively correlated with overdose deaths and death rates in large population counties. Xylazine adulteration is associated with 319 more overdose deaths [95 percent CI: 147–491 deaths], 10 percent of all unintentional overdose deaths in Ohio, over the nine-month period. Our estimates predict that if all fentanyl had been adulterated with xylazine over these nine months, this would have led to an additional 519 deaths.Discussion:Although the data covers a limited time period, our estimates provide evidence that xylazine-adulterated fentanyl is likely to lead to additional overdose deaths as it continues to spread across the US, blunting the initial signs of a declining trend in overdose deaths. If the findings can be extrapolated to the rest of the country, it is likely that overdose deaths would have fallen more substantially in 2023 if xylazine had not already been so prevalent in large parts of the US.Keywords: Xylazine, Fentanyl, Overdose deaths, Ohio

Introduction

We estimate the potential dangers of the combination of fentanyl with xylazine in Ohio, a state that has had a particularly severe overdose crisis in recent years. Xylazine is a domestically available veterinary tranquilizer used on large animals with no approved use in humans. Xylazine first appeared in combination with heroin in Puerto Rico in the 2000s, later was found in Philadelphia (Torruella, 2011), and is now becoming more prevalent across the US (Friedman et al., 2022; Montero, Bourgois & Friedman, 2022). Xylazine is inexpensive and can extend the short-acting effects of fentanyl, which may make it attractive both to suppliers and people who use drugs (PWUD), although people who prefer to use fentanyl without xylazine may not have that option (Friedman et al., 2022; Montero, Bourgois & Friedman, 2022).

Xylazine is a particularly worrisome adulterant as it is a sedative that may not be affected by naloxone (Ball et al., 2022; Nunez, DeJoseph & Gill, 2021), potentially making drug overdose more likely and less easily reversible.1 Xylazine is associated with severe wounds in PWUD (Ciccarone et al., 2025; Zagorski et al., 2023). Furthermore, xylazine adulterated opioids may be associated with more severe or complicated withdrawal symptoms (D’Orazio et al., 2023; Quijano et al., 2023), reducing the effectiveness of treatment for substance use disorder. On the other hand, xylazine may give fentanyl longer “legs” (Friedman et al., 2022), increasing the duration of a fentanyl dose. Hence, PWUD may be consuming fewer xylazine-adulterated fentanyl doses per day relative to unadulterated fentanyl, perhaps reducing the likelihood of overdose.

Whether xylazine adulteration is causing more or fewer overdose deaths is an open research question. Xylazine-related deaths have been increasing along with its availability (Cano, Daniulaityte & Marsiglia, 2024). As more than 97 percent of xylazine-related deaths have co-occurring fentanyl (Spencer et al., 2023), it is unknown whether xylazine is making overdose more likely than fentanyl on its own. While xylazine prevalence has increased, aggregate US overdose deaths have started to fall. However, this correlation does not mean xylazine is causing fewer deaths. Rather, it could be that overdose deaths would be even lower without xylazine-adulteration. To help fill this critical knowledge gap, we estimate the relationship between xylazine and overdose deaths in Ohio by using the county-level prevalence of xylazine in state crime lab data as a potential exogenous measure of the variation in exposure to xylazine. To our knowledge, this is the first research attempting to estimate the relative change in risk of overdose death from xylazine adulteration. State crime labs in Ohio were mandated to test all samples for xylazine starting on March 29, 2023, so we analyze the time period from April through December 2023, the most recent month with available data. Given the limited county-month observations available of xylazine prevalence, our findings should be taken as preliminary until more data is available. We leave it to future research to test whether the risks from xylazine adulteration are growing or shrinking over time.

Data

Ohio Bureau of Criminal Investigations (BCI) Crime Lab Data is used as a proxy for drug supply, building on our earlier research (Rosenblum et al., 2024; Rosenblum, Unick & Ciccarone, 2020). The data is received in raw form and is cleaned for misspellings, dropping unclear or unknown lab results, dropping duplicates, dropping miscellaneous drugs (e.g. over-the-counter medications, steroids) and cannabis, etc. Each observation is the crime lab’s chemical analysis of a single drug sample seized by law enforcement. The size of crime lab samples over the time-period of interest ranges from less than 0.1 g to as large as a few kilograms. However, most are small amounts with a median weight of about 0.5 g. The observation lists all detected drugs in the sample. However, there is no measure of the purity of a drug or the relative proportions of the different drugs identified within a sample. For example, an observation may have fentanyl and cocaine listed, but we cannot determine whether the sample is 99 percent fentanyl and 1 percent cocaine or 1 percent fentanyl and 99 percent cocaine or if the sample is 1 percent fentanyl, 1 percent cocaine, and 98 percent a non-drug filler. The BCI data includes the county where the drug was seized but has no information about the person who the drug was seized from, such as that person’s county of residence. Appendix Table A1 presents descriptive statistics for the prevalence of the main drug categories used in the estimates.

As was true in the earlier research papers, Hamilton County relies on local county crime labs outside of the state BCI system for their drug tests, and is therefore dropped from the analysis. For the other 87 counties over the time period of interest, April through December 2023, the cleaned data consists of 19,529 crime lab samples of which 2057 tested positive for xylazine.2 96.8 percent of xylazine in the BCI data is found combined with fentanyl, so the main measure of xylazine prevalence in the data is the number of county-month observations of xylazine-fentanyl combinations. Approximately one-third of these observations contain some other drug (cocaine, methamphetamine, etc.), although, with the exception of cocaine, the frequency of any one of these drugs is low. See Appendix Table A2 for the frequency of different xylazine combinations. In Appendix B, Table B1 and Table B2, we present estimates that separate the xylazine and fentanyl samples with and without cocaine into two independent variables to test whether one or the other is more less correlated with overdose death. However, as there is not a substantial difference in estimated coefficients between these subtypes, for our main estimates we focus on the prevalence of all xylazine-fentanyl combinations, regardless of other drugs that may be present.

The data for the outcome of interest, unintentional overdose deaths and death rates by county of residence, are from the Ohio Department of Health (ODH).3 Our analysis uses additional public data for county-level controls: monthly unemployment (from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics) and per capita amounts of prescription opioids and opioid treatment (i.e. buprenorphine) drugs dispensed at the county-quarter level from the Ohio Automated RX Reporting System Quarterly County Data. The authors had no access to information that could identify individuals during or after data collection.4 Data are aggregated to the county-month level, yielding 783 observations (87 counties over 9 months).

Overview of fentanyl, cocaine, methamphetamine, and xylazine prevalence in crime lab data and unintentional overdose deaths in Ohio in 2023

Before presenting the estimation strategy and results, it is useful to provide an overview of what the data shows in Ohio over the time period of interest. Figure 1 shows trends of some of the common drugs in the BCI data over 2023. BCI crime lab samples with fentanyl fell from about 30 percent of illicit drug samples to 25 percent over 2023. There was an increase of a similar magnitude in cocaine samples. Keeping in mind that comprehensive xylazine testing in Ohio only started in April 2023, there was a substantial increase in the prevalence of xylazine in fentanyl, rising from 35 to 45 percent of fentanyl samples from April to December. These are averages and there is wide variation across counties in Ohio of the prevalence of different drugs. For example, in Butler County 71.4 to 91.4 percent of fentanyl samples test positive for xylazine each month from April to December 2023, while it is only 7.5 to 25.5 percent in Trumbull County. As cocaine samples rose, the percent that were combined with synthetic opioids fell from about 20 percent to 15 percent over the year. There was little change in the prevalence of methamphetamine or methamphetamine-synthetic opioid mixes over 2023. Putting this together, the BCI data indicates a shift away from fentanyl, which is increasingly being mixed with xylazine, and a shift towards non-mixed cocaine.

Figure 2 shows that monthly unintentional overdose deaths declined in Ohio over 2023. The largest decline was in larger population counties, where monthly deaths fell by approximately 100 over the year or by about one third. Thus, if changes in illicit drug supply are driving down drug overdose deaths, it may be due to the relative fall in fentanyl, rise of cocaine (and, in particular, less cocaine contaminated with fentanyl), the prevalence of xylazine, or factors not captured by our data. CnoXit is the number of county-month BCI lab tests that test positive for cocaine, but not xylazine. Either FnoXit or CnoXit may contain other drugs. In Rosenblum, Unick and Ciccarone (2020), we investigated the role of different synthetic opioids in overdose deaths, such as carfentanil and other fentanyl analogs. In 2023 almost all crime lab samples with a non-fentanyl synthetic opioid also contain fentanyl, so it is difficult to disentangle the different synthetic opioids in the more recent data. Thus, fentanyl in the estimates should be interpreted as fentanyl potentially in combination with additional synthetic opioids. As in our previous research, in addition to the entire state, we separately estimate the above equation for small, medium, and large population counties.6 However, one should be cautious in interpreting estimates for the small population counties over this limited time period given the small numbers of overdose deaths and BCI crime lab samples in these counties.

Zit is a vector of time-varying county-level controls: the county-month numbers of positive tests for other illicit and prescription drugs from the BCI crime lab data,7 county-quarter per capita prescribed amounts of prescription opioids (buprenorphine and non-buprenorphine as separate variables) and county-month unemployment rates. These controls are based on availability, and there may be omitted variables that bias the estimates. However, any non-time varying differences across counties are controlled for by αi, county fixed effects. Furthermore, any general time trends across the state are controlled for by γt, month fixed effects. Thus, for example, if law enforcement intensity was increasing across the state in a similar way, these fixed effects would control for that change over time. The month fixed effects also remove general time trends across the state in changes in the illicit drug market. For example, if fentanyl presence is declining in a similar way across all counties, we will not find any correlation between fentanyl presence and overdose deaths. Hence, the focus of the estimates is comparing differences across counties over the time period. Robustness checks are performed using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analysis with death rates instead of death counts as the dependent variable and fractions of drug samples within a county-month testing positive for specific drugs rather than drug counts.

For the estimates to represent causal effects, it must be that xylazine was not preferentially going to counties where overdose deaths were expected to be rising or falling relatively faster than in other counties. In other words, given our controls, to interpret the estimates as causal, one must assume that changes over time in overdose deaths in xylazine counties with little change in xylazine prevalence represent a good counterfactual to counties where xylazine prevalence is rising. The estimates would not be causal if the PWUD who were more risk-taking and likely to die from an overdose were demanding xylazine, and that is why there is a positive relationship between xylazine presence and overdose deaths. Given that PWUD likely have little market power to influence the extent of xylazine adulteration and limited purchasing options (Friedman et al., 2022; Montero, Bourgois & Friedman, 2022), it is plausible that xylazine is exogenously imposed on illicit drug markets by drug sellers. Even if one does not interpret the estimates as the causal effects of xylazine adulteration, they can still be used to determine correlations, predict what we expect to occur if xylazine adulteration continues to increase, and determine plausible bounds on the negative implications of xylazine for overdose deaths.

Estimation Results

The Poisson regression estimates of the relationship between the number of BCI crime lab tests that are positive for the specified drug and overdose deaths are presented in Table 1. Xylazine in combination with fentanyl is positively correlated with overdose deaths at the 1 percent level in Columns 1 and 4, indicating that the positive relationship is driven by large population counties. We would not interpret these estimates as saying there is no effect of xylazine on small and medium size counties, rather, given the data limitations, the estimates cannot yet detect any statistically significant relationship in small and medium population counties. There is limited presence of xylazine over the time period in small population counties: 64.4 percent of small population county-month observations have zero xylazine detections, compared to 34.3 and 19.7 percent in medium and large population counties respectively. Small population counties have far fewer BCI crime lab samples: 40.7 percent of small population county-month observations have zero fentanyl detections with or without xylazine. However, conditional on having at least one fentanyl-positive BCI sample, the fraction of these samples adulterated with xylazine are similar across county size: 40.1 percent in small population, 35.3 percent in medium population, and 44.4 percent in large population counties. Hence, xylazine adulteration is present throughout Ohio, even if the BCI data is more limited in more rural areas. The reasons for the varied prevalence of xylazine adulteration across specific counties and over time are unclear at present.

The estimates related to xylazine for large population counties show that every extra xylazine-fentanyl sample is associated with an increase in the number of overdose deaths in a month by about 2.6 percent (95 percent CI: 1.2–4.0 percent). On average there were about 5.25 such xylazine samples per month per large population county. Thus, the estimates predict about a 13.65 percent (95 percent CI: 6.3–21.0 percent) increase in overdose deaths due to xylazine. Given that the average number of overdose deaths in large population counties is 10 per month, the estimates indicate that 1.365 deaths (95 percent CI: 0.63–2.1 deaths) per county per month can be attributed to xylazine, which sums to an extra 319 deaths (95 percent CI: 147–491 deaths) from April to December 2023 or about 15 percent of the 2149 unintentional overdose deaths in the large population counties over this time period (or about 10 percent of the 3226 total unintentional overdose deaths in Ohio). If xylazine presence represents an exogenous change in the illicit drug market, one not driven by consumer demand or changes in the risk of overdose deaths, then one could interpret these estimates as the causal effect of an increase in xylazine adulteration on unintentional overdose deaths. Drugs without xylazine are not correlated with overdose deaths at statistically significant levels, which may be due to the relative stability of the prevalence of these drugs compared to xylazine over the short time period of interest.

We can use the estimates to predict what would happen if all fentanyl was adulterated with xylazine. In large population counties, on average there were 13.77 BCI lab tests that contained fentanyl (with or without xylazine). If all of these were adulterated with xylazine, the estimates predict a 35.8 percent increase in overdose deaths or 3.58 extra deaths per month per county or about 838 more overdose deaths over the large population counties over the nine-month time period. Given that 319 of these deaths are attributed to actual xylazine prevalence, the estimates predict that complete xylazine adulteration would lead to an additional 519 deaths. To summarize, in the context of Ohio’s declining overdose deaths, our evidence suggests that the presence of xylazine is leading to higher overdose deaths than there would be without xylazine, and any increases in xylazine presence are likely to push against future declines in overdose deaths. Since people will likely react to mitigate their overdose risks if xylazine prevalence increases (that is, the relationship is unlikely to be linear as we go from zero to 100 percent xylazine adulteration), we can think of this estimate as a plausible short-term upper bound on the consequences of complete xylazine adulteration.

Limitations

The principal limitation of this article, as in most research in this area, is a lack of comprehensive data. We only have nine months of data to analyze, and, crucially, do not know the extent of xylazine adulteration before April 2023. The crime lab data does not measure the amount of xylazine in a given sample, only whether it is present or not, so it may be that there are more or less dangerous levels of xylazine in the drug supply which we cannot observe and are changing over time. Thus, the estimates should be thought of as a snapshot during the transition from no xylazine to prevalent xylazine. People may already be adapting to the presence of xylazine, in which case our estimates are not necessarily applicable to locations which are just starting to see xylazine emerge or to places where xylazine is more entrenched. For example, opioid smoking, which has become increasingly prevalent (Ciccarone et al., 2024; Eger et al., 2024a; Karandinos et al., 2024; Kral et al., 2021) and has been associated with lower mortality and non-fatal overdose risk relative to injection use (Karandinos, Unick & Ciccarone, 2025; Megerian et al., 2024), may be accelerating partially in response to increasing xylazine adulteration (Eger et al., 2024b).

Public policy could interact with an individual’s ability to respond to xylazine adulteration. For example, Ohio is a state where xylazine test strips are illegal, making it impossible to know whether one’s drugs are adulterated or not. Whereas in a state where xylazine test strips are legal and easily available, one could argue xylazine adulteration may be more difficult to impose by drug sellers. However, more study is needed to know whether xylazine might influence choice and markets.

Several research articles treat the BCI crime lab data as a proxy for the prevalence of different drugs in the illicit drug supply (Hall et al., 2021; Rosenblum et al., 2024; Zibbell et al., 2019). However, the BCI crime lab data may not represent the true state of the illicit drug market in Ohio. For example, if changes in xylazine presence represent a change in law enforcement intensity rather than a change in the composition of illicit drugs, and greater xylazine presence in the BCI data is actually greater law enforcement intensity against sellers and buyers of xylazine-adulterated drugs, then the positive correlation between xylazine presence and overdose deaths may really be an unintended consequence of supply disruption as found by Ray et al. (2023). Controlling for the total number of seizures in a county-month would control for this law enforcement intensity. The estimates above are robust to including total seizures as a control variable. In addition, the estimates are robust to using the percentage of seized drugs in a county-month that contain xylazine rather than the number of seizures, which would also help to account for the potential bias from law enforcement intensity. However, xylazine presence may still be correlated with other such omitted variables that may be the underlying cause of the estimated correlations.

Last, as we only look at overdose deaths and death rates as the outcomes of interest, we are missing the potentially severe morbidity effects of xylazine. It may be that wounds and tissue infections have created a far more severe public health cost than the mortality effects would suggest on their own. Future research on this topic is warranted.

Discussion

A major contribution of this article is to provide the first estimates of the predicted rise in overdose deaths due to the spread of xylazine. By analyzing the differential spread of xylazine across counties in Ohio we find that, even over a time period of falling overdose deaths, there were relatively more deaths in counties with a greater presence of xylazine in BCI crime lab tests. We only find a statistical relationship in counties with larger populations, which together represent the bulk of the state’s population and overdose deaths. However, as xylazine spreads, it may begin to adversely affect the other counties as well. The clear fall in overdose deaths in Ohio from 2022 to 2023, and indeed in the US as a whole, was a welcome change from the previous trend towards ever higher overdose deaths. However, this research shows that this decline would likely have been even larger without xylazine, and that xylazine will make it more difficult to reduce overdose deaths in the future.

Our findings, using aggregated cross-state data, are an important contrast to smaller scale research that suggests that xylazine may make illicit drugs less dangerous. Love et al. (2023), for example, compares overdose severity in emergency departments across seven states for 321 people presenting with likely opioid overdose and finds lower rates of coma and cardiac arrest among those who tested positive for xylazine, although almost all of the patients in the study survived and there was no statistically significant difference in mortality rates for patients who tested positive for xylazine compared those who tested negative. As that article focuses on people who survive overdose, it cannot inform us as to the role of xylazine in deaths, which is where our estimates can help fill the knowledge gap.

The introduction of xylazine to the fentanyl market is likely similar to the introduction of fentanyl to the heroin market, where the initial combinations of fentanyl and heroin were imposed by the supply side of the market rather than through a demand for stronger opioids from people who use heroin Mars, Rosenblum and Ciccarone (2019). As PWUD continue to be exposed to and adapt to xylazine adulteration, this could lead to both higher risk of death for some PWUD and lower risk for those who adapt by choosing not to consume opioids at all or altering their consumption practices. Our research suggests, at least over the limited time period, that xylazine has created a net increase in the risk of overdose death.
