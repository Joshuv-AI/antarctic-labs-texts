From FPT Decision to FPT Enumeration
Nadia Creignou, Timo Camillo Merkl, Reinhard Pichler, Daniel Unterberger

Abstract
Fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) algorithms have been successfully applied to many intractable problems – with a focus on decision and optimization problems. Their aim is to confine the exponential explosion to some parameter, while the time complexity only depends polynomially on the instance size. In contrast, intractable enumeration problems have received comparatively little attention so far. The goal of this work is to study how FPT decision algorithms could be turned into FPT enumeration algorithms. We thus inspect several fundamental approaches for designing FPT decision or optimization algorithms and we present ideas how they can be extended to FPT enumeration algorithms.



1
Introduction

Many practically relevant computational problems
in a wide range of applications
are intractable.
A meanwhile well-established
approach of dealing with intractable problems is
to aim at fixed-parameter tractable (
\mathsf{FPT}
) algorithms
(see
[
10
,
21
]
for comprehensive collections of

\mathsf{FPT}
-results). That is, the exponential explosion can be
confined to some parameter (typically some structural property of the problem instances
or of the desired solution) while the time complexity only depends polynomially on
the size of the problem instance.

\mathsf{FPT}
-algorithms have been mainly developed for
decision problems
(e.g., does there exist a solution?) or
optimization problems
(e.g., what is the max/min value attainable by all solutions?). However, in many settings, the user might be interested in
all
(or at least a significant portion) of the solutions or optimal solutions to a problem. This is particularly the case when querying data or knowledge base systems, where the user is usually interested in retrieving all (or at least a significant portion) of the answers
rather than just a simple “yes”. We are thus faced with an
enumeration problem
, whose task is to output the entire solution set without duplicates
(i.e., outputting the same solution twice).
Note that even
seemingly simple problems may have a huge (potentially exponential) number of solutions.
Hence, as was shown in the foundational paper by
Johnson et al.
[
14
]
, even the
notion of tractability has to be redefined. The enumeration complexity class that
is often considered as an analog of the decision complexity class
\mathsf{P}
is
\mathsf{DelayP}
,
i.e., “polynomial delay”. It
consists of those problems which have an algorithm that requires polynomial time
in the size of the instance to output the first solution, to output any further solution,
and to terminate after the last solution.
The study of enumeration problems and the development of
\mathsf{DelayP}
-algorithms has received a lot
of attention,
especially in the areas of graph theory and databases, see
e.g.,
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
15
,
17
]
.

But what happens if the task of finding the first or a further solution is intractable?
A natural approach
is to borrow ideas from decision and optimization problems and to aim for

\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
, i.e., algorithms that find the first or next solution
in
\mathsf{FPT}
-time. Surprisingly, this line of research has received little attention so far;
notable exceptions are

[
8
,
9
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
19
]
.
The aim of our work is to shed some light on this under-researched area and to
provide assistance to algorithm developers in designing
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
enumeration algorithms.
To this end, we revisit the most common techniques in
the design of
\mathsf{FPT}
decision and optimization algorithms
and look for their extension to

\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
enumeration algorithms.
For the basic
\mathsf{FPT}
approaches considered here, we will mainly follow the
presentation in
[
10
]
. Apart from kernelization

[
9
,
13
]

and tree decompositions (in particular, enumeration algorithms via
Courcelle’s Theorem
[
7
]
), these techniques have not yet made their way into enumeration.

Among the enumeration methods that we apply are adaptations and extensions
of typical enumeration approaches

[
18
]

such as “flashlight algorithms” (which search for solutions in a top-down traversal of
some tree structure and repeatedly use a
decision algorithm as a “flashlight”
to see if a certain branch
contains solutions before expanding it) or exploiting the closure
under union of
\mathsf{DelayP}

[
22
]
.
A particular
challenge of enumeration problems comes from the requirement that duplicates
have to be avoided. Here, we strongly draw inspiration from the landmark paper
of Lawler
[
16
]
, where a basic technique for avoiding duplicates by
appropriate partitioning of the solution space was presented.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
, we recall some
fundamental definitions of parameterized enumeration. The main part of the paper is
devoted to the design of
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
algorithms by carrying over basic
\mathsf{FPT}
-methods
from decision algorithms, namely partition algorithms as an
extension of bounded search trees (in Section
3
),
enumeration algorithms for a union of (not disjoint!) “
\mathsf{FPT}
-many” subsets of solutions
(in Section
4
), iterative compression-based enumeration
algorithms (in Section
5
) and dynamic programming algorithms
(in Section
6
). The design of enumeration
algorithms via these basic
\mathsf{FPT}
-methods is illustrated by instructive examples, and,
moreover, we propose formalizations to generalize these ideas.
We conclude with Section
7
.
Further details
are provided in
the appendix.

2
Parameterized Complexity and Enumeration

We assume familiarity with basic notions of complexity and graph theory.
A
parameterized problem
is defined by a ternary predicate
Q\subset\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Sigma^{*}
for some alphabet
\Sigma
. A tuple
(I,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
is an instance,

k
is its parameter, and
Q(I,k)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{S\in\Sigma^{*}\mid(I,k,S)\in Q\}

is its solution set.
The task of deciding whether
Q(I,k)\neq\emptyset

is the
decision problem
Q
, while the task of enumerating
Q(I,k)
without duplicates
is the
enumeration problem
Q
.
An instance
(I,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
with
Q(I,k)\neq\emptyset
is a
yes-instance
, otherwise a
no-instance
. We will denote the size of an instance
(I,k)
by
|(I,k)|
.

Definition 1

Let
Q
be a decision problem. We say
Q
can be decided in
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
if there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that, for any instance

(I,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
, decides if
Q(I,k)\neq\emptyset
with a runtime bounded by
h(k)p(|(I,k)|)
for some computable function
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and polynomial
p
. We call the class of all problems admitting
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
algorithms
\mathsf{FPT}
.

An
enumeration algorithm

\mathcal{A}
for an enumeration problem
Q
is an
algorithm which, when given an instance
(I,k)
of
Q
as input,
outputs the elements of
Q(I,k)

without duplicates.
An enumeration problem is in the class
\mathsf{DelayP}
, if it has an algorithm with a polynomial bound
on its
delay
.
By “delay” we mean the maximum time between two consecutive solutions, including
the time until the first solution is output and the time after the last solution is output.
The natural parameterized analog is
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
, which we define next.

Definition 2

Let
Q
be an enumeration problem. We say
Q
can be enumerated with
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
if there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
which, for any instance
(I,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
, enumerates all solutions in
Q(I,k)
with a delay bounded by
h(k)p(|(I,k)|)
for some computable function
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and polynomial
p
. We call the class of all problems
Q
admitting such an
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
enumeration algorithm
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

3
Partition Algorithms

Search tree algorithms, which work by iteratively splitting a given problem instance into sub-instances,
are among the most commonly used techniques in
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithms
for decision problems. The correctness of such algorithms hinges on the fact that, in case of a yes-instance,
at least one of the sub-instances resulting from a splitting step leads to a feasible solution.
In case of enumeration, we have to be careful that
the splitting step
partitions
the set of solutions, so as to preserve all solutions
and avoid duplicates.
We therefore start our discussion of
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
enumeration algorithms by studying various forms of
partition algorithms
.

3.1
Bounded Search Tree Partition Algorithms

We know from decision algorithms
that,
if the total size of the search tree is bounded by a function of the parameter only and every step takes
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
,
then such an algorithm runs in
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
.
To carry these ideas over to enumeration algorithms, several extensions are required.
On one hand, we still need bounds (that only depend on the parameter
k
) on the breadth and depth of the search tree.
In addition, following the philosophy of
[
16
]
, we now have to make sure that, with every splitting step, (i)
every
solution is
contained in some branch, (ii)
no
solution is contained in
more than one branch
, and (iii) the solutions of the

instances at the leaf nodes
of the search tree can be enumerated with
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

We illustrate these ideas on the

Feedback Vertex Set in Tournaments

(FVST, for short)
problem.
For a directed graph
G=(V,A)
, we call
S\subseteq V
a
feedback vertex set
, if
G[V\setminus S]
(that is the subgraph of
G
induced by the vertex set
V\setminus S
) is acyclic. A
Tournament

T=(V,A)
is a directed graph with exactly one edge between any two vertices.
In the FVST enumeration problem, we are given a tournament
T=(V,A)

and a parameter
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and we want to enumerate all feedback vertex sets of size at most
k
.

The FVST decision problem can be shown to be in
\mathsf{FPT}
via bounded search trees
(see
[
10
]
). Below we show how we can get
an enumeration
algorithms satisfying the above requirements (i) – (iii)
by maintaining additional information.

Proposition 1

The
Feedback Vertex Set in Tournaments
problem is in
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

Proof

Our algorithm for enumerating the feedback vertex sets of a tournament
T
maintains the problem instance
(T,k)
together
with two vertex sets
C,F\subseteq V(T)
as additional information. The intended meaning of these vertex sets is as follows:
We store in
C
vertices of
T
that we definitely want to delete and in
F
the vertices that we definitely want to retain.
Hence, our enumeration algorithm recursively searches for
feedback vertex sets
U
in the tournament
T[V\setminus C]

having
U\cap F=\emptyset
. Initially, we set
F=C=\emptyset
.

A tournament has a directed cycle iff it has a directed triangle (see
[
10
]
).
We can find such a triangle (or confirm that none exists) in time
O(|V|^{3})
. If none exists,
then we can output all feedback vertex sets
S
of size
\leq k
with
C\subseteq S
and

S\cap F=\emptyset

with polynomial delay.
Otherwise, since each feedback vertex set must have non-empty intersection with this triangle, we branch
by adding any non-empty subset of the triangle vertices disjoint from
F
to
C

(
\leq 7
possibilities), reduce
k
accordingly, and add
the remaining triangle vertices to
F
. We recursively apply these steps.

We verify that the requirements on a bounded search tree partition algorithm are fulfilled: the breadth of the search tree
is bounded by 7 and the depth is bounded by
k
(since the parameter is strictly decreased in each sub-instance).
Clearly, the splitting preserves all solutions and the additional information in the sets
F,C
excludes duplicates.
Finally, for a sub-instance with
k=0
, we know that the solution set is empty if the subgraph of
T
induced by
V\setminus C

contains a cycle; otherwise we output the solution
S\supseteq C
as described above.

Generalization and formalization.

Intuitively, we need a method
f
to create child instances of a given instance such that the solution space is partitioned by the solution space of the child instances. We recursively apply
f
until the instances are fully processed, i.e., until all solutions can be easily enumerated with a method
f_{0}
. To limit the size of the possible search tree and to formalize when instances are fully processed, we need a function
\gamma
, which assigns each instance
(I,k)
a natural number
m
such that the tree rooted at
(I,k)
has a height of at most
m
(where
m
can, in turn,
only depend on the parameter
k
),
and
m=0
if and only if the instance is fully processed. The following definition is intended to capture these requirements.

Definition 3

For an enumeration problem
Q
, we call a tuple of functions
(f,\gamma)
a
bounded search tree partition algorithm
if, for some computable functions
b,h\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, the following conditions hold:

•

f\colon\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N})
is an
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
function such that for all
(I,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
:

–

\#f(I,k)\leq b(k)
,

–

for all
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in f(I,k)
,
|(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})|\leq|(I,k)|
and
k^{\prime}\leq k
,

–

Q(I,k)=\bigcup_{(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in f(I,k)}Q(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})
,

–

for
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime}),(I^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime})\in f(I,k)
,
\displaystyle(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\neq(I^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime})\Rightarrow Q(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\cap Q(I^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime})=\emptyset
,

•

\gamma\colon\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is an
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
function such that for all
(I,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
:

–

\gamma(I,k)\leq h(k)
,

–

if
\gamma(I,k)\neq 0
, then for all
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in f(I,k)
,
\gamma(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})<\gamma(I,k)
.

Additionally, we require that there must exist a function
f_{0}
, which enumerates
Q(I,k)
with
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
when
\gamma(I,k)=0
.

In the definition above, the first condition on
f
controls the breadth of the search tree,
while the function
\gamma
is a measure that decreases at each step,
and thus bounds the depth of the search tree.
Both are bounded by a function of
k
, which thus also applies to
the size of the search tree.
The two last conditions on
f
ensure that traversing the search tree will provide all solutions with no repetitions. Therefore, we get the following result.

Proposition 2

Let
Q
be a parameterized enumeration problem. If
Q
has a
bounded search tree partition algorithm
(f,\gamma)
, then
Q
is in
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

To fit our FVST algorithm from the proof of Proposition
1

into this general framework, we consider a generalized version of FVST (we will call it GFVST) where
instances are extended by sets
C
and
F
. Then the FVST problem corresponds to the
special case of GFVST with
C=F=\emptyset
. Function
f
defines one splitting step, where an instance of GFVST gives rise to up to
b(k)=7
new instances.

\gamma
is initially set to
k
and decreased by the number of vertices
added to
C
in each splitting step. To trigger the application of function
f_{0}
for outputting all solutions of a given instance, we set
\gamma
to 0 if no more directed triangle exists in the graph. Of course,
\gamma
also becomes 0 when the
“budget” of allowed vertex deletions has been exhausted.
For further details, see
Appendix
0.A
.

3.2
Flashlight Partition Algorithms

Next, we aim at relaxing
the bound on the size
of the search tree.
More specifically, we bound both the breadth and the depth by an
\mathsf{FPT}
-function rather than by a function of the parameter
k
alone.
To guarantee that we find a new solution after
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
, we are only allowed to explore a branch in the search tree if it leads to a feasible solution and we have to go through the tree in a depth first manner.
This can be guaranteed by using a “flashlight” algorithm, i.e., a decision algorithm that decides in
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
whether an instance has a solution or not.
Again, we need to ensure that the branching properly partitions the solution space and that solutions of instances at leaf nodes can be enumerated with
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

We illustrate the idea of flashlight partition algorithms by considering the enumeration variant of
the
Closest String
problem, which is defined as follows:
we are given a set of strings
X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
of length
L
over an alphabet
\Sigma
and a parameter
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The task is to find all strings
s
of length
L
such that
d_{H}(s,x_{j})\leq k
for
each

1\leq j\leq n
, where
d_{H}(x,y)
is the Hamming distance between strings
x
and
y
.
We call such a string
s
a
center string
.

\mathsf{FPT}
-membership of the decision variant of
Closest String
(i.e., does there exist a center string?)
can be shown by a bounded search tree algorithm (see
[
10
]
).
However, for an enumeration algorithm, we again need to maintain more information to make sure that
every splitting of a problem instance
partitions
the solution set.

Proposition 3

The
Closest String
problem is in
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

Proof

Our algorithm for enumerating the center strings of a given instance
(X,k)

maintains, in addition to the instance, a string
\omega\in\Sigma^{*}
. The idea of
\omega
is that
we only search for center strings with prefix
\omega
. Initially, we set
\omega
to the empty string.
As flashlight, we use an
\mathsf{FPT}
-algorithm for the

Closest String
decision problem, checking if there exists a center string with prefix
\omega
.
Such an
\mathsf{FPT}
-algorithm is
easily obtained by adapting the bounded search tree algorithm from
Section 3.5 in
[
10
]
.

Now let
X
be an instance of the

Closest String
enumeration problem together with a prefix
\omega
as additional information.
We split this problem instance into sub-instances by iterating through all possible characters
\sigma\in\Sigma

and tentatively generating all possible prefixes
\omega\cdot\sigma
.
However,
from these, we only keep the yes-instances (which can be determined in
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}

by the flashlight).

The depth of the search tree at an instance
(X,k)
with additional information
\omega
is therefore
|\omega|\leq L
,
where
L
the length of the strings in
X
. The breadth is bounded by the size of the alphabet
\Sigma
. Clearly,
fixing the prefix of solutions partitions the solution space.
Moreover, no solution gets lost if we only
keep those sub-instances that contain a solution according to the flashlight.
Thus, in total, by traversing this search tree in a depth first fashion and outputting the solutions at the leaves, we get an
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
algorithm.

Generalization and formalization.

As with bounded search tree partition,
we need to make sure that each step can be done in
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}

and
partitions the solution space, and that the solutions at the leaf nodes can be enumerated with
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
.
The maximal depth
\gamma
may now also depend on
|I|
, with the dependence being polynomial.
An
\mathsf{FPT}
-decision “flashlight” algorithm is thus needed to check the existence of solutions.
We formalize these requirements in the following definition.

Definition 4

For an enumeration problem
Q
, we call a tuple of functions
(f,\gamma)
a
flashlight partition algorithm
if, for some computable function
h\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and polynomial
p
, the following conditions hold:

•

f\colon\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N})
is an
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
function such that for all
(I,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
:

–

for all
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in f(I,k)
,
|(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})|\leq|(I,k)|
and
k^{\prime}\leq k
,

–

for all
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in f(I,k)
,
Q(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
,

–

Q(I,k)=\bigcup_{(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in f(I,k)}Q(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})
,

–

for
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime}),(I^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime})\in f(I,k)
,
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\neq(I^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime})\Rightarrow Q(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\cap Q(I^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime})=\emptyset
.

•

\gamma\colon\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is an
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
function such that for all
(I,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
:

–

\gamma(I,k)\leq h(k)p(|(I,k)|)
,

–

if
\gamma(I,k)\neq 0
, then for all
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in f(I,k)
,
\gamma(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})<\gamma(I,k)
.

Additionally, we require that there must exist a function
f_{0}
, which enumerates
Q(I,k)
with
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
when
\gamma(I,k)=0
.

The “flashlight” is implicit in the function
f
, which (in
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
)
only generates subproblems
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})
with non-empty solution set (by the second condition on
f

in Definition
4
).
The depth of the search tree is bounded by an
\mathsf{FPT}
-function because of the measure
\gamma
that decreases at each step along a branch. Together with the “flashlight”, which guarantees that
no branch of the search tree is explored in vain, we thus get
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
.
We note that this method is related to the algorithmic paradigm of self-reducibility studied
in
[
9
]
.

Proposition 4

Let
Q
be a parameterized enumeration problem. If
Q
has a flashlight partition algorithm
(f,\gamma)
, then
Q
is in
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

Again, to fit our
Closest String
algorithm
from the proof of Proposition
3

into this framework, we consider a generalized version of this problem
where instances are extended by a prefix
\omega
. The
Closest String
problem
then corresponds to the special case with empty prefix
\omega
. Crucially,
the function
f
now serves two purposes: on one hand, it splits the current instance into new instances by appending one more character to
\omega
; on the other hand, it serves as “flashlight” and only retains those new instances which have a solution that extends the current prefix.
Initially,
\gamma
is set to
L
. It is then decremented in every splitting step,
when we extend the prefix by one character.
The function
f_{0}
for enumerating all solutions of an instance
when
\gamma
reaches the value 0 is particularly simple in this case: we just need
to output the prefix
\omega
(which now has length
L
) of the current instance.
For further details, see
Appendix
0.B
.

3.3
Solution Search Partition Algorithms

We now want to
remove the restriction on the depth of the search tree
altogether.
The idea is to alternately perform two steps, namely (1) find a solution and (2) split the
remaining search space. Intuitively, we thus try to find a spanning tree of the solution space.
As before,
the algorithm consists in traversing a tree. However,
unlike the two previous methods, this tree is not implicitly made up of sub-instances and partial solutions, with final solutions only at the leaves or computable from the leaves.
Now, each node of the tree constitutes an instance from which a solution
S
of the original instance can be produced
in
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
.
Clearly, in the splitting step, we need to create sub-instances that partition the solutions of
(I,k)

excluding the solution
S
.
We repeat this branching, exploring the search tree depth-first, until we arrive at a no-instance, at which point we consider the remaining branches.

To maintain
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
between the output of two successive solutions, we can use the “alternative output” technique (see
[
23
,
18
]
), which prevents the backtracking through long paths without outputting any solution: it
outputs the solutions
before
the recursive call when the current depth-first search level is even,
and
after
the recursive call when it is odd.

We now construct a solution search partition algorithm for

Integer Linear Programming with k Variables
parameterized by the number of variables:
let
k
and
m
be positive integers, let
A
be an
m\times k
matrix with integer coefficients and
b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and let
k
be the parameter.
The task is to enumerate all vectors
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
satisfying the system of inequalities
Ax\leq b
.
Note that the number of solutions is potentially infinite.
To ensure that we can output each solution in

\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
(and implicitly eliminate the problem of an infinite solution space),
we assume that the absolute values of the components of a solution vector
are bounded by some function
2^{h(k)p(L)}
, where
L
is the instance-size,

h
is a computable function, and
p
is a polynomial.
Otherwise, it could happen that simply outputting a single solution cannot be done in
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
due to its size.
This can be easily enforced by adding appropriate entries to
A
and
b
.
Crucially, one can find in
\mathsf{FPT}
-time (w.r.t.
k
)
one solution vector where all components
have an
\mathsf{FPT}
-bounded binary representation (see
[
10
, Theorem 6.5]
).
We denote this algorithm by
\mathcal{A}
.

Proposition 5

Integer Linear Programming with k Variables
is in

\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

Proof

We utilize the
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, which either finds a solution vector where all components have
\mathsf{FPT}
-bounded size (in binary representation) or detects that no such solution exists.
If a solution has been found, say
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
, then we create new instances by adding (in)equalities of the form
(x_{1}=s_{1})\land\cdots\land(x_{i}=s_{i})\land(x_{i+1}>s_{i+1})
or
(x_{1}=s_{1})\land\cdots\land(x_{i}=s_{i})\land(x_{i+1}<s_{i+1})
for some
0\leq i<k
.
This produces
2k
sub-instances for any given instance.
Moreover, these sub-instances clearly partition the solution space of a given problem instance while excluding
the current solution
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
.
For each variable, we only need to keep up to two (in)equalities of this form (i.e., the most restrictive ones).
Hence, the number of (in)equalities in any given instance explored through our algorithm is at most
2k
more than in the original instance.
In other words, the size of all sub-instances thus produced is polynomially bounded in the size of the input problem instance.
Hence, by applying the “alternative output” technique (see
[
23
,
18
]
) recalled above, we thus achieve

\mathsf{FPT}
delay,
even without a bound on the depth of the search tree.

Generalization and formalization.

We now remove the depth bound
\gamma
entirely, and thus the focus is on finding a method by which, for any given solution
S
, we can find (and calculate in
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
) child instances for which the solution spaces are disjoint and exclude precisely
S
. We need a method
f

to generate these child instances, but also a method
g
to find a solution to any given instance. Also, if no solution exists, we want
f
to return the empty set.
The following definition captures these requirements.

Definition 5

For an enumeration problem
Q
, we call a tuple of functions
(f,g)
a
solution search partition algorithm
, if the following conditions hold:

•

g\colon\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\to\Sigma^{*}
is an
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
function such that for all
(I,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
:

–

g(I,k)
computes a solution
S\in Q(I,k)
, if it exists.

•

f\colon\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Sigma^{*}\to\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N})
is an
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
function such that for all
(I,k,S)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Sigma^{*}
:

–

if
Q(I,k)=\emptyset
, then
f(I,k,S)=\emptyset
,

–

Q(I,k)\setminus\{S\}=\bigcup_{(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in f(I,k,S)}Q(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})
,

–

for all
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in f(I,k,S)
,
|(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})|\leq|(I,k)|
and
k^{\prime}\leq k
,

–

for all
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime}),(I^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime})\in f(I,k,S)
,
(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\neq(I^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime})\Rightarrow Q(I^{\prime},k^{\prime})\cap Q(I^{\prime\prime},k^{\prime\prime})=\emptyset
.

Proposition 6

Let
Q
be a parameterized enumeration problem. If
Q
has a solution search partition algorithm
(f,g)
, then
Q
is in
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

For
Integer Linear Programming with k Variables

g
is the original
\mathsf{FPT}
algorithm, which we can apply directly as we do not need to generalize the problem setting for our enumeration. Function
f
is used to create the
2k
sub-instances with added inequalities. It is also responsible for
eliminating redundant (in)equalities to make sure that
we never get an instance with more than two additional (in)equalities per variable
(compared with the original system of inequalities).
The condition
k^{\prime}\leq k
on instances produced by
function
f
is trivially satisfied since we never add a variable.

4
Union Enumeration

In Section
3
, we discussed
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
algorithms that partition the solution space into parts that can be efficiently enumerated individually.
As we have outlined, for some problems, this can be naturally achieved.
However, for other problems and other classical techniques to design
\mathsf{FPT}
decision algorithms, preventing this overlap is less natural (e.g., in the problem considered below).
Thus, we next discuss a method for dealing with non-empty intersections
of some parts.

More concretely, suppose that the solution space
\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{S}_{i}

is given as a union, such that the
\mathcal{S}_{i}
’s are not
pairwise disjoint.
It would be tempting to enumerate one subset
\mathcal{S}_{i}
after the other and, before outputting
the next solution
S\in\mathcal{S}_{i}
,
check if it was already output when processing
\mathcal{S}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{S}_{i-1}
. However,
it may happen that we encounter a long sequence of already output solutions and, therefore,

\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
is no longer guaranteed. Instead, we use a technique developed in
[
22
, Sec. 2.5]
for enumerating elements of a union of sets without duplicates.
Besides the efficient enumerability of each set in the union (in our case with
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
), this technique requires membership checks to be efficiently doable (in our case in
\mathsf{FPT}
).
Thus, intuitively, we seek to first split the solution space into
\mathsf{FPT}
-many subsets of solutions such that, (i)
every
solution is part of some subset, (ii) every subset can be
enumerated
with
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
, and (iii)
checking
whether a solution is part of a subset can be done in
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
.

A clear application of this method is for derandomized color coding algorithms.
Thus, we will illustrate the method by considering the
Longest Path
problem, which asks for
simple paths
, i.e., paths that contain every vertex at most once, of length
k
in a given graph
G
, where
k
is the parameter. We here consider the length of a simple path to be the number of vertices it contains instead of the number of edges.

Proposition 7

Longest Path
is in
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

Proof

We are given a graph
G=(V,E)
and an integer
k
.
The idea of color coding introduced in
[
1
]
applied to
Longest Path
is the following:

1.

Introduce
k
colors and color each vertex
v\in V
in one color
\gamma(v)\in\{1,\dots,k\}
.

2.

For
C=\{1,\dots,k\}
, find a
C
-
colorful
path in
G
for
\gamma
, i.e., a path where every color
i\in C
appears exactly once on the path.

We first discuss how to find all colorful paths in
G
under a given coloring
\gamma\colon V\rightarrow\{1,\dots,k\}
.
For that, we follow a dynamic programming approach and compute, for every
C\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}
and
u\in V
,
the following set
P(C,u)
:

\displaystyle P(C,u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{v\in V{}\mid{}

\displaystyle\text{there exists a }C\text{-colorful path }(v_{1},\dots,v_{|C|})\text{ in }G\text{ such that }

\displaystyle v_{|C|-1}=v,v_{|C|}=u,\text{ and }C=\{\gamma(v_{1}),\dots,\gamma(v_{|C|})\}\}.

That is, for a vertex
u
and subset of colors
C\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}
, we keep track of those vertices
v
that are the predecessors of
u
on
C
-colorful paths that end in
u
.
Then, clearly,
P(\{1,\dots,k\},u)\neq\emptyset
if and only if there is a colorful path (of length
k
) that ends in
u
.

To compute all sets
P(C,u)
, we start by iterating through the edges
vw\in E,\gamma(w)\neq\gamma(v)
and add
v
to
P(\{\gamma(w),\gamma(v)\},w)
(symmetrically for
w
as well).
Then, for
|C|>2
and
\gamma(u)\in C
, we compute
P(C,u)
, using the equality

\displaystyle P(C,u)=\{v\in V\mid vu\in E\text{ and }P(C\setminus\{\gamma(u)\},v)\neq\emptyset\}.

Thus, in total, we can compute all
P(C,u)
in
\mathsf{FPT}
-time.
Moreover, we can enumerate all
\{1,\dots,k\}
-colorful paths in
G
for
\gamma
with
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
by using these sets.
Concretely, we can simply use
k
nested loops: The first loop iterates through all
v_{1}\in V
such that
P(\{1,\dots,k\},v_{1})\neq\emptyset
.
The second loop iterates through all
v_{2}\in P(\{1,\dots,k\},v_{1})
.
The
i
-th loop iterates through all
v_{i}\in P(\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus\{\gamma(v_{1}),\dots,\gamma(v_{i-2})\},v_{i-1})
.
By construction, none of the considered sets can be empty and each
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
considered is a unique
\{1,\dots,k\}
-colorful path because it neither shares a color with
v_{1},\cdots,v_{i-2}
nor with
v_{i-1}
.

Note that for a given coloring
\gamma
and path
\pi
, it is easy to decide if the algorithm described above will output
\pi
.
To do this, simply check whether
\pi
is
\{1,\dots,k\}
-colorful for
\gamma
.

Next we will discuss the colorings we use in more detail.
For this, we make use of
perfect hash families
.
A perfect hash family is a set of colorings
\Gamma
(using
k
colors) such that for any subset
\pi\subseteq V
of size
k
there exists a coloring
\gamma\in\Gamma
that colors the vertices
v\in\pi
with unique colors, i.e., if
u,v\in\pi,u\neq v
, then
\gamma(u)\neq\gamma(v)
.
According to
[
20
]
, we can find such a family of colorings
\Gamma
of size
e^{k}k^{O(logk)}\log n
in time
e^{k}k^{O(logk)}n\log n
, i.e., in
\mathsf{FPT}
-time.
Let
\Pi_{\gamma}
be the
\{1,\dots,k\}
-colorful paths for
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
Note that every path in
G
of length
k
is
\{1,\dots,k\}
-colorful for some
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

In total, we have that the set of all paths of length
k
is
\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\Pi_{\gamma}
, we can enumerate each
\Pi_{\gamma}
in
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
, and we can check membership of
\Pi_{\gamma}
in
\mathsf{FPT}
(actually also polynomial time).
Now let us apply the technique developed in
[
22
]
for avoiding duplicates.
To that end, we do the following:
We take an arbitrary order of the colorings
\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{|\Gamma|}\}=\Gamma
.
Then, we start enumerating
\Pi_{\gamma_{1}}
until the algorithm described above finds its first solution
\pi_{1}
.
At that point, we pause the enumeration of
\Pi_{\gamma_{1}}
and check whether one of
\Pi_{\gamma_{2}},\dots,\Pi_{\gamma_{|\Gamma|}}
contains
\pi_{1}
.
If this is the case, we discard
\pi_{1}
and simply start enumerating
\Pi_{\gamma_{2}}
up to the first solution
\pi_{2}
.
At that point, we also pause the enumeration of
\Pi_{\gamma_{2}}
and check whether one of
\Pi_{\gamma_{3}},\dots,\Pi_{\gamma_{|\Gamma|}}
contains
\pi_{3}
.
We continue this process recursively.
That is, we are searching for the first
\Pi_{\gamma_{i}}
whose next solution is
\pi_{i}
but which is not part of any
\Pi_{\gamma_{i+1}},\dots,\Pi_{\gamma_{|\Gamma|}}
.
Now we can output
\pi_{i}
and
not
output
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{i-1}
(they are discarded from
\Pi_{\gamma_{1}},\dots,\Pi_{\gamma_{i-1}}
).
Note that the solutions
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{i-1}
will be output later using a different coloring
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
We apply this recursive procedure until for every
\Pi_{\gamma_{i}}
every
\pi_{i}\in\Pi_{\gamma_{i}}
was either output or discarded.

Each recursive procedure call requires at most
|\Gamma|
-many calls to a
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
enumeration procedure and
|\Gamma|
-many
\mathsf{FPT}
membership calls.
Thus, in total,
Longest Path
is in
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

Generalization and formalization.

Intuitively, our union enumeration methods seeks to first split the solution space into subsets of solutions such that these subsets cover the entire solution space but they are, in general, not disjoint. To combine the enumeration algorithms of these subsets into an enumeration of the entire solution space while avoiding duplicates, we assign an instance
(I,k)
a finite number of identifiers (e.g. certain colorings on a graph)
c(I,k)=\{\gamma_{1},\cdots\gamma_{l}\}
, and we need a function
f
that enumerates a subset of solutions
P_{i}\subseteq Q(I,k)
for each identifier
\gamma_{i}
without taking too long between consecutive solutions. While we require that these
P_{i}
cover the entire set
Q(I,k)
, there may be pairwise overlaps. As has been discussed above, in order to guarantee

\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
, we use a technique developed in
[
22
]
for enumerating elements of a union of sets without duplicates. Besides the efficient enumerability of each set in the union (in our case
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
), this technique requires an (in our case
\mathsf{FPT}
) function
g
to decide whether a solution
S
belongs to the solution set
P_{i}
of a given identifier
\gamma_{i}
in order to quickly check if there is any overlap between solution sets.
We formalize this idea in the following definition.

Definition 6

For an enumeration problem
Q
, we call a tuple of functions
(c,f,g)
a
union enumeration algorithm
if, for some computable function
h\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and polynomial
p
, the following conditions hold:

•

c\colon\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*})
and
c(I,k)
can be computed in time

O(h(k)p(|(I,k)|))
,

•

f\colon\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Sigma^{*}\to\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*})
is such that for all
(I,k,\gamma)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Sigma^{*}
:

–

\bigcup_{\gamma\in c(I,k)}f(I,k,\gamma)=Q(I,k)
,

–

for every
\gamma\in c(I,k)
, the delay of
f(I,k,\gamma)
is
O(h(k)p(|(I,k)|))
,

•

g\colon\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Sigma^{*}\times\Sigma^{*}\to\{0,1\}
is such that for all
(I,k,\gamma,S)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Sigma^{*}\times\Sigma^{*}
:

–

g(I,k,\gamma,S)=1
if and only if
{S\in f(I,k,\gamma)}
,

–

g(I,k,\gamma,S)
can be computed in time
O(h(k)p(|(I,k)|))
.

It remains to make explicit how to efficiently enumerate a union of non-necessarily disjoint sets.
This is done in the proof of the following proposition (for details, see
Appendix
0.D
).

Proposition 8

Let
Q
be a parameterized enumeration problem. If
Q
has a union enumeration algorithm
(f,g,c)
, then
Q
is in
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

To fit our
Longest Path
algorithm from the proof of Proposition
7

into this framework, we would first compute a perfect hash family
\Gamma
and,
for given instance
I=G=(V,E)
, set

c(G,k)=\Gamma
. Moreover, we define
f(G,k,\gamma)
as the
set of
\{1,\dots,k\}
-colorful paths, and
g(G,k,\gamma,\pi)
to indicate if

\pi
is a
\{1,\dots,k\}
-colorful path. As discussed above, all of these steps can be done in
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
, resp.
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
.

5
Iterative Compression

Iterative compression is another classic method to develop
\mathsf{FPT}
decision algorithms. Intuitively, the basic idea
is to start with a solution
S_{\text{sub}}
of a small sub-instance
(I_{\text{sub}},k)
, and to gradually increase the size of the sub-instance while maintaining a solution until we arrive back at the original instance
(I,k)
.
Maintaining the solution
S_{\text{sub}}
and growing the instance
(I_{\text{sub}},k)
is done in two steps:
(1) We start by growing
I_{\text{sub}}
to
I_{\text{next}}
while, at the same time, we increase the parameter
k
to
k^{\prime}
.
Here, the increase in the parameter and the access to a solution of the smaller instance should help us to efficiently find a solution
S_{\text{next}}^{\prime}
of
(I_{\text{next}},k^{\prime})
.
(2) Then, we
compress

S_{\text{next}}^{\prime}
down to a solution
S_{\text{next}}
of
(I_{\text{next}},k)
.

We thus naturally arrive at a solution of
(I,k)
if it exists.
To transform a decision iterative compression algorithm into an enumeration one, it seems natural to simply transform the compression step into an enumeration algorithm.
That is, instead of compressing
S_{\text{next}}^{\prime}
down to a single solution of
(I_{\text{next}},k)
, we should aim at an algorithm that manages to enumerate
all
solutions of
(I_{\text{next}},k)
with the help of
S_{\text{next}}^{\prime}
.
In fact, the adaptation is only necessary in the very last compression step, i.e., where
(I_{\text{next}},k)
is our original instance.

We will illustrate how an iterative compression decision algorithm can be transformed into an enumeration algorithm with the well-known

Vertex Cover
, i.e.,
given a graph
G=(V,E)
, we are looking for vertex covers of size
\leq k
where
k
is the parameter.
Recall, that a set
S\subseteq V
is a vertex cover if
G[V\setminus S]
only contains isolated vertices.

Proposition 9

Vertex Cover
is in
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

Proof

Let
(G,k)
be an instance of
Vertex Cover
.
For the graph
G=(V,E)
with
V=\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{n}\}
, let
G_{i}
denote the subgraph induced by the vertex set
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i}\}
.
These graphs
G_{i}
will be the sub-instances considered by the iterative compression algorithm.
We can do the following:

First, note that, trivially,
\emptyset
is a vertex cover of
G_{0}=(\emptyset,\emptyset)
and, hence,
(G_{0},k)
can be our starting instance and
\emptyset
our initial solution
S_{0}
.
Then, inductively, for a vertex cover
S_{i}
of
G_{i}
of size
\leq k
, the set
S^{\prime}_{i+1}=S_{i}\cup\{v_{i+1}\}
is a vertex cover of
G_{i+1}
of size
\leq k+1
.
To compress
S^{\prime}_{i+1}
to a vertex cover of size
k
of
G_{i+1}
, we observe that we can partition the set of
all vertex covers of
G_{i+1}
according to their intersection with
S^{\prime}_{i+1}
.
Conversely, given a vertex cover
S
of
G_{i+1}
, we can split
S^{\prime}_{i+1}
into subsets

C,F\subseteq S^{\prime}_{i+1}
with
S^{\prime}_{i+1}\cap S=C
and
S^{\prime}_{i+1}\setminus S=F
.
Let
N_{G_{i+1}}(F)
be the union of the neighbors of the vertices
F
in
G_{i+1}
, excluding
F
itself.
Clearly, if a vertex cover
S
of
G_{i+1}
does not contain any of the vertices in
F
, then it must contain all of
N_{G_{i+1}}(F)
. In fact, there exists a vertex cover
S
having
S\cap S_{i+1}=C
if and only if
C\cup N_{G_{i+1}}(F)
is a vertex cover.
Hence, to compress
S^{\prime}_{i+1}
, it suffices to iterate over all partitionings
S^{\prime}_{i+1}=C\cup F
.
Note that there are only up to
\mathsf{FPT}
-many partitions.
For each partition, we simply have to check whether
S_{i+1}=C\cup N_{G_{i+1}}(F)
is a vertex cover of size at most
k
.

As long as
i+1<n
, we can use
the first
vertex cover
S_{i+1}
of size
\leq k
of

G_{i+1}
as the compressed solution. (The decision procedure would also do this at
i+1=n
.)
Thus, we proceed with
S_{i+1}
to
G_{i+2}
.
In contrast, at
i+1=n
, we now have to output
all
vertex covers of
G_{i+1}=G
of size
\leq k
.
The good thing is that the compression procedure nicely allows for that.
To do so, we simply go over all
S_{n}=S_{i+1}=C\cup N_{G}(F)
that are vertex covers of size
\leq k
.
Then, for each such
S_{n}
, we output every
\hat{S}_{n}\supseteq S_{n}
where
\hat{S}_{n}\setminus S_{n}\subseteq V\setminus S^{\prime}_{n}
and
|\hat{S}_{n}|\leq k
.
That is, we output
S_{n}
and all supersets of
S_{n}
with

\leq k
elements,
obtained by only adding vertices from
V\setminus S^{\prime}_{n}
.
This guarantees that we will never produce duplicates.
Moreover, it is easy to see that this enumeration can be done with
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
.
Thus, in total, this enumeration version of iterative compression solves
Vertex Cover
with
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
.

Generalization and formalization.

To transform decision algorithms based on iterative compression into enumeration
algorithms, we describe the
growing
and
compression
step very abstractly.
Concretely, we
grow
the instances
I
one character
\sigma\in\Sigma
at a time.
Thus, from a solution
S_{\text{sub}}
of
(I_{\text{sub}},k)
we want to be able to determine a solution
S^{\prime}_{\text{next}}
of
(I_{\text{sub}}\cdot\sigma,k+1)
in the growing step, and then compress it down to a solution
S_{\text{next}}
of
(I_{\text{sub}}\cdot\sigma,k)
.
These two steps are performed by the functions
g
and
c
, respectively, in the definition below.
Note that for the iterative compression method to work, we need solvability to be preserved when taking sub-instances.
Hence, we restrict ourselves here to enumeration problems
Q
which
are such that for any character
\sigma\in\Sigma
, if
Q(I\cdot\sigma,k)\neq\emptyset
, then
Q(I,k)\neq\emptyset
. Note that, while we formalize these steps as growing and compressing
k
by 1 and
I
by a single character, these steps obviously also work for other ways of growing and compressing instances and solutions.

Definition 7

Let
Q
be a enumeration problem such that for any character
\sigma\in\Sigma
, if
Q(I\cdot\sigma,k)\neq\emptyset
, then
Q(I,k)\neq\emptyset
.
We call a tuple of functions
(g,c)
an iterative compression algorithm for
Q
,
if
the following conditions hold:

•

g\colon\Sigma^{*}\times\Sigma\times\mathbb{N}\times\Sigma^{*}\to\Sigma^{*}
is an
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
function such that, for
(I,k,S)\in Q
,
we have
g(I,\sigma,k,S)\in Q(I\cdot\sigma,k+1)
for any
\sigma\in\Sigma
,

•

c\colon\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}\times\Sigma^{*}\to\mathcal{P}(\Sigma^{*})
is a function which, for any
(I,k+1,S)\in Q
, enumerates all
S^{\prime}\in Q(I,k)
with
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
.

An
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
algorithm based on this definition works as follows.
We first restrict our problem to a small part, where finding a solution is trivial (e.g., a small or empty subgraph for graph problems or a constant sized
I
in general).
Then we apply two steps repeatedly: The first step, corresponding to the function
g
, is the growing step, in which we take our reduced instance and solution and reintroduce deleted information one step at a time, increasing both instance and parameter such that we can easily find a new solution
S
to this new instance
(I^{\prime},k+1)
.
The second step, corresponding to the function
c
, is the compression step, where we use
S
to find all solutions in
Q(I^{\prime},k)
(and stop if none are found). We continue rebuilding and then compressing (until the first solution is found) until we arrive at our initial instance and can now output all solutions in
Q(I,k)
.
Note that we could actually allow the parameter in each iteration of
g
to grow by a function
b(k)
,
provided that
c
can compress the solution down to size
k
.

Proposition 10

Let
Q
be a parameterized problem. If
Q
has an iterative compression algorithm
(g,c)
, then
Q
is in
\mathsf{DelayFPT}
.

To relate our algorithm from the proof of Proposition
9

to this framework, we have used a slightly more general growing function
g
, which
allowed us to extend a solution by yet another vertex (and not just a single character). The function
c
takes a vertex cover
S
of size
k+1
as input and
enumerates all vertex covers of size
\leq k
of the same graph. This enumeration
is done by partitioning the resulting vertex covers according to their
intersection with
S
.

6
Dynamic Programming

Dynamic programming (DP) is a widely used method
for solving problems by
splitting instances recursively into ever smaller sub-instances,
and ultimately inductively combining the solutions
of smaller sub-instances to solutions of bigger ones.
DP also has many applications in
the area of fixed-parameter algorithms, see, e.g., the

problem revisited in Section

.
Above all,
DP algorithms use a table

, whose size is bounded by

, to
represent the sub-instances of interest.
Then the minimum machinery needed are

from smaller sub-instances to bigger ones, “backlinks” (from a bigger sub-instance to smaller ones) to keep track of the “provenance” of each entry in

The DP-based enumeration algorithm discussed in Section

was rather straightforward, as every sub-instance
had a unique backlink.
However, in general, quite some additional care is required to avoid duplicates.

To illustrate the additional measures often needed to get a proper

\mathsf{FPT}
-enumeration algorithm via DP, we consider the
Steiner Tree
problem.
That is, we are given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
with an edge weight function assigning positive weights to each edge and a subset
K\subseteq V
of “terminal nodes”.
A

for

in a graph

is a connected subgraph
H
of
G
that contains
K
. The weight of a subgraph

is the sum of its edge weights.
Then the enumeration problem consists of enumerating all Steiner trees with minimum weight.
The decision problem (i.e., does there exist a Steiner tree of weight
\leq B
for some bound

)
is known to be
\mathsf{FPT}
w.r.t.
k=|K|
. It has an elegant
\mathsf{FPT}
-algorithm (see Section 6.1.2 in
[
10
]
), which works as follows:

W.l.o.g., we may assume that
|K|>1
and that each terminal has degree 1 in
G
and its neighbor is a non-terminal.
The first assumption is justified as otherwise
(K,\emptyset)
is the only minimal weight Steiner tree and the second assumption is justified as otherwise we can simply modify
G
to have this property.
That is, for each
k\in K
a new neighbor
v_{k}\not\in V
to
v_{k}
with an arbitrarily weighted edge.
Then,
K^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{v_{k}\mid k\in K\}
are the new terminal nodes for
G^{\prime}=(V\cup K^{\prime},E\cup\{kv_{k}\mid k\in K\})
.
Note that
(G,K)
and
(G^{\prime},K^{\prime})
admit the same minimal weight Steiner tree (modulo adding/removing
K^{\prime}
).

The data structure to be propagated by the DP algorithm consists
of entries
T[D,v]
with
D\subseteq K
and
v\in V\setminus K
. Then
T[D,v]=w
means that the minimum weight of a Steiner tree of

D\cup\{v\}
in
G
is

. The idea of the vertex
v
(which of course does not necessarily have degree 1 in
G
) is
that we combine Steiner trees of “sub-instances” (i.e., two disjoint subsets
D_{1},D_{2}\subseteq K
) by merging them at vertex
v
to get a Steiner tree of
D_{1}\cup D_{2}
. In the sequel,
we will refer to Steiner trees with minimal weight simply as “Steiner trees” (omitting the minimality).
Moreover, we will denote the set of Steiner trees of
D\cup\{v\}
in
G
as
\mathcal{S}(D,v)
and call them
the “solutions” of
T[D,v]
. The set of Steiner trees of
G
is obtained as the union of those
\mathcal{S}(K,v)
, such that

T[K,v]
is minimum over all
v
.

The data structure
T
is filled in as follows: as initialization step of
T
,
we compute all entries of the form
T[\{t\},v]=\mathrm{dist}(t,v)
, where
t\in K

,
and
\mathrm{dist}(t,v)
denotes the distance
(i.e., the minimal weight of a connecting path)
from

to
v

Then, existing entries of

are combined recursively via the formula

T[D,v]=\min_{\parbox[t]{42.0pt}{\scriptsize\mbox{$u\in V\setminus K$} \\
\mbox{$\emptyset\neq D^{\prime}\subsetneq D$}}}\{T[D^{\prime},u]+T[D\setminus D^{\prime},u]+\mathrm{dist}(u,v)\}

(1)

Intuitively, a Steiner tree for
D\cup\{v\}
is obtained by merging Steiner trees
for
D^{\prime}\cup\{u\}
and
(D\setminus D^{\prime})\cup\{u\}

at vertex
u
and then adding a minimal weight connecting path from

u
to
v
. This includes the special case that
u=v
.
It is shown in
[
10
]
that
one thus gets all entries
T[D,v]
with
\emptyset\neq D\subseteq K

and
v\in V\setminus K
. Hence, deciding if a
Steiner tree of weight
\leq B
exists comes down to checking
if an entry
T[K,v]\leq B
exists.
To also output a solution in case of a yes-instance, one has to maintain backlinks
for each application of the above recursion and assemble a Steiner tree of
D\cup\{v\}
by merging
two subtrees and adding a shortest path as described above.
Maintaining such backlinks and
combining solutions from smaller sub-instances to solutions of bigger ones
is routine in the area of DP algorithms.

However, if we want to enumerate
all
Steiner trees,
we are immediately faced with the challenge of avoiding
duplicates
.
Indeed, an entry
T[D,v]
can possibly be
obtained by combining entries
T[D^{\prime},u_{1}]
and

but also by combining entries

and/or

.
Likewise, the solutions of

(i.e., Steiner trees of

obtained by combining solutions of
\mathcal{S}[D^{\prime},u_{1}]
and
\mathcal{S}[D\setminus D^{\prime},u_{1}]

and the solutions of
\mathcal{S}[D,v]

obtained by combining solutions of
\mathcal{S}[D^{\prime\prime},u_{2}]
and
\mathcal{S}[D\setminus D^{\prime\prime},u_{2}]

are not necessarily disjoint.
Hence, recursively iterating through all possible
backlinks, starting from the

\mathcal{S}[K,v]
with minimal weight,
would inevitably produce duplicates.

To achieve unique provenance, intuitively, we have to make sure that for each Steiner tree
S\in\mathcal{S}[D,v]
there is a unique partitioning
D=D^{\prime}\cup D\setminus D^{\prime}
and a unique merge node

.
This requires several modifications
compared with the decision algorithm, namely: (1) we

(2) we extend entries in table

is a bit indicating if, in the Steiner trees of

represented by

has degree 1 (indicated by

) or degree

(indicated by

),
and (3) we impose the restriction that the recursion in Equation (
1
) may only be applied if

contains the smallest

has degree 1 in all Steiner trees thus represented.
Analogously, we adapt the definition of
\mathcal{S}[D,v,b]

We claim that, with these modifications, we indeed achieve a unique provenance of each entry

and that we can recurse through the backlinks of these entries so as to output all Steiner trees
of a given problem instance with
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}

Conclusion

We have revisited several well-established methods for designing
\mathsf{FPT}
decision algorithms
in order to transform them into
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
enumeration algorithms.
We have illustrated by various examples the subtle differences and the special care required for enumeration algorithms –
in particular, the need to obtain all solutions and to avoid duplicates.

While we explored the design of
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
enumeration algorithms starting from
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-time}
techniques, one could also proceed in the
other direction and adapt enumeration techniques to the parameterized setting. For instance, the well-known reverse-search method
[
2
]
,
as well as the new and very promising proximity search method
[
5
]
would probably be worth a systematic investigation from the
\mathsf{FPT}
point of view.

T[D^{\prime},u,1]
, i.e.,
u
has degree 1 in all Steiner trees thus represented.

Analogously, we adapt the definition of
\mathcal{S}[D,v,b]
.

It remains to show that these measures indeed make the provenance unique.
Let
S
be an arbitrary Steiner tree in
\mathcal{S}[D,v,1]
seen as rooted in
v
.
Thus,
v
has degree
1
and we can follow the descendants of
v
until we find the first node
u\in V
of degree
\geq 2
in
S
(if there is no such node, then
|D|=1
).
Then, there is one child
c
of
u
whose subtree contains the smallest terminal
d_{\min}
in
D
.
Let
S_{c},S_{u}
be subtrees rooted in
u
where
S_{c}
contains
c
and its descendants, and
S_{u}
the remainder.
We denote the terminals (leaves) of
S_{c}
by
D^{\prime}
and the remaining terminals by
D\setminus D^{\prime}
.
Then
S_{c}\in\mathcal{S}[D^{\prime},u,1]
while
S_{u}\in\mathcal{S}[D\setminus D^{\prime},u,0]\cup\mathcal{S}[D\setminus D^{\prime},u,1]
.
Furthermore,
S
is composed of
S_{c},S_{u}
, and a minimal path from
u
to
v
.
For
S\in\mathcal{S}[D,v,0]
, the only difference is that
u=v
.

We note that all of these choices are unique, and thus, the provenance is unique.
We could not have chosen a
u
further down as then some edges would be counted twice in the recursion, i.e., once in a
T[\cdot,u]
part and once in
\mathrm{dist}(u,v)
.
Furthermore, as
D^{\prime}
must contain the smallest terminal and
u
has to have degree 1 in the left sub-Steiner tree, also
D^{\prime}
cannot be chosen differently.

Towards an
\mathsf{FPT}\text{-delay}
-enumeration algorithm for the
Steiner Tree
problem, the only obstacle remaining
is to discuss how to recurse through the backlinks.
To that end, we start by picking an arbitrary terminal
k\in K
and its unique neighbor
v_{k}\in V\setminus K
.
All Steiner trees have to go through
v_{k}
and are, thus, exactly
\mathcal{S}[K,v_{k},0]
.
Then, for arbitrary terminals
D\subseteq K
, vertices
v\in V\setminus K
, and
b\in\{0,1\}
, to enumerate the Steiner trees
\mathcal{S}[D,v,b]
we recursively iterate through all
u
that were used in the recursion for
T[D,v,b]
.
This gives us a split
D=D^{\prime}\cup D\setminus D^{\prime}
and in 3 nested loops we simply iterate through
S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}[D^{\prime},u,1]
,
S^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}[D\setminus D^{\prime},u,0]\cup\mathcal{S}[K\setminus D^{\prime},u,1]
, and shortest paths
P
between
u
and
v
.
Each combination of
S^{\prime},S^{\prime\prime}
, and
P
constitutes a unique Steiner tree.
