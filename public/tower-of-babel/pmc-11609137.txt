Treatment of metastatic breast cancer by stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) and stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)
Authors: Xiaomin Li, Yu Sun, Liang Tang, Yan Li, Xiaoqin Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeThe role of local ablative radiotherapy (stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT)/stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)) in the management of metastatic breast cancer (mBC) patients remains unclear. This study aimed to assess the efficacy of SBRT/SRS in oligometastatic and oligoprogressive breast cancer patients.MethodsTotally 80 mBC patients with oligometastatic disease (OMD) and 80 with oligoprogressive disease (OPD) to ≤5 metastatic lesions were retrospectively analyzed. The endpoint was overall survival and progression-free survival, and univariate and multivariate analyses were performed for survival analysis.ResultsTotally 160 mBC cases (80 OMD and 80 OPD cases) were analyzed, with a total of 291 treated metastases. In the study of OMD, we analyzed 30 cases with oligo-recurrence and 50 cases with sync-oligometastases. The median follow-up time was 46 months, and 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS rates for all patients were 89.8%, 77.6%, and 67.3%, respectively, and the 1-, 2-, and 3-year PFS rates were 71.4%, 44.9%, and 34.7% respectively. In multivariate analysis (MVA), treatment for oligometastases and non-triple-negative status predicted favorable OS. In patients with oligometastases, median OS was 58 months, and 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS rates were 100%, 91.7%, and 83.3%, respectively; median OS in patients with oligoprogression was 35 months, and 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS rates were 80%, 64%, and 52%, respectively. In mBC cases with limited brain metastases administered SRS, poor OS was detected in patient age under 45 years (P = 0.041), triple-negative cases (P = 0.025), and those with OPD (P = 0.022). In OMD, a significant improvement in PFS was observed in the oligo-recurrence group compared to the sync-oligometastases group (P = 0.013).ConclusionPatients administered local ablative radiotherapy (SBRT/SRS) for oligometastases have better overall survival than those treated for oligoprogression. SBRT/SRS may be beneficial for young and non-triple-negative mBC cases. The presence of oligo-recurrence can predict a favorable prognosis of oligometastases in patients with mBC treated with SBRT/SRS.

Introduction

Totally 90% metastatic breast cancer cases present with oligometastatic disease (5 or less metastases) [1, 2]. In 1995, Hellman and Weichselbaum firstly introduced the concept of oligometastatic disease (OMD) [3], a clinical state between local–regional disease and widespread metastasis. Currently, this subset of patients with oligometastasis is characterized by a limited number of metastatic lesions (≤5), which is amenable to local ablative radiotherapy, including stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) [4]. Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) is increasingly applied for the treatment of extracranial oligometastases, accurately localizing and delivering a high dose of radiation to the tumor with limited toxicity. When SBRT is employed for central nervous system tumors, it is termed stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), which is an important treatment option for brain metastases. Several prospective and retrospective studies have demonstrated local ablative radiotherapy for oligometastatic cancer achieves durable tumor control and prolongs overall survival [5–9]. Besides, the classification of oligometastases into oligo-recurrence and sync-oligometastases has been extensively studied in various cancer types, primarily lung cancer, as highlighted by Niibe et al. and Chang et al. [10, 11]. These classifications generally aim to differentiate between patients based on the timing and control status of the primary tumor. Oligo-recurrence refers to a situation where a patient with a primary tumor that is controlled or in remission develops a limited number (typically 1–5) of new metastases. Sync-oligometastases refers to a condition where a patient has an active primary tumor along with a limited number (typically 1–4) of metastases.

As a more recent term, oligoprogressive disease (OPD) is an emerging indication of SBRT/SRS as a treatment option for metastatic cases. Different from OMD, OPD is characterized by progression of a limited number of metastases, with all other metastatic lesions systematically controlled [12]. Patients with OPD initially show oligometastatic disease or polymetastatic or widely metastatic disease. OPD results from clones resistant to systemic therapy in a limited number of sites [13, 14]. Local ablative radiotherapy (SBRT/SRS) for these clones may prolong disease control of other lesions sensitive to systemic treatment and delay the need for changing systemic therapy [15].

Despite the growing number of publications reporting local ablative radiotherapy (SBRT/SRS) for treating metastatic cancers, mostly focusing on lung, prostate and colorectal cancers [16–18], the role of SBRT/SRS in the management of metastatic breast cancer, particularly progressive disease, requires further investigation, due to tumor heterogeneity.

This study focuses on local ablative radiotherapy (SBRT/SRS) for metastatic breast cancer with oligometastases and oligoprogression.

Materials and methods

Patient population

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University, and each patient provided signed informed consent. From 2000 to 2023, the electronic medical records and radiotherapy data of 160 female metastatic breast cancer patients administered local ablative radiotherapy were retrospectively reviewed. Inclusion criteria were: (1) oligometastases, metastatic lesions ≤5; (2) oligoprogression, only progressing lesions (≤5), with all other metastases controlled.

Subgroup analysis was performed for two subtypes, which included the following: (1) non-triple-negative breast cancer, i.e., luminal-HER2-negative breast cancer (hormone receptor (estrogen receptor [ER] and/or progesterone receptor [PR])-positive and HER2-negative) and HER2-positive breast cancer (HER2 overexpressed and amplified with any ER/PR status); (2) triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), i.e., ER negative, PR negative, and HER2 neither overexpressed nor amplified.

Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy planning was determined by treating physicians based on patients’ clinical parameters, including tumor size and location. The gross target volume (GTV) was delineated on CT imaging, MRI and PET scans (if available). The planning treatment volume (PTV) was obtained with 3–5 mm uniform margin around the GTV. For lung or liver metastases, four-dimensional CT (4D-CT) was performed for more accurate delineation of the GTV.

Patients were followed up at 1 month after SBRT and underwent CT ± MRI at 3, 6, 12, 18 and 24 months, and yearly thereafter.

Outcomes and statistical analysis

The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the first SBRT to death or the last follow-up visit, and progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from the first SBRT to local or distant progression, or die. OS and PFS were determined by the Kaplan–Meier method and the log-rank test. Cox regression was performed for univariate analysis (UVA) to compare the subgroups. Factors with P < 0.2 in UVA were entered in the multivariate analysis (MVA) model to identify independent predictive factors. SPSS version 26.0 (IBM) was employed for data analysis.

Results

The characteristics of metastatic breast cancer patients treated with local ablative radiotherapy and lesions treated are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. A total of 160 breast cancer patients treated with ≤5 metastases, with 212 extra-cranial lesions treated with SBRT and 79 brain metastases treated with SRS, were retrospectively enrolled between 2000 and 2023. 160 patients at a median age of 45 years (26–73) underwent local ablative radiotherapy: 80 patients were treated for oligometastases and 80 patients for oligoprogression. Of these patients, 79 patients (49.4%) their metastases occurred metachronously, and 40 patients (25.0%) were triple negative subtype. In our study, the most common metastatic site was the lung with 49 patients having lung metastases for a total of 95 lesions (32.6%) treated with SBRT. Brain and liver were the next most common metastatic sites with 79 lesions (27.1%) and 74 lesions (25.4%) respectively. A majority of patients (n = 68, 42.5%) had one metastatic lesion. The outcomes of patients treated with SRS for brain metastases (1–2 lesions) were also shown in Tables 4 and 5. A total of 69 breast cancer patients underwent intracranial SRS for a limited number (1–2 lesions) of brain metastases, and controlled extra-cranial metastases. Discussion

Growing evidence suggests benefits for SBRT in the treatment of metastatic cancers, with breast cancer cases with 5 or fewer metastases having a more favorable prognosis compared with cases with other primary sites [19, 20]. However, the role of SBRT in the management of metastatic breast cancer patients remains unclear. This study retrospectively assessed the clinical outcomes of breast cancer patients with oligometastatic or oligoprogressive disease administered SBRT in addition to systemic therapy.

In the MVA model, treatment indication and molecular subtype were significant predictors of OS in metastatic breast cancer patients. The difference in OS between the OMD and OPD groups was statistically significant. The biological features of tumors in both groups may be different. In patients with OMD, radical treatment targets all metastatic lesions to achieve complete remission. In OPD cases, local ablative radiotherapy may delay the changes of systemic therapies and potentially decrease toxicity from such treatments, thus resulting in favorable prognosis. Moreover, the 2-year OS rate of OMD cases in this study was 91.7%, which was comparable to that reported by other metastatic breast cancer studies, i.e., 2-year OS rates of 57–100% [9, 21–25]. A 2-year OS of 64% was detected for OPD patients in this study. By contrast, SBRT conferred no benefit in a metastatic breast cancer population examined by a randomized phase II study [26]. The overall OS rate in this study was 77.6%, which was comparable to that reported by Weykamp et al. (62%) [23]. Weykamp et al. also retrospectively examined SBRT for metastatic breast cancer. Totally 70% patients had OMD, and 30% had OPD. Moreover, a high proportion of lung and liver metastases (66%) and consequently a low proportion of bone metastases (33%) constituted the population of the latter study, similar to the design of this study. In subgroup analysis, TNBC cases had poorer overall survival compared with patients with other subtypes (P = 0.015), with 2-year OS rates of 66.7% and 81.1%, respectively. These results suggest that non-triple-negative mBC patients with oligometastases are the ideal population for SBRT.

Individuals with a limited number of brain metastases and controlled extracranial lesions are frequently examined in daily clinical practice. To date, SRS is considered an important treatment option for limited brain metastases [27, 28]. Moreover, breast cancer cases with brain metastasis generally have more favorable outcomes versus individuals affected by other primary cancers with brain metastasis due to more effective systemic therapies [29]. Currently, many publications mostly focused on the outcomes of extra-cranial stereotactic body radiotherapy for metastatic cancers, and few studies have reported the efficacy of radical local radiotherapy in a mixed cohort of brain and extra-cranial metastatic lesions in breast cancer cases. Totally 69 patients in the OMD (n = 33) and OPD (n = 36) groups administered SRS for 1–2 brain metastases and SBRT for extra-cranial lesions after standard systemic therapy were included in the present study. Radical local radiotherapy resulted in prolonged progression-free survival and overall survival: about half of all patients administered SRS had no local or distant progression or died at 1 year. Breast cancer cases with limited brain metastases and extracranial metastatic lesions may benefit from SBRT plus SRS. Regarding age at diagnosis, patients under 45 years had a 2-year OS of 100%, which was higher compared with that of cases over 45 (50%). Excellent overall survival was also detected in non-triple-negative breast cancer cases compared with TNBC patients. The subgroup of patients with limited brain metastases, and young and non-triple-negative breast cancer could be the ideal population of a randomized trial to assess less intensive treatments following local ablative radiotherapy.

Our study subdivided oligometastatic breast cancer patients into oligo-recurrence and sync-oligometastases categories. Patients in the oligo-recurrence group demonstrated significantly better progression-free survival (PFS) compared to those in the sync-oligometastases group. However, no significant differences in overall survival (OS) were observed between the two groups. Oligo-recurrence: According to existing literature, this classification often reflects a scenario where the primary tumor is controlled, and patients typically have fewer metastases. Studies, such as those by Niibe et al. [10, 11], have demonstrated that patients with controlled primary tumors and oligometastases tend to have a better prognosis. Our finding of better PFS in this group supports these observations. Sync-oligometastases: this group is characterized by an active primary tumor and limited metastases, often associated with more complex clinical scenarios. Existing research suggests that these patients may experience more challenging treatment responses and potentially worse outcomes compared to those with controlled primary tumors. Our observation of poorer PFS in the sync-oligometastases group aligns with these findings. The significant difference in PFS between the two groups highlights the importance of the primary tumor’s status (controlled vs. active) in disease progression. Our results support the notion that the oligo-recurrence classification might be a useful prognostic indicator for PFS in breast cancer patients. The lack of significant differences in OS could be attributed to several factors. It is possible that while the classification impacts disease progression, it may not have a substantial effect on long-term survival, particularly if treatments for metastatic disease are similarly effective across both groups. This observation suggests that other factors, such as treatment modalities, overall health, and response to therapy, might play a more significant role in determining OS.

This study had limitations. The current data were from a single-center retrospective analysis, and the sample size was small.

In summary, this study demonstrated overall survival and progression-free survival benefit for local ablative radiotherapy (SBRT/SRS) in metastatic breast cancer cases with oligometastases and oligoprogression. Patients with oligometastases had enhanced overall survival than those with oligoprogression. Triple-negative mBC cases have poor overall survival. Our study also contributes to the understanding of how oligo-recurrence and sync-oligometastases classifications influence PFS in breast cancer patients. While significant differences in PFS were observed, the implications for OS remain less clear. Further multicenter prospective studies are warranted for the management of metastatic breast cancer.
