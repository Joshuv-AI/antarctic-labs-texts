Application of AI-facilitated handwashing monitoring system on food handlers
Authors: Yuan Guo, Qian Fang, Dan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Food handlers are a major route of foodborne disease transmission, yet handwashing in real food-service settings is often rushed and inconsistently performed. In a two-visit field study, 46 university canteen food handlers performed habitual (ad-hoc) handwashing during one visit and a standardized procedure guided by an AI-based computer-vision system during the other, with hand swabs collected before and after washing and enumerated on Plate Count Agar (PCA), Violet Red Bile Glucose Agar (VRBGA), and Baird-Parker Agar (BPA). AI-facilitated handwashing achieved significantly greater reductions than ad-hoc washing specifically for VRBGA-detected Enterobacteriaceae (p < 0.05), revealing an indicator-specific effect on transient, hygiene-relevant microorganisms. An ex vivo porcine-skin model and 16S rRNA gene sequencing converged to explain this pattern: handwashing preferentially removes transient microorganisms while exposing resident skin-associated bacteria, demonstrating that the choice of microbial indicator critically shapes how hand-hygiene efficacy is judged. Food handlers found the system highly acceptable, valuing it as a real-time reminder that reinforces correct technique and duration. Collectively, this work provides the first field-based microbiological evidence that AI-facilitated monitoring efficiently supports and improves hand-hygiene compliance, while reframing how such interventions should be evaluated in food-service settings.

Introduction

Food handlers have been implicated with a major role in the spread of many foodborne diseases (Table 1). According to surveillance data from the National Environmental Assessment Reporting System (NEARS) (2014–2022), contamination by infectious food workers is a leading contributing factor to bacterial and viral foodborne outbreaks, occurring through both bare-hand and gloved-hand contact1. In the United States, an analysis of 800 outbreaks from 2017 to 2019 found that approximately 40% were attributed to ill or infectious food workers2. A report analyzed 816 reports with 80,682 cases involving food workers collected from 1927 until 2006, and identified the leading associated pathogens including human norovirus, Salmonella enterica, hepatitis A virus, Staphylococcus aureus, Shigella spp., Streptococcus Streptococcus Lancefield groups A and G, and parasites Cyclospora, Giardia, and Cryptosporidium3. It is noteworthy that many of the outbreaks were due to asymptomatic infected food handlers, so that prevention of the infected workers from attending the working premises is challenging4,5. Effective handwashing thus becomes one of the most important strategies in the prevention of foodborne disease transmission via food handlers6.

Despite the recognized importance of hand hygiene, compliance with recommended handwashing practices remains inconsistent in real food-service environments7,8. Observational studies have shown that food workers may wash hands too briefly, skip procedural steps, or fail to wash at required moments during routine work7,9–11. Static posters and conventional training can communicate correct procedures, but they do not provide real-time feedback on whether handwashing is performed with sufficient duration, coverage, and technique12. This gap is particularly important in high-risk settings, such as ready-to-eat food preparation and food production environments, where transient microorganisms acquired from hands, food-contact surfaces, raw materials, or the working environment may contribute to contamination risk13,14.

Artificial intelligence (AI)-based computer vision systems have therefore emerged as potential tools for real-time hand hygiene monitoring and compliance support15. By recognizing handwashing gestures, assessing procedural completion, and providing immediate feedback, such systems may help standardize handwashing behavior and support long-term hygiene management in food-service settings. Previous work also suggests that AI-based monitoring can be acceptable to food industry stakeholders across different managerial levels15. However, the microbiological impact of AI-facilitated handwashing under real food-service conditions remains insufficiently characterized. In this study, we evaluated an AI-facilitated handwashing monitoring system among university canteen food handlers by comparing its microbiological impact with conventional ad-hoc handwashing, examining microbiota shifts after washing, and assessing food-handler feedback on system usability and acceptance.

Results

Microbial change after AI-facilitated handwashing

To evaluate the microbiological impact of AI-facilitated handwashing under real food-service conditions, we compared microbial changes after conventional ad-hoc handwashing and AI-facilitated standardized handwashing among food handlers. Forty-six food handlers working at the student canteens of the National University of Singapore (NUS) participated in the handwashing experiments conducted between August 26, 2022 and March 22, 2023 (NUS-IRB-2022-324). Hand swabs were collected before and after each handwashing event during two visits, in which participants performed ad-hoc handwashing in one visit and AI-facilitated standardized handwashing in the other. The hand-swab samples were analyzed using three culture-based assays. Plate Count Agar (PCA), a non-selective microbiological growth medium, was used to estimate total viable bacterial counts. Violet Red Bile Glucose Agar (VRBGA) was used to detect Enterobacteriaceae, a bacterial family mainly associated with the gastrointestinal tract of vertebrates, including humans; therefore, VRBGA-detected microorganisms in this study were interpreted as indicators of working-environment contamination or transient microorganisms on human hands. Baird-Parker Agar (BPA) counts were used as presumptive coagulase-positive staphylococci, many members of which are associated with resident skin flora16.

Significant before-after reductions were observed for PCA and VRBGA counts, but not for BPA counts (p < 0.05; Table 2). VRBGA generated significantly higher microbial load reductions than PCA and BPA (p < 0.05). The difference between ad-hoc washing and AI-facilitated washing was thus analyzed with the data generated by PCA and VRBGA. As a result, AI-facilitated washing was significantly more effective in reducing microbial loads than the ad-hoc washing only when detected with VRBGA (p < 0.05; Table 2B). The distribution of microbial load reductions from all handwashing events is shown in Fig. 1. Overall, 78% and 86% of recorded handwashing events resulted in reduced microbial loads when assessed using VRBGA after ad-hoc and AI-facilitated washing, respectively (Fig. 1C and D). In contrast, fewer events showed reductions when assessed using PCA (60% and 64%; Fig. 1A and B) or BPA (56% and 52%; Fig. 1E and F). These results indicated that the measurable advantage of AI-facilitated washing was most evident for VRBGA-detected microorganisms.

The finding that AI-facilitated improvement was detected by VRBGA suggested that bacterial removability from skin surfaces may differ among microbial groups. We therefore used an ex vivo porcine skin model to examine the removability of representative bacteria. S. aureus SA01 and methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 33591 were selected as representatives of Staphylococcaceae because staphylococci are common members of resident skin-associated microbiota16. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Salmonella Typhimurium ATCC 14028 were selected as representatives of Enterobacteriaceae, which have been widely used in challenge studies for evaluating cross-contamination risks within food-processing environments17,18. Bacterial suspensions were inoculated onto pre-treated porcine skin to simulate bacterial contamination on human hands, followed by washing and swab collection.

The ex vivo washing results showed that bacterial removability differed markedly among the tested strains. Staphylococcus strains were indeed found with stronger adhesion to the skin surfaces, as washing with and without soaps both induced significantly higher reductions for E. coli ATCC 25922 and S. Typhimurium ATCC 14028 than S. aureus SA01 and the MRSA ATCC 33591 (p < 0.05, Fig. 2). In addition, plain soap produced significantly greater reductions than water alone for all four strains (p < 0.05; Fig. 2), whereas antimicrobial soaps generally did not provide additional reductions compared with plain soap (p > 0.05; Fig. 2). These findings supported the use of plain soap as the standardized washing condition in the food-handler study and avoided introducing antimicrobial formulations as an additional confounding factor.

Together, the human results and ex vivo findings suggested that handwashing outcomes were strongly influenced by the microbial groups present on hands. AI-facilitated washing showed its clearest measurable benefit against Enterobacteriaceae-associated microorganisms, which were more consistent with transient contamination than resident skin microbiota. These findings also raised the possibility that post-wash plate counts may be influenced by shifts in the recovered hand microbiota, which was further examined by 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

Microbiota shifts after handwashing

After handwashing, the microbiota composition on human hands is expected to be changed, as indicated by previous studies19,20. To further examine whether plate-count changes after handwashing were associated with shifts in the recovered hand microbiota, selected samples were subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Specifically, three individuals with the largest increases in PCA counts after handwashing were selected as the Inc (increase) group, with samples collected before and after handwashing denoted as Inc(Before) and Inc(After), respectively (Fig. 3A). In parallel, three individuals with the largest decreases in PCA counts after handwashing were selected as the Dec (decrease) group, with samples collected before and after handwashing denoted as Dec(Before) and Dec(After), respectively (Fig. 3B).

The overall diversities and richness of microbes present on the hands did not show significant reductions after hand washing for both two types of individuals (data not shown). However, differences in microbial community composition were observed in the Inc group. At the operational taxonomic unit (OTU) level, samples collected before and after handwashing from individuals with post-wash PCA increases showed distinct clustering in the principal component analysis plot (A and B, Fig. 3C). LEfSe (Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size) analysis further identified taxonomic groups that differed between Inc(Before) and Inc(After) samples (Fig. 3D). Notably, reads assigned to the Staphylococcaceae family were more abundant after handwashing than before handwashing. Delving into the genus level (Figs. 3G), an elevation in the relative abundance of the Staphylococcus genus was also evident following hand washing. In contrast, such trends were not observed in individuals with the PCA count reductions after handwashing (Fig. 3E and F). Together, these results were consistent with the ex vivo and culture-based findings, suggesting that post-wash plate counts may reflect not only the overall reduction of microorganisms, but also changes in the composition of the recovered hand microbiota. In particular, the relative enrichment of skin-associated taxa such as Staphylococcaceae/Staphylococcus in selected samples may help explain why PCA counts did not always decrease after handwashing.

Food-handler feedback on the AI-facilitated handwashing monitoring system.

A post-session survey conducted during the second visit revealed that the AI-facilitated handwashing monitoring system was generally well-accepted. Food handlers reported high perceived usefulness, indicating that the system helped their overall handwashing performance (8.81 ± 1.05 out of 10, Fig. 4). Participants rated the system most highly for its role as a practical reminder in daily settings, specifically highlighting the step-by-step on-screen guidance, prompts to switch hands and gesture correction (9.02 ± 0.94 out of 10, Fig. 4). A comparatively lower score was assigned to the role in teaching the correct technique (7.14 ± 1.63 out of 10, Fig. 4). This may be attributed to frequent surveillance and training noted by 24 out of 43 respondents, suggesting a perception that they were already familiar with the procedure and did not require fundamental instruction.

Although 79% (34/43) of participants confidently claimed to adhere to the standardized procedure, observations indicated superficial compliance. Specifically, 71% (24/34) of handwashing events involved running through all required steps but with insufficient duration during ad-hoc handwashing. This tendency to rush aligned with perceived barriers identified in the survey. 28% (12/43) of food handlers felt the AI-facilitated procedure “lasted too long,” particularly during peak hours, suggesting that time pressure compromises handwashing quality. Furthermore, psychological barriers persisted. Despite assurances of data anonymization, 19% (8/43) of participants expressed concern regarding potential disciplinary actions under “camera monitoring”. However, this perceived supervision could paradoxically encourage better compliance by motivating food handlers to follow the recommended steps and duration.

Discussion

This study provides field evidence that AI-facilitated handwashing can improve the reduction of hygiene-relevant transient microorganisms under real food-service conditions. The clearest microbiological advantage of the AI-facilitated procedure was observed for VRBGA-detected microorganisms, whereas comparable improvements were not observed for total viable counts on PCA or presumptive staphylococci on BPA. These findings should therefore not be interpreted as demonstrating broad microbial superiority of AI-facilitated washing across all hand-associated microorganisms. Instead, they indicate a targeted effect on transient hygiene-related microorganisms, which are among the primary microbial targets of handwashing in food-service settings. This interpretation is important because effective hand hygiene in food handling is intended to reduce microorganisms acquired from the working environment, raw materials, food-contact surfaces, or gastrointestinal contamination pathways, rather than to sterilize the hands or eliminate resident skin microbiota21,22. Although the additional reduction achieved by AI-facilitated washing compared with ad-hoc washing was modest in magnitude, the direction and specificity of this effect support the role of standardized, AI-guided washing in improving control of hygiene-relevant transient indicators. Thus, the main microbiological contribution of the AI-facilitated system lies in improving removal of microbial groups most aligned with the intended purpose of handwashing, rather than producing a universal reduction of all detectable bacteria.

The indicator-specific response is also biologically plausible. Handwashing involves mechanical friction, rinsing, and redistribution of microorganisms on the skin surface, which may remove loosely attached transient microorganisms while simultaneously increasing the recovery of resident skin-associated bacteria through the detachment of squamous epithelial cells or exposure of deeper surface-associated microflora23. Staphylococcaceae, as typical resident skin taxa, are structurally embedded within the deeper stratum corneum, hair follicles, and sebaceous microenvironments24,25. The more thorough mechanical friction of a standardized, rigorous multi-step procedure may therefore be more likely than cursory ad-hoc washing to dislodge such cells and expose these deeper-seated organisms; while our design did not directly quantify this effect, it offers a plausible explanation for why total viable counts did not consistently decrease and why PCA counts increased in some cases after standardized washing. Consistent with this interpretation, samples with post-wash PCA increases showed enrichment of skin-associated taxa such as Staphylococcaceae/Staphylococcus, and the ex vivo porcine skin model likewise showed that Enterobacteriaceae were more readily removed than Staphylococcus strains. Plate-count outcomes may be further influenced by methodological factors, as no single hand-sampling method fully captures the hand microbiota19,20 and recovered counts can be affected by competition among heterotrophic bacteria26. Together, these findings indicate that food-handler hands act as an interface between the human body and the working environment, where transient microorganisms rather than the total culturable community, are the more appropriate targets for evaluating handwashing performance. This also explains why VRBGA was the most responsive indicator for detecting the targeted effect of AI-facilitated washing in this study. Because the 16S analysis was performed on six individuals representing extreme PCA responses, however, these microbiota findings should be regarded as exploratory and mechanistic rather than definitive population-level evidence.

The ex vivo model also informed the choice of washing agent used in the field protocol. Plain soap produced greater reductions than water alone for all tested bacteria, whereas antimicrobial soaps did not show a clear additional benefit over plain soap under most conditions. This is consistent with previous reports of limited additional benefit from antimicrobial soaps outside professional healthcare settings27,28, as well as regulatory concerns over certain antibacterial ingredients and their long-term use29,30; for example, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) banned over-the-counter antibacterial soaps containing triclosan and triclocarban in 2016, as these were not shown to be more effective than plain soap in preventing illness31. These results indicate that AI-facilitated handwashing does not need to rely on specialized antimicrobial products to provide operational value; its strength lies instead in improving the consistency and completeness of a standard handwashing procedure.

Beyond the microbiological outcome, an important contribution of the AI-facilitated system is its ability to convert handwashing from a habit-driven and inconsistently performed practice into a guided, feedback-enabled, and monitorable hygiene process. Although there is consensus over the importance of hand hygiene in food handling across the world, hitherto there is no standardized handwashing procedure developed for food handlers. Recommendations from food safety regulation authorities of different countries are various to a certain extent. For instance, FDA and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommend five-step procedures including wet, lather, scrub (for at least 20 s), rinse and dry32,33. Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) is more specific about the scrubbing step identifying the fingers, palms, wrists, back of hands and under nails but requires a shorter period of 15 seconds34. In Singapore, Singapore Food Agency (SFA) recommends food handlers to follow an eight-step procedure to clean hands (wash palms, in between fingers, on the back of hands, at the base of both thumbs, on the back of fingers, on fingernails, on both wrists, rinse and wipe dry with paper towels), hand rubbing with soap and water should last for at least 30 seconds35. In practice, posted handwashing pictograms and conventional training do not always override personal habits or workplace time pressure12. The AI system used in this study provided functions that static posters or simple written instructions cannot provide, including real-time step guidance, gesture recognition, hand-switch reminders, duration control, and corrective feedback during washing. These functions are operationally important because poor handwashing is often caused not by complete lack of knowledge, but by incomplete execution, skipped steps, insufficient duration, and inconsistent attention during routine work9,15.

Nevertheless, because the comparison was between conventional ad-hoc washing and a standardized AI-facilitated procedure, this study cannot fully separate the effect of AI guidance from those of procedural standardization, timed washing, camera-based observation, or increased participant awareness, and the observed benefit may therefore reflect their combined effect. This does not negate the value of the system but defines the appropriate interpretation of its added role. The value of AI in this context lies not only in whether it produces a large immediate log reduction in every microbial indicator, but also in its potential to support reproducible hand hygiene behavior in real food-service workflows. Future studies should directly compare AI-facilitated monitoring with timer-only, poster-only, and observation-only controls to determine which components contribute most strongly to behavioral and microbiological outcomes. The food-handler feedback further suggests that AI-assisted monitoring is acceptable as a long-term compliance-support tool if implemented in a transparent and non-punitive manner. Participants generally perceived the system positively, particularly as a reminder or support for completing the washing procedure. The relatively lower score for teaching value should not necessarily be interpreted as a weakness of the system; rather, it may indicate that many food handlers already possessed basic handwashing knowledge and benefited more from real-time procedural reinforcement than from additional instruction. In regulated food-service settings, where handlers are typically already trained in correct procedures, the principal value of the system lies not in teaching technique but in safeguarding the consistency, duration, and completeness of each step. This is particularly relevant given that self-assessed compliance tends to overestimate actual performance, as reflected by the gap between the high proportion of participants who were confident in their adherence and the rushing observed during habitual washing. Viewed in this light, the system functions as a supportive reinforcement tool rather than a punitive monitoring measure.

It is worth noting that this study was conducted under real-world conditions. Many previous handwashing efficacy studies have relied on artificially inoculated target pathogens or surrogates followed by selective detection36–39. Such approaches are valuable for controlled efficacy testing, but they may not fully reproduce the ecological complexity of human hands in active food-service environments. Human hands harbor abundant and highly variable microbiota shaped by temporal, anatomical, intrinsic, and extrinsic factors, and introduced pure cultures may not behave in the same way as naturally acquired microorganisms40. By evaluating food handlers at their own work premises, this study captured a more realistic combination of resident skin microbiota and transient microorganisms acquired during routine work. This ecological complexity also reinforces that AI-facilitated handwashing should not be viewed as a standalone replacement for other hygiene barriers, such as environmental sanitation, glove use where appropriate, exclusion of ill workers, food-contact surface control, and broader food safety training. The persistence of residual microbiota further supports the need for complementary barriers. Similarly, another field study found 10.4% non-compliance for coagulase-positive staphylococci, consistent with the notion that staphylococci can persist on hands following routine washing41. The same study also reported no significant difference between preparation and serving-only staff, suggesting that hand hygiene gaps may be facility-wide rather than role-specific and underscoring the importance of standardized training and system-level control41. Collectively, for high-risk scenarios such as ready-to-eat food preparation for vulnerable populations, a multi-barrier approach combining AI-monitored washing with subsequent sanitization or gloving remains essential.

The present study has limitations, including the modest magnitude of the VRBGA difference between ad-hoc and AI-facilitated washing, the selected subset used for microbiota analysis, and the lack of long-term deployment. These limitations do not negate the observed field benefit, but define the appropriate scope of interpretation and the next steps required for wider implementation. Future randomized crossover studies with larger cohorts, longer monitoring periods, objective behavioral compliance metrics, and full-cohort microbiome profiling will be important to determine how AI-facilitated handwashing can be most effectively integrated into routine food-service hygiene management. In addition, the sensitivity and detection accuracy of the AI system itself are expected to improve with continued use, as accumulating handwashing data and periodic model retraining allow the gesture-recognition algorithms to be progressively refined15.

Conclusion

Overall, this study demonstrates the field feasibility and practical value of AI-facilitated handwashing monitoring for food handlers under real food-service conditions. Although the microbiological improvement was indicator-specific rather than universal, AI-facilitated washing showed its clearest advantage in reducing VRBGA-detected microorganisms, supporting its role in controlling transient hygiene-related contamination. The ex vivo and 16S findings further indicate that handwashing outcomes are strongly shaped by the different removability and recovery of transient and resident hand microbiota, highlighting the importance of selecting appropriate microbial indicators when evaluating hand hygiene interventions. Beyond microbial reduction, the AI system provides real-time guidance, feedback, and procedural reinforcement, transforming handwashing into a more standardized and monitorable hygiene practice. Together with the positive food-handler feedback, these findings suggest that AI-facilitated monitoring can serve as an acceptable, supportive, and non-punitive tool for long-term compliance support. Therefore, AI-facilitated handwashing represents a practical strategy to strengthen hand hygiene management, particularly in high-risk and ready-to-eat food-service settings.

Methods

Field handwashing study with food handlers

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National University of Singapore (NUS-IRB-2022-324). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment.

Forty-six food handlers working at the student canteens of NUS participated in handwashing experiments conducted between 26 August 2022 and 22 March 2023. Each participant was visited twice in two separate weeks during active working hours. During the first visit, participants washed their hands according to their own habit (defined as “ad-hoc washing”). During the second visit, participants washed their hands following an AI-powered computer-vision system designed to facilitate compliant handwashing (Suzhou Aikor Intelligent Technology Co. Ltd., Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). The system provided an on-screen interface guiding users through a defined seven-step scrubbing sequence (palm to palm, back of hands, between fingers, back of fingers, base of thumbs, fingernails, and wrists; defined as “AI-facilitated washing”). Participants first viewed an instructional video for familiarization. During the trial, the system used real-time gesture-recognition algorithms to monitor and validate hand movements, with each step lasting approximately 4–5 s. During each step, the AI continuously analyzed the video stream to assess gesture accuracy and provided immediate corrective feedback, requiring users to adjust their technique when rubbing did not match the standard; it also tracked scrubbing duration for each hand and prompted users to switch hands to ensure adequate coverage. A plain soap without antimicrobial components was provided during both visits.

Before and after each handwashing event (hands were first patted dry with a single-use paper towel), each hand was swabbed for 30 s using an autoclaved cellulose sponge stick (Chinco, USA), covering the palms, dorsum, fingers (including fingertips), and interdigital web spaces. Sponge sticks were individually placed in sterile stomacher bags containing 3 mL 0.1% peptone water (Oxoid) and transported to the laboratory on ice within 2 h. All sampling was performed by the same microbiologist throughout the study. Among the 46 participants who completed the first visit, 44 subsequently completed the second visit. A short survey was conducted after the second visit to collect the feedback from the food handlers using the AI-facilitated handwashing monitoring system, with 43 out of 46 participants completing the survey.

Sample processing and bacterial enumeration

Each swab specimen was squeezed and pressed within the stomacher bag for 30 s before analysis. A 1 mL aliquot of each sample was serially diluted ten-fold in buffered peptone water (BPW; Oxoid). For total viable counts, 100 µL of each dilution was spread-plated on PCA (Oxoid) and incubated at 37 °C for 72 h, adapted from ISO 4833-1 with the incubation temperature set to 37 °C to better reflect conditions on the human hand. For presumptive coagulase-positive staphylococci, 100 µL of each dilution was spread-plated on BPA (Oxoid) and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, in accordance with ISO 6888-1. For Enterobacteriaceae, BPW suspensions were enriched at 37 °C for 18 h, streaked onto VRBGA (Oxoid), and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, with counts expressed as most probable number (MPN) following ISO 21528-1.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

S. aureus SA01 was isolated from human hands in our laboratory. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) ATCC 33591, E. coli ATCC 25922, and S. Typhimurium ATCC 14028 were obtained from the ATCC. Strains were subcultured from frozen stocks onto Tryptone Soya Agar (TSA; Oxoid) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. For inoculum preparation, a single colony was transferred into Tryptone Soya Broth (TSB; Oxoid) and incubated with agitation at 37 °C overnight. Overnight cultures were washed and diluted in sterile 0.1% peptone water before use.

Ex vivo washing experiment

Porcine ear skin was selected as an ex vivo model owing to its histological and lipid-composition similarity to human skin42–44. Fresh, untreated porcine ears were obtained from a local wet market, washed with tap water, and cut into 2 × 2 cm sections. Sections were sterilized by immersion in 70% ethanol for 30 min, rinsed with sterile deionized water in a biosafety cabinet, placed in sterile Petri dishes, and stored at −80 °C, following procedures adapted from previous ex vivo skin studies45–47. Prior to experimentation, skin samples were thawed overnight at 4 °C and held under UV light in a biosafety cabinet for 30 min to ensure sterility and evaporate residual moisture. Porcine ear skin used in the ex vivo experiments was obtained as food material from a commercial wet market; no live-animal procedures were performed.

A 100 µL volume of bacterial suspension was spot-inoculated onto each skin section to achieve a final density of approximately 4–5 log CFU/sample. Inoculated skin was placed in a desiccator containing saturated KCl solution to maintain humidity and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to allow the inoculum to dry. To simulate handwashing, skin samples were rubbed with UV-sterilized gloved hands inside a biosafety cabinet for 30 s. For soap-treated groups, 500 µL of commercial soap was applied during rubbing; formulations comprised one plain soap (without antimicrobial compounds) and three antimicrobial soaps (AM1 containing chloroxylenol, AM2 containing triclocarban, and AM3 containing o-cymen-5-ol). A gentle rinse with 30 mL sterile deionized water was then performed to remove residue. Each skin sample was subsequently swabbed for 30 s using an autoclaved cellulose sponge stick (Chinco, USA) in two perpendicular directions (0° and 90°) and placed in a sterile stomacher bag containing 3 mL 0.1% peptone water (Oxoid) for downstream analysis.

16S rRNA gene sequencing and bioinformatics

Three pairs of handwashing samples with the highest increases and three pairs with the highest decreases in PCA counts were further analyzed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted using the GeneJET Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3-V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using fusion primers, and the libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ platform (PE300) at BGI Tech Solutions (HK) Ltd., generating approximately 100,000 clean tags per sample. The raw read files have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under Accession No. PRJNA1040014.

Sequencing data were processed using the standard bioinformatics pipeline of BGI Tech Solutions. After quality filtering and removal of adaptor and chimeric sequences, high-quality clean tags were clustered into OTUs at a 97% similarity threshold48, and representative sequences were taxonomically annotated against the Greengenes reference database49. Differential abundance among groups was assessed using LEfSe via the Galaxy implementation50. Downstream analyses and figure generation were conducted in Python and GraphPad Prism (v10.6.0).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v29.0.1.0) and GraphPad Prism (v10.6.0). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Paired normally distributed data were analyzed using a paired t-test, while non-normally distributed paired data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Comparisons among more than two groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
