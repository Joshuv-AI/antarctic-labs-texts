Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a chronic infectious granulomatous disease caused primarily by the airborne bacillus Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis, M. Tb) and, less commonly, by other members of the M. tuberculosis complex, such as M. bovis and M. africanum[1]. TB continues to be one of the most significant infectious diseases worldwide, particularly in developing countries, where it ranks as the second leading cause of death from infectious etiologies after human immunodeficiency virus[2]. Diagnosis of TB typically relies on the detection of M. tuberculosis in clinically suspected patients. Conventional methods, such as identifying acid-fast bacilli (AFB) in specimens like cerebrospinal fluid, have several limitations – chiefly the low bacterial load often present in confirmed samples, which reduces diagnostic sensitivity[3]. The clinical manifestations of TB include fever, weight loss, and night sweats, along with additional symptoms determined by the disease’s location[1]. Pulmonary TB, the most common form, develops when bacilli reach the pulmonary alveoli. Extrapulmonary TB occurs when the infection spreads beyond the lungs via hematogenous or lymphatic routes, particularly in individuals with compromised immune systems. This dissemination can involve various anatomical sites, including the lymph nodes, skin, meninges, and bones[4]. Although TB can affect the maxillofacial region, such presentations are extremely rare. Mandibular TB accounts for less than 2% of skeletal TB cases, and diagnosis is often challenging due to its nonspecific clinical features[5]. In the pediatric population, however, tuberculous lymphadenitis – specifically involving the submandibular lymph nodes – is a more frequent presentation that can mimic these skeletal or dental pathologies. Tuberculous lymphadenitis – the enlargement of lymph nodes due to TB infection – is a relatively common extrapulmonary manifestation, especially in children encountering new antigens[6]. This report describes a rare case of mandibular TB in an 11-year-old male child who presented with swelling on the right side of the mandible. The initial clinical impression suggested a benign condition; however, further investigations confirmed TB infection. This case underscores the importance of considering tuberculosis in the differential diagnosis of atypical maxillofacial swellings to ensure timely and appropriate management.

Case presentation

An 11-year-old child presented to a private maxillofacial surgery clinic with a painful swelling on the right side of the mandible that had persisted for 8 months. The child had previously been evaluated by several otolaryngologists and dentists and had received multiple courses of antibiotics, none of which resulted in clinical improvement. Clinical examination revealed a hard, tender, reddish nodule with ill-defined borders and firm adherence to the underlying tissues (Fig. 1a). Initial radiographic evaluation using a panoramic radiograph showed no definitive septic source of the lesion. The image also demonstrated mixed dentition, with several teeth undergoing normal replacement (Fig. 2b). Ultrasonography identified two nodules beneath the right mandible, measuring 8 × 4 mm and 6 × 8 mm, both of which had increased in size compared with previous imaging studies (Fig. 2a). Additional findings included several small hypoechoic nodules medial to the right sphenoid bone, the largest measuring less than 1 cm and considered suspicious. Under local anesthesia, a diagnostic incision and drainage was performed at the most fluctuant point of the right submandibular swelling. Upon dissection, the lesion yielded a thick, purulent discharge followed by characteristic caseous, cheesy, necrotic material. A thorough curettage of the abscess cavity was performed to remove necrotic tissue for sampling. The site was irrigated with saline and a primary dressing was applied. No extensive surgical resection or lymph node dissection was required once the histopathological diagnosis was confirmed, as the patient responded well to medical therapy.

The collected specimens were submitted for microbiological and histopathological evaluation. Microbiological cultures were negative for identifiable pathogens. However, histopathological analysis demonstrated extensive granulomatous inflammation with caseating necrosis and the presence of Langhans giant cells – findings consistent with tuberculosis. The patient was referred to the Tuberculosis Control Center, where he was initiated on a standard Category 1 (CAT1) antitubercular regimen for drug-susceptible tuberculosis. The treatment followed a 2-month intensive phase consisting of a daily fixed-dose combination of Isoniazid (10 mg/kg), Rifampicin (15 mg/kg), Pyrazinamide (35 mg/kg), and Ethambutol (20 mg/kg). This was followed by a 4-month continuation phase of daily Isoniazid and Rifampicin. To prevent peripheral neuropathy, daily Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) supplementation was also provided. The patient showed significant clinical improvement within the first 2 months, with complete resolution of the mandibular swelling and absence of pain by the end of the 6-month course (Fig. 1b).

Discussion

Tuberculosis remains one of the leading infectious diseases worldwide and continues to pose a significant global health burden, particularly in developing countries[7]. Although TB can involve various anatomical sites, involvement of the jawbones is rare. When present, the maxilla is more frequently affected than the mandible[4]. Our case represents a specific manifestation of this rarity, where the infection presented as isolated submandibular tuberculous lymphadenitis rather than direct osseous involvement. The clinical presentation of maxillofacial TB is variable, ranging from superficial lesions to more aggressive forms that may cause extensive destruction of soft and hard tissues[4].

Pulmonary TB is generally easier to diagnose due to more apparent clinical and radiographic findings, along with the possibility of confirming infection through detection of AFB in sputum samples. In contrast, extrapulmonary TB presents a diagnostic challenge because its clinical and radiographic features are often nonspecific[4]. Many cases reported in the literature required extensive diagnostic workup due to pronounced or atypical symptoms[1,4,5,8,9]. In the present case, the relatively small size and nonspecific nature of the swelling contributed to an initial underestimation of its clinical significance.

Tuberculous lymphadenitis accounts for a considerable proportion of extrapulmonary TB cases in children[8]. It commonly presents with lymph node enlargement, pain, and overlying skin changes. Based on duration, lymphadenitis may be classified as acute (≤2 weeks), subacute (2–6 weeks), or chronic (>6 weeks)[6]. The prolonged duration of swelling in this patient (8 months) clearly indicated a chronic condition and warranted further investigation.

A review of the literature reveals a limited number of reported cases involving tuberculosis of the mandibular lymph nodes[1,4,5,8,9]. This underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating persistent or atypical mandibular swellings in pediatric patients. Furthermore, this case emphasizes the critical role of histopathological examination in establishing an accurate diagnosis and avoiding unnecessary or prolonged empirical antibiotic therapy.

In the present case, the diagnosis was established primarily through histopathological findings, which demonstrated granulomatous inflammation with caseating necrosis and Langhans giant cells, consistent with tuberculosis. Although microbiological cultures were negative, this is not uncommon due to the paucibacillary nature of extrapulmonary TB. Advances in diagnostic techniques, such as polymerase chain reaction and nucleic acid amplification tests, offer more rapid and specific detection of M. tuberculosis[9]. Additionally, the detection of culture filtrate protein antigens may further support the diagnosis in certain cases[3].

Facial swelling and pain in children are frequently attributed to odontogenic infections, often leading to the prescription of broad-spectrum antibiotics[1]. However, persistent or atypical presentations should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses, including tuberculosis.

Regarding management, the infection in this case was classified as Category I, and the patient was treated according to standard antitubercular therapy protocols.

This case demonstrates that once a diagnosis of TB is confirmed via biopsy, extensive surgical debridement or lymphadenectomy is often unnecessary, as systemic antitubercular therapy provides excellent regional control. This non-invasive management strategy is particularly beneficial in the pediatric population to avoid the risks of general anesthesia and potential surgical complications such as nerve injury or scarring[10].

Conclusions

Oral manifestations may serve as early indicators of underlying systemic diseases. Therefore, a thorough clinical and dental examination is essential for the early detection of such conditions. Histopathological evaluation plays a crucial role in determining the nature of atypical lesions and in guiding appropriate management, thereby preventing unnecessary or inappropriate antibiotic use. Early diagnosis and timely initiation of appropriate therapy are key factors in achieving favorable patient outcomes.
