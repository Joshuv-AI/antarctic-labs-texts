Proteomic analysis of the eating quality of humped cattle beef from Rikaze cattle under various cooking methods
Authors: Hao Zheng, Xiaolong Zhao, Jing Li, Ping Wang, Simin Li, Liang Li, Zhendong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study used proteomic analysis to evaluate how four cooking methods impact the quality of beef from Rikaze humped cattle. Conventional boiling, high-pressure boiling, roasting and frying produced varying numbers of differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) in Longissimus dorsi and hind leg beef samples compared to the raw meat. Principal component analysis, hierarchical clustering and correlation analysis revealed 11 and 18 proteins significantly associated with meat tenderness and colour, respectively. Myosin heavy chain 7 (MYH7), myosin light chain 2 (MYL2) and myosin light chain 6B (MYL6B), involved in the sarcomere (GO:0030017) and cardiac muscle contraction (map04260) pathways were negatively correlated with tenderness, indicating that their decreased abundance contributes to improved tenderness after cooking. The cytoskeletal protein α-crystallin B chain (CRYAB) showed a positive correlation with shear force, suggesting a role in toughness. PGAM2, ALDOA and PKM, participating in ADP metabolism (GO:0046031) and glycolysis (map00010), influenced meat texture, while glycolytic enzymes ALDOA, CKM and PKM promoted changes in lightness (L*). MYBPC2 and VDAC2 were negatively correlated with L*. EEF1G, PGP, PPIA and PRDX2 were negatively correlated with redness (a*), and DES was positively correlated with yellowness (b*). Overall, wet-heat cooking altered mainly skeletal, heat-shock and cytoskeletal proteins and enhanced tenderness, while proteins related to energy metabolism and oxidative stress were closely linked to colour development. These key proteins could serve as potential biomarkers for predicting the eating quality of humped cattle beef and provide a basis for the development of high-value beef products.Keywords: Cooking methods, Proteomics, Beef eating quality, Differentially abundant proteins, Rikaze humped cattle, Meat texture

Introduction

Rikaze humped cattle are a unique ruminant livestock breed endemic to the Tibet region of China. They are a cross between Indian cattle and local Tibetan cattle that were selectively bred over a long period, leading to the establishment of a unique taxon (Yang et al., 2002). Beef from humped cattle is notable for being rich in vitamins and minerals, especially iron, and having a high nutritional value. In addition to the nutritional content of the meat, its eating quality characteristics are also important in the consumer's eating experience (O'Quinn et al., 2018). These characteristics include sensory indicators such as tenderness, chewiness and colour. Achieving the best eating quality requires appropriate thermal processing (cooking). In the Tibet region of China, meat processing methods include boiling, pressure cooking, roasting and frying. Pressure cooking (a form of thermal processing) enhances flavour compounds while altering the sensory properties of the meat. Similarly, boiling, high-pressure boiling, frying and roasting are the main cooking processes in Chinese cuisine, imparting flavour, colour and texture to meat products.

Building on these quality considerations and regional cooking practices, the study of protein composition and functional properties under various heat treatments is a significant area in meat science research (Das & Rajkumar, 2013). The effects of traditional Chinese home cooking methods on the nutrition and quality of meat have been investigated (Sobral et al., 2018). The organoleptic characteristics of meat (colour, texture and water retention) are impacted by the type of heat treatment and its duration and temperature (Mora et al., 2011; Pathare & Roskilly, 2016) because of changes in the protein component of the meat. For example, the red colour of cooked meat depends primarily on the concentration of myoglobin, its redox state and the degree of thermal denaturation (Becker et al., 2016). The tenderness of cooked meat is determined primarily by muscle contraction due to oxidative cross-linking of proteins, and changes in the distribution and migration of water (Wang et al., 2023). Protein is the main component of meat and undergoes structural transformations during heat processing, including oxidation, degradation, denaturation and aggregation (Tian et al., 2016). These processes lead to changes in the arrangement of proteins (Di Luccia et al., 2015) and impact the structure and quality of the cooked meat (Yu et al., 2016).

Quantitative proteomics is a major tool in the study and characterization of proteins and related biological processes that affect meat quality (D'Alessandro & Zolla, 2012). Unlike genomics and transcriptomics, proteomics can directly measure functional molecules and comprehensively analyse protein modifications. It is also more sensitive, enabling the detection of low-abundance protein changes in complex samples. Cellular mechanisms underlying meat quality traits can be elucidated using quantitative proteomics (Agregán et al., 2022). Wu et al. (2025) found that both soaking- and un-soaking-high-temperature pressure cooking significantly degraded the collagen structure of basa fish maw, reducing its texture, shear force and water holding capacity. Zhang et al. (2024) used quantitative proteomics to show that black tea enhances meat tenderness by influencing pathways related to protein oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid degradation, amino acid degradation and peroxisomes in beef stew. Zhang et al. (2023) boiled sea cucumber body wall samples and found that the most abundant proteins in the water-soluble fraction were mainly structural proteins, such as collagens, microfibril-associated proteins, glycoproteins and muscle proteins. This caused progressive disintegration of the network of collagen fibres and FMs and gelatinization, which softened the muscle texture and decreased hardness and shear force values. Jia Wei et al. (2021) reported that steaming is the most effective thermal process for improving the tenderness of goat meat. Using quantitative proteomics, Tianyi Yang et al. (2023) showed collagen, troponin, myoglobin and haemoglobin have significant roles in colour changes during processing of post-mortem chicken muscle exudate. These proteins have potential as indicators of colour stability and changes during thermal processing. However, there have been few quantitative proteomics studies investigating the effects of cooking processes on meat quality, and further such studies are needed in this area (Song et al., 2021).

This study hypothesizes that (i) Different cooking methods affect eating quality traits (tenderness, colour, etc.) of Rikaze humped beef by regulating the abundance of differential proteins related to sarcomere structure, energy metabolism and oxidative stress, and (ii) These differential proteins can serve as biomarkers for predicting meat quality. We evaluate the effects of various heat treatments (conventional boiling, high-pressure boiling, roasting and frying) on the Longissimus dorsi (L. dorsi) muscle and hind leg meat of humped cattle. Quantitative proteomics provides information on patterns of protein changes in the beef to gain insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying changes in meat quality by linking the biological functions of differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) to heat-induced changes in texture and structure. The objectives are to identify protein biomarkers (e.g., MYH7, MYL6B, ALDOA) that are strongly associated with eating quality traits such as tenderness and colour, and to clarify the molecular mechanisms by which they influence meat quality through pathways related to sarcomere structure, energy metabolism and oxidative stress. The results provide a foundation for optimizing thermal processing conditions to improve the quality of humped cattle beef and to support the development of high-value beef products.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The cattle were sourced in May 2023 from the San Alin village in Ketsoxiong Township, Sanzuz District, Shigatse City, Tibet Autonomous Region of the People's Republic of China. The study was conducted in accordance with experimental procedures approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Department of Science and Technology of the Tibet Autonomous Region, China. The Ethics Committee of the Tibet College of Agriculture and Animal Husbandry approved the sample collection and experimental protocols (No. NYLL-03).

Six Rikaze humped cattle (three bulls and three females) were slaughtered at a local commercial abattoir. Their mean age was approximately 18 months (540 ± 20 days) and mean carcass weight was 215 ± 23 kg. After exsanguination, carcasses were immediately transferred to a chilling room (0–4 °C, forced-air). L. dorsi (n = 6, mean weight 4034 g) and hind leg muscle samples (n = 6, mean weight 4263 g) were excised 1 h postmortem in the chiller as a standardized early postmortem time point and to minimize aging effects. Samples were maintained at 0–4 °C during handling and transport (temporarily placing in a − 20 °C portable cooler to prevent a rise in temperature) and analysed within 12 h to minimize early biochemical changes. Visible fat and connective tissue were removed prior to application of the following cooking processes: L. dorsi: Raw (RA), Conventional Boiled (BA), High-Pressure Boiled (HA), Roasted (TA) and Fried (FA); Hind leg meat: Raw (RB), Conventional Boiled (BB), High-Pressure Boiled (HB), Roasted (TB) and Fried (FB).

The following reagents were used: urea (NH2)2CO (Sigma-Aldrich), protease inhibitor (Merck Millipore), BCA kit (Beyotime), trypsin (Promega), trichloroacetic acid (TCA; Sigma-Aldrich), tetraethylammonium bromide (TEAB; Sigma-Aldrich), acetone (Hangzhou Hanko Chemical Co.), DL-dithiothreitol (Sigma-Aldrich), iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich), formic acid (Fluka), acetonitrile (ThermoFisher Scientific), acetic acid (Hangzhou Gaojing Fine Chemical Co.), coomassie brilliant blue (Beyotime), 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution (Servicebio), ethanol (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.), trichloromethane (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.) and tert-butyl alcohol (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co.).

Instruments and equipment

The following hardware was used: Agilent 1100 liquid chromatograph equipped with a VWD detector, constant temperature magnetic stirrer (08-2 T; Shanghai Meiyingpu Instrumentation Manufacturing Co.), solid phase microextraction device (57330-U; Supelco); 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS solid phase microextraction needle (57348-U; Supelco), gas chromatography mass spectrometer (Agilent 6890 N-5973 GC–MS), gas chromatography column (HP-INNOWax; 60 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm), meat tenderness meter (Konica Minolta CR-10 Plus), NULL texture analyser (TA touch), colourimeter (Konica Minolta CR-400), carbon dioxide supercritical vacuum dryer (Guangzhou Jingying Technology Instrument Co.) and scanning electron microscope (Gemini SEM 300; Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Experimental methods

Cooking methods

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the L. dorsi and hind leg meat were trimmed into strips (1 × 1 × 5 cm) and subjected to four different heat treatments as follows:

Changes in colour and morphology of Longissimus dorsi and hind leg beef of humped cattle under various thermal processing methods.

(1) Conventional boiling: Water was preheated to 80 °C, meat samples were added, and the pot was brought to a gentle boil. On reaching 100 °C, cooking was timed for 60 min, maintaining at 100 °C. Meat was removed and immediately cooled in a 4 °C water bath before surface water was removed using blotting paper. Endpoint core temperature was 100 °C.

(2) High-pressure boiling: Water was heated to 80 °C in a household pressure cooker before meat samples were added and boiled at 120 °C for 25 min. Meat was removed and immediately cooled in a 4 °C water bath before surface water was removed using blotting paper. Endpoint core temperature was 120 °C.

(3) Roasting: An oven was preheated for 30 min (maximum 120 °C, minimum 100 °C) before meat samples were placed inside on a baking tray and roasted for 30 min, turning after 15 min. Meat was removed and immediately cooled in a 4 °C water bath before oil-absorbent paper was used to absorb excess juices. Endpoint core temperature was 100 °C.

(4) Frying: Cooking oil was preheated in a pan before meat samples were fried, blanched in hot water, drained, transferred to a preheated induction cooker and finally fried for 5 min at 180 °C. Meat was removed and immediately cooled in a 4 °C water bath before oil-absorbent paper was used to absorb excess juices. Blanching was used to remove surface blood, reduce surface moisture and standardize surface temperature before frying. Endpoint core temperature was 100 °C.

Determination of texture

A NULL texture analyser with TA/0.5S probe was used to carry out texture profile analysis (TPA) of the meat samples. Five of the 1 × 1 × 5 cm meat strips were aligned by direction of the muscle fibres and measurements were taken (pre-test, test and post-test speeds all set at 1 mm/s) using a force-trigger point (force type) and a trigger point value of 5 gf. Consistent conditions were applied to all the samples, facilitating the evaluation of hardness (N),springiness (mm), adhesiveness (N/m), chewiness (mJ) and resilience (N).

Warner-Bratzler shear force

Meat was sampled parallel to the direction of the muscle fibres and cut into 1 × 1 × 4 cm cubes, and the Warner-Bratzler shear force was measured using a tenderness meter with a silicon photosensitive light receiving element, a pulsed xenon lamp light source and a measurement aperture of φ8 nm.

Meat colour analysis

The colour of the meat was measured using a colourimeter. Colour was measured at three positions on the cross-section and the mean value was calculated. The parameters L*, a*, and b* represented brightness, redness, and yellowness, respectively.

Scanning electron microscopy

Beef samples were cut into small cubes measuring 3 × 3 × 3 mm, following the direction of the vertical muscle fibres. These cubes were fixed in 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde solution for seven days, then rinsed with distilled water and dehydrated by immersion in a series of ethanol solutions (25%, 50%, 70%, 95%, 100%) (v/v), each step lasting 60 min. This was followed by degreasing in trichloromethane for three days before the solvent was replaced with a mixture of anhydrous ethanol and tert-butanol (1:1, v/v), then tert-butanol, once each for 60 min. After drying for one day in a carbon dioxide supercritical vacuum desiccator, gold sputtering was performed at a pressure of 10−2 bar and a current of 40 mA. Samples were then examined under a scanning electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV to obtain micrographs of the microstructure of the meat at x500 magnification.

Proteomics

Protein extraction

For each treatment, samples of equal weight from six animals were pooled into a composite sample and analysed in triplicate. All frozen beef samples were removed from −80 °C storage, 30 mg was weighed into a mortar pre-cooled with liquid nitrogen, and ground to a fine powder in the presence of liquid nitrogen. For each group of samples, four times the volume of lysis buffer (8 mol/L urea, 1% [m/v] protease inhibitor) was added to the powdered sample prior to ultrasonication. The lysed samples were then centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min at 4 °C and the supernatants were transferred to new centrifuge tubes. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA kit.

Trypsin digestion

An equal amount of each meat sample was enzymatically digested in a consistent volume of lysate buffer. TCA (20%) (m/v) was slowly added while vortexing to ensure thorough mixing and samples were allowed to precipitate at 4 °C for 2 h. Samples were centrifuged at 4500g for 5 min and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate pellet was washed 2–3 times with pre-cooled acetone, dried, then 300 μL of 200 mmol/L TEAB was added and sonicated to break up the pellet. Trypsin was added at a ratio of 1:50 (trypsin:protein, m/m) and allowed to digest overnight. Dithiothreitol was then added to a final concentration of 5 mmol/L and the volume of the solution was reduced for 30 min at 56 °C. Finally, iodoacetamide was added to a final concentration of 11 mmol/L and the solution was incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

Peptides were separated using a Vanquish Neo ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system after dissolution in mobile phase A (aqueous solution of 0.1% [v/v] formic acid and 2% [v/v] acetonitrile). Mobile phase B was 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in 90% (v/v) acetonitrile. The mobile phase gradient was as follows: 0–14.5 min, 6% to 22% B; 14.5–17.5 min, 22% to 34% B; 17.5–19 min, 34% to 80% B; and 19–20 min, held at 80% B. The flow rate was 700 nL/min. The separated peptide fragments were ionised in the ESI ion source and analysed in the Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (MS). The ion source voltage was 2300 V and the FAIMS compensation voltage (CV) was −45 V. Both peptide parent ions and their secondary fragments were monitored using the high-resolution Orbitrap MS. The first-order MS scanning range was 350–1400 m/z and scanning resolution was 60,000. Secondary MS scanning began at 120 m/z at a resolution of 15,000. Data acquisition used a data-independent scanning (DIA) procedure in which peptide ions from multiple continuous m/z windows were followed into the HCD collision cell after a primary scan and were fragmented using 27% fragmentation energy, followed by secondary mass spectrometry. To maximize utilization of the mass spectra, the automatic gain control was set to 1e−6 and the maximum injection time to 22 ms.FDR ≤ 1%, with FC 1.5 as the criterion for determining significant fold change, P < 0.05 .

Data analysis and bioinformatics

Unique peptides were used for protein quantification. DIA data were analysed in the DIA-NN (v.1.8) search engine using the default software parameters. The database used was Bos_taurus_9913_PR_20230529.fasta, which contains 37,506 sequences. Trypsin/P was selected for the digestion mode, with the maximum number of missed cuts being 1. DAPs were identified by fold changes greater than 1.5 or less than 1/1.5 between the various cooking treatments, specifically RA versus BA, HA, TA and FA, and RB versus BB, HB, TB and FB when P < 0.05. Proteomic analyses were conducted by Jingjie Bio Co. Ltd. (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) using eggnog-mapper (v.2.1.6) and the eggNOG database (v.5/0.0.2, http://eggnog5.embl.de/#/app/home) for gene ontology (GO) annotation, and diamond (v.2.0.11.149) annotation software, the KAAS database (accessed 3 April 2015, http://www.genome.jp/kegg/kaas/), KEGGmapper (v.5.0, http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/mapper.html) and KOBAS (v.3.0, http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) for KEGG pathway analysis. The criteria FDR ≤ 1% and FC ≥ 1.5 were applied to determine significant fold changes at P < 0.05 (Griss et al., 2020; Jassal et al., 2020; Kolmykov et al., 2020).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation based on three independent samples. A mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS v.26 to evaluate differences between the four heat treatments of L. dorsi and hind leg meat, with P < 0.05 being considered statistically significant. Muscle type was treated as a random factor to account for natural variability among different samples, allowing for broader applicability of the results. By contrast, heat treatments were considered fixed factors as the study specifically compared the effects of the four predetermined cooking methods on meat quality. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed using GraphPad Prism (v.8.0.1), SIMCA (v.14.1) and Origin 2018c (v.9.5.1).

Shear force values for BA, HA, BB and HB were significantly lower than for TA, FA, TB and FB. This can be attributed to conventional boiling and high-pressure boiling relying on heat transfer via convection and conduction through liquids. The presence of wet heat causes expansion of muscle tissues, leading to structural disintegration, increased myofibrillar dissolution and fragmentation, which contribute to lower textural properties. By contrast, roasting employs a radiative heat source, exposing meat to heat over a long period via convection of dry, hot air that alters the microstructure of muscle fibres, exacerbates disorganisation of muscle tissue and induces contraction of the fibres (Yang et al., 2023). Concurrently, formation of a surface crust layer further elevates chewiness and hardness while reducing tenderness, consistent with previous mechanistic insights into heat-induced textural deterioration. Meat colour analysis

Colour is an important characteristic of meat and a crucial indicator when a consumer is assessing the quality of a meat product, while also influencing sensory perceptions. Changes in muscle proteins, such as alterations in myofibrillar structure, oxidation and denaturation of sarcoplasmic proteins, play a vital role in determining meat colour (Liu et al., 2021). The parameters L*, a* and b* represent the lightness, redness and yellowness of the meat, respectively (Table 3). There was no significant difference in L* between RA and RB (raw meat). L* also did not differ between conventional and high-pressure boiling of a given muscle type, but both boiling methods differed from roasting and frying. There was no difference in a* between RA and RB (raw meat), and it did not differ across the heat treatments of each muscle type. Meat redness (a*) is attributed mainly to myoglobin and haemoglobin content and their redox states, while changes in L* stem primarily from protein denaturation and structural protein precipitation (Liu et al., 2022). There was no difference in b* between RA and RB (raw meat), and no difference between conventional and high-pressure boiling of a given muscle type, but both boiling methods differed from roasting and frying (Table 3).

This study demonstrates that the thermal processing methods (which operate at different temperatures, have different modes of heat transfer and exhibit varying heating medium coefficients) led to various changes in muscle colour. Frying resulted in more variable cooked samples than conventional boiling,high-pressure boiling and roasting. The differing effects are due to the various cooking conditions causing different degrees of denaturation and degradation of structural and pigmented proteins, which amplifies light scattering and reflection due to increased gelation of collagen, resulting in higher L* values. Heating also causes the complete coagulation of myosin molecules and oxidation of pigments (haemoglobin moieties), which results in opaque dark brown meat and lower a* values (Yang et al., 2023). Further analysis of the proteins involved is needed to elucidate further the mechanisms underlying these colour changes.

GO pathway analysis of DAPs

To investigate their potential biological functions related to meat quality traits, the DAPs were annotated using GO analysis to identify enriched biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF) and cellular components (CC). Differential proteins were expressed mainly in BP, such as cellular metabolic processes, organic matter metabolic processes, primary metabolic processes, and bioregulatory processes, showing that metabolic processes play an important role in meat quality traits (Yang et al., 2023).

GO pathway analysis revealed various enriched biological processes, as shown in Fig. 4, including GO:0006090 pyruvate metabolism, GO:0030049 muscle filament sliding and GO:0046031 ADP metabolism. DAPs in these processes were associated with metabolic proteins (e.g., ALDOA, MIF, PGAM2, PKM, GOT1, PADI2 and PGP) and skeletal muscle proteins (e.g., DES, MYBPC2, MYH7, MYL2 and MYL6B). This suggests that metabolic pathways and skeletal muscle proteins are important in determining meat quality traits.

Gene Ontology (GO) pathway analysis of enriched biological processes in beef under various thermal processing methods.

The molecular functions of DAPs were related mainly to GO:0003779 actin binding, GO:0003824 catalytic activity, GO:0004601 peroxidase activity, GO:0008307 structural constituents of muscle and GO:0017022 myosin binding, among others. DAPs in these processes were associated with molecular functions (e.g., EEF1A2, EEF1G, GOT1, MACROD1, MIF, MYH7, PGAM2, PGP, PPIA, PRDX6 and PRDX2) and skeletal muscle proteins (e.g., CRYAB, HSPB1, MYL2, BIN1, MYBPC2 and MYL6B). These proteins are essential for maintaining the structural and functional properties of muscle tissue during thermal processing and also impact its colour, so they may be responsible for the changes observed in cooked meat quality.

The cellular components of DAPs were associated mainly with GO:0016459 myosin complex, GO:0030016 myofibrils, GO:0030017 sarcomeres, GO:0043292 contractile fibre and GO:0097512 cardiac myofibrils, among others. These DAPs are vital in the maintenance of cell shape and performance of other critical functions. Thus, GO enrichment analysis provides valuable insight for potential strategies to address the eating quality of L. dorsi and hind leg meat of humped cattle under different heat treatments.

KEGG enriched pathways analysis of DAPs

Proteins do not function in isolation in living organisms but cooperate in a series of biochemical reactions that facilitate performance of their biological functions. To gain insight into the network of relevant protein–molecule interactions, the secondary functions of the primary KEGG classification were categorized. The top seven most highly enriched functions are illustrated in a bubble diagram (Fig. 5), the vertical axis being the KEGG pathway descriptions and the horizontal axis indicating the degree of enrichment of DAPs in each function after log2 transformation (larger fold enrichment signifying higher enrichment). The colour of the datapoint reflects the P value of the enrichment, with deeper blue representing stronger significance. The size of the datapoint indicates the number of DAPs in each KEGG pathway, with large dots indicating higher numbers. The enriched KEGG pathways include map 00010 glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (3 proteins), map 00620 pyruvate metabolism (2) and map 04260 cardiac muscle contraction (2), among others. This KEGG pathway analysis of DAPS related to beef eating quality shows that meat tenderness was positively correlated with slow muscle oxidation and negatively correlated with fast glycolytic properties (Zhao et al., 2013), while tenderness was related to energy metabolism (López-Pedrouso et al., 2021). Moreover, these pathways were enriched with some important proteins related to beef eating quality, namely PGAM2, PKM, ACYP2, ALDOA, CKM, GOT1, MIF, MYH7 and MYL2.

PCA and HCA

The unsupervised techniques PCA and HCA were used to elucidate similarities and patterns among the various heat-treated L. dorsi and hind leg meat samples (Gagaoua et al., 2019). As illustrated in Fig. 6B, PCA indicated that the first principal component (PC1) accounted for approximately 25.8% of the total variation, while PC1 and PC2 explained 43.4%. The PC1 vs PC2 score plots categorized the cooked and raw meat into ten clearly distinguishable groups: RA, RB, HA, HB, BA, BB, TA, TB, FA and FB.

Unsupervised analysis of protein expression in beef under various thermal processing methods. (A) Hierarchical cluster analysis, and (B) Principal component analysis.

As illustrated in the dendrogram in Fig. 6A, the shortest distances between groups occur between clusters 3 and 5, suggesting that roasting and frying produce similar protein expression patterns for a given muscle type. Notably, the longer distances observed between roasted and fried hind leg meats indicate that these treatments produced greater changes in protein expression in hind leg than in L. dorsi meat. Cluster 7 also shows there is a significant difference between hind leg and L. dorsi cooked by conventional boiling. In addition, the proteins expressed in meat subjected to high-pressure boiling are distinct from those treated by other cooking methods—high-pressure boiling samples (cluster 4) are clearly separated from those subjected to conventional boiling, roasting and frying. Notably, both cuts of meat fall within the same cluster, suggesting that, in terms of protein expression patterns, high-pressure boiling generates the least variation within hind leg and L. dorsi meat of humped cattle.

The volcano plots in Fig. 7 compare the DAPs in raw and thermally processed hind leg and L. dorsi muscle samples and reveal up-regulated (red dots), down-regulated (blue dots) and unchanged (grey dots) proteins. In line with the morphological observations and texture analyses, high-pressure boiling had the most pronounced impact on muscle texture, with L. dorsi showing a greater number of DAPs than hind leg meat. Specifically, compared to RA, 19 proteins were up-regulated and 447 were down-regulated in BA; 137 were up-regulated and 528 were down-regulated in HA; 34 were up-regulated and 337 were down-regulated in TA; and 46 were up-regulated and 376 were down-regulated in FA. Similarly, compared to RB, 20 proteins were up-regulated and 427 were down-regulated in BB; 321 were up-regulated and 528 were down-regulated in HB; 61 were up-regulated and 498 were down-regulated in TB; and 73 were up-regulated and 541 were down-regulated in FB. Previous studies show that the high temperatures associated with boiling and steaming methods facilitate enzymatic hydrolysis and protein alterations, making muscle tissue more susceptible to disintegration (Desai et al., 2016). Consequently, cooking at high temperatures results in lower meat shear force values, which is consistent with observed PCA and HCA results.

Proteomic analyses related to beef quality traits

Applying protein biomarkers to predict and ultimately control meat tenderness is increasingly desirable. Screening of the DAPs in both muscle types by various methods revealed the relationship between meat quality traits and protein abundance (Table 5, Table 6, Table 7, Table 8, Fig. 8). The tables list DAPs with correlation coefficients exceeding ±0.6, with positive values indicating positive correlations with meat quality indices, and negative values indicating negative correlations. Eleven and 18 proteins were significantly associated with tenderness and colour, respectively. Notably, proteasome subunit beta type-2 (PSMB2) (r = 0.779) was involved in protein hydrolysis, starch-binding domain-containing protein 1 (STBD1) (r = 0.677) in the metabolic pathway, and protein-arginine deaminase-2 (PSAD) (r = 0.677) and phosphotriesterase-associated protein (PTER) (r = 0.812) in the hydrolase pathway. Protein-arginine deaminase type 2 (PADI2) (r = 0.677), myosin heavy chain 7 (MYH7) (r = −0.719), myosin light chain 2 (MYL2) (r = −0.639), myosin light chain 6B (MYL6B) (r = −0.860) and the skeletal muscle protein peroxisomal dismutase-6 (PRDX6) (r = 0.667) have roles in regulating the turnover of phospholipids, reduce oxidative stress and help in determining the tenderness of beef (Carlson et al., 2017). Phosphorylation is also closely linked to tenderness in meat. Phosphoglycerate mutase (PGAM2) (r = 0.833). In addition, ‘heat shock proteins’ such as heat shock protein β-1 (HSPB1) (r = −0.662) and the cytoskeleton-associated α-crystallin b chain (CRYAB) (r = 0.783) are also relevant to meat quality.

The concentration of myoglobin and its oxidation state are crucial in determining chromaticity parameters in beef (Gagaoua, Terlouw et al., 2021). Various oxidative enzymes influence myoglobin levels, while other proteins, such as haemoglobin and cytochrome C, also contribute to the colour of meat (Nam et al., 2018). Light scattering in meat is attributed primarily to variations in myocyte lattice spacing, myofilament segment length, and myoplasmic protein distribution. L* values are determined by changes in the surface charge of myofibrillar proteins, whereas myofilament lattice spacing is influenced by pH, protein solubility and the integrity of cytoskeletal and myofilament proteins (Purslow et al., 2019). The present data reveal a complex relationship between proteins and meat colour. Table 6, Table 7, Table 8 show that a large number of proteins (18 in total) were correlated with meat colour parameters. Several proteins related to energy metabolism and oxidative stress showed correlations with L*, namely ALDOA (r = 0.608), CKM (r = 1), MACROD1 (r = 0.756), ACYP2 (r = 0.813), PCMT1 (r = 0.685), PKM (r = 0.636) and A1BG (r = 0.611), in addition to other proteins: BIN1 (r = 0.651*), EEF1A2 (r = 0.890), GOT1 (r = 0.754), MYBPC2 (r = −0.768), MIF (r = 0.761) and VDAC2 (r = −0.633). Correlations were seen between a* and EEF1G (r = −0.837), PGP (r = − 0.609), PPIA (r = −0.750) and PRDX2 (r = −0.647), and between b* and DES (r = 0.651). Notably, these correlated proteins include the glycolytic enzymes ALDOA and PKM, along with CKM which regulates intracellular ATP concentrations by storing and utilising high-energy phosphate species as phosphocreatine. This suggests that glycolytic proteins play a significant role in influencing meat colour.

Discussion

Texture properties remained broadly consistent under the four thermal processing methods. RA and RB exhibited significantly lower hardness, Springiness, chewiness and Adhesiveness than the cooked groups. Conventional boiling and roasting tended to increase the hardness and chewiness of both types of meat more than high-pressure boiling and frying. The overall trend was consistent with the findings of Xu et al. (2023). L. dorsi was generally more tender than hind leg meat, and conventional boiling and high-pressure boiling produced more tender meat than roasting and frying. The trends in tenderness under roasting and frying were consistent with changes observed in goose meat by Wołoszyn et al. (2020), while the trends under boiling and roasting were consistent with changes in goat meat reported by Jia et al. (2021). L* and b* colour values under conventional and high-pressure boiling were higher than under roasting and frying.

Most previous studies have focused on changes in tenderness and colour under thermal processing, while the underlying mechanisms have been insufficiently explored. Further research is needed to better understand the relationship between cooking procedures and meat quality. Eleven and 18 proteins were significantly associated with tenderness and colour, respectively. Notably, PSMB2 (r = 0.779) was involved in protein hydrolysis, STBD1 (r = 0.677) in the metabolic pathway, and PSAD (r = 0.677) and PTER (r = 0.812) in the hydrolase pathway. Skeletal muscle proteins included MYH7 (r = −0.719), MYL2 (r = −0.639) and MYL6B (r = −0.860). These influenced meat quality by participating in the sarcomere (GO:0030017) pathway and the cardiac muscle contraction (map04260) pathway. Studies have shown that processing conditions determine the degree of thermal denaturation of different muscle proteins, and each protein affects the eating quality of processed meat products in a manner closely related to its structure (Yu et al., 2016). Relaxation of the myofibrillar structure may be the main cause of differences in tenderness of beef under different heat treatments. Thus, these three skeletal muscle proteins are candidate biomarkers of beef tenderness. Myosin interacts with actin through the actin–myosin complex and myosin light chains (MYL); if the binding between myosin or actomyosin and actin is weakened during heat treatment, meat texture will be affected (Zuo et al., 2025). Notably, these proteins were correlated with the glycolytic enzymes ALDOA and PKM, and with CKM, which regulates intracellular ATP concentrations by storing and utilising high-energy phosphate species as phosphocreatine. This suggests that glycolytic proteins play a significant role in influencing meat colour (Gagaoua et al., 2020). PRDX6 (r = 0.667) has a role in regulating the turnover of phospholipids, reducing oxidative stress and determining the tenderness of beef (Carlson et al., 2017). We found that it is involved in the catalytic activity (GO:0003824) pathway and, together with PRDX2, participates in the peroxidase activity (GO:0004601) pathway. Moreover, Zuo et al. (2025) demonstrated that oxidative stress and PRDX6 play key roles in regulating protein degradation pathways in beef, including cytoskeletal reorganization, extracellular matrix remodelling and amino acid metabolism, which are core biological processes affecting beef tenderness. The heat shock protein HSPB1 (r = −0.662) and the cytoskeletal protein CRYAB (r = 0.783) were identified. HSPB1 regulates the maintenance and dynamics of the cytoskeleton, and CRYAB is associated with the cytoskeletal network of microfilaments, microtubules and intermediate filaments (Wettstein et al., 2012). Phosphoglycerate mutase (PGAM2) (r = 0.833), another glycolytic enzyme, exhibits greater stability in its non-phosphorylated form than its phosphorylated form. Our study found that it is involved in the ADP metabolism (GO:0046031) pathway and the glycolysis (map00010) pathway. Pyruvate kinase is more active when phosphorylated, catalyzing the conversion of phosphoenolpyruvate and ADP to 2-pyruvate and ATP, which may affect meat quality (Jia et al., 2021). ALDOA can promote the cross-linking of adjacent actin filaments or the binding of troponin to thin filaments, thereby strengthening energy supply in regions with active contraction. This binding or cross-linking may alter myofibrillar spacing (Hughes et al., 2019). The increased abundance and activity of ALDOA involved in the map00010 pathway may indicate enhanced glycolytic activity during processing, which is often positively associated with tenderness (Reiche et al., 2019). This may partly explain the mechanism underlying the association between ALDOA and tenderness. PKM, which also participates in the glycolysis (map00010) pathway with PGAM2 and ALDOA, catalyses the dephosphorylation of phosphoenolpyruvate to form pyruvate, and its high abundance may reflect an increased potential for pyruvate production. This enzyme has also been identified as a candidate biomarker for beef colour (Gagaoua et al., 2020).

The concentration of myoglobin and its oxidation state are crucial in determining chromaticity parameters in beef (Gagaoua, Warner et al., 2021). Various oxidative enzymes influence myoglobin levels, while other proteins, such as haemoglobin and cytochrome C, also contribute to the colour of meat (Nam et al., 2018). Light scattering in meat is attributed primarily to variations in myocyte lattice spacing, myofilament segment length, and myoplasmic protein distribution. L* values are determined by changes in the surface charge of myofibrillar proteins, whereas myofilament lattice spacing is influenced by pH, protein solubility and the integrity of cytoskeletal and myofilament proteins (Purslow et al., 2019). The present data reveal a complex relationship between proteins and meat colour. Table 6, Table 7, Table 8 show that a large number of proteins (18 in total) were correlated with meat colour parameters. Several proteins related to energy metabolism and oxidative stress showed correlations with L*, namely ALDOA (r = 0.608), CKM (r = 1), MACROD1 (r = 0.756), ACYP2 (r = 0.813), PCMT1 (r = 0.685), PKM (r = 0.636) and A1BG (r = 0.611), in addition to other proteins: BIN1 (r = 0.651), EEF1A2 (r = 0.890), GOT1 (r = 0.754), MYBPC2 (r = −0.768), MIF (r = 0.761) and VDAC2 (r = −0.633). Correlations were seen between a* and EEF1G (r = −0.837), PGP (r = − 0.609), PPIA (r = −0.750) and PRDX2 (r = −0.647), and between b* and DES (r = 0.651). Notably, these correlated proteins include the glycolytic enzymes ALDOA and PKM, along with the intracellular ATP regulator CKM. This suggests that glycolytic proteins play a significant role in influencing meat colour.

These findings provide new insights into the effects of various heat treatments on protein parameters and tenderness in meat. The identified proteins are associated with colour and tenderness during thermal processing and may serve as potential indicators of tenderness and colour stability, or other variations in heated-treated meat products. The proteomics results elucidate the mechanisms by which heat treatments affect humped beef quality, thereby offering selection strategies for the industrial processing of this meat. Given the relatively small sample size, we acknowledge the limitations of this study. Future work should validate our findings in other breeds and, by integrating proteomic, metabolomic, lipidomic and sensory data, will further elucidate the mechanisms underlying flavour and texture changes.

Conclusion

This study used quantitative proteomics to investigate the mechanisms underlying eating quality differences in Rikaze humped cattle beef under different cooking methods. A total of 3138 proteins were identified. Compared with raw meat, 466 (BA) and 447 (BB) DAPs were found in L. dorsi and hind leg muscle, respectively, after conventional boiling, 665 (HA) and 903 (HB) after high-pressure boiling, 371 (TA) and 559 (TB) after roasting, and 422 (FA) and 614 (FB) after frying. PCA, HCA and correlation analyses identified 11 and 18 proteins that were significantly associated with tenderness and colour, respectively. Of these, MYH7, MYL2 and MYL6B affected meat quality by participating in the sarcomere (GO:0030017) and cardiac muscle contraction (map04260) pathways, and were negatively correlated with tenderness. The cytoskeletal protein CRYAB was positively correlated with shear force. PGAM2, ALDOA and PKM participated in the ADP metabolism (GO:0046031) and glycolysis (map00010) pathways and influenced meat texture. The glycolytic enzymes ALDOA, CKM and PKM promoted changes in L*, while MYBPC2 and VDAC2 were negatively correlated with L*. EEF1G, PGP, PPIA and PRDX2 were negatively correlated with a*, and DES was positively correlated with b*. These proteins are potential biomarkers and could provide valuable insights into meat eating quality. Future studies should validate these biomarkers in commercial processing lines and explore synergistic effects with post-processing technologies (e.g., sous-vide and enzymatic tenderization) to maximize economic returns while maintaining sensory quality.
