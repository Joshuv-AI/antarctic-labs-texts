Diversity of swimming behavior in larval Lepidoptera
Authors: David Chung-Te Cheng, Dezene P W Huber, Amanda D Roe, Akito Y Kawahara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLepidoptera are a highly diverse lineage of predominantly terrestrial insects, yet their larvae occupy diverse habitats where they may experience unexpected contact with water or accidental submersion. Such exposure may have favored adaptations that enable terrestrial lepidopteran larvae to survive underwater for short periods. Using a combination of personal and crowd-sourced observations, we document a striking diversity of behaviors displayed by Lepidoptera (32 species across 16 families) when suddenly exposed to water. This includes swimming, which we observed in 4 lepidopteran families. We developed a behavioral ontology comprising 9 distinct behaviors and 5 non-directional behaviors, each observed on the water surface. Our study highlights a largely overlooked dimension of Lepidoptera biology and establishes a framework for future research on the significance of this behavior.

Introduction

Lepidoptera, commonly known as moths and butterflies, are an ecologically and taxonomically diverse lineage of insects. With more than 160,000 described species, they occupy nearly every terrestrial habitat on Earth (Wright et al. 2026). However, less than 1% can be considered exclusively aquatic, with larvae that develop entirely within water (Mey and Speidel 2008, Yanoviak and Frederick 2014, Pabis 2018). Despite the sister order Trichoptera being primarily aquatic, for the overwhelming majority of Lepidoptera, water is thought to be a hazardous environment, and exposure is typically incidental or accidental rather than adaptive. Aquatic habitats pose numerous challenges for terrestrial insects, including the need for specialized mechanisms to enable respiration, locomotion, and buoyancy control, as well as strategies to prevent drowning or being carried away by currents (Gustafsson et al. 2014).

Terrestrial Lepidoptera may occasionally encounter water, especially if they inhabit areas due to living in proximity to lentic or lotic systems—for example, many larvae feed on plants growing along lake margins and riverbanks, or on tree branches overhanging water. They may also become submerged after falling into temporary puddles during or following storms. Frequent exposure to water may have led some Lepidoptera to possess behavioral or morphological adaptations that facilitate temporary survival in water. In a recent study, Hayashi and Sugiura (2021) examined the swimming behavior of 13 Japanese species of terrestrial lepidopteran caterpillars across 4 families and reported swimming and non-swimming behavior. They described 2 distinct forms of swimming movements—including an undulatory motion or an abdominal flick from 6 species. Their study provided the first formal description of coordinated swimming behavior in terrestrial Lepidoptera and demonstrated that such responses are present across phylogenetically diverse taxa.

Building on the work of Hayashi and Sugiura (2021), we compiled personal observations, solicited records from colleagues and community (citizen) scientists, and reviewed all available literature on this topic and related insect groups focusing on North America, a much broader geographic range. Our study expands the known diversity of water-related behaviors in Lepidoptera, introduces a classification and terminology of swimming behavioral types, and where possible, includes supporting video documentation. We also discuss the potential functional, ecological, and evolutionary significance of these behaviors and highlight key directions for future research.

Most caterpillars observed in the various video sources were identified to at least the family level, and all taxonomic identifications were confirmed by all authors. Low resolution videos, or those in which caterpillars could not be reliably identified to at least the family level were excluded from analysis.

For behavioral videos generated specifically for this study (Supplementary Table 1), caterpillars were collected using forceps or sticks and gently placed on the surface of water in a plastic container to record their behavioral respose. Water was obtained from a nearby pond, puddle, or other freshwater source. Each caterpillar was observed for 10 to 60s, during which its behavioral response was recorded. Photographs and videos were captured using a smartphone camera. Following observation, each caterpillar was returned to its original habitat. The observer did not target specific taxa or habitats and was not instructed to search for particular species; rather, each observation was conducted opportunistically as a caterpillar was encountered in the field.

Each author independently reviewed all video recordings and classified behaviors as either swimming or non-swimming (Fig. 2). All authors reviewed recordings and compared their classifications and together reached a consensus behavioral assignment for each caterpillar (Table 1). In some cases, a single caterpillar exhibited more than 1 behavior during an observation, often transitioning from 1 type to another (e.g. Wiggle followed by S-wave), and such instances were noted (see Supplementary Table S1). For quantitative summaries, each behavior was counted independently in Table 1; for example, if both S-wave and Wiggle behaviors were observed in a single trial, each was counted once.

Terminology

Multiple families of Lepidoptera are known to include species with aquatic or semi-aquatic larvae. These include the Crambidae subfamily Acentropinae (Solis 2019, Bethin et al. 2026) and the Cosmopterigidae genus Hyposmocoma, from Hawaii (Rubinoff and Schmitz 2010). A total of 15 families are reported to inhabit semi-aquatic environments (Pabis 2018). Interestingly, some species in the Erebidae and Noctuidae have been observed to swim (Hayashi and Sugiura 2021). The distinction between aquatic and semi-aquatic conditions, however, can be complex. In this study, we define “aquatic” species as those whose caterpillars live entirely submerged for at least 1 full instar. “Semi-aquatic” refers to species whose larvae do not remain fully submerged and can survive without continuous access to water. By contrast, “terrestrial” species are those that interact with water only incidentally and are unable to survive prolonged immersion. Subsequent discussion of relationships between family-group taxa of Lepidoptera follows the higher-level classification proposed in Kawahara et al. (2019).

Results

Summary of Observations

We compiled caterpillar behavioral data from field and laboratory observations conducted by the authors and collaborators, as well as videos of swimming caterpillars obtained from publicly available repositories. In total, we analyzed 83 caterpillars (60 from direct observations and 23 from crowdsourced records), representing 32 identified species across 16 lepidopteran families.

Our observations revealed a striking diversity of behaviors exhibited upon water exposure. Of the 83 caterpillars studied, 117 behavioral events were documented (Table 1), as some individuals displayed multiple distinct behaviors within a single observation period. Detailed metadata associated with each observation are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2 for the personal and crowd-sourced datasets, respectively.

Caterpillar Swimming Ontology

We classified observed similarities and differences in caterpillar swimming to develop an ontological framework for terrestrial Lepidoptera. Upon initial contact with the water surface, caterpillars either remained supported by surface tension or sank below the surface. Those that sank often exhibited a set of distinct responses, including defecation, bubble formation at the spiracles, or the appearance of a plastron-like air layer enveloping the body.

Those that remained afloat displayed a range of behaviors described below and illustrated in Fig. 1, which shows eight different caterpillars, one in each panel, showing the larvae on or in the water. Exemplar videos for each behavior are provided in the online supplementary materials. Of the 16 lepidopteran families represented in our study, directional swimming behavior was recorded in 5 families: Crambidae, Erebidae, Lasiocampidae, Noctuidae, and Pterophoridae (Table 1). Erebidae and Lasiocampidae exhibited the greatest behavioral diversity, with 4 and 3 directional types observed, respectively (Erebidae: n = 16, S-wave, W-wave, Tail-flick; Lasiocampidae: n = 7, S-wave, Side-flick, Tail-flick). The remaining families displayed 1 or 2 types each: Crambidae (n = 2, S-wave, W-wave), Noctuidae (n = 2, Tail-flick), and Pterophoridae (n = 1, Side-flick).

Non-directional behaviors were widespread, observed in 15 families, 11 of which showed only non-directional responses (Table 1). Sphingidae (n = 4) consistently exhibited the partially submerged “teaspoon” behavior (Fig. 1B), with the head and thorax above the water and the remainder below. Two families (Hesperiidae and Notodontidae) lacked water surface behaviors and sank; however, each was represented by only a single observation.

Discussion

Diversity and Morphology: Facilitating Aquatic Survival in Terrestrial Lepidoptera

Insects evolved primarily as a terrestrial lineage, with a few specialized groups independently recolonizing aquatic environments (Pabis 2018). Caddisflies, the sister order of Lepidoptera, are predominantly aquatic. This close relationship between Lepidoptera and Trichoptera offers a valuable comparative framework for understanding the evolution of aquatic locomotion (Grimaldi and Engel 2005, Sukatsheva and Sinitshenkova 2023). While most caddisfly females swim underwater to oviposit, larval swimming remains poorly studied—perhaps because many larvae are case-bearing and sedentary, while others construct retreats or capture nets (Frandsen et al. 2024). Reports describing larval swimming are available—Triaenodes bicolor (Tindall 1964) and T. tardus (Gall et al. 2011, Leptoceridae)—with the latter hypothesized to swim among aquatic vegetation for foraging and shelter. Additional trait data have been collected for this broad group of aquatic larvae, providing greater insight to aquatic locomotion in Trichoptera and aquatic Lepidoptera (Vieira et al. 2006, Schmidt-Kloiber and Hering 2015), primarily focused on aquatic biomonitoring, rather than the biomechanics of aquatic locomotion.

Aquatic locomotion in terrestrial Lepidoptera had not been formally documented other than the work of Hayashi and Sugiura (2021). Most research has focused on the larval stages of inherently aquatic or semi-aquatic Lepidoptera, such as Acentropinae and Pyraustinae (Crambidae), Hyposmocoma (Cosmopterigidae), or Arctiinae (Erebidae), which have at least 1 life stage under water (Welch 1919, Yen 2004, Mey and Speidel 2008, Rubinoff and Schmitz 2010, Pabis 2018, Solis 2019). This research has largely overlooked a broader range of aquatic behaviors that are present in primarily terrestrial caterpillars.

Building on Hayashi and Sugiura (2021), our study documents a wider suite of swimming behaviors across distantly related Lepidoptera in a larger geographic region (North America versus Japan), several behaviors of which are described here for the first time. Most behaviors that we observed (i.e. S-wave, W-wave, Tail-flick, and Side-flick) occurred on the air–water interface, a mode of locomotion also referred to as “water-walking” (e.g. Hu and Bush 2005). Water-walking requires that an insect’s body weight be supported by surface tension and that propulsion be generated without breaking the free surface. For small insects, hydrophobic surfaces and other morphological features help resist wetting and help maintain support at the air-water interface; once the body becomes wet, surface-tension support is reduced and the risk of sinking increases (Bush et al. 2007). Bush and Hu (2006) provide a comprehensive review of the physical forces governing insect floatation and locomotion.

Adaptations to minimize getting wet include the presence of hairs or setae, micro- or nano-structural surface roughness, and hydrophobic cuticular coatings (Meyer-Rochow 2016). Hairs and setae are common on caterpillars, although their density and morphology can vary widely. These structures serve diverse functions—sensory, defensive, and adhesive (Wagner and Hoyt 2022)—and in an aquatic context, can trap air, increase buoyancy, and alter the contact angle with the water surface (Bush et al. 2007). Fine microsculpture on hairs further enhances air retention and buoyancy (Balmert et al. 2011). Air trapped within the setal layer may help prevent drowning, as demonstrated by Meyer-Rochow (2016), who showed that caterpillars of Lemyra imparilis (Erebidae) sank when hairs were removed, whereas intact, hairy individuals remained afloat and were able to move across the water surface. Lepidoptera species that we observed exhibited various degrees of hairiness (Supplementary Table S1), which may coincide with swimming ability.

Hairs can also facilitate plastron formation, enabling submerged insects to continue respiring. For example, Malacosoma castrensis (Lasiocampidae), which feeds in tidal habitats, can survive prolonged submergence using long, thick hairs that generate a stable plastron maintained by hair microstructure (Kovalev et al. 2020). We observed similar plastron formation in several submerged individuals, such as larvae of Hesperiidae (Supplementary Video 23). Further study of hair ultrastructure across species may clarify how these traits contribute to buoyancy, plastron stability, and movement along the water surface.

The Four Directional Movement Types

Propulsion on the water surface has been extensively studied in various insects and arthropods, including water striders, fishing spiders, and amphibious centipedes (Bush and Hu 2006). Movement across the water surface requires a complex interaction of hydrodynamic, aerodynamic, and surface tension forces that are generated as the organism deforms the water surface, producing forward thrust (Bush and Hu 2006, Bush et al. 2007). Modes of locomotion include rowing and walking (e.g. water striders, fishing spiders) as well as meniscus climbing (e.g. larvae of Galerucella nymphaeae [Chrysomelidae]). These behaviors may resemble terrestrial gaits, such as walking, or may be specifically adapted to the physical constraints of the water surface.

Anterograde crawling, or stepping, is a common form of locomotion in terrestrial caterpillars (van Griethuijsen and Trimmer 2014), including most species we observed. This gait involves sequential waves of movement that originate at the posterior end and propagate forward through each appendage, propelling the body ahead. The “W-wave” pattern we observed was most like anterograde crawling, although it was recorded infrequently (Erebidae, Crambidae, Table 1). Because anterograde crawling relies on a tension-based “environmental skeleton” that requires firm substrate contact (van Griethuijsen and Trimmer 2014), the air–water interface offers insufficient resistance for effective grip. Consequently, this gait is likely to be inefficient and therefore uncommon in aquatic settings.

In contrast, the “S-wave” movement was the most frequently observed form of directional movement and was almost exclusively seen in Erebidae. This movement resembles the undulatory movement described by Hayashi and Sugiura (2021); this represents an anguilliform mode of locomotion (Stin et al. 2024), in which thrust is generated through rhythmic body undulations. We recorded this form of motion in several species, including L. dispar, Parapoynx sp., and Spilosoma virginica. The S-wave motion is strikingly distinct from the terrestrial anterograde crawling gait used by these caterpillars.

Anguilliform locomotion is well documented among aquatic and semi-aquatic invertebrates. For example, the amphibious centipede Scolopendra subspinipes uses a different gait in water than on land, producing posteriorly traveling body waves that generate forward propulsion (Yasui et al. 2019). Similar undulatory patterns have been reported in aquatic dipteran larvae (Ceratopogonidae), which “swim” by propagating lateral waves along the body (Mullen and Hribar 1988). Free-swimming polychaete worms also exhibit S-wave locomotion, although their undulations propagate from posterior to anterior, with forward movement generated through interactions between the setae and surrounding water (Gemmell et al. 2024). Because terrestrial caterpillar crawling simiarly invovles waves of body movement that propagate from the posterior end, the S-wave swimming observed in caterpillars may represent an extension of this underlying locomotor pattern.

A third mode of movement, the “tail-flick” behavior, was previously described by Hayashi and Sugiura (2021) and observed in our study. This behavior involves rapid flicking of the posterior abdominal segments, reminiscent of the initial movements associated with anterograde crawling (van Griethuijsen and Trimmer 2014). Comparable behaviors have been described in Diptera, including mosquito larvae, which flex their posterior abdominal segments to produce thrust during “wriggle swimming” (Walker and Merritt 1991). The side-flick motion we observed in a pterophorid larva appears to closely resemble tail-flicking, further suggesting possible convergent evolution among distantly related taxa.

Non-Directional Movement

Non-directional behaviors were observed across nearly all lepidopteran families examined. These behaviors can be broadly classified as fully passive (e.g. doughnut, hotdog), in which the caterpillar remains immobile and suspended on the water surface, or partially active, where the individual moves but fails to generate coordinated forward propulsion (e.g. wiggle, crawl, teaspoon). Both may represent defensive responses, with doughnut and wiggle postures resembling antipredator behaviors seen in caterpillars (Greeney et al. 2012). A related phenomenon, meniscus climbing, known from other water-walking insects (Hu and Bush 2005), may provide an analogue. For example, larvae of the water lily leaf beetle, G. nymphaeae, can move along the water surface by altering body shape to generate propulsion through capillary action without using their limbs (Bush et al. 2007). Behaviors that appear passive or non-directional in Lepidoptera may in fact involve similar mechanisms, enabling limited movement along the surface or facilitating escape through manipulation of surface tension.

The “teaspoon” behavior was distinctive and observed exclusively in Sphingidae. In this posture, the caterpillar hangs vertically, with the posterior half of its body submerged and the other half above the surface. Although this position may seem to offer little obvious advantage, we hypothesize that in shallow water, the robust bodies of sphingid larvae may allow them to contact the substrate and propel themsleves forward by kicking against the sediment (see Supplementary Videos 69 and 74). The ability of the anterior body to remain above the surface—despite typically breaking surface tension leading to sinking—is unexpected and highlights the need for high-speed videography under controlled conditions to clarify the biomechanical basis of these behaviors.

Many caterpillars remained motionless immediately following water exposure. For example, in Supplementary Video 33, a noctuid caterpillar first adopted a doughnut posture—possibly a defensive reaction to contact—then gradually extended its body and transitioned to undulatory wiggling motions. These sequences suggest that swimming responses are triggered by tactile or hydrodynamic cues and may represent emergency escape mechanisms. In contrast, we also observed cases in which caterpillars, particularly those feeding on aquatic or riparian plants, actively approached the water’s edge and exhibited efficient directional swimming (S-wave pattern; Supplementary Videos 65 and 73). Such behavior likely facilitates movement between vegetation patches while reducing predation risk during transit (Hayashi and Sugiura 2021). The ability of terrestrial caterpillars to sense and respond to water surfaces, transitioning from non-directional to directional locomotion, suggests an evolved and functionally adaptive response to transient aquatic environments.

Conclusions and Future Directions

Our dataset substantially expands the number and taxonomic breadth of documented instances of swimming in terrestrial caterpillars, building on the work of Hayashi and Sugiura (2021). We documented swimming behavior in 16 lepidopteran families and developed the first behavioral ontology describing caterpillar responses to water. Among taxa exhibiting active swimming, Lasiocampidae larvae moved exclusively by tail-flicking, whereas Erebidae displayed greater behavioral diversity but predominantly used S-wave undulations. Most behaviors occurred across multiple families, with only the teaspoon behavior, represented by 4 observations, restricted to a single family. The recurrence of similar behaviors across distantly related lineages raises the possibility that they arise from shared features of caterpillar morphology, musculature, or neural control. Although these behaviors may primarily facilitate escape following accidental contact with water, surface swimming could also contribute to dispersal between host plants or across flooded or fragmented habitats. The biomechanical and neural mechanisms generating directional movement, and the extent to which similar behaviors have evolved independently across Lepidoptera, remain important areas for future investigation.

Even behaviors that involve little or no active swimming (e.g. crawl, doughnut, hotdog, teaspoon, wiggle) may provide functional advantages by helping caterpillars remain afloat or drift with surface currents until they reach vegetation or other solid surfaces. Similarly, maintaining a plastron during submersion may enhance buoyancy and reduce the risk of drowning. Collectively, these findings reveal unexpected behavioral and morphological diversity in how terrestrial caterpillars interact with water and highlight the need for further quantitative and biomechanical studies to determine the functional significance of these behaviors.

Although our study fills a major knowledge gap in caterpillar swimming behavior, it has several limitations. Sampling was opportunistic and biased toward large, late-instar larvae, particularly Lymantriinae and Malacosoma, which are conspicuous and frequently encountered in habitats influenced by human activity. These factors may have contributed to their disproportionate representation in our dataset. Future studies should employ standardized experiments and high-speed videography to quantify swimming kinematics, test the functional roles of morphology and setation, and determine whether similar behaviors occur in lepidopteran lineages not represented here. Broader sampling across developmental stages, body sizes, habitats, and phylogenetic lineages will also be important for determining the ecological and evolutionary origins of these behaviors. Continued documentation by both professional and amateur entomologists will help reveal the full diversity of swimming behavior in terrestrial caterpillars.
