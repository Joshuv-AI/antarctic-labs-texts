Atmospheric turbulence sensing with structured terahertz beams containing orbital angular momentum
Authors: Ruiyi Shen, Miranda van Iersel, Yasaman Ghasempour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clear-air turbulence poses a persistent hazard to aviation, yet it evades conventional weather radar and remains difficult to detect. Existing optical sensing methods falter under severe turbulence and are extremely vulnerable to misalignment between airborne nodes. Here we introduce an atmospheric sensing scheme built on structured terahertz (THz) beams carrying orbital angular momentum (OAM). We derive and experimentally validate an analytical model that maps turbulence-induced OAM modal crosstalk to the refractive index structure constant of the atmosphere. Our simulations and experiments demonstrate that this THz-OAM approach remains robust under severe turbulence where optical systems saturate, and offers orders-of-magnitude greater misalignment resilience. Furthermore, this scheme also enables localization of the dominant turbulent clusters through understanding the interaction of diverging THz-OAM beams with the turbulent medium in the reciprocal propagation paths and simultaneously monitoring water vapor to identify turbulence precursors. This work establishes structured THz-OAM beams as a robust framework for remote atmospheric turbulence inference.

Introduction

Atmospheric turbulence, characterized by stochastic, small-scale, vibrant air motions, presents significant challenges to environmental sensing, aerospace engineering, and optical communication systems due to its random and fast-changing nature in both spatial and temporal domains. In commercial aviation, for example, intense turbulence can impose severe aerodynamic loads that can compromise the structural integrity of aircraft and lead to passenger and crew injuries1,2. The standard method for turbulence detection utilizes onboard X-band (8–12 GHz) weather radar, which infers air motion from the Doppler information of backscattered signals from precipitation particles such as raindrops and snowflakes3–5. This information can guide pilots in taking detours from known turbulent atmospheres. A limitation of this technique, however, is that it fails to detect clear-air turbulence, a phenomenon commonly observed at high altitudes in non-precipitating atmospheres, posing a substantial risk6,7. Beyond aviation, accurate profiling of clear-air turbulence is also important for understanding the dynamics of the Earth’s atmosphere, potentially improving numerical weather prediction, and advancing climate studies8–11. Therefore, the development of a robust, real-time system for accurately characterizing turbulence intensity, especially for clear-air turbulence, is of great importance to both scientific research and practical applications.

Most existing clear-air turbulence detection techniques onboard aircraft are confined to reactive, in-situ methods, such as wind sensors and pilot reports. These methods are fundamentally limited because they only offer turbulence estimation upon encounter, rather than providing the remote sensing capability required for advanced warning and proactive avoidance. To address this gap, a few preliminary works have demonstrated the possibility of remote turbulence detection in the optical domain12,13. One prominent approach is to use LIDAR, which interrogates the atmosphere with a pulsed infrared laser to measure the backscattered signal from small aerosols and therefore infer turbulence-induced Doppler velocity shifts in the atmosphere14–16. The efficacy of this approach is often constrained by low signal-to-noise ratios, either due to low aerosol concentrations in clear, dry air, or from significant signal attenuation due to poor weather conditions such as fog17–19.

Alternatively, atmospheric turbulence fundamentally alters the propagation of electromagnetic waves; therefore, electromagnetic measurements can provide insights into the properties of the turbulent medium. Specifically, turbulent eddies induce random fluctuations in the refractive index of the atmosphere, which in turn impose phase and amplitude aberrations on a propagating wavefront20–22. This principle inspires the use of optical scintillometers, which measure path-integrated turbulence strength by analyzing the intensity fluctuations (i.e., scintillation) of a laser beam13,23,24. However, the short wavelengths intrinsic to the optical regime introduce fundamental limitations. First, under moderate-to-strong turbulence conditions, optical systems suffer from saturation, where the scintillation index plateaus with increasing turbulence strength25,26. While optical systems offer advantages in characterizing weak turbulence, often analyzed along vertical or slant paths for fields including astronomical imaging and free-space optical communication, a broader understanding of turbulence dynamics across moderate to strong regimes is also essential for applications operating along predominantly horizontal paths, such as environmental monitoring and aviation safety, where severe turbulence events can threaten the safe operation of aircraft. Furthermore, optical systems are extremely sensitive to misalignment between the transmitting and receiving nodes, which can usually be caused by mechanical vibrations on mobile platforms. The minute beam wander can introduce intensity fluctuations that overwhelm the turbulence-induced signatures, making the system unreliable for mobile platforms27,28.

To overcome these limitations, particularly for horizontal-path turbulence sensing relevant to aviation, we propose and demonstrate a sensing modality based on structured light at terahertz (THz) frequencies. This approach provides two major advantages over conventional optical techniques. First, the THz regime extends the operational dynamic range of turbulence characterization. Since the strengths of turbulence effects scale with wavelength, THz beams are less susceptible to saturation effects under moderate-to-strong turbulence. Additionally, THz beams are more robust to misalignment, which is common for mobile platforms. This is because the phase tilt induced by angular misalignment is proportional to the wavenumber (k = 2π/λ), which is orders of magnitude smaller for THz waves, resulting in more stable and reliable measurements. While shifting to even longer wavelengths, such as millimeter-wave or microwave bands, further prevents saturation and reduces misalignment sensitivity, it introduces extreme beam divergence from an equivalent transmit aperture, making it impossible to be deployed on mobile nodes. In contrast, the THz bands represent an optimal convergence point that escapes the saturation limits of the optical regime, while restricting beam divergence to a manageable scale. Despite offering this ideal physical balance, the shift to the THz frequencies presents its own sensing constraints. A backscattering-based approach analogous to LIDAR in THz frequencies is invisible to small aerosols in the atmosphere. Furthermore, the weaker interaction with turbulent eddies makes forward-propagating intensity-based measurements (i.e., from a transmitter to a separate receiver) less sensitive. Despite the insensitivity in intensity, the phase aberrations imposed by the random refractive index fluctuations persist29–31. Hence, probing the phase front distortions becomes a promising modality for robust THz turbulence sensing.

While prior works in optics have explored using structured light to probe turbulence, they typically rely on coherent detection to directly analyze the complex amplitude and phase profiles of the distorted field32–34. Due to the lack of synchronization between bistatic nodes (i.e., when the transmitter and receiver are not co-located) and the stringent hardware requirements, directly measuring the complex received phase profile is impractical. Instead, we employ an alternative indirect approach to bypass the need for coherent field detection. By projecting the distorted phase front onto an orthogonal basis, we transform the problem of profiling phase aberrations into an incoherent measurement of power distribution across discrete modes to infer turbulence-induced phase changes. Specifically, beams carrying orbital angular momentum (OAM), characterized by their helical phase fronts, offer an ideal basis due to the spatial orthogonality in their mode order, l, in a non-turbulent medium35–37. Interestingly, propagation through atmospheric turbulence corrupts the phase front. This disrupts the orthogonality between distinct OAM modes and induces inter-modal interference, or crosstalk, where power from a transmitted mode, lTX, is distributed into other modes, l ≠ lTX38–40. Our key insight is that the magnitude of modal crosstalk is a direct function of the phase variance induced by the turbulence, and hence, provides a quantitative measure of turbulence strength. This framework transforms the complex problem of measuring random phase aberrations across a beam profile into an incoherent measurement of power distribution across discrete modes. Moreover, this technique offers capabilities beyond path-integrated turbulence estimation. Given that the distribution of atmospheric turbulence is not always homogeneous along the horizontal propagation path (e.g., localized patches of turbulence), we demonstrate that the discrepancy between crosstalk measurements from reciprocal propagation paths (i.e., transmitting THz beams from node A to B vs. B to A) can be used to infer the center location of dominant turbulence clusters along the propagation path. As existing works on turbulence profiling exploit complex, longitudinally structured beams that do not apply in the THz regime due to far-field radiation, our method leverages the natural divergence of THz-OAM beams for turbulence localization. While prior work has viewed OAM crosstalk induced by turbulence as a problem for OAM-based communication systems that needs to be mitigated41–43, here we treat this phenomenon as an opportunity for turbulence sensing.

In this work, we establish and validate a comprehensive framework for turbulence sensing with THz-OAM beams. We develop a sensing-oriented analytical model that provides a direct quantitative link between the measured modal crosstalk and the refractive index structure constant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{n}^{2}$$\end{document}Cn2). This model is rigorously validated through extensive wave-optics simulations and lab experiments with custom-designed, 3D-printed phase plates to emulate the phase aberrations induced by propagation through atmospheric turbulence. Our experimental results demonstrate a root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 2.22 dB in turbulence characterization across weak to severe turbulence strengths. A comprehensive comparative analysis further highlights the fundamental advantages of the THz modality over optical frequencies, demonstrating its ability to quantify severe turbulence in regimes where an analogous optical system saturates and fails, and its superior resilience to platform misalignment. This work opens possibilities for remote atmospheric diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and the fundamental study of turbulence statistics in the THz regime. The proposed turbulence sensing framework establishes structured THz waves as a robust modality for addressing a critical challenge in aviation safety and climate science.

Numerical validation and performance analysis

To validate our analytical model and quantify the performance advantages of turbulence sensing at THz frequencies compared to the optical regime, we conduct a series of wave-optics simulations. Our simulations are based on a widely used split-step beam propagation method, where the turbulence-induced refractive fluctuations are represented by a sequence of random phase screens, and the field propagation between adjacent screens is treated as free-space diffraction calculated with the Rayleigh–Sommerfeld diffraction integral48–51. We simulate a 5-km atmospheric path divided into 20 equally spaced discrete segments, each representing a 250-m propagation distance. The first segment is modeled as free-space propagation, allowing the beam to develop from the source. At the beginning of each of the subsequent segments, we apply a random phase screen that follows Kolmogorov turbulence statistics to the beam’s complex electric field. We obtain the final beam profile after sequentially propagating through each segment. We consider a 50-cm transmitting aperture and a receiver aperture that is sufficiently large to capture the expanded OAM beam after propagation. At the receiver, the distorted field is decomposed via an inner product with an ideal OAM basis to calculate the power distribution across different analyzed modes. Our analytical model in Eq. (7) specifically quantifies the interference between any transmitted mode lTX and received mode lRX as a measurable crosstalk magnitude Rturb(lTX, lRX). Therefore, to validate the model, our analysis focuses on quantifying the power leakage between specific (lTX, lRX) pairs. To account for the randomness of each generated turbulence phase screen and ensure statistically robust results, we perform a Monte Carlo analysis by averaging the resulting crosstalk amplitude ratios over 100 independent repetitions under each simulated environment.

First, we validate the analytical model presented in Eq. (7) by calibrating it against simulation data at 300 GHz. This frequency was selected as a representative value for the THz regime (corresponding to a 1 mm wavelength) that falls within an atmospheric transmission window. The modal power spectrum for OAM beams propagating through turbulence typically follows a normal distribution, with power decaying exponentially away from the transmitted mode lTX46,52. In addition to the operating turbulence strength range, another critical performance metric for a system intended for airborne platforms is its resilience to angular misalignment, a factor often overlooked in prior studies that assume perfectly aligned systems55,56. For lateral misalignment, as long as the beam remains within the receiver aperture, the effect is largely independent of wavelength. In contrast, the system’s response to angular misalignment (i.e., tilt) reveals a significant frequency dependence. The fundamental challenge here is that an angular tilt can distort the wavefront, leading to high modal crosstalk that is indistinguishable from turbulence-induced effects. Therefore, such misalignment can severely compromise the accuracy of turbulence sensing from the OAM cross-mode interference patterns. We model the effect of a small angular tilt by applying a linear phase ramp across the beam’s wavefront. At the receiver plane, for misalignment angles θx and θy in the Cartesian coordinates, the received field is modified by adding a new phase term to the ideal electric field profile E(x, y, z) as 8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{tilt}(x,y,z)=E(x,y,z)\exp (ik(x\sin {\theta }_{x}+y\sin {\theta }_{y})).$$\end{document}Etilt(x,y,z)=E(x,y,z)exp(ik(xsinθx+ysinθy)).

Note that this tilt-induced phase ramp is directly proportional to the wavenumber k. We simulate the effect of angular tilt on both a 300 GHz THz system and a 1550 nm optical system, assuming the same physical propagation parameters. To ensure a fair comparison, the turbulence regime chosen in Fig. 3c corresponds to comparable Fried parameter (r0) and crosstalk magnitudes, rather than identical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{n}^{2}$$\end{document}Cn2 values, as the optical system enters the strong saturation regime at turbulence levels that are still weak for the THz systems according to our previous discussion. As shown, angular tilts as small as 2.5 μrad induce significant deviations of the simulated crosstalk from the calibrated model in the optical system that completely overwhelm the turbulence-induced signatures. This artifact is so large that it yields crosstalk magnitudes exceeding the theoretical maximum under a perfectly aligned system, causing a complete breakdown of the sensing framework. The THz system, in contrast, exhibits a negligible impact from the same amount of angular misalignment. While the absolute tilt tolerance necessitates an active stabilization and misalignment mitigation system for any airborne turbulence detection system, the THz system provides an angular error budget much larger than that of the optical system. This significantly relaxes the requirement for real-time adaptive alignment and proves that the THz-OAM modality offers potential for a more robust turbulence sensing system with nodal mobility. Furthermore, while optical systems typically rely on micro-electromechanical mirrors for angular correction, THz systems can leverage fast electronic beamforming via phased arrays or metasurfaces. In addition, lateral offsets between the transmit beam axis and the receiver aperture can also introduce additional modal crosstalk. We evaluate the impact of lateral nodal misalignment in Supplementary Note 8. Importantly, active beam tracking and beamforming strategies can be implemented to maintain both lateral and angular misalignment57–60.

Experimental validation

To provide a proof-of-concept validation of our sensing modality, we conduct lab-scale experiments using emulated turbulence phase screens (see Methods and Supplementary Note 7 for more details). A general schematic of our setup is shown in Fig. 4a. We generate OAM-carrying beams at 300 GHz using a 3D-printed spiral phase plate configured at lTX = 1 with an aperture of 3.81 cm. To emulate the effect of propagation through turbulence, we introduce two custom-designed, 3D-printed phase plates that impose phase aberrations following Kolmogorov statistics. We place the two plates at 0.1 m and 0.35 m, respectively, along a 0.6-m propagation path. We first aim to directly characterize the effect of these phase screens on the beam’s complex field. We fabricate 10 sets of turbulence phase plates, corresponding to a Fried parameter, r0, ranging from 28 cm down to 1.76 cm (covering weak to strong turbulence strength with D/r0 ∈ [0.21, 3.26] when normalized to the beam diameter D). We first mount the receiver on a 2D translational stage and use it as a coherent field probe, performing a raster scan to capture the electric field intensity and phase across the beam profile. As illustrated in the field heatmaps in Fig. 4b, the helical wavefront and the donut-shaped intensity profile are progressively corrupted as turbulence strength increases. Subsequently, we numerically decompose the measured field data into different OAM modes, which quantitatively confirms that the power leakage from transmitted mode lTX = 1 into adjacent modes becomes progressively stronger with smaller r0. The minor modal impurities and profile distortion visible in the baseline case without turbulence are expected experimental artifacts as detailed in Supplementary Note 7. While direct field profiling is ideal for crosstalk measurement, a practical sensor at THz wavelengths requires a more direct power measurement and cannot rely on the slow raster scans. To validate our turbulence estimation modality, we implement a mode-selective detection scheme. We custom-designed receiver phase plates that perform a conjugate phase transformation, converting a specific OAM mode into a Gaussian beam that can be focused into the receiver for incoherent power measurement. Importantly, without relying on the reconstruction of the full received field, this measurement scheme reduces the overhead and computational complexity in conventional optical OAM sensing. Since our framework targets the measurement of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{n}^{2}$$\end{document}Cn2, which is a statistical parameter that is typically considered stationary over second-to-minute scales61–63, the rapid processing from our incoherent measurement scheme allows for acquiring more independent turbulence response samples within a short time window. This ensures a robust and real-time statistical estimation of dynamic turbulence responses.

Although our previous analysis is generalized to any arbitrary (lTX, lRX) pairs, as discussed in Supplementary Notes 6 and 7, we select a single receiving mode lRX = 0 adjacent to lTX = 1 with the highest crosstalk magnitude to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio of the crosstalk measurement. We designed two corresponding receiving phase plates to measure the power remaining in the transmitted mode and the power redistributed to the adjacent mode, respectively. To generate robust statistics for each turbulence strength, we emulate 100 independent turbulence realizations by randomly rotating each of the phase plates relative to each other. The received amplitudes at lRX = 1 and lRX = 0 are recorded for each turbulence realization, and the average measured signal amplitude between the two modes is calculated as the normalized crosstalk.

As shown in Fig. 4c, the experimental data show excellent agreement with both wave-optics simulations and the calibrated analytical model from Eq. (7). Furthermore, we show in Fig. 4d that the mean estimation error for the experimental data remains low across the entire tested range, achieving an overall RMSE of 2.22 dB, while that for the simulation data is 0.64 dB. Interestingly, the measured error is not uniformly distributed across the sensing range, but rather dominated by the error spike in the weakest turbulence regime, which is an expected artifact from this framework. Our THz-OAM-based modality senses turbulence by measuring a crosstalk signal that approaches zero as turbulence vanishes. In this regime, the turbulence-induced crosstalk is comparable to, or even smaller than, a static background crosstalk floor caused by experimental nonidealities (e.g., minor 3D printing fabrication error, imperfect alignment, etc.). A discussion of the accuracy of phase plate fabrication and the impact of an imperfect experimental setup is provided in Supplementary Note 7. As turbulence strength increases, the desired crosstalk signal quickly grows to overwhelm this artifact floor, where the estimation error drops significantly. Because our sensing framework targets the moderate-to-strong turbulence regime where modal crosstalk dominates the hardware imperfections, the static modal impurities have a negligible impact on the overall sensing robustness.

In addition, the simulation results predict an increase in the variance of the estimation error (shaded region) in the stronger turbulence regime. We investigate this phenomenon by plotting the measured amplitude distributions in Fig. 4e. Under weak turbulence (r0 = 28 cm), the signal distributions for the transmitted and crosstalk modes are narrow and well-separated. However, under strong turbulence (r0 = 1.8 cm), the distributions broaden and overlap significantly. This higher statistical variance in power measurements due to increased random phase fluctuation explains the higher variance in turbulence estimation, which can potentially be improved with increased averaging. The agreement between experiment, simulation, and theory in our results validates our core sensing principle. While these measurements provide validation of using THz-OAM beams for turbulence sensing, an optical benchmark under the same physical turbulence conditions, aperture size, and propagation distance is not practical in our experimental platform as a direct comparison. However, the saturation of crosstalk of an OAM optical link and the extreme sensitivity to angular misalignment have been explored in the literature46,64. Instead, we conduct a supplementary experiment that evaluates the improved tolerance of OAM modal crosstalk to angular misalignment for our THz systems, as detailed in Supplementary Note 8.

While our lab-scale setup utilizes complex benchtop equipment as a proof-of-concept (details discussed in “Methods” and Supplementary Note 7), practical deployment on mobile nodes requires significantly more compact hardware. Since our proposed framework only requires incoherent power measurements, wideband optical sources and physical delay lines connecting the transmitter and receiver are not required. Instead, a more compact system can be implemented using solid-state THz transceivers (e.g., frequency multipliers based on Schottky diodes)65. To realize OAM generation and reception, practical systems may use reconfigurable beamforming hardware, such as analog beamforming arrays or reflect arrays66,67. The availability of such devices allows for practical deployment on standard commercial aircraft. Future distributed or synthetic aperture implementations may further reduce the hardware complexity of large-aperture OAM transmitters and receivers at THz68.

Localizing the center of turbulence with THz-OAM beams

In previous discussions, we assumed a statistically uniform turbulent medium, for which our method provides a path-integrated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{n}^{2}$$\end{document}Cn2. In many practical scenarios, however, the turbulence distribution can be inhomogeneous, consisting of localized, dominant clusters69,70. Under these scenarios, a simple path-integrated average is insufficient as it does not offer insights into advanced warning, such as guiding pilots to detour from a strong turbulent patch. Therefore, it is highly desired to localize the center of the dominant turbulence. Although prior work on turbulence localization in the optical regime exists, it typically requires complex systems, such as using pairs of crossed beams that require large and bulky receiving apertures71, or exploiting longitudinally structured beams that cannot be generated at long distances at THz frequencies due to far-field approximations56,72.

We demonstrate a method of localizing the center of a dominant turbulent cluster along a horizontal propagation path by leveraging the natural divergence of THz-OAM beams. Specifically, the underlying principle is that the magnitude of turbulence-induced crosstalk is weighted by the beam’s effective diameter, D(z), at location z along the propagation path. To exploit this, as shown in Fig. 5a, we consider two reciprocal propagation paths between two transceivers: one forward path transmitting from node A (position z = 0) and receiving at node B (position z = Z), and a reciprocal backward path from node B to node A. As illustrated, the THz-OAM beam diameter naturally increases with propagation distance (see Supplementary Note 10 for the full relationship). Assuming identical transceivers, the beam diameter is a function of the distance propagated from its source. Therefore, for the forward path (A → B), a turbulent cluster at location z interacts with a beam that has propagated a distance z, while for the reciprocal backward path (B → A), the same cluster interacts with a beam that has propagated a distance Z − z. This asymmetry is most pronounced for turbulence clusters near either node. For example, a turbulent cluster near node B (i.e., at z ≈ Z) interacts with a wide forward beam (which has propagated nearly the full distance Z) but a narrow backward beam (which has just started propagating). This results in stronger crosstalk for the forward propagating path Rturb,FW(lTX, lRX) than that of the backward propagation Rturb,BW(lTX, lRX) between the same transmitted and received mode pair. Importantly, our model theoretically assumes a dominant turbulent cluster on a calm background. In practice, however, the atmosphere always has a non-zero background turbulence. Therefore, we investigate the performance when a dominant cluster is superimposed on this background. As shown in Fig. 5d, when the background turbulence becomes comparable in strength to the dominant cluster, the medium begins to behave as a homogeneous path. Consequently, the forward-backward asymmetry diminishes, and the crosstalk ratio converges to 1. This result offers an insight into the sensitivity threshold of the inhomogeneity required for effective localization.

It is also worth noting that our proposed turbulence localization method is not applicable to equivalent optical systems because of their quasi-collimated beams that exhibit negligible beam divergence over comparable distances. Thus, rather than treating it as a limitation, we leverage the natural divergence of THz-OAM beams, transforming a perceived disadvantage into a powerful sensing technique for localizing dominant turbulent clusters (see Supplementary Note 10). While this technique primarily determines the center location of the turbulent cluster rather than its full profile, such information offers important insight for hazardous turbulence avoidance. Further extensions to jointly determine the location and scale of turbulence are also possible (see Supplementary Note 11).

Additionally, the THz regime offers potential for simultaneously monitoring water vapor concentration leveraging THz-specific molecular absorption lines. Discussion

In this study, we establish a comprehensive framework for atmospheric turbulence sensing using structured THz beams carrying OAM. Our approach is based on the principle that random phase aberrations introduced by the refractive index fluctuations in turbulence corrupt the THz-OAM wavefront, and, therefore, compromise the orthogonality between independent OAM modes. We develop a physics-guided analytical model that establishes a direct quantitative link between OAM modal crosstalk and turbulence strength, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{n}^{2}$$\end{document}Cn2, and rigorously validate this model through both wave-optics simulations and lab experiments with emulated turbulence phase screens. Our experimental results demonstrate an accurate turbulence strength estimation with an RMSE of 2.22 dB across a wide range of turbulence conditions. In addition, we comprehensively analyze the fundamental advantages of the THz modality over conventional optical systems. We show that THz-OAM sensing provides an extended dynamic range for turbulence estimation under severe conditions relevant to aviation safety, and demonstrates improved resilience to the angular misalignments common to airborne platforms. Furthermore, we demonstrate through both simulations and experiments that the distinctive characteristics of THz-OAM radiation enable capabilities extending beyond conventional path-integrated turbulence estimation. By leveraging the natural divergence of THz-OAM beams, we show that asymmetries in the forward and return OAM crosstalk patterns can be analyzed to pinpoint the location of the dominant turbulent cluster within an inhomogeneous medium. Interestingly, we also establish that the THz-based turbulence sensing can enable atmospheric water vapor monitoring, which potentially helps turbulence prediction that guides preventative measures. This work reframes crosstalk in THz-OAM beams from a communication impairment into a powerful sensing mechanism, establishing a robust platform for real-time atmospheric turbulence characterization and aviation hazard avoidance.

Methods

Experimental setup

All experimental measurements were performed with a scaled table-top turbulence emulation setup, as shown in Supplementary Note 7. For THz signal generation and detection, we use the T-Ray 5000 THz time-domain spectroscopy system by TeraMetrix. The transmitter and receiver modules are each equipped with a fiber-coupled photoconductive antenna that is excited by ultra-short pulses, providing raw time-domain measurements with a flat frequency response between 100 and 400 GHz. The THz transmitter generates a Gaussian beam that is modulated by a spiral phase plate designed for lTX = 1 to produce a beam carrying OAM for turbulence sensing. The THz-OAM beam propagates over a 0.6-m path, which includes two emulated turbulence phase screens equally dividing the space between 0.1 m and 0.6 m.

For initial OAM field characterization, the receiver is mounted on a 2D translational stage and operates as a coherent field probe, performing a raster scan to sample the complex electric field across the beam profile. Although this method allows for full numerical modal decomposition, it is not scalable for a practical THz sensor. Therefore, for the primary turbulence estimation validation, we implement a mode-selective incoherent detection scheme. We design and fabricate two receiver phase plates based on Fresnel optics that combine the functions of OAM mode conjugation and focusing. These plates are designed to convert a specific incoming OAM mode (lTX = 1 for the transmitted mode and lRX = 0 for the adjacent crosstalk mode) into a Gaussian beam, which is then focused onto the broadband THz detector for power measurement.

Due to power limitations, our measurements have been limited to a table-top scale. However, this experimental setup is physically equivalent to a much longer propagation path through the atmosphere, a principle established by scaling via the Fresnel number, F = a2/(λZ), where a is the transmitting aperture size and Z is the total path length. For instance, our lab setup, which uses a 3.81 cm transmitter aperture over a 0.6 m path, can be scaled to a 5-km propagation path with an equivalent aperture of approximately 3.48 m in diameter. Turbulence phase plate fabrication

To emulate the phase aberrations induced by atmospheric turbulence in a controlled lab environment, we design and fabricate phase plates based on the standard Fourier transform method for generating random phase screens with Kolmogorov statistics.
