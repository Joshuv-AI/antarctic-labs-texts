Psychometric properties of the Arabic version of the Scale on Community Care Perceptions (SCOPE) among nurses: a methodological study
Authors: Talal Ali F. Alharbi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global strategic shift toward community-based healthcare requires a motivated nursing workforce, yet robust instruments to assess Arabic-speaking nurses’ perceptions of this field are lacking.AimThis study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and psychometrically validate the Arabic version of the Scale on Community Care Perceptions (SCOPE) among registered nurses in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA methodological cross-sectional design was employed with a sample of 374 registered nurses. Following World Health Organization guidelines for translation and cultural adaptation, the instrument’s psychometric properties were rigorously evaluated. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted to test the construct validity of the hypothesized three-factor structure. Internal consistency was assessed using Composite Reliability (CR), while convergent and discriminant validity were examined using Average Variance Extracted (AVE) and the Fornell-Larcker criterion.ResultsThe CFA confirmed the original three-factor model—Future Profession, Affective Component, and Placement—demonstrating good model fit (χ²/df = 1.947; CFI = 0.932; RMSEA = 0.050). All standardized factor loadings were significant, ranging from 0.638 to 0.783. The scale exhibited accepted reliability, with CR values ranging from 0.86 to 0.94. Furthermore, AVE values exceeded 0.50, and discriminant validity was established, confirming the distinctiveness of the constructs.ConclusionThe Arabic version of the SCOPE is a psychometrically sound instrument with robust validity and reliability. Notably, the original three-factor structure was preserved in its entirety without item deletion—a key strength that distinguishes this validation from prior adaptations. It offers educators and health policymakers a reliable metric to evaluate nursing perceptions, facilitating data-driven workforce planning to meet national health transformation goals.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

The global healthcare landscape is undergoing a profound transformation, characterized by a strategic shift from hospital-centric models toward community-based and primary health care (PHC). This paradigm change is driven by the imperative to enhance accessibility, contain costs, manage the rising burden of chronic diseases, and promote preventive health at the population level [1]. The World Health Organization has long advocated for strengthened PHC as the cornerstone of equitable and sustainable health systems. This shift is particularly salient in regions like the Middle East, where nations are actively reforming their health sectors to address demographic changes and evolving disease profiles [2]. In the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, this vision is operationalized through ambitious national strategies, most notably Saudi Vision 2030, which explicitly prioritizes the enhancement of preventive care, community health services, and primary care accessibility. The success of such transformative agendas is inextricably linked to a competent, motivated, and adequately distributed nursing workforce, the largest segment of healthcare professionals globally [3].

Within this context, nurses’ perceptions, attitudes, and professional intentions toward community and primary care emerge as critical determinants of health system performance. Positive perceptions are foundational for recruiting and retaining nurses in community settings, roles that are often perceived as less prestigious or professionally rewarding compared to acute care specialties [4]. The affective connection nurses feel toward community care, the quality of their experiential learning during community placements, and their resultant career intentions directly influence workforce distribution, the quality of community-based services, and ultimately, patient outcomes. Therefore, accurately measuring and understanding these multifaceted perceptions is not merely an academic exercise but a strategic necessity for effective nursing workforce planning, curriculum development in nursing education, and evidence-based health policy formulation [5].

Importantly, nurses’ perceptions of community care should be understood within a broader framework of professional readiness for systemic healthcare transformation. The contemporary nursing workforce is confronted with a dual imperative: adapting to community-based service delivery models while simultaneously navigating the integration of disruptive innovations, including artificial intelligence (AI) and digital health technologies. Recent evidence demonstrates that nurses’ readiness for technological innovation, encompassing knowledge, attitudes, and confidence toward AI, mirrors the same structural dimensions of professional readiness implicated in community care adoption: cognitive appraisal, affective engagement, and behavioral intention [6]. Nurses who exhibit limited knowledge of AI yet express optimism about its potential for improving care quality and alleviating burnout share a psychological profile with those who are open to community roles but require structured support to consolidate that readiness into sustained professional commitment [6, 7]. This parallel underscores that perceptions of community care are not an isolated construct but rather one expression of a broader professional orientation toward healthcare transformation. Validating instruments that capture these perceptions is therefore foundational to understanding how nurses position themselves within rapidly evolving health systems.

Despite this critical need, a significant gap exists in the availability of robust, culturally adapted instruments to assess these complex perceptions among Arabic-speaking nursing populations. While several tools have been developed to measure attitudes toward community care, they have predominantly been conceptualized, validated, and applied within Western socio-cultural and healthcare contexts [8]. The direct application of such instruments in distinct cultural settings like Saudi Arabia is methodologically precarious, as linguistic translation alone cannot guarantee the preservation of semantic, conceptual, and metric equivalence. Differences in educational systems, clinical placement structures, professional hierarchies, and societal views on community roles can fundamentally alter how scale items are interpreted and responded to [9]. Consequently, there is a pressing need for instruments that are not only linguistically translated but also psychometrically validated within the specific population of intended use to ensure they reliably measure the intended constructs.

The SCOPE, developed by Van Iersel et al. (2018), is a theoretically grounded tool designed to address this measurement need [10]. Its development was motivated by the observed discrepancy between the growing demand for community nurses and nursing students’ limited interest in this field in Western countries. The scale is premised on the theory that clinical placement experiences (cognitive evaluation) and affective engagement (emotional response) are powerful influencers of future professional behavior. The original SCOPE is a 35-item instrument comprising: (1) a quantitative section with 33 items across three core subscales—Future Profession (FP) (17 items, assessing career intention), Affective Component (AC) (11 items, measuring emotional valuation), and Perception of Community Care as a Placement (PCCP) (5 items, evaluating the learning environment); and (2) two qualitative items exploring placement preference and the reasons underlying that choice. Its initial psychometric validation among Dutch nursing students supported a three-factor structure, with the quantitative subscales demonstrating acceptable to good internal consistency [10].

Empirical literature underscores the relevance of the SCOPE’s constructs. Studies consistently show that well-supported, positive clinical placements in community settings can enhance attitudes and increase willingness to pursue community health careers, while poor experiences can deter interest. The affective dimension acts as a key mediator, translating experience into sustained professional interest. However, the cross-cultural validity of the SCOPE’s structure cannot be assumed. For example, a recent adaptation and validation study in Turkey [11] could not confirm the original three-factor model via CFA. Instead, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) yielded a six-factor solution for the Turkish version, indicating that the factorial structure may vary across cultures. This finding highlights a critical knowledge gap and underscores the necessity of conducting rigorous psychometric validation within each new cultural and linguistic context, rather than assuming structural invariance.

Notably, the psychometric properties of the SCOPE scale, while established in its original Dutch/English format and subsequently in a few other languages, remain unevaluated in an Arabic-speaking, Middle Eastern nursing population. This represents a clear knowledge gap. The absence of a validated Arabic instrument impedes the ability of researchers, educators, and policymakers in Saudi Arabia and the broader Arab region to reliably assess current perceptions, evaluate the impact of educational interventions, diagnose barriers to community nursing recruitment, or conduct meaningful cross-cultural comparisons [12]. Validating the SCOPE scale in this context is therefore a prerequisite for generating locally relevant, high-quality evidence to inform practice and policy.

The significance of this psychometric validation study is multifaceted. For nursing education, a validated Arabic SCOPE scale provides a reliable outcome measure to evaluate the effectiveness of community health curricula and placement programs. Educators can use it to identify which aspects of the learning experience (e.g., mentorship quality, perceived utility) are strong or weak, enabling targeted improvements [13, 14]. For nursing management and health policy, the tool offers a diagnostic mechanism to understand workforce attitudes. By pinpointing whether reluctance toward community roles stems from negative perceptions of the work environment (Placement), a lack of emotional connection to the work (Affective Component), or weak career intention (Future Profession), leaders can design more nuanced and effective retention and recruitment strategies [15, 16]. For research, this study contributes a validated metric for future investigations, enabling studies on predictors of community care career choice, longitudinal tracking of perception changes, and cross-cultural analyses of nursing attitudes [17]. Ultimately, by providing a psychometrically sound measurement tool, this study supports data-driven decisions aimed at strengthening the community nursing workforce, a critical objective for achieving national health goals like those outlined in Saudi Vision 2030.

Methods

Design

This study utilized a quantitative, methodological cross-sectional research design to rigorously evaluate the psychometric properties of the Arabic version of the SCOPE scale, following strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) checklist [18]. Methodological research is critically important to ensure that the scale developed in one linguistic or cultural context maintain their theoretical structure and measurement precision when applied to a new population. Specifically, this study sought to confirm the dimensionality of the SCOPE scale using CFA and to establish its utility as a robust assessment tool for nursing education and practice in the Middle East.

Participants and setting

Target population and setting

The target population consisted of registered nurses (RNs) currently employed in Qassim University Medical City and Al-Qassim National Hospital. The study was conducted across a varied range of healthcare settings to ensure the sample reflected a broad spectrum of nursing experiences, ranging from acute care settings to community-based practice. To be eligible for inclusion, participants met the following criteria: (a) held a valid licensure as a registered nurse in Saudi Arabia; (b) were currently practicing in either a clinical or community health setting; (c) possessed fluency in reading and comprehending Arabic to ensure accurate interpretation of the translated items; and (d) voluntarily agreed to participate. Nurses who were on extended leave (e.g., sabbatical, maternity leave) or who held purely administrative roles with no patient interaction or clinical oversight were excluded to ensure the data reflected active professional perceptions.

Sampling strategy and sample size

A convenience sampling method was employed to recruit participants. While probability sampling is ideal, convenience sampling is frequently utilized in psychometric validation studies to access specific professional populations efficiently. The final sample consisted of 374 registered nurses. This sample size was determined to be adequate for Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) and CFA. Methodological literature suggests a minimum sample size of 200–300 for stable factor solutions [19], or a ratio of at least 10 participants per estimated parameter [20]. With 33 items in the original pool and a three-factor structure, the sample of 374 exceeds these recommendations, ensuring sufficient statistical power to detect model misspecifications and validating the robustness of the parameter estimates.

Instrument: The SCOPE scale

Original instrument

The SCOPE scale was originally developed to assess nursing perceptions of community care [10]. The instrument is theoretically grounded in the premise that positive placement experiences and affective engagement influence future career intentions. It consists of a three-factor structure; future profession (FP), assessing the intention to pursue a career in community health, affective component (AC), measuring the emotional response and valuing of community care work, and perception of community care as a placement (PCCP), Evaluating the quality of clinical learning environments and mentorship in community settings.

Translation and cultural adaptation

To ensure semantic, idiomatic, and conceptual equivalence between the original English scale and the Arabic version, a rigorous translation and adaptation process was conducted following the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines and standard practices for cross-cultural adaptation [21]. (1) Two independent bilingual experts (fluent in English and Arabic) translated the instrument from English to Arabic. One translator was a nursing academic aware of the study concepts, while the second was a linguistic expert naïve to the specific constructs, ensuring everyday language usage. (2) The two forward translations were compared, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus to create a preliminary Arabic version. (3) Two different independent translators, who had never seen the original instrument, back-translated the preliminary Arabic version into English. This step verified that the meaning of the items had not been altered during translation. (4) A panel comprising five experts in nursing education, and community health reviewed the pre-final version. They assessed the content validity and cultural relevance of the items for the Saudi nursing context. Minor modifications were made to phrasing to enhance clarity and cultural appropriateness. (5) The pre-final Arabic version was pilot-tested with a small group of 30 nurses (excluded from the main study) to assess face validity and readability. Feedback confirmed that the items were clear and easy to understand.

Ethical consideration

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Committee of Health Research Ethics at Qassim University, Saudi Arabia (Ethical Approval No: 21-19-04). All procedures performed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. Confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing all data, and participation was voluntary with the right to withdraw at any stage.

Data collection procedures

Data collection took place over a three-month period. An online survey using Microsoft forms was utilized to distribute the questionnaire. The survey link was disseminated through professional nursing networks, hospital internal email systems, and official social media groups for nurses. To control for potential response bias, the survey was anonymous, and no personal identifiers (such as names or employee IDs) were collected. The opening page of the online survey served as an informed consent form, detailing the study’s purpose, the estimated time for completion (approx. 10 min), the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of the data. The online form restricted for only one response from each IP address. Participants were informed that they could withdraw at any time by closing the browser. Submission of the completed questionnaire was considered indicative of informed consent.

Statistical analysis

Data were extracted from the online platform and imported into SPSS Version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) for initial screening. The dataset was examined for missing values, outliers, and normality. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion if less than 5%, or expectation-maximization techniques, if necessary, though the online survey settings minimized missing responses. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies) were calculated to characterize the sample.

To evaluate the construct validity and psychometric properties of the Arabic version of the SCOPE scale, a CFA was conducted using AMOS software (v.26). Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation was employed to test the measurement model based on the hypothesized three-factor structure: Future Profession (FP), Affective Component (AC), and Placement (PCCP). Model fit was evaluated using a combination of absolute and incremental fit indices, adhering to the recommended criteria of cut-off by [20, 22]: Chi-square (χ²) test, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). While a non-significant χ² is ideal, it is highly sensitive to sample size; therefore, the normed chi-square χ²/df was used, with values < 3.0 indicating good fit and values up to 5.0 considered acceptable. Regarding CFI and TLI, Values > 0.90 were considered indicative of acceptable fit, and values > 0.95 indicated excellent fit. For RMSEA, Values < 0.06 indicated good fit, and values < 0.08 indicated acceptable fit. The 90% Confidence Interval (CI) for RMSEA was also examined to assess the precision of the fit estimate.

Convergent Validity was assessed to ensure that items sharing a common construct significantly shared variance. Criteria for convergent validity included: (a) standardized factor loadings ≥ 0.50 (Hair et al., 2010), (b) Average Variance Extracted (AVE) ≥ 0.50, and (c) Composite Reliability (CR) ≥ 0.70. To establish Discriminant Validity indicating that the factors were distinct constructs, the Fornell-Larcker criterion was applied. Discriminant validity is supported if the square root of the AVE for each construct is greater than its correlation with any other construct in the model [23]. Internal consistency to ensure Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and CR, with a threshold of > 0.70 indicating acceptable reliability.

Discussion

The present study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Arabic version of the SCOPE scale among registered nurses in Saudi Arabia. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to validate this instrument within an Arab Middle Eastern context. The findings provide robust empirical evidence supporting the construct validity, discriminant validity, and internal consistency reliability of the scale, suggesting that the three-factor structure of the original instrument is replicable and stable across cultures.

Interpretation of the factor structure

The primary finding of this study is the confirmation of the three-factor structure—Future Profession, Affective Component, and Placement (PCCP)—using CFA. The model fit indices indicated a strong alignment between the theoretical model and the observed data. The normed chi-square (χ²/df) of 1.947 falls well within the strict range for good fit, and the RMSEA of 0.050 (90% CI: 0.046–0.055) suggests that the model accounts for the data with very little error of approximation. Similarly, the CFI (0.932) and TLI (0.927) exceeded the standard heuristic of 0.90, confirming acceptable model fit.

These results align with, and extend, the findings of the original scale development study by Van Iersel et al. (2018), who identified these overarching constructs. However, our findings diverge significantly from the Turkish validation study conducted by Ozen et al. (2022). In their adaptation with nursing students, Ozen and colleagues attempted a CFA of the original three-factor model but reported poor fit indices (CFI = 0.713; RMSEA = 0.094), leading them to reject the original structure. Consequently, they employed EFA and derived a considerably different six-factor structure (e.g., splitting constructs into “Working Perception,” “Professional Practices,” and “Attractiveness”). Furthermore, the Turkish study necessitated the deletion of five items (items 18, 26, 27, 28, and 31) to achieve acceptable psychometric properties. In contrast, our study successfully confirmed the original three-factor structure without deleting any items, demonstrating that the Arabic version preserves the theoretical integrity of the SCOPE scale more faithfully than the Turkish version.

It is important to note that both the original Dutch validation by Van Iersel et al. (2018) and the Turkish adaptation by Ozen et al. (2022) were conducted with undergraduate nursing students a population whose perceptions of community care are still being formed through clinical education [10, 11]. In contrast, the present study recruited registered nurses with active professional experience. This distinction is theoretically meaningful: professional maturity fosters a more integrated and coherent understanding of community care practice, affective engagement, and placement (PCCP) quality. Experienced nurses have already navigated the transition from academic preparation to clinical reality, consolidating their professional identity in ways that students have not yet achieved. This cognitive and professional integration likely yields a cleaner factor structure that mirrors the original theoretical framework—one where Future Profession, Affective Component, and Placement (PCCP) represent genuinely distinct yet interrelated dimensions of community care perception. This structural invariance in the Arabic version is crucial for comparative research, as it implies that the instrument measures the same latent traits in Saudi nurses as it does in Western populations, a stability that appears less robust when the instrument is applied to student populations, as evidenced by both the Turkish adaptation and the original Dutch validation context.

Validity and reliability

Convergent validity

The study demonstrated strong evidence of convergent validity. All 33 standardized factor loadings were statistically significant (p < .001) and robust, ranging from 0.64 to 0.78. According to Hair et al. (2010), loadings above 0.50 are considered practical significant, and loadings above 0.70 are indicative of a well-defined structure. The fact that the majority of items loaded highly on their respective factors suggests that the translation process successfully captured the nuance of the original English items without losing their semantic meaning [24].

Furthermore, the AVE values for Future Profession (0.50), Affective Component (0.52), and Placement (PCCP) (0.55) all met or exceeded the 0.50 threshold. This indicates that the latent constructs capture more variance in the observed items than is attributable to measurement error. This is a critical indicator of psychometric quality, often difficult to achieve in translated scales due to subtle shifts in meaning. The success here likely reflects the rigorous forward-backward translation protocol and expert review process employed.

To contextualize these findings within contemporary nursing instrument development, the CR and AVE values obtained in the present study are comparable to those reported in the recently developed and validated Nurse-Patient Therapeutic Interaction Scale (NuPTIS; Erkayiran & Aslan, 2025), which demonstrated high internal consistency and robust construct validity through an analogous multi-step CFA protocol [25]. The convergence of psychometric outcomes across these independently developed instruments reinforces the methodological rigor of the present validation and affirms that the Arabic SCOPE meets the benchmarks of modern nursing measurement science.

Discriminant validity

Discriminant validity was established via the Fornell-Larcker criterion. The square roots of the AVEs (ranging from 0.708 to 0.742) were consistently higher than the inter-construct correlations. This confirms that while the factors are related, they remain statistically distinct. Interestingly, the correlation analysis revealed a strong positive relationship (r = .72) between Placement (PCCP) and the Affective Component. The correlations between Future Profession and the other two constructs were moderate (r = .29 and 0.31), suggesting that the intention to pursue a career in this field is a distinct outcome, related to but separate from current feelings and environmental experiences. However, an interesting divergence from the original study was observed in the inter-construct relationships. Van Iersel et al. (2018) reported the strongest correlation between Placement and Future Profession (r = .62), implying that for Dutch students, the practical experience was the primary driver of career intent. Theoretically, this makes intuitive sense: a nurse’s emotional valuation of community care (Affective) is likely heavily influenced by the quality of their clinical exposure and mentorship in that setting (Placement/PCCP) [26–28]. Despite this strong correlation, the discriminant validity testing confirms they are not the same construct; one measures the environment, while the other measures the internal emotional response.

Reliability

The internal consistency of the Arabic SCOPE scale was more than accepted and notably higher than that reported in the original development study. CR values ranged from 0.86 to 0.94. In comparison, Van Iersel et al. (2018) reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.696 to 0.862. Similarly, while Ozen et al. (2022) reported a high total reliability (α = 0.91), their subscale reliabilities varied significantly (ranging from 0.617 to 0.887) [11], with some factors showing lower consistency than those observed in our study. Most significantly, the Placement (PCCP) subscale in the current study showed considerably higher reliability (CR = 0.86) compared to the original Dutch study (α ≈ 0.70). This suggests that the translated items regarding clinical mentorship and learning environments resonated clearly with the Saudi nursing workforce. This improvement in reliability might reflect the distinct structure of post-graduate nursing practice in Saudi Arabia compared to the student populations originally studied, or it may indicate that the Arabic translation achieved a high degree of internal coherence. These values confirm that the Arabic SCOPE produces stable and precise measurements suitable for use in high-stakes assessments or research.

The affective component: Internal psychological determinants

The emergence of the Affective Component (AC) as a psychometrically robust and theoretically coherent subscale in this validation warrants deeper consideration of the internal psychological mechanisms that may shape nurses’ emotional valuation of community care. The AC captures nurses’ emotional engagement with community settings—their sense of meaning, belonging, and professional fulfillment in roles that are structurally distinct from, and often perceived as less prestigious than, acute hospital care. An underexplored determinant of this affective orientation is the stability of a nurse’s professional self-esteem.

Self-esteem stability—defined as the degree of consistency and security in one’s self-evaluative responses across time and situations—has emerged as a distinct and clinically meaningful construct in nursing research [29]. Unlike trait self-esteem level, self-esteem stability captures the variability of self-worth, with lower stability associated with heightened sensitivity to environmental cues, social comparison, and professional threat. Nurses with unstable self-esteem may be more vulnerable to the prestige differential between hospital and community settings, interpreting a community placement not as a professional opportunity but as a signal of diminished status. Conversely, nurses with stable self-esteem are better equipped to derive intrinsic meaning from community roles, sustaining positive affective engagement irrespective of external prestige hierarchies. This theoretical connection suggests that interventions targeting self-esteem stability—through reflective supervision, professional identity development programs, and peer mentorship—may serve as upstream levers for improving AC scores among nurses resistant to community deployment. Future research should examine self-esteem stability as a moderator of the relationship between community placement experience and affective outcomes, using validated instruments such as the Turkish-adapted Self-Esteem Stability Scale (SESS; Erkayiran et al., 2026) [29], which has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties in a nursing sample (CFI = 0.99; RMSEA = 0.03; α = 0.86; test-retest r = .84).

Implications for nursing practice, education, and research

The validation of the Arabic SCOPE scale has significant implications for the healthcare sector in Saudi Arabia and the broader Arab region.

For Nursing Education

As nursing curricula increasingly shift towards community-based care, driven by national health initiatives like Saudi Vision 2030, educators need reliable tools to assess student readiness. The Arabic SCOPE can be used to evaluate the effectiveness of community health rotations. For example, if scores on the Placement (PCCP) subscale are low, academic institutions can investigate clinical sites to improve mentorship quality. Similarly, tracking Affective Component scores can help educators identify whether curricula are successfully instilling the values of preventative and community healthcare.

For Clinical Nursing

Nurse managers and policymakers can utilize this tool to assess workforce attitudes toward community deployment. Recruitment into PHCs is often challenging due to the prestige gap between hospital and community nursing. By using the SCOPE scale, administrators can identify specific barriers—whether they are environmental (Placement/PCCP) or attitudinal (Future Profession)—and design targeted interventions to improve retention and recruitment in community settings.

For Research

This study provides researchers with a validated metric for future investigations. The scale can now be used as a dependent variable in intervention studies designed to improve community care perceptions, or as a predictor variable in studies examining nursing career trajectories.

Limitations

Despite the study’s strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design captures perceptions at a single point in time. While this is sufficient for establishing factorial validity, it prevents the assessment of test-retest reliability or causal inferences regarding how perceptions change over time. Because test-retest reliability was not assessed in the present study, the temporal stability of SCOPE scores among Saudi nurses remains unknown. Longitudinal studies are therefore recommended to track the stability of these scores throughout a nurse’s career, providing evidence of both measurement consistency and the dynamic nature of community care perceptions over time.

Second, the study utilized a convenience sample of nurses in Saudi Arabia. While the sample was sufficient for statistical analysis, the generalization of findings to nurses in other Arabic-speaking countries (e.g., Egypt, Jordan, UAE) should be approached with caution, given potential variations in healthcare systems and Arabic dialects. Future research should aim for multi-national studies to establish cross-cultural invariance across the Middle East.

Third, as with all self-report measures, there is a potential for social desirability bias, where participants may rate their perceptions of care more favorably than they genuinely feel. Future validation efforts could benefit from correlating SCOPE scores with objective behavioral outcomes, such as actual employment uptake in community settings or performance evaluations.

Finally, predictive validity was not assessed in this study. Future research should examine whether scores on the Future Profession subscale actually predict which nurses eventually choose careers in community health.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the Arabic version of the SCOPE scale is a psychometrically sound instrument that demonstrates robust construct validity, discriminant validity, and high internal consistency reliability. The findings support the three-factor structure of Future Profession, Affective Component, and Placement (PCCP) within the Saudi nursing context. A particularly noteworthy strength of this validation is the preservation of the original three-factor structure in its entirety, without the deletion of any items—a finding that stands in contrast to the Turkish adaptation, which required item removal and yielded a divergent six-factor solution. This structural fidelity affirms the suitability of the Arabic SCOPE for use with registered nursing professionals and enhances its utility for cross-cultural comparative research. This validated tool fills a critical gap in the existing literature, providing educators, managers, and researchers with a reliable method to assess and understand nurses’ perceptions of community care. Its application can facilitate evidence-based improvements in nursing education and workforce planning, ultimately contributing to the strengthening of community health services in the region.
