Gut and Gastric Dysbiosis in Hyperemesis Gravidarum: A Scoping Review of Microbiome-Targeted Evidence and Translational Pathways
Authors: Neilvy Ratnadewi, Farid Husin, Johanes Cornelius Mose, Mas Rizky Anggun Adipurna Syamsunarno, Setyorini Irianti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHyperemesis gravidarum (HG) is defined by severe nausea and/or vomiting beginning in early pregnancy, impaired ability to eat or drink normally, and a marked restriction of daily activities. Although gastrointestinal dysbiosis has been proposed in HG, its role remains uncertain within the emerging growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15)-centered model of susceptibility.MethodsGuided by PRISMA-ScR, this scoping review searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, publisher websites, and reference lists for contemporary evidence published between 1 January 2021 and 2 May 2026. Pre-2021 foundational publications were excluded from the charted evidence map and included-study count and were cited only as methodological or biological background.ResultsThirty-six sources of evidence were synthesized across five thematic domains. Direct human studies identified associations between HG and altered microbial diversity or composition, as well as gastric Helicobacter pylori exposure, but taxonomic findings were inconsistent and no reproducible diagnostic signature emerged. Mendelian randomization implicated Coprococcus 2, Ruminococcus, and Turicibacter, whereas observational studies could not resolve whether dysbiosis preceded symptoms or followed vomiting, dietary restriction, medication exposure, or hospitalization. Pregnancy-context and mechanistic studies supported plausible pathways involving bile acids, short-chain fatty acids, intestinal barrier function, inflammation, and gut–brain signaling, but these remain indirect for HG. GDF15-centered endocrine susceptibility had the strongest mechanistic support. Pregnancy probiotic and prebiotic studies provided safety and proof-of-concept data, while HG-specific efficacy evidence remained insufficient.ConclusionCurrent evidence supports dysbiosis as an associated modifier, consequence, or endotype marker rather than an established independent cause of HG. Priority studies should use Windsor-compatible definitions, early longitudinal sampling, harmonized sequencing and metabolomic methods, concurrent assessment of GDF15 and nutritional status, and biologically stratified trials of confirmed H. pylori infection or reproducible functional dysbiosis.

Introduction

Hyperemesis gravidarum (HG) is a condition that begins in early pregnancy and is characterized by severe nausea and/or vomiting, inability to eat or drink normally, and marked restriction of daily activities, with symptom onset typically before 16 weeks of gestation.1,2 Dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, weight loss, nutritional compromise, hospitalization, psychosocial distress, and adverse maternal and fetal outcomes are important manifestations or sequelae rather than defining features.1–4 Variability in diagnostic definitions across clinical guidelines, observational studies, and intervention trials has complicated comparisons across studies and presents a particular challenge for microbiome research, as microbial composition is highly sensitive to gestational age, symptom duration, dietary intake, hydration status, medication exposure, antibiotic use, acid-suppressive therapy, and hospitalization.5–7

The conceptual understanding of HG has changed substantially over the past decade. Advances in human genetics, biomarker discovery, and mechanistic research have established growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) as the principal biological driver of HG susceptibility. Fetoplacental GDF15 signals through the growth differentiation factor receptor alpha-like (GFRAL) pathway in the hindbrain, whereas interindividual variation in maternal sensitivity to GDF15 appears to influence disease severity.8–10 This paradigm has largely replaced earlier psychogenic theories and human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)-centered hypotheses, providing a more coherent neuroendocrine explanation for severe pregnancy-associated nausea and vomiting. Nevertheless, the central role of GDF15 does not exclude additional biological mechanisms. Rather, it establishes the foundation of a GDF15-centered systems biology framework within which gut and gastric dysbiosis should be interpreted. Alterations in the maternal microbiome may modulate disease severity by amplifying emetic signaling, influencing inflammatory responses, reflecting nutritional deprivation and treatment-related changes, or identifying biologically distinct disease endotypes within the broader GDF15-sensitive phenotype.

Pregnancy is accompanied by coordinated physiological adaptations involving the gut microbiome, host metabolism, immune regulation, and nutritional homeostasis. Longitudinal studies have demonstrated that the composition and function of the maternal gut microbiome change dynamically throughout gestation and are influenced by dietary patterns, geographical location, parity, metabolic status, lipid metabolism, and immune adaptation.11–15 These physiological changes are particularly relevant to HG because persistent vomiting rapidly alters dietary intake, reduces consumption of fiber, protein, fat, and micronutrients, disrupts gastric acidity and gastrointestinal motility, and increases exposure to antiemetics, antibiotics, and acid-suppressive medications. Consequently, distinguishing cause from consequence remains challenging. Dysbiosis may precede the onset of symptoms in susceptible women, Conversely, severe vomiting, starvation, dehydration, ketonuria, acid–base disturbances, and therapeutic interventions may substantially reshape the gut microbiome after disease onset.

Several microbiome-related aspects warrant comprehensive evaluation within the context of HG. First, Helicobacter pylori, a gastric microorganism with well-established gastrointestinal pathogenicity, has consistently been associated with NVP and HG, and recent pregnancy-specific studies further support associations between this infection and severe NVP, dyspepsia, and adverse pregnancy outcomes.16–20 Second, direct evidence from gut microbiome studies is beginning to emerge through Mendelian randomization analyses, stool-based microbiome investigations, and large nested case–control studies evaluating NVP severity.19–22 Third, microbial metabolites, bile acid metabolism, intestinal permeability, mucosal inflammation, and gut–brain communication represent biologically plausible mechanistic pathways linking microbial alterations with nausea and vomiting, although much of the available evidence remains indirect and not specific to HG.20,23,24

Previous reviews have primarily addressed HG diagnosis and management, nutritional consequences, or broad pathophysiology, while direct gut and gastric microbiome evidence remains fragmented across the clinical, genetic, and translational literature.2,3,5,25 A new scoping review is timely because contemporary microbiome methods and GDF15-related genetic and translational evidence have expanded rapidly and require interpretation within a common evidence hierarchy. Accordingly, this review aimed both to map the extent and characteristics of the evidence and to synthesize its implications. Its novelty lies in separating direct HG microbiome findings from contextual pregnancy and mechanistic literature, then integrating both within a contemporary GDF15-centered systems biology framework.

Methods

Study Design and Reporting Framework

This scoping review was conducted to systematically map the nature, extent, characteristics, and distribution of the available evidence on gut and gastric dysbiosis in HG. A scoping review methodology was selected because the available evidence remains limited, heterogeneous, and methodologically diverse, precluding meaningful quantitative synthesis. The review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodological guidance for scoping reviews and was reported in accordance with the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). Selected principles from the PRISMA 2020 Statement were also incorporated to enhance reporting transparency.26–28

Consistent with JBI guidance, formal critical appraisal was not undertaken because the objective of this review was evidence mapping rather than assessment of methodological quality or estimation of pooled effects. Instead, each included study was systematically charted according to study design, study population, microbiome-related construct, principal findings, and relevance to the review objectives. This structured approach facilitated a comprehensive overview of the existing literature, identified knowledge gaps, and informed priorities for future research while preserving the exploratory purpose of a scoping review.

Objectives and Review Questions

Objectives

The primary objective of this scoping review was to provide a comprehensive map of contemporary evidence on gut and gastric dysbiosis in hyperemesis gravidarum (HG) and severe nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP). Specifically, the review sought to synthesize current evidence on alterations in the maternal gut microbiome, gastric microbial exposure, microbial metabolites, inflammatory pathways, nutritional factors, and microbiome-targeted interventions in HG. The review also aimed to identify major knowledge gaps and priorities for future research. Particular emphasis was placed on interpreting microbiome-related evidence in the context of emerging evidence on GDF15 biology to strengthen biological interpretation and inform future translational research. The review objectives and research questions were developed using the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute to ensure that the scope of the review was clearly defined and aligned with scoping review methodology.

Review Questions

This scoping review was guided by four questions: (1) What direct evidence links alterations in the gut microbiota, microbial genetic determinants, or gastric microbial exposures to hyperemesis gravidarum or severe nausea and vomiting of pregnancy? (2) Which biological mechanisms involving gut microbial dysbiosis, Helicobacter pylori, microbial metabolites, bile acid metabolism, intestinal permeability, inflammation, nutritional disturbances, gut–brain signaling, and GDF15–GFRAL signaling are supported by the available evidence? (3) What evidence exists regarding microbiome-targeted interventions, including probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, and other microbiome-based therapeutic approaches, and what is known about their maternal and fetal safety? (4) Which methodological limitations constrain the current evidence, and which study designs should be prioritized to generate robust and clinically meaningful findings in future microbiome research on HG?

Eligibility Criteria

The eligibility criteria were developed using the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework recommended in the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodological guidance for scoping reviews and are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.27 The criteria were designed to capture direct evidence on gut and gastric dysbiosis in HG while retaining a clearly labeled contextual evidence stream from pregnancy microbiome research. Direct HG evidence was treated as the analytical core; contextual pregnancy evidence was used as a comparator and for mechanistic interpretation, not as proof of HG-specific dysbiosis.

Studies were eligible if they investigated pregnant women with HG, severe nausea and vomiting of pregnancy, or pregnancy cohorts directly relevant to gastrointestinal microbiome physiology. Eligible concepts included gut or gastric microbiota, dysbiosis, Helicobacter pylori, microbial metabolites, bile acid metabolism, inflammatory and nutritional pathways, and microbiome-targeted interventions. Human pregnancy studies were prioritized. Animal-only primary studies were excluded; preclinical findings were considered only when reported within peer-reviewed mechanistic or translational syntheses and were used solely to explain a defined pathway, such as microbial metabolite signaling, intestinal barrier function, or gut–brain communication. This pathway-specific use constituted the “essential biological context” retained in the review.

The formal database search was restricted to 1 January 2021 through 2 May 2026 to capture the period in which contemporary pregnancy microbiome methods and GDF15-centered genetic and translational evidence expanded most rapidly. Backward citation searching was used only to identify pre-2021 foundational publications for narrative background and methodological context. Publications before 1 January 2021 were excluded from the charted evidence map, Tables 1–5, and the included-study count. Conference abstracts, non-peer-reviewed reports, animal-only primary studies, and records without a pregnancy-relevant microbiome concept were also excluded. Information Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and the Web of Science Core Collection to identify studies relevant to gut and gastric dysbiosis in HG. To maximize the completeness of the evidence, supplementary searches were performed on publisher websites and targeted hand-searching of reference lists and leading journals in obstetrics, microbiology, gastroenterology, and nutrition.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (where applicable) and free-text terms related to hyperemesis gravidarum, nausea and vomiting of pregnancy, gut and gastric microbiota, dysbiosis, Helicobacter pylori, microbial metabolites, bile acids, probiotics, and GDF15. Database-specific search strings were adapted to the indexing system and search functionality of each database to optimize retrieval of relevant studies. The complete database-specific search strategies are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

The search strategy was developed iteratively and refined through preliminary searches to maximize sensitivity while maintaining adequate specificity for the review objectives.

All database and supplementary searches were completed on 2 May 2026. Database-specific limits and complete reproducible strategies are reported in Supplementary Table S2.

To improve transparency and reproducibility, bibliographic information was verified using PubMed, PubMed Central (PMC), publisher websites, Digital Object Identifier (DOI) records, and official journal webpages whenever these resources were available. Because access to subscription databases was institution-dependent, the Scopus and Web of Science search strategies were structured to be reproducible and should be rerun immediately before manuscript submission to capture newly published studies and generate updated search histories and deduplication records in accordance with PRISMA-ScR recommendations.

Study Selection and Data Charting

Study selection was conducted in two sequential stages in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). First, titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers (N.R. and F.H.) to identify studies potentially relevant to the review objectives. Potentially eligible records subsequently underwent full-text assessment according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Any disagreements regarding study selection or eligibility assessment were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Data from eligible studies were independently charted by the same two reviewers using a standardized data-charting form developed a priori. The form was refined iteratively during pilot charting to ensure consistent extraction across heterogeneous study designs while maintaining alignment with the review objectives and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance. Extracted fields included citation, year, study design, setting and population, HG definition, gestational age at symptom onset and diagnosis, sample type and gestational age at biospecimen collection, microbiome or gastric-exposure measure, sequencing or analytical platform, covariates, principal findings, limitations, evidence classification, and relevance to HG. The complete blank form is provided as Supplementary Table S3.

Consistent with the objectives of a scoping review, the purpose of data charting was to characterize the nature, extent, characteristics, and distribution of the available evidence rather than to evaluate methodological quality or generate quantitative summary estimates. This approach enabled the identification of evidence clusters, research gaps, and future research priorities while preserving the exploratory purpose of evidence mapping.

Evidence Integration Strategy

The five high-level thematic domains were specified a priori based on the PCC framework and review questions: direct HG microbial evidence; contextual pregnancy microbiome dynamics; clinical and nutritional manifestations; molecular mechanisms; and interventions. During pilot charting, operational boundaries and descriptive labels were refined to accommodate heterogeneous study designs without changing the five-domain structure. Two reviewers checked domain assignments and resolved differences by consensus.

The 36 eligible sources of evidence formed the charted evidence map. Sources of evidence were classified as direct HG evidence, pregnancy-context evidence, mechanistic or translational context, or intervention evidence. Foundational publications outside the contemporary search window were excluded from the evidence map and included-study count and were cited only to explain historical, methodological, or biological context; they were not used to claim a contemporary HG-specific microbial effect.

Synthesis was narrative and explicitly separated evidence levels. Repeated human associations were described as established associations; Mendelian randomization or mechanistic signals were described as emerging evidence; and pathway interactions lacking direct HG confirmation were labeled hypothesis-generating or speculative. This approach reduced the risk of converting contextual plausibility into causal inference.

Accordingly, conclusions about dysbiosis were anchored primarily in direct human HG studies, while contextual literature informed interpretation, methodological comparison, and research prioritization.

Results

Search Results

The study selection process is illustrated in Figure 1 in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR). A comprehensive search of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, publisher websites, and hand-searched sources identified 275 records. Following the removal of 83 duplicate records, 192 unique records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 65 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, 29 were excluded, and 36 sources of evidence met the predefined inclusion criteria and were subsequently charted for thematic evidence synthesis.

Characteristics and Thematic Organization of the Included Evidence

The 36 charted sources of evidence encompassed a broad range of study designs, reflecting the multidisciplinary nature of contemporary research on gut and gastric dysbiosis in HG. The evidence included observational studies, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case–control investigations, multicenter clinical studies, Mendelian randomization analyses, genome-wide association studies, sequencing-based microbiome studies, systematic reviews, narrative reviews, and translational investigations. Collectively, these study designs provided complementary evidence spanning descriptive epidemiology, microbial characterization, molecular mechanisms, clinical outcomes, and potential therapeutic applications.

The included studies also represented multiple levels of biological investigation. Direct human microbiome studies evaluated alterations in gut microbial diversity, taxonomic profiles, and gastric microbial exposure, whereas contextual evidence described physiological pregnancy-associated microbiome remodeling and microbial metabolite production. Additional studies explored nutritional consequences, inflammatory responses, molecular susceptibility pathways involving GDF15 and host genetics, and emerging microbiome-targeted interventions. Together, these studies provided an integrated overview of current evidence spanning microbial ecology, host biology, and clinical translation. The geographical distribution of the included sources of evidence reflected broad international research activity, encompassing populations from Europe, Asia, North America, the Middle East, and multinational collaborative cohorts. Advances in high-throughput sequencing, multi-omics integration, and genetic epidemiology were particularly evident among more recent publications, illustrating the rapid evolution of microbiome research in maternal health during the past five years. The overall characteristics of the retained evidence are summarized in Table 1–5.

Unlike systematic reviews that primarily evaluate intervention effectiveness, this scoping review was designed to map the breadth and characteristics of the available evidence on gut and gastric dysbiosis in HG. Accordingly, the retained evidence encompassed a heterogeneous body of literature, including observational studies, genomic investigations, mechanistic studies, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, and translational research. Collectively, these sources of evidence provided complementary perspectives on microbial alterations, pregnancy-associated microbiome dynamics, host biological responses, molecular mechanisms, and emerging microbiome-targeted interventions relevant to HG.

As prespecified in the Methods, the included evidence was organized into five thematic domains according to primary research focus rather than publication chronology. Operational labels were refined during pilot charting, but the domain structure was not derived post hoc from the results. The five domains comprised (A) Direct Evidence of Gut and Gastric Microbial Alterations in Hyperemesis Gravidarum (n = 7); (B) Pregnancy Microbiome Dynamics, Microbial Metabolites, and Functional Host–Microbiome Interactions (n = 13); (C) Clinical Manifestations, Nutritional Status, and Inflammatory Responses Associated with Hyperemesis Gravidarum (n = 8); (D) Molecular Mechanisms Underlying Hyperemesis Gravidarum: GDF15 Biology, Genetic Susceptibility, and Host–Microbiome Interactions (n = 4); and (E) Microbiome-Targeted Interventions and Translational Evidence (n = 4).

Domain A: Direct Evidence of Gut and Gastric Microbial Alterations in Hyperemesis Gravidarum

This domain synthesizes the strongest available human evidence directly examining gastrointestinal microbial alterations associated with HG. Seven charted sources of evidence comprised four gut-microbiome studies and three studies of Helicobacter pylori exposure.16–22 The methods were not interchangeable: the evidence included stool-based 16S rRNA sequencing, symptom-severity comparisons, observational gastric-exposure studies, and Mendelian randomization using host-genetic instruments.

The stool-based studies reported altered community composition and diversity in HG, but the direction and identity of taxonomic differences were not consistent enough to define a reproducible signature.19,21,22 Koçak et al reported reduced diversity and altered relative abundance, whereas the nested case–control study linked overall microbiome composition to nausea and vomiting severity rather than to a single diagnostic taxon.19,22 Mendelian randomization identified genetically predicted associations involving Coprococcus 2, Ruminococcus, and Turicibacter, but these instrument-based estimates do not demonstrate that measured abundances precede HG in pregnancy.20 Thus, the most defensible synthesis is an association with microbial community disruption, not a validated HG microbiome biomarker.

Among these approaches, the two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis provided the strongest causal-inference design by using host-genetic instruments; however, it did not establish temporal changes in measured pregnancy microbiota.20

Evidence regarding the gastric microbiome was derived primarily from studies evaluating Helicobacter pylori. Across three independent studies, maternal Helicobacter pylori infection was consistently associated with HG, pregnancy-related gastrointestinal disorders, or adverse pregnancy outcomes.16–18 Although these investigations focused on a single gastric microorganism rather than the overall gastric microbiome, they currently represent the strongest evidence regarding gastric microbial involvement in HG.

Overall, the evidence synthesized in this domain indicates that both gut microbial alterations and gastric microbial exposure have been investigated using observational, sequencing-based, genetic, and epidemiological approaches. Collectively, the available evidence supports an association between gastrointestinal microbial alterations and HG, although it remains insufficient to establish a reproducible microbial signature or a definitive causal pathway.16–22

Domain B: Pregnancy Microbiome Dynamics, Microbial Metabolites, and Functional Host–Microbiome Interactions

This domain is retained as a contextual comparator rather than direct evidence of HG pathogenesis. Thirteen eligible sources of evidence described physiological pregnancy-associated microbiome remodeling, microbial metabolites, and host–microbiome interactions.12–15,23,24,29–35

Their role is to define the background against which HG-specific findings should be interpreted and to prevent normal gestational change from being mislabeled as disease-specific dysbiosis.

Studies addressing pregnancy microbiome dynamics consistently reported that normal pregnancy is accompanied by progressive remodeling of maternal microbial communities. Across the eligible longitudinal studies and narrative reviews, gestation was characterized by coordinated alterations in gut microbial composition, diversity, and predicted metabolic function, accompanied by reciprocal interactions with maternal immune, endocrine, and metabolic systems. Collectively, the eligible contemporary evidence identifies physiological microbiome remodeling as an integral component of maternal adaptation to pregnancy.14,15,29,30,34

Evidence on microbial metabolites and functional host–microbiome interactions extended these observations beyond taxonomic profiles alone. Multi-omics and observational studies demonstrated coordinated alterations in microbial metabolic pathways, including bile acid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, short-chain fatty acid production, immune regulation, intestinal barrier integrity, inflammatory signaling, and host metabolic adaptation. Narrative reviews further emphasized that functional microbial activity and metabolite production represent integral components of host–microbiome communication during pregnancy.12,13,23,24,31–33,35

Across uncomplicated pregnancy cohorts, changes in composition, diversity, metabolite production, and host metabolic or immune adaptation varied by population and analytical platform. Functional pathways involving bile acids, short-chain fatty acids, barrier integrity, and immune signaling were more consistent conceptually than any single taxon. Because these studies were not conducted specifically in HG, they support biological plausibility and comparator selection but cannot establish HG-specific causation.

Collectively, the available evidence indicates that physiological pregnancy-associated microbiome remodeling provides the biological context within which disease-associated dysbiosis in hyperemesis gravidarum should be interpreted. These findings further support the view that functional microbial adaptations may modify disease expression without constituting independent primary pathogenic mechanisms.

Domain C: Clinical Manifestations, Nutritional Status, and Inflammatory Responses Associated with Hyperemesis Gravidarum

The evidence synthesized in this domain consistently demonstrates that HG is associated with systemic clinical manifestations extending well beyond severe nausea and/or vomiting. Across diverse study designs, the included evidence described nutritional compromise, inflammatory responses, metabolic disturbances, impaired maternal well-being, and adverse pregnancy-related outcomes among women with HG.1–4,6,7,25,36,37

Nutritional impairment was one of the most consistently reported findings. Prospective cohort studies, observational investigations, and narrative reviews described maternal weight loss, dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, reduced dietary intake, micronutrient deficiencies, and increased nutritional risk among women with HG. Several studies further emphasized the importance of nutritional assessment and supportive nutritional management throughout pregnancy.3,4,6,37

Inflammatory alterations were also frequently reported across the included evidence. Clinical observational studies identified altered inflammatory biomarkers together with metabolic disturbances in women with HG compared with uncomplicated pregnancies. Complementary narrative reviews further summarized interactions between gastrointestinal physiology, inflammatory regulation, and maternal adaptation during pregnancy.2,25,36

In addition to nutritional and inflammatory findings, several studies described broader maternal and healthcare consequences associated with HG. These included impaired health-related quality of life, repeated hospitalization, increased healthcare utilization, and adverse maternal and pregnancy outcomes. Collectively, these observations consistently illustrate the substantial clinical burden associated with severe nausea and vomiting during pregnancy.4,6,7

Overall, the evidence synthesized in this domain consistently demonstrates that HG is characterized by nutritional compromise, inflammatory alterations, metabolic disturbances, and significant maternal morbidity across diverse clinical settings and study designs. Together, these findings summarize the principal clinical manifestations reported in the current literature and complement the microbiome-related evidence summarized in the preceding domains.2–4,6,7,25,36,37

Domain D: Molecular Mechanisms Underlying Hyperemesis Gravidarum: GDF15 Biology, Genetic Susceptibility, and Host–Microbiome Interactions

The four charted sources of evidence in this domain examined molecular mechanisms relevant to hyperemesis gravidarum, including genetic susceptibility, placental signaling pathways, and host–microbiome interactions. The evidence comprised one translational study, one multi-ancestry genome-wide association study (GWAS), and two narrative reviews that synthesized current mechanistic evidence relevant to HG.9,10,38,39

Two complementary studies by Fejzo and colleagues identified growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) as the principal molecular pathway investigated in relation to HG. The translational study integrated human genetic, biochemical, and experimental evidence, whereas the subsequent multi-ancestry genome-wide association study further evaluated genetic susceptibility across diverse populations. Together, these studies consistently identified GDF15 signaling as the predominant molecular pathway underlying susceptibility to severe nausea and vomiting during pregnancy.9,10

The remaining evidence expanded the molecular perspective beyond genetic susceptibility. Narrative reviews summarized current knowledge regarding host–microbiome communication, gut–brain signaling, immune regulation, endocrine pathways, and microbially derived signaling molecules involved in gastrointestinal physiology during pregnancy. These publications synthesized emerging evidence describing interactions between maternal microbial communities and host biological systems relevant to pregnancy.9,10,38,39

Overall, the evidence synthesized in this domain demonstrates that contemporary molecular investigations of HG have increasingly converged on GDF15-centered endocrine signaling while also recognizing broader host–microbiome interactions and gut–brain communication as complementary biological pathways. Collectively, these studies establish the current molecular framework underlying HG while providing the mechanistic basis for interpreting subsequent translational evidence within a GDF15-centered systems biology framework.9,10,38,39

Domain E: Microbiome-Targeted Interventions and Translational Evidence

Four charted sources of evidence evaluated microbiome-targeted strategies relevant to pregnancy: probiotic supplementation with gastrointestinal and quality-of-life outcomes, systematic evidence on probiotic or prebiotic safety, maternal microbiome modulation with neonatal colonization outcomes, and targeted Helicobacter pylori eradication in selected pregnant patients with HG.40–43

The evidence therefore spans distinct interventions and populations rather than a single therapeutic class. Probiotic, prebiotic, and synbiotic approaches provide pregnancy-safety and biological proof-of-concept data, while Helicobacter pylori-directed therapy addresses a defined gastric infection. None of these sources of evidence establishes routine efficacy for unselected HG populations. Reported signals included improved gastrointestinal function and quality of life with probiotic supplementation, generally reassuring pregnancy safety findings for probiotics and prebiotics, measurable effects on maternal or neonatal microbial composition, and preliminary clinical improvement after Helicobacter pylori eradication.40–43 These findings identify testable strategies but remain heterogeneous in intervention, outcome, and relevance to HG.

The current implication is therefore selective and research-focused: test microbiome-targeted interventions only in well-phenotypes subgroups, distinguish infection-directed treatment from general microbiome modulation, and incorporate gestational-age-appropriate maternal and fetal safety outcomes. Routine microbiome-directed treatment for HG is not supported by the present evidence.40–43

Discussion

Principal Findings

This scoping review mapped 36 eligible sources of evidence and synthesized their implications for HG biology and translation. Five domains were examined: direct gastrointestinal microbial alterations; contextual pregnancy microbiome dynamics; clinical and nutritional manifestations; molecular mechanisms; and microbiome-targeted interventions. The central finding is not merely that the literature is small, but that its evidentiary layers differ: GDF15-centered susceptibility is strongly supported, gastrointestinal dysbiosis is repeatedly associated with HG but heterogeneous, and proposed microbe-endocrine interactions remain largely hypothesis-generating.9,10,12–22,29–35,38–42

Direct human microbiome evidence remains limited and methodologically heterogeneous and not directly comparable. Stool studies differed in diagnostic criteria, gestational age at sampling, population, diet and medication control, sample handling, 16S sequencing region, reference database, diversity metrics, and covariate adjustment.19,21,22 Some reported reduced diversity or altered community composition, but specific taxa did not replicate consistently. Mendelian randomization reduced conventional confounding but relied on host-genetic instruments derived from external datasets and did not directly measure the microbiota during pregnancy.20 These conflicting findings support cautious interpretation: community disruption is an emerging association, whereas a reproducible HG-specific taxonomic signature is not established.

By contrast, evidence regarding gastric microbial exposure, particularly Helicobacter pylori, is considerably more consistent. Pregnancy-specific observational studies continue to demonstrate associations between maternal Helicobacter pylori infection and HG, severe nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP), dyspeptic symptoms, and selected adverse pregnancy outcomes.16–18,43 Although these associations should not be interpreted as proof of causality, they suggest that gastric microbial exposure represents one of the most consistently investigated microbial factors associated with HG and remains clinically relevant in appropriately selected patients. Nevertheless, the heterogeneity of diagnostic methods, regional prevalence, and host susceptibility indicates that Helicobacter pylori is unlikely to represent a universal explanation for HG but rather one component of a multifactorial disease process.

The most strongly supported mechanistic advance is GDF15-centered endocrine susceptibility. Translational and multi-ancestry genetic studies identify fetoplacental GDF15 and maternal sensitivity through GFRAL signaling as the leading biological explanation for severe pregnancy-related nausea and vomiting.9,10,38 By contrast, microbiome-mediated modulation of this pathway is an emerging hypothesis, and specific gut–brain, barrier, immune, or metabolite interactions remain speculative until tested directly in longitudinal HG cohorts. This evidence hierarchy allows the microbiome to remain biologically relevant without overstating causality.

Accordingly, the evidence synthesized in this review supports a revised conceptual interpretation of dysbiosis in HG. Instead of representing an isolated primary cause, gut and gastric dysbiosis is more appropriately interpreted as a biological modifier, disease amplifier, downstream consequence, or endotype marker operating within a complex biological network involving endocrine signaling, host genetics, immune regulation, metabolism, nutrition, gastrointestinal physiology, and maternal adaptation to pregnancy.12–15,23,24,29–35,38,39 Pregnancy itself is accompanied by physiological remodeling of the maternal microbiome, while persistent vomiting, reduced nutritional intake, dehydration, altered gastric motility, medication exposure, and inflammatory responses associated with HG may further reshape gastrointestinal microbial communities.3,11–15,25,29–39 Consequently, the relationship between dysbiosis and HG is likely to be bidirectional and may differ across clinically distinct biological subgroups.

Methodological evolution also explains why historical and contemporary claims must be separated. Pre-2021 publications were excluded from the charted evidence map and included-study count. Koren et al (2012) is cited solely as foundational background for normal gestational microbiome remodeling, whereas the formal 2021–2026 search captured recent sequencing, Mendelian randomization, genome-wide association, metabolomic, and multi-omics approaches.10–15,23,24,32,33,38,39 Statements about change over the past decade therefore derive from clearly separated foundational context and contemporary included studies, not from an expansion of the eligibility window.

Collectively, the review answers both parts of its objective. The mapped literature shows where evidence is concentrated, and the synthesis indicates what it currently supports: an established GDF15-centered susceptibility pathway; associated but heterogeneous gut and gastric dysbiosis; plausible nutritional, inflammatory, metabolite, and gut–brain modifiers; and insufficient HG-specific intervention evidence. This hierarchy provides the basis for focused clinical translation and research priorities.

Reinterpreting the Role of Dysbiosis in Hyperemesis Gravidarum in the GDF15 Era

The relationship between gastrointestinal dysbiosis and hyperemesis gravidarum has become increasingly complex as contemporary evidence has shifted the mechanistic focus toward fetoplacental endocrine signaling. Historically, microbial dysbiosis was frequently proposed as a potential primary driver of HG because alterations in gastrointestinal microbial communities have been linked to inflammatory activation, increased intestinal permeability, microbial metabolite production, and gut–brain communication.23,24,29–35,38,39 However, the emergence of robust genetic and translational evidence identifying fetoplacental growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) as the principal biological determinant of maternal susceptibility necessitates a reassessment of these earlier hypotheses.9,10,38

Rather than representing competing explanations, the available evidence suggests that GDF15 signaling and gastrointestinal dysbiosis may operate as complementary biological processes at different levels of HG pathophysiology. Current evidence indicates that GDF15 provides the principal endocrine signal underlying susceptibility to nausea and vomiting, whereas gastrointestinal microbial alterations may influence disease severity, symptom persistence, nutritional consequences, inflammatory responses, and clinical heterogeneity. This interpretation is consistent with the limited but growing body of direct human microbiome evidence, which demonstrates microbial alterations in women with HG but has not established a consistent taxonomic signature or causal microbial pathway.19–22

This distinction is particularly important because pregnancy itself is accompanied by physiological remodeling of the maternal gut microbiome. Longitudinal studies have demonstrated dynamic gestational changes in microbial diversity, taxonomic profile, and microbial metabolic activity even in uncomplicated pregnancies.11–15,29,30,32–35,38,39 Consequently, microbial alterations identified in women with HG cannot automatically be interpreted as disease-specific abnormalities. Instead, these observations should be interpreted within the broader physiological adaptations of pregnancy and the substantial nutritional and metabolic disturbances associated with prolonged vomiting, dehydration, reduced dietary intake, altered gastric emptying, and exposure to antiemetic medications.3,4,25,36,37

The concept of dysbiosis as a biological modifier rather than an isolated cause also provides a more coherent explanation for the substantial clinical heterogeneity observed in HG. Women with apparently similar clinical phenotypes frequently demonstrate marked differences in disease severity, nutritional compromise, recurrence risk, and treatment response.2,4,7,37 This variability is unlikely to be explained by a single pathogenic pathway alone. Instead, it is plausible that GDF15-mediated endocrine susceptibility interacts with maternal genetics, gastrointestinal microbial composition, immune responses, nutritional status, environmental exposures, and host metabolic adaptation to generate multiple biological endotypes across the HG spectrum.9,10,12,15,23,24,38

An endotype-based interpretation also reconciles several observations that previously appeared contradictory. For example, not all women with detectable Helicobacter pylori infection develop HG, while many patients with severe HG demonstrate no evidence of active gastric infection.16–18 Likewise, gut microbiome studies consistently report altered microbial communities, yet the specific taxa associated with HG vary considerably across populations and analytical platforms.19–22 These discrepancies suggest that microbial alterations alone are unlikely to determine disease occurrence. Instead, microbial factors may modify disease expression in biologically susceptible individuals or under specific physiological conditions.

Another important implication of this reinterpretation concerns causality. Most currently available microbiome studies remain observational and therefore cannot determine whether dysbiosis precedes HG or develops secondary to persistent vomiting and nutritional compromise. Although Mendelian randomization provides stronger support for potentially causal associations between specific microbial taxa and HG susceptibility, these analyses infer causality indirectly through host genetic instruments rather than through direct longitudinal observation of microbial dynamics.20 Consequently, reverse causation, residual confounding, and bidirectional host–microbiome interactions remain important considerations when interpreting existing evidence.

Taken together, the available evidence supports a conceptual shift from a microbiome-centric model toward a GDF15-centered systems biology framework of HG. Within this framework, fetoplacental endocrine signaling centered on GDF15 appears to constitute the principal biological driver of disease susceptibility, whereas gastrointestinal dysbiosis is more appropriately interpreted as a dynamic modifier interacting with endocrine, immunological, metabolic, nutritional, and neural pathways to influence disease severity and clinical phenotype. This integrated interpretation accommodates both the consistency of recent molecular discoveries and the growing body of microbiome research without overstating the current level of evidence.

Current evidence does not support viewing gastrointestinal dysbiosis as an independent primary cause of HG; rather, dysbiosis should be interpreted as a biological modifier, disease amplifier, downstream consequence, or endotype marker operating within a GDF15-centered systems biology framework.

Integration of Pregnancy Microbiome, Nutrition, Inflammation, and Clinical Phenotypes

Although the available evidence supports GDF15-centered endocrine signaling as the principal biological determinant of HG susceptibility, the clinical expression of HG is unlikely to be explained by endocrine pathways alone. Pregnancy represents a unique physiological state characterized by coordinated adaptations involving the maternal microbiome, immune regulation, metabolism, gastrointestinal function, and nutritional homeostasis. Consequently, the severity and clinical heterogeneity of HG are likely to reflect dynamic interactions among these interconnected biological systems rather than disruption of a single pathway.3,4,11–15,29,30,32–35,38,39

Longitudinal studies consistently demonstrate that pregnancy is accompanied by progressive remodeling of the maternal gut microbiome, including alterations in microbial diversity, taxonomic profiles, and metabolic activity. These physiological adaptations are thought to facilitate maternal metabolic flexibility and fetal growth while simultaneously influencing immune tolerance and intestinal function.11–15 Importantly, these changes occur even in uncomplicated pregnancies, indicating that microbiome remodeling is a normal component of gestation rather than a pathological process. Therefore, interpretation of microbiome alterations in HG requires careful distinction between physiological pregnancy-associated changes and disease-related dysbiosis.

Within this physiological context, persistent nausea, recurrent vomiting, reduced dietary intake, dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, and progressive maternal weight loss characteristic of HG may further perturb gastrointestinal microbial ecosystems.3,4,25,36,37 Reduced nutritional diversity limits substrate availability for beneficial microbial fermentation, whereas prolonged fasting and altered gastrointestinal transit may influence microbial composition and metabolic output. These nutritional disturbances may also contribute to impaired production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bile acid dysregulation, and altered microbial metabolite signaling, which have increasingly been recognized as important mediators of host–microbiome communication.23,24,44

Inflammation represents another potential interface linking gastrointestinal dysbiosis with clinical manifestations of HG. Several studies included in this review reported altered systemic inflammatory biomarkers and nutritional indices among women with HG, suggesting that systemic inflammatory responses may accompany severe disease.36 Although the directionality of these associations remains uncertain, experimental and translational evidence indicates that microbially derived metabolites, intestinal barrier integrity, and gastrointestinal immune activation can influence inflammatory signaling pathways involved in nausea and emesis.39,45–48 These observations support the concept that inflammatory responses may amplify disease severity in susceptible individuals, even if they do not constitute the primary initiating mechanism.

An additional layer of complexity arises from gut–brain communication. Microbial metabolites, bile acids, vagal signaling, and neuroimmune mediators have been increasingly implicated in bidirectional communication between the gastrointestinal tract and central emetic pathways.23,24,30,44–48 Although much of this evidence originates from experimental models and non-pregnant populations, these mechanisms provide biologically plausible pathways through which gastrointestinal microbial alterations could modify symptom severity or persistence during pregnancy. Nevertheless, direct evidence demonstrating these mechanisms specifically in HG remains limited, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation.

Collectively, these findings support the view that HG should be understood as a multifactorial syndrome whose clinical expression emerges from dynamic interactions among endocrine susceptibility, physiological pregnancy adaptation, gastrointestinal microbial ecology, nutritional status, immune regulation, and neurogastrointestinal signaling. Rather than functioning as isolated biological processes, these components appear to operate within an integrated host–microbiome network in which disruption of one system may influence multiple downstream pathways. This perspective provides a biological explanation for the marked heterogeneity observed in clinical presentation, disease severity, and treatment response among women with HG.

From a research perspective, this integrated framework also highlights the importance of moving beyond single-biomarker investigations. Future investigations should integrate longitudinal microbiome profiling with comprehensive nutritional assessment, inflammatory biomarkers, metabolomics, and endocrine measurements—including GDF15—and host genetic profiling to better characterize biologically distinct HG endotypes. Such multidimensional approaches may facilitate more accurate disease stratification and improve understanding of the complex interactions underlying HG pathophysiology.12,13,32,33,38,39

Collectively, these observations reinforce the proposed GDF15-centered systems biology framework, in which endocrine susceptibility, physiological microbiome remodeling, nutritional compromise, inflammatory responses, and host adaptation interact to generate the diverse clinical phenotypes of HG. This integrated perspective provides the biological rationale for precision medicine approaches that combine endocrine, microbiome, nutritional, metabolic, and inflammatory profiling to improve disease stratification and guide future therapeutic development.

Clinical and Translational Implications

The proposed framework does not currently justify routine microbiome testing or treatment for HG, because direct evidence in HG remains limited and heterogeneous and no reproducible diagnostic signature has been prospectively validated.19–22,49 Its near-term research value lies in improving phenotyping through standardized documentation of Windsor-compatible symptoms, gestational age at symptom onset and diagnosis, nutritional status, medication and acid-suppressive exposure, Helicobacter pylori status when clinically indicated, and gestational age and treatment status at biospecimen collection before microbial results are interpreted.1,2,16–19,22,49 Future studies should determine whether these variables, together with GDF15 and inflammatory or metabolic markers, can support clinically useful decision models; prospective validation is required before implementation.9,10,36

Similarly, among microbiome-targeted approaches, targeted management of Helicobacter pylori infection remains a clinically relevant but selective therapeutic strategy. Observational studies consistently demonstrate an association between Helicobacter pylori infection and HG, while recent clinical studies suggest that eradication therapy may improve maternal outcomes in carefully selected patients.16–18,43 Nevertheless, routine screening or empirical eradication therapy for all women with HG cannot currently be recommended because regional variation in infection prevalence, differences in diagnostic accuracy, antimicrobial resistance, and limited availability of pregnancy-specific intervention trials must be carefully considered.50–53 Clinical management should therefore continue to follow established obstetric and infectious disease guidelines rather than microbiome-directed interventions alone.

Another important clinical and translational implication emerging from this review concerns biomarker development. Current evidence suggests that no single microbial taxon or microbial metabolite has yet demonstrated sufficient diagnostic accuracy to serve as an independent biomarker for HG. Conversely, GDF15 has emerged as the most consistently validated endocrine biomarker associated with susceptibility to HG. Rather than viewing microbiome-based and endocrine biomarker strategies as competing approaches, future biomarker development may benefit from integrating endocrine biomarkers with microbiome profiles, inflammatory markers, nutritional assessments, clinical phenotyping, and biological stratification. Such multidimensional biomarker models are likely to reflect the biological complexity and heterogeneity of HG more accurately than any individual marker alone and may provide a practical foundation for future precision medicine approaches.

Beyond biomarker development, the evidence synthesized in this review also supports a broader precision medicine approach to HG management. Recognition that dysbiosis may function as a biological modifier rather than an independent primary pathogenic mechanism suggests that microbiome-targeted interventions are unlikely to demonstrate uniform effectiveness across all patients. Instead, therapeutic benefit is likely to depend on underlying biological endotypes characterized by differences in endocrine susceptibility, microbial composition, nutritional status, inflammatory responses, and metabolic adaptation. This concept is consistent with broader developments in systems biology and personalized medicine across multiple gastrointestinal and metabolic disorders.23,24,32,33,38,39

Future research should proceed in a staged sequence. First, enroll high-risk participants before or at symptom onset and collect serial stool, blood, dietary, medication, and symptom data alongside GDF15. Second, harmonize HG definitions, sampling, 16S or metagenomic workflows, reference databases, and core outcomes. Third, replicate the taxa signaled by Mendelian randomization (Coprococcus 2, Ruminococcus, and Turicibacter) while prioritizing functional readouts such as bile acids, short-chain fatty acids, barrier markers, and inflammatory mediators. Fourth, test stratified interventions: Helicobacter pylori-directed treatment only in confirmed infection and probiotic, prebiotic, or synbiotic strategies only in adequately powered trials with maternal and fetal safety outcomes.9,10,20,23,24,40–44

Taken together, these findings suggest that the greatest translational value of microbiome research in HG may not lie in identifying a single therapeutic microorganism, but rather in improving biological stratification, understanding disease heterogeneity, and facilitating precision medicine approaches that integrate microbial, endocrine, metabolic, nutritional, and clinical information. As the evidence base continues to mature, microbiome science is likely to complement—rather than replace—existing endocrine and obstetric models of HG pathophysiology.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

This scoping review integrates direct human microbial evidence, pregnancy-context comparators, clinical consequences, GDF15 biology, and intervention studies within one evidence hierarchy. It follows Joanna Briggs Institute guidance and PRISMA-ScR reporting, separates charted from contextual sources, and provides the data-charting form as Supplementary Material.26–28 The synthesis emphasizes implications and uncertainty rather than treating all evidence types as equivalent.

Several limitations constrain interpretation. Direct HG microbiome studies are few and differ in diagnostic criteria, gestational age, population, diet, medication exposure, sample collection, sequencing platform, sequenced region, reference database, bioinformatic pipeline, diversity metric, and covariate adjustment.19–22 These differences likely contribute to conflicting taxonomic findings. Most studies are observational and vulnerable to reverse causation because vomiting, starvation, dehydration, acid suppression, antibiotics, and hospitalization can alter the microbiome after symptom onset. Mendelian randomization provides stronger causal inference but depends on instrument validity and external population comparability.20 Contextual pregnancy and non-HG mechanistic literature cannot establish HG-specific pathways. The 2021–2026 restriction may omit relevant older clinical work; pre-2021 publications were therefore cited only as background and were not counted as included evidence.

The highest-priority next study would be a prospective, multicenter early-pregnancy cohort with pre-symptom or earliest-symptom sampling, Windsor-compatible case definition, repeated microbiome and metabolomic measurement, concurrent assessments of GDF15, diet, medication exposure, nutritional status, and inflammation, and harmonized outcomes. Replication should focus on microbial taxa implicated by Mendelian randomization analyses and functional pathways involving bile acids, short-chain fatty acids, and intestinal barrier integrity. Intervention trials should then be restricted to biologically defined subgroups, including confirmed Helicobacter pylori infection or reproducible functional dysbiosis. These priorities align with the James Lind Alliance agenda on HG causation, prevention and early intervention, clinically useful markers, nutritional requirements, and comparative treatment effectiveness, while refining those broad questions into specific designs for testing microbiome-related hypotheses.54

Conclusion

This review identifies a clear evidence hierarchy. GDF15-centered endocrine susceptibility is the strongest established biological framework for HG; gut and gastric dysbiosis are repeatedly associated with the disease, but the findings remain heterogeneous; and proposed microbial effects on inflammation, metabolism, barrier function, and gut–brain signaling are plausible but not yet confirmed as HG-specific causal mechanisms.

The most promising near-term targets are reproducible functional endotypes rather than a universal HG microbiome. Candidate endotype-defining features may include microbial taxa implicated by Mendelian randomization analyses (Coprococcus 2, Ruminococcus, and Turicibacter), Helicobacter pylori-positive disease, and metabolite pathways involving bile acids and short-chain fatty acids; each should be evaluated alongside GDF15, nutritional status, medication exposure, and gestational age at sampling.20,23,24,43,44

Research should first establish temporality and reproducibility in prospective multicenter cohorts, then test stratified interventions. Helicobacter pylori eradication should be evaluated only in patients with confirmed infection, while probiotic, prebiotic, or synbiotic strategies require HG-specific randomized trials with maternal and fetal safety outcomes. Current evidence does not support routine microbiome testing or treatment for unselected patients with HG.

These priorities directly address James Lind Alliance questions on cause, prevention and early intervention, useful clinical markers, nutrition, and comparative treatment effectiveness.54 By narrowing those stakeholder priorities to early longitudinal sampling, standardized methods, functional microbial readouts, and biologically stratified trials, the field can move from descriptive associations toward clinically meaningful mechanisms and interventions.
