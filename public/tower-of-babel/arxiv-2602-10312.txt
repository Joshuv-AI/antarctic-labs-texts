R2RAG-Flood: A reasoning-reinforced training-free retrieval augmentation generation framework for flood damage nowcasting Publication type: ORIGINAL ARTICLE
Lipai Huang, Kai Yin*, Chia-Fu Liu, Ali Mostafavi

Abstract
R2RAG-Flood is a reasoning-reinforced, training-free retrieval-augmented generation framework for post-storm property damage nowcasting. Building on an existing supervised tabular predictor, R2RAG-Flood constructs a reasoning-centric knowledge base of labeled tabular records, where each sample contains structured predictors, a compact natural language text-mode summary, and a model-generated reasoning trajectory. During prediction, R2RAG-Flood issues context-augmented prompts that retrieve and condition on relevant reasoning trajectories from nearby geospatial neighbors and canonical class prototypes, allowing the large language model (LLM) backbone to emulate and adapt prior reasoning rather than learn new task-specific parameters. Predictions follow a two-stage procedure that first resolves property damage occurrence and then refines severity within a three-level Property Damage Extent (PDE) categorization, with a conditional downgrade step that corrects over-predicted severity. In a case study of Harris County, Texas at the 12-digit Hydrologic Unit Code (HUC12) scale, the supervised tabular baseline trained directly on structured predictors attains 0.714 overall accuracy and 0.859 damage class accuracy (for medium and high PDE classes). When instantiated with seven LLM backbones, R2RAG-Flood attains 0.613–0.668 overall accuracy and 0.757–0.896 damage class accuracy, approaching the supervised baseline and outputs a structured rationale for each prediction. Using a severity-per-cost efficiency metric derived from API pricing and GPU instance costs, lightweight R2RAG-Flood variants achieve much higher efficiency than both the supervised tabular baseline and larger LLM backbones (35.9–81.9 vs 28.6 for the baseline, and 4.0–6.2 for high-capacity LLMs), while requiring no task-specific training or fine-tuning.

Flood hazards impose heavy physical and financial burdens on cities worldwide, with U.S. flood-related losses averaging about 40 billion dollars annually over the past four decades and likely to rise as events become more frequent and intense
(
Yildirim et al. (2022)
;
Slater & Villarini (2016)
;
Musselman et al. (2018)
)
. To support response and recovery, recent flood risk assessment studies have developed models tailored to post-storm damage assessment and nowcasting. At the building scale, vision-based approaches estimate lowest floor elevation from street-view imagery, improving inputs for depth-damage and vulnerability analysis
(
Ho et al. (2024)
;
Ho et al. (2025)
)
. For nowcasting based on claim evidence and geospatial predictors, supervised tabular pipelines such as
FloodDamageCast
achieve strong results within their target regions
(
Liu et al. (2024)
)
. The trade-off is the upstream effort required to assemble task-specific datasets, engineer predictors, train and calibrate separate models for each study area, and address severe class imbalance through specialized augmentation. These requirements make it harder to scale and slower to deploy in new regions.

We introduce
R2RAG-Flood
, a reasoning-reinforced, training-free retrieval-augmented generation framework for post-storm Property Damage Extent (PDE) nowcasting. PDE is an ordinal indicator of post-storm property damage severity derived from claim evidence. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pairs a large language model (LLM) with an external retriever that supplies relevant context at inference time
(
Lewis et al. (2020)
;
Guu et al. (2020)
;
Wei et al. (2022)
)
. Conditioning the LLM on retrieved evidence can improve factuality, interpretability, and transfer across regions
(
Martelo et al. (2024)
;
Xie et al. (2025)
)
. Beyond flood damage, disaster-management work has released retrieval benchmarks for disaster scenarios, such as
DisastIR

(
Yin, Dong, Liu, Huang et al. (2025)
)
. Follow-on efforts have proposed retrievers designed for these settings, exemplified by
DMRetriever

(
Yin, Dong, Liu, Lin et al. (2025)
)
. In place of repeated region-specific retraining,
R2RAG-Flood
relies on retrieval and prompting. It also runs an offline feature-distribution divergence analysis using Jensen–Shannon and Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistics
(
Menéndez et al. (1997)
;
Massey Jr (1951)
)
to construct a divergence-informed feature profile. This profile provides a feature ordering used to generate compact text-mode summaries and feature-level divergence statistics used to guide free-shot selection and post-check rules, without task-specific training or fine-tuning of the LLM backbones.

The numerical distribution of PDE severity is strongly skewed in claim-based flood damage datasets. Claim reporting is incomplete for many affected locations due to practical and administrative factors, while filed claims often correspond to substantial losses
(
Mobley et al. (2021)
)
. This makes direct regression on claim amounts ill-posed and sensitive to reporting noise and extreme values, so we frame PDE nowcasting as an ordinal classification problem. To construct PDE labels, we discretize claim evidence into a small number of ordered severity levels using domain-informed, distribution-based thresholds that reflect hydrologic and built-environment context. This domain-guided discretization yields more stable class boundaries for retrieval-augmented reasoning than KNN-based grouping commonly used in conventional ML pipelines
(
Abdullahi et al. (2024)
)
, especially for rare severe cases. In supervised learning, imbalance is often handled through class reweighting and data augmentation, including conditional tabular synthesis methods such as CTGAN
(
Xu et al. (2019)
;
Patki et al. (2016)
)
. In
R2RAG-Flood
, inference is driven by retrieval and prompting rather than fitting new parametric models, so synthetic augmentation is not a central requirement.

The main flow of
R2RAG-Flood
is illustrated in Figure
1
. For labeled examples in the knowledge base, we convert tabular predictors into text-mode summaries under a fixed variable reference dictionary
(
Parikh et al. (2020)
)
, and the LLM generates DeepSeek-R1 style reasoning trajectories conditioned on these summaries and known PDE levels
(
Guo et al. (2025)
)
. We store the resulting reasoning trajectories to form a reasoning-centric knowledge base that supports retrieval and prompting
(
Yao et al. (2022)
)
. A divergence-informed feature profile guides text-mode ordering, free-shot selection from the reasoning pool, and the downgrade checks used during prediction. During inference,
R2RAG-Flood
conditions on the target text-mode summary, retrieved neighbor reasoning
(
Khandelwal et al. (2019)
)
, and free-shot prototypes and hard-boundary cases selected from the reasoning pool
(
Brown et al. (2020)
)
to output both the predicted PDE level and accompanying prediction reasoning. To keep retrieved evidence hydrologically comparable and geographically relevant, neighbors and free-shots are drawn from 12-digit Hydrologic Unit Code (HUC12) watershed groups
(
Luukkonen et al. (2023)
)
, with a global fallback when local examples are sparse
(
Brown et al. (2020)
)
. We then apply a rule-based downgrade check when hard-gate violations or evidence conflicts indicate that the initial severity decision is not supported
(
Swaminathan et al. (2024)
;
Xin et al. (2021)
)
. For evaluation, we report standard classification metrics and complement them with instance-level reasoning scores (Section
4.3
). We also define a severity-per-cost efficiency metric that combines predictive performance with deployment cost computed from API pricing and GPU instance cost history, enabling direct comparisons between the supervised baseline and multiple LLM backbones within
R2RAG-Flood
.

The novel contributions of this work are threefold:

1

We introduce
R2RAG-Flood
, a reasoning-reinforced, training-free retrieval-augmented generation framework for post-storm PDE nowcasting that builds a reasoning-centric knowledge base and performs context-augmented prediction without task-specific training or fine-tuning of the LLM backbones.

2

We design a reasoning-aware retrieval and post-check pipeline that integrates neighbor reasoning and free-shots selected from a reasoning pool as prototypes and hard-boundary cases within hydrologically and geographically comparable groups. We define a rule-based downgrade mechanism guided by divergence-based feature cues and propose instance-level reasoning metrics to evaluate rationale structure and faithfulness.

3

In a PDE nowcasting use case, we benchmark
R2RAG-Flood
across multiple LLM backbones. The results show accuracy close to the supervised tabular baseline for this setting, improved robustness relative to a text-mode-only variant, and higher severity-per-cost efficiency for lightweight backbones than for the supervised baseline and larger-capacity LLMs.

KNOWLEDGE BASE CONSTRUCTION AND RETRIEVAL FRAMEWORK

R2RAG-Flood
organizes historical labeled examples into a structured knowledge base and uses retrieval-augmented prompting to produce PDE predictions with accompanying reasoning for new targets. This section describes how we build the variable reference dictionary, quantify feature-distribution divergence across PDE levels, construct the reasoning-centric knowledge base, and implement the retrieval and prompting workflow used for prediction.

2.1
Variable Reference Dictionary and Feature Families

All predictors used in this study are compiled into a unified Variable Reference Dictionary to keep feature semantics consistent across knowledge base construction and prediction prompts. The dictionary covers the core predictors that describe built environment, topography, hydrology, and event conditions, together with the PDE label. Each entry records the feature abbreviation as key and its full name, physical unit, and a short description as values. The Variable Reference Dictionary is used as meta-information when converting tabular inputs into text-mode summaries. By explicitly encoding each feature’s meaning and unit, it helps the LLM interpret numerical values consistently.

2.2
Feature Distribution Divergence Analysis

To support both occurrence and severity decisions, we quantify how distinct each predictor distribution is across PDE levels using a feature-distribution divergence analysis. The resulting divergence scores serve two roles in
R2RAG-Flood
. They define a feature ordering used to generate compact text-mode summaries, and they provide a divergence-informed feature profile used to select representative free-shots from the reasoning pool.

For each feature
f_{i}
, we score distributional separation under two comparisons. The first compares the low PDE class against the union of the damage classes (medium and high), targeting the occurrence boundary. The second compares the medium and high PDE classes, targeting the severity boundary. For each comparison, we compute the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) statistic
(
Massey Jr (1951)
)
and the Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence
(
Menéndez et al. (1997)
)
:

D^{(i)}_{\mathrm{KS}}=\sup_{x}\big|F^{(i)}_{a}(x)-F^{(i)}_{b}(x)\big|

(1)

where
F^{(i)}_{a}
and
F^{(i)}_{b}
are the cumulative distributions of feature
f_{i}
for groups
a
and
b
. Let
P^{(i)}_{a}
and
P^{(i)}_{b}
be the corresponding probability densities. The JS divergence is

D^{(i)}_{\mathrm{JS}}=\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P^{(i)}_{a}\|M^{(i)}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P^{(i)}_{b}\|M^{(i)}\right)

(2a)

M^{(i)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(P^{(i)}_{a}+P^{(i)}_{b}\right)

(2b)

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
is the Kullback–Leibler divergence. We then define a divergence score as a weighted combination of JS and KS:

\mathrm{Score}^{(i)}=W_{\mathrm{JS}}\,D^{(i)}_{\mathrm{JS}}+W_{\mathrm{KS}}\,D^{(i)}_{\mathrm{KS}}.

(3)

where
W_{\mathrm{JS}}
and
W_{\mathrm{KS}}
are weights with
0<W_{\mathrm{JS}},W_{\mathrm{KS}}\leq 1
. We compute Eq.
3
for both comparisons and obtain ordered feature lists for occurrence and severity. Because text-mode generation is length-constrained, predictors with higher divergence scores are placed earlier so that the most class-separating evidence appears prominently in each narrative summary. The same divergence-informed profile is later used to select diverse prototypes and near-boundary free-shots from the reasoning pool, and it provides supporting signals when defining downgrade rules in Section
2.6.2
.

2.3
Text-Mode Generation from Tabular Records

Inspired by table-to-text work such as ToTTo
(
Parikh et al. (2020)
)
, we convert JSONized tabular predictors into compact text-mode summaries. The template prompt in Table
1
is applied uniformly to both labeled examples and prediction targets. Each text mode is limited to at most 120 words and only paraphrases feature values. It must not infer risk or predict damage. To enforce consistent structure, the prompt provides an ordered feature list from Section
2.2
and a legend of variable meanings and units from the Variable Reference Dictionary. The ordered list ranks predictors by their distributional separation across PDE levels, so the most discriminative features are described first and lower-ranked features appear later or are omitted under the length limit. This keeps text modes compact while preserving the evidence most relevant to PDE separation.

2.4
LLM Reasoning Trajectories for Labeled Examples

For labeled tabular records, we prompt the LLM to generate reasoning trajectories that explain the observed PDE label given the text-mode summary. We follow the DeepSeek-R1 style
(
Guo et al. (2025)
)
and wrap each trajectory in a
<think>...</think>
block followed by a
<answer>...</answer>
block. The
<answer>
content is constrained to exactly match the known PDE label. These trajectories make the occurrence and severity logic explicit, and they form a reasoning pool that can be retrieved as geographically comparable evidence to guide predictions for new targets. Table
2
guides the reasoning into two stages. It first resolves occurrence (low PDE class versus damage class), then resolves severity (medium PDE class versus high PDE class) when the outcome is nonzero. The prompt emphasizes interpretable cues and requires exactly one conflict sentence beginning with “Despite” to surface a clear conflict between supporting and opposing evidence. This required “Despite” sentence forces the trajectory to acknowledge counter-evidence and explain why the labeled class can still be justified under competing signals, rather than only listing supportive cues. The
<answer>
tag is constrained to match the provided label.

2.5
Free-Shot Construction

Free-shots are selected after reasoning trajectory generation. For each labeled tabular record, we already have the tuple {
record
,
text_mode
,
reasoning
} stored in the knowledge base. We then construct a free-shot library for each HUC12 group and when a HUC12 contains too few labeled examples to support stable selection, we fall back to a global library built from all labeled examples. Selection is guided by the divergence-informed feature
profile
from Section
2.2
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the selected feature set and let
w_{j}\in(0,1)
be the normalized cue weight for feature
j\in\mathcal{F}
. For a record
r
and an ordinal PDE level
\ell\in\{\mathrm{Low},\mathrm{Medium},\mathrm{High}\}
, we compute per-feature standardized distances

z_{r,j}^{(\ell)}=\frac{\lvert x_{r,j}-\mu_{\ell,j}\rvert}{\sigma_{\ell,j}+\epsilon}

(4)

where
x_{r,j}
is the value of feature
j
for record
r
, and
\mu_{\ell,j}
and
\sigma_{\ell,j}
are the mean and standard deviation of feature
j
among records at level
\ell
within the same HUC12 (with
\epsilon>0
for numerical stability). We then define a divergence-weighted z-distance

d_{r}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{F}}w_{j}\,z_{r,j}^{(\ell)}

(5)

Within each HUC12, for each PDE level
\ell
, we select two prototypes as the labeled records with the smallest
d_{r}^{(\ell)}
. Intuitively, these cases are closest to the feature profile typical of the corresponding level under the most discriminative predictors. For transparency, we also retain the per-feature contributions
w_{j}z_{r,j}^{(\ell)}
and report the top contributors in
why_selected
(as shown in Appendix
D
).

To expose difficult decisions, we select near-boundary cases for two ordinal boundaries: the occurrence boundary between low PDE and the damage class, and the severity boundary between medium and high PDE class. For each record
r
, we compute
d_{r}^{(\mathrm{Low})}
,
d_{r}^{(\mathrm{Med})}
, and
d_{r}^{(\mathrm{High})}
using the same definition in Eq.
5
. The occurrence margin is

m_{r}^{\mathrm{occ}}=\Big\lvert d_{r}^{(\mathrm{Low})}-\min\!\big(d_{r}^{(\mathrm{Med})},d_{r}^{(\mathrm{High})}\big)\Big\rvert

(6)

and the severity margin is

m_{r}^{\mathrm{sev}}=\Big\lvert d_{r}^{(\mathrm{Med})}-d_{r}^{(\mathrm{High})}\Big\rvert

(7)

For each boundary, we keep the closest examples from both adjacent PDE levels. We package these cases as free-shots with their paired text-mode summaries and reasoning trajectories, so the prompt can compare the target against representative prototypes and near-boundary contrasts. Each free-shot stores the selected record profile, the weighted distance or boundary margin used for selection, per-feature contribution terms, and the paired text mode and reasoning. This makes the selection auditable and the examples directly reusable during retrieval-augmented prompting.

2.6
Retrieval and Prediction

For each target record,
R2RAG-Flood
converts tabular predictors into a text-mode summary (Section
2.3
) and predicts PDE through a context-augmented prompt. The prompt conditions on labeled neighbors within a 1 km radius and conditional free-shots drawn from the HUC12-level library, with a global fallback when local examples are sparse. Table
3
specifies the strict JSONL output format and requires a reasoning trajectory for every prediction.

2.6.1
Context-Conditioned Prompting with Neighbors and Free-Shots

The prompt centers on the target text-mode summary and conditionally adds neighbors and free-shots. Neighbors are labeled records whose centroids fall within 1 km of the target. Each neighbor is provided with its PDE label, distance (km), rank, text mode, and reasoning, with smaller distance and lower rank treated as more influential
(
Khandelwal et al. (2019)
;
Borgeaud et al. (2022)
)
. When fewer than three neighbors are available, the prompt is supplemented with free-shots selected from the reasoning pool. Prototypes represent typical evidence patterns for each PDE level, and hard-boundary cases represent near-boundary contrasts for the occurrence and severity decisions. We apply the following injection rules:

•

with three valid neighbors, do not inject free-shots;

•

with two neighbors, add one prototype per PDE level and one hard-boundary case aligned with the nearest neighbor’s level;

•

with one neighbor, add one prototype per PDE level and two hard-boundary cases, emphasizing severity contrast when the neighbor is high damage and occurrence contrast otherwise;

•

with no neighbors, add two prototypes per PDE level and two hard-boundary cases to cover both occurrence and severity contrasts.

The LLM outputs one PDE label in
<answer>
with supporting reasoning in
<think>
.

2.6.2
Conditional Downgrade Mechanism

To reduce over-predicted upper-severity assignments,
R2RAG-Flood
applies a rule-based downgrade step on the ordered label space
\{0,1,2\}
for the three PDE levels, without introducing an abstain class
(
Xin et al. (2021)
;
Swaminathan et al. (2024)
)
. The downgrade logic is defined as a fixed template and cue sets, and is executed during prediction via prompt instructions together with a simple post-check of the model output. Specifically, after the LLM generates an initially predicted label in
<answer>
, we re-read the corresponding
<think>
and apply the
DOWNGRADE_RULE
in Listing
1

,

(Appendix

The output label is either kept unchanged or reduced by one level when the trajectory indicates insufficient support for the initial severity decision. This post-check is most useful for backbones that tend to assign overly high severity under weak evidence.

CASE STUDY: HARRIS COUNTY AND BASELINE MODEL

3.1
Study Area and Data Aggregation

The case study focuses on Harris County, Texas, during Hurricane Harvey (August 25 to September 3, 2017). The study area and resulting Property Damage Extent (PDE) distribution are shown in Figure
2
. To quantify residential damage severity, we use flood claims as a proxy by combining National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) claims with FEMA Individual Assistance (IA) records, thereby capturing both insured and uninsured impacts. Building-level signals and contextual predictors are aggregated to a uniform 500 m
\times
500 m grid. Each grid cell is also assigned to a Huc12 watershed, which supports hydrologically consistent grouping used later in retrieval.

Figure 2
:
Study area and Harris County PDE category distribution.

3.2
PDE Labeling

We derive PDE labels from claim evidence and treat PDE nowcasting as an ordinal classification task. Let the normalized PDE amount follow the same construction as in
Liu et al. (2024)
. We map the normalized values into three ordered levels:

Low

=0
,
Medium

=(0,1]
, and
High

>1
. Under this scheme, the resulting class distribution is 44.7% for low, 40.6% for medium, and 14.6% for high damage. The discretization yields clearer separation between low, medium, and high damage regimes, and it provides a consistent target definition for both the supervised baseline and
R2RAG-Flood
. The spatial pattern of the resulting PDE levels is broadly consistent with hydrologic exposure and urbanization. High-PDE cells cluster along major channels and within dense, developed corridors, while low-PDE cells are more prevalent in peripheral and less developed areas (Figure
2
). This supports the use of the three-level labeling as a physically interpretable target for the Harris County use case.

3.3
Predictors and Data Sources

A total of 14 predictors are compiled from multiple open-source datasets to describe the built environment, topography, hydrology, and event conditions in Harris County (Table
4
). Relative to
FloodDamageCast
, we retain the same physical interpretation but adapt the representation of building density to the 500 m grid resolution. Specifically, we replace raw building area with the Floor Area Ratio (FAR), computed by converting gross floor area from square feet to square meters and normalizing by grid cell area
(
Joshi & Kono (2009)
)
. FAR is the total building floor area within a cell divided by the cell land area. Higher FAR values indicate denser development, and FAR greater than 1.0 reflects multistory or vertically concentrated urban form. These predictors serve two roles. First, they are the structured inputs to the supervised baseline in Section
3.4
. Second, they define the inputs for
R2RAG-Flood
text-mode generation and reasoning prompts, together with the variable reference dictionary and the divergence-based feature analysis described in Section
2.2
.

4.2
Prediction Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate predictive performance for the supervised baseline and all
R2RAG-Flood
variants using overall accuracy, macro-F1, damage-class accuracy, an ordinal severity score, and recall for the high-damage class (class 2). Overall accuracy and macro-F1 are computed over the three-class label space
\{0,1,2\}
. Damage-class accuracy focuses on the damage class subset by restricting evaluation to samples with
y_{i}\in\{1,2\}
. To reflect ordinal distance between labels, we define a severity score on the
\{0,1,2\}
scale. Let
y_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
and
\hat{y}_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
denote the true and predicted PDE level for sample
i
. The per-sample score is

s_{i}^{\mathrm{sev}}=1-\frac{\lvert y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}\rvert}{2},

(8)

which equals
1.0
for an exact match,
0.5
when off by one level, and
0.0
when off by two levels. The dataset-level severity score is

\mathrm{SeverityScore}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}}s_{i}^{\mathrm{sev}},

(9)

where
N=|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}|
. This is a linear transform of the mean absolute error on ordinal labels, a common criterion for ordinal classification
(
Cardoso & Sousa (2011)
)
. We also report
\mathrm{Recall}_{2}
for class 2 to quantify how often severe-damage cells are correctly identified, which is important for prioritizing response and recovery.

4.3
Reasoning Evaluation Metrics

We evaluate the
<think>
rationale with five instance-level metrics. Let
\hat{y}_{i}\in\{0,1,2\}
be the predicted PDE level for sample
i
, and let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}
denote the test set with
N=|\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}|
.

Label–reasoning alignment (LRA)

LRA tests whether the rationale implies the same label as the final prediction. Let
\phi_{\mathrm{lab}}(r_{i})\in\{0,1,2\}
be the label inferred from
r_{i}
by a lightweight label-inference procedure. The per-sample score is

\ell_{i}^{\mathrm{LRA}}=\mathbf{1}\bigl\{\phi_{\mathrm{lab}}(r_{i})=\hat{y}_{i}\bigr\},

(10)

and the dataset-level score is

\mathrm{LRA}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}}\ell_{i}^{\mathrm{LRA}}.

(11)

Salient feature coverage (SFC)

SFC measures whether the rationale mentions the predictors highlighted by the divergence-based feature analysis. Let
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sal}}
be the salient feature set derived from the ordered feature list and the occurrence/severity subsets, and let
\mathcal{E}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sal}}
be the salient features explicitly mentioned in
r_{i}
. The per-sample score is

\mathrm{SFC}_{i}=\frac{|\mathcal{E}_{i}|}{|\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{sal}}|},

(12)

with dataset average

\mathrm{SFC}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}}\mathrm{SFC}_{i}.

(13)

Feature direction consistency (FDC)

FDC checks whether directional statements in the rationale agree with feature values and domain priors. For sample
i
, let
\mathcal{U}_{i}
be the set of features described in
r_{i}
with an implied magnitude category or risk direction, and let
\mathcal{C}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{i}
be those that are consistent with the underlying numeric values and priors. We define

\mathrm{FDC}_{i}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{i}|}{|\mathcal{U}_{i}|}&\text{if }|\mathcal{U}_{i}|>0\\
1&\text{if }|\mathcal{U}_{i}|=0\end{cases}

(14)

and

\mathrm{FDC}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}}\mathrm{FDC}_{i}.

(15)

Prototype alignment score (PAS)

PAS quantifies whether the rationale follows prototype-style patterns. For each class
c
, prototype construction (Section
2.5
) provides prototypes with feature-level contribution terms. Let
\mathcal{S}_{c,k}
be the top-
K
features of prototype
k
in class
c
. For a test sample with
\hat{y}_{i}=c
, let
k^{\star}(i)
be the nearest prototype in feature space and define

\mathcal{P}_{i}=\mathcal{S}_{c,k^{\star}(i)}\cap\mathcal{E}_{i}.

(16)

Then

\mathrm{PAS}_{i}=\frac{|\mathcal{P}_{i}|}{|\mathcal{S}_{c,k^{\star}(i)}|},

(17)

and

\mathrm{PAS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}}\mathrm{PAS}_{i}.

(18)

Boundary tradeoff score (BTS)

BTS evaluates whether rationales for near-boundary samples articulate a tradeoff between risk-increasing and protective factors. Using the occurrence and severity margins defined in Section
2.5
, we form a boundary subset
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{test}}
by thresholding these margins. For each
i\in\mathcal{B}
, let
\phi_{\mathrm{bnd}}(r_{i})\in\{0,1\}
indicate whether
r_{i}
mentions at least one risk-increasing cue and at least one protective cue and uses explicit tradeoff language to justify
\hat{y}_{i}
. We define

\mathrm{BTS}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\phi_{\mathrm{bnd}}(r_{i}).

(19)

These metrics quantify whether each rationale supports the predicted label, mentions salient predictors, follows directional priors, matches prototype patterns, and describes tradeoffs near decision boundaries.

4.4
Ablation Configurations

We conduct an ablation study to isolate the effects of retrieved context and the downgrade check in
R2RAG-Flood
. All configurations use the same test set and identical decoding settings, and differ only in the context fields injected into the prediction prompt.

(I)

Text-mode only.
The prompt includes only the target text mode summaries. The LLM outputs a PDE label and its reasoning trajectory.

(II)

I + Neighbor reasoning.
We add up to three labeled neighbors within 1 km, each with its PDE label, text mode summaries, and reasoning trajectory.

(III)

II + Free-shots.
If fewer than three neighbors are available, we inject free-shots (prototypes and hard-boundary cases) from the HUC12-level library, with a global fallback when local examples are sparse (Section
2.5
).

(IV)

III + Downgrade.
We enable the rule-based downgrade check (Listing
1
) and apply it to the predicted output.

5.4
Ablation Study

Macro-F1 increases as additional context is introduced, and the pattern holds across backbones (Figure
3
). Using only the target text mode yields the lowest macro-F1. Adding labeled neighbor reasoning within 1 km improves macro-F1 for every backbone, indicating that geographically proximate analogs and their rationales provide useful local evidence beyond the target description alone. Injecting free-shots when fewer than three neighbors are available produces neutral or positive changes across backbones. Gains are clearer for models with higher prototype alignment and boundary tradeoff scores, consistent with more systematic use of prototypes and near-boundary contrasts when local neighbor evidence is limited. Enabling the downgrade check produces a modest effect on macro-F1 that is typically neutral or slightly positive. This behavior is consistent with the post-check correcting a subset of over-severe assignments without introducing many new errors. The ablation trends support the full
R2RAG-Flood
design: neighbor retrieval provides the primary accuracy lift, free-shots stabilize sparse-neighborhood cases, and the downgrade check adds a lightweight calibration step on top of the prompted decision.

6
Conclusions

R2RAG-Flood
is a training-free retrieval-augmented framework for post-storm Property Damage Extent (PDE) nowcasting from tabular geospatial predictors. We evaluate it in a Harris County Hurricane Harvey use case and treat
FloodDamageCast*
as a supervised reference on the same split and PDE definition. The workflow organizes labeled records into a reasoning-centric knowledge base, where each sample stores structured predictors, a compact text-mode summary, and a reasoning trajectory. A feature-distribution divergence analysis produces a divergence profile, including a feature order list and feature-level separation statistics at both the occurrence and severity boundaries. This profile supports text-mode generation under length constraints, HUC12-level free-shot libraries (prototypes and hard-boundary cases) with a global fallback, and a fixed downgrade-rule template with cue sets used during prediction. In the results, adding geographically local neighbor reasoning and conditional free-shots improves over a text-mode-only prompt, while the downgrade check mainly acts as a stabilizer by correcting a subset of over-severe assignments when the rationale does not support the proposed class. Reasoning metrics track whether rationales reference salient predictors and articulate tradeoffs near ordinal boundaries.

In the Harris County evaluation, seven LLM backbones run the same retrieval and prompting pipeline without any task-specific fine-tuning. Performance stays within a narrow range across backbones and remains close to the supervised reference in overall accuracy and ordinal severity score. We summarize cost efficiency as the severity score per unit cost. The accounting reflects EC2 training and inference for
FloodDamageCast*
, token-based API charges for GPT backbones, measured GPU time for locally deployed open-source models under quantized inference, and an amortized fixed expense for API subscription. Under this efficiency metric, smaller backbones can be attractive when deployment cost is the primary constraint, while larger backbones remain reasonable when higher severity score or high-damage recall is the priority.

Limitations are tied to the scope and the level of automation in the current study. We evaluate a single Harris County Hurricane Harvey use case, so the reported trends may not generalize to other regions, storm types, or claim-reporting regimes. We also consider a fixed set of LLM backbones under fixed decoding settings, while model behavior can vary with temperature and other sampling controls. Reasoning quality is assessed through automated scores that apply uniform rules across the test set, which may miss domain-specific nuances. The cost comparison is based on evaluation-time accounting and serves as a relative efficiency benchmark, this is not a full life-cycle cost analysis under a specified operational workload. Future work will expand evaluation across additional events and regions and incorporate complementary evidence sources such as remote sensing imagery and text damage reports. We will also test multiple decoding settings, including several temperature levels per backbone, to characterize robustness and cost–performance tradeoffs. A practical next step is to introduce domain experts through structured surveys that elicit how they would summarize tabular inputs into text-mode descriptions and how they would articulate reasoning trajectories, enabling expert-derived guidance to replace or augment prompt-written templates. Prototype and hard-boundary example selection will likewise shift toward expert-informed criteria, so that free-shot libraries and downgrade cues better reflect regional context and evolving hazard conditions.
