AI-based diagnostic evaluation of GPT-4o for crown-fracture detection on maxillary periapical radiographs: effects of prompt detail and customization
Authors: Enes Mustafa Aşar, Büşra Muslu Dinç, Zeynep Şeyda Yavşan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/aimArtificial intelligence (AI) and large language models (LLMs) are rapidly entering dental imaging workflows. We conducted a diagnostic evaluation of GPT-4o for crown-fracture detection on periapical radiographs and examined how prompt detail and customization (prompt-based; no fine-tuning) affect performance in a positives-only dataset.MethodsIn this single-center, retrospective study, 90 different anonymized maxillary periapical radiographs with at least one crown fracture were evaluated by standard GPT-4o (GPT-4o) and customized GPT-4o (CGPT-4o). Both variants were accessed via a commercial interface (no API parameter control). Customization was achieved via a custom GPT with task instructions and in-context examples; no model parameter fine-tuning was performed. Two different prompts were used: a detailed prompt (DP) and a short prompt (SP). The performance of four different test groups (GPT-4o + DP, GPT-4o + SP, CGPT-4o + DP, CGPT-4o + SP) in detecting crown fractures from periapical radiographs was evaluated. Each group evaluated 90 radiographs in 5 independent runs, yielding a total of 1800 responses. Outputs were scored on an ordinal rubric (0 = incorrect, 1 = partially correct, 2 = correct) by three pediatric dentists. The reference standard was the blinded, independent assessment of these experts with consensus. A proportional-odds mixed model assessed the main and interaction effects of Model and Prompt on the odds of higher ordinal correctness, with random intercepts for radiograph (and runs) and adjustment for fracture grade (G1–G3).ResultsThe analysis revealed that both the main and interaction effects of models and prompts were statistically significant. Specifically, CGPT-4o generated higher odds of ordinal correctness than GPT-4o, and detailed prompts were associated with higher odds of ordinal correctness compared to short prompts. There was a significant Model×Prompt interaction, indicating that correctness depended on the specific model–prompt pairing. Among the four combinations, GPT-4o, with short prompts, exhibited the lowest odds of correctness, whereas no statistically significant differences were observed among the remaining three combinations.ConclusionsThe crown fracture detection performance of GPT-4o was significantly affected by prompt design and customization. Especially for short prompts, customization improved detection performance considerably, and using detailed prompts with the standard GPT-4o improved ordinal correctness. The findings demonstrate the critical importance of task-oriented configuration and prompt engineering in the clinical application of AI-based language models in dental traumatology. The dataset comprised only positive cases from a single center and was limited to the maxillary anterior region. Accordingly, we used an ordinal (0–1–2) localization outcome; specificity and ROC–AUC could not be estimated, and external validity (generalizability) is limited.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08528-8.

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies are developing rapidly and beginning to play a role in our lives. In the field of dentistry, they have improved the accuracy and precision of diagnoses, the effectiveness of clinical decisions, and oral care systems [1]. In recent years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have become widespread in healthcare. They can offer various conveniences in diagnosis and decision-making processes [2]. LLMs that utilize text data have revolutionized the field of Natural Language Processing (NLP). OpenAI’s Generative Pre-Trained Transformer (GPT) series, particularly pioneering in this field, has advanced the trajectory and capabilities of NLP [3].

As OpenAI releases its latest models —language models with image interpretation capabilities, such as GPT-4 V(ision) and GPT-4o —widespread adoption has begun. This situation has paved the way for visual data to be evaluated using LLMs, creating opportunities for radiographic assessment [4]. LLMs that acquire the ability to read visual data create opportunities in areas such as image analysis and ease of diagnosis, both of which are fundamental to radiology [5]. Furthermore, the ChatGPT Plus version offers text-based customization without fine-tuning GPT language models. This speciality enables the development of more optimized language models for various clinical data evaluation tasks.

Radiographic evaluation is of vital importance for diagnosis, treatment planning, and monitoring dental health. It is one of the indispensable diagnostic tools for diagnosing dental caries and periodontal diseases, evaluating fractures caused by trauma, detecting various anomalies, guiding endodontic and implant treatments, and monitoring the results of applied treatments [6]. Traumatic dental injuries (TDIs) affecting permanent teeth are common in children and adolescents. Crown fractures and luxation injuries are the most frequently encountered traumas [7]. In these trauma cases, the most appropriate clinical outcomes are sought through accurate diagnosis, appropriate treatment planning, and regular follow-up. Radiographic evaluations before and after trauma are indispensable methods in diagnosis and treatment planning [8].

As the ChatGPT language model has been continuously developed and expanded through reinforcement learning, NLP, and advanced machine learning methodologies, clinicians and the public have begun to turn to these resources for dental trauma [9]. In existing literature, there are studies on the performance of AI-based chatbots in answering dentistry- and TDI-related questions [9–12]. However, to date, there have been limited studies examining the use of image-based inputs in this context [13].

Moreover, multimodal LLMs may hallucinate image–text assertions and are sensitive to prompt phrasing and contextual cues, which can threaten reproducibility and clinical reliability [14–16]. Recent studies have begun to explore the capabilities of multimodal LLMs in dental radiographic interpretation. GPT-4 V and GPT-4o have been tested for detecting supernumerary teeth [14], diagnosing oral and maxillofacial pathologies [15], analyzing hand–wrist radiographs [16], and classifying periodontal diseases [17]. In parallel, prompt engineering — the systematic design of input queries to optimize model outputs — has emerged as a key factor influencing LLM performance in medical imaging tasks [18–20]. However, no study has systematically isolated the effects of prompt detail and prompt-based customization on crown-fracture detection in dental traumatology.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in dental traumatology to evaluate crown-fracture detection using periapical radiographs interpreted by both standard and customized vision-enabled GPT-4o, while explicitly testing the influence of prompt design on performance.

We conducted a diagnostic evaluation of GPT-4o and its customized variant for detecting crown fractures on maxillary periapical radiographs and examined how prompt detail and customization (prompt-based; no fine-tuning) affect model outputs in a positive-only dataset. We prespecified three hypotheses: (H1) Customized GPT-4o (CGPT-4o) yields higher odds of ordinal correctness (0 = incorrect, 1 = partially correct, 2 = correct) than standard GPT-4o; (H2) a detailed prompt yields higher odds of ordinal correctness than a short prompt; and (H3) there is a Model×Prompt interaction such that correctness depends on the specific pairing. Accordingly, the study assessed a single prespecified outcome—ordinal correctness (0/1/2); no secondary outcomes were planned. We chose this ordinal outcome a priori because partially correct responses (e.g., fracture perceived but wrong tooth and/or incomplete localization) are common in a positive-only setting, and a binary definition would discard clinically relevant information.

Material and method

This research was conducted with the approval of the Selcuk University Faculty of Dentistry Non-Invasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee in Turkey (Decision No:2025/36). The Committee approved the use of fully de-identified archival radiographs without individual patient consent. Therefore, radiographs obtained directly from the faculty archive without patient consent were anonymized and used.

The performance of the GPT-4o language model, which can evaluate visual inputs, in detecting crown fractures in dental maxillary periapical radiographs was investigated. Furthermore, the extent to which the responses of the text-based LLMs to the posed questions are affected by the question format was analyzed. In addition, the effect of customizing the GPT-4o model for the clinical task in question on detection success was also studied.

Study design

This was a single-center, retrospective diagnostic evaluation of GPT-4o (standard and customized) for crown-fracture detection on maxillary periapical radiographs. This diagnostic accuracy study was prepared in accordance with STARD-AI (Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies involving Artificial Intelligence). A completed STARD-AI checklist is provided as Supplementary File. The study used two models and two prompt types. Test groups and experimental design

Using G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf) and targeting 1–β = 0.95 at α = 0.05 (two-sided) to detect a small-to-moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.25), the minimum total sample size was N = 341, corresponding to ≈ 85 radiographs per group. We analyzed 90 radiographs per group (N = 360), yielding an achieved power of 96.1%.

LLM index test (GPT-4o / CGPT-4o), operation and scoring

The index test was the GPT-4o–based LLM applied to each periapical radiograph under four conditions (Model: standard GPT-4o vs. CGPT-4o; Prompt: short [SP] vs. detailed [DP]). To standardize input, a single operator uploaded a radiograph image and entered prompts; for each radiograph, the memory feature was disabled, and a new chat session was initiated. Each group processed the same 90 maxillary periapical radiographs across five independent runs (total 1,800 LLM outputs).

For every radiograph image, the model produced (i) the presence of a crown fracture and (ii) tooth-level localization, i.e., the specific numbered tooth/teeth judged to be fractured. “Localization” denotes correct tooth identity, not pixel-wise marking. Because the LLM occasionally generated extra narrative despite the prompts, free-text answers were parsed and normalized using only the information needed for scoring. Tooth identity taken as Fédération dentaire internationale (FDI) numbers; if other notations appeared, they were mapped to FDI (Universal, Palmer, or verbal descriptors such as “left central incisor” → 21).

LLM outputs were evaluated by three independent raters using a pre-specified ordinal scoring rubric (0–2). Prior to the main evaluation, the raters underwent a calibration session to ensure consistent interpretation of the scoring criteria. All discrepancies during the evaluation were resolved by consensus. No formal pre-consensus inter-rater reliability analysis was performed for this scoring stage [14]:

Prior to the main scoring phase, the three raters underwent a calibration session in which the ordinal rubric was reviewed and practiced on a pilot set of LLM outputs not included in the final analysis. Three pediatric dentists (E.M.A., B.M.D., Z.Ş.Y.; >5 years’ experience) independently assigned 0, 1, or 2 scores according to the rubric above; discrepancies were resolved by consensus in a separate session, blinded to model identity and prompt condition.

Reference standard and radiographic fracture grading (G1–G3)

Two independent pediatric dentists (≥ 5 years’ experience) independently labeled, per radiograph, the FDI tooth/teeth with a crown fracture and the fracture grade (G1–G3) using a standardized data sheet, viewing de-identified images on a calibrated display; no clinical data were used. Across cases, crown fractures appeared as a loss of coronal tooth structure with the fractured fragment detached; no image showed an attached fragment. In five radiographs that depicted two fractured maxillary anterior teeth with discordant grades, a radiograph-level summary grade was defined using a highest-grade rule (i.e., the highest grade present within that radiograph). Inter-rater agreement for fracture detection and grading was quantified using a two-way mixed-effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.925, 95% CI 0.851–0.964), based on the independent evaluations of the two pediatric dentists prior to consensus, indicating excellent reliability (Table 1). Consequently, the analytic dataset reflects, for each radiograph, the distribution of maximum fracture severity across the series. Both raters were fully blinded to all LLM outputs and to model/prompt assignment; labeling was completed before any LLM runs.

Customized GPT-4o creation

A CGPT-4o model was developed using the GPTs interface accessible through the GPT Plus subscription. A new GPT was created by clicking the ‘Create’ option in the GPTs tab. On the opened configuration page, the model name was set to ‘Crown Fractures Detection Model’. In the Description section, the statement ‘Dental assistant identifying crown fractures using FDI numbering with high sensitivity.’ was entered, and in the Instructions section, descriptions of the radiographs to be sent to the model and information on the identification of crown fractures were added with examples. GPT-4o was selected as the proposed model.

Ten different maxillary periapical radiographs containing at least one crown fracture were opened and labelled using the Microsoft Designer application (web version, accessed via Windows 11). The borders of all teeth in each radiograph were drawn with a pencil tool. The right and left sides of the images were indicated by text, the right side was labelled yellow, and the left side was labeled blue with a pencil. The tooth numbers and names according to the FDI were written on the central and lateral teeth in the same colours, corresponding to their positions. In teeth with crown fractures, fracture lines and missing crown areas were marked with a red pen, and the phrase “crown fracture” was also written in red text in these areas. To maintain visual integrity, all pencil drawings were set to 90% transparency, and text was set to 50% transparency (Fig. 5). Two versions (labelled and unlabelled) were created for each maxillary periapical radiograph, yielding a total of 20 images. These images were presented to the model in labelled and unlabelled pairs, with one radiograph per chat session. Thus, only one pair of radiographs was used in each interaction; with this configuration, it was aimed that the model could learn the patterns in the labelled image and make inferences on the unlabelled version. Along with the images, all labelling details, such as colour descriptions, tooth names, and numbers, have been added as text (Fig. 5). The model was instructed to learn from these specimens and to detect fractures on subsequent unlabelled periapical radiographs based on the visual patterns observed in the labelled specimens. In this process, both labelled and unlabelled versions of the same radiograph were presented to the model in each chat session, and training was performed with a total of 20 visual data with one or two crown fractures. During customization of the GPT-4o model, it was not possible to load more than 20 images due to platform limitations. Radiographs employed for the customization process were entirely distinct from those used in the primary test dataset. To ensure unbiased evaluation, a separate set of periapical images featuring crown fractures was used exclusively for model training. We provided 20 exemplary pairs (labeled/unlabeled) only as prompt content to illustrate fracture patterns and FDI numbering. These examples were separate from the test set and served solely for in-context guidance; the model’s weights were not modified (i.e., no training/fine-tuning occurred).

Standardization of radiographic data and prompt preparation

All periapical radiographs used were fully anonymized, with no patient information or embedded metadata. Images were obtained from the institution’s secure PACS (Picture Archiving and Communication System) archive and used for research purposes only. To ensure confidentiality and impartiality in all interactions with the GPT-4o and CGPT-4o models, the ‘memory’ function of the models was disabled, and a new chat session was started for each image in both language models. Thus, data confidentiality was maintained, and previous data did not influence the models.

All maxillary periapical radiographs were acquired on a single intraoral x-ray unit (Siray Plus; Zhuhai Siger, China) using a standardized pediatric protocol. The tube potential was kept at ~ 60–70 kVp, and the tube current at ~ 4–7 mA (unit-fixed). At the same time, the anterior region and patient dose were adjusted to achieve diagnostic quality at the lowest reasonable dose (60 kV, 4 mA, 20 s/ms). A phosphor storage plate (PSP) receptor system (Soredex scanner) with the exact plate sizes (31 × 41 mm, Soredex plate size 2) was used for all acquisitions. Images were exported from PACS using the default profile (JPEG, 470 × 620 px, 150 dpi; default brightness/contrast), with no window/level, brightness/contrast, or filtering applied. During evaluation, all images were presented on the same calibrated display under consistent ambient lighting; display adjustments were not permitted.

Each periapical radiograph was uploaded to the chat interface as an external file and evaluated with two different standardized prompts. These two different query structures were considered within the scope of prompt engineering. The aim is to assess the impact of the prompt format on the model’s decision-making. All evaluations were performed across separate chat sessions to ensure the model did not retain its previous responses, allowing each radiograph-prompt combination to be analyzed independently and unbiasedly. This standardized structure enabled a reliable comparison of the impact of variables, such as model customization and prompt type, on diagnostic sensitivity.

Statistical analysis

An Ordinal General Mixed Model was employed to investigate the effects of the model, the prompt, and their interaction on response sensitivity. Initially, the analysis assessed the statistical significance of potential differences between the two models and the two prompts. Subsequently, the significance of differences among the four combinations arising from the model–prompt interaction was examined. Crown fracture grade was additionally included as a covariate in order to control for its potential influence on response sensitivity. The analysis was performed using the proportional odds logistic regression approach. The model achieved convergence appropriately, and Wald statistics were employed to evaluate the fixed effects. Inter-rater agreement for fracture detection/grading was summarized using a two-way mixed-effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) on all 90 images.

Results

The performance of standard and customized versions of GPT-4o, and of short and detailed prompt types, in detecting crown fractures in maxillary incisors was evaluated. The models’ responses were scored by sensitivity level, and the scores for each combination were analyzed using statistical tests.

To illustrate the qualitative impact of prompt design and model customization on a single case, Fig. 6 presents the outputs from all four model–prompt combinations for the same periapical radiograph. In this representative example, the standard model with a short prompt failed to identify the fracture (Score 0), whereas a detailed prompt enabled partial detection (Score 1); both customized model conditions achieved correct detection and localization (Score 2).

Under short prompts, GPT-4o produced a much larger share of incorrect responses (29.8%) and fewer correct responses (22.4%) than CGPT-4o (2.7% incorrect; 36.4% correct). With detailed prompts, the two models were more similar: GPT-4o yielded 0.7% incorrect, 72.2% partially correct, and 27.1% correct, whereas CGPT-4o yielded 8.9% incorrect, 56.7% partially correct, and 34.4% correct. Overall, detailed prompting sharply reduced GPT-4o’s error rate (− 29.1% points) and shifted outputs from “incorrect” to “partially/correct,” while CGPT-4o showed only minor changes (Fig. 7).

Fracture-grade distribution

Across the 90 maxillary anterior periapical radiographs, 24 images contained two fractured incisors. In 5 of these double-fracture images, the two teeth had discordant grades; per the pre-specified rule, we assigned a radiograph-level grade equal to the maximum of the two. Only one tooth-level G1 fracture was observed, and it co-occurred with a G2 fracture in a double-fracture image; after applying the maximum-grade rule, this image contributed to G2 at the radiograph level. Consequently, radiograph-level grade frequencies were G3 = 31 (34.4%) and G2 = 59 (65.6%), with no G1 cases at the radiograph level.

Effects of models and prompts on response sensitivity (main and interactions)

The effect of the model on response sensitivity was found to be significant (χ²=66.399, p < 0.001). Responses generated by CGPT-4o were more likely to be accurate compared to those generated by GPT-4o (OR = 2.296, 95%CI [1.880–2.804], p < 0.001). Prompt type also had a significant effect on response sensitivity (χ²=28.801, p < 0.001). Responses obtained with detailed prompts were more likely to be correct than those obtained with short prompts (OR = 1.667, 95%CI [1.369–2.030], p < 0.001). The interaction between model and prompt was also significant in predicting response sensitivity (χ²=59,816, p < 0.001). Unlike the positive main effects, the interaction effect between model and prompt was negative (OR = 0.207, 95%CI [0.139–0.309], p < 0.001). Crown fracture grade, when included as a covariate, did not exert a significant effect on response sensitivity (OR = 1.078, 95%CI [0.857–1.355], p > 0.05), which further underscores that the main findings were robust and not confounded by fracture severity. When all four model-command combinations were evaluated together, GPT-4o with short commands produced significantly fewer correct responses than the other combinations. In comparison, no statistically significant difference was observed among the remaining three combinations. Although this difference is not statistically significant, it is noteworthy that, contrary to the positive main effects, CGPT-4o’s short command condition showed slightly higher average response sensitivity than the detailed command condition.

Both model type and prompt customization had a positive effect on response sensitivity. CGPT-4o performed better than GPT-4o. Detailed prompts were superior to short prompts. When the interaction between the model and the prompts was also evaluated, a noticeable improvement in model performance was observed when detailed prompts were given to GPT-4o. While GPT-4o benefited substantially from detailed prompts, CGPT-4o exhibited slightly better performance with short prompts. This crossover pattern underscores that the effectiveness of prompt design is not universal but rather conditional on the model employed. Consequently, the choice of model and prompt configuration becomes crucial, as their interplay can markedly alter the expected outcome.

Discussion

This study aimed to compare the success of the standard and customized versions of the GPT-4o language model, with short and detailed prompts, in detecting crown fractures on maxillary periapical radiographs. The findings revealed that model type and prompt structure significantly affected diagnostic sensitivity. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use a multimodal LLM for crown-fracture detection on periapical radiographs, and our results indicate that customization and prompt details can improve sensitivity. All three predefined hypotheses have been supported by the data: (H1) the CGPT-4o provided higher correctness than the GPT-4o; (H2) detailed prompts prevailed over short prompts; and (H3) correctness varied depending on the specific matching of model and prompt. The GPT-4o benefited significantly from detailed prompts, whereas sensitivity to short/detailed prompts was minimal in the CGPT-4o.

In recent years, various AI-based models have been developed in the field of dental traumatology to diagnose dental trauma using radiographic images. Deep learning models such as Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), YOLOv5, and Faster R-CNN have shown promising results in the detection of different types of traumatic lesions. Bani Hani et al. [21] reported that a CNN model that can distinguish four different fracture types in pediatric periapical radiographs provides 96–99% sensitivity. On the other hand, while the sensitivity of hybrid models in the detection of mandibular fractures reached 87%, false positives increased in some regions [22, 23]. Networks trained with three-dimensional imaging techniques such as CBCT and micro-CT also show impressive results for early detection of root fractures and cracks [24–27].

Various studies have been published evaluating the performance comparison of language models such as GPT in different areas of dental traumatology. In particular, the quality, consistency, and clinical suitability of ChatGPT, Gemini, and similar models in traumatic dental injuries have been analyzed in many studies. Öztürk et al. [9] compared ChatGPT-3.5 and 4.0 in terms of providing information on traumatic dental injuries, noting that ChatGPT-4.0 provided higher-quality responses to some questions, but that both versions had limitations in terms of readability. Mustuloğlu and Deniz [11] reported in their study on avulsion management that five different chatbots performed differently in terms of sensitivity and consistency, with the highest sensitivity observed in ChatGPT-4.0. Tokgöz Kaplan and Cankar [12] demonstrated that Gemini scored higher than ChatGPT-3.5, particularly in multiple-choice and true-false questions. Similarly, Güven et al. [10] also found that Gemini and ChatGPT-4.0 were significantly superior to ChatGPT-3.5 in terms of overall response quality. Kuru et al. [28], however, compared different language models and emphasized that some models fail to provide reliable references, with Gemini having the lowest sensitivity rate. These findings suggest that language models hold promise in fields such as public health education and first aid information, but are not sufficient on their own for clinical decision-making processes. Current studies in the field of dental traumatology have generally focused on text-based analyses of LLMs. However, there are various studies in the literature evaluating the diagnostic and sensitivity performance of LLMs on dental radiographs in different fields.

Multimodal versions of LLMs can now process visual and auditory data in addition to text. GPT-4o is one of these advanced models and offers new research opportunities, especially in areas such as medical imaging [18, 29–31]. The text quality of ChatGPT-4 in automated reports based on panoramic radiographs was found to be similar to that of students, but omitted important diagnostic details [32]. In another study using GPT models, Suárez et al. [33] reported a sensitivity rate of 38% in the detection of impacted wisdom teeth, while Kahalian et al. [34] showed that the addition of visual cues significantly increased the sensitivity from 30%. Durmazpınar and Ekmekçi [35] reported that ChatGPT-4 outperformed students with 91.4% sensitivity in endodontic diagnoses. Aşar et al. [14] reported that the customized GPT 4 V model detected supernumerary teeth 91% correctly; Eroğlu et al. [17] reported that the GPT model achieved 59.5%, 50.5%, and 84% success in staging, grading, and extent determination in periodontology, respectively.

There are very few studies in the literature on the customization or fine-tuning of large language models such as GPT for clinical tasks using visual patterns, specifically in dentistry [14, 36]. While model training in traditional AI systems is achieved using large volumes of labeled data sets, GPT-like large language models can be customized in different ways beyond this type of raw data training. Customization in these models can be achieved through two primary methods: The first is fine-tuning, where the model is retrained for specific tasks [36]. This process requires a high level of technical expertise and computational power, making it a more complex configuration. A second option is to optimize the command prompts for the model according to the desired task to make it more efficient [14].

The CGPT-4o model used was configured for a specific clinical task —namely, the detection of crown fractures in maxillary periapical radiographs —using only user-level prompt engineering, without any fine-tuning. The model was introduced with labeled examples, but no direct updates were made to its parameters. In this regard, the study does not involve a fine-tuning process in the technical sense, but rather a rule-based customization process supported by visual patterns. The high diagnostic sensitivity achieved demonstrates that large language models can provide meaningful outputs in clinical applications even without extensive fine-tuning processes, simply through user-level configurations. The model’s success has improved, particularly in the accurate interpretation of tooth numbers and radiograph-patient orientations, thereby significantly increasing the score 2 ratio.

The findings of our research reveal that LLMs have potential in visual analysis. In our study, the ability of GPT models to identify crown fractures from periapical radiographs was evaluated. When considering the total of correct (2 points) and partially correct (1 point) responses, success rates exceeding 90% were achieved in the GPT-4o + DP, CGPT-4o + DP, and CGPT-4o + SP groups, except for the GPT-4o + SP group. These results indicate performance nearly as good as that of deep learning-based AI models specialized and trained for specific tasks. When we controlled for radiographic fracture grade using the G1–G3 scheme, our results remained essentially the same: model type and prompt detail stayed significant, and their interaction persisted. This indicates that the observed improvements reflect better image understanding rather than an over-representation of easier or harder cases. The gains, therefore, do not hinge on fracture grade type.

In addition, it should be noted that a partially correct output (Score 1) would not be clinically actionable in isolation, no clinician would initiate treatment based on a mislocalized fracture identification. The ordinal rubric was designed to capture the gradient of model behavior, not to define clinical acceptability thresholds. Distinguishing between complete failure (Score 0) and partial pattern recognition with localization error (Score 1) is methodologically essential, as these represent fundamentally different failure modes with distinct implications for model improvement.

In our study, some radiographs were scored as 0 across all groups. This situation indicates that the AI algorithm may need to handle complex data and that these instances may be repeated. The inability to distinguish specific visual patterns or the limited interpretation of anatomical differences may be considered the reason of this situation. Customized LLMs using visual data have the potential to reduce possible diagnostic and visual deficiencies. Therefore, both customized approaches and classic GPT models offer promising results for image analysis.

Analyses conducted on the models show that both prompt inputs and model customization increase response sensitivity. However, it has also been shown that this improvement in response sensitivity performance is model-dependent. While a noticeable improvement is seen in the standard model with detailed prompts, they show minimal benefit in task-specific customized models. These results indicate that the task-focused information provided to the model during customization effectively serves as detailed prompt inputs. The absence of a statistically significant difference between CGPT-4o and GPT-4o when detailed prompts were used deserves particular attention. Our study was adequately powered for this comparison, so we consider this equivalence to reflect a genuine convergence in performance rather than a statistical artifact. We believe that detailed prompts functionally compensate for the lack of task-specific customization in the standard model. A detailed prompt provides explicit contextual cues that overlap considerably with the information already embedded in the custom GPT’s system instructions, including the clinical task definition, the expected output structure, and the diagnostic focus. When both sources of task guidance converge, performance appears to reach a comparable ceiling. This interpretation is also consistent with the strongly negative interaction effect we observed. The parameter estimates in Table 2 carry important clinical implications. The customized model’s 2.3-fold higher odds of correctness suggest that even without parameter fine-tuning, providing a model with task-specific visual exemplars and structured instructions can create a meaningful diagnostic advantage. This improvement may arise because the exemplars establish a frame of reference for tooth numbering conventions and fracture morphology, reducing hallucination and mislocalization errors. Detailed prompts were associated with approximately 1.7 times higher odds of achieving a correct response, suggesting that the way a clinical question is formulated can meaningfully influence diagnostic output quality. Perhaps the most informative finding, however, is the strongly negative interaction: customization and prompt detail appear to serve overlapping compensatory functions, and providing both may yield diminishing returns. This may explain why the standard model with short prompts fell behind all other combinations: it was the only condition where the model received no task-specific guidance at all, neither through customization nor through the prompt itself. In summary, a careful examination of the evaluation results in our study shows that detailed prompts reduce errors in the standard model, shifting it toward partial correctness. In contrast, the customized model can produce correct responses more frequently, even with short prompts.

When evaluating the practical applicability of models, if a visual evaluation is desired using a standard model, a detailed prompt is required. However, if the evaluation is to be performed on a task-focused, customized model from the outset, short and clear prompts will be more beneficial. We find it useful to consider this pattern through the lens of clinical education. A well-trained specialist tends to perform reliably with minimal guidance because much of the relevant domain knowledge has been internalized, whereas a novice typically needs detailed, step-by-step instructions to reach a comparable level. Our customized model behaved similarly to the specialist: it maintained consistent performance whether the prompt was short or detailed, suggesting that the task-specific knowledge embedded during customization may have reduced its dependence on external cues. The standard model, by contrast, resembled the novice: it required the detailed prompt to compensate for the absence of built-in task knowledge. From a practical standpoint, this suggests that clinicians who lack the time or resources to create a custom GPT may still achieve competitive results by carefully crafting detailed prompts. On the other hand, when clinical circumstances demand brief prompts, task-specific customization may become important to maintain output quality. Ultimately, rather than using a single standard model and prompt system, determining the most suitable model and prompt for the task and tailoring them to the specific domain can help achieve more reliable results.

In our study, presenting the same radiographic images to the model with both short and detailed prompts enabled direct evaluation of the effect of prompt engineering on AI performance. The findings show that the standard GPT-4o model responds significantly better to detailed and explanatory prompts; however, the CGPT-4o model, which was customized with visual examples beforehand, responds with slightly higher sensitivity to short, clear, and direct prompts, albeit not at a statistically significant level. This suggests that customized models require less additional explanation and that simple prompts enable the model to use previous visual patterns more effectively. Studies on the effect of prompt structure on model performance are increasing in the literature. In a study focusing on implant-supported prosthesis planning, it was shown that target-oriented and specific prompts produced more reliable responses than general statements [19]. Similarly, in a study conducted with a fine-tuned GPT-4 model, it was reported that prompts containing contextual content increased model sensitivity from 62.7% to 73.6% [37]. Another study comparing different prompt strategies emphasized that information-rich but long prompts provide advantages in terms of sourcing, but may negatively affect the readability of responses and user experience [38]. A study on oral lesion diagnosis indicated that accurate, clear, and specific prompts significantly increased diagnostic accuracy [39]. On the other hand, Koopman and Zuccon [20] found that adding extra evidence or guidance to prompts reduced model accuracy from 80% to 63%; Powers et al. [40] showed that model performance decreased as prompt length increased. Additionally, some studies have shown that information-based but excessively long prompts also reduce efficiency [41] and that different prompt techniques may offer advantages in other clinical contexts [42]. Our study is one of the first to comparatively examine the effect of prompt engineering in the field of dental traumatology using visual-based radiographs. The findings indicate that model success depends not only on the model type but also on the structure and purpose of the prompt, such as those found in literature. Therefore, in the integration of LLM-based systems into clinical applications, prompt design should be considered a critical factor at least as crucial as model training.

This study has some limitations. First, only maxillary anterior periapical radiographs containing crown fractures were used. In clinical practice, periapical radiographs are not taken of anterior teeth unless there is any pathological condition or suspicion of trauma, so images of cases without fractures or pathology (negative cases) were not available. This situation limited the creation of a negative control group and, consequently, the calculation of specific diagnostic performance metrics such as specificity, accuracy, precision, and F1 score. As only diseased specimens were included, our results reflect diagnostic sensitivity. Clinical accuracy (sensitivity + specificity) could not be evaluated, and this reduces the generalizability and clinical applicability of the findings. Future studies incorporating both positive and negative cases are warranted to establish full diagnostic accuracy. Importantly, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence that the model can serve as a standalone diagnostic or clinical screening tool. Rather, the results represent a methodological assessment of the effects of prompt design and model customization on detection performance within a positive-only dataset. Furthermore, our results were obtained using GPT-4o accessed via the commercial ChatGPT interface on a specific date (15 July 2025), where generation parameters were not user-configured. These findings may not be directly transferable to earlier model versions, to future model updates, or to API-based implementations where explicit parameter control could yield different output distributions.

In addition, although the three raters who scored LLM outputs underwent a calibration session and resolved all discrepancies by consensus, a formal pre-consensus inter-rater reliability analysis was not performed for the scoring procedure. The ICC reported in Table 1 pertains to the two independent pediatric dentists who established the reference standard for fracture detection and grading, not to the three-rater scoring process. Future studies should report separate reliability metrics for each evaluation stage.

Additionally, the number of images used for prompt-based configuration was limited to 20 due to platform constraints. A broader and more diverse training dataset could enhance the model’s generalization capacity and more reliably demonstrate its clinical performance. We intentionally evaluated performance under routine clinical conditions, using PACS-exported images with a fixed pixel matrix and DPI and no pixel-level preprocessing. Brightness/contrast were left at default and not standardized, which enhances external validity but may limit visibility of subtle fracture lines; results therefore reflect default rather than optimized windowing. Future work will test robustness under alternative display settings and acquisition parameters. Furthermore, since the evaluations were limited to the anterior region and maxillary teeth, the model’s ability to detect fractures in the posterior areas or mandibular teeth could not be assessed. The results of the study may limit the generalizability of the model due to its testing on only a specific anatomical region and a limited number of cases. Future work will expand to a larger, more heterogeneous multi-center study to improve generalizability and enable stratified analyses. As a complementary next step, we also plan a controlled in-vitro validation using standardized phantoms to isolate acquisition variables and confirm generalizability.

Clinical implications

In dental traumatology, a fast and accurate diagnosis process is critical for treatment planning. In this context, AI-supported systems can assist the physician in the preliminary assessment process, facilitating time management by enabling early differentiation of possible fracture cases. In situations of high clinical intensity, the use of such systems, particularly as a screening tool, can help physicians to focus their attention on critical cases.

By developing trauma-focused or emergency referral-focused GPT models with prompts and visual patterns, guidance can be provided to patients who do not have access to rapid and emergency healthcare services. Teledentistry systems can be used to determine the urgency of treatment based on images. By referring patients to the nearest specialist, time loss after trauma can be prevented. This enables patients to access the right clinics earlier for early interventions, which play a significant role in treatment success.

Additionally, this study has revealed cases where GPT models cannot consistently detect certain fractures. This situation shows that the models still have room for improvement in interpreting visual patterns. However, the overall success rates achieved indicate that these technologies can reach a level where they can provide diagnostic support even to clinicians with limited experience when properly customized. The integration of such AI models into clinical applications should be done carefully and in a controlled manner; they should be positioned as auxiliary systems rather than decision-makers. Advanced validation studies with large patient groups will form the basis for the routine clinical use of these models.

Conclusion

In this paper, it is demonstrated that the sensitivity of crown fracture detection on maxillary anterior periapical radiographs is significantly affected by both model customization and prompt design, and all three predetermined hypotheses are confirmed: Customization and detailed prompts generally improve sensitivity; however, the final performance depends on the chosen model-prompt match. The standard GPT-4o model showed significant benefit when using DP, while the CGPT-4o model remained effective with short and clear prompts. Of the four options, the standard GPT-4o + SP performed the poorest; the other three were similar. When considered in clinical practice, there is no single “best prompt”; the model-prompt match should be selected and validated together. Since the results are based on fracture cases only, the results are limited by sensitivity; multicenter studies including negative cases are required for specificity and overall accuracy.
