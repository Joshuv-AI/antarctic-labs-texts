Model-oriented Graph Distances via Partially Ordered Sets
Armeen Taeb *, F. Richard Guo *, Leonard Henckel *

Abstract
A well-defined distance on the parameter space is key to evaluating estimators, ensuring consistency, and building confidence sets. While there are typically standard distances to adopt in a continuous space, this is not the case for combinatorial parameters such as graphs that represent statistical models. Existing proposals like the structural Hamming distance are defined on the graphs rather than the models they represent and can hence lead to undesirable behaviors. We propose a model-oriented framework for defining the distance between graphs that is applicable across many different graph classes. Our approach treats each graph as a statistical model and organizes the graphs in a partially ordered set based on model inclusion. This induces a neighborhood structure, from which we define the model-oriented distance as the length of a shortest path through neighbors, yielding a metric in the space of graphs. We apply this framework to both probabilistic graphical models (e.g., undirected graphs and completed partially directed acyclic graphs) and causal graphical models (e.g., directed acyclic graphs and maximally oriented partially directed acyclic graphs). We analyze the theoretical and empirical behaviors of model-oriented distances. Algorithmic tools are also developed for computing and bounding these distances.

1
Introduction

Graphs provide an intuitive framework for encoding and reasoning about the dependency structure among a set of variables.
In statistics, they have become popular in two closely related but different contexts:
probabilistic
modeling and
causal
modeling.
For the former context, graphs typically encode conditional independence relations among a set of (factual) random variables
(
Lauritzen 1996
)
; for certain scenarios, they can also encode generalized versions of conditional independence and other constraints
(
Evans 2016
;
Richardson
et al. 2023
)
— we refer to these as
probabilistic graphical models
.
Among other applications, these graphs lead to sound and efficient algorithms for probabilistic reasoning
(
Lauritzen and
Spiegelhalter 1988
;
Lunn et al. 2000
)
.
For the latter context, a graph models a causal system by encoding the relations of its building blocks, namely counterfactual (as well as factual) random variables; as such, the graph not only describes the system’s behavior as things naturally occur, but also its behavior under interventions
(
Wright 1934
;
Spirtes, Glymour and
Scheines 2000
;
Pearl 2009
)
— we use the term
causal graphical models
to refer to graphs like this.
These graphs are used to determine if a causal effect can be identified from observational data
(
Huang and
Valtorta 2006
;
Shpitser and
Pearl 2006
)
, and if so, to also inform the best way to estimate the effect
(
Rotnitzky and
Smucler 2020
;
Henckel, Perković and
Maathuis 2022
;
Guo, Perković and
Rotnitzky 2023
)
.
There is also a large literature on learning probabilistic or causal graphs from data, a problem known as structure learning or causal discovery; the reader is referred to
Drton and Maathuis 2017
;
Glymour, Zhang and
Spirtes 2019
for two recent surveys.

In whichever context these graphs are used, a key unresolved issue is how to define a suitable distance between them.
Having a distance is essential for several tasks, including benchmarking causal discovery algorithms
(
Scutari, Graafland and
Gutiérrez 2019
;
Rios, Moffa and
Kuipers 2023
)
, characterizing their statistical performance
(
Jin, Zhang and
Zhang 2012
;
Ke, Jin and Fan 2012
;
Han et al. 2016
)
or finding a consensus graph among a collection of graphs through a Frechét mean
(
Ferguson and Meyer 2023
;
Wang, Kolar and
Drton 2025
)
. Other potential applications include constructing ball-like confidence regions for a graph and and conducting sensitivity analysis with respect to potential mistakes in the causal graph.
However, defining a suitable distance is challenging for two reasons.
First, there are many types of graphs, such as undirected graphs (UGs), directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), completed partially directed acyclic graphs (CPDAGs), and maximally oriented partially directed acyclic graphs (MPDAGs), each with its own edge types and rules on how edges may be configured. Second, each type of graphs rely on a set of
semantics
(sometimes called
Markov properties
) to be interpreted as probabilistic or causal models, and a good distance between two graphs should reflect how close they are in terms of the models they represent.
That is, we seek a semantic rather than structural definition of the distance.
Despite these challenges, several distances have been proposed in the literature
(
Tsamardinos, Brown and
Aliferis 2006
;
Peters and
Bühlmann 2015
;
Viinikka, Eggeling and
Koivisto 2018
)
, which we divide into two categories —
model-agnostic
and
task-oriented
distances.

Arguably, the most popular distance is the structural Hamming distance (SHD)
(
Tsamardinos, Brown and
Aliferis 2006
)
. It counts the number of edges that differ between two graphs with various proposals for how to weigh different types of edge mismatches
(
Perrier, Imoto and
Miyano 2008
)
. Irrespective of the concrete weighing the SHD is computationally inexpensive, flexible with respect to the graph class, and guaranteed to define a metric.
It is, however,
model-agnostic
— it disregards the specific models the graphs represent and is solely defined on the graphs themselves.
This results in undesirable behaviors.
First, for a pair of graphs, the SHD provides a fixed answer regardless of how the graphs are interpreted, whether probabilistically or causally.
Second, the SHD does not depend on the set of graphs in consideration — it is agnostic to constraints posed by the graph type and domain specific considerations (e.g., one may wish to consider acyclic graphs).
Finally, by construction the SHD treats each edge individually. This ignores that not all edge configurations may give a valid graph in the graph class under consideration. We illustrate these issues with an example.

To address the limitation of model-agnostic distances,

task-oriented

In the context of causal inference, the central task is to identify a causal effect of interest, i.e., to express the effect as a functional of the observed distribution that holds under every causal system that is compatible with a graph.

Peters and

1.1
Our contributions

We introduce a
model-oriented
framework that defines distance between graphs
through
the models they represent.
Given a class of graphs of the same type (e.g., the collection of CPDAGs over five vertices), our approach begins by interpreting a probabilistic or causal graph as a statistical model, namely a set of distributions over a common collection of random variables — factual or counterfactual — that obey the constraints encoded by the graph.
This viewpoint naturally leads to the organization of graphs into a
partially ordered set
(poset), where the ordering is defined by model inclusion: for two graphs
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
, we say
\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
if and only if the model represented by
\mathcal{G}
is contained by the model represented by
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
. Posets are equipped with a natural neighborhood notion called covering elements.
Exploiting this neighborhood notion, we define our distance as the length of a shortest path between two graphs when moving only through neighbors.
By construction, our distance is a metric and applicable to many classes of graphs of varying type.

We study in detail the model-oriented distance for four graph types, listed in order of increasing complexity of their associated posets: probabilistic UGs, causal DAGs, probabilistic CPDAGs, and causal MPDAGs.
For each, we study algorithms for computing or bounding the model-oriented distance. Here, a key ingredient is a graphical characterization of model containment, and whenever possible, of neighborhood.
When the latter proves difficult, we study the neighborhood for a restricted class of graphs where the characterization is tractable.
Further, we show that poset-theoretic properties, such as gradedness and semimodularity, can facilitate computing the distances.
We also show that, for CPDAGs and MPDAGs, the model-oriented distance can differ substantially from the structural Hamming distance.
For CPDAGs and MPDAGs restricted to polytrees, we also prove that the model-oriented distance admits several simplifications that make it easier to compute and interpret.

By establishing that many statistical graph classes are naturally equipped with partial orderings that can be studied using theoretical tools from order theory, we open a new avenue for investigating graphical models and their properties. Partial orders, for example, have been shown to be natural and useful structures for model selection over combinatorial objects, including graphs
(
Taeb, Bühlmann and
Chandrasekaran 2023
)
. Similarly, neighborhood notions are central to score-based causal discovery methods where they used to decide how to explore the space of all graphs in a class while searching for a score-minimizing graph
(
Chickering 2002
)
.

2
Background

2.1
Graphical preliminaries

We consider graphs with nodes representing random variables and edges between them representing probabilistic or causal dependencies between these variables.
Basic graphical concepts are briefly described in below.

2.1.1
Node, edge, subgraph, cycle, PDAG

We write a graph over vertex set
\mathrm{V}
with
k\geq 1
types of edges (e.g., undirected ‘
-
’ and directed ‘
\rightarrow
’) as
\mathcal{G}=(\mathrm{V},\mathrm{E}^{1},\dots,\mathrm{E}^{k})
. Depending on the edge type
i
, edge set
\mathrm{E}^{i}
is either a set of unordered tuples (
(1,2)=(2,1)
) or a set of ordered tuples (
(1,2)\neq(2,1)
). In either case, we forbid self-loops and let
\mathrm{E}^{i}\subseteq(\mathrm{V}\times\mathrm{V})\setminus\cup_{i\in\mathrm{V}}\{(i,i)\}
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
A graph
\mathcal{G}_{1}=(\mathrm{V},\mathrm{E}_{1}^{1},\dots,\mathrm{E}_{1}^{k})
is said to be a
subgraph
of
\mathcal{G}_{2}=(\mathrm{V},\mathrm{E}_{2}^{1},\dots,\mathrm{E}_{2}^{k})
if
\mathrm{E}_{1}^{i}\subseteq\mathrm{E}_{2}^{i}
for each edge type
i
.
Given a graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathrm{V},\mathrm{E}^{1},\dots,\mathrm{E}^{k})
and a subset of vertices
\mathrm{V}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathrm{V}
, the
induced graph
of
\mathrm{V}^{\prime}
is
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{V}^{\prime}}:=(\mathrm{V}^{\prime},{\mathrm{E}^{1}}^{\prime},\dots,{\mathrm{E}^{k}}^{\prime})
where
{\mathrm{E}^{i}}^{\prime}:=\mathrm{E}^{i}\cap(\mathrm{V}^{\prime}\times\mathrm{V}^{\prime})
for
i=1,\dots,k
. A graph is
simple
if between every two vertices there is at most one edge (of any type). Throughout, we only consider simple graphs. A cycle is a sequence of vertices
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{k}
(
k\geq 3
) such that there is an edge between every two adjacent vertices,
v_{k}=v_{1}
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{k-1}
are all distinct.
A
directed cycle
is a cycle of the form
v_{1}\rightarrow v_{2}\rightarrow\dots\rightarrow v_{k}
.
In contrast, a
partially directed cycle
is a cycle where the edges can be directed or undirected such that (i) at least one edge is directed and (ii) all the directed edges in the cycle point to the same direction.
A graph is
chordal
if every cycle of length 4 or more has a chord — an edge connecting two non-consecutive vertices.
A
partially directed acyclic graph
(PDAG) is a simple graph with two types of edges, directed and undirected, such that there is no directed cycle.

2.1.2
Undirected graphs (UGs)

An undirected graph is a graph consisting of undirected edges.
Suppose a random vector
X=(X_{v}:v\in\mathrm{V})
is indexed by vertices of the undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathrm{V},\mathrm{E})
.
An undirected graph
\mathcal{G}
defines a probabilistic model for the distribution of
X
through a set of semantics known as the Markov properties of UGs. In the literature, there are four notions of such semantics: factorization, as well as, global, local and pairwise Markov properties, which are not equivalent in general; see
Lauritzen 1996
.
The exact choice is not essential for our approach. Yet, for simplicity, in
Section

2.2
we will assume regularity conditions so that these four notions are equivalent.

2.1.3
Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)

A directed acyclic graph is a PDAG with only directed edges.
Depending on the semantics chosen, a DAG can be interpreted either as a probabilistic model or a causal model.
For the purpose of this paper, we only consider DAGs as causal models since distinct DAGs (over the same set of vertices) may encode the same probabilistic model — these DAGs are called
Markov equivalent
— and we require the map from graph to model to be injective. For probabilistic modeling, we instead use a special type of graphs to represent the Markov equivalence classes of DAGs, which is described next.

2.1.4
Completed partially directed acyclic graphs (CPDAGs)

A CPDAG, also known as an essential graph, is a partially directed acyclic graph (PDAG) that represents a set of DAGs that are Markov equivalent.
By a classical result
(
Verma and
Pearl 1990
;
Andersson, Madigan and
Perlman 1997
)
, two DAGs are Markov equivalent if they share (1) the same
skeletons
(e.g., edges ignoring the directions) and (2) the same set of
v-structures
(i.e., unshielded colliders).
We have a v-structure at a vertex
v_{2}
if there are two edges, e.g.,
v_{1}\rightarrow v_{2}
and
v_{2}\leftarrow v_{3}
, pointing to
v_{2}
, but there is no edge between
v_{1}
and
v_{3}
.
Given a class of Markov equivalent DAGs, the CPDAG
\mathcal{G}
representing the class can be obtained as follows.
First, let
\mathcal{G}
have the same skeleton as any (and hence every) DAG in the class.
Then, for each edge in the skeleton, orient the edge accordingly if the corresponding edge points to the same direction in every DAG in the equivalence class; otherwise, let the edge be undirected.
In the rest of this paper, we only consider a CPDAG
\mathcal{G}
as the probabilistic model associated with any (and consequently every) DAG in the corresponding Markov equivalent class.
It is worth mentioning that CPDAGs, or a larger type of graphs introduced below, can also be used to represent causal models. Not every PDAG is a valid CPDAG; see
Section

B.1
for details.

2.1.5
Maximally oriented partially directed acyclic graphs (MPDAGs)

A CPDAG
v_{1}-v_{2}-v_{3}
represents three DAGs, namely
v_{1}\rightarrow v_{2}\rightarrow v_{3}
,
v_{1}\leftarrow v_{2}\leftarrow v_{3}
and
v_{1}\leftarrow v_{2}\rightarrow v_{3}
, which are equivalent probabilistic models but distinct causal models.
In the causal context, suppose we also have the
background knowledge
that
v_{2}
temporally precedes
v_{3}
.
Then, we can narrow down to
v_{1}\rightarrow v_{2}\rightarrow v_{3}
or
v_{1}\leftarrow v_{2}\rightarrow v_{3}
, which together can be represented by a PDAG
v_{1}-v_{2}\rightarrow v_{3}
.
PDAGs like this are MPDAGs, which represent the conjunction of several distinct causal models that are observationally Markov equivalent.
They generalize CPDAGs by incorporating additional background (or experimental) knowledge on the orientation of certain edges
(
Hauser and
Bühlmann 2012
;
Perkovic 2020
;
Hauser and Bühlmann 2015
)
.
In the context of causal modeling, CPDAGs are special cases of MPDAGs (without any additional background knowledge). Therefore, in the rest of this paper, we will only focus on the probabilistic models represented by CPDAGs and the causal models represented by MPDAGs. Not every PDAG is valid MPDAG; see
Section

B.2
for structural constraints that an MPDAG must obey.

2.2
Graphs as statistical models

For the four types of graphs we introduced, we describe the models they represent. To ensure precision in deriving the corresponding model-oriented distances we do so rigorously, but the reader may set aside the technical details on first reading.

2.2.1
Graphs as probabilistic models

UGs and CPDAGs represent probabilistic models for a random vector
(X_{v}:v\in\mathrm{V})
.
Suppose
X
takes value in a product measurable space
\mathcal{X}=\prod_{v\in\mathrm{V}}\mathcal{X}_{v}
. Let
\mu=\prod_{v\in V}\mu_{v}
be a product measure over
\mathcal{X}
.
Let
P
be the law of
X
.
We use the nonparametric model

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}:=\left\{P:\text{$P$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\mu$}\right\}

as our ground set (here ‘
\mathrm{o}
’ stands for observational) so all the models are subsets of
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}
.
For
P\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}
, we use
p:=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P/\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
to denote its density.

For UGs, as explained in
Section

2.1.2
, we pose additional regularity condition on the ground set such that four Markov properties all lead to the same model definition.
Let the ground set be

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{+}:=\left\{P\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}:p>0\text{ $\mu$-a.e.}\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{G})
be the set of cliques of
\mathcal{G}
.
An undirected graph
\mathcal{G}
represents the model

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{\textsc{ug}}(\mathcal{G}):=\left\{P\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{+}:p(x)=\prod_{\mathrm{C}\in\mathcal{\mathrm{C}}(\mathcal{G})}\varphi_{\mathrm{C}}(x_{\mathrm{C}})\text{ for functions $\varphi_{\mathrm{C}}$}\right\},

i.e., densities that factorize in terms of the cliques of
\mathcal{G}
.
It can be shown that the model can also be written in terms of UG’s global Markov property
(
Lauritzen 1996
, §3.2.1)
as

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{\textsc{ug}}(\mathcal{G})=\big\{P\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{+}:\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B},\mathrm{C}\subset\mathrm{V}\text{ are disjoint},\;\text{$\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$ are separated by $\mathrm{C}$ in $\mathcal{G}$}\\
\implies X_{\mathrm{A}}\mathrel{\text{\scalebox{1.07}{$\perp\mkern-10.0mu\perp$}}}X_{\mathrm{B}}\mid X_{\mathrm{C}}\text{ under $P$}\big\}.

(1)

For a CPDAG
\mathcal{G}
, it represents the probabilistic model consisting of densities that factorize according any (and hence every) DAG
\mathcal{D}
represented by
\mathcal{G}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{D}
is in the Markov equivalence class represented by
\mathcal{G}
), given by

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{\textsc{cpdag}}(\mathcal{G}):=\left\{P\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}:\,p(x)=\prod_{v\in{V}}p_{v}(x_{v}|x_{{\text{pa}_{\mathcal{D}}(v)}})\text{ for some DAG $\mathcal{D}$ represented by $\mathcal{G}$}\right\},

where
p_{v}
is a conditional density function.
This model can be equivalently described in terms of the global or local Markov property of any DAG represented by
\mathcal{G}
; this holds without requiring positivity, so we choose
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}
as the ground set above.
The model is equivalent to the following global Markov property
(
Lauritzen 1996
, Theorem 3.27)
:

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{\textsc{cpdag}}(\mathcal{G})=\big\{P\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}:\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B},\mathrm{C}\subset\mathrm{V}\text{ are disjoint},\;\text{$\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$ are d-separated by $\mathrm{C}$ in $\mathcal{D}$}\\
\implies X_{\mathrm{A}}\mathrel{\text{\scalebox{1.07}{$\perp\mkern-10.0mu\perp$}}}X_{\mathrm{B}}\mid X_{\mathrm{C}}\text{ under $P$}\big\},\quad\text{for any DAG $\mathcal{D}$ represented by $\mathcal{G}$}.

(2)

For separation and d-separation, the reader is referred to
Lauritzen 1996
.

2.2.2
Graphs as causal models

We follow
Zhao 2025
in defining causal models associated with DAGs and MPDAGs.
Consider a causal system that generates observations of a random vector
X=(X_{v}:v\in\mathrm{V})
, which takes value in a product measurable space
\mathcal{X}\equiv\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{V}}
.
Here we use notation
\mathcal{X}_{I}:=\prod_{v\in\mathrm{I}}\mathcal{X}_{v}
for every
\mathrm{I}\subseteq\mathrm{V}
.
A causal model is a set of joint distributions of counterfactual variables as well as factual (i.e., observed) variables, both of which are part of the
potential outcome schedule
:

X(\cdot):=\left(X_{v}(x_{I}):v\in V,I\subseteq V,x_{I}\in\mathcal{X}_{I}\right),

where
X_{v}(x_{I})
is the potential outcome of variable
v
under the intervention that imposes value
x_{I}
on
X_{I}
.
In the special case when
I=\emptyset
, the margin
(X_{v}(\emptyset):v\in V)
consists of all the factual variables, whose law is called the
observed distribution
.
The potential outcome schedule is a stochastic process that takes value in
\mathcal{X}^{\prod_{I\subseteq V}\mathcal{X}_{I}}
, which can be equipped with a suitable
\sigma
-algebra to form a measurable space.
We use
P
to denote the law of
X(\cdot)
(i.e., a probability measure over the measurable space). Similar to the definition of
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}
, we consider the ground causal model to be

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}:=\big\{P:P\left(X(x_{I},x_{I^{\prime}})=X(x_{I})\mid X_{I^{\prime}}(x_{I})=x_{I^{\prime}}\right)=1,\\
\text{ for every disjoint $I,I^{\prime}\subseteq V$ and every $x\in\mathcal{X}$}\big\},

(3)

where ‘
\mathrm{c}
’ stands for causal.
In words,
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}
is the set of distributions of
X(\cdot)
that maintain
consistency
through recursive substitution (see, e.g.,
Shpitser, Richardson and
Robins 2022
, §41.2
): in a world that sets
X_{I}
to be
x_{I}
, if we observe
X_{I^{\prime}}
to be
x_{I}^{\prime}
, then the observed
X
equals
X(x_{I},x_{I^{\prime}})
. Any causal model that we discuss next will be a subset of
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}
.

A DAG
\mathcal{D}
represents the causal model

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\textsc{dag}}(\mathcal{D}):=\left\{P\in\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}:\text{$P$ is causal Markov with respect to $\mathcal{D}$}\right\}.

(4)

Let
\Pa_{\mathcal{D}}(v)
denote the parent set of
v
in
\mathcal{D}
. We say
P
is causal Markov with respect to
\mathcal{D}
if it satisfies the following two properties:

1.

For every
v\in\mathrm{V}
,
\mathrm{I}\subseteq\mathrm{V}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
X_{v}(x_{\mathrm{I}})=X_{v}(x_{\mathrm{I}\cap\Pa_{\mathcal{D}}(v)})
holds with probability 1;

2.

For every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the one-step-ahead counterfactuals
\left\{X_{v}(x_{\Pa_{\mathcal{D}}(v)}):v\in V\right\}
are mutually independent.

In conjunction with
Eq.

3
, these two properties imply that under any intervention (or no intervention),
(X_{v}(x):v\in\mathrm{V})
can be constructed from a set of independent basic building blocks, the one-step-ahead counterfactuals under that intervention, through recursive substitution.
Note that the second property does not impose cross-world independence of the counterfactuals; see also
Robins 1986
;
Richardson and
Robins 2013
.

An MPDAG
\mathcal{G}
represents the set of Markov equivalent DAGs that also obey any extra orientation encoded by
\mathcal{G}
.
A DAG
\mathcal{D}
is represented by
\mathcal{G}
if
\mathcal{D}
can be obtained by orienting the undirected edges in
\mathcal{G}
without introducing any directed cycles or v-structures.
The causal model represented by MPDAG
\mathcal{G}
is simply defined in terms of a disjunction:

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\textsc{mpdag}}(\mathcal{G})=\bigcup\big\{\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\textsc{dag}}(\mathcal{D}):\text{$\mathcal{D}$ is a DAG represented by $\mathcal{G}$}\big\}.

3
A general recipe for model-oriented distance using partially ordered sets

Let
\mathfrak{G}
be a finite collection of graphs
over a common set of vertices
\mathrm{V}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be an associated model map (i.e.,
semantics
) that assigns a model to each graph in the class.
Given
(\mathfrak{G},\mathcal{M})
, our goal is to define an
\mathcal{M}
-oriented distance
d(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
for every
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}
.
For example, for
\mathrm{V}=\{1,2,3,4,5\}
, we can consider defining distances for
(\mathfrak{G}_{1},\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{\textsc{ug}})
and
(\mathfrak{G}_{2},\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{\textsc{ug}})
with

\mathfrak{G}_{1}:=\{\text{all UGs over $\mathrm{V}$}\},\quad\mathfrak{G}_{2}:=\{\text{all UGs without cycle over $\mathrm{V}$}\}.

In Section
3.1
, we use the model map
\mathcal{M}
to define our model-oriented distance between
\mathcal{G}_{s}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
. In Section
3.2
, we provide an equivalent but more constructive characterization of this distance using the poset underlying
\mathfrak{G}
. Finally, in Section
3.3
, we describe how the poset structure may enable faster computation of the model-oriented distance.

3.1
Model-oriented distance

Our approach for defining a model-oriented distance is based on the following notion of neighborhood.

Definition 1
(Neighbor).

For
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}
and
\mathcal{G}_{1}\neq\mathcal{G}_{2}
, we call
\mathcal{G}_{1}
a neighbor of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
if either
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{2})
or
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{2})\subseteq\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{1})
holds, and there does not exist a third, different graph
\mathcal{G}_{3}\in\mathfrak{G}
such that
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{3})
is nested between
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{1})
and
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{2})
.

By construction, neighborhood is a symmetric relation.
Intuitively,
\mathcal{G}_{2}
is a neighbor of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
if it represents the simplest refinement or coarsening of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
’s model, with no graph lying strictly between them in the model hierarchy. The model-oriented distance between two graphs counts the minimal number of such basic refinement or coarsening operations to transform one model into the other, which we define as follows.

Definition 2
(Model-oriented distance).

Given
(\mathfrak{G},\mathcal{M})
, for
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}
, the
model-oriented distance
between
(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
is the minimal number of steps through neighbors to move from
\mathcal{G}_{s}
to
\mathcal{G}_{t}
.

We would like our distance to be a metric, i.e., a distance function
d:\mathfrak{G}\times\mathfrak{G}\to[0,\infty)
that satisfies the following conditions:

1.

Symmetry:
d(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})=d(\mathcal{G}_{t},\mathcal{G}_{s})
,

2.

Anti-symmetry:
d(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})=0
if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{s}=\mathcal{G}_{t}
,

3.

Triangle inequality:
d(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})\leq d(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G})+d(\mathcal{G}_{t},\mathcal{G})
for any
\mathcal{G}\in\mathfrak{G}
.

The significance of the symmetry and anti-symmetry properties is straightforward. We illustrate the importance of triangle inequality from both statistical and algorithmic perspectives. From a statistical perspective, when proving consistency or convergence of an estimator, one often introduces an intermediate “ideal" object (e.g., the population version of the estimator) to bridge the gap between the data and the truth. The triangle inequality ensures that the distance between an estimated graph and the true graph can be controlled via the intermediate graph. From an algorithmic perspective, the triangle inequality helps in enumerating graphs within a small distance of a reference graph. For example, when the distance takes integer values, one can use the triangle inequality to enumerate all graphs within distance two of a reference graph by first finding all graphs at distance one, and then enumerating all graphs that are distance one from those. Without the triangle inequality, this decomposition is no longer possible, making enumeration much more expensive.

For the model-oriented distance to be a metric, we require the following two conditions.

Condition 1
(Injectivity).

For any
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}
and
\mathcal{G}_{s}\neq\mathcal{G}_{t}
, we have
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{s})\neq\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{t})
.

Condition 2
(Connectedness).

For every
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}
, there exists a finite sequence of graphs
\mathcal{G}_{s}=\mathcal{G}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{G}_{k}=\mathcal{G}_{t}
with
k\geq 1
such that every
\mathcal{G}_{i},\mathcal{G}_{i+1}
are neighbors.

Theorem 1
(Metric).

The model-oriented distance in
Definition

2
is a metric if and only if
(\mathfrak{G},\mathcal{M})
satisfies
1
and
2
.

Let the model-oriented distance be denoted by
d
. Under
1
, function
d
can be viewed as the shortest-path distance, which is a metric, defined on an undirected graph with vertex set
\mathfrak{G}
and edges incident to neighbors.

2
ensures that the graph is connected so
d
is finite. For the reverse direction, if
1
fails then there exists
\mathcal{G}_{s}\neq\mathcal{G}_{t}
with
d(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})=0
. If
2
fails, then there exist
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}\in\mathcal{G}
with
d(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2})
undefined.
∎

For natural choices of
(\mathfrak{G},\mathcal{M})
, we argue that
1
and
2
are easily satisfied. In fact, in
Section

A.1
, we show that all the four models
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{\textsc{ug}},\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{\textsc{cpdag}},\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\textsc{dag}},\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{c}}_{\textsc{mpdag}}
discussed in this paper satisfy
1
. Typically,
2
can be ensured by the checking either of the two following stronger condition.

Condition 3
(Least element).

There exists a graph
\mathcal{G}_{\hat{0}}\in\mathfrak{G}
such that
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{0}})\subseteq\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G})
for every
\mathcal{G}\in\mathfrak{G}
.

When it exists,
\mathcal{G}_{\hat{0}}
is the unique least element in
\mathfrak{G}
and is usually taken to be the
empty graph
, which represents the smallest model. This condition has the following dual.

Condition
3
′
(Greatest element).

There exists a graph
\mathcal{G}_{\hat{1}}\in\mathfrak{G}
such that
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{1}})\supseteq\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G})
for every
\mathcal{G}\in\mathfrak{G}
.

Defining distance through the minimal number of basic operations to transform one object to the other is common in other combinatorial settings. In the context of rankings or permutations, the Kendall’s tau distance
(
Kendall 1938
)
counts the smallest number of adjacent swaps needed to align two rankings. In clustering, the variation of information
(
Meila 2005
)
counts the smallest weighted number of merges or splits needed to align two partitions. In both cases, the distance reflects how many basic, interpretable operations are required to transform one model into another.
Here, we use the notion of neighbor to provide a parsimonious and model-oriented definition of what the basic operations are.
In what follows, we use posets to formalize our approach.

3.2
Formulation of model-oriented distance via posets

Definition
2
provides a succinct yet principled strategy for defining a model-oriented distance across a wide range of graph classes. However, since the definition is implicit, it is not immediately clear how to compute the distance for any specific class. We now provide a constructive characterization of the model-oriented distance which we both use to study under what conditions it simplifies (see Sections
3.3
and
4
) and to develop algorithmic tools for computing it (see Section
5
).

Definition 3
(Poset, cover, connectedness, least element, comparability).

A
poset
(partially ordered set)
\mathcal{L}:=(L,\preceq)
is a collection
L
of elements and a relation
\preceq
that is reflexive (
x\preceq x,~\forall x\in L
), transitive (
x\preceq y,y\preceq z\Rightarrow x\preceq z,~\forall x,y,z\in L
), and anti-symmetric (
x\preceq y,y\preceq x\Rightarrow x=y,~\forall x,y\in L
). We use notation
x\prec y
if
x\preceq y
and
x\neq y
. An element
y\in L

covers

x\in L
, denoted as
x\lessdot y
, if
x\prec y
and there is no
z\in L
with
x\prec z\prec y
. The poset
\mathcal{L}
is connected if for every
x,y\in L
, there exists a finite sequence of
k\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
elements
x=x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}=y
such that either
x_{i+1}\gtrdot x_{i}
or
x_{i+1}\lessdot x_{i}
for every
i=1,2,\dots,k-1
. An element
x\in L
is called the
least (greatest) element
if
x\preceq y
(
x\succeq y
) for all
y\in L
. Elements
x,y\in\mathcal{L}
are said to be
comparable
if either
x\preceq y
or
y\succeq x
; otherwise, they are
incomparable
.

Definition 4
(Model-oriented poset).

Given a graph class
\mathfrak{G}
and an associated model map
\mathcal{M}
that satisfy
1
, the corresponding
model-oriented poset
is
\mathcal{L}:=(\mathfrak{G},\preceq)
with ‘
\preceq
’ given by model containment: for
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}
, we say
\mathcal{G}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{2}
if
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{2})
.

Reflexivity and transitivity of ‘
\preceq
’ hold by definition, while the anti-symmetry follows from the injectivity of
\mathcal{M}(\cdot)
(
1
).
The poset
\mathcal{L}
offers a natural way to organize the graphs
\mathcal{G}\in\mathfrak{G}
based on the models they represent.
The neighbors in
Definition

1
are exactly the covering relations in the poset:
\mathcal{G}_{2}
is a neighbor of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
if either
\mathcal{G}_{2}
covers
\mathcal{G}_{1}
or
\mathcal{G}_{1}
covers
\mathcal{G}_{2}
.
Note that
2
is equivalent to requiring that the poset
\mathcal{L}
is connected, which is typically ensured by the existence of a least element
\mathcal{G}_{\hat{0}}
(usually the empty graph) in
\mathfrak{G}
.

In this case, the partial order ‘

\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}

\mathcal{G}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{2}
if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{1}
is a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
.

Further,
\mathcal{G}_{2}

\mathcal{G}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{2}

\mathcal{G}_{2}

\mathcal{G}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{2}

We note that the ‘shortest path length’ is a standard way to measure distance in posets

, although its use has primarily been confined to the order theory literature.

3.3
Poset structures that facilitate the computation of model-oriented distance

Having established that the model-oriented distance corresponds to the shortest-path distance in the Hasse diagram of the model-oriented poset, we can compute it by using using the well-known
A^{*}
algorithm. In general, however, this can be expensive, particularly because it requires repeatedly identifying all neighbors of a graph while traversing many candidate paths in the Hasse diagram. Often, however, we can exploit additional structure in the poset to simplify computation — either by deriving a closed-form expression for the distance (eliminating the need for
A^{*}
), by streamlining the neighborhood search, or by reducing the number of paths that must be considered. In the following, we study several such structural properties in general, as we will rely on them when analyzing the model-oriented distances corresponding to probabilistic UG and CPDAG models, as well as causal DAGs and MPDAG models (see Section
4
). We also examine the conditions under which the model-oriented distance reduces to an edge-by-edge distance similarly to the SHD.

3.3.1
Graded poset

A connected poset
\mathcal{L}
is
graded
if there exists a function
\rank:\mathfrak{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{G}_{1}\lessdot\mathcal{G}_{2}
implies
\rank(\mathcal{G}_{2})=\rank(\mathcal{G}_{1})+1
and
\rank(\mathcal{G})=0
for a minimal
\mathcal{G}\in\mathfrak{G}
. It can be shown that if it exists, the rank function is unique. A graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}
is called a
minimal
element if there exists no
\mathcal{G}\in\mathfrak{G}
such that
\mathcal{G}\prec\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
(
\mathcal{G}\succ\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
).

If the model-oriented poset is graded, we can exploit this property to simplify computation in two ways. First, we obtain the following closed form characterization of the model-oriented distance between graphs comparable within the poset.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}=(\mathfrak{G},\preceq)
is a graded poset with rank function
\mathrm{rank}(\cdot)
.
For any
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}
with
\mathcal{G}_{s}\succeq\mathcal{G}_{t}
, every downward path from
\mathcal{G}_{s}
to
\mathcal{G}_{t}
has length
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{s})-\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{t})
, which also equals
d_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
.

This is a well-known result that follows from the property that in a graded poset every
maximal chain
that is bounded between two comparable elements are of the same length. A subset of
\mathfrak{G}
is a
chain
(i.e., totally ordered set) if every pair of elements are comparable. A chain is
maximal
if it is not contained by a larger chain. For completeness, we provide a short proof in
Section

C.1
.

Second, gradedness makes it significantly easier to enumerate all neighbors of a given graph
\mathcal{G}
.
Recall that
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
is a neighbor of
\mathcal{G}
in
\mathcal{L}
if either

\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\preceq\mathcal{G}
and there is no distinct graph
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
with

\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\preceq\tilde{\mathcal{G}}\preceq\mathcal{G}
,
or
\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
and there is no distinct graph
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
with

\mathcal{G}\preceq\tilde{\mathcal{G}}\preceq\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
.
In general, enumerating all neighbors can be cumbersome,
since one must check for the absence of these intermediate elements. If
\mathcal{L}
is graded, this process becomes much simpler since two graphs can only be neighbors if their ranks differ by exactly one.
Combined with the requirement that one graph is either above or below the other in the poset,
this condition becomes both necessary and sufficient. This characterization is particularly useful when the rank function has a simple form.
For instance, we will show in Section
4.1
that the poset underlying undirected graphs (UGs) is graded with the rank of a graph equal to its number of edges.
All neighbors of a UG
\mathcal{G}
are therefore UGs
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
that differ by exactly one edge and are comparable to
\mathcal{G}
.
In a non-graded poset, neighboring graphs may differ more substantially;
for example, we show in Section
4.4
that for causal MPDAGs, a neighboring graph may have many more edges.

3.3.2
Down-up and up-down distance, semi-lattice, semimodularity

To reduce the number of paths we need to explore, it is natural to consider the shortest path among a restricted set of paths between two graphs in the Hasse diagram.
Specifically, for
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}
, we consider the set of
down-up
and
up-down
paths between them, denoted by
\mathrm{P}_{\downarrow\uparrow}{(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})}
and
\mathrm{P}_{\uparrow\downarrow}{(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})}
respectively.
A path is down-up if it takes the form of

\mathcal{G}_{s}=x_{0}\gtrdot\cdots\gtrdot x_{i^{\ast}}\lessdot\cdots\lessdot x_{d}=\mathcal{G}_{t},

where
i^{\ast}\in\{0,\dots,d\}
is the inflection point; chains (
i^{\ast}=0
or
i^{\ast}=d
) are special cases of down-up paths. Conversely, an up-down path takes the form of

\mathcal{G}_{s}=x_{0}\lessdot\cdots\lessdot x_{i^{\ast}}\gtrdot\cdots\gtrdot x_{d}=\mathcal{G}_{t}.

The next result characterizes when down-up (up-down) paths connect every pair of graphs.

Proposition 2
.

The set of down-up paths
\mathrm{P}_{\downarrow\uparrow}{(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2})}
is non-empty for all
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}
if and only if
\mathcal{L}
has a least element (
3
). Similarly, the set of up-down paths
\mathrm{P}_{\uparrow\downarrow}{(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2})}
is non-empty for all
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}
if and only if
\mathcal{L}
has a greatest element (
3
′
).

It is clear that the existence of a least element guarantees a down-up path between any pair of graphs in
\mathfrak{G}
, since all elements share a common lower bound. Conversely, suppose
\mathcal{L}
does not have a least element. Then there must exist at least two minimal elements
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}
(
\mathcal{G}_{1}\neq\mathcal{G}_{2}
), which share no common lower bound.
Hence, no down-up path can exist between
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
.
The proof for the up-down paths follows similarly.
∎

The posets of all graph types (UGs, DAGs, CPDAGs, and MPDAGs) we consider in this paper have the empty graph as the least element. Further, except for DAGs, all of them also have a greatest element. See
Table

1
for a summary.

The down-up and up-down paths lead to the definition of down-up and up-down distances.

Definition 5
(Down-up and up-down distance).

Under
3
, the down-up distance is defined as
d_{\mathcal{L},\downarrow\uparrow}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}):=\min\{\mathrm{length}(p):p\in\mathrm{P}_{\downarrow\uparrow}{(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})}\}
for every
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}
.
Under
3
′
, the up-down distance is defined as
d_{\mathcal{L},\uparrow\downarrow}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}):=\min\{\mathrm{length}(p):p\in\mathrm{P}_{\uparrow\downarrow}{(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})}\}
for every
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}
.

Unlike
d_{\mathcal{L}}
, the down-up and up-down distances may not be metrics as they are not guaranteed to obey the triangle inequality. Nevertheless, by definition, they are upper bounds on
d_{\mathcal{L}}
and are generally easier to compute. In particular, when
\mathcal{L}
is graded, the down-up or up-down distances take a closed form.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}=(\mathfrak{G},\preceq)
is graded with rank function
\mathrm{rank}(\cdot)
. Then, under
3
, we have

d_{\mathcal{L},\downarrow\uparrow}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})=\rank(\mathcal{G}_{s})+\rank(\mathcal{G}_{t})-2\max_{\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}}\rank(\mathcal{G}),\quad\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}.

Similarly, under
3
′
, we have

d_{\mathcal{L},\uparrow\downarrow}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})=2\min_{\mathcal{G}\succeq\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}}\rank(\mathcal{G})-\rank(\mathcal{G}_{s})-\rank(\mathcal{G}_{t}),\quad\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}.

Any down-up path between
\mathcal{G}_{s}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
moves downward from
\mathcal{G}_{s}
, and then upward from an inflection point
\mathcal{G}
to
\mathcal{G}_{t}
. Since
\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{s}
and
\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{t}
, Proposition
1
implies that the length of the path is
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{s})+\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{t})-2\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G})
. Therefore, the shortest down-up path corresponds to an inflection point
\mathcal{G}
that maximizes
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G})
over all
\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{t}
. The proof for the up-down distance follows similarly.
∎

The following additional simplification requires notions of meet and join semi-lattice structures. In particular, graph
\mathcal{G}_{3}\in\mathfrak{G}
is the
least upper bound
(or
join
) of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, denoted as
\mathcal{G}_{1}\vee\mathcal{G}_{2}
, if
\mathcal{G}_{3}\succeq\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}
and
\mathcal{G}_{3}\preceq\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
for every
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
satisfying
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\succeq\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}
. The
greatest lower bound
(or
meet
) of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
is defined similarly and is denoted as
\mathcal{G}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{G}_{2}
.
Poset
\mathcal{L}
is a
join (meet) semi-lattice
if
\mathcal{G}\vee\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
(
\mathcal{G}\wedge\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
) exists for every
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}
.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}=(\mathfrak{G},\preceq)
is graded with rank function
\mathrm{rank}(\cdot)
. Under
3
, if
\mathcal{L}
is also a meet semi-lattice, we have

d_{\mathcal{L},\downarrow\uparrow}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})=\rank(\mathcal{G}_{s})+\rank(\mathcal{G}_{t})-2\rank(\mathcal{G}_{s}\wedge\mathcal{G}_{t}),\quad\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}.

Similarly, under
3
′
, if
\mathcal{L}
is also a join semi-lattice, we have

3.3.3
Relation to edge-by-edge distances

Recall that SHDs measure the distance between two graphs by counting how many edges are mismatched. They are therefore by construction edge-by-edge style distance, in the sense that they can be computed considering each edge at a time. Different types of mismatches are often assigned different weights
(
Perrier, Imoto and
Miyano 2008
)
. For PDAGs, for example, there are two common variants of SHD
(
Tsamardinos, Brown and
Aliferis 2006
)
defined as follows. Consider encoding a PDAG
\mathcal{G}
over
\mathrm{V}
with adjacency matrix

A_{i,j}:=\begin{cases}1,\quad&\text{$i-j$ or $i\rightarrow j$}\\
0,\quad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},\quad i,j\in\mathrm{V}.

Definition 7
(Structural Hamming distance).

Let PDAGs
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}
over a vertex set be encoded by adjacency matrix
A^{s},A^{t}
respectively. The two variants of SHD are given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \SHD_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})&:=\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{I}\left[A^{s}_{i,j}\neq A^{t}_{i,j}\text{ or }A^{s}_{j,i}\neq A^{t}_{j,i}\right],\\
\SHD_{2}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})&:=\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{I}\left[A^{s}_{i,j}\neq A^{t}_{i,j}\right]+\mathbb{I}\left[A^{s}_{j,i}\neq A^{t}_{j,i}\right].\end{aligned}

Here,
\mathbb{I}[\cdot]
is the indicator function. In the following corollary to
Theorem

2
, we study for general graph-types when the model-oriented distance also behaves edge-by-edge, and discuss the implications for certain PDAGs considered in this work.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose we consider graphs
\mathfrak{G}
with
k
edge types (see Section
2.1.1
) that define a poset
\mathcal{L}
, in which they are ordered based on subgraph containment; that is,
\mathcal{G}_{1}=(V,E^{1}_{1}\cup\dots\cup E_{1}^{k})\preceq\mathcal{G}_{2}=(V,E^{1}_{2}\cup\dots\cup E^{k}_{2})
if
E^{i}_{1}\subseteq E^{i}_{2}
for all
i
. Further, suppose
\mathcal{L}
is graded with
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|E_{i}|
for any
\mathcal{G}\in\mathfrak{G}
, and that it is lower semimodular. Then,
d_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|E^{1}_{i}\triangle E^{2}_{i}|
, where
\triangle
denotes the symmetric set difference.
If the graph class contains only undirected edges, this is equal to
\SHD_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
. If the class contains only directed edges, this is equal to
\SHD_{2}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
.

Since the poset is lower semimodular, we have from Theorem
2
that
d_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})=d_{\mathcal{L},\downarrow\uparrow}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
. By construction,
\mathcal{L}
is a meet semi-lattice with
\mathcal{G}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{G}_{2}=(V,E_{1}^{1}\cap E_{2}^{1}\cup\dots\cup E_{1}^{k}\cap E_{2}^{k})
. Appealing to Corollary
1
, we then have that:
d_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})=\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{s})+\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{t})-2\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{s}\wedge\mathcal{G}_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|E^{1}_{i}\triangle E^{2}_{i}|
. ∎

Effectively, this shows that the model-oriented distance is an edge-by-edge distance whenever the partial ordering can be determined by examining one edge at a time
(subgraph containment) and the Hasse diagram is sufficiently dense (lower semimodularity).
We will see that this typically holds for graph classes that impose few constraints, such as UGs and DAGs,
but not for CPDAGs or MPDAGs.
Which version of the SHD it coincides with then depends on whether the edges are directed or undirected.
In particular, as we will see in the next section, for UGs with model map
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{o}}_{\textsc{ug}}
, the model-oriented distance is equivalent to
\SHD_{1}
.
However, the model-oriented distance diverges from
\SHD_{1}

for all other graph types considered in this paper.

4.3.1
Upper and lower bounds on
d_{\mathcal{L}}

The lack of semi-lattice and semimodularity structures makes computing our model-oriented distance between CPDAGs much harder than UGs and DAGs. First,

is no longer applicable and indeed, as we demonstrate in
Example

1
, the SHD behaves very differently from our distance. Further, by
Theorem

2
, because the poset is not semimodular, up-down and down-up distances can only provide upper bounds on the model-oriented distance. Indeed, consider the example in
Fig.

5
, where the shortest path, taking a ‘zigzag’ shape, is of length 4, while both the shortest down-up and up-down paths have length 8.

Fig 5:
The model-oriented distance between probabilistic CPDAGs
\mathcal{G}_{s}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
is 4, as shown by the shortest path in red. Meanwhile, the up-down distance is 8 (blue path) and the down-up distance is also 8 (orange path).

Due to these difficulties, in
Section

5
, we propose using the
\text{A}^{\ast}
algorithm to compute

. As an informed search algorithm,
\text{A}^{\ast}
relies on good, custom-made lower and upper bounds on the distance to speed up the search: the lower bound, often called the
heuristic function
in the literature, allows one to disregard some paths without discarding a potentially optimal path; the upper bound let one prune all the paths that are longer than the current best solution. In this section, we investigate lower and upper bounds on
d_{\mathcal{L}}
between CPDAGs.

To formulate the lower bound we need some notation. For a PDAG
\mathcal{G}
, we use
\sk(\mathcal{G})
to denote its
skeleton
, which is the set of edges after undirecting all the edges in
\mathcal{G}
. We also use
\vstr(\mathcal{G})
to denote the set of triplets
(a,b,c)
that form a v-structure at
b
in
\mathcal{G}
(see
Section

2.1.4
); here we do not distinguish between
(a,b,c)
and
(c,b,a)
. Let us fix two CPDAGs
\mathcal{G}_{s}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
over a vertex set
\mathrm{V}
. Let us fix two CPDAGs
\mathcal{G}_{s}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
over a vertex set
\mathrm{V}
. Define the set

\mathrm{S}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}):=\Big\{(a,b,c)\subseteq\mathrm{V}\times\mathrm{V}\times\mathrm{V}:(a,b),(b,c)\in\sk(\mathcal{G}_{s})\cap\sk(\mathcal{G}_{t}),\\
(a,c)\not\in\sk(\mathcal{G}_{s})\cup\sk(\mathcal{G}_{t}),\text{ and }(a,b,c)\in\vstr(\mathcal{G}_{s})\triangle\vstr(\mathcal{G}_{t})\Big\},

which consists of v-structures that appear in only one of the two graphs.
The set above can always be
partitioned
as

\mathrm{S}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})=\mathrm{S}_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathrm{S}_{L},\quad L\geq 1,

where two triplets
(a,b,c)
and
(d,e,f)
are placed in the same partition if they have any vertex in common (e.g.,
a=d
or
a=e
).

For a pair of vertices
(i,j)
, we define operations
\OP_{(i,j),\text{CE}}
(cover edge) and
\OP_{(i,j),\text{ER}}
(edge removal) that map a subset of
\mathrm{S}
to a subset of
\mathrm{S}
, given by

\begin{array}[]{ll}\OP_{(i,j),\text{CE}}(\mathrm{T})&:=\mathrm{T}\setminus\{(i,k,j):k\in\mathrm{V}\},\\
\OP_{(i,j),\text{ER}}(\mathrm{T})&:=\mathrm{T}\setminus\{(i,j,k),(j,i,k):k\in\mathrm{V}\}\end{array},\quad\mathrm{T}\subseteq\mathrm{S}.

Here,
\OP_{(i,j),\text{CE}}
removes v-structures of the form
i\rightarrow k\leftarrow j
by adding a
covering edge
(see
Lemma

2
) between
i
and
j
;
\OP_{(i,j),\text{ER}}
removes v-structures of the form
i\rightarrow j\leftarrow k
or
j\rightarrow i\leftarrow k
by removing the edge between
i
and
j
.
For each partition
\mathrm{S}_{l}
, consider the minimum number of operations taken to eliminate all the v-structures in the partition, namely

m_{\text{op}}(\mathrm{S}_{l}):=\min\left\{m\in\mathbb{N}:\exists~\mathrm{op}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{op}_{m}\text{ such that }\mathrm{op}_{1}\circ\dots\circ\mathrm{op}_{m}(\mathrm{S}_{i})=\emptyset\right\},\quad l=1,\dots,L,

where each
\mathrm{op}_{k}
is either
\OP_{(i,j),\text{CE}}
or
\OP_{(i,j),\text{ER}}
for some
(i,j)
that can depend on
k
. Because the v-structures from two different partitions share no common vertex by construction and this property is maintained by both operations, we conclude that

m_{\text{op}}(\mathrm{S}):=\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{\text{op}}(\mathrm{S}_{l})

lower bounds the number of steps taken to align the set of v-structures between
\mathcal{G}_{s}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
.

Property 1
(Upper bound).

For CPDAGs
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}
, we have

d_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})\leq\min\left\{d_{\mathcal{L},\downarrow\uparrow}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}),d_{\mathcal{L},\uparrow\downarrow}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})\right\}.

Property 2
(Lower bound).

For CPDAGs
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}
, we have

d_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})\geq|\sk(\mathcal{G}_{s})\triangle\sk(\mathcal{G}_{t})|+2m_{\text{op}}(\mathrm{S}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})).

We prove Property
2
in Appendix
F.1
. Property
1
follows from recalling that the model-oriented distance is upper bounded by both the down-up and the up-down distance (both exist for CPDAGs since the poset has a least and greatest element), and appealing to Proposition
3
.

4.3.2
Restriction to polytrees

We have established that the model-oriented distance for CPDAGs is inherently more complex than that for UGs or DAGs, reflecting both the increased structural complexity of the model-oriented poset and the richer graphical nature of CPDAGs themselves. We now study how the model-oriented distance may simplify if we restrict the class of CPDAGs we consider. Specifically, we focus on CPDAGs that are also polytrees, that is, CPDAGs with an acyclic skeleton. This class frequently arises in the causal inference literature
(
Rebane and Pearl 1987
;
Sepehr and Materassi 2019
;
Grüttemeier, Komusiewicz and
Morawietz 2021
;
Tramontano, Monod and

Améndola
et al. 2023

Let

be the collection of completed partially directed graphs over the node set

that are polytrees. Here, the model-oriented distance between two graphs
\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t}\in\mathfrak{G}_{\text{poly}}
is the number of neighbor steps needed to transform
\mathcal{G}_{s}
to
\mathcal{G}_{t}
, where each step is a move to a CPDAG that is
also
a polytree. Formally, let
\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}}=(\mathfrak{G}_{\text{poly}},\preceq)
be the poset underlying
\mathfrak{G}_{\text{poly}}
using the same ordering relationship as the unrestricted setting (this is a subposet of
\mathcal{L}
). Then, the model-oriented distance

can be characterized as follows.

Theorem 3
.

For any pair of graphs
\mathcal{G}_{s}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
in
\mathfrak{G}_{\text{poly}}
, we have
d_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})=d_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}},\downarrow\uparrow}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
.

We prove this result in Appendix
F.2
. The proof proceeds by showing that
\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}}
is lower semimodular and then appeals to Theorem
2
to obtain the desired result.

4.3.3
Relationship to Separation Distance

There exist another distance for CPDAGs and probabilistic graphical model encoding only conditional independence statements more broadly, called separation distance
(
Wahl and Runge 2025
)
. It measures the distance between two graphs by counting the number of conditional independence statements encoded by only one of them. Similarly to our class of distances, the separation distance is a metric and model oriented, albeit on a different model interpretation. However, the separation distance framework implicitly assumes that any collection of conditional independence statements forms a valid conditional independence model. This is similar to how the SHD on CPDAGs implicitly assumes any simple graph with direct and undirected edges is a valid CPDAG (see Example
1
). Conditional independence statements, however, may imply other such statements
(
Geiger, Verma and
Pearl 1989
)
. In fact, determining which sets of triples correspond to a conditional independence model and which of these in turn correspond to a probabilistic graphical model is a challenging problem that has not yet been fully resolved
(
Sadeghi 2017
)
. There are two notable consequences of this idiosyncrasy. First, the separation distance between sparse graphs tends to be much larger than between dense graphs. Second, the separation distance between two graphs increases substantially when a disconnected node is added to both. We provide an example illustrating this and discuss the separation distance paradigm more thoroughly in Appendix
H.2
.

4.4
Causal MPDAGs

Suppose the graph class
\mathfrak{G}
is the set of MPDAGs over vertex set

with

as the model map. The following result provides a graphical characterization for model containment.

Proposition 9
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{G}_{2})

is a directed subgraph of
\mathcal{D}_{2}

We prove Proposition
9
in

underlying MPDAGs. This poset consists of MPDAGs, ordered according to subgraph containment: graphs

As with CPDAGs, the model

Given the importance of gradedness for computing and deriving simplified characterizations of

There does not exists a function

. It constructs a pair of MPDAGs

In addition to a lack of graded structure in
\mathcal{L}
, we do not have an explicit graphical characterization of neighbor structure. This makes it difficult to enumerate neighbors and compute

or the down-up and up-down distances

Definition 8
(Pseudo-rank).

For any MPDAG
\mathcal{G}\in\mathfrak{G}
, we define its pseudo-rank, denoted
\mathrm{pseudo}\text{-}\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G})
, by the the number of directed edges in
\mathcal{G}
plus twice the number of undirected edges.

The pseudo-rank function serves as a surrogate for rank in a non-graded poset (see

11

Proposition 11
(Sufficient condition for covering).

Consider
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}\in\mathfrak{G}
with
\mathcal{G}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{2}

\mathcal{G}_{1}\lessdot\mathcal{G}_{2}
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}\in\mathfrak{G}
satisfies
\mathcal{G}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{2}
and differs from both. By the pseudo-rank condition, the skeletons of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and

\mathcal{G}_{2}

\mathcal{G}_{1}

\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{G}_{1}

\mathcal{G}_{2}

\mathcal{G}_{2}

4.4.1
Restriction to polytrees for MPDAGs

Similar to the case of CPDAGs, we can restrict our attention to a subclass of MPDAGs that are polytrees. Let
\mathfrak{G}_{\text{poly}}
be the collection of MPDAGs over node set
V
that are polytrees. Define the subposet
\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}}=(\mathfrak{G}_{\text{poly}},\preceq)
as the restriction of
\mathcal{L}
to this subclass and let
d_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
denote the corresponding model-oriented distance. Let
d_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}},\mathrm{pseudo}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})

\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}}
is graded with pseudo-rank as its ranking function. We formalize this next.

Theorem 4
.

The poset
\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}}
is graded with rank function
\mathrm{pseudo}\text{-}\mathrm{rank}(\cdot)
. Thus, the model-oriented distance
d_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
is equal to the pseudo-distance
d_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}},\mathrm{pseudo}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
.

We prove Theorem
4
in Appendix
G.4
. This result confirms that in the polytree setting, the pseudo-rank function defines a proper grading on
\mathcal{L}_{\text{poly}}

4
provides concrete justification for using the pseudo-rank-based distance more broadly.

Recall that when restricting CPDAGs to polytrees, the corresponding poset is lower semimodular, allowing us to appeal to Theorem
2
to conclude that the model-oriented distance equals the down-up distance. However, we show in Appendix
E.3
that the poset obtained by restricting MPDAGs to polytrees is not lower semimodular. Thus, a similar simplification is not possible and we need to rely on the

5.2.1
Computing an upper bound based on down-up or up-down distances

Consider first the down-up distance
d_{\mathcal{L},\downarrow\uparrow}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
. One can adapt Algorithm
1
to search for the shortest down-up path between
\mathcal{G}_{s}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
. This adaptation requires only a few conceptual changes: (i) The neighbor selection step is restricted to graphs that are covered by the current graph, ensuring that each move is downward.
(ii) The search terminates early when a graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{t}
is reached. At this point, the upward portion of the path is not explicitly traversed; instead, its length is computed directly as
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{t})-\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})
. This uses the fact that in a graded poset, the shortest upward path from
\mathcal{G}_{1}
to
\mathcal{G}_{2}
with
\mathcal{G}_{1}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{2}
has length exactly
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{2})-\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{1})
; see Proposition
1
. (iii) The down-up distance between any graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
in this poset is given by
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{t})+\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})-2\max_{\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}^{\prime},\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{t}}\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G})
, which motivates the following heuristic
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{t})+\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})-2|\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{t})\cap\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})|
. In Appendix
F.3
, we prove that for any two CPDAGs
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
,
\max_{\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{2}}\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G})\leq|\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{1})\cap\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{2})|
, so the heuristic is a valid lower bound on the down-up distance and is therefore admissible.

Note that we check whether
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{t}
using the characterization in
Kocka, Bouckaert and
Studený 2001
, which provides necessary and sufficient conditions for conditional dependence inclusion between two CPDAGs. Since this check can be computationally costly, we consider a necessary criterion for
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
to be the inflection point in the shortest down-up path between
\mathcal{G}_{s}
and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
. Appendix
F.1
shows that the optimal inflection point is any maximizer of

6
Discussion

This paper introduces a model-oriented framework for defining distances between graphs within a given class, where each graph is treated as a statistical model and the collection of graphs is organized as a poset based on model inclusion. The resulting metric, derived from the shortest path through neighboring models, provides a principled way to measure distance across various graph classes including causal DAGs, probabilistic CPDAGs, and causal MPDAGs.

Our work opens several avenues for future research. First, for efficient computation of the model-oriented distance for MPDAGs, it is important to develop an explicit graphical characterization of the neighborhood structure. Second, one can consider a weighted version of the shortest path, where a weight is assigned to each covering pair in the poset. One possible approach for selecting these weights is to base them on information-theoretic notions, as was done in the context of distances between two partitions
(
Meila 2005
)
. Moreover, computational efficiency in executing the A* algorithm can be substantially improved through better heuristics, parallelization, and memory management. The authors are currently pursuing a separate project to reduce the time it takes to compute the model-oriented distance. Finally, the proposed framework is applicable to other graph classes including local independence graphs for capturing dependence structure of events that occur in time
(
Didelez 2008
;
Mogensen and
Hansen 2020
)
, maximal ancestral graphs
(
Richardson and
Spirtes 2002
)
and partial ancestral graph
(
Spirtes, Glymour and
Scheines 2000
)
that account for model dependencies in the presence of latent variables.

C.1
Simplification due to gradedness: proof of Proposition
1

Consider any path
p\in L(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})
from
\mathcal{G}_{s}
to
\mathcal{G}_{t}
where
\mathcal{G}_{s}\preceq\mathcal{G}_{t}
. Let
(x_{1}:=\mathcal{G}_{s},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}:=\mathcal{G}_{t})
be the sequence of graphs specifying this path. By definition, for each
i=2,\dots,k
, either
x_{i}
covers
x_{i-1}
or
x_{i-1}
covers
x_{i}
. Since the poset is graded, we know that for each step, the rank increases or decreases by one, i.e.
|\mathrm{rank}(x_{i})-\mathrm{rank}(x_{i-1})|=1
. Note, by telescoping sum, we have:

\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{t})-\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{s})=\sum_{i=2}^{k}\mathrm{rank}(x_{i})-\mathrm{rank}(x_{i-1})

A term in the decomposition above is equal to
1
when the change from
x_{i-1}
to
x_{i}
is an upward move in the poset (
x_{i-1}\preceq x_{i}
), and is
-1
when the change from
x_{i-1}
to
x_{i}
is downward. Hence,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{t})-\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{s})&=\#\text{ upward moves}-\#\text{ downward moves}\\
&\leq\#\text{ upward moves}+\#\text{ downward moves}=\mathrm{length}(p).\end{aligned}

We have thus shown that the length of any path from
\mathcal{G}_{s}
to
\mathcal{G}_{t}
is greater than or equal
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{t})-\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{G}_{s})
. Note that the lower-bound can be achieved by considering an upward path from
\mathcal{G}_{s}
to
\mathcal{G}_{t}
with the sequence
(x_{1}:=\mathcal{G}_{s},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}:=\mathcal{G}_{t})
where
x_{i}
covers
x_{i-1}
for every
i=2,3,\dots,k
. Here, the inequality above becomes an equality since
\#\text{ downward moves}=0
.

F.1
Lower bound on
d_{\mathcal{L}}
: proof of Property
2

Any path from
\mathcal{G}_{s}
from
\mathcal{G}_{t}
must involve removing edges that are in the skeleton of
\mathcal{G}_{s}
and not in the skeleton of
\mathcal{G}_{t}
and adding edges that are in the skeleton of
\mathcal{G}_{t}
but not in the skeleton of
\mathcal{G}_{s}
. Since every edge addition or deletion increases the path length by one, we have that
d_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})\geq|\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{s})\setminus\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{t})|+|\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{t})\setminus\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{s})|
.

The lower bound above does not account for v-structures that are present in one but not in the other. Specifically, suppose
\mathcal{G}_{s}
has a v-structure
i\rightarrow j\leftarrow k
with
i
and
k
not connected, and
\mathcal{G}_{t}
does not have this v-structure, but has an edge between
i
and
j
, an edge between
j
and
k
, and no edge between
i
and
k
. Thus, any path from
\mathcal{G}_{s}
to
\mathcal{G}_{t}
must remove this v-structure, which can only be done if and only if at some step at least one of the following happens:
(i)
the edge between
i
and
j
and/or between
j
and
k
is removed, and
(ii)
there is a covering edge that is added between
i
and
k
. For both
(i)
and
(j)
, an extra operation afterwards is needed: for
(i)
, it is to add back the edge, and for
(ii)
, it is to remove the covered edge. Similar argument can be made if
\mathcal{G}_{t}
has a v-structure that
\mathcal{G}_{s}
does not.

It is natural to define the set:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S:=\Big\{(a,b,c)\subseteq V\times V\times V:&(a,b),(b,c)\subseteq\sk(\mathcal{G}_{s})\cap\sk(\mathcal{G}_{t}),(a,c)\not\in\sk(\mathcal{G}_{s})\cup\sk(\mathcal{G}_{t}),\\
&\text{exactly one of }(a,b,c)\in\vstr(\mathcal{G}_{s})\text{ and }(a,b,c)\in\vstr(\mathcal{G}_{t})\Big\},\end{aligned}

and say that associated with every element in
S
, we need at least two operations. That is, in addition to
|\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{s})\setminus\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{t})|+|\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{t})\setminus\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{s})|
, at least
2|S(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})|
additional steps must be involved in any path from
\mathcal{G}_{s}
to
\mathcal{G}_{t}
. However,
2|S|
may overcount the number of operations needed. For example, removing an edge between
i
and
j
may account for elements
(i,j,k)
and
(i,j,m)
in
S
, or adding a covered edge between
i
and
k
may account for elements
(i,j,k)
and
(i,m,k)
in
S
.

To avoid overcounting the number of operations, we consider the following strategy. For any pair
(i,j)
, we define two operations. The first, denoted
\OP_{(i,j),\text{CE}}(\cdot)
, takes a subset of
S
, and removes all the elements of the form
(i,k,j)
or
(j,k,i)
for any
k
; this operation is associated with adding a covered edge between
i
and
j
. The second, denoted
\OP_{(i,j),\text{ER}}(\cdot)
, removes all elements of the form
(i,j,k)
,
(k,j,i)
,
(j,i,k),(k,i,j)
for any
k
; this operation is associated with deleting an edge between
i
and
j
. Let
\mathrm{OP}=\{\OP_{(i,j),\text{CE}},\OP_{(i,j),\text{ER}}\text{ for all }\text{ vertices }(i,j)\}
be the collection of all such operations. Our goal is to find the smallest number of operations from
\mathrm{OP}
such that, when applied consecutively to
S
, the resulting set is empty. That is, we seek a sequence of operations
(\mathrm{op}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{op}_{m})
where each step
\mathrm{op}_{i}\in\mathrm{OP}
, such that
\mathrm{op}_{1}\odot\dots\odot\mathrm{op}_{m}(S)=\emptyset
, and
m
is minimized. Let the minimum be
m_{\text{op}}
. Then, it follows that:

d_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G}_{s},\mathcal{G}_{t})\geq|\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{s})\setminus\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{t})|+|\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{t})\setminus\mathrm{sk}(\mathcal{G}_{s})|+2m_{\text{op}}.

On the Idiosyncrasies of the structural interventional distance and separation distance

H.1
Structural intervention distance

The structural intervention distance (SID) measures the distance between a candidate graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{guess}}
and a ground-truth graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{true}}
by counting the number of interventional distributions of the form
f(y\mid do(X=x))
that would be incorrectly inferred if parent adjustment were applied according to
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{guess}}
, while the true causal model conforms to
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{true}}
. SID was originally proposed as an alternative to SHD, aiming to account for the fact that the graphs being compared are
causal
rather than purely structural. In this sense, it represents an attempt to define a model-oriented distance specifically for causal DAGs.

However, as noted by
Peters and
Bühlmann 2015
, SID is not a metric. The SID between a pair of DAGs is zero if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{guess}}
is a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{true}}
. Consequently, SID does not satisfy the standard metric properties. It fails to ensure that
d_{\mathrm{SID}}(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2})=0
if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{1}=\mathcal{G}_{2}
, is not symmetric and does not obey the triangle inequality. It is, however, possible to symmetrize SID by averaging the two asymmetric distances for each pair of graphs. The resulting symmetrized SID satisfies the first two axioms of a metric, but as we will show with an example, it still fails to satisfy the triangle inequality.

H.2
Separation distance

We interpret a probabilistic graphical model as the collection of all random variables satisfying the conditional independence constraints encoded by the graph. A closely related and popular alternative is to interpret a probabilistic graphical model directly as the collection of conditional independence statements encoded by the graph or more formally the set of all triples
(X,Y,Z)
corresponding to a conditional independence statement of the form
X\perp\!\!\!\!\perp Y\mid Z

(
Studenỳ 1992
)
. Based on this interpretation of a CPDAG as a collection of triples
(
Wahl and Runge 2025
)
have proposed an alternative distance notion called the separation distance. It defines the distance between two CPDAGs as the size of the symmetric difference of the two corresponding sets of triples. Similarly to our class of distances, the separation distance is a metric and model oriented, albeit on a different model interpretation. It can also be generalized to any class of probabilistic graphical models that encode only conditional independence statements.
In fact any separation distance can be viewed as a minimal path distance on a Hasse diagram: Consider the set of all triples
X,Y,Z\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
and the poset structure induced by subset inclusion on this set. This poset forms a lattice, with the meet and join between two sets of triples defined as their intersection and union, respectively. As a result, the minimal path distance on the corresponding Hasse diagram equals the down-up distance, which in turn equals the size of the symmetric difference between the two sets.

However, the fact that the separation distance is implicitly defined on the set of all triples of subsets of the node set, while ignoring which of these correspond to a CPDAG or even valid conditional independence models for that matter has two serious consequences.
First, the separation distance between sparse graphs tends to be much larger than between dense graphs, simply because the former encode more separation statements to begin with and as a result dropping an additional statement typically triggers a cascade of implications. Second, the separation distance between two graphs increases substantially when a disconnected node is added to both. We now illustrate these characteristics with an example.

Example 3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{1}
denote the empty CPDAG,
\mathcal{G}_{2}
a CPDAG with a single (undirected) edge,
\mathcal{G}_{3}
the fully connected CPDAG, and
\mathcal{G}_{4}
the same graph with one (undirected) edge removed, all on
n
nodes. Since all d-separation statements hold in
\mathcal{G}_{1}
, it can be represented by the set of all triples of subsets on the set of
n
nodes. In
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, on the other hand, all statements hold except those of the form

\mathrm{V}_{i}\perp_{\mathcal{G}_{2}}\mathrm{V}_{j}\mid Z,\quad Z\subseteq V\setminus\{\mathrm{V}_{i},\mathrm{V}_{j}\}.

As a result, the separation distance between
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
is the size of this latter set, that is,
2^{n-1}
. Furthermore, for every disconnected node we add to both
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, their distance doubles. The CPDAG
\mathcal{G}_{3}

\mathcal{G}_{4}
encodes the single statement

\mathrm{V}_{i}\perp\mathrm{V}_{j}\mid\mathrm{V}\setminus\{\mathrm{V}_{i},\mathrm{V}_{j}\}
. Hence, the separation distance between
\mathcal{G}_{3}
and
\mathcal{G}_{4}
equals
1
. Again adding a disconnected node to both graphs doubles this distance.

In contrast,
d_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2})=d_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{G}_{3},\mathcal{G}_{4})=1

\mathcal{G}_{2}
is a covering element of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{4}
of
\mathcal{G}_{3}
in
\mathcal{L}
. This reflects the fact that none of the triples intermediate between the triple representing
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and that representing
\mathcal{G}_{2}
correspond to a CPDAG, or even to a valid conditional independence model. Furthermore, these distances remain unchanged if we add a disconnected node to all four graphs.
