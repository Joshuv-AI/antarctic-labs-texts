Multi-stromal organoid co-culture modelling reveals epithelial–fibroblast heterogeneity in pancreatic cancer and pancreatitis
Authors: Wenlong Li, Muntadher Jihad, Eloise G Lloyd, Marta Zaccaria, Gianluca Mucciolo, Gideon Nsubuga, Alwin Koonan-Lonappan, Joaquín Araos Henríquez, Priscilla S W Cheng, Sneha Harish, Sally Mills, Paul M Johnson, Weike Luo, Alejandro Alonso Montero, Rebecca Brais, Alex Deamer, Ania M Piskorz, Julia Jones, Jodi L Miller, Bristi Basu, Paul D W Kirk, Mireia Vallespinos, Giulia Biffi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInteractions between epithelial cells and fibroblasts influence disease progression and treatment response in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). While the diversity of fibroblasts in PDAC is increasingly recognized, it remains unclear how these cells differ from fibroblasts found in pancreatic inflammation. Chronic pancreatitis is a stroma-rich inflammatory disease and a risk factor for PDAC, making it a useful setting to study how epithelial cells and fibroblasts change during disease. Here we compare fibroblast diversity and epithelial–stromal interactions in pancreatitis and PDAC using human samples, mouse models and mouse pancreatitis-derived epithelial organoids. We also developed pancreatitis and PDAC organoid co-cultures containing pancreatic stellate cells, fibroblasts and mesothelial cells. Combining in vitro and in vivo models better reflected human disease than mouse models alone. Overall, our findings reveal distinct epithelial and fibroblast features in pancreatitis and PDAC and provide models to identify disease-specific markers and therapeutic vulnerabilities.Subject terms: Pancreatic cancer, Cancer microenvironment, Cancer models

Main

The high mortality rate of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is largely attributed to late diagnosis and treatment resistance, with contributions from an extensive non-cancerous stroma1. Within this stroma, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are key determinants of PDAC progression and therapy response2,3. PDAC CAFs are phenotypically and functionally heterogeneous, typically classified as inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs) and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs), with additional populations described within and across these states4–8. Whether fibroblasts in PDAC resemble those in non-malignant pancreatic diseases is less clear. Comparing CAFs in PDAC with fibroblasts in pancreatic conditions that predispose to this malignancy could identify cancer-specific fibroblast states relevant to interceptive, diagnostic and therapeutic PDAC strategies.

In humans, chronic pancreatitis is a persistent inflammatory state and a risk factor for PDAC9, characterized by increased fibrosis and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition that contribute to disease progression10. The ECM of chronic pancreatitis shares features with that in PDAC in mouse models and human tissues11, potentially because activated, ECM-producing pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs) are, at least in part, precursors of PDAC CAFs and contributors to pancreatitis fibrosis12–14.

Mouse models have advanced our understanding of epithelial cell reprogramming during pancreatic injury and inflammation, and its contribution to malignant transformation15–18. However, fibroblast reprogramming during pancreatitis, and how fibroblast and epithelial cell–fibroblast crosstalk differs between pancreatitis and PDAC, remain underexplored despite evidence that pancreatitis fibroblasts are heterogeneous14,19. This is partly owing to the lack of pancreatitis models that enable dissection of epithelial cell–fibroblast crosstalk. The most commonly used mouse model involves caerulein-induced pancreatic injury, which promotes digestive enzyme release from acinar cells, acinar-to-ductal metaplasia (ADM) and fibrosis20. Additional pancreatitis models may enable orthogonal validation and capture a broader range of human pancreatitis- and PDAC-specific features.

Here, we aimed to identify epithelial and fibroblast features characteristic of pancreatic inflammation or malignancy by analysing mouse and human pancreatitis and PDAC tissues, alongside pancreatitis- and PDAC-derived organoid/multi-stroma co-culture models.

Results

Myofibroblasts are enriched in human and mouse PDAC relative to pancreatitis

We first investigated whether fibroblast composition differs between PDAC and associated chronic pancreatitis in patient samples (Supplementary Table 1). Histological analyses of human PDAC tissues and matched adjacent pancreata with chronic pancreatitis revealed modest differences in overall collagen deposition, but distinct fibroblast populations, with higher expression of myofibroblast marker alpha smooth muscle actin (αSMA) in PDAC CAFs and higher levels of fibroblast marker platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRα) in pancreatitis fibroblasts (Fig. 1a,b and Extended Data Fig. 1a). To assess whether epithelial cells and fibroblasts show disease-specific features in PDAC compared with pancreatitis from non-PDAC patients, we analysed a published single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset of human PDAC (n = 5) and chronic pancreatitis19 (n = 4) (Fig. 1c, Extended Data Fig. 1b–d and Supplementary Table 2). myCAF and iCAF populations were identified in PDAC, whereas five fibroblast subsets were detected in pancreatitis: WT1HighCD55High, PI16High, FAPHigh, DCNHighDPP4High and CXCL12High (Extended Data Fig. 1e,f). Myofibroblastic signatures (for example, smooth muscle contraction and collagen formation) were upregulated in PDAC CAFs compared with pancreatitis fibroblasts, while proliferation-associated (for example, G2M checkpoint) and inflammatory (for example, inflammatory response) signatures were upregulated in PDAC malignant cells compared with pancreatitis ductal cells (Fig. 1d,e). To validate these findings, we integrated two published single-nuclei RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq) datasets21,22 to generate a combined dataset of human PDAC (n = 4) and chronic pancreatitis (n = 3) (Fig. 1f, Extended Data Fig. 1g–i and Supplementary Table 3). This confirmed the upregulation of proliferation-associated and inflammatory pathways in PDAC malignant cells compared with pancreatitis ductal cells and revealed increased inflammatory pathways (for example, interferon gamma response) in pancreatitis fibroblasts relative to PDAC CAFs (Fig. 1g,h). Together, these findings indicate that epithelial and fibroblast transcriptomes differ markedly between human pancreatitis and PDAC, suggesting a more myofibroblastic phenotype in PDAC CAFs and a more inflammatory phenotype in pancreatitis fibroblasts.

Given the limited availability of human chronic pancreatitis samples and datasets, we established mouse models of pancreatitis and compared them with mouse normal pancreas and PDAC tissues to improve understanding of epithelial and fibroblast differences between pancreatitis and PDAC (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2a). PDACs were obtained from C57BL/6J-background KrasLSL-G12D/+; Trp53LSL-R172H/+; Pdx1-Cre (KPC) genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs)23, while pancreatitis tissues were obtained from C57BL/6J mice treated with the cholecystokinin analogue caerulein20. We modelled pancreatitis and acute pancreatitis by treating mice with caerulein for 6 weeks or 2 days, respectively. Normal pancreata were obtained from C57BL/6J mice that were either untreated or injected with phosphate-buffered saline (vehicle) for 6 weeks. As expected, caerulein treatment reduced plasma amylase and lipase activity levels and pancreas weights in acute pancreatitis and pancreatitis mice compared with normal mice, consistent with acinar damage and pancreatic atrophy24 (Extended Data Fig. 2b). Pancreas weights were reduced the most in pancreatitis mice.

Pancreatitis is associated with loss of acinar cells through ADM, increased immune cell infiltration and fibrosis25. We therefore used immunohistochemistry to compare these features among pancreatitis, acute pancreatitis and normal pancreas tissues. Expression of the ductal marker cytokeratin 19 (CK19) was significantly increased in pancreatitis and acute pancreatitis compared with normal pancreas tissues, indicating elevated ADM (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 2c). Pancreatitis and acute pancreatitis also showed significantly increased immune cell infiltration compared with normal pancreas tissues, as indicated by increased expression of F4/80 and Ly6G, which mark macrophages and neutrophils, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 2d). Finally, fibrosis was significantly elevated in pancreatitis and acute pancreatitis compared with normal pancreas tissues, as detected using fibroblast markers PDGFRα, podoplanin (PDPN) and αSMA, and Masson’s trichrome stain for collagen (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 2c,e). Pancreatitis tissues also had higher levels of all these fibrosis markers than acute pancreatitis tissues (Extended Data Fig. 2c). These data are consistent with evidence that prolonged inflammatory regimens in mice better recapitulate features of the chronic inflammatory state associated with increased PDAC risk in patients20. Thus, we focused on pancreatitis (rather than acute pancreatitis) mouse models to further investigate fibroblast and epithelial cell differences between pancreatic inflammation and malignancy.

To evaluate stromal composition in mouse pancreatitis and PDAC, we analysed these tissues by immunohistochemistry. Levels of ADM and macrophage infiltration were similar between conditions, whereas neutrophil infiltration was significantly greater in PDAC compared with pancreatitis (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 2d). Fibroblast composition also differed: PDGFRα, PDPN and collagen were significantly higher in pancreatitis compared with PDAC tissues, whereas myofibroblast abundance (αSMA) was higher in PDAC, consistent with increased myofibroblastic signatures in human PDAC CAFs relative to pancreatitis fibroblasts (Fig. 2b, Extended Data Fig. 2e,f and Supplementary Table 4).

To further characterize normal pancreas, pancreatitis and PDAC tissues, we performed Visium HD spatial transcriptomics on a subset of samples analysed by immunohistochemistry (Supplementary Table 5). Normal pancreas contained few fibroblasts, ductal cells and immune cells, with acinar cells comprising the dominant population (Fig. 2c). By contrast, fibroblasts were the most abundant cell type in mouse pancreatitis, whereas malignant cells and fibroblasts dominated in PDAC (Fig. 2d,e). Pseudobulk analysis of fibroblasts revealed distinct transcriptional reprogramming in pancreatitis and PDAC relative to normal fibroblasts, with myofibroblastic signatures preferentially upregulated in PDAC CAFs (Extended Data Fig. 2g).

Together, these data suggest that epithelial and stromal cells differ between PDAC and pancreatitis tissues, with myofibroblasts being more abundant in mouse and human PDAC.

Mouse models recapitulate epithelial and fibroblast features of human pancreatitis and PDAC

To further investigate stromal differences between mouse pancreatitis and PDAC, we performed snRNA-seq of pancreatitis (n = 6) and GEMM-derived PDAC (n = 4) tissues (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 6). Malignant and non-malignant cell populations in GEMM-derived PDACs resembled those previously identified in organoid-derived PDACs7,26, including CAFs that sub-clustered into myCAF, iCAF and apCAF populations (Extended Data Fig. 3a–g). In pancreatitis, we identified four fibroblast subsets: ‘Fibro 1’ (Wt1HighCd55High), ‘Fibro 2’ (PdgfraHigh), ‘Fibro 3’ (FapHigh) and ‘Fibro 4’ (Wt1HighDcnHighDpp4High) (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 3h,i). Integration with multiplex RNA in situ hybridization (ISH) and immunofluorescence (IF) confirmed that these subsets are spatially interspersed and have distinct secretory programmes, including Il34HighWT1High Fibro 1, Pi16HighPDGFRαHigh Fibro 2, VegfcHighFapHigh and/or Cxcl12HighFapHigh Fibro 3, and Muc16HighWT1High Fibro 4 (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 3i,j). Comparative snRNA-seq analyses showed that Fibro 3 was the most similar pancreatitis fibroblast subset to PDAC CAFs, on the basis of enrichment of the PDAC CAF signature (top 50 marker genes) (Fig. 3d) and semi-supervised single-cell annotation using variational inference (scANVI) mapping27 (Fig. 3e). Mapping to CAF subsets suggested that Fibro 3 was most similar to iCAFs (43%), Fibro 2 to myCAFs (42%) and Fibro 1 and Fibro 4 to apCAFs (56% and 82%) (Extended Data Fig. 3k). Pseudotemporal trajectory analysis further suggested that Fibro 3 represents a direct precursor state of PDAC CAFs (Fig. 3f).

To further compare mouse PDAC CAFs and pancreatitis fibroblasts, we assessed pathways altered in the snRNA-seq dataset. Consistent with human scRNA-seq analyses, myofibroblastic signatures were enriched in PDAC CAFs compared with pancreatitis fibroblasts, whereas inflammatory signatures were enriched in pancreatitis fibroblasts (Fig. 3g). Among these, interferon responses were highest in Fibro 4 (Extended Data Fig. 3l). Consistent with immunohistochemistry, snRNA-seq also showed increased Pdgfra expression in pancreatitis fibroblasts and higher Acta2 expression (encoding αSMA) in PDAC CAFs (Extended Data Fig. 3m). CellChat analysis highlighted differences in ECM-associated ligands (for example, collagens and fibronectin (Fn1)) involved in fibroblast-to-epithelial cell signalling in pancreatitis and PDAC (Fig. 3h,i). NicheNet analysis further inferred a more dominant role for myofibroblast-related mediators (for example, Tgfb1, Col15a1 and Tnc) in PDAC CAF-to-malignant cell crosstalk than in pancreatitis fibroblast-to-ductal cell crosstalk (Fig. 3j and Extended Data Fig. 3n).

Together, these findings raised the question of whether myofibroblastic features of PDAC CAFs are conserved across genetically distinct tumour contexts. Our previous work showed that distinct PDAC genetic profiles shape the malignant cell transcriptome and stroma28,29. We therefore tested whether increased myofibroblastic CAF features in KRASG12D p53-mutant PDACs relative to pancreatitis fibroblasts are also preserved in SMAD4-deficient KRASG12D p53-mutant PDACs. We focused on SMAD4 loss because it is frequent in human PDAC and we previously showed that SMAD4-deficient PDACs shift from myCAFs to iCAFs28,30. We established orthotopically grafted PDAC mouse models using either KPC GEMM-derived organoids (KP PDAC) or these organoids CRISPR-engineered to delete Smad428 (KPΔS PDAC) (Fig. 3k). We generated and integrated snRNA-seq data from KP (n = 3) and KPΔS (n = 3) PDACs with the mouse pancreatitis dataset (Extended Data Fig. 3o,p and Supplementary Table 7). CAF signatures from both PDAC models (top 50 marker genes) were enriched in Fibro 3, consistent with KPC GEMMs (Extended Data Fig. 3q). CAFs from both KP and KPΔS PDACs also showed increased myofibroblastic signatures compared with pancreatitis fibroblasts, again mirroring KPC GEMMs (Fig. 3l,m).

We next asked whether fibroblasts from pancreatitis mouse models and PDAC GEMMs recapitulate human fibroblast gene expression patterns. Using human scRNA-seq and mouse snRNA-seq datasets, we focused on curated protein-coding orthologues. We identified 2,737 orthologues with significantly higher expression (P adjusted <0.05) in mouse PDAC CAFs compared with pancreatitis fibroblasts (‘mouse PDAC CAF orthologues’) and 3,011 orthologues with significantly higher expression in human PDAC CAFs compared with pancreatitis fibroblasts (‘human PDAC CAF orthologues’). Of the human PDAC CAF orthologues, 28% (n = 829/3,011) overlapped with mouse PDAC CAF orthologues (P = 3.47 × 10−59) (Supplementary Table 8). For pancreatitis fibroblast markers, we identified 6,125 mouse and 6,461 human orthologues with significantly higher expression in pancreatitis fibroblasts compared with PDAC CAFs (‘mouse pancreatitis fibroblast orthologues’ and ‘human pancreatitis fibroblast orthologues’, respectively). Of the human pancreatitis fibroblast orthologues, 54% (n = 3,494/6,461) overlapped with mouse pancreatitis fibroblast orthologues (P = 5 × 10−256) (Supplementary Table 8). Thus, these pancreatitis and PDAC mouse models significantly recapitulate gene markers of human pancreatitis and PDAC fibroblasts.

Our analyses also suggested that some features of human PDAC CAFs and pancreatitis fibroblasts are not fully captured in mouse models. Mapping mouse snRNA-seq signatures (the top 50 markers compared with other fibroblast subsets) (Supplementary Table 9) onto the human scRNA-seq dataset using AUCell scoring31 showed that Fibro 2 and Fibro 3 aligned more closely with human pancreatitis fibroblasts than Fibro 1 and Fibro 4, whereas mouse CAF signatures better aligned with the human myCAF cluster (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b). Reciprocal mapping of human signatures (Supplementary Table 2) onto mouse snRNA-seq revealed that the human iCAF signature was better represented in mouse PDAC CAFs than the human myCAF signature (Extended Data Fig. 4c). Among human pancreatitis fibroblast signatures, the PI16High and the DCNHighDPP4High subsets were more accurately captured by mouse models (Extended Data Fig. 4d). Pathway enrichment analysis of shared genes showed that mouse pancreatitis models capture both myofibroblastic and inflammatory signatures of human disease, whereas PDAC mouse models preferentially recapitulate human myofibroblastic programmes over inflammatory signatures (Extended Data Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 8). This is notable because patients in our PDAC cohort had an average age of >65 years, and we recently showed that aged PDAC mouse models better recapitulate the biology of older patients, particularly inflammatory signatures, than the younger mice used here32. Incorporating age and genetic diversity into future models should therefore help capture the nuances of human fibroblast biology.

We next assessed whether epithelial gene expression differences between mouse pancreatitis and PDAC recapitulate those in humans. Analysis of mouse epithelial cell clusters identified two pancreatitis ductal cell populations, termed ‘Ductal 1’ and ‘Ductal 2’, and one PDAC malignant cell population (Fig. 3n). The PDAC malignant cell signature (top 50 marker genes) was enriched in Ductal 1 compared with Ductal 2 cells, suggesting that Ductal 1 cells are transcriptomically closer to PDAC malignant cells (Extended Data Fig. 4f). This was supported by pseudotemporal trajectory analysis, which inferred a dominant trajectory connecting pancreatitis Ductal 1 cells to PDAC malignant cells (Fig. 3o). Analysis of pancreatitis Ductal 1 and Ductal 2 cells with KP and KPΔS malignant cells suggested that this connection is independent of PDAC SMAD4 status (Extended Data Fig. 4g,h). CellChat analysis inferred greater communication between Fibro 3, which is transcriptionally closer to PDAC CAFs, and Ductal 1 cells than with other cell types (Extended Data Fig. 4i), potentially indicating an early shift towards malignant-like communication. We therefore asked whether this inferred interaction corresponded to a distinct spatial organization. Visium HD spatial transcriptomics showed that Fibro 3 and Ductal 1 populations were interspersed throughout pancreatitis tissues rather than confined to a discrete niche (Extended Data Fig. 4j). To validate these spatial relationships at single-cell resolution, we performed multiplex RNA ISH for Fap combined with IF for secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) and E-cadherin. Because Spp1 was more highly expressed in Ductal 2 than Ductal 1 cells (Extended Data Fig. 3i), SPP1 enabled discrimination between ductal populations. This analysis confirmed that Fibro 3 do not form an isolated niche with Ductal 1 cells (Extended Data Fig. 4k). Instead, pancreatitis tissues show marked spatial heterogeneity, with SPP1Low Ductal 1 and SPP1High Ductal 2 cells often adjacent within the same ADM structures.

To further compare PDAC and pancreatitis epithelial cells, we analysed the malignant cluster against each pancreatitis ductal cluster. PDAC malignant cells upregulated proliferation-associated pathways compared with pancreatitis Ductal 2 cells, whereas ECM-associated and inflammatory signatures were enriched in PDAC malignant cells compared with Ductal 1 cells (Fig. 3p). Thus, mouse PDAC malignant cells capture proliferation- and inflammation-associated signatures also observed in human PDAC malignant cells relative to pancreatitis ductal cells.

Finally, we used a similar cross-species strategy to assess how well pancreatitis and PDAC epithelial cells in mouse models recapitulate human epithelial gene expression. We identified 3,804 orthologues with significantly higher expression in mouse PDAC malignant cells compared with pancreatitis ductal cells (‘mouse PDAC malignant orthologues’) and 1,751 orthologues with significantly higher expression in human PDAC malignant cells compared with pancreatitis ductal cells (‘human PDAC malignant orthologues’). Of the human PDAC malignant orthologues, 43% (n = 755/1,751) overlapped with mouse PDAC malignant orthologues (P = 1.1 × 10−81) (Supplementary Table 8). We also identified 5,378 mouse and 4,593 human orthologues with significantly higher expression in pancreatitis ductal cells compared with PDAC malignant cells (‘mouse pancreatitis ductal orthologues’ and ‘human pancreatitis ductal orthologues’, respectively). Of the human pancreatitis ductal orthologues, 47% (n = 2,160/4,593) overlapped with mouse pancreatitis ductal orthologues (P = 8.98 × 10−115) (Supplementary Table 8). These data indicate that pancreatitis and PDAC epithelial cells from mouse models significantly capture human gene expression patterns.

Together, these data reveal heterogeneous fibroblast and epithelial cell populations in pancreatitis and highlight candidate precursors of PDAC CAFs and malignant cells. They also support further comparative analyses of epithelial cells derived from our mouse models to investigate biology relevant to the respective human diseases.

Pancreatitis organoids recapitulate in vivo ductal cell gene expression

PDAC epithelial organoid/PSC co-cultures have advanced understanding of CAF heterogeneity by identifying iCAFs, myCAFs and malignant cell-derived mediators of their reprogramming4,33. We reasoned that an analogous pancreatitis model could distinguish fibroblast and epithelial features of inflammatory and malignant states. We therefore generated pancreatitis epithelial organoids34, hereafter ‘P organoids’, from pancreata of C57BL/6J mice treated with caerulein for 6 weeks, and compared them with normal pancreatic organoids (hereafter, ‘N organoids’) and PDAC organoids derived from C57BL/6J normal pancreata and KPC GEMM tumours, respectively (Fig. 4a). At day 1 post-isolation, N organoids showed the expected budding ductal-like morphology, and PDAC organoids formed large cystic clusters35 (Fig. 4b). By contrast, P organoids formed small, relatively disorganized cystic structures, suggesting a distinct origin from N organoids. After two to three passages, N, P and PDAC organoids had similar morphologies, although P organoids grew more slowly (Extended Data Fig. 5a–c). Consistently, RNA-seq showed enrichment of proliferation-associated signatures in N and PDAC organoids compared with P organoids (Fig. 4c,d, Extended Data Fig. 5d,e and Supplementary Table 10). As expected, N, P and PDAC organoids expressed ductal epithelial markers, including Sox9, Pdx1 and Krt1935 (Extended Data Fig. 5f).

RNA-seq revealed marked transcriptomic differences among N, P and PDAC organoids. Compared with N organoids, P organoids upregulated ECM-associated and inflammatory pathways, including JAK-STAT signalling (Fig. 4c,e and Extended Data Fig. 5g). These pathways were also enriched in P organoids relative to PDAC organoids, consistent with higher STAT3 activation, indicated by phospho-STAT3 levels, in P compared with PDAC organoids (Fig. 4d,e and Extended Data Fig. 5g,h). Conversely, as expected, the PDAC malignant cell signature derived from snRNA-seq profiles was enriched in PDAC organoids (Extended Data Fig. 5i). Importantly, gene signatures upregulated in pancreatitis Ductal 1 and Ductal 2 snRNA-seq profiles relative to PDAC malignant cells were enriched in P organoids, although with heterogeneity across lines (Extended Data Fig. 5i,j). Accordingly, the 4,145 genes expressed at higher levels in P than PDAC organoids (‘P organoid markers’) significantly overlapped with genes enriched in mouse Ductal 1 and Ductal 2 snRNA-seq profiles relative to PDAC malignant cells (‘pancreatitis Ductal 1 markers’ and ‘pancreatitis Ductal 2 markers’, respectively) (Extended Data Fig. 5k and Supplementary Table 8).

Together, these findings show that P organoids retain features of in vivo pancreatitis ductal cells and provide a model to investigate epithelial cell–fibroblast crosstalk in pancreatitis.

Fibroblast signalling more strongly shapes pancreatitis ductal cells than PDAC malignant cells

To investigate epithelial–fibroblast crosstalk in PDAC and pancreatitis, we analysed five culture conditions: P organoids, PDAC organoids or PSCs alone, to assess cell-intrinsic gene expression, and P or PDAC organoids co-cultured with PSCs, to assess the impact of fibroblast signalling on epithelial gene expression (Fig. 5a,b and Extended Data Fig. 6a). PSC numbers were higher in P co-cultures than in PDAC co-cultures or PSC monocultures, indicating that pancreatitis epithelial cells promote PSC expansion more effectively than PDAC malignant cells (Fig. 5c). Consistent with mouse tissue immunohistochemistry and snRNA-seq, P co-cultures also showed higher fibroblast-to-epithelial cell ratios than PDAC co-cultures (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 6b,c). Thus, P and PDAC organoid/PSC co-cultures capture epithelial–fibroblast features characteristic of pancreatitis and PDAC in vivo.

Consistent with tissue transcriptomic analyses, proliferation-associated signatures were enriched in PDAC compared with P organoids, regardless of culture condition (Fig. 5e, Extended Data Fig. 6d and Supplementary Table 11). By contrast, pathways upregulated in P relative to PDAC organoids were largely PSC-dependent. These included PDGF, transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and NOTCH signalling, known mediators of epithelial–stromal crosstalk2. Although both P and PDAC organoids upregulated inflammatory and ECM-associated pathways in co-culture, these enrichments were highest in P organoid co-cultures (Extended Data Fig. 6e,f). Thus, both organoid types respond to PSC-derived cues, but distinct epithelial–fibroblast crosstalk operates in pancreatitis and PDAC. Finally, the mouse pancreatitis Ductal 1, but not Ductal 2, signature was enriched in P organoids from co-cultures relative to monocultures, suggesting that the in vivo Ductal 1 state may partly depend on stromal signalling (Extended Data Fig. 6g).

To determine whether PSC co-cultures induced epithelial gene expression patterns resembling mouse pancreatitis and PDAC tissues, we applied our cross-model strategy. We identified 5,018 genes expressed at higher levels in P than PDAC co-cultures (‘P organoid markers from PSC co-cultures’) and overlapped them with ‘pancreatitis Ductal 1 markers’ and ‘pancreatitis Ductal 2 markers’ defined from mouse snRNA-seq profiles. P organoid markers from PSC co-cultures significantly overlapped with both pancreatitis Ductal 1 and Ductal 2 markers (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Table 8). This also identified genes upregulated in both ductal populations, or selectively in Ductal 1 or Ductal 2 cells, relative to PDAC malignant cells (Fig. 5f,g). Consistent with pathway enrichments in P compared with PDAC co-cultures, these included ligands and receptors involved in PDGF (for example, Pdgfd and Pdgfc), TGF-β (for example, Tgfbr1 and Sparc) and NOTCH (for example, Notch2) signalling.

We next asked whether P organoids from PSC co-cultures recapitulate ductal gene patterns from human pancreatitis tissues. We identified 4,553 orthologues of the P organoid markers from PSC co-cultures (‘P organoid orthologues from PSC co-cultures’) and compared them with human pancreatitis ductal orthologues. Overall, 39% (n = 1,777/4,593) of human pancreatitis ductal orthologues overlapped with P organoid orthologues from PSC co-cultures (Supplementary Table 8). Of these, 55% (n = 977/1,777) also overlapped with mouse pancreatitis ductal orthologues (for example, SOX9, SOX4, DPP4, PDGFD, FGFR2 and IL6ST) (Supplementary Table 8). SOX9 marks ADM and is expressed in PDAC36,37, SOX4 maintains pancreatic epithelial progenitor identity38, while DPP4 remains less defined in diseased and malignant pancreas39. We validated higher SOX9, SOX4 and DPP4 protein levels in P compared with PDAC organoids (Fig. 5h). This cross-species, cross-model comparison also identified 800 human pancreatitis ductal orthologues shared with P organoid orthologues from PSC co-cultures but not with in vivo mouse pancreatitis ductal orthologues (for example, STAT3, NFKB1 and MMP24) (Supplementary Table 8), suggesting that P organoid/PSC co-cultures complement mouse models for dissecting human pancreatitis ductal gene patterns.

We then asked whether PDAC organoids from PSC co-cultures recapitulate malignant cell profiles from PDAC tissues. Genes upregulated in PDAC compared with P organoid co-cultures (‘PDAC organoid markers from PSC co-cultures’; n = 5,047) significantly overlapped with mouse PDAC malignant markers (Fig. 5i,j and Supplementary Table 8). Orthologues of PDAC organoid markers from PSC co-cultures also overlapped with 46% (n = 798/1,751) of human PDAC malignant orthologues (Supplementary Table 8), of which 47% (n = 372/798) were also upregulated in mouse PDAC malignant orthologues. Shared orthologues included proliferative (for example, TOP2A) and metabolic (for example, SLC2A1) markers, as well as PDAC-enriched markers such as GCNT3 and MUC5AC35. CDKN2A, which encodes human p16 and p14 through alternative reading frames and is frequently lost in PDAC30, was also upregulated in mouse and human PDAC malignant cells compared with pancreatitis ductal cells in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 5i,j and Supplementary Table 8). Separate transcript analysis showed that Cdkn2a-202 (encoding p19, the mouse orthologue of p14), but not Cdkn2a-201 (encoding p16), was upregulated in PDAC compared with P organoids, and protein analysis confirmed higher p19, but not p16, in PDAC organoids (Fig. 5k,l). To determine whether mutant KRAS or p53 drives p19 upregulation in PDAC, we compared p19 protein abundance across a panel of organoid models, including KrasLSL-G12D/+; Pdx1-Cre (KC) GEMM-derived pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia organoids, and KPC GEMM-derived PDAC organoids that had not undergone p53 loss of heterozygosity (LOH; hereon, ‘KPC organoids’). Comparing N, KC, KPC and PDAC organoids showed that p19 increased modestly from N to KC organoids and was highest in KPC and PDAC organoids, suggesting an association between Kras/Trp53 status and increased p19 in PDAC malignant cells relative to pancreatitis ductal cells (Extended Data Fig. 6h).

PSCs affected P organoid gene expression more than PDAC organoid gene expression (Fig. 5e). Whereas only 1,042 genes differed between PDAC organoid mono- and co-cultures, 3,389 differed between P mono- and co-cultures (Supplementary Table 11). To estimate how many P and PDAC organoid markers from PSC co-cultures were regulated by PSC-derived cues, we compared co-culture markers with genes upregulated in each organoid type in co-culture relative to monoculture. This identified 20% (n = 1,016/5,018) of P organoid markers from PSC co-cultures that were also induced by PSC-derived cues (for example, Tgfbr1 and Sparc) (Fig. 5m and Extended Data Fig. 6i). By contrast, only 3% (n = 138/5,047) of PDAC organoid markers from PSC co-cultures were induced by PSC-derived cues, and most PDAC organoid markers from PSC co-cultures (for example, Top2a and Dusp6) were unchanged between mono- and co-cultures (Extended Data Fig. 6j,k).

Together, P and PDAC organoid/PSC co-cultures recapitulate in vivo gene expression patterns of mouse and human epithelial cells in pancreatitis and PDAC, and indicate that pancreatitis ductal cell transcriptomes are more strongly shaped by fibroblast signalling than PDAC malignant cell transcriptomes.

Organoid/PSC co-cultures partially capture in vivo fibroblast differences in pancreatitis and PDAC

We next asked how pancreatitis and PDAC epithelial cells differentially reprogramme fibroblasts by analysing PSCs from monocultures and from co-cultures with P or PDAC organoids. PSCs were activated in both co-culture settings relative to monoculture, with reduced quiescent fibroblast markers (Plin4 and Kitl) and increased inflammatory (Il6 and Cxcl1) and myofibroblastic (Tgfb1 and Col1a1) markers4,12,33,40 (Fig. 6a and Extended Data Fig. 7a). Consistently, inflammatory and myofibroblastic signatures were enriched in both PSC co-cultures compared with PSC monocultures (Extended Data Fig. 7b and Supplementary Table 12). However, inflammatory markers (Il1a, Il6 and Cxcl1) and pathways were higher in PSCs from PDAC co-cultures (Fig. 6a–c).

To identify mediators of this enhanced fibro-inflammatory phenotype, we examined known epithelial-derived ligands that induce inflammatory fibroblast and myofibroblast states in PDAC. Malignant cell-secreted TGF-β induces PDAC myCAF formation, whereas tumour necrosis factor (TNF, encoded by Tnf) and interleukin 1 alpha and beta (IL-1α and IL-1β) drive iCAF formation7,33. Tnf and Tgfb1 expression, and secreted TGF-β protein levels, were comparable in P and PDAC organoid mono- and co-cultures (Fig. 6d,e and Extended Data Fig. 7c). By contrast, Il1a expression and secreted IL-1α protein levels were higher in PDAC organoid mono- and co-cultures, while Il1b increased only in PDAC co-cultures (Fig. 6d,e and Extended Data Fig. 7d). NicheNet analysis also inferred Il1a and Il1b as candidate mediators of PDAC organoid-to-PSC crosstalk (Extended Data Fig. 7e,f). We therefore tested whether IL-1 signalling contributes to the stronger inflammatory state of PSCs from PDAC co-cultures. Conditioned media from both P and PDAC organoids induced inflammatory markers (Il1a, Cxcl1, Csf3, Lif and Il6) in PSCs, with higher levels induced by PDAC organoids (Extended Data Fig. 7g). IL-1α neutralization attenuated this phenotype in both conditions, most strongly in the PDAC condition, and Il1r1 knockout PSCs showed impaired inflammatory-marker induction compared with wild-type controls, with the greatest impairment in the PDAC condition (Extended Data Fig. 7g,h). Thus, elevated IL-1α production in PDAC, relative to P organoids, contributes to the enhanced inflammatory phenotype of co-cultured PSCs.

We next asked whether P or PDAC organoids induced PSC gene expression profiles resembling fibroblasts in pancreatitis and PDAC tissues. Genes upregulated in PSCs from P compared with PDAC co-cultures (‘P PSC markers’) significantly overlapped with mouse pancreatitis fibroblast markers, and orthologues of P PSC markers (‘P PSC orthologues’) overlapped with human pancreatitis fibroblast orthologues (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Table 8). By contrast, genes upregulated in PSCs from PDAC compared with P co-cultures (‘PDAC PSC markers’) did not significantly overlap with mouse PDAC CAF markers (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Table 8). Orthologues of PDAC PSC markers (‘PDAC PSC orthologues’) also did not overlap with human PDAC CAF orthologues (Supplementary Table 8). This limited overlap is consistent with the absence, in PDAC co-cultured PSCs, of pathways such as myofibroblastic signatures that were enriched in PDAC CAFs from mouse and human tissues relative to pancreatitis fibroblasts.

Thus, P and PDAC organoid/PSC co-cultures only partially recapitulate in vivo fibroblast gene expression differences in pancreatitis and PDAC, suggesting that additional stromal cell types may be needed to better capture fibroblast phenotypes seen in vivo.

Cross-model analysis identifies epithelial markers of pancreatitis and PDAC

Previous work showed that PSCs generate only a subset of PDAC CAFs, with pancreatic fibroblasts and mesothelial cells also serving as CAF precursors6,8,13,41–43. We reasoned that pancreatitis fibroblasts may also arise from multiple origins. Indeed, mouse pancreatitis snRNA-seq revealed four fibroblast subsets, two of which expressed mesothelial markers (for example, Wt1) (Extended Data Fig. 3i). We therefore optimized organoid co-culture models to better mimic in vivo fibroblast heterogeneity in pancreatitis and PDAC.

During PSC line generation, we also established mixed-fibroblast and mesothelial cell lines from normal pancreata of untreated C57BL/6J mice (Extended Data Fig. 7i). In monolayer culture, mixed-fibroblasts resembled PSCs, whereas mesothelial cells showed cobblestone-like morphology44 (Extended Data Fig. 7j). Mixed-fibroblast and mesothelial lines expressed fibroblastic (Acta2, Col1a1, Pdgfra and Pi16) and mesothelial (Msln, Wt1 and Krt19) markers, respectively, and lacked immune, endothelial, pericyte and epithelial markers (Extended Data Fig. 7k–m). Both lines had higher CD200 than PSCs, especially mesothelial cells, and showed higher major histocompatibility complex type II (MHCII) after interferon-γ (IFNγ) stimulation (Extended Data Fig. 7n). Together, these data suggest that mixed-fibroblast lines comprise fibroblasts, mesothelial cells and, potentially, PSCs.

To model epithelial crosstalk with heterogeneous stroma, we co-cultured P or PDAC organoids with green fluorescent protein (GFP)+ PSCs, TdTomato+ mixed-fibroblasts and TdTomato+ mesothelial cells, hereafter ‘multi-stroma’ co-cultures (Fig. 7a,b and Extended Data Fig. 8a–c). PSC numbers significantly increased in P, but not PDAC, multi-stroma co-cultures relative to multi-stroma cultures (that is, stromal cells cultured without organoids), indicating that pancreatitis epithelial cells promote PSC expansion more effectively than PDAC malignant cells even in complex stroma (Fig. 7c). PSCs also outnumbered mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells in both co-cultures, and P co-cultures had higher stroma-to-epithelial ratios than PDAC co-cultures, driven mainly by PSC expansion (Fig. 7c,d and Extended Data Fig. 8d,e).

We next assessed how multi-stroma shaped P and PDAC organoid transcriptomes by scRNA-seq of co-cultures containing GFP+ PSCs, TdTomato+ mixed-fibroblasts, TdTomato+ mesothelial cells and P or PDAC organoids (Fig. 7e and Extended Data Fig. 8f). Consistent with the epithelial heterogeneity observed in mouse tissues, we identified two P organoid clusters (‘P organoid 1’ and ‘P organoid 2’) and one PDAC organoid cluster (Fig. 7e). The PDAC malignant signature was higher in PDAC than P organoids, and higher in P organoid 1 than P organoid 2 cells, while pancreatitis Ductal 1 markers were enriched in P organoid 1 compared with P organoid 2 cells (Extended Data Fig. 8g,h). Proliferation-associated pathways were upregulated in PDAC organoids relative to P organoid 2 cells, whereas ECM-associated and inflammatory signatures were enriched relative to P organoid 1 cells (Fig. 7f and Supplementary Table 13). Thus, while inflammatory signatures are higher in P than PDAC organoids in monoculture, stromal co-culture reverses this pattern, inducing PDAC organoid inflammatory programmes that mirror those enriched in PDAC relative to pancreatitis epithelial cells in tissues. This suggests that stromal input is required for in vivo-like inflammatory pathway activation in PDAC malignant cells.

We then compared P and PDAC organoid markers from multi-stroma co-cultures with epithelial markers from tissues using our cross-model and cross-species strategy. P organoid markers from multi-stroma co-cultures overlapped with mouse pancreatitis Ductal 1 and Ductal 2 markers, whereas PDAC organoid markers from multi-stroma co-cultures overlapped with mouse PDAC malignant markers (Supplementary Table 8). P and PDAC organoid orthologues from multi-stroma co-cultures also overlapped with human pancreatitis ductal and PDAC malignant orthologues, respectively (Supplementary Table 8).

Because PSC and multi-stroma co-cultures both captured in vivo epithelial profiles, we integrated transcriptomic datasets from mouse models and both co-culture systems to define shared epithelial markers. This identified 177 pancreatitis ductal markers (for example, Sox9 and Stat1) and 254 PDAC malignant markers (for example, Muc5ac and Cd44) (Supplementary Table 14). Multiplex IF confirmed higher MUC5AC+CK19+ and CD44+CK19+ epithelial cells in mouse PDAC than pancreatitis tissues, whereas SOX9+CK19+ and STAT1+CK19+ epithelial cells were enriched in pancreatitis (Fig. 7g). Because PDAC genetics can alter malignant cell features28,29, we tested whether marker differences between pancreatitis and KRASG12D p53-mutant (KP) PDAC were maintained in other genetic contexts. We analysed orthotopic KRASG12D p53-null (KΔP) or SMAD4-deficient KP (KPΔS) PDAC models (Fig. 7h). MUC5AC+CK19+ and CD44+CK19+ epithelial populations were also higher, though less markedly, in KΔP and KPΔS PDACs than pancreatitis (Extended Data Fig. 8i). STAT1+CK19+ cells were moderately increased in pancreatitis compared with these PDACs, while SOX9+CK19+ cells were significantly enriched in pancreatitis (Fig. 7i and Extended Data Fig. 8i). Thus, PDAC malignant cells across genetic contexts share features that distinguish them from inflamed non-malignant epithelial cells, while retaining genotype-specific profiles.

Altogether, combining in vitro and in vivo models enables identification of epithelial markers differentially enriched in pancreatitis and PDAC tissues.

We next asked whether pancreatitis and PDAC epithelial cells differentially reprogramme stromal cells of distinct origins. PDAC organoids induced myofibroblastic and inflammatory signatures across PSCs, mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells (Fig. 8b). However, cell type-specific responses were evident. Mixed-fibroblasts and PSCs, but not mesothelial cells, downregulated additional inflammatory pathways when cultured with PDAC versus P organoids (Fig. 8b). Mixed-fibroblasts, but not PSCs or mesothelial cells, also downregulated interferon responses and upregulated collagen-associated pathways when cultured with PDAC versus P organoids. These mixed-fibroblast-specific changes were also seen in PDAC CAFs versus pancreatitis fibroblasts from mouse tissues, suggesting that mixed-fibroblasts contribute to in vivo gene signatures.

CellChat analysis suggested that PDAC malignant cells communicate more with PSCs, but less with mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells, than P ductal cells (Extended Data Fig. 8j). It also revealed changes in TGF-β, FN1 and IL-1 pathways (Extended Data Fig. 8k). Within the IL-1 pathway, Il1a was inferred as the dominant malignant-to-stromal ligand in PDAC organoid/multi-stroma co-cultures, supporting our organoid monoculture and organoid/PSC co-culture findings.

To understand how mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells help mirror in vivo myofibroblastic enrichment, we treated PSCs, mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells with TGF-β or IL-1α to induce myofibroblastic or inflammatory markers, respectively33. Mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells were more responsive to TGF-β, upregulating myofibroblastic markers (Tgfb1, Ctgf, Ncam1, encoding CD56) more strongly than PSCs (Extended Data Fig. 9a). Conversely, PSCs were more responsive to IL-1α, showing greater induction of inflammatory markers than mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 9b). Consistently, STAT3 activation, a downstream effector of IL-1 signalling in PDAC iCAFs33, was higher in PSCs than in other stromal cells after IL-1α treatment (Extended Data Fig. 9c). STAT3 activation was also higher in PSCs cultured with PDAC than P organoid-conditioned media, whereas mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells showed the opposite pattern (Extended Data Fig. 9d). Thus, mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells are less responsive to IL-1α, favouring enrichment of myofibroblastic features in PDAC.

Our data suggest that other P organoid-secreted factors may promote inflammatory phenotypes, including JAK/STAT activation, in mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells. We focused on leukaemia inhibitory factor (LIF), a potent STAT3 activator previously shown to mediate STAT3 activation in PDAC iCAFs33. Lif expression was higher in P organoid monocultures and co-cultures than in PDAC counterparts, and was higher in pancreatitis Ductal 2 cells than Ductal 1 cells or PDAC malignant cells (Extended Data Fig. 9e,f). LIF treatment robustly induced STAT3 activation in both mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells (Extended Data Fig. 9g). Together, these data show that stromal cells of distinct origins respond differently to epithelial-derived cues in pancreatitis and PDAC, underscoring the importance of stromal heterogeneity in disease modelling.

We next tested whether P and PDAC organoid/multi-stroma co-cultures better capture fibroblast markers from mouse and human tissues using our cross-model and cross-species strategy. We identified genes upregulated in P versus PDAC multi-stroma (‘P multi-stroma markers’) and in PDAC versus P multi-stroma (‘PDAC multi-stroma markers’) and overlapped these with pancreatitis fibroblast and PDAC CAF markers, respectively. Both overlaps were larger than those observed for PSC co-cultures: 48% of P PSC markers (n = 438/905) (Fig. 6f) and 60% of P multi-stroma markers (n = 1,006/1,684) (Fig. 8c) overlapped with pancreatitis fibroblast markers (Supplementary Table 8). Similarly, only 15% of PDAC PSC markers overlapped with PDAC CAF markers (n = 174/1,155; P = 0.798) (Fig. 6g), whereas 31% of PDAC multi-stroma markers did so significantly (n = 220/707; P = 4.88 × 10−25) (Fig. 8d and Supplementary Table 8). Human cross-species comparisons showed similar significant overlap of P PSC orthologues and P multi-stroma orthologues with human pancreatitis fibroblast orthologues, whereas PDAC multi-stroma orthologues, unlike PDAC PSC orthologues, significantly overlapped with human PDAC CAF orthologues (Supplementary Table 8). We then used organoid/multi-stroma and mouse datasets to identify fibroblast markers enriched in pancreatitis (for example, PDGFRA and CD34) or PDAC (for example, ACTA2, NCAM1, TNC, FN1 and RUNX1) for protein validation (Supplementary Table 14). Multiplex IF confirmed increased PDGFRα+αSMA−PDPN+ fibroblasts in pancreatitis and αSMA+PDGFRα−PDPN+ fibroblasts in PDAC (Fig. 8e). FN1+PDGFRα−αSMA+ fibroblasts were also more abundant in PDAC than pancreatitis, whereas FN1+αSMA−PDGFRα+ fibroblasts were not significantly different, findings validated in KΔP and KPΔS PDAC tissues (Fig. 8e and Extended Data Fig. 9h–j). RUNX1+PDPN+, TNC+PDPN+ and CD56+PDPN+ fibroblasts were enriched in PDAC relative to pancreatitis regardless of SMAD4 status, and CD34+PDPN+ fibroblasts were enriched in pancreatitis across all PDAC comparisons (Fig. 8f,g and Extended Data Fig. 9k,l). In human PDAC and matched adjacent chronic pancreatitis tissues, CD34+αSMA−PDGFRα+ fibroblasts were enriched in chronic pancreatitis, while FN1+PDGFRα−αSMA+ fibroblasts were enriched in PDAC (Fig. 8h and Supplementary Table 1). Thus, organoid/multi-stroma co-cultures better recapitulate disease-specific in vivo fibroblast heterogeneity than organoid/PSC co-cultures and help identify discrete stromal populations.

Given that chronic pancreatitis is a PDAC risk factor, we asked whether inflammation-reprogrammed stromal cells respond differently to PDAC-derived signals than naïve stromal cells. We established mixed-fibroblast lines from pancreatitis mice (‘P-mixed-fibroblasts’) and confirmed their elongated morphology and absence of immune, endothelial, pericyte or epithelial markers (Extended Data Fig. 10a–d). Similar to naïve mixed-fibroblasts, P-mixed-fibroblasts expressed higher myofibroblastic and mesothelial markers than PSCs (Extended Data Fig. 10e). Unlike naïve PSCs, mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells, P-mixed-fibroblasts upregulated inflammatory markers more strongly in response to P than PDAC organoid-conditioned media (Extended Data Figs. 10f–h and 7g). We then compared flow-sorted multi-stromal populations from sequential P-to-PDAC co-cultures (‘P-to-PDAC multi-stroma’) with those from standard P or PDAC multi-stroma co-cultures (Extended Data Fig. 10i and Supplementary Table 15). Along the P-to-PDAC transcriptional axis, P-to-PDAC multi-stroma occupied an intermediate state between P and PDAC multi-stroma (Extended Data Fig. 10j). P-to-PDAC multi-stroma upregulated myofibroblastic and inflammatory signatures compared with P multi-stroma but remained less myofibroblastic than PDAC multi-stroma (Extended Data Fig. 10k,l). Thus, pancreatitis fibroblasts can be reprogrammed by malignant cues while retaining features of their inflammatory state.

To infer potential stromal cells of origin, we mapped in vitro-derived PSCs, mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells from P or PDAC co-cultures onto in vivo pancreatitis fibroblast or PDAC CAF references using semi-supervised scANVI. In pancreatitis, Fibro 1 and Fibro 4 strongly overlapped with mesothelial cells (85%), with minor similarity to mixed-fibroblasts (7–11%) or PSCs (4–8%) (Fig. 8i). Fibro 2 was most similar to mixed-fibroblasts (51%), whereas Fibro 3 showed similarity to both mixed-fibroblasts (33%) and PSCs (38%) (Fig. 8i). Pseudotemporal analysis further suggested a stronger connection between mixed-fibroblasts and Fibro 3 (Fig. 8j). PDAC CAFs instead showed a more composite signature, and pseudotemporal analysis suggested a stronger connection between mixed-fibroblasts and PDAC CAFs (Fig. 8k,l). Although in vivo lineage tracing will be needed to define stromal origins definitively, these findings support multiple potential origins for pancreatitis fibroblasts and PDAC CAFs.

In summary, P and PDAC organoid/multi-stroma co-cultures better capture in vivo fibroblast gene expression in pancreatitis and PDAC and highlight potential contributions of distinct stromal cell types to fibroblast populations in tissues.

Discussion

Our study uncovers previously unappreciated aspects of fibroblast heterogeneity and epithelial cell–fibroblast crosstalk in mouse and human pancreatitis and PDAC. We show that PDAC and pancreatitis differ in epithelial and fibroblast features, with myofibroblastic signatures enriched in PDAC CAFs, and that multi-stromal organoid co-cultures better capture disease-relevant epithelial and fibroblast states than organoid/PSC co-cultures. These models identify disease-specific epithelial reprogramming of stromal cells of distinct origins, infer potential in vivo origins of PDAC and pancreatitis fibroblasts, and support protein-level validation of cross-species and cross-model markers enriched in PDAC or pancreatitis.

Human chronic pancreatitis samples without PDAC are rare, and available scRNA-seq/snRNA-seq datasets often lack epithelial cells or fibroblasts, underscoring the value of mouse and in vitro models. Our cross-species analyses showed that mouse models and multi-stroma co-cultures capture many, but not all, human disease features, suggesting that some human fibroblast programmes, including inflammatory signatures in PDAC CAFs, remain underrepresented. These differences may reflect immune composition, tumour kinetics, inflammation duration, malignant cell states, mouse age or genetics, all of which can shape epithelial and fibroblast features and epithelial–stromal crosstalk28,32,45,46. Future studies should therefore further incorporate genetic and age-related diversity.

Because chronic pancreatitis increases PDAC risk, defining disease-specific epithelial–fibroblast crosstalk could inform PDAC interception, diagnosis and therapy. Our data suggest that pancreatitis fibroblasts can be reprogrammed by malignant cues while retaining a more inflammatory and less myofibroblastic state, potentially reflecting an intermediate stromal state during PDAC evolution in inflamed pancreas. Although further in vivo studies are needed, our findings suggest that malignant–stromal interactions differ depending on whether PDAC arises in normal or inflamed tissue. PDAC stromal-specific markers also appear more consistently conserved across genetic profiles than malignant cell-specific markers when compared with pancreatitis, supporting stromal biomarkers for early PDAC detection in these high-risk patients. We also identify candidate epithelial–fibroblast signalling axes, including IL-1 and LIF/STAT3, whose combined targeting could be tested for effects on PDAC progression, interception or prevention, especially as reducing chronic pancreatitis may lower PDAC risk47,48. Multi-stromal organoid co-cultures may provide tractable platforms for testing such targets and probing stromal-to-stromal crosstalk. Future versions could extend culture duration, adjust stromal ratios, distinguish mesothelial cells and mixed-fibroblasts fluorescently, and include additional PDAC genotypes plus endothelial and immune cells.

We used caerulein-based models because they are widely used to study pancreatitis in mice, but additional models, including GEMMs carrying hereditary pancreatitis mutations, could complement this work20,49,50. We focused on 6-week caerulein treatment because it produced stronger fibrosis and ADM, but shorter acute pancreatitis regimens also alter fibroblast and epithelial transcriptomes51. Whether epithelial–fibroblast crosstalk differs between acute and prolonged pancreatitis remains important, because chronic, but not acute, pancreatitis increases PDAC risk in humans52. Analysing caerulein-treated KC GEMMs could also clarify which epithelial-to-fibroblast signals depend on oncogenic mutations, given that PDAC genetics shape epithelial–stromal communication28,46.

In PDAC, PDGFRα and αSMA mark iCAF- and myCAF-enriched states, respectively53. Here, PDGFRα+αSMA− fibroblasts were enriched in pancreatitis, whereas αSMA+PDGFRα− fibroblasts were enriched in PDAC, consistent with more inflammatory pancreatitis fibroblasts and more myofibroblastic PDAC CAFs. Myofibroblastic features may therefore be more malignancy-specific, but therapeutic targeting requires caution: broad depletion of collagen I or αSMA+ cells could promote PDAC progression54,55, whereas selective targeting of myCAF subsets could reduce metastasis and therapy resistance7,56. Our study also links pancreatitis fibroblast subsets to markers with known disease roles: WT1 is enriched in Fibro 1 and Fibro 4, and WT1+ stromal cells can contribute to tumour-promoting PDAC CAFs57; Fibro 4 co-expresses Muc16 and interferon-response genes; Fibro 1 expresses Il34; Fibro 3 expresses Cxcl12 and Vegfc, suggesting immunomodulatory and angiogenic roles58; and Pi16HighPdgfraHigh Fibro 2 may correspond to progenitor-like universal fibroblasts43.

MyCAF and iCAF states first identified in PDAC were later found in other malignancies, suggesting that insights from pancreatitis fibroblast heterogeneity may inform other inflammatory diseases, and vice versa. Fibroblast heterogeneity is also described across inflammatory conditions59–63. For example, in rheumatoid arthritis, Fap and Pdpn mark fibroblasts broadly, whereas Thy1 expression defines populations with distinct roles59. In pancreatitis fibroblasts, Thy1 and Fap are heterogeneous, whereas Pdpn is more uniform, suggesting that future perturbation studies may reveal shared fibroblast functions across inflammatory diseases.

Methods

Ethical regulations

Animal procedures were reviewed by the Cancer Research UK Cambridge Institute (CRUK-CI) Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Body, approved by the UK Home Office and conducted under project license PP4778090 in accordance with institutional and national regulations. Patients’ specimens were acquired and analysed under the Pancrea_tive protocol (reference: REC21/WM/0119), which was approved by the West Midlands – Black Country Research Ethics Committee.

Human tissues

Human tissues for histological analyses were obtained from surgical resections performed at Addenbrooke’s Hospital (Cambridge, UK). Written informed consent was acquired from patients before specimens’ acquisition. Participants were not compensated. Samples were anonymized to the researchers. Available clinical information and pathology annotations are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Mouse models

Male and female C57BL/6J mice (strain number 632, Charles River, RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories. Male and female C57BL/6J-background KrasLSL-G12D/+; Trp53R172H/+; Pdx1-Cre (KPC) GEMMs23 were bred in-house and palpated from 2 months of age to monitor tumour formation, which was confirmed by ultrasound-based imaging. All animals were housed according to the UK Home Office Code of Practice for the Housing and Care of Experimental Animals under strict barrier conditions, maintaining defined microbiological health status and absence of ectoparasites. Mice were kept at 21 °C and 55% relative humidity on a 12-h light/dark cycle, with expanded rodent diet (Labdiet) and filtered water ad libitum. Environmental enrichment included nesting material, a retreat area and chew blocks.

To model pancreatitis and acute pancreatitis, 8–9-week-old C57BL/6J mice were treated with the cholecystokinin analogue caerulein (C9026, Sigma-Aldrich) in endotoxin-free Dulbecco′s PBS (TMS-012-A, Sigma-Aldrich). To model acute pancreatitis, nine male mice were administered 100 μg kg−1 buprenorphine (57446/4003, Virbac) subcutaneously 1 h before 6–8 hourly intraperitoneal injections of 75 μg kg−1 caerulein per day for 2 days (modified from Lugea et al.64). Then, 48 h after the first caerulein dose on the second day of treatment, samples were harvested. To model pancreatitis, 17 male and 5 female mice were treated with 250 μg kg−1 caerulein twice daily for 6 weeks.

To establish orthotopic transplantation mouse models of PDAC, single cells (10,000 cells per mouse) prepared from organoid cultures were resuspended as a 35 μl suspension of 50% Matrigel in PBS and injected into the pancreas of 8–10-week-old male (all KPΔS, all KΔP and all but two KP) or female (two KP for multiplex IF) C57BL/6J mice. KPΔS PDAC organoids were established from KPC GEMM−derived PDACs that had undergone LOH of the Trp53 WT allele29, and were CRISPR-modified to delete Smad428. KΔP PDAC organoids (T113) were derived from KrasLSL-G12D/+; Trp53fl/fl; Pdx1-Cre (KPPC) GEMM PDACs29. No maximal tumour size/burden is defined in our project licence, mice are euthanized when they show severe clinical signs (for example, lethargy, hypothermia) or before.

Amylase and lipase activity

Mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane, cardiac puncture was performed and blood was collected in Multivette 600 K3E tubes containing EDTA (15.1671, Sarstedt). Plasma was isolated by centrifugation at 9,600g for 6 min at 4 °C, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Amylase and lipase activity levels in plasma were measured using kits from Abcam (Ab102523 and Ab102524, respectively).

Normal, pancreatitis and PDAC organoids

All mouse PDAC organoids used in this study have undergone LOH of the Trp53 WT allele29, apart from the KPC organoid line used in Extended Data Fig. 6h. Pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia KC organoids (C57BL/6J-background) were previously described29. PDAC KPC organoids (C57BL/6J-background) T6-LOH, T8-LOH and GB-T12-LOH were previously described7,29. New mouse normal pancreas (GB-N21-23, GB-N25 and GB-N27), pancreatitis (GB-P1-5) and PDAC (GB-T11-LOH and GB-T13-LOH) organoids were established from male mice7,29. In brief, C57BL/6J-background male caerulein-treated pancreata or KPC tumours were digested with collagenase type XI (C9407, Sigma-Aldrich) and dispase (17105-041, Gibco). A few modifications were applied to generate pancreatitis organoids34. GB-T11-LOH and GB-T13-LOH were cultured with 10 μM Nutlin-3a (SML0580, Sigma-Aldrich) from passage 0 (ref. 7). Please note that contrary to other PDAC organoids used in this study, GB-T11-LOH organoids retain expression of p16. Organoids were propagated in complete media35 at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

PSCs, mixed-fibroblasts, mesothelial cells and P-mixed-fibroblasts

Mouse PSCs (SV40-immortalized, C57BL/6J-background, PSC4, PSC5 and GB-PSC23) were previously described and were established from the normal pancreata of untreated C57BL/6J mice4,7. Il1r1 KO (PSC4 G1, guide 5; PSC5 C9, guide 2) PSCs were also previously described7,33. New mouse pancreatic mixed-fibroblasts (GB-Fibro27, GB-Fibro28, GB-Fibro39 and GB-Fibro40) and mesothelial cells (GB-PSC36-M-H11, GB-PSC36-M-G1 and GB-PSC36-M-G3) were obtained while generating additional PSC lines from the normal pancreata of untreated male and female C57BL/6J mice, respectively. In brief, for each line, two and a half pancreata from C57BL/6J mice were run on a density gradient centrifugation method with Histodenz (D2158, Sigma-Aldrich) and Gey’s Balanced Salt Solution (G9779, Sigma-Aldrich)4. While PSCs were established from the layer that forms in between the two interphases, mixed-fibroblasts were established by outgrowth from the pellets following centrifugation. Mesothelial cells were established by enriching for cobblestone-like cells found in mixed-fibroblast preparations by single-cell plating in 96-well plates. P-mixed-fibroblasts (GB-P-Fibro1 to 5) derive from the pancreata of pancreatitis mouse models and were established by outgrowth of settled partially undigested material while generating pancreatitis organoids (from the supernatant). Mixed-fibroblasts, mesothelial cells and P-mixed-fibroblasts were immortalized using the pLVX-SV40 LT-IRES-tdTomato plasmid from Professor Tuveson4. PSCs, mixed-fibroblasts, mesothelial cells and P-mixed-fibroblasts were cultured in DMEM (41966029, Gibco) containing 5% FBS at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cell line authentication for stromal cell lines was performed using short tandem repeat analysis at the CRUK-CI in 2023 (PSC4 and PSC5) and 2025 (GB-Fibro27, GB-Fibro28, GB-PSC36-M-H11 and GB-PSC36-M-G1). The results confirmed 92% similarity across these cell lines. Mycoplasma testing was performed before each freezing and always tested negative.

In vitro cell treatments and organoid-conditioned media experiments

PSCs, mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells were treated in Matrigel in reduced media (that is, 5% FBS/DMEM), organoid-conditioned media or reduced media with 20 ng ml−1 human TGF-β1 (T7039, Sigma), 1–2 ng ml−1 mouse IL-1α (400-ML-005/CF, R&D Systems) and 5 ng ml−1 mouse LIF (250-02-25UG, ThermoFisher) for as long as specified in the figure legends. PSCs were cultured with P or PDAC organoid-conditioned media with 3 μg ml−1 IL-1α neutralizing antibody (MAB4001, R&D Systems; RRID:AB_2124216) or 3 μg ml−1 IgG isotype control (400902, BioLegend) for as long as specified in the figure legends.

Reverse transcription qPCR analyses

RNA (200 ng to 1 μg) of cell lines, PDAC organoids and mouse liver tissues was reverse transcribed using TaqMan reverse transcription reagents (N808 0234, Applied Biosystems). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using gene-specific TaqMan probes (ThermoFisher) (Supplementary Table 16) and TaqMan master mix (4440040, Applied Biosystems).

ELISA

Conditioned media samples were collected from P and PDAC organoids cultured alone or in co-culture with PSCs in reduced media for 3.5 days. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) were used to detect mouse IL-1α (MLA00, R&D Systems) and TGF-β1 (BMS608-4, ThermoFisher).

Organoid growth assays

For organoid growth assays, 5,000 single cells were plated in 50 μl of 100% Matrigel on 24-well plates (Corning/Nunc) and cultured in 500 μl of complete media35. Growth was followed for 120 h with an Incucyte organoid module (Sartorius) with four measurements of the organoid area per condition every 3 h. Data were normalized to the first measurement on day 0.

Western blot analyses

Cells were harvested in Cell Recovery Solution (354253, Corning) supplemented with protease inhibitors (11836170001, Roche) and phosphatase inhibitors (4906837001, Roche) and incubated for 30 min on ice. Cells were pelleted at 1,500g for 5 min and lysed in 0.1% Triton X-100, 15 mmol l−1 NaCl, 0.5 mmol l−1 EDTA, 5 mmol l−1 Tris, pH 7.5 supplemented with protease inhibitors (11836170001, Roche) and phosphatase inhibitors (4906837001, Roche). Cells were incubated on ice for 30 min, briefly vortexed and pelleted at 16,800g for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration of the collected supernatant was determined using the DC protein assay (5000113-5, Bio-Rad). Standard procedures were used for western blotting. Primary antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 16. Total proteins were blotted after stripping the respective phosphorylated proteins. In brief, membranes were incubated with a stripping buffer (100 mM glycine with 1% SDS in water, pH 2.5) on a shaker for 20 min at room temperature, washed three times with 0.1% Tween 20 in TBS before blocking and following standard procedures for western blotting. Proteins were detected using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). Western blots shown are representative examples and have been repeated with at least two lines per condition.

Immunohistochemical and histological analyses

For flow cytometric analysis, cells were stained with anti-mouse CD326 (EpCAM)-AlexaFluor 488, PDPN-APC/Cy7 and LIVE/DEAD Fixable Blue Dead Cell stain. Cells were resuspended in PBS with DAPI and sorted with a BD FACSMelody cell sorter.

For cell sorting of P, PDAC and P−to-PDAC organoid/multi-stroma co-cultures, we used GFP+ Rosa26 KO PSCs carrying the LentisgRNA-EFS-GFP-neo (LRGN) plasmid7 and TdTomato+ mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells, which were immortalized using the pLVX-SV40 LT-IRES-tdTomato plasmid4. For P-to-PDAC co-cultures, P organoids were co-cultured with 30,000 stromal cells as above. To establish P-to-PDAC co-cultures, PDAC organoids were co-cultured with 30,000 flow-sorted stromal cells (10,000 PSCs and 20,000 combined mixed-fibroblasts/mesothelial cells) per Matrigel dome for 3.5 days in reduced media before processing and sorting as organoid/PSC co-cultures.

Bulk RNA-seq analyses

Samples were collected in 1 ml of TRIzol Reagent (15596018, Invitrogen). Although only two PSC lines were used, these samples do not represent simple technical replicates. Each co-culture condition involved a distinct PSC–organoid pairing, while PSC monocultures were derived from independent flow-sorting experiments using lines at different passages. RNA was extracted using the PureLink RNA mini kit (12183018A, Invitrogen). RNA concentration was measured using a Qubit and RNA quality was assessed on a TapeStation 4200 (Agilent) using the Agilent RNA ScreenTape kit. mRNA library preparations were performed using 55 μl of 10 ng ml−1 per sample (RNA integrity number ≥8.8). Illumina libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq6000 except for cells from organoid/multi-stroma co-cultures, which were sequenced on a NovaSeqX. Raw FASTQ files were aligned to the GRCm38 (release 109, kmer 23) reference genome using the quasi-alignment tool Salmon67. The resulting transcript quantification files were combined, and transcript-level estimates were aggregated to the gene level using the R package tximport68. Genes with fewer than ten total counts across all samples were excluded from downstream analyses. Differential expression analysis (DEA) was performed using the R package DESeq269. Genes with an adjusted P value <0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the R package clusterProfiler70 against the Hallmark, Reactome and C2 canonical pathway collection (C2.cp.v5.1) downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB)71. Genes were pre-ranked on the basis of the metric: −log10(P value) × sign(log2(fold change)). Pathways were considered significantly enriched at a false discovery rate <0.25 and an absolute normalized enrichment score (|NES|) >1.5. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed using the GSVA R package72, utilizing the average GSVA score across samples per condition.

Analyses are included in Supplementary Table 10 (organoids in complete media), Supplementary Tables 11 and 12 (organoids and PSCs from co-cultures) and Supplementary Table 15 (organoids and multi-stroma from co-cultures).

To quantify the transcriptional state transition among P, P-to-PDAC and PDAC multi-stroma conditions, a transcriptional axis between P and PDAC was defined using the variance-stabilized transformation from DESeq269. Centroids representing the mean expression profile for each condition were computed, and the vector connecting the P and PDAC centroids was used to define the direction of transcriptional change. Individual samples were projected onto this axis via scalar projection, yielding a continuous score representing their relative position between the P and PDAC transcriptional states. Higher projection scores indicate greater similarity to the PDAC state, whereas lower scores indicate greater similarity to the P state.

scRNA-seq analyses of organoid/multi-stroma co-cultures

PDAC or P organoids were co-cultured with 30,000 stromal cells (10,000 each of GFP+ Rosa26 KO PSCs and TdTomato+ mixed-fibroblasts and mesothelial cells) per Matrigel dome for 3.5 days in reduced media, as described in the ‘Flow cytometry and cell sorting’ section. 10x Genomics 3′ V4 4-Plex (that is, on-chip multiplexing; OCM) libraries were generated following the 3′ OCM protocol utilizing recommended reagents and conditions. Libraries were quality controlled via Qubit and TapeStation then pooled to 10 nM, loaded via qPCR targeting the P5/P7 adaptors and sequenced to a depth of ~40,000 reads per cell on the NovaSeqX Plus device. Each OCM FASTQ file contains four samples. To demultiplex them, CellRanger (10x Genomics) ‘multi’ function was used following the recommended workflow and a modified Mouse mm10 (2020-A) reference genome that included the reporter genes, Tdtomato and Gfp. From the resulting output, the raw_feature_bc_matrix files were used as input for CellBender to remove ambient RNA, with an fpr of 0.01 (ref. 73). Each sample underwent standard quality control using Scanpy74. For preliminary quality control filtering, low-quality cells and outliers were excluded on the basis of total counts (1st and 99th percentiles), gene counts (<100 genes) and mitochondrial gene content (>5%). Doublets were detected, and later removed, using the SOLO model implemented in scvi-tools27,75. Finally, all samples were concatenated into a single Anndata object and integrated using scvi-tools27 using ‘SampleID’ as a batch key. After integration, data was clustered using the Leiden algorithm based on the top 2,000 highly variable genes and the top 30 PCs76. Cell clusters were annotated on the basis of known gene marker and reporter gene expression. DEA was applied using the model-based analysis of single-cell transcriptomics (MAST) method77, incorporating sample ID and batch ID (when applicable) as a latent variable. Genes for GSEA were ranked on the basis of the log2 fold change derived from this analysis. Analyses are included in Supplementary Table 13.

Single-nuclei isolation and RNA-seq of mouse tissues

Single nuclei isolation of pancreatitis and PDAC tissues was performed as previously described26. We submitted 35K nuclei for PDAC and 70K for pancreatitis tissues. These samples were processed using the same pipeline for OCM samples above. To validate malignant cell identity, copy number variation (CNV) analysis was performed using a python implementation of inferCNV (Trinity CTAT Project; https://github.com/broadinstitute/inferCNV) to estimate the copy number status across cell types with a fibroblast cell cluster as reference and a 250-gene window size. Metadata and analyses are included in Supplementary Tables 6 and 7.

NicheNet

NichenetR78 was applied to bulk RNA-seq and snRNA-seq data. Genes of interest were defined from the corresponding DEA as significant DEGs (adjusted P <0.05, |log2fold change|>1).

CellChat

CellChat R package79 was utilized on Seurat objects with default recommended settings.

Mapping stromal populations to in vivo fibroblasts

To map transcriptional correspondence between in vitro stromal cell populations and in vivo pancreatitis fibroblast or PDAC CAF states, we used a probabilistic framework based on scVI and scANVI, implemented via scArches27,80. A reference model was trained on in vivo fibroblast and CAF subclusters using 4,000 highly variable genes (a latent dimension of 20) and extended to a scANVI classifier with subcluster labels and an ‘Unknown’ placeholder to maintain label consistency. In vitro stromal cells were analysed separately for pancreatitis and PDAC conditions. Each query AnnData object was initialized as ‘Unknown’, projected into the reference latent space via scArches and fine-tuned for 20 epochs. For each in vitro cell, soft label probabilities indicated transcriptional similarity to in vivo fibroblast or CAF subtypes. In addition, in vivo pancreatitis fibroblast subclusters were projected onto in vivo CAF references to assess potential state transitions. Cluster-level mean probabilities were summarized as normalized confusion-matrix heat maps. All analyses were done on a Mac M1 processor and the accelerator was set to ‘mps’.

PAGA

Pseudotemporal trajectories of annotated cell types were inferred using partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA)81, implemented in Scanpy via the sc.tl.paga function.

Gene signature scoring

Marker gene lists were identified for multiple cell clusters from the scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq datasets using the rank_genes_groups function in Scanpy and ‘wilcoxon’ as the preferred method74. Genes with P < 0.05 and expression in at least 10% of cells within the target cluster were retained. Gene lists used are included in Supplementary Tables 2, 6,7 and 9. The AUCell method that is implemented in the decoupleR python library was used to enrich gene lists on sc/snRNA-seq data31,82.

Visium HD spatial transcriptomics of mouse tissues

Image processing and spatial registration

Histological and CytAssist images were prepared following 10x Genomics Space Ranger specifications. Whole-slide H&E images in SVS format were converted to TIFF format at full resolution (downsampling factor of 1) using QuPath65 to preserve native pixel density. Owing to morphological variability and sample handling inconsistencies, manual alignment between CytAssist images and H&E images was performed to ensure precise spatial correspondence. The resulting spatial registration transformation matrix was exported as a JSON file and provided as input to the Space Ranger pipeline.

Read alignment and spatial binning

Raw FASTQ files, paired with registered CytAssist and H&E images, were processed using Space Ranger v4.0.1 (10x Genomics). Sequencing reads were aligned to the mm10 (2020-A) reference genome using the Mouse Probe Set v2 for feature quantification. To enhance signal robustness while maintaining near single-cell resolution, gene expression counts were aggregated into 8-µm spatial bins for all downstream analyses.

Quality control and deconvolution analysis

Spatially aware quality control was performed using SpotSweeper83 to identify and remove low-quality capture locations. Default parameters were applied, adjusting the number of nearest neighbours (k = 48) to accommodate the square grid geometry of the Visium HD platform. Cell-type deconvolution was performed using the Python package flashdeconv84 with default parameters to estimate cellular composition within individual capture locations. To maximize tissue-specific resolution, context-dependent reference datasets were used. PDAC samples were deconvolved against a mouse PDAC scRNA-seq dataset32; normal pancreatic tissues utilized a mouse endocrine cell scRNA-seq dataset (control samples)85 combined with non-malignant cells from a mouse PDAC scRNA-seq dataset32; pancreatitis samples utilized the mouse snRNA-seq dataset generated in this study.

Downstream analysis

Downstream normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering and visualization, were performed using standard spatial transcriptomics workflows in Scanpy. Spatial data handling and visualization were conducted using Squidpy86 alongside the spatialdata, spatialdata-io and spatialdata-plot Python packages87. For DEA of fibroblast cluster spatial bins, a pseudobulk aggregation approach was applied. Spatial bins assigned to fibroblasts were subsetted and raw counts were aggregated at the sample level. The DEA was performed using default parameters of decoupleR and PyDESeq269,82,88. GSEA was performed as done for bulk RNA-seq. Metadata and analyses are included in Supplementary Table 5.

scRNA-seq of human chronic pancreatitis and PDAC tissues

Raw FASTQ files of four human chronic pancreatitis (excluding the autoimmune sample) and five human PDAC (untreated) samples were obtained from Dimitrieva et al.19 and are accessible at the database of Genotypes and Phenotypes (dbGaP) under study accession number phs003751.v1.p1. The sample metadata and dataset analysis are presented in Supplementary Table 2. scRNA-seq analysis was performed as above but using the reference genome GRCH38 (refdata-gex-GRCh38-2024-A).

snRNA-seq of human chronic pancreatitis and PDAC tissues

Count matrices of four human PDAC and three human chronic pancreatitis samples were obtained using two different publicly available datasets (‘Data availability’ section) and were downloaded and read into Scanpy. Preliminary quality control filtering was performed as done for the organoid/multi-stroma co-cultures. Samples were integrated using scVI, with ‘DataID’ used as a batch key. Cells were clustered using Leiden clustering based on 3,000 highly variable genes and the top 30 PCs and annotated on the basis of known markers. The sample metadata and dataset analysis are included in Supplementary Table 3.

Pathway enrichment analysis

Enricher function from clusterprofiler70 was used to perform pathway enrichment analysis on genes (based on EntrezID) shared by in vivo mouse and human PDAC CAFs or in vivo mouse and human pancreatitis fibroblasts, as well as on genes unique to human PDAC CAFs or human pancreatitis fibroblasts relative to their mouse counterparts (Extended Data Fig. 4e). Gene lists are included in Supplementary Table 8.

Hypergeometric tests and the Jaccard index

Hypergeometric tests, odds ratios and the Jaccard index were calculated for overlapping genes between two gene lists using the GeneOverlap R package and are listed in Supplementary Table 8. The universe was set to 18,000 (for mouse comparisons) or 16,076 (for non-redundant mouse-to-human orthologues for cross-species comparisons) to represent the number of curated protein-coding genes and including CDKN2A and CDKN2B (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/GeneOverlap.html).

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses, data processing and visualization were carried out using R, Python, bash, Morpheus (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus) and GraphPad Prism software. Statistical analyses between two groups were performed using non-parametric two-sided Mann–Whitney tests. Statistical analyses between three or more groups were performed using a non-parametric one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc controlling for multiple comparisons with the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. These statistical methods are non-parametric and therefore do not require assumptions of normality or equal variances. The Jaccard index and hypergeometric test were used to calculate the significance of overlaps. All statistical details of experiments are specified in the figure legends and/or panel figures, including how significance was defined and how many independent experimental replicates where performed, when relevant. Exact P or q values (calculated in R) are provided in the Source data whenever figure panels report <0.0001 (calculated in GraphPad Prism). Each measurement/replicate (n) shown corresponds to a distinct sample (that is, individual mice, cell lines, organoid lines, cell/organoid pairs or different passages of the same organoid/cell line, as indicated in the figure legends) unless otherwise specified. Potential covariates (for example, batch ID, organoid type and cell line) were addressed and modelled as fixed effects within the analytical design (DESeq2 formula design) or regressed out to account for known sources of technical variation during batch-effect correction. A file containing numerical source data not included in Supplementary Tables 1–16 is available. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were determined on the basis of similar prior studies showing they were sufficient for biologically meaningful conclusions7,28. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were typically neither randomized nor carried out blindly (for example, because pancreatitis and PDAC models are clearly distinguishable) to avoid swapping of organoid lines. Randomization was performed for mice treated with caerulein or PBS. Data analyses were blind whenever possible (for example, multiplex IF analyses across PDACs with distinct genetics).
