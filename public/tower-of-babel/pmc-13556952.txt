Nanoemulsion adjuvanted COBRA HA vaccine enhances protective antibody titers against H1N1 and H3N2 influenza viruses
Authors: Matthew H Thomas, Ted M Ross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdjuvants can enhance the elicited immune responses to recombinant hemagglutinin (HA) proteins following intramuscular vaccination. In this study, computationally optimized broadly reactive antigen (COBRA) designed HA proteins were adjuvanted with the W805EC oil-in-water nanoemulsion (“NE01”) and used to vaccinate mice. The elicited influenza-specific humoral and cellular immune responses were evaluated. Mice vaccinated with adjuvanted COBRA H1 and H3 HA vaccine candidates had higher antibody titers compared to mice vaccinated with unadjuvanted HA proteins. Mice administered COBRA HA plus NE01 had higher hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) specific antibody titers against a panel of H1N1 and H3N2 influenza viruses, as well as higher levels of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secreting splenocytes. Mice vaccinated with adjuvanted COBRA HA proteins had statistically less weight loss following challenge, as well as undetectable viral lung titers compared to mice administered unadjuvanted vaccine.KEYWORDS: Influenza, hemagglutinin, adjuvant, antibodies

Introduction

Influenza viruses cause significant morbidity and mortality, particularly in young children, the elderly, pregnant women, or immunocompromised individuals.1,2 Vaccination is the most effective strategy to reduce the impact of influenza virus infection and is critical for combating influenza virus infection and disease.3,4 However, vaccine effectiveness, as well as the magnitude and quality of the vaccine-induced immune responses, varies considerably depending on the vaccine platform, formulation, antigen content, route of administration, and host immune history, particularly prior influenza exposure through infection or vaccination.5–7 Adjuvants are important for improving the quality and breadth of immune response to influenza vaccination.8 The hemagglutinin (HA) protein is the major surface glycoprotein of the virus responsible for mediating entry into respiratory epithelial cells. Anti-HA head neutralizing antibodies block HA binding to sialic acid receptors with hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) titers serving as the major correlate of protection.9–12

Current commercial influenza vaccines are split-inactivated viruses, recombinant hemagglutinin (HA) protein, or live-attenuated influenza virus vaccines13,14 with variable effectiveness varying each year, ranging from 10–60% protective efficacy.15 These commercial vaccines need to be reformulated annually, largely due to the frequent mutations (antigenic drift) of these surface glycoprotein hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA).16 The vaccine strains can be mismatched to circulating influenza strains and result in less effective protective immune responses.17 Computationally optimized broadly reactive antigen (COBRA) methodology was developed to address this issue and generate a broadly reactive HA proteins using a layered consensus sequence approach from thousands of human clinical isolates to capture conserved epitopes.18 These recombinant COBRA-based H1 and H3 HA proteins elicit antibody responses that recognize many viral strains over multiple seasons.19–21

In order to enhance the effectiveness of elicited immune responses, recombinant protein‑based vaccines can be mixed with adjuvant(s) that enhance immune responses to an antigen by increasing antigen uptake and presentation by immune cells.22 Only a few adjuvants have been approved for human use, but several experimental compounds are currently under investigation.23 Emulsion adjuvants are manufactured by high-speed emulsification of ionic and nonionic surfactants, ethanol, soybean oil and purified water.24 The NE01 nanoparticle emulsion formulation is thermostable at various temperatures and induces strong systemic and mucosal immune responses as an intranasal adjuvant for seasonal influenza vaccines.24,25

In this study, NE01 was formulated with COBRA HA proteins to enhance protective immune responses against H1N1 and H3N2 influenza HA proteins administered intramuscularly to vaccinate mice. Overall, mice vaccinated with rHA plus NE01 adjuvant had statistically higher hemagglutination-inhibition (HAI) and neutralizing titers, as well as broader T cell responses. Mice were protected against viral infection with no detectable virus in the lungs compared to mice vaccinated with unadjuvanted rHA protein. Overall, the NE01 nanoemulsion was effective as an intramuscular adjuvant for recombinant hemagglutinin vaccination.

Materials and methods

Vaccine antigens

COBRA HA antigens, Y2 and NG5, were formulated with NE01 adjuvant and used to vaccinate mice intramuscularly. The NG5 COBRA HA was designed from wild-type HA sequences that circulated from October 2018 to April 2021.26 The Y2 H1 COBRA HA was designed from wild-type HA sequences that circulated from May 2014 to September 2016, as previously described.20 Each full-length, 566 amino acid, COBRA HA sequence was modified for recombinant protein expression by removing the transmembrane domain of the HA and replacing it with a T4 fold-on domain, an AviTag, and a 6x Histidine tag. The modified sequence was then cloned into the pcDNA3.1+ plasmid vector (Azenta Life Sciences, Burlington, MA, USA). Each individual plasmid was then transfected into HEK293T suspension cells (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) to generate soluble recombinant HA (rHA) protein as previously described.27 In brief, the soluble proteins were collected and purified from cell culture supernatants via immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC), and the concentration of each rHA was determined using conventional bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) kits (Thermo Fisher). To assess purity and size, 250 ng of each His-tagged rHA trimer was subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) for 30 min at 200 V and stained with Coomassie blue (Thermo Fisher). The molecular weights of the experimental and control bands fell within the expected range of ~60 to 90 kDa for His-tagged trimeric HA. Each recombinant HA was also assessed for HA specificity by a Western Blot probing with an influenza group 1 specific mouse monoclonal antibody (Immune Technology Corp, NY, USA, clone 15B7, Cat#IT-003–001M14) or a group 2-specific mouse monoclonal antibody (Immune Technology Corp, clone 34C9, Cat#IT-003–0423M13) at 1:2000, and a secondary horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat-anti-mouse IgG (Southern Biotech, AL, USA) antibody at 1:4000. Visualization was achieved using Clarity ECL substrate (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) and the membranes were imaged using a Chemi-Doc imaging system (Bio-Rad) as previously described.27

Propagation of influenza viruses

Influenza viruses used in this study have been previously described.28 H1N1 Influenza viruses used in this study include: A/Singapore/6/1986 (SG/86), A/California/07/2009 (CA/09), A/Michigan/45/2015 (MI/15), A/Brisbane/02/2018 (BR/18), A/Guangdong/SWL1536/2019 (GD/19) A/Victoria/2570/2019 (VC/19), and A/Victoria/4897/2022 (VC/22). The H3N2 Influenza viruses used in this study include: A/Panama/2007/1999 (PN/99), A/Switzerland/9715293/2013 (SW/13), A/Kansas/2017 (KS/17), A/Switzerland/2017 (SW/17) A/Hong Kong/2671/2019 (HK/19), A/Tasmania/503/2020 (TS/20), A/Darwin/9/2021 (DR/21) and A/Massachusetts/18/2022 (MA/22).

Pre-immunization infection and/or vaccination

DBA/2 (female, 6 to 8-week-old) mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and housed in microisolation cages with ad libitum access to feed and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Cleveland Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC approval #2935). Mice were infected intranasally with SG/86 (1 × 104 plaque-forming units [pfu]/50 μL) H1N1 and PN/99 (1 × 105 pfu/50 μL), H3N2 influenza viruses in 50 µL sterile PBS (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Mice were weighed daily for 5 d post-infection to monitor disease progression. At 30 d post-infection, mice were vaccinated intramuscularly with 3 mg of the next‑generation influenza recombinant proteins Y2 and NG5 HA protein without an adjuvant or formulated with 60% preprepared solution diluted 1:3 using provided formulation buffer of the oil-in-water nanoemulsion adjuvant, NE01 (BlueWillow Biologics, Ann Arbor, MI, USA).29 The NE01 adjuvant was manufactured under engineering specifications by high-shear homogenization of water, ethanol, cationic and non-ionic surfactants, and highly refined soybean oil to form an oil-in-water nanoemulsion with mean particle size of approximately 460 nm.29 For mock-vaccination, mice were intramuscularly inoculated in 50 µL sterile 1x PBS mixed with adjuvant. Mice were boosted with the same vaccine and adjuvant combinations at day 21. Blood samples were collected at days 14, 34, 41 and spleens (n = 4) were collected at day 30. On day 42, mice were challenged with BR/18 (H1N1; 4.1 × 104 pfu/50 µL). Mice were weighed and monitored for signs of morbidity and mortality for 14 d post-challenge. Lungs (n = 3 per vaccine group) were collected at 3 d post-challenge (dpc) for determination of viral lung titers. To determine virus titer, Cumulative clinical scores (CS) were reported for each animal as previously described.28 Mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane until unconscious and euthanized with CO2 by inhalation for 3 min and then confirmed by cervical dislocation.

Serological assays

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) was used to confirm immunogenicity of the COBRA vaccine as well as to measure IgG1 and IgG2a titers against the COBRA vaccine antigens as previously described.28,30 The hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) assay was used to evaluate HA-directed serum antibodies using a protocol adapted from the World Health Organization (WHO) Manual for the laboratory diagnosis and virological surveillance of influenza31 and as previously described.28,30

Microneutralization assay

Prior to the assay, pooled sera samples from each vaccine group were prepared and heat‑treated at 56°C for 45 min. On the day of the assay, each well of a 96-well flat‑bottom microtiter plate (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) was filled with 50 μL of virus diluent (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin, 13.5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA), 2.5% 1 M HEPES buffer, 2 μg/mL TPCK trypsin (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). An additional 40 μL of virus diluent was added to the first column of microtiter plate, followed by the addition of 10 μL of pooled heat-treated serum for an initial 1:10 serum dilution. Two-fold serial dilutions of 50 μL were transferred across the plate to column 10, discarding the final 50 μL. Next, 50 μL of virus which has been previously titered to 100x the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (100x TCID50) was added to each well excluding column 12. An extra 50 μL of virus diluent was added to column 12 to establish negative control wells. The plates were then incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Following incubation, 100 μL of Madin-Darby canine kidney MDCK-SIAT-1 cells at a concentration of 1.5 × 105-2.0 × 105 cells/mL in virus diluent were added to every well of the plate. The plates were then incubated at 37°C + 5% CO2 for 18–20 h. The next day, the media was removed from the plates and each well was washed with 200 μL of PBS. The PBS was then removed, and the wells were fixed with ice-cold fixative solution (80% Acetone diluted in PBS and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The fixative solution was then decanted, and the plates were allowed to air‑dry for 1 h. The plates were then washed three times with 200 μL wash buffer (PBS + 0.03% Tween-20). The plates were then decanted and 100 μL of rabbit anti-influenza-A-NP polyclonal antibody (1 mg/mL, Cat. no. PA5–81661, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) diluted 1:3000 in blocking buffer (PBS + 0.03% Tween-20 + 14.5% BSA) was added to each well. The plates were then moved to a humidified chamber to incubate at RT for 1 h. The plates were then washed three times with 200 μL wash buffer. After washing, the plates were decanted and 100 μL of goat-anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) HRP (1 mg/mL, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) diluted 1:2000 in blocking buffer was added to each well. After the addition of the secondary antibody, the plates were moved to a humidified chamber and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Following incubation, the plates were washed five times with 200 μL wash buffer. The wash buffer was then decanted and 100 μL of substrate (one phosphate citrate tablet (Sigma, Saint Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in 100 mL diH2O, followed by the addition of five o-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (OPD) tablets (Sigma, Saint Louis, MO, USA), and 50 μL of 30% H2O2) was added to each well and allowed to develop for 3–5 min at room temperature. Once a color change started to occur in the negative control wells, column 12, 100 μL of stop solution (2N H2SO4 (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA)) was added to each well. The plates were then read at 492 nm using a BioTek Epoch 2 plate reader (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with Gen6 software. The 50% neutralization titer was determined by averaging the optical density (O.D.) values of the virus control wells, column 11, minus the background signal, average of column 12, and using that value as the 100% infection titer. The values in the experimental wells were then compared to the 100% infection O.D. value, and a 50% inhibition titer was calculated for each experimental group using a linear interpolation of the Log2 serum titers above and below the 50% neutralization O.D. value. All plates were run in duplicate, and the neutralization titers were averaged between the plates.

Antibody secreting cell ELISpot

Spleens collected from vaccinated mice were homogenized into single‑cell suspensions, resuspended in Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher) +10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Thermo Fisher), and stored at −80°C as previously described.30 Antibody Secreting Cell ELISpot was performed as previously described.30 Spots were imaged using a CTL ImmunoSpot analyzer (CTL) and counted using ImmunoSpot software version 7·0·38·2 Professional SC.

IFN-γ secreting cell ELISpot

Synthetic peptides

About 14 and 15-mer peptides from Influenza A/New York/18/2009 (H1N1) pdm09 (Catalog No. NR-19245) and Influenza Virus A/Perth/16/2009 (H3N2) (Catalog No. NR-19266) were obtained from BEI Resources. The 70-peptide array overlapping by seven amino acids spans the whole sequence of the HA protein of the strains A/New York/18/2009 (H1N1) pdm09 (GenPept: ACU13097) and A/Perth/16/2009 (H3N2) (GenPept: ACS71642.1). Each odd-numbered peptide (peptide1 to peptide139) (n = 70) from a given protein was combined in 20 mapping sub-pools as previously described at a final concentration of 2 µg/mL per peptide in assays.32

Enzyme-linked cytokine immunospot assay

T-cell enzyme-linked cytokine immunospot (ELISpot) assays were performed using Mouse ELISpot Kit from Cellular Technology Limited (CTL) (Shaker Heights, OH, USA – Catalog No. mIFN-γ/IL4–1 M/10) as previously described.30 The data was acquired using the CTL ImmunoSpot® reader (CTL, Shaker Heights, OH, USA) and analyzed with ImmunoSpot® Version 7 software. Each spot corresponds to an individual cytokine-secreting cell. Background responses from negative control wells were subtracted to normalize the data across all experimental groups.

Detection of viral lung titers by plaque assay

Virus titers in the lungs of infected mice were evaluated at 3 d post-challenge via the viral plaque assay. To perform this assay, Madin-Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) cells were plated in 6-well cell culture plates with 2 ml of MDCK cell medium (100 U/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin, 10% FBS, and 25 mM HEPES in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)) and allowed to incubate at 37°C with 5% CO2 until they reached confluency. At that time, frozen lung tissue was thawed and homogenized in 1 ml of wash medium (DMEM containing 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 µg/ml streptomycin) by muddling the tissue through a 70 µm nylon cell strainer using the plunger of a syringe. Each sample homogenate was serially diluted 10-fold in duplicate across a microtiter plate in Wash Medium. After the MDCK cells were washed three times with 2 ml of Wash Medium, 100 µl of sample dilutions (undiluted to 1 × 10−5 dilution) were added to 100 µl of Wash Medium in each well. The cells were incubated at room temperature for 1 h with intermittent agitation. Then, the cells were washed twice more and overlaid with 2 ml of 1.6% agarose combined 1:1 with 2x plaque medium (2x MEM amino acids, 40 mM HEPES, 2x L-glutamine, 50 mg/ml gentamicin, 17.86 mM NaHCO3, 1 µg/ml TPCK-Trypsin, 200 U/ml penicillin and 200 µg/ml streptomycin). To allow plaque formation, the cells were incubated for 2–4 d at 37°C with 5% CO2. Once visible plaques formed in the wells, the overlays were removed, and the cells were fixed with 2 ml of 10% neutral buffered formalin for 10 min, stained with 1% crystal violet, and washed with water. The number of plaques in each well was determined, and the virus titers for each sample were defined as the number of plaque-forming units (PFU)/ml of lung homogenate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). One-way ANOVA was used to analyze lung virus titers after influenza viral challenge and to analyze ELISpot counts between vaccine groups. Two-way ANOVA was used to analyze the total IgG antibody titers for each of the vaccine antigens between all vaccine groups and to analyze influenza strain-specific HAI titers between vaccination groups. Weight loss, survival, and clinical scores following BR/18 or SW/13 challenge were also analyzed using two-way ANOVA.

Results

Assessment of serological responses following vaccination

The goal of this study was to formulate a bivalent COBRA rHA vaccine with NE01 oil-in-water adjuvant to enhance immune responses following intramuscular vaccination. Naïve DBA/2J mice were infected with two historical influenza viruses to simulate pre-existing immunity to influenza. These mice were then administered two vaccinations of the Y2 H1 COBRA HA mixed with the NG5 H3 COBRA HA with or without NE01 adjuvant (Figure 1). All mice seroconverted to SG/86 and PN/99 following infection to establish a pre-immunity with hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) titers ranging from 1:80 to 1:1024 (Supplemental Figure 1).

Mouse vaccination groups and vaccination regimen. Mice (n = 12/group) were intranasally infected with H1N1 (SG/86) and H3N2 (PN/99) viruses or mock‑infected with PBS plus NE01 adjuvant on day 0. The animals were allowed to rest for 28 d and then were vaccinated intramuscularly at days 0 and 21 with COBRA rHA with or without NE01 adjuvant or mock‑vaccinated with PBS plus NE01. Blood was collected at days −14, 14, 34, and 41. Nine days following the final vaccination, spleens were harvested from four mice for analyses. Mice were challenged with the H1N1 influenza virus, A/Brisbane/02/2018 (BR/18), at day 42. Lungs were harvested from three mice at day 45 and the remaining mice (n = 5) were observed for morbidity until day 56.

Following vaccination, mice vaccinated with COBRA HA Y2 and NG5 proteins mixed with NE01 had higher anti-HA IgG binding titers following two vaccinations than mice vaccinated with COBRA HA only (Figure 2) that was predominately composed of IgG1 antibodies (Figure 3). There were no detectable anti-HA antibodies following the first vaccination. Antibody responses following the final vaccination were analyzed for hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) activity (Figure 4). Mice vaccinated with COBRA Y2 HA protein mixed with NE01 had, on average, seroprotective HAI titers (≥1:40) against all six H1N1 influenza virus strains isolated from 2009 to 2022, with almost all mice seroconverting following vaccination (Figure 4(A)). Whereas, mice vaccinated with unadjuvanted COBRA Y2 HA protein had 4–8 fold lower average HAI titers compared to mice administered adjuvanted COBRA Y2 HA vaccine. Approximately 50% or more of the mice did not seroconvert following vaccination.

Mice vaccinated with COBRA NG5 HA formulated with NE01 also had statistically higher HAI titers than mice vaccinated with unadjuvanted COBRA NG5 HA protein against a panel of H3N2 influenza viruses isolated from 2017 to 2023 (Figure 4(B)). On average, mice administered the adjuvanted NG5 HA vaccine seroconverted to four of the six H3N2 influenza viruses. In contrast, mice administered unadjuvanted vaccine have an average HAI of ≥1:40 against only the TS/20 H3N2 influenza virus with the average HAI titer against the other H3N2 influenza viruses ≥1:20 (Figure 4(B)).

Mice vaccinated with Y2 and NG5 HA proteins mixed with NE01 adjuvant had 2–4 higher neutralization titers against BR/18 and HK/19 influenza viruses compared to mice vaccinated with unadjuvanted COBRA HA proteins (Figure 5). Mice had similar neutralizing titers against TS/20 following COBRA HA vaccine with or without NE01 adjuvant. No neutralizing titers were detected in mock‑vaccinated mice.

Adjuvanted rHA vaccines elicited a higher frequency of antibody secreting antigen-specific splenocytes than unadjuvanted rHA vaccines

Spleens were collected 14 d following the second vaccination (day 30) and were assessed for H1 and H3 HA-specific antibody secreting cells (ASCs) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secreting cells (Figures 6 and 7). Mice vaccinated with the Y2 and NG5 COBRA rHA proteins and mixed with NE01 adjuvant had an average of ~40–60 ASCs per 1 million HA-specific ASCs (Figure 6). Mice vaccinated with unadjuvanted rHA proteins had 2–3-fold fewer HA-specific ASCs than mice vaccinated with adjuvanted HA vaccines (Figure 6).

NE01 adjuvanted HA COBRA vaccines elicited a higher frequency of IFN-γ secreting antigen-specific splenocytes than unadjuvanted HA vaccines

Spleens were collected 9 d following the second vaccination (day 30), homogenized, pooled per each vaccination group, and assessed for H1 and H3 HA-specific interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secreting cells (Figure 7). On average, mice vaccinated with COBRA rHA proteins adjuvanted with NE01 had statistically higher number of H1-specific IFN-γ secreting cells than mice vaccinated with unadjuvanted rHA (Figure 7). Mice vaccinated with COBRA rHA proteins adjuvanted with NE01 had 16 times more H1 HA-specific IFN-γ secreting cells than mice vaccinated with unadjuvanted rHA (Figure 7(A)) and four times more H3 HA-specific IFN-γ secreting cells than mice administered unadjuvanted vaccine (Figure 7(B)).

Influenza virus challenge

Mice were challenged with the BR/18 (H1N1) influenza virus and assessed for morbidity and mortality (Figure 8). Mock‑vaccinated mice challenged rapidly lost weight and had, on average, 86% of their original weight by day 3 post-challenge (Figure 8(A)) with high viral titers ranging from ~10^3 to 10^5 pfu/mL (Figure 8(B)). All mice vaccinated with Y2 HA plus the NE01 adjuvant survived BR/18 challenge with little weight loss (>5% of original body weight) and no clinical symptoms. Mice that were vaccinated with unadjuvanted vaccine had similar weight loss as mice vaccinated with adjuvanted vaccine (Figure 8(A)). However, there were no detected viral lung titers in any vaccinated mouse following this infection, but one unadjuvanted vaccinated mouse had high viral lung titers following infection (Figure 8(B)).

Discussion

Current influenza vaccines primarily target the surface glycoprotein hemagglutinin (HA), since the elicitation of protective antibodies against HA is crucial to neutralizing virus infections.16 The first approved recombinant HA (rHA) vaccine, Flublok, is an egg-free HA vaccine product approved for administration to adults. The HA is expressed using insect cells and the vaccine contains three times the dose of wild-type H1N1, H3N2, and influenza B HA proteins compared to standard split-inactivated influenza vaccines. In general, recombinant protein-based vaccines often need to use either higher doses of antigen or the inclusion of an adjuvant to reduce the dose and effectiveness of the vaccine, particularly in the elderly or immunocompromised populations.23

Commercial influenza vaccines include the recommended circulating H1N1, H3N2, and influenza B viral antigens in vaccine formulation each season.33 These influenza vaccine components are updated every 1–2 y due to antigen drift of circulating influenza strains7. Our group developed a methodology called computationally optimized broadly reactive antigen (COBRA) to generate in-silico designed HA sequences.18,34,35 The COBRA methodology incorporates HA sequences from recently circulating influenza viruses to generate various consensus sequences. Over multiple rounds, these sequences are layered to build a consensus HA sequence that contains epitopes from a variety of strains.18,36,37 The presentation of these epitopes enhances vaccine‑induced immune responses by recalling a more heterogeneous population of memory cells compared to vaccines comprised of wild-type specific components.38,39 The COBRA HA vaccines effectively protect against influenza virus‑induced disease and death.20,21,40,41

In this study, COBRA H1 and H3 HA proteins were recombinantly expressed as protein subunits and formulated with the NE01 nanoemulsion adjuvant. When NE01 was delivered with COBRA HA intramuscularly to mice, the vaccine elicited HA-specific IFN-γ T-cells. NE01 is an oil-in-water nanoemulsion adjuvant that was designed for intranasally delivered vaccines to enhance immune responses by facilitating antigen uptake, promoting mucosal immunity (IgA), and activating Th1/Th17 cellular pathways.42,43 This adjuvant enables efficient antigen trafficking to lymphoid tissues, often activating toll-like receptors (TLRs) and the NLRP3 inflammasome to stimulate balanced systemic and mucosal immune responses.42 When NE01 is mixed with a recombinant protein, the adjuvant can induce both mucosal and cell‑mediated immunity to elicit full protection against SARS‑CoV-2, H5N1 influenza, and herpes simplex-2 virus.24,29,44,45 Since people are exposed early in life to influenza viruses through vaccination and/or infection, these vaccine candidates were evaluated in mice exposed to historical H1N1 and H3N2 influenza viruses.46,47 Mice vaccinated with these COBRA HA antigens and mixed with NE01 had enhanced HA-specific antibodies, HAI and neutralizing titers compared to mice that received unadjuvanted vaccine. These immune responses were similar to immune responses observed in mice vaccinated with these vaccines formulated with the MF59-like adjuvant, Addavax,48 indicating that the NE01 adjuvant is as effective as this type of adjuvant approved for human use. In both models, the adjuvant increased the antibody titers against a panel of H1N1 and H3N2 influenza viruses and stimulated stronger antigen‑specific cellular immune responses than unadjuvanted COBRA HA subunit vaccines without using higher HA doses in the mouse model. Overall, the addition of NE01 to these next-generation broadly reactive HA proteins may be an effective strategy to enhance immunity and reduce the dose of the vaccine and maintain a single‑dose regimen each season.
