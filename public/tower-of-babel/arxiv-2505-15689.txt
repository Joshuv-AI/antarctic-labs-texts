Weighted Lagrange Multiplier Method for Robust Source-Independent Waveform Inversion Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE
Ali Gholami, Kamal Aghazade, Akshay Vishwakarma

Abstract
The Lagrange multiplier method has proven highly effective for mitigating the ill-conditioning of full waveform inversion (FWI), enabling robust and computationally efficient algorithms that converge to accurate velocity models even from poor initial estimates. Classical multiplier-based FWI methods optimize an augmented Lagrangian (AL) functional with a scalar penalty parameter that uniformly weights wave-equation constraint violations. While this balances data fit and wave-equation satisfaction, it applies uniform relaxation across the model, disregarding source locations and the natural decay of seismic energy. We propose a weighted proximal-point Lagrangian formulation that introduces spatially varying regularization, applying weaker enforcement near sources and progressively stronger enforcement with increasing distance. This compensates for the energy decay, promotes balanced wave-equation enforcement, and improves the convexity of the optimization landscape. The method also eliminates the need for explicit source signature estimation and relaxes the requirement for sources to lie on finite-difference grid points, increasing practical applicability. Enhanced computational efficiency is achieved through our dual-space ADMM implementation, which avoids repeated LU factorizations of the forward operator. Only a few LU factorizations are required, with all subsequent iterations solved via efficient forward–backward substitution, making the approach scalable to large-scale 2D and 3D problems. Numerical experiments on challenging synthetic benchmarks demonstrate that the proposed method broadens the basin of attraction of the AL objective, improves robustness to poor initial models and strong noise, and achieves faster, more stable convergence compared with standard multiplier-based methods.

Introduction.

Full waveform inversion (FWI) is an advanced PDE-constrained optimization method for high-resolution imaging of subsurface physical properties such as velocity, density, and anisotropy. These parameters appear as coefficients in the wave equation, which governs seismic wave propagation. In the frequency domain, the monochromatic forward wavefield
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, generated by a point source at
\mathbf{x}_{s}
in a medium with spatially varying squared slowness
\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, satisfies the Helmholtz equation

\Delta\mathbf{u}(\mathbb{x})+\omega^{2}\mathbf{m}(\mathbb{x})\mathbf{u}(\mathbb{x})=\delta(\mathbb{x-x}_{s})f(\omega),

(1)

where
\omega
is the angular frequency,
\Delta
is the Laplacian,
f(\omega)
is the source signature, and
\delta
is the Dirac delta. Sampling the solution at receiver locations via a restriction operator
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{r}\times n}
yields the recorded data
\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}
. FWI seeks model parameters
\mathbf{m}
that minimize the data misfit while satisfying the wave equation, leading to the PDE-constrained optimization problem

\minimize_{\mathbb{u,m}}~\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{d}\|_{\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}}^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbb{A(m)u}=\mathbf{b},

(2)

where
\|\mathbf{d}\|_{\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}}^{2}=\mathbf{d}^{\!\top}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}\mathbf{d}
denotes a weighted
\ell_{2}
-norm, and
(\cdot)^{\top}
represents the Hermitian (conjugate) transpose.
The matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}
is a positive-definite weighting matrix that scales the relative contribution of each data component in the misfit term.
Here,
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{m})=\omega^{2}\text{diag}(\mathbf{m})+\boldsymbol{\Delta}
is the discretized Helmholtz operator, and
\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x})=\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s})f(\omega)
is the source term.
For clarity, the formulation is presented for a single source with fixed receivers, though the extension to multiple sources or nonstationary acquisitions is straightforward.

1.1
Challenges of FWI.

Despite its high resolution, FWI faces several well-known challenges
[
39
,
54
,
53
,
50
,
29
,
46
,
40
,
14
,
19
,
35
,
11
]
:

(i)

Nonconvexity and cycle skipping: When the starting model is too far from the truth, the predicted data can be out of phase by more than half a cycle (cycle skipping), causing convergence to local minima.

(ii)

High computational cost: Each iteration requires solving large PDE systems, demanding multiple LU factorizations or iterative solutions, leading to significant computational and memory cost.

(iii)

Limited data coverage: Low-frequency and long-offset data—critical for recovering the smooth background velocity—are often sparse or missing due to acquisition constraints.

(iv)

Noise sensitivity: Field data is contaminated by unknown noise, which complicates inversion and causes instability.

(v)

Unknown source signature: Accurate knowledge of the source wavelet
f(\omega)
is crucial for waveform matching. Its misestimation can bias the solution and introduce artifacts.

(vi)

Grid-alignment requirement: Standard finite-difference discretizations require that sources and receivers coincide with grid points, which restricts modeling flexibility and can introduce significant numerical errors if the true acquisition geometry does not align with the grid, which is the case in practical applications. This limitation can also necessitate impractically fine grids or interpolation methods to accurately implement acquisition geometry.

Current Approaches and State of the Art.

Various strategies have been proposed to mitigate these issues. Reduced-space (or reduced) formulations eliminate
\mathbf{u}
by solving the wave equation exactly, leading to an optimization problem solely over
\mathbf{m}

[
39
,
34
]

,

[

]
and Wavefield Reconstruction Inversion (WRI)
[

]

[
46
]
.
Lagrangian and augmented Lagrangian (AL) formulations retain wavefields as independent variables but also introduce Lagrange multipliers to enforce the wave equation more strictly
[
26
,
38
,
2
]
. These multiplier-based methods combine the stability of penalty methods with the accuracy of constraint enforcement, improving robustness and reducing nonlinearity, but at the cost of additional variables and computational work.

Several efforts have focused on improving efficiency. Strategies include reusing LU factorizations over multiple iterations
[
1
,
8
]
and reducing the number of factorizations per frequency via iterative refinement
[
3
]
. Weighted norms for both data misfit and penalty terms have also been explored to improve convexity and convergence
[
41
,
33
]
.

Source signature estimation has been addressed by treating

[
16
,
4
]
. Alternatively, annihilator-based approaches
[
29
,
46
]
eliminate explicit source estimation altogether and promote focused source extensions, enhancing stability and robustness to initial models. However, these methods are still based on penalty formulations, which may limit their convergence speed and final accuracy

.

1.3
Contributions.

This work advances frequency-domain FWI within the framework of the multiplier method, a powerful approach for solving constrained optimization problems
[
23
,
12
]
. The multiplier method augments the classical Lagrangian with a quadratic penalty term, combining the stability of penalty methods with the rigorous constraint enforcement of Lagrange multipliers. When applied to FWI, the multiplier formulation can be expressed either in the source space
[
26
,
2
]
or the data space
[
40
,
19
,
20
]
, depending on whether source or data multipliers are used.

We build on the source-space formulation and introduce a computationally efficient, robust, and practically applicable weighted multiplier waveform inversion approach that is suitable for both the frequency domain and the time domain implementations. Our contributions are threefold:

(i)

General weighted proximal-point Lagrangian formulation.
We propose a general weighted proximal-point Lagrangian objective function that introduces explicit weighting operators for the data misfit, model regularization, and multiplier terms. This formulation provides a unified and flexible framework for controlling the relative influence of each component of the objective function. Crucially, the classical Lagrangian, penalty, and augmented Lagrangian formulations arise as special cases of this general framework, allowing us to recover and compare existing methods within a single unifying theory. Properly designed weighting strategies enable us to improve both the convexity and conditioning of the problem, leading to faster and more stable convergence.

(ii)

Spatially varying regularization for improved robustness and convexity.
Classical multiplier-based FWI employs a scalar penalty parameter
\mu
that uniformly weights wave-equation constraint violations
[
2
,
35
,
32
]
. We replace this scalar parameter with a spatially varying penalty function that increases with distance from the source. This weighting compensates for the natural decay of seismic energy, leading to more balanced enforcement of the wave equation. It brings the flexibility to focus the inversion to specific regions. This localization property allows to apply techniques like layer-stripping, where the model can be determined sequentially in small spatial subdomains. It result in improved convexity of the optimization landscape, a wider basin of attraction for the AL objective, and greater robustness to poor starting models and strong noise.

(iii)

Elimination of source-related practical constraints.
By incorporating distance-dependent penalty weights, the proposed method removes the need for explicit source signature estimation and relaxes the requirement that sources be placed exactly on finite-difference grid points. This significantly improves modeling flexibility and makes the method more suitable for realistic acquisition geometries where source and receiver locations rarely coincide with grid nodes.

(iv)

Dual-space formulation for computational efficiency.
We adopt a new dual formulation of the algorithm, where the focus is to estimate accurate Lagrange multipliers (dual variables)
[
5
]
. This eliminates explicit updates over model parameters, effectively framing the inversion as a source inverse problem. The resulting dual ADMM algorithms (e.g., Algorithms
6
and
7
) requires only a few LU factorizations of the forward operator per frequency, while subsequent iterations rely solely on efficient forward–backward substitutions, making the method scalable for large-scale inversions.

Comprehensive numerical experiments on challenging synthetic benchmarks demonstrate that the proposed approach improves robustness, accelerates convergence, and enhances computational scalability compared with standard FWI methods.

2
Method.

The Lagrange multiplier methods, enhanced with proximal regularization
[
42
]
, offers a robust algorithmic framework for constrained optimization problems such as (
2
). The proximal-point Lagrangian associated with (
2
) is defined as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{PL}}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{m};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},\mathbf{m}_{k})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{d}\|_{\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}}^{2}+\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbb{A(m)u}-\mathbf{b}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}_{k}\|_{\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}\|_{\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}}^{2}.

(3)

Here, the variable
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is the Lagrange multiplier (or dual variable), which simultaneously serves as the adjoint wavefield in the inversion process
[
37
]
and as a source-extension term that drives the forward wavefield
[
44
]
. These two wavefields — forward and adjoint — constitute the fundamental building blocks of the gradient and Hessian information, making
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
one of the most critical variables for updating the model and ultimately solving the inverse problem.
\mathbf{m}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}
are prior estimates of the model and multiplir, in the sense of proximal point methods
[
42
]
.
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
and
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}
are diagonal weighting matrices with positive weighting coefficients on the diagonal and zero elsewhere. The notation
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product.

The quadratic regularization (proximal) terms in (
3
) play a crucial role in stabilizing and regularizing the inversion problem and has an important convexification effect. Removing these terms reduces the objective function to the standard Lagrangian formulation
[
26
,
7
]
, which is often more difficult to optimize due to the non-smooth and potentially ill-conditioned nature of the Lagrangian term. The quadratic form of the proximal terms smooths the objective landscape, improves conditioning, and provides a natural mechanism for controlling step sizes. As a result, the proximal formulation generally exhibits better convergence properties and improved numerical stability compared with directly solving the original Lagrangian.

Relationships between different methods.

Several well-known objective functions can be derived from the proximal-point Lagrangian (

The standard augmented Lagrangian function

(4)

2.2
Optimization.

The main computational challenge lies in solving the following min-max problem

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+1},\mathbf{m}_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{u}}\max_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{PL}}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{m};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},\mathbf{m}_{k}).

(11a)

Various approaches can be adopted for this step. A natural starting point for such nonlinear problems is Newton’s method. This approach, which updates the model, wavefield, and multipliers simultaneously in an all-at-once manner, offers robust local convergence properties. This is done by performing an inner iterative loop, indexed by
l
, that updates
(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{m})
simultaneously as:

\mathbf{u}^{l+1}=\mathbf{u}^{l}+\delta\mathbf{u}^{l},\quad\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{l+1}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{l}-\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{l},\quad\mathbf{m}^{l+1}=\mathbf{m}^{l}+\delta\mathbf{m}^{l},

(12)

where the Newton direction
(\delta\mathbf{u}^{l},\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{l},\delta\mathbf{m}^{l})
satisfies the linear system

\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{PL}}(\mathbf{u}^{l},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{l},\mathbf{m}^{l};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},\mathbf{m}_{k})\begin{bmatrix}\delta\mathbf{u}^{l}\\
\delta\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{l}\\
\delta\mathbf{m}^{l}\\
\end{bmatrix}=-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{PL}}(\mathbf{u}^{l},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{l},\mathbf{m}^{l};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},\mathbf{m}_{k}).

(13)

The gradient and Hessian of
\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{PL}}
are defined as

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{PL}}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{m};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},\mathbf{m}_{k})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{P}^{\!\top}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{d})+\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{m})^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\\
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\\
\text{diag}(\omega^{2}\mathbf{u})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}_{k})\end{bmatrix}

(14)

and

\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{PL}}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{m};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},\mathbf{m}_{k})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{P}^{\!\top}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}\mathbf{P}&\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{m})^{\!\top}&\text{diag}(\omega^{2}\boldsymbol{\lambda})\\
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{m})&-\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}&\text{diag}(\omega^{2}\mathbf{u})\\
\text{diag}(\omega^{2}\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{\top}&\text{diag}(\omega^{2}\mathbf{u})^{\top}&\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}\end{bmatrix}.

(15)

This approach can be challenging for solving large-scale problems because the variables
\mathbf{u}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
are source dependent and must be kept in memory. The associated algorithm is summarized in Algorithm

2.5
Alternating direction methods.

Building on the fundamental Lagrangian formulations and their Newton-based solutions presented in Sections
2.2
–
2.4
, we acknowledge that while these methods provide robust convergence in principle, their direct application to large-scale FWI problems is often hampered by significant computational costs. Specifically, the repeated calculation and factorization of large, model-dependent Hessian matrices in each inner iteration (as observed in Algorithms
1
,
2
and
3
) can be prohibitively expensive due to high memory and processing demands. To overcome these challenges and achieve practical scalability for FWI, we now turn to Alternating Direction Methods (ADMs). These methods strategically decompose the complex, coupled optimization problem into a sequence of simpler subproblems. By judiciously fixing certain variables during inner iteration loops, ADMs allow for highly efficient updates that avoid the frequent re-factorizations characteristic of full Newton schemes, thereby significantly reducing computational demand and memory footprint.
The optimization problem (
11a
) can be decomposed into coupled saddle-point subproblems solved in sequence. The first subproblem is a saddle-point system in
(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, with the model
\mathbf{m}
held fixed. Once
\mathbf{u}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
are updated, we switch to a second saddle-point system in
(\mathbf{m},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, keeping
\mathbf{u}
fixed. This approach effectively solves the
3\times 3
block nonlinear system as a sequence of two coupled
2\times 2
systems, leading to the following iteration:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+\frac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle=\argminimax_{\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{PL}}(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{m}_{k};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},\mathbf{m}_{k})

(21a)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{m}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\argminimax_{\mathbf{m},\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{PL}}(\mathbf{u}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{m};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},\mathbf{m}_{k})

(21b)

where
(\mathbf{u}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+\frac{1}{2}})
is the saddle point of
\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{PL}}
for a fixed
\mathbf{m}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}
and

(\mathbf{m}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+1})
is the saddle point of
\mathcal{L}_{\texttt{PL}}
for the fixed values of
\mathbf{u}_{k+1}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}
.
In what follows, we explain how these subproblems can be solved efficiently.

(i)

Multiplier-oriented approach.

This formulation is based on the matrix inversion identity (

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{k+1}-\mathbf{b}]+\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}.

(32b)

Here, the dominant computational cost arises from solving for the forward wavefield, which requires inverting a sparse Hessian operator

\mathbf{u}_{l}
, the half-step multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\tfrac{1}{2}}
, and the model
\mathbf{m}_{l}
are updated in an inner loop (indexed by
l
), with
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}
held fixed. The inner loop is performed for a prescribed number of iterations
maxit
or until convergence. Once the inner loop terminates, the multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+1}
is updated using the final wavefield and model obtained from the inner loop.

(ii)

ADMM.

Here, only a single inner iteration is performed per outer step, so that the model, wavefield, and multiplier are updated once at each iteration
k
. In practice, this means that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+\frac{1}{2}}
,
\mathbf{u}_{k+1}
, and
\mathbf{m}_{k+1}
are computed sequentially from the current state
(\mathbf{m}_{k},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})
, after which the multiplier is updated to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+1}
. This synchronous update scheme avoids nested loops and typically is the most traditional form of ADMM, as summarized in Algorithm
5

[
2
,
35
,
19
]
.

(iii)

ADMM with frequent dual updates.
In this variant, the model
\mathbf{m}
is held fixed during the inner iterations, while the wavefield
\mathbf{u}
and the multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
are updated repeatedly. At each inner iteration
l
, a tentative model increment
\delta\mathbf{m}_{\,l+1}
is computed based on the current wavefield and half-step multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\,l+1/2}
, but the model itself is not immediately updated. The multiplier is then updated using the tentative model
\mathbf{m}+\delta\mathbf{m}_{\,l+1}
, allowing it to respond promptly to changes in the model direction. After completing the prescribed number of inner iterations
maxit
, the model is updated once using the final increment
\delta\mathbf{m}_{\texttt{maxit}}
. The corresponding steps are summarized in Algorithm
6
. In this variant, rather than updating the model immediately, the model corrections are accumulated across the inner iterations. Once the inner loop converges or reaches a predefined iteration limit
maxit
, the model is updated in a single step using the accumulated correction. This strategy enhances the accuracy of the forward-adjoint wavefields used in the model update, potentially improving the overall efficiency of the algorithm. Moreover, because the model remains fixed throughout the inner loop, the forward and adjoint wavefields can be refined using a single LU factorization of the forward operator associated with the current model, thereby maintaining computational efficiency.

3.1

\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}

The proximal regularization is controlled by the spatially varying weights
w_{\texttt{S}}(\mathbb{x})
(encoded in
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}

\mu

is to balance the amplitudes of the wave-equation residuals. Without such weighting, the standard
\ell_{2}

3.1.1
On the parameters
\sigma
and
\epsilon
.

These parameters are crucial for achieving robust inversion. In the following, we present practical strategies for selecting appropriate values.

The parameter
\epsilon
controls the behavior of the distance-weight function in the vicinity of the source location
\mathbf{x}_{\texttt{s}}
.
When
\epsilon\ll 1
, its effect on the shape of the weighting function
w_{\texttt{S}}
is negligible, but it has a strong influence on the inverse weighting function
w_{\texttt{S}}(\mathbf{x})^{-1}
, which directly regularizes the multipliers. Since these multipliers act as extended sources in the inversion,
\epsilon
provides the flexibility for concentrating their energy in specific regions of the space.

Our strategy for choosing
\epsilon
is motivated by the fact that propagating waves interfere constructively as long as the phase shift is smaller than
\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\texttt{w}}
, where
\lambda_{\texttt{w}}
is the wavelength of the propagating wave
[
43
]
. Consequently, we aim to impose stronger regularization within a neighborhood of radius
\frac{1}{4}\lambda_{\texttt{w}}
around the source. This penalization encourages the estimated extended source to remain concentrated in that region.
At the source location and at a distance of
\frac{1}{4}\lambda_{\texttt{w}}
, the regularization levels are given by

w_{\texttt{S}}(\mathbf{x}_{\texttt{s}})^{-1}=\epsilon^{-2}\quad\text{and}\quad w_{\texttt{S}}\!\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{\texttt{s}}\pm\frac{1}{4}\lambda_{\texttt{w}}\right)^{-1}=\left[1-(1-\epsilon)\exp\!\!\left(-\frac{\lambda_{\texttt{w}}^{2}}{32\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]^{-2}.

(46)

We define the amplitude ratio
\gamma>1
as

\gamma=\frac{w_{\texttt{S}}(\mathbf{x}_{\texttt{s}}+\frac{1}{4}\lambda_{\texttt{w}})}{w_{\texttt{S}}(\mathbf{x}_{\texttt{s}})},

(47)

and solving for
\epsilon
yields

\epsilon=\frac{\sinh{\!(\frac{\lambda_{\texttt{w}}^{2}}{64\sigma^{2}})}}{\gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}\,\sinh{\!(\frac{\lambda_{\texttt{w}}^{2}}{64\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\ln{\gamma})}}.

(48)

3.2
Design of
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}
.

This operator acts in the data space and rescales the contribution of each data component in the misfit term of the objective function. In a Bayesian framework, such weighting operators can be interpreted as the inverses of the data covariance operators
[
41
]

3.2.1
On selection of
w_{\texttt{D}}
.

A common strategy is to define
w_{\texttt{D}}(\mathbf{x})
as a function of the source–receiver offset, thereby emphasizing specific portions of the data. For example, to strengthen the contribution of large-offset data in crustal-scale imaging, one may use
[
36
]

w_{\texttt{D}}(\mathbf{x}_{\texttt{r}})=\exp(\beta\ln\|\mathbf{x}_{\texttt{r}}-\mathbf{x}_{\texttt{s}}\|_{2})=\|\mathbf{x}_{\texttt{r}}-\mathbf{x}_{\texttt{s}}\|_{2}^{\beta}

(49)

where
\beta
is a tuning parameter. This weighting reduces the relative influence of short-offset data, which typically contain limited information about deep structures, and enhances the sensitivity to long-offset information.

3.2.2
Selection of regularization parameter
\mu
.

The regularization parameter
\mu
, defining the data weighting matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}^{-1}(\mu)
(
39a
), must be carefully chosen to balance data fitting and wave-equation enforcement. As
\mu\to\infty
, the algorithm strictly enforces the wave equation, potentially at the cost of poor data fitting. In this limit, the wave equation is satisfied exactly, but the regularization effect vanishes. In this case, the algorithm reduces to conventional reduced-space FWI
[
39
]
. Conversely, as
\mu\to 0
, the algorithm emphasizes data fitting while effectively ignoring the wave equation.

In the weighted formulation of the algorithm, the data weighting matrix appears only in the first subproblem associated with estimating the adjoint wavefield, solving (
60
). Here we show how to properly determine an appropriate value for
\mu
.

(i)

Discrepancy Principle.

It can be determined adaptively using the discrepancy principle, which selects
\mu
to satisfy the condition
\|\mathbb{r}(\mu)\|_{2}^{2}=\delta^{2}
, where

\mathbb{r}(\mu)=\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}-\mathbb{S}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+1/2}^{\mu}

(50)

is the residual and
\delta
is an upper bound on the norm of the noise contaminating the data. However, the discrepancy principle requires prior knowledge of the noise level, which may not be available in practical scenarios.

(ii)

Generalized cross-validation
[
55
]
.

GCV is a widely used method for selecting the regularization parameter in discrete ill-posed problems without requiring prior knowledge of the noise level. Based on GCV, an optimal regularization parameter should provide good predictions of missing or unseen data values.
For a given forward operator
\mathbf{S}
and data residual
\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}
, the optimum parameter is defined as

\mu=\arg\min_{\mu}\phi_{\texttt{GCV}}(\mu)=\frac{\|\mathbb{r}(\mu)\|_{2}^{2}}{\left[\texttt{Trace}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{{\dagger}}\right)\right]^{2}}.

(51)

where
Trace
denotes the trace of a matrix and
\mathbf{S}^{{\dagger}}=\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\mathbb{S}^{\top}\left[\mathbb{S}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\mathbb{S}^{\top}+\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}^{-1}(\mu)\right]^{-1}
is the regularized inverse operator. At each iteration, the GCV function can be computed efficiently by using Generalized Singular Value Decomposition (GSVD).
The GSVD of the matrix pair
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\mathbb{S}^{\top}
and
\text{diag}(\mathbf{w}_{\texttt{D}}^{-1})
is given by:

\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\mathbb{S}^{\top}=\mathbf{U}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{S}}\mathbb{Z}^{\top},\quad\text{diag}(\mathbf{w}_{\texttt{D}}^{-1})=\mathbb{V}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}\mathbb{Z}^{\top},

(52)

where
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbb{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{r}\times n_{r}}
are unitary matrices,
\mathbb{Z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{r}\times n_{r}}
is nonsingular, and
\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{S}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n_{r}}
and
\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{r}\times n_{r}}
are diagonal matrices satisfying
\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{S}}^{\top}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{S}}+\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}^{\top}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}=\mathbb{I}
. Accordingly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{r}(\mu)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{{\dagger}})\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}

(53)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}^{-1}\left[\mathbf{S}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\mathbf{S}^{\top}+\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}^{-1}\right]^{-1}\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}

(54)

\displaystyle=\mu\mathbf{Z}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}^{\top}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}\mathbb{Z}^{\top}\left[\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{S}}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{S}}\mathbb{Z}^{\top}+\mu\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}^{\top}\mathbb{V}^{\top}\mathbb{V}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}\mathbb{Z}^{\top}\right]^{-1}\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}

(55)

\displaystyle=\mu\mathbb{Z}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}^{\top}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}\left[\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{S}}^{\top}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{S}}+\mu\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}^{\top}\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}\right]^{-1}\mathbb{Z}^{-1}\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}.

(56)

Then, the GCV function can be computed as

\phi_{\texttt{GCV}}(\mu)=\frac{\|\mathbf{Z}\text{diag}(\mathbb{w}(\mu))\mathbf{Z}^{-1}\delta\mathbf{d}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbb{w}(\mu)\|_{1}^{2}},\quad[\mathbb{w}(\mu)]_{i}=\frac{\mu[\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}]_{ii}^{2}}{\mu[\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{D}}]_{ii}^{2}+[\mathbb{\Gamma}_{\texttt{S}}]_{ii}^{2}}.

(57)

where
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product.

(iii)

Residual Whiteness Principle (
[
9
,
30
]
).

The RWP is an automatic, parameter-free strategy for selecting regularization parameters in variational inverse problems, particularly in image processing tasks such as restoration, deblurring, and super-resolution
[
9
,
30
]
.
The core idea is that the residual
\mathbb{r}(\mu)
should behave like a realization of white noise if the regularization parameter
\mu
is chosen correctly. Conversely, a poor choice of
\mu
typically produces coherent artifacts in the residual, resulting in non-white behavior. To quantify this, a non-negative whiteness measure (
\phi_{\texttt{RWP}}(\mu)
) is defined. A common choice is the kurtosis of the Fourier spectrum of the residual, computed as the ratio between the
\ell_{4}
-norm to the
\ell_{2}
-norm raised to the fourth power:

\phi_{\texttt{RWP}}(\mu)=\frac{\|\texttt{fft}[\mathbb{r}(\mu)]\|_{4}^{4}}{\|\texttt{fft}[\mathbb{r}(\mu)]\|_{2}^{4}}.

(58)

The RWP then selects the optimal parameter by minimizing this whiteness measure:

\mu=\arg\min_{\mu}\phi_{\texttt{RWP}}(\mu)=\frac{\|\texttt{fft}\left[\mathbf{Z}\text{diag}(\mathbb{w}(\mu))\mathbf{Z}^{-1}\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}\right]\|_{4}^{4}}{\|\texttt{fft}\left[\mathbf{Z}\text{diag}(\mathbb{w}(\mu))\mathbf{Z}^{-1}\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}\right]\|_{2}^{4}}.

(59)

3.3
Design of
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
.

The matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
is a model-space weighting operator that appears only in the model-update stage of the algorithm. Its design can serve multiple purposes. The simplest choice is a diagonal weighting matrix. When
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
is chosen as a scaled identity matrix, the update corresponds to zero-order Tikhonov regularization
[
49
]
, which forces the updated model
\mathbf{m}_{k+1}
to remain close to the previous estimate
\mathbf{m}_{k}
and thereby stabilizes the inversion.

More generally,
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
can be designed to perform localized updates, similar in spirit to layer-stripping techniques. In this case, the diagonal weights are constructed to focus the update near specific regions—such as the shallow part of the model in surface-acquisition geometries—and are progressively relaxed to allow deeper updates as the iterations proceed.

Alternatively,
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
can be defined as a finite-difference operator to penalize model roughness, effectively imposing smoothness constraints on the solution.

4
Interpretation of the weighted dual algorithm.

The algorithm iteratively estimates a set of Lagrange multipliers that both predict the recorded data and satisfy the wave equation. We provide an implementation details of the proposed algorithm:

(i)

Estimate the global regularization parameter
\mu
for building the data weighting matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}^{-1}=\mu\mathbf{I}
. Methods based on the Generalized Cross-validation (GCV) and Residual Whiteness Principal are presented in Subsection
3.2.2
for estimation of
\mu
.

(ii)

For a given source multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l}
, the predicted data are computed in the background model
\mathbf{m}
as
\mathbf{d}^{\texttt{pre}}=-\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l}
which are used to compute the data residuals,
\delta\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{d}^{\texttt{pre}}
. The matrix
\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{m})
is precomputed. The weighting matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}
gains multiplier energy at and near the physical source location by
w_{\texttt{S}}(\mathbf{x})^{-1}
(Figure
1
). This weighting process serves as a regularization mechanism, guiding the inversion toward a more focused source representation
[
29
]
. The data residual is then back-propagated by the adjoint operator
\mathbb{S}^{\top}
after deblurring by the data-space Hessian matrix
\left(\mathbb{S}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\mathbb{S}^{\top}+\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}^{-1}\right)
, giving the adjoint wavefield
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}
(line
5
). The preconditioning role of matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}
for estimating
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l}
and will be explored more in the next section.

The inversion process begins with an initial guess of zero multipliers,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}=\mathbb{0}
(line
3
). Thus, the adjoint wavefield in the first iteration is computed simply by a back-propagation of the recorded data. Unlike first-order Born algorithms like reverse time migration (RTM) which propagate only the first order reflections, in this algorithm all the events in the data including first arrivals, diving waves, and multiples will be back-propagated.

(iii)

The inversion of the data-space Hessian ensures that, for sufficiently small values of
\mu
, the computed
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}
, when interpreted as an equivalent source term, accurately reproduces the observed data when propagated through the model
\mathbf{m}
. This property reduces the risk of cycle-skipping by aligning the predicted and observed wavefields despite potential kinematic mismatches in
\mathbf{m}
. The forward wavefield
\mathbf{u}_{l+1}
is computed in line
6
by propagating the total extended source,
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l})
.

(iv)

The model perturbation
\delta\mathbf{m}_{l+1}
is then estimated through the zero-lag cross-correlation of the forward and adjoint wavefields, akin to the standard imaging condition in RTM (line
7
).

(v)

The multipliers are updated (line
8
) based on the estimated
\delta\mathbf{m}_{l+1}
and
\mathbf{u}_{l+1}
and the process is repeated for
maxit
iterations (or until the residual between successive multiplier estimates falls below a predefined threshold, indicating convergence).

(vi)

Finally, the model is updated with the final perturbation
\delta\mathbf{m}_{\texttt{maxit}}
(line
10
). If necessary, the updated model can used to repeat the inner loop.

We demonstrate how distance-weight function precondition each subproblem in FWI using multiplier methods.

4.1
Estimation of adjoint wavefield.

Regarding the adjoint wavefield (line
5
), we note that for a computed data
\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}=\mathbf{d}+\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l}
, the adjoint wavefield
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}
is a regularized solution to inverse source problem

\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}=\delta\mathbf{d}_{l},

(60)

given by the following weighted
\ell_{2}
-norm regularization
[
48
]

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}\|_{\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\|_{\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}}^{2}.

(61)

The regularized solution is obtained by computing the gradient and setting the result equal to zero:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}+\mathbf{I}\right]^{-1}\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}

(62a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}\left[\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}+\mathbb{I}\right]^{-1}\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}

(62b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})^{\top}\left[\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})^{\top}+\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}^{-1}\right]^{-1}\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}.

(62c)

Note that we derived (
62b
) from (
62a
) by using the identity
(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}+\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{I})^{-1}
which is a special case of the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula
[
25
]
.
To demonstrate the role of this regularization, we notice that there are two main challenges in solving the inverse source problem defined by
\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{m})\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}
:
(i) Underdetermined nature:
The matrix
\mathbb{S}
is of size
n_{r}\times n
, where
n_{r}\ll n
, leading to an underdetermined system with infinitely many possible solutions. A straightforward minimum-norm solution,
\mathbb{S}^{\top}(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{S}^{\top})^{-1}\delta\mathbf{d}_{l}
, does not account for the physics of wave propagation or the localized nature of the true source
[
46
]
.
(ii) Energy decay of seismic waves:
The seismic wave energy naturally decay with distance from the source.Each row of the matrix
\mathbb{S}
corresponds to a Green’s function associated with a receiver, with larger amplitudes near the receiver and smaller amplitudes further away. Consequently, the columns of
\mathbb{S}
exhibit non-uniform energy distribution, with higher energy concentrated near receiver locations
[
31
]
. This uneven energy distribution contributes to the ill-conditioning of the system. Therefore, the unweighted regularization tends to favor solutions where the energy tend to concentrate near the receivers, reflecting the higher energy of
\mathbb{S}
’s columns in these regions.

The weighting matrices adjust for these drawbacks, giving more weights to the columns of
\mathbb{S}
near the source. This adjustment mitigates the bias introduced by the unweighted solution, allowing for more physically meaningful and spatially focused estimates of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}
. This weighting approach not only improves the conditioning of the system, but also ensures that the estimated solution align more closely with the expected physics of seismic wave propagation.

We demonstrate the preconditioning role of the distance-weight function using a transmission problem. The model consists of a homogeneous background velocity of 2 km/s (Figure
2
a), spanning 2 km in both depth and lateral extent, discretized on a 10 m grid. A single source is placed near the top of the model at
\mathbb{x}_{s}=(1000\,\text{m},50\,\text{m})
, with 200 receivers equally spaced every 10 m along the bottom boundary at a depth of 1.99 km.
We computed a 6 Hz wavefield,
\mathbf{u}^{*}
, in the true model and then construct an
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{m})\mathbf{u}^{*}-\mathbf{b}
using a homogeneous model
\mathbf{m}
. The result, shown in Figure
2
b, serves as the ground truth for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
. The figure clearly illustrates the decay of amplitudes with distance from the source.
In Figure
2
c, we display the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the columns of the forward operator
\mathbb{S}
, reshaped into a matrix form, showing that the larger amplitudes are concentrated near the receivers.
We generated a synthetic dataset
\delta\mathbf{d}=\mathbb{S}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
, where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
is shown in Figure
2
b. For the first attempt, we inverted the data using a damped least-squares
\mathbb{S}^{\top}\left(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{S}^{\top}+\mu\mathbb{I}\right)^{-1}\delta\mathbf{d}
with a small damping parameter. The reconstructed solution is shown in Figure
2
d. The energy of the solution is smeared, extending from the true source location at the top of the model toward the receiver positions at the bottom.
In the second attempt, we computed the solution using the weighted approach (
61
) with
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}^{-1}=\mu\mathbb{I}
and
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}
built from the weights
w_{\texttt{S}}(\mathbb{x})
in (

) with
\sigma=200
and
\epsilon=0.001
, shown in Figure

f. The solution, shown in Figure

4.2
Estimation of forward wavefield.

After computing
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}
, the term
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l})
acts as the total source
[
21
]
. The weight
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}
emphasizes the energy around the physical source location. The resulting source term is then used to solve the wave equation for the forward wavefield,
\mathbf{u}_{l+1}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{m})^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l})
, effectively injecting a spatially focused extended source
[
46
,
44
]
. It is important to note that the source signature is implicitly estimated during the first subproblem by fitting the data, and this estimate is progressively refined through iterations.

4.3
Estimation of model update.

The other effect of distance-dependent regularization is their role in left preconditioning the linear system associated with the model update.
Simple computations show that the model perturbation
\delta\mathbf{m}_{l+1}
is a regularized solution to

\text{diag}(-\omega^{2}\mathbf{u}_{l+1})\delta\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}},

(63)

given by the following weighted Tikhonov regularization

\delta\mathbf{m}_{l+1}=\arg\min_{\delta\mathbf{m}}\frac{1}{2}\|\text{diag}(-\omega^{2}\mathbf{u}_{l+1})\delta\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}\|_{\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\delta\mathbf{m}\|_{\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}}^{2}.

(64)

Here, the matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}
modulates the relative contributions of the forward and adjoint wavefields in the model reconstruction process.
Its effect is to downweight the wavefields near the source positions while assigning greater weights to regions farther away.
As seen from (
63
), the extended-source term
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+\frac{1}{2}}
becomes highly concentrated near the sources, enforcing the wave equation more strictly at greater distances. This causes the model updates to be largest near the sources and to gradually decay with distance.
An intuitive interpretation of this mechanism, and its benefit for convexity, can be given in the context of a surface-acquisition scenario, where sources and receivers are located on the surface of the model.
In such cases, the weighting encourages model updates to focus first near the surface — where the relationship between data and model is approximately linear — and then progressively penetrate deeper into the subsurface, effectively reconstructing the model layer by layer.
This behavior resembles the classical layer-stripping approach
[
54
]
, which improves inversion stability by sequentially building up the model from shallow to deep regions.

4.4
Scale of the multipliers.

Finally, the distance-dependent regularization plays a crucial role by effectively scaling the multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
within the algorithm.
The multiplier update equation (
45d
) can be interpreted as a steepest-ascent iteration for maximizing the dual function
[
12
]
. Under this interpretation, the weighting matrix
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}
acts as a spatially varying step-size operator, controlling how the multipliers are updated across the domain. In particular, larger weights lead to faster updates at locations farther from the source, whereas smaller weights slow the updates near the source. This mechanism enforces the wave-equation constraint more strictly in regions distant from the source, which helps to promote a more focused and spatially balanced source extension.

4.5
Acceleration.

Since the model parameters
\mathbf{m}
remain fixed during the inner loop of the dual algorithm, Algorithm
7
, basically it can be considered as a fixed-point iteration

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+1}=g(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l}),

(65)

where
g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is the fixed-point mapping describing a single iteration of the inner loop of Algorithm
7
.
Equation (
65
) is a fixed-point problem for the multipliers associated to the given model
\mathbf{m}
.
This allows one to apply acceleration methods such as the Anderson acceleration
[
10
,
6
]
to increase convergence of the inner loop.
Anderson acceleration aims at speeding up convergence of the iteration to a fixed point where the residual
f(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\boldsymbol{\lambda}-g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
vanishes.
Its main idea is to use the residuals of the latest
h+1
estimates
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l-1},...,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l-h}
to find a new
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+1}
giving a small residual. This is achieved via a linear combination of these previous estimates under the fixed-point mapping:

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{h}\theta_{j}g(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l-h+j})

(66)

where the weights
\theta_{j}
are determined by solving a least-squares problem:

\theta=\arg\min\Big\|\sum_{j=0}^{h}\theta_{j}f(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{l-h+j})\Big\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_{j=0}^{h}\theta_{j}=1.

(67)

6
Discussions.

We have introduced a general weighted proximal Lagrangian objective function for FWI and developed several algorithms for its solution. The performance of the proposed approach critically depends on the proper design of the weighting functions
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}},\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}},\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}
, which weight the data residual, the model parameters, and the Lagrange multipliers, respectively. Beyond the impact of these weighting functions, the proposed dual-space ADMM implementation significantly enhances computational efficiency. This is achieved by avoiding repeated LU factorizations of the forward operator during inner iterations, relying instead on efficient forward–backward substitutions. Further acceleration of these inner iterations was facilitated by Anderson acceleration
[
10
]
.

In this work, we did not attempt a detailed design of
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}
, the data weighting matrix, leaving it for future investigations.
However, as outlined in Subsection
3.2
, various strategies exist, such as emphasizing specific data portions based on source–receiver offset, which could be explored in future work. The regularization parameter
\mu
, associated with
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}
, was adaptively updated using the Residual Whiteness Principle (RWP) method
[
9
,
30
]
. Similarly, we set
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
to be a scalar multiple of the identity matrix, thereby applying a homogeneous regularization over the entire model space. However,
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
can be designed much more flexibly. For example, a space-variant weighting function could be employed to concentrate model updates in specific regions of interest. Increasing
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
with depth would emphasize near-surface updates, potentially improving the conditioning of the inversion. Alternatively,
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
could be replaced by a discrete finite-difference operator to promote smoothness, or more advanced regularizations such as total-variation (TV) or hybrid Tikhonov–TV regularization could be incorporated to preserve sharp contrasts while maintaining stability
[
22
]
.

Among the three weighting functions, we focused in particular on
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}
, introducing a distance-dependent weighting function that plays a key role in eliminating the explicit source signature from the optimization problem
[
29
]
. This property significantly simplifies practical implementation by relaxing the need for source signature estimation or source alignment with grid points.
This is achieved by compensating for the natural decay of seismic energy and promoting a focused multiplier estimates, thereby improving the convexity of the objective function and leading to more robust and focused model updates. This mechanism causes model updates to be largest near the sources and receivers, resembling a layer-stripping approach that improves stability by sequentially building the model from shallow to deep regions. The proposed weighting function contains two free parameters,
(\sigma,\gamma)
. In all presented numerical examples, we set
\gamma=10
and chose
\sigma
as a scale factor in the range
[1,3]
of the dominant wavelength of the propagating wavefield. Numerical experiments consistently demonstrate the method’s enhanced resilience to cycle skipping, its ability to handle off-grid sources and receivers, and its robustness against significant levels of random Gaussian noise.

To further explore the effect of
(\sigma,\gamma)
, we performed a sensitivity study by constructing weighting functions over a grid of parameter values and running 20 iterations of the weighted DMWI algorithm. Figure
26
(a)–(d) shows the mean-squared error (MSE) across the
(\sigma,\gamma)
-parameter space for the four numerical examples considered. Two main observations can be drawn: (i) The inversion results are significantly more sensitive to
\sigma
than to
\gamma
. For
\sigma>\lambda_{\mathrm{w}}
, the inversion remains stable and yields satisfactory reconstructions over a broad range of
\gamma
, suggesting that
\gamma
acts primarily as a secondary tuning parameter.
(ii) The MSE shows a clear trend with respect to
\sigma
: small
\sigma
values lead to higher MSE, indicating poor model recovery due to an overly localized weighting function, whereas larger
\sigma
values improve model reconstruction and stability.

The distance-dependent dual method proposed in this paper shares similarities with matched source waveform inversion (MSWI)
[
29
,
47
]
, but there are three key differences:

(i)

MSWI relies on a penalty-based objective function, whereas our method employs a proximal-point Lagrangian objective function. This distinction provides two major advantages: (1) the AL approach enables more accurate solutions to the inverse problem through the incorporation of source multipliers, and (2) it reduces the sensitivity of the optimization process to fine-tuning of the regularization parameter
\mu
, as demonstrated in
[
21
]
, unlike the penalty formulation in which the penalty parameter has a profound influence on the optimization problem
[
46
]
.

(ii)

MSWI depends on accurately inverting the normal operator associated with the extended source, expressed in our notation as
\mathbb{S}^{\top}\mathbb{S}+\mu\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}
(
[
29
]
, their Equation 11).
The forward operator
\mathbb{S}
can be computed once and reused for all sources. However, this operator resides in the source space, is dense, and is inherently ill-conditioned. In MSWI, the authors addressed this by employing iterative Krylov subspace methods to solve the resulting system. Nevertheless, the combination of the operator’s ill-conditioned nature and its source-dependent characteristics poses significant challenges for large-scale problems (see
[
29
]
, their DISCUSSION section). In contrast, our formulation solves the system in the data space
[
19
]
, requiring the inversion of the data-space Hessian matrix
\mathbb{S}\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}^{-1}\mathbb{S}^{\top}+\mu\mathbf{I}
. This approach allows the inversion of the normal operator by direct methods even for large-scale implementations and efficient selection of the regularization parameter
\mu
by using standard methods like GCV and RWP.

(iii)

In MSWI, the optimization is performed over both the model parameters and the source, requiring operator updates at each iteration. In contrast, the proposed method adopts a dual formulation, where the source multipliers are estimated for each frequency while keeping the background model fixed. This approach requires only a single LU factorization per extended source inversion, significantly improving computational efficiency and reducing the overall cost of inversion, making it highly suitable for large-scale problems.

For a given challenging initial model, the proposed weighted method can successfully converge to accurate results by inverting data at the lowest available frequency, a scenario where standard multiplier-based methods typically fail to converge to the true solution and become trapped in local minima due to cycle skipping. However, while the proposed weighting approach significantly broadens the basin of attraction and extends the frequency range for accurate inversion, numerical results also indicate that it cannot entirely eliminate non-convexity at very high frequencies, where both weighted and unweighted methods may eventually fail. This suggests areas for further methodological refinement.

7
Conclusions.

We introduced a weighted multiplier method for full-waveform inversion (FWI), incorporating distance-dependent penalty weights to improve the conditioning of traditional augmented Lagrangian (AL) approaches. Our work specifically focused on the design and impact of the distance-dependent penalty weights, demonstrating their critical role in the method’s performance. By spatially varying the penalty parameter, the method compensates for the natural scaling of wave equation residuals, ensuring more effective constraint enforcement across the model domain. Compared to traditional methods that use a uniform penalty parameter, the proposed variable weighting approach eliminates the need for explicit source signature estimation, enhances resilience to cycle skipping, and accelerates convergence. Furthermore, the algorithm relaxes the requirement for sources and receivers to be positioned on finite-difference grid points, increasing its flexibility in practical applications. While this study primarily focused on the design of
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{S}}
, with
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
set as a simple scalar regularization and
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}
largely kept uniform for experimental focus, the flexible framework allows for more sophisticated designs of these weighting functions to further enhance control over the inversion.
In this framework, FWI is reformulated as a source estimation problem within a fixed-point iteration scheme implemented via dual-space Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM). This approach ensures that model parameters, and consequently the required LU factorizations of the forward operator, remain fixed during the inner loops of each source multiplier inversion, significantly reducing computational costs to only a few factorizations per frequency. Further acceleration of the inner iterations was achieved through the application of Anderson acceleration. Numerical experiments on challenging models demonstrated the method’s ability to accurately reconstruct complex subsurface features without relying on source signature estimation, low-frequency data, or a highly accurate initial model. These results highlight the method’s potential as a robust and efficient approach for FWI. Future work will explore more advanced designs for
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{D}}
and
\mathbf{W}_{\!\texttt{M}}
, and extend the method’s application to even more complex geological structures and 3D scenarios.
