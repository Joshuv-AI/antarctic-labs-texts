Emulating extended Lyman-alpha haloes around star-forming galaxies 2015 Emulating extended Lyman-alpha haloes around star-forming galaxies – A
Pengfei Li, Zheng Zheng

Abstract
Extended Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly\alpha} emission is commonly observed around star-forming galaxies, opening a window for probing the neutral hydrogen gas in the circumgalactic medium (CGM). In this paper, we develop a prescription of spherically symmetric CGM gas properties and build emulators to model circularly-averaged surface brightness (SB) profiles of the extended Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly\alpha} emission. With CGM gas properties parametrized by the density, velocity, and temperature profiles, a self-shielding calculation is carried out to obtain the neutral gas distribution with ionizing photons from the ultraviolet (UV) background and star formation in the galaxy. Our calculation reveals three types of systems with distinct neutral gas distribution: non-shielded systems with the CGM being highly ionized across all radii, shielded systems with a neutral gas shell shielding the UV background, and transitional systems in between. Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly\alpha} SB profiles are obtained through Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly\alpha} radiative transfer (RT) simulations, performed for the CGM models with three kinds of Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly\alpha} sources: the star formation from central and satellite galaxies, and the recombination in the CGM. We build emulators to efficiently predict Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly\alpha} SB profiles for given model parameters and Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly\alpha} sources, based on Gaussian process regression. After being trained with only 180 RT simulations for each Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly\alpha} source, the emulators reach an overall accuracy at the level of ∼ 20 \sim 20 per cent. By applying the emulators to fit mock Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly\alpha} SB profiles constructed from our model, we find a reasonable recovery of model parameters, indicating the potential of extracting physical information of the CGM and galaxies from the observed extended Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly\alpha} emission.

Introduction

The extended
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission is predicted to exist around star-forming galaxies
(
Zheng et al. 2011b
, e.g.)
.
The discovery of such
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
haloes (LAHs; e.g.
Steidel
et al. 2011
) has led to numerous follow-up observational and theoretical investigations.
The resonant interactions between
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
photons and neutral hydrogen atoms (
\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}
) connect the extended
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission with the underlying gas, providing a valuable window for studying the circumgalactic medium (CGM) of star-forming galaxies.
In this paper, we aim to build a model for the surface brightness (SB) profiles of the extended
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission and demonstrate its power for constraining physical model parameters by fitting mock profiles.

Because of the low SB of the extended
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission, LAHs were first revealed and detected by stacking narrow-band images of star-forming galaxies

(
Hayashino
et al. 2004
;
Steidel
et al. 2011
;
Matsuda
et al. 2012
;
Feldmeier
et al. 2013
;
Jiang
et al. 2013
;
Xue et al. 2017
;
Wu
et al. 2020
;
Kakuma
et al. 2021
;
Kikuchihara
et al. 2022
;
Kikuta
et al. 2023
;
Zhang
et al. 2024
)
, over a large redshift range
(
Momose
et al. 2014
;
Momose
et al. 2016
, e.g.)
. They are also observed around individual star-forming galaxies in the local universe, based on effective narrow-band observations from space
(
Lallement et al. 2011
;
Hayes
et al. 2014
;
Guaita
et al. 2015
;
Bridge
et al. 2018
;
Runnholm
et al. 2023
)
.
The extended
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission can be found in spectroscopic observations as well, such as in the long exposure, long-slit spectra
(
Rauch
et al. 2008
)
. With the commissioning of integral field units (IFU) on large telescopes, LAHs can now be measured and investigated around individual star-forming galaxies
(
Wisotzki
et al. 2016
;
Wisotzki
et al. 2018
;
Leclercq
et al. 2017
;
Erb
et al. 2018
;
Gallego
et al. 2018
;
Leclercq
et al. 2020
;
Matthee
et al. 2020
;
Kusakabe
et al. 2022
;
Claeyssens
et al. 2022
;
Lujan Niemeyer
et al. 2022a
;
Lujan Niemeyer
et al. 2022b
;
Guo
et al. 2024a
;
Guo
et al. 2024b
)
.

Interpreting LAHs requires not only understanding the intrinsic production mechanisms of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission, but also accounting for the interactions between the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission and the neutral hydrogen gas around galaxies, i.e. the radiative transfer (RT) effects.

The
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
production mechanisms mainly include star formation, fluorescence, and cooling
radiation. The ionizing photons from massive stars produced during star formation can ionize the neutral hydrogen atoms in the interstellar medium, then the subsequent recombination produces
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission
(
Partridge &
Peebles 1967
)
.
The ionizing photons from star formation and ultraviolet (UV) background can propagate to the CGM and ionize the neutral hydrogen atoms, followed by the production of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission through recombination, which is typically called fluorescent
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission
(
Haiman &
Rees 2001
;
Kollmeier
et al. 2010
, e.g.)
.
Finally,
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
photons can be produced as part of the cooling radiation from the gas accreted from the intergalactic medium (IGM) and collisionally excited in the CGM
(
Dijkstra &
Loeb 2009
;
Faucher-Giguère
et al. 2010
;
Goerdt et al. 2010
;
Cantalupo et al. 2014
;
Rosdahl &
Blaizot 2012
;
Ledos
et al. 2023
)
.

After
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
photons are produced, they experience a large number of scatterings with neutral hydrogen atoms in the CGM, which causes diffusion in both space and frequency. Such an RT process depends on the density, velocity and temperature of the neutral hydrogen gas, which requires theoretical modelling for properly interpreting LAHs.

Various theoretical frameworks have been explored to understand LAHs, ranging from analytically solving RT equations to numerical RT simulations.
Analytical solutions of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles and spectra are obtained for a
power-law gas density profile in the optically thick regime
(
Lao &
Smith 2020
)
.
A more flexible framework is to perform Monte Carlo RT simulations with an analytical setup of gas properties, such as multi-phase gas with clumpy and diffuse
components or outflowing gas
(
Dijkstra &
Kramer 2012
;
Gronke &
Dijkstra 2016
;
Song
et al. 2020
;
Chang et al. 2023
, e.g.)
.
Such models have been successfully applied to fit
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
spectra and SB profiles of observed LAHs
(
Song
et al. 2020
;
Erb et al. 2023
;
Garel
et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. Another framework is to carry out Monte Carlo RT calculations for galaxies in hydrodynamic galaxy formation simulations
(
Laursen &
Sommer-Larsen 2007
;
Zheng et al. 2010
;
Verhamme
et al. 2012
)
, which can generally produce
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles similar to observations
(
Lake et al. 2015
;
Smith
et al. 2019
;
Byrohl
et al. 2021
;
Mitchell et al. 2021
)
.

In this paper, we construct a model of extended LAHs, aiming to interpret observed
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles. It captures the key physical components, including multiple sources of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
production and RT in the CGM. Given that RT is computationally expensive, we develop emulators to improve the efficiency of modelling extended LAHs.

We organize the paper as follows. In Section
2
, we present the model for the neutral hydrogen gas, the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}

sources, and the calculation of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles from RT simulations.
In Section
3
, the design and construction of emulators for
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles are introduced and the performance of emulators is examined and assessed. We apply the emulators to model mock
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles in Section
4
to demonstrate the potential of extracting CGM and galaxy properties from observations of LAHs.
In Section
5
, we summarize the work and discuss possible improvements.

Throughout this paper, whenever necessary, we adopt a spatially-flat
\Lambda\rm CDM
cosmological model consistent with the constraints from
WMAP5

(
Komatsu
et al. 2009
)
, which has
\Omega_{\rm m}=0.28
,
\Omega_{\rm b}=0.046
,
\sigma_{8}=0.82
,
n_{\rm s}=0.96
, and
H_{0}=100h\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}Mpc^{-1}}
with
h=0.7
. The logarithm
\log
in this paper is base 10.

2
Model Description

In this section, we describe our model of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles around star-forming galaxies. There are three major components: neutral hydrogen (
\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}
) gas
properties, intrinsic
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
sources, and
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
RT simulations. The CGM in our model is assumed to be spherically symmetric, described by density, velocity, temperature, and ionizing fraction profiles. The ionizing fraction profile is obtained through a self-shielding calculation. The intrinsic
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
sources include star formation from central galaxies, CGM recombination, and
star formation from satellite galaxies. With gas properties and
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
sources, we perform RT simulations to produce
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles.

2.1
Gas properties

We model gas density closely following
Padmanabhan et al. 2016
. The total gas mass
M_{\rm g}
within the viral radius is determined by the host halo mass
M_{\rm h}

(
Barnes &
Haehnelt 2010
)
,

M_{\rm g}=f_{\rm b}M_{\rm h}\exp{\left[-\left(\frac{v_{\rm c,0}}{v_{\rm c}(M_{\rm h})}\right)^{3}\right]},

(1)

where
f_{\rm b}=\Omega_{\rm b}/\Omega_{\rm m}
is the global baryon fraction,
v_{\rm c}(M_{\rm h})

is the circular velocity at the viral radius
R_{\rm vir}
, and
v_{\rm c,0}=50\rm km\,s^{-1}
.
Halo mass is defined to have a mean matter density (within
R_{\rm vir}
) of
18\pi^{2}
times the critical density at a given redshift.

Following
Maller &
Bullock 2004
, the gas density profile is modelled with a modified Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile
(
Navarro
et al. 1996
)
to account for the baryonic effects,

\rho(r)=\frac{\rho_{\rm 0}}{(c_{\rm g}r/R_{\rm vir}+0.75)(c_{\rm g}r/R_{\rm vir}+1)^{2}}.

(2)

The gas concentration parameter
c_{\rm g}
is modelled as
(
Macciò et al. 2007
)

c_{\rm g}=c_{\rm 0}\left(\frac{M_{\rm h}}{10^{11}{\rm M}_{\odot}}\right)^{-0.109}\frac{4}{1+z},

(3)

where
c_{\rm 0}
is a free parameter and
z
is the redshift.
With the gas concentration and the total gas mass [equation (
1
)],
\rho_{0}
is determined through

M_{\rm g}=\int_{0}^{R_{\rm vir}}\rho(r)4\pi r^{2}dr.

(4)

We further add the mean gas density
\bar{\rho}_{\rm b}
of the universe
to equation (
2
) to describe the IGM at large radii, and the total gas density profile is

\rho_{\rm g}(r)=\rho(r)+\bar{\rho}_{\rm b}.

(5)

The dashed curve in the top panel of Fig.
1
shows one example of the total hydrogen density profile, which is the total gas density profile times the hydrogen mass abundance 0.76.

The radial velocity profile of the gas is modelled following
Sadoun et al. 2017
as

v(r)=\frac{v_{\rm B}(r)}{1+e^{-20(r/R_{\rm vir}-1)}}.

(6)

The quantity
v_{\rm B}(r)
is the average radial velocity around a dark matter halo of mass
M_{\rm h}
at redshift
z
, calculated based on the extended Press–Schechter formalism in
Barkana 2004
.
Outside of the viral radius, this form of
v(r)
essentially follows
v_{\rm B}(r)
, which appears as infall close to the halo and approaches to Hubble flow at large radii.
Within the halo viral radius, we assume that the gas only has random motion, and therefore modify the velocity profile
v_{\rm B}(r)
as in equation (
6
) such that the radial velocity
v(r)
approaches to zero.
In reality, the velocity distribution can be much more complex with inflow, outflow and turbulence. For simplicity, we use the above velocity profiles to demonstrate our framework. One example of the velocity profile is shown in the bottom panel of Fig.
1
.

The gas temperature is relevant for two calculations. For the self-shielding calculation (Section
2.2
), we fix it to be
10^{4}\rm K
, around the peak of the cooling rate. For the RT calculation (Section
2.4
), we fix the temperature to be
10^{6}\rm K
to account for the gas random motion (e.g. turbulence).

For our later calculations with the model, we set the boundary of the gas distribution to be
10R_{\rm vir}
. This is motivated by several reasons. First, if the virial radius of
10^{11}M_{\odot}
haloes at
z\sim 3
is used as a reference,
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
haloes around individual galaxies are detected at a radius beyond the halo virial radius
(
Leclercq
et al. 2017
, e.g.)
. For
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
haloes detected through stacking narrow-band images, they can even reach a few times the virial radius
(
Momose
et al. 2016
;
Lujan Niemeyer
et al. 2022a
, e.g.)
. Next, the neutral gas outside the virial radius (IGM) can still affect the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
RT, based on theoretical investigations
(
Dijkstra
et al. 2007
;
Zheng et al. 2011a
, e.g.)
and observational study
(
Momose et al. 2021
, e.g.)
. In particular, a significant fraction of scatterings can occur in the infall region outside of the virial radius, which influence the spectral and spatial distribution of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
photons
(
Sadoun et al. 2017
, e.g.)
.

2.2
Self-shielding calculation

\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
photons interact with neutral hydrogen atoms. Given the gas profile in equation (
5
), we solve the gas ionization structure by performing a self-shielding calculation. We consider two types of ionizing sources, the star formation in central galaxies and the UV ionizing background.

For the ionizing photons from star formation, the production rate
Q
is related to the star formation rate (SFR) as

\begin{split}\log(Q/{\rm s^{-1}})=\ &53.8+\log[{\rm SFR}/({\rm M}_{\odot}{\rm yr^{-1}})]\\
-&\ 0.0029[9+\log(Z/Z_{\odot})]^{2.5},\end{split}

(7)

based on the Salpeter initial mass function and a constant SFR
(
Schaerer 2003
;
Dijkstra
et al. 2007
)
.
We fix the metallicity
Z
to be solar metallicity (
Z/Z_{\odot}=1)
.
Following
Zheng et al. 2010
, we connect SFR to halo mass,

{\rm SFR}/({\rm M}_{\odot}{\rm yr^{-1}})=0.68[M_{\rm h}/(10^{10}h^{-1}{\rm M}_{\odot})].

(8)

The frequency-dependent luminosity
L_{\nu}
of ionizing photons is assumed to
follow a power law,
L_{\nu}=\beta Qh_{\rm p}(\nu/\mathrm{\nu_{\rm L}})^{-\beta}
, with the normalization
coming from
Q=\int_{\mathrm{\nu_{\rm L}}}^{\infty}L_{\nu}/(h_{\rm p}\nu)d\nu
,
where
h_{\rm p}
is the Plank constant, and
\mathrm{\nu_{\rm L}}
is the
\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}
Lyman-limit frequency.
Following
Sadoun et al. 2017
, we fix
\beta=3
.

For the UV ionizing background, we adopt the model in
Haardt &
Madau 2012
to obtain the redshift-dependent

\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}
photoionization rate
\Gamma_{\rm UV}
. We assume a constant intensity

I_{\nu,0}
between
\mathrm{\nu_{\rm L}}
and
4\mathrm{\nu_{\rm L}}
(the
\mathrm{He\textsc{ii}}
Lyman-limit), which is derived from

\Gamma_{\rm UV}=4\pi\int_{\mathrm{\nu_{\rm L}}}^{4\mathrm{\nu_{\rm L}}}I_{\nu,0}/(h_{\rm p}\nu)a_{\nu}d\nu
,
with
a_{\nu}
being the photoionization cross section.

With the two ionizing sources, we perform a self-shielding calculation to determine the neutral
hydrogen fraction
x_{\rm\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}}(r)\equiv n_{\rm\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}}(r)/n_{\rm H}(r)
as a function of radius
r
, where

n_{\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}}
and
n_{\rm H}
are the number densities of neutral and total hydrogen, respectively.
This is achieved by solving the photoionization equilibrium equation

x_{\rm\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}}(r)\Gamma_{\rm tot,ss}(r)=\alpha_{\rm B}(T)[1-x_{\rm\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}}(r)]n_{\rm e},

(9)

where
\alpha_{\rm B}
is the case-B recombination coefficient at temperature
T
. The electron number density is calculated as
n_{\rm e}=0.82(1-x_{\rm\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}})\rho_{\rm g}/m_{\rm H}
, accounting for the contribution from singly ionized helium, with
m_{\rm H}
being the mass of a hydrogen atom. The total photoionization rate
\Gamma_{\rm tot,ss}
comes from the attenuated ionizing radiation from the central star formation and the UV background,
\Gamma_{\rm tot,ss}=\Gamma_{\rm cen,ss}+\Gamma_{\rm UV,ss}
, with

\begin{split}\Gamma_{\rm cen,ss}(r)=&\int_{\mathrm{\nu_{\rm L}}}^{\infty}\frac{f_{\rm esc}L_{\nu}\exp[-\tau_{\rm\nu,cen}(r)]}{4\pi r^{2}h_{\rm p}\nu}a_{\nu}d\nu\end{split}

(10)

and

\begin{split}\Gamma_{\rm UV,ss}(r)=&\int_{4\pi}d\Omega\int_{\mathrm{\nu_{\rm L}}}^{\infty}\frac{I_{\nu,0}\exp[-\tau_{\rm\nu,UV}(r,\bm{\hat{n}})]}{h_{\rm p}\nu}a_{\nu}d\nu.\end{split}

(11)

In equation (
10
),
f_{\rm esc}
is the escaping fraction of central ionizing photons, and
\tau_{\rm\nu,cen}(r)
is the photoionization optical depth from the centre to radius
r
.
In equation (
11
),
\tau_{\rm\nu,UV}(r,\bm{\hat{n}})
is the photoionization optical depth from
10R_{\rm vir}
to radius
r
along direction
\bm{\hat{n}}
.

The self-shielding calculation is performed in an iterative way
(
Zheng &
Miralda-Escudé 2002a
)
. We initialize
x_{\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}}(r)
under the optically thin condition (i.e.
\tau_{\rm\nu,cen}=0
and
\tau_{\rm\nu,UV}=0
). We then recompute the optical depths by integrating the
\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}
density profile, and update
x_{\rm\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}}
using equation (
9
).
This process is repeated until the maximum fractional difference of
x_{\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}}
between two
consecutive iterations is smaller than
10^{-5}
. The solid curve in the top panel of Fig.
1
shows one example of the neutral hydrogen density profile after the self-shielding calculation.

2.3
Intrinsic
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
sources

There are three intrinsic
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
sources in our model, star formation in the central galaxy, recombination in the CGM, and star formation in satellite galaxies, denoted as central, CGM, and satellite sources, respectively.
In principle, besides the recombination, the collisional de-excitation of neutral hydrogen atoms following the collisional excitation can lead to another extended
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
source in the CGM.
Because its modelling procedure is similar to the recombination in the CGM, for our purpose of proof of concept, we choose not to consider it in this paper.

For the central source, the intrinsic
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
luminosity is related to
\rm SFR
following
Zheng et al. 2010
,

L_{\rm cen}/({\rm erg\,s^{-1}})=(1-f_{\rm esc})10^{42}[{\rm SFR}/({\rm M}_{\odot}\,{\rm yr^{-1}})],

(12)

where the
1-f_{\rm esc}
factor accounts for the escaping of ionizing photons from the star-forming region.
For the CGM source, the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emissivity at each radius is estimated to follow two-thirds of the recombination rate
(
Dijkstra 2014
)
,
\epsilon(r)=(2/3)\alpha_{\rm B}n_{\rm e}n_{\rm H}(1-x_{\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}})
.
For the satellite source, we describe it as a galaxy located at radius
R_{\rm sat}
with
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
luminosity
L_{\rm sat}
.

r_{\rm p}
in the bottom two rows is put in units of arcsecond.

2.4
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
RT and model
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles

To calculate
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles for given gas models and intrinsic
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
sources, we run RT simulations adopting the method in
Zheng &
Miralda-Escudé 2002b
. With each
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
source (central, CGM, or satellite), we run the RT simulation with
10^{5}
photons for each training or testing model of the emulators. For each source, the photons are equally weighted to reproduce the total
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
luminosity of the source.

First, we initialize
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
photons at the line centre in the lab frame
1
1

1

In principle, it is more physical to initialize the frequency at the line centre in the rest frame of the atom, resulting in a thermally broadened (Gaussian) distribution in the lab frame. In practice, this initialization and our initialization are almost equivalent in terms of the subsequent frequency distribution of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
photons in the lab frame. The reason is that the bulk gas velocity in our model is typically smaller than the thermal velocity (with one-dimensional dispersion of
\sim 130\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}
for the assumed
T=10^{6}{\rm K}
). The first a few scatterings encountered by the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
photons would bring them toward the line centre in the rest frame of atoms, giving rise to a thermally broadened frequency distribution in the lab frame.

for different sources.
The
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
photons of central and satellite sources are launched at the centre of the system and at the position of the satellite (radius
R_{\rm sat}
), respectively.
While for the CGM source, to determine the launching position of each photon, a random radial direction is chosen and then the radius is sampled according to the probability distribution of the volume-weighted
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emissivity,
P(r)\propto\epsilon(r)r^{2}
.
Then we trace each photon’s propagation until it escapes the system at
10R_{\rm vir}
, after which we record its frequency, propagating direction, and last scattering position.
Every scattering is considered in our RT calculation, with no acceleration scheme applied.

For central and CGM sources, given the spherical symmetry, we can split photons into different projected radius
r_{\rm p}
bins. For photons with total luminosity
L_{\rm A}
within a radial bin of annulus area
A
centred at
r_{\rm p}
, the surface brightness is calculated as
(
Lake et al. 2015
)

{\rm SB}(r_{\rm p})=\frac{L_{\rm A}}{4\pi A(1+z)^{4}}.

(13)

For satellite sources, we choose the radial direction passing the satellite as the
z
-axis, with the origin at the centre of the system.
With a satellite at a given position, we collect photons within a narrow bin of an observing direction
\mu\pm\Delta\mu/2
, where
\mu
is the cosine of the angle between the direction towards the observer and the
z
-axis. Then we calculate the circularly averaged surface brightness profiles following equation (
13
).
Each satellite
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profile corresponds to only one satellite source in a system. For modelling simplicity, satellite sources do not have their own gaseous halos and make no contribution to ionizing photons.

Throughout this paper, we take the bin size of the projected radius to be
\Delta r_{\rm p}=0.2\ \rm arcsec
(motivated by observations; e.g.
Leclercq
et al. 2017
), and the angular bin size of satellite sources to be
\Delta\mu=0.1
.

The other type has strongly peaked neutral density profiles near the centre and nearly flat
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles around the centre followed by a sharp drop, for reasons opposite to the previous case.
We call these two types of systems as
non-shielded
and
shielded
systems for later references.
The
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
photons in shielded systems experience significantly more scattering events in the inner region compared to those in non-shielded systems, leading to a stronger frequency diffusion away from the line centre and an easier escape from smaller radii.
Such RT processes lead to much steeper
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles in shielded systems than those in non-shielded systems.
The transitions between these two types of systems are discussed in Appendix
A
.
The system type depends on the combination of model parameters. For the specific parameter combinations in the first column, only non-shielded systems are present.

3
Construction of the Emulators

To model the observed
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles, we need to explore the parameter space and run RT simulations for each model, which would be time-consuming. To make the parameter exploration more efficient, we adopt the emulator technique, which builds a fast mapping from model parameters to
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles. We describe the design of the emulators and their performance in this section.

3.1
Latin hypercube design

To build an emulator, we first need to run RT simulations to obtain
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles at a number of positions in the parameter space, and convolve them with the adopted PSF.
The chosen parameters need to have a good coverage of the entire parameter space.
Additionally, we want the number of these positions to be small to reduce the number of RT simulations.
These two requirements can be achieved through the Latin hypercube design (LHD).
The first step of an LHD is to discretize a
d
dimensional space into
n^{d}
cubes uniformly, where
n
is the number of grids for each dimension.
Then
n
points are populated into the discretized space, with each row of any dimension having one point and one point only.
In this work,
n
is the number of models for which we run RT simulations and
d
is the number of model parameters.

To generate an LHD with given
n
and
d
, we need to draw
n
points in a
d
-dimensional space. The coordinates of the
i
-th point is
\left(X_{1}^{(i)},X_{2}^{(i)},...,X_{d}^{(i)}\right)
, whose component is denoted as
X_{k}^{(i)}
with
k=1
,
2
, …, and
d
. For a given
k
, the components
X_{k}^{(i)}
of the
n
points are drawn from the permutation of
1
,
2
, …, and
n
.
A simple example of an LHD is to distribute these points diagonally in this
d
dimensional space.
However, such a design does not cover the parameter space effectively, and an optimization algorithm needs to be applied for better coverage.

We first randomly initialize a
d
-dimensional LHD with
n
points.
For each pair of points, we exchange their
k
-th coordinate components and calculate the entropy of the LHD after the exchange.
Only the LHD with the largest entropy is kept among these exchanges.
The above process is repeated from
k=1
to
k=d
.
Then the process is iterated until the fractional difference of entropies between two consecutive iterations is smaller than 0.01.
The entropy of an LHD is defined as
\ln|S|
, with the matrix
S
having elements

S_{ij}=\exp\left[-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(x_{k}^{(i)}-x_{k}^{(j)}\right)^{2}\right],

(14)

where
x_{k}^{(i)}=X_{k}^{(i)}/n

(
Ye
et al. 2000
)
, and
|S|
is the determinant of the matrix
S
.
We further require the parameter distribution to be symmetric about the centre of the parameter space during both the initialization and optimization processes to speed up the optimization, which is called the symmetric Latin hypercube design (SLHD;
Ye
et al. 2000
).

N=180

.

We run RT simulations for the
N=180
training models in Section
3.1
and calculate their
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles.
By interpolating these simulated profiles from training models, we can efficiently obtain
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles for any model without running RT simulations. We adopt the Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) to carry out the interpolation.

GPR is a non-parametric regression technique, assuming a multivariate Gaussian distribution of the targeting quantity
(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{N})^{T}\equiv\bm{y}
,

\mathcal{L}(\bm{y})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2}|C|^{1/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{y}-\bm{\tilde{y}})^{\rm T}\mathcal{C}^{-1}(\bm{y}-\bm{\tilde{y}})\right].

(15)

In our case, the quantities in
\bm{y}
are
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB values of the 180 training profiles at a fixed projected radius.
Every component in vector
\bm{\tilde{y}}
takes the median value of
y_{1}
,
y_{2}
, …, and
y_{N}
.
The covariance matrix
\mathcal{C}
of this distribution is modelled by the so-called kernel function, such as a Gaussian function or Mat
\rm\acute{e}
rn series
(
Rasmussen &
Williams 2006
)
.
In this paper, we use the first-order Mat
\rm\acute{e}
rn kernel to build emulators,

\mathcal{C}_{ij}=\sigma^{2}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{3}\left|x_{k}^{(i)}-x_{k}^{(j)}\right|}{l_{k}}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}\left|x_{k}^{(i)}-x_{k}^{(j)}\right|}{l_{k}}\right).

(16)

The parameter
x_{k}^{(i)}
is the scaled model parameter following the same definition as in Section
3.1
.
For the two hyper-parameters, the amplitude parameter
\sigma
describes the global variation of
\bm{y}
, and the scale parameter
l_{k}
describes the model correlation length for the
k
-th model parameter.
We further add a noise kernel to the above kernels for all
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
sources,

\mathcal{N}_{ij}=\sigma_{{\rm sn},i}^{2}\delta_{ij},

(17)

where
\sigma_{{\rm sn},i}
corresponds to the shot noise of the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB value
y_{i}
from RT simulations.
The shot noise is estimated as the square root of the simulated photon number for a given projected radius bin.
Including the noise kernel can suppress the contribution of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB values associated with small photon number counts during training and predicting processes of the emulator.

To determine the hyper-parameters in the emulator, we need to maximize the likelihood in equation (
15
), which is called the training of the emulator. We use the
emcee
package
(
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
to explore the hyper-parameter space and adopt the hyper-parameters with the largest likelihood.
Once the training is done, we can predict the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB value at a fixed projected radius for a given model without running RT simulations
(
Rasmussen &
Williams 2006
)
,

y^{(\rm p)}=\mathcal{C}^{(\rm pt)}\left(\mathcal{C}^{(\rm tt)}\right)^{-1}\bm{y}^{(\rm t)},

(18)

where
\bm{y}^{(\rm t)}
represents the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB values from the
N
training models,
y^{(\rm p)}
represents the predicted (mean)
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB value for a given model,
\mathcal{C}^{(\rm tt)}
is the
N\times N
covariance matrix among the training models calculated through equation (
16
)-(
17
), and
\mathcal{C}^{(\rm pt)}
is the
1\times N
cross-covariance matrix between the predicting model and the training models calculated through equation (
16
), with
i
and
j
denoting the predicting model and the training models, respectively. The cross-covariance matrix has no contribution from the shot noise kernel in equation (
17
).
The GPR can also estimate the uncertainty of the predicted value
(
Rasmussen &
Williams 2006
)
,

\sigma^{(\rm p)}=\sqrt{\mathcal{C}^{(\rm pp)}-\mathcal{C}^{(\rm pt)}\left(\mathcal{C}^{(\rm tt)}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}^{(\rm pt)}\right)^{\rm T}},

(19)

where
\mathcal{C}^{(\rm pp)}
is simply
\sigma^{2}
from equation (
16
) (with
i=j
denoting the given model).

3.3
Implementation of the emulators

We build an individual emulator at each fixed projected radius
r_{\rm p}
for central and CGM sources, and at each fixed projected radius
r_{\rm p}
and each fixed observing direction
\mu
for satellite sources.
To address the large dynamical range of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB values, we remove the
(1+z)^{4}
cosmological dimming effect, take the logarithm of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB values, and normalize them by subtracting the median and dividing the standard deviation of the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB values at a fixed projected radius.
After the emulators are constructed, the cosmological dimming effect is incorporated into the emulated
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles.

The input model parameters with their ranges are
\log(M_{\rm h}/{\rm M}_{\odot})=[10,12]
,
z=[2,6]
,
\log c_{0}=[0.0,1.2]
, and
\log f_{\rm esc}=[-1.5,-0.5]
for central and CGM sources, and additionally
R_{\rm sat}/R_{\rm vir}=[0.1,2]
and
\mu=[-1,1]
for satellite sources.
We replace the satellite position parameter
R_{\rm sat}/R_{\rm vir}
with the satellite projected position parameter
R_{\rm sp}\equiv R_{\rm sat}\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}
(in
\rm arcsec
) when constructing the emulators for satellite sources. The new parameter
R_{\rm sp}
can provide better emulating performance because it denotes the projected radius of the peak
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB values. All model parameters are rescaled to be between zero and one.

As discussed in Section
2.4
, the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles can be categorized into non-shielded and shielded systems. We find that adding information about the types of the systems can improve the emulating performance. To achieve that, we introduce a derived model parameter to the above free model parameters for all
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
sources, the peak neutral hydrogen density
\log n_{\rm\mathrm{H\textsc{i}},max}
, defined as the maximum value in the neutral hydrogen density profile after the self-shielding calculation. We find that non-shielded and shielded systems have very different values of
\log n_{\rm\mathrm{H\textsc{i}},max}
(Appendix
A
). This parameter will also be rescaled as mentioned above.

In general, the emulators work well for non-shielded and shielded systems.
But for systems in between, dubbed transitional systems, the emulator performance can become complicated.
Therefore, in our following presentations, we focus on examining the emulator performance and applications for non-shielded and shielded systems, and discuss those for transitional systems in Appendix
A
.

In this section, aiming at applying the emulators to observational data, we use them to fit mock
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles and constrain model parameters.
We discuss the fitting procedure and investigate the recovery of model parameters.

We construct mock
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles based on our model. First, we run RT simulations on 92 testing models selected by the SLHD technique, and obtain their
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles convolved with a typical MUSE PSF of
\rm FWHM=0.7\,arcsec
.

Next, we add noises to the simulated profiles mimicking MUSE observational conditions.
For each system, we first find the observed wavelength range that covers 90 per cent of the simulated
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
photons
(
Wisotzki
et al. 2016
)
, excluding 5 per cent of photons on either tail of the distribution.
Then, we calculate the sky surface brightness by integrating the sky background spectrum, provided by
MUSE exposure time calculator
, within the selected wavelength range.
We set the exposure time to be
36000\,\rm s
and the overall telescope and instrument efficiency to be
0.9
.
Then, we obtain the number of photons received from both the sky and the source, and assign the Poisson noise to the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB value at each radius.

To fit the model to a mock
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profile, we construct the likelihood function

\mathcal{L}_{\rm fit}=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{r_{\rm p}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{i}}\exp\left[-\frac{\left(y_{i}^{(\rm o)}-y_{i}^{(\rm p)}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right],

(20)

where
N_{r_{\rm p}}
is the number of projected radius bins,
y^{(\rm o)}_{i}
and
y^{(\rm p)}_{i}
are the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB values of the
i
th projected radius bin from mock observations and emulators, respectively, and
\sigma_{i}
is the corresponding uncertainty. For uncertainties, we include those from the mock observations (
\sigma^{(\rm o)}_{i}
) and from the emulators [
\sigma^{(\rm p)}_{i}
; equation (
19
)], added in quadrature. Notice that all the terms in the likelihood function are calculated in the linear space of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB values instead of the logarithmic space.
We use the
emcee
package
(
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
to explore the model parameter space.

Below we show our modelling results for two cases, Cen+CGM and Cen+CGM+Sat.
Since the redshifts of LAHs are usually known from observations, we fix the redshifts in modelling
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles.
We treat the peak neutral hydrogen density
n_{\rm\mathrm{H\textsc{i}},max}
as a free parameter as well, given our emulator setup (Section
3.3
).

We first focus on modelling
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles with central and CGM sources. In Fig.
8
, we show fitting examples for non-shielded systems in the top row and for shielded systems in the bottom row.
The grey points are the mock
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles combining central and CGM sources based on randomly selected models in Fig.
5
, and the coloured lines are emulated profiles from the best-fitting models.
The

5
Summary and Discussions

In this paper, based on
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
RT simulations with an analytically parametrized model of gas distribution, we develop emulators for the SB profiles of the extended
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission (on scales of kpc to tens of kpc) around star-forming galaxies. We summarize our main results below.

1.

We construct a parametrized model for
\mathrm{H\textsc{i}}
gas in and around haloes of star-forming galaxies, described by the density, velocity, and temperature profiles.
The self-shielding calculation is performed to obtain the neutral hydrogen densities, accounting for ionizing photons from both the central star formation and the UV background.
It reveals three distinct types of neutral gas distribution, the non-shielded system with the entire system being highly ionized, the shielded systems with the gas forming a shell of a high neutral fraction that shields the inner part from the UV background, and the transitional system in between.
The type of a system depends on model parameters such as halo mass, gas concentration, escaping fraction of ionizing photons from the central galaxy, and redshift.

2.

Three
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
sources are considered in our model, including star formation in the central galaxy, recombination in the CGM, and star formation in the satellite galaxy.
For a given gas model, we run
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
RT simulation for each source to obtain the corresponding
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profile.
In general, the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles of central and CGM sources tend to have similar shapes, with those of central sources being more concentrated and dominated in the inner region.
For satellite sources, a bump can show up in the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
profile around the projected satellite position.
The three types of the neutral gas distribution lead to different features of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles, in particular for central and CGM sources.
The
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles of non-shielded systems approximately drop exponentially.
For shielded systems, the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles drop fast at the centre, creating a concentrated core followed by a roughly exponential tail.
For transitional systems, the central core becomes much more extended compared to shielded systems.

3.

Emulators are constructed for
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles for each of the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
sources based on GPR, which can efficiently predict
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles for given model parameters.
The SLHD technique is adopted to uniformly sample the model parameter space to obtain the models for training the emulators.
With only 180 training models, we demonstrate that the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles predicted by the emulators can properly reproduce those from RT simulations for most cases.
The emulators function well for non-shielded and shielded systems, with the overall accuracy at the level of
\sim 14
per cent and
\sim 30
per cent (Table
1
), respectively.
For transitional systems, they occupy a small fraction of the parameter space.
Their
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles are sensitive to the change of model parameters, which makes it challenging to build emulators with good performance.

4.

We test the emulators by applying them to fit mock
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles.
The mock
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles are produced from a set of testing models, which are different from the training models.
The emulators can fit the mock
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles well, with constraints on parameters like halo mass and gas concentration agreeing well with the underlying truths.

While the model we present in this paper is adequate for the pilot study of developing the emulator framework, more physical ingredients can be added to improve the model.
First, one important ingredient is the gas outflow, which is ubiquitous in star-forming galaxies and affects the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles
(
Zheng &
Miralda-Escudé 2002b
;
Dijkstra &
Loeb 2008
;
Dijkstra &
Kramer 2012
, e.g.)
.
The turbulent motion of gas is simply treated in our model by introducing a scale-independent effective temperature, while the turbulence can couple the gas density and velocity on different scales
(
Kimm
et al. 2019
;
Kimm et al. 2022
;
Chen
et al. 2023
;
Munirov &
Kaurov 2023
, e.g.)
.
The dust in the CGM is another ingredient to be considered
(
Hayes
et al. 2013
;
Duval et al. 2014
;
Laursen
et al. 2013
, e.g.)
.
The collisional excitation/ionization can also be included in the model, which can affect the neutral hydrogen density profiles (through self-shielding calculation) and the intrinsic
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emissivity (i.e. cooling radiation).
All these ingredients collectively shape the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
spectra and modify the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles.
Second, the density, velocity, and temperature distributions of the CGM gas are far from being uniform and isotropic, as a result of processes and structures related to the gas accretion and outflow, leading to anisotropic
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission
(
Laursen &
Sommer-Larsen 2007
;
Verhamme
et al. 2012
;
Zheng &
Wallace 2014
;
Smith
et al. 2022
;
Blaizot
et al. 2023
, e.g.)
.
Finally, the IGM attenuation suppresses the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission and reshapes the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission properties
(
Byrohl &
Gronke 2020
, e.g.)
.
Incorporating these ingredients in the model can help us better understand
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles and
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission in general.

While the performance of our emulators is reasonable, there are possible ways to further improve it. In general, it helps the emulator performance by increasing the number of training models, combining different kernel functions, and re-parametrizing the model.
In our work, we find that the sharp change in
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles between non-shielded and shielded systems makes it difficult to obtain uniformly accurate emulators across the whole parameter space.
With good descriptions of boundaries that separate non-shielded, shielded, and transitional systems in the parameter space, it is possible to build separate emulators for each type of the systems to improve the performance.
Applying dimensional reduction techniques (e.g. Principle Components Analysis) before training emulators allows us to extract important features of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles, potentially making the emulators more robust and reducing the number of emulators to be trained.
Our current emulators are separately built at each fixed radius, but in principle, the correlation of
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB values among different radii can also be accounted for (e.g. with the so-called multi-scale emulation technique;
Dumerchat &
Bautista 2024
) to further improve the emulator accuracy.
While we focus on
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles in this work, our study can be extended to emulate the IFU data cube of the
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission.

Our emulators can be applied to fit
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles based on galaxy formation simulations
(
Lake et al. 2015
;
Byrohl
et al. 2021
;
Byrohl &
Nelson 2023
, e.g.)
.
By comparing the gas distribution from our modelling results and that from galaxy formation simulations, we can validate our modelling choices, understand the connections between model parameters and CGM properties, and provide valuable insights for future model developments.
We will apply our emulators to model
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles of individual star-forming galaxies from IFU observations
(
Leclercq
et al. 2017
, e.g.)
and stacked
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
SB profiles from narrow-band imaging observations
(
Kikuta
et al. 2023
, e.g.)
, which will not only probe the CGM gas but also advance our understanding of the extended
\mathrm{Ly\alpha}
emission.
