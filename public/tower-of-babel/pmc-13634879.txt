Pregnancy-Related AKI in South Asia: Etiology, Practice Patterns, Outcomes, and System Barriers
Authors: Priti Meena, Rubina Naqvi, Krishna Kumar Agrawaal, Shubhathi Kar, Bimal Pandey, Mohammad Ahad Qayyum, Eranga Wijewickrama, Dilushi Wijayaratne, Sayful Hasan, Jagdish Lamsal, Vinant Bhargava
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Pregnancy-related acute kidney injury (PrAKI) remains an important cause of maternal and fetal morbidity, with a disproportionately high burden in low- and middle-income countries.1, 2, 3 Despite remarkable changes in obstetric care across South Asia, regional data on its etiologic spectrum, management practices, and outcomes remain limited. We conducted a multinational survey of 234 nephrologists from India (n = 106), Pakistan (n = 75), Nepal (n = 24), Bangladesh (n = 22), Sri Lanka (n = 5), and Maldives (n = 2) to understand clinician-reported PrAKI etiologies, diagnostic and treatment practices, outcomes, and barriers to care. Detailed country-wise findings are provided in Table 1. The survey highlights an evolving spectrum of PrAKI in South Asia. Septic abortion–associated acute kidney injury, historically an important cause, was reported to account for <10% of PrAKI by most respondents, whereas puerperal sepsis, obstetric hemorrhage, and hypertensive disorders remained important contributors. However, diagnostic capacity has not evolved uniformly with this changing spectrum. ADAMTS13 testing was reported to be available to only 9.4% of respondents in India, 14.7% in Pakistan, 4.2% in Nepal, and none in Bangladesh, whereas full thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) genetic evaluation was reported to be performed in > 25% of cases by only 32.1%, 8.0%, 4.2%, and 0%, respectively. Although hemodialysis was universally available, access to disease-specific therapy remained limited; eculizumab was reported to be available by only 40% of respondents in India, 25.3% in Pakistan, 13.6% in Bangladesh, and 8.3% in Nepal, with actual use substantially lower. Reported outcomes were suboptimal. Clinician-reported median estimated complete renal recovery was only 40%–50%. Chronic kidney disease progression in >25% of survivors was reported by 44.6% and 44.0% of respondents from India and Pakistan, respectively, whereas end-stage kidney disease progression >10% was reported by 42.9% and 53.3%, respectively. Delayed presentation and referral were the most frequently identified barriers across countries. The reported outcomes in our survey were broadly consistent with previously published literature.1,4,5 In a recent systematic review from India, 12.8%–35% of women progressed to chronic kidney disease and up to 30% remained dialysis-dependent. Similarly, a global meta-analysis reported 72.5% complete renal recovery, 8.5% dialysis dependence, and 2.4% progression to end-stage kidney disease, whereas another systematic review identified hypertensive disorders, obstetric hemorrhage, and sepsis as the predominant etiologies of PrAKI. The lower complete renal recovery reported by our respondents (40%–50%) may reflect the restriction of survey participants in tertiary referral centers, where more severe, renal replacement therapy-requiring, and complex PrAKI cases are more commonly managed. Supplementary Figure S1 shows some key suggestions to improve PrAKI care in low- and middle-income countries and/or low-income countries. The spectrum of PrAKI in South Asia reflects a changing landscape in which progress in maternal health care coexists with persistent gaps in PrAKI care. The decline in septic abortion–related acute kidney injury reflects important progress in maternal health care; however, the persistent burden of preventable kidney injury, delayed recognition, and poor long-term outcomes highlights the substantial work that remains.
