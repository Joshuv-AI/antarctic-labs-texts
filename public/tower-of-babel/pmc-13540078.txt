Adequate tetanus toxoid immunization and its correlates among married women with a recent live birth in Afghanistan: Insights from a national survey
Authors: Muhammad Haroon Stanikzai, Essa Tawfiq, Zainab Ezadi, Massoma Jafari, Omid Dadras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to determine the prevalence and correlates of adequate tetanus toxoid (TT) immunization among married women in Afghanistan. This cross-sectional study analyzed data from the 2022–2023 Afghanistan Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS), focusing on 12,450 married women aged 15–49y who had a live birth in the two years preceding the survey. Multivariable logistic regression determined the correlates of adequate TT immunization. The prevalence of adequate TT immunization was 49.9% (95% CI 48.2%–51.6%). The odds of adequate TT immunization were higher in women with primary (AOR 1.32) and secondary/higher (AOR 1.57) education, in women who used 1–3 (AOR 3.06) and ≥4 (AOR 4.67) antenatal care (ANC) visits, those living in Central High region (AOR 4.20), those with access to media (AOR 1.29), and in women belonging to second (AOR 1.33), third (AOR 1.25) and fourth (AOR 1.26) household wealth quintiles. On the other hand, the likelihood of adequate TT immunization was lower in women aged 40–49y (AOR 0.76) and those living in the East (AOR 0.76) and West (AOR 0.68) regions of Afghanistan. Only half of married women in Afghanistan had adequate TT immunization, highlighting substantial gaps in coverage. Strengthening health education, improving equitable access to immunization services, and enhancing communication through mass media; particularly targeting older, less-educated, and economically disadvantaged women, are essential to improve TT coverage and progress toward maternal and neonatal tetanus elimination in Afghanistan.KEYWORDS: Afghanistan, immunization, married women, tetanus toxoid, TT, vaccination

Background

Tetanus is a bacterial infection caused by Clostridium tetani, a microorganism that produces a potent neurotoxin responsible for the disease’s severe manifestations.1,2 It is a non-contagious but life-threatening illness that continues to contribute significantly to neonatal and maternal mortality in numerous low-and middle-income countries (LMICs). Maternal and neonatal tetanus occur when mothers engage in unhygienic practices during delivery.3

In Afghanistan, home deliveries remain common. The 2018 Afghanistan Health Survey (AHS 2018) reported that 41% of women gave birth at home,4 while the 2023 Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS 2022–2023) found that 32.5% of women delivered without assistance from a skilled birth provider.5 These practices are concerning because they substantially increase the risk of neonatal tetanus. Infants born to mothers who lack complete tetanus toxoid (TT) immunization are particularly vulnerable.6 Without adequate immunization, tetanus infection can lead to severe muscle spasms, respiratory failure, and death. Globally, unvaccinated individuals in low-income countries face a mortality rate of about 50%, with more than 30,000 cases reported each year.7

Recognizing the global threat posed by maternal and neonatal tetanus, the World Health Assembly has prioritized maternal and neonatal tetanus elimination as a global public health objective, with measurable progress recorded over the past two decades.8 Although there have been considerable improvements over the last twenty years,6 the administration of TT vaccines among pregnant women remains low in Afghanistan. According to the 2015 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS 2015), only 53.9% of Afghan women had sufficient TT vaccination coverage during their most recent pregnancy.9

According to World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations, women with no prior record of TT vaccination should receive a series of five properly spaced doses. The first two doses should be administered one month apart during the first pregnancy. The third dose is given at least 6 months after the second dose, followed by one dose in each subsequent pregnancy or at intervals of no less than 1 y, culminating in 5 doses.10 However, only 34% of women with a live birth in the 5 y preceding the AHS 2018 received two or more injections during their last pregnancy.4

Evidence from other LMICs highlights that TT immunization coverage is influenced by a range of individual and contextual factors. A multilevel analysis across six countries found that factors such as marital status, wealth index, age at first birth, decision about women’s health care, parity, sex of household head, antenatal care (ANC) visits, postnatal checkup, distance to health facility, and health insurance were identified as significant predictors of sufficient TT vaccination.11 Additionally, other studies have indicated that factors such as place of delivery,12,13 decision on women’s health care,14,15 and community illiteracy,16 also correlate with TT vaccination rates.

Given its effectiveness, TT vaccination is an essential component of antenatal care and a cornerstone of efforts to prevent maternal and neonatal deaths due to tetanus.17 Nevertheless, an estimated 79 million women and their infants worldwide remained unprotected, leaving them susceptible to infection and preventable death.18 The TT vaccine is affordable and can be administered at any point during pregnancy to safeguard women of childbearing age and their newborns from tetanus during childbirth.19,20 It is estimated that widespread maternal TT immunization could reduce neonatal mortality by 94%.17

To our knowledge, only one previous study, in Afghanistan, has investigated associations between residential area and sufficient antenatal TT immunization among pregnant women, using data from the Afghanistan DHS 2015.9 However, further work is necessary to determine the contemporary prevalence of adequate TT immunization among married women in Afghanistan. Additionally, other correlates, including regional disparities, should be explored. Therefore, in this study, we report the contemporary prevalence of adequate TT immunization and its correlates among married women who had a live birth in the past two years prior to the survey of MICS 2022–23 in Afghanistan.

Methods

Study design and data source

In this cross-sectional study, we used data from the Afghanistan MICS 2022–2023.21 The MICS 2022–2023 used a nationally representative sample and applied a two-stage cluster sampling design. The sampling design and data collection process are reported elsewhere.21 During the survey, trained surveyors collected data from women of reproductive age, 15–49 y old, who answered questions related to child and maternal health and nutrition. For this study, we analyzed data from 12,450 married women who had a live birth in the past two years prior to the survey (Figure 1).

Final sample size and schematic presentation of the sample selection.

Study variables

Outcome variable

The outcome was adequate TT immunization, and was defined based on the WHO guidelines and operationalized as (i) receiving two or more doses of TT during current pregnancy; or (ii) one dose of TT during current pregnancy and one dose during before current pregnancy; or (iii) two doses of TT within 3 y before current pregnancy; or (iv) three doses of TT within 5 y before current pregnancy; or (v) four doses of TT within 10 y before current pregnancy. From the above, we created a binary outcome variable (adequate TT immunization vs. inadequate TT immunization).10

Independent variables

The independent variables were woman’s age (15–29, 30–39, and 40–49 y), woman’s education level (no formal education, primary education, and secondary or higher education), education level of the household head (no formal education, primary education, and secondary or higher education), place of residence (urban vs. rural), regions of Afghanistan (Central, Central high, East, East north, East south, North, South, West),21 ANC utilization (no visit, 1–3 visits, ≥4 visits), household wealth index (lowest quintile up to highest quintile), birth order (first child vs. second and more children), and media access (yes vs. no). Media access was defined as “yes” if the woman watched TV daily, or she listened to the radio daily, or she read newspapers, and as “no,” otherwise.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics of women and to explore the distribution of adequate TT immunization across categories of explanatory variables. The chi-square test was used to assess the association between each independent variable and TT immunization status. To identify determinants of adequate TT immunization, binary logistic regression analysis was employed. Both bivariate and multivariable models were fitted. Variables with p-values < .25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model, in accordance with recommendations from previous literature.3,11,14–16,22 Because ANC utilization and type of ANC provider were conceptually overlapping variables, and because women with no ANC visit would also have no ANC provider, type of ANC provider was not retained in the final multivariable model. Multicollinearity among variables included in the final model was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and no evidence of problematic multicollinearity was observed. The results are presented as crude odds ratios (CORs) and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses accounted for the complex sampling design by applying sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units (PSUs) to ensure representativeness of the national population. Data analysis was performed using STATA version 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).23 We regarded a two-sided p-value of .05 as statistically significant. As a post-hoc sensitivity analysis, we repeated the multivariable logistic regression after stratifying the sample by ANC utilization status, comparing women who used ANC services with those who did not. This analysis was conducted to assess whether the association between rural residence and adequate TT immunization differed according to ANC service use, given that TT vaccination is commonly delivered or discussed during ANC contacts (See Supplementary File 1).

Results

Table 1 presents baseline characteristics of women, by status of TT uptake (adequate vs. inadequate). The weighted percentage of adequate TT vaccination was 49.9% (95% CI 48.2% −51.6%). Out of 12,450 women, 60.5% were 15–29 y of age. Statistically significant differences in TT uptake were observed across several characteristics, including the education level of women and the head of the household, residential area, household wealth status, regions of residence, ANC utilization, birth order, and media access. In general, women with higher educational attainment, greater household wealth, and better access to healthcare and media were more likely to have received adequate TT immunization. For example, TT uptake was notably higher among women in the fourth wealth quintile (22.0% of adequately immunized women vs. 17.5% of inadequately immunized women), among those who attended four or more ANC visits (43.0% vs. 23.8%), and among those with media access (24.0% vs. 16.1%) (Table 1).

Findings from the multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 2) indicate that women aged 40–49 y were significantly less likely to have received adequate TT immunization compared with those aged 15–29 y (AOR 0.76; 95% CI 0.62–0.93). Similarly, women residing in the East (AOR 0.76; 95% CI 0.59–1.00) and West (AOR 0.68; 95% CI 0.53–0.88) regions were less likely to be adequately immunized compared with those in the Central region. In contrast, higher odds of adequate TT immunization were observed among women with primary (AOR 1.32; 95% CI 1.05–1.67) and secondary or higher education (AOR 1.57; 95% CI 1.28–1.92) compared with those without formal education, as well as women who had 1–3 ANC visits (AOR 3.06; 95% CI 2.65–3.53) and those with ≥4 ANC visits (AOR 4.67; 95% CI 4.02–5.44) compared with women who did not use ANC services. Likewise, women in the second (AOR 1.33; 95% CI 1.12–1.57), third (AOR 1.25; 95% CI 1.05–1.48), and fourth (AOR 1.26; 95% CI 1.04–1.53) household wealth quintiles were more likely to be adequately immunized compared with those in the lowest quintile.

Regional variations were also evident: women residing in the Central High region were more than four times as likely to have adequate TT immunization as those in the Central region (AOR 4.20; 95% CI 2.94–6.00). Access to media was another significant factor, with women with media exposure having higher odds of adequate immunization than those without such access (AOR 1.29; 95% CI 1.08–1.54). Rural residence was not significantly associated with adequate TT immunization in the main multivariable model, although the direction of the association suggested higher odds among rural women compared with urban women (AOR 1.21; 95% CI 0.98–1.48; p = .059). In the post-hoc stratified analysis, rural residence was not associated with adequate TT immunization among women who did not use ANC services (AOR 0.81; 95% CI 0.55–1.20), whereas rural women who used ANC services had higher odds of adequate TT immunization than urban women who used ANC services (AOR 1.31; 95% CI 1.07–1.60). This pattern suggests that the rural–urban difference may partly depend on ANC contact rather than reflecting a general rural advantage in TT immunization.

Discussion

This study analyzed the prevalence and correlates of adequate TT immunization among married women with a recent live birth in Afghanistan. The findings revealed that only 49.9% of married women had received adequate TT immunization. Several factors, including women’s age, education, ANC utilization, media access, household wealth, and residential region, were associated with adequate TT immunization.

The observed prevalence of adequate TT immunization (49.9%) is consistent with previous findings from Afghanistan (51%),9 and other LMICs, such as Ethiopia (52.2%),1 East Africa (47.0%),2 and Sub-Saharan Africa (51.5%).24 These comparable rates across LMICs underscore that insufficient TT coverage remains a widespread public health challenge. Given that childbirth often carries a risk of tetanus infection, particularly under unhygienic delivery conditions, inadequate TT immunization coverage poses serious implications for maternal and neonatal health. The findings of this study therefore highlight the urgent need for strengthened, targeted interventions to enhance TT immunization coverage among Afghan married women.

In the present study, the association between age and adequate TT immunization differed after adjustment. Women aged 30–39 y had lower crude odds of adequate TT immunization, but this association disappeared in the adjusted model, suggesting that their lower crude uptake was likely explained by other factors such as education, region of residence, ANC use, birth order, media access, and household wealth. In contrast, women aged 40–49 y remained significantly less likely to have adequate TT immunization even after adjustment. In Afghanistan, this may be partly because childbearing commonly begins earlier in life, and women who give birth at older reproductive ages may often be higher-parity women who rely more on previous pregnancy and birth experiences and perceive less need for routine ANC or preventive services.25 This is particularly important because TT vaccination is closely linked to ANC and is a core component of maternal preventive care.26 Therefore, lower engagement with ANC among older women may reduce opportunities for TT counseling, vaccination, or documentation. This pattern aligns with evidence from other LMICs,22,24 and earlier research in Afghanistan.9 Prior studies from Afghanistan similarly report that older women are less likely to utilize essential maternal and child health services.26–28 These findings suggest that age-specific strategies are needed to increase TT immunization coverage, ensuring that older women remain engaged in routine maternal healthcare.

Women’s educational attainment plays a crucial role in the optimal utilization of maternal healthcare services. We observed that women with primary and secondary/higher education had greater odds of receiving adequate TT immunization than those with no formal education. This finding corroborates earlier studies conducted in East Africa,2 and Ethiopia,1 emphasizing that education enhances women’s health literacy, decision-making autonomy, and awareness of vaccination benefits.29 Similar evidence from Afghanistan highlights the crucial role of maternal education in promoting utilization of maternal and child healthcare services.26,28 However, the recent restrictions on women’s secondary and higher education in the country are worrisome and could have dire consequences for the hard-won gains in maternal and child health.30,31 In light of these findings, this study advocates for female education in the country. Additionally, community-based interventions are warranted to improve reproductive health literacy and expand awareness of the importance of adequate TT immunization among married women.

Although household head education was associated with adequate TT immunization in the crude analysis, this association became non-significant after adjustment. This attenuation may suggest that household head education partly operates through related factors, such as women’s own education, household wealth, media access, and ANC utilization. In Afghanistan, the education of the household head may influence women’s ability to reach ANC through household decision-making, permission, transport, and financial support.32 However, because TT vaccination is commonly integrated into ANC contacts,26 the immediate decision to understand counseling, recall prior doses, ask questions, and accept vaccination may depend more directly on the woman’s own education and health literacy.33 In conservative household structures, women’s use of maternal health services may also be shaped by other family members, including husbands, mothers-in-law, and mothers, which were not captured in this analysis.34 Therefore, household-level interventions remain important, but they should be combined with direct health education for women.

ANC utilization emerged as one of the strongest correlates of adequate TT immunization in this study. Women who used ANC services had substantially higher odds of adequate TT immunization than those who did not, which is consistent with previous evidence from Afghanistan,9 and other settings.35,36 ANC visits provide an important platform for maternal and child healthcare services, including health facility delivery, postnatal care, family planning, counseling, and immunization.26 ANC contacts provide an important opportunity to screen women’s tetanus vaccination history, administer indicated TT doses, and counsel women on the timing and benefits of adequate immunization for preventing maternal and neonatal tetanus.10 Nonetheless, only 43% of pregnant women in Afghanistan received the previously recommended four ANC visits,32 indicating a substantial service-coverage gap. Improving ANC attendance and quality, including systematic counseling on TT immunization and clearer assessment of prior TT dose history during ANC contacts, could therefore contribute to higher and more equitable TT coverage.

ANC contact may also help explain the observed rural-residence pattern. Although rural residence was not significantly associated with adequate TT immunization in the main adjusted model, the post-hoc stratified analysis showed that rural women had higher odds of adequate TT immunization only among those who used ANC services. This suggests that the rural–urban pattern should not be interpreted as a general rural advantage in TT immunization; rather, it may reflect the role of ANC as a key point of contact through which rural women who engage with services receive vaccination counseling or TT immunization. In Afghanistan, this pattern may also be shaped by local service-delivery dynamics, including the historical emphasis of the Basic Package of Health Services (BPHS) on primary care, maternal and child health services, immunization, and community-based delivery through community-level health workers.37 Female health workers are particularly important for women’s access to care in Afghanistan’s conservative sociocultural context, where many women may face barriers to receiving care from male providers.38 At the same time, insecurity, displacement, funding instability, and disruptions to routine services may affect immunization delivery differently across settings and may particularly undermine access for displaced, marginalized, or otherwise hard-to-reach populations.39,40 However, because the MICS data did not directly measure conflict exposure, displacement status, outreach intensity, or contact with community health workers or traditional birth attendants, these explanations should be considered plausible contextual interpretations rather than confirmed mechanisms.

The prevalence of adequate TT immunization was uneven in the present study, varying across eight regions of Afghanistan. Women living in the Central High region had significantly higher odds of adequate TT immunization, whereas those in the East and West regions were less likely to be adequately immunized. Such disparities echo findings from other LMICs, where regional inequality in maternal healthcare access is well documented.14,24,29,41 These differences may stem from variations in infrastructure, security, healthcare availability, or outreach capacity across Afghanistan’s regions. Addressing these inequalities will require region-specific strategies and further research to understand contextual barriers to immunization in underserved areas.

Another key determinant identified in this study was media access. Women with exposure to mass media were more likely to receive adequate TT immunization than those without such access. This finding aligns with numerous studies across LMICs that underscore the importance of media exposure in shaping awareness and utilization of maternal and child health services.1,2,42,43 Evidence from Afghanistan also highlights the influential role of media campaigns in improving maternal and child healthcare practices.44,45 Given that mass-media promotion has long been a component of public health programming in Afghanistan,27,46 sustained and innovative media-based interventions could be pivotal in further increasing TT immunization coverage.

Lastly, socioeconomic status emerged as an important factor. The existing evidence in the Afghan literature demonstrates significant wealth-related disparities in access and utilization of maternal healthcare services.28,32 Similarly, in this study, women from wealthier households (second to fourth quintiles) had higher odds of adequate TT immunization compared with those from the poorest households. This finding mirrors results from Sub-Saharan Africa,24 East Africa,2 and elsewhere,41 reflecting the consistent influence of household wealth on healthcare utilization. However, this association was not observed among women in the highest wealth quintile, suggesting a non-linear relationship between household wealth and TT uptake. In Afghanistan, this pattern may be less related to the direct cost of the vaccine itself, as routine immunization is generally provided through public services, and more related to differences in service pathways, outreach exposure, recall, documentation, and health-seeking behavior.47 Women in middle wealth quintiles may have sufficient resources to overcome transportation and opportunity costs while still being reached through public ANC, outreach, and immunization services. In contrast, the apparent advantage among the wealthiest women may be partly explained by other factors included in the adjusted model, such as education, residence, region, ANC use, and media access. Wealthier women may also be more concentrated in urban areas and may use facility-based or private ANC services where TT vaccination may not always be emphasized, remembered, or documented in the same way as public immunization outreach.29 These results emphasize the importance of addressing economic barriers that limit immunization access among socioeconomically disadvantaged women, for instance through community outreach, transportation support, or incentive-based immunization programs.

Limitations

While our study provides important insights into the contemporary prevalence and correlates of adequate TT immunization among married women in Afghanistan, several limitations inherent to cross-sectional surveys should be acknowledged. First, data collected in the MICS survey were self-reported and are prone to information and recall biases, which might affect the validity of the findings. Second, there is a temporal mismatch for some women who may have received the TT vaccine long before the sampling period for this study, because the outcome includes TT doses up to ten years before the current pregnancy of the women who had a live birth in the past two years prior to the survey. Third, there may be a potential measurement bias, because out of the 6,101 women with adequate TT immunization reported in this study, 4,092 of them (67.1%) could show their vaccination card at the time of survey, and 799 of women (13.1%) said they had a vaccination card, but they could not find it at the time of survey, and the remaining 1,210 women (19.8%) did not have a vaccination card; therefor, the last two categories of women reported their TT vaccination from memories. Fourth, the MICS data restricted our evaluation of factors affecting adequate TT immunization among study participants. Future research should explore the influence of other factors, such as family planning use, indexed birth status, distance to a health facility, and maternal knowledge of TT doses, on the uptake of adequate TT immunization. Lastly, the cross-sectional nature of MICS data makes it difficult to establish a temporal relationship between the associated factors and adequate TT immunization.

Conclusion

This study revealed that only half of Afghan married women with a recent live birth have received adequate TT immunization, underscoring a persistent gap in protection against maternal and neonatal tetanus. The findings highlight that women’s age, education, ANC utilization, residential region, household wealth, and media access significantly influence TT immunization uptake. To accelerate progress toward maternal and neonatal tetanus elimination, Afghanistan’s immunization strategy should adopt more geographically and culturally targeted approaches. Regions with lower TT coverage, particularly the East and West, should be prioritized for intensified outreach, mobile ANC-immunization services, and high-risk district activities. Health education should also be tailored to older and less-literate women through culturally acceptable channels, including female community health workers, midwives, and trusted local or religious leaders. In addition, health-promotion efforts should not rely on television alone; radio messages and community-based in-person sessions may be more accessible for women with low literacy and restricted mobility. Integrating these strategies into ANC contacts, community outreach, and improved TT documentation could help increase equitable protection among women and newborns in Afghanistan.
