Radiofrequency ablation of long-standing persistent atrial fibrillation in a patient with dextrocardia, interrupted inferior vena cava and mirrored persistent left superior vena cava
Authors: Yu-Lan Ma, Lu-Zhen Wang, Xu-Ming Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCatheter ablation is an established treatment for atrial fibrillation (AF). However, AF ablation in patients with complex congenital anatomical variants, including mirror dextrocardia, interrupted inferior vena cava (IVC), and persistent left superior vena cava (PLSVC), remains technically challenging, with limited published clinical experience.Case presentationWe report a 60-year-old female patient with long-standing persistent AF and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, who had mirror dextrocardia, interrupted IVC, and PLSVC. The procedure was performed via a left internal jugular vein approach for transseptal puncture and ablation. The strategy included bilateral pulmonary vein isolation, linear ablation of the left atrial roof, mitral isthmus, and hepatic venous - tricuspid isthmus, plus electrical isolation of the PLSVC, all guided by intracardiac echocardiography and electroanatomic mapping.OutcomesAt 17 months of follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no documented atrial arrhythmia recurrence. Left ventricular ejection fraction improved from 36% to 46%.ConclusionsThis single case demonstrates that in patients with complex anatomical variants and long-standing persistent AF, AF ablation incorporating targeted PLSVC electrical isolation via a left internal jugular vein approach under intracardiac echocardiography guidance is feasible, safe, and clinically effective. Further experience is needed to confirm the generalizability of this strategy.

Introduction

Dextrocardia with situs inversus, interrupted inferior vena cava (IVC), and mirrored persistent left superior vena cava (PLSVC) are all rare congenital heart malformations. Dextrocardia with situs inversus occurs in approximately 0.02% of the general population [1]. Congenital interruption of the IVC occurs in approximately 0.15% in the general population and 8% of patients with dextrocardia [2–3]. The incidence of PLSVC is approximately 0.5% in the general population and about 3%-10% in patients with congenital heart disease [4]. The incidence of the coexistence of the three anatomical abnormalities in the general population is still unknown. Currently, there are published reports on radiofrequency (RF) ablation for persistent atrial fibrillation (AF) in patients with dextrocardia and interrupted IVC [5]; however, no treatment reports exist for patients with the aforementioned anatomical abnormalities complicated by mirrored PLSVC. Such intricate anatomical anomalies considerably elevate the challenges associated with ablation. Contemporary AF guidelines emphasize individualized strategies and multimodality imaging for complex ablation cases [6]. Recent reports have further highlighted the technical difficulties of transseptal puncture and vascular access in patients with congenital venous anomalies [7–10]. In addition, the role of ICE in facilitating transseptal puncture under atypical anatomies has also been validated [7, 8]. We present a case of long-standing persistent AF accompanied by dextrocardia, interrupted IVC, and mirrored PLSVC.

Timeline of clinical course

Onset of symptoms: Palpitations persisted for 5 years, with worsening bilateral lower extremity edema in the past 2 weeks.

Initial diagnosis: Long-standing persistent atrial fibrillation complicated by heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), combined with congenital mirror dextrocardia, interrupted IVC, and PLSVC.

Treatment: Medical optimization for heart failure followed by radiofrequency catheter ablation.

Case report

A 60-year-old female patient (body weight 80 kg) presented with palpitations for more than 5 years, which had worsened with bilateral lower limb edema over the preceding two weeks. She had been recently diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus and had no prior history of cardiovascular disease or other comorbidities. The patient had a known preoperative diagnosis of congenital mirror dextrocardia before admission. After admission, chest radiography was performed first to confirm dextrocardia with situs inversus (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, electrocardiography (ECG) was obtained using a mirrored lead configuration, which confirmed AF (Fig. 1B). Transthoracic echocardiography and 64-slice multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) venography performed 24 h before ablation revealed mirror dextrocardia, segmental absence of the IVC at the hepatic segment, dilatation and tortuosity of the azygos vein draining into the left superior vena cava (SVC), and PLSVC (Fig. 2A and D). MDCT venography excluded intracardiac thrombus. The patient exhibited significantly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF = 36%), left atrial anteroposterior diameter of 42 mm, and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter of 60 mm. These malformations were further confirmed by intraoperative angiography (Fig. 2E and F). The patient was diagnosed with long-standing persistent AF complicated by HFrEF. After optimization of heart failure therapy, RF catheter ablation was scheduled.

Vascular access strategy

The procedure was performed under conscious sedation with continuous intravenous fentanyl infusion. Following right femoral vein puncture, an initial 3000 IU of heparin was administered intravenously. A ViewFlex™ Xtra intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) catheter (Abbott) was inserted via the femoral vein and positioned in the azygos vein at the left atrial level to exclude thrombus. A quadripolar catheter was advanced via the femoral vein through the azygos vein into the connection between the left SVC and right atrium (RA), and placed within the PLSVC. Due to segmental absence of the IVC, conventional transfemoral atrial septal puncture was not feasible. Therefore, the left internal jugular vein was selected as the primary access for atrial septal puncture and ablation. An Agillis™ NxT steerable introducer sheath (Abbott) was advanced into the RA over a 0.035” hydrophilic J-tipped guidewire via the left internal jugular vein. Superior approach via internal jugular vein has been reported as a safe and feasible alternative in patients with interrupted IVC or inaccessible femoral access [8].

Mapping protocol

An Advisor High-Density Grid Mapping Catheter (HD; Abbott) was introduced through the Agillis sheath, and three-dimensional models of the RA and PLSVC were constructed using the EnSite 3000 NavX system. During reconstruction, prominent muscular sleeve potentials were identified within the PLSVC. Following successful transseptal puncture, the HD catheter was reinserted to reconstruct three-dimensional geometries of the left atrium (LA) and bilateral pulmonary veins (PVs).

Ablation strategy

All ablations were performed using a TactiCath™ Contact Force Ablation Catheter (A-TCSE-DD; Abbott). The procedural access routes, catheter pathways, and ablation strategy are illustrated in Fig. 3.

Right-sided ablation

Linear ablation of the hepatic venous-tricuspid isthmus was initially performed from the ventricular aspect at the 6 o’clock position of the tricuspid valve toward the hepatic vein (Fig. 4). This was followed by circumferential ablation along the mitral isthmus within the PLSVC under electrogram guidance (Fig. 4).

Atrial septal puncture

Atrial septal puncture was performed using a steerable curved long sheath and guidewire under combined fluoroscopic and ICE guidance, without a Brockenbrough needle. ICE guidance significantly improves the safety and accuracy of transseptal puncture in complex anatomies [6]. Under fluoroscopy, the coronary sinus electrode served as an anatomical reference to position the sheath and guidewire at the fossa ovalis. The sheath curvature was adjusted, and ICE was used to confirm the spatial relationship between the puncture site and the LA, as well as to verify a distinct tenting sign ensuring adequate tissue apposition (Fig. 5A). Transseptal crossing was achieved using high-frequency electrocautery in pulsed RF mode (25 W, 2 s), rather than manual mechanical puncture. The guidewire was then advanced directly into the LA (Fig. 5B). An additional 5000 IU of heparin was administered after successful puncture.

Left-sided ablation

Bilateral PV antral isolation was performed first, followed by linear ablation of the LA roof and mitral isthmus (Fig. 4). After intravenous administration of midazolam (1 mg), synchronized direct-current cardioversion (200 J) was delivered to restore sinus rhythm.

RF power and Lesion Size Index (LSI) parameters

RF energy was delivered in power-controlled mode at 35–40 W, with contact force maintained at 5–20 g. LSI target: 3.8-4.0 for the LA posterior wall and PLSVC; 4.5 for the LA anterior wall, LA roof, mitral isthmus, and hepatic venous-tricuspid isthmus. A lower LSI was selected for the LA posterior wall due to its proximity to the esophagus and thin-walled structure, supported by clinical evidence confirming balanced safety and efficacy. For the PLSVC, a conservative LSI was adopted given the limited evidence in this anatomical variant; efficacy was verified by post-ablation exit and entrance block.

Endpoint verification

The HD catheter was used to assess acute procedural success. Complete electrical isolation of all PVs and bidirectional block of the mitral isthmus and LA roof were confirmed.

Induction testing with burst pacing via the coronary sinus electrode induced counterclockwise atrial flutter around the tricuspid annulus. A residual gap was detected along the hepatic venous-tricuspid isthmus line, and supplementary ablation was applied to achieve bidirectional block. Durable electrical isolation of the PLSVC was also confirmed. No atrial arrhythmias were inducible following isoproterenol infusion and repeated burst stimulation.

Total procedural time was 212 min, total RF application time was 65 min, and total fluoroscopy time was 30.8 min.

Amiodarone was continued for three months after the ablation procedure. At the 17-month follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic, and ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring showed sinus bradycardia and premature atrial contractions, with no recurrence of atrial arrhythmias. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed an improvement in LVEF from 36% (pre-ablation baseline) to 46%.

Discussion

Guidelines advocate for RF ablation therapy in individuals with AF complicated by HFrEF to enhance left ventricular function by reinstating sinus rhythm [11]. However, there is scant information about the execution of ablation in patients with dextrocardia, interrupted IVC, and mirrored PLSVC, particularly in cases with long-standing persistent AF exacerbated by HFrEF.

To our knowledge, this appears to be the first reported case of bilateral PVs circumferential isolation, in conjunction with linear ablation of the roof, mitral isthmus, and hepatic venous - tricuspid isthmus, as well as circumferential ablation of the mirrored PLSVC, utilizing a single transseptal access in a patient exhibiting dextrocardia, interrupted IVC, and mirrored PLSVC.

The interruption of the IVC in this patient rendered the standard transfemoral venous approach for atrial septal puncture unfeasible. We therefore chose the left internal jugular vein as the access route. In cases with complex anatomy and nonstandard approaches, ICE plays a key role in identifying the fossa ovalis during transseptal puncture via the SVC approach, because the ICE tenting sign can replace the “snap-in” sign typically observed with the IVC approach. A steerable transseptal sheath (Agillis) was used. Adjustment of its curvature allowed reliable cannulation of the optimal atrial septal puncture site.

Given the patient’s history of long-standing persistent atrial fibrillation, isolated pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) may not be sufficient to eliminate the arrhythmia substrate in this complex case. Therefore, individualized additional linear ablation was performed in this patient, tailored to the unique anatomical characteristics (mirror dextrocardia, PLSVC) and the specific AF substrate, rather than as a universal recommendation.

The study by Kim et al. [12] established that among 32 patients with AF and PLSVC, PLSVC triggered AF in 48.4% of cases and served as the driver of AF in 46.9% of cases. In total, PLSVC was either a trigger or a contributor to AF in 22 patients (68.8%). They further validated that isolation of the PLSVC can diminish the recurrence rate of AF. Recent studies further confirmed that PLSVC can serve as a source of focal atrial tachycardia and macroreentrant arrhythmias [13]. The necessity of PLSVC isolation in complex anatomy has also been emphasized [13]. First, the PLSVC exhibits inherent arrhythmogenicity because it contains well-developed myocardial sleeves extending from the atrial wall into the venous wall, similar to the pulmonary veins [12, 13]. These myocardial sleeves contain cardiomyocytes with abnormal automaticity, triggered activity, and heterogeneous conduction, forming a critical non-pulmonary vein trigger source for AF. Second, the myocardial sleeves and veno-atrial electrical connections provide direct pathways for rapid electrical signals to fire into the atrium, initiating AF and maintaining reentrant circuits [12, 13]. In this case, robust myocardial sleeve potentials were recorded inside the PLSVC, confirming its role as a functional arrhythmogenic focus. Third, the PLSVC is anatomically adjacent to the mitral isthmus and may form epicardial electrical connections across the isthmus [12–14]. In our case, circumferential ablation within the PLSVC appeared to eliminate these potential epicardial bridging connections, which significantly facilitated achievement of bidirectional block across the mitral isthmus. This is particularly important in mirror-dextrocardia anatomy, where conventional endocardial block is difficult to achieve [11]. Fourth, elimination of the PLSVC arrhythmogenic substrate removes a key source of recurrent AF triggers and reentry circuits. Combined with pulmonary vein isolation and linear ablation, PLSVC isolation directly reduces the risk of late recurrence and helps maintain long-term sinus rhythm, thereby promoting reverse cardiac remodeling and improvement of left ventricular function [13, 14]. The established muscle sleeve potentials in the PLSVC can not only propagate across the RA, acting as a trigger for AF, but also establish a connection with the mitral isthmus via the epicardium, so creating a mechanism for sustaining AF. Given the protracted duration of AF in this patient, we conducted circumferential ablation within the mirrored PLSVC, which significantly facilitated the bidirectional block of the mitral isthmus and the electrical separation of the endovenous trigger point. Our experience is consistent with recent reports highlighting the safety and efficacy of individualized ablation strategies in complex congenital anomalies [10, 15, 16].

Inability to establish bidirectional block following linear ablation may result in the development of iatrogenic atrial flutter. To prevent repeat atrial septal puncture, burst stimulation was administered to this patient upon conversion to sinus rhythm, without confirming the bidirectional block of the hepatic venous - tricuspid isthmus, resulting in a counterclockwise atrial flutter reliant on the hepatic venous - tricuspid isthmus. During the supplemental ablation at the hepatic venous - tricuspid isthmus gap, the patient’s atrial flutter ceased, underscoring the need of confirming bidirectional block following linear ablation. Despite the heightened interventional surgical complexity due to intricate anatomical anomalies, all linear ablations in the patient finally resulted in bidirectional block. This offers a robust assurance for the patient’s positive prognosis, encompassing the absence of atrial arrhythmias recurrence and the restoration of left ventricular function.

This study has several limitations inherent to a single-case report design. First, the findings are based on only one patient with a rare combination of mirror dextrocardia, interrupted inferior vena cava, and mirrored PLSVC; therefore, the generalizability of the reported procedural strategy and ablation approach remains limited. Second, long-term efficacy beyond 17 months of follow-up is unknown, and longer observation periods are required to confirm the durability of sinus rhythm maintenance. Third, the procedural strategy was highly individualized according to the patient’s unique anatomy, and the technical feasibility may vary depending on operator experience, equipment availability, and specific anatomical variations. Fourth, we did not perform invasive electrophysiological assessment of myocardial sleeve distribution or conduction properties within the PLSVC before ablation; thus, detailed mechanistic evidence is preliminary. Fifth, as a non-randomized observational report, no comparison with alternative approaches (e.g., surgical ablation, other endovascular strategies) could be conducted to determine superiority or equivalence. Accordingly, the conclusions should be interpreted cautiously, and further cumulative experience and larger series are warranted to validate the safety, efficacy, and procedural standards of this approach in patients with similar complex anatomical anomalies.
