The effect of Bartholin gland diseases on female sexual function, sexual distress, and genital self-image: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Funda Dağıstanlı, Pınar Fatma Yıldız, İhsan Şafak, Mustafa Ayhan Ekici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBartholin gland disease impairs sexual function, yet most studies focus on surgical technique rather than the broader sexual health burden, and none has systematically examined sexual distress or genital self-image in this population. We aimed to characterize the sexual health burden of Bartholin gland disease across four domains: pain (Visual Analogue Scale [VAS]), sexual function (Female Sexual Function Index [FSFI]), sexual distress (Female Sexual Distress Scale-Revised [FSDS-R]), and genital self-image (Female Genital Self-Image Scale [FGSIS]), at two time points (before and after treatment) and to compare findings against matched healthy controls.MethodsThis prospective cohort study enrolled 25 women diagnosed with a Bartholin gland cyst or abscess (patient group) and 25 age-matched healthy controls at a single training and research hospital. Patients were evaluated twice: at presentation before any treatment (T1) and after symptom resolution (T2). Controls were assessed once. Outcome measures were the VAS (applied to patients only), FSFI (cut-off <26.55), FSDS-R (cut-off ≥11), and FGSIS. Sample size was calculated a priori (d = 0.80, α = 0.05, power = 80%; G*Power 3.1).Results and DiscussionGroups were comparable for age (36.1 ± 6.6 vs. 34.2 ± 7.6 years, P = .472), body mass index (BMI), and parity. At T1, 48.0% of patients had clinically significant sexual dysfunction (FSFI <26.55) and 16.0% met the distress threshold (FSDS-R ≥ 11). All four scales improved significantly from T1 to T2 (P < .001 for each). By T2, FSFI (31.72 ± 3.34 vs. 31.11 ± 1.87, P = .187), FSDS-R (1.44 ± 1.69 vs. 0.56 ± 0.65, P = .068), and FGSIS (24.40 ± 2.33 vs. 23.20 ± 1.94, P = .066) no longer differed from controls. Notably, FGSIS was already similar between patients and controls at T1 (22.92 ± 3.28 vs. 23.20 ± 1.94, P = .784). VAS pain scores fell from 5.96 ± 2.64 to 1.04 ± 0.20 (effect size dz = 1.906). T1 FSFI scores did not differ by pathology type (cyst vs. abscess, P = .479) or surgical procedure (P = .866). Strong correlations were observed between FSFI and FSDS-R (r = −0.829, P < .001) and between VAS and FSDS-R (r = +0.561, P = .004). Bartholin gland disease carries a measurable sexual health burden that substantially resolves after treatment. Genital self-image appears to remain intact even during active disease. Systematic screening with FSFI and FSDS-R should be integrated into routine management; surgical decision-making should account for the higher dyspareunia risk associated with marsupialization.

Introduction

Bartholin glands, also known as the major vestibular glands, are a pair of pea-sized exocrine glands located at approximately the 4- and 8-o’clock positions of the vaginal vestibule.1,2 Although their anatomy and physiology remain incompletely understood (with significant gaps in knowledge regarding embryology, innervation, and vasculature), they are presumed to contribute mucinous secretion during sexual arousal, supporting lubrication and mucosal integrity.1–3 Ductal obstruction leads to cyst formation; secondary infection produces an abscess. Roughly 2%–3% of women develop a symptomatic cyst or abscess at some point, and these account for ~2% of all gynaecological outpatient visits.1,4,5 Incidence peaks between ages 20 and 29 and declines thereafter as the glands undergo involution.4

Bartholin gland disease can impair all phases of the sexual response cycle, specifically desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, and satisfaction. This impairment occurs not only through pain but also through the psychological burden of a visible vulvar lesion.6–8 Because the Bartholin glands contribute directly to vaginal lubrication, their dysfunction has a clear physiological pathway to dyspareunia and dryness.1,9 Beyond the acute episode, recurrent disease may lead to sexual avoidance and relationship difficulties.7,8

Treatment options include Word catheter drainage, marsupialization, gland excision, CO₂ laser, and silver nitrate sclerotherapy.1,2,5 Reif et al. found that pain, measured by Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), reached minimal levels by day 6 in patients treated with a Word catheter, and sexual activity resumed within the same period.9 In a randomised Turkish trial of 134 patients, Aydogan Mathyk et al. reported that FSFI scores were below the dysfunction threshold in both the marsupialization and excision arms (22.33 ± 5.15 vs. 24.76 ± 4.32, respectively), with the marsupialization group showing a significantly higher postoperative dyspareunia rate (15.1% vs. 2.9%, P = .01).6 Beyazit et al. similarly found that FSFI improved significantly three months after both procedures, though the pre-treatment mean was notably low (18.9 ± 7.0), possibly reflecting a more severely affected cohort.7 Di Donato et al. compared CO₂ laser with cold-knife surgery and observed superior post-treatment FSFI, lubrication, and pain scores in the laser arm, while both groups showed significant improvement from baseline.8 Across all these studies, pre-treatment FSFI values fell below 26.55, indicating that sexual dysfunction is present before any surgical intervention.

Despite these findings, the literature has important gaps. Most studies assess sexual function only at the time of surgery rather than during the symptomatic episode, few include healthy control groups, and none has examined sexual distress or genital self-image as distinct dimensions. The FSDS-R and Female Genital Self-Image Scale (FGSIS), two instruments developed specifically to capture distress and body image related to sexuality,10,11 have not been applied to this population.

The present study aimed to characterize the sexual health burden of Bartholin gland disease across four validated domains, encompassing pain, sexual function, sexual distress, and genital self-image, by applying VAS, FSFI, FSDS-R, and FGSIS at two time points in patients and comparing findings against a matched healthy control group. Specifically, we asked: does Bartholin gland disease impose a clinically significant and measurable burden across multiple sexual health domains, as assessed by validated self-report instruments, and does this burden resolve following treatment to levels comparable to those of healthy controls?

Methods

This prospective cohort study was conducted in the Gynaecology and Obstetrics outpatient clinic of Bolu Abant İzzet Baysal University Training and Research Hospital between April 2024 and March 2026. No specialized diagnostic devices were used; diagnosis was established by clinical examination. This observational cohort study was reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines; the completed checklist is provided as Supplementary Material. Women aged ≥18 years with a newly diagnosed Bartholin gland cyst or abscess, sexually active with a regular partner, in the reproductive period, and without malignancy or pelvic anatomical abnormality were eligible for the patient group. Additional exclusion criteria were prior genital surgery, language barrier precluding communication, vaginismus, no prior sexual intercourse, and postmenopausal status. Of the 33 patients initially screened for eligibility, four were excluded before enrolment (two postmenopausal, two in the first postpartum month without resumed sexual activity) and a further four did not attend the post-treatment follow-up visit (T2) and were therefore excluded from the analysis. The final patient group comprised 25 women who completed both assessments. The control group consisted of 25 women selected consecutively from those attending for routine gynaecological check-up, with no history of Bartholin gland disease, matched for age and reproductive status, who completed the scales once; VAS was not administered to controls because it specifically measures active Bartholin-related symptoms.

A standardized data collection form was administered to all participants, covering sociodemographic characteristics (age, education, marital status, BMI, smoking), obstetric and gynaecological history (gravida, parity, menstrual pattern, sexual history), medical background, and, for patients only, Bartholin gland-specific clinical data (pathology type, treatment and surgical method, recurrence, culture and pathology results, symptom severity). Four validated outcome measures were used. The VAS (0–10) was applied to assess dyspareunia, vaginal burning, and vaginal dryness; dyspareunia scores are reported as the primary pain outcome, consistent with prior studies in this population.9 The FSFI covers six domains (desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain) across 19 items (total score 2–36; cut-off <26.55 for sexual dysfunction).12,13 The FSDS-R assesses sexuality-related personal distress across 13 items (total score 0–52; cut-off ≥11 for clinically significant distress).10,13 The FGSIS measures women’s attitudes towards their own genitalia across seven items (total score 7–28; higher scores reflect a more positive self-image).11,14 Turkish-validated versions were used for FSFI, FSDS-R, and FGSIS.13–15 In the patient group, all scales were administered at the initial clinic visit before any treatment was started (T1), and re-administered after clinical symptom resolution was confirmed by the physician (T2): 4–6 weeks after incision and drainage (note: Word catheter was not used in any drainage case in this cohort) and 6–8 weeks after marsupialization or excision. In the control group, FSFI, FSDS-R, and FGSIS were administered once at the routine check-up visit.

Statistical analysis and sample size

Sample size was calculated a priori using the FSFI difference reported by Aydogan Mathyk et al.6 as the effect size reference (d = 0.80, α = 0.05, power = 80%); a minimum of 25 participants per group was required (G*Power 3.1).

Data were analysed with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Shapiro–Wilk tests guided the choice of parametric or non-parametric methods. T1 vs. T2 comparisons used paired t-test (FSFI) or Wilcoxon signed-rank test (VAS, FSDS-R, FGSIS). Patient vs. control comparisons used Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were compared with chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Spearman correlation examined associations among scales. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 (two-tailed).

Research ethics standards compliance

The study protocol was approved by the Bolu Abant İzzet Baysal University Local Ethics Committee (Decision no: 2024/38, Date: 19.03.2024). All participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The most common pathology was abscess (64.0%) and the most frequently performed procedure was marsupialization (36.0%); recurrence was noted in 56.0% of patients. T1 FSFI scores did not differ by pathology type or surgical procedure (both P > .20; Table 2); these exploratory subgroup findings are discussed in detail below.

Discussion

This study assessed sexual health in women with Bartholin gland disease across four validated domains, encompassing pain, sexual function, sexual distress, and genital self-image, at two time points, with a matched healthy control group. The findings show a clear pattern: active disease impairs sexual function and distress, treatment resolves that impairment, and genital self-image remains intact throughout.

Nearly half the patients (48.0%, n = 12) met the FSFI sexual dysfunction threshold (<26.55) at T1. This proportion is broadly consistent with active-disease values from comparable Turkish cohorts: Aydogan Mathyk et al. reported pre-treatment FSFI of 22.33 ± 5.15 (marsupialization arm) and 24.76 ± 4.32 (excision arm),6 while Beyazit et al. found a mean of 18.9 ± 7.0.5 Our T1 mean of 26.18 ± 4.39 is slightly higher than both, likely because we enrolled cyst and abscess patients together and assessed them before any intervention rather than on the day of surgery. By T2, the mean rose to 31.72 ± 3.34, no longer significantly different from controls (P = .187), and only two patients (8.0%) remained below the dysfunction cut-off. Di Donato et al. observed a similar post-treatment normalisation following both CO₂ laser and cold-knife surgery.8

The decision to analyse cysts and abscesses together, and to pool surgical subgroups, warrants clarification. We acknowledge that the subgroup comparisons were substantially underpowered (post-hoc power 9.6% for d = 0.273), which precludes conclusions regarding true equivalence; they should be regarded as exploratory. The observed effect size (d = 0.273, mean FSFI difference 1.2 points) falls below the 2.5-point minimum clinically important difference for the FSFI, suggesting limited clinical relevance even if a larger study reached statistical significance. The null findings across surgical subgroups (P = .866) further support the view that sexual morbidity is driven by the pathology itself rather than its subtype or management modality.

A secondary finding of clinical interest concerns recurrence. In our cohort, 56.0% of patients had experienced at least one prior episode, with a mean surgical history of 2.3 procedures in the recurrent group versus 0.7 in non-recurrent patients. Patients with recurrence had significantly lower T1 FSFI scores (24.49 ± 4.55 vs. 28.32 ± 3.21, P = .020, Cohen’s d = 0.971), suggesting that repeated disease episodes compound the sexual health burden beyond what is attributable to a single acute episode. Earlier definitive management may therefore reduce cumulative sexual burden in recurrent disease.6

Pain at T1 was moderate-to-high (mean VAS 5.96 ± 2.64), dropping sharply to 1.04 ± 0.20 after treatment (dz = 1.906). Reif et al. documented a similar rapid trajectory in patients treated with Word catheter, with pain reaching minimum levels by day 6.9,17 The separate measurement of dyspareunia, vaginal burning, and dryness, rather than a single composite pain score, is a methodological feature that allowed us to characterize symptom profiles more precisely. The strong VAS–FSDS-R correlation (r = +0.561, P = .004) confirms what clinicians generally observe: when pain subsides, distress subsides with it.18,19 This has a direct practical implication: effective analgesia and prompt treatment are not just comfort measures; they are sexual health interventions.

At T1, 16.0% of patients (n = 4) exceeded the clinical distress threshold (FSDS-R ≥ 11). None remained above it at T2, and no control participant reached it. A systematic search of PubMed and Web of Science identified no prior study applying FSDS-R in this population; its inclusion here fills a gap, because the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition and International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision definitions of female sexual dysfunction require personal distress as a criterion, and distress may be present even when FSFI scores are above the dysfunction cut-off. The strong inverse correlation with FSFI (r = −0.829, P < .001) replicates the psychometric relationship documented by ter Kuile et al. in the general population,13 and confirms that both instruments capture related but not redundant constructs in this specific clinical group.

The most surprising result was the FGSIS. Active Bartholin gland disease produces visible vulvar asymmetry and swelling, yet genital self-image at T1 was indistinguishable from that of controls (22.92 ± 3.28 vs. 23.20 ± 1.94, P = .784). This non-significant result should be interpreted with caution: it does not demonstrate formal equivalence between groups, as no pre-specified equivalence or non-inferiority framework was applied. However, the observed effect size was d = 0.104 (genuinely small by conventional benchmarks), suggesting that the lack of difference is unlikely to reflect insufficient power alone. It is also worth noting that the clinical presentation of Bartholin gland disease is heterogeneous in terms of external visibility. In our cohort, visible vulvar pathology was confirmed by clinical examination in all patients and documented on the standardized data collection form. However, the degree of external asymmetry varies considerably between patients: cysts typically cause unilateral distension of the labium majus to a variable extent, whereas abscesses more consistently produce pronounced fluctuant swelling with surrounding erythema.20,21 In patients with smaller or less visible cysts, the absence of a genital self-image difference may therefore reflect limited visual impact in addition to the psychological resilience argument outlined above. Two explanations are plausible. Women may frame Bartholin gland disease as a temporary medical event, distinct from their stable sense of their own body; accordingly, an episodic swelling does not alter a dispositional self-image. Alternatively, the FGSIS, as Herbenick and Reece noted at validation,11 measures trait-level attitudes with high temporal stability, and is therefore insensitive to state-level physical changes. The moderate FSFI–FGSIS correlation (r = +0.535, P = .006) confirms that genital self-image does relate to sexual function in this group, but it is not the primary driver of the dysfunction; pain and distress are.

The correlation matrix at T1 tells a coherent story. FSFI and FSDS-R move in opposite directions (r = −0.829), confirming that poorer function accompanies greater distress, a relationship well-established in the general female population11 but documented here for the first time in a Bartholin gland cohort. VAS correlates positively with FSDS-R (r = +0.561) and negatively with FSFI (r = −0.456), placing pain at the centre of the sexual health burden: it worsens both function and distress simultaneously. FGSIS correlates positively with FSFI (r = +0.535), suggesting that women with a more positive genital self-image also report better sexual function, though whether this is cause, consequence, or a shared personality disposition cannot be determined from cross-sectional data.

Strengths

The prospective design with two measurement time points, the simultaneous use of four validated instruments covering distinct domains, and the inclusion of a matched control group are the main methodological strengths. A priori sample size calculation, rather than the post-hoc justification common in this literature, provides a sounder statistical foundation. Post-hoc analyses confirm adequate power for all primary comparisons (between-group: d ≥ 1.46, power > 99%; paired: dz ≥ 1.2, power > 99%). The concurrent assessment of VAS, FSFI, FSDS-R, and FGSIS in a single cohort enables a more complete characterisation of the sexual morbidity profile than previously available: prior studies applied FSFI alone at the time of surgery, without capturing pre-treatment burden, distress, or genital self-image.

Limitations

The most important limitation of this study is the small sample size (n = 25 per group). Although the sample was adequately powered for the primary between-group comparisons (post-hoc power > 99%), the small number of participants substantially limits the generalisability of the findings and renders subgroup analyses, including cyst-versus-abscess and surgical-procedure comparisons, exploratory at best and should be interpreted with considerable caution. T2 timing was not fully standardized: drainage patients were assessed 4–6 weeks after treatment and surgical patients 6–8 weeks, introducing some heterogeneity in recovery stage that may affect between-group comparability at T2. The asymmetric design (longitudinal measurement in patients versus a single assessment in controls) is a recognized limitation; however, single-point control data are standard in comparative sexual function research. The study did not measure several confounders known to influence female sexual function, including relationship quality, partner sexual function, psychological status (anxiety, depression), socioeconomic factors, and hormonal contraceptive use. We cannot exclude their contribution. A small subset used medications with known sexual side effects; their scores did not deviate from the group average. The follow-up period of 4–8 weeks is relatively short; sexual function and genital self-image are multidimensional outcomes that may continue to evolve over months, and it remains unclear whether the reported improvements are sustained. Finally, the study is from a single centre with a predominantly Turkish population; replication in diverse, multicentre cohorts is needed to establish generalisability.

Conclusion

Bartholin gland disease causes measurable, clinically significant impairment in sexual function and sexual distress during the active phase, with sexual health outcomes recovering to levels comparable to those of healthy controls after treatment. Genital self-image, by contrast, appears to remain intact even during active disease. These findings support routine screening with FSFI and FSDS-R from the initial clinic visit and highlight the need to account for the higher post-marsupialization dyspareunia risk in surgical decision-making.6,7 Larger multicentre studies with standardized long-term follow-up are needed to confirm these findings.
