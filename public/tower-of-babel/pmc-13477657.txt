Advances in NIR-II Fluorescence Laparoscopy for Precision Diagnosis and Theranostics of Urological Oncology
Authors: Quanyong Liu, Sha Li, Mingquan Xu, Shuo Rong, Yangbing Wei, Jiawei Li, Fan Jia, Shuai Li, Haojun Zhang, Jinghong He, Chao Liu, Xiaofeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecision surgical intervention in urological oncology is inherently constrained by the limited penetration depth and lack of tactile feedback in traditional endoscopy. Near infrared II (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm) fluorescence laparoscopy overcomes these limitations by drastically suppressing photon scattering and tissue auto-fluorescence, facilitating sub-millimeter deep-tissue navigation. However, navigating the urological system requires circumventing unique physiological barriers, notably dense adipose encapsulation and fluid-filled cavities. This review comprehensively evaluates the translational integration of high-performance InGaAs hardware architectures and advanced nanomedicines designed to conquer these specialized anatomical niches. We trace the evolution of NIR-II molecular probes from leveraging the tail fluorescence of ICG to biomarker-driven active targeting (eg, PSMA, CAIX, Nectin-4) and tumor micro-environment (TME)-responsive smart nanoplatforms. By meticulously modulating supramolecular physical chemistry, such as leveraging lipid critical packing parameter (CPP) phase transitions and mitigating aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ). These platforms achieve spatially synchronized diagnostic imaging and targeted phototheranostic ablation. Preclinically and in early human trials, this synergy enables precise primary lesion delineation, trans-adipose pelvic lymph node mapping, and neurovascular bundle (NVB) preservation across prostate, renal, and bladder malignancies. It also fosters systemic anti-tumor immunity through immunogenic cell death. Despite these breakthroughs, broad clinical translation remains impeded by formidable nanotoxicological hurdles, particularly reticuloendothelial system sequestration and the accelerated blood clearance (ABC) phenomenon, alongside robotic surgery integration challenges. To rewrite standard-of-care guidelines, future paradigms must prioritize “safe-by-design” architectures, logic-gated multi-receptor targeting to eliminate diagnostic ambiguity, and large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials evaluating long-term oncological outcomes.

Introduction

With the advancement of precision surgery, the core philosophy of surgical intervention for urological tumors has shifted from “maximal tolerance” to “minimal necessity”, aiming to optimize organ function preservation while ensuring radical tumor resection.1–3 However, traditional laparoscopic surgery is hindered by significant intraoperative limitations due to the loss of direct tactile feedback and the restricted imaging depth of conventional white-light endoscopy.4 For instance, in nephron-sparing surgery (NSS), the millimeter-scale precision in tumor boundary delineation directly dictates surgical margin status.5 In muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), the thoroughness of metastatic lymph node dissection often conflicts with the risk of postoperative complications.6 In radical prostatectomy (RP), the preservation of delicate NVBs remains heavily reliant on the surgeon’s clinical experience.7

The imperative for specialized intraoperative navigation in urological oncology stems from its highly distinct anatomical microenvironments, characterized by deep pelvic locations, dense perirenal and periprostatic adipose tissue encapsulation, and dynamic fluid-filled cavities (eg, the bladder and collecting systems). While conventional imaging modalities have advanced significantly, they remain fundamentally constrained in this specific surgical context. Preoperative MRI and CT provide excellent static anatomical staging but completely lack the real-time, dynamic spatial feedback required to guide surgical dissection. PET/PSMA imaging offers exquisite molecular sensitivity for systemic metastasis, yet it involves ionizing radiation and cannot be continuously monitored during laparoscopic procedures. Intraoperatively, traditional blue-light cystoscopy (using 5-ALA) is highly susceptible to urinary autofluorescence and is physically restricted to superficial mucosal evaluation, lacking the penetration depth to assess muscle-invasive disease or trans-adipose lymph nodes. Furthermore, while near infrared I (NIR-I, 700–900 nm) fluorescence has entered clinical practice, severe photon scattering within thick pelvic fat and high background signals in vascularized renal parenchyma drastically compromise its signal-to-background ratio (SBR).

To overcome these multifactorial barriers, the spectral window for bio-optical imaging has progressed into the NIR-II region. Compared with NIR-I, NIR-II offers distinct physical advantages predicated on the wavelength-dependent power-law decay of tissue reduced scattering coefficients (μ’s∝λ−n), enabling superior deep-tissue penetration and spatial resolution.8–11 Furthermore, with autofluorescence nearly eliminated due to the fundamental energy mismatch between the lower-energy NIR-II photons and endogenous biomolecules, the SBR is substantially enhanced.12 Translating this theory into clinically viable tools, specifically achieving “See and Treat” theranostics, requires the organic integration of dedicated cooled InGaAs laparoscopic hardware and highly engineered targeted molecular probes.13 By meticulously manipulating the competitive deactivation pathways of the excited state, these nanoplatforms facilitate a seamless transition from diagnostic localization to targeted phototheranostic intervention.

While recent systematic reviews have extensively covered radionuclide-based theranostics and emerging liquid biopsy biomarkers in urologic oncology, comprehensive summaries focusing on intraoperative optical navigation remain remarkably scarce.14,15 Furthermore, although NIR-II fluorescence theranostics has been recently reviewed in the context of superficial or thoracic malignancies, literature deeply bridging sophisticated nanoprobe chemistry with the specific biomechanical and optical constraints of the urological system is conspicuously absent.16 The primary novelty of this review lies in its precise urological focus and its rigorous integration of thermodynamic material science with clinical surgical decision-making. Unlike generalized reviews, we specifically address how molecular designs must be tailored to conquer urological barriers such as modulating renal clearance kinetics to bypass the urinary “washout effect” and leveraging structural phase transitions to navigate fluid-filled cavities. By establishing a clear “structure-property-performance” logical chain, this review systematically synthesizes recent breakthroughs in NIR-II tissue optics, high-performance hardware architectures, and microenvironment-responsive nanoprobes. Importantly, to bridge the gap between bench and bedside, this review explicitly demarcates the current translational landscape. We clarify which technologies have successfully transitioned into early human trials (eg, advanced InGaAs hardware and the off-label utilization of clinically approved dyes) and which sophisticated smart nanoplatforms (eg, TME-responsive and logic-gated architectures) remain firmly within the preclinical experimental stage. Finally, we provide a critical appraisal of the nanotoxicological bottlenecks impeding broad clinical adoption, delineating a strategic roadmap toward logic-gated multi-receptor architectures and next-generation precision management of urological malignancies.

Physical Mechanisms of NIR-II Fluorescence Imaging

In the continuous evolution of precision surgery, the paramount challenge for intraoperative navigation lies in the accurate and real-time localization of target structures within complex human anatomical environments.17 The urological system, characterized by dense fatty encapsulation and fluid-filled cavities (primarily urine), presents two formidable obstacles for conventional navigation. NIR-II fluorescence imaging has emerged as a transformative tool, offering superior solutions to these challenges predicated on its unique physical properties.18

The suboptimal performance of traditional imaging windows (visible and NIR-I) in biological tissues is primarily attributed to two physical phenomena: scattering and autofluorescence.19 As light propagates through complex media such as cells and interstitial fluids, multiple scattering events occur due to refractive index discontinuities, resulting in image blurring, a phenomenon known as the scattering effect.20 Concurrently, autofluorescence refers to the background signals generated by endogenous biomolecules upon excitation by short-wavelength light. This tissue-derived background noise interferes with target signals, drastically reducing the SBR.

In contrast, the imaging advantages of NIR-II stem from its longer wavelengths. According to Rayleigh’s scattering law, the scattering intensity is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength (λ−4) (Figure 1a).21 This implies that as the photon wavelength transitions from the NIR-I to the NIR-II window, the scattering intensity decreases by an order of magnitude, allowing for a more ballistic light path, deeper tissue penetration, and significantly improved spatial resolution. Furthermore, tissue autofluorescence attenuates sharply beyond 900 nm and becomes negligible within the NIR-II region (Figure 1b).21 Consequently, the NIR-II window provides an exceptionally low background noise environment and a substantially enhanced SBR, establishing the physical foundation for resolving the intricate navigation challenges inherent in urological surgery. The pelvicalyceal system, ureters, and bladder within the urological tract constitute a dynamic, fluid-filled environment (predominantly urine), which presents a formidable obstacle for light-mediated intraoperative navigation.23 As illustrated in Figure 1c, water molecules exhibit intense vibrational overtones and combination bands at specific wavelengths, resulting in prominent intrinsic absorption peaks primarily centered around 1450 nm and 1900 nm, with additional weaker bands near 970 nm and 1200 nm.21,24,25 In terms of tissue optical parameters, the absorption coefficient (μa) of water at 1450 nm is approximately 30 cm−1, which is nearly 200 times higher than that at 1064 nm (0.15 cm−1). These absorption peaks cause severe attenuation of propagating photons, leading to the extinction of fluorescence signals.

This physical characteristic directly dictates the strategic selection of NIR-II sub-windows. Generally, the NIR-II window is categorized into NIR-IIa (1000–1300 nm) and NIR-IIb (1500–1700 nm).26,27 Because the NIR-IIb band is located closer to the primary water absorption peak, it is significantly more susceptible to aqueous interference than NIR-IIa.27 During imaging within hydrous environments, such as a urine-filled bladder or a hydronephrotic renal pelvis, the 1000–1300 nm range of the NIR-IIa window functions as a relatively “water-transparent” window compared to other absorption-affected bands.21 Consequently, in endourological procedures like transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) or ureteroscopy, where aqueous media are present in the lumen, the 1000–1300 nm band is the preferred choice for superior fluorescence navigation.21

Organs within the urological system, particularly the kidneys and the prostate, are typically encapsulated by thick layers of adipose tissue. The kidneys are suspended and protected by the perirenal fat capsule, while the prostate is deeply embedded within pelvic fat and fascia. This adipose environment poses a significant challenge for traditional NIR-I imaging. In the NIR-I window, light undergoes intense Mie scattering within the fat; because the size of adipocytes is comparable to the wavelength of light, photons experience large-angle deflections, leading to profound image blurring.22

The resolution to this challenge lies in the wavelength-dependent optical properties of adipose tissue. In summary, the unique anatomical environment of urological surgery necessitates a strategic selection of NIR-II sub-windows that balances the mitigation of water absorption with the maximization of penetration depth through adipose tissue. The 1000–1300 nm band within the NIR-IIa window (1000–1400 nm) achieves an optimal equilibrium between these two constraints, representing the most advantageous spectral range for current urological applications. Moreover, the engineering hardware and targeted probe technologies associated with this band are relatively more mature. Conversely, while the NIR-IIb window (1500–1700 nm) theoretically offers lower scattering intensity and near-zero autofluorescence, it faces a significantly higher risk of signal attenuation in aqueous environments.22,32 Therefore, at the current stage, accelerating the clinical translation of the NIR-IIa window remains the primary direction for optimizing urological surgical navigation.

NIR-II Laparoscopic Hardware Systems and Key Technologies

Translating NIR-II fluorescence imaging into a reliable intraoperative navigation tool requires video-rate real-time performance (at least 25–30 FPS) to maintain the surgeon’s hand-eye coordination and avoid iatrogenic injury.33,34 Traditional silicon-based sensors (CCD/CMOS) are limited by a wide bandgap (~1.1 eV), rendering them insensitive to photons beyond 1000 nm.31 Reconciling this material limitation necessitates the utilization of narrow-bandgap (~0.73 eV) Indium Gallium Arsenide (InGaAs) focal plane array (FPA) detectors.35 To suppress ambient thermal dark current that can completely obscure weak tumor fluorescence, multi-stage thermoelectric cooling below −80 °C must be integrated into the endoscopic lens modules.36 Once deeply cooled, modern InGaAs detectors achieve an exceptional quantum efficiency (QE > 80%) within the 1000–1300 nm range, delivering high SBR images at millisecond-scale exposure times to secure dynamic real-time feedback. Crucially, this exquisite hardware sensitivity establishes a stringent sensitivity-matching paradigm required to resolve the delicate modulation of radiative versus non-radiative decay channels within engineered nanoprobes. Because precise supramolecular engineering often relies on splitting excited-state populations to optimize quantum yield, the resulting radiative transitions can be highly transient or attenuated. To achieve pixel-level spatial fusion without the macroscopic parallax errors inherent in multi-camera setups, a common field-of-view co-axial optical path design is preferred.39,40 This configuration guides both visible (400–700 nm) and NIR-II signals through a single broadband transmission lens before splitting them via a customized dichroic beam splitter to their respective high-definition CMOS and cooled InGaAs sensors. Software-level homography transformation matrices provide real-time geometric and chromatic calibration for every frame, seamlessly overlaying sub-millimeter deep-tissue fluorescence onto visible anatomical fields.41,42 Beyond basic overlay, this precise coordinate mapping enables advanced augmented reality (AR) integration. Recent clinical studies in urology have demonstrated that integrating patient-specific 3D virtual models, reconstructed from preoperative MRI, directly into the dynamic surgical view via AR significantly improves spatial orientation. In robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP), for instance, AR-assisted intraoperative navigation has been clinically proven to enhance the precise identification of the prostatic capsule and minimize mechanical traction on the NVBs, while advanced registration algorithms dynamically compensate for soft-tissue deformations induced by pneumoperitoneum pressure.43–48

The clinical utility of these multimodal hardware streams is maximized by real-time intraoperative AI enhancement engines.49,50 Rather than relying on standard statistical denoising, these engines deploy physics-informed deep learning (PiDL) models. Particularly in the context of urological challenges, traditional black-box AI struggles with the heterogeneous optical properties of the pelvic cavity. By treating the urological environment as a turbid scattering medium governed by the radiative transfer equation, PiDL algorithmically “inverts” the scattering process. This enables the system to aggressively “de-scatter” images and reconstruct highly distorted sub-millimeter boundaries, such as the cavernous nerves buried under pelvic fat.51

Strong clinical and preclinical evidence increasingly supports this AI-assisted approach. Recent validations in robotic surgical setups demonstrate that AI-driven semantic segmentation algorithms can autonomously track fat-encapsulated critical structures (eg, the obturator nerve or renal hilum) with high intersection-over-union (IoU) accuracy at video-rate (30 FPS), maintaining structural fidelity even during tissue traction or unexpected intraoperative bleeding.52 Furthermore, these PiDL architectures transcend traditional optical modeling by utilizing the real-time dynamic perfusion kinetics of TME-responsive nanoprobes as core physical input parameters. By embedding empirical time-intensity curves (TIC) and pH/enzyme-triggered activation velocity constants directly into the network’s boundary constraints, the AI mathematically formalizes biological mass transport equations. This allows the enhancement engine to algorithmically differentiate between passive, non-specific background diffusion in normal tissues and authentic, biomarker-driven compartmental enrichment at tumor margins, ensuring that the surgeon’s decision to resect is based on objective, mathematically verified pathological boundaries rather than subjective visual interpretation.49,50,53–57

NIR-II Targeted Molecular Probes for Urological Tumors

Prostate cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and bladder cancer represent the three primary malignancies of the urological system.58 The unique anatomical features of this system, characterized by a fluid-filled bladder, highly vascularized kidneys, and a deeply situated prostate encapsulated by dense adipose tissue, pose formidable challenges for optical imaging. Conventional NIR-I probes are frequently hindered by substantial non-specific accumulation in healthy tissues or severe signal attenuation at depth, precluding the precise identification of micrometastatic lesions.59 This section systematically explores the clinical translation potential of NIR-II probes, following a technical trajectory from general passive targeting to biomarker-specific active targeting and, ultimately, to microenvironment-responsive smart systems (summarized in Table 1). NIR-II Extension of Clinically Approved General Probes

Prior to the clinical approval of dedicated NIR-II fluorophores, leveraging currently FDA-approved dyes represents the most efficient pathway for clinical translation. Although the primary emission peak of ICG is centered in the NIR-I window (~822 nm), recent studies have identified clinically significant “off-peak emission” (or tail fluorescence) at wavelengths exceeding 1000 nm.60 This tail emission significantly mitigates tissue scattering and autofluorescence.61 When integrated with high-sensitivity InGaAs detectors, it facilitates deeper tissue penetration than traditional NIR-I imaging.59 While the primary advantage of these general probes is their high translatability, their efficacy is constrained by a reliance on the passive Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect. This often leads to non-specific accumulation in the highly vascularized renal parenchyma, potentially obscuring early-stage tumor margins. Consequently, the development of active-targeting probes is imperative.

Biomarker-Driven Specific Probes

Probes equipped with precise molecular guidance are pivotal for overcoming the inherent heterogeneity of urological tumors. In the design of such probes, the trade-off between excretion kinetics and the imaging window constitutes the core engineering consideration. Typically, small molecules or nanoclusters with a molecular weight below 40 kDa (hydrodynamic diameter < 5.5 nm) are primarily eliminated via renal filtration.62 To circumvent the chronic biosafety risks associated with reticuloendothelial system (RES) deposition of larger inorganic nanomaterials, contemporary material science platforms favor the fabrication of smart supramolecular nano-assemblies.63 These integrated nanomedicines maintain a larger hydrodynamic size (>10–20 nm) during early circulation to exploit the passive EPR effect or long-circulating biomarker binding; subsequently, they undergo precise structural disassembly into sub-5.5 nm renal-clearable fragments triggered by specific local physiological cues or structural degradation.63 This rapid renal clearance characteristic exerts a “double-edged sword” effect on urological imaging: while it expeditiously reduces blood-pool background and enhances the systemic SBR, the transient accumulation of high-concentration probes within the renal tubules and collecting systems generates significant intrinsic fluorescence noise, which may obscure micrometastatic lesions within the renal parenchyma.63

To reconcile this contradiction, contemporary probe design has shifted from pursuing rapid excretion alone to the precision modulation of surface charge balance and PEGylation, aiming to construct an optimal “imaging kinetic window”.64 Research suggests that moderately increasing PEG chain length or introducing zwitterionic surfaces can decouple the renal excretion peak from the tumor uptake peak on the chronological axis, while maintaining high tumor enrichment.65 For instance, NIR-II probes developed for PSMA have achieved distinct stratification into the vascular phase (1–5 min), parenchymal phase (15–30 min), and excretory phase (>60 min) by tuning their hydrophobic core-shell structures.18,66,67 This kinetic-based “time-resolved imaging” strategy enables surgeons to capture the maximum target-to-background ratio before the onset of significant urinary background interference (Figure 3). Thermodynamic Design of Smart Probes: The Structure-Property-Performance Relationship

While receptor-mediated targeting dictates where the probe localizes, TME-responsive smart probes address the critical challenge of constitutive background signals within the systemic circulation. Through sophisticated structure-activity design, these probes remain fluorescently silent during circulation and are selectively activated only upon encountering specific stimuli within the TME.

Molecular Mechanisms of Quenching and Activation

The background suppression of smart probes is predicated on fundamental physicochemical mechanisms. Prevailing design strategies involve exploiting photo-induced electron transfer or fluorescence resonance energy transfer effects.68 Prior to infiltration into the tumor tissue, the fluorescence signal is turned off due to intramolecular energy transfer to quenching moieties or through aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ) effects, wherein the tight π−π stacking of planar conjugated fluorophores in the aggregated state facilitates dense molecular packing and accelerates non-radiative decay channels.69 Upon exposure to tumor-specific triggers such as acidic pH (5.5–6.5), elevated ROS, or overexpression of specific cathepsins, sensitive chemical linkages within the probe (eg, thioketal or peptide bonds) undergo cleavage. This molecular cleavage reinstates the isolated state of the fluorophores or alters the frontier molecular orbital gap, thereby blocking the non-radiative pathways and instantaneously triggering robust NIR-II fluorescence to enhance the TBR by orders of magnitude.69

Nanocarriers as Engineering Platforms

Nanocarriers provide a highly versatile engineering platform for the construction of TME-responsive theranostic systems, effectively overcoming the intrinsic limitations of free molecular dyes.69 To achieve deep integration of NIR-II optical navigation and localized intervention, the precise modulation of supramolecular chemistry and thermodynamic drug loading mechanisms (ie, achieving the lowest energy state for secure payload retention) is paramount For instance, in the design of advanced pH-sensitive liposomes, a highly specific multicomponent lipid matrix, typically comprising dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC), dioleoylphosphatidylethanolamine (DOPE), cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHEMS), and cholesterol (CHOL) are rationally engineered.70 Within this architectural framework, DPPC acts as the primary structural backbone with a defined gel-to-liquid crystalline phase-transition temperature (Tm~ 41°C), while CHOL critically blunts the enthalpy of this phase transition, modulating membrane fluidity and cohesive energy to prevent premature drug leakage during systemic circulation at physiological pH 7.4.70 DOPE serves as the fusogenic driver, and CHEMS functions as the pH-responsive molecular trigger.71

This compartmentalized architecture enables the simultaneous, high-efficiency co-loading of multidimensional therapeutic agents governed by distinct physicochemical driving forces. Hydrophilic chemotherapeutic agents, such as Doxorubicin (DOX), are actively concentrated within the expansive aqueous core driven by a transmembrane chemical potential gradient (eg, an ammonium sulfate gradient), which induces intra-liposomal crystallization to maximize thermodynamic stability.71,72 Conversely, lipophilic NIR-II fluorophores and photothermal agents, such as ICG or IR780, are seamlessly partitioned and anchored within the hydrophobic lipid bilayer, driven by the minimization of systemic free energy through hydrophobic interactions during the thin-film hydration and subsequent extrusion processes.72 By confining these optical agents within the lipid matrix, intramolecular π−π stacking is spatially restricted, effectively suppressing ACQ before reaching the target site.69

Upon entering the acidic TMEor endo/lysosomal compartments (pH 5.5–6.5), the protonation of the carboxylic acid groups in CHEMS neutralizes its negative surface charge.71 This fundamental chemical shift dramatically alters the lipid CPP.71 By reducing the effective headgroup area (a) relative to the hydrophobic tail volume (v) and length (l), the molecular geometry of the CHEMS/DOPE complex is transformed from a stable cylinder (CPP~ 1) to an extreme cone shape (CPP> 1).71 This severe geometric distortion and subsequent thermodynamic instability compromise the bilayer integrity, physically driving a rapid morphological transition from a stable lamellar phase (Lα) to a highly leaky inverted hexagonal phase (HII).71

The instantaneous collapse of the liposomal membrane triggers a spatial-temporally synchronized “burst release”: the hydrophilic DOX is expelled from the aqueous core to induce localized cytotoxicity, while the bilayer-anchored ICG is fully exposed to the aqueous environment to facilitate robust NIR-II fluorescence imaging and generate localized photothermal ablation upon specific laser excitation.72 Such precision at the material science level provides a robust foundation for integrating molecular imaging with targeted theranostic interventions (Figure 4a).72 Figure 4Schematic illustration of tumor microenvironment (TME)-responsive smart nanoplatforms and dual-locked “AND-gate” logicactivatable architectures for precision urological oncological theranostics. Enzyme- and Redox-Responsive Architectures for Precision Margin Delineation

While pH-responsive systems exploit the bulk extracellular acidity of tumors, overcoming the complex heterogeneity of urological malignancies requires broader stimuli-responsive architectures, notably enzyme-activated and redox-responsive smart probes.73

Tumor invasion and metastasis are heavily dependent on the degradation of the extracellular matrix, a process driven by the localized overexpression of specific proteases, particularly Matrix Metalloproteinases (eg, Cathepsin B and Matrix Metalloproteinases) at the tumor’s invasive front. Enzyme-responsive NIR-II probes are typically engineered by conjugating a fluorophore to a quenching moiety via a highly specific peptide linker (eg, the PLGVRG sequence). During systemic circulation, the close proximity of the fluorophore and quencher ensures complete signal silence via FRET or photo-induced electron transfer mechanisms. Upon encountering the MMP-rich invasive margin, the peptide linker is selectively cleaved, physically separating the quencher from the fluorophore and triggering robust NIR-II emission.74

The surgical relevance of this enzymatic de-quenching is profound. Because MMPs are highly concentrated at the advancing edges of a tumor, these probes specifically illuminate the microscopic infiltrative tentacles that extend far beyond the macroscopic tumor pseudocapsule. During NSS or radical prostatectomy, these tentacles are completely invisible under conventional white-light evaluation. The real-time chemical feedback from MMP-cleavage directly dictates surgical action, compelling the surgeon to extend the resection by a critical few millimeters into seemingly normal tissue to secure a definitive R0 (negative) margin.

Furthermore, to mitigate the risk of false-positive signals generated by elevated enzyme levels in benign inflammatory tissues (a common occurrence in the urological tract, such as prostatitis), dual-stimuli responsive systems utilizing “AND-gate” logic have been developed. For example, nanoprobes crosslinked with disulfide bonds remain intact until they encounter the exceptionally high concentration of Glutathione (GSH) characteristic of the intracellular tumor cytosol (ranging from 2–10 mM, significantly higher than extracellular fluids).75 By integrating GSH-mediated structural degradation with an secondary trigger (such as pH or MMPs), the probe requires simultaneous satisfaction of multiple TME conditions to activate. Surgically, these logic-gated thermodynamic architectures act as an ultimate fail-safe mechanism against diagnostic ambiguity. They ensure that the scalpel is guided exclusively by authentic malignant signals, allowing the surgeon to confidently spare adjacent critical structures, such as the delicate NVBs or the external urethral sphincter, without compromising oncological radicality (Figure 4b).76

Prostate Cancer

As one of the most prevalent malignancies in men worldwide, prostate cancer managed via RP presents a long-standing dilemma: balancing thorough oncological radicality with the preservation of urinary and sexual functions.58,82 The prostate is situated deep within the pelvic cavity, a confined space surrounded by the cavernous nerves (which control erectile function) and the external urethral sphincter complex (essential for urinary control). During conventional surgery, these delicate nerve fibers are highly susceptible to injury during the dissection of the prostatic capsule and seminal vesicles, as surgeons must rely primarily on subjective clinical experience.83 Consequently, the incidence of postoperative urinary incontinence and erectile dysfunction remains significantly high.84

The emergence of NIR-II fluorescence imaging provides a transformative solution to this problem. Compared to the traditional NIR-I window, the ultra-low photon scattering of NIR-II allows for the maintenance of sub-millimeter spatial resolution even when penetrating several centimeters of periprostatic adipose tissue.85 The deep integration of these physical optical advantages with PSMA-targeted strategies has catalyzed a new generation of molecular-level surgical platforms. This technology not only overcomes the limitations of traditional navigation, such as poor visualization and imprecise boundary identification, but also empowers surgeons with the ability to accurately localize tumor margins and deep metastatic lymph nodes intraoperatively.

Delineation of Primary Lesions and Determination of Surgical Margins

Achieving negative surgical margins (R0 resection) is paramount for improving the prognosis of prostate cancer, as positive surgical margins (PSM) significantly increase the risk of biochemical recurrence.86 In conventional white-light surgery, surgeons rely solely on visual and tactile cues to identify extracapsular extension, making it challenging to accurately delineate infiltration margins. Given that PSMA is highly overexpressed on the surface of over 90% of prostate cancer cells, it serves as an ideal molecular imaging target.87,88 The recently developed PSMA-OSP12 nanoparticle probe, constructed via covalent conjugation of organic semiconducting polymers with targeting ligands, has demonstrated exceptional performance in xenograft models, achieving a peak TBR of 7.40±1.28, significantly outperforming traditional probes.89

In terms of clinical translation, the ICG-PSMA-D5 probe has successfully undergone first-in-human clinical validation. By conjugating ICG with a PSMA ligand exhibiting sub-nanomolar affinity (Ki = 0.39 nM), this probe confirms the feasibility of radiation-free, real-time intraoperative navigation.80 Under laparoscopic systems, fluorescence imaging clearly outlines tumor boundaries and facilitates the identification of occult micrometastatic lesions invisible under white light. Notably, NIR-II imaging can resolve cancerous infiltrations as small as 0.47 mm in diameter. This precision at the molecular level empowers surgeons to identify and resect positive areas within macroscopically normal tissues.18,90

However, the translation of mono-targeted PSMA probes into routine surgical practice is significantly impeded by profound biological and diagnostic limitations.91 Relying solely on PSMA introduces inherent diagnostic ambiguity due to its heterogeneous expression profile. Clinically, PSMA expression is notoriously down-regulated in highly aggressive or advanced variants, such as neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC) subclones. During radical prostatectomy, this false-negative presentation can fatally mislead the surgeon into leaving behind aggressive residual disease at the surgical margin. Conversely, PSMA is not exclusively prostate-specific; its expression can be significantly upregulated in the neovasculature of benign inflammatory tissues (eg, severe prostatitis) or even adjacent normal ganglia.92 This creates a severe false-positive dilemma on the operating table: if a surgeon interprets an inflammatory false-positive signal as a malignant margin, it may precipitate the unnecessary, irreversible excision of the NVB, directly compromising the patient’s postoperative erectile and urinary continence.

Therefore, the future of precision prostatectomy navigation must pivot away from mono-targeting toward “logic-gated dual-receptor” nanoplatforms (eg, simultaneously targeting PSMA and GRPR via an AND-gate activation mechanism), which represents the only viable path to eliminate diagnostic ambiguity and balance oncological radicality with functional preservation.

Pelvic Lymph Node Mapping Through Adipose Tissue

Pelvic lymph nodes are common sites for early prostate cancer metastasis; therefore, accurate identification of sentinel lymph nodes (SLNs) and micrometastases is critical for surgical decision-making.93 However, the efficacy of conventional imaging is limited because lymph nodes are typically embedded within thick layers of adipose tissue surrounding the iliac vessels. In this context, the physical advantages of NIR-II are particularly prominent: at these wavelengths, the scattering coefficient of adipose tissue is substantially reduced, and tissue penetration depth is significantly enhanced, enabling the system to effectively “see through” fatty layers.22,28–30 Preclinical data indicate that PSMA-targeted NIR-II probes can detect metastatic lymph nodes with a median diameter of only 1.9 mm, far surpassing the sensitivity of traditional imaging modalities.85 This deep-imaging capability supports real-time in vivo SLN mapping, allowing surgeons to shift from blind extended pelvic lymph node dissection toward the precise resection of fluorescence-positive nodes only, thereby ensuring oncological safety while minimizing complications such as lymphedema.

Protection of the NVB

Postoperative quality of life is heavily dependent on the preservation of the NVB.94 The NVB traverses the posterolateral surface of the prostate with a width of only 2–5 mm, making it highly vulnerable to injury during capsular dissection. NIR-II fluorescence imaging provides a breakthrough tool for NVB visualization. In a clinical study focused on pelvic autonomic nerves, intraoperative NIR-II imaging successfully identified key structures including the obturator nerve, genitofemoral nerve, and superior hypogastric plexus (Figure 5a).95 Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the SBR of NIR-II was significantly superior to that of NIR-I, enabling the clear visualization of fine nerve branches as small as 0.47 mm in diameter.95 This dynamic real-time feedback effectively circumvents anatomical displacements caused by intraoperative tissue traction. In summary, PSMA-targeted NIR-II fluorescence imaging is reshaping the paradigm of prostate cancer surgery. By integrating highly specific molecular probes with high-penetration hardware architectures, surgeons can, for the first time, achieve integrated navigation encompassing primary lesion delineation, deep-seated lymph node visualization, and fine nerve protection. Renal Tumors

The primary objective of NSS, particularly Robotic-Assisted Partial Nephrectomy (RAPN), is to achieve the surgical “Trifecta”: negative surgical margins, minimized Warm Ischemia Time (WIT), and the absence of postoperative complications.96 However, the profuse blood supply of the kidney and the challenge of locating totally endophytic tumors under conventional white light pose significant obstacles to intraoperative margin determination and renal function preservation. Leveraging its superior tissue penetration and spatial resolution, NIR-II fluorescence imaging enables the precise localization of even completely endophytic tumors within the laparoscopic field, facilitating efficient margin delineation within the confined abdominal space.97–99

Precision Localization of Tumor Margins and Endophytic Lesions

The localization of totally endophytic tumors essentially depends on the effective differentiation between the intrinsic fluorescence background of the renal parenchyma and the tumor-specific signals. When employing targeted probes, such as CAIX-targeted agents for clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC)—clinical protocols must strictly adhere to the dynamic excretion profile.100

To mitigate interference from renal excretory background, the strategy of intraoperative time-window interception has been proposed: during the early arterial phase post-injection, differences in vascular density are used to identify tumor blood supply; subsequently, in the parenchymal phase, active targeting mechanisms are utilized to lock onto tumor boundaries.101 Critically, margin marking must be completed within the 10–20 min window before the probe filters through the nephrons into the renal pelvis, causing a “washout effect” (Figure 5b). This kinetic-based dynamic navigation effectively prevents the “whole-kidney illumination” phenomenon caused by subsequent urinary signal overflow.102 Furthermore, the intraoperative use of diuretics or temporary ureteral occlusion can further modulate the excretion rate, artificially extending the high-contrast imaging window to secure vital time for the radical resection of complex tumors during NSS.103

Super-Selective Renal Artery Clamping

The renal parenchyma is exquisitely sensitive to warm ischemia; a WIT exceeding 25–30 min often results in irreversible nephron damage.97 NIR-II-guided super-selective clamping techniques enable the precise identification of third- or fourth-order arterial branches supplying the tumor, allowing for localized ischemia restricted to the tumor area while maintaining continuous perfusion of the healthy parenchyma. Prospective matched-pair clinical analyses demonstrate that NIRF-guided vascular mapping enables successful super-selective clamping (often achieving zero-ischemia for healthy tissue) in a majority of patients.104 This real-time mastery of subtle vascular anatomy serves as the technological foundation for minimizing hemorrhage and preserving renal function without compromising oncological margins.

Assessment of Renal Parenchymal Reperfusion and Debates on Long-Term Benefits

Following wound suturing, the reperfusion status of the renal parenchyma can be verified in real-time via intravenous fluorescence injection. The ultra-low autofluorescence background of the NIR-II window allows surgeons to detect subtle ischemic areas or occult bleeding at the suture site, ensuring maximal salvage of functional parenchyma.

However, the long-term benefits of this technology remain a subject of critical academic reflection. Recent retrospective cohort studies indicate that the fluorescence-guided group demonstrates a measurable superiority in estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR) preservation at short-term follow-up (eg, 3 to 6 months postoperatively), alongside reduced operative blood loss.105 However, while current primary clinical evidence validates the utility of ICG-NIRF for immediate intraoperative metrics and short-term functional recovery, robust prospective trials with larger cohorts are still required to definitively prove long-term functional or oncological benefits over standard approaches.106,107 Current heterogeneity across studies including variations in dosage, imaging windows, and hardware platforms, further limits its standardized large-scale implementation.

Urothelial and Bladder Cancer

In contrast to solid organs, the diagnosis and treatment of bladder cancer depend on the organic integration of microscopic identification within an aqueous endoluminal environment and the complex anatomical navigation of the entire pelvis. Conventional fluorescence cystoscopy is significantly hindered in clinical practice by limited penetration depth and interference from urinary autofluorescence.108,109 By integrating nanotechnology with NIR-II imaging, surgeons are poised to construct a comprehensive, multidimensional dynamic navigation system encompassing the lumen, muscle layer, lymphatic chain, and ureter.

Deep Imaging and Precision Resection of Carcinoma in Situ (CIS)

While TURBT with mapping biopsies remains the initial diagnostic standard for flat CIS, its highly occult and multifocal nature often precludes complete physical resection.110 Consequently, current clinical guidelines strongly recommend subsequent intravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) immunotherapy as the standard of care.110 The utilization of targeted probes, such as those directed against Nectin-4, CD47, and recently, Trop-2, enables a paradigm shift from passive visualization to active, precise identification. Unlike 5-ALA excited blue light, which is restricted to the mucosal surface, Nectin-4-targeted near-infrared probes leverage around 690 nm excitation to achieve an effective tissue penetration depth of 1–2 cm.

Recent preclinical models have validated that these targeted probes can overcome the limitations of conventional blue-light cystoscopy, providing deeper molecular visual support for TURBT.111 Furthermore, to meet the clinical demand for rapid and precise surgical margin evaluation, cutting-edge research introduced a novel Trop-2-targeting cyclic peptide conjugated with ICG (TTP-ICG). While initially developed for sub-millimeter margin assessment in breast cancer within the NIR-II window, this TTP-ICG probe demonstrates exceptional translational potential for urological oncology.112 Given that the Trop-2 antigen is highly overexpressed in non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), adopting such peptide-based dynamic targeted strategies could profoundly revolutionize the precise differentiation of flat malignant lesions from normal urothelium.

Furthermore, addressing the critical limitation of ACQ experienced by conventional dyes in aqueous endoluminal environments, NIR-II probes based on aggregation-induced emission luminogens (AIEgens) have achieved breakthrough advancements. The physical chemistry underlying this aggregation-induced emission phenomenon is fundamentally rooted in the Restriction of Intramolecular Motions mechanism. In a highly dilute or solvated state, the active rotors within the AIEgen molecular backbone (such as phenyl rings) continuously dissipate excited-state energy through low-frequency intramolecular rotations and vibrations, functioning as prominent non-radiative decay channels. However, upon transitioning into the complex, fluid-filled environment of the bladder, hydrophobic interactions drive the rapid self-assembly of these molecules. This supramolecular aggregation drastically elevates steric hindrance, creating a physical confinement effect that effectively freezes the intramolecular rotational and vibrational pathways. By shutting down these non-radiative relaxation channels, the exciton is forced to decay radiatively. Consequently, rather than experiencing the ACQ typical of conventional dyes, the fluorescence quantum yield of AIEgens in aqueous environments is robustly amplified, translating optical physics into precise surgical navigation.26

Upon forming aggregates in the hydrous environment of the bladder, the luminous efficiency of AIEgens does not attenuate but rather is significantly amplified. The latest multifunctional NIR-II AIEgen nanoplatforms not only provide ultra-high contrast fluorescence and photoacoustic dual-modal imaging to pinpoint occult CIS lesions but also trigger in situ PTT/PDT effects. This photophysical ablation effectively induces immunogenic cell death (ICD) of tumor cells, synergizing profoundly with the mechanism of conventional BCG immunotherapy and realizing true visualized precision theranostics for bladder CIS. When delivered via intravesical instillation, these innovative probes (such as X-ray-activated lanthanide nanotransducers) can directly bypass the urothelial barrier to accumulate within the tumor mass. This route physically circumvents the systemic background interference typical of intravenous administration, thereby dramatically enhancing the SBR during real-time navigation (Figure 5c).

Despite the impressive photophysical performance of intravesical NIR-II nanoplatforms in preclinical models, their clinical feasibility in treating bladder CIS faces severe physiological and anatomical constraints unique to the urological tract.113 First, the pharmacokinetics of intravesical administration are dictated by continuous urinary dynamics. The constant inflow of ureteral urine persistently dilutes the administered probe concentration, while the natural dynamic voiding cycles limit the effective structural interaction window with flat CIS lesions to merely 1–2 hours. Consequently, probes must possess ultra-rapid binding kinetics to secure cellular uptake before being physically washed out. Second, the urothelium presents an extreme physiological barrier. The outermost umbrella cells are sealed by impenetrable tight junctions and covered by a highly anionic glycosaminoglycan (GAG) layer.114 This GAG layer acts as a repulsive electrostatic shield, drastically retarding the passive paracellular and transcellular diffusion depth of bulky nanoparticles into the deep muscularis propria, where invasive roots may lie.

Consequently, simply amplifying the optical quantum yield of fluorophores is insufficient for clinical translation. Future engineering paradigms must focus on chemical integration with active transport mechanisms or specific motifs such as cationic cell-penetrating peptides, chitosan-based mucoadhesive anchors, or localized mild photothermal fluid convection—to forcefully breach the GAG shield and artificially prolong tissue-retention kinetics under constant urinary washout.

Precise Mapping of Pelvic Lymph Node Drainage and Optimization

In radical cystectomy (RC), the status of pelvic lymph node involvement serves as a critical determinant of patient prognosis.115 However, precise localization of deep lymph nodes remains a formidable challenge for traditional optical imaging systems, largely due to the severe photon scattering and attenuation caused by the thick adipose tissue covering the pelvic vasculature.116 To address this limitation, recently developed gold nanoclusters (AuNCs), synthesized via a charge-complementary ligand strategy, achieve an ultra-high quantum yield (Φ=3.8%) that substantially amplifies the fluorescence signal intensity in the NIR-II.117 The exceptional in vivo performance of these nanoclusters is largely attributed to the thermodynamic remodeling of the nano-bio interface driven by the charge-complementary ligand strategy. Thermodynamically, the alternating positive and negative charges of the paired ligands induce strong ion-dipole interactions with surrounding water molecules, constructing a highly rigid and dense hydration layer. When non-specific serum proteins approach the nanocluster, disrupting this firmly anchored hydration layer requires overcoming a massive entropic penalty and a thermodynamically unfavorable positive enthalpy change (ΔG>0). This energy barrier fundamentally impedes protein adsorption, endowing the AuNCs with exceptional stealth capabilities against protein corona formation during systemic circulation. Concurrently, this high-density complementary ligand network provides robust surface passivation, effectively eliminating surface trap states on the gold core. By rigidly anchoring the metal-centered excitons and minimizing vibrational non-radiative relaxation, this supramolecular thermodynamic design flawlessly converts the inherently weak luminescence of the metal core into an ultra-high NIR-II fluorescence quantum yield.117 Operating within the 1050 nm core optical window, this high-performance tracer overcomes the traditional tissue-penetration barrier, successfully navigating through several millimeters to centimeters of overlying pelvic fat to yield high-contrast, real-time visualization of deep obturator and internal iliac lymph nodes.117 Through this high-sensitivity molecular tracing technology, surgeons can transition from conventional blind empirical extended dissection toward individualized precision dissection.115,116 This paradigm shift ensures the meticulous clearance of occult metastatic foci while effectively minimizing regional morbidity, such as lymph leakage and lower-limb lymphedema, typically associated with extensive pelvic lymphadenectomy.115

Intraoperative Ureteral Visualization and Avoidance of Iatrogenic Injury

The anatomical localization and protection of the ureters are paramount for preventing iatrogenic complications during extensive pelvic surgeries.105 Renal-clearable organic NIR-II dye clusters (eg, IR-FEP1000) utilize unique pharmacokinetic pathways to become highly concentrated in urine via renal filtration following intravenous administration.118 This “excretory imaging” transforms the ureters into clearly visible landmarks within a complex, hemorrhagic, or anatomically distorted pelvic field, effectively assisting the surgeon in localization.118,119 This technique is particularly valuable for managing emergency scenarios, such as accidental intraoperative transection or occult ligation.119,120 This real-time, non-invasive visual feedback revolutionizes the traditional paradigms that rely on retrograde catheterization or palpation, offering a superior safety solution for complex urological procedures.118,120

NIR-II Image-Guided Precision Theranostics

With the evolution of precision surgery, the therapeutic paradigm for urological tumors has shifted from simple physical resection toward molecularly guided precise clearance and functional reconstruction.121,122 Leveraging high-performance NIR-II fluorescence imaging, the construction of “Theranostics” platforms has emerged as a prominent research frontier in this field.18,123,124

Localized Precision Ablation

Upon the precise delineation of tumor boundaries, the realization of the “See and Treat” paradigm represents the central objective of integrated theranostics.131 Novel theranostic platforms constructed from aggregation-induced emission (AIE) materials or engineered through-space charge-transfer nanoparticles offer an ideal solution for this clinical need by providing superior light-to-heat conversion efficiency.81,132 These AIE-based probes exhibit exceptional photothermal conversion efficiency, generating instantaneous hyperthermia upon laser excitation to induce irreversible denaturation and necrosis of tumor proteins. The oxygen-independent nature of PTT renders it particularly suitable for the ablation of residual foci situated in anatomical blind spots, such as those adjacent to the NVB.

Furthermore, in conjunction with the robust free radicals generated by AIE probes via the Type I mechanism under hypoxic conditions, PDT can induce profound oxidative stress damage at the molecular level.133,134 Simultaneously, the localized temperature elevation induced by PTT promotes microvascular vasodilation and enhances regional oxygenation, thereby providing sufficient substrates for PDT and achieving deep synergistic efficacy.135 This precision in spatio-temporal control enables surgeons to strictly confine thermal and oxidative damage within a millimeter-scale range, ensuring the thorough clearance of primary lesions or microscopic residues while maximizing the preservation of adjacent delicate nerve and vascular functions. In minimally invasive laparoscopic surgery, surgeons can introduce specialized fiber-optic probes through the laparoscopic trocar without exchanging primary instruments, achieving real-time, in situ “See and Treat” intervention under NIR-II fluorescence monitoring (Figure 6b).136

Systemic Immune Activation

The ultimate goal of theranostics extends beyond the eradication of the primary tumor to the suppression of distant micrometastasis driven by circulating tumor cells (CTCs).137 The emergence of photo-immunotherapy (PIT) marks a transition in therapeutic logic from localized physical clearance to systemic immune mobilization.138 When tumor cells are destroyed via NIR-II-guided specific PTT/PDT, they undergo robust ICD, leading to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, such as ATP and calreticulin (CRT).139 Crucially, the molecular cascades dictating this ICD process are distinct yet synergistic depending on the ablation modality.

NIR-II-mediated PTT rapidly disrupts intracellular proteostasis through severe heat shock, denaturing cytosolic proteins and triggering profound endoplasmic reticulum stress. This severe ER stress is the primary driver for the pre-apoptotic translocation of CRT to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane, where it serves as a potent “eat-me” signal for phagocytes.140 Conversely, NIR-II-mediated PDT, particularly via the robust Type I ROS generation under hypoxic conditions, induces direct oxidative damage to vital organelles (eg, mitochondria and lysosomes).141 This mitochondrial dysfunction leads to the active secretion of ATP (a “find-me” signal) and the extracellular release of High Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1), an essential nuclear protein that acts as an immunostimulatory cytokine.139 The orchestrated emission of these specific damage-associated molecular pattern (CRT, ATP, HMGB1) bypasses the tolerogenic clearance typical of normal apoptosis.

Serving as endogenous adjuvants, these signals significantly promote the maturation of dendritic cells and activate tumor-specific CD8+ T lymphocytes, effectively transforming the cold tumors commonly encountered in urology into hot tumors, a therapeutic paradigm that synergizes perfectly with prodrug-based lipid metabolism remodeling for reinforced cell death and systemic immunity.138,142 Furthermore, with the aid of responsive fluorescence probes, surgeons can even monitor the kinetic signatures of immune activation in real-time.141 This “in situ vaccine” effect not only reinforces the efficacy of local clearance but also establishes long-term systemic immune memory, providing durable protection against complications such as bone metastasis in prostate cancer or lymphatic recurrence in bladder cancer (Figure 6c).137

Biosafety, Pharmacokinetics, and Translational Toxicology of NIR-II Nanoplatforms

In vivo Pharmacokinetics and RES Sequestration

The transition of NIR-II nanomedicines from preclinical rodent models to human clinical trials hinges on a profound understanding of their in vivo pharmacokinetics and long-term tissue distribution.30,63 Upon intravenous administration, nanostructures face immediate opsonization by serum proteins, triggering recognition and subsequent sequestration by the macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system (RES), primarily localized in the liver and spleen.63 For relatively large nanoplatforms (hydrodynamic diameter > 20 nm), such as rare-earth doped nanoparticles (RENPs) or semiconducting polymer nanoparticles, this active RES entrapment results in prolonged tissue retention with a half-life (t1/2) extending from days to months.62,63

Crucially, a major immunological bottleneck for surface-functionalized platforms is the ABC phenomenon.63 Repeated administrations of PEGylated nanocarriers, such as those decorated with DSPE-PEG or consolidated within liposomal matrices—can stimulate the splenic marginal zone B cells to secrete anti-PEG IgM antibodies.63 Upon subsequent dosing, these circulating antibodies rapidly bind to the surface of the nanomedicines, triggering complement activation (via the classical pathway) and drastically accelerating hepatic Kupffer cell-mediated sequestration.30,77 This immune-mediated clearance prematurely truncates the diagnostic or therapeutic kinetic window, severely hindering longitudinal multi-dose theranostic strategies. Consequently, chronic entrapment within hepatic and splenic sinusoidal endothelial cells intensifies regulatory concerns regarding long-term histopathological alterations, microcirculatory disturbances, and chronic inflammatory responses.77

Clearance Kinetics: The Renal-Urinary Contradiction in Endourology

To circumvent the safety bottlenecks associated with chronic RES sequestration, contemporary material science heavily favors the fabrication of renal-clearable NIR-II nanoconstructs.63 Nanomaterials with a rigid hydrodynamic diameter below the physiological renal filtration threshold (~5.5nm), such as ultra-small gold nanoclusters (AuNCs) or organic molecular dye aggregates, can be efficiently eliminated from the systemic circulation via glomerular filtration, drastically reducing hepatic cargo burden.62,117

However, this rapid renal clearance kinetics exerts a complex, paradoxical effect during urological surgical navigation.11,63 The high-concentration accumulation of fluorophores within the renal tubules, ureters, and bladder creates intense intrinsic urinary fluorescence background noise.107 This “washout effect” can profoundly blind the surgeon’s visual field when delineating endophytic ccRCC or bladder CIS.101,110 Reconciling this renal-urinary contradiction requires precise surface charge engineering, such as employing zwitterionic coatings or matrix-metalloproteinase (MMP) responsive shield-shedding mechanisms to artificially separate the peak of tumor receptor binding from the onset of massive urinary excretion on the chronological axis.64,118

Acute and Chronic Nanotoxicological Profiles

Comprehensive evaluation of translational nanotoxicology demands strict discrimination between inorganic heavy-metal-based agents and organic supramolecular assemblies.59,62 Inorganic NIR-II contrast agents (eg, quantum dots containing cadmium or lead, and lanthanide-based crystals) present severe chronic toxicity risks due to the potential leaching of toxic free ions under the acidic microenvironment of intracellular lysosomes.22,79 These free heavy metal ions can induce robust oxidative stress, intracellular ROS generation, mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) depolarization, and subsequent DNA fragmentation or apoptosis in healthy parenchymal organs.77,133

Conversely, organic nanoplatforms including pH-responsive liposomes, biodegradable polymeric micelles, and AIEgens demonstrate superior translational biosafety profiles.69 Formulated from endogenous lipid matrices or highly biocompatible organic backbones, these smart nanomedicines undergo spontaneous enzymatic degradation or hydrolysis into non-toxic small-molecule metabolites.71,81 For instance, following the stimuli-triggered phase transition or morphological collapse, the constituent phospholipids, cleaved CHEMS molecules, and cholesterol fractions seamlessly enter endogenous lipid metabolic pathways.71,81 This cellular biocompatibility effectively prevents long-term intracellular structural accumulation, systemic immunogenicity, or chronic tissue toxicity, establishing a clear translational advantage over inorganic counterparts.

Strategic Paradigms for Enhanced Clinical Translation

Surmounting the biosafety threshold to achieve formal FDA or NMPA approval necessitates a paradigm shift in nanomaterial design. Next-generation NIR-II theranostic architectures must prioritize “safe-by-design” principles.11,22 This covers two prominent technical trajectories: first, the clinical optimization of off-peak tail emission from clinically pre-approved organic dyes (such as ICG extensions); and second, the clinical development of stimuli-responsive supramolecular nano-assemblies that maintain a stable, macro-sized geometry during blood circulation for precise multi-targeted navigation, yet undergo structural disassembly into renal-clearable sub-5.5 nm components upon localized TMEactivation.60,63

Conclusions and Future Perspectives

NIR-II fluorescence laparoscopy system, through the deep integration of high-sensitivity tissue optics and intelligent theranostic nanoplatforms, is fundamentally reshaping the surgical paradigm of minimally invasive urology. By transcending the intrinsic physical constraints of biological tissues, this multidisciplinary technology provides unprecedented molecular-level anatomical visualization and localized precision ablation capabilities. From achieving sub-millimeter neuroprotection during radical prostatectomy and enabling super-selective arterial clamping in NSS, to triggering in situ ICD for occult CIS in bladder cancer, this full-chain framework successfully facilitates the dual clinical goals of radical tumor resection and maximal functional anatomy preservation. However, bridging the chasm between preclinical feasibility and standardized clinical utility requires a rigorous and transparent demarcation of current translational readiness. At present, the landscape is distinctly bifurcated. On the clinical front, advanced InGaAs-based laparoscopic hardware and the off-label utilization of clinically approved dyes (eg, ICG for its NIR-II tail emission) or early-phase targeted peptides (eg, ICG-PSMA-D5) have successfully transitioned into first-in-human trials and early commercial validation, establishing a solid foundation for multi-spectral intraoperative fluorescence guidance.78 Conversely, the vast majority of sophisticated molecular architectures discussed, including TME-responsive liposomes, AIEgens, and logic-gated nanoplatforms remain strictly within the experimental and preclinical domain. Transitioning these experimental marvels into the operating room necessitates a structured, multi-dimensional roadmap that systematically addresses current biological, engineering, and regulatory bottlenecks.

Material and Physicochemical Dimension: Elevating the “Structure-Property-Performance” Paradigm

To surmount the formidable biosafety barriers impeding regulatory FDA/NMPA approval, next-generation molecular probe design must strictly adhere to a rigorous “structure-property-performance” logical chain. Future engineering paradigms must systematically optimize three critical parameters: high NIR-II brightness, low chronic toxicity, and tightly regulated metabolic and clearance profiles.144,145

Firstly, to combat the severe photon attenuation caused by the aqueous endoluminal environment (eg, urine in the bladder or renal pelvis), probes must exhibit exceptionally high NIR-II quantum yields and brightness. Strategies such as leveraging AIEgens to restrict intramolecular motion, developing macromolecular wrapping networks, or utilizing charge-complementary gold nanoclusters are paramount to maintaining robust signal integrity through fluid barriers.146,147

Secondly, securing low chronic toxicity requires a firm commitment to safe-by-design supramolecular architectures. Preclinical investigations reveal that conventional inorganic nanomaterials and rigid polymer systems are highly susceptible to chronic sequestration by the RES, yielding a concerning prolonged tissue retention and potential for chronic nanotoxicity. Furthermore, the immunologically mediated ABC phenomenon triggered by repeated administrations of standard PEGylated nanocarriers prematurely truncates the dynamic imaging window. To eliminate these bottlenecks, conventional PEGylation should be replaced with alternative stealth surface chemistries, such as zwitterionic coatings or biomimetic cell membranes, to minimize opsonization and ensure superior biocompatibility.

Finally, controlling appropriate metabolic and clearance profiles is arguably the most demanding hurdle for urological applications. These platforms must leverage stimuli-responsive size-transmutation strategies: maintaining a stable, macro-sized geometry (>20 nm) during initial circulation to maximize passive tumor enrichment, yet undergoing instantaneous structural degradation into sub-5.5 nm renal-clearable non-toxic metabolites exclusively upon local TME activation. By precisely modulating these pharmacokinetic clearance curves, smart nanomedicines can artificially decouple the peak of tumor receptor binding from the onset of massive urinary excretion on the chronological axis, effectively resolving the paradoxical renal-urinary contradiction that historically blinded endoluminal visualization via the urinary washout effect, while achieving highly coordinated synergistic cancer theranostics.148–150

Crucially, this structural optimization must transition from conventional mono-targeted pathways toward intelligent, logic-gated multi-receptor responsive nanoplatforms (eg, AND-gate mechanisms) to eliminate diagnostic ambiguity and false-positive margin identification, setting an unprecedented benchmark for oncological safety.

Engineering and Hardware Dimension: Robotic Integration and Digitalized Quantification

From an engineering standpoint, translating the physical benefits of the “water-transparent” NIR-IIa window into real-world operating rooms necessitates overcoming severe hardware bottlenecks. Modern urology has fully entered the era of robot-assisted surgery; however, existing Indium Gallium Arsenide (InGaAs) focal plane array detectors remain constrained by their excessive bulkiness and stringent multi-stage cryogenic thermoelectric cooling (TEC) requirements. Seamlessly integrating these sensitive optical configurations into narrow laparoscopic trocars (eg, the Da Vinci robotic system) without sacrificing spatial resolution or real-time video frame rates constitutes an urgent industrial challenge.

Concurrently, the intraoperative visual field must be augmented by real-time intraoperative AI enhancement engines. Rather than relying on standard statistical denoising, these frameworks should deploy PiDL models that mathematically incorporate light transport physics within biological media to “de-scatter” images and reconstruct distorted sub-millimeter vascular, pelvic nerve, or neural boundaries. Furthermore, the conspicuous absence of unified, cross-center calibration protocols for fluorescence intensity leaves surgeons without an objective, standardized baseline for defining positive surgical margins. Deeper, interdisciplinary collaboration between academia and the medical device industry is urgently required to develop advanced multispectral fusion endoscopic systems, driving the clinical transition from subjective, qualitative imaging to standardized, digitalized quantitative measurement.

Clinical and Evidence-Based Dimension: Confirming Long-Term Oncological Superiority

Finally, within the rigorous framework of evidence-based medicine, a massive chasm remains between demonstrating intraoperative “feasibility” and confirming long-term clinical “superiority”. Current clinical translations are predominantly restricted to first-in-human pioneering trials or small-sample observational cohorts, providing low-level clinical evidence. Isolated improvements in immediate intraoperative metrics, such as a localized reduction in warm ischemia time during partial nephrectomy or the identification of a single metastatic sentinel lymph node through dense adipose tissue, are insufficient to rewrite established global clinical guidelines.

To transform these innovative optical tools into standard-of-care treatments, large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are indispensable. Future clinical endpoints must expand beyond intraoperative technical feasibility to critically evaluate long-term oncological outcomes, including biochemical recurrence-free survival (BCR-free survival) and overall survival (OS), balanced against comprehensive patient quality of life (QoL) metrics such as urinary continence and sexual potency recovery rates. Moving forward, the confluence of physics-informed deep learning enhancement engines, intelligent “safe-by-design” nanomedicines, and next-generation InGaAs robotic hardware promises to catalyze an era of autonomous, intelligent molecularly guided surgery, delivering tangible precision medicine dividends to patients with urological malignancies.
