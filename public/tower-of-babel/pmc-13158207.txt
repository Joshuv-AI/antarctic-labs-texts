Beyond the Procedure: Revisiting the Technical Determinants of Sleeve Gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y Gastric Bypass
Authors: Dongjae Jeon, Mira Yoo, So Hyun Kang, Tae-Han Kim, Yun-Suhk Suh, Young Suk Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic and bariatric surgery has become an increasingly technique-sensitive field in which even modest intraoperative variations may influence postoperative physiology and long-term outcomes. Although sleeve gastrectomy and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass are well-established procedures, substantial heterogeneity persists in how key technical steps are performed. This review revisits several high-impact technical determinants that remain clinically relevant in routine practice, focusing on antral preservation and bougie calibration in sleeve gastrectomy, and pouch size and gastrojejunostomy (GJ) stoma diameter in Roux-en-Y gastric bypass. In sleeve gastrectomy, more aggressive antral resection and smaller bougie calibers may confer a modest short-term weight-loss advantage, but these benefits appear to be offset by trade-offs involving reflux, stricture, and postoperative intolerance. In Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, smaller gastric pouches and avoidance of an excessively wide GJ are generally associated with more favorable weight-loss outcomes, although reproducibility of construction may be more important than any single numeric target. Overall, current evidence does not support one universally ideal technical configuration. Rather, it supports a balanced strategy based on standardized, reproducible baseline technique, avoidance of extremes, and selective personalization according to patient phenotype, risk profile, and treatment goals.Keywords: Bariatric surgery; Gastrectomy; Gastric bypass; Anastomosis, surgical; Treatment outcome

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) has evolved from a purely procedure-centered discipline into an increasingly technique-sensitive field, in which even modest intraoperative variations can translate into clinically meaningful differences in long-term outcomes [1]. In contemporary practice, sleeve gastrectomy (SG) remains the dominant procedure in many countries, including Korea, whereas Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) is frequently selected when stronger metabolic effect or reflux control are desired [2,3,4]. However, procedure names alone have limited predictive value.

Although SG and RYGB are both established operations, substantial heterogeneity persists in how key technical steps are performed across institutions and surgeons [5,6]. In SG, antral preservation and bougie calibration are key determinants of postoperative efficacy and tolerance [7,8]. In RYGB, pouch size and gastrojejunostomy (GJ) stoma diameter influence weight trajectory and ulcer risk [9].

This review focuses on high-impact technical determinants that are actionable in routine practice: 1) antral preservation and bougie size in SG, and 2) pouch size and GJ stoma diameter in RYGB. These factors were prioritized because they are directly modifiable during surgery, remain highly variable across surgeons and institutions, and have comparatively richer clinical evidence linking technical variation to postoperative efficacy, tolerance, and long-term weight outcomes. Our aim is to synthesize available data into a practical model of standardized baseline technique.

SG: ANTRAL PRESERVATION

The extent of antral preservation in SG remains a central unresolved technical issue. In practice, this is typically defined by the distance from the pylorus (DFP) to the first staple firing, often contrasting more aggressive antral resection at approximately 2 to 3 cm with longer antral preservation [7]. Although anatomically straightforward, this technical choice may influence sleeve compliance, intragastric pressure profile, gastric emptying dynamics, and postoperative tolerance [10,11]. Studies have shown that patients with a DFP of 2 to 3 cm may achieve better weight-loss outcomes during the first 2 postoperative years than those with a DFP of 5 to 8 cm [7,12]. No significant differences have been observed in surgical complications such as postoperative bleeding or staple-line leak, whereas vomiting has been reported significantly more often in the shorter-DFP group [12]. In contrast, the literature on reflux remains conflicting, likely because of heterogeneity in diagnostic definitions and reporting standards for reflux-related outcomes [13,14].

Reports on long-term outcomes beyond 2 years remain limited. Gambardella et al. [13] reported that the 2 cm DFP group showed higher %TWL through 12 months than the 6 cm DFP group (32.9% vs. 30.1%), but this advantage was no longer significant after 24 months (36.8% vs. 35.7%); likewise, no significant differences were observed in mean %TWL or the rates of recurrent weight gain and suboptimal clinical response at 3- and 5-year follow-up [13]. By contrast, Demirpolat et al. [15] found no significant difference in %TWL at 1 year between the DFP 2 to 3 cm and DFP 5 to 6 cm groups (40.4% vs. 38.3%), whereas the shorter-DFP group showed significantly higher %TWL at 3 (39.1% vs. 35.6%) and 5 years (37.3% vs. 30.4%); notably, the rate of revision surgery for recurrent weight gain also differed significantly between groups (1.4% vs. 11.0%). Hamantepe et al. [16] similarly reported that, among patients with body mass index (BMI) >50 kg/m2, the DFP 3 cm group achieved higher %TWL at 2 and 5 years than the DFP 6 cm group. Given that patients with very high BMI may be less likely to achieve satisfactory postoperative weight outcomes even after MBS, these findings suggest that the technical impact of antral resection may merit particular consideration in this subgroup, although further validation remains necessary [17].

Although conflicting findings persist, a DFP of around 4 to 5 cm is generally regarded as an acceptable standard [18,19,20]. Overall, a balanced approach that considers both weight-loss efficacy and postoperative intolerance, together with a personalized strategy centered on baseline characteristics, is likely to be most appropriate.

SG: BOUGIE SIZE

Bougie calibration is another technical determinant of sleeve geometry and functional behavior. It is generally accepted that smaller bougie diameters may increase the restrictive effect and may improve weight-loss outcomes, whereas larger calibers may provide better functional tolerance [21]. However, no consensus has been reached regarding the optimal bougie size, with reported diameters ranging from less than 32 Fr to 50 Fr [22].

Early meta-analyses based largely on observational studies comparing bougie sizes below and above 40 Fr suggested that smaller bougies might be associated with a higher leak rate, without providing a meaningful advantage in weight-loss outcomes for up to 3 years [22,23]. With the subsequent accumulation of evidence and declining contemporary leak rates, the issue was revisited to determine whether smaller or mid-sized bougies might better balance efficacy and perioperative safety [8,24,25].

A network meta-analysis categorized bougie size as ≤32 Fr, 33–36 Fr, 36–40 Fr, and >40 Fr, and found that the ≤32 Fr and 33–36 Fr groups achieved similarly favorable excess weight-loss outcomes, whereas the 33–36 Fr group showed the lowest risk of overall complications and staple-line leak, suggesting this range as the most balanced option in clinical practice [8]. In a more recent meta-analysis, bougie size was categorized as 32–36 Fr, 37–40 Fr, and >40 Fr to assess both weight-loss efficacy and complication profiles [21]. Using %EWL as the outcome measure, the smallest group demonstrated significantly greater weight loss than the largest group, with weighted mean differences (WMDs) of 6.7% at 12 months and 5.4% at 24 months. However, this difference diminished over time, with the WMD decreasing to 3.2% at 36 months, suggesting attenuation of the weight-loss difference over time. Notably, complications including stricture and staple-line leak were more frequent in the smallest group. The mid-range bougie group generally demonstrated intermediate findings for both weight-loss efficacy and complication risk.

Overall, the use of a 32–36 Fr bougie may be associated with superior short-term weight loss, but evidence supporting its long-term advantage remains limited. Comparative analyses of complication profiles between 32–36 Fr and 36–40 Fr bougies have yielded inconsistent findings across studies. In expert consensus statements, opinion appears to favor the 36–40 Fr range, and survey data on real-world practice likewise indicate that more than 80% of respondents use bougies within this interval [19,20,26]. SG: TOWARD A BALANCED SLEEVE

SG is now understood as more than a purely restrictive procedure, with its metabolic and weight-loss effects arising in part from accelerated gastric emptying, reduced fasting ghrelin, and enhanced postprandial incretin responses such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and PYY [27,28]. In this context, technical determinants of SG, including antral preservation and bougie calibration, are better regarded as factors that shape sleeve geometry and function rather than as surrogates for the overall restrictive effect. Although a more aggressive degree of restriction may confer a modest weight-loss advantage, this potential benefit appears to be offset by trade-offs involving reflux, stricture, and food intolerance [13,21].

As adjunctive GLP-1-based pharmacotherapy is increasingly being adopted for patients with suboptimal clinical response or recurrent weight gain after MBS, accepting these trade-offs for only a modest weight-loss advantage may become progressively less compelling [29,30]. In this setting, technical moderation aimed at balancing weight-loss efficacy with postoperative tolerance may be more clinically meaningful than pursuing maximal restriction alone.

RYGB: POUCH SIZE

Pouch size is one of the most extensively studied technical determinants in RYGB. Overall, larger pouches tend to be associated with less favorable weight-loss trajectories, whereas smaller pouches may improve restrictive efficiency and reduce the risk of marginal ulceration at the GJ [9,31]. However, no clear consensus has yet been reached regarding the optimal pouch size. Based on studies evaluating the relationship between pouch volume, weight-loss outcomes, and postoperative complications, a gastric pouch volume of less than 40 mL has generally been suggested as an acceptable target [32,33].

A further challenge is that, although directly modifiable intraoperative variables such as total stapler length and stapler firing count have been explored, their relationship with actual gastric pouch volume remains insufficiently defined [31,34]. Of note, Ren et al. [35] proposed that a pouch measuring approximately 4 cm in width and 6 cm in length when constructed with a linear stapler would correspond to a volume of roughly 25 to 35 mL. Although this estimate was based on a simple geometric calculation rather than on direct validation against measured pouch volume, it may still provide an acceptable and practical reference for intraoperative pouch construction when interpreted in conjunction with data from other studies addressing stapler length and firing number [31,34].

More importantly, reproducibility may be the key issue. Although no single universally accepted metric exists for creating a consistently small gastric pouch in RYGB, several technical principles have been proposed. These include entry into the lesser sac at a fixed distance below the gastroesophageal junction, most commonly about 5 to 6 cm, often guided by the interval between the first and second branches of the left gastric vessels [33,36,37]. Standardized technical reports also consistently advocate a narrow lesser-curvature pouch, full posterior mobilization, and vertical stapling toward the angle of His to prevent formation of a redundant posterior pouch [37,38].

RYGB: GJ STOMA DIAMETER

GJ stoma diameter is another technically important determinant in RYGB because it influences both long-term weight control and anastomotic morbidity. Across the available literature, a wider outlet has generally been associated with less favorable weight-loss outcomes and with recurrent weight gain over time, whereas an overly restrictive stoma increases the risk of clinically significant stenosis [9,39]. Precise definition of an optimal target remains elusive, and existing evidence does not support a single universally accepted GJ diameter. This uncertainty is compounded by methodological heterogeneity, as studies frequently mix circular-stapled, linear-stapled, and hand-sewn techniques [9,39].

From a practical standpoint, focused evaluation of linear-stapled GJ may be informative, as it represents a clinically relevant and commonly performed approach in current RYGB practice [40,41]. Ramos et al. [42] reported that a 15-mm anastomosis was associated with greater BMI reduction than a 45-mm anastomosis, albeit with a 1.56% stenosis rate. In another study, a 30-mm anastomosis was associated with a lower rate of therapy failure than a 45-mm anastomosis at 3 years [43].

Analyses based on endoscopic measurements may also offer clinically informative complementary evidence, although the internal diameter intended intraoperatively may not correspond precisely to the diameter later assessed endoscopically. Heneghan et al. [44] defined an enlarged stoma as a diameter greater than 2 cm, adopting criteria proposed in earlier studies. Although they identified an inverse relationship between stoma diameter and weight loss, they also noted that the mean stoma diameter in patients who achieved successful weight loss was 2.1 cm, raising the possibility that this cutoff may have been set too low.

Still, the overall pattern appears to support avoidance of both extremes. Given the heterogeneity in entry-hole closure techniques and their potential influence on the ultimately achieved stoma diameter, a linear-stapled GJ fashioned using a 25- to 30-mm stapler application may represent a pragmatic mid-range approach.

Beyond pouch size and GJ stoma diameter, limb-length configuration remains another important but less well standardized technical issue in RYGB [45,46,47,48]. However, because the present review focuses on selected determinants that are more directly actionable in routine practice and supported by comparatively clearer procedure-level comparative data, limb length is mentioned here only as an unresolved contextual consideration rather than reviewed in detail. CONCLUSIONS

MBS should be viewed not only through the lens of procedure choice, but also through the technical details that shape postoperative physiology and long-term outcomes. Based on the currently available literature, a reasonable baseline approach in routine practice may include a DFP of approximately 4 to 5 cm and a bougie size of 36 to 40 Fr in SG, together with creation of a reproducibly small gastric pouch, generally below 40 mL, and avoidance of an excessively wide GJ, with a 25 to 30 mm linear-stapled application representing a pragmatic reference range in RYGB. These parameters should be interpreted as practical reference points rather than rigid universal targets, because the evidence remains heterogeneous and patient-specific tailoring remains important. Overall, the most defensible current strategy is not pursuit of maximal restriction, but adoption of a standardized, reproducible baseline technique with selective personalization according to patient profile and treatment goals.
