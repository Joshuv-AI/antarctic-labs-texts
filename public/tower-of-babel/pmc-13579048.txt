Gonadotropin-resistant ovary syndrome in a hypergonadotropic patient with FSHR/FSHB polymorphisms and abnormal karyotype
Authors: Aleksandra Ivanoska Trajcevski, Anne Benedicte Juul, Lea Langhoff Thuesen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGonadotropin-resistant ovary syndrome (GROS) is an endocrine disorder often associated with infertility due to a poor ovarian response to exogenous gonadotropins during controlled ovarian stimulation. The etiology of GROS is not completely elucidated. Pharmacogenetic studies suggest that polymorphisms in the genes encoding the follicle-stimulating hormone receptor (FSHR) and the FSH beta-subunit (FSHB) may contribute to the pathogenesis of GROS in some patients. We report a case of a 26-year-old woman with polymorphisms in the FSHR and FSHB genes who fulfilled the clinical and paraclinical criteria for GROS except for having a normal female karyotype. Karyotype analyses revealed low-frequency mosaicism for Turner syndrome, which, like GROS, may predispose to premature ovarian insufficiency (POI). This finding warrants regular follow-up, early detection of POI, and timely initiation of hormone replacement therapy. This case describes a woman with both GROS and mosaicism for monosomy X and triple X, a combination not previously described in the available literature. The combination supports an oocyte-related cause of infertility and suggests oocyte donation as the most effective treatment option for patients with a similar genetic profile. The patient achieved a live birth following in vitro fertilization with a donated oocyte.

Introduction

Gonadotropin-resistant ovary syndrome (GROS), also known as Savage Syndrome, was first described in 1967 [1]. GROS is a special form of premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) characterized by the hypersecretion of gonadotropins, the presence of normal primordial follicles in ovarian biopsies, a normal antral follicle count (AFC) by ultrasound scan, normal anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels, and resistance to stimulation with gonadotropins [2]. To date, only a limited number of case reports have described successful fertility treatment in patients with GROS using their own oocytes, resulting in live birth. In most cases, pregnancy has been achieved through assisted reproductive technologies, including, in some instances, in vitro maturation (IVM) [3]. However, a substantial proportion of these patients ultimately require oocyte donation (OD). Infertility due to GROS represents a rare form of hypergonadotropic hypogonadism. According to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) classification of ovulatory disorders, hypergonadotropic hypogonadism reflects impaired ovarian function with elevated gonadotropins [4]. Diagnostic thresholds for follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) vary across guidelines, with historically used cutoffs of >40 IU/L and more recent European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology criteria using >25 IU/L. It has been proposed that AFC, AMH, genetic counseling, and gene sequencing analysis get implemented into the classification algorithms to improve etiological differentiation of ovulatory disorders. Such approaches may facilitate the distinguish between POI and GROS and help identify patients who may benefit from IVM, including those with follicle-stimulating hormone receptor (FSHR) mutations [5]. FSHR as well as luteinizing hormone receptors, are expressed in ovarian granulosa and theca cells, and receptor mutations and polymorphisms may variably influence ovarian sensitivity to FSH and LH. Both inactivating and activating variants has been described. Loss-of-function mutations in FSHR are associated with ovarian dysgenesis and primary and secondary amenorrhea, whereas gain-of-function mutations may result in an exaggerated ovarian response to controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) [4, 6, 7]. We present a case of a hypergonadotropic patient with infertility that demonstrated an insufficient response to COS and was found to carry polymorphisms in the FSHR and FSH beta-subunit (FSHB) genes.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old woman with oligomenorrhea (40- to 45-day cycles) was referred to the Fertility Clinic at Hvidovre Hospital. Her male partner had normal semen parameters. The patient had no prior pregnancies. Before referral, the couple had undergone four intrauterine insemination cycles at a private fertility clinic, where an insufficient response to COS was reported, and no pregnancy was achieved. Laboratory investigations demonstrated elevated gonadotropin levels, while estradiol and AMH levels were within the reference range (Table 1). Although the patient's hormonal profile suggested relatively preserved ovarian steroidogenesis, the elevated gonadotropin levels were consistent with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism according to the FIGO classification of ovulatory disorders [4]. Due to oligomenorrhea, the patient was pretreated with 6 mg oral estradiol for 12 days, followed by 10 days of vaginal progesterone. On cycle day 2, ovarian stimulation was initiated with 150 IU subcutaneous (SC) Menopur® (a combination of FSH and LH) and 150 IU SC Puregon® (FSH). After 12 days of stimulation, insufficient follicular development was observed, and the dose of both Menopur® and Puregon® was increased to 225 IU each. Stimulation was continued for a total of 23 days. On cycle day 23, two mature follicles were identified, and ovulation was triggered with 375 µg recombinant human chorionic gonadotropin (Ovitrelle®). Oocyte retrieval was performed 36 hours later; no oocytes were obtained despite follicular flushing. The follicular fluid rapidly developed a gelatinous consistency upon contact with the Petri dish.

Diagnostic assessment

The patient was referred to the department of genetics for a second opinion where the following examinations were conducted. Chromosome analysis (EPC00120) was performed to look for 45,X or 47,XXX cell lines in mosaicism with 46,XX. Three cell lines were identified: one with trisomy X (6 cells), one with monosomy X (2 cells), and one with a normal female karyotype, 46,XX (42 cells), consistent with low-frequency mosaicism for monosomy X and trisomy X. An additional interpretative comment accompanied the result: “An increased incidence of POI has been reported in women with mosaicism for trisomy X and/or monosomy X.” The patient had no phenotypic features or clinical manifestations of Turner syndrome. Testing for Fragile X was negative. Polymerase chain reaction analysis of the CGG repeat region in the FMR1 gene showed no expansion. The patient was heterozygous for two alleles with 30 ± 1 and 40 ± 1 CGG repeats, respectively, which fall within the normal range of variation. Regarding the FSHB and the FSHR genes, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as shown in Table 2 were detected. Testing for cytochrome P450 11A1, cytochrome 450 17A1, and cytochrome P450 12A2 antibodies (IgG) showed levels below the reference range. To exclude a coexisting autoimmune etiology of POI, ovarian IgG and adrenocortical IgG antibodies were tested, and no antibodies were detected. Bone mineral density was assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which showed normal bone density.

Treatment

The couple were counseled regarding the following treatment options, IVM, which is not performed in Denmark, and OD. They chose to proceed with fertility treatment using donated oocytes.

Outcome and follow-up

Following four frozen embryo transfers with blastocysts derived from donated oocytes and one missed abortion, the patient achieved an ongoing pregnancy, resulting in live birth of a healthy child delivered spontaneously at 39 weeks and 3 days of gestation. During pregnancy, FSH levels were adequately suppressed (FSH < 1.0 IU/mL). Based on the karyotype, the patient was referred to a specialized Turner syndrome clinic, where she undergoes annual follow-up for early detection of POI. There was no indication for hormone replacement therapy prior to the pregnancy. Due to the increased risk of cardiovascular disease in women with Turner syndrome who conceive following OD [8], the patient was followed up in a specialized unit for pregnant women with heart disease.

Discussion

We report a case of GROS with poor response to COS and a genetic ovulatory disorder according to the FIGO classification. Elevated gonadotropin levels in the presence of normal estradiol and AMH were consistence with a hypergonadotropic profile with preserved ovarian reserve. Despite pretreatment with oral estradiol and progesterone and prolonged high-dose gonadotrophin stimulation, no oocytes were retrieved. Chromosome analyses revealed low-frequency mosaicism for monosomy X and trisomy X and the patient was formally diagnosed with mosaic for Turner syndrome. Although this may have contributed to the patient's hypergonadotropic state, the overall clinical and endocrine profile-including normal AMH, age-appropriate AFC, preserved ovarian morphology, and absence of Turner stigmata, was more consistent with GROS. The presence of a 47,XXX cell line may partly explain the relatively preserved ovarian reserve, as women with 45,X/47,XXX mosaicism often exhibit milder ovarian impairment compared with nonmosaic 45,X karyotypes. However, the coexistence of mosaic Turner syndrome introduces diagnostic complexity, and the phenotype is likely multifactorial. Genetic analysis demonstrated the FSHB −211 GG genotype, consistent with normal endogenous FSH production, together with FSHR variants associated with variation in receptor expression (FSHR −29 AG) and sensitivity (FSHR-2039 GG). These findings suggest impaired FSH signaling contributing to variable ovarian response. Approximately 25 SNPs have been identified in the promoter region of FSHB gene, many of which with limited clinical relevance. Several studies have evaluated the FSHB −211 G>T variant in combination with the FSHR c.2039 G>T polymorphism (Asn680Ser, rs6166) [9, 10]. The T allele of the FSHB −211 G>T variant has been associated with higher serum FSH levels in both fertile and infertile women [9]. In contrast, the FSHR c.2039 A>G has been associated with modest influence on ovarian responsiveness. Higher serum FSH levels have been observed in carriers of the FSHB −211 T allele in combination with the FSHR 2039 GG genotype [10]. This genotype was also identified in our patient and is considered to have modest effects mainly related to FSH sensitivity. Similarly, La Marca et al [11] reported reduced FSH levels in women carrying the FSHB −211 GT/TT with FSHR c.2039 AA compared with GG/GG genotypes. Another functionally relevant FSHR promoter polymorphism is FSHR−29 G>A (rs1394205) which may influence receptor expression and ovarian response during assisted reproduction. In our patient, a previously unreported combination of abovementioned three SNPs in FSHR and FSHB was identified. These variants are unlikely to be the primary cause of hypergonadotropic hypogonadism and are best considered genetic modifiers. Although we focused on FSHR polymorphism and karyotype abnormalities, other genetic factors may influence FSH-receptor signaling. For example, inactivation of guanine nucleotide–binding protein, alpha stimulating has been implicated in impaired gonadotropin signaling; however, this was not evaluated in the present case. This highlights the complexity of the underlying pathophysiology and the need for individualized diagnostic approaches. In 2021, a panel of experts conducted a Delphi consensus process to evaluate the influence of SNPs in genes encoding gonadotropins and gonadotropin receptors on the COS outcomes, resulting in a set of conclusions and individualized treatment recommendations [12]. No standardized treatment protocol exists for GROS; high-dose gonadotropin stimulation is commonly attempted but often with limited response. Consequently, alternative approaches such as IVM, in which immature oocytes are retrieved from small follicles and matured in vitro, may be considered in selected cases. However, when these strategies fail, OD remains the most effective treatment option. The early follicular phase long-acting protocol has been proposed to improve ovarian response in patients with GROS, as gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist administration may facilitate oocyte retrieval and endometrial receptivity [13, 14]. Whether IVM represents an optimal treatment strategy for patients with insufficient response to COS remains unclear and requires further investigation [5, 15-18]. FSHR genotyping may help to identify patients who may benefit from individualized approaches, including IVM [19]. Hippo pathway disruption via laparoscopic ovarian incision has been used to introduce in vivo activation and follicular growth in a subset of GROS patients [20]. In conclusion, GROS represents a rare and heterogeneous condition associated with significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case highlights the complexity of overlapping genetic and endocrine factors contributing to ovarian dysfunction. Further research is needed to improve understanding of the underlying GROS pathophysiology and to optimize management strategies. Advances in assisted reproductive technologies may provide additional insight into the condition and facilitate the development of novel therapeutic approaches.
