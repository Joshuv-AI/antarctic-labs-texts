Perceived opportunities and risks of using deepfake technology in grief treatment: a qualitative study among Dutch bereaved people and mental health care workers
Authors: Jip Martijn van Oostrum, Defne Akalin, Lonneke Lenferink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Deepfake technology, capable of simulating realistic representations of deceased people, is receiving growing attention as a potential technological tool in grief treatment. While its use has been explored in trauma treatment, its application in grief treatment remains unstudied.Objective: We examined how Dutch bereaved people and mental healthcare workers (MHCWs) perceive the usefulness and risks of using deepfake technology in grief treatment.Method: After viewing a videoclip of a deepfake grief intervention, participants listed three reasons for and against its use. We conducted a qualitative content analysis of open-ended survey responses from 159 bereaved people (86% women, Mage = 55.09) and 126 MHCWs (81% women, Mage = 39.75).Results: Bereaved people found deepfakes emotionally meaningful, envisioning them as a way to say goodbye, reconnect, or find closure. MHCWs acknowledged therapeutic potential, particularly as a supplement to established techniques (e.g. exposure, imagery rescripting), but highlighted ethical issues regarding misrepresentation, informed consent, and client safety. Both groups raised concerns about psychological risks, including hindrance of loss acceptance and emotional dependency, especially for vulnerable clients.Conclusions: Bereaved people valued opportunities for reconnection with the deceased, while MHCWs warned that such reconnection might impede grief processing. Both groups agreed that deepfakes may have therapeutic value if applied ethically, under therapist guidance, and with safeguards. Because participants were asked about hypothetical use, results reflect perceptions, not clinical effectiveness. Our findings underscore the need for ethical guidelines to balance bereaved people’s needs with professional responsibility when exploring the therapeutic effects of deepfake technology in treatment.

Introduction

Prolonged grief disorder (PGD) includes intense longing for, and/or preoccupation with, the deceased and other symptoms disrupting daily functioning, at least six (in ICD-11; World Health Organization [WHO], 2019) or 12 months after the death of a loved one (in DSM-5-TR; American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022). Population-based studies indicate that 3–5% of bereaved people meet the criteria for self-rated ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR PGD (Rosner et al., 2021; Treml et al., 2024). There is empirical evidence that PGD can be treated. However, there is insufficient evidence that PGD can be prevented (Avis et al., 2025; Wittouck et al., 2011).

Cognitive–behavioural therapy (CBT) and Complicated Grief Therapy (CGT) are examples of evidence-based treatments for PGD, which typically focus on helping people accept the reality of loss, reduce avoidance behaviour, and engage in meaningful activities (Boelen et al., 2007; Bryant et al., 2014; Lenferink et al., 2023; Reitsma et al., 2023; Rosner et al., 2014; Shear et al., 2005; Shear et al., 2014; Shear et al., 2016; Szuhany et al., 2021). However, the effectiveness of treatments remains small to moderate (Johannsen et al., 2019; Komischke-Konnerup et al., 2021), and a significant proportion of clients do not recover fully. Overall, this suggests room for improvement in grief treatment, sparking the possibility of introducing new technologies, such as deepfake technology, to enhance existing evidence-based techniques.

Deepfake technology utilises artificial intelligence, including machine learning and deep learning, to generate and manipulate audio and visuals that appear authentic but are fake. If applied in grief treatment, deepfake technology could be used to manipulate a picture of the deceased, utilising facial mapping technology, where the deepfake image of the deceased person's face is controlled by the therapist's voice (Westerlund, 2019). This technique allows the deepfake representation of the deceased to appear as though they are speaking (see Figure 1). Deepfake therapy has yielded promising results in trauma treatment in a study by van Minnen et al. (2022). They explored and applied the use of deepfake technology as a tool for perpetrator confrontation for sexual violence-related posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and moral injury. They found that these realistic simulations may help victims confront and access complex emotions that were otherwise difficult to access using traditional methods.

Using deepfake may also be helpful in grief treatment as a technology supporting existing evidence-based therapeutic techniques. Deepfake technology enables a realistic way of exposing clients to painful emotions, memories, or images related to the loss, which could be particularly useful for clients struggling with traditional techniques in grief and trauma treatment, like the empty-chair method, imaginary exposure, imagery rescripting, and letter-writing (Bryant et al., 2014; Field & Filanosky, 2009; Hagl et al., 2015; Lechner-Meichsner et al., 2024; Shear, 2015; Wagner et al., 2005).

Across these existing evidence-based techniques, two shared therapeutic ingredients are used, namely structured exposure to loss-related stimuli (e.g. emotions, memories, or images) and therapist-guided interpersonal processing (e.g. imaginal dialogue), which may help clients articulate and integrate distressing loss-related experiences (Boelen et al., 2006; Maccallum & Bryant, 2013; Shear, 2010). Furthermore, bereaved people with elevated levels of unfinished business (i.e. unexpressed or unresolved issues between the bereaved person and the deceased; Holland et al., 2018) or unsatisfactory goodbyes, which are relatively common after sudden or violent loss (Klingspon et al., 2015), may use deepfake therapy to have virtual interactions with the deepfaked deceased, potentially finding a sense of emotional resolution that was not possible in real life. In this sense, deepfake technology can be conceptualised as a therapist-guided technological tool, and not a stand-alone treatment that may make imaginal dialogue and imagery-based techniques related to communicating with the deceased more concrete and emotionally evocative by providing realistic visual and auditory cues.

Despite these promising possibilities, there are ethical concerns regarding the use of deepfake technology in grief treatment. First, it may blur the line between reality and fiction when simulating a deceased loved one. This could result in difficulties in distinguishing between the fake interaction and people's memories. Second, the use of deepfake technology in grief treatment may fuel maladaptive grieving patterns (Hoek et al., 2025). More specifically, a simulated conversation with the deepfake of a deceased may offer initial comfort, but could eventually foster dependency and interfere with acceptance of the loss. This aligns with grief theories stating that approach or proximity-seeking behaviours towards the deceased could serve as avoidance of the reality of the irreversibility of the loss (Boelen et al., 2006; Eisma & Lenferink, 2023; Shear, 2012). These insights underscore the importance of carefully considering both the drawbacks and benefits of utilising deepfake technology in grief treatment.

To examine the potential usefulness of deepfake technology in grief treatment, it is essential to gather perspectives from both bereaved people and Mental Health Care Workers (MHCWs). Earlier research highlighted the importance of including both end-users when developing and implementing new tech-supported mental health interventions to ensure safety, effectiveness, and relevance (Egan et al., 2024; Lenferink et al., 2021; Mohr et al., 2017; Torous et al., 2018; van Dijk et al., 2025).

Understanding stakeholder perspectives is essential given the ethical and clinical challenges associated with introducing deepfake technology into bereavement care. This study therefore aimed to map perceived reasons for and against the use of deepfake technology in grief treatment, as reported by bereaved adults and MHCWs. By identifying perceived opportunities and risks across these stakeholder groups, the findings are intended to inform future research.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional survey study is part of a larger project focusing on perceptions of Dutch bereaved people and MHCWs on the usefulness and nonsense of using deepfake technology in grief treatment. All participants gave informed consent. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the University of Twente (ID: 240204). Dutch people were eligible to participate if their partner, family member, or friend died at least 12 months earlier. No inclusion criteria were formulated for PGD intensity in order to include a heterogeneous sample.

For MHCWs, participation was open to people who were actively treating clients in the field of mental health. We did not specify inclusion criteria regarding specific areas of expertise and years of experience. However, MHCWs were required to spend at least one hour a week actively treating clients.

For bereaved people, invitation emails were sent to those who had completed a self-test to monitor their grief reactions on a Dutch website providing information about grief (treatment) (www.rouwbehandeling.nl). From these, a random selection of eligible people who agreed to be contacted for future research was invited to participate. Additionally, bereaved people were encouraged to share the invitation with others.

MHCWs were contacted via email through the researchers’ networks. Several Dutch professional organisations for psychologists shared the invitation with their members. Flyers with study information were posted on social media platforms, such as LinkedIn. MHCWs were additionally asked to share the invitation with their colleagues. This invitation was titled ‘The sense and non-sense of using deepfake technology in grief treatment’, with the goal to recruit bereaved people and MHCWs for and against the idea of using deepfake technology in grief treatment to obtain both perspectives.

Procedures

Both bereaved people and MHCWs completed online surveys between April and June 2024. Once participants used the link, they were directed to the survey, where they were asked to provide information about their background, the loved one who had died (for bereaved people), and their professional background (for MHCWs) .1 Participants were instructed to view a short 27 s videoclip from a Dutch documentary (Nejal, 2020). This videoclip depicts bereaved parents engaging in a therapist-guided, live deepfake-interaction with their deceased daughter (see Appendix 1 for the instruction and transcript of the videoclip). This videoclip was used as a standardised primer to provide a shared reference across participants for what such an interaction could look like in a therapeutic context. In this documentary, state-of-the-art deepfake technology is used (https://deeptherapy.ai/), which is the same as was used in PTSD treatment from van Minnen et al. (2022).

Measures used for bereaved people

Motivations for (not) using deepfake technology

After watching the previously described videoclip, bereaved people were asked to provide their three most important reasons to use deepfake technology in grief treatment with the question: ‘What are, according to you, the three most important reasons to use deepfake technology in grief treatment?’ followed by three text boxes in which participants were able to provide their reasons. This was followed by an inquiry about their three most important reasons to not use deepfake technology in grief treatment with the question: ‘What are, according to you, the three most important reasons to not use deepfake technology in grief treatment?’2

Prolonged grief intensity

Bereaved people completed the 22-item Traumatic Grief Inventory – Self Report Plus (TGI-SR+), which assesses PGD intensity (Lenferink et al., 2022). They rated the frequency of their symptoms in the past month using a five-point scale (1 = never, 2 = rarely, 3 = sometimes, 4 = frequently, 5 = always). Total scores were created by summing all items. A score of ≥71 was considered a probable PGD case. The Dutch TGI-SR + has good psychometric properties (Lenferink et al., 2022). Cronbach’s alpha in this study was α = .94.

Sociodemographic and loss-related characteristics

Questions related to background characteristics were: gender (1 = man, 2 = woman, 3 = other), age, country of birth, and highest obtained educational level (1 = primary school, 2 = high school, 3 = vocational education, 4 = college or university). Bereaved people indicated whether they had received support from a psychologist, therapist, or psychiatrist before the death of their loved ones (1 = yes, 2 = no), had received support related to the death of their loved ones, and whether they were currently receiving support related to the death of their loved ones (1 = yes, 2 = no). Additionally, we inquired if the participant had previously used deepfake technology in grief treatment (1 = yes, 2 = no). Bereaved people also responded to the following statement: ‘If I had access to deepfake technology in grief therapy, I would use it.’ with answer options 1 = completely disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, and 5 = completely agree. The latter two answer options were used as indicators for willingness to use deepfake technology in grief treatment. Considering the loss-related characteristics, we asked about the relationship to the deceased, the age of the deceased at the time of death, the date of death, cause of death (1 = physical illness, 2 = accident, 3 = suicide, 4 = homicide or manslaughter, 5 = other, namely), and perceived unexpectedness of the death (1 = not unexpected, 2 = a little unexpected, 3 = unexpected, 4 = very unexpected, 5 = completely unexpected).

Measures used for MHCWs

Motivations for (not) using deepfake technology

The same videoclip, followed by open-ended questions, was presented to MHCWs.

Sociodemographic and professional characteristics

Background characteristics were gender (1 = man, 2 = woman, 3 = other), age, country of birth, and highest achieved level of education (1 = primary school, 2 = high school, 3 = vocational education, 4 = college or university). MHCWs were asked about their current job function, workplace, years of experience in mental healthcare since completing their studies, the average number of clients treated per week, the average number of treatment hours per week, and what psychological disorders they treated most frequently. Additionally, MHCWs were asked if they had experience with treating grief symptoms (1 = yes, 2 = no) and, if yes, were asked about their average time treating grief symptoms per week in percentages. Lastly, MHCWs were asked if they had ever used deepfake technology in psychological treatment (1 = yes, 2 = no) and, if so, whether they had used it in grief treatment (1 = yes, 2 = no). MHCWs also responded to the following statement: ‘If I had access to deepfake technology in grief therapy, I would use it.’ using the same 5-point Likert scale as for bereaved people. Again, the highest two answer options were used as indicator for willingness to use deepfake technology in grief treatment.

Data analysis

After cleaning the data (for details see Appendix 2), we conducted an inductive qualitative content analysis of open-ended responses (Graneheim & Lundman, 2004; Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). Responses were divided into meaningful units. Each unit was coded with a subtheme. Subthemes were subsequently grouped into overarching main themes. Subthemes were labelled as closely as possible to participants’ wording to reflect explicit content rather than impose predefined theoretical constructs. Each motivation for or against using deepfake technology in grief treatment could contain multiple units and thus multiple subthemes. For example, one participant wrote: ‘I think it helps with exposure and could help to accept loss’. This was divided into two units labelled with the subthemes ‘Exposure’ and ‘Helps accepting loss’. See our structured pseudonymised datafile linking each unit to its assigned subtheme and main theme via https://doi.org/10.17026/SS/OHG6LG.

To avoid inflation due to verbosity, each subtheme could be counted only once per participant within each prompt category (reasons for vs. reasons against). Subtheme counts therefore represent the number of participants who mentioned that subtheme at least once within a category. Main theme counts represent the sum of participant-mentions across the subthemes belonging to that main theme (i.e. a participant may contribute to multiple subthemes within a main theme). A three-stage process was used to code the open-text responses (see Appendix 3 for details and Appendix 4 for an example).

A second independent rater (the second author) independently coded a random subset of 20% of the data. Agreement was defined as the alignment in underlying meaning, regardless of exact phrasing. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved between the first and last authors and used in subsequent discussions to refine the coding framework applied to the full dataset.

Agreement was calculated as the percentage of reasons where both raters identified overlapping subthemes, excluding blank responses. Agreement percentages are provided descriptively to indicate convergence on meaning rather than as statistical reliability estimates. For bereaved people, the reasons in favour and against using deepfake technology reached an agreement of 77% and 70%, respectively. For MHCWs, the reasons in favour and against using deepfake technology reached an agreement of 67% and 80%, respectively.

We approached the analysis from an interpretive qualitative stance, treating themes as co-constructed through engagement with participants’ accounts. Given the ethical and emotional sensitivity of the topic, we acknowledge that researchers’ backgrounds may shape interpretive decisions. The research team consisted of a senior grief researcher with extensive qualitative research experience (last author) and two psychology graduate students (first and second authors). These positions, alongside our personal experiences with loss and mental health care, informed our interpretation of the data. We adopted a critical-realist approach, treating participants’ accounts as both expressions of lived experience and as processes of meaning-making situated within broader social, cultural, and technological contexts that shape adaptation to loss. To enhance transparency and credibility, the first author conducted iterative coding under the supervision of the last author, with regular discussions to resolve ambiguities and refine coding definitions.

Results

Sociodemographic and loss-related characteristics of bereaved people

The 159 bereaved people were aged on average 55.09 years (SD = 12.93) and most identified as women (86%) (see Table 1). Regarding the relationship to the deceased, 64% had lost a partner and 17% a parent. The primary cause of death was physical illness (67%) followed by suicide (11%). The mean PGD score was 54.28 (SD = 16.18) and 15% scored above the cut-off for probable PGD cases. None of the bereaved people had received grief support using deepfake technology. In total, 39 bereaved people (25%) were willing to use deepfake technology in grief treatment if they had access to it. Discussion

This study explored how 159 bereaved people and 126 MHCWs perceive the potential value and risk of using deepfake technology in grief treatment. In total, 749 textual responses from bereaved people and 654 from MHCWs were qualitatively analysed. About one out of four bereaved people and one out of three MHCWs indicated that they were willing to use deepfake technology in grief treatment if given access.

Deepfake technology in grief treatment to target unfinished business

Our primary finding was that bereaved people and MHCWs considered deepfake technology potentially valuable to address unexpressed or unresolved issues between the bereaved person and the deceased, which is also defined as ‘unfinished business’ (Holland et al., 2018). Bereaved people often expressed the desire to say things that were left unsaid, such as unexpressed feelings, questions, or relationship issues, which are especially relevant after sudden or violent loss (Klingspon et al., 2015).

Similarly, MHCWs acknowledged that unfinished business is frequently targeted in grief treatment with techniques, such as letter writing, imaginal exposure, or imaginary rescripting. MHCWs expressed the potential for deepfake to be used as a helpful tool to help resolve this unfinished business, especially for people struggling with emotional avoidance. This suggests that deepfake technology may serve as a technological tool to enhance current evidence-based grief interventions for people with PGD, such as the ‘empty chair method’, or imaginal exposure, especially for clients who struggle with imagination (Jordan & Neimeyer, 2003; Shear, 2015; van Minnen et al., 2022). Mechanistically, this positions deepfake as a therapist-guided tool that may make imaginal dialogue and imagery-based techniques more concrete by externalising the conversational ‘other’ and providing richer cues to support engagement with avoided loss-related material (Shear, 2010).

Contrasting perspectives of reconnection with the deceased

A second main finding revealed a conflicting perspective between bereaved people and MHCWs about using deepfake technology in grief treatment. Bereaved people often expressed a wish to ‘see’ or ‘speak with’ the deceased, considering this ‘reconnection’ with the deceased as potentially comforting and meaningful. However, MHCWs recognised this simulated reconnection as clinically risky and potentially countertherapeutic. MHCWs argued that simulating reconnection with the deceased could hinder facing the reality, or irreversibility, of the loss, which is one of the main goals of cognitive behavioural therapy, which is the first-choice treatment for PGD treatment (Boelen et al, 2006; Boelen et al., 2021; Reitsma et al., 2023; Shear, 2010). This contrast can be interpreted in light of continuing-bonds perspectives, where ongoing connection may be experienced as supportive, but may also become problematic depending on how the bond is maintained (Eisma & Nguyen, 2023; Field & Filanosky, 2009). More specifically, externally expressed continuing bonds (e.g. illusions or hallucinations of the deceased) have been associated with post-loss distress, whereas internally expressed continuing bonds (e.g. beliefs that the deceased communicates through dreams) were not associated with post-loss distress (Field et al., 2013; Field & Filanosky, 2009).

It seems therefore critically important that deepfake technology is not used as a tool to simulate reconnection with the deceased. Because an unbounded, unguided, or stand-alone simulation may externalise the bond with the deceased in ways that reinforce avoidance of the reality of the loss (Eisma & Lenferink, 2023; Field & Filanosky, 2009). This co-occurrence of approach and avoidance behaviours, also referred to as the approach-avoidance hypothesis, appears to be characteristic to people with PGD (Eisma & Lenferink, 2023). If deepfake technology is considered to be used in bereavement care, psychoeducation is of utmost importance regarding the potential usefulness and limitations of deepfake technology. However, it is of utmost importance that the concerns raised in both groups are examined in scientific research before considering its adoption in clinical practice (Hoek et al., 2025).

Ethical concerns

Both groups highlighted the potential for harm when simulating a deceased person's image, voice, or behaviour. Bereaved people expressed concerns about the simulation being perceived as too artificial or inconsistent with their memory of the deceased. These reflections align with broader critiques of grief bots and posthumous AI, where authors highlight the moral ambiguity and potential dissonance created when simulated interaction deviates too far from memory or expectation (Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska, 2024). MHCWs mainly expressed concerns regarding the lack of consent of the deceased, the risk of misrepresenting the deceased person, possible misuse of deepfake materials, and misleading bereaved people with simulations.

The concerns raised align with existing literature, which highlights the risks of simulating the dead without proper boundaries or involvement from next of kin (Hoek et al., 2025; Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska, 2024). The clinical implementation of deepfake-based interventions would require clear protocols co-developed by clinicians, ethicists, and bereaved families, and evaluated in scientific research, before implementation in clinical practice is considered, to ensure the respectful and safe application of deepfake technology in bereavement care.

Strengths and limitations

One of the key strengths is the dual qualitative approach, which captures the opinions of both bereaved people and MHCWs. This approach formed a nuanced understanding of the potential usefulness and nonsense of using deepfake technology in grief treatment. Second, participants were recruited through materials titled: ‘The usefulness and nonsense of using deepfake technology in grief treatment’. This likely contributed to a sample including people from both sides of the argument.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the participants only reflected on a hypothetical situation rather than a lived experience with a deepfake intervention, which can lead to an overestimation or underestimation of its impact and real-world application. Second, recruitment of bereaved people took place via a website about grief treatment and social media, likely introducing self-selection bias towards people who are digitally engaged and actively seeking grief-related information. This limits the generalisability to all bereaved people. Additionally, the MHCWs sample was convenience-based, relatively experienced with treating trauma and grief disorders, predominantly female, and highly educated. The latter two are not surprising as most worked in the Netherlands where a university degree is needed to work as a licensed psychologist and of whom the majority is female (Dutch Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sport, 2026). However, our findings may therefore not generalise to MHCWs with other gender identities or those who work in domains other than trauma and grief. Third, generalising our findings to other countries may be limited, as this study reflects the Dutch context where mental healthcare is reimbursed, but deepfake-based services (e.g. Deeptherapy) are not, and where access and perceptions may differ from countries with more limited care or no such services. Fourth, findings should be interpreted as Dutch stakeholder perceptions after viewing a short videoclip of a deepfake session, rather than evidence of clinical effectiveness. Fifth, when analysing the data, we distinguished participant-derived terms from established psychological constructs, noting that overlap (e.g. avoidance, dependency, exposure and acceptance) can cause misinterpretation across everyday, stakeholder, and clinical contexts. Future research should define, operationalise, and examine these constructs’ potential interpretive discrepancies. Furthermore, future research should examine what factors hinder or benefit the willingness in using deepfake-technology in grief treatment from the perspective of bereaved people and MHCWs. This allows for examination of profiles of people who may be more or less receptive to such interventions (cf. Lopez-Castroman et al., 2021).

Conclusion

This study provides a preliminary account of how bereaved people and MHCWs perceive the potential usefulness and nonsense of deepfake technology in grief treatment. While bereaved people and MHCWs emphasised the emotional value of it, particularly in addressing unfinished business, MHCWs also reflected more critically on therapeutic risks, ethical concerns, and clinical boundaries. Deepfake may hold promise as a technological adjunct tool to evidence-based interventions in specific contexts, such as PGD treatment targeting unfinished business, but only under carefully controlled conditions, considering contraindications, such as risk for strong emotional dependency and dissolution tendencies. As digital tools increasingly enter the realm of mental health care, this study opens a crucial dialogue on how new technologies might not only reshape the therapeutic process, but challenge our most fundamental understandings of presence, loss, and human connection.
