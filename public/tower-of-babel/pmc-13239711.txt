Clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction and operational efficiency of AI-powered chatbots in medicine and healthcare: protocol for an AI-aided scoping review
Authors: Ellen Goossens, Pamela Mfouth Kemajou, Pascal Coorevits, Sebastian Probst, Dimitri Beeckman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionArtificial intelligence (AI)-powered chatbots are increasingly integrated into healthcare to support administrative processes, health education and chronic disease management. These systems simulate human dialogue through natural language processing and machine learning, enabling dynamic and context-aware interactions. Despite their rapid adoption, there is limited synthesis of existing research describing how these technologies are applied across different healthcare contexts and what outcomes have been reported. This scoping review aims to map and describe the existing literature on the use of AI-powered chatbots in healthcare with a focus on clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction and operational efficiency. It will identify the types of studies conducted, their key characteristics and existing research gaps to guide future research.Methods and analysisFollowing the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines, a comprehensive search will be conducted across Medline (PubMed), CINAHL, Embase, Web of Science, The Cochrane Library and MedRxiv from database inception to 10 September 2025. Studies published in English, French, Dutch or German, involving AI-powered chatbots in any healthcare context reporting on clinical outcomes and/or patient satisfaction and/or operational efficiency will be included. Studies without full-text availability, protocols, trial registrations, reviews and studies conducted solely in educational settings will be excluded. Title and abstract screening will be supported by ASReview LAB, an AI-based active learning tool to enhance efficiency. Screening and data extraction will be conducted independently by two reviewers with disagreements resolved by a third reviewer. Findings will be synthesised narratively and presented using structured evidence tables categorised by chatbot type, clinical healthcare context and reported outcomes.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required, as this study involves the analysis of published data only. The results of this scoping review will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal, presentations at academic conferences and established professional networks.Trial registration numberOpen Science Framework (OSF), https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8UE3B.

Introduction

Rationale

The digital transformation of healthcare accelerated the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into clinical and organisational processes.1 Among these innovative digital tools, AI-powered chatbots - also referred to as conversational agents - have emerged as versatile tools capable of simulating human conversation through text, speech or both,27 using natural language processing and machine learning.136 8 Their applications in healthcare range from administrative support9 - such as appointment scheduling, sending reminders,1 10 disseminating healthcare information1 9 11 and managing electronic health records9 11 - to more complex roles including health education,7 9 care management,9 12 behaviour change promotion,9 emotional support1 7 and chronic disease management.12

Recent advances in generative AI, particularly following the release of systems such as OpenAI’s ChatGPT in 2022, have expanded the capabilities, accessibility and real-world applicability of chatbot technologies.13 This shift from rule-based and retrieval-based systems towards generative models has contributed to a rapidly growing and increasingly fragmented body of literature across diverse healthcare settings and use cases.4 14 15

Despite this growth, the current evidence remains fragmented and dispersed across disciplines, clinical settings and study designs. Studies vary widely in designs, populations, settings and reported outcomes, while also highlighting challenges related to accuracy, reliability, bias and ethical considerations such as privacy and misinformation.19 1618 This variability makes it difficult to obtain a comprehensive understanding of how AI-powered chatbots influence healthcare delivery.

Existing reviews have provided valuable insights, but are often limited in scope. Many focus on specific clinical domains (eg, mental health,19 20 oncology2124 or reproductive health25 26) or on isolated aspects such as usability, acceptability or technical performance,7 9 27 rather than offering a mapped overview across healthcare contexts. In addition, several reviews predate the widespread adoption of generative AI technologies, limiting their relevance to the current landscape.13

Importantly, the impact of AI-powered chatbots in healthcare is inherently multidimensional. It spans clinical outcomes (eg, health status and treatment effects),16 28 patient satisfaction (eg, user experience and perceived quality of care)29 30 and operational efficiency (eg, workflow optimisation and resource utilisation).31 These domains represent complementary dimensions of healthcare evaluation and are widely used in health services research to assess interventions across clinical effectiveness, patient experience and system-level performance.32 33

To date, no scoping review has yet mapped the existing literature on AI-powered chatbots in healthcare across these three domains while accounting for recent advances in generative AI. A comprehensive overview of the literature is therefore needed to clarify how these technologies are currently applied, to identify patterns and gaps in the evidence, and to inform future research and implementation.

A scoping review is particularly appropriate for this purpose, as it allows for a comprehensive and structured mapping of emerging, fragmented evidence across a broad range of study designs and outcomes.

Research question

What is known from existing literature about the use of AI-powered chatbots in medicine and healthcare, particularly in relation to clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction and operational efficiency?

Objectives

The main objective of the scoping review is to map and describe the existing evidence on the application of AI-powered chatbots in healthcare and describe their reported associations with clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction and operational efficiency.

Methods and analysis

This scoping review protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines.28 The scoping review will be conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews.29 The review is scheduled to be conducted on 10 September 2025.

Information sources

The databases Medline (PubMed), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), Embase, Web of Science and The Cochrane Library, as well as preprints from recognised repositories (eg, medRxiv), will be searched to identify relevant records. The database search, conducted on 10 September 2025, covered all publications from inception to that date. All retrieved records will be imported into Rayyan for deduplication.

The search strategy is developed in collaboration with an information specialist from the Knowledge Centre for Health in Ghent. It was initially designed for the Medline (PubMed) search engine and subsequently adapted for use in the other aforementioned databases. Preprints will also be screened and included if they meet the eligibility criteria.

Data management

Selection process

Following the database search, all retrieved references will be imported into the reference management software EndNote 2025 (V.22) for initial data storage.30 All retrieved records will then be transferred to Rayyan, an AI-powered systematic review management platform, for an initial round of deduplication.31 Deduplication is particularly critical in AI-aided screening, as duplicate records can disproportionately influence the AI model’s classification algorithm.32 Rayyan’s built-in duplicate detection tool, supplemented by manual review, will be used to identify and remove duplicates.31 33 Among available tools, Rayyan has demonstrated the highest sensitivity for deduplication and offers an average time saving of 40%.

Title and abstract screening will be supported by ASReview LAB (V.2.0), an AI tool that applies active learning to prioritise the most relevant records for human review.34 35 Active learning allows the model to iteratively select which documents to present for labelling, thereby reducing the manual screening burden while maintaining methodological rigour.36 In ASReview LAB, these labels are binary (eg, relevant or irrelevant) and are assigned to an article’s title and abstract.35 A PRISMA flow diagram will be used to report the study selection process in the final review.

Step 1: preparatory screening and model training

Two independent reviewers will begin by screening a random sample of 1% (177 of 17 700 articles) to establish a shared understanding of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Following this preparatory screening, discrepancies will be discussed to reach a consensus between the two reviewers. If consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will be consulted.

This step also enables a preliminary estimate of the total number of relevant records by calculating the proportion of relevant records in the sample and extrapolating to the full dataset.37 This estimate will inform the stopping criterion for the AI-assisted screening.

Step 2: AI-aided screening

The full dataset will be uploaded into two locally run instances of ASReview LAB, where it will be independently screened by two reviewers. The consensus labels from the preparatory step will serve as training data for the active learning model. Minimum one article has to be labelled as relevant and one labelled as irrelevant in ASReview LAB.35 The model will be configured using ELAS u4 with the default settings: (1) Feature extractor: Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency, (2) Classifier: Support Vector Machine, (3) Sampling strategy: balanced sample weight resampling and (4) Query strategy: maximum certainty. These settings have demonstrated strong performance across multiple datasets.38 The model will iteratively present records for labelling, retraining after each decision to improve its predictions.38

A stopping criterion is used to determine when to end the active learning process in screening software. Screening will continue until the following SAFE stopping criteria are met: (1) All predefined key (landmark) articles have been presented by the model, (2) At least twice the estimated number of relevant articles has been screened, (3) A minimum of 10% of the dataset has been reviewed and (4) No relevant articles have been identified in the last 100 screened records.37 Before starting the screening process, the research team will first put together a list of key articles to guide the review.

Once both reviewers independently meet the stopping criterion, each reviewer will review the records suggested only to the other by the AI model. Any remaining discrepancies will be resolved through discussion. If consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will be consulted.

At this phase, Reviewer 1 will undertake a second round of AI-aided screening of titles and abstracts using a more complex and customised model. This model will employ mpnet Sentence Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers as the feature extractor and a random forest classifier with balanced sample weighting and a mixed querying strategy (95% maximum-certainty and 5% uncertainty).38

It is generally advisable to commence screening with a simpler model and advance to a more computationally demanding one once a sufficient quantity of labelled data has been accumulated, as complex models typically require larger training sets to achieve optimal performance.39 Accordingly, the consensus decisions on inclusion and exclusion reached by the research team in the preceding steps 1 and 2 will serve as the training dataset for this model.

The output generated from this screening process will subsequently be reviewed by Reviewer 1, ensuring that each article identified by the AI model undergoes a second human screening. The output of this screening step will then again be passed to reviewer 2 for independent screening of a randomly selected sample of the studies suggested by the model. Any discrepancies in classification between reviewers 1 and 2 will be resolved through discussion and consensus within the research team.

Step 3: full-text screening

All articles retained after title and abstract screening will undergo full-text review by two independent reviewers. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, adjudication by a third reviewer. This ensures that every article suggested by the AI model is reviewed by at least two human reviewers. To ensure the review remains up to date, an email alert will be set up to notify the first reviewer of newly published studies during the review period. These articles will be screened using the same process.

Data collection process

Data will be extracted using Elicit, a free online AI research assistant designed to automate elements of researchers’ workflows related to evidence synthesis and text extraction.4042 Elicit will facilitate the data collection process by generating a preliminary data extraction table.

A standardised data extraction form will be used to capture the key characteristics and findings of each included study. The first reviewer will extract data using Elicit and verify the preliminary extraction table. Subsequently, the second reviewer will validate the extracted information. All extracted data will be validated by at least one human reviewer; in this study, validation will be performed by two reviewers.

To ensure the extraction form is comprehensive and aligned with the review objectives, it will be piloted using a sample of six studies. Any necessary adjustments will be made based on this pilot. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer consulted if needed.

In cases where data are missing or unclear, the corresponding authors will be contacted via email. An initial request will be followed by a second reminder after 4 weeks if no response is received. If no reply is obtained after two attempts, the data will be considered unobtainable. The search and selection process will be summarised in a PRISMA flowchart.

Outcomes and prioritisation

The three outcome domains - clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction and operational efficiency - were selected to reflect key and complementary dimensions of healthcare evaluation. These domains represent interconnected levels of health system impact, ranging from patient-level experiences to clinical effectiveness and system-level performance. Their selection is grounded in health services research, where digital health interventions are commonly evaluated across clinical effectiveness, patient experience and organisational performance.43 44

The following outcomes will be considered in this scoping review:

Clinical outcomes: clinical outcome is defined as measurable changes in patient’s symptoms, overall health status, functional ability and quality of life resulting from healthcare interventions.45 In the context of AI-powered chatbots, these may include outcomes such as symptom improvement, disease management or treatment adherence.16 46

Patient satisfaction: patient satisfaction is defined as the degree to which patients perceive the provided information, communication and overall care experience as meeting their expectations, needs and preferences.47 This includes aspects such as usability, trust, perceived usefulness and user experience.48 49

Operational efficiency: operational efficiency in healthcare is defined as the ability of the healthcare system to deliver services effectively, measured by the alignment between planned and actual healthcare services provided. It reflects how well resources are used to ensure timely, cost-effective and high-quality care.50 In studies of AI-powered chatbots, this may include outcomes such as reduced workload, improved workflow or resource utilisation.51

In addition, studies will be included if they report on at least one of the three predefined domains, to ensure comprehensive coverage of the evidence base. Any additional outcomes reported in the included studies will also be documented to support a broad mapping of the literature.

By examining clinical, patient-reported and operational outcomes, this review aims to provide a more integrated understanding of the role and impact of AI-powered chatbots in healthcare systems.

Data synthesis

Data extraction will be conducted as outlined above. A narrative and descriptive synthesis will be employed to provide a comprehensive overview of the findings from the included studies. Extracted data will be categorised according to key themes and outcome domains. Each theme will be summarised descriptively, highlighting methodologies, results and conclusions. The synthesis will explore both commonalities and differences across studies to identify patterns, gaps and inconsistencies in the evidence base. Variability in study design, intervention characteristics and outcome measures will be examined to assess fragmentation and its potential influence on findings. Finally, the synthesis can serve as a framework for clinical practice and inform clinical decision-making.52

Ethical approval is not required for this scoping review, as it involves the analysis of previously published studies. No primary data collection or access to patient data will be conducted.

The findings of this scoping review will be reported following the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines to ensure transparency and methodological rigour. The protocol has been registered in the Open Science Framework (OSF) and is available at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8UE3B to ensure transparency and replicability.

Discussion

AI-powered chatbots are widely integrated into healthcare systems to streamline workflows and enhance service delivery.49 5355 Their applications range from administrative support to direct patient care, with growing research describing their use across various clinical contexts.1 10 However, despite their expanding role, no scoping review to date has yet summarised the current evidence on the use of AI-powered chatbots in relation to clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction and operational efficiency. Understanding this evidence base is essential to map the current evidence landscape, to clarify how chatbots are being applied in practice and to highlight areas requiring further research.

This review aims to provide an overview of existing research, identify thematic trends and highlight gaps in knowledge regarding AI-powered chatbot applications in healthcare and medicine. Existing studies are dispersed across disciplines and settings, and use cases, making it difficult to obtain a cohesive overview of current trends, applications and knowledge gaps. The planned scoping review is therefore timely and necessary to synthesise the breadth and scope of research in this area and to inform future developments in digital healthcare. By identifying gaps in existing studies, this review will inform future research priorities and guide the development and evaluation of AI-based interventions.

Conceptual considerations and assumptions

This review is based on several underlying assumptions. First, it assumes that included studies provide sufficiently detailed descriptions of AI-powered chatbot interventions to allow meaningful classification and comparison. Second, it assumes that reported outcomes - such as clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction and operational efficiency - can be meaningfully interpreted across fragmented study designs and contexts.

Ethical and practical considerations

Beyond methodological considerations, the implementation of AI-powered chatbots in healthcare raises important ethical and practical challenges. Technical limitations - such as inaccuracies, hallucinations and limited contextual understanding – may affect the reliability of chatbot-generated information. In parallel, ethical concerns include bias, data privacy, informed consent and the potential of misinformation.1618

These challenges are particularly relevant in clinical settings, where inappropriate or biased outputs may negatively affect patient safety, autonomy and trust in healthcare systems. Recent research also highlights the importance of human-computer collaboration, emphasising that AI systems should support rather than replace clinical decision-making.17 18 Enhancing transparency, explainability and appropriate human oversight is therefore essential to ensure safe and responsible implementation of AI-powered chatbots in healthcare.

Methodological considerations

In addition to mapping the evidence, this protocol contributes to methodological innovation by incorporating AI-assisted screening using ASReview LAB. This tool, which applies active learning to prioritise relevant records, has been shown to reduce screening workloads by an average of 60.02% in systematic reviews.56 By decreasing the number of records requiring manual review, AI tools also help reduce labelling errors, reported to occur in up to 21% of cases among experienced reviewers and up to 58% among less experienced ones.57

Nevertheless, the use of AI-assisted screening introduces important limitations. A key concern is the risk of false negatives, whereby relevant studies may not be identified during the initial screening process and therefore remain unavailable for subsequent human review. This limitation arises from the model’s reliance on iterative learning, which prioritises a subset of records and may result in the omission of up to 5% of relevant studies.58

To mitigate this risk, several safeguards are incorporated into the screening process. These include predefined stopping criteria, the use of key (landmark) studies and multiple rounds of model training and validation. In addition, all records identified by the AI model will be reviewed by at least two independent human reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. A random subset of included studies will also be independently checked to further enhance reliability.

Despite these measures, the possibility of missed studies cannot be fully excluded and will be considered when interpreting the findings. More broadly, these challenges reflect the ongoing balance between efficiency gains and potential risks when integrating AI into evidence synthesis workflows.59 Furthermore, AI-assisted approaches do not eliminate the potential for bias, as final inclusion decisions remain dependent on human judgment. Additionally, given the rapidly expanding body of literature in this field, some ongoing or yet-to-be-published studies may not be captured.

Strengths and limitations

This protocol has several strengths. It is based on established methodological guidance for scoping reviews and applies a transparent and reproducible approach to study identification, selection and data extraction. In addition, the integration of AI-assisted screening supports the feasibility of managing large and rapidly expanding bodies of literature.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of AI-assisted screening introduces a residual risk of missing relevant studies (false negatives), despite the implementation of safeguards such as stopping criteria, landmark studies and dual human review. Second, the expected fragmentation in study designs, populations and outcome measures may limit the comparability of findings. Third, the inclusion of only selected languages may lead to the exclusion of relevant studies published in other languages. Fourth, given the rapid pace of developments in AI, newly emerging evidence may not be captured at the time of the search. Finally, as with all evidence syntheses, the findings of this review will depend on the quality, completeness and consistency of reporting in the included studies.
