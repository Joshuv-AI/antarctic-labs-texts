ABSTRACT
PurposeBrentuximab vedotin (BV) represents a therapeutic advancement in the treatment of CD30-positive lymphomas, yet its clinical utility is often limited by BV-induced peripheral neuropathy (PN). Currently, evidence-based, BV-specific guidelines for the assessment and management of PN are scarce, resulting in variability in clinical practice. This study aimed to establish international expert consensus on the optimal assessment, monitoring and management strategies for BV-induced PN in patients with lymphoma.MethodsA modified Delphi panel was conducted, engaging a total of 13 experts from eight different countries and representing haematology, neurology, dermatology, nursing and allied health disciplines. Panellists participated in three rounds of structured surveys, with statements developed based on a targeted literature review and guidance from an expert steering committee. Consensus was defined as ≥ 70% agreement/disagreement. Consensus was achieved on 60/87 (69%) statements across domains including risk factor assessment, symptom monitoring, pharmaceutical and non-pharmaceutical management.ResultsKey recommendations include routine risk profiling before BV initiation, use of validated assessment tools at baseline and throughout therapy, multidisciplinary collaboration, and early intervention for worsening PN. Areas of uncertainty and variability in PN management remain, particularly regarding management of intermediate-grade PN and optimal non-pharmacological strategies.ConclusionThese international consensus recommendations provide practical guidance for improving clinical management of BV-induced PN and highlight the importance of early identification, personalised care and multidisciplinary collaboration. Further research is needed to address persistent gaps and inform future clinical guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00520-026-11314-5.

Introduction

Brentuximab vedotin (BV) is an antibody–drug conjugate composed of a CD30‑directed monoclonal antibody linked to the microtubule-disrupting agent monomethyl auristatin E, enabling selective targeting of CD30-expressing malignant cells [1, 2]. It is indicated for the treatment of classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL), primary cutaneous and systemic anaplastic large cell lymphoma (sALCL), peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) and cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL) [3, 4]. BV has demonstrated significant clinical efficacy for patients with CD30-positive haematologic malignancies, which are often associated with high disease burden and poor outcomes, across frontline and relapsed/refractory settings, offering improved benefits over conventional therapies [2, 5–10].

However, the clinical benefits of BV are limited by treatment-related adverse events, most notably peripheral neuropathy (PN), occurring in up to two‑thirds of patients [11]. The risk and severity of PN is related to the cumulative dose and duration of BV, alone or in combination with other neurotoxic agents (e.g. vinblastine) [11]. Extended courses of single-agent BV, such as for CTCL or sALCL, are associated with a greater severity and duration of PN compared with shorter regimens used with combination chemotherapy [11].

BV-induced PN typically manifests as sensory symptoms, including tingling, numbness, and burning sensations in a distal, symmetrical distribution affecting the hands and feet. Less frequently, motor PN may present with cramps and distal muscle weakness in the upper and lower extremities, and as autonomic PN features, such as orthostatic hypotension or gastrointestinal dysautonomia [11]. While often reversible, complete resolution may take months to years [11, 12]. Some patients experience persistent symptoms that negatively impact quality of life (QoL) [1, 11, 13].

In pivotal clinical trials and real-world studies, PN may lead to BV dose modifications and discontinuation, though modifications have not demonstrated impact on the therapeutic efficacy of BV [6, 12, 14]. Nonetheless, increased hospitalisation rates, emergency department visits and outpatient visits represent a substantial healthcare cost of PN associated with BV [15].

The American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) have published guidelines on the prevention and management of chemotherapy-induced PN (CIPN) [16, 17]. Additionally, North American experts have recently published practical recommendations focused specifically on the management of BV-induced PN in patients with lymphoma [1]. However, a gap remains for internationally developed, nuanced, practical guidelines for clinicians managing BV-induced PN specifically. Published literature has highlighted that there is no gold standard tool for assessing, grading and monitoring BV‑induced PN [11]. BV dosing can range from 1.2 mg/kg every two weeks (e.g. BV + AVD regimen), to 1.8 mg/kg every 3 weeks (e.g. BV with etoposide, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, dacarbazine and dexamethasone [BrECADD], BV + CHP), and treatment duration depends on lymphoma type and patient response [3]. Therefore, specific pharmacologic management options, including dose modifications, remain unclear. Further uncertainty surrounds individual risk factors for PN [11]. While multidisciplinary teams play a key role in effective management of PN, there are management inconsistencies across settings [11].

The aim of this modified Delphi study was to establish international expert consensus on optimal strategies for assessment, monitoring and treatment of BV-induced PN in patients with lymphoma, while identifying areas for future research, ultimately providing practical recommendations for all healthcare professionals (HCPs) involved in the management of BV-induced PN.

Materials and methods

Steering committee and Delphi panellists

Three experts were invited to form an international steering committee (SC), comprised of a neurologist, haematology oncologist, and dermatology oncologist from Spain, Australia, and Brazil. SC members were identified and invited by Costello Medical, independently of the study sponsor, based on their clinical and academic expertise in BV-induced PN and lymphoma management. Selection aimed to ensure representation across specialties and geography, considering factors including relevant publications, professional society involvement, guideline development activities and clinical experience in the field. The SC reviewed the Delphi panel protocol, approved the scope, and led the development of Delphi panel statements. The SC did not participate in consensus voting.

Eligible panellists were individuals with clinical or academic expertise in treating patients with PN; selection aimed to ensure multidisciplinary representation across professions involved in the recognition, assessment and management of BV-induced PN, including neurology, haematology-oncology, dermatology-oncology, nursing and rehabilitation. Panellists were identified through targeted literature searches and guided by the SC. Recruitment was conducted in stages to achieve a target panel size of up to 15 participants while maintaining representation across predefined specialties. A target panel size of up to 15 participants was selected pragmatically, consistent with Delphi methodology, which emphasises purposive selection of relevant experts rather than formal statistical sample-size calculation [18, 19]. The target size was intended to provide sufficient multidisciplinary representation while remaining feasible for iterative consensus development across multiple rounds. In total, 26 experts were invited to participate via email, of whom 15 agreed to participate.

The study sponsor did not participate in the identification of the SC or panellists, development of the consensus statements, or voting.

Study design

The Delphi methodology was selected as a recognised and robust process for eliciting expert consensus. The study was conducted and reported in line with ACCORD and CREDES guidelines [20, 21]. This study was not registered prospectively.

Surveys were distributed using the Costello Medical Delphi App, a bespoke web-based application for consensus voting. Statements comprised of 6-point Likert scales (strongly agree, agree, slightly agree, slightly disagree, disagree or strongly disagree), multiple-choice, and open-response questions. A ‘not applicable’ response option was provided to allow panellists an option when a statement fell outside their expertise. Panellists were invited to contextualise their responses with free-text comments.

A pre-defined consensus threshold of ≥ 70% of respondents was set, in agreement with the SC [22]. For Likert-scale items, consensus was reached when ≥ 70% of respondents selected ‘strongly agree/agree’ or ‘strongly disagree/disagree’. ‘Slightly agree’ and ‘slightly disagree’ responses were considered neutral for consensus calculations. For multiple-choice questions, consensus was defined as ≥ 70% of panellists selecting a single option. Panellists who selected ‘not applicable’ in a specific statement were removed from the consensus calculations. Consensus was not calculated for open-response questions.

Statements were grouped into the following topics: the relationship between BV and PN, the diagnostic process and consideration for PN, pharmaceutical management of BV-induced PN, non-pharmaceutical management of BV-induced PN and future directions and research priorities.

Round 1 statements were developed using findings from a targeted literature review (TLR) and guidance from the SC. Targeted searches were conducted in PubMed to identify literature published from 2014–2024 that described the management of PN in patients with lymphoma treated with BV, with a focus on practical management. Published evidence from the TLR was used to identify key themes, areas of clinical uncertainty and evidence gaps relevant to the management of BV-induced PN, which informed the development of Round 1 statements. A summary of the key evidence informing statement development across each topic area is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statements in Rounds 2 and 3 were informed by the results, free-text responses and input from the SC based on the preceding round(s). Statements that achieved consensus were removed from subsequent rounds. Non-consensus statements were reviewed by the SC alongside panellists' free-text comments. Statements close to consensus were typically rephrased and re-evaluated. Statements with lower levels of agreement were generally not carried forward unchanged; however, where the topic was considered clinically important, substantially revised or new statements informed by panellist feedback could be added in subsequent rounds. Panellists could view the anonymised aggregated response from the preceding round for related statements.

No patients were involved in the study, which was not submitted for approval by an ethics committee or institutional review board. Panellists gave informed consent to participate in the study.

Results

Of the 15 individuals who agreed to participate, 13 experts completed all three rounds: three haematologists, three neurologists, one clinical researcher specialising in PN, three nurses, two dermatology oncologists, and one occupational therapist (OT)/physiotherapist. This group represented experts from Asia and the Pacific (Hong Kong, Australia), Europe (Spain, Italy, United Kingdom, Greece, Norway), and South America (Brazil). Two invited individuals did not complete the Round 1 survey and were excluded from subsequent rounds.

Three survey rounds were conducted between 16th July and 14th November 2025 (Fig. 1). Across all three rounds, 60/87 (69%) statements measuring agreement reached consensus (34/53 [64%] in Round 1; 21/27 [78%] in Round 2 and 5/7 [71%] in Round 3); one statement in Round 1 was a scoping question, with free-text responses. Exploring the relationship between BV and PN

Panellists identified several risk factors associated with BV-induced PN, including prior neurotoxic therapy (e.g. vincristine) (77%), current neurotoxic therapy (e.g. vinblastine) (77%), high cumulative BV dose (85%) and comorbidities (e.g. diabetes) (85%). There was consensus that routine risk factor profiling should be performed before initiating BV therapy (92%), including assessment of personal and family history of hereditary neuropathies (85%). Potential risks of PN should be discussed with patients prior to initiation of BV (85%).

Early indicators of neuropathy, including progressive sensation loss (77%) and paraesthesia (85%), were highlighted, and signs warranting immediate intervention, such as distal weakness (77%), loss of balance resulting in falls (85%), and rapid progression of sensation loss (83%).

Whether there was a relationship between lymphoma type (cutaneous vs non-cutaneous) and the prevalence (40%) and severity (30%) of BV-induced PN was unclear based on voting; panellists agreed that this area requires further research (80%). Consensus was reached that moderate-to-severe PN (Grade ≥ 2) is unlikely to resolve in the absence of BV dose adjustments (83%).

It was agreed that patients who have pre-existing Grade ≥ 3 PN should be excluded from receiving BV (92%), but that the decision to exclude a patient who has persistent/ongoing PN from receiving BV should be made on a case-by-case basis, including consideration of the severity of prior neuropathy, lifestyle and availability of alternative treatments (100%).

Diagnostic process and considerations for PN

Early diagnosis

Consensus was reached that early diagnosis of PN is the most effective way to minimise severity and optimise patient outcomes (92%). The panel agreed that communicating to patients that early detection of PN can help them continue BV treatment longer encourages timely symptom reporting (85%), and that providing clear patient resources on PN symptoms is important for education (92%).

All panellists agreed that HCPs should ask patients about symptoms of PN at every visit (100%), while remaining alert to the risk of underreporting, particularly in patients with poorer prognoses (77%).

Assessment tools

There was consensus that validated tools should be used to assess PN at baseline and at least every three cycles of BV (77%), and employed at the first presentation of symptoms (77%). The Total Neuropathy Score – clinical (TNSc) was recognised as an effective method for diagnosing and grading BV-induced PN (92%), whereas consensus was not reached on the effectiveness of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE; 58%) [23, 24]. The panel supported the use of the TNSc as a diagnostic (100%) and monitoring tool (92%), particularly alongside the CTCAE and patient-reported outcomes. However, only half reported routinely using the TNSc in clinical practice (50%; Supplementary Table 3).

Key diagnostic considerations and escalation

Panellists noted that nerve conduction studies (NCS) are not routine for diagnosis of BV-induced PN, primarily due to feasibility and utility concerns for typical PN presentations. However, it was acknowledged that NCS should be considered in specific scenarios, such as severe neuropathy (85%), diagnostic uncertainty (77%), atypical presentations (77%), or suspected Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS; 100%). Immunomodulatory treatments, including intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange, were supported in confirmed cases of BV-induced inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathies, specifically GBS (100%).

There was consensus that progression from mild to severe PN generally occurs gradually over at least two treatment cycles (83%). The panel recognised the importance of considering patient lifestyle and occupation when discussing PN diagnosis and its impact on QoL (92%).

Consensus was not reached on the primary responsibility for diagnosing and grading PN lying with nurses (69% disagree; Supplementary Table 3); in Round 2, 85% of panellists agreed that this responsibility should fall to the treating physician. There was broad agreement that patients should be referred for neurology consultation during treatment if they show rapid progression of PN (92%), atypical neuropathy course (100%), uncontrolled neuropathic pain (85%), or motor weakness (85%).

Pharmaceutical management and dose modifications for BV-induced PN

The panel reached consensus that BV dose reduction is more effective at mitigating PN than prolonging the interval between doses (82%). For patients with baseline PN, starting BV at a lower dose was considered appropriate (77%); consensus was not reached for initiating at a lower dose based on multiple risk factors (69%).

Recommendations by PN grade and BV regimen

Consensus was reached that, for patients with Grade 1 PN, no dose modifications of BV are required (77%). For Grade 2 PN on a regimen of BV + CHP, dose reduction from 1.8 mg/kg to 1.2 mg/kg up to a maximum of 120 mg every three weeks was supported (70%). In patients with Grade ≥ 2 PN on BV + AVD, modification of vinblastine dose may be considered before adjusting the dose of BV (78%). The panel expressed that, in their experience, management of BV dosage for patients with Grade 2 PN on monotherapy may sometimes differ from the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC), highlighting the need for practical flexibility (83%).

For Grade 3 PN on BV + AVD (HL), withholding BV until improvement to Grade ≤ 2 before resuming at a reduced dose of 0.9 mg/kg (maximum 90 mg every two weeks) reached consensus (80%). There was no consensus for recommendations for Grade 3 sensory neuropathy on BV + CHP and as a monotherapy (Supplementary Table 4). Consensus was reached that, for Grade 4 PN, BV should be permanently discontinued (92%).

Retreatment after PN

Consensus was reached that decisions on whether BV retreatment should occur at the standard dose for patients with a history of PN should be guided by both the current severity and the patient’s past experience of PN (100%). It was emphasised that clinicians should consider the patient’s prognosis and the availability of alternative treatments (85%).

Patient involvement and pain management

Panel members agreed that patient choice is central to decision-making regarding BV dose modifications (100%), and that shared decision-making, incorporating both patient preferences and clinical expertise, is essential (85%). Patient lifestyle was regarded as an important factor in pain management decisions (92%). The panel agreed that analgesics, including duloxetine, pregabalin, and gabapentin, may be considered for managing BV-induced neuropathic pain, but recognised variability in their effectiveness (92%). There was a lack of consensus on whether the use of pain medications during BV treatment risks masking PN symptoms, limiting its monitoring, or on whether pain or symptomatic medications should be considered only once PN is established (Supplementary Table 4).

Other drug-related considerations

There was consensus that patients receiving BV should avoid excessive intake of pyridoxine (vitamin B6), given its potential to exacerbate PN (73%).

Non-pharmaceutical management of BV-induced PN

Physical therapy was identified as an effective non-pharmaceutical option for alleviating symptoms of BV-induced PN (77%). All panellists agreed that physiotherapists and OTs can provide interventions that improve physical QoL for patients with BV-induced PN (100%). Additionally, psychologists and counsellors play a key role in supporting the psychological wellbeing of these patients (77% agree). Panellists noted in free-text responses that nurses, treating physicians and social workers involved in routine oncology care may support provision of accessible psychological support.

There was consensus on the need for further research to evaluate the efficacy of other non-pharmaceutical interventions for BV-induced PN (92%).

Future directions and research priorities

All three statements on progress towards clarifying the management of BV-induced PN in Round 1 reached consensus. The panel highlighted the need for clear, evidence-based guidelines on the diagnosis (77%), pharmaceutical management (85%) and non-pharmaceutical management (85%) of BV-induced PN. There was consensus that additional research is needed to identify predictive factors for severe or irreversible PN (92%). It was agreed that a greater emphasis on patient-centred care is needed in management decisions concerning BV-induced PN, including the integration of patient and public involvement and engagement (77%).

Discussion

Early identification, patient education and routine assessment are critical for optimal management

Early identification and proactive management of risk factors are fundamental to minimising the burden of BV-induced PN. Current best practice and expert consensus recommend comprehensive risk factor profiling, including detailed patient and family histories, prior to initiating BV [16, 25].

Prompt recognition of escalating neuropathy is facilitated by the identification of early clinical “red flags,” which extend beyond the common sensory (e.g. paraesthesia, numbness) and motor symptoms (e.g. distal weakness, cramps) to include less frequently recognised autonomic manifestations such as orthostatic hypotension and constipation. Importantly, moderate-to-severe demyelinating BV-induced PN, although uncommon, requires specific multidisciplinary management approaches [26, 27].

Timely recognition of PN symptoms were emphasised as key elements of effective management, highlighting the need for regular, structured symptom assessment and ongoing patient education. Caregivers and families play a critical role in symptom recognition and care plan adherence, while patient reluctance to report PN symptoms, often due to concerns about losing access to BV and affecting their prognosis, can delay intervention [11, 28]. Equipping patients and caregivers with sufficient knowledge and resources (e.g. self-management information or symptom assessment tools) is essential to encourage early symptom reporting without compromising treatment efficacy.

Oncology patients with high clinical complexity benefit significantly from care by specialist or advanced practice nurses, who provide individualised care and foster patient engagement in treatment decisions [29]; however, responsibilities across the management pathway may vary across settings. Achieving optimal management requires the use of robust, validated tools for assessment and monitoring

While the Delphi panel reaffirmed the value of employing robust, validated assessment tools to ensure timely detection and intervention, practical barriers may complicate implementation in real-world practice. The TNSc was recognised as a valuable instrument for assessing PN, considered complementary to patient-reported outcome measures such as the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire – CIPN (EORTC QLQ-CIPN20) [30]. Yet, routine implementation of the TNSc in clinical practice is often constrained by time and resource limitations.

The TNS-nurse, a streamlined tool (sensory, motor, autonomic, pin-prick and vibration), may be a practical alternative for symptom monitoring [31]. Nevertheless, panellists acknowledged that even short-form tools are difficult to integrate and implicate additional demands on limited nursing resources; previous expert consensus has indicated that questionnaires with more than three items may be too onerous in clinical practice [25].

Given these feasibility concerns, greater emphasis may be warranted on involving patients directly in the assessment of PN as part of broader symptom management. Patient-reported outcome tools, such as the QLQ-CIPN20 and Patient-Reported Outcomes CTCAE (PRO-CTCAE) scale, may be preferable alternatives or adjuncts to clinical assessment tools to improve treatment decision-making in routine care [32, 33].

Agreement was reached on dose modification in some scenarios

While clear consensus was reached on the approaches to management of Grade 1 and 4 neuropathy, which aligned with product labelling, there was less consensus regarding intermediate CTCAE grades [3]. The management of Grade 3 PN in patients treated with BV, whether as monotherapy or in combination with CHP for sALCL and other CD30 + peripheral T-cell lymphomas, was a particular area of uncertainty.

Grade 3 and above PN is relatively uncommon, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 6% in patients with cHL [34]. Consequently, direct clinical experience in managing this level of severity is limited, and there is a lack of robust data on the rate and likelihood of symptom resolution compared to Grade 2 PN [11]. Patients at greatest risk of developing Grade 3 PN, such as those with high cumulative exposure to neurotoxic agents in the relapsed/refractory setting, typically have a poorer prognosis due to limited treatment options and advanced disease [6]. Free-text responses from the Delphi panel consistently recognised that Grade 3 PN warrants prompt intervention due to its significant impact on patients’ QoL, aligning with the SmPC for BV [3]. In this context, optimal management, whether dose reduction, delay, or discontinuation, should be tailored to prognosis, treatment stage and lymphoma subtype.

Similarly, varying approaches to managing Grade 2 neuropathy were highlighted, particularly when BV is used as a monotherapy, with panellists indicating that dose modification would be context-dependent. Free text responses indicated, for example, that it is more likely that the treating physician would modify dosing if Grade 2 PN occurred in the patient’s third cycle of treatment, compared with their penultimate one.

Contrary to the prescribing information label, when BV is used in combination with AVD, the panel agreed that vinblastine could be reduced before considering modifications to BV dose. Indeed, real-world evidence has suggested that, in practice, some HCPs will choose to modify vinblastine in this regimen (17% of patients) when managing PN [12]. Emerging trial evidence suggests that, for patients with early-stage cHL, omitting vinblastine doses (i.e. a regimen of BV + AD) resulted in a much-improved toxicity profile, with predominantly Grade 1 PN reported and no cases of Grade 3 and above neuropathy [35].

Shared decision-making and patient-centred care are essential

The panel emphasised the importance of dialogue with patients about PN, including discussion of risk factors, early symptoms and the potential impact on daily functioning and QoL. The impact of PN is individual, and even lower grades of neuropathy may be significant for people reliant on fine motor skills or dexterity. For example, musicians, surgeons, artists or professionals reliant on precise hand movements may experience a disproportionate impact on their work and overall well-being, even at lower grades of neuropathy. When discussing the implications of PN, HCPs should be careful to consider this, including an emphasis on the risk of being unable to work, if applicable. HCPs should be mindful that it is often difficult for patients to envision the impact of PN on their life, if they have not experienced it before.

While it was noted that clinicians’ expertise should be used to inform decisions around dose modifications, a shared decision-making approach was emphasised by the panel. Shared decision-making ensures patients are fully informed about the risks and benefits of various management approaches, including the rationale behind dose modifications and interruptions.

Supportive pharmacological and non-pharmaceutical approaches play a key role

Pain medications (e.g. duloxetine, pregabalin and gabapentin) may be considered for managing BV-induced PN; however, as robust evidence regarding their efficacy is limited, they should not be considered a primary solution for PN management [17].

The roles of physiotherapists, OTs and nurses in providing supportive care to enhance physical QoL was emphasised. Existing evidence demonstrates that exercise therapy is effective in managing CIPN, leading to improvements in physical function (including balance and strength) as well as reductions in pain and overall symptom burden [36]. OTs can support improvement in physical QoL symptoms with sensory re‑education and graded tactile tasks targeting somatosensory discrimination [37].

Given the emotional toll of PN, psychologists can help patients and families adapt and improve QoL [38]. Social workers may be appropriate in cases where severe PN leads to dependence or challenges in daily living [39].

Optimal referral timing to allied health professionals (AHPs) for psychological and/or physical QoL support was not explored in the panel. However, SC members emphasised a proactive referral, to ensure interventions are in place prior to the development of severe PN symptoms. Neurologists and the treating clinical team should be familiar with local referral pathways to AHPs, ensuring streamlined communication and coordinated patient care.

Overall, while non-pharmaceutical and multidisciplinary strategies are valued, more evidence is required to define their efficacy, best practices and specific points of intervention within the care pathway [16, 17].

Limitations

While the Delphi method is valuable for areas lacking robust empirical evidence, recommendations are influenced by the subjective experiences and clinical practices of the panellists and should be interpreted within this context. Although the panel included a range of HCPs, pharmacists were not represented. Inclusion of pharmacists may have provided additional perspectives on pharmacological management and medication optimisation. Additionally, participants from the United States and Canada were excluded because separate activities focused on BV-induced PN management were understood to be ongoing in these regions at the time of study design. The study therefore sought to capture perspectives from other international regions and should not be interpreted as representing a global consensus. Although this Delphi panel includes representation across multiple countries outside of North America, recommendations may not be generalisable to all regions or healthcare systems. Finally, areas in which evidence is sparse, or consensus was not reached, highlight the need for further research to address remaining gaps.

Conclusion

This Delphi panel addressed a key gap in the management of BV-induced PN management, establishing consensus on foundational principles for assessment and monitoring, management practices by dose modification and multidisciplinary support. Further research is required to clarify nuances in dose modification, efficacy of non-pharmaceutical interventions and referral pathways. However, these consensus recommendations lay the groundwork for future guideline development to optimise the management of BV-induced PN, with the ultimate goal of improving patient outcomes and QoL.
