NPY promotes macrophage polarization and hepatic fibrogenesis via PI3K/MAPK-dependent neuroimmune mechanisms
Authors: Qiang Lin, Xuyang Zheng, Weijia Dou, Qiangshan Bai, Shiyang Liang, Zhifu Kou, Hongwei Guo, Yao Ding, Shuguang Zhao, Jingjie Wang, Jikai Yin, Zhenxiong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuropeptide Y (NPY) is a key regulator of neuroimmune homeostasis. However, its role in the intrahepatic immune microenvironment and hepatic fibrosis remains unclear. We aimed to elucidate whether NPY participates in hepatic fibrogenesis via neuroimmune crosstalk and to dissect the underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsLiver tissues from cirrhotic and non-cirrhotic human subjects were collected. Hepatic fibrosis was induced in mice via intraperitoneal carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄) injection or the 3,5-diethoxycarbonyl-1,4-dihydrocollidine (DDC) diet. The distribution of NPY and its receptors was detected in human and mouse hepatic tissues. NPY-knockout mice were used to evaluate the impact of NPY deletion on hepatic fibrosis, inflammation, and macrophage polarization. Hepatic NPY receptors were silenced in vivo, and the corresponding receptors or downstream kinases were blocked in vitro, to define the underlying signaling mechanisms.ResultsNPY was elevated in fibrotic human and mouse livers and primarily secreted by intrahepatic sympathetic nerves. NPY1R and NPY2R (but not NPY4R/NPY5R) were increased and localized in hepatic macrophages. NPY deletion mitigated liver injury, collagen deposition, and inflammation, and suppressed both M1 and M2 macrophage polarization. In vivo knockdown of NPY1R or NPY2R recapitulated these protective effects. In vitro, NPY enhanced M1 polarization via NPY1R/NPY2R-coupled PI3K–PAK1–c-Jun signaling, whereas it promoted M2 polarization through the PI3K–PAK1–RSK cascade.ConclusionNPY drives both M1 and M2 macrophage polarization and accelerates hepatic fibrogenesis through NPY1R/NPY2R–PI3K–MAPK-dependent neuroimmune signaling. These findings identify NPY and its receptors as potential therapeutic targets for hepatic fibrosis.Graphical AbstractIntrahepatic sympathetic nerve-derived NPY promotes M1 and M2 macrophage polarization through NPY1R/NPY2R–PI3K–PAK1 cascades (c-Jun for M1, RSK for M2), driving hepatic stellate cell activation via profibrotic cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, TGF-β, and PDGF-BB). Solid lines: experimentally validated pathways. Introduction

Hepatic fibrosis constitutes a common pathological hallmark of chronic liver diseases (CLDs), characterized by excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) produced by activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) [1, 2]. Efficacious anti-fibrotic therapies are still lacking, and CLDs remain widely underdiagnosed and suboptimally managed. Consequently, most patients with early-stage hepatic fibrosis progress to advanced liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). These end-stage liver diseases account for approximately 1.2 million deaths annually, representing 2.4% of global mortality [3].

The hepatic inflammatory microenvironment creates a permissive niche that sustains the activation of HSCs and propels fibrotic progression [4, 5]. Neuroimmune crosstalk is essential for tissue homeostasis and has attracted growing attention over recent decades [6, 7]. Neuroimmune signaling is involved in the pathogenesis of multiple human disorders and represents a promising therapeutic target [8, 9]. In chronic liver diseases, neuroimmune communication critically modulates inflammation, fibrogenesis, malignant transformation, and tissue remodeling [10, 11]. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying intrahepatic neuroimmune interplay remain poorly elucidated. Uncovering these regulatory pathways will facilitate the development of novel neuroimmune-targeted therapeutic strategies for hepatic fibrosis.

Neuropeptide Y (NPY), a polypeptide neurotransmitter, is broadly expressed in the central and peripheral nervous systems [12–14]. Six NPY receptor subtypes (NPY1R–NPY6R) have been identified, among which NPY1R, NPY2R, NPY4R, and NPY5R primarily mediate mammalian physiological processes [15]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that NPY regulates diverse biological processes including appetite, energy metabolism, nociception, emotional processing, and cardiovascular function [16, 17]. NPY receptors are abundantly expressed on multiple immune cell populations, including macrophages, T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, and neutrophils. These receptors exert critical regulatory effects during immune-associated pathological conditions such as infection, inflammatory disorders, and autoimmune diseases [17, 18]. Previous studies have reported that hepatic NPY expression was markedly upregulated in patients with autoimmune hepatitis and liver cirrhosis [19, 20]. Nevertheless, the exact biological functions of intrahepatic NPY in regulating the hepatic immune microenvironment and fibrogenesis remain poorly defined.

Hepatic macrophages are core innate immune cells of the liver and critically regulate chronic inflammation and progressive fibrogenesis. They are also key targets of neuroimmune regulation [5, 21]. Macrophages exhibit prominent heterogeneity and dynamic phenotypic plasticity, of which the M1/M2 polarization paradigm is the most widely recognized classification system [22]. M1 and M2 macrophage subsets exert context-specific pro-fibrotic and anti-fibrotic functions. Classically activated M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines to amplify inflammatory responses and activate HSCs, whereas alternatively activated M2 macrophages release pro-fibrotic mediators that promote ECM accumulation [23].

NPY is known to regulate macrophage function in multiple peripheral tissues. We therefore hypothesized that intrahepatic nerve-derived NPY mediates local neuroimmune crosstalk by acting on hepatic macrophages, and in turn modulates the progression of hepatic fibrosis. Accordingly, the present study was designed to characterize the regulatory roles of NPY in the fibrotic hepatic immune microenvironment. We also dissected the underlying mechanisms of NPY-mediated neuroimmune signaling and identified candidate targets for precision therapy of hepatic fibrosis.

Materials and methods

Human liver samples

Human hepatic tissue specimens were collected from Tangdu Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University. The specimens comprised two groups: 6 from patients with hepatic hemangioma (benign liver disease) and 6 from patients with liver cirrhosis. None of the benign specimens showed hepatic fibrosis or hepatic steatosis (> 5% steatosis) upon pathological examination. This study was performed in strict accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures involving human specimens were approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Tangdu Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University.

Animals

Wild-type specific-pathogen-free (SPF) C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University. Systemic NPY knockout (NPY-sKO) mice were obtained from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. All experimental mice were housed in an SPF environment with stable and standardized breeding conditions. Mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups using a random number generator. Investigators performing histological scoring, image analysis, and molecular quantification were blinded to group allocation. All animal experiments were conducted in strict compliance with the ethical guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Fourth Military Medical University.

Construction of the hepatic fibrosis model and pharmacological ablation of sympathetic nerves

The CCl₄-induced hepatic fibrosis model was established by intraperitoneal injection of 10% CCl₄ dissolved in olive oil (2 mL/kg) twice weekly for 5 or 10 weeks. A cholestatic liver fibrosis model was established by feeding mice with 0.1% DDC-supplemented diet for either 3 or 6 weeks. For the CCl₄ model, control mice received an equal volume of olive oil via intraperitoneal injection following the same schedule. For the DDC model, control mice were fed standard chow without DDC supplementation. Body weight, food intake, and general condition of all experimental mice were monitored and recorded weekly. Upon completion of the induction period, serum specimens were harvested from mice in each group and delivered to Servicebio (China) for quantification of serum ALT and AST levels. For pharmacological sympathetic denervation, mice received intraperitoneal injections of 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA; 150 mg/kg) once daily for 5 consecutive days before CCl₄ or DDC administration. During the subsequent fibrosis induction period, 6-OHDA administration was maintained twice weekly to sustain sympathetic denervation. Mice in the sympathetic nerve-preserved groups were administered an equal volume of normal saline via the same route and schedule.

Construction and tail vein injection of adeno-associated virus (AAV)

Macrophage-targeted NPY1R- and NPY2R-knockdown mouse models were constructed using adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) vectors. To mitigate the intrinsic hepatotropism of AAV8, the viral vectors were engineered to contain F4/80 promoter and encode short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting mouse NPY1R or NPY2R. This F4/80 promoter design has previously been reported to preferentially drive shRNA expression in hepatic macrophages while largely sparing parenchymal hepatocytes [24]. AAV vectors for NPY1R knockdown (AAV-shNPY1R), NPY2R knockdown (AAV-shNPY2R), and the negative control (AAV-shNC) were packaged and produced by Haixing Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Six-week-old wild-type (WT) mice were randomly assigned to three groups: AAV-shNC control group, AAV-shNPY1R group, and AAV-shNPY2R group. Each mouse received a 100 µL viral solution (1.5 × 1011 viral genomes) via tail vein injection. Three weeks after the initial injection, hepatic fibrosis was induced with CCl₄ for 10 weeks or DDC for 6 weeks. The corresponding AAV vectors were repeatedly injected via the tail vein throughout the induction period.

Isolation and culture of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)

Femurs and tibias were aseptically harvested from WT mice, and all adherent muscle and connective tissues were thoroughly dissected and removed. The bone marrow cavities were repeatedly rinsed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The collected cell suspension was filtered through a cell strainer and centrifuged to pellet the cells. Red blood cell lysis buffer was subsequently used to eliminate erythrocytes according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by two washes with PBS to obtain purified primary BMDMs. Isolated BMDMs were resuspended and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL M-CSF. For M1 polarization, BMDMs were treated with 100 ng/mL LPS plus 20 ng/mL IFN-γ. For M2 polarization, cells were treated with 20 ng/mL IL-4 plus 10 ng/mL IL-13. M0 macrophages were cultured in M-CSF alone without additional polarizing stimuli. To explore the regulatory effects of NPY on macrophage polarization, M0, M1, and M2 macrophages were incubated with 2 µmol/L NPY. PBS served as the vehicle control. To elucidate the underlying signaling mechanisms, polarized macrophages were co-treated with NPY and specific pathway inhibitors. The inhibitors included BIBP3226 (NPY1R inhibitor, 1 µmol/L), BIIE0246 (NPY2R inhibitor, 1 µmol/L), LY294002 (PI3K inhibitor, 20 µmol/L), IPA-3 (PAK1 inhibitor, 5 µmol/L), SP600125 (JNK inhibitor, 10 µmol/L), and SCH772984 (ERK inhibitor, 10 µmol/L). Alterations in NPY-mediated macrophage polarization were assessed following pharmacological inhibition of the corresponding targets. For each independent experiment, cells were randomly allocated to treatment groups using a random number generator.

Western blotting

Tissue and cell specimens were lysed in RIPA buffer to isolate total protein, and protein content was quantified by the BCA method. Total protein was separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk diluted in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature with gentle shaking. The membranes were then incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. Finally, target protein bands were visualized using an Enhanced ECL chemiluminescence kit. Band intensities were quantified with Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, USA) and normalized to GAPDH.

Histological analysis

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) Staining: Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by gradient dehydration, clearing, and paraffin embedding. Consecutive 4-µm-thick tissue sections were prepared. After dewaxing, hydration, antigen retrieval, and endogenous peroxidase blockade, the sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with primary and secondary antibodies, followed by 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining. All sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral balsam. Histological images were acquired using an Olympus optical microscope. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining: Paraffin sections were dewaxed and rehydrated following the same protocol used for IHC staining. The sections were successively stained with hematoxylin and eosin, then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral balsam. Hepatic histopathological changes, including hepatocyte degeneration, necrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration, were evaluated under a light microscope. Injury severity was quantified using the Ishak scoring system (0–3 points for hepatocyte injury and 0–3 points for inflammatory infiltration). All histological evaluations were performed independently by two blinded pathologists, and the average scores were calculated for statistical analysis. Masson’s Trichrome Staining: Paraffin sections were dewaxed and rehydrated as described above. The sections were sequentially stained with hematoxylin, Ponceau S-acid fuchsin, phosphomolybdic acid, and aniline blue, and then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral balsam. Stained tissue sections were imaged using an Olympus light microscope. Immunofluorescence (IF) Staining: Dewaxing, rehydration, and antigen retrieval of paraffin sections were performed as described for IHC. The tissue sections then underwent permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100, and were subsequently incubated with 5% BSA prepared in PBS for blocking. After sequential incubation with primary and fluorescent secondary antibodies, cell nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Fluorescence images were captured and analyzed using an Olympus fluorescence microscope. All histological and immunohistochemical images were quantitatively analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from hepatic tissues or cultured cells using Trizol reagent. Reverse transcription of isolated RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA) was performed with the PrimeScript RT Master Mix kit (Takara, Japan). Specific forward and reverse primers were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). qRT-PCR assays were conducted using the TB Green Premix Ex Taq kit (Takara, Japan) following the manufacturer’s standard protocols.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Samples and standard solutions were added to 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. Subsequently, biotin-labeled antibodies were added, and the plates were incubated for 60 min at 37 °C. After five washes with washing buffer, avidin-biotin complex (ABC) working solution was introduced, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing, 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate solution was applied, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 15–20 min. The reaction was finally terminated with stop solution, and the concentrations of target molecules were quantified based on the standard curve.

RNA sequencing analysis

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from hepatic tissues and non-parenchymal cells (NPCs) were isolated in accordance with a previously published protocol [25]. The extracted total RNA was submitted to Shanghai Bioprofile Technology Co., Ltd. for high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). After purification and library construction, paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA). After quality control filtering of the raw sequencing data, high-quality clean reads were retained and aligned against the species-matched reference genome. The resulting alignment files were subsequently used to calculate gene expression levels. Gene expression levels were quantified based on the alignment results. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups of samples were screened using the criteria of |log2fold change| ≥ 1 and P < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis was subsequently performed to characterize the biological functions of the identified DEGs.

Flow cytometry analysis

Single-cell suspension of hepatic NPCs (1 × 10⁶ cells/mL in PBS) was incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. After two washes with PBS, cell samples were analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). The acquired data were analyzed using CytExpert software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical comparisons were performed based on independent biological replicates. Data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Pairwise comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent-samples t-test, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied for multiple-group comparisons. All data analysis and graph generation were performed using SPSS 23 (IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.02 (GraphPad Software, USA). Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05.

Results

Sympathetic nerve-derived NPY is upregulated in liver fibrosis

To elucidate NPY expression patterns in hepatic fibrosis, we first analyzed a public liver RNA sequencing dataset (GSE77627), which contained samples derived from 14 healthy individuals and 22 cirrhotic patients. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that NPY expression was significantly upregulated in cirrhotic liver tissues (Fig. 1A–C). To validate these sequencing findings, we collected liver tissues from 6 cirrhotic patients and 6 non-fibrotic control subjects. Immunofluorescence staining was performed to quantify hepatic NPY levels. Consistently, immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated a marked expansion of NPY-positive areas in cirrhotic liver specimens (Fig. 1D–I). Given the limited size of the clinical cohort (n = 6 per group), these human findings should be regarded as exploratory and were therefore further verified in two independent murine fibrosis models induced by CCl₄ and DDC, respectively. Immunofluorescence staining showed that the hepatic NPY-positive area expanded in a time-dependent manner during fibrotic progression in both models relative to controls (Fig. 1J–O; Fig. S1A–F). Consistent with these morphological observations, Western blotting further revealed that NPY protein levels were markedly elevated in the livers of cirrhotic patients and progressively increased in a fibrosis severity-dependent manner in the livers of CCl₄- and DDC-induced fibrotic mice (Fig. 1P–S; Fig. S1G–H). To determine the anatomical distribution and cellular origin of hepatic NPY, we performed double immunofluorescence staining for NPY and the sympathetic nerve marker tyrosine hydroxylase (TH). Strong colocalization between NPY (green) and TH (red) signals was observed in mouse liver tissues (Fig. 1T; Fig. S1I). This indicated that intrahepatic NPY was predominantly localized to sympathetic nerve fibers. To further confirm the sympathetic origin of fibrotic hepatic NPY, we pharmacologically ablated sympathetic nerves in CCl₄ and DDC fibrotic mice using 6-OHDA. Sympathetic denervation markedly reduced the hepatic NPY-positive fluorescent area in fibrotic mice compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 1T–V; Fig. S1I–K). These findings demonstrated significant NPY upregulation in hepatic fibrosis. NPY was primarily derived from intrahepatic sympathetic nerve fibers, and its elevation was closely correlated with fibrotic progression.

Hepatic macrophages are important target cells of NPY-positive sympathetic nerves

To identify the receptors mediating NPY’s effects on hepatic fibrosis, we examined the expression of NPY1R, NPY2R, NPY4R, and NPY5R in clinical liver fibrosis samples by immunofluorescence. NPY1R and NPY2R were markedly upregulated in cirrhotic livers compared with non-fibrotic controls. In contrast, NPY4R and NPY5R were undetectable by immunofluorescence in either normal or fibrotic tissues (Fig. 2A–B). Consistent with clinical observations, similar receptor expression patterns were recapitulated in both CCl₄-induced and DDC-induced hepatic fibrosis models. Immunofluorescence staining revealed progressive increases in NPY1R and NPY2R expression during fibrosis progression. NPY4R and NPY5R remained undetectable in both normal and fibrotic mouse livers (Fig. 2C–D; Fig. S2A–B). Western blotting further demonstrated marked upregulation of NPY1R and NPY2R protein levels in the livers of cirrhotic patients, as well as their time-dependent upregulation in the livers of CCl₄- and DDC-induced fibrotic mice (Fig. 2E–J; Fig. S2C–E). To further clarify the cellular localization of NPY1R and NPY2R, we conducted double immunofluorescence staining for receptors and macrophage-specific markers. In human cirrhotic liver specimens, NPY1R and NPY2R fluorescence signals (green) exhibited robust colocalization with the macrophage marker CD68 (red) (Fig. 2K–L). Similarly, in mouse fibrotic liver tissues, NPY1R and NPY2R fluorescence signals (green) showed prominent colocalization with the murine macrophage marker F4/80 (red) (Fig. 2M–N; Fig. S2F–G). These findings suggested that hepatic macrophages represent important cellular targets of NPY signaling, implicating sympathetic nerve–macrophage neuroimmune crosstalk in hepatic fibrosis progression.

NPY knockout alleviates hepatic injury and fibrosis

To further elucidate the function of NPY in hepatic fibrogenesis, we used systemic NPY-knockout (NPY-sKO) mice. The effects of NPY ablation on liver injury and fibrosis progression were evaluated by histological staining, serological assays, and qRT-PCR. H&E staining showed that wild-type (WT) mice with hepatic fibrosis exhibited severe hepatocellular degeneration and necrosis, accompanied by robust inflammatory infiltration. In contrast, NPY-sKO fibrotic mice displayed markedly ameliorated hepatic damage and reduced inflammatory cell accumulation (Fig. 3A; Fig. S3A). Serum ALT and AST levels were significantly elevated in WT fibrotic mice but markedly reduced in NPY-sKO fibrotic mice (Fig. 3B–C; Fig. S3B–C). Ishak scoring further confirmed that NPY-sKO fibrotic mice exhibited significantly lower injury scores than their WT counterparts (Fig. 3D; Fig. S3D). Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated a marked deposition of collage and obvious fibrous septa formation in the liver tissues of WT fibrotic mice. Conversely, the collagen-positive area was dramatically reduced in NPY-sKO mice (Fig. 3A, E; Fig. S3A, E). Immunohistochemical staining further verified that the positive areas of α-SMA and Col1a1 were markedly smaller in NPY-sKO liver tissues than in WT controls (Fig. 3A, F, G; Fig. S3A, F, G). qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that the mRNA levels of fibrosis-related genes (α-SMA, Col1a1, and Col1a2) were significantly lower in NPY-sKO fibrotic livers than in WT fibrotic livers (Fig. 3H–J; Fig. S3H–J). These findings demonstrated that NPY knockout significantly mitigated hepatocellular injury and attenuated the progression of hepatic fibrosis.

NPY knockout mitigates hepatic inflammatory cell infiltration and inflammatory responses

We further explored the impact of NPY knockout on the intrahepatic inflammatory microenvironment during hepatic fibrogenesis. Immunohistochemical staining was used to assess inflammatory cell infiltration. The positive areas of F4/80 (macrophage marker), CD11b (monocyte/macrophage marker), CD3 (T lymphocyte marker), and Ly6g (neutrophil marker) in hepatic tissues of NPY-sKO fibrotic mice were significantly reduced compared with WT fibrotic counterparts (Fig. 4A–E; Fig. S4A–E). These observations demonstrated that NPY ablation markedly alleviated the infiltration of macrophages, neutrophils, and T lymphocytes in fibrotic liver tissues. qRT-PCR revealed a significant decrease in hepatic transcript levels of the proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β in NPY-sKO fibrotic mice relative to WT fibrotic controls (Fig. 4F–G; Fig. S4F–G). Consistent with the transcriptional alterations, serum levels of TNF-α and IL-1β were also markedly decreased in NPY-sKO fibrotic mice (Fig. 4H–I; Fig. S4H–I). Taken together, these results indicated that NPY knockout suppressed inflammatory cell recruitment, particularly macrophage infiltration, and ameliorated intrahepatic inflammation during hepatic fibrosis.

NPY knockout suppresses hepatic macrophage recruitment and M1/M2 polarization

We further investigated the impact of NPY knockout on hepatic macrophage polarization during the progression of hepatic fibrosis. Immunofluorescence staining showed that the areas positive for M1 markers (CD86 and iNOS) and M2 markers (CD206 and Arg1) were significantly reduced in NPY-sKO fibrotic livers compared with WT controls (Fig. 5A–E; Fig. S5A–E). Consistent with these findings, qRT-PCR analysis confirmed the same changes at the mRNA level. CD86, iNOS, CD206, and Arg1 were markedly downregulated in NPY-sKO fibrotic livers compared with WT counterparts (Fig. 5F–I; Fig. S5F–I). Western blotting further confirmed the reduced expression of CD86, CD206, α-SMA, and Col1a1 proteins in NPY-deficient hepatic tissues under fibrotic conditions (Fig. 5J–N; Fig. S5J–N). Flow cytometry data showed a significantly lower proportion of F4/80⁺ hepatic macrophages in NPY-sKO fibrotic mice than in WT fibrotic mice (Fig. 5O, S; Fig. S5O, S). Subsequent phenotypic analysis demonstrated that the proportions of pro-inflammatory F4/80⁺CD86⁺ M1 macrophages, anti-inflammatory F4/80⁺CD206⁺ M2 macrophages, and pro-inflammatory F4/80⁺Ly6c⁺ macrophages were all substantially reduced in NPY-sKO fibrotic livers (Fig. 5P–V; Fig. S5P–V). These results suggested that NPY promoted hepatic macrophage recruitment and facilitated both M1 and M2 polarization.

In vivo knockdown of NPY1R and NPY2R ameliorates macrophage M1/M2 polarization and hepatic fibrosis

To further validate the distinct roles of NPY1R and NPY2R in hepatic fibrogenesis in vivo, we generated macrophage-targeted NPY1R- and NPY2R-knockdown mice by tail vein injection of F4/80-driven AAV8-shRNA vectors. Western blotting and double immunofluorescence staining confirmed efficient receptor silencing in hepatic macrophages. AAV-sh-NPY1R and AAV-sh-NPY2R markedly reduced the expression of their respective receptors in macrophages from both CCl₄- and DDC-induced fibrotic mice (Fig. 6A–H; Fig. S6A–H). Immunofluorescence staining analyses revealed that the positive areas of M1 markers (CD86 and iNOS) and M2 markers (CD206 and Arg1) were significantly decreased in the livers of the sh-NPY1R and sh-NPY2R groups compared with the sh-NC control group (Fig. 6I–M; Fig. S6I–M). H&E staining revealed severe hepatocellular degeneration, necrosis, and extensive inflammatory infiltration in the sh-NC fibrotic mice. In contrast, mice in the sh-NPY1R and sh-NPY2R groups exhibited remarkably attenuated hepatic injury and reduced inflammatory cell accumulation (Fig. 6N; Fig. S6N). Ishak scoring further demonstrated that NPY1R- and NPY2R-knockdown mice had significantly lower hepatic injury scores than sh-NC controls (Fig. 6N–O; Fig. S6N–O). Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated that in vivo knockdown of either NPY1R or NPY2R significantly reduced collagen deposition in fibrotic liver tissues (Fig. 6N, P; Fig. S6N, P). Consistent with the histological improvements, immunohistochemical staining revealed marked reductions in fibrotic markers. The α-SMA- and Col1a1-positive areas were significantly smaller in the sh-NPY1R and sh-NPY2R groups than in the sh-NC group (Fig. 6N, Q, R; Fig. S6N, Q, R). Taken together, these results indicated that targeted knockdown of NPY1R or NPY2R in hepatic macrophages effectively suppressed M1/M2 polarization and alleviated the progression of hepatic fibrosis.

Distribution of NPY1R and NPY2R in M1/M2 macrophages and key signaling pathways in NPY-mediated neuroimmune dysregulation

To elucidate the roles of NPY1R and NPY2R in M1 and M2 polarization of macrophages and potential signaling pathways, we first analyzed the distribution of NPY1R and NPY2R in M1 and M2 macrophages via double immunofluorescence staining. Both receptors were abundantly expressed in CD86⁺ M1 and CD206⁺ M2 macrophages (Fig. 7A–D; Fig. S7A–D). This suggests that NPY1R and NPY2R simultaneously mediated M1 and M2 macrophage polarization during hepatic fibrosis. To further identify potential signaling pathways regulated by NPY, hepatic non-parenchymal cells were isolated from WT and NPY-sKO fibrotic mice for RNA sequencing. In CCl₄-induced fibrotic livers, a total of 744 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened in NPY-sKO mice compared with WT counterparts, including 337 upregulated and 407 downregulated genes (Fig. 7E–G). In the DDC-induced fibrosis model, 262 DEGs were screened, comprising 150 upregulated and 112 downregulated genes between NPY-sKO and WT fibrotic mice (Fig. S7E–G). GO enrichment analysis showed that DEGs derived from the CCl₄ model were primarily enriched in biological processes closely associated with immune receptor activity, inflammatory response, cellular response to cytokine stimulus, cytokine receptor activity, and extracellular matrix (Fig. 7H). In parallel, analysis of the DDC model revealed that the most significantly enriched GO terms were related to regulation of developmental process, extracellular matrix, regulation of cell differentiation, and responses to growth factors (Fig. S7H). KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated that DEGs from both CCl₄ and DDC fibrosis models were significantly enriched in immune- and inflammation-related signaling pathways, with prominent enrichment in the PI3K and MAPK signaling cascades (Fig. 7I; Fig. S7I). Collectively, these transcriptomic results suggested that the PI3K and MAPK signaling pathways were critical downstream mechanisms by which NPY modulated macrophage polarization, thereby mediating neuroimmune dysregulation in fibrotic livers.

NPY promotes macrophage M1 polarization via PI3K–JNK signaling and M2 polarization via PI3K–ERK signaling

We first examined the effects of NPY on macrophage polarization and downstream signaling. Polarization-related genes were profiled by qRT-PCR in PBS- or NPY-treated M0, M1, and M2 macrophages. NPY markedly elevated mRNA levels of M1 signature genes (iNOS, IL-1β, TNF-α) in M1 macrophages and robustly upregulated M2 marker transcripts (Arg1, TGF-β1, CD163) in M2 macrophages. In contrast, NPY did not alter the transcript levels of these genes within resting M0 macrophages and failed to induce reciprocal phenotypic switching between M1 and M2 subsets (Fig. 8A). To define the receptor subtypes underlying NPY-mediated macrophage polarization, we used selective antagonists against NPY1R (BIBP3226) and NPY2R (BIIE0246). In M1 macrophages, NPY increased the CD86 protein and PI3K/JNK phosphorylation (p-PI3K/PI3K, p-JNK/JNK) without altering total PI3K or JNK1/2 levels (Fig. 8B–G). ELISA and qRT-PCR confirmed that NPY stimulation upregulated the secretion of IL-1β and TNF-α and elevated mRNA levels of M1 signature genes (Fig. 8H–I, R–T). Co-treatment with either BIBP3226 or BIIE0246 fully abolished NPY-induced effects. Both antagonists blocked CD86 upregulation, PI3K/JNK phosphorylation, pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and transcriptional induction of iNOS, IL-1β, and TNF-α (Fig. 8B–I, R–T). In M2 macrophages, NPY upregulated the CD206 protein level and increased PI3K and ERK phosphorylation (p-PI3K/PI3K, p-ERK/ERK) without altering total PI3K or ERK1/2 protein levels (Fig. 8J–O). NPY also enhanced the secretion of TGF-β and PDGF-BB and elevated the mRNA expression of M2 signature genes (Fig. 8P–Q, U–W). Blockade of NPY1R or NPY2R abrogated all NPY-triggered M2 phenotypic alterations, including CD206 upregulation, kinase phosphorylation, pro-fibrotic factor secretion, and transcription of Arg1, TGF-β1, and CD163 (Fig. 8J–Q, U–W).

We next dissected the PI3K-dependent downstream signaling cascades using small-molecule inhibitors targeting PI3K (LY294002), PAK1 (IPA-3), JNK (SP600125, M1-specific), and ERK (SCH772984, M2-specific). In M1 macrophages, NPY elevated CD86 protein expression and increased the phosphorylation of PAK1 and c-Jun without changing total PAK1 abundance. LY294002 or IPA-3 completely suppressed NPY-triggered PAK1-c-Jun activation and CD86 upregulation. By contrast, SP600125 blocked c-Jun phosphorylation and CD86 induction without affecting p-PAK1 levels. Total PAK1 protein levels remained comparable across all M1 treatment groups (Fig. S8A–F). All three inhibitors abolished NPY-stimulated IL-1β/TNF-α secretion and the transcriptional upregulation of M1 marker genes (Fig. S8G–H, Q–S). In M2 macrophages, NPY upregulated CD206 and enhanced PAK1 and RSK phosphorylation without altering total PAK1 and RSK protein levels. LY294002 or IPA-3 eliminated NPY-dependent PAK1 and RSK activation and CD206 elevation, whereas SCH772984 suppressed RSK phosphorylation and CD206 upregulation without interfering with p-PAK1 accumulation. Total PAK1 and RSK protein levels did not differ significantly among M2 groups (Fig. S8I–N). All inhibitors abolished NPY-induced TGF-β/PDGF-BB secretion and the transcriptional activation of M2 signature genes (Fig. S8O–P, T–V). Collectively, these pharmacological data support the notion of a signaling axis whereby NPY exerted divergent effects on macrophage polarization through distinct PI3K–PAK1 branches: amplification of pro-inflammatory M1 polarization via the PI3K–PAK1–c-Jun cascade and promotion of pro-fibrotic M2 polarization through the PI3K–PAK1–RSK pathway.

Discussion

In the present study, we identified a novel sympathetic nerve-derived NPY-centered neuroimmune regulatory axis involved in hepatic fibrogenesis. By integrating clinical specimen validation, in vivo animal modeling, and in vitro molecular mechanistic experiments, we revealed that intrahepatic sympathetic nerve-derived NPY remodeled the hepatic immune microenvironment by modulating macrophage polarization. This study established a previously unreported neuroimmune crosstalk mechanism underlying hepatic fibrogenesis and provided novel insights into the complex pathophysiological processes of chronic liver fibrosis.

The hepatic autonomic nervous system plays a central role in neuroimmune crosstalk, and is essential for maintaining homeostasis of the hepatic immune microenvironment [26]. Intrahepatic autonomic nerves comprise sympathetic and parasympathetic fibers. Sympathetic fibers originate from the celiac ganglia and distribute predominantly within the portal tracts and perisinusoidal spaces. Parasympathetic fibers derive from the vagus nerve and localize around the portal tracts and bile ducts [27–30]. By releasing neurotransmitters such as norepinephrine and acetylcholine, these fibers regulate hepatic blood flow, metabolism, and bile secretion [10, 27, 31]. They also interact with multiple hepatic immune cells, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and T lymphocytes [10]. Sympathetic activation is generally considered pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic, as sympathetic ablation or adrenergic receptor inhibition alleviates inflammatory liver injury and fibrogenesis [32–34]. In contrast, the vagus nerve suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokine production in Kupffer cells via the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR) [35, 36]. We observed a marked increase in sympathetic fiber density within fibrous septa and portal tracts, with NPY⁺ fibers showing the most prominent expansion, consistent with the findings of Miyazawa et al. [20]. However, categorizing the sympathetic system as uniformly pro-fibrotic and the parasympathetic system as anti-fibrotic is an oversimplification. Autonomic fibers secrete diverse neurotransmitters that act on distinct receptors across multiple intrahepatic cell subsets [37]. Sympathetic nerves can also restrain hepatic inflammation and fibrosis. Norepinephrine limits the activation of pro-inflammatory macrophages via the β₂ adrenergic receptor (ADRB2) [38]. Sympathetic nerve-derived vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGF-C) promotes hepatic lymphangiogenesis, thereby exerting anti-fibrotic effects [39]. Our data add a further layer to this complexity, showing that NPY⁺ sympathetic fibers drive macrophage M1/M2 polarization through NPY1R and NPY2R and thereby promote hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis. Defining the precise nerve fiber subsets, neurotransmitters, receptors, and target cells involved in this crosstalk will facilitate the development of targeted anti-fibrotic agents.

At the molecular level, NPY engaged distinct downstream signaling pathways depending on the polarization state of macrophages. NPY alone failed to polarize resting M0 macrophages. Instead, it selectively amplified ongoing polarization programs—enhancing M1 polarization via the PI3K–PAK1–c-Jun cascade and M2 polarization via the PI3K–PAK1–RSK pathway—without inducing M1-to-M2 or M2-to-M1 conversion. A similar state-dependent effect of NPY on macrophage function has been reported by Dimitrijevic et al. [40]. This biphasic pattern may arise from conformational plasticity of NPY receptors, which can remodel upon ligand binding in response to microenvironmental cues [41–43], or from differences in receptor post-translational modifications across polarization states. Resolving receptor–ligand complex structures by cryo-electron microscopy and monitoring receptor dynamics with live-cell FRET/BRET biosensors may help clarify these mechanisms. It should be noted that our conclusions are primarily derived from pharmacological experiments—in which small‑molecule inhibitors are prone to off‑target effects—and that these findings lack genetic validation. Therefore, future genetic studies (e.g., siRNA‑ or CRISPR‑mediated silencing of pathway components) will further consolidate our inferences regarding these signaling pathways. It should also be acknowledged that the M1/M2 paradigm, although experimentally useful, oversimplifies the heterogeneity of hepatic macrophages in fibrotic liver, where macrophage activation states exist along a continuum and individual cells may co-express markers of both phenotypes. The M1 and M2 definitions used here should therefore be regarded as operational classifications, because of its experimental tractability and wide recognition, but it does not capture the full spectrum of macrophage heterogeneity. Beyond macrophages, NPY may also act directly on HSCs, which express NPY receptors and respond to NPY stimulation [34, 44]. In this study, NPY enhanced the secretion of TNF-α and IL-1β from M1 macrophages and of TGF-β and PDGF-BB from M2 macrophages. All of these cytokines potently activate HSCs and stimulate ECM synthesis [45–47]. These findings delineate an NPY-driven neuroimmune–stromal cascade in which NPY⁺ sympathetic fibers polarize macrophages to release pro-fibrotic mediators that secondarily activate HSCs. Because activated HSCs can in turn modulate hepatic macrophages [48, 49], a reciprocal NPY–macrophage–HSC loop may further amplify fibrogenesis. Dissecting these multicellular interactions will be a major focus of our future studies.

Although our data demonstrated that NPY promotes macrophage polarization in liver fibrosis, the immunomodulatory effects of NPY vary considerably across diseases [50, 51]. This variability can be attributed to at least three factors. First, NPY receptor subtypes are expressed in a tissue-specific manner [52]. Hepatic macrophages mainly express NPY1R and NPY2R, with no detectable NPY4R or NPY5R, whereas cardiac macrophages predominantly express NPY1R in acute myocardial infarction [50]. Such receptor diversity may directly determine the functional outcome of NPY signaling. Second, disease microenvironments differ in their inflammatory and cytokine profiles, which may synergize with or antagonize NPY signaling. For example, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) elevates NPY expression in adrenal and celiac ganglion neurons [53], and TNF-α and IL-1β upregulate NPY1R in periodontal ligament stem cells [54]. These findings indicate that inflammatory cues modulate NPY receptor availability. Third, macrophages from different tissues display distinct phenotypes and NPY responsiveness. NPY1R activation induces both M1 and M2 polarization in white adipose tissue [55], consistent with our findings, but suppresses M1 while promoting M2 polarization in acute myocardial infarction [50]. Therefore, future clinical translation requires the development of personalized therapeutic regimens tailored to distinct disease types and pathological stages.

The mode of NPY intervention also deserves careful consideration. NPY is a core neurotransmitter that regulates appetite, metabolism, cardiovascular activity, mood, and pain sensation [16, 17]. Systemic NPY1R/NPY2R blockade would thus carry substantial risks. NPY1R knockout or antagonism induces anorexia and anxiety in animals, and NPY2R inhibition alters food intake and emotional behavior [52, 56]. Moreover, NPY1R/NPY2R antagonists may reverse NPY-mediated vasoconstriction and provoke hypotension [52]. By contrast, selective inhibition of NPY1R/NPY2R on hepatic macrophages represents a more feasible translational strategy. This approach directly targets the pathogenic cell population identified here while sparing the physiological functions of central and peripheral NPY. The anti-fibrotic efficacy of our F4/80 promoter-driven AAV8-shRNA knockdown of macrophage NPY1R/NPY2R provides in vivo proof-of-concept for such a cell-targeted approach, although the macrophage specificity of this viral strategy was inferred from the F4/80 promoter design and prior validation [24] rather than directly demonstrated across all hepatic cell types in the present study. Clinically, liver-homing nanocarriers or macrophage-directed, ligand-conjugated antagonists could achieve similar selectivity, and biased ligands that suppress intrahepatic NPY signaling while preserving central NPY functions offer another alternative. Future studies should compare the delivery efficiency, durability, and safety of these macrophage-targeted strategies with those of systemic NPY blockade.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the clinical cohort was small (n = 6 per group) and restricted to viral cirrhosis. Because hepatic neuroimmune landscapes may differ across etiologies, our observations require validation in larger multicenter cohorts covering alcoholic, biliary, and NASH-associated cirrhosis. Accordingly, conclusions drawn from the human specimens should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Second, NPY is highly expressed in the brain and other tissues [12–14], so global NPY knockout mice cannot distinguish liver-specific NPY effects from systemic NPY loss. Although F4/80 promoter-driven AAV8-shRNA knockdown of macrophage NPY1R/NPY2R provided complementary validation, macrophage- and neuron-specific conditional knockout models would offer more definitive evidence. In addition, the macrophage specificity of the F4/80 promoter-driven AAV8 approach was not directly validated across non-macrophage hepatic cell populations in this study, and residual transgene expression in other liver cell types cannot be fully excluded. Third, only CCl₄-induced toxic and DDC-induced cholestatic fibrosis models were examined. Alcohol-associated liver injury and HFD-induced NASH models are needed to verify the broader applicability of our findings. Fourth, this study focused on macrophages; whether NPY also regulates HSCs, hepatocytes, and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells remains to be determined. Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics will help resolve macrophage heterogeneity upon NPY stimulation and map the spatial distribution of NPY and its receptors within the fibrotic liver.

Conclusion

Collectively, intrahepatic sympathetic nerve-derived NPY exacerbates hepatic fibrogenesis by driving M1 polarization via NPY1R/NPY2R–PI3K–PAK1–c-Jun signaling and M2 polarization via the NPY1R/NPY2R–PI3K–PAK1–RSK cascade. The present study expands mechanistic insights into neuroimmune regulation of chronic liver injury and identifies macrophage NPY1R/NPY2R as candidate therapeutic targets for liver fibrosis. Given the potential metabolic and cardiovascular side effects induced by systemic NPY receptor inhibition, future translational studies should prioritize liver- or macrophage-targeted delivery strategies to reduce off-target toxicities and improve the safety of NPY-based anti-fibrotic interventions.
