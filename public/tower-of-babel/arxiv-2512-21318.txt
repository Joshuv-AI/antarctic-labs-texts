Independence Complexes of Hexagonal Grid Graphs
Himanshu Chandrakar, Anurag Singh

Abstract
The independence complex of a graph is a simplicial complex whose faces correspond to the independent sets of G G . While independence complexes have been studied extensively for many graph classes, including square grid graphs, relatively little is known about planar hexagonal grid graphs. In this article, we study the topology of the independence complexes of hexagonal grid graphs H 1 × m × n H_{1\times m\times n} . For m = 1 , 2 , 3 m=1,2,3 and n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 , we determine their homotopy types. In particular, we show that the independence complex of the hexagonal line tiling H 1 × 1 × n H_{1\times 1\times n} is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of two n n -spheres, and m = 2 m=2 and m = 3 m=3 we obtain recursive descriptions that completely determine the spheres appearing in the homotopy type. Our proofs rely on link and deletion operations, the fold lemma, and a detailed analysis of induced subgraphs.

Introduction

In topological combinatorics, independence complexes constitute a central and well-studied class of graph-based simplicial complexes. For a graph
G
, the independence complex
\operatorname{Ind}\left(G\right)
is the simplicial complex with vertex set
V\left(G\right)
, where a subset
\sigma\subseteq V\left(G\right)
is a face of
\operatorname{Ind}\left(G\right)
if and only if
\sigma
is an independent set in
G
. Despite this elementary definition, independence complexes often exhibit remarkably rich and subtle topological behaviour. Their significance is further highlighted by their close connection with matching complexes: the matching complex of a graph can be realized as the independence complex of its line graph. Consequently, many developments in the study of matching complexes draw heavily on results and techniques originating from the theory of independence complexes (see, for instance,
[
4
,
16
]
).

Over the years, numerous authors have contributed to the study of independence complexes for a wide variety of graph families. Notable examples include powers of cycles
[
1
]
, ternary graphs
[
11
,
19
]
, generalized Mycielskian of complete graphs and categorical products of complete graphs
[
17
]
, forests and claw-free graphs
[
5
,
10
]
, as well as directed trees and related graph families
[
6
,
12
]
. Alongside these results, several powerful methods have been developed to analyze the topology of independence complexes, including the matching tree algorithm, the star cluster method, the fold lemma, and the systematic use of link and deletion operations (see, for instance,
[
2
,
3
,
6
,
10
,
14
,
15
]
). Furthermore, in
[
8
]
, Jonsson studies complexes of forests in a matroid-theoretic framework, leading to generalizations of independence complexes of matroids. Additionally, his work serves as a systematic reference for a wide range of simplicial complexes arising from graphs.

Among graph families with an underlying geometric structure, polygonal grid graphs have played a particularly important role. The independence complexes of square grid graphs have been studied extensively, for example by Bousquet-Mèlou et al.
[
3
]
, Benjamin et al.
[
4
]
, and Matsushita et al.
[
14
,
15
]
, leading to detailed descriptions of their homotopy types in several cases.

In contrast, comparatively little is known about the independence complexes of planar hexagonal grid graphs. The hexagonal tiling has a fundamentally different local structure from the square grid, with lower vertex degrees and distinct adjacency patterns, which give rise to new combinatorial and topological phenomena. Early work in this direction can be traced back to Thapper
[
18
]
, who studied the independence complexes of square and hexagonal grids in cylindrical settings using the matching tree algorithm. Subsequently, Jonsson
[
9
]
investigated the occurrence of special homology classes, known as cross cycles, in the independence complexes of periodic grids formed from squares, triangles, and hexagons.

While these works provide valuable insight into independence complexes of periodic and cylindrical grids, the corresponding complexes for
planar
hexagonal grid graphs remain largely unexplored. In this article, we initiate a systematic study of this problem by determining the homotopy type of the independence complex of

2.1
Basics of graph theory

A
graph

G
is an ordered pair
\left(V(G),E(G)\right)
, where
V(G)
is the set of vertices and

is the set of edges. We define the

of the vertex

in

, denoted by

as,

N_{G}\left(v\right)=\left\{u\in V(G)\ \middle|\ u\neq v\ \text{and}\ \left\{u,v\right\}\in E\left(G\right)\right\}.

Similarly, the
closed neighborhood
of the vertex
v
in
G
, denoted by
N_{G}[v]
is defined as,

N_{G}[v]=N_{G}(v)\sqcup\left\{v\right\}.

When the underlying graph is clear from the context, we omit the subscript and write
N(v)
and

to denote the open and closed neighborhoods of

Let
S
be a subset of
V(G)
. The
induced subgraph
of
G
on
S
, denoted by
G\left[S\right]
, is the graph whose vertex set is
S
and whose edge set consists of all edges of
G
that have both vertices in
S
. We denote the induced
subgraph

by
G\setminus H
, where
H\subset V\left(G\right)
. We now proceed to formally define the hexagonal grid graphs.

2.3
Topological preliminaries and tools for independence complexes

We follow Hatcher’s
Algebraic Topology

[
7
]
and Kozlov’s
Combinatorial Algebraic Topology

[
13
]
as our primary references for standard definitions and terminology.

A
simplicial complex

\mathcal{K}
is a non-empty family of subsets of a finite set
V
, referred to as the vertex set, with the property that it is closed under taking subsets, that is, for every
\tau\in\mathcal{K}
and
\sigma\subset\tau
, we have
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}
. We call an element
\sigma\in K
a
face
of
K
. A face consisting of
k+1
vertices is called a
k
-dimensional face, and the maximum dimension among all faces is called the dimension of the simplicial complex. The maximal faces of a simplicial complex, that is, those faces not properly contained in any other face, are called
facets
. A subset
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{K}
is called a
subcomplex
of
\mathcal{K}
if
\mathcal{L}
itself is a simplicial complex and every face of
\mathcal{L}
is also a face of
\mathcal{K}
. We now discuss some important subcomplexes associated with a given simplicial complex, which will be used extensively throughout this article. The link and deletion of a face in a simplicial complex are defined as follows.

Definition 2.1

(
[
13
, Definitions 2.12 and 2.13]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a simplicial complex, and
\sigma
be a face of
\mathcal{K}
. Then,

1.

The
link
of
\sigma
in
\mathcal{K}
, denoted by
\mathrm{lk}_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\sigma\right)
,

\mathrm{lk}_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\sigma\right)=\left\{\tau\in\mathcal{K}\ \middle|\ \sigma\cap\tau=\emptyset,\ \sigma\cup\tau\in\mathcal{K}\right\}.

2.

The
deletion
of
\sigma
in
\mathcal{K}
, denoted by
\mathrm{del}_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\sigma\right)
, is

\mathrm{del}_{\mathcal{K}}\left(\sigma\right)=\left\{\tau\in\mathcal{K}\ \middle|\ \sigma\nsubseteq\tau\right\}.

Observe that when
K
is the independence complex of a graph
G
, that is,
K=\operatorname{Ind}(G)
, the link and deletion of a vertex
v\in V(G)
admit particularly simple descriptions. Specifically,

\displaystyle\mathrm{lk}_{\operatorname{Ind}(G)}(v)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ind}\bigl(G\setminus N_{G}[v]\bigr);

\displaystyle\mathrm{del}_{\operatorname{Ind}(G)}(v)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Ind}\bigl(G\setminus\{v\}\bigr).

These identities follow directly from the definitions of the link and deletion of a vertex in a simplicial complex, as well as those of the independence complex. Henceforth,
\operatorname{Ind}\left(G\setminus N_{G}\left[v\right]\right)
and
\operatorname{Ind}\left(G\setminus\left\{v\right\}\right)
denote the link and deletion of
v
in
\operatorname{Ind}(G)
, respectively. With this notation in place, we state the following lemma, which will be used extensively in what follows.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
1
, Proposition 3.1]
)
.

Let
v
be a vertex of
G
. If the inclusion map

\operatorname{Ind}\left(G\setminus N_{G}\left[v\right]\right)\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Ind}\left(G\setminus\left\{v\right\}\right)

is null-homotopic, then

\operatorname{Ind}\left(G\right)\simeq\operatorname{Ind}\left(G\setminus\left\{v\right\}\right)\vee\Sigma\left(\operatorname{Ind}\left(G\setminus N_{G}\left[v\right]\right)\right).

The key aspect of
Lemma

2.1
lies in the requirement that the inclusion map be null-homotopic for the stated homotopy equivalence to hold. In this article, we employ two methods to establish the null-homotopy of the relevant inclusion maps.

The first method is based on comparing the dimensions of the link and the deletion of a chosen vertex. More precisely, if the spheres appearing in the homotopy type of the deletion have strictly larger dimensions than those appearing in the homotopy type of the link, then the inclusion map is null-homotopic.

The second method relies on a basic result from homotopy theory, which says that any continuous map
f\colon Y\to X
that factors through a contractible space is homotopic to a constant map, and hence is null-homotopic. A general statement of this fact appears as a problem in Hatcher’s book (see
[
7
, Problem 0.10]
). Precisely, we state the following lemma, which we will use to establish null-homotopy in such situations.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
j\colon Y\hookrightarrow Z
and
i\colon Z\hookrightarrow X
be inclusion maps. If
Z
is contractible, then the composite inclusion
i\circ j\colon Y\hookrightarrow X
is null-homotopic.

We say that a vertex
w
is
folded
using a vertex
v
in a graph
G
if
N(v)\subseteq N(w)
. The following lemma, commonly known as the
fold lemma
for independence complexes, asserts that under this condition, the removal of a vertex from the graph preserves the homotopy type of the associated independence complex.

Lemma 2.3
.

(
[
5
, Lemma 2.4]
)
For two distinct vertices
v
and
w
of a graph
G
with
N(v)\subseteq N(w)
,
\operatorname{Ind}(G)
collapses onto
\operatorname{Ind}\left(G\setminus\left\{w\right\}\right)
, that is,

\operatorname{Ind}(G)\simeq\operatorname{Ind}(G\setminus w).

The following lemma describes the independence complex of a graph that can be expressed as the disjoint union of two graphs.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
2
, Remark 2.2]
)
.

If a graph
G
is the disjoint union of two graphs
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, then the independence complex of
G
is homotopic to the join of the independence complexes of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, that is,

\operatorname{Ind}(G)\simeq\operatorname{Ind}(H_{1})*\operatorname{Ind}(H_{2}).

In particular, if
H_{1}
consists of a single vertex, then
\operatorname{Ind}(G)
is a cone over
\operatorname{Ind}(H_{2})
, and hence contractible. Similarly, if
H_{1}
is the path graph on two vertices, that is,
P_{2}
, then
\operatorname{Ind}(G)\simeq\Sigma\operatorname{Ind}(H_{2})
.

Along with these results, we also need the homotopy type of the independence complexes of paths and cycles, which will be used extensively throughout this article.
