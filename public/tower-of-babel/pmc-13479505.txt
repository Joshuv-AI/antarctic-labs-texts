Multicentre randomised comparative effectiveness trial of cleavage-stage versus blastocyst-stage embryo transfer in patients with few embryos (PRECISE): implementation of a study protocol in reproductive medicine

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith improvements in in vitro culture techniques, there has been a steady shift in practice to transfer in vitro fertilisation (IVF) embryos at the blastocyst-stage to increase pregnancy rates per embryo transfer (ET) and reduce the risk of multiple pregnancy. In patients with fewer embryos (≤5 zygotes), fresh cleavage-stage ET is still commonly performed to reduce the risk of cycle cancellation if no embryo survives to the blastocyst-stage in vitro. However, evidence for improved outcomes with cleavage-stage embryo transfer in this subgroup of patients is lacking. We will compare blastocyst-stage to cleavage-stage ET with regard to cumulative live birth rates (CLBR) per oocyte retrieval and other patient-centred cycle outcomes in patients with ≤5 zygotes.Methods and analysisMulticentre, randomised pragmatic comparative effectiveness trial with superiority design comparing blastocyst-stage to cleavage-stage ET in patients with ≤5 zygotes. This trial will enrol and randomise 1126 women aged 18–44 undergoing IVF to either fresh cleavage-stage or blastocyst-stage ET at eleven IVF centres. Surplus embryos will be vitrified at the blastocyst-stage. The primary outcome is the CLBR per oocyte retrieval. Secondary outcomes include: multiple pregnancy, pregnancy loss <20 weeks and cycle cancellation. Exploratory outcomes include: live birth per fresh ET, ectopic pregnancy, time to pregnancy, stillbirth, preterm birth, financial burden, patient distress and facilitators and barriers for patient involvement and implementation. All primary analyses will be conducted according to the intent-to-treat principle. We also will report per-protocol analyses as a sensitivity analysis. A multidisciplinary stakeholder team, including a 5-patient stakeholder panel, will participate in study design, conduct and oversight.Ethics and disseminationThe Institutional Review Board at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) provides central regulatory oversight. Results will inform timing of embryo transfer in patients with ≤5 zygotes and will be disseminated through academic publications, meeting presentations and communications to advocacy groups and patient participants.Trial registration numberNCT06746129.

Introduction

An infertility diagnosis is a major life event that results in a wide range of socio-cultural, emotional, physical and financial problems1 2 and can have significant detrimental effects on quality of life.3–5 In the USA, 13.4% of women and 15.8% of married men are infertile and 12.2% have used infertility services.6 7 Since the first in vitro fertilisation (IVF) pregnancy in 1978, IVF has evolved into the most effective treatment for all types of infertility. IVF patients with ≤5 embryos on day 1 of development (zygotes) often have a poorer prognosis and are excluded from clinical trials, resulting in a lack of high-quality data on how to best treat these patients. Often, poor oocyte/embryo yield is associated with poor quality of embryos due to abnormal chromosome content (aneuploidy) or other factors (eg, gene regulatory, epigenetic) resulting in low live birth rates and a high risk of cycle cancellation. Cycle cancellation is associated with significant distress and may result in discontinuation of treatment in up to two-thirds of IVF patients.8 There also is concern that in patients with few embryos, the developmental potential of poor quality embryos (due to factors other than aneuploidy) may be particularly sensitive to the developmental niche/laboratory culture conditions the embryo encounters, which are affected by the timing of embryo transfer (ET).

Embryos created through IVF generally are transferred into the uterus at either the cleavage-stage (post-fertilisation day (p.f.d.) 3) or blastocyst-stage (p.f.d. 5–7). Early in the history of IVF, most clinicians transferred cleavage-stage embryos because embryos often did not develop to the blastocyst-stage in vitro given the available culture media and techniques at that time.9 Currently, with improvements in laboratory techniques, blastocyst ET, which mirrors when embryos reach the endometrial cavity during natural conception, has become increasingly common. More than 200 000 embryo transfers are performed every year in the USA which included 19 788 cleavage-stage and 181 482 blastocyst-stage embryo transfers in 2021. For patients with >5 zygotes on day 1 of embryo development, fresh blastocyst ET increases live birth rates per ET, allowing for transfer of fewer embryos and thereby reducing the risk for multiple pregnancies when compared with cleavage-stage ET. However, it remains a critical unanswered question whether embryos that did not develop into a blastocyst in vitro would have resulted in a pregnancy if transferred into the uterus at the cleavage-stage.10 This concern is particularly prevalent if a cycle yields only few developing embryos, which may result in cycle cancellation if none of the embryos reaches the blastocyst-stage. Even in unselected IVF populations, only 50%–60% of zygotes will reach the blastocyst-stage.

Overall, there are evidence-based arguments supporting each day of ET. Culture of embryos to p.f.d. 5–7 results in self-selection of embryos and provides the opportunity to choose the most viable embryo(s), thereby decreasing the total number of embryos transferred and the number of ET procedures per IVF cycle11 as well as time to pregnancy and cost. ET cancellation rates, due to lack of an embryo to transfer, have been shown to be higher for blastocyst ET, which is consistent with self-selection.12 Thus, it remains conceivable that culture to the blastocyst-stage results in the loss of a subset of embryos that may have survived in vivo. On the other hand, because human embryos reach the uterine cavity on p.f.d. 4–5, the endometrium may not provide the appropriate developmental environment for cleavage-stage embryos.12 Thus, while cleavage-stage ET could benefit some patients with few embryos, it also may be associated with adverse outcomes or a lower chance of pregnancy. In many cases, a single blastocyst can be transferred (single embryo transfer) without lowering the pregnancy rate per ET. As a consequence, the likelihood of multi-fetal gestation and its associated maternal and neonatal complications is reduced.13 However, it is unclear whether this benefit extends to patients with few embryos who are often older and more likely to have high rates of embryo aneuploidy, effectively lowering the risk of multiple pregnancies. In addition, several meta-analyses suggest a higher incidence of preterm births and large-for-gestational-age babies with blastocyst ET.14–16

Previous randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing pregnancy outcomes for cleavage-stage vs blastocyst ET have almost exclusively focused on the general IVF population and not patients with few embryos and thus poorer prognosis.12 17–22 In their systematic review, Glujovsky et al did not detect a difference in the cumulative live birth rate (CLBR) per oocyte retrieval (OR) between blastocyst-stage and cleavage-stage ET in unselected patients.12 However, there was significant heterogeneity and risk of attrition bias among the RCTs. Despite these caveats, there is now a general consensus that, for good prognosis patients with >5 zygotes, it is beneficial to transfer blastocyst-stage rather than cleavage-stage embryos to reduce the rate of multi-fetal pregnancies and associated complications; however, this question remains unanswered for patients with ≤5 zygotes.

To inform the design of the current RCT, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to compare cycle outcomes after blastocyst-stage vs cleavage-stage ET in 3844 patients who underwent IVF at Boston IVF in 2014–2022 and had 5 or fewer zygotes. To reduce bias, we performed propensity score matching (PSM) and modified Poisson regression analysis to adjust for potential confounders related to pregnancy outcomes (age at retrieval, number of retrieved oocytes, number of previous IVF cycles, patient Body Mass Index, gravidity, parity, estradiol level at time of trigger and cycle year). This analysis yielded a CLBR per OR of 35.8% for blastocyst and 22.1% for cleavage-stage ET (table 1). However, this analysis is likely prone to residual confounding by indication because patients with a poor prognosis are more likely to receive a cleavage-stage ET to avoid cycle cancellation. Thus, new studies reporting live birth per retrieval, as well as other adverse outcomes, are urgently needed, particularly in patients with few embryos.23

The PRECISE (PooR Embryo Yield Cleavage Stage vs blaStocyst Embryo Transfer) trial ultimately aims to answer, from a clinical perspective, whether the intra-uterine or in vitro environment is superior in maximising the developmental potential of cleavage-stage embryos. We hypothesise that, in patients with ≤5 zygotes, fresh blastocyst-stage ET is superior to fresh cleavage-stage ET with regard to the primary outcome of CLBR per OR. The rationale for this hypothesis is based on (1) our own PSM analysis of outcomes in patients with ≤5 zygotes (table 1) and (2) the premise that any beneficial interactions between cleavage-stage embryos and the reproductive tract would normally occur in the Fallopian tube during natural conception and not the uterus. This is a pragmatic trial and surplus embryos will be vitrified at the blastocyst-stage in both treatment arms.

Methods and analysis

Study setting and site selection

This RCT will be conducted within a network of 11 infertility centres in the USA performing ~17 000 IVF cycles per year (see ‘Study Sites’). The criteria for choosing study sites were: (1) clinician buy-in: demonstrated by prior participation in joint research studies (2) institutional experience with RCTs: demonstrated by site PI with previous RCT experience as PI or site-PI of at least one RCT (3) expected number of eligible patients: estimated using historic data of patients fulfilling the inclusion/exclusion criteria at each site (4) study population diversity: inclusion of some sites with high proportions of patients from ethnic minorities and low socio-economic status (UCSF, CNY Fertility). The Clinical Coordinating Center (CCC) for this trial is based at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC). The Data Coordinating Center (DCC) resides at Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) Biostatistics. An executive committee (EC) oversees day-to-day study operations. A stakeholder advisory group (SAG) and steering committee (SC) meet monthly and an independent data and safety monitoring board (DSMB) meets semi-annually.

Eligibility

Women are eligible if they are aged 18–44 years and undergo an autologous IVF cycle with ≤5 zygotes on day 1 of development and no more than two previous IVF cycles. Potential participants are not eligible if they plan preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) on all embryos, have a history of recurrent pregnancy loss (≥3), have a Body Mass Index >40 kg/m2, have uterine abnormalities (such as congenital malformations, polyps and submucosal fibroids), or plan the use of a gestational carrier. In addition, participants will be withdrawn from the study after consent and before randomisation if they meet any of the additional exclusion criteria (eg, elevated progesterone ≥1.5 ng/mL, delayed fertilisation) in table 2 during their IVF cycle.

Patient screening and consent

The intervention for this RCT (blastocyst-stage or cleavage-stage ET) is delivered 2 or 4 days after patients with ≤5 embryos are confirmed to be eligible for randomisation on day 1 after fertilisation. Because of the short timeframe between eligibility confirmation and the intervention, many candidate participants will be consented ahead of time. Approximately 15%–20% of patients undergoing IVF will have ≤5 zygotes, though this can be higher or lower depending on the IVF programme. To optimise identification of eligible patients, we will employ a multilayer screening approach: All patients scheduled for a pre-IVF visit will be screened for eligibility by the study staff. To conserve resources, we will consent patients with a higher likelihood of having ≤5 zygotes based on a validated predictor of oocyte yield (anti-Muellerian hormone, AMH).24 25 Using Boston IVF data, we have identified a cut-off value for AMH of ≤4.9 ng/mL, which identify patients with ≤5 zygotes with 95% sensitivity and 12%–25% specificity (depending on age). Further screening of patients will occur at cycle start and during stimulation in patients with total estradiol ≤400 pg/mL on day 5 of stimulation or ≤8 developing follicles on the day of trigger. Participants must meet eligibility criteria, provide informed consent and agree to be randomised. We will use virtual remote consenting and educational materials developed with input from patient stakeholders. A patient is considered enrolled and eligible for randomisation after the institutional consent form is signed and all eligibility criteria are met.

Interventions

We are comparing two evidence-based IVF interventions, blastocyst-stage and cleavage-stage ET.12 22 Cleavage-stage ET will be the comparator for the study because it is often considered the default treatment in this patient group. The study protocol is shown in figure 1. During the IVF cycle, stimulation protocols, trigger agents and fertilisation (IVF or intracytoplasmic sperm injection) will follow standard clinical practice. On p.f.d. 1, patients with ≤5 zygotes who remain eligible for the study will be randomised to either blastocyst-stage or cleavage-stage fresh ET. Any developing embryos not transferred will be vitrified at the blastocyst-stage. The minimum embryo quality for transfer will be a developmental stage of 1 day behind expected development (morula for blastocyst arm, 2-cell for cleavage-stage arm).26 Following the fresh ET, clinical care will follow standard practice with a pregnancy test on p.f.d. 13. If negative, we will follow the outcomes of all subsequent frozen embryo transfer(s) cycle(s) resulting from this OR until live birth or until all embryos from this cycle have been transferred. We will follow the outcomes of these frozen embryos irrespective of whether a participant opts for another OR prior to transferring all frozen embryos from the study cycle. The number of embryos transferred per ET will follow American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) guidelines. All IVF-related costs including trial interventions will be covered by insurance or patients directly, as per usual clinical practice.

Randomisation and masking

We will randomise participants in a ratio of 1:1 stratified by enrolment site, age (<38 and ≥38 years), by embryo number (1–2 and 3–5 zygotes), and by prior IVF cycles (0 and 1–2) to ensure balance on these key predictors of the primary CLBR per OR outcome. The study flowchart is shown in figure 2. We will use a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-compliant secure web application (REDCap)27 28 to perform random permuted block randomisation. Participants will be informed of the treatment arm allocation on the day of randomisation. Due to the nature of the intervention, neither participants nor clinical staff can be blinded to allocation. The full randomisation table, including upcoming randomisation assignments, will be concealed from all study team members who recruit and enrol patients to ensure allocation concealment during the consenting and screening process.

Stakeholder involvement

A multidisciplinary SAG, including 5 patient stakeholders, participated in the development of the research question and selection of clinical outcomes to ensure relevance to patients. During the trial, the SAG will provide advice on patient recruitment, consent, retention and patient-centred, culturally appropriate study materials for shared decision making. The SAG also will be involved in the analysis, interpretation and dissemination of the study results. The involvement of the SAG will go beyond an advisory capacity as various SAG members will take on active collaborative roles such as developing a lay language patient brochure and patient-centred counselling guidelines for recruitment, eliciting feedback from potential participants and supporting study sites (eg, recruitment training). In addition, SAG members will play a key role as co-investigators on study leadership committees. Patient stakeholders will serve as active members of the study team and function as advisors, collaborators and decision makers. Patient stakeholders will have significant input on study design, scope and implementation strategies, advise on optimal recruitment/retention approaches, and develop educational materials for shared decision making, collaborate with the study team to develop meaningful and culturally appropriate study materials such as consent forms, survey/interview questions and be involved in the decision making as co-investigators on leadership committees. To ensure continued high impact, equitable and meaningful engagement, we will formally evaluate the quality of stakeholder engagement, satisfaction and impact. Semi-annually, we will invite all SAG members to complete a structured survey, which will include two validated measures for stakeholder engagement: the 9-item condensed Research Engagement Survey Tool (REST)29 and the 22-item Patient Engagement in Research Scale (PEIRS-22).30 With the addition of open-ended questions, this survey will address the individual engagement experience of SAG members, group dynamics, equity and inclusiveness of engagement and the degree to which partners’ perspectives are validated and valued, effectiveness of communication, meeting structure, involvement, respect and trust, and SAG areas of interest for the next year. Specifically, the PEIRS-22 covers the context, process and outcome/impact of engagement from a patient partner perspective and shows good internal consistency, floor and ceiling effect, structural and construct validity and reliability. Importantly, this questionnaire exhibits consistency across different demographic groups.30 All respondents who report low engagement quality or quantity as low on the REST survey (defined here as responses of ‘Poor’, ‘Fair’, ‘Never’ or ‘Rarely’ on at least two items) as well as all patient SAG members will be invited to participate in semi-structured interviews yearly. These interviews will be solution-oriented and geared towards identifying ways in which stakeholder engagement can be improved. In addition, SAG members will be reminded at each meeting that they are welcome to provide feedback via email or telephone to the SAG leadership or SAG staff coordinator. The results of the survey and interviews will be used in a feedback loop with the SAG leadership and other SAG members to improve on patient engagement throughout the feasibility phase. The SAG leadership team and SAG staff coordinator will meet at least twice monthly and will report back the results of the semi-annual surveys and qualitative interviews to the SAG during full SAG meetings.

Study assessments and outcomes

The CCC and DCC will develop data collection tools, data dictionaries, data collection instructions and the encrypted REDCap database to ensure standardisation of data management across all study sites and systematic data collection from all study participants. Data for analysis will come both from clinical IVF data and obstetric outcome data routinely collected by the clinical team and entered into patients’ electronic health records (EHR). The timeline for data collection is shown in figure 1. For each patient, a study research assistant will independently re-confirm inclusion and exclusion criteria and will then transfer data without direct identifiers related to recruitment/eligibility, consent, randomisation assignment, IVF cycle and pregnancy outcomes and complications from the electronic health record into the REDCap database at four timepoints throughout the cycle.

For patient-reported outcomes, we will send questionnaires to patients using a secure electronic system. We will collect demographic information and reproductive history from the participant and their partner, if they have one. We also will collect data on family out-of-pocket expenses, time off work, wage losses, need for childcare coverage and travel through serial administration of validated Health Service Utilization and Cost of Care Questionnaires from McMaster University,31 which have previously been used in multi-centre RCTs.32–34

We will assess the pregnancy outcomes of all transferred embryos from the initial OR from cycle start to delivery (~40 weeks) or until all embryos from the OR are transferred. The primary outcome is the CLBR per OR (‘take home baby rate’) defined as the proportion of participants with a live birth ≥22 weeks of gestation resulting from implantation of any embryos generated with oocytes from the initial OR. Secondary outcomes include multi-fetal pregnancy, pregnancy loss <20 weeks of gestation, cycle cancellation and perinatal outcomes. We also will collect and evaluate additional exploratory outcomes related to adverse pregnancy events (eg, ectopic pregnancy), financial burden, patient satisfaction and facilitators and barriers for involvement and implementation (see table 3). In addition, we will collect information about embryo development. We will collect this information for the per-protocol analysis regardless of whether participants choose to continue with their assigned transfer day. Follow-up data collection will be performed as per standard clinical care and Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology (SART)/Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reporting requirements.

Sample size

We plan to enrol and randomise n=1126 study participants (563/group). For the primary outcome of CLBR per OR, assuming rates aligned with the preliminary retrospective PSM analysis described above (table 1) and allowing 10% loss to follow-up and 10% contamination (ie, inflating the sample size by 20%), this sample size provides an estimated power of 99% and 91% for a CLBR risk difference of 13.7% (35.8% vs 22.1%, relative risk (RR): 1.62) and 10.0% (32.1% vs 22.1%, RR: 1.45), respectively. Holding other inputs constant, this sample size retains 80% power even if the difference in CLBR is as small as 8.4% (30.3% vs 22.1%, RR: 1.37). These power calculations were based on an analytically derived formula for modified Poisson regression.35 Additional details about the sample size calculation for primary, secondary, exploratory outcomes and key subgroup analyses can be found in the Appendix. Note, we chose to base our power estimates on this retrospective data analysis so that we could generate preliminary CLBR estimates among a population of patients that most closely reflects the target study population for this trial (in particular, among IVF patients with ≤5 embryos). Although there are prior randomised trials of cleavage-stage vs blastocyst-stage transfers, these trials analysed a different study population as they enrolled participants with >5 embryos. We do not believe that data from these studies would as accurately reflect the anticipated CLBRs among those with ≤5 embryos compared with a retrospective analysis specifically designed to mimic the target study population with ≤5 embryos in this current trial.

Statistical analysis plan

All primary analyses will be conducted according to the intent-to-treat principle. Statistical reporting will adhere to Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.36 The primary outcome for this study is cumulative live birth (CLB) per OR. By design, we limit data collection in this study to one OR per participant, meaning that CLB is a binary outcome. We will fit a modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors to estimate the RR for CLB between randomisation groups (blastocyst-stage vs cleavage-stage ET), adjusting for the categorically defined randomisation stratification factors (ie, site, age, embryo number and prior IVF cycles).37 We will conduct a 2-sided 5% significance level test of the randomisation group term to evaluate the superiority of blastocyst-stage vs cleavage-stage ET. For each secondary outcome, we will use a modified Poisson regression model to estimate the RR of blastocyst-stage vs cleavage-stage ET, adjusting for the categorically defined randomisation stratification factors. A Bonferroni multiplicity correction will be used when reporting the significance of secondary outcome analyses, to maintain an overall family-wise error rate of 5%. Model-based estimates and 95% CIs without multiplicity correction will be calculated and reported for primary and secondary outcomes. We will also collect and report additional exploratory outcomes in this trial (eg, ectopic pregnancy, time to pregnancy, preterm birth). For each exploratory outcome, we will compute descriptive statistics, generate data visualisations, and conduct appropriate regression modelling and report model-based estimates and 95% CIs for comparing the intervention groups.

We will conduct sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of results for the primary outcome. When data are missing, we will provide thorough summaries of reasons for missingness, proportions of missing data and evaluate differences in the characteristics of patients with and without missing data. To determine if results are sensitive to missing data, we will refit models using multiple imputation for missing data.38 To examine whether intervention contamination during the study impacts results, we will conduct a per-protocol analysis39 using inverse probability weighting and a contamination-adjusted intention-to-treat analysis using instrumental variable methods.40

Exploratory subgroup analyses will examine differences in the CLBR based on number of zygotes (1–2 vs 3–5), participant age (<38 vs ≥ 38 years), prior IVF cycles (0 vs 1–2), intended PGT-a use on surplus embryos (yes vs no), embryo quality (poor vs average vs good quality embryos undergoing single embryo transfer), race (in particular African American, Asian and Hispanic),41 42 ethnicity and socioeconomic position. For each potential moderator, we will fit an expanded modified Poisson regression model adding terms for the moderator and a moderator-by-intervention interaction. We will report interaction effect estimates and subgroup-specific RRs with 95% CIs in a descriptive manner (eg, using a forest plot).43

Mixed methods and qualitative data analysis plan

We will use a mixed-methods approach with a modified Quality from the Patient’s Perspective for IVF (QPP-IVF) satisfaction questionnaire44 and the Infertility-Related Stress Scale45 in combination with semi-structured interviews to determine the effect of ET timing on patient satisfaction and stress levels in participants who achieved a live birth and on closure and acceptance in those who did not get pregnant. We will determine how patients weigh the five patient-centred fertility treatment dimensions46 relative to each other for both comparators. We will use the RE-AIM QuEST (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance, Qualitative Evaluation for Systematic Translation) mixed-methods framework47 48 to identify facilitators and barriers for patient involvement in decision making and implementation/dissemination of blastocyst ET in routine infertility care settings. RE-AIM QuEST qualitative data will be analysed using directed content analysis49 50 in an iterative, multi-step process. We will conduct a rigorous, prospective evaluation of the cost burden to patients in both treatment arms.

Discussion

The PRECISE trial is the first large RCT comparing blastocyst-stage and cleavage-stage ET in patients with few embryos. There is a clear need for this RCT because both types of transfer are routinely performed at many IVF centres without high-quality evidence to guide clinical decision making. This trial will provide a comprehensive assessment of pregnancy outcomes for each intervention including the rate of live birth per retrieval; analyse patient satisfaction and cost burden; and identify barriers and facilitators for involvement of patients in the decision-making process; as well as for the implementation of study findings. One strength of the trial is the primary outcome of cumulative live birth rate per oocyte retrieval which was chosen as the most important outcome metric for IVF patients and providers in consultation with the stakeholder advisory groups for the trial. In addition, we defined our primary outcome this way, as it captures both direct and indirect effects of fresh transfer timing. For example, one indirect benefit of cleavage-stage fresh transfer may be that it increases the total number of viable embryos (fresh and frozen) that are ultimately transferred from a single oocyte retrieval. This indirect benefit would not be captured if we limited the primary outcome to live birth rate per initial (fresh) embryo transfer. Nonetheless, ‘live birth per fresh embryo transfer’ is one of our pre-specified exploratory outcomes (see table 3), which will be evaluated and reported alongside the primary CLBR results. A further strength of the trial is the large SAG which includes 5 IVF patients to ensure emphasis on patient-relevant outcomes and provide comprehensive guidance and input on all aspects of our study, including the trial design, intervention and outcomes as well as the implementation and dissemination of findings.

This trial evaluates the comparative effectiveness of starting with a fresh cleavage-stage embryo transfer vs starting with fresh blastocyst-stage transfer and then freezing the remaining embryos at the blastocyst-stage. Thus, this is a pragmatic trial which compares two commonly performed strategies for embryo transfer but cannot answer the (equally interesting but academic) question of whether transfer of all cleavage-stage or all blastocys-stage embryos results in better outcomes in patients with few embryos. However, the trial data will allow direct comparison of cleavage-stage vs blastocyst embryo transfer in patients who transfer all of their embryos in the fresh transfer cycle.

The study population for this trial with 5 or fewer embryos includes patients from 18 to 44 years of age and the effect of cleavage-stage vs blastocyst-stage embryo transfer could differ for young vs older patients. The majority of patients with few zygotes will naturally be in the older age group and will have higher aneuploidy rates. However, aneuploidy rates are expected to be the same in each arm due to the randomisation and age-stratification and because cleavage-stage vs blastocyst culture is unlikely to differentially affect ploidy status. Thus, different rates of aneuploidy for younger vs older patients will not affect the validity of our randomised comparisons. Ultimately, this trial is testing whether, in this poor prognosis group of patients with few embryos, other factors affecting egg quality beyond ploidy, such as cytoplasmic or epigenetic factors, may determine whether ‘poor quality’ euploid embryos do better with a cleavage-stage vs blastocyst-stage embryo transfer. We have recently shown that live birth rates per transfer do not differ significantly between young women with and without diminished ovarian reserve or poor ovarian response (<5 oocytes) after euploid single-embryo transfers (56.0% and 60.5%, respectively).51 Live birth rates for transfer of euploid embryos in the general IVF population gradually decline by only ~10% between younger (<35 years) and older patients (>42 years)52 indicating that age-dependent factors other than aneuploidy play a (limited) role in determining the quality of euploid embryos. In addition, due to age-independent factors, even in younger patients, ~40% of euploid embryos do not result in a successful pregnancy. Whether the timing of embryo transfer can modulate the effect of both age-dependent and age-independent factors on the developmental potential of euploid embryos in older or younger or all patients with few embryos is not known and will be investigated in this trial. Nevertheless, the heterogeneity of the study population due to the inclusion of younger patients instead of restricting the study population to older patients or those with diminished ovarian reserve could also be viewed as a limitation of the study as it could affect the expected effect size of outcome measures. To tease out whether the between-group difference might vary by age or other prognostic factors, we do plan to conduct pre-specified subgroup (ie, heterogeneity of treatment effect, HTE) analyses, which are described in the Supplement. Regardless of the presence or absence of HTE, our study remains adequately powered to detect an aggregate difference between arms of 13.7% (>99% power) or even as small as 8.4% (80% power). It is possible that incorrect dosing or ineffective drug administration by patients could affect the number of eggs retrieved and therefore the number of embryos generated in a cycle. However, we anticipate these to be relatively rare incidents. For the most part, having few embryos will be a marker for underlying reproductive issues (age-related or age-unrelated) which could affect the quality of euploid embryos and the pregnancy potenial after cleavage-stage vs blastocyst embryo transfer.

The results of this large pragmatic trial will replace a clinical decision based on empiric physician preference and experience and, as a result, does not interfere with the logistics of routine IVF care. However, the main challenge for this trial is the relatively low number of patients with ≤5 zygotes on p.f.d. 1 and the possibility that patients may opt for another OR to bank embryos and freeze all embryos. This is overcome by the size of the participating IVF programmes which allows for limiting recruitment to patients with up to two prior cycles, thereby reducing drop-out and contamination. This trial will have important implications for the clinical practice of IVF and may result in the adoption of routine blastocyst culture for all patients. This would result in higher rates of single embryo transfers, reduced risk of multi-fetal pregnancy, as well as simplified laboratory protocols and reduced costs at a time when reducing financial barriers to IVF is urgently needed.

Ethics and dissemination

Institutional review board approval

The institutional review board (IRB) at BIDMC approved the study protocol and consent form on 13 June 2025 (2024P000892) and serves as the central IRB. All eleven approved sites rely on the central IRB. Study documents and protocol modifications will be available on the PRECISE website. We will seek formal amendment to the protocol for any modifications which may impact on the conduct of the study, potential benefit of the patient or may affect patient safety, including changes of study objectives, study design, patient population, sample sizes, study procedures or significant administrative aspects. Such amendments will be approved by the IRB prior to implementation. At the discretion of the principal investigator the IRB may be notified of minor administrative changes to the protocol and/or clarifications that have no effect on the way the study is to be conducted.

Availability of data and material

Final study datasets will be stored locally and securely at the DCC at MGH for long-term storage and access. Deidentified participant-level data that underlie the results reported in this article (text, tables, figures and appendices) including data dictionaries and the study protocol as well as the statistical analysis plan will be made available on request beginning 9 months following article publication. These data will be shared with investigators to perform individual participant data meta-analysis and whose proposed use of the data has been approved by an independent review committee (‘learned intermediary’) identified for this purpose. Proposals should be directed to wneuhaus@bidmc.harvard.edu. To gain access, data requestors will need to sign a data use agreement.

Intra-Study data sharing

The principal investigators will oversee the intra-study data sharing process. The DCC at MGH and the CCC at BIDMC will have access to all study data from all study sites to monitor participant safety and data accuracy and integrity centrally throughout the trial. All investigators at individual study sites will have direct access to their own site’s data. To ensure confidentiality, all scientific data will be collected and stored in REDCap, a HIPPA-compliant electronic data capture system with multiple levels of security features ensuring access control, audit control, data integrity, user authentication, and transmission security.

Ethics and dissemination

The Committee on Clinical Investigation BIDMC provides central regulatory oversight with recruitment sites ceding to BIDMC. Women between 18 and 44 years of age undergoing IVF will be randomised to either blastocyst-stage or cleavage-stage ET. Results will inform timing of embryo transfer in patients with ≤5 zygotes and allow patients and other stakeholders to balance the risks and benefits of blastocyst-stage or cleavage-stage ET. We will disseminate the results through academic publications, meeting presentations and communications to advocacy groups and patient participants.

Dissemination policy

The scientific integrity of the project requires that the data from all study sites be analysed study-wide and reported as such. Individual centres will not report the data collected from its centre alone and outcome data will not be presented prior to the release of mainline results. Recommendations as to the timing of presentation of such endpoint data and the meetings at which they might be presented will be given by the principal investigator. Every attempt will be made to reduce the interval between the completion of data collection and the release of the study results to an absolute minimum. We expect to take about 3 to 4 months to compile the final results paper for an appropriate journal. The study results will be released to the participating physicians, referring physicians, patients and the general medical community. Publication of outcomes data will occur at project conclusion, consistent with normal scientific practices. For manuscripts submitted for publication, we will follow the authorship criteria established by the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors.53 All participating institutions will follow Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) data sharing guidelines.
