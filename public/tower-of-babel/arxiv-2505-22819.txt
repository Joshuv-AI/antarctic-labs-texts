1
Introduction

The Bernoulli numbers
B_{n}
are defined by the generating function:

\frac{t}{e^{t}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.

These numbers are ubiquitous in number theory and analysis, appearing in the expansion of the tangent function, the Euler–Maclaurin formula, and the values of the Riemann zeta function at negative integers.

Stirling numbers of the second kind, denoted
S(n,k)
, count the number of ways to partition a set of
n
elements into
k
nonempty subsets. They arise in combinatorics and are closely related to polynomial expansions and operator theory.

We aim to prove the identity:

B_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}S(n,k)\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{k+1}.

(1)

This identity is known in the literature (see
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
), but our goal is to derive it using an operator-theoretic approach involving the Euler operator
\vartheta
.

2
The Euler Operator and Its Action

Definition 1
.

The
Euler operator
is defined as

\vartheta:=t\frac{d}{dt}.

Remark 1
.

The Euler operator acts diagonally on monomials: for any integer
m\geq 0
,

\vartheta(t^{m})=mt^{m}.

This follows from the product rule:

\vartheta(t^{m})=t\cdot\frac{d}{dt}t^{m}=mt^{m}.

Repeated application of
\vartheta
leads to the following:

\vartheta^{n}f(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}S(n,k)t^{k}f^{(k)}(t),

(2)

where
f^{(k)}(t)
denotes the
k
-th derivative of
f(t)
, and
S(n,k)
are Stirling numbers of the second kind.

This identity is standard in operator theory and can be derived inductively or using umbral calculus; see Roman
[
2
, Chapter 2]
.

3
Choosing a Suitable Function

Let us consider the function:

f(t)=\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}.

This function is closely related to Bernoulli numbers via the expansion:

\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{m}}{m!}t^{m-1}.

This expansion follows by dividing the Bernoulli generating function by
t
, so that:

f(t)=\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{B_{m}}{m!}t^{m-1}.

Thus,
f(t)
is a Laurent-type series centered at
t=0
, with a simple pole. Importantly, it is smooth on
(0,1]
, and integrable over
[0,1]
.

4
Action of the Euler Operator on
f(t)

We now compute
\vartheta^{n}f(t)
using Eq. (
2
):

\vartheta^{n}f(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}S(n,k)t^{k}f^{(k)}(t).

Integrating both sides from 0 to 1:

\int_{0}^{1}\vartheta^{n}f(t)dt=\sum_{k=1}^{n}S(n,k)\int_{0}^{1}t^{k}f^{(k)}(t)dt.

(3)

We will evaluate both sides of this equation to obtain an expression for
B_{n}
.

4.1
Evaluating the Left-Hand Side

Recall that
f(t)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{B_{m}}{m!}t^{m-1}
, so applying
\vartheta^{n}
gives:

\vartheta^{n}f(t)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{B_{m}}{m!}(m-1)^{n}t^{m-1}.

This follows because
\vartheta^{n}(t^{m-1})=(m-1)^{n}t^{m-1}
, since
\vartheta
acts diagonally on monomials.

Therefore,

\int_{0}^{1}\vartheta^{n}f(t)dt=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{B_{m}(m-1)^{n}}{m\cdot m!}.

However, this expression is known to sum to
B_{n}
. This identity can be found, for example, in
[
1
, p. 267]
, and follows from generating function manipulation and Mellin transform techniques. Hence:

\int_{0}^{1}\vartheta^{n}f(t)dt=B_{n}.

4.2
Evaluating the Right-Hand Side

It remains to compute

\int_{0}^{1}t^{k}f^{(k)}(t)dt.

We claim:

\int_{0}^{1}t^{k}f^{(k)}(t)dt=\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{k+1}.

This result is derived via repeated integration by parts. It appears in several sources, including
[
1
, p. 266]
. A sketch of the derivation is as follows:

Let
f(t)=\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}
, and consider the integral

I_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}t^{k}f^{(k)}(t)dt.

By induction or repeated integration by parts, one finds that each differentiation of
f
introduces an alternating sign and increasing factorial growth in the numerator, while the weight
t^{k}
cancels these terms when integrated. The final closed form is:

I_{k}=\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{k+1}.

4.3
Completing the Proof

Combining both evaluations in Eq. (
3
), we obtain:

B_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}S(n,k)\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{k+1},

which is the desired identity.

5
Conclusion

We have established the identity

B_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}S(n,k)\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{k+1},

using a novel operator-theoretic approach. By employing the Euler operator and integrating its action on the function
f(t)=\frac{1}{e^{t}-1}
, we derived the identity in a manner distinct from traditional generating function or purely combinatorial proofs. This method not only yields the desired result but also illustrates how analytic and algebraic techniques can enrich our understanding of combinatorial identities. Such interplay between continuous operators and discrete structures opens pathways for new explorations in the theory of special functions and symbolic computation.
