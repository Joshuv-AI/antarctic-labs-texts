Symbolic and non-symbolic geometric abilities across development and in individuals with dyscalculia and dyslexia
Authors: Elisa Castaldi, Giovanni Anobile, Moreno Marazzi, Stefano Federici, Paola Angelelli, Marco Turi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGeometry is fundamental to mathematical thinking, yet its foundations and difficulties in learning disorders remain poorly understood. Here, we investigated the development of geometric abilities and their relationship with developmental dyscalculia and dyslexia. Seventy-nine children and adults, including individuals with and without learning difficulties, completed behavioral measures of non-symbolic geometry and a paper-and-pencil assessment of symbolic geometry. Geometric abilities continued to develop from early adolescence into young adulthood and non-symbolic geometry predicted symbolic geometry independently of age, verbal reasoning, non-verbal reasoning and visuospatial working memory. Adults with dyscalculia, most of whom also had dyslexia, showed impairments in both non-symbolic and symbolic geometry that persisted after controlling for visuospatial working memory, whereas individuals with dyslexia without dyscalculia performed comparably to controls. These findings suggest that non-symbolic geometry contributes to formal geometric competence and that geometric difficulties may be a persistent feature of developmental dyscalculia, with implications for education and clinical assessment.

Introduction

Since ancient times, humans have relied on geometry in everyday life to navigate, construct monuments, and interpret the rich spatial structure of the environment. Geometry has profoundly shaped architecture, science and art, reflecting both our perceptual experience of space and our capacity to formalize spatial intuitions into abstract symbolic systems. Together with arithmetic, geometry constitutes a fundamental branch of mathematics and a core component of formal education. Despite its central role in human knowledge, however, the developmental origins of our geometric capability remain poorly understood. Clarifying its foundations is crucial for both informing more effective educational practices and for supporting students who experience learning difficulties in mathematics. Recent OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) assessments have documented a marked decline in mathematical achievement since 2018, including substantial difficulties in the acquisition of geometric concepts.1 Despite these concerning trends, the cognitive profile of students who experience difficulties in learning geometry has been only scarcely investigated.

Previous studies have proposed that the geometrical competence taught at school (i.e., formal or symbolic geometry) may build upon a non-symbolic, preverbal system that supports basic geometric intuitions.2,3,4 This proposal suggests that the roots of geometry may lie in evolutionarily ancient cognitive systems rather than in formal instruction alone. This core non-symbolic geometric intuition is not unique to humans, but is shared with several animal species5 who use it for spatial reorientation.6 In humans it is present early in life: even within the first days of life, infants respond to a range of geometric cues, including angular size, overall shape and relative length.7,8,9,10 These early sensitivities likely support the emergence of representations of angles and spatial configurations, as well as the encoding of object distance relative to environmental landmarks.11 Evidence for early and culture-independent geometric intuition also comes from studies on the Amazonian Munduruku and on the Himba populations of Namibia, who have little formal education and limited linguistic tools to describe geometric properties.2,12,13 Dehaene et al.2 showed that both adults and children from the Munduruku group could identify the “odd-one-out” among sets of figures differing in specific geometric features (e.g., parallelism), indicating a spontaneous ability to grasp basic geometric principles even in absence of formal geometrical education. More recently, Amalric et al.12 reported that Munduruku participants and preschool children were as proficient as educated Western adults in performing inferences based on a non-symbolic and non-verbal “geometry language”. In this task, a dot appeared sequentially at different locations around an octagon, generating geometric paths of varying complexity, from simple circular trajectories to more complex structured patterns. Participants were able to anticipate the continuation of these sequences, indicating a spontaneous sensitivity to abstract geometric regularities that does not rely on formal schooling or explicit linguistic scaffolding. Collectively, these studies point to the existence of a phylogenetically ancient, education- and language-independent intuition for geometry.14,15

A central question that follows concerns the relationship between these forms of non-symbolic geometric intuition and the formal, symbolic geometric competence taught in school (hereafter referred to interchangeably as formal, symbolic or school-acquired geometry). Previous studies have assessed formal geometry using a variety of tasks, including geometrical problem solving, declarative geometric knowledge and geometry-specific visuospatial abilities. In the present study, we focused on the latter two components, which minimize the contribution of arithmetic calculations and capture complementary aspects of formal geometric competence by assessing respectively explicit knowledge of geometric concepts and the application of formally acquired geometric principles.

Giofrè et al.16 found that, in 11th- and 12th-grade students (17–18 years old), performance on the geometrical odd-one-out task originally devised by Dehaene et al.2 was positively correlated with scores on a set of multiple-choice questions assessing advanced geometry (such as determine the measure of the angle formed by the intersection of a transversal with two parallel lines). In a subsequent study, Giofrè et al.17 reported that, in 4th- and 5th-grade students (around 9 years old), performance on the same geometrical odd-one-out task was significantly associated with both geometrical problem-solving ability and declarative geometric knowledge (e.g., concepts such as concave and parallelogram), as measured by the standardized Geometria test.18 However, this relationship was no longer significant once verbal and non-verbal working memory as well as intelligence (g-factor) were taken into account. Still, a portion of variance in the odd-one-out task was not shared with intelligence measures, suggesting that performance in non-symbolic geometry may capture specific geometric intuitions that are at least partially independent of general cognitive abilities.

The link between non-symbolic and symbolic geometry has also been demonstrated using other measures of non-symbolic geometry. Castaldi et al.19 reported that, in primary school children (3rd–5th grades, 7–11 years old), performance on the geometrical inference task originally developed by Amalric et al.12 predicted symbolic geometry achievement. In that study, geometry achievement was assessed using the same standardized battery employed by Giofrè et al.,17 although focusing on partially different subtests: one subtest measuring geometry-specific visuospatial abilities, in addition to the one evaluating declarative geometric knowledge. The association between the geometrical inference task and symbolic geometry achievement remained significant even after controlling for verbal reasoning, suggesting a relative specificity of this link (r = 0.56). However non-verbal reasoning and visuospatial working memory were not measured, leaving open the possibility that these domain-general factors may, even partially, account for the observed relationship between non-symbolic and symbolic geometry.

Further research is therefore needed to disentangle how different measures of non-symbolic geometry (for example, the odd-one-out task versus the less-studied geometrical inference task) develop across age and uniquely contribute to symbolic geometry once multiple general cognitive abilities are jointly considered.

Surprisingly, there has been very limited research on geometric learning difficulties. Bizzaro et al.20 examined two groups of neurotypical children (5th–6th graders, around 11 years old) who were selected based on their high or low performance in solving geometrical problems, while being otherwise matched for age, verbal intelligence and basic arithmetic skills. None of the children had a clinical diagnosis of intellectual disability or learning difficulties in any academic domain (including arithmetic). Children identified as low performers in solving geometrical problems also performed worse on working memory tasks (both verbal and visuospatial) as well as on arithmetic problem solving compared to those identified as high performers. Interestingly, however, the performance gap between the two groups was larger for geometric than arithmetic problems, suggesting that solving geometric problems does not rely on calculation and arithmetic skills alone and that difficulties in geometric and arithmetic domains can be at least partially dissociated. Indeed, the low performers also obtained lower scores on a test assessing geometry-specific visuospatial abilities that do not involve calculation.

While this study suggests that specific difficulties in geometrical learning can be observed in neurotypical children, to date only two studies have investigated geometric abilities in individuals with learning disorders: one focused on children with non-verbal/visuospatial learning disability,21 and the other on developmental dyslexia.22

The nonverbal learning disability is characterized by difficulties in processing visuospatial and nonverbal information.23,24,25 Children with this disorder also exhibit poor arithmetic performance, although mostly due to impairments in visuospatial processing rather than in numerical understanding or arithmetic concepts per se.26 Mammarella et al.21 found that difficulties also extend to geometry: they tested among 11–13-year-old children with visuospatial learning disability and reported poorer performance in the odd-one-out task compared with neurotypical peers matched for age and verbal reasoning. However, these geometric difficulties were not specific and largely attributable to weak visuospatial working memory, as controlling for this factor eliminated the group differences.

More recently, a study reported that some children with dyslexia aged 7–16 years, show difficulties in different subcomponents of mathematics.22 About 9% of the sample with dyslexia exhibited specific geometric impairments, performing worse than both neurotypical peers and dyslexic children without math difficulties on the odd-one-out task. Their impairments were selective: calculation and arithmetic fact retrieval were intact and other general cognitive abilities were largely unaffected.

In individuals with developmental dyscalculia, a previous report identified visuo-spatial memory impairments27; however, geometric abilities have not yet been systematically investigated. Developmental dyscalculia is characterized by persistent difficulties in learning number facts and arithmetical procedures,28,29 as well as in mastering basic numerical skills such as counting.30,31,32 The impairment in processing numerical information is not limited to formal arithmetic or symbolic Arabic numbers, but also extends to magnitude perception, including perception of numerical quantities33,34,35,36 and time perception.37,38,39,40,41,42 Beyond numerical difficulties, individuals with developmental dyscalculia can manifest visual perceptual impairments, including abnormal visual crowding and reduced global motion sensitivity.43,44 More recently, one study reported reduced sensitivity to 2D form coherence (glass patterns) and 3D structure-from-motion in adults with developmental dyscalculia, impairments that could not be explained by associated reading difficulties.45 These findings suggest that basic visual mechanisms supporting form perception may be altered in dyscalculia, potentially contributing to difficulties in geometry. Importantly, not all non-symbolic processes or magnitude-related functions appear to be impaired in dyscalculia. For instance, the perception of individual object size,46 average size,36 and groupitizing—namely, the facilitation of numerosity estimation through perceptual grouping—has been shown to be preserved in children and adults with dyscalculia.47 However, because the assessment of geometric abilities is rarely included in the diagnostic batteries for developmental dyscalculia, this domain may be overlooked, raising the risk of neglecting an important aspect of the disorder.

The present study had two main aims. First, we sought to extend previous work on the relationship between non-symbolic and symbolic geometry by examining a broader developmental period spanning early adolescence (11–14 years) and young adulthood (20–29 years). Unlike previous studies, which focused on more restricted age ranges (e.g., preschool children, primary school children and older adolescents or adults), we assessed two complementary measures of non-symbolic geometry (the odd-one-out and geometric inference tasks) together with two distinct components of symbolic geometry (declarative geometric knowledge and geometry-specific visuospatial abilities). This design allowed us to determine whether geometric abilities continue to mature into adulthood and whether the relationship between symbolic and non-symbolic geometry remains after jointly accounting for verbal reasoning, non-verbal reasoning, visuospatial working memory and age. Second, we aimed to investigate whether adults with developmental profiles involving dyscalculia exhibit impairments in geometric processing (both symbolic and non-symbolic), even when tasks do not require arithmetic calculations, and whether such difficulties are also observed in dyslexia without dyscalculia, while controlling for general cognitive abilities. We addressed this second aim in adults with learning disabilities (university students), for whom reduced variability and greater compensatory opportunities allow any observed difficulty to reflect a genuine impairment rather than a delayed developmental trajectory or limited educational exposure.

Results

Typical development of non-symbolic and symbolic geometry

One group of neurotypical children aged 11–14 and one group of neurotypical adults aged 20–29, were tested with different measures of non-symbolic and symbolic geometry. Non-symbolic geometry was assessed with both the odd-one-out task and the geometrical inference task, adapted from the paradigms of Dehaene et al.2 and Amalric et al.,12 respectively, whereas symbolic geometry was evaluated through measures of declarative geometric knowledge and geometry-specific visuospatial abilities, using subtests from the Geometria test18 (Figure 1). We then asked whether this developmental trend specifically reflected improvements in non-symbolic and symbolic geometry per se, or whether it was primarily driven by maturation in domain-general cognitive skills. To this aim, the composite indices of the two geometry domains were entered into a repeated-measures linear mixed-effects model with age group (children vs. adults) as a between-subject factor and geometry domain (non-symbolic vs. symbolic, both as composite indices) as a within-subject factor. Verbal reasoning, non-verbal reasoning and visuospatial working memory were included as covariates. The model included fixed effects of age group, geometry domain, their interaction and the cognitive covariates, together with a participant-specific random intercept. No random slopes were included. The link between non-symbolic and symbolic geometry in neurotypical adults and children

We next examined whether performance in non-symbolic geometry could predict performance in symbolic geometry. We first examined zero-order correlations among the four geometry tasks and found that all measures were significantly correlated (Table S1). We then examined whether performance in non-symbolic geometry could predict performance in symbolic geometry, independently of other general cognitive abilities and age. To this aim, we combined the two age groups and computed residual scores for each test after regressing out the effects of verbal reasoning, non-verbal reasoning, visuospatial working memory and age. Residuals from the two non-symbolic tasks and the two symbolic tasks were averaged to derive composite indices for each geometry domain and correlated with each other. This analysis revealed a robust and significant association between symbolic and non-symbolic geometric performance across participants (r = 0.59, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.36, 0.75], LogBF = 3.2, Figure 3). The correlation between non-symbolic and symbolic geometry was consistent across different non-symbolic tasks. Specifically, it was observed for both the odd-one-out task (r = 0.46, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.20, 0.66], LogBF = 1.5) and the geometric inference task (r = 0.51, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.25, 0.69], LogBF = 2.1). We next quantified the extent to which general cognitive abilities, age and non-symbolic geometry performance explain variance across the different symbolic geometry subtests. Overall, these results indicate that non-symbolic geometry contributes uniquely and substantially to performance in both declarative geometric knowledge and geometry-specific visuospatial abilities, beyond the influence of age and general cognitive skills. However, whereas declarative knowledge appears to rely mainly on domain-general abilities, with non-symbolic geometry accounting for only a small, albeit significant, portion of variance, geometry-specific visuospatial performance shows the opposite pattern, with most of the variance explained by non-symbolic geometry and little to no contribution from domain-general skills. Importantly, by entering non-verbal reasoning and visuospatial working memory in the first step of the model, we accounted for variance associated with these visually demanding domain-general abilities and found that non-symbolic geometry explained additional variance in symbolic geometry.

Non-symbolic and symbolic geometry in individuals with dyscalculia and dyslexia

After characterizing the developmental trajectory of non-symbolic and symbolic geometry in neurotypical individuals, we compared the performance in neurotypical adults, which served as a control group (CTR), with that of age-matched individuals with dyscalculia, most of whom also had dyslexia, and with a group of individuals with dyslexia without dyscalculia. For brevity, we will refer to the first group as “dyscalculia group” (DYSC), and to the second as “dyslexia group” (DYSL).

Group comparisons of age, mental calculation and general cognitive abilities are reported in the supplemental information and Table S4. Briefly, the three groups were matched for age and visuospatial working memory, whereas the dyscalculic group differed from both the control and dyslexic groups in mental calculation abilities (both p < 0.001). It also differed from the control group in verbal reasoning (p = 0.02) and showed a trend toward a significance difference from the dyslexic group in non-verbal reasoning (p = 0.051).

(C and D) Symbolic geometry: mean accuracy in the tests assessing (C) declarative geometric knowledge and (D) geometric-specific visuospatial abilities. Mean accuracies are shown for neurotypical controls (CTR), individuals with dyscalculia (DYSC) and individuals with dyslexia without dyscalculia (DYSL). Error bars represent standard errors of the mean. p values refer to Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons from linear mixed-effects models without covariates, while the smaller italicized p values represent the same comparisons after including visuospatial working memory as covariate. p values that were not significant in either models (with or without covariate) are not shown in the figure or reported as ns (not significant).

A similar pattern was observed in the geometrical inference task (Figure 4B), in which the dyscalculia group scored on average 79% (SD = 12) of correct responses, compared to 87% (SD = 7) in both the neurotypical controls and the dyslexia group. The linear mixed-effects model including group as a fixed factor revealed a significant main effect of group (F(2, 54) = 5.6, p = 0.006). Bonferroni-corrected post hoc tests showed significant differences between the dyscalculia group and both the neurotypical controls (p = 0.01, Cohen’s d = 1.3) and the dyslexia group (p = 0.028, Cohen’s d = 1.4). These effects remained significant even when visuospatial working memory was added as covariate (F(2, 53) = 5.4, p = 0.007; DYSC vs. CTR: p = 0.017, Cohen’s d = 1.3; DYSC vs. DYSL: p = 0.020, Cohen’s d = 1.4).

We then turned to symbolic geometry and observed an overall lower performance across most subtests and geometric categories in dyscalculic individuals compared to the other groups (see supplemental information and Figure S1B). The declarative geometric knowledge (Figure 4C) was poorer in the dyscalculia group, who scored on average 44% (SD = 19) of correct responses, compared to 63% (SD = 17) in the neurotypical controls and 60% (SD = 18) in the dyslexia group. A linear mixed-effects model including group as a fixed factor showed a significant main effect of group (F(2, 54) = 6.3, p = 0.004), with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons indicating lower performance in the dyscalculia group relative to both the neurotypical controls (p = 0.004, Cohen’s d = 1.5) and the dyslexia group (p = 0.046, Cohen’s d = 1.3). These effects remained significant even when visuospatial working memory was added as covariate (F(2, 53) = 5.9, p = 0.005; DYSC vs. CTR: p = 0.006, Cohen’s d = 1.4; DYSC vs. DYSL: p = 0.038, Cohen’s d = 1.3).

Finally, geometry-specific visuospatial abilities were also lower in the dyscalculia group, who scored on average 77% (SD = 15) of correct responses, compared to 90% (SD = 9) in the neurotypical controls and 82% (SD = 13) in the dyslexia group (Figure 4D). The linear mixed-effects model including group as a fixed factor revealed a significant main effect of group (F(2, 54) = 6.7, p = 0.002), with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc tests showing lower performance in the dyscalculia group relative to the neurotypical controls (p = 0.002, Cohen’s d = 1.6). This effect remained significant even when visuospatial working memory was added as covariate (F(2, 53) = 6.1, p = 0.004; DYSC vs. CTR: p = 0.003, Cohen’s d = 1.5).

Overall, these findings indicate that adults from groups in which dyscalculia is present exhibit a generalized impairment in both non-symbolic and symbolic geometry. Individuals with dyslexia without comorbid dyscalculia performed comparably to neurotypical controls in all tasks, whereas the difficulties observed in individuals with dyscalculia were largely specific to this group, as they also differed from the dyslexia group on most measures, with the exception of geometry-specific visuospatial abilities. These differences could not be explained by weaknesses in visuospatial working memory, as they persisted even after controlling for this factor.

Discussion

The present study replicated and extended previous findings on the relationship between non-symbolic and symbolic geometry while providing the first systematic investigation of geometric abilities in adults with developmental profiles involving dyscalculia.

Compared with previous developmental studies, our design combined a broader developmental age range, multiple measures of both symbolic and non-symbolic geometry and simultaneous control of several domain-general cognitive abilities. This allowed us to characterize the developmental trajectory of geometric abilities more comprehensively than previous investigations.

We show that geometric abilities continue to develop between early adolescence (11–14 years) and young adulthood (20–29 years) and that non-symbolic geometry robustly predicts symbolic geometric competence, independently of age and general cognitive factors. Strikingly, both non-symbolic and symbolic geometry were impaired in adults with developmental profiles in which dyscalculia is present, even relative to other learning disorders, and these difficulties were not fully accounted for by visuospatial working memory.

A previous study9 administered the odd-one-out task across a broad age range of neurotypical individuals (6–55 years) and reported that participants younger than 12 years performed significantly worse than adults (18+ years), whereas adolescents aged 12–17 years did not differ from adults. The authors concluded that performance on the odd-one-out task reaches a plateau at 12 years of age. In our sample, children were slightly older (mean age around 13 years) yet they still performed below adult levels, with 83% compared to 87% of correct responses. The slightly prolonged developmental window observed in the present study may reflect differences in the adult samples: adults in Izard et al.9 ranged from 18 to 55 years, whereas our adult group was limited to 20–29 years, potentially reducing variability or shifting the reference level of performance. Future studies should investigate whether performance on this task declines with advancing age after the thirties.

For the geometrical inference task, no previous study has systematically investigated typical developmental changes across a broad and continuous age range. Available evidence has so far been limited to comparisons between widely separated age groups, rather than to a more fine-grained characterization of developmental progression across intermediate stages (e.g., 5–6-year-old children scoring around 49% versus adults aged 20–46 scoring around 78%).12 Our earlier work showed that performance increases between 7 and 11 years of age, reaching approximately 72%.19 The present data further indicate continued improvement in the 11–14-year range (up to around 84%), with performance reaching about 90% in adults aged 20–29, again possibly suggesting a peak in young adults that may be followed by a decline at later ages.

We also found that performance in non-symbolic geometry predicted the one in symbolic geometry in neurotypical participants. This is in line with previous research16,17; however, unlike Giofrè et al.,17 we found that this relationship remained significant even after controlling for visuospatial working memory and other general cognitive abilities. The discrepancy between these findings may depend on several factors, including the type of symbolic geometry assessed. Giofrè et al.17 tested the ability to solve geometric problems and declarative geometric knowledge as indicators of a single latent construct of geometrical achievement. They reported that while non-symbolic geometry, measured with the odd-one-out task, correlated with symbolic geometry, both with each subtests individually and with the integrated dimension of geometrical achievement, the latter association was no longer significant once working memory and fluid intelligence were controlled for, and no subtest-specific results were reported. In the present study, we included the same test evaluating declarative geometric knowledge; however, rather than assessing the ability to solve geometric problems, we measured geometric-specific visuospatial abilities, a measure that was not included in the definition of geometrical achievement by Giofrè et al.17 These methodological distinctions are relevant because different aspects of symbolic geometry may draw on distinct cognitive resources. Solving geometrical problems is likely to rely more heavily on general intelligence and visuospatial working memory than tasks assessing declarative geometric knowledge or geometric-specific visuospatial abilities. Moreover, differences in how visuospatial working memory and general cognitive functions are operationalized and the age range of the participants may also contribute to the divergent outcomes. Nevertheless, beyond these methodological differences, our hierarchical regression analyses align with the conclusions by Giofrè et al.17 showing that a substantial portion of the variance in symbolic geometric competence (approximately 28%), is accounted for by general cognitive abilities, at least for tests assessing declarative geometric knowledge. The influence of these general abilities on geometry-specific visuospatial abilities was smaller and not clearly significant (≈15%, p = 0.06), whereas age and non-symbolic geometry emerged as stronger predictors, accounting for 26% and 23% of the variance, respectively. Therefore, these findings suggest that the link between non-symbolic and symbolic geometry is robust, but its strength depends on the specific components of symbolic geometry assessed and on the extent to which they rely on domain-general cognitive abilities.

After examining the development of non-symbolic and symbolic geometric abilities and their relationship in neurotypical participants, the current study next investigated geometric abilities in individuals with learning disabilities. We found that difficulties in both non-symbolic and symbolic geometry characterize adults with developmental profiles in which dyscalculia is present. This is remarkable considering that none of the tasks used involve arithmetic calculations (such as solving geometric problems).

Only a few studies have investigated geometric skills in individuals with learning disabilities, namely in children with non-verbal learning disabilities21 and children with dyslexia.22

In the present study, we tested adults with a high level of education (university students), who had ample opportunities throughout their lives to develop compensatory strategies. This makes it unlikely that the observed difficulties simply reflect delayed development or limited educational exposure. This finding is particularly striking given that some other impairments associated with dyscalculia, such as reduced precision in numerosity perception, are often more evident in children and tend to disappear in adulthood,33,36,46,48 likely as a result of schooling-dependent refinement of perceptual abilities.49 Importantly, the pattern of group differences was unchanged after controlling for visuospatial working memory, indicating that the observed impairments cannot be accounted for by this domain-general ability. Individuals with dyslexia without comorbid dyscalculia were also included in the current study and this group performed similarly to neurotypical controls in all tasks. Overall, the persistence of geometric difficulties into adulthood and their largely selective occurrence in profiles involving dyscalculia, with the exception of geometry-specific visuospatial abilities, for which the dyscalculia group differed from the control but not the dyslexia group, suggests that impairments in geometric processing may represent a core and enduring feature of dyscalculia.

Finally, future studies should investigate the neural mechanisms underlying the geometric difficulties observed in the current study. In neurotypical individuals, detecting the shapes regularity,50 inferring the trajectory of geometric patterns51,52 and establishing the correctness of sentences about formal geometry53,54 recruit a broad fronto-parietal network as well as ventral visual regions.53,54 Both dorsal55,56,57,58,59,60,61 and ventral62,63,64 regions also play an important role in arithmetic and numerical processing and functional and structural abnormalities have been identified across all these areas in developmental dyscalculia.65,66 Together, these observations suggest that geometric impairments may arise from alterations in distributed fronto-parietal and ventral networks, an issue that warrants direct neuroimaging investigation.

In sum, the present findings refine current models of geometric cognition by showing that both symbolic and non-symbolic geometry continue to mature from early adolescence into adulthood and that their relationship is not simply a by-product of general cognitive development. Most importantly, they demonstrate that impairments in geometric processing characterize developmental profiles involving dyscalculia that persist in adulthood. This finding, together with the predictive association between non-symbolic and symbolic geometry observed in neurotypical participants, underscores the importance of understanding the foundations of geometry to better elucidate the cognitive mechanisms underlying geometry learning and its difficulties, thereby informing targeted educational interventions and more sensitive clinical practice.

Limitations of the study

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Children and adults completed the symbolic Geometria test under different testing conditions (group vs. individual administration), which may have contributed to age-related differences in symbolic performance. In addition, the non-symbolic tasks used slightly different response displays (an animal-shaped cursor for children and a black square for adults), and although this modification was intended to facilitate engagement in younger participants, its influence on developmental comparisons cannot be completely excluded. Moreover, adults completed the version of the Geometria test developed for adolescents because normative data for adults are not currently available. Although this may have introduced ceiling effects, adult performance nevertheless showed sufficient variability to detect individual differences. An additional limitation concerns the specificity of the observed association between symbolic and non-symbolic geometry. Although we controlled for verbal reasoning, non-verbal reasoning, visuospatial working memory and age, we did not include independent measures of non-geometric magnitude processing, such as area or continuous magnitude discrimination, which have been linked to geometric performance.67,68 Consequently, we cannot exclude that broader magnitude-related mechanisms contribute to the shared variance observed across the geometry tasks. Yet, the two non-symbolic tasks employed in the present study do not require explicit discrimination of continuous quantities such as area or length, but instead require participants to identify geometric relationships and regularities. Nevertheless, future studies should directly compare geometric and non-geometric magnitude representations to establish the degree to which the present findings reflect mechanisms that are specific to geometric processing. Finally, a further limitation concerns the relatively low reliability of the declarative geometric knowledge measure, which may have affected the strength of its association with non-symbolic geometry and warrants further investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Seventy-nine participants took part in the study, including 22 children and 57 adults.

The child sample consisted of 22 neurotypical individuals with no learning disabilities (14 females, mean age = 12.8 years, SD = 0.5, min-max: 11.1–13.9).

The adult sample comprised 25 neurotypical participants with no learning disabilities (14 females, mean age = 23.0 years, SD = 2.4), 18 with dyscalculia (16 females, mean age = 23.2 years, SD = 3.5), the majority of whom (15/18) also met criteria for dyslexia, and 14 with dyslexia but no dyscalculia (6 females, mean age = 23.5 years, SD = 2.6).

All participants were Italian and their native language was Italian.

Neurotypical children with no history of learning or medical disorders were recruited from a lower secondary school in Perugia, while neurotypical adults were recruited from university students at the University of Salento. Both control groups (children and adults) were assessed using subtests from the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC) or the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS),69 using the subtests Similarities and Matrices as indices of verbal and nonverbal reasoning respectively. All control participants were also administered the Corsi Block-Tapping Test to assess visuospatial working memory. In addition, the Mental Calculation subtest from the battery for developmental dyscalculia (children)70 or from the LSC-SUA battery (adults)71 as well as a standardized Word Reading list were administered. For organizational reasons five children did not complete the mental calculation test and other three did not complete the reading test, while all adults completed all tests. None of the neurotypical participants scored more than two standard deviations below the standardized mean on either the mental calculation or reading accuracy tests.

Adults with developmental dyscalculia and dyslexia were recruited through the learning disability screening office at the University of Salento and systematically assessed using the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS).69 The WAIS subtests Similarities and Matrices were used as indices of verbal and nonverbal reasoning, respectively, as in the neurotypical groups. Participants were also assessed with the LSC-SUA,71 the only standardized battery currently available in Italy for the diagnosis of learning disabilities in adults, assessing reading, writing and calculation skills. The calculation section includes subtests assessing number dictation, number reading, mental calculation, arithmetic facts, approximate calculation and number-to-digit transcription, whereas the reading section evaluates words, non-words and text reading accuracy and speed. Participants were classified as having developmental dyscalculia if they scored below normative values (below two standard deviations, in accordance with the Italian guidelines for the diagnosis of specific learning disorders) on at least two calculation subtests. When the same criterion was also met in the reading tests which included word and non-word reading and passage reading, participants were classified as having comorbid dyslexia. Individuals included in the dyslexia group were required to demonstrate performance below two standard deviations on at least two reading subtests and within the normal range on all calculation tests. Visuospatial working memory was also assessed using the Corsi Block-Tapping Test.

Testing on the adult sample was approved by the Ethics Committee for Research in Psychology of the University of Salento (n. 71084 of 5 May 2021) and by the Data Protection Officer (DPO) of the University.

The experimental procedures for the neurotypical children sample were approved by the local ethic committee “Comitato Universitario di Bioetica Università degli Studi di Perugia, Prot. n. 908 del 12-1-2021”.

Informed consent was obtained from all adult participants and from the parents of all child participants prior to the study.

Method details

General procedure

Children completed two separate testing sessions, each lasting approximately 1 h. In the first session, formal geometric abilities were assessed using a standardized Italian paper-and-pencil battery (Geometria Test, described below).18 Testing was conducted collectively in the classroom. Five adults (two teachers and three experimenters) were present to prevent communication between participants and provide guidance when needed.

During the second session, the same experimenters, blinded to each child’s performance on the Geometria Test, administered the non-symbolic tasks individually, with task order pseudorandomized across participants. Additionally, measures of verbal and non-verbal reasoning, calculation, reading and visuospatial working memory were collected in the same session.

Adults were tested individually within one session of about one and a half hour.

Stimuli for the non-symbolic geometry tasks were created and presented using MATLAB R2017B and PsychToolbox routines. They were displayed on 12.3″ touchscreen tablets (Microsoft Surface Pro) with a resolution of 2736 × 1824 pixels and a refresh rate of 60 Hz.

Non-symbolic geometry tests

Non-symbolic, intuitive geometry skills were assessed using adapted versions of the paradigms originally developed by Dehaene et al.2 and by Amalric et al.12 We refer to these tasks as the “geometrical odd-one-out task” and the “geometrical inference task” respectively.

Geometrical odd-one-out task

In each trial, six figures were displayed on the screen until the participant identified, by pointing, the one that differed from the others according to a specific geometric principle (Figure 1A). Over 45 slides, each containing a 3x2 array of displays, several geometrical principles were tested, the first two served as training. The geometric principles tested included topology, Euclidean geometry, geometric figure concepts, symmetry or chirality, metric properties and geometric transformations. Once the response was given, the experimenter pressed an external spacebar to record the answer. For each participant, we calculated the overall percentage of correct responses, as well as the percentage of correct responses for each geometric principle, averaged across all items assessing that principle. The internal reliability of this task was moderate (mean Spearman–Brown corrected split-half reliability: r = 0.65, 95% CI [0.40, 0.83]; see quantification and statistical analysis section for details of the estimation procedure).

Geometrical inference task

In each trial, participants viewed a symmetrical circular grid (9° diameter) containing eight gray dots, which remained visible throughout the experiment (Figure 1B). Trials tested sequences of spatial locations across two distinct blocks, referred to here as the first and second run. During the first run, a figure of an animal (for children) or of a black square (for adults) (both approximately 1.5° in size) appeared sequentially in five locations. Each appearance lasted 1000 ms, separated by a fixed 300 ms inter-stimulus interval. After these five “jumps,” the cursor disappeared, and participants were asked to indicate its location for the next three steps, continuing along the geometric path implied by the preceding sequence. Incorrect responses were signaled with a brief auditory cue and the program automatically restarted the sequence from the beginning, including the correct position for the previous error. Participants then completed the full sequence. Upon completion of the first run, the screen went blank, and the second run began after the experimenter pressed the space bar on an external keyboard. This second run was similar to the first, except that only the first three positions were cued by the program. Once finished, a new trial began, presenting a different spatial sequence. Across the experiment, eight distinct spatial configurations were presented, each associated with a complexity measure (K) reflecting the degree of geometric regularity: higher K values corresponded to lower regularity and greater complexity.12 Unlike the original study, in which sequences were randomized, the current study presented the eight spatial configurations in a fixed blocked order for all participants, progressing from simpler to more complex sequences based on difficulty measures for 5-year-olds reported in Amalric et al. (2017). The order of sequences was: repeat (K5), repeat +2 (K7), 2-arcs (K8), squares (K8), segments (K7), diagonals (K7), rectangles (K10) and crosses (K7). Following Amalric et al.,12 only a single exemplar of each sequence was presented, with the cursor moving clockwise as indicated by the arrows in Figure 1B. In contrast to the original study, in which the “4-segments” sequence was repeated four times to test all axial symmetries, it was presented only once in the present experiment. For each participant, we calculated the overall percentage of correct responses. The internal reliability of this task was moderate (mean Spearman–Brown corrected split-half reliability: r = 0.57, 95% CI [0.34, 0.77]; see quantification and statistical analysis section for details of the estimation procedure).

Symbolic geometry assessment

Participants’ symbolic geometry skills were assessed using a standardized, paper-and-pencil Italian test of geometric reasoning, the Geometria Test.18 The test includes two main subtests: one evaluating declarative geometrical knowledge (geometry knowledge subtest, 8 items) and one assessing geometric-specific visuospatial abilities (visuospatial abilities subtest, 24 items). Although these subtests rely on partially distinct cognitive processes, both were developed to assess complementary components of formal (symbolic) geometric competence acquired through schooling. Whereas the geometry knowledge subtest measures explicit knowledge of geometric concepts and terminology, the visuospatial abilities subtest evaluates the application of formally acquired geometric principles (e.g., the concept of intersection and equivalent solids) to the analysis and mental manipulation of geometric figures. The manual reports Cronbach Apha of 0.46 for the geometric knowledge task and of 0.72 for the visuospatial ability task. In the geometry knowledge subtest, participants were asked questions designed to assess their understanding of basic geometric concepts (Figure 1C). These included questions such as identifying the type of angle represented in a figure, determining when two lines intersect, classifying the type of angle shown, specifying the measures of the angles of a trapezoid, understanding when two segments are considered congruent, recognizing when two angles are supplementary, describing the geometric principle that through three non-collinear points passes a single plane, defining the value of pi and the properties of prisms, cylinders, pyramids and cones. The visuospatial abilities subtest included exercises aimed at evaluating participants’ ability to mentally manipulate and reason about geometric forms (Figure 1D). Exercises required participants to identify a figure constructed from specific blocks, mentally combine multiple figures to determine the resulting shape, visualize the folding of a sheet of paper and select the correct folded figure, count the number of cubes composing a given structure, recognize a simple figure embedded within a more complex one and locate and shade the area of intersection among multiple figures. Most items, with the exception of the last two in the visuospatial abilities section, required participants to choose the correct answer from three alternatives.

For each participant, the percentage of correct responses was calculated separately for the geometry knowledge and visuospatial subtests. The Geometria test is standardized for children up to the third grade of lower secondary school. Children completed the version appropriate for their grade level, whereas adults were administered the version designed for the highest grade, as no adult-specific version is available. Because normative data do not extend to adults, all analyses were conducted using raw scores that were not age-corrected.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses included linear mixed models, partial correlations, hierarchical regression and ANOVA, as specified in the result section. p-values for post hoc comparisons are all reported with Bonferroni correction. In addition to frequentist statistics, base-10 Log Bayes Factors (LogBFs) were computed for the correlation analyses and for the descriptive group comparisons reported in Table S4, to quantify the relative evidence for the alternative versus the null hypothesis. Conventionally, a LogBF greater than 0.5 is interpreted as providing substantial evidence for a difference between groups (supporting the alternative hypothesis), whereas a LogBF less than −0.5 indicates substantial evidence for the absence of a difference (supporting the null hypothesis). Absolute LogBF values exceeding 1 or 2 are considered to represent strong and decisive evidence, respectively.

To assess the internal reliability of the non-symbolic tasks, we estimated split-half reliability. At each of 10,000 iterations, the available trials for each participant were randomly divided into two non-overlapping halves (without replacement). Mean accuracy was computed separately for each half, yielding two accuracy estimates per participant. Pearson’s correlation between the two-halves was then calculated across participants and the resulting correlation coefficient was adjusted using the Spearman–Brown correction to estimate the reliability of the full task. Reliability was summarized as the mean Spearman–Brown corrected coefficient across iterations together with its 95% bootstrap confidence interval (2.5th–97.5th percentiles).

Analyses were conducted with MATLAB R2017B (https://it.mathworks.com), SPSS v.25 (https://www.ibm.com) and JASP (version 0.8.6, The JASP Team, 2020, https://jasp-stats.org). Figures were generated using OriginPro 2015 (https://www.originlab.com).
