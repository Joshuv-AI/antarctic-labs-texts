Functional compartmentalization of the anconeus: A three‐dimensional study of muscle morphology and innervation patterns
Authors: Jocelynn G McGee, Catherine Amara, John Tran, Anne M R Agur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe function of the anconeus has been previously described for the muscle as a whole. No studies were found that explored the functional neuromuscular partitioning based on 3D muscle architecture and innervation patterns, which is paramount to understanding changes in pathological muscle. The purpose of this study was to investigate 3D muscle morphology and intramuscular innervation of the anconeus, quantify architectural parameters, and propose functional implications of the findings. The fiber bundles, aponeuroses, and intramuscular innervation of the anconeus (n = 8 embalmed specimens, mean age 81 ± 12 years) were serially dissected, digitized, and modeled in 3D. Muscle morphology and intramuscular innervation patterns were determined and architectural parameters computed. The 3D models and data were used to assess muscular partitioning and propose functional implications of the findings. The anconeus was found to be morphologically partitioned into three parts, proximal, distal, and deep, based on fiber bundle orientation, attachment sites, and architectural parameters. The nerve to anconeus supplied the proximal part and underlying deep part. The nerve bifurcated to supply the distal part via medial and lateral branches or superficial and deep branches. In conclusion, the proximal part could act as an abductor of the ulna, the distal part as an initial elbow extensor, and deep part as a stabilizer and tensor of the elbow joint capsule. The in‐depth 3D mapping of the anconeus will enable the development of in vivo ultrasound protocols to provide further insight into the in vivo functions of the three parts of the anconeus.Keywords: anconeus, intramuscular innervation, muscle architecture, three‐dimensional modeling

INTRODUCTION

The anconeus is a small, triangular muscle located on the posterior aspect of the elbow. The muscle attaches proximally to the lateral epicondyle and distally to the olecranon and posterior surface of the ulna (Dalley II et al., 2023). Functionally, the anconeus has been described as a muscle that stabilizes the elbow, abducts the ulna during pronation of the forearm, and assists the triceps brachii to extend the elbow joint (Basmajian & Griffin, 1972; Dalley II et al., 2023; Ray et al., 1951). Functional and EMG studies have not investigated the anconeus muscle function beyond the whole muscle level. To gain an in‐depth understanding of the functions of the anconeus, it must be determined whether the muscle is neuromuscularly partitioned. Neuromuscular partitioning is present if a region of a muscle has a distinct intramuscular innervation and/or muscle architecture (English et al., 1993). Knowledge of neuromuscular partitioning is essential for identifying functional differences between parts of a muscle, which can assist in interpreting pathological changes. Previous studies of the anconeus muscle morphology, architecture, and intramuscular innervation were not volumetric and did not delineate neuromuscular partitioning. The current study is unique as it investigated the muscle morphology, architecture, and intramuscular innervation throughout the muscle volume using digitization and three‐dimensional (3D) modeling. The collection of Cartesian coordinate data of fiber bundles, aponeuroses, and intramuscular nerves enabled 3D mapping of the anconeus and volumetric measurements of architectural parameters in 3D space.

Muscle architecture has defined parameters which are determinants of function. These include fiber bundle length (FBL), an indicator of relative muscle excursion capability, pennation angle (PA), a factor in relative force generating capability, and physiological cross‐sectional area (PCSA), an indicator of relative force generating capability (Gans & De Vree, 1987; Li et al., 2014; Shaw et al., 2017). There are few studies of the anconeus that have quantified architectural parameter(s) (Bergin et al., 2013; Coriolano et al., 2009; Grewal et al., 2019; Molinier et al., 2011; Ng et al., 2012; Pereira, 2013). If architectural parameters were quantified, they were not volumetric and used two‐dimensional (2D) superficial fiber bundle sampling, reported for the muscle as a whole. Pereira (2013) measured mean FBL and PA at the anterior (FBL 19.5 mm; PA 30–45°) and posterior (FBL 31.7 mm; PA 0°) margins of the muscle, whereas Ng et al. (2012) reported a mean PA of 71 ± 12°. However, PCSA of the anconeus was not reported. Without assessing three‐dimensional (3D) architecture, it is difficult to determine if the anconeus is divided into functionally distinct parts.

Studies investigating innervation of the anconeus have focused on the extramuscular course and branches to their entry point into the muscle belly. No intramuscular innervation dissection studies were found, with the exception of one Sihler's staining study. In this study, Pereira (2013) used Sihler's staining to clear the muscle fiber bundles, exposing the intramuscular innervation within the muscle volume. Using this method, it is not possible to localize the intramuscular nerves relative to fiber bundle architecture, which is required for determining neuromuscular partitioning.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to: (1) dissect, digitize, and model in 3D the muscle morphology and intramuscular innervation of the anconeus; (2) quantify FBL, PA, and PCSA; and (3) assess neuromuscular partitioning and propose functional implications of the findings.

METHODS

Eight formalin‐embalmed cadaveric specimens of the elbow region with a mean age of 79 ± 11 years were used in this study. Specimens with evidence of musculoskeletal deformity and/or pathology/previous surgery were excluded. Approval was received from the Health Science Research Ethics Board, University of Toronto (Protocol number: 27210).

Each specimen was serially dissected, digitized, and modeled in 3D. Digitization was carried out using a MicroScribe™ Digitizer with an accuracy of 0.05 mm (Immersion Corporation, San Jose, CA, USA). Fiber bundle digitization has a high intra‐ and inter‐reliability of 0.926 and 0.989, respectively (Bradshaw, 2018). The specimens were photographed and laser scanned (Faro® Laser ScanArm; FARO Technologies, Lake Mary, FL) throughout dissection and digitization.

The skin and subcutaneous tissue were removed, and the specimen was stabilized with metal plates in the embalmed position (anatomical position with the elbow joint extended and the forearm in supination). Three screws were placed in bony landmarks (lateral epicondyle, olecranon, and posterior ulna) to enable reconstruction of digitized data.

The fiber bundles and intramuscular innervation were dissected and digitized simultaneously. First, the extramuscular portion of the nerve to anconeus (NtoA) was dissected to its entry point into the muscle belly. Next, the fiber bundles on the posterolateral surface of the anconeus were exposed and delineated between attachment sites and subsequently digitized. The digitized fiber bundles were carefully removed to reveal the NtoA and its branches. The NtoA and its visible branches were then digitized. The digitized nerves were removed to reveal the underlying fiber bundles. This process was repeated throughout the entire muscle volume.

The digitized and laser scanned data of each specimen were imported into AutoDesk Maya (AutoDesk Inc., San Rafael, CA) and reconstructed into 3D models using plug‐ins developed in the laboratory. The models were used to determine if the anconeus was compartmentalized into different parts based on musculoaponeurotic geometry and intramuscular innervation. The entry point of NtoA into the superior aspect of the muscle belly, located on a line between the lateral epicondyle and olecranon, was quantified using the Maya measurement tool (AutoDesk Inc., San Rafael, CA).

FBL, PA, and PCSA were quantified using software developed in the laboratory (Lee et al., 2015). Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviations, range) were calculated for each parameter and compared between specimens. A one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess significant differences in FBL, PA, and PCSA between the partitions of the anconeus. If a one‐way ANOVA demonstrated a significant difference, a pairwise two‐tailed T‐test was conducted (p < 0.05).

The musculoaponeurotic morphology, architectural parameters, and intramuscular innervation were used to determine if muscular partitioning was present and to propose functional implications of the findings.

RESULTS

Muscle morphology

There was variability in the size of the anconeus muscle in each specimen. The total number of fiber bundles digitized per specimen ranged from 179 to 750, which reflects the difference in observed muscle volume (range: 3390.3–11517.2 mm3). This was a time‐consuming process that enabled 3D volumetric visualization of musculoaponeurotic morphology (Figure 1).

(c) DeP and deep portion of DiP. (d) DeP. Other parts removed. Blue dotted line: Joint capsule attachment to the lateral epicondyle. Pink dotted line: Joint capsule attachment to the proximal ulna.

The anconeus attached proximally to the lateral epicondyle of the humerus, and distally to the lateral surface of the olecranon and proximal part of the posterior surface of the ulna. The anconeus was found to be partitioned into three parts: proximal (PP), distal (DiP), and deep (DeP), based on fiber bundle orientation, attachment sites, and architectural parameters (Figure 1).

The PP attached superiorly to the lateral epicondyle of the humerus, and inferiorly to the superficial lateral surface of the olecranon and superior part of the posterior border of the ulna (Figure 1a). The fiber bundles were transversely orientated between attachment sites. The PP and the proximal fiber bundles of the DiP were located superficial to the DeP (Figure 1a,c). The DeP attached proximally to the elbow joint capsule and distally to the interosseus membrane and to the superior part of the posterior border of the ulna, deep to the PP and proximal DiP (Figure 1d). The fiber bundles of the DeP had a transverse trajectory superiorly and a more oblique trajectory inferiorly.

The DiP was the largest part of the muscle and was located inferior to the PP (Figure 1a). The DiP had an extensive aponeurosis that converged to its attachment on the lateral epicondyle (Figure 1b). Proximally, the aponeurosis was superficial with fiber bundles attaching to its deep surface. More distally, the aponeurosis became intramuscular and had fiber bundles attaching to both surfaces (Figure 1a–).

The DiP was divided into superficial and deep divisions based on their attachment to the aponeurosis. The fiber bundles of the superficial division (SDiP) attached to the superficial surface of the aponeurosis, while the fiber bundles of the deep division (DDiP) attached to its deep surface (Figure 1b,c). The proximal attachment of both divisions of the DiP was to the lateral epicondyle via the aponeurosis. Both divisions had a distal attachment to the posterior border of the ulna, inferior to the attachments of the PP. The fiber bundles of the DiP became progressively more oblique from superior to inferior.

The mean PA of the DiP was less than that of the PP and DeP, whereas the SDiP and the DDiP had similar mean PA (Figure 2b). The PP, DiP, and DeP were all significantly different from each other (Table 1). The mean PA of the SDiP and DDiP was not significantly different (Table 2). The large standard deviation of mean PA was likely due to differences in fiber bundle orientation within each part.

The DiP, as a whole, had the greatest mean PCSA in comparison to the PP and DeP (Figure 2c). The mean PCSA of the SDiP was significantly greater than the DDiP. The DiP as a whole had significantly different mean PCSA from the PP and DeP. The PP and DeP were similar in mean PCSA and not significantly different.

Intramuscular innervation

All three parts of the anconeus were innervated by the nerve to anconeus (NtoA), a branch of the radial nerve, that traveled intramuscularly through the medial head of triceps brachii, emerging at its inferomedial border. The NtoA then entered the muscle belly of the anconeus at the level of a line connecting the lateral epicondyle and olecranon (Figure 3a). The nerve entry point was found to be 2.3 ± 0.2 cm from the olecranon.

(d and e) Nerve models of medial‐lateral branching pattern. Anterior, Ant; Lateral, Lat; Lateral epicondyle, LE; Medial, Med; Olecranon, O; Posterior, Post; Posterior border of Ulna, PU.

On entry into the muscle belly of the anconeus, the NtoA traveled inferiorly in a fascial plane in between the PP and DeP (Figures 3b,c, 4 and 5a). As the nerve coursed in the fascial plane, it gave off superficial branches to the PP and deep branches to the DeP. Upon entering the DiP, the NtoA bifurcated into two branches, both innervating the DiP. Two different innervation patterns of the DiP were observed: medial and lateral branches or superficial and deep branches (Figures 3 and 5). In the first pattern, the medial and lateral branches lay in the same intramuscular plane, both branches innervating the SDiP and DDiP (Figures 3c–e and 4c). In the second pattern, the superficial branch innervated the SDiP, while the deep branch innervated the DDiP (Figures 4a,b and 5c–e).

DISCUSSION

The technique of 3D digitization and modeling facilitated a detailed analysis of the anconeus architecture and innervation patterns. The volumetric approach of collecting data at the fiber bundle and aponeurotic level enabled the exploration of functional neuromuscular partitioning of the anconeus.

Morphology

In the current study, the anconeus was found to be partitioned into three parts: proximal (PP), distal (DiP), and deep (DeP), based on the spatial arrangement of the attachment of fiber bundles. Bergin et al. (2013) partitioned the anconeus into transverse (AT) and longitudinal (AL) parts based on superficial fiber bundle orientation, whereas Coriolano et al. (2009) partitioned the anconeus into four parts: proximal, medial, distal, and terminal, to measure the length of fiber bundle superficially. Previous studies have not identified partitioning of the anconeus based on musculoaponeurotic attachment sites. Commonly, the anconeus has been described as a small, triangular muscle spanning between the lateral epicondyle and the posterior border of the ulna (Coriolano et al., 2009; Dalley II & Agur, 2023; Jiménez‐Díaz et al., 2021; Molinier et al., 2011; Pereira, 2013; Standring, 2005).

Three studies were found that reported the presence of a tendinous aponeurosis. Pereira (2013) described an aponeurosis that spanned 70% of the muscle length and Coriolano et al. (2009) found a “tendinous expansion” to cover 2/3 of the lateral inferior border. The aponeurosis was found to provide attachment sites for muscle fiber bundles in all three studies (Coriolano et al., 2009; Molinier et al., 2011; Pereira, 2013). In the current study, the SDiP and DDiP were found to attach to the superficial and deep surfaces of the aponeurosis, respectively. The PP and DeP did not have aponeurotic attachment.

Architectural parameters

In the anatomical studies found in the previous literature, only two architectural parameters, FBL and PA, were quantified for the anconeus as a whole. Fiber bundles were sampled from the superficial surface of the muscle, but not volumetrically.

Two studies, one anatomical and one ultrasound, were found that quantified FBL. Coriolano et al. (2009) in the anatomical study measured FBL using a ruler in four parts of the anconeus: proximal, medial, distal, and terminal. The mean FBL of the proximal part was 22 ± 3 mm (2.2 ± 0.3 cm), the medial part 30 ± 4 mm (3.0 ± 0.4 cm), the distal part 22 ± 4 mm (2.2 ± 0.4 cm), and the terminal part 27 ± 8 mm (2.7 ± 0.8 cm). In the current study, the neuromuscular partitions of the anconeus had mean FBL ranging from 18.8 ± 5.4 mm (DiP) to 24.3 ± 6.4 mm (PP). In the ultrasound study, Stevens et al. (2014) reported a greater mean FBL (38 ± 7 mm) for the anconeus at rest compared with both the current study and the findings of Coriolano et al. (2009), which were measured in cadaveric specimens.

Ng et al. (2012) in a study of functional anconeus free flap for thenar reconstruction, quantified PA at four sites on the superficial surface of the anconeus using digital photographs of the specimens. The mean PA was 71 ± 12°. In the current volumetric study where up to 750 fiber bundles were digitized per specimen, the mean PA of the anconeus was found to be 20.96 ± 13.01°. The large standard deviation indicated diverse fiber bundle orientation within the muscle volume.

Further in vivo study of the architectural parameters/morphology of the anconeus at rest and in pronation and extension of the elbow is needed to provide insight into the function of the PP, DeP and DiP, and the SDiP and DDiP.

Innervation

In the literature, the NtoA entry point has been consistently localized at the proximal border of the muscle belly, at the level of the olecranon and lateral epicondyle (Jiménez‐Díaz et al., 2021; Maniglio et al., 2022). Maniglio et al. (2022) used a line between the olecranon and lateral epicondyle to measure the nerve entry point. Relative to this line, the nerve entry point was found to be 57% from the olecranon, whereas in the current study the nerve entry point was 46.8 ± 5.9% (23 ± 2 mm) from the olecranon.

In the previous literature, the intramuscular innervation of the anconeus has only been studied using Sihler's staining. Sihler's staining renders the soft tissues transparent and stains the nerves within the muscle volume (Mu & Sanders, 2009). In total, four anconeus specimens have been stained, two in a study by Lim et al. (2004), and two in Pereira (2013). Lim et al. (2004) reported that “the main intramuscular nerve trunk runs perpendicular to the direction of the muscle fibers giving off side branches that run parallel to the fibers”. Both Pereira (2013) and the current study reported one main intramuscular nerve that coursed distally in the center of the muscle belly that divided into two branches at its mid‐point. Since the muscle tissue had been made transparent using the Sihler's technique, it was not possible to trace the intramuscular nerves relative to muscle partitions and fascial planes.

In the current study, the NtoA coursed in a fascial plane, between the PP and DeP, sending muscular branches anteriorly to the DeP and posteriorly to the PP. On entering the DiP, the NtoA was found to bifurcate into either superficial and deep branches or medial and lateral branches. Pereira (2013) reported the presence of two branches, one superficial and one deep. In the current study, when superficial and deep branches were present, the superficial branch was found to innervate the SDiP, and the deep branch the DDiP. When medial and lateral branches were present, the medial branch innervated the DiP medially, while the lateral branch innervated the DiP laterally.

The current 3D anatomic study is unique, as it provided precise localization of the innervation of the anconeus within the muscle volume. Also, the delineation of the intramuscular innervation relative to the parts of the anconeus was possible.

Functional implications of muscular partitioning

Functionally, previous studies have focused on the anconeus as a whole. In early EMG studies, Ray et al. (1951) concluded that the anconeus is an abductor of the ulna during pronation of the forearm, whereas Basmajian and Griffin (1972) emphasized the role of the anconeus as a stabilizer rather than a pronator. Gleason et al. (1985) in another EMG study found an increase in the amplitude of individual motor unit action potentials and recruitment of additional action potentials on lateral movement, that is, abduction of the ulna. The contribution of the anconeus during extension of the elbow joint has been reported to be minimal and described as an “accessory function” (Coriolano et al., 2009; Miguel‐Andres et al., 2017; Molinier et al., 2011). Pereira (2013) further found that the function of the anconeus as an elbow extensor “decreases in influence with increasing elbow flexion angles”. Coriolano et al. (2009) investigated the anatomic and architectural characteristics of the anconeus. Based on a design index calculation, it was suggested that the anconeus was primarily a force producing muscle. Pereira (2013) found two neuromuscular compartments that were postulated to have different functional characteristics. The posterior and deep aspects were described as having a role in initial elbow extension and the anterior superficial aspect providing posterolateral stability to the elbow joint.

In the current study, the morphology and architectural parameters suggest that the three parts of the anconeus have distinct functional characteristics. The PP was found to have the greatest mean FBL and the lowest mean PCSA, suggesting greatest excursion capability. The architectural parameters and horizontal fiber bundle orientation suggest that the PP could be an abductor of the ulna during pronation of the forearm. The PP and DeP both had attachments to the elbow joint capsule; however, the DeP, located deep to the PP, occupied the largest area of attachment, which continued to the olecranon and proximal posterior border of the ulna. The mean PCSA and mean FBL of the DeP were between that of the PP and DiP, suggesting this part could function primarily as a stabilizer and a tensor of the joint capsule to prevent impingement between the bony surfaces of the elbow joint on extension. In contrast, the distal part had the greatest mean PCSA and the lowest mean FBL. The DiP was the only part that had fiber bundle attachment to an aponeurosis, increasing the surface area for fiber bundle attachment, as fiber bundles attached to both anterior and posterior surfaces of the aponeurosis. These findings suggest that the DiP was the primary force generator, resulting in initial weak elbow extension.

The mean FBL of SDiP was 0.7 mm shorter than the DDiP, indicating that both subdivisions had similar excursion capabilities. However, the SDiP had a greater mean PCSA than the DDiP, suggesting that the SDiP had a greater relative force generating capability than the DDiP. These data suggest that both parts will act together through the aponeurosis to aid in elbow extension.

Studies of elbow joint pain are scarce in comparison to other peripheral joints. Gangatharam (2021) proposed in a case report that anconeus syndrome could be a potential cause for lateral elbow pain. Knowledge of anconeus morphology and innervation is necessary to understand changes in pathologic muscle.

The results of the current study can be used to develop an in vivo ultrasound protocol to assess the architectural parameters/morphology of the anconeus at rest and in contracted states during pronation of the forearm and elbow extension. The results of this study will provide insight into the in vivo function of the PP, DiP, and DeP, as well as the SDiP and DDiP.

Limitations

One limitation of the current study is the use of embalmed cadaveric specimens which may have differences in muscle architecture parameters such as PCSA and FBL when compared with live tissue. However, in previous publications it was reported that the differences in embalmed and live tissue are minimal (Anderson, 2006; Coleman & Kogan, 1998). Future research may be necessary to assess potential differences between different specimen preservation techniques.

CONCLUSION

Dissection, digitization, and 3D modeling enabled volumetric analysis of the intramuscular innervation patterns, muscle morphology, and architectural parameters of the anconeus. The anconeus was found to have three parts, each with distinct morphology and architectural parameters, suggesting differing functional characteristics. The PP may act as an abductor of the ulna during pronation of the forearm, the DiP could functionally assist the triceps in initial elbow joint extension, and the DeP could function primarily as a stabilizer and a tensor of the elbow joint capsule. The findings of the current study provide an evidence‐based foundation for the development of an in vivo ultrasound protocol to investigate the morphology and architectural parameters of the three parts of the anconeus at rest, during pronation of the forearm and extension of the elbow. The in vivo ultrasound protocols and normative data could provide a baseline to study morphologic and architectural changes resulting from anconeus muscle pathology.
