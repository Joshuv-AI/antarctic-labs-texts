Human-specific sequence features in HTT exon 1 promote toxic misprocessing via splicing factor SRSF7
Authors: Camilla Maffezzini, Raffaele Iennaco, Andrea Scolz, Simone Maestri, Christian Landles, Georgina F. Osborne, Marta M. Bronzini, Camilla Trovesi, Thomas Carzaniga, Luca Casiraghi, Jimena Quiros Ramirez, Thomas F. Vogt, Dan P. Felsenfeld, Tommaso Bellini, Chiara Zuccato, Gillian P. Bates, Elena Cattaneo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Expansion of CAG repeats in HTT exon 1 is the acknowledged driver of Huntington’s disease. Alternative processing of HTT pre-mRNA generates the truncated HTT1a transcript, translated into a toxic peptide. While its dependence on CAG length is well documented, the role of adjacent sequences - particularly the Proline-Rich Domain (PRD) - remains unexplored. Using our HuntEx1-engineered mouse embryonic stem cell platform, we show that human PRD promotes HTT1a production, whereas its replacement with mouse PRD in an otherwise human exon 1 markedly reduces HTT1a levels. Mechanistically, we find that the PRD shapes mRNA structure, and motif analysis identifies Serine-Arginine Splicing Factor 7 (SRSF7) binding sites in mouse but not in human PRD. Their targeted mutation confirms SRSF7’s regulatory role in suppressing HTT1a production. Our findings establish the PRD as a key cis-regulator of HTT1a biogenesis, demonstrating that HTT toxicity also depends on sequence context, and highlighting splicing-based, PRD-focused therapeutic avenues.

Introduction

Since the discovery of the genetic determinant of Huntington’s disease (HD), research has primarily focused on the expansion-prone poly-glutamine (polyQ) tract encoded by exon 1 of the huntingtin gene (HTT), which comprises 67 exons1. Numerous studies have investigated how the expanded CAG tract promotes cellular dysfunction2–5. However, the contribution of the flanking sequences to disease pathogenesis has received comparatively little attention. Exon 1 of HTT consists of three distinct domains: an N-terminal sequence of 17 amino acids (N17)6, the expandable polyQ stretch and a proline-rich domain (PRD) characterised by an interrupted run of prolines7, followed by a short C-terminal sequence.

In the presence of a pathological CAG repeat, aberrant splicing at the exon 1-exon 2 junction occurs, leading to the production of HTT1a transcript8–11. This transcript includes the 5′UTR, exon 1, and a portion of intron 1, and terminates at cryptic polyadenylation (polyA) sites8,9. It is subsequently translated into the HTT1a protein, which is highly aggregation-prone and contributes to transcriptional and neuronal dysfunction (Supplementary Fig. 1a)12–15.

Although the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely defined, HTT1a production strongly correlates with CAG repeat length9,10,16. CAG expansion directly disrupts splicing across multiple model systems17–20, including knock-in mice8,11,21 and human samples9,16, where HTT1a levels increase with polyQ length. This is coupled to a reduction in full-length HTT transcripts, shifting expression toward the toxic HTT1a protein22.

In recent years, studies based on mutant HTT over-expression in cellular models have highlighted a role for the PRD in HTT aggregation7,23–25 and axonal transport26. Notably, in a genetically precise HD cell system, the human PRD sequence enhances mutant HTT-driven cellular phenotypes, while the mouse PRD mitigates these effects27. Moreover, studies in human populations have identified single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) at the polyQ-PRD boundary that modulate age of onset and CAG repeat instability28–30. Together, these findings implicate the PRD in clinically relevant aspects of HD and raise the still unexplored question of its role in HTT1a production and polyA site activation at the HTT locus.

Here, we address this gap through a structure-function study using a broad panel of engineered mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), carrying distinct exon 1 variants. Our aim is to elucidate how mutant HTT exon 1 sequences influence splicing regulation at the locus and to identify candidate splicing factors involved in this deleterious process.

Results

Human HTT exon 1 mis-splicing is regulated by the PRD sequence

Among HD mouse models, those harbouring a human N-terminal fragment (e.g., R6/2 and N171-82), or a human exon 1 embedded within the mouse HTT locus (e.g., CAG140 or the widely used zQ175 mouse model), exhibit earlier symptom onset12,31,32 compared with knock-in mice carrying a comparable CAG repeat length within an otherwise mouse exon 133–36. To test whether the increased toxicity of human exon 1 is driven by the PRD – where human and mouse exon 1 primarily diverge1,35 through promotion of aberrant splicing and increased HTT1a production, we employed our HuntEx1 stem cell platform, based on Recombinase-Mediated Cassette Exchange (RMCE) technology36.

This consists of a panel of engineered mESC lines, each harbouring a distinct HTT exon 1 allele in heterozygosity within an otherwise identical genetic background. The system exploits both the well-established proliferation and differentiation properties of stem cell cultures, as well as the rapid and highly efficient RMCE exchange. Together, these features enable flexible comparative gene-function studies in a mouse context and allow direct comparison with established animal models. The RMCE cassette includes the mouse Htt 5’UTR, mouse Htt or human HTT exon 1, and the first 2150 bp of mouse intron 1, and was inserted into two foundational cell lines: one retaining a wild-type mouse allele (RMCE/+), and the other null for Htt (RMCE/-).

We focused on a subset of the HuntEx1 cell lines (Fig. 1a), comprising a mouse allelic lines (m7Q/+, m48Q/+, m72Q/+, m107Q/+, in which exon 1 is mouse-derived) and a humanized allelic series (h7Q/+, h20Q/+, h45Q/+, h72Q/+, in which exon 1 is human-derived), as well as chimeric lines in which the PRD domain was exchanged between species. These include m7QhP/+, m72QhP/+, m104QhP/+ lines (carrying a human PRD within mouse exon 1), and h7QmP/+, h20QmP/+, h72QmP/+ lines (carrying a mouse PRD within the human exon 1). Following confirmation of pluripotency marker expression (OCT3/4 and SOX2; Supplementary Fig. 1b), we quantified HTT1a levels in proliferating cells by qPCR.

In the mouse allelic series, Htt1a remained stable across CAG lengths (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1c, grey columns). In contrast, human exon 1 lines exhibited an increase in HTT1a with CAG expansion (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1c, blue columns). Notably, at comparable CAG lengths (e.g. 72Q), human exon 1 drove significantly more HTT1a than its mouse counterpart, implicating flanking domains in splicing regulation (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data 2 for full statistical analysis, compare m72Q/+ with h72Q/+).

Given the low sequence conservation of the PRD (Supplementary Fig. 1d), we replaced the human PRD with the mouse version (generating the h7QmP/+, h20QmP/+, h72QmP/+ lines; “PRD swap” in Fig. 1a), which reduced HTT1a levels to those observed in mouse alleles (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1c, blue-grey columns; Supplementary Data 2 for full statistical analysis). Conversely, insertion of the human PRD into mouse exon 1 (generating the m7QhP/+, m72QhP/+, m104QhP/+ lines, Fig. 1a) did not increase Htt1a (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1c, grey-blue columns; Supplementary Data 2 for full statistical analysis), suggesting that the human PRD contributes to alternative processing but requires its native sequence context. Overall, these findings indicate that multiple regions within huntingtin exon 1 contribute to HTT1a generation, with the human PRD playing a key role in splicing regulation.

To exclude differential full-length Htt expression as a confounder, we measured full-length transcripts using primers targeting exons 3 and 6 (Supplementary Fig. 1e), as well as total Htt transcripts using primers in the 5’UTR that detect both Htt1a and full-length Htt (Supplementary Fig. 1f). No significant differences in expression were observed among lines (Supplementary Data 2 for full statistical analysis). Consistent with the transcript data, western blotting using the fragment-specific S830 antibody37 confirmed increased HTT1a in h72Q/+ relative to m72Q/+, which was reduced in the h72QmP/+ line (Fig. 1c, d). Notably, this assay also detected a significant increase in Htt1a in the m104QhP/+ line compared with m107Q/+, while no signal was detected in lines carrying wild-type CAG tracts (m7Q/+ and h20Q/+ lines; Fig. 1c, d).

To control for species-specific antibody recognition, we employed Homogeneous Time Resolved Fluorescence (HTRF) assays using the 2B7-MW8 antibody pair38, which detect the N-terminus and the final eight amino acids of HTT exon 1, respectively. MW8 is a neo-epitope antibody to HTT1a that requires exposure of the C-terminal proline, whose specificity has been well characterised37,38. The results (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Data 3 for full statistical analysis) largely confirmed the S830 western blot findings and closely matched those obtained using intron 1-targeting primers. Collectively, these data indicate that the observed signal reflects differential pre-mRNA processing, rather than proteolytic cleavage of HTT protein. This distinction is important as it underscores the contribution of DNA/RNA sequence features, particularly within the PRD, to HD pathology.

To further assess full-length Htt expression, we performed HTRF with MAB5490 and D7F7 antibodies, which revealed minor differences unrelated to the exon 1-specific effects. This suggests that, similar to transcription, translation of full-length HTT is not affected by the cassette exchanges (Supplementary Fig. 1g and Supplementary Data 3 for full statistical analysis). Additional HTRF assays using alternative antibody combinations confirmed the presence of mouse or human PRDs in the respective chimeric lines (2B7-CHDI-90001414, Supplementary Fig. 2a; 2B7-4C9, Supplementary Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data 3 for full statistical analysis). Moreover, the 2B7-MW1 assay showed increased mutant HTT levels in lines carrying expanded CAG repeats, with the strongest signal observed in h72Q/+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 2c and Supplementary Data 3 for full statistical analysis), consistent with the higher binding affinity of the MW1 antibody for HTT fragments with expanded polyQ tracts39.

Together, these findings support a role for the PRD in regulating HTT1a production. Importantly, the observation that the human PRD alone, in an otherwise mouse exon 1, is not sufficient to increase Htt1a levels suggests a contribution from surrounding human exon 1 sequences.

To this end, we investigated whether the sequence-dependent regulation of HTT1a observed in proliferating stem cells also is maintained in disease-relevant neuronal models. We first investigated transcriptomic data from days in vitro (DIV) 28 m7Q/+, h72Q/+, h72QmP/+ neuronal cultures to detect reads mapping to HTT intron 1 (Supplementary Fig. 3a). As the dataset comprises poly-adenylated mRNAs, intron 1 reads are likely derived from alternatively processed transcripts rather than nascent RNA. Genome-wide coverage showed no global differences in Htt expression across lines (Supplementary Fig. 3b). However, focused analysis of reads spanning exon 1 to exon 2 showed increased intron 1 signal in h72Q/+ neurons, but not in h72QmP/+ neurons (Supplementary Fig. 3c and Fig. 2b). Reads mapping to intron 2 did not show a similar pattern (Supplementary Fig. 3d), supporting the notion that human HTT exon 1 specifically promotes activation of a polyA site within intron 1. Substitution of the human PRD with the mouse sequence instead favoured exon 1-exon 2 splicing.

Consistent with transcriptomic data, qPCR analysis on DIV28 neurons confirmed elevated HTT1a levels in lines carrying human exon 1, which were reduced upon replacement with the mouse PRD (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Data 2 for full statistical analysis). In contrast, lines carrying mouse exon 1 showed no significant changes in Htt1a expression, regardless of PRD identity (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Data 2 for full statistical analysis). Levels of full-length Htt (Supplementary Fig. 3e) remained unchanged, excluding global expression differences as a cause of altered HTT1a levels (Supplementary Data 2 for full statistical analysis).

Western blot using the S830 antibody mirrored transcript-level results, with HTT1a protein levels corresponding to HTT1a transcript abundance (Fig. 2d, e).

Quantification by HTRF of HTT1a (2B7-MW8, Fig. 2f, Supplementary Data 3 for full statistical analysis) and full-length Htt (D7F7-MAB5490, Supplementary Fig. 3f and Supplementary Data 3 for full statistical analysis) showed no significant differences across neuronal lines, despite clear differences in proliferating cells. This may reflect limited statistical power or the selective loss of neurons expressing higher levels of the toxic HTT1a protein.

To determine whether increased HTT1a expression correlates with neuronal phenotypes, we designed an shRNA (sh534) targeting an intronic region near the cryptic polyA sites in mouse intron 1. We transduced m7Q/+ and h72Q/+ proliferating cultures with either sh534 or a non-targeting construct, followed by differentiation for 28 DIV (Fig. 2g). qPCR confirmed elevated HTT1a levels in h72Q/+ control cultures and efficient HTT1a knockdown in sh534-treated cells, with no effect on full-length Htt, confirming shRNA specificity (Fig. 2h). Morphological analysis of MAP2-positive neurons showed significantly reduced total dendritic length (TDL) in HD cultures with control shRNA, consistent with published phenotypes27,41. Notably, sh534 treatment rescued these dendritic deficits, directly implicating HTT1a in dendritic pathology (Fig. 2i, j and Supplementary Data 4).

In this experimental context, these data demonstrate that HTT1a contributes to neuronal toxic phenotypes and reinforce the clinical relevance of its production.

Putative SRSF7 binding is PRD-specific and linked to mRNA structure

To identify candidate splicing factors that may differentially bind HTT exon 1 sequences, we used two publicly available computational tools – RBPsuite42 and RBPmap43 – to predict consensus RNA-Binding Protein (RBP) sites within the full HTT1a transcript expressed in our cell lines. This sequence included the murine Htt 5’UTR, exon 1 (either mouse or human, both with 72 CAGs), and a portion of murine intron 1 extending to the predicted polyA sites (Fig. 3a). We first analysed the outputs from each tool independently and then combined them, yielding 321 predicted RBPs, some shared between the tools, and others unique (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Data 5). At low stringency, many predicted binding sites clustered within the PRD region of both human and mouse exon 1 sequences (Supplementary Data 5). For further analysis, we focused on 57 RBPs predicted by both methods. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that these proteins are predominantly involved in splicing and mRNA processing (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The predicted RBPs were largely conserved across species, differing primarily in the location of their binding sites (Supplementary Data 5). Due to the relatively short exon 1 and extended intron 1, most consensus sites mapped to intronic regions. To refine these predictions and map RBPs binding sites, with a focus on SRSF7, we integrated them with mRNA secondary structure models for both mouse Htt1a (from murine 5’UTR to the second cryptic polyA site) and human HTT1a (from human 5’UTR to the first cryptic polyA site, Supplementary Fig. 4b, c).

Messenger RNA secondary structure is influenced by sequence variations, which can alter the likelihood of cryptic polyA site activation and, consequently, impact splicing outcomes44–46.

To test this hypothesis, we used an established RNA secondary structure prediction tool RNAfold47 – to model base pairing interactions within 5’UTR-Htt exon 1 sequences. These sequences varied in polyQ length, and either the exon 1 backbone or the PRD domain (Fig. 4a). Predictive and experimental data have previously been reported for human HTT exon 1, showing that the PRD is not affected by CAG length48. Building on this evidence, we assessed sequence variations in polyQ length, and either the exon 1 backbone or the PRD domain (Fig. 4a). Predictions from RNAfold confirmed previously reported strong base pairing within the CAG repeat region48,49, which was independent of exon 1 species. In addition, the PRD domain emerged as a key modulator of overall mRNA folding. Mountain plots describing positional entropy along the nucleotide sequence indicated that higher entropy corresponds to more flexible structures capable of adopting multiple conformations. Entropy was elevated at the polyQ domain regardless of species, suggesting CAG repeats are conformationally dynamic and – though to a lesser extent – also associated with the human PRD (Supplementary Fig. 5a). PRD swap experiments supported these predictions: insertion of the human PRD into m72Q promoted base pairing with the polyQ region (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 5e), whereas insertion of the mouse PRD in h72Q abolished this interaction (Fig. 4e). These base-pairing patterns were driven primarily by the PRD sequence.

It has been reported that RBPs preferentially bind single-stranded RNA50, and that SRSF7 cannot interact with CNNC motifs when they are base-paired51. We therefore revisited our RBP consensus sites predictions and identified one putative SRSF7 site in the mouse PRD (sequence: CACCAC), predicted to reside in a loop region, and thus potentially more accessible (Fig. 4b), and one site in the human N17 (sequence: GGCGAC), embedded within a helical structure and base-paired with the 5’UTR, likely reducing its accessibility (Fig. 4c)52. In cells carrying PRD swaps, these predictions changed accordingly: no SRSF7 consensus site was detected in lines combining murine exon 1 with the human PRD (e.g., m72QhP/+ line), consistent with loss of accessible SRSF7 binding sites. Conversely, both sites were present in lines containing human exon 1 and murine PRD (Fig. 4e). These predictions align with our experimental data and suggest that SRSF7 binding to the mouse PRD in a human exon 1 context may attenuate the splicing abnormalities typically associated with human HTT exon 1.

These findings support the hypothesis that the PRD is a key determinant of HTT exon 1 mRNA secondary structure and point to a mechanistic role for PRD-dependent RNA folding in modulating splicing events, including HTT1a production.

Building on these insights, we next tested the functional relevance of SRSF7 in regulating HTT1a production. We over-expressed HA-tagged SRSF7 in a panel of HuntEx1 cell lines that share the same CAG repeat length but differ in their PRD or exon 1 context (Supplementary Fig. 6a). SRSF7 over-expression led to a modest but significant reduction in HTT1a transcript levels specifically in h72Q/+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 6e and Supplementary Data 9), with no effect on full-length Htt mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 6f and Supplementary Data 9). This was accompanied by a corresponding reduction in HTT1a protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 6g, h), consistent with decreased transcript abundance.

To directly test the contribution of the predicted SRSF7 binding site within the mouse PRD, we generated mutant HuntEx1 lines carrying targeted alterations in the CACCAC motif. These lines maintained expression of the pluripotency markers OCT3/4 and SOX2 (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Taken together, these data identify SRSF7 as a key modulator of HTT1a production. We propose that the final PRD proline stretch plays an important role in the regulation of human HTT exon 1-exon 2 splicing (Fig. 6e). Meanwhile, additional, yet unexplored, factors – such as the putative SRSF7 binding site identified in the human N17 domain – may also contribute to the unique susceptibility of human exon 1 to splicing alterations.

Discussion

The production of the HTT1a transcript – encoding the highly toxic HTT1a protein – has emerged as a central contributor to HD pathogenesis, particularly in relation to CAG repeat expansions16,19. However, the role of cis-elements within exon 1 in promoting this alternative pre-mRNA processing event remains poorly understood. Using the modular HuntEx1 stem cell platform, we explored whether exon 1 sequence context, beyond the CAG tract itself, influences HTT1a generation.

Our multimodal approach revealed that the PRD-encoding region plays a significant role in modulating HTT exon 1 splicing, particularly in the human context. Notably, the introduction of a mouse PRD into human huntingtin exon 1 significantly reduced HTT1a production.

This effect may represent one of the earliest molecular differences underlying the more aggressive phenotype observed in transgenic mice expressing a human HTT exon 1, compared to knock-in models carrying the mouse version14,53. Although human and mouse PRDs encode similar amino acid sequences, they diverge at the nucleotide level, particularly in CCG content and structural organisation. The mouse PRD lacks long uninterrupted CCG stretches, whereas the human counterpart contains an initial CCGCCA motif followed by 7–10 CCG repeats (Supplementary Fig. 1d). These subtle differences substantially impact RNA secondary structure and, consequently, splicing factor engagement.

This finding supports several broader reflections. First, it reinforces the idea that local RNA structure – shaped by both repeat tracts and their flanking sequences – is a key modulator of HTT splicing. Previous studies have shown that CAG tracts can form stable hairpins48,49. In line with this, our free-energy-based analyses indicate that the PRD interacts with the CAG tract to form stem-loop structures. These structures may affect splice site accessibility and regulatory element exposure, providing a mechanistic link between sequence variation and transcript diversity. Second, our data challenge a simplistic view that HTT1a production is determined solely by CAG length. In cells with a fully murine exon 1, we did not observe any CAG-dependent increase in Htt1a within the timeframe of in vitro differentiation, despite prior in vivo evidence8. This discrepancy may reflect delayed accumulation of somatic CAG expansions or indicate additional regulatory layers that are species- or context-specific.

Overall, our data suggest that the human exon 1 sequence promotes RNA structures or exposes motifs that favour HTT1a formation. This is consistent with findings on clinically relevant SNPs, including variants in the second proline-coding triplet of the PRD28–30. The correlation between a SNP in the second CCA triplet and age of onset – and its possible impact on RNA folding observed in our study – highlights the potential pathogenic importance of DNA and/or RNA spatial organisation.

Changes in RNA secondary structure can significantly affect how RBPs engage with transcripts, and our predictive analysis indicates that this is a crucial factor to consider when developing splicing-targeted therapeutic approaches. Among the RBPs analysed, SRSF7 emerged as the only factor with predicted high-affinity binding motifs in the mouse PRD, pointing to a species-specific interaction that matches with HTT1a expression data. This observation aligns with SRSF7’s known roles in polyadenylation site selection and splice regulation54. Surface plasmon resonance analysis confirmed preferential binding of the highly conserved SRSF7 protein to the end of the mouse PRD. While SRSF6 – another SR protein that binds to CAG sequences – was previously implicated in HTT1a production, this was later refuted, raising the possibility of alternative or additional partners55.

Disruption or insertion of the predicted CACCAC SRSF7 recognition motif in the mouse or human PRD, respectively, significantly altered HTT1a levels, supporting a model where SRSF7 interaction with exon 1 is modulated by both sequence and structure. In this model, high-affinity SRSF7 binding to the mouse PRD promotes canonical splicing and full-length Htt production. This explains the low Htt1a levels in lines with a fully murine exon 1 or a human exon 1 containing the mouse PRD, and is further supported by the increase observed when the CACCAC motif is mutated to CGCCGC. In contrast, the lack of SRSF7 binding to the human PRD promotes mis-splicing and HTT1a generation, as suggested by our results from lines bearing a fully human exon 1, and corroborated by reduced HTT1a expression upon insertion of the CACCAC motif into the human PRD (Fig. 6b, c). The absence of increased HTT1a when a human PRD is introduced into the murine exon 1 context may be explained by the lack of a consensus SRSF7 binding site in both the PRD and the murine N17 domain. In such cases, SRSF7 may fail to bind, allowing other splicing regulators to compensate, suggesting a more complex regulatory network than this model alone implies.

Interestingly, the predicted SRSF7 binding site in the mouse PRD consistently maps to a loop, whereas the site in the human N17 sequence lies within a stem formed through pairing with the Htt 5’UTR. The CACCAC sequence may contribute to this structure, as its insertion or removal reshapes that region, expanding or restricting the loop (Supplementary Fig. 7f, g). Although additional experimental validation is needed to support these structural predictions, the correlation between structural alterations and HTT1a expression warrants further investigation. Given that RNA structure can regulate accessibility of binding motifs56,57, these local architectures likely influence SRSF7 activity. The relative occupancy of these sites may also coordinate the activity of additional splicing factors acting alongside SRSF7 to regulate intron retention at the HTT exon 1 locus. While our findings demonstrate the involvement of SRSF7 in HTT1a production, global alterations in RBPs networks cannot be excluded, as this is a highly redundant and interconnected system. Future studies will address the potential large-scale rewiring of this regulatory network.

Understanding the mechanisms driving HTT1a production is critical given the established link to neuronal toxicity12–15. We extend this evidence by showing that modulation of HTT1a alters dendritic morphology in neurons (Fig. 2i, j).

More broadly, our study contributes to the growing evidence that HTT transcript regulation is highly complex. The gene’s length, intronic content, and repetitive regions create a regulatory landscape in which transcriptional processivity, RNA polymerase II elongation rate10, post-transcriptional RNA modifications such as m6A58,59, and RNA structure converge to influence splicing decisions. Understanding this architecture is essential not only for elucidating HD pathogenesis but also for developing effective RNA-targeted therapies.

For instance, efforts to selectively silence HTT1a – or to correct exon 1 splicing using antisense oligonucleotides – will benefit from precise knowledge of the sequence and structural determinants governing this process60. Our findings demonstrate that even subtle variations outside the CAG tract can shift splicing outcomes, presenting both challenges and opportunities for therapeutic intervention.

In summary, the interplay between sequence, structure, and splicing factors within HTT exon 1 is critical for the production of pathogenic HTT1a. The species-specific effects observed underscore the importance of using human sequences and patient-relevant sequences to study disease mechanisms. Further investigation of how RBPs such as SRSF7 interact with HTT RNA, and how these interactions are shaped by RNA structure, may reveal new strategies to suppress toxic transcript generation in HD.

Methods

Mouse Embryonic Stem (mES) cell lines

E14 ES cell line61-derived Recombinase-Mediated Cassette Exchange mES cell lines (RMCE/- and RMCE/+ cells) were previously generated and characterised36.

HTT knock-in cell generation

HTT exon 1 constructs were cloned in pUC57 vectors (Genscript) and integrated by Flp-mediated recombination to reconstitute a genomic portion spanning the region enclosed by a PuroR∆TK cassette (from Htt 5’UTR to 2150 bp within mouse Htt intron 1). Supplementary Data 1 lists the HTT exon 1 sequences that were inserted in each recombined cell line.

Two million RMCE/+ cells were co-transfected with 4 µg RMCE donor construct and 4 µg pCAG-Flp:eGFP (Addgene, #13788) by Amaxa™ mES nucleofection kit (Lonza). The pools of transfected cells were then seeded at low density (4-8.000 cells/cm2) for clonal selection. When the transfection efficiency peak was reached (72 h post-transfection), cultures were treated with 1 μM ganciclovir for one week. After clonal expansion, cassette recombination was assessed by PCR with primers mapping to the FRT site (FRT_FW, 5’-TGCTCTCCTTCCCAGTTTAACA-3’ and FRT_RW, 5’-CAAAACCGTTCCGGCTTCAG-3’) or to the puromycin resistance element that is ablated after the desired cassette exchange (FRT_pCMV_RW, 5’-GTGAAGAATGTGCGAGACCCA-3’).

HTT exon 1 was amplified with the primers ex1_FW (5’-CCCCATTCATTGCCTTGCTG-3’) and ex1_RW (5’-GGTCGGTGCAGCGGCTCCT-3’), PCR products were purified with the QIAquick PCR-purification Kit (QIAGEN), and Sanger sequenced (GATC – Eurofins).

Of all picked and analysed clones, ~70% had recombined and, of these, ~ 90% with the expected exon 1 sequence. With the only exception of the h7Q/+ culture, which is monoclonal, n ≥ 3 clones that correctly carried the expected HTT exon 1 were finally pooled to constitute the original cell line.

Mouse ES cell culture

Mouse ES cells were cultured in Glasgow Minimal Essential Medium (GMEM), supplemented with 2mM L-glutamine (Gibco, #25030081), 10% heat-inactivated Foetal Bovine Serum (vol/vol, EuroClone, #ECS0186L), 100 μM Non-Essential Amino Acids (Gibco, #11140-035), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, #11360-039), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, #31350-010), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin (EuroClone, #ECB3001D) and freshly added 1000 U/mL ESGRO murine Leukemia Inhibitor Factor (LIF, Millipore, #ESG1107).

Cultures were maintained on 0.2% porcine skin gelatine coating (Sigma-Aldrich, #G2625) and passaged when reaching 80-90% confluence with 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (vol/vol, Gibco, #15400-054). Cells were seeded at comparable confluences ( ≈ 70%) and passage numbers (39–45) when performing the experiments. The genetic background was identical in all lines used.

Neuronal cortical differentiation

On DIV-1, 1 × 104 mES cells/cm2 were plated in supplemented GMEM upon dissociation with 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (vol/vol, Gibco, #15400-054).

From the day after (DIV0) until replating of the culture (DIV12, see below), cells were maintained in DDM medium, composed of 1:1 DMEM/F12 (Gibco, #21331-020) supplemented with 1:100 N2 (Gibco, #17502-048), 2mM L-glutamine (Gibco, #25030081), 100 μM Non-Essential Amino Acids (Gibco, #11140-035), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, #11360-039), 0.5 mg/mL BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, #A3059), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, #31350-010), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin (EuroClone, #ECB3001D). Moreover, 1 µM cyclopamine (Sigma, #C4116) was added from DIV2 to DIV10.

On DIV12, cultures were dissociated with Accutase (Millipore, #SF006) and plated at a density of 8 × 104 cells/ cm2 on tissue culture plates coated with 10 ng/μL Poly-D-Lysine (Sigma-Aldrich, #P6407) and 3 ng/μL Laminin (ThermoFisher, #23017015). From then and until the differentiation endpoint (DIV28), cells were grown in N2B27, with partial medium changes every second day. N2B27 is a 1:1 mixture of DMEM/F12 and neurobasal culture media with 1:200 N2 (Gibco, #17502-048), 1:100 B27 without vitamin A (Gibco, #12587-010), 2mM L-glutamine (Gibco, #25030081), 200 μM Non-Essential Amino Acids (Gibco, #11140-035), 2 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, #11360-039), 1 mg/mL BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, #A3059), 0.2 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, #31350-010), 100 μ/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin (EuroClone, #ECB3001D).

Genomic DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted with the NucleoSpin Tissue® kit (Macherey-Nagel, #740952). DNA samples were then quantified by spectrometer analysis on a NanoDrop1000 (ThermoScientific).

RNA extraction

RNA was isolated with TRIzol reagent (ThermoFisher, #15596026). The obtained RNA was quantified on Nanodrop1000 and run on a 1.0% agarose gel to evaluate its integrity. Potential DNA contamination was removed by treatment with the DNA-free™ kit (ThermoFisher, #AM1906).

Real-Time qPCR

1 µg RNA was reverse transcribed using iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, #1708890). qPCR was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad) and analysed with CFX Manager Software (Bio-Rad) using either SYBR or Taqman chemistry. Primer pairs and probes are listed in Supplementary Data 6.

Protein lysates preparation and Western Blot

Proteins were extracted in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 1% nonidet P40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate (wt/vol)), with the fresh addition of 1 mM PMSF and protease inhibitor (ThermoScientific, #1861281). Lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for 30 min. Supernatants were collected, and protein concentration was measured with Pierce-BCA Protein assay kit (ThermoScientific #23225). 30–50 μg of protein extracts were loaded on SDS-PAGE gels and ran in 1X Tris/Glycine/SDS Buffer (Biorad, #1610772). Gels were then transferred to a Trans-Blot Turbo Midi Nitrocellulose Transfer Pack (Biorad, #1704159) and stained with total protein stain (Ponceau S, Sigma-Aldrich #P7170). After washing in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) supplemented with 0.1% Tween-20 (vol/vol), membranes were blocked in 5% BSA (wt/vol, Biorad, #170-6404) in TBS-Tween and incubated with the specific primary antibodies at + 4 °C overnight. The day after, membranes were washed 4 times for 10 minutes in TBS-Tween at room temperature. They were then incubated at room temperature for 1 h with 1:3000 secondary horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibodies and newly washed for 3 times with TBS-Tween, with 10 min incubation between washes. Immunoreactive bands were detected by Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Biorad, #170-5061) and visualised with ChemiDocMP Imaging System (Biorad).

Primary and secondary antibodies are listed in Supplementary Data 7. The band's intensity was measured by the ImageJ software. Uncropped and unprocessed scans of all Western Blots are supplied in the Source Data file.

Homogeneous time resolved fluorescence (HTRF)

For the establishment of HTRF assays, the optimisation of antibody concentrations and lysate titrations was performed on proliferative cultures of HuntEx1 mES cell lines (m7Q/+, m107Q/+, h20Q/+, h72Q/+, and h72QmP/+) to determine optimal conditions of analysis. Briefly, cell pellets of one million cells were harvested and snap frozen at − 80 °C, and homogenates were prepared in ice-cold 1% Triton-X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer with complete protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Roche). For optimisation of antibody concentration, the terbium donor concentration was maintained at 1 ng/well, whilst titrating in anti-HTT d2 or Alexa-488 acceptor at either 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 or 40 ng per well, whilst for optimisation of the lysate dilution a serial dilution of 400, 200, 100, 50, 25, or 12.5 k cells/well was assessed, in triplicate. The antibody concentrations and cell numbers used per assay were: (1) soluble HTT1a (mouse or human), 2B7-Tb (1 ng): MW8-d2 (40 ng), 400 k cells; (2) soluble full-length (total) HTT (mouse + human), D7F7-Tb (1 ng): MAB5490-d2 (40 ng), 400k cells; (3) soluble mouse (total) HTT, 2B7-Tb (1 ng): CHDI-90001414-d2 (40 ng), 400 k cells; (4) soluble human (total) HTT, 2B7-Tb (1 ng): 4C9-Alexa488 (40 ng), 400k cells; 5) soluble mutant (total) HTT, 2B7-Tb (1 ng): MW1-d2 (20 ng), 400 k cells. For the comparison of proliferative cultures of all HuntEx1 mES lines by HTRF, lysate dilutions of cell homogenates to a final volume of 10 μL were pipetted in triplicate into a 384-well (pure white, low volume, conical) proxiplate (Greiner Bio-One). Antibodies were added per well in 5 μL HTRF detection buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 0.2 M KF, 0.1% bovine serum albumin, 0.05% Tween-20) with complete protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Roche). Plates were incubated for 3 h on an orbital shaker (250 rpm) at room temperature, before reading on an EnVision (Revvity) plate reader using optimised HTRF detection parameters as described previously22,38. N = 3 experimental replicates, n = 3 technical replicates.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC)

Cells were fixed in 4% Paraformaldehyde (PFA, Sigma-Aldrich #J19943.K2) for 20 min at room temperature. They were then permeabilized in permeabilization buffer (0.5% Triton X-100 (vol/vol) in PBS) and blocked for 1 h in blocking buffer (0.2% Triton X-100 (vol/vol), 5% normal goat serum (vol/vol) in PBS). Primary antibodies were diluted in a 1:1 mix of blocking solution and PBS and applied at 4 °C overnight. The day after, cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the appropriate conjugated secondary antibodies, diluted 1:500 in a 1:1 solution of blocking buffer and PBS and to 1:1000 Hoechst 33258 (5 μg/mL, Molecular Probes, Invitrogen #J19943.K2). Coverslips were mounted with ProLong® Diamond Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific #J19943.K2). Z-stack images were acquired with an IN Cell Analyser 6000 microscope (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) and processed with the ImageJ software (US National Institutes of Health). Primary and secondary antibodies are listed in Supplementary Data 7.

Morphological neuronal quantification

Total dendritic length was measured on 30 randomly selected fields per experiment (n = 3 independent experiments) per cell line, with a total of approximately 400 DIV28 MAP2 positive cells per line. MAP2-positive processes were first mapped with the ‘Freehand Line’ tool in the ImageJ software; the resulting tracing was then automatically measured by ImageJ itself.

RNA-seq bioinformatic analysis

Sequencing reads were pre-processed with nf-core rnaseq v3.14.0-gb89fac3 Nextflow pipeline62,63. Specifically, sequencing reads were trimmed and quality filtered with fastp v0.23.464 and their sequencing quality was checked with FastQC v0.12.165. Reads generated from residual ribosomal RNA were then identified and removed with SortMeRNA v4.3.466. Filtered reads were aligned to the GRCm39 mouse reference genome with STAR v2.7.10a67, and the resulting alignments were compressed and saved in sorted BAM files with Samtoolsv1.1768. RSEM v1.3.169 was then used to estimate gene expression levels from BAM files, using annotations from Ensembl v.110 GTF file for the GRCm39 reference genome. BAM files were visually inspected in the IGV genome browser70. Gene counts were then imported in R environment with tximport function71 and a DESeq object was created with DESeqDataSetFromTximport function from DESeq2 package v1.12.372. Genes with less than 5 counts in at least three samples were discarded. The library size was then evaluated for each sample with the counts function from the DESeq package. A txdb object was created from the GTF file with makeTxDbFromGFFfunction from the GenomicFeatures v1.52.273 package, and the coordinates of introns 1 and 2 of the main isoform of HTT (“ENSMUST00000080036”) were extracted. BAM files for reads alignments mapping to the HTT gene were imported in R using the readGAlignmentPairs function from GenomicAlignments v1.36.074, and the number of reads mapping to intron 1 and intron 2 of the HTT transcript, normalised by library size, was calculated with findOverlaps function from GenomicAlignmentspackage. The normalised fraction of HTT intron 1 reads, partially overlapping with HTT1a transcript, was plotted for each genotype with ggplot2 v3.5.075. As a negative control, the normalised fraction of HTT intron 2 reads was plotted for each genotype.

Generation of SRSF7-p2A-eGFP over-expressing lines

HA-tagged SRSF7 (genebank ID BC025529.1) cloned into a pcDNA3.1(+)-p2A-eGFP construct was purchased from Genscript. Cells were transfected with 4 µg DNA (in 6-MWs) using Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher Scientific, #L3000015), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Exogenous SRSF7 expression was assessed by western blot and immunocytochemistry 72 h post-transfection. See Supplementary Data 8 for plasmid sequence and map.

HTT1a knockdown

The HTT exon 1 intronic region-targeting shRNA534 or a non-targeting 29-mer scrambled shRNA cassette was cloned in a SHC001 pLKO hPGK-puro lentiviral vector (customised, ThermoFisher Scientific).

Cells were infected when they reached 70% confluence with 9 transducing units (TU) per cell and differentiated as described above. The day after replating (DIV13), cultures were again infected. HTT exon 1 silencing was assayed by qPCR at the end point of differentiation, DIV28.

Secondary mRNA structure predictions

The sequences of interest were pasted into either RNAfold (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi) webserver.

For RNAfold analysis, the minimum free energy and partition function were calculated, and isolated base pairs were avoided.

RBP consensus sites predictions

The sequences of interest were pasted into either RBPmap (https://rbpmap.technion.ac.il/index.html) or RBPsuite (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/RBPsuite/) webservers.

For RBPmap analysis, database assembly was on GRCm38/mm10, and both stringency levels (default and high) were considered.

Biosensor reagents

Human full-length SFRS7 (NP_001026854.1, 1 a.a. − 238 a.a.) recombinant protein with GST-tag at N-terminal obtained from wheat germ was purchased from Abnova (Taipei, Taiwan). Biotinylated RNA sequences were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA) with Ultramer synthesis. Their sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 6. Biotin CAPture kit and Biacore consumables were obtained from Cytiva (Wilmington, DE, USA). All the buffers and reagents were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany) and prepared using Milli-Q pure water.

Biosensor construction and assay

The interaction between RNA sequences and Human SFRS7 was characterised by Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) using a Biacore 1 K system (Cytiva). The Biotin CAPture Kit was used for the interaction studies according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A Sensor Chip CAP (Cytiva) was docked, and the surface was conditioned with three 1-minute pulses of the Regeneration Solution (8 M guanidine-HCl, 1 M NaOH).

The Biotin CAPture reagent was injected at a flow rate of 2 μL/min for 5 min over control and active flow cells to achieve a stable capture platform.

Then the specific capture probe RNA sequences prepared in Running Buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 125 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 2 mM EDTA, 9.5 mM glutathione reduced form), at concentration of 2 μM, was injected at 10 μL/min for 4 min, only on active cell, until a capture level of approximately 400 RU was reached. Afterwards, the target was injected at 30 μL/min for 2 min. Finally, the regeneration of the surface after each analysis cycle was achieved by alkaline denaturation using the Regeneration Solution at 30 μL/min for 2 min.

Single-cycle kinetics experiments

Experiments were performed at 25 °C using Single Cycle Kinetics. In this approach, several successive increasing concentrations of the analyte are injected, and only one regeneration step is performed at the end of the complete binding cycle. A concentration series of the Human SFRS7 ranging from 1 nM to 300 nM was prepared in Running Buffer. The concentrations were injected sequentially over the captured ligand and reference surfaces in a single cycle, from lowest to highest, with an association time of 120 s and a final dissociation time of 600 s at a flow rate of 30 μL/min, and the concentrations of the protein used were 0, 1, 3, 10, 30, 100 and 300 nM. The resulting sensorgrams were double-referenced by subtracting both the reference flow cell signal and the average of the “blank” (Running Buffer-only) injections.

Data sets were processed and analysed using the Biacore 1-series Evaluation Software.

SPR Data processing and non-linear regression

The binding isotherms were generated by plotting the RU values against the corresponding analyte concentrations in GraphPad Prism (version [10.6.1]).

To determine the binding parameters, the specific binding data were analysed using a four-parameter logistic (4PL) sigmoidal model (X is concentration). During the fitting procedure, the HillSlope was constrained to 1.0 to assume a standard 1:1 stoichiometry. The dissociation constant (Kd) and the maximal binding capacity (Top) were calculated from the resulting regression.

For the analysis of non-specific binding and negative controls, the same 4PL model was applied. To ensure comparability and statistical consistency, the Top value was constrained to the value previously derived from the specific binding analysis. This approach allowed for an accurate estimation of the relative binding efficiency and the evaluation of non-specific contributions under identical saturation assumptions. All data were expressed as mean ± SD from two independent replicates.

Statistical analysis

Data from qPCRs were analysed with one-way ANOVA or unpaired t tests, as specified in each figure legend. Quantification of dendritic length in neuronal cultures was assayed with Kruskal-Wallis test. Multiple comparisons between cell lines were performed by applying Tukey’s or Dunn’s post-hoc tests, as specified in the figure legends.

All statistical analyses were performed by GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.2).
