Magma Ocean Waves and Thermal Variability on Lava Worlds
Mohammad Farhat, Eugene Chiang

Abstract
Lava worlds are rocky planets with dayside skins made molten by stellar irradiation. Tidal heating on these shortest-period planets is more than skin deep. We show how orbital eccentricities of just a few percent (within current observed bounds and maintained secularly by exterior companions) can create deep magma oceans. “Lava tidal waves” slosh across these oceans; we compute the multi-modal response of the ocean to tidal forcing, subject to a coastline at the day-night terminator and a parameterized viscous drag. Wave interference produces a dayside heat map that is spatially irregular and highly time-variable; hotspots can wander both east and west of the substellar point, and thermal light curves can vary and spike aperiodically, from orbit to orbit and within an orbit. Heat deposited by tides is removed in steady state by a combination of fluid, mushy, and solid-state convection in the mantle. For Earth-sized planets with sub-day periods, the entire mantle may be tidally liquified.

We supply these calculations to argue that eccentricities of a few percent are plausible for some (not all) short-period rocky planets. Making the case for eccentricity — or other sources of time-dependent tidal strain, such as obliquity or asynchronous rotation
— is not, however, the focus of this paper. Our goal instead is to explore what the thermodynamic consequences of tidal heating are for lava worlds. Can tidal stresses be so large that, instead of substellar magma “ponds” only
\sim
100 m deep
(
Kite et al. 2016
)
, the entire dayside is covered by a magma ocean penetrating into the mantle, perhaps even to the metallic core? Current models of tidal dissipation in rocky exoplanets have mostly presumed the mantle is solid (viscoelastic); if melt is considered, it is only crudely accounted for by adjusting parameters like solid-state viscosity and shear modulus. By contrast,

Tyler et al. 2015
,
Hay et al. 2020
, and

Aygün & Čadek 2024

Here we provide such a treatment for short-period exoplanets,
extending our earlier work
(
Farhat et al. 2025
)
to study the time-dependent, fluid-dynamical response of lava worlds to eccentricity tides. We model how a molten magma layer of arbitrary depth flows across the dayside hemisphere, as the planet is flexed repeatedly from apoapse to periapse and back — we are interested in the dynamical (as opposed to equilibrium) tide, i.e. the “sloshing” of the magma ocean as it is forced by multiple terms in the tidal potential.

.

This paper is organized as follows. In section

, we
lay out our method for solving the Laplace Tidal Equations governing a dayside magma ocean forced by eccentricity tides
and damped by a simple prescriptive Rayleigh drag. The free parameters

, the ocean thickness
H

\sigma_{\rm R}
. Section

II
Theory of Dynamical Lava Tides

We are interested in modeling the lava flows within a hemispherical magma ocean on the
dayside of a short-period rocky planet, subject to eccentricity tidal
forcing from the host star. The melt fraction in the silicate ocean is assumed
to be large enough (
\gtrsim 0.2
–0.6 as suggested by experiments;
see e.g.
Scott & Kohlstedt 2006
;
Costa et al. 2009
) that the ocean’s
behavior is that of a viscous fluid, even allowing for suspended
solid inclusions
(
Tonks & Melosh 1993
;
Abe 1997
, e.g.)
.

II.1
Laplace tidal equations

We utilize the classical Laplace tidal equations (LTEs) in the strongly viscous, creep flow limit
(
Tyler et al. 2015
;
Farhat et al. 2025
)
. On a spherical planet with radius
R_{\rm p}
, harboring a surface magma ocean of uniform thickness
H
, the linear LTEs comprise a coupled system of momentum and mass conservation
(
Longuet-Higgins 1968
, e.g.)
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\vec{u}+\sigma_{\rm R}\vec{u}+\vec{f}\crossproduct\vec{u}=g\gradient(\zeta_{\rm eq} - \zeta),

(1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\zeta+\gradient\cdot{H\vec{u}}=0.

(1b)

Here
t
is time;
\vec{u}=u_{\theta}\hat{\theta}+u_{\lambda}\hat{\lambda}
is the horizontal velocity field of the lava tidal flow in polar coordinates (
\theta
being the co-latitude and
\lambda
the longitude);
g
is the gravitational acceleration at the surface;
\zeta
is the tidally induced vertical displacement;
\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}
is the equilibrium tidal displacement;
\gradient=R_{\rm p}^{-1}\left[\hat{\theta}\partial_{\theta}+\hat{\lambda}(\sin\theta)^{-1}\partial_{\lambda}\right]
is the two-dimensional horizontal gradient operator;
f=2\Omega_{\rm p}\cos\theta
is the Coriolis parameter for a planet rotating with velocity
\Omega_{\rm p}
; and
\sigma_{\rm R}
is the Rayleigh drag frequency. The latter is the inverse of the timescale over which a tidally excited wave can be brought to rest by
various damping mechanisms, including friction at the bottom of the oceanic layer,
viscosity, pressure drag against suspended solids, and Darcy-like dissipation when the magma ocean is close to the rheological transition.

II.2
Tidal forcing potential

The tidal forcing from the star enters via the equilibrium tidal displacement
\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}
, as given by the equipotential surface induced by the tidal potential
U_{\rm T}
(
\zeta_{\mathrm{eq}}=U_{\rm T}/g
). We focus in this work on eccentricity tides. By virtue of the periodic nature of the forcing, the tidal potential is resolved in time as a Fourier series,
and in space using Associated Legendre Functions
(P_{p}^{q})

(
Kaula 1968
, e.g.)
:

U_{\rm T}=\mathfrak{Re}\left\{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{p=2}^{\infty}\sum_{q=0}^{n}U_{p,q}^{{\rm T};k}P_{p}^{q}(\cos\theta)e^{i[\sigma_{q}^{k}t+q\lambda]}\right\},

(2)

where
\sigma_{q}^{k}=q\Omega_{\rm p}-kn_{\rm orb}
is the tidal forcing frequency,
n_{\rm orb}
is the mean motion of the planet, and

U_{p,q}^{{\rm T};k}=\frac{GM_{\star}}{a_{\rm p}}\left(\frac{R_{\rm p}}{a_{\rm p}}\right)^{p}A_{p,q,k}(e_{\rm p}).

(3)

Here
G
is the gravitational constant,
M_{\star}
is the mass of the perturbing host star,
a_{\rm p}
is the planet’s semi-major axis,
e_{\rm p}
is its eccentricity, the dimensionless eccentricity functions

\displaystyle A_{p,q,k}(e_{\rm p})

\displaystyle=(2-\delta_{q,0}\delta_{k,0})(1-\delta_{q,0}\delta_{k<0})

\displaystyle\times\sqrt{\frac{2(p-q)!}{(2p+1)(p+q)!}}P_{p}^{q}(0)X_{k}^{-(p+1),q}(e_{\rm p}),

(4)

where the Kronecker delta function

\delta_{i,j}=1
if
i=j
and 0 otherwise, and
X_{k}^{p,q}(e_{\rm p})
are the Hansen eccentricity functions
(
Hughes 1981
;
Laskar 2005
, e.g.)
. We show in Appendix
C
our method of computing the Hansen coefficients.

II.3
Solution strategy

Farhat et al. 2025
provided a closed-form analytical solution to the LTEs (Eqs.
1a
-
1b
) for a global oceanic shell in the highly damped regime, i.e. when friction dominates and Coriolis effects are negligible.
Here, the magma ocean is restricted to a hemispherical shell on the dayside of the planet, and we retain the Coriolis term in the equation (for our parameters, it will be of secondary importance).
We proceed to solve the LTEs semi-analytically, using an approach similar to that adopted for the Earth’s (paleo-)oceans
(
Longuet-Higgins 1968
;
Webb 1980
;
Hansen 1982
;
Tyler 2011
;
Farhat et al. 2022
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2023
)
, but with relatively amplified values for
\sigma_{\rm R}
to
study the damped, creeping flow regime of the lava fluid.

With
\vec{x}
being the tidally driven horizontal magma displacement (i.e.
\partial_{t}\vec{x}=\vec{u})
, the momentum equation is rewritten as:

\left[\partial_{t}^{2}+(\sigma_{\rm R}+\vec{f}\crossproduct)\partial_{t}\right]\vec{x}+g\gradient(\zeta-\zeta_\mathrm{eq})=0.

(5)

Following
Proudman 1920a
, we apply Helmholtz’s theorem
(
Arfken & Weber 1999
, e.g.)
to decompose
\vec{x}
into curl-free and divergence-free vector fields:

\vec{x}=\gradient\Phi+\gradient\Psi\crossproduct\hat{r},

(6)

where
\Phi
is a divergent displacement potential function,
\Psi
is a rotational displacement stream function, and
\hat{r}
the radial direction in spherical coordinates.

II.4
Boundary conditions

One needs to define the boundary conditions for each component of the flow. Lava flows must be bounded within the magma ocean’s coastlines on the terminator line separating the dayside from the nightside. This impermeability condition is
\vec{x}\cdot\hat{n}=0
, where
\hat{n}
designates the normal to the terminator lying in the local horizontal plane. Without another boundary condition allowing us to solve the two fields
\Phi
and
\Psi
separately, we follow the tradition of imposing the impermeability condition on both components simultaneously
(
Gent & McWilliams 1983
, e.g.)
:
\gradient\Phi\cdot\hat{n}=0
and
\gradient\Psi\crossproduct\hat{r}\cdot\hat{n}=0
. The former condition implies that the gradient of
\Phi
vanishes in the direction normal to the terminator, a Neumann boundary condition; the latter condition
\gradient\Psi\cdot(\hat{r}\crossproduct\hat{n})=0
implies that the stream function has zero tangential derivative and is therefore constant along the coastline, a Dirichlet boundary condition.

Thus the potential and stream functions, defined on a compact connected domain
\mathcal{O}
corresponding to the magma ocean basin, satisfy on the ocean coastline
\mathcal{\partial O}
either a Neumann or Dirichlet boundary condition
(
Unno et al. 1979
)
. As such, these functions can be expanded in terms of orthogonal eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{r},\psi_{r}\}

satisfying the wave equations

\displaystyle\gradient^{2}\phi_{r}+\mu_{r}\phi_{r}=0,\hskip 28.45274pt\hat{n}\cdot\gradient\phi_{r}|_{\partial O}=0,

(7a)

\displaystyle\gradient^{2}\psi_{r}+\nu_{r}\psi_{r}=0,\hskip 28.45274pt\psi_{r}|_{\partial O}=0,

(7b)

with the horizontal Laplacian defined as
\gradient^{2}=(R_{\rm p}\sin\theta)^{-2}\left[\sin\theta\partial_{\theta}\left(\sin\theta\partial_{\theta}\right)+\partial_{\lambda}^{2}\right]
. Here the subscript
r
is a harmonic index (more on this label below). With
\Psi
having zero tangential derivative on the terminator, we have set the Dirichlet boundary value to
\psi_{r}=0
.

II.5
Series solutions

The solutions to the latitudinal components of Eqs. (
7
) are the Associated Legendre Functions
P_{n}^{m}
, while the longitudinal solutions take the form

\exp( im\lambda)
:

\displaystyle\phi_{r}=\frac{\alpha_{nm}}{R_{\rm p}}P_{n}^{m}(\cos\theta)\exp( im\lambda),

(8a)

\displaystyle\psi_{r}=-i\frac{\alpha_{nm}}{R_{\rm p}}P_{n}^{m}(\cos\theta)\exp( im\lambda).

(8b)

We have chosen
(n,m)
to expand the harmonics of the tidal response, as distinct from the
(p,q)
labels used earlier to expand the forcing (Eq.
2
). Each
(p,q)
forcing harmonic excites a mix of different
(n,m)
response harmonics.

In the case of a spherical fluid shell, the response is expanded in a series of Hough modes
(
Hough 1898
)
, where for a given tidal forcing mode
(p,q)
the shell responds as a sum of modes with the same
m=q
but various

(
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2018
;
Farhat et al. 2022

. The one-to-one forcing-to-response modal mapping characteristic of pure solid tides is broken by the Coriolis force acting on the fluid. For our hemispherical shell, the zonal symmetry on the sphere is also broken, so each forcing mode excites a spectrum of basin modes with different

or misalignments in the system’s spin-orbit geometry

)
.

(9)

Introducing the time-dependent coefficients
p_{r}(t)
and
p_{-r}(t)
, we write the potential and stream functions as:

\displaystyle\Phi(\theta,\lambda,t)=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}p_{r}(t)\phi_{r}(\theta,\lambda),

(10a)

\displaystyle\Psi(\theta,\lambda,t)=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}p_{-r}(t)\psi_{r}(\theta,\lambda).

(10b)

Note that despite the (historical) notation which might suggest otherwise,
p_{r}
and
p_{-r}
are independent coefficients, i.e., in general, one cannot be derived from the other. Substituting equations (
7
) and (
10
) into the continuity equation (
1b
), we recover the vertical tidal displacement of the lava waves:

\zeta=H\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\mu_{r}p_{r}\phi_{r},

(11)

for constant free parameter
H
.
Finding the tidal flow patterns reduces to solving for the time-dependent coefficients
p_{r}
and
p_{-r}
. We delegate the details of the solution method to Appendix
B
; the executive summary is that we
substitute all the aforementioned expansions into the LTEs (Eqs.
1
),
take the dot product of the momentum equation with
\gradient\phi_{r}
to obtain one set of equations, repeat with
\gradient\psi_{r}\crossproduct\hat{r}
to obtain a second set, and integrate over the oceanic domain
\mathcal{O}
to obtain a coupled linear system for the coefficients
p_{r}
and
p_{-r}
:

\displaystyle-\sigma^{2}p_{r}-i\sigma\sigma_{\rm R}p_{r}+gH\mu_{r}p_{r}-g{\zeta}_{\rm eq;r}

\displaystyle-\frac{2i\sigma\Omega_{\rm p}}{\mu_{r}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\beta_{r,s}p_{s}

\displaystyle=0,

(12)

\displaystyle-\sigma^{2}p_{-r}-i\sigma\sigma_{\rm R}p_{-r}-\frac{2i\sigma\Omega_{\rm p}}{\nu_{r}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\beta_{-r,s}\partial_{t}p_{s}

\displaystyle=0.

(13)

We have used
\partial_{t}\rightarrow i\sigma_{q}^{k}
(Eq.
2
) and replaced
\sigma_{q}^{k}
with
\sigma
for short. The above system is solved for all
p_{r}
and
p_{-r}
for a given
\sigma
. The variable
\zeta_{\rm eq;r}
corresponds to the
r
th harmonic of the equipotential surface,
i.e.:

\zeta_{\rm eq;r}=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\phi_{r}\zeta_{\rm eq}dA,

(14)

with
dA
denoting an area element of the ocean domain.
The gyroscopic coefficients
\beta_{r,s}
(Eqs.
B
) encode the effect of rotational distortion to the tidal waves and the effect of the boundary conditions imposed by the terminator. We ignore here the effect of solid deformation on the magma ocean dynamics, which can enter through the effects of self-attraction and loading between the ocean and the underlying deforming solid mantle
(
Unno et al. 1979
, e.g.)
. These effects are secondary and can be absorbed into the free parameter
\sigma_{\rm R}

(
Farhat et al. 2022
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2023
, see e.g.)
.

II.6
Summary of solution method

With our problem formally set up,
we solve for the tidal lava flow as follows:

(
i
)

A 2-D mesh grid representing the dayside surface of the planet is established.

(
ii
)

On each grid point, and for a given tidal forcing frequency

\sigma
, the equilibrium tide is defined, and the system of Equations (
12
)–(
13
) is solved. In practice, the infinite system must be truncated
at a certain order
r_{\rm max}
. We test the solution dependence on
r_{\rm max}
and find that
r_{\rm max}=100
suffices.

(
iii
)

With the coefficients
p_{r}
and
p_{-r}
solved for at a specific forcing frequency, we recover the potential and stream functions
\Phi
and
\Psi
, respectively.

(
iv
)

Taking the gradients of these functions, we recover the complex displacement vector field

\hat{x}(\sigma,\theta,\lambda)=\gradient\Phi+\gradient\Psi\times\hat{r}
. We use the hat symbol on
\hat{x}
to denote that this is just one component of the displacement field specific to
\sigma
; later in step (vi) we will sum over all
\sigma
.

(
v
)

The complex velocity vector field is obtained by taking

\hat{u}(\sigma,\theta,\lambda)=i\sigma\hat{x}(\sigma,\theta,\lambda)
.

(
vi
)

The
time-dependent velocity field is finally computed by summing over the Fourier spectrum and taking the real part:

\vec{u}(\theta,\lambda,t)=\mathfrak{Re}\left\{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{q=0}^{p}\hat{u}(\sigma_{q}^{k},\theta,\lambda)\exp(i\sigma_q^kt)\right\}.

(15)

II.7
Evaluating the tidally dissipated power

With the velocity field in hand, the thin-shell Rayleigh drag produces a stress (force per area) of
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\rm R}=-\rho_{\rm m}H\,\sigma_{\rm R}\,\vec{u}
, where
\rho_{\rm m}
is the magma ocean’s density. The instantaneous rate at which this stress does work on the flow, i.e. the tidal dissipation per unit area, is

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\rm T}(\theta,\lambda,t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\rm R}\cdot\vec{u}

\displaystyle=\rho_{\rm m}H\sigma_{\rm R}\vec{u}(\theta,\lambda,t)\cdot\vec{u}(\theta,\lambda,t),

(16)

The total tidal heating in the ocean basin is obtained by integrating over the domain
\mathcal{O}
, and averaging over
a particular interval of time:

\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}=\left\langle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\rho_{\rm m}H\sigma_{\rm R}\vec{u}(\theta,\lambda,t)\cdot\vec{u}(\theta,\lambda,t)dA\right\rangle.

(17)

In practice we take the time average
\langle...\rangle
over 10 orbital periods.

In addition to tidal heating, we also compute the stellar insolation, modeling the dayside as a gray Lambertian surface. As the planet orbits its star, at each time
t
, we solve Kepler’s equation to obtain the eccentric anomaly
E_{\rm p}
and the star-planet distance

r_{\rm p}(t)=a_{\rm p}\,(1-e_{\rm p}\cos E_{\rm p}).

(18)

The bolometric stellar flux is then

F_{\star}(t)=\frac{L_{\star}}{4\pi\,r_{\rm p}^{2}(t)}=\sigma_{\rm SB}\,T_{\star}^{4}\left(\frac{R_{\star}}{r_{\rm p}(t)}\right)^{2},

(19)

where
\sigma_{\rm SB}
is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant, and
L_{\star}
,
R_{\star}
, and
T_{\star}
are the luminosity, radius, and effective temperature of the host star. The cosine of the local stellar zenith angle is

\mu_{0}(\theta,\lambda)=\sin\theta\,\cos\lambda
(where the substellar point is located at
\theta=\pi/2
and
\lambda=0
). The local absorbed insolation flux is then

\mathcal{P}_{\rm ins}(\theta,\lambda,t)=(1-A_{\rm B})\,F_{\star}(t)\,\mu_{0}(\theta,\lambda)\,\mathcal{H}\!\left[\mu_{0}(\theta,\lambda)\right],

(20)

where
A_{\rm B}=0.1
is the Bond albedo
(
Essack et al. 2020
)
and
\mathcal{H}
is the Heaviside step function that sets the nightside (
\mu_{0}\leq 0
) to zero incident flux. We have neglected any dependence on wavelength, reflectance angle, stellar limb darkening, and atmospheric scattering.
The effective surface temperature
T_{\rm s}
assumes blackbody radiation and full redistribution on the planet:

T_{\rm s}\equiv\left(\frac{\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}+\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm ins}}{4\pi R_{\rm p}^{2}\epsilon\sigma_{\rm SB}}\right)^{1/4}

(21)

where
\epsilon
is the planetary emissivity
(
Henderson et al. 1996
,
{\sim}0.9
in the infrared; e.g.)
.

H\rightarrow 0

To understand why
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}
peaks, we simplify the LTEs, keeping only a few ingredients: linear momentum with Rayleigh drag, hydrostatic pressure, and a forcing term
\hat{f}_{\rm T}
having only one frequency
\sigma
; plus mass continuity, all for just one horizontal mode of spherical degree
\ell
. The momentum equation simplifies to:

\left(-i\sigma+\sigma_{\rm R}\right)\hat{u}=-igk_{\ell}\hat{\zeta}+\hat{f}_{\rm T},

(22)

where we used
\partial_{t}\rightarrow i\sigma
and
\gradient\rightarrow ik_{\ell}
, with
k_{\ell}
being the spatial wavenumber of the forcing mode (
k_{\ell}=\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}/R_{\rm p}
). The continuity equation becomes

-i\sigma\hat{\zeta}+iHk_{\ell}\hat{u}=0.

(23)

The above two equations combine to yield the velocity:

\hat{u}=\frac{\sigma\hat{f}_{\rm T}}{gHk_{\ell}^{2}-\sigma^{2}+i\sigma\sigma_{\rm R}},

(24)

which is similar in form to the displacement amplitude (but not the velocity amplitude) of a damped forced oscillator with natural frequency
c_{\rm g}k_{\ell}
, where
c_{\rm g}\equiv\sqrt{gH}
is the phase speed of surface gravity waves in the shallow water limit.
The time-averaged dissipated power is

\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}=\rho_{\rm m}H\sigma_{\rm R}|\hat{u}|^{2}=\rho_{\rm m}H\sigma^{2}\sigma_{\rm R}\frac{|\hat{f}_{\rm T}|^{2}}{(gHk_{\ell}^{2}-\sigma^{2})^{2}+(\sigma\sigma_{\rm R})^{2}}

(25)

from which we can discern various limiting behaviors:

•

For
H\rightarrow 0
, the ocean has negligible mass, and the prefactor
\rho_{\rm m}H
causes
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}\propto H\rightarrow 0
.

•

For sufficiently large
H
, the natural mode frequency
c_{\rm g}k_{\ell}
becomes larger than the damping frequency
\sigma_{\rm R}
(which in turn is larger than the forcing frequency
\sigma
, by construction). In the limit that the natural frequency dominates the denominator of (
24
),
|\hat{u}|\propto H^{-1}
and
\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}\propto H(1/H^{2})\propto H^{-1}
, which explains why the curves decline toward larger
H
in Figure
3
. In this regime, waves traveling at speed
c_{\rm g}
cross a wavelength
1/k_{\ell}
in a time
1/(k_{\ell}c_{\rm g})
that is much shorter than either the forcing period
1/\sigma
or the damping timescale
1/\sigma_{\rm R}
.

•

For
H
between these limits, the dissipated power reaches an extremum at

H_{{\rm max}\,\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{gk_{\ell}^{2}}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\sigma_{\rm R}}{\sigma}\right)^{2}}.

(26)

Many studies (e.g.
Tyler 2011
;
Kamata et al. 2015
;
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2019
) are concerned with
the weakly damped limit (
\sigma_{\rm R}\ll\sigma)
where Eq. (
26
) implies the resonance condition
\sigma\approx\sqrt{gHk_{\ell}^{2}}
, when the forcing frequency matches the shallow water wave frequency.
By contrast, we are here concerned with the limit of strong damping,
\sigma_{\rm R}\gg\sigma
, whence Eq. (
26
) implies

\left.\sigma\right|_{{\rm max}\,\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}}\approx\frac{(c_{\rm g}k_{\ell})^{2}}{\sigma_{\rm R}},

(27)

and
H_{{\rm max}\,\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}}\propto\sigma_{\rm R}
at fixed
\sigma
, roughly consistent with Figure
3
.

For an analogy describing these limits, one can imagine shaking a pan of honey at fixed cadence. When the layer is very thin, it moves with the pan like a rigid sheet; there is insufficient depth to build surface slope or shear. When the layer of honey is very deep, most of it idles while a thin viscous layer at the bottom of the pan is stirred.
At some intermediate thickness, the whole column moves and shears coherently, maximizing the work done per cycle and thus the dissipated power.

IV
Magma oceans in thermal equilibrium

The previous section left the magma ocean thickness
H
as a free parameter. Here we relate
H
to the thermodynamics of the underlying mantle. We will identify steady states where the heat deposited by tidal dissipation can be carried to the surface by mantle convection and ultimately radiated away. These thermal equilibria determine
H
.

IV.1
Interior structure profiles

We follow
Moore 2003
;
Moore 2006
and search for thermal equilibrium states that balance internal heating with convective cooling, using a one-dimensional energy balance. We continue to treat our planet as airless (for studies that relax this assumption, see e.g.
Zahnle et al. 2015
;
Lichtenberg et al. 2021
;
Nicholls et al. 2024
).

For a viscous layer heated from below and cooled from above, the onset of convection is governed by the Rayleigh-Bénard criterion
(
Chandrasekhar 1961
;
Turcotte & Schubert 2002
, e.g.)
:

Ra\equiv\frac{\rho g\alpha\Delta T_{\rm sa}H^{3}}{\eta\kappa}>Ra_{\rm c}\approx 1708

(29)

where
Ra
is the Rayleigh number,
Ra_{\rm c}
is the critical threshold for convection,
\rho
is the density,
\alpha
the thermal expansivity,
H
the layer’s thickness,
\eta
the dynamic viscosity,
\kappa
the thermal diffusivity, and
\Delta T_{\rm sa}
the superadiabatic temperature difference. The latter is the excess of the actual top-to-bottom temperature drop over the adiabatic drop, taken across the entire ocean thickness
H
. We assume that excess is localized to a thin boundary layer at the ocean surface
(
Schubert et al. 2001
, e.g.)
. Thus we evaluate

\Delta T_{\rm sa}=T_{\rm p}-T_{\rm s}

(30)

where
T_{\rm s}
is the actual surface (blackbody) temperature (eq.
21
), and
T_{\rm p}
is the surface potential temperature, defined
as the temperature a parcel would have if brought adiabatically from the interior to the surface. Formally
T_{\rm p}
is a free parameter; it is a proxy for the mantle adiabat which is determined not only by tidal heating but also by radiogenic heating and the gravitational heat of formation, and the details of the planet’s formation history. In section
IV.3
, we will see that, given a planet mass, radius, and orbital period, only a certain value of
T_{\rm p}
allows for a stable thermal equilibrium between tidal heating and convective cooling.

For
Ra\gg Ra_{\rm c}
, the bulk interior relaxes toward an adiabat, while the superadiabatic gradient is confined to boundary layers that regulate the net heat flux
(
Schubert et al. 2001
, e.g.)
. Experiments constrain the dynamic viscosity of near-liquidus peridotitic melt at low pressures to be
{\sim}0.01{-}0.1~{\rm Pa~s}

(
Dingwell et al. 2004
;
Liebske et al. 2005
, e.g.)
, increasing to 100 Pa s for smaller melt fractions
(
Kushiro 1986
;
Rubie et al. 2003
, e.g.)
. For the magma ocean parameters we will determine later (section
IV.3
),
Ra\gg Ra_{\rm c}
. As the ocean cools by convection, crystallization may build compositional inversions: dense cumulates or a compacting mush atop lighter material. Such stratified arrangements are Rayleigh-Taylor (RT) unstable
(
Chandrasekhar 1961
;
Turcotte & Schubert 2002
;
Solomatov 2015
, e.g.)
and overturn on timescale
\tau_{\rm RT}
, where
2
2

2

This form of
\tau_{\rm RT}
corresponds to the fastest linear mode in a boundary layer. In the limit of long wavelength perturbations in a finite slab, one obtains
\tau_{\rm RT}^{-1}\propto d^{2}

(
Elkins-Tanton et al. 2003
;
Elkins-Tanton et al. 2005
)
.

\tau_{\rm RT}^{-1}\sim C_{\rm RT}\Delta\rho gd/\eta,

(31)

with
\Delta\rho
being the density contrast in the biphasic layer of thickness
d
, and
C_{\rm RT}
is a geometric coefficient of order unity
(
Wilcock & Whitehead 1991
;
Brown 1989
, e.g.)
. If
\tau_{\rm RT}
is long relative to the thermal evolution timescales, stratification persists and impedes large scale convective overturn. However, for magma ocean parameters,
\tau_{\rm RT}\sim 10^{-1}-10^{2}
yrs for Earth-like planets, and possibly shorter for larger planets, and is thus always shorter than secular cooling timescales
(
Solomatov 2015
, see e.g.)
. Consequently, RT continuously removes density inversions as they form, maintaining a well-mixed interior that is adiabatic in bulk over timescales of interest.

To see how a rocky planet with prescribed
(R_{\rm p},M_{\rm p})
divides into liquid, solid, and intermediate “mushy” phases, we follow the treatment adopted for the Earth in
Farhat et al. 2025
, except that we rescale all functions therein from the depth domain to the pressure domain assuming hydrostatic balance. Details of the procedure are in Appendix
D
.
For a prescribed potential temperature
T_{\rm p}
, one obtains smooth radial profiles of temperature, pressure, and density.

IV.2
Convective flux in the tidally heated mantle

The convective heat flux carried by the magma ocean is commonly parameterized in terms of a conductive heat flux:

\mathcal{F}_{\rm conv}=Nu\,C_{\rm p}\,\rho\,\kappa\,\Delta T_{\rm sa}/H

(32)

where
c_{\rm p}
is the isobaric specific heat (so that
c_{\rm p}\rho\kappa
is the thermal conductivity). The dimensionless Nusselt number
Nu>1
quantifies the efficiency of turbulent convection. Two Nusselt-Rayleigh laws are widely used
(
Heslot et al. 1987
;
Siggia 1994
;
Grossmann & Lohse 2000
, e.g.)
:

\displaystyle\text{Soft turbulence (lower $Ra$):}\quad Nu

\displaystyle=0.089Ra^{1/3}

(33)

\displaystyle\text{Hard turbulence (higher $Ra$):}\quad Nu

\displaystyle=0.22Ra^{2/7}Pr^{-1/7}

(34)

with
Pr=\eta/(\rho\kappa)
denoting the Prandtl number. The former describes a regime where viscous boundary layers sandwiching the convective region are laminar, with heat transfer throttled by boundary layer conduction and plume shedding. The latter pertains to turbulent boundary layers. Equating the two prescriptions gives a crossover at

Ra_{*}=\left(\frac{0.22}{0.089}Pr^{-1/7}\right)^{21}

(35)

above which hard turbulence should prevail. For silicate melts (
Pr\sim 10-10^{4}
),
Ra_{*}\lesssim 10^{6}
. It is likely that
Ra>Ra_{*}
(by orders of magnitude), in which case the hard turbulence law should hold. However, because the hard and soft prescriptions scale with
Ra
very similarly, the value for
Nu
predicted by hard turbulence is at most a factor of a few smaller than that predicted by soft turbulence. We elect to use the soft relation (
33
) as it gives larger
Nu
, i.e. stronger convective cooling, as a conservative test for whether tidal heating can sustain melt.

with the tidal power

Convection in the magma ocean thus responds only to the secular average of tidal heating. That tidal heating includes not only dissipation in the fluid ocean, but also dissipation in the solid portion of the mantle, which we model with a simplified Maxwell rheology (Appendix

Thermal equilibria

We solve for thermal equilibria where convection carries tidally generated heat from the mantle to the surface, whereupon it is radiated to space.

P_{\rm orb}

For the interior structure profiles shown in Figure

(

Efroimsky 2012

the Maxwell time
\tau_{\rm M}=\eta(T_{\rm p})/\mu_{\rm s}

\sigma\tau_{\rm M}\gg 1

\sigma\tau_{\rm M}\ll 1

For
T_{\rm p}\gtrsim 1600~{\rm K}

curve and then the liquidus. A surface fluid layer is created atop a mushy layer which in turn overlies the solid mantle. The presence of fluid tides within the magma ocean causes a second peak to emerge in the left panel of Figure

T_{\rm p}\approx 3500

T_{\rm p}\gtrsim 1600~{\rm K}

Timescales

To reach one of the stable equilibria for a given
P_{\rm orb}

C_{\rm m}(T_{\rm p})\frac{dT_{\rm p}}{dt}=\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}(T_{\rm p},P_{\rm orb})-\mathcal{P}_{\rm conv}(T_{\rm p},P_{\rm orb}),

(38)

(39)

where
R_{\rm m}=0.46R_{\rm p}
is the thickness of the mantle,
\rho
is the density profile,
c_{\rm p}
is the isobaric specific heat,
H_{\rm f}
is the latent heat of fusion, and
F_{\rm m}
is the melt fraction; all these quantities are defined in Appendix
D

T_{\rm p;eq}

T_{\rm p;0}

\tau_{\rm settle}=\int_{T_{\rm p;0}}^{T_{\rm p;eq}}\frac{C_{\rm m}(T_{\rm p})}{\big|\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}(T_{\rm p},P_{\rm orb})-\mathcal{P}_{\rm conv}(T_{\rm p},P_{\rm orb})\big|}\,dT_{\rm p}.

(40)

Computing
\tau_{\rm settle}
on the
(P_{\rm orb,}T_{\rm p})

P_{\rm orb}\lesssim 3.5~{\rm days}
settle onto the stable, high temperature equilibrium branch over
\tau_{\rm settle}\sim 10^{2}-10^{3}~{\rm yr}
, while those with
3.5\lesssim P_{\rm orb}\lesssim 80~{\rm days}
settle onto the solid-state thermal equilibrium over
\tau_{\rm settle}\sim 10^{5}-10^{6}~{\rm yr}
.

energy is converted into heat (a small fraction of the energy comes from the spin since the planet is not perfectly synchronous). The loss of orbital energy leads to orbital decay on the timescale

\tau_{\rm a}\equiv\frac{a_{\rm p}}{|\dot{a}_{\rm p}|}=\frac{GM_{\star}M_{\rm p}}{2a_{\rm p}\overline{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm T}}\,.

(41)

V
Summary and Outlook

Stellar irradiation may create “puddles” of melt on the dayside surfaces of short-period rocky planets. By contrast, a non-zero orbital eccentricity (or obliquity, or asynchronous rotation) provides a heat source that penetrates more deeply: gravitational tides, raised by the host star, which repeatedly strain the planet’s interior. We have investigated the possibility that tidal heating
melts large portions of the mantle into magma oceans. The tidal response of this ocean may not be captured by the usual
theories of viscoelasticity. Instead, the fluid ocean may support long-wavelength (shallow-water) gravity waves that can propagate across the dayside while being damped by viscosity and friction. The dissipation of such “lava tidal waves” can lead to potentially observable, time-variable thermal emission.

Eccentricity tides depend on eccentricity. For practically all known short-period rocky
exoplanets, eccentricities measured from stellar radial velocities are

\lesssim 0.05
–0.1. But
eccentricities of a few percent suffice for tidal heating to
qualitatively alter the global thermodynamics. Plausibly, in a subset of systems, eccentricities of this order may be secularly forced and
maintained over timescales comparable to
the system age, by exterior companions with measurably
eccentric orbits (Figures
1
and
12
, Appendix
A
).

Our solutions to the Laplace tidal equations for
creep flow across the dayside hemisphere, coupled to interior models for fluid and solid-state convection, taught us the following:

i
)

The larger the orbital eccentricity, the
broader the spectrum of lava waves that are driven. At
eccentricities of a few percent, wave interference causes the
dayside-averaged brightness temperature to vary aperiodically, on timescales faster than the orbital period by factors of 2–4 (Figures
4
and
5
). The dayside hotspot wanders, mostly eastward of the substellar point but sometimes westward (Figure
6
). The amplitudes of these variations may be observable — of order hundreds of K for the dayside-averaged brightness temperature, and tens of degrees for the hotspot longitude — if the tidal power exceeds the stellar insolation in thermal equilibrium, a condition that depends on model details.

ii
)

At orbital periods shorter than
\sim
3 days and eccentricities of a few percent, tidal heating creates deep magma oceans. For smaller Earth-sized planets, the fluid lava ocean can extend all the way to the
core-mantle boundary. For larger super-Earths, the ocean, still hundreds of kilometers deep, overlies a mushy two-phase layer that transitions to a
solid viscoelastic lower mantle (Figure

10
). These interior profiles represent
stable, self-consistent thermal equilibria where the energy
deposited into the planet interior by tides (plus radioactivity and the
heat of formation) is transported steadily out of the mantle by a combination of fluid and solid-state convection (capped by stellar insolation as a boundary condition; Figure
9
). Fluid and semi-fluid equilibria can persist against orbital (semimajor axis) decay for times comparable
to or longer than the system age (Figure
11
), assuming eccentricity can
be maintained by external forcing (Figure
12
).

Failing eccentricity, other sources of tidal heating are planetary obliquity and asynchronous rotation (e.g.
Makarov 2012
;
Correia & Delisle 2019
;
Millholland & Laughlin 2019
). Our qualitative results on the thermal behavior of tidally heated rocky worlds, namely the possibility of deep fluid oceans and aperiodic variability in surface thermal emission, should be independent of the origin of tidal stress.

Are lava waves driven by eccentricity tides a root cause for the dramatic time variability observed in 55 Cnc e (see Introduction)? The current upper limit of
e_{\rm p}\lesssim 0.05

(
Bourrier et al. 2018
)
does not rule out significant tidal heating, but there is no clear mechanism (read: exterior perturber) by which a percent-level eccentricity for 55 Cnc e can be maintained (
Ferraz-Mello & Beaugé 2025
). More accurate eccentricity measurements for more short-period planets, together with synoptic studies, are welcome.

Other proposed explanations for time variability in ultra-short period planets like 55 Cnc e invoke climate feedbacks from silicate cloud cycles (
Loftus et al. 2025
; see also
Bromley & Chiang 2023
), stochastic degassing from the planet interior
(
Heng 2023
)
, and
circumplanetary/circumstellar dust/gas tori fed by planetary outflows

(
Valdés et al. 2023
;
Patel et al. 2024
)
. These proposals, which involve surface/atmosphere dynamics in one way or another, are not incompatible with forcing by lava tides. As equilibrium vapor pressures are exponentially sensitive to surface temperature, an irregularly heated magma ocean may drive large aperiodic variations in atmospheric mass. The chemistry and elemental exchange between ocean and atmosphere are expected to change continuously as regions of active degassing and condensation shift across the planet. Tidally driven thermal pulses may also power vertical flows and atmospheric escape.

Our assumption that the planet nightside is solid (effectively frozen) may be questioned. Day and night hemispheres are generally equally stressed by tides, and we have shown that depending on the magma ocean thickness, tidal heating may overpower stellar insolation. Heat may also be transported across the terminator by
atmospheric flows
(
Castan & Menou 2011
, e.g.)
; the pressure head of the dayside ocean percolating melt through a permeable nightside
(
Bear 1972
;
Turcotte & Schubert 2002
, e.g.)
; or horizontal convection in the planet interior
(
Hughes & Griffiths 2008
;
Noto et al. 2025
, e.g.)
. Day-night thermal contrasts as probed by phase curves may thus be muted by tidal heating.

Appendix A
Maintaining eccentricity by secular forcing

We discuss how the inner planet’s nonzero eccentricity,
e_{\rm p}\neq 0
, may be maintained by secular forcing from an exterior eccentric planet. We define the normalized angular momentum vector
\vec{j}_{\rm p}=\sqrt{1-e_{\rm p}^{2}}~\hat{n}_{\rm p}
and the Lenz vector
\vec{e}_{\rm p}=e_{\rm p}\hat{u}_{\rm p}
, where
\hat{n}_{\rm p}
and
\hat{u}_{\rm p}
are unit vectors pointing in the directions of the
orbital normal and periapse, respectively. Analogous vectors with subscript ‘c’ refer to the orbit of the exterior companion of mass
M_{\rm c}
, semi-major axis
a_{\rm c}
, and eccentricity
e_{\rm c}
.
The orbit-averaged multipolar interaction between two planets in terms of these vectors is well documented in studies of hierarchical triples

(
Tremaine et al. 2009
;
Correia et al. 2011
;
Hamers 2020
;
Farhat & Touma 2021
;
Farhat et al. 2023
, e.g.)
. The secular Hamiltonian is

H_{\rm S}=\frac{GM_{\rm c}\mu_{\rm p}}{a_{\rm c}}\left(\Psi_{\rm quad}+\Psi_{\rm oct}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{4})

(A1)

where we have dropped the constant Keplerian terms,
\mu_{\rm p}=M_{\rm p}M_{\star}/(M_{\star}+M_{\rm p})
is the reduced mass of
the inner planet with
M_{\rm p}
the inner planet’s mass and
M_{\rm\star}
the star’s mass,
\alpha=a_{\rm p}/a_{\rm c}
, and the quadrupolar and octupolar terms of the disturbing function (assuming coplanar orbits) read as

\Psi_{\rm quad}=-\frac{3}{4}\varepsilon_{\rm q}e_{\rm p}^{2}

(A2)

\Psi_{\rm oct}=-\frac{15}{16}\varepsilon_{\rm q}\varepsilon_{\rm o}e_{\rm p}\cos\Delta\varpi+\mathcal{O}(e_{\rm p}^{2})

(A3)

with
\Delta\varpi=\varpi_{\rm c}-\varpi_{\rm p}
equal to the difference in periapse longitudes between the outer and inner orbits, and the dimensionless coefficients

\varepsilon_{\rm q}=\alpha^{2}(1-e_{\rm c}^{2})^{-3/2},\hskip 14.22636pt\varepsilon_{\rm o}=\alpha\frac{M_{\rm\star}-M_{\rm p}}{M_{\rm\star}+M_{\rm p}}\frac{e_{\rm c}}{1-e_{\rm c}^{2}}.

(A4)

The equations governing the eccentricity evolution of each orbit are

\frac{d\vec{e}_{k}}{dt}=-L_{k}^{-1}\left(\vec{j}_{k}\times\nabla_{\vec{e}_{k}}H_{\rm S}+\vec{e}_{k}\times\nabla_{\vec{j}_{k}}H_{\rm S}\right)-\gamma_{\rm p}\vec{e}_{\rm p}\delta_{k{\rm p}},

(A5)

with
k
= ‘p’ or ‘c’,
\delta_{k{\rm p}}
the Kronecker delta,
L_{\rm p}=\mu_{\rm p}\sqrt{G(M_{\star}+M_{\rm p})a_{\rm p}(1-e_{\rm p}^{2})},
and
L_{\rm c}=\mu_{\rm c}\sqrt{G(M_{\star}+M_{\rm c})a_{\rm c}(1-e_{\rm c}^{2})}
with
\mu_{\rm c}=M_{\star}M_{\rm c}/(M_{\star}+M_{\rm c})
the reduced mass of the companion. The last term models the tidal damping of the inner planet (ignored for the outer planet) characterized by the rate
\gamma_{\rm p}

a_{\rm p}(1-e_{\rm p}^{2})=\rm constant,
which gives

\gamma_{\rm p}=\frac{1-e_{\rm p}^{2}}{2e_{\rm p}^{2}\tau_{\rm a}},

(A6)

with
\tau_{\rm a}
given by Eq. (
41
).

Considering
\Psi_{\rm quad}
only, and taking the appropriate derivatives of
H_{\rm S}
, we find:

\displaystyle\frac{d\vec{e}_{\rm p}}{dt}\Bigg|_{\rm quad}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}n_{\rm p}\frac{M_{\rm c}}{M_{\star}+M_{\rm p}}\alpha^{3}(1-e_{\rm c}^{2})^{-3/2}\vec{j}_{\rm p}\crossproduct\vec{e}_{\rm p}

\displaystyle\equiv\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p;quad}~\vec{j}_{\rm p}\crossproduct\vec{e}_{\rm p},

(A7)

where
n_{\rm p}
is the mean motion of the inner planet. Equation (

e_{\rm p}

n_{\rm c}

\frac{d\vec{e}_{\rm c}}{dt}\Big|_{\rm quad}=\dot{\varpi}_{\rm c;quad}\,\vec{j}_{\rm c}\times\vec{e}_{\rm c},

(A8)

with a rate given by

\dot{\varpi}_{\rm c;quad}=\frac{3}{4}\,n_{\rm c}\,\frac{M_{\rm p}}{M_{\star}+M_{\rm c}}\,\alpha^{2}+\mathcal{O}(e_{\rm p}^{2}).

(A9)

Considering the octupolar term to first order in
e_{\rm p}

\frac{d\vec{e}_{\rm p}}{dt}\Bigg|_{\rm oct}=-\frac{15}{16}\frac{GM_{\rm c}}{a_{\rm c}}\varepsilon_{\rm q}\varepsilon_{\rm o}L_{\rm p}^{-1}\vec{j}_{\rm p}\crossproduct\vec{e}_{\rm c}\equiv\vec{S},

(A10)

an in-plane vector perpendicular to
\vec{e}_{\rm c}
of magnitude

{s}=|\vec{S}|=\frac{5}{4}\varepsilon_{\rm o}\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p;quad},

(A11)

(A12)

where

\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p;GR}=\frac{3GM_{\star}}{c^{2}a_{\rm p}}\frac{n_{\rm p}}{1-e_{\rm p}^{2}},

c
is the speed of light. Adding effects together, we have:
\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p}=\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p;quad}+\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p;GR}+\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p;tide}
and
\dot{\varpi}_{\rm c}\equiv\dot{\varpi}_{\rm c;quad}
.

Consider a fixed right-handed basis
(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\hat{\boldsymbol{y}},\hat{\boldsymbol{z}})
with
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}=\hat{n}_{\rm p}=\hat{n}_{\rm c}
, such that
\vec{e}_{k}=e_{kx}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}+e_{ky}\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}
. The vector equations (
A5
) are resolved in two dimensions as:

\displaystyle\dot{e}_{{\rm p}x}

\displaystyle=\,-\,\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p}\,e_{{\rm p}y}\;+\;\frac{s}{e_{\rm c}}\,e_{{\rm c}y}\;-\;\gamma_{\rm p}e_{{\rm p}x},

(A15)

\displaystyle\dot{e}_{{\rm p}y}

\displaystyle=\;\;\,\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p}\,e_{{\rm p}x}\;-\;\frac{s}{e_{\rm c}}\,e_{{\rm c}x}\;-\;\gamma_{\rm p}e_{{\rm p}y},

(A16)

\displaystyle\dot{e}_{{\rm c}x}

\displaystyle=\,-\,\dot{\varpi}_{\rm c}\,e_{{\rm c}y}\;+\;\frac{s}{e_{\rm c}}\frac{L_{\rm p}}{L_{\rm c}}\,e_{{\rm p}y},

(A17)

\displaystyle\dot{e}_{{\rm c}y}

\displaystyle=\;\;\,\dot{\varpi}_{\rm c}\,e_{{\rm c}x}\;-\;\frac{s}{e_{\rm c}}\frac{L_{\rm p}}{L_{\rm c}}\,e_{{\rm p}x}.

(A18)

Using the complex Poincaré eccentricity variables
z_{\rm p}\equiv e_{{\rm p}x}+ie_{{\rm p}y}=e_{\rm p}e^{i\varpi_{\rm p}}
and
z_{\rm c}\equiv e_{{\rm c}x}+ie_{{\rm c}y}=e_{\rm c}e^{i\varpi_{\rm c}}
, the evolution of the system is recast as:

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{\rm p}-i\,\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p}\,z_{\rm p}+\gamma_{\rm p}z_{\rm p}=-\,i\,\frac{s}{e_{\rm c}}\,z_{\rm c},

(A19)

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{\rm c}-i\,\dot{\varpi}_{\rm c}\,z_{\rm c}=-\,i\,\frac{s}{e_{\rm c}}\frac{L_{\rm p}}{L_{\rm c}}\,z_{\rm p}.

(A20)

Let each orbit have a free and a forced mode (
z_{k}=z_{k}^{(0)}+z_{k}^{(\rm forced)}
). We presume the free mode of the inner orbit
z_{\rm p}^{(0)}
is damped to zero by tides. For stationary solutions (
\dot{z}_{\rm p}=\dot{z}_{\rm c}=0
), equation (
A20
) gives

z_{\rm c}^{(\rm forced)}=\frac{s}{\dot{\varpi}_{\rm c}\,e_{\rm c}}\frac{L_{\rm p}}{L_{\rm c}}\,z_{\rm p}.

(A21)

Substituting
z_{\rm c}=z_{\rm c}^{(0)}+z_{\rm c}^{(\rm forced)}
into (
A19
) with
\dot{z}_{\rm p}=0
gives

\Big(\gamma_{\rm p}-i\,\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p}+\frac{s^{2}}{e_{\rm c}^{2}}\frac{L_{\rm p}}{L_{\rm c}}\frac{1}{-\,i\,\dot{\varpi}_{\rm c}}\Big)z_{\rm p}=-\,i\,\frac{s}{e_{\rm c}}\,z_{\rm c}^{(0)}.

(A22)

Hence the complex forced solution is

z_{\rm p}^{(\rm forced)}=\frac{-\,i\,\dfrac{s}{e_{\rm c}}}{\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm p}-i\,\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p}+\frac{1}{-i\dot{\varpi}_{\rm c}}\frac{s^{2}}{e_{\rm c}^{2}}\frac{L_{\rm p}}{L_{\rm c}}\,}z_{\rm c}^{(0)}.

(A23)

Taking the modulus, and taking hereafter
e_{\rm c}\approx e_{\rm c}^{(0)}
yields the inner forced eccentricity

e_{\rm p}^{(\rm forced)}=\frac{s}{\left|\,\gamma_{\rm p}-i\,\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p}+\dfrac{1}{-i\dot{\varpi}_{\rm c}}\dfrac{s^{2}}{(e_{\rm c}^{(0)})^{2}}\dfrac{L_{\rm p}}{L_{\rm c}}\,\right|}.

(A24)

In the limit of negligible back-reaction
(L_{\rm p}/L_{\rm c}\simeq(M_{\rm p}/M_{\rm c})\sqrt{a_{\rm p}/a_{\rm c}}\rightarrow 0)
, Eq. (
A24
) reduces to

~e_{\rm p}^{(\rm forced)}\simeq\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p}^{2}+\gamma_{\rm p}^{2}}}.

(A25)

The inner-planet eccentricity increases from the outer-planet octupolar potential through
s
, and decreases from precession and tidal damping.

Since the forced
e_{\rm p}
enters on both sides of
(
A24
), we solve for it by fixed point iteration,
starting with
e_{\rm p}=(5/4)\varepsilon_{\rm o}
(the solution
without extra precession, damping, or backreaction). Sample solutions
for
e_{\rm p}

for our fiducial super-Earth are plotted as colored contours in

, for either
M_{\rm c}=10M_{\oplus}
or

M_{\rm c}=1M_{\rm J}
, with a fixed damping rate
\gamma_{\rm p}=2\times 10^{-7}
yr
-1
. The latter, estimated from
Eq. (
A6
) with
e_{\rm p}=0.05
and
\tau_{\rm a}=10^{9}~

(Figure

is generally smaller than

\dot{\varpi}_{\rm p}
.

In Figure

\alpha\gtrsim 0.2
and not near a mean-motion resonance, the multipole series converges slower than in more hierarchical systems. To first order in

the hexadecapole (

The quasi-equilibrium of Eq. (

) is maintained as long as the outer planet is eccentric. We now estimate the timescale for circularizing the outer orbit. We first define the Angular Momentum Deficit (AMD) of orbit

{\rm AMD}_{k}\equiv\Lambda_{k}-L_{k}=\Lambda_{k}\left(1-\sqrt{1-e_{k}^{2}}\right)

(A26)

where the circular-orbit angular momentum

\Lambda_{k}=\mu_{k}\sqrt{G(M_{\star}+M_{k})a_{k}}.

(A27)

The only way the total AMD of the system can change is through dissipation, i.e. tides.
When tides act only on the inner planet, the time derivative of the total AMD of the two-planet system is

\frac{d(\rm AMD)}{dt}=\left(1-\sqrt{1-e_{\rm p}^{2}}\right)\dot{\Lambda}_{\rm p}-\Lambda_{\rm p}\frac{e_{\rm p}^{2}}{\sqrt{1-e_{\rm p}^{2}}}\gamma_{\rm p}.

(A28)

Setting

\frac{de_{\rm c}}{dt}=\frac{\left(1-\sqrt{1-e_{\rm p}^{2}}\right)\dot{\Lambda}_{\rm p}-\Lambda_{\rm p}\dfrac{e_{\rm p}^{2}}{\sqrt{1-e_{\rm p}^{2}}}\gamma_{\rm p}}{\Lambda_{\rm p}\dfrac{e_{\rm p}}{\sqrt{1-e_{\rm p}^{2}}}\left(|\mathcal{K}|+e_{\rm c}\dfrac{d|\mathcal{K}|}{de_{\rm c}}\right)+\Lambda_{\rm c}\dfrac{e_{\rm c}}{\sqrt{1-e_{\rm c}^{2}}}}.

(A30)

A23

\mathcal{K}

, so that
e_{\rm p}^{(\rm forced)}=|\mathcal{K}|e_{\rm c}^{(\rm 0)}
. Since migration is much slower than eccentricity damping, we set
\dot{\Lambda}_{\rm p}=0
.
Approximating
e_{\rm p}\sim e_{\rm c}\ll 1

A30

\frac{de_{\rm c}}{dt}\simeq-\dfrac{L_{\rm p}\gamma_{\rm p}|\mathcal{K}|^{2}}{L_{\rm c}+L_{\rm p}|\mathcal{K}|^{2}}e_{\rm c}

(A31)

which gives the outer-orbit circularization timescale

(A32)

Appendix B
Solution method for the Laplace Tidal Equations

We describe the method used to derive the linear system of Equations (
12
) and (
13
). The approach is similar to that in
Farhat et al. 2022
and
Auclair-Desrotour et al. 2023
but we provide it in full here for reference.

We first establish a useful identity. If one combines the identity

\gradient\cdot\left(\Phi\gradient\Psi\crossproduct\hat{r}\right)=\left(\gradient\Psi\crossproduct\hat{r}\right)\cdot\gradient\Phi

(B1)

with Gauss’s theorem

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\gradient\cdot\left(\Phi\gradient\Psi\crossproduct\hat{r}\right)dA=\oint_{\partial\mathcal{O}}\Phi\left(\gradient\Psi\crossproduct\hat{r}\right)\cdot\hat{n}\,d\ell,

(B2)

dA
being an infinitesimal area element of the ocean domain
\mathcal{O}
, and
d\ell
being a length element on the terminator, one obtains

\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left(\gradient\Phi\right)\cdot\left(\gradient\Psi\crossproduct\hat{r}\right)dA=\oint_{\partial\mathcal{O}}\Phi\left(\gradient\Psi\crossproduct\hat{r}\right)\cdot\hat{n}\,d\ell.

(B3)

The right hand side of this equation is zero, by virtue of the imposed boundary condition on the stream function. Then the curl-free and divergence-free components of the tidal flow on the left hand side are orthogonal.

Returning to the coupled system of LTEs, substituting the series expansions of
\Phi
and
\Psi
in the momentum conservation equation (
1a
), and then taking the dot product with
\gradient\phi_{r}
, we get:

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}^{2}p_{s}+\sigma_{\rm R}\partial_{t}p_{s}+gHp_{s}\mu_{s}-g{\zeta}_{\mathrm{eq},s}\right)\gradient\phi_{s}\cdot\gradient\phi_{r}

\displaystyle+\left(\partial_{t}^{2}p_{-s}+\sigma_{\rm R}\partial_{t}p_{-s}\right)\left(\gradient\psi_{s}\crossproduct\hat{r}\right)\cdot\gradient\phi_{r}

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}p_{s}\left(\vec{f}\crossproduct\gradient\phi_{s}\right)\cdot\gradient\phi_{r}

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}p_{-s}\left[\vec{f}\crossproduct\left(\gradient\psi_{s}\crossproduct\hat{r}\right)\right]\cdot\gradient\phi_{r}=0.

(B4)

Using Green’s first identity, we compute the product of the gradient of the two eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{O}}\gradient\phi_{s}\cdot\gradient\phi_{r}dA

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\mathcal{O}}\phi_{s}\left(\gradient\phi_{r}\cdot\hat{n}\right)d\ell-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\phi_{s}\gradient^{2}\phi_{r}dA

\displaystyle=\mu_{r}\delta_{r,s}.

(B5)

The latter equality follows from the first term on the right hand side vanishing due to the boundary condition of the potential function, and using the eigenvalue equation with the normalization condition of the eigenfunctions on the second term.
Integrating Eq. (
B4
) over the magma ocean domain, we find

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}^{2}p_{s}+\sigma_{\rm R}\partial_{t}p_{s}+gHp_{s}\mu_{s}-g{\zeta}_{\mathrm{eq},s}\right)\mu_{r}\delta_{r,s}

\displaystyle+\left(\partial_{t}^{2}p_{-s}+\sigma_{\rm R}\partial_{t}p_{-s}\right)\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left(\gradient\psi_{s}\crossproduct\hat{r}\right)\cdot\gradient\phi_{r}\,dA

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}p_{s}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\vec{f}\cdot\left(\gradient\phi_{s}\crossproduct\gradient\phi_{r}\right)dA

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}p_{-s}\int_{\mathcal{O}}\left(\vec{f}\cdot\hat{r}\right)\left(\gradient\phi_{r}\cdot\gradient\psi_{s}\right)dA=0.

(B6)

The second term vanishes by virtue of the identity we established in Eq. (
B3
). Using
\vec{f}=2\Omega\cos\theta\hat{r}
we rearrange the equation to obtain:

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}^{2}p_{r}+\sigma_{\rm R}\partial_{t}p_{r}+gHp_{r}\mu_{r}-g{\zeta}_{\mathrm{eq},r}\right)\mu_{r}

\displaystyle-2\Omega\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\partial_{t}p_{s}\int\cos\theta\hat{r}\cdot\left(\gradient\phi_{r}\crossproduct\gradient\phi_{s}\right)dA

\displaystyle+2\Omega\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\partial_{t}p_{-s}\int\cos\theta\left(\gradient\phi_{r}\cdot\gradient\psi_{s}\right)dA=0.

(B7)

Going through a similar procedure but starting by taking the dot product of the momentum equation with
\gradient\psi_{r}\crossproduct\hat{r}
instead of
\gradient\phi_{r}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}p_{-r}+\sigma_{\rm R}\partial_{t}p_{-r}-\frac{2\Omega}{\nu_{r}}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\partial_{t}p_{s}\int\cos\theta\left(\gradient\phi_{s}\cdot\gradient\psi_{r}\right)dA

\displaystyle-\frac{2\Omega}{\nu_{r}}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\partial_{t}p_{-s}\int\cos\theta\hat{r}\cdot\left(\gradient\psi_{r}\crossproduct\gradient\psi_{s}\right)dA=0.

(B8)

Following previous works
(
Proudman 1920b
;
Proudman 1920a
;
Longuet-Higgins & Pond 1970
;
Webb 1980
;
Farhat et al. 2022
)
, we identify in the system of Eqs. (
B
) and (
B
) the so-called gyroscopic coefficients:

\displaystyle\beta_{r,s}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\cos\theta\,\hat{r}\cdot\left(\gradient\phi_{r}\crossproduct\gradient\phi_{s}\right)dA,

\displaystyle\beta_{r,-s}

\displaystyle=\ \ \,\int_{\mathcal{O}}\cos\theta\,\gradient\phi_{r}\cdot\gradient\psi_{s}dA,

\displaystyle\beta_{-r,s}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\cos\theta\,\gradient\psi_{r}\cdot\gradient\phi_{s}dA,

\displaystyle\beta_{-r,-s}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{O}}\cos\theta\,\hat{r}\cdot\left(\gradient\psi_{r}\crossproduct\gradient\psi_{s}\right)dA,

(B9)

with
\beta_{-s,r}=-\beta_{r,-s}
. With these definitions, we recast the system of Eqs. (
B
) and (
B
) into:

\partial_{t}^{2}p_{r}+\sigma_{\rm R}\partial_{t}p_{r}+gH\mu_{r}p_{r}-g{\zeta}_{\mathrm{eq},r}+\frac{2\Omega}{\mu_{r}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{s=\infty}\beta_{r,s}\partial_{t}p_{s}=0,

(B10)

\partial_{t}^{2}p_{-r}+\sigma_{\rm R}\partial_{t}p_{-r}+\frac{2\Omega}{\nu_{r}}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{s=\infty}\beta_{-r,s}\partial_{t}p_{s}=0.

(B11)

Transforming this system to the Fourier domain using the tidal forcing frequency
\sigma
, i.e.
\partial_{t}=i\sigma
, we recover Eqs. (
12
)-(
13
) in the main text.

Appendix C
Hansen Coefficients

We compute the Hansen coefficients
X^{n,m}_{k}(e_{\rm p})
as the Fourier coefficients in the mean anomaly
M_{\rm p}
of the
2\pi
-periodic function

f(M_{\rm p})\equiv\left(\tfrac{r_{\rm p}}{a_{\rm p}}\right)^{n}e^{\,imv_{\rm p}(M_{\rm p})},

(C1)

namely

X^{n,m}_{k}(e_{\rm p})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\left(\frac{r_{\rm p}}{a_{\rm p}}\right)^{n}e^{\,imv_{\rm p}(M_{\rm p})}e^{-ikM_{\rm p}}\,\mathrm{d}M_{\rm p},

(C2)

or equivalently,

\left(\frac{r_{\rm p}}{a_{\rm p}}\right)^{n}e^{\,imv_{\rm p}}=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty}X^{n,m}_{k}(e_{\rm p})\,e^{\,ikM_{\rm p}},

(C3)

with the Kepler relations
M_{\rm p}=E_{\rm p}-e_{\rm p}\sin E_{\rm p}
,
{r_{\rm p}}/{a_{\rm p}}=1-e_{\rm p}\cos E_{\rm p}
, and

v_{\rm p}=\operatorname{atan2}\!\bigl(\sqrt{1-e_{\rm p}^{2}}\sin E_{\rm p},\,\cos E_{\rm p}-e_{\rm p}\bigr)
.
Practically, we sample
M_{{\rm p},j}=j\,\Delta M_{\rm p}
uniformly on
[0,2\pi]
with
N=2^{p}
points (so
\Delta M_{\rm p}=2\pi/N
), solve Kepler’s equation for
E_{{\rm p},j}
, form
f_{{\rm p},j}=(1-e_{\rm p}\cos E_{{\rm p},j})^{n}\,e^{\,imv_{{\rm p},j}}
, and evaluate the discrete Fourier transform

Z_{k}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}f_{{\rm p},j}\,e^{-ikM_{{\rm p},j}}.

(C4)

For elliptic motion the coefficients
X^{n,m}_{k}(e_{\rm p})
are real, so numerically we set
X^{n,m}_{k}(e_{\rm p})=\real\{Z_{k}\}
.
This FFT implementation is consistent with the classical definition and properties of Hansen coefficients
(
Hughes 1981
)
, their generalized connection to Laplace coefficients
(
Laskar 2005
)
, and modern disturbing-function expansions with small
e_{\rm p}
scalings
(
Mardling 2013
)
.

Appendix D
Mantle phases

We first structure the phase diagram by adopting parameterized cubic spline radial functions for the solidus,
T^{\rm sld}(r)
, the liquidus,
T^{\rm liq}(r)
, and the temperature profile
T^{\rm crit}(r)
corresponding to a critical melt fraction
F_{\rm m}=40\%

(
Zhang & Herzberg 1994
;
Fiquet et al. 2010
;
Miyazaki & Korenaga 2019
)
. For temperatures above (below)
T^{\rm liq}
(
T^{\rm sld}
), the silicate mantle layer is fully molten (solidified), while the
T^{\rm crit}
profile in between defines the rheological transition from a deformable solid matrix that is penetrated by a network of percolating melt to a fluid magma layer with suspended solids, and around which a regime of mushy magma exists
(
Abe 1997
, e.g.)
.

To construct the mantle adiabats living in this phase diagram, we integrate the standard entropy conserving gradient for a chosen mantle potential temperature
T_{\rm p}
and a reference pressure
P_{0}
and following it downward:

\frac{{\rm d}T}{{\rm d}P}\Bigg|^{\rm ad}=\frac{\alpha(P,T)\,T}{\rho(P,T)\,C_{\rm p}(P,T)},

(D1)

where
\alpha
is thermal expansivity,
\rho
is the density, and
C_{\rm p}
is the isobaric heat capacity. These material properties, along with the phase diagram functions, are evaluated on the fly using the cubic spline fits and parametrized relations delineated explicitly in
Miyazaki & Korenaga 2019
and
Korenaga 2023
. The only modification we implement is that, while the relations therein are Earth depth dependent, we move to a pressure dependent scale for an arbitrary
(R_{\rm p},M_{\rm p})
by assuming hydrostatic balance and rescaling the outer boundary gravity. We assume that the mantle volume of each planet is proportionally similar to that of the Earth
(
Stacey & Davis 2008
,
\sim 84\%
of the planetary volume, e.g. )
, which allows us to define the pressure base at the core-mantle boundary.

In the biphasic regime where mantle temperatures lie between the solidus and the liquidus, the adiabatic profile of Eq. (
D1
) is modified to include the latent heat of fusion à la Eq. (39) of
Farhat et al. 2025
:

\frac{dT}{dP}\Bigg|_{T^{\rm sld}>T>T^{\rm liq}}=\frac{dT}{dP}\Bigg|^{\rm ad}+\frac{H_{\rm f}}{C_{\rm p}}\gamma,

(D2)

with
H_{\rm f}
denoting the latent heat of fusion, linearly interpolated from
6\times 10^{5}
J kg
-1
at the surface to

9\times 10^{6}
J kg
-1
at the core-mantle boundary, and
\gamma
defining the gradient of melt with pressure
(
Langmuir et al. 1992
)
:

\gamma=\left({\frac{dT}{dP}\Bigg|^{\rm ad}-\frac{dT^{\rm sld}}{dP}}\right)\left({\frac{H_{\rm f}}{C_{\rm p}}+\frac{dT}{dF_{\rm m}}}\right)^{-1}.

(D3)

Finally, we define for the mantle a radial viscosity profile that follows the Arrhenius equation, only modified to include the effect of partial melt within the solid matrix, i.e.,

\eta(r,T)=\eta_{\rm s}(r)\exp\{-BF_{\rm m}\}\exp\left\{\frac{E_{\rm a}}{RT^{s}(r)}\left(\frac{T^{s}(r)}{T}-1\right)\right\}.

(D4)

Here
\eta_{\rm s}
is the viscosity profile of the solid background ignoring the effect of the melt, i.e., at the solidus temperature;
B
is an experimentally fitted parameter that ranges between 10 and 40
(
Mei et al. 2002
, e.g.,
B\sim 26
for diffusion creep; )
,
E_{\rm a}=300\ {\rm kJ\ mol^{-1}}
is the activation energy, and
R=8.314462\ {\rm J\ mol^{-1}\ K^{-1}}
is the ideal gas constant
(
Korenaga 2006
;
Tiesinga et al. 2021
)
.

Appendix E
Tidal Heating in the Solid Viscoelastic mantle

We consider the solid part of the mantle to be incompressible, with viscosity
\eta_{\rm s}
and shear modulus
\mu_{\rm s}
, and we adopt a Maxwell rheology to describe its strain under tidal stresses
(
Efroimsky 2012
;
Bagheri et al. 2019
, e.g.)
. We set
\mu_{\rm s}=75~{\rm GPa}

(
Tobie et al. 2005
, e.g.)
. The Maxwell time and dimensionless frequency are
\tau_{\rm M}=\eta_{\rm s}/\mu_{\rm s}
and
x=|\sigma|\tau_{\rm M}.
The complex shear modulus is

\hat{\mu}_{\rm s}(\sigma)=\mu_{\rm s}\frac{i\,x}{1+i\,x}.

(E1)

The degree-n tidal Love numbers associated with the solid interior is given by
(
Munk & MacDonald 1960
, e.g.)

k_{n}(\sigma)=\frac{3}{2(n-1)}\frac{1}{1+\tilde{\mu}_{n}(\sigma)},

(E2)

where we have introduced the dimensionless effective shear modulus
\tilde{\mu}_{n}(\sigma)=A_{n}\hat{\mu}_{\rm s}(\sigma)/{\mu}_{\rm s}
, with the gravitational coupling
A_{n}
captured by

A_{n}=\frac{4(2n^{2}+4n+3)\pi R_{\rm s}^{4}\mu_{\rm s}}{3nGM_{\rm s}},

(E3)

with
R_{\rm s}
and
M_{\rm s}
denoting the radius and mass of the solid part of the mantle, respectively. The imaginary part of the Love number, governing dissipation, is then

\imaginary\left\{{k}_{n}(\sigma)\right\}=-\,\frac{3}{2(n-1)}\,\frac{A_{n}\,x}{1+\bigl(1+A_{n}\bigr)^{2}x^{2}}.

(E4)

Tidal heating in the solid then follows

\mathcal{P}_{\rm T;s}=\frac{R_{p}}{8\pi G}\sum_{s=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{n}(2n+1)\sigma_{m}^{s}\bigl|U^{T;s}_{n,m}\bigr|^{2}\imaginary\left\{{k}_{n}\bigl(\sigma)\right\},

(E5)

with
U^{T;s}_{n,m}
given by Eq. (
3
).
