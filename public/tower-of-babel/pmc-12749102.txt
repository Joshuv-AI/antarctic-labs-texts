Endoscopic nasopharyngectomy for the management of refractory CSF rhinorrhea following cerebellopontine angle tumor surgery: illustrative case

Postoperative CSF leaks, manifesting as wound dehiscence or rhinorrhea, are the second most frequent complication following vestibular schwannoma (VS) resection, with an incidence of 5%–15% in open surgical procedures.1,2 Each of the three main surgical approaches for VS—translabyrinthine, middle fossa/subtemporal, and retrosigmoid/suboccipital craniotomy—can create a communication between the fluid-filled intracranial compartment and the pneumatized air cell system of the temporal bone.3 Among these, the eustachian tube (ET) is a critical pathway, frequently implicated in CSF rhinorrhea.4,5

While conservative management is effective for traumatic CSF leaks, postoperative CSF leaks refractory to initial measures often require surgical intervention.6 Traditional open exploration aimed to directly repair identifiable CSF leak sites. However, for persistent CSF rhinorrhea unresponsive to standard interventions, transnasal endoscopic approaches have emerged as less invasive alternatives, reducing morbidity associated with reoperation.

Endoscopic endonasal ET obliteration (EEETO) has been described extensively, focusing on occluding the ET or nasopharyngeal orifice via packing techniques.6,7 Despite its success rates, failure occurs in up to 20% of cases. Endoscopic nasopharyngectomy, initially described in oncological contexts, has been predominantly used for recurrent nasopharyngeal carcinoma.8 Here, we report the first known case of transnasal endoscopic nasopharyngectomy with pedicled nasoseptal flap (NSF) closure for a lateral skull base CSF leak in a patient refractory to multiple prior interventions.

Illustrative Case

Clinical History

A 31-year-old male underwent retrosigmoid resection of a 27-mm left cerebellopontine angle acoustic neuroma. During the initial surgery, the porus was closed with fat grafting and fibrin sealant. Postoperatively, the patient developed left-sided CSF rhinorrhea. Initial management included supine positioning with head elevation at 45° and placement of a lumbar drain. Because of the persistent leak, he was referred to our otorhinolaryngology clinic.

A cul-de-sac technique, involving closure of the external auditory canal and packing of the ET via a middle fossa approach, was initially performed, as residual hearing was absent in the affected ear (Fig. 1). Reexploration of the prior craniotomy was deemed unfavorable due to scarring and anatomical complexity.

Postoperative axial high-resolution CT image of the left temporal bone. Following cul-de-sac closure, the left mastoid air cells are completely opacified and sclerotic, indicating prior surgical obliteration. The left ET and adjacent peritubal air cells are visible as potential pathways for persistent CSF leakage, despite the obliteration of the middle ear and canal closure.

Subsequent Interventions

Two months later, the patient presented with recurrent, intermittent CSF rhinorrhea and fluctuating scalp swelling at the previous middle fossa craniectomy site. Transnasal endoscopic ET obliteration was performed: the nasopharyngeal orifice was accessed unilaterally via the left nostril, the ET mucosa was de-epithelialized using a microdebrider, and the lumen was occluded with Histoacryl glue and packed with Surgicel (Fig. 2). A 5-Fr vertebral angiography catheter was inserted through the endoscope from the tubal ostium into the ET. The middle ear cavity and ET were filled with 10 ml of contrast medium through the catheter (Fig. 2 left). The N-butyl-2 cyanoacrylate (NBCA)–Lipiodol embolizing mixture was administered with 2-ml syringes. The middle ear cavity was filled with the liquid embolizing agent (Fig. 2 right).

EEETO. Right: Contrast filling of the middle ear and ET. Left: NBCA-Lipiodol embolization of the middle ear cavity.

Despite this, recurrent CSF rhinorrhea developed 6 weeks postoperatively, leading to placement of a lumboperitoneal shunt. While initially effective, symptoms recurred, and imaging revealed pneumocephalus and retrocochlear communication between air cells and the ET lumen, along with bony dehiscence along the ET.

Surgical Technique

A type 3A nasopharyngeal endoscopic resection was performed according to the modified Castelnuovo classification.9 Following induction of hypotensive general anesthesia, a contralateral Hadad flap was harvested and temporarily positioned in the nasopharynx.10 An ipsilateral endoscopic medial maxillectomy was performed, including removal of the inferior turbinate, medial maxillary wall, and nasolacrimal duct. The sphenopalatine, greater palatine, and palatovaginal arteries, along with the vidian bundle, were ligated, and the medial pterygoid plate was drilled. The cartilaginous ET and peritubal muscles were excised. The previously harvested NSF was rotated and secured to ensure complete coverage of the ET defect (Fig. 3).

Intraoperative image showing the placement of the pedicled NSF. The NSF, harvested and rotated into position, is secured to ensure complete contact with the remnant ET surface to achieve complete obliteration The asterisk indicates the Hadad flap.

Postoperative Course

The patient experienced an uncomplicated recovery and was discharged within a few days. One-month postoperative nasal endoscopy showed satisfactory healing and mucosalization (Fig. 4). Serial follow-up, including imaging, demonstrated no pneumocephalus or disease recurrence. Temporary ipsilateral cheek numbness resolved spontaneously within 4 months. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no CSF rhinorrhea or pneumocephalus recurrence.

Follow-up CT image obtained 1 year postoperatively, demonstrating no signs of pneumocephalus or disease recurrence, confirming complete resolution of symptoms and a successful long-term outcome.

Discussion

CSF leaks are well-documented complications following lateral skull base surgeries, often arising from persistent air cell tracts or inadequately sealed surgical defects. These leaks present a significant technical challenge, as achieving a watertight and durable closure is essential. In the present case, despite initial prophylactic measures, including the obliteration of the middle ear orifice of the ET, persistent air cell tracts extending into the mastoid or internal auditory canal (IAC) likely facilitated the continued passage of CSF into the middle ear, leading to recurrent CSF rhinorrhea.

Initial management of CSF leaks typically involves conservative approaches, such as bed rest and lumbar drainage. Lumbar drainage has been shown to be effective in 61%–71% of cases and remains a widely accepted first-line treatment. However, the optimal duration of lumbar drainage is still undefined, with most clinicians restricting its use to 2–5 days to minimize the risk of complications, particularly infections associated with prolonged catheterization.1,11,12

The limited success of conservative therapy has been highlighted in several studies. Mangus et al. reported a 59% success rate for nonsurgical management of CSF rhinorrhea, underscoring the need for early surgical intervention in cases of persistent or high-output leaks.2 When surgical intervention is indicated, the approach often involves revisiting the original surgical site, unroofing the IAC, and obliterating adjacent mastoid air cells with bone wax, fat, or muscle grafts. Despite these conventional techniques, failure rates as high as 38% have been reported in some series.3

In the present case, the patient continued to experience persistent CSF rhinorrhea following VS resection, despite multiple interventions including CSF diversion (via lumbar drainage and placement of a programmable lumboperitoneal shunt), abdominal fat grafting, and occlusion of the ET orifice. During subsequent procedures, the ET lumen was catheterized transnasally and completely obliterated with Histoacryl glue, yet the leak recurred. This recurrence was likely due to a patulous air cell tract at the petrous apex, which allowed CSF to bypass the surgical seal and enter the osseous ET or medial segment of the ET through peritubal air cells, thus undermining previous repair efforts and predisposing the patient to meningitis.

Endoscopic transnasal obliteration of the ET has been increasingly reported as a viable option for managing refractory CSF leaks (Table 1).6,7,12–17 First introduced by Kwartler et al., this technique involves mucosal de-epithelialization, cauterization, and packing of the ET orifice.12 Taghi et al. reported successful resolution of a persistent CSF rhinorrhea following retrosigmoid craniotomy by initially managing the leak with middle ear obliteration, followed by endoscopic ET cauterization, cartilage plugging, and mucosal flap coverage.7 Similarly, Orlandi and Shelton treated 3 patients with persistent leaks by cauterizing the ET orifice and performing mucosal cerclage of the torus tubarius, all without recurrence.13 Lucke-Wold et al. described a complex case managed with mastoidectomy, fat grafting, and fascia flap placement; persistent leakage was ultimately controlled with endoscopic cauterization, acellular matrix grafting, and coverage with an NSF.14

Lemonnier et al. used acellular dermal grafts for endoscopic ET closure but reported a 33.3% failure rate, with 1 patient developing meningitis, necessitating revision surgery.6 Mehta et al. also reported successful closure of CSF leaks using autologous fat packing of the ET following middle fossa meningioma resection.17 Despite these successes, the overall primary success rate for ET obliteration procedures remains around 65.2%, with outcomes varying based on technique and patient-specific anatomical factors.16

Patel et al. proposed a multimodal technique combining ET de-epithelialization, fat plugging, and coverage with a pedicled NSF, which yielded favorable outcomes, although 1 patient required revision within 4 weeks.16 In the current case, the recurrence of the leak following endoscopic ET occlusion may have been influenced by dynamic movements of the torus tubarius and soft palate musculature, which can compromise the stability of intranasal reconstructions. Although the earlier use of a Hadad flap might have improved initial outcomes, the patient expressed a preference to avoid multiple surgeries. Consequently, a definitive solution was pursued via endoscopic nasopharyngectomy with pedicled Hadad flap reconstruction. This approach facilitated complete ET obliteration without reentering the primary surgical field and proved to be an effective method for resolving the persistent CSF leak.

Although NSF usage for CSF leaks via the ET has been reported, its combination with nasopharyngectomy in cases with high-volume leaks is novel. In our case, persistent leakage and patient preference for definitive management led to this combined approach, which to our knowledge has not been previously described.

Observations

A 31-year-old man developed CSF rhinorrhea following retrosigmoid resection of a 27-mm left cerebellopontine angle acoustic neuroma. The porus was initially closed with fat grafting and fibrin sealant; however, postoperative CSF rhinorrhea developed and persisted despite lumbar drainage and conservative measures. A middle fossa approach with cul-de-sac closure of the external auditory canal and ET packing was performed, but the leak recurred 2 months later. EEETO using NBCA-Lipiodol and Surgicel was then attempted, yet recurrent rhinorrhea and pneumocephalus developed 6 weeks postoperatively. Imaging demonstrated persistent communication between air cells and the ET lumen, with bony dehiscence along the tube. The patient subsequently underwent endoscopic nasopharyngectomy with excision of the cartilaginous ET and peritubal muscles, followed by reconstruction using a pedicled NSF. The postoperative course was uneventful, with complete mucosalization at 1 month and no recurrence of CSF rhinorrhea or pneumocephalus at the 1-year follow-up.

Lessons

To the best of our knowledge, the application of NSF following nasopharyngectomy for this specific indication has not been previously described in the literature. As a second-line intervention, endoscopic nasopharyngectomy followed by NSF reconstruction offers a more durable solution for repairing CSF rhinorrhea resulting from lateral skull base defects, particularly after failed initial attempts, and may serve as an effective alternative prior to considering open surgical approaches. This case underscores the paramount importance of a multidisciplinary approach involving both the otorhinolaryngology and neurosurgery teams in VS surgery to minimize complications and optimize patient outcomes.
