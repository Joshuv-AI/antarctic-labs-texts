A multifeature machine learning and resting-state EEG study reveals differences in beta oscillation in late-life depression with or without mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Shihan Tian, Gaohong Lin, Yijie Zeng, Qiang Wang, Yicheng Lin, Kexin Yao, Zihan Wang, Zhidai Xiao, Jiayu Chen, Youxuan Zheng, Haoye Tan, Zhangying Wu, Mingfeng Yang, Danyan Xu, Xuanzi Li, Jingyi Lao, Shuang Liang, Yunheng Chen, Qin Liu, Jiafu Li, Yujing Gan, Mingyong Zeng, Ben Chen, Yuping Ning, Xiaomei Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLate-life depression (LLD) often co-occurs with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and patients with LLD and MCI (LLD-MCI) have an increased risk of progression to Alzheimer’s disease (AD). However, differences in resting-state neural oscillation and cognitive impairment in LLD patients remain unclear. In this cross-sectional study, electroencephalography (EEG) was used to analyse, local rhythm activity and large-scale network communication to differentiate LLD patients with and without MCI.MethodsWe enrolled 113 participants: 74 with LLD (50 with LLD-MCI and 24 with LLD-non-MCI) and 39 healthy older adults (HOAs). All participants underwent comprehensive neuropsychological assessments. Spectral power and source-level functional connectivity (Phase-Locking Value, PLV) were analysed across multiple frequency bands. A machine learning framework using nested stratified cross-validation was implemented to evaluate the potential of EEG features in classifying LLD clinical subtypes.ResultsLLD-MCI patients exhibited a distinct dissociation in the beta band: significantly reduced spectral power in the left frontal cortex contrasted with extensive hyperconnectivity primarily centred on the right lateral orbitofrontal cortex (rLOFC). Complementary analyses also revealed widespread hyperconnectivity in the theta band in the LLD-MCI group. The Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) model achieved superior performance in distinguishing LLD-MCI patients from LLD-non-MCI patients, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.82 and an accuracy of 78.38%. Feature importance analysis revealed rLOFC-mediated beta synchronisation as the most discriminative biomarker.ConclusionOur findings suggest that beta-band oscillatory disruption—characterised by local power deficits and network hyperconnectivity—may represent a potential neurobiological signature of cognitive vulnerability in LLD patients. Whether this hyperconnectivity reflects a compensatory or pathological process remains a hypothesis for further validation. EEG metrics provide significant diagnostic value for the precise clinical subtyping and early identification of cognitive decline in the LLD population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08126-6.

Introduction

Late-life depression (LLD), a neuropsychiatric disorder characterised by persistent depressive symptoms, affects 13.3% of the elderly population worldwide [1]. This disorder not only induces emotional distress but also drives progressive cognitive decline and significant deterioration in quality of life [2, 3]. Notably, compared with younger adults with depression, elderly patients are more susceptible to medical comorbidities and cognitive impairments [4]. Epidemiological studies indicate that a substantial proportion of LLD patients present with comorbid mild cognitive impairment (MCI), which is a transitional state characterised by measurable cognitive deficits below dementia thresholds [5]. This comorbidity is clinically significant, as longitudinal studies have shown that compared with LLD patients without MCI, LLD patients with MCI are more likely to progress to Alzheimer’s disease (AD), with faster cognitive decline and poorer treatment response [6–10]. However, the diagnosis of MCI can vary because of differences in neuropsychological test batteries and cut-off values. Moreover, compared with HOAs, patients with LLD often have poorer compliance during cognitive assessments. Therefore, there is a pressing need for a noninvasive, rapid, and relatively objective diagnostic approach.

EEG offers a unique window into the neurodynamics of cognition–emotion interactions with millisecond resolution [11, 12]. We focus on the beta (13–30 Hz) and gamma (30–70 Hz) bands, which are vital for network stability and information integration [13]. While reduced high-frequency activity is often linked to synaptic loss in patients with dementia [14, 15], our previous work revealed elevated power in patients with LLD, suggesting the presence of neural hyperexcitability [16, 17]. The trajectory of LLD-MCI remains debated: cognitive impairment might dampen this activity or, conversely, exacerbate it. Specifically, excessive beta synchronisation may emerge as a pathological hallmark, reflecting failed inhibitory control or compensatory recruitment during early neurodegeneration [18]. Therefore, we propose that specific beta and gamma patterns characterise the distinct cognitive subtypes of LLD.

Unlike power spectral analysis, source-level EEG functional connectivity assesses phase synchronisation between brain regions across multiple frequency bands. In LLD and MCI patients, abnormal connectivity between the frontal and limbic systems has been documented, particularly involving the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), a hub that integrates emotional regulation and cognitive networks, which demonstrates state-specific connectivity alterations [19–21], and convergent evidence links frontal-temporal alterations to cognitive deficits in LLD patients [22]. Moreover, alterations in beta-band functional connectivity are strongly correlated with memory function and neural activity in older adults [23–25]. However, source-level connectivity characteristics in individuals with comorbid LLD and MCI remain poorly characterised.

To address this research gap, this study integrates power spectral analysis with source-level functional connectivity. This approach quantifies beta- and gamma-band oscillatory strength, revealing intrinsic neural signatures underlying depressive symptom–cognitive function interactions while overcoming the spatial resolution limitations of EEG through high-precision delineation of interregional temporal synchrony [26–30]. In this study, the impact of cognitive impairment on brain network organisation in LLD patients was investigated and a multifeature machine learning model was developed to identify cognitive subtypes, providing neurobiological evidence for precise clinical subtyping and neuromodulation targets.

On the basis of aforementioned background, we propose three specific hypotheses: (1) compared with healthy older adults, LLD patients generally exhibit elevated beta power; (2) individuals with comorbid LLD and MCI display functional connectivity patterns distinct from those of patients with LLD alone; and (3) these beta-band spectral and connectivity features serve as robust biomarkers for effectively distinguishing LLD cognitive subtypes via machine learning.

Methods

Participants

A total of 113 participants were recruited between January 2021 and December 2023 from both outpatient and inpatient units of the Geriatric Neuroscience Center at the Affiliated Brain Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University, including 74 patients with LLD and 39 HOAs.

The diagnosis of LLD was established on the basis of the DSM-IV criteria using the Structured Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals aged between 60 and 85 years; (2) those meeting the DSM-IV Axis I diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder (single or recurrent episode); and (3) those exhibiting right-handedness with normal or corrected-to-normal visual and auditory acuity.

The LLD group was stratified into two subgroups: 50 LLD-MCI patients and 24 LLD-non-MCI patients. The diagnosis of MCI comorbidity was determined according to Petersen’s criteria, which include subjective cognitive complaints, objective impairment in at least one cognitive domain (> 1.5 SD below age- and education-adjusted norms), preserved daily functioning, and nondementia status (Clinical Dementia Rating [CDR] = 0.5). Two certified psychiatrists reached a diagnostic consensus.

A control group of 39 HOAs, matched to the LLD group in terms of age and sex and free from emotional or cognitive disorders, was concurrently recruited. The exclusion criteria for all participants included (1) any other DSM-IV Axis I disorder; (2) severe or unstable medical conditions (e.g., stroke, delirium, Parkinson’s disease, or brain tumours); (3) secondary causes of depression (e.g., hypothyroidism or substance use); (4) current alcohol use meeting the dependence criteria; or (5) a history of head injury involving loss of consciousness for > 30 min. All enrolled participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Institutional Review Board protocols.

Clinical assessments

Standardized neuropsychiatric assessments were conducted prior to EEG recording. All participants underwent a comprehensive evaluation by board-certified psychiatrists, which integrated clinical interviews with neuropsychological profiling. Demographic data (sex, age and years of education) and clinical characteristics, including pharmacotherapy regimens, were documented. Antidepressants were classified into selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and noradrenergic/specific serotonergic antidepressants (NaSSAs). Anxiolytics were categorised as benzodiazepines (BZDs) or nonbenzodiazepine agents (e.g., buspirone/tandospirone). Depression severity was quantified using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17).

A multidimensional cognitive battery, administered by trained neuropsychologists, was used to assess the following domains: (1) global cognition: Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE); (2) memory: Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT-N5) and Working Memory Test (WMT); (3) information processing speed: Trail Making Test-A (TMT-A) and Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT); (4) language: Verbal Fluency Test (VFT) and Boston Naming Test (BNT); (5) visuospatial skills: Rey‒Osterrieth Complex Figure Test (Rey-O) and Clock Drawing Test (CDT-4); and (6) executive functioning: Trail Making Test-B (TMT-B) and Stroop Color-Word Test (STROOP-C).

EEG data recording and preprocessing

All participants were instructed to abstain from caffeine-containing beverages, alcohol, and nicotine and to discontinue any sedative medications for at least 24 h prior to the commencement of the experimental procedures. All EEG sessions were conducted in the morning to minimise circadian effects. Data were recorded using a 64-channel HydroCel Geodesic Sensor Net (Electrical Geodesics Inc.) with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. The recordings were referenced to the vertex (Cz), using the common (COM) sensor as ground. Electrode impedances were maintained below 50 kΩ. This threshold aligns with the manufacturer’s guidelines for high-input impedance amplifiers (> 200 MΩ), ensuring high signal fidelity without skin abrasion [31].

Preprocessing was performed using EEGLAB v14.1.1 in MATLAB R2022b. A zero-phase Hamming windowed sinc FIR filter was applied (bandpass: 1–70 Hz; notch: 48–52 Hz), followed by segmentation into 2-second epochs. Defective channels were reconstructed via spherical spline interpolation, and ocular/cardiac artefacts were removed using independent component analysis (ICA). Finally, epochs with voltage deviations exceeding ± 100 µV were excluded before rereferencing to the global average. Detailed statistics regarding data quality, including the average number of interpolated channels, removed ICA components, and retained epochs per subject, are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S4.

Power spectrum analysis

The scalp was partitioned into eight regions of interest (ROIs) covering the left and right hemispheres across the frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital lobes [32]. The specific electrode configurations for each ROI are detailed in Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Fig. S1. Clean epochs were decomposed using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm with a Hanning window at 0.5 Hz resolution. The primary frequency bands of interest were defined as beta (13–30 Hz) and gamma (30–70 Hz) bands and were subdivided into low gamma (30–48 Hz) and high gamma (52–70 Hz) bands [33–38]. Additionally, to provide a comprehensive spectral profile, analyses were extended to include the theta (4–8 Hz) and alpha (8–13 Hz) bands. With respect to power quantification, absolute power (µV2) was first computed for each single epoch. These power spectra were then averaged across all the valid epochs per participant to ensure signal stability. Power values were subsequently averaged across frequency bins within each band and across electrodes within each ROI. Finally, a natural logarithm transformation (ln µV2) was applied to the aggregated power values to normalize the data distribution for statistical analysis.

Functional connectivity analysis

To investigate the underlying neural networks, source localisation was first conducted using the Brainstorm toolbox within the MATLAB environment [39]. The procedure involved coregistration of individualised head models and electrode coordinates to the standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI152) template. The forward model was computed using the Boundary Element Method (BEM), and the inverse problem was resolved through Standardized Low-Resolution Brain Electromagnetic Tomography (sLORETA). Cortical parcellation was performed by averaging source activities across 68 anatomically defined regions according to the Desikan–Killiany atlas, thereby generating source-level time series for subsequent connectivity analysis.

Following source reconstruction, functional connectivity was evaluated using the Phase-Locking Value (PLV) [40]. The PLV is a phase-based metric ranging from (0, 1) that quantifies the absolute phase synchronisation between neural signals, where 0 indicates no synchronisation and 1 reflects perfect phase locking. We selected the PLV as the primary metric because it captures the full extent of phase synchronisation, including both lagged and zero-lag interactions. While we acknowledge that the PLV is sensitive to volume conduction—which typically manifests as zero-lag correlations—it is well documented that genuine zero-lag synchronisation mediates critical neural integration mechanisms (e.g., feature binding), which might be overlooked by conservative, lag-based measures such as wPLI or imaginary coherence [41]. Furthermore, performing the analysis in the source space partially mitigates the spatial smearing effects inherent to sensor-level data.

Nevertheless, to validate the robustness of our results against volume conduction artefacts, we conducted a supplementary analysis using the Weighted Phase Lag Index (wPLI), a metric insensitive to zero-lag interactions. The details and results of this validation analysis are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Machine learning algorithm

To evaluate the diagnostic potential of EEG, we constructed a focused feature space of 40 candidate variables, comprising 32 ROI-based beta/gamma power features and 8 functional connectivity links identified via NBS. We implemented a rigorous nested stratified cross-validation (CV) framework to prevent data leakage and ensure unbiased evaluation. The outer loop employed 5-fold stratification, strictly preserving the original clinical ratio (approximately a 2:1 ratio of LLD-MCI to LLD-non-MCI). Crucially, all preprocessing steps—including Z-score standardisation and univariate feature selection (ANOVA F-value) to identify the top 10 most discriminative features—were performed exclusively within the training folds of each iteration, ensuring that the test data remained completely unseen during the feature selection and model construction phases.

We compared four classifiers: Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), and K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN). To address class imbalance, we utilised stratified sampling and applied balanced class weighting (specifically for RF and SVM) to adjust misclassification penalties. The hyperparameters were optimized via an inner 3-fold grid search: RF (n_estimators: 50–200; max_depth: 5–15), SVM (C: 0.1–10; kernel: RBF), KNN (k: 3–9; metric: Manhattan/Euclidean), and LDA (shrinkage: 0.1–0.9; solver: LSQR). The detailed hyperparameter tuning configurations and optimal parameters are listed in the Supplementary Materials.

Model generalisation was evaluated on independent test sets using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), and the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUC-PR) to address the potential bias from class imbalance. Furthermore, to assess the clinical utility of the models, we performed Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) to quantify the net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. To further validate stability given the sample size, we employed a stratified bootstrap aggregating strategy with 1,000 resampling iterations. Statistical significance was assessed via a permutation test (1,000 shuffles), with the significance level set at p < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 27 and MATLAB. Group differences in demographic, clinical, and neuropsychological variables were assessed with chi-square tests (categorical variables) and one-way ANOVA (continuous variables). To compare the resting-state spectral power among the LLD-MCI, LLD-non-MCI, and HOA groups, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed, adjusting for covariates (sex, age, education years, and medication status). The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Differences in functional connectivity were evaluated using Network-Based Statistic (NBS), a cluster-based permutation method implemented in MATLAB NBS Toolbox 1.2 [42, 43]. Covariates were regressed out, and significance was determined through 5,000 permutations. Given the sample size (n = 113) and group comparisons (k = 3), the threshold was set to F = 7.32, identifying a significant network cluster (p < 0.001).

Significant main effects were found across all clinical and cognitive measures (all p < 0.001). With respect to depression severity (HAMD-17; F = 45.022; η² = 0.323), both LLD subgroups scored significantly higher than the HOA group did, but importantly, there was no significant difference between LLD-MCI and LLD-non-MCI patients. Post hoc analyses revealed two distinct patterns of cognitive function. A hierarchical impairment pattern (LLD-MCI < LLD-non-MCI < HOA) was observed in the working memory (WMT) and language domains (VFT and BNT), indicating that LLD-non-MCI patients performed worse than HOA controls did despite not meeting the MCI criteria. In contrast, for global cognition (MMSE) and other domains, including memory (AVLT-N5), processing speed (SDMT and TMT A), visuospatial skills (Rey-O and CDT-4), and executive functions (Stroop C and TMT B), the LLD-MCI group performed significantly worse than the other groups did, while the LLD-non-MCI group showed performance comparable to that of the HOA controls.

In the theta and alpha bands, although LLD patients generally exhibited altered power compared with HOA controls, no statistically significant differences were observed between the LLD-MCI and LLD-non-MCI subgroups (all p > 0.05; Supplementary Tables S8, S9), suggesting that low-frequency oscillations may not robustly differentiate cognitive status within LLD patients in this cohort.

In contrast, high-frequency oscillations displayed distinct group differences. Within the beta band, ANCOVA revealed significant group effects in the left frontal (p = 0.026, ηp² = 0.069), left temporal (p = 0.039, ηp² = 0.061), and right temporal (p = 0.041, ηp² = 0.061) regions (Supplementary Table S6). Post hoc tests (Supplementary Table S7) revealed that compared with LLD-non-MCI patients, LLD-MCI patients showed significantly reduced beta power specifically in the left frontal region (p = 0.038; Cohen’s d = 0.600).

Furthermore, the gamma band exhibited more widespread significance. Compared with LLD-non-MCI patients, LLD-MCI patients had reduced gamma power in the left frontal region (p = 0.037; Cohen’s d = 0.603). Notably, subdivision of the gamma band revealed that this reduction was driven primarily by high gamma activity in the left frontal region (p = 0.039; Cohen’s d = 0.597), whereas low gamma activity did not significantly differ between the LLD subgroups (p = 0.057; Cohen’s d = 0.548). These findings confirm that high-frequency power deficits, particularly in the left frontal cortex, persist as a key feature of LLD-MCI even after accounting for medication effects.

Post hoc comparisons revealed that, compared with the LLD-non-MCI group, the LLD-MCI group exhibited a significantly increased subnetwork (size: 8 edges) in the beta band, primarily centred on the rLOFC (Figs. 3 and 4).

Complementary analyses in the theta band revealed a similar landscape of extensive hyperconnectivity in LLD-MCI patients. Owing to the high density of significant edges, these connections were grouped into anatomical ROIs for visualisation, highlighting robust frontotemporal coupling as a key feature (Fig. 5). In contrast, no significant connectivity alterations were found in the alpha band. Furthermore, to assess the potential influence of volume conduction, a wPLI analysis was performed, which yielded no statistically significant group differences (all p > 0.05). This suggests that the observed alterations in this cohort are characterised primarily by phase synchronisation, including zero-lag interactions, which are captured by the PLV but minimised by the wPLI.

Machine learning classification

To classify LLD patients with and without MCI, we developed four machine learning models using a nested cross-validation framework. Among the classifiers, the LDA model demonstrated superior performance, with an AUC of 0.82 and an accuracy of 78.38% (sensitivity = 84.00%, specificity = 66.67%; Fig. 6). A detailed comparison of the performance metrics for all the models is provided in Table 2. Feature importance analysis revealed that the top 10 discriminative features were predominantly located in the beta band (Fig. 7). Notably, the top-ranked feature corresponded to functional connectivity between the rLOFC and the rITG, followed by the connection between the rLOFC and the lPOR. These findings align with our functional connectivity results, highlighting the critical role of rLOFC-mediated beta synchronisation in discriminating MCI status. Furthermore, DCA and PR curves are provided in Supplementary Fig. S2 and S3, respectively, to demonstrate the clinical net benefit and performance robustness.

Discussion

In this study, we identified beta-band oscillations as the primary electrophysiological signature distinguishing LLD patients with MCI from those without MCI. Notably, while gamma activity effectively distinguished LLD patients from healthy ageing patients, it lacked specificity for cognitive subtypes; thus, our findings highlight beta oscillations as a promising candidate for detecting cognitive vulnerability. Our analysis revealed a distinct dissociation between local neuronal activity and large-scale network synchronisation within this frequency band. Specifically, compared with patients in the LLD-non-MCI group, patients in the LLD-MCI group exhibited significantly reduced beta power localised to the left frontal cortex. In stark contrast to this local deficit, functional connectivity analysis demonstrated a pattern of hyper-synchronisation in the beta band, primarily centred on the right lateral orbitofrontal cortex (rLOFC). This strengthened connectivity formed a widespread network involving temporal, parietal, and occipital regions. Importantly, our machine learning analysis identified these specific rLOFC-mediated beta connections as the most discriminative features, confirming their robustness in characterising the MCI status within the LLD cohort.

This observed dissociation between local power reduction and contralateral hyperconnectivity suggests a specific network reorganisation mechanism underlying cognitive vulnerability in LLD patients. The decrease in the left frontal beta aligns with impaired top-down executive control, constituting the electrophysiological basis of local dysfunction. In contrast, the enhanced rLOFC connectivity with distributed posterior regions may represent a hypothetical “scaffolding” mechanism—an adaptive interhemispheric recruitment to compensate for left-sided deficits [44]. However, this hyperconnectivity could alternatively indicate pathological hypersynchrony arising from compromised inhibitory control [45]. This complexity aligns with recent insights viewing early neurodegeneration as a phase of specific topological alterations rather than simple functional decline [46]. Just as directed connectivity shifts characterise the initial network dysfunction in the Alzheimer’s continuum [47], our findings suggest that right-lateralised beta hyperconnectivity may represent a parallel, prodromal network signature.

Complementing the group-level statistics, our machine learning analysis further validated the robustness of these electrophysiological signatures for individual classification. The Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) classifier achieved the highest performance (AUC = 0.82), outperforming nonlinear models and suggesting that the LLD-MCI features are distinct and linearly separable [48]. Crucially, the hierarchy of feature weights indicates that functional connectivity has greater discriminatory power than local spectral activity [49]. The model relied primarily on beta-band connections anchored in the rLOFC—linking to temporal and parietal nodes—which consistently ranked higher than isolated beta or gamma power. This dominance of network metrics over local activity confirms that the specific connectivity pattern is the most reliable predictor [50], providing a transparent and interpretable model structure rather than an opaque “black box” prediction.

The findings presented here have significant translational implications. Given its accessibility and sensitivity to early functional changes, resting-state EEG is well positioned as a scalable triage tool, potentially for detecting alterations prior to MRI-visible atrophy [51]. We envision a tiered workflow in which the identified beta-band alterations and rLOFC-hub hyperconnectivity serve as objective “red flags” or decision points, warranting referral for comprehensive neuropsychological or neuroimaging confirmation. Therapeutically, identifying the rLOFC as a central hub offers a specific target for precision neuromodulation (e.g., rTMS) to reinforce compensatory mechanisms [23, 52]. While this study establishes potential targets, future large-scale cohorts are necessary to define standardised diagnostic thresholds for routine practice.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the relatively small sample size relative to the complexity of the high-dimensional features raises concerns regarding statistical power and reproducibility, while single-centre recruitment limits generalisability. Second, the reliance on a standard template (MNI152) rather than individual MRIs for source localisation restricts anatomical accuracy, implying that the precise spatial boundaries of the rLOFC hubs should be interpreted with caution. Third, the primary connectivity metric (PLV) is sensitive to volume conduction; notably, supplementary analyses using the leakage-robust wPLI did not yield significant differences, suggesting that the observed networks may either involve zero-lag synchronisation (which wPLI discards) or partially reflect field spread effects. Fourth, the absence of AD-specific biomarkers, such as amyloid-β or tau protein levels, restricts our ability to directly link these electrophysiological findings to underlying AD pathology. Therefore, the observed beta-band alterations should be interpreted as markers of general cognitive vulnerability within the LLD population rather than definitive signatures of the AD continuum. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, highlighting the need for longitudinal cohorts to validate the predictive value of these electrophysiological signatures.

Conclusion

This study suggests that resting-state EEG captures neurophysiological distinctions between the cognitive subtypes of LLD. Specifically, LLD-MCI patients exhibited characteristic beta-band power alterations and hyperconnectivity, with the rLOFC serving as a central hub. These findings underscore the potential utility of beta-band EEG features in distinguishing LLD patients with and without MCI, offering preliminary evidence to support the early identification of cognitive decline in this population.
