Large language models surpass domain-specific architectures for antepartum electronic fetal monitoring analysis
Sheng Wong, Ravi Shankar, Beth Albert, Gabriel Davis Jones

Abstract
Foundation models (FMs) and large language models (LLMs) demonstrate remarkable capabilities across diverse domains through training on massive datasets. These models have demonstrated exceptional performance in healthcare applications, yet their potential for electronic fetal monitoring (EFM)/cardiotocography (CTG) analysis, a critical technology for evaluating fetal well-being, remains largely underexplored. Antepartum CTG interpretation presents unique challenges due to the complex nature of fetal heart rate (FHR) patterns and uterine activity, requiring sophisticated analysis of long time-series data. The assessment of CTG is heavily based on subjective clinical interpretation, often leading to variability in diagnostic accuracy and deviation from timely pregnancy care. This study presents the first comprehensive comparison of state-of-the-art AI approaches for automated antepartum CTG analysis. We systematically compare time-series FMs and LLMs against established CTG-specific architectures. Our evaluation encompasses over 500 CTG recordings of varying durations reflecting real-world clinical recordings, providing robust performance benchmarks across different modelling paradigms. Our results demonstrate that fine-tuned LLMs achieve superior performance compared to both foundation models and domain-specific approaches, offering a promising alternative pathway for clinical CTG interpretation. These findings provide critical insights into the relative strengths of different AI methodologies for fetal monitoring applications and establish a foundation for future clinical AI development in prenatal care.

3
Methodology

3.1
Data Preparation

CTG recordings were obtained from the Oxford Maternity (OxMat) database, a longitudinal repository of antepartum CTGs collected at the John Radcliffe Hospital, Oxford, UK, between 1991 and 2024
khan2024oxmat
. The CTG recordings contain FHR and toco signals of varying lengths. The dataset includes over 250 clinical variables encompassing maternal and neonatal outcomes such as Apgar scores, cord blood gas values, birthweights, and delivery types. The database has been extensively used in various CTG research tasks.

Based on these clinical variables, CTG signals were categorised into two cohorts: Adverse Pregnancy Outcome (APO) and Normal Pregnancy Outcome (NPO). The adverse pregnancy outcome required recordings from fetuses with one of these outcomes: antepartum or intrapartum stillbirth, neonatal death within 24 hours of birth, fetal asphyxia, among other criteria. Selection criteria required recordings to have less than 50% missing data and a minimum duration of 10 minutes. To reflect real-world clinical settings, recordings were preserved in their original lengths, up to one hour, and no additional cleaning was done. All CTG signals were downsampled from 4 Hz to 1 Hz to standardise processing requirements. The final dataset comprised 8,348 NPO and 2,015 APO recordings, which were split into training and test sets, with the test set containing 250 NPO and 250 APO recordings for balanced evaluation. The complete cohort development has been previously described
davis2025performance
.

3.2
Model Setup

We evaluated ten models across three distinct categories: traditional DL architectures, time-series foundation models, and LLMs. This comprehensive selection enables comparison across different modeling paradigms and computational scales.

Two time-series foundation models,

and
Moment

For LLMs, we evaluated three architectures:
Llama 3.2
(4B parameters)

,

MediPhi

These models were adapted for CTG classification by replacing decoder layers with binary classification heads and fine-tuning using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). To reduce computational overhead and mitigate potential issues with long context windows, we applied a striding technique with a window of 60 seconds and a stride of 60 seconds, effectively sampling alternating minute-long segments from the CTG recordings. This approach enabled model training on a single 32GB RTX 5090 GPU. The processed signals were converted to text format and combined with task-specific prompts.

3.3
Training & Evaluation

Traditional DL models were trained from scratch on our training set, with 5% reserved for validation. Training continued for up to 100 epochs with early stopping triggered when validation AU-ROC failed to improve for five consecutive epochs. Time-series foundation models followed a similar fine-tuning protocol with the same validation split and stopping criteria. For LLMs, we employed LoRA-based fine-tuning with a rank of 8 and scaling factor of 16, specifically adapting the query, key, value, and output projection layers. To reduce computation overhead, these models were fine-tuned for 3 epochs using the same 5% validation split for monitoring performance. All model families were trained and evaluated on the same datasets to ensure comparability of results.

All models were trained to predict binary classification of CTG recordings as either APO or NPO. Model performance was primarily evaluated using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AU-ROC) as the primary metric, chosen for its ability to assess discriminative capability across different classification thresholds. Secondary metrics included sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy to provide a comprehensive performance overview.

3.4
Ablation Study

To comprehensively evaluate the robustness and generalisability of our models, we conducted three ablation studies examining different aspects of model performance under constrained conditions.

1.

Limited Data Scenario:
We evaluated model performance on a reduced dataset of 1,500 CTG recordings to simulate clinical scenarios where data availability may be limited. This is particularly relevant for smaller clinical facilities or resource-constrained settings where accumulating large datasets for model training may be impossible. By examining how different model architectures perform with reduced training size, we are able to assess which approaches maintain acceptable performance when data scarcity is a constraint.

2.

Uterine Activity Signal Ablation:
In clinical practice, toco signals frequently suffer from quality issues, including incomplete recordings or complete absence. To evaluate model robustness to missing toco information, we conducted an ablation test using only FHR signals for classification. This ablation study involved zeroing out all toco signals in the test data while using the originally trained models, thereby simulating scenarios where toco data becomes unavailable during inference. This approach allows us to assess whether models trained on complete CTG data can maintain diagnostic accuracy when UA signals are absent, reflecting real-world clinical situations where UA may fail or produce unreliable measurements after model deployment.

3.

Temporal Dependency Analysis:
To investigate the importance of temporal ordering in CTG interpretation, we systematically disrupted the sequential structure of the recordings while preserving local patterns. Specifically, we segmented each CTG recording into one-minute chunks and randomly shuffled their order before model inference. We also applied random masking to segments of the CTGs to further test the models’ dependence on continuous temporal information. This dual perturbation approach preserves short-term temporal features within each minute while eliminating long-range temporal dependencies across the recording. By comparing model performance on temporally shuffled and masked, versus intact sequences, we can quantify whether the architecture relies on long-range temporal patterns versus local feature detection.

Limited Data Scenario

When evaluating model performance under data-constrained conditions, as detailed in Table

The reduction in training data from the complete dataset to 1,500 samples resulted in AU-ROC decreases ranging from 2% to 5% across all models, reflecting the expected impact of reduced training data on model generalization.

Similarly, this pattern suggests that while LLMs retain strong discriminative capabilities from FHR signals alone, the absence of UA information disrupts their learned decision boundaries, necessitating threshold adjustment for practical deployment.

4.4
Discussion

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of state-of-the-art AI approaches for automated antepartum CTG analysis, comparing traditional DL models, time-series FMs, and LLMs across a dataset of 500 CTG recordings. Our findings demonstrate that fine-tuned LLMs consistently outperform both domain-specific architectures and FMs, achieving an AU-ROC of 0.853 with Llama 3.2, representing a significant advancement over previously reported performance metrics in CTG classification.

The superior performance of fine-tuned LLMs persisted across all ablation studies, including scenarios with limited training data and missing uterine activity signals. This robustness suggests that LLMs successfully learn complex temporal dependencies inherent in CTG recordings, effectively capturing both short-term patterns and long-range temporal relationships crucial for accurate fetal well-being assessment. The temporal dependency analysis further confirmed that LLMs develop meaningful representations of sequential CTG patterns rather than relying solely on aggregate features, as evidenced by their performance degradation when temporal ordering was shuffled.

However, it should be noted that the observed performance decline under temporal shuffling may not exclusively indicate temporal dependency learning, as the shuffling process could also introduce loss of information or disrupted non-temporal patterns that the models use for classification. Therefore, while our results strongly suggest temporal learning capabilities, we cannot definitively attribute performance degradation solely to the loss of temporal structure.

Our evaluation of GPT-5-mini without fine-tuning yielded particularly revealing results. Despite being a state-of-the-art model, GPT-5-mini demonstrated no meaningful discriminative ability for CTG classification, achieving performance worse than all trained models and exhibiting a strong bias toward positive predictions. This finding contrasts sharply with recent studies reporting successful CTG interpretation using zero-shot LLMs, though it should be noted that our evaluation set of 500 recordings substantially exceeds the sample sizes of fewer than 50 recordings used in those studies. The discrepancy suggests that previous positive results may not generalise to larger, more diverse and noisier clinical datasets.

Among traditional approaches, the CTG-specific architecture NeuroFetalNet consistently outperformed generalised time-series models such as Informer and TimesNet, achieving an AU-ROC of 0.795 compared to 0.766. This performance gap indicates that incorporating domain-specific architectural designs tailored to provide meaningful advantages over generic time-series approaches. However, even the best domain-specific model performed substantially lower than fine-tuned LLMs, suggesting that the benefits of large-scale pre-training and transfer learning outweigh specialised architectural innovations when sufficient fine-tuning data is available.

An unexpected finding emerged regarding medical domain pre-training. MediPhi, despite being specifically trained on medical texts, did not demonstrate superior performance compared to general-purpose LLMs when fine-tuned for CTG analysis
corbeil2025modular
. This suggests that generic language understanding capabilities may be more valuable than specialised medical knowledge for learning CTG pattern recognition.

Additionally, the computational requirements for processing long context windows in hour-long CTG recordings present significant infrastructure challenges. Our approach of using stride-based sampling to reduce context length enabled training on consumer-grade GPUs, but this may sacrifice important temporal information. Future implementations should carefully balance computational efficiency with model performance, potentially exploring smaller LLM architectures that maintain acceptable accuracy while reducing resource requirements.

Our preliminary experiments with zero-shot inference using general LLMs such as Llama 3.2 without fine-tuning revealed severe limitations, including frequent hallucinations and inconsistent outputs that rendered them unsuitable for clinical use. This finding reinforces the necessity of supervised fine-tuning for medical applications, even when using advanced pre-trained models. Additionally, while we maintained original hyperparameters from published works to ensure fair comparison, traditional DL models might benefit from extensive hyperparameter optimisation specific to CTG data, which could potentially narrow the performance gap with LLMs.

5
Conclusion

Across 500 antepartum CTG recordings, fine-tuned LLMs achieved higher discriminative performance than foundation time-series models and CTG-specific architectures, with the best AU-ROC reaching 0.853. Gains persisted in sensitivity analyses, although performance fell when temporal structure was disrupted or when uterine activity signals were withheld, indicating reliance on sequential information and the contribution of contraction patterns. Zero-shot inference with a large proprietary model performed poorly on this larger, noisier test set.

These results position fine-tuned LLMs as a credible option for automated CTG interpretation. However, clinical use will require prospective, multicentre validation; assessment of calibration, decision thresholds, and false positive burden; monitoring for distribution shift; and evaluation of equity, safety, and cost-effectiveness. Reporting should include rigorous error analyses and decision-analytic impact, not accuracy alone. Future work should test parameter-efficient adaptation methods for long sequences, explore multimodal integration with clinical covariates, and compare human–AI teaming against clinician-only review in pragmatic trials. Until such evidence is available, LLMs should be considered decision-support tools rather than replacements for expert interpretation.
