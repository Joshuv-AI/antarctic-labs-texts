Integrated molecular and histological profiling of preeclampsia suggests angiogenic dysregulation associated with maternal and fetal outcomes
Authors: Elif Gelenli Dolanbay, Meryem Hocaoglu, Tugay Mert, Hacer Inan Gungor, Gozde Calıskan Bender, Seyma Ozkanli, Handan Ankarali, Reyhan Ayaz Bilir, Zeynep Nur Komurcu, Damla Dokmeci Guney, Abdulkadir Turgut, Unal Uslu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preeclampsia (PE) is a potentially life-threatening multisystem disorder affecting maternal and fetal health and is associated with impaired angiogenesis, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and apoptosis. In this study, we characterized molecular, histopathological, and clinical alterations in placentas from 30 women with PE compared with 30 normotensive controls. Placental tissues underwent macroscopic evaluation, histopathology, immunohistochemistry for α-Klotho, endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), placental growth factor (PlGF), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), alongside TUNEL assay for apoptosis. PE pregnancies were associated with earlier delivery, lower neonatal birth weight, reduced placental weight and volume, and decreased maternal total protein and albumin levels. Histologically, PE placentas exhibited increased syncytial knots, intervillous fibrin deposition, distal villous hypoplasia, chorangiosis, avascular villi, and calcifications. Immunohistochemical analyses indicated reduced expression of α-Klotho, eNOS, VEGF, and PlGF, with increased TNF-α expression and higher apoptotic index. α-Klotho expression correlated positively with angiogenic markers, placental weight, and birth weight, and negatively with TNF-α and apoptosis. Multivariate regression analyses suggested that PE is associated with increased placental apoptosis and TNF-α expression, as well as reduced α-Klotho, eNOS, VEGF, and PlGF expression, and with lower birth weight, primarily mediated by prematurity. Early-onset PE exhibited more pronounced α-Klotho reduction with relatively preserved eNOS expression, suggesting potential compensatory endothelial responses, while magnesium sulfate or antihypertensive therapy did not significantly influence molecular profiles. These findings suggest that PE is linked to coordinated alterations in angiogenic, endothelial, and inflammatory pathways associated with placental structural changes and adverse perinatal outcomes. Observed alterations in α-Klotho and angiogenic markers highlight potential pathways for further investigation into targeted molecular interventions. This study is limited by its single-center, and relatively small sample size, which may limit generalizability, and causal relationships cannot be established; further multi-center prospective studies are warranted.

Introduction

Hypertensive diseases during at gestational time period, affecting %10 of all pregnant women worldwide, are the leading cause of chronic disability, excessive morbidity and the death of mothers and their infants1. Almost one tenth of all maternal deaths are associated with gestational hypertension disorders in Africa and Asia, while the quarter of maternal deaths in Latin America have been associated with these complications1.

Among the hypertensive diseases, preeclampsia (PE) is one of the three leading causes of maternal mortality and morbidity, fetal and perinatal complications around worldwide1,2. Preeclampsia is complicated multifactorial illness whose origin is yet unknown but researchers continue their researches about pathophysiology of its to facilitate diagnosis and treatment2.

Recent studies have further improved the traditional diagnostic criteria for preeclampsia. The criteria for preeclampsia were defined as accompanying significant proteinuria or unless present of proteinuria; presence of one or more organ dysfunction or some severe clinical symptoms when a pregnant woman is over 20 weeks of gestation and newly onset the systolic blood pressure above the 140mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure above the 90 mmHg2.

Despite the different ideas for PE pathophysiology, it is well known that preeclampsia is invariably linked to defective cytotrophoblast invasion and improper physiological changes3. Also, an increased maternal systemic inflammatory reaction has been identified, as well as an unbalanced both of circulating angiogenic and anti-angiogenic factors4. Given all of this, the placenta plays a critical role in the development of preeclampsia4. In recent years, immunological, angiogenic, hypoxic molecules and cell apoptozis have been investigated in many studies on placentas to elucidate the pathogenesis of PE. However, the data obtained from these studies could not be evaluated to form an integrity.

Hypoxia or hypoxic reoxygenation has been implicated in the upregulation of TNF-α synthesis by the placenta. Studies on human placental cell cultures have demonstrated that hypoxic conditions or hypoxia-reoxygenation events lead to increased TNF-α production5,6.

Angiogenic factors may also affect the invasion process in complicated pregnancy states such as preeclampsia. Hypoxia may cause local pseudovasculogenesis by causing local overexpression of angiogenic factors such as VEGF and angiopoietins associated with preeclampsia7. Impaired trophoblast invasion and impaired maternal spiral artery formation following inadequate or faulty angiogenesis have been shown as early placental defects in preeclampsia8. Impaired invasion and spiral artery formation have been shown to lead to defective utero-placental circulation and subsequent placental ischemia9.

Abnormal decidual and placental villous vasculature are hallmarks of preeclampsia. Taylor et al. demonstrated that serum placental growth factor (PlGF) levels are significantly reduced in PE10. They proposed that decreased PlGF synthesis disrupts normal placental angiogenesis through direct and indirect interactions with other vasculotropic growth factors. Furthermore, low PlGF levels in early gestation have been linked to an increased risk of developing preeclampsia. In 2003, Polliotti evaluated 20 preeclamptic and 60 healthy pregnant women and found that serum PlGF and VEGF levels were significantly lower in the preeclamptic group compared to normotensive pregnant women, highlighting their potential as predictive biomarkers for preeclampsia before clinical symptoms manifest11. It was emphasized that evaluation of serum PlGF and VEGF levels may be useful in predetermining patients who have not yet developed maternal syndrome but may develop pre-eclampsia.

Nitric oxide (NO) is a biological amine synthesized from the amino acid L-arginine via nitric oxide synthetase (NOS) and is present in all mammals. Endothelial NOS (eNOS) is one of the three isoforms of NO produced predominantly in endothelial cells12. Syncytiotrophoblast cells in normal placental villi have been identified as the source of eNOS13. However, studies on eNOS expression in preeclamptic placentas have yielded inconsistent results14,15. Consequently, eNOS immunolocalization may serve as a potential marker of endothelial dysfunction in PE.

The α-Klotho protein, a key regulator of oxidative stress and endothelial function, is expressed in the female reproductive system, including the breast, ovaries, oviduct, uterus, and placenta15,16. In placental tissue, α-Klotho is predominantly localized to the brush border of the syncytiotrophoblast, with lower expression in cytotrophoblast cells15. Ongoing studies conducted by our research team are also investigating the role of circulating maternal microRNAs as potential biomarkers for PE pathogenesis17,18.

Rather than aiming to identify novel individual biomarkers, the present study focuses on integrating clinical severity, placental histopathology, angiogenic and inflammatory marker expression, and apoptosis within the same cohort. This integrative approach allows evaluation of how these placental alterations collectively relate to maternal blood pressure severity and neonatal outcomes. Unlike previous studies that focused on individual biomarkers, our study uniquely integrates molecular, histological, and clinical data in the same cohort to explore these relationships.

Accordingly, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the combined impact of angiogenesis, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, hypoxia-related pathways, and apoptotic cell death on disease severity and clinical outcomes using placental expression of TNF-α, VEGF, PlGF, eNOS, α-Klotho, and TUNEL assay for the detection of apoptotic DNA fragmentation.

Specifically, this study aimed to:

(i)assess clinical, histological, and molecular placental alterations in PE compared with normotensive pregnancies;(ii)explore the relationships between placental molecular markers and maternal–fetal outcomes;(iii)evaluate early- versus late-onset PE and treatment subgroups to explore potential differences reflecting disease severity and placental dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Study population and tissue collection

This study was supported by the Istanbul Medeniyet University Scientific Research Projects Coordination Unit (Project No: T-GAP-2021-1717). Ethical approval was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul Medeniyet University Göztepe Training and Research Hospital (Decision No: 2020/0414; Date: June 24, 2020). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional ethics committee and with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study. Between April 2022 and December 2023, 30 women with PE and 30 women with normal pregnancy who had hospitalized in the Gynaecology and Obstetrics Clinic of Istanbul Medeniyet University, Prof. Dr. Süleyman Yalcın City Hospital were included in the study Preeclampsia was defined as new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks of gestation, characterized by a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, accompanied by proteinuria (≥ 0.3 g/day). In the absence of proteinuria, pre-eclampsia was diagnosed based on new-onset hypertension with evidence of maternal organ dysfunction, including thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 100,000/µL), renal insufficiency (serum creatinine > 1.1 mg/dL), impaired liver function (a twofold or greater increase in AST and/or ALT levels), pulmonary edema, or new-onset cerebral or visual symptoms (e.g., headache, blurred vision, flashes of light, or scotoma)19. Patients diagnosed with PE before 34 weeks of gestation were considered as early-onset PE. Patients in the control group were selected from participants who were healthy, had a singleton pregnancy, delivered without complications, and maternal age matched with the patients in the PE group. Exclusion criteria are: multiple pregnancy, intrauterine death, pregestational and gestational diabetes, chronic hypertension, comorbidities (e.g. rheumatological disease, epilepsy, hepatitis, etc.), pregnancy complications (e.g. gestational cholestasis, abruption of placenta, placenta accreata, etc.). Clinical, demographic and laboratory characteristics of pregnant women in the patient and control groups were recorded.

Postpartum placenta samples of 30 PE and 30 control group patients who met the inclusion criteria were evaluated macroscopically in the Pathology Department of Istanbul Medeniyet University Göztepe Training and Research Hospital and histologically and immunohistochemically in the Histology and Embryology Laboratory of Istanbul Medeniyet University Faculty of Medicine.

Histological evaluation

The placenta specimens were macroscopically analysed to determine the size and weight. The umbilical cord location, the condition, number and size of the cotyledons were evaluated. After evaluation, two 2 × 1 × 0.5 cm samples from the central and peripheral regions of the placenta were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formaldehyde. Placenta sampling included full-thickness placental blocks. Subsequently, the tissues were processed by automatic tissue processor (LOGOS Microwave Hybrid Tissue Processor, Milestone, Valbrembo, Italy) and embedded in paraffin blocks. Sections obtained from the paraffin blocks at a thickness of 5 μm were stained with haematoxylin & eosin and periodic acid schiff (PAS) staining for light microscopic evaluation. Histologic evaluation criteria included increased syncytial knots, increased intervillous fibrin-villous agglutination, distal villous hypoplasia, villous oedema, chorangiosis, avascular villi and calcification20–22. Histological changes were recorded as present or absent. All sections were evaluated with olimpos BX53 light microscope.

Immunohistochemical study

Sections obtained from paraffin blocks on positively charged slides at a thickness of 5 μm were processed for immunohistochemical studies. The sections were kept overnight in an oven at 60 ᵒC, deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated with decreasing alcohol series. Tris-EDTA buffer (pH = 9) was used for antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was suppressed by using Ultravision Hydrogen Peroxide Block (TA125H2Q2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Large Volume Ultra V Block (TA125UB, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) was used to prevent nonspecific background staining. Sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies (TNF alpha; 1/200 dilution, sc-130349, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. Dallas, Texas, USA, PLGF; 1/400 dilution, NBP2-67067, Novus Biologicals, LLC, Centennial, CO, USA, α-Klotho; 1/400 dilution, sc-515940, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. Dallas, Texas, USA, NOS3; 1/200 dilution, sc-376751, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. Dallas, Texas, USA, VEGF; 1/100 dilution, sc-53462, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. Dallas, Texas, USA) at + 4 ᵒC. UltraVision Large Volume Detection System Anti-Polyvalent, HRP (RTU, TP125HL, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) kit was used for secondary antibody reaction. Phosphate-buffered saline was used as washing solution at all steps. Reaction products were demonstrated with 3,3-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) substrate kit (TA125HD, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) which produced a brown insoluble precipitate at immunopositive sites. After counterstaining with hematoxylin, sections were passed through a rapidly increasing series of alcohols and xylene and covered with synthetic mounting medium. Sections were imaged with Olympos DP21 light microscope camera. Four randomly selected fields of stained areas were examined at x400 magnification. H-score was calculated by evaluating the staining intensity (absent, weak, moderate, strong) and percentage of cells23. Each antibody was assessed by calculating H-scores separately for the peripheral (P) and central (C) villous regions, and their arithmetic means were used as overall expression levels.

TUNEL assay

ApopTag In Situ Apoptosis Detection Kit (S7101, Millipore Corp., San Francisco, CA) for terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP-mediated nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated with decreasing alcohol series. The slides were digested with Proteinase K (S3020, DAKO Agilent Technologies, Inc. Santa Clara, CA, USA) for 15 min at room temperature and endogenous peroxidase activity was suppressed by using Ultravision Hydrogen Peroxide Block (TA125H2Q2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The slides were incubated with TdT for 1 h at 37 ᵒC. The DNA fragments were then allowed to bind to a peroxidase-conjugated anti-digoxigenin antibody for 30 min at room temperature. The antibody complexes were visualized with DAB chromogen. Phosphate-buffered saline was used as washing solution at all steps. The sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and covered with synthetic mounting medium. The slides were evaluated with olimpos BX53 light microscope. For the assessment of apoptotic index of tissues, TUNEL positive and negative nuclei in ten high-power fields (x400 magnifications) with staining were counted semiquantitatively, the ratio of TUNEL positive nuclei were assesed as apoptotic index of the slides24.

All immunohistochemical and TUNEL assessments were independently scored by two observers who were blinded to the clinical data. Interobserver agreement was calculated using kappa statistics to ensure scoring reliability.”

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are expressed as number (percentage).

Between-group comparisons (preeclampsia vs. normotensive controls) were initially performed using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test.

Correlation analyses between clinical parameters, placental histopathological features, and immunohistochemical markers were conducted using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients, depending on data distribution. These analyses were considered exploratory and descriptive, aiming to identify patterns of association rather than to infer independent effects.

To assess whether preeclampsia independently predicted placental molecular markers and clinical outcomes, multivariate linear regression analyses were performed. For immunohistochemical outcomes (apoptosis index, eNOS, α-Klotho, VEGF, PlGF, and TNF-α expression), preeclampsia status was entered as the primary independent variable, with maternal age, smoking status (yes/no), and gestational age at delivery included as a priori confounders based on biological plausibility and known associations with placental pathology.

Additional multivariate models were constructed for neonatal outcomes. Birth weight was analyzed using a model adjusted for gestational age at delivery, small for gestational age (SGA) status, and placental weight. The 1-minute Apgar score was analyzed using a model adjusted for gestational age at delivery, birth weight, and SGA status. Regression coefficients (B), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p values are reported. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

The demographic characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. Maternal age was similar between the preeclampsia and control groups (30 ± 1.04 vs. 30.47 ± 1.03 years, p = 0.751). Gravidity (1.97 ± 0.21 vs. 2.83 ± 0.25, p = 0.007) and parity (0.80 ± 0.19 vs. 1.30 ± 0.19, p = 0.036) were significantly higher in the control group. The frequency of previous abortions did not differ between groups (17% vs. 23.3%, p = 0.519). Smoking prevalence was higher in the preeclampsia group (23.3% vs. 3.3%), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.052). Pre-pregnancy BMI, calculated from maternal height and self-reported pre-pregnancy weight, was comparable between groups (27.59 ± 1.24 vs. 25.83 ± 1.01, p = 0.301).

Obstetric and neonatal outcomes

Obstetric outcomes are summarized in Table 2. Maternal BMI at delivery, calculated from height and weight measured upon hospital admission, did not differ significantly between groups (32.03 ± 1.13 vs. 29.6 ± 0.90 kg/m², p = 0.167). Both systolic and diastolic blood pressures were significantly higher in the preeclampsia group (149.90 ± 3.38 vs. 116.73 ± 2.15 mmHg and 90.33 ± 2.29 vs. 71.73 ± 1.94 mmHg, respectively; both p < 0.001). Antenatal corticosteroid administration was more frequent in patients with preeclampsia (30% vs. 3.3%, p = 0.006).

Gestational age at delivery was significantly lower in the preeclampsia group (36.08 ± 0.47 vs. 37.72 ± 0.25 weeks, p = 0.004). There were no significant differences between groups in mode of delivery, incidence of oligohydramnios, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), or small-for-gestational-age (SGA) rates. Birth weight was significantly lower in the preeclampsia group (2533.97 ± 131.41 g vs. 3248.10 ± 80.15 g, p < 0.001). The 1-minute Apgar score was reduced in the preeclampsia group (6.10 ± 0.40 vs. 7.37 ± 0.27, p = 0.036), while 5-minute Apgar scores were similar. Fetal sex distribution did not differ significantly between groups.

Laboratory findings

Laboratory parameters are summarized in Table 3. Hemoglobin, leukocyte count, platelet count, AST, ALT, LDH, and creatinine levels were similar between groups (all p > 0.05). In contrast, total protein and albumin levels were significantly lower in the preeclampsia group (p = 0.017 and p < 0.001, respectively). Proteinuria was markedly more frequent among patients with preeclampsia (76.7% vs. 10%, p < 0.001).

Placental macroscopic and histological findings

Placental macroscopic findings revealed significantly lower placental volume (472.14 ± 32.30 vs. 575.02 ± 31.98, p = 0.027) and weight (459.13 ± 30.65 vs. 624.27 ± 37.64, p < 0.001) in the preeclampsia group, whereas cotyledon number and umbilical cord insertion site did not differ. Histological examination demonstrated significantly higher frequencies of syncytial knot increase, intervillous fibrin deposition, distal villous hypoplasia, villous edema, chorangiosis, avascular villi, and calcification in the preeclampsia group (all p < 0.001). These findings are summarized in Table 4 and illustrated in Fig. 1.

Immunohistochemical findings

Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated significant differences in the expression of all evaluated markers between groups. α-Klotho expression was markedly lower in the preeclampsia group (203.75 ± 2.82) compared to controls (279.54 ± 5.80, p < 0.001). The positive correlation between α-Klotho and pro-angiogenic markers (VEGF, PlGF) and its inverse relationship with TNF-α and apoptosis highlights α-Klotho as a potential key regulator of placental homeostasis in PE. Similarly, eNOS, PlGF, and VEGF expressions were significantly reduced in the preeclampsia group (216.76 ± 4.47, 127.80 ± 4.30, 46.10 ± 2.60, respectively) relative to the control group (276.92 ± 5.48, 284.79 ± 5.41, 225.58 ± 5.58, respectively; all p < 0.001). In contrast, TNF-α expression was significantly elevated in the preeclampsia group (161.91 ± 5.21) compared with the control group (18.38 ± 0.98, p < 0.05). TUNEL assay revealed a significantly higher apoptotic index in the preeclampsia than the control group (5.71 ± 0.35 vs. 0.53 ± 0.09, p < 0.001), consistent with the representative immunohistochemical images shown in Figs. 2 and 3 and the statistical comparisons illustrated in Fig. 4.

Multivariate regression analyses of placental molecular markers

After adjustment for maternal age, smoking status, and gestational age at delivery, multivariate linear regression analyses identified preeclampsia as an independent predictor of placental molecular alterations. Preeclampsia was independently associated with an increased apoptosis index (B = 5.13, 95% CI: 3.78–6.48, p < 0.001) and increased TNF-α expression (B = 136.47, 95% CI: 119.34–153.59, p < 0.001).

Conversely, preeclampsia independently predicted reduced expression of endothelial and angiogenic markers, including eNOS (B = − 61.42, 95% CI: −78.37 to − 44.69, p < 0.001), α-Klotho (B = − 73.50, 95% CI: −87.08 to − 59.91, p < 0.001), PlGF (B = − 159.16, 95% CI: −176.17 to − 142.15, p < 0.001), and VEGF (B = − 182.61, 95% CI: −194.78 to − 170.45, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that the observed molecular changes were not solely attributable to differences in gestational age or maternal characteristics.

Multivariate regression analyses of neonatal outcomes

In multivariate analysis adjusting for gestational age at delivery, SGA status, and placental weight, preeclampsia remained an independent predictor of reduced birth weight. The presence of preeclampsia was associated with an average decrease of 209.6 g in birth weight (B = − 209.61, 95% CI: −392.55 to − 26.67, p = 0.025).

In contrast, when the 1-minute Apgar score was analyzed in a model adjusted for gestational age at delivery, birth weight, and SGA status, preeclampsia was not identified as an independent predictor (p = 0.165). This suggests that the lower Apgar scores observed in univariate analyses were largely mediated by prematurity and fetal growth restriction rather than by preeclampsia itself.

Subgroup and treatment analyses

Comparison between early-onset (n = 9) and late-onset (n = 21) preeclampsia subgroups revealed no significant difference in the apoptotic index (5.43 ± 0.89 vs. 5.83 ± 0.44, p = 0.651). eNOS expression was significantly higher in the early-onset group compared to the late-onset group (231.55 ± 9.31 vs. 210.43 ± 4.46, p = 0.028). α-Klotho expression in the central region of the placenta was significantly lower in early-onset preeclampsia (212.14 ± 6.86 vs. 231.32 ± 5.60, p = 0.046), whereas no significant differences were observed between subgroups for peripheral α-Klotho expression, PlGF, VEGF, or TNF-α (all p > 0.05). Histological evaluation demonstrated no statistically significant differences between the subgroups for syncytial knot increase, intervillous fibrin deposition, distal villous hypoplasia, villous edema, chorangiosis, avascular villi, or calcification. However, avascular villi were present in all patients (100%) with early-onset preeclampsia compared to (71.4%) of those with late-onset preeclampsia, a difference that did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.141).

Comparison of patients with preeclampsia based on magnesium sulfate treatment status (n = 14 for treated, n = 16 for nontreated) revealed that VEGF expression in the peripheral region of the placenta was significantly lower in the non-treated group compared to the treated group (55.66 ± 2.98 vs. 75.54 ± 9.59, p = 0.045). Magnesium sulfate treatment was associated with significantly higher VEGF expression in the peripheral region of the placenta, suggesting a potential protective role against placental angiogenic failure. No significant differences were observed between the groups in the apoptotic index, eNOS, α-Klotho, PlGF, or TNFα-expression (all p > 0.05). Histological evaluation revealed no statistically significant differences between the groups for all assessed features. However, increased intervillous fibrin deposition was observed in all patients (100%) who did not receive magnesium sulfate therapy, compared to 85.7% of those who received it (p > 0.05). Similarly, calcification was detected in all patients (100%) without magnesium sulfate therapy, whereas it was present in 78.6% of those who received treatment (p > 0.05).

Comparison between methyldopa-treated (n = 11) and non-treated (n = 19) preeclampsia patients were compared according to methyldopa treatment status, no statistically significant differences were found in the apoptotic index, expression levels of any immunohistochemical markers (eNOS, α-Klotho, PlGF, VEGF, TNF-α), or the frequency of histopathological alterations (all p > 0.05).

Given the limited sample size within the early- and late-onset and treatment-based subgroups, multivariate regression analyses were not performed for subgroup comparisons to avoid overfitting and unstable parameter estimates. Subgroup analyses were therefore considered exploratory.

Correlation analyses

Exploratory correlation analyses were performed to examine the relationships between selected clinical severity parameters and placental molecular findings. Higher systolic blood pressure during pregnancy was positively correlated with the apoptotic index (r = 0.602, p < 0.001) and TNF-α expression (r = 0.614, p < 0.001), while showing significant inverse correlations with angiogenic and protective markers, including eNOS (r = − 0.558, p < 0.001), α-Klotho (r = − 0.535, p < 0.001), PlGF (r = − 0.474, p = 0.001), and VEGF expression (r = − 0.498, p = 0.001).

Placental weight was positively associated with placental angiogenic marker expression, including eNOS (r = 0.314, p = 0.036), α-Klotho (r = 0.464, p = 0.001), PlGF (r = 0.464, p = 0.001), and VEGF (r = 0.370, p = 0.012). In contrast, higher placental weight was inversely correlated with the apoptotic index (r = − 0.388, p = 0.009) and TNF-α expression (r = − 0.560, p < 0.001).

These correlations were interpreted as reflecting overall disease severity rather than independent biological relationships; therefore, selected key outcomes were further examined using multivariate regression analyses to account for potential confounding factors.

Discussion

The placenta is a central organ in pregnancy, orchestrating fetal development through the exchange of oxygen, nutrients, and waste products between maternal and fetal circulation. Beyond this transport role, it functions as a dynamic endocrine organ, producing hormones that regulate maternal physiology, support fetal growth, and influence both maternal and fetal health throughout gestation and postpartum25–27. PE is a complex, multisystem disorder characterized by new-onset hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation, resulting from maternal endothelial dysfunction and impaired placental perfusion28. PE increases long-term maternal risks for cardiovascular disease, stroke, and metabolic complications, while offspring are prone to preterm birth, fetal growth restriction, perinatal mortality, and neurodevelopmental delays22,28. Its pathophysiology involves impaired angiogenesis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and maladaptive fetoplacental responses, linking molecular disruptions to clinical outcomes.

In this study, we performed a systematic and integrated evaluation of placental tissues from preeclamptic and normotensive pregnancies. By combining molecular (IHC, TUNEL), histological, macroscopic, clinical, and laboratory data, we aimed to provide a holistic understanding of PE pathophysiology. Molecular analyses focused on angiogenic and endothelial regulators—α-Klotho, eNOS, PlGF, VEGF, TNF α—and apoptosis. Histopathology assessed syncytial knots, intervillous fibrin deposition, distal villous hypoplasia, villous edema, chorangiosis, avascular villi, and calcifications. Macroscopic assessments included placental volume, weight, cotyledon number, and umbilical cord insertion. Clinical and laboratory evaluations incorporated maternal age, parity, gravidity, BMI, gestational age at delivery, birth weight, Apgar scores, cesarean section rates, pregnancy complications, and protein/albumin levels. Subgroup analyses explored early- versus late-onset PE and the impact of magnesium sulfate or antihypertensive therapy, which should be interpreted cautiously given the modest sample size.

Maternal age did not differ significantly between PE and control groups, suggesting that age alone is insufficient to predict risk when parity and comorbidities are considered22. Women with PE had lower parity and gravidity, consistent with the higher prevalence of PE in primiparous women29. PE pregnancies delivered earlier and resulted in neonates with lower birth weights, reflecting placental insufficiency2,22. One-minute Apgar scores were lower in PE, while five-minute scores were comparable, indicating selective impairment of early neonatal adaptation. Cesarean rates and oligohydramnios prevalence did not differ, highlighting that PE primarily affects fetal growth rather than obstetric management. Laboratory analyses revealed lower total protein and albumin levels in PE pregnancies, reflecting endothelial dysfunction and glomerular injury30,31. Other markers, including AST, ALT, LDH, platelet counts, hemoglobin, leukocyte counts, and creatinine, were comparable, emphasizing the specificity of protein and albumin as early indicators of endothelial compromise.

Placental morphology reflected molecular derangements. PE placentas exhibited reduced weight and volume2,3, with increased syncytial knots, intervillous fibrin, distal villous hypoplasia, villous edema, chorangiosis, avascular villi, and calcifications8,20,22,32. Histologically, these features indicate accelerated villous maturation and compensatory adaptations to hypoxia. Intervillous fibrin deposition, perivillous thrombi, and leukocyte infiltration highlight maternal inflammatory contributions, reinforcing the link between histological changes and impaired fetal growth.

At the molecular level, α-Klotho deficiency emerged as a central feature of PE placentas, inversely correlating with apoptosis and TNF-α expression, and positively correlating with eNOS, VEGF, PlGF, and placental weight33–35. α-Klotho normally exerts anti-apoptotic, antioxidative, and pro-angiogenic effects, making its reduction a critical contributor to endothelial dysfunction. eNOS downregulation reduces nitric oxide availability, promoting vasoconstriction and hypoperfusion14,36. Reduced VEGF and PlGF further contribute to distal villous hypoplasia, avascular villi, and chorangiosis, while elevated TNF-α reflects inflammatory stress correlated with maternal blood pressure and apoptotic indices3,6,37. Although sFlt-1 is implicated in PE36,38,39, our findings indicate that α-Klotho, VEGF/PlGF, and eNOS alterations likely intersect with sFlt-1–related pathways, providing mechanistic insight without direct measurement. Immunohistochemistry confirmed these molecular disruptions, and early-onset PE cases displayed lower α-Klotho but relatively higher eNOS, suggesting localized compensatory endothelial responses. Notably, magnesium sulfate treatment was associated with relatively higher VEGF expression, suggesting potential protective effects on placental angiogenesis.

Correlation analyses revealed that maternal age and gravidity positively correlated with α-Klotho, PlGF, and VEGF levels, and negatively with apoptosis. Later gestational age at PE diagnosis correlated with improved maternal and fetal outcomes. Higher pre-pregnancy and gestational BMI were associated with increased systolic blood pressure and decreased VEGF expression. Elevated maternal blood pressure correlated with reduced placental weight, α-Klotho, eNOS, PlGF, VEGF, and increased apoptosis and TNF-α, highlighting the integration of maternal hemodynamics with placental molecular and structural alterations. Placental weight positively correlated with birth weight and pro-angiogenic markers. Multivariate regression analyses confirmed that preeclampsia independently predicted molecular alterations and lower birth weight after adjustment for gestational age and BMI, while 1-minute Apgar was mediated primarily by gestational age and fetal growth.

Translational Implications: This integrated dataset emphasizes that endothelial, angiogenic, and inflammatory dysregulation are interconnected in PE. Therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring α-Klotho, enhancing eNOS activity, rebalancing VEGF/PlGF signaling, or suppressing TNF-α could mitigate placental apoptosis and improve perfusion. Early-onset PE may involve compensatory endothelial mechanisms, informing timing and targeting of interventions.

Limitations

Limitations of this study include a modest sample size, particularly within the early-onset PE subgroup (n = 9), which may limit the generalizability of some findings. The study focused exclusively on normotensive controls and PE pregnancies, precluding an evaluation of other placental pathologies such as gestational hypertension or intrauterine growth restriction without PE. While sFlt-1 was not directly measured, the observed alterations in α-Klotho, VEGF/PlGF, and eNOS may intersect with sFlt-1–related pathways, providing potential insights into underlying angiogenic imbalances. Additionally, although multivariate regression was used to adjust for gestational age and maternal characteristics, some correlations might still be influenced by unmeasured confounders. As an observational study, direct causality cannot be inferred; therefore, the α-Klotho-related mechanisms identified here require functional validation in future experimental studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PE placentas show a molecular profile characterized by reduced expression of α-Klotho, eNOS, and angiogenic factors (VEGF, PlGF), alongside increased TNF-α levels and apoptosis. These molecular alterations appear to be associated with placental structural changes and some adverse clinical outcomes, such as lower birth weight and placental volume. Integrating molecular, histological, and clinical data provides a framework for further exploring PE as a spectrum of placental dysfunction. While the small sample size of early-onset cases necessitates cautious interpretation, these observations may support future investigations into the role of α-Klotho and angiogenic markers, both in experimental models and clinical studies, to better understand placental pathophysiology and maternal–fetal outcomes.
