Abscopal effect in a biopsy-proven chromophobe renal cell carcinoma before planned cryoablation
Authors: Hamza Furmli, Abdullah Alenezi, Syed Naqvi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe abscopal effect, defined as regression of untreated tumors following localized therapy, is rare in renal cell carcinoma (RCC). We present a case of a 49-year-old male who was incidentally found to have a 1.2 cm enhancing mass in the medial lower pole of the left kidney. MRI confirmed a small solid neoplasm, and percutaneous biopsy confirmed histopathological chromophobe RCC diagnosis. The patient was scheduled for cryoablation. On pre-procedural multiphasic CT, the lesion had completely regressed, leaving only a 6 mm cortical scar without enhancement. Cryoablation was aborted, and the patient was placed on active surveillance. This case illustrates the abscopal effect in primary RCC, likely triggered by biopsy as the inciting local event. Recognition of this rare immune-mediated phenomenon emphasizes the importance of confirmatory imaging before intervention and highlights the evolving interplay between radiology, minimally invasive procedures, and tumor immunology.Keywords: Renal cell carcinoma, Chromophobe, Abscopal effect, Biopsy, Cryoablation, Immunology

Introduction

Small renal masses (SRMs) are to increasingly identified with cross-sectional imaging, with up to 85% representing renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [1]. Treatment is tailored to tumor size, histology, and patient comorbidity, ranging from nephron-sparing surgery to ablation or surveillance [2]. The abscopal effect, first described by Mole in 1953, refers to regression of tumors outside the field of localized therapy [3]. It has been most frequently reported in lymphoma and melanoma, usually after radiotherapy. Theoretical explanation includes tumor antigen release and dendritic cell activation trigger systemic T-cell responses [4,5]. RCC is a highly immunogenic tumor and has been associated with spontaneous regression, particularly of pulmonary metastases after nephrectomy [6]. However, the abscopal effect in RCC remains rare and poorly documented [7]. We report a case of biopsy-proven chromophobe RCC that completely regressed prior to cryoablation, consistent with an abscopal effect triggered by percutaneous biopsy as thought be immune-priming insult.

Case report

A 49-year-old male presented to emergency department with abdominal pain. Initial ultrasound revealed multiple hepatic lesions consistent with hemangiomas. CT abdomen confirmed stable hepatic hemangiomas and incidentally identified a 1.2 cm hyperdense and enhancing lesion in the medial lower pole of the left kidney.

Follow up outpatient contrast-enhanced MRI demonstrated a 1.1 × 0.9 cm enhancing cortical mass. Patient was referred to Urology and subsequently to Radiology for percutaneous Ultrasound guided biopsy.

An ultrasound-guided biopsy yielded tissue diagnostic for chromophobe RCC. Histopathology showed eosinophilic cells with perinuclear clearing, positive for PAX8, CK7, and c-KIT, with patchy CD10 positivity; CAIX, AMACR, and vimentin were negative.

A multidisciplinary tumor board recommended percutaneous cryoablation. Interventional radiology consultation confirmed technical feasibility.

On the day of scheduled ablation, pre-procedural multiphasic contrast enhanced CT and targeted US demonstrated no enhancing lesion. Instead, a 6 mm non-enhancing cortical hypodensity with parenchymal loss and capsular retraction at site of know solid lesion. Cryoablation was aborted. The patient was placed on active surveillance.

Discussion

The abscopal effect is an immune-mediated phenomenon in which local therapy induces regression of distant or untreated tumors [3]. Although classically described after radiotherapy, it has also been observed following surgery or ablation [4,8]. Mechanistically, tumor antigen release during local injury promotes dendritic cell priming and systemic T-cell responses, with subsequent regression of residual tumor tissue [5].

RCC is strongly immunogenic, historically demonstrating rare spontaneous regressions and responsiveness to cytokine and checkpoint-based immunotherapies.6 Documented cases of abscopal effect in RCC are rare, usually following radiotherapy.7 Our case highlights a primary RCC that regressed after biopsy alone, extending the scope of the abscopal phenomenon. The mechanical and immunologic disruption from biopsy may have been sufficient to trigger regression, overlapping with mechanisms described after radiotherapy [[9], [10]].

Chromophobe RCC represents about 5% of RCC and typically demonstrates indolent behavior and favorable outcomes [[11], [12]]. Its biology may render it more susceptible to immune-mediated involution. Prior reports have documented regression of biopsy-proven RCCs, including chromophobe subtype, supporting this observation [13].

From a radiology practice perspective, this case highlights several important considerations. Confirmatory imaging prior to intervention is essential and, in this instance, avoided an unnecessary procedure. At the same time, apparent resolution on imaging should be interpreted with caution. Even when a lesion is no longer visible, the possibility of residual or recurrent disease cannot be excluded. Close imaging follow-up is therefore important, as recurrence has been reported in similar contexts and may occur after an interval period [14]. Ongoing surveillance allows for early detection and timely management if the lesion reappears. This case also reflects the broader interaction between image-guided procedures and tumor biology. Even minimally invasive interventions such as biopsy may have unintended immunologic effects, an area that remains incompletely understood and warrants further study [15].

Although re-biopsy was not performed, the lack of enhancement on multiphasic imaging, together with prior histologic confirmation, supports true regression as the most likely explanation. Continued follow-up will be important to assess the durability of this response (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5).
